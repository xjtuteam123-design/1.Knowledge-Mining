# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Matrix phase
- α-Al dendritic cells with eutectic α-Al matrix, Si particles, and intermetallic particle (IMP) cell boundaries

## Intermetallic phases in Al–Si–Mg–Cu alloy
- **β-AlFeSi** (Fe-rich, Chinese script morphology, grey contrast)
  - Average area: 12.5 μm²
- **Q-Al₅Cu₂Mg₈Si₆** (Cu-rich, bright contrast, fine blocky morphology)
  - Average area: 8.15 μm²
- **Mg₂Si** (Chinese script morphology, darkest colour)
- **Eutectic Si** particles (dark grey)

## Intermetallic phases in Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni alloy
- **γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni**: coarse block-like morphology, rhombohedral crystal structure, space group R3̅2H, lattice parameters a = b = 4.10 Å, c = 39.97 Å
  - Average area: 23.8 μm²
- **δ-Al₃NiCu**: fine needle-like morphology, orthorhombic structure, space group Pnma, a = 6.59 Å, b = 7.35 Å, c = 4.80 Å
  - Average area: 4.70 μm²
- **Q-Al₅Cu₂Mg₈Si₆**: hexagonal structure, space group P6̅, a = b = 10.39 Å, c = 4.01 Å
  - Average area: 8.68 μm²

## Eutectic Si particle roundness
- As-cast: Al–Si–Mg–Cu = 0.22 μm; Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni = 0.19 μm
- T6-treated: Al–Si–Mg–Cu = 0.58 μm; Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni = 0.59 μm

## Grain boundary / phase boundary features
- KAM value at the γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni and Al matrix phase boundary was 3.3 times higher than in the Al matrix (before corrosion)
- Maximum KAM value at pit-matrix boundary after corrosion: ~3° (three times higher than before corrosion)

**The paper does not discuss**: solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, dispersion strengthening, ODS strengthening, or grain-boundary strengthening — these strengthening categories are not directly relevant to this Al-Si alloy study. The strengthening discussion focuses on load transfer to rigid phases (eutectic Si and IMPs) and dislocation hindrance.

# Mechanical Properties

## Tensile test conditions
- Universal tensile testing machine, 100 kN load
- Temperatures: room temperature, 200, 250, 300, 350 °C (±2 °C)
- Standard: ASTM E8/E8M-22
- Strain rate: 10⁻³ s⁻¹
- Specimen heating time: 30 min before testing
- Extensometer used for strain measurement

## Key findings
- **Low temperatures (RT to 250 °C)**: Al–Si–Mg–Cu alloy exhibited higher elongation, tensile strength, and yield strength than Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni alloy
- **Above 250 °C**: Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni alloys demonstrated similar or higher tensile and yield strengths compared to Al–Si–Mg–Cu alloy
- **Elongation**: increased with testing temperature at the expense of strength for both alloys
- **Fracture mode at low temperatures**: brittle fracture with less than 1% elongation for the Ni-containing alloy
- **Fracture mode at high temperatures (300–350 °C)**: ductile failure with higher elongation, characterized by elongated dendritic cells

## Fracture surface observations
- As-cast Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni tested at 200 °C: coarse γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni IMPs present near fractured surface; interdendritic fracture mode
- At higher temperatures (300, 350 °C): larger elongated cells due to matrix softening; cracks initiate in eutectic regions and propagate through dendritic cells
- At 200 °C: γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni and δ-Al₃NiCu particles remained uncracked; very fine cracks nucleated in disintegrated eutectic Si particles
- At 350 °C: large microvoids formed within γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni and eutectic Si particles; small microvoids within fine δ-Al₃NiCu particles

**Specific numerical strength values are not provided in the paper text.** The paper presents graphical data (Fig. 6), but does not list specific tensile or yield strength values in the text.

**Not reported**: hardness, elastic modulus, fracture toughness, creep performance, rupture life, density, compressive strength, compressive plasticity

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

**Note**: This paper investigates aqueous corrosion (salt spray pitting corrosion), not high-temperature oxidation.

## Corrosion test details
- Salt spray testing: 480 h
- Corrosion products removed before surface assessment
- Corrosion rate calculated using ASTM G01 equation: Corrosion rate = W × K / (A × t × D)
  - K = 87,600 mm/yr

## Corrosion results
- Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni alloy exhibited significantly higher number density of corrosion pits
- Corrosion pits in the Ni-containing alloy were deeper
- Corrosion pits initiated in the eutectic region and penetrated through thickness and depth
- Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni alloy showed larger weight loss and higher corrosion rate than the base alloy (specific values in graphical form, Fig. 9(e))

## Volta potential / Electron work function (EWF) results
- γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni: EWF = 4.27 eV; ΔVPD vs Al matrix = 0.38 V
- δ-Al₃NiCu: EWF = 4.26 eV; ΔVPD vs Al matrix = 0.36 V
- Q-phase: EWF = 4.22 eV; ΔVPD vs Al matrix = 0.25 V
- Eutectic α-Al matrix adjacent to γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni: EWF = 3.99 eV

## Corrosion mechanisms discussed
- Microgalvanic coupling between cathodic IMPs and anodic Al matrix
- γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni acts as the primary cathode in Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni alloy
- High dislocation density at IMP-matrix interface lowers EWF locally, amplifying localized pitting
- Ni addition degrades corrosion resistance
- Mg₂Si particles heavily oxidized near pits
- Isolated pits were surrounded by high density of Cu-rich Q-phase particles in Al–Si–Mg–Cu alloy
- In Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni alloy, isolated pits localized around γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni phase

**Not discussed**: oxidation weight gain, protective oxide layers, high-temperature oxidation, oxygen embrittlement, nitrogen embrittlement, grain-boundary oxidation

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## 1. Ni (Nickel)

**[Element]**
Ni

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni casting alloys (automotive piston and engine component alloys)

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
0.50 wt% Ni (compared to 0.03 wt% Ni in base alloy)

**[Main Function]**
High-temperature alloy strengthening through formation of thermally stable Ni-rich intermetallic particles (IMPs), specifically γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni and δ-Al₃NiCu phases.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
Ni induces formation of coarse block-like γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni (rhombohedral, R3̅2H, a = 4.10 Å, c = 39.97 Å) and fine needle-like δ-Al₃NiCu (orthorhombic, Pnma, a = 6.59 Å, b = 7.35 Å, c = 4.80 Å). At low temperatures, these IMPs hinder dislocation motion and transfer load from the eutectic α-Al matrix, contributing to strengthening. At high temperatures, increased dislocation mobility leads to dislocation accumulation at matrix-particle interfaces, causing void nucleation and coalescence within IMPs, which reduces strengthening contribution. KAM at γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni/matrix interface is 3.3 times higher than in the Al matrix, indicating residual stress and high dislocation density.

**[Property Effect]**
- At temperatures up to 250 °C: Ni-containing alloy showed lower elongation, tensile strength, and yield strength than the base alloy
- Above 250 °C: Ni-containing alloy showed similar or higher tensile and yield strengths
- At low temperatures: brittle fracture with less than 1% elongation
- At high temperatures (300–350 °C): ductile failure with higher elongation
- Corrosion: Increased corrosion rate and deeper pitting; larger weight loss after 480 h salt spray
- The paper states: "The presence of coarse block-like γ-Al7Cu4Ni and thin needle-like δ-Al3NiCu IMPs contributed to low-temperature alloy strengthening during tensile tests. However, void nucleation and coalescence within the IMPs facilitated ductile fracture and alloy rupture, resulting in a lower tensile strength at high temperatures."

**[Potential Side Effects]**
- Significant degradation of corrosion resistance due to strong galvanic coupling between cathodic Ni-rich IMPs and anodic Al matrix
- Promotes localized pitting corrosion, especially around γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni particles (primary cathode)
- At low temperatures, promotes brittle fracture behavior
- Higher fraction of coarse IMPs associated with stress localization

**[Original Evidence]**
- Abstract, Results Section 3.3, Discussion Section 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, Fig. 3, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 11, Fig. 12, Fig. 13, Fig. 15

## 2. Cu (Copper)

**[Element]**
Cu

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Al–Si–Mg–Cu and Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni casting alloys

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
1.04 wt% Cu (Al–Si–Mg–Cu alloy); 1.03 wt% Cu (Al–Si–Mg–Cu–Ni alloy)

**[Main Function]**

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**

- Isolated pits in Al–Si–Mg–Cu alloy were surrounded by a high density of Cu-rich Q-phase particles

**[Potential Side Effects]**
- Cu-rich phases act as cathodic sites promoting localized corrosion
- Increased Cu content drastically reduces corrosion resistance

# Key Conclusions

1. **Ni addition leads to distinct microstructural features**: Ni alloying forms coarse block-like γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni and fine needle-like δ-Al₃NiCu phases in Al–Si–Mg–Cu alloys.

2. **Low-temperature strengthening**: At low temperatures, both γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni and δ-Al₃NiCu IMPs substantially contribute to tensile strength, but result in brittle fracture with less than 1% elongation.

3. **High-temperature softening**: With increasing temperature, void formation and coalescence within the γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni and δ-Al₃NiCu particles reduce tensile strength, leading to a transition to ductile fracture behavior.

4. **Cathodic nature of IMPs**: Both γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni and δ-Al₃NiCu phases exhibit a cathodic electrochemical nature, attributed to their high Volta potential difference (VPD) with the adjacent eutectic matrix. γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni exhibits the highest ΔVPD (0.38 V), followed by δ-Al₃NiCu (0.36 V) and the Cu-rich Q-phase (0.25 V).

5. **Primary corrosion site**: γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni particles act as primary sites for localized pitting corrosion, exacerbated by high misorientation at interphase boundaries. Stress localization around γ-Al₇Cu₄Ni particles amplifies localized corrosion, facilitating advancement of the corrosion front in the depth direction.

6. **Trade-off**: Cu and Ni alloying leads to higher tensile strength at elevated temperatures compared to Al–Si–Mg alloys, but also increases corrosion susceptibility. The combined cathodic nature of Cu- and Ni-rich IMPs and high dislocation density at IMP-matrix interfaces negatively affect both high-temperature strength and localized pitting corrosion.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Not a refractory alloy study**: This paper is fundamentally outside the scope of refractory high-temperature alloys. It is an aluminum-silicon casting alloy study. The findings should not be extrapolated to Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based, or refractory high-entropy alloy systems.

- **No specific numerical strength values**: The paper presents mechanical property data graphically (Fig. 6, Fig. 15) but does not provide specific numerical values for yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, or elongation in the text. Exact values must be read from the figures.

- **Corrosion rate values not explicitly stated**: The corrosion rate plot is shown in Fig. 9(e), but specific numerical values are not listed in the text.

- **No oxidation resistance data**: Despite the title mentioning "high-temperature tensile strength," the paper does not investigate high-temperature oxidation behavior (oxidation weight gain, oxide layer type, etc.). The corrosion study is limited to aqueous salt spray testing.

- **Applicable scope**: Automotive Al-Si casting alloys for engine and transmission components; the trade-off between high-temperature strength and corrosion resistance is relevant to piston alloys.

- **Alloy categories not covered**: Nb-W alloys, Mo-W alloys, W-based alloys, refractory high-entropy alloys, refractory multi-principal element alloys, ODS alloys, and additively manufactured refractory alloys are not studied or discussed in this paper.
