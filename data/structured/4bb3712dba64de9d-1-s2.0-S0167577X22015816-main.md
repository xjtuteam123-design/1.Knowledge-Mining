# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Improved oxidation resistance and excellent strength of nickel-based superalloy at 1100 ℃ by determining critical Cr-Al value
- **Authors**: Y. Shen, Q. Ju, G.H. Xu, X.L. Du, Y.W. Zhang, Y. Zhu, J. Huang, T.Y. Wang, Z. Liu
- **Journal**: Materials Letters
- **Year**: 2022
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.matlet.2022.133226
- **Alloy system studied**: Ni-18Co-18W-based nickel superalloy with varying Cr and Al contents
- **Research objective**: To determine the lowest critical Cr-Al value required for the formation of a continuous Al₂O₃ layer at 1100 ℃ while maintaining excellent high-temperature strength, and to compare the properties with commercial Haynes 230 and Haynes 188 alloys.
- **Material category**: Wrought nickel-based superalloy (not a refractory high-entropy alloy or refractory multi-principal element alloy).

# Alloy System and Research Object

The paper investigates a nickel-based superalloy with a base matrix of Ni-18Co-18W (wt.%). The research focuses on determining the critical combination of Cr and Al that enables (1) the formation of a continuous protective Al₂O₃ oxide layer at 1100 ℃ for oxidation resistance, and (2) the maintenance of sufficient Cr solid-solution strengthening and W₆C carbide precipitation strengthening for high-temperature strength. The alloy is compared against two commercial cobalt/nickel-based wrought superalloys: Haynes 230 and Haynes 188.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **Melting**: Vacuum induction melting (VIM) from raw elements.
- **Remelting**: Electroslag remelting (ESR).
- **Homogenization**: 1200 ℃ for 20 h in air.
- **Hot working**: Hot forged.
- **Intermediate annealing**: Annealed at 1200 ℃.
- **Hot rolling**: Hot rolled.
- **Final solution treatment**: 1200 ℃ for 1 h (to recrystallize and obtain uniform microstructure).
- **Commercial alloys**: Haynes 230 and Haynes 188 were solution treated at 1200 ℃ for 1 h.
- The paper does not specify rolling reduction ratios, forging temperatures, or cooling rates after heat treatment.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase**: The matrix is implied to be a Ni-Co solid-solution matrix (face-centered cubic, FCC) based on the Ni-18Co-18W base composition. The paper does not explicitly state "FCC" or perform phase identification.
- **Precipitates**: W₆C carbide precipitates are observed below the oxide layer in the 18Cr3.5Al alloy (Fig. 2f). In 12Cr and 15Cr alloys, Cr is consumed below the oxide layer due to poor oxidation resistance, causing W₆C to dissolve back into the matrix (Fig. 2a–e).
- **Oxide phases**: 
  - 12Cr3Al, 15Cr3Al, 15Cr3.2Al: Outer Cr₂O₃ layer (>5 μm thick) + discontinuous massive Al₂O₃ in the internal oxidation zone (depths: 10 μm, 15 μm, 22 μm respectively).
  - 18Cr3.2Al: Outer Cr₂O₃ volatilized/shed; inner Al₂O₃ became continuous but non-uniform.
  - 15Cr3.5Al and 18Cr3.5Al: Continuous and uniform Al₂O₃ layer with total oxide layer thickness of 3.1 μm and 2.5 μm, respectively.
- **Grain boundary wetting**: The paper describes the Al₂O₃ distribution in terms of complete vs. incomplete wetting of grain boundaries by the second solid phase. Low Al content (15Cr3Al, 15Cr3.2Al) leads to incomplete wetting with Al₂O₃ distributed along grain boundaries. Higher Al content (15Cr3.5Al, 18Cr3.5Al) leads to complete wetting with a continuous surface Al₂O₃ layer. The transition from incomplete to complete wetting is described as a "true surface phase transition."
- **Grain size**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Elemental segregation**: Not discussed in detail; observed Cr depletion below the oxide layer in alloys with poor oxidation resistance.
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed**: Solid-solution strengthening by Cr and W; carbide (W₆C) precipitation strengthening; the paper does not discuss ODS, grain-boundary strengthening in detail, or oxide dispersion strengthening.

# Mechanical Properties

The paper reports mechanical properties in graphical form (Fig. 3) without providing exact numerical tables. The following specific values are stated:

- **Rupture life at 1000 ℃ / 40 MPa**:
  - 18Cr3.5Al alloy: **115 h** (explicitly stated; largely exceeding Haynes 230 and Haynes 188).
  - Haynes 230 and Haynes 188: Not numerically stated; described as lower than 115 h.
- **Tensile strength at 1100 ℃**: Shown in Fig. 3; no exact numerical values given in the text. The paper states: "The tensile strength of these alloys is similar to that of Haynes 230 and Haynes 188."
- **Room-temperature plasticity (elongation)**: Shown in Fig. 3; no exact numerical values given. The paper states that increasing Al content decreases room-temperature plasticity, and increasing Cr content increases it.
- **Trends from Fig. 3**:
  - Increasing Cr content improves high-temperature tensile strength, room-temperature plasticity, and rupture life (attributed to solid-solution strengthening by Cr).
  - Increasing Al content improves rupture life but decreases room-temperature plasticity.
  - Only 18Cr3.2Al and 18Cr3.5Al achieve tensile strength and rupture life better than Haynes 230 and Haynes 188 above 1000 ℃.
- **Hardness**: Not reported.
- **Creep**: Constant-load creep tests at 1000 ℃ / 40 MPa; only rupture life reported.
- **Elastic modulus**: Not reported.
- **Density**: Not reported.
- **Strain rate**: Not reported.
- **Fracture toughness**: Not reported.

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

- **Oxidation test conditions**: Isothermal exposure at 1100 ℃ in air for 100 h; specimens 10 mm × 20 mm × 3 mm.
- **Oxidation weight gain**: Shown in Fig. 1a. The weight gain curves do not completely follow the parabolic law. 15Cr3Al, Haynes 230, and Haynes 188 show a decreasing trend in weight gain after 48 h due to CrO₃ volatilization.
- **Oxidation products**:
  - Poor-oxidation-resistance alloys (12Cr3Al, 15Cr3Al, 15Cr3.2Al): Outer Cr₂O₃ layer (>5 μm), discontinuous massive internal Al₂O₃, internal oxidation depths of 10–22 μm.
  - 18Cr3.2Al: Outer Cr₂O₃ volatilized; non-uniform but continuous inner Al₂O₃.
  - 15Cr3.5Al and 18Cr3.5Al: Continuous, uniform Al₂O₃ layer; total oxide thickness 3.1 μm and 2.5 μm, respectively.
- **CrO₃ volatilization**: Above 1000 ℃, Cr₂O₃ converts to volatile CrO₃, causing loss of oxidation resistance. This is confirmed by surface morphology of 15Cr3Al at 24 h (mainly Cr₂O₃) vs. 100 h (partial Al₂O₃ areas due to Cr₂O₃ volatilization).
- **Critical Cr-Al determination**: 
  - Minimum combination for complete Al₂O₃ layer: 15Cr3.5Al (wt.%).
  - Optimal combination for both oxidation resistance and high-temperature strength: 18Cr3.5Al (wt.%).
- **High-temperature embrittlement / oxygen embrittlement**: Not explicitly discussed, though the internal oxidation zones in low-Al alloys imply oxygen ingress.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

---

**Card 1: Cr (Chromium)**

- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: Ni-18Co-18W-based wrought nickel superalloy (this paper); also applicable to Haynes 230 and Haynes 188 class superalloys.
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 12–22 wt.% in this study. Critical values: 15 wt.% minimum for complete Al₂O₃ formation; 18 wt.% optimal for combined strength and oxidation resistance.
- **[Main Function]**: 
  1. Provides oxidation resistance by forming Cr₂O₃ at temperatures below ~1000 ℃.
  2. Provides solid-solution strengthening for high-temperature tensile strength, room-temperature plasticity, and rupture life.
  3. At sufficient levels (≥18 wt.%), maintains matrix solubility conditions that allow W₆C precipitation rather than W dissolution.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**: 
  - Solid-solution strengthening: Cr atoms in Ni-Co matrix increase resistance to dislocation motion.
  - Cr₂O₃ formation: Protective oxide at <1000 ℃; above 1000 ℃, Cr₂O₃ converts to volatile CrO₃, losing protection.
  - Cr depletion below oxide layer in poor-oxidation-resistance alloys causes W₆C to dissolve into the matrix.
  - Sufficient Cr (18 wt.%) preserves "solution space" balance so that W precipitates as W₆C strengthened phase.
- **[Property Effect]**: 
  - Increasing Cr from 12 to 18 wt.% improves tensile strength at 1100 ℃, room-temperature plasticity, and rupture life at 1000 ℃/40 MPa (specific numerical values only given for rupture life of 18Cr3.5Al: 115 h).
  - Oxidation resistance improves with increasing Cr up to 18 wt.% when combined with ≥3.5 wt.% Al.
- **[Potential Side Effects]**: 
  - Above 1000 ℃, Cr₂O₃ converts to volatile CrO₃, causing oxide spallation and loss of protection.
  - High Cr content (>18 wt.%) may not be necessary and could limit space for other strengthening elements.
- **[Original Evidence]**: 
  - "The increase of Cr content can improve the tensile strength and rupture life at high temperature and plasticity at room temperature of alloys. This is mainly attributed to the solution strengthening of Cr."
  - Fig. 2 shows Cr depletion below oxide layers in 12Cr and 15Cr alloys.
  - "Cr₂O₃ layer... will be converted into volatile CrO₃ above 1000 ℃ and lose the oxidation resistance."

**Card 2: Al (Aluminum)**

- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: Ni-18Co-18W-based wrought nickel superalloy (this paper).
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 3.0–3.5 wt.% in this study. Critical values: 3.5 wt.% minimum for complete wetting and continuous Al₂O₃; 3.0–3.2 wt.% insufficient at 15 wt.% Cr.
- **[Main Function]**: 
  1. Promotes formation of continuous Al₂O₃ protective oxide layer above 1000 ℃, providing oxidation resistance when Cr₂O₃ volatilizes.
  2. Improves rupture life at high temperature.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**: 
  - Al addition drives internal oxidation of Al to form Al₂O₃.
  - At insufficient Al content (3.0–3.2 wt.% at 15 wt.% Cr), Al₂O₃ forms discontinuous masses along grain boundaries (incomplete wetting).
  - At sufficient Al content (≥3.5 wt.% with 15–18 wt.% Cr), Al₂O₃ forms a continuous surface layer via complete wetting—a "true surface phase transition."
- **[Property Effect]**: 
  - Rupture life improves with increasing Al content (no specific numerical values except 18Cr3.5Al: 115 h at 1000 ℃/40 MPa).
  - Room-temperature plasticity decreases with increasing Al content (no specific numerical values given).
  - Oxidation resistance improves dramatically when complete Al₂O₃ layer forms: total oxide thickness 3.1 μm (15Cr3.5Al) and 2.5 μm (18Cr3.5Al) after 100 h at 1100 ℃.
- **[Potential Side Effects]**: 
  - "The addition of Al is detrimental to the plasticity of the alloy, resulting in the difficulty of fabricability."
- **[Original Evidence]**: 
  - "15Cr3.5Al is the minimum value that can form a complete Al₂O₃ layer."
  - "The complete wetting of solid phase on the surface is beneficial to the oxidation resistance."
  - "With the increase of Al content, the rupture life of the alloy can be improved, but the plasticity at room temperature of the alloy are decreased."

**Card 3: W (Tungsten)**

- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: Ni-18Co-18W-based wrought nickel superalloy (this paper); also Haynes 230 (14 wt.% W) and Haynes 188 (14 wt.% W).
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 18 wt.% in experimental alloys; 14 wt.% in Haynes 230 and Haynes 188.
- **[Main Function]**: 
  1. Solid-solution strengthening of the Ni-Co matrix.
  2. Forms W₆C carbide precipitates for precipitation strengthening when sufficient Cr is present to maintain matrix solubility limits.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**: 
  - W atoms in solid solution provide lattice distortion and solid-solution strengthening.
  - W₆C precipitates observed below the oxide layer in 18Cr3.5Al (Fig. 2f).
  - In 12Cr and 15Cr alloys, Cr depletion below the oxide layer causes W₆C to dissolve back into the matrix, losing the precipitate strengthening effect.
- **[Property Effect]**: 
  - Contributes to high-temperature tensile strength and rupture life (indirectly; specific numerical contribution not isolated).
  - The paper states: "The strength improvement can be achieved by reducing Cr and increasing the solid solubility of W."
- **[Potential Side Effects]**: 
  - High W content increases density.
  - If Cr is insufficient to maintain W₆C precipitation, W dissolves in matrix and strengthening is reduced.
- **[Original Evidence]**: 
  - "W₆C precipitates can be observed below the oxide layer of 18Cr3.5Al in Fig. 2(f)."
  - "The experiment confirmed that the strength improvement can be achieved by reducing Cr and increasing the solid solubility of W."
  - "The high temperature strength of these alloys is mainly attributed to carbide strengthening and solution strengthening of Cr and W."

**Card 4: Cr + Al Combination**

- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: Ni-18Co-18W-based wrought nickel superalloy (this paper).
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 
  - Minimum for complete Al₂O₃ formation: 15Cr + 3.5Al (wt.%).
  - Optimal for combined oxidation resistance and high-temperature strength: 18Cr + 3.5Al (wt.%).
- **[Main Function]**: Simultaneously achieves:
  1. Oxidation resistance via continuous Al₂O₃ formation at 1100 ℃.
  2. High-temperature strength via Cr solid-solution strengthening and W₆C precipitation.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**: 
  - Cr provides matrix solubility conditions that allow W₆C precipitation.
  - Al promotes complete wetting and continuous Al₂O₃ surface layer formation.
  - The Cr-Al balance determines whether Al₂O₃ forms as discontinuous internal precipitates (incomplete wetting) or a continuous surface layer (complete wetting).
- **[Property Effect]**: 
  - 18Cr3.5Al alloy rupture life at 1000 ℃/40 MPa: 115 h (largely exceeding Haynes 230 and Haynes 188).
  - Tensile strength at 1100 ℃ similar to Haynes 230 and Haynes 188.
  - Total oxide layer thickness after 100 h at 1100 ℃: 2.5 μm (18Cr3.5Al) vs. >5 μm Cr₂O₃ + 10–22 μm internal oxidation for low-Al alloys.
- **[Potential Side Effects]**: 
  - Increasing Al beyond 3.5 wt.% reduces room-temperature plasticity and fabricability.
- **[Original Evidence]**: 
  - "The lowest critical value of Cr-Al for the formation of continuous Al₂O₃ layer of wrought nickel-based superalloy at 1100 ℃ was determined 18Cr3.5Al as on the basis of ensuring the excellent high temperature strength."
  - "The alloy exhibits improved oxidation resistance at an optimal value of 18Cr3.5Al."

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

---

**Mechanism 1: Solid-Solution Strengthening**

- **Related elements**: Cr, W, Co (in Ni-Co matrix).
- **Typical alloys**: Ni-18Co-18W-xCr-yAl alloys; Haynes 230; Haynes 188.
- **Key compositions**: 18 wt.% Co, 18 wt.% W, 12–18 wt.% Cr.
- **Microstructural changes**: Cr and W atoms dissolved in Ni-Co FCC matrix; no specific lattice parameter or dislocation density data reported.
- **Property improvements**: 
  - Increasing Cr from 12 to 18 wt.% improves tensile strength at 1100 ℃ and room-temperature plasticity (no specific numerical values given).
  - Rupture life improves with higher Cr (18Cr3.5Al: 115 h at 1000 ℃/40 MPa).
- **Risks and limitations**: 
  - Limited solid-solution capacity of the matrix: "The solution capacity of the alloy matrix has reached its limit."
  - Cr volatilization above 1000 ℃ reduces oxidation protection.
  - W solid solution may be replaced by W₆C precipitation depending on Cr availability.
- **Original evidence**: "The increase of Cr content can improve the tensile strength and rupture life at high temperature and plasticity at room temperature of alloys. This is mainly attributed to the solution strengthening of Cr."

---

**Mechanism 2: Carbide Precipitation Strengthening (W₆C)**

- **Related elements**: W, C.
- **Typical alloys**: Ni-18Co-18W-xCr-yAl alloys; Haynes 230; Haynes 188.
- **Key compositions**: 18 wt.% W, 0.1 wt.% C, with ≥18 wt.% Cr to stabilize W₆C.
- **Microstructural changes**: W₆C precipitates form below the oxide layer when sufficient Cr is present (observed in 18Cr3.5Al, Fig. 2f). In 12Cr and 15Cr alloys, W₆C dissolves into the matrix due to Cr depletion.
- **Property improvements**: Contributes to high-temperature strength and rupture life (not isolated numerically in this study).
- **Risks and limitations**: 
  - W₆C stability depends on Cr content and matrix composition; Cr depletion under oxidizing conditions can dissolve W₆C.
  - Above 1000 ℃, enhanced phases redissolve in commercial alloys.
- **Original evidence**: "W₆C precipitates can be observed below the oxide layer of 18Cr3.5Al in Fig. 2(f). However, due to the poor oxidation resistance, Cr is consumed below the oxide layer and depleted, resulting in W₆C solid solution into the matrix in 12Cr and 15Cr alloys."

---

**Mechanism 3: Oxidation-Resistant Alloying (Cr + Al)**

- **Related elements**: Cr, Al.
- **Typical alloys**: Ni-18Co-18W-xCr-yAl alloys; Haynes 230; Haynes 188.
- **Key compositions**: Minimum: 15Cr3.5Al (wt.%); Optimal: 18Cr3.5Al (wt.%).
- **Microstructural changes**: 
  - Formation of continuous, uniform Al₂O₃ surface layer via complete wetting.
  - Transition from incomplete to complete wetting of grain boundaries by Al₂O₃ with increasing Al content.
  - Total oxide thickness reduced from >5 μm (Cr₂O₃) + 10–22 μm internal oxidation to 2.5–3.1 μm (continuous Al₂O₃).
- **Property improvements**: 
  - Excellent oxidation resistance at 1100 ℃ for 100 h.
  - Significantly reduced oxidation weight gain and internal oxidation depth.
- **Risks and limitations**: 
  - Al addition reduces room-temperature plasticity and fabricability.
  - Cr₂O₃ alone fails above 1000 ℃ due to CrO₃ volatilization.
  - Insufficient Al (3.0–3.2 wt.%) results in discontinuous Al₂O₃ and deep internal oxidation (10–22 μm).
- **Original evidence**: "15Cr3.5Al and 18Cr3.5Al alloys show excellent oxidation resistance at 1100 ℃." "The combination of 15Cr3.5Al or 18Cr3.5Al are the best choice to form a complete Al₂O₃ layer."

---

**Mechanism 4: Grain-Boundary Wetting / Surface Phase Transition**

- **Related elements**: Al (via Al₂O₃ formation).
- **Typical alloys**: Ni-18Co-18W-xCr-yAl alloys.
- **Key compositions**: Al ≥ 3.5 wt.% for complete wetting.
- **Microstructural changes**: 
  - Incomplete wetting: Al₂O₃ distributed along grain boundaries as discontinuous massive precipitates (15Cr3Al, 15Cr3.2Al).
  - Complete wetting: Al₂O₃ forms continuous surface layer (15Cr3.5Al, 18Cr3.5Al).
  - Described as a "true surface phase transition."
- **Property improvements**: Complete wetting of oxide phase on the surface is beneficial to oxidation resistance.
- **Risks and limitations**: Transition requires sufficient driving force from Al content; below the critical value, internal oxidation dominates.
- **Original evidence**: "The distribution behavior of Al₂O₃ phase is closely related to the so-called complete and incomplete wetting of grain boundaries by the second solid phase... The transition from incomplete wetting to complete wetting occurs with an increase in Al content, and it is a true surface phase transition."

# Key Conclusions

1. **Critical Cr-Al value**: The lowest critical Cr-Al combination for forming a continuous Al₂O₃ layer at 1100 ℃ in Ni-18Co-18W-based wrought nickel superalloy is **15Cr3.5Al (wt.%)**; the optimal value for simultaneously ensuring excellent high-temperature strength is **18Cr3.5Al (wt.%)**.

2. **Oxidation resistance**: The 18Cr3.5Al alloy forms a continuous and uniform Al₂O₃ layer after 100 h at 1100 ℃, with a total oxide layer thickness of 2.5 μm, significantly better than alloys with lower Al (15Cr3Al, 15Cr3.2Al) that form discontinuous internal Al₂O₃ and thick Cr₂O₃ layers (>5 μm) with internal oxidation depths of 15–22 μm.

3. **Mechanical properties**: The tensile strength of the experimental alloys is similar to Haynes 230 and Haynes 188. The rupture life of 18Cr3.5Al at 1000 ℃/40 MPa reaches **115 h**, largely exceeding Haynes 230 and Haynes 188.

4. **Cr function**: Increasing Cr content improves high-temperature tensile strength, room-temperature plasticity, and rupture life through solid-solution strengthening. Sufficient Cr (18 wt.%) also enables W₆C precipitation strengthening by preventing Cr depletion under the oxide layer.

5. **Al function**: Increasing Al content improves oxidation resistance and rupture life but decreases room-temperature plasticity and fabricability.

6. **W function**: Strength can be improved by reducing Cr content to increase the solid solubility of W in the matrix; W₆C precipitates provide additional strengthening when Cr is sufficient.

7. **Design strategy**: The paper demonstrates that oxidation resistance (via Al₂O₃ formation) and high-temperature strength (via Cr solid-solution and W₆C carbide precipitation) can be simultaneously achieved by carefully selecting the Cr-Al balance.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. **Specific numerical values limited**: The paper presents mechanical property data primarily in graphical form (Fig. 3) without providing a table of exact tensile strength, elongation, or rupture life values for all alloys. Only the rupture life of 18Cr3.5Al (115 h at 1000 ℃/40 MPa) is explicitly stated.

2. **Microstructural characterization limited**: No transmission electron microscopy (TEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), or electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) data are presented. Phase identification of the matrix (implied FCC) and W₆C precipitates is not rigorously confirmed. Grain size is not reported.

3. **Single oxidation condition**: Oxidation testing was performed only at 1100 ℃ for 100 h in air. No cyclic oxidation or longer-term data are provided.

4. **Limited creep data**: Only constant-load rupture life at a single condition (1000 ℃/40 MPa) is reported; minimum creep rate, stress exponent, and activation energy are not given.

5. **No fracture toughness or fatigue data**: These properties are not reported.

6. **Fabricability not quantified**: While the paper notes that increasing Al reduces plasticity and fabricability, no workability tests (e.g., hot ductility, rolling reduction limits) are reported.

7. **Applicability**: The conclusions are specific to Ni-18Co-18W-based wrought superalloys with C at 0.1 wt.% and processed via VIM + ESR + homogenization + hot forging + hot rolling + solution treatment. Extension to other alloy systems or processing routes is not discussed.

8. **Environmental and ppm-level impurities**: Impurity levels (O, N, H, S) and their effects are not discussed.

9. **CrO₃ volatilization quantification**: The paper qualitatively discusses Cr₂O₃ → CrO₃ volatilization at 1100 ℃ but does not quantify volatilization rates or mass loss due to CrO₃ evolution.
