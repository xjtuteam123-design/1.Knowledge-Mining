# Paper Metadata
- **Title**: Microstructure and mechanical properties of Ta–Ti alloy by plasma-activated sintering
- **Authors**: Shiquan Zhang, Jian Zhang, Shuai Ma, Shuai Ge, Jiawei Hou, Yi Sun, Guoqiang Luo, Qiang Shen
- **Journal**: Vacuum
- **Year**: 2022 (Available online 1 October 2022)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.vacuum.2022.111554
- **Material category**: Ta-based alloy (Ta–Ti binary system), prepared by plasma-activated sintering (PAS); not a W-based, Mo-based, or refractory high-entropy alloy.

# Alloy System and Research Object
- **Alloy system studied**: Ta–Ti binary alloys, with nominal compositions of Ta–2Ti and Ta–4Ti (wt.%).
- **Research objective**: To overcome the low room-temperature strength and intergranular fracture of pure tantalum caused by strong adsorption of interstitial oxygen during sintering. The work introduces Ti to consume oxygen by forming TiO₂ and to inhibit oxygen dissolution in the Ta matrix, thereby improving densification and mechanical properties.
- **Material category**: Ta-based alloy prepared by plasma-activated sintering (PAS); second-phase (TiO₂) dispersion-strengthened refractory alloy.

# Alloy Composition
- **Alloy samples and nominal compositions** (all in wt.%):
  - Pure Ta (0 wt.% Ti)
  - Ta–1Ti (1 wt.% Ti) — mentioned in fracture morphology discussion (Fig. 8b), though not listed in all tables
  - Ta–2Ti (2 wt.% Ti) — primary optimized composition
  - Ta–3Ti (3 wt.% Ti) — mentioned in fracture morphology discussion (Fig. 8d), though not listed in all tables
  - Ta–4Ti (4 wt.% Ti)
- **Raw powder details**:
  - Pure Ta powder: purity 99.99%, particle size 10.5 μm
  - Ti powder: purity 99.99%, particle size 3–5 μm
  - Tantalum powder oxygen content: 0.05 wt.% (atomic percentage 0.556 at.%), as determined by an oxygen and nitrogen analyzer
- **Alloying elements**: Ta (matrix), Ti (alloying addition, nominal 1–4 wt.%).
- **Impurities**: Oxygen in starting Ta powder = 0.05 wt.% (0.556 at.%); residual oxygen is the key impurity of concern. No explicit mention of C, N, H contents.
- **Contains W, Mo, Nb, Zr, Hf, V, Cr, Al, Si, Re, B, Y, La**: Not present in this work, except that W is mentioned in literature comparisons (Ta–10W, Ta–10W-1.5TiC). The studied alloys do not contain W, Mo, Nb, Zr, Hf, V, Cr, Al, Si, Re, C, B, Y, or La as intentional additions.

# Processing and Heat Treatment
- **Powder preparation**: Pure Ta powder (99.99%, 10.5 μm) and Ti powder (99.99%, 3–5 μm) were ball-milled using a low-energy ball milling apparatus for 5 h.
- **Ball milling conditions**: Argon protection; ball-to-material weight ratio = 10:1; rotation speed = 120 rpm.
- **Consolidation method**: Plasma-activated sintering (PAS), using a PAS machine from Elenix company, Japan.
- **Mold**: High-strength graphite mold, 20 mm in diameter.
- **Sintering atmosphere**: Vacuumed to 3 × 10⁻² Pa, then fed with high-purity argon gas, keeping furnace air pressure at 10² Pa.
- **Heating rate**: 100 °C/min.
- **Sintering temperature**: 1500 °C.
- **Applied pressure**: 50 MPa.
- **Holding time**: 3 min.
- **Cooling**: Furnace cooling to room temperature.
- **Deformation/rolling/forging/annealing/aging**: Not performed in this work.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution
- **Matrix phase**: BCC Ta matrix rich in Ta with small amounts of dissolved Ti and little oxygen. XRD showed only tantalum peaks; no intermetallic compounds formed because Ta and Ti form a continuous solid solution.
- **Second phases / precipitates**: Dispersed TiO₂ oxide particles enriched in Ti and O. EBSD identified TiO₂ (red color) as the oxide phase. TEM/EDX of the Ta–2Ti alloy confirmed the dispersed particles are Ti–O oxide.
- **Grain size**: Not explicitly quantified. EBSD IPF images show an equiaxed grain structure with no obvious preferential orientation. The paper states that increasing Ti content to 4 wt.% accelerates inter-diffusion and leads to grain coarsening.
- **Grain boundary features**: In pure Ta, many pores are distributed around grain boundaries; oxygen-rich pores hinder densification. In Ta–Ti alloys, pores disappear gradually; TiO₂ oxide particles are dispersed and, in the Ta–4Ti alloy, surround the Ti phase, forming a hard shell.
- **Elemental segregation**:
  - Pure Ta: enriched with oxygen in cavities; O-rich pores present.
  - Ta–2Ti: dispersed particles enriched with O and Ti are formed.
  - Ta–4Ti: O-rich particles grow bigger than in Ta–2Ti; Ti agglomeration occurs; TiO₂ forms an oxide layer at Ti agglomeration sites.
- **Precipitate size and distribution**: TiO₂ oxide particles are nearly uniformly distributed in the Ta matrix. Particle size increases with Ti content from 2 wt.% to 4 wt.%.
- **Dislocation structure after deformation**: The Ta matrix in the deformed Ta–2Ti alloy contains a high density of dislocations, while TiO₂ oxide shows no deformability and no obvious dislocations. The Ta/TiO₂ phase interface remains well-bonded and hinders dislocation slip.
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed**: Dispersion strengthening by TiO₂ oxide particles; purification of Ta matrix by Ti gettering oxygen to avoid interstitial oxygen embrittlement and brittle Ta₂O₅ oxide at grain boundaries.

# Mechanical Properties
- **Test method**: Three-point bending test; sample size 2 × 3 × 20 mm³ (three parallel samples); prepared by electric discharge machine; tested on an Instron 5966 testing system.
- **Strain rate**: 5 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹.
- **Relative density**:
  - Pure Ta: 93%
  - Ta–2Ti: 99.2%
  - Ta–4Ti: 99.6%
- **Bending strength / plasticity** (from engineering bending stress–strain curves, Fig. 7):
  - Pure Ta: low strength and plasticity, typical brittle fracture.
  - Ta–2Ti: ultimate bending strength = 1345 MPa; good plasticity = 16%.
  - Ta–4Ti: poor bending strength = 380 MPa; strength and plasticity decrease simultaneously compared with Ta–2Ti.
  - Ta–1Ti and Ta–3Ti are mentioned in fracture morphology but specific bending values for these compositions are not explicitly provided in the text.
- **Fracture morphology**:
  - Pure Ta: many pores at grain boundaries and inside grains; low bonding force between grains; lower strength; intergranular fracture.
  - Ta–1Ti: more dimples on fracture surface; ductile fracture.
  - Ta–2Ti: combination of ductile fracture and transgranular fracture; dimples deep and dense; transgranular fracture more likely in Ti-rich regions.
  - Ta–4Ti: intergranular fracture becomes the main fracture mode; cracks propagate along grain boundaries due to oxide layer and stress gradient from difference in linear expansion coefficient between oxide layer, internal Ti phase, and Ta matrix.
- **Comparison with literature (from discussion, not original measurements in this paper)**:
  - Ta–10W alloy: ultimate bending strength 786 MPa (from Ref. [28])
  - Ta–10W-1.5TiC: ultimate bending strength 912 MPa (from Ref. [28])
  - Ta–Ni alloy: compressive yield strength only 101 MPa (from Ref. [12])
  - Ta–9.2W-0.5Hf: tensile strength 865 MPa, elongation 25% (from Ref. [16])
  - W–7Ni–3Fe by SPS: relative density 96.7% at 1250 °C (from Ref. [19])
  - W alloy by Huang et al.: compressive strength 2078 MPa, strain 40% at 1200 °C (from Ref. [20])
- **Hardness, elastic modulus, fracture toughness, creep, rupture life, high-temperature strength**: Not reported in this paper.
- **Density**: Not explicitly given as absolute values; theoretical density calculated by the mixing rule; relative density reported (93–99.6%).

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability
- **Oxidation behavior**: The paper does not study high-temperature oxidation resistance or oxidation weight gain. The role of oxygen is considered only as a residual impurity in the sintering process, not as a service oxidation issue.
- **Oxide layer type**: TiO₂ formed in situ during sintering; no protective Al₂O₃, Cr₂O₃, SiO₂, HfO₂, or ZrO₂ layer is discussed.
- **Oxygen-related embrittlement**: Yes, this is a central topic. Pure Ta is embrittled by interstitial oxygen in solid solution and by Ta–O oxide (e.g., Ta₂O₅) formation at grain boundaries. Ti addition reacts with residual oxygen to form TiO₂, preventing oxygen from dissolving interstitially in Ta and avoiding brittle Ta₂O₅ at grain boundaries.
- **High-temperature embrittlement / nitrogen embrittlement / grain-boundary oxidation**: Only oxygen-related grain-boundary issues are discussed; nitrogen embrittlement and high-temperature service embrittlement are not studied in this work.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Card 1: Ti (Titanium) as Getter and Sintering Aid in Ta–Ti Alloys

[Element or Element Combination]
Ti (Titanium), added to Ta matrix

[Applicable Alloy System]
Ta–Ti binary alloys produced by plasma-activated sintering (PAS)

[Addition Amount or Composition Range]
Nominal additions of 1, 2, 3, and 4 wt.% Ti; optimized composition is 2 wt.% Ti.

[Main Function]
- Acts as a sintering aid to improve densification.
- Acts as an oxygen getter: reacts with residual oxygen to form TiO₂, purifying the Ta matrix.
- Prevents oxygen from dissolving interstitially in Ta lattice.
- Avoids formation of brittle Ta₂O₅ at Ta grain boundaries.
- Provides dispersion strengthening through in-situ TiO₂ particles.

[Microstructural Mechanism]
- Ti has a high affinity for oxygen; during sintering, Ti reacts with residual oxygen to form TiO₂, which is thermodynamically more stable than Ta₂O₅.
- The diffusion coefficient of Ta in Ti is approximately 10³ times greater than that of Ta in Ni, Fe, or Cu, making Ti a more effective sintering aid than those elements (based on literature).
- Ta and Ti form a continuous solid solution; no intermetallic compounds form.
- With appropriate Ti addition (2 wt.%), dispersed TiO₂ particles form in the Ta matrix.
- With excessive Ti addition (4 wt.%), TiO₂ particles grow larger and form an oxide layer at Ti agglomeration sites, causing stress gradients and interfacial cracks.

[Property Effect]
- Relative density: increases from 93% for pure Ta to 99.2% for Ta–2Ti and 99.6% for Ta–4Ti.
- Ultimate bending strength: Ta–2Ti reaches 1345 MPa; Ta–4Ti shows only 380 MPa.
- Plasticity (from bending tests): Ta–2Ti exhibits 16% plasticity with ductile + transgranular fracture; pure Ta is brittle with intergranular fracture; Ta–4Ti becomes brittle again with intergranular fracture.
- Compared with Ta–10W (786 MPa) and Ta–10W-1.5TiC (912 MPa) from literature, Ta–2Ti shows significantly higher bending strength.

[Potential Side Effects]
- Excessive Ti addition (4 wt.%) leads to grain coarsening; forms an oxide layer at Ti agglomeration; creates stress gradient due to differences in linear expansion coefficient among oxide layer, Ti phase, and Ta matrix; causes cracks along phase interfaces and grain boundaries; reduces strength and plasticity; promotes intergranular brittle fracture.
- Titanium has a lower melting point than Ta and accelerates inter-diffusion when added in larger amounts, promoting grain coarsening.

[Original Evidence]
- Section 1 (Introduction): "Ti is a promising choice for alloying tantalum since the diffusion coefficient of Ta in Ti is approximately 10³ times greater than that of Ta in Ni, Fe, or Cu."
- Section 3.1: "The densification of pure tantalum alloy is only 93% while the densification reaches 99.2% with the addition of 2 wt% Ti."
- Section 3.1: "With the addition of Ti, the diffraction peaks shift to a higher angle, indicating that adding Ti could consume the oxygen and inhibit the interstitial solution of O in Ta."
- Section 3.3: Thermodynamic calculations show that TiO₂ formation has lower Gibbs free energy and is more stable than Ta₂O₅.
- Section 3.4: "Ta–2Ti alloy exhibits high ultimate bending strength of 1345 MPa... First, TiO₂ oxide is dispersed in the Ta matrix, providing a good dispersion-strengthening effect."
- Section 4 (Conclusions): "The Ta – 2Ti with proper Ti addition exhibits high ultimate bending strength of 1345 MPa and good plasticity of 16%."

## Card 2: Oxygen (O) as Interstitial Impurity and Embrittlement Agent in Ta

[Element or Element Combination]
O (Oxygen), as residual impurity in Ta powder

[Applicable Alloy System]
Ta and Ta–Ti alloys prepared by PAS

[Addition Amount or Composition Range]
Starting Ta powder oxygen content: 0.05 wt.% (0.556 at.%).

[Main Function]
- Detrimental impurity: dissolves interstitially in Ta and forms Ta–O compounds (e.g., Ta₂O₅), causing embrittlement and hindering densification.

[Microstructural Mechanism]
- Oxygen with small atomic size dissolves into the Ta lattice, increasing the lattice constant (XRD peak shift to smaller angle in pure Ta after sintering).
- Oxygen-rich pores form in pure Ta, which promote non-densifying transport mechanisms such as evaporation–condensation and surface diffusion, causing rapid grain growth and hindering densification.
- Oxygen can react with Ta to form Ta–O oxide in grain boundaries, causing intergranular fracture.
- In Ta–Ti alloys, Ti reacts preferentially with oxygen to form TiO₂, preventing interstitial oxygen dissolution and Ta₂O₅ formation.

[Property Effect]
- Pure Ta with O-rich pores and interstitial oxygen shows low strength, low plasticity, and intergranular fracture.
- Ta–2Ti, where oxygen is gettered by Ti, achieves 1345 MPa bending strength and 16% plasticity.
- The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for oxygen content after sintering or for the mechanical property degradation of Ta–O alloys alone; these are inferred qualitatively from comparisons between pure Ta and Ta–Ti alloys.

[Potential Side Effects]
- Oxygen embrittlement: interstitial O and Ta–O compounds lead to brittle intergranular fracture.
- O-rich pores inhibit densification and degrade mechanical properties.
- In Ta–Ti alloys with excess Ti, oxygen-rich oxide layers at Ti agglomeration can cut interconnection between grains and promote cracks.

[Original Evidence]
- Section 3.1: "From the element distribution shown below in Fig. 5, the Ta matrix contains a small quantity of oxygen... the bigger lattice constant in the Ta matrix than that in pure Ta is attributable to the solid solution of elements O."
- Section 3.1: "Pure tantalum is enriched with oxygen elements in the cavities and the presence of these O-rich pores is an important obstacle to the densification of the pure Ta alloy."
- Section 3.3: "During sintering, some oxygen is dissolved in Ta, while some react with the element Ta to form Ta–O oxide in the grain boundaries of the Ta matrix, both of which cause the embrittlement of Ta and result in the intergranular fracture of pure tantalum alloy."

## Card 3: TiO₂ (Titanium Dioxide) as In-Situ Dispersion Strengthening Phase

[Element or Element Combination]
TiO₂ (Titanium dioxide) in-situ formed particles

[Applicable Alloy System]
Ta–Ti alloys prepared by PAS

[Addition Amount or Composition Range]
Not measured quantitatively; formed by reaction of 2–4 wt.% Ti with residual oxygen during sintering. EBSD shows a small amount of TiO₂ (red color) uniformly distributed in the Ta matrix.

[Main Function]
- Dispersion strengthening of Ta matrix.
- Purification of Ta by gettering oxygen.

[Microstructural Mechanism]
- TiO₂ particles are nearly uniformly distributed in the Ta matrix.
- TiO₂ oxide phase is hard and shows no deformability; after bending, the Ta matrix contains a high density of dislocations, while TiO₂ has no obvious dislocations.
- The Ta/TiO₂ phase interface remains well-bonded and hinders dislocation slip, providing reinforcement.
- With excessive Ti, TiO₂ forms an oxide layer at Ti agglomeration and causes stress gradients and interfacial cracks.

[Property Effect]
- Ta–2Ti with dispersed TiO₂: ultimate bending strength = 1345 MPa; plasticity = 16%.
- Pure Ta without TiO₂: low strength and low plasticity, brittle fracture.
- Ta–4Ti with larger TiO₂ and oxide layers: bending strength drops to 380 MPa with intergranular fracture.

[Potential Side Effects]
- Large TiO₂ particles and TiO₂ layers at Ti agglomeration cause stress concentration, interfacial cracking, grain-boundary crack propagation, and brittle fracture when Ti addition is excessive.

[Original Evidence]
- Section 3.1: "In the Ta – Ti alloy, the titanium oxide particles distribute almost uniformly in the matrix (Fig. 6(a–b))."
- Section 3.4: "The phase interface between Ta and TiO₂ remains well-bonded and hinders the dislocation slip (Fig. 10d), providing high reinforcement."
- Section 4 (Conclusions): "The dispersed TiO₂ oxide enhances the Ta – Ti alloys based on the dispersion-strengthening effect."

## Card 4: Ti + O Combination (Getter Reaction)

[Element or Element Combination]
Ti + O (Titanium–oxygen gettering reaction)

[Applicable Alloy System]
Ta–Ti alloys, PAS-consolidated

[Addition Amount or Composition Range]
Ti nominal 2 wt.% is optimal; Ta powder has 0.05 wt.% O. Thermodynamic calculations show reactions Ti + O₂ = TiO₂, 4Ta + 5O₂ = 2Ta₂O₅, and 5/2Ti + Ta₂O₅ = 5/2TiO₂ + 2Ta are all thermodynamically favorable, but TiO₂ is the most stable product.

[Main Function]
- Purification of Ta matrix by preferential reaction of Ti with residual oxygen.
- In-situ formation of TiO₂ dispersion strengthening particles.

[Microstructural Mechanism]
- Ti reacts with residual oxygen during sintering to form TiO₂ before oxygen can dissolve interstitially in Ta or form brittle Ta₂O₅ at grain boundaries.
- Ti can also react with Ta₂O₅ to produce Ta and TiO₂, promoting sintering.
- Thermodynamic calculations show TiO₂ has a lower Gibbs free energy than Ta₂O₅, so Ti is more likely to react with oxygen than Ta.

[Property Effect]
- Enables high densification (99.2% for Ta–2Ti).
- Enables high bending strength (1345 MPa) and plasticity (16%) in Ta–2Ti.
- Avoids intergranular fracture caused by oxygen segregation to grain boundaries.

[Potential Side Effects]
- With excess Ti, oxide layer formation at Ti agglomeration creates stress gradients and cracks, reducing strength and plasticity.

[Original Evidence]
- Section 3.3, reactions (1)–(3) and Fig. 9: Gibbs free energy of reactions; TiO₂ formation is thermodynamically favored.
- Section 3.3: "Therefore, Ti is easier absorbed and reacted with oxygen than Ta which plays a role in purifying the Ta matrix."

## Mechanism 1: Oxide Dispersion Strengthening by In-Situ TiO₂

[Mechanism]
Oxide dispersion strengthening by in-situ TiO₂ particles

[Related Elements]
Ti, O; TiO₂ particles

[Typical Alloys]
Ta–2Ti (wt.%) prepared by PAS; also observed in Ta–4Ti but with detrimental excessive Ti response.

[Key Compositions]
Ta–2Ti (wt.% nominal) with TiO₂ dispersed in the Ta matrix.

[Microstructural Changes]
- TiO₂ particles nearly uniformly distributed in Ta matrix.
- Hard TiO₂ particles do not deform; Ta matrix accumulates high-density dislocations.
- Ta/TiO₂ interface remains well-bonded and blocks dislocation slip.

[Property Improvements]

- Excess Ti (4 wt.%) causes TiO₂ particle coarsening, Ti agglomeration, oxide layer formation, stress gradients, interfacial cracks, grain-boundary crack propagation, intergranular fracture, and lower bending strength (380 MPa).
- Balance between Ti addition for oxygen gettering and excess Ti for avoiding oxide layer embrittlement must be maintained.

[Original Evidence]

- Section 3.4: "The phase interface between Ta and TiO₂ remains well-bonded and hinders the dislocation slip (Fig. 10d), providing high reinforcement."

## Mechanism 2: Oxygen Purification / De-Embrittlement (Avoiding Interstitial Oxygen and Ta₂O₅)

[Mechanism]
Oxygen purification of Ta matrix via Ti gettering to prevent interstitial oxygen embrittlement and brittle Ta₂O₅ grain-boundary oxide formation

[Related Elements]
Ti, O, Ta

[Typical Alloys]
Ta–Ti alloys (optimized at Ta–2Ti wt.%)

[Key Compositions]
Ta–2Ti with 0.05 wt.% O in starting Ta powder

[Microstructural Changes]
- Without Ti: O-rich pores at grain boundaries and inside grains; oxygen dissolves interstitially in Ta; Ta–O oxide forms in grain boundaries.

[Risks and Limitations]
- If Ti addition is too high (4 wt.%), oxide layer formation at Ti agglomeration cuts grain interconnection and causes stress gradients and cracks.
- The mechanism is specifically applicable to Ta-based systems with oxygen contamination; it does not address oxidation resistance at high-temperature service.

# Key Conclusions
- Ti addition to Ta via PAS can significantly improve densification, from 93% relative density for pure Ta to 99.2% for Ta–2Ti and 99.6% for Ta–4Ti.
- Ti acts as an oxygen getter, reacting with residual oxygen to form TiO₂; this purifies the Ta matrix and avoids interstitial oxygen dissolution and brittle Ta₂O₅ at grain boundaries.
- Ta–Ti alloys consist mainly of a dense Ta matrix and dispersed TiO₂ second phase; no intermetallic compounds form because Ta and Ti form a continuous solid solution.
- The optimized Ta–2Ti alloy exhibits high ultimate bending strength of 1345 MPa and good plasticity of 16% (based on bending test), attributed to dispersion strengthening by TiO₂ and oxygen purification.
- Excessive Ti (4 wt.%) leads to grain coarsening, Ti agglomeration, oxide layer formation, interfacial stress gradients, and brittle intergranular fracture, reducing bending strength to 380 MPa.
- The Ta–2Ti alloy outperforms Ta–10W (786 MPa) and Ta–10W-1.5TiC (912 MPa) in bending strength (from literature comparison).
- Plasma-activated sintering is an effective method to prepare uniform, dense Ta–Ti alloys with fine microstructure and good mechanical properties.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope
- The paper focuses only on room-temperature three-point bending tests; no high-temperature mechanical properties, tensile properties, compressive properties, fracture toughness, creep, hardness, elastic modulus, or density absolute values are reported.
- No service oxidation resistance testing is performed; the role of oxygen is considered only as a sintering impurity.
- No grain size quantification, precipitate size distribution statistics, or dislocation density measurements are provided.
- The results are specific to Ta–Ti binary alloys prepared by PAS at 1500 °C, 50 MPa, 3 min holding time, 100 °C/min heating rate, and with specific powder sizes and purities. Extrapolation to other Ta alloys, other fabrication methods, or other temperatures is not established in this paper.
- The strengthening mechanisms discussed are inferred from microstructure and bending behavior; in-situ observation of dislocation–particle interaction is limited to post-deformation TEM.
- The bending strength and plasticity values are engineering bending stress–strain data, not tensile data; direct comparison with tensile or compressive strength values should be made with caution.
- The paper does not provide specific numerical values for the mechanical properties of Ta–1Ti and Ta–3Ti; only Ta–2Ti and Ta–4Ti are reported quantitatively for bending, though fracture morphologies for Ta–1Ti and Ta–3Ti are mentioned.
- Thermodynamic calculations are presented for possible reactions, but the calculations rely on tabulated thermodynamic data and do not consider all possible intermediate oxides or kinetic barriers.
