# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Precipitate Shearing by Strongly Coupled Dislocation Pairs

- **Related elements:** Ni, Al, Ti (γ′ forming elements)
- **Typical alloys:** Ni-Co-based superalloys with fine γ′ precipitates
- **Key compositions:** This alloy: 4.5 wt.% Ti, 2.3 wt.% Al; γ′ volume fraction ~41%
- **Microstructural changes:** Dislocation pairs shear γ′ precipitates. In sample A0 (γ′ = 31 nm), the spacing of dislocation pairs is approximately 10–15 nm, smaller than the γ′ precipitate size, facilitating strong dislocation coupling pair formation.
- **Property improvements:** Contributes to high-temperature strength when γ′ precipitates are fine.
- **Risks and limitations:** As γ′ size increases, the CRSS for strong-pair shearing increases, and this mechanism is replaced by other mechanisms (SF shearing and Orowan looping).
- **Original evidence:** Section 3 (Results): "In A0, both dislocation pairs and isolated SFs are observed (Fig. 2a). The spacing of dislocation pairs is approximately 10 to 15 nm, smaller than the γ′ precipitate size, facilitating the formation of strong dislocation coupling pairs [7]."

---

## Stacking Fault Shearing of γ′ Precipitates

- **Related elements:** Co (reduces SFE), Ni, Ti, Al
- **Typical alloys:** Ni-Co-based superalloys with low SFE
- **Key compositions:** 25 wt.% Co; calculated SFE = 17.7 mJ/m²
- **Microstructural changes:** a/2〈101〉 dislocations dissociate into a/3〈112〉 and a/6〈121〉 partial dislocations, forming isolated or continuous stacking faults within γ′ precipitates.
- **Property improvements:** SFs contribute to strength and plasticity; continuous SFs are precursors to micro-twin formation.
- **Risks and limitations:** At small strains (5%), thermally driven atomic rearrangement to form micro-twins is difficult without pre-existing continuous SFs.
- **Original evidence:** Section 4 (Discussion): "To reduce the stress of the interface and the energy of the structure, the isolated SFs (Fig. 2a) form when some a/2〈101〉 dislocations cut into the precipitates and decompose into two partial a/3〈112〉 and a/6〈121〉 dislocations [11]."

---

## Orowan Looping/Bypassing of γ′ Precipitates

- **Related elements:** Ni, Al, Ti
- **Typical alloys:** Ni-Co-based superalloys with coarse γ′ precipitates
- **Key compositions:** γ′ size ≥ 122 nm (sample A200)
- **Microstructural changes:** Dislocations bow around and bypass γ′ precipitates, leaving dislocation loops. Orowan loops are observed in A4, A24, and A200 at 5% strain; Orowan looping becomes the dominant mechanism in A200 where CRSS_Orowan is significantly smaller than CRSS_SF.
- **Property improvements:** Provides an alternative deformation pathway that can be favored when precipitate shearing is difficult.
- **Risks and limitations:** Orowan looping produces fewer SFs and micro-twins, potentially reducing work-hardening contributions from MT boundaries.
- **Original evidence:** Section 4 (Discussion): "However, in A200, the CRSS of Orowan looping became significantly smaller than that of SFs. As a result, the dislocations tended to bypass the γ′ precipitates to form Orowan loops, and only some dislocations cut through the precipitated phase to produce the isolated SFs [13]."

---

## Micro-twin (MT) Formation

- **Related elements:** Co (SFE reduction), Ti, Al (γ′ size control)
- **Typical alloys:** Ni-Co-based superalloys with low SFE and intermediate γ′ sizes
- **Key compositions:** γ′ size optimum ~47 nm for peak MT formation in this alloy at 20% strain and 760 °C
- **Microstructural changes:** Micro-twins form from continuous stacking faults under thermal activation and higher applied stress. At 20% strain, MTs are observed in all samples. The number, length, and width of MTs increase initially then decrease with γ′ size: peak at A4 (47 nm), reduced in A24 (63 nm) and A200 (122 nm).
- **Property improvements:** MTs form stable boundary networks contributing to strength and plasticity (stated in Introduction).
- **Risks and limitations:** MT formation requires continuous SFs and sufficient defect accumulation for atomic rearrangement. At 5% strain, MTs are limited; at 20% strain, MTs become the predominant mode.
- **Original evidence:** Section 3 (Results): "With an increase in the size of the precipitated phase, the number, length, and width of MTs exhibited an initial increase (Fig. 3a and b) followed by a subsequent decrease (Fig. 3b–d)." Section 4 (Discussion): "the low CRSS of SFs favored the generation of high-density SFs, leading to the formation of MTs under the influence of thermal activation (Fig. 2e) [12]."

# Key Conclusions

1. **γ′ precipitate size governs deformation mechanism selection at 760 °C.** At 5% strain, the deformation mechanism transitions from dislocation coupling pairs (31 nm γ′) → SFs and dislocation loops (47 nm γ′) → SFs and some MTs (63 nm γ′) → dislocation loops and isolated faults with no MTs (122 nm γ′).

2. **Strain level controls micro-twin formation.** At 5% strain, micro-twin formation is limited due to insufficient continuous SFs and inadequate defect accumulation for atomic rearrangement. At 20% strain, micro-twins become the predominant deformation mode in all samples.

3. **An optimal γ′ precipitate size exists for peak micro-twin formation.** Micro-twins reach peak intensity (highest number, length, and width) in the alloy with 47 nm-sized γ′ precipitates (sample A4) at 20% strain and 760 °C.

4. **The competition among deformation mechanisms is governed by critical resolved shear stress (CRSS).** The relative CRSS values for strong-pair shearing (τ_S), stacking fault shearing (τ_SF), and Orowan looping (τ_Orowan) determine which mechanism dominates at a given γ′ size.

5. **Modulating γ′ precipitate size offers a strategy for tailoring deformation mechanisms and, by extension, alloy design.** "The optimizing of γ′ precipitate size can significantly enhance the formation of micro-twins in Ni-Co-based superalloys. This strategy of modulating the deformation mechanism through precipitate size regulation offers valuable insights for the design of advanced alloys."

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Not a refractory alloy paper.** This paper studies a Ni-Co-based superalloy, not a Nb-W-, Mo-W-, W-based, refractory high-entropy, or additively manufactured refractory alloy. The relevance to refractory alloy literature retrieval is limited to the general principle that precipitate size control can tailor deformation mechanisms in precipitation-strengthened alloys.
- **No macroscopic mechanical property values.** The paper does not report yield strength, UTS, compressive strength, elongation, hardness, or creep data. Deformation mechanisms are characterized exclusively via TEM.
- **Single testing temperature.** Only 760 °C compression is studied. Behavior at other temperatures (e.g., 800 °C, 1000 °C, 1200 °C) is not investigated.
- **Single strain rate.** Only 3 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ is used. Strain-rate sensitivity is not explored.
- **No oxidation or environmental testing.** Oxidation resistance, embrittlement, and service stability are not addressed.
- **Limited composition range.** Only one alloy composition is studied. The effects of varying Co, Ti, Al, W, Mo, and Nb contents are not systematically investigated.
- **CRSS values are calculated, not directly measured.** The CRSS estimates rely on input parameters (γ_APB, G, ν, SFE) that are partly derived from JMatPro calculations and literature values, which may introduce uncertainties.
- **γ′ precipitate size range limited to 31–122 nm.** Behavior outside this size range is unknown based on this paper alone.
