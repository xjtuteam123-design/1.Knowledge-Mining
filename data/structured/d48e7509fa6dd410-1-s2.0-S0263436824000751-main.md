---

# Alloy System and Research Object

This study investigates a tungsten heavy alloy with nominal composition **90 W–4.9Ni–2.1Fe–3.0Cu (wt.%)**. The alloy belongs to the classical tungsten heavy alloy family, specifically the W-Ni-Fe group with an elemental Cu additive. The material is a two-phase metal matrix composite consisting of quasi-spherical BCC tungsten particles embedded in a tougher FCC nickel-based matrix. The research objective is to lower the sintering temperature through mechanical alloying (nano-grain refinement) and Cu alloying addition, thereby enabling co-sintering with steels for nuclear reactor and kinetic energy penetrator applications.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Powder Processing (Mechanical Alloying)
- **Method**: High-energy ball milling using an 8000D Dual Mixer/Mill machine.
- **Milling media**: Tungsten carbide canister with two tungsten carbide balls, each 11.2 mm in diameter.
- **Ball-to-powder ratio**: 1:4.
- **Total powder mass**: 80 g.
- **Milling time**: 10 h.
- **Milling speed**: 200 rpm.
- **Milling cycle**: 1 h milling followed by 1 h pause.
- **Process control agent (PCA)**: None used.

## Cold Compaction
- **Method**: Uniaxial hydraulic press.
- **Pressure**: 450 MPa.
- **Dwell time**: 2 min.
- **Compact dimensions**: Cylindrical billets, 10 mm diameter × 10 mm height (three samples).

## Liquid Phase Sintering
- **Sintering temperature**: 1250 °C.
- **Sintering time**: 120 min (2 h).
- **Atmosphere**: Hydrogen (5 vol%) mixed with argon.
- **Furnace**: Electrical resistance heating tube furnace (Carbolite Gero, UK).
- **Cooling**: Furnace-cooled to room temperature.

No homogenization, rolling, forging, or aging treatments were applied. Hot isostatic pressing, spark plasma sintering, and additive manufacturing were not used in this study.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## After Mechanical Alloying (Powder State)
- **Crystal structure of W**: Maintained BCC structure after ball milling.
- **XRD observations**:
  - Peak broadening.
  - Peak shift toward higher angles.
  - Reduction in peak intensity.
  - Disappearance of diffraction peaks for Ni, Cu, and Fe (indicative of solid solution formation).
- **Grain size of W after milling**: Reduced from **33 nm (as-received)** to **15 nm** (Scherrer equation analysis).
- **Solid solution formation**: Smaller substitutional solute atoms (Cu: 0.128 nm, Fe: 0.124 nm, Ni: 0.125 nm atomic radii) dissolved into W lattice (W atomic radius: 0.137 nm), reducing interplanar spacing (d-spacing) and introducing residual tensile stress in tungsten particles, causing diffraction peak shift to higher angles.
- **Particle morphology**: Fine flake morphology of the milled powder particles.

## After Liquid Phase Sintering
- **Matrix phase**: FCC Ni-Fe-Cu metallic binder matrix (nickel-based tough matrix).
- **Tungsten phase**: BCC quasi-spherical tungsten grains; mostly polygonal in shape after sintering.
- **W grain growth**: Tungsten grains coalesced to larger sizes through dissolution of smaller/spherical grains in the liquid phase and precipitation on larger/polygonal grains (Ostwald ripening mechanism).
- **Grain size after sintering**: Not explicitly quantified in the original paper; described qualitatively as grown and coalesced to larger size.
- **W-matrix interface**: Strong interfacial integrity; no debonding or tungsten pullout observed.
- **Porosity**:
  - Relative density achieved: **~88%**.
  - Residual porosity attributed to:
    - Formation of closed microporosity from reduction of absorbed hydrogen by particle surface oxide layer to water vapor.
    - Sporadic spherical pores observed in micrographs.
    - Occasional entrapped pores inside growing tungsten grains (indicated by arrows in Fig. 3c).
- **Elemental distribution**: Homogeneous distribution of W, Ni, Fe, and Cu in the sintered alloy confirmed by EDS area mapping.
- **Sintered density comparison**: The 88% relative density at 1250 °C is significantly higher than previously reported 78% (at 1250 °C) and 85% (at 1300 °C) for similar alloy composition [reference 3].

## Solid-State and Liquid-Phase Sintering Sequence
The sintering process involved:
1. Solid-state diffusion at elevated temperature (slow).
2. Formation of Ni-Fe-Cu-rich liquid phase at W interparticle boundaries.
3. Grain rearrangement facilitated by liquid phase.
4. Dissolution of smaller W particles in the liquid and precipitation on larger W particles.
5. W grain coalescence and skeletal structure formation.

Evidence of solid-solid W particle bonding without liquid (flat fracture interfaces, Zone II, Fig. 5b) and liquid-coated interfaces (rounded features, Zone II, Fig. 5c) is presented.

# Mechanical Properties

## Hardness
- **Vickers microhardness**: **380 ± 8 HV** (equivalent to 3.73 ± 0.08 GPa).
- **Test load**: 1 kgf.
- **Dwell time**: 15 s.
- **Standard**: ASTM E384–17.
- **Number of measurements**: 10, diagonally across the sample surface.
- **Comparison**: Significantly higher than the average value of 298 HV reported in a summary of 184 studies [reference 10]; exceptionally higher than the alloy with same composition sintered at the same temperature, and marginally higher than alloys sintered at 1350–1400 °C [references 3, 19].

## Compressive Properties
- **Test standard**: ASTM E9–09 (2018).
- **Testing machine**: Instron Universal Testing Machine 5589.
- **Crosshead speed**: 0.050 mm/min.
- **Sample dimensions**: 10 mm diameter × 10 mm height; three samples tested.
- **Atmosphere**: Ambient temperature (room temperature), not explicitly specified as inert.
- **Yield strength**: **910 MPa**.
- **Ultimate compressive strength**: **1326 MPa** (at 18% plastic deformation).
- **Failure strain (compressive plasticity)**: **25.6%**.
- **Compressive toughness**: **25.5 KJ/cm³** (total area under stress-strain curve).

## Fracture Behavior
- **Fracture mode**: Almost entirely **intergranular**.
- **Crack propagation paths**: Through W-W boundaries and W-matrix interfaces.
- **Slip features**: Slip steps at W-W boundaries; parallel lines of fracture in sporadic matrix patches.
- **Interfacial integrity**: No debonding or tungsten pullout observed, indicating strong W-matrix interfacial bonding.
- **Strain hardening**: Strong strain hardening from yield (910 MPa) to ultimate compressive stress (1326 MPa) at 18% plastic deformation, attributed to:
  - Impeded dislocation slip in the alloy matrix by W-atom-alloyed stronger Ni-Fe-Cu matrix.
  - Large number of fine W grains and associated W-matrix interfaces generating high dislocation impedance.
  - Dislocation buildup at W-matrix and W-W boundaries.

- Not measured or reported in the original paper.

## Tensile Properties
- Not measured (only compressive tests were performed).

## Creep Performance
- Not evaluated in this study.

## Fracture Toughness
- Not measured as K_IC; only compressive toughness (energy absorbed, 25.5 KJ/cm³) was reported.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## W (Tungsten)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Tungsten heavy alloy (W-Ni-Fe-Cu); W-Ni-Fe classical heavy alloys; W-Ni-Cu heavy alloys.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 90 wt.% in this study; typically 90–97 wt.% in tungsten heavy alloys.
- **Main Function**: Provides high density, high melting temperature, high hardness, superior radiation shielding, and high mechanical strength; forms BCC quasi-spherical grains embedded in the ductile matrix.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: BCC tungsten grains form the hard particulate reinforcement in the two-phase composite; W dissolves in liquid Ni-Fe-Cu during liquid phase sintering and reprecipitates on larger W grains (Ostwald ripening) causing grain growth and polygonalization; W atoms also dissolve into the FCC matrix, providing solid-solution strengthening; the fine W grains and large W-matrix interfacial area impede dislocation slip.
- **Property Effect**:
  - Contributes to high density (theoretical 17.19 g/cm³).
  - Microhardness 380 ± 8 HV.
  - Compressive yield strength 910 MPa.
  - Ultimate compressive strength 1326 MPa.
  - The presence of fine W grains and W-matrix interfaces enhances strength through dislocation impedance.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Brittleness of the BCC W phase; high melting temperature of pure W (requiring very high sintering temperatures, ~2500 °C) necessitates liquid phase sintering or alloying additions; W grain coarsening during sintering can occur.
- **Original Evidence**: "tungsten heavy alloy are two phase metal matrix composites, where quasi-spherical body centered cubic (BCC) crystal structured brittle tungsten particles are embedded in a nickel based tougher face centered cubic (FCC) crystal structured matrix" (Section 1); "tungsten grains grew mostly in a polygonal shape, coalesced to a large size, and had strong interfacial integrity with the nickel ‑ iron ‑ copper matrix" (Abstract).

## Ni (Nickel)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Tungsten heavy alloy (W-Ni-Fe-Cu); W-Ni-Fe classical heavy alloys; W-Ni-Cu heavy alloys.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 4.9 wt.% in this study.
- **Main Function**: Forms the ductile FCC matrix; provides ductile phase toughening by enhancing the fracture toughness of tungsten through deliberate incorporation of a ductile phase into a hard and brittle material; lowers the melting point of the binder phase.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Nickel has very high solubility for tungsten (45 wt% at 1480 °C), facilitating dissolution of W particles at edges, diffusion through the liquid, and precipitation on larger particles; forms Ni-Fe-Cu liquid at sintering temperature; the Ni-based FCC matrix is softer than BCC W, and dislocation slip initiates in this matrix during yielding; W atoms dissolved in the Ni-rich matrix provide solid-solution strengthening; dislocations accumulate at the W-matrix interface.
- **Property Effect**:
  - Enhances ductility and fracture toughness through ductile phase toughening mechanism.
  - Higher matrix strength due to W solid-solution strengthening.
  - Yield strength 910 MPa and ultimate compressive strength 1326 MPa are partly attributed to the strengthened Ni-Fe-Cu matrix.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Original Evidence**: "The presence of the face-centered matrix enhances the fracture toughness of tungsten by enhancing its ductility using 'ductile phase toughening' mechanism, which involves deliberate incorporation of a ductile phase into a hard and brittle material" (Section 1); "Very high solubility of tungsten in nickel and iron (e.g., 45 wt% and 30 wt% at 1480 °C, respectively)" (Section 1).

---

## Fe (Iron)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Tungsten heavy alloy (W-Ni-Fe-Cu); W-Ni-Fe classical heavy alloys.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 2.1 wt.% in this study.
- **Main Function**: Along with Ni, forms the tough FCC binder matrix; promotes ductile phase toughening; solubility of W in Fe (30 wt% at 1480 °C) is significant for liquid phase sintering.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Dissolves in the Ni-rich liquid during sintering; forms the Ni-Fe-Cu liquid phase that penetrates W interparticle boundaries and facilitates dissolution-reprecipitation of W grains; Fe atoms contribute to solid-solution strengthening of the matrix when dissolved along with Ni and Cu.
- **Property Effect**: Contributes to the ductility and toughness of the matrix phase; participates in matrix strengthening; overall alloy strength values (yield 910 MPa, UCS 1326 MPa) reflect the combined matrix strengthening effect.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Original Evidence**: "Very high solubility of tungsten in nickel and iron (e.g., 45 wt% and 30 wt% at 1480 °C, respectively), helps in the dissolution of in the nickel rich liquid from the particles edges, diffuse in the liquid and precipitate on larger particles helping densification and formation of the characteristic rounded tungsten grains" (Section 1).

---

## Cu (Copper)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Tungsten heavy alloy (W-Ni-Fe-Cu); W-Ni-Cu classical heavy alloys.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 3.0 wt.% in this study.
- **Main Function**: Reduces the sintering temperature of W-Ni-Fe heavy alloys; improves the sintering response by lowering the melting point of the binder phase; considered the most suitable additive to effectively reduce sintering temperature without compromising mechanical properties.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Copper has large mutual solubility with nickel and iron without forming intermetallic compounds; the Cu-Ni-Fe liquid phase acts as an easy and preferential W diffusion medium during liquid phase sintering; the Cu-containing liquid penetrates tungsten grain boundaries; Cu dissolves into W lattice during mechanical alloying and contributes to solid-solution formation.
- **Property Effect**: Enables relatively low temperature liquid phase sintering at 1250 °C with 88% relative density. The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for the individual contribution of Cu to mechanical properties; however, the combined alloy achieves yield strength 910 MPa and UCS 1326 MPa.
- **Potential Side Effects**: The dissolution enthalpy for W in Cu is significantly larger (less favorable) than in Ni and Fe; the paper notes this in the context of matrix strengthening: "the dissolution enthalpy for tungsten in nickel and iron is significantly smaller than that of copper" (Section 3.3).
- **Original Evidence**: "Copper has large mutual solubility in nickel and iron without formation of intermetallic compounds and considered to most suitable additive element to effectively reduce the sintering temperature of tungsten heavy alloy without compromising mechanical properties" (Section 1); "the interparticle liquid phase acted as an easy and preferential tungsten diffusion media during the liquid phase sintering process" (Section 3.2).

## Ni+Fe+Cu Combination (Binder Matrix Elements)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Tungsten heavy alloy (W-Ni-Fe-Cu).
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 4.9 wt.% Ni + 2.1 wt.% Fe + 3.0 wt.% Cu (total 10 wt.%).
- **Main Function**: Forms the low-melting-point liquid phase during sintering; provides the tough FCC matrix; collectively reduces the sintering temperature; supports liquid phase sintering through W dissolution-diffusion-reprecipitation.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: During sintering, a thin-layered multi-element (Ni, Fe, Cu) liquid phase forms along W interparticle boundaries; the liquid penetrates W grain boundaries, dissolves smaller W grains, and reprecipitates W on larger grains; W atoms dissolved in the matrix provide solid-solution strengthening; the matrix phase acts as the dislocation slip initiation site during mechanical loading; dislocations accumulate at matrix-W interfaces.
- **Property Effect**:
  - Enables low-temperature (1250 °C) liquid phase sintering with 88% relative density.
  - Microhardness 380 ± 8 HV.
  - Compressive yield strength 910 MPa.
  - Ultimate compressive strength 1326 MPa.
  - Failure strain 25.6%.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Residual porosity due to trapped water vapor from surface oxide reduction by hydrogen; sporadic spherical pores; inability to achieve full density (limited to 88% relative density).
- **Original Evidence**: "in the liquid phase sintering of tungsten ‑ nickel ‑ iron ‑ copper (W-Ni-Fe-Cu) alloys, at the end on initial solid-solid diffusion bonding among the tungsten particles the sintering mechanism involved in induction of a thin-layered multi-element (i.e., nickel, iron, and copper) liquid phase along the tungsten-interparticle boundaries" (Section 3.2).

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related Elements**: W, Ni, Fe, Cu.
- **Typical Alloys**: W-Ni-Fe-Cu tungsten heavy alloy (this study); W-Ni-Fe classical heavy alloys.
- **Key Compositions**: 90 W–4.9Ni–2.1Fe–3.0Cu (wt.%).
- **Microstructural Changes**: Mechanical alloying forms W-based solid solution with Ni, Fe, and Cu atoms diffusing into the W lattice; smaller solute atoms (Ni 0.125 nm, Fe 0.124 nm, Cu 0.128 nm) substitute for larger W atoms (0.137 nm), causing lattice contraction and residual tensile stress; the Ni-Fe-Cu matrix also becomes strengthened by W atoms dissolved during liquid phase sintering.
- **Property Improvements**:
  - High microhardness 380 ± 8 HV.
  - Compressive yield strength 910 MPa.
  - Ultimate compressive strength 1326 MPa.
  - The W-atom-strengthened Ni-Fe-Cu matrix and fine W grains generate high impedance to dislocation slip.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Higher W dissolution in matrix does not inherently limit ductility in this study; however, residual porosity (12%) limits full densification and may compromise other properties if tested under tension.

## Grain Refinement Strengthening (Nano-crystallinity from Mechanical Alloying)

- **Related Elements**: W (grain size reduction); facilitated by high-energy ball milling.
- **Typical Alloys**: W-Ni-Fe-Cu (this study); W-Ni-Fe systems processed by ball milling.
- **Key Compositions**: 90 W–4.9Ni–2.1Fe–3.0Cu.
- **Microstructural Changes**: W grain size reduced from 33 nm to 15 nm during 10 h high-energy ball milling; repeated severe plastic deformation, dislocation accumulation, subgrain formation, and low-angle-to-high-angle grain boundary transitions produce nano-crystalline W grains; XRD showed peak broadening and peak shifts.
- **Property Improvements**: Nano-crystalline W enables lower sintering temperature (1250 °C vs. conventional ~1480–2500 °C for W heavy alloys); contributes to high mechanical properties after sintering.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Nano-grains can coarsen during liquid phase sintering; the final sintered W grain size is not quantified and is described as largely grown and coalesced.

---

## Liquid Phase Sintering-Enabled Densification and Microstructural Homogenization

- **Related Elements**: Ni, Fe, Cu (binder phase elements); W (solid particles).
- **Typical Alloys**: W-Ni-Fe-Cu (this study); W-Ni-Fe; W-Ni-Cu.
- **Key Compositions**: 90 W–4.9Ni–2.1Fe–3.0Cu.
- **Microstructural Changes**: Liquid phase formation at W interparticle boundaries; W dissolution-diffusion-reprecipitation (Ostwald ripening); polygonalization of W grains; coalescence of W grains; elimination of some powder-processed porosity; homogeneous element distribution; strong W-matrix interfacial bonding.
- **Property Improvements**: 88% relative density at low temperature (1250 °C); uniform microstructure; high hardness and compressive strength.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Residual porosity (~12%) from trapped water vapor and sporadic spherical pores; incomplete densification; isolated pores may be entrapped within growing W grains.

---

## Ductile Phase Toughening

- **Related Elements**: Ni and Fe (ductile FCC binder phase).
- **Typical Alloys**: W-Ni-Fe-Cu (this study); W-Ni-Fe classical heavy alloys.
- **Key Compositions**: 4.9 wt.% Ni + 2.1 wt.% Fe (within 90 W heavy alloy).
- **Microstructural Changes**: Ductile FCC matrix surrounds brittle BCC W grains; the matrix enhances fracture toughness by blunting crack propagation; no debonding at W-matrix interfaces, indicating strong interfacial integrity.
- **Property Improvements**: Failure strain of 25.6% under compression; enhanced ductility relative to pure brittle W; strong work hardening up to 18% plastic strain.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Fracture remains intergranular; cracks propagate through W-W boundaries and W-matrix interfaces; the matrix has slip-induced cracks at high strains.

# Key Conclusions

1. High-energy ball milling (10 h, 200 rpm, BPR 1:4) successfully mechanically alloyed elemental W, Ni, Fe, and Cu powders into a single-phase solid solution with tungsten grain size reduced to **15 nm** (from 33 nm as-received).
2. Relatively low temperature liquid phase sintering at **1250 °C** for 120 min in 5% H₂/Ar was effective in producing a **~88% dense** tungsten heavy alloy.
3. The sintered microstructure consisted of polygonal-shaped tungsten grains, coalesced to larger sizes, with strong W-matrix interfacial integrity and homogeneous elemental distribution; no visible powder-processed porosity or debonding was observed.
4. Mechanical properties were:
   - Microhardness: **380 ± 8 HV**
   - Compressive yield strength: **910 MPa**
   - Ultimate compressive strength: **1326 MPa**
   - Compressive failure strain: **25.6%**
   - Compressive toughness: **25.5 KJ/cm³**
5. The applied compressive stress induced **intergranular crack propagation** through W-W boundaries and W-matrix interfaces, with slip steps at W-W boundaries and slip-induced cracks in the matrix.
6. The combined approach of nano-crystallinity (ball milling) and Cu alloying addition successfully reduced the sintering temperature from typical tungsten heavy alloy sintering temperatures while retaining rather good mechanical properties, although full density was not achieved.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Density limitation**: The relative density achieved was only ~88%; full density was not achieved. This leaves ~12% porosity, which could compromise properties in service, especially for radiation shielding applications depending on density.
- **Intergranular fracture**: The material failed almost entirely by intergranular fracture under compression, indicating potential limitations for tensile and impact applications.
- **No high-temperature mechanical testing**: All mechanical tests were conducted at ambient temperature. The paper does not address high-temperature strength, creep, or microstructural stability at elevated temperatures.
- **No oxidation evaluation**: Oxidation resistance was not tested. The addition of Cu may affect oxidation behavior, but this was not explored.
- **No tensile testing**: Only compressive properties were measured; tensile properties, fracture toughness (K_IC), and ductile-to-brittle transition temperature behavior were not evaluated.
- **No quantification of post-sintering W grain size**: Although the pre-sintering W grain size was measured (15 nm), the final sintered W grain size was not quantitatively evaluated.
- **Applicable scope**: The findings are directly applicable to tungsten heavy alloys (W-Ni-Fe-Cu) intended for low-temperature co-sintering with other metallic components (e.g., steel), radiation shielding, kinetic energy penetrators, counterbalance weights, and potentially nuclear sector applications. The approach may not be directly transferable to higher-melting-point refractory alloys such as Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloys without modifying processing parameters.
