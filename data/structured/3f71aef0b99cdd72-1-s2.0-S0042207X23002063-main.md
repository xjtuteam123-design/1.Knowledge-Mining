# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase:** α-Mg (hexagonal close-packed, HCP).
- **Second phase (main strengthening phase):** β′ phase (metastable precipitate).
  - Crystal structure: bottom-centered orthogonal (BCO) structure.
  - Lattice constants: a_β = 2a_α = 0.64 nm, b_β = 8d_(10-10)α = 2.27 nm, c_β = c_α = 0.52 nm.
  - Orientation relationship with α-Mg matrix: (001)_β // (0001)_α, [100]_β // [2-1-10]_α.
  - Morphology: fine round flake, dispersed in the α-Mg matrix.
  - Distribution: perpendicular to the basal plane on the cylindrical plane of the HCP Mg structure.
- **Equilibrium phase after long-time high-temperature creep:** β phase (face-centered cubic, FCC).
  - Lattice constant: a_β = 0.73 nm.
  - Morphology: coarse long strip (transformed from β′ phase).
  - β phase is the thermodynamic equilibrium phase of Mg-Gd alloys.
- **Grain boundary features:**
  - Before creep: relatively straight grain boundaries.
  - After creep at 200 °C/70 MPa for 100 h: grain boundary sliding occurs, forming curved grain boundaries.
  - A bright white precipitation-free zone (PFZ) forms near grain boundaries after creep.
- **Dislocation substructure after creep:**
  - At 250 °C/70 and 90 MPa: scores of dislocations appear; dislocation slip and climb are evident.
  - At 300 °C/50 MPa: dislocation walls formed by slipping and climbing, arranged with large numbers of dislocations, exhibiting dislocation creep characteristics.
- **Grain size:** Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Elemental segregation:** The O elemental mapping shows that O does not aggregate at grain boundaries and phase boundaries.
- **Precipitate size and distribution:**
  - Effective diameter (d_t) and thickness (t_t) of the β′ round flake precipitates: Not explicitly stated with numerical values in the original paper. The paper states that β′ phase maintains a large volume fraction and high density.
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed:**
  - **Solid-solution strengthening:** Not explicitly emphasized as a primary mechanism in the aged alloy.
  - **Precipitation strengthening (Orowan strengthening):** Strong Orowan strengthening effect from β′ round flake precipitates.
  - **Coherent strengthening:** Obvious coherent strengthening effect due to the highly coherent interface between β′ phase and α-Mg matrix, atomic radius difference between Gd/Y/Sm and Mg, and atomic concentration difference.
  - **Dispersion strengthening:** Discussed through Orowan mechanism equations (Eqs. 4 and 5).
  - **Grain-boundary strengthening/grain refinement:** Not a primary mechanism for this alloy; grain boundary sliding is a creep mechanism rather than strengthening mechanism.
  - **ODS strengthening:** Not applicable.
  - **Second-phase strengthening:** Yes, β′ phase second-phase strengthening.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Gd (Gadolinium)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Mg-Gd-Y-Sm-Zr (GWS1123) magnesium-rare earth alloy.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 11 wt% (nominal); 11.05 wt% (measured).
- **Main Function:** Major rare-earth alloying element; forms the metastable β′ strengthening precipitate; provides high-temperature strength and creep resistance.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Gd promotes the precipitation of β′ phase during aging. The β′ phase has a bottom-centered orthogonal structure with lattice constants a_β = 0.64 nm, b_β = 2.27 nm, c_β = 0.52 nm, and is coherent with α-Mg matrix with orientation (001)_β // (0001)_α, [100]_β // [2-1-10]_α. Atomic radius difference between Gd and Mg creates elastic strain fields that hinder dislocation movement.
- **Property Effect:**
  - UTS at 250 °C: 319 MPa (26.6% higher than room-temperature UTS of 252 MPa).
  - TYS at 250 °C: 246 MPa (22.4% higher than room-temperature TYS of 201 MPa).
  - Excellent creep resistance at 200–250 °C/50–110 MPa and 300 °C/50 MPa (steady-state creep rate 1.48 × 10⁻⁸ s⁻¹ at 300 °C/50 MPa).
- **Potential Side Effects:** During long-term high-temperature exposure (250 °C creep), β′ transforms to coarse β equilibrium phase (FCC, a = 0.73 nm), which reduces strengthening and creep resistance. No other specific side effects reported.
- **Original Evidence:** Abstract; Fig. 3 (mechanical properties); Fig. 7 (TEM and SAED); Fig. 8 (c, d) (β phase transformation). "The β′ phase is the main strengthening phase in the peak-aged Mg–Gd alloys."

## Gd+Y+Sm (Rare-earth combination)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Mg-Gd-Y-Sm-Zr (GWS1123) magnesium-rare earth alloy.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## β′ Phase Precipitation Strengthening (Orowan Strengthening)

- **Related Elements:** Gd, Y, Sm (rare-earth solutes).
- **Typical Alloys:** Mg-Gd-Y-Sm-Zr (GWS1123); peak-aged Mg-Gd-based alloys.
- **Key Compositions:** Mg-11Gd-2Y-3Sm-0.5Zr.
- **Microstructural Changes:** During aging at 225 °C/12 h, fine round flake β′ precipitates form, dispersed in the α-Mg matrix on cylindrical planes perpendicular to the basal plane. β′ has a bottom-centered orthogonal structure (a = 0.64 nm, b = 2.27 nm, c = 0.52 nm) and is coherent with α-Mg.
- **Property Improvements:**
  - At 250 °C: UTS = 319 MPa, TYS = 246 MPa (26.6% and 22.4% higher than room temperature values).
  - Excellent creep resistance at 200–250 °C/50–110 MPa and 300 °C/50 MPa.
  - Steady-state creep rate at 300 °C/50 MPa = 1.48 × 10⁻⁸ s⁻¹.
- **Risks and Limitations:**
  - The β′ phase is only stable up to about 250 °C. Above this temperature, it transforms to coarse β equilibrium phase (FCC, a = 0.73 nm), reducing strengthening and creep resistance.
  - Creep at 250 °C/70–90 MPa causes coarsening and phase transformation, leading to loss of Orowan strengthening.
- **Original Evidence:** Section 3.3; Eqs. (4) and (5) for Orowan strengthening; Fig. 7; Fig. 8.

## Coherent Strengthening

- **Related Elements:** Gd, Y, Sm.
- **Typical Alloys:** Mg-Gd-Y-Sm-Zr (GWS1123).
- **Key Compositions:** Mg-11Gd-2Y-3Sm-0.5Zr.
- **Microstructural Changes:** The highly coherent interface between β′ phase and α-Mg matrix, combined with atomic radius differences (Gd, Y, Sm vs. Mg) and atomic concentration differences, creates an elastic strain field in the α-Mg matrix that hinders dislocation movement.
- **Property Improvements:** Contributes to the high-temperature strength and creep resistance of GWS1123 alloy.
- **Risks and Limitations:** Coherent strengthening diminishes when β′ loses coherency or transforms to β phase at elevated temperatures.
- **Original Evidence:** Section 3.3: "There is a highly coherent interface between β′ phase and α-Mg matrix... it will inevitably cause lattice dislocation in α-Mg matrix, form an elastic strain field, hinder dislocation movement, and produce obvious coherent strengthening effect."

## Grain-Boundary Sliding Creep Mechanism (at 200 °C)

- **Related Elements:** Not element-specific; related to grain boundary characteristics and PFZ.
- **Typical Alloys:** GWS1123.
- **Key Compositions:** Mg-11Gd-2Y-3Sm-0.5Zr.

## Dislocation Slip and Climb Creep Mechanism (at 250–300 °C)

- **Related Elements:** Not element-specific.
- **Typical Alloys:** GWS1123.
- **Key Compositions:** Mg-11Gd-2Y-3Sm-0.5Zr.
- **Microstructural Changes:**
  - At 250 °C/70–90 MPa: scores of dislocations appear; dislocation slip and climb occur.

# Key Conclusions

1. The GWS1123 (Mg-11Gd-2Y-3Sm-0.5Zr) alloy exhibits superior room-temperature and high-temperature tensile strength. At 250 °C, UTS = 319 MPa and TYS = 246 MPa, which are 26.6% and 22.4% higher than the room-temperature values (252 MPa and 201 MPa, respectively).
2. The alloy has excellent creep resistance at 200–250 °C/50–110 MPa and 300 °C/50 MPa. The steady-state creep rate at 300 °C/50 MPa is 1.48 × 10⁻⁸ s⁻¹.
3. The effect of creep temperature on creep resistance is greater than that of stress. Increasing temperature from 200 °C to 300 °C increased creep strain by 5.8–29.1 times and steady-state creep rate by 10–798 times.
4. The good heat resistance, strong Orowan strengthening effect, and obvious coherent strengthening effect of the β′ phase are responsible for the high-temperature strength and creep resistance of GWS1123 alloy.
5. The creep mechanism of GWS1123 alloy at 200–300 °C/50–110 MPa is mainly grain boundary slip, dislocation slip, and climb:
   - At 200 °C (n = 2.2): grain boundary slip creep.
   - At 250 °C (n = 3.33): dislocation slip creep.
   - At 300 °C (n = 7.37): power law fails; non-basal dislocation motion dominates.
6. After creep at 250 °C/70–90 MPa, the fine round flake β′ phase transforms into coarse long strip β equilibrium phase (FCC, a = 0.73 nm). Curved grain boundaries, dislocation tangles, and dislocation walls form, confirming grain boundary sliding and dislocation creep mechanisms.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Material class limitation:** This paper studies a Mg-RE (magnesium-rare earth) alloy, which is fundamentally different from Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based, W-based, or refractory high-entropy alloys. The mechanisms, temperatures, and alloying concepts discussed here are not directly transferable to refractory alloy systems.
- **Temperature range limitation:** The alloy is designed for applications below ~300 °C. The β′ strengthening phase is only stable up to ~250 °C. This is far below the service temperatures of refractory alloys (typically ≥1000 °C).
- **Creep stress range:** 50–110 MPa, which is much lower than typical stress levels for structural refractory alloys.
- **Oxidation resistance:** The paper does not investigate oxidation resistance; melting was performed under protective atmosphere to control O impurity, but no high-temperature oxidation behavior is reported.
- **Processing route limitation:** Only vacuum induction melting + casting + solution + aging was studied. No powder metallurgy, additive manufacturing, or thermomechanical processing routes are evaluated.
- **Grain size data:** Not reported; the role of grain size on mechanical and creep properties cannot be quantified from this paper.
- **Isolated element effects:** The paper does not systematically isolate the individual contributions of Gd, Y, Sm, and Zr to the observed properties; only the combined alloy performance is reported.
- **Applicability to refractory alloys:** This paper is NOT applicable to refractory high-temperature alloy design or research, as it addresses a completely different material class (lightweight Mg alloys) with service temperatures below 300 °C.
