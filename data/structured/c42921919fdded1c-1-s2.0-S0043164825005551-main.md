# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Effect of B4C incorporation on microstructure, mechanical properties and tribological behavior against Si3N4 counterpart of CoCrW alloy
- **Authors**: Chen Tang, Shasha Yang, Dongbiao Li, Chengguo Sui, Minghui Chen, Fuhui Wang
- **Journal**: Wear
- **Year**: 2025
- **DOI**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.wear.2025.206286
- **Alloy System Studied**: CoCrW alloy (Stellite alloy), B4C-reinforced CoCrW
- **Research Objective**: To investigate the effect of B4C incorporation on microstructure, mechanical properties, and tribological behavior of CoCrW alloy against Si3N4 counterpart from room temperature to 700 °C, with potential application as high-temperature bearing sleeves of aero-engine
- **Material Category**: Co-based alloy, carbide-reinforced CoCrW alloy, B4C-reinforced CoCrW composite

# Alloy System and Research Object

The paper studies a CoCrW alloy (Stellite alloy) and a B4C-reinforced variant prepared by spark plasma sintering. The research focuses on the influence of B4C addition on microstructure evolution (particularly carbide modification), mechanical properties (hardness, compressive properties), and high-temperature tribological behavior against Si3N4 counterparts. The material is proposed for high-temperature bearing sleeve applications in aero-engines with a maximum operational temperature of 700 °C.

The matrix is a Co-based alloy with Cr and W additions, containing chromium carbides (Cr7C3). With the addition of 1 wt.% B4C, the carbide undergoes heterogeneous growth with B incorporation, forming Cr7(C,B)3 lath carbides.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Powder Mixing
- **Method**: High-energy ball milling
- **Duration**: 10 h
- **Ball-to-powder mass ratio**: 10:1

## Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS)
- **Equipment**: FCT HPD 250, Germany
- **Heating rate**: 25 °C/min
- **Sintering temperature**: 1100 °C
- **Holding time**: 15 min
- **Pressure**: 40 MPa
- **Vacuum**: 0.1 Pa

After sintering, the alloys were cut into φ6 × 9 mm for compression tests and 32 × 5 × 4.5 mm for tribological tests. The surfaces were sanded with 400#, 1000#, and 2000# SiC sandpapers and then polished until free of scratches.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Phase Constituents

- **0B alloy**: γ-Co matrix (γ and ε phases) + granular Cr7C3 carbides (dark gray secondary phase)
- **1B alloy**: γ/ε-Co matrix + Cr7(C,B)3 lath carbides + residual submicron B4C particles

During SPS, B4C is partly decomposed, leading to the formation of Cr7(C,B)3 by introducing B into Cr7C3. Residual submicron-scale B4C particles are observed adjacent to Cr7(C,B)3.

## Carbide Morphology and Distribution

- **0B alloy**: Fine granular Cr7C3 carbides distributed at grain boundaries and within alloy grains
- **1B alloy**: Large lath-shaped Cr7(C,B)3 carbides, sparsely distributed

The heterogeneous growth of carbide occurs on the basis of B4C particles, with directional growth (or heterogeneous nucleation growth) fundamentally different from the homogeneous nucleation of fine carbides in the 0B alloy. The nucleation core of B4C particles (or unreacted B4C particles) at the end of Cr7(C,B)3 is still present.

## Boron Doping Effect

B doping in chromium carbide increases the stacking fault energy (SFE) and deformation resistance of the carbide. TEM analysis after compressive deformation (2000 MPa, 10 s) shows regular atomic arrangement inside Cr7(C,B)3 without obvious misalignments in 1B alloy, while the 0B alloy shows high density of stacking faults in both matrix and carbides.

## Mixing Enthalpy (Table 3)

| ΔH_mix (kJ/mol) | Co | Cr | C | B |
|------------------|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| Co | 0 | −4 | −42 | −24 |
| Cr | | 0 | −61 | −31 |
| C | | | 0 | −10 |
| B | | | | 0 |

The large negative mixing enthalpy between Cr and C (−61 kJ/mol) compared to Cr and B (−31 kJ/mol) favors the formation of Cr-C-B ternary compound, with chromium carbide being more stable than its boride.

## Subsurface Deformation After Wear

- **0B alloy at RT**: Continuous crushing layer develops at worn subsurface with numerous microcracks
- **1B alloy at RT**: Almost no plastic deformation layer beneath worn scar; cracks confined to lamellar Cr7(C,B)3
- **0B alloy at 700 °C (5 min)**: 1.98 μm thick deformed layer with oxide doping; numerous low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs, 2° ≤ θ ≤ 15°)
- **1B alloy at 700 °C (5 min)**: No deformation area at subsurface; few LAGBs and localized misorientation
- **0B alloy at 700 °C (30 min)**: Deformation layer reduced to 1.39 μm with transition layer of Cr-rich oxide
- **1B alloy at 700 °C (30 min)**: Glaze layer grows slowly to 1.45 μm; continuous Cr2O3 layer forms at interface

- Ultimate plasticity strain increased by ~23%

## Deformation Behavior After 2000 MPa Compression for 10 s

- **0B alloy**: Large area dislocation tangles (DTs) formed in front of carbides; high density of stacking faults (SFs) in both matrix and carbides
- **1B alloy**: Small amount of DTs; regular atomic arrangement inside Cr7(C,B)3 without obvious misalignments; low density DTs and SFs in matrix

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

## High-Temperature Sliding Oxidation Behavior

- **Tribo-oxide film formation temperature (T_tof)**:
  - 1B alloy: T_tof = 450 °C (semi-continuous tribo-oxide film forms)
  - 0B alloy: T_tof appears later (oxidation wear transition completed at 500 °C)

- **At 500 °C**:
  - 0B: Incomplete tribo-oxidation film, delaminated during sliding, remains as oxide islands
  - 1B: More continuous tribo-oxide film, small quantity of cracks in matrix and carbides

- **At 700 °C (5 min)**:
  - 0B: Discontinuous tribo-oxide layer, no obvious Co and Cr enrichment; 1.98 μm deformed layer beneath
  - 1B: Continuous Co-rich glaze layer (1.27 μm thick) without deformation area beneath

- **At 700 °C (30 min)**:
  - 0B: Co-rich glaze layer forms, but with transition layer of Cr-rich oxide and deformation layer (1.39 μm) containing microcracks
  - 1B: Glaze layer grows slowly to 1.45 μm; continuous Cr2O3 layer forms at interface between glaze layer and alloy

## Internal Oxidation Resistance

- 0B alloy: Friction-induced microcracks provide fast diffusion channels for oxygen; abundant dislocations and stress concentrations prevent formation of continuous Cr2O3 layer; transition layer of Cr2O3 and Co matrix formed
- 1B alloy: Dense protective glaze layer forms quickly; continuous Cr2O3 forms beneath, reducing diffusion rate of metal and oxygen, avoiding internal tribo-oxidation

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## [B4C (Boron Carbide)]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
CoCrW alloy (Stellite alloy); also referenced for Fe-, Co-, Ni-based superalloys, Ti-, Al-, Cu-based alloys

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
1 wt.% B4C (1B alloy); B4C particle size 1–10 μm

**[Main Function]**
Modifies carbide shape and distribution; promotes heterogeneous carbide growth; acts as B and C source for Cr7(C,B)3 formation; improves strength and tribological properties

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
During SPS at 1100 °C, B4C is partially decomposed. B diffuses into chromium carbide, forming Cr7(C,B)3. The mixing enthalpy between Cr and C (−61 kJ/mol) is more negative than Cr and B (−31 kJ/mol), favoring Cr-C-B ternary compound formation. Carbide formation occurs through heterogeneous nucleation growth on B4C particles, producing lath-shaped carbides instead of granular carbides. Residual submicron B4C particles remain adjacent to Cr7(C,B)3.

**[Property Effect]**
- Hardness increased from 508 Hv to 567 Hv
- Yield strength increased from 1535 MPa to 1740 MPa
- Compressive strength increased from 2210 MPa to 2535 MPa
- Ultimate plasticity strain increased from 26% to 32%
- Room-temperature COF reduced from 0.64 to 0.41
- Room-temperature wear rate reduced from 2.64 × 10^−5 to 1.31 × 10^−5 mm³N⁻¹m⁻¹
- At 700 °C (30 min), COF reduced from 0.42 to 0.25, wear rate reduced from 1.94 × 10^−5 to 6.53 × 10^−6 mm³N⁻¹m⁻¹

**[Potential Side Effects]**
Residual B4C particles remain as submicron-scale dark black phases adjacent to Cr7(C,B)3, indicating incomplete decomposition during SPS at 1100 °C.

## [B (Boron) Doping in Chromium Carbide]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
CoCrW alloy with B4C addition, resulting in Cr7(C,B)3

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
1 wt.% B4C addition (providing B for doping)

**[Main Function]**
Increases stacking fault energy (SFE) of chromium carbide; enhances deformation resistance of carbide; promotes lath morphology

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
B doping into Cr7C3 lattice forms Cr7(C,B)3. TEM after 2000 MPa compression shows regular atomic arrangement inside Cr7(C,B)3 without obvious misalignments, while undoped Cr7C3 in 0B alloy shows high density of stacking faults. This indicates B doping increases SFE and deformation resistance of carbide.

**[Property Effect]**
1B alloy shows higher strength and plasticity than 0B alloy. Sparse large lath carbides in 1B alloy allow more dislocation pile-up before crack nucleation, improving ductility while maintaining strength. At high temperatures, Cr7(C,B)3 retains high resistance to deformation and supports tribo-oxide film formation.

**[Potential Side Effects]**
Cracks are generated and propagated inside Cr7(C,B)3 during wear from room temperature to 300 °C, absorbing frictional shear work. This is beneficial for wear resistance but indicates some brittleness of carbides.

**[Original Evidence]**
"The doping of boron has effectively increased the stacking fault energy (SFE) and deformation resistance of carbide" (Section 4.1). "B diffuses into the chromium carbide, which leads to the formation of Cr7(C, B)3" (Section 4.1). Table 3 shows mixing enthalpy values.

## [Cr7C3 / Cr7(C,B)3 (Chromium Carbide)]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
CoCrW alloy (Stellite alloy), B4C-reinforced CoCrW

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
1.01 wt.% C in base powder; carbides form during sintering. 0B alloy contains granular Cr7C3; 1B alloy contains lath Cr7(C,B)3.

**[Main Function]**
Primary strengthening/secondary phase; wear resistance enhancement; high-temperature deformation resistance; supports tribo-oxide film formation; contributes to glaze layer stability

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
In 0B alloy, fine granular Cr7C3 distributed at grain boundaries and within grains impedes dislocations, leading to earlier yielding and cracking. In 1B alloy, large lath Cr7(C,B)3 with B doping provides higher deformation resistance and sparser distribution, allowing more dislocation pile-up before crack nucleation. The carbide yield strength (σc) is much higher than the matrix (σm). At high temperature, Cr7(C,B)3 protrudes and counts against the Si3N4 ball alone, reducing adhesive wear. Carbide shape change (granular to lath) and distribution modification are the key factors improving strength and plasticity simultaneously.

**[Property Effect]**
- 1B alloy hardness: 567 Hv vs 508 Hv for 0B
- 1B yield strength: 1740 MPa vs 1535 MPa for 0B
- 1B compressive strength: 2535 MPa vs 2210 MPa for 0B
- 1B ultimate plasticity: 32% vs 26% for 0B
- Room-temperature COF: 0.41 (1B) vs 0.64 (0B)
- Room-temperature wear rate: 1.31 × 10^−5 (1B) vs 2.64 × 10^−5 (0B) mm³N⁻¹m⁻¹
- 700 °C COF: 0.25 (1B) vs 0.42 (0B)
- 700 °C wear rate: 6.53 × 10^−6 (1B) vs 1.94 × 10^−5 (0B) mm³N⁻¹m⁻¹

**[Potential Side Effects]**
Cracks propagate inside carbides during sliding/wear. In 0B alloy, fine dense carbides lead to earlier yielding or cracking; dislocations impeded to form high-density DTs. In 1B alloy, microcracks in lath carbides absorb frictional shear work, which is beneficial but indicates carbide brittleness.

**[Original Evidence]**
"During the sintering process, heterogeneous growth of carbide is occurred and B diffuses into the chromium carbides. The shape and distribution of the precipitated carbides has been changed" (Section 1). "In carbide-strengthened superalloys, the primary strengthening mechanism lies in the widely distributed secondary phase" (Section 4.1). Table 2 lists mechanical properties.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

**Related Elements**: W, Cr in Co matrix
**Typical Alloys**: CoCrW alloy (0B and 1B)
**Key Compositions**: 27.61 wt.% Cr, 6.98 wt.% W in Co matrix
**Microstructural Changes**: γ-Co matrix with solid-solutioned Cr and W. During high-temperature softening, the matrix deforms more easily.
**Property Improvements**: Base hardness of 508 Hv in 0B alloy; matrix provides toughness for carbide-reinforced composite.
**Risks and Limitations**: High-temperature softening of matrix leads to increased wear rates above 100 °C; the matrix alone cannot resist severe deformation under high-temperature sliding.

## Carbide Precipitation Strengthening (Cr7C3 / Cr7(C,B)3)

**Related Elements**: Cr, C, B
**Typical Alloys**: CoCrW alloy (0B with Cr7C3; 1B with Cr7(C,B)3)
**Key Compositions**: 1.01 wt.% C; 1 wt.% B4C addition in 1B
**Microstructural Changes**: 
- 0B: fine granular Cr7C3 at grain boundaries and within grains, impeding dislocations effectively but leading to early yielding
- 1B: large lath-shaped Cr7(C,B)3, sparsely distributed with B doping increasing SFE and deformation resistance of carbide
**Property Improvements**: 1B vs 0B: yield strength 1740 vs 1535 MPa; compressive strength 2535 vs 2210 MPa; hardness 567 vs 508 Hv; and simultaneous increase in ultimate plasticity strain (32% vs 26%)
**Risks and Limitations**: Fine dense carbides in 0B cause earlier yielding or cracking. In 1B, microcracks form inside lath carbides during compressive stress, absorbing frictional shear work during sliding.

## Oxide Dispersion / Oxide Film Formation Strengthening (Tribo-Oxide Film)

**Related Elements**: Co, Cr, O
**Typical Alloys**: CoCrW alloy (0B and 1B)
**Key Compositions**: Co-rich oxides and Cr2O3 formed during high-temperature sliding
**Microstructural Changes**: At ≥450 °C (1B) or ≥500 °C (0B), oxide debris accumulates on worn surfaces, forming tribo-oxide film. At 700 °C, film becomes dense glaze layer. Beneath glaze layer in 1B, continuous Cr2O3 layer forms at interface.
**Property Improvements**: 
- COF reduced to 0.25 (1B, 700 °C, 30 min) from 0.42 (0B)
- Wear rate reduced to 6.53 × 10^−6 (1B, 700 °C, 30 min) from 1.94 × 10^−5 (0B) mm³N⁻¹m⁻¹
- Glaze layer protects against internal oxidation and reduces frictional shear stress
**Risks and Limitations**: Unstable tribo-oxide film at 500 °C acts as third-body abrasive; oxide film delamination causes severe wear in 0B alloy; formation requires stable subsurface to avoid oxide film spallation.

## Grain-Boundary / Dislocation Strengthening (Subsurface Integrity)

**Related Elements**: B, C (via carbide modification)
**Typical Alloys**: CoCrW-B4C alloy (1B)
**Key Compositions**: 1 wt.% B4C
**Microstructural Changes**: 1B alloy shows few low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) after 5 min sliding at 700 °C, indicating superior resistance to plastic deformation and fatigue wear; 0B shows numerous LAGBs, dislocation accumulation, and microcracks.
**Property Improvements**: Stable subsurface leads to rapid glaze layer formation (5 min at 700 °C for 1B vs 30 min for 0B); prevents internal oxidation; supports long-term glaze layer stability.
**Risks and Limitations**: 0B suffers from LAGB accumulation, crack nucleation, and oxygen diffusion channels, accelerating wear degradation.

## Oxidation-Resistant Alloying (Co-Rich Glaze Layer + Continuous Cr2O3)

**Related Elements**: Co (for Co-rich oxide debri), Cr (for Cr2O3 protective layer)
**Typical Alloys**: CoCrW alloy (0B and 1B)
**Key Compositions**: Co-rich oxides (Co3O4-like glaze layer); Cr2O3 continuous layer in 1B alloy
**Microstructural Changes**: At 700 °C, Co-rich oxide debris sinter on worn surface to form glaze layer. In 1B, continuous Cr2O3 forms beneath glaze layer at interface, preventing oxygen diffusion. In 0B, transition layer of Cr2O3 + Co matrix forms, but discontinuities and microcracks prevent protective Cr2O3 layer formation.
**Property Improvements**: 1B achieves perfect compatibility between tribological properties and oxidation resistance at 700 °C; COF of 0.25, wear rate of 6.53 × 10^−6 mm³N⁻¹m⁻¹.
**Risks and Limitations**: 0B suffers from microcrack-supported selective oxidation, preventing continuous Cr2O3; 500 °C tribo-oxide film is insufficient for protection.

# Key Conclusions

1. **Heterogeneous carbide growth with B4C**: Addition of 1 wt.% B4C leads to heterogeneous growth of chromium carbide on B4C nucleation sites with B diffusion into Cr7C3, forming Cr7(C,B)3. Carbide shape changes from granular to lath, improving mechanical properties with yield strength of 1740 MPa, compressive strength of 2535 MPa, and ultimate plasticity strain of 32%.

2. **Room-temperature tribological improvement**: The large lath carbides in 1B alloy provide sufficient strength to prevent wear degradation; the worn subsurface is almost free of cracking and deformation. COF is 0.41 and wear rate is 1.31 × 10^−5 mm³N⁻¹m⁻¹, half that of 0B alloy.

3. **High-temperature wear resistance**: At 100–450 °C, 1B maintains better wear resistance than 0B due to excellent deformation resistance of Cr7(C,B)3. The wear rate of 1B is always lower than 0B from room temperature to 700 °C.

4. **Tribo-oxide film transition**: Tribo-oxide film appears preferentially at 450 °C in 1B alloy vs 500+ °C in 0B alloy. Above 500 °C, oxidation wear becomes the dominant mechanism for both alloys.

5. **Glaze layer formation and stability at 700 °C**: 1B alloy achieves dense continuous glaze layer within 5 min; COF reduces to 0.25 and wear rate to 6.53 × 10^−6 mm³N⁻¹m⁻¹ after 30 min. Continuous Cr2O3 forms beneath glaze layer, avoiding internal oxidation.

6. **Subsurface integrity**: 1B alloy avoids fatigue wear due to large lath carbides providing strength and stable subsurface layer, enabling oxide debris to sinter readily and remain stable.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. **Alloy system specificity**: The study is limited to CoCrW alloy (Stellite alloy) with B4C addition. The results cannot be directly extrapolated to other Co-based, Ni-based, or Fe-based superalloys without considering different interaction energetics and phase formation behavior.

2. **Temperature range**: The maximum testing temperature is 700 °C, which is below the operational temperatures of many advanced refractory or high-temperature alloys. The conclusions are applicable to aero-engine bearing sleeves operating within this temperature range.

3. **B4C content**: Only 1 wt.% B4C content was studied. The optimal B4C content for best trade-off between mechanical properties, tribological properties, and processability remains unexplored.

4. **Tribological configuration**: The tribological tests were conducted against Si3N4 counterparts with fixed load (10 N), speed (24 mm/s), and reciprocating motion. The conclusions should not be extrapolated to other configurations, loads, or speeds without additional testing.

5. **Processing method**: The alloys were prepared only by SPS; the microstructure and properties may differ for cast, powder metallurgy, or additively manufactured variants.

6. **Time-dependent service behavior**: The long-term stability of the glaze layer and Cr2O3 layer during extended service beyond 30 min has not been established.

7. **The paper does not address refractor metal systems (Nb-W, Mo-W, etc.)**: The study is specifically on a Co-based alloy, and all extracted knowledge is limited to the CoCrW system. The paper does not contain information on refractory high-entropy alloys, additively manufactured refractory alloys, or other refractory metal systems.
