# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## As-deposited microstructure
- **Matrix phases**: Ti3Al (α2 phase) and TiAl (γ phase). The γ phase is the matrix phase.
- **Volume fractions (as-deposited)**: γ = 87.7%; α2 = 12.3%
- **Macrostructure**: Columnar crystals; larger columnar grains penetrate multiple deposition layers due to epitaxial crystallization and remelting of the lower surface during the upper deposition process.
- **Lamellar cluster size**: ~40 μm
- **Lamellar width**: ~58.6 nm
- **Phase orientation**: α2 and γ follow the Blackburn orientation relationship: α2{0001}//γ{111}
- **Texture (as-deposited)**:
  - α2{0001} pole figure maximum: 109.2
  - γ{111} pole figure maximum: 19.9
- **Elemental distribution**: Al and Cr contents are relatively high in the lamellae, but lower between lamellae. Nb is evenly distributed.

## Annealed microstructures
- **Phase constitution**: Both α2 and γ phases remain; no new phases after annealing.
- **Effect of annealing temperature**:
  - With increasing annealing temperature, lamellar size expands gradually; γ lamellae at edges of lamellar clusters become coarser/ thicker; more α2 lamellae precipitate between lamellae.
  - At 1320 °C/2h/FC, lamellar size in clusters becomes clearly coarser.
  - At 1380 °C/2h/FC, microstructure is fully lamellar; lamellar cluster size increases sharply from ~40 μm to ~370 μm; grain boundaries become zigzagged.
- **Equiaxed grain precipitation**:
  - At 1260 °C/1h/FC secondary annealed: a small amount of equiaxed γ grains are precipitated at grain boundaries between lamellar clusters, which is stated to be advantageous for plastic deformation.
  - At 1320 °C/1h/FC secondary annealed: lamellar thickness increases dramatically; average thickness of each layer = 2.3 μm; angle between lamellar clusters = 90°, stated to be not conducive to plastic deformation under tension.
- **Volume fractions of α2 phase after annealing**:
  - 1200 °C/2h/FC: 5.7% α2 / 94.3% γ
  - 1260 °C/2h/FC: 8.5% α2 / 91.5% γ
  - 1320 °C/2h/FC: 4.9% α2 / 95.1% γ
  - 1380 °C/2h/FC: 11.1% α2 / 88.9% γ
  - 1260 °C/1h/FC + 1260 °C/1h/FC: 4.5% α2 / 95.5% γ
  - 1320 °C/1h/FC + 1320 °C/1h/FC: 12.2% α2 / 87.8% γ
- **Texture strength of annealed alloys**:
  - γ{111} texture strength:
    - 1200 °C/2h/FC: 13.1
    - 1260 °C/2h/FC: 17.2
    - 1320 °C/2h/FC: 22.0
    - 1380 °C/2h/FC: 25.2
    - 1260 °C/1h/FC + 1260 °C/1h/FC: 16.4
    - 1320 °C/1h/FC + 1320 °C/1h/FC: 17.8
  - α2{0001} texture strength:
    - 1200 °C/2h/FC: 65.3
    - 1260 °C/2h/FC: 74.8
    - 1320 °C/2h/FC: 66.9
    - 1380 °C/2h/FC: 98.9
    - 1260 °C/1h/FC + 1260 °C/1h/FC: 67.5
    - 1320 °C/1h/FC + 1320 °C/1h/FC: 127.6
- **Strengthening/grain-boundary considerations discussed**:
  - Hall-Petch behavior: larger grain/lamellar size leads to worse mechanical properties.
  - Grain-boundary equiaxed γ precipitates at secondary annealing are advantageous for plastic deformation.
  - Increased α2 phase fraction is associated with poorer room-temperature plasticity due to fewer slip systems in the hexagonal α2 phase.

Compared to 1260 °C/2h/FC:

- 750 °C: 3.8% vs. 2.8% (increased by 1.36 times)
- 850 °C: 4.8% vs. 3.3% (increased by 1.45 times)

## Room-temperature ductility / yield behavior
- No obvious yield phenomenon before fracture at room temperature; the alloys are relatively inflexible.
- At 750 °C: a certain yield phenomenon appears before fracture; better stability.
- At 850 °C: a clear yield phenomenon before fracture; crystals in the alloy slip more easily. The paper attributes this to the B2 phase transforming into disordered β phase with more slip systems at high temperatures, facilitating dislocation slip.

## Fracture behavior
- Room temperature: cleavage-dominated fracture; larger cleavage planes correspond to worse room-temperature mechanical properties.
- 750 °C: quasi-cleavage fracture mechanism with cleavage surfaces and tearing edges; few dimples. Fracture surface becomes uneven; cleavage plane orientation changes from horizontal to tilted.
- 850 °C: fracture surface returns to relatively flat; grain boundary strength is weaker than grain strength under tension at 850 °C; cleavage surfaces and tearing edges dominate; no dimples; ductile fracture transition temperature has not been reached.

## Hardness, elastic modulus, density
- Not measured or reported for the studied LAM Ti-48Al-2Cr-5Nb alloy in this paper. The introduction mentions general TiAl density (~4 g/cm³), elastic modulus (160–180 GPa), melting point (>1450 °C), and creep strength (~900 °C) as general literature values, but no measured values for the tested alloy are provided.
- Hardness is not reported for this alloy. Reference [11] is mentioned in the introduction: 7.0% Nb γ-TiAl alloy had hardness of HV2000, strength 1390 MPa, and 24.5% plastic deformation, but these are cited literature values for a different SLM alloy, not the studied composition.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## 1. Grain-Boundary / Lamellar-Size Strengthening (Hall-Petch)

- **Related Elements**: Not element-specific; governed by processing and heat treatment.
- **Typical Alloys**: Ti-48Al-2Cr-5Nb high Nb-TiAl alloy (LAM-fabricated)
- **Key Compositions**: Ti-48Al-2Cr-5Nb (nominal at.%)
- **Microstructural Changes**: As annealing temperature increases, lamellar size and lamellar cluster size increase. At 1380 °C/2h/FC, lamellar cluster size increases from ~40 μm to ~370 μm.
- **Property Improvements**: The as-deposited alloy has higher tensile strength than annealed alloys at room temperature and high temperatures because its smaller lamellar size provides greater strength according to the Hall-Petch relation.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Higher annealing temperature leads to coarser microstructure and reduced tensile strength. “According to Hall-Petch’s formula [23], the larger the grain size, the worse the alloy’s mechanical properties.”
- **Original Evidence**: Section 3.4, Hall-Petch discussion.

## 2. Equiaxed Grain Boundary Precipitation (Secondary Annealing)

- **Related Elements**: Ti, Al, Cr, Nb (γ phase formation)
- **Typical Alloys**: Ti-48Al-2Cr-5Nb high Nb-TiAl alloy (LAM-fabricated)
- **Key Compositions**: Ti-48Al-2Cr-5Nb (nominal at.%)
- **Microstructural Changes**: Secondary annealing at 1260 °C/1h/FC + 1260 °C/1h/FC leads to the precipitation of fine equiaxed γ grains at grain boundaries between lamellar clusters, which is not observed after single annealing at 1260 °C/2h/FC.
- **Property Improvements**: This microstructure is stated to be “advantageous for plastic deformation.” Elongation improves from 1.5% to 1.6% at room temperature and 2.8% to 3.8% at 750 °C and 3.3% to 4.8% at 850 °C compared to 1260 °C/2h/FC. Tensile strength at room temperature increases from 363 MPa to 493 MPa.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Secondary annealing at higher temperature (1320 °C/1h/FC + 1320 °C/1h/FC) leads to dramatic lamellar coarsening and poor mechanical properties, so the benefit is specific to the lower temperature secondary annealing schedule.
- **Original Evidence**: Fig. 8(e), Fig. 9(e), Fig. 13.

# Key Conclusions

1. The as-deposited Ti-48Al-2Cr-5Nb thin-walled sample consists mainly of α2/γ lamellae with volume fractions of 87.7% γ and 12.3% α2. Lamellar cluster size is ~40 μm, and lamellar width is ~58.6 nm.

2. Annealing does not change the phase constitution (still α2 + γ), but changes the microstructure significantly:
   - Increasing annealing temperature thickens γ lamellae at the edges of lamellar clusters.
   - More α2 lamellae precipitate between lamellae.
   - The texture strength of the γ(111) orientation gradually increases.

3. As-deposited alloy has higher tensile strength than annealed alloys at room temperature and high temperatures.

4. The best elongation was obtained in the 1260 °C/1h/FC + 1260 °C/1h/FC secondary annealed condition:
   - Room temperature: 1.6%
   - 750 °C: 3.8%
   - 850 °C: 4.8%
   - Compared to 1260 °C/2h/FC, elongation increased by 1.06, 1.36, and 1.45 times, respectively.

5. Ti-48Al-2Cr-5Nb secondary annealed at 1260 °C/1h/FC showed significantly better tensile strength than the single-annealed 1260 °C/2h/FC counterpart:
   - Room-temperature tensile strength increased from 363 MPa to 493 MPa (~1.36 times).

6. Tensile strength generally decreases with increasing annealing temperature, consistent with Hall-Petch behavior.

7. Fracture mode at room temperature and 750 °C is quasi-cleavage; no ductile fracture transition is observed up to 850 °C. The alloy shows stable high-temperature microstructure and better high-temperature tensile properties.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Alloy system scope**: The paper studies Ti-48Al-2Cr-5Nb, a γ-TiAl intermetallic, not a refractory BCC alloy, high-entropy alloy, or ODS alloy. The results are specific to this composition and processing route and should not be directly transferred to Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory multi-principal element systems.
- **No composition screening**: Only one nominal composition is studied; no variation of Nb, Cr, or Al content in the LAM alloy is experimentally tested.
- **No oxidation data**: The paper does not report experimental oxidation resistance for the studied alloy, despite the introduction citing high Nb-TiAl alloys’ oxidation advantages.
- **Mechanical property data**: The original text only explicitly states a few tensile strength values; most values are presented graphically in Fig. 13(d) without a complete tabulation.
- **Hardness, creep, fracture toughness**: Not measured in this paper.
- **Additive manufacturing process variations**: Only one set of LAM process parameters (1.4 kW laser power, 7 mm/s scanning speed, 5.7 g/min powder feed rate) is studied.
- **Element-function knowledge cards**: Since the paper does not study W, Mo, Ta, Zr, Hf, TiC, HfC, ZrC, Y2O3, La2O3, Si, B, or Re in the alloy, no knowledge cards can be generated for those elements from this paper.
- **Cited literature values**: The introduction references hardness of HV2000, 1390 MPa strength, and 24.5% plastic deformation for a 7% Nb SLM γ-TiAl alloy [11]; these are from a different study and are not measured in this paper. They should not be attributed to the studied alloy.
