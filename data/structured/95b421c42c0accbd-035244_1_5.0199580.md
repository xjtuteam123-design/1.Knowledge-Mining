- **Research Objective**: To investigate the microstructural evolution and strengthening mechanisms of Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x alloys in response to varying Mo content, for aero-engine applications

# Alloy System and Research Object

The paper studies a new series of refractory high-entropy alloys denoted as Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x (x = 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30), abbreviated as Mo0, Mo5, Mo10, Mo15, Mo20, Mo25, and Mo30 respectively.

The base composition Nb3TaTi3Zr is a Nb–Ta–Ti–Zr system RHEA capable of forming a single BCC structure. Mo is systematically added to replace the base composition to enhance high-temperature strength and adjust mechanical properties.

**Motivation**: Conventional nickel-based alloys and titanium alloys are only suitable for temperatures below 1000 °C. Refractory HEAs demonstrate remarkable specific strength in the range of 1000–1600 °C. Existing RHEAs often exhibit high density; reducing high-density elements like Ta (and Zr) while adjusting Mo content can effectively control high-temperature yield strength.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

**Matrix phase**: BCC (body-centered cubic)

**Phase evolution with Mo content**:
- Mo content < 25%: single BCC solid solution phase
- Mo content = 30%: BCC matrix + FCC phase (Zr-rich) precipitated between dendrites

**Dendritic solidification**: All seven alloys exhibit distinct dendritic solidification mode with pronounced composition segregation evidenced by deep contrast between dendrites in backscattered electron images (BEIs).

**Elemental segregation**:
- Interdendritic region: enriched in Ti and Zr (lower melting points)
- Dendritic region: enriched in Mo, Nb, and Ta (higher melting points)
- This segregation follows the general law of solidification, where high-melting-point elements solidify first (enriching dendrites) and low-melting-point elements solidify last (enriching interdendrites)
- When Mo content < 25%, the composition difference between dendritic and interdendritic regions remains relatively stable
- When Mo content reaches 30%, a significant and abrupt increase in elemental difference is observed, correlated with FCC (Zr-rich) phase precipitation

**Grain size / Subgrain size**: 
- The subgrain size (D), obtained from XRD curve fitting using Jade software, decreases with increasing Mo content
- At Mo30, the trend reverses due to FCC phase precipitation

**Dislocation density**: Increases with increasing Mo content, but reverses at Mo30 due to FCC precipitation

**Microstrain**: Increases with increasing Mo content, consistent with increased lattice distortion (δ increases from 3.49% at Mo0 to 4.32% at Mo30)

**Lattice constant**: Decreases with increasing Mo content (XRD diffraction peaks shift to higher angles), attributed to the smaller atomic radius of Mo relative to other elements and increasingly negative mixing enthalpy.

**Grain size values**: Not explicitly stated as specific numbers in the original paper; only trends are reported.

**Precipitate details**: FCC phase is Zr-rich, precipitates between dendrites in Mo30 alloy. Precipitate size and distribution are not quantified.

## [Mo]

**Applicable Alloy System**: Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x refractory high-entropy alloys (BCC-based RHEA); also referenced in HfMoNbTaTiZr equiatomic RHEA

**Addition Amount or Composition Range**: x = 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 at.% Mo replacing the base Nb3TaTi3Zr composition

**Main Function**: 
- Significant enhancement of high-temperature strength
- Increase of room-temperature yield strength
- Increase of hardness
- Lattice constant reduction (due to smaller atomic radius)
- Modification of microstructural features (subgrain refinement)

**Microstructural Mechanism**:
- Solid-solution strengthening via lattice distortion: Mo has a smaller atomic radius than other constituent elements, reducing lattice constant and creating local strain fields that impede dislocation motion
- As Mo content increases, dislocation density increases and subgrain size decreases, providing additional pinning and fine-grain strengthening
- Mo participates in dendritic segregation: Mo is enriched in dendrites (higher melting point element)
- Mixing enthalpy (ΔHmix) becomes progressively more negative with Mo addition (from +2.25 kJ/mol at x = 0 to −3.20 kJ/mol at x = 30), indicating reinforced interatomic binding force and reduced atomic bond length
- At x = 30, Mo addition (combined with high total Mo content) promotes FCC phase precipitation between dendrites

**Property Effect**:
- Room-temperature yield strength: 729 MPa (x = 0) → 1202–1400 MPa (x = 25)
- Yield strength at 1200 °C: 62 MPa (x = 0) → 498 MPa (x = 30)
- Microhardness: 247 HV (x = 0) → 471 HV (x = 30)
- Plasticity decreases with Mo addition: alloys with Mo < 15 at.% show compression strain > 30%; Mo25 shows 16%; Mo30 shows only 5%
- Theoretical melting point (Tm) increases from 2437 K (x = 0) to 2575 K (x = 30)

**Potential Side Effects**:
- Significant loss of room-temperature ductility/plasticity as Mo content increases
- At Mo30, severe room-temperature embrittlement (compression strain limit only 5%) due to FCC phase precipitation
- Increased mixing enthalpy negativity may promote intermetallic formation tendency
- Increased electronegativity difference (Δχ from 8.36 to 29.65%) facilitates intermetallic compound formation

**Original Evidence**:
- Abstract: "as the Mo content increases, the strength of the alloys significantly increases while their plasticity diminishes"
- Table I: alloy parameter calculations demonstrating ΔHmix, δ, Δχ trends
- Fig. 5(c): yield strength vs. Mo content
- Fig. 5(d): microhardness vs. Mo content
- Fig. 6: subgrain size, dislocation density, and microstrain trends
- Literature reference 14 (Tseng et al.): equiatomic HfNbTaTiZr vs. HfMoNbTaTiZr comparison showing Mo enhances high-temperature strength

## [Ti + Zr]

**Applicable Alloy System**: Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x RHEAs (and Nb–Ta–Ti–Zr system RHEAs generally)

**Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Part of the base composition Nb3TaTi3Zr (Nb:Ta:Ti:Zr = 3:1:3:1 in atomic ratio)

**Main Function**: 
- Contribute to BCC solid solution formation
- Ti and Zr are lower-melting-point elements that partition to interdendritic regions during solidification
- Zr enrichment is associated with FCC phase formation at high Mo content (Mo30)

**Microstructural Mechanism**:
- During dendritic solidification, Ti and Zr (lower melting points) are the last to solidify, enriching the interdendritic regions
- Ti and Zr have similar atomic radius and electronegativity to other constituents, helping form a single BCC structure in the base Mo0 alloy
- Zr has a substantially larger atomic radius compared to other elements, which contributes to increased segregation when its local concentration rises (Mo30 case)
- The FCC phase precipitated at Mo30 is Zr-rich

**Property Effect**:
- The base Mo0 alloy has a room-temperature yield strength of 729 MPa and microhardness of 247 HV with good plasticity (compression strain > 30%)
- The original paper does not provide specific values for the individual contribution of Ti or Zr.

**Potential Side Effects**:
- Zr segregation (large atomic radius) can promote second-phase precipitation, contributing to embrittlement
- Both Ti and Zr are relatively lower-density refractory elements but their segregation behavior can create microstructural inhomogeneity

**Original Evidence**:
- Fig. 2: element distribution in dendritic and interdendritic regions showing Ti and Zr enrichment in interdendrites
- Section III.A: "The interdendritic region has higher Ti and Zr contents than the dendritic region"
- Fig. 3(b) and related discussion: FCC phase in Mo30 is Zr-rich

## [Nb + Ta]

**Applicable Alloy System**: Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x RHEAs (and Nb–Ta–Ti–Zr system RHEAs generally)

**Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Part of the base composition Nb3TaTi3Zr (Nb = 3, Ta = 1 in the non-equiatomic ratio)

**Main Function**: 
- High-melting-point refractory elements that form the BCC matrix
- Enriched in dendritic regions during solidification
- Provide basis for high-temperature mechanical strength

**Microstructural Mechanism**:
- Nb and Ta have higher melting points, so they solidify first and are enriched in dendritic regions
- These elements help establish the BCC crystal structure of the alloy

**Property Effect**:
- The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for the individual contribution of Nb or Ta.
- The base Nb3TaTi3Zr alloy (Mo0) exhibits a room-temperature yield strength of 729 MPa and a high-temperature yield strength of 62 MPa at 1200 °C.

**Potential Side Effects**:
- Ta is a high-density element; the paper notes that reducing high-density elements such as Ta (and Zr) can alleviate the high-density issue of RHEAs

**Original Evidence**:
- Introduction: "the Nb–Ta–Ti–Zr system RHEAs are capable of forming a single BCC structure with similar atomic radius and electronegativity, as well as low mixing enthalpy, ultimately exhibiting excellent mechanical properties"
- Introduction: "to alleviate this issue, the content of high-density elements, such as Ta and Zr, can be reduced"
- Fig. 2 and Section III.A: Mo, Nb, and Ta enriched in dendrites

## [FCC (Zr-rich) Precipitate Phase]

**Applicable Alloy System**: Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x RHEAs (specifically Mo30)

**Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Forms at Mo content = 30 at.%; Zr-rich FCC phase

**Main Function**: 
- Precipitated phase strengthening
- Enhanced high-temperature yield strength at 1200 °C
- Acts as a barrier to dislocation movement

**Microstructural Mechanism**:
- Precipitates between dendrites in the Mo30 alloy
- Formation driven by: increasingly negative ΔHmix (net bonding force increase and intermetallic formation tendency), increased atomic radius difference (lattice distortion making solid solution formation less favorable), and increased electronegativity difference (promoting intermetallic compound formation)
- At room temperature, the FCC phase significantly impedes dislocation movement, causing stress concentration and subsequent fracture

**Property Effect**:
- Mo30 alloy: yield strength at 1200 °C = 498 MPa (significant increase from Mo0 = 62 MPa)
- Room temperature: Mo30 alloy is exceedingly brittle, with a compression strain limit of only 5%
- At 1200 °C, enhanced slip and dislocation mobility between crystals reduce stress concentration, enabling uniform deformation despite the FCC phase

**Potential Side Effects**:
- Severe room-temperature embrittlement due to dislocation motion blockage by FCC precipitates
- Stress concentration during compression leads to premature fracture

**Original Evidence**:
- Section III.A: "the FCC phase (Zr-rich) precipitated between the dendrites of the Mo30 alloy"
- Section III.B: "This brittleness is attributed to the precipitation of the FCC phase in the alloy at this composition, which significantly impedes dislocation movement, leading to stress concentration and subsequent fracture during compression"
- Section IV.B: "the FCC precipitated phase is the reason for the sudden increase in the high-temperature yield strength of the Mo30 alloy at 1200 °C"

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

---

## 1. Solid-Solution Strengthening

**Related Elements**: Mo (primary focus), with the lattice host composed of Nb, Ta, Ti, Zr

**Typical Alloys**: Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x single BCC phase alloys (x < 25%); applicable to BCC RHEAs generally

**Key Compositions**: Mo content x = 0–25 at.% (single BCC range)

**Microstructural Changes**:
- Lattice constant decreases as Mo content increases (XRD peaks shift to higher 2θ angles), because Mo has a smaller atomic radius
- Atomic radius difference (δ) increases from 3.49% (Mo0) to 4.26% (Mo25), increasing lattice distortion
- Microstrain increases with Mo content (measured from XRD fitting; trend consistent with ΔHmix becoming more negative)
- Mixing enthalpy (ΔHmix) decreases from +2.25 kJ/mol (Mo0) to −2.58 kJ/mol (Mo25), indicating stronger interatomic binding forces

**Property Improvements**:
- Room-temperature yield strength: 729 MPa (Mo0) → 1202–1400 MPa (Mo25)
- Microhardness: 247 HV (Mo0) → increasing values (up to 471 HV at Mo30)
- High-temperature yield strength at 1200 °C: 62 MPa (Mo0) → increases progressively (498 MPa at Mo30, which also involves precipitate strengthening)

**Risks and Limitations**:
- Plasticity decreases as Mo content increases
- Excessive solid-solution strengthening can reduce ductility
- Increased electronegativity difference (Δχ: 8.36% → 28.17%) promotes intermetallic compound formation tendency, which may limit the applicable range of single-phase solid-solution alloys

**Original Evidence Location**: Section IV.B "Strengthening mechanism"; Fig. 4 (XRD peak shifts); Table I (alloy parameter calculations); Fig. 6 (microstrain trend)

---

## 2. Fine-Grain / Subgrain Strengthening

**Related Elements**: Mo (content increase drives subgrain refinement)

**Typical Alloys**: Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x RHEAs (x = 0–30)

**Key Compositions**: Mo content x = 0–30 at.%

**Microstructural Changes**:
- Subgrain size (D) decreases with increasing Mo content (until x = 25; reverses at x = 30 due to FCC phase precipitation)
- Dislocation density increases with increasing Mo content

**Property Improvements**:
- According to the Hall–Petch relationship (σs = σ0 + k/D^1/2), decreasing subgrain size increases yield strength
- Combined with solid-solution strengthening, contributes to the increase in room-temperature yield strength from 729 MPa to 1202–1400 MPa

**Risks and Limitations**:
- Higher dislocation density increases strength but reduces plasticity
- At Mo30, the trend reverses due to FCC precipitation, changing the dominant strengthening mechanism

**Original Evidence Location**: Fig. 6 (subgrain size and dislocation density trends); Section IV.B: "fine-grain strengthening also plays a role" and "According to the Hall–Petch formula: σs = σ0 + k/D^1/2, the yield strength of the alloy increases as the grain size decreases"

---

## 3. Precipitate (Second-Phase) Strengthening

**Related Elements**: Zr-rich FCC phase precipitate (indirectly related to Mo content, as precipitation occurs at Mo30)

**Typical Alloys**: Mo30(Nb3TaTi3Zr)70 RHEA specifically

**Key Compositions**: Mo content x = 30 at.%

**Microstructural Changes**:
- Zr-rich FCC phase precipitates between dendrites
- The large atomic radius of Zr relative to other elements contributes to increased segregation at this composition
- Subgrain size trend reverses (increases) at Mo30 due to FCC precipitation

**Property Improvements**:
- Sudden increase in high-temperature yield strength at 1200 °C: 498 MPa (Mo30) vs. 62 MPa (Mo0)
- At 1200 °C, enhanced slip and dislocation mobility reduce stress concentration, allowing uniform deformation despite the FCC phase

**Risks and Limitations**:
- Severe room-temperature embrittlement: compression strain limit only 5% at Mo30
- FCC precipitates impede dislocation movement, causing stress concentration and premature fracture at room temperature

**Original Evidence Location**: Section III.A (FCC phase identification); Section III.B (brittleness explanation); Section IV.B: "the FCC precipitated phase is the reason for the sudden increase in the high-temperature yield strength of the Mo30 alloy at 1200 °C"

## 4. High-Temperature Softening Resistance (via Melting Point Elevation)

**Related Elements**: Mo

**Typical Alloys**: Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x RHEAs

**Key Compositions**: Mo content x = 0–30 at.%

**Microstructural Changes**:
- Theoretical melting point (Tm) increases from 2437 K (x = 0) to 2575 K (x = 30)

**Property Improvements**:
- At 1200 °C, increased Tm leads to decreased high-temperature softening effect
- Contributes to enhanced high-temperature yield strength (62 → 498 MPa from x = 0 to x = 30)
- Combined with solid-solution and fine-grain strengthening maintaining effectiveness at high temperature

**Risks and Limitations**: 
- No direct risks noted; elevated Tm also implies more challenging processing temperatures for future manufacturing

**Original Evidence Location**: Table I (Tm values); Section IV.B: "At 1200 °C, the alloys exhibit not only solid solution strengthening and fine grain strengthening observed at room temperature but also an increase in the Tm with the Mo content. # Key Conclusions

1. **Microstructure evolution**: All seven Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x alloys exhibit coarse dendrite morphology with composition segregation. Mo, Nb, and Ta (higher melting points) are enriched in dendrites, while Ti and Zr (lower melting points) are enriched in interdendrites.

2. **Phase constitution**: For Mo content < 25 at.%, the alloys form a single BCC structure. At Mo = 30 at.%, a Zr-rich FCC phase precipitates between dendrites, forming a BCC + FCC dual-phase structure.

3. **Lattice and microstructural trends**: With increasing Mo content, lattice constant decreases, subgrain size decreases (until Mo25), dislocation density increases, and microstrain increases. The trends reverse at Mo30 due to FCC phase precipitation.

4. **Mechanical properties**:
   - Room-temperature yield strength: 729 MPa (Mo0) → 1202 MPa (reported in conclusion; the text also mentions 1400 MPa peak for Mo25) as Mo content increases
   - Room-temperature plasticity decreases: Mo < 15% alloys show >30% compression strain; Mo25 shows 16%; Mo30 shows 5%
   - High-temperature yield strength at 1200 °C: 62 MPa (Mo0) → 498 MPa (Mo30)
   - Microhardness: 247 HV (Mo0) → 471 HV (Mo30)

5. **Strengthening mechanisms**: The primary strengthening mechanisms are solid-solution strengthening (via lattice distortion from Mo addition), fine-grain strengthening (via Hall–Petch effect from reduced subgrain size), and precipitate strengthening (via FCC Zr-rich phase at Mo30). At 1200 °C, increased melting point (Tm) from Mo addition also reduces high-temperature softening.

6. **Phase formation driving factors**: The decreasing ΔHmix with Mo content (becoming more negative), increasing atomic radius difference (δ), and increasing electronegativity difference (Δχ) collectively promote FCC phase precipitation at Mo30.

7. **Optimal composition**: The Mo30 alloy exhibits exceptional mechanical properties at 1200 °C, but Mo25 offers a better balance of room-temperature strength and plasticity for moderate-temperature applications.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

**Reported limitations/gaps**:

1. **No tensile testing**: Only compression tests were performed. No data on room-temperature or high-temperature tensile properties are provided.

2. **Limited high-temperature testing**: High-temperature compression was only tested at 1200 °C. No data at 800 °C, 1000 °C, 1400 °C, or 1600 °C are reported.

3. **No oxidation testing**: No oxidation resistance experiments were performed, despite the alloys being designed for aero-engine applications.

4. **No creep or rupture life data**: No creep performance or stress-rupture life tests were reported.

5. **No fracture toughness**: Fracture toughness was not measured or discussed.

6. **As-cast condition only**: All samples were tested in the as-cast state. No post-cast heat treatments (homogenization, annealing, aging) were performed, so the potential to mitigate segregation or optimize precipitate distribution is unexplored.

7. **Internal data inconsistency**: The paper reports Mo25 room-temperature yield strength as both 1202 MPa (abstract and conclusion) and 1400 MPa (Section III.B). This needs clarification.

8. **Subgrain size values not provided**: Only qualitative trends of subgrain size, dislocation density, and microstrain are reported, not actual numerical values.

9. **Applicable scope**: The findings are specifically applicable to Mo–Nb–Ta–Ti–Zr system RHEAs prepared by vacuum arc melting. Extrapolation to other processing routes (e.g., additive manufacturing, powder metallurgy) or other refractory alloy systems should be done with caution.

10. **Density considerations**: Although the paper mentions the high density of typical RHEAs as a concern motivating the reduction of Ta and Zr, the actual density of the studied alloys is not reported.
