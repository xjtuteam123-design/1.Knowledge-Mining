# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Enhancement of mechanical properties and oxidation resistance of TiAl alloy with addition of Nb and Mo alloying elements
- **Authors**: Lihan Feng, Bo Li, Qiang Li, Yimin Gao, Ziyi Pei, Chenyu Liang
- **Journal**: Materials Chemistry and Physics
- **Year**: 2024
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.matchemphys.2024.129148
- **Alloy system studied**: TiAl-based intermetallic alloys with Nb and Mo additions (TiAl-xNb and TiAl-xNb-yMo)
- **Material category**: TiAl intermetallic alloy (γ-TiAl based alloy) with β-stabilizing element additions

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Research objective**: To investigate the effect of Nb and Mo content on the microstructure, mechanical properties, and oxidation resistance of TiAl alloys prepared by vacuum non-self-consuming arc melting.
- **Base alloy**: Ti-50 at.% Al (binary TiAl alloy)
- **Material category**: TiAl-based intermetallic alloy, not a refractory high-entropy alloy or Nb-W-based alloy. However, the paper is relevant to the general domain of high-temperature structural alloys and contains transferable knowledge about β-stabilizing elements, solid-solution strengthening, and oxidation-resistant alloying.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **Melting method**: Vacuum non-self-consuming arc melting (laboratory melting furnace MSM20-7) under argon atmosphere.
- **Ingot form**: Button-shaped ingots, diameter 25 mm.
- **Remelting**: Each composition was remelted at least 4 times to ensure composition homogeneity.
- **Crucible**: Water-cooled copper crucible (leading to rapid cooling rate).
- **Post-processing heat treatment**: Not reported. The alloys were studied in the as-cast condition.
- **Rolling, forging, annealing, aging, HIP, SPS, or additive manufacturing**: Not used in this study.
- **Specimen preparation for testing**: Ground with 400#, 800#, 1000#, and 1500# sandpapers; polished with diamond suspension; etched with 1 vol% HF + 5 vol% HNO₃ + 94 vol% H₂O solution for 5 s.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase**: γ (TiAl) phase and α₂ (Ti₃Al) phase forming a lamellar (α₂ + γ) colony structure.
- **Additional phase**: β phase, which orders to form brittle β₀ phase during cooling and solidification. The β phase content is low and not detected by XRD due to rapid cooling from the water-cooled copper crucible.
- **Microstructure of 1# (Ti-50Al)**: (α₂ + γ) colonies with network-like γ phase.
- **Microstructure of 2#–5# (Nb additions)**: Brighter dotted precipitation phases (β phase) appear in the network (Al-rich γ phase), resulting from β-segregation (S-segregation).
- **Microstructure of 6#–8# (Mo + Nb additions)**: Bright irregular lines of β phase precipitates appear inside the (α₂ + γ) colonies (α-segregation), producing a lamellar structure composed of α₂ + γ + β triple phases.
- **Microsegregation mechanisms discussed**:
  - **β-segregation**: During β→α transformation, Al atoms are discharged into α phase, while Nb and Ti diffuse into β phase, resulting in Al-lean, Nb-rich, Ti-rich β phase at colony boundaries (dotted bright phase).
  - **S-segregation**: Occurs at high Al content (>44 at.%) and low β-stabilizing element content during L→β transition.
  - **α-segregation**: Excess β-stabilizing elements in α phase cause α→α₂ + γ + β transformation, producing lamellar structure with β phase lines within colonies.
- **TEM observations (7# specimen)**: Matrix lamellar structure consists of alternating thick and thin colonies. SAED confirms α₂ phase along [1 1 2] zone axis and γ phase along [20 2] zone axis.
- **Grain size values**: Not explicitly quantified in the original paper. Grain refinement by Nb addition is mentioned as reported in Ref. [16], but no specific grain size values are provided for this study.
- **Precipitate size and distribution**: Not quantitatively reported; qualitatively described as dotted β phase at grain/colony boundaries (Nb-only additions) or line-like β phase within lamellar colonies (Nb+Mo additions).
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed**: Solid-solution strengthening (Nb and Mo), Hall-Petch strengthening (grain refinement by Nb), and the detrimental effect of brittle β₀ phase when Nb or Mo content is excessive.

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

- **Test conditions**: Isothermal oxidation at 800 °C for 100 h in air.
- **Specimen dimensions**: 5 mm × 10 mm × 1 mm.
- **Weight gain measurement**: High-precision (0.1 mg) electronic balance; measurement interval every 10 h.

**Oxidation weight gain after 100 h**:
- 1#: Highest weight gain (specific value not extracted in excerpt)
- 7# (TiAl-5Nb-5Mo): Lowest, 0.291 mg/mm²
- 8# (TiAl-5Nb-7Mo): 0.366 mg/mm²
- Trend: Oxidation weight gain decreases with Nb addition (1#–5#), and further decreases with Mo addition up to 5 wt% Mo (7#), but increases when Mo reaches 7 wt% (8#).

**Oxidation kinetics** (power-law equation (ΔM)ⁿ = kₙt):

| Specimen | n | k (mg² mm⁻⁴ s⁻¹) |
|----------|---|-------------------|
| 1# | 2.32 | 2.37 × 10⁻⁷ |
| 2# | 2.53 | 1.91 × 10⁻⁸ |
| 3# | 2.59 | 9.48 × 10⁻⁹ |
| 4# | 2.61 | 8.53 × 10⁻⁹ |
| 5# | 2.76 | 1.24 × 10⁻⁹ |
| 6# | 2.85 | 3.66 × 10⁻¹⁰ |
| 7# | 3.09 | 3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ |
| 8# | 2.98 | 1.49 × 10⁻¹⁰ |

- All n values > 2, indicating parabolic oxidation kinetics.
- 7# has the lowest oxidation rate constant (3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ mg² mm⁻⁴ s⁻¹).

**Oxide layer structure** (all specimens after 800 °C/100 h oxidation):
- Main phases: TiO₂ and Al₂O₃ (TiO₂ has strongest XRD peak intensity).
- Three-layer structure from top to bottom:
  1. Top layer: TiO₂-rich (Al-poor, Ti-rich)
  2. Second layer: Al₂O₃-rich (Al-rich, Ti-poor)
  3. Third layer: Mixed Al₂O₃ + TiO₂-rich layer (higher TiO₂ at top of mixed layer)
- Oxide film thickness: 7# has the thinnest at 1.832 μm. 8# has slightly thicker and less dense/complete top layer.
- Surface morphology: Nb and Mo additions make surface oxides smaller and denser; 7# has the densest and smoothest oxidized surface.

**Mechanistic explanations from the paper**:
- Nb improves oxidation resistance by: (1) reducing oxygen vacancy concentration when +5 or +6 valence ions are incorporated in the scale, suppressing TiO₂ growth; (2) increasing Al activity and promoting Al diffusion to form continuous Al₂O₃ film.
- Mo improves oxidation resistance by reducing O concentration in the matrix, impeding Ti diffusion, and promoting Al diffusion to induce continuous Al₂O₃ film formation.
- Excess Nb and Mo generate complex oxides containing alloying elements, making the oxide film loose and reducing oxidation resistance (8# behavior).

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Nb (Niobium)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: TiAl-based (γ-TiAl) alloys; not directly applicable to Nb-W-based or Mo-W-based refractory alloys.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 1–7 wt.% Nb in Ti-50Al; combined with 3–7 wt.% Mo in TiAl-5Nb-xMo.
- **Main Function**: β-phase stabilizing element; solid-solution strengthening; grain refinement; improvement of oxidation resistance; improvement of room-temperature compressive plasticity.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Nb dissolves in both α₂ and γ phases, causing solution strengthening and increasing γ phase volume fraction. It shifts the α-transus line to the Al-rich side, narrowing the (α + γ) phase field and changing the solidification path from α-phase solidification to solidification with β-phase precipitation. Nb segregates to β phase during β→α transformation, producing Al-lean, Nb-rich β-segregation. Grain refinement is caused by changing the solidification path.
- **Property Effect**:
  - Compressive strength: 2010 MPa (0 Nb) → 2204 MPa (1 wt% Nb) → 2283 MPa (3 wt% Nb) → 2440 MPa (5 wt% Nb) → 2070 MPa (7 wt% Nb) at room temperature.
  - Fracture strain: 30.9% (0 Nb) → 37.6% (1 Nb) → 37.5% (3 Nb) → 39.6% (5 Nb) → 34.6% (7 Nb).
  - Oxidation weight gain at 800 °C/100 h decreases with Nb addition.
  - Oxidation rate constant decreases from 2.37 × 10⁻⁷ (1#) to 1.24 × 10⁻⁹ mg² mm⁻⁴ s⁻¹ (5#).
- **Potential Side Effects**: Adding too much Nb (7 wt.% in this study) produces brittle β₀ phase, decreasing compressive strength and fracture strain. Excess Nb can generate complex oxides containing alloying elements, making the oxide film loose.
- **Original Evidence**: “Nb solutes both in the α₂ phase and γ phase in TiAl alloys, which causes solution strengthening and increases the volume fractions of γ phase.” “The compressive strength of the alloys increased firstly with the increasing Nb content and decreased when the content of Nb exceeded 7 wt%.” Table 2, Fig. 6, Table 3.

## Mo (Molybdenum)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: TiAl-based (γ-TiAl) alloys with Nb; not directly applicable to Nb-W-based or Mo-W-based refractory alloys.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 3–7 wt.% Mo combined with 5 wt.% Nb (TiAl-5Nb-xMo, x = 3, 5, 7).
- **Main Function**: Stronger β-phase stabilizing element than Nb; solid-solution strengthening; improvement of oxidation resistance.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Mo causes α-segregation when excess β-stabilizing elements are present in the α phase, leading to α→α₂ + γ + β transformation and precipitation of β phase as bright irregular lines within lamellar colonies. Mo has a more positive effect on oxidation resistance based on its effect on ion diffusion and solubility of the matrix. Mo reduces oxygen concentration in the matrix, impedes Ti diffusion, and promotes Al diffusion, inducing continuous Al₂O₃ film formation.
- **Property Effect**:
  - Compressive strength at room temperature: 1710 MPa (3 wt% Mo) → 2250 MPa (5 wt% Mo) → 1870 MPa (7 wt% Mo), all with 5 wt% Nb.
  - Fracture strain: 25.9% (3 Mo) → 37.1% (5 Mo) → 28.9% (7 Mo).
  - Oxidation weight gain at 800 °C/100 h: 7# (5 Mo) has lowest (0.291 mg/mm²); 8# (7 Mo) increases to 0.366 mg/mm².
  - Oxidation rate constant: 7# (5 Mo) has lowest at 3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ mg² mm⁻⁴ s⁻¹.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Adding too much Mo (7 wt.% in this study) produces brittle β₀ phase, reducing strength and plasticity. Excess Mo generates complex oxides containing alloying elements, making the oxide film loose and reducing oxidation resistance.
- **Original Evidence**: “Mo has stronger β-phase stability than Nb.” “Mo has a more positive effect on the oxidation resistance of the alloy based on the effect on the ion diffusion and solubility of the matrix.” “Adding too much Nb and Mo will generate complex oxides containing alloying elements, which will make the oxide film loose and reduce the oxidation resistance of the alloys.” Table 2, Fig. 6, Table 3.

## Nb + Mo Combination

- **Applicable Alloy System**: TiAl-based (γ-TiAl) alloys.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 5 wt.% Nb + 3–7 wt.% Mo (TiAl-xNb-yMo series); optimal at 5 wt.% Nb + 5 wt.% Mo.
- **Main Function**: Combined β-stabilizing effect; synergistic solid-solution strengthening; enhanced oxidation resistance.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Nb alone produces dotted β phase at grain boundaries (β-segregation); Mo addition causes β phase to precipitate as bright irregular lines inside the α₂/γ lamellar structure (α-segregation). The combination changes the solidification path and produces α₂ + γ + β triple-phase lamellar structure.
- **Property Effect**:
  - Optimal combination TiAl-5Nb-5Mo (7#): compressive strength 2250 MPa, fracture strain 37.1%, microhardness (specific value not explicitly extracted but 8# has highest at 381.7 HV), lowest oxidation weight gain 0.291 mg/mm² at 800 °C/100 h, thinnest oxide film 1.832 μm, lowest oxidation rate constant 3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ mg² mm⁻⁴ s⁻¹.
- **Potential Side Effects**: At 7 wt.% Mo + 5 wt.% Nb (8#), brittle β₀ phase forms and complex oxides loosen the oxide film, reducing both mechanical properties and oxidation resistance.
- **Original Evidence**: “The optimal compressive strength of the alloy, i.e. 2250 MPa, was obtained with 5 wt% Nb and 5 wt% Mo addition.” “The TiAl–5Nb–5Mo alloy also showed the best oxidation resistance at 800 °C.” Abstract, Table 2, Fig. 6.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related Elements**: Nb, Mo
- **Typical Alloys**: TiAl-1Nb through TiAl-7Nb, TiAl-5Nb-3Mo through TiAl-5Nb-7Mo
- **Key Compositions**: Nb up to 5 wt.% optimal; Mo up to 5 wt.% optimal (both in combination)
- **Microstructural Changes**: Nb dissolves in α₂ and γ phases, increasing γ phase volume fraction; Mo acts as stronger β-stabilizer, causing α-segregation with β phase lines within lamellar colonies.
- **Property Improvements**: Microhardness increases from 1# to 8# with increasing Nb and Mo content; 8# has highest at 381.7 HV. Compressive strength increases with Nb up to 5 wt.% (2440 MPa) and with Mo up to 5 wt.% in combination (2250 MPa for 7#).
- **Risks and Limitations**: Excessive Nb (7 wt.%) or Mo (7 wt.%) leads to brittle β₀ phase formation, reducing compressive strength (2070 MPa for 5#, 1870 MPa for 8#) and fracture strain. Solid-solution strengthening alone does not explain the degradation at high Nb/Mo contents.

## Brittle β₀ Phase (Detrimental Second-Phase Effect)

- **Related Elements**: Nb, Mo (excessive additions)
- **Typical Alloys**: TiAl-7Nb (5#), TiAl-5Nb-7Mo (8#)
- **Key Compositions**: Nb > 7 wt.%; Mo > 5 wt.% (with 5 wt.% Nb)
- **Microstructural Changes**: Ordered β₀ phase forms from β phase during cooling and solidification; β phase precipitates as dotted particles at colony boundaries (Nb only) or line precipitates within lamellar colonies (Nb + Mo).
- **Property Improvements**: None; detrimental.
- **Risks and Limitations**: β₀ phase is brittle and reduces both compressive strength and fracture (compressive) strain. TiAl-7Nb (5#) drops to 2070 MPa and 34.6% strain; TiAl-5Nb-7Mo (8#) drops to 1870 MPa and 28.9% strain, compared with 2440 MPa/39.6% for TiAl-5Nb (4#) and 2250 MPa/37.1% for TiAl-5Nb-5Mo (7#).
- **Original Evidence**: “Adding too much Nb and Mo could introduce brittle β₀ phase, resulting in the decrease of mechanical properties of the alloys.” Conclusion (3).

## Hall-Petch/Grain Refinement Strengthening

- **Related Elements**: Nb (and to a lesser extent Mo)
- **Typical Alloys**: TiAl-xNb series (2#–5#)
- **Key Compositions**: Nb additions changing the solidification path
- **Microstructural Changes**: Nb changes the solidification path of TiAl alloys, leading to grain refinement (as reported in Ref. [16] and cited in this paper).
- **Property Improvements**: Combined with solid-solution strengthening, contributes to increased compressive strength from 2010 MPa (1#) to 2440 MPa (4#) and increased fracture strain from 30.9% to 39.6%.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Grain refinement benefit is offset when excessive Nb introduces brittle β₀ phase. The original paper cites Ref. [16] for grain refinement by Nb but does not provide quantitative grain size measurements in this study.
- **Original Evidence**: “The main reason is due to the Hall-Petch strengthening and solid solution strengthening of Nb.” Section 3.2. “The grain refinement caused by the addition of Nb was also reported in Ref. [16].”

## Oxidation-Resistant Alloying

- **Related Elements**: Nb, Mo
- **Typical Alloys**: TiAl-xNb and TiAl-xNb-yMo series
- **Key Compositions**: Optimal at 5 wt.% Nb + 5 wt.% Mo (7#); 1# without Nb/Mo has the poorest oxidation resistance.
- **Microstructural Changes**: Formation of continuous Al₂O₃ protective oxide layer; denser and smoother oxidized surfaces; thinner oxide films (1.832 μm for 7# vs. thicker for 1#).
- **Property Improvements**: Oxidation weight gain at 800 °C/100 h decreases from highest (1#) to lowest 0.291 mg/mm² (7#); oxidation rate constant decreases from 2.37 × 10⁻⁷ (1#) to 3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ mg² mm⁻⁴ s⁻¹ (7#).
- **Risks and Limitations**: Excessive Nb+Mo (8#) generates complex oxides containing alloying elements, making the oxide film loose, incomplete, and less protective; oxidation weight gain increases to 0.366 mg/mm² and oxide film is less dense.
- **Original Evidence**: “Nb is beneficial to improve the oxidation resistance of TiAl alloys”; “Mo can reduce the concentration of O in the matrix, impede the diffusion of Ti, and promote the diffusion of Al to induce the generation of continuous Al₂O₃ oxide film.” Section 3.3.

# Key Conclusions

1. The as-cast TiAl alloys exhibit typical lamellar microstructure consisting of γ and α₂ phases with a small amount of ordered β₀ phase. Nb and Mo additions as β-stabilizing elements change the solidification path and introduce β phase in the form of dotted precipitates at colony boundaries (Nb only) or line precipitates within lamellar colonies (Nb + Mo).

2. Microhardness increases monotonically with increasing Nb and Mo content; TiAl-5Nb-7Mo (8#) has the highest value of 381.7 HV, attributed to solid-solution strengthening.

3. Compressive strength and fracture strain improve with Nb addition up to 5 wt.% (2440 MPa, 39.6% strain) but decrease when Nb reaches 7 wt.% (2070 MPa, 34.6%) due to brittle β₀ phase. With Mo addition, TiAl-5Nb-5Mo (7#) achieves 2250 MPa and 37.1% strain; further Mo addition to 7 wt.% (8#) degrades properties (1870 MPa, 28.9%).

4. Oxidation resistance is enhanced by both Nb and Mo; TiAl-5Nb-5Mo (7#) has the lowest oxidation weight gain (0.291 mg/mm² at 800 °C/100 h), lowest oxidation rate constant (3.04 × 10⁻¹¹ mg² mm⁻⁴ s⁻¹), and thinnest oxide layer (1.832 μm). Excessive alloying additions (8#) generate complex oxides that loosen the oxide film and reduce oxidation resistance.

5. Oxide layer structure consists of TiO₂-rich top layer, Al₂O₃-rich intermediate layer, and mixed Al₂O₃+TiO₂ bottom layer; this structure is not changed by Nb/Mo addition.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Alloy system limitation**: The paper focuses exclusively on TiAl-based intermetallic alloys, not on Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based, or refractory high-entropy alloys. The element-function knowledge cards for Nb and Mo are specific to γ-TiAl systems and may not be directly transferable to refractory BCC alloys or multi-principal element refractory alloys without independent validation.
- **Testing temperature**: Mechanical properties were tested only at room temperature; no high-temperature (e.g., 800–1600 °C) strength data were provided. Oxidation resistance was evaluated only at 800 °C.
- **Mechanical property scope**: Only compressive properties and microhardness were measured. Tensile properties, fracture toughness, creep, fatigue, and high-temperature strength were not investigated.
- **Microstructure quantification**: Grain size, precipitate size, precipitate volume fraction, and β₀ phase volume fraction were not quantitatively measured. The presence of β phase was inferred qualitatively from SEM and TEM, and was not detected by XRD due to low volume fraction.
- **Cooling rate effect**: The paper acknowledges that vacuum arc melting with a water-cooled copper crucible produces rapid cooling, resulting in low β phase content. The observed microstructures may differ from those in conventionally cast or additively manufactured TiAl alloys with slower cooling rates.
- **Oxidation mechanism**: The proposed oxidation mechanisms (ion diffusion effects, Al activity changes) are based on cited literature rather than direct experimental evidence in this study. Direct confirmation via oxygen tracer diffusion or atom probe tomography was not performed.
- **Composition range**: The study covers a narrow composition range (1–7 wt.% Nb, 3–7 wt.% Mo). The identified performance degradation beyond ~5 wt.% Nb and ~5 wt.% Mo should not be extrapolated to other TiAl compositions or other alloy systems without further study.
- **Applicability to refractory alloys**: The knowledge about β-stabilizing elements, β₀ brittle phase formation, and oxidation resistance mechanisms is specific to TiAl intermetallics. It should not be assumed to apply to Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based, or refractory high-entropy alloys, which have fundamentally different crystal structures (BCC vs. ordered L1₀ γ phase) and oxidation behaviors.
