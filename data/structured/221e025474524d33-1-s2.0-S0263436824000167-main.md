# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title**: A Zr-45Ti-54Al-3V alloy with excellent elevated temperature mechanical properties: Millimeter-scale coarse grains with initial subgrains
- **Authors**: Hao Xue, Junting Luo, Chunxiang Zhang
- **Journal**: International Journal of Refractory Metals and Hard Materials
- **Year**: 2024
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.ijrmhm.2024.106568
- **Alloy system studied**: Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V (wt.%) titanium-zirconium alloy, also referred to as 47Zr alloy
- **Research objective**: To improve the elevated temperature (500 °C) mechanical properties of a Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V alloy by preparing millimeter-scale coarse grains with initial subgrains via electric field-assisted high-temperature compression (EFHC)
- **Material category**: Zr-Ti-Al-V alloy (titanium-zirconium alloy). Not a Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloy. This is a β/α titanium alloy with high Zr content.

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy nominal composition**: Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V in weight percent (wt.%), designated as 47Zr
- **Raw materials**:
  - Sponge Zr (Zr + Hf ≥ 99.5 wt.%)
  - Sponge Ti (99.7 wt.%)
  - Industrially pure Al (99.5 wt.%)
  - V (99.9 wt.%)
- **Phase constitution**: α phase (hexagonal close-packed, HCP) and β phase (body-centered cubic, BCC). The alloy is a two-phase α+β titanium alloy.
- **Alloy category note**: This alloy is based on Ti-Zr with Al and V additions. It is not a refractory metal alloy of the Nb-W/Mo-W systems, nor a refractory high-entropy alloy. It is included as a comparison/reference case for high-temperature structural titanium alloys.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Original alloy fabrication (as-forged 47Zr)
1. **Melting**: Vacuum consumable electro-arc furnace, melted three times to ensure uniform chemical composition.
2. **First forging/breakdown**: Held at 1050 °C (above β transus temperature) for 1.5 h, then multiple breakdowns to completely break coarse grains.
3. **Second holding**: Held at 930 °C for 1.5 h.
4. **Final hot forging**: Performed, then lathed into bars 43 mm in diameter.

## EFHC treatment
- **Equipment**: Spark plasma sintering (SPS) machine (SPS-3.20 MK-IV; Sumitomo Coal Mining Co., Ltd.)
- **Specimen dimensions**: Cylindrical, 22 mm diameter, 32 mm height.
- **Atmosphere**: Vacuum, below 10⁻³ Pa.
- **Heating procedure**:
  - Room temperature to 571 °C: manual pulse current control, total 10 min.
  - 571 °C to 600 °C: manual control, 2 min.
  - Above 600 °C: 50 °C/min heating rate.
  - Slowed near target temperature to prevent overshoot.
- **Holding conditions**: Target temperature (1200 °C or 1400 °C) under uniaxial pressure of 10 MPa for 10 min.
- **Two treatment conditions**: 1200 °C-EFHC and 1400 °C-EFHC.

## Pre-hot-deformation characterization
- 1400 °C-EFHC sample was heated to 500 °C and held for 5 min, then water quenched, to obtain microstructure before hot deformation.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## As-forged 47Zr
- **Grain morphology**: Large grains of 300–400 μm, with a few small grains of ~100 μm.
- **Microstructure type**: Widmanstätten microstructure, composed of acicular α phase and β phase.
- **Average width of acicular α phase**: 53 nm.
- **Phase volume fractions**: 63% β-phase, 37% α-phase (calculated from XRD).
- **Orientation relationship**: α and β phases follow the Burgers OR: (101)β//(0001)α, [11̄1]β//[2̄11̄0]α.
- **α variants**: Two distinct α variants observed; attributed to double diffraction between matrix and [110]β//[0001]α, (11̄2)β//(101̄0)α, and [1̄10]β//[0001]α, (1̄1̄2)β//(101̄0)α habit planes.
- **Elemental segregation**: No obvious composition difference between α and β phases.

## EFHC-treated samples
- **Phase constitution**: Only α and β phases present; EFHC treatment does not change phase types. At 1400 °C, a new α″ phase is observed by TEM (low content, not detected by XRD).
- **XRD peak shift**: Diffraction peaks of both α and β phases shifted to lower 2θ angles compared to standard patterns (JCPDS 044-1294 and 044-1288) due to lattice constant increase from Zr addition (Zr atomic radius 0.16 nm vs Ti 0.145 nm).
- **α phase volume fractions**:
  - As-forged: 37% α
  - 1200 °C-EFHC: increased α content (not explicitly stated numerically in text; shows increase)
  - 1400 °C-EFHC: ~90% α
- **Grain size after EFHC**:
  - 1200 °C-EFHC: average grain size 1.3 mm
  - 1400 °C-EFHC: average grain size 1.2 mm
  - Both are millimeter-scale coarse grains.
- **Subgrains**: Present inside grains after EFHC; number increases at higher treatment temperature (1400 °C).
- **α-phase morphology**:
  - 1200 °C-EFHC: lamellar α phase, width ~127 nm, precipitated mainly along 〈211̄0〉 direction, 60° angle between same crystal-orientation families.
  - 1400 °C-EFHC: α phase broadened to 243 nm; α″ phase observed with (101)β//(020)α″, [001]β//[200]α″ orientation relationship with β matrix.
- **Dislocation walls and recrystallization cores**: Observed in 1400 °C-EFHC sample, indicating recrystallization during EFHC.
- **Elemental distribution**: Area and point EDS scans show uniform distribution of solute atoms; no obvious composition difference between α and β phases.
- **Widmanstätten microstructure**: Retained after EFHC.

## Hot-deformed (500 °C) microstructure
- **Grain refinement**: Grain size reduced compared to pre-deformation due to recrystallization refinement.
- **Microstructure**: Still Widmanstätten morphology; phase morphology unchanged during hot compression.
- **Continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX)**: Dominant mechanism during 500 °C deformation.
- **Subgrain rotation**: Confirmed by EBSD pole figures; LAGBs (e.g., 11.77° and 12.28° misorientations) separating subgrains, with progressive orientation accumulation.
- **Misorientation evolution**: Accumulated misorientation (point to origin) increases near-monotonously with slight local misorientation changes, indicating progressive grain rotation.
- **HAGB/LAGB criteria**: LAGBs 2–15°, HAGBs >15°, Brandon criterion.

# Mechanical Properties

## Testing conditions
- **Temperature**: 500 °C
- **Strain rate**: 0.6 mm/min
- **Equipment**: Inspekt Table-100KN universal testing machine
- **Specimens per group**: Three specimens for reliability

## Tensile properties at 500 °C
| Condition | Yield strength (MPa) | Tensile strength (MPa) | Elongation at breaking point (%) |
|-----------|---------------------|----------------------|--------------------------------|
| As-forged 47Zr | 298 | 536 | 24.4 |
| 1200 °C-EFHC | ~406 (36.2% increase vs as-forged) | ~621 (15.8% increase vs as-forged) | 25.2 |
| 1400 °C-EFHC | 425 | 671 | 29.7 |

The paper states:
- After 1200 °C-EFHC: elongation 25.2%, tensile strength +15.8%, yield strength +36.2%.
- After 1400 °C-EFHC: yield strength 425 MPa, tensile strength 671 MPa, elongation 29.7%.
- Compared with as-forged (536 MPa tensile strength, 24.4% elongation): 1400 °C-EFHC increases tensile strength by ~25.2% and elongation by ~21.7%.

## Compressive properties at 500 °C
- **1200 °C-EFHC**: compressive strength ~1400 MPa
- **1400 °C-EFHC**: compressive strength ~1400 MPa
- Both significantly higher than as-forged sample.
- Both EFHC samples show good elevated temperature compression deformation ability.

## Fracture morphology
- **As-forged**: Cleavage steps and tearing ridges; typical brittle fracture.
- **1200 °C-EFHC**: River-like fracture pattern with small, shallow dimples; increased toughness.
- **1400 °C-EFHC**: Cleavage steps with microvoids on sides; microvoid aggregation fracture characteristics; larger dimple diameters than 1200 °C-EFHC; deeper gullies and micropores; highest plasticity.
- All conditions show steps, river-like patterns, and ravines along contraction direction → brittle quasi-cleavage mode overall.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## [Zr]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V (wt.%) alloy, also relevant to Ti-Zr and Zr-based structural alloys.

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
Nominal ~47 wt.% Zr (balance); measured α-phase Zr content 43.82–48.22 wt.%, β-phase 42.04–49.07 wt.%; area scans 45.57–47.33 wt.% after EFHC.

**[Main Function]**
Enhances strength and lattice constant of Ti-based alloy; participates in α phase stabilization; increases lattice resistance.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
Zr has a larger atomic radius (0.16 nm) than Ti (0.145 nm), leading to an increase in Ti crystal lattice constant and XRD peak shifts to lower 2θ angles. Zr addition promotes α phase formation at high temperature. Zr partitions approximately equally between α and β phases with no strong segregation.

**[Property Effect]**
- 500 °C yield strength: 298 MPa (as-forged), 425 MPa (1400 °C-EFHC)
- 500 °C tensile strength: 536 MPa (as-forged), 671 MPa (1400 °C-EFHC)
- 500 °C elongation: 24.4% (as-forged), 29.7% (1400 °C-EFHC)
- The paper does not isolate the incremental effect of Zr alone; it contributes to overall alloy strength and phase constitution.

**[Potential Side Effects]**
- Higher Zr content increases lattice constant and may alter phase stability.
- Zr addition shifts XRD peaks but does not create new phases.
- No specific embrittlement or workability issues with Zr are discussed in this paper.

**[Original Evidence]**
"Zr (with an atomic radius of 0.16 nm) to Ti (with an atomic radius of 0.145 nm) leads to an increase in the lattice constant of the Ti crystals. According to Bragg's law, the crystal plane spacing between the atoms is proportional to the lattice constant, which will cause the overall XRD diffraction peak to shift towards a smaller angle." (Section 3.2)

## [Al]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V (wt.%) alloy; α phase stabilizer in titanium alloys.

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
Nominal 5 wt.% Al. Measured: α-phase 2.25–5.85 wt.%, β-phase 4.19–6.35 wt.% depending on location and treatment.

**[Main Function]**
Alpha phase stabilizer; contributes to solid-solution strengthening of the α phase.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
Al is an α-stabilizing element in Ti alloys. It partitions into the α phase and affects the volume fraction of α phase. The paper reports the α phase volume fraction increases with EFHC temperature (from 37% as-forged to ~90% at 1400 °C-EFHC).

**[Property Effect]**
The increased α phase content contributes to higher lattice resistance (HCP α has fewer slip systems than BCC β) and therefore higher strength. Specific Al contribution values are not isolated: "The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for the isolated effect of Al."

**[Potential Side Effects]**
Not discussed in this paper.

**[Original Evidence]**
"the as-forged sample contains 63% β-phase and 37% α-phase, and the α-phase content is seen to increase with the increase in the EFHC temperature. After the EFHC treatment at 1400 °C, the 47Zr alloy contains about 90% α phase." (Section 3.2)

## [V]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V (wt.%) alloy; β phase stabilizer in titanium alloys.

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
Nominal 3 wt.% V. Measured: α-phase 2.32–4.81 wt.%, β-phase 3.24–5.31 wt.%.

**[Main Function]**
Beta phase stabilizer; maintains β phase at lower temperatures.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
V is a β-stabilizing element in Ti alloys. It partitions into β phase and helps stabilize the BCC β phase. The paper observes Widmanstätten microstructure with both α and β phases.

**[Property Effect]**
Contributes to two-phase α+β microstructure. Specific V contribution to strength not isolated. "The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for the isolated effect of V."

**[Potential Side Effects]**
Not discussed.

**[Original Evidence]**
EDS analyses show V content in both α and β phases with slight enrichment in β (e.g., α: 2.44–4.18 wt.%, β: 4.26–4.50 wt.% in as-forged sample; Tables 1, 3).

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-solution strengthening

**[Related Elements]**
Zr, Al, V, Ti (matrix elements)

**[Typical Alloys]**
Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V (47Zr) alloy

**[Key Compositions]**
Zr ~47 wt.%, Ti 45 wt.%, Al 5 wt.%, V 3 wt.%

**[Microstructural Changes]**
Zr substitution (larger atomic radius 0.16 nm vs Ti 0.145 nm) increases lattice constant of Ti crystals; uniform solute distribution after EFHC treatment confirmed by EDS.

**[Property Improvements]**
Contributes to overall strength: 500 °C tensile strength 536–671 MPa, yield strength 298–425 MPa depending on treatment.

**[Risks and Limitations]**
The paper does not quantify the isolated contribution of solid-solution strengthening. Uniform solute distribution achieved by EFHC is emphasized but not correlated to a specific strength increment.

**[Original Evidence]**
"the addition of Zr (with an atomic radius of 0.16 nm) to Ti (with an atomic radius of 0.145 nm) leads to an increase in the lattice constant of the Ti crystals." (Section 3.2); "solute atoms are evenly distributed in the whole sample" (Section 3.2).

## Phase fraction strengthening (α/β phase ratio)

**[Related Elements]**
Al (α stabilizer), V (β stabilizer)

**[Typical Alloys]**
Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V (47Zr) alloy

**[Key Compositions]**
As-forged: 37% α, 63% β; 1400 °C-EFHC: ~90% α

**[Microstructural Changes]**
α phase volume fraction increases with EFHC temperature. HCP α phase has fewer slip systems than BCC β phase, providing higher lattice resistance to dislocation slip.

**[Property Improvements]**
Higher α phase content in EFHC samples → higher elevated temperature strength compared to as-forged. 500 °C tensile strength: 536 MPa (as-forged, 37% α) → 671 MPa (1400 °C-EFHC, ~90% α).

**[Risks and Limitations]**
Higher α phase content may reduce ductility in some cases; however, this paper reports simultaneous improvement in strength and elongation due to combined effects of α content and subgrain structure.

**[Original Evidence]**
"there are more α phases in the EFHC sample than in the as-forged sample. In terms of crystal structure, hexagonal α-titanium has better strength because it has fewer slip systems than body-centered cubic β-titanium. Therefore, more α phase represents higher lattice resistance." (Section 4)

## Interface resistance strengthening (α/β phase boundaries + LAGBs)

**[Related Elements]**
All alloying elements; phase boundary types

**[Typical Alloys]**
Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V (47Zr) alloy

**[Key Compositions]**
As-forged: 37% α; EFHC samples: higher α content and more LAGBs.

**[Microstructural Changes]**
Increase in α phase content increases α/β phase interface area. EFHC treatment introduces more LAGBs (subgrain boundaries). LAGBs provide interface resistance to dislocation slip.

**[Property Improvements]**
Higher interface resistance contributes to higher elevated temperature strength in EFHC samples. 500 °C yield strength: 298 MPa (as-forged) → 425 MPa (1400 °C-EFHC).

**[Risks and Limitations]**
The contribution of interface resistance is entangled with phase fraction and LAGB density effects; no isolated quantification provided.

**[Original Evidence]**
"The increase of α phase content also represents the increase of α/β phase interface, which provides more interface resistance for dislocation slip. In addition, the EFHC sample has more LAGB than the as-forged sample, which also provides a rich interface resistance." (Section 4)

## Subgrain-assisted dynamic recrystallization (plasticity enhancement)

**[Related Elements]**
Not element-specific; microstructural feature

**[Typical Alloys]**
Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V (47Zr) alloy after EFHC

**[Key Compositions]**
Initial subgrains introduced by EFHC treatment at 1200 °C and 1400 °C.

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Subgrains present inside millimeter-scale coarse grains.
- During hot deformation at 500 °C:
  - High-density dislocations generated in initial subgrains.
  - New subgrains form within initial subgrains via dislocation rearrangement (polygonization).
  - Continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX) with subgrain rotation.
  - Dislocation walls and recrystallization cores observed in 1400 °C-EFHC.
  - Grain refinement occurs during hot deformation.

**[Property Improvements]**
- Elongation at 500 °C: 24.4% (as-forged) → 29.7% (1400 °C-EFHC).
- Fracture mode changes from brittle cleavage (as-forged) to quasi-cleavage with microvoid aggregation and dimples (EFHC), indicating improved toughness.
- Good elevated temperature compression deformation ability.

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- Coarse grains are generally considered undesirable for strength, but here they enhance plasticity through dynamic recovery and recrystallization.
- The mechanism relies on elevated temperature deformation (500 °C) to activate dynamic recrystallization.
- Subgrain rotation/misorientation accumulation is temperature-sensitive.

**[Original Evidence]**
"The dynamic recovery and continuous dynamic recrystallization during hot deformation are the main reasons of elevated temperature plasticity improvement of EFHC sample." (Section 5); "The deformation and remodeling process of the above substructures is usually referred to as polygonization." (Section 4); "when the alloy deforms at 500 °C, continuous dynamic recrystallization plays a dominant role in the deformation behavior." (Section 4)

# Key Conclusions

1. **EFHC process for grain growth and uniformity**: Electric field-assisted high-temperature compression (EFHC) at 1200 °C or 1400 °C under 10 MPa for 10 min promotes rapid grain growth to millimeter scale (1.2–1.3 mm) through enhanced atomic diffusion by pulsed current. This also results in uniform solute element distribution.

2. **Phase evolution**: α phase volume fraction increases from 37% (as-forged) to ~90% after 1400 °C-EFHC. At 1400 °C, a new α″ phase forms with (101)β//(020)α″, [001]β//[200]α″ orientation relationship.

3. **Subgrain introduction**: EFHC treatment introduces subgrains within coarse grains, with subgrain number increasing at higher treatment temperature (1400 °C).

4. **Mechanical property improvements**:
   - 1400 °C-EFHC provides the best combination: 500 °C yield strength 425 MPa, tensile strength 671 MPa, elongation 29.7%.
   - Tensile strength and elongation after 1400 °C-EFHC increased by ~25.2% and ~21.7% compared with as-forged 47Zr.
   - Both EFHC temperatures give compressive strength ~1400 MPa at 500 °C with good compression deformation ability.

5. **Plasticity mechanism**: Dynamic recovery and continuous dynamic recrystallization during hot deformation (500 °C) are the main reasons for elevated temperature plasticity improvement. Polygonization and subgrain rotation occur via dislocation climb and interface migration.

6. **Strength mechanism**: Elevated temperature strength of EFHC samples is higher than as-forged because EFHC samples have more α phase (higher lattice resistance) and more LAGBs (higher interface resistance).

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Alloy system**: The paper focuses on a Zr-45Ti-5Al-3V (wt.%) titanium-zirconium alloy (47Zr). This is not a Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloy. The conclusions are specific to two-phase α+β Ti-Zr alloys and may not transfer directly to refractory metal systems (Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory HEAs).
- **Temperature range**: Mechanical properties tested only at 500 °C. No data above 500 °C; no room-temperature mechanical data reported for the EFHC-treated samples in this paper (though reference is made to literature showing conventional Zr alloys <800 MPa strength).
- **Oxidation resistance**: Not investigated. No oxidation tests, oxidation kinetics, or oxidation layer characterization reported.
- **Impurity analysis**: No C, O, N, H levels reported.
- **Quantitative element-function isolation**: The paper does not isolate the individual contributions of Zr, Al, or V to strength. The strengthening discussion attributes improvements collectively to higher α phase content, more LAGBs, and interface resistance.
- **Long-term service stability**: Creep, fatigue, and rupture life not investigated.
- **Applicability to refractory alloys**: The paper serves as a reference for how electric-field processing (SPS-based EFHC) can be used to engineer coarse-grain + subgrain microstructures to simultaneously improve strength and plasticity at elevated temperature. The principle of subgrain-assisted CDRX may be relevant to refractory alloys but requires experimental validation in refractory systems.
- **Texture effects**: Texture may be generated during hot deformation, which could affect quantitative phase analysis. The paper used multiple XRD peaks to mitigate texture effects but acknowledged this as a potential issue.
