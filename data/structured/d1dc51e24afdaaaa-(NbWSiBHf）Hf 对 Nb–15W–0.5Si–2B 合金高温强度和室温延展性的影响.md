# Paper Metadata

- **Title:** Effect of Hf on high-temperature strength and room-temperature ductility of Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B alloy
- **Authors:** P. Zheng, J.B. Sha, D.M. Liu, S.K. Gong, H.B. Xu
- **Journal:** Materials Science and Engineering A
- **Year:** 2008
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.msea.2006.09.135
- **Alloy system studied:** Nb–W–Si–B–Hf alloys (Nb-based refractory alloy)
- **Material category:** Nb-based refractory alloy with Nb solid-solution (NbSS) dominant microstructure and minor Nb–B boride phase; not an RHEA, not a silicide-matrix composite
- **Research objective:** To investigate the balance between room-temperature ductility and high-temperature strength as a function of Hf concentration in Nb-based solid solution, and to determine whether a NbSS-dominant microstructure (free of Nb5Si3) can achieve sufficient high-temperature strength without sacrificing room-temperature ductility.

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Base alloy:** Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B (mol%)
- **Hf additions:** 0, 5, 10, 15 mol%
- **Sample designations in paper:** 0Hf, 5Hf, 10Hf, 15Hf (corresponding to Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B, Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B–5Hf, Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B–10Hf, Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B–15Hf)
- **Alloy design philosophy:** Si content reduced to 0.5 mol% to avoid formation of the brittle Nb5Si3 phase; W maintained at 15 mol% due to its effectiveness for high-temperature strength; B added at 2 mol% for solid-solution hardening; Hf added for solid-solution strengthening and potential ductilization of the NbSS.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase:** Nb solid solution (NbSS), predominant/overwhelming phase. BCC structure.
- **Second phases:** Nb–B boride, present as a minority grey phase with volume fraction less than 5%. No Nb5Si3 silicide phase observed; no Hf-based solid solution observed.
- **Hf behavior:** Hf, W, and Si solidified entirely in the NbSS.
- **Microstructure independence of Hf:** SEM images of 0Hf and 15Hf alloys show no visible difference; morphology and volume fraction of boride are independent of Hf content.
- **Lattice parameter:** Increases from 0.3327 nm for Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B (0Hf) to 0.3407 nm for 15Hf alloy — a 2.4% increment.
- **Grain size:** Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Grain boundary features:** Not explicitly discussed.
- **Elemental segregation:** The paper notes that Hf, W, and Si solidified entirely in the NbSS; boride constitutes the minority phase. Beyond this, no specific segregation data are provided.
- **Precipitate size and distribution:** Not explicitly quantified; boride volume fraction <5%.
- **Discussion of strengthening mechanisms in paper:** Solid-solution hardening is the primary mechanism discussed. The paper does not discuss precipitation strengthening, dispersion strengthening, ODS, or grain-boundary strengthening in detail for this alloy.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## W (Tungsten)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Nb–W–Si–B–Hf alloys (Nb-based refractory alloys with NbSS-dominant microstructure)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 15 mol% (fixed in all alloys studied)
- **Main Function:** High-temperature strength enhancement of NbSS via solid-solution hardening.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Solid-solution hardening; W is described as "most efficient for improving the high-temperature strength of the NbSS phase" (citing Refs [6,7]). Structural difference between bcc Nb and bcc W leads to solid-solution hardening. W solidifies entirely in the NbSS.
- **Property Effect:** With fixed 15 mol% W, base alloy Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B achieves σ₀.₂ = 295 MPa at 1200 °C and 180 MPa at 1400 °C; combined with Hf, 15Hf alloy reaches σ₀.₂ = 448 MPa at 1200 °C and 365 MPa at 1400 °C.
- **Potential Side Effects:** The paper notes that W increases high-temperature strength, but not explicitly linked to loss of ductility in this paper. However, the paper implicitly notes the classic trade-off: high W contents with Nb5Si3 formation lead to low room-temperature ductility in silicide-matrix alloys.
- **Original Evidence:** "15 mol% W was maintained because W was most efficient for improving the high-temperature strength of the NbSS phase [6,7]." Section 3.1: "Hf, W and Si solidified entirely in the NbSS."

## Si (Silicon)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Nb–W–Si–B–Hf alloys (Nb-based refractory alloys)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 0.5 mol% (fixed in all alloys studied)
- **Main Function:** Solid-solution hardening; Si content was deliberately kept low to avoid formation of the brittle Nb5Si3 phase.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Solid-solution hardening of NbSS; Si solidifies entirely in the NbSS (at 0.5 mol%). At higher Si contents (e.g., 18 mol% in the earlier Nb–10Mo–10Ti–15W–18Si composite), Nb5Si3 silicide forms.
- **Property Effect:** At 0.5 mol%, no Nb5Si3 observed, enabling high room-temperature compressive ductility (11.5–17%). The paper does not isolate Si's specific contribution to strength in these alloys.
- **Potential Side Effects:** Higher Si content leads to Nb5Si3 formation, which degrades room-temperature ductility to as low as ~1% in silicide-matrix alloys.
- **Original Evidence:** "Si content was reduced to 0.5 mol% to avoid formation of the Nb5Si3." Section 3.1: "No Nb5Si3 and Hf-based solid solution were observed."

## B (Boron)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Nb–W–Si–B–Hf alloys (Nb-based refractory alloys)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 2 mol% (fixed in all alloys studied)
- **Main Function:** Solid-solution hardening of NbSS; also forms Nb–B boride as minority phase.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** B is added for solid-solution hardening. A small fraction of Nb–B boride forms (volume fraction <5%) as a second phase. The paper notes that the boride's effect on bulk properties is the same for all alloys since its morphology and volume fraction are independent of Hf content.
- **Property Effect:** The base alloy with 2 mol% B (0Hf) has σ₀.₂ = 775 MPa at RT and 180 MPa at 1400 °C. The specific contribution of B to strength is not isolated in the paper.
- **Potential Side Effects:** Boride formation consumes a small fraction of the microstructure; the paper suggests that the boride does not significantly affect the Hf-dependent property trends. At less than 5 vol%, the boride is not considered detrimental to ductility in these alloys.
- **Original Evidence:** "Two percent B was added also for the solid-solution hardening." Section 3.1: "the minority grey phase is the Nb–B boride with a volume fraction less than 5%."

## Hf (Hafnium)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Nb–W–Si–B–Hf alloys (Nb-based refractory alloys with NbSS-dominant microstructure)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 0, 5, 10, 15 mol% studied; optimal properties at 15 mol%
- **Main Function:** Solid-solution strengthening of NbSS at both room and high temperatures, without degrading room-temperature ductility; potential ductilizing/toughening element for NbSS.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Hf has a large atomic size misfit of approximately 9% relative to Nb and a crystal structural difference (hcp Hf vs. bcc Nb). Both factors contribute to solid-solution hardening. Hf increases the lattice parameter of NbSS from 0.3327 nm (0Hf) to 0.3407 nm (15Hf), a 2.4% increment, which "may achieve a strong elastic interaction for non-screw dislocations." Hf solidifies entirely in the NbSS. For ductilization, the paper proposes (based on analogy to Ti) that Hf may lower the Peierls–Nabarro barrier energy for edge dislocation emission on (1 1 0) and (1 1 2) planes, thereby increasing fracture toughness and ductility of NbSS.
- **Property Effect:**
  - Room temperature: σ₀.₂ increases from 775 MPa (0Hf) to 870 MPa (15Hf) (~12% increment at 15Hf); σ_max increases from 1365 MPa (0Hf) to 1680 MPa (15Hf) (~23% increment); ε_max remains high: 12.0% (0Hf) to 17.0% (15Hf).
  - 1200 °C: σ₀.₂ increases from 295 MPa (0Hf) to 448 MPa (15Hf), a ~52% increase.
  - 1400 °C: σ₀.₂ increases from 180 MPa (0Hf) to 365 MPa (15Hf), a ~103% increase (from 180 to 365 MPa).
  - Vickers hardness: increases linearly from 294 HV (0Hf) to 400 HV (15Hf).
  - Ductility: No degradation; at 15Hf, ε_max = 17%, better than 0Hf (12%).
- **Potential Side Effects:** Hf is a heavy, relatively dense element, but density changes are not discussed in the original paper. The paper does not identify any negative side effects of Hf addition within the studied range (0–15 mol%).
- **Original Evidence:** "Hf with a melting point of 2754 K and a larger atomic size misfit of as high as 9% to Nb was also greatly expected to harden the NbSS." Tables 1 and 2. Section 4: "the Hf addition benefits to both the strength and ductility of the NbSS." Lattice parameter and hardness data from Section 4.

## Ti (Titanium) [Referenced, not directly tested]

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Nb-based refractory alloys (NbSS) — from cited literature (Chan, Refs [10,11])
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** Not specified in this paper; cited from prior work.
- **Main Function:** Ductilization and toughening of NbSS; Ti and Hf are "the only alloying elements that do not increase the BDTT of the NbSS."
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Theoretical calculations (Ref [11]) indicate that unstable stacking energy in NbSS is insensitive to Ti addition; however, the Peierls–Nabarro barrier energy for emission of edge dislocations on (1 1 0) and (1 1 2) planes decreases with increasing Ti addition. This lowering of the Peierls–Nabarro barrier energy increases both fracture toughness and ductility of the NbSS.
- **Property Effect:** Increased fracture toughness and ductility of NbSS; does not increase BDTT. Specific numerical values are not provided in this paper.
- **Potential Side Effects:** Not discussed in this paper.
- **Original Evidence:** "Chan [10] pointed out that Hf and Ti are the only alloying elements that do not increase the BDTT of the NbSS, and on the other hand, that Hf and Ti are the only toughening and ductilizing elements for Nb."

## W + Si + B + Hf (Combined Alloying)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Nb–W–Si–B–Hf alloys (Nb-based refractory alloys)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** W 15 mol% + Si 0.5 mol% + B 2 mol% + Hf 0–15 mol%
- **Main Function:** Combined solid-solution hardening of NbSS to achieve high-temperature strength comparable to silicide-matrix Nb alloys without sacrificing room-temperature ductility.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Each element contributes to solid-solution hardening: W (most efficient for high-temperature strength), Si (solid-solution hardening at low content), B (solid-solution hardening), Hf (large size misfit ~9%, structural difference hcp/bcc). Together they increase lattice parameter from 0.3327 to 0.3407 nm.
- **Property Effect:** 15Hf alloy achieves σ₀.₂ = 365 MPa at 1400 °C, comparable to Nb–10W–10Si with ~27% Nb5Si3 (σ₀.₂ = 330 MPa at 1670 K), but with RT ductility >11% vs. negligible ductility for the silicide-matrix alloy.
- **Potential Side Effects:** None identified in this paper within the studied composition range.
- **Original Evidence:** Conclusion (ii) and Section 4, last paragraph.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related Elements:** W, Si, B, Hf
- **Typical Alloys Covered:** Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B, Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B–(5, 10, 15)Hf
- **Key Compositions:** W 15 mol%, Si 0.5 mol%, B 2 mol%, Hf 0–15 mol%
- **Microstructural Changes:** All elements solidify entirely in the NbSS; Hf causes large lattice parameter expansion (0.3327 → 0.3407 nm at 15Hf, a 2.4% increase). No precipitate phases (except minor Nb–B boride <5 vol%) are formed.
- **Property Improvements:**
  - Room temperature: σ₀.₂ increases from 775 to 870 MPa (Hf effect); σ_max from 1365 to 1680 MPa.
  - 1200 °C: σ₀.₂ increases from 295 to 448 MPa.
  - 1400 °C: σ₀.₂ increases from 180 to 365 MPa.
  - Hardness: 294 → 400 HV.
- **Risks and Limitations:** The paper notes that general engineering materials often see a strength-ductility trade-off, but in this system, Hf addition improves both strength and ductility. The paper does not explicitly identify a ductility penalty from solid-solution strengthening within the studied range. Size misfit and structural difference are identified as the three factors affecting solid-solution hardening rate (along with solubility limit) per Ref [9].

## Carbide or Boride Precipitation Strengthening

- **Related Elements:** B (as Nb–B boride)
- **Typical Alloys Covered:** Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B–(0–15)Hf
- **Key Compositions:** 2 mol% B fixed
- **Microstructural Changes:** Forms a minority Nb–B boride phase with volume fraction <5%; morphology and volume fraction independent of Hf content.
- **Property Improvements:** The paper explicitly states that the boride's effect on bulk properties is at the same level for all alloys, so it does not contribute to the Hf-dependent property trend.
- **Risks and Limitations:** The paper does not discuss boride as a hardening or embrittlement phase; the low volume fraction (<5%) appears tolerable with ε_max of 11.5–17%.
- **Note:** This is not a classical precipitation-strengthened system with aged precipitates; the boride is formed during solidification and annealing.

## Ductilization / Toughening of NbSS

- **Related Elements:** Hf (and Ti, by analogy from cited literature)
- **Typical Alloys Covered:** Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B–(0–15)Hf (Hf effect); Ti-containing NbSS (cited literature)
- **Key Compositions:** Hf 0–15 mol%; Ti (from literature)
- **Microstructural Changes:** No microstructural phase change; Hf dissolves entirely in NbSS. Lattice parameter increases significantly.
- **Property Improvements:** Room-temperature compressive ductility (ε_max) remains high (11.5–17%) despite significant solid-solution hardening; no degradation with Hf addition; 15Hf alloy shows ε_max = 17%, highest among all alloys studied.
- **Risks and Limitations:** The ductilization mechanism by Hf is not directly verified in this paper; it is inferred by analogy to Ti. The authors state: "The corrective explanation of the ductilization of the NbSS by Hf, however, could be given only when the theoretical calculations combine with the TEM observations."
- **Mechanism discussion:** Two candidate mechanisms: (1) unstable stacking energy sensitivity (theoretical work on Ti suggests insensitivity), (2) lowering of Peierls–Nabarro barrier energy for edge dislocation emission on (1 1 0) and (1 1 2) planes (favored by theoretical calculations for Ti).

# Key Conclusions

1. The Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B–(0–15)Hf alloys display a Nb solid-solution dominant microstructure; no Nb5Si3 or Hf-based solid solution was observed, and only minor Nb–B boride (<5 vol%) is present.

2. Hf addition produces outstanding solid-solution hardening at both room and high temperatures without degradation of room-temperature ductility.

3. At 1400 °C, the 0.2% compressive yield strength increases from 180 MPa (0Hf) to 365 MPa (15Hf); at 1200 °C, from 295 to 448 MPa.

4. Room-temperature compressive ductility is high for all alloys: ε_max ranges from 11.5% to 17%, with the 15Hf alloy showing the highest value (17%), improving over the base alloy (12%).

5. The 15Hf alloy's high-temperature strength at 1400 °C is comparable to that of Nb–10W–10Si containing ~27% Nb5Si3 (330 MPa at 1670 K), while retaining far superior room-temperature ductility (>11% vs. negligible). This suggests Nb5Si3-free Nb-based alloys may replace NbSS/Nb5Si3 structures for high-temperature applications, even with less than 5% boride.

6. The solid-solution hardening mechanism involves Hf's large atomic size misfit (~9%) relative to Nb and the structural difference between hcp Hf and bcc Nb. Hf increases the NbSS lattice parameter by 2.4% at 15 mol% Hf.

7. Ductilization by Hf is hypothesized to occur via lowering of the Peierls–Nabarro barrier energy for edge dislocation emission (by analogy to Ti), but direct verification requires theoretical calculations combined with TEM observations.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Alloy system specificity:** The results are specific to Nb–15W–0.5Si–2B–(0–15)Hf alloys in the as-cast and annealed condition; extrapolation to other Nb-based alloys or other refractory systems should be done with caution.
- **Test mode:** Only compressive tests were performed; tensile properties, fracture toughness, and creep resistance were not measured.
- **Test atmosphere:** High-temperature tests were performed in vacuum; air performance is not addressed.
- **Oxidation resistance:** Not tested; no conclusions about oxidation resistance or high-temperature environmental stability can be drawn.
- **Microstructural characterization:** SEM only; TEM verification of dislocation mechanisms and ductilization mechanism was not performed.
- **No thermomechanical processing:** Alloys tested in as-cast + annealed (1700 °C/50 h) condition; no working, forging, or deformation processing was applied.
- **Grain size:** Not reported; grain-boundary contributions cannot be assessed.
- **Composition notation:** All compositions in mol%, not wt%; conversion to wt% is not provided.
- **Ductilization mechanism:** The proposed Hf ductilization mechanism (Peierls–Nabarro barrier energy reduction) is inferred from Ti and not experimentally verified for Hf in this paper.
- **Density and density trade-offs:** Not reported, though Hf is known to increase density.
- **Impurities:** Impurity levels (C, O, N, H) are not reported, though purity of starting elements was ≥99.9 mol%.
- **Small sample size:** Only four alloy compositions (0, 5, 10, 15 mol% Hf) were tested; finer compositional optimization is not possible from this dataset.
