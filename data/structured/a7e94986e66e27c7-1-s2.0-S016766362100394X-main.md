# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system**: Ni-based superalloys — specifically the face-centered cubic (fcc) Ni matrix phase and the L1₂-type ordered Ni₃Al precipitate phase
- **Alloying elements studied**: Re, Ru, Ta, Mo, W (refractory elements), each substituting individually into Ni and Ni₃Al
- **Study type**: First-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations
- **Research objective**: To systematically compare the effects of Re, Ru, Ta, W, and Mo on:
  - Generalized stacking fault energy (GSFE) of Ni and Ni₃Al
  - Ideal tensile strength and ideal shear strength of Ni and Ni₃Al
  - Electronic structure origins of the strengthening effects

# Alloy Composition

This is a computational study where individual alloying elements are substituted at one atomic site. No multiple-component alloy compositions with co-existing elements are provided in the sense of a bulk alloy.

- **Binary model systems investigated**:
  - Ni–X, where X = Re, Ru, Mo, W, Ta
  - Ni₃Al–X, where X = Re, Ru, Mo, W, Ta
- **Alloying elements**: Re, Ru, Ta, Mo, W
- **Elements not present in this study**: Nb, Zr, Hf, Ti, V, Cr, Al (only as Ni₃Al base), Si, C, B, Y, La, O
- **Site preference**: For Ni₃Al, the refractory elements Re, Ru, Ta, Mo, and W are reported to prefer the Al sublattice site. The computational scheme substitutes one alloying atom X for one Al atom in Ni₃Al.
- **Atomic percentage**: Each supercell contains exactly one substitutional alloying atom. The corresponding concentration values are:
  - For Ni–X GSFE supercell: 1 X atom in 48 total atoms → ~2.08 at.%
  - For Ni₃Al–X GSFE supercell: 1 X atom replacing Al in a 48-atom supercell (with composition Ni₃Al⁻ᵀ)
  - For ideal strength calculations, smaller unit cells are used
- **Weight percentage**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper
- **Impurities (C, O, N, H)**: Not discussed / not applicable in this computational study

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## [Re (Rhenium)]
- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni matrix phase and Ni₃Al precipitate phase in Ni-based superalloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Single substitutional atom in supercell (~2.08 at.% for 48-atom GSFE supercell; exact concentration not stated as design target)
- **Main Function**: Solid-solution strengthening; increase dislocation slip resistance (γ_us); high-temperature creep strengthening in Ni
- **Microstructural Mechanism**:
  - In Ni: Re–d and Ni–d orbital hybridization with peaks at Fermi level; strong covalent-like bonding
  - In Ni₃Al: Re–d orbitals form deep and wide pseudogap near the Fermi level; strong directional charge distribution between Re and Ni (covalent-like bond)
- **Property Effect**:
  - Ni–Re: γ_us increases from 273 to 301 mJ/m²; γ_isf decreases from 149 to 48 mJ/m²; ideal tensile strength +21.6% (9.0 GPa at ε=0.08); ideal shear strength +18.4% (4.5 GPa at ε=0.14)
  - Ni₃Al–Re: γ_us [112̄] increases from 227 to 432 mJ/m²; ideal tensile strength +32.6% (11.4 GPa at ε=0.10); ideal shear strength +48.7% (5.8 GPa at ε=0.20)
  - Re has the strongest strengthening effect on both Ni and Ni₃Al among the five elements studied
- **Potential Side Effects**:
  - For Ni₃Al: Increases γ_csf (208→524 mJ/m²), γ_APB (198→494 mJ/m²), γ_SISF (21→142 mJ/m²), which the paper links to reduced creep strength of Ni₃Al
  - Anomalous GSFE curve — Ni₃Al–Re cannot form a locally stable CSF configuration at 1.0 b_p
- **Original Evidence**: Tables 1 and 2; Figs. 2, 5, 6(b), 7(b), 8(b,d); Conclusions (1)–(3)

## [Ru (Ruthenium)]
- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni matrix phase and Ni₃Al precipitate phase in Ni-based superalloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Single substitutional atom in supercell (~2.08 at.% for 48-atom GSFE supercell; exact concentration not stated as design target)
- **Main Function**: Solid-solution strengthening; strongest effect on Ni matrix among test elements (tied with Re for γ_us) but weakest effect on Ni₃Al among the five elements
- **Microstructural Mechanism**:
  - In Ni: Ru–d and Ni–d hybridize with peaks at Fermi level
  - In Ni₃Al: Ru–d orbital shows no pseudogap at Fermi level; weaker hybridization with 1NN atoms compared with W; covalent-like bond between Ru and Ni is weaker and more easily disrupted
- **Property Effect**:
  - Ni–Ru: γ_us increases from 273 to 308 mJ/m² (highest among the five); γ_isf decreases from 149 to 95 mJ/m²; ideal tensile strength +14.9% (8.5 GPa); ideal shear strength +13.2% (4.3 GPa)
  - Ni₃Al–Ru: γ_us [112̄] increases from 227 to 339 mJ/m² (smallest increase among the five); ideal tensile strength +13.9% (9.8 GPa); ideal shear strength +25.6% (4.9 GPa)
  - Ru is the only element studied whose strengthening effect is weaker in Ni₃Al than in Ni (for tensile strength)
- **Potential Side Effects**:
  - In Ni₃Al, Ru barely changes γ_csf, γ_APB, and γ_SISF compared with pure Ni₃Al, meaning it does not reduce creep strength of Ni₃Al as much as Re, Mo, W, Ta
  - Ni₃Al–Ru reaches ideal tensile strength under smaller strain (ε=0.08) than Ni₃Al–Re (ε=0.10), indicating lower ductility
- **Original Evidence**: Tables 1 and 2; Figs. 2, 5, 6(a), 7(a), 8(a,c); Conclusions (1) and (3)

## [Ta (Tantalum)]
- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni matrix phase and Ni₃Al precipitate phase in Ni-based superalloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Single substitutional atom in supercell (~2.08 at.% for 48-atom GSFE supercell; exact concentration not stated as design target)
- **Main Function**: Solid-solution strengthening; stronger effect on Ni₃Al than on Ni
- **Microstructural Mechanism**:
  - In Ni: Ta–d and Ni–d hybridize but hybridization peaks do not fall at Fermi level (unlike Ru, Re, Mo, W)
  - In Ni₃Al: Ta–d orbital forms deep pseudogap near Fermi level, similar to Re, Mo, W
- **Property Effect**:
  - Ni–Ta: γ_us increases from 273 to 276 mJ/m² (smallest effect on Ni); γ_isf decreases from 149 to 86 mJ/m²; ideal tensile strength +5.4% (7.8 GPa); ideal shear strength +5.3% (4.0 GPa) — weakest strengthening effect on Ni among the five
  - Ni₃Al–Ta: γ_us [112̄] increases from 227 to 394 mJ/m²; ideal tensile strength +19.8% (10.3 GPa); ideal shear strength +35.9% (5.3 GPa)
- **Potential Side Effects**:
  - In Ni₃Al: Increases γ_csf (208→588 mJ/m²), γ_APB (198→585 mJ/m²), γ_SISF (21→347 mJ/m²), linked to reduced creep strength of Ni₃Al
  - Anomalous GSFE curve — Ni₃Al–Ta cannot form a locally stable CSF configuration
- **Original Evidence**: Tables 1 and 2; Figs. 2, 5, 6(e), 7(e); Conclusions (1)–(2)

## [W (Tungsten)]
- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni matrix phase and Ni₃Al precipitate phase in Ni-based superalloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Single substitutional atom in supercell (~2.08 at.% for 48-atom GSFE supercell; exact concentration not stated as design target)
- **Main Function**: Solid-solution strengthening; stronger effect on Ni₃Al than on Ni
- **Microstructural Mechanism**:
  - In Ni: W–d and Ni–d hybridize with peaks at Fermi level
  - In Ni₃Al: W–d orbital forms deep pseudogap near Fermi level

- **Property Effect**:
  - Ni–W: γ_us increases from 273 to 289 mJ/m²; γ_isf decreases from 149 to 59 mJ/m²; ideal tensile strength +10.8% (8.2 GPa); ideal shear strength +10.5% (4.2 GPa)

  - In Ni₃Al: Highest γ_csf (611 mJ/m²), γ_APB (599 mJ/m²), and γ_SISF (281 mJ/m²) among studied elements — most unfavorable for CSF stability; linked to reduced creep strength of Ni₃Al

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni-based superalloys

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening (via Refractory Elements in Ni and Ni₃Al)
- **Related elements**: Re, Ru, Ta, Mo, W
- **Typical alloys**: Ni-based single-crystal superalloys containing Re, Ru, Ta, Mo, W
- **Key compositions**: Single substitutional solute atom in Ni or Ni₃Al supercell (~2.08 at.% in 48-atom supercell)
- **Microstructural changes**: Lattice distortion and local d–d orbital hybridization between solute and Ni atoms; pseudogap formation near Fermi level for Re, Mo, W, Ta in Ni₃Al
- **Property improvements**:
  - All five elements increase unstable stacking fault energy (γ_us) of both Ni and Ni₃Al, indicating higher dislocation slip barrier
  - Ideal tensile strength of Ni increases by +5.4% (Ta) to +21.6% (Re); ideal shear strength by +5.3% (Ta) to +18.4% (Re)
  - Ideal tensile strength of Ni₃Al increases by +13.9% (Ru) to +32.6% (Re); ideal shear strength by +25.6% (Ru) to +48.7% (Re)
- **Risks and limitations**:
  - Ru provides only modest strengthening of Ni₃Al compared with Re, Mo, W, Ta
  - Ta provides only weak strengthening of Ni matrix
  - Re, Mo, W, Ta change the GSFE curve of Ni₃Al so that a stable CSF configuration cannot form
- **Original evidence**: Section 3.1–3.3; Tables 1 and 2

## Creep Strengthening via Intrinsic Stacking Fault Energy Reduction (Ni matrix)
- **Related elements**: Re, Ru, Mo, W, Ta
- **Typical alloys**: Ni matrix phase in Ni-based superalloys
- **Key compositions**: Single substitutional solute atom
- **Microstructural changes**: Lower γ_isf means wider stacking fault, making dislocation cross-slip and climb more difficult
- **Property improvements**:
  - γ_isf of Ni decreases from 149 mJ/m² to: Ru 95, Re 48, Mo 55, W 59, Ta 86 mJ/m²
  - Lower γ_isf is beneficial to creep strength of Ni
- **Risks and limitations**: Only qualitative creep implication; steady-state creep rate not directly calculated
- **Original evidence**: Section 3.1, Fig. 2(a), Table 1; conclusion (1)

## Creep Strength Reduction via CSF/APB/SISF Energy Increase (Ni₃Al)
- **Related elements**: Re, Mo, W, Ta (not Ru)
- **Typical alloys**: Ni₃Al precipitate phase in Ni-based superalloys
- **Key compositions**: Single substitutional solute atom on Al sublattice
- **Microstructural changes**: Re, Mo, W, Ta significantly increase γ_csf, γ_APB, and γ_SISF of Ni₃Al; Ru has little effect
- **Property effects**:
  - γ_csf [112̄]: Ni₃Al 208 → Re 524, Mo 553, W 611, Ta 588 mJ/m² (Ru: 242)
  - γ_APB [101̄]: Ni₃Al 198 → Re 494, Mo 531, W 599, Ta 585 mJ/m² (Ru: 185)
  - γ_SISF [2̄11]: Ni₃Al 21 → Re 142, Mo 238, W 281, Ta 347 mJ/m² (Ru: 29)
  - Paper concludes these elements are “not conducive to improve the creep strength of Ni₃Al”
- **Risks and limitations**: This is a theoretical inference from fault energies; not experimentally validated
- **Original evidence**: Section 3.1, Table 2; conclusion (1)

## Electronic-Structure-Induced Bond Strengthening (Pseudogap Mechanism)
- **Related elements**: Re, Mo, W, Ta (not Ru)
- **Typical alloys**: Ni₃Al precipitate phase
- **Key compositions**: Single substitutional solute atom on Al sublattice
- **Microstructural changes**: d orbitals of Re, Ta, Mo, W form deep and wide pseudogap near Fermi level in Ni₃Al; Ru does not
- **Property improvements**: Explains why Re, Mo, W, Ta strengthen Ni₃Al more than Ni; Ni₃Al–Re ideal tensile strength +32.6%, shear +48.7%
- **Risks and limitations**: Pseudogap size is related to bond strength but direct quantitative correlation not established
- **Original evidence**: Section 3.3, Figs. 6–7; conclusion (2)

## Anomalous GSFE Curve / CSF Instability
- **Related elements**: Re, Mo, W, Ta (not Ru)
- **Typical alloys**: Ni₃Al–X
- **Key compositions**: Single substitutional solute atom
- **Microstructural changes**: Ni₃Al–Re, Ni₃Al–Mo, Ni₃Al–W, Ni₃Al–Ta do not show typical bell-shaped GSFE curve with peak at 0.5 b_p; cannot form locally stable CSF at 1.0 b_p; strong W–d/Ni–d hybridization at Fermi level during CSF formation
- **Property effects**: Not directly tied to a specific property value; related to the unusually high γ_csf values
- **Risks and limitations**: Ru does not induce this anomaly
- **Original evidence**: Section 3.1, Fig. 2(b), Fig. 3; Table 2

# Key Conclusions

1. Alloying elements Re, Ru, Ta, Mo, and W can improve the creep strength of Ni (via reduction of γ_isf) but reduce the creep strength of Ni₃Al (via increase of γ_csf, γ_APB, γ_SISF).

2. Among the five elements, Re and Ru are the most effective for improving the strength of the Ni matrix. For Ni₃Al, Ru has the weakest strengthening effect.

3. Except for Ru, Re, Mo, W, and Ta strengthen Ni₃Al more significantly than Ni. This is attributed to the deep and wide pseudogap formed by their d orbitals near the Fermi level in Ni₃Al.

4. Ru cannot form strong covalent-like bonds in Ni₃Al like Re does. Under tensile strain, the charge distribution around Ru–Ni is more easily destroyed, leading to lower ideal tensile strength and lower ductility for Ni₃Al–Ru compared with Ni₃Al–Re.

5. The strengthening sequences are:
   - Ni: Ni–Re ≈ Ni–Ru ≈ Ni–Mo ≈ Ni–W > Ni–Ta > Ni
   - Ni₃Al: Ni₃Al–Re > Ni₃Al–W ≈ Ni₃Al–Mo > Ni₃Al–Ta > Ni₃Al–Ru > Ni₃Al

6. Re has the strongest strengthening effect on both phases: Ni ideal tensile strength +21.6%, shear +18.4%; Ni₃Al ideal tensile strength +32.6%, shear +48.7%.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Limitations**:
  - The study is purely computational (first-principles DFT). No experimental validation of GSFE curves, ideal strengths, or creep implications is provided.
  - The model systems are binary (Ni–X) or ternary (Ni₃Al–X) with a single substitutional solute atom. Real Ni-based superalloys contain multiple alloying elements simultaneously, and synergistic effects are not captured.
  - The allocation of elements to specific sites (Al site in Ni₃Al) is based on prior studies and may not capture the full site distribution in real alloys.
  - The concentration of each alloying element is fixed by the supercell size (~2.08 at.% for GSFE), which may not match actual superalloy compositions.
  - The paper does not provide direct experimental data for high-temperature strength, creep rate, or rupture life; the creep conclusions are inferred from stacking fault energy calculations.
  - No oxidation, corrosion, or embrittlement behavior is assessed.

- **Applicable Scope**:
  - Ni-based superalloys, particularly single-crystal grades containing Re, Ru, Ta, Mo, W
  - Qualitative ranking of solid-solution strengthening contributions of refractory elements in Ni and Ni₃Al phases
  - Mechanistic interpretation of d-orbital hybridization and pseudogap formation effects on ideal strength
  - Guidance for alloy design of Ni-based superalloys, not refractory high-temperature alloys such as Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based, refractory high-entropy alloys, or additively manufactured refractory alloys
