- **Authors:** L.C. Liu, J.B. Jin, J.T. Zheng, Z.Y. Xu, S.F. Zhou
- **Journal:** Solid State Communications
- **Year:** 2026 (Available online 6 November 2025)

- **Alloy system studied:** HCP Zr-based alloys with alloying elements Sn, Fe, Cr, Cu, and Nb
- **Research objective:** To comparatively examine how alloying elements (Sn, Fe, Cr, Cu, and Nb) modulate the grain boundary stability, cohesive strength, Young's modulus, and fracture toughness of HCP Zr {10¯11} grain boundary using density functional theory (DFT)
- **Material category:** Zirconium-based nuclear structural alloy (not a Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloy system). This paper deals with HCP Zr alloy grain boundaries relevant to nuclear fuel cladding applications.

# Alloy System and Research Object

This paper is a first-principles computational study of the HCP Zr {10¯11} grain boundary and the effects of five alloying elements (Sn, Fe, Cr, Cu, Nb) on grain boundary cohesion and fracture toughness. The research is motivated by the use of Zr alloys in nuclear reactor cladding and pressure vessel applications. It does not involve refractory high-temperature alloys, Nb-W alloys, Mo-W alloys, or additively manufactured refractory alloys. The material system is zirconium, not a refractory high-entropy alloy or multi-principal element alloy.

# Alloy Composition

The paper investigates binary Zr₉₅X₁ and ternary Zr₉₄X₁Y₁ computational models where one or two Zr atoms in the {10¯11} grain boundary supercell are replaced by alloying elements. The systems studied are:

**Binary systems (Zr₉₅X₁, where one Zr atom in the 7th layer of the GB model is substituted):**
- Zr₉₅Sn₁: 95 Zr atoms + 1 Sn atom (≈1.04 at.% Sn)
- Zr₉₅Fe₁: 95 Zr atoms + 1 Fe atom (≈1.04 at.% Fe)
- Zr₉₅Cr₁: 95 Zr atoms + 1 Cr atom (≈1.04 at.% Cr)
- Zr₉₅Cu₁: 95 Zr atoms + 1 Cu atom (≈1.04 at.% Cu)
- Zr₉₅Nb₁: 95 Zr atoms + 1 Nb atom (≈1.04 at.% Nb)

**Ternary co-alloyed systems (Zr₉₄X₁Y₁, where two Zr atoms at the 7th layer are replaced):**
- Zr₉₄Cr₁Nb₁: 94 Zr atoms + 1 Cr atom + 1 Nb atom
- Zr₉₄Sn₁Nb₁: 94 Zr atoms + 1 Sn atom + 1 Nb atom
- Zr₉₄Sn₁Cr₁: 94 Zr atoms + 1 Sn atom + 1 Cr atom

**Impurities (C, O, N, H):** Not addressed in this paper. This is a pure computational study without impurity considerations.

**Elements present among those of interest:** The paper includes Nb, Cr, and Fe (refractory-adjacent/transition metals) and Sn and Cu. It does not include W, Mo, Ta, Zr as an alloying addition (Zr is the matrix), Hf, Ti, V, Al, Si, Re, C, B, Y, La, or O.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase:** HCP (hexagonal close-packed) α-Zr matrix phase
- **Grain boundary type:** {10¯11} HCP Zr grain boundary
- **Grain boundary model:** 12-layer supercell containing 96 atoms, dimensions 12.18 × 6.47 × 30.36 Å³
- **Bulk lattice parameters used:** a = 3.239 Å, c/a = 1.593
- **Second phases/precipitates:** Not applicable — no precipitates or second phases are modeled in this GB study
- **Grain size:** Not applicable — single GB model
- **Grain boundary energy (γ_GB) of pure Zr {10¯11} GB:** 58.37 mJ/m² (literature value: 64.8 mJ/m²)
- **Calculated γ_GB of alloyed systems:**
  - ZrCr: 10.66 mJ/m²
  - ZrFe: 12.84 mJ/m²
  - ZrNb: 41.06 mJ/m²
  - ZrCu: 15.25 mJ/m²
  - ZrSn: 56.84 mJ/m²
- **Elemental segregation:** Cr, Fe, and Nb exhibit negative segregation energies (E_seg < 0), indicating spontaneous GB segregation. Cu and Sn exhibit positive segregation energies, indicating thermodynamic repulsion from the GB core.
- **Segregation energies (E_seg):**
  - ZrCr: −0.392 J/m² (literature: −0.37 J/m²)
  - ZrFe: −0.57 J/m² (literature: −0.568 J/m²)
  - ZrNb: −0.123 J/m² (literature: −0.10 J/m²)
  - ZrCu: +0.806 J/m²
  - ZrSn: +0.037 J/m² (literature: +0.06 J/m²)
- **Charge density analysis:** Zr–Fe bonds at Zr₉₅Fe₁ GB are denser than Zr–Zr bonds in pure Zr, indicating stronger bonding; Zr–Sn bonds are sparser, indicating weaker bonding.
- **ELF analysis:** Zr–Nb bonds exhibit ELF peaks indicating ~50% stronger covalent character compared to Zr–Zr interactions.

**Strengthening/weakening mechanisms discussed:** The paper discusses grain boundary cohesion strengthening/weakening via electronic structure modification (charge density redistribution and bond directional character). It does not discuss solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, dispersion strengthening, grain refinement, or ODS strengthening in the classical sense — the focus is specifically on GB cohesion and fracture toughness.

# Mechanical Properties

The paper reports computational predictions of cohesion strength (work of separation), Young's modulus along [101], and fracture toughness:

**Work of separation (W_sep):**
- Pure Zr {10¯11} GB: 3.341 J/m²
- Zr₉₅Cr₁: 3.364 J/m² (+0.69%)
- Zr₉₅Fe₁: 3.408 J/m² (+2.01%)
- Zr₉₅Nb₁: 3.382 J/m² (+1.23%)
- Zr₉₅Cu₁: 3.240 J/m² (−3.02%)
- Zr₉₅Sn₁: 3.251 J/m² (−2.69%)
- Zr₉₄Cr₁Nb₁: 3.546 J/m² (+6.14%)
- Zr₉₄Sn₁Nb₁: 3.376 J/m² (+1.05%)
- Zr₉₄Sn₁Cr₁: 3.372 J/m² (+0.93%)

**Young's modulus along [101] direction (E_<101>):**
- Pure Zr: 70 GPa
- Zr₉₅Cr₁: 75.2 GPa (+7.43%)
- Zr₉₅Fe₁: 72.3 GPa (+3.29%)
- Zr₉₅Nb₁: 78.1 GPa (+11.57%)
- Zr₉₅Cu₁: 68.7 GPa (−1.86%)
- Zr₉₅Sn₁: 69.1 GPa (−1.29%)
- Zr₉₄Cr₁Nb₁: 69.2 GPa (−1.14%)
- Zr₉₄Sn₁Nb₁: 72.9 GPa (+4.14%)
- Zr₉₄Sn₁Cr₁: 72.2 GPa (+3.14%)

**Fracture toughness (K_IC) derived from Griffith crack theory:**
- Pure Zr: 0.967 MPa·m⁰·⁵
- Zr₉₅Cr₁: 1.006 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+4.03%)
- Zr₉₅Fe₁: 0.993 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+2.67%)
- Zr₉₅Nb₁: 1.028 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+6.31%)
- Zr₉₅Cu₁: 0.943 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (−2.48%)
- Zr₉₅Sn₁: 0.948 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (−2.00%)
- Zr₉₄Cr₁Nb₁: 0.991 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+2.48%)
- Zr₉₄Sn₁Nb₁: 0.992 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+2.59%)
- Zr₉₄Sn₁Cr₁: 0.987 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+2.07%)

**Other mechanical properties:** Room-temperature yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, compressive strength, high-temperature strength, elongation, creep performance, rupture life, strain rate, hardness, and density are not reported in this computational study.

**Loading mode:** Computational ideal tensile tests along the [101] direction using first-principles calculations. No experimental mechanical testing is reported.

**Atmosphere/testing conditions:** Not applicable — purely computational DFT study.

## Sn (Tin)

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
HCP Zr-based nuclear alloys (Zr-Sn systems, e.g., Zr-4 type alloys)

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
~1.04 at.% Sn in Zr₉₅Sn₁ model; ~1.04 at.% Sn in Zr₉₄Sn₁Nb₁ and Zr₉₄Sn₁Cr₁ ternary models

**[Main Function]**
Sn is commonly used in Zr alloys for solid-solution strengthening and corrosion resistance enhancement; however, at the grain boundary, Sn weakens cohesion and reduces fracture toughness.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
- Sn exhibits a positive segregation energy (+0.037 J/m²), indicating thermodynamic repulsion from the GB core.
- Zr–Sn bonds at the GB are more isotropic (less directional) than Zr–Zr bonds, leading to weaker bonding.
- Charge density analysis shows Zr–Sn bonds are sparser than Zr–Zr bonds, weakening chemical bonding.
- Sn reduces both W_sep and Young's modulus, creating a synergistic embrittlement pathway.

**[Property Effect]**
- W_sep decreases from 3.341 to 3.251 J/m² (−2.69%)
- Young's modulus decreases from 70 to 69.1 GPa (−1.29%)
- Fracture toughness decreases from 0.967 to 0.948 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (−2.00%)
- Grain boundary energy decreases slightly from 58.37 to 56.84 mJ/m²

**[Potential Side Effects]**
- Grain boundary embrittlement
- Reduced cohesion strength
- Reduced fracture toughness
- Reduced Young's modulus
- Intergranular fracture risk in LWR environments

**[Original Evidence]**
Table 1; Section 3, paragraphs on W_sep, E_<101>, and K_IC; Fig. 2(c) charge density; Fig. 4(c) charge density difference showing isotropic Zr–Sn bonding.

## Fe (Iron)

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
HCP Zr-based nuclear alloys (Zr-Fe systems)

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
~1.04 at.% Fe in Zr₉₅Fe₁ model

- Young's modulus increases from 70 to 72.3 GPa (+3.29%)
- Fracture toughness increases from 0.967 to 0.993 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+2.67%)
- GB energy decreases from 58.37 to 12.84 mJ/m²

**[Potential Side Effects]**
- Not explicitly stated in the original paper. No embrittlement or negative effects are reported for Fe at the GB.

**[Original Evidence]**
Table 1; Fig. 2(b) charge density; Fig. 4(b) charge density difference showing directional Zr–Fe bonding; statement that "Zr-Fe bonding in {10¯11} GB of Zr₉₅Fe₁ is more directional than that of Zr-Zr in pure Zr."

## Cr (Chromium)

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
HCP Zr-based nuclear alloys (Zr-Cr systems, e.g., Zr-4 type alloys)

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
~1.04 at.% Cr in Zr₉₅Cr₁ model; ~1.04 at.% Cr in Zr₉₄Cr₁Nb₁ and Zr₉₄Sn₁Cr₁ ternary models

**[Main Function]**
Cr enhances grain boundary stability, cohesion strength, Young's modulus, and fracture toughness of HCP Zr {10¯11} GB. Cr also mitigates Sn-induced embrittlement in co-alloyed systems.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
- Cr exhibits a strongly negative segregation energy (−0.392 J/m²), indicating strong spontaneous GB segregation.
- Cr substantially reduces GB energy from 58.37 to 10.66 mJ/m², the lowest among all single-doped systems.
- Cr addition increases Young's modulus most significantly (+7.43%), which is the primary mechanism driving fracture toughness enhancement.
- In Zr₉₄Sn₁Cr₁, Cr addition mitigates Sn-decreased cohesion strength, removing the cohesion loss caused by Sn.

**[Property Effect]**
- W_sep increases from 3.341 to 3.364 J/m² (+0.69%)
- Young's modulus increases from 70 to 75.2 GPa (+7.43%)
- Fracture toughness increases from 0.967 to 1.006 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+4.03%)
- GB energy decreases from 58.37 to 10.66 mJ/m²
- Zr₉₄Cr₁Nb₁: W_sep = 3.546 J/m² (+6.14%), K_IC = 0.991 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+2.48%)
- Zr₉₄Sn₁Cr₁: W_sep = 3.372 J/m² (+0.93%), K_IC = 0.987 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+2.07%)

**[Potential Side Effects]**
- Not explicitly stated in the original paper. No embrittlement or negative effects are reported for Cr at the GB.

**[Original Evidence]**
Table 1; statement that "Cr/Fe/Nb exhibit spontaneous segregation, reducing grain boundary energy compared to pure Zr, thereby enhancing interfacial stability"; statement that "co-alloying strategies with Cr/Nb effectively mitigate Sn-induced embrittlement, recovering pure Zr's fracture toughness."

## Grain-Boundary Cohesion Strengthening via Alloying

**[Related Elements]**
Cr, Fe, Nb (beneficial); Cu, Sn (detrimental)

**[Typical Alloys]**
HCP Zr nuclear alloys (Zr-Sn, Zr-Fe, Zr-Cr, Zr-Nb binary and ternary systems)

**[Key Compositions]**
~1.04 at.% single dopant, ~2.08 at.% total in ternary co-doped models

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Cr, Fe, Nb reduce GB energy: Cr (10.66 mJ/m²), Fe (12.84 mJ/m²), Nb (41.06 mJ/m²) vs. pure Zr (58.37 mJ/m²)
- Cr, Fe, Nb exhibit negative segregation energies, accumulating at the GB
- Charge density analysis: Zr–Fe bonds denser than Zr–Zr; Zr–Sn bonds sparser than Zr–Zr
- ELF analysis: Zr–Nb bonds ~50% stronger covalent character than Zr–Zr

**[Property Improvements]**
- Cr: W_sep +0.69%, E +7.43%, K_IC +4.03%
- Fe: W_sep +2.01%, E +3.29%, K_IC +2.67%
- Nb: W_sep +1.23%, E +11.57%, K_IC +6.31%
- Cr+Nb co-alloying: W_sep +6.14%
- Sn+Nb co-alloying: W_sep +1.05%, K_IC +2.59%
- Sn+Cr co-alloying: W_sep +0.93%, K_IC +2.07%

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- Cu and Sn reduce GB cohesion: Cu W_sep −3.02%, Sn W_sep −2.69%
- Cu and Sn reduce Young's modulus and fracture toughness
- Sn embrittlement must be mitigated by co-alloying with Cr or Nb

## Modulus-Driven Toughening Mechanism

**[Related Elements]**
Cr, Fe, Nb

**[Typical Alloys]**
Zr-Cr, Zr-Fe, Zr-Nb binary systems

**[Key Compositions]**
~1.04 at.% dopant

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Young's modulus enhancement is more significant than cohesion enhancement for Cr, Fe, and Nb
- E_<101> increases of 7.43% (Cr), 3.29% (Fe), and 11.57% (Nb) are larger than corresponding W_sep increases (0.69%, 2.01%, 1.23%)

**[Property Improvements]**
- Fracture toughness increase in Cr/Fe/Nb-doped systems is governed by modulus augmentation rather than cohesion enhancement
- K_IC = √(4·W_sep·E_hkl) from Griffith crack theory

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- Modulus-driven toughening is effective only when cohesion is not simultaneously reduced
- Cu and Sn reduce both modulus and cohesion, creating synergistic embrittlement

## Synergistic Embrittlement via Cohesion Loss + Modulus Reduction

**[Related Elements]**
Cu, Sn

**[Typical Alloys]**
Zr-Cu, Zr-Sn binary systems

**[Key Compositions]**
~1.04 at.% Cu or Sn

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Positive segregation energies (Cu: +0.806 J/m²; Sn: +0.037 J/m²) indicate thermodynamic repulsion from GB
- Zr–Sn bonds more isotropic and less directional than Zr–Zr bonds
- Charge density redistribution weakens Zr–Zr bonding at GB

**[Property Improvements]**
None — all properties deteriorate

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- Cu: W_sep −3.02%, E −1.86%, K_IC −2.48%
- Sn: W_sep −2.69%, E −1.29%, K_IC −2.00%
- Both elements simultaneously reduce cohesion and modulus, leading to synergistic embrittlement pathways
- Intergranular fracture is a predominant failure mode in Zr alloy cladding in LWR environments

## Co-Alloying to Mitigate Sn-Induced Embrittlement

**[Related Elements]**
Sn + Cr, Sn + Nb

**[Typical Alloys]**
Zr-Sn-Cr and Zr-Sn-Nb ternary alloys

**[Key Compositions]**
~1.04 at.% Sn + ~1.04 at.% Cr or Nb

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Zr–Nb bonds in Zr₉₄Sn₁Nb₁ exhibit enhanced ELF peaks (~50% stronger covalent character than Zr–Zr)
- Cr and Nb segregate to the GB and restore bond strength
- Ordering of K_IC: Zr₉₅Nb₁ > Zr₉₄Sn₁Nb₁ > pure Zr > Zr₉₅Sn₁

**[Property Improvements]**
- Sn+Nb: W_sep 3.376 J/m² (+1.05% vs pure Zr), K_IC 0.992 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+2.59%)
- Sn+Cr: W_sep 3.372 J/m² (+0.93% vs pure Zr), K_IC 0.987 MPa·m⁰·⁵ (+2.07%)
- Both co-alloying strategies recover pure Zr's fracture toughness despite Sn's embrittling tendency

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- Ternary systems do not fully reach the fracture toughness of the binary Zr–Nb system
- The mitigation is partial; the K_IC gap between binary Zr-Nb and ternary Zr-Sn-Nb remains

# Key Conclusions

1. Cr, Fe, and Nb exhibit spontaneous grain boundary segregation (negative E_seg) in HCP Zr {10¯11} GB, reducing GB energy and enhancing interfacial stability.

2. Cu and Sn exhibit positive segregation energies, indicating thermodynamic repulsion from the GB core.

3. Cr, Fe, and Nb increase work of separation, Young's modulus, and fracture toughness of the Zr {10¯11} GB:
   - Nb provides the largest fracture toughness enhancement (+6.31%) and Young's modulus increase (+11.57%)
   - Cr provides the largest GB energy reduction (58.37 → 10.66 mJ/m²) and the most significant modulus-driven toughening (+7.43% E)
   - Fe provides the largest work of separation increase among binary systems (+2.01%)

4. Cu and Sn substantially diminish cohesion strength, Young's modulus, and fracture toughness. Cu (−2.48% K_IC) and Sn (−2.00% K_IC) induce synergistic embrittlement through combined cohesion loss and modulus reduction.

5. Co-alloying Cr+Nb produces the maximum W_sep enhancement (+6.14%), exceeding individual dopant effects.

6. Co-alloying with Cr or Nb effectively mitigates Sn-induced embrittlement, recovering pure Zr's fracture toughness in Sn-containing alloys.

7. Fracture toughness enhancement in Cr/Fe/Nb-doped systems originates primarily from Young's modulus augmentation rather than cohesion enhancement.

8. Electronic structure analysis reveals:
   - Zr–Fe bonds are denser and more directional than Zr–Zr bonds → enhanced bonding
   - Zr–Sn bonds are sparser and more isotropic → weakened bonding
   - Zr–Nb bonds exhibit ~50% stronger covalent character → enhanced bonding

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Computational nature:** This is a purely DFT-based first-principles study. No experimental validation of the predicted GB properties is provided in this paper. All properties (W_sep, E_<101>, K_IC, γ_GB, E_seg) are computed values.

- **Simple GB model:** The study uses a single symmetric {10¯11} HCP Zr GB model with a 12-layer supercell containing only 96 atoms. This represents only one specific GB type and does not capture the diversity of GB characters found in real polycrystalline Zr alloys.

- **Low dopant concentration:** The modeling uses only ~1 at.% single dopant (~2 at.% for ternary systems). Real Zr nuclear alloys may contain different concentrations and multiple alloying elements simultaneously.

- **No temperature effects:** DFT calculations are effectively zero-temperature. The thermodynamic analysis does not account for finite-temperature segregation behavior, vibrational entropy, or temperature-dependent mechanical properties.

- **No irradiation effects:** The study does not model neutron irradiation damage, which is critical for nuclear applications and can significantly alter GB segregation and fracture behavior.

- **No precipitate or second-phase effects:** Real Zr alloys contain intermetallic precipitates (e.g., Zr(Cr,Fe)₂, Zr₃Fe, Zr-Nb precipitates) that influence GB cohesion and fracture behavior. This study only addresses solute segregation at the GB.

- **No oxide or hydride effects:** Real Zr cladding alloys develop oxide layers and hydrides during service; these are not considered in the model.

- **Not a refractory alloy study:** This paper concerns HCP Zr nuclear materials, not Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloy systems. The findings are specific to hexagonal close-packed Zr and may not be transferable to BCC refractory metals.
