# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Powder Preparation (Nano W Powder)
- Wet chemical method:
  - 40 g (NH₄)₁₀[H₂W₁₂O₄₂]·4H₂O (APT) dissolved in 240 mL distilled water
  - 30 mL concentrated nitric acid added, reacted 30 min under stirring and ultrasonic
  - 300 mL ethanol added, reacted 3 h
  - Filtered, washed with ethanol, vacuum dried at 70 °C for 20 h
  - Calcined in argon atmosphere at 480 °C for 1 h
  - Reduced in hydrogen atmosphere at 600 °C for 3 h and 800 °C for 6 h
  - Resulting particle size: 200–500 nm

## Powder Extrusion Printing (PEP)
- Printing device: UP-R200 (Shenghua 3D Co., Ltd.)
- Layer thickness: 50 μm
- Hatch distance: 200 μm
- Printing speed: 10 mm/s
- Printing strategy: zig-zag pattern
- Binder: water-soluble; powder-to-binder mass ratio 91.3:8.7

## Debinding
- Medium: deionized water
- Temperature: 55 °C
- Mass loss after debinding: 4.8 ± 0.3%

## Sintering (in flowing H₂, 99.999 vol%)
- Pre-sintering: heated slowly to 1400 °C
- Final sintering temperatures: 1650 °C, 2000 °C, 2300 °C
- Holding time: 6 h at each final temperature
- Atmosphere: flowing H₂
- Mass loss of pre-sintered samples at 1400 °C: 8.7 ± 0.4%
- Mass loss of samples sintered at 1650–2300 °C: ~8.7 ± 0.3% (close to binder content, indicating complete binder removal)

## LPBF (Comparative Samples)
- Equipment: Renishaw AM400, 400 W continuous Nd:YAG fiber laser
- Powder: spherical pure W, 15–45 μm
- Laser volumetric energy density: 600–1000 J/mm³ (optimal from previous work [34])
- Achieved relative density: 98.3 ± 0.3% (from previous work)
- No substrate preheating reported for LPBF samples in this study

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase**: BCC tungsten (JCPDS: 04-0806, lattice parameter a = 0.3165 nm)
- **Second phases**: None detected by XRD (no carbides or oxides of W detected)
- **Grain size**:
  - Sintered at 1400 °C: 0.71 ± 0.2 μm
  - Sintered at 1650 °C: 3.4 ± 0.5 μm
  - Sintered at 2000 °C: 9.8 ± 0.3 μm
  - Sintered at 2300 °C: 20.3 ± 0.5 μm
  - LPBF-prepared pure W: ~20 μm
- **Grain morphology**: Equiaxed grains at 2000 °C and 2300 °C (confirmed by IPF/EBSD)
- **Relative density**:
  - Sintered at 1400 °C: 50.5 ± 3%
  - Sintered at 1650 °C: 76.5 ± 5%
  - Sintered at 2000 °C: 99.1 ± 0.4%
  - Sintered at 2300 °C: 99.3 ± 0.4%
  - LPBF-prepared pure W: 98.3 ± 0.3% (from previous work [34])
  - Samples sintered at 2000 °C using micron-sized powder: 68.5 ± 5%
- **Dimensional shrinkage**:
  - Sintered at 1400 °C: 5.3 ± 0.4%
  - Sintered at 1650 °C: 5.3 ± 0.4%
  - Sintered at 2000 °C: 23.5 ± 0.4%
  - Sintered at 2300 °C: 23.6 ± 0.5%
- **Pores/sintered necks**: Abundant pores and sintered necks at 1400 °C and 1650 °C; no pores or impurity particles observed at 2000 °C and 2300 °C
- **Elemental segregation**: Not explicitly quantified; tungsten oxide impurities noted at grain boundaries of LPBF samples (from prior work [34])
- **Grain boundary features**: Grain boundaries in the sintered samples are described as more tortuous with smaller grain size, enhancing resistance to crack propagation
- **Microcracks**: Network-distributed microcracks along grain boundaries observed in LPBF-prepared pure W samples; no cracks in PEP-prepared samples sintered at 2000 °C and 2300 °C
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed**: Fine-grain strengthening (grain-boundary strengthening), high relative density (defect reduction); the paper explicitly discusses that higher strength is related to fine-grain strengthening and high relative density

## [Sintering Temperature (2000 °C)]

[Applicable Alloy System]
Pure tungsten (PEP-manufactured)

[Addition Amount or Composition Range]
Sintering temperature 2000 °C, holding time 6 h, flowing H₂ atmosphere

[Main Function]
Optimal sintering temperature to achieve high relative density (99.1 ± 0.4%), fine grain size (9.8 ± 0.3 μm), and highest compressive strength (1290 ± 10 MPa).

[Microstructural Mechanism]
At 2000 °C, complete densification occurs without excessive grain growth; equiaxed grains are formed; pore elimination is complete; no impurity particles observed. Grain boundaries are tortuous, increasing resistance to crack propagation.

[Property Effect]
- Compressive strength: 1290 ± 10 MPa (higher than 1138 ± 8 MPa for samples sintered at 2300 °C)
- Relative density: 99.1 ± 0.4%
- Grain size: 9.8 ± 0.3 μm
- Dimensional shrinkage: 23.5 ± 0.4%

[Potential Side Effects]
No significant side effects for 2000 °C sintering within the scope of this study. Sintering at higher temperature (2300 °C) leads to coarser grains (20.3 ± 0.5 μm) and lower compressive strength (1138 ± 8 MPa) despite slightly higher relative density (99.3 ± 0.4%).

[Original Evidence]
"The sintering temperature of the pure tungsten samples with the highest compressive strength (1290 ± 10 MPa) was determined to be 2000 °C." (Abstract). "Compared with other sintered samples, defect-free samples sintered at 2000 °C had finer grains (9.8 μm) while maintaining high relative density (99.1 ± 0.4%)." (Abstract)

## [Fine-Grain Strengthening in Pure W]

[Applicable Alloy System]
Pure tungsten (PEP-manufactured, sintered)

[Addition Amount or Composition Range]
Grain size: 9.8 ± 0.3 μm (2000 °C sintered) vs. 20.3 ± 0.5 μm (2300 °C sintered)

[Main Function]
Increases compressive strength by increasing grain boundary volume fraction and crack propagation resistance.

[Microstructural Mechanism]
Finer tungsten grains → higher volume fraction of grain boundaries → enhanced coordination of grain boundaries during compression deformation → avoidance of stress concentration → increased strength. Smaller grain size → more tortuous grain boundary → increased resistance to crack propagation → further strength increase.

[Property Effect]
- Compressive strength (9.8 μm grains): 1290 ± 10 MPa
- Compressive strength (20.3 μm grains): 1138 ± 8 MPa
- Testing conditions: room temperature, strain rate 1 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹

[Potential Side Effects]
Not explicitly discussed.

[Original Evidence]
"On the premise that the relative densities of samples sintered at 2000 and 2300 °C are similar and meet the requirements, the grain size is the dominating factor in determining the compressive strength. The finer the tungsten grains, the higher the volume fraction of grain boundaries. This would enhance the coordination of grain boundaries during compression deformation, thereby avoiding the occurrence of stress concentration and ultimately increasing the strength of the sample." (Section 3.2)

## [LPBF High Residual Stress + Oxide Impurities at Grain Boundaries]

[Applicable Alloy System]
Pure tungsten (LPBF direct AM)

[Addition Amount or Composition Range]
Not applicable (processing-induced phenomenon)

[Main Function]
Primary cause of microcrack formation in LPBF pure tungsten.

[Microstructural Mechanism]
Rapid and repeated localized heating/cooling cycles and high thermal gradients in LPBF induce high residual stresses. Tungsten oxide impurities at grain boundaries increase DBTT and reduce grain boundary strength. Combined effect of high residual stress + high DBTT + low grain boundary strength → microcrack formation. Maximum Von Mises residual stress exceeds yield stress, acting as critical stress for crack initiation and propagation.

[Property Effect]
- LPBF pure W relative density: 98.3 ± 0.3% (best achievable from prior work)
- Network-distributed microcracks along grain boundaries observed
- LPBF grain size: ~20 μm

[Potential Side Effects]
Microcracks are described as "common and unavoidable" in LPBF pure W; they are distributed along grain boundaries in a network pattern.

[Original Evidence]
"The rapid and repeated localized heating/cooling cycles and high thermal gradients brought about by LPBF can induce high residual stresses in metal samples. Due to the combined effect of high residual stress provided by LPBF and high DBTT and low grain boundary strength due to tungsten oxide impurities at grain boundaries, microcracks would form in pure tungsten samples prepared by LPBF." (Section 4, Discussion)

# Key Conclusions

1. **PEP is a feasible indirect AM method for pure tungsten**: Tungsten nanopowder (200–500 nm) was successfully used as precursor for powder extrusion printing to manufacture pure W green parts. After debinding (55 °C in deionized water) and sintering (flowing H₂, 6 h hold), defect-free, high-relative-density, fine-grained pure W parts were obtained.

2. **Optimal sintering temperature is 2000 °C**: At this temperature, samples achieved a relative density of 99.1 ± 0.4% and a fine grain size of 9.8 ± 0.3 μm. Sintering at higher temperature (2300 °C) yielded only marginally higher relative density (99.3 ± 0.4%) but significantly coarser grains (20.3 ± 0.5 μm), leading to lower strength.

3. **Nanopowder superiority over micron powder**: At the same sintering temperature (2000 °C), samples using nanopowder achieved 99.1 ± 0.4% relative density, while samples using micron-sized powder achieved only 68.5 ± 5% relative density. This is attributed to the higher surface energy and sintering activation of nanopowder, which promotes atomic diffusion, sintered neck formation, and grain boundary diffusion.

4. **Mechanical performance**: The highest compressive strength was 1290 ± 10 MPa for samples sintered at 2000 °C, compared to 1138 ± 8 MPa for samples sintered at 2300 °C. This strength is higher than that of pure W prepared by direct AM and traditional methods (as shown in Fig. 5b comparison). The dominating strengthening factor, given similar relative densities, is fine grain size.

5. **Crack avoidance via PEP**: Unlike LPBF pure W, which exhibits network-distributed microcracks along grain boundaries due to high residual stress, high DBTT, and low grain boundary strength (from tungsten oxide impurities), PEP-sintered samples showed no cracks. This is because PEP avoids the high thermal gradients and rapid heating/cooling cycles inherent to LPBF.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Pure tungsten only**: The study focuses exclusively on unalloyed pure tungsten. The findings may not be directly transferable to tungsten alloys or other refractory metal alloys without additional investigation.
- **Mechanical testing limited**: Only room-temperature compressive properties were measured. High-temperature compressive properties, tensile properties, hardness, fracture toughness, creep behavior, and other mechanical properties were not investigated.
- **Oxidation behavior not studied**: The paper does not evaluate high-temperature oxidation resistance or other service stability issues (e.g., oxygen/nitrogen embrittlement) of the PEP-manufactured pure tungsten parts.
- **No microstructural anisotropy analysis**: While LPBF samples are mentioned to have microstructural anisotropy, the paper does not provide detailed crystallographic texture or anisotropy data for either LPBF or PEP samples.
- **Post-sintering heat treatment not explored**: The study does not investigate the effect of post-sintering heat treatments, annealing, or hot isostatic pressing on the final properties.
- **Impurity analysis limited**: While tungsten oxide impurities are mentioned at grain boundaries of LPBF samples, quantitative impurity analysis (ppm-level C, O, N, H) for the PEP-sintered samples is not provided.
- **Comparative data derived from literature**: Compressive strength values for direct AM and traditional methods are taken from references and presented graphically (Fig. 5b); exact numerical values from those references are not tabulated in this paper.
- **Creep and high-temperature performance not evaluated**: Given tungsten's typical applications as plasma-facing material or high-temperature structural component, the absence of creep data and elevated-temperature mechanical property data is a notable gap.
- **Only one binder system used**: The water-soluble binder system with a fixed powder-to-binder ratio (91.3:8.7) was used; other binder systems may yield different debinding/sintering behavior.
- **Substrate preheating effects in LPBF not directly studied in this work**: The paper references prior literature that substrate preheating (673 K and 1273 K) did not eliminate microcracks in LPBF W, but this study itself did not vary LPBF preheating parameters.
