# Paper Metadata
- **Title**: Theoretical study of mechanical and thermodynamic properties of W–Fe alloys: Promising ultra-high temperature alloy materials
- **Authors**: Meiyun Zhang, Xunjie Wang, Yun Chen, Houdao Cai, Hongying Li, Diyou Jiang
- **Journal**: Vacuum
- **Year**: 2024
- **DOI**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.vacuum.2024.113047
- **Alloy system studied**: W–Fe binary alloys
- **Research objective**: To predict the mechanical and thermodynamic properties of W–Fe alloys using first-principles calculations, and to assess whether Fe doping can improve ductility, high-temperature bulk modulus, thermal expansion behavior, and thermal conductivity of tungsten for ultra-high temperature and nuclear fusion applications.
- **Material category**: W-based alloy (binary W–Fe alloy); computationally designed refractory alloy for plasma-facing and high-temperature applications.

# Alloy System and Research Object

This is a **computational/theoretical study** based on first-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations. No experimental alloy fabrication or mechanical testing is reported.

- **Alloy system**: W–Fe binary substitutional solid solutions with cubic crystal structure.
- **Structures studied**:
  - Pure W
  - W15Fe1 alloy (6.25 at.% Fe)
  - W14Fe2 alloy (12.5 at.% Fe)
  - W13Fe3 alloy (18.75 at.% Fe)

The W13Fe3 alloy was found to be dynamically unstable and was excluded from mechanical and thermodynamic property analyses.

**Not applicable.** This is a purely computational study using density functional theory. No experimental processing, melting, powder metallurgy, heat treatment, mechanical alloying, additive manufacturing, rolling, forging, or annealing procedures are described.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase**: BCC (body-centered cubic) structure. All three W–Fe alloy models retain cubic crystal symmetry after optimization.
- **Second phases/precipitates**: None. The models assume complete substitutional solid solution of Fe in the W lattice.
- **Grain size / grain boundaries / segregation**: Not applicable; this is a single-crystal DFT model without grain boundaries.
- **Elemental ordering**: Fe atoms are found to prefer a uniform and ordered W lattice position in the supercell, indicating that W and Fe can form ordered substitutional solid solutions in the computational model. The paper tested disordered configurations at 12.5 at.% Fe and found that the ordered configuration has lower total energy than disordered configurations.
- **Lattice constant**:
  - Pure W: a = b = c = 3.176 Å (calculated), 3.170 Å (reference [53]), 3.1652 Å (experimental [54])
  - W15Fe1: 3.157 Å
  - W14Fe2: 3.136 Å
  - Lattice constant decreases with increasing Fe content because Fe has a smaller atomic radius than W.
- **Cell volume**:
  - Pure W: 32.03 Å³
  - W15Fe1: 31.46 Å³
  - W14Fe2: 31.08 Å³
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed**: This paper does not discuss conventional experimental strengthening mechanisms such as grain-boundary strengthening, precipitation strengthening, or ODS strengthening. Instead, it analyzes intrinsic alloy behavior at 0 K and elevated temperatures through elastic constants, electronic structure, and lattice dynamics.

## High-Temperature Bulk Modulus

- **W15Fe1**: Below 870 K, bulk modulus decreases with temperature and is lower than pure W. Above 870 K, bulk modulus rapidly increases with temperature and becomes significantly greater than pure W, exhibiting high-temperature alloy characteristics.
- **W14Fe2**: Bulk modulus decreases with temperature and is significantly lower than pure W at all temperatures. Excessive Fe doping (12.5 at.%) reduces high-temperature bulk modulus.

## Service Stability Issues

- The paper mentions that W has poor radiation stability, high yield strength, low ductility, poor fracture toughness, low-temperature brittleness, and high ductile-brittle transition temperature as motivating issues for alloying.
- The W–Fe alloy is noted as an important connection component between the first wall armor material and structural steel in fusion reactors; the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients is critical for joint stability.
- No direct discussion of high-temperature embrittlement, oxygen embrittlement, nitrogen embrittlement, or grain-boundary oxidation is provided in the context of the W–Fe alloys studied.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Fe in W–Fe Alloys

- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: W–Fe binary alloys, W-based alloys for nuclear fusion plasma-facing components and ultra-high temperature applications.
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 6.25 at.% Fe (W15Fe1); 12.5 at.% Fe (W14Fe2); 18.75 at.% Fe (W13Fe3) was also modeled but found dynamically unstable.
- **[Main Function]**: Ductility improvement; potential improvement of high-temperature bulk modulus and thermal conductivity at appropriate levels; reduction of thermal expansion at high temperatures.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**:
  - Fe substitution reduces lattice constant due to smaller atomic radius.
  - Fe changes electronic structure: conduction band shifts left with increasing Fe, valence band widens, electron delocalization enhances.
  - PDOS analysis shows W–Fe bonding interaction; W15Fe1 has stronger W–Fe bonding than W14Fe2 at the Fermi level.
  - Bader charge analysis: Fe gains charge (electronegativity Fe 1.8 > W 1.7); W atoms near Fe lose charge.
- **[Property Effect]**:
  - At 0 K: Yield/bulk modulus decreases with Fe addition (B: 315.4 → 308.1 → 302.5 GPa for 0, 6.25, 12.5 at.% Fe); shear modulus decreases (146.7 → 136.2 → 130.3 GPa); Young's modulus decreases (381.0 → 356.0 → 341.8 GPa); hardness decreases (13.17 → 11.79 → 11.09 GPa).
  - Ductility indicators improve: B/G increases from 2.150 to 2.263 to 2.322; Poisson's ratio increases from 0.299 to 0.307 to 0.312; Cauchy pressure increases from 35.45 to 40.57 to 42.70 GPa.
  - At high temperature (>870 K), W15Fe1 shows bulk modulus higher than pure W; W14Fe2 remains below pure W.
  - Thermal expansion: W15Fe1 is significantly lower than pure W above 1400 K; W14Fe2 is higher than pure W.
  - Thermal conductivity at 2300 K: W15Fe1 exceeds pure W; W14Fe2 is significantly lower.
  - Melting point decreases with Fe addition (3646.6 → 3509.4 → 3348.8 K).
- **[Potential Side Effects]**:
  - Reduced 0 K mechanical strength (bulk, shear, Young's modulus, hardness).
  - Reduced melting point.
  - Decreased Debye temperature (weakened interatomic bonding).
  - At 12.5 at.% Fe, high-temperature bulk modulus decreases, thermal conductivity remains low, and thermal expansion increases, which are unfavorable.
  - W13Fe3 (18.75 at.% Fe) is dynamically unstable.
- **[Original Evidence]**:
  - Table 3 (elastic constants and mechanical parameters); Fig. 3 (bulk modulus vs. temperature); Fig. 6 (thermal expansion, Grüneisen constant, thermal conductivity); Table 5 (Debye temperature); Section 3.2 (formation enthalpy and dynamic stability); Section 3.6 (electronic structure).

## W in W–Fe Alloys

- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: W–Fe binary alloys, W-based alloys for plasma-facing materials.
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 93.75 at.% in W15Fe1; 87.5 at.% in W14Fe2; 81.25 at.% in W13Fe3.
- **[Main Function]**: Provides high melting point, high-temperature strength, low thermal expansion, high thermal conductivity, low sputtering yield, low hydrogen isotope storage.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**: W forms the BCC matrix; provides strong atomic bonding reflected in high Debye temperature; dominates electronic DOS near the Fermi level.
- **[Property Effect]**:
  - Strength: higher bulk/shear/Young's modulus with increasing W content.
  - Hardness: higher with more W.
  - Melting point: higher with more W.
  - Thermal conductivity at room temperature: pure W 49.57 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹.
- **[Potential Side Effects]**: Poor radiation stability, high yield strength, low ductility, poor fracture toughness, low-temperature brittleness, high ductile-brittle transition temperature.
- **[Original Evidence]**: Introduction Section; Table 3; Table 5; Fig. 6.

## W + 6.25 at.% Fe Combination (W15Fe1)

- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: W–Fe binary alloy.
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 6.25 at.% Fe.
- **[Main Function]**: Optimized balance between ductility improvement and retention of high-temperature performance.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**: Fe atoms ordered in W lattice; strongest W–Fe bonding among the compositions studied; moderate conduction band shift enhancing electron delocalization.
- **[Property Effect]**:
  - Bulk modulus above 870 K exceeds pure W.
  - Thermal expansion coefficient significantly lower than pure W above 1400 K.
  - Thermal conductivity exceeds pure W above 2300 K.
  - Grüneisen constant: 1.75–1.93.
  - Ductility improved relative to pure W (B/G = 2.263; ν = 0.307).
- **[Potential Side Effects]**: "Noise" phenomenon in thermal expansion, Grüneisen constant, and thermal conductivity at high temperatures due to non-uniform atomic distribution; reduced 0 K strength; reduced melting point to 3509.4 K; lower room-temperature thermal conductivity (36.37 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ at 300 K vs. 49.57 for pure W).
- **[Original Evidence]**: Fig. 3; Fig. 6; Table 3; Table 5; Section 3.4.

## W + 12.5 at.% Fe Combination (W14Fe2)

- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: W–Fe binary alloy.
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 12.5 at.% Fe.
- **[Main Function]**: Further ductility improvement but with degraded high-temperature and thermal properties.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**: Fe ordered in W lattice with weaker W–Fe bonding than W15Fe1; larger lattice contraction.
- **[Property Effect]**:
  - Ductility highest among the studied compositions (B/G = 2.322; ν = 0.312; C′ = 42.70 GPa).
  - Bulk modulus lower than pure W and W15Fe1 at all temperatures.
  - Thermal expansion coefficient increases rapidly with temperature, higher than pure W and W15Fe1.
  - Thermal conductivity lowest among the three (34.24 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ at 300 K; 1.76 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ at 2600 K).
  - Grüneisen constant: 1.76–2.90.
  - No "noise" phenomenon during thermal expansion.
- **[Potential Side Effects]**: High-temperature bulk modulus degradation; lowest thermal conductivity; highest thermal expansion; reduced melting point to 3348.8 K; higher formation enthalpy (0.117 eV/atom).
- **[Original Evidence]**: Fig. 3; Fig. 6; Table 2; Table 3; Table 5; Section 3.4.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Softening/Weakening (Fe Substitution in W)

- **[Related elements]**: Fe substitution for W.
- **[Typical alloys]**: W–Fe binary alloys (W15Fe1, W14Fe2).
- **[Key compositions]**: 6.25 at.% Fe; 12.5 at.% Fe.
- **[Microstructural changes]**: Fe substitution decreases lattice constant; changes electronic structure; weakens W–W bonding network; reduces density.
- **[Property improvements]**: Improved ductility (higher B/G, Poisson's ratio, Cauchy pressure); improved anisotropy; potential suppression of thermal expansion and enhancement of high-temperature bulk modulus at optimal Fe content (6.25 at.%).
- **[Risks and limitations]**: Reduced 0 K strength, hardness, and melting point; excessive Fe (12.5 at.%) degrades high-temperature bulk modulus and thermal conductivity; 18.75 at.% Fe leads to dynamic instability.

## Ductility Improvement via Alloying

- **[Related elements]**: Fe; also noted in the introduction for Cr, Ti, Hf, Zr, V, Ta, Co, Tc, Mo, Re.
- **[Typical alloys]**: W–Fe alloys; W–Re alloys (noted for reducing ductile-brittle transition temperature).
- **[Key compositions]**: 6.25 at.% Fe (W15Fe1); 12.5 at.% Fe (W14Fe2).
- **[Microstructural changes]**: Increase in metallic bonding character (Cauchy pressure > 0); enhanced electron delocalization; reduced covalent character.
- **[Property improvements]**: B/G increases from 2.150 (pure W) to 2.322 (W14Fe2); Poisson's ratio increases from 0.299 to 0.312; Cauchy pressure increases from 35.45 to 42.70 GPa.
- **[Risks and limitations]**: Trade-off with reduced strength and melting point; Fe alloying alone may not address all issues such as radiation stability and fracture toughness.

## High-Temperature Bulk Modulus Enhancement (Optimal Fe Doping)

- **[Related elements]**: Fe at low concentration.
- **[Typical alloys]**: W15Fe1 (6.25 at.% Fe).
- **[Key compositions]**: 6.25 at.% Fe.
- **[Microstructural changes]**: Strong W–Fe bonding; moderate lattice contraction; electronic contribution to free energy is about one-third of phonon contribution.
- **[Property improvements]**: Bulk modulus exceeds pure W above 870 K, reaching significantly higher values at elevated temperatures.
- **[Risks and limitations]**: "Noise" in high-temperature thermodynamic properties due to non-uniform Fe distribution; effect not observed in W14Fe2 (12.5 at.% Fe), indicating composition sensitivity.

## Thermal Expansion Suppression at High Temperatures

- **[Related elements]**: Fe at optimal content.
- **[Typical alloys]**: W15Fe1 (6.25 at.% Fe).
- **[Key compositions]**: 6.25 at.% Fe.
- **[Microstructural changes]**: Nonlinear inward thermal expansion behavior; self-limitation due to local non-uniform temperature distribution.
- **[Property improvements]**: Thermal expansion coefficient lower than pure W above 1400 K; exhibits low expansion behavior at high temperatures.
- **[Risks and limitations]**: Only observed for 6.25 at.% Fe; 12.5 at.% Fe shows higher thermal expansion than pure W.

## Thermal Conductivity Enhancement at Ultra-High Temperatures

- **[Related elements]**: Fe at optimal content.
- **[Typical alloys]**: W15Fe1 (6.25 at.% Fe).
- **[Key compositions]**: 6.25 at.% Fe.
- **[Microstructural changes]**: Modified Grüneisen constant and Debye temperature.
- **[Property improvements]**: Thermal conductivity exceeds pure W above 2300 K.
- **[Risks and limitations]**: Lower thermal conductivity at room and moderate temperatures; W14Fe2 shows significantly lower thermal conductivity at all temperatures.

# Key Conclusions

1. **Dynamic stability**: W15Fe1 and W14Fe2 are dynamically stable; W13Fe3 is dynamically unstable (imaginary phonon frequencies). Structural stability weakens with increasing Fe content, as indicated by increasing formation enthalpy (0.068 eV/atom for W15Fe1; 0.117 eV/atom for W14Fe2).

2. **High-temperature bulk modulus**: Doping 6.25 at.% Fe in W promotes bulk modulus enhancement above 870 K, exhibiting ultra-strong high-temperature alloy characteristics. Excessive Fe (12.5 at.%) reduces high-temperature bulk modulus.

3. **Thermal expansion**: W15Fe1 shows thermal expansion coefficient lower than pure W above 1400 K, with simultaneous "noise" phenomenon. W14Fe2 shows rapidly increasing thermal expansion coefficient with temperature, with no "noise." 6.25 at.% Fe can suppress thermal expansion at high temperatures; 12.5 at.% Fe suppresses the "noise" but increases expansion.

4. **Thermal conductivity**: Thermal conductivity decreases with increasing Fe content at low temperatures; W15Fe1 exceeds pure W above 2300 K. At 2600 K: pure W = 4.51 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹, W15Fe1 = 4.78 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹, W14Fe2 = 1.76 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹. At 300 K: pure W = 49.57 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹, W15Fe1 = 36.37 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹, W14Fe2 = 34.24 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹.

5. **0 K mechanical properties**: Fe doping reduces mechanical strength, melting point, and hardness, but improves ductility (B/G, ν, C′) and anisotropy.

6. **Grüneisen constant**: W15Fe1 = 1.75–1.93; W14Fe2 = 1.76–2.90; follows same trend as thermal expansion.

7. **Oxygen surface interaction**: Bridge site has the lowest O adsorption energy on W and W–Fe (100) surfaces, being most sensitive to O.

8. **Electronic contribution**: Electronic contribution to free energy is about one-third of phonon contribution in W–Fe alloys, and cannot be ignored.

9. **Debye temperature**: Decreases with increasing Fe content (365 → 360 → 359 K), indicating weakened atomic bonding, but remains high, suggesting strong interatomic bonding.

10. **Ordered vs. disordered Fe distribution**: Total energy of ordered W14Fe2 configuration (−1562.788 eV) is lower than disordered configurations. Fe atom distribution does not significantly affect mechanical properties; disordered configurations yield mechanical parameters very close to the ordered configuration.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Computational study only**: All properties are predicted by first-principles DFT calculations using the PW91 functional and VASP. No experimental validation is provided for the W–Fe alloys studied. The authors explicitly state that all calculated properties need experimental verification.

- **Temperature range**: Thermodynamic properties calculated from 0 to 3000 K. Mechanical properties at 0 K only; no finite-temperature mechanical properties, creep, or fracture toughness are provided.

- **Model limitations**: 2 × 2 × 2 supercell with 16 atoms; only ordered Fe configurations primarily considered; no grain boundaries, dislocations, vacancies, or other defects; no consideration of Fe precipitates or two-phase microstructures.

- **Functional limitations**: GGA-PW91 underestimates lattice constants, which introduces errors in elastic constants. C44 and shear modulus are lower than experimental values due to this functional limitation.

- **No experimental processing or microstructure**: No arc melting, powder metallurgy, heat treatment, or other processing routes are discussed. No grain size, precipitate, or segregation data.

- **Oxidation resistance**: Only a brief O adsorption energy study on (100) surfaces; no oxide scale formation, oxidation kinetics, or long-term service stability data.

- **Applicable scope**: The conclusions are most relevant for predicting intrinsic alloying effects of Fe in W-based solid solutions for ultra-high temperature and nuclear fusion applications, particularly for guidance on optimal Fe content for ductility improvement, high-temperature bulk modulus, thermal expansion suppression, and thermal conductivity.

- **Not covered**: Radiation stability, actual ductile-brittle transition temperature measurements, fracture toughness, experimental creep, and actual joining to structural steel are not addressed.
