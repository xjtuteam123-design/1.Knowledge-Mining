# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Microstructure and Mechanical Properties of a Fiber Welded Socket-Joint Made of Powder Metallurgy Molybdenum Alloy
- **Authors**: Miao-Xia Xie, Yan-Xin Li, Xiang-Tao Shang, Xue-Wu Wang, Jun-Yu Pei
- **Journal**: Metals
- **Year**: 2019
- **DOI**: 10.3390/met9060640
- **Alloy system studied**: Nanostructured high-performance molybdenum alloy (NS Mo) strengthened by 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃ nanoparticles
- **Research objective**: To investigate the role of welding heat input in fiber laser welding of socket joints made of powder metallurgy NS Mo alloy, specifically focusing on the effects on microstructure, grain boundary segregation, and mechanical properties.
- **Material category**: Powder metallurgy Mo-based alloy (La₂O₃ dispersion-strengthened molybdenum alloy, ODS-type Mo alloy)

# Alloy System and Research Object

The base material is a newly developed nanostructured molybdenum alloy (NS-Mo) strengthened by nano-doping. It was developed by Liu et al. (2013, Nature Materials) and selected as a candidate material for cladding tubes in nuclear fuel applications. The alloy is produced through powder metallurgy followed by multi-pass hot rolling.

Key material characteristics:
- Compactness: approximately 99.6%
- Strengthening phase: 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃ nanoparticles dispersed on the Mo matrix
- Base metal room-temperature tensile strength: 718 MPa
- Base metal room-temperature elongation: approximately 40% (as referenced from Liu et al.)
- Base metal microhardness: 250–260 HV
- Manufacturing: powder metallurgy and multi-pass hot rolling; the tube was strengthened by 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃ nanoparticles with preformation and multiple rolling. The end plug was also produced by powder metallurgy and hot rolling but with a less complex process than the tube.

# Alloy Composition

The original paper does not provide a full chemical composition breakdown in at.% or wt.% for the NS Mo alloy except for:

- **La**: 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃ (as strengthening nanoparticles)
- **Mo**: Balance (matrix)

The paper does not report ppm-level impurities (C, O, N, H) of the base metal. However, oxygen segregation at grain boundaries is discussed in detail after welding, with MoO₂ identified as the segregated phase.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Base Metal Processing
- **Powder metallurgy** followed by hot rolling for both the tube and end plug
- Tube manufacturing process: preformation and multiple rolling
- Compactness of NS Mo: approximately 99.6%
- The special liquid-liquid mixing preparation method was employed to realize dispersed distribution of nano-sized La₂O₃ particles
- NS Mo is also a work-hardened material (contains dislocations from processing)

## Welding Process
- **Fiber laser continuous welding (CW)** using an IPG YLS-4000 system
- Maximum output: 4 kW
- Central wavelength: 1070 nm
- Focal spot size: 0.2 mm in diameter
- Focus position: 1 mm above the workpiece surface
- Shielding gas: Argon
- Preheating: Resistance wire heating to approximately 450 °C before welding

## Welding Parameters

| Sample | Laser Power (kW) | Welding Speed (cm/min) | Defocusing Distance (mm) | Heat Input (J/cm) |
|--------|-----------------|------------------------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| Joint 1 | 2.5 | 600 | 1 | 250 |
| Joint 2 | 1.2 | 20 | 1 | 3600 |

Notably, Joint 1 used a high welding speed (600 cm/min = 6 m/min) with low heat input (250 J/cm), while Joint 2 used a low welding speed (20 cm/min = 0.2 m/min) with high heat input (3600 J/cm). The welding speed ratio was 30:1 while the laser power ratio was only about 2:1, meaning heat input was essentially controlled by welding speed.

Welding time for Joint 1: approximately 0.29 s (very short).

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Base Metal Microstructure
- Fine grains with rolled morphology (visible in Figure 1)
- Nanoparticles of La₂O₃ dispersed on the Mo matrix
- Matrix phase: BCC (molybdenum, implied, not explicitly stated in this paper)
- Work-hardened structure (dislocations present from processing)

## Fusion Zone (FZ) Microstructure
- **Joint 1 (Low heat input, 250 J/cm)**:
  - Mainly slender columnar grains
  - Average grain size: approximately 42 µm (standard deviation: 15 µm)
  - HAZ contained recrystallized equiaxed crystals

- **Joint 2 (High heat input, 3600 J/cm)**:
  - Mixed morphology of coarse columnar crystals and coarse equiaxed crystals
  - Average grain size: approximately 131 µm (standard deviation: 40 µm)
  - HAZ appeared as significantly coarse recrystallization microstructures

The equiaxed crystal morphology observed in the FZ of both joints was more likely the cross-section of curved columnar crystals. Columnar grains grew along the direction of maximum temperature gradient (perpendicular to the rear edge of the molten pool). At high welding speed (Joint 1), the molten pool was prolonged, causing columnar grains to grow along the horizontal direction. At low welding speed (Joint 2), the large curvature of the rear edge of the molten pool allowed columnar crystals to develop along the welding direction.

## Grain Boundary Segregation
- **MoO₂ segregation** was identified at grain boundaries in both joints via TEM analysis
- Joint 2 (high heat input) showed more serious MoO₂ segregation than Joint 1
- EDS confirmed that oxygen contents at grain boundaries were higher than those in grain interiors in both joints
- Oxygen content at GBs in Joint 2 was significantly higher than in Joint 1
- Mechanism: Reduced total grain boundary area in coarse-grained Joint 2 led to aggravated segregation of MoO₂

## Porosity Defects
- Both number of porosity defects and pore sizes in the FZ of Joint 2 were larger than those of Joint 1

# Mechanical Properties

## Room-Temperature Tensile Strength

| Sample | Tensile Strength (MPa) | Percentage of BM Strength |
|--------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Base Metal (BM) | 718 | 100% |
| Joint 1 (250 J/cm) | 568 | 79% of BM |
| Joint 2 (3600 J/cm) | 252 | 35% of BM |

- Tensile test conditions: room temperature, tensile rate of 0.5 mm/min, CSS-88100 electronic testing machine
- Both joints fractured in the FZ without plastic deformation before failure
- Joint 1 fractured along the center line of the FZ
- Joint 2 fractured along the FZ-HAZ interface at the tube side

## Microhardness
- BM microhardness: 250–260 HV (measured under 100 gf load, 30 s holding time)
- Both joints showed softened heated zones
- Minimum hardness occurred at the FZ-HAZ interface in both joints
- Minimum microhardness of the softened zone of both joints was similar and was less than BM by about 20%
- Joint 1 had a considerably narrower softened zone than Joint 2
- FZ hardness was significantly decreased and lower than the HAZ and BM, attributed to the decomposition of La₂O₃ during welding (La₂O₃ melting point: 2207 °C, lower than Mo melting point: 2622 °C)
- HAZ softening attributed to decreased number of dislocations after the welding thermal cycle (annealing effect)

## Fracture Morphology
- **Joint 1**: Mixed intergranular and transgranular fracture
  - Cleavage fracture (river-like patterns): approximately 70% of total fracture area (at 0°, 60°, 120°, 300° positions)
  - Intergranular fracture: approximately 30% of total fracture area (at 80° and 140° positions)

- **Joint 2**: Mixed intergranular and transgranular fracture
  - Cleavage fracture (river-like patterns): approximately 50% of total fracture area (at 30° and 90° positions)
  - Intergranular fracture: approximately 50% of total fracture area, attributed to severe MoO₂ segregation at GBs

## Weld Geometry
- Joint 1 (low heat input): High aspect ratio of FZ; uniform widths and depths of FZ over the whole circumference
- Joint 2 (high heat input): Low aspect ratio of FZ; width and depth of FZ increased gradually along the welding direction due to heat accumulation

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

The paper does not directly address oxidation resistance of the alloy in service conditions. However, it does address oxygen-related embrittlement during welding:

- **Oxygen embrittlement**: MoO₂ segregation at grain boundaries is identified as a critical factor causing poor welded joint strength
- MoO₂ melting point: 2300 °C
- Large numbers of porosity defects and larger pore sizes were found in high heat input joint (Joint 2)
- No protective oxide layer was discussed

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## La₂O₃

**Element or Element Combination**: La₂O₃ (Lanthanum oxide nanoparticles)

**Applicable Alloy System**: NS Mo (nanostructured molybdenum alloy), powder metallurgy Mo-based ODS alloy

**Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃

**Main Function**: Oxide dispersion strengthening (ODS) of molybdenum matrix; provides dispersed nanoparticles that enhance mechanical properties at room temperature

**Microstructural Mechanism**: Nano-sized La₂O₃ particles dispersed on the Mo matrix; special liquid-liquid mixing preparation method was used to achieve dispersed distribution of nano-sized La₂O₃ particles

**Property Effect**: The base metal with 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃ achieved a tensile strength of approximately 718 MPa and elongation of approximately 40% at room temperature. Microhardness of 250–260 HV.

**Potential Side Effects**: During fusion welding (laser welding), La₂O₃ particles decompose because La₂O₃ melting point (2207 °C) is lower than that of Mo (2622 °C). This releases oxygen into the molten pool, allowing MoO₂ to form and segregate at grain boundaries, weakening the joint.

**Original Evidence**: "The tube was strengthened by the 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃ nanoparticles dispersed on the Mo matrix" (Section 2); "Owing to the melting point of La₂O₃ (i.e., 2207 °C) being lower than that of Mo (i.e., 2622 °C), La₂O₃ particles in the FZ will decompound during the laser welding process. Thus, the microhardness of the FZ was significantly decreased and lower than those of the HAZ and BM." (Section 3.3)

## MoO₂

**Element or Element Combination**: MoO₂ (Molybdenum dioxide)

**Applicable Alloy System**: Mo-based alloys welded joints, particularly NS Mo with La₂O₃ doping

**Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not applicable (unintended byproduct of welding)

**Main Function**: Negative phase—causes grain boundary weakening and embrittlement in welded joints

**Microstructural Mechanism**: MoO₂ segregates on grain boundaries after laser welding due to its lower melting point (2300 °C). During welding, La₂O₃ decomposes, releasing oxygen that reacts with Mo to form MoO₂. This compound has a high probability of gathering on grain boundaries. Coarser grains (from high heat input) reduce total GB area, thereby causing more excessive MoO₂ aggregation at GBs.

**Property Effect**: The tensile strength of welded joints decreased from 568 MPa (low heat input, 250 J/cm) to 252 MPa (high heat input, 3600 J/cm). Intergranular fracture proportion increased from approximately 30% to 50% with increased MoO₂ segregation. The minimum microhardness decreased by about 20% compared to BM.

**Potential Side Effects**: Grain boundary embrittlement, reduced joint strength, transition from cleavage fracture to intergranular fracture

**Original Evidence**: "MoO₂ was generated and had a great probability to gather on GBs, due to its lower melting point (2300 °C). Previous research already showed that the discontinuous distribution of MoO₂ at the GBs of the FZ was the main reason causing a reduction of the strengths of Mo alloy joints" (Section 3.4.1); "Thermal analysis (TEM) results confirmed the MoO₂ segregation at grain boundaries... The segregation of joint 2 was more serious." (Section 3.4.2)

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Oxide Dispersion Strengthening (ODS)

**Related Elements**: La₂O₃ nanoparticles

**Typical Alloys**: NS Mo (nanostructured molybdenum alloy)

**Key Compositions**: 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃ dispersed in Mo matrix

**Microstructural Changes**: Nano-sized La₂O₃ particles dispersed on the Mo matrix via special liquid-liquid mixing preparation method

**Property Improvements**: Base metal tensile strength of 718 MPa and elongation of about 40% at room temperature; microhardness of 250–260 HV

**Risks and Limitations**: During fusion welding, La₂O₃ decomposes (due to lower melting point than Mo), releasing oxygen that forms MoO₂ which segregates to grain boundaries, weakening welded joints significantly. The ODS strengthening mechanism is destroyed in the fusion zone.

**Original Evidence**: "The NS Mo alloy developed by Liu et al. is strengthened by nano-doping and has a strength of about 700 MPa and an elongation of about 40% at room temperature." (Introduction); "the microhardness of the FZ was significantly decreased... La₂O₃ particles in the FZ will decompound during the laser welding process."

## Grain Boundary Strengthening (via Grain Refinement)

**Related Elements**: None (process-controlled)

**Typical Alloys**: NS Mo alloy welded joints

**Key Compositions**: Not applicable (composition unchanged)

**Microstructural Changes**: Low heat input (250 J/cm) produced fine grains (42 µm) while high heat input (3600 J/cm) produced coarse grains (131 µm). Finer grains increase total grain boundary area, thereby reducing the intensity of MoO₂ segregation per unit area of GB.

**Property Improvements**: Joint 1 (42 µm grains) had a tensile strength of 568 MPa (79% of BM) compared to 252 MPa (35% of BM) for Joint 2 (131 µm grains). Finer grains reduced intergranular fracture proportion from 50% to about 30%.

**Risks and Limitations**: Grain refinement cannot fully prevent MoO₂ segregation—reduced segregation intensity is still present and causes joint weakening compared to the base metal.

**Original Evidence**: "The coarse grains in joint 2 caused by higher heat input would lead to the total area of GBs being reduced, and consequently, aggravate the segregation of MoO₂ at GBs." (Section 3.4.2)

## Work Hardening

**Related Elements**: Not applicable

**Typical Alloys**: NS Mo base metal (hot-rolled powder metallurgy material)

**Key Compositions**: 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃ doped Mo

**Microstructural Changes**: Hot rolling introduced dislocations and fine grains with rolled morphology into the Mo matrix

**Property Improvements**: Base metal tensile strength of 718 MPa

**Risks and Limitations**: During welding, the welding thermal cycle causes annealing of the HAZ, reducing dislocation density and causing softening (minimum hardness about 20% lower than BM)

**Original Evidence**: "The NS Mo BM considered in this work is also a type of work-hardened material. Therefore, softening of the HAZ in the welded joints might be largely due to the decreased number of dislocations after the welding thermal cycle." (Section 3.3)

# Key Conclusions

1. **Heat input inversely correlates with joint strength**: When welding heat input decreased from 3600 J/cm (1.2 kW, 20 cm/min) to 250 J/cm (2.5 kW, 600 cm/min), the tensile strength of welded joints increased from approximately 250 MPa to approximately 570 MPa.

2. **Grain refinement from low heat input**: Lower heat input (i.e., higher welding speed) produced significantly refined grains in the fusion zone—approximately 42 µm versus 131 µm for high heat input.

3. **MoO₂ grain boundary segregation is the primary cause of joint weakness**: MoO₂ segregation at grain boundaries was identified as the most critical factor degrading the strength of Mo alloy welded joints. Higher heat input produced coarser grains, reducing total grain boundary area and aggravating MoO₂ segregation.

4. **Intergranular fracture increases with MoO₂ segregation**: Joint 2 (high heat input) displayed approximately 50% intergranular fracture area compared to about 30% for Joint 1 (low heat input).

5. **Softening occurs in all weld zones**: Both FZ and HAZ of both joints were softened during welding. The minimum microhardness in the softened zone of both joints was less than that of BM by approximately 20%. FZ softening is attributed to La₂O₃ decomposition; HAZ softening is attributed to reduced dislocation density.

6. **Low heat input improves weld quality**: Joint 1 (low heat input) produced more uniform weld geometry with regular and smooth surface morphology. Joint 2 (high heat input) showed significant spattering and gradually increasing weld width and depth along the welding direction due to heat accumulation.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. **Preliminary nature of results**: The authors explicitly state that "the results are rather preliminary and there is still a long way to go before applying it in industry."

2. **Limited parameter range**: Only two welding parameter combinations were studied (250 J/cm and 3600 J/cm). A broader range of heat inputs would provide more comprehensive understanding.

3. **Specific alloy system**: The study focuses on a specific nanostructured molybdenum alloy (NS Mo with 0.5 wt.% La₂O₃). The findings may not be directly transferable to other Mo alloys, such as TZM, or other refractory alloys.

4. **No oxidation resistance quantification**: The paper does not provide quantitative measurement of oxygen content or oxidation weight gain in the base metal or welded joints.

5. **No high-temperature mechanical testing**: All mechanical property evaluations were performed at room temperature. The paper does not provide high-temperature strength, creep, or rupture data for the welded joints.

6. **Not addressed**: The paper does not discuss the effect of preheating temperature (450 °C) on the results, nor does it explore post-weld heat treatment as a potential mitigation strategy.

7. **Sample size and reproducibility**: The paper does not explicitly state the number of replicate tests performed, though joint positions (0°, 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°) were used for microstructural characterization.
