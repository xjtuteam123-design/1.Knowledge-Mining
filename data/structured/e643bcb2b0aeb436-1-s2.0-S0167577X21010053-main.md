# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system:** Ti-Nb-O ternary alloys, with martensitic microstructure after solution treatment and water quenching.
- **Nb content range:** 15 to 22.5 wt.%
- **Oxygen content range:** 0.15 to 0.40 wt.%
- **Material category:** Martensitic Ti alloy (Ti-Nb-O with α″ orthorhombic martensite). Not a refractory alloy; not a high-entropy alloy; not an ODS alloy; not additively manufactured.
- **Research objective:** Investigate whether oxygen can increase mechanical strength while maintaining a low Young's modulus in martensitic Ti-Nb alloys with relatively low Nb contents, targeting biomedical implant applications with reduced stress shielding.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase:** Predominantly orthorhombic α″ martensite in all ST/WQ alloys. The transition from hexagonal α′ martensite to α″ occurs at a Nb content lower than 15 wt% (the paper states the transition took place close to 14 wt% Nb according to Bönisch et al. [8]).
- **Second phases / retained phases:** 
  - Retained β phase and ω_ath (athermal omega) phase were identified in Ti-20Nb-xO and Ti-22.5Nb-xO alloys only.
  - In Ti-15Nb and Ti-17.5Nb alloys, β phase was not found; however, the paper cautions that XRD may fail to detect very low volume fractions, and TEM foils are small, so the absence of β is not absolutely conclusive.
- **Orientation relationship:** β[011] // α″[001] // ω[11-20]. Two crystallographic variants of the martensitic phase were observed.
- **Grain size:** Not explicitly reported in the main text.
- **Grain boundary features:** Not discussed.
- **Elemental segregation:** Not discussed.
- **Precipitate size and distribution:** Not applicable; the paper discusses retained β and ω phases, not precipitates.
- **Martensite morphology:** Typical martensite plates/laths observed by SEM-SE.
- **Effect of oxygen on phases:** Oxygen content did not change the type of phases formed after ST/WQ. However, the paper states that oxygen could have had some effect on volumetric fractions (oxygen is known to suppress α″ by decreasing martensite start temperature, potentially leading to higher retained β fraction), but this could not be evaluated with the experimental setup used.
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed:** Solid-solution strengthening by oxygen is specifically mentioned: "Their mechanical strength can be increased with oxygen addition due to solid-solution strengthening without significantly changing the elastic modulus." No carbide/boride precipitation strengthening, oxide dispersion strengthening, grain-boundary strengthening, or grain refinement is discussed in this paper.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Oxygen (O, as interstitial)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Martensitic Ti-Nb-O alloys (Ti-15Nb to Ti-22.5Nb, wt.%).
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 0.15 to 0.40 wt.% O, intentionally added via TiO₂ powder during arc-melting.
- **Main Function:** Solid-solution strengthening (interstitial strengthening), increasing mechanical strength and hardness while maintaining the low Young's modulus of martensitic Ti-Nb alloys.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Oxygen acts as an interstitial solid-solution strengthener in the α″ martensite phase. The paper states: "Their mechanical strength can be increased with oxygen addition due to solid-solution strengthening without significantly changing the elastic modulus." Oxygen did not change the type of phases formed after ST/WQ. The paper notes that oxygen is known to suppress α″ phase by decreasing martensite start temperature, which could increase retained β fraction, but this effect was not quantifiable in this study.
- **Property Effect:** 
  - Hardness increased with oxygen addition for all Nb contents. For Ti-15Nb: from 200 ± 3 HV (0.15O) to 282 ± 3 HV (0.40O). For Ti-17.5Nb: from 199 ± 1 HV to 274 ± 1 HV. For Ti-20Nb: from 188 ± 1 HV to 278 ± 3 HV. For Ti-22.5Nb: from 205 ± 4 HV to 283 ± 2 HV. Statistical analysis showed oxygen had a much more pronounced effect than Nb.
  - Compressive strength increased with oxygen addition. Specific numerical yield/ultimate values are not provided in the main text.
  - Young's modulus was **not** significantly affected by oxygen content within the same Nb level. Ti-15Nb alloys maintained Young's modulus around 59 GPa at all oxygen levels (57.8–60.3 GPa).
  - Compressive ductility decreased with oxygen addition: Ti-15Nb and Ti-17.5Nb did not fracture at 0.15–0.25 wt.% O, but fractured at ~0.45 and ~0.35 strain, respectively, at 0.40 wt.% O. Ti-20Nb and Ti-22.5Nb fractured at all oxygen levels, with strain near 0.40 and 0.35 at 0.40 wt.% O.
- **Potential Side Effects:** Reduced compressive ductility (unfavorably for some applications), but "still exhibited a good plasticity under compression."
- **Original Evidence:** Abstract; Introduction paragraph 2; Section 3 Results and discussion, paragraphs discussing Fig. 3a–c and Young's modulus; Conclusions; Table 1.

## Niobium (Nb)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Martensitic Ti-Nb-O alloys.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 15 to 22.5 wt.% Nb.
- **Main Function:** Lowering Young's modulus (relative to other Ti alloys) and stabilizing the orthorhombic α″ martensite phase at low Nb contents. At higher Nb contents (≥20 wt.%), it promotes retention of β phase and formation of athermal ω (ω_ath) phase after water quenching.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** 
  - Martensitic Ti-Nb alloys exhibit a minimum elastic modulus around 15 wt.% Nb (cited from Ozaki et al. [4]).
  - The transition from hexagonal α′ to orthorhombic α″ martensite occurs at Nb contents below 15 wt% (close to 14 wt.% according to Bönisch et al. [8]).
  - Higher Nb content leads to higher volume fractions of retained β phase and ω_ath particles after quenching, which raises Young's modulus.
  - Retained β and ω_ath phases were found only in Ti-20Nb and Ti-22.5Nb samples.
- **Property Effect:** 
  - Young's modulus: Ti-15Nb alloys showed lowest modulus (~57.8–60.3 GPa). Modulus increased to ~67.6–72.1 GPa for Ti-17.5Nb and ~76.6–81.7 GPa for Ti-20Nb/22.5Nb alloys. The increase in modulus with Nb in this range is attributed to increasing retained β and ω_ath fractions.
  - Hardness: Nb content had a smaller and less consistent effect than oxygen. Hardness values varied only slightly with Nb at fixed oxygen levels (e.g., at 0.15O: 188–205 HV across Nb range; at 0.40O: 274–283 HV).
  - Ductility: Higher Nb content decreased ductility, probably due to higher volume fractions of β and ω phases.
- **Potential Side Effects:** Higher Nb content raises Young's modulus (within 15–22.5 wt.% range) and reduces ductility. Nb is expensive; employing lower Nb amounts leads to lower densities and melting temperatures.
- **Original Evidence:** Introduction paragraphs 1 and 2; Section 3 Results and discussion, paragraphs discussing Fig. 1c–d, Fig. 2, Fig. 3c–d; Conclusions.

## Ti-15Nb (element combination)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Martensitic Ti-Nb-O alloys for biomedical applications.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 15 wt.% Nb, balance Ti, with oxygen 0.15–0.40 wt.%.
- **Main Function:** Provides the lowest Young's modulus (~59 GPa) among the studied compositions, making it the most promising for biomedical implants where low stiffness is desired to reduce stress shielding.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Nb content of 15 wt.% is close to the minimum in the Young's modulus vs. Nb content relationship for martensitic Ti-Nb alloys. At this Nb content, no retained β or ω phases were detected by XRD/TEM, and the microstructure is predominantly α″ martensite. Oxygen addition strengthens the α″ martensite by solid-solution strengthening without changing the phase constitution.
- **Property Effect:** Young's modulus: 57.8–60.3 GPa (lowest among studied alloys). Hardness: 200 ± 3 (0.15O), 238 ± 4 (0.25O), 282 ± 3 (0.40O) HV. Compressive behavior similar or even superior to Ti-6Al-4V and Ti-Cp. Ti-15Nb alloys with 0.15 and 0.25 wt.% O did not fracture in compression; at 0.40 wt.% O, they fractured at ~0.45 strain.
- **Potential Side Effects:** At 0.40 wt.% O, ductility is reduced (fracture at 0.45 strain).
- **Original Evidence:** Abstract; Section 3, paragraph discussing Fig. 3d–e; Conclusions.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-solution strengthening (interstitial oxygen)

- **Related Elements:** Oxygen (interstitial) in Ti-based martensitic (α″) alloys.
- **Typical Alloys:** Ti-15Nb-xO, Ti-17.5Nb-xO, Ti-20Nb-xO, Ti-22.5Nb-xO (x = 0.15–0.40 wt.% O).
- **Key Compositions:** Oxygen 0.15–0.40 wt.% in Ti-Nb base alloys.
- **Microstructural Changes:** Oxygen does not change the type of phases formed after ST/WQ. It may affect volumetric fractions of phases (suppresses α″ by decreasing martensite start temperature), but this was not conclusively evaluated.
- **Property Improvements:** 
  - Hardness: increased from ~188–205 HV (0.15O) to ~274–283 HV (0.40O) depending on alloy. Statistical analysis showed oxygen effect was much more pronounced than Nb effect.
  - Compressive strength: increased with oxygen addition.
  - Young's modulus: essentially unchanged; oxygen did not significantly affect elastic modulus, allowing high strength/low modulus combination.
- **Risks and Limitations:** Reduced compressive ductility at higher oxygen contents (0.40 wt.% leads to fracture at 0.35–0.45 strain depending on Nb content). The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for yield strength or ultimate compressive strength.
- **Original Evidence:** Abstract; Introduction paragraph 2; Results and discussion paragraphs on Fig. 3a–c; Conclusions: "Their mechanical strength can be increased with oxygen addition due to solid-solution strengthening without significantly changing the elastic modulus."

## Phase-stabilization control via Nb content (martensite vs. retained β and ω)

- This is not a strengthening mechanism per se, but it is discussed as a composition–phase–property relationship. Higher Nb leads to retained β and ω_ath, increasing modulus and reducing ductility. Lower Nb (15 wt.%) minimizes these phases and lowest modulus. This relates to phase selection and modulus control rather than strengthening. No precipitation, dispersion, grain-boundary, or grain-refinement strengthening mechanisms are discussed in this paper.

# Key Conclusions

1. The microstructures of martensitic Ti-Nb-O alloys after solution treatment at 1200 °C for 1 h followed by water quenching were mainly composed of orthorhombic α″ martensite. Transition from α′ to α″ occurs at Nb content below 15 wt.%.

2. Retained β and ω_ath phases were only found in Ti-20Nb and Ti-22.5Nb samples (not in Ti-15Nb or Ti-17.5Nb). Oxygen content did not affect the type of phases formed.

3. Both Nb and oxygen contents had a significant effect on hardness and compressive strength, but the effect of oxygen was much more pronounced. Hardness and strength increased with oxygen addition, whereas smaller or no relevant changes were caused by Nb.

4. Oxygen content did not have a significant effect on Young's modulus; only Nb content significantly affected the elastic constant. Ti-15Nb alloys exhibited the lowest elastic moduli, around 59 GPa.

5. It is possible to increase mechanical strength (via oxygen solid-solution strengthening) while maintaining low elastic modulus, primarily for Ti-15Nb alloys, making them promising for biomedical applications. Their compressive behavior is similar or superior to Ti-6Al-4V and Ti-Cp under the same compression conditions.

6. Higher Nb contents led to higher volume fractions of β and ω phases, causing increased Young's modulus and decreased compressive ductility.

- **Scope limitation:** This paper investigates Ti-Nb-O biomedical martensitic alloys, not refractory high-temperature alloys. There is no relevance to Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloy systems. No high-temperature mechanical properties, creep, oxidation resistance, or refractory-specific behavior are reported.

- **Testing temperature and atmosphere:** Not explicitly stated for mechanical tests (presumably room-temperature air).
