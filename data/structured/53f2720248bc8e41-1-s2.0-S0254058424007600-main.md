# Alloy System and Research Object

The paper studies a model Ni-based superalloy composed of:
- **γ matrix phase**: Pure Ni with FCC (Face-Centered Cubic) structure
- **γ′ precipitate phase**: Ni₃Al with L1₂ ordered structure

Two model variants are constructed:
1. **0% Co structure**: γ/γ′ Ni-based superalloy without Co doping
2. **5% Co structure**: γ/γ′ Ni-based superalloy with 5% Co doping

The γ′ phase volume fraction in the model is **72.9%**. The lattice mismatch δ for the γ/γ′ interface system is **2.8%**, calculated using α_γ = 3.520 Å and α_γ′ = 3.620 Å.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

This is a computational study using molecular dynamics simulation. No experimental processing or heat treatment is involved. The computational procedures are:

**Model Construction:**
- EAM (Embedded Atom Method) potential proposed by Mishin for Ni-Al-Co alloy system (2013) was used
- Model built using Device Studio and LAMMPS open-source software
- 3-dimensional cubic atomic models
- γ phase size: 40a_γ × 40a_γ × 40a_γ
- γ′ phase size: 35a_γ × 35a_γ × 35a_γ (fills the hollowed-out middle of γ phase)
- γ and γ′ phases have almost the same size and shape

**Simulation Procedure:**
- Software: LAMMPS
- Boundary conditions: Periodic boundary conditions
- Orientation: X-axis [001], Y-axis [010], Z-axis [100]
- Tensile deformation along X-axis direction
- **Relaxation**: NVT ensemble, time step 1 fs, 100,000 relaxation steps, to minimize total potential energy and internal stress caused by lattice mismatch
- **Tensile process**: NPT ensemble
- **Strain rate**: 3.0 × 10⁹ s⁻¹
- **Temperatures simulated**: 300 K, 600 K, 900 K, 1200 K
- **Visualization**: OVITO software
- **Dislocation analysis**: Dislocation Extraction Algorithm (DXA)

No heat treatment, mechanical alloying, powder metallurgy, sintering, rolling, forging, annealing, aging, additive manufacturing, arc melting, vacuum melting, induction melting, hot pressing, hot isostatic pressing, or deformation processing is described in this work.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

**Initial Microstructure (Idealized Model):**
- **γ matrix phase (Pure Ni)**: FCC structure, Ni atoms at face centers and vertices
- **γ′ precipitate phase (Ni₃Al)**: L1₂ ordered structure, with Al atoms replacing Ni atoms at each vertex of the γ phase
- **γ′ volume fraction**: 72.9%
- **Lattice mismatch**: δ = 2.8% (α_γ = 3.520 Å, α_γ′ = 3.620 Å)
- **Interface**: a/2⟨110⟩ dislocation network forms at the γ/γ′ interface during the equilibrium process before deformation

**Phase Evolution During Deformation:**
- Initial strain: structure mainly composed of FCC structure
- With increasing strain: FCC content gradually decreases; HCP (Hexagonal Close Packed) and Other structure content continuously rises
- Higher temperature → earlier and faster FCC → Other transformation (amorphization)
- Higher temperature → faster FCC → HCP conversion rate
- Generation of HCP promotes dislocation generation, proliferation, and slip

**Effect of Co on Phase Structure:**
- At 300 K and 600 K: 5% Co structure produces more HCP structure in the elastic deformation stage than 0% Co structure
- At 900 K and 1200 K: 5% Co structure contains less HCP phase in the plastic deformation stage
- At 300 K–600 K, 5% Co structure produces multiple layers of lamellar HCP phase that intrude into the γ′ phase
- At 900 K and 1200 K, HCP structure no longer appears as layered structure
- Co addition at 300 K–600 K hinders the formation of dislocation loops in the elastic stage
- At higher temperatures (900 K–1200 K), dislocation loops exist in both structures

**Dislocation Types:**
- Shockley partial dislocations (a/6⟨112⟩): dominated, movable
- Stair-rod dislocations: immovable, promote strain hardening
- Perfect, Hirth, and Frank dislocations: negligible
- Dislocation reaction: a/2⟨110⟩ → a/6⟨112⟩ + SF + a/6⟨121⟩

**Stacking Faults:**
- 5% Co structure has lower stacking fault energy (SFE) than 0% Co structure
- Lower SFE leads to significantly longer Shockley partial dislocation length
- Co atoms pin Shockley dislocations, leading to more cross-entanglement and formation of Stair-rod dislocations or Lomer-Cottrell locks
- At yield strain, 5% Co structure shows a large number of stacking faults entering deep into γ′ phase with more complete stacking fault zones

No discussion of grain size, elemental segregation (beyond Co distribution between γ and γ′), precipitate size, ODS strengthening, dispersion strengthening, grain-boundary strengthening, or second-phase strengthening is present in this paper.

# Mechanical Properties

All values below are from molecular dynamics simulation at a strain rate of 3.0 × 10⁹ s⁻¹.

**Tensile Strength:**

|-------------|-------|-------|------------|
| 300 K | ~6.89 GPa (inferred from difference) | 5.68 GPa | 1.21 GPa |

> Note: The paper states that the tensile strength of the 5% Co structure at 300 K is 5.68 GPa, which is 1.21 GPa lower than the 0% Co structure. Therefore, 0% Co at 300 K ≈ 6.89 GPa. The difference decreases to 0.95 GPa at 1200 K.

**Flow Stress:**

|-------------|-------|-------|
| 300 K | 4.62 GPa | Higher than 0% Co |
| 1200 K | 3.80 GPa | Lower than 0% Co |

**Elastic Modulus Trend:**
- Decreases with increasing temperature for both structures
- Most obvious decline at 1200 K
- Addition of Co slows down the rate of elastic modulus decrease with temperature
- Co addition reduces elastic modulus compared to 0% Co

**Yield Stress and Yield Strain:**
- Both decrease with increasing temperature for both structures
- 5% Co structure has lower yield stress and lower yield strain than 0% Co structure at all temperatures tested

**Temperature Effect on Tensile Strength:**
- Maximum difference in tensile strength among different temperatures for 0% Co structure: 1.13 GPa
- Maximum difference in tensile strength among different temperatures for 5% Co structure: 0.87 GPa

**Summary of Co Effect on Mechanical Properties:**
- Reduces yield stress, yield strain, and elastic modulus
- Increases flow stress at 300 K–600 K
- Flow stress advantage decreases at 900 K
- Flow stress advantage disappears completely at 1200 K (5% Co flow stress is lower than 0% Co)

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## [Co]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Ni-based superalloy (γ/γ′ two-phase system, γ-Ni FCC + γ′-Ni₃Al L1₂)

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
5% Co (atomic percentage, as modeled in molecular dynamics simulation; the exact at.%/wt.% designation is not explicitly stated in the original paper)

**[Main Function]**
Controversial element in Ni-based superalloys. In this simulation, Co affects dislocation evolution, stacking fault formation, phase transformation (FCC ↔ HCP), and consequently flow stress. At 300–600 K, Co increases flow stress; at higher temperatures, this advantage diminishes and reverses.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
- Co preferentially dopes into the γ phase (lowest potential energy, most stable position)
- Lowers stacking fault energy (SFE) of the structure
- Lower SFE leads to longer Shockley partial dislocations
- Co pins Shockley dislocations, promoting cross-entanglement of dislocations
- Promotes formation of immovable Stair-rod dislocations and Lomer-Cottrell locks through dislocation reactions
- At 300–600 K in the elastic stage: promotes formation of more lamellar HCP phase that intrudes into γ′ phase, hinders dislocation loop formation
- At 300–600 K: leads to more stationary Stair-rod dislocations in plastic deformation stage
- At yield strain: produces more stacking faults entering deep into γ′ phase, accelerating structural deformation
- At 900–1200 K: effects on HCP formation and dislocation evolution diminish or vanish

**[Property Effect]**
- **Reduces** yield stress and yield strain at all temperatures tested (300–1200 K). Specific numerical values: tensile strength of 5% Co structure at 300 K is 5.68 GPa, which is 1.21 GPa lower than 0% Co structure. The difference in tensile strength decreases to 0.95 GPa at 1200 K.
- **Reduces** elastic modulus compared to 0% Co structure.
- **Increases** flow stress at 300–600 K (most obvious increase at 300–600 K); 0% Co flow stress at 300 K is 4.62 GPa, dropping to 3.80 GPa at 1200 K. At 1200 K, 5% Co flow stress is lower than 0% Co.
- **Reduces** tensile strength at all temperatures; tensile strength difference between structures decreases from 1.21 GPa at 300 K to 0.95 GPa at 1200 K.
- **Slows down** the rate of stress decline during plastic deformation with increasing temperature.
- The flow stress advantage of 5% Co weakens with increasing temperature and disappears completely at 1200 K.

**[Potential Side Effects]**
- Reduces yield stress, yield strain, and elastic modulus (at all temperatures simulated)
- Reduces tensile strength at all temperatures simulated
- At high temperatures (≥900 K), Co addition can reduce HCP formation and dislocation density, leading to lower flow stress (at 1200 K, 5% Co has lower flow stress than 0% Co)
- May reduce creep life at high temperatures (cited from Reference [20]: M.V. Nathal and L.J. Ebert found that high Co content at 1093 °C/152 MPa reduces creep life due to γ′ phase dissolution temperature decreasing with increasing Co, leading to faster particle coarsening)

**[Original Evidence]**
- “The findings indicate that 5 % Co doping reduces the yield stress, yield strain, and elastic modulus of the structure, while increasing the flow stress at 300 K – 600 K; however, the advantage decreases at 900 K and disappears completely at 1200 K.” (Abstract)

- “The 5 % Co structure always exhibits lower tensile strength than the 0 % Co structure regardless of the temperature change... from 1.21 Gpa at 300 K to 0.95 Gpa at 1200 K.”
- “The addition of Co at 300 K and 600 K will lead to more HCP structure in the elastic deformation stage than the 0 % Co structure”

## [Ni₃Al (γ′ phase)]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Ni-based superalloy (γ/γ′ two-phase system)

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**

# Key Conclusions

1. The yield stress and yield strain of both 0% Co and 5% Co structures decrease with increasing temperature (300 K → 1200 K).

2. The addition of 5% Co reduces yield stress, yield strain, and elastic modulus compared to the 0% Co structure at all temperatures tested.

3. The addition of 5% Co increases flow stress at 300–600 K. This flow stress advantage weakens at 900 K and disappears completely at 1200 K, where the 5% Co structure has lower flow stress than the 0% Co structure.

4. The flow stress increase with Co at 300–600 K is attributed to Co-induced formation of more stationary Stair-rod dislocations during plastic deformation. Co promotes this by lowering stacking fault energy, which lengthens Shockley partial dislocations, facilitates cross-entanglement, and promotes Stair-rod dislocation or Lomer-Cottrell lock formation.

5. At 300–600 K during the elastic stage, Co addition leads to more lamellar HCP phase that intrudes into the γ′ phase and hinders the formation of dislocation loops. This reduces tensile strength and yield properties while accelerating plastic deformation.

6. At yield strain, the 5% Co structure shows more pronounced stacking faults entering deep into the γ′ phase, accelerating structural deformation.

7. At 900–1200 K, the effects of Co on HCP formation and dislocation evolution diminish significantly. At 1200 K, the 5% Co structure has lower dislocation density (Stair-rod, Shockley, and total) than the 0% Co structure.

8. Higher temperature generally leads to:
   - Lower flow stress, yield stress, yield strain, and elastic modulus
   - Lower dislocation density
   - Earlier and faster FCC → Other (amorphous) transformation
   - Faster FCC → HCP transformation

9. Tensile strength difference between 0% Co and 5% Co structures decreases with increasing temperature, from 1.21 GPa at 300 K to 0.95 GPa at 1200 K.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. **This is a computational study using molecular dynamics simulation with the Mishin EAM potential for the Ni-Al-Co system.** The results are not experimental measurements and may differ from real alloy behavior. The generalizability of the conclusions to real materials requires experimental validation.

2. **Very high strain rate**: The strain rate used (3.0 × 10⁹ s⁻¹) is extremely high and typical of MD simulations but orders of magnitude above experimental strain rates. This can affect deformation mechanisms and may not reflect realistic deformation behavior.

3. **Idealized microstructure**: The model uses pure Ni for the γ phase and stoichiometric Ni₃Al for the γ′ phase, which is highly idealized compared to real Ni-based superalloys that contain many other alloying elements (Cr, Mo, W, Ta, Re, Ru, Ti, etc.). Real superalloys have complex chemistry in both phases. The conclusions regarding Co effects may not directly transfer to multi-component commercial alloys.

4. **Limited Co concentration**: Only two compositions are studied: 0% Co and 5% Co. The concentration-dependent behavior of Co cannot be established from this study alone.

5. **Specific model geometry**: The model has a γ′ volume fraction of 72.9%, which is specific to this simulation. Real superalloys have varying γ′ fractions, and the Co effects may depend on the γ′ volume fraction.

6. **Periodic boundary conditions and nanoscale model**: The model size (253,408 atoms) is nanoscale with periodic boundaries, which may not capture grain boundaries, defects, voids, or other microstructural features present in real alloys.

7. **No discussion of oxidation**: This paper does not address oxidation resistance, which is an important consideration for high-temperature applications of Ni-based superalloys.

8. **No discussion of creep**: Despite citing literature on Co's effect on creep properties (Reference [20]), this paper only studies monotonic tensile behavior. The creep implications of Co addition are not simulated.

9. **Temperature range limited to 300–1200 K**: Real Ni-based superalloy service temperatures can extend above 1200 K (up to ~1400 K in some applications). The behavior at higher temperatures is not directly studied.

10. **Single orientation**: Tensile deformation is performed only along the [001] direction. Crystal orientation can significantly affect mechanical properties in anisotropic single-crystal superalloys.

11. **No dislocation loop formation discussion at low temperature with Co**: The paper discusses that Co at 300–600 K hinders dislocation loop formation, but the mechanism for this is not fully clarified. The authors state that “the mechanism of the influence is not clear” regarding dislocation length and Co addition.

12. **The lattice constants used** (α_γ = 3.520 Å, α_γ′ = 3.620 Å) are specific to the model and may not reflect real alloy compositions with additional alloying elements.

13. **The attribution of 5% Co as at.% vs wt.% is not explicitly stated** in the paper. This ambiguity affects compositional interpretation.

14. **The conclusions regarding Co's contradictory effects** (increasing flow stress while decreasing yield stress and tensile strength) reflect the complexity of Co's role but may not generalize to all temperature-strain rate regimes or alloy compositions. The controversial nature of Co's effect in Ni-based superalloys noted in the introduction remains an open question.
