# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system studied**: Nb-based alloys, specifically:
  - Pure Nb (commercially pure niobium)
  - Nb-1Zr (Nb-1 wt.% Zr)
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C (Nb-1 wt.% Zr-0.1 wt.% C)
- **Material category**: Refractory metal alloys (Nb-W-based not applicable; this is Nb-Zr and Nb-Zr-C alloy system)
- **Research objective**: 
  1. To study the deformation behaviour of Niobium, Nb-1Zr and Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy over a wide temperature range (25 to 1700 °C) and strain rate range (10⁻³–10 s⁻¹ and 1000 s⁻¹)
  2. Optimization of hot working conditions
  3. Identification of deformation mechanisms
  4. Structure–property correlation with deformation mechanisms
  5. Influence of strain on the evolution of dynamic recrystallization in Nb alloys
  6. Kinetics studies of dynamic recrystallization of Nb-1Zr and Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy

# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **Starting material condition**: Annealed Nb, Nb-1Zr and Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy
- **Hot deformation**: Uniaxial compression tests in Gleeble 3800 thermo-mechanical simulator
  - Sample geometry: Solid cylindrical samples, diameter 10 mm, height 15 mm
  - Temperature range: 900–1700 °C in steps of 100 °C (for Nb: 700–1500 °C)
  - Strain rates: 10⁻³ to 10 s⁻¹
  - Atmosphere: Vacuum of 10⁻³ mbar
  - Heating: Resistance heating with type-C thermocouple spot-welded to sample surface; accuracy ±1 °C
  - Sample held at test temperature for 5 minutes before deformation
  - Deformed to true strain of 0.6
  - Quenched with argon gas to retain microstructure; average cooling rate 75 °C s⁻¹ for initial 7 s
  - Friction reduction: WC platens with Ta foil and graphite between sample and platen
  - For high strain rates of 1 and 10 s⁻¹, deformation was adiabatic; temperature rise (ΔT) calculated using ΔT = (1/ρCp)∫σdε

- **Quasistatic deformation from room temperature to 700 °C**: Zwick screw-driven machine
  - Sample geometry: Solid cylindrical, 5 mm diameter, 5 mm length
  - Strain rate: starting 10⁻³ s⁻¹ with strain rate change tests (10⁻³ ↔ 10⁻⁴ ↔ 10⁻² s⁻¹)
  - Stress relaxation: four successive 15-second relaxation runs at each stress level
  - Atmosphere: room temperature to 400 °C in air; 500–700 °C with glass coating
  - Temperature stability: ±1 °C

- **High strain rate deformation**: Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB)
  - Temperature: 25–400 °C in 100 °C steps
  - Strain: 0.25
  - Average strain rates: Nb-1Zr ≈ 1700 s⁻¹; Nb-1Zr-0.1C ≈ 1100 s⁻¹
  - Lubricant: molybdenum disulphide powder
  - Striker bar length: 0.5 m
  - Striker bar velocities: Nb-1Zr 8.19–8.97 m/s; Nb-1Zr-0.1C 10.41–12.85 m/s

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase**: Body-centred cubic (bcc) Nb solid solution (for all three materials)
- **Second phases in Nb-1Zr-0.1C**:
  - Carbide precipitates: (Nb, Zr)C (fcc structure) in recrystallized condition; hexagonal Nb2C in as-cast condition; orthorhombic (Nb, Zr)2C and hexagonal (Nb, Zr)3C2 in deformed condition (from literature cited in thesis)
- **Starting grain sizes**:
  - Nb: large grains with straight grain boundaries (well annealed)
  - Nb-1Zr: average grain size ~138 µm
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C: average grain size ~30 µm
- **Deformed microstructure**:
  - Nb: fine recrystallized grains at 1200–1300 °C and 0.1 s⁻¹; equiaxed larger grains at 1400–1500 °C indicating DRX; flow localization at 800 °C and 1 s⁻¹; elongated grains at 900 °C and 0.003 s⁻¹ indicating dynamic recovery; grain size decreased with increasing strain rate at 1400 °C
  - Nb-1Zr: equiaxed grains; average grain size increased with decreasing strain rate and increasing temperature. At 1500 °C, 0.1 s⁻¹: 329 µm; at 1600 °C, 0.01 s⁻¹: 1290 µm; at 1600 °C, 0.1 s⁻¹: 567 µm; at 1700 °C, 0.1 s⁻¹: 668 µm; at 1500 °C, 0.003 s⁻¹: 827 µm; at 1500 °C, 0.01 s⁻¹: 613 µm. Small new recrystallized grains at boundaries of larger grains at 1600 °C and 0.01 s⁻¹
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C: fine grains surrounding larger grains (necklace structure) at 1500–1700 °C and 10⁻¹ s⁻¹; serrated high angle grain boundaries at 0.001 s⁻¹ and 1500–1600 °C; at 0.01 s⁻¹ serration only at 1600 °C; at 0.1 s⁻¹ serration prominent at 1700 °C; incomplete DRX indicated by heterogeneous microstructures
- **DRX characterization**:
  - Grains partitioned using Grain Orientation Spread (GOS) < 2° as recrystallized
  - Number fraction of recrystallized grains higher at higher strain rates
  - Necklace structure characteristic of discontinuous dynamic recrystallization (dDRX)
  - At 1500 °C and 0.1 s⁻¹, true strain 0.6: fine grains ~5 µm forming necklace structure; at strain 0.9: recrystallization fraction 30% (highest); at strain 1.2: 27% (due to substructure formation in DRX grains)
  - At 1600 °C and 0.1 s⁻¹: recrystallization fraction increased with strain, highest at 1.2
- **Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM)**:
  - Nb-1Zr: KAM decreased with increasing temperature at fixed strain rate; decreased with increasing strain rate at fixed temperature; signifies recovery/recrystallization
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C: at 1500 °C, local average misorientation lowest at strain 0.9 (low GND density); at 1600 °C, increased with strain

# Mechanical Properties

- **Flow stress behavior**:
  - Nb: strong dependence on temperature and strain rate; work hardening at 700–800 °C; sharp yield point at 700 °C and low strain rates (3×10⁻³ and 10⁻¹ s⁻¹) indicating solute-dislocation interaction; softening (DRX) at T ≥ 900 °C and low strain rates; softening onset temperature increased with strain rate; no softening at 10 s⁻¹ up to 1500 °C
  - Nb-1Zr: work hardening at 900–1200 °C; transition to steady state with increasing temperature; at 0.003 s⁻¹ transition at 1300 °C; at 0.01 s⁻¹ at 1400 °C; at 0.1 s⁻¹ at 1500 °C; at 1 s⁻¹ at 1600 °C; at 10 s⁻¹ hardening at all temperatures; peak stress followed by softening at 1400–1700 °C and 0.003–0.01 s⁻¹ (DRX); steady state at 0.1 s⁻¹ and 1500–1700 °C (DRV)
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C: flow stress virtually independent of temperature and strain rate below 1000 °C; high strain hardening at 1000–1400 °C and >0.1 s⁻¹; initial rapid work hardening followed by steady state at strain rates <0.1 s⁻¹ and 1000–1700 °C
- **Strain rate sensitivity (m)**:
  - Nb: high m domain 0.12–0.2 at 1200–1500 °C and 10⁻²–1 s⁻¹; low m domain (0.05) at 800 °C and ~0.1 s⁻¹; peak m = 0.19
  - Nb-1Zr: high m domain 0.15–0.25 at 1200–1500 °C and 10⁻³–10⁻¹ s⁻¹; also 1600–1700 °C and 10⁻¹–1 s⁻¹; peak m = 0.24
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C: single domain starting at ~1300 °C, maximum m = 0.24 at 1700 °C and 2×10⁻³ s⁻¹; peak m = 0.25; m increased from slightly positive to ~0.24 with increasing temperature and decreasing strain rate
  - m nearly same for Nb-1Zr and Nb-1Zr-0.1C from RT to 600 °C; Nb-1Zr higher than Nb-1Zr-0.1C from 600–1400 °C; Nb-1Zr m increased up to 1300 °C then decreased; Nb-1Zr-0.1C m increased linearly from 900–1700 °C
- **Kinetic analysis parameters**:
  - Nb: Q = 246 kJ mol⁻¹, n = 7.5, V = 200–700 b³, peak m = 0.19
  - Nb-1Zr: Q = 259 kJ mol⁻¹, n = 4.9, V = 230–650 b³, peak m = 0.24
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C: Q = 361 kJ mol⁻¹, n = 5, V = 200–500 b³, peak m = 0.25
  - Self-diffusion activation energy for Nb: Q_SD ≈ 374–400 kJ mol⁻¹
- **Activation volume**:
  - Decreased with increasing stress from 700 to 200 b³ (high temperature domain)
  - At low temperatures (25–600 °C, 10⁻³ s⁻¹): <100 b³ (annihilation of dislocations at grain boundaries)
  - High temperature: 200–700 b³ (dislocation intersection/kink-jog motion)
- **Low temperature activation energy** (Table 4.5):
  - Nb-1Zr at 25 °C: 68 kJ mol⁻¹; at 100 °C: 122 kJ mol⁻¹; at 200 °C: 223 kJ mol⁻¹
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C at 25 °C: 141 kJ mol⁻¹; at 100 °C: 183 kJ mol⁻¹; at 200 °C: 229 kJ mol⁻¹
- **Dynamic strain aging (DSA)**:
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C at 700 °C and 10⁻³ s⁻¹: serrations and negative strain rate sensitivity
- **High strain rate flow stress**:
  - Both Nb-1Zr and Nb-1Zr-0.1C: flow stress decreased with temperature; slight increase at 400 °C compared to 300 °C possibly due to DSA
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C flow stress higher than Nb-1Zr
- **Flow stress regimes**:
  - Low temperature regime (RT–600 °C): flow stress decreases with temperature
  - Intermediate temperature regime (600–900 °C): flow stress insensitive to temperature
  - High temperature regime (1000–1700 °C): flow stress decreases with temperature
- **Comparative flow stress**:
  - Nb-1Zr-0.1C showed ~3× higher flow stress than Nb and Nb-1Zr at 900–1000 °C for given strain rate
  - Solid solution strengthening of Zr in Nb appeared insignificant compared to precipitation hardening (from literature vs. present study)
- **Hardness**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Elastic modulus**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Density**: Nb density 8.57 g cm⁻³ (mentioned as lowest among refractory metals)
- **Creep/rupture data**: Not directly measured in this study; compared with literature creep data at 1000–1027 °C
- **Dislocation exhaustion rate**: ρm/ρmo decreased to ~8% at end of first 15-s relaxation at 600 °C for Nb-1Zr-0.1C; ~6% after four relaxation cycles

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Zr in Nb-based alloys

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Nb-1Zr, Nb-1Zr-0.1C (Nb-Zr and Nb-Zr-C alloys)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 1 wt.% Zr (≈0.98 at.% Zr)
- **Main Function**: Solid-solution strengthening of Nb matrix
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Zr dissolves in Nb solid solution (bcc). In presence of C, Zr also participates in carbide formation as (Nb, Zr)C. The thesis notes that "Zr addition to Nb acts as a solid solution strengthener." However, the comparison of flow stress data in this thesis suggests that "In comparison to precipitation hardening, solid solution strengthening of Zr in Nb appears insignificant" at 900–1000 °C.
- **Property Effect**: Nb-1Zr has higher flow stress than pure Nb (creep strength of Nb-1Zr higher than Nb at 1027 °C per Davidson et al.). Activation energy for deformation of Nb-1Zr (Q = 259 kJ mol⁻¹) similar to Nb (246 kJ mol⁻¹). Stress exponent n = 4.9.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly stated, but Zr contributes to carbide formation in C-containing alloys.
- **Original Evidence**: "Zr addition to Nb acts as a solid solution strengthener" (Synopsis, Section 1); "The addition of 0.1wt.% of carbon provides dispersion strengthening to Nb matrix due to Nb and Zr carbides" (Synopsis).

## C in Nb-Zr-C alloys

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Nb-1Zr-0.1C (Nb-Zr-C alloy)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 0.1 wt.% C nominal; actual measured 0.067 wt.% C
- **Main Function**: Precipitation strengthening via carbide formation; dispersion strengthening
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: C combines with Nb and Zr to form carbide precipitates: (Nb, Zr)C (fcc), Nb2C (hexagonal), (Nb, Zr)2C (orthorhombic), (Nb, Zr)3C2 (hexagonal). Carbides act as barriers to dislocation motion. At high temperature deformation, the barriers change from dislocation-dislocation or solute atom-based process to dislocation interacting with precipitates.
- **Property Effect**: Nb-1Zr-0.1C showed ~3× higher flow stress than Nb-1Zr at 900–1000 °C. Activation energy for deformation Q = 361 kJ mol⁻¹, significantly higher than Nb (246) and Nb-1Zr (259). Higher activation energy implies higher sensitivity of strain rate to changes in temperature. n = 5. High m domain: 1300–1700 °C and 10⁻³–1 s⁻¹, with peak m = 0.25.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly stated in this thesis, but literature cited suggests room-temperature ductility of Nb-based alloys may decrease with increasing C content (Ding et al., cited in background as reference [72]).
- **Original Evidence**: "The addition of 0.1 wt.% of carbon promotes precipitation which was found to be effective in improving the creep strength of Nb-1Zr alloy" (Introduction, Section 1.1); "Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloys has a higher flow stress (three fold) for given strain rate (higher creep strength) as compared to that of Nb and Nb-1Zr. The higher strength of Nb-1Zr-0.1C can be attributed to the presence of fine (Nb, Zr)C precipitates" (Section 4.5.1); "For Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy C is in form of carbides and the barriers to dislocation motion change from dislocation-dislocation or solute atom based process to dislocation interacting with precipitate. This possibly results in the substantial increase in Q for Nb-1Zr-0.1C as compared to Nb-1Zr" (Section 4.5.1).

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related Elements**: Zr in Nb matrix; also V, Cr, Mo, W, Hf, Ti as reported in literature for Nb alloys
- **Typical Alloys**: Nb-1Zr, Nb-3Zr-0.34C, Nb-3Zr-0.4C (latter two from literature)
- **Key Compositions**: 1 wt.% Zr in Nb
- **Microstructural Changes**: Zr in solid solution in bcc Nb; lattice distortion; solute-dislocation interaction evidenced by sharp yield point at 700 °C in Nb
- **Property Improvements**: Modest increase in flow stress over pure Nb. In this thesis, Zr solid solution strengthening appears less significant than carbide precipitation strengthening at 900–1000 °C
- **Risks and Limitations**: The thesis notes: "In comparison to precipitation hardening, solid solution strengthening of Zr in Nb appears insignificant" at 900–1000 °C. In the high m domain, Nb-1Zr has n = 4.9 and Q = 259 kJ mol⁻¹.
- **Original Evidence**: Section 4.5.1 and 1.1; "At higher temperature of 1095 °C addition of zirconium and vanadium give the largest increase in strength of Nb due to solid-solution strengthening" (Section 2.3, citing Begley and Bechtold).

## Carbide Precipitation Strengthening

- **Related Elements**: C combined with Nb and Zr (also Ta, Hf, Ti in other Nb alloys)
- **Typical Alloys**: Nb-1Zr-0.1C, Nb-3Zr-0.34C, Nb-3Zr-0.4C, Nb35Ti6Al5Cr8V with C additions (literature)
- **Key Compositions**: 0.067–0.1 wt.% C in Nb-1Zr
- **Microstructural Changes**: Formation of (Nb, Zr)C, Nb2C, (Nb, Zr)2C, (Nb, Zr)3C2 precipitates. In deformed Nb-1Zr-0.1C, needle-shaped (Nb, Zr)2C and rectangular (Nb, Zr)3C2 precipitates observed (from literature). Recrystallization anneal dissolves prior carbides and reprecipitates as fcc (Nb, Zr)C. Fine precipitates at grain boundaries and within grains.
- **Property Improvements**: ~3× higher flow stress at 900–1000 °C compared to Nb-1Zr; activation energy Q = 361 kJ mol⁻¹ (significantly higher than Nb-1Zr's 259 kJ mol⁻¹); improved creep strength; n = 5; peak m = 0.25
- **Risks and Limitations**: Needle-shaped precipitates within grains (as-cast); at low strain rates and high temperatures, grain growth may occur after DRX
- **Original Evidence**: Section 4.5.1, 2.4, and references [47, 63, 73, 76, 77, 79]

## Dynamic Recrystallization (DRX) as Restoration and Grain Refinement Mechanism

- **Related Elements**: Not element-specific; temperature and strain-rate dependent
- **Typical Alloys**: Nb, Nb-1Zr, Nb-1Zr-0.1C
- **Key Compositions**: All three alloy systems studied
- **Microstructural Changes**: 
  - Necklace structure of fine recrystallized grains along serrated grain boundaries (characteristic of discontinuous DRX or dDRX)
  - Fine grains ~5 µm at 1500 °C, 0.1 s⁻¹, strain 0.6 for Nb-1Zr-0.1C
  - Equiaxed grains at high strains (1.2)
  - GOS < 2° used to identify DRX grains
  - Recrystallization fraction 30% at 1500 °C, 0.1 s⁻¹, strain 0.9 for Nb-1Zr-0.1C
- **Property Improvements**: Flow softening after peak stress; reduced flow stress; grain refinement; strain rate sensitivity m = 0.12–0.25 in DRX domain
- **Risks and Limitations**: Incomplete DRX may result in heterogeneous microstructures; grain growth at higher temperatures and lower strain rates after DRX completion
- **Original Evidence**: Sections 4.1.7, 4.1.8, 4.1.9; "The presence of serrated grain boundaries and the necklace structure observed in Nb alloys at high temperature confirm the occurrence of discontinuous dynamic recrystallization" (Conclusions, Section 5.3)

## Dynamic Recovery (DRV)

- **Related Elements**: Not element-specific
- **Typical Alloys**: Nb, Nb-1Zr, Nb-1Zr-0.1C
- **Key Compositions**: All three alloy systems
- **Microstructural Changes**: Elongated grains perpendicular to deformation direction; subgrain formation; low angle boundaries; KAM decrease with increasing temperature
- **Property Improvements**: Flow stress saturation (no softening); lower m than DRX domain
- **Risks and Limitations**: May lead to flow localization at low temperatures
- **Original Evidence**: Nb elongated grains at 900 °C and 0.003 s⁻¹ (Fig. 4.13); Nb-1Zr steady-state flow at 0.1 s⁻¹ and 1500–1700 °C attributed to DRV (Section 4.1.2)

## Dislocation Core Diffusion (High Temperature Deformation Mechanism)

- **Related Elements**: Not element-specific
- **Typical Alloys**: Nb, Nb-1Zr, Nb-1Zr-0.1C
- **Key Compositions**: All three alloy systems
- **Microstructural Changes**: Dislocation interaction with precipitates (Nb-1Zr-0.1C) or solute (Nb-1Zr)
- **Property Improvements**: Activation energy Q lower than self-diffusion (Q_SD ≈ 400 kJ mol⁻¹); Q = 246–361 kJ mol⁻¹
- **Risks and Limitations**: Not explicitly stated
- **Original Evidence**: "The activation energy of deformation suggests that dislocation core diffusion is the dominant mechanism during high temperature deformation (from 1300 to 1700 °C)" (Conclusions, Section 5.4); Q ~ (2/3) Q_SD implies grain boundary or dislocation core diffusion

# Key Conclusions

1. **Flow stress behavior**: Nb and its alloys showed a high strain rate sensitivity domain at temperature >1300 °C and strain rate 10⁻³ to 0.1 s⁻¹. Flow stress decreased from RT to 600 °C, was insensitive from 600 to 900 °C, and decreased again above 1000 °C.

2. **Optimum hot working conditions**:
   - Pure Nb: 1200–1500 °C and 10⁻²–1 s⁻¹, m = 0.12–0.2, Q = 246 kJ mol⁻¹, n = 7.5
   - Nb-1Zr: 1200–1500 °C and 10⁻³–0.1 s⁻¹, plus 1600–1700 °C and 10⁻¹–1 s⁻¹, m = 0.15–0.25, Q = 259 kJ mol⁻¹, n = 4.9
   - Nb-1Zr-0.1C: 1300–1700 °C and 10⁻³–1 s⁻¹, maximum m at 1700 °C and 0.002 s⁻¹, m up to 0.24, Q = 361 kJ mol⁻¹, n = 5

3. **Comparative strength**: At same deformation conditions, Nb-1Zr-0.1C showed higher flow stress and finer microstructure compared to Nb-1Zr. Nb-1Zr-0.1C showed ~3× higher flow stress at 900–1000 °C.

4. **DRX microstructures**: Samples deformed within high m domain revealed dynamically recrystallized microstructure. Necklace structure of recrystallized grains for Nb-1Zr-0.1C seen along serrated grain boundaries at strain 0.6 and 0.9 for 1500 and 1600 °C, respectively.

5. **Texture**: Predominant texture along <001> poles for all deformation conditions of Nb-1Zr and Nb-1Zr-0.1C in the high m domain.

6. **DRX kinetics**: Avrami time exponent n ≈ 4.8–5.1 for Nb-1Zr-0.1C at 0.1 s⁻¹ and 1500–1600 °C, suggesting ideal Avrami recrystallization for homogeneous, constant-rate nucleation; critical strain εc = 0.62 εp at 1500 °C and 0.1 s⁻¹ (εp = 0.29, εc = 0.18).

7. **Deformation mechanism**: Dislocation core diffusion dominates at high temperature (1300–1700 °C); annihilation of dislocations at grain boundaries dominates at low temperature (activation volume <100 b³); dislocation intersection/kink-jog motion at high temperature (activation volume 200–700 b³).

8. **Dynamic strain aging**: Observed in Nb-1Zr-0.1C at 700 °C and 10⁻³ s⁻¹.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Limitations**:
  - This study focuses exclusively on deformation behavior; no oxidation resistance tests, fracture toughness, or tensile tests reported in this thesis
  - No quantitative carbide precipitate size and distribution measurements directly reported in this thesis (only cited from literature)
  - The comparison with literature creep data at 1000–1027 °C suggests the present hot deformation data for Nb-1Zr-0.1C lies in the power-law breakdown regime rather than the power-law regime studied in creep literature
  - The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for room-temperature yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, elongation, hardness, or elastic modulus
  - The thesis was completed in 2018 and may not include the most recent developments in refractory alloy research
  - High strain rate tests limited to 400 °C max
  - No additively manufactured refractory alloys studied

- **Applicable Scope**:
  - Nb-based alloys for high-temperature nuclear applications
  - Hot working process optimization for Nb, Nb-1Zr, and Nb-1Zr-0.1C
  - Discontinuous dynamic recrystallization (dDRX) in bcc refractory metals
  - Strain rate sensitivity mapping as tool for hot workability optimization
  - Comparison of solid-solution strengthening (Zr) vs. precipitation strengthening (C-based carbides) in Nb alloys
