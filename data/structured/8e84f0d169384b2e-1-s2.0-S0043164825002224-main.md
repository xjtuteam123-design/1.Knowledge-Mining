- **Authors**: Guijiang Diao, Anqiang He, Dong Zhang, Yunqing Tang, Yefei Zhou, Zhen Xu, Keyang Chen, Aakash Kumar, Seyed Elias Mousavi, Mingyu Wu, Wengang Chen, D.L. Chen, D.Y. Li

- **Alloy system studied**: Al3Ti-based medium-entropy alloys (Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu system)
- **Research objective**: To develop lightweight Al3Ti-based medium-entropy alloys with improved high-temperature mechanical properties and wear resistance by alloying Al3Ti with Cr, Mn, Fe, and Cu, achieving D022-to-L12 transformation and forming in-situ hard second phases.

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu medium-entropy alloy (MEA) system based on Al3Ti intermetallic.
- **Material category**: Lightweight Al3Ti-based medium-entropy alloys. Not a refractory high-temperature alloy in the sense of Nb/W/Mo/Ta-based systems. Service temperature target is 300–500 °C.
- **Research gap addressed**: Aluminum alloys generally work below 200–300 °C; titanium alloys have density >4 g/cm³ and high cost. The target is a lightweight alloy with density 3–4 g/cm³ for medium-temperature range 300–500 °C.
- **Research approach**: Single-phase D022-Al3Ti is brittle. Alloying with third elements (Cr, Mn, Fe, Cu) transforms tetragonal D022 into cubic L12 with more slip systems; excess alloying elements form in-situ hard B2 and D8a second phases to compensate strength loss from the transformation.

---

# Alloy Composition

Four Al3Ti-based MEA samples were studied (all in atomic percent, at.% unless otherwise noted):

| Alloy | Al (at.%) | Ti (at.%) | Cr (at.%) | Mn (at.%) | Fe (at.%) | Cu (at.%) | Density (g/cm³) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Al63Ti25Cr3Mn3Fe3Cu3 | 63 | 25 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 | ~3.65 |
| Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 | 60 | 20 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | ~3.88 |
| Al55Ti20Cr5Mn10Fe5Cu5 | 55 | 20 | 5 | 10 | 5 | 5 | ~4.08 |
| Al50Ti20Cr10Mn10Fe5Cu5 | 50 | 20 | 10 | 10 | 5 | 5 | ~4.27 |

- **Reference material**: Al-matrix composite reinforced with 30 wt% SiC particles (Al/SiCp). Also compared with Ti6Al4V alloy.
- **Impurities**: The original paper does not report C, O, N, H contents.
- **W, Mo, Nb, Ta, Zr, Hf, V, Re, B, Si, Y, La, O additions**: None. The only elements deliberately added are Al, Ti, Cr, Mn, Fe, Cu. SiC appears only in the reference Al-matrix composite, not as an alloying addition to the MEAs.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **Processing method**: Arc melting in a pure argon atmosphere.
- **Details**: High-purity Al, Ti, Cr, Mn, Fe, Cu powders were manually mixed and pressed into disc-shaped bulk samples, then melted in an arc melting furnace under pure argon. Each alloy sample was re-melted four times to reduce compositional inhomogeneity.
- **Heat treatment**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper. No homogenization, annealing, aging, rolling, forging, or HIP treatments are reported.
- **Additive manufacturing**: Not used in this paper.
- **Powder metallurgy/mechanical alloying**: Not used; arc melting of pressed powders was the route.
- **Deformation**: No thermomechanical deformation processing reported.
- **Cooling method**: Cast structure after arc melting; explicit cooling procedure is not described.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase**: Ordered FCC L12 phase, with composition described as (Al,M)3Ti where M = Cr, Mn, Fe, Cu in minor amounts. In the Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy, the L12 phase composition is approximately Al 62.33, Ti 22.99, Cr 2.94, Mn 2.94, Fe 3.62, Cu 5.17 (at.%).
- **Second phases**:
  - **B2 phase**: Ordered BCC. Composition for Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy: Al 58.71, Ti 12.57, Cr 5.51, Mn 4.55, Fe 10.76, Cu 4.57 (at.%). Fe is more concentrated in B2.
  - **D8a phase**: Ordered FCC with Mn23Th6-type large crystal lattice, commonly found in Al-Ti-M alloys. Composition for Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy: Al 57.99, Ti 3.72, Cr 9.20, Mn 7.41, Fe 7.23, Cu 6.21 (at.%). Cr and Mn are more concentrated in D8a.
- **Phase transformation from parent compound**: Starting D022 (tetragonal) Al3Ti transformed to L12 (cubic).
- **Volume fractions of phases** (determined by ImageJ on SEM-BSE images):

| Alloy | L12 (vol.%) | B2 (vol.%) | D8a (vol.%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Al63Ti25Cr3Mn3Fe3Cu3 | 94 | 5 | 1 |
| Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 | 80 | 15 | 5 |
| Al55Ti20Cr5Mn10Fe5Cu5 | 70 | 15 | 15 |
| Al50Ti20Cr10Mn10Fe5Cu5 | 55 | 25 | 20 |

- **Elemental segregation trends**:
  - Al and Ti concentrated in L12.
  - Cr, Mn, Fe, Cu concentrated in second phases (B2 and D8a).
  - Fe preferentially partitions to B2.
  - Cr and Mn preferentially partition to D8a.
  - Cu shows only very slight segregation.
  - Mn addition promotes D8a formation: increasing Mn from 5 to 10 at.% raises D8a from 5 to 15 vol.% while B2 remains unchanged.
  - Cr promotes D8a formation. Increase of Cr from 5 to 10 at.% raises D8a from 15 to 20 vol.%.
  - Decrease in Al content at constant Ti content produces an elevated Ti/Al ratio; excess Ti is preferentially incorporated into B2, increasing B2 fraction.
- **Grain size**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Grain boundary features**: Not discussed.
- **Precipitate size and distribution**: Bright D8a phase is described as tiny; quantitative size not reported.

# Mechanical Properties

## Room-temperature compressive properties

| Alloy | Yield strength (MPa) | Peak/fracture strength (MPa) | Fracture strain (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Al63Ti25Cr3Mn3Fe3Cu3 | 409 | 1068 | 20.5 |
| Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 | 519 | 1253 | 18.0 |
| Al55Ti20Cr5Mn10Fe5Cu5 | 650 | 1366 | 16.7 |
| Al50Ti20Cr10Mn10Fe5Cu5 | 716 | 1307 | 12.4 |

Compression strain rate: ~1 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.

## Elevated-temperature compressive properties

- **Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy at 500 °C**: yield strength 442 MPa; reasonable plasticity retained. Tested on Gleeble 3800-GTC at ~1 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.
- The paper notes the alloy has σ_peak/σ0.2 = 2.41 ratio at room temperature as a safety margin.

## Hardness

- **Macro-hardness (Rockwell, HRA, 60 kgf, room temperature)**: Hardness increases with increasing third alloying element content. All lightweight MEAs show much higher hardness than Al/SiCp composite.
- **Vickers hardness (2 kgf load)** at elevated temperatures for Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy:
  - Room temperature: high hardness, always above Ti6Al4V at all tested temperatures.
  - At 500 °C: ~270 HV2.
  - Approximate hardness decreases with temperature from room temperature through 100 °C, 300 °C, to 500 °C: 6.1%, 12.5%, 19.5% respectively.
- **Ti6Al4V comparison**: hardness decreases by 8.2%, 16.4%, 29.6% over the same intervals.
- **Al/SiCp comparison**: hardness decreased by 11.9% from RT to 100 °C and 32.7% by 300 °C.
- **Room-temperature hardness of Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 is higher than Ti6Al4V**, though Ti6Al4V yield strength at RT is higher.

## Elastic modulus
- Not measured or reported in this paper. The paper cites Al3Ti Young's modulus of ~216 GPa in the introduction, but the MEAs' moduli are not provided.

## Density
- Al63Ti25Cr3Mn3Fe3Cu3: ~3.65 g/cm³
- Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5: ~3.88 g/cm³
- Al55Ti20Cr5Mn10Fe5Cu5: ~4.08 g/cm³
- Al50Ti20Cr10Mn10Fe5Cu5: ~4.27 g/cm³

## Other properties
- Fracture toughness, creep, rupture life, strain-rate sensitivity data, elastic modulus: Not measured in this paper.

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

- **Oxidation during wear**: The Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy, containing passive elements Ti, Cr, and Al, showed higher resistance to oxidation caused by frictional heating during sliding wear compared to Al/SiCp composite, as evidenced by EDS oxygen linescans at room temperature and 300 °C.
- **Oxidation weight gain**: Not specifically measured.
- **Oxide scale type**: Not directly analyzed. The paper suggests a protective oxide film or scale containing passive elements Ti, Cr, and Al helps reduce wear damage at elevated temperatures.
- **Oxidation temperature and duration**: Oxidation is discussed only in the context of frictional heating during wear tests at room temperature and 300 °C; no dedicated isothermal oxidation tests were performed.
- **Effect of alloying elements on oxidation resistance**: Ti, Cr, and Al are described as passive elements that improve oxidation resistance during wear.
- **High-temperature embrittlement / oxygen embrittlement**: Not directly discussed for these alloys.
- **Thermal stability**: The ordered L12, B2, and D8a phases provide strong resistance to softening at elevated temperatures compared to solid-solution alloys. Long-range ordering impedes atomic diffusion and dislocation movement.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Al (Aluminum)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu lightweight MEAs based on Al3Ti.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 50–63 at.% in the four studied alloys.
- **Main Function**: Primary constituent of L12 matrix and overall lightweight character. Forms passive oxide for wear-oxidation resistance.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Al and Ti are concentrated in the L12 matrix phase. Excess Ti/Al ratio promotes B2 formation when Al content decreases.
- **Property Effect**: Decreasing Al content from 63 to 50 at.% (with increasing third elements) correlates with higher hardness and compressive strength but lower fracture strain. Specific values are listed above.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Excessive Al reduction with fixed Ti content raises Ti/Al ratio and promotes B2 formation, increasing hardness but reducing ductility.
- **Original Evidence**: Composition tables and phase composition data in Table 1; L12 composition Al 62.33 at.% in Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy.

## Ti (Titanium)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu lightweight MEAs based on Al3Ti.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 20–25 at.%.
- **Main Function**: Essential to L12 (Al,M)3Ti matrix and gives elevated strength and thermal stability; also contributes to oxidation resistance.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Ti is concentrated in L12 phase. Excessive Ti relative to Al promotes B2 formation.
- **Property Effect**: Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy retains 442 MPa yield strength at 500 °C, surpassing Ti6Al4V at that temperature.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Excess Ti (low Al/Ti ratio) leads to more B2 phase and reduced ductility.
- **Original Evidence**: DISCUSSION in section 3.1; Ti distribution in Table 1; Fig. 6c.

## Cr (Chromium)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu lightweight MEAs based on Al3Ti.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 3–10 at.% in studied alloys.
- **Main Function**: Helps transform D022 to L12 structure; promotes D8a phase formation; provides passive element for oxidation resistance during wear.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Cr preferentially partitions to D8a phase (9.20 at.% Cr in D8a vs 2.94 at.% in L12 for the Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy). Increasing Cr from 5 to 10 at.% increases D8a from 15 to 20 vol.%; B2 fraction increase is secondary and attributed to Al decrease rather than direct Cr effect.
- **Property Effect**: Increasing Cr content raises strength and hardness but reduces ductility. Specific strength values for Cr=10 at.% are: yield 716 MPa, peak 1307 MPa, strain 12.4% (Al50Ti20Cr10Mn10Fe5Cu5).
- **Potential Side Effects**: More Cr means more brittle D8a phase, which reduces compressive plasticity and can reduce wear resistance due to surface fragmentation.
- **Original Evidence**: Table 1; Section 3.1; Fig. 6c; conclusion (a).

## Mn (Manganese)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu lightweight MEAs based on Al3Ti.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 3–10 at.%.
- **Main Function**: Helps transform D022 to L12; strongly promotes D8a phase formation.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Mn concentrates in D8a (7.41 at.% Mn in D8a for the Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy). Increasing Mn from 5 to 10 at.% raises D8a from 5 to 15 vol.% while B2 does not change.
- **Property Effect**: Increasing Mn content from 5 to 10 at.% raised yield from 519 to 650 MPa and peak from 1253 to 1366 MPa in Al55Ti20Cr5Mn10Fe5Cu5; strain decreased from 18.0 to 16.7%.
- **Potential Side Effects**: More Mn-promoted D8a reduces ductility and may lead to wear surface fragmentation at excessive levels.
- **Original Evidence**: Section 3.1; Table 1; Tables 2 and 3.

## Fe (Iron)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu lightweight MEAs based on Al3Ti.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 3–5 at.% in studied alloys.
- **Main Function**: Contributes to D022-to-L12 transformation; partitions preferentially to B2 phase; contributes to in-situ hard second phases.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Fe is more concentrated in B2 phase (10.76 at.% Fe in B2 vs 3.62 at.% in L12 for Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5).
- **Property Effect**: Fe is part of the third-element addition that increases hardness and strength. Not separately varied; no isolated Fe-level comparison.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Over-alloying with Fe and other transition elements increases brittle second phases.
- **Original Evidence**: Table 1; Section 3.1.

## Cu (Copper)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu lightweight MEAs based on Al3Ti.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 3–5 at.% in studied alloys.
- **Main Function**: Helps transform D022 to L12; present in all phases; minor segregation.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Cu shows very slight segregation. Cu content in L12 is relatively high among the third elements (5.17 at.% in L12) compared to Cr and Mn.
- **Property Effect**: No isolated comparison; part of overall ternary element addition.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not discussed separately.
- **Original Evidence**: Table 1; Section 3.1.

## Cr + Mn + Fe + Cu (combined addition)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Al-Ti-based lightweight MEAs.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Total third-element addition ranges from 12 at.% (3+3+3+3) to 30 at.% (10+10+5+5).
- **Main Function**: Combined transformation of D022 to L12 for ductility; in-situ formation of B2 and D8a hard second phases for strengthening.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: The four elements concentrate in B2 and D8a phases while L12 remains Al-Ti rich. Increasing their total content increases B2 and D8a volume fractions at the expense of L12.
- **Property Effect**: Higher total content increases hardness and compressive yield strength but reduces fracture strain. E.g., Al63Ti25Cr3Mn3Fe3Cu3 (hardest L12-rich, 94 vol.% L12): 409 MPa yield, 20.5% strain. Al50Ti20Cr10Mn10Fe5Cu5 (55 vol.% L12): 716 MPa yield, 12.4% strain.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Decrease in ductility; possible surface fragmentation during wear leading to wear resistance decrease at high second-phase fractions.
- **Original Evidence**: Fig. 9a; Tables 2 and 3; Conclusions (a) and (b).

## SiC (in reference Al-matrix composite only)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Al-matrix composite (reference material, not the MEA alloy system).
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 30 wt% SiC particles.
- **Main Function**: Reinforcement of Al matrix for strength and wear resistance.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: SiC particles are the dark phase dispersed in the Al matrix. At 300 °C, Al matrix softens and fails to retain particles, causing particle detachment or extrusion with plastically deformed matrix.
- **Property Effect**: Lower hardness than all Al3Ti-based MEAs; larger wear volume loss (97.3 × 10⁶ μm³ at RT; 71.8 × 10⁶ μm³ at 300 °C) than MEAs.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Severe plastic deformation at elevated temperature; severe transfer of Al to Si3N4 counter ball.
- **Original Evidence**: Fig. 7; Figs. 8, 10, 12; Table 3.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## D022-to-L12 Phase Transformation (ductility improvement)

- **Related elements**: Cr, Mn, Fe, Cu as third alloying elements.
- **Typical alloys**: Al3Ti-based MEAs; representative alloy Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5.
- **Key compositions**: Third-element contents between 3 and 10 at.% each.
- **Microstructural change**: Tetragonal D022 Al3Ti transforms to cubic L12 (Al,M)3Ti with more slip systems.
- **Property improvement**: Compressive fracture strain from brittle (not numerically given for binary Al3Ti in this paper) to 12.4–20.5% at room temperature for the MEAs.
- **Risks and limitations**: L12 formation alone lowers strength; second phases needed for compensation.
- **Original evidence**: Introduction; Fig. 1; conclusions (a).

## Second-Phase Strengthening (B2 and D8a)

- **Related elements**: Cr, Mn, Fe, Cu; excess Ti.
- **Typical alloys**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu lightweight MEAs.
- **Key compositions**: B2 phase rich in Fe and Ti; D8a phase rich in Cr and Mn.
- **Microstructural change**: In-situ formation of hard ordered B2 and D8a intermetallic second phases within L12 matrix during arc melting.
- **Property improvement**: Increasing B2 + D8a volume fraction from 6 to 45 vol.% raises yield strength from 409 to 716 MPa at room temperature; increases hardness.
- **Risks and limitations**: Decrease in compressive fracture strain from 20.5 to 12.4%; excessive second phases reduce wear resistance due to surface fragmentation.
- **Original evidence**: Figs. 8 and 9a; Tables 2 and 3.

## Long-Range-Order (LRO) Strengthening / Thermal Stability

- **Related elements/Phases**: Ordered L12 matrix, ordered B2, ordered D8a.
- **Typical alloys**: Al3Ti-based MEAs, particularly Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5.
- **Key compositions**: 80 vol.% L12 + 15 vol.% B2 + 5 vol.% D8a.
- **Microstructural change**: Long-range ordering in intermetallic phases effectively impedes atomic diffusion and dislocation movement.
- **Property improvement**: Limited hardness decrease with temperature: 6.1% (100 °C), 12.5% (300 °C), 19.5% (500 °C), much better than Ti6Al4V (8.2%, 16.4%, 29.6%). Yield strength 442 MPa at 500 °C surpasses Ti6Al4V.
- **Risks and limitations**: Not discussed.
- **Original evidence**: Section 4; Fig. 16; conclusion (d).

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related elements**: Minor amounts of Cr, Mn, Fe, Cu in L12 phase.
- **Typical alloys**: Al3Ti-based MEAs.
- **Key compositions**: L12 contains 2.94 Cr, 2.94 Mn, 3.62 Fe, 5.17 Cu (at.%).
- **Microstructural change**: Third elements in solid solution in L12.
- **Property improvement**: The paper attributes most strengthening to second phases rather than to solid solution in L12 explicitly. The paper contrasts L12/B2/D8a ordered structures with “solid solution alloys,” stating ordered structures have superior high-temperature performance.
- **Risks and limitations**: Excessive third-element addition leads to brittle second phases rather than remaining in solid solution.
- **Original evidence**: Table 1; discussion in Section 4.

## Oxidation-Resistant Alloying

- **Related elements**: Ti, Cr, Al as passive elements.
- **Typical alloys**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu MEAs.
- **Key compositions**: Al 60 at.%, Ti 20 at.%, Cr 5 at.% in representative alloy.
- **Microstructural change**: Formation of protective oxide film or scale during elevated-temperature wear.
- **Property improvement**: Lower oxygen intensity on MEA worn surface compared to Al/SiCp at both room temperature and 300 °C; better high-temperature wear resistance.
- **Risks and limitations**: The paper notes phases are all Al-based intermetallics and oxidation is integrated over the contact zone; phase-level differences in oxidation sensitivity are not considered large.
- **Original evidence**: Figs. 11 and 13; Section 3.3; Section 4.

## Wear Resistance Mechanism (Hard Phase Retention)

- **Related elements/phases**: L12 matrix + B2 + D8a phases.
- **Typical alloys**: Al-Ti-Cr-Mn-Fe-Cu MEAs.
- **Key compositions**: Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 and Al55Ti20Cr5Mn10Fe5Cu5 optimal for wear resistance.
- **Microstructural change**: Balanced volume fractions of ductile L12 matrix and hard B2/D8a phases; at appropriate fractions, abrasive wear dominated.
- **Property improvement**: Wear volume loss at RT: 50.5 × 10⁶ μm³ for Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 and 48.7 × 10⁶ μm³ for Al55Ti20Cr5Mn10Fe5Cu5, compared to 97.3 × 10⁶ μm³ for Al/SiCp. At 300 °C: 61.6 and 53.2 × 10⁶ μm³ vs 71.8 × 10⁶ μm³ for Al/SiCp.
- **Risks and limitations**: Excessive second phases (Al50Ti20Cr10Mn10Fe5Cu5 with 45 vol.% B2 + D8a) reduce plasticity/toughness and cause surface fragmentation during wear.
- **Original evidence**: Table 3; Figs. 10 and 11; Section 3.3.

# Key Conclusions

1. Alloying Al3Ti with Cr, Mn, Fe, and Cu transforms the brittle tetragonal D022 structure into the ductile cubic L12 structure, with B2 and D8a second phases formed in situ.
2. Increasing the contents of Cr, Mn, Fe, and Cu increases B2 and D8a volume fractions at the expense of L12, leading to higher hardness and compressive strength but reduced compressive fracture strain.
3. All Al3Ti-based MEAs exhibit superior wear resistance compared to an Al-matrix composite reinforced with 30 wt% SiC particles at room temperature and 300 °C.
4. The representative Al60Ti20Cr5Mn5Fe5Cu5 alloy achieves 442 MPa yield strength and ~270 HV2 hardness at 500 °C, outperforming Ti6Al4V at that temperature. Its thermal stability is attributed to long-range ordering in L12, B2, and D8a phases.
5. Al3Ti-based MEAs are promising candidates for medium-temperature (300–500 °C) lightweight mechanical and tribological applications bridging the gap between aluminum and titanium alloys.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Not a refractory alloy system**: The studied alloys are Al-Ti-based lightweight intermetallics, not Nb/W/Mo/Ta-based refractory alloys or refractory high-entropy alloys. Applicable temperature range is 300–500 °C, far below typical refractory alloy use temperatures (800 °C and above).
- **No dedicated high-temperature (>500 °C) mechanical or oxidation tests**: Mechanical performance above 500 °C was not tested. Thermal stability evidence rests mainly on hardness retention from room temperature to 500 °C and a single 500 °C compression test.
- **No dedicated oxidation tests**: Oxidation-related statements are inferred from oxygen EDS during wear; no isothermal oxidation mass gain or oxide phase identification was performed.
- **No statement of impurities**: C, O, N, H levels are not reported.
- **Grain size / grain boundary information**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Elastic modulus, fracture toughness, creep, fatigue**: Not measured.
- **Fabrication**: Arc melting only; no comparison with powder metallurgy, AM, casting with hot working, etc.
- **Wear testing**: Ball-on-disc with Si3N4 ball, 5 N load, ~2 cm/s at RT and 300 °C only. No tests in vacuum/inert atmosphere to separate mechanical from oxidative wear.
- **The paper is published in Wear (special issue: Wear of Materials 2025)**, and tribology is a major focus; microstructure-deformation correlations are qualitatively discussed but not quantified (e.g., no dislocation analysis).
