# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: ZrTiO4 secondary phase effects on ductility and toughness of molybdenum alloys
- **Authors**: Bo-Liang Hu, Song-Wei Ge, Jia-Yu Han, Xing-Jiang Hua, Shi-Lei Li, Hai-Rui Xing, Long-Teng Yuan, Xiang-Yang Zhang, Kuai-She Wang, Ping Hu, Alex A. Volinsky
- **Journal**: Materials Letters
- **Year**: 2022 (Volume 307, Article 131077; Available online 13 October 2021)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.matlet.2021.131077
- **Alloy system studied**: Mo-ZrTiO4 (Mo-ZT) molybdenum alloy system
- **Research objective**: To investigate the effects of ZrTiO4 secondary phase content (0.5–3 wt%) on the ductility, toughness, and tensile properties of molybdenum alloys, and to understand the plasticity-toughening mechanisms.
- **Material category**: Mo-based alloy with ZrTiO4 secondary phase (nano/micron particle dispersion-strengthened molybdenum alloy)

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system**: Mo-ZT (Mo-ZrTiO4) molybdenum alloy
- **Research object**: Six molybdenum alloy samples with varying ZrTiO4 secondary phase content (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, and 3 wt%)
- **Base metal**: Molybdenum (Mo)
- **Secondary phase**: ZrTiO4 (zirconium titanate)
- **Material category**: Mo-based alloy with oxide-based secondary phase reinforcement; not a refractory high-entropy alloy; not an ODS alloy in the strict Y2O3/La2O3 sense, but a ceramic-oxide-reinforced molybdenum alloy

# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **ZrTiO4 powder preparation**: Nano-coprecipitation method; ZrTiO4 powder size 20–40 nm; single-crystal structure. Some powder was reduced under argon atmosphere and in air; the air-reduced powder had higher diffraction peak intensity and crystallinity (XRD, Fig. 1b).
- **Mixing**: Molybdenum powder mixed with ultrasonically dispersed ZrTiO4. Mixture dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 4 h.
- **Pressing**: Cold isostatic pressing at 180 MPa for 10 min.
- **Sintering**: Hydrogen atmosphere sintering at 1780 °C.
- **Rolling**: The sintered samples were hot-rolled, warm-rolled, and cold-rolled to a 0.5 mm plate.
- **Annealing**: Some samples were annealed at 900 °C for 2 h.
- **No arc melting, vacuum melting, induction melting, mechanical alloying, hot pressing, spark plasma sintering, hot isostatic pressing, or additive manufacturing methods** were used in this paper.
- **Deformation amount**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper (only final plate thickness of 0.5 mm given).

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase**: Molybdenum (BCC structure implied but not explicitly stated in this paper).
- **Secondary phase**: ZrTiO4, present in both nanoparticle (20–40 nm starting powder size) and micron particle forms, depending on the alloy composition.
  - At 0.5 wt% ZrTiO4: the secondary phase is in the form of a small number of nanoparticles.
  - At 1 wt% ZrTiO4: the secondary phase mainly exists as micron particles.
  - With increasing content (1.5–2 wt%): the volume fraction of both nano- and micron particles begins to increase.
  - At 3 wt%: the volume fraction of nanoparticles decreases and the volume fraction of micron particles continues to increase.
- **Grain size**: The paper does not explicitly report grain size values. Subgrain areas are reported for deformed samples:
  - Mo-1ZT: average subgrain area = 0.73 μm².
  - Mo-1.5ZT: subgrain area ≈ 0.85 μm².
  - Mo-2.5ZT: subgrain area ≈ 3.4 μm² (no small subgrains formed).
- **Grain boundary features**: Not explicitly discussed in the original paper.
- **Elemental segregation**: Not explicitly discussed.
- **Precipitate size and distribution**: 
  - Starting ZrTiO4 powder: 20–40 nm, good morphology and dispersibility, single crystal (Fig. 1a).
  - After processing, ZrTiO4 exists as both nanoparticles and micron particles, with distribution dependent on ZrTiO4 content.
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed**:
  - The paper focuses on the **dispersion/particle interaction with dislocations** as the main ductility/toughness mechanism. ZrTiO4 nanoparticles with good interface bonding inhibit long-distance dislocation movement, promote uniform dislocation distribution, and prevent the formation of small subgrains.
  - **Interface bonding**: The interface electron density of ZrTiO4/Mo reached 6.5 × 10⁵ e/nm³, much higher than TiC, ZrC, TiO2, ZrO2, and La2O3 (~1.5 × 10⁵ e/nm³), as reported in the authors' prior work (reference [6]).
  - Solid-solution strengthening, grain-boundary strengthening, ODS strengthening, and carbide/boride precipitation strengthening are not explicitly discussed.

# Mechanical Properties

**Tensile properties of rolled alloys** (from Fig. 2a and 2c):
- All rolled alloys have tensile strength > 1000 MPa.
- Mo-3ZT (rolled): 1078 MPa (highest strength) with elongation also reported (exact value not explicitly stated in the text).
- Mo-2.5ZT (rolled): 1028 MPa (lowest strength among rolled alloys).
- Tensile tests were carried out using an Instron 8801 fatigue tester with a tensile speed of 0.5 mm/min.
- Samples prepared according to ASTM E8 standard (metal sheet tensile samples).

**Tensile properties of annealed alloys** (from Fig. 2b, 2d, and text):
- Mo-1ZT: elongation = 8.5% (reported in TEM section); another location states "8.6%".
- Mo-1.5ZT: elongation = 16.3%.
- Mo-2.5ZT: elongation = 25.4% (reported in Conclusions); another location states "25% elongation (annealed)"; the abstract states "25% elongation (annealed)".
- Comparison: Mo-2.5ZT elongation increased by 166% compared with Mo-1ZT alloy (from 8.6% to 23%, per Section 3.3; also stated as "from 8.6% to 23%").
- The abstract states: "The Mo-ZT alloy with 2.5 wt% ZrTiO4 has the ultimate tensile strength above 1,000 MPa (rolled) and 25% elongation (annealed), which is higher than previously reported molybdenum alloys."

**Hardness** (from Fig. 2e):
- Rolled alloy hardness: 325–367 HV range.
- Annealed alloy hardness: 286–326 HV range.
- Mo-2ZT alloy has the lowest hardness in both states.
- Mo-2.5ZT has the highest hardness.
- Hardness tested with a 401MVD digital micro Vickers hardness tester, 200 g load, 15 s dwell time.

**Dislocation density** (from TEM analysis, Section 3.3):
- Deformed sample at 25% elongation: dislocation density = 1.5 × 10¹⁰ /cm².

**Comparison with other Mo alloys** (Fig. 2f): TZM and Mo-ZrO2 alloys mechanical properties are compared, but specific numerical values for TZM and Mo-ZrO2 are not explicitly stated in the text of this paper.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## ZrTiO4

### [Element or Element Combination]
ZrTiO4 (zirconium titanate, a ceramic oxide containing Zr, Ti, and O)

### [Applicable Alloy System]
Mo-based alloys (Mo-ZT alloy system)

### [Addition Amount or Composition Range]
0.5–3 wt% ZrTiO4 added to molybdenum; optimum was 2.5 wt% for ductility and 3 wt% for rolled tensile strength.

### [Main Function]
Strengthening and toughening. ZrTiO4 secondary phase simultaneously increases tensile strength and elongation of molybdenum alloys; significantly improves ductility/toughness without decreasing room-temperature strength.

### [Microstructural Mechanism]
- The starting ZrTiO4 powder is 20–40 nm, single crystal, with good dispersibility.
- ZrTiO4 nanoparticles have strong interface bonding with the Mo matrix: interface electron density 6.5 × 10⁵ e/nm³, which is much higher than TiC, ZrC, TiO2, ZrO2, and La2O3 (~1.5 × 10⁵ e/nm³).
- ZrTiO4 nanoparticles inhibit long-range dislocation movement, promote uniform dislocation distribution, and prevent the formation of small subgrains during deformation.
- With 0.5 wt% ZrTiO4, only a small number of nanoparticles form; at 1 wt%, ZrTiO4 mainly exists as micron particles; at 2.5 wt%, nanoparticle volume fraction increases significantly; at 3 wt%, micron particle volume fraction increases and nanoparticle fraction decreases.
- More plastic deformation energy is stored within grains when nano-ZrTiO4 particles are abundant (Mo-2.5ZT), leading to enhanced plasticity.

### [Property Effect]
- Rolled tensile strength: Mo-2.5ZT = 1028 MPa; Mo-3ZT = 1078 MPa; all > 1000 MPa.
- Annealed elongation: Mo-1ZT = 8.5–8.6%; Mo-1.5ZT = 16.3%; Mo-2.5ZT = 25.4% (166% increase over Mo-1ZT).
- Rolled hardness: 325–367 HV; annealed hardness: 286–326 HV.
- Dislocation density at 25% elongation = 1.5 × 10¹⁰ /cm².
- Subgrain area: Mo-1ZT = 0.73 μm²; Mo-1.5ZT ≈ 0.85 μm²; Mo-2.5ZT ≈ 3.4 μm².
- Testing temperature: room temperature.

### [Potential Side Effects]
- At 1 wt% ZrTiO4, the secondary phase mainly exists as micron particles, resulting in uniform dislocation hindrance failure and formation of small subgrains, limiting ductility.
- At 3 wt%, nanoparticle volume fraction decreases while micron particle fraction increases, which may reduce the ductility-enhancing effect compared with 2.5 wt%.
- The paper does not report potential issues such as oxidation problems, hot cracking, increased density, or reduced workability.

### [Original Evidence]
- Section 1 (Introduction): "Our team has confirmed that ZrTiO4 has a strong bonding with the molybdenum matrix. The interface electron density of ZrTiO4 reached 6.5 × 10⁵ e/nm³, which is much higher than TiC, ZrC, TiO2, ZrO2, and La2O3 (~1.5 × 10⁵ e/nm³)."
- Section 3.3: "The main reason is that when the content of the micro-particles in the alloy reaches a certain value, the ZrTiO4 starts to be dispersed and distributed in the nano-phase."
- Fig. 2 and Table 1 provide composition and mechanical property data.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Nanoparticle dispersion strengthening / plasticity-toughening mechanism

### [Related Elements]
ZrTiO4 nanoparticles (containing Zr, Ti, O); Mo matrix.

### [Typical Alloys]
Mo-ZT alloy system (Mo-0.5ZT, Mo-1ZT, Mo-1.5ZT, Mo-2ZT, Mo-2.5ZT, Mo-3ZT).

### [Key Compositions]
2.5 wt% ZrTiO4 for optimal ductility; 3 wt% ZrTiO4 for highest rolled tensile strength.

### [Microstructural Changes]
- At low ZrTiO4 content (0.5 wt%): small number of nanoparticles.
- At 1 wt%: mainly micron particles, causing non-uniform dislocation hindrance and formation of small subgrains (average area 0.73 μm²).
- At 1.5 wt%: increased micron and nano particles, larger subgrains (≈0.85 μm²).
- At 2.5 wt%: significantly increased nanoparticle volume fraction; uniform dislocation distribution; no small subgrains formed; subgrain area ≈3.4 μm².
- Dislocation density at 25% elongation: 1.5 × 10¹⁰ /cm².

### [Property Improvements]
- Elongation (annealed) increased from 8.5–8.6% (Mo-1ZT) to 25.4% (Mo-2.5ZT), a 166% increase.
- Tensile strength (rolled) maintained above 1000 MPa: 1028 MPa (Mo-2.5ZT) to 1078 MPa (Mo-3ZT).
- Room-temperature ductility and toughness improved without strength loss.

### [Risks and Limitations]
- If nanoparticle fraction is insufficient (e.g., at 1 wt% where micron particles dominate), the dislocation movement cannot be uniformly hindered, and small subgrains form, limiting ductility.
- At 3 wt%, the nanoparticle fraction decreases while micron particle fraction increases, so the ductility enhancement mechanism may weaken.
- The paper does not address high-temperature mechanical performance, creep behavior, oxidation resistance, or long-term microstructural stability of the ZrTiO4 dispersion at elevated temperatures.

# Key Conclusions

1. The Mo-ZT alloy system was successfully prepared with ZrTiO4 secondary phase content ranging from 0.5 to 3 wt% via powder metallurgy, cold isostatic pressing, hydrogen sintering at 1780 °C, and multi-step rolling to 0.5 mm plate, followed by annealing at 900 °C for 2 h.

2. The tensile strength of rolled Mo-ZT alloys is above 1000 MPa regardless of ZrTiO4 content. Mo-3ZT has the highest rolled strength at 1078 MPa, and Mo-2.5ZT has 1028 MPa.

3. Ductility reaches a maximum at 2.5 wt% ZrTiO4 with an annealed room-temperature elongation of 25.4%, which is higher than previously reported molybdenum alloys.

4. Compared with Mo-1ZT alloy, the Mo-2.5ZT alloy elongation increased by 166% (from 8.6% to 23%, or from 8.5% to 25.4% depending on the section cited).

5. The main ductility-improving mechanism is the presence of nano-sized ZrTiO4 particles with excellent interface bonding to the Mo matrix (interface electron density 6.5 × 10⁵ e/nm³). These nanoparticles inhibit long-range dislocation movement, promote uniform dislocation distribution, prevent the formation of small subgrains, and store more plastic deformation energy within grains.

6. Microstructure evolution with ZrTiO4 content: at 0.5 wt% nanoparticles dominate; at 1 wt% micron particles dominate; at 2.5 wt% nanoparticle volume fraction increases significantly; at 3 wt% micron particles dominate again with decreased nanoparticle fraction.

7. Hardness ranges from 325–367 HV (rolled) and 286–326 HV (annealed). Mo-2.5ZT has the highest hardness and Mo-2ZT the lowest in both states.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Material system**: The results are specific to Mo-ZrTiO4 alloys prepared by the powder metallurgy route described. Generalization to other Mo-based alloys or other secondary phase types (carbides, other oxides) requires caution.
- **Testing temperature**: Only room-temperature tensile properties were tested. High-temperature strength, creep properties, and oxidation resistance were not evaluated.
- **No at.% data**: The paper only reports wt.% values; no atomic percentage values are given.
- **No impurity analysis**: Levels of C, O, N, and H impurities were not reported.
- **The ZrTiO4 phase content in the final consolidated alloy**: The as-designed composition is reported in Table 1, but the actual retained ZrTiO4 phase fraction after sintering, rolling, and annealing was not quantified by analytical methods (e.g., Rietveld refinement or chemical analysis).
- **Elemental segregation**: Not investigated.
- **Grain size**: Grain size values before and after deformation are not explicitly reported; only subgrain areas from TEM are given.
- **Comparison with other Mo alloys**: Fig. 2f mentions TZM and Mo-ZrO2 alloys, but specific numerical comparison data are not provided in the text.
- **Fracture toughness**: Although the article title mentions "toughness," fracture toughness values (e.g., K_IC) are not reported; toughness is inferred from elongation and interface bonding arguments.
- **The interface electron density of 6.5 × 10⁵ e/nm³**: This value is cited from the authors' prior work (reference [6]) and is not independently measured or confirmed in this paper. It should be treated as prior evidence, not a new measurement.
- **Elongation values discrepancy**: The paper reports both 8.5% and 8.6% for Mo-1ZT elongation, and both 23% and 25.4% for Mo-2.5ZT elongation in different sections. The abstract cites 25% for Mo-2.5ZT and Section 3.3 states elongation increased from 8.6% to 23%. This minor inconsistency should be noted when citing exact values.
