- **Authors**: Xin Zeng, Fangjie Li, Xie Zhou, Weizhi Yan, Jiuxiao Li, Dongye Yang, Qin Shen, Xiaowei Wang, Min Liu
- **Journal**: Materials Chemistry and Physics
- **Year**: 2023
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.matchemphys.2023.127314
- **Alloy system studied**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 high-entropy alloys (x = 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5)
- **Research objective**: To understand the phase stability at intermediate temperature (600 °C) and mechanical behavior changes of dual-phase AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 HEAs after annealing
- **Material category**: Refractory high-entropy alloy / multi-principal element alloy (Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni system, not a refractory-metal HEA); dual-phase FCC/BCC high-entropy alloy

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 high-entropy alloys with equal valence electron concentration (VEC = 8.00)
- **Sample designations**: Fe0.5 (x = 0.5), Fe1.5 (x = 1.5), Fe2.5 (x = 2.5), Fe3.5 (x = 3.5)
- **Key research focus**: 
  - Phase stability at intermediate temperature (600 °C for 50 h)
  - FCC-to-BCC phase transformation during annealing
  - Effect of Fe content on phase constitution and mechanical properties
- **Not in scope**: This paper does NOT study Nb-W alloys, Mo-W alloys, W-based alloys, or additively manufactured refractory alloys.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Matrix and Secondary Phases

- **Matrix phase**: FCC/L12 (disordered FCC solid solution containing ordered L12 particles)
- **Second phase**: BCC/B2 (ordered B2 phase formed in the BCC solid solution)
- **Phase morphology**: 
  - α regions: Primary FCC/L12 phase (light gray)
  - β regions: Lamellar eutectic in inter-dendrite regions (dark gray)
  - β1 regions: Thick or needle-like thin precipitates within dendrites

## Grain Structure

- Dendritic solidification structure with three distinctive regions: primary dendrites (α), inter-dendritic lamellar eutectic (β), and precipitates within dendrites (β1)

## Phase Transformation

- **FCC-to-BCC phase transformation** occurred during annealing at 600 °C for 50 h
- Phase transition rate (ratio of added BCC/B2 fraction after annealing to FCC/L12 fraction in homogenized state) was highest for Fe0.5 and Fe1.5 alloys, lower for Fe2.5 and Fe3.5
- The transformation involved two possible mechanisms: coarsening of original BCC/B2 phase and precipitation of new BCC/B2 phase
- Phase interface served as channel for "short-diffusion" of Al and Ni elements during annealing

## KAM Analysis

- Annealed samples exhibited higher KAM values (green color in EBSD maps) compared to homogenized samples, indicating increased geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) and strain concentration due to phase transformation-induced lattice distortion

## Elemental Partitioning

- α regions (FCC/L12): Enriched in Co, Cr, Fe
- β/β1 regions (BCC/B2): Enriched in Al, Ni
- β1 phase has higher Fe and Cr content than β phase

## Grain Size

- Not explicitly stated as numerical values in the original paper. "A tiny coarsening in the phase of Fex alloys were found after annealing at 600 °C for 50 h" is mentioned, but specific grain size values are not provided.

## Testing Conditions

- Geometry: Rectangular dog-bone shape, gauge dimensions 5 × 1.7 × 1 mm³
- Strain rate: 10⁻³ s⁻¹ (engineering strain rate)
- Temperature: Room temperature (not explicitly stated as "room temperature" but standard tensile testing is implied; no elevated temperature tensile tests were performed)
- Loading mode: Uniaxial tension
- Atmosphere: Not explicitly stated

## Fracture Behavior

- Homogenized Fe0.5: Ductile fracture in FCC/L12 phase + brittle-like fracture in BCC/B2 phase; cracks propagated along phase boundaries; nearly pure shear fracture
- Fe1.5, Fe2.5, Fe3.5 (both states): Dimples observed (ductile fracture)
- Annealed Fe0.5: No dimples; cracks along FCC/L12 and BCC/B2 phase boundaries; premature fracture (~9% elongation)

## Strain-Hardening Behavior

- Strain-hardening rate curves divided into five stages (I–V)
- Dual-phase structure produces back stress hardening via geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) accumulated near phase interfaces
- Two ascent stages (II and IV) observed in most alloys
- Stage V disappeared in annealed Fe0.5 due to premature fracture
- Annealed alloys generally showed higher strain-hardening rates than homogenized samples

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

Not applicable to this paper. The study focuses on intermediate-temperature phase stability (600 °C) and mechanical behavior; oxidation resistance, oxidation layers, or high-temperature oxidation behavior are not discussed.

**Thermal stability considerations**:
- The paper discusses phase stability at intermediate temperature (400–900 °C range mentioned in introduction; specifically 600 °C in this work)
- HEA solid solution stability is challenged at intermediate temperatures due to decreased ΔSmix contribution to ΔGmix and increased diffusion rates
- The FCC-to-BCC transformation is an indication of limited phase stability, not oxidation-related

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Fe (Iron)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 high-entropy alloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: x = 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5 in AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5; phase compositions: 10.53–43.41 at.% Fe in FCC phase (homogenized); 6.10–31.56 at.% Fe in BCC/B2 phase
- **Main Function**: FCC phase stabilizer; controls dual-phase fraction; improves plasticity/ductility
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Increasing Fe content increases FCC/L12 phase volume fraction (from 74% at Fe0.5 to 92% at Fe3.5, homogenized state); Fe decreases atomic size difference δ, which weakens distortion energy and lowers the driving force for FCC-to-BCC transformation
- **Property Effect**:
  - Homogenized: UTS decreases from 968 MPa (Fe0.5) to 753 MPa (Fe3.5); elongation increases from 16% to 30%
  - Annealed: UTS of Fe1.5, Fe2.5, Fe3.5 enhanced to 1039, 1013, 877 MPa respectively; elongation 21%, 25%, 26% respectively
  - Fe-rich α (FCC) phase contains more Fe; β1 phase has higher Fe than β phase
- **Potential Side Effects**: Excessive Fe reduces strength (via increased soft FCC fraction); Fe0.5 alloy (lower Fe) exhibits premature fracture after annealing due to large BCC phase fraction
- **Original Evidence**: Table 1, Table 2, Fig. 3e–f, Section 4 "The increasing content of Fe element in the Fex alloys reduced the δ, which might weaken the distortion energy and lower the ability of transformation from FCC to BCC phase."

## Al + Ni (Element Combination)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 high-entropy alloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Al: 8.96–31.28 at.% in measured phases; Ni: 27.54–51.31 at.% in measured phases
- **Main Function**: BCC/B2 phase formers; contribute to hard phase formation
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Al and Ni have strong negative mixing enthalpy, leading to segregation and formation of BCC/B2 phase (β/β1) within FCC/L12 grains; phase interfaces serve as channels for "short-diffusion" of Al and Ni during annealing, promoting BCC/B2 grain growth
- **Property Effect**: Al-Ni-rich BCC/B2 phase provides strength (hard phase); FCC/L12 provides ductility (soft phase)
- **Potential Side Effects**: Excessive BCC/B2 fraction (as in annealed Fe0.5, 34 vol.%) leads to premature fracture and reduced elongation (9%)
- **Original Evidence**: Table 1, Fig. 2, Section 4 "the β/β1 regions were rich in Al and Ni"; "the segregation of Al and Ni elements would occur within the primary FCC/L12 grains for the relatively high degree of negative mixing enthalpy between Al and Ni"

## Co + Cr (Element Combination)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 high-entropy alloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Co: 9.21–21.77 at.% in measured phases; Cr: 1.79–11.64 at.% in measured phases
- **Main Function**: FCC/L12 phase formers/stabilizers
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Co, Cr (and Fe) enrichment in α regions (FCC/L12); primary solidification product is Cr, Fe-rich FCC/L12 phase
- **Property Effect**: Contributes to ductile FCC phase; higher Co, Cr content associated with higher elongation in low-Fe alloys
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not discussed in detail
- **Original Evidence**: Table 1 "The α regions were rich in Co, Cr and Fe"

## Cr (Individual Element)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 high-entropy alloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 1.79–11.64 at.% in measured phase compositions; nominal 0.5 molar fraction relative to AlCo
- **Main Function**: FCC/L12 phase stabilizing element; partitions to FCC phase
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Cr enrichment observed in α (FCC/L12) regions; Cr is a BCC-former in conventional alloys but here stabilizes FCC phase via partitioning
- **Property Effect**: Contributes to solid-solution strengthening in FCC phase; not individually quantified
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not discussed in this paper
- **Original Evidence**: Table 1 shows Cr enrichment in α regions (e.g., 11.06 at.% in Fe0.5 homogenized α-H)

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related elements**: Al, Co, Cr, Fe, Ni
- **Typical alloys**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 (all four variants)
- **Key compositions**: All alloys studied
- **Microstructural changes**: Concentrated multi-principal-element solid solutions in FCC/L12 and BCC/B2 phases
- **Property improvements**: Baseline strength of all alloys; homogenized Fe0.5 shows YS = 515 MPa, UTS = 968 MPa
- **Risks and limitations**: Contributions not separately quantified; lattice distortion increases after annealing due to phase transformation

## Dual-Phase / Heterogeneous Structure Strengthening (Back Stress Hardening)

- **Related elements**: Al, Ni (BCC/B2 formers) + Co, Cr, Fe (FCC formers)
- **Typical alloys**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5
- **Key compositions**: All four variants with FCC/L12 + BCC/B2 dual-phase structure
- **Microstructural changes**: Soft FCC/L12 phase + hard BCC/B2 phase; plastic deformation incompatibility leads to geometrically necessary dislocation (GND) accumulation at phase interfaces
- **Property improvements**: 
  - Back stress hardening enhances strain-hardening capability and uniform elongation
  - Annealed Fe2.5: YS = 372 MPa, UTS = 1013 MPa, ε = 25% (better than homogenized)
  - Annealed Fe1.5: UTS = 1039 MPa (up from 866 MPa)
- **Risks and limitations**: 
  - When BCC phase fraction exceeds ~25%, inhomogeneous deformation may lead to premature fracture via cleavage in BCC phase (as in annealed Fe0.5 with 34% BCC/B2)
  - Cracks propagate along phase boundaries
- **Original evidence**: Section 4 "The GNDs are claimed to block the motion of the dislocations and generate a long-range back stress hardening, enhancing both strain hardening capability and uniform elongation of the alloys"; Fig. 5

## Phase Transformation Strengthening (FCC→BCC during Annealing)

- **Related elements**: All alloying elements; phase stability governed by thermodynamic parameters Ω and δ
- **Typical alloys**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 (all variants)
- **Key compositions**: All alloys studied
- **Microstructural changes**: FCC-to-BCC/B2 phase transformation during 600 °C/50 h annealing; BCC/B2 fraction increases: Fe0.5: 26→34%, Fe1.5: 25→33%, Fe2.5: 8→12%, Fe3.5: 8→11%
- **Property improvements**: 
  - Annealed Fe1.5: UTS increased 866→1039 MPa
  - Annealed Fe2.5: YS increased 316→372 MPa, UTS 766→1013 MPa, elongation maintained at 25%
  - Annealed Fe3.5: UTS increased 753→877 MPa
- **Risks and limitations**: 
  - Annealed Fe0.5 lost ductility (16%→9%) due to excessive BCC/B2 phase (34%)
  - Lattice distortion-induced strain concentration (high KAM) can promote early fracture
- **Original evidence**: Fig. 3e–f, Table 2

## Thermal Stability / Phase Stability Control

- **Related parameters**: Ω (thermodynamic parameter combining ΔSmix and ΔHmix), δ (atomic size difference)
- **Typical alloys**: AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5
- **Key compositions**: Fe0.5 has lower Ω and higher δ → less stable; Fe3.5 has higher Ω and lower δ → more stable
- **Microstructural changes**: Higher Ω and lower δ reduce the extent of FCC-to-BCC phase transformation at 600 °C
- **Property improvements**: Better retention of phase constitution during intermediate-temperature annealing
- **Risks and limitations**: Ω and δ alone cannot strictly predict phase transformation fraction; more convincing thermodynamic evidence is needed
- **Original evidence**: Fig. 7, Section 4 "the higher Ω and lower δ were helpful to enhance the thermal stability of HEAs under an intermediate temperature"; "using the two parameters (Ω and δ) to illustrate the phase stability of the Fex alloy is superficial"

# Key Conclusions

1. A FCC-to-BCC phase transformation occurred in dual-phase AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 HEAs during annealing at 600 °C for 50 h, resulting in increased BCC/B2 phase fraction and optimized phase distribution.

2. Higher Ω (thermodynamic parameter) and lower δ (atomic size difference) enhance thermal stability of HEAs at intermediate temperatures, leading to less FCC-to-BCC transformation.

3. Fe content significantly affects phase constitution: higher Fe → higher FCC/L12 fraction (up to 92 vol.% in homogenized Fe3.5) and better resistance to FCC→BCC transformation.

4. The increased BCC/B2 fraction after annealing improved mechanical properties for most alloys through:
   - Hard phase strengthening
   - Back stress hardening from GND accumulation at phase interfaces
   - Optimization of phase distribution

5. The AlCoCr0.5Fe2.5Ni2.5 alloy achieved the best optimization: YS 316→372 MPa, UTS 766→1013 MPa, elongation maintained at 25% after annealing.

6. Excess BCC/B2 phase (annealed Fe0.5, 34%) causes premature fracture (elongation 9%) due to cleavage of BCC phase and crack propagation along phase boundaries.

7. The dual-phase structure produces back stress hardening via geometrically necessary dislocations, enhancing both strain-hardening capability and uniform elongation.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. **No high-temperature mechanical testing**: Only room-temperature tensile tests were performed. The actual mechanical behavior at intermediate temperature (400–900 °C) was not directly measured. The conclusions about suitability for intermediate-temperature service are based on room-temperature properties after intermediate-temperature annealing.

2. **No oxidation resistance data**: The paper does not address oxidation or corrosion behavior at any temperature.

3. **Phase stability mechanism not fully established**: The authors acknowledge that using Ω and δ parameters is "superficial" and that more convincing thermodynamic evidence is needed to explain the FCC→BCC transformation mechanism.

4. **No creep or creep-rupture data**: Long-term mechanical stability under load at elevated temperature was not evaluated.

5. **No fatigue or fracture toughness data**: Only tensile ductility and fracture surface observation were provided.

6. **Grain size not quantified**: Specific grain size values for the phases are not provided.

7. **Applicability limited to specific alloy system**: Conclusions apply to AlCoCr0.5FexNi2.5 with VEC = 8.00; generalization to other HEA systems requires caution.

8. **No composition-optimization beyond Fe variation**: Other alloying elements (Al, Co, Cr, Ni) were not varied in this study.

9. **Cooling rate effects not discussed**: The cooling method after annealing and homogenization is not specified, which may affect the resultant phase fractions.

10. **Annealing time fixed at 50 h**: The kinetics of FCC→BCC transformation over longer times or at different intermediate temperatures are not characterized in this study.
