- **Authors:** William L. Maag and William F. Mattson
- **Journal / Source:** NASA Technical Note, NASA TN D-6149
- **Year:** February 1971
- **DOI:** Not explicitly stated in the original paper (NASA TN D-6149)
- **Performing Organization:** Lewis Research Center, National Aeronautics and Space Administration, Cleveland, Ohio
- **Report Number:** NASA TN D-6149 (E-5822)
- **Alloy System Studied:** Pure tantalum, Ta-8W-2Hf (T-111), pure molybdenum, Mo-0.5Ti-0.08Zr-0.03C (TZM), Mo-1.2Ti-0.25Zr-0.15C (TZC)
- **Research Objective:** To provide experimental biaxial steady-state creep data for five refractory metals and alloys using internally pressurized thin-wall tubes, to compare biaxial creep data with uniaxial creep data using the von Mises criterion for plastic flow, and to develop steady-state creep-rate correlations for each material.
- **Material Category:** Refractory metals and alloys, including tantalum-based alloy (T-111) and molybdenum-based alloys (TZM and TZC)

# Alloy System and Research Object

This study investigates biaxial creep behavior of five refractory materials: pure tantalum, T-111 (Ta-8W-2Hf), pure molybdenum, TZM (Mo-0.5Ti-0.08Zr-0.03C), and TZC (Mo-1.2Ti-0.25Zr-0.15C). These materials have application as cladding materials for high-temperature fuel pins in liquid-metal-cooled nuclear reactors and space power nuclear reactors.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

**Material Procurement and Fabrication:**
- All materials were procured from commercial vendors in the form of bar stock or seamless tubing.
- The original form for each material was an arc-melted ingot.
- The ingot was extruded to a 1.905 cm (0.75 in.) diameter bar or tube.
- Materials were recrystallized prior to machining into final test tube dimensions.
- Test specimens were machined from both extruded tube stock and bar stock.
- Tubes were drilled and lapped.
- End caps of the same material were welded to both ends.
- All welding was performed by electron-beam method in a vacuum of 10⁻⁶ torr or by gas tungsten-arc method in an argon-purged dry box.

**Heat Treatment:**
- After machining and assembly into test specimens, each tube received a 1-hour heat treatment at 1922 K (3000 °F) before pressurization and testing commenced.
- For the TZM combined correlation, uniaxial comparison specimens were heat treated 1 hour at 1700 K (2200 °F) before creep testing.
- For T-111, all biaxial and uniaxial data represent a heat treatment of 1 hour at 1922 K (3000 °F) prior to creep testing.

**Test Specimen Geometry:**
- Tube length: 6.35 or 7.62 cm (2.5 or 3 in.)
- Outside diameter: 1.59 ± 0.00254 cm (0.625 ± 0.001 in.)
- Wall thickness: 0.0254 ± 0.00127 cm (0.010 ± 0.0005 in.), 0.0508 ± 0.00254 cm (0.020 ± 0.001 in.), or 0.1016 ± 0.00254 cm (0.040 ± 0.001 in.)
- Pressurizing tube: 0.3175 cm (0.125 in.) outside diameter, 0.0635 cm (0.025 in.) wall thickness, 30.48 cm (12 in.) long
- Inside and outside surface finish: 64 rms or better

# Mechanical Properties

**Test Conditions:**
- Temperature range: 1340 to 1922 K (1950 ° to 3000 °F)
- Effective stress range: 2.76×10⁶ to 2.21×10⁸ N/m² (400 to 32,000 psi)
- Effective creep rates: 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁶ hr⁻¹
- Test durations: 100 to 1000 hours
- Diametral strain: 0.1 to 30 percent
- Loading mode: Internally pressurized thin-wall tubes, biaxial stress state (tangential-to-axial stress ratio ≈ 2:1)
- Atmosphere: Argon gas environment

**Test Methodology:**
- Internally pressurized thin-wall tubes with diameter-to-wall-thickness ratios greater than 15
- Tangential creep rate determined by measuring outside diameter before and after each test
- No correction for true strain was used
- Primary creep included in total strain measurement
- Tests conducted at constant temperature and pressure

**Creep Rate Correlations (Equation: ε̇ = A exp(-ΔH/RT) σⁿ):**

**Tantalum:**
- Biaxial only: ε̇ = 4.779×10⁻¹⁸ σ³·⁰⁵⁴ exp(-248101/RT)
- Biaxial + Uniaxial: ε̇ = 1.356×10⁻²⁵ σ⁴·⁶⁹¹ exp(-364064/RT)
- Biaxial stress exponent n: 3.1
- Combined stress exponent n: 4.7
- Biaxial activation energy ΔH: 248 kJ/g-mole
- Combined activation energy ΔH: 364 kJ/g-mole
- Variability (one standard deviation, ε̇max/ε̇): 1.8 (biaxial), 1.9 (combined)
- Note: Biaxial test results for pure tantalum were the poorest of the materials tested, possibly due to appreciable primary creep not considered in the investigation.

**T-111:**
- Biaxial only: ε̇ = 1.073×10⁻¹⁵ σ³·⁰⁰⁸ exp(-378205/RT)
- Biaxial + Uniaxial: ε̇ = 1.755×10⁻¹⁴ σ²·⁷⁰⁶ exp(-415215/RT)
- Biaxial stress exponent n: 3.0
- Combined stress exponent n: 2.7
- Biaxial activation energy ΔH: 378 kJ/g-mole
- Combined activation energy ΔH: 345 kJ/g-mole
- Variability: 3.0 (biaxial), 2.7 (combined)
- The biaxial correlation represents the majority of the creep rate data within a factor of ±1.5.
- Data included three heats of material and both extruded and bored tube specimens.
- The combined correlation represents the majority of the data within a factor of ±1.8, including eight different heats of material.

**Molybdenum:**
- Biaxial only: not explicitly stated in the text (see Figure 6)
- Biaxial + Uniaxial: ε̇ = 6.061×10⁻²⁸ σ⁴·⁵⁴⁴ exp(-327478/RT)
- Biaxial stress exponent n: 5.3
- Combined stress exponent n: 4.5
- Biaxial activation energy ΔH: 501 kJ/g-mole
- Combined activation energy ΔH: 327 kJ/g-mole
- Variability: 1.5 (biaxial), 2.0 (combined)
- Molybdenum biaxial data correlated satisfactorily; primary creep did not appear to be a significant factor.
- Strains for molybdenum materials were generally much greater than for tantalum materials.

**TZM:**
- Biaxial only: ε̇ = 4.273×10⁻¹² σ³·⁰³⁰ exp(-481038/RT)
- Biaxial + Uniaxial: ε̇ = 5.598×10⁻¹² σ³·¹²⁷ exp(-508841/RT)
- Biaxial stress exponent n: 3.0
- Combined stress exponent n: 3.1
- Biaxial activation energy ΔH: 481 kJ/g-mole
- Combined activation energy ΔH: 509 kJ/g-mole
- Variability: 1.8 (biaxial), 4.2 (combined)
- Biaxial correlation represents only one heat of material.

**TZC:**
- Biaxial only: ε̇ = 4.760×10⁻²² σ⁴·⁷⁰³ exp(-557204/RT)
- Biaxial + Uniaxial: ε̇ = 7.289×10⁻²¹ σ⁵·¹²¹ exp(-703987/RT)
- Biaxial stress exponent n: 4.7
- Combined stress exponent n: 5.1
- Biaxial activation energy ΔH: 557 kJ/g-mole
- Combined activation energy ΔH: 704 kJ/g-mole
- Variability: 1.8 (biaxial), 4.2 (combined)
- Biaxial data correlated satisfactorily, but correlation constants were not in agreement with ideal values.
- The reason for this apparent nonconformity is not known.

**Ideal Values (from reference 8):**
- Stress exponent n: 4 to 6 for pure metals; 2 to 4 for alloys
- Activation energy ΔH for tantalum: 460 kJ/g-mole
- Activation energy ΔH for molybdenum: 502 kJ/g-mole

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

- The original paper does not explicitly discuss oxidation resistance.
- Contamination control during testing was addressed:
  - Each test specimen was wrapped in 0.0025 cm (1 mil) thick tungsten foil.
  - All tubes were positioned in a molybdenum can packed with tungsten wool.
  - High-purity argon gas (oxygen content less than 1 ppm) was used for the test environment.
  - Purging procedure: furnace evacuated to 10⁻² torr, filled with argon to 1.22×10⁵ N/m² (3 psig); tube specimens internally pressurized to 7.92×10⁵ N/m² (100 psig) with argon and exhausted; repeated five times.
- Table I shows contaminant changes during testing were maintained at an acceptably low level.
- Oxygen pickup for tantalum specimens was the most significant contaminant change (+270 ppm for 0.0254 cm wall thickness, +120 ppm for 0.0508 cm, +105 ppm for 0.1016 cm).
- T-111 showed lower oxygen pickup (+91 ppm, +111 ppm, +35 ppm, +4 ppm, +1 ppm, +7 ppm, +1 ppm).
- No explicit discussion of oxidation weight gain, oxide layer formation, or high-temperature oxidation behavior.

## Ti + Zr + C (Carbide-forming elements in molybdenum alloys)

### [Applicable Alloy System]
TZM (Mo-0.5Ti-0.08Zr-0.03C) and TZC (Mo-1.2Ti-0.25Zr-0.15C)

### [Addition Amount or Composition Range]
- TZM: 0.49 wt.% Ti, 0.11 wt.% Zr (Note: Nominal 0.08 wt.% Zr; actual 0.11 wt.%), 0.014 wt.% C
- TZC: 1.19 wt.% Ti, 0.088 wt.% Zr, 0.17 wt.% C

### [Main Function]
Carbide precipitation strengthening of molybdenum-based alloys

### [Microstructural Mechanism]
Ti and Zr are carbide-forming elements that form stable carbides (TiC, ZrC) in the molybdenum matrix, providing precipitation strengthening. Although the original paper does not provide detailed microstructural characterization, the higher Ti and C content in TZC compared to TZM leads to higher creep activation energies. Specific precipitate characterization is not explicitly stated.

### [Property Effect]
- TZM: Stress exponent n = 3.0-3.1, activation energy ΔH = 481-509 kJ/g-mole, close to ideal value for Mo (502 kJ/g-mole)
- TZC: Stress exponent n = 4.7-5.1, activation energy ΔH = 557-704 kJ/g-mole
- TZM biaxial correlation was satisfactory with stress and energy constants agreeing closely with ideal values.
- TZC biaxial data correlated satisfactorily but correlation constants were not in agreement with ideal values.
- At 1922 K, TZM effective stresses range from 1.75×10⁷ N/m² while TZC shows values up to 4.07×10⁷ N/m².
- Hardness: TZM 285-292 DPH (vs. 279-287 DPH for pure Mo)

### [Potential Side Effects]
TZC showed poor correlation between biaxial and uniaxial data (variability 4.2), and the reason for apparent nonconformity of biaxial and uniaxial creep properties is not known. TZC has higher hardness (285-292 DPH) compared to pure molybdenum (279-287 DPH).

### [Original Evidence]
Table II gives TZM composition as 0.49 wt.% Ti, 0.11 wt.% Zr, 0.014 wt.% C. TZC composition as 1.19 wt.% Ti, 0.088 wt.% Zr, 0.17 wt.% C. Nominal compositions stated in text as Mo-0.5Ti-0.08Zr-0.03C for TZM and Mo-1.2Ti-0.25Zr-0.15C for TZC.

# Key Conclusions

1. The biaxial and uniaxial creep properties of tantalum, T-111, molybdenum, TZM, and TZC are equivalent when compared on the basis of effective stress and strain rate as defined by the von Mises criterion for plastic flow.

2. Steady-state creep-rate correlations were determined for each material for both biaxial data and a combination of biaxial and uniaxial data:
   - T-111, molybdenum, and TZM correlations represented the experimental data very closely, and the correlation constants agreed with generally accepted values.
   - Tantalum correlations were not too satisfactory, probably because the biaxial data included a significant amount of primary creep.
   - TZC correlation adequately represented the biaxial data but the combined biaxial and uniaxial data resulted in a relatively poor correlation.

3. For design purposes, the correlations that combine the effective biaxial and uniaxial data better represent the materials because they include a greater variety of data and hence a more representative sample.

4. The empirical equation used (ε̇ = A exp(-ΔH/RT) σⁿ) generally represented the experimental creep-rate data within plus and minus a factor of 5, which is reasonably good agreement for predicting refractory-metal creep rate.

5. The energy term ΔH is analogous to the activation energy for self-diffusion, and the exponent for the stress function should agree with accepted values of 4 to 6 for pure metals and 2 to 4 for alloys. This was the result for most of the materials tested.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

**Limitations:**

1. The biaxial test results for pure tantalum were the poorest of the materials tested, possibly because tantalum exhibits an appreciable amount of primary creep which was not considered by this investigation.

2. Primary creep may have been a significant factor for pure tantalum and possibly molybdenum, but was not considered significant for the refractory alloys T-111, TZM, and TZC.

3. No correction for true strain was used in calculating creep rates.

4. For TZC, the combination of biaxial and uniaxial data resulted in a poorer correlation; the reason for this apparent nonconformity is not known.

5. The molybdenum combined (biaxial + uniaxial) correlation was poorer than biaxial alone, possibly due to different heat treatments and much higher carbon contents (140 and 250 ppm) for the uniaxial comparison materials.

6. The paper does not provide detailed microstructural characterization (phase identification, precipitate analysis, grain size quantification).

7. The paper does not discuss oxidation resistance, tensile properties, or ductile-to-brittle transition behavior.

**Applicable Scope:**

1. The correlations are valid for the following test conditions:
   - Temperature range: 1340 to 1922 K (1950 ° to 3000 °F)
   - Effective stress range: 2.76×10⁶ to 2.21×10⁸ N/m² (400 to 32,000 psi)
   - Creep rates: 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁶ hr⁻¹
   - Test durations: 100 to 1000 hours

2. The materials tested were arc-melted ingots, extruded, recrystallized, and heat treated 1 hour at 1922 K (3000 °F) prior to creep testing.

3. The biaxial correlations represent internally pressurized thin-wall tube loading conditions (tangential-to-axial stress ratio ≈ 2:1).

4. The data are applicable to refractory materials for high-temperature fuel-pin cladding applications in liquid-metal-cooled nuclear reactors and space power nuclear reactors.

5. The comparison between biaxial and uniaxial creep data assumes the von Mises criterion for plastic flow is applicable to these refractory metals and alloys.
