# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Physics-assisted machine-learning approach for modeling the temperature-dependent ultimate strengths of superalloys, with comparison to medium- and high-entropy alloys
- **Authors**: B. Steingrimsson, X. Fan, B.M. Adam, P.K. Liaw
- **Journal**: Materials Chemistry and Physics
- **Year**: 2025
- **DOI**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matchemphys.2025.130618
- **Alloy system studied**: Superalloys (Ni-based, Co-based, Fe–Ni-based), medium-entropy alloys (MEAs), and high-entropy alloys (HEAs), including refractory BCC HEAs (MoNbTaVW, MoNbTi, AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr, HfMoNbTaW)
- **Research objective**: Develop and validate a physics-assisted machine-learning approach (bilinear-log and trilinear-log models) for predicting temperature-dependent ultimate strengths of superalloys, MEAs, and HEAs; introduce and characterize the break temperature, T_break, as a key design parameter; extend the model to account for strain rate and composition dependence.
- **Material category**: This is primarily a modeling paper. Materials covered include Ni-based superalloys (γ/γ′ precipitation-strengthened and solid-solution strengthened), Co-based superalloys, Fe–Ni-based superalloys, and BCC refractory high-entropy alloys (MoNbTaW, MoNbTaVW, MoNbTi, HfMoNbTaW).

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Ti (Titanium)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni-based superalloys (γ′ phase); Co-based superalloys; refractory HEAs (AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper; “high (Ti + Al) content” in Inconel X750 is noted.
- **Main Function**: Strengthens γ′ precipitate; increases APB energy; contributes to lattice mismatch between γ and γ′.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Ti increases the γ′ phase lattice parameter, contributing to γ/γ′ lattice mismatch strengthening; Ti concentrations in γ′ are “crucial for controlling the APB energy.”
- **Property Effect**: Higher Ti content contributes to increased high-temperature strength and a flatter UTS vs. temperature response (e.g., Inconel X750). The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for Ti effect alone.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly discussed in this paper.
- **Original Evidence**: “Models for the APB energy exist, that incorporate compositions, grain parameters and precipitate properties, and that show that concentrations of Ti, Ta, and Nb in the γ′ precipitate phase, in particular, are crucial for controlling the APB energy.”

## Ta (Tantalum)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni-based superalloys; refractory HEAs (MoNbTaW, MoNbTaVW, AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper.
- **Main Function**: Solid-solution strengthening in BCC refractory HEAs; contributes to γ/γ′ lattice mismatch and APB energy in Ni-based superalloys; high-temperature strength retention.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Ta increases γ′ lattice parameter and strengthens solid-solution hardening through atomic size mismatch and elastic interactions in BCC HEAs.
- **Property Effect**: Refractory HEAs containing Ta (MoNbTaW, MoNbTaVW) exhibit exceptional high-temperature strengths up to ~1630 °C, “far above the limits of ~830 °C for the existing superalloys.”
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly discussed in this paper.
- **Original Evidence**: “The MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW refractory BCC HEAs have been shown to possess exceptional strengths at temperatures up to ~1630 °C.”

## Nb (Niobium)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni-based superalloys (γ″ phase); refractory HEAs (MoNbTi, MoNbTaVW)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper; γ″ stoichiometry Ni3Nb noted.
- **Main Function**: Forms γ″ phase (Ni3X with X = Nb or V) in Ni-based superalloys; provides solid-solution strengthening in BCC refractory alloys; contributes to APB energy.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: γ″ phase consists of body-centered-tetragonal lattice with ordered arrangement of Ni and Nb atoms; strengthening through coherency-hardening and order-hardening mechanisms.
- **Property Effect**: Inconel 718 (Nb-bearing) shows high ultimate strength in mid-to-low temperature regime; γ″ phase can be used to harvest strengthening at relatively low temperatures. The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for Nb effect alone.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly discussed in this paper.
- **Original Evidence**: “The γ ″ phase is found in Ni-based superalloys, such as Inconel 718, which feature a significant contribution of Niobium or Vanadium. The stoichiometry of the γ ″ phase is Ni 3 X with X = Nb or V.”

## V (Vanadium)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni-based superalloys (Inconel 718); refractory HEAs (MoNbTaVW)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper.
- **Main Function**: Forms γ″ phase (Ni3X with X = Nb or V) in Ni-based superalloys; solid-solution strengthening in BCC refractory HEAs.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: γ″ phase with BCT lattice provides coherency-hardening and order-hardening.
- **Property Effect**: MoNbTaVW exhibits least decline in UTS with increasing temperatures among studied alloys; exceptional strengths up to ~1630 °C.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly discussed in this paper.
- **Original Evidence**: Same as Nb evidence; “MoNbTaVW exhibits the least decline in UTS with increasing temperatures.”

## Mo (Molybdenum)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Refractory BCC HEAs (MoNbTi, MoNbTaVW); Ni-based superalloys (Inconel N06230)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper; equiatomic proportion in MoNbTi, MoNbTaVW.
- **Main Function**: Solid-solution strengthening; enhances high-temperature strength retention in BCC refractory HEAs.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Large atomic size mismatch and modulus mismatch contribute to solute strengthening; edge dislocation strengthening identified as important in MoNbTi.
- **Property Effect**: MoNbTi exhibits a strength plateau in intermediate temperature regime (900 K–1300 K); MoNbTaVW shows least decline in UTS with increasing temperature.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly discussed in this paper.
- **Original Evidence**: “MoNbTi refractory MEA” analysis by Jones et al.; “MoNbTaVW exhibits the least decline in UTS with increasing temperatures.”

## W (Tungsten)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Refractory BCC HEAs (MoNbTaW, MoNbTaVW, HfMoNbTaW)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper.
- **Main Function**: Solid-solution strengthening; high-temperature strength retention.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Atomic size mismatch and elastic interactions; edge dislocations may control strength in W-containing refractory HEAs.
- **Property Effect**: MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW exhibit exceptional strengths up to ~1630 °C. MoNbTaVW shows least decline in UTS with increasing temperature.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly discussed in this paper.
- **Original Evidence**: “The MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW refractory BCC HEAs have been shown to possess exceptional strengths at temperatures up to ~1630 °C.”

## Al (Aluminum)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni-based superalloys (γ′ phase); Co-based superalloys; refractory HEA AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper; “high (Ti + Al) content” in Inconel X750.
- **Main Function**: Stabilizes γ′ phase; contributes to γ′ volume fraction and high-temperature strength; oxidation resistance (with Cr).
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Al promotes γ′ formation; γ′ strengthens through APB and cross-slip locking mechanisms.
- **Property Effect**: Inconel X750 (high Ti+Al) shows least decline in UTS with increasing temperatures among superalloys; AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr shows highest UTS in mid-to-low temperature regime.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Oxidation issues not discussed in this paper.
- **Original Evidence**: “Inconel X750 is known to possess significant γ′ phase precipitation hardening ability due to its high (Ti + Al) content.”

## Cr (Chromium)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni-based, Co-based, Fe–Ni-based superalloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper.
- **Main Function**: Oxidation resistance; solid-solution strengthening; carbide formation (M23C6).
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Cr promotes formation of protective Cr2O3 oxide layers (not explicitly detailed in this paper); Cr forms M23C6 carbides at grain boundaries.
- **Property Effect**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly discussed in this paper.
- **Original Evidence**: Not explicitly discussed in this paper (only implied through reference to M23C6 carbides).

## C (Carbon)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Ni-based superalloys; Fe-based superalloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified in this paper.
- **Main Function**: Forms carbides (M6C, M23C6) at grain boundaries; prevents grain-boundary sliding.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Grain-boundary carbides pin grain boundaries; M23C6 at lower temperature, M6C at higher temperature.
- **Property Effect**: Grain-boundary carbides contribute to creep resistance and high-temperature strength by preventing grain-boundary sliding. Specific numerical values not provided.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Carbide phase stability can influence flow stress over temperature; not explicitly discussed as detrimental in this paper.
- **Original Evidence**: “Grain boundary carbides play a significant role in preventing grain boundary sliding as well, such that their phase stability can also influence changes in the flow stress over temperature.”

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-solution strengthening

- **Related Elements**: Mo, W, Ta, Nb, V, Ti, Cr, Al (in BCC refractory HEAs and FCC superalloys)
- **Typical Alloys**: MoNbTaW, MoNbTaVW, MoNbTi, Hastelloy X, Haynes 230, Haynes 188, Inconel 625
- **Key Compositions**: Equiatomic refractory HEAs; Cr–Ni–Mo–W solid-solution superalloys
- **Microstructural Changes**: Random atomic distribution; local concentration fluctuations; lattice distortion; atomic size misfit.
- **Property Improvements**: High-temperature strength retention; T_break values in Table 1 (e.g., Hastelloy X plate: 719.1 °C; Haynes 230 plate: 724.7 °C).
- **Risks and Limitations**: Solid-solution strengthening alone may not provide adequate low-temperature strength; diffusion-controlled weakening above T_break.

## Precipitation strengthening (γ′ and γ″ phases)

- **Related Elements**: Al, Ti, Nb, Ta, V
- **Typical Alloys**: Inconel X750, Inconel 718, Waspaloy, Haynes 282, Rene 41, Udimet 720, Nimonic 263
- **Key Compositions**: γ′ Ni3(Al,Ti); γ″ Ni3(Nb,V)
- **Microstructural Changes**: Ordered FCC L12 γ′ precipitates; BCT γ″ precipitates; APB-carrying second phases.
- **Property Improvements**: Relatively insensitive UTS to temperature below T_break; anomalous strength humps in some alloys; T_break up to ~1332 °C for Inconel 718.
- **Risks and Limitations**: γ′ dissolution above ~900 °C; APB energy decreases with temperature; limited applicability above γ′ solvus.

## Grain-boundary strengthening

- **Related Elements**: C, Cr, Mo, W (as carbide formers)
- **Typical Alloys**: Inconel 617, Haynes 230, Nimonic 263
- **Key Compositions**: M23C6 and M6C carbides at grain boundaries
- **Microstructural Changes**: Grain-boundary carbide precipitation; reduced grain-boundary sliding.
- **Property Improvements**: Enhanced creep resistance; higher temperature stable strength.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Phase stability issues; possible grain-boundary embrittlement (not explicitly discussed in this paper).

## Dislocation-based strengthening (screw vs. edge control)

- **Related Elements**: Mo, W, Ta, Nb, V (BCC refractory HEAs); all solute species in FCC HEAs
- **Typical Alloys**: MoNbTi, MoNbTaVW, HfMoNbTaW
- **Key Compositions**: Equiatomic BCC HEAs
- **Microstructural Changes**: Sessile screw dislocations at low temperature; edge-controlled mechanism at intermediate temperature; cross-kink breaking; nonplanar kink pairs.
- **Property Improvements**: Strength plateau in intermediate temperature regime (900–1300 K for MoNbTi); exceptional high-temperature strength up to 1630 °C.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Plateaus not fully understood; DSA via cross-core diffusion may add complexity; models assume single-phase random alloys, not applicable to precipitation-strengthened superalloys.

## Short-range ordering strengthening

- **Related Elements**: All solute combinations in MEA/HEAs
- **Typical Alloys**: Referenced for BCC refractory HEAs; not applied to superalloys in this paper
- **Key Compositions**: Not quantified in this paper.
- **Microstructural Changes**: Local ordering of atoms; may increase or decrease strength depending on character.
- **Property Improvements**: Potential additional strengthening; recent theory by Nag and Curtin [45].
- **Risks and Limitations**: “Origin of the ‘strength plateau’ is not fully understood”; models account for SRO only to limited extent.

# Key Conclusions

1. **Bilinear-log model outperforms single-exponential model**: Across 104 superalloy configurations, the bilinear-log model achieved an MSE of 0.00370 (log domain) and 909 MPa² (linear domain), compared to 0.1164 (log domain) and 41,713 MPa² (linear domain) for the single-exponential model.
2. **Break temperature, T_break, is a fundamental design parameter**: T_break separates low-temperature diffusionless glide from high-temperature diffusion-controlled climb/glide. Operating temperatures should remain below T_break for reliable performance. Typical T_break values are summarized in Table 1.
3. **Physics-based justification**: The bilinear-log model is grounded in Senkov et al.'s observation that diffusion processes become noticeable above T > 0.4 T_m, with T_break/T_m constrained to [0.35, 0.55].
4. **Trilinear-log model for anomalous UTS phenomena**: Some γ′ or γ″-strengthened superalloys (e.g., Haynes 282, Inconel 903 notch bar, Monel 400 annealed, Nimonic PK33 heat-treated bar, Nimonic PE11, Udimet 720) exhibit a hump or plateau requiring a third exponential.
5. **Refractory BCC HEA comparison**: MoNbTaVW exhibits the least decline in UTS with temperature among studied alloys; MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW retain strength up to ~1630 °C, exceeding the ~830 °C limit of existing superalloys.
6. **Model extensions proposed**: The bilinear-log model can be extended to incorporate strain-rate dependence [Eqs. (2)–(4)] and composition-dependent physics inspired by Maresca-Curtin dislocation models [Eqs. (16)–(20)].
7. **Strengthening mechanisms identified**: γ′ APB strengthening, γ″ coherency/order strengthening, solid-solution strengthening, grain-boundary carbide strengthening, and BCC dislocation mechanisms (screw vs. edge) all contribute to the temperature-dependent strength behavior.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Not an experimental study**: This paper compiles existing data from commercial datasheets and literature; no new alloys were manufactured or tested.
- **Single-phase assumption in dislocation models**: The analytical models by Maresca-Curtin assume single-phase, statistically homogeneous random alloys. Precipitation-strengthened superalloys with multi-phase microstructures are not covered by these models.
- **Limited treatment of oxidation**: Oxidation resistance is mentioned but not modeled or quantitatively assessed.
- **Data availability**: Detailed compositions, processing parameters, and microstructural parameters (grain size, precipitate size/volume fraction) are not systematically provided; data referenced from Ref. [9] Table S1 were noted to have “significant scatter.”
- **T_break constraints**: The physics-induced constraint 0.35 ≲ T_break/T_m ≲ 0.55 is assumed, but some configurations in Table 1 (e.g., Inconel X750 with T_break = 1258 °C; Inconel 718 with T_break = 1332 °C) exceed this range relative to nominal solvus temperatures, indicating limitations of the constraint.
- **No specific alloy composition tables**: The paper references commercial superalloy compositions but does not include a composition table, limiting direct extraction of element quantities.
- **Anomalous UTS phenomenon**: For alloys exhibiting the anomalous hump, neither single-exponential nor bilinear-log models may be accurate; trilinear-log or piecewise linear regression is needed.
- **Strain-rate extension not validated**: The strain-rate-dependent extensions [Eqs. (2)–(4)] and composition-dependent extensions [Eqs. (16)–(20)] are proposed but “validation will be the subject of future research.”
