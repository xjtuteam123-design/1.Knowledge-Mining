- **Alloy system studied:** β-Ti binary alloys with transition metal substitutional doping (Ti₁₋ₓMₓ, M = Fe, Mo, Nb, Ni)
- **Research objective:** To systematically investigate the structural stability, electronic, and mechanical properties of Ti₁₋ₓMₓ (M = Fe, Mo, Nb, Ni) binary alloys using first-principles calculations based on Density Functional Theory (DFT), with the goal of understanding how substitutional transition metal doping can tune mechanical properties for aerospace and biomedical applications.

# Mechanical Properties

All mechanical properties are calculated from first-principles DFT, not experimentally measured.

**Elastic constants (C₁₁, C₁₂, C₄₄) in GPa:** Provided in Table 1 for all alloy compositions. Key examples:
- Pure β-Ti (0% M): C₁₁ = 108.23 GPa, C₁₂ = 115.56 GPa, C₄₄ = 59.00 GPa
- Ti₀.₅Fe₀.₅ (50% Fe): C₁₁ = 383.13 GPa, C₁₂ = 103.76 GPa, C₄₄ = 77.57 GPa
- Ti₀.₅Mo₀.₅ (50% Mo): C₁₁ = 244.34 GPa, C₁₂ = 150.60 GPa, C₄₄ = 13.52 GPa
- Ti₀.₅Nb₀.₅ (50% Nb): C₁₁ = 153.84 GPa, C₁₂ = 135.04 GPa, C₄₄ = 13.84 GPa
- Ti₀.₅Ni₀.₅ (50% Ni): C₁₁ = 235.42 GPa, C₁₂ = 167.67 GPa, C₄₄ = 68.68 GPa

**Bulk modulus B (GPa):** Increases linearly with M concentration for all four alloy systems. Pure β-Ti: B = 113.12 GPa. At 50% M: Ti–Fe B = 196.88 GPa; Ti–Mo B = 181.85 GPa; Ti–Nb B = 141.31 GPa; Ti–Ni B = 190.25 GPa.

**Shear modulus G (GPa):** Increases linearly with M concentration for all four alloy systems, with significant enhancement compared to pure Ti (G = 12.05 GPa). At 50% M: Ti–Fe G = 98.38 GPa; Ti–Mo G = 22.88 GPa; Ti–Nb G = 11.85 GPa; Ti–Ni G = 51.72 GPa.

**Young's modulus E (GPa):** Pure β-Ti: E = 34.92 GPa. At 50% M: Ti–Fe E = 253.01 GPa; Ti–Mo E = 65.88 GPa; Ti–Nb E = 34.59 GPa; Ti–Ni E = 142.26 GPa. Ti₁₋ₓFeₓ and Ti₁₋ₓNbₓ alloys show increasing Young's modulus with M content, while Ti₁₋ₓMoₓ and Ti₁₋ₓNbₓ alloys show a minimum value for Young's modulus at certain concentrations. The Young's modulus of Ti₁₋ₓMoₓ and Ti₁₋ₓNbₓ alloys is comparable to that of human bone (approximately 10–30 GPa range), supporting biomedical applications.

**B/G ratio:** All Ti₁₋ₓMₓ alloys have B/G values greater than 1.75 (ranging from approximately 2.00 to 15.93), confirming ductility according to Pugh's criterion. Pure β-Ti: B/G = 9.39.

**Poisson's ratio ν:** All calculated values exceed 0.26, indicating ductility. Pure β-Ti: ν = 0.449. At 50% M: Ti–Fe ν = 0.286; Ti–Mo ν = 0.440; Ti–Nb ν = 0.459; Ti–Ni ν = 0.375.

**Tensile strength (from stretching model):** The tensile strength increases linearly with M content for all Ti₁₋ₓMₓ alloys. At 31.25% M content, the tensile strength follows the order Fe > Mo > Ni > Nb (based on the description that "the addition of β-stabilizing elements Fe and Mo can more effectively enhance the tensile strength of Ti₁₋ₓMₓ alloys compared to Nb and Ni"). Stress-strain curves are presented in Fig. 6 for Ti₁₋ₓMoₓ alloys at varying Mo concentrations and for all Ti₁₋ₓMₓ alloys at 31.25% M content. Specific numerical tensile strength values are not explicitly provided in the text; the results are presented graphically.

- **Applicable Alloy System:** β-Ti₁₋ₓFeₓ binary alloys (Ti-based alloys, not refractory alloys)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 6.25 at.% to 50 at.% Fe substituting Ti in β-Ti supercell
- **Main Function:** β-phase stabilizer; solid-solution strengthening element; tensile strength enhancement

However, the significant increase in Young's modulus may make the alloy stiffer, and the lowest Poisson's ratio value (0.286 at 50% Fe) approaches the ductile-to-brittle transition threshold (0.26). The original paper does not provide specific negative side effects for Fe addition.
- **Original Evidence:** Fig. 2(b) shows formation energy decreases with Fe content; Fig. 5 shows B, E, G variations; Fig. 3 shows PDOS hybridization strengthening; Abstract and conclusions state Fe addition significantly improves structural stability and tensile strength.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening (Substitutional Doping)

- **Related Elements:** Fe, Mo, Nb, Ni (substitutional transition metal dopants in β-Ti)
- **Typical Alloys:** β-Ti₁₋ₓMₓ binary alloys (M = Fe, Mo, Nb, Ni)
- **Key Compositions:** 6.25 at.% to 50 at.% substitutional doping of Ti by Fe, Mo, Nb, or Ni
- **Microstructural Changes:** Substitutional M atoms occupy Ti lattice sites in the bcc β-Ti structure. Lattice contraction occurs for Fe, Mo, and Ni (smaller atomic radius than Ti), while lattice expansion occurs for Nb (larger atomic radius than Ti). The formation energy decreases with increasing M concentration, indicating enhanced β-phase stability. Strong hybridization between M-d and Ti-3d orbitals is observed in PDOS analysis, intensifying with increasing M concentration.
- **Property Improvements:** Linear increase in bulk modulus B and shear modulus G with increasing M concentration (e.g., B increases from 113.12 GPa for pure β-Ti to 141.31–196.88 GPa at 50% M; G increases from 12.05 GPa to 11.85–98.38 GPa). Tensile strength increases linearly with M content. All alloys retain ductility (B/G > 1.75; Poisson's ratio > 0.26). Fe and Mo provide the most effective tensile strength enhancement.
- **Risks and Limitations:** Low Nb concentration alloys (6.25% and 12.5% Nb) are mechanically unstable (negative C₁₁ − C₁₂). Mo and Nb produce relatively low shear modulus improvements. High Fe content reduces Poisson's ratio to 0.286, approaching the brittle threshold. The improvement in Young's modulus is element-dependent: Fe and Ni increase E significantly, while Mo and Nb produce a minimum E value. All calculations are at 0 K; high-temperature properties are not addressed.

## Tensile Strength Enhancement via Bond Strengthening

- **Related Elements:** Fe, Mo, Nb, Ni (transition metals)
- **Typical Alloys:** β-Ti₁₋ₓMₓ binary alloys
- **Key Compositions:** 6.25 at.% to 50 at.% M
- **Microstructural Changes:** The PDOS analysis shows that resonant peaks between Ti and M atoms strengthen with increasing M concentration, indicating enhanced hybridization between M-d and Ti-3d orbitals. This stronger orbital hybridization corresponds to stronger Ti–M bonds.

## β-Phase Stability via Transition Metal Doping

- **Related Elements:** Fe, Mo, Nb, Ni (β-stabilizing elements)
- **Typical Alloys:** β-Ti₁₋ₓMₓ binary alloys
- **Key Compositions:** 6.25 at.% to 50 at.% M
- **Microstructural Changes:** The formation energy decreases linearly with increasing M concentration, indicating that higher M content stabilizes the β phase. The elastic constant C₁₁ − C₁₂ increases monotonically with M concentration, indicating improved elastic stability of the β phase. However, low-concentration Ti–Nb alloys exhibit negative C₁₁ − C₁₂, indicating mechanical instability.
- **Property Improvements:** Enhanced structural stability enables tunable mechanical properties. The stabilization of the β phase at high M concentrations supports the design of β-Ti alloys with predictable property trends.
- **Risks and Limitations:** At low Nb concentrations (6.25% and 12.5%), the β phase is mechanically unstable, indicating that a minimum Nb content is required for stable β-phase Ti–Nb alloys. All calculations are performed at 0 K; temperature-dependent phase stability is not addressed.

# Key Conclusions

1. The formation energy of Ti₁₋ₓMₓ (M = Fe, Mo, Nb, Ni) binary alloys decreases with increasing substitutional atomic M concentration, indicating enhanced structural stability of the β phase at higher M content (x = 6.25% to 50%).

2. The elastic constants C₁₁ − C₁₂ increase monotonically with M concentration, confirming improved mechanical stability. All alloys studied are mechanically stable except Ti₀.₉₃₇₅Nb₀.₀₆₂₅ and Ti₀.₈₇₅Nb₀.₁₂₅, which exhibit negative tetragonal shear moduli.

3. Bulk modulus B and shear modulus G increase linearly with M concentration for all four alloy systems. The bulk modulus of Ti₁₋ₓMₓ alloys is significantly enhanced compared to pure β-Ti (113.12 GPa).

4. Young's modulus E increases in Ti₁₋ₓFeₓ and Ti₁₋ₓNbₓ alloys, while Ti₁₋ₓMoₓ and Ti₁₋ₓNbₓ alloys reach a minimum value. The Young's modulus of Ti₁₋ₓMoₓ and Ti₁₋ₓNbₓ alloys is comparable to human bone, supporting biomedical implant applications.

5. All Ti₁₋ₓMₓ alloys exhibit ductility according to Pugh's criterion (B/G > 1.75) and Poisson's ratio (ν > 0.26).

6. Tensile strength of Ti₁₋ₓMₓ alloys increases linearly with M concentration. At 31.25% M, tensile strength follows the order Fe > Mo > Ni > Nb. The enhancement in tensile strength is attributed to enhanced Ti–M bond strength from orbital hybridization between M-d and Ti-3d states.

7. Fe and Ni have the most pronounced effect on Young's modulus, while Mo and Nb have relatively weaker effects but produce lower Young's modulus values suitable for biomedical applications.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

It is a first-principles computational study of β-Ti binary alloys with transition metal substitutional doping. The mechanical properties (B, G, E, tensile strength, B/G ratio, Poisson's ratio) are calculated from elastic constants and stress-strain simulations, not from experimental measurements.

- **Temperature dependence not addressed:** The properties are calculated only at 0 K. High-temperature mechanical properties, creep, oxidation resistance, and other service stability characteristics relevant to refractory alloy applications are not investigated.

- **Concentration range:** The study investigates discrete concentrations from 6.25% to 50% in 6.25% increments. The effects of continuous concentration variation and lower or higher concentrations are not addressed.

- **Specific numerical values for tensile strength:** The paper presents tensile strength results graphically (Fig. 6) but does not provide specific numerical tensile strength values in the text. The reader must refer to the graphs for quantitative tensile strength data.

- **Applicable scope:** The findings are relevant to the design of β-Ti alloys for aerospace and biomedical applications where low Young's modulus, high strength-toughness compatibility, and β-phase stability are desired. The results provide a theoretical framework for compositional optimization of Ti-based alloys but require experimental validation for practical applications.

- **Not directly applicable to refractory alloys:** The paper's focus on Ti-based alloys with Fe, Mo, Nb, Ni substitutional doping is not directly relevant to refractory high-temperature alloys (Nb-W, Mo-W, refractory HEAs). However, the computational methodology for evaluating the effect of substitutional alloying elements on phase stability and mechanical properties could be conceptually extended to other alloy systems, including refractory alloys.
