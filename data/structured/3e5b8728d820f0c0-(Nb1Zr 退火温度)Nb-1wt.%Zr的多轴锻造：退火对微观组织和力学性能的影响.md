# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Multi-axial forging of Nb-1wt.%Zr: Effect of annealing on microstructure and mechanical properties
- **Authors**: Rajeev Kapoor, Apu Sarkar, Ananta N. Behera, Saurav Sunil
- **Journal**: Materials Science & Engineering A
- **Year**: 2020 (Volume 772, Article 138805; available online 10 December 2019)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.msea.2019.138805
- **Alloy system studied**: Nb-1wt.%Zr (niobium-zirconium dilute solid solution alloy)
- **Material category**: Nb-based refractory alloy (Nb-Zr binary alloy)
- **Research objective**: To subject Nb-1wt.%Zr to severe plastic deformation at room temperature via multi-axial forging (MAF) in a closed channel die to an effective strain of 4.6, then investigate the effect of subsequent annealing (450–700 °C for 1 h) on microstructural evolution, recrystallization behavior, thermal stability, and mechanical properties.

# Alloy System and Research Object

The paper studies **Nb-1wt.%Zr**, a dilute solid-solution niobium-based refractory alloy. Niobium alloys of this class are used for biomedical applications as well as conventional high-temperature applications, and are relatively easy to deform at room temperature compared to other refractory metals such as Mo- and Ta-based alloys. The study focuses on severe plastic deformation processing (multi-axial forging) followed by annealing to produce an ultrafine-grained (UFG) microstructure with improved strength and retained ductility.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Initial Condition
- Nb-1wt.%Zr in the annealed condition (equiaxed coarse grains ranging from 10 to 400 μm, with a median grain size of 100 μm).

## Multi-Axial Forging (MAF)
- **Technique**: Multi-axial forging in a **closed channel die** (plane-strain compression).
- **Sample dimensions**: Cuboidal samples of 10 × 19.8 × 20 mm³.
- **Temperature**: 25 °C (room temperature).
- **Speed**: 1 mm/s.
- **Lubricant**: MoS₂.
- **Number of pressings**: Six passes, with the sample returned to its original configuration every three pressings.
- **Effective strain**: ε_eff = 4.6 (theoretical value for six pressings was 4.8; actual measured value was 4.6 due to small errors in completely filling the die in each pass).
- **Flow stress during MAF**: Average value between 400–450 MPa for the first few passes and about 500–550 MPa for the last few passes (note: measured force includes frictional forces between sample faces and die walls, so could be higher than uniaxial compression).

## Post-MAF Annealing
- Separate MAF samples were annealed for **1 h** each at:
  - **450 °C**
  - **550 °C**
  - **600 °C**
  - **700 °C**
- Annealing temperatures were selected based on DSC results.

## Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)
- Carried out from 25 to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min to determine the temperature range of recovery.
- At temperatures higher than 300 °C, an increased heat flow rate was observed, suggesting the start of recovery.
- Beyond 450 °C, a significant heat flow peak was observed, possibly representing significant recovery and/or recrystallization and some grain growth.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Initial (Coarse-Grained) Condition
- Equiaxed coarse grains ranging from 10 to 400 μm.
- Median grain size: 100 μm.
- Large fraction of high-angle boundaries.

## As-MAF Condition (ε_eff = 4.6)
- Significant refinement of microstructure to an ultrafine-grained structure.
- Median grain size (boundary spacing, φ > 2°): approximately 1 μm; median value of boundary spacing about 0.9 μm (range from about 0.25 μm to about 3.5 μm).
- Inhomogeneous microstructure with fragmented large grains containing significant substructure.
- Large fraction of **low-angle boundaries**: fraction of high-angle boundaries (f_hab) was only **0.12**.
- High dislocation density, estimated GND density in the range of ~3 × 10¹⁴ to 4.5 × 10¹⁴ m⁻².
- Average KAM (kernel average misorientation) for φ < 2°: 0.57°; for φ < 5°: 0.85°.

## Annealed Conditions (1 h each)
- **450 °C**: Retained fine microstructural features present after MAF, with better-defined grain boundaries; f_hab increased to 0.29. Boundary spacing shifted to lower values (median about 0.8 μm). Recrystallized fraction (GOS < 2° criterion) = 0.56.
- **550 °C**: Subgrains still present; number of high-angle boundaries increased; f_hab increased to 0.35. Recrystallized fraction = 0.63.
- **600 °C**: Similar to 550 °C; f_hab increased to 0.37. Recrystallized fraction = 0.65. Non-uniform distribution of high-angle boundaries (some regions lacked them entirely).
- **700 °C**: Classic **bimodal microstructure** with fine grains interspersed between larger ones. Large recrystallized grains approximately 5–15 μm in size with high-angle boundaries and no subgrains within. Recrystallized fraction varied from 56% (one area) to 75% (larger area), indicating non-uniform recrystallization. Most larger grains had GOS < 2°, implying recrystallized grains had undergone significant grain growth.

## Recrystallization Identification
- Grains with **GOS (Grain Orientation Spread) < 2°** were considered recrystallized.
- Grains were defined as regions bounded by boundaries having φ > 2°.
- Area fraction of recrystallized grains (f_GOS<2°):
  - As-MAF: 0.28
  - 450 °C / 1 h: 0.56
  - 550 °C / 1 h: 0.63
  - 600 °C / 1 h: 0.65
  - 700 °C / 1 h: 0.56–0.75 (area-dependent due to non-uniformity)

## Phase Constitution
- Single-phase BCC solid solution (Nb-Zr). No second phases, carbides, oxides, or precipitates are reported in this paper.

The paper does not discuss solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, dispersion strengthening, or ODS strengthening in the sense of second-phase particles; the strengthening analysis is primarily based on **grain boundary (Hall-Petch) hardening and dislocation (Taylor) hardening**.

## Strength Increment Analysis
- MAF increased UTS by about 4 times relative to the coarse-grained condition (from ~140 to 535 MPa).
- The paper estimated that the increase in strength after MAF arose **more from the increase in dislocation density (Taylor hardening) than from the reduction in grain size (Hall-Petch hardening)**.
- Quantitative estimates (Table 1 of the paper):

| Condition | Criterion | Average d (μm) | Hall-Petch term (MPa) | ρ_GND (m⁻²) | Taylor term (MPa) |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|----------------------|-------------|------------------|
| Coarse-grained | — | 100 | 25 | 10¹² | 20 |
| As-MAF | φ > 2° | 1.5 | 179 | 3 ± 0.5 × 10¹⁴ | 345 ± 30 |
| As-MAF | φ > 5° | 25 | 48 | 4.5 ± 0.5 × 10¹⁴ | 425 ± 25 |

- Estimated total strength (with σ₀′ = 35 MPa):
  - Using φ = 2° cut-off: 179 + 345 + 35 = 559 MPa
  - Using φ = 5° cut-off: 48 + 425 + 35 = 508 MPa
- The experimentally measured yield stress was 500 MPa, which is closer to the φ = 5° cut-off estimate, suggesting 5° is a reasonable cut-off for boundaries contributing to Hall-Petch vs. Taylor hardening.
- Constants used: k_HP = 250 MPa·μm⁰·⁵ for Nb–1Zr; α = 0.5; M (Taylor factor) = 2.8 for BCC; G (shear modulus) = 44.3 GPa for Nb; b (Burgers vector) = 3.3 Å for Nb.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## [Solid-Solution Strengthening]

- **Related elements**: Zr (solute), impurities (O, N, C)
- **Typical alloys**: Nb-1wt.%Zr (this study); Nb-Zr alloys in literature
- **Key compositions**: Zr 1 wt.%, O 0.04 wt.%, N 0.06 wt.%, C 0.01 wt.%
- **Microstructural changes**: Solid-solution strengthening is assumed constant before and after deformation. The term σ_ss (solid-solution hardening) is combined with σ₀ (inherent lattice resistance) into a constant σ₀′ = 35 MPa. This term does not change with deformation or annealing.
- **Property improvements**: The value of σ₀′ = 35 MPa is inferred from the measured yield stress of the coarse-grained condition (80 MPa) minus the estimated Hall-Petch term (25 MPa) and Taylor term (20 MPa).
- **Risks and limitations**: The paper does not present a detailed analysis of solid-solution strengthening; it is treated as a constant background term. The paper notes the inferred σ₀′ value differs from literature, possibly due to impurity differences.

## [Dislocation (Taylor) Hardening]

- **Related elements**: Not element-specific; related to severe plastic deformation (MAF)
- **Typical alloys**: Nb-1wt.%Zr after MAF
- **Key compositions**: Not composition-specific; ε_eff = 4.6 (six passes MAF)
- **Microstructural changes**: MAF increased the GND density from ~10¹² m⁻² (coarse-grained) to ~3–4.5 × 10¹⁴ m⁻². In-grain misorientation (KAM) was highest in the as-MAF condition. Annealing progressively reduced KAM and GND density: 450 °C caused a marginal shift to lower KAM; 550 and 600 °C had very similar lower KAM distributions; 700 °C resulted in the lowest KAM (lowest dislocation density).
- **Property improvements**: Estimated Taylor hardening contribution increased from 20 MPa (coarse-grained) to 345–425 MPa (as-MAF), depending on the boundary misorientation cut-off criterion (φ < 2° vs. φ < 5°). The paper concluded that the increase in strength after MAF arose more from increased dislocation density than from grain size reduction.
- **Risks and limitations**: High dislocation density prevents further strain hardening, as evidenced by low uniform elongation (2.8% for as-MAF). The KAM-based GND density estimates are acknowledged as estimates to show trends.

## [Grain-Boundary (Hall-Petch) Hardening]

- **Related elements**: Not element-specific
- **Typical alloys**: Nb-1wt.%Zr (all conditions)
- **Key compositions**: Not composition-specific
- **Microstructural changes**: Median grain size (boundary spacing for φ > 2°) decreased from 100 μm (coarse-grained) to ~1 μm (as-MAF), with subsequent slight refinement to ~0.8 μm after annealing at 450–600 °C. At 700 °C, bimodal grain size distribution with large recrystallized grains of 5–15 μm surrounded by fine/deformed regions. Fraction of high-angle boundaries increased from 0.12 (as-MAF) to 0.29 (450 °C), 0.35 (550 °C), and 0.37 (600 °C).
- **Property improvements**: Estimated Hall-Petch contribution increased from 25 MPa (coarse-grained) to 48–179 MPa (as-MAF), depending on the boundary criterion. With annealing at 450 °C, boundary spacing (φ = 5°) was 3 μm, giving a Hall-Petch term of 152 MPa. As annealing temperature increased from 450 to 600 °C, low-angle boundaries transformed to high-angle boundaries, with more boundaries contributing to Hall-Petch hardening and fewer to Taylor hardening; however, both effects resulted in decreasing overall strength.
- **Risks and limitations**: The contribution of low-angle boundaries to Hall-Petch hardening is debatable; the paper considered two cut-off criteria (2° and 5°). The 700 °C annealed condition showed significant grain growth, reducing Hall-Petch contribution.

## [Grain Refinement (UFG via Severe Plastic Deformation)]

- **Related elements**: Not element-specific; processing technique-specific (MAF)
- **Typical alloys**: Nb-1wt.%Zr; Nb; Nb-Zr; V; Ta; Mo
- **Key compositions**: Not composition-specific
- **Microstructural changes**: MAF reduced median grain size from 100 μm to ~1 μm (boundary spacing for φ > 2°). Compared to other SPD techniques: ECAP of Nb produced grain sizes of 0.4–1.7 μm; ECAP of Nb–1Zr produced 250–500 nm; HPT of Nb produced 100–350 nm (from literature cited in this paper). MAF produced similar refinement to ECAP at the same effective strain.
- **Property improvements**: Grain refinement contributed to the strength increase, but less than the dislocation density increase. The Hall-Petch term increased from 25 MPa to 48–179 MPa.
- **Risks and limitations**: Ductility dropped significantly (44% → 16%). Thermal stability is limited: 1 h annealing at up to 700 °C did not fully homogenize the microstructure. Grain growth occurred significantly at 700 °C. The paper notes previous work [Ref. 22] showing grain growth in Nb–1Zr only beyond 700 °C annealing temperature.

## Appendix: Bimodal Grain Size Distribution and Ductility

- **Related elements**: Not element-specific
- **Typical alloys**: Nb-1wt.%Zr after MAF + 700 °C / 1 h annealing
- **Key compositions**: Not composition-specific
- **Microstructural changes**: Annealing at 700 °C produced a classic bimodal microstructure with large recrystallized grains (5–15 μm) surrounded by fine subgrains and deformed regions.
- **Property improvements**: Ductility improved from 16% (as-MAF) to 28% (700 °C annealed) while strength remained higher than the initial coarse-grained condition. Uniform elongation improved substantially to 18% from 2.8% (as-MAF).
- **Risks and limitations**: The 700 °C annealing already produced 3–10 μm recrystallized grains; annealing at higher temperatures would further grow the grains and lead to softening. The paper concluded additional studies are required to optimize conditions to obtain a fully recrystallized submicron grain size.

# Key Conclusions

1. Nb-1wt.%Zr can be successfully subjected to severe plastic deformation at room temperature via multi-axial forging in a closed channel die to an effective strain of 4.6 without material failure.

2. MAF increased the ultimate tensile strength from ~120–140 MPa (initial coarse-grained annealed condition) to 535–540 MPa, approximately a 4-fold increase.

3. The strengthening after MAF was estimated to arise **more from the increase in dislocation density (Taylor hardening)** than from the reduction in grain size (Hall-Petch hardening): The Taylor term increased by 345–425 MPa, while the Hall-Petch term increased by 48–179 MPa, depending on the boundary misorientation cut-off criterion used.

4. Annealing for 1 h at 450–600 °C led to a gradual reduction in strength, an increase in the fraction of high-angle boundaries (from 0.12 to 0.37), and onset of recrystallization (recrystallized fraction increased from 0.28 to 0.65), but no improvement in total ductility (remained ~16%).

5. Annealing at 700 °C for 1 h produced a bimodal grain size distribution with large recrystallized grains (5–15 μm) surrounded by fine subgrains/deformed regions, resulting in moderate ductility of 28% (total) and 18% (uniform), while strength remained higher than the initial coarse-grained condition.

6. A 1 h anneal at any temperature up to 700 °C does not completely homogenize the microstructure—neither full recovery nor a fully recrystallized microstructure was achieved.

7. The optimal goal of retaining MAF strength while increasing ductility was not fully achieved; annealing at temperatures higher than 700 °C would lead to further grain growth and softening. Additional studies are required to obtain a fully recrystallized submicron grain size in Nb-1Zr.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Mechanical testing only at room temperature**: All tensile tests were conducted at 25 °C. No high-temperature mechanical property data are reported, despite Nb-based alloys being candidate materials for high-temperature service.
- **No oxidation resistance data**: The paper does not report any oxidation testing or oxidation-related characterization. Therefore, conclusions regarding high-temperature service stability are restricted to microstructural thermal stability (recovery, recrystallization, grain growth) rather than oxidation behavior.
- **Single alloy composition**: Only Nb-1wt.%Zr is studied; no systematic variation of Zr content or comparison with pure Nb is performed in this work (comparisons are made only with literature data from other studies).
- **Strength estimates are estimates**: The paper explicitly states that the Hall-Petch and Taylor term calculations are estimates intended to show trends, not precise quantitative predictions.
- **Microstructural heterogeneity**: The MAF microstructure and the annealed microstructures are inhomogeneous, with non-uniform recrystallization. Some EBSD measurements (e.g., fraction recrystallized at 700 °C) vary significantly depending on the sampled area (56% vs. 75%), indicating local variability.
- **Friction in MAF**: The measured flow stress during MAF includes frictional forces between the sample and die walls, so it may overestimate the true flow stress.
- **Applicability**: The results are applicable to dilute Nb-Zr solid-solution alloys processed by severe plastic deformation (specifically multi-axial forging). The processing–microstructure–property relationships may not directly transfer to other refractory alloy systems (e.g., Mo-based or W-based alloys) or to high-alloy multi-principal-element alloys without additional verification.
