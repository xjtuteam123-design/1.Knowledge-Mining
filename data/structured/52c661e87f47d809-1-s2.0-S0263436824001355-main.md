# Paper Metadata
- **Paper title**: Laser powder bed fusion of pure Nb and Nb + WC powder mixture
- **Authors**: Eloisa P. Cardozo, Ana Sofia C.M. D'Oliveira, Moataz M. Attallah
- **Journal**: International Journal of Refractory Metals and Hard Materials
- **Year**: 2024 (Available online 12 April 2024)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.ijrmhm.2024.106687
- **Alloy system studied**: Pure Nb and Nb + 2.5 wt% WC powder mixture processed by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF)
- **Research objective**: To investigate the processability of pure Nb and Nb + 2.5 wt% WC powder mixture by LPBF, with a full optimization cycle exploring the impact of laser power, scan speed, hatch spacing, and layer thickness on build integrity. The study specifically examines the relationship between processing parameters, oxygen pick-up, carbide dissociation, and resulting hardness.
- **Material category**: Additively manufactured refractory alloy (Nb-based); LPBF-processed pure Nb and Nb-WC powder blend

# Alloy System and Research Object
- The study focuses on two material systems processed by LPBF:
  1. Pure Nb (elemental Nb powder, irregular morphology, supplied by Elite Material)
  2. Nb + 2.5 wt% WC powder mixture (elemental Nb powder blended with fine WC particles)
- The WC addition amount (2.5 wt%) was selected based on previous experiments and relevant literature (Ref. [16]).
- The choice of WC was made to better understand the effect of oxygen pick-up in the case of carbide dissociation, since W has complete solubility in Nb and does not induce significant changes in the solidification of the pure metal.
- The paper does not study Nb-W, Nb-Mo, Nb-Mo-W-Zr, Mo-based, W-based, or refractory high-entropy alloy systems.

# Processing and Heat Treatment
**Processing Method**: Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF)
- Equipment: Concept Laser M2 system
- Laser: Continuous-wave Nd-YAG fiber laser, maximum output 400 W
- Atmosphere: Controlled Argon atmosphere down to < 1000 ppm
- Scan strategy: Island scan strategy (5 mm × 5 mm islands) to reduce impact of residual stresses
- Base plate: Ti plate, no preheating
- Build area: 90 mm × 90 mm
- Build geometry: 5 mm cubes

**Process Parameters (Table 1)**
- Laser power: 275, 300, 325, 350, 375 W
- Scan speed: 400, 600, 800, 1000 mm/s
- Hatch spacing: 0.05, 0.11 mm
- Layer thickness: 30, 60 μm (most builds at 30 μm; 60 μm resulted in poor densification)

**Energy Density Equation Used**: E = P/(v·h), where E is energy density, P is laser power, v is scan speed, h is hatch spacing

**Optimal Conditions**
- Maximum densification occurred at 11.67 J/mm²
- Highest density (99.9%) achieved at P = 350 W, v = 600 mm/s, h = 0.05 mm, t = 30 μm
- Build rates for densities > 99%: 0.9 to 3.3 mm³/s
- Layer thickness 30 μm: density range 94.3% to 99.9%
- Layer thickness 60 μm: densities < 90%

**Heat Treatment**: No post-processing heat treatment was performed. All characterizations were on as-built specimens.

**Powder Preparation**
- Powder mixture performed in a rotating bottle over rollers to avoid altering particle morphology or size

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution
**Phase Constitution**
- Pure Nb builds: Single-phase BCC structure regardless of energy density
- Nb + WC builds: XRD confirms reduced crystal lattice distortion compared to pure Nb builds
- No Nb oxides detected in as-processed build microstructures, confirming the oxygen saturation limit was not reached
- WC particles: Evidence suggests the carbide particles were melted/dissociated during processing (WC melting point = 2870 °C)

**Grain Structure**
- Pure Nb cube: Blocky grain structure, typical of pure/single-phase metals processed using LPBF with island scan strategy
- Nb + WC cube: Refined microstructure compared to pure Nb, suggesting a small number of carbides may have survived dissociation
- Grain size (EBSD measurement):
  - Pure Nb: GS_Nb = 82 ± 44 μm
  - Nb + WC: GS_Nb+WC = 47 ± 20 μm

**Solidification Cracks**
- No solidification cracks observed along grain boundaries in either material
- Attributed to the low DBTT of Niobium (~200 °C) compared to other refractory metals like W (~400 °C)

**Porosity**
- Nb + WC builds showed larger porosity versus pure Nb builds
- Large and spherical pores were observed in Nb + WC builds, typical features of gas pores
- Eliminating porosity in Nb + WC builds proved to be a challenge

**Interstitial Oxygen**
- Oxygen remains in solid solution at interstitial sites in the BCC Nb structure after LPBF
- Oxygen causes asymmetric lattice distortions when compressed in octahedral interstitial sites
- XRD peak shifts toward higher angles with increasing energy density, indicating increased lattice distortion proportional to energy density

**Strengthening Mechanisms Discussed**
- Solid-solution strengthening (interstitial oxygen and substitutional W)
- Grain-boundary strengthening / Hall-Petch (noted but overridden by oxygen effect)
- The paper does not discuss precipitation strengthening, ODS strengthening, or carbide precipitation strengthening as active mechanisms

# Mechanical Properties
**Hardness Testing**
- Method: Vickers microhardness on top surface (normal to build direction)
- Load: 100 g, 10 s dwell
- 10 measurements averaged

**Pure Nb Hardness**
- Average hardness: 291 ± 11 HV₀.₁
- ~2.3× higher than cast Niobium billets reported in literature
- Hardness range varied from 3 to 26 HV deviation (similar to Nb + WC)
- Trend: Higher hardness at intermediate energy densities, reaching a plateau, then decreasing at higher energy densities (attributed to possible keyhole porosity)
- Reference comparison: Previous LPBF/Nb study reported 149 ± 8 HV₀.₁ vs. cold rolled Nb sheets at 50 HV₀.₁

**Nb + WC Hardness**
- Average hardness: 250 ± 16 HV₀.₁
- ~15% lower than pure Nb builds
- Hardness deviation range: 3 to 26 HV

**Tensile Strength Estimates (converted from hardness per Ref. [33])**
- Pure Nb: 291 HV → 941 MPa
- Nb + WC: 250 HV → 821 MPa
- Note: The Hall-Petch equation predicts higher strength with grain refinement (Nb + WC has finer grains), but pure Nb showed higher strength, indicating other mechanisms (oxygen pick-up) dominate.

**Hardness Trend with Energy Density**
- Pure Nb: Hardness increases with energy density up to a plateau, then decreases
- Nb + WC: Intermediate energy densities result in lower hardness (opposite trend to pure Nb)

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability
**Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)**
- Temperature range: 60 °C to 1350 °C
- Heating rate: 20 K/min
- Atmosphere: Both inert (argon) and oxidative (air)

**TGA Results in Argon (Fig. 13a)**
- Both materials show very small mass variation
- Nb + WC build: Small mass reduction, associated with further release of trapped CO or CO₂
- Pure Nb build: Small mass increase, related to the purity of the argon gas used

**TGA Results in Air (Fig. 13b)**
- Nb + WC builds showed less significant mass gain than pure Nb builds
- This is attributed to the lower interstitial oxygen content in Nb + WC builds due to carbide dissociation and subsequent CO/CO₂ formation
- The higher grain boundary density of Nb + WC builds (finer grains) did not contribute to the oxidation rate as much as the higher oxygen content in pure Nb builds

**Oxidation Layer Type**
- The paper does not specifically identify Al₂O₃, Cr₂O₃, SiO₂, HfO₂, or ZrO₂ oxide layers. Nb oxides are mentioned as possible after high-temperature exposure to air, but specific oxide compositions are not detailed.

**Oxygen Embrittlement Issues**
- Oxygen pick-up in pure Nb builds increases with energy density
- Oxygen remains in solid solution at interstitial sites
- Oxygen causes asymmetric lattice distortions interacting with edge and screw dislocations, resulting in hardening
- The oxygen solubility limit in Nb is up to ~10 at% at 1915 °C

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

**[O (Oxygen)]**

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Pure Nb processed by LPBF; Nb-based additive manufactured alloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Up to ~10 at% solubility limit in Nb at 1915 °C. Measured oxygen content in builds: pure Nb builds with O = 750 ppm; Nb + WC builds with O = 800 ppm. Specific oxygen pick-up values for different energy densities are presented in Table 2 but numerical values are not fully reproduced in the extracted text.
- **Main Function**: Interstitial solid-solution strengthening; lattice distortion hardening
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Oxygen occupies octahedral interstitial sites in BCC Nb, creating large shear and dilatational distortions that interact with both edge and screw dislocations, resulting in dislocation pinning and increased stored dislocation density. XRD peak shifts toward higher angles confirm lattice distortion proportional to energy density.
- **Property Effect**: Hardness increase. Average pure Nb LPBF hardness = 291 ± 11 HV₀.₁, 2.3× higher than cast Nb billets. Literature reports strengthening effect of ~0.19 MPa/ppm oxygen (per Cordero et al.). Higher energy density → higher oxygen pick-up → higher hardness.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Excessive hardness increases beyond what would be expected from grain refinement alone; may impair ductility; higher oxygen content increases oxidation rate in air at high temperature (1350 °C); oxygen-induced weakening of grain boundaries is reported in other refractory metals (e.g., Mo) though not confirmed for Nb in this study.
- **Original Evidence**: "Oxygen, as an interstitial solute in the body centered cubic Nb, causes a considerable distortion in the crystal lattice. The distortions interact with both edge and screw dislocations resulting in a pinning effect helping to increase the dislocation density stored and the yield strength" (Introduction, citing Refs [14,15]). "Analysis of the oxygen content in the as-received Nb powders and the LPBF processed cubes, Table 2, revealed that the higher the energy density, the higher is the oxygen pick-up in the build." "Oxygen pick-up plays a definitive role under the processing conditions tested" (Section 3.2).

**[WC (Tungsten Carbide)]**

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Nb + WC powder mixture processed by LPBF; Nb-based AM alloys with carbon addition
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 2.5 wt% WC; WC particle average size 0.6 μm
- **Main Function**: Oxygen reduction / de-oxidation agent through carbide dissociation; potential grain refinement; wetting improvement
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: WC particles melt/dissociate during LPBF (WC melting point = 2870 °C). The dissociated C reacts with interstitial oxygen in the Nb solid solution to form CO or CO₂, which is trapped in the material during solidification, forming large spherical gas pores where oxygen atoms concentrate. The removal of oxygen from solid solution accounts for reduced lattice distortion. A small number of carbides may survive dissociation, contributing to grain refinement (grain size reduced from 82 ± 44 μm to 47 ± 20 μm). Substitutional W from WC dissolves into Nb solid solution, providing solid-solution strengthening but less effectively than interstitial oxygen.
- **Property Effect**: Average hardness of Nb + WC builds = 250 ± 16 HV₀.₁, 15% lower than pure Nb builds (291 ± 11 HV₀.₁). Converted tensile strength: Nb = 941 MPa vs Nb + WC = 821 MPa. Reduced oxidation mass gain in air at 1350 °C. Improved processability: no apparent macro defects such as delamination or macro cracks even at high scanning speeds, attributed to improved wetting from fine WC powders.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Melting/dissociation of WC during LPBF leads to porosity formation in Nb + WC builds (large spherical gas pores); eliminating porosity proved challenging; reduction of interstitial oxygen leads to lower hardness (unintended softening compared to pure Nb).
- **Original Evidence**: "Melting of the WC (Tm = 2870 °C) can account for the porosity in Nb + WC builds because the WC dissociation offers the opportunity for C to react with the interstitial oxygen retained in the metallic Nb, thus reducing the oxygen strengthening measured in pure Nb cubes" (Section 3.2). "The addition of WC decreased the hardness, a consequence of the less significant strengthening effect of substitutional W, instead of interstitial oxygen in solid solution" (Conclusions). "The removal of oxygen from solid solution accounts for the lower hardness of Nb + WC builds contrasting with the harder builds processed with pure Niobium due to the oxygen trapped in solid solution" (Section 3.2).

**[W (Tungsten)]**

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Nb + WC powder mixture processed by LPBF; Nb-W systems
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: From dissociated 2.5 wt% WC addition; not separately quantified
- **Main Function**: Substitutional solid-solution strengthening
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: W has complete solubility in Nb, dissolving substitutionally without inducing significant changes in solidification of the pure metal.
- **Property Effect**: Solid-solution strengthening of substitutional W in Nb is reported in literature (Chaudhary et al.), but it is not as effective as interstitial solid-solution strengthening gained from oxygen (0.19 MPa/ppm per Cordero et al.). No specific numerical hardness increase attributed to W alone is provided in this paper.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **Original Evidence**: "although a hardness increase might also be expected from solid solution strengthening of substitutional W in Nb as reported by Chaudhary et al. [27], this is not as effective as the interstitial solid solution strengthening gained from oxygen retained in the microstructure of pure Nb" (Section 3.2).

**[C (Carbon, from WC dissociation)]**

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Nb + WC powder mixture processed by LPBF; Nb alloys with carbon addition for de-oxidation
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: From dissociated 2.5 wt% WC
- **Main Function**: De-oxidation agent; reacts with interstitial oxygen to form CO or CO₂
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: C from dissociated WC reacts with oxygen in Nb solid solution to form CO or CO₂, which is trapped during solidification, forming large spherical gas pores where oxygen atoms concentrate. This removes oxygen from solid solution, reducing lattice distortion. Niobium oxides can be directly reduced using carbon.
- **Property Effect**: Reduced interstitial oxygen content in Nb solid solution; lower hardness (250 vs 291 HV₀.₁); reduced lattice distortion (confirmed by XRD); reduced oxidation mass gain at high temperature.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Formation of gas porosity (CO/CO₂ trapped as large spherical pores); elimination of porosity in Nb + WC builds proved challenging.
- **Original Evidence**: "Analysis of builds with similar oxygen content (O_Nb = 750 ppm; O_Nb+WC = 800 ppm) corroborates the hypothesis that C from the dissociated WC carbides combines with the oxygen in the Nb_ss solid solution. As CO or CO₂ forms, it is trapped in the material during solidification, resulting in large porous where oxygen atoms concentrate" (Section 3.2). "The study from Braun et al. [34] also corroborates this hypothesis, reporting that an outgassing of carbon monoxide results from the alloying of molybdenum with carbon, diminishing the effects of oxygen in solid solution."

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

**Mechanism: Solid-Solution Strengthening (Interstitial Oxygen)**

- **Related Elements**: O (oxygen) as interstitial solute in BCC Nb
- **Typical Alloys**: Pure Nb processed by LPBF
- **Key Compositions**: Oxygen content up to solubility limit (~10 at% at 1915 °C); measured builds with oxygen levels ~750 ppm
- **Microstructural Changes**: Oxygen occupies octahedral interstitial sites; asymmetric lattice distortions; XRD peak shifts toward higher angles with higher oxygen content; single-phase BCC retained without oxide formation
- **Property Improvements**: Hardness 291 ± 11 HV₀.₁ (2.3× higher than cast Nb); converted tensile strength ~941 MPa; strengthening rate ~0.19 MPa/ppm
- **Risks and Limitations**: Higher oxygen content increases oxidation rate in air at 1350 °C; hardness increase may not translate to improved ductility; oxygen pick-up is process-dependent (increases with energy density)
- **Original Evidence**: Section 3.1 and 3.2; Table 2; Figs. 4, 5, 11, 13

**Mechanism: Solid-Solution Strengthening (Substitutional W)**

- **Related Elements**: W (tungsten)
- **Typical Alloys**: Nb + WC powder mixture processed by LPBF
- **Key Compositions**: 2.5 wt% WC added, with W dissolving substitutionally in Nb
- **Microstructural Changes**: Substitutional dissolution of W in BCC Nb
- **Property Improvements**: Solid-solution strengthening reported in literature (Chaudhary et al.); less effective than interstitial oxygen strengthening
- **Risks and Limitations**: Not effective enough to compensate for loss of interstitial oxygen strengthening (net hardness decrease)
- **Original Evidence**: "although a hardness increase might also be expected from solid solution strengthening of substitutional W in Nb as reported by Chaudhary et al. [27], this is not as effective as the interstitial solid solution strengthening gained from oxygen" (Section 3.2)

**Mechanism: Grain-Boundary Strengthening / Grain Refinement (Hall-Petch)**

- **Related Elements**: WC addition leading to grain refinement
- **Typical Alloys**: Nb + WC powder mixture processed by LPBF
- **Key Compositions**: 2.5 wt% WC
- **Microstructural Changes**: Grain refinement from 82 ± 44 μm (pure Nb) to 47 ± 20 μm (Nb + WC); possible survival of small number of carbide particles contributing to refinement
- **Property Improvements**: Hall-Petch equation predicts tensile strength increase with grain refinement
- **Risks and Limitations**: Despite finer grains, Nb + WC builds showed lower strength (821 MPa vs 941 MPa converted), indicating oxygen content dominates over grain size effects in this system; higher grain boundary density did not accelerate oxidation rate
- **Original Evidence**: "The Hall-Petch equation predicts that tensile strength might increase with grain refining. However, in spite of the coarser grain size of the pure Nb builds a higher strength was measured indicating that other mechanisms are active. Oxygen pick-up plays a definitive role" (Section 3.2)

**Mechanism: De-oxidation via Carbide Dissociation (Oxygen Removal from Solid Solution)**

- **Related Elements**: C (from WC), O
- **Typical Alloys**: Nb + WC powder mixture processed by LPBF; related to Mo + C alloying (Braun et al.)
- **Key Compositions**: 2.5 wt% WC; measured oxygen ~800 ppm in Nb + WC builds
- **Microstructural Changes**: WC melts (Tm = 2870 °C); C reacts with O in solid solution to form CO/CO₂; large spherical gas pores form where oxygen atoms concentrate; reduced lattice distortion (XRD confirmation)
- **Property Improvements**: Reduced oxygen content in solid solution; lower hardness (250 HV₀.₁); reduced oxidation rate at 1350 °C in air
- **Risks and Limitations**: Formation of gas porosity; porosity elimination challenging in Nb + WC builds; loss of oxygen strengthening effect
- **Original Evidence**: "The hypothesis that oxygen was removed from the solid solution and reacted with C from the dissociated carbides is also substantiated by the reduction in the distortion of the crystal lattice when processing the Nb + WC mixture identified in XRD analysis" (Section 3.2)

**Mechanism: Additive Manufacturing-Oriented Composition Control (Oxygen Pick-up Sensitivity to Energy Density)**

- **Related Elements**: O (from processing atmosphere)
- **Typical Alloys**: Pure Nb LPBF
- **Key Compositions**: Energy density range covered: ~5 to 14 J/mm²
- **Microstructural Changes**: Higher energy density → higher oxygen pick-up → greater lattice distortion; oxygen remains in solid solution at interstitial sites
- **Property Improvements**: Hardness increases up to plateau at intermediate energy densities, then decreases (possible keyhole porosity at high energy density)
- **Risks and Limitations**: Oxygen pick-up continuous at high energy density → excessive hardness potential; oxidation rate increase; delicate balance needed
- **Original Evidence**: "Analysis of the oxygen content in the as-received Nb powders and the LPBF processed cubes, Table 2, revealed that the higher the energy density, the higher is the oxygen pick-up in the build" (Section 3.1)

# Key Conclusions
1. LPBF can produce pure Nb parts with density exceeding 99% using non-spherical Nb powder; highly dense Nb + WC builds can also be achieved.
2. The relationship between energy density and build density does not follow a clear trend for the parameter ranges tested; some Nb + WC builds contained large porosity.
3. Maximum densification occurred at 11.67 J/mm² for both pure Nb and Nb + WC.
4. In pure Nb builds, higher energy density led to higher oxygen pick-up, resulting in increased hardness.
5. The addition of WC decreased hardness by ~15% (250 vs 291 HV₀.₁), attributed to less significant strengthening effect of substitutional W compared to interstitial oxygen in solid solution.
6. WC dissociation during LPBF reduces oxygen trapped in builds (via CO/CO₂ formation), consequently slowing the oxidation rate in air at 1350 °C.
7. Oxygen pick-up plays a definitive role in the mechanical response, overriding grain size effects.
8. No solidification cracking observed along grain boundaries in either system, attributed to the relatively low DBTT of Nb (~200 °C).
9. Process window: P = 350 W, v = 600 mm/s, h = 0.05 mm, t = 30 μm achieved 99.9% density with build rates from 0.9 to 3.3 mm³/s, wider than previously reported for Nb LPBF.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope
1. The study only examines pure Nb and Nb + 2.5 wt% WC; no other alloy compositions or WC fractions were investigated.
2. No post-processing heat treatment was applied; results are specific to as-built LPBF condition.
3. Mechanical testing was limited to hardness measurements; no uniaxial tensile or compressive testing was performed. Tensile strength values are estimates converted from hardness values using Ref. [33].
4. No fracture toughness, creep, rupture life, or strain-rate sensitivity data are provided.
5. High-temperature properties were only assessed through TGA (oxidation behavior); no high-temperature mechanical property measurements are reported.
6. The WC dissociation mechanism is inferred from hardness, XRD, and oxygen content analyses, but direct microstructural evidence of surviving carbides is limited (EBSD indicates possible carbide survival but is not conclusive).
7. The specific oxygen content values for different energy densities are presented in Table 2 of the original paper but specific numerical values are not fully reproduced in the provided text.
8. The study uses non-spherical Nb powder with 33 μm average particle size; results may not directly transfer to spherical or gas-atomized powders.
9. Builds were performed on a Ti plate without preheating; results may differ with substrate preheating or different substrate materials.
10. Applicable primarily to Nb-based LPBF processing; extrapolation to other refractory metals (Mo, W, Ta) should be done with caution, as their DBTT and oxygen affinities differ significantly.
