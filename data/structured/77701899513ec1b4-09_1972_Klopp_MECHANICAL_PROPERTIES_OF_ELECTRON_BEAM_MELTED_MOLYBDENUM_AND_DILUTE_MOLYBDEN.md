# Paper Metadata

- **Title:** MECHANICAL PROPERTIES OF ELECTRON-BEAM-MELTED MOLYBDENUM AND DILUTE MOLYBDENUM-RHENIUM ALLOYS
- **Authors:** William D. Klopp and Walter R. Witzke
- **Journal/Report:** NASA Technical Memorandum, NASA TM X-2576 (Lewis Research Center)
- **Year:** 1972 (June)
- **DOI:** Not explicitly stated in the original paper (NASA TM report)
- **Alloy system studied:** Mo–Re dilute alloys (unalloyed Mo, Mo–3.9 at.% Re, Mo–5.9 at.% Re, Mo–7.7 at.% Re)
- **Research objective:** To determine the effects of rhenium at concentrations less than 10 atomic percent on the low-temperature ductility, low- and high-temperature tensile properties, high-temperature creep properties, and recrystallization and grain-growth behavior of molybdenum.
- **Material category:** Mo-based alloy (dilute Mo–Re alloy), electron-beam-melted refractory alloy

---

# Alloy System and Research Object

The paper investigates electron-beam-melted unalloyed molybdenum and three dilute molybdenum–rhenium alloys (Mo–3.9 at.% Re, Mo–5.9 at.% Re, Mo–7.7 at.% Re). The primary focus is on the "ductilizing" or "solution softening" effect of rhenium in Group VIA metals at alloying levels below 10 atomic percent. A secondary objective was to characterize mechanical properties, recrystallization temperature, and grain-growth behavior.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Melting
- Starting materials: –325 mesh Mo powder (99.97 wt.% purity) and –200 mesh Re powder (99.98 wt.% purity)
- Powders were blended, compacted isostatically into electrodes ~3 cm diameter × 61 cm length
- Electrodes were **triple electron-beam melted** into 6 cm diameter ingots, 10–15 cm long

## Extrusion
- Extrusion billets: 5 cm diameter × 8–13 cm length
- Extrusion temperatures: 1425 °C (2600 °F) for unalloyed Mo; 1540 °C (2800 °F) for Mo–3.9 Re; 1455 °C (2650 °F) for Mo–5.9 Re and Mo–7.7 Re
- Reduction ratios: 8 for Mo and Mo–3.9 Re; 6 for Mo–5.9 Re and Mo–7.7 Re
- No difficulties encountered during extrusion

## Swaging
- Swaging temperatures: 1095–815 °C (2000–1500 °F) for unalloyed Mo; 1175–925 °C (2150–1700 °F) for the alloys
- Final rod diameters: 0.66 cm (0.26 in) for unalloyed Mo, Mo–3.9 Re, and Mo–7.7 Re; 0.66 cm for Mo–5.9 Re
- Reduction percentages during swaging: 89% (Mo), 91% (Mo–3.9 Re), 91% (Mo–5.9 Re), 91% (Mo–7.7 Re)

## Rolling
- Rolling temperatures: 1125–955 °C (2060–1750 °F) for unalloyed Mo sheet; 1150–925 °C (2100–1700 °F) for alloys
- Final sheet thickness: 0.084 cm (0.033 in) for Mo sheet; 0.089 cm (0.035 in) for Mo–3.9 Re; 0.102 cm (0.040 in) for Mo–5.9 Re and Mo–7.7 Re
- Reduction during rolling: 92% (Mo sheet), 81% (Mo–3.9 Re sheet), 95% (Mo–5.9 Re and Mo–7.7 Re sheet)
- Rolling produced 81–95% cold work for the sheet products (i.e., sheets with 81–92% cold work per annealing data)

## Heat Treatments (Annealing)
- Annealing for recrystallization studies: 1 hour at 540 °C to 1980 °C (1000 °F to 3600 °F)
- Bend test specimens annealed at 480 °C (900 °F), 925 °C (1700 °F), 1095 °C (2000 °F), 1260 °C (2300 °F), 1370 °C (2500 °F)
- Tensile specimen annealing: 1315 °C (2400 °F) or 1425 °C (2600 °F) for 1 hour, depending on specimen
- Low-temperature bend heat treatment conducted in induction-heated hydrogen-atmosphere tube furnace with W / W–25Re thermocouple
- Annealing atmosphere for tensile specimens not explicitly stated as separate from bend specimens; high-temperature tensile tests conducted in vacuum at 10⁻³ N/m² (10⁻⁵ torr)

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Phase Constitution
- The paper does not explicitly describe the phase constitution (e.g., BCC matrix) of the Mo–Re alloys. However, reference is made to the "similarities in the molybdenum-rhenium and tungsten-rhenium phase diagrams." Given the dilute rhenium levels (<7.7 at.%), the alloys are expected to remain single-phase BCC solid solutions. Not explicitly stated in the original paper.

## Grain Size and Recrystallization
- **Recrystallization temperature (1 hour, 100% recrystallization):**
  - Unalloyed Mo: **925 °C (1700 °F)** — low due to high purity of electron-beam-melted Mo
  - Mo–3.9 Re: **1260 °C (2300 °F)**
  - Mo–5.9 Re and Mo–7.7 Re: little or no change from 1260 °C (2300 °F) as Re content increased from 3.9 to 7.7 at.%
- **Annealed grain sizes after 1 hour at 1425 °C (2600 °F):**
  - Decreased with increasing Re content (see Figure 9)
  - Unalloyed Mo: ~58 μm (from Table III bend data at 1260 °C; not directly the same condition)
  - Grain sizes after recrystallization: 0.006 mm (6 μm) at 0 at.% Re down to ~0.002 mm (2 μm) at 7.7 at.% Re (from Figure 9)
- **Worked subgrain sizes:** 1.2–2.9 μm in as-rolled sheet and as-swaged rod

## Elemental Segregation
- Not explicitly discussed in the original paper.

## Precipitates/Second Phases
- No carbide, oxide, boride, silicide, or other second-phase precipitates are reported. The alloys are treated as dilute solid solutions.

## Strengthening Mechanisms Discussed
- **Solid-solution strengthening**: Rhenium promotes "normal solid solution strengthening" at elevated temperatures.
- **Solution softening**: Below ~10 at.% Re, rhenium reduces hardness and improves low-temperature ductility.
- **Grain refinement**: Re additions decrease annealed grain size.
- **Recrystallization retardation**: Re increases recrystallization temperature.

## High-Temperature Creep Properties

Creep tests were performed in vacuum at 980–1650 °C (1800–3000 °F) under single-load and step-load conditions. Data follow the power-law relation: ε̇ = k σⁿ.

**Table VI: Selected creep data**

| Alloy | Condition | Test temperature (°C) | Stress (MN/m²) | Steady creep rate (sec⁻¹) | Stress dependency, n |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 Mo | As-rolled | 1205 | 23.0 | 0.22×10⁻⁶ | 6.2 |
| 100 Mo | As-swaged | 1315 | 13.8–31.0 | 0.086×10⁻⁶–4.4×10⁻⁶ | 4.8 |
| Mo–3.9 Re | As-rolled | 980 | 47.4–134 | 0.03×10⁻⁶–1.2×10⁻⁶ | 3.5 |
| Mo–5.9 Re | As-swaged | 1315 | 34.5–82.7 | 0.083×10⁻⁶–60×10⁻⁶ | 7.5 |
| Mo–7.7 Re | As-swaged | 1315 | 41.4–82.7 | 0.49×10⁻⁶–160×10⁻⁶ | 8.2 |

- **At 1315 °C (2400 °F):** Mo–5.9 Re has a 100% greater (i.e., double) creep strength than unalloyed Mo (stress for same creep rate).
- **At 1650 °C (3000 °F):** Creep strength increases with Re content up to 5.9 at.% Re, then decreases slightly at 7.7 at.% Re. This plateau is similar to W–Re alloys.
- **Stress dependency (n) trends:** n increases with increasing strength. At 1315 °C: n = 4.8 for unalloyed Mo; n = 7.5 for Mo–5.9 Re. This contrasts with the frequent observation of decreasing n with increasing alloy content.
- **Rupture life data** are provided in Table VI for several creep conditions.

## Hardness
- Diamond pyramid hardness (Table VII) for annealed recrystallized materials:
  - Unalloyed Mo: typically 173–177 kg/mm² (after recrystallization at 980–1425 °C)
  - Mo–3.9 Re: 146–159 kg/mm² after recrystallization (lower than unalloyed Mo)
  - Mo–5.9 Re: 165–169 kg/mm² after recrystallization
  - Mo–7.7 Re: 170–183 kg/mm² after recrystallization
- Hardness decreases with increasing annealing temperature until complete recrystallization, then varies with grain size.

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

The original paper does not discuss oxidation resistance, oxidation weight gain, oxide layer type, or oxygen/nitrogen embrittlement. No oxidation testing was performed. The focus was on mechanical properties under vacuum or low-temperature conditions.

**Relevant limitation:** High-temperature tensile and creep tests were conducted in vacuum (10⁻⁵ torr), so the data are applicable to inert or vacuum service conditions only. The paper does not address air-exposure or oxidizing-environment performance.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Rhenium (Re)

**[Element]:** Re (Rhenium)

**[Applicable Alloy System]:** Dilute Mo–Re alloys (<10 at.% Re); also relevant to W–Re alloys (referenced). Group VIA metals.

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]:** 3.9 at.%, 5.9 at.%, 7.7 at.% Re in Mo.

**[Main Function]:** 
1. Low-temperature ductilization (solution softening): reduces ductile-brittle transition temperature.
2. Solid-solution strengthening at elevated temperatures.
3. Increases recrystallization temperature and refines grain size.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]:** 
- Solution softening below ~10 at.% Re reduces low-temperature hardness and promotes ductility.
- At elevated temperatures, Re acts as a normal solid-solution strengthener.
- Re retards recrystallization and reduces annealed grain size.

**[Property Effect]:**
- Bend transition temperature reduction: addition of 6 at.% Re reduces bend transition temperature by about 165 °C (300 °F). The Mo–7.7 Re alloy has a worked bend transition temperature below −195 °C and recrystallized bend transition temperature of −130 °C (−200 °F).
- Tensile ductile-brittle transition temperature reduction: Mo–7.7 Re in recrystallized condition has a transition temperature of −170 °C (−270 °F) compared with 65 °C (150 °F) for unalloyed Mo (reduction of ~235 °C).
- 980 °C tensile strength: ~2× increase with 5 at.% Re.
- 1315 °C tensile strength: ~70% increase with 5.9 at.% Re; ~60% increase with 7.7 at.% Re.
- 1315 °C creep strength: ~100% increase (double) with 5.9 at.% Re.
- Recrystallization temperature: increased from 925 °C to 1260 °C with only 3.9 at.% Re.
- Annealed grain size at 1425 °C: reduced from ~0.006 mm to ~0.002 mm with increasing Re content.

**[Potential Side Effects]:** 
- Creep strength decreases slightly when Re content is increased from 5.9 to 7.7 at.% at 1650 °C (creep-strength plateau).
- Not explicitly stated as causing density increase, workability problems, or brittle phase formation in this paper.

**[Original Evidence]:** Abstract, Summary, Figures 2–9, Tables III–VII. Key sentence: "The addition of 6 atomic percent rhenium reduces the transition temperature of molybdenum by about 165 °C (300 °F)."

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related elements:** Re
- **Typical alloys:** Mo–Re (3.9, 5.9, 7.7 at.%) dilute alloys
- **Key compositions:** Mo–5.9 at.% Re, Mo–7.7 at.% Re
- **Microstructural changes:** Single-phase solid solution; no precipitation reported.
- **Property improvements:** 
  - 980 °C ultimate tensile strength: ~2× increase with 5 at.% Re.
  - 1315 °C tensile strength: ~70% increase with 5.9 at.% Re; ~60% increase with 7.7 at.% Re.
  - 1315 °C creep strength: ~100% increase with 5.9 at.% Re.
- **Risks/limitations:** Creep strength plateaus or decreases slightly at 7.7 at.% Re at 1650 °C.

---

## Solution Softening (Low-Temperature Ductilization)

- **Related elements:** Re (also platinum-group metals)
- **Typical alloys:** Mo–Re (<10 at.% Re), W–Re
- **Key compositions:** Mo–7.7 at.% Re gives largest transition temperature reduction.
- **Microstructural changes:** Not explicitly tied to microstructural features; attributed to solute effects on dislocation mobility.
- **Property improvements:** 
  - Bend transition temperature reduced by ~165 °C (300 °F) with 6 at.% Re.
  - Tensile transition temperature reduced by ~235 °C (7.7 at.% Re, recrystallized).
  - Mo–7.7 Re recrystallized tensile DBTT = −170 °C vs. 65 °C for unalloyed Mo.
- **Risks/limitations:** The mechanism is described as "solution softening" rather than conventional solid-solution strengthening; effect is limited to ≤10 at.% Re.

---

## Grain-Boundary Strengthening / Grain Refinement

- **Related elements:** Re
- **Typical alloys:** Mo–Re
- **Key compositions:** Mo–7.7 Re
- **Microstructural changes:** Re decreases annealed grain size from ~0.006 mm (2 μm? see Figure 9 axis) to smaller sizes at 1425 °C. Re also delays recrystallization.
- **Property improvements:** Finer grains may contribute to lower DBTT and improved ductility.
- **Risks/limitations:** Not explicitly analyzed as a strengthening mechanism in this paper.

---

## Recrystallization Retardation

- **Related elements:** Re
- **Typical alloys:** Mo–Re
- **Key compositions:** Mo–3.9 Re raises recrystallization temperature from 925 °C to 1260 °C.
- **Microstructural changes:** Delayed recrystallization during annealing; smaller recrystallized grain size.
- **Property improvements:** Retains worked microstructure to higher service temperatures; may enhance high-temperature strength.
- **Risks/limitations:** Limited benefit beyond 3.9 at.% Re for recrystallization temperature.

# Key Conclusions

1. Rhenium additions of 3.9–7.7 at.% significantly reduce the ductile-brittle transition temperature of electron-beam-melted molybdenum in both bend and tensile tests and in both worked and recrystallized conditions.
2. The maximum ductility improvement is shown by Mo–7.7 at.% Re, which has a recrystallized tensile ductile-brittle transition temperature of −170 °C (−270 °F) compared with 65 °C (150 °F) for unalloyed Mo.
3. Rhenium promotes normal solid-solution strengthening at elevated temperatures. At 1315 °C, Mo–5.9 Re has a 70% greater tensile strength and 100% greater creep strength than unalloyed Mo.
4. At 1650 °C, tensile strength increases by about 50% with 7.7 at.% Re.
5. Rhenium increases the recrystallization temperature of Mo from 925 °C to 1260 °C (for 3.9 at.% Re) and decreases annealed grain size at 1425 °C.
6. The mechanical behavior trends of dilute Mo–Re alloys are similar to those observed previously for dilute W–Re alloys.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Alloy system coverage:** Only dilute Mo–Re alloys (0–7.7 at.% Re) are studied. Results may not extrapolate to higher Re contents (e.g., Mo–35 Re or W–26 Re).
- **Processing route:** Only electron-beam-melted material is studied. Powder metallurgy, arc melting, or additive manufacturing routes are not assessed.
- **Test atmosphere:** High-temperature tensile and creep tests were conducted in vacuum (10⁻⁵ torr). No oxidation resistance or air-exposure data are provided.
- **Interstitial content:** Only moderately low O, N, C values are reported; the effect of higher or lower interstitial contents on DBTT is not systematically studied.
- **Phase constitution:** The paper assumes single-phase BCC solid solutions but does not provide direct phase identification or XRD analysis.
- **No second-phase strengthening:** No carbide, boride, or oxide dispersion strengthening is investigated.
- **Time frame:** Data reflect 1972-era electron-beam melting and testing capabilities; modern processing may yield different impurity levels or microstructures.
- **Not stated:** Elastic modulus values, density, oxidation behavior, fracture toughness, and strain-rate sensitivity are not reported in this paper.
