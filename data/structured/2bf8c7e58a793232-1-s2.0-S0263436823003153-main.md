# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Synergistic effects between potassium bubbles and high-energy-rate forging enabled ultrahigh strength and ductility of tungsten alloy
- **Authors**: Ning Dang, Youyun Lian, Jiupeng Song, Shaowei Dai, Binyou Yan, Feng Fan, Jianbao Wang, Xiang Liu
- **Journal**: International Journal of Refractory Metals and Hard Materials
- **Year**: 2023
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.ijrmhm.2023.106415
- **Alloy system studied**: Potassium-doped tungsten (K-doped W, also referred to as AKS-W)
- **Material category**: W-based alloy, potassium-bubble dispersion-strengthened W alloy
- **Research objective**: To improve both strength and ductility of W-based alloys through synergistic effects of K bubble doping and two-step high-energy-rate forging (HERF), targeting plasma-facing material (PFM) applications in fusion reactors.

# Alloy System and Research Object

The paper studies potassium-doped tungsten (AKS-W). AKS stands for “Al–K–Si” doped tungsten (the conventional non-sag tungsten doping route), although the final sintered material contains K at 83 ppm, O at 53 ppm, Al ≤ 20 ppm, Si ≤ 20 ppm, balance W. The material is a W-based dispersion-strengthened alloy where the dispersoid is nanometer-scale potassium-filled bubbles rather than a solid ceramic phase.

Two material states are compared in the paper:

- **S-AKS-W**: as-sintered K-doped W ingot.
- **HERF-AKS-W**: two-step high-energy-rate forged K-doped W.

The paper notes that the amount of doped K is typically lower than 100 ppm, which is two to three orders of magnitude lower than carbide and oxide dispersoid additions.

The final HERF-AKS-W composition is the same as S-AKS-W since HERF is only a thermomechanical processing step.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Powder preparation
- Solid–liquid doping process: tungsten blue oxide (TBO) powders were mixed with salts containing K, Al, Si in solution.
- Drying and reduction by H2 to form W-based powder particles doped with K, Al, Si.
- Acid washing to remove residual elements not incorporated into W grains → K-doped W powder.

## Consolidation
- Cold isostatic pressing at 195 MPa for 180 s.
- High-temperature sintering at 2045 °C for 6 h.
- Result: cylindrical ingot, diameter 53.5 mm, height 86.5 mm → **S-AKS-W**.

## High-energy-rate forging (HERF)
- High-speed pneumatic rammer at 40 MPa pressure and 20 m/s speed.
- **Two hits / two-step HERF**:

| Step | Heating temperature | Atmosphere | Initial → final thickness |
|---|---|---|---|
| First strike | 1550 °C | Hydrogen atmosphere furnace, forging in air | Cylinder → 26 mm thickness |
| Second strike | 1350 °C | Not explicitly stated | 26 mm → 8.9 mm |

- Overall height reduction rate: **89.71%**.
- First strike height reduction rate: 69.94% (accounting for approximately 79% of the total reduction).
- Final product: disk-shaped samples of approximately 70 mm diameter → **HERF-AKS-W**.

No post-forging annealing is reported.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## S-AKS-W (as-sintered)
- Equiaxed grain structure.
- Average grain size: **7.66 μm**.
- K bubbles randomly distributed along grain boundaries.
- Average K bubble size: **149 nm**.
- Relative density: **92.3%**.
- Mainly high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs).
- No obvious preferred orientation.
- Residual pores present due to incomplete densification.
- Bubbles likely contain potassium aluminium silicate rather than pure K because sintering temperature (2045 °C) is lower than typical direct sintering temperatures for non-sag tungsten wire.

## HERF-AKS-W (forged)
- Laminated / pancake-shaped grain microstructure with strong elongation along the radial direction (RD).
- Average grain width (cross-section): **1.54 μm**.
- Average grain length (cross-section): **8.52 μm**.
- Grain aspect ratio: **~5.53:1**.
- Relative density: **99.6%**.
- Preferred orientations of <111> and <100> along the forging direction (FD).
- Low-angle grain boundary (LAGB) fraction:
  - Radial cross-section: **87.27%** LAGBs (2°–10° misorientation).
  - Axial cross-section: **75.54%** LAGBs.
- Average K bubble size reduced to approximately **53 nm** (from ~149 nm in S-AKS-W).
- K bubbles are denser and more uniformly distributed along grain boundaries after HERF.
- No distinct bubble tubes or rows observed, unlike traditional K-doped W rods/wires.
- Bubble size distribution follows a Gaussian distribution.
- Increased dislocation density (including edge or mixed-type dislocations) due to HERF.
- No second phases other than K bubbles are reported. The matrix remains BCC W.

The paper emphasizes that K bubbles in HERF-AKS-W form by deformation and break-up of pre-existing larger bubbles, followed by spheroidization and stabilization under the balance of Laplace pressure and internal gas pressure.

# Mechanical Properties

## Density and hardness

| Sample | Relative density (%) | Vickers hardness (HV) |
|---|---|---|
| S-AKS-W | 92.3 | 328.5 ± 10.4 |
| HERF-AKS-W | 99.6 | 457.4 ± 3.6 |

Hardness increase after HERF: approximately **39%**.

## Tensile properties

Tensile tests were conducted using an Instron 5967 machine under ambient conditions at a constant loading rate of 0.06 mm/min. Specimen cross-section: 1.2 × 0.75 mm², gauge length: 5 mm.

| Temperature (°C) | Ultimate tensile strength, UTS (MPa) | Total elongation, TE (%) |
|---|---|---|
| RT (~25 °C) | **1393** | **1.8** |
| 50 | **1268** | **9.22** |
| 100 | **1177** | **19.62** |
| 200 | **915** | **33.12** |
| 400 | **708** | **29.11** |

Note: Elongation was calculated based on the point of yield to minimize influence of elastic elongation from the tensile instrument.

## Ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT)
- DBTT criterion used in this paper: the lowest temperature at which a sample undergoes a minimum elongation of 5% without failure.
- HERF-AKS-W DBTT: **between room temperature and 50 °C**.
- At RT the sample fractures in a largely brittle manner (TE = 1.8%), while at 50 °C it shows 9.22% elongation.

## Comparison with other W alloys
The paper provides extensive comparison in Fig. 4:

- Most dispersion-strengthened W alloys (carbides, oxides) have DBTT > 100 °C with UTS < 850 MPa.
- TiC-, HfC-, and most Y2O3-strengthened W alloys show negligible elongation at 100 °C with UTS < 1000 MPa.
- Swaged and swaged+HERF W-Y2O3 show elongation > 20% at 100 °C but lower UTS than HERF-AKS-W.
- At 100 °C, HERF-AKS-W has comparable UTS to HERFed pure W and K-doped W but much higher elongation.
- At 200 °C, the elongation of 33.12% is reported to be the highest observed in W-based alloys.

## Fracture behavior
- RT: predominantly transgranular cleavage fracture with river-like cleavage patterns, plus a small number of intergranular cracks and microcracks along grain boundaries.
- 50 °C: mixed brittle and ductile fracture, rough surface with numerous small hills (typical ductile feature).
- 100–400 °C: fracture surfaces dominated by deformed grains with knife-edge features, indicating plastic deformation through slip.

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

The original paper does not report experimental oxidation resistance data. However, it discusses several service-stability relevant aspects:

- K-doped W exhibits thermal conductivity almost indistinguishable from pure W, which is important for PFM applications.
- K bubbles serve as defect traps, absorbing and dispersing irradiation defects, and helping to prevent formation of voids and H/He bubbles, thereby enhancing radiation resistance and plasma etching resistance.
- K-doped W exhibits superior mechanical properties at temperatures > 1700 °C due to excellent high-temperature stability of K bubbles; K is almost insoluble in W and avoids Ostwald ripening typical of carbide and oxide dispersoids.
- Grain boundary impurity levels are low (K 83 ppm, O 53 ppm, Al ≤ 20 ppm, Si ≤ 20 ppm), which enhances grain boundary cohesion and reduces intergranular brittleness.
- The paper does not discuss high-temperature oxidation weight gain, oxide layer formation, or oxygen/nitrogen embrittlement behavior explicitly; oxidation resistance data are not provided.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

---

## K (Potassium doping into W)

### Applicable Alloy System
W-based alloys, specifically K-doped W (AKS-W) for plasma-facing material applications.

### Addition Amount or Composition Range
Sintered ingot: **83 ppm K** in W (initial K-doped W powder contains 147 ppm K); typical K doping level is less than 100 ppm, two to three orders of magnitude lower than carbide or oxide dispersoid additions.

### Main Function
- Forms nano-sized K bubbles along grain boundaries, providing dispersion strengthening, grain boundary pinning, defect trapping, and radiation resistance improvement.
- Reduces DBTT and enhances high-temperature stability compared to carbide/oxide dispersoids.
- Preserves thermal conductivity close to that of pure W.

### Microstructural Mechanism
- During sintering, K-containing compounds (potassium silicates, potassium aluminosilicates) decompose and K remains trapped in the W matrix as gaseous bubbles because of large atomic radius and very low solubility.
- K bubbles pin grain boundaries, preventing grain coarsening during high-temperature processing.
- K bubbles act as defect traps for irradiation defects, preventing void and H/He bubble formation.
- At temperatures above the boiling point of K, vapor pressure inside bubbles increases, generating a stress field at the gas/solid interface and enhancing dispersion strengthening.
- Because K diffusion in W lattice is very difficult, Ostwald ripening of bubbles is avoided.

### Property Effect
- UTS at RT: 1393 MPa; at 100 °C: 1177 MPa; at 200 °C: 915 MPa (HERF-AKS-W).
- DBTT lowered to between RT and 50 °C.
- Elongation at 100 °C: 19.62%; at 200 °C: 33.12%.
- High-temperature mechanical properties superior above 1700 °C due to bubble stability (qualitative statement).
- Thermal conductivity “almost indistinguishable from pure W” (qualitative statement).

### Potential Side Effects
- K doping slightly reduces thermal diffusivity, though specific heat remains high, so overall thermal conductivity is nearly unchanged.
- Residual Al and Si impurities may remain if not fully removed by acid washing.

### Original Evidence
- Table 1: composition data.
- Section 1: “K-doped W exhibits a thermal conductivity that is almost indistinguishable from that of pure W”.
- Section 4.1: K bubble formation stages and characteristics.
- Fig. 6: K bubble SEM images and size distributions.

## Al + Si co-doping (dopant carriers in W)

### Applicable Alloy System
W-based alloys (AKS-W), where Al and Si are used as dopant carriers to introduce K.

### Addition Amount or Composition Range
Sintered ingot: **Al ≤ 20 ppm, Si ≤ 20 ppm**; initial K-doped W powder contains 28 ppm Al and 229 ppm Si.

### Main Function
- Al and Si serve as carriers that form potassium silicates and potassium aluminosilicates during powder doping.
- These compounds migrate to interparticle necks and subsequently decompose, releasing K that forms bubbles.

### Microstructural Mechanism
- During sintering, potassium silicates decompose at approximately 1560 °C; potassium aluminosilicates decompose rapidly only when temperature exceeds 2100 °C.
- Al and Si have relatively lower atomic radii and strong solubility in W matrix, so they tend to diffuse to the surface.
- Because sintering in this work was performed at 2045 °C (below 2100 °C), a significant portion of potassium aluminium silicate remains in the matrix rather than decomposing.

### Property Effect
The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for the individual effect of Al or Si on mechanical properties.

### Potential Side Effects
- Incompletely decomposed potassium aluminosilicate may remain as the main bubble component instead of pure K.
- Residual Al and Si can contribute to impurity content; however, final concentrations are low (≤ 20 ppm).

### Original Evidence
- Section 2.1 and Table 1.
- Section 4.1: “the main component in bubbles is potassium aluminium silicate instead of K”.

## O (Oxygen impurity in W)

### Applicable Alloy System
K-doped W (AKS-W).

### Addition Amount or Composition Range
Sintered ingot: **53 ppm O** (initial K-doped W powder: 2180 ppm O).

### Main Function
O is an impurity; its low final concentration contributes to high grain boundary cohesion and reduced intergranular brittleness.

### Microstructural Mechanism
The paper does not present a specific mechanistic discussion of O; rather, it treats low O content as a favorable feature because grain boundary impurity content remains very low. O is mostly removed during hydrogen reduction and acid washing.

### Property Effect
The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for the individual effect of O on mechanical properties. However, the low O content is cited as one reason for strong grain boundary adhesion, leading to transgranular cleavage rather than intergranular fracture.

### Potential Side Effects
High O content in W-based alloys is known to reduce grain boundary cohesion and ductility; the paper mentions that impurities such as O, N, and P generated by second-phase particles can reduce GB cohesion.

### Original Evidence
- Table 1.
- Section 4.2: “Due to the limited presence of impurities and the abundance of grain boundaries, the concentration of impurities within the boundaries remains very low. This enhances grain boundary cohesion and reduces intergranular brittleness.”

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

---

## Grain boundary strengthening + grain refinement (HERF + K bubble pinning)

### Related Elements
K (via bubbles), W (matrix)

### Typical Alloys
HERF-AKS-W, K-doped W, HERF-processed pure W, HERF W-Y2O3

### Key Compositions
K: 83 ppm; W: balance

### Microstructural Changes
- HERF reduces residual porosity (relative density from 92.3% to 99.6%).
- Average grain width reduced from 7.66 μm (as-sintered) to 1.54 μm.
- Grain morphology changes from equiaxed to laminated/pancake-like with aspect ratio ~5.53:1.
- High fraction of LAGBs: 87.27% (radial) and 75.54% (axial).
- K bubble average size reduced from 149 nm to 53 nm, with higher number density.
- Increased dislocation density.
- Formation of <111> and <100> preferred orientations along FD.

### Property Improvements
- UTS at RT: 1393 MPa; at 100 °C: 1177 MPa; at 200 °C: 915 MPa.
- Hardness: 457.4 HV (vs 328.5 HV for S-AKS-W, ~39% increase).
- DBTT between RT and 50 °C.
- Elongation at 100 °C: 19.62%; at 200 °C: 33.12%.

### Risks and Limitations
- Grain refinement alone tends to increase strength but may reduce ductility unless accompanied by beneficial dislocation character changes and texture evolution.
- The strengthening effect of grain refinement follows Hall–Petch behavior.

### Original Evidence
- Section 4.2: “the high strength primarily arises from grain boundary strengthening and grain refinement brought by both HERF and K bubbles.”
- Fig. 3: EBSD maps and grain size distributions.
- Table 2 and Table 3.

## K bubble dispersion strengthening

### Related Elements
K bubbles in W

### Typical Alloys
K-doped W, AKS-W

### Key Compositions
K: 83 ppm in sintered ingot

### Microstructural Changes
- Nano-sized K bubbles distributed along grain boundaries; average size 53 nm after HERF.
- Bubbles pin grain boundaries and hinder boundary movement during high-temperature processing.
- Bubbles serve as defect traps and inhibit void/H/He bubble formation.
- At >1700 °C, gaseous K inside bubbles generates a stress field at the gas/solid interface, enhancing dispersion strengthening.

### Property Improvements
- High-temperature mechanical properties maintained at >1700 °C (qualitative).
- Radiation resistance and plasma etching resistance enhanced (qualitative).
- Thermal conductivity remains nearly equal to pure W.
- Contributes to overall low DBTT and high strength.

### Risks and Limitations
- Large bubbles in as-sintered state are less effective; HERF is needed to fragment and refine them.
- If sintering temperature is below ~2100 °C, bubbles may contain potassium aluminosilicate instead of pure K, which may alter bubble stability.

### Original Evidence
- Section 4.1, Fig. 6 and Fig. 7.
- Section 1: “the formed K bubbles can serve as defect traps…”

## Dislocation strengthening / work hardening with high dislocation density

### Related Elements
W (deformed by HERF), K bubbles (indirect effect)

### Typical Alloys
HERF-AKS-W, HERF pure W, HERF W-Y2O3

### Key Compositions
K: 83 ppm; W: balance

### Microstructural Changes
- HERF increases dislocation density in W matrix.
- Increased density of edge or mixed-type dislocations with higher mobility.
- Polygonisation of dislocations leads to formation of polygonised dislocation walls (PDWs), which transform into partially transformed boundaries (PTBs) and finally into grain boundaries.

### Property Improvements
- Contributes to high UTS and hardness.
- Contrary to basic work hardening theory, reported W alloys exhibit improved ductility despite high dislocation density, attributed to increased edge/mixed dislocation density.

### Risks and Limitations
- The paper explains that high dislocation density typically decreases ductility in BCC metals, but in practice W-based metals show improved ductility because of the specific character of dislocations introduced.

### Original Evidence
- Section 4.2.

## Texture engineering (<111> and <100> preferred orientation)

# Key Conclusions

1. A high-performance K-doped W disk (HERF-AKS-W) was successfully fabricated by introducing K dopant into W followed by two-step HERF.
2. During HERF, large K bubbles (149 nm) in the as-sintered ingot fragmented into smaller bubbles (53 nm) with higher number density, creating a more compact arrangement.
3. The resulting microstructure features laminated grains with refined width (~1.54 μm), elongated morphology (aspect ratio ~5.53:1), <111> and <100> preferred orientations, high fraction of LAGBs, increased dislocation density, and near-full densification (99.6% relative density).
4. HERF-AKS-W exhibits exceptional combined strength and ductility:
   - UTS at RT: 1393 MPa;
   - TE at 100 °C: 19.62%;
   - TE at 200 °C: 33.12%;
   - DBTT between RT and 50 °C.
5. The 33.12% elongation at 200 °C is reported as the highest elongation observed in W-based alloys at this temperature.
6. The improved properties result from synergistic strengthening and ductilizing effects between K bubbles and HERF, including grain boundary strengthening, grain refinement, LAGB enhancement, texture evolution, increased density of mobile dislocations, and K bubble dispersion strengthening.
7. The work demonstrates a promising strategy for designing refractory metals with excellent strength and low-temperature ductility for plasma-facing material applications.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- The paper focuses on one composition (K-doped W with K ~83 ppm) and one processing route (two-step HERF); general applicability to other W alloy compositions or other refractory metal systems is suggested but not experimentally demonstrated.
- No post-forging heat treatment or recrystallization behavior is reported; long-term microstructural stability of the refined grains and bubbles remains unclear.
- No oxidation resistance data are reported; the paper discusses thermal conductivity and irradiation resistance considerations but not oxidative degradation.
- No creep, fatigue, or fracture toughness data are reported.
- The DBTT criterion is defined as 5% elongation, which may not be directly comparable to definitions used elsewhere.
- The mechanical tests were performed on miniature dog-bone specimens (cross-section 1.2 × 0.75 mm², gauge length 5 mm), and size effects on measured properties are not discussed.
- The paper states that bubbles after sintering at 2045 °C may largely contain potassium aluminium silicate rather than pure K, which may influence high-temperature bubble behavior; this point is not explored in detail.
- The mechanisms proposed for improved ductility (e.g., increased edge/mixed dislocation density, LAGB-induced strain compatibility) are based on microstructural observations and references, but direct evidence such as TEM dislocation character analysis is not presented in this paper.
- The performance claims at temperatures >1700 °C are based on literature and qualitative arguments, not direct experiments in this paper.
