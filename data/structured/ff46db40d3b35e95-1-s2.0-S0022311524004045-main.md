# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title**: Mechanical property and strengthening mechanism of ZrC nanoparticle dispersion-strengthened Mo containing FeCrAl alloys
- **Authors**: Yunxia Gao, Jiashuai Tang, Hui Wang, Rui Liu, Xia Huang, Qianfeng Fang, Xianping Wang
- **Journal**: Journal of Nuclear Materials
- **Year**: 2024 (Volume 600, Article 155302; available online 23 July 2024)
- **DOI**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnucmat.2024.155302
- **Alloy system studied**: FeCrAl-based ferritic alloy with Mo solid-solution addition and ZrC nanoparticle dispersion
- **Material category**: Oxide/dispersion-strengthened FeCrAl ferritic alloy (ZrC nanoparticle dispersion-strengthened, Mo solid-solution strengthened), prepared by powder metallurgy (high-energy ball milling + spark plasma sintering + hot forging); not a refractory high-entropy alloy, not additively manufactured.
- **Research objective**: To improve the high-temperature mechanical strength (especially at 800 °C) of FeCrAl accident-tolerant fuel (ATF) cladding alloys by combining Mo solid-solution strengthening with ZrC nanoparticle dispersion strengthening, and to clarify the strengthening mechanisms.

---

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy family**: FeCrAl ferritic alloys for accident-tolerant fuel (ATF) cladding in light water reactors (LWRs).
- **Base alloy**: Fe-13Cr-5Al (wt.%).
- **Strengthening strategy studied**: Simultaneous addition of Mo (solid-solution strengthener) and nanosized ZrC particles (dispersion/precipitation strengthener).
- **Note**: Although the paper is published in a nuclear materials journal and involves FeCrAl alloys rather than refractory high-entropy alloys or Nb/W/Mo-based refractory alloys, the ZrC dispersion strengthening and Mo solid-solution strengthening mechanisms discussed are relevant to refractory alloy design principles. The paper is included here because ZrC is a refractory carbide and Mo is a refractory metal.

# Alloy Composition

Three alloy samples were studied. All compositions are nominal, given in weight percent (wt.%).

| Sample ID (used in paper) | Cr (wt.%) | Al (wt.%) | Mo (wt.%) | ZrC (wt.%) | Fe (wt.%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FeCrAl (raw) | 13 | 5 | 0 | 0 | Bal. |
| FeCrAl-ZrC | 13 | 5 | 0 | 1 | Bal. |
| FeCrAlMo-ZrC | 13 | 5 | 2 | 1 | Bal. |

- **Alloying elements present**: Fe, Cr, Al, Mo, Zr, C.
- **Starting powders**:
  - Mo powder: 2 μm, 99.95% purity.
  - ZrC powder: 50 nm, 99% purity.
  - Fe-13Cr-5Al powder: 33 μm, 99% purity, self-prepared by gas atomization method.
- **Impurity elements**: The original paper does not report ppm-level C, O, N, or H content in the final alloys. However, TEM elemental mapping detected Al and O signals in the nanoparticles, indicating the presence of some alumina particles (Al₂O₃) in addition to ZrC. The paper states this alumina formation is “unwished” because it consumes Al from solid solution.
- **ZrC content in FeCrAlMo-ZrC alloy**: 1 wt.% ZrC nominal.
- **Mo content in FeCrAlMo-ZrC alloy**: 2 wt.% Mo nominal.

# Processing and Heat treatment

## Powder preparation and consolidation

1. **High-energy ball milling (mechanical alloying)**:
   - Mixed powders of Mo, ZrC, and Fe-13Cr-5Al were mechanically alloyed.
   - Planetary ball mill.
   - Milling time: up to 24 h.
   - Rotation rate: 320 rpm.
   - Note: The Discussion section mentions ball-milling conditions of “300 rpm for 20 h,” which appears to be an inconsistency or refers to a slightly different condition; the Experimental section states 320 rpm and up to 24 h. The original text contains both values.

2. **Spark plasma sintering (SPS)**:
   - Milled powders loaded into a cylindrical graphite die with inner diameter of 30 mm.
   - Atmosphere: flowing high-purity argon.
   - Heating rate: 100 °C/min.
   - Sintering temperature: 1150 °C.
   - Holding time: 5 min.
   - Uniaxial pressure: 50 MPa.
   - Cooling rate: 100 °C/min to room temperature.

3. **Hot forging**:
   - Temperature: 1100 °C.
   - Thickness reduction: ~60%.
   - Purpose: achieve higher density.
   - Resulting relative density: above 99.5% for all three alloys.

## Post-consolidation annealing (thermal stability study)

- **Annealing temperature**: 1000 °C.
- **Annealing times**: 1 h, 5 h, 20 h.
- **Atmosphere**: high-purity Ar.
- Samples studied: FeCrAlMo-ZrC only.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Matrix phase

- The paper does not explicitly state the matrix crystal structure (BCC vs. FCC). However, FeCrAl alloys are well-established ferritic (BCC) steels. The original paper does not explicitly state “BCC” or “ferritic matrix phase” in the microstructural characterization sections; it is implied by the alloy designation as FeCrAl ferritic alloy used for ATF cladding.

## Grain size

- **FeCrAl (raw)**: average grain size ≈ 2.53 μm. Inhomogeneous grain structure composed of some coarse equiaxed grains and a large number of fine equiaxed grains.
- **FeCrAlMo-ZrC**: average grain size ≈ 0.49 μm (refined significantly, approximately 5× smaller than raw FeCrAl). Similar inhomogeneous structure.
- Reason for inhomogeneity (stated in paper): (1) size inhomogeneity of raw FeCrAl powder from ultrasonic atomization; (2) ball-milling conditions insufficient for homogeneous grain size distribution.
- After annealing at 1000 °C for 1 h and 20 h: no significant grain growth observed by EBSD, indicating excellent grain-size stability.

## Second-phase particles / precipitates

- **ZrC nanoparticles**:
  - Morphology: near-spherical.
  - Size distribution: 5–30 nm.
  - Density: 1.2 × 10¹⁵ m⁻².
  - Distribution: homogeneously dispersed both at grain boundaries and inside grains.
  - Pinning effect on grain boundaries prevents grain coarsening during high-temperature sintering (Zener pinning mechanism).
  - Pinning effect on dislocations during deformation.
- **Alumina (Al₂O₃) particles**:
  - Detected by HAADF elemental mapping (signals from Zr, C, Al, O).
  - Formed in small quantity.
  - Considered undesirable because it consumes Al from solid solution, potentially degrading steam oxidation resistance.
- **Mo distribution**:
  - Homogeneously dissolved into the FeCrAlMo-ZrC alloy matrix.
  - No Mo aggregation observed after ball milling, confirmed by HAADF elemental mapping.
- **Dislocations**:
  - High-density dislocation tangles observed in some grains (TEM).
  - Nanoparticles pin dislocations during deformation.

## Elements in second phases

- ZrC: Zr and C.
- Alumina: Al and O.
- Mo: in solid solution, not in second phases.

## Strengthening mechanisms discussed in the paper

The paper explicitly discusses:
1. **Solid-solution strengthening** (Mo).
2. **Precipitation/dispersion strengthening** (ZrC nanoparticles).
3. **Grain-boundary strengthening / fine-grain strengthening** (Hall–Petch, promoted by synergistic effects of Mo and ZrC).
4. **Zener pinning** of grain boundaries by nanoparticles.
5. **Grain refinement** promoted by Mo (reduces grain growth rate) and ZrC (pins grain boundaries).
6. Not explicitly discussed: ODS strengthening via Y₂O₃ or La₂O₃ (only mentioned as an alternative/comparison in the Introduction); no borides or silicides.

# Mechanical Properties

## Tensile properties at room temperature (RT)

| Alloy | UTS (MPa) | Total Elongation (%) |
|---|---|---|
| FeCrAl | 757 | 20.3 |
| FeCrAl-ZrC | 844 | 16.9 |
| FeCrAlMo-ZrC | 935 | 14.8 |

- FeCrAlMo-ZrC UTS at RT is 24% higher than FeCrAl, 11% higher than FeCrAl-ZrC.

## Tensile properties at 400 °C

| Alloy | UTS (MPa) | Total Elongation (%) |
|---|---|---|
| FeCrAl | 590 | 19.2 |
| FeCrAl-ZrC | 738 | 16.6 |
| FeCrAlMo-ZrC | 880 | 14.2 |

## Tensile properties at 800 °C

| Alloy | UTS (MPa) | Total Elongation (%) |
|---|---|---|
| FeCrAl | 55 | 68.5 |
| FeCrAl-ZrC | 89 | 35.5 |
| FeCrAlMo-ZrC | 124 | 25.3 |

- FeCrAlMo-ZrC UTS at 800 °C is 125% higher than FeCrAl (55 MPa) and 34.7% higher than FeCrAl-ZrC (89 MPa).
- The improvement is more significant at higher temperature.

## Testing conditions

- Machine: Instron-5967.
- Strain rate: 1 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.
- Test temperatures: RT, 400 °C, 800 °C.
- Sample cross-section: 1.5 × 1.0 mm².
- Gage length: 5 mm.
- Number of samples per condition: 5.
- Holding time at high temperature before testing: at least 20 min to ensure homogeneous temperature.

## Ductility observations

- TE of all three alloys at RT and 400 °C is almost constant (i.e., temperature has little influence on ductility below 500 °C).
- At 800 °C, TE increases significantly: FeCrAl TE = 68.5%; FeCrAl-ZrC TE = 35.5%; FeCrAlMo-ZrC TE = 25.3%.
- FeCrAlMo-ZrC has lower ductility at 800 °C than the other two alloys, attributed to Mo + ZrC combined influence on plastic deformation.
- Fracture mode at 800 °C: ductile dimple fracture for all samples; FeCrAl dimples are larger and deeper; FeCrAlMo-ZrC shows some cleavage facets on fracture surfaces, indicating lower ductility.

## Hardness (Vickers microhardness, at RT)

| Alloy | Hardness (HV) |
|---|---|
| FeCrAl | ~271.5 HV (calculated from 12.8% difference) |
| FeCrAl-ZrC | ~295.4 HV (calculated from 3.7% difference) |
| FeCrAlMo-ZrC | 306.3 HV |

- FeCrAlMo-ZrC hardness is 12.8% higher than FeCrAl and 3.7% higher than FeCrAl-ZrC.
- The paper notes that ZrC nanoparticles may play a major role in hardness improvement compared with Mo, because of the higher hardness of ZrC nanoparticles.
- Tester: Huayin 310HVs Vickers microhardness tester.
- Load: 200 g.
- Dwell time: 15 s.
- At least 5 indentations per sample.

## Thermal stability after annealing at 1000 °C (FeCrAlMo-ZrC only)

- Mechanical properties measured at 400 °C after annealing:

| Annealing time at 1000 °C | UTS (MPa) | TE (%) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 h (as-prepared) | 880 | 14.2 |
| 1 h | 872 | — |
| 5 h | ~872 (remains constant) | 15.9 |
| 20 h | ~872 (remains constant) | ~15.9 (remains stable) |

- After 1 h annealing, UTS decreases slightly from 880 MPa to 872 MPa; after 5 h and 20 h, UTS remains constant.
- TE increases from 14.2% to 15.9% after 5 h, then remains stable.

- Hardness at RT after annealing:

| Annealing time at 1000 °C | Hardness (HV) |
|---|---|
| 0 h | 306.3 |
| 1 h | 320.8 |
| 5 h | ~320.8 (stable) |
| 20 h | ~320.8 (stable) |

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

- The paper focuses on mechanical properties and does not present dedicated oxidation resistance experiments (no oxidation weight gain, no oxide layer thickness, no oxidation temperature/duration data).
- However, the following oxidation-related information is extracted:
  - FeCrAl alloys in general have excellent high-temperature steam oxidation resistance (Introduction, citing references).
  - FeCrAl oxidation kinetics are two orders of magnitude below Zr alloys at 1500 °C (citing Pint et al.).
  - **Concern raised**: The formation of small Al₂O₃ particles in FeCrAlMo-ZrC consumes Al from solid solution, which could degrade steam oxidation resistance. This is explicitly stated as “unwished” in the paper.
- No high-temperature embrittlement, oxygen embrittlement, nitrogen embrittlement, or grain-boundary oxidation data are reported in this paper (these are not the focus).
- Service stability: Thermal stability at 1000 °C for up to 20 h was evaluated via tensile properties, hardness, and EBSD; FeCrAlMo-ZrC showed superior thermal stability (no significant grain growth, stable strength and hardness).

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## ZrC

- **Applicable Alloy System**: FeCrAl ferritic alloys (Fe-13Cr-5Al based), prepared by powder metallurgy (high-energy ball milling + SPS + hot forging).
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 1 wt.% ZrC nanoparticles (starting particle size 50 nm; final dispersed particle size 5–30 nm; density 1.2 × 10¹⁵ m⁻²).
- **Main Function**: Precipitation/dispersion strengthening; grain-boundary pinning (grain refinement); dislocation pinning; high-temperature strength improvement.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**:
  - ZrC nanoparticles homogeneously dispersed at grain boundaries and inside grains.
  - Pinning of grain boundaries prevents grain coarsening during high-temperature sintering (Zener pinning).
  - Pinning of dislocations during deformation hinders dislocation movement.
  - High melting point (3540 °C), high hardness, low thermal neutron absorption cross section of Zr.
- **Property Effect**:
  - FeCrAl-ZrC (1 wt.% ZrC) vs. FeCrAl: UTS at RT 844 MPa vs. 757 MPa (+11.5%); UTS at 800 °C 89 MPa vs. 55 MPa (+61.8%).
  - Hardness: FeCrAl-ZrC ~295.4 HV vs. FeCrAl ~271.5 HV.
  - The paper notes ZrC is likely the major contributor to hardness increase compared with Mo.
- **Potential Side Effects**:
  - Reduced total elongation: FeCrAl-ZrC TE = 16.9% at RT (vs. 20.3% for FeCrAl) and 35.5% at 800 °C (vs. 68.5% for FeCrAl).
  - Pinning effect on dislocations may cause stress concentration and deteriorate ductility.
  - Formation of some alumina (Al₂O₃) particles alongside ZrC consumes Al from solid solution, potentially degrading steam oxidation resistance.
- **Original Evidence**: Table 1; Fig. 5; Fig. 6; Table 2; Fig. 3; text in Sections 3.1, 3.2, and Discussion.

## Mo

- **Applicable Alloy System**: FeCrAl ferritic alloys (Fe-13Cr-5Al based), prepared by powder metallurgy.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 2 wt.% Mo (Fe-13Cr-5Al-2Mo-1ZrC).
- **Main Function**: Solid-solution strengthening; reduction of grain growth rate (indirect grain refinement); high-temperature strength improvement.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**:
  - Mo homogeneously dissolved into FeCrAlMo-ZrC matrix (no aggregation observed by HAADF).
  - Mo causes lattice distortion and produces an elastic stress field that hinders dislocation movement (solid-solution strengthening).
  - Mo reduces grain growth rate at a certain temperature, contributing to fine-grained microstructure.
- **Property Effect**:
  - FeCrAlMo-ZrC vs. FeCrAl-ZrC: UTS at RT 935 MPa vs. 844 MPa (+10.8%); UTS at 400 °C 880 MPa vs. 738 MPa (+19.2%); UTS at 800 °C 124 MPa vs. 89 MPa (+39.3%).
  - FeCrAlMo-ZrC vs. FeCrAl: UTS at RT 935 MPa vs. 757 MPa (+23.5%); UTS at 800 °C 124 MPa vs. 55 MPa (+125.5%).
- **Potential Side Effects**:
  - Reduced total elongation at 800 °C: 25.3% for FeCrAlMo-ZrC vs. 35.5% for FeCrAl-ZrC and 68.5% for FeCrAl.
  - Combined with ZrC, influences plastic deformation and leads to some cleavage on fracture surface.
- **Original Evidence**: Table 2; Fig. 1; Fig. 2; Fig. 6; Discussion section; reference [23] for Mo effect on grain growth rate.

## Mo + ZrC (synergistic combination)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: FeCrAl ferritic alloys (Fe-13Cr-5Al-2Mo-1ZrC).
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 2 wt.% Mo + 1 wt.% ZrC.
- **Main Function**: Combined solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, and fine-grain strengthening; maximizes high-temperature strength.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**:
  - Synergistic effect of Mo (reduces grain growth rate) and ZrC (Zener pinning of grain boundaries) promotes grain refinement: average grain size reduced from 2.53 μm (FeCrAl) to 0.49 μm (FeCrAlMo-ZrC).
  - Fine-grain strengthening described by Hall–Petch relationship.
  - ZrC particles pin dislocations inside grains; Mo creates lattice distortion.
- **Property Effect**:
  - FeCrAlMo-ZrC has highest UTS at all tested temperatures: 935 MPa (RT), 880 MPa (400 °C), 124 MPa (800 °C).
  - Hardness: 306.3 HV, highest among the three alloys.
  - Superior thermal stability: after annealing at 1000 °C for 20 h, UTS remains ~872 MPa and hardness ~320.8 HV (at RT); no significant grain growth.
- **Potential Side Effects**:
  - Reduced ductility at 800 °C (TE = 25.3%) compared with FeCrAl (68.5%) and FeCrAl-ZrC (35.5%).
  - Inhomogeneous grain structure persists due to powder size inhomogeneity and insufficient milling.
- **Original Evidence**: Figs. 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8; Tables 2; Discussion; Conclusions.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening (Mo)

- **Related Elements**: Mo.
- **Typical Alloys**: Fe-13Cr-5Al-2Mo-1ZrC; FeCrAlMo alloys; referenced Fe-Cr-Al-Mo-Nb alloys.
- **Key Compositions**: 2 wt.% Mo in this study.
- **Microstructural Changes**: Mo homogeneously dissolved in matrix; no aggregation; lattice distortion; elastic stress field.
- **Property Improvements**: UTS at 800 °C increased from 89 MPa (FeCrAl-ZrC) to 124 MPa (FeCrAlMo-ZrC); UTS at RT from 844 MPa to 935 MPa.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Reduced ductility; the original paper does not quantify the individual contribution of Mo vs. ZrC to total strengthening.

## Carbide Precipitation / Dispersion Strengthening (ZrC)

- **Related Elements**: Zr, C.
- **Typical Alloys**: Fe-13Cr-5Al-1ZrC; Fe-13Cr-5Al-2Mo-1ZrC.
- **Key Compositions**: 1 wt.% ZrC nanoparticles (50 nm starting size; dispersed size 5–30 nm).
- **Microstructural Changes**: Near-spherical ZrC nanoparticles dispersed at grain boundaries and inside grains; high density 1.2 × 10¹⁵ m⁻²; dislocation pinning; grain-boundary pinning.
- **Property Improvements**: UTS at 800 °C increased from 55 MPa (FeCrAl) to 89 MPa (FeCrAl-ZrC); UTS at RT from 757 MPa to 844 MPa.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Reduced ductility; some undesirable alumina formation consumes Al; particle pinning can cause stress concentration and deteriorate ductility.

## Grain-Boundary Strengthening / Fine-Grain Strengthening (Hall–Petch)

- **Related Elements**: Mo + ZrC (synergistic).
- **Typical Alloys**: Fe-13Cr-5Al-2Mo-1ZrC.
- **Key Compositions**: 2 wt.% Mo + 1 wt.% ZrC.
- **Microstructural Changes**: Grain size refined from 2.53 μm to 0.49 μm; nanocomposite particles pin grain boundaries (Zener pinning); Mo reduces grain growth rate.
- **Property Improvements**: Overall UTS and hardness increase; superior grain-size stability after 20 h at 1000 °C.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Inhomogeneous grain structure remains due to raw powder size inhomogeneity and milling conditions.

## Oxide Dispersion Strengthening (Y₂O₃ / Al₂O₃ comparison)

- **Related Elements**: Y, Al, O (for Y-Al oxide ODS); Al, O (for alumina).
- **Typical Alloys**: ODS-FeCrAl with Y₂O₃; FeCrAl with ZrC in this study.
- **Key Compositions**: 1 wt.% ZrC (this study); Y₂O₃ additions in referenced ODS-FeCrAl literature.
- **Microstructural Changes**: This study detected alumina particles (Al₂O₃) alongside ZrC; referenced work notes coarser Y-Al oxide particles in ODS-FeCrAl with Y₂O₃ can be detrimental to strength.
- **Property Improvements**: ZrC dispersion provides stronger precipitation strengthening than coarse Fe₂Nb Laves phases (200 nm) due to smaller size (5–30 nm) and higher density.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Alumina formation consumes Al, degrading steam oxidation resistance; coarse oxide particles in ODS alloys reduce strengthening effectiveness. The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for alumina volume fraction.

## Precipitation Strengthening Comparison: ZrC vs. Fe₂Nb Laves Phase

- **Related Elements**: Zr, C vs. Nb.
- **Typical Alloys**: Fe-13Cr-5Al-2Mo-1ZrC vs. Fe-13Cr-4.5Al-2Mo-1.5Nb.
- **Key Compositions**: 1 wt.% ZrC (5–30 nm particles) vs. Fe₂Nb Laves phase (~200 nm particles).
- **Microstructural Changes**: Smaller, higher-density ZrC particles produce better precipitation strengthening than coarser Fe₂Nb Laves phases.
- **Property Improvements**: FeCrAlMo-ZrC UTS at RT 935 MPa vs. Fe-13Cr-4.5Al-2Mo-1.5Nb UTS 692 MPa at RT; and 124 MPa vs. 95 MPa at 800 °C.
- **Risks and Limitations**: Original paper does not discuss the full property trade-off of Nb addition (e.g., neutron penalty of Nb) in detail; only mechanical comparison is provided.

# Key Conclusions

1. FeCrAlMo-ZrC alloy (Fe-13Cr-5Al-2Mo-1ZrC, wt.%) exhibits the highest ultimate tensile strength and acceptable ductility at RT, 400 °C, and 800 °C among the three studied alloys.
2. At 800 °C, the UTS of FeCrAlMo-ZrC is 124 MPa, which is 125% higher than raw FeCrAl (55 MPa) and 34.7% higher than FeCrAl-ZrC (89 MPa).
3. FeCrAlMo-ZrC has the highest hardness (306.3 HV), 12.8% higher than FeCrAl and 3.7% higher than FeCrAl-ZrC.
4. FeCrAlMo-ZrC maintains high strength and favorable ductility after annealing at 1000 °C for 20 h, demonstrating superior thermal stability.
5. Strengthening mechanisms identified:
   - Solid-solution strengthening by Mo.
   - Precipitation/dispersion strengthening by nanosized ZrC particles.
   - Fine-grain strengthening promoted by synergistic effects of Mo (reduces grain growth rate) and ZrC (Zener pinning of grain boundaries).
6. ZrC nanoparticles (5–30 nm, density 1.2 × 10¹⁵ m⁻²) pin dislocations and grain boundaries, leading to higher strength.
7. Mo is homogeneously dissolved in the matrix and causes lattice distortion, contributing to solid-solution strengthening.
8. The paper concludes that below ~500 °C, temperature has little effect on ductility of FeCrAl-based alloys; at 800 °C, ductility increases significantly, possibly due to dissolution of small amounts of second phases and enhanced dislocation activity.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **This paper is not about Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloys.** It concerns FeCrAl ferritic steel for nuclear cladding applications. It is included as a knowledge source for ZrC dispersion strengthening and Mo solid-solution strengthening principles that may be transferable to refractory alloy design.
- **Alloy system limitation**: The matrix is Fe-Cr-Al ferritic steel, which has a much lower melting point and different phase stability than refractory metal alloys. Direct transfer of numerical strengthening values to Nb/W/Mo-based alloys is not appropriate.
- **No oxidation experiments performed**: The paper does not report oxidation weight gain or oxide layer characterization. Oxidation resistance is only discussed in the Introduction citing other work and as a concern regarding alumina formation.
- **No creep or long-term high-temperature mechanical data**: The thermal stability study is limited to annealing at 1000 °C for up to 20 h followed by tensile/hardness testing at RT or 400 °C; no in-situ high-temperature creep tests are reported.
- **Inconsistency in processing details**: The Experimental section states ball milling at 320 rpm for up to 24 h, while the Discussion section mentions 300 rpm for 20 h. This discrepancy is in the original text and should be noted.
- **Grain structure inhomogeneity**: The authors acknowledge that the grain size distribution is inhomogeneous due to raw powder size variability and insufficient milling, which may affect the generalizability of the Hall–Petch analysis.
- **No intermediate annealing temperature studied**: Only 1000 °C annealing was used for thermal stability; no data are available for 800 °C or 1200 °C stability.
- **No ppm-level interstitial content reported**: The paper does not provide measured C, O, N, H impurity levels, which could affect mechanical properties and phase formation.
- **The paper does not separately quantify the contribution of each strengthening mechanism**: Solid-solution strengthening, dispersion strengthening, and grain-boundary strengthening are discussed qualitatively; no quantitative separation is provided.
- **The paper does not report absolute density, elastic modulus, fracture toughness, or creep rupture life**: Only relative density and UTS/TE/hardness are given.
- **No specimen orientation/texture analysis reported**: EBSD is used for grain size, but texture information is not discussed.
- **Ductility loss at 800 °C**: The trade-off between higher strength and reduced ductility in FeCrAlMo-ZrC at 800 °C is not fully explained and may be a concern for cladding applications.
- **No additively manufactured refractory alloy content**: This paper does not involve LPBF, SLM, EBM, or DED.
