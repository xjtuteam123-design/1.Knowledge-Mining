# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system studied:** Ti6Al4V (Ti64) matrix reinforced with FeCoNiCrMo high-entropy alloy (HEA), processed by selective laser melting (SLM).
- **Material category:** Additively manufactured titanium alloy / HEA-reinforced titanium matrix composite (heterostructured alloy). Not a refractory alloy system; this is a Ti-based alloy with a non-refractory FeCoNiCrMo HEA addition.
- **Research objective:** To enhance the dynamic mechanical properties (strain-rate hardening, energy dissipation, strength-ductility balance under high strain rate) of Ti6Al4V by SLM fabrication of a heterostructured Ti64–FeCoNiCrMo alloy, specifically investigating dynamic compressive behavior under high strain rates (1500–3500 s⁻¹) and temperatures (20–300 °C).
- **Motivation:** Conventional Ti64 exhibits minimal strain-rate hardening and is susceptible to adiabatic shear banding (ASB) under dynamic loading, limiting its use in impact, blast, or ballistic conditions.

# Alloy Composition

- **Base alloy:** Ti6Al4V (Ti64), gas-atomized spherical powder, particle size 15–53 µm, purchased from Ningbo Zhongyuan Co., Ltd.
- **Reinforcement:** FeCoNiCrMo high-entropy alloy powder, equiatomic (FeCoNiCrMo composition implied by the name; specific at.% of each element not explicitly stated in this paper), particles size 15–53 µm, produced by Ningbo Zhongyuan Co., Ltd.
- **Composite composition:** Ti64 + 3 wt% FeCoNiCrMo HEA (mixed powder).
- **Powder mixing:** Ti64 and 3 wt% HEA powders mixed for 16 h at low speed (70 rpm) in a three-dimensional powder mixer.
- **Impurities (C, O, N, H, etc.):** Not explicitly stated in the original paper.
- **W, Mo, Nb, Ta, Zr, Hf, Ti, V, Cr, Al, Si, Re, C, B, Y, La, O:** Mo and Cr are present as constituents of the FeCoNiCrMo HEA. Ti, Al, V are from Ti64. No W, Nb, Ta, Zr, Hf, Si, Re, C, B, Y, La, or O contents are explicitly reported.

- **Powder feedstock:** Spherical Ti64 powder (gas atomized, 15–53 µm) blended with FeCoNiCrMo HEA powder (15–53 µm) at 3 wt% HEA.
- **Mixing:** 16 h at 70 rpm in a three-dimensional powder mixer.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phases (as printed / as mixed):**
  - Ti64 powder: α/α′-phase peaks (hexagonal close-packed, HCP) observed via XRD.
  - FeCoNiCrMo HEA powder: FCC-phase peaks observed via XRD.
  - Mixed Ti64-3.0 wt% FeCoNiCrMo powder: peaks from both α/α′-phase and FCC-phase, with Ti64 peaks more prominent (Ti64 is the dominant phase).
- **Post-dynamic-loading phases:**
  - β-phase is present and is stabilized by FeCoNiCrMo (HEA acts as β-stabilizer).
  - Martensitic transformation β → α′ occurs during dynamic loading.
  - At 300 °C and 3571 s⁻¹: β-phase peaks more pronounced; α′-phase peaks weaker (higher temperature stabilizes β-phase).
  - At 20 °C and 1660 s⁻¹: mixture of β-phase and α′-phase observed.
- **Precipitates:**
  - Under high-temperature, high-strain-rate impact (300 °C, 3571 s⁻¹), numerous precipitated phases appear (referred to as “HEA precipitates” in Fig. 4g–i) that are not detectable by XRD due to low concentrations. The exact chemical identity/crystal structure of these precipitates is not explicitly identified.
- **Grain size:**
  - Refined grain size of 2–5 µm observed in impacted microstructures (Fig. 4f).
- **Grain boundary features:** Grain boundaries observed in refined microstructure (2–5 µm grains).
- **Deformation microstructures (post-impact, TEM):**
  - Deformation twins and mechanical twins in α-Ti (Fig. 4b, c).
  - Dislocation tangles (Fig. 4e).
  - Microcracks (Fig. 4d).
  - α/β phase boundaries play a significant role in deformation.
  - Heterogeneous deformation: some severely deformed regions coexist with minimally damaged regions.
- **Elemental segregation:** Not explicitly discussed.
- **Effects of alloying on phase:** FeCoNiCrMo serves as a β-stabilizer in titanium alloys under dynamic loading conditions.
- **Mechanisms discussed in paper:**
  - β → α′ martensitic transformation.
  - Grain refinement (2–5 µm).
  - Dislocation entanglement / tangling.
  - Strain-rate hardening (dislocation motion hindered at higher strain rates).
  - Thermal softening (easier dislocation motion at elevated temperatures).
  - Heterostructure-enabled synergistic strain partitioning.
  - Precipitation strengthening under high-temperature/high-strain-rate impact (precipitates “play a role in strengthening the material under higher strain rates and temperatures”).

# Mechanical Properties

All mechanical property data are from Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) dynamic compression tests unless otherwise noted.

- **Testing method:** Split Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) for dynamic compression; quasi-static tests also conducted via Hopkinson setup at strain rate of 2 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.
- **Strain rates tested (dynamic):** 1500 s⁻¹, 2500 s⁻¹, 3500 s⁻¹ (also individual tests at 1660 s⁻¹, 2069 s⁻¹, 3263 s⁻¹, 3571 s⁻¹).
- **Temperatures tested:** 20 °C, 150 °C, 300 °C.
- **Peak stress:**
  - At 20 °C and 3500 s⁻¹: peak stress approximately 2100 MPa (with 18–22% increase in peak stress at higher strain rates, attributed to dislocation entanglement). The paper states: “At 20 °C, peak stress increases by 18–22% (~2100 MPa) due to dislocation entanglement under high strain rates.”
  - At 150 °C and 300 °C: flow stress decreases by 25–30% due to thermal softening (compared to 20 °C).
- **Quasi-static compressive behavior (2 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹):**
  - At 20 °C: strain hardening (strength increases with strain).
  - At 150 °C: early softening (thermal softening starts to reduce strength).
  - At 300 °C: extensive plastic flow, thermal softening dominant.
- **Strain-rate hardening:** Peak stress increases with increasing strain rate at 20 °C; still present but reduced at 150 °C and 300 °C.
- **Thermal softening:** At 300 °C, substantial drop in peak stress and increased ductility.
- **Previous quasi-static data (from Ref. [3], cited in this paper):** SLM-processed Ti64/FeCoNiCrMo heterostructured alloy with tensile strength 1366 MPa and ductility 8.7%. (This is from the authors' prior work, not newly measured in this paper.)
- **Deformation observations:**
  - At 20 °C and up to 2069 s⁻¹: samples maintain cylindrical shape (uniform stress distribution).
  - At strain rates exceeding 3263 s⁻¹: localized flattening of samples.
  - At 300 °C: homogeneous deformation characterized by plastic upsetting.
  - Critical strain-rate threshold for uniform deformation: ~3263 s⁻¹.
- **Hardness:** Not reported.
- **Fracture toughness:** Not reported.
- **Creep performance / rupture life:** Not reported.
- **Elastic modulus:** Not reported.
- **Density:** Not reported.
- **Loading mode:** Dynamic compression (SHPB); quasi-static compression.
- **Atmosphere:** Not explicitly stated for mechanical tests (room temperature tests presumably in air; elevated temperature tests at 150 °C and 300 °C, atmosphere not specified).

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## FeCoNiCrMo (High-Entropy Alloy Addition)

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Ti6Al4V (Ti64) matrix composite fabricated by selective laser melting.
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 3 wt% FeCoNiCrMo HEA powder blended with Ti64 powder. Equiatomic FeCoNiCrMo composition implied; exact elemental at.% of Fe, Co, Ni, Cr, Mo not given in this paper.
- **Main Function:** Serves as a β-phase stabilizer in titanium alloys; promotes heterogeneous microstructure; enhances dynamic mechanical properties (strain-rate hardening, energy dissipation); improves strength-ductility balance under dynamic loading.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:**
  - Stabilizes the β-phase (BCC) under dynamic loading, as evidenced by XRD (β-phase more pronounced at 300 °C; β and α′ mixture at 20 °C).
  - Under high-strain-rate/high-temperature impact (300 °C, 3571 s⁻¹), forms “HEA precipitates” not detectable by XRD due to low concentrations. These precipitates contribute to strengthening.
  - Promotes heterostructure formation with spatial variations in deformation behavior (deformation twins, dislocation tangles, phase-dependent strain accommodation).
  - Enables β → α′ martensitic transformation during dynamic loading.
- **Property Effect:**
  - Peak stress at 20 °C and 3500 s⁻¹: ~2100 MPa.
  - Peak stress increases by 18–22% at 20 °C with increased strain rate.
  - Flow stress decreases by 25–30% at 150–300 °C due to thermal softening.
  - Prior work (Ref. [3]) reported tensile strength 1366 MPa and ductility 8.7% for SLM Ti64/FeCoNiCrMo.
- **Potential Side Effects:**
  - Not explicitly discussed in this paper. However, heterogeneous microstructure leads to non-uniform deformation under impact; localized flattening at high strain rates (>3263 s⁻¹); microcracks observed post-impact.
- **Original Evidence:**
  - “It is concluded that, FeCoNiCrMo serves as a β-stabilizer in titanium alloys under dynamic loading conditions.” (Section 3, Results and Discussion)
  - “At 300 °C and 3571 s⁻¹, the β-phase peaks are more pronounced, while the α′-phase peaks become weaker, indicating that the higher temperature stabilizes the β-phase.” (Section 3)
  - Fig. 4g–i: “High-temperature (300 °C) impact specimen showing HEA precipitates and dislocations.”
  - “These precipitates play a role in strengthening the material under higher strain rates and temperatures.”

**Note:** The paper does not provide element-specific knowledge cards for Mo, Cr, Fe, Co, or Ni individually. The HEA is treated as a combined addition. No individual element-function relationships for Mo or Cr are explicitly discussed.

# Key Conclusions

1. **FeCoNiCrMo HEA as β-stabilizer:** FeCoNiCrMo serves as a β-phase stabilizer in the Ti64 matrix under dynamic loading conditions, confirmed by XRD showing β-phase stabilization at 300 °C and β/α′ coexistence at 20 °C.
2. **Strain-rate hardening at 20 °C:** The alloy exhibits strain-rate hardening, with peak stress increasing by 18–22% and reaching approximately 2100 MPa at 20 °C and 3500 s⁻¹, attributed to dislocation entanglement at high strain rates.
3. **Thermal softening at elevated temperature:** At 150–300 °C, flow stress decreases by 25–30% due to thermal softening, with increased ductility and more homogeneous plastic flow (plastic upsetting) at 300 °C.
4. **Phase transformation:** β → α′ martensitic transformation occurs during dynamic loading; at higher temperature (300 °C), β-phase is stabilized and α′ peaks weaken.
5. **Microstructural refinement and deformation features:** Post-impact microstructure shows refined grains of 2–5 µm, mechanical twins in α-Ti, dislocation tangles, microcracks, and heterogeneous deformation.
6. **Precipitate formation under severe conditions:** At 300 °C and 3571 s⁻¹, numerous HEA precipitates form (not detectable by XRD) and contribute to strengthening.
7. **Heterostructure benefits:** The heterostructured design enables synergistic strain partitioning and reduces strength-ductility trade-offs; deformation transitions from strain hardening at 20 °C to uniform plasticity at 300 °C.
8. **Critical strain-rate threshold:** Below ~3263 s⁻¹, samples retain cylindrical shape; above this threshold, localized flattening occurs due to insufficient energy for homogeneous deformation and inherent microstructural inhomogeneity.
9. **Application potential:** The alloy offers improved impact resistance for aerospace and marine applications under thermomechanical loading conditions.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Material system scope:** This is a Ti6Al4V-based alloy with a non-refractory FeCoNiCrMo HEA addition. It is not a refractory metal alloy (Nb-, Mo-, W-based, or refractory HEA) and should not be interpreted as such.
- **Alloy composition detail:** The exact at.% or wt.% of Fe, Co, Ni, Cr, Mo in the FeCoNiCrMo HEA is not provided in this paper; only the nominal equiatomic designation and the 3 wt% addition level are given.
- **SLM process parameters:** Not reported in this paper; the full fabrication details are in the authors' prior work (Ref. [3], J. Alloy. Compd. 978 (2024) 173435).
- **Microstructural characterization:** The precipitate phase(s) formed under high-temperature impact are not identified by crystal structure or composition; no SEM-EDS, TEM-EDS, or diffraction analysis of precipitates is reported.
- **Mechanical testing scope:** Only dynamic compression (SHPB) and limited quasi-static compression are reported. No tensile data, fracture toughness, fatigue, creep, or hardness data are presented in this paper (tensile properties from prior work are cited).
- **Thermal range:** Tests were performed only up to 300 °C; behavior at higher temperatures is not characterized.
- **Oxidation behavior:** No oxidation or corrosion testing is reported.
- **Atmosphere control:** Test atmosphere is not explicitly stated.
- **Speculation vs. confirmed conclusions:** The statement that lower temperatures and strain rates promote martensitic transformation “likely due to localized stress concentrations overriding bulk stabilization” is presented as a likely explanation, not a confirmed mechanism; it should not be treated as a definitive conclusion.
