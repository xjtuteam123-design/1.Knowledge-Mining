# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title:** A crystal plasticity-based micromechanical model for precipitate shearing: Application to cyclic softening of polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys
- **Authors:** Suketa Chaudhary, Bhargav Sudhalkar, Namit Pai, Mithun Palit, Zafir Alam, R. Sankarasubramanian, Indradev Samajdar, Anirban Patra
- **Journal:** International Journal of Fatigue
- **Year:** 2025 (Volume 190, Article 108582; available online 3 September 2024)
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijfatigue.2024.108582
- **Alloy system studied:** Polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys (PM-processed Ni-based superalloy, referred to as Alloy 1; Nimonic-90, referred to as Alloy 2)
- **Research objective:** To develop and implement a micromechanical constitutive model for precipitate shearing within a dislocation density-based crystal plasticity framework to simulate cyclic softening in polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys, and to validate the model against experimental low cycle fatigue data for two alloys with different initial microstructures.
- **Material category:** Ni-based superalloys (precipitation-strengthened, polycrystalline, powder metallurgy processed); not a refractory high-temperature alloy, Nb-W-based alloy, Mo-W-based alloy, refractory high-entropy alloy, or additively manufactured refractory alloy.

# Alloy System and Research Object

The paper focuses on **polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys**, not refractory high-temperature alloys. The specific alloys studied are:

1. **Alloy 1**: A PM-processed polycrystalline Ni-based superalloy manufactured by Defence Metallurgical Research Laboratory (India). Microstructure consists of primary γ′ₚ precipitates, cuboidal-shaped secondary γ′ₛ and tertiary γ′ₜ precipitates homogenized in the γ phase. Mean grain sizes: γ phase = 27.42 μm; primary γ′ₚ phase = 3.8 μm. Secondary γ′ₛ size: 200–300 nm; tertiary γ′ₜ size: 10–30 nm. Volume fraction of γ′ is nearly 50%.

2. **Alloy 2**: Nimonic-90 superalloy. Microstructure consists of spherical γ′ precipitates in the γ matrix, average precipitate size 25 nm, coherent in the γ matrix with average grain size 300±30 μm. Estimated γ′ volume fraction fγ′ ≈ 0.21 (calculated based on Dyson’s approach).

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

**Alloy 1:**
- Matrix phase: γ (FCC) phase with homogenized secondary γ′ₛ and tertiary γ′ₜ precipitates.
- Primary γ′ₚ precipitates: micron-sized (mean grain size 3.8 μm), treated as a separate phase in simulations.
- Secondary γ′ₛ precipitates: cuboidal-shaped, size 200–300 nm.
- Tertiary γ′ₜ precipitates: size 10–30 nm.
- Grain size of γ phase: 27.42 μm.
- Volume fraction of γ′ is nearly 50%.
- After cyclic deformation at 0.9% strain amplitude to 500 cycles, the size of secondary γ′ₛ precipitates reduced from ≈200–300 nm to ≈91 nm due to shearing.
- TEM-based Precession Electron Diffraction (TEM-PED) revealed dislocation activity in the vicinity of γ′ precipitates (Fig. 4c).
- SEM image analysis of sheared precipitates (Fig. 3b) estimated that roughly 45% of precipitates were sheared (Appendix C).

**Alloy 2:**
- Matrix phase: γ matrix with spherical γ′ precipitates.
- Average precipitate size: 25 nm, coherent in the γ matrix.
- Average grain size: 300±30 μm.
- Estimated γ′ volume fraction fγ′ ≈ 0.21 (also reported as fγ′ = 0.25 in Basoalto et al. [80]).
- No primary γ′ₚ precipitates reported; simulations assumed a homogenized γ matrix with γ′ precipitates (secondary/tertiary not considered separately).

**Precipitate strengthening mechanisms discussed:**
- Strong-pair coupling (for precipitates larger than threshold size d₀ᵢ)
- Weak-pair coupling (for precipitates smaller than threshold size d₀ᵢ)
- Anti-Phase Boundary (APB) energy-dependent strengthening
- For Alloy 1: initial strengthening due to strong-pair coupling, transitioning to weak-pair coupling as precipitate size decreases during cyclic deformation.
- For Alloy 2: weak-pair coupling active from beginning due to small initial precipitate size (25 nm).

**Other strengthening mechanisms considered:**
- Solid-solution strengthening (solute concentrations; coefficients from Roth et al. [75])
- Dislocation strengthening (Taylor hardening)
- Grain size strengthening (Hall–Petch)
- Backstress evolution (Armstrong–Frederick type kinematic hardening)

# Mechanical Properties

The paper focuses on **low cycle fatigue (LCF) behavior** at room temperature.

**Alloy 1 experimental LCF tests (performed in this study):**
- Temperature: room temperature
- Strain amplitudes: 0.9% and 1.8%
- Strain-controlled, fully reversed loading (R = −1)
- Nominal strain rate: 2 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹
- Test machine: MTS-Landmark, 100 kN load cell capacity
- Strain measured with 10 mm extensometer
- Specimen initially loaded in tension during 1st cycle
- Tests not performed till failure (to allow microstructure characterization)
- At 0.9% strain amplitude: peak stress increased from 1st cycle to 30th cycle, then decreased.
- At 1.8% strain amplitude: peak stress increased up to 10th cycle, then decreased.
- Material at 1.8% strain amplitude fails much earlier (around 54 cycles) compared to 0.9% strain amplitude.

**Alloy 1 model predictions:**
- Hysteresis loops predicted for cycles 1, 30, 100 at 0.9% and cycles 1, 10, 45 at 1.8%.
- Model over-predicts elastic–plastic transition for both strain amplitudes.
- Quantitative differences in peak stress predictions ≈5–7%, especially for 1.8% case.
- Predicted mean precipitate size at end of simulation ≈50 nm, qualitatively comparable to TEM-measured sheared precipitate size 91±6 nm.

**Alloy 2 (Nimonic-90) experimental data from Ref. [66]:**
- Incremental step tests with repeated loading blocks of varying strain amplitude.
- Fully reversed constant strain amplitude tests at 1.1%, 0.85%, and 0.6% strain amplitude.
- Room temperature.
- Nominal strain rate: 3 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.
- Peak stress values increase with increase in strain amplitude.

**Alloy 2 model predictions:**
- Predicted cyclic stress–strain response for block 1 and block 80 of incremental step test.
- Model calibrated at 1.1% and 0.6%; validated at 0.85%.
- Peak stress evolution predicted reasonably well for all three strain amplitudes.

**High-temperature prediction (Alloy 1):**
- Preliminary prediction at 923 K, 0.9% strain amplitude.
- Flow stress at high temperature lower than room temperature.
- Qualitatively similar increase in peak stress followed by delayed cyclic softening predicted at 923 K.
- Comparison with experimental data from Gopinath et al. [87] on Alloy 720Li: qualitatively similar trends.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## 1. Precipitation Strengthening (γ′ Precipitate Strengthening)

- **Related Elements:** Al, Ti (γ′ formers); Ni (matrix)
- **Typical Alloys:** Alloy 1 (PM-processed polycrystalline Ni-based superalloy), Alloy 2 (Nimonic-90)
- **Key Compositions:** Alloy 1 matrix: Al 8.75 at.%, Ti 5.70 at.%, Ni 52.80 at.%; Alloy 1 primary γ′ₚ: Al 12.78 at.%, Ti 9.71 at.%, Ni 61.90 at.%; Alloy 2: Al 3.92 at.%, Ti 2.67 at.%, Ni 53.19 at.%
- **Microstructural Changes:** Precipitate shearing during cyclic deformation reduces precipitate size (Alloy 1: from 200–300 nm to ≈91 nm after 500 cycles at 0.9% strain amplitude; model predictions: from 250 nm to ≈50 nm). For Alloy 1, there is a transition from strong-pair coupling to weak-pair coupling as precipitate size decreases; for Alloy 2, weak-pair coupling is active from the beginning due to small initial size (25 nm).
- **Property Improvements:** Precipitate strengthening contributes significantly to overall strength (volume fraction nearly 50% for Alloy 1, ≈21% for Alloy 2). As precipitates are sheared and decrease in size, their contribution to strengthening decreases, leading to cyclic softening.
- **Risks and Limitations:** Cyclic softening is the primary risk, as repeated shearing reduces precipitate size and strengthening contribution. The paper notes that the model does not account for discrete/individual dislocation-precipitate interactions (mean field approximation) and does not consider nanoscale mechanisms dependent on stacking fault energy.
- **Original Evidence:** Section 2.1 (precipitate strengthening equations); Section 2.3 (precipitate shearing model); Figs. 6–8, 10–11; Appendix C (statistics of precipitate shearing).

# Key Conclusions

1. A micromechanical constitutive model has been developed and implemented in a dislocation density-based crystal plasticity framework to simulate microstructure evolution due to precipitate shearing and the resultant cyclic softening in polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys.

2. The model explicitly accounts for the evolution of volume number density and size of γ′ precipitates, as well as their shapes (cuboidal for Alloy 1, spherical for Alloy 2), using appropriate geometric factors (Gₐ and Gᵥ).

3. The proposed model is different from previous micromechanical models in that it considers the rate of evolution of the areal density of precipitates as a function of the cyclic crystallographic shearing rate, rather than cumulative irreversible slip steps or slip distance. The model also enforces conservation of volume fraction (Eqs. 9–13).

4. For Alloy 1 (PM-processed polycrystalline Ni-based superalloy with primary, secondary, and tertiary γ′ precipitates), the model predicts initial cyclic hardening followed by cyclic softening at both 0.9% and 1.8% strain amplitudes. The softening is attributed to a transition from strong-pair coupling to weak-pair coupling of dislocation-precipitate interactions as precipitates are sheared and reduce in size.

5. For Alloy 2 (Nimonic-90 with spherical γ′ precipitates, initial size ≈25 nm), the weak-pair coupling mechanism is present from the beginning of deformation, and cyclic softening is primarily due to reduction in precipitate size and its contribution to precipitate strengthening.

6. Experimental TEM microstructure characterization confirmed precipitate shearing in Alloy 1, with secondary γ′ₛ precipitate size reducing from ≈200–300 nm to ≈91 nm after cyclic deformation. SEM analysis estimated that roughly 45% of precipitates were sheared.

7. The model was validated by predicting the cyclic deformation and softening behavior for a strain amplitude (0.85%) not used in calibration for Alloy 2.

8. Preliminary high-temperature prediction at 923 K for Alloy 1 shows qualitatively similar peak stress evolution with delayed cyclic softening, consistent with experimental observations from Gopinath et al. [87] on Alloy 720Li.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

This paper is **not directly applicable to refractory high-temperature alloys** such as Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based, refractory high-entropy alloys, or additively manufactured refractory alloys. The paper focuses exclusively on **Ni-based superalloys** with FCC γ matrix and L1₂ γ′ precipitates, which have fundamentally different crystal structures, deformation mechanisms, and strengthening mechanisms compared to BCC refractory alloys.

**Specific limitations of the paper:**

- The crystal plasticity model is a local model that does not consider size effects due to strain gradient effects near matrix-precipitate interfaces.
- The mean field precipitate strengthening model does not consider individual dislocation-precipitate interactions.
- The model assumes a single mean size for precipitates at each material point, rather than a distribution of precipitate sizes.
- The model does not account for discrete/individual nanoscale shearing mechanisms dependent on stacking fault energy or temperature-dependent APB energy variations.
- The model does not consider tertiary γ′ₜ precipitate shearing (microstructure characterization at such fine resolution was not possible).
- The model does not address fatigue life prediction; it only predicts cyclic softening behavior.
- High-temperature cyclic deformation and softening, which is the expected operating condition for superalloys, was not addressed in this work (only preliminary prediction shown in Appendix D).
- The model does not consider oxidation effects, which are relevant for high-temperature service.
- The experimental validation of microstructure evolution is limited; more detailed characterization (HRTEM) is suggested for future work.

**Applicable scope:** The model and framework are applicable to polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys with γ′ precipitates subjected to low cycle fatigue loading at room temperature. Extension to refractory alloys would require fundamental changes to account for BCC crystal structure, different slip systems, different precipitate types (carbides, oxides), and different strengthening mechanisms.
