# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title:** Comparison of microstructure and mechanical properties of GH4099 superalloys manufactured by L-DED and L-PBF
- **Authors:** Xinyu Zhang, Yongfeng Liang, Gengwu Ge, Gang Yang, Feng Yi, Junpin Lin
- **Journal:** Materials Letters
- **Year:** 2025
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matlet.2025.138686
- **Alloy system studied:** GH4099 Ni-based superalloy (Ni-Cr-Mo-Co-W-Al-Ti-Fe-C)
- **Research objective:** To compare the microstructure and mechanical properties of GH4099 superalloy manufactured by two laser-based additive manufacturing techniques: laser-powder bed fusion (L-PBF) and laser-directed energy deposition (L-DED), including the evolution of microstructure after hot isostatic pressing (HIP) and heat treatment (HT).
- **Material category:** Additively manufactured Ni-based superalloy (not a refractory alloy system; the paper concerns GH4099, a Ni-Cr-Mo-Co-W-Al-Ti alloy processed via L-DED and L-PBF).

# Alloy System and Research Object

- GH4099 Ni-based superalloy manufactured by two laser-based additive manufacturing methods: L-DED (laser-directed energy deposition) and L-PBF (laser-powder bed fusion).
- Both as-built and HIP + heat-treated conditions were investigated.
- Mechanical testing was performed at room temperature and 900 °C.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

**L-PBF parameters:**
- Laser power (P): 270 W
- Scanning rate (v): 1300 mm/s
- Powder bed thickness: 40 μm
- Scanning spacing: 0.11 mm

**L-DED parameters:**
- Laser power (P): 900 W
- Scanning rate (v): 6 mm/s
- Powder output: 0.3 r/min
- Overlap: 0.8 mm

**Post-processing (applied to both L-PBF and L-DED specimens):**
- Hot isostatic pressing (HIP): 1200 °C, 180 MPa, 2 h, furnace cooling (FC)
- Solution treatment: 1200 °C for 2 h, air cooling (AC)
- Aging: 800 °C for 8 h, air cooling (AC)

The paper does not mention any homogenization, rolling, forging, or additional deformation processing.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

**As-built condition:**
- Both L-DED and L-PBF alloys showed obvious forming marks, including overlap between different tracks and layers, leading to dendritic/cellular crystals with complex directions inside the melt pool boundaries.
- Dendritic/cellular structure size: ~5 μm in L-DED; ~500 nm in L-PBF.
- Dislocation entanglement in cellular form observed; dislocations mainly concentrated at boundaries of dendrites in L-PBF.
- In L-DED, dislocation structure at dendrite boundary as well as high-density dislocations and stacking faults displayed inside the fine grains.
- Segregation of C and heavy elements causes the dendritic/cellular structure (cited from prior studies).

**After HIP + HT:**
- In L-DED alloy:
  - Element segregation transformed into carbides distributed along the dendrites.
  - All grains showed strong recovery.
  - Twinning occurred only in fine grains.
  - Final grain structure: bimodal distribution (~50 μm and ~500 μm).
- In L-PBF alloy:
  - Dissolution of dendritic/cellular structure.
  - Carbides present.
  - Twinning occurred in all grains, with twinning boundaries accounting for ~70% of the total grain boundaries.
  - Significant grain refinement (~30 μm).
- Both alloys exhibited similar characteristics after HIP + HT: dissolution of dendritic/cellular structure, twin formation, and carbide formation.
- The formation of carbides and twinning after heat processes is related to the high residual stress caused by the AM process. M and C atoms tend to accumulate around the dislocation network, beneficial for carbide precipitation. Dislocations and stacking faults can cause twinning due to recovery.

**Phase constitution notes:**
- Matrix phase: Ni-based austenite (γ) is implied by the superalloy context, but the specific matrix phase (e.g., γ/γ′) is not explicitly discussed in detail.
- The paper mentions carbides (M23C6 is referenced from literature but not explicitly confirmed in this paper) and twinning boundaries as microstructural features.
- No explicit discussion of γ′ precipitates, though reference to “precipitated phases” is made in the high-temperature deformation context.

**Strengthening mechanisms discussed:**
- Grain-boundary strengthening / grain refinement (Hall-Petch).
- Intragranular carbide pinning.
- Twinning boundary hindrance.
- Dislocation and stacking fault activation during deformation.
- At high temperature: intragranular precipitation phase dispersion and pinning of intergranular carbides.

# Mechanical Properties

- Tensile specimens were obtained perpendicular to the building direction (BD).
- Tests performed according to Chinese standard GB/T 228.1–2010.
- Strain rate: 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at both room temperature (RT) and 900 °C.

**Room temperature:**
- L-PBF alloy exhibited higher strength than L-DED alloy.
- Strength difference described as “100 MPa lower” for L-DED in the conclusions.
- L-DED alloy showed better plasticity than L-PBF alloy at RT.
- Exact numerical values for yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and elongation are not reported in the text; they are presented in Fig. 3a as stress-strain curves but not tabulated.
- The paper compares RT mechanical property distributions of traditional, L-DED, and L-PBF GH4099 alloys via Fig. 3b, citing refs [6, 12–14]; AM alloys exhibit superior performance.

**High temperature (900 °C):**
- The strength of L-DED and L-PBF alloys is similar.
- Elongation of the L-DED alloy is nearly half that of the L-PBF alloy at 900 °C.
- The paper states that L-DED plasticity is “about half of L-PBF alloy.”
- Exact numerical values for 900 °C yield strength, UTS, and elongation are not reported; data are in Fig. 3c.

**Deformation mechanisms:**
- At RT: plastic deformation occurs by activating stacking faults and dislocations to reduce strain concentration.
- At 900 °C: strength is mainly based on intragranular precipitation phase dispersion and pinning of intergranular carbides to hinder dislocation initiation and migration. Both bypassing and cutting mechanisms occur simultaneously. Twinning and stacking faults can release energy during tensile process. Cracks initiate and propagate along grain boundaries.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Grain-Boundary Strengthening / Grain Refinement (Hall-Petch)

- **Related Elements:** Not element-specific; process-dependent (L-PBF vs L-DED)
- **Typical Alloys:** GH4099 Ni-based superalloy manufactured by L-PBF and L-DED
- **Key Compositions:** GH4099 (Ni-18.24Cr-4.09Mo-6.53Co-4.09W-1.25Al-1.25Ti-0.92Fe-0.05C, wt.%)
- **Microstructural Changes:** L-PBF produces finer grains (~30 μm after HIP + HT) with twinning boundaries accounting for ~70% of total grain boundaries; L-DED produces a bimodal grain size distribution (~50 μm and ~500 μm after HIP + HT) with twinning only in fine grains.
- **Property Improvements:** At RT, L-PBF alloy exhibits higher strength than L-DED alloy (approximately 100 MPa higher) due to finer grain size. L-DED alloy has better RT plasticity. The paper explicitly states: “Grain size determines the mechanical properties between two alloys. Compared with L-DED alloy, L-PBF alloy has finer grains, resulting in higher strength. This inevitably leads to low plasticity.”
- **Risks and Limitations:** At high temperature (900 °C), grain boundaries become weak links. “The strengthening of grain boundaries is weakened, and the strength of the two is similar.” “Grain boundaries will become weak links, and cracks will initiate and propagate along the grain boundaries.” Fine grain size of L-PBF does not yield strength advantage at 900 °C. L-DED alloy exhibits low plasticity at 900 °C (about half of L-PBF).
- **Additional Note:** Twinning boundaries act as grain boundaries and contribute to grain refinement strengthening at RT.

## Intragranular Carbide Precipitation Strengthening (Carbide Pinning)

- **Related Elements:** C, M elements (Mo, Cr, W), heavy element segregation
- **Typical Alloys:** GH4099 Ni-based superalloy (AM-processed)
- **Key Compositions:** 0.05 wt.% C with 4.09 wt.% Mo, 18.24 wt.% Cr, 4.09 wt.% W
- **Microstructural Changes:** High-density dislocations in AM materials attract M and C atoms, leading to carbide nucleation at dislocation networks. After HIP + HT, element segregation transforms into carbides distributed along dendrites. Intragranular carbides form within grains; intergranular carbides form along grain boundaries.
- **Property Improvements:** Additional intragranular carbide pinning contributes to superior mechanical properties of AM GH4099 compared to traditionally processed GH4099. At 900 °C, “intragranular carbides provide higher strength.” Pinning of intergranular carbides hinders dislocation initiation and migration at high temperature.
- **Risks and Limitations:** At 900 °C, precipitated phases soften; mechanisms of dislocation migration, bypassing, and cutting occur simultaneously. The paper notes this as part of the high-temperature deformation mechanism.
- **Additional Note:** The formation of carbides after heat treatment is related to the high residual stress from AM processing.

## Twinning-Induced Strengthening

- **Related Elements:** Process-dependent; related to residual stress, dislocations, and stacking faults
- **Typical Alloys:** GH4099 Ni-based superalloy (AM-processed)
- **Key Compositions:** GH4099 (as above)
- **Microstructural Changes:** High residual stress from AM leads to twin nucleation during HIP + HT. L-PBF alloy exhibits twinning in all grains with twin boundaries ~70% of total GBs; L-DED alloy shows twinning only in fine grains. Dislocations and stacking faults can cause twinning due to recovery.
- **Property Improvements:** Twinning boundary hindrance contributes to higher mechanical properties in AM alloys compared to traditional processing. At 900 °C, “Twinning and stacking faults can release energy during the tensile process.”
- **Risks and Limitations:** At 900 °C, twinning does not effectively strengthen grain boundaries; cracks initiate and propagate along grain boundaries.
- **Additional Note:** Twinning boundaries serve a dual role: they act as strengthening barriers at RT and energy-releasing mechanisms during high-temperature deformation.

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related Elements:** Cr, Mo, Co, W, Al, Ti (all alloying elements)
- **Typical Alloys:** GH4099 Ni-based superalloy
- **Key Compositions:** See above composition
- **Microstructural Changes:** Heavy elements (Mo, W, Cr) segregate during rapid solidification, creating dendritic/cellular structures. Dislocation entanglement at dendrite boundaries contributes to strengthening. The paper states: “segregation of C and heavy elements causes this dendritic/cellular structure.”
- **Property Improvements:** Not explicitly quantified in this paper for solid-solution strengthening. AM alloys obtain “additional intragranular carbide pinning and twinning boundary hindrance” compared to traditional processing, “thus possessing higher mechanical properties.”
- **Risks and Limitations:** At 900 °C, solid-solution strengthening is secondary to precipitation and carbide pinning; precipitated phases soften.
- **Additional Note:** The rapid solidification rates in L-PBF (10⁶–10⁸ K/s) and L-DED (10²–10⁶ K/s) enhance element segregation and dislocation density, which are precursors for carbide precipitation and twin nucleation after HT.

## Dislocation Strengthening

- **Related Elements:** Not element-specific; process-dependent (laser power and cooling rate)
- **Typical Alloys:** GH4099 Ni-based superalloy (L-DED and L-PBF)
- **Key Compositions:** GH4099 (as above)
- **Microstructural Changes:** In L-PBF, dislocations are mainly concentrated at dendritic boundaries. In L-DED, the dislocation structure is present at the dendrite boundary, with high-density dislocations and stacking faults displayed inside the fine grains. Dislocation entanglement exists in cellular form.
- **Property Improvements:** High-density dislocations attract M element segregation, leading to intragranular carbide formation and twin nucleation after heat treatment, thereby enhancing strength. At RT, plastic deformation occurs by activating stacking faults and dislocations to reduce strain concentration.
- **Risks and Limitations:** At high temperature, recovery reduces dislocation strengthening effectiveness.
- **Additional Note:** The higher dislocation density and finer cellular structure of L-PBF (500 nm) vs L-DED (5 μm) contribute to the grain refinement difference after HIP + HT.

## Additive Manufacturing-Oriented Composition Control

- **Related Elements:** C, heavy elements such as Mo and W
- **Typical Alloys:** GH4099 Ni-based superalloy (L-PBF and L-DED)
- **Key Compositions:** 0.05 wt.% C, 4.09 wt.% Mo, 4.09 wt.% W, 18.24 wt.% Cr
- **Microstructural Changes:** Rapid solidification leads to element segregation and dislocation entanglement. The as-built dendritic/cellular structure size differs: ~5 μm for L-DED, ~500 nm for L-PBF. After HIP + HT, microstructural evolution includes dissolution of dendritic/cellular structure, twin formation, and carbide precipitation. Higher residual stress in AM promotes twin nucleation and carbide formation.
- **Property Improvements:** AM GH4099 alloys exhibit superior mechanical properties compared to traditional processes at RT. The paper states: “Compared with traditional processes, Laser-AM alloys exhibit increased intragranular carbides after heat treatment, rapid condensation leading to element segregation and dislocation entanglement, as well as residual stress induced twin nucleation, effectively improving mechanical properties.”
- **Risks and Limitations:** At 900 °C, the grain boundaries weaken, eliminating the strength advantage of finer-grained L-PBF alloy. L-DED alloy has low plasticity at 900 °C (about half of L-PBF), limiting its application. The paper notes: “the plasticity of L-DED alloy is low (about half of L-PBF alloy), which is equivalent to limiting the further application of this technology.”
- **Additional Note:** The paper emphasizes that without understanding the microstructural and functional response differences between L-PBF and L-DED, applications for this technology/material combination will be severely limited.

# Key Conclusions

1. GH4099 superalloy with excellent performance was prepared by both L-PBF and L-DED laser additive manufacturing methods.
2. The as-built microstructure of both processes consists of dendritic/cellular structures with complex directions inside melt pool boundaries, with L-DED structure size ~5 μm and L-PBF structure size ~500 nm.
3. After HIP (1200 °C, 180 MPa, 2 h, FC) + solution (1200 °C, 2 h, AC) + aging (800 °C, 8 h, AC), both alloys exhibited dissolution of dendritic/cellular structures, formation of carbides, and twin formation. L-PBF showed full twinning (~70% of grain boundaries) with fine grains (~30 μm); L-DED showed bimodal grain distribution (~50 and ~500 μm) with twinning only in fine grains.
4. At room temperature, L-PBF alloy has higher strength (approximately 100 MPa higher) due to finer grain size and stronger grain-boundary strengthening, while L-DED alloy has better plasticity.
5. At 900 °C, both alloys have similar strength because grain-boundary strengthening is weakened and strength is mainly governed by intragranular precipitation dispersion and intergranular carbide pinning. L-DED alloy elongation is nearly half that of L-PBF alloy.
6. Compared to traditional processing, laser-AM GH4099 alloys have increased intragranular carbides after heat treatment; rapid solidification leads to element segregation and dislocation entanglement; residual stress induces twin nucleation; these factors effectively improve mechanical properties.
7. The high-temperature plastic anisotropy—particularly the low plasticity of L-DED alloy—limits further application of this technology.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Limitations:**
  - This paper does not report explicit numerical values for yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, elongation, hardness, density, elastic modulus, fracture toughness, or creep performance. Data are presented only graphically in Fig. 3.
  - The exact type and composition of carbides (e.g., M23C6 vs MC) are not experimentally confirmed in this paper; M23C6 is referenced from previous literature (ref [10]).
  - No quantitative analysis of γ′ phase volume fraction, size, or composition is provided.
  - No oxidation or environmental resistance data.
  - The paper does not discuss the effect of specimen orientation on anisotropic properties, though specimens were taken perpendicular to the building direction only.
  - No atom-probe or elemental mapping data beyond what is stated in the text.

- **Applicable Scope:**
  - The findings are applicable to additively manufactured GH4099 Ni-based superalloys processed by L-PBF and L-DED with similar processing parameters and post-processing (HIP at 1200 °C/180 MPa/2 h + solution at 1200 °C/2 h + aging at 800 °C/8 h).
  - The comparison between L-PBF and L-DED processing is relevant to other Ni-based superalloys processed by laser AM, particularly regarding grain size, twin formation, carbide precipitation, and the resulting RT/high-temperature mechanical behavior.
  - The paper notes that this study is relevant for materials with similar composition ranges (Ni-Cr-Mo-Co-W-Al-Ti systems with ~0.05 wt.% C).
