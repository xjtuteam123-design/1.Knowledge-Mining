# Paper Metadata
- **Title:** Influence of thermal and radiation effects on microstructural and mechanical properties of Nb–1Zr
- **Authors:** Keith J. Leonard, Jeremy T. Busby, Steven J. Zinkle
- **Journal:** Journal of Nuclear Materials 414 (2011) 286–302
- **Year:** 2011
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jnucmat.2011.04.018
- **Alloy system studied:** Nb–1Zr (Nb-1 wt.% Zr)
- **Research objective:** To examine microstructural changes and corresponding effects on mechanical properties, electrical resistivity, and density of Nb–1Zr following neutron irradiation up to 1.8 dpa at 1073, 1223, and 1373 K, compared with thermally aged material for similar exposure times of ~1100 h. The study aimed to resolve whether severe radiation-induced degradation in tensile properties occurs at 1073–1373 K when interstitial solute pickup is minimized.
- **Material category:** Nb-based alloy (dilute Nb-Zr alloy), nuclear structural material

# Alloy System and Research Object
- **Alloy:** Nb–1Zr, produced by Cabot Supermetals Inc., heat number 236884, as-rolled stock
- **Measured composition:** 0.95 wt.% Zr, with 22 ppm C, 130 ppm O, and 46 ppm N
- **Melting point of Nb:** 2740 K
- **Matrix:** β-Nb (BCC)
- **Precipitate phase:** ZrO₂ (zirconium oxide), both cubic ZrO₂₋ₓ (Fm-3m) and monoclinic ZrO₂ structures

# Processing and Heat Treatment
The processing history and heat treatment conditions for the Nb–1Zr material are as follows:

1. **As-received:** Rolled sheet stock (Cabot Supermetals Inc., heat number 236884)

2. **Specimen preparation:**
   - Miniature SS-3 sheet tensile specimens (0.76 × 1.52 × 7.62 mm gage; 25.4 mm overall length)
   - Fabricated by Mo-wire electrical discharge machining (EDM) with water coolant
   - Gage lengths parallel to rolling direction
   - Polished to 32 finish or better
   - Electrochemically cleaned in HF/HNO₃/H₂O mixture

3. **Annealing:** Vacuum annealed at 1835 K for 1 hour in a Nb–1Zr box
   - Vacuum < 1.3 × 10⁻⁴ Pa
   - Heating rate: ~20 K/min
   - Furnace cooled at ~100 K/min for first minutes

4. **Thermal aging:** 1100 h (equivalent to two HFIR cycles)
   - 1098 K
   - 1248 K
   - 1398 K
   - Performed in sealed alloy 600 canisters with alumina inner sleeves; Nb and Ta foil getters used

5. **Neutron irradiation:**
   - HFIR peripheral target locations
   - Temperatures: 1073 K (0.93 dpa), 1223 K (0.89 dpa and 1.77 dpa), 1373 K (0.73 dpa)
   - One cycle = 550 h; two cycles = 1100 h
   - He fill gas for 1073/1223 K; Ne for 1373 K
   - Displacement energy for Nb and Zr = 40 eV

6. **Density measurement:** Immersion density method (ASTM C135-96) using Fluorinert™ Liquid FC-43

7. **Hardness testing:** Vickers microhardness, 1 kg load, 15 s dwell

8. **Tensile testing:** ASTM E8-04, strain rate 0.001 s⁻¹, vacuum < 2.6 × 10⁻⁴ Pa for high-temperature tests

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution
The microstructure of Nb–1Zr evolves significantly with thermal aging and irradiation conditions:

## As-Annealed (1835 K, 1 h):
- Equiaxed, defect-free grain structure
- Mean grain size: 70 μm
- No evidence of ZrO₂ precipitation
- Low density of network dislocations (likely from TEM foil handling)

## Thermally Aged (1100 h):

### 1098 K aging:
- Precipitation visible along grain boundaries and grain interiors (TEM only)
- Grain boundary precipitates: Zr-rich, crystallographic information not obtainable due to small size
- Intragranular precipitates: plate-like morphology, average size 32 nm, density 9.3 × 10²⁰ m⁻³
- Plate normal direction close to Nb ⟨001⟩; plate directions near ⟨110⟩

### 1248 K aging:
- Larger precipitates, reduced density
- Two types of grain boundary precipitates:
  - Smaller (<50 nm): cubic Fm-3m structure, lattice constant 0.459 nm
  - Larger: monoclinic ZrO₂
- Intragranular cubic plate-shaped precipitates: average size 160 nm, density < 1 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³
- Monoclinic intragranular precipitates also present in lower concentration
- Baker–Nutting (B-N) orientation relationship: Nb[001]//ZrO₂₋ₓ[110], Nb(200)//ZrO₂₋ₓ(002)

### 1398 K aging:
- Plate-like precipitates: average size ~475 nm, density ~8 × 10¹⁷ m⁻³
- Monoclinic grain boundary precipitates with high degree of {001} twinning
- Intragranular precipitates show B-N orientation relationship
- Monoclinic intragranular precipitates also present, ~same size as cubic, no internal twinning

## Irradiated:

### 0.9 dpa at 1073 K:
- Cavities (faceting on {110} and {200} planes): 5–60 nm, average ~20 nm, density ≤ 3 × 10¹⁷ m⁻³
- Void swelling: ~1 × 10⁻⁴ %
- Intragranular precipitates: plate-shaped, rectangular morphology, average 12 nm, density ~3 × 10²⁰ m⁻³
- Precipitate and cavity distribution non-uniform
- Grain boundaries precipitate-free
- Dislocation pinning at precipitates

### 0.9 dpa at 1223 K:
- Cavities: average 35 nm, density < 1 × 10¹⁷ m⁻³, swelling ~10⁻⁴ %
- Intragranular precipitates: average 48 nm, density ~2 × 10¹⁹ m⁻³
- Non-uniform distribution
- Dislocation pinning observed

### 1.8 dpa at 1223 K:
- No cavities observed
- Intragranular precipitates: average 75 nm, density 5–8 × 10¹⁸ m⁻³
- Predominantly monoclinic ZrO₂ at grain boundaries
- Reduced dislocation tangles compared to lower dose

### 0.7 dpa at 1373 K:
- Low-angle subgrain structures (elongated, L:W ~ 6:1) indicating dynamic recrystallization during tensile testing
- Cubic and monoclinic ZrO₂ at high-angle grain boundaries
- Intragranular plate precipitates: average 260 nm, density ~1 × 10¹⁸ m⁻³
- Cubic lattice constant: 0.485 nm (vs. 0.459 nm in 1398 K aged)

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability
This paper does not directly address oxidation resistance of the Nb–1Zr alloy in air or oxidizing environments. Oxidation-related discussion is limited to:

- **Internal oxidation / oxygen gettering:** Oxygen present as an impurity (130 ppm) reacts with Zr to form ZrO₂ precipitates during thermal aging and irradiation.
- **Precipitate formation:** Zr acts as an oxygen getter, converting dissolved oxygen into stable ZrO₂ oxide particles.
- **Impurity control:** Special precautions were taken during annealing and aging to minimize pickup of O, C, N from the environment (Nb box gettering, Ta foil gettering, vacuum/inert gas conditions).

**Service stability issues identified:**
- In aged material, grain boundary precipitation of ZrO₂ led to intergranular fracture and reduced ductility
- The paper mentions a "potential embrittlement created by the presence of grain boundary precipitation"
- Uniform elongation and total elongation both remain acceptable (>3% and >12% respectively) for all conditions examined
- Dynamic strain aging observed in annealed material at 1073 K but absent in aged samples (impurities consumed by precipitates)

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Zr + O (Zirconium-Oxygen Combination)

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Nb–1Zr dilute Nb-based alloy

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
Zr: 0.95 wt.% (approximately 0.9 at.%); O: 130 ppm (initial interstitial impurity)

**[Main Function]**
Zirconium acts as an oxygen getter, reacting with interstitial oxygen to form stable ZrO₂ oxide particles. The ZrO₂ precipitates provide significant precipitation strengthening (dispersion-like strengthening) to the Nb matrix, especially at temperatures up to ~1100 K.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
Oxygen dissolved in the Nb matrix binds with Zr to form ZrO₂ precipitates during elevated temperature aging or irradiation. The precipitates nucleate as cubic ZrO₂₋ₓ (Fm-3m space group, lattice constant 0.459 nm) following the Baker–Nutting orientation relationship with the β-Nb matrix (Nb[001]//ZrO₂₋ₓ[110], Nb(200)//ZrO₂₋ₓ(002)). On further growth, particles transform to monoclinic ZrO₂. Precipitates form both intragranularly (plate-like, aligned with Nb ⟨001⟩) and at grain boundaries. The cubic-to-monoclinic transformation is size-dependent, with monoclinic particles generally larger. Grain boundary monoclinic ZrO₂ in material aged at 1398 K exhibits {001} twinning.

**[Property Effect]**
- **Hardness at 1098 K aging (1100 h):** 116 kg/mm² (from 91 kg/mm² in annealed) with precipitate density 9.3 × 10²⁰ m⁻³ and average size 32 nm
- **Tensile strength at 1073 K test:** YS = 161–205 MPa for aged 1098 K (vs. 76–128 MPa annealed); UTS = 242–252 MPa (vs. 185–196 MPa annealed)
- **Strengthening effect decreases with increasing precipitate size and decreasing density**
- **Electrical resistivity:** Decrease of 4.6–9.5 nΩ-m in aged materials due to solute redistribution
- **Density:** Decrease of ~0.5–0.6% at lowest aging temperature (1098 K), diminishing with higher temperature
- **Grain boundary precipitation contributes to intergranular fracture and reduced tensile ductility**

**[Potential Side Effects]**
- ZrO₂ precipitation at grain boundaries leads to intergranular fracture and reduced ductility in aged material
- Non-uniform precipitate distribution in irradiated material creates obstacle-free channels for dislocations, leading to strain softening
- Consumes free O that may otherwise affect void nucleation
- The presence of ZrO₂ precipitates results in measurable density decrease

Tables 2, 3; Figs. 2–8, 15–17; pp. 288–302

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

The Nb–1Zr alloy is primarily a substitutional solid solution in the annealed condition, with Zr dissolved in the Nb BCC matrix. However, the paper notes that this alloy is not as mechanically strong as other Nb-base alloys such as PWC-11 (Nb–1Zr–0.1C), FS-85 (Nb–10W–28Ta–1Zr), or C-103 (Nb–10Hf–1Ti) below 1200 K, indicating that the solid-solution strengthening contribution of 1% Zr is relatively modest.

## Oxide Precipitation Strengthening (ZrO₂ Precipitates)

The dominant strengthening mechanism in aged/irradiated Nb–1Zr is oxide precipitation strengthening via ZrO₂ particles. Key characteristics:
- Intragranular plate-like precipitates aligned with B-N orientation relationship
- Maximum strengthening at 1098 K aging with precipitates averaging 32 nm and density 9.3 × 10²⁰ m⁻³
- Dislocation pinning at precipitates observed during tensile deformation
- Strengthening decreases with coarsening at higher temperatures

## Dispersion Strengthening

The paper suggests that further work is needed to evaluate "dispersion strengthening effects of the Zr-oxide phase." ZrO₂ particles act as dispersoids that pin dislocations, similar to ODS-type strengthening.

## Grain-Boundary Strengthening and Grain-Size Effects

The paper notes that:
- As-annealed grains are 70 μm and remain unchanged during aging
- Grain boundary precipitation of ZrO₂ leads to intergranular fracture (weakening grain boundaries relative to precipitation-hardened grain interiors)
- Subgrain formation (dynamic recrystallization) observed in 0.7 dpa/1373 K material during tensile testing

## Radiation-Induced Strengthening

- Cavities (voids) formed at 0.9 dpa at 1073 K (density ≤ 3 × 10¹⁷ m⁻³) and 1223 K (< 1 × 10¹⁷ m⁻³) contribute little to strengthening
- Dislocation pinning at precipitates contributes to hardening
- At 1.8 dpa/1223 K, no cavities observed
- Below 800 K (from literature), radiation hardening and embrittlement observed
- Above ~1050 K, thermally driven precipitate evolution dominates mechanical response

## Oxidation-Resistant Alloying

The paper identifies Zr as an oxygen getter in Nb, converting dissolved oxygen into stable ZrO₂ precipitates. This has implications for reducing oxygen embrittlement in Nb alloys, though the overall alloy is not oxidation-resistant in air environments (not tested).

# Key Conclusions
1. **Thermal aging has a stronger influence on mechanical properties than neutron irradiation to 0.9 dpa** for aging/irradiation times of ~1100 h in the 1073–1398 K range.

2. **Neutron irradiation to 1.8 dpa at 1223 K produced nearly identical mechanical properties to thermally aged material with similar time at temperature, indicating thermal processes dominate microstructural evolution.

3. **ZrO₂ precipitation is the dominant microstructural change.** Cubic structured zirconium oxide particles form first, transforming to monoclinic structure with growth. Intragranular plate-like precipitates follow the Baker–Nutting orientation relationship to the β-Nb matrix.

4. **Maximum strengthening occurs at the lowest aging temperature (1098 K)** with the highest precipitate density (9.3 × 10²⁰ m⁻³) and smallest size (32 nm), producing hardness of 116 kg/mm² and tensile strengths exceeding all other conditions.

5. **Cavity swelling is negligible** at all conditions examined (~10⁻⁴%), with void densities ≤ 3 × 10¹⁷ m⁻³. Density changes are predominantly due to precipitate formation.

6. **All aged and irradiated specimens maintained acceptable ductility**: uniform elongation > 3% and total elongation > 12% for all conditions tested.

7. **Intergranular fracture observed in 1248 K aged material** is attributed to grain boundary ZrO₂ precipitation and strain hardening in grain interiors, not to irradiation-induced embrittlement. The reduced severity compared to W-containing alloys (Cb-752, FS-85, T-111) is attributed to the absence of W solid-solution hardening.

8. **Nb–1Zr shows only moderate degradation in physical and mechanical properties** in the dose-temperature range examined and should be suitable for nuclear applications under these conditions.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope
- **Dose range limited:** Maximum irradiation dose was 1.8 dpa, substantially below doses achieved in earlier literature studies (11–50 dpa). Further work is needed at higher doses.
- **Temperature range limited:** Temperatures below 1000 K were not studied, where radiation hardening becomes more pronounced. Literature data suggest radiation hardening and embrittlement at < 800 K.
- **Impurity sensitivity:** Group V metals (Nb, V, Ta) are highly susceptible to O, C, N pickup during processing and testing. This study included special precautions (Nb boxes, Ta getters, vacuum/inert conditions) but confirms that impurity interactions dominate over radiation effects in this temperature range.
- **Irradiation environment specifics:** Irradiation was performed in inert gas (He, Ne) inside sealed Nb capsules, not in a liquid metal environment (e.g., Li) used in some prior studies. This difference in gettering environment may affect O availability and thus precipitate formation.
- **The alloy is specific to Nb–1Zr**, which is relatively weak compared to more complex Nb-base alloys (e.g., FS-85, PWC-11, C-103) that contain W, Hf, Ta for strengthening. The absence of W is identified as a mitigating factor for embrittlement severity.
- **The paper does not address oxidation resistance in air or oxygen-containing environments**, nor does it present creep data.
- **Transmutation effects** were not significant at the doses examined; at higher doses, transmutation-induced elements could alter behavior.
- **The study does not examine the effect of pre-irradiation thermal aging or thermomechanical treatments** on subsequent irradiated performance, which the authors identify as future work that may help control dispersion strengthening of the Zr-oxide phase and mitigate low-temperature brittleness.
- **The result that thermal aging dominates over irradiation effects is specific to the temperature range of 1073–1373 K** and would not be expected to apply at lower temperatures where radiation hardening dominates.
