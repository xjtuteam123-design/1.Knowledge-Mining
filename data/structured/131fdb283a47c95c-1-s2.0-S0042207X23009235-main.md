# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy:** Nb521 (nominal composition Nb–5W–2Mo–1Zr, wt.%)
- **Material category:** Nb-based refractory alloy processed by additive manufacturing (SLM)
- **Research objective:** To fabricate dense Nb521 alloy by Selective Laser Melting, characterize the as-built microstructure, and evaluate tensile properties at room temperature and 1200 °C. The study aims to explore the feasibility of SLM for Nb alloys and understand the relationship between SLM process, as-built microstructure, and mechanical properties.
- **Motivation:** Additive manufacturing can achieve complex geometries not attainable by conventional methods; Nb alloys are hard to process conventionally (high hot-working difficulty, poor machinability, high buy-to-fly ratio); SLM is especially attractive for aerospace applications.

# Alloy Composition

**Nominal alloy composition (Nb521):** Nb–5W–2Mo–1Zr, wt.% (as stated in the paper: "Nb-5W-2Zr-1Zr, wt.%" is a typographical error in the abstract; the correct composition is Nb–5W–2Mo–1Zr, wt.%, consistent with Table 1 and the text in Section 1).

**Measured compositions (wt.%):**

| Sample | Mo | Ta | W | Zr | C | O | Nb |
|--------|-----|-----|-----|-----|-------|-------|-----|
| Powder (PREP) | 1.86 | 0.30 | 4.89 | 1.64 | 0.006 | 0.005 | Bal. |
| SLM bulk | 1.84 | 0.31 | 4.98 | 1.57 | 0.005 | 0.080 | Bal. |

**Key composition observations:**
- Oxygen increased from 0.005 wt.% in the powder to 0.080 wt.% after SLM processing.
- Carbon content remained essentially unchanged (~0.005–0.006 wt.%).
- Ta is present at 0.30–0.31 wt.% as a minor impurity/constituent.
- The alloy does not contain Hf (unlike C103), making it more economical.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

**Powder fabrication:**
- Plasma Rotating Electrode Process (PREP) using alloy rods as raw material
- Powder size range used for SLM: 15–53 μm (sieved)

**SLM process:**
- Protective atmosphere: high-purity argon (≥99.999%)
- Oxygen level in building chamber: 300–500 ppm (monitored by oxygen sensor with closed-loop ventilation control)
- Laser parameters:

| Laser power (W) | Hatch distance (μm) | Layer thickness (μm) | Scan speed (mm/s) | VED (J/mm³) |
|-----------------|---------------------|----------------------|---------------------|-------------|
| 180 | 120 | 30 | 350 | 143 |

- Volumetric energy density (VED) calculated as: VED = P/(v·h·t)
- Sample dimensions: 135 mm × 20 mm × 6 mm
- Build orientation: samples built with long dimension as shown in Fig. 1; tensile loading direction perpendicular to build direction (Z)

**Heat treatment:** No heat treatment was applied. The samples were characterized and tested in the as-built condition.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

**Matrix phase:** Single-phase BCC grains; no non-equilibrium phases detected by EBSD.

**Grain size:**
- X-Y plane: 14.2 ± 17.5 μm (area-weighted)
- X-Z plane: 12.2 ± 15.7 μm (area-weighted)
- Grain size does not follow Gaussian distribution (standard deviation larger than average)

**Grain morphology:**
- Direction-dependent grain shape: grains elongated along laser scanning direction on X-Y plane; columnar grains aligned with build direction (Z) on X-Z and Y-Z planes
- Epitaxial grain growth across fusion boundaries observed
- Melt pool width: 60–80 μm

**Grain boundary features:**
- High fraction of low-angle grain boundaries (5°–15°): 40.58% on X-Y plane and 48.37% on X-Z plane
- Low-angle grain boundaries tend to align with high-angle grain boundaries and perpendicular to laser track direction

**Texture:**
- Medium-strength <111> || build direction (Z) solidification texture
- Maximum intensities: 6.124 MRD (X-Y plane) and 5.055 MRD (X-Z plane)

**Dislocation structure:**
- High dislocation density observed by TEM
- Sub-micron dislocation cell structures within grains

**Second phases/precipitates:**
- Nano-dispersoids of ZrO₂ (<30 nm) identified by STEM-EDS
- ZrO₂ particles enriched in Zr and slightly enriched in O
- No carbides (Nb₂C or (Nb,Zr)C) found in the as-built microstructure
- Dislocation bowing around particles observed, indicating particle-dislocation interaction

**Porosity:**
- Overall porosity below 1.25 ± 0.37%
- Relative density by Archimedes method: 99.0 ± 0.1%
- Primary pore type: lack-of-fusion pores with irregular shapes
- Porosity varies by plane and position (bottom/middle/top):

| Plane | Bottom | Middle | Top |
|-------|--------|--------|-----|
| X-Y | 0.28 ± 0.11% | 0.46 ± 0.18% | 0.37 ± 0.14% |
| X-Z | 1.25 ± 0.37% | 0.92 ± 0.32% | 0.65 ± 0.31% |
| Y-Z | 0.37 ± 0.10% | 0.61 ± 0.27% | 0.95 ± 0.34% |

**Strengthening mechanisms discussed:**
- Solid-solution strengthening from Mo and W in the matrix
- Dispersion strengthening from oxides (ZrO₂)
- High dislocation density strengthening
- The paper explicitly discusses solid-solution strengthening, dispersion strengthening, and dislocation strengthening as the operative mechanisms.

**Comparison with literature data (cited in the paper):**
- Wrought Nb521 room-temperature ultimate strength: 320–620 MPa
- Wrought Nb521 room-temperature fracture elongation: 12–20%
- EBM-processed Nb521 room-temperature ultimate strength: 500–550 MPa
- SLM + HIP Nb521 (Chen et al.): YS 530 MPa, UTS 679 MPa, elongation 5.9% at room temperature
- Rolled and annealed Nb-W-Mo-Zr alloy at ~1200 °C: tensile strength 175 MPa, fracture elongation 44%
- SLM-processed Nb521 at 1200 °C: tensile strength >220 MPa (present study), higher than conventionally processed counterpart

**Fracture behavior:**
- Room temperature: transgranular cleavage fracture with river patterns; pores visible on fracture surfaces
- 1200 °C: quasi-cleavage fracture with cleavage surfaces and fibrous patterns; pores visible on fracture surfaces
- Fracture mode transition from brittle to ductile expected with increasing temperature; dynamic recrystallization starts at 1400 °C in Nb521 (from literature)

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

**Oxygen pickup during processing:**
- Oxygen content increased from 0.005 wt.% (powder) to 0.080 wt.% (SLM bulk)
- Oxygen entered the alloy during powder handling and SLM processing despite argon atmosphere (300–500 ppm oxygen in chamber)
- Oxygen solubility in Nb-Zr alloy is extremely low: <0.003 wt.% at 1450 °C (from Pérez and Massih reference [31])

**Oxide formation:**
- Zr preferentially combines with O rather than forming (Nb,Zr)C when both C and O are present, based on thermodynamic calculations cited from Chen et al. [8]
- ZrO₂ nano-dispersoids (<30 nm) formed in the as-built microstructure
- No carbides (Nb₂C or (Nb,Zr)C) observed; rapid cooling in SLM suppresses Nb₂C precipitation

**Oxidation-related embrittlement:**
- ZrO₂ dispersoids exhibit excellent hardening effect at room and high temperatures but lead to brittle fracture at room temperature
- Formation of oxides instead of carbides in Nb alloys is unfavorable for ductility (citing Sankar et al. [30])
- Oxygen penetration of 500 ppm caused ~16% increase in hardness near surface of wrought C103 Nb alloy (from Sankar et al. [30])

**High-temperature test environment:**
- 1200 °C tensile tests conducted in high vacuum to avoid oxidation during testing
- Diffusion vacuum pump system used

**[Zr (Zirconium)]**

**Applicable Alloy System:** Nb521 (Nb–5W–2Mo–1Zr, wt.%), Nb-Zr-based refractory alloys

**Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 1.64 wt.% (powder), 1.57 wt.% (SLM bulk)

**Main Function:** In the presence of oxygen, Zr forms ZrO₂ nano-dispersoids that provide dispersion strengthening; Zr is intended to contribute to carbide precipitation in conventionally processed Nb alloys

**Microstructural Mechanism:** In the SLM-processed Nb521, oxygen from powder handling and chamber atmosphere reacts with Zr to form nano-sized ZrO₂ particles (<30 nm) dispersed in the BCC matrix. When both C and O are present, Zr preferentially combines with O rather than forming (Nb,Zr)C based on thermodynamic calculations. The extremely low solubility of O in Nb-Zr alloy (<0.003 wt.% at 1450 °C) drives ZrO₂ formation. Dislocation bowing around particles was observed, indicating strong particle-dislocation interaction.

**Property Effect:** ZrO₂ dispersoids contribute to high strength at room temperature (YS ~540 MPa, UTS ~624 MPa) and 1200 °C (YS ~198 MPa, UTS ~222 MPa), which are comparable to or higher than conventionally processed counterparts. However, ZrO₂ formation instead of carbide formation is unfavorable for ductility; room-temperature elongation was only ~8.3%.

**Potential Side Effects:** ZrO₂ dispersoids lead to brittle fracture at room temperature; formation of oxides instead of carbides deteriorates ductility in Nb alloys; Zr consumed by ZrO₂ is unavailable for carbide precipitation strengthening

**Original Evidence:** "Nano-dispersoids of ZrO₂ were found in the as-built microstructure whereas carbides were absent" (Abstract); "In the Nb-Zr-O system, Nb can be in binary eutectic equilibria with ZrO₂ [16] and the solubility of O in an Nb-Zr alloy is extremely low (<0.003 wt% at 1450 °C [31]). When C and O are both present in Nb521 alloy, based on thermodynamic calculations [8], Zr preferentially combine with O rather than forming the (Nb, Zr)C" (Section 3.1); "The ZrO₂ dispersoids exhibit excellent hardening effect at room and high temperatures, but lead to brittle fracture at room temperature" (Section 3.2)

**[C (Carbon)]**

**Applicable Alloy System:** Nb521 (Nb–5W–2Mo–1Zr, wt.%), Nb-C-Zr system

**Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 0.006 wt.% (powder), 0.005 wt.% (SLM bulk)

**Main Function:** In conventionally processed Nb521, even a low concentration of C (e.g., 0.006 wt.%) is sufficient to facilitate carbide precipitation (Nb₂C, (Nb,Zr)C) for precipitation strengthening

**Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 0.005 wt.% (powder) → 0.080 wt.% (SLM bulk); building chamber oxygen level 300–500 ppm during SLM

# Key Conclusions

1. **Feasibility of SLM for Nb521:** Dense Nb521 alloy samples (relative density 99.0 ± 0.1%, porosity <1.25 ± 0.37%) were successfully fabricated by selective laser melting. The primary pore type is lack-of-fusion.

2. **As-built microstructure:** The SLM-processed Nb521 is single-phase BCC with average grain sizes of 14.2 ± 17.5 μm (X-Y plane) and 12.2 ± 15.7 μm (X-Z plane). The microstructure features a medium-strength <111> || Z solidification texture, high fraction of low-angle grain boundaries (40.58% on X-Y, 48.37% on X-Z), high dislocation density, and dislocation cell structures.

3. **Second phase formation:** The only dispersoid type observed in the as-built samples is ZrO₂ (<30 nm). No carbides (Nb₂C or (Nb,Zr)C) were found. This is attributed to oxygen pickup during powder handling and SLM processing (oxygen increased from 0.005 to 0.080 wt.%), with Zr preferentially combining with O rather than C, and rapid cooling suppressing Nb₂C precipitation.

4. **Mechanical properties:** At room temperature, the SLM-processed Nb521 exhibits YS 539.9 ± 7.4 MPa, UTS 623.6 ± 0.9 MPa, and elongation 8.3 ± 0.9%. At 1200 °C, average YS 198.0 MPa, UTS 222.0 MPa, elongation 7.9–12.9%. Strengths are comparable to or higher than conventionally processed counterparts, but ductility is significantly lower due to high dislocation density, porosity, and oxide dispersoids.

5. **Fracture behavior:** Room-temperature fracture is transgranular cleavage with river patterns; 1200 °C fracture is quasi-cleavage with cleavage surfaces and fibrous patterns. Pores are visible on fracture surfaces of both test temperatures.

6. **Atmosphere control is critical:** The oxygen level of 300–500 ppm in the building chamber and powder handling in air were sufficient to cause ZrO₂ formation instead of carbide precipitation. Future process optimization should consider stress-relief treatment to reduce dislocation density, parameter optimization to reduce lack-of-fusion pores, and better control of competitive carbide/oxide precipitation.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. **Limited sample size:** Due to the high cost and limited availability of PREP-fabricated Nb521 powders, only 2 tensile samples were tested at each temperature (room temperature and 1200 °C), and 3 room-temperature samples total (the paper states 2 samples were tested per temperature, though Table 5 lists 3 RT samples). This limits statistical confidence in the reported values.

2. **No heat treatment evaluated:** The study only covers the as-built condition. No stress-relief, annealing, HIP, or other post-processing treatments were evaluated. The authors suggest stress-relief treatment as a future optimization direction.

3. **No oxidation resistance testing performed:** While the paper discusses oxygen pickup during processing and its effect on microstructure, no dedicated high-temperature oxidation resistance testing was conducted. The 1200 °C tensile tests were performed in high vacuum to avoid oxidation.

4. **No creep testing:** The 1200 °C tensile test was performed using a creep testing system but was conducted as a short-term tensile test, not a creep test. No creep properties (rupture life, creep rate, etc.) are reported.

5. **Limited comparison with literature:** The paper compares with literature data for conventionally processed and EBM-processed Nb521, but the comparison is qualitative rather than a systematic comparative study under identical testing conditions.

6. **Scope limited to Nb521 alloy:** The findings are specific to the Nb521 (Nb–5W–2Mo–1Zr, wt.%) alloy processed by SLM with the specific parameters used. Generalization to other Nb alloys or AM processes should be made cautiously. However, the authors note consistency with observations from SLM-processed C103 (high dislocation density, cell structures) and SLM-processed Nb521 with HIP (ZrO₂ formation).

7. **The abstract contains a typographical error in the alloy composition:** "Nb-5W-2Zr-1Zr, wt.%" should be "Nb-5W-2Mo-1Zr, wt.%" based on Table 1 and the text body. This should be noted when citing the alloy composition.

8. **The effect of porosity on mechanical properties** is discussed qualitatively (pores visible on fracture surfaces, stress concentration at pores) but no quantitative analysis correlating pore size/distribution with mechanical properties was performed.
