# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title:** Mechanical and high-temperature characterization of additively manufactured Ni-superalloys at SALSA neutron strain diffractometer
- **Authors:** W. Warzanskyj, B. Özcan, J. Luo, R. Bordas, T. Schenk, J. Cormier, T. Pirling, J. L. Ocaña, S. Cabeza
- **Journal:** Nuclear Instruments and Methods in Physics Research, Section A
- **Year:** 2024
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nima.2024.169709
- **Alloy system studied:** Ni-based superalloys (Inconel 718 and Waspaloy)
- **Research objective:** To demonstrate in-situ neutron diffraction setups for high-temperature residual stress measurement and mechanical testing of additively manufactured Ni-superalloys; characterize residual stress evolution during heat treatment and lattice strain response during tensile loading from room temperature to 650°C
- **Material category:** Additively manufactured Ni-based superalloys (not refractory alloys). This paper does **not** concern Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based, refractory high-entropy alloys, or ODS refractory alloys.

---

# Alloy System and Research Object

This paper investigates two non-refractory Ni-based superalloys:

1. **Waspaloy** (powder-based Laser Metal Deposition / LMD)
   - Strengthened by γ′ phase (Ni₃(Al,Ti))
   - Contains no Nb, no γ″ phase

2. **Inconel 718 (IN718)** (wire-based LMD)
   - Strengthened by γ′ and γ″ phases
   - Two conditions studied: As-Built (AB) and Direct-Aged (DA; 720°C/8 h + 620°C/8 h, furnace cooling)

**Important caution:** The information in this paper is about **Ni-superalloys**, not refractory high-temperature alloys, Nb-W-based alloys, Mo-W-based alloys, refractory high-entropy alloys, or refractory ODS alloys. If you are using this card for a refractory alloy database, its transferability is very limited. The insights concern neutron diffraction methodology, LMD residual stress evolution, and high-temperature in-situ tensile behavior of FCC Ni-based superalloys.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Waspaloy (powder LMD)
- **Manufacturing method:** Laser Metal Deposition (LMD) using powder feedstock
- **Sample geometry:** Wall with dimensions 10.2 × 55 × 36.2 mm³
- **Laser power:** 500 W
- **Traverse speed:** 200 mm/min
- **Substrate:** Hastelloy X plate
- **Deposition strategy:** Central line first, then line by line to fill the other side, and finally the perimeter skin
- **Feedstock powder:** Homogeneous size distribution, D50 ≈ 60 μm
- **Heat treatment:** In-situ heat treatment at 760°C for 5.5 h

## IN718 (wire LMD)
- **Manufacturing method:** Laser Metal Deposition (LMD) using wire feedstock
- **Sample geometry:** Prisms 15 × 15 × 120 mm³
- **Laser power:** 900 W
- **Wire feeding rate:** 1200 mm/min
- **Feedstock wire thickness:** 1 ± 0.01 mm
- **Substrate:** Low carbon steel plate
- **Scanning strategy:** ±45° rotation after each layer
- **Heat treatment for DA condition:** 720°C for 8 h + 620°C for 8 h, then furnace cooling

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Waspaloy
- **Matrix:** γ (FCC Ni-based solid solution)
- **Strengthening phase:** γ′ (ordered L1₂ intermetallic, Ni₃(Al,Ti))
- **γ′ morphology:** Homogeneously distributed globular particles (dark contrast) along dendritic structures (light gray contrast)
- **γ′ volume fraction in as-built condition:** Reported as having precipitated very rapidly during LMD, with an estimated volume fraction of 15–20% (cited from literature [34,35])
- **Lattice misfit:** Positive misfit of γ′ in Waspaloy (unlike most Ni-superalloys with negative misfit), leading to compressive residual stresses in grains upon cooling to RT
- **Second phases:** No carbides or Laves phases explicitly discussed for Waspaloy in this paper. The paper states Waspaloy “does not contain Nb element,” so the only strengthening phase is γ′.

## IN718
- **Matrix:** γ (FCC Ni-based solid solution)
- **Strengthening phases:** γ′ and γ″ (Ni₃Nb, ordered DO₂₂ body-centered tetragonal)
- **AB microstructures:** Script-like Laves phase in interdendritic regions; major secondary phase in as-built condition is Laves phase
- **DA microstructure:** γ′ + γ″ precipitates shown in bright contrast; Laves phase is retained after DA treatment
- **Porosity:** Large black holes in micrographs are pores nucleated during fabrication
- **Elemental segregation:** Not explicitly discussed in this paper; Laves phase is known to consume Nb and Mo, but this is referenced rather than measured here

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Nb

- **Element or Element Combination:** Nb (columbium)
- **Applicable Alloy System:** IN718, a wrought/AM Ni-based superalloy
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 5.1 ± 4.0 wt.% Nb in IN718 (Table 2 of paper)
- **Main Function:** Forms γ″ phase (Ni₃Nb, D0₂₂ body-centered tetragonal) when combined with Ni; major precipitation-hardening element in IN718. Also contributes to the formation of Laves phase in as-built LMD microstructures.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** γ″ is coherent with the γ matrix but tetragonal, inducing larger lattice misfit and higher internal stresses than γ′; precipitate precipitates during DA heat treatment (720°C/8 h + 620°C/8 h) and can also form during in-situ re-heating/re-annealing of AM layers. Laves phase is a Nb-rich secondary phase often observed in interdendritic regions of as-built IN718.
- **Property Effect:** IN718 in DA condition has much higher yield strength than AB condition at 25, 450, and 650°C (e.g., 960 MPa vs 544 MPa at RT; 810 vs 360 MPa at 450°C; 620 vs 450 MPa at 650°C). The paper attributes the higher yield strength of DA to γ′ + γ″ precipitation. The γ(220) lattice Young's modulus in DA (215 GPa) is higher than AB (203 GPa) at RT, but at 650°C, AB and DA are nearly identical (190 vs 191 GPa).
- **Potential Side Effects:** Laves phase formation consumes Nb and Mo, thereby reducing the amount of γ′/γ″ forming elements in the as-built condition; can be brittle and reduce mechanical properties if excessive. The paper does not explicitly report embrittlement, but it identifies Laves as the major secondary phase in AB condition. Also, the paper states that in the AB condition at 650°C, the yield stress is higher than at 450°C, which may be due to the onset of γ′ + γ″ precipitation during soaking time before tensile testing, indicating possible microstructural instability during high-temperature service.
- **Original Evidence:** “The microstructure of wire LMD-IN718 consists of a γ matrix, which is strengthened by metastable γ″ phase, Ni₃Nb, and stable γ′ phase, Ni₃(Al,Ti), if an ageing treatment is performed.” “In the samples studied in this paper, the major secondary phase in as-built conditions is Laves, considering its volume fraction (Fig. 1b).” “The higher yield stress in the DA condition is mainly due to the γ’ + γ’’ intermetallic precipitation induced by the DA heat treatment.”

## Ti

- **Element or Element Combination:** Ti (titanium)
- **Applicable Alloy System:** Up to 3 wt.% Ti in Waspaloy and up to 0.9 wt.% Ti in IN718
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** Waspaloy 3 ± 0.5 wt.% Ti; IN718 0.9 ± 0.3 wt.% Ti
- **Main Function:** Forms γ′ phase (Ni₃(Al,Ti)); a primary strengthening precipitate in Ni-based superalloys.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Ti substitutes for Al in the ordered L1₂ γ′ phase, increasing anti-phase boundary energy and thereby increasing precipitation strengthening efficiency; in Waspaloy, γ′ precipitation is very rapid during LMD (seconds), leading to 15–20% γ′ volume fraction after deposition and reheating by subsequent layers.
- **Property Effect:** In Waspaloy, rapid γ′ precipitation during AM processing induces compressive residual stresses at room temperature due to positive lattice misfit. The paper reports Waspaloy as-built normal stresses around −700 MPa in center/front lines and +150 MPa in side line; transversal stresses around −150 to −400 MPa. The paper does not separate the individual effect of Ti from Al or other γ′ forming elements.
- **Potential Side Effects:** Rapid γ′ formation in Waspaloy (positive misfit) can lead to a complex residual stress state after LMD. The paper states: “The γ′ phase has a negative lattice misfit with the matrix in most systems, but has a positive misfit for Waspaloy, leading to compressive stresses within the grains when cooled to RT. These compete with the thermal tensile stresses and result in a compressive residual stress state.” This suggests Ti (and possibly Al) contribute indirectly to compressive residual stress development, which may affect dimensional stability and service performance.
- **Original Evidence:** “Waspaloy is found to have a homogeneously distributed γ′ phase (globular particles with dark contrast) along the dendritic structures (light gray contrast) (Fig. 1a).” “This promotes the precipitation of γ’ + γ’’ strengthening phases by also retaining Laves phase to enhance the toughness, tensile strength, and plastic deformability at the same time.”

## Al

- **Element or Element Combination:** Al (aluminum)
- **Applicable Alloy System:** Both Waspaloy and IN718
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** Waspaloy 1.4 ± 0.2 wt.% Al; IN718 0.5 ± 0.3 wt.% Al
- **Main Function:** Forms γ′ phase Ni₃Al; oxidation resistance improvement (not specifically discussed in this paper, but briefly noted for context if relevant elsewhere); solid-solution strengthening in Ni superalloys (minor role).
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Al enters γ′ as L1₂ ordered phase; together with Ti stabilizes γ′; contributes to γ′ volume fraction and therefore to precipitation strengthening. The paper does not explicitly discuss the oxidation role of Al, so any oxidation effect is not backed by evidence from this paper.
- **Property Effect:** The paper does not isolate the contribution of Al. In combination with Ti, Al is essential for γ′ precipitation; e.g., in Waspaloy AB residual stress state is partly due to γ′ precipitates with volume fraction of 15–20% after LMD, contributing to compressive residual stress at RT. Specific quantitative property changes attributable solely to Al are not provided.
- **Potential Side Effects:** Not reported in this paper.
- **Original Evidence:** “Waspaloy, since it does not contain Nb element, the only strengthening phase is γ′.” The corresponding micromechanism is “precipitation of γ’ + γ’’ strengthening phases” during DA heat treatment.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Precipitation strengthening via γ′/γ″

- **Related elements:** Nb (for γ″), Ti and Al (for γ′)
- **Typical alloys:** IN718 (γ′ and γ″), Waspaloy (γ′)
- **Key compositions:** IN718 with 5.1 wt.% Nb; Waspaloy with 1.4 wt.% Al and 3 wt.% Ti
- **Microstructural changes:** In Waspaloy, rapid γ′ precipitation during LMD gives 15–20% volume fraction, homogeneously distributed globular particles; in IN718, γ′+γ″ precipitation occurs after DA (720°C/8 h + 620°C/8 h) and induces γ′+γ″ precipitates while retaining Laves phase
- **Property improvements:** IN718 DA yield strength increased from 544 MPa (AB) to 960 MPa at RT; from 360 to 810 MPa at 450°C; from 450 to 620 MPa at 650°C. Also, γ(220) lattice elastic modulus slightly higher in DA at RT (215 GPa vs 203 GPa) but comparable at 650°C. The paper reports that at 650°C, the AB condition yield stress (450 MPa) is higher than at 450°C (360 MPa), which may be due to the onset of γ′+γ″ precipitation during soaking before tensile testing; thus even AB IN718 can dynamically strengthen at 650°C.
- **Risks and limitations:** At high temperature (650°C), matrix softening becomes dominant, limiting strengthening; the paper states “the matrix softening mechanism becomes dominant at 650°C, resulting in an exponential strain increase.” Also, the effectiveness of γ′+γ″ resistance decreases at higher temperatures, and the paper notes that in AB condition at 650°C, dynamic precipitation may lead to microstructural instability and early plastic deformation.

## Precipitation strengthening via γ′ (Waspaloy)

- **Related elements:** Ti, Al
- **Typical alloys:** Waspaloy (powder LMD)
- **Key compositions:** 1.4 wt.% Al, 3 wt.% Ti
- **Microstructural changes:** Homogeneously distributed globular γ′ particles along dendritic structures; 15–20% γ′ volume fraction after LMD; γ′ has positive misfit in Waspaloy, leading to compressive intergranular residual stresses at RT.
- **Property improvements:** The paper does not provide tensile properties of Waspaloy; rather, it reports residual stress distribution and evolution. As-built Waspaloy normal stresses: center and front lines approx. −700 MPa; side line approx. +150 MPa; transversal stresses approx. −150 to −400 MPa. Hardly relaxes during in-situ heat treatment at 760°C/5.5 h.
- **Risks and limitations:** The paper reports: “Neutron diffraction in-situ heat treatment at 760°C has shown that residual stresses in LMD-Waspaloy hardly relax. This is related to the fact that the γ′ phase has already precipitated in the AB condition and hardly evolved in the subsequent thermal annealing.” Additionally, “increasing the holding time at high temperature may result in very limited stress relaxation in Waspaloy unless the heat treatment is carried out at higher temperatures.” Thus, Waspaloy AM components may possess high residual stresses that are not easily relaxed by conventional post-heat treatment at 760°C, which could be a concern for service stability.

## Laves phase formation

- **Related elements:** Nb, Mo, Fe, Cr, Si (more broadly); in this paper, primarily Nb with minor contributions from Mo
- **Typical alloys:** IN718 (as-built LMD)
- **Key compositions:** 5.1 wt.% Nb, 3.1 wt.% Mo
- **Microstructural changes:** Script-like Laves phase in interdendritic spacings in AB condition; this morphology appears when the sample is not exposed to Laves formation temperature for a long time. As exposure time increases, Laves phase becomes more globular.
- **Property improvements:** Not directly mentioned as providing a benefit, but the paper notes that retaining Laves phase after DA heat treatment is intended “to enhance the toughness, tensile strength, and plastic deformability at the same time,” although no quantitative data are provided to support this claim.
- **Risks and limitations:** Laves phase is known as a brittle phase; consuming Nb, it reduces available Nb for γ″ formation. The paper does not quantify this effect, but the presence of Laves as “the major secondary phase in as-built conditions” suggests it could limit as-built properties.

## Solid-solution strengthening

- **Related elements:** Cr, Mo, Co (and Fe) in Ni; not specifically quantified as solid-solution strengthening in this paper.
- **Typical alloys:** IN718 and Waspaloy
- **Key compositions:** Cr approx. 19 wt.% and Mo 3.1 wt.% in IN718; Cr approx. 19.5 wt.%, Mo 4.25 wt.%, Co 13.5 wt.% in Waspaloy.
- **Microstructural changes:** Not addressed explicitly in this paper; the paper focuses on precipitation, residual stress, and lattice strain evolution. The microstructural analysis does not include elemental partitioning in the γ matrix or lattice parameter measurements.
- **Property improvements:** Not explicitly quantified; the main mechanical differences were attributed to precipitation of γ′/γ″, not to solid-solution strengthening.
- **Risks and limitations:** Not addressed in this paper.

# Key Conclusions

This paper is a **methodology-focused paper on neutron diffraction at high temperature** applied to Ni-superalloys. Its conclusions are not about alloy design or composition–property relationships typical of refractory alloys. Instead, the key conclusions are:

1. **New induction heating setup at SALSA (ILL, Grenoble):** The induction coil can concentrate the magnetic field on the sample to achieve heating rates up to 50°C/s, which is higher than previous reports (up to 15°C/s). Such high rates were intended to limit microstructural evolution during ramping to the target temperature, allowing the use of room-temperature or extrapolated d₀ values.

2. **In-situ heat treatment of LMD-Waspaloy at 760°C:** Residual stresses in Waspaloy hardly relax during heat treatment at 760°C for 5.5 h. This is attributed to the fact that γ′ phase has already precipitated in the as-built condition and does not evolve significantly during subsequent thermal annealing. The paper suggests that increasing the holding time at high temperature may result in very limited stress relaxation unless the heat treatment is carried out at higher temperatures.

3. **Residual stress state of as-built Waspaloy:** The as-built residual stress distribution is not homogeneous; center and front lines are highly compressive in the normal direction (~−700 MPa), while the side line is tensile (~+150 MPa). The difference is attributed to the laser scanning strategy (perimeter vs internal filling). The compressive stress is associated with the positive misfit of γ′ in Waspaloy, competing with thermal tensile stresses.

4. **In-situ tensile characterization of LMD-IN718:** The γ(220) lattice strain response was compared with macroscopic strain. In AB condition at RT, the lattice strain continues linearly while the macroscopic strain increases exponentially beyond elastic limit; at 650°C, the lattice strain increases exponentially after yield, while the macroscopic strain remains linear-like. This is interpreted as: (i) γ(220) has higher stiffness than average in FCC; (ii) DA condition shows higher yield strength due to γ′+γ″ precipitates; (iii) at 650°C, matrix softening and dynamic precipitation of γ′+γ″ during tensile loading cause a transition at lower applied stress. The FWHM of γ(220) peaks increases linearly with applied stress in the elastic regime, then becomes constant, and finally increases exponentially when plasticity onset; the threshold is higher in the DA condition (950 MPa at RT) compared to AB condition (550 MPa at RT).

5. **The d₀ stress-free reference problem:** The paper discusses in detail the challenges of d₀ determination for in-situ high-temperature neutron diffraction. Using feedstock powder as d₀ reference can be problematic due to chemical differences between powder and printed material and non-equilibrium AM microstructure. The powder reference is considered acceptable for monitoring relative changes and for Waspaloy AB residual stress estimation; the compressive stress values were not inconsistent and were assumed to be an effect of γ′ precipitation.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

This paper is explicitly and exclusively focused on **Ni-based superalloys** (IN718 and Waspaloy) produced by **Laser Metal Deposition** (LMD). It does **not** study refractory high-temperature alloys, Nb-W-based alloys, Mo-W-based alloys, W-based alloys, refractory high-entropy alloys, refractory multi-principal element alloys, ODS alloys (except a brief mention in the introduction), or additively manufactured refractory alloys.

**If you require information on refractory alloys, this paper's data are not directly transferable.** Its value for a refractory-alloy database is limited to the following general concepts:

- A demonstration that induction heating at high heating rates can be used for in-situ neutron diffraction up to 760°C (and in principle higher), allowing characterization of residual stress and lattice strain without inducing significant microstructural changes during ramping.
- The methodology for using d₀ reference approaches at high temperatures, including the use of feedstock powder; this could potentially be applied to refractory alloy powders if chemical homogeneity between powder and printed material is verified.
- The observation that rapid precipitation during AM (e.g., γ′ in Waspaloy in seconds) may generate residual stresses that are not easily relaxed by subsequent heat treatments at moderate temperatures; this is a general concept potentially relevant to any rapidly solidifying alloy with fast precipitation, though not demonstrated for refractory systems.
- The analysis of elastic-to-plastic transition using FWHM evolution and lattice strain vs macroscopic strain can be applied to BCC refractory alloys as an experimental technique, but no refractory-specific data are provided in this paper.

**Critical limitations:**
- The paper lacks oxidation data, so no conclusion about oxidation resistance (Al₂O₃, Cr₂O₃, SiO₂, HfO₂, ZrO₂) can be derived.
- No alloy design guidance or composition optimization is provided for refractory alloys.
- No elevated-temperature mechanical properties beyond 650°C are reported (no 800°C, 1000°C, 1200°C, 1400°C, 1600°C+ data).
- Creep, fracture toughness, hardness, density, and elastic modulus of Waspaloy are not reported or only reported as constants taken from literature.
- The paper does not test refractory alloys.
- The paper relies on literature values for d₀ and DEC in some cases; for Waspaloy, E and ν were taken from cast samples (not AM-specific), which is a known limitation of the study.

---

**Bottom line:** Do not use this paper as a direct source of composition–property relationships for refractory Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloys. It has high value as a methodological reference for high-temperature neutron diffraction testing of AM alloys, but its material-specific data are restricted to Ni-superalloys IN718 and Waspaloy.
