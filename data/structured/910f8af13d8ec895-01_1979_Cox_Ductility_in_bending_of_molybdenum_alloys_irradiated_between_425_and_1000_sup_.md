# Paper Metadata

- **Title:** The Ductility in Bending of Molybdenum Alloys Irradiated Between 425 and 1000°C
- **Authors:** B. L. Cox and F. W. Wiffen
- **Journal:** Not explicitly stated in the provided text, but the paper appears to be from Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) work sponsored by the Office of Fusion Energy, U.S. Department of Energy
- **Year:** 1979
- **DOI:** Not provided in the original text
- **Alloy system studied:** Mo-based alloys (unalloyed Mo, Mo-0.5Ti, TZM)
- **Research objective:** To evaluate the effect of neutron irradiation at temperatures between 425 and 1000°C on the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) of molybdenum and molybdenum alloys, using three-point bending tests
- **Material category:** Mo-based alloys for fusion reactor first wall applications (Mo and Mo-Ti-Zr alloys), in the context of neutron-irradiated refractory alloys. Not ODS, not additively manufactured, not refractory high-entropy alloy.

---

# Alloy System and Research Object

The materials studied are molybdenum-based alloys intended for fusion reactor first wall and structural components. Three materials were investigated:
1. **Low-carbon arc-cast molybdenum (Mo)** – unalloyed base metal, annealed at 1200°C
2. **Mo-0.5% Ti** – alloyed with 0.5 wt% Ti, annealed at 1400°C
3. **TZM** – alloyed with 0.5 wt% Ti and 0.09 wt% Zr, annealed at 1500°C

The alloys are not refractory high-entropy alloys, not ODS alloys, and not additively manufactured; they are conventional molybdenum-based alloys produced by arc melting and recrystallized by annealing.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **Fabrication route:** Arc-cast (specifically described as “low-carbon arc-cast molybdenum” for the unalloyed Mo). The original paper does not describe whether Mo-0.5Ti and TZM were also arc-cast, but the context suggests conventional arc-melted molybdenum alloys.
- **Thermomechanical processing:** Sheet specimens approximately 0.4 mm thick were used.
- **Annealing:** Pre-irradiation annealing was performed at the temperatures listed in Table 1 (1200°C for Mo, 1400°C for Mo-0.5Ti, 1500°C for TZM) to produce fully recrystallized microstructures.
- **Specimen preparation:** Bend test specimens were cut to 2.5 × 3 mm with an abrasive saw and electropolished just before testing to produce a smooth surface on the tension side of the beam.
- **Irradiation conditions:** Irradiated in static gas-filled capsules in the EBR-II fast reactor (Row 2), with parameters given in Table 2:

| Temperature (°C) | T/Tm | Gas Atmosphere | Fluence (n/m², >0.1 MeV) | Displacement Level (dpa) | Helium Level (at. ppm) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 425 | 0.24 | He | 2.5 × 10²⁶ | 11 | 0.4 |
| 585 | 0.30 | He | 2.5 × 10²⁶ | 11 | 0.4 |
| 790 | 0.37 | Ar | 2.5 × 10²⁶ | 11 | 0.4 |
| 1000 | 0.44 | Ar | 4.4 × 10²⁶ | 19 | 0.7 |

The paper notes that the fusion reactor defect production rates are 7.5 dpa, 47 at. ppm He, and 95 at. ppm H per MW·y/m² of reactor operation.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Pre-irradiation state:** All three materials had fully recrystallized microstructures with a sheet texture; the smallest grain dimension was in the sheet thickness direction. The SEM fractography confirmed the recrystallized structure.
- **Base phase:** BCC molybdenum solid solution (in the alloys).
- **Second phases or precipitates:** Not explicitly discussed for the pre-irradiation condition. No carbides (TiC, ZrC), oxides, silicides, or borides are explicitly mentioned in the original paper.
- **Irradiation-induced microstructural changes (from Ref. 4, cited in this paper):** The dominant microstructural features after irradiation include:
  - **Voids:** The highest void concentration was found after irradiation at 585°C, with void concentrations decreasing in the order 425, 790, and 1000°C.
  - **Dislocation loops:** Very high concentration of small dislocation loops after irradiation at 425°C; loop concentrations after 425 and 585°C irradiation were too high to measure. Loops are expected to provide the major part of the strengthening.
  - **Dislocation networks:** Dislocation density decreased with increasing irradiation temperature and was higher in the two alloys than in unalloyed molybdenum.
- **Grain boundary features:** Grain boundary decohesion (separation) was a key fracture mode. The paper discusses whether alloying raises grain boundary decohesion strength relative to unalloyed Mo.
- **Elemental segregation:** Not explicitly stated.
- **Precipitate size/distribution:** Not relevant; no precipitate strengthening was discussed.
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed:** Irradiation-induced matrix hardening (void hardening, dislocation loop hardening, dislocation network hardening). The paper does not discuss solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, ODS, or grain refinement in the conventional sense.

# Mechanical Properties

The paper reports qualitative bend test results rather than quantitative tensile data. Key results:

- **DBTT (unirradiated):** Below room temperature for all three materials.
- **DBTT (irradiated, all temperatures):** Above room temperature for all irradiation temperatures.
- **DBTT as a function of irradiation temperature:**
  - 425°C irradiation: DBTT in the range of 50 to 200°C (implied from text; the paper says “425°C irradiation having produced a DBTT in the range of 50 to 200°C”).
  - 585°C irradiation: DBTT between 550 and 700°C.
  - 790°C irradiation: DBTT not explicitly stated as a precise range; from Fig. 2, the shaded DBTT zone for 790°C irradiation appears roughly between 200 and 500°C.
  - 1000°C irradiation: DBTT above 22°C but below 400°C (from earlier tensile tests on rod specimens from the same irradiation experiment, consistent with current results).
- **DBTT values from earlier tensile tests cited in the paper:** For a strain rate of 3 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹, irradiation at ~450°C resulted in a DBTT between 550 and 650°C (Ref. 2). Two rod specimens of molybdenum irradiated at ~1000°C showed DBTT above 22°C but below 400°C.
- **Webster et al. (Ref. 5) data:** For irradiation temperatures of 465 to 680°C, brittle failures at room temperature with elongation less than 0.4% in all cases and not measurable in most cases. DBTTs in the range of 450 to 720°C were inferred.
- **Test details:** Three-point bending with distance between supports of 2.03 mm (0.080 in.), maximum bend angle near 45°. Load-bearing points were tungsten wire of 0.51 mm (0.020 in.) diameter. Crosshead speed was 8.5 μm/s, corresponding to an approximate strain rate at the tensile surface of 4 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹. Specimens for elevated-temperature tests were held at temperature for 30 min before testing. Temperature uncertainty ±5°C. Vacuum was typically 3 mPa (2 × 10⁻⁵ torr).
- **Yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, elongation, fracture toughness, creep, rupture life, hardness, elastic modulus, density:** Not reported in the original paper. The paper states that load-deflection curves did not give useful yield point information for specimens that bent without breaking.
- **Fracture modes:** Cleavage, grain boundary decohesion/separation, and ductile tearing (dimple rupture). The transition with increasing test temperature goes from cleavage → grain boundary separation → ductile tearing.

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

- Oxidation resistance was not studied in this paper. No oxidation weight gain, oxide layer type, or oxidation temperature/duration is reported. The tests were performed in vacuum (3 mPa), not in oxidizing environments.
- The service stability concern in this paper is **neutron irradiation embrittlement**, not high-temperature oxidation.
- The paper concludes that molybdenum alloy structures in a fusion reactor could not survive a reactor shutdown because the DBTT is above room temperature for all irradiation temperatures investigated. Unless molybdenum alloys more resistant to irradiation embrittlement can be developed, they are unlikely to be usable for fusion reactor first wall or structural components in high-flux regions.
- **High-temperature embrittlement concerns:** The paper documents irradiation-induced embrittlement, with DBTT shifted to 550–700°C after 585°C irradiation. This is distinct from oxygen or nitrogen embrittlement; the embrittlement is irradiation-induced.
- **Grain-boundary issues:** Grain boundary decohesion is a significant fracture mode. The paper notes that molybdenum generally shows more grain boundary separation than Mo-0.5Ti or TZM at the same test temperature.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## [Titanium (Ti)]

**Applicable Alloy System:** Mo-based alloys (Mo-0.5Ti, TZM)

**Addition Amount or Composition Range:** 0.5 wt% Ti in Mo-0.5Ti and TZM

**Main Function:** Minor alloying addition; in the context of this paper, Ti appears to raise the grain boundary decohesion strength relative to unalloyed Mo, as indicated by the observation that Mo-0.5Ti showed 100% cleavage in unirradiated tensile tests at −196°C while unalloyed Mo showed 10–20% grain boundary separation plus cleavage.

**Microstructural Mechanism:** The original paper does not provide a detailed microstructural mechanism for Ti. The differences in fracture mode between Mo and the two Ti-containing alloys suggest alloying has raised the grain boundary decohesion stress, but the specific mechanism (e.g., grain boundary segregation) is not explicitly stated in the original paper.

**Property Effect:** For the two most embrittling irradiations (585°C and 790°C), the alloys (Mo-0.5Ti, TZM) had lower DBTTs than unalloyed Mo. The paper attributes this to the effect of alloying on raising grain boundary decohesion stress. However, the overall differences among the three materials were minor compared to the differences due to irradiation conditions.

**Potential Side Effects:** Not explicitly discussed in the original paper.

**Original Evidence:** “Mo-0.5 Ti showed 100% cleavage” in tensile tests of control specimens at −196°C while “molybdenum showed 10 to 20% grain boundary separation.” “The differences in fracture mode between the Mo and the two alloys suggests that alloying has raised the decohesion strength, relative to that of unalloyed Mo.” “For the two most embrittling irradiations, at 585 and 790°C, the alloys had lower DBTTs than did the unalloyed molybdenum.”

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## [Irradiation-Induced Matrix Hardening (Void Hardening, Dislocation Loop Hardening)]

**Related Elements:** Not applicable — this is a defect-induced mechanism, not an alloying-element mechanism.

**Typical Alloys:** Mo, Mo-0.5Ti, TZM (as studied in this paper)

**Key Compositions:** Any Mo-based alloy under fast neutron irradiation at 425–1000°C to ~11 dpa.

**Microstructural Changes:** Irradiation produces voids, small dislocation loops, and dislocation networks. After 425°C irradiation, a very high concentration of small dislocation loops dominates. After 585°C irradiation, void concentration is highest. Dislocation density decreases with increasing irradiation temperature. Loop concentrations after 425 and 585°C were too high to measure.

**Property Improvements:** Not applicable — this mechanism degrades ductility. It raises the flow stress and shifts the DBTT to higher temperatures.

**Risks and Limitations:** Irradiation-induced matrix hardening is the primary cause of embrittlement. Raising the flow stress shifts the intersection with brittle fracture stresses (cleavage stress, grain boundary decohesion stress) to higher temperatures, thus raising the DBTT. The most severe embrittlement was produced by irradiation at 585°C (DBTT 550–700°C), corresponding to the highest void density. After 425°C irradiation, the lower DBTT (50–200°C) is attributed to susceptibility to deformation channeling with high local ductility, since the dominant feature is a high concentration of small dislocation loops rather than voids.

**Original Evidence:** “The effect of irradiation temperature on the DBTT is thus achieved through the effectiveness of the matrix hardening produced at each temperature. Hence raising the flow stress shifts the intersection with the brittle fracture stresses to higher temperatures, thus raising the DBTT (Fig. 7).” “The highest void concentration was found after the irradiation at 585°C [4] with void concentrations decreasing in the order 425, 790, and 1000°C.”

## [Grain Boundary Decohesion Resistance (Alloying Effect)]

**Related Elements:** Ti, Zr

**Typical Alloys:** Mo-0.5Ti, TZM

**Key Compositions:** 0.5 wt% Ti (Mo-0.5Ti); 0.5 wt% Ti + 0.09 wt% Zr (TZM)

**Microstructural Changes:** The paper suggests that alloying raises the grain boundary decohesion stress relative to unalloyed Mo, but the specific microstructural mechanism is not elaborated.

**Property Improvements:** The alloys showed lower DBTTs than unalloyed Mo for the two most embrittling irradiations (585°C and 790°C). The effect is attributed to raising grain boundary decohesion stress.

**Risks and Limitations:** The overall effect of alloying was minor compared to the effect of irradiation temperature. The original paper explicitly states: “Alloying at the concentrations in the two alloys tested had a relatively minor effect on the DBTT.”

**Original Evidence:** “The differences in fracture mode between the Mo and the two alloys suggests that alloying has raised the decohesion strength, relative to that of unalloyed Mo.” “For the two most embrittling irradiations, at 585 and 790°C, the alloys had lower DBTTs than did the unalloyed molybdenum. This may be related to the effect of the alloying on raising grain boundary decohesion stress.”

## [Cleavage Stress Resistance]

**Related Elements:** Not applicable — intrinsic to bcc Mo.

**Typical Alloys:** Mo, Mo-0.5Ti, TZM

**Key Compositions:** All three materials studied.

**Microstructural Changes:** Cleavage fracture occurs mainly through larger-than-average grains; grain boundary separation occurs for grains of average or smaller than average size.

**Property Improvements:** The cleavage stress of molybdenum and molybdenum alloys is little affected by irradiation (from Ref. 2, cited in this paper). The cleavage stress in Mo and Mo-0.5Ti was previously found to be almost identical and essentially independent of temperature.

**Risks and Limitations:** Cleavage is the predominant failure mode at the lowest test temperatures. The cleavage stress is relatively temperature independent, while the grain boundary decohesion stress decreases with increasing temperature.

**Original Evidence:** “The cleavage stress of molybdenum and molybdenum alloys is little affected by irradiation [2].” “The cleavage stress in Mo and Mo-0.5% Ti was previously found to be almost identical, and essentially independent of temperature [2].”

# Key Conclusions

1. Irradiation of Mo, Mo-0.5% Ti, and TZM at temperatures from 425 to 1000°C to fluences producing 11 dpa resulted in DBTTs in bending of 100 to 700°C.
2. All irradiation temperatures resulted in DBTTs above room temperature; the exact DBTT level was a strong function of irradiation temperature.
3. The most severe embrittlement was produced by irradiation at 585°C, resulting in a DBTT between 550 and 700°C.
4. Alloying with Ti (and Zr in TZM) at the concentrations tested had a relatively minor effect on the DBTT overall, but for the two most embrittling irradiations (585 and 790°C), the alloys had lower DBTTs than unalloyed Mo, possibly due to raised grain boundary decohesion stress.
5. The DBTT shift with irradiation temperature could not be quantitatively related to the observed microstructures, though the highest void density at 585°C corresponds to the highest DBTT; the 425°C irradiated material showed a lower DBTT possibly due to dislocation loop-dominated hardening susceptible to channeling.
6. The increase in DBTT to above room temperature for all irradiation temperatures suggests that molybdenum alloy structures in a fusion reactor could not survive a reactor shutdown. Unless molybdenum alloys more resistant to irradiation embrittlement can be developed, they are unlikely to be usable for fusion reactor first wall or structural components in high-flux regions.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Test geometry limitation:** The available sample geometry restricted testing to three-point bending over a range of test temperatures; tensile tests were not performed in this work.
- **DBTT criterion limitation:** The criterion used to define brittle failure in this work is “far less restrictive than in many other investigations.” A test resulting in a crack but with some ductile failure at the tensile face was defined as being above the DBTT, in contrast to Webster et al. who defined brittle behavior as less than 50% reduction in area in a tensile test.
- **Comparison difficulty:** Differences in definition of “brittle fracture” and other experimental differences make comparison with other published results difficult.
- **Microstructure correlation limitation:** The DBTT shift with irradiation temperature could not be quantitatively related to the observed microstructures. Only qualitative comparisons were possible.
- **Helium effects not isolated:** The He levels produced in the experiment were low (0.4–0.7 at. ppm), well below anticipated fusion reactor levels (47 at. ppm per MW·y/m²). The observed embrittlement is therefore dominated by displacement damage; effects of higher He levels are not addressed.
- **Limited alloy range:** Only three materials (Mo, Mo-0.5Ti, TZM) were studied; the range of alloying additions is narrow, so effects of higher Ti/Zr concentrations or other alloying elements (e.g., Re, W, Nb) are not covered.
- **Fully recrystallized microstructure only:** All specimens had fully recrystallized microstructures before irradiation. The paper notes this “undoubtedly had a major influence on the high amount of grain boundary separation in many of the brittle failures,” but probably is not a determining factor between ductile or brittle behavior. Cold-worked or stress-relieved microstructures (which Webster et al. found to fail by transgranular cleavage rather than intergranular separation) were not tested in this work.
- **No quantitative tensile, creep, or fatigue data:** The paper does not report yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, elongation, fracture toughness, or creep performance. Only qualitative bend test results are provided.
- **Applicable scope:** The results apply to molybdenum and dilute Mo-Ti(-Zr) alloys in fully recrystallized condition, irradiated in a fast reactor at 425–1000°C to ~11 dpa, tested in bending in vacuum. The results are most relevant to fusion reactor first wall service where irradiation embrittlement is a primary concern.
