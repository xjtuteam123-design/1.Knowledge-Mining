# Paper Metadata

- **Title:** Microstructural stability and mechanical properties of advanced FeCrAl alloy cladding after thermal exposure at 800 °C ~1200 °C
- **Authors:** Xiao Qin, Ruiqian Zhang, Peinan Du, Jingyuan Pei, Qianfu Pan, Huiqun Liu, Yu Cao
- **Journal:** Journal of Nuclear Materials
- **Year:** 2025 (Volume 607, Article 155653)
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jnucmat.2025.155653
- **Alloy system studied:** FeCrAl alloy cladding (ferritic Fe-Cr-Al-Mo-Nb-Ti-Si)
- **Material category:** FeCrAl alloy cladding (accident-tolerant fuel cladding for light-water reactors)
- **Research objective:** To investigate the microstructural stability and mechanical properties of FeCrAl alloy cladding during isothermal exposure at 800 °C ~1200 °C for times up to 100 h, with emphasis on thermal stability of grains and Laves precipitates, grain growth kinetics, and degradation mechanisms of mechanical properties.

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system:** FeCrAl alloy cladding (annealed tube)
- **Nominal composition:** Fe-12.5Cr-3.8Al-1.8Mo-1Nb-0.2Ti-0.2Si (wt.%)
- **Cladding geometry:** Outer diameter ~9.5 mm, wall thickness ~0.35 mm
- **Initial condition:** Annealed at 800 °C for 1 h
- **Microstructure summary:** Recrystallized grains with an equivalent average size of 10 μm; nearly complete recrystallization (~94%); dominant γ-fiber texture (<111>//ND); Laves precipitates homogeneously dispersed within α-matrix and at grain boundaries.
- **Matrix phase:** Single-phase body-centered cubic (BCC) α-Fe (ferrite)
- **Second phase:** Fe₂Nb-type Laves precipitates, specifically (Fe,Cr)₂(Nb,Mo) stoichiometry

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

**Matrix phase:**
- Single-phase body-centered cubic (BCC) α-Fe (ferrite)
- Dilatometry confirmed no phase transition from 25 °C to 1175 °C
- JMatPro-V13 thermodynamic simulation predicted Laves precipitates below 1100 °C and Cr-rich phases below 600 °C

**Second phases/precipitates:**
- Fe₂Nb-type Laves precipitates with composition (Fe,Cr)₂(Nb,Mo)
- Initial area fraction: 4.55%
- Initial average particle size: 407 nm
- Laves precipitates were spherical or elliptical in the matrix; coarser precipitates at grain boundaries showed faceted morphology
- Laves precipitates preferentially nucleated at grain boundaries, especially at triple points (trifurcated grain boundaries), followed by coherent grain boundaries, and then within grains

**Grain size:**
- Initial (as-received): ~10 μm equivalent average grain size
- After 800–900 °C exposure: grain size remained stable (~12 μm) after 100 h
- After 1000 °C/100 h or 1100 °C/10 h: abnormal grain growth occurred
- Grain size regions categorized into: fine grains (~10 μm), coarse grains (~100 μm), abnormally large grains (up to 400 μm)

**Grain boundary features:**
- Nearly complete recrystallization (94%) in as-received condition
- High-angle boundaries defined as θ ≥ 15°
- Abnormally large grains showed dominance of <111> oriented (γ-fiber) grains

**Elemental segregation:**
- Laves precipitates were enriched in Mo, Nb, and Si
- Segregation at α/Laves interface showed gradual decrease of Fe and Cr concentrations and increase of Nb and Mo concentrations, indicating diffusion-controlled precipitation

**Precipitate size and distribution:**
- Initial: 407 nm average size, 4.55% area fraction
- At 800 °C: particle size remained stable at approximately 450 nm
- At 900 °C/100 h and 1000 °C/10 h: abnormal coarsening of Laves precipitates at grain boundaries
- Coarse Laves precipitates at trifurcated grain boundaries reached approximately 2.5 μm
- At 1000 °C and above: gradual dissolution of Laves precipitates

**Strengthening mechanisms discussed:**
- Solid-solution strengthening (matrix hardening, σₘ, including σ₀ friction stress and σₛ solution strengthening)
- Precipitation strengthening (σₚ, Ashby-Orowan equation)
- Grain boundary strengthening (σ_GB, Hall-Petch relationship)
- Dislocation strengthening (σ_d)
- The paper does not focus on oxide dispersion strengthening or additive manufacturing-oriented composition control

# Mechanical Properties

**Room-temperature tensile properties (as-received, AR):**
- 0.2% Yield Strength (YS): 491 ± 7 MPa
- Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS): 731 ± 10 MPa
- Total Elongation (TE): 40.5 ± 0.5%

**Room-temperature tensile properties after 10 h exposure:**
| Condition | 0.2% YS (MPa) | UTS (MPa) | TE (%) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|--------|
| 800 °C-10 h | 471 ± 8 | 701 ± 9 | 45.1 ± 0.1 |
| 900 °C-10 h | 461 ± 7 | 681 ± 8 | 54.0 ± 0.3 |
| 1000 °C-10 h | 451 ± 5 | 651 ± 7 | 48.3 ± 0.4 |
| 1100 °C-10 h | 441 ± 6 | 531 ± 7 | 20.8 ± 0.2 |

**Vickers hardness:**
- As-received: approximately 220 HV (initial hardness)
- After 800 °C and 900 °C annealing: approximately 210 HV (less affected by annealing time)
- After 1000–1200 °C annealing: drops sharply from 220 HV to approximately 160 HV (27% decrease)
- Hardness test load: 1 kgf, dwell time 12 s

**Work hardening response:**
- Work hardening decreases with increasing thermal exposure temperature
- The 1100 °C-10 h sample exhibited the lowest work hardening and elongation

**Tensile test conditions:**
- Room-temperature tensile test
- Nominal strain rate: 10⁻³ /s
- Standard: ASTM E8/E8M-21
- Tensile axis parallel to tube longitudinal direction
- Specimen length: 100 mm

**Elastic modulus / physical properties:**
- Shear modulus (μ): 81 GPa (room temperature)
- Poisson's ratio (ν): 0.3
- Burgers vector (b): 0.248 nm
- Lattice parameter (a): 0.287 nm
- Taylor factor (M): 3.0
- Calculated friction stress (σ₀): 21.6 MPa

**Strengthening mechanism calculations:**
- Solid solution strengthening contribution (σₛ): 343.8 MPa
- Hall-Petch coefficient (k_GB): 160 MPa/μm² (fitting) or ~500 MPa/μm² (from literature [13])
- Dislocation density: 9.77 × 10⁻¹² m⁻² (AR), 8.41 × 10⁻¹² m⁻² (800 °C-10 h), 6.07 × 10⁻¹² m⁻² (900 °C-10 h), 3.64 × 10⁻¹² m⁻² (1000 °C-10 h), 1.21 × 10⁻¹² m⁻² (1100 °C-10 h)
- Particle diameter (Dₚ): 401 ± 32 nm (AR), 431 ± 27 nm (800 °C-10 h), 491 ± 38 nm (900 °C-10 h), 1111 ± 67 nm (1000 °C-10 h), 601 ± 35 nm (1100 °C-10 h)
- Volume fraction of particles (fₚ): 10.5 ± 0.2% (AR), 14.3 ± 0.3% (800 °C-10 h), 11.2 ± 0.3% (900 °C-10 h), 2.6 ± 0.1% (1000 °C-10 h), 0.2 ± 0.11% (1100 °C-10 h)

**Fracture morphology:**
- As-received: typical dimple ductile behavior, most dimples below 2 μm, some large dimples occasionally observed
- 800 °C-10 h: dimples larger and deeper than AR
- 900 °C-10 h: uniform dimple size distribution, corresponding to maximum TE
- 1000 °C-10 h: flat dimples with Laves precipitates within dimples
- 1100 °C-10 h: lamellar cracking on entire fracture surface; significant change in fracture morphology

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

**Oxidation layer type:**
- Al₂O₃ (aluminum oxide) layer formed on the surface after thermal exposure at 1100 °C for 10 h
- EDS elemental map showed oxide layer rich in Al and O; literature reports it as Al₂O₃

**Oxidation temperature and duration:**
- Significant surface oxidation observed at 1100 °C for 10 h
- Surface darkened at higher annealing temperatures, indicating thicker oxide layer

**Effects of alloying elements on oxidation resistance:**
- Cr and Al in FeCrAl alloys improve high-temperature oxidation resistance by forming a protective oxide layer
- The Al₂O₃ layer formation consumes Al from the substrate, reducing solid solution strengthening

**Potential issues:**
- Oxidation of the surface leads to premature cracking of the oxide layer, promoting crack initiation and propagation
- Oxide layer delaminates from the substrate during tensile deformation
- Surface oxidation consumes alloying elements, resulting in reduced solid solution strengthening
- The paper does not explicitly mention oxygen embrittlement, nitrogen embrittlement, or high-temperature embrittlement, but the significant decrease in elongation at 1100 °C-10 h is attributed to the oxide layer formed by thermal exposure

**[Related Elements]**

**[Related Elements]**

**[Risks and Limitations]**
Laves precipitates are unstable at temperatures above 1000 °C; dissolution reduces precipitation strengthening and Zener pinning pressure; abnormal coarsening of Laves precipitates at grain boundaries can lead to grain boundary decohesion

# Key Conclusions

**(1) Initial Microstructure Stability:**
FeCrAl alloy cladding exhibits equiaxed grains of about 10 μm, thermally stable γ-fiber (<111>//ND) oriented grains, and homogeneously distributed Laves precipitates within the α-matrix. The cladding tube has a homogeneous microstructure in both longitudinal and cross-sectional planes.

**(2) Thermal Stability Temperature Limits:**
The thermal stability temperature of grains is approximately 1000 °C, while the thermal stability temperature of Laves precipitates is approximately 900 °C. Exposure to temperatures above these limits results in coarsening of grains and Laves precipitates.

**(3) Grain Growth Kinetics:**
The activation energy for grain growth (Q_g) of FeCrAl cladding is 483.3 kJ/mol, determined by Arrhenius fitting with grain growth exponent n = 2.

**(4) Laves Precipitate Evolution:**
The precipitation and dissolution of Laves precipitates are in a competitive relationship controlled by temperature and time. Laves precipitates preferentially grow at coherent and trifurcated grain boundaries, with a coherent orientation relationship between Laves precipitates within the matrix and the α matrix. Peak aging time is temperature dependent (800 °C for 1 h and 900 °C for 0.1 h).

**(5) Mechanical Property Degradation:**
After thermal exposure at 1100 °C for 10 h, the room-temperature tensile strength and elongation decrease from 734 MPa and 40% to 538 MPa and 21%, respectively. The degraded mechanical properties are attributed to coarse grains, dissolved Laves precipitates, and surface oxidation. Specifically, the 0.2% YS decreased from 491 MPa (AR) to 441 MPa (1100 °C-10 h).

**(6) UTS-Hardness Relationship:**
There is a linear relationship between UTS and hardness at room temperature, which can be used to predict the mechanical properties of FeCrAl cladding and avoid large-scale testing.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

**Limitations:**
- The paper focuses on only one nominal composition (Fe-12.5Cr-3.8Al-1.8Mo-1Nb-0.2Ti-0.2Si, wt.%); the effect of composition variations on microstructural stability is not addressed.
- The thermal exposure periods are limited to 100 h maximum, which may not fully represent long-term in-service behavior under accident scenarios.
- The study uses room-temperature mechanical properties as the primary indicator of degradation; high-temperature mechanical properties after thermal exposure are not directly tested.
- The paper does not quantitatively evaluate high-temperature strength (e.g., 800 °C, 1000 °C, 1200 °C) of the cladding after thermal exposure.
- Surface oxidation is not quantified (no weight gain data or oxide thickness measurements).
- Creep performance and fatigue behavior are not evaluated.
- The grain growth exponent n is assumed to be 2 based on theoretical consideration for pure metals or high temperatures, but this assumption is not experimentally verified.
- The initial thermomechanical processing history is referenced to previous work but not fully detailed in this paper.

**Applicable Scope:**
- FeCrAl alloy cladding for accident-tolerant fuel cladding in light-water reactors (LWRs)
- Thermal exposure conditions relevant to loss-of-coolant accidents (800–1200 °C)
- Microstructural stability and mechanical property degradation assessment
- The findings are specific to annealed FeCrAl cladding tubes with the studied composition and processing route; extrapolation to other FeCrAl compositions or geometries should be performed with caution.
