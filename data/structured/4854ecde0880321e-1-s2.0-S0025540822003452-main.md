- **Research objective**: To investigate the effects of Hf content on microstructure, mechanical properties, damping behavior, and strain recovery characteristics of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys, and to establish relationships between microstructure and mechanical/functional properties.
- **Material category**: Ti-Nb-based shape memory alloy (β-titanium shape memory alloy). # Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system**: Ti-Nb-Hf ternary shape memory alloy system, specifically Ti-22Nb-xHf with nominal Hf contents of 1 at.%, 2 at.%, and 3 at.%.
- **Phase constitution target**: β parent phase (BCC) + α″ martensite phase (orthorhombic).
- **Application orientation**: Ni-free biomedical superelastic alloys; low-temperature applications (cryogenics, space exploration); surgical stents; orthopedic implants.
- **Research focus**: The effect of Hf substitution for Ti on (1) phase constitution and phase stability via electron concentration (e/a), bond order (Bo), and metallic average d-electron orbital energy level (Md); (2) mechanical properties (yield stress, maximum fracture stress, elongation); (3) strain recovery characteristics (shape memory effect, superelasticity); (4) damping capacity (tan δ).

# Processing and Heat Treatment

1. **Melting**: Arc melting under Ar atmosphere from high-purity Ti, Nb, and Hf. The ingots were repeatedly melted at least six times to achieve compositional homogeneity.
2. **Homogenization**: 850 °C for 6 h.
3. **Hot rolling**: Homogenized ingots were hot-rolled into sheets with a thickness of 3 mm.
4. **Intermediate heat treatment**: Hot-rolled sheets were heat treated at 850 °C for 1 h.
5. **Cold rolling**: Cold rolling with a reduction of 70% (final thickness approximately 0.9 mm).
6. **Solution treatment**: Specimens were encapsulated in sealed quartz tubes under Ar protection and solution treated at 900 °C for 3 h.
7. **Cooling method**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper (presumably water quenching or air cooling after solution treatment, but the paper does not specify).

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phases**: α″ martensite phase (orthorhombic) and β parent phase (BCC) coexist in all Ti-Nb-Hf alloys, irrespective of Hf content.
- **Phase identification by XRD**: β phase identified by (110)β reflection; α″ martensite identified by (020), (112), (130), and (113) reflections.
- **Effect of Hf on phase volume fraction**: With increasing Hf content, the intensity of the (110)β peak gradually increases, while the intensity of α″ martensite peaks becomes weaker. This indicates that Ti substitution with Hf results in a reduction in the volume fraction of α″ martensite and an increase in the percentage of β parent phase.
- **Lattice constants of α″ martensite** (Table 2):

| Composition | a (nm) | b (nm) | c (nm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ti77Nb22Hf1 | 0.318 | 0.488 | 0.465 |
| Ti76Nb22Hf2 | 0.320 | 0.489 | 0.468 |
| Ti75Nb22Hf3 | 0.326 | 0.492 | 0.475 |

The increase in lattice parameters is attributed to the larger atomic radius of Hf compared with Ti.

- **Grain size**: Equiaxed grains characterized by lathy α″ martensite and featureless β parent phase. Hf addition refines the grain size slightly: from approximately 85 μm (1 at.% Hf) to 45 μm (3 at.% Hf).
- **ω phase**: Not directly observed by TEM or XRD in this study, but inferred from Bo-Md calculations. The Ti-Nb-Hf alloys first approach the β+ω/β boundary and then remain away from it as Hf content increases, implying that the amount of ω phase gradually increases and then decreases with increasing Hf content. The paper specifically attributes the higher strength of Ti76Nb22Hf2 (2 at.% Hf) to ω phase precipitation strengthening.
- **Elemental segregation**: Not explicitly discussed in the original paper.
- **Precipitate size and distribution**: Not explicitly characterized in the original paper (ω phase precipitates are inferred but not directly measured).

- **Trend in mechanical properties**: Both yield stress and maximum fracture stress first increase and then decrease with increasing Hf content, peaking at 2 at.% Hf. Elongation decreases monotonically from 45.5% (1 at.% Hf) to 37.3% (3 at.% Hf).
- **Elastic modulus**: Increases from 20.5 GPa (1 at.% Hf) to 75.3 GPa (3 at.% Hf). The paper suggests this is due to the higher volume fraction of ω phase in higher Hf alloys (E_ω > E_α′ > E_α″ ≈ E_β).
- **Fracture mode**: Ductile fracture characterized by many dimples in all Ti-Nb-Hf alloys. Some torn edges at the bottom of dimples are observed in higher Hf content alloys, consistent with the reduced elongation.
- **Strain hardening**: All alloys show ductility with larger elongation and higher strain hardening.

# Strain Recovery and Damping Characteristics

## Shape Memory Effect (SME)

- **Loading-unloading testing**: Pre-strains of 2%, 4%, 6%, and 8%.
- **Complete recovery**: All alloys can completely recover under 2% pre-strain, irrespective of Hf content.
- **Residual strain onset**: Residual strain appears at 4% pre-strain and becomes increasingly larger with increasing pre-strain.
- **Recoverable strain (εr)**: Increases with increasing pre-strain. The optimal recoverable strain of 4.7% is achieved at 6% pre-strain in Ti75Nb22Hf3 (3 at.% Hf).
- **Shape memory effect strain (εsme)**: Increases from 2% to 6% pre-strain, then declines when pre-strain exceeds 6%. The shape memory effect strain continuously decreases with increasing Hf content under the same pre-strain conditions, attributed to the lower volume fraction of martensite phase in higher Hf alloys. Maximum SME strain of 2.8% at 6% pre-strain in Ti77Nb22Hf1 (1 at.% Hf).

## Cyclic Loading-Unloading (Training)

- **Constant strain cycling**: 3% strain, loading-unloading cycles.
- **First cycle residual strain**: Decreases with increasing Hf content (from 1.01% to 0.97%).
- **Cycle evolution**: Residual strain gradually decreases with further cycling. Stress-strain curves show higher work hardening and smaller hysteresis loops after several cycles.
- **Full recovery**: All Ti-Nb-Hf alloys can fully recover after five loading-unloading cycles.

## Damping Capacity

- **Measurement conditions**: DMA, single cantilever clamp, frequency 0.5 Hz, oscillation amplitude 0.05%, step heating procedure with 5-min isothermal hold at each target temperature. Sample size: 30 mm × 4 mm × 1 mm.
- **Temperature-dependent damping (Fig. 3)**: A single damping capacity peak appears at approximately 200 °C within the heating range of -50 to 400 °C, attributed to reverse martensitic transformation. The reverse martensitic transformation peak temperature (Ap) slightly increases with increasing Hf content.
- **Martensitic transformation temperatures** (Table 3):

| Composition | As (°C) | Ap (°C) | Af (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ti77Nb22Hf1 | 107.92 | 198.5 | 315.95 |
| Ti76Nb22Hf2 | 114.36 | 204.06 | 314.02 |
| Ti75Nb22Hf3 | 123.38 | 208.75 | 308.71 |

- **Maximum damping capacity (temperature sweep)**: 0.0236, optimized by controlling Hf content.
- **Amplitude-dependent damping (Fig. 11a)**: Damping capacity first increases and then decreases with increasing amplitude. Maximum value obtained at approximately 200 μm amplitude. Maximum damping capacity is 0.03174 for Ti76Nb22Hf2 (2 at.% Hf), approaching the damping capacity (0.035) of Ti-Nb-Ta-Zr-O alloys.
- **Frequency-dependent damping (Fig. 11b)**: Damping capacity ranges from 0.0008 to 0.0035. The dependence suggests relaxation-type internal friction related to movement of grain boundaries, phase boundaries, twin interfaces, and dislocations. Different frequency dependence trends observed for different Hf contents.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Hf (Hafnium)

### [Applicable Alloy System]
Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys (β-Ti-based SMAs); also reported in Ti-Ta-Hf and Ti-Ni-Cu-Hf shape memory alloys.

### [Addition Amount or Composition Range]
1 at.% to 3 at.% Hf (nominal) in Ti-22Nb-xHf alloys. Actual Hf contents: 1.07 at.%, 1.80 at.%, and 2.51 at.%.

### [Main Function]
- Tailors phase constituents (β parent phase volume fraction vs. α″ martensite volume fraction) by controlling electron concentration (e/a), bond order (Bo), and metallic average d-electron orbital energy level (Md).
- Enhances strength via solid solution strengthening, grain refinement, and ω phase precipitation strengthening.
- Optimizes strain recovery characteristics and damping capacity.
- Increases elastic modulus.

### [Microstructural Mechanism]
- **Solid solution strengthening**: Hf has a larger atomic radius than Ti, increasing lattice parameters (a from 0.318→0.326 nm; b from 0.488→0.492 nm; c from 0.465→0.475 nm for α″ martensite), leading to lattice distortion and stronger solid solution strengthening.
- **Grain refinement**: Hf addition reduces grain size from approximately 85 μm (1 at.% Hf) to 45 μm (3 at.% Hf).
- **Phase fraction control**: Hf substitution for Ti reduces electron concentration from 4.1915 to 4.1850, which decreases the volume fraction of α″ martensite and increases β parent phase volume fraction. The paper notes that a single β phase exists when e/a > 4.20; the studied range (4.1850–4.1915) corresponds to α″ + β coexistence.
- **ω phase precipitation**: Bo-Md mapping suggests the amount of ω phase first increases and then decreases with increasing Hf content. The 2 at.% Hf alloy is inferred to contain the highest ω phase volume fraction, contributing to precipitation strengthening.

### [Property Effect]
- **Yield strength**: Increases from 374 MPa (1 at.% Hf) to 440 MPa (2 at.% Hf), then decreases to 395 MPa (3 at.% Hf).
- **Maximum fracture stress**: Increases from 515 MPa (1 at.% Hf) to 552 MPa (2 at.% Hf), then decreases to 490 MPa (3 at.% Hf).
- **Elongation**: Decreases from 45.5% (1 at.% Hf) to 37.3% (3 at.% Hf).
- **Elastic modulus**: Increases from 20.5 GPa (1 at.% Hf) to 75.3 GPa (3 at.% Hf).
- **Recoverable strain**: Maximum 4.7% at 6% pre-strain in 3 at.% Hf alloy.
- **SME strain**: Maximum 2.8% at 6% pre-strain in 1 at.% Hf alloy; decreases with increasing Hf content.
- **Damping capacity**: Maximum 0.03174 in 2 at.% Hf alloy (amplitude ~200 μm); maximum 0.0236 in temperature sweep.
- **Martensitic transformation temperature (Ap)**: Slightly increases from 198.5 °C (1 at.% Hf) to 208.75 °C (3 at.% Hf).

### [Potential Side Effects]
- Reduces volume fraction of α″ martensite, thereby reducing shape memory effect strain.
- Decreases maximum fracture strain (elongation) from 45.5% to 37.3%.
- Higher Hf content (3 at.%) leads to excess β phase volume fraction and lower ω phase volume fraction, causing a drop in strength.
- Significantly increases elastic modulus (from 20.5 to 75.3 GPa), which may reduce the superelastic strain window.

### [Original Evidence]
- Fig. 1 (optical micrographs showing grain refinement); Fig. 2 and Table 2 (XRD patterns and lattice constants); Fig. 4 and Fig. 5 (tensile curves and mechanical properties); Fig. 6 (Bo-Md mapping); Fig. 8 and Fig. 9 (strain recovery); Fig. 3 and Fig. 11 (damping); Table 3 (martensitic transformation temperatures).
- Key sentence: "A higher strength and larger recoverable strain can be obtained in Ti-Nb shape memory alloy modified by 2 at.% Hf due to grain refinement, precipitation strengthening and solution strengthening."

## Nb (Niobium)

### [Applicable Alloy System]
Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys (β-Ti-based SMAs).

### [Addition Amount or Composition Range]
Nominal 22 at.% Nb; actual Nb contents 19.15, 18.78, and 18.50 at.% across the three alloys. (Note: The Nb content decreases slightly as Hf increases, because Hf partially substitutes for Ti in the nominal composition.)

### [Main Function]
- β-phase stabilizer: Nb stabilizes the BCC β parent phase, enabling the β↔α″ martensitic transformation and superelasticity.
- Contributes to low elastic modulus and good biocompatibility.

### [Microstructural Mechanism]
- Nb raises the electron concentration (e/a), promoting β phase stability. The paper states that a single β phase exists when e/a > 4.20. The studied alloys with e/a = 4.1850–4.1915 lie in the α″+β two-phase region.
- The actual Nb content decreases with increasing Hf content, which reduces Nb's β-stabilizing effect and contributes to the slight increase in martensitic transformation temperature with Hf addition.

### [Property Effect]
- The paper does not isolate the independent effect of Nb variation. The observed increase in Ap with Hf addition is partially attributed to the accompanying reduction in actual Nb content: "It has been reported that the martensitic transformation temperature decreases with increasing Nb content."

### [Potential Side Effects]
- Not explicitly stated in the original paper.

### [Original Evidence]
- Table 1 (actual chemical composition); Section 3, discussion of electron concentration and martensitic transformation temperature.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid Solution Strengthening

### [Related Elements]
Hf (substituting for Ti)

### [Typical Alloys]
Ti-22Nb-xHf (x = 1, 2, 3 at.%)

### [Key Compositions]
1–3 at.% Hf addition

### [Microstructural Changes]
- Hf has a larger atomic radius than Ti, causing lattice expansion: α″ martensite lattice constants increase from a=0.318 nm, b=0.488 nm, c=0.465 nm (1 at.% Hf) to a=0.326 nm, b=0.492 nm, c=0.475 nm (3 at.% Hf).
- XRD peaks of (110)β and (112)α″, (113)α″ shift to lower diffraction angles with increasing Hf content, confirming lattice parameter increase.

### [Property Improvements]
- Contributes to the increase in yield strength from 374 MPa (1 at.% Hf) to 440 MPa (2 at.% Hf).
- Contributes to the increase in maximum fracture stress from 515 MPa to 552 MPa.

### [Risks and Limitations]
- Continued increase in Hf content (3 at.%) does not further increase strength; instead, strength drops, indicating that solid solution strengthening is offset by other microstructural factors (increased β phase volume fraction, reduced ω phase volume fraction).

### [Original Evidence]
- "First, the Hf addition has a strong solution strengthening effect in improving the mechanical properties of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys."
- Table 2; Fig. 2 (XRD peak shifts).

## Precipitation Strengthening (ω phase)

### [Related Elements]
Hf (indirectly, through Bo-Md parameter control)

### [Typical Alloys]
Ti-22Nb-xHf (x = 1, 2, 3 at.%)

### [Key Compositions]
2 at.% Hf appears to optimize ω phase precipitation strengthening.

### [Microstructural Changes]
- ω phase precipitates are inferred from Bo-Md mapping (Fig. 6). The amount of ω phase first increases and then decreases with increasing Hf content.
- The 2 at.% Hf alloy is expected to contain the highest ω phase volume fraction among the three alloys (closest approach to the β+ω/β boundary).

### [Property Improvements]
- Higher yield strength (440 MPa) and maximum fracture stress (552 MPa) in 2 at.% Hf alloy, attributed to ω phase precipitation strengthening.
- Higher elastic modulus with increasing Hf content (from 20.5 to 75.3 GPa) is attributed to the larger volume fraction of ω phase (E_ω > E_α′ > E_α″ ≈ E_β).

### [Risks and Limitations]
- Excessive Hf (3 at.%) leads to lower ω phase volume fraction and loss of precipitation strengthening, contributing to reduced strength.
- ω phase is not directly observed in this study; its presence is inferred from Bo-Md calculations.

### [Original Evidence]
- "the precipitation of the ω phase plays an important role in the superior mechanical strength"
- "the larger number of β parent phases and the lower volume fraction of ω precipitates in Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with 3 at.% Hf are the main factors in determining the lower mechanical strength"
- Fig. 6 (Bo-Md mapping).

## Work Hardening / Training-Induced Cyclic Stability

### [Related Elements]
Not element-specific; a microstructural mechanism related to dislocation multiplication and residual martensite.

All compositions (1–3 at.% Hf).

# Key Conclusions

1. **Phase constitution**: Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with 1–3 at.% Hf are composed of coexisting α″ martensite and β parent phase. The volume fraction of β parent phase increases with increasing Hf content, caused by decreasing electron concentration (e/a from 4.1915 to 4.1850).

2. **Mechanical properties**: Hf addition enhances strength and optimizes strain recovery characteristics. The highest yield strength (440 MPa) and maximum fracture stress (552 MPa) are achieved in Ti-22Nb-2Hf alloy, attributed to grain refinement, solution strengthening, and ω phase precipitation strengthening. Higher Hf content (3 at.%) reduces strength due to a larger β phase volume fraction and fewer ω precipitates. Elongation decreases from 45.5% to 37.3% with increasing Hf content.

3. **Strain recovery**: Under 2% pre-strain, all alloys show complete recovery. The maximum recoverable strain of 4.7% is achieved at 6% pre-strain in Ti-22Nb-3Hf, while the maximum SME strain of 2.8% is achieved at 6% pre-strain in Ti-22Nb-1Hf. Training (cyclic loading-unloading at 3% strain) enables perfect superelasticity with a fully recoverable strain of approximately 2% after five cycles in all alloys.

4. **Damping capacity**: The maximum damping capacity (tan δ) of 0.03174 is obtained in Ti-22Nb-2Hf (2 at.% Hf), which approaches the damping capacity (0.035) of Ti-Nb-Ta-Zr-O alloys.

5. **Elastic modulus**: Increases significantly with Hf content (from 20.5 GPa to 75.3 GPa), attributed to the increasing ω phase volume fraction (E_ω > E_α′ > E_α″ ≈ E_β).

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Alloy system scope**: This paper studies Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys for biomedical and functional applications. It is NOT a refractory high-temperature alloy study. It does not involve Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based, refractory high-entropy, or additively manufactured refractory alloys.
- **Temperature range**: All mechanical tests, strain recovery characterizations, and damping measurements (except the DMA temperature sweep up to 400 °C) are conducted at room temperature. No high-temperature strength, creep, or oxidation resistance data are provided.
- **ω phase characterization**: The presence and volume fraction of ω phase are inferred from Bo-Md calculations, not directly observed by TEM or XRD. This represents an indirect conclusion that should be treated with caution.
- **Mechanism of martensitic transformation temperature change**: The slight increase in Ap with Hf content is partially attributed to the accompanying decrease in actual Nb content, not solely to the direct effect of Hf. This conclusion is based on a cited reference rather than a direct experiment in this study.
- **Frequency dependence of damping**: The frequency-dependent damping data show different trends for different Hf contents, and the paper does not provide a comprehensive explanation for these differences; the relaxation-type internal friction is attributed to multiple possible sources (grain boundaries, phase boundaries, twin interfaces, dislocations) without quantitative decomposition.
- **Oxidation resistance**: Not addressed in this study.
- **ppm-level impurities**: Carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, and hydrogen contents are not reported, although oxygen content is known to affect ω phase formation and mechanical properties in Ti-Nb-based alloys (as cited in reference [35]).
- **Cooling method after solution treatment**: Not explicitly stated.
- **High-temperature embrittlement or oxygen embrittlement**: Not discussed.
- **Trade-offs identified**: Increasing Hf content (1) strengthens the alloy up to 2 at.% Hf but degrades strength at 3 at.% Hf; (2) reduces elongation; (3) reduces shape memory effect strain; (4) increases elastic modulus, which may affect the superelastic strain window; (5) slightly increases martensitic transformation temperatures.
