# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title:** Effect of processing parameters on the densification, microstructure and crystallographic texture during the laser powder bed fusion of pure tungsten
- **Authors:** A.T. Sidambe, Y. Tian, P.B. Prangnell, P. Fox
- **Journal:** International Journal of Refractory Metals & Hard Materials
- **Year:** 2019 (Volume 78, pages 254–263; available online 06 October 2018)
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijrmhm.2018.10.004
- **Material category:** Additively manufactured refractory alloy (pure tungsten fabricated by laser powder bed fusion, LPBF/SLM)
- **Alloy system studied:** Pure tungsten (W) processed by laser powder bed fusion

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system:** Pure tungsten (commercial purity W), fabricated by laser powder bed fusion (also referred to as selective laser melting, SLM).
- **Research objective:** The work aims to extend LPBF to refractory metals, specifically pure tungsten. The primary goal is to identify barriers that limit the effectiveness of LPBF processing of pure tungsten, and to evaluate relationships between process variables and part quality using process maps. Results are quantified in terms of part density, defects, microstructure, and crystallographic texture.
- **Motivation:** Tungsten possesses the highest melting point, highest density, and lowest thermal expansion coefficient of any pure metal, making it attractive for ultra-high temperature and radiation shielding applications. Conventional fabrication methods (casting, forging, machining) are limited by tungsten's high hardness, brittle nature, and high-temperature strength; LPBF can produce complex shapes with small dimensions not achievable by conventional techniques.

# Alloy Composition

- **Alloy type:** Pure tungsten, commercial purity.
- **Powder:** Plasma-spheroidised commercial purity tungsten powder supplied by Tekna Advanced Materials (Macon, France).
- **Chemical composition (wt.%):**
  - W: > 99.9
  - Al: 0.001
  - Ta: 0.003
  - Ti: 0.001
  - O: 0.009
  - Mo: 0.003
  - Residual: < 0.001
- **Impurity levels:** Oxygen is specified as 0.009 wt.% (low initial oxygen level). Other impurities (Al, Ta, Ti, Mo) are at ppm-level (0.001–0.003 wt.%).
- **Notably absent elements:** The paper does not use W alloys with Re, Zr, Hf, C, B, Y, La, or oxide dispersoids; the study focuses on pure tungsten only.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **Processing route:** Laser Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF), also referred to as Selective Laser Melting (SLM); additive manufacturing.
- **Equipment:** Renishaw AM125 LPBF system (Stone, UK).
- **Laser source:** High-powered ytterbium fibre laser, wavelength 1070 nm.
- **Laser parameters:**
  - Maximum laser power: 200 W (continuous wave mode).
  - Maximum scanning speed: 2000 mm/s.
  - Laser beam diameter: 43 μm at the target surface.
  - Constant laser power used in experiments: 200 W.
  - Constant exposure time: 200 μs.
  - Constant layer thickness: 50 μm.
- **Build atmosphere:** Argon atmosphere with initial residual oxygen content of < 1800 ppm (0.18%).
- **Substrate:** Commercially pure titanium substrate (used because a tungsten plate was not available; reduces energy required to melt the first few layers when the plate is cold).
- **Scan strategy:** ‘Snaking’ (meander) scan strategy; laser angle rotated by 67° between each layer.
- **Variable processing parameters:**
  - Hatch distance (spacing): 0.115 mm, 0.135 mm, 0.155 mm.
  - Point distance: 20 μm or 29 μm.
- **Sample naming convention:** S1HD1 to S2HD3, where S = scanning speed, HD = hatch distance.
- **Specific parameter sets and corresponding volumetric (3D) laser energy densities (J/mm³):**

| Specimen | Hatch Distance (mm) | Point Distance (μm) | Exposure Time (μs) | Apparent Scan Speed (mm/s) | Volumetric Energy Density (J/mm³) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1HD1 | 0.115 | 20 | 200 | 100 | 348 |
| S1HD2 | 0.135 | 20 | 200 | 100 | 296 |
| S1HD3 | 0.155 | 20 | 200 | 100 | 260 |
| S2HD1 | 0.115 | 29 | 200 | 145 | 240 |
| S2HD2 | 0.135 | 29 | 200 | 145 | 204 |
| S2HD3 | 0.155 | 29 | 200 | 145 | 179 |

- **Energy density formula:** The input laser energy density (3D specific energy input) was calculated as:
  - ℇ_Density = P_laser / (ν_scan × S_hatch × t_layer)
  - where P_laser = laser power, ν_scan = laser scan speed, S_hatch = hatch spacing, t_layer = layer thickness.
- **Specimen geometry:** Square section blocks measuring L = 10 mm, W = 10 mm, H = 5 mm; and L = 5 mm, W = 5 mm, H = 10 mm. For XCT, smaller pillars of 1 mm × 1 mm × 5 mm were sectioned from the test blocks.
- **Post-processing:** No heat treatment, HIP, forging, or rolling was applied. Specimens were analysed in the as-built condition. Specimens were sectioned, ground, polished to 1 μm using Al2O3 powder suspension for porosity and microstructure analysis. Electrolytic etching used 5% sodium hydroxide solution in a cooled electrolytic cell, at approximately −20 °C and 40 V.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase:** Body-centred cubic (bcc) tungsten, single-phase.
- **Second phases/precipitates:** No precipitates or second phases are reported. The material is pure tungsten.
- **Microstructure morphology:** Elongated columnar grain structure in the build direction, formed by epitaxial re-growth mechanism. Grains regrow epitaxially from the fusion boundary; growth direction controlled by melt pool shape and raster strategy.
- **Grain boundaries:** Presence of high angle grain boundaries (HAGBs, > 15°). Grain boundaries may act as crack initiation sites in this brittle material.
- **Cracking:** Micro cracks and defects observed in specimens due to micro- and macro-scale residual stress. Cracking occurred both at grain boundaries and as surface cracks. The cracking is attributed to residual stress build-up combined with tungsten’s high ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT; Table 1 lists 250–400 °C), high elevated-temperature strength, and low ductility below DBTT. Crack formation is linked to tungsten’s bcc lattice and partial covalent character of bonding.

**Grain size:**
- Average grain size (diameter) in x–z build plane: 11.8 μm for S1HD1 (higher energy density, 348 J/mm³); 11.3 μm for S1HD3 (lower energy density, 260 J/mm³).
- Majority of grains had diameter below 10 μm in both specimens.
- Maximum grain diameter: 153 μm for S1HD1; 137 μm for S1HD3.
- Increasing hatch distance to 155 μm (decreasing laser energy density) led to coarser columnar grains, suggesting that the maximum grain diameter is influenced by the melt-pool overlap between neighbouring tracks; when hatch spacing is increased, re-melted grains from previous pass grow into melt pool from next track to a distance controlled by re-melted width.

**Crystallographic texture:**
- Strong <111>//Z fibre texture (parallel to build direction), maximum intensity of 10 times random for S1HD1.
- For S1HD3, a stronger single component with 111 pole also aligned close to Y in transverse direction; described as {111}<011> using build reference frame (though this may be coincidental due to large grain size relative to EBSD map area).
- The <111>//Z fibre texture is unusual; BCC metals generally show <100> growth direction during solidification, leading to <001>//Z fibre texture in powder bed AM. The origin of this texture is unclear and requires further investigation. The paper postulates that tungsten’s high thermal conductivity and high liquid surface tension produce a more bowl-shaped melt pool with more inclined solidification front relative to build direction (Z), and combined with the 67° rotation between layers, this may explain the unusual texture. The angle between {111} planes in BCC metals (~70°) is close to the 67° rotation angle, which may be relevant in selecting the stronger single {111}<011> component seen in S1HD3.

**Elemental segregation:** Not explicitly discussed in the original paper (pure tungsten, no alloying elements for segregation analysis).

**Strengthening mechanisms discussed:** The paper does not discuss alloy-based strengthening mechanisms such as solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, or dispersion strengthening. It discusses processing-related microstructural control (columnar grain structure, crystallographic texture, grain size) and the role of residual stress and DBTT in cracking.

# Mechanical Properties

- **Room-temperature mechanical properties:** Not measured in this study. The paper explicitly states: “In order to ascertain whether these densification levels are suitable for parts that can be used in high performance applications, suitable mechanical testing and performance evaluation would need to be carried out, and this has not been done in this study.”
- **High-temperature strength:** Not measured in this study.
- **Yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, compressive strength, elongation, compressive plasticity, fracture toughness, creep performance, rupture life, hardness, elastic modulus:** Not measured in this study.
- **Density:**
  - Bulk density (Archimedes method): Relative density ranged between 95% and 97%.
  - Optically determined density (ODD): Ranged from 94 to 98% under different process conditions.
  - Maximum relative density achieved: 98% (limited by available beam power and process conditions).
  - Reference density of tungsten at 25 °C: 19.2 g/cm³ (cited from Table 1).
  - Liquid density: 17.6 g/cm³ (Table 1).
- **Densification trend:** Density increased with increasing laser energy density, up to ~350 J/mm³, showing clear correlation between measured density and increasing laser energy density. Beyond this, it is probably not possible to eliminate the final 2% of defects with the current setup.
- **Melting point:** 3422 °C (Table 1).
- **Thermal conductivity:** 174 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹ (Table 1).

- **Surface tension force:** 2.361 N/m (Table 1).
- **Ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT):** 250–400 °C (Table 1). Below 250 °C tungsten does not normally show plastic deformation; significant ductile behaviour usually only occurs above 400 °C.

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

- **Oxidation resistance:** Not directly measured in this study (no oxidation weight gain, oxidation layer type, or oxidation temperature data are reported).
- However, the paper notes that oxidation is a phenomenon that can hinder successful tungsten LPBF processing and “must be avoided” (cited from previous studies [12, 21–24]). The paper references prior work indicating that oxidation has been identified as a concern in LPBF of tungsten.
- The build was conducted in an argon atmosphere with initial residual oxygen content of < 1800 ppm (0.18%), suggesting environmental control is important to avoid oxygen pickup during processing.
- **Service stability issues discussed:**
  - High-temperature embrittlement: Tungsten’s DBTT well above room temperature (250–400 °C) makes it susceptible to cracking from residual stress build-up during LPBF.
  - Oxygen embrittlement: Not explicitly discussed as an embrittlement mechanism for the as-built material, though oxidation is noted as a processing concern.
  - Grain-boundary oxidation: Not discussed.
  - Nitrogen embrittlement: Not discussed.

**[W] (Pure Tungsten)**

- **Applicable Alloy System:** Pure tungsten processed by LPBF/SLM (refractory metal additive manufacturing).
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range:** > 99.9 wt.% W (commercial purity).
- **Main Function:** Base metal for ultra-high temperature and radiation shielding applications; highest melting point (3422 °C), highest density (19.2 g/cm³), and lowest thermal expansion coefficient of any pure metal.
- **Microstructural Mechanism:** Body-centred cubic (bcc) lattice with partial covalent character to bonding, which leads to a DBTT well above room temperature (250–400 °C). High thermal conductivity (174 W·m⁻¹·K⁻¹) and high liquid surface tension (2.361 N/m) are expected to produce a relatively deep, bowl-shaped melt pool, which influences the crystallographic texture.

The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for strength or ductility.
- **Potential Side Effects:** Susceptible to cracking from residual stress build-up due to high elevated-temperature strength and low ductility below DBTT; micro-cracking is the most likely method to relieve strain during LPBF cooling; columnar grain structure and strong texture may produce anisotropy in mechanical properties; high thermal conductivity may result in greater variability in local thermal conditions during large-area scanning; oxidation during processing can hinder successful LPBF tungsten processing.

# Key Conclusions

1. **Process feasibility:** LPBF can be used to melt pure tungsten powder and produce functional parts. Test samples were fabricated with satisfactory visual quality using laser energy densities ranging from 179 to 348 J/mm³.

2. **Densification:** Density increased with increasing laser energy density, with relative densities reaching up to 98% at approximately 350 J/mm³. Beyond this, it is probably not possible to eliminate the final 2% of defects with the beam power and process conditions available. Bulk densities (Archimedes) ranged 95–97%; optically determined densities ranged 94–98%.

3. **Defects:** Micro cracks and defects were present due to micro- and macro-scale residual stress. X-ray computed tomography showed irregular lack-of-fusion flaws, porosity, and cracking along grain boundaries. The volume fraction of defects appeared to increase with build height in the higher-energy-density sample (S1HD1), indicating that internal stresses can vary with position relative to build height. The sample fabricated with lower energy density (S1HD3, 260 J/mm³) had a larger volume of pores and defects compared to S1HD1 (348 J/mm³).

4. **Microstructure:** A columnar grain structure was generated by an epitaxial re-growth mechanism, as noted in other AM processes with pure metals. Average grain size was 11.3–11.8 μm; maximum grain diameter was 137–153 μm. Increasing hatch distance (decreasing energy density) led to coarser columnar grains.

5. **Crystallographic texture:** Strong <111>//Z fibre texture (parallel to build direction), maximum intensity of 10 times random for S1HD1. This is unusual for BCC metals, which typically show <100>//Z fibre texture in powder bed AM. The texture is postulated to be related to tungsten’s high thermal conductivity and surface tension producing a deeper, bowl-shaped melt pool, combined with the 67° raster rotation between layers.

6. **Cracking mechanism:** Surface cracks and micro-cracks are unlikely to be solidification cracks; they are linked to residual stress build-up, tungsten’s high elevated-temperature strength, and low ductility below its DBTT (250–400 °C). Tungsten’s high yield strength at temperature reduces plastic relaxation upon cooling, and the bcc lattice with partially covalent bonding leads to a DBTT well above room temperature.

7. **Application potential:** The density and quality of the W specimens are sufficiently high for applications such as medical radiation shielding, nuclear imaging (W collimators), and other plasma-facing environments. LPBF of tungsten offers advantages for producing components with high complexity and accuracy that are impossible to produce using conventional methods.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. **No mechanical testing:** This study did not measure any mechanical properties (yield strength, UTS, elongation, hardness, fracture toughness, creep, etc.). The authors explicitly state that suitable mechanical testing would need to be carried out for high-performance applications.

2. **Relative density ceiling:** The maximum achieved relative density was 98%; the final 2% of defects could not be eliminated with the available 200 W laser power and beam diameter (43 μm). Higher laser energy density, finer powder, a narrower particle size distribution, and reduced layer thickness are suggested as potential means to reduce defects further.

3. **XCT resolution limitation:** Because of tungsten’s high density and X-ray absorption, the effective XCT resolution was limited to defects/porosity/cracks > 10 μm in thickness. Defects smaller than this could not be resolved.

4. **Cracking remains unsolved:** Despite achieving up to 98% density, cracking due to residual stress and tungsten’s high DBTT remains a significant issue. The paper does not propose a demonstrated solution (e.g., substrate heating, alloying, or post-process heat treatment) to eliminate cracking. Preheating or alloying to lower DBTT are not explored.

5. **Texture origin not confirmed:** The unusual <111>//Z fibre texture is attributed to a postulate (deep melt pool + 67° raster rotation), but the origin is not experimentally verified. The authors state that the origin of the different texture is currently unclear and needs further investigation.

6. **Single material system:** The study is limited to pure tungsten. No W alloys (e.g., W-Re, W-Ni-Fe, W-Ta, W-Mo, or carbide/oxide dispersion-strengthened W) were investigated. Conclusions about processing behaviour, texture, and cracking cannot be directly extended to W alloys or other refractory alloys without further investigation.

7. **Substrate effect not systematically studied:** A commercially pure Ti substrate was used because a W plate was unavailable. The paper notes that substrate thermal properties significantly affect melt pool size and solidification rate, but this was not systematically varied in this study.

8. **Oxidation issues not quantified:** The initial powder oxygen content (0.009 wt.%) and build atmosphere oxygen level (< 1800 ppm) are reported, but no post-build oxygen analysis of the specimens is provided. The impact of oxygen pickup during LPBF on final part properties is not quantified.

9. **Post-processing not explored:** No heat treatment, HIP, or surface finishing procedures were applied or investigated. The paper notes that recrystallization-type heat treatments are unlikely to be effective for grain refinement in near-net-shape LPBF tungsten because high dislocation density cannot be introduced, and tungsten lacks phase transformations.

10. **Applicability to other refractory alloys:** Caution is needed when extrapolating these findings to Nb-based, Mo-based, W-based, or refractory multi-principal element alloys. The paper does not discuss solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, ODS, or oxidation-resistant alloying; it is a process parameter study for pure tungsten LPBF only.
