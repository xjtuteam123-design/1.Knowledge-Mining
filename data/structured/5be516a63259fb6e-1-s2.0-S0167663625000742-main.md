# Processing and Heat Treatment

Not applicable in the conventional sense. This is a computational simulation study using molecular dynamics:

- **Simulation code:** LAMMPS
- **Interatomic potential:** Embedded Atom Method (EAM) potential developed by Mishin (2004) for the Ni-Al system
- **Ensemble:** NVE
- **Time step:** 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ s (1 fs)
- **Boundary conditions:** Free boundary conditions on all three axes
- **Simulation temperature:** 0.01 K (near absolute zero to suppress thermally activated dislocation climb)
- **Relaxation:** 100,000 timesteps (100 ps) after energy minimization by conjugate gradient (CG) method
- **Shear strain rate:** ±1 × 10⁸ s⁻¹ for both loading and unloading
- **Maximum shear strain:** γ_yz = 2%
- **Cycle duration:** 400 ps (200 ps loading + 200 ps unloading)

**No arc melting, powder metallurgy, additive manufacturing, homogenization, rolling, forging, annealing, or aging processes are described.**

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Matrix and Precipitate Phases

- **γ matrix phase:** FCC Ni
- **γ′ precipitate phase:** L1₂ ordered Ni₃Al
- **Interfacial dislocation network:** 〈100〉/〈100〉 cubic dislocation network at the γ/γ′ interface due to the 1.4% lattice mismatch

## Model Configurations

Two atomistic model geometries were constructed:

**Model A:** Half γ phase / half γ′ phase configuration
- Purpose: Study dislocation interaction with the central region of the γ′ phase
- Box dimensions: ~40 nm (X), 50 nm (Y), 30 nm (Z)
- Crystallographic orientations: X = [2̄11̄], Y = [01̄1], Z = [111]
- Atom count: ~5,400,000

**Model B:** 10 nm γ phase channel embedded within γ′ phase
- Purpose: Study dislocation interaction with γ phase channel regions
- Box dimensions: ~40 nm (X), 50 nm (Y), 30 nm (Z)
- Crystallographic orientations: X = [2̄11̄], Y = [01̄1], Z = [111]
- Atom count: ~5,400,000
- Notes: Channel width (10 nm) is smaller than real alloys (30–60 nm) but within the same order of magnitude

## Dislocation Structures Observed

- **Perfect screw dislocations** initially introduced in the γ phase, which rapidly dissociate into **two Shockley partials** due to low stacking fault energy
- **Interfacial dislocation network** (existing from lattice mismatch)
- **Lomer-Cottrell lock structures** formed at the γ/γ′ interface
- **Stacking fault (SF) bands** formed in the γ phase channels
- **Orowan loops** observed forming around large γ′ precipitates
- Dislocation coloring (DXA convention): Blue = perfect dislocations, Green = Shockley partials, Purple = stair-rod partials

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

The following strengthening mechanisms are discussed in the context of cyclic strengthening (fatigue) of Ni-based single crystal superalloys, **not** refractory alloys:

### 1. Interfacial Dislocation Network Strengthening
- **Related elements:** N/A (structural feature of γ/γ′ interface)
- **Typical alloys:** Ni-based single crystal superalloys
- **Key compositions:** Ni/Ni₃Al system with 1.4% lattice mismatch
- **Microstructural changes:** 〈100〉/〈100〉 cubic dislocation network at the γ/γ′ interface; formation of stable link points; decomposition of interfacial dislocations into SF bands and Lomer-Cottrell locks
- **Property improvements:** Enhanced dislocation deposition capacity; impedes dislocations attempting to penetrate γ′ phase; provides cyclic strengthening
- **Risks and limitations:** Under prolonged cyclic loading, interfacial dislocation network can decompose; actual multi-element effects not captured in simplified model

### 2. γ′ Phase Obstruction Strengthening
- **Related elements:** Ni, Al (as ordered Ni₃Al)
- **Typical alloys:** Ni-based single crystal superalloys
- **Key compositions:** L1₂ ordered Ni₃Al precipitates
- **Microstructural changes:** High potential energy of γ′ phase impedes dislocation entry; stress stratification at γ/γ′ interface; leading Shockley partial can enter but trailing partial is stress-neutralized
- **Property improvements:** Traps dislocations at γ/γ′ interface; prevents dislocation penetration into γ′ phase
- **Risks and limitations:** Simulation at 0.01 K suppresses dislocation climb; real alloys have more complex behavior

### 3. Dislocation Pile-up (Barrier Formation) Strengthening in γ Channels
- **Related elements:** N/A (structural feature)
- **Typical alloys:** Ni-based single crystal superalloys
- **Key compositions:** Narrow γ phase channels between γ′ precipitates
- **Microstructural changes:** Two forms of dislocation barriers: (1) SF bands and Lomer-Cottrell locks from decomposition of interfacial dislocation network; (2) SF bands from decomposition of emission dislocations within γ phase channel
- **Property improvements:** Hinders subsequent dislocation motion; σ*_pile (yz component) reaches up to 120 MPa for most strongly hindered configuration
- **Risks and limitations:** Simplified model does not capture multi-element solid solution effects; dislocation climb suppressed at 0.01 K

### 4. Slip Plane Transition Mechanism ({111} → {100})
- **Related elements:** N/A (crystallographic mechanism)
- **Typical alloys:** Ni-based single crystal superalloys
- **Key compositions:** FCC γ phase with L1₂ γ′ precipitates
- **Microstructural changes:** When emission dislocations encounter the interfacial dislocation network, two Shockley partials on {111} planes recombine into a perfect dislocation on {100} plane via the reaction: a/6⟨211⟩{111} + a/6⟨12̄1⟩{111} → a/2⟨110⟩{100}
- **Property improvements:** The perfect dislocation on {100} plane can be more effectively trapped at the γ/γ′ interface; increases cyclic strengthening
- **Risks and limitations:** This mechanism requires interaction with the interfacial dislocation network; without such interaction, the slip plane transition does not occur

### 5. Cross-slip Trapping After Unloading
- **Related elements:** N/A (dislocation mechanism)
- **Typical alloys:** Ni-based single crystal superalloys
- **Key compositions:** N/A
- **Microstructural changes:** During unloading, dislocations entering γ′ phase experience cross-slip from {111} to {1̄11} plane due to stress stratification at γ/γ′ interface
- **Property improvements:** Prevents dislocations from detaching from γ/γ′ interface; contributes to cyclic strengthening
- **Risks and limitations:** Not explicitly stated in the original paper

# Key Conclusions

1. **The interfacial dislocation network in Ni-based single crystal superalloys exhibits excellent capacity for dislocation deposition**, particularly for dislocations attempting to penetrate the γ′ phase. This operates through two mechanisms: (a) formation of stable link points at the γ/γ′ interface from reactions between emission dislocations and the interfacial dislocation network, and (b) obstructive effect of the γ′ phase on dislocation movement.

2. **A slip plane transition from {111} to {100} was captured** when emission dislocations encounter the interfacial dislocation network, involving the reaction: a/6⟨211⟩{111} + a/6⟨12̄1⟩{111} → a/2⟨110⟩{100}.

3. **Stress stratification at the γ/γ′ interface** was observed: atoms on the γ phase side experience negative shear stress, while those on the γ′ side experience positive shear stress. This causes the trailing Shockley partial to be stress-neutralized and unable to enter γ′ phase. During unloading, the leading dislocation cross-slips, trapping dislocations at the interface.

4. **Two distinct forms of dislocation barrier pile-up in the γ phase** were identified: (a) reactions between emission dislocations and the interfacial dislocation network leading to SF band and Lomer-Cottrell lock formation from interfacial dislocation decomposition; (b) SF band formation from decomposition of emission dislocations moving within the γ phase channels.

5. **The plastic stress from dislocation pile-up (σ*_pile, yz component)** reaches up to 120 MPa for the most strongly hindered dislocation configuration.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

## Limitations Stated in Original Paper

1. **Simplified alloy composition:** The study uses the Ni/Ni₃Al binary system, neglecting the multiple solid solute elements present in actual Ni-based single crystal superalloys. This simplification overlooks TCP phase precipitation and other multi-element effects.

2. **Low simulation temperature (0.01 K):** Suppresses dislocation climb behavior, whereas cyclic strengthening mechanisms in real Ni-based single crystal superalloys at service temperatures are likely more complex.

3. **Assumption of dislocation origin:** The study constructs dislocations only in the γ matrix phase, assuming dislocations predominantly originate from γ. It cannot evaluate the impact of superdislocations formed within the γ′ phase on cyclic plasticity.

4. **Free boundary conditions:** Used because the models do not satisfy symmetry requirements for periodic boundary conditions. This causes minor local stress concentrations, though the paper states the impact on results is minimal.

5. **Smaller γ channel width:** The 10 nm channel width in simulations is smaller than the 30–60 nm width in actual alloys, though within the same order of magnitude.

6. **Dislocation creation method affects stress magnitude:** The dislocation insertion method causes discrepancies between initial stress at the end of the first dislocation emission and the beginning of the second dislocation emission.
