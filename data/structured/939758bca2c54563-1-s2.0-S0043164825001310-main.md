# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **Crystal growth**: DD6 was grown in accordance with the (100) crystallographic direction.
- **Orientation verification**: The (110) and (111) orientations were tested by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker, USA) using 40 kV and 40 mA with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm) to determine the angle of offset from the (100) crystallographic direction.
- **Cutting**: Wire cutting was used to cut (110) and (111) crystal planes with a cutting accuracy of 5° or less. After cutting, XRD was used to verify crystal plane orientation.
- **Sample dimensions**: 10 mm × 10 mm × 3 mm
- **Surface preparation**: Chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP) using UNIPOL-1200S polishing device (Shenyang Kejing Auto-instrument Co., Ltd., China) to achieve surface roughness of less than 5 nm.
- **Cleaning**: All samples were sonicated in ethanol for 5 min, rinsed with deionized water, and dried with nitrogen gas.
- **No information** on arc melting, vacuum melting, powder metallurgy, additive manufacturing, homogenization, rolling, forging, annealing, aging, or specific heat treatment parameters is provided because this is a study on an as-received commercial single crystal alloy using crystallographic orientation selection rather than processing route development.

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

**Oxidation/wear-induced surface layer evolution at high temperatures on (100) plane:**
- EDS analysis of wear tracks (Fig. 7a–c): O, Ni, Al, Si, and Cr detected on all surfaces. Si is attributed to debris from Si₃N₄ balls; absence of N suggests wear debris oxidized to SiO₂.
- As temperature increases (RT → 300 °C → 600 °C), surface oxygen content gradually rises, indicating enhanced oxidation of NBSC superalloy.
- Ni content decreases, while Al and Cr contents remain relatively unchanged, suggesting the primary component of the surface oxide layer is **nickel oxide**.
- The oxide layer, formed under high temperature and pressure, evolves into a dense **glaze layer** during continuous friction, acting as a solid lubricant and reducing COF.

**Wear mechanisms across temperatures:**
- All temperatures: combination of abrasive wear, adhesive wear, and oxidative wear.
- RT (Fig. 7d): dominated by furrow-like scars from abrasive wear; minor adhesive wear (white bright spots).
- 300 °C (Fig. 7e): adhesive wear dominant (many white bright spots).
- 600 °C (Fig. 7f): glaze layer formation significantly reduces COF; oxidative wear becomes predominant; abrasive and adhesive wear reduced.

**Glaze layer cross-section observations (TEM):**
- RT: inhomogeneous surface layer ~0.61 μm thick on wear track; Ni and Al content basically same as substrate; slight change in oxygen content.
- 600 °C: homogeneous white material ~0.17 μm thick (glaze layer); increased Si, Al, O signals and decreased Ni signal; glaze is probably an oxidized mixture of Si, Ni, and Al.
- Wear debris is continuously melted and compacted, forming a non-uniform glaze layer; because glaze layers are unevenly distributed and not completely removed, wear depth variations occur.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

The paper does not focus on strengthening mechanisms (solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, ODS, grain-boundary strengthening, etc.). It focuses on **crystallographic anisotropy-dependent wear mechanisms** rather than alloy strengthening design. The relevant mechanistic discussion is as follows:

**Mechanism: Crystal slip system orientation-dependent wear (anisotropic wear resistance)**  
- **Related elements**: Not element-specific; related to crystal orientation and slip system geometry.  
- **Typical alloys**: DD6 Ni-based single crystal superalloy  
- **Key compositions**: DD6 nominal composition (see Alloy Composition table).  
- **Microstructural changes**: Slip systems and their orientation relative to sliding direction govern localized plastic deformation during wear.  
  - (100) plane: slip systems along [001], [010], [0–10], [001] → 90° periodicity  
  - (110) plane: two slip systems [110] and [1–10] → 180° periodicity  
  - (111) plane: three bidirectional slip systems [1–10], [01–1], [10–1], [101], [01–1], [0–11] → 60° minimum periodicity  
- **Property improvements**: When the angle between sliding direction and slip system is smaller, resolved shear stress on the slip plane is larger, promoting dislocation motion and localized plastic deformation, which accelerates material exfoliation and increases wear susceptibility.  
- **Risks and limitations**: The direction of lowest wear resistance depends on crystallographic plane and slip system orientation; designing for maximum wear life requires optimizing grain orientation relative to loading/sliding direction.  
- **Original evidence**: Section 3.3, Fig. 15, Fig. 16.

**Mechanism: Protective lubricating oxide glaze layer at high temperature**  
- **Related elements**: Ni, Al, Si (from counterpart debris), O.  
- **Typical alloys**: DD6 NBSC superalloy  
- **Key compositions**: Ni-based matrix with Al and Cr; Si from Si₃N₄ counterface.  
- **Microstructural changes**: At 600 °C, an oxidized mixture of Ni, Al, and Si forms a dense, homogeneous glaze layer (~0.17 μm) on wear tracks.  
- **Property improvements**: Reduces COF from 0.65 (RT) to 0.35 (600 °C); reduces abrasive and adhesive wear, making oxidative wear predominant.  
- **Risks and limitations**: Glaze layer is unevenly distributed, cannot be completely removed, leading to non-uniform wear depth; at 300 °C, no stable lubricating layer, adhesive wear dominant.  
- **Original evidence**: Section 3.2, Figs. 6–10.

# Key Conclusions

1. The surface wear of DD6 NBSC superalloys shows clear crystallographic anisotropy. The wear depths of (100), (110), and (111) crystal planes exhibit periodicities of 90°, 180°, and 60°, respectively.
2. At the nanoscale, the same periodicities are observed, indicating that crystallographic anisotropy persists across scales.
3. The anisotropy in wear is attributed to the different slip systems on each crystal plane and the angle between the sliding direction and the slip system direction. A smaller angle leads to larger resolved shear stress on the slip plane, promoting dislocation motion and localized plastic deformation, which makes the material more susceptible to wear.
4. (110) plane has the largest average wear depth, while (111) has the smallest (most wear resistant). This correlates with mechanical properties: (111) plane has the highest elastic modulus (171.9 GPa) and hardness (6.7 GPa).
5. At high temperatures (300 °C, 600 °C), the wear anisotropy persists on (100) plane, though the strict periodicity is disrupted by the formation of a non-uniform protective glaze layer.
6. The glaze layer composed of oxidized Ni, Al, and Si acts as a solid lubricant, reducing the COF from 0.65 at RT to 0.35 at 600 °C on the (100) plane.
7. The cross-scale anisotropic damage mechanism is driven by differences in wear resistance due to slip-system orientation along specific crystal directions and surfaces. On the same crystal plane, wear track depth is more pronounced in directions aligning with the slip system.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Limitations:**
  - The paper studies a Ni-based single crystal superalloy (DD6), not a refractory high-temperature alloy (Nb-W, Mo-W, Nb-W-Mo-Zr, refractory high-entropy alloy, ODS refractory alloy, or additively manufactured refractory alloy). Therefore, the extracted knowledge does not apply to refractory alloy composition design or refractory alloy strengthening mechanisms.
  - The paper does not investigate composition-dependent strengthening mechanisms, microstructural precipitate evolution, or high-temperature oxidation resistance from an alloy design perspective.
  - No tensile, compressive, creep, fracture toughness, rupture life, or density data are reported.
  - No quantitative oxidation weight gain, oxidation layer phase identification, or oxidation kinetics are reported.
  - Nanoindentation values are reported only for (111) plane; explicit numerical values for (100) and (110) planes are not provided in the text.
  - The effect of individual alloying elements on wear or mechanical behavior is not analyzed.
  - High-temperature wear tests were performed only on the (100) plane; (110) and (111) planes at high temperature were not tested.
  - The material is single crystal, so no grain-boundary-related phenomena (strengthening, oxidation, embrittlement) are relevant.
  - The paper is a tribological study; its conclusions about crystallographic orientation-dependent wear may not be directly transferable to polycrystalline refractory alloys or multi-phase refractory high-entropy alloys.

- **Applicable scope:**
  - The paper is useful for understanding crystallographic anisotropy-dependent wear behavior of FCC Ni-based single crystal superalloys.
  - It provides insight into orientation-related slip system geometry and its effect on wear, which may inform crystallographic texture control in other BCC/FCC alloys, but direct transfer to refractory BCC alloys is not supported by the original paper.
