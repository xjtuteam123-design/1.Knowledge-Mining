- **Authors:** Shaofu Huang, Xiangling Zeng, Xian Du, Zhen Peng, Jing Li, Rui Wang, Jun Liu, Binggong Yan, Jian Liu, Zhihai Cai, Haidou Wang, Chao Liu
- **Journal:** Vacuum
- **Year:** 2023
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.vacuum.2023.111900
- **Alloy system studied:** NbMoTaNiCr multi-principal alloy (also referred to as refractory high-entropy alloy, RHEA)
- **Research objective:** To design and develop a combined cable wire made of NbMoTaNiCr multi-principal alloy and fabricate it via GTAW rotary wire arc additive manufacturing; to study the microstructure and mechanical properties of the formed layer
- **Material category:** Refractory multi-principal element alloy / refractory high-entropy alloy (RHEA); additively manufactured refractory alloy (wire arc additive manufacturing, GTAW-WAAM)

# Alloy System and Research Object

- The paper studies a five-element NbMoTaNiCr multi-principal alloy, specifically Nb37.7Mo14.5Ta12.6Ni28.16Cr7.04 (at.%).
- The alloy is described as belonging to the broad family of refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) / multi-principal element alloys, although its calculated ΔS_mix is 1.45R, which does not strictly meet the high-entropy definition.
- The research focuses on fabricating this alloy by GTAW rotary wire arc additive manufacturing and evaluating microstructure, phase stability, and mechanical properties from room temperature to 1100 °C.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **Manufacturing method:** Gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW) rotary wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM)
- **Filler material:** NbMoTaNiCr combined cable wire (1.6 mm diameter)
- **Substrate:** 304 stainless steel
- **Shielding gas:** 99.999% high-purity argon
- **Process parameters (optimized by orthogonal experiments):**
  - Current: 110 A
  - Electrode spacing: 2 mm
  - Wire feeding speed: 6 mm/s
  - Overlaying speed: 110 mm/min
  - Gas flow rate: 19 L/min
- **Deposition mode:** Single-pass multilayer
- **Pre-processing of substrate:** Hand-held sander to remove oxide layer, ultrasonic cleaning in acetone, wiped with alcohol, dried at 100 °C for 1 h
- **Post-deposition heat treatment:** None reported. The paper explicitly describes the as-deposited condition only.
- **Thermal analysis:** DSC performed from 0 to 1400 °C at 15 °C/min under argon; this is characterization, not a heat treatment applied to the alloy.
- No arc melting, induction melting, powder metallurgy, mechanical alloying, hot pressing, SPS, HIP, rolling, forging, homogenization, annealing, or aging processes were applied to this alloy in this paper (these are mentioned only as literature comparisons).

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Room-temperature phases (XRD, Fig. 3):
- Main phase: Face-centered cubic (FCC) solid solution
- Minor phase: μ-phase (a topologically close-packed, TCP phase)
- No BCC or B2 phase at room temperature
- XRD peaks described as narrow and sharp, indicating high crystallinity
- Lattice constant of FCC phase: a = 359.56 pm (calculated from Bragg equation)
- Theoretical lattice constant from rule of mixtures: 331.25 pm; deviation <9%

## High-temperature phases (XRD at 750 °C, Fig. 9):
- BCC, FCC, B2, and μ-phase coexist at 750 °C
- Proportion of μ-phase significantly increased at high temperature compared to room temperature
- DSC shows exothermic/endothermic peaks between 650–750 °C, indicating crystalline transformation; above this range, no other peaks, indicating high thermal stability

## TEM analysis (Fig. 6):
- Bright-field image shows uniform contrast, no second phase or precipitate observed
- Selected area diffraction pattern along [111] zone axis shows disordered FCC diffraction spots only; no ordered spots
- Paper concludes: "the alloy has a single-phase FCC structure, which is consistent with the XRD diffraction results." (Note: TEM did not detect the μ-phase mentioned in XRD)

## Microstructure morphology:
- Fine and uniform dendrites
- No micro-segregation (stated in one section), though elsewhere "certain dendrite segregation" is noted
- Mo and Ta tend to concentrate in dendrites
- Cr and Ni mainly distributed in interdendritic regions
- Nb distributed more uniformly

## Elemental segregation rationale (based on mixing enthalpy, Table 5):
- Mo and Ta have higher melting points → solidify first → form dendrite arms
- Ni–Ta and Ni–Nb have the most negative mixing enthalpies (−29 and −30 kJ/mol, respectively), indicating strong bonding
- Mo–Nb: +6 kJ/mol; Mo–Ta: +5 kJ/mol; Nb–Cr: +7 kJ/mol; Ta–Cr: +7 kJ/mol; Ni–Cr: +7 kJ/mol; Nb–Ta: 0 kJ/mol
- Ni and Cr have relatively low melting points → interdendritic

## Grain size:
- The paper states the grains are "small" due to non-equilibrium solidification but does not give a specific grain size value (μm or nm). "The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for grain size."

## Precipitates / second phases:
- μ-phase (TCP) present at room temperature (small amount) and increased at high temperature
- B2 phase present at 750 °C
- No carbides, borides, oxides, or other ceramic particles (ZrC, HfC, TiC, NbC, TaC, HfO2, ZrO2, Y2O3, La2O3) are reported
- No ODS/dispersion strengthening
- No explicit discussion of solid-solution strengthening mechanism beyond general statement of lattice distortion and solid-solution strengthening effect

# Mechanical Properties

## Hardness (Fig. 7):
- Average Vickers microhardness at room temperature: **911 HV** (average of six test points; test load 1000 N, holding 15 s)
- Paper notes this is "much higher than for other HEAs systems with solid-solution structures reported in the literature, reaching the hardness level of an amorphous alloys"
- Paper also states: "the Vickers hardness of the reported BCC phase-structured RHEAs does not exceed 800 HV"

## Compression tests (Fig. 8):
- Specimen size: ø 4 mm × 6 mm cylinders
- Testing machine: DOLI-DDL200 universal testing machine

### Room temperature (25 °C):
- Yield strength: **545.5 MPa**
- Fracture strain: **9.5%**

### 750 °C:
- Yield strength: **597 MPa**
- Fracture strain: **6.5%**

### 1100 °C:
- Yield strength: **490 MPa**
- Fracture strain: **6.8%**

## High-temperature comparison:
- Yield strength decrease from 750 to 1100 °C: only 107 MPa
- Inconel 718 at 1000 °C: ~200 MPa (cited from Ref. [31])
- The paper states the NbMoTaNiCr layer has "good structural stability at high temperatures"

## Fracture morphology (Fig. 10):
- River patterns and deconstruction (cleavage) steps at all temperatures → cleavage fracture (the paper uses the term "deconstruction fracture")
- Some shallow dimples at 1100 °C, indicating better plastic toughness at 1100 °C than at 750 °C
- Fracture mechanism: cleavage fracture, independent of temperature

## Other mechanical properties:
- No tensile properties, creep, fracture toughness, elastic modulus, or density reported.
- "The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for tensile strength, creep performance, fracture toughness, elastic modulus, or density."

## Explanation for high strength and limited ductility:
- High hardness/yield strength attributed to: lattice distortion from large atomic size differences, solid-solution strengthening, grain refinement from non-equilibrium solidification of WAAM, and μ-phase presence
- Poor ductility at elevated temperature attributed to second-phase activation of cracks along grain boundaries; dislocation accumulation at μ-phase/FCC interfaces leading to stress concentration
- At 750 °C, BCC and B2 phases form, FCC fraction decreases, μ-phase fraction significantly increases → increase in yield strength and decrease in fracture strain compared to room temperature
- At 1100 °C, lattice slip becomes easier, dislocation pile-up and pinning weaken → strength decreases, fracture strain slightly increases

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

- The paper does not report direct oxidation weight gain, oxidation layer characterization, or oxidation resistance testing.
- No discussion of Al2O3, Cr2O3, SiO2, HfO2, ZrO2, or other protective oxide scales.
- No discussion of oxygen embrittlement, nitrogen embrittlement, grain-boundary oxidation, or high-temperature environmental stability.
- Thermal stability was assessed via DSC up to 1400 °C: crystalline transformation at 650–750 °C; no further peaks above this range; the paper interprets this as "high thermal stability."
- No high-temperature embrittlement mechanisms (other than μ-phase-assisted crack initiation at grain boundaries) were discussed in the context of service stability.

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related elements:** Nb, Mo, Ta, Ni, Cr (multi-principal element solid solution)
- **Typical alloys:** NbMoTaNiCr
- **Key compositions:** Nb37.7Mo14.5Ta12.6Ni28.16Cr7.04 (at.%)
- **Microstructural changes:** Large atomic size differences (radii: Nb 1.43 Å, Mo 1.36 Å, Ta 1.47 Å, Ni 1.62 Å, Cr 1.28 Å) induce a high degree of lattice distortion; Ni with large atomic radius causes lattice expansion of FCC; lattice constant 359.56 pm vs. theoretical 331.25 pm
- **Property improvements:** High hardness (911 HV); high room-temperature yield strength (545.5 MPa); high-temperature yield strength (597 MPa at 750 °C, 490 MPa at 1100 °C)
- **Risks and limitations:** The large atomic size differences also contribute to brittle phase formation tendency; solid-solution strengthening alone cannot explain the high hardness, which also benefits from μ-phase and fine grains.

## Grain Refinement

- **Related elements:** Not element-specific; process-controlled
- **Typical alloys:** NbMoTaNiCr fabricated by GTAW rotary WAAM
- **Key compositions:** Same as above
- **Microstructural changes:** Non-equilibrium rapid solidification of arc melting produces fine and uniform dendrites; rotating arc stirs the melt pool, increases cooling rate, increases internal subcooling and nucleation mass, refining dendrite grain size
- **Property improvements:** High hardness (911 HV); the paper attributes high hardness partly to small grain size
- **Risks and limitations:** The paper does not provide a specific grain size value, so quantitative grain-size strengthening contribution cannot be assessed from the original paper.

## Second-Phase Strengthening (μ-phase)

- **Related elements:** All five elements contribute to μ-phase TCP formation
- **Typical alloys:** NbMoTaNiCr
- **Key compositions:** μ-phase present as minor phase at RT; proportion significantly increased at 750 °C
- **Microstructural changes:** α-FCC/μ-phase lattice mismatch causes dislocation accumulation at interfaces; at 750 °C, BCC and B2 phases also form, reducing FCC fraction
- **Property improvements:** High hardness (911 HV); enhanced high-temperature strength (yield strength 597 MPa at 750 °C)
- **Risks and limitations:** Hard and brittle at room temperature; stress concentration at FCC/μ-phase interfaces leads to fracture; second-phase activation of grain-boundary cracks reduces ductility at elevated temperature; fracture strain drops from 9.5% (RT) to 6.5% (750 °C)

## High-Temperature Phase Stability (BCC+B2+μ at elevated temperature)

- **Related elements:** All five elements; Ni stabilizes FCC, while Mo/Ta promote higher-temperature phases
- **Typical alloys:** NbMoTaNiCr
- **Key compositions:** FCC+μ at RT → BCC+FCC+B2+μ at 750 °C
- **Microstructural changes:** Phase transformation in 650–750 °C range; BCC and B2 form at 750 °C; μ-phase proportion increases
- **Property improvements:** Yield strength increases from 545.5 MPa (RT) to 597 MPa (750 °C); high-temperature strength (490 MPa at 1100 °C) far superior to Inconel 718 (~200 MPa at 1000 °C)
- **Risks and limitations:** Ductility decreases at 750 °C (fracture strain 6.5%) due to BCC/B2 formation and μ-phase increase; BCC phase has poor elastic-plastic fracture toughness

## Additive Manufacturing-Oriented Composition Control (Cable Wire Design)

- **Related elements:** Composition controlled by combining elemental wires: 3 Nb, 2 Ni–Cr, 1 Ta, 1 Mo
- **Typical alloys:** NbMoTaNiCr (this work); also MoNbTaWTi (Ref. [31]) and AlCoCrFeNi (Ref. [32]) as literature comparisons
- **Key compositions:** Cable wire composition designed to reach target alloy composition after WAAM
- **Microstructural changes:** Coupled self-rotating arc due to twisted cable wire structure enhances forming quality; rotating arc stirs melt pool, refining grains
- **Property improvements:** Enables wire-based AM of RHEAs that cannot be drawn into wires due to poor room-temperature ductility; high material utilization (~100%); no environmental pollution
- **Risks and limitations:** Arc stability in cable-wire electrode forming is challenging; the paper uses GTAW rotary WAAM to solve this; no discussion of hot cracking susceptibility.

# Key Conclusions

1. The GTAW rotary WAAM process using a twisted stranded cable wire successfully fabricated a high-quality NbMoTaNiCr multi-principal alloy layer with uniform weld bead, good surface quality, and no obvious cracks or pores.

2. At room temperature, the alloy is mainly composed of FCC solid solution plus a small amount of μ-phase; at 750 °C, BCC, FCC, B2, and μ-phase coexist, with μ-phase proportion significantly increased.

3. The microstructure consists of fine, uniform dendrites with elemental segregation: Mo and Ta concentrate in dendrites; Cr and Ni in interdendritic regions; Nb is more uniform.

4. Average room-temperature Vickers hardness: **911 HV**, described as higher than all other reported solid-solution HEAs, reaching amorphous alloy hardness levels.

5. Compressive yield strength: **545.5 MPa at 25 °C**, **597 MPa at 750 °C**, **490 MPa at 1100 °C**; fracture strains: **9.5%, 6.5%, 6.8%**, respectively.

6. High-temperature strength of NbMoTaNiCr is much better than Inconel 718 (~200 MPa at 1000 °C), indicating good high-temperature structural stability.

7. Fracture mechanism is cleavage fracture at all temperatures (river patterns and cleavage steps), with some shallow dimples at 1100 °C indicating slight ductility improvement.

8. The phase transformation FCC→BCC+B2+μ at elevated temperature is both beneficial (higher strength) and detrimental (reduced ductility) to high-temperature performance.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- No oxidation resistance testing was performed; oxidation behavior of this alloy remains unknown from this paper.
- No tensile, creep, fatigue, or fracture toughness data are provided; high-temperature assessment is limited to compressive loading.
- The paper does not provide quantitative grain size values, making grain-boundary strengthening quantification impossible.
- The TEM analysis showing "single-phase FCC" appears inconsistent with XRD showing FCC+μ-phase. The paper does not resolve this inconsistency; the μ-phase may not have been captured in the selected-area diffraction region or was below TEM detection.
- The substrate is 304 stainless steel; dilution effects and interfacial behavior between substrate and deposit are not discussed.
- The alloy has no W, though W is a common refractory element; this alloy may not represent the full refractory HEA space.
- Hardness claim of "911 HV higher than all other systems with solid-solution structure" should be treated as authors' assertion; the "original paper" makes this claim based on literature comparison but does not include exhaustive comparative data.
- Mechanical property values are from compression tests only and may not translate directly to tensile service conditions.
- The paper does not report density, elastic modulus, specific impurity levels (C, N, H), or detailed microstructural quantification (grain size, phase volume fractions).
- Applicable scope: Refractory multi-principal alloys fabricated by wire arc additive manufacturing; relevant to aeroengine and high-temperature structural material applications where compressive loading dominates and rapid fabrication of complex parts is needed, but tensile and oxidation data are still required for industrial qualification.
