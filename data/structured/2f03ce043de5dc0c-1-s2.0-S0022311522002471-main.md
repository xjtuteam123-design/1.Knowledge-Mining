# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title**: Progress in additive manufacturing of pure tungsten for plasma-facing component applications
- **Authors**: A.v. Müller, D. Dorow-Gerspach, M. Balden, M. Binder, B. Buschmann, B. Curzadd, T. Loewenhoff, R. Neu, G. Schlick, J.H. You
- **Journal**: Journal of Nuclear Materials
- **Year**: 2022
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.jnucmat.2022.153760
- **Alloy system studied**: Pure tungsten (W)
- **Research objective**: To report recent progress in additive manufacturing (AM) of pure tungsten by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), covering the influence of raw powder characteristics on part quality, fabrication of complex lattice structures, use of AM W as preforms for W-Cu composites, and thermal shock performance of LPBF W
- **Material category**: Additively manufactured refractory alloy (pure tungsten via LPBF); W-Cu composite (with AM W preform)

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system**: Pure tungsten (W), a body-centred cubic (bcc) refractory metal
- **Material category**: Additively manufactured refractory metal; W-Cu composite (W preform + Cu infiltrated)
- **Application context**: Plasma-facing components (PFCs) and plasma-facing material (PFM) for magnetic confinement thermonuclear fusion devices, including DEMO, ITER, and future reactors
- **Why W is chosen**: W is the preferred PFM due to its high threshold energy for physical sputtering and low retention of hydrogen isotopes
- **Key challenge**: W has a particularly high ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT), making it prone to microcrack formation during LPBF, even with substrate preheating up to 1000 °C

# Alloy Composition

- **Primary material**: Pure tungsten (W). No deliberate alloying additions are described.
- **Impurity content in W powders (combustion analysis)**:
  - Oxygen (O): measured in all three W powders investigated (values shown graphically in Fig. 4; no numeric values listed in the text).
  - Carbon (C), sulphur (S), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N): “Relevant levels with respect to the other analysed impurities (C, S, H, N) were not measured.”
- **Element-specific composition data**: 
  - The paper does not investigate W alloys with Nb, Mo, Ta, Zr, Hf, Ti, V, Cr, Al, Si, Re, C, B, Y, La, or O additions beyond the O contamination in base W powders.
  - A Cu-infiltrated W-Cu composite is fabricated using a W preform; Cu content or volume fraction is not numerically specified, but the preform is described as a graded honeycomb structure with varying wall thickness along the building direction to achieve a higher W volume fraction at the joint to W PFM tiles.
- **Other relevant elements mentioned**: Ag–Cu braze used for soldering samples onto Cu holders (not part of the W alloy itself).

- Scanning speed: 510 mm/s

- Layer thickness: 40 μm
- Substrate preheating: 1000 °C

**LPBF-W-2 sample (rather inadequate parameters, increased porosity)**:

- Scanning speed: 210 mm/s

- Layer thickness: 40 μm
- Substrate preheating: 600 °C
- Facility: Aconity ONE

**Earlier reported parameter recommendation (from references [6,7] within the paper)**:
- Deposited energy density: approximately 250 J/mm³ (for laser power of 400 W with substrate preheating of 1000 °C)

**Additional facility used**:
- SLM 250 HL (Fraunhofer IGCV, 86159 Augsburg, Germany) for powder-quality comparison tests

## Powder Types Investigated
1. **Spheroidised W powder** (supplier: Tekna Advanced Materials Inc., product name: W-25) — almost only round, comparably small particles; sharp particle size distribution (PSD)
2. **Partly spheroidised W powder** (supplier: Global Tungsten & Powders Corp., product name: WD 200) — morphology ranges from completely spheroidised to polygonal; broad PSD
3. **Polygonal W powder** (supplier: H.C. Starck GmbH, product name: HC 4000) — standard polygonal powder with comparably large particles; broad PSD; exhibits largest particle sizes

## Conventional Reference W
- Supplier: Plansee SE, Austria
- Processing route: Single forged and recrystallised at 1600 °C for 1 h

## Post-Processing
- All samples for thermal shock testing: grinded and polished to a mirror-like surface finish
- Samples soldered with Ag–Cu braze on actively cooled Cu holders
- No homogenization, rolling, forging, annealing, or aging applied to LPBF W beyond as-built condition (except grinding/polishing of thermal shock specimens)

## W-Cu Composite Fabrication
- Additively manufactured W preform built onto bulk W PFM tiles by LPBF (Aconity ONE; spheroidised W powder W-25)
- Preform melt infiltrated with Cu (performed by Louis Renner GmbH, 85232 Bergkirchen, Germany)
- Assembly machined to final dimensions

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase**: Body-centred cubic (bcc) W
- **Grain size**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper
- **Grain boundary features**: Not explicitly discussed beyond crack presence
- **Elemental segregation**: Not discussed in the original paper
- **Second phases or precipitates**: Not reported
- **Oxide dispersoids**: Not applicable (pure W, no intentional ODS additions)
- **Key microstructural defect features**:
  - **Microcrack defects**: W consolidated by LPBF “typically exhibits microcrack defects after the fabrication throughout the material” (confirmed by several references [7, 11–16, 28–31]). Even substrate preheating up to 1000 °C does not inhibit microcrack formation.
  - **Porosity**: LPBF-W-1 sample exhibits a defective layer with higher porosity at a depth of about 1 mm, probably due to a disturbance during layer build-up. LPBF-W-2 sample exhibits considerably more porosity than LPBF-W-1.
  - **Cooling rates and thermal gradients in LPBF**: Cooling rates higher than 10⁴ K/s; spatial temperature gradients approximately 10² K/mm to 10⁴ K/mm.
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed**: 
  - The original paper does not discuss solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, dispersion strengthening, grain-boundary strengthening, grain refinement, ODS strengthening, or second-phase strengthening in the context of the LPBF W material. The focus is on defect formation (microcracks, porosity) and thermal shock performance.

# Mechanical Properties

- **Room-temperature yield strength**: Not stated in the original paper
- **Ultimate tensile strength**: Not stated
- **Compressive strength**: Not stated
- **High-temperature strength**: Not stated (thermal shock survival is reported, but no strength values are given)
- **Elongation / compressive plasticity / fracture toughness**: Not stated
- **Creep performance / rupture life**: Not stated
- **Hardness**: Not stated
- **Elastic modulus**: Not stated
- **Density**: Not reported numerically. LPBF-W-1 is described as having “higher mass density” than LPBF-W-2 due to lower porosity.
- **Testing temperature for thermal shock**: Base temperature of 700 °C (achieved by steady state heat load of 10 MW/m²)
- **Loading mode for thermal shock**: Electron beam pulses (JUDITH 2 electron beam facility)
- **Thermal shock loading parameters**:
  - Power densities: 0.14 GW/m² to 0.55 GW/m² (assuming power absorption of 0.55 by W)
  - Pulse duration: 0.48 ms
  - Repetition frequency: 25 Hz
  - Number of pulses: 10⁵ thermal shocks per test condition
  - Heat flux factors (HFF): 3, 6, 9, and 12 MW m⁻² √s (some sources omit the unit); HFF 3 corresponds to 0.14 GW/m² or 0.07 MJ/m²; HFF 12 corresponds to 0.55 GW/m² or 0.26 MJ/m²
  - Cooling circuit: water at 70 °C, pressure 20 MPa, flow velocity ~25 m/s
  - Test interruption: every 10³ pulses (40 s), interrupted for 20 s (100 on/off cycles)
- **Thermal shock results**:
  - No significant changes in cool-down behaviour observed during or after tests
  - LPBF W samples survived 10⁵ HFF 12 thermal shocks without loss of material or extensive crack defects from a macroscopic viewpoint
  - Recrystallised reference W exhibited cracks on loaded surface with depths up to about 200 μm after 10⁵ HFF 3 transients
  - In LPBF W samples, cracks were omnipresent throughout the material, but no significant difference could be identified between loaded and unloaded areas after HFF 3
  - With increasing HFF (3, 6, 12), more new cracks appeared on sample surfaces of LPBF-W-1
- **Ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT)**: W has a particularly high DBTT; no specific value is given in this paper.

- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Cu infiltrated into porous W preform (melt infiltration); exact Cu volume fraction not numerically specified

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

Note: The paper does not investigate traditional strengthening mechanisms such as solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, or ODS. The following entries reflect the mechanisms discussed in the context of LPBF W.

## [LPBF Process Parameter Control for Defect Reduction]

- **Related elements**: None (pure W); possible powder characteristics (spheroidisation, particle size distribution)
- **Typical alloys**: Pure W, LPBF W
- **Key compositions**: Pure W; recommended energy density ~250 J/mm³; laser power 400 W; substrate preheating 1000 °C
- **Microstructural changes**: 
  - Microcracks form throughout LPBF W even with 1000 °C preheating due to the high DBTT of W and the high thermal gradients (10² to 10⁴ K/mm) and cooling rates (>10⁴ K/s) during LPBF
  - Porosity is influenced by powder morphology and process parameters; inadequate parameters (e.g., 375 W, 210 mm/s, 600 °C preheating) lead to increased porosity
  - Spheroidised W powder yields better part quality (lower porosity, better surface quality) compared to partly spheroidised or polygonal powders
- **Property improvements**: 
  - Higher mass density with suitable parameters (LPBF-W-1 vs LPBF-W-2)
  - Better surface quality and lower porosity with spheroidised powder
  - Thermal shock survival up to 10⁵ HFF 12 pulses (0.55 GW/m², 0.48 ms) without macroscopic loss of material or extensive cracking
- **Risks and limitations**: 
  - Microcrack defects persist even with preheating up to 1000 °C
  - W has a particularly high DBTT, making LPBF processing inherently challenging
  - Processor parameter choices significantly affect porosity and density
  - Defective layers (e.g., 1 mm deep porous layer due to build disturbance) can occur
- **Original Evidence**: Section 1 states: “it was identified that a deposited energy density of approximately 250 J mm⁻³ for a laser power of 400 W together with a substrate preheating of 1000 °C represent reasonable manufacturing parameters”; Section 2 discusses powder morphology and PSD influence; Section 5 describes sample parameters and thermal shock results.

## [Powder Morphology and Particle Size Distribution Control for LPBF W Part Quality]

- **Related elements**: None (pure W)
- **Typical alloys**: LPBF W
- **Key compositions**: 
  - Tekna W-25: spheroidised, sharp PSD
  - Global Tungsten WD 200: partly spheroidised, broad PSD
  - H.C. Starck HC 4000: polygonal, largest particle sizes, broad PSD
- **Microstructural changes**: 
  - Spherical powder particles yield better flowability and powder bed density (implied as paramount for consistent layer formation)
  - Spheroidised powder yields notably better LPBF results in terms of porosity and surface quality for thin-walled test parts
- **Property improvements**: 
  - The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for density, porosity, or surface roughness. Qualitative comparison shows: spheroidised > partly spheroidised > polygonal in part quality.
- **Risks and limitations**: 
  - Different powders may require individually optimised LPBF parameters; results shown in Fig. 5 were obtained with the same laser exposure parameters, so optimisation could improve poorer powders
- **Original Evidence**: Fig. 2 (SEM images), Fig. 3 (PSD), Fig. 5 (built part quality); Section 2: “it is known that the characteristics of the raw powder material have a significant influence on the process stability and quality of the consolidated material in powder bed based AM processes like LPBF.”

## [Thermal Shock Resistance of LPBF W vs Conventional W]

- **Related elements**: None (pure W)
- **Typical alloys**: LPBF W (LPBF-W-1, LPBF-W-2), conventional forged + recrystallised W reference
- **Key compositions**: Pure W; LPBF-W-1 (400 W, 510 mm/s, 80 μm hatch, 40 μm layer, 1000 °C preheating); reference W (single forged, recrystallised 1600 °C for 1 h, Plansee SE)
- **Microstructural changes**: 
  - LPBF W exhibits omnipresent microcracks and porosity; no significant difference in cracks between loaded and unloaded areas after HFF 3 thermal shocks
  - Recrystallised reference W shows new cracks up to 200 μm depth on loaded surface after HFF 3
  - LPBF-W-1 shows surface crack increase with increasing HFF (3 → 6 → 12)
- **Property improvements**:
  - Thermal performance of LPBF W appears comparable to conventionally manufactured W based on surface temperature progression during HFF 3 tests
  - LPBF W survived 10⁵ HFF 12 thermal shocks without macroscopic damage
  - In reference [34], not all ITER-grade W and W alloys produced by powder injection molding could sustain HFF 12 thermal shocks without macroscopic damage, illustrating high thermal shock resistance of LPBF W
- **Risks and limitations**: 
  - Microcracks pre-exist throughout LPBF W; they cannot be differentiated from thermal shock-induced cracks in cross sections
  - A defective 1 mm porous layer was present in LPBF-W-1, though it did not lead to unacceptable surface damage
- **Original Evidence**: Fig. 8, 9, 10, 11; Section 5; Statement: “This is an encouraging result indicating that the thermal performance and stability of W fabricated by means of LPBF is comparable to that of conventionally fabricated W despite the presence of microcrack defects.”

# Key Conclusions

1. **Powder characteristics strongly influence LPBF W part quality**: Spheroidised W powders (e.g., Tekna W-25) are best suited for LPBF processing, yielding notably better results in terms of porosity and surface quality than partly spheroidised or polygonal powders when using the same laser exposure parameters.

2. **Complexly shaped W lattices are feasible by LPBF**: Anisotropic W lattice structures were successfully fabricated by LPBF, demonstrating that design freedom of AM can be exploited for pure W despite its challenging properties. These lattices are being investigated as plasma limiter components for DEMO.

3. **LPBF W can serve as preform for tailored W-Cu composites**: Additively manufactured W honeycomb/graded structures were successfully used as preforms for liquid Cu infiltration to produce a PFC mock-up heat sink, demonstrating the feasibility of tailored W-Cu composite structures.

4. **LPBF W can survive intense thermal shock loads**: W consolidated by LPBF was tested in JUDITH 2 electron beam facility with 10⁵ thermal shocks at heat flux factors up to HFF 12 (0.55 GW/m², 0.48 ms, 700 °C base temperature). The material exhibited no macroscopic loss or extensive cracking, and its thermal performance was comparable to conventionally fabricated W despite the presence of microcrack defects.

5. **Microcracks remain an inherent challenge in LPBF W**: Even with substrate preheating up to 1000 °C, microcrack formation is not inhibited. This is attributed to W's high DBTT and the extreme thermal gradients and cooling rates during LPBF.

6. **LPBF W is a worthwhile candidate for further PFC investigation**: The encouraging thermal shock results imply that further investigation of additively manufactured W for highly loaded PFC applications in future fusion devices is justified.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

**Limitations**:
- The paper is a progress report rather than a comprehensive material characterization study; mechanical properties (yield strength, UTS, elongation, hardness, fracture toughness), density values, and quantitative oxidation data are **not provided**.
- The thermal shock tests used only 10⁵ pulses; no higher cycle counts or longer exposure durations were reported.
- Oxygen contamination was measured but not reported with specific numerical values; C, S, H, N were stated as not detected at relevant levels, but no quantification or detection limits are given.
- The comparison between powder types used identical LPBF parameters, which may not represent optimally tuned parameters for each powder.
- The defective 1 mm porous layer in LPBF-W-1 samples could confound interpretation of thermal shock results, though it did not cause unacceptable surface damage.
- No microstructural characterization (grain size, phase analysis, texture) of the as-built LPBF W is presented in this paper; these aspects are referenced from earlier publications [6, 7].

**Applicable Scope**:
- Results apply specifically to pure W processed by LPBF.
- Thermal shock performance data are specific to the JUDITH 2 e-beam loading conditions (0.48 ms pulses, 25 Hz, HFF 3–12, 700 °C base temperature, 10⁵ pulses).
- The W-Cu composite demonstration is specific to a honeycomb/graded W preform infiltrated with Cu, targeted at divertor PFC heat sink applications.
- The powder quality comparison is relevant to LPBF W processing with three specific commercial W powders (Tekna W-25, Global Tungsten & Powders WD 200, H.C. Starck HC 4000).
- The work is oriented toward magnetic confinement fusion PFC applications (DEMO, ITER); generalizability to other high-temperature structural applications is not addressed.
