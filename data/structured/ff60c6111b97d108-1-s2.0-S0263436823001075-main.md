# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title**: Effect of niobium on the mechanical strength of the laser beam welding joints of molybdenum
- **Authors**: Liang Liang Zhang, Yang Zhou, Lin Jie Zhang, Jie Ning, Yun Jun Sun, Suck-Joo Na
- **Journal**: International Journal of Refractory Metals and Hard Materials
- **Year**: 2023
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.ijrmhm.2023.106207
- **Alloy system studied**: Mo-based alloy (specifically 0.25 wt% La2O3 dispersion-reinforced Mo alloy) with Nb alloying of laser beam welding joints
- **Research objective**: To improve the low grain boundary bonding strength and plasticity of Mo fusion welding joints by adding Nb during laser beam welding, and to clarify the influence of Nb on microstructure and joint strength
- **Material category**: Mo-based alloy (ODS Mo alloy with La2O3 dispersion reinforcement), laser beam welded joint with Nb alloying

# Alloy System and Research Object

The base material is a 0.25 wt% La2O3 dispersion-reinforced Mo alloy manufactured by Jinduicheng Molybdenum Co., Ltd. The research focuses on laser beam welded socket joints between a Mo clad tube and end plug. Nb was introduced into the fusion zone via Nb rings of varying thickness (0.025–0.15 mm) preset at the butt position before welding.

The authors state that Mo and its alloys have good chemical stability, high-temperature strength, and corrosion resistance, making them ideal candidates for nuclear fuel cladding to replace Zr alloy cladding. However, poor fusion weldability and grain boundary brittleness limit their application in engineering structures.

# Alloy Composition

**Base material**: 0.25 wt% La2O3 dispersion-reinforced Mo alloy (manufactured by Jinduicheng Molybdenum Co., Ltd.)

**Alloying element added to fusion zone**: Nb (introduced via Nb rings provided by Ningxia Orient Tantalum Industry Co., Ltd.)

**Sample designations and Nb ring thicknesses**:
| Sample designation | Nb foil/ring thickness (mm) |
|---|---|
| Pure Mo (unalloyed joint, also referred to as "LW") | 0 |
| Mo + 0.025Nb-W | 0.025 |
| Mo + 0.050Nb-W | 0.050 |
| Mo + 0.075Nb-W | 0.075 |
| Mo + 0.100Nb-W | 0.100 |
| Mo + 0.150Nb-W | 0.150 |

**Atomic percentages or weight percentages of Nb in the fusion zone**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper. The paper only reports the Nb foil thickness as an alloying parameter.

**Impurity elements discussed**: O, C, N. The paper notes that ppm-level O segregation at grain boundaries is the primary cause of grain boundary brittleness in Mo. Olds and Rengstorff reported that 1–2 ppm O could raise the DBTT to room temperature, while N required 30 ppm. The BM itself contains small amounts of O, C, and other impurity elements; O from the protective atmosphere may also enter the molten pool during welding.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

**Welding method**: Laser beam welding (LBW)
- Laser machine: SPI Qbue LT 2 kW single-mode fiber laser
- Laser wavelength: 1070 nm
- Minimum focal spot diameter: 0.05 mm
- Laser power: 1.6 kW
- Welding speed: 2.2 m/min
- Defocus amount: 0
- Number of welding turns: 5
- Laser held still while workpiece rotated
- Welding carried out under argon protection

**Nb introduction method**: Nb ring preset on a step on the Mo rod before welding, placed at the butt position of the socket joints to realize alloying during laser beam welding. Nb ring thickness varied from 0.025 to 0.15 mm.

**Heat treatment**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper. The welding process was carried out without explicit preheating or post-weld heat treatment.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

**Matrix phase**: BCC Mo matrix

**Grain structure**:
- Fusion zone (FZ): coarse columnar crystals
- Heat-affected zone (HAZ): severely grown and coarsely recrystallized grains
- Base metal (BM): retained original rolled structure
- The metallographic morphology of Nb-alloyed joints showed no significant difference from unalloyed (LW) joints.

**Second phases and precipitates**:

In unalloyed joints (LW):
- Lamellar MoO2 flakes and chains on grain boundary surfaces
- Mo2C phase (mentioned as potentially forming in FZ)

In Nb-alloyed joints:
- Spherical Nb2O5 particles (confirmed by TEM diffraction)
- Irregular polygonal NbC particles (confirmed by TEM diffraction)
- NbO2 (detected by XPS, intermediate oxide)
- NbCO (detected by XPS)
- MoO2 (significantly reduced amount compared to unalloyed joints)

**Precipitate size and distribution**:
- As Nb content increased, the number of spherical particles increased significantly.
- Average precipitate size increased from approximately 100 nm to approximately 300 nm as Nb content increased.
- When Nb foil thickness reached 0.15 mm, the precipitate morphology on grain boundary surfaces tended to become polygonal.
- Nb2O5 was distributed both in grain interiors and at grain boundaries; at some grain boundaries, the amount of Nb2O5 was much higher than in grain interiors.
- The more Nb2O5 distribution locations there were, the less MoO2 there was (EBSD results).

**Elemental segregation**:
- EDS line scan showed Nb mainly distributed in the FZ.
- EDS area scan showed Nb uniformly distributed in the FZ without macroscopic segregation.
- O segregates at grain boundaries in unalloyed Mo joints.

**Grain boundary features**:
- Unalloyed joints: O segregation at grain boundaries, MoO2 lamellar phase epitaxially grown on {110}Mo plane, Mo-O-Mo sandwich structure with single atomic layer O at grain boundary
- Nb-alloyed joints: Nb2O5 and NbC precipitates at grain boundaries replacing MoO2

**Strengthening mechanisms discussed**:
- Solid-solution strengthening of Nb in Mo
- Second-phase strengthening (NbC precipitation)
- Grain boundary bonding strength improvement by reducing O and MoO2
- Fine grain strengthening (discussed as general mechanism for Mo, not specifically achieved here)

# Mechanical Properties

**Hardness (micro-Vickers, 50 gf load, 15 s holding time)**:

Average FZ hardness values:
- LW (unalloyed) joint FZ: 200.5 HV
- Mo + 0.025Nb-W FZ: 213.4 HV
- Mo + 0.05Nb-W FZ: 218.8 HV
- Mo + 0.075Nb-W FZ: 231.0 HV
- Mo + 0.1Nb-W FZ: 233.0 HV
- Mo + 0.15Nb-W FZ: 239.1 HV

HAZ hardness: increased with increasing distance from weld beam center, approximately 200–220 HV
BM hardness: 240–250 HV
The HAZ showed softening due to recrystallization during the welding heat cycle.

**Tensile properties (tested by Instron universal testing machine at 0.1 mm/min)**:

Ultimate tensile strength (UTS):
- Pure Mo (unalloyed/LW) joint: 169.8 MPa (lowest)
- Mo + 0.025Nb-W: 259.6 MPa
- Mo + 0.05Nb-W: 379.7 MPa
- Mo + 0.075Nb-W: 426.7 MPa (highest)
- As Nb amount increased, UTS tended to increase then decrease.
- Base metal (BM) tensile strength: 680 MPa
- The best Nb-alloyed joint (426.7 MPa) reached 62.7% of BM strength.

**Fracture locations**:
- LW joints: all fractured close to center of the weld bead (WB)
- Mo + 0.025Nb-W: partly at WB center, partly near fusion line (FL)
- Mo + 0.05Nb-W: partly at WB center, partly near FL, rarely in HAZ
- Mo + 0.075Nb-W: partly at WB center, partly near FL, significant portion in HAZ
- Mo + 0.1Nb-W: mostly at WB center and near FL
- Mo + 0.15Nb-W: mostly at WB center and near FL

**Fracture mode**: Intergranular fracture (indicated by coarse columnar crystals visible on fracture surfaces)

**Elongation/plasticity**: Not explicitly stated in the original paper.

**High-temperature strength**: Not tested in this study. However, the paper notes that NbC has a very high decomposition temperature (3500 °C), which is beneficial for improving high-temperature strength.

**Creep performance**: Not tested in this study.

**Density, elastic modulus**: Not tested in this study.

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

The paper does not directly test oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures. However, the following oxidation-related information is relevant:

- The primary problem addressed is O segregation at grain boundaries and MoO2 formation at grain boundaries, which causes grain boundary embrittlement.
- MoO2 has a lamellar Mo-O-Mo structure with weak Mo–O bond strength (per Magneli and Andersson).
- When MoO2 precipitates at the Mo grain boundary, a single atomic layer of O forms at the Mo grain boundary. The Mo–Mo metal bond is replaced by a Mo–O covalent bond, weakening the grain boundary bonding strength.
- Addition of Nb preferentially forms NbO2 and Nb2O5, reducing free O and MoO2 at grain boundaries.

The paper is not about high-temperature oxidation of the alloy in service, but about oxygen control during welding and the resulting grain boundary state.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Nb (Niobium)

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Mo-based alloys, specifically ODS Mo (0.25 wt% La2O3) laser beam welded joints.

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
Introduced via Nb rings of 0.025–0.15 mm thickness at the butt position of socket joints. The exact Nb content in the FZ in at.% or wt.% is not quantified in the original paper.

**[Main Function]**
- Reduce O content at grain boundaries and suppress MoO2 formation
- Form Nb2O5 and NbC second phases for grain boundary strengthening
- Indirectly improve grain boundary bonding strength
- Improve fusion zone tensile strength

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
- Nb has higher affinity for O than Mo (Gibbs free energy of formation of NbO2 and Nb2O5 is much lower than that of MoO2), so it preferentially combines with O, reducing free O at grain boundaries.
- Nb preferentially combines with C to form NbC rather than Mo2C (Gibbs free energy of NbC at 1200 K is 64,410 J/mol vs. 59,217 J/mol for Mo2C, with NbC lower).
- NbC precipitates in liquid Mo with high-temperature phase Nb2C (3500 °C) precipitating first in the liquid.
- NbC has a lattice mismatch of 0.67% between {111}NbC and {110}Mo, suggesting a coherent or semicoherent interface with strong bonding to the Mo matrix.
- Nb2O5 precipitates as spherical particles, unlike lamellar MoO2.

**[Property Effect]**
- FZ hardness increased from 200.5 HV (unalloyed) to 239.1 HV (Mo + 0.15Nb-W).
- UTS increased from 169.8 MPa (unalloyed) to 426.7 MPa (Mo + 0.075Nb-W), an improvement of approximately 2.5×.
- The maximum UTS achieved was 62.7% of BM strength (680 MPa).

**[Potential Side Effects]**
- As Nb content increased beyond 0.075 mm foil thickness, UTS decreased (from 426.7 MPa to lower values for Mo + 0.1Nb-W and Mo + 0.15Nb-W joints), suggesting an optimal Nb addition level.
- Excessive Nb may increase precipitate size (100 nm to 300 nm), and morphology changes from spherical to polygonal at 0.15 mm foil thickness.
- The paper notes Nb is less effective in solid-solution strengthening of Mo than other elements (Ti, Re, Hf) but has relatively smaller effect on plasticity.

**[Original Evidence]**
- "The maximum tensile strength of joints with Nb alloyed could reach 426.7 MPa" (Abstract)
- "The hardness values increased from 213.4 HV to 239.1 HV when the thickness of Nb foil addition increased from 0.025 mm to 0.15 mm" (Conclusions)
- "Nb mainly existed in the form of Nb2O5 and NbC in the fusion zone (FZ) of joints with Nb alloyed" (Abstract)
- "The average size of the precipitated phase increased from 100 nm to approximately 300 nm" (Section 4.1)
- Fig. 10, 11, 12, 13 show SEM images of precipitates on grain boundary surfaces.
- Fig. 15 TEM analysis confirmed spherical particles are Nb2O5 and polygonal particles are NbC.

Mo-based ODS alloy (base material of this study).

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**

Dispersion reinforcement of the Mo base material.

**[Property Effect]**
The BM strength is reported as 680 MPa, which includes the contribution of La2O3 dispersion strengthening, but specific La2O3 strengthening values are not isolated in the original paper.

**[Potential Side Effects]**

## C (Carbon) in Mo-Nb system

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Mo-Nb laser beam welded joints.

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
C is present as an impurity in the BM; no intentional C addition is made. The BM contains small amounts of C impurities.

**[Main Function]**
- Combines with Nb to form NbC, providing second-phase strengthening
- When no Nb is present, combines with Mo to form Mo2C

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
- NbC has FCC structure with lattice constant a = 4.47 Å; Mo has BCC structure with a = 3.14 Å.
- Lattice mismatch between {111}NbC and {110}Mo is 0.67%, suggesting coherent lattice structure.
- NbC forms strong bond with Mo matrix.
- NbC decomposition temperature is very high (3500 °C).

**[Property Effect]**
NbC plays a second-phase strengthening role in Mo, improving grain boundary bonding strength. Specific values beyond the overall joint UTS improvement are not separately quantified in the original paper.

**[Potential Side Effects]**
Mo2C formed in unalloyed joints (along with MoO2) contributes to grain boundary weakness, but Mo2C is less detrimental than MoO2 according to the paper.

**[Original Evidence]**
- "Nb is easier to combine with C to form NbC than Mo. The Gibbs free energy of NbC (1200 K) was 64,410 J/mol" (Introduction)
- "The irregular polygonal particles were NbC" (Section 4.1, Fig. 15)
- "δ ({111}NbC, {110}Mo) = 0.67%" (Section 4.2)

## O (Oxygen) in Mo grain boundaries

**[Applicable Alloy System]**
Mo-based alloys, especially fusion welding joints.

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**
O is an impurity. Olds and Rengstorff reported that 1–2 ppm O could raise the DBTT to room temperature. N required 30 ppm for similar effect.

**[Main Function]**
O segregates at grain boundaries and forms MoO2, causing severe embrittlement in unalloyed Mo joints.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**
- MoO2 has a lamellar Mo-O-Mo structure with weak Mo–O bond strength.
- MoO2 precipitates epitaxially on {110}Mo plane as {110}Mo // {010}MoO2.
- A single atomic layer of O forms at the Mo grain boundary.
- Mo–Mo metal bonds are replaced by Mo–O covalent bonds, weakening grain boundary strength.
- MoO2 formation temperature is 2300 °C.

**[Property Effect]**
- Unalloyed Mo joint UTS was only 169.8 MPa (compared to BM strength of 680 MPa).
- Formation of MoO2 at grain boundaries is cited as the primary reason for the low strength.

**[Potential Side Effects]**
O embrittlement is the primary issue. Even ppm-level O can raise DBTT to room temperature.

**[Original Evidence]**
- "1–2 ppm of O could raise the ductile–brittle transition temperature (DBTT) to room temperature" (Introduction, referencing Olds and Rengstorff)
- "MoO2 has a Mo-O-Mo lamellar structure and the strength of the Mo–O bond is weak" (Introduction)
- "When MoO2 precipitates at the Mo grain boundary, a single atomic layer of O is formed at the Mo grain boundary" (Introduction)

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

**[Related Elements]**
Nb

**[Typical Alloys]**
Mo-Nb laser beam welded joints

**[Key Compositions]**
Nb foil thickness 0.025–0.15 mm added to FZ

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Nb and Mo form a replacement solid solution.
- Nb atomic radius is closer to Mo than Ti, C, Re, and Hf, so the lattice distortion and solubility are greater.
- Nb is uniformly distributed in the FZ without macroscopic segregation.

**[Property Improvements]**
- FZ hardness increased from 200.5 HV to 239.1 HV with Nb addition.
- Part of the hardness increase is attributed to solution strengthening of Nb in Mo.

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- Nb is less effective in solid-solution strengthening of Mo than Ti, Re, or Hf due to smaller atomic radius difference.
- However, Nb has a relatively smaller effect on joint plasticity compared to other solid solution elements that cause large lattice distortions.

## Carbide or Boride Precipitation Strengthening (NbC)

**[Related Elements]**
Nb + C

**[Typical Alloys]**
Mo-Nb laser beam welded joints

**[Key Compositions]**
Nb added via 0.025–0.15 mm thick Nb rings; C present as impurity

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Irregular polygonal NbC particles precipitating at grain boundaries and in grain interiors.
- NbC precipitate size increases from ~100 nm to ~300 nm with increased Nb addition.
- NbC has lattice mismatch of 0.67% with Mo matrix suggesting coherent or semicoherent interface.
- NbC forms strong bond with Mo matrix.

**[Property Improvements]**
- Overall UTS improvement from 169.8 MPa (unalloyed) to 426.7 MPa (Mo + 0.075Nb-W).
- NbC decomposition temperature is very high (3500 °C), beneficial for high-temperature strength.

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- Excessive Nb may lead to oversized precipitates (up to 300 nm) and polygonal morphology, which may contribute to the decrease in UTS at high Nb levels.

## Oxide Dispersion Strengthening (Nb2O5 and La2O3)

**[Related Elements]**
Nb + O forming Nb2O5; La2O3 in base material

**[Typical Alloys]**
Mo-Nb laser beam welded joints (Nb2O5); ODS Mo alloy (La2O3)

**[Key Compositions]**
- Nb2O5 forms from Nb + O in welding FZ
- Base material contains 0.25 wt% La2O3

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Spherical Nb2O5 particles at grain boundaries and in grain interiors.
- Nb2O5 replaces MoO2 at grain boundaries, reducing O embrittlement.
- NbO2 forms first, then combines with additional O to form Nb2O5 as temperature decreases.

**[Property Improvements]**
- Reduction of free O at grain boundaries and suppression of MoO2 formation.
- Joint UTS improved to 426.7 MPa.
- The improved grain boundary bonding strength is partly due to Nb2O5 replacing detrimental lamellar MoO2.

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- Nb2O5 is spherical and does not have the lamellar structure of MoO2, so it is less detrimental to grain boundary strength.
- However, excessive oxide formation may still reduce mechanical performance (UTS decreased at higher Nb levels).

## Grain Boundary Bonding Strength Improvement

**[Related Elements]**
Nb (via Nb2O5 and NbC formation)

**[Typical Alloys]**
Mo-Nb laser beam welded joints

**[Key Compositions]**
Nb foil thickness of 0.075 mm (optimal for maximum UTS)

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Nb preferentially combines with O, reducing free O and MoO2 at grain boundaries.
- NbC precipitates at grain boundaries, forming strong coherent/semicoherent interfaces with Mo matrix.
- Nb2O5 distribution inversely correlates with MoO2 distribution.

**[Property Improvements]**
- UTS increased from 169.8 MPa (unalloyed) to 426.7 MPa (Mo + 0.075Nb-W).
- FZ hardness increased from 200.5 HV to 239.1 HV.

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- The strengthening effect peaks at 0.075 mm Nb foil thickness; further Nb addition reduces UTS.
- Grain boundary strengthening via Nb is indirect (via O scavenging and second-phase precipitation), not direct.

## Grain Boundary Embrittlement Control (O and MoO2 Suppression)

**[Related Elements]**
Nb (O scavenger)

**[Typical Alloys]**
Mo fusion welding joints (laser beam welded)

**[Key Compositions]**
Nb foil thickness 0.025–0.15 mm

**[Microstructural Changes]**
- Nb forms NbO2 and Nb2O5, reducing free O in FZ.
- MoO2 amount significantly reduced.
- Formation of Nb2O5 inhibits formation of MoO2 (inverse distribution relationship).

**[Property Improvements]**
- UTS improved by 2.5× (from 169.8 to 426.7 MPa).
- Reduction of brittle intergranular fracture tendency.

**[Risks and Limitations]**
- Cannot completely eliminate MoO2 (EBSD results show MoO2 still present in Nb-alloyed joints).
- The effectiveness depends on Nb distribution and competition between Nb2O5 and MoO2 formation during rapid cooling.

# Key Conclusions

1. Nb additions of 0.025–0.15 mm thickness were introduced to the FZ of Mo laser beam welded joints. The maximum tensile strength of unalloyed joints was 169.8 MPa, while Nb-alloyed joints reached up to 426.7 MPa (using 0.075 mm Nb foil), representing 62.7% of the BM strength (680 MPa).

2. The microstructures of Nb-alloyed and unalloyed joints showed no significant differences: both exhibited coarse columnar crystals in the FZ.

3. FZ micro-Vickers hardness increased with Nb content: from 200.5 HV (unalloyed) to 239.1 HV (Mo + 0.15Nb-W).

4. In unalloyed joints, lamellar MoO2 was observed on grain boundary surfaces of fractures in the FZ, causing grain boundary embrittlement.

5. In Nb-alloyed joints, spherical or polygonal Nb2O5 and NbC particles were observed on grain boundary surfaces of fractures. TEM analysis confirmed spherical particles as Nb2O5 and irregular polygonal particles as NbC.

6. Nb addition reduces free O in the FZ and MoO2 at grain boundaries by preferentially forming NbO2 and Nb2O5 (lower Gibbs free energy of formation than MoO2). NbC precipitates in liquid Mo and acts as second-phase strengthening particles.

7. NbC has a lattice mismatch of 0.67% between {111}NbC and {110}Mo, suggesting coherent or semicoherent interface bonding with the Mo matrix, enabling strong grain boundary strengthening.

8. The optimal Nb addition appears to be at 0.075 mm foil thickness (UTS of 426.7 MPa); higher Nb additions (0.1–0.15 mm) resulted in decreased UTS, likely due to oversize precipitates (up to 300 nm) and morphological changes.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. The exact Nb content in the FZ in at.% or wt.% is not quantified in the original paper; only Nb foil thickness is reported. This makes it difficult to translate the findings to specific Nb concentration ranges.

2. The welding parameters are fixed (1.6 kW laser power, 2.2 m/min welding speed, 5 welding turns). The effect of varying welding parameters on Nb distribution and second-phase formation is not investigated.

3. The paper does not test high-temperature mechanical properties. The claim that NbC improves high-temperature strength is based on its high decomposition temperature (3500 °C) and is not experimentally verified.

4. The study does not measure elongation, ductile-to-brittle transition temperature, fracture toughness, or creep properties.

5. The paper does not quantify the amount of O or C in the FZ before and after Nb addition, so the exact O scavenging efficiency of Nb is not quantified.

6. The tensile tests were performed on socketed joints rather than standard tensile specimens. The UTS values reflect the joint strength, not the bulk material strength.

7. The applicability is limited to laser beam welded Mo-La2O3 ODS alloy joints with Nb ring alloying. The findings may not directly transfer to arc welding, electron beam welding, or powder metallurgy processes.

8. The study focuses on thin-walled Mo clad tube and end plug joints for nuclear fuel cladding applications. The generality to thicker sections or structural applications is not established.

9. The La2O3 dispersion in the BM is not analyzed in detail; its interaction with the Nb alloying process and FZ microstructure is not addressed.

10. The XPS analysis may have depth limitations (<10 nm measurement depth), so the quantified elemental states may only represent the surface state of grain boundary fractures rather than bulk precipitate composition.
