# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Revealing the room temperature superplasticity in bulk recrystallized molybdenum
- **Authors**: Wenshuai Chen, Xiyao Li, Shenbao Jin, Lunwei Yang, Yan Li, Xueliang He, Wanting Zhang, Yinxing Wu, Zhilin Hui, Zhimin Yang, Jian Yang, Wei Xiao, Gang Sha, Jiangwei Wang, Zenglin Zhou
- **Journal**: Nature Communications
- **Year**: 2023
- **DOI**: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-44056-7
- **Alloy system studied**: Pure molybdenum (Mo)
- **Material category**: BCC refractory metal, powder metallurgy Mo, hot-rolled and annealed pure Mo
- **Research objective**: To overcome the intrinsic room-temperature brittleness and recrystallization-induced intergranular brittleness of molybdenum, and to achieve room-temperature superplasticity in bulk recrystallized pure Mo through GB engineering (controlling O segregation at grain boundaries), texture control, and LAGB fraction optimization.

# Alloy System and Research Object

- Alloy system: Pure Mo (rather than Mo-W or other Mo-based alloys)
- Purity: Matrix Mo content >99.9 wt%
- Category: Bulk recrystallized refractory metal
- Application scope: High-temperature structural materials for aerospace, radar communication, equipment manufacturing, nuclear industry
- Research focus: Room-temperature superplasticity mechanism of fully recrystallized pure Mo produced by powder metallurgy + Y-type hot rolling + annealing

# Alloy Composition

The paper studies pure Mo with trace impurities, not intentionally alloyed Mo.

**Sample A impurities (ppm, by mass):**

- O: 32 ppm
- N: 11 ppm
- Ni: 4.18 ppm

**Sample B impurities (ppm, by mass):**
- C: 20 ppm
- O: 34 ppm
- N: 8 ppm
- Ni: 2.85 ppm
- Fe: 5.45 ppm

**Mo matrix:** >99.9 wt% in both samples.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

**Processing route:**
1. Raw material: Pure Mo powders, purity ≥ 99.9 wt%, Fisher particle size of 3.0 ± 0.2 μm
2. Cold isostatic pressing: 200 MPa for 10 min (in soft rubber sleeve)
3. High-temperature sintering: Hydrogen atmosphere at 1900 °C for 6 h, cooled in furnace
4. Turning: to diameter of 50 mm
5. Y-type hot rolling: At 1300 °C to obtain hexagonal prismatic Mo bars
   - Sample A: diameter 18 mm, deformation/reduction rate 87%
   - Sample B: diameter 12 mm, deformation/reduction rate 94%
6. Annealing: Heated to 800–1900 °C at 10 K/min in hydrogen atmosphere for 120 min, furnace cooled

**Heat treatment details:**
- Annealing temperatures studied: 800, 900, 1000, 1100, 1200, 1700, 1900 °C
- Annealing time: 120 min
- Atmosphere: Hydrogen
- Heating rate: 10 K/min
- Cooling: Furnace cooling
- Reference microstructure in main study: 1200 °C annealed samples (A-1200, B-1200)

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

**As-rolled state:**
- Slender fibrous structure along rolling direction
- Sample B has finer and more uniform fibrous grains than A due to higher deformation

**As-annealed state (above 1000 °C):**
- Full recrystallization evidenced by equiaxed grains in both A and B
- Hardness evolution confirms full recrystallization above 1000 °C
- Abnormal grain growth: Sample A above 1700 °C; Sample B above 1900 °C

**Texture (after 1200 °C annealing):**
- Sample A-1200: 51% <110>//RD texture, slight buildup of <001>//RD texture
- Sample B-1200: 74.8% <110>//RD texture (recrystallized grains inherit orientation of previously deformed grains via strain-induced GB migration)

**Grain boundary character (after 1200 °C annealing):**
- Both samples dominated by HAGBs, fraction >80% (confirming full recrystallization)
- Sample A-1200: 11% LAGBs, 2% 1.5° sub-GBs
- Sample B-1200: 19% LAGBs, 7% 1.5° sub-GBs

**Grain size distribution:**
- Both samples inherit average grain size of sintered Mo
- Sample B-1200 has higher proportion of ultra-fine grains ≤3 μm compared to A-1200

**No second-phase particles, carbides, or oxides** are reported to deliberately form in these samples.

# Mechanical Properties

**As-rolled state:**
- Sample A: Tensile strength 680 MPa, total elongation 45%, uniform elongation about 20%
- Sample B: Tensile strength 720 MPa, total elongation 61%, uniform elongation 20%

**Fully recrystallized state (above 1000 °C annealing):**
- Yield strength: kept above ~400 MPa
- Fracture strength: kept above ~510 MPa
- Sample A: Total elongation typically <50%
- Sample B: Total elongation >90%, maximum 108.7% after 1200 °C annealing (RT superplasticity)
- Sample A-1900 and B-1900: elongation declines due to abnormal grain coarsening; B-1900 still reaches ~50%

**Strain-rate dependence:**
- Sample B-1000 retains uniform elongation of 60% at strain rate 3 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹

**Test conditions:**
- Loading mode: Quasi-static uniaxial tension at room temperature
- Main strain rate: 3 × 10⁻² s⁻¹
- Gauge length: 25 mm

**Hardness evolution:**
- Used to confirm full recrystallization above 1000 °C; exact values not extracted in this paper from Figure S3

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

**Not the primary topic of this paper.**
- No oxidation weight gain or oxidation layer data presented.
- Service stability mention: Mo-based materials with aggressive alloying elements (e.g., rare-earth elements) cannot be used under ultra-high vacuum (≤10⁻⁸ Pa) due to decomposition of Mo-RE-O composite oxides above 1500 °C.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## Card 1: O (Oxygen) at grain boundaries

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Pure Mo, technically pure Mo (100–1000 ppm impurity level)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 32–34 ppm bulk O content in samples; GB O segregation: 1.22 at% at LAGB of A-1200, 1.29 at% at HAGB of A-1200; 0.19 at% at LAGB of B-1200, 0.55 at% at HAGB of B-1200
- **Main Function**: Harmful impurity; O segregation at GBs significantly weakens GB cohesion and induces brittle intergranular fracture.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Slight O segregation at GBs tends to form Mo-O ion bonds and create a charge density depletion region, which reduces GB cohesion. O weakening effect on GB is stronger than N.
- **Property Effect**: Reducing GB O segregation (from >1 at% in A-1200 to 0.19–0.55 at% in B-1200) largely eliminates GB brittleness, enabling dislocation transmission across LAGBs and contributing to elongation of 108.7% in B-1200.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Intergranular fracture, GB cracking, reduced RT ductility, increased susceptibility to recrystallization-induced brittleness.
- **Original Evidence**: “The O and Ni segregations at a 6°[001] LAGB in sample A-1200 are 1.22 at% and 0.04 at%, respectively”; “The O segregation at a 4.5° LAGB in sample B-1200 is as low as 0.19 at%” (Fig. 6); “the segregation of O and N can significantly reduce the interface bonding” (Grain boundary chemistry section); “reducing the O concentration should be a practical strategy to enhance the GB fracture strength.”

## Card 2: N (Nitrogen) at grain boundaries

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Pure Mo, technically pure Mo
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 8–11 ppm bulk N; GB N segregation: 0.33 at% at HAGB of A-1200, 0.43 at% at HAGB of B-1200; no N segregation at LAGBs in either sample
- **Main Function**: Harmful impurity; N segregation at GBs reduces interface bonding; however, weakening effect of N is weaker than O.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: N segregation forms similar charge depletion effects; observed only at HAGBs (>15° misorientation), consistent with previous literature.
- **Property Effect**: N segregation contributes to weakened GB cohesion; total O+N segregation at HAGB of A-1200: 1.62 at%, B-1200: 0.98 at%; lower O+N in B-1200 helps delay GB cracking.
- **Potential Side Effects**: GB decohesion, but less severe than O.
- **Original Evidence**: “A nitrogen segregation of 0.33 at% also occurs at this HAGB” (A-1200) and “the N segregation of 0.43 at%” (B-1200) (Fig. 6); “the weakening effect of O element on GBs is stronger than that of N” (Grain boundary chemistry section).

## Card 3: Ni (Nickel) at grain boundaries

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Pure Mo, technically pure Mo (trace impurity)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Bulk Ni: 4.18 ppm (A), 2.85 ppm (B); GB Ni segregation at LAGB: 0.04 at% (A-1200), 0.11 at% (B-1200); at HAGB: 0.18 at% (A-1200), 0.06 at% (B-1200)
- **Main Function**: Trace Ni at GBs is beneficial for interface bonding / cohesion.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Ni segregation helps improve cohesion of matrix GBs; it also delays dislocation pile-up by decreasing the Peierls valley strength of screw dislocation motion.
- **Property Effect**: In B-1200, trace Ni segregation (0.11 at% at LAGB, 0.06 at% at HAGB) combined with ultralow O enables enhanced ability of bonding rotation and reformation across GBs; validates numerous dislocation transmissions across LAGBs.
- **Potential Side Effects**: Seemingly minor, but not explicitly discussed as detrimental; Ni content in A-1200 is higher than B-1200, and Ni is prone to segregate at HAGB in A-1200.
- **Original Evidence**: “The Ni segregation at HAGBs in sample A-1200 is 0.18 at%, which is higher than 0.06 at% at HAGBs of sample B-1200”; “the segregation of Ni and Fe is undoubtedly beneficial for interface bonding” (Grain boundary chemistry section); “the trace segregation of Ni (0.11 at%) can not only help to improve the cohesion of the matrix GBs” (Discussion).

## Card 4: Fe (Iron) at grain boundaries

- **Applicable Alloy System**: Pure Mo, technically pure Mo (trace impurity)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Bulk Fe: 7.31 ppm (A), 5.45 ppm (B); GB Fe segregation: 0.02 at% at LAGB of A-1200, 0.02 at% at LAGB of B-1200, 0.12 at% at HAGB of A-1200, 0.22 at% at HAGB of B-1200
- **Main Function**: Trace Fe at GBs is beneficial for interface bonding, similar to Ni.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Fe segregation at GBs improves GB cohesion; Ni+Fe combined segregation at HAGB: 0.22 at% (A-1200), 0.12 at% (B-1200).
- **Property Effect**: Not explicitly separately quantified; combined Ni+Fe segregation is considered beneficial for interface bonding, but in A-1200, higher O+N segregation (1.62 at%) overwhelms this benefit.
- **Potential Side Effects**: None explicitly discussed.
- **Original Evidence**: “the segregation of Ni and Fe is undoubtedly beneficial for interface bonding” (Grain boundary chemistry; Table 1).

## Card 5: Processing route

- **Element or Combination**: Y-type hot rolling + annealing texture/GB engineering (process parameter)
- **Applicable Alloy System**: Pure Mo, and potentially applicable to BCC refractory metals and alloys
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Hot rolling deformation rate 87% (A) vs 94% (B); annealing 1200 min at 1000–1700 °C
- **Main Function**: High deformation rate increases recrystallized <110>//RD texture fraction and LAGB fraction; controls dynamic recovery to retain fine grains and reduce O segregation at GBs.
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Y-type hot rolling resembles extrusion+drawing; drawing force along rolling direction rotates <110> orientation to deformation direction; fully dynamic recovery produces uniform recrystallization cores which compete and grow during annealing, reducing grain size; more GBs lead to lower solute element segregation per GB.
- **Property Effect**: B-1200 achieves 108.7% RT elongation, yield strength >400 MPa, fracture strength >510 MPa; increased LAGB fraction (19%) enables dislocation transmission and stress release.
- **Potential Side Effects**: At very high annealing temperature (1900 °C), abnormal grain growth reduces ductility; as-rolled B has slightly lower texture and LAGB ratio than A due to more dynamic recovery/recrystallization.
- **Original Evidence**: “the ratio of <110> //RD texture in sample B-1200 increases to 74.8%”; “the proportion of LAGBs in fully-recrystallized B-1200, especially 1.5° GBs, increased significantly, with 19% for LAGBs and 7% for 1.5° GBs respectively”; “a more uniform recrystallized core is formed and competes with each other to grow during annealing, significantly reducing the size of recrystallized grains, and more GBs lead to lower solute element segregation” (Discussion).

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## 1. Intergranular fracture and embrittlement mechanisms

- **Main causes**: O segregation at GBs (dominant); N segregation (secondary); recrystallization-induced GB enrichment of impurities; high sensitivity of GB cleavage in BCC Mo.
- **Oxygen embrittlement**: O at GBs forms Mo-O ionic bonds and creates charge density depletion region, reducing GB cohesion; LAGB O segregation in A-1200 reaches 1.22 at% with IFE of 2.79 atoms/nm²; HAGB O segregation 1.29 at% with IFE of 2.95 atoms/nm².
- **Key quantified deltas**: In B-1200, LAGB O segregation is near-zero (0.19 at% which equals matrix content), and HAGB O segregation is 0.55 at%, much lower than A-1200.

## 2. Solid-solution softening and strengthening

- **Related elements**: Ni (trace) at GBs; Re mentioned from literature (not studied in this paper for Mo).
- **Mechanism in this paper**: Trace Ni decreases Peierls valley strength of screw dislocation motion; delays dislocation pile-up at GBs.
- **Property improvement**: Not explicitly quantified as a direct strength increase, but considered beneficial for enhanced dislocation mobility and GB cohesion.

## 3. Grain boundary engineering (GBE) for ductility enhancement

- **Key strategy**: Reduced O segregation + trace Ni segregation + high LAGB fraction.
- **LAGB fraction**: B-1200 has 19% LAGBs and 7% 1.5° sub-GBs vs 11% LAGBs and 2% 1.5° sub-GBs in A-1200.
- **Dislocation transmission**: In B-1200, dislocations can traverse LAGBs, with transmission capability decreasing with increasing GB angle; critical angle ~15°.
- **Effect**: Stress concentration at some HAGBs is released by coordinated deformation via neighboring LAGBs; delays GB cracking; contributes to RT superplasticity.

## 4. Texture engineering

- **Related elements**: Not element-specific; controlled by process.
- **Typical texture**: <110>//RD soft texture.
- **Key compositions**: 74.8% in B-1200 vs 51% in A-1200.
- **Microstructural change**: High <110>//RD texture enables development of straight screw dislocations and weaving dislocation networks; grains divert to <110> direction during uniaxial tension.
- **Property improvement**: High <110>//RD ratio enables more uniform intragranular plastic deformation and stronger coordinated deformability, enabling 108.7% tensile elongation in B-1200.
- **Risks**: Not explicitly discussed as risks; but texture is not sufficient alone (B-1200 with high <110>//RD but different annealing temperatures does not show simple linearity with elongation).

## 5. Dislocation network / storage capacity engineering

- **Mechanism**: Weaving dislocation networks formed by straight screw dislocations act as effective walls to subdivide grains, increasing dislocation storage ability; reduces mean free path for crack propagation.
- **Observed differences**: A-1200 develops dense short dislocations; B-1200 develops long-straight screw dislocations and weaving networks.
- **Effect**: In B-1200, dislocation cells form between dislocation walls at 70% strain; enhanced dislocation storage reduces pile-up at GBs; contributes to delayed fracture.

## 6. ODS/Precipitation/Dispersion strengthening

- **Not applicable in this paper** (pure Mo only); paper contrasts its results with Mo alloys strengthened by second phases (Mo-La₂O₃, Mo-TiC-ZrC-C, Mo-ZrO₂, Mo-Y₂O₃, Mo-Y-Ce-O, ZrC-dispersed Mo), but does not study these mechanisms.

# Key Conclusions

1. **Room-temperature superplasticity achieved in bulk recrystallized pure Mo**: Maximum tensile elongation of 108.7% at RT after 1200 °C annealing of sample B (94% hot-rolling deformation rate).

2. **Primary mechanism**: Synergy of low O segregation at GBs, high <110>//RD soft texture fraction, and high LAGB fraction:
   - Ultralow O at GBs (0.19–0.55 at% range) eliminates inherent GB brittleness.
   - Trace Ni segregation (0.11 at%) enhances GB cohesion and screw dislocation mobility.
   - High <110>//RD texture (74.8%) promotes straight screw dislocation formation and weaving dislocation networks.
   - High LAGB fraction (19%) enables dislocation transmission across LAGBs and stress release.

3. **Dislocation-GB interaction angle dependence**: Dislocation transmission across GBs declines with increasing misorientation angle; critical transition identified at ~15°.

4. **Mechanical properties retention**: Even after annealing at 1000–1700 °C, sample B retains yield strength >400 MPa and fracture strength >510 MPa, with superplastic elongation >90%.

5. **General implication**: The strategy of controlling GB chemistry (oxygen) + texture + LAGB fraction is expected to be applicable to a broad class of refractory metals and alloys.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. **Material scope**: The paper studies pure Mo, not Mo-W or other Mo-based alloys; conclusions about O segregation, texture, and LAGB effects may need verification for alloyed systems.

2. **Rare-earth element stability**: The paper explicitly states that Mo-based alloys with aggressive alloying elements (e.g., rare earths) cannot be used above 1500 °C under ultra-high vacuum (≤10⁻⁸ Pa) due to Mo-RE-O oxide decomposition; alloying strategies become ineffective in delaying recrystallization above 1500 °C.

3. **High temperature mechanical properties**: Although the Mo bars have good structural stability at high temperature, the paper does not provide quantitative high-temperature tensile/creep data; its focus is RT superplasticity.

4. **Abnormal grain growth**: At annealing temperature above 1700 °C (A) or 1900 °C (B), abnormal grain growth occurs and total elongation decreases sharply (though B-1900 retains ~50%).

5. **Effect of Ni+Fe vs O+N at HAGBs**: The paper states that the HAGB in A-1200 has some Ni+Fe segregation (0.22 at%) but the much higher O+N (1.62 at%) induces GB decohesion, while B-1200 has lower O+N (0.98 at%) and Ni+Fe (0.12 at%); this indicates that beneficial metallic segregation cannot fully offset the detrimental effect of high O+N.

6. **Not explicitly addressed**: Quantitative oxidation resistance data, creep performance, fracture toughness, high-temperature yield strength values; the paper relies on literature comparison for mechanical properties of other Mo materials.

7. **Strain rate**: RT superplasticity is reported at 3 × 10⁻² s⁻¹; the paper also shows uniform elongation 60% at 3 × 10⁻⁴ s⁻¹ for B-1000, but does not characterize superplastic behavior at intermediate strain rates or elevated temperatures.
