# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title**: Atomic-scale insights into the strength and plasticity enhancement of Ni-based superalloys with refinement dispersion of precipitates
- **Authors**: Yihui Yin, Shiqing Lu, Lusheng Wang, Kun Song, Xia Huang, Jun Ding, Siliang Yan
- **Journal**: Chemical Physics Letters
- **Year**: 2025 (Volume 861, Article 141837; available online 17 December 2024)
- **DOI**: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cplett.2024.141837
- **Alloy system studied**: Ni-based superalloy (γ/γ′ two-phase system)
- **Material category**: Ni-based superalloy (not a refractory alloy; this is a Ni-Al-based γ/γ′ model system)
- **Research objective**: To reveal the effect of dispersion distribution (refinement) of γ′ precipitates on strength and plasticity at the atomic scale in Ni-based superalloys using molecular dynamics simulations at room temperature (300 K).

> **Note**: This paper does not study Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloy systems. It is a molecular dynamics simulation study of a Ni-based superalloy with Ni₃Al (γ′) precipitates in a Ni (γ) matrix. All extracted data is preserved as originally reported.

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Alloy system**: Ni-based superalloy, modeled as a two-phase Ni (γ matrix) / Ni₃Al (γ′ precipitate) system.
- **Material category**: Ni-based superalloy (γ/γ′ precipitate-strengthened system). It is not a refractory alloy even though the assigned expertise domain includes refractory alloys. The paper is outside the specified refractory alloy categories, but I extract its reported data faithfully below.
- **Research method**: Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation using LAMMPS; models built with Atomsk; visualization and analysis with OVITO.
- **Interatomic potential**: EAM (Embedded Atom Method) alloy potential for the Ni-Al system (Purja Pun & Mishin, Phil. Mag. 89, 2009).
- **Simulation temperature**: 300 K (room temperature).
- **Simulation pressure**: 0.1 bar.
- **Simulation box**: 100 × 100 × 1 nm (quasi-2D thin film with scaling boundary conditions along X and Y; periodic boundary condition along Z).
- **Tensile loading**: Velocity of 7.7 × 10³ nm/ps along the Y-axis; total tensile strain of 30%.
- **Initial crack**: Edge crack measuring 15 × 2 nm.
- **Research objective**: Elucidate the influence law and action mechanism of γ′ phase dispersion and refinement on room-temperature mechanical strength and toughness of Ni-based single-crystal superalloys from the atomic scale.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

No experimental processing or heat treatment is reported. This is a molecular dynamics simulation study:

- **Energy minimization**: Conjugate gradient method used to obtain the initial atomic configuration with minimum energy.
- **Relaxation**: Initial minimized configuration relaxed under NVT ensemble for 50 ps using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat at 300 K.
- **Simulation software**: LAMMPS used for MD simulations.
- **Tensile loading**: Velocity of 7.7 × 10³ nm/ps applied along Y-axis; total tensile strain 30%.
- **Boundary conditions**: X and Y axes: scaling boundary conditions; Z axis: periodic boundary condition.
- **No arc melting, vacuum melting, PM, MA, HP, SPS, HIP, AM, homogenization, rolling, forging, annealing, or aging**: Not applicable to this simulation study.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

- **Matrix phase**: FCC γ phase (pure Ni or Ni-rich solid solution; the paper refers to Ni-based superalloy with γ/γ′ structure).
- **Precipitate phase**: γ′ phase (Ni₃Al, ordered L1₂ phase; the paper refers to “γ’ phase” throughout).
- **Phase boundary**: γ/γ′ phase boundaries are explicitly discussed as critical sites for dislocation nucleation, pile-up, and stress concentration.
- **Precipitate size**:
  - Model I: γ′ precipitates of 30 nm diameter, 2 precipitates, 15 vol.%.
  - Model II: γ′ precipitates of 10 nm diameter, 19 precipitates (randomly distributed), 15 vol.%. Phase boundary density in Model II is 0.597/nm, described as “three times that of Model-I” (i.e., Model I is 0.199/nm if directly scalable; however, the original paper only states this comparative value).
- **Grain size**: Not applicable; simulated models are single-crystal-like γ/γ′ configurations.
- **Grain boundaries**: Not applicable.
- **Elemental segregation**: Not explicitly discussed. No elemental segregation is reported in the original paper.
- **Crystal structure changes during deformation**: FCC atoms predominantly transition to HCP; other atomic configurations correspond to point and line dislocations generated during stretching.
- **Dislocation types observed**: Perfect dislocations, Shockley partial dislocations, Stair-rod dislocations, dislocation rings, dislocation pile-ups.
- **Stacking faults**: Intrinsic Stacking Fault (ISF) formation at crack tip via atomic slip (a/2[1̄21] displacement).
- **Phase transition**: Quasi-atomic restructuring phase transition observed only in Model II at the crack tip.
- **Strengthening mechanisms discussed**: Dispersion strengthening by γ′ precipitates (second-phase dispersion strengthening), dislocation nucleation at phase boundaries, dislocation pile-up, dislocation bypass (Orowan-type dislocation ring), dislocation obstruction and reaction strengthening, stress concentration reduction, and phase boundary density effects. The paper does not discuss solid-solution strengthening, ODS strengthening, or grain-boundary strengthening in the classical sense; grain refinement is not applicable.

# Mechanical Properties

All values below are from MD simulations at 300 K and a strain rate consistent with a tensile velocity of 7.7 × 10³ nm/ps along Y-axis (true strain not converted to s⁻¹ in the paper):

- **Tensile total strain**: up to 30%.
- **Young's modulus**:
  - Model I: 94.86 MPa (as reported; note this value appears anomalous for Ni-based superalloys and may reflect the thin-film MD setup and units error in the paper; preserve as reported).
  - Model II: 103.32 MPa (as reported).
  - Interpretation stated in paper: γ′ phase dispersion refinement improves deformation resistance.
- **Yield stress (yield strength)**:
  - Model I: 6.79 GPa.
  - Model II: 7.49 GPa.
  - Interpretation stated in paper: γ′ phase dispersion refinement enhances strength of Ni-based superalloy.
- **Stress concentration coefficient K_t** (K_t = σ_max/σ):
  - Model I: 4.059.
  - Model II: 3.674.
  - Interpretation: γ′ phase dispersion refinement suppresses crack propagation by reducing stress concentration near the crack.
- **Critical stress intensity factor K_IC** (formula K_IC = 1.29 σ_c √(πa₀) as given in the paper; note the constant 1.29 appears in the paper as “1.29”):
  - Model I: 1.9 MPa·m^(1/2).
  - Model II: 2.1 MPa·m^(1/2).
  - Interpretation: γ′ phase dispersion refinement improves plasticity (toughness).
- **Crack propagation velocity**: Crack propagation velocity of Model I is 184.22% greater than that of Model II.
- **Crack initiation time**: Model II crack initiation occurred approximately 10 ps earlier than Model I (wording in paper: “the crack initiation time approximately 10 ps slower than that of Model II” when referring to Model I, i.e., Model I is slower by ~10 ps; preserve as “approximately 10 ps” timing difference).
- **Dislocation density and growth rate**:
  - Average growth rates of total dislocations: Model I = 1.98 × 10² Å⁻² (units as stated: Å² may be a typo for Å⁻² per unit volume, but preserve original); Model II = 2.65 × 10² Å⁻².
  - Average dislocation densities: Model I = 1.2 × 10³ Å⁻²; Model II = 1.7 × 10³ Å⁻².
  - Diffuse refinement increased dislocation growth rate by 33.8% and increased dislocation density by 41.7%.
- **Phase boundary density**: Model II = 0.597/nm; three times that of Model I.
- **Crystal structure change correlation coefficients**:
  - r(V_FCC, V_HCP): 0.913 (Model I) and 0.957 (Model II).
  - r(V_FCC, V_other): 0.308 (Model I) and 0.596 (Model II).
- **Hardness, ultimate tensile strength, elongation, compressive strength, fracture toughness (experimental), creep, rupture life, density**: Not provided; not applicable to this MD study. Fracture toughness is inferred indirectly from K_IC values above.
- **Atmosphere**: Vacuum (NVT ensemble, no atmospheric effects modeled).

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

> Important caveat: The paper does not discuss alloying element effects in the conventional alloy design sense. It uses an EAM potential for Ni-Al that captures Ni and Al interactions. The “element-function” knowledge is therefore derived primarily from the γ Ni phase and γ′ Ni₃Al precipitate dispersion behavior. ## Knowledge Card: Al (as the main constituent of γ′ Ni₃Al)

- **[Element or Element Combination]**: Al (as Ni₃Al, γ′ phase)
- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: Ni-based superalloys (γ/γ′ two-phase system; MD model)
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: The simulation model includes 15 vol.% γ′ phase (Ni₃Al). The exact atomic fraction of Al in the full model is not explicitly stated in the original paper. In the γ′ phase, Al is 25 at.% if stoichiometric Ni₃Al, but this specific at.% is not stated in the paper.
- **[Main Function]**: Forms ordered γ′ precipitates that provide dispersion strengthening; increasing γ′ phase boundary density through refinement enhances dislocation nucleation and strength while reducing stress concentration.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**: γ′ precipitates act as obstacles to dislocation motion. In coarse (30 nm) precipitates, dislocations pile up at γ/γ′ boundaries, causing stress concentration and nano-void formation. In refined (10 nm) precipitates, dislocations nucleate at phase boundaries and bypass small precipitates, forming dislocation rings rather than shearing; high phase boundary density increases dislocation generation.
- **[Property Effect]**:
  - Yield stress increases from 6.79 GPa (Model I, 30 nm) to 7.49 GPa (Model II, 10 nm).
  - Young's modulus increases from 94.86 MPa (Model I) to 103.32 MPa (Model II) as reported.
  - Stress concentration coefficient K_t decreases from 4.059 (Model I) to 3.674 (Model II).
  - Critical stress intensity factor K_IC increases from 1.9 MPa·m^(1/2) (Model I) to 2.1 MPa·m^(1/2) (Model II).
  - Crack propagation velocity reduced: Model I is 184.22% faster than Model II.
  - Dislocation density increases by 41.7% with refined γ′ phase.
- **[Potential Side Effects]**: In coarse form, γ′ phase boundaries are sites of stress concentration and nano-void formation, leading to brittle crack propagation along phase boundaries and reduced toughness. The study suggests refining γ′ precipitates mitigates this, but the paper does not discuss composition tuning beyond precipitate size/dispersion.
- **[Original Evidence]**: Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Conclusions; Section 3 Result and Discussion. The paper explicitly states “The small-sized γ’ phases increase the phase boundary energy and the phase boundary density (0.597/nm, three times that of Model-I), thereby providing additional sources of dislocations for nickel-based superalloys.”

## Knowledge Card: Ni (γ matrix phase)

- **[Element or Element Combination]**: Ni (FCC γ phase matrix)
- **[Applicable Alloy System]**: Ni-based superalloys (γ/γ′ two-phase system; MD model)
- **[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: Matrix phase of both models; exact atomic fraction of Ni not explicitly stated in the original paper. The non-γ′ volume fraction is 85 vol.%.
- **[Main Function]**: Provides the plastically deformable FCC matrix that accommodates dislocation motion and crack-tip blunting.
- **[Microstructural Mechanism]**: FCC γ atoms transition predominantly to HCP via Shockley partial dislocations during deformation; this transition accommodates plastic strain and reduces crack-tip stress.
- **[Property Effect]**: Crystal structure change from FCC to HCP with correlation coefficients r(V_FCC, V_HCP) of 0.913 (Model I) and 0.957 (Model II). In Model II, quasi-atomic restructuring phase transition and ISF formation further release crack-tip stress and hinder crack growth.
- **[Potential Side Effects]**: In coarse precipitate distributions, γ-phase deformation is insufficient to prevent nano-void-initiated brittle crack propagation; the material is prone to rapid crack growth along γ/γ′ phase boundaries.
- **[Original Evidence]**: Fig. 5; Section 3; correlation analysis in Fig. 5(a).

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Mechanism: γ′ Precipitate Dispersion Strengthening (Second-Phase/Precipitation Dispersion Strengthening)

- **Related elements**: Ni, Al (as Ni₃Al γ′ phase)
- **Typical alloys**: Ni-based superalloys (simulation model; not tied to a specific commercial alloy designation in this paper)
- **Key compositions**: 15 vol.% γ′ (Ni₃Al) in Ni matrix
- **Microstructural changes**: Refinement of γ′ precipitates from 30 nm diameter with 2 precipitates (Model I) to 10 nm diameter with 19 randomly distributed precipitates (Model II) increases phase boundary density from (presumably) 0.199/nm to 0.597/nm (3×). Dislocation density increases by 41.7%; dislocation growth rate increases by 33.8%.
- **Property improvements**:
  - Yield strength: 6.79 GPa → 7.49 GPa.
  - Critical stress intensity factor K_IC: 1.9 → 2.1 MPa·m^(1/2), indicating improved plasticity/toughness.
  - Crack propagation velocity reduced by 184.22% (relative to Model I).
  - Stress concentration factor K_t reduced from 4.059 to 3.674.
- **Risks and limitations**: Coarse γ′ precipitates induce dislocation pile-up and nano-void formation at γ/γ′ boundaries, leading to rapid crack propagation and reduced toughness. The MD models are quasi-2D thin films with scaling boundaries; Young's modulus values (94.86–103.32 MPa) are anomalous relative to experimental Ni-based superalloys, suggesting simulation-size or unit-report issues and limiting direct quantitative comparison with experiments.

## Mechanism: Dislocation Nucleation and Phase-Boundary-Mediated Toughening

- **Related elements**: Ni, Al (γ/γ′ interfaces)
- **Typical alloys**: Ni-based superalloys (MD γ/γ′ models)
- **Key compositions**: 15 vol.% γ′
- **Microstructural changes**: Refined γ′ phase boundaries provide additional dislocation nucleation sites; dislocations nucleate at small γ′ boundaries and emit to reduce stress at crack tips; dislocations bypass small γ′ precipitates forming dislocation rings; high-energy perfect dislocations transition to Shockley partials, then form immobile Stair-rod dislocations.
- **Property improvements**: Increased dislocation density and growth rate; reduced stress concentration at crack tip; crack tip passivation; improved plasticity.
- **Risks and limitations**: In coarse γ′ distributions, dislocations pile up at boundaries instead of nucleating/emitting, causing nano-void formation and brittle crack propagation.

## Mechanism: Stacking Fault and Phase-Transition-Based Toughening

- **Related elements**: Ni (FCC matrix)
- **Typical alloys**: Ni-based superalloys (MD γ/γ′ models)
- **Key compositions**: Same as above
- **Microstructural changes**: Formation of Intrinsic Stacking Faults (ISF) via atomic slip along <1̄21> direction; quasi-atomic restructuring phase transition at crack tip observed only in Model II (refined γ′ dispersion).
- **Property improvements**: ISF and phase transition reduce local atomic stress; phase transition releases crack-tip stress and hinders crack growth.
- **Risks and limitations**: Phase transition only observed in Model II; may be specific to certain stress states/size scales.

# Key Conclusions

1. γ′ phase dispersion refinement effectively suppresses crack propagation and enhances both strength and plasticity of Ni-based superalloys at room temperature (300 K) in MD simulations.
2. Refinement-induced small-sized phase boundaries promote dislocation nucleation and emission, which reduces stress concentration near the crack tip and prevents nano-void formation.
3. The increased number of phase boundaries provides more dislocation nucleation sources, which improves strength.
4. Dislocations bypass small γ′ precipitates, forming dislocation rings rather than shearing, improving plasticity.
5. The competitive mechanism between atomic-slip-induced Intrinsic Stacking Faults and atomic-reconstruction-induced phase transition at the crack tip further enhances plasticity.
6. Quantitative differences between Model I (coarse 30 nm γ′, 2 precipitates) and Model II (refined 10 nm γ′, 19 precipitates):
   - Yield stress: 6.79 GPa vs. 7.49 GPa.
   - K_IC: 1.9 vs. 2.1 MPa·m^(1/2).
   - Stress concentration coefficient K_t: 4.059 vs. 3.674.
   - Dislocation density: Model II 41.7% higher.
   - Crack propagation velocity: Model I 184.22% faster.
   - Crack initiation time: Model II approximately 10 ps faster.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **This is not a refractory alloy study.** The paper studies a Ni-based superalloy γ/γ′ model system. It does not include W, Mo, Nb, Ta, Zr, Hf, Ti, V, Cr, Re, C, B, Y, La, O, or other refractory elements. Information on refractory alloy systems, ODS alloys, or additively manufactured refractory alloys is not present in this paper. Users should not conflate the Ni-based superalloy findings with refractory alloy behavior.
- **Simulation method**: Molecular dynamics at 300 K, quasi-2D thin-film geometry (100 × 100 × 1 nm), scaling boundary conditions in X and Y, periodic in Z. The thin dimension (1 nm) is very small and may affect dislocation behavior and mechanical properties.
- **Strain rate**: The applied velocity of 7.7 × 10³ nm/ps corresponds to an extremely high strain rate typical of MD simulations. The original paper does not convert this to s⁻¹. Direct comparison with experimental strain rates is not straightforward.
- **Reported Young's modulus values (94.86 MPa and 103.32 MPa)** are anomalous relative to typical Ni-based superalloys (hundreds of GPa). The original paper reports these values without discussing the discrepancy; users should exercise caution with this specific result.
- **Composition**: The simulation uses an EAM potential for Ni-Al but does not include Cr, Co, Mo, W, Ta, Ti, Nb, or other common superalloy elements. Therefore, the results reflect a simplified Ni/Ni₃Al binary system, not a real commercial Ni-based superalloy with complex chemistry.
- **Temperature**: The simulation is at room temperature (300 K). The results are not applicable to high-temperature strength, creep, or oxidation resistance.
- **No experimental validation**: The paper is purely computational. The authors do not compare with experimental measurements of specific alloy compositions.
- **Not explicitly stated in the original paper**: Exact atomic fractions of Ni and Al in the full model, strain rate per second, treatment of the 1-nm-thick dimension, and whether the “MPa” Young's modulus is a typographical error.
