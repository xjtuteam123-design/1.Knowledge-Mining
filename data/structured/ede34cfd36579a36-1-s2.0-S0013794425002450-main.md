# Paper Metadata

- **Paper title**: Semi-coherent nanoprecipitates induced by Nb microalloying enable high-strength and hydrogen-resistant interstitial high-entropy alloy
- **Authors**: Zhenghong Fu, Pengfei Wu, Xu Zhang, Zhiming Li, Yong Zhang
- **Journal**: Engineering Fracture Mechanics
- **Year**: 2025
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.engfracmech.2025.111044
- **Alloy system studied**: Fe-Mn-Co-Cr-C interstitial high-entropy alloy (iHEA) with Nb microalloying
- **Research objective**: To simultaneously improve strength and hydrogen embrittlement (HE) resistance by introducing semi-coherent nano-NbC precipitates in Nb-microalloyed FeMnCoCr-C interstitial high-entropy alloy
- **Material category**: Interstitial high-entropy alloy (iHEA); face-centered cubic (FCC) structural high-entropy alloy with TRIP/TWIP effects

# Alloy System and Research Object

The paper studies two variants of Fe-Mn-Co-Cr-C interstitial high-entropy alloys:
- **0Nb iHEA**: Fe₄₉.₅Mn₃₀Co₁₀Cr₁₀C₀.₅ (at.%)
- **0.05Nb iHEA**: Fe₄₉.₄₅Mn₃₀Co₁₀Cr₁₀C₀.₅Nb₀.₀₅ (at.%)

A comparison alloy CoCrFeMnNi (Cantor alloy, equiatomic) was also tested under identical processing for HE resistance benchmarking.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

The processing route for both iHEAs was as follows:
1. **Vacuum arc melting**: under high-purity Ar protection, repeated at least five times
2. **Hot rolling**: at 1,000 °C to a thickness reduction of 50%
3. **Homogenization annealing**: at 1,200 °C for 2 h, followed by water quenching
4. **Cold rolling**: to a thickness reduction of 50%
5. **Recrystallization annealing**: at 850 °C for 30 min (achieves full recrystallization and NbC precipitation)

The CoCrFeMnNi comparison alloy was prepared using the same process as the current iHEAs.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## Matrix Phase
- **Both 0Nb and 0.05Nb iHEAs**: Single-phase FCC structure
- Confirmed by selected-area diffraction pattern (insert in Fig. 2a)
- Fully recrystallized with random crystal orientation after annealing at 850 °C for 30 min

## Grain Size
- **0Nb iHEA**: mean grain size 5.9 μm
- **0.05Nb iHEA**: mean grain size 2.1 μm (approximately 2.8 times smaller than 0Nb)
- Nb microalloying stabilizes grain boundaries and inhibits grain growth during recrystallization

## Second Phases / Precipitates
- **0Nb iHEA**: No particles observed inside grains; homogeneous elemental distribution
- **0.05Nb iHEA**: NbC carbide nanoparticles, uniformly dispersed in grain interiors; some coarse particles along grain boundaries
  - NbC particle shape: oval and hexagonal
  - NbC particle size: primarily 5–10 nm; mean size 8.36 nm
  - NbC/matrix interface: semi-coherent
  - Lattice parameter of NbC: 0.42 nm
  - Lattice parameter of FCC matrix: 0.36 nm
  - Theoretical lattice misfit δ: 15.38%
  - Cube-on-cube orientation relationship: [011]matrix // [011]NbC and (200)matrix // (200)NbC
  - Misfit dislocation array spacing: L = 1.88 nm
  - GPA analysis: tensile strains ~5% and compressive strains ~10% inside NbC

## Annealing Twins
- Many annealing twins observed on IPF maps of both alloys

## Dislocations and Stacking Faults
- **0Nb iHEA**: individual dislocations and stacking faults (SFs) observed in grain interior
- **0.05Nb iHEA**: dislocations, SFs, and nanoparticles observed in grain interior and at grain boundaries

## Elemental Segregation
- 0Nb iHEA: EDS mappings confirm homogeneous distribution of the five elements
- 0.05Nb iHEA: NbC precipitates confirmed by element distribution map

## Strengthening Mechanisms Discussed
- Solid-solution strengthening (interstitial C)
- Precipitation strengthening (NbC nanoparticles)
- Grain-boundary strengthening / grain refinement (Nb microalloying)
- TRIP/TWIP effects in the matrix
- Second-phase strengthening by semi-coherent nano-NbC

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

The paper does not discuss oxidation resistance. The focus is on hydrogen embrittlement resistance. The following HE-related information is relevant:

- **Hydrogen embrittlement resistance**: The 0.05Nb iHEA shows the best HE resistance among the three tested alloys (strength loss 17.4%, plasticity loss 58.4%)
- **Hydrogen trapping**: TDA shows low-temperature desorption peak at ~120 °C (diffusible hydrogen in matrix) and high-temperature desorption peak at ~460 °C (irreversible traps). The 0.05Nb iHEA shows higher peak temperature and content at high temperature compared to 0Nb iHEA, attributed to hydrogen dissolved at the NbC/γ semi-coherent interface
- **Hydrogen trapping site density (Nt)**: Calculated as 2.67 × 10⁻⁹ mol/mm³ for 0.05Nb iHEA, consistent with literature range of 10⁻¹⁰–10⁻⁷ mol/mm³
- **Hydrogen adsorption energy**: At semi-coherent NbC/matrix interface, adsorption energy can reach 81.8 kJ/mol (cited from Shi et al. for NbC/α-Fe interface)
- **Cracking mode**: 0Nb iHEA shows almost complete intergranular (IG) cracking under in-situ hydrogen charging; 0.05Nb iHEA shows significantly reduced IG cracking percentage

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

**Related elements**: C (interstitial), Fe, Mn, Co, Cr (substitutional)

**Typical alloys**: Fe-Mn-Co-Cr-C interstitial high-entropy alloy (iHEA)

**Key compositions**: Fe₄₉.₅Mn₃₀Co₁₀Cr₁₀C₀.₅ (at.%)

**Microstructural changes**: Interstitial C atoms in FCC lattice create lattice distortion; no additional phase transformation at room temperature

**Property improvements**: Interstitial C contributes to strength; the paper does not quantify the individual solid-solution contribution separate from other strengthening mechanisms

**Risks and limitations**: Interstitial C can form chromium carbides, which are detrimental to hydrogen embrittlement resistance; excessive C may lead to coarse carbide precipitation

## Carbide Precipitation Strengthening (NbC)

**Related elements**: Nb + C

**Typical alloys**: Fe₄₉.₄₅Mn₃₀Co₁₀Cr₁₀C₀.₅Nb₀.₀₅ (at.%) iHEA; also applied in high-strength steels and CoCrFeMnNi HEA

**Key compositions**: 0.05 at.% Nb + 0.5 at.% C → nano-NbC particles (mean size 8.36 nm)

**Microstructural changes**:
- Semi-coherent NbC/γ interface with 15.38% misfit
- Misfit dislocation array spacing 1.88 nm
- Cube-on-cube orientation relationship
- Uniformly dispersed nano-NbC in grain interiors

**Property improvements**:
- YS increased to 695 MPa
- UTS increased to 1,049 MPa
- Enhanced HE resistance: strength loss 17.4%, plasticity loss 58.4%
- Hydrogen trapping site density 2.67 × 10⁻⁹ mol/mm³

**Risks and limitations**:
- Coarse NbC particles (e.g., ~42 nm in CoCrFeMnNi with high Nb + C content) may have different interface characteristics (coherent vs semi-coherent)
- Coarse particles may serve as crack initiation sites
- Interface coherency changes with particle size; misfit increases from 15% to 20% as size increases from 7.5 nm to 21.1 nm

## Grain-Boundary Strengthening / Grain Refinement

**Related elements**: Nb (through NbC precipitation and grain boundary pinning)

**Typical alloys**: Fe₄₉.₄₅Mn₃₀Co₁₀Cr₁₀C₀.₅Nb₀.₀₅ (at.%) iHEA

**Key compositions**: 0.05 at.% Nb microalloying

**Microstructural changes**:
- Grain size reduced from 5.9 μm to 2.1 μm
- NbC nanoparticles pin dislocations and grain boundaries during recrystallization
- More grain boundaries per volume

**Property improvements**:
- Contributes to strength improvement (combined with precipitation strengthening)
- Reduced TRIP/TWIP effect due to smaller grain size (inhibits martensite and twin growth)
- Lower local stress concentration at grain boundaries under hydrogen charging

**Risks and limitations**:
- Grain refinement suppresses TRIP/TWIP, which may affect work hardening capacity
- The paper does not separately quantify grain-boundary strengthening contribution from precipitation strengthening

## TRIP/TWIP Effects

**Related elements**: Mn, C (controlling SFE)

**Typical alloys**: Fe-Mn-Co-Cr-C interstitial high-entropy alloy (iHEA)

**Key compositions**: Fe₄₉.₅Mn₃₀Co₁₀Cr₁₀C₀.₅ (at.%) with SFE = 18 mJ/m²

**Microstructural changes**:
- ε-martensite transformation during straining
- Deformation twins
- Stacking faults
- In 0.05Nb iHEA, NbC and grain refinement suppress growth of ε-martensites and deformation twins

**Property improvements**:
- TRIP/TWIP contribute to strength-ductility synergy
- Hydrogen promotes TRIP/TWIP by lowering SFE, which can be detrimental

**Risks and limitations**:
- Impinging ε-martensites and DTs at grain boundaries induce stress concentration and intergranular cracking in hydrogen environment
- Suppression of TRIP/TWIP by NbC and grain refinement reduces grain boundary stress concentration but also reduces work hardening

## Hydrogen-Resistant Alloying (Irreversible Hydrogen Trapping)

**Related elements**: Nb, C (forming NbC); also V, Ti (VC, TiC as alternatives cited in literature)

**Typical alloys**: Fe-Mn-Co-Cr-C iHEA with Nb microalloying; also applied in high-strength steels

**Key compositions**: 0.05 at.% Nb → nano-NbC with semi-coherent interface

**Microstructural changes**:
- Misfit dislocation cores at NbC/matrix interface as deep hydrogen trapping sites
- Hydrogen adsorption energy: up to 81.8 kJ/mol at semi-coherent interface
- Hydrogen trapping site density: 2.67 × 10⁻⁹ mol/mm³
- Reduced diffusible hydrogen in matrix

**Property improvements**:
- Strength loss under hydrogen: 17.4% (vs 36.5% for 0Nb)
- Plasticity loss under hydrogen: 58.4% (vs 83.8% for 0Nb)
- TE under hydrogen: 16% (highest among tested iHEAs, comparable to CoCrFeMnNi)

**Risks and limitations**:
- Interface coherency depends on particle size; too small particles (<4.1 nm) form coherent interfaces, large particles increase misfit (up to 20% at 21.1 nm)
- The exact optimum particle size for maximizing hydrogen trapping while maintaining strength contribution is not established in this paper
- The paper does not address long-term stability of the hydrogen trapping capacity under cyclic hydrogen exposure

# Key Conclusions

1. **Grain refinement via Nb microalloying**: Adding 0.05 at.% Nb to Fe₄₉.₅Mn₃₀Co₁₀Cr₁₀C₀.₅ iHEA reduces grain size from 5.9 μm to 2.1 μm after recrystallization at 850 °C for 30 min.

2. **Semi-coherent nano-NbC formation**: Nano-NbC particles with mean size 8.36 nm are formed; they are semi-coherent with the FCC matrix with 15.38% misfit and cube-on-cube orientation relationship.

3. **Simultaneous strength and HE resistance improvement**: Nb microalloying increases YS to 695 MPa and UTS to 1,049 MPa while significantly enhancing HE resistance (strength loss reduced from 36.5% to 17.4%; plasticity loss reduced from 83.8% to 58.4%).

4. **Mechanism of HE resistance improvement**: The semi-coherent NbC/matrix interface acts as deep hydrogen trapping site (trapping site density 2.67 × 10⁻⁹ mol/mm³), reducing diffusible hydrogen. NbC particles also suppress dislocation slip, inhibiting growth of ε-martensites and deformation twins, thereby reducing stress concentration at grain boundaries and intergranular cracking.

5. **Suppression of harmful chromium carbides**: Nb preferentially combines with C, inhibiting formation of chromium carbides that are harmful to HE resistance.

6. **Feasibility of concurrent strength and HE resistance enhancement**: The work demonstrates that semi-coherent nano-carbide introduction is an effective strategy to overcome the strength-HE resistance trade-off.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

1. **Alloy system specific**: The conclusions are drawn from Fe-Mn-Co-Cr-C interstitial high-entropy alloys with TRIP/TWIP effects. The strategy may not be directly applicable to refractory alloys, BCC-structured HEAs, or alloys without metastable austenite.

2. **Microalloying level**: The Nb addition is at a very low level (0.05 at.%). The effects at higher Nb contents are not investigated; literature suggests that higher Nb + C content in CoCrFeMnNi leads to coarse NbC (~42 nm) with coherent interface, which may have different hydrogen trapping characteristics.

3. **No direct measurement of hydrogen diffusivity**: The paper does not directly measure hydrogen diffusion coefficients; it relies on TDA, trapping site calculations, and mechanical response.

4. **Tensile testing only**: Mechanical properties are evaluated only under uniaxial tension; other loading modes (compression, fatigue, creep) are not addressed.

5. **Room-temperature HE testing**: HE resistance is evaluated at room temperature under electrochemical hydrogen charging; the behavior at elevated temperatures or under gaseous hydrogen is not addressed.

6. **Quantification of strengthening contributions**: The paper does not quantitatively separate the contributions of grain refinement, solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, and TRIP/TWIP effects to the overall strength.

7. **Oxidation resistance**: Not addressed in this paper; hydrogen embrittlement is the primary environmental concern studied.

8. **Thermal stability**: While NbC is stated to have high thermal stability (decomposition temperature ~1,200 °C), the long-term thermal stability of the semi-coherent interface and its hydrogen trapping capacity is not investigated.

9. **Comparison alloy limitation**: The CoCrFeMnNi comparison alloy is included for HE resistance benchmarking but is not processed identically in terms of optimized thermomechanical treatment (though the paper states it was "prepared by using the same process as the current iHEAs").
