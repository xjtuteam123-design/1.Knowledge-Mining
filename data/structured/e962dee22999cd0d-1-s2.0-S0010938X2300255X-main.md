# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Long term oxidation of NiCoCrAlY coated Ni-based superalloys: A comparison of observed and simulated interdiffusion
- **Authors**: K.A. Kane, R.R. Pillai, M.J. Lance, B.A. Pint
- **Journal**: Corrosion Science
- **Year**: 2023
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.corsci.2023.111213
- **Alloy system studied**: NiCoCrAlY coating on three Ni-based superalloys (PWA 1483, MARM247, CMSX-4)
- **Research objective**: To study interdiffusion rates between NiCoCrAlY coatings and Ni-based superalloy substrates during long-term 900 °C oxidation, compare observed rates with Thermo-Calc/DICTRA simulations, and project coating lifetimes based on β-NiAl depletion criteria.
- **Material category**: NiCoCrAlY coated Ni-based superalloys (MCrAlY-type bondcoat / superalloy diffusion couple system). Not a refractory high-temperature alloy, Nb-W-based alloy, Mo-W-based alloy, refractory high-entropy alloy, or additively manufactured refractory alloy. This paper is about MCrAlY coatings on Ni-based superalloys.

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **System category**: MCrAlY-type coating / Ni-based superalloy diffusion couple
- **Coating**: NiCoCrAlY (same composition used for all substrates)
- **Substrates studied**: 
  1. PWA 1483 (abbreviated 1483) – single crystal, 2nd generation superalloy
  2. MARM247 (abbreviated 247) – first generation polycrystalline directionally solidified superalloy
  3. CMSX-4 (abbreviated X4) – single crystal, 2nd generation superalloy
- **Substrate selection rationale**: The three superalloys were chosen based on their varying amounts of Al, ranging from 7.3 at% for 1483, 11.8 at% for 247, and 13.0 at% for X4.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

## Coating Deposition
- **Process**: High velocity oxygen fuel (HVOF) deposition, standard commercial-type process
- **Substrate preparation**: Grit blasted with alumina prior to HVOF deposition
- **Coating thickness**: 
  - X4 substrates: nominally 200 μm
  - 247 and 1483 substrates: nominally 180 μm

## Post-Deposition Heat Treatment
- **Vacuum anneal**: 4 h at 1080 °C in vacuum of 10⁻⁴ Pa (10⁻⁶ Torr)

## Oxidation Testing
- **Exposure temperature**: 900 °C
- **Exposure durations**: 5 kh, 10 kh, 20 kh (500 h cycles)
- **Atmosphere**: Air + 10 ± 1% H₂O
- **Heating procedure**: Heated to temperature over ~4 h in alumina tube with flowing argon, then held for 500 h in flowing air + 10% H₂O
- **Carrier gas flow**: 500 ml/min
- **Water introduction**: Distilled water atomized into gas stream above its condensation temperature; injected water measured to calibrate concentration
- **Cycle procedure**: Samples taken out, weighed, and visually inspected after each 500 h cycle

## Computational Modeling
- **Software**: Thermo-Calc/DICTRA
- **Databases**: TCNI8 and MOBNI4
- **Modeling approach**: Single region diffusion couple with discrete composition change at the interface; rule of mixtures as homogenization function
- **Elements considered**: 
  - X4 and 247: Ni-Co-Cr-Al (quaternary)
  - 1483: Ni-Co-Cr-Al (quaternary) and Ni-Co-Cr-Al-Ti (quinternary)
- **Phases considered**: β, γ, γ′ only (mobility data for other phases unavailable in MOBNI4)

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## As-Deposited Coating Microstructure
- Thermo-Calc phase maps calculated initial 20% γ-phase and 80% β-phase at nominal 23 at% Al coating composition
- Image analysis determined ratio closer to 1:1 γ:β
- Discrepancy attributed to Al loss to alumina formation during HVOF deposition
- Phase maps at 18 at% Al (representing "as-sprayed" composition) calculated near 1:1 β:γ ratio

## Microstructure After 900 °C Exposure (20 kh, X4 system)
From ambient interface inward:
1. Alumina TGO (thermally grown oxide)
2. TGO β-depletion zone (TBZ) – γ-phase region
3. NiCoCrAlY coating – primarily β/γ
4. β-depleted interdiffusion zone (IDZ) – comprised of γ and γ′
5. Physical coating-substrate interface – marked by α-Al₂O₃ grit from substrate preparation
6. Region containing topologically close packed (TCP) precipitates in γ matrix (substrate side)
7. γ/γ′ X4 superalloy substrate

## Phase Transformations
- β-NiAl → γ′ transformation during interdiffusion
- TCP phase formation: Previous calculations predicted TCP phases as σ-phase; EDS analysis indicated enrichment in Re and W
- β-NiAl depletion occurs via two mechanisms:
  - Al oxidation at the ambient interface (TGO formation → TBZ)
  - Coating-substrate interdiffusion (→ IDZ)

## Elemental Diffusion Across Coating-Substrate Interface
- **Diffusing from coating into substrate**: Al (primary diffusing species), Cr, Co
- **Diffusing from substrate into coating**: Ni, Ta, W, Ti
- Al and Cr diffusion into substrate increases their activities at interface; combined with low mobility of refractory elements (Mo, Re, W), results in enrichment of refractory elements at the interface and TCP phase formation

## Phase Map Analysis
- Quaternary Ni-Co-Cr-Al phase maps at 900 °C show:
  - β-NiAl depletion occurs due to both Al loss and reduction in γ′ suppression as Co and Cr concentrations decrease
  - Co suppresses γ′ formation
  - TGO β-depletion zone forms as Al content decreases and Cr/Co locally enrich, encouraging γ-phase formation

## Coating Recession / Kirkendall Effect
- Coating recession observed over 5–20 kh in all systems
- Physical coating-substrate interface observed to move away from substrate
- Up to 38% volume decrease accompanying β → γ′ transformation
- Phase transformation volume shrinkage cannot fully account for observed recession
- Movement attributed to Kirkendall effect
- At 900 °C, interdiffusion sufficiently slow such that interfacial movement rather than void formation occurs (void formation reported at 950 °C, 1000 °C, 1050 °C, and ≥1100 °C in prior literature)

## β-Depletion Observations
- TGO and TBZ widths did not change significantly over 5–20 kh for X4 system
- β/γ microstructure slightly depleted while simultaneously coarsening
- IDZ width increases with exposure time
- β-depletion in vicinity of oxidizing interface increased for 20 kh 1483 sample

## Strengthening Mechanisms Discussed
This paper does not discuss traditional strengthening mechanisms (solid-solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, etc.) of refractory alloys. The "mechanism" concepts in this paper relate to:
- β-NiAl depletion mechanisms (oxidation-induced and interdiffusion-induced)
- TCP phase formation mechanisms
- γ′ phase suppression by Co
- Kirkendall effect-driven interfacial movement

# Oxidation Resistance and Service Stability

## Oxidation Testing Conditions
- Temperature: 900 °C
- Atmosphere: Air + 10 ± 1% H₂O
- Duration: 5–20 kh (500 h cycles)

## Oxidized Layer
- **TGO composition**: Alumina (Al₂O₃)
- Alumina TGO forms at ambient interface, locally consuming Al and depleting β-NiAl

## TGO Thickness
- Average TGO thickness did not change significantly over 5–20 kh for all coating-substrate systems
- Average TGO and TBZ widths reported (Fig. 6)

## TBZ (TGO β-depletion Zone)
- Width did not significantly change over 5–20 kh for most systems
- Exception: 20 kh NiCoCrAlY-1483 sample showed increased β-depletion near oxidizing interface
- γ-phase region forms adjacent to TGO; enriches in Cr and Co content with continued exposure
- Al content in γ-phase adjacent to TGO in X4 system decreased from ~13 at% at 5 kh to ~10 at% at 10 kh, remaining at ~10 at% at 20 kh

## Protective Oxide Considerations
- β-phase acts as Al reservoir, maintaining Al concentration high enough in TGO vicinity to encourage alumina formation
- γ-phase enriched region near TGO may be detrimental to alumina formation if localized oxide/coating spallation occurs, as high Cr and Co content suppresses Al diffusion from β/γ region

## Potential Issues
- **TCP phase formation**: Mo, Re, W enrichment at coating-substrate interface promotes TCP phase formation; appreciable TCP phase precipitated on substrate side of interface after 20 kh
- **Substrate element penetration**: Ta, W, Ti observed in IDZ (~200–250 μm) of NiCoCrAlY-X4 system at 20 kh; Ti and Ta can stabilize γ′; W and Re promote TCP formation
- **Oxide degradation**: When substrate elements reach TGO vicinity, less protective oxides (TiO₂, spinels) may form and initiate spallation
- **Coating recession due to Kirkendall effect**: All systems showed significant coating recession after 5–20 kh

## Effect of Substrate Al Content on Lifetime
- Higher substrate Al content → lower β-depletion rate
- X4 (13.0 at% Al): lifetime ~40 kh
- 247 (11.8 at% Al): lifetime ~37.5 kh
- 1483 (7.3 at% Al): lifetime ~31.5 kh

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## [Al in NiCoCrAlY coating]

[Applicable Alloy System]
NiCoCrAlY coating on Ni-based superalloys

[Addition Amount or Composition Range]
23.0 at% (nominal coating composition); reduced to ~18 at% Al in as-sprayed condition due to oxidation during HVOF deposition

[Main Function]
Provides Al reservoir for alumina TGO formation; determines coating lifetime via β-NiAl phase (β-phase acts as Al reservoir)

[Microstructural Mechanism]
Outward diffusion of Al from coating into substrate is the primary mechanism of β-NiAl depletion. TGO formation locally consumes Al, creating γ-phase depleted region (TBZ). β-NiAl phase acts as Al reservoir maintaining high Al concentration in TGO vicinity for alumina formation.

[Property Effect]
Higher initial substrate Al content results in lower β-depletion rate:
- 1483 (7.3 at% Al substrate): lifetime ~31.5 kh
- 247 (11.8 at% Al substrate): lifetime ~37.5 kh
- X4 (13.0 at% Al substrate): lifetime ~40 kh

[Potential Side Effects]
Al diffusion into substrate increases Al activity at interface, contributing (with Cr increase and refractory element concentration) to TCP phase formation.

[Original Evidence]
"The primary mechanism then of ß -NiAl depletion is the outward diffusion of Al from the coating into the substrate." (Section 4.1)
"higher Al content in substrate → lower β-depletion rates" (Section 5, Conclusions)

## [Co in NiCoCrAlY coating / Ni-based superalloy]

[Applicable Alloy System]
NiCoCrAlY coating on Ni-based superalloys

[Addition Amount or Composition Range]
Coating: 18.0 at% Co; substrates: 8.8–9.9 at% Co

[Main Function]
Suppresses γ′ phase formation; use of Co extends the two-phase β/γ field towards lower temperatures

[Microstructural Mechanism]
Co suppresses formation of γ′ phase. In the coating, Co diffuses into the substrate (opposite direction to Al). Co and Cr enrich in the IDZ while Al reaches a minimum.

[Property Effect]
Co mobility is low compared to Al; β-depletion dominated by Al diffusion rather than Co diffusion. Co depletion from coating via interdiffusion is not detrimental from a β-depletion standpoint.

[Potential Side Effects]
γ-phase region near TGO enriches in Co with continued exposure; high Co content suppresses Al diffusion from β/γ region, potentially detrimental to alumina formation if spallation occurs.

[Original Evidence]
"The two-phase β / γ field can be extended towards lower temperatures via the judicious addition of Co, which suppresses formation of γ′" (Section 4.1)
"Co and Cr reach a maximum [in the IDZ]" (from Fig. 8 and Section 4.1)

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

**Note**: This paper does not discuss traditional strengthening mechanisms of refractory alloys. The relevant mechanisms are:

## [Interdiffusion mechanisms in MCrAlY-coated Ni-based superalloys]

Related elements: Ni, Co, Cr, Al, Ti, Ta, W, Mo, Re

Typical systems: NiCoCrAlY coating on PWA 1483, MARM247, CMSX-4

Key compositions:
- Coating: 42.6Ni-16Cr-23Al-18Co-0.4Y (at%)
- Substrates with varying Al: 7.3 at% (1483), 11.8 at% (247), 13.0 at% (X4)

Microstructural changes:
- Formation of β-NiAl depleted IDZ (γ + γ′ phases)
- Formation of TGO β-depletion zone (γ-phase)
- TCP phase precipitation on substrate side of interface
- Kirkendall effect-driven interfacial movement

Property effects (lifetime):
- β-depletion rates correlated with substrate Al content
- Simulated lifetimes at 900 °C: 1483 ~31.5 kh, 247 ~37.5 kh, X4 ~40 kh

Risks/limitations:
- DICTRA simulations overpredict diffusion rates due to underrepresentation of γ′ phase in quaternary (Ni-Co-Cr-Al) composition models
- TCP phase formation not captured in current models (mobility data unavailable in MOBNI4)
- Grain microstructure effects neglected

## [β-NiAl phase depletion as lifetime criterion]

Related elements: Ni, Al

Typical systems: NiCoCrAlY, MCrAlY bond coats

Key mechanism:
- Oxidation: Al consumed by TGO formation → γ-phase TBZ formation
- Interdiffusion: Al diffuses from coating into substrate → β-depletion, γ/γ′ IDZ formation
- β → γ′ transformation involves up to 38% volume decrease

Property effect:
- Complete β-depletion defines coating end-of-life
- TGO and TBZ widths did not change significantly over 5–20 kh
- IDZ width increases with exposure time

# Key Conclusions

1. **Coating recession observed in all systems**: Significant coating recession due to interdiffusion occurred over 5–20 kh of 900 °C exposure in all coated superalloy systems. Phase transformation volume shrinkage (38% β → γ′) cannot fully account for the recession; Kirkendall effect-driven interfacial movement is the primary cause.

2. **TGO stability**: TGO growth rates and associated β-depletion at the oxidizing interface did not change significantly over 20 kh for most systems. Exception: 20 kh NiCoCrAlY-1483 sample showed increased β-depletion in vicinity of oxidizing interface.

3. **Primary diffusion direction**: Al is the primary diffusing species from the coating into the substrates during interdiffusion. Cr and Co also diffuse into substrate; Ni, Ta, W, and Ti diffuse into coating.

4. **Substrate Al content correlates with lifetime**: Lower β-depletion rates correlated with higher Al content substrates. Projected 900 °C coating lifetimes:
   - 1483 (7.3 at% Al): ~31.5 kh
   - 247 (11.8 at% Al): ~37.5 kh
   - X4 (13.0 at% Al): ~40 kh

5. **DICTRA modeling performance**:
   - Quaternary Ni-Co-Cr-Al compositions reproduced interdiffusion differences between 247 and X4 systems reasonably well
   - Inclusion of Ti for 1483 (necessary for γ′ microstructure) resulted in inaccurate, slowest interdiffusion rates
   - Simulations generally overpredicted diffusion rates due to underprediction of γ′ phase fraction in the substrate
   - Effect of oxidation on β-depletion approximated as constant extent at the oxidizing interface

6. **TCP phase formation**: TCP phases (predicted σ-phase, enriched in Re and W) precipitated on substrate side of coating-substrate interface; not captured in current DICTRA simulations due to unavailable mobility data in MOBNI4.

7. **Future modeling needs**: Must incorporate full superalloy element compositions, account for TCP phases, include oxidation mechanism changes from substrate element diffusion to TGO vicinity, and develop methods for grain microstructure effects.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

## Limitations of the study
- **Substrate coating coverage**: Coupons coated on only one face; mass change data not informative for NiCoCrAlY oxidation kinetics
- **DICTRA modeling limitations**:
  - Only Ni-Co-Cr-Al considered for 247 and X4; Ti additionally included for 1483
  - Re, W, Ta, Mo not included in simulations
  - Only β, γ, γ′ phases considered (MOBNI4 mobility data unavailable for TCP phases)
  - TCP phase formation effect on interdiffusion not captured
  - Al loss during HVOF deposition not accounted for in initial coating composition
  - Grain microstructure effects neglected
- **Quinary composition for 1483**: Ti inclusion resulted in least accurate interdiffusion rate simulation; possible issues with Ti mobility data in MOBNI4 or the 0.01 at% Ti incorporation in coating
- **Experimental scope**: Exposures limited to 20 kh; some time thresholds not experimentally observable
- **Correlation of β-depletion at oxidizing interface and from interdiffusion**: Assumed not related for most systems, but may break down for 20 kh 1483 sample

## Applicable scope
- NiCoCrAlY coatings on Ni-based superalloys at 900 °C in air + 10% H₂O
- Long-term oxidation and interdiffusion behavior in stationary gas turbine conditions
- β-NiAl depletion-based lifetime prediction for MCrAlY-type coatings
- DICTRA/CALPHAD simulation of interdiffusion in coating/superalloy systems
- Not directly applicable to refractory high-temperature alloys, Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based alloys, or refractory high-entropy alloys
