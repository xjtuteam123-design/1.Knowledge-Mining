# Paper Metadata

- **Title**: Evolution of microstructure and mechanical properties of Ti-Nb-Zr and Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn alloys in severe plastic deformation
- **Authors**: A.Yu. Eroshenko, E.V. Legostaeva, P.V. Uvarkin, A.I. Tolmachev, M.A. Khimich, V.P. Kuznetsov, S.I. Stepanov, I.A. Vorontsov, G.Zh. Mukanov, Yu.P. Sharkeev
- **Journal**: Materials Letters
- **Year**: 2025 (Volume 382, Article 137805; available online 30 November 2024)
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.matlet.2024.137805
- **Alloy system studied**: Ti-Nb-Zr (TNZ) and Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn (TNZTS) β-titanium alloys

# Alloy System and Research Object

- **Material category**: β-Ti alloys (biomedical titanium alloys) with BCC β-phase matrix; not a refractory high-entropy alloy or Nb-W/Mo-W-based refractory alloy in the traditional sense. The alloys contain Nb, Zr, Ta, Sn (and Ti as the base element), but they are β-titanium alloys for medical implant applications.
- **Research objective**: To study the influence of severe plastic deformation (SPD) on the microstructure and mechanical properties of Ti-Nb-Zr and Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn alloys, specifically the formation of ultrafine-grained (UFG) structures and their impact on strength, ductility, and Young's modulus.

# Processing and Heat Treatment

- **Starting material**: Hot-rolled alloy rods smelted at VSMPO-AVISMA Corporation (Verkhnyaya Salda, Russia).
- **Severe plastic deformation (SPD)**: The UFG structure was obtained by SPD including uniaxial pressing and multi-pass rolling in grooved rolls, followed by pre-recrystallization annealing. Specific SPD temperature, strain per pass, total strain, and annealing parameters are not explicitly stated in the original paper, except that pre-recrystallization annealing was performed (details referenced to Ref. [8]).
- **Coarse-crystalline (CG) state**: Recrystallization annealing of UFG samples at 800 °C for 1 h.
- **No arc melting, powder metallurgy, mechanical alloying, hot pressing, spark plasma sintering, hot isostatic pressing, or additive manufacturing** was used in this study.

# Microstructure and Phase Constitution

## TNZ alloy

### CG state
- **Matrix**: Equiaxed grains of BCC β-phase with average grain size 15 μm.
- **Second phase**: Spherical particles of hexagonal simple (HS) α-phase along matrix grain boundaries, size 30–50 nm.
- **ω-phase**: Ellipsoidal ω-phase (HCP) particles, 6–10 nm, within matrix grains; confirmed by low-intensity extra reflections in SAED patterns.
- **Phase constitution**: β + α + ω.

### UFG state (after SPD)
- **β-phase**: Average structural element size 0.2 μm.
- **α-phase**: Non-equilibrium α-phase, average size 0.03 μm (30 nm).
- **ω-phase**: Average size 0.007 μm (7 nm).
- **Phase constitution**: β + α + ω.
- **Microstructural features**: Cellular-network dislocation structure in subgrains; numerous extinction contours along subgrain boundaries and fragments; SAED shows point reflections arranged circumferentially with azimuthal blur, indicating large- and small-angle misorientations of sub- and grain boundaries.
- **XRD**: Only single β-phase identified due to low volume fraction of α and ω phases.

## TNZTS alloy

### CG state
- **Matrix**: Completely crystallized equilibrium structure of polyhedral β-phase grains, average grain size 45 μm.
- **ω-phase**: Ellipsoidal ω-phase particles present; SAED shows β- and ω-phase reflections.
- **Phase constitution**: β + ω (no α-phase reported).

### UFG state (after SPD)
- **β-phase**: Average subgrain size 0.3 μm.
- **ω-phase**: Average size 0.008 μm (8 nm).
- **Phase constitution**: β + ω (no α-phase reported).
- **Microstructural features**: Grain refinement and grain-subgrain structure formation; cellular-network dislocation structure; extinction contours along subgrain boundaries; azimuthal blur in SAED indicating large- and small-angle misorientations.

## Lattice parameters (aβ)
- CG TNZ: 0.3305 nm
- UFG TNZ: 0.3300 nm
- CG TNZTS: 0.3313 nm
- UFG TNZTS: 0.3305 nm
- Pure β-Ti reference: 0.3282 nm (from Ref. [9])
- The lattice parameter in CG state exceeds pure β-Ti due to alloying with larger atomic radius elements (Nb, Zr, Ta, Sn). In UFG state, the lattice parameter decreases due to SPD-induced lattice distortion.
- SPD leads to an increase in relative intensity of the (110) X-ray line and intensity redistribution of β-phase reflections, indicating formation of primary crystallographic orientation (texture).

## Strengthening mechanisms discussed
- **Substructure strengthening** (from β-phase grain/subgrain refinement and dislocation structure)
- **Dispersion hardening** (from α-phase and ω-phase nanoparticles in TNZ; ω-phase only in TNZTS)
- **Solid-solution strengthening** is implied by lattice parameter expansion from alloying, but not explicitly discussed as a quantified mechanism.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## [Nb]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**: Ti-Nb-Zr (TNZ) and Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn (TNZTS) β-Ti alloys

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 39.5 wt.% (nominal) / ≤ 39.53 wt.% (measured); approximately 39.5 wt.% in both alloys

**[Main Function]**: β-phase stabilizing element; enables retention of BCC β-Ti phase at room temperature, which contributes to low Young's modulus; solid-solution strengthening via lattice expansion.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**: As a β-stabilizer, Nb suppresses martensitic transformation and promotes BCC β-phase retention. The larger atomic radius of Nb compared to Ti expands the β-phase lattice parameter (aβ = 0.3305 nm for CG TNZ vs. 0.3282 nm for pure β-Ti), contributing to solid-solution strengthening. In the TNZTS alloy, combined with Zr, Ta, and Sn, Nb stabilizes the β-phase so effectively that α-phase does not form during SPD.

**[Property Effect]**: Contributes to low Young's modulus (62–66 GPa range). The exact individual contribution of Nb to strength is not isolated in the paper, but the overall alloy achieves σ_uts = 0.580 GPa (CG) and 1.13 GPa (UFG) for TNZ, and 0.51 GPa (CG) and 0.78 GPa (UFG) for TNZTS. The original paper does not provide specific numerical values for Nb's individual contribution.

**[Potential Side Effects]**: The original paper does not explicitly discuss negative effects of Nb. However, high Nb content (39.5 wt.%) contributes to the alloy's density and cost; no specific ductility reduction is attributed to Nb alone.

**[Original Evidence]**: "This is due to the alloying with elements (Nb, Zr, Ta, Sn) the atomic radius of which is greater than that of high-temperature phase of pure titanium" (Section 3, regarding lattice parameter expansion). Nb is identified as a β-stabilizer in Section 1: "Low modulus β-Ti alloys of the latest generation, are compositionally complex alloys mainly based on the Ti–Nb system with addition of the Zr, Ta, and Sn elements."

## [Ta]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**: Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn (TNZTS) β-Ti alloy

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 2 wt.% (nominal) / ≤ 2 wt.% (measured)

**[Main Function]**: β-phase stabilizing element; contributes to β-phase stabilization in combination with Nb, Zr, and Sn, preventing α-phase formation during SPD.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**: Ta is a β-stabilizer in Ti alloys. In combination with Nb, Zr, and Sn, it stabilizes the β-phase sufficiently to suppress α-phase formation during SPD, resulting in a (β + ω) structure instead of (β + α + ω). Ta's larger atomic radius also contributes to β-phase lattice expansion (aβ = 0.3313 nm for CG TNZTS, the highest among all samples).

**[Property Effect]**: As part of the multicomponent β-stabilizing combination, Ta helps maintain ductility and Young's modulus after SPD. The TNZTS UFG alloy retains ε_f = 12% and E = 61 GPa. ## [Nb + Zr + Ta + Sn (multicomponent β-stabilizing combination)]

**[Applicable Alloy System]**: Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn (TNZTS) β-Ti alloy

**[Addition Amount or Composition Range]**: 39.5 wt.% Nb + 5 wt.% Zr + 2 wt.% Ta + 2 wt.% Sn (nominal)

**[Main Function]**: Collective β-phase stabilization that suppresses α-phase formation during SPD; maintains ductility and low Young's modulus while allowing strength improvement through substructural hardening.

**[Microstructural Mechanism]**: The combination of four β-stabilizing/neutral elements stabilizes the BCC β-phase so effectively that α-phase does not precipitate during SPD. Only the athermal ω-phase forms as a secondary phase. This results in a (β + ω) UFG structure rather than the (β + α + ω) structure seen in the simpler TNZ alloy. The main hardening contribution comes from substructural strengthening of the β-phase matrix, not dispersion hardening.

**[Property Effect]**: After SPD, strength increases modestly (σ_uts from 0.51 to 0.78 GPa, +53%; H_μ from 2.02 to 2.4 GPa, +19%; σ₀₂ from 0.43 to 0.47 GPa, +9%), while ductility (ε_f: 13% → 12%) and Young's modulus (E: 63 → 61 GPa) are essentially preserved. The increase is less significant than in TNZ alloy (σ_uts +95%).

**[Potential Side Effects]**: The strength improvement after SPD is less significant than in alloys where α-phase also contributes to dispersion hardening.

**[Original Evidence]**: "Multi-component alloying of the Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn alloy with Nb, Zr, Ta, Sn elements stabilizes the β-phase and does not reduce the alloy ductility and Young's modulus. At the same time, the increase in the strength properties in this case turns out to be less significant" (Abstract). "Alloying with the Nb, Zr, Ta, Sn elements of the TNZTS alloy provides the β-phase stabilization. Therefore, the main contribution to the alloy hardening due to SPD is made by substructural strengthening of the matrix β-phase. The dispersion hardening (ω-phase) is of lesser significance" (Section 3).

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## Solid-Solution Strengthening

- **Related elements**: Nb, Zr, Ta, Sn (all alloying elements with larger atomic radius than Ti)
- **Typical alloys**: Ti-Nb-Zr (TNZ), Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn (TNZTS)
- **Key compositions**: 39.5 wt.% Nb, 5 wt.% Zr, 2 wt.% Ta, 2 wt.% Sn
- **Microstructural changes**: The lattice parameter aβ in CG state (0.3305 nm for TNZ, 0.3313 nm for TNZTS) significantly exceeds that of pure β-Ti (0.3282 nm), confirming solid-solution lattice expansion. In UFG state, lattice parameter decreases (0.3300 nm for TNZ, 0.3305 nm for TNZTS) due to SPD-induced lattice distortion.
- **Property improvements**: Baseline strength of both alloys in CG state (σ_uts = 0.58 GPa for TNZ, 0.51 GPa for TNZTS) is attributable in part to solid-solution strengthening from these alloying elements. The original paper does not provide a quantitative breakdown of the solid-solution strengthening contribution.
- **Risks and limitations**: The paper does not explicitly discuss risks of solid-solution strengthening. High alloying content may affect density and cost.

---

## Substructure/Grain-Boundary Strengthening (SPD-induced)

- **Related elements**: Not element-specific; structural mechanism induced by SPD in both TNZ and TNZTS alloys
- **Typical alloys**: Ti-Nb-Zr (TNZ), Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn (TNZTS)
- **Key compositions**: Ti-39.5Nb-5Zr and Ti-39.5Nb-5Zr-2Ta-2Sn (wt.%)
- **Microstructural changes**: SPD (uniaxial pressing + multi-pass rolling + pre-recrystallization annealing) refines grains/subgrains to 0.2–0.3 μm for β-phase, creating cellular-network dislocation structure, subgrain boundaries with large- and small-angle misorientations, and primary crystallographic texture. CG β-phase grain size: 15 μm (TNZ) and 45 μm (TNZTS); UFG β-phase: 0.2 μm (TNZ) and 0.3 μm (TNZTS).
- **Property improvements**: In TNZTS alloy (β + ω structure), substructural strengthening of β-phase is the main hardening mechanism, raising σ_uts from 0.51 to 0.78 GPa (+53%) and H_μ from 2.02 to 2.4 GPa (+19%) while maintaining ductility (13% → 12%) and Young's modulus (63 → 61 GPa). In TNZ alloy, substructural strengthening contributes alongside dispersion hardening: σ_uts from 0.58 to 1.13 GPa (+95%) and H_μ from 2.09 to 2.67 GPa (+28%).
- **Risks and limitations**: In TNZ alloy, substructural strengthening combined with α-phase dispersion hardening causes ductility reduction from 18% to 12% (~50% decrease) and Young's modulus reduction by 6.5%. Fracture mode changes from grain-boundary dimple rupture (26–31 μm dimples) to subgrain-boundary fracture (15–18 μm dimples with 0.2–0.8 μm fine dimples).

---

## Dispersion Hardening by α-phase Nanoparticles

- **Related elements**: Not element-specific; phase-specific. α-phase is a Ti-rich HCP phase, facilitated by the absence of sufficient β-stabilizing elements (i.e., only Nb and Zr in TNZ alloy, without Ta and Sn).
- **Typical alloys**: Ti-Nb-Zr (TNZ) alloy only (α-phase does not form in TNZTS due to stronger β-stabilization)
- **Key compositions**: Ti-39.5Nb-5Zr (wt.%)
- **Microstructural changes**: In CG state, α-phase exists as spherical particles at grain boundaries (30–50 nm); after SPD, non-equilibrium α-phase nanoparticles with average size 0.03 μm (30 nm) are distributed in the UFG β-matrix.
- **Property improvements**: The additional presence of α-phase in TNZ alloy (compared to TNZTS) leads to more significant strength improvement after SPD: σ_uts = 1.13 GPa vs. 0.78 GPa for TNZTS (44% higher); H_μ = 2.67 GPa vs. 2.4 GPa (11% higher). Yield strength: 0.46 GPa vs. 0.47 GPa (comparable).
- **Risks and limitations**: The α-phase hardening contribution simultaneously reduces ductility (ε_f = 12% vs. 12% for TNZTS UFG, but TNZ CG had higher starting ductility of 18% vs. 13%) and lowers Young's modulus (62 GPa for TNZ UFG vs. 61 GPa for TNZTS UFG; and TNZ CG E=66 GPa dropped by 6.5% to 62 GPa).

---

## Dispersion Hardening by ω-phase Nanoparticles

- **Related elements**: Not element-specific; ω-phase is an HCP phase that forms athermally in β-Ti alloys during quenching or SPD.
- **Typical alloys**: Ti-Nb-Zr (TNZ) and Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn (TNZTS)
- **Key compositions**: Both Ti-39.5Nb-5Zr and Ti-39.5Nb-5Zr-2Ta-2Sn (wt.%)
- **Microstructural changes**: Ellipsoidal ω-phase particles, 6–10 nm in CG TNZ matrix grains; 7 nm average in UFG TNZ; 8 nm average in both CG and UFG TNZTS.
- **Property improvements**: In TNZTS alloy, ω-phase dispersion hardening is of lesser significance than substructural strengthening; the paper does not quantify the individual ω-phase contribution. The presence of ω-phase contributes to hardening in both alloys but to a lesser extent than α-phase or substructure.
- **Risks and limitations**: The paper implies that ω-phase alone provides less strengthening than the combination of α + ω phases. No specific mechanical property losses are attributed solely to ω-phase in this paper.

# Key Conclusions

1. Severe plastic deformation (SPD) of TNZ alloy leads to the formation of an ultrafine-grained (β + α + ω) structure with average structural element sizes of 0.2 μm (β), 0.03 μm (α), and 0.007 μm (ω).

2. SPD of TNZTS alloy results in an ultrafine-grained (β + ω) structure with average structural element sizes of 0.3 μm (β) and 0.008 μm (ω). The α-phase is suppressed due to stabilization of the β-phase by the multicomponent Nb + Zr + Ta + Sn alloying.

3. For TNZ alloy after SPD: yield strength increases by 10% (0.42 → 0.46 GPa), ultimate tensile strength increases by 95% (0.58 → 1.13 GPa), microhardness increases by 28% (2.09 → 2.67 GPa); ductility decreases by ~50% (18% → 12%) and Young's modulus decreases by 6.5% (66 → 62 GPa).

4. For TNZTS alloy after SPD: yield strength increases from 0.43 to 0.47 GPa, ultimate tensile strength increases from 0.51 to 0.78 GPa, microhardness increases from 2.02 to 2.4 GPa; ductility remains essentially unchanged (13% → 12%) and Young's modulus is essentially preserved (63 → 61 GPa).

5. The more significant strength improvement in TNZ alloy is attributed to the additional dispersion hardening contribution of α-phase nanoparticles, which are absent in TNZTS alloy due to β-phase stabilization by Ta and Sn additions.

6. The main hardening mechanism in TNZTS alloy is substructural strengthening of the β-phase matrix, with ω-phase dispersion hardening playing a lesser role.

7. Fracture mode changes from grain-boundary dimple rupture in CG state (dimple sizes 26–31 μm) to subgrain-boundary fracture in UFG state (dimple sizes 15–18 μm, with 0.2–0.8 μm fine dimples).

8. SPD induces lattice distortion, leading to a decrease in β-phase lattice parameter in both alloys, and creates a primary crystallographic orientation (texture) as evidenced by changes in XRD line intensities.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope

- **Alloy system limitation**: This paper studies biomedical β-Ti alloys (Ti-Nb-Zr and Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta-Sn), not refractory high-temperature alloys, Nb-W-based alloys, Mo-W-based alloys, or refractory high-entropy alloys. The extracted knowledge is not directly transferable to refractory high-temperature alloy systems.
- **Composition representation**: Compositions are given only in wt.%, not at.%. Impurity levels of C, O, N, H are not reported (only Fe ≤ 0.03 wt.%).
- **Processing details**: Specific SPD parameters (temperature, strain, number of passes) and pre-recrystallization annealing parameters are not provided in the paper; they are referenced to Ref. [8].
- **Testing conditions**: Test temperature for mechanical testing is not explicitly stated (assumed to be room temperature). Strain rate is not reported.
- **Missing mechanical properties**: No high-temperature mechanical properties, creep, fatigue, fracture toughness, or hardness at elevated temperature are reported.
- **No oxidation resistance data**: The paper does not discuss high-temperature oxidation resistance, oxide layer formation, or environmental embrittlement. This is expected since the alloys are designed for biomedical, not high-temperature structural, applications.
- **Microstructural quantification limitations**: XRD identified only β-phase due to low volume fractions of α and ω phases; TEM/SAED was necessary to confirm the presence of α and ω phases.
- **Single SPD route**: Only one SPD processing route (uniaxial pressing + multi-pass rolling + pre-recrystallization annealing) was investigated. Effects of other SPD methods (e.g., ECAP, HPT) or different annealing conditions are not explored.
- **Young's modulus measurement**: Young's modulus was measured using DUH-211S Dynamic Ultra Micro Hardness Tester, which may differ from values obtained by tensile testing or ultrasonic methods.
- **Biomedical context**: The paper's conclusions are oriented toward medical implant applications (biocompatibility, low Young's modulus). No cytotoxicity, corrosion resistance in body fluids, or in vivo performance data are provided.
