# Paper Metadata
- **Title**: Atomic-scale bonding strength and failure mechanisms of HfC/Ni/Ni₃Al interfaces on low-index crystal planes: A combined HRTEM and first-principles study
- **Authors**: Qin Wang, Furong Liu
- **Journal**: Vacuum
- **Year**: 2024
- **DOI**: 10.1016/j.vacuum.2024.113658
- **Alloy system studied**: Ni-based superalloy (DZ125 directionally solidified nickel-based superalloy) with HfC carbides and Ni/Ni₃Al matrix phases
- **Research objective**: To investigate the cohesive strength, stability, and failure mechanisms of HfC/Ni and HfC/Ni₃Al interfaces in DZ125 superalloy using first-principles calculations combined with HRTEM and mechanical testing
- **Material category**: Ni-based superalloy system (DZ125), specifically focusing on carbide/matrix interfaces. **Note**: This paper is not a refractory high-temperature alloy paper (not Nb-W, Mo-W, or refractory high-entropy alloy). It is a Ni-based superalloy study, which lies outside the typical refractory alloy category but involves HfC carbide/matrix interface analysis that is tangentially relevant to carbide-reinforced systems.

# Alloy System and Research Object
- **Alloy system**: DZ125, a Chinese grade directionally solidified Ni-based superalloy
- **Matrix phases**: γ (Ni solid solution) and γ′ (Ni₃Al intermetallic)
- **Carbide phase**: MC-type carbides, specifically HfC (with Ta and Ti substituting partially for Hf)
- **Research focus**: Interfacial bonding strength and fracture mechanisms at HfC/Ni and HfC/Ni₃Al interfaces
- **Sample preparation**: Two variants prepared by directional solidification:
  - Sample A: without HfO₂ addition
  - Sample B: with 200 ppm HfO₂ addition
- **General note on related alloy systems**: This paper investigates carbides and matrix interfaces in a Ni-based superalloy, not a refractory alloy per se. However, the understanding of carbide/matrix interface bonding has implications for carbide-strengthened refractory systems.

# Element-Function Knowledge Cards

## [Hf] (in carbide formation)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: DZ125 Ni-based superalloy (MC carbide phase)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Sample B contains ~40% Hf in MC carbide; HfO₂ addition of 200 ppm used in Sample B
- **Main Function**: Forms stable HfC carbide; controls carbide morphology and interface bonding with matrix
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Hf-rich carbides tend to be blocky; Hf-C covalent bonds form at carbide/matrix interfaces; larger atomic radius of Hf causes lattice distortion and increases interfacial energy; Hf-C bond is weaker than Ta-C bond
- **Property Effect**: Hf-rich carbides (blocky) appear to have weaker interfacial bonding with matrix compared to Ta-rich carbides; W_ad for HfC/Ni and HfC/Ni₃Al interfaces is mostly negative (e.g., -2.574 to 7.477 J/m² range, with most models negative)
- **Potential Side Effects**: Hf-rich carbides may be more susceptible to interfacial cracking; larger atomic radius may degrade interfacial stability
- **Original Evidence**: "The carbides in Sample B are characterized by tiny size, with a higher concentration of Hf compared to Ta"; "Hf-rich carbides tend to form smaller block shapes"

## [Ta] (in carbide formation)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: DZ125 Ni-based superalloy (MC carbide phase)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Ta present at lower level than Hf in Sample B carbides; Ta-rich in Sample A carbides; in referenced study: Ti₀.₁₂₅Ta₀.₆₂₅Hf₀.₂₅C (skeletal) vs Ti₀.₁₂₅Ta₀.₂₅Hf₀.₆₂₅C (blocky)
- **Main Function**: Improves interfacial bonding strength at carbide/matrix interface; promotes skeletal/rod-like carbide morphology
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Ta has more valence electrons than Hf; Ta-C covalent bonds are stronger than Hf-C bonds; Ta-Ni metal bonds form at interface; Ta reduces interfacial energy while increasing elastic strain energy
- **Property Effect**: W_ad increases with Ta doping (order: Ta2 > Ta1 > Ta0, using HfC(001)/Ni₃Al(011) as example); Ta-rich carbides promote rod-like/skeletal growth
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly stated
- **Original Evidence**: "Introducing Ta atoms at the interface improves the W_ad of the HfC/Ni/Ni₃Al model"; "Ta1 refers to the substitution of one Ta atom for Hf atoms at the interface, and the order of W_ad is Ta2 > Ta1 > Ta0"

## [C] (carbide-forming element)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: DZ125 Ni-based superalloy
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Not explicitly quantified; present in MC carbides
- **Main Function**: Forms MC carbides with Hf, Ta, Ti; creates Ni-C covalent bonds at interface
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: C atoms accumulate electrons at the interface; Ni-C covalent bonds (bond length 1.7–2.2 Å) are primary contributors to interfacial cohesion; Al-C bonds also form (bond length 1.8–2.9 Å)
- **Property Effect**: Strong Ni-C covalent bonds improve interfacial cohesion; Bridge4 model (HfC(001)/Ni₃Al(011)) has shortest Ni-C bonds (1.9–2.0 Å) and highest W_ad (7.477 J/m²)
- **Potential Side Effects**: Brittle carbide phases can serve as crack initiation sites
- **Original Evidence**: "The interfacial cohesion is attributable to strong Ni-C covalent bonds"; "bond length and density of Ni-C and Al-C covalent bonds are the determining factors"

## [HfO₂] (oxide addition)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: DZ125 Ni-based superalloy
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: 200 ppm (Sample B); 0 ppm (Sample A)
- **Main Function**: Influences carbide morphology and composition through oxide-particle effects on carbide precipitation (mechanism not detailed in this paper, referencing prior work)
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Presence of HfO₂ leads to Hf-rich carbides; without HfO₂, Ta-rich carbides form
- **Property Effect**: Sample A (no HfO₂): Ta-rich skeletal carbides; Sample B (with 200 ppm HfO₂): Hf-rich blocky carbides
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not explicitly stated in this paper
- **Original Evidence**: "The carbides in Sample A are dispersed in a linear or skeleton shape have higher Ta content compared to Hf. The carbides in Sample B are characterized by tiny size, with a higher concentration of Hf compared to Ta."

## [Ti] (in carbide formation)

- **Applicable Alloy System**: DZ125 Ni-based superalloy (MC carbide)
- **Addition Amount or Composition Range**: Referenced composition Ti₀.₁₂₅Ta₀.₆₂₅Hf₀.₂₅C and Ti₀.₁₂₅Ta₀.₂₅Hf₀.₆₂₅C
- **Main Function**: Participates in MC carbide formation; listed as carbide-forming element alongside Hf, Ta
- **Microstructural Mechanism**: Not detailed in this paper beyond presence in carbides
- **Property Effect**: Not explicitly quantified
- **Potential Side Effects**: Not stated
- **Original Evidence**: "MC carbides primarily consist of C, Hf, Ta, and other elements"; "Ti atoms replace Hf in the carbides" (from referenced literature)

# Strengthening Mechanism Knowledge Cards

## [Carbide Precipitation Strengthening / Interface Bonding]

- **Related Elements**: Hf, Ta, C, Ti
- **Typical Alloys**: DZ125 Ni-based superalloy; MC carbide-containing Ni-based superalloys
- **Key Compositions**: MC carbides with varying Hf/Ta ratios; 200 ppm HfO₂ (Sample B) vs no HfO₂ (Sample A)
- **Microstructural Changes**: MC carbides form at interfaces with various orientation relationships; Hf-rich carbides are blocky; Ta-rich carbides are skeletal/rod-like; interface bonding through Ni-C, Ni-Hf, Al-C, Al-Hf bonds

- **Risks and Limitations**: Most interface models have negative W_ad, indicating poor interfacial bonding; interface is primary crack initiation site under mechanical stress; Hf-rich carbides may be more susceptible to interfacial failure

- **Key Compositions**: Ta substituting for Hf at HfC/matrix interface (Ta0, Ta1, Ta2 variants)
- **Microstructural Changes**: Ta reduces interfacial energy, increases elastic strain energy, forms stronger Ta-C covalent bonds and Ta-Ni metal bonds; promotes skeletal/rod-like carbide morphology
- **Property Improvements**: Higher W_ad, lower interfacial energy, more stable interface; improved fracture resistance

# Key Conclusions
1. Of 32 interface models (HfC(001)/Ni(001), HfC(011)/Ni(001), HfC(111)/Ni(001), HfC(001)/Ni₃Al(011), HfC(111)/Ni₃Al(111)), the majority exhibit negative adhesion work (W_ad), indicating interfacial instability.
2. Bridge4 model in HfC(001)/Ni₃Al(011) shows highest W_ad (7.477 J/m²) and lowest interface energy (3.345–3.192 J/m²), indicating best stability.
3. Interfacial cohesion is primarily due to strong Ni-C covalent bonds; Ni-Hf metal bonds contribute but to a lesser extent.
4. Fracture toughness analysis (Griffith's theory) shows most models are prone to interface fracture; crack initiation occurs at carbide/matrix interfaces, confirmed by room-temperature tensile tests and 760 °C/725 MPa endurance tests.
5. Ta doping improves interfacial adhesion; order of W_ad is Ta2 > Ta1 > Ta0.
6. Ta-rich carbides (skeletal/rod-like) have better interfacial bonding and lower interface energy than Hf-rich carbides (blocky); elastic strain energy dominates for Ta-rich carbides, while interfacial energy dominates for Hf-rich carbides.
7. Strong Ni-C covalent bonds (bond length 1.9–2.0 Å in Bridge4) are the key determinant of interfacial bonding strength.

# Limitations and Applicable Scope
- **Not a refractory high-temperature alloy paper**: This paper focuses on Ni-based superalloys (DZ125), not Nb-W-based, Mo-W-based, or refractory high-entropy alloys. The extracted knowledge is applicable to Ni-based superalloys with MC carbide strengthening, not to refractory alloys.
- **Theoretical nature**: Primary contributions are from first-principles calculations; experimental validation is qualitative (fracture morphology, EDS) rather than quantitative mechanical property measurement.
- **Mechanical properties not fully reported**: Specific yield strength, UTS, elongation, and other quantitative mechanical property values are not provided in the extracted text.
- **Sample composition not fully specified**: The full bulk composition of DZ125 is not provided; only carbide compositions and HfO₂ addition levels are stated.
- **Oxidation resistance**: Not investigated in this paper; no oxidation data available.
- **Temperature range**: Endurance testing at 760 °C only; high-temperature properties above 1000 °C not studied.
- **Single interface type studied**: Focus only on HfC/matrix interfaces; other carbides (e.g., TiC, TaC) interfaces not directly modeled (except Ta-substituted HfC).
- **Fracture toughness is theoretical**: Griffith-based predictions not experimentally validated.
- **Uncertainty in Hf content**: Approximate 40% Hf in carbides stated based on EDS; not a precise chemical analysis.
- **The paper does not address**: Solid-solution strengthening in a refractory alloy context, oxidation resistance, high-temperature strength above 760 °C, or any refractory metal-based alloy system.
