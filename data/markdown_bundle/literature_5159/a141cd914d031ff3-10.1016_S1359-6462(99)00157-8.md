Introduction
Superalloys [1] are the strongest heat-resistant alloys and are widely used in gas turbine blades of
aircraft engines and power plants. They have been developed from cast polycrystals to directionally
solidified columnar crystals and recently to single crystals in order to prevent grain boundary sliding
which reduces creep resistance. As a result, no plastic forming technique is applicable to the formation
of today’s advanced single-crystal superalloys. Superplasticity [2] is a phenomenon whereby some
crystalline materials exhibit exceptionally large elongation during tensile deformation without fracture.
Two types of superplasticity have been reported: “structural superplasticity” observed in polycrystalline
materials having fine equiaxial grains, and “internal stress superplasticity” observed in metal matrix
composites (MMCs) which are subjected to a cyclic temperature change. Although structural super-
plasticity has already been found in polycrystalline metals [3] and ceramics [4], it cannot, in principle,
apply single-crystal materials having no grain boundaries. Internal stress superplasticity, however, has
a potential of forming above superalloys into complicated shapes.
Internal stress superplasticity has been reported in several MMCs such as Al-SiC (whisker [5] and
particle [6]), Al-Be [7], Al-Al
3Ni [8] and Al-Si [9] composites. Although MMCs having a fine-grained
matrix deform superplastically due to grain-boundary sliding [10], the mechanism [11] of internal stress
superplasticity is not due to grain-boundary sliding but dislocation creep of the matrix. By applying a
cyclic temperature change, a large internal stress is induced by a mismatch of thermal strain between
the matrix and the inclusions. The creep deformation of the matrix is then biased to the direction of an
external load, and the average strain rate is proportional to the externally applied stress. This linear
relationship between the strain rate and the applied stress causes Newtonian viscous flow, and thus the
superplastic deformation of MMCs occurs.
Here we report the superplastic deformation in advanced Ni-base single-crystal superalloys, which
have hard
g9 particles dispersed in the ductile g matrix, utilizing internal stress superplasticity.

Experimental Procedure
Directionally solidified (DS) and single-crystal (SC) Ni-base superalloys Mar-M247 [12] were prepared
for compression and tensile creep tests, respectively. The nominal composition was 5.6wt%Al,
9.2wt%Co, 8.1wt%Cr, 1.4wt%Hf, 0.5wt%Mo, 3.2wt%Ta, 0.7wt%Ti and 10wt%W with the remainder
being Ni. The specimens were solution-treated at 1503 K for 2 h and aged at 1089 K for 24 h to produce
the microstructure where cuboidal
g9 precipitates of 0.5 mm size were embedded in a Ni-rich g matrix.
The volume fraction of g9 precipitates was about 70%. Creep tests were performed above 1300 K to
prevent rafting [13], which is a phenomenon whereby the g9 phase forms rafts perpendicular to the
tensile direction during creep deformation. The specimens under a constant load parallel to the ^001&
direction were subjected to a cyclic temperature change by induction heating in vacuum using a
previously reported apparatus [7]. The temperature profile was a triangular waveform having a
temperature range of 100 K and heating and cooling rates of 610 K/s.

We stopped both creep tests when the true strain reached 0.73 and observed the outward and inward
appearances. Figures 1B, 1C and 1D show the specimens before deformation, after isothermal creep and
after thermal cycling creep, respectively, and Figs. 1E and 1F are the inside optical micrographs
corresponding to Figs. 1C and 1D, respectively. Sharp cracks are observed on the surface of the
isothermal creep specimen [Fig. 1C], and exaggerated cavitation is observed inside [Fig. 1E]. On the
contrary, no crack on the surface [Fig. 1D] and no cavitation inside [Fig. 1F] are observed after thermal
cycling creep. This uniform deformation without cracks or cavitations under thermal cycling conditions
is a characteristic of internal stress superplasticity.
Next, we performed a thermal cycling tensile creep test (1343 ;1443 K, 610 K/s) using SC
superalloy. An external load was applied so that the specimen was subjected to an almost constant stress
of 5.0 MPa. Figures 2A, 2B and 2C are photographs of the specimens before deformation, after thermal
cycling creep and after thermal cycling creep at a slightly higher temperature range, respectively. Large
elongation of 100% (true strain of 0.7) without fracture was achieved in 40 h under an appropriate
temperature condition [Fig. 2B]. A diffuse neck developed in the center since the present heating
apparatus causes a temperature difference of about 50 K between the center and ends of the gage
section. On the other hand, we performed the isothermal tensile creep test at 1423 K under the applied
stress of 13 MPa, but null elongation of less than 1% in 40 h was obtained. This result indicates that
the thermal cycling causes a large tensile elongation in a very short period.
Figures 2D, 2E and 2F show SEM (scanning electron microscope) micrographs of the longitudinal
section before the deformation, just after the deformation and after post-creep heat treatment, respec-
tively. While the cuboidal
g9 particles are arranged regularly before deformation [Fig. 2D], they
changed their shapes into spherical and their volume fraction into about 10% during the deformation
[Fig. 2E]. Because of the dissolution of the
g9 phase into the g matrix at these temperatures [14] the
microstructure resembles typical particle dispersed MMCs. On the other hand, the fractured section of
the specimen shown in Fig. 2C had a microstructure where
g9 precipitates were perfectly dissolved in
the g matrix. Such a single phase cannot produce the internal stress by thermal cycling, and thus internal
stress superplasticity disappeared.
After the tensile creep test, the specimen shown in Fig. 2B was again solution-treated and aged under
the same conditions as before the creep test. The longitudinal microstructure [Fig. 2F] after post-creep
heat treatment has fine cuboidal
g9 particles coherently in the g matrix, similar to the specimen before
deformation [Fig. 2D]. This result indicates that the superplastically deformed specimen maintains the
original single-crystal structure and high creep strength.

Conclusions
We have demonstrated the plastic forming of single-crystal superalloys, for which conventional plastic
forming processes are not applicable, using internal stress superplasticity. Since most superalloys have
a common microstructure consisting of the
g-g9 system, the present result can be applied to the
superplastic forming of many other advanced superalloys.
