abstract
The progress on thermal mechanical fatigue (TMF) of Ni-based single crystal superalloys is summarized
from an industrial perspective. The TMF behaviors of bare alloys at relatively high stress regime are well
studied. However, the understanding of coated alloys is fairly limited, particularly at low stress region
pertinent to application conditions. The potential in ﬂuence of microstructure, chemistry, oxidation and
bond coat on TMF life is discussed. Research directions for future studies are proposed.

1. Introduction
The thermal ef ﬁciency of turbine engines has been improved
signiﬁcantly due to the increased operating temperature. The main
technology enablers for such increase include (1) new single
crystal superalloys with improved capability, (2) thermal barrier
coating and (3) advanced internal cooling of turbine blades [1,2].
The latter two technologies make the blades capable of working at
temperatures above the melting point of superalloys. However, the
downside of internal cooling is the large temperature variation
created on the blade surface between the cooling holes ( Fig. 1 ).
This temperature gradient often results in large thermal stresses
and leads to the ultimate thermal fatigue failure. As indicated in
Table 1 , fatigue cracking is the leading failure mode in air-cooled
ﬁrst-stage blades. The percentage of fatigue-crack related failure is
signiﬁcantly higher in air-cooled blades than in solid blades.
Therefore, thermal mechanical fatigue (TMF) behavior of single
crystal superalloys has attracted considerable attentions in recent
years [3– 38].
Thermal mechanical fatigue can be classi ﬁed into in-phase,
clock-wise diamond, counter-clock diamond and out-of phase TMF
based on the strain-temperature relationship [4]. It is generally
believed that out-of-phase (OP) TMF cycle is the most damaging
one, where a hold at the maximum compressive strain is applied
at the maximum temperature [5,6]. This type of TMF cycle was
designed to simulate the strain-temperature conditions experi-
enced by the hot spots on a blade surface in a typical engine cycle.
The hot spots are under thermal compressive strain during start-
up (take-off) when the core temperature is high, and then re-
versed to tensile after the engine is shut-down as a result of stress
relaxation at high temperature. A similar sustained-peak-LCF
(SPLCF) test has also been investigated recently [39– 41]. SPLCF test
is an isothermal LCF test with a hold at the maximum compressive
strain. The stress – strain curves for both tests are compared in
Fig. 2 .
In this paper, the recent progress in TMF of single crystal su-
peralloys is reviewed. The current achievements and challenges
are discussed, and potential industrial needs are highlighted for
future studies.
2. Factors affecting the TMF life of single crystal alloys
Many TMF studies have been performed on single crystal alloys
during the past three decades. However, comparison of the data
and mechanisms across the literature seems to be challenging due
to the varying test conditions and alloy compositions. In this sec-
tion, the in ﬂuence of test parameters, microstructure, alloy
chemistry, oxidation and coating on the TMF behavior of single
crystal superalloys is summarized.

test conditions. The key variables in an OP-TMF test include:

2.2. Effect of microstructure
The TMF life of single crystal alloys was reported to be affected
by their γ′ size, γ′ volume fraction and microstructural homo-
geneity [4,7,8,13– 15]. The life of TMS82 þ alloy depends strongly
on the γ′ volume fraction when tested at 400 – 900 °C and 1-h hold
[13]. The cycle to failure (Nf) is peaked at approximately 70 vol%,
see Fig. 6, at which the best creep strength was also observed [1 7].
Above 70%, eutectic γþγ′ structure is developed. The positive
dependence of Nf on γ′ volume fraction was attributed to the in-
creased amount of γ/γ′ interfaces. However, the authors observed
in a separate study that the TMS-75 alloy containing 40 vol% γ′ has
a longer TMF life than the alloy with 60% γ′, while the latter has a
better creep resistance [14]. A SCA425 þ alloy with a larger γ′ size
(0.65 μm) was reported to have a better TMF life than the one with
a smaller size (0.35 μm), even though the alloy with the ﬁner size
is supposed to have a longer rupture life [18]. These results suggest
that the TMF life of single crystal superalloys is not solely de-
termined by their creep capability.
Microstructural heterogeneity was observed to have a positive
impact on TMF life [8,17]. The presence of residual eutectic γþ γ′
microstructure in a partially-solutioned PWA-1480 alloy was
proposed to be responsible for its superior TMF life [17]. The for-
mation of extensive TCP bands along the dendrite cores in an aged
CMSX-4 alloy was suggested to improve its TMF life by promoting
more homogenous deformation [8]. However, no difference in TMF
life was observed between fully-solutioned and partially-solu-
tioned STAL-15 alloys [4]. More studies are needed in this area.
Crystal orientation has a strong effect on the TMF life of single
crystal alloys. Under the same strain range, the TMF lives for
o0014 crystal are much longer than those with o1014 and
o1114 orientations, see Fig. 3 [9 ,19]. However, the effect of or-
ientation on TMF life is much less signi ﬁcant when compared per
mechanical stress, because the Young ’s modulus at o001 4 is
much lower than those at other crystal orientations [20]. A similar
dependence has been observed in isothermal LCF tests of single
crystal alloys [42,43].
2.3. Effect of alloy chemistry
There are very limited studies on the effect of alloy chemistry
on TMF life of single crystal superalloys. Sakamoto et al. tested the
TMF lives of 98 single crystal alloys from 1st to 5th generation at
400– 900 °C with a strain range 1.28% [13]. They concluded that
alloys with a lower stress relaxation (i.e. better rupture) often have
a better TMF life ( Fig. 7 ). Re, Ru and Ta were found to be the most
effective elements in reducing stress relaxation; Re/Ru additions
was proposed to reduce the stacking fault energy in
γ phase and
thus reduce dislocation mobility; Ta addition strengthens the γ′
phase from dislocation cutting. They also suggested that high yield
strength will improve TMF property by reducing tensile yielding
and by mitigating microstructural degradation; Re and W seem to
be the best strengtheners. Note that the test condition used in
their study resulted in relatively low TMF lives (mostly below 200
cycles). The best one among the tested alloys was a 1st generation
PWA 1480 alloy containing no Re/Ru but high Ta (12 wt%).
Morerare et al. investigated the effect of Si addition on the TMF
life of STAL-15 alloy [4]. With Si addition, the TMF life increases
approximately by a factor of two across the tested strain ranges.
They suggested that Si addition improves the oxidation resistance

A systematic study on the role of alloying additions in thermal
fatigue of single crystal superalloys is still needed, particularly at
the conditions close to ﬁeld experience. The effect of alloying on
the creep of single crystal superalloys has been fairly well under-

That progress incubated the development of advanced creep-re-
sistant alloys such as N6 and TMS-238. Similar efforts are merited
in understanding the TMF behavior of single crystal superalloys.
2.4. Effect of oxidation
Due to time and resource limits, many of the laboratory TMF
tests were run at a relatively high strain range and a short hold
time as compared to what the actual engine parts subjected to. Fox
example, the operation cycles for land-based engine can be as long
as hundreds to thousands hours, while the typical hold times in
TMF tests are often few minutes. Accordingly, the time-dependent
factors such as oxidation may not be well represented in labora-
tory TMF tests. One good illustration is the quick drop of TMF life
at low strain range as shown in Fig. 4 , where oxidation and dif-
fusion-controlled deformation play a much more important role
than at high strain range [10].
TMF crack initiation and growth can be signi ﬁcantly altered by
oxidation. Fleury and Remy observed in bare AM1 alloy that cracks
initiated from surface or subsurface micropores at higher strain
range, but from oxidation spikes at lower strain ranges [19]. They
concluded that oxidation-induced cracking appears to be the
dominant damage mechanism in the lifetime of interest for tur-
bine blades. Oxidation-assisted crack initiation from specimen
surface was also reported in other studies [6,21– 24]. The shorter
TMF life of SR99 alloy compared to CMSX-4 alloy was attributed to
its poor oxidation resistance, which led to early crack initiation
[24]. Oxidation can also increase crack growth rate by forming
oxidation-damage zone at crack tip, or by enhancing oxygen dif-
fusion along slip bands [22,39,44]. In turn, oxidation can be ac-
celerated by TMF cycling [25]. Fig. 8 shows a TEM micrograph of
the oxidation-damage zone ahead the tip of a thermal fatigue
crack in N5 alloy. The cuboidal γ/γ′ microstructure was converted
to γ subgrains due to the outward diffusion of Al and the inward
diffusion of oxygen. The crack tip was ﬁlled with oxides and the
surrounding area was loaded with heavy dislocation networks.
Similarly, the fatigue cracks seen on ﬁeld parts were heavy
oxidized ( Fig. 9 ). Therefore, the contribution of oxidation shall be
adequately represented in TMF tests in order to better simulate the
damaging modes seen on engine parts.
2.5. Effect of bond coat
Due to the relatively high operation temperature, blades are
often coated with bond coat to protect the base alloy from en-
vironmental attack and to extend the life of thermal barrier
coating. There are generally two types of bond coats: i.e., diffu-
sional coating (PtAl, NiAl) and overlay coating (Co/NiCrAlY). Bond
coats have much better oxidation resistance but poor low-tem-
perature ductility than the base superalloys. Their ductilities
(strain-to crack), depending on coating thickness and chemistry,
are often less than 1% at temperatures below 550 °C as shown in
Fig. 10 [23 ,45– 47]. The overlay coatings with a two-phase micro-
structure are slightly more ductile than the single-phase diffu-
sional coating [48].
Due to coating brittleness, TMF cracks were often observed to
initiate from bond coat surface in coated specimens, particularly at
relatively high strain ranges [26]. Some of these coating cracks
propagate into the base alloy at a direction perpendicular to the
coating surface (i.e., loading axis). A similar crack growth mode
was observed on ﬁeld parts as shown in Fig. 9 . Compared to bare
alloys, the TMF lives of coated alloys at high strain range are often
lower due to the early crack initiation in coating [6,27,28]. How-
ever, the coated specimens may outperform the bare ones at low
strain ranges because the oxidation-induced cracking is delayed by
coating [6,28]. The in ﬂuence of coating on the TMF life of single
crystal superalloys deserves additional attentions in future studies.
3. TMF mechanism
As brie ﬂy mentioned in previous section, the initiation and
growth of TMF cracks are strongly affected by coating. Therefore,
the following discussion is separated into bare and coated alloys.
3.1. Bare alloys

causes: 1) the interaction of slip bands or deformation twins with
specimen surface, and 2) micropores or oxidation spikes formed
on the surface [6,9,19,22,29]. When a TMS-75 alloy was tested
without a compressive hold, the main crack was observed to

The TMF failure mode of bare alloys depends strongly on the
strain range. For the same CMSX-4 alloy tested at 100 – 850 °C, the
failure mode changed from crystallographic fracture along {1 1 1}
plane at 1.3% strain (stage-I, Fig. 1 1b) to randomly non-crystal-

those with non-crystallographic fracture ( Fig. 1 1c). It has been
proposed previously that stage-I crack initiation in nickel-base
alloys is anticipated at low temperatures when dislocation climb
and cube slip are not activated, leading to highly localized de-
formation at high strain ranges [19,22,49]. However, at low strain
range and/or at high temperature the deformation turns to be
more homogenous and the damaging effect of oxidation becomes
signiﬁcant due to the extended life, resulting in stage-II crack in-
itiation along {001} plane. Stage II crack initiation was observed in
AM1 alloy at both high and low strain ranges tested between 600 –
10 0 0°C; the cracks were initiated from surface oxides at low strain
ranges and from surface/subsurface micropores at high strain
ranges [19]. It was suggested that formation of stage-II crack can
be originated from (a) dislocations are con ﬁned to
γ channels or γ/
γ′ interfaces along {001} planes, (b) simultaneously slip along
{1 1 1} planes gives rise to crack growth along a {100} plane or c)
the material ahead of the crack tip embrittled by oxidation and the
brittle material then cracks perpendicular to the maximum tensile
stress [48– 51].
3.2. Coated alloys
Investigations on the TMF failure mode of coated alloys are
limited [6,10,23,26– 28]. Irrespective of the applied strain ranges,
all the cracks were observed to initiate from the coating at mul-
tiple locations in the early stage of life. The cracks propagate into
the substrate initially in stage II mode (perpendicular to the coat/
substrate interface) and then turn to stage I mode later [6,27]. The
early cracking of coating in TMF tests was attributed to a) its low
ductility at low temperature, b) poor strength at high temperature,
c) the existence of grain boundaries and d) surface roughness in-
duced by rumpling [6,23,27]. In a NiAl-coated PWA 1484 alloy
tested between 400 – 110 0°C, numerous microcracks were devel-
oped along the grain boundaries of coating in less than 8% of total
TMF life [27]. The valleys on the rumpled coating surface were
observed to be the preferred sites for crack initiation [26]. The
martensitic transformation taking place in NiAl coat during TMF
cooling cycle may also contribute to crack initiation [6].

4. Summary
As brie ﬂy summarized in this paper, great progress has been
achieved in understanding TMF behavior of bare single crystal
alloys at relatively high strain ranges. Relatively few studies were
performed on coated alloys in the low-strain and long-life region
pertinent to ﬁeld service conditions. Crack initiation and growth
depends strongly on the test conditions. The observations on bare
alloys may not be applicable to coated alloys because the crack
initiation and propagation modes are altered by the presence of
coating. Therefore, it is paramount important to perform TMF tests
in a condition close to the target application environment.
In order to con ﬁdently predict the TMF life of single crystal
alloy parts, the following questions should be addressed in future
studies:
1. Can the TMF data observed at high-strain regime be extra-
polated to the low-strain region?
2. Are the TMF characteristics observed in one alloy applicable to
other alloys?
3. Are the observations made on bare alloys applicable to coated
alloys?
4. What is the impact of oxidation on the TMF lives of various
alloys in long-life regime?
5. Is the TMF test re ﬂecting the thermal stress condition experi-
enced by engine parts?
6. How is the TMF life affected by alloying additions?
7. How is the TMF life in ﬂuenced by microstructure such as
γ′ size
and homogeneity?
A better understanding of these topics will signi ﬁcantly ad-
vance the life modeling of engine parts. It will also lay the foun-
dation for developing next-generation thermal fatigue resistant
superalloys.
