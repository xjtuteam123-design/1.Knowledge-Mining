Abstract 
A high strength Mg-11Gd-4.5Y-1Nd-1.5Zn-0.5Zr (wt.%) alloy has been successfully 
fabricated via hot extrusion, cold rolling and ageing treatment. The alloy exhibits an 
average 0.2% proof stress (YS) of 481.6 s19.9 MPa, average ultimate tensile strength 
(UTS) of 517.4s27.5 MPa and an average elongation to failure of 2.0 s0.4 % at room

502.0 MPa, an UTS of 546.8 MPa and an elongation to failure of 2.6 %. The high 
strength of this alloy is attributed to the fine grains, stacking faults (SFs), long period 
stacking ordered (LPSO) phase, and precipitates Mg

1. Introduction 
The rare-earth (RE) containing Mg alloys have been investigated for decades 
because of their excellent mechanical properties. Normally, the heavy RE elements 
such as Gd, Dy and Y have a high solubility in magnesium at elevated temperatures. 
In contrast, the light RE such as Nd and Ce has a small solubility in magnesium. For 
example, the solubility of Y and Nd is 12.5 wt.% and 3.6 wt.% at eutectic temperature 
in pure Mg, respectively [1]. In comparison with Y, Gd has a much higher solubility 
of 23.5 wt.% at Mg-Gd eutectic temperature 548°C, which declines sharply to 3.8 wt.% 
at 200 °C [1]. Consequently, Gd is expected as a promising candidate for producing 
high strength Mg alloys because of its effective solution and precipitate strengthening 
[2-7]. Zn addition in magnesium can significantly enhance the ageing hardening 
response and mechanical properties of Mg-RE alloy s due to the fact that it can reduce

the solubility of RE in /g68-Mg matrix [8]. In addition, Zn is an essential element for 
the formation of long period stacking ordered (LPSO) phase in Mg-RE-Zn alloys [9]. 
The strengthening mechanism of LPSO phase has been attracting increasing attentions 
because of its strong plastic anisotropy and layered structure [10-13]. The deformation 
behaviour of LPSO phase largely depends on the loading direction. Therefore, the 
deformation behaviour and mechanical properties of alloys containing the LPSO phase 
are considered to be sensitive to microstructure and loading direction. The deformation 
behaviours and strengthening mechanisms of the LPSO phase are well studied under 
compression, but needs to be further investigated under tension. 
Up to now, the most successful commercial Mg-RE alloys have been realized as 
the use of Mg-Y-Nd-Zr alloys, identified as WE54 (Mg-5.1 wt.%Y-3.3 wt.%RE-0.5 
wt.%Zr) and WE43 (Mg-4.0 wt.%Y-3.3 wt.%RE-0.5 wt.%Zr) alloys [14-16]. The 
high strength of these alloys is contributed to the precipitation of metastable 
/g69/g99 
phase and equilibrium Mg 5RE phase [14, 15]. Another major contribution is the 
development of high strength Mg-Y-Zn alloys which was produced by rapidly 
solidified powder metallurgy (RS P/M) [17]. This alloy exhibits remarkable 
mechanical properties with an yield strength (YS) of 610 MPa and an elongation of 
5%. The ultra-fine grain size and LPSO phase are mainly responsible for the 
improvement of the strength. However, the producing process is more complicated 
compare to the conventional wrought processes, such extrusion and rolling [18, 19].

Hot extrusion is an effective technique to refine microstructure and to improve 
the mechanical properties of Mg alloys [18, 20]. A high-strength 
Mg-10Gd-5.7Y-1.6Zn-0.7Zr (wt.%) alloy with an yield strength of 473 MPa and an 
elongation of 8 % has been successfully fabricated by hot extrusion and subsequent 
ageing [18]. Its excellent mechanical properties are attributed to the fine precipitates 
formed during ageing and thermomechanical processing. Warm rolling is another 
effective process to refine the microstructure and to produce large Mg alloy sheets 
with good mechanical properties [21-23]. Normally, a large total rolling reduction is 
required for producing a high strength Mg-RE alloy. For example, a high-strength 
Mg-8.2Gd-3.8Y-1Zn-0.4Zr (wt.%) alloy with a YS of 426 MPa and an elongation to 
failure of 4.5% was produced via warm rolling and ageing [21]. The final reduction is 
96%. However, for the cast magnesium alloys, due to their poor workability caused 
by their intrinsic crystal structure (hcp, hexagonal-closed packed), large grain size, 
coarse secondary phases and possible casting defects [24-26], normally, the warm 
rolling are carried out at high temperatures with a small rolling reduction per pass in 
order to avoid the cracking [23]. An initial extrusion prior to cold rolling can refine 
microstructure and reduce casting defects [27, 28]. As a result, the extruded Mg alloy 
can achieve a high workability and avoid the cracking in the following cold rolling 
process [27]. Thus, a combination process of hot-extrusion and cold rolling may be a 
considerable method to produce high strength Mg-RE alloys.

The purpose of the present work is to produce an ultra-high strength 
Mg-RE-Zn-Zr alloy via combined hot-extrusion, cold rolling and ageing treatment. 
The mechanical properties of the present alloy and the microstructural evolution 
during thermomechanical processes will be investigated. Furthermore, the 
deformation behaviour and the strengthening mechanisms of LPSO phase under 
tension will be discussed.

4. Discussion 
The improvement of excellent streng th can be explained by multiple 
strengthening mechanisms based on the microstructural observations. 
4.1Strengthening by LPSO phase

## Page 17

16 
 
 
The 14H-LPSO phase is expected to enhance the strength of alloys via short-fiber 
strengthening mechanism due to its large particle size, high aspect ratio and 
directional distribution along the ED [11, 12]. The layered interface of LPSO phase is 
parallel to the (0001)
14H-LPSO. Since the majority of 14H-LPSO particles are aligned 
with the ED, the (0001) 14H-LPSO planes are therefore parallel to the ED as well. 
Moreover, it has been found that the (0001)<11 2 0> basal slip is the dominant slip 
system in the LPSO phase at room temperature [10]. Thus, the Schmid factor for the 
basal slip is negligible when the load is applied along the ED. Consequently, the basal 
slip is effectively suppressed. When the appli ed stress exceeds a critical value, other 
slip systems could be activated due to the requirement by deformation 
accommodation. As a result, the LPSO phase deforms, in which the shear bands form 
(Fig.6d). The shear bands propagate perpendicular to the layered interface, which is 
(0001)
14H-LPSO plane, without any cracks. The 14H-LPSO phases could therefore be 
sheared via the prismatic slip rather than basal slip. Since the activation of prismatic 
slip requires a higher CRSS compared to that of basal slip, the LPSO phase itself can 
directly act as a reinforcement contributing to the improvement of the yield strength. 
In addition, there are no any cracks at the coherent interface between the LPSO phase 
and /g302-matrix, demonstrating that the interface is not a desirable nucleation site for 
micro-cracks during deformation. The stability of this interface contributes to the 
improvement of strength and ductility [10, 13]. The LPSO phase hinders the 
propagation of micro-cracks during the deformation process. Once micro-cracks form

## Page 18

17 
 
 
at an interface in the LPSO phase, they would be blocked from propagating easily into 
the neighboring interfaces [13]. This is the advantage of the layer structure phase. 
With increasing applied stress, the cracks initiate at the LPSO particles when the 
coherent interfaces are no longer able to hinder the nucleation of micro-cracks and its 
propagation. As shown in Fig. 2, the cracks propagate roughly perpendicular to 
layered interfaces in LPSO phase. Such direction is the same as that of shear bands as 
shown in Fig. 6d. Thus, the prismatic slip is considered to be the dominate slip system 
when a tensile load is applied along the layered interface or (0001)
14H-LPSO plane. 
Once the applied stress beyond the critical value, the cracks would occur on the 
prismatic plane of LPSO phase. In comparison with LPSO phase, the cracks can 
hardly initiate in /g302-Mg matrix between the adjacent LPSO particles. Instead, the cracks 
cross the LPSO phase and propagate into the nearby /g302-Mg matrix along grain 
boundaries. Actually, during tensile deformation, the cracks initiate at the LPSO phase.

Since the Mg 5RE phase could pin the grain boundary, it impedes the growth of 
recrystallized grains during hot extrusion. Therefore, the grains are refined by the 
particle pinning. In addition, the Mg
5RE phase itself acts as an enforcement to 
strengthen the studied alloy via precipitation strengthening mechanism. LPSO phase, 
as a hard phase compared to the /g68-Mg matrix, can induce the stress concentration in 
the nearby region due to the incompatible deformation between the LPSO phase and 
/g68-Mg matrix [11, 13]. Consequently, the LPSO phase promotes the dynamic 
recrystallization and refines the recrystallized grains. As for the E-R samples, the 
increasing volume fraction of Mg 5RE phase can contribute to the improvement of 
mechanical properties via precipitation stre ngthening mechanism. The grain size 
decreases with increasing the rolling reduction (Table 3). The yield strength is 
therefore improved according to the Hall-Petch relationship. The associate 
strengthening mechanism of sub-grains will be discussed in section 4.4. 
The texture in the present alloy is weaker compared to that in the conventional 
wrought pure magnesium of 11.6 MRD, AZ31 of 13.1 MRD and ZE10 of 2.7 MRD

[30, 32]. The weak texture is attributed to the addition of a large amount of RE [30-32,

rolling, indicating that the cold rolling has negligible effects on the texture evolution. 
The arrangement of recrystallized grains thereby keeps almost unchanged. Two 
texture components exist in the present alloy. One component is the typical “basal” 
texture as observed in RE-containing wrought ZE10 [30, 31]. The angular distribution 
of basal poles is significantly titled toward the TD, whereas is not significantly 
broader toward the RD. Another is the unusual prismatic texture component which 
tilts 90 ° from the usual basal tex tures. It implies that the (10

formation of the prismatic texture component is thought to be a result of preferential 
growth of some specific recrystallized grains over other components. Grains with c
-axis titled away from the ND toward the tensile axis are more favorable for the 
activation of basal slip and for the formation of tensile twinning during tension. Thus, 
the “basal” texture component improves the YS when the tensile stress is applied 
along the ED/RD. Meanwhile, the prismatic texture component weakens the YS but 
improves the ductility. The effects of such texture on the mechanical properties are 
determined by the competition of these two texture components. This prismatic 
texture component was rarely reported, its formation mechanism needs to be further 
investigated.

4.3 Strengthening by SFs and /g69/g99phases 
The cluster of intrinsic SFs is observed in both E sample and E-R samples. They 
are parallel to basal plane of Mg matrix. The specific distribution of SFs provides 
effective barriers to the movement of non-basal dislocations and the nucleation of 
twinning [13]. As shown in Fig. 6b, RE and Zn atoms segregate at layers of the SFs. 
The segregation is attributable to the atomic size difference between the solute and the 
solvent. The atomic radius is 0.18 nm for Gd, 0.18 nm for Y, 0.181 nm for Nd, 0.134 
nm for Zn and 0.16 nm for Mg. When a Mg atom is replaced by a RE atom, a large 
positive misfit would form together with a compression strain field; while replacing a 
Mg atom by a Zn atom therefore leads to a neg ative misfit and an tensile strain field. 
The segregation allows the SFs act as obstacles to hinder the gliding of dislocations. 
In the E-14.5R sample, as shown in Fig. 8, most dislocations are bounded by the SFs 
after cold rolling. The planar arrays of dislocations which slide on the non-basal plane 
are interrupted when they cross the SFs as indicated by black arrows in Fig. 8b. When 
these dislocations travel through the SFs, they cross slip to other parallel planes. Thus, 
the SFs themselves could strengthen the alloy via hindering the movement of non-basal 
dislocation. The dislocation tangles form in the area with a dense SFs distribution. It is 
well known that the interaction of dislocation is relatively weak if they have a same 
Burgers vector on the same slip plan. The work-hardening effect is small in the area 
where single slip dominates. However, remarkable effect of work-hardening is 
provided as soon as the secondary slip systems become active [36]. The majority of

4.4 Grain boundary compatibility stress, non-basal slip and 
sub-grains 
In Fig. 8, the abundant non-basal a type and < c+a> type dislocations distribute 
inside grains after cold rolling. Some dislocations are assembled into dislocation walls 
and sub-grain boundaries near the grain boundaries. In the plastic deformation, the 
applied stress can easily concentrate at the grain boundaries and deformed zones, and 
such applied stress is considered as grain boundary compatibility stress [42, 43]. The 
effects of grain boundary compatibility stress on the slip systems have been 
investigated in many metals [42-45]. The previous investigations suggested that such 
stress can activate the second slip system adjacent to grain boundaries. Thus, the 
compatibility stress is regarded as the main driving force to activate the dislocation 
walls and sub-grain boundaries in this case. 
The dislocations arrange themselves in configurations characteristic of dislocation 
walls and sub-grain boundaries in order to meet the requirement of energy release. This 
process is termed as recovery. Apparently, the recovery takes place at room 
temperature in the present investigation. The sub-grain walls can act as obstacles to the

## Page 24

23 
 
 
movement of dislocations. It is difficult for the dislocations to penetrate the small 
sub-grains than the large ones, since the size-effect in the sub-grain hardening, i.e. 
smaller sub-grains are harder than the large on e [36]. The work hardening effect of 
sub-grains may enhance the strength, but weaken the ductility. Several investigations 
indicate the sub-grain affects the strength at room temperature in a similar way to that 
the grain does, i.e. the contribution to the strength is inversely proportional to the 
sub-grain size [46]. In the investigated grain, the size of sub-grain is less than 400 nm 
after cold rolling. The nano-scale sub-grains therefore play an important role in 
strengthening the alloy. 
 
5. Conclusions 
This work has successfully prepared Mg-11Gd-4.5Y-1Nd-1.5Zn-0.5Zr alloy 
(wt.%) with an ultra-high strength via combination of hot extrusion, cold rolling and 
ageing treatment: 
1. E-14.5R-T5 sheet has a high mechanical property with an average YS of 481.6 s
19.9 MPa, an average UTS of 517.4 s27.5 MPa and an average elongation to 
failure of 2.0 s0.4 % at room temperature. One E-14.5R-T5 specimen shows a 
YS of 502.0 MPa, an UTS of 546.8 MPa and an elongation of 2.6 %. 
2. The fine recrystallized grains strengthen the alloy. The fine grain size is 
attributed to the pinning effects of Mg 5RE particle on grain boundaries. The

## Page 25

24 
 
 
Mg5RE phase improves the mechanical properties via precipitation 
strengthening mechanism. 
3. LPSO phase strengthens the alloy via a short-fiber strengthening mechanism. 
The prismatic slip is the dominate slip system of the LPSO phase when tensile 
load is along (0001)
LPSO plane. The cracks initiate at LPSO phase under tension. 
4. Two texture components simultaneously exist in the E, E-R samples; one is the 
typical Mg-RE basal texture; another one is the unusual prismatic texture. Cold 
rolling has little effect on the texture. 
5. The grain boundaries compatibility stress contributes to the activation of the 
non-basal a type and <c+a> type dislocations and to the formation of nanoscale 
sub-grains during cold rolling. Recovery takes place at room temperature. The 
Sub-grain enhances the strength but decrease the ductility of the alloy. 
6. The basal plate precipitates are intrinsic SFs with a stacking sequence of 
ACACACBCBCB. The existence of SFs facilitates the formation of dislocation 
tangles and enhances work-hardening so that they can improve the YS, but 
decrease the ductility of the alloy. 
7. Cold rolling could enhance the ageing hardening effect, since the high density 
of dislocations facilitates the nucleation of 
/g69/g99 phase during ageing treatment. 
The increments of YS for E-T5, E-10R-T5 and E-14.5R-T5 samples after 
ageing are 70.8 MPa, 89.2 MPa and 81.0 MPa, respectively.
