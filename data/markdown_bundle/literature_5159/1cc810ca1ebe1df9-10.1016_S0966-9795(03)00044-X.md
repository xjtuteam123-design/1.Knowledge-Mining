High temperature strength of Mo–Mo3Si–Mo5SiB2
molybdenum silicides
J.H. Schneibel
Metals and Ceramics Division, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak Ridge, TN 37831-6115, USA
Received 1June 2002; accepted 3 February 2003
Abstract
Ternary and quaternary intermetallic alloys consisting of the phases Mo3Si, Mo5SiB2 (‘‘T2’’) anda-Mo (Mo solid solution) were
fabricated by melting and casting as well as powder-metallurgy (PM). A newly developed PM technique allowed the fabrication of
microstructures with a nearly continuousa-Mo matrix. The creep strengths were determined by slow compression testing at an
initial strain rate of 10/C05 s/C01 at temperatures ranging from 1200 to 1400/C14C. Alloying with niobium improved the creep strength
much more than alloying with tungsten. This result is rationalized in terms of the atomic size diﬀerence between Mo and Nb. Not
only the composition but also the topology and scale of the microstructure had a pronounced inﬂuence on the creep strength. As
expected, coarse microstructures with isolateda-Mo particles resulted in higher creep strengths than microstructures with a con-
tinuous a-Mo matrix.

1. Introduction
High-temperature structural Mo–Si–B alloys consist-
ing of a-Mo, Mo3Si, and the T2 (Mo5SiB2) phase were
pioneered by Berczik[1,2]. The phase triangle for these
alloys is indicated schematically inFig. 1. While Berc-
zik’s alloys are not as oxidation resistant as alloys with
higher Si concentrations consisting exclusively of brittle
silicides [3–5] they generally exhibit higher fracture
toughness values because of the presence of thea-Mo
phase [6].
Since Mo–Mo
3Si–Mo5SiB2 silicides are potential
high-temperature structural materials, knowledge of
their high temperature strength and in particular creep
strength is important. Nieh et al.[7] recently examined
the high temperature strength and creep strength of a
Mo–Si–B alloy consisting of similar volume fractions of
a-Mo and the T2 phase with Mo
3Si as a minor phase.
They found that its tensile strength was comparable to
that of ultrahigh temperature W–Re–ThO
2 alloys.
Schneibel et al.[8] recently determined the tensile and
compressive creep strength of a cast and annealed Mo–
Si–B alloy containing approximately 38 vol.%a-Mo, 32
vol.% Mo
3Si, and 30 vol.% T2. The intent of the pre-
sent work is to examine (a) the inﬂuence of solid solu-
tion alloying additions and (b) the inﬂuence of the
topology and scale of the microstructure on the creep
strength of Mo–Si–B silicides.

starting materials in a partial pressure of argon (70

(by weight) except for the boron which was 99.5% pure.

remelted 6 times. Prior to metallographic examination
all cast alloys were annealed for 24 h at 1600

alloy powders. In order to introduce additionala-Mo
(Mo solid solution) these powders were either blended
with Mo powder (2–8mm size) or vacuum annealed at

/C14C to remove silicon from their surface regions[9].

The temperature was measured with a thermocouple
inserted in a hole in the lower ram near the specimen.
The creep strength was deﬁned as the engineering ﬂow
stress at a plastic deformation of 2%.

3.2. Creep properties
The purpose of the creep measurements presented
below is not a precise identiﬁcation of the operating
creep mechanisms, which would be diﬃcult since these
materials contain 3 or 4 phases. Instead, empirical
equations for the creep strength will be derived and a
qualitative understanding of the dependence of the
creep strength on composition and microstructure will
be given. Some of the data shown was published in ear-
lier papers[8,16].
Fig. 7 shows an engineering stress–strain curve for
Mo–12Si–8.5B deformed at 1400
/C14C. After an initial rise
the stress reaches a plateau region where it varies only
slowly suggesting steady-state creep. At lower tempera-

showed usually a maximum stress near 2% plastic strain

The values for the apparent stress exponents and
activation energies of silicides vary widely and are diﬃ-
cult to interpret in terms of the underlying mechanisms.
The stress exponents in the present work are typically
near 3. The arc-melted polycrystalline (Mo,Cr)
3Si–
(Mo,Cr)5Si3 alloys investigated by Raj et al.[20] exhibit
stress exponents between 2.4 and 2.9. As pointed out by
Raj et al., stress exponents near 3 indicate that the creep
strength is controlled by viscous dislocation glide.
However, the absence of inverse creep in Raj’s silicides
[20] and in Mo–12Si–8.5B [8] does not allow one to
conclude that viscous dislocation glide is the controlling
creep mechanism. Our alloys consist of the Mo
3Si phase
which according to Raj[20] has a stress exponent near
3, the non-deformable T2 phase, and thea-Mo phase
with a stress exponent of 4.5[22]. The experimentally
found stress exponents with typical values near 3 appear
therefore reasonable. Alloy PM1, however, which has
the ﬁnest microstructure, exhibits a stress exponent of
2.1. This is consistent with Raj et al.’s (Mo,Cr)
3Si–
(Mo,Cr)5Si3 PM alloy [20] which exhibits stress expo-
nents between 1.6 and 1.8. It is surprising, on the other
hand, that the stress exponent of Nieh et al.’s[7] PM
Mo–9.4Si–13.8B alloy with a ﬁne microstructural size of
about 3 mm is 2.8.
Although a mechanistic analysis is not possible with
the available data, our results show that the creep
response of Mo–Si–B-based silicides can be controlled
very eﬀectively by alloying additions as well as the
topology and scale of the microstructure. Niobium
additions are particularly eﬀective in increasing the
creep strength. Coarse microstructures are, as expected,
beneﬁcial for the creep strength. While a continuousa-
Mo matrix is desirable from a fracture toughness point
of view, it tends to reduce the creep strength.
4. Conclusions
In this work the inﬂuence of alloying additions and
microstructure on the creep strength of Mo–Si–B alloys
has been studied. Tungsten additions improve the creep
strength only slightly, whereas niobium additions are
very eﬀective. Niobium additions increase the depen-
dence of the creep strength on the temperature (the
apparent activation energy) dramatically. The micro-
structure has also a strong inﬂuence on the creep
strength. Whereas ﬁne-scale microstructures can be
strong at ‘‘low’’ temperatures (1200
/C14C) they tend to be
relatively weak at ‘‘high’’ temperatures (1400 /C14C).
Microstructures with isolateda-Mo particles tend to be
stronger than microstructures with a continuousa-Mo
matrix. This work thus oﬀers strategies for improving
the creep strength of Mo–Si–B alloys. However, in
optimizing the creep strength other requirements such
as adequate fracture toughness and oxidation resistance
have to be considered as well.
