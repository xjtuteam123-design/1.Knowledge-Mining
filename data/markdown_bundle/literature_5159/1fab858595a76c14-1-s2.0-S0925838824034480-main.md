ABSTRACT

laminated composite strategy to achieve a 17 – 4PH/H13 heterostructure alloy. Also, the effect of its hard-soft

1. Introduction
AISI H13 hot-work die steels with the nominal composition of C (0.3 
~ 0.4 wt%), Cr (4 ~ 6 wt%) and microalloying elements of Mo and V 
have been widely applied in forging, hot rolling, die casting and hot- 
extrusion molding because of good high-temperature microstructural 
stability and secondary-carbide precipitation strengthening [1] . In the 
high-temperature service, the high mechanical loading compels the die 
steel to suffer from severe wear and fatigue cracking [2] . The damaged 
dies always prolong the production cycles and increase manufacturing 
costs for enterprises. Also, the increasing demand for complex topo -
logical structures stimulates the technological revolution of die-steel 
manufacturing [3] .
Recently, many attempts have been adopted to manufacture the H13 
die steel by coaxial powder-feeding laser melting deposition (LMD), 
selected laser melting (SLM), and electron-beam powder bed fusion. 
Among these additive manufacturing (AM) technologies, LMD accu -
mulates layer-by-layer alloys at a planned scan path using the laser beam 
to fuse the coaxial feeding powders [4] . Compared with traditional 
casting, the distinctive advantages of numberless molten pools and rapid 
cooling rates render LMD-ed alloys with finer grain dimensions, slighter 
dendritic segregation, and denser dislocation substructures [5] . To date, 
LMD has achieved highly dense ( ≥ 99 %) H13 steels well. The typical 
microstructure of AM-ed H13 steels comprises martensite, retained 
austenite, and tiny carbides [6] . Meanwhile, the complex heat history 
provides an austenitization heating and subsequent martensitic trans -
formation in a rapid-cooling process, in which the in-situ quenching is 
beneficial to exceptionally high strength yet undesirable ductility [7] . 
Generally, the H13 bulks with poor ductility are sensitive to cracking. 
Sun et al. found that large amounts of martensite plus a few retained 
austenite in the SLM-ed H13 steels led to a high yield strength ( σ

steel, and their laminated alloy, respectively. Generally, the formation of 
dimples is mainly due to the coalescence and growth of micro-voids. 
Usually, the widespread dimples in the fracture surface indicate good 
ductility [22] . Cleavage fracture shows the special feature of brittle 
fracture with low energy propagating along the crystallographic plane 
with low refractive index [23] . A large area of small dimples with 
average dimensions of less than 10 µ m and a minor number of cleavage 
river patterns are observed in the fracture surface of the 17 – 4PH steel, 
indicating that a fracture mode is dominant by ductile fracture. As for 
the H13 steel, the fracture morphology is filled with abundant cleavage 
river patterns without the dimples, further demonstrating a brittle 
fracture. Microscopic images of two different layers are visible on the 
fracture surface after fracture in the H13/17 – 4PH alloy. The fracture 
surface of laminated alloy exhibits a vivid boundary between the 
17 – 4PH and H13 layers, in which the fracture mode in the H13 layer is 
still governed by brittle fracture whereas the fracture mode in the 
17 – 4PH layer is also the ductile fracture but the fraction of cleavage 
stripes in the 17 – 4PH layer increases compared with the 17 – 4PH steel.
Generally, many researchers attempted to clarify the underlying 
mechanism of collaborative strength-ductility improvement in lami -
nated heterogeneous alloys. For the strain-hardening mechanism, the 
microstructure and microhardness results find that our laminated alloy 
possesses two alternate layers: one is filled with fine lath martensite, 
which is also called a “ harder ” layer; the other one includes coarsen lath 
martensite, which is known as “ softer ” layer. During tension, the softer 
layers first experience plastic deformation and the harder layer never 
yields without deformation. As the deformation continues, the plastic 
instability is always delayed since the microcracks do not rapidly 
propagate through the harder/softer interfaces [24] . Based on the 
Consid ´ ere criterion, the delayed plastic instability is conducive to the 
enhancement of uniform deformation in the laminated heterostructure 
alloys [25] . For the strengthening mechanism, the fusion of pool 
boundaries first pushes the interstitial C atoms of the H13 layer to 
diffuse into the 17 – 4PH layer, resulting in the refinement of lath 
martensite in the 17 – 4PH layer and ultimately enhancing the strength of 
the softer layer. Secondly, the little decreased σ
b 
is attributed to the 
release of local stress between the softer (first deforming) to the harder 
(non-deforming) zones. The suppression of necking behavior will lead to

H13/17 – 4PH alloy is supposed to suppress the cracks propagation to

Besides, it is reported that a superior combination of microhardness and 
ductility is considered to improve wear resistance [63] . The 
H13/17 – 4PH laminated alloy possessing high strength yet sufficient 
ductility is critical for superior wear resistance. It is worth noting that 
our H13/17 – 4PH laminated alloy shows comparable wear resistance at 
600 ℃ to that at RT. First, Cr content has a remarkable impact on 
high-temperature oxidation to suppress the outward diffusion of Fe 
cations [64] . The fresh metals of the H13 steel are rapidly oxidized at

growth suppression of oxide by comparative high Cr content, the 
H13/17 – 4PH alloy that contains a continuous compact oxide layer is 
considered to embrace a superior wear resistance compared with the 
H13 steel at 600 ℃ . Given these, the H13/17 – 4PH alloy provides a 
reference for the re-manufacturing of the die steels applied in 
high-temperature conditions.
4. Conclusion
In summary, a laminated heterostructure H13/17 – 4PH alloy is suc -
cessfully manufactured by LMD. Its microstructure, phase structure, 
mechanical properties and wear resistance are systematically investi -
gated in detail. The main conclusions can be addressed below.
(1) the laminated alloy mainly comprises two alternately stacked 
martensite layers with a layer width of 350 µ m. The 
microstructure of the 17 – 4PH layer is composed of coarse lath 
martensite, residual austenite and petty ferrite. The H13 layer 
consists of fine martensite and residual austenite plus tiny 
carbides.
(2) Such a “ 1 + 1 ” laminated heterostructure engineering strategy can 
remarkably refine the mechanical performance of LDM-ed H13 
steel from brittle to ductile fracture. The H13/17 – 4PH alloy 
displays a tensile yield strength of ~ 1370.8 MPa and ductility of 
~ 5.2 %.
