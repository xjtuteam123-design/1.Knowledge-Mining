Deformation of high b-phase fraction Zr–Nb alloys
at room temperature
S. Cai, M.R. Daymond ⇑, R.A. Holt

Received 25 November 2011; received in revised form 17 February 2012; accepted 18 February 2012
Available online 6 April 2012
Abstract
A series of in situ compression tests has been carried out at room temperature for three dual-phase Zr–Nb alloys with diﬀerent Nb
contents. Changes in the Nb content change the relative fraction of b-phase present in the alloy. The evolution of interphase and inter-
granular strain was monitored during deformation by neutron diﬀraction. Surprisingly the strength diﬀerence between the three alloys is
caused principally by changes in the a-phase strength rather than being due to changes in the b-phase volume fraction; the yield strength
of the Nb-rich b-phase is /C24 400 MPa and is almost unchanged in the three alloys. The strength of the a-phase signiﬁcantly changed
between alloys due to the combined eﬀects of increasing oxygen content and decreasing grain size. The change in strengthening mech-
anisms in the a-phase is crystallographically anisotropic, e.g. more change was observed for prismatic slip than for pyramidal slip. As a
result, the mechanical anisotropy of the a-phase decreases with increasing strength. Depending on the b/a strength ratio, the b-phase can
be either weaker or stronger than the average material strength. A soft b-phase helps to produce compressive residual stresses in the
a-phase, while a strong b-phase produces tensile residual stresses in a-phase. A combined ﬁnite-element method and elastoplastic
self-consistent multiscale model is used to interpret the experimental results. Despite the inability to capture the rapid stress relaxation
observed, the combined method is shown to be eﬀective in interpreting the deformation behavior of these dual-phase Zr–Nb alloys. The
results are relevant to the study of other two-phase alloys, particularly titanium-based alloys.

1. Introduction
Many of the properties of alloys with a hexagonal crys-
tal structure, such as zirconium and titanium, are depen-
dent on the crystallographic texture. This arises from the
signiﬁcant anisotropy of properties at the crystallographic
level, including for example elasticity, thermal expansion
and plasticity [1].
In their pure form both zirconium and titanium exhibit
a hexagonal close packed (hcp) a-phase which is stable at
low temperatures, and a body-centered cubic (bcc) b-phase
stable at elevated temperatures. Alloying elements can
aﬀect the relative stability of the phases at a given temper-
ature, and produce two-phase ( a + b) regimes at intermedi-
ate temperatures.
The Zr–Nb binary phase diagram in the vicinity of the
a/b transus is a monotectoid with the monotectoid reaction
at /C24 600 /C176C and 20 wt.% Nb. Thus for the Zr–Nb binary
system with more than /C24 0.6 wt.% but less than 20 wt.%
Nb present, on cooling from the high temperature
b
Zr-phase, there is precipitation of aZr below the b ! a + b
phase transition ( /C24 860 /C176C for 2.5% Nb) [2]. At tempera-
tures below /C24 600 /C176C, eutectoid decomposition occurs under
equilibrium conditions and the remaining bZr transforms to
aZr (/C24 0.5 wt.% Nb) and bcc bNb (/C24 95 wt.% Nb). However,
this reaction is very slow and thus at room temperature the
material typically consists of a
Zr and metastable bZr, with
the Nb mostly in the bZr-phase. The Nb content in the aZr
is <1 wt.%, while in the bZr it is /C24 20 wt.%.

Due to good mechanical strength, high corrosion and
creep resistance, combined with low neutron absorption,
Zr-based alloys are used extensively in nuclear power appli-
cations. For example Zr–2.5 Nb is used, among other
things, as the pressure tube material for CANDU reactors
[3]. Understanding alloy deformation mechanisms is of
interest in predicting in-reactor deformation and to provide
guidance for reactor maintenance [4]. The mechanical
behavior of two-phase alloys depends on the properties
of each phase and the interaction between them during
deformation. It has been suggested that in Zr–2.5 Nb the
b-phase aﬀects the deformation mechanism of the a-phase
and thus the texture evolution during processing [5]. Due to
the relatively small volume fraction of b-phase ( /C24 10%)
present at room temperature, its contribution to the defor-
mation of Zr–2.5 Nb is often neglected for the purposes of
modeling the behavior, and is still poorly understood. With
the increasing demand for new materials for future nuclear
power plants and to develop more accurate models to sim-
ulate current Zr–Nb alloys, understanding the contribution
of the b-phase to the mechanical response of Zr–Nb alloys
becomes necessary, especially considering recent develop-
ments in high b-fraction Ti alloys [6].
In (a + b) dual-phase Ti alloys, texture evolution during
deformation strongly depends on the geometrical arrange-
ment and on the volume fraction of the constituent phases
[7]. Further, due to elastic and plastic phase interactions,
the deformation modes operating in each phase can be dif-
ferent from those operating in the single-phase state [8].I n
most cases, it has been concluded that the b-phase is softer
than the a-phase; the b-phase suppresses hc + ai slip and
twinning in the a-phase [9]; increases the activity of the
prismatic hai slip and helps to accommodate the hci com-
ponent of strain [10]. The inﬂuence of the b-phase increases
with increasing b/a ratio [10,11]. Bearing in mind that
Zr–Nb alloys have similar microstructures and crystal
structures to a/b Ti alloys, similar eﬀects of the b-phase
on a-phase deformation systems might be expected [12].
However, in our previous study of the deformation of a
hot-rolled Zr–2.5 Nb plate material [13], the yield stress
of the b-phase was found to be higher than that of the a-
phase. The high
b-phase strength was attributed partially
to the small grain size but mostly to the segregation of
the b-stabilizing elements, particularly Nb, providing sig-
niﬁcant solid solution strengthening. In our recent study
[14] of the deformation of a 100% b
Zr material with a rela-
tively large grain size ( /C24 200 lm), the strength was found to
be as high as /C24 550 MPa, attributed to solute hardening
from the O [15] and Nb [16] contents. This indicates the
need for more detailed and complete studies of b-phase
properties and their inﬂuence on properties of Zr–Nb
alloys. In this paper, we report studies on the room temper-
ature deformation of three Zr–Nb alloys with diﬀerent Nb
contents. Deformation experiments carried out in situ
within a neutron spectrometer allow study of the average
phase properties and of the responses of individual families
of crystal orientations during deformation. With the aid of
a new modeling approach using a combined self-consistent
and FE scheme [17], the inﬂuence of the microstructure
change (e.g. grain size and volume fraction) on the defor-
mation mechanisms can be interpreted.
2. Materials and experimental method
Three ZrNb alloys, termed 5% Nb, 10% Nb and 15% Nb
based on their diﬀerent Nb contents, were provided by
Atomic Energy of Canada Ltd. They were manufactured
by Teledyne Wah Chang in the form of extruded bars,
50 mm in diameter. The chemical compositions are listed
in Table 1 ; the alloying elements were well homogenized
within the bar but are segregated between the a-a n d
b-phases. The volume fractions of the a-phase based on
the Zr–Nb binary phase diagram [18] are /C24 75%, 50% and
25% respectively in these materials at room temperature.
However, the oxygen ( a-phase stabilizer) and iron ( b-phase
stabilizer) contents are diﬀerent in the materials. Due to the
presence of these minority elements the a-phase volume
fractions, obtained by Rietveld reﬁnement [19] from the
diﬀraction spectra obtained from these materials, are
/C24 79%, 60% and 33%, respectively. Metallographic exami-
nation showed a bimodal distribution in all three as-
received (hot extruded) alloys, with occasional huge grains
embedded inside a ﬁne matrix. Heat treatments were intro-

the b-phase distributed between the a-grains. With increas-
ing Nb content, the a-phase decreases in grain size as well
as in volume fraction. In the 15% Nb alloy, the a-phase,
which has a feather-like morphology, is distributed inside
the b-matrix. Compression loading samples were cut to a
size /C24 8 /C2 20 mm with the longitudinal direction parallel

3. Experimental results and discussion
3.1. Macroscopic mechanical properties
Typical macroscopic mechanical responses obtained are
plotted in Fig. 3 . Based on the three tests of each material,
the Young’s moduli are /C24 78 ± 9 GPa, 71 ± 4 GPa and
66 ± 3 GPa for the 5% Nb, 10% Nb and 15% Nb alloys
respectively (irrespective of sample orientation). The 0.2%
yield strengths were 358 ± 7 MPa, 483 ± 3 MPa and
547 ± 15 MPa respectively in the transverse samples. Thus
with increasing Nb content (i.e. increasing fraction of b-
phase), the Young’s modulus decreases. This is in agreement
with a b-phase modulus of /C24 59 GPa [14], compared to an a-
phase modulus of /C24 100 GPa [28]. Correspondingly the yield
strength increases with increasing Nb content. For the 5%
Nb and 10% Nb, the yield strength of the transverse samples
was slightly ( /C24 20 MPa) higher than that of the axial sam-
ples; for the 15% Nb the yield strength was approximately
the same for transverse and axial samples. Finally, with
increasing yield strength, the hardening rate decreased.

4. Modeling
To predict the deformation behavior of the two-phase
ZrNb alloys, a successful modeling method should be capa-
ble of modeling the mechanical properties on both the mac-
roscopic and microscopic scales. On the microscopic scale,
a model should be able to simulate the average phase
behaviors as well as the evolution of the intergranular
strains. The elastoplastic self-consistent (EPSC) model
has shown considerable success for the single-phase materi-
als [17,32]. Recently, a new modeling approach combining
the ﬁnite-element method (FEM) and the EPSC model has
been shown to be eﬀective in modeling the deformation
behavior of multiphase materials [17,32]. In this modeling
approach, FEM is used to calculate the average phase
responses and the calculated stress or strain of each phase
is used as the boundary condition to an EPSC model to
simulate the interactions among the individual grain fami-
lies in each phase. This method has been used in modeling
the deformation behavior of a hot rolled Zr–2.5 Nb plate
[28]. In the present paper, the same method will be com-
pared to the experimental results from the three Zr– x Nb
alloys reported above.
Details of the model and the procedures used to deter-
mine model parameters are given in Ref. [28]. In brief, a
simple FEM unit cell is constructed to capture the general
features of the microstructure, such as the volume fraction
and relative geometry of each phase. A simple FEM unit
cell model cannot capture the complicated microstructures
shown in Fig. 1 , where phases are locally randomly
arranged and each grain has diﬀerent crystal orientation.
Here the complicated microstructure is simpliﬁed to a unit
cell in which the a-phase is designed as a cube wrapped by
the b-phase on three adjacent planes. Perfect bonds were
applied between phases, i.e. no phase boundary sliding.
Each phase was provided with the average phase properties
(elastic and plastic) calculated based on the single-crystal
properties and texture distribution, using EPSC (see
below).

6. Discussion
The strength diﬀerence observed for loading in the axial
and transverse directions ( Fig. 3 ) can be explained by the
texture dependence of strength of the a-phase. The fraction
of resolved basal plane normal is slightly higher in the
transverse than in the axial direction ( Table 2). The hci axis
is the plastically hardest direction, thus the a-phase is
stronger in the transverse direction, resulting in higher
0.2% yield strengths. The inﬂuence of the a-phase decreases
with a decrease in its volume fraction. The a-phase texture
of the 15% Nb alloy is relatively random compared with
the other two alloys, which decreases the strength anisot-
ropy. The b-phase texture is weak in all samples, thus for
the 15% Nb alloy (highest b fraction), the yield strengths
of the axial and transverse samples are almost the same.
Another minor contribution to the small strength diﬀeren-
tial of the 15% Nb, as will be discussed later, is related to
the smaller strength anisotropy of the a crystal in the
15% Nb alloys than that seen in the other two alloys.
From our previous study on a hot rolled Zr–2.5 Nb [13],
where the b-phase was found to be stronger than the
a-phase, one might attribute the increasing strength of
the three alloys to the increasing volume fraction of the
b-phase. However, the development of the phase and indi-
vidual grain family strains gives a diﬀerent explanation.
From Fig. 4 , assuming the Poisson constraints between
the a and b-phases can be ignored, we can roughly estimate
the phase yield strengths by multiplying the Young’s mod-
ulus and the yield point strains in the loading direction. For
example, in 5% Nb the ﬁrst inﬂection point of the a-phase
indicates its yielding, thus the lattice strain at this point,

The strength increase of the a-phase in these alloys is
likely related to the increasing O content ( Table 1 ) due to
the solid strengthening mechanism [15,43–45] as well as
to the grain size reﬁnement shown in Fig. 1. The grain size
reﬁnement is attributed to increasing Nb content. As the
Nb content increases, the b to-a phase transformation tem-
perature decreases, which reduces the growth rate of the
precipitating a-phase, and results in a ﬁner resultant a-
grain size.
This analysis suggests that the macroscopic strength
increment from 5% Nb to 15% Nb can be mainly attributed
to the strength increase of the a-phase, which is supported
by the increased lattice strains at yield ( Figs. 6–8 ). The
strength of the {1 0 /C2210 }

strengthening mechanism. This is conﬁrmed when consid-

of the prismatic hai slip increases faster than those of basal
hai and pyramidal hc+ ai slips (in terms of percentage
increase), as Nb content is increased. For example, the
CRSS of the compressive pyramidal hc + ai slip showed lit-
tle change between the 10% Nb and 15% Nb, while the
CRSS of the prismatic hai slip increased steadily. The
anisotropic strengthening mechanism may be associated
with the eﬀect of oxygen on diﬀerent slip systems. Bailey
[46] studied dislocation structures in deformed zirconium
with two diﬀerent oxygen and nitrogen contents and found
that the stress for prismatic hai slip increased less rapidly
than that for basal hai slip with increasing oxygen and
nitrogen contents, implying an anisotropic eﬀect of oxygen
on diﬀerent slip systems. The study of Churchman in sin-
gle-crystal Ti [47] showed that interstitial atoms like oxygen
and nitrogen obstruct dislocation slip on (1 0 /C221 /C2210 ) a n d
(0 0 0 1) planes, but have less eﬀect on slip on the (1 0 /C2211 )
plane. A similar eﬀect might be expected in a-Zr, consider-
ing it has both a similar crystal structure and c/a ratio as a-
Ti. Akhtar [48] studied the deformation mechanism of sin-
gle-crystal Zr with interstitial (O) content of /C24 260 ppm and
found that the CRSS of pyramidal slip is /C24 10–20 times
higher than that of prismatic slip. Similarly, Tome ´ et al.
[49] and Oliver et al. [50] have obtained very high pyrami-
dal-to-prismatic CRSS ratios for a pure zirconium poly-
crystal with interstitial (C + O + N) element less than
100 ppm. However, this CRSS ratio is typically found to
be in a range of only /C24 2–4 in polycrystalline Zr alloys with
higher interstitial content (O = /C24 900–1500 ppm, C = /C24 40–
160 ppm and N = /C24 20–60 ppm) [40,41,51,52]. All of these
observations conﬁrm the likely anisotropic strengthening
mechanism of interstitial atoms in a-Zr and provide an
explanation to the current experimental results; the large
CRSS increment of the prismatic slip from 5% Nb to
15% Nb is caused by both the increasing oxygen content
and the decreasing grain size, while if the pyramidal
hc + ai
and basal slips are less strongly inﬂuenced by oxygen con-
tent, the observed changes are thus presumably more asso-
ciated with the decreasing grain size. The dispersion in the
phase size and the morphology of the phases are also diﬀer-

the diﬀerences in strength. Since the eﬀects of grain size
(average value and distribution), grain morphology and
the oxygen content are combined here, we are unable to
separate these factors unambiguously. One can make an
estimate of the grain size eﬀect from the Hall–Petch coeﬃ-
cient derived from the data given by Cheadle in Ref. [53];
for Zr–2.5 Nb with a grain size reduction from /C24 10 to
/C24 1 lm, the eﬀect is a few tens of MPa, and is thus of the
right order of magnitude to explain the observed results.

than the b-phase, which makes the a-phase yield earlier
than it would without the b-phase. For example, in 10%

though it has the higher yield strength. Therefore, the exis-
tence of the b-phase decreases the Young’s modulus of the
bulk material and causes early microscopic yield. This
eﬀect increases with increasing b-phase volume fraction,
see Section 4.1. In the plastic region, the b-phase inﬂuence
depends on the relative b/a strength ratio. If the b-phase is
stronger than the a-phase, it increases the macroscopic
material strength, as in 5% Nb. Otherwise, it decreases
the material strength as in 10% Nb and 15% Nb. Consider-

diﬀerent crystal orientations and diﬀerent properties, the

in the 10% Nb, even though the average phase behavior

b-phase between the a-grains accommodates the hci-com-
ponent strains and delays the onset of the hc + ai slip
(and twinning). However, for the a/b transformed micro-
structures [23] that we have here we believe that this is unli-
kely; according to the Burgers relationship between the
phases and the habit plane of the a in the b, the c-axis lies
in the long dimension of the plate-like a-grains [57], i.e.
accommodation of strains in the c-direction of the a-grains
by relaxation of the b-phase is unlikely.

the a and b-phases may not be fully captured. It is evident
that the FEM unit cell model is too simple compared to the
real microstructures of Fig. 1 . For example, the phase
geometry in the FEM separates materials with ”average”
phase properties, rather than individual grains; this would
be expected to result in some error in calculating load dis-
tribution between phases, and perhaps in the calculation of
thermal stresses. Further, in reality, each individual a-grain
has a plate-like shape with diﬀerent aspect ratios in the
three alloys. However, since grains are randomly orientated
with respect to the principal sample directions, in the FEM
each phase was designed as having a cubic shape for sim-
plicity. This simpliﬁcation may underestimate the con-
straint along the longitudinal grain axis and overestimate
the constraint in the short grain axis. Also in the FEM
model, a perfect bond was imposed on the a–b interface
and thus no boundary glide was allowed. However, since
the a–b grain boundary glide has been found to be an
important deformation mode in dual-phase Ti alloys at
room temperature [58], it may be expected to occur in
Zr–Nb alloys as well. Thus the FE model may overestimate
the constraint between the a and b-phase during plastic
deformation. Second, in the EPSC model, for dislocation
slip to be activated, the stress must be kept on the yield sur-
face during each increment. Once the resolved shear stress
(rss) of a certain slip system exceeds the critical shear stress
(s), s is then increased to the value of the rss instead of
altering the stress state [39]. The eﬀect is negligible if very
small steps are taken and the system continues to harden;
the scheme prevents a slip system from being “too” soft
no matter how low the hardening rate given to that system,
and does not allow for relaxation in a given slip system. In
reality stress relaxation may be caused by dynamic recov-
ery, twinning, grain boundary gliding and strain-induced
phase transformations (though not the last in the materials
studied here). It can also be caused by mechanisms such as
strain aging [59], where a high stress is initially required to
pull dislocations away from interstitial atoms that pin
them; once the dislocations break free from the interstitials,
they can move at a lower stress. All of these are not treated
by the current EPSC model and could contribute to the
failure to predict the soft responses of the {1 0 /C2210 }

Fig. 8 d). Further, EPSC is able to treat the eﬀect of grain
shape by providing a certain aspect ratio along the princi-
pal sample directions only if grains are highly aligned in
these directions. It is unable to capture the inﬂuence of
grain shape if grains are randomly orientated with respect
to sample directions such as Fig. 1 . Grain shape changes
aﬀect the thermal residual stress and the evolution of inter-
granular stress. Nonetheless, these limitations do not pre-
vent the model from giving an overall good simulation
and providing signiﬁcant insight into the operating defor-
mation modes.
7. Conclusions
In situ room temperature compression tests have been
carried out for three ZrNb alloys, (5% Nb, 10% Nb and
15% Nb). Time-of-ﬂight neutron diﬀraction was applied
to monitor the evolution of the interphase and intergranu-
lar strains during deformation along both the loading and
the Poisson directions. The experimental data were inter-
preted by a multi-scale modeling approach. The following
were found:
1. The Young’s modulus is /C24 78 ± 9 GPa, 71 ± 4 GPa and
66 ± 3 GPa and the yield strength r
0.2 is /C24 358 ± 7 MPa,
483 ± 3 MPa and 547 ± 15 MPa for 5% Nb, 10% Nb
and 15% Nb respectively. In these three alloys, the
strength of the b-phase is determined as /C24 400 MPa
and remains almost unchanged. The strength of the a-
phase signiﬁcantly increased from 5% Nb to 15% Nb,
caused by the combined eﬀect of increasing oxygen con-
tent and decreasing grain size.
2. The solute strengthening of oxygen in the a-phase is
anisotropic. The CRSS of prismatic slip increased more
rapidly with increasing oxygen content than that of
basal and pyramidal slip. As a result, the mechanical
anisotropy of the a-phase decreased with increasing
strength.
3. Depending on the b/a strength ratio, the b-phase can
either decrease or increase the material strength relative
to the a-phase strength. A soft b-phase produces com-
pressive residual stresses in the a-phase.
4. The combination of FEM and EPSC is shown to be an
eﬀective method in modeling the deformation behavior
of dual-phase materials. It quantitatively estimates the
phase properties and the development of phase con-
straint during deformation. By iteratively ﬁtting the
experimental data of the lattice strain development for
