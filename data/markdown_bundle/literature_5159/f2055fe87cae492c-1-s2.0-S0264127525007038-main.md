1. Introduction
The most durable components for commercial gas turbine engines 
are made from cast & wrought Ni-based superalloys due to their high- 
temperature strength, corrosion resistance, and creep resistance [ 1 , 2 ]. 
Service lives of these components may span over 1,000 h, operating at 
temperatures of 800 
◦
C for loads reaching 600 MPa and beyond [ 3 , 4 ]. 
Therefore, the most highly alloyed grades, such as Waspaloy, Haynes ® 
282 ® , and Ren ´e 41 are required to engineer more efficient next- 
generation gas turbine engines. However, their manufacturability and 
in-service properties are often limited due to grain boundary (GB) 
cracking [ 5 , 6 ]. To address these issues, microalloying additions of B (in 
some cases combined with C or Zr) have been shown to improve GB 
cohesion and strength in first-principle studies [ 7 , 8 ], during creep 
testing [ 9 , 10 ], additive manufacturing [ 11 , 12 ], and hot working 
[ 13 , 14 ].
The typical microstructure of Ni-based superalloys consists of a face- 
centered cubic (fcc) γ -matrix and L1

[ 1 , 2 ]. It is known that microalloying additions such as B, C, and Zr have 
complex interactions with solutes in the proximity of GBs [ 15 ]. Equi -

improve GB cohesion and strength [ 8 , 18 ]. However, excessive additions 
may instead promote the formation of GB- γ’ [ 6 , 12 ], carbides (e.g., M

The elastic and plastic anisotropy of Ni-based superalloys is well- 
established for many polycrystalline cast & wrought grades [ 27 – 30 ]. 
Inherently, local variations in their strain and stress response on the 
scale of individual grains have been shown experimentally [ 23 , 31 ]. 
They can be predicted using the crystal plasticity finite element method 
(CP-FEM) [ 32 , 33 ]. Roters et al. [ 34 ] provide an extensive review on the 
wide applicability of CP-FEM to single- and multi-phase materials to 
understand their mechanical behavior at the mesoscale. Alabort et al. 
[ 33 ] applied such methodology to predict damage initiation in a poly -
crystalline Ni-based superalloy. Free and open source CP-FEM capabil -
ities are currently further developed such as the Neper and FEPX 
software packages by Quey & Kasemer [ 35 ].
However, at the atomic-scale, solute segregation decorating GBs and 
interfaces may have an additional impact on the respective interface 
cohesion and strength. The presence of carbides and/or borides in -
troduces additional interfaces between each other and to the sur -
rounding γ -matrix, all of which may be prone to decohesion, 
fragmentation, and cracking [ 25 , 36 – 38 ]. For example, first-principles 
calculations have shown that GB and interface cohesion is facilitated 
via bond sharing and/or the displacement of deleterious elements 
[ 7 , 8 , 18 , 39 ]. Sanyal et al. [ 8 , 39 ] demonstrated this for Cr and S located 
at Σ 5 boundaries in Ni, and Mo and O located at the Ni / Cr
5
B
3 
interface. 
More recently, Ebner et al. [ 7 ] showed that Mo and B can replace 
deleterious solutes from GBs. Some of the current authors recently re -
ported on the interaction of micro-alloying elements with GB pre -
cipitates [ 40 ]. Furthermore, solute segregation close to interfaces can 
promote the formation of interface-stabilized phases, also known as 
complexions [ 41 – 43 ]. Such local changes in the structure of GBs and 
interfaces may promote the accumulation of strain and thus provide 
failure initiation sites [ 44 ].
Hence, three mechanisms responsible for interface decohesion and 
subsequent GB cracking require consideration in cast & wrought Ni- 
based superalloys: The strain and stress distributions at the mesoscale, 
ranging between 100 s and 1,000 s of nanometers [ 45 , 46 ], the nanoscale 
structure, and the sub-nanoscale chemical environment of the GB 
microstructure. Strain and stress accumulation across GBs have been 
linked to crack nucleation, and interface decohesion [ 33 , 47 ]. The 
nanoscale GB structure and geometry impact dislocation accumulation 
as well as the interface energy [ 48 , 49 ]. Similarly, the chemical envi -
ronment may introduce additional phases or influence the cohesive 
energy [ 24 , 48 , 49 ]. However, a study investigating these mechanisms 
systematically in the presence of carbides and borides decorating GBs in 
cast & wrought Ni-based superalloys is currently unavailable.
Thus, we attempt to address this knowledge gap by correlating in- 
situ tensile testing, CP-FEM modeling, and high-resolution character -
ization to reveal the origins of interface decohesion and GB cracking in 
the Ni-based superalloy Ren ´e 41. In-situ tensile testing and electron 
microscopy are used to reveal a sequence of mechanisms before the 
onset of GB cracking. Engineering strain and stress are reproduced using 
CP-FEM modeling. The local atomic-scale interface structure and 
composition of the γ -matrix and GB-M
2
B, M
6
C, or M
23
C
6 
are revealed by 
high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and atom 
probe microscopy (APM) and are linked to interface cohesion phe -
nomena. We provide insights into the GB microstructure – property 
relationship and illustrate the complex roles of different GB precipitates 
during the initiation of GB cracking in the Ni-based superalloy Ren ´e 41.

3. Results
3.1. Mechanical properties
Fig. 2 provides an overview of the mechanical properties acquired by 
miniature tensile testing (in dark blue, acquired by some of the current 
authors in [ 40 ]) and new in-situ tensile testing data (in dark red). Tur -
quoise and red lines refer to the calibrated CP-FEM models, respectively. 
As the CP-FEM model calibration is based on miniature tensile testing, 
their applicability may be restricted until a strain of ~ 10 %. The 
experimental miniature tensile test was used to calibrate the model, 
hence, these datasets correlate well. The in-situ CP-FEM model appro -
priately represents the in-situ tensile test without further modifications. 
Note that the model somewhat underestimates the engineering stress 
here, but consistently lies within the error margin of the experimental 
data. A considerably higher ductility is observed during in-situ tensile 
testing when compared to miniature tensile testing with larger cross- 
section in the gauge region [ 40 ]. In contrast, the gauge length geome -
try in the present study likely provides near- plane stress conditions 
resulting in negligible stress across the thickness, and thus, increased 
elongation. Furthermore, fewer crack nucleation sites also may be 
present, as will be discussed later.

4. Discussion
4.1. In-situ tensile testing
The engineering strain – stress curves in Fig. 2 provide a satisfactory 
match between experimental and CP-FEM data, and thus confirm the 
applicability of the current CP-FEM model. A reduced work hardening 
behavior of in-situ tensile testing samples relates to considerations in 
literature. High-temperature tensile testing of MAR – M 247 [ 62 ] caused 
considerable work hardening outside of the gauge length. Ren ´e 41 may 
exhibit a similar behavior during ambient temperate testing. The high 
strain during in-situ tensile testing in Fig. 1 is attributed to the reduced 
number of GBs within the gauge length and thus, the presence of fewer 
potential crack nucleation sites. Nonetheless, intragranular cracking as 
the result of γ -matrix / GB-M
2
B interface delamination is observed 
during in-situ tensile testing, which corresponds to the reported failure 
mechanism of Ren ´e 41 [ 6 ]. GB cracking nucleation sites are observed at 
Σ 9-type boundaries but are not exclusive to them. Indeed, Σ 9-type 
boundaries exhibit many properties comparable to random HAGBs, such 
as relatively high GB energy [ 63 ], as well as low energy to form dislo -
cations [ 64 ]. HAGBs with high GB energy have been reported to be 
prone to cracking in cast & wrought as well as AM processed Ni-based 
superalloys [ 6 , 11 ].

5. Conclusions
This study provides insights into the detailed mechanism of GB 
cracking in the cast & wrought Ni-based superalloy Ren ´e 41 where grain 
boundaries are decorated by various co-precipitates and solutes, 
considering strain and stress distributions at the mesoscale, the nano -
scale structure, and the nanoscale chemical environment of the GB 
microstructure. Mechanical properties are studied via in-situ tensile 
testing and CP-FEM modeling. Sequential interface decohesion and the 
onset of GB cracking are identified, whereas the local interface structure 
and composition revealed by high resolution microscopy provide in -
sights into the origins of GB cracking. The following conclusions on the 
grain boundary microstructure-property relationship can be drawn: 
• In-situ tensile testing shows the sequence of slip band formation, 
followed by interface decohesion around M
6
C and M
23
C
6 
carbides, 
and GB-M
2
B borides.
• The most severe interface decohesion is observed at γ -matrix / GB- 
M
2
B interfaces. These interfaces are initiation sites for GB cracking.
• In contrast to hypotheses in the literature, local equivalent strain and 
stress accumulations at GBs show no strong correlation with exper -
imentally observed GB cracking as revealed by CP-FEM. Similarly, 
interfaces between the γ -matrix and GB-M
2

6

23

transformations.
• The interface GB- γ’ / GB-M
2

cohesion. However, the interface γ -matrix / GB-M
2
B is insufficiently 
enriched in B and, thus, shows embrittlement. These interfaces 
exhibit the most severe decohesion and are therefore considered as 
origin for GB crack nucleation.
In its current alloy design iteration of Ren ´e 41, we remark that 
reduced B and C concentrations may be advisable, as 0.07 at.% B and 
0.14 at.% C are sufficient to produce nearly continuous GB decorations. 
These detailed insights into the local mechanical response of GB mi -
crostructures decorated by various co-precipitates are not only valuable 
to understand GB cracking in Ren ´e 41. The advanced understanding of 
the origins of GB cracking are transferrable to a range of compositionally 
similar grades of Ni-based superalloys such as Waspaloy or Haynes ® 
282 ® . Future efforts in microstructure engineering should seek path -
ways to avoid the frequent occurrence of undesirable interfaces between 
co-precipitates, via adjustments to alloy chemistry and processing.
