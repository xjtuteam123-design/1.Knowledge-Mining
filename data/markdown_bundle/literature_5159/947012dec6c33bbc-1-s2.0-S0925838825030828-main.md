1. Introduction
TA15 (Ti-6.5Al-1Mo-1V-2Zr), a near- α titanium alloy, exhibits 
excellent room-temperature and high-temperature strength, superior 
thermal stability, and welding performance, along with exceptional 
corrosion resistance, which makes it particularly suitable for the pro -
duction of critical components in aircraft engines and gas turbines [1 – 3] . 
Nevertheless, most components in aero engines feature intricate shapes 
and internal structures, often necessitating integrative structural design, 
which continues to pose significant challenges for manufacture through 
conventional methods [4] . Additionally, TA15 alloy inherently possesses 
high reactivity, low thermal conductivity, and substantial deformation 
resistance [5] . Consequently, it is imperative to investigate novel 
manufacturing techniques to address the challenges posed by complex 
structural configurations.
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), which fabricates complex structures 
through layer-by-layer melting and solidification, demonstrates signifi -
cant advantages in lightweight design and functional integration 
manufacturing [6 – 8] . However, two core challenges persist in LPBF 
processing of TA15 alloy: Firstly, rapid melting and solidification 
induced by the high-energy beam promotes porosity and lack-of-fusion 
defects, degrading relative density and mechanical properties [9] . Sec -
ondly, ultra-fast cooling generates acicular α ’ martensite, enhancing 
strength but compromising ductility, necessitating precise parameter 
control to achieve a balance between strength and toughness [10] . 
Current studies indicate that optimizing scanning speed and hatch 
spacing enables grain refinement and defect reduction [11 – 14] , thus 
rational adjustment of the energy density window enhances sample 
performance.
Meanwhile, the development of TA15 alloy was initially aimed at 
addressing the challenges of high-temperature service environments 
encountered in aerospace applications [15] . Therefore, by conducting 
mechanical tests under thermal conditions to systematically evaluate 
their strength and deformation behaviors can better reflect the actual 
usage conditions [16] . Wang et al. [17] studied the strength of 
LPBF-fabricated TA15 alloy in service environments ranging from 500

3.2. Phase analysis and microstructure evolution
Fig. 3 (a) shows the XRD spectra of the original TA15 alloys powder 
and samples A-D. There is no significant β phase diffraction peak in 
either the original powder or the LPBF-fabricated TA15 alloys. It was 
evident that all of the diffraction peaks corresponded to HCP titanium, 
and the TEM image ( Fig. 5 (b)) confirmed that the HCP phase was α ’ 
martensite due to its acicular morphology, which is consistence with the 
research of Gu et al. [22] and Attar et al. [23] . Because of the protection 
of Ar atmosphere during the LPBF process, the XRD spectra of all sam -
ples did not show any other ’ s peaks of rutile or anatase. It can be 
observed that compared with the original powder, the XRD diffraction 
peaks of LPBF-formed samples shift toward lower angles( Fig. 3 a-c). This 
phenomenon originates from the synergistic effects of rapid solidifica -
tion and non-equilibrium phase transformation [24] , resulting in an 
expansion of interplanar spacing [25] . In addition, with the increase of 
energy density, the diffraction peak gradually moves to lower angle, as 
presented in Fig. 3 (b), which indicates the lattice has been distorted. 
Here, the diffraction peaks of original powder distributed at 2 θ = 40.5, 
were taken for a comparison. When the energy density increases, the 
heating effect of laser on the powder material in LPBF process is 
enhanced, resulting in the thermal expansion inside material [26] . This 
thermal expansion induces an increase in lattice volume and interplanar 
spacing [27] . According to the Bragg ’ s law [28] : 2dsin θ = n λ , an increase 
in interplanar spacing d necessitates a corresponding decrease in the 
angle θ . Therefore, the diffraction peak shift to a lower angle.
The XRD spectra of original powder and sample B in scanning plane 
(xy-plane) and building plane (xz = plane) were also compared, as shown 
in Fig. 3 (c). It can be found that the peak intensity of (102) and (110) in 
xy-plane is significantly higher than that of in xz-plane. During the LPBF 
process, variations in heating and cooling rates along the xy-plane and 
xz-plane can lead to the differences in crystal growth rates and orien -
tations [29] . What ’ s more, the residual stress may occur in the alloy due 
to the temperature gradient and rapid cooling. The bottom surface is 
subject to greater constraints, resulting in higher residual stress, and the 
non-uniform distribution of residual stresses inside the material may 
also affect the diffraction behavior of the crystal [30] . This intensified

The surface morphologies of four samples in xz-plane are shown in 
Fig. 4 . The columnar grains growing along the building direction, is 
decorated by a large number of acicular martensite with a width of 
nanometers and a length of less than 200 μ m ( Fig. 4 (a) and (c), (d) and

layer, the laser can penetrate several previously deposited layers during 
a layer-by-layer melting process, which causes the previously deposited 
columnar grain to resolidify and epitaxial along a steep temperature 
gradient in the building direction [37] . With the increase of laser power, 
the width of the columnar crystal generally increases, which mainly 
depends on the width of the molten pool and its heat-affected zone [38, 
39] . When the other parameters are held constant, the width of the 
molten pool and the width of the heat-affected zone are proportional to 
the input laser power [40] . The columnar crystal morphology of the four 
samples can be more clearly seen in the IQ diagrams ( Fig. 4 (b), (e), (h) 
and (k)). The average width of the columnar grains is measured by the 
Image J analysis software, and the width of columnar crystal is 13.1 
± 0.2 μ m for sample A ( Fig. 4 (b)), 15.5 ± 0.2 μ m for sample B ( Fig. 4
(e)), 18.3 ± 0.2 μ m for sample C ( Fig. 4 (h)), and 21.3 ± 0.2 μ m for 
sample D ( Fig. 4 (k)).

The EBSD crystal orientation and inverse pole figures (IPFs) of four 
samples along the building direction are shown in Fig. 6 . The columnar β 
grain growing along the building direction can be clearly seen in the 
longitudinal section [49] , which is consistent with the observation in 
Fig. 4 . The acicular martensite interlace with each other and have a 
variety of grain orientations can be observed in the four samples. The 
majority of grains have reddish color and green color, indicating the 
LPBF-fabricated TA15 alloy shows (0001) and (10 10) orientations. As 
the increase of the energy density, the red areas slightly increase while 
the blue-green regions modestly decrease in the EBSD maps, suggesting 
that the (0001) orientation is strengthened while the (10 10) and (2 1 10) 
orientations are weakened [50] . It is evident that, despite the similarity 
in shape, the presence of α ’ martensite on both sides of the columnar 
grain boundary reveals different orientations, as indicated by their 
distinct colors, signifying that they are two separate α ’ martensite grains
[51] . This indicates that α ’ martensite only nucleates and grows within 
the primary β grain, and does not extend beyond the β grain boundary to 
the adjacent β grain, which also has been confirmed by Wu at al [52] .

dimples are shallower than those of in sample B, indicating the slightly 
reduce of ductility [24] .
In order to study the mechanical properties of high-temperature 
stability of LPBF-fabricated TA15 alloy, sample B was selected for 
thermal tensile tests at different temperature of 200 ℃ , 400 ℃ and 
600 ℃ . The thermal tensile performance and fracture morphologies are 
shown in Fig. 10 and Fig. 11 , respectively. As the tensile temperature 
increased from room temperature to 200 ℃ , the ultimate tensile strength 
slight decreased from 1200 ± 5 MPa to 1175.31 ± 5 MPa, the yield 
strength decreased from 1046 ± 5 MPa to 1020.16 ± 5 MPa while the 
ductility maintained in 11.47 ± 0.5 %. When the tensile temperature 
increased to 400 ℃ , the ultimate tensile strength reduced to 1000.61 
± 5 MPa, the yield strength reduced to 854.52 ± 5 MPa and ductility 
decreased to 10.89 ± 0.5 %. Nevertheless, at the tensile temperature of 
600 ℃ , the ultimate tensile strength, yield strength and ductility still 
remained 938.62 ± 5 MPa, 785.62 ± 5 MPa and 10.8 ± 0.5 %, respec -
tively, which is only 21.7 %, 24.9 % and 0.2 % lower than the room 
temperature tensile specimens. With increasing temperature, the yield 
strength decreases at a slower rate relative to the decrease in tensile 
strength. At 200 ℃ , the yield ratio (the ratio of yield strength to tensile 
strength) is 0.868, while the yield ratio decreased to 0.854 at 400 ℃ . 
Further increased the tensile temperature to 600 ℃ , the yield ratio 
reduced to 0.837.
The strain hardening rates of sample B at various temperature were 
analyzed to further understand the performance degradation mecha -
nism with the increase of temperature. As shown in Fig. 10 (b), the strain 
hardening rates of four samples are almost the same at the elastic 
deformation stage, indicating the yield strength is not significantly 
reduced [24] . However, during the plastic deformation process, as the 
temperature rises, the work hardening rate of the samples decreases 
progressively, suggesting the ultimate tensile strength is decreased with 
the increase of the temperature. In addition, the strain hardening rate at 
room temperature is significantly higher than that at 200 – 600 ℃ , and 
the rate of decline is also considerably gentler compared to 200 – 600 ℃ , 
which is consistence with the result of Fig. 10 (a). Furthermore, a 
diminished work hardening rate in the high temperature facilitates 
localized plastic deformation during the tensile process. Stress concen -
tration in these regions rapidly induces plastic instability, thereby trig -
gering premature necking. The resultant stress within the necked zone 
escalates drastically, driving the specimen to fracture at relatively low 
total strain levels [66] . Therefore, despite elevated temperature typically 
activating additional slip systems [67] , the plastic instability becomes 
the limiting factor that constrains significant ductility enhancement of 
LPBF-fabricated TA15 alloy at the high temperature.
The fracture morphology of sample B after tensile test at different 
temperatures is shown in the Fig. 11 . At all temperature conditions, 
cleavage steps and uniform distribution of dimples were observed on the 
fracture surface, indicating a typical brittle and ductile hybrid fracture
[68] . However, the morphology of dimples changed significantly at 
different temperatures. At lower temperatures, the dimples are smaller 
and deeper ( Fig. 11 (a)-(d)), while at higher temperatures they become 
larger and shallower ( Fig. 11 (e)-(h)).

4.2. The effects of microstructure on the high temperature mechanical 
properties
For the purpose of investigating microstructural evolution during the 
tensile process with different temperatures, the metallographies carried 
out near the fracture position of samples at different temperatures were 
shown in Fig. 16 . After tensile deformation, the primary martensite 
exhibited further elongation along the loading direction, contributing to 
localized strain accommodation. When the tensile temperature 
increased from room temperature to 200 ℃ , the width of acicular α ’ 
martensite gradually increased ( Fig. 16 (b) and (h)) and the average 
width of primary martensite rises from 1.096 ± 0.05 μ m to 1.206 
± 0.05 μ m ( Fig. 16 (j)). The length-to-width ratio decreases progres -
sively, and the grain coarsening results in a reduction of fine grain 
strengthening effects, thus diminishing the ultimate tensile strength 
[84] . Increased the tensile temperature to 400 ℃ , results in an average 
width of 1.365 ± 0.05 μ m ( Fig. 16 (m)), the aspect ratio decreased 
further, and the acicular martensite gradually changed into short 
rod-like, as shown in Fig. 16 (c) and (k). With the increase of temperature 
to 600 ℃ , the average width of primary martensite reaches 1.682 
± 0.05 μ m, representing a 53.5 % increase compared to the value at 
room temperature ( Fig. 16 (p)). Meanwhile, the β grain boundary can be 
more easily observed ( Fig. 16 (d) and (n)), and the sample surface may 
be oxidized. The oxide layer at the grain boundaries is denser and more 
brittle, facilitating easier removal during etching and thus revealing 
distinct grain boundaries. These can be used to explain why the yield 
strength and tensile strength of TA15 alloy decrease with the increase of 
temperature. In addition, owing to the intensification of atomic thermal 
motion at elevated temperatures, dislocation slip and climb become 
more facile, as shown in Fig. 16 (e)-(o), thereby diminishing the defor -
mation resistance of the alloy [85] .

5. Conclusions
In this work, the densification, microstructure evolution, high tem -
perature tensile strength and fatigue behavior of LPBF-fabricated TA15 
alloy have been studied. The major conclusions are as follows: 
(1) The LPBF process parameters have an important effect on the 
internal defects of TA15 alloy samples. As the laser energy den -
sity increases, the relative density initially rises and then de -
creases, peaking at 99.81 % under optimal energy conditions. At 
a low energy density, irregular lack-of-fusion defects are gener -
ated in the sample due to the low temperature and high viscosity 
of the molten pool, while at high energy density, spherical pores 
are formed after the gas solidified due to the convection move -
ment of the molten pool. Both irregular unfused holes and 
spherical pores will reduce the density of the LPBF-fabricated 
sample.
(2) The hierarchical distribution of martensitic structures can be 
observed in the LPBF-fabricated TA15 alloy, comprising primary, 
secondary, tertiary and quaternary variants. These structures 
emerge with increasing energy density, however, the higher en -
ergy densities result in coarsening of the primary and secondary 
martensitic phases.

by the optimized process reach 1200 ± 5 MPa and 1046
