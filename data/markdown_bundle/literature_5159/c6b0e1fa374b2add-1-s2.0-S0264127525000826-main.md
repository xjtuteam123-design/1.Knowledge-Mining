ABSTRACT
The high strength of biomedical titanium alloys is primarily attained through precipitation strengthening of the α 
phase. However, the plasticity and elastic modulus of these alloys are often limited due to their inherent 
strength – toughness tradeoff and the high-modulus α phase. Metastable β -type Ti – Nb alloys were subjected to 
equal-channel angular pressing (ECAP) deformation and aging treatment in this study. The lattice defects and 
high distortion induced by the deformation can provide nucleation sites and drive the precipitation during aging, 
as well as hinder the dissolution of the low-modulus stress-induced martensitic (SIM) α ′ ′ phase introduced during 
the deformation, which results in the formation of fine and dispersed nanosized intermediate phases ( α + ω + α ′ ′ ) 
after short-term aging. As a result, a Ti – Nb alloy with a high strength (~1021 MPa), low elastic modulus (~59 
GPa), and good elongation (~12 %) is obtained. Furthermore, the competition between the ω and α phases is 
observed. The comprehensive mechanical properties of the alloy prepared by ECAP deformation combined with 
aging treatment in this study surpasses those of many reported titanium alloys prepared by traditional defor -
mation or thermal treatment, which provides a valuable reference for the production of advanced biomedical 
titanium alloys.
1. Introduction
Metastable β titanium alloys have significant application potential in 
the biomedical field due to their light weight, good biocompatibility, 
and absence of toxic elements. However, the inherent tradeoff between 
strength and toughness is the main problem of β titanium alloys sub -
jected to the traditional deformation and heat treatment. Numerous 
research studies have been carried out to address this issue. It has been 
reported that achieving a combination of a high strength and plasticity 
should be easier in twinning-induced plasticity/ transformation-induced 
plasticity (TWIP/TRIP) titanium alloys [1] . Alternatively, advanced 
thermomechanical processes should be explored. Recently, a Ti – 11V 
alloy has been reported to form a kink structure after being subjected to 
cold forging followed by the traditional rolling and aging processes; this 
alloy thus exhibited a combination of a high strength and toughness [2] . 
Liu et al. also obtained near- β titanium alloys with a high strength and 
plasticity using a two-stage aging treatment [3] . The 
Ti – 25Nb – 3Zr – 3Mo – 2Sn (Ti – Nb) alloy is a metastable β -type titanium 
alloy with a low elastic modulus designed for biomedical applications. It 
is a TWIP/TRIP titanium alloy designed based on the “ d-electron alloy 
design ” theory. The deformation process of the Ti – Nb alloy involves the 
simultaneous formation of dislocation slip, stress-induced martensitic 
(SIM) α ′ ′ phase, stress-induced ω phase, and mechanical twins, which 
endow it with a good combination of strength and toughness [4 – 7] . The 
traditional deformation and aging processes usually endow the alloy 
with dispersion strengthening through precipitation of the α phase [8] . 
However, the precipitation of the α phase, which has a high elastic 
modulus, also significantly increases the elastic modulus of the alloy [9] . 
Therefore, it is important to further investigate how a high strength, low 
elastic modulus, and good plasticity can be simultaneously obtained in 
biomedical Ti – Nb alloys.
The properties of β titanium alloys are affected by the type, 
morphology, size, and distribution of the precipitated phases. By opti -
mizing the thermomechanical treatment process to control the phase 
transition and microstructure, the properties of the alloy can be effec -
tively tailored. In general, the ω

low elastic modulus. Hsu et al. prepared a Ti – 13Nb – 7Sn – 4Mo alloy with

a combination of a high strength and a low elastic modulus [23] . The α ′ ′ 
phase is a non-equilibrium phase, which decomposes when heated. The 
decomposition modes include α ′ ′ → β and α ′ ′ → α , and the phase transi -
tion kinetics is related to the temperature and heating rate [24 – 26] . It 
has been reported that crystal defects, such as dislocations, grain 
boundaries, and phase boundaries, can suppress the decomposition of 
the martensitic α ′ ′ phase and make the reverse martensitic transition 
incomplete [27,28] . Therefore, by accurately optimizing the volume 
fraction and morphology of several phases ( ω , α , and α ′ ′ phases) to 
harness potential advantages from their interactions, the properties of 
the alloy can be effectively tailored.
In this work, the phase composition, microstructure, and mechanical 
properties of the Ti – Nb alloy after room-temperature ECAP deformation 
and aging treatment were studied. The deformation mechanism, pre -
cipitate behavior, and strengthening/toughening mechanism of the 
Ti – Nb alloy were analyzed. Furthermore, the effects of the ECAP 
deformation (grain size, twins, and SIM α ′ ′ phase) on the aging behavior 
of the alloy are discussed. This study is important for understanding the 
deformation behavior and phase transformation of β titanium alloys so 
as to promote the development of advanced biomedical titanium alloys.

Through careful observation, it can be found that the tensile 
stress – strain curves of the ST sample and ECAP-deformed samples show 
a clear “ double-yield ” phenomenon, which is not shown for the aging- 
treated samples. This double-yield phenomenon is due to the genera -
tion of the stress-induced martensite during the tensile process. The 
stress corresponding to the first yield point is the critical stress at which 
martensite forms. Due to the generation of stress-induced martensite, 
stress is released, and a short plateau appears in the curve. The second 
yield point is the true yield stress of the alloy. The critical stress values at 
which martensite forms for the ST, 1P, and 4P samples are 179, 267, and 
431 MPa, respectively. For alloys with the same composition and sub -
jected to the same deformation conditions, such as temperature and 
strain rate, the critical stress at which martensite forms is dominated by 
the grain size. With increasing number of ECAP deformation passes, the 
grain size decreases, and the critical stress at which martensite forms 
increases gradually. However, due to the appearance of precipitated 
phases in the aged samples, this critical stress value increases [31] . 
When the critical stress at which martensite forms is higher than the 
yield stress of the alloy, the tensile stress – strain curve does not show the 
double-yield phenomenon.

3.2. Phase transition
Fig. 3 a shows the XRD patterns of the ST sample and the ECAP- 
deformed samples. The ST sample is mainly composed of the β phase 
and a small amount of the quenched martensitic hcp- α ’ (002) phase. 
Clear peaks corresponding to the orthorhombic- α ′ ′ (002), α ′ ′ (021), and 
hcp- ω (112) phases appear in the 1P sample, indicating that a transition 
from the SIM α ′ ′ phase and the stress-induced ω phase occurs during one 
pass of ECAP deformation, and the ω -phase content is low. After four 
passes of ECAP deformation, the stress-induced ω phase disappears. The 
volume fraction of the α ′ phase in the ST sample is about 9%, and the 
volume fraction of the SIM α ′ ′ phase in the 1P and 4P samples is about 
37% and 32%, respectively.
Fig. 3 b shows the XRD diffraction patterns of the ST sample and the 
ECAP-deformed samples after different aging treatments. After 60 min 
of aging treatment, small amounts of ω and α phases precipitate in the ST 
sample. After 240 min, the ω phase disappears, and a significant content 
of α phase. The ECAP deformation can drastically reduce the time it 
takes for the secondary phases to precipitate. After 10 min of aging 
treatment, the ω phase formations in the 1P sample. After 60 min of 
aging treatment, the ω phase disappears, and the α phase precipitates. 
The α phase precipitates in the 4P sample after 30 min of aging 
treatment.
By comparing the intensities of the XRD diffraction peaks of the 1P 
sample and the corresponding samples aged for 10 – 60 min, it can be 
seen that a large amount of α ′ ′ phases induced by the ECAP deformation 
decomposes rapidly during the aging process and transform into the β 
(110) phase. With the increase in aging time, the intensity of the β (110) 
diffraction peak decreases, and a small amount of the β (110) phase 
transforms into the ω phase; then, the ω phase disappears, and the α 
phase precipitates. Therefore, the phase transformation sequence of the 
Ti – Nb alloy involves α ′ ′ → β and β → ω → α . Due to the limited accuracy 
of the XRD measurements, it was not possible to investigate the prepared 
samples when the phase content was extremely low or the size of pre -
cipitates was extremely small, and more accurate microstructural and 
phase composition analyses were conducted using EBSD and TEM.

3.3.2. Microstructural evolution after aging treatment
Fig. 6 shows the TEM microstructure of the 1P sample after 10 min of 
aging treatment. It can be seen that a large number of fine needle-like α 
phases particles have precipitated in the deformation twins, and the α 
phases are distributed in two intersecting directions, while almost no α 
phase has precipitated in the β matrix ( Figs. 6 a and 6c). A large number 
of ellipsoidal ω phases particles have precipitated in the matrix, with 
their average size being about 20 nm, while the ω phases particles might 
not satisfy the diffraction condition in the twins ( Figs. 6 g and 6h).

morphology of the ω phase particles changes from ellipsoidal to short 
rods due to the rapid consumption of ω by α phase [36] . Therefore, 
deformation twinning is beneficial to the precipitation of the α phase. 
The precipitation of a large number of α phases in the twins inhibits the 
formation of the ω phase, while the formation of a large number of ω 
phases in the matrix inhibits the generation of the α phase. This 
competition between the α and ω phases has also been previously found 
in different alloys [37 – 41] . Through careful observation, it can be found

In summary, the precipitation of the Ti – Nb alloy during aging 
treatment involves the phase transformations α ′ ′ → β and β → ω → α . 
ECAP deformation is beneficial to accelerate the precipitation of the α 
phase as well as the ω → α transformation during aging. Non-equilibrium 
structures, such as twins and the SIM α ′ ′ phase, provide nucleation sites 
for phase precipitation. And moreover, the distortions induced by the 
deformation act as a driving force for the nucleation and growth of the 
precipitated phase. During the aging process of the ST sample, the coarse 
α phase preferentially precipitates along the grain boundaries. However, 
in the ECAP-deformed samples, a large number of twins exist in a 
complex network structure, which makes the distribution of the 
precipitated phases more dispersed and their size smaller.
4. Discussion
4.1. Evolution of the deformation mechanisms
The molybdenum equivalent ( Mo
eq
) of the Ti – Nb alloy is 10.19, and 
the bond order ( Bo ) and metal d-orbital energy level ( Md ) are 2.846 and 
2.442, respectively, which is on the Ms = RT (room temperature) line of 
the d-electron alloy design diagram. This indicates that the Ti – Nb alloy 
is a TWIP/TRIP titanium alloy, and the deformation process simulta -
neously induces phase transformations and the formation of mechanical 
twins [5] . The deformation mechanism of the alloy is affected by many 
factors, such as the β -phase stability, content of interstitial O, tempera -
ture, strain rate, loading method, grain size, and texture [44,45] . In this 
study, it is found that for a given deformation condition, the evolution of 
the deformation mechanism mainly depends on the β -phase stability and 
the grain size.

mode evolves as follows: SIM α ′ ′ phase → {332} < 113 > twins → {112} 
< 111 > twins → dislocation slip [46] . Below, the evolution of these se -
quences of transformations is analyzed: (1) As the critical stress for 
triggering the SIM α ′ ′ phase is lower than the yield stress of the alloy, the 
SIM α ′ ′ phase is generated first during the deformation process. More -
over, the lower the content of the β -stabilizing element Nb, the lower the 
β -phase stability, the higher the corresponding Ms temperature, and the 
lower the stress required for the SIM α ′ ′ phase formation. Therefore, the 
SIM α ′ ′ phase is generated in regions with a low Nb content [47] . (2) As 
the SIM α ′ ′ phase is formed, {332} < 113 > twins are created due to SIM 
α ′ ′ -assisted {332} < 113 > twinning, which is typical of metastable β ti -
tanium alloys. The SIM α ′ ′ phase is formed during the loading process, 
and {130} < 310 > α ′ ′ twins are formed in the α ′ ′ phase. During the 
unloading process, the reverse transition α ′ ′ → β occurs, resulting in the 
formation of {332} < 113 > twins [48] . (3) Due to the unstable lattice 
structure of the O ′ phase in metastable β titanium alloys, the {332} 
< 113 > twins form easily due to the presence of fewer atomic shear 
variables, while the {112} 〈 1 1 1 〉 twins require additional recombina -
tion; this results in the {332} < 113 > twins being easily formed in β 
phases with a low stability and the {112} 〈 1 1 1 〉 twins being easily 
generated in β phases with a high stability [49] . The Nb content in the 
regions without the SIM α ′ ′ phase is relatively high, which is equivalent 
to a high β -phase stability. Therefore, with the increase in strain, the 
formation of the {332} < 113 > twins is inhibited, while the {112} 〈 1 1 1 〉
twins are favored. In other words, with the increase in strain during 
ECAP deformation of the Ti – Nb alloy, there are more {112} 〈 1 1 1 〉 twins 
formations ( Fig. 4 ).
During the ECAP deformation of the Ti – Nb alloy, with the increase in 
the number of deformation passes, the grain size decreases, and twin -
ning becomes increasingly less likely to occur [50] . The critical stress for 
the precipitation of martensite also increases, and a high strain induces 
the reverse transformation α ′ ′ → β [51,52] . Therefore, the volume frac -
tions of twins and the SIM α ′ ′ phase gradually decrease after reaching a 
peak. The 2P sample exhibits the maximum volume fraction of the SIM 
α ′ ′ phase (~51%), and the 3P sample exhibits the maximum volume 
fraction of twins (~57%). Moreover, with increasing strain, the stress- 
induced ω phase is decomposed due to the dislocation slip on the 
{112} slip plane [53] . In summary, the early-stage deformation mech -
anism of the Ti – Nb alloy during room-temperature ECAP deformation 
involves dislocation slip, {332} < 113 > twins, {112} < 111 > twins, SIM 
α ′ ′ phase, and stress-induced ω -phase. With the increase in strain, 
dislocation slip becomes the predominant deformation mechanism of 
the alloy.
4.2. Effect of ECAP deformation on the aging behavior
The phase transformation sequence of the Ti – Nb alloy during aging is 
β → ω → α . The α -phase precipitates and the β matrix obey the Burgers 
orientation relationships (BORs): [1 – 11] β // [11 – 20] α and (110) β // 
(0002) α [54] . In addition, the β and α phases may satisfy the 
Pitsch – Schrader orientation relationship and the Potter orientation 
relationship [55,56] . According to the symmetry of the body-centered 
cubic (BCC) lattice structure, 12 types of α -phase variants satisfying 
the BOR can coexist in a single β grain. These 12 variants do not appear 
with equal probability, and the type of variant that is generated depends 
on the heat treatment process, applied stress, presence of dislocations, 
grain boundary misorientation of β / β , and deformation. For example, 
dislocations with {112} slip systems are more inclined to induce the 
precipitation of α -phase variants with specific orientations [57] . The ω 
phase is an intermediate metastable phase that is formed during the β → 
α transition, which takes place in two stages: β → β + ω and β + ω → β +
α . In general, during the aging process of the ST sample, the ellipsoidal ω 
phase is formed first in the β matrix. As time increases, the ω phase 
decomposes, and multiple oriented α phases precipitate along the grain 
boundaries. The α phase continues to grow inward until it covers the β 
matrix (as shown in Fig. 1 e). However, the lattice defects produced by 
deformation affect the morphology, variant type, distribution, and size 
of the precipitated phase.
Fig. 9 shows a schematic of the microstructural evolution of the 
Ti – Nb alloy during room-temperature ECAP deformation and subse -
quent aging treatment. Fig. 10 shows the morphology and size of the 
precipitated phase of the 1P sample after 10 min of aging; it can be 
observed that both the α and ω phases are nanoscale phases. After ECAP 
deformation, a large number of lattice defects, such as dislocation slip, 
SIM α ′ ′ phase, and twins, are formed, which divide the microstructure 
into a complex network and provide a large number of nucleation sites 
for the precipitates during the aging process. And moreover, the high 
distortion inside the deformed alloy can provide a driving force for the 
nucleation and growth of the precipitated phase. Therefore, the effect of 
the deformation degree on the precipitated phase in the aged alloy is 
very important.
Indeed, different microstructures are formed after the aging treat -
ment due to the different deformation degrees of the 1P and 4P samples. 
The microstructure differences between these samples mainly consist of 
the grain size, volume fraction of the SIM α ′ ′ phase, type and volume 
fraction of twins, and the presence or lack of the stress-induced ω phase. 
Below, each of these factors is analyzed: 
(1) Grain size. The average grain sizes of the 1P and 4P samples are 
about 43 and 7 μ m, respectively. The smaller the grain size, the 
higher the number of interfaces in the structure, and the greater 
the number of nucleation points for the precipitated phase.
(2) SIM α ″ phase. The fast α ′ ′ → β transition is a non-diffusion phase 
transition controlled by the lattice strain between the two phases

elastic modulus of the alloy. Therefore, the incomplete decom -

(3) Deformation twins. During the ECAP deformation, twins intersect 
and refine, and secondary twins are formed inside the primary 
twins, which is also a characteristic of {332} < 113 > twins [62] . 
Moreover, the thermal stability of twins is high, and no decom -
position occurs during short-term aging. Therefore, a large 
number of defects are formed in the twin region, and stress is 
concentrated there, which leads to the precipitation of the 
nanoscale ω phase in the twins and its rapid transformation into 
the α phase. After 10 min of aging treatment for the 1P sample, 
the ω phase inside the twins shows a short rod-like morphology 
due to its partial transformation into the α phase, while the ω 
phase in the matrix, which has not yet started to transform, shows 
an ellipsoidal morphology. This fine and dispersed precipitate 
network is beneficial to the strength and plasticity of the alloy.
(4) Stress-induced ω phase. The stress-induced ω phase exists in the 
1P sample, while the ω phase in the 4P sample decomposes with 
increasing strain. However, it has been reported that the stress- 
induced ω phase is almost the same as the athermal ω phase 
and does not play a role in the nucleation of the α phase [63] .
In summary, ECAP deformation accelerates the precipitation of the 
Ti – Nb alloy during aging and gives rise to the formation of finer and 
more dispersed precipitates in the region of the lattice with a high 
density of defects, which is consistent with previous reports [18] . Thus, 
a suitable deformation combined with aging can be utilized to effec -
tively optimize the alloy and endow it with excellent comprehensive 
performance.
4.3. Effect of ECAP deformation and aging treatment on the strength and

4.3.1. Effect of ECAP deformation and aging treatment on the strength

is mainly attributed to fine-grain strengthening and dispersion 
strengthening. With the increase in the number of deformation passes, 
the grains become smaller, which gives rise to Hall – Petch strengthening.

twins induced by the deformation cause dispersion strengthening. At the 
same time, the deformation process induces the phase transition and the 
formation of mechanical twins, which result in the TWIP/TRIP effect 
and change the lattice orientation, so that more dislocation slips are 
activated, leading to a good plasticity.

5. Conclusions
In this study, the nanoscale α + ω + α ′ ′ structure was induced in the β 
matrix by ECAP deformation and subsequent high-temperature short- 
term aging treatment, thus a biomedical Ti – Nb alloy with high strength, 
low elastic modulus, and good plasticity was prepared. The phase 
transition, microstructure, and mechanical properties of the Ti – Nb alloy 
were systematically studied during room-temperature ECAP deforma -
tion and the subsequent aging treatment. The conclusions can be sum -
marized as follows: 
1) With increasing strain, the grain size decreases. The room- 
temperature ECAP deformation mechanism of the Ti – Nb alloy 
gradually evolves from a combination of dislocation slip, {332} 
< 113 > twins, {112} < 111 > twins, SIM α ′ ′ phase, and stress-induced 
ω phase to dislocation slip. The strengthening mechanism includes 
the Hall – Petch strengthening and dispersion strengthening caused by 
nanoscale secondary twins and the SIM α ′ ′

elastic modulus of the alloy.
2) The precipitation sequence of the Ti – Nb alloy consists of two tran -
sitions, namely α ′ ′ → β and β → ω → α . The twins and SIM α ′ ′ phase 
induced by ECAP deformation divide the microstructure into a 
complex network. These lattice defects provide sites for the nucle -
ation of the precipitated phases, and the high distortion energy is a 
driving force for precipitation. Therefore, after deformation and 
aging treatment, finely dispersed nanoscale α and ω precipitated 
phases are formed.
3) The 1P sample after 10 min of aging exhibits a high ultimate tensile 
strength (~1012 MPa), low elastic modulus (~59 GPa), and good 
plasticity (~12%). The improvement of strength after aging is 
attributed to the precipitation of the nanoscale α and ω phases as well
