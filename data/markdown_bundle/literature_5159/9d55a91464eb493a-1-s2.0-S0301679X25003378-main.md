1. Introduction
The advent of high-entropy alloys (HEAs), distinguished by their 
high configurational entropy, has profoundly broadened the composi -
tional and structural landscape of metallic materials [1] . As the number 
of primary constituent elements surpasses three, these alloys predomi -
nantly yield chemically intricate and distorted solid solutions rather 
than intermetallic compounds [2] . Thus, they are frequently designated 
as multi-component alloys or chemically complex alloys (CCAs). 
Notably, modulating the intrinsic or additional heterogeneous structures 
within CCA systems has been shown to address the inherent limitations 
of traditional metallic materials, particularly the strength-ductility 
trade-off and the degradation of wear resistance at elevated tempera -
tures [3 – 6] . To date, various types of heterogeneous structures have 
been developed in CCAs at multiple scales, including: (i) localized 
atomic-level chemical ordering [7] ; (ii) indistinguishable nanoscale 
clusters or complexes embedded in the matrix [4,8] ; (iii) precipitates, 
lamellar eutectic phases, and martensite induced by compositional 
segregation [9 – 11] ; (iv) an array of lattice defects such as twins, 
stacking faults, and grain boundaries [12,13] ; and (v) graded grain-size 
distributions ranging from nanometers to micrometers [14,15] . Among 
these, heterogeneous precipitation emerges as a particularly effective 
strengthening mechanism, fostering compositional fluctuations while 
establishing secondary phase interfaces within the CCAs. These pre -
cipitates not only govern dislocation nucleation and propagation during 
deformation but also influence tribochemical reactions during wear.

2. Experimental procedures
2.1. Material preparation
The bulk TV and MW samples synthesized from high-purity 
elemental precursors: Cr (99.6 %, ~300 mesh), Fe (99.6 %, ~300 
mesh), Ni (99.6 %, ~300 mesh), Nb (99.5 %, ~400 mesh), Ti (99.6 %, 
~400 mesh), V (99.5 %, ~400 mesh), Mo (99.5 %, ~400 mesh), W 
(99.5 %, ~400 mesh), and B (99.7 %, 2 – 5 μ m). The elemental powders, 
mixed in predetermined atomic percentages, were combined with 
cemented carbide balls in a high-energy ball milling device (FRITSCH, 
Germany) at a ball-to-powder weight ratio of 4:1. Intermittent dry 
milling was conducted for 38 hours at 300 r/min, followed by wet 
milling for 5 hours at 200 r/min using anhydrous ethanol as a process 
control agent. The 45-hour milled powders were then consolidated into 
bulk form using SPS (LABOX-3010KF, Japan) at 1100 ± 15 ℃ for 10 min 
under a constant pressure of 35 MPa. The average heating rate was 65 
℃ /min, and the vacuum pressure was maintained below 5 Pa. The as- 
sintered samples ( Ø 50 mm × 8 mm) were polished to remove a 
~1 mm surface contamination layer, and then machined into bulk 
samples of various sizes for testing.

2.3. Microstructural and compositional characterizations
Constituent phases and lattice parameters in the ball-milled powders 
and bulk CCAs were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD, EMPY -
REAN, Netherlands) with CuK α radiation. The microstructural features 
and phase compositions of the bulk CCAs were examined with scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS-Sigma 300), equipped with a back -
scatter electron (BSE) detector and energy dispersive spectrometry 
(EDS). The volume fraction of each phase was estimated using ImageJ 
software. Grain orientations and sizes of the two HEAs were character -
ized by electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD, Nordly-Max3) with 
Oxford-Channel5 collection software. Plastic strain distribution between 
grains was evaluated using kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps, 
where the KAM value at each point indicates its deviation from the 
average orientation of the entire grain. Wear mechanisms on the sliding 
surfaces between tribo-pairs were analyzed via SEM (Quanta-450FEG) 
and EDS, while the cross sections of the worn track were also charac -
terized by SEM. The tribochemical reactions of worn surfaces were 
examined using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 
250XI).
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Heterogeneous precipitates in the TV and MW CCA
Fig. 1 presents the XRD patterns of the as-blended powders, MA 
powders, and bulk samples. Initially, distinct peaks corresponding to all 
elements are observed. After 43 hours of ball milling, peak intensities 
decrease significantly, with some alloying element peaks (Ti, V, and Mo) 
disappearing, suggesting extensive atomic interdiffusion and the for -

driven by severe plastic deformation and enhanced atomic diffusivity, 
which also contribute to local lattice distortion, as evidenced by peak 
broadening [23] . In the TV alloyed powder mixture, Cr, Fe, and Ni peaks 
remain prominent, while those of Nb, Ti, and V are less pronounced.

weaker peaks than Cr, Fe, and Ni, indicating preferential dissolution of 
certain elements into the matrix. Following SPS at 1100

The "selective solid solution" effect among alloying elements plays a 
crucial role in governing recrystallization behavior during sintering. The 
severe plastic deformation imposed during ball milling disrupts equi -
librium, creating a supersaturated nanostructured solid solution with 
high defect densities [29,32] . Upon SPS, Joule heating facilitates defect 
motion and enhances atomic diffusivity, reducing activation barriers for 
solute redistribution [22,25] . Consequently, phase transformations and 
densification occur via diffusion-driven recombination of constituent 
elements. The extent of recrystallization in both CCAs is further influ -
enced by sintering parameters such as holding time and heating rate. 
The rapid cooling inherent to SPS, combined with the sluggish diffusion 
kinetics of CCAs, suppresses grain coarsening in recrystallized regions 
while preserving coarse-grained non-recrystallized regions. Ultimately, 
the intrinsic chemical compositions of the TV and MW CCAs dictate their 
phase evolution, multicomponent co-diffusion behavior, and resultant 
microstructural heterogeneity, setting the foundation for their distinct 
mechanical and tribological properties.
3.3. Mechanical properties and strengthening mechanisms
Fig. 7 a displays the engineering (solid lines) and true (dotted lines) 
compressive stress-strain curves for TV and MW CCAs. The stress-strain 
response reveals that TV CCA exhibits a yield strength of 1.4 GPa, an 
ultimate compressive strength of 1.9 GPa, and a fracture strain of 
20.7 %, whereas MW CCA demonstrates higher yield and ultimate 
compressive strengths of 2.0 GPa and 2.3 GPa, albeit with a lower 
fracture strain of 12.1 %. True stress-strain data indicate that TV CCA 
retains a higher work-hardening capacity than MW CCA. Despite these 
differences, both CCAs exhibit remarkable plastic deformability at high 
stress levels exceeding 1.5 GPa. The temperature-dependent hardness 
evolution ( Fig. 7 b) further supports these findings, with TV and MW 
CCAs showing a gradual decrease in Vickers hardness from 4.7 GPa and 
6.1 GPa at room temperature to 3.5 GPa and 4.1 GPa at 800

tively. The close correlation between hardness and compressive strength 
underscores the intrinsic strengthening mechanisms governing the me -

teractions, which also dictate the yield strength and strain-hardening 
behavior under compression. The higher hardness of MW CCA at all 
tested temperatures aligns with its superior yield strength, indicating a

variations in alloying composition and microstructural features, which 
modulate the interactions between dislocations and strengthening

governed by the dynamic evolution of the tribolayer, resulting from the

spalling of this oxide layer compromises its ability to resist oxidative and 
adhesive wear, accelerating wear-induced degradation [33,41] . At 
800

promoting glaze layer formation that reduces material transfer and 
oxidation [5,43] . However, the development of a stable glaze layer is a 
dynamic process involving oxidized product diffusion, oxide layer 
delamination, and material accumulation. These thermomechanical in -
teractions necessitate additional frictional energy dissipation, leading to 
rougher sliding surfaces and increased COF [13] .
The wear resistance of both CCAs at 800

cracks and pits. The high-density dual precipitates in MW CCA enhance 
its hot hardness, improving the load-bearing capacity of the worn sub -
surface under high-temperature sliding conditions. Additionally, het -
erogeneous precipitates act as pinning agents for grain boundaries, 
suppressing grain coarsening induced by boundary sliding and rotation 
[13,20] . The stabilization of the subsurface layer is crucial for main -
taining the integrity and durability of the glaze layer.
