Abstract Kanthal APMT/C210is an FeCrAlMo alloy optimized for continuous service
up to 1,250 /C176C( *2,300 /C176F). Rapid solidiﬁcation powder metallurgy applied on this
FeCrAlMo composition provided an oxide dispersion strengthened microstructure.
The alloy exhibits an attractive combination of resistance to oxidation and corrosion
and excellent form stability. In this study, oxidation and corrosion properties were
investigated, as well as mechanical properties at elevated temperature. It was shown
that an adherent alumina layer on the alloy surface formed during service that
provided excellent resistance to corrosion attacks in most industrial atmospheres
and gave great advantages compared to chromia forming high temperature Ni-base
alloys in terms of maximum operating temperature and life. Focus was set on
oxidation and creep properties but also other important aspects are discussed.

Introduction and Background
This summary paper describes the oxidation, creep and some basic characteristics of
the alloy Kanthal APMT/C210, designated APMT in the following. APMT is an alumina
forming, powder metallurgical, dispersion strengthened FeCrAlMo alloy. The alloy
is optimized for service as a construction material in oxidizing environments up to
as high as 1,250 /C176C( *2,300 /C176F) in continuous operation.
The two key main criteria to be satisﬁed for any material to be used in
constructions working at very high temperatures is that it has sufﬁcient oxidation
and corrosion resistance to withstand the environment and it has the mechanical
strength at the working temperature needed for the component to maintain its
function for a required service time. In this paper, focus is therefore set on these two
basic aspects of material properties.
Traditionally wrought or cast Ni-base alloys are used for high temperature
construction, but the limited oxidation resistance above 1,000–1,100 /C176C restricts the
service temperature in oxidizing environments. Conventional wrought FeCrAl high
temperature alloys are on the other hand well known for their superior oxidation
resistance up to 1,300 or even 1,400 /C176C. This outstanding resistance to oxidation is
based on their ability to form a very slow growing and protective alumina scale
during service. However, the relatively low mechanical strength of these ferritic
alloys at elevated temperatures strongly limits their application in mechanically
stressed components.
Dispersion strengthening offer a potential means to raise high temperature
strength. This has been realized by introducing powder metallurgy in the primary
process. Historically, mechanical alloying (MA) has successfully been applied to
introduce a distribution of small refractory inclusions. This production method
indeed results in excellent high temperature strength but it suffers the drawback of
very high production cost and sometime less predictable application results.
Mechanically alloyed FeCrAl alloys were produced in large scale for several years,
but commercial production ceased in 2007.
An alternative and more effective production route, rapid solidiﬁcation powder
metallurgy (RSP) was therefore developed to create a distribution of small
refractory inclusions. Kanthal APM
TM was introduced 1989 and was the ﬁrst
powder metallurgical FeCrAl using this technology. The alloy is mainly used for
resistance heating in high temperature industrial and semiconductor electric
furnaces up to as much as 1,400 /C176C material temperature and also for gas or
electrically heated radiant tubes with material temperatures up to about 1,250 /C176C.
APMT was introduced in 2004 as the next step in terms of further improved hot
strength and retained alumina protection up to 1,300 /C176C[ 1]. The APMT alloy may
be characterized as an oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) RSP alloy although the
inclusions are of several types. Not as creep resistant as a mechanically alloyed
material, APMT nevertheless combined the excellent oxidation resistance of
wrought FeCrAl alloys with a sufﬁciently high creep resistance to allow for design
of moderately loaded components up to the maximum recommended continuous
operation temperature 1,250 /C176C( *2,300 /C176F). This is a temperature range where

Experimental Procedures
Samples about 15 9 10 9 1.5 mm were used for all oxidation tests. All sample
surfaces were mechanically polished and then thoroughly cleaned. An STA 409
instrument and synthetic air composed of 80 % N
2 and 20 % O 2 with a constant
ﬂow was used for TG experiments.
High quality alumina crucibles were used for the 20 h-cyclic oxidation in order
to contain the samples and the spalled oxide. All crucibles were covered
immediately outside the furnace to avoid loss of spalled oxide. The atmosphere
was ambient laboratory air which was normally relatively dry during the test period.
Weighing was done ﬁrst for the gross mass, the crucible including sample and
spalled oxide. The crucible was then weighed after the oxidized sample was gently
taken out from the crucible. The accumulated gross gain, spalled oxide and net
sample mass gain could all be calculated based on these weighing data.
Fractured and polished sections of the oxide layer were studied with SEM
equipped with EDX and light optical microscopy. Creep test were done on 5 mm Ø
5C25 creep samples machined from tube walls (Table 1; Fig. 2).

certain critical load the failures are typically determined by the oxidation process. In

APMT (as well as many FeCrAl type alloys) has fortunately a quite well behaved
way of degrading by time from oxidation—a protective alumina skin that grows
slowly at the surface and Al in the alloy diffusing sufﬁciently fast to the surface
where it combines with oxygen and gets incorporated into the alumina scale. The
scale thickens with time, theoretically as a function of square root of the exposure
time. In reality the growth may be lower or higher than parabolic due to material
changes with time and the impact of thermal cycling.
During exposure, thermal cycling and also mechanical damage often cause micro
cracks or even macro defects in the oxide scale. Oxidation limited lifetime is
therefore reached when the aluminium level beneath the oxide layer is too low to
support the continued alumina formation in those defects. Lifetime prediction would
be substantially easier if this critical Al level was a universal or at least an alloy
speciﬁc constant. In contrast, it is found that the critical Al level is a function of
several parameters, such as exposure temperature, amount of cycling and sample
dimensions and topography to name a few. For sections larger than a few tenths of a
mm this level is often found to be in a range from 1 to 3 wt% Al remaining in the
alloy which means that typically 2–4 wt% of Al is available to form an oxide scale
before oxidation failure occur. An recent summary of the theory for life time
prediction is given by Dryepondt et al. [ 2]. To predict lifetime with simultaneous
creep and oxidation is a quite complex matter. An attempt to display the two
phenomena simultaneously was done by Jo¨ nsson et al. [ 1].

Due to the extremely dense alumina scale formed and the low permeability of
carbon containing species, the carburization properties of APMT are excellent in
comparison to chromia forming materials [ 6]. Further, APMT is almost entirely
insensitive to metal dusting, a catastrophic selective oxidation most active in a
temperature range between 500 and 900 /C176C at low oxygen partial pressure in
combination with high carbon activity and is also very stable towards sulphur attack
compared to Ni base alloys. The protective alumina scale that forms at very low
levels of oxygen potential is part of the reason for the good behavior. Another factor
is that low melting NiS compounds that may form on Ni containing alloys for

High Temperature Creep
In general, the creep rupture strength for APMT is at similar levels as high
temperature Ni base alloys above 900 /C176C, see Fig. 8a. Worth to note is that well
deﬁned rupture and creep rate data exists for APMT in the temperature range
1,100–1,300 /C176C (2,000–2,400 /C176F) which is a range where Ni-base alloys suffer from
severe oxidation and at the higher temperatures also loss of structural integrity due
to initiating grain boundary sliding (Figs. 8, 9).

room temperature is reduced to &12 % after exposure at 1,200 /C176C for 168 h.
Design Factors
The APMT alloy is readily welded with TIG/GTAW which is by far the most
commonly used joining method today. Generally, using APMT as a ﬁller material
gives the best result. Preheating to approximately 250 /C176C and post-weld anneal to
850 /C176C is necessary. The ﬁne distribution of refractory inclusions is disrupted in the
weld zone and the consequence is loss of creep strength. In applications the loss of
strength is handled by design so that welds are placed at positions where the stress
level or the temperature are below their peak levels. Another possibility is to
increase the section thickness at the position of the weld.
In addition to TIG welding there are several welding techniques that can be used
such as laser or MIG welding, that might have advantages in certain situations.
Brazing has been shown to give good results when it is possible to prepare the gap to
adequate tolerance and to perform a heat cycle in vacuum.

Very good welds with maintained creep strength have been demonstrated by
using friction stir welding [ 7]. The material is joined by mechanical stirring using a
special tool that provides heat and plasticizing by friction heat. The alloy is below
the melting point during the process which is a great advantage especially with ODS
materials since the distribution of inclusions is fully maintained. No ﬁller is used
which is an additional advantage from phase integrity point of view.
Contributing to the excellent form stability observed for APMT [1] is the fact that
the density of APMT is 7.25 vs. 8 g cm
-3 of a Fe35 Ni25Cr that gives a lower stress
in APMT in such high temperature applications like radiant tubes or furnace rollers
where the own weight plays a role.
Also important is a smaller thermal expansion coefﬁcient of the ferritic APMT
compared to commonly used austenitic Ni-base alloys. In general, a smaller
expansion is desirable since designing for thermal dimension changes becomes
increasingly difﬁcult with larger movements. When two different alloys need to be
mounted or even welded together the differential is also a factor to consider.
As an example, the thermal expansion from room temperature to 1,000 /C176C
(1,832 /C176F) is approximately 1.47 % for APMT whereas the corresponding value is
1.77 % for alloy 601 which is a difference in favor of APMT of approximately
0.3 %. This difference corresponds to 3 mm less movement per m of component
length (Fig. 10).
From the magnitude of these thermal movements, that easily exceeds 0.1 % even
for moderately small temperature changes, it is clear that allowance for these
movements is crucial in order to not exceed the plasticity limit of the material.
Experience has in fact shown that inability to design for these thermal movements is
a common reason for premature failures. The material phenomenon involved is low
cycle thermal fatigue and gives rise to bending, buckling and eventually cracking.
Quantitative data has been presented by Andersson et al. [ 8].

Conclusions
APMT is an advanced powder metallurgical FeCrAlMo alloy optimized for service
as a construction material in the temperature range up to 1,250 /C176C( *2,300 /C176F). It
combines excellent resistance to oxidation and hot corrosion with high creep
resistance and excellent form stability.
The formation of an adherent alumina layer on the alloy surface during service
provides an excellent resistance to corrosion attacks in most industrial atmospheres
and gives great advantages compared to conventional chromia forming high
temperature materials in terms of maximum operating temperature and life.
By using rapid solidiﬁcation powder metallurgy, an ODS microstructure is
achieved. The result is an increased mechanical strength up to a level that allows for
design of mechanically loaded components up to the maximum operation
temperature. APMT therefore offers unique design opportunities for exposed
components in a temperature range where Ni-base alloys degrade rapidly due to
accelerated oxidation and even grain boundary softening.
