ABSTRACT
The single-phase face-centered-cubic Al 7Co24Cr21Fe24Ni24 (in atomic percent, at%) high entropy alloy lacks
suﬃcient hardness and strength at room temperature, which restricts its structural application in the future. To
strengthen this material, the (Al 7Co24Cr21Fe24Ni24)100-xCrx (x = 0, 11, 20, and 26) high entropy alloys were
prepared and investigated. We found that with increasing the Cr content, the volume fraction of the BCC phase
enriched with Al, Fe, and Cr elements increased gradually. In the BCC-contained HEAs, ordered B2 particles were
always dispersed in the BCC solid solution, which resulted in a dramatic enhancement of comprehensive me-
chanical properties. Nanoindentation measurements showed that both the nanohardness and the elastic modulus
values of the BCC phase are much greater than that of the FCC phase. The room-temperature compression test
showed that the yield strength signi ﬁcantly increased from 249 MPa to 1649 MPa with the slight sacri ﬁce of the
ductility, along with increasing the Cr content. More importantly, the (Al
7Co24Cr21Fe24Ni24)74Cr26 alloy exhibits
outstanding compression behavior, with the high yield strength (1649 MPa) and fracture strength (2830 MPa) as
well as good compressive plastic strain of 24.9%. Furthermore, to facilitate the optimization of the compre-
hensive properties of alloys, the close correlation between the microstructures and mechanical properties was
also discussed.
1. Introduction
In recent years, a new class of multi-component alloys termed as
high-entropy alloys (HEAs) were proposed by Yeh et al. [1], which
contain ﬁve or more elements with equi or near-equi elemental frac-
tions, each of the elements ranging from 5 to 35 at. percentage (at%).
As an outcome of a novel alloy-designed concept, the HEAs have at-
tracted much attention and greatly broadened the alloy ﬁeld [1–4].
The traditional metallurgical theory suggests that multiple alloying
elements will lead to the formation of intermetallic compounds, which
may not only cause brittleness but also lead to much di ﬃculty in the
analysis [5–9]. However, the HEAs tend to form single-phase structures
with a high symmetry, such as face-centered cubic (FCC), body-cen-
tered-cubic (BCC), and hexagonal-close-packed (HCP) phases, due to
their high mixing entropy [10–12]. Numerous research activities de-
monstrated that the HEAs exhibit several special intrinsic character-
istics, for example, the high con ﬁguration entropy, sluggish atomic
diﬀusion, and large lattice distortion, which could enhance the forma-
tion and stabilization of solid-solution phases and impede dislocation
motion, thereby improving the mechanical properties [13–15].
Unfortunately, single-phase HEAs could not easily achieve a reasonable
balance between the strength and plasticity, especially the single-phase
FCC HEAs [16–18]. Previous studies indicated that the single-phase
FCC structure is insu ﬃciently strong for practical applications [15].
Therefore, further hardening and strengthening should be done on the
FCC matrix. Compared with other methods, the precipitation hard-
ening, solid-solution strengthening, and the second-phase strengthening
are easily achieved by the elemental addition [19–22]. For example, Zr,
Nb, and Ta were added to the CoCrFeNi matrix, which were found as a
beneﬁcial factor for improving the hardness and strength [23–25].B y
the addition of 23.8 at% Al to the CoCrFeNi matrix, the crystalline
structure changed from single FCC to FCC + B2 + BCC structures,
which is responsible for the increment of hardness and yield strength
[26]. Furthermore, He et al. [27] ﬁnd that both the yield and ultimate
tensile strength of the CoCrFeNi HEA are drastically increased by minor
alloy additions of Al and Ti. However, for most brittle-phase-containing
(e.g, Laves-phase-containing or BCC phase-containing) HEAs, the plas-
ticity is relatively poor. As mentioned in previous literatures, the HEA's
mechanical properties strongly depend on their microstructure [27,28].
It is worth noting that when the BCC and B2 phases formed a weave-like

2. Experimental procedures
Ingots with a nominal composition of (Al 7Co24Cr21Fe24Ni24)100-xCrx
(all compositions in this study are in the atomic percentage (at%), and
x = 0, 11, 20, and 26) HEAs (denoted as Cr0, Cr11, Cr20, and Cr26,
respectively, hereafter) were prepared by the vacuum-arc-melting
method. The purity of the raw materials of Al, Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni metals
is higher than 99.5 at%. To ensure the composition homogenization, all
of these ingots were remelted 6 times. The crystalline structure was
identiﬁed by X-ray di ﬀraction (XRD) with a scanning rate of 2°/min,
from 20° to 100°. The samples used for the microstructure investigation
were cut from the ingots, grounded, and polished to the mirror. The
polished samples were chemically etched to delineate grains and grain
boundaries by the aqua regia solution [HCl: HNO
3 = 3:1, volume
percent (vol%)]. The microstructure was characterized by the SEM
(S4800) equipped with the energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) and
optical microscopy (Zeiss Axiovert 200 MAT). The volume fractions of
diﬀerent phases were calculated by the software Image J [4]. The na-
noindetation experiments were performed at room temperature, which
used a Hysitron Triboindenter (TI-900) equipped with the in-situ
atomic force microscope (AFM), and with a Berkovich diamond tip. The
eﬀective tip radius was about 450 nm. For accuracy, the samples for the

1000 μNs

3. Results
3.1. Structural evolution as a function of the Cr concentration
The XRD patterns from the as-cast alloys (Cr0, Cr11, Cr20, and
Cr26) are shown in Fig. 1 a. Apparently, there is a crystal structure
transition from single FCC to FCC plus BCC phases with increasing the
Cr content. For the Cr0 alloy, only a single FCC crystal structure is
observed. However, when the addition of Cr is 11 at%, the (1 1 0)
BCC
peak near the (1 1 1) FCC peak begins to appear in the alloy. It is worth
noting that in the Cr11 alloy, a weak peak already commences at almost
the same 2θ degree as the (211) BCC peak of the Cr20 alloy, suggesting
the formation of a low volume fraction of the BCC phase. Similarly, a
weak peak of the BCC phase begins to appear at 2 θ ≈ 65° in the Cr20
alloy. The detailed evolution of the (110)
BCC is demonstrated in Fig. 1b.
It is noticed that this peak shifts towards a higher 2 θ angle when the
addition of Cr is at 11 at%. However, when the Cr concentration further
increased, the (110)
BCC peak exhibits towards a much lower 2θ angle.
The results showed that the shifting of the (110) BCC peak is complicated
in the Crx alloys, which may indicate that the duplex BCC solid solu-
tions formed.
The SEM images of the as-cast Crx alloys are presented in Fig. 2. The
Cr0 alloy exhibits the single FCC equiaxed grains (see Fig. 2a), which is
consistent with the XRD patterns, as presented in Fig. 1. Fig. 2(b –d)
show microstructural morphologies of the Cr11, Cr20, and Cr26 sam-
ples, respectively. In Fig. 2b, few light regions (identi ﬁed as the BCC
phase) are found to be located along grain boundaries; which corre-
spond to the minor peaks mentioned in the XRD analysis ( Fig. 1 ). With
further increasing the Cr content, the light region also expanded. The
BCC phase gradually increased in the Cr20 and Cr26 alloys ( Fig. 2c and
d), and the volume fraction of the FCC phase decreased correspond-
ingly. The result in Fig. 2e showed that the volume fraction of the BCC
phase gradually increased with increasing the Cr content. The EDS of
Crx is presented in Table 1 . The results indicated that the FCC solid
solution is depleted in Al, Fe, and Cr, while enriched in Co and Ni. On
the contrary, the BCC phase is enriched in Al, Fe, and Cr, but depleted
in Co and Ni.
To obtain the detailed information on the microstructure, the TEM
examinations on Cr11 and Cr20 samples have been conducted, and
their results are shown in Fig. 3 . Both the dark- ﬁeld image and the
selected-area electron-di ﬀraction pattern clearly con ﬁrmed that those
three alloys all consist of a mixture of FCC and BCC phases. And there
are two kinds of BCC phases in these alloys. Based on the dark- ﬁeld
TEM image in Fig. 3a, it is revealed that the amount of cubic-shaped
ordered B2 particles (about 100 –150 nm in dimension) inter-pre-
cipitated in the disordered BCC phase. Compared with the Cr11 alloy,
the cubic-shaped B2 particles in the Cr20 alloy are much smaller (see
Fig. 3b), and the B2 phase is closer to each other. These results in-
dicated that the increment of the Cr content promoted the ordering
degree in Crx alloys.
3.2. Nanoindentation
The measurement of the di ﬀerent phases in mechanical properties is
a challenging task due to the small scale. The main advantage of the
nanoindentation technique is that it possesses the ability to measure a
material's properties in an extremely small scale [31]. Zhang et al. [32]
have used the nanoindetation method to study the mechanical prop-
erties of the individual phase in rare-contained Gd xCoCrCuFeNi HEAs,
and found the hexagonal structure (HS) phase was harder than the FCC
matrix.
During the nanoindentation test, three things should be noticed. The
ﬁrst is to choose the maximum indentation load. In the current na-
noindentation experiment, the maximum load was 6000 μN. Secondly,
two indents should not be too close to each other to avoid the work
hardening of the test samples. The last is that the inﬂ uence of di ﬀerent
orientations
of the indented phases in the nanoindentation tests should
be considered. It is because that the grain orientation has an e ﬀect on
the activation of di ﬀerent slip systems, leading to possible variations in
indentation behaviors [33–35]. Therefore, more than 20 measurements
were performed on the test samples to maintain accuracy.

4.2. Relationship between the structures and mechanical properties
Liu and the co-workers [47] have already shown that the Cr addi-
tion improved the compressive strength of the AlCoCr xCuFeNi alloy
system. Xiao et al. [48] investigated that the addition of Cr increased
the yield strength and fracture strength of AlCoCuFeNi-(Cr,Ti) by
21.2% and 27.9%, respectively. In the current work, similarly, the Cr
addition could signi ﬁcantly a ﬀect the mechanical behaviors of the
(Al
7Co24Cr21Fe24Ni24)100-xCrx alloys as shown in Fig. 6. Increasing the
Cr content resulted in the formation and increase of the BCC phase,
which has a bene ﬁcial e ﬀect on the mechanical behavior of the Cr x
alloys. Since the nano-hardness values of the BCC phase (4.56 GPa in
Cr11, and 5.76 GPa in Cr20) are much greater than those of the FCC
phase (3.92 GPa in Cr11, and 4.11 GPa in Cr20), the structural trans-
formations resulted in the increased macro-hardness. Compared with
the Cr0 alloy, the Vickers hardness of Cr11, Cr20, and Cr26 increased
by 2.1, 3, and 3.7 times, respectively. In agreement with the results of
the Vickers hardness, the yield strength also increased, which increased
by 3.04, 6.65, 11, and 13.3 times in Cr11, Cr20, and Cr26, respectively.
The increment of the Vickers hardness and strength can be attributed to
many factors, such as precipitation hardening, solid-solution strength-
ening, and second-phase strengthening. Intriguingly, the Cr26 alloy
exhibits the remarkable combination of high strength and ductility
superior to that of other second-phase-strengthened FCC/BCC –based
HEAs [23,25,36–39], as depicted in Fig. 7.

lattice friction during dislocation motion, and thus increases the duc-
tility of the samples [53]. The FCC structure has the closest packing slip
plane, while no true close-packed slip planes have been found in the
BCC structure [53]. Hence, the critical stress for slip in the BCC struc-
ture is basically greater than that of the FCC structure. Thus, the duc-
tility of Crx alloys decreased with the increasing of the Cr concentra-
tion.
5. Conclusion
In this study, the (Al
7Co24Cr21Fe24Ni24)100-xCrx HEAs were pre-
pared. The corresponding microstructures and mechanical properties
were systematically investigated. The main conclusions were shown as
follows:
1. Microstructural analysis revealed that the (Al 7Co24Cr21Fe24Ni24)100-
xCrx HEAs possessed varying structures from FCC to FCC plus BCC
phases.
2. Nanoindetation measurements were carried out on the FCC and BCC
phases, respectively. The nanohardness and elastic modulus of the
BCC phase are much greater than those of the FCC phase. The na-
nohardness values of the FCC and BCC phases in the Cr26 alloy are
4.75 GPa and 6.83 GPa, respectively.
3. The macro-mechanical tests showed that the increment of the Cr
element signiﬁcantly increased the hardness and strength due to the
precipitation hardening, solid-solution strengthening, and second-
phase strengthening. The hardness increased from 140 HV to 510
HV. The yield strength increased from 249 MPa to 1649 MPa.
Among the alloys in the current system, the Cr26 alloy demonstrates
the best comprehensive mechanical properties, whose yield
strength, fracture strength, and compressive plastic strain are
1649 MPa, 2830 MPa, and 24.9% respectively.
4. The observed phases in the current (Al
7Co24Cr21Fe24Ni24)100-xCrx
alloys could not be fully predicted, using the existing criteria sug-
gesting that more work could be conducted for the understanding of
the phase transition in HEAs.
