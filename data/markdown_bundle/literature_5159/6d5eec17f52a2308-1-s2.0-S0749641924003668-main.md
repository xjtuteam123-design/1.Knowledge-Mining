ABSTRACT
Nickel-based single crystal superalloys exhibit exceptional yield strength and creep resistance 
owing to their distinctive two-phase microstructure. This in silico study reported the hidden 
relationship between the moir ´e patterns and sample sizes, which govern the formation of inter -
facial dislocation networks (IDNs). The moir ´e superlattice arises from lattice misfit, and its 
compatibility with the γ ′ phase size determines the integrity of IDNs, resulting in size-dependent 
dislocation patterns. Smaller models (size < 25 nm) display discrete dislocation networks due to 
high residual stress, while larger ones (size > 25 nm) maintain uniformly distributed perfect 
dislocation networks. These initial IDNs contribute to pseudo-elastic behavior and influence the 
dislocation activities. Specifically, smaller models experience intensified dislocation pile-up, 
resulting in higher plastic strength and lower ductility. This study provides insights into γ ′ 
phase size effects on moir ´e patterns and mechanical behaviour across the elastic to plastic regimes 
in nickel-aluminium superalloys, offering valuable guidance for their modeling and experimental 
design.

2. Introduction
Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys are commonly used in turbine blades of various aero-engines and industrial gas turbines due 
to their distinctive mechanical properties at high temperatures ( Eswarappa Prameela et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 2022 ; Pollock, 2016 ; Xia 
et al., 2020 ; Zhang et al., 2022a ). Such remarkable mechanical characteristics stem from their unique two-phase microstructure, 
comprising L1
2 
ordered Ni
3
Al precipitates ( γ ′ phase) embedded in a ductile disordered face-centered cubic (FCC) Ni matrix ( γ phase) 
( Chatterjee et al., 2021 ; Fan et al., 2024 ; Qu et al., 2024 ). Due to the discrepancy in lattice parameters between the γ and γ ′ phases, 
interfacial dislocation networks (IDNs) tend to form at the γ / γ ′ phase boundary to alleviate mismatch stresses ( Long et al., 2016 ; Parsa 
et al., 2024a ). Numerous experiments conducted over the past decades have delved into the structure and evolution of IDNs ( Li et al., 
2022b , 2022c ; Ming et al., 2014 ; Yu et al., 2020 ; Yuling et al., 2017 ). It is widely recognized that IDNs can enhance the mechanical 
performance of nickel-based single-crystal superalloys by influencing the dislocation behavior of γ phase, particularly in terms of creep 
resistance ( Li et al., 2022b ). On the one hand, IDNs can form junctions by dislocation reactions with incoming matrix dislocations 
through short-range effects, thereby impeding the entry of matrix dislocations and shearing γ ′ precipitates. On the other hand, they can 
generate long-range stress fields, significantly altering the mobility of dislocations in the matrix channel, leading to dislocation pile-up 
around the phase interface ( Huang et al., 2018b ; Li et al., 2024 ). Moreover, a dense network is more effective than a sparse one in 
impeding dislocations in the γ phase from traversing the γ / γ ′ interface ( Liu et al., 2015 ; Wang et al., 2015 ).
Given that IDNs originate from the mismatch stress, which is directly linked to the size and morphology of the γ ′ precipitates, 
considerable experimental efforts have been devoted to tailoring the size of the γ ′ precipitates. It has been demonstrated that gradient 
nanostructured metals exhibit unparalleled mechanical properties ( Meher et al., 2018 ; Shang et al., 2023 ), such as enhanced fracture 
and fatigue resistance ( Li et al., 2020 ). Studies indicate that Ni-based superalloys with a multimodal distribution of γ ′ precipitates tend 
to exhibit superior mechanical characteristics ( D.M. and H.J., 2014 ; Zhang et al., 2022b ). Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys tend 
to form two distinct types of γ ′ precipitates under varying heat treatment conditions: primary γ ′ precipitates and secondary γ ′ pre -
cipitates. The secondary γ ′ precipitates also contribute to impeding dislocation motion within the γ matrix channels ( Kakehi, 2000 ; Li 
et al., 2018 ). Typically, the temperature and duration of the first aging process determine the size of the primary γ ′ precipitates ( Parsa 
et al., 2024b ; Xu et al., 2023 ). The cooling rate following the first aging process is crucial for the precipitation of secondary γ ′ pre -
cipitates, as it affects the longer-range compositional equilibrium within the γ matrix ( Li et al., 2018 ; Yardley et al., 2016 ). Moreover, 
during the rafting of the primary γ ′ phase under creep conditions, atomic redistribution results in the formation of a significant number 
of secondary γ ′ precipitates, whose size and quantity increase with the progression of creep time ( Xiang et al., 2016 ). The size of 
primary γ ′ precipitates is approximately 500 nm, while secondary γ ′ precipitates range from 10 to 100 nm ( He et al., 2020 ; Qu et al., 
2020 ; Xiang et al., 2016 ; Yardley et al., 2016 ). Reducing the initial aging temperature or shortening the aging time significantly 
decreases the size of the primary γ ′ precipitates ( Parsa et al., 2024b ; Xu et al., 2023 ).
As the size of γ ′ precipitates reduces to submicron- or even tens of nanometers, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations become 
crucial complement to experiments for studying the evolution of IDNs and their interactions with matrix dislocations ( Chen et al., 
2023 , 2022 ; Cui et al., 2022 ; Dong et al., 2024 ; Sun et al., 2022 ). For this purpose, two different γ / γ ′ models have been frequently 
established, including the layered model ( Borovikov et al., 2023 , 2024 ; Cui et al., 2022 ; Khoei et al., 2022 ; Wu et al., 2021a ) and the 
cuboidal model ( Li et al., 2022a ; Liu et al., 2020 ; Yue et al., 2021 ). The layered model combines one layer of γ phase with one layer of γ ′ 
phase, requiring periodic boundary conditions along the two in-plane directions to maintain the interface, thus overlooking the size or 
boundary effect. In contrast, the cuboidal model closely mimics the real microstructure by embedding a cubic γ ′ phase inside the γ 
phase, naturally exhibiting size effect. Extensive MD simulations based on these models have been conducted to assess influential 
factors on IDNs, such as temperature ( Khoei et al., 2022 ; Li et al., 2022a ), load form ( Chen et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2022a ), interface type 
( Khoei et al., 2022 ; Yin et al., 2022 ), voids ( Cui et al., 2022 ; Khoei and Kianezhad, 2023 ), and alloying element ( Khoei et al., 2022 ; Wu 
et al., 2021a ; Yue et al., 2021 ).

4.3. Pseudo-elasticity
Dislocation patterns present in the model could remarkably impacts its mechanical characteristics. As an illustration, we performed 
uniaxial tensile and compressive tests on various models constructed with a same initial interfacial spacing. As depicted in Fig. 5 a, 
different models exhibit asymmetric stress-strain curves under tension and compression, consistent with the tension-compression 
asymmetry observed in FCC crystals ( Salehinia and Bahr, 2014 ; Xie et al., 2014 ). Distinct deformation characteristics are evident 
across different models. Notably, compared to larger models featuring PDN, smaller models (with CDN) exhibit noticeable wavy 
features in their stress-strain curves (see the inset of Fig. 5 a). The reduced slope of these wavy stress-strain curves suggests that the 
models with CDN display a smaller apparent elastic modulus compared to their counterparts with PDN.
According to the atomic configurations, the models featuring CDN contains glissible Shockley dislocations scattering across the PB 
and γ -phase. The triggering tensile or compressive stress for the slip of Shockley dislocation is very low due to the localized high atomic 
residual stress. Consequently, localized movement of Shockley dislocations in γ -phase already occurs during the early stage of 
deformation, resulting in a wavy stress-strain curve. With such deformation feature, models featuring CDN is regarded as displaying 
pseudo-elasticity prior to abundant plastic deformation as they lack a strictly defined elastic deformation stage. As plotted in Fig. 5 b, 
the sample is unable to restore to its initial status after unloading.

gesting minimal dislocation activities prior to yielding. It is observed from the atomic configurations that the PDN remains almost 
unchanged due to its overall stability before massive dislocation propagation occurs ( Fig. 5 c). Under tensile deformation, the pref -

In addition, higher tensile plastic flow stress than that under compression can be explained by their distinct deformation mech -
anisms ( Supplementary Movie V2 and V3 ). Under tensile deformation, the probability of dislocation collision increases as there are 
four active slip planes, which can form dense and intertwined dislocation networks that consist of interspersed Shockley partials and 
sessile dislocations. Uniformly distributed sessile dislocations as well as Stacking fault tetrahedra (SFT)( Wei Wang et al., 2013 ) 
consisting of SFs and stair-rod dislocations cause strain hardening at low temperatures ( Fig. 9 a). In contrast, only two active slip planes 
are generally observed under compression, which leads to the formation of sessile dislocations at the intersection of these two planes. 
Consequently, strain localization ensues, alongside repetitive slip motion, resulting in non-uniform distribution of dislocations and SFs 
( Fig. 9 b). Furthermore, the advancing SFs under compression also penetrate through the γ ′ phase, and the PB is sheared, forming 
obvious steps, reducing the strength of the sample. As depicted in Fig. 9 c, the PB sliding and migration under compression, quantified 
by non-affine displacement ( Cao, 2020 ) are considerably more drastic than under tension. In other words, the sample exhibits more 
homogeneous plastic deformation under tension compared to compression, and thus exhibiting higher tensile plastic flow stress.

6. Conclusion
Generally, the inherent existing lattice misfit within the two-phase Ni-Al alloy induces moir ´e patterns at the interface, leading to 
different initial dislocation patterns. Smaller models tend to exhibit a corrupted dislocation network due to excessive residual stress, 
whereas larger models maintain a perfect dislocation network comprised of diamond dislocation networks. Specifically, the 
compatibility between the moir ´e superlattice parameter and the γ ′ phase size significantly impacts the integrity of the diamond 
dislocation networks. These initial dislocation patterns not only contribute to the pseudo-elastic behaviour of the model but also 
influence its plastic deformation. Smaller models feature narrower γ -channels undergo more dislocation stacking events, resulting in 
the formation of sessile dislocations that hinder dislocation movement during the plastic flow stage. In contrast, larger models 
experience more homogenized deformation, leading to enhanced ductility.
It is noted that advanced nickel-based superalloys contain more than ten alloying elements beyond Ni and Al, presenting either in 
the γ or γ ′ phase. Hence, a future study is needed to justify the influence of alloying elements on the moir ´e patterns and thus the patterns 
of IDNs. Moreover, while our current work focused on the evolution of IDNs with a cuboidal model with varying lattice parameters, 
further exploration is desired, particularly regarding the dynamic evolution of moir ´e patterns under rafting process experienced by the 
γ ′ phase during service.
In summary, this work not only elucidates the hidden link between lattice misfit-induced moir ´e patterns and the size of the γ ′ phase, 
but also provides a comprehensive understanding of how these moir ´e patterns govern the IDNs and, consequently, the mechanical 
behaviour of Ni-Al alloy across the elastic to plastic regimes. The insights gained provide valuable guidelines for future modeling 
efforts on Ni alloys and for experimentalists working on Ni alloys with γ ′ precipitates of varying sizes.
