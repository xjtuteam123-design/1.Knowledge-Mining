Processing, microstructure and low-temperature properties 
of directionally solidified NiAl/NiAlNb alloys 
R.D. Reviere a, R.D. Noebe b and B.F. Oliver a 
a The University of Tennessee, Department ofMaterials Science, Knoxville, TN 37996, USA 
b NASA Lewis Research Center, Cleveland, OH 44135, USA 
Received 13 April 1992 
A containerless levitation zone processing technique was used to directionally solidify two Ni-Al-Nb alloys in an attempt to 
improve the fracture strength and ductility of creep resistant, NiAl-based intermetallics through microstructural control. Direc- 
tional-solidification processing was successful in producing aligned in-situ composite microstructures, while eliminating grain 
boundaries. One alloy consisted of an aligned eutectic microstructure composed of the intermetallic phases NiAl and NiAlNb 
while the other alloy consisted of globular NiAl dendrites surrounded by aligned eutectic regions. However, both alloys were brittle 
at room temperature and exhibited relatively low fracture strengths. The lack of ductility in these alloys was attributed to the high 
hardness and low toughness of both the NiAl and NiAlNb constituent phases. 
1. Introduction 
Intermetallic systems are being extensively stud- 
ied in the pursuit of new materials to replace super- 
alloys. NiAl is one such ordered intermetallic that 
has received attention because of its superior oxi- 
dation resistance, high melting point, relatively low 
density, high stiffness, and high thermal conductiv- 
ity [ 11. The major drawback to this B2 ordered in- 
termetallic is its low room temperature toughness and 
ductility and generally poor creep strength. In re- 
sponse to this latter problem, Nb has been added to 
polycrystalline NiAl as a high-temperature strength- 
ening agent with at least marginal success [ 2-61. Nb 
has also been successfully used as a toughening agent 
in intermetallic systems such as MoSi, [ 71, NbA13 
[8], Nb5Sij [9,10] and TiAl [ 111. Unfortunately, 
Nb additions have not had the same beneficial effect 
on the toughness of NiAl [ 5 1. This is due in part be- 
cause Nb is not appreciably soluble in NiAl but in- 
stead forms the very brittle Laves phase NiAlNb. 
Furthermore, all prior studies on Ni-Al-Nb alloys 
[ 2-6 ] have been on polycrystalline materials and all 
but one [4] relied on processing by powder metal- 
lurgy techniques. Associated with powder processing 
techniques are problems with purity and occasion- 
Elsevier Science Publishers B.V. 
ally porosity due to incomplete consolidation. These 
problems may be partly responsible for the low duc- 
tility/toughness of these materials. In addition, the 
polycrystalline nature of these alloys would be det- 
rimental to both creep strength and low-temperature 
fracture resistance. 
Zone processing can be used to produce aligned 
eutectic microstructures, or in-situ composites, that 
are fully dense and are of higher purity than the pre- 
cursor cast ingots [ 121. Consequently, the present 
work was initiated to determine if any benefits in 
mechanical behavior could be obtained through di- 
rectional-solidification processing of Ni-Al-Nb al- 
loys. Two NiAl/NiAlNb alloys were selected for in- 
vestigation. The eutectic composition for NiAl and 
NiAlNb was chosen so that a completely aligned eu- 
tectic microstructure could be generated. In addi- 
tion, an Al-rich alloy containing a much higher vol- 
ume fraction of NiAl was picked since this alloy 
would have a lower density and would be expected 
to have superior oxidation resistance compared to 
the eutectic alloy [ 131. This paper reports on the zone 
processing, microstructure and room-temperature 
properties of the two NiAl/NiAlNb alloys. The creep 
behavior of these materials is reported elsewhere

2. Crystal growth and experimental procedures 
Nickel, aluminium and a niobium-nickel master 
alloy were induction melted and cast into a copper 
mold. Final dimensions of each ingot after cropping 
were approximately 19 cm in length by 2.5 cm in di- 
ameter. The ingots were subsequently loaded into an 
atmosphere-controlled, directional-solidification unit 
[ 121 and the central region of each bar was direc- 
tionally solidified under containerless conditions. 
During zone processing, a hyperboloid liquid zone 
was established. This volume of liquid, 2.5 cm in di- 
ameter at the freezing solid/liquid (S/L) interface 
and approximately 2 cm in height, was constrained 
by electromagnetic levitation and was made to trav- 
erse the bar by the downward movement of the bar/ 
zone assembly relative to a stationary induction coil. 
The oscillating magnetic field induces violent mix- 
ing currents in the liquid zone during processing, 
which promotes a planar S/L interface [ 151. 
Through contactless sensing devices it was possi- 
ble to process the ingots under full computer control. 
This was accomplished via an infrared pyrometer, 
which was used to monitor the melt temperature near 
the S/L interface, and a video camera connected to 
a computer accessible image buffer, which moni- 
tored the melt diameter. More detailed information 
on this processing unit and its operation has been 
presented previously [ 12 1. 
Two alloy compositions were initially chosen for 
study. One was near the NiAl/NiAlNb eutectic com- 
position as reported by Kornilov et al. [ 16 ] and had 
a measured composition, in atomic percent (a/o), 
of Ni-41.75Al-16.5Nb (near eutectic alloy). This 
ingot was processed at 0.64 cm/h in purified helium. 
The other ingot had an Al-rich composition, stated 
in atomic percent, of Ni-47.5Al-8.9Nb- 1 C (Al-rich 
alloy). It was processed at 1.9 1 cm/h in purified he- 
lium. These processing velocities were chosen as a 
matter of experimental convenience. These compo- 
sitions were determined, after processing, by induc- 
tively coupled plasma emission spectroscopy. The Al- 
rich alloy was intentionally doped with C in an effort 
to improve its creep strength [ 141 and contained ap- 
proximately 1 at% carbon after directional solidifi- 
cation. Both alloys are shown, fig. 1, superimposed 
on the 900°C isotherm of the Ni-Al-Nb system as 
determined by Markiv et al. [ 17 1.

The as-cast and directionally solidified (DS) 
structures of each ingot were analyzed optically and 
in a scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped 
with an energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS) 
using real as opposed to virtual standards. These real 
standards aided in obtaining a more accurate phase 
analysis when EDS was used. In addition, the DS 
materials were characterized by X-ray diffraction. 
The Al-rich alloy was also characterized by electron 
microprobe analysis (EMPA). 
Microhardness (DPH), of the components in the 
aluminium-rich alloy and of the near eutectic alloy, 
was determined with a Vickers indenter using a load 
of 200 g and a dwell time of 10 s. The load was cho- 
sen high enough that the hardness was essentially in- 
dependent of load. At the same time, it met the cri- 
terion of being low enough to insure that the indents 
in the primary NiAl phase, of the Al-rich alloy, did 
not overlap onto the neighboring eutectic region. The 
average and standard deviations of 10 indents are 
reported.

3.4x28.6 mm in size, were electro-discharge ma- 
chined from the length of the as-processed ingots. 
These specimens were subjected to four-point bend- 
ing in the narrow dimension in order to produce the 
highest possible deflection for this sample size. The 
test results were used to determine the alloys’ room- 
temperature fracture strengths, which were calcu-

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Microstructural characterization 
The induction melted and chill-cast microstruc- 
tures of the two alloys are shown in fig. 2. The Al- 
rich alloy, fig. 2a, consisted of primary NiAl den- 
drites surrounded by interdendritic regions of NiAl/ 
NiAlNb eutectic. In contrast, the cast structure of the 
near eutectic alloy, fig. 2b, was primarily composed 
of randomly oriented regions of NiAl/NiAlNb eu- 
tectic. However, slight deviation from the eutectic 
composition and/or segregation during casting 
caused the occasional formation of blocky dendrites 
such as the NiAl dendrites pictured in fig. 2b. 
The microstructure of both alloys was modified by 
directional-solidification processing. For the Al-rich 
alloy, the eutectic regions were aligned parallel to the 
growth direction with an average lamellar width of 
3.2 urn for the NiAl and 2.0 urn for the NiAlNb con-

present but had spherodized and coarsened, fig. 3a.

alloy consisted solely of an aligned eutectic structure

Microhardness data for the zone processed ingots 
are given in table 2. The hardness of the eutectic 
phase in both alloys was the same within experi- 
mental scatter and was within close agreement with 
a previously reported value of 690 DPH [ 16 1. The 
eutectic region was about 35% harder than the pri- 
mary NiAl phase in the Al-rich alloy. This is due to 
the high hardness of the NiAlNb intermetallic phase 
which was measured at 865 DPH on a hypereutectic 
alloy not reported here. The hardness of the primary 
NiAl phase in the Al-rich alloy is greater than that 
for relatively pure binary NiAl, 260 DPH, but is in 
agreement with values for nonstoichiometric NiAl 
and/or solid-solution strengthened alloys [ 181. EDS 
results indicated that the dendritic NiAl phase in the 
Al-rich alloy was actually Ni-rich and was solid-so- 
lution strengthened by Nb and C, which is consistent 
with the high hardness.

4. Conclusions 
Directional solidification of an Al-rich and a near 
eutectic NiAl/NiAlNb alloy was accomplished. The 
containerless processing procedure used resulted in 
alloys which were fully dense, and in the case of the 
near eutectic alloy, consisted of completely aligned 
lamellae of NiAl and NiAlNb in the form of an in- 
situ composite. However, this microstructure had no 
beneficial impact on the low-temperature fracture 
strength and ductility of the alloy due to the limited 
ductility and toughness of both the NiAl and NiAlNb 
phases.
