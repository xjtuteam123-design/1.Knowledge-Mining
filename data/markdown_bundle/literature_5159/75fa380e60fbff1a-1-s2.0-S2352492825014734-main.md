ABSTRACT
This study investigates the microscale deformation behavior of TaMoCrTi high-entropy alloy through 
orientation-resolved micropillar compression testing. Micropillars with dimensions of 5 μ m height and 2 – 2.5 μ m 
diameter were tested along three distinct crystallographic orientations: [ 2 31 39 ] , [ 177 ] , and [ 55 15 82 ] . At this 
microscale, the alloy exhibits yield strengths ranging from 1844 MPa to 1977 MPa at room temperature while 
showing appreciable plasticity up to 20 % compressive strain. Detailed microstructural analysis reveals pre -
dominantly {112}-type slip as the primary deformation mechanism, with orientation-dependent activation of slip 
systems. The [ 177 ] orientation shows the highest microscale yield strength of 1976.7 MPa due to simultaneous 
activation of two slip systems. While acknowledging the significant contribution of size effects to the measured 
strength values, this study provides valuable insights into the fundamental deformation mechanisms of 
TaMoCrTi alloy.
1. Introduction
The development of aerospace vehicles represents a frontier in space 
technology, presenting unprecedented challenges for thermal protection 
materials [1] . These materials must simultaneously withstand extreme 
mechanical loads and temperatures exceeding 1200
◦
C while maintain -
ing structural integrity. Traditional nickel-based superalloys, despite 
their widespread use in aerospace applications, typically exhibit sig -
nificant strength degradation above 1000
◦
C, limiting their utility in 
aerospace environments where operating temperatures routinely exceed 
this threshold [2,3] .
The search for materials capable of meeting these demanding re -
quirements has led to increased interest in refractory high-entropy al -
loys (RHEAs) [4 – 6] . These novel materials, characterized by 
multi-principal element compositions including significant quantities 
of refractory elements, have emerged as promising candidates for 
high-temperature structural applications [7 – 10] . Recent studies have 
demonstrated that RHEAs can maintain significant strength at temper -
atures exceeding 1200
◦
C, surpassing the capabilities of conventional 
superalloys [11 – 15] . This exceptional strength retention is attributed to 
their unique atomic structures and complex compositional nature, 
which often result in slower diffusion kinetics and enhanced micro -
structural stability at elevated temperatures.
Among various RHEA systems, TaMoCrTi has garnered particular 
attention due to its distinctive combination of properties [16 – 18] . Pre -
vious research has shown that this alloy system exhibits excellent 
oxidation resistance through the formation of a protective CrTaO ₄ layer, 
addressing a critical requirement for high-temperature applications 
[16] . However, a comprehensive understanding of its mechanical 
behavior, particularly the relationship between crystallographic orien -
tation and deformation mechanisms, remains limited. This knowledge 
gap hinders the optimization of composition and processing parameters 
for specific aerospace applications.
This study aims to bridge this knowledge gap through a systematic 
investigation of the orientation-dependent deformation behavior in 
TaMoCrTi alloy using micropillar compression testing [19] . While 
acknowledging the inherent size effects associated with microscale 
testing, this approach provides valuable insights into fundamental

2. Method
The TaMoCrTi alloy was synthesized via vacuum arc melting under 
high-purity argon atmosphere (99.999 %). The raw materials (purity 
> 99.9 %) underwent four remelting cycles to ensure chemical homo -
geneity, followed by heat treatment at 1000
◦
C for two hours in an 
evacuated quartz tube (10
 4 
Pa) and subsequent water quenching to 
room temperature.
Microstructural characterization employed multiple complementary 
techniques. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis was con -
ducted using a Talos F200s operating at 200 kV. X-ray diffraction (XRD) 
patterns were collected using a Rigaku D/max-2500PC diffractometer 
with Co K α radiation at 40 kV and 200 mA. TEM samples were prepared 
through conventional mechanical grinding followed by twin-jet 
electropolishing.
Micropillars for compression testing were fabricated using a focused 
ion beam (FIB) system (FEI Helios NanoLab 600i) operating at 30 kV for 
rough milling and 5 kV for final surface cleaning. The micropillars were 
designed with dimensions of 5 μ m height and 2 – 2.5 μ m diameter (aspect 
ratio ~2 – 2.5:1) to minimize tapering effects while maintaining stability 
during compression. Three different crystallographic orientations were 
selected based on electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis to 
investigate orientation-dependent mechanical behavior. These ori -
entations — [ 2 3139 ] , [ 177 ] , and [ 551582 ] — were chosen to represent 
different positions within the standard stereographic triangle, facili -
tating the study of orientation-dependent slip system activation. All 
orientations were indexed within the [ 001 ] - [ 011 ] - [ 111 ] stereographic 
triangle to ensure consistent analysis.

nanoindenter (Hysitron TI950) equipped with a flat punch diamond tip 
(10 μ m diameter). Tests were performed under displacement-controlled

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural characteristics
The as-homogenized microstructure of the TaMoCrTi alloy exhibits 
several noteworthy features that directly influence its mechanical 
behavior. Bright field TEM imaging ( Fig. 1 a) reveals a BCC structure 
with contrast features that appear as dark spots. These features are not 
precipitates but rather contrast artifacts arising from local strain fields 
due to the significant atomic size mismatch among constituent elements. 
High-resolution TEM analysis ( Figs. 1 c and 1 d) shows well-defined lat -
tice fringes corresponding to {110} planes, with measured interplanar 
spacing consistent with the BCC structure. The lattice parameter deter -
mined from high-resolution TEM analysis (~0.302 nm) aligns well with 
XRD measurements ( Fig. 1 b), confirming the structural uniformity 
across different length scales.
Chemical composition analysis through EDX ( Table 1 ) reveals Cr 
(19.4 at%), Mo (27.1 at%), Ta (27.9 at%), and Ti (25.6 at%) distribu -
tions that deviate slightly from equiatomic ratios but maintain the high- 
entropy characteristics. The remarkably uniform elemental distribution 
observed in EDX mapping ( Fig. 1 e) indicates effective atomic mixing 
during processing, which is crucial for maintaining consistent mechan -
ical properties throughout the material.
3.2. Mechanical response and orientation dependence
The mechanical behavior of TaMoCrTi alloy, investigated through 
micropillar compression along three distinct crystallographic orienta -
tions, reveals strong orientation dependence of microscale properties. 
Engineering stress-strain curves ( Fig. 2 ) demonstrate yield strengths 
ranging from 1844.3 MPa to 1976.7 MPa. It is important to acknowl -
edge that these values are significantly influenced by the size effect 
inherent to micropillar testing, as the strength of materials at this scale is 
known to increase with decreasing specimen size according to the 
relationship σ ∝ D
-n
, where D is the specimen diameter and n is a 
material-dependent exponent typically ranging from 0.2 to 0.8 for BCC 
metals. Using a conservative value of n = 0.2, the extrapolated bulk 
yield strength at a reference size of 40 μ m would be approximately 
1059 – 1134 MPa. While this extrapolated strength is lower than the 
measured micropillar values, it remains substantial compared to many 
conventional alloys, inferring promising mechanical performance even 
in bulk form.
Post-compression analysis of micropillar morphology ( Fig. 3 ) pro -
vides evidence of orientation-dependent deformation mechanisms. The 
deformation traces observed on the micropillar surfaces were carefully 
analyzed to determine the active slip planes. While the slip traces appear 
to align primarily with {112}-type planes, it is important to note that 
slip in BCC metals often occurs on wavy, non-planar surfaces due to 
frequent cross-slip between {110} and {112} planes. The apparent 
prominence of {112}-type slip in this alloy is interesting and warrants 
further investigation in future studies.
The [ 2 31 39 ] -oriented micropillar shows distinct slip traces that 
appear to correspond predominantly to ( 211 ) plane activation, while the 
[ 177 ] orientation exhibits intersecting slip traces suggesting concurrent 
activation of multiple slip systems. The [ 55 15 82 ] orientation displays 
primary slip that appears aligned with the ( 1 21 ) plane. These observa -
tions are generally consistent with Schmid factor predictions, though the 
exact identification of slip planes in BCC micropillars can be challenging 
due to the cylindrical geometry and potential cross-slip behavior.

alloy, the deformed micropillars were sectioned using FIB and charac -
terized through TEM and TKD analyses, as shown in Fig. 4 . The TEM 
images in Figs. 4 a,b, and c reveal varying dislocation structures across 
the different orientations, while the corresponding Kernel Average 
Misorientation (KAM) maps in Fig. 4 d, e, and f provide quantitative

3.3. Deformation mechanisms and slip system analysis
Detailed analysis of slip system activation through Schmid factor 
calculations ( Table 2 ) reveals a correlation between predicted and 
observed slip systems. The maximum Schmid factors for each orienta -
tion (0.4771, 0.4952, and 0.4815 for [ 2 31 39 ] , [ 177 ] , and [ 55 15 82 ]
respectively) generally correspond to the slip systems that appear to be 
activated based on post-deformation analysis.
TEM characterization of dislocation structures ( Fig. 5 ) provides in -
sights into the nature of dislocations present after deformation. While 
the precise determination of dislocation character (screw vs. edge) re -
quires careful analysis using multiple diffraction conditions, the 
observed dislocation configurations suggest a mixed character with both 
screw and edge components present. The preference for particular 
dislocation types may influence the deformation behavior and strain 
hardening characteristics of the alloy.
The mechanical behavior observed in this study can be interpreted in 
the context of several strengthening mechanisms operating at the 
microscale. First, the lattice distortion caused by atomic size mismatch 
among constituent elements (Cr: 0.128 nm, Mo: 0.139 nm, Ta: 
0.146 nm, Ti: 0.147 nm) contributes to solution strengthening. This 
distortion creates local stress fields that impede dislocation motion, 
contributing to the observed strength. Second, the complex local atomic 
environments in this high-entropy alloy likely modify the energy land -
scape for dislocation motion, potentially affecting the preference for 
certain slip systems and dislocation configurations.
The stable plastic flow observed even at high strains indicates 
effective strain hardening mechanisms. The interaction between dislo -
cations on different slip systems, particularly evident in the [ 177 ]

In single-crystal metallic materials under compression, the resolved 
shear stress acting on dislocations exhibits a significant dependence on 
crystallographic orientation. The relationship between resolved shear 
stress ( τ ), loading direction, slip plane, and slip direction is expressed as: 
τ = σ ⋅ cos ϕ⋅ cos λ = σ ⋅ m. where σ is the applied stress, ϕ is the angle be -
tween the slip plane normal and the loading axis, λ is the angle between 
the slip direction and the loading axis, and m is the Schmid factor.
When a single slip system is activated, the critical resolved shear 
stress ( τ
critical
) required to initiate slip relates to the material ’ s yield 
strength ( σ

) via: σ
yield
= τ

sponds to lower yield strength ( σ

), as slip initiates more easily. 
However, under single-slip conditions, work hardening is limited due to 
the absence of dislocation interactions, resulting in minimal strain- 
hardening effects. When multiple slip systems are activated, the yield 
strength still depends on the Schmid factor of the primary slip system. 
Enhanced work hardening arises from interactions between disloca -
tions, which impede further dislocation motion. This increased work 
hardening capacity promotes homogeneous plastic deformation and 
delays the onset of necking or fracture, thereby significantly improving 
ductility. This orientation-dependent interplay between Schmid factor, 
slip system activation, and work hardening underpins the anisotropy of 
mechanical properties in single-crystal metals.
It is important to emphasize that the microscale mechanical behavior 
observed here cannot be directly extrapolated to predict bulk mechan -
ical properties, particularly tensile ductility, which often differs signif -
icantly from compressive behavior and is strongly influenced by factors 
such as grain boundaries, texture, and pre-existing defects. Nevertheless, 
the insights gained into fundamental deformation mechanisms provide 
valuable guidance for future bulk-scale studies and alloy development 
efforts.
When combined with the previously reported formation of protective 
CrTaO ₄ oxide scales [13] , the deformation characteristics identified in 
this study suggest potential for TaMoCrTi alloy in aerospace applications 
where both mechanical reliability and oxidation resistance are essential. 
However, comprehensive evaluation at the bulk scale, including 
high-temperature mechanical testing and characterization of tensile 
properties, would be necessary to fully assess its suitability for specific 
applications.

(1) At the microscale (2 – 2.5 μ m diameter), the alloy exhibits high 
yield strengths ranging from 1844 MPa to 1977 MPa with 
appreciable plasticity under compression.
(2) The deformation behavior shows strong orientation dependence, 
with the [ 177 ] orientation exhibiting the highest strength due to 
simultaneous activation of multiple slip systems.
(3) Analysis of slip traces suggests predominant activation of {112}- 
type slip systems, though the complex nature of slip in BCC 
metals and the limitations of micropillar geometry make precise 
determination challenging.
(4) The deformation mechanisms identified at the microscale provide 
valuable insights for understanding the fundamental behavior of 
TaMoCrTi alloy, though direct extrapolation to bulk properties 
requires caution due to significant size effects.
These findings contribute to the understanding of deformation 
mechanisms in refractory high entropy alloys and provide guidance for 
future studies aimed at developing these materials for high-temperature 
structural applications in aerospace and other demanding environments.
