ABSTRACT 
Near-equiatomic single-phase twining-induced plasticity (TWIP) high-entropy alloys (HEAs) exhibit a good 
combination of strength and ductility, but their modest yield strength requires further improvement. Here, we 
propose a strategy for markedly enhancing their strength while retaining satisfactory ductility, taking advantage 
of the temperature dependence of the stacking fault energy. The room-temperature strength of a representative 
TWIP HEA was improved by the cryogenic pre-deformation-induced dislocations, martensite, nanotwins, and 
stacking faults. The tensile properties were further tuned by subsequent annealing to obtain partially recovered 
or recrystallized microstructures. The influence of regulated microstructures on the yield strength was clarified 
by neutron diffraction line profile analysis. The room temperature tensile yield strength of the HEA increased 
from 275 to 1012 MPa while retaining an elongation of 21.9%. The yield strength and elongation were then 
further altered to 826.9 MPa and 42.6%. This study presents possibilities for fabricating advanced HEAs by 
tuning the substructures. 
Conventional alloys such as steels, aluminum alloys, and magnesium 
alloys are generally designed using a host metal with minor amounts of 
other elements for optimizing their mechanical properties. In contrast, 
the more recently developed high-entropy alloys (HEAs) contain mul -
tiple principal metallic elements [ 1 , 2 ]. Face-centered cubic (FCC) 
single-phase CoCrFeMnNi and CoCrFeNi HEAs exhibit a good combi -
nation of strength and ductility at various temperatures [ 1 , 3 – 6 ], and 
hence are the most widely investigated alloys. Accordingly, numerous 
near-equiatomic FCC-phase HEAs that show better mechanical perfor -
mance than the equiatomic varieties, by virtue of the reduced 
stacking-fault energy (SFE), have been developed [ 7 – 12 ]. The SFE of the 
CoCrFeMnNi and CoCrFeNi HEAs is measured to be 27 – 30 mJ/m
2 
at 
room temperature [13] , where mechanical twinning occurs in the HEAs 
during plastic deformation, and the twinning becomes more preferred 
with a further reduction in the SFE. Furthermore, FCC → hexagonal 
close-packed (HCP) strain-induced martensitic transformation (SIMT) 
occurs when the SFE becomes extremely low [14] . However, the cor -
relation between the SFE and the plastic deformation mechanism of 
HEAs is more complex compared with that of dilute alloys, attributed to 
the local complexity of HEAs [ 15 , 16 ]. Twinning and SIMT enhance the 
strain hardening rate, resulting in high strength and good ductility in 
non-equiatomic HEAs; these phenomena constitute twinning-induced 
plasticity (TWIP) and transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) effects 
[ 9 – 14 , 17 ]. 
The main drawback of single-phase HEAs is the moderate yield 
strength, which restricts the maximum allowable stress for load-bearing 
applications. Grain boundary strengthening and precipitation strength -
ening are two of the most widely utilized strategies for elevating yield 
strength. However, ductility often decreases dramatically with 
decreasing grain size [ 4 , 18 , 19 ], and brittle precipitates are readily 
formed during thermomechanical treatment [ 20 – 22 ]. Furthermore, 
introducing desirable precipitates into the alloys requires precise design 
and careful regulation of the precipitation, which is more difficult in 
HEAs than in dilute solid solutions because of the composition 
complexity. The formation of brittle precipitates leads to a notable 
reduction in ductility [ 21 – 25 ]. Besides, introducing a minute amount of 
interstitial solutes and/or the formation of chemical short-range 
order/local undulation could elevate the yield strength of HEAs [ 26 , 
15 , 16 ]. On the other hand, deformation-induced defects also strengthen 
alloys, where the dislocations, stacking faults (SFs), twin boundaries, 
and phase boundaries act as obstacles for dislocation motion [ 27 – 29 ]. 
Thus, it is feasible to improve strength by introducing defects. Moreover,

Fig. 4 a – c shows the room-temperature tensile properties of the CR 
( Fig. 4 a), HT2, and HT3 samples ( Fig. 4 b), along with their comparison 
( Fig. 4 c). The tensile property of the sample before CR is also inserted as 
a reference, where the yield strength and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) 
were notably elevated by CR; The yield strength increased from 275.5 
MPa (before CR) to 642.9 MPa (10% CR), 1012.1 MPa (20% CR), and 
1189.6 MPa (30% CR). It decreased to 565.4 MPa, 826.9 MPa, and 
828.2 MPa in the HT2 samples, and further decreased to 524.4 MPa, 
475.8 MPa, and 495.4 MPa in the HT3 samples. The UTS increased from 
739.1 MPa (before CR) to 878.3 MPa, 1117.9 MPa, and 1266.4 MPa in 
the CR samples. It decreased to 840.5 MPa, 973.9 MPa, and 999.3 MPa

874.4 MPa in the HT3 samples. The tensile elongation retained a satis -
factory value ( > 35%) in the 10% CR, HT2, and HT3 samples. These 
results demonstrate that the tensile properties can be balanced by tuning 
the defects. The CR samples exhibit a very limited work-hardening 
ability, particularly when they contain a high density of defects and a 
large fraction of the HCP phase (30% CR). The defects and HCP phase 
can be tuned, and the HCP can be reversely transformed into the FCC 
phase. As a result, the work-hardening ability, strength, and ductility 
can be optimized. The CR-generated defects and HCP phase extend the 
realm of possibilities for tuning the microstructure and mechanical 
properties of the TWIP HEA, which is one new point of our study.

In summary, we succeeded in regulating the room-temperature 
tensile properties of an FCC single-phase TWIP HEA by pre-cryo- 
deformation for introducing defects and subsequent annealing at 
elevated temperatures (773 – 973 K) for further tuning the microstruc -
tures. The FCC → HCP strain-induced transformation occurred at cryo -
genic temperature, which has not been observed at ambient 
temperature. Moreover, the HCP → FCC thermally activated trans -
formation occurred at high temperatures. These results indicate a tem -
perature dependence of the phase stability, SFE, and plasticity behavior 
of the HEA. The grain size, HCP phase, dislocation density, nano twins, 
and stacking faults in the samples were characterized, and the room- 
temperature tensile properties were investigated. The cryo- 
deformation-induced dislocations and subsequent partial recrystalliza -
tion contributed to enhancing the yield strength, which was quantita -
tively estimated. Furthermore, the HCP martensite, twins, and stacking 
faults also contributed to the strength. The strengthening effect could be 
further tuned by heat treatment. The yield strength was increased from 
275 MPa (before rolling) to 1012 MPa (20%) and 1189 MPa (30%) while 
retaining an elongation of 12.5 and 21.9%. After annealing at 873 K for 
1 h, the yield strength and elongation were 826.9 MPa and 42.6%, 
respectively. The present study sheds light on an effective fabrication 
method for high-performance single-phase HEAs by regulating cryo -
genically pre-deformation-induced defects. The influence of the pre- 
existing defects on plasticity behavior warrants further investigation, 
which is crucially important for optimizing their mechanical 
performance.
