Ti

lowering the temperature of the cast copper mold. Mechanical characterization results indicate that the alloy

1. Introduction 
The quest for high-strength and tough metallic structural materials 
has long been at the forefront of materials science research. The light -
weight high-entropy alloys (HEAs) with a body-centered cubic (BCC) 
structure, composed of elements such as Al, Ti, Zr, Nb, and V, have 
attracted considerable attention due to their exceptional mechanical 
properties [1 – 8] . Although AlNbTiVZr-based HEAs exhibit higher 
strength compared to traditional lightweight alloys, such as titanium 
and aluminum alloys, their elevated cost due to the high and diverse 
element content restricts their economic viability for practical industrial 
applications. Therefore, research is required to explore methods for 
further enhancing the alloy ’ s mechanical properties, improving its 
cost-effectiveness, and promoting its application in critical components. 
As is well-known, ordered strengthening is an effective method to 
enhance the strength of alloys. It has demonstrated that by controlling 
the Ti content and thermomechanical processing of FeCoNi alloys, a 
substantial increase in strength can be achieved through the precipita -
tion of abundant dispersed nanoscale Ni
3
Ti precipitates [9,10] . How -
ever, due to the inherent lack of slip systems in body-centered cubic 
(BCC) structured HEAs, the introduction of ordered precipitates has 
been shown to further degrade their ductility at room temperature [6, 
11 – 14] . It has been shown that HEAs, due to the diversity and 
complexity of their elemental composition, form ordered phase struc -
tures of nanoclusters on multiple atomic scales [15,16] . The ordered 
phase can effectively impede dislocation slip and avoid stress 
concentration, thus preventing premature plastic instability and prom -
ising a synergistic enhancement of alloy strength and ductility [17,18] . 
For instance, An et al. reported that, in the HfNbTiVAl
10 
HEA, the B2 
precipitates effectively impede the movement of dislocations, thereby 
activating more slip bands, leading to TE values of about 30.0% and YS 
values of nearly 1400 MP [19] . 
The key challenge is to efficiently introduce a large number of or -
dered phase structures into the HEA. Previous research efforts have 
relied on high temperature, long annealing processes to provide suffi -
ciently long periods of time for the formation of ordered phases in 
localized regions with more compatible alloying elements [3,20,21] . 
However, this approach invariably leads to grain coarsening, thereby 
diminishing the mechanical properties of the material. In contrast to 
methods that induce solid-state phase transformations through aging to 
generate ordered phases, the strategy of preserving compositional fluc -
tuations in the liquid state or non-equilibrium phase through rapid so -
lidification (RS), while concurrently refining grain structures, offers an 
efficient means to synergistically ordered strengthening and 
fine-grained strengthening effects. Li et al. discovered that employing an 
undercooling approach to grain refinement and inducing the precipita -
tion of ordered phases results in a threefold increase in strength 
compared to traditional casting methods for CoCrFeNi high-entropy 
alloys [22] . Therefore, the RS process is a promising method to tune 
the microstructure and properties of high entropy alloys.

It can be observed that the morphology of the B2 phase in the T10 
alloy resembles a needle-shaped structure. This kind of precipitate 
structure is typically formed in high-Al content single-phase BCC HEAs 
[27] . Previous studies have shown that the lattice mismatch ( ε ) between 
the precipitated phase and the matrix, that is, the surface tension, has a 
significant impact on the precipitation morphology of the second phase. 
The small lattice mismatch ε ( < 0.7%) would lead to the precipitates 
being shaped in a spherical shape. When the ε is between 0.7% and 
2.0%, the second phase tend to precipitate in a cubic shape. Further 
increasing the ε to > 2.0%, the precipitate phase will be transformed 
into a reticular structure [28] . In this study, the BCC matrix and the B2 
precipitation phase showed a completely coherent relationship, with 
only a small ε of about 0.12%. Therefore, the B2 phase should be a 
spherical form, theoretically, in the equilibrium crystallization process 
(some spherical B2 phase can be observed in the T10 sample, as shown 
in Fig. 2 a). The significant increase in the B2 phase in the T10 alloy is 
attributed to the strong directional bonding between Al and Zr elements 
in the liquid phase after heating to the molten state [14,29] . Compared 
to the T25 alloy, the T10 alloy has a lower temperature at the inner wall 
of the copper mold during solidification, resulting in greater under -
cooling and faster solidification rates. As a result, the Al-Zr-rich B2 phase

In order to further investigate the effect of B2 phase on the plasticity 
of the Al

3
V HEA, we characterized the deformation struc -
ture of the T25 and T10 alloys near the fracture surface using TEM and 
presented in Fig. 4 . It has been observed that there are several high- 
density dislocation walls (HDDWs) formed inside the grains. This phe -
nomenon arises because, in the initial stages of plastic deformation, as 
strain increases, dislocations shear through these B2 ordered domains, 
leading to the disruption of these domains and subsequently reducing 
the slip resistance for subsequent dislocations [27,44,45] . Dislocations 
rarely shear out of the slip bands, so they rapidly accumulate and 
saturate within the slip bands, forming HDDWs. 
For the T10 sample, it can be seen from the Fig. 4 d and 4 e that the 
dislocation channels are wider and more densely distribute inside the 
grains compared to the T25 sample. This can be attributed to the 
increased quantity and volume of the ordered B2 phase in the T10 alloy 
resulting from RS, expanding the "slip plane softening" region [46] . 
Furthermore, the diffraction pattern in the upper right corner of the 
figure only shows diffraction spots corresponding to BCC and B2 phases, 
indicating that the influence of twinning can be disregarded. As shown 
in Fig. 4 c, the red region 1 has a higher content of B2 phase, as indicated 
by yellow arrows, than the blue region 2, and therefore has more 
dislocation channels form and cross-slip is activated as well as a higher 
density of dislocation accumulation. As a result, the T10 alloy exhibits a 
relatively slower rate of stress accumulation and a slower reduction in 
strain hardening rate at the onset of plastic deformation. 
This process involves stress concentrations that lead to necking, 
causing the Θ of the material to continue to decrease after the yield point 
(as shown in Fig. 3 c). As shown in Fig. 4 f, in the regions where dislo -
cations shear the B2 phase, the slip planes soften, making it easier for 
dislocations originally confined around the first dislocation wall to 
spread, forming more dislocation bands. This phenomenon is known as 
plastic deformation localization [47] . The emergence of new HDDWs 
prevents dislocations from piling up and entangling within the slip 
bands, reducing stress accumulation and leading to the recovery of 
strain hardening rate (as observed in Fig. 3 c). Therefore, the increased 
presence of the B2 phase in the T10 alloy results in more slip channels 
and larger softening regions. This leads to a slower decline in strain 
hardening during stress concentration stages and a longer duration of 
the recovery stage, contributing to the excellent fracture elongation 
observed in the T10 alloy.
