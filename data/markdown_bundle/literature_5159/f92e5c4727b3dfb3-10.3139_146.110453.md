Tensile properties of ultrafine grained Mg-3%Al
alloy studied at elevated temperature
The aim of this paper is to present an overview of the tensile
behavior of ultrafine grained Mg-3%Al alloy. The bulk
ultrafine grained Mg alloy was synthesized via consolida-
tion of mechanical alloying. The grain size of the sample
was calculated from X-ray diffraction line profile analysis.
Tensile properties of ultrafine grained samples tested at
temperatures of 25 8C to 250 8C are compared with that of
a coarse grain microcrystalline sample. Plastic deformation
of the ultrafine grained sample exhibits a combination of
work hardening (15 – 48 % plastic strain) and perfect plastic
behavior characterized by a constant flow stress. At room
temperature, the sample showed a gain in strength of about
two times and enhanced ductility compared to the micro-
crystalline sample. The strain rate sensitivity was observed
to be three times higher in the present ultrafine grained
sample. A stress drop with increasing test temperatures
was observed in both the samples. The effect of temperature
on flow behavior in terms of work hardening rate, tempera-
ture sensitivity, ductility, yield strength, and tensile strength
are also examined precisely. The present results show
important evidence of the temperature dependence of the
mechanical properties of the ultrafine grained Mg-3%Al
alloy in comparison to the microcrystalline sample.
Keywords: Mg alloy; Mechanical alloying; Ultrafine
grained; Tensile properties
1. Introduction
Developments in the processing of ultrafine grained (UF)
metals (grain size from 100 to 500 nm) from powder pre-
cursors are currently attracting some interest in the fields
of materials design and engineering. The major issue for
metallic alloys is to obtain the optimum combinations of
strength, toughness and corrosion resistance. It is well
documented that metals exhibit high strength through
reduction in grain size. Such grain size refinements are
characterized by the well-known Hall–Petch relationship
[1, 2]. But in most literature it is reported that such a trend
of strengthening is at the expense of ductility, as measured
by the percentage of elongation in a tensile test [3, 4]. How-
ever, the ductility plays an important role in many structural
materials to avoid a catastrophic failure during load bearing
applications. Recently it has been observed that the ten-
dency to the loss of work hardening on deformation is due
to the very low dislocation storage efficiency inside the tiny
grains [5]. The plastic instabilities in tension in the form of
inhomogeneous and localized deformation such as necking
and shear banding, nucleation of cracks, impurities, large
internal stresses and the absence of strain hardening contri-
bute to lowering ductility in UF metals [6]. However, there
is no universal agreement on the effect of grain refinement
on ductility even at quasi-static strain rates. A few studies
have shown both high strength and ductility by reduction
of grain size to sub-micron or below 100 nm length scales
[7 – 9]. In this study, UF grained bulk Mg-3%Al alloy ex-
hibits high strength as well as enhanced ductility at room
temperature as compared to the microcrystalline (MC) sam-
ple.
There are very few studies concerning the deformation
behavior of UF grained Mg at elevated temperatures, while
many structural applications of this lightest of metallic ele-
ments and its alloys are required to operate at elevated tem-
peratures of up to 250 8C [10, 11]. Computer simulations
suggest that materials with very small grain size experience
enhanced grain boundary sliding due to a large network of
grain boundaries that can be easily mechanically and ther-
mally activated, providing a possible mechanism of plastic
deformation [12, 13]. However, experimental evidence
and a clear idea of the deformation mechanisms for these
metals are still in progress.
In this paper, we report temperature dependent tensile
properties of UF grained bulk Mg-3%Al alloy at various
test temperatures in comparison to those of a coarse grained
counterpart. The temperature dependent flow stresses are
characterized by work hardening rate and temperature sen-
sitivity. The rheological parameter, strain-rate sensitivity,
m, for both samples is investigated at room temperature.
The enhanced m is probably responsible for the gain in duc-
tility of the UF sample at room temperature. The failure
strain of the UF grained sample exhibits a non-monotonic
dependence on the test temperature. Han and Lavernia
[14] reported a similar observation for UF grained Al-
7.5%Mg alloys.
2. Experimental procedures

alloying followed by compaction, sintering, and hot extru-

X-ray diffraction line profile analysis to be about 148 nm
and the average grain diameter of MC samples was mea-
sured optically to be about 12 lm. Tensile specimens were

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Tensile properties
The variations of plastic flow behavior are demonstrated in
Fig. 1. Before plastic flow starts, the stress increases rapidly
(in the elastic regime) at the microstrain range (not shown
in figure), followed by strain hardening. The flow curve is
composed of three different regions.
Region I: higher hardening domain where stress increases
steeply and then increases at a decreasing rate
in a linear fashion at micro strain deformations.
Region II: a narrow domain of parabolic hardening where
flow stress increases slowly with increase in
strain parabolically.
Region III: plastic flow at constant or negligible amount of
linear variation (increase or decrease) of flow
stress. This stage is characterized by near per-
fect plastic flow.
Figures 2 and 3 show the true stress–strain responses of
UF and MC samples respectively. Tensile tests were con-
ducted at various temperatures at constant strain rate of
_e ¼ 4 /C1 10/C0 4s/C0 1 in air. Reducing grain size by mechanical
alloying (MA) was found to significantly improve yield
strength, tensile strength, and ductility at RT. It was
found that failure occurred in the UF sample after 22 %
of deformation and no apparent necking was detected at
room temperature. This sample exhibits nearly twice the
yield strength and ductility in comparison with the MC
sample. Dislocation movement and twinning are the
dominant mechanisms for ductility in the hexagonal
closed-pack (hcp) structures. The reduction in grain size
to sub-micron reduces the possible distance of dislocation
glide. In this situation, the deformation mechanisms are
dominated by stress-activated diffusion along grain
boundaries [16]. Thus, grain boundary deformation pro-
cesses, such as grain boundary diffusion creep, interfacial
sliding and grain boundary shear, enhanced the percent-
age of elongation in UF grained samples at room tem-
perature.

UF sample. The variation of true stress ( Dr) in region III

Both the samples revealed a strong effect on tensile
properties at elevated temperatures. The rise in test tem-
perature leads to a dramatic reduction in yield strength
and tensile strength (except for MC sample at 50 8C
where it exhibits higher tensile strength than at RT). It
is generally accepted that a rise in temperature reduces
flow stress by lowering activation barriers associated
with the atomic mechanisms of deformation. The ducti-
lity measured by the percentage of elongation was in-
creased gradually to about 41 % near to 100 8Ci nU F

peratures, the ordering of non-equilibrium boundaries
impedes grain boundary sliding at an early stage of plas-
tic deformation [19] and may cause low failure strain.
Strain hardening followed by perfect plastic flow was
observed in each stress–strain curve of the UF samples

noticeable macroscopically. The maximum range of line-
ar hardening region (Region I) was observed at 50 8C test
temperature. However, with further increase in test tem-

creased. Macroscopic observation of the fractured sam-

sample does not exhibit perfect plastic flow (region III)

3.3. Work hardening rate
The flow stress dependence of work hardening rate (WHR)
is defined as the slope of the true stress–strain curve beyond
the yield point. The flow curve of the metallic metal is often
expressed by the Holloman equation [22],
r ¼ Ken ð2Þ
where K and n are material constants known as the strength
coefficient and hardening exponent, respectively. The val-
ues of K and n can be obtained from the linear fitting of
double log plot of flow curves.
Figure 6 represents the WHR as a function of flow
strain for MC and UF samples tested at RT and 250 8C.
The data points of WHR were obtained from the slopes of
the flow curves of Figs. 3 and 4. A constant strain rate
(_e ¼ 4 /C1 10/C0 4s/C0 1) was applied. It was observed that for all
the cases the WHR decreases with the true strain. The
curves can be divided into three segments.
Segment I: steady linear decrease of WHR.
Segment II: an exponential decrease of WHR towards zero
value.
Segment III: a constant WHR (near zero) with increase in
plastic strain, which is described by perfect
plastic flow.
At room temperature, the UF sample exhibited a higher val-
ue of hardening rate compared to the MC sample in the
initial stage of plastic flow for a short period. The higher
value of WHR in a fine-grained sample is attributed to
deformation mechanisms associated with dislocation activ-
ity, where Hall–Petch behavior holds. Kim and Estrin [23]
proposed a similar conclusion for metallic materials with
grain size from micrometer down to nanometer. The value
of WHR rapidly decreases and is followed by a constant
value near zero with increase in strain, which suggests that
perfect plastic flow progresses against WH. This trend of
hardening rate with true strain is consistent with the UFG
1050 commercial purity aluminum processed by equal
channel angle pressing (ECAP) [24]. However, MC sam-
ples exhibit lower initial values of WHR in comparison to
UF samples and they gradually decrease with true strain in
the same deformation condition. At room temperature, this
sample does not show the third segment of flow behavior
as discussed above. At the deformation temperature of
250 8C, the WHR at the beginning of plastic deformation
was reduced near to one-fourth that of RT for both the sam-
ples. A weak reduction in WHR with true strain was ob-
served. However, with the further increase in strain, a con-
stant value (near zero) of WHR was observed. This result
is in agreement with the bibliographic result of Al-2124/

3.4. Fracture properties
Figures 7 and 8 show the tensile fracture surface of MC and
UF samples respectively at room temperature and at 2508C.
At room temperature, the fracture surface of the MC sample
(as shown in Fig. 7a) reveals the typical brittle morphology
of fracture described by multiplicity of cleavage events as
the main crack propagates. The macroscopic observation
indicates that MC samples experience uniform deformation
to failure, leading to low elongation. However, in contrast,
the UF sample fractured at RT revealed that the sub-micron
grains maintained their initial shape to failure. This indi-
cates that the overall deformation occurs uniformly. Some
dimpled features with weak crack propagation were ob-
served as shown in Fig. 8a, which is clear evidence of plas-
tic deformation. The range of dimple sizes is approximately
from 500 nm to 2 lm, which is about three times larger than
the average grain size. The fracture surfaces at the tempera-
ture of 250 8C are shown in Fig. 7b and Fig. 8b for MC and
UF samples respectively. It can be seen that the morphol-
ogy of the fracture surfaces is noticeably different from
those tested at RT. A significant amount of microvoids as
well as cluster of grains are seen in Fig. 8b, leading to inter-
facial sliding during high temperature which may contri-
bute to the lowering of ductility. A similar observation was
made in the fractured samples of cryomilled UF grained
Al-7.5%Mg alloy tested at 100 – 3008C by Han and Laver-
nia [14].
4. Conclusions
The tensile tests revealed that the UF samples exhibited a
significant improvement in strength and ductility at room
temperature. A continuous drop in strength and increase in
elongation to failure was noticed with the increase in test
temperatures in both UF and MC samples. However, it is
interesting to note that the elongation to failure with test
temperatures was not monotonic. The influence of tempera-
ture on tensile properties is a competing process between
work hardening and a small amount of thermal softening,
which affects the flow behavior. These effects are mani-
fested by a decrease in flow stresses with increasing tem-
perature for UF and MC materials. However, the effect is
more pronounced in UF samples than in the MC ones at
near or below 100 8C. At higher temperatures the effect is
more significant in the MC samples.
