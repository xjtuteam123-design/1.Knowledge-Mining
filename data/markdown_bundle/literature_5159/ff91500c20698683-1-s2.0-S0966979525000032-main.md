ABSTRACT
Nanocrystalline AlCoCrCuFeNi high entropy alloy with 1 at. % Y was prepared by mechanical alloying and its 
microstructural stability was examined up to 1173 K (0.75 T
m
). The lattice parameter analysis of the as-milled 
alloys shows a dual-phase structure, where Cu and Cr are identified as the host lattices for the FCC (0.362 nm) 
and BCC (0.288 nm) phases, respectively. The effect of Y addition on the microstructural evolution during 
annealing was studied by X-ray diffraction (XRD), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Molecular dy -
namics (MD) simulation was incorporated to track the atomistic behaviour of Y. Microhardness measurement 
was utilized to study the ability to retain strength after high temperature exposure. The FCC phase fraction is 
found to increase monotonically with increase in the annealing temperature. The Y addition helps in stabilizing 
an average grain size of ~31 nm after annealing at 1173 K, along with retaining 82 % of the hardness (~8.56 ±
0.3 GPa) observed for the ball-milled base alloy. The theoretical framework corroborated with the MD simulation 
elicited that the “ thermodynamic ” stabilization mechanism controls the superior coarsening resistance of the 
alloy. The strength predicted by the Hall-Petch analysis is in good agreement with the experimental result, 
further substantiating the stabilization of the nanocrystalline microstructure in the alloy annealed at 1173 K.
1. Introduction
The high entropy alloys (HEAs), whose origin can be traced back to 
the year 2004, are usually composed of five or more elements with the 
atomic percentage of each component varying from 5 % to 35 %. The 
alloy system must possess a configurational entropy of at least 1.36 R 
[ 1 ]. The central idea for developing HEAs is the high configurational 
entropy combining elements into one or a few basic solid solutions, 
which is preferred to the brittle intermetallic compounds. They also 
offer a combination of impressive properties, which are unattainable 
with the conventional alloy ’ s terminal compositions [ 1 – 10 ].
Initially, the HEAs were developed by using traditional arc melting 
and casting method for fabrication. It is worth mentioning that the large 
difference between melting points and densities of the constituent 
alloying elements makes the process of alloy fabrication by melting 
difficult. In addition, undesirable post-melting problems like pore 
development, grain growth, elemental segregation and phase separation 
may result in deterioration of mechanical properties [ 2 , 4 , 5 ]. On the 
contrary, mechanical alloying (MA) is a well-established 
non-equilibrium method for alloy development, which is scalable and 
always carried out at the ambient temperature. Following the initial 
work of Varalakshmi et al. [ 3 ], there is a paradigm shift in the preferred 
method of HEA synthesis toward the MA route to develop products 
having nanocrystallite (NC) microstructure, that demonstrates excep -
tional improvement in strength by overcoming the previously 
mentioned difficulties [ 3 – 5 ].
Alloys prepared in the form of powder or powder mixtures of more 
than one phase by the MA route need to be consolidated by a subsequent 
sintering process involving elevated temperature. However, the densi -
fication during sintering is accompanied by microstructural coarsening. 
Ganji et al. [ 11 ] have reported that mechanical alloying for 60 h yields a 
grain size of 10 ± 6 nm; however, the corresponding bulk nanocrystal -
line AlCoCrCuFeNi high entropy alloy consolidated by spark plasma 
sintering at 1023 K possesses a bimodal grain size 112 ± 46 nm and 
1550 ± 500 nm. A higher sintering temperature, say 1173 K (0.75 T
m
) 
leads to superior densification of this alloy [ 12 ]. The densification 
observed during sintering of the mechanically alloyed (MAed) NC-HEAs 
is accompanied by grain growth, and if this is severe, it may eclipse all its

annealing have been examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and trans -

Subsequently, the effect of grain size alterations on microhardness has 
been evaluated. The grain size of the annealed alloy has been compared 
to the values predicted using models based on segregation and cluster 
drag. The thermodynamic stability map constructed to calculate the 
“ stable ” grain size regime, has been compared with the experimental 
observation. Additionally, molecular dynamics (MD) simulation has 
been employed to investigate the atomic-level influence of Y on micro -
structural stabilization of HEA to support the results of the experiments. 
Furthermore, the Hall-Petch model has been employed to elucidate the 
primary strengthening mechanism of the dual-phase alloy.

with an equiatomic distribution of the metallic elements (Al, Co, Cr, Cu,

representation of the alloy ’ s atomic structure. The atomic interactions 
were modelled using the Modified Embedded Atom Method (MEAM) 
potentials [ 22 ], with parameters for the alloy obtained from Ko et al. 
[ 23 ]. MEAM was chosen due to its ability to accurately model metallic 
materials and alloys, capturing the many-body interactions that occur in 
these systems.The simulation cell, measuring 25 nm per side, contains 
3 – 4 grains based on grain size estimation using the Common Neighbor 
Analysis (CNA) method. This estimation is analogous to the experi -
mental approach in optical microscopy (Linear Intercept Method, also 
called the Heyn Intercept Method), where a straight line is drawn across 
the surface and the average grain size is calculated from the number of 
intersected grains. While this grain size does not match the experimental 
values directly, it represents an analogue to the bulk microstructures to 
understand the bulk behavior of Y-doped HEA. This approach has been 
chosen for enabling us to investigate bulk-like behavior and grain 
boundary phenomena.
The primary objective of the MD simulations was to explore the 
behaviour of Y atoms within the HEA matrix. Initially, the Y atoms were 
randomly distributed throughout the alloy. A Nos ´e-Hoover thermostat 
[ 24 ] was employed to maintain the target temperature, using a time step 
of 1 fs (fs), with each simulation running for a total of 1 ns (ns), corre -
sponding to 1,000,000 time steps. The HEA system was annealed at 
constant temperatures ranging from 873 K to 1173 K in 100 K intervals, 
mirroring the experimental conditions. The equilibration process 
involved gradually heating the system to the desired temperature, fol -
lowed by constant-temperature, constant-pressure (NPT) ensemble 
simulations to allow for equilibration. Temperature and pressure fluc -
tuations were monitored to ensure system stability during equilibration.
After reaching equilibrium, production runs were conducted for 1 ns 
with a time step of 1 fs. Data on atomic positions, velocities, tempera -
ture, and pressure were collected every 100 fs for in-depth analysis, 
while snapshots of atomic configurations were captured every 5 ps to 
track atomic trajectories and monitor Y atom migration. LAMMPS MD 
software [ 25 ] was used for all simulations, with atomic visualization and 
analysis performed using OVITO [ 26 ].
To accurately identify grain boundaries and differentiate between 
various atomic structures, the CNA method [ 27 ] was employed. This 
approach is highly effective in detecting defects and distinguishing the 
local atomic environments by examining the arrangement of neigh -
bouring atoms. In Fig. 1 (b), the system is visualized using CNA colour -
ing, with the GBs highlighted in white, and the high entropy matrix 
shown using the green atoms. The atomic trajectories trace the behavior 
of Y atoms during annealing, offering valuable insights into how these 
dopant atoms interact with the HEA matrix, and contribute to the alloy ’ s 
overall structural properties.

relative measure of the coarsening resistance of the alloy. Comparison of 
the plots in Fig. 4 (b) indicates that (i) on annealing at 1173 K, crystallite

while the post-annealing FCC crystallite size (~12 nm) in the Y-added

addition has greatly hindered the growth of FCC crystallite. Further -

both FCC and BCC parent phases decreases with the increase in the 
annealing temperature, indicating progressive solute rejection with an 
increase in annealing temperature [ 18 ].

6. Summary and conclusions
Nanostructured AlCoCrCuFeNi-1 at. % Y high entropy alloy with an 
average grain size of 11 ± 3 nm has been prepared by mechanical 
alloying. This alloy shows excellent resistance to coarsening on 
annealing for 1 h at temperatures up to 1173 K, and retains the nano -
scale microstructure (mean grain size after annealing at 1173 K is ~31 
nm). The annealed alloy also maintains about 82 % of the hardness (8.56 
± 0.3 GPa) found in the MAed base high entropy alloy. Both the 
modified Wynblatt and Ku surface segregation model, and the thermo -
dynamically stable grain size analysis, have shown that the grain 
boundary solute excess mitigated the coarsening of the nanoscale 
microstructure. The MD simulation suggests progressive migration and 
grain boundary enrichment of Y during annealing. Furthermore, the 
regular nanocrystalline solution (RNS) model also indicates that the 
microstructural stability is attained in the Y-doped alloy through the 
overall free energy reduction by solute (Y) segregation. The grain size 
measured experimentally by TEM resides well within the predicted 
“ stable nanocrystalline ” region. Prediction of hardness based on modified 
Hall-Petch relation established for the dual-phase structure shows good 
correlation with the experimentally obtained data. These observations 
support the efficacy of the Y-doping in the microstructure of the present 
high entropy alloy to retain strength even after exposure at high 
temperature.
