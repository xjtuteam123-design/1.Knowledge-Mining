1. Introduction
Developments of new alloys with high strength and ductility
have always been the unremitting pursuit for practical applica-
tions. Over the past decade or so, high entropy alloys (HEAs)
have attracted considerable attentions due to their unique proper-
ties, such as high hardness, excellent strength-ductility/toughness
combination, and good wear, oxidation, corrosion and fatigue resis-
tance, which shows great potentials in aerospace, automotive and
other ﬁelds [1–16,49]. HEAs, as a new type of structural solid
solution material, have been initially deﬁned as alloys which are
composed of at least ﬁve elements in equal or near-equal atomic
proportions, usually ranging from 5 to 35 at.% [15]. Afterwards, the
deﬁnition has been expanded to non-equiatomic multicomponent
alloys.
Following this deﬁnition, numerous HEAs with a simple
solid-solution structure of face-centered-cubic (FCC), body-

of strength and ductility could be further tailored by adjusting the
fractions of FCC or BCC phases and microstructure for this HEA and
some other HEAs [22].

Previous studies have indicated that incorporation of Al into
FCC-dominant HEAs (for instance CrFeCoNi system) can introduce
BCC/B2 phases to form a dual-phase structure for their as-cast
alloys [23–25]. Changing the Al content of CrFeCoNiAlx could gen-
erate various phase constituents with different BCC fractions and
the microstructures. Joseph et al. [24] compared the microstructure
and strength/ductility of the CrFeCoNiAlx (x = 0.3, 0.6 and 0.85)
HEAs and found that as Al content increases, the microstructure
evolved from single-phase FCC to dual-phase FCC + BCC and single-
phase BCC structure. As could be expected, the dual-phase alloy
shows a higher strength than that of the single-phase FCC alloy and
a larger elongation than that of the single-phase BCC alloy. Other
researchers have also investigated high-temperature deformation
behavior, microstructure and phase evolutions during heat treat-
ment of the CrFeCoNiAlx HEA systems [26–31]. However, up to now,
there is a lack of systematic research on the room-temperature
deformation behavior of the dual-phase CrFeCoNiAlx HEAs, as well
as the inﬂuences of the substructure evolutions in the FCC and BCC
phases on their tensile properties. Additionally, the two constituent
phases could play different roles during room-temperature defor-
mation, and the hard B2/BCC phase with a lamellar morphology
acted as a strengthening phase which may have different effects on
strengthening from those second-phase-strengthened HEAs, such
as the L12-strengthened CrFeCoNi HEA [32]. As a result, to reveal
the deformation mechanism of the dual-phase CrFeCoNiAlx HEA
is of great importance for designing the microstructure and phase
constituent to achieve optimal mechanical properties.
In the present study, the as-cast dual-phase CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA
was selected to investigate its tensile deformation behavior. Casting
is an important step that enables the production of complex-shape
components. The as-cast microstructures could be controlled by
adjusting the solidiﬁcation conditions. For simplicity, drop cast was
adopted here to form a relatively uniform microstructure. The sub-
structure evolutions in the FCC and B2/BCC phases during plastic
deformation and their inﬂuences on the tensile properties were
analyzed.
2. Experimental procedures
The CrFeCoNiAl0.7 (at. %) HEA castings were prepared by electro-
magnetic induction melting in a high-purity argon atmosphere. The
pure metals and master alloys of Ni-Al and Fe-Al (purity > 99.9 wt. %)
were used as starting materials to minimize the evaporation of Al.
Melting was performed in a MgO crucible using a vacuum induc-
tion furnace operating at 5−30 kW. Prior to melting, the furnace
was ﬁrst evacuated to ∼5 × 10−2 Pa and then backﬁlled with pure
Ar and evacuated twice to dilute the residual air inside the chamber,
ﬁnally backﬁlled a pressure of ∼5 × 104 Pa. The starting materials
were induction-melted and poured into a cylindrical graphite mold
(Ф40 mm × 66 mm). For testing the room-temperature tensile per-
formances, the ﬂat dog-boned-shape specimens with a gauge size
of 10 mm (in length) ×2.5 mm (in width) × 1.2 mm (in thickness)
were cut by electric discharge machining (EDM) from the ingot.
The tensile experiment was carried out through a SHIMADZU AG-
100KN materials testing machine with a nominal strain rate of 1.0
× 10−3 s−1 at room temperature. For the accuracy of experimental
results, all the tensile tests were repeated at least twice. Moreover,
in order to explore the microstructure evolution and deformation
mechanism during the room-temperature deformation, the tensile
tests were stopped at the true strains of 2 % and 7 %, followed by
microstructural analysis in the middle parts of gauge regions in the
tensile specimens.
The samples were cut by EDM from the ingot and deformed
specimens, and then manually ground with SiC adhesive papers
to a grit size of 7 /H9262m, ﬁnally mechanically polished with diamond
suspension down to 1 /H9262m. After mechanical polishing, microstruc-
ture and phase composition of the alloys were investigated using
X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) techniques. The XRD anal-
ysis was performed using a SHIMADZU XRD-7000S diffractometer
using Cu K/H9251 radiation (/H9261 =1.5418 Å) with the range of 20∼90◦ at
a scanning speed of 5◦/min. The microstructures were conducted
utilizing an FEI Quanta 200 microscope equipped with an energy-
dispersive (EDS) detector, operating in the back-scatter electron
(BSE) mode at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Thin sheets either
from the tensioned samples or extracted from the ingots by a cut-
ting machine were ground down to foils with a thickness of about
100 /H9262m using ﬁne SiC papers. Discs with a diameter of 3 mm were
punched out of the foils and the electron transparent regions were
produced in a twin jet polishing system at 40 V using a mixture
consisting of 90 vol.% glacial acetic acid and 10 vol.% HClO4 at room
temperature. TEM and selected area electron diffraction (SAD) anal-
ysis were performed using a Tecnai G2 F30 FEI microscope operated
at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. As-cast microstructures
Fig. 1(a) shows the XRD pattern of the as-cast CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA.
It is evident that except for the diffraction peaks corresponding to
the FCC phase, there are some diffraction peaks appearing at around
44.6◦ and 82◦. By comparing with the XRD standard database, these
diffraction peaks are determined to correspond to BCC phase. Thus,
the as-cast CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA has a typical dual-phase microstruc-
ture composing of FCC plus BCC phases. Basing on their respective
diffraction peaks, the lattice parameters of FCC and BCC phases
are calculated to be ∼3.583 Å and 2.876 Å, respectively, which
shows no signiﬁcant difference from the results reported by the
previous study [33]. The representative EBSD images of the cast
CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA are presented in Fig. 1(b) and (c), which shows
a typical dual-phase lamellar morphology. The volume fractions of
FCC and BCC phases are identiﬁed to be about 77.3 % and 22.7 %,
respectively.
The representative SEM-BSE micrograph of the as-cast
CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA is shown in Fig. 2(a), which indicates a lamel-
lar morphology of alternative grey and dark contrasts. An enlarged
TEM bright ﬁeld (BF) image in Fig. 2b clearly shows the lamel-
lar feature. SAD patterns of the two phases were indexed to be
FCC and ordered B2 phases, respectively. Although peaks belong-
ing to ordered phases were not detected in the XRD analysis, the
SAD pattern of BCC phase distinctly shows the ordered (100) spot,
indicative of an ordered BCC phase (B2). The chemical compositions
of two phases were measured by TEM-EDS analysis, whose results
indicate that the FCC phase is relatively enriched in Fe (30.25 at.%)
and Cr (28.78 at.%), while Al (20.52 at.%) and Ni (28.95 at.%) are
enriched in the B2 phase. The composition distribution is consis-
tent with the previous study just with a slightly difference probably
due to different solidiﬁcation conditions (Fig. 3).

3.3. Fracture morphology
The fracture morphology of the CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA after fracture
is shown in Fig. 7(a). The fracture surface of the HEA mainly presents
the characteristics of intergranular fracture. Compared with the
hard B2 phase, the soft FCC phase is more prone to undergo plas-
tic deformation, thus generating a large amount of dislocations.
As the plastic deformation progresses, dislocations slip within the
FCC phase and pile up at the FCC/B2interfaces, resulting in the
local stress concentration. The level of the local stress concen-
tration enhances with the increase of dislocations accumulation.
Micro-cracks are to generate once the stress concentration exceeds
the bonding strength of FCC/B2 interfaces. The adjacent micro-
cracks can aggregate to produce micro-voids. After nucleation,

Meanwhile, the fracture surface of the HEA contains a few elon-

undergoes much less deformation. Due to the difference in the
strain of FCC and B2 phases, the B2 phase can be separated from
the FCC phase at the bottom of the grooves, thus resulting in the
slender groove structure. It has been reported that the formation of
the elongated grooves can absorb much energy during the ductil-

to the improvement of fracture toughness of the present HEA [40].
Moreover, occasional river patterns can be found on the fracture
surface, as marked by red cycle in the inset of Fig. 7(a). It may origin
from the brittle fracture of B2 phase.
Fig. 7(b) shows the high-magniﬁcation SEM image of the as-cast
CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA. It is evident that the tensile fracture surface
presents the characteristic of the micro-void aggregation fracture,
showing lots of parabola-shaped dimples on the fracture surface.
Generally, the geometry of the dimples mainly depend on the stress
state acting on the micropores. In the present work, the micropores
are formed through the aggregation of the adjacent microcracks
generated at the FCC/B2 interfaces. Under the action of tensile
stress, the micropores can grow along the plane. However, due
to the difference in the resistance of FCC and BCC phases to the
micropores propagation, the micropores expand easily within the
soft FCC phases, while their propagations towards the hard BCC
are restricted. As a result, elongated parabola-shaped dimples are
formed on the fracture surface of the HEA.
3.4. Deformation mechanism during tensile deformation
To reveal the deformation mechanism of the cast CrFeCoNiAl0.7
HEA, the strain-induced substructure evolution at different inter-
rupted strains was characterized by TEM. After a relatively small
tensile strain of ∼2 %, there are a few dislocations appearing in the
soft FCC phase, while inside the hard BCC phase, no obvious planar
dislocations can be observed, as shown in Fig. 8(a). It indicates that
the HEA deforms through dislocation-slipping mechanism in the
soft FCC phase at the early stage of deformation, in which the dislo-
cation nucleation and slipping are much easier than that in the hard
BCC phase. The different sub-structures in the FCC and BCC phase is
mainly ascribed to their different crystal structure. In general, the
critical resolved stress for onset of dislocation slip in FCC phases is
lower than that in BCC phases. The initiation of dislocations in the
FCC phase of the current HEA should be easier compared with the
BCC phase. Thus, the dislocations are found in the FCC phases while
they are nearly absent in the latter. The plastic deformation in the
CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy has been proved to be governed
by the planar slip of 1/2 < 110 > -type dislocations on {111}-type
planes [41]. As shown in Fig. 8(b), the FCC phase shows typical

the increase of stress concentration, which is beneﬁcial to improve
the tensile strength of the HEA. Additionally, the dislocation wall
is a typical short-range obstacle to dislocations slip, which can act
as a grain boundary. During the tensile deformation, the disloca-
tion walls can accommodate a number of geometrically necessary
dislocations, thus leading to the improvement of work-hardening
capacity.

deformation twin. Fig. 11 displays the schematic diagram of the
formation mechanism of the deformation twin in the FCC phase.
Fig. 11(a) is the project along (111) plane of a defect-free crystal in
FCC phase and the stacking sequence is ABCABC. . .. As the strain
is increased and dislocation slip is hindered, which consequently
triggers the occurrence of the dislocation reaction. Firstly, a perfect
dislocation in the FCC phase dissociates into two Shockley par-
tial dislocations, leading to a stacking fault, as shown in Fig. 11(b).
Afterwards, the stacking fault generates on the adjacent slip plane
through the same way as above, which is superimposed onto the
stacking fault formed previously one by one. Eventually, the nan-
otwins are formed through the overlay of several stacking faults, as
shown in Fig. 11(c).
According to the calculated results reported by Liu et al. [48],
the energy barrier for the heterogeneous nucleation of the defor-
mation twins on the stacking faults is only about 10 % of that for
the homogeneous nucleation in the deformed microstructure.
The dissociation reaction of a perfect dislocation during the
room-temperature tensile can generate stacking faults, which can
serve as the potential heterogeneous nucleation sites of the defor-
mation twins. In the present study, the necessary conditions for the
heterogeneous nucleation are in place. In this case, as long as some
measures (for instance heat treatment) are taken to delay the fail-
ure slightly, the twinning mechanism could be activated and govern
the plastic deformation.
4. Conclusions
In this study, the microstructure, room-temperature tensile
properties and deformation behaviors of the cast dual-phase
CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA has been investigated in details. Based on our
observations, the following conclusions can be drawn.
(1)
 The as-cast CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA is composed of a dual-phase
structure of 77.3 vol. % FCC phase plus 22.7 vol. % B2 phase.
The FCC phase is enriched with Fe and Cr elements, while the
contents of Al and Ni are much higher in the BCC phase.
(2) The cast CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA exhibits excellent room-
temperature tensile properties with a high yield strength
of 578 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of 1015 MPa and a
relative large elongation to fracture of ∼12 %.
(3) The plastic deformation of the cast CrFeCoNiAl0.7 HEA at room
temperature is mainly governed by the dislocation glide. Both
the misﬁt dislocations and the phase boundaries of FCC and B2
phases can act as strong barriers to the dislocation slip during
plastic deformation.
