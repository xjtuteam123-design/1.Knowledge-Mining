ABSTRACT
The macroscopic morphology and microstructure evolution mechanism of the FGH95 alloy were investigated 
with regard to fracture under low-cycle fatigue at different temperatures. The fracture modes of the FGH95 alloy 
at 420 
◦
C, 510 
◦
C, and 600 
◦
C were found to fall into two main categories: shear fracture at low temperatures 
(420 
◦
C, 510 
◦
C) and I type fracture at high temperatures (600 
◦
C). The change in fracture mode can be attributed 
to the increase in strength of the precipitated phase with temperature. At low temperatures, the dislocation 
structure moves mainly by cutting, bypassing the precipitated phase by climbing at high temperatures. The 
different movement of the dislocations leads to the different fracture types of FGH95 alloy at different 
temperatures.
1. Introduction
High-temperature powder metallurgy alloys exhibit high strength 
and fatigue resistance, as well as excellent performance in mitigating 
crack propagation at high temperatures [1] . The FGH95 alloy is 
currently utilized in turbine disks of aero engines, which are frequently 
subjected to cyclic loading. Consequently, the damage caused by high- 
temperature low cycle fatigue (LCF) represents a significant factor 
influencing the service life of FGH95 alloy. The study of nickel-based 
high-temperature alloy LCF properties and deformation mechanisms is 
affected by a number of factors, including temperature [2,3] , strain rate 
[4,5] , loading frequency [6] , and crystal structure orientation [7,8] .
The FGH95 alloy is a nickel-based high-temperature alloy that ex -
hibits a high volume fraction of the precipitate phase [9] , and temper -
ature has a significant effect on the deformation behavior of nickel- 
based high temperature alloys [10 – 12] . Kim et al. [13] found that fa -
tigue life of solid solution strengthened Hastelloy X alloy would be 
significantly different at different temperatures, and the high tempera -
ture fatigue life is lower than the low temperature one at high strain 
condition, and high temperature life is higher than low temperature 
fatigue life, and high temperature life is higher than low temperature life 
under low strain conditions, which is mainly due to the fact that 
oxidation affects the fatigue life by influencing and extension at high 
temperatures. Chu et al. [14] found that cyclic hardening in fatigue tests 
at different temperatures is caused by high density of dislocated shear 
precipitation phases, and that the damage is mainly caused by the 
combination of fatigue and creep at 900 
◦
C. Ma et al. [15] discussed the 
limiting values of tensile asymmetry of high temperature alloys at 
different temperatures. There have also been many studies on the 
dislocation structure and the form of motion of slip bands at different 
temperatures [16 – 18] , Li et al. [10] pointed out that the slip bands, as 
the main slip features at different temperatures, become weaker in the 
degree of intrusion and extrusion with the increase of temperature. 
Antolovich et al. [19] observed that the dislocations are mainly stored at 
the interface of two phases during the deformation process at high 
temperatures. The current research mainly focuses on the study of 
deformation behavior on nickel-based high-temperature alloys LCF, and 
there are few studies on the evolution mechanism related to the evolu -
tion mechanism from the macroscopic fracture morphology and 
microscopic features, while the study of fracture morphology is more 
focused on the crack extension aspect, and there is not yet a clear 
mechanism for the fracture mode transition of the fracture.

Specimens for LCF testing were prepared in accordance with the 
Chinese standard GB/T 15248 – 2008. The fatigue specimens had a gauge 
length of 5.5 mm and were constructed with the geometric dimensions

the gauge length segment. The stress, strain, and number of cycles to 
failure were recorded for each specimen.
Following the LCF tests, the phase structure of the specimens was

FEI Sirion 400 SEM was employed to observe the fracture surfaces. The

For further investigation of the microstructure, a Talos F200x TEM was

The relationship between temperature and fatigue life is not a simple 
linear one, as illustrated in Fig. 3 . In accordance with the test conditions 
T420 and T510, at elevated stress levels, the fatigue life at T510 is 
shorter than at T420. However, the inverse is true at low stress levels. In 
contrast to the other two conditions, the fatigue life at all stress levels is 
higher under test condition T600. As observed in previous studies 
[13,14] , the fitted curves for T420 and T510 intersect. At equivalent 
stress levels, the fatigue life at T600 is markedly longer than at T420 and 
T510, indicating that the deformation mechanism of the FGH95 alloy 
may undergo a transformation at T600. As the temperature increases, 
the fatigue life of the alloy improves, a trend also observed in GH4049 
[20] . This phenomenon is common in nickel-based superalloys, which 
are often precipitation-hardened. With temperature changes, the 
strengths of the matrix phase and precipitate phase also change. Under 
cyclic loading, the interaction between dislocation structures and the 
matrix and precipitate phases varies [13,21] , leading to the observed 
increase in fatigue life with rising temperature.
As illustrated in Fig. 4 , the cyclic stress – strain curves of specimens 
subjected to a peak stress of 1265 MPa were examined at varying tem -
peratures. In accordance with the T420 condition illustrated in Fig. 4 (a), 
the material evinces unambiguous cyclic hardening, with stress rising 
precipitously and stabilising during the initial cycles. This phenomenon 
is indicative of a pronounced resistance to plastic deformation at this 
temperature. Under the T510 condition in Fig. 4 (b), the stress peaks are 
relatively lower, and a slight softening trend appears as the number of 
cycles increases, suggesting that the material has good cyclic stability at 
this intermediate temperature. Under the T600 condition in Fig. 4 (c), 
the material shows a pronounced cyclic softening effect, with peak stress 
gradually decreasing as the number of cycles increases, indicating a 
significantly enhanced plastic deformation capacity in a high- 
temperature environment.

3.4. Mechanism of microstructure evolution
In order to ascertain the dominant factors of microstructural evolu -
tion leading to different fracture types, TEM characterization of the 
specimens of T420 and T600 was conducted. Initially, the structural 
composition of the specimens was analyzed by Tem-Energy Dispersive 
Spectrometer (TEM-EDS), as illustrated in Fig. 8 . The TEM-EDS results 
indicate that the FGH95 alloy comprises a matrix phase framework 
comprising Co and Cr elements and a precipitated structure comprising 
Ni, Al, and Ti elements. The precipitated phase was identified through 
selected area diffraction (SAED) and was found to be γ’ phase. Addi -
tionally, elemental analysis revealed the presence of unmelted Zr within 
the alloy, which is a consequence of the powder metallurgical process.
In order to elucidate the mechanism of microstructure evolution at 
different temperatures, TEM characterization of T420 and T600 speci -
mens was conducted, and Fig. 9 depicts the dislocation structure of 
FGH95 alloy following fatigue testing at various temperatures. At T420, 
the dislocation structure of the FGH95 alloy is comprised primarily of 
two distinct types of dislocations: dislocation I and dislocation II ( Fig. 9
(a) ). Dislocation I is predominantly a shear γ’ precipitation phase (yel -
low arrow in Fig. 9 (a) ). Additionally, a small quantity of dislocation II is 
wrapped around the γ’ precipitation phase to form a dislocation ring-like 
structure (black arrow in Fig. 9 (a) ). The γ’ precipitate phase is also 
indicated by a white arrow in Fig. 9 (a) ). At T600, the dislocation 
structure within the alloy is primarily represented by dislocation II, 
which forms a dislocation ring structure surrounding the γ’ precipitated 
phase (black arrow in Fig. 9 (b) ). As illustrated in Fig. 9 , the dislocation 
morphology within the alloy under LCF tests at varying temperatures 
exhibits a stark contrast in relation to the precipitated phase. At lower 
temperatures (T420), the dislocations are predominantly in the form of 
shear γ’ precipitated phase within the alloy. Conversely, at higher 
temperatures (T600), the dislocations form a dislocation ring around the 
γ’ precipitated phase. This interaction between dislocations and 
precipitated phases at different temperatures has been extensively 
studied by other researchers [26,27] . At low temperatures, the strength 
of the γ matrix phase of nickel-based alloys is higher than that of the γ’ 
precipitated phase. Conversely, at high temperatures, the γ matrix phase 
exhibits lower strength than the γ’ precipitated phase [10] , which im -
plies that the mode of dislocation movement at different temperatures is 
primarily influenced by the strengths of these two phases. With regard to 
the γ’ precipitated phase, the deformation mechanism can be broadly 
divided into two categories. At low temperatures, both the γ matrix 
phase and the γ’ precipitated phase possess the capacity to resist shear 
stress and positive stress. Furthermore, the two phases are capable of 
undergoing shear deformation in response to the shear stress compo -
nent. However, with the increase in temperature, the strength of the γ’ 
precipitated phase gradually increases, and the dislocations are pri -
marily deformed within the γ matrix phase. As illustrated in Fig. 9 (a) , 
the T420 sample, subjected to a lower temperature, demonstrates 
deformation of the dislocation structure through the shearing of both the 
γ matrix and γ’ precipitated phase. During the deformation process, the 
morphology of the γ’ precipitated phase is altered by the shearing force. 
The γ’ phase undergoes a transformation from an undeformed square 
[28] to a deformed diamond shape (illustrated by the white arrow). As 
illustrated in Fig. 9 (b) , the T600 sample displays greater γ’ precipitated 
phase strength than γ matrix phase strength at a higher temperature. The 
dislocation structure primarily traverses the matrix phase via bypassing 
the γ’ precipitated phase. Consequently, the γ’ precipitated phase 
morphology in the T600 sample manifests as a regular square, thereby 
substantiating the assertion that the motion modes of the dislocation 
structure during deformation at varying temperatures are distinct.

precipitated phase in the form of dislocation I, with a minor portion of 
the dislocation structure bypassing the γ’ precipitated phase in the form 
of dislocation II. In contrast, under high-temperature loading conditions, 
the γ’ precipitated phase is strengthened, and the majority of

The formation of the macroscopic fracture morphology ( Fig. 4 ) can 
be explained by the microstructural evolution mechanism ( Fig. 9 ). At 
low temperatures (T420 and T510), dislocations primarily move along 
the slip surface, resulting in crack initiation along the slip zone. This 
ultimately leads to shear failure of the alloy. At higher temperatures 
(T600), dislocations slip on the slip surface of the matrix phase and 
climb through the phase boundary between the two phases, resulting in 
crack initiation at the interface between the two phases. The fracture 
morphology exhibits a straight shape under axial loading conditions. 
The fracture angle θ decreases as the temperature rises, which is pri -
marily due to the initial dominance of shear. However, as the temper -
ature increases, the dislocations gradually assume a dominant role in the 
climb, resulting in a gradual decrease in the fracture angle θ .
4. Conclusion
In this study, the fracture angles at different temperatures (T420, 
T510, and T600) were found to be significantly different when con -
ducting LCF tests at three temperatures. The macroscopic morphology 
and microscopic characteristics were also studied, and the following 
conclusions were drawn: 
(1) The relationship between fatigue life and temperature is not 
strictly inverse; under certain loading conditions, fatigue life may 
actually increase with temperature.
(2) The FGH95 alloy exhibits distinct fracture modes at varying 
temperatures. At low temperatures (T420, T510), the predomi -
nant failure mechanism is fracture along the grain surface (shear 
fracture). Conversely, at high temperatures (T600), the dominant 
fracture mechanism is amorphous fracture (I type fracture).
(3) The micro-mechanism of the different fracture modes at different 
temperatures of the alloy is primarily attributable to the disparate 
motion of the dislocation structure. At low temperatures, the 
dislocation structure cuts the matrix phase and the precipitated 
phase. With the increase in temperature, the strength of the 
precipitated phase is enhanced, and the dislocations move around 
the precipitated phase by climbing up.
