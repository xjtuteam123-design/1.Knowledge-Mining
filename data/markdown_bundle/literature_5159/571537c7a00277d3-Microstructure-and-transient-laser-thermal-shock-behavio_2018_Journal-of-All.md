abstract
Tungsten is one of the most promising candidates for plasma-facing material for fusion. In this experi-
ment, WeTiCeY2O3 composites were successfully prepared by the wet chemical method. The properties
of the W eTiCeY2O3 composites were studied through ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscopy,
high-resolution transmission electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction analysis, thermal conductivity, and
transient laser thermal shock. During the sintering of the composite powder, TiC and Y 2O3 precipitations
converted to Y-Ti-O precipitations. After transient laser thermal shock, the composites underwent
melting, cracking, and resolidi ﬁcation, resulting in changes in surface morphology and internal structure
of the composites.

1. Introduction
Tungsten and its alloys are the most promising candidates for
plasma-facing material (PFM) for ITER owing to their high melting
point, good thermal conductivity, and low sputtering rate. How-
ever, these materials also exhibit disadvantages, including low
temperature brittleness, recrystallization brittleness, and irradia-
tion brittleness, thus limiting their practical applications [ 1e3].
In a fusion reactor, PFMs will experience a series of particle ﬂux
and the accompanying energy ﬂux. During their lifetime, PFMs are
subjected to long periods of continuous high-energy heat load and
high-strength transient heat load. A steady heat load will occur
over long periods, whereas the maximum heat ﬂux of a typical ITER
steady state operation will approximate 5 e10 MW/m2. Compared
with that in a steady-state heat load operation, the heat load in
instant heat load operations is higher, resulting in material damage,
such as plasma rupture, edge-localized modes, and vertical
displacement events [ 4,5], and plastic deformation, cracking, and
melting of tungsten materials [ 6].
The addition of dispersed second-phase components, such as
carbides [7,8] and oxides [ 9,10], can inhibit the growth of tungsten
grains, impede dislocation movement, enhance the binding force of
grain boundaries, and reduce the ductile ebrittle transition tem-
perature (DBTT) of tungsten [ 11], thereby improving the properties
of tungsten-based materials. Oxide- or carbide-doped tungsten
composites powders can be obtained by mechanical alloying
[12,13] or wet chemical methods [ 14e16]. Various studies show the
effectiveness of wet chemical methods in preparing high-
performance carbide- or oxide-dispersion-reinforced tungsten-
based materials. Compared with mechanical alloying, the wet
chemical method features the advantages of easy operation,
absence of impurities during preparation, and easy mass
production.
In this experiment, W eTiCeY
2O3 composites were successfully
prepared by wet chemical method. Composite properties and laser
beam thermal shock resistance were studied.

2.2. Characterization
Crystal phases, particle size distribution, and microstructure of the
prepared powders were investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD), a
laser particle size analyzer, and ﬁeld-emission scanning electron
microscopy (FE-SEM), respectively. The density of the sintered block
was measured by the Archimedes method. Thermal conductivity and
microhardness of the composites were measured by laser thermal
conductance and the Vickers hardness test, respectively. The com-
posite microstructures were observed using FE-SEM and high-
resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM).
2.3. Thermal shock performance test
Transient high thermal shock tests were performed with the
LSW-1000 laser instrument at room temperature. The experiments
were conducted under argon atmosphere protection, with argon
ﬂow velocity of 10 L/min. The energy distribution of the laser beam
was Gaussian-like shaped. The laser beam current was 60 A and
pulsed with a frequency of 0.5, 1, and 2 Hz, The duration of the
pulses was 1 ms for all frequencies. The energy density of laser
thermal shock measured 5.3, 10.6, and 21.2 MW/m 2. After the
thermal shock tests, surface damage of the composites was
observed by FE-SEM.

3.4. Thermal conductivity of the composites
According to the thermal diffusivity of the samples at different
temperatures, the corresponding thermal conductivity can be
calculated. Fig. 4 (a) and (b) show the thermal diffusivity and ther-
mal conductivity, respectively, of the samples at 300, 500, 700, 900,
and 1 100 K. Results showed that the thermal diffusivity of
WeTiCeY2O3 composites decreased remarkably with increasing
temperature. A similar trend was observed with thermal conduc-
tivity, consistent with that of pure tungsten. For metals, electron
conduction plays a leading role in thermal conduction. For
tungsten, the thermal conductivity of electrons decreases with
increasing temperature [ 19,20]. Therefore, the thermal conductiv-
ity of pure tungsten decreased with increasing temperature. For the
WeTiCeY2O3 composites, TiC and Y 2O3 were randomly distributed
across the tungsten matrix. Several factors, such as thermal con-
ductivity of the matrix and thermal conductivity and distribution of
the second phase, affect the thermal conductivity of composites.
Material density signi ﬁcantly affects the thermal conductivity of
the composites. But the main factor is the thermal conductivity of
the matrix. At room temperature, the thermal conductivity of
WeTiCeY2O3 composites approximated 148.38 W/m $K, and the
thermal conductivity of pure tungsten is 168 W/m $K. The thermal
conductivity of this composite is slightly lower than that of pure
tungsten. However, compared with the experimental results of Liu
[21], the thermal conductivity of the prepared composites was
higher than those of W-1 wt% La 2O3 and W-1 wt% Y 2O3. This result
may be related to the high density of W eTiCeY2O3 composites.

3.6. Thermal shock response of the composites
When the laser beam current frequency was 0.5 Hz, laser ther-
mal shock energy density approximately measured 5.3 MW/m 2.
After the thermal shock test with a power density of 5.3 MW/m 2,
the morphology of W eTiCeY2O3 composites surface damage was
observed, as shown in Fig. 6 (a). The range of the damage area
approximated 310 mm. The surface under thermal shock was
divided into three regions: melting zone, which is at the center
position of laser shock; the heat-affected zone; and the unaffected
sample edge.
The coarsest major crack in the melting zone passed through the
central area of the impact area. In addition to the major crack, a few
secondary cracks formed in the entire damage area with a narrow
width, and these cracks expanded along the grain boundary
(Fig. 6(b)). When the energy density of the laser beam exceeds the
failure threshold, thermal shock will cause the formation of a crack
network and surface roughening. Material strength was relatively
weak at the grain boundary [ 26]; thus, the crack expanded easily
along the grain boundary. The widest crack approximated 1 mm, as
shown in Fig. 6(c). The main reason for the cracking occurred in the
laser beam shocked sample is that during the loading process, the
tungsten surface absorbs a large amount of heat which leads to a
sharp rise in temperature (exceeding the DBTT) and is in a state of
ductile. In addition, due to local transient heating, the tungsten
surface generates a large thermal stress. Once the stress exceeds
yield strength of the composite, the material will undergo plastic
deformation; and when the thermal shock disappeared, the ma-
terial will cool down to the DBTT of tungsten very quickly. At this
time, the material behaves as brittleness. Therefore, this deforma-
tion will directly lead to the material surface cracking. Cracks
usually bypass or terminate in the second phase, whose existence
will play an important role in preventing crack growth. However,
after a transient high-power density thermal load, several cracks
formed in the second phase ( Fig. 6(d)). Although the second phase
can impede crack growth, some secondary particles possess
insufﬁcient strength to prevent their own cracking. According to
the analysis above, two new phases in second-phase particles may
exist with the emergence of new interfaces. Similar to the grain
boundary, the strength at the interface was extremely low to resist
crack propagation.
After increasing the laser beam frequency to 1 and 2 Hz, the
thermal shock test of the material was carried out at a power
density of 10.6 and 21.2 MW/m2, respectively. Fig. 7(a) and (d) show
the unenlarged damaged area of the composite material compared
with that in Fig. 6(a), but a visible difference was observed between
the propagation paths of the major crack. Under high-power-
density laser beam, the major crack on the composite surface
showed no signs of passing through the center of the damaged area.

The second phase yielded a low melting point. When tungsten
melted after thermal shock, the second-phase TiC and Y 2O3 had
already melted. Thus, when a high-temperature laser beam was
directed at the specimen, the second-phase liquid diffused and
sputtered around the argon ﬂow and then gathered at the edge of
the molten pool [ 28]. This phenomenon explains the sole presence
of tungsten in the energy spectrum of the molten region. Resoli-

different grain shapes, that is, the resolidi ﬁed area comprised
columnar grains [ 28,29], whereas the unaffected area presented
polyhedral crystals. This difference resulted from thermal expan-
sion and irreversible plastic deformation caused by compressive
stress of the grains during heat loading [ 26]. Nanostructure for-
mation occurs as a result of material diffusion to release laser pulse-
induced residual and lateral stresses [ 30].
After transient thermal shock, a certain range of melting zone
and a large number of crack networks will form on the damaged
surface of tungsten. Changes will also occur in surface morphol-
ogies and internal structure of tungsten. This condition may change
the performance of the material. Therefore, further research should
be devoted to improving thermal shock resistance of tungsten-
based materials.

4. Conclusion
WeTiCeY2O3 composite powder was prepared by wet chemical
method. During sintering, TiC and Y 2O3 reacted to produce new
oxide particles, such as Y2TiO5,Y 2TiO6, and Y2TiO7. The formation of
YeTieO oxide particles led to better composite performance at
high temperatures and heavy irradiation conditions. After the
transient thermal shock test of the samples by laser beams with
different power densities, the range of damage areas were
approximately 300 mm. A large number of crack networks appeared
on the damaged surface of the material. Cracks extended along the
interface of the grain boundaries and phase boundaries. Such re-
sults were attributed to the relatively low strength of the interface.
In addition, the tungsten resolidi ﬁed into nanoparticles under
transient thermal load. The surface morphology and internal
structure of tungsten changed after thermal shock, affecting its
performance. Thus, improving the thermal shock resistance of PFM
bears importance in the ﬁeld of nuclear fusion.
5. Outlook
The formation of Y-Ti-O oxide particles led to better composite
performance at high temperatures and heavy irradiation condition.
This improvement might take place however, is strongly depended
on the particle size and distribution. We hope to ﬁnd out the effect
of this oxide particle on the properties of composite materials
through further research.
