1. Introduction
Advanced ultra-supercritical (A-USC) thermal power 
plants with a steam temperature of 973 K and a steam pres-
sure of 35 MPa are expected to improve the power gen-
eration efficiency in fossil-fired power generation systems.
1) 
However, ferritic heat-resistant steels used in existing steam 
turbines such as high-chromium steels and modified 9Cr-
1Mo steels are unsuitable for applications in environments 
above 900 K, due to limitations in mechanical properties 
associated with the microstructural degradation at high 
temperatures.
1,2) Therefore, Ni-based superalloys are being 
considered as alternative materials to ferritic heat-resistant 
steels. However, the high material costs and the difference in 
thermal expansion coefficients between the Ni-based alloys 
and the ferritic steels are significant concerns.
3–5) Thus, it 
is required to develop new ferritic heat-resistant steels that 
exhibit sufficient strength at 973 K.
In recent years, ferritic Fe–Al–Ni-based alloys in which 
B2-type NiAl precipitates are dispersed in a body centered 
cubic (bcc) matrix have been developed as new ferritic 
heat-resistant alloys.
6–19) Stallybrass et al. reported that 
alloys containing the NiAl precipitates with a diameter of 
several micrometers exhibit high strength at both ambient

The room temperature (RT) ductility of ferritic Fe–Al–Ni-based alloys containing B2-type NiAl precipitates 
was improved by the refinement of the matrix. The microstructure and tensile properties of Fe–Al–Ni–Cr–
Mo–Mn alloys after annealing at 1 223 K followed by air cooling were investigated focusing on Mn con-
tent. The matrix of the alloy containing 4 at% Mn consists of coarse equiaxed 
/uni03B1 (bcc) grains and fine grain 
regions composed of the /uni03B1 and /uni03B3 (fcc) grains. Fine grain regions are formed through the phase transforma-
tion between the /uni03B1 and /uni03B3 phases during annealing and air cooling. In the heat treatment process, the 
precipitation of (FeCr) 2Mo Laves phase is effective in the refinement of the matrix grains due to grain 
boundary pinning effect. The alloy with fine grain regions in the matrix exhibits not only large elongation 
(approximately 14.7%) at RT but also high strength, compared with modified 9Cr-1Mo and 316 stainless 
steels in the temperature range from RT to 973 K. The fine grain regions which are fractured by ductile 
fracture mode are responsible for the large ductility of the alloy at RT. Moreover, the high strength of the 
alloy at high temperatures is attributed to the high stability of the NiAl precipitates at 973 K.

order to further decrease the intergranular fracture at RT, it 
is necessary to reduce the stress concentration at the grain

In this study, we aimed to further improve the RT ductil -
ity of ferritic Fe–Al–Ni-based alloys strengthened by nano-
sized NiAl precipitates. Fine grain regions were introduced 
in the matrix by utilizing the phase transformation between 
the 
/uni03B1 and /uni03B3 phases and the precipitation of the Laves phase 
during heat treatment and subsequent cooling by the addi-
tion of Mn, which is an austenite-stabilizing element, into 
the Fe-11.3Al-6.0Ni-9.1Cr-1.8Mo alloy. After the process 
of annealing followed by air cooling, the microstructure, 
RT, and high temperature tensile properties of the Mn-
doped alloys were investigated focusing on Mn content. 
Furthermore, the phase transformation behavior of the alloys 
and the evolution process of fine grain regions were also 
investigated from systematic microstructure observations for 
aged alloys followed by water cooling.
2. Experimental Procedure
To promote the phase transformation from the 
/uni03B1 to /uni03B3 
phase, the studied alloys were prepared by replacing Al in 
the Fe-11.3Al-6.0Ni-9.1Cr-1.8Mo alloy with Mn. Fe-(11.3-x)
Al-6.0Ni-9.1Cr-1.8Mo-xMn (x = 0, 1.8, 3.5) (at.%) alloys 
were fabricated by plasma arc melting using high-purity 
Fe, Al, Ni, Cr, Mo, and Mn in an Ar atmosphere. Hereaf -
ter, these alloys are referred to as 0Mn, 2Mn, and 4Mn 
depending on the rounded Mn content of the alloys. The 
chemical compositions of these alloys are listed in Table 
1. The ingots were flipped and remelted at least six times 
to ensure chemical homogeneity. The alloys were homog-
enized at 1 373 K for 48 h in an Ar atmosphere followed 
by furnace cooling and then subjected to hot rolling for a 
90% reduction in thickness at 1 223 K in air. The hot-rolled 
plates of 0Mn, 2Mn, and 4Mn were then annealed at 1 223 
K for 1 h in an Ar atmosphere followed by air cooling (AC) 
(hereafter annealed alloys). In order to investigate the phase 
transformation behavior of 4Mn, solutionized alloys (1 473 
K, 1 h, Ar atmosphere, water quenching (WQ)) were aged 
at 1 123 K, 1 173 K, 1 223 K, 1 273 K, and 1 323 K for 2 
min in an Ar atmosphere followed by WQ (hereafter aged 
alloys). Additionally, the stability of the NiAl precipitates in 
annealed 4Mn was evaluated by microstructure observations 
for the alloy subjected to heat treatment at 973 K for 1 h in 
an Ar atmosphere followed by WQ.
The microstructures of these alloys were characterized 
by a scanning electron microscopy (SEM), an electron 
backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and a transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM). The SEM and TEM observations were 
carried out at accelerating voltages of 15 kV and 300 kV, 
respectively. The chemical composition of the matrix and 
precipitates in the alloys was analyzed by an energy disper-
sive spectroscopy (EDX). The samples for the microstruc -
ture observations were polished mechanically using silicon 
carbide emery papers and electrochemically in a perchloric 
acid: acetic acid (10: 90 vol%) solution at RT to remove 
oxidized surface. The dissolution temperature (T
c) of the 
NiAl precipitates was measured by a differential scanning 
calorimetry (DSC) at a heating rate of 20 K min
−1.
The tensile properties of the alloys in the temperature 
range between RT and 973 K were examined using an 
Instron-type testing machine with an initial strain rate of 
1.7 × 10
–4 s–1. The tensile tests at RT and high temperatures 
were conducted in air and vacuum, respectively. The tensile 
specimens with gauge dimensions 5.0 × 1.5 × 1.0 mm were 
cut from the alloy plates after annealing at 1 223 K for 1 h 
in Ar atmosphere followed by AC. The specimens were also 
polished mechanically and electrochemically before tensile 
tests. The fractographs of the specimens after the tensile-
deformation to fracture were observed by SEM.

3.3. Mechanical Properties of Annealed Alloys
Stress–strain (S–S) curves of the annealed 0Mn, 2Mn, and 
4Mn tensile-deformed at RT are shown in Fig. 6. It should 
be noted that 
/uni03C30.2 of the annealed alloys is much higher than 
that of the modified 9Cr-1Mo steel ( /uni03C3y = 550 MPa).34) The 
interaction between dislocation and the NiAl precipitates is 
affected strongly by the morphology of the precipitates. In 
a previous study, nearly straight dislocations that cut the 
fine NiAl precipitates with D

annealed 0Mn after tensile deformation at RT. 29) Therefore, 
it is supposed that the NiAl precipitates in 2Mn and 4Mn 
are also sheared by the dislocations because there is no sig-
nificant difference between the morphology of precipitates 
for the alloy with and without Mn.
As shown in Fig. 6. EL of the alloys increased slightly 
from 9.2% to 10.4% with a 2 at% Mn addition. Teng et 
al. reported that reducing the Al content in the matrix is 
effective in improving the ductility of ferritic heat-resistant 
steels by weakening the effect of solid solution hardening.
9) 
Thus, the improvement in the ductility of 2Mn is probably 
due to a decrease in the Al content of the matrix associated 
with the substitution of Mn for Al. Moreover, EL of 4Mn 
is approximately 15%, which is much larger than that of 
2Mn. This drastic increase in the elongation of the alloy is 
difficult to understand simply by the influence of Al content 
of the matrix. The variations in fracture mode related to the 
microstructure should have a great influence on the high 
ductility of 4Mn.

4. Conclusions
In this study, the microstructures and tensile properties 
of Fe–Al–Ni–Cr–Mo–Mn alloys with B2-type NiAl precipi-
tates were examined focusing on the effect of Mn addition. 
The following conclusions are drawn:
(1) After annealing at 1 223 K for 1 h followed by AC, 
0Mn and 2Mn exhibit a coarse equiaxed 
/uni03B1 grain microstruc-
ture, while 4Mn has a microstructure composed of coarse 
equiaxed 
/uni03B1 grains and fine grain regions that include /uni03B1 and 
/uni03B3 grains.
(2) The fine grain regions of annealed 4Mn are formed 
by the phase transformation between /uni03B1 and /uni03B3 phases, which 
involves the precipitation and dissolution of the (Fe, Cr)2Mo 
Laves phase, during annealing at 1 223 K followed by AC. 
In particular, the Laves phase acts as a nucleation site for 
the fine 
/uni03B1 grains in the prior fcc regions and suppresses 
the grain growth of the /uni03B1 grains due to the grain boundary 
pinning effect.
(3) The annealed alloys contain the NiAl precipitates 
with a D p of approximately 25 nm. The influence of Mn 
addition on the morphology of the NiAl precipitates is insig-
nificant. T
c of the NiAl for these alloys is higher than opera-
tion temperature of A-USC thermal power plants (973 K).
(4) The annealed 4Mn exhibited high /uni03C30.2 (approxi-
mately 907 MPa) and large elongation (approximately 
14.7%) at RT. The suppression of brittle intergranular frac-
ture due to formation of fine grain regions is responsible for 
improving large ductility at RT.
(5) The annealed 4Mn shows higher strength than the 
modified 9Cr-1Mo steel and the 316 stainless steel in all 
temperature ranges from RT to 973 K. This is because NiAl 
precipitates exist stably without changing the morphology 
up to 973 K.
