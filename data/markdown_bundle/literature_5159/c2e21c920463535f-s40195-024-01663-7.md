Phase and Mechanism of Its Portevin–Le Chatelier (PLC) Effect

Abstract
In this study, the high-temperature stability and the generation mechanism of the Portevin–Le Chatelier (PLC) effect in 
solid-solution Mg–1Al–12Y alloy with different heat treatment processes were investigated by adjusting the content of long-
period stacking ordered (LPSO) phases. It was found that the content of LPSO phases in the alloys differed the most after 
heat treatment at 530 °C for 16 h and 24 h, with values of 13.56% and 3.93% respectively. Subsequently, high-temperature 
tensile experiments were conducted on these two alloys at temperatures of 150 °C, 200 °C, 250 °C, and 300 °C. The results 
showed that both alloys exhibited the PLC effect at temperatures ranging from 150 to 250 °C. However, at a temperature 
300 °C, only the alloy with a greater concentration of LPSO phases exhibited the PLC effect, whereas the alloy with a lower 
proportion of LPSO phases did not exhibit this phenomenon. Additionally, both alloys exhibited remarkable high-temperature 
stability, with the alloy containing a greater percentage of LPSO phases also demonstrating superior strength. The underly-
ing mechanism for this phenomenon lies in the exceptional high-temperature stability exhibited by the second phase within 
the alloy. Furthermore, the LPSO phase effectively obstructs the movement of dislocations, and it also undergoing kinking 
to facilitate plastic deformation of the alloy. The results indicate that the PLC effect can be suppressed by reducing disloca-
tion pile-up at grain boundaries, which leads to a decrease in alloy plasticity but an increase in strength. The presence of the 
PLC effect in the WA121 alloy is attributed to the abundant dispersed second phase within the alloy, which initially hinders 
the movement of dislocations, leading to an increase in stress, and subsequently releases the dislocations, allowing them to 
continue their movement and thereby reducing in stress.

1 Introduction
In recent times, the use of magnesium (Mg) alloys has sig-
nificantly increased in various industries such as automotive, 
aerospace, medical devices, energy storage, and others. This 
is attributed to their exceptional characteristics including 
high specific stiffness, low density, excellent biocompat-
ibility, and superior hydrogen storage capacity [1 –4]. Nev-
ertheless, Mg alloys exhibit inherent limitations in terms 
of strength and poor plasticity, particularly when subjected 
to high-temperature conditions, which constrains their pro-
spective applications. Alloying is a successful approach to 
enhance the mechanical characteristics of Mg alloys [ 5]. 
Among the numerous elements that can be used for alloying 
with Mg, rare earth (RE) elements have demonstrated their 
effectiveness in improving the mechanical properties of Mg 
alloys [3, 5]. The addition of RE elements to Mg alloys has 
given rise to the development of the LPSO phase, which has 
emerged as a prominent precipitate phase in Mg-RE alloys, 
drawing substantial research interest. An increasing body 
of research has demonstrated that the LPSO phase exhibits 
high hardness, elastic modulus, exceptional thermal stabil-
ity, impediment to dislocation movement, and inhibition of

grain boundary migration. Consequently, the presence of the 
LPSO phase enhances the strength, stiffness, damping per -

tensile strength (UTS) of the extruded Mg94Ni3Y3 (at.%) 
alloy, which exhibits the LPSO phase, at room temperature is 
510 MPa. The main reason for this high UTS is the low vol-
ume fraction of non-DRXed grains and the existence of the 
LPSO phase. In summary, the presence of the LPSO phase 
significantly enhances the mechanical properties of Mg 
alloys, thereby playing a crucial role in their improvement.
However, with the addition of RE elements, plastic defor-
mation instability occurs in Mg alloys, usually referred to 
as the Portevin–Le Chatelier (PLC) effect, which is charac-
terized by stress serration phenomenon in the stress–strain 
curve [13]. This phenomenon has been observed in a variety 
of alloy systems, including steel, aluminum, Mg, copper, and 
titanium alloys [14]. Extensive investigations have been con-
ducted on this phenomenon in various materials, including 
steel, copper, and aluminum alloys. Based on findings from 
various studies, it is widely accepted that the PLC effect is a 
result of the interplay between dislocations and precipitates 
[15]. However, other studies suggest that solute atoms in the 
alloy can pin dislocations through lattice diffusion, result-
ing in the PLC effect [16]. Subsequent studies have shown 
that solute atoms have a lower diffusion rate compared to 
the mobility rate of dislocations, suggesting that solute 
atoms may not effectively hinder the movement of disloca-
tions [17]. Only by increasing strain can the ability of solute 
atoms to impede dislocations be enhanced. In summary, the 
underlying mechanism responsible for the occurrence of the 
PLC effect remains uncertain. Furthermore, in recent years, 
the study of the PLC effect in Mg alloys has gained atten-
tion. Despite numerous proposed mechanisms, there is still a 
limited understanding of the fundamental mechanism behind 
the PLC effect and its impact on the mechanical proper -
ties of alloys. As a result, more comprehensive research is 
required, considering the unique characteristics of each alloy 
system.
Mg–Al alloys have garnered significant interest among 
researchers due to their exceptional mechanical proper -
ties, ability to be cast, and cost-effectiveness. However, the 
main second phase in Mg–Al alloys is β-Mg 17Al12, which 
exhibits poor high-temperature stability, limiting its applica-
tions at high temperatures [18]. The addition of Y element 
in Mg–Al alloys has been found to not only suppress the 
formation of β-Mg17Al12, but also promote the formation of 
 Al2Y and LPSO phases [19], both of which exhibit excel-
lent high-temperature stability. This can lead to an effec -
tive enhancement in the mechanical properties of Mg–Al 
alloys at both room temperature and elevated temperatures. 
So far, multiple types of LPSO phases, including 10H, 12R, 
14H, 18R, and 24R [20], have been identified in various 
systems such as Mg–Zn–Y [ 21, 22], Mg–Cu–Y [ 23, 24], 
Mg–Zn–Gd [25, 26], and Mg–Mn–Gd [27]. After subjecting 
the Mg–1Al–12Y (wt%) alloy to a 2-h solution treatment at 
540 °C, Yuan et al. found that the 18R-LPSO phase formed 
within the grain interior [28]. Later, Chen et al. analyzed the 
LPSO phase produced in Mg–Al–Y (at.%) alloys with dif-
ferent Al to Y ratios after annealing at 450 °C, 500 °C, and 
550 °C for 30 or 60 days, followed by water quenching. They 
found that the thermally stable LPSO phases produced in the 
final Mg–Al–Y alloys were 18R-LPSO and 10H-LPSO [29]. 
It has been reported that the LPSO phase in the Mg–Al–Y 
system can suppress the formation of β-Mg 17Al12 and 
 Mg24Y5 phases, thereby improving the high-temperature 
mechanical stability of magnesium alloys [30, 31].
Nevertheless, the investigation into the impact of the 
LPSO phase on the high-temperature mechanical proper -
ties of Mg–Al–Y alloys remains an ongoing process. Conse-
quently, the control of LPSO phase content and distribution 
within the alloy emerges as a pivotal factor in the prepara-
tion of Mg–Al–Y alloys with exceptional high-temperature 
mechanical performance. Since the precipitation behavior 
in alloys is often highly dependent on accurate thermody -
namic phase diagrams, the calculation of phase diagram 
(CALPHAD) method is an important approach. Therefore, 
using the phase diagram of Mg-Y binary alloy plotted by 
Meier et al. [32], this study first designed different heat treat-
ment processes to control the content of the LPSO phase in 
Mg–Al–Y alloys. Subsequently, mechanical property tests 
were performed on alloys with vary LPSO contents, reveal-
ing the presence of the PLC effect during tensile experi-
ments on Mg–Al–Y alloys. As far as we are aware, no prior 
research has explored the occurrence of the PLC effect 
within the Mg–Al–Y alloy system.
Consequently, in this study, high-temperature tensile 
experiments were carried out on Mg–Al–Y alloys with 
varying LPSO contents to examine the impact of the LPSO 
phase on the high-temperature mechanical properties of the 
alloys, determine the mechanism behind the occurrence of 
PLC effect, and assess its influence on the microstructure 
of the alloys.
2 Experimental
The Mg–1Al–12Y (wt%) alloy employed in the experiments 
was prepared by melting high-purity materials, including 
pure Mg (99.99 wt%), pure Al (99.99 wt%), and an inter -
mediate alloy Mg–30Y (wt%), under a protective atmos-
phere composed of Ar and SF6 using a resistance furnace. 
The molten alloy at 750 °C was poured into a preheated 
steel mold at 200 °C. The mold had dimensions of ϕ500 
mm × 1000 mm. Subsequently, wire cutting method was 
used to prepare the required specimens for subsequent

D8-Advance A25) with a copper target, employing a scan-

elemental composition distribution of the alloy was deter -
mined using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, 
Merlin compact). The samples used were derived from 
the central portion of the ingots, cut in a direction perpen-
dicular to the casting orientation, utilizing an electrical 
discharge wire cutting machine. The samples had dimen-
sions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 2.5 mm. For XRD experiments, 
the sample surfaces were polished using sandpaper from 
coarse to fine grits until no visible scratches were present. 
Subsequently, electrolytic polishing was performed using 
a 7% perchloric acid/ethanol solution at −40 °C, with a 
polishing voltage of 25 V and a polishing current of 0.3 A, 
for SEM imaging and EDS analysis. After electropolish -
ing, the samples were etched with a conventional mix of 
picric acid solution for optical microscopy imaging pur -
poses. For transmission electron microscope (TEM) sam-
ples, they were first thinned to a thickness of 40 μm using 
sandpaper. Afterwards, further thinning was performed 
using a twin-jet electrolytic thinning instrument with a 
4% perchloric acid/ethanol solution as the electrolyte solu-
tion at 33 V and −35 °C, with a transparency setting of 35. 
Next, the morphology of the LPSO phase in the samples 
thinned by the twin-jet electrochemical thinning device 
was determined using a JEM-2100F TEM with an operat-
ing voltage of 200 kV.
Subsequently, high-temperature tensile tests were con-
ducted on the heat-treated alloys using a 100 kN high-
temperature electronic universal material testing machine 
(Instron-5982, Instron) at a strain rate of 0.2 mm/min. The 
tensile specimens were cut from the ingot perpendicular 
to the casting direction. All samples displayed a dog bone 
shape, measuring 15 mm in gage length, 4 mm in width, 
and 2.5 mm in thickness. The tensile tests of the samples 
were conducted at temperatures of 150 °C, 200 °C, 250 °C, 
and 300 °C, with all samples being kept at the target tem-
perature for 10 min. Three samples were tested for each 
parameter, and the median results were used. The post-
tensile fracture surfaces were utilized for subsequent SEM 
and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD, Oxford Instru-
ments C-Nano +) analysis to characterize the fracture mor -
phology and microstructure in the vicinity of the fracture. 
The EBSD experiments used a step size of 1.7 μm, and all 
EBSD data were analyzed using AZtecCrystal software.

4 Discussion
The experimental findings indicated that the grain size, Al2Y 
phase content, and distribution within the alloy remained 
relatively consistent across various heat treatment processes. 
Nevertheless, significant variations were observed in the 
content of the formed LPSO phase. Subsequent high-tem-
perature tensile tests were conducted on two alloys: F530-16, 
characterized by a higher LPSO phase content, and F530-24, 
which had a relatively lower LPSO phase content. These 
tests demonstrated that F530-16 exhibited higher mechanical 
properties compared to F530-24, accompanied by a stronger 
PLC effect. In the following sections, we will delve into the 
underlying reasons for these changes in alloy microstruc-
ture and examine the role of the second phase during tensile 
deformation.

lead to a delay in dynamic recovery due to solute drag or

of the PLC effect can result in a substantial quantity of dis-
locations within the alloy. Dislocation motion on basal and 
prismatic planes usually carries larger plastic strains, while 
the PLC effect inhibits basal slip but activates non-basal 
slip, resulting in a considerably higher accumulation of dis-
location density within grains subjected to the PLC effect 
as opposed to those that are not. This means that due to 
the PLC effect and the limitations of dynamic recovery, the 
F530-16 alloy stretched at 300 °C will have a significantly 
higher GNDs density than the F530-24 alloy. This is consist-
ent with the data shown in Table  2. Upon examining both 
alloys subjected to stretching at 250 °C, it becomes appar -
ent that the F530-16 alloy, featuring a higher LPSO phase 
content, exhibits significantly elevated GNDs compared to 
the F530-24 alloy, which possesses a lower LPSO phase 
content at the same temperature. This is because the LPSO 
phase, as a good barrier to hinder dislocation motion, causes 
stress concentration.
Furthermore, upon analyzing the GNDs maps of alloys 
both with and without PLC effect, it is evident that the dis-
location density at grain boundaries in alloys exhibiting the 
PLC effect is considerably lower than in alloys lacking the 
PLC effect. A substantial accumulation of dislocations is 
noticeable at the grain boundaries in alloys without PLC 
effect, as depicted in Fig.  10h. Wang et al. [40] found that 
the mechanism of grain boundary rotation is dislocation 
climb, rather than grain boundary sliding or diffusion creep. 
In alloys with PLC effect, the presence of LPSO phase and 
its effective hindrance to dislocations lead to a significant 
increase in dislocation density within the grains, while the 
dislocation accumulation at the grain boundaries is minimal, 
which inhibits grain rotation. On the contrary, in alloys with-
out PLC effect, a substantial accumulation of dislocation 
occurs at the grain boundaries, which hinders the inhibition 
of grain rotation. As shown in Fig.  10c, g, the grains of the 
F530-16 alloy stretched at 300 °C also undergo rotation, 
and there is also a large accumulation of dislocations at the 
grain boundaries. This is attributed to the fact that the alloy 
experiences a minimal PLC effect at 300 °C, resulting in a 
relatively modest impact on grain rotation. It is well known 
that grain rotation plays a crucial role in coordinating the 
plastic deformation of the alloy, and the suppression of grain 
rotation leads to a reduction in the alloy’s plasticity, aligning 
with the experimental results.
To determine the primary slip systems that were acti-
vated in various heat-treated alloys following deformation 
at different temperatures, an in-grain misorientation axes 
(IGMA) analysis was carried out using the EBSD data. This 
analysis offers insights into the local lattice rotations gener-
ated by dislocation slip [41]. In this study, an IGMA was 
carried out using orientation angles spanning from 0.5° to

4.2 Effect of the Second Phase on the Properties
According to Sun et al. [ 46], the electronegativity values 
of Mg, Al, and Y are 1.31, 1.61, and 1.22, respectively. 
Thus, it can be deduced that the propensity for Y and Al to 
undergo a chemical reaction and form compounds is more 
favorable compared to the propensity for Mg and Al to react. 
In addition, there are studies that have calculated the sta-
bility of Al-Y using first-principles calculations [47], and 
the results show that Al2Y phase has a stronger formability 
than Al3Y phase. Therefore, the combination of Al and Y 
will ultimately form a stable Al2Y phase. Whether it is a 
cast alloy (Fig.  2a) or an alloy subjected to various heat 
treatment procedures (Fig.  4), the distribution of the Al2Y 
phase within the alloy is random, rather than being con-
fined to grain boundaries. This occurrence can be attributed 
to the favorable lattice match between the Al2Y phase and 
the α-Mg matrix [ 48], which facilitates the Al2Y phase to 
serve as a suitable nucleation site for the α-Mg matrix. This 
 Al2Y phase is mostly distributed in the grain matrix, and 
Hao et al. [49] believed that this distribution method can 
provide good strengthening by reducing its cleavage action 
on grain boundaries.
According to the image presented in Fig.  8, the pres-
ence of the Al2Y phase on the alloy’s fracture surface can 
be classified into two distinct types: deformation, denoted 
by the orange boxes, and fracture, indicated by the red 
boxes. The deformation of the Al2Y phase can enhance 
the formability of the WA121 alloy. The Al2Y phase is a 
hard particle with a high melting point, and it is harder 
than the matrix [50]. It can effectively impede disloca-
tions, thereby enhancing the strength of the alloy, through 
its strong pinning effect. However, the presence of hard 
secondary phases in the matrix usually leads to local stress 
concentration, which is detrimental to formability [51]. 
The occurrence of cracks in the Al2Y phase within the 
alloy suggests that the Al2Y phase inherently impedes dis-
locations movement. When stress concentrated reaches a 
certain threshold, the Al2Y phase experiences cracking. 
The presence of the Al2Y phase serves as an effective 
means to augment the alloy’s strength.

WA121 alloy, can enhance the strength and formability of 
the alloy by inhibiting the movement of dislocations while 
undergoing kinking deformation [28]. In the WA121 alloy,

4.3 Impact of the LPSO Phase on the PLC Effect
A schematic diagram illustrating the obstruction of dis-
location motion by various second phases in the alloy is 
presented in Fig. 15, based on the above discussion. Due to 
the hardness of the second phases in the alloy, dislocation 
movement is hindered when it reaches the second phases, 
causing the dislocation to bypass the second phases and 
continue to move due to the occurrence of the Orowan 
mechanism [60]. When the dislocation reaches an impen-
etrable second phase, contact and bending occur (pinning 
process), which leads to an increase in stress. The degree 
of bending increases further as the stress increases until 
the dislocation escapes the obstruction of the second phase 
(unpinning process), resulting in a reduction in stress. 
After the dislocation continues to move for a distance, 
it is obstructed by the second phase again, repeating the 
above process. This is the main reason for the occurrence 
of Type A and Type C stress serrations in Fig.  7.
From Fig.  7, it can be observed that as the tempera-
ture increases, the type of PLC effect gradually changes 
from Type A to C, and finally weakens to almost invis-
ible. For Type A stress serrations, the pinning and unpin-
ning processes described in Fig.  15 are the cause of their 
occurrence. For Type C stress serrations, the load transfer 
between the LPSO phase and the α-Mg matrix weakens 
due to the increase in deformation temperature [61], lead-
ing to softening. This can be observed in the stress–strain 
curve of the F530-16–250 alloy (Type C). In the early 
stage of tension, most of the serrations are discontinuous, 
which means that the unpinning process of the dislocation 
is very difficult. In other words, during high-temperature 
deformation, the dislocation will continue to move for a 
distance due to the twisting of the LPSO phase during 
the unpinning from the LPSO phase. At the same time, 
the resistance to slip of the LPSO phase decreases with 
increasing temperature [61]. This is why only alloys with 
a higher LPSO phase content exhibit the PLC effect at 
300 °C, and very weak stress serrations can be observed 
at this temperature.

5 Conclusions
The aim of this study was to regulate the quantity of the 
LPSO phase within the initial WA121 alloy through the 
utilization of diverse heat treatment methods. Subse -
quently, the mechanical properties and microstructure of 
two alloys with the most significant disparity in LPSO 
phase content were assessed following distinct heat treat-
ment processes. The relationship between the PLC effect 
and texture, the influence of the second phase on the 
mechanical properties of the alloy, and the influence of 
the LPSO phase on the PLC effect were analyzed. The 
main results of this study are as follows:
1. The grain size of the WA121 alloy did not undergo a sig-
nificant increase following various heat treatment pro-
cesses. Furthermore, the content and distribution of the 
 Al2Y phase remained relatively unchanged, with notable 
alteration observed solely in the LPSO phase content. 
The highest LPSO phase content, reaching 13.56%, 
was observed in the WA121 alloy that underwent cool-
ing within the furnace following 16 h of insulation at 
530 °C.
2. The stress–strain curves of the F530-16 and F530-24 
alloys, featuring varying LPSO phase contents, exhibited 
PLC effects at 150 °C, 200 °C and 250 °C. However, it is 
worth noting that only the F530-16 alloy, characterized 
by a higher LPSO phase content, exhibited a showed 
PLC effects at 300 °C. In alloys experiencing the PLC 
effect, the robust hindrance of LPSO phases to disloca-
tion motion is responsible for a significant increase in 
dislocation density within the grain interior, while dis-
location accumulations at grain boundaries are relatively 
sparse. This phenomenon results in the suppression of 
grain rotation.
3. The remarkable high-temperature stability of the WA121 
alloy can be attributed to the effective impediment of 
dislocation motion by both the Al2Y and LPSO phases at 
elevated temperatures. Additionally, the PLC effect plays 
a role in inhibiting grain rotation. Simultaneously, the 
LPSO phase accommodates plastic deformation of the 
alloy by undergoing kinking. The strength contributed 
by the LPSO phase was quantitatively assessed, reveal-
ing that the LPSO phase continues to provide significant 
strength at high temperatures. This is the reason for the 
WA121 alloy’s capacity to maintain favorable mechani-
cal properties under elevated temperatures conditions.
4. The occurrence of PLC effects is due to the hindering 
effect of a large amount of dispersed second-phase par-
ticles on dislocation motion, which triggers the Orowan 
mechanism to allow for continuous dislocation motion. 
The reason why only alloys with a high content of LPSO 
phase showed PLC effects at 300 °C is that the hindering 
effect of the LPSO phase on slip decreases with increas-
ing temperature, resulting in the inability of the second 
phase in alloys with a low content of LPSO phase to 
effectively anchor the dislocations.
