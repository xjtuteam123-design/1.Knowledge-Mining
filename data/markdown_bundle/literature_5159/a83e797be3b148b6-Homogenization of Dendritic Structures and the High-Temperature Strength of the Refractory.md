H.B. HENDERSON, T. VOISIN, A.P. PERRON, S.K. MCCALL, and J.T. MCKEOWN
MoNbTaVW, a pioneering refractory high-entropy alloy (RHEA), is renowned for its
exceptional strength at elevated temperatures. Like most RHEAs, it solidiﬁes into a dendritic
microstructure with steep concentration gradients, necessitating heat treatment for equilibra-
tion. Steep concentration gradients typically complicate mechanical property analysis and
modeling, key challenges for high-throughput alloy discovery. This study examines the eﬀects of
high-temperature homogenization (1800 /C176 C for 8 hours) on the microstructure and mechanical
properties of MoNbTaVW across temperatures up to 1200 /C176 C. Postmortem microstructural
analysis, coupled with chemical, crystallographic, and mechanical assessments, revealed that
yield strength was surprisingly insensitive to homogenization, with less than a 3 pct diﬀerence
between as-cast and heat-treated materials. Nanoindentation conﬁrmed minimal nanohardness
changes across the dendritic structure at room temperature. However, homogenization
signiﬁcantly enhanced high-temperature work hardening, producing higher peak compressive
strength. Both as-cast and homogenized MoNbTaVW exhibited room-temperature strengths of
1400 MPa, exceeding previously reported values. These ﬁndings demonstrate that high-tem-
perature treatment enables microstructural homogenization without compromising strength, a
unique behavior among RHEAs. While not universally applicable, this insight highlights the
importance of understanding microstructural development in experimental alloys and oﬀers a
pathway to design alloys that achieve homogeneous-like properties without heat treatment.

I. INTRODUCTION
HIGH-TEMPERATURE structural alloys are an
enabling technology for applications that crosscut the
energy, aerospace, and defense industries. A promising
avenue leading toward the development of these
next-generation alloys is the investigation of refractory
high-entropy alloys (RHEAs).
[1–6] Presently, most inves-
tigations conducted on RHEAs are based on as-cast
microstructures due to the high temperatures (in excess
of 1500 /C176 C) required to homogenize the solidiﬁcation
microstructures in Mo, Ta, and W-containing alloys.
[3]
RHEAs, especially those with high melting-point
variability or reduced solubility among the constituent
elements, tend to solidify dendritically with signiﬁcant
interdendritic chemical segregation occurring. [7] Lower
melting-temperature elements ( < 2500 /C176 C) like Ti, Zr,
Cr, V, Hf, and Nb tend to concentrate in the interden-
dritic regions, while high-melting-temperature elements
(> 2500 /C176 C) like Mo, Ta, and especially W concentrate
in the dendrites. While the microstructure in many cases
maintains a single-phase body-centered cubic (BCC)
microstructure, the eﬀect of what is often a steep
composition gradient within the microstructure on the
mechanical performance is not well understood for
refractory RHEAs.
In this work, we investigate the equiatomic, sin-
gle-phase, and BCC alloy MoNbTaVW for its excep-
tional high-temperature strength (greater than 800 MPa
yield strength at 1000 /C176 C).
[3,4] While there have been no
studies focusing on the homogenization of as-cast
MoNbTaVW speciﬁcally, work has examined homoge-
nized MoNbTaW after a heat treatment at 1800 /C176 C for
168 hours in the context of nanomechanical properties [8]
and more recently at 2000 /C176 C for 24 hours, in which
particular attention was placed on short-range

II. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
A. Synthesis and Heat Treatment
High-purity elemental Mo, Nb, Ta, V, and W were
melted together in an Arcast200 Casting Arc Melter in
an ultra-high-purity (UHP) Ar atmosphere after three
vacuum purge and UHP Ar backﬁll cycles. The ~ 200 g
alloyed button was ﬂipped and remelted three times
while being magnetically stirred. The as-cast button was
sectioned for chemical analysis using a cubic boron
nitride blade in a high-speed water-cooled saw. Chem-
ical analysis of the as-cast material was performed by
IMR Test Labs. O content was measured via inert gas
fusion while the quantity of other elements was deter-
mined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission
spectrometry following the ASTM E 1409-13 and
CAP-017U standards, respectively. The results of the
analysis are detailed in Table I. Note the relatively low
oxygen content of 0.15 at. pct (200 wppm). Test
cylinders were electrical discharge machined (EDM)
from the button and the EDM recast layer was ground
away to a 600-grit surface ﬁnish prior to heat treatment
and testing.
The homogenization heat treatment was performed in
a Thermal Technology SE 503 tungsten refractory
furnace. Test cylinders, a W crucible, and W foil
wrapping were triple solvent sonicated in (1) acetone,
(2) isopropyl alcohol, and (3) methanol to remove
surface contamination. The W foil wrapped test cylin-
ders were placed in the W crucible and, after three
vacuum purge and UHP He backﬁll cycles, heated in a
ﬂowing UHP He atmosphere to 1800 /C176 C at a rate of
20 /C176 C min
/C0 1 and held at 1800 /C176 C for 8 hours before
cooling back to RT at 20 /C176 C min /C0 1. In order to avoid
the sublimation of V from MoNbTaVW during heat
treatment in vacuum over 1400 /C176 C, it was also impor-
tant to run at or above atmospheric pressure. [14] Oxygen
is a well-known embrittling agent in refractory alloys. In
addition to being a potent solid solution strengthener,
even trace amounts of oxygen can segregate to grain
boundaries or form oxide precipitates, which signiﬁ-
cantly reduce ductility and fracture toughness. Numer-
ous studies documented that oxygen contamination
leads to intergranular fracture and a marked increase
in brittle behavior, particularly at lower tempera-
tures.
[22–24] As a result, strict control of oxygen content
during alloy processing and handling is essential to
maintain the mechanical integrity of refractory alloys.
The cleanliness of the furnace was veriﬁed in prior heat
treatments of CrMoNbV and TaW alloys, which both
showed no measurable increase in O content by inert gas
fusion analysis after 8 hours at 1800 /C176 C.
[7]

IV. RESULTS/DISCUSSION
A. Microstructural Characterization
The microstructure of the alloy was observed before
and after homogenization at 1800 /C176 C for 8 hours.
Figures 1 summarizes the light optical microscopy
(LOM) (a and g), backscatter electron (BSE) imaging
(b and h), EDS (c to e and i to k), and XRD (f and l)
pre- and post-heat treatment. Focusing in on the LOM
in Figures 1(a) and (g), several features are immediately
apparent:
1. The grain morphology is representative of a cast
structure. The grains near the bottom of both
imaged cylinders (nearest the water-cooled Cu
hearth) are small and equiaxed, and toward the
top of the samples, the grains begin to elongate in
the direction of heat flow creating a more columnar
solidification morphology. The same grain struc-
ture/morphologies of grains exist in the homoge-
nized sample, which implies some amount of
microstructural stability even after 8 hours at
1800 /C176 C. This builds on the knowledge of previous
studies which have shown microstructural stability
in thin films of MoNbTaVW up to 1 hour at
1600 /C176 C.
[14]

2. Grain size varies greatly throughout the as-cast
button depending on the relative distance of the
region to the hearth and how many passes/remelts
an area received during the arc melting process. A
formal grain size comparison before and after
homogenization is not presented due to the large
variability in grain size observed across samples,
though it is expected that some amount of grain
growth occurred during the heat treatment. Gener-
ally, grain size was measured to be between 50 and
300 lm in both conditions.
3. Significant porosity is present throughout the but-
ton, especially in the interdendritic regions. The
interdendritic liquid is last to solidify and can be
deprived of liquid as the surrounding material
solidifies and contracts leaving shrinkage porosity.
The larger 20 to 50 lm pores may have been due to
turbulence and/or lack of fusion during the melting/
re-melting process. In the homogenized sample, an
increase in the pore density could also be driven by
the Kirkendall effect.
[9] During interdiffusion, the
light species V in the interdendrite region may
diffuse more rapidly than the heavy species W in the
dendrite. The difference in diffusion rates could lead
to a net flux of vacancies toward the interdendrite,
resulting in the nucleation and growth of Kirkendall
voids at the interface. The formation of Kirkendall
voids is a noted problem in W-Ta and W-V binary
alloys where the voids cause a substantial decrease
in fracture toughness.
[28] While the compression
tests in this work are relatively insensitive to defects
like the observed porosity, these types of defects will
need to be processed out of the material before
appreciable tensile performance can be achieved.
Moving to examine the BSE imaging Figures 1(b) and
(h), the Z-contrast provided by the technique shows in
(b) the brighter, higher Z dendrites that are ﬁrst to
solidify are surrounded by the darker lower Z interden-
dritic regions. Some evidence of the dendritic structure is
still visible in the homogenized sample (h) indicating
that complete chemical homogenization was not
achieved even with the high-temperature heat treatment.
This is in line with earlier studies on similar composi-
tions
[9] 2000 /C176 C for 24 hours, [29] 1400 /C176 C for 19 hours,
and[8] 1800 /C176 C for 7 days which all showed partial
homogenization. However, the contrast is signiﬁcantly
reduced and the boundaries between the dendritic and
interdendritic regions have become diﬀuse. Both images
contain examples of interdendritic porosity, indicated by
the outlined arrows. The porosity in (h) provides insight
into where the interdendritic regions existed prior to
homogenization.
EDS data of the as-cast sample shown in Figures 1(c)
through (e) corroborate the observations from the BSE
images. (c and d) are EDS composition maps of V and
W, respectively, from the outlined region shown in (b),
while (e) is the composition data from the EDS line scan
indicated by the white arrow in (b). The composition
maps visually conﬁrm dendrites are enriched in W and
depleted of V with the opposite true for the interden-
dritic regions. The line scan across the dendrite arms
further elaborates on the trends, showing the dendrites
are enriched with high-density, high melting-point Ta
and W and depleted in the lower-density, lower melt-
ing-temperature Nb and V. Conversely, the interden-
dritic region is enriched in V and Nb and depleted in Ta
and W. The distribution of Mo, a medium-density
refractory metal, was relatively uniform throughout the
microstructure.
Diﬀuse segregation of V and W is evident throughout
the scanned region in (i and j). A comparison of the line
scans (e and k) shows that the mean maximum chemical
variation across the dendritic and interdendritic regions
prior to homogenization was 13 ± 4 at pct V and 14 ± 3
at pct W. After homogenization, the mean maximum
chemical variation dropped to 2 ± 1 at pct V and 5 ± 3
at pct W, representing a substantial decrease in the
dendritic segregation present prior to heat treatment.
Additionally, the ‘‘wavelength’’ of the compositional
variation also increased signiﬁcantly from about 10 to
50 lm resulting in a more gradual compositional change
in addition to the decrease in the mean maximum
diﬀerence.
X-ray diﬀraction pattern of both the as-cast and
homogenized conditions are shown in Figures 1(f) and
(l) and indicate the samples maintained the anticipated
BCC crystal structure with no secondary phases
detected. The lattice parameter of the as-cast and
homogenized alloys was a
ac = 3.197 and ah = 3.194,
respectively. The fully bcc microstructure and lattice
parameters of MoNbTaVW are consistent with previous
results in literature
[29] which reported a lattice parameter
of a = 3.1832. The larger lattice parameter determined
in this study could be due to the more Ta-rich
composition used in this study (19.9 vs 15.6 at pct).
The small peaks at 29 deg and around 48 deg are
artifacts of the mounting media used to secure the
specimens.
Overall, while the as-cast grain structure remained
similar through the heat treatment, signiﬁcant diﬀusion
was able to occur across the dendritic microstructure
over 8 hours at 1800 /C176 C, leading to a more homoge-
neous composition.
B. Bulk Mechanical Characterization
Bulk mechanical characterization was used to under-
stand how the changes in microstructure after homog-
enization altered material properties and performance.
Figure 2 summarizes the data gathered from compres-
sive thermomechanical characterization. Figure 2(a)
shows the compliance-corrected engineering stress vs
engineering strain curves for compression tests at room
temperature (RT), 1000 /C176 C, and 1200 /C176 C for the as-cast
(blue) and homogenized (red) alloy. The immediate
feature of note in Figure 2(a) is that the compressive
yield strength of MoNbTaVW appears insensitive to
homogenization across all temperatures. The trend is
further apparent in Figure 2(b) where 0.2 pct oﬀset yield
strength is plotted as a function of temperature. At each
temperature, there is less than a 30 MPa diﬀerence in
yield strength between the as-cast and homogenized test
cylinders.

A potential explanation for the invariance of yield
strength between the as-cast and homogenized material
is a concession between solid solution strengthening and
the Hall–Petch eﬀect. As the grain growth occurs during
the homogenization heat treatment, the strength of the
alloy is reduced, while the interdiﬀusion of the inter-
dendritic and dendritic regions may lead to an increase
in strength. If these two strengthening mechanisms
counterbalance one another, it could lead to the
appearance of a static yield strength across both
conditions. However, the Hall–Petch relationship pre-
dicts diminishing returns at larger grain sizes like those
observed for both conditions. Due to large grain sizes
present throughout both conditions (50 to300 lm), the
Hall–Petch eﬀect is disregarded as a major contributor
to the yield strengths observed.
Focusing on the RT compression tests, the as-cast and
homogenized samples diﬀer quite signiﬁcantly from
measurements reported in literature. The stress/strain
curve reported in Senkov et al.
[29] shows a yield strength
of 1246 MPa and work hardening to a peak compressive
strain of 1270 MPa followed by failure at 0.017 strain.
In this work, the as-cast and homogenized samples were
observed to have much higher yield strengths and
compressive strains to failure (1410 and 1400 MPa and
0.06 and 0.04 compressive strain to failure, respectively).
The diﬀerence in yield strengths observed between
studies is likely due to the more stochastic nature of
brittle failure mechanisms that are observed in MoN-
bTaVW at RT and highlights the important challenge of
creating low porosity, homogeneous material either
through controlled casting practices or thermomechan-
ical processing.
The yield strengths measured at 1000 /C176 C and 1200 /C176 C
agree with previously reported literature values. The
slightly higher ( ~ 75 MPa) strength reported at 1200 /C176 C
may be due to a slight diﬀerence in Ta content between
studies (19.9 at pct in this work vs 15.6 at pct in
Reference 29), the higher strain rate used in this work (8

A consistent and obvious eﬀect of homogenization
was the change in the work hardening rate at elevated
temperatures. In contrast to the as-cast material, the
homogenized samples show a signiﬁcant increase in
work hardening behavior, achieving peak compressive
strengths 200 MPa higher than the as-cast samples while
yield strength remained unchanged (Figure 2). While the
exact mechanism responsible for this observed behavior
is not the focus of this work, there are several potential
avenues for future investigation to determine the under-
lying mechanism. Some possibilities include homoge-
nization leading to the relief of residual stress present
from solidiﬁcation. The reduction in geometrically
necessary dislocations could allow for a diﬀerent dislo-
cation structure to form in the as-cast vs the homoge-
nized material.
[30,31] Another possibility could be an
increase in the solute pinning eﬀect after homogeniza-
tion leading to a reduction in kink migration. [32] At RT,

V. CONCLUSIONS
This work aimed to elucidate the eﬀect of homoge-
nization on the microstructural and mechanical proper-
ties of the RHEA MoNbTaVW. Key ﬁndings include:
/C15 High-temperature homogenization at 1800 /C176 C for
8 hours successfully reduced interdendritic segrega-
tion of the sluggishly diffusing element W from a
peak-to-trough gradient of 14 ± 3t o5 ± 3 at pct and
increased the length scale of dendritic microstructure
significantly.
/C15 Despite the drastic change in compositional distri-
bution the yield strength of MoNbTaVW was shown
to be insensitive to homogenization from RT to
1200 /C176 C. This is a coincidental feature of MoN-
bTaVW and not generalizable to RHEAs.
/C15 A room-temperature yield strength of 1400 MPa
was measured, higher than previously reported in
literature.[29]
/C15 Homogenization led to a significant increase
(200 MPa) in peak compressive strength at 1000 /C176 C
and 1200 /C176 C, compared to as-cast material, indicat-
ing a distinct change in the work hardening behavior
of MoNbTaVW.
/C15 Postmortem microstructural analysis showed similar
deformation mechanisms (kink band formation and
slip lines) were active in both the as-cast and
homogenized materials at 1000 /C176 C and that defor-
mation was not localized to the interdendritic
regions.
/C15 Nanoindentation and modeling revealed that there is
no significant strength variation between interden-
dritic and dendritic regions despite the pronounced
micro-segregation present in the as-cast material.
Additionally, there is less than a 20 MPa change in
strength between the as-cast and homogenized
material at all temperatures studied in this work
(RT, 1000 /C176 C, and 1200 /C176 C).
/C15 The MC model underpredicted the strength of
MoNbTaVW by about 250 MPa at the elevated
temperatures in this work, alluding to the contribu-
tion of strengthening mechanisms aside from edge
dislocation glide and interaction.
Homogenizing to such high temperatures, especially
for bulk parts, poses signiﬁcant challenges for some
industries. Therefore, the insensitivity of the yield
strength of MoNbTaVW to homogenization is a poten-
tial advantage for its adoptability. However, it is crucial
to note that the tests in this study are all compressive.
The dendritic structure may lead to early failure in
tension, a condition highly sensitive to material
defects. While the yield strength of MoNbTaVW
appears unaﬀected by heat treatment, other properties
such as creep, fatigue, and corrosion resistance may be
impacted. With further validation, the MC model could
help determine the necessity of homogenization heat
treatment, especially as high-throughput alloy discovery
methods become more prevalent. Future research will
focus on the eﬀects of added oxygen and the underlying
behavior of dislocations under various processing con-
ditions. With further research, tailored solidiﬁcation
