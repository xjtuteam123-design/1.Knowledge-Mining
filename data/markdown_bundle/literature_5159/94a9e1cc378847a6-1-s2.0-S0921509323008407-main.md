ABSTRACT 
The role and behavior of primary MC carbide and their effects on the microstructure and stress rupture property 
of the cast Ni-based superalloys during long-term thermal exposure at elevated temperature are systemically 
investigated in this work. It is found that the chemical compositions of primary MC are proportional to the Nb/Ti 
and (Ti + Nb)/(Mo + W) ratios of alloys. During long-term thermal exposure, the thermal stability of primary MC 
can be quantitatively evaluated by its decomposition degree defined as D

associated with the Nb/Ti and (Mo + W)/Cr ratios of alloys. The decomposition process, degree, form and 
mechanism of primary MC are revealed in detail. The STEM observations with the EDS analyses show that 
primary MC decomposition is an inter-diffusion process of elements between the primary MC carbide and γ 
matrix, which can increase the volume fraction of γ’ phase, restrain the precipitation of topologically close- 
packed (TCP) phases and promote the coarsening of grain boundaries (GBs). The combination of these micro -
structural evolutions, together with the appropriate Al + Ti + Nb and W + Mo contents in the matrix, eventually 
results in a remarkable improvement in the stress rupture property of long-term thermally exposed cast Ni-based 
superalloys. 
1. Introduction 
In the past decades, the inlet temperature of turbines is increasingly 
approaching the incipient melting point of Ni-based superalloys with the 
development of aero engines and industrial gas turbines. However, 
much efforts are still being made to further advance their potential 
temperature capacity [ 1 ]. For example, improving the thermal stability 
of microstructures by modifying the chemical compositions is one 
promising method of upgrading the operating performance of alloys at 
elevated temperatures. 
The microstructure of Ni-based superalloys typically consists of γ 
matrix, γ’ phase, MC carbide, secondary carbides and TCP phases [ 2 ]. 
The γ matrix can be regarded as a closed system containing all the other 
phases. The secondary carbides ( e.g., M
23
C
6 
and M
6
C) and TCP phases ( e. 
g., σ and μ ) both consume Cr, W and Mo, while the γ’ phase and MC 
carbide share Ti, Nb, Ta and Hf; hence, a complicated dynamic balance 
of element interdiffusions is expected to occur among these phases at 
elevated temperatures. Once any of the phases precipitate(s), decom -
pose(s) or dissolve(s) into the matrix, the balance is broken, and 
consequently the microstructure and properties of the alloys will be 
altered due to the redistribution of elements in the system [ 3 , 4 ]. 
It is well known that MC carbide is metastable in Ni-based superal -
loys at elevated temperatures. It tends to decompose and release C into 
the supersaturated matrix, which can change the microstructure of al -
loys as follows: i) the C combines with Cr, W and Mo to form the sec -
ondary carbides, suppressing the precipitation of TCP phases [ 5 , 6 ]; ii) 
the formation and growth of secondary carbides at GBs accelerates the 
boundaries coarsening and has a complex influence on the stress rupture

2. Experiments 
The compositions of investigated alloys are listed in Table 1 . The A0 
alloy is a copy of the K446 alloy containing the stable primary MC 
carbide [ 6 ], while the other alloys are its derived varieties by modifying 
the contents of Al, Ti, Nb, Cr, W or Mo. For the A0 - A4 alloys, the Nb/Ti 
ratio decreases from 0.46 to 0.03, with the (Ti + Nb) content invariable. 
The A3 alloy has the same Nb/Ti ratio as the K452 alloy in which the 
primary MC is unstable [ 9 ]. The A5 - A8 alloys are designed based on the 
A4 alloy, with the Nb/Ti ratio keeping the lowest value of 0.03. In these 
alloys, the (Ti + Nb)/(Mo + W) ratio increases from 0.38 to 0.88, 
whereas the (Mo + W)/Cr ratio reduces from 1.07 to 0.22. 
The Nb/Ti and (Ti + Nb)/(Mo + W) ratios are changed respectively 
in order to study the decomposition degree (or thermal stability) of 
primary MC, whereas varying the (Mo + W)/Cr ratio aims to examine 
the decomposition form. The Al content is higher in the A1 and A6 alloys 
than in the other alloys, which leads to the less (Ti + Nb)/Al ratios and 
facilitates the precipitation of the η phase when the primary MC de -
composes during thermal exposure.

The preferred formation order of MC carbide is HfC, TaC, NbC, and 
TiC in Ni-based superalloys [ 10 ]. That is, Nb combines with C to form 
MC more easily than Ti during solidification. In the A0 - A4 alloys with

gradual decline in the Nb/Ti and (Ti + Nb)/(Mo + W) ratios of the 
primary MC. Furthermore, it is revealed that a good proportional rela -

Nb/Ti ratio of alloys, as displayed in Fig. 1 . For example, the Nb/Ti 
ratios of the carbide are about three times higher than that of the cor -
responding alloys.

from the A5 to A8 alloy cannot be well interpreted only based on the 
compositions of the carbide. In other words, the compositions of the 
alloys should be considered in order to better understand the decom -

another key factor affecting the primary MC decomposition, in addition 
to the Nb/Ti and (Ti + Nb)/(Mo + W) ratios of primary MC. Cr, W, and 
Mo all participate in the decomposition reaction between the primary 
MC and γ matrix, and Cr has a much larger diffusion coefficient than W 
and Mo [ 19 ]. Thus, a big (Mo + W)/Cr ratio means to decelerate the 
interdiffusion process which occurs during the primary MC decompo -

primary MC in the A3 and A4 alloys has better thermal stability than that

3.3. Influence of primary MC decomposition on the microstructure of 
thermally exposed alloys 
3.3.1. γ ’ phase 
The weight percentage contents of the γ’ forming elements Al + Ti +
Nb are similar for the A0 and A2 - A4 alloys, that is, 5.3, 5.5, 5.3 and 5.4, 
respectively; however, the corresponding atomic percentage contents 
are obviously different in these alloys, regardless of the weight per -
centage contents, as exhibited in Table 1 . This is probably an important 
reason why the amount of γ’ phase is varied to some extent in the alloys 
( Fig. 8 (a)). 
There are several other factors probably affecting the amount of γ’ 
phase, besides the atomic percentage content of γ’ forming elements: 
firstly, when the primary MC forms during solidification, it consumes a 
gradually reduced amount of Ti + Nb and hence leaves more the two 
elements in the matrix in the A0, A2 - A4 alloys ( Table 2 ); secondly, the 
Al + Ti + Nb content consumed by the γ’ phase declines with the 
decreased Nb/Ti ratio in the thermally exposed alloys, meaning that it 
becomes richer in the matrix ( Table 3 ); and finally, the concentration of 
Ti + Nb released from the decomposed primary MC rises with the 
increased D
t 
value ( Fig. 3 ), which is also beneficial to the precipitation of 
γ’ phase [ 22 , 23 ]. All of these aspects mentioned above contribute to the 
enrichment of γ’ forming elements in the matrix and accordingly 
accelerate its precipitation, inducing an increase in its volume fraction 
with the decreased Nb/Ti ratio in the A0, A2 - A4 alloys ( Fig. 8 (a)). The 
size of γ’ phase does not change regularly with the decreased Nb/Ti ratio 
( Fig. 8 (b)), which suggests that the increment of volume fraction of γ’ 
phase is determined by the formation of more γ’ nucleus, instead of its 
growth with the decreased Nb/Ti ratio. Our previous research work [ 24 ] 
also confirmed this phenomenon. 
Comparing Table 1 with Table 3 , one can find that the Al, Ti and Nb 
contents in the γ’ phase vary proportionally to the Nb/Ti ratio of the 
alloys thermally exposed for 10000 h, as demonstrated in Fig. 9 . The 
accordant tendency of Al and Nb reveals that they have a similar role in 
the formation of γ’ phase, which is just opposite to that of Ti. In the 
reference [ 25 ], the Al, Ti and Nb contents of γ’ phase in the heat treated 
Nimonic 80A alloy display a similar law as the present alloys, suggesting 
that the chemical composition of γ’ phase probably linearly changes 
with that of the alloys during the whole long-term thermal exposure at 
elevated temperature.

3.3.3. Grain boundaries (GBs) 
Based on the foregoing investigations, it can be well understood that 
with the decreased Nb/Ti or (Mo + W)/Cr ratios of alloys, the primary 
MC decomposition gradually becomes severe, and a greater amount of C 
is released into the γ matrix. Since C diffuses along the GBs several or -
ders of magnitude faster than within the grain [ 30 ], it tends to gather 
towards the GBs during long-term thermal exposure at elevated tem -

carbides and promote the coarsening of boundaries. Accordingly, the 
GBs become wider with the reduced Nb/Ti or (Mo + W)/Cr ratio in the 
A0 - A8 alloys. A thin film of γ’ phase decorated by dispersed carbide

however, the presence of a wide and continuous carbide chain could

4. Conclusions 
This work makes clear that the chemical composition and thermal 
stability of primary MC in cast Ni-based superalloys can be regularly 
modified by carefully tailoring the Nb/Ti, (Ti + Nb)/(Mo + W), (Mo +
W)/Cr and (Nb + Ti)/Al ratios of alloys, which significantly influences 
the microstructure and mechanical properties of the alloys as follows: 
(1) The chemical compositions of γ’ phase and primary MC are 
revealed to be closely related to that of alloys. The composition of 
primary MC proportionally varies with the Nb/Ti and (Ti + Nb)/ 
(Mo + W) ratios of the alloys; and, the composition of γ’ phase in 
the long-term thermally exposed alloys presents a good propor -
tional relationship with the Nb/Ti ratio of the alloys. 
(2) During long-term thermal exposure or service, the thermal sta -
bility of primary MC can be quantitatively expressed by its 
decomposition degree which is defined as D
t
= ( V
0
- V
t
)/ V
0 
× 100%. 
The decomposition degree (or thermal stability) of primary MC is 
mainly determined by the (Mo + W)/Cr and Nb/Ti ratios of al -
loys, while the decomposition form relies on the (Mo + W)/Cr 
and (Nb + Ti)/Al ratios. The role of (Ti + Nb)/(Mo + W) ratio in 
the primary MC decomposition needs to be further clarified in the 
future. 
(3) STEM observation combining EDS analyses indicates that when 
primary MC decomposes, it releases C, Ti, Nb, W and Mo into the 
reaction area, whereas the γ matrix acts as the source of Ni, Cr, Al, 
Ti, Nb, W and Mo for the decomposition products. The concen -
tration gradients of various elements between the primary MC 
and γ matrix are the main driving force for the primary MC 
decomposition at elevated temperature. 
(4) Appropriate degree of primary MC decomposition can to certain 
extent increase the volume fraction of γ’ phase, restrain the 
precipitation of TCP phases and accelerate the coarsening of GBs. 
The combination of these microstructural variations, together 
with the appropriate Al + Ti + Nb and W + Mo contents in the 
matrix, effectively improves the stress rupture properties of long- 
term thermally exposed alloys.
