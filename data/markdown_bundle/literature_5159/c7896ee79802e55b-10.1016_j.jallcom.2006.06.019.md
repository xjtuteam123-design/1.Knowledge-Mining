Abstract
Composite materials allow one to associate fragile and ductile phases in order to obtain a good combination of properties. In situ composites
obtained through eutectic transformation may present a combination of phases under a condition of equilibrium. Samples of the eutectic Ni–Ni3Si
alloy were arc-melted and characterized in the as cast and directionally solidiﬁed (DS) conditions. The as cast microstructure was reﬁned and
uniform, while the DS microstructure was aligned in the direction of heat extraction and presented a plane solid/liquid interface. The alloy displayed
an interesting combination of relatively high strength and tensile ductility at room temperature (3.7% total strain; UTS = 810 MPa), and a modulus
of elasticity E = 264 MPa. High temperature tests showed that the yield strength decreased almost linearly as the temperature rose. No brittle to
ductile transition temperature was found.

1. Introduction
Considerable performance improvements can often be
achieved in a structural material by controlling its microstruc-
ture [1], which is also applicable to composite materials. Modern
industry, particularly the transport and energy generation sec-
tors, requires structural materials whose mechanical and chem-
ical properties remain unaltered at high temperatures [2]. The
materials most commonly used for application at temperatures
other than room temperature are high strength steels, titanium
alloys and, for the most critical high temperature applications,
nickel-based superalloys. In addition, there are the intermetallic
compounds, for which few applications as structural materials
have so far been found, despite intensive research and their
promising potential. The factors that prevent the wider use of
intermetallic compounds are their low ductility and low fracture
toughness at room temperature and their poor creep resistance
at high temperatures [3]. One possible way to improve their low
ductility and low fracture toughness is by combining intermetal-
lic compounds with more ductile phases [4], which are more
interesting when the combination occurs under conditions of

2. Experimental procedure
Commercially pure Ni and Si were arc-melted under an argon atmosphere,
followed by directional solidiﬁcation (DS) using Bridgman equipment. A
10.0 mm/h growth rate was applied. The thermal gradient was about 100
◦C/cm
and the hot zone temperature was set at 1350 ◦C. The samples, with a nominal
composition of Ni–21.4Si (at.%), the eutectic composition, were conventionally
prepared for optical microscopy using a 50 ml H
2O, 50 ml HCl, and 1 g FeCl3
etchant. Mechanical compression, tensile and fracture toughness tests were con-
ducted in an MTS system. The material’s hardness was also determined. The
tensile tests were performed according to the ASTM E8M-95a standard [8],a t
a strain rate of 10
−4 s−1. The true-stress–true-strain curve, as well as the strain
hardening exponent and the strength coefﬁcient, were mathematically deter-
mined [9]. The fracture toughness tests were performed partially according to
the ASTM E-399 standard[10]. Since regular acceptable fatigue precracks were
quite difﬁcult to obtain, the three-point bending tests were conducted using
samples without precracks. Hence, the K
Q parameter was determined using
a 40.0 mm span and a crosshead speed of 0.005 mm/s. The COD curve was
recorded and the P
5 load, PQ and KQ were calculated following the ASTM E-
399 criteria. Vickers hardness was determined at room temperature and at 100◦C
intervals from 200 to 800◦C, using a device built specially for this purpose and
a load of 10.0 kgf applied for 10 s. The modulus of elasticity and Poisson’s ratio
were determined dynamically using a cylindrical specimen with a diameter of
10.0 mm. The standard through-transmission technique was employed using
coupled longitudinal and shear transducers with active diameters of 6.35 mm.
3. Results and discussion
Despite the uncontrolled cooling condition, the microstruc-
tural characterization revealed as cast ingots with uniform
microstructures devoid of primary phases and exhibiting eutec-
tic colonies, with small lamellar spacing due to the high heat
extraction rates typical of solidiﬁcation in water-cooled copper
hearths. The microstructure was found to be lamellar. If a binary
eutectic system presents an almost symmetrical phase diagram,
with solid phases possessing comparable volume fractions, the
arrangement that is formed may be lamellar [11]. In the case
of eutectic Ni–Ni
3Si, the volume fractions of /H9251-Ni and Ni 3Si
phases just below the eutectic temperature, which were identi-
ﬁed through the phase diagram, were 39% and 61%, respectively.

librium condition, presented a microstructure aligned along the
heat extraction direction, exhibiting few defects and present-

toughening mechanism when it causes crack deviation, work-

from the fragile to the ductile phase and when it diminishes

4. Conclusions
The results obtained led us to conclude that: (1) the as cast
structure presented a uniform microstructure devoid of primary
phases and contained eutectic colonies with small lamellar spac-
ing due to the solidiﬁcation conditions. (2) The directionally
solidiﬁed structure presented a microstructure aligned along
the heat extraction direction, with few defects and a plane
solid/liquid interface. (3) Despite the high volume of the brit-
tle intermetallic Ni
3Si phase, the alloy showed an interesting
combination of relatively high strength and tensile ductility at
room temperature. (4) The high temperature tests revealed that
the yield strength decreased almost linearly with rising tempera-
ture, and no brittle to ductile transition temperature was detected,
a fact that was attributed to the /H9251-Ni effect.
