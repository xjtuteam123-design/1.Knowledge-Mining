Abstract 
Artificial ageing characteristics and high temperature tensile properties of the Al -7Si-1Cu-0.5Mg 
(wt.%) alloy modified with micro-additions of Mo and Mn, were examined. The modified alloy 
formed microstructure containing Cu- and Mg -rich phases along with Mo- and Mn-rich 
dispersoids of complex morphology. During artificial ageing, the alloy reached a peak hardness 
of 97 HRF after a cycle at 200°C for 1 h. At room temperature, the major impact of Mo and Mn 
additions was an increase in ductility by 3.5 times and in ultimate tensile strength by 4%. With 
increasing the testing temperature from 25°C to 300°C, the yield strength and tensile strength of 
the modified alloy in T6 condition decreased from 195 MPa to 54 MPa and from 324 MPa to 139 
MPa, respectively. It was found that the alloy strength and ductility are affected by the secondary 
dendritic arm spacing. The coarse intermetallics influenced the crack propagation. When at room 
temperature, the cleavage -type brit tle fracture was dominant at higher temperature the fully 
ductile cracking mode was present. The role of Mn and Mo-containing dispersoid phases in 
modification of the alloy properties is discussed. 
 
 
Keywords: Al–Si–Cu–Mg alloy, Mo,Mn-rich intermetallics; ageing; hardness; elevated 
temperature strength.

1. Introduction 
 
Aluminum alloys are promising candidates as lightweight automotive materials for improving 
vehicles performance and fuel efficiency [1, 2]. In the family of cast Al -Si alloys, the Al -Si-Cu-
Mg grades are frequently used for transportation vehicles . Due to their high strength to weight 
ratio, good thermal conductivity, excellent castability, hot tear resistance and feeding 
characteristics, which allow casting intricate shapes such as engine blocks, cylinder heads or 
chassis components where these grades comprise 85% to 90% of the total aluminum cast parts 
produced by the automotive industry [3]. 
The Al -Si alloy system has been explored for decades and its modification for high 
temperature applications has been a subject of many studies [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11] . To develop 
new alloy for high temperature applications, some basic rules were es tablished [12]. As 
discussed in [13], the alloying elements should (i) be capable of forming thermal ly stable 
strengthening phases, (ii) show low solid solubility in the Al matrix, (iii) have low diffusivity in 
the Al matrix, and (iv) retain the ability for the alloy to be conventionally solidified . The Al-
alloys are designed to form precipitates, which control the alloy performance. The Al-Si-Cu-Mg 
grades are combined with transition metals s uch as Cr, Mn, Zr or Ti . to form primary and/ or 
secondary precipitates with little or no solubility in the aluminum matrix. Due to their low 
diffusivity in aluminum, these alloying additio ns form nano-size precipitates, either during 
solidification or duri ng subsequent thermal treatment. These fine precipitates called dispersoids 
delay or prevent static recrystallization during processing. The effectiveness of a particular 
dispersoid in controlling the grain structure depends on its size, distribution, and coherency [14, 
15]. 
 Age hardening is commonly employed in high-strength Al alloys. A lthough a large 
number of alloys have different proportions and combinations of alloying elements, the 
mechanism of hardening has perhaps been studied most extensively for the Al -alloys containing 
Cu and Mg. Cu and Mg are added to increase the strength of the alloy, but this also leads to a 
reduction in ductility [16, 17]. The strength and ductility are affected by factors such as (i) if the 
Cu and Mg are present as coarse phases after solidification, (ii) atoms in solid solution, (iii) GP 
zones formed at room temperature, or (iv) precipitates formed during artificial ageing [8]. The 
coarse phase s which may form during solidification are the Al 2Cu phase and the Q-

Al5Mg8Si6Cu2 phase in Al -Si-Cu-Mg alloys [18, 19] , while the π-Al8Mg3FeSi6 phase and the 
Mg2Si phase form in Al-Si-Mg alloys. These coarse phases do not contribute to the strength, and 
their degree of influence on ductility depends on their distribution and size relative to the Si 
particles [20]. The precipitates are formed during ageing and hinder the movement of 
dislocations, thereby increasing the material strength. The effectiveness of precipitation 
hardening depends on the volume fraction and the dispersion of the precipitate phases, their 
mean size and the mean free path between them . Sjölander and Seifeddine [21, 22] studied the 
ageing characteristics of Al -Si-Cu-Mg alloys. After solution treatment and natural ageing at 
room temperature for 24 h day prior to artificial ageing in a forced circulation air furnace at 
170°C or 210°C fo r various times, they concluded that the peak yield strength decreas ed as the 
ageing temperature increased. Mohammad et al. [23] reported that the precipitation sequence for 
Al-Si-Cu-Mg alloys is similar to Al -Cu-Mg and Al -Si-Mg, but more complex, as the Q′′ 
precipitates and the θ′ precipitates can be formed. Cu increases the fraction tendency of the β′′ 
precipitates, but it also can form the Q′′ precipitates. The β′′ precipitates are more significant to 
improve the alloy streng th than the Q′′ precipitates. For wrought alloys it was reported that the 
fraction of the Q′′ precipitates increases with natural ageing and artificial ageing time and 
temperature [23, 22, 21] . The Mg -to-Si r atio controls the formation of metastable Mg -rich 
precipitates. The kinetics of precipitation and the phase composition are changed by the excess 
of Si in solid solution. Similarly, stable phases are rich of Mg while metastable phases are 
enriched of Si [23, 24, 25, 26, 27] . Studies [15, 28, 13] also reported aging characteristics of the 
Al-Si-Cu-Mg alloy modified with additions of Mo, Cr, Ti, V and Zr. It was concluded that 
transition metals play an important role in alloy performance. Farkoosh et al. [15, 28] studied the 
influence of Mo and Mn additions in Al-Si-based alloy with lower Cu content. However, there is 
no research on the effect of Mo and Mn in Al -Si-based alloy with higher addition of Cu and Mg 
content than used in our study . It is also not clear how secondary dendritic arm spacing ( SDAS) 
influences the modified alloy properties. 
 The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of Mo and Mn additions on artificial 
ageing time and temperatures of the Al-Si-Cu-Mg alloy. A correlation was examined between 
the SDAS value and alloy performance. In addition, the deformation behaviour of the Al–Si–Cu–
Mg alloy with additions of Mn and Mo at various temperatures under tensile loading was 
examined.

2. Experimental 
 
The experimental alloys were prepared in an induction furnace by melting commercial master 
alloys of Al-50Cu, Al-35Si, Al-10Mn, Al-6.1Mo in wt.% and pure metals, Al and Mg to achieve 
the selected alloy chemistry, as listed in Table 1. As the reference base, the Al-Si-Cu-Mg alloy 
without additions of Mo and Mn was used. Details of the casting procedure are described in [29]. 
The test samples, having SDAS of 10, 25 and 34 μm, were collected from the cast wedge . After 
casting, the Al -Si-Cu-Mg-(Mn-Mo) alloy having SDAS of 25 μm was subjected to solution 
treatment followed by artificial ageing (AA). The heat treatment was performed in an air 
circulating resistance furnace with a temperature accuracy of ±5°C. The solutionizing was 
conducted in two steps at temperatures of 510°C for 0.5 h and at 525°C for 4.5 h [13]. Following 
water quench, artificial ageing was performed at temperatures of 100, 150, 200 and 250°C for 
0.5, 1, 15, 22 and 43 h. 
 For microstructural studies, samples were prepared following standard metallographic 
technique described in [30]. The microstructural observations were performed using an optical 
microscope (OM), equipped with Cleme x software which was used to measure eutectic silicon 
size and a scanning electron microscope (SEM), JEOL JSM 6380LV, equipped with energy -
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX; Oxford) . Hardness was measured us ing a Büehler 
Rockwell tester under F scale. An average hardness of at least 5 measurements was calculated 
and plotted with respect to ageing time. Tensile tests for both the as -cast and T6 peak ag ed 
conditions were performed on sub–size rectangular bar samples with a gauge length of 25 mm 
and a cross-section of 6 mm×6 mm . Tests were performed using a computerized United Tensile 
Testing machine (Model: STM, 50kN) following the ASTM: E8M–11 standard at temperatures 
of 25°C, 200°C and 300°C with a deviation of ±5 °C at a strain rate of 10 –3 s–1. Details of tensile 
testing were described in [13]. After tensile tests, t he fracture surfaces were examined using 
SEM to find out the alloy deformation characteristics.

3.2 Effect of artificial ageing (AA) on hardness 
The Al-Si-Cu-Mg alloy modified with addition s of Mo-Mn alloy reached a hardness of 74  1.7 
HRF in the as-cast condition. After solution treatment at 510°C followed by 525 °C its hardness 
decreased to 62  2.1 HRF. The hardness of the artificially aged samples is plotted in 2D and 3D 
format as a function of time (Fig. 4). The graph shows that the alloy hardness was more affected 
by the ageing temperature than by the ageing time. For a geing at lower temperatures up to 
150°C, the alloy hardness continuously increased up to 96  0.5 HRF after 15 h exposure. After 
1 h of exposure at 200°C, which is considered the optimum ag eing condition for this alloy , 
known as peak ageing, hardness reached 97  1.0 HRF. Then, with increasing ageing time from 
1 h to 43 h, hardness decreased to 841.3 HRF. With increasing the ageing temperature to 
300°C, the hardness decreased to 431.9 HRF which was even lower than the hardness of the as-
cast and solution treated conditions. Sjölander and Seifeddine [22] reported that Al -Si-Cu-Mg 
alloy achieved the peak aging at a temperature of 170°C after 20 h. As of our previous study , 
additions of Cr‒Zr‒V‒Ti in Al-Si-Cu-Mg alloy increased the peak ageing temperature to 200°C 
[13]. Similarly, the present study showed similar behaviour. The holding time was significantly 
reduced to 1 h , which can be considered as advantageous to design the energy efficient heat 
treatment process.

4. Discussion 
 
The microstructure of cast Al–Si–Cu–Mg alloy can be enhanced by controlling several factors 
such as the degree of modification of the primary and secondary dispersoid phases , SDAS, heat 
treatment techniques, as well as alloy chemistry. In a ddition, the heat treatment process and 
parameters considerably influence the ageing characteristics and tensile properties of the cast Al–
Si–Cu–Mg alloys. The addition of Mo and Mn in Al –Si–Cu–Mg base alloy forms complex 
microstructures. The presented results are explai ned in the following sections by considering the

AA characteristics, the effects of testing temperatures, yield strength, and the behavior of 
precipitates, especially Mn/Mo–rich dispersoid phases on the alloy performance. 
 
4.1 Influence of time and temperature on aging 
The high strength of the Al –Si–Cu–Mg alloys was enhanced by ageing for appropriate times at 
one or two intermediate temperatures to form finely dispersed precipitate s. In this stage, the heat 
treatment must be performed not only below the equilibrium solvus temperature, but also below 
a metastable miscibility gap known as the Guinier -Preston (GP) zone solvus line for having 
homogenized precipitation. The critical temperature also increases with vacancy concentration 
[23, 44, 13]. The super-saturation of vacancies allows diffusion and clustering of solute atoms to 
occur much faster than expected from equilibrium diffusion coefficients. As seen in Fig. 4, the 
changes of hardness of the modifi ed alloy were greatly controlled by ageing parameters i.e., 
temperatures and soaking time intervals. It is seen that as the ageing time and temperature 
growth, primarily a continuous growth in hardness is noticed. After obtaining the peak value, the 
hardness reduces with further increases of the aging time and temperature above 150°C. A 
similar phenomenon of AA of Al alloys was reported by several studies [45, 22, 46, 13] . 
However, the modified alloy required a higher peak aging temperature of 200°C for 1 h to 
achieve maximum hardness. The time to achieve the peak hardness decreases with increasing 
temperature. At the same time, the maximum hardness drops as a result of increasing 
temperature. The results also described that the precipitation hardening behavior of the modified 
alloy was controlled by an intricate arrangement of time and temperature parameters. As 
reported by Eivani and Taheri [45], the hardness variations could be described through the ability 
to obstruct a movement of dislocations by impurity atoms, i.e. foreign particles of dispersoid 
phases. As the alloy solutionizing at 525°C was followed by water quenching, the vacancy 
concentration will be increased [47]. The density of the metastable precipitates or GP zones 
grows with the aging time and temperature [45, 48]. In contrast, ageing at higher temperatures 
and longer time causes a reduction of hardness due to the incorporation of the fine precipitates 
into larger ones. The strength of the alloy is determined by the size, distribution and the 
coherency of the precipitates in the matrix. The precipitates lose their coherency due to the 
ageing at higher temperature and longer time, resulting in lower hardness. Instead, the interaction 
with the dislocations could be described by the Friedel effect and the Orowan mechanism [21].

Small and not-too-hard precipitates are generally sheared by the moving dislocations (Friedel 
effect). When the precipitates are bigger and stronger enough, the moving dislocations pass the 
precipitates by bowing. The precipitates strength increases with increasing si ze of the 
precipitates until it can be shared by dislocations. With further increase of the size of 
precipitates, the shearing processes become difficult which leads to the more favorable condition 
for the dislocations to pass the precipitates via the Orow an mechanism, which results a reduction 
in hardness with further increase in precipitate size. When the fraction of precipitates shearing 
and bowing by the dislocations is equal, the alloys achieve peak strength. As seen in the hardness 
plot, after 9 h ageing at 100 °C and 150 °C the alloy did not reach the peak hardness. This could 
be attributed to the fact that the age hardening process is a diffusion-controlled process. At 100 
°C and 150 °C , the diffusion rate of Mn/Mo atoms in Al-matrix was very slow , resulting in a 
lower fraction of precipitates, which does not lead to peak hardness. 
 
4.2 Influence of temperature on the alloy strength 
As seen in Fig. 1, the experimental results show that the Al–Si–Cu–Mg modified alloy samples 
containing Mn/Mo at the T6 peak ageing condition , tested at different temperatures , exhibited 
significantly higher yield strength and better ultimate tensile strength compared to the base alloy 
in similar conditions. This is attributed to the fact that the addition of Mn and Mo to the Al–Si–
Cu–Mg alloy form thermally stable dispersoid precipitates during solidification of the alloy in 
addition with the Cu-containing phases that together increased the alloy strength. Also, the 
detrimental Fe –containing phase reacts with Mn and Mo forms thermally stable precipitates 
which improved the alloy performance. At the same time, the presence of Mn also changes the 
morphology of the β-AlFeSi which is beneficial for improvement of alloy properties (Fig. 1c, d). 
As reported in previous stud ies [43, 33, 41], the marked enhancement in alloy strength could be 
credited to both the composite–like role of Cu–, Fe–, and Mg–containing Si precipitates (Fig. 1), 
the Mn/Mo-rich dispersoid precipitates and the nano –sized precipitates which were uniformly 
distributed in the aluminum matrix or along with the eutectic silicon precipitates. The presence 
of these micro – and nano–sized precipitates (Fig. 1) would effectively inhibit the movement of 
dislocations duri ng tensile loading, which substantially improved the strength of the modified 
alloy (Fig. 6). Furthermore, the addition of the Mn and Mo to Al–Si-Cu-Mg alloys changes the 
morphology of the primary precipitates , which could also be another key factor for improving

13 
 
the alloy strength [8]. Therefore, it could be stated that the bulk–shaped Mn-Mo–rich dispersoid 
phases enhanced the alloy strength. 
As stated earlier, with increasing testing temperature, the YS and UTS of the Al–Si–Cu–
Mg alloy modified with addition of Mn-Mo decreased linearly. This was primarily due to the 
change in the microstructure and the presence of thermally stable dispersoid precipitates, as also 
reported in [33, 43, 8] . The deformation in alloys is directly associated with the formation, 
motion and storage of dislocations, which start to move at a stress far below a value considered 
as essential to deform a defect–free crystal [49]. Therefore, the improvement of alloy strength 
mostly depends on generating internal obstacles including grain boundaries, precipitates, and 
other dislocations to the motion of dislocations [50, 51] . Also, the density of dislocations 
increases with increasing plastic deformation and results in more frequent interactions impe ding 
the motion of dislocation [52]. When the testing temperature increases, the cross -slips are 
thermally activated by climbing of dislocations causing a reduction in material s trength [31, 34]. 
However, the fine precipitates and dispersoid phases show strong re sistance to the motion of 
dislocations by pinning at the grain and sub–grain boundaries during all thermal and mechanical 
processing of aluminum alloys resulting in the improvement of the alloy high temperature 
properties [53, 54] [34, 55]. 
Another interesting phenomenon was seen for the behavior of dispersoid precipitates 
present on the fracture surfaces of the modified alloy. As shown in Fig. 7 and Fig. 8, mixed-type 
(brittle pa rticle and ductile matrix) fracture dominated at room temperature while ductile-type 
fracture was identified at 300°C. The brittle fracture could be controlled by silicon particles and 
dispersoid precipitates , especially Mn-Mo–rich phases. A brittle to ductile transition was 
identified at the testing temperature of 300°C . During tensile loading, particles experience 
multiple cracking without any de -bonding, which is an indication of the strong interfacial 
bonding between the particles and Al –matrix at the testing temperature of 25°C (Fig. 10a, c). 
However, the fractured particles are de -bonded from the matrix and from the sub –surfaces for 
the samples tested at 300°C temperature ( Fig. 10e, f). This could be caused by the fact that 
softening of the solid occurs with increasing temperature beyond 0.7Tm (melting temperature) in 
which the solid loses its general properties. At the same time, the dislocations are piled up at the 
interface of the particles and matrix resulting in de-bonding [56, 43].

5. Conclusions 
 
The artificial ageing characteristics and tensile properties of the Al -Si-Cu-Mg alloy modified 
with additions of Mn and Mo were examined. Based on the microstructures, SDAS, hardness and 
deformation behavior at elevated temperatures, the following conclusions can be drawn: 
 
In the as-cast state, the Al–Si–Cu–Mg-based alloy modified with additions of Mn and Mo 
was composed of dendritic α -Al, fibrous -like eutectic Si, and Cu, M g and Fe -based phases 
blocky Al 2.1Cu, blocky Al 8.5Si2.4Cu, Chinese script/needle -like Al5.2CuMg4Si5.1 and needle-like 
Al14Si7.1FeMg3.3 which were also identified in the base alloy . In addition, micro sized Mn and 
Mo-rich phases are α-Al17Fe3.2Mn0.8Si2, Al65Cu20Fe6Mn9, Al13Fe8Mn33Mo and 
Al0.78Fe4.8Mn0.27Mo4.15Si2. 
 
With i ncreasing the time and temperature of artificial aging , hardness of the modified 
alloy increased initially . After reaching the maximum hardness, it decreased with increasing 
ageing time. The peak ageing parameters for the modified alloy were 200°C for 1h and the 
maximum hardness was 9 7 HRF. Ageing at lower temperatures of 100°C and 150°C required 
longer time, more than 9h to reach peak hardness. 
 
The SDAS affected the flow stress during alloy tensile deformation. For the as‒cast state, 
a reduction in the SDAS from 34 m to 10 m for the modified alloy decreased the UTS from 
232 MPa to 227 MPa accompanied by an increment in the alloy ductility from 7.75% to 9.36%. 
 
The tensile tests carried out at different temperatures showed that the micro-additions of 
Mn and Mo significantly altered the base alloy properties. The modified alloy in the T6 peak 
aging condition achieved 7% higher UTS and 16% higher ductility in comparison with the base 
Al–Si–Cu–Mg alloy tested in similar conditions. It is also evident that with increasing the testing 
temperature from 25 °C to 300°C, the UTS of the modified alloy in T6 state decreased about 
50%.

Fractographic analysis of tensile-tested alloy showed that a mixed-type fracture with 
cleavage-type intergranular fracture and brittle fracture of particle s and ductile f racture of the 
aluminum matrix was identified at room temperature while ductile-type fracture was noticed at 
300°C. Multiple cracks were seen in the Mn-Mo–rich dispersoid phases, indicating the strong 
interfacial bonding between the precipitates and the matrix. 
 
It can be attributed that the addition of Mn and Mo form a complex phases with higher 
Cu and Mg contents, which enhance the alloy performa nce. At the same time, the formation of 
thermally stable Mn - and Mo -rich phases sustained at higher temperature which improved the 
alloy high temperature properties.
