Macroscopic mechanical properties depend on microstructural states. Here, the deformation behavior 
of Ni‑based superalloys around inclusions is analyzed under misfit strains and external loads using 
a phase‑field model coupled with small‑strain plasticity. A central inclusion in a matrix is subjected 
to uniaxial tensile loading. As the lattice misfit magnitude increases, tensile deformation lowers the 
macroscopic yield stress, strain hardening, and stress triaxiality. The model also predicts stress and strain 
accumulation around the inclusions, influenced by the misfit sign. Higher stress levels form near the 
top and bottom surfaces of the inclusions and tend to accumulate along the loading direction, thereby 
affecting microcracking.
Introduction
Ni-based superalloys are well known for their exceptional 
high-temperature properties, including superior creep resist -
ance and excellent resistance to hot corrosion and oxidation. 
As a result, components manufactured from these alloys 
routinely operate under extremely high thermal conditions. 
Recent research has shown significant improvements in their 
elevated temperature mechanical performance, including 
enhanced creep resistance, outstanding high-temperature 
fatigue resistance, and improved hot corrosion and oxidation 
resistance [1 , 2]. Due to these extraordinary properties, Ni-
based superalloys are used in producing turbine blades, aero -
space engine components, and critical parts for nuclear power 
systems, all of which demand excellent thermal stability and 
mechanical integrity [1 ].
Typically, single-crystal Ni-based superalloys consist of 
a two-phase microstructure composed of precipitates ( γ ′ ) 
embedded in a matrix ( γ ). Their remarkable creep resistance is 
achieved by the characteristic microstructure comprising cuboi-
dal-shaped L12 crystal structures, whereas the matrix consists of 
disturbed face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal structures [ 1]. Ni-
based superalloys consist of different alloying elements, namely 
Cr, Co, Al, Mo, W , Ti, Ta, Re, and Ru. Among these, Re, Ru, 
and Co are particularly effective at enhancing high-temperature 
creep resistance, while other elements play a key role in promot-
ing the nucleation of ( γ ′ ) phase precipitates.
In applications such as aerospace and gas turbines, these 
components must endure cyclic loading, making them suscep -
tible to fatigue damage over their operational lifespan. Moreover, 
severe thermal and mechanical loading conditions often lead to 
continuous, time-dependent deformation, further challenging 
their long-term performance.
On a microscopic scale, Ni-based superalloys contain inclu-
sions (carbides or other nonmetallic inclusions), voids of differ-
ent shapes and sizes, and many distinctive grains. T o accurately 
predict fatigue life in these materials, it is necessary to know the 
governing mechanistic drivers for crack initiation originating 
from the matrix and these inclusions at the microscale. Identify-
ing these mechanistic drivers in metallic materials is challeng -
ing, because, in alloys, the microstructures evolve continuously 
due to lattice misfit and externally applied load. This evolution 
generates complex deformation fields, including plastic strains, 
forming microstructures with different shapes, morphologies, 
and orientations.
The characteristic microstructural evolution of γ′ phase 
is known as directional coarsening [1 , 3, 4]. Additionally, this 
results in an increase in the γ channel width resulting in modi-
fication of the lattice misfit. The lattice misfit is considered an

important material property that strongly affects the mechani-

of internal stresses [ 5]. These internal stresses have a definite 
role in deformation behavior at a microscopic level. Thus, 
it is important to understand the fundamental deformation 
mechanisms of the face-centered cubic (FCC) materials sys-
tem at the microscale, which can provide unified theories of 
the major microstructural effects driving fatigue. Hence, in 
this work, we evaluate the stresses and strains in the plastic 
region in the presence of lattice misfit and external loading.
Typically, Ni-based superalloys exhibit a positive lattice 
misfit at room temperature that becomes negative at higher 
temperatures, primarily due to the larger thermal expansion 
coefficient of γ phase [ 6]. The magnitude of lattice misfit 
depends on the chemical composition of both γ and γ ′ phases, 
as well as temperature [7 ]. Because the atomic diameters of 
most alloying elements in Ni-based superalloys exceed that of 
Ni, incorporating these elements into the Ni lattice generally 
increases the lattice parameter of the γ phase. In contrast, the 
γ′ phase is composed of Ni and Al. Although Al is larger than 
almost all other alloying elements (except Ta and Ti), substi -
tuting these elements at the Al sites effectively reduces the γ ′ 
lattice parameter. Hence, the lattice misfit in superalloys can 
be tailored by controlling the segregation of alloying elements 
in the γ and γ′ phases [ 2].
Mughrabi et al. [8 ] noted that experimentally isolating the 
effect of lattice misfit on the strength of superalloys is challeng-
ing. Consequently, micromechanical models are often employed 
to evaluate how lattice misfit influences their mechanical prop-
erties. Changes in lattice misfit directly affect the interfacial 
coherency stress, leading to significant variations in the γ /γ′ 
microstructure and coherency strains and coarsening kinetics 
[9].
Phase-field modeling approaches have become a powerful 
tool for capturing complex mesoscopic-scale microstructural 
interactions and evolution in multi-phase systems. By coupling 
the relevant thermodynamic and kinetic driving forces with the 
phase-field model, it is possible to gain deeper insight into the 
evolution of lattice misfits and their consequent effects on the 
material’s microstructure and properties.
Extensive research on Ni-based superalloys has been con-
ducted using various techniques, including dislocation dynam-
ics, crystal plasticity, and time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau 
(TDGL) models. For example, Zhou et al. [ 10] combined plas-
ticity and phase-field methods to investigate γ′ rafting and creep 
deformation in Ni–Al under external load. In their approach, 
plasticity was modeled through a dislocation density frame-
work, revealing that plasticity dominates the rafting process 
and depends on the sign of the lattice misfit. Similarly, Gaubert 
et al. [11] employed an elasto-viscoplastic model to examine the 
γ′ microstructural evolution during creep loading in Ni-based 
superalloys. By incorporating a crystal plasticity approach, they 
demonstrated that plastic strains accelerate rafting kinetics and 
lead to misalignment of the rafts.
Takuya et al. [12] used the Prandtl–Reuss model with kin-
ematic hardening to simulate plastic strains and study the stress 
evolution during grain growth, observing that large stresses 
form around interfaces and migrate as grains coarsen. Collec-
tively, these studies highlight the significance of lattice misfit, 
elastic inhomogeneity, mechanical fields, and external loads as 
driving forces in rafting phenomena and raft kinetics.
However, incorporating plasticity into such models is chal-
lenging. Accurately capturing plastic deformation and damage 
mechanics at multiple scales necessitates complex constitutive 
equations, resulting in highly nonlinear governing equations 
that increase both computational cost and complexity [13–15]. 
In an effort to integrate nonlinear mechanical equilibrium equa-
tions within a phase-field framework, Ammar et al. [16] pro -
posed a model that couples plasticity to phase-field simulations. 
Despite these advancements, only a few studies have employed 
phase-field approaches to investigate the microstructural mech-
anisms related to microcracking in Ni-based superalloys under 
the combined effects of misfit strains and external loading.
For this purpose, the phase-field model developed by 
Mushongera et al. [17, 18] for multicomponent formulation is 
used and coupled with the plasticity calculation. The new model 
considers the contribution of elastic inhomogeneity, lattice 
misfit, and mechanical effects in determining the mechanistic 
driving forces for crack initiation during deformation. Addi-
tionally, the plasticity is accounted for using a set of nonlinear 
equations that are expressed in the form of the diffuse interface 
using a homogenization scheme [19, 20]. The stresses and strains 
developed in both phases are interpolated over the phase-field 
variable. Plastic behavior is calculated using a rate-independent 
J2 (second invariant of the deviatoric stress tensor) plasticity 
theory based on the Prandtl–Reuss model. This comprises the 
von Mises yield criterion, linear isotropic hardening, and asso-
ciated flow rule [19, 21]. The effect of misfits on deformations 
during the loading cycle is also discussed. The model is validated 
using analytical solutions for known cases.

The matrix, containing a misfitting inclusion, was assumed to

the proposed model, simulation studies were carried out to

The plot clearly shows that for higher eigenstrain values, the 
yield stress is higher, and therefore, the materials show higher 
strength. Typically, dislocations that are introduced through 
eigenstrains nucleate with increasing strain. However, inclu-
sion brings inhomogeneity in the material and weakens the 
material’ s strength [22]. The stress–strain curve for eigen-
strain e0 =− 0.2% is placed lower and this is due to the early 
nucleation of dislocations. The plot also shows that the mate-

Their studies show that for a larger natural misfit, a small-strain 
hardening will happen, and the material becomes softer as the 
lattice misfit decreases.
A similar study was conducted by Gao et al. [ 7] to evaluate 
the deformation behavior of single-crystal superalloys under the 
influence of positive and negative lattice mismatch. Additionally, 
their simulations were carried out in 3D using discrete dislo-
cation dynamics (DDD). The results showed that the deforma-
tion rate under the tensile loading was lower, whereas the strain 
rate was higher for a positive lattice mismatch. However, for a 
negative lattice mismatch, the opposite holds true. Furthermore, 
Mughrabi [5] also observed that at higher temperatures, posi-
tive lattice misfit shows a higher deformation resistance than the 
negative lattice misfit ( γ ′ ) hardened Ni-based superalloys. Zenk 
et al. [24] conducted experimental studies on a Cobalt-based 
superalloy to determine the γ /γ ′ lattice misfit and the mechani-
cal properties under compressive loading at a rate of 10–4  s−1 and 
a temperature of 1050 °C. Results exhibited that the yield stress 
decreases as the lattice misfits increase. Under the same test con-
ditions, a lattice misfit of 0.3% has a yield strength that is 34 MPa 
higher than one with a lattice misfit of 0.73%. However, in this 
study, the yield strength was increased by 1.5% when lattice misfit 
e0 =− 0.2% was compared to e0 = 0.2% . It is observed that the 
present simulation results predict a similar trend.

By treating the matrix as a reference phase, inclusions 
naturally function as stress concentration sites. Under zero 
strain, the system remains in a stress-free state. During loading, 
stress accumulates around the inclusion and interface regions, 
largely due to lattice misfits inducing residual stresses at the 
matrix–inclusion interface. Under an external load, these highly 
stressed regions tend to deform plastically, particularly where 
the matrix has a lower yield stress than the inclusion. Plastic 
deformation is thus most pronounced near the inclusion and in 
thin interfacial zones.
Lattice misfit strains alter the local stress state by influencing 
the elastic–plastic transition and can, therefore, shift the onset 
of plastic deformation in high-concentration regions. Because 
misfit strains are a driving force in dislocation movement and 
a principal mechanism of plastic deformation [6, 7, 26], under-
standing their role is crucial. Earlier studies have shown that 
local stresses develop through dislocation interactions at the 
interface during plastic deformation [27]. When the inclusion 
and matrix share homogeneous elastic constants, the applied 
strain alone does not create additional eigenstrain in an elas -
tic framework; consequently, only minimal changes in inclu -
sion morphology arise from external loading [28]. However, 
additional misfits are introduced during the loading cycle in 
a plastic framework, redistributing local stresses and influenc-
ing the overall deformation response. Hence, understanding the 
interplay between misfit strain and external loading is key to 
accurately predicting deformation behavior.

Additionally, von Mises stress decreases as it moves toward the 
boundary in the γ channel. The applied strain is effectively trans-
mitted along the γ channels, because the strain compatibility 
between the matrix and the inclusion causes localized deforma-
tion, disturbing the local microstructure. These localized stresses 
and internal stresses result in stress concentration, particularly 
in the direction parallel to the applied tensile load. Meanwhile, 
the vertical γ channels exhibit lower stress suggesting that their 
deformation is less influenced by the applied load direction. This 
difference in stress concentration happens due to the difference 
in the strength between the two phases and due to higher resist-
ance offered in the horizontal direction against the stretching in 
the vertical direction [27].
When the lattice misfit increases, atoms are displaced from 
their equilibrium positions to adjacent sites, thereby maintain-
ing lattice continuity near the interface. This displacement leads 
to elevated elastic strain energy and interfacial energy. Conse-
quently, the internal stress arising from a higher lattice misfit, 
along with localized stresses and applied strain intensifies stress 
concentrations around the inclusion. In particular, significant 
stress builds up around the elastically soft [100] and [010] direc-
tions of the inclusions, suggesting that debonding may occur 
through a loss of interfacial energy. As the lattice misfit grows, 
stress concentrations likewise increase, further promoting 
debonding between atoms at the interface.
Ashutosh et al. [27] conducted a related study on deforma-
tion behavior, noting that dislocations can influence void nuclea-
tion and growth. The present findings align with similar obser-
vations in the literature for materials exhibiting both negative 
and positive misfits, underscoring how phase interactions lead 
to direction-dependent stress distributions.
The plastic deformation in the system occurs mostly due 
to the slip and climb of dislocations [30]. Interface surfaces are 
formed due to the presence of inclusion, and the movement 
of dislocations begins from the γ /γ ′ interface [27]. The mate-
rial deforms plastically when the yield criterion f (σ (φ), εpl ) is 
exceeded. Figure  5(a)–(d) shows the distribution of von Mises 
equivalent plastic strains obtained from the simulation for dif-
ferent lattice misfits during tensile loading. There is no local 
axial strain distribution within the elastic region during the 
loading cycle. Additionally, as the deformation starts progress-
ing, the lattice misfit changes its position to maintain lattice 
continuity, potentially giving rise to vacancies.
The stress near the inclusion is high because of the mismatch 
in lattice structure with the surrounding material and the local-
ized stresses. High stresses developed in these lattice structures 
exceed the point where the material can deform without per -
manent damage. As a result, plastic strain, or permanent defor-
mation, occurs around the inclusion. Due to this, local strain 
concentration is noticed at the top and bottom of the inclusion. 
However, the location of higher and lower strain regions is con-
trolled by the sign of eigenstrain and depends on the loading 
conditions. For positive lattice misfit, the plastic strain fields 
include the emergence of cross-shaped areas with lower strains 
in the [100] direction and spherical regions with higher strains 
[010] [Fig. 5(a) and (b)]. In contrast, for the negative lattice mis-
fit, higher strains are seen in the [100] direction, whereas lower 
strains are in the [010] direction, as shown in Fig. 5(c) and (d).
Ashutosh et al. [27] showed that strain concentration near 
the interfacial region strongly affects deformation. Moreover, 
axial strain concentration sustains plastic flow through the 
continual emission of dislocations, a process that relies on the 
diffusion of atoms or vacancies [2, 6]. Lattice misfits act as pref-
erential sites for dislocation nucleation within the γ matrix [6]. 
With increasing deformation, strain concentrations around 
inclusions escalate, leading to the formation of microcracks at 
sites of high stress. These microcracks subsequently promote 
interfacial debonding through void nucleation; once formed, 
the voids expand along the interface, culminating in fracture.

Concluding remarks
The combined effect of misfit strains and external loading on the 
deformation mechanism in the presence of inclusions was stud-
ied in this work. A multi-phase and multicomponent phase-field 
model is proposed and developed. Plastic variables are calculated 
using the J2 plasticity theory. This study contains a homogene-
ous two-phase material with a spherical inclusion at the center 
to evaluate the effect of lattice misfits and external loading on the 
mechanical properties of Ni-based alloy. Numerical simulations 
are conducted under the applied tensile loading. As the lattice mis-
fit increases in magnitude, the simulations predicted the material 
would become softer. Additionally, lower yield stress and stress 
triaxiality are predicted.
Evolving von Mises stresses maximum values are found in 
the vicinity of the inclusion’s upper and lower boundaries and 
depend on the sign of lattice misfit. For positive misfit, the maxi-
mum stresses were seen along the [010] direction, whereas it was 
found along the [100] direction for negative misfit. For all the 
lattice misfits, simulations show a clear transition from the elas-
tic region to the plastic region. Plastic strain localization occurs 
in the vicinity of interfaces between the matrix and inclusions. 
However, the model predicted higher and lower regions of the 
plastic strain, and it depends on the sign of misfit. Microscopic 
scale results obtained from the proposed model agree well with 
the macroscopic findings. The magnitude and sign of lattice mis-
fits affect the micromechanical properties under external loading. 
Moreover, the developed model effectively predicts the stresses 
and strain evolution during plastic deformation occurring in the 
vicinity of the matrix and inclusion interfaces.
Methods
The phase-field model in this work is formulated by defining 
a function that includes information about thermodynam -
ics, interfacial, and material properties. To differentiate the 
multiphases and multicomponent present in the system, order 
parameters (ϕ ) are defined inside the interface (physically 
separated) regions varying continuously between 0 and 1 to 
describe a diffuse interface. This approach results in identify -
ing the defined phases as continuous fields and eliminating the 
need to explicitly track the interface. In contrast, sharp inter -
face models have a sharp curve at the interface that needs to be 
tracked. Additionally, the phase-field models possess inherent 
capabilities of capturing accurate location of phase boundaries 
and movement of the interface. Within the diffuse interface, 
grain boundaries are defined by a finite interface width ξ.

described by Mushongera et al. [17, 18] incorporates the impacts

ered macroscopic, and the stress and strain fields are periodic 
within the unit cell. Furthermore, the external load is applied 
by considering strain-controlled boundary conditions.
The proposed phase-field model equations are solved 
using the explicit finite difference method. A Jacobi relaxa-
tion method is introduced in the phase-field model to solve 
the mechanical equilibrium of the system numerically. For 
all the phase-field simulations, a time step of /Delta1t= 5 × 10−3 
s is applied. All simulations are carried out using an in-house 
code. The resulting simulation results are presented in a 
dimensionless form.
