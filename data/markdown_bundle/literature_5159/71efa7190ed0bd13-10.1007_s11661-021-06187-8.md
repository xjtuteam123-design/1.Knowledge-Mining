ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Mechanistic Modeling of Cyclic Softening and Slip
Localization in Ni-Based Superalloys
KWAI S. CHAN
Ni-based superalloys are typically strengthened with ordered fcc c0 precipitates (L1 2 structure)
which impart a number of hardening mechanisms including anomalous hardening due to cross
slip from {111} to {010} planes. On the other hand, the presence of shearable c0 precipitates
promotes cyclic softening and slip localization. The onset of cyclic softening and slip
localization processes in c0-strengthened Ni-based superalloys is still poorly understood and has
not been modeled. In this investigation, the salient mechanisms responsible for cyclic softening
and slip localization in Ni-alloys containing c0 precipitates are reviewed and the pertinent
information is utilized to develop mechanistic models for predicting the cyclic response, slip
localization, and fatigue life of this class of engineering alloys. Mechanistic modeling reveals
that cyclic softening and slip localization can be attributed to three processes: (1) stable expan-
sion of superkinks with subcritical kink heights, (2) shearing, and (3) bowing of c0 precipitates.
The onset of cyclic softening commences in the microplastic regime and occurs when the
accumulated shear strain along the operative {111} plane exceeds the average c0 size. The roles
of cyclic softening and slip localization in reducing high-cycle fatigue strength and fatigue life
are elucidated and discussed.

I. INTRODUCTION
IT is well-known that Ni-based superalloys derive
most of their high strengths from the presence of c0
precipitates in the microstructure, which are ordered
face-centered cubic (fcc) phase with the L1 2 structure. In
particular, the c0 precipitates instigate several strength-
ening mechanisms including weak-pair coupling, [1–3]
strong-pair coupling, [4] anomalous hardening [5–7] due
to cross slip from {111} planes to {001} planes, [8–11] and,
among others, bowing of dislocations [2] around c0
precipitates.[3] On the other hand, shearing of c0 precip-
itates under fatigue loading has been shown to result in
cyclic softening and slip localization, [12–21] which in
turns often leads to crystallographic cracking along
{111} slip planes and facet formation in Ni-based
superalloys. Slip localization along (111} planes has
been shown to inﬂuence the low-cycle fatigue (LCF) and
high-cycle fatigue (HCF) strengths of Udimet 720. [22]
Cyclic softening and fatigue cracking along slip bands
have been reported for a number of wrought Ni-base
alloys.[12–22] Merrick[12] observed cyclic softening in
wrought Alloy 901, Waspaloy, and Alloy 718 during
fatigue testing at ambient and elevated temperatures.
Slip in these alloys involved shearing and possibly
dissolution of c0 and c00 precipitates (Alloy 718) and the
fatigue cracks nucleated and propagated along the
active {111} slip planes. The cyclic response of Was-
paloy was studied at room temperature by Stoltz and
Pineau[13] as a function of c0 size, ranging from 8 to 90
nm. They reported that cyclic softening in Waspaloy
occurred when small c0 precipitates (8 nm) were sheared,
while stable, saturated hysteresis loops were obtained
when bowing of dislocations around c0 occurred when
the c0 size exceeded 25 nm. Similarly, cyclic softening
was observed by Lerch et al .[14] during fatigue of
Waspaloy at 298 K to 1973 K (25 /C176 C to 800 /C176 C) in
materials heat-treated to exhibit coarse-grained (125
lm) and ﬁne-grained (16 lm) microstructures contain-
ing either ﬁne (5 to 8 nm) c0 or coarse (90 to 300 nm) c0.
Subsequent work on NIMONIC 80A, [15,16] 90,[17]
PE16,[18,19] and Alloy 720 Li [21] supported the notion
that shearing of ﬁne c0 precipitates led to cyclic
softening, while cyclic saturation was observed in
NIMONIC 115 [20] with large primary c0 size (770 nm).
These results indicate that the c0 size plays an important
role in inﬂuencing the cyclic stress–strain response of
Ni-based alloys. Besides shearing of c0 precipitates,
cyclic softening in Ni-based alloys has been attributed to
disordering and/or dissolution of the c0 phase. Trans-
mission electron microcopy studies [12,13,19] of fatigued
Ni-based alloys provided direct evidence of shearing of
c0 precipitates and the reduction of sheared c0

Many of these Ni-based alloys also exhibit bilinear

In recent years, there has been numerous eﬀorts to
develop yield stress [3,23–26] and constitutive models [27,28]
for Ni-based superalloys. These eﬀorts were focused on
the development of physics-based constitutive models
for predicting the yield stress and/or stress–strain
response of Ni-based alloys subjected to monotonic
loadings. To the author’s knowledge, none of the
existing physics-based yield stress or constitutive models
for Ni-based superalloys consider the relevant cyclic
hardening or softening mechanisms and are not capable
of predicting the cyclic yield stresses or the cyclic
stress–strain curves. In general, the cyclic stress–strain
response of structural alloys including Ni-based alloys
are characterized by plotting the stress amplitude as a
function of fatigue cycles to depict either a cyclic
hardening or a cyclic softening curve. Alternately, the
cyclic stress amplitude of the saturated hysteresis loops
can be plotted against the plastic strain amplitude to
form the cyclic stress–plastic strain curve. [20] Currently,
these cyclic stress–strain response of Ni-based alloys are
entirely formulated empirically by ﬁtting to experimen-
tal data without considering the underlying dislocation
or deformation mechanisms. Thus, there is a need for a
physics-based mechanistic model for predicting the
cyclic stress–strain response including cyclic hardening
and/or cyclic softening in Ni-based superalloys.
The aim of this article is to report the results of an
investigation focused on the development of a physics-
based constitutive model for predicting the cyclic
stress–strain response of Ni-based superalloys. The
approach utilized to develop the physics-based cyclic
constitutive model for Ni-based superalloys is outlined
in Figure 1. First, the cyclic hardening and cyclic
softening responses of several wrought Ni-based alloys
in the literature are analyzed and the results are utilized
to identify the pertinent mechanisms and microstruc-
tural parameter(s) responsible for the onset of cyclic
softening. As presented in Section II, most of the cyclic
stress vs fatigue cycle data for Ni-based superalloys in
the literature are available for ambient temperatures,
only limited sets of cyclic stress vs. cycle data are
available for elevated temperatures. The cyclic softening
mechanisms, which are shearing and dislocation bowing
of c0 particles, are expected to be diﬀerent from those at
elevated temperatures higher than 923 K (650 /C176 C),
which may include thermal softening, coarsening and
rafting of c0 precipitates. Thus, the eﬀorts of this study
are focused on cyclic softening at ambient and interme-
diate temperatures ( < 923K) where thermal instability
of the c0 particles is unlikely to occur and can be ignored.
The c0 shearing and dislocation bowing mechanisms are
expected to be pertinent from ambient temperature to
elevated temperatures as high as 923 K (650 /C176 C).
Second, the formulation of a physics-based mechanistic
model for treating cyclic softening and slip localization
in Ni-based superalloys is presented in Section III.A
cyclic constitutive model for Ni-based superallloys is
presented in Section IV. In Section V, the cyclic consti-
tutive model is applied to evaluate the interrelations
between cyclic stress–strain response and strain-life
curves, as well as to treat LCF/HCF interactions.
Besides developing a physics-based cyclic constitutive
model for Ni-based superalloys, some of the signiﬁcant
contributions of this study include: (1) demonstrating
the need for including cyclic softening mechanisms for
representing the cyclic stress–strain response; (2) iden-
tifying the important roles played by edge character of
superkinks on cyclic softening and slip localization
along {111} planes; (3) establishing the direct relation
between two-stage cyclic strain exponents and the
bilinear strain-life curves; and (4) determining the origin
of LCF/HCF interactions and the corresponding fatigue
life debits observed in Ni-based superalloys such as
Udimet 720 Li.

VI. DISCUSSION
One of the signiﬁcant ﬁndings of this investigation is
that both the cyclic hardening and the cyclic softening
regimes in Ni-based superalloys can be described in
terms of the Ramberg–Osgood type power-laws [38] with
positive n1 values for the cyclic hardening regime and
negative n2 values for the cyclic softening regime. The
transition from the cyclic hardening regime to the cyclic
softening regime at a given plastic strain range occurs at
the peak stress and at the number of fatigue cycles ( N*)
that corresponds to shearing of c0 particles into two
halves. Small c0 particles that are less than 20 nm in
diameter can be cut by dislocations via the weak-pair
coupling mechanism and the reduction in the c0 size
lowers the ﬂow stress required to continue subsequent
slip. In comparison, the ﬂow stress associated with the
strong-pair coupling mechanisms is increased when the
c0 size is reduced. Thus, shearing of secondary c0 may not
immediately lead to cyclic softening if the c0 particles are
suﬃciently large so that a large number of fatigue cycles
is required to reduce the c0 size below the 20 nm limit
required to instigate c0 shearing by the weak-pair
coupling mechanism. For c0 particles with size greater
than 90 nm, bowing of c0 particles by dislocations may
be activated and prevent the occurrence of c0 shearing.
Under this circumstance, cyclic softening is replaced by
cyclic saturation since the c0 size is not reduced by
bowing of dislocations around c0 particles. By virtue of
diﬀerent operative deformation mechanisms, the cyclic
softening exponent (n 2) is most negative at small c0 sizes
(< 20 nm) and the n2 value becomes less negative with
increasing c0 size. At very large c0 sizes (>570 nm), the n2
value approaches zero (cyclic saturation) or becomes
slightly positive (cyclic hardening) as shown in
Figure 11(b).
The c0 size in Ni-based superalloys typically exhibits
as much as three size distributions due to the presence of
primary, secondary, and tertiary c0 precipitates in the
microstructure. The relatively large variations in the n1
and n2 values with c0 sizes may be attributed to the
presence of bimodal or tri-model size distributions, as
well as variations of superkink density and operative
softening mechanisms (weak-pair coupling, strong-pair
coupling, and dislocation bowing) in individual c0
precipitates. The roles played by the superkinks are
two folds: (1) the shear characters of the superkinks
allow cross slip of the superkinks from {111} to
{010}[9,42] which subsequently become immobilized on
{010},[9,42] and (2) the edge characters of the superkinks
are mobile and allow lateral propagation of the super-
kinks on {111} planes. [9,35] Subsequent modeling work
by Sun [35] showed that the ﬂow stress for continued
lateral expansion of the mobile edge characters of the
superkinks depend on the kink height. In particular, the
ﬂow stress is increased when the kink height exceeds the
critical kink height but is reduced when this stability
criterion is not met.
The results of the current investigation also indicate
that a cyclic softening term is required in the cyclic
constitutive model in order to provide a realistic
description of the cyclic stress–strain response of
Ni-based superalloys. For example, the cyclic constitu-
tive equation based on a power-law relation between
stress amplitude and plastic strain amplitude, Eq. [ 27],
does not predict cyclic saturation or cyclic softening, as
shown by the solid curve in Figure 12(a). A cyclic
softening term such as that shown in Eqs. [ 25]a n d[ 26]i s
required in order to provide cyclic saturation or cyclic
softening responses after a number of fatigue numbers.
Until now, this point is often overlooked in the literature
and the current cyclic constitutive model appears to be
the ﬁrst constitutive model to treat cyclic softening and/
or slip localization.
As shown in Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, the onset of cyclic
softening does not occur in the ﬁrst fatigue cycle. The
number of fatigue cycles at the onset of cyclic softening
increases with decreasing strain ranges. After the onset
of cyclic softening, the ﬂow resistance of the operative
slip system(s), typically on {111} planes, is reduced such
that subsequent slip tends to continue on the active slip
system(s), thereby resulting in slip localization. Latent
hardening and slip irreversibility tend to occur in the
cyclic hardening regime and is unlikely to continue after
the onset of cyclic softening. Thus, slip localization can
be expected to be triggered once cyclic softening occurs.
Slip localization can occur on one active {111} slip
plane, which would lead to shearing of c0 into several
smaller particles. [19] The shearing of c0 by slip on
multiple parallel {111} slip planes is consistent with
the results of a multiscale study by Leon-Cazares et al .
[43] who investigated the evolution of slip line spacing
and slip steps associated with {111} slip bands in PM

After the onset of cyclic softening ( N > N*), cyclic
hardening transitions to cyclic softening rapidly and a
change from a positive n1 slope to a negative n2 slope
occurs rather abruptly. In many instances, n2 appears to
be related to n1 but with a negative sign. The n2 regime
occurs even in materials with secondary c0 precipitates
which are suﬃciently large such that a reduction in the c0
size to below 20 nm may be diﬃcult. At large c0 sizes (>
20 nm), disordering and/or dissolution of sheared c0 may
not occur or be feasible for triggering localization of slip
along {111} planes. A detailed examination of the cyclic
softening and slip localization processes during fatigue
loading at a constant plastic strain range reveals that a
change in the c0 size due to shearing of c0 can trigger
cyclic softening when the density of the post-critical
superkinks is substantially reduced in the operative
{111} slip bands. This triggering event can happen even
during shearing of secondary c0 particles and occur prior
to separation of large c0 particles into two halves, as well
as the disordering and dissolution of sheared particles.
In a previous overview paper, Gerold and Karnthaler [44]
indicated that planar slip in many metallic alloys may
originate from short range order or small shearable
precipitates, in which slip either under monotonic or
cyclic loading can destroy short range clustering and
ordered precipitates. The results of this investigation
suggests a mechanism in which the planarity of slip can
be attributed to slip localization along {111} slip planes
due to propagation of stable superkinks with kink
height below the critical kink value such that slip occurs
at zero or negative strain hardening. On this basis, the
destruction of short range clustering and ordered
precipitates may also be a manifestation of the propa-
gation characteristics of subcritical superkinks.
Recently, Stocker et al .[45] reported that planar disloca-
tion structures were observed in NIMONIC 75 and 80A
in the HCF (10 6 cycles) and in the Very High-Cycle
(VHCF or 10 7 cycles) regimes.
Cyclic hardening and cyclic softening in Ni-based
superalloys depend on dislocation interactions with the
c0 particles. The cyclic softening mechanisms commence
with the shearing of c0 particles into two halves along a
single {111} plane, which subsequently leads to drastic
changes in the density of post-critical kink heights below
the critical values. The cyclic softening exponent is most
negative at small c0 sizes (less than 20 nm), but increase
(become less negative) with increasing c0 sizes. In
contrast, the cyclic hardening exponent depends on an
increase of post-critical superkink density and its values
increases with the number of {111} slip systems acti-
vated and the sum of the squares of the volume fractions
of primary and secondary c0 precipitates. The diﬀerent
dependence of the cyclic hardening exponent ( n1)a n d
the cyclic softening exponent ( n2) are shown in
Figure 11(a) and (b), respectively. Furthermore,
Figure 11(a) shows the cyclic softening exponent ( n2)
appears not to correlate with the volume fractions of c0
while the cyclic hardening exponent ( n1) appears not to
correlate with the c0 size. These diﬀerences in cyclic
response, which are delineated clearly in Figures 9 and
12, can be attributed to diﬀerences in the cyclic
hardening and softening mechanisms. TEM studies
indicated that planar {111} slip occurred in polycrys-
talline and single crystal Ni-based superalloys in the
LCF, HCF, and VHCF regimes. [45–48] Furthermore,
in-situ scanning electron microscopy revealed the for-
mation and propagation of slip band cracking along
{111} planes. [49] In PM Rene 88DT, nucleation of slip
band cracks occurred on {111} planes that were parallel
to twin boundaries. [50] In all cases, the dislocation
structures within the localized slip bands consist of
planar arrays resemble those observed in persistent slip
bands.[16, 47,48] It should also be noted that c0 shearing by
{111} slip is prevalent at ambient and elevated temper-
atures up to 923 K (650 /C176 C). Above 923 K (650 /C176 C),
cyclic softening mechanisms may occur such that slip
localization may not be induced by c0 shearing alone.
For examples. {001} may be operative and dislocation
climb on {001} and {111} at temperatures above 1023 K
(750 /C176 C). At even higher temperatures, thermal soften-
ing, coarsening or rafting of c0 can also occur and lead to
cyclic softening.
Another important ﬁnding of this investigation is the
development of a set of quantitative models for treating
slip localization, fatigue crack nucleation, and fatigue
failure life. Speciﬁcally, Eq. [ 4] provides the plastic strain
range and slip localization relation, while Eq. [ 32] gives
the relation between plastic strain range and the cycles
to crack nucleation and failure, respectively. Compar-
ison of model calculations and experimental data shows
that shearing of c0 leads to cyclic softening and slip
localization along {111} slip bands by virtue of changes
in the dislocation structure and reductions in the
post-critical superkink density. As a result, Ni-based
superalloys strengthened with c0 precipitates are prone
to planar slip and cracking along {111} planes, even
though the ordered fcc c0 phase (L12 structure) is known
to promote anomalous hardening. From the results of
this study, it is apparent that the dichotomy can be
explained on the basis that cyclic hardening and cyclic
softening in Ni-based superalloys is controlled by the
density of superkinks with kink heights that are greater
than the critical kink height. Cyclic hardening is
observed when the density of post-critical superkinks
(h > /C22h) is high. In contrast, cyclic softening and slip
localization result when the reverse is true. After slip
localization, fatigue cracks nucleate on {111} slip bands.
Both the nucleation and failure lives of these slip band
cracks exhibit bilinear cyclic strain-life curve responses,
as shown in Figure 13. The origins of the bilinear
strain-life curves can be traced back to the bilinear cyclic
stress–strain curves, as shown in Eqs. [ 31] and [ 32]. It
should also be noted that cyclic softening, slip localiza-
tion, and crack nucleation can all lead to load drops. It
is expected that the magnitude of the load drops
increases with the progression from cyclic softening,
slip localization, and then to crack nucleation. It is also
a common practice to use a speciﬁed percentage of the
load drops as an indication of the onset of crack
nucleation. Based on the results of this study, it is
apparent that the strain-life curve developed on the basis

In summary, a physics-based cyclic constitutive model
has been developed in this investigation on the basis of
cyclic hardening and cyclic softening mechanisms. The
cyclic hardening mechanisms are based on expansions of
superkinks with kink height above a critical kink height,
while the cyclic softening mechanisms, which include
dislocation shearing and bowing of precipitates, operate
at superkinks with subcritical kink heights. The impor-
tance of a cyclic softening term in the cyclic constitutive
model is emphasized as its existence is required to attain
cyclic saturation, slip localization, and fatigue crack
nucleation. The proposed cyclic constitutive model has
been utilized to treat and analyze LCF/HCF interac-
tions and potential reduced HCF lives due to prior LCF
loading in the Ni-based superalloy Udimet 720.
VII. CONCLUSIONS
The cyclic softening and slip localization processes in
Ni-based superalloys are analyzed in order to establish
pertinent constitutive relations and strain-life prediction
models. The conclusions reached in this investigation
are as follows:
1. Both cyclic hardening and cyclic softening
responses in wrought Ni-based superalloys can be
described in terms of the Ramberg-Osgood type
power-law constitutive relations between stress
amplitude and plastic strain amplitude. Cyclic
hardening is characterized by a positive strain
hardening value, while cyclic softening is character-
ized by a negative strain hardening value. Multiple
stages of cyclic hardening and cyclic softening are
possible.
2. The onset of cyclic softening in wrought Ni-based
superalloys occurs at the peak stress as the result of
shearing of c0 precipitates into two halves by

## Page 17

weak-pair coupling and/or strong-pair coupling
mechanisms. Cyclic saturation occurs when c0
shearing is replaced by bowing of c0 by dislocations
when the c0 precipitates are too large to be sheared
effectively. The transition from c0 shearing to
bowing occurs at a c0 size in the range of about
500 to 600 nm or greater.
3. Slip localization along {111} planes appears to be
caused by shearing of c0 particles which, in turns,
trigger a drastic reduction of post-critical disloca-
tion density to fall below the critical kink height,
and promote cyclic softening.
4. Cyclic stress amplitude vs. plastic strain amplitude
curve is different from cyclic stress amplitude vs.
cumulative plastic strain curve or stress amplitude
vs. fatigue cycle curve. Cyclic softening can be
modeled in terms of the cumulative plastic strain
approach that includes the number of fatigue cycles
explicitly in the constitutive model. Conventional
cyclic stress amplitude vs. cyclic plastic strain
amplitude curve is valid only at fatigue cycles prior
to the onset of cyclic softening.
5. Bilinear cyclic stress–strain curves lead to bilinear
cyclic plastic strain range vs fatigue life curves.
Changes in the slope of the plastic strain amplitude
vs. fatigue life in the high-cycle fatigue (HCF)
regime results in a drastic decrease of the HCF
strength at 10 6 cycles from the low-cycle fatigue
(LCF) strength at 10 4 cycles.
6. A set of mechanistic models have been developed
and utilized to treat cyclic softening and slip
localization along {111} planes, fatigue crack nucle-
ation and crystallographic fracture along planar
{111} slip bands, as well as LCF/HCF interactions.
