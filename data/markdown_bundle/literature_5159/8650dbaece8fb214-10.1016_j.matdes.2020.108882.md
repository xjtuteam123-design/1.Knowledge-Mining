Fire resistance of structural steels used in buildings is typically evaluated based on the ratio of the yield strength
at 600 °C to that at room temperature. Fire-resistant steel shows a yield strength ratio of more than 2:3 between
600 °C and room temperature and achieves an excellent high-temperature strength. Alloying elements such as
Mo and Nb are used to improve the strength at elevated temperatures. In this study, the effects of the addition
of Mo and Nb on the ﬁre resistance of steel were carefully investigated using SEM, TEM, in-situ TEM, EPMA,
3D-APT, positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy, and ﬁrst principles calculations. The ﬁre resistance in Mo
and Nb added steel (Mo + Nb) was shown to be drastically improved compared to plain carbon steel
(C\\Mn). The improvement in the high-temperature strength in Mo + Nb was attributed to the precipitation
of ﬁne Nb-rich MX particles and a solid solution of Mo and Nb hindering the dislocation movement, and thereby
minimizing the dislocation annihilation at elevated temperature. The solid solution of Mo and Nb lowers the va-
cancy formation energy and allows a vacancy to form more easily, leading to a large lattice distortion which re-
sults in a slower dislocation mobility in Mo + Nb.

1. Introduction
Building structures are increasing in height, and there is an ongoing
demand for ensuring the safety of skyscrapers against frequently occur-
ring natural disasters such as earthquakes and typhoons, as well as so-
cial disasters such as ﬁres. Signi ﬁcant attention has been paid to the
stability of buildings against ﬁres, which can cause a rapid collapse
[1–21]. Fire resistance in buildings is highly dependent on the steel
used, which is the backbone material of a building structure. The steel
used in a building construction with enhanced tensile properties at
elevated temperature is called ﬁre-resistant steel [3–6,10–14].
The ﬁre resistance in steels is generally measured using the ratio of
yield strength at 600 °C to that at room temperature (RT), and this
ratio for ﬁre-resistant steel is higher than 2:3. Meanwhile, plain carbon
steel has the ratio within the range of 1:3 to 1:2 and a steep decline in
yield strength at over 500 °C. With an increase in temperature, precipi-
tation and growth of the precipitates, a decrease in the concentration of
solid solution elements, annihilation of the dislocations, and recovery
and/or recrystallization can occur in steel. The high temperature
strength of steel is reduced owing to these microstructural changes
when heated. Attempts to secure such a high-temperature strength
have been studied using various methods such as controlling the precip-
itation behavior or hindering the movements of the dislocations at high
temperature [1–5,9,12–15,18]. In addition, some studies have applied
bainite microstructures with a high density of dislocations to improve
the ﬁre resistance [ 6,10,19,21]. Mo and Nb are often used as critical
alloying elements to increase the strength at both RT and high temper-
atures. Mo strengthens the steel through solid solution or precipitation
of Mo carbides, or through transformation to hard phases such as bai-
nite and martensite due to an enhanced hardenability. Nb plays several
roles in steel, precipitating ﬁne Nb-rich MX particles or increasing the
hardenability for greater strengthening. The effects of Mo and Nb on
the ﬁre resistance of steel have been indirectly examined in previous
studies using specimens held at high temperature or after applying a
tensile test at elevated temperature [1–21]. However, there are few pa-
pers to focus on dislocations behavior at high temperature, which is a
critical factor for strengthening. In this study, we investigated directly
the inﬂuence of Mo and Nb and the dislocation characteristics during
heating to 600 °C through an in-situ TEM technique using a heating
stage and discussed the results based on ﬁrst principles calculations
and positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy (PALS) measurements.
2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Materials
The chemical compositions of the examined steels are listed in
Table 1. To investigate the effects of Mo and Nb on the ﬁre resistance,
two types of steel were prepared, namely, C \\Mn as a reference with
only 0.1 wt% carbon (C) and 1.5 wt% manganese (Mn) and Mo + Nb
containing 0.3 wt% molybdenum (Mo) and 0.05 wt% niobium (Nb). In
addition, 10 kg ingots of the examined steel were fabricated using a vac-
uum induction melting furnace. The ingots were soaked at 1200 °C for
2 h, followed by hot-rolling into plates 11 mm thick at higher than
900 °C, and then air-cooling, as shown in Fig. 1.
2.2. Microstructure examination
The microstructures were characterized using optical microscopy
(OM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-7001F, JEOL) and trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL). The OM and SEM
samples were prepared using a 1% picral solution (1 g of picric acid
+5 ml HCL + 100 ml ethyl alcohol) after mechanical polishing. The
samples for TEM were thin discs of 3 mm in diameter, mechanically
polished to approximately 70 μm in thickness and then electrochemi-
cally polished in a twin-jet polisher using a solution of 10% perchloric
acid and 90% methanol at −30 °C. The concentrations of Mo and Nb in
a solid solution were analyzed on 20 points in the specimens before
and after a tensile test at 600 °C using an electron probe x-ray micro an-
alyzer (EPMA, JXA-8530F, JEOL).
To acquire the dislocation behavior at elevated temperature, an
experiment was carried out using an in-situ TEM heating holder
(652 double tilt heating holder, Gatan Inc.). TEM samples were
heated up to 600 °C at a rate of 100 °C/min, and held at the saturation
temperature for 5 min. The change in the microstructure was cap-
tured in real-time using a CCD camera (Orius SC200D, Gatan Inc.)
with a frame rate of 30 fps. A frame-by-frame analysis of the in-situ
obtained video clip was executed using Image-J software. (version
1.52). To ensure the dislocation d ensity measurement, the samples
were aligned to low index zone-axis where the g⸱b invisible condition
is effectively avoided. In addition, we tried to making the dislocation
imaging at relatively thinner region (below about 100 nm) in the
TEM samples to prevent from overlaps between the dislocations
along the viewing direction. The number of dislocations in the ac-
quired TEM images was manually counted by using the Image-J soft-
ware, then divided by the total area of the image to make the
dislocation density in a dimension of /m

2.3. Tensile tests
Tensile testing was conducted at RT using sub-sized specimens with
dimensions of 5 mm in thickness, 6 mm in width, and 25 mm in gauge
length at a crosshead speed of 2 mm/min using a universal tensile test-
ing machine (INSTRON 5882, INSTRON) according to ASTM E8M.
For high-temperature tensile testing, small round specimens were
machined with 6 mm diameter, 30 mm gauge length, 6 mmﬁllet radius,
and 36 mm length of the reduced parallel section in accordance with
ASTM E8M. The tests were carried out after holding at 600 °C for
20 min, and under crosshead speeds of 0.21 and 1.5 mm/min before
and after yielding, respectively, using a tensile testing machine
(AG-250KNXPLUS, SHIMADZU) in compliance with ASTM E21. All ten-
sile specimens were machined in the longitudinal direction of the
hot-rolled plates.
2.4. First principles calculations
All ﬁrst principles electronic structure calculations are conducted
within the density functional theory (DFT) using the Vienna ab-initio
simulation package (VASP). To simulate the solution of M atoms
(M = Cr, Mo, Nb, Ni, and Mn) in ferrite, a 3 × 3 × 3 supercell with 54 lat-
tice sites, as shown inFig. 2(a), was used. A vacancy is assumed to be po-
sitioned at the closest atomic position of each substitutional solute
atom. The total energies were obtained using a Perdew-Burke-
Ernzerhof (PBE) realization of the generalized gradient approximation
(GGA) for the exchange-correlation potential. A plane-wave basis set
with a cut-off energy level of 720 eV was used in all calculations. The in-
tegrations over the three-dimensional Brillouin zone were calculated by
a tetrahedron method over a 4 × 4 × 4 Monkhorst-Pack mesh for the
supercell. The initial lattice parameters were adapted from experimen-
tal values of ferrite. An internal atomic relaxation was achieved when
the change in the total energy was smaller than 1.0 × 10

4. Conclusions
In this study, the effects of Mo and Nb on the ﬁre resistance in steel
were investigated and the following conclusions were obtained.
(1) Fire resistance, namely, the ratio of yield strength at 600 °C to
that at RT was drastically improved in Mo + Nb compared to
that in C\\Mn.
(2) The improvement in the high-temperature strength in Mo + Nb
was attributed to the precipitation of ﬁne Nb-rich MX particles
and a solid solution of Mo and Nb hindering the dislocation
movement, and thereby minimizing the dislocation annihilation
at elevated temperature, which was directly observed through
an in-situ TEM technique using a heating stage.
