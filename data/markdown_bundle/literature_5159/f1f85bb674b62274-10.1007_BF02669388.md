Effect of Solutes in Binary Columbium 
(Nb) Alloys on Creep Strength 
M. J. KLEIN AND A. G. METCALFE 
The effect of seven different solutes in binary columbium (Nb) alloys on creep strength was 
determined from 1400 to 3400~ (760 to 1871~ for solute concentrations to 20 at. pct using 
a new method of creep strength measurement. The technique permits rapid determination 
of approximate creep strength over a large temperature span. All of the elements were 
found to increase the creep strength of columbium except tantalum. This element did not 
strengthen columbium until the concentration exceeded 10 at. pct. Hafnium, zirconium, and 
vanadium strengthed columbium most at low temperatures and concentrations, whereas 
tungsten, molybdenum, and rhenium contributed more to creep strength at high tempera- 
tures and concentrations. 
COLUMBIUM (Nb) is an attractive metal for use as a 
structural material at high temperatures. It is easy to 
fabricate, ductile at low temperatures, has a low den- 
sity for a refractory metal, and can be adequately pro- 
tected from oxidation by coatings. In addition, colum- 
bium forms the base for a family of creep-reistant al- 
loys; yet there is little published information concern- 
ing the effect of specific solutes on the creep strength 
of columbium. In the present study, a rapid creep test- 
ing technique was utilized to show the effect of seven 
different solutes in binary columbium-base alloys on 
creep strength. The technique yields only approximate 
creep strengths but is of value in showing relative 
strengths and in providing background creep data 
needed for this alloy system. In turn, these results 
form the basis for the second part of this investigation, 
the effect of solute interactions on the creep strength 
of ternary alloys, presented in a second paper. 
In this study, the contribution of grain size and other 
structural features to creep strength is not distin- 
guished from solid solution strengthening. In effect, 
the strength imparted to columbium by solutes is con- 
sidered without attributing their strength contribution 
to a specific source or strengthening mechanism. This 
phenomenological approach was used because it would 
have been almost impossible to test the number of al- 
loys and to cover the range of composition and temper- 
ature within the time available, if structure and 
strengthening mechanisms were also analyzed. 
Concentrations are given in at. pct, and the creep 
rate is 10 -5 per s, unless otherwise noted. The sym- 
bols, a and T are used as abbreviations for true stress 
and temperature.

the true stress required to yield a steady-state creep 
rate of 10 -5 per s. This creep rate was selected because 
it permitted completion of a test in a convenient length

a large number of specimens and separate tests would

Creep strength was determined over a range of tem- 
perature with the new method of measurement using 
only a single test specimen rather than a specimen at 
each test temperature. The specimen was crept at a 
high temperature, and the stress was adjusted until a 
steady-state creep rate of approximately 10 -5 per s was 
attained. The temperature was decreased, and the 
stress was increased until the creep rate was again 
about 10 -5 per s. This procedure was repeated until the 
entire stress-temperature profile had been determined. 
All specimens were initially held at 3000~ (1649~ for 
30 min to allow the structure to stabilize before begin- 
ning the tests. The time required to attain steady-state 
creep after load adjustments varied from almost zero 
to about 60 min (3600 s). In general, the longer times 
were required at the lower temperatures. Steady-state 
creep was maintained for about 20 rain (600 s). Tests 
were conducted in a vacuum of 10 -7 to 10 -8 Torr (1.3 
 10 -5 to 1.3  10 -6 N/m 2) and both strain and load were 
continuously measured and automatically recorded. 
The vacuum creep testing machine was equipped with 
a high-temperature strain extensometer and a Fullman 
type lever arm. 1 The true stress was determined from 
the load by allowing for the reduction in specimen cross 
section induced by specimen elongation. This test tech- 
nique is designated as "single specimen method" in 
this paper, and the conventional method which requires 
a different specimen and separate test at each temper- 
ature is designated as "multiple specimen method." 
Comparison of Methods 
The single and multiple specimen methods were com- 
pared using some commercial columbium-base alloys 
(D43, C103, C129Y, Cb-lZr)and some special alloys 
fabricated for these tests (Cb-2.5 Zr, Cb-5 Zr, Cb- 
2.5V). The two methods, in general, did not givethe 
same absolute value of creep strength at a given tem- 
perature for these alloys. However, the relative

Reproducibility 
The cr-T profiles derived using the single specimen 
method were found to give reasonably consistent 
strength values for different specimens of the same 
alloy. Table II lists the strength of Cb-2.5 V derived 
from tests of specimens fabricated from different arc- 
melted buttons. The greatest difference in strength on 
a percentage comparison occurs at the highest temper- 
atures, where the stress to maintain a creep rate of 
10 -5 per s is low. 
Effect of Vacuum 
For refractory alloys, there is always the question 
of interstitial contamination of the specimen during 
creep testing and the possible effect this might have 
on creep behavior. Therefore, tests were made using 
the single specimen method to determine the effect of 
vacuums in the range 10 -5 to 10 -8 Torr (1.3  10 -2 to 
1.3  10 -6 N/m 2) on creep strength. Although alloys 
were tested that were susceptible to interstitial con- 
tamination, no effect of vacuum on creep strength was 
detected for these short duration tests. For example, 
creep strengths derived for Cb-5 Zr tested in vacuums 
of different intensity are listed in Table III. The differ- 
ences in strength values do not appear to be beyond the 
normal scatter expected for this measuring technique.

ANALYSES OF STRENGTHENING 
IN BINARY ALLOYS 
The ~-T profiles presented above show the effect of 
solute concentration and temperature on the creep 
strength of binary columbium-base alloys. It is desir- 
able to present these data in a clear and systematic 
way to facilitate analysis of the effect of solutes on 
creep strength. The three variables, strength, temper- 
ature, and composition, describe a surface in space, 
but the data can be presented effectively using two- 
dimensional plots and some parameters. In the follow- 
ing sections, the data of Fig. 1 are replotted using the 
later methods. 
Effect of Temperature and Concentration

strength of columbium can be represented by use of a 
strength parameter, SB, where

S B = O, the alloy has the same strength as columbium, 
and the solute has no effect on strength. 
In these analyses, the concentration is given in at. 
pct to facilitate comparison of the strengthening effec- 
tiveness of different solutes, and the temperature is 
designated as a fraction of the melting temperature of 
columbium (TM) even though the melting temperatures 
of some of the alloys are significantly different than 
that of columbium. This temperature scheme is a con- 
venient way to compare the strength of the various al- 
loys at the same temperature and is of more value in 
selecting alloys for practical applications than com- 
parisons made at the same homologous temperatures. 
In this regard, the effect of solutes on the melting point 
is but one of a number of parameters that have been 
correlated with the creep strength of alloys (e.g., elas- 
tic modulus, atomic size mismatch, short-range order) 
in an effort to explain the effect of substitutional sol- 
utes on creep strength.

As shown in Fig. 2, all of the solutes except tantalum 
(Fig. 2(f)) increase the creep strength of columbium 
over the temperature span studied. Tantalum weakens 
columbium or at least does not strengthen it for con- 
centrations to 10 at. pct. At 20 at. pct tantalum, the 
binary alloy is two to three times stronger than co- 
lumbium (i.e., S B = 1 to 2). Figs. 2(a) and (b) clearly 
show that the reactive elements hafnium and zirco- 
nium increase the strength of columbium most at low 
temperatures. These elements are generally consid- 
ered to be dispersion strengtheners since they combine 
with interstitial elements in the columbium to form a 
dispersed phase. The interaction between these ele- 
ments and the interstitials in columbium certainly con- 
tributes to strength at the lower temperatures through 
formation of dispersed phases and the resultant stabil- 
ization of structure. However, at the higher tempera- 
tures, the dispersed phases tend to agglomerate and/or 
dissolve, and therefore, contribute little to strength. 
Under such conditions, these elements must contribute 
to strength principally through solid solution effects. 
The values of S B for Cb-1 Hf at 0.7 and 0.78T M are not 
shown because of the scatter in the value of S B where 
the strength of the alloy and columbium are low and do 
not differ significantly. 
In contrast with the results for the reactive solutes 
hafnium and zirconium, the solutes molybdenum, tung- 
sten, and rhenium (Figs. 2(c), (d), and (e)) contribute 
more to the strength of columbium at higher tempera- 
tures. The effect of vanadium (Fig. 2(g)) on creep 
strength is somewhat similar to zirconium and hal-

nium in that vanadium contributes most to strength at 
the lower temperatures. The highest value of S B is for 
Cb-10 W at 0.7T M. At this temperature, the creep 
strength of this alloy is 13.5 times greater than the 
strength of columbium. 
The variation in creep strength with solute concen- 
tration in the binary alloys is shown more clearly in 
Fig. 3. The creep strengths of Cb-Hf and Cb-Zr alloys 
are most sensitive to concentration at 0.ST M and least 
sensitive at 0.78T M (Fig. 3(a) and 3(b)). At the latter 
temperature, the slope of the SB-Concentration profile 
is almost horizontal, showing little effect of concentra- 
tion on creep strength. In this regard, the greatest in- 
crease in creep strength occurs upon addition of the 
first at. pct of these solutes, and there is a peak in 
creep strength between 10 and 20 pct hafnium and be- 
tween 10 and 15 pct zirconium. Although there may be 
two phases present in the Cb-20 Hf and Cb-15 Zr alloys 
at the lower test temperatures, this cannot account for 
the drop in strength at high concentrations because the 
strength drop occurs at the highest test temperature 
where only a single phase would be present. 
The effect of solute concentration on creep strength 
is quite different for the Cb-Mo, Cb-W, and Cb-Re sys- 
tems. The creep strength of these alloys is most sensi- 
tive to concentration (greatest slope) at the highest 
temperature (0.78TM) and least sensitive at the lowest 
temperature (0.4TM). In addition, the concentrations 
to about 2.5 pct tend to strengthen less than equivalent 
higher concentrations. This is in contrast with the re- 
sults for hafnium and zirconium where the addition of 
the first at. pct produced the sharpest increase in creep 
strength. Low concentrations of zirconium or hafnium 
in columbium combine for the most part as interstitial 
dispersions that are effective strengtheners of colum- 
bium, especiall3r at low temperatures. The partition of 
higher concentrations of these solutes may favor solu- 
tion .where their contribution to strength is reduced 
relative to their contribution when combined as a dis- 
persed phase. Low concentrations of molybdenum, 
tungsten, and rhenium, on the other hand, may partition 
to sites that contribute little to structure strengthening 
such as coarse precipitates or grain boundaries where- 
as higher concentrations may partition primarily to 
solid solution where they contribute more to strength 
through lattice distortion, changes in elastic modulus, 
and other bulk lattice properties. 
As shown in Fig. 3(f), tantalum does not strengthen 
columbium until the concentration exceeds 10 at. pct. 
In contrast, the creep strength of vanadium shows a 
variation with concentration that is similar to the re- 
active elements, hafnium and zirconium.
