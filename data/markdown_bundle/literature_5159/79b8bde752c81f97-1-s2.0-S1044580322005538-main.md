ABSTRACT 
Iron-based 3d-transition group high-entropy alloys (HEAs), with remarkable wear resistance, high hardness, and 
excellent mechanical properties, are usually produced by a multiple-step high-energy-consumption smelting 
process. This study offers a novel process for fabricating FeCoNiCu HEAs by two-step hydrogen reduction using a 
mixture of Fe
2
O
3
, CoO, NiO, and CuO, after which a nearly full dense crack-free HEA was successfully produced. 
The alloy has a face-centered cubic (FCC) structure and an oxygen level as low as 0.10 wt%. After the first 
hydrogen reduction at 500 
◦
C for 2 h, the oxygen level decreased from 23.30 wt% to 2.05 wt% and the powder 
obtained consisted of the elemental metal powder with some minor alloying. A second hydrogen reduction 
occurred during sintering at temperatures above 800 
◦
C, in which near-full densification ( > 99% relative density) 
was achieved at temperatures as low as 900 
◦
C. All the compositions were homogeneously distributed in the alloy 
when the temperature was > 900 
◦
C, and a Cu-enriched FCC-structured secondary phase with two types of shapes 
was precipitated in the alloy. The final alloy showed excellent mechanical properties at room temperature, with a 
hardness of ~150 HV, compression strength up to 2.2 GPa, compression strain of > 12%, tensile strength of 475 
MPa, and tensile elongation of 7.9%. 
1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), also known as multi-principal element 
alloys, are composed of at least four principal elements with equal or 
near-equal molar ratios (5% to 35% each). The materials have attracted 
considerable attention from researchers worldwide since Yeh published 
the first article on them in 2004 [ 1 ]. 3d-transition group HEAs combined 
with major elements, such as Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, V, Cr, Mn, and Ti, exhibit 
remarkable properties, such as high wear resistance [ 2 – 4 ], excellent 
mechanical properties [ 5 – 11 ], good magnetic characteristics [ 12 – 15 ], 
and high corrosion resistance [ 16 – 18 ]. These materials can be applied in 
various fields, including aerospace, marine engineering, transportation, 
advanced nuclear energy, and electronic devices. 
Presently, 3d-transition group HEAs are usually produced by melting 
processes [ 19 ], such as arc melting [ 20 ], vacuum melting [ 21 ], and 
induction melting [ 22 ]. However, the melting processes for fabricating 
HEAs have the disadvantages of high energy consumption, heavy 
pollution, and long processing times. Furthermore, the processes may 
cause defects, such as composition segregation, vacancies, pores, coarse 
grains, and severe internal pressure residuals during thermal expansion 
and condensation, which require multistep follow-up processing to 
attain better alloy properties. Therefore, a novel approach for preparing 
HEAs with excellent performance, low pollution, and high efficiency is 
of great importance, especially in the era of carbon neutrality.

2.2. Characterization and testing 
The samples for metallographic examination were prepared by pol -
ishing the as-sintered samples with colloidal silica. The microstructure 
was examined under a scanning electron microscope (TESCAN MIRA4 
LMH, Czech Republic). The phase constituents were identified by XRD 
(PANalytical Empyrean) using the characteristic wavelength of the K α 
line for Cu (1.5418 Å). The sintered density was determined using the 
Archimedes method, and the oxygen concentration in the powder was 
measured using a combustion analyzer (LECO TCH600). 
The hardness values of the samples were analyzed using a digital 
Vickers hardness tester (HVS-50). Compressed cylindrical samples ( ϕ 6 
mm × 17 mm) and tensile bars were prepared from the as-sintered 
specimens using electrical discharge machining. Compression and ten -
sile measurements were conducted using an electronic universal testing 
machine (Instron 5969) at the strain rates of 0.5 and 0.1 mm/min, 
respectively. Each data point is the average of three measurements.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Comparison between one-step and two-step reductions 
A one-step hydrogen reduction of the compact oxide mixture was 
attempted. The mixture was reduced at 1000 
◦
C for 2 h in a hydrogen 
atmosphere. The cross section of the reduced sample is shown in Fig. 4 
(a), indicating that significant cracks formed inside and outside the 
reduced sample. 
The formation of cracks in the one-step reduction occurred because 
the direct reduction of the oxide mixture to the metal involved a sig -
nificant volume change of approximately 25.6 wt%. For comparison, the 
oxide mixture was processed using the two-step hydrogen reduction 
method, first at 500 
◦
C for 2 h, and followed by another reduction at 
1000 
◦
C for 2 h. The cross section of the reduced sample is shown in 
Fig. 4 (b). Based on the results, the two-step reduction method success -
fully prevents the formation of cracks, leading to crack-free parts. 
3.2. Pre-reduction

C for 2 h at

element. In addition, with increasing temperature, more Cu was found 
to be segregated in the bright-phase regions.

4. Conclusions 
In this study, an FeCoNiCu HEA was successfully produced through a 
two-step hydrogen reduction process. A mixture of Fe
2
O
3
, CoO, NiO, and 
CuO was pre-reduced and sintered in a hydrogen atmosphere, producing 
a nearly full dense crack-free FeCoNiCu HEA with an FCC structure and 
oxygen level as low as 0.10 wt%. The design of the pre-reduction step 
was beneficial for controlling the shape and avoiding the formation of 
cracks. The temperature for preparing the nearly full dense FeCoNiCu 
HEA could be as low as 900 
◦
C, and full homogenization was achieved at 
~1000 
◦
C. The ultrafine grains were observed at 800, 900, and 1000 
◦
C, 
and heating at temperatures above 1000 
◦
C causes severe grain growth. 
The best tensile properties with an ultimate tensile strength of 475 MPa, 
elongation of 7.9%, and the highest compressive strength of > 2.2 GPa 
were achieved at 1000 
◦
C. This study offers a novel method for the 
preparation of alloys through the direct hydrogen reduction of oxides.
