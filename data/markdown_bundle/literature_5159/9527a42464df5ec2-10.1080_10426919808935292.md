Abstract
A new nickel-based superalloy (Putalloy) containing rhenium and platinum
has been designed by a computer program. The program uses an iterative process
to ensure that the alloy composition has an electron hole number N, of less than
2.0. Values ofN' less than 2 are expected to reduce the formation of topologically­
close-packed and geometrically-close-packed phases in superalloys after prolonged
exposure in service. This in turn should significantly improve creep strength. Stress
rupture tests at several temperatures indicate that the creep strength of this new
alloy is indeed superior to that of some of the existing polycrystalline Ni-based
superalloys. Other mechanical properties such as tensile strength, and yield strength,
of the as-cast and solution treated Putalloy samples were found to be comparable
to conventional and existing Ni-based superalloys at room temperature and at 500
and 800°C. The oxidation resistance of putalloy was found to be superior to the
existing nickel based superalloys.

1.0 Introduction
Superalloys are high-temperature,
heat-resistant materials that are capable
of retaining high mechanical strength at
elevated temperatures. They have good
corrosion and oxidation resistance, and
superior creep and rupture strength at
elevated temperatures. In general, there
are three basic groups (I) of superalloys:
nickel-based, nickel and iron-based, and
cobalt-based. Because of their superior
strength (2-6) at elevated temperatures
nickel-based superalloys are the most
widely used superalloys in high­
temperature applications. These
applications include aircraft, marine and
industrial gas turbines, space vehicles,
rocket engines, nuclear reactors, steam
power plants, etc. These alloys contain
several alloying elements such as Cr, Ti,
AI, Ta, and W. Chromium primarily
provides the high-temperature oxidation
and corrosion resistance characteristics,
and other alloying elements such as
titanium, aluminum, cobalt, tungsten, etc.
provide the high-temperature strength and
creep resistance properties. The alloying
elements in these superalloys constitute
about 50 wt. % of total elements with
nickel making up the balance.
Existing Ni-based superalloys can be
routinely used in applications reaching
approximately - 85% of their melting
temperature. Since existing superalloys
are limited to temperatures below 1300°C,
there is a need to design alloys with higher
melting temperatures so that even higher
operating temperatures can be realized.
Higher operating temperatures will help
in reducing the cost of energy generation
672
in devices using these materials because
of increased thermal efficiency when
operating at these higher temperatures. In
this investigation, a new Ni-based
superalloy containing small amounts (4
to 5%) of rhenium and (1%) platinum has
been developed with improved
mechanical properties as compared to
existing superalloys. The basic principle
behind the alloy design is presented in a
subsequent section with the physical and
mechanical properties of this new alloy
being more fully described in the paper.The
computer program for the alloy design is
presented in Section 8.0.

mismatch strain between the y and t'

superalloys also depends on the volume
fraction (21) of the t: phase. The
morphology of the y' phase is typically
spheroidal for lower volume fraction (less

volume fraction alloys. The morphology

composition of the alloy and the lattice
mismatch between y and y'. Recent
studies (22-23) have shown that by
changing the molybdenum content of
these alloys and altering the aluminum­
titanium ratios, different morphologies

the stress rupture and the fatigue strengths

44) for how the TCP phases cause a
reduction in the creep rupture strength.

(a) Fracture site paths: The brittle a
platelets originating at grain boundary
carbides have an ideal morphology for
the initiation and propagation of crack

strength of the alloy.

grain into fewer sections (45). This
effectively acts as a reduction in grain
diameter and thereby reduces the
creep rupture strength.

oxidation. It is recognized (50) that
addition of platinum in these superalloys

and Pt are good candidate materials for

and reach a peak value after 100 hours of

Ni-based superalloy (Putalloy) has
been designed so that its electron hole
number Nv is 2.0. This is expected to
prevent the formation of TCP and
GCP phases in the alloy, and to
optimize mechanical and creep
rupture strengths.
