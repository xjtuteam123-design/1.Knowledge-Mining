Superalloys: methodological aspects of thermal analysis
Damian Migas1  · Tomasz Maciąg2
Received: 3 November 2021 / Accepted: 23 June 2022 / Published online: 19 July 2022 
© Akadémiai Kiadó, Budapest, Hungary 2022
Abstract
A notable number of scientific works aided by thermal analysis methods are available in the literature concerning superal-
loys. The most frequently applied techniques are DSC, DTA, and TG. Although types of thermal effects investigated by 
various authors are limited, it is not difficult to find debatable interpretations of curves and characteristic temperatures, even 
in the case of recent papers. This circumstance was motivation for the work. In this study, basing on literature data and own 
experiences, we would like to discuss some methodological aspects of thermal analysis. This need results from multiplicity 
of ways that different authors determine the same characteristic temperatures. The research involves interpretation of heating 
and cooling curves (DTA, DSC) in view of determination of characteristic temperatures (solidus, liquidus), order–disorder 
transitions, and other thermal effects typical for Ni- and Co-based superalloys. We discuss different approaches in analysis 
of thermal effects, present in literature data, and draw the reader's attention to some potential problems related to data inter-
pretation. The intention is to point some disputed methodological aspects and possibly systematize interpretation of effects 
present in thermal analysis of superalloys.
Keywords Cobalt-based superalloys · Nickel-based superalloys · DSC · DTA · γ′ · Solidus · Liquidus · Solvus
Introduction
Superalloys are multicomponent alloys for high-temperature 
applications. They combine excellent mechanical properties 
and high oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures. These 
materials reflect over 80 years of scientific and engineering 
advances. The development of superalloys was driven after 
World War II, by aviation industry, needing more efficient jet 
engines. Since 1940s, several Fe-, Co-, and Ni-based alloys 
were developed [ 1–3]. The high mechanical properties of 
are typically achieved by precipitation strengthening, solid 
solution strengthening, carbide/boride/nitride formation, or 
some combination of these mechanisms. Among superal-
loys, the highest application importance may be attributed to 
Ni-based ones. Nickel superalloys have become the premier 
materials for applications in hot section of a jet engine. The 
essential factor for high-temperature strength of these alloys 
is high volume fraction of ordered L12 γ′ Ni3Al phase, which 
is an ordered intermetallic phase coherently embedded in 
disordered γ-Niss matrix [4]. In XXI century, the analogous 
strengthened mechanism was observed and well described 
for alloys based on Co [5]. The discovery of γ′-Co3(Al, W) 
phase has driven reconsideration of Co-based superalloys 
which historically have served a minor role compared to the 
Ni-based ones.
The development of superalloys is supported by thermal 
analysis. Thermal analysis techniques are easily used by 
authors working with these materials. Mostly, differential 
scanning calorimetry (DSC), differential thermal analysis 
(DTA), and thermogravimentry (TG) methods are used to 
characterize superalloys. These techniques are useful for 
determination of maximum operating temperature, melting 
and crystallization behavior of alloys, specific heat capac-
ity, oxidation resistance, etc. [6 –8]. In the case of super -
alloys, typical characteristic temperatures determined by 
DTA and DSC methods are solidus ( TS), liquidus (TL), and 
solvus (Tγ′-solvus). Sometimes, the investigators try to deter -
mine Curie temperature (T c), temperatures connected with 
precipitation of γ/γ′ eutectic or carbides formation, and other.

study, which aims in discussion of methodological aspects

Materials and methods
To show some real examples of DTA or DSC plots, different 
self-made cobalt and nickel superalloys were used. All mate-
rials were prepared by vacuum induction melting (VIM). 
The alloys were vacuum melted at proper temperature for 
approximately 10 min. The molten alloys were casted into a 
hollow cylindrical graphite mold. The nominal composition 
of all obtained castings is shown in Table 1.
The first used research technique was differential ther -
mal analysis (DTA). The measurements were carried out 
by NETZSCH STA 449 F3 Jupiter thermal analyzer. The 
heating and cooling rates between 10 and 30 °C  min−1 were 
used. The mass sample mass was varied from 0.5 to 1.5 g. 
The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) studies were 
carried out using multi-HTC high-temperature calorimeter 
from Setaram. The measurements were performed under the 
Ar protection; the heating rate was 5 °C  min−1.
Results
Solidus temperature
For determination of superalloys' properties and design of 
heat treatment, researchers often investigate solidus tempera-
ture. It is obvious for most of investigators that melting of 
alloys starts with deviation of DTA curve from baseline. 
However, there are different approaches in determination of 
solidus onset. Several authors practice linear extrapolation 
method. This approach considers intersection of a linear fit 
to the downward sloping linear section of the melting peak 
and the extrapolation of the baseline as TS. The example 
studies presenting extrapolated onset of T S are following 
[10–14].
According to Boettinger et al. [15], common practice of 
using linear extrapolation method may lead to significant 
error. This opinion is based on shape of thermal effect cor -
responding to melting of solid solution alloys. The use of 
extrapolated onset temperature (TS) can be easily applied for 
pure metals or alloys containing eutectics. For such types of 
materials, onset is quite sharp and the DTA curve is linear 
after the onset. However, other alloys have no linear por -
tion near the onset of melting, and thus determination of an 
extrapolated onset is impeded. The authors propose using 
first deviation from baseline method, which is character -
ized by lower deviation from the true melting point and 
smaller heating rate dependence compared to that of onset 
determined by the extrapolation procedure. This approach is 
widely applied in DTA and DSC studies of alloys [16– 18]. 
Figure 1 shows examples of TS determination for two differ-
ent Co-based alloys. In the first case (Fig. 1a), the difference 
between extrapolated melting start onset (1286 °C) and the 
onset based on first deviation from baseline is only 6 °C. 
However, the use of extrapolated onset for investigation of 
solidus temperature in the case of second alloy (Fig.  1b) 
results in ca. 60 °C difference.
Although in many cases, extrapolation of solidus onset 
does not cause any considerable difference [14, 18, 19], 
many researchers determine extrapolated solidus onset, 
which considerably differ from the point of deviation of 
DTA curve from baseline [12, 13, 20–26]. In the last case, 
the authors decided to use point of first deviation from base-
line as solidus temperature [26].
The another practice at issue is determination of solidus 
temperature using cooling curve [27–32]. In general, cooling 
curves of alloys other than eutectic exhibit presence of two

Liquidus temperature
The another characteristic temperature for superalloys, often 
investigated by DSC and DTA, is liquidus. Investigation 
of this point may be even more troubling than in the case 
of solidus temperature. In general, liquidus can be easily 
evaluated using cooling curves (Fig.  2a). Most of research-
ers interpret T L on cooling curves the same. However, for 
some alloys, this method could cause considerable error. 
Furthermore, researchers use also heating curves to evalu-
ate liquidus temperature. Both approaches have their draw -
backs and limitations that the readers should be aware of. 
Taking into account the method based on heating plot, the 
various researchers consider maximum of endothermic peak 
(1) [36, 37] or return of the DTA signal to baseline (2) [38, 
39] (Fig. 4).
Consideration of the second point (2) as a liquidus may 
cause an error. The shape and temperature of DTA after 
signal returns to baseline after completion of melting have 
no metallurgical or thermodynamic meaning as it measures 
only heat transfer efficiency [15]. Moreover, this temperature 
is strongly affected by heating rate (Fig.  5a) and to a lesser 
extent by other factors (Fig.  5b). Although T peak can also 
be affected by cooling rate, the impact of this parameter 
on results is substantially lower compared to that of T endset 
(Fig. 5a). The difference may be reduced by determination of 
extrapolated Tendset, which sometimes can be met in papers. 
Taking into consideration issues connected with Tendset, it is 
more appropriate to consider the peak temperature. How -
ever, relevance of its application depends on numerous fac-
tors, including freezing range or partition coefficient.
The consideration of T peak as liquidus temperature is 
highly dependent on partition coefficient k. For the alloys 
containing elements with k > 1, Tpeak has no relationship to 
TL. Boettinger et al. [15] have explained this effect based 
on binary alloys. In the case of k < 1, more of the latent 
heat is released higher in the freezing range compared to 
the case for k > 1. For the second example, more of the 
latent heat is released lower in the freezing range. Fur -
thermore, investigation of T L using heating plot is more

The other way to investigate T L is analysis of cooling 
curves in view of the crystallization peak onset (Fig. 4, point 
3). Taking into account interpretation of thermal effects, the 
method is unambiguous and does not cause any problems. 
Although TL temperature determined using cooling plots is 
dependent on cooling rate, the impact may be reduced by 
extrapolation of measured values to TL at heating rate equal 
0. However, the problem with this method lies in undercool-
ing effect [8, 42, 43]. The extent of this effect on T L can be 
measured by difference between nucleation temperature (TN) 
and real liquidus temperature. For various alloys, the differ-
ence may be no more than few degrees; however, it is pos-
sible to be more than 20 °C. The undercooling is affected by 
solidification conditions and alloy composition. The T L–TN 
difference is more pronounced if the heterogeneous crys-
tallization is impeded, e.g., by lack of nonmetallic inclu-
sions easing nucleation. Furthermore, undercooling effect 
also depends on melting history and may be intensified by 
superheating [42, 43].
Liquidus temperature may be determined by heating or 
cooling curves; however, both methods have some restric -
tions. The approach should be chosen consciously and 
adjusted to the type of analyzed alloy. Moreover, similarly 
to TS, it is needed to include curves to a manuscript and 
indicate TL on an adequate plot. Taking into account heat-
ing curves, it is not advised to use T endset. However, if the 
exact TL determination is crucial, it is always recommended 
to perform measurements using different heating/cooling 
rates and calculate liquidus temperature corresponding to 
rate 0 K  min−1. The examples of T L determination using 
heating and cooling curves for two different multicomponent 
superalloys are shown in Fig. 6. In both cases, the values of 
liquidus temperature determined by heating plots are close 
to those of cooling plots.

ordering is connected with maximum operating temperature 
for an superalloy due to substantial deterioration of strength 
above this temperature. There are several methods used to 
estimate the transition temperature. The most popular are 
DTA and DSC. Most of authors which reveal γ ′ solvus for 
superalloys with certain chemical composition use the pre-
viously mentioned techniques. Although thermal analysis-
assisted determination of T γ′-solvus is common, there is no 
clear recommendation how to execute this. What is more, 
the literature review showed that various scientists use dif-
ferent interpretation of thermal effects corresponding to γ ′ 
transitions. Since the issue is not clear, it is worth to discuss 
it in detail.
The first research on the ordering effect in alloys dates 
back to the first half of the twentieth century. The substantial 
contribution in this regard was made by Bragg and Williams 
[44]. They have studied effect of thermal agitation on atomic 
arrangement in alloys. In general, we should not consider 
γ′ solvus as a change from one phase to another, but with 
continuous transition of atomic arrangement within a single 
phase. The degree of order (S ) in long-range-ordered state 
(LRO) decreases slowly with increasing temperature. At 
critical temperature Tc, the degree of order drops abruptly to 
zero. No order or superlattice exists above critical tempera-
ture. If ordering diminishes continuously well below Tc, and 
disappears at T c, the transition is a second-order transition 
(IIO-OD). A1/L1 2 transition is considered as a first-order 
transition (IO-OD) due to abrupt change of order degree at 
Tc. The transition is irreversible; however, some differences 
between Tc on heating and cooling may appear [44].
The example DSC traces of alloy exhibiting A1/L1 2 
transition may be seen in Fig.  7. The effects connecting 
with disordering and ordering of L12-Co3(Al,W) phase are 
marked (Fig.  7a). At lower temperatures (below 900 °C), 
double-phase region γ(Coss) + γ′ may be observed. At higher 
temperatures, until melting, only γ phase is present due to 
order–disorder transition of the L12 phase. These thermal 
effects are widely used for determination of γ′ solvus. In 
view of a literature review, different characteristic points are 
considered by investigators. Numerous researchers consider 
Tpeak (2) as critical temperature (Fig.  7b) [5, 45–49]. This 
approach seems to be the most popular, especially taking 
into account papers on Co-based superalloys. Neverthe-
less, a lot of researchers use T endset (3) [7, 32, 50, 51]. The 
onset temperature of the thermal effect (1) is rarely used 
as Tγ′-solvus, whereas is worth discussion [52]. Taking into 
consideration cooling plots, majority of the investigators 
determine γ′ ordering temperature as T onset of the exother -
mic peak (4).
Numerous researchers using T endset (Fig.  7b) refer to 
Sponseller [7]. The author considers this point as the γ′ dis-
solution temperature, whereas it provides no physical basis 
for this interpretation. Moreover, the author revealed that as 
the alloy is more homogenous, the range between T peak and 
solvus is lower. According to Kainuma et al. [53], order–dis-
order transition (L10/A1) may be determined as extrapolated 
onset of an endothermic peak corresponding to the transi-
tion. The authors imply that also L12/A1 transition may be 
easily investigated in this way. Then, the authors presented 
the example DSC plots for alloy which undergoes 2O-OD 
transition [53, 54]. Moreover, the transition of β phase in 
Cu–Zn system is considered to exhibit typical thermal 
effects for 2O-OD [55]. The DSC curves corresponding to 
Ni- and Co-based superalloys (Fig.  7) are very similar to 
those typical for second-order transition [54, 55]. The simi-
larity may cause that numerous authors indicate character -
istic points for L12/A1 transition (IO-OD) in analogous way 
to that of IIO-OD. Although order–disorder transition of γ ′ 
is of first-order type, Tonset of endothermic peak on heating is 
rather not used as γ′ solvus. In many cases, this temperature 
is hard to detect or is not corresponding to results obtained 
by other methods. The earlier referred researchers [53] use 
Tendset on heating as γ′ solvus in their works [ 50, 56–59],

Conclusions
In view that superalloys are materials designated for high-
temperature applications, studies on their thermal behavior 
are crucial. The DTA and DSC curves may provide variety 
of information in this regard. Although these methods are 
popular and have been used for decades, some methodologi-
cal aspects are still not fully clear. In our study, we collected 
numerous examples of different approaches for interpreta -
tion of plots, presented by investigators involved in analysis 
of superalloys. The numerous examples from other studies 
were indicated to draw attention to complexity of proper 
thermal analysis. There are different issues and nuances 
which cause that interpretation for a given alloy may be 
correct, but misleading for another one. The aim was to 
undertake a discussion on different methodological aspects 
of thermal analysis of superalloys.
We discussed difficulties in determination of solidus 
temperature by cooling curves, as well as validity of using 
extrapolated TS onset on heating plots. Furthermore, we ana-
lyzed problems and limitations connected with determina-
tion of liquidus temperature on both heating and cooling. 
Furthermore, detailed consideration on L12/A1 order–dis -
order transitions in superalloys was took into account. The 
authors hope that the remarks and discussion included in this 
work will be helpful for the readers in their studies.
