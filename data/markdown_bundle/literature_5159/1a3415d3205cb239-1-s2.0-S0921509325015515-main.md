Various processing routes have been explored to fabricate ultrafine- 
grained and nanocrystalline tungsten alloys. These include mechanical 
alloying [ 11 , 12 ] and chemical synthesis approaches [ 13 ], with consol -
idation achieved via pressureless sintering [ 11 ] and spark plasma sin -
tering (SPS) [ 12 ]. Recently, binder jetting 3D printing (BJ3DP) — an 
indirect additive manufacturing technology — has been successfully 
applied to fabricate tungsten heavy alloys (WHAs), such as 
95W-3.5Ni-1.5Fe. This method enables near-full densification (relative 
density up to 99.9 %) and low porosity (0.029 %) by avoiding 
fusion-induced thermal stress and elemental segregation, which are 
inherent to laser-based additive manufacturing [ 14 ]. Specifically, 
BJ3DP-fabricated 95W-3.5Ni-1.5Fe exhibits a dual-phase microstruc -
ture (15 – 30 μ m tungsten particles uniformly distributed in γ -NiFe binder 
phase) and enhanced ductility (elongation: 17.1 ± 0.5 %), out -
performing conventional liquid phase sintering (LPS) counterparts 
(elongation: 7 – 9 %) [ 14 ]. However, these methods often result in mi -
crostructures with micron or submicron-sized grains due to accelerated 
grain growth during high-temperature sintering, limiting their me -
chanical properties to hardness values in the range of 2.3 – 8.9 GPa, 
which are comparable to conventional tungsten alloys [ 15 ]. A signifi -
cant advance was made through high-pressure and high-temperature 
(HPHT) sintering, which enables full densification while suppressing 
grain growth, yielding bulk nanocrystalline W-Ti alloys with grain sizes 
below 50 nm and exceptional hardness up to 16.9 GPa [ 6 ].
Incorporation of nonmetallic constituents including boron, carbon, 
nitrogen, oxygen, and silicon has proven effective for strengthening 
metallic systems [ 16 – 18 ]. Titanium alloys exhibited 47 % and 73 % 
yield strength enhancements with 2 atomic percent oxygen and nitrogen 
additions respectively [ 15 ]. Fe-Ni alloys doubled their tensile strength 
through 1.1 at% carbon incorporation [ 16 ]. Boron doping at 0.05 at% 
enhanced Fe-Al alloy strength twofold at 527 
◦

carbon addition elevated CoCrFe alloy yield strength by 100 % at 600 
◦

[ 21 , 22 ]. This strategic elemental modification strategy demonstrates 
significant potential for improving both elevated-temperature [ 16 – 18 ] 
and ambient-temperature mechanical performance in metallic materials 
[ 20 – 23 ].
The pursuit of tungsten-based alloys for advanced engineering ap -
plications is persistently hampered by a critical trade-off: achieving high 
strength and hardness often comes at the expense of ductility and 
thermal stability, particularly at elevated temperatures. Conventional 
strategies, such as solid-solution strengthening or micro-scale composite 
formation, frequently reach a performance ceiling. Nanocrystalline 
structures offer a pathway to unprecedented strength, but their inherent 
thermodynamic instability leads to rapid grain coarsening and property 
degradation under thermal loads. Furthermore, the role of interstitial 
elements like carbon in stabilizing such nanostructures and modulating 
their mechanical behavior in complex tungsten-titanium systems re -
mains insufficiently explored, representing a significant knowledge gap. 
To address these challenges, this investigation proposes a novel dual- 
strategy integrating extreme grain refinement and precise carbon 
doping within (W

alloy systems. We hypothesize that the 
controlled incorporation of carbon will not only provide additional 
strengthening but also segregate to grain boundaries, thereby enhancing 
interfacial cohesion and imparting exceptional thermal stability to the 
nanocrystalline matrix. This work aims to establish a fundamental un -
derstanding of these synergistic effects, the findings of which are critical 
for the development of next-generation ultra-high-temperature mate -
rials capable of operating in extreme environments.

3. Results
3.1. Optimized mechanical alloying process
Conventional mechanical alloying processes inherently face impu -
rity contamination challenges due to abrasive wear between high- 
hardness nanopowders and milling media during high-velocity 
impacts. In this experimental configuration, tungsten carbide (WC) 
impurities were generated from ball/jar interactions. To mitigate WC 
contamination, an optimized three-stage mechanical processing proto -
col was implemented: Primary Milling Stage: Initial 24 h processing of 
feedstock powder in WC containers without post-milling cleaning. Sur -
face Passivation Stage: Secondary 24 h treatment of identical- 
composition powder using the contaminated milling system, enabling 
powder deposition on container surfaces. Final Processing Stage: Ter -
tiary 24 h refinement cycle producing contamination-controlled powder 
for experimental use. This sequential approach facilitates the formation 
of protective powder layers on milling surfaces prior to final powder 
collection, effectively minimizing direct WC-media contact during crit -
ical processing phases.
X-ray diffraction analysis of various MAed W powder batches are 
presented in Fig. 1 . The third batch demonstrates exclusive tungsten 
phase identification, with all diffraction peaks corresponding to pure W. 
Implementation of secondary processing established a dense tungsten 
coating on milling media (WC containers and balls), effectively pre -
venting direct particle-media collisions during subsequent processing 
stages. This protective mechanism significantly reduced WC contami -
nation, as evidenced by XRD spectra where WC phase detection was 
confined to first-batch powders ( Fig. 1 ). Quantitative analysis via CS- 
8800 high-frequency induction combustion furnace revealed carbon 
content reductions from 0.0674 ± 0.02 wt% (first batch) to 0.0238 ±
0.01 wt% (third batch). Correspondingly, WC content decreased from 
1.095 ± 0.32 wt% to 0.387 ± 0.16 wt%, confirming effective impurity 
control. These results validate the optimized three-stage milling pro -
tocol ’ s efficacy in producing WC-contamination-free powders through 
sequential surface passivation and contamination barrier formation.
