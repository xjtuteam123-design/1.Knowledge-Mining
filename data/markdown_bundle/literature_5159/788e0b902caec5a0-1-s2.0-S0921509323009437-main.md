1. Introduction 
High-temperature Ti alloys are the key structural materials of so -
phisticated aero-engines [ 1 , 2 ]. Laser additive manufacturing offers a 
high accuracy and flexibility near-net-shape technique for fabricating 
large and complex high-temperature Ti components, revolutionizing the 
conventional process [ 3 , 4 ]. However, conventional high-temperature Ti 
alloys, such as Ti1100 [ 2 ], IMI834 [ 5 ], and Ti60 [ 6 ] are of near- α type 
[ 7 ] and are mainly destined for casting and forging processing routes, 
being unsuitable for additive manufacturing due to their wide solidifi -
cation temperature range, or the difference between the liquidus and 
solidus temperatures of the alloy, above 100 
◦
C in general [ 8 ]. Such wide 
solidification temperature ranges would impede the melt flow and cause 
microstructure inhomogeneities [ 9 ]. The high solidification rate of ad -
ditive manufacturing, about 2 – 3 times faster than those of conventional 
casting, also aggravates the problem. On the other hand, 
additive-manufacturing-orientated Ti alloys are of α + β dual-phase-type 
with insufficient high-temperature performance, as represented by 
Ti – 6Al – 4V [ 10 , 11 ], TC11 [ 12 , 13 ], TC17 [ 14 ], and TC21 [ 15 ]. In 
particular, the most widely used Ti – 6Al – 4V alloy shows excellent ad -
ditive manufacturability, with a narrow solidification range of ~13
◦
C 
and a low surface roughness of 60 – 80 μ m at as-deposited state, but its 
high-temperature strengths usually fall below 450 MPa at 600 
◦
C [ 8 , 16 ]. 
Therefore, it is necessary to develop high-temperature Ti alloys with 
balanced mechanical properties and appropriate additive 
manufacturability. 
To design such an alloy, the composition origin of Ti – 6Al – 4V should 
be first of all explained. The problem is especially complicated by solid 
solutions presenting chemical short-range orders [ 8 , 17 , 18 ]. In the 
recent decade, our team have been engaged in developing a so-called 
cluster-plus-glue-atom model to describe the chemical short-range 
ordering in terms of a molecule-like structural unit, which covers a 
nearest-neighbor cluster plus a few next-neighbor glue atoms, expressed 
in the cluster formula form as [cluster](glue atoms) [ 19 – 21 ]. This model 
has been proved useful in the composition optimization of a large va -
riety of industrial alloys [ 22 – 25 ]. In a previous work [ 26 ], the compo -

2. Material and methods 
2.1. Cluster-plus-glue-atom model 
Common industrial alloys are mostly based on solid solutions. 
Therefore, the accurate modeling of chemical short-range ordering in 
solid solution is the prerequisite for alloy composition design. Dong et al. 
[ 19 – 21 ] proposed a cluster-plus-glue-atom model to describe the 
short-range-order structures in quasicrystals, metallic glasses, as well as 
in solid solutions. This model identifies a structural unit as the compo -
sition carrier of the material, which covers a nearest-neighbor cluster 
plus a few next-neighbor glue atoms. This chemical unit is then formu -
lated as [cluster](glue atoms), where the square-bracketed part [cluster] 
refers specifically to the nearest-neighbor coordination polyhedron and 
is matched with the parenthesis part (glue atoms) positioned at the 
next-neighbor sites. 
The existence of such a cluster-plus-glue-atom structural unit was 
theoretically proven using Friedel oscillations theorem [ 20 , 33 , 34 ]. 
Friedel oscillation describes the charge screening behavior of an impu -
rity solute in a solid solution. The effective pair potential of Friedel os -
cillations is expressed as φ

nearest-neighbor cluster plus a few next-neighbor glue atoms. This 
structure unit is also regarded as the composition gene of the material

2.2. Composition design for Ti-6.8Al-6.8Zr-2.3V-2.1Mo-0.7Nb 
The composition formula of Ti-6.8Al-6.8Zr-2.3V-2.1Mo-0.7Nb has 
been detailed in Ref. [ 31 ] and the design is briefly reviewed here. 
To achieve high-temperature performance and appropriate additive 
manufacturability, the targeted alloy should be near- α to ensure high- 
temperature strengths, like all the high-temperature Ti alloys, and at 
the same time, it should contain a proper amount of β phase stabilizers, 
just like Ti – 6Al – 4V, to ensure a good additive manufacturability. The 
design strategy is therefore based on the composition formula of 
Ti – 6Al – 4V [ 26 ], with modifications to enhance the β stability.

based alloy and its composition should be rooted in the chemical

nearest-neighbor coordination polyhedral clusters of hexagonal close-
