abstract
The internal characteristics of the m phase in Ni-base single crystal superalloys aged at 1 100 /C14 C for 1000 h
were investigated via spherical aberration-corrected transmission electron microscopy. There were a
large number of planar defects on the (001) plane at the growth tip of the m phase. HAADF-STEM results
show that these planar defects were mainly of two types. 1) A twin structure with a mirror plane of the
middle atomic planes of the Zr
4Al3 sheet, which was generated by shearing of atoms in the MgCu 2
sheet along the [1-10] direction. 2) A mirror-symmetrical stacking fault of two Zr 4Al3 sheets, which was
formed by the pulling out of a single MgCu 2 sheet. The planar defects were mainly caused by coordi-
nation of the distortions of the m phase and the matrix. Segregation of Mo, Cr, and Re elements occurred
near stacking faults, rather than twin structures.

1. Introduction
Ni-base single-crystal superalloys are widely used in the prep-
aration of turbine blades for aircraft engines because of their
excellent high-temperature mechanical properties, creep resis-
tance, and good organizational stability [ 1e3]. In order to meet the
increasingly stringent design requirements of high-performance
aircraft engines, a large number of refractory elements have been
added to Ni-base single crystal superalloys. This greatly improves
the creep resistance of the alloys; however, excess alloying ele-
ments will generate brittle topologically close-packed (TCP) phases
[4e7]. The precipitation of TCP phases consumes a large amount of
the alloying elements, which will affect the solid solution
strengthening effect of the matrix [ 8,9]. Besides, TCP phases run
through both the g and g0 phases, and become the origin of cracks
and expansion channels, seriously affecting the high-temperature
mechanical properties and service life of superalloys [ 10]. The ex-
istence of TCP phases has restricted the development of high-
temperature alloys and some aircraft engines. However, several
issues concerning 1) the mechanism of action of alloy elements on
TCP phases and 2) the internal characteristics of TCP phases remain
under dispute.
Among various types of TCP phases, the m phase is more com-
mon and has received much attention for its various morphologies
and unclear effects on creep resistance [ 11e14]. In binary A-B sys-
tems, the m phase is based on the ideal stoichiometry A 7B6, where A
is an IVB transition element like Fe, Co, or Ni, and B is a VB or VIB
transition element like W or Mo [ 15]. Its structure is isotypic with
Fe7W6, which has a rhombohedral cell containing 13 atoms [ 16].
Previous studies have focused mainly on the morphology, forma-
tion and in ﬂuence of alloying elements on the m phase [17,18]. Few
have focused on the internal characteristics of the m phase. With the
development of electron microscopy, signi ﬁcant progress has been
made in research at the atomic scale, providing powerful tech-
niques for the solution of the above problems. This study mainly
uses modern electron microscopy techniques to study planar de-
fects in the m phase at the atomic scale. This provides a theoretical

2. Experimental
The experimental alloy was directionally solidi ﬁed as single
crystal bars with dimensions of 14 mm /C2 150 mm along the [001]
growth orientation. The bars were formed at the Institute of Metal
Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The primary chemical
composition of the experimental alloy was ﬁne-tuned to TMS-138
[19] and is listed in Table 1 . The heat treatment process was
130 0 /C14 C/1 h þ 1340 /C14 C/5 h with air cooling and 1 100 /C14 C/
4h þ 870 /C14 C/20 h with air cooling. In order to facilitate the gen-
eration of the TCP phase, the alloy was aged at 1 100 /C14 C for 1000 h.
After high-temperature exposure, pieces of 300 mm thickness
were cut from the samples by electrical discharge machining. Then
they were ground to a thickness of 80
mm using silicon carbide
paper. Disk-shaped specimens 3 mm in diameter were mechani-
cally punched out and further ground to about 25 mm in thickness
for transmission electron microscope (TEM) examination. Finally,
thin foils were thinned by the twin jet polishing technique using
10% perchloric acid solution in ethanol at 20 V and /C0 20 /C14 C. For the
analysis of TCP precipitates, ion beam milling techniques were
applied after the twin jet polishing technique to ensure uniform
thinning of the embedded TCP phases. Using a Gatan MODEL 691
PIPS Precision Ion Polishing System, the sample was milled on both
sides with Ar ions for 30 min under high vacuum (5 /C2 10/C0 5 Torr),
with 3 kV accelerating voltage and a 3 /C14 ion gun angle to expand the
thin area and remove ion beam damage.
Microstructural evolution observations were carried out with a
Tecnai T20 TEM operated at 200 kV, with a double tilt sample
holder which allows sample tilting of up to 60 /C14 for structural
analysis. The chemical composition was analyzed using a Super-X
energy dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS) detector equipped on
a FEI-Thiems spherical aberration-corrected TEM operating at
300 kV.

3.1. Structural identi ﬁcation of the TCP phases

phase had a rhombohedral structure, and its formula for calculating

Fig. 2 shows a chemical analysis of elemental segregations in the

3.3. Planar defects in the m phase
In order to determine the structure of the lamellar contrasts in
Fig. 1(b), we examined diffraction spots from the lamellar contrast
region, as shown in Fig. 4. We found that the layers were parallel to
the (001) plane of the m phase and there were twin spots and slight
streaks in the diffraction pattern of the m phase on the [1 10] zone
axis. So, we can initially con ﬁrm the existence of (001) planar de-
fects in the m phase, such as twins and stacking faults, which in-
duces lamellar contrasts compared to the perfect m phase. Since the
regions where the planar defects exist and the regions with perfect
m phases satisfying Bragg conditions are different, the intensities of
their corresponding diffraction contrasts are different, resulting in
the lamellar contrasts shown in Fig. 1(a) and (b).
To further analyze the type and structure of planar defects in the
m phase, we obtained high-angle circular dark ﬁeld (HAADF) images
at different positions of the m phase in the STEM mode, as shown in
Figs. 5 and 6 . The HAADF-STEM images are also called Z-contrast
images, where the contrast of an image is proportional to the
square of the atomic number Z. The larger the atomic number of an
element, the brighter the atoms.

upper region for ease of observation. Compared with the perfectly
alternating stacks of MgCu 2 and Zr 4Al3 sheets seen in Fig. 5 , the
stacking order in the planar defect areas has changed in Fig. 6. There
are both twins and stacking faults in the m phase, named Type 1 and
Type 2 faults, respectively. The mirror planes of the Type 1 twins
are the middle atomic planes of the Zr 4Al3 sheets, and were
discovered by Carvalho et al. [ 24] in 2000. The Type 2 stacking
faults are mirror-symmetrical stacking faults of two Zr 4Al3 sheets.
4. Analysis
4.1. The forming process of the planar defects in m phase
The formation processes of Type 1 twins and Type 2 stacking
faults are different. Type 1 twin formation has been described in
detail by Carvalho et al. [ 24], but we do not consider this descrip-
tion to be accurate. If their description is accurate and the twins are
formed by swapping the positions of the W and Co atoms, the
arrangement of atoms on the (1 10) plane would be as shown in
Fig. 7. The structure formed by swapping the positions of the W and
Co atoms is similar to a twin structure, but the atoms on each side
of the twin surface are not perfectly symmetrical (see the atoms in
the green dashed box in Fig. 7 ), which is different from the
experimentally observed results ( Fig. 6 ). So, we believe that this
generation mechanism is ﬂawed. In our opinion, Type 1 twin
crystals are generated by the shearing of atoms in the MgCu
2 sheets
along the [ 1e10] direction. The speci ﬁc process is shown in Fig. 8 .
The perfect m phase structure in Fig. 8 (a) consists of a stack of
alternating MgCu 2 and Zr 4Al3 sheets. There are three layers of

The process of Type 2 stacking fault formation is simpler than
that of Type 1 twins. One Type 2 stacking fault is formed by taking
out a layer of MgCu 2 sheet from the perfect m phase. During the
formation of Type 2 faults, a layer of the MgCu 2 structure is
extracted (as shown by the green dashed line in Fig. 9(a)). Assuming
that the upper atoms do not move, the bottom atoms will move
along the [/C0 1 10] direction byj1/6[/C0 1 10]amj (about 1.13 Å) and along
the [001] direction by j1/6cmj (about 4.2 Å). Then, a Type 2 stacking
fault will be formed where two Zr 4Al3 sheets become adjacent, as
shown in Fig. 9(c), which can also be considered as a single layer of
twins.
4.2. The cause of planar defects in the m phase
In the early stage of precipitation, the precipitated m phase has
heavily layered contrasts throughout entire m phase [ 25]. After
1000 h of high-temperature exposure, the planar defects are mainly
distributed in the frontal area of m phase growth rather than
throughout the entire m phase (Fig. 1(a)). We think that the planar
defects are mainly caused by coordination of the distortions be-
tween the m phase and the matrix.
There is a certain orientation relationship (001) m//(1-10)g be-
tween the m phase and the matrix during the growth process [ 26],
but the difference in structure between the two phases cannot be
completely coherent. There is a lattice distortion, as shown in
Fig. 10. The calculated lattice constant of the matrix in the alloy is
about 3.559 Å. The lattice constant of the m phase is a ¼ b ¼ 4.819 Å
and c ¼ 25.326 Å. Both of them cannot completely coincide in the
process of forming the interface. When forming Type 1 twins,
MgCu2 shears to form mirror-symmetrical structures. The height of
the m phase does not change, but it adjusts the shape of the inter-
face between the m-phase and the substrate, as shown in Fig. 1 1.I n
the formation of Type 2 faults, a layer of MgCu 2 is taken out. The
height of the Zr4Al3 layer is higher than that of the MgCu2 layer. The
presence of this defect can adjust the height of the m phase to make
it more compatible with the matrix, as shown in Fig. 12 . The
combination of Type 1 and Type 2 planar defects can make the m
phase more compatible with the matrix, thereby reducing the
interface distortion energy and making the structure more stable.

4.3. Elemental segregation along the defects
In Fig. 3 , component distributions along the m particle growth
direction are presented in great detail. However, it is still unknown
whether the tip of the m phase, i.e., the area where planar defects
are clustered, has elemental segregation. The Super-X energy-
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy detector makes it possible to observe
the atomic level component distribution around the planar defects.
Fig. 13 shows the HAADF image and several elemental maps
obtained from the same area. Type 1 twins and Type 2 stacking
faults are obvious in Fig. 13(a). Fig. 13(b)-(f) present the component
distributions of Al, Ni, Mo, Cr, and Re elements, respectively. It is
interesting that the component distributions around the Type 1
twins are almost identical to those of the perfect m phase, while
segregation of Mo, Cr, and Re elements occurs near the Type 2
stacking faults. Besides, Ni and Al are de ﬁcient at the Type 2
stacking faults, which is consistent with reports in the literature
[27].

5. Conclusions
There are lamellar contrasts at the tip and the middle of the lath-
like TCP phase in Ni-base single crystal superalloys subjected to
1000 h high-temperature exposure at 1 100 /C14 C. In this study, a
spherical aberration-corrected electron microscope was used to
study the internal characteristics of the m phase. The following
conclusions can be drawn:
(1) The lath-like TCP phase was identi ﬁed to be a m phase with
the lattice parameters of a ¼ b ¼ 4.819 Å and c ¼ 25.326 Å.
(2) Compared with the surrounding g0 phase in the background,
the m particles are rich in Cr, Co, Re, Mo, and W elements, but
poor in Ni, Ta, and Al elements. In the region with lamellar
contrasts, the content of elemental Mo increases, while the
Ni content decreases.
(3) The lamellar contrasts at the tip of the m phase are planar
defects on the (001) plane.
(4) The HAADF-STEM results show that these planar defects are
mainly of two kinds. One is a twin structure with a mirror
plane of the middle atomic planes of the Zr 4Al3 sheets, which
is generated by shearing of atoms in the MgCu 2 sheet along
the [ 1e10] direction. The other is a mirror-symmetrical
