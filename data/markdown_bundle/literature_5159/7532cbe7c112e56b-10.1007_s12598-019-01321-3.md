Abstract Mechanical behavior of Inconel 625 alloy man-
ufactured by selective laser melting (SLM) was experi-
mentally studied. Tensile tests were performed at various
temperatures (20, 540, 760, 815, 870, 950 and 1000 /C176C).
The microstructures and fracture mechanism were inves-
tigated using optical microscopy (OM), scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive spectroscopy
(EDS). The SLM Inconel 625 exhibits excellent plastic
deformation at low temperature. However, signiﬁcant
embrittlement for the SLM Inconel 625 was found at ele-
vated temperatures compared to low temperature and tra-
ditional forging process. Through microstructure studies,
the non-homogenous microstructure is a key factor causing
the intergranular cracking mode at elevated temperature.
Moreover, carbides were found to form at the grain
boundary at elevated temperatures. These carbides could
weaken the strength of the grain boundary at elevated
temperatures, which decreased the tensile strength and
ductility of the alloy processed by SLM.
Keywords Nickel-based superalloy; Selective laser
melting; Embrittlement; High temperature; Microstructure
1 Introduction
Additive manufacturing (AM) of metals provides great
ﬂexibility in producing components with low complexity
of design process, short time for design iteration, complex
structure design, low wastage of materials and reduced
part’s number [1, 2]. Selective laser melting (SLM), one of
the most promising AM methods for high-temperature
superalloy, has attracted a considerable amount of work in
many ﬁelds including aerospace, automobile and biomed-
ical industries due to their special technical advantages
[3–5]. Extensive studies have been carried out to focus on
the SLM process conditions [ 2, 6–8] and post-process
conditions [ 9–13] such as heat treatment and hot isostatic
pressing (HIP). However, as a result of high reliability
requirement for the application of SLM superalloys, their
microstructure and failure mechanism should be carefully
investigated, especially at high temperatures [ 14].
During SLM process, the line-by-line scanning of a
high-powered laser is essentially a nonequilibrium process
[15]. Highly localized heats with considerable power act on
the powder in a very short time. The powder in a limited
space experiences a complex thermal cycle with rapid
heating up to several thousand K /C1s
-1. A steep temperature
gradient occurs from the local melted material to the part
buckets [ 16]. After the laser moves away, the material
solidiﬁes rapidly. Many parameters including those of
powder (quality and performance) and process (i.e., laser
power, scan speed and layer thickness) will inﬂuence the
quality of the SLM components. Compared to traditional
forged and cast superalloy, a high level of residual stress,
inhomogeneous microstructures and phase distributions
exist in as-built SLM superalloy [ 3]. Thus, heat treatment
(HT) and HIP are considered as the most effective methods

to improve the mechanical behavior of the as-built SLM
superalloys. Although the mechanical performance of the
material can be improved through the optimization of the
SLM process and post-process methods, harmful
microstructures and phases cannot be totally avoided.
Furthermore, material performance requirements for com-
ponents subjected to different loads and service environ-
ments cannot be the same. Though the mechanical
behavior of SLM superalloy at room temperature has
received many attentions, the high-temperature mechanical
behavior is not clearly understood.
The microstructure and mechanical behavior of SLM
superalloy have been intensively studied with respect to
various parameters. Kanagarajah et al. [ 17] studied the
effect of microstructure and temperature on the mechanical
properties of SLM Inconel 939 superalloy. They found that
the samples tested at 750 /C176C showed a clearly decreased
ductility compared to those tested at room temperature.
They attributed the reason to the embrittlement of the
specimens which was caused by the precipitates formed at
elevated temperatures. The mechanical behavior of SLM
Inconel 625 has been extensively studied by many
researchers [ 18–21]. The room temperature mechanical
properties such as fracture strength, yield strength and
elongation were compared to those obtained from tradi-
tional processes. Most of the researches conﬁrm sufﬁcient
static strength and ductility for AM superalloy except for
Yadroitsev et al.’s ﬁnding [ 21] where embrittlement (only
9% elongation) was found. Combination of brittle and
ductile fracture from scanning electron microscopy (SEM)
observation was primarily explained.
Kreitcberg et al. [ 22] studied the microstructure and
mechanical properties of the Inconel 625 alloy processed
by laser powder-bed fusion after the following post-treat-
ments including stress relief (SR) annealing, solution
treatment and HIP. Vertical specimens are found to be
signiﬁcantly more ductile than their horizontal counter-
parts. After SR process, the overall elongation is signiﬁ-
cantly improved compared to that of the as-built
specimens. The ductility of the material at 760 /C176C
decreased compared to room temperature ductility. The
results were not coincided with traditional forged or cast
Inconel 625. Trosch et al. [ 23] studied mechanical prop-
erties of SLM Inconel 718 at room temperature and ele-
vated temperatures (450 and 650 /C176C). The parts processed
by SLM have better mechanical properties than forged and
cast material at room temperature. Meanwhile, they have
similar properties with forged material at elevated tem-
peratures. The optimization of microstructure with a high
proportion of intragranular d-phase in SLM Inconel 718
was an effective way to improve high-temperature
mechanical property. Mostafaei et al. [ 24] investigated the
effect of solution and aging on the microstructure and
mechanical properties of powder-bed binder jet-printed
nickel-based superalloy 625. They found that the aging
treatment resulted in the formation of intermetallic phases
such as Ni
3Nb and Ni 2 (Cr, Mo) as well as carbides such as
NbC and Cr 23C6 in the microstructure. While the micro-
hardness and tensile strength of the aged specimen
increased due to these phases, the ductility value decreased.
Li et al. [ 25] studied microstructural evolution character-
istics of Inconel 625 alloy from SLM to HT. The result
revealed that after annealing at high temperatures, random
grain growth accompanied by dislocation annihilation and
twinning occurs. O¨ zgu¨ r et al. [ 26] discovered that aging HT
led to the formation of some carbides and intermetallic
phases in the microstructure. Therefore, the microstructure
has an important effect on the mechanical properties of
additional manufactured superalloy, especially the carbides
and intermetallic phases formed during the manufacture
process and HT process.
On the whole, most of the published works on SLM
superalloy (i.e., Inconel 625) were performed at room
temperature [ 18–21]. Only few works were carried out at
elevated temperature [ 17, 22] with maximum temperature
of 760 /C176C. Because Inconel 625 is typically a promising
candidate material in chambers and low-pressure turbine
blades for gas turbines, aero engines and rocket engines
[24, 27, 28], their service temperature typically designed
for 850 /C176C with some hot spots that can be up to 1000 /C176C
in combustion chambers. Since the SLM Inconel 625 is
manufactured by line-by-line process, the microstructure
and high-temperature mechanical property should be
carefully addressed for its application. Thus, service tem-
peratures up to 1000 /C176C were considered in the present
work. The microstructure, mechanical properties and fail-
ure mechanism were discussed.

2.2 Specimen preparation
The building direction is parallel to the axial of the spec-
imen as shown in Fig. 3a. The specimen is a plate and the
thickness of the gage length is the same as that of the plate.
The semi-ﬁnished specimens were fabricated by a laser
powder-bed fusion system (BLT-S300) with a margin of
0.5 mm in each direction based on the size shown in
Fig. 3b. The beam spot size is 0.1 mm. The layer thickness
is 0.04 mm. After SLM process, the as-built specimens
were subjected to solution HT (1100 /C176C/1 h/argon) to
relieve the residual stress and homogenize the material’s
microstructure. No hot isostatic pressing was performed on
the specimens. Then, the specimens were machined to ﬁnal
size of test specimens (Fig. 3b) with a surface roughness of
0.4 lm and the thickness of the specimen is 3.0 mm.

In order to relieve the residual stress and homogenize the

Subsequently, so as to clarify the failure mechanism of
SLM Inconel 625, the fracture surfaces of the tested

were not homogenous, which may result in inconsistent

3 Results and discussion
3.1 Mechanical properties
The engineering stress and strain curves of the SLM
Inconel 625 in vertical direction tested at 20, 540, 760, 815,
870, 950 and 1000 /C176C are shown in Fig. 6. The mechanical
properties of the SLM Inconel 625 show strong tempera-
ture dependency.
As shown in Fig. 6, the material exhibits signiﬁcant
hardening behavior at room temperature, but less hardening
at higher temperatures. The plastic deformation behavior is
the same as that for traditional material. The temperature
dependence of ultimate strength and yield strength of the
tested specimen are shown in Fig. 7. Both the ultimate and
yield strengths decrease as temperature increases. The
ultimate strengths of SLM Inconel 625 compared to tra-
ditional forged one reveals a slight decrease within 10%, as
well as the yield strengths, decreased within 15%. Thus,
SLM Inconel 625 has excellent strength compared to that
produced by forged process.
An interesting phenomenon is found. The ductility of
the SLM Inconel 625 decreases with temperature

Figure 9 shows the fracture surface examination of the
specimens tested at room temperature and 870 /C176C. A large
number of dimples are found on the fracture surface at
room temperature. The distribution of the dimples is non-
homogenous, indicating non-homogeneity in the
microstructure of the alloy processed by SLM. Though it is
detrimental to the deformation coordination, the material
still exhibits excellent plastic deformation at low temper-
ature, and it is consistent with the elongation measurement
from the tensile test. However, dimple does not exist on the
fracture surface for specimens deformed at high tempera-
tures. The cracks grow along the grain boundary which
could be treated as intergranular cracking. Cleavage fea-
tures of the fracture surface indicate that typical brittle
fracture occurs at high temperature. Additionally, Fig. 10
shows microstructure of the surface near the fracture sec-
tion for the specimens tested at 20, 540, 760, 815, 870 and
1000 /C176C. Obviously, the grains were deformed in loading
direction at 20 and 540 /C176C. However, the grains in
Fig. 10c–f is almost the same with those in Fig. 5 before
tensile test, indicating that small plasticity occurs among
the grains. Plenty of intergranular cracks are observed for
specimens tested at 760, 815, 870 and 1000 /C176C.
Owing to the non-homogenous grain distributions,
inconsistent deformations occur among the grains, result-
ing in grain boundary cracking at high temperatures.
Therefore, non-homogenous grain causes intergranular
cracking, resulting in a poor ductility of the superalloy. In
brief, based on microscopic observations, failure of the
SLM Inconel 625 at low temperature is ductile, whereas
brittle failure occurs at high temperatures. A valuable
information that low ductility for SLM Inconel 625 is
mainly dependent on the intergranular cracking at high
temperatures is established. It may be a key factor for low
plastic tolerance for SLM Inconel 625 superalloy at high
temperatures.
SEM and EDS analysis were conducted on fractured
specimens after tensile testing, as shown in Figs. 11 and
12. Figure 11 shows the microcracks along the grain
boundary at high temperatures, especially among the triple
intersections. Because the orientations among the adjacent
grain are different, the elastic and plastic properties are not
the same. In order to satisfy the compatibility equation of
deformation before crack initiation, large stresses were
developed in these locations, resulting in microcracks with
1–2 lm in length indicated by the arrows in Fig. 11. After
the microcracks initiate along the grain boundary, the
adjacent cracks link together to form relative larger cracks,
and the bearing area of the specimens decreases until
failure.
No obvious slip bands are observed at high temperature
as shown in Fig. 11c–e. However, slip bands are clearly
shown in Fig. 11b at room temperature, and it indicates
that the SLM Inconel 625 has a large plastic deformation at
room temperature. This phenomenon is caused by the
movement of the dislocation inside the crystal under the
tensile stress. In addition, the grains are stretched in the
loading direction. A few scattered micro holes are found in
the grain boundary and within the grain. However, they do
not participate or develop during the fracture procedure,
and have tiny inﬂuence on the failure.
From Fig. 12b, c, it is observed that there are precipi-
tates at the grain boundary. In addition, there is evidence
that most of the precipitates along grain boundary are often
accompanied by the formation of microcracks (Fig. 12b).
This offers a valuable information that the precipitates

To clarify the identity of the precipitates, the matrix and
the precipitate along grain boundary were selected for the
element analysis using EDS (Fig. 12b, c). From EDS
analysis, it is evident that the precipitates are richer in Mo
and Nb compared to matrix. Moreover, C, Mo and Nb are
accumulated across the precipitates at grain boundary, as
shown in Fig. 12d–h and Table 2. This indicates that the
precipitates are carbides. However, carbides are not formed
at the temperature of 540 /C176C (Fig. 12a). Kreitcberg et al.
[22] think that the carbide particles which form and grow at
the grain boundaries during tensile testing could weaken
grain boundary, resulting in severe embrittlement at ele-
vated temperature. Also, they found the segregation of Mo
and Nb in the area of carbides (M

Mo and Nb are uniformly distributed in Fig. 12b, c, M 6C
carbides [ 24] are formed. Therefore, the carbides
accumulated along the grain boundary could weaken the
strength of the grain boundary. Moreover, these carbides
would intensely reduce the ductility of the alloy at elevated
temperatures. Since the carbides are commonly found
within the grain boundary at high temperature, they are
absent at room temperature tests. It could be asserted that
these carbides accelerate the formation of microholes or
cracks at high temperatures. Additionally, formation of
carbide precipitates at the grain boundary prevents the
grain boundary from sliding, leading to the poor ductility
displayed at high temperature.
4 Conclusion
Temperature effect on mechanical behavior of Inconel 625
alloy manufactured by SLM was experimentally studied
from room temperature up to 1000 /C176C. Abnormal embrit-
tlement was ﬁrstly found at high temperature and explained
by microstructure examinations.
The ultimate stress and yield stress of SLM Inconel 625
showed a slight decrease compared to those of forged
Inconel 625. However, the elongation of the SLM Inconel
625 decreased with temperature increasing, which was
much different from that of forged Inconel 625 as well as
most of the metal materials. Intergranular cracking
decreased the ductility of SLM Inconel 625 at high tem-
peratures. The non-homogenous distribution of grains and
the carbides formed at the grain boundary is the critical
