Ternary as-cast Ti 55−xNbxAl45, Ti53−xNbxAl47, Ti55−xTaxAl45, and Ti 53−xTaxAl47 alloys, where x = 0, 
4, 8, and 12, and Ti 45Nb8−xTaxAl47 and Ti 47Nb8−xTaxAl45 quaternary alloys, where x = 2, 4, and 6, 
melted in a laboratory arc furnace from pure components (~99.9 wt.%) are studied by X-ray 
diffraction (XRD), scanning and transmission electron microscopy (SEM and TEM), differential 
thermal analysis (DTA), and compression testing. The alloys are found to consist mainly of a 
superfine lamellar structure of decomposition β → α → α + γ → α2 + γ and contain tiny islands of 
the γ phase and also the β phase at 12 at.% Nb or Ta. The CALPHAD approach is used to obtain a 
thermodynamic description of the Al −Nb−Ta−Ti system, which adequately reproduces the 
experimental data. The compression tests performed at room temperature show that the ternary 
alloys with 45 at.% Al are characterized by higher strength but lower plasticity than the alloys with 
47 at.% Al. For the Ti 46Nb8Al46 and Ti 46Ta8Al46 alloys, high strength in the temperature range 
20−800°C and a drop in plasticity to 2 −4% at 200 −600°C are revealed. Among the quaternary 
alloys examined, the best combination of strength and plasticity (σ02 = 1043 MPa, σult = 1612 MPa, 
εpl = 12.4%) is exhibited by the Ti47Nb4Ta4Al45 alloy. 
Keywords: TiAl, lamellar structure, strength, plasticity, Al–Nb–Ta–Ti, CALPHAD, phase equilibria 
INTRODUCTION 
Two-phase α2 + γ titanium-aluminide alloys [1 −7] (the notation and structure of the phases are shown in 
Table 1) are considered to be the most promising candidat es to replace existing heat-resistant structural materials 
with high specific strength in applications above 600ºC. Depending on their composition and thermal treatment, 
these alloys exhibit different types of structures [16]: from lamellar to equiaxed grain structure, as well as feather-
like, Widmanstätten, and other structures, each possessing its own mechanical properties. 
The first-generation titanium-aluminide materials are Al−Ti binary alloys [7], whose range of compositions 
is shown with dashed lines in the phase diagram (Fig. 1). According to Kumar and other researchers [2, 17−23], thin 
layers of the α2 phase (harder but more brittle than γ) form in these alloys (for example, Ti −46 at.% Al) along with

The second-generation alloys are doped (for example, with V, Nb, Cr, or Mo) to optimize the content, size, 
and shape of α2 phase lamellas. Appel [24] proposes the following composition for the second-generation alloys: 
Ti–(45−49) Al–(0 −2) Cr, Mn, V–(0.5 −5) Nb, W, Mo–(0 −1) Si, B, C (at.%). After melting followed by 
thermomechanical treatment, these alloys reac h a yield stress of 700 MPa at 700 ºC [24 −27] and, hence, may be 
recommended for automotive and aerospace applications. 
Niobium has become the major doping agent in the third-generation alloys. It is believed that niobium 
additions increase heat- and oxidation-resistance and improve plasticity [28 −32]. The activation energy of diffusion 
increases and thus promotes refinement of the structure and enhancement of its thermal stability. The composition 
of the third-generation two-phase α2 + γ alloys is most often represented as Ti–(45 −47)Al–x(Nb, dM, B, C) (at.%) 
[7, 33] ( dM is another d metal). An example is the Ti–45Al–8Nb–0.2 C (TNB-V2) alloy developed at the GKSS 
Research Center (Germany) [4, 33−35].

phase (metastable), α → γm, during cooling at a relatively low rate. Further ageing of the single-phase γm alloys 
leads to a homogenous convoluted α2 + γ microstructure. Hence, to reach th e optimal structure and properties of

The structure, phase composition, and phase transformation of the Al −Nb−Ti alloys are well studied owing 
to great practical interest. The published results have been analyzed in critical reviews [39, 40] and assessed 
recently using thermodynamic modeling (CALPHAD) [10]. The Al−Ta−Ti system has not been studied so well, but 
overview papers have been published [41 −44] and thermodynamic assessment has been reported in [11]. Note that 
the papers [10] and [11] represent key experimental studies covering the region of two-phase α2 + γ alloys of the 
ternary systems in questions to support their thermodynamic descriptions. 
Although the structure of alloys, phase equi libria, and phase transformations of the Al −Nb−Ti and 
Al−Ta−Ti systems are understood well, there are insufficient published data to identify the interrelation between the 
content of doping niobium and tantalum additions in the two-phase α2 + γ alloys and their mechanical properties. 
Only do some papers, for example [24], focus on the structure and properties of a series of the alloys 
(Ti−5Nb−45Al, Ti−10Nb−45Al, and Ti −10Nb−48Al, content in at.%). Many publications focus on Ti −8Nb−46Al 
[4, 6, 7, 35, 36, 45 −56], Ti−9Nb−46Al [30, 32, 57 −61], or Ti −8Ta−46Al (at.%) [7, 36 −38, 62−64] alloys. Data on 
niobium alloys of other compositions are provided in [5, 6, 10, 19, 25 −27, 29−32, 47, 53, 65 −76] and on tantalum 
alloys in [11, 67−69]. 
We focused our main efforts on system atic studies intended to ascertain the effect of niobium and tantalum 
doping on the mechanical properties of titanium-aluminide alloys, such as their compressive strength and plasticity 
in the range from room temperature to 80 0ºC. To examine the mechanical properties, we used the same alloys as in 
[10, 11], with 0, 4, 8, and 12 at.% Nb or Ta at 45 or 47 at.% Al, Ti–8Nb–46Al and Ti–8Ta–46Al (at.%) alloys, and 
Al−Nb−Ta−Ti ternary alloys with 8 at.% Nb +Ta at 45 and 47 at.% Al.

STARTING MATERIALS AND EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE 
The test ingots of 20 g in weight were produced by melting iodide titanium (99.9 wt.% Ti), lump aluminum 
(99.99 wt.% Al), niobium NBSh-00 (99.9 wt.% Nb, 0.05 wt.% Ta, 0.02 wt.% Fe, <0.03 wt.% O, 0.009 wt.% Ti, and 
0.009 wt.% Si), or/and tantalum (99.9 wt.% Ta) with alloys of composition Ti–8Nb–46Al or/and Ti–8Ta–46Al 
(at.%), produced at the ACCESS e. V. [51] and used as mast er alloys, and with a series of other master alloys. The 
ingots were melted in a laboratory arc furnace with a nonconsumable tungsten electrode on a water-cooled copper 
hearth. The furnace was vacuumed to 5 −7 Pa, purged with argon of grade A (impurities of 0.01 wt.% N 2, 
0.003 wt.% O 2, 3 ⋅ 10−5 
kg/m3 moisture), and filled with argon to r each about 60 kPa for melting. During the 
melting, the argon pressure increased to about 100 kPa. To homogenize the ingots, they were flipped and melted 
three times. The weight loss was small (<0.4 wt.%) af ter melting, but some corrections were employed to 
compensate for composition using energy-dispersive spectra (EDS) (Table 2). The cooling rate of ingots was about 
100 ºC/sec. 
The oxygen content of the Ti −55Al, Ti–8Nb–46Al, Nb −74Al, Ti–8Ta–46Al, and Ta −70Al (at.%) master 
alloys did not exceed 0.02–0.04 wt.% and the N and H contents were below the minimally measurable thresholds 
(<0.001 wt.% N and 0.003 wt.% H). 
The ingots were examined with X -ray diffraction (XRD), metallography, optical microscopy, and scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM) using a Gemini 1550 microscope with an INCA analyzer. The composition of the alloys 
and their structural constituents (phases) was determined from energy-dispersive spectra using Al −Nb−Ti and 
Al−Ta−Ti alloys with well established compositions as refe rence. Some alloys were examined with transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM) using a JEM-100CX microscope. The diffraction patterns were taken from polished and 
etched samples using a DRON-3 diffractometer (Cu-Kα radiation). 
The temperatures of solid-phase transformations and melting were determined with differential thermal 
analysis (DTA) using W/W–20Re wire thermocouples de signed by Kocherzhinsky [77, 78]. Crucibles made of 
Sc2O3 were used. The DTA experiments were performed in high-purity helium during heating and cooling at a rate 
of 20 or 40 °C/min. The thermocouples were calibrated against secondary (Al, Au, Pd, Pt, Rd) and auxiliary (Fe and

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Structure and properties of Ti 55−xNbxAl45, Ti 53−xNbxAl47, Ti 55−xTaxAl45, and Ti 53−xTaxAl47 alloys, where 
x = 0, 4, 8, 12. The γ phase is identified in all alloys subj ected to X-ray diffraction (XRD) and the α2 phase in most 
of them (Table 2). As expected, the diffraction patterns show strong texture since they are taken from polished 
sections. The microstructure of the alloys testifies that grains of the high-temperature phase decomposed during 
cooling are coarse (Figs. 2 and 3). As is known [81, pp. 403 −436], as-cast titanium-alumin ide materials of similar 
composition have mainly lamellar st ructure, in which lamellas of the α2 and γ phases are oriented mostly at a right 
angle to the crystallization front. All alloys show a superfine lamellar structure of decomposition 
β → α → α + γ → α2 + γ with submicron-thickness lamellas visibl e at large magnifications (major gray 
component). Niobium and tantalum doping leads to dendritic segregation, which can be seen from light and dark 
layers enriched with heavy or light components, respectively. The darkest homogeneous grains were identified as 
the γ phase, which apparently separated from the last drop of the melt. The light-colored layers mostly represent the 
structure of decomposition as well. Only do alloys with 12 at.% Nb or Ta contain superfine bright grains, which can 
be considered dependin g on their composition and the phase ratio [10, 11] as the stabilized β phase. The X-ray 
diffraction patterns of alloy samples containing 12 at.% Nb or Ta ascertain the assumption that the β phase is 
stabilized⎯they contain one or two strong reflections of the cubic phase ( β-Ti, Nb, Al) or (β-Ti, Ta, Al). The (111) 
reflection typical of the β0 phase of CsCl type was not observed at 2 θ ≈ 48.2°. Considering th e low content of the 
cubic phase, the diffraction patterns cannot allow a unique conclusion on its structure, CsCl ( B2) or W (A2) crystal 
structural type. That is, it is not known for certain whether the β(A2) → β0(B2) ordering, which might have 
occurred according to the phase diagrams, has actually taken place [10, 11]. The lattice parameters of the identified 
phases depend little on the composition of alloys: a = 400 −403 pm and c = 407 −409 pm for the γ phase; a = 
= 575−577 pm and c = 462 −463.5 pm for the α2 phase; and about 322 −323 pm for the cubic phase ( β/β0). 
Reflections of low intensity could not be identified at 2θ = 341° and 2θ = 39.8° in some diffraction patterns (Table 2). 
According to XRD and microstructural analysis (SEM ), it can be concluded that the structures of 
Al−Nb−Ti and Al −Ta−Ti alloys are largely similar in the composit ion range in question. At the same time, the 
microstructure of the Ti55−xNbxAl45 and Ti55−xTaxAl45 samples, where x = 4, 8, and 12, is different from that of the 
Ti53−xNbxAl47 and Ti53−xTaxAl47 samples: coarser (in some cases to 1 mm) grains of the high-temperature α phase 
form in the samples containing 47 at.% Al than those of the samples containing 45 at.% Al. This structural 
difference between these two groups of alloys, substantially influencing their mechanical behavior, can be 
explained using the ternary phase diagrams constructed in [10, 11]. The vertical sections of the Al −Nb−Ti (Fig. 
4a−c) and Al −Ta−Ti (Fig. 4 d−f) systems at 45 at.% and 47 at.% Al and 8 at.% Nb or Ta show that the high-
temperature β phase crystallizes primarily in all alloys of interest. However, in the alloys with 47 at.% Al the α 
phase forms during perit ectic transformation L + β → α (i.e. in the course of solidi fication), whereas in the alloys 
with 45 at.% Al it forms from the β phase in the course of solid-state transformations.

Study of Ti 46Nb8Al46 and Ti 46Ta8Al46 alloys. Compression testing in the range from room temperature to 
800°C (Figs. 6 and 7) has shown that the strength of the Ti46Nb8Al46 alloys with yield stress σ02 = 910 MPa and the 
Ti46Ta8Al46 alloys with yield stress σ02 = 800 MPa at room temperature is clos e to that of the hardest alloys with 
45 at.% Al and their plasticity ( εpl = 11.6% for Ti 46Nb8Al46 and εpl = 9.0% for Ti 46Ta8Al46) to the alloys with 
47 at.% Al. 
The data shown in Fig. 7 testify that the Ti 46Nb8Al46 alloy retains high strength ( σ02 = 910−1030 MPa) in 
the entire temperature range in question. The strength of σ02 = 1200 MPa at 200 °C that falls beyond this 
temperature range can be attributed to the isotropic coarse -grained structure of the as- cast samples. The orientation 
of grains in this sample, which are much coarser than in other samples, promot es high strength. The heat resistance 
of the alloy remains high ( σ02 = 1030 MPa) even at 800 °C and quite high plasticity ( εpl = 13%). The Ti 46Ta8Al46 
alloy has somewhat lower strength in the range between 20 and 600 °C. Nevertheless, its heat resistance even 
increases and practically equals that of the niobium alloy at 800 °C. The plasticity of the two alloys is virtually the

CONCLUSIONS 
X-ray diffraction analysis and scanning electron and tr ansmission microscopy have shown that the as-cast 
Ti55−xNbxAl45, Ti 53−xNbxAl47, Ti 55−xTaxAl45, and Ti 53−xTaxAl47 alloys (where x = 0, 4, 8, 12) and 
Ti45Nb8−xTaxAl47 and Ti 47Nb8−xTaxAl45 quaternary alloys (where x = 2, 4, 6) mainly contain a superfine lamellar 
structure of decomposition β → α → α + γ → α2 + γ with islands of the γ phase for all compositions and the β 
phase at 12 at.% Nb or Ta. 
Using the CALPHAD approach, thermodynamic assessment of the Al−Nb−Ta−Ti systems has been carried 
out for the first time, which adequately represents experimental results. 
According to compression testing at room temperature, the as-cast Ti 55−xNbxAl45 and Ti55−xTaxAl45 alloys 
(where x = 0, 4, 8, 12) have higher strength but lower plasticity than the Ti53−xNbxAl47 and Ti53−xTaxAl47 alloys. 
The Ti 46Nb8Al46 and Ti 46Ta8Al46 alloys show high strength (~800 −1050 MPa) in the range from room 
temperature to 800 °C (compression testing) and a drop in plasticity (to 2 −4%) at 200 −600°C. The Ti47Nb4Ta4Al45 
alloy demonstrates the best combination of strength and plasticity ( σ02 = 1043 MPa, σult = 1612 MPa, εpl = 12.4%) 
among the quaternary alloys.
