A New Alumina-Forming Ni-Co-Cr Base Alloy for Service
in Gas Turbine Engine Combustors and Other
High-Temperature Applications
Lee M. Pike, S. Krishna Srivastava, and Alison K. Forsythe
(Submitted August 10, 2018; in revised form December 20, 2018)
HAYNES/C210 233/C212 alloy based on the Ni-Co-Cr-Mo-Al system has been developed for service at high tem-
peratures. The origins of 233 alloy can be traced to a couple of Haynes alloys: 230 alloy and 214 alloy. The
former is a premier high-temperature alloy with superb creep strength and is widely accepted in the gas
turbine industry. It is also chromia forming. The alumina-forming 214 alloy with its 4.5% Al content
possesses outstanding oxidation resistance at high temperatures, but its creep strength above /C241800 /C176F
(982 /C176C) is very poor. The new 233 alloy attempts to bridge this performance. This paper will present
preliminary test results for 233 alloy from the products of the ﬁrst production heats, including the tensile
and creep properties of the alloy, microstructure, oxidation properties, as well as fabricability. These results
will be compared and discussed with those of popular gas turbine alloys such as 230, 214, 617, and X alloys.
The preliminary test results indicate that HAYNES 233 alloy has a unique combination of excellent oxi-
dation resistance and creep-rupture strength while at the same time being readily fabricable. These
properties make the alloy an attractive candidate for a wide variety of gas turbine and other high-tem-
perature applications.
Keywords alloy development, creep and stress rupture, HAYNES
233 alloy, mechanical, oxidation, superalloys
1. Introduction
Many components of gas turbine engines, such as the
combustor, are fabricated into complex geometries using thin
gauge sheet or plate materials. For these applications, a number
of commercial superalloys are available which can be described
as ‘‘readily fabricable.’’ This term can be deﬁned as applying to
alloys which can be conventionally hot worked (isothermal
conditions not necessary), cold worked, welded, machined, etc.
These readily fabricable superalloys can be divided into
different families based on their key properties.
1.1 Gamma-Prime Formers
These include Waspaloy alloy, 282 /C210 alloy, 263 alloy, X-750
alloy, and others (Ref 1, 2). These alloys are characterized by
their high creep-rupture strength. However, these alloys are
generally not used above 1600-1700 /C176 F (871 to 927 /C176 C) at the
most due to their low strength at temperatures above the
gamma-prime solvus. Furthermore, these alloys generally have
very good oxidation resistance at their use temperatures, but at
higher temperatures their oxidation is not as good.
1.2 Alumina-Formers
These include 214 /C210 alloy and HR-224 /C210 alloy, but not the
ODS alloys (which do not have the requisite fabricability). The
alloys in this family have excellent oxidation resistance at
temperatures as high as 2100 /C176 F (1149 /C176 C) due to their
formation of a protective alumina scale (Ref 3, 4). They are
used at these very high temperatures, but only in speciﬁc non-
structural applications. Their use in structural components is
limited due to poor creep strength.
1.3 Solid-Solution Strengthened Alloys
These include 230 /C210 alloy, HASTELLOY /C210 X alloy, 617
alloy, and others. As their name implies, these alloys derive
their high creep strength primarily from the solid-solution
strengthening effect, as well carbide formation (Ref 5-7). This
strengthening remains effective even at very high tempera-
tures—well above the maximum temperature of the gamma-
prime formers, for example. Most of the solid-solution
strengthened alloys have very good oxidation resistance due
to the formation of a protective chromia scale (Ref 8, 9).
However, their oxidation resistance is not comparable to the
alumina-formers, particularly at the very high temperatures,
such as 2100 /C176 F (1149 /C176 C).
When the attributes of these various alloy families are
considered, it is clear that there are no readily fabricable
superalloys commercially available which combine both high
creep strength and excellent oxidation resistance at tempera-
tures up to 2100 /C176 F (1149 /C176 C). However, in the effort to

2. Alloy Research and Development
With the recognized need for a readily fabricable superalloy
with high creep strength and excellent oxidation resistance up
to 2100 /C176 F (1149 /C176 C), an alloy research and development
program was initiated at Haynes International. The end result of
this program was HAYNES 233 alloy—a readily fabricable
superalloy possessing both high creep strength and excellent
oxidation resistance up to 2100 /C176 F (1149 /C176 C). The nominal
composition of 233 alloy is Ni-19Co-19Cr-7.5Mo-3.3Al plus
minor elemental additions. The alloy has been produced on a
production scale using the conventional VIM–ESR (vacuum
induction melting–electroslag remelting) method commonly
used to manufacture fabricable superalloys. The remainder of
this paper will present the attributes and properties of the new
233 alloy as measured during the initial assessments of
production material. Note that all data presented here were
collected from full production-scale heats melted and processed
to ﬁnal gauge and ﬁnal annealed in the Haynes International
mill (i.e., no data taken from laboratory produced material are
included).
The typical microstructure of 233 alloy sheet is shown in the
micrograph in Fig. 1. This sample was in the solution annealed
condition (typical annealing temperature range is 2125 to
2150 /C176 F (1163 to 1177 /C176 C). The grain size of this sample was
measured as ASTM 4, which is within the typical range for this
product. Scattered primary carbides can be observed in the
microstructure, but the grain boundaries are seen to be largely
free of precipitation. In this condition, the sheet material is quite
soft ( /C2489 HRB) and ductile making it readily cold formable.
3. Oxidation Resistance
The excellent oxidation resistance of 233 alloy is a result of
the formation of a protective alumina scale. This scale is
stable up to very high temperatures. For the purpose of
determining the oxidation resistance of 233 alloy, the ﬁrst test
performed was a static oxidation test at 2100 /C176 F (1149 /C176 C).
This is a very high temperature for a nickel-base alloy and was
selected so that the test could easily discern the relative
oxidation resistance of various high-temperature alloys. The
testing was performed for 1008 h in a ﬂowing air environment.
The samples were cycled to room temperature once a week
(total of six cycles). After testing, the oxide was chemically
descaled from the samples. The samples were weighed, and the
metal loss was calculated from the difference in weight
compared to the samples prior to the test. The descaled samples
were mounted in metallographic mounts and examined to
measure internal oxidation. The average metal-affected values
were calculated as the sum of the metal loss and the average
internal penetration. Further details of the static oxidation test
methodology may be found elsewhere (Ref 10). The results of
the static oxidation test of 233 alloy are given in Table 1 along
with results for several other high-temperature alloys tested
under identical conditions (Ref 11, 12).
The measured metal loss was very low for 233 alloy as well
as 214 alloy, being 0.2 and 0.1 mils (5 and 3 lm), respectively.
For 230, 617, X, and 188 alloys, the metal loss was much
higher, ranging from 1.0 to 8.6 mils (25 to 218 lm). Similarly,
the average metal affected (metal loss + average internal
penetration) was the same low value for both 233 and 214
alloys: 0.5 mils (13 lm). This is well below the values ranging
from 4.4 to 10.7 mils (112 to 272 lm) for 230, 617, X, and 188
alloys. A micrograph of the cross section of the descaled 233
alloy sample tested at 2100 /C176 F (1149 /C176 C) is shown in Fig. 2
along with similar micrographs for 214, 230, and 617 alloys. It
is evident from the micrographs that the alumina-forming 233
and 214 alloys exhibited very little oxidation attack compared
to both the chromia-forming 230 and 617 alloys. Extensive
internal oxidation can be seen in both of the latter alloys. The
results of this test clearly indicate that the alumina-formers,
such as 233 alloy, are signiﬁcantly more oxidation resistant
than chromia-formers under these conditions. More extensive
oxidation testing of the alloy, including static oxidation testing
at other temperatures and dynamic oxidation testing, is
currently in progress.
4. Creep Strength
In this section, the creep-rupture strength of 233 alloy in the
temperature range of 1700 to 2100 /C176 F (927 to 1149 /C176 C) will be
discussed. Since the solvus temperature of the gamma-prime
phase is 1767 /C176 F (964 /C176 C), it will either be absent or very
coarse with a lower volume fraction when tested at these
temperatures; therefore, gamma-prime strengthening is not a
major contributor to the high creep strength. The high creep
strength of 233 alloy in this temperature range can be attributed
primarily to the presence of Mo as a potent solid-solution
strengthener and to favorable carbide strengthening. During the
alloy research which led to the development of 233 alloy, a
screening test of 1800 /C176 F (982 /C176 C)/2.5 ksi (17 MPa) was used
as a good indicator of creep-rupture strength. The results of
creep testing of sheet material under these conditions of 233

5. Age-Hardened Condition
HAYNES 233 alloy was primarily developed for use at very
high temperatures, particularly 1700 to 2100 /C176 F (927 to
1149 /C176 C); this range is mostly above the gamma-prime solvus
of 1767 /C176 F (964 /C176 C). At these high temperatures, the high creep
strength of the alloy does not depend on the gamma-prime
phase. However, 233 alloy may also be considered for use in
low-to-intermediate temperatures where gamma-prime
strengthening may be a beneﬁt. The following age-hardening
heat treatment has been devised for 233 alloy: 1650 /C176 F
(899 /C176 C)/4 h + 1450 /C176 F (788 /C176 C)/8 h. The application of this
heat treatment signiﬁcantly increases the strength of the alloy.
For example, the RT (room temperature) yield strength of one
batch of sheet material increased from 50.4 ksi (348 MPa) to
112.9 ksi (778 MPa) when subjected to the age-hardening
treatment. The alloy maintains good ductility in the age-
hardened condition, having a respectable RT tensile elongation
of 27.3%. The age-hardened tensile properties from RT to
1600 /C176 F (871 /C176 C) are given in Table 2. Additionally, the yield
strength of age-hardened 233 alloy as a function of temperature
is compared in Fig. 6 to other commercially available alloys
(Ref 12, 14-16). Not surprisingly, it is seen that age-hardened
233 alloy is considerably stronger than the non-age-hardenable
230 alloy at all temperatures. More notably, age-hardened 233
alloy exhibits improved yield strength versus the age-hardened
263 and 282 alloys at most temperatures. The only exception
being that 282 alloy has somewhat higher yield strength at
temperatures above 1400 /C176 F (760 /C176 C). When compared to age-
hardened Waspaloy, age-hardened 233 alloy was less strong
from RT to 1200 /C176 F (649 /C176 C), but from 1400 to 1600 /C176 F (760
to 871 /C176 C) the two alloys had virtually the same yield strength.
In summary, the yield strength of age-hardened 233 alloy
compared very well to other fabricable alloys. Finally, the data
in Table 2 indicate that the ductility of age-hardened 233 alloy
remained greater than 20% up to 1600 /C176 F (871 /C176 C).
The creep strength of age-hardened 233 alloy was also
found to compare well with other readily fabricable alloys. The
comparative 1% creep lives of age-hardened 233 alloy as well
as several other alloys are plotted in Fig. 7 using a Larson–
Miller plot (LM constant assumed to be 20). The temperature
range studied was 1200 to 1700 /C176 F (649 to 927 /C176 C). It was
found that 233 alloy had creep strength essentially the same as
282 alloy for most conditions except at the very high end of the
LMP range (higher temperature and lower stress). Both 233
alloy and 282 alloy had better creep strength than 263 alloy and
Waspaloy alloy at all conditions. Also shown on the plot for
comparative purposes is 230 alloy which had much lower creep
strength. Recall that in the higher temperature range, 230 alloy
had essentially the same creep strength as 233 alloy (see
Fig. 4). However, at the lower temperatures the fact that 233 is
age-hardenable gives it a considerable creep strength advantage
over 230 alloy.

6. Fabricability
Since 233 alloy is a very new product, the data to determine
fabricability are fairly scant. However, some valuable data have
been collected.
6.1 Cold Formability
One measure of cold formability is the RT tensile properties.
The average yield strength and ultimate tensile strength of
several gauges of 233 alloy sheet in the mill-annealed condition
were 53.2 ksi (367 MPa) and 122.5 ksi (845 MPa), respec-
tively. The low yield strength implies that cold forming can be
performed without excessive applied force. Moreover, the
average RT elongation of 57.2% will allow for very good cold-
forming characteristics.
6.2 Weldability
Initial studies of the weldability of 233 alloy were conducted
using GTAW (gas tungsten arc welding) on 0.063 ¢¢ (1.6 mm)
sheet. RT tensile properties of the autogenously transverse
welded sheet are shown in Table 3. Tests were conducted in
three conditions: as-welded, welded + annealed, and
welded + annealed + age hardened. In the as-welded condi-
tion, the RT tensile properties compared well to base metal
properties. For example, the UTS (ultimate tensile strength)
value of the as-welded material was 93% of the annealed base
metal value. Welded + annealed properties were not much
different from the as-welded material. When the alloy was
welded + annealed + age hardened, the properties were favor-
able to the base metal in the age-hardened condition. In this
case, the UTS value was 92% of the age-hardened base metal
value. More extensive studies of welded material, including
samples welded with matching ﬁller metal, are underway and
will be presented at a later time.
6.3 Strain-Age Cracking Resistance
A key measure of fabricability of Al-containing superalloys
is the resistance to strain-age cracking. Strain-age cracking
typically occurs during heat up in the ﬁrst post-weld heat
treatment (usually a solution anneal) of a welded component.
The cracking has been associated with residual stresses in the
material which are created by shrinkage due the solidiﬁcation
of the weld metal and to the formation of gamma-prime during
the post-weld heat treatment. Since one key factor affecting
whether an alloy is susceptible to strain-age cracking is the
amount of gamma-prime forming elements such as Al and Ti,
the composition of HAYNES 233 alloy was chosen to limit
these elements as much as possible while still allowing for
sufﬁcient Al to ensure that the alloy would be an alumina-
former. Therefore, once the production material of 233 alloy
was produced, a critical check was to experimentally test the
alloy/C213s resistance to strain-age cracking. To do so, the Gleeble-

7. Summary
A new fabricable superalloy, HAYNES 233 alloy, has been
developed for use in gas turbine engine components, such as
the combustor. Oxidation, mechanical, and fabricability tests of
production-scale sheet material of 233 alloy were presented in
this paper. The new alloy possesses both high creep strength
and oxidation resistance up to 2100 /C176 F (1149 /C176 C). The
excellent oxidation resistance is achieved by the presence of
a critical amount of Al—sufﬁcient to allow the formation of a
protective alumina scale, but not so much as to severely limit
the fabricability of the alloy. The high creep strength at
temperatures above 1700 /C176 F (927 /C176 C) can be attributed to a
combination of solid-solution and carbide strengthening. For
applications at intermediate temperatures, 233 alloy can be age
hardened to signiﬁcantly increase both tensile and creep
strength. Additional testing is ongoing to more fully character-
ize the new 233 alloy. This includes additional static oxidation,
tensile, creep-rupture, weldability, and fabricability tests as well
as dynamic oxidation, low-cycle fatigue (LCF), physical
properties, etc.
