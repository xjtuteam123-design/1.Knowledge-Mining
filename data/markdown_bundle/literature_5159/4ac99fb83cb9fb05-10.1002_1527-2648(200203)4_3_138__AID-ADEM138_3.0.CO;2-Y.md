leading to a more attractive and economical processing route.
In conclusion, a whole manufacturing and processing
route has been developed at ONERA to produce and consoli-
date pre-alloyed TiAl powders. Different processing condi-
tions were applied in order to assess the potential of the PM
route with respect to the casting route. The latter leads to bet-
ter tensile properties whereas the former offers the possibility
of lowering the extrusion temperature. However, it is worth
pointing out that a higher extrusion temperature tends to en-
hance significantly room-temperature tensile elongations.
Additionally, high-temperature treatments are recommended
to ensure better homogenization, which in turn guarantees
improved mechanical properties of PM specimens. Therefore,
more development work on alloy chemistry and REP process-
ing would still be required to achieve a good compromise be-
tween process properties and subsequent mechanical proper-
ties.
With regard to secondary processing, ingot and powder
extrusion routes lead similarly to fine recrystallized micro-
structures, which are regarded as potentially attractive for sub-
sequent hot-forging operations at relatively low temperatures.
Accordingly, specimens prepared from extruded PM material
were successfully forged at 900 C with a high reduction ratio.
Once optimized, this processing route is expected to be com-
mercially viable for the production of gas turbine components.

Superalloys and Stainless Steels

and high-alloyed steels to give joints with high-temperature
resistance and corrosion resistance.

The main characteristic of these filler metals is their relatively
high content of metalloids like boron, phosphorus, and silicon.
These elements are necessary to reduce the melting tempera-
tures of the Ni and the Ni–Cr matrix down to suitable brazing
temperatures between 1000 and 1250 C.
[3] Furthermore, these
metalloids are responsible for the excellent wetting and flow
behavior on stainless steels as well as on Ni-based superalloys.
Besides all advantages, the use of metalloids in Ni-based
filler metals can also lead to the formation of brittle hard
phases like Ni borides or Cr silicides within the brazing seam.
The negative effect of these brittle phases is a reduction of the
joint strength and a locally reduced corrosion resistance due
to a Cr depletion.
[4] Moreover, due to their brittle behavior
the Ni-based brazing alloys are not rollable, and hence can be
produced only as powder or as melt-spun foil.
To reduce or to avoid hard phase formation it is necessary
to use long brazing times or heat treatment after solidifica-
tion. Short diffusion distances for boron, silicon, or phos-
phorus are advantageous, so that from the construction view-
point small brazing gaps (<50 lm) are recommended.
However, all measures lead to a prolongation of the process
time and hence to an increase in the product costs.
The use of Hf, instead of the conventional metalloids, as a
melting-point depressant for Ni-based filler metals represents
a metallurgical approach to producing ductile Ni-based filler
metals with moderate brazing temperatures below 1240 C.
[5]
In this communication this approach is discussed in detail
and appropriate experimental investigations are described.
The basis of the filler metal development was the Ni-Hf
eutectic (Ni
5Hf) on the Ni-side of the binary Ni–Hf phase dia-
gram at 1190 C, see Figure 1.
Compared to borides with an microhardness of 2000–
2600 HV0.05, the Ni 5Hf-phase has a microhardness of
400 HV0.05. Thus, relatively good ductility of the Hf-contain-
ing filler metals can be predicted. Starting from this binary
system, different components like Cr, Mo, Co, Ti were added
in order to improve the wetting and the flow behavior as well
as the corrosion resistance and the mechanical properties of
the filler metal. Numerous wetting tests and differential ther-
mal analysis (DTA) measurements showed that the Ni–Hf–Cr
system possesses especially promising features.
The melting range of the alloys produced was determined
by DTA.
[6] Figure 2 shows a DTA plot of a Ni15Hf13Cr filler
metal. The dashed line indicates the temperature curve. The
DT curve is typical of the Ni–Hf–Cr alloys. The melting behav-
ior of such alloys is characterized by an intense endothermic
peak at 1200–1240 C during heating, which corresponds to
the melting of the Ni
5Hf phase. After passing a melting range
where liquid and solid phases coexist, complete melting of all
filler metal is reached at temperatures above 1350 C. Under
cooling conditions the curve is similar, but shifted to lower
temperatures due to undercooling effects. The added Cr
slightly increases the melting temperature of the alloys.
The wetting tests were carried out in an Astro vacuum
furnace. The wetting behavior of the alloys was analyzed
regarding the parameter brazing temperature, the substrate
material, and the quality of the used vacuum. Owing to
the high oxygen affinity of Hf, good melting and flow behav-
ior could only be achieved at vacuum pressure greater than
21 0
–4 mbar. The wetting tests were carried out at tempera-
tures between 1220 and 1270 C with a dwell time of 10 min.

The microstructures of the brazing seams of the samples
based on stainless steel or Ni superalloys were analyzed by
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and optical microscopy.
Figure 4 and Figure 5 show the microstructure of a steel joint

tional relation between the hardness, the yield stress, and ten-
sile strength. Hence, with the measurement of the hardness of
the filler metals, it is possible to estimate their deformability.
The rollability of the alloys was determined by cold-roll tests:
some of the alloys had to be annealed between each rolling
step.

The mechanical strengths of the brazed Inconel and steel
samples were analyzed using tensile testing according to the
German technical standard DIN 8525. This method allows the
absolute strength of the joints to be determined. The test spec-
imens were brazed at 1235 C for 10 min. The brazing gap
varied in the range 50–70 lm. It is important to note that the
tensile strength of a brazed joint increases with decreasing
joint space and with the strength of the base material. Table 2
summarizes the results of the investigations.
It was observed that the steel joints had higher tensile
strengths than the brazed Inconel samples. The highest tensile
strength of 587 MPa was obtained using a Ni15Hf13Cr filler
metal. All the joints failed in the brazing seams and the in-
spection of the failure surfaces by SEM showed the presence
of the intermetallic phases Ni
7Hf2 and Ni10Hf7.
This communication summarizes the results of wetting
and corrosion tests on metalloid-free Ni–Hf–Cr brazing alloys
and gives an overview of their mechanical behavior. The best
wetting and flow behavior on stainless steel and Inconel 600
substrates was observed using brazing temperatures of
1235 C and a vacuum pressure of less than 2  10
–4 mbar. No
brittle phase were formed during the brazing process because
of the absence of B, Si, or P as melting-point depressant ele-
ments. Hence, these new Ni-based filler metals are rollable
and possesses high tolerances in terms of brazing gaps.
For the evaluation of the aqueous corrosion behavior of
the Hf-containing brazing alloys potentiodynamic investiga-
tions as well as aging trials in different electrolytes were car-
ried out. The potentiodynamic measurements in the diluted
acetic acid show that the break-through potential of 200 mV
for the Ni–Hf–Cr alloys are very similar to stabilized steels.
The joint samples that were aged in different aqueous solu-
tions showed galvanic corrosion, and in all cases the eutectic
network within the joints had a weaker corrosion resistance
than the Ni–Cr solid solution.
TGA results showed that the oxidation resistance of the fil-
ler metals is poorer than that of the Inconel ground material
but significant higher than that of stainless steel 1.4301.
The steel samples brazed with the new Ni–Hf–Cr filler
metals show tensile strengths up to 587 MPa, in the range of
conventional Ni-based filler metals.
