Abstract 
Nb-1Zr-0.1C is a Niobium based refractory metal alloy having an excellent combination of high temperature properties like high 
melting point (~2500 oC), adequate high temperature strength (~200 MPa) and creep strength for long duration, better 
compatibility for alkali liquid metal, resistance to thermal s hock & radiation and low ductile to brittle transition temperatur e 
(DBTT). These properties make this alloy a potential candidate for several structural applications including compact high 
temperature reactors. Welding of this alloy is a difficult task due to its high melting point and its reactive nature. In compa rison 
to the conventional welding processes, high energy density sources like electron beam and laser beam which can produce deep 
penetrations with narrow heat affected zones are more suitable to weld the components made of Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy. This paper 
describes the development of electron beam welding procedure for Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy. Welding parameters were developed and 
weld bead was characterized by metallography and different zones of the weld bead were identified and studied. The results of 
the welding trials and the tests performed on the welds and the major observations & conclusions drawn from the experience of 
welding Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy are presented in this paper.

niobium alloys are characterized by better fabricability, higher ductility and lower density. It can be worked to 
achieve a wide range of strength and elasticity, has the least density among refractory metals and possesses 
superconducting and nuclear properties. Because of such pr operties, it finds applications in electrolytic capacitors,

Niobium and its alloys are one of the most useful and widely used refractory materials. 
Nb-1Zr alloy received considerable attention in the past , but its strength at high temperatures is moderate. 
Therefore, Niobium (Nb) - Zirconium (Zr) - Carbon (C) alloy i.e. Nb -1Zr-0.1C was developed by adding 0.1% 
carbon to promote the formation of very fine carbide precipitates for strengthening [1]. The carbide strengthened 
Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy is much more creep resistant than so lid-solution strengthened Nb-1Zr due to the presence of 
stable fine carbides of Zr and Nb. The creep strength of the Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy at 1350 K was found to be about five 
times that of Nb-1Zr alloy [2]. Niobium based alloy Nb-1Zr-0.1C is one of the most promising refractory metal 
alloys having an excellent combination of high temperature properties like, high melting point (~2500

high temperature strength (~200 MPa) and creep strength for long duration, better compatibility for alkali liquid 
metal, resistance to thermal shock & radiation and low ductile to brittle transition temperature (DBTT). These 
properties make this alloy a potential candidate for several structural applications at temperatures above 1000

compact high temperature nuclear reactors and space nuclear fission reactors. 
Applications of Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy call for the welding of this alloy. The welding of this alloy is a difficult task 
due to its high melting point and its reactive nature. This alloy has high affinity for oxygen, nitrogen & hydrogen at 
high temperature and during welding if the alloy is exposed to these gases it rapidly reacts with them and its 
physical properties are deteriorated. Hence welding is genera lly carried out under inert gas shielding or preferably 
inside welding chambers filled with high purity inert gases or under vacuum environment. In conventional welding 
processes like Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW), Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW) etc heat input to achieve a 
given depth of penetration is sufficiently high which leads to grain growth and recrystallization in the Heat Affected 
Zone (HAZ) of the weld. This causes rise in the transition temperature of the weld making it brittle and sensitive to 
surface notches, cracks and craters. Thus in comparison to the conventional welding processes, high energy density 
beam welding processes like electron beam welding (EBW) and laser beam welding (LBW) which can produce deep 
penetrations with low heat inputs are more suitable to weld the components made of Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy. 
Donnelly and Slaughter [3], who studied Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW) and electron beam welding of 
different refractory alloys, reported that defect free welds could be made inside an evacuated chamber back filled 
with high purity argon. They also observed that the arc welded specimens show a higher ductile-to-brittle transition 
temperature in comparison with the EBW welded specimens [3]. Central-line cracking in the weld zone and micro-
cracks in the heat affected zone (HAZ) were observed at certain heat inputs in some niobium alloys welded by 
GTAW and EBW processes [4]. Michael Santella et al, who investigated manual gas tungsten arc welded specimens 
of Nb-1Zr alloy, reported that the weld zone shows a higher hardness than that of the base metal [5]. As Nb-alloys 
are very reactive in nature and they react easily with the atmosphere at welding temperatures, their welding must be 
performed in high purity inert gas or in high vacuum and the parts to be welded must be cleaned properly [6]. 
In this paper development of electron beam welding procedure for Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy is described. Welding 
parameters were developed by taking number of bead on plate welding trials. Weld bead was characterized by 
metallography and different zones of the weld bead we re identified and studied. Weld microstructure was 
characterized by both optical and electron microscopy. Microhardness profile across the width of the weld was 
generated. The results of the welding trials & the tests performed on the welds and the major observations & 
conclusions drawn from the experience of welding Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy are also presented. 
 
 
2. Welding Procedure Development 
2.1. Electron beam welding process 
Electron beam welding is a fusion welding process where source of heat for fusing the faying surfaces is obtained

from EB gun. The heating source is the stream of electrons in space striking the job at high speed. EB gun consists 
of electron emitter (filament and/or cathode) for thermionic emission of electrons, bias or grid cup for shaping the 
beam, anode for accelerating the electrons and electromagnetic lens for focusing and deflection of electrons. Thus an 
EB gun generates, shapes and accelerates the stream of electrons. This stream of electrons is called electron beam 
(EB). This EB is concentrated and intercepted at the jo int where welding is accomplished as a result of intense 
localized heating. The electrons penetrate a short distance and give up their kinetic energy in the form of heat. There 
are two modes in which welds can be made in EB. In case of high energy density beam (HEDB) mode, a hole is 
melted through the material called keyhole. The hole containing the metal vapour moves along the joint by moving 
either the electron gun or the work piece. The metal at the front melts and flows around to the rear where it solidifies 
[7]. Keyhole technique is a unique characteristic of HEDB processes such as EBW, Laser and Plasma. In conduction 
mode, where no keyhole is formed, melting takes place by conduction of heat from the surface at lower power 
density. This mode gives shallow penetration similar to that of conventional arc welding processes. The schematic 
illustrating EBW process is shown in Fig. 1.

Advantages of EBW Process are: 
• Ability to produce deep and narrow welds with very low heat input. 
• Narrow HAZ and fewer thermal effects. 
• Completion of welds in one pass and multiple passes are not required. 
• High vacuum environment during welding results in freedom from weld contamination. 
• High melting rates and higher welding speed. 
• Capability of welding higher thickness joints. 
 
Limitations of EBW Process are: 
• Cost of EBW machine is high. 
• Requirement of precise joint fit-up leading to difficulty in joint preparation. 
• Productivity reduced due to pump down time of work chamber. 
• Electron beam is deflected due to magnetic field, hence nonmagnetic or degaussed work pieces and fixtures are 
required to be used.

2.3. Weld Coupons 
Weld coupons used for Electron Beam Welding trials were made of Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy prepared through melting 
route. Weld coupons were in the form of sheets of thickness 3.2 mm. These specimens were surface ground and 
further etched before conducting the welding trials. 
2.4. Welding trials 
Welding parameters for EBW can be classified in three categories as follows [8]: 
• Electrical Parameters: Accelerating voltage, Beam current, Focus current, Beam spot size, Beam Deflection, 
Slope up, Slope down, Overlap and Slope profile. 
• Mechanical Parameters: Welding speed, Direction, Speed ramping details, Wire/filler feed rate with direction (if 
any), position and angle. 
• Other parameters: Working distance or gun to job distance, vacuum in the gun chamber and vacuum in the 
welding chamber 
 
In EBW process each of the above welding parameters has effect on the weld profile. However the important 
parameters, which considerably influe nce the weld profiles in EBW are accelerating voltage, beam current, focus 
current, welding speed, working distance and chamber pressure. Since no past experience of welding Nb-1Zr-0.1C 
alloy existed with us, series of welding trials were conducted to study the effect of important EBW parameters on 
the weld bead. Welds in both Bead on plate (BOP) and butt joint configuration were produced on 3.2 mm thick 
sheets of Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy using the aforementioned electron beam welding machine. Autogenous butt weld joints 
were made on 3.2 mm thick sheet specimens of Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy with machined surfaces and during welding the 
specimens were held firmly using a fixture to prevent distortion. Different EB welds were produced by varying 
beam current from 30 to 60 mA and welding speed from 600 to 1200 mm/min (10 to 20 mm/sec). During these 
welding trials the specimens were kept inside an evacuated chamber (vacuum of the order of 10
-5 torr) at the beam 
focus position and the accelerating voltage was kept at 60 kV. The welded specimens were visually inspected and 
sectioned for microscopic examination. Depth of penetra tion and weld bead width were measured and few results 
are tabulated in Table 1.

3. Welding Results & Observations 
Following observations were made after the welding trials on the effect of welding parameters on weld profile in 
Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy: 
• Beam Current: For a given set of welding parameters, depth of penetration and weld width increases with 
increase of beam current. 
• Welding Speed: For a given set of welding parameters, the depth of penetration decreases with increasing 
welding speed. However it is observed that the depth of penetration is not linearly related to the inverse of the 
welding speed. 
 
As shown in Fig. 3, electron beam welds under visual examination showed bright coloration i.e. without any 
oxidation, as the welds were performed under vacuum. From the data given in Table 1, it may be noticed that full 
depth of penetration i.e. 3.2 mm was achieved at 60 mA beam current, 60 kV accelerating voltage and using welding 
speed in the range of 10 - 16.67 mm /sec. The linear heat input (H), i.e. the ratio of power to welding speed was 
calculated and found varying from 108 to 300 J/mm for different power and speeds. From Table-1, it is seen that the 
depth and the width of the welds were found to increase with an increase in the heat input and the aspect ratio (i.e. 
depth to width ratio) of the welds was found in the range 1 to 1.18.

5. Conclusions 
• Electron beam welding procedure & parameters were developed for welding of Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy to produce 
acceptable and defect free welds. 
• The relationship between different processing parameters like accelerating voltage, beam current, welding speed 
and depth / width of weld was studied. 
• The penetration depth & width of the weld were found increasing with increase in the heat input. 
• In EBW, 3.2 mm thick sheets of Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloy could be welded successfully at a power of about 3.6 kW 
using the welding speed in the range of 10-16.67 mm/sec. 
• Defect free welds were produced which was confirmed by examining the welds under both optical & scanning 
electron microscope. 
• The hardness values of the weld region and HAZ were found increasing with the increase in ratio of power to the 
welding speed i.e. linear heat input.
