1. Introduction 
The fields of aerospace, petrochemical equipment, and energy con -
version devices have put forward urgent needs for structural materials 
with excellent high-temperature mechanical performances [ 1 – 3 ]. The 
widely developed Ni-based superalloys seem unable to break the tem -
perature limit for applications, because the temperature gap between 
the γ’ and γ phases is very narrow [ 4 ]. Compared with the Ni-based 
superalloys, the Co-based superalloys possess higher operating temper -
ature because of their higher melting point [ 5 ]. The structural perfor -
mances of traditional Co-based superalloys originate mainly from 
solid-solution and carbide strengthening, and the strengthening effect 
is weaker than that of coherent ordered γ’ phases in Ni-based superal -
loys. Fortunately, the design of Co-Al-W-based superalloys with similar 
γ + γ’ microstructure as Ni-based superalloys overcomes this limitation. 
This kind of alloy has attracted much attention because of its excellent 
strength and creep resistance [ 6 ]. 
However, it is reported that the L1

4. Discussion 
4.1. High-temperature mechanical properties 
Compared with some other alloys shown in Fig. 2 , the current alloy 
exhibits excellent tensile mechanical properties over a wide temperature 
range. For example, the commercial Waspaloy alloy, a precipitation- 
hardened Ni-based superalloy, has a strength of about 800 MPa below 
600 
◦
C. In comparison, the current alloy has a strength of more than 
1400 MPa below 600 
◦
C, which is 1.75 times that of Waspaloy alloy. At 
700 
◦
C, the current alloy still has an ultra-high strength of 1000 MPa. 
More notably, the strength of this alloy is essentially unchanged from 
room temperature to 600 
◦
C, unlike the CoCrFeMnNi HEAs which 
exhibit continuous decrease in strength over the entire test temperature 
range. The excellent strength stability of the current alloy from room 
temperature to 600 
◦
C is due to the fact that nanoscale precipitates 
provide a large number of phase boundaries to hinder the movement of 
dislocations, thus preventing the softening with increasing the temper -
ature [ 21 ]. 
From room temperature to elevated temperatures, the temperature 
dependence of the yield strength or hardness of a material changes 
markedly at a certain temperature, which is called the transition tem -
perature ( T
T
) [ 21 ]. Wang et al. [ 21 ] state that the T
T 
can effectively 
describe the transition between deformation mechanisms. Furthermore, 
the ratio of T
T 
to T
m 
( T
m 
= 1834 K is the melting point [ 22 ]) is defined as 
the homologous temperature. In most metals and alloys, the homologous 
temperature is about 0.5 T
m
, and the maximum value is 0.55 T
m 
[ 23 ]. 
However, Yeh et al. [ 21,24 ] also found a series of alloys with homolo -
gous temperatures higher than 0.55, such as In718 and In718H with 
homologous temperatures of 0.66 and 0.64, respectively. For the current 
alloy, the T
T 
is about 644 
◦

◦
C (i.e., 0.5 T
m 
~ 0.55 T

basically consistent with the results in this study that the strength starts 
to decrease rapidly when the test temperature is higher than 700 
◦

tensile mechanical properties of many superalloys from room tempera -
ture to elevated temperatures have been studied, showing a similar 
phenomenon with the current alloy [ 26 , 27 ].

4.2. Deformation mechanism at elevated temperatures 
In order to further understand the relative deformation mechanisms 
of the alloy at elevated temperatures, the microstructures after tensile 
fracture are characterized by TEM. Fig. 9 presents the microstructure 
characteristics after tensile fracture at 600 
◦
C. In addition to stacking 
faults, deformation twins also appear ( Fig. 9 (a) and Fig. 9 (b)). These 
nanoscale deformation twins can act as barriers to dislocation move -
ment. The propagation of stacking faults is hindered and stops at the 
twin boundaries, which promotes the effective strain hardening of the 
alloy. Moreover, the stacking fault energy of the matrix increases with 
the increase of temperature, resulting in the cross-slip of 1/2 < 110 >
total dislocations from the {111} to the {100} slip planes, as exhibited in 
Fig. 9 (c).
