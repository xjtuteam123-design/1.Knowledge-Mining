The microstructure and hardness of Fe–Co-based Hiperco r alloys were investigated.
Scanning electron microscopy revealed elongated grains in the as-received (cast and
cold-rolled) alloys, and samples containing Nb had second phase precipitates.
Annealing of alloys for 1 to 3 h at temperatures in the range 700 to 800 °C resulted in
grains becoming equiaxed. In the Nb-containing alloys, the original precipitates
dissolved and new precipitates appeared. The rate of grain growth decreased with
increasing Nb content, suggesting that Nb or Nb-containing precipitates were
responsible for a reduction in the grain boundary mobility. The hardness as a function
of grain size in the annealed samples of all of the alloys could be plotted on the same
Hall–Petch curve, indicating that the yield strength of these alloys is governed by the
grain size, independent of the alloy composition and volume fraction of precipitates.
Fe–Co alloys have technological applications as soft
magnet materials displaying high magnetic saturation,
moderate core loss, and high-yield strength.
1–4 An im-
portant commercial use of these alloys is in the area of
aircraft power applications. For these applications, the
alloys must display high-temperature stability with re-
gard to microstructure as well as to the mechanical and
magnetic properties. In this communication, the micro-
structure and hardness as a function of grain size of Hi-
percor Fe–Co-based alloy sheets with three different
chemical compositions were investigated. The grain size
of the alloys was increased by annealing under a reducing
atmosphere to induce grain growth. It was observed that,
for a given annealing temperature, the kinetics of grain
growth varied for the different compositions as has been
previously reported.
3,4 However, it was found that, for all
three alloys, the hardness dependence on grain size could
be plotted on the same Hall–Petch curve. This strongly
suggests that although various impurities and second
phase precipitates observed in the different alloys lead to
different grain refining effects, the only important param-
eter with regard to the determining the plastic behavior is
the grain size.
Sheets of Hipercor alloys 27, 50, and 50HS were ob-
tained from Carpenter Technology Corporation (M.S.
Masteller
5). The sheet thicknesses were 0.014 in. for the
27 and 50 alloys and 0.006 in. for the 50HS alloy. The
compositions are given in Table I. The alloys had been
produced by casting and then cold-rolled into sheet form.
Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the as-received
materials revealed evidence of second phase precipitates
in Hipercor alloys 50 and 50HS (see Fig. 1). These pre-
cipitates have been observed previously and have been
identified as a carbonitride phase.
3–5
Samples of each composition were annealed in a re-
ducing atmosphere for 1 to 3 h at temperatures in the
range 700 to 800 °C in order to induce grain growth.
Scanning electron micrographs of samples subjected to
different anneals are shown in Fig. 2. In all of the an-
nealed samples, grains had become equiaxed (having
been elongated in the as-received materials due to the
rolling), and the original second phase precipitates had
disappeared (see Fig. 2). However, in the alloy 50 and
alloy 50HS specimens, a new second phase precipitate
appeared in the middle of grains and especially at the

Room-temperature microhardness tests were per-
formed on all of the annealed samples. To avoid in-plane
orientational dependence of mechanical properties as re-
ported in Refs. 3 and 4 the Knoop hardness was meas-
ured on the polished cross section of the sheets normal to
the rolling direction, with the long indent normal to the
thickness direction. It was found that all of the hardness
results could be put on the same Hall–Petch curve (hard-
ness versus inverse square root of grain size); see Fig. 3.
The fact that a “universal” Hall–Petch curve could be
obtained from all of the hardness data implies that the
room-temperature plasticity of Hipercor alloys is a func-
tion of grain size only and not a function of the chemical
composition, the precipitate volume fraction, or particle
density. The lack of a significant precipitation hardening
effect is presumably because there is a relatively small
volume fraction of precipitates whose contribution is
negligible compared to the grain hardening effect. Also,
any work hardening due to the cold-rolling was evidently
removed during annealing. With regard to alloy design,
this behavior suggests that there is great latitude in vary-
ing the chemical composition to improve the magnetic
