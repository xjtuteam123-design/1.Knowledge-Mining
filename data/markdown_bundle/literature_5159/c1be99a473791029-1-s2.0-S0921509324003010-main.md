ABSTRACT 
By investigating the relation between microstructure evolution and tensile responses of a BTi6431S alloy at room 
temperature and 700 
◦
C, this study finds an annealing-induced ductile-to-brittle transition phenomenon in the 
near- α titanium alloy. The high strength of the as-received BTi6431S alloy is rooted in the high dislocation 
density and collective dislocation behavior in the α phase. However, after annealing at 650 
◦
C, the alloy exhibits 
brittle fracture at room temperature. Using multiscale characterization methods, we attribute this phenomenon 
to the presence of brittle precipitates and the change of dislocation structures near the surface of BTi6431S ti -
tanium alloy. Silicides are generated in the vicinity of the surface region due to the relatively high dislocation 
density on the surface after the rolling process. The room temperature ductility can be restored by removing the 
near-surface area. When the alloy is tested at 700 
◦
C, most of the near-surface brittle silicides and dislocations 
can be eliminated, because the deformation is mainly affected by dislocation behavior and dynamic recrystal -
lization at high temperature. The findings in this study can advance the understanding of alloying effects on the 
mechanical behavior of high-temperature titanium alloy. The results suggest that inhibiting silicide formation 
and controlling the environmental temperature during service are potential approaches for maintaining the 
mechanical performance of this near- α titanium alloy. 
1. Introduction 
High-temperature titanium alloy is widely applied in aerospace for 
its lightweight [ 1 ], excellent corrosion resistance [ 2 ], high tensile 
strength [ 3 ], and low creep rate [ 4 ]. To satisfy the mechanical 
requirement at working temperature, many approaches have been pro -
posed, including strengthening by forming precipitates [ 5 ], refining the 
grain size [ 6 ], and inducing phase transitions [ 7 ]. Heat treatment pro -
cesses generally accompany these complicated modifications of the so -
lute solution, precipitation, and defects. The heat treatment effectively 
reduces internal stress, significantly promotes the evolution of disloca -
tion structures, and changes the concentration of precipitates. Although 
the microstructure after heat treatment should strongly influence the 
mechanical behavior, detailed experimental studies on this topic are 
sparse. 
BTi6431S (Ti-6.5Al – 3Sn – 3Zr – 3Nb – 3Mo – W-0.2Si), along with other 
near- α titanium alloys like IMI-834 [ 8 ], Ti-1100 [ 9 ], BT-36 [ 10 ], KIMS 
[ 11 ] and Ti600 [ 12 ], has extensive use in applications requiring resil -
ience to high temperatures ranging from 600 to 650 
◦
C, such as in jet 
engines and gas turbines [ 2 , 13 ]. However, these titanium alloys are 
subjected to sustained high temperatures in their operational environ -
ments and must endure cyclic temperature changes at room and high 
temperatures. This imposes stringent demands on their material prop -
erties. However, studies have indicated that when near- α titanium alloys 
operate after temperatures exceeding 600 
◦
C, their mechanical proper -
ties at room temperature experience a drastic reduction [ 11 , 14 ]. 
Consequently, titanium alloy components exposed to high-temperature 
conditions may exhibit rapid failure when returned to a 
room-temperature environment, limiting their practical service life. 
To delve into this phenomenon, researchers have conducted a series 
of experiments. For instance, Narayana et al. [ 11 ] observed a significant 
reduction in the elongation of a high-temperature titanium alloy, KIMS, 
to 4.07% after treatment at 650 
◦
C, indicating substantial property 
degradation at room temperatures. Jia et al. [ 14 ] observed a decrease in

Silicides play an important role in the mechanical properties of near- 
α titanium alloys, and heat treatment conditions influence the precipi -
tation behavior of silicides in near- α titanium alloys. As the temperature 
and duration of heat treatment increase, silicides precipitate in the 
α -phase and undergo coarsening [ 16 ]. The studies by Jia et al. [ 14 , 17 ] 
delved into the relationship between silicides and the interior micro -
structure of titanium alloys. They observed that silicides tend to accu -
mulate in regions with higher dislocation density after heat treatment of 
the forged Ti60 titanium alloy. This suggests a close relation between the 
precipitation of silicides and the microstructure, with defects such as 
dislocations potentially providing a favorable environment for silicide 
formation. Ankem et al. [ 18 ] focused on the precipitation behavior of 
silicides at grain boundaries, revealing that titanium alloys are prone to 
form silicides at grain boundaries during heat treatment. These grain 
boundary silicides adversely affect tensile ductility and may lead to 
intergranular fractures. Furthermore, the coarsening of silicides is an 
important factor in reducing the plasticity of titanium alloys [ 19 – 21 ]. 
Coarse silicides impair the thermal stability and plasticity of titanium 
alloys and may adversely affect fatigue properties. This phenomenon has 
been reported in many kinds of high-temperature titanium alloys 
[ 22 – 25 ]. 
Despite these findings, systematic studies on the dislocation behavior 
of high-temperature titanium alloys during thermal cycling or annealing 
remain sparse. A study by Qu et al. [ 26 ] has discussed the deformation 
mechanism controlled by dislocation climb/slip in the deformation 
behavior of high-temperature titanium alloys; however, the evolution of 
the dislocation structure during the deformation process was not 
involved. Research by Balasundar et al. [ 27 ] highlighted the effect of 
grain boundary pinning on dislocation motion, while micrographs to 
illustrate the morphology and structure of dislocations are absent. Lee 
and Chen [ 28 ] focused on the dislocation substructure in titanium al -
loys; however, how the dislocation substructure evolves during the 
deformation process remains unknown. 
However, BTi6431S is a representative near- α titanium alloy with 
high application potential. The ultimate tensile strength of Ti60 at room

reach 1.2 GPa, but the elongation is only 4% [ 30 ]. In contrast, the ul -

2.2. Material characterizations 
For surface microstructure characterization, a two-step sample pol -
ishing process was employed, commencing with mechanical polishing 
and followed by electrochemical polishing. The sample surface under -
went grinding using 180 to 2000 grit sandpaper, succeeded by me -
chanical polishing using a diamond abrasive paste with particle sizes 
ranging from 3 μ m to 0.25 μ m. Subsequently, electrochemical polishing 
occurred in a solution comprising 10 mL of sulfuric acid and 100 mL of 
methyl alcohol at 22 
◦
C and 30 V for 30 s. Microstructures and fractures 
were scrutinized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss 
Merlin) operated at a voltage of 15 kV and a current of 1 nA. Grain 
orientation and grain boundary misorientation angle were characterized 
through electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) on a field-emission SEM 
with a voltage of 20 kV and a current of 5 nA. The EBSD data were 
analyzed using OIM software (EDAX). 
For the investigation of the microstructure of the tensile specimen, 3 
mm disks were punched out at a region 1 mm away from the fracture 
surface. The central region of the 3 mm disk underwent thinning to 
electron transparency through twin-jet polishing (TenuPol-5, Struers), 
employing specified voltage and temperature conditions with an elec -
trolyte comprising 10% hydrochloric acid and 90% methanol. Micro -
scopic characterization of precipitates and dislocations was performed 
using a scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM, FEI Talos 
F200X) operated at 200 keV.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Room temperature tensile property 
The tensile properties of both as-received and annealed BTi6431S 
were investigated at room temperature. The microstructure of the as- 
received BTi6431S is predominated by large α grains and sparse β 
phases distributed along the α grain boundaries. In α grains, thick 
dislocation walls and dislocation tangles are formed by the 
manufacturing process, while β phases possess few dislocation struc -
tures. According to Taylor ’ s law [ 43 ], the strength of metals increases 
with an elevated dislocation density. In the case of the as-received 
BTi6431S, the impeding effect of existing dislocations contributes to 
the high room temperature strength observed in BTi6431S. After 
annealing at 700 
◦
C, dislocation walls and tangles are organized into 
small sub-grains. Although the sub-grains can exhibit a similar pinning 
effect to dislocations as dislocation walls and tangles, they have a 
smaller obstacle strength. Therefore, the yield strength was slightly 
reduced after annealing. 
The annealing process also causes an increase in low-angle grain 
boundaries and a decrease in high-angle grain boundaries, which is 
caused by the growth of grains. High-angle grain boundaries are asso -
ciated with greater misorientation between adjacent grains, and an in -
crease in their proportion is often linked to increased strength but 
decreased plasticity [ 44 ]. This is because high-angle grain boundaries 
can act as barriers to dislocation movement, hindering plastic defor -
mation and potentially leading to stress concentration at these bound -
aries. By contrast, generally, the low-angle grain boundaries should have 
resulted in less effect on strength and caused an increase in ductility 
because it is much easier for a dislocation to transmit through low-angle

A detailed analysis of the fracture morphology indicated that the 
portion of cleavage feature increased on the surface of the annealed 
sample and gradually transited into dimples near the interior. This dif -
ference in microstructure and fracture behavior between the surface and 
interior was confirmed by increased ductility after removing 150 μ m of 
the surface. Enrichment of Zr and Si at the grain boundaries on the 
surface, with the formation of silicide, was observed after annealing but 
not in the interior region. This enrichment at the surface is consistent 
with previous findings of silicide precipitation in the BTi6431S [ 15 ]. 
Recent studies also indicate that dislocations are initially generated in 
the initial stages of plastic deformation and become self-organized on 
the surface first [ 38 ]. In the annealed sample, dislocations were likely 
first generated on the surface near the silicide and grain boundaries. 
These silicides could act as a stress concentration point, and are rela -
tively more brittle than the Ti alloy matrix, which could assist the crack 
generation and interfacial decohesion [ 16 ], leading to the transition 
from ductile to brittle behavior. 
In titanium alloys (e.g. Ti60), it has been confirmed that silicides 
tend to precipitate at locations with higher dislocation density [ 14 , 17 ]. 
Many studies also showed that silicide precipitation can be promoted by 
introducing dislocations [ 16 ]. As the BTi6431S was received in the form 
of rolled plates, the rolling process can lead to higher dislocation density 
and finer grains near the material surface [ 46 – 49 ]. The EBSD results in 
Fig. 5 also prove that the grains on the surface are finer than those of the 
interior. Therefore, the high dislocation density and grain boundaries on 
the surface after rolling probably make it easy for silicide to precipitate 
on the surface. 
4.2. Tensile properties at 700 
◦
C 
When subjected to deformation at 700 
◦
C, the elongation of the Ti 
alloy increased to 130%, accompanied by the disappearance of cleavage 
fracturing morphology. Subsequently, the fracturing morphology 
transformed into large and deep dimples covered with thick oxide 
layers. This change is attributed to the thermal activation dislocation 
slip systems in the HCP α -phase. 
To understand the mechanical behaviors of BTi6431S under high- 
temperature tension, the evolution of dislocations is examined across 
different strain levels. At a low strain level of 8%, dislocation movement 
is assisted by thermal activation and is concentrated at the grain 
boundaries. Upon a plastic strain of 55%, dislocation movement is 
driven by thermal activation, annihilation, and movement. The 
competition of these processes eventually culminated in the piling up of 
dislocations to form sub-grain boundaries. With a higher strain of 76%, 
numerous mobile dislocations activate, initiating slip across sub-grain 
boundaries in the α phase. The basal slip should predominantly 
govern the planar slip bands across these boundaries. It has been re -
ported that the basal slip system tends to be operated at high tempera -
tures [ 50 ]. The variation in dislocation density with strain is associated 
with both the dislocations ’ storage and the dislocations ’ thermal re -
covery. Near- α titanium alloys with similar compositions might exhibit 
similar phenomena attributed to this dislocation structure. The dislo -
cation structures in Ti6242Si, as reported by Joseph et al. [ 51 , 52 ], are 
predominated by the feature of planar dislocation structure induced by 
cross-slip, which aligns well with our findings. Similarly, the dislocation 
grids observed in IMI834, as reported by Wang et al. [ 53 ], closely re -
sembles the characteristics revealed in our study. 
During high-temperature tensile experiments, silicide is no longer 
identified as the primary factor that modifies the deformation behavior. 
Recent studies have proposed that silicide at grain boundaries in tita -
nium alloys during elevated temperatures hinders oxidation, thereby 
improving tensile properties [ 54 ]. In this work, the mitigation of 
annealing-induced embrittlement should be mainly induced by the 
decomposition of surface precipitates and dislocation structure. Further 
study will be done to inhibit silicide formation by the design of 
composition and heat treatment processes. Although this study has 
identified the major contributor to the abnormal embrittlement phe -
nomenon by using a posteriori approach, the process for the fracture 
mode change remains unclear. The intrinsic reason for the 
annealing-induced embrittlement is the heterogeneous deformation 
process in different regions in the high-strength near- α titanium alloy. 
Crystal plasticity simulation and other multiscale computational ap -
proaches should provide insight into solving such problem [ 55 , 56 ], 
which is also one of our interests in the future study. 
5. Conclusion 
In this work, we found an annealing-induced embrittlement of high- 
temperature Ti alloy. Such phenomenon is attributed to the formation of 
surface silicide and dislocation structure change on the surface. Removal 
of surface region and heating to high temperature are practical ap -
proaches that can mitigate the transition of brittle facture mode. The 
essential conclusions are summed up as follows. 
(1) After annealing, despite the increase in grain size and a reduction 
in the portion of high-angle grain boundaries, the reduction in 
yield strength is marginal. This is attributed to the dislocation 
pinning effect from the annealing-induced sub-grain boundaries. 
(2) Silicides are observed on the surface post-annealing but are ab -
sent in the interior of the annealed samples. The ductility loss can 
be restored after removing the surface area with a thickness of 
about 150 μ m. This observation implies that the transition of 
fracture mode primarily roots in the silicides. The silicide at the 
grain boundary can act as a stress concentration point, assist the 
decohesion along interfaces, and lead to fractures in the early 
stages of plastic deformation. 
(3) When tensile tested at 700 
◦
C, the ductility of BTi6431S experi -
ences a substantial increase. This is mainly accompanied by the 
formation of sub-grains and a reduction in dislocation density, 
ultimately contributing to a decrease in high-temperature 
strength. Most importantly, silicide can no longer induce brit -
tleness, and the as-received and annealed BTi6431S exhibits 
ductile fracture characteristics.
