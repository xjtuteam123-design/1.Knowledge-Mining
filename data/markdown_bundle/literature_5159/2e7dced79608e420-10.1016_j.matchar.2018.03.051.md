Abstract 
Ni-based superalloys are extensively used in high temperature, high stress applications. The presence of 
coherent γʹ-Ni3Al precipitates provides the requisite elevated temperature strength and creep resistance 
in these alloys . Th erefore, quantification of the precipitate size distribution and volume fraction is 
essential for accurate model predictions and future alloy design. Current γʹ volume fraction 
characterization techniques suffer from a lack of precision and repeatability. Without a reproducible 
procedure, unacceptable variations and errors have plagued the past 6 decades of superalloys research 
and has inhibited the implementation of accurate precipitation models for these alloys. Through the use 
of high resolution, low kV , scanning electron microscopy and state -of-the-art image post -processing, a 
new, accurate γʹ characterization technique is presented. Using the new technique, secondary γʹ volume 
fractions in superalloy ME3 were calculated, and confirmed through phase extraction, thus opening the 
possibility for validation of presently used superalloy precipitation databases. 
Keywords: Superalloys, precipitate, volume fraction characterization, image processing 
1. Introduction 
Ni-base superalloys are an essential material for high temperature and high stress applications i.e.: the 
hot section of gas turbine engines. The primary source of their superior high temperature properties 
may be attributed to the presence of ordered Ni3Al γʹ precipitates coherently embedded in the face

## Page 3

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
centered cubic solid solution γ matrix [1–3]. Multiple studies have demonstrated the significant impact 
these precipitates have on a superalloy’s high temperature mechanical properties [4–6]. Likewise, 
further analysis has found that slight variations in the volume fractions (VFs) of secondary and the 
smaller tertiary precipitates, can have a significant impact on an alloys overall creep performance [7,8]. 
As a result, predicting the precipitate microstructure for varying chemistry and heat treatment steps is 
critical to predicting the overall properties of the alloy. As a result, the creation of multiple precipitation 
modeling packages and superalloy thermodynamic databases have been developed [9–11]. Despite the 
progress these models have achieved, they have yet to be adequately validated due to the difficulties 
associated with accurately characterizing the γʹ precipitates [12]. Presently, a universally accepted 
technique for measuring γʹ volume fractions does not exist within the superalloy community mainly due 
to the limitations of the various characterization methods. 
A frequently used method for measuring γʹ precipitate volume fractions is imaging γʹ-etched surfaces 
using a backscatter electron (BSE) detector in a scanning electron microscope (SEM) [12–15]. 
Unfortunately, significant variations in the etching and image segmentation techniques employed from 
study to study may result in differences in the measured volume fraction values [12,15,16]. Without an 
established standard procedure, this variation may lead to significantly different results as etching times, 
image segmentation steps, and SEM imaging parameters will all affect the final volume fraction results. 
Moreover, stereological considerations from BSE contrast arising from sub-surface precipitates and 
voids from the etchant are usually not considered [12]. Sudbrack et al. [16] did take into account these 
complicated stereological concerns but did not present an automatic segmentation process that could 
accurately calculate volume fractions in different SEM images and instead used manual traces to 
segment the precipitates. Though this manual process can provide accurate measurements, it is time 
consuming and doesn’t take into account measurement errors associated with varying etch times and 
etchant chemistry. Higher resolution analysis through transmission electron microscopy (TEM) has also

been performed with varying success [14,17]. Although TEM provides higher resolution images, thus 
mitigating errors associated with measuring smaller tertiary γʹ precipitates, other problems can arise. 
TEM sample preparation is a resource and time intensive process and many research institutions will not 
be able to perform the analysis. Furthermore, the overall sample size is small and may not provide a 
statistically valid representation of the overall precipitate microstructure, especially for Focused Ion 
Beam (FIB) produced TEM foils and needles [18]. This problem becomes even more evident with volume 
fraction measurements through atom probe or 3-D microstructural tomography [19]. Another time and 
resource intensive precipitate volume fraction technique is a combination of wt.% measurements 
through chemical phase extraction and inductively coupled plasma (ICP) mass spectroscopy of the two 
phases (γ, γʹ) [17,20]. Phase extraction can also suffer from measurement errors. For example, the grid 
paper used to capture the chemically extracted precipitates may be unable to retain the nano-scale 
tertiary γʹ precipitates, under-estimating the overall volume fraction [20]. Additionally, the lack of visual 
confirmation using this technique makes mitigating these measurement errors difficult. 
Herein is described a new characterization technique that accurately measures the overall γʹ volume 
fraction of super-solvus superalloys (i.e. no primary γʹ precipitates), using advanced, high resolution SEM 
and state-of-the-art image processing. Furthermore, the analysis minimizes the errors associated with 
etching, segmentation, stereological concerns, and contrast from sub-surface features. The accurately 
measured volume fraction values are further validated through chemical phase extraction combined 
with ICP mass spectroscopy measurements.

2.3 SEM and Phase Extraction Analysis 
The high resolution SEM volume fraction analysis was performed on a Carl Zeiss Auriga Crossbeam SEM-
FIB workstation utilizing an in-chamber Everhart-Thornley (ET) secondary electron (SE) detector with low 
accelerating voltage (3kV) at a 5mm working distance. The low accelerating voltage minimizes the fringe 
contrast around the slightly etched γʹ precipitates and ensures that minimal sub-surface or Z contrast is 
acquired. The Zeiss Auriga employs a compucentric stage that allows users to rotate a sample 180 
degrees without changing the overall sample position under the beam as well as 360 degree scan 
rotation capabilities. SEM images were taken with a pixel resolution of 2048x1382. For γʹ phase 
extraction, a solution of 1g ammonium sulfate-1g citric acid -100 mL H2O electrolyte was used. The 
extracted precipitates were captured using a .1μm mesh. In order to accurately convert the measured 
wt.% of extracted γʹ precipitates to volume fraction, inductively coupled plasma (ICP) mass spectroscopy 
measurements of the separated γʹ and γ phases were compared to ICP measurements of ME3 before 
the extraction.

Inherent to SE imaging in the SEM is the ‘shadowing effect’ caused by the position of the in-chamber ET 
detector which can be seen on the left edge of the precipitates. Because the ET detector is more likely to 
collect emitted secondary electrons that have a clear line of sight to the detector, any topological 
feature that faces away from the detector (especially when there is no direct line-of sight to the 
detector) will appear to be in shadow in the ET detector’s reconstructed SEM image (as noted in the 
schematics shown in Figure 1). Conversely, the right side of the precipitates appears bright due to a high 
amount of electrons reaching the detector from that area and the ‘edge effect’ where more secondary 
electrons tend to be emitted from corners [21,22]. Both effects can cause problems in post processing 
the image to segment the desired morphology; γ’ precipitates from the γ matrix in Ni base superalloys in 
this instance. To mitigate this problem two images were acquired of the same location, each taken at a

Step 4: Calculate a shear angle by comparing the topmost and bottommost x offsets and shear the 
moving image by the calculated shear angle.

the moving image.

3.2 Image Processing 
Using MIPAR Image Analysis Software [23], a recipe (see data availability ) was developed to 
automatically segment the secondary and tertiary γʹ precipitates from the γ matrix using the high 
resolution SEM images after the rotation averaging step as shown in Figure 2(a) . The recipe comprised 
of 34 individual image processing operations . To simplify the sequence, the operations were grouped 
into 9 processing steps , where each step focused on a particular milestone . The steps, and their 
respective operations, are listed below and the results during certain steps are revealed in Figure 2. 
 
Figure 2: (a) The averaged image of the two rotated SEM images. The SEM image after (b) step 2, (c) step 4, (d) 
step 6, (e) Step (7) and (f) step 9, where both the secondary (green) and tertiary (red) γʹ precipitates have been 
fully segmented from the γ matrix. 
 
Step 1 - Reduce Noise: A Wiener filter with a kernel of 5x5 pixels was used to reduce sparse detector 
noise in the image, while retaining important feature edges . The 5x5 kernel size will adequately suite

detector noise across a range of image pixel densities and will have negligible effect on feature edges as 
long as those features have a reasonable pixel density (above five pixels). 
Step 2 - Find Background: Canny-based edge detection, as shown in Figure 2(b), was used to select 
everything except most secon dary particle edges. Its strength parameter was set to maximize the 
number of identified edges, until a higher strength would add undesired artifacts. Unselected edges 
were eroded to join disconnections. The erosion strength was chosen to ensure all discon nections were 
comfortably joined. Both the edge detection and erosion strengths will need adjustment for other 
microstructures, but as shown in section 3.3, will perform consistently without adjustment within a 
given microstructure. The largest remaining f eature in the image, which represented the background γ 
matrix, was then retained for subsequent removal. 
Step 3 - Select Secondary Particles: The selected background was removed from the eroded edge image, 
leaving only secondary γʹ particles and artifacts. These were then re -dilated to account for the initial 
edge erosion. 
Step 4 - Remove Artifacts: Using k -means clustering, the Wiener -filtered image was clustered into 3 
classes: light, medium, and dark as shown in Figure 2(c) , based solely on pixel intensity . The light class 
was selected and removed from the secondary γʹ selection, since these pixels were deemed to represent 
excited volumes of the γ matrix immediately outside of the γʹ particle boundaries. Remaining artifacts 
which exhibited high roughness (i.e. strong perimeter concavity) were then removed. 
Step 5 - Remove Holes: Intra-particle holes were removed and the particles were then over -dilated to 
allow for subsequent auto boundary determination. 
Step 6 - Optimize Secondary Boundaries: Using MIPAR’s optimization engine [23] the particles were 
eroded until a maximum “Selection Match” between the particles and Wiener -filtered image was

4. Discussion 
4.1 Etching and Segmentation Errors 
The use of a low kV accelerating voltage and the ET detector, combined with the elimination of an 
etching step were all chosen to alleviate many of the errors associated with previous area fraction 
measurement techniques [12,24]. As previously discussed, a common SEM technique used in the past to 
estimate the volume fraction of γʹ precipitates was to image a γʹ-etched surface using a BSE detector as 
this gave a contrast that could be segmented using image processing tools. Still, limitations existed with 
this imaging technique as bias from stereological, segmentation, and etching variations could all skew 
the measurements from their actual values. With these biases present, a universal volume fraction 
measurement technique could not be produced among research groups and variations from different 
studies were inevitable. One concern with etched samples when imaged with a BSE detector is how to 
quantifiably segment the resulting image. The difficulty of this technique is highlighted below in Figure 
4.

4.2 Stereology Considerations 
For the assumption that area fraction measurements are an accurate representation of the actual 
volume fraction in the specimen, the SEM images analyzed need to be actual two dimensional 
representations of the sample volume [28,29]. This means that only contrast from the surface should be 
incorporated into the volume fraction measurements. Section 4.1 already revealed that this is a problem 
when using a BSE detector. If sub-surface precipitates are included in the image, then a stereological 
correction would need to be applied [29]. To explore the interaction volumes associated with 15 kV 
electrons used for the back scatter SEM images and 3kV electrons used for the secondary electron 
images, the Monte Carlo simulation program, CASINO, was employed using the composition of ME3 
given in Table 1. [30]. The results are shown below in Figure 8.

A simulation of 1 million electrons was performed and the depth that escaped backscatter electrons 
reached, i.e. the electrons that would be used to create a SEM image, was recorded. Using the 
composition of the ME3 alloy for the simulation, it was found that when using a 15kV accelerating 
voltage, the interactive depth of a BSEs reaches up to 400 nm (blue line). This supports the findings 
made in section 4.1 where subsurface features and particles were observed in the BSE images. When 
the accelerating voltage was reduced to 3kV, the interactive depth of BSEs was reduced by an order of 
magnitude, to around 40nm. At the moment, CASINO cannot simulate the penetration depth for SEs, 
still it has been well established that SEs penetrate a sample an order of magnitude less than BSEs 
[31,32]. Indeed, multiple studies have calculated the mean escape depth for secondary electrons in a Ni 
sample to be only around 1-2nm [21,33–35]. Furthermore, the contrast displayed by an ET detector is 
dominated by surface topological features and not from Z-contrast [31]. This implies that even if a 
subsurface particle or feature was imaged it would not display a significant contrast variation from its 
surroundings and would not be segmented with the γʹ precipitates. Therefore, the foundational 
stereological relationship where area fraction equals volume fraction is not violated by the technique 
outlined in this study [36].

5. Conclusions 
This study presented a new method for determining the volume fraction of secondary and tertiary γʹ 
precipitates in Ni-base superalloys using low kV secondary electron SEM imaging and image processing. 
The new technique was found to be accurate and eliminated many of the errors and assumptions that 
have plagued previous γʹ volume fraction measurements in the past. From this work the following 
conclusions can be drawn. 
(i) The new volume fraction technique eliminates the common biases associated with using 
BSE detectors to image etched surfaces. 
(ii) Monte Carlo simulations confirm that the use of ET detectors, combined with a low kV 
accelerating voltage produce a SEM image that is a true 2-dimensional representation of the 
3-dimensional microstructure. 
(iii) The contrast averaging and the MIPAR image processing recipe can accurately segment the 
slightly etched γʹ phase from the γ matrix for a multitude of precipitate shapes and sizes. 
(iv) Phase extraction combined with ICP mass spectroscopy have confirmed the volume fraction 
results obtained using the new SEM+MIPAR technique. 
In combination, the new volume fraction characterization technique described throughout the study will 
improve precipitate model validation and eliminates uncontrolled volume fraction measurement 
differences between research groups.
