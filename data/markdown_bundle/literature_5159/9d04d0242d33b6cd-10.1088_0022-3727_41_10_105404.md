Abstract
We demonstrate a powder metallurgy synthesis route for porous Ti–6Al–4V structures by
consolidation of Ti–6Al–4V alloy powders and describe the inﬂuence of the processing
conditions. Ti–6Al–4V foams with 50 vol% porosity having high strength were fabricated
by low temperature solid state deformation at temperatures below 873 K. The open cellular
structure consists of continuously connected Ti–6Al–4V struts and homogeneously distributed
pores with nominal diameters between 10 and 50 µm and 150–500 µm length.
(Some ﬁgures in this article are in colour only in the electronic version)
1. Introduction
Titanium and Ti-based alloys, particularly Ti–6Al–4V , are
the most widely used metallic materials for biomedical
applications, such as for dental and medical implants due to
their reliable mechanical properties, superior biocompatibility
and good corrosion resistance [ 1, 2]. However, solid metallic
implant materials are difﬁcult to bond directly to living bone
as a consequence of their limited surface conditions [ 3].
Thus, surface modiﬁcation by chemical coating processes with
scaffold materials, such as hydroxyapatite (HA), on the surface
is typically required [4]. Also the fabrication of porous metallic
foams implant materials has been investigated to suggest
an interconnected porous structure to provide the necessary
space for cell ingrowth and vascularization [ 5, 6]. Therefore,
acceleration of the HA precipitation can be attained when
porous metals with rough surfaces are used because their open
cellular structure permits the ingrowth of the bone tissue and
the transport of the body ﬂuid combined with a large surface
area [3–7].
Ti–6Al–4V is difﬁcult to deform due to its limited
ductility, which requires very high loads for processing [ 8].
Fabrication processes for Ti–6Al–4V alloy foams are based on
powder metallurgy and can be classiﬁed into powder sintering
or deposition methods and pressurized pore expansion
processes [ 9]. The main limitation of the powder sintering
processes is that the pore size and shape are dictated by the size
and shape of the initial starting Ti alloy powder [ 9]. Also the
weak bonding between the powders due to their large size with
increasing volume fraction of porosity results in relatively low
compression strength [ 10]. Pressurized expansion processes
are more favourable for foaming as they exhibit pores
with shape, size, connectivity and volume fraction that are
independent of those in the original powder [11]. However, the
critical limitation of the pressurized expansion processes is that
it is a complicated processing route, requiring high-pressure
and high-temperature equipment together with multiple heat
treatment steps [ 9].
Recently, a new solid state fabrication method was
reported for fabricating porous metallic glass foams with
uniformly dispersed pores by employing sequential steps of
mechanical milling, warm extrusion and dissolution of the
fugitive phase (i.e. a second phase that is removed as a
ﬁnal processing step) [ 12, 13]. The processing variability

were fabricated by warm extrusion of Ti–6Al–4V powders
blended with a fugitive Cu powder.
2. Experiments
To produce the Ti–6Al–4V precursor containing 50 vol%
of a fugitive Cu phase, the 90Ti–6Al–4V (wt%) powders
with a particle size of <44 µm (Reade Advanced Materials,
USA) were blended with Cu powders of sizes between 20
and 44 µm. Cylindrical precursor rods 12 mm in diameter
and 30 mm in length were produced by warm extrusion of
the blended powders at two different temperatures, 803 K
(foam A) and 873 K (foam B). All powder handling steps were
carried out in a glove box under a puriﬁed Ar atmosphere
(less than 3 ppm O
2 and H 2O). Porous Ti–6Al–4V foams
were obtained through dissolution of the Cu phase in the
extruded precursor by immersion into a 50 : 50 concentrated-
HNO
3 :H 2O solution for 48 h at room temperature (the details
of the synthesis conditions have been reported elsewhere)
[12, 13]. Microstructural characterization was performed
using a Rigaku DMAX 2500 x-ray diffractometer with
monochromatic Cu K
α radiation from a 3.5 kW rotating anode
source and a JEOL 5910L V scanning electron microscope
(SEM) for microstructural characterization. The compression
test specimens have a length ( l) of 6 mm and width ( w)o f
3 mm, with a w/l ratio of 0.5. Uniaxial compression tests
were conducted on an Instron Model 8563 at room temperature
at a constant cross head speed and an initial strain rate of
1×10
−3 s−1. Hardness measurements were done with a Wilson
Tukon micro-Vickers hardness tester calibrated according to
ASTM speciﬁcations using a 100 g load and an indent duration
time of 10 s. The density of the foam calculated from the
volume fraction and the density of the precursor sample was
determined by the Archimedes method in water using a Mettler
Toledo-A T261 Delta-Range density measurement system. The
oxygen content of starting powders and foams was measured
by inert-gas fusion and was found to be around 2.9 wt%.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) data, ﬁgure 2, obtained from

transformation during the process because the employed
warm extrusion temperature below 873 K is a relatively low
temperature for inducing α to β transition [ 8]. As shown
in ﬁgure 2, the starting Ti–6Al–4V alloy powders exhibit
sharp crystalline diffraction peaks coming from the α Ti-rich
majority phase and the x-ray diffraction results obtained
from the Ti–6Al–4V foams also show sharp crystalline peaks
corresponding to an α Ti-rich phase. A peak broadening of the
x-ray diffraction peaks (see inset of ﬁgure 2(a)) was observed
for the Ti–6Al–4V foam when the processing temperature
increases from 803 to 873 K. In contrast to pure Ti where
twinning is occasionally observed upon quasi-static loading
at ambient temperature, mechanical twinning in Ti–6Al–4V is
difﬁcult to observe, except when the strain rate is very high, for
example in shock loading [15]. The Ti–6Al–4V alloy deforms
on basal slip systems at low temperature instead of prismatic
or pyramidal slip systems [ 8]. Therefore, the x-ray diffraction
peak broadening is related to the change in the slip system
from single, basal-plane slip to multiple slip on prismatic and
pyramidal planes [16].
The strength of the porous type B foam was assessed
from room-temperature, uniaxial compression tests. A typical
stress–strain curve is shown in ﬁgure2(b); the loading direction
is parallel to the extrusion direction. The foam exhibits
an average maximum strength of 257 MPa with 82% strain.
The strength was veriﬁed from the converted hardness values
obtained by the indentation experiments [ 17]. In the case
of porous metals, the deformation under the indenter is not
constrained by the surrounding material due to densiﬁcation.
Thus, the indentation test acts like a uniaxial compression
test, and the maximum strength is simply equal to the
hardness [18]. From this suggestion, the maximum strengths
converted from the micro-Vickers hardness measurements
of the porous Ti–6Al–4V foams, which were deformed at
different processing temperatures, are presented in table1. The
maximum strength of the Ti–6Al–4V type B foam fabricated
by warm extrusion at 873 K is 232 MPa. This value is close to
the maximum strength obtained from the uniaixal compression
test and 5 times higher than that of the Ti–6Al–4V type A foam
processed at 803 K.
The mechanical properties of the porous metals agree well
with what is predicted by the Gibson and Ashby model [ 19].
According to this model, the yield strength of an open cell
foam, σ
f , should be related to the relative density given by
equation (1)
σf/σs = C(ρf/ρs)n, (1)
where σs and ρs are the yield strength and the density of the
solid material and ρf is the density of the foam, respectively.
The constant C describes the geometry of cellular metals and
the density exponentn depends on the deformation mechanism
of the cell. The experimental data indicate that C is around
0.3 and the strength of the foam is proportional to the strength
of the solid ( n = 3/2) [ 18]. The estimated yield strength
of the Ti–6Al–4V foam processed at 873 K is 95 MPa, as
calculated according to equation ( 1) using a measured density
of 2.20 g cm
−3 for the foam, and the reported yield strength and
density of dense solid Ti–6Al–4V (902 MPa and 4.43 g cm −3,
respectively) [ 20]. Even though the actual measured yield
strengths of the Ti–6Al–4V foams given in table 1 are much
lower compared with the calculated yield strength based on
the density of the Ti–6Al–4V foam, the maximum strength
of Ti–6Al–4V foam processed at 873 K increases up to
257 MPa combined with large plasticity. Such an enhancement
of mechanical properties together with superplasticity by

demonstrated by Sergueeva et al [21]. Therefore, the increase

The SEM image in ﬁgure 3(a) obtained from the surface
of Ti–6Al–4V type A foam ligaments that were deformed at
803 K shows well developed slip lines, which are aligned along
the slip plane direction. Due to the low volume fraction of the
body centred cubic (bcc)β phase (<10 vol%), the deformation
behaviour of the Ti–6Al–4V alloy is dominated by theα phase,
which has a hexagonal closed packed (hcp) structure with
a limited number of slip systems. Therefore, the majority
of plastic strain is accommodated by the hcp α phase [ 8].
Also, as shown in ﬁgure 3(b), shear bands induced from shear
localization are observed on the surface of the Ti–6Al–4V
foam. Semiatin et al reported shear localization during non-
isothermal equal channel angular extrusion (ECAE) when the
Ti–6Al–4V alloy cannot deform either by slip or twinning due
to geometrical constraints [ 23]. The probability of the effect
of shear deformation increases as the deformation temperature
decreases [ 24]. The shape of the slip lines on the surface
of the Ti–6Al–4V foams fabricated at 873 K changes from
straight lines aligned following the slip plane to wavy shape
lines (see ﬁgure 3(c)) because of the transition of the slip
systems for the deformation of the α phase from single basal
slip to multiple slip on prismatic and pyramidal planes. Similar
microstructural features were also observed in Mg-based
nanocomposites [25].

4. Conclusions
1. High strength Ti–6Al–4V foam was fabricated using
solid state powder metallurgy at controlled temperatures
followed by chemical removal of a fugitive phase to yield
uniformly distributed micropores.
