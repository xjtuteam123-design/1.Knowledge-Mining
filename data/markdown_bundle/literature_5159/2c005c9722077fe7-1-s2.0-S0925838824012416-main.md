ABSTRACT 
Oxygen-rich ZrTiAlV alloy reinforced by spherulite ZrO phase was prepared by in-situ semi-solid processing. The 
results of high temperature (500
◦
C) mechanical properties test confirmed that alloy elongation after semi-solid 
processing at 1500
◦
C was close to that for the as-forged material, whereas yield and tensile strength were 
increased by 66.4% and 30.8%, respectively. SEM and EBSD results confirm that morphology selection of non- 
equilibrium growth is mainly controlled by the initial core shape, with dendrites always growing along the 
< 110 > direction following the Twin-Plane-Reentrant-Edge mechanism. Improved alloy strength is attributed to 
fine grain strengthening and precipitation strengthening caused by the ZrO phase, and α phase spheroidization 
ensures alloy plasticity. We also propose a crack deflection toughening mechanism for the alloy. 
1. Introduction 
Zirconium (Zr) and its alloys offer advantages such as low thermal 
neutron absorption and excellent corrosion resistance, making them 
candidates for aerospace applications in extreme environments [1 – 3] . 
However, the relatively low strength (approximately 800 MPa) of con -
ventional Zr alloys does not meet the mechanical performance re -
quirements for structural applications. In this context, a new room 
temperature high strength alloy with the nominal composition of 
Zr – 45Ti – 5Al – 3 V(wt%, 47Zr) have been developed by Liang et al. [4] , 
and on this basis developed oxygen-rich 47Zr with high 
room-temperature strength and good oxidation resistance. However, 
compared with elevated temperature Ti alloys for aerospace applica -
tions, the elevated temperature strength or plasticity of oxygen-rich 
47Zr is still low [5] . 
In Zr/Ti alloys, hexagonal α phase has better strength because it has 
fewer slip systems than body-centered cubic β phase [6] . The mechan -
ical properties of Zr/Ti alloys are mainly determined by the micro -
structure features such as the morphology, size, distribution, and 
volume fraction of the α precipitate phase in the β phase [7] . However, 
the microstructure of Zr/Ti alloy is greatly affected by temperature, and 
the high temperature strength of the alloy cannot be well improved by 
controlling the microstructure only. Therefore, it is necessary to intro -
duce strengthening phases that can exist stably at high temperatures. 
Dispersion strengthening is often used as an effective means to 
enhance the mechanical properties of Zr/Ti alloys [8 – 10] . There are 
many kind of dispersoids, i.e. whiskers [11,12] , intermetallic com -
pounds [13,14] or ceramics [15,16] . In general, in-situ formed hard and 
fine dispersoids are effective for improving the mechanical responses of 
the composite materials, compared to those produced via traditional 
mixing processes. Ti

4.2. Strengthening and toughening mechanism 
The alloy tensile curves confirm the alloy has good strength and 
plasticity after SSP at 1450
◦
C and 1500
◦
C, i.e., under spherulite ZrO 
phase morphology. Therefore, the discussion of the strengthening and 
toughening mechanism mainly focuses on the spherulite ZrO, as shown 
in Fig. 21 . 
Fine grain strengthening and precipitation strengthening are the 
fundamental reasons for increased alloy strength. From Figs. 4 and 8 , it 
can be seen that the more ZrO content, the smaller the grain size of the 
alloy. This is because the pinning of ZrO at the grain boundary inhibits 
the grain boundary migration. Secondly, from Fig. 22 , it can be seen that 
ZrO is a hard phase, which can hinder the movement of dislocations in 
the alloy and play a hardening effect. 
The spheroidization of α phase ensures the plasticity of the alloy. 
When ZrO spherulites are uniformly distributed on the matrix, the two 
needle-like α phase tips are hindered by ZrO phase particles, which can 
only grow along the thickness direction and gradually evolve into a short 
rod-like (spheroidized) α phase, ensuring alloy plasticity. The advantage 
of spherulites over dendrites is that spherulites can hinder the growth of 
needle-like α phase in multiple directions, while the hindering range of 
two-dimensional planar dendrites is limited, so the spheroidization ef -
fect of α phase at 1550
◦
C and 1600 
◦
C is not ideal. This is part of the 
reason for its poor plasticity. The main reason is related to the planar 
dendrite morphology, which will be discussed in Section 4.3 .

4.3. Fracture mechanism 
Inclusions are inevitable in the steel smelting process, and hence 
controlling inclusion content and morphology has become a major focus 
for industrial processing [35] . Similarly, ZrO morphology has important 
influences on 47Zr-O alloy mechanical properties. 
As shown in Figs. 5, 14 (c, d) and 23 (a), the spherulites are 
composed of several ZrO particles, and the propagation of cracks in them 
can be divided into two cases: transgranular and intergranular. In terms 
of transgranular, the hardness of ZrO is much higher than that of the 
matrix, and the energy required for the crack to pass through the ZrO is

5. Conclusions 
This work proposed preparing 47Zr-O alloy with ZrO phase by in-situ 
semi-solid processing, and investigated the resultant alloy microstruc -
ture, mechanical properties, growth law for ZrO, strengthening and 
fracture mechanisms. The main conclusions can be summarized as 
follows: 
1) The alloy consists of α , α ", β , and ZrO phases, where α and α " phases 
have (0 – 1 1 – 1) α //(0 0 2) α ", [2 – 1 1 0] α //[-4 2 0] α " orientation 
relationship. The ZrO phase was divided into two categories, with 
ZrO phase for SSP treated at 1450
◦
C and 1500
◦
C producing spher -
ulites, and at 1550
◦
C and 1600
◦
C producing dendrites. Alloy grains 
were greatly refined compared with the as-forged material, with the 
smallest grain size achieved (82 μ m) for 1500
◦
C. 
2) Spherulite ZrO provides the 47Zr-O alloy with good strength and 
plasticity. Alloy elongation at 1500
◦
C was close to the as-forged 
material, whereas YS and UTS increased by 66.4% and 30.8%, 
respectively. 
3) Morphological selection of non-equilibrium growth was mainly 
controlled by the initial core shape. Spherulite formation was 
attributed to multi-faceted nucleation sites provided by the poly -
hedral core, and the dendrite growth process was similar to that for 
2D nuclei. Dendrite growth was always along the < 110 > direction 
following the Twin-Plane-Reentrant-Edge mechanism. 
4) Improved alloy strength was attributed to fine grain strengthening 
and precipitation strengthening caused by the ZrO phase, and α 
phase spheroidization ensured alloy plasticity. Cracks produced by 
thermal deformation will be deflected many times when they en -
counters spherulites, consuming crack propagation energy and hence 
improving alloy fracture toughness.
