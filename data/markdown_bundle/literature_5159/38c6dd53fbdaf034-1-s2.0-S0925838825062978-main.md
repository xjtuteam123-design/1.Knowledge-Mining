1. Introduction
Alloys such as Inconel 718 and Inconel 625 are high-performance 
materials suitable for wide-temperature-range applications, demon -
strating excellent strength, ductility, toughness, and oxidation resistance 
from liquid nitrogen temperatures up to 800
◦
C [1 – 3] . Although these 
alloys offer well-balanced comprehensive properties, their specific 
strength at low to medium temperatures is not particularly outstanding, 
largely due to the high density and substantial proportion of nickel. 
Moreover, nickel-based superalloys exhibit poor additive manufactur -
ability, making it challenging to fabricate complex components from 
these materials using additive manufacturing techniques. The high 
nickel content also contributes to significantly elevated material costs. 
Therefore, developing new wide-temperature-range alloys with higher 
specific strength, improved additive manufacturability, and lower cost 
holds considerable importance for replacing nickel-based superalloys in 
a limited subset of applications.
The emergence of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) provides a pathway for 
developing new wide-temperature-range alloys. Common single-phase 
FCC structure HEAs, such as CoCrNi [4,5] , CoCrFeMnNi [6 – 8] , and 
CrFeCoNi [9] , exhibit excellent strength and ductility at both liquid 
nitrogen temperature and room temperature, making them suitable for 
low-temperature and room temperature applications. However, the 
strength of these alloys often decreases severely with increasing tem -
perature, and this high-temperature softening limits their 
high-temperature application. When Al element is added to CrFeCoNi 
alloys, BCC superlattice phases such as B2 form, which significantly 
increases the high-temperature strength while reducing the alloy density 
[10] . Utilizing this strategy shows promise for preparing new 
wide-temperature-range alloys that are lightweight, possess high spe -
cific strength, and high ductility.
Numerous researchers have studied AlCrFeCoNi system HEAs

2. Experiment
2.1. Sample preparation
Laser powder deposition was conducted in an argon-protected 
chamber. The light source is a YLS-4000 fiber laser with a wavelength 
of 1064 nm, and the processing head was a YC52. The laser power was 
set to 1.7 kW, the laser spot diameter was 2.8 mm, and the scanning 
speed was 700 mm/min. The oxygen content in the argon protection 
chamber was maintained below 100 ppm, and the water content was 
controlled below 150 ppm. The deposition strategy is shown in Fig. 1 , 
with an overlap rate of 0.5 and a layer thickness of approximately 
0.7 mm. The substrate was FV520B stainless steel. The powder has a 
composition of Al
0.85
CrFeCoNi
1.5 
(atomic ratio), produced by argon 
atomization. It exhibits a sphericity of ≥ 96 %, a particle size range of 
53 – 150 μ m, an apparent density of 3.46 g/cm³, and an oxygen content 
below 250 ppm. Finally, two bulk samples with the same size of 75 mm 
× 43 mm × 25 mm were obtained. Fig. S1 presents a macroscopic 
photograph of the prepared samples.
2.2. Microstructure characterization
Optical microscopy (OM) was used for low-magnification metallo -
graphic observation. A JEOL JSM-7001F scanning electron microscope 
(SEM) was employed for secondary electron imaging and electron 
backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis. The EBSD analysis was carried 
out with a scanning step size of 0.1 μ m. The specimen was first me -
chanically polished, followed by vibratory polishing with a silica 
colloidal suspension. Finally, to remove the surface stress-strain layer, 
the specimen was electrophished at room temperature in a 10 % CrO ₃ 
solution for 2 – 6 s. Samples for OM and secondary electron imaging were 
mechanically ground and polished, then etched with 10 vol% aqua regia 
for 5 – 10 s before observation. Tensile fracture surfaces were ultrasoni -
cally cleaned in anhydrous alcohol prior to observation.
A Titan G2 60 – 300 transmission electron microscope (TEM) was 
used to characterize the morphology, structure, and chemical compo -
sition of different phases. TEM foils were ground to a thickness of ~100 
μ m using sandpaper and then thinned by twin-jet electro-polishing using 
a solution of 15 % perchloric acid in 85 % ethanol at 20
◦
C and 25 V.
XRD analysis was performed using an X ′ Pert PRO with a copper 
target at a scanning speed of 2 
◦
/min. The XRD sample was mechanically 
ground and polished, followed by vibratory polishing with silica sol, but 
without etching.

As discussed in Section 3.1 , the prepared Al

a eutectic structure. Fig. 9 shows the phase distribution, inverse pole 
figures (IPF) for the FCC and BCC ordered superlattice phases, and the 
interface misorientation angle distribution map of the alloy, obtained 
using EBSD technology. As shown in Fig. 9 a, both phases are fine-scaled, 
on the order of sub-micrometers, with a small number of micron-sized 
FCC phases present. The volume fraction of the FCC phase is 52 %, 
while that of the BCC ordered superlattice phase is 48 %. Combining 
Fig. 9 b and c, the orientation of the BCC ordered superlattice phase is 
relatively consistent, while the FCC phase, within regions of consistent 
BCC phase orientation, exhibits different orientations. This indicates 
that during the eutectic reaction, the BCC ordered superlattice phase is 
the leading phase. This phase forms a three-dimensional interconnected 
network, and no large-sized BCC ordered superlattice phases are pre -
sent. These characteristics are key reasons why the alloy maintains good 
strength and plasticity despite a high volume fraction of the brittle BCC 
ordered superlattice phase. The leading BCC ordered superlattice phase

This orientation relationship between the two phases is the second 
important reason for the alloy ’ s good strength and plasticity. This 
relationship reduces the interfacial energy and endows the interface 
with good bonding strength and stability.
Figs. 4S and 5S present the TEM bright-field images and EDS 
elemental mapping data of samples subjected to the same thermal his -
tory as the tensile specimen tested at 500
◦
C. Different regions of a laser 
powder deposited component undergo distinct thermal histories, which 
readily lead to varied microstructural characteristics. Consequently, 
conventional laser powder deposited alloys often exhibit poor consis -
tency in mechanical properties. As shown in Figs. 4S and 5S, the 
microstructural features and the chemical element concentrations 
within each phase are similar to those of the as-deposited sample, with 
no significant differences. This can likely be attributed to the fact that 
the prepared Al
0.85
CrFeCoNi
1.5 
alloy possesses a eutectic structure with 
fine-scale phases. Temperature variations primarily induce changes in 
the chemical composition of the two phases and migration of the phase 
boundaries. Thus, the Al
0.85
CrFeCoNi
1.5 
alloy prepared in this study 
demonstrates good consistency in mechanical properties across different 
locations. Furthermore, the influence of temperature on the mechanical 
properties of the alloy is mainly reflected in its effect on the intrinsic 
mechanical properties of the different constituent phases. At low tem -
peratures, the strength of the FCC phase increases while it retains 
considerable ductility; however, the BCC ordered superlattice phase 
undergoes further embrittlement. This results in an overall increase in 
the alloy ’ s strength but a reduction in its elongation. At elevated tem -
peratures ranging from 300
◦
C to 700
◦
C, although the temperature 
dependence of the FCC phase ’ s strength is weaker than that of the BCC 
phase, the inherent strength of the FCC phase is not particularly high. 
Moreover, in the alloy prepared in this study, no coherent strengthening 
phases are present within the FCC phase, leading to significant softening 
of the FCC phase. This results in an increased strength disparity between 
the two phases and a notable degradation in both the strength and 
ductility of the alloy. As illustrated in Fig. 3S, the alloy exhibits distinct 
intergranular fracture characteristics at 700

In the prepared Al
0.85
CrFeCoNi
1.5 
alloy, the BCC ordered superlattice 
phase forms a three-dimensional network-like brittle structure, 
restricting dislocation motion predominantly to the FCC phase. Based on 
Fig. 3 c and Fig. S4 , the thickness of the FCC phase is on the order of 
300 nm, while that of the BCC ordered superlattice phase is approxi -
mately 150 nm. According to the work of Li et al [24] ., in eutectic alloys 
with alternating soft and hard phases and phase dimensions below the 
micrometer scale, the contribution of back-stress strengthening induced 
by high-density hetero-interfaces can reach approximately 40 %. Simi -
larly, structural refinement contributes comparably, also accounting for 
about 40 % of the overall strength. These two mechanisms are the pri -
mary contributors to the high strength of the alloy. It should be noted 
that, although numerous studies have discussed the strengthening 
mechanisms in eutectic alloys, achieving highly accurate quantitative 
analysis remains challenging.
