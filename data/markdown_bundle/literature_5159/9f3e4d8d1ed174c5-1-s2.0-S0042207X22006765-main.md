ABSTRACT 
Tantalum-based alloys have developed rapidly in the fields of aerospace, nuclear industry, weapons, and national 
defense heavy industries due to their high melting point, low ductility-brittle transition temperature, and well 
ductility. However, pure tantalum exhibits low room-temperature strength and intergranular fracture, which is 
attributable to its strong adsorption to interstitial oxygen during sintering. In this work, high-strength Ta – Ti alloy 
is achieved by introducing Ti to consume oxygen by forming TiO
2 
and inhabiting the solution of oxygen in Ta 
alloys. The Ta – Ti alloys mainly consist of dense Ta matrix and dispersed TiO
2 
second phase. With the addition of 
2 wt% Ti, the Ta – Ti alloy with high densification of 99.2% not only exhibits high ultimate bending strength of 
1345 MPa from dispersion strengthening by TiO
2 
oxides, but also has good plasticity of 6% with a transgranular 
fracture and numerous dimple. The present work may facilitate the development and industrial application of Ta- 
based alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Refractory tantalum alloys are the potential materials for aerospace, 
new energy, and the nuclear industry because of their high density, high 
melting point, high ductility, corrosion resistance, and low ductile-to- 
brittle transition temperature [ 1 – 5 ]. Especially, the combination of 
high-density and excellent ductility makes tantalum alloys an attractive 
candidate to replace copper in advanced warhead materials [ 6 – 8 ]. 
However, poor sinterability and medium strength inhibit its applica -
tions. The affinity for oxygen in tantalum makes Ta alloys difficult to 
densify during sintering and suffer from poor strength and plasticity 
[ 9 – 11 ]. 
Alloying elements have been demonstrated to provide activation and 
improve the densification and strength of Ta alloys. Filgueira et al. [ 12 ] 
added elements Ni, Fe, and Cu with 1 wt% to Ta alloys as sintering aids 
and found that Ni was more effective in improving the densification by 
increasing the activation energy of Ta sintering. However, the 
compressive yield strength of Ta – Ni was only 101 MPa. With the 
development of the first nature principle in recent years, Song et al. [ 13 ] 
used the discrete variational cluster method (DVM) to point out the 
potential of Ti to increase the strength reduction modulus of tantalum. 
In addition, the second phase with dispersion distribution formed in the 
Ta alloys is demonstrated to strengthen the alloys [ 14 – 16 ]. Wang et al. 
[ 14 ] fabricated Ta – W – Si alloys by spark-plasma sintering (SPS), in 
which the reaction between Ta and Si promotes the diffusion of Ta is 
beneficial for the densification of the alloy. The formed TaSi
2 
silicide 
improves the strength of Ta alloys. Zhang et al. [ 16 ] prepared a 
Ta-9.2W-0.5Hf alloy with high tensile strength of 865 MPa and elon -
gation of 25% by hot isostatic pressing and annealing, the dispersed 
hafnium oxide in the grain boundaries strengthen the Ta alloy based on 
second-phase strengthening. 
For fabrication technology, plasma-activated sintering (PAS) has 
multiple advantages, such as higher heating rates, axial pressure, and 
field-assisted sintering. PAS is demonstrated to achieve rapid densifi -
cation at a relatively low sintering temperature under a vacuum envi -
ronment, in which the sintered alloys exhibit uniform microstructure 
and excellent comprehensive performance [ 17 , 18 ]. The density of the 
W – Ni – Fe alloy prepared by Xiang et al. [ 19 ] can reach 96.7% at 1250 
◦
C. 
This sintering temperature is much lower than the sintering temperature 
required for conventional pressureless sintering. The tungsten alloy 
prepared by Huang et al. [ 20 ] at 1200 
◦
C has a high strength-plasticity 
combination with compressive strength of 2078 MPa and a strain of 
40%. Activated sintering was introduced to fabricate tantalum alloy 
with the help of the addition of sintering aids. Such as adding nickel and

2. Experimental 
Pure Ta powder (purity 99.99%, particle 10.5 μ m) and Ti powder 
(purity 99.99%, particle 3 – 5 μ m) were balls milled by low-energy ball 
milling apparatus for 5 h with a nominal composition of Ta – 2Ti and 
Ta – 4Ti (wt.%, hereafter). Tantalum powder has an oxygen content of 
0.05 wt% and an atomic percentage of 0.556% as determined by an 
oxygen and nitrogen analyzer. The ball milling process is carried out 
under the protection of argon with the ball to a material weight ratio is 
10:1 and a rotation speed of 120 rpm. The mixed powder was directly 
loaded into a high-strength graphite mold (20 mm in diameter) and 
subsequently consolidated by a PAS machine (Elenix company, Japan). 
When sintering with PAS, the apparatus was first vacuumed to 3 × 10
 2

to elevate the sample to the final sintering temperature of 1500 
◦

pressure of 50 MPa. The holding time is 3 min and then cooled to room 
temperature in the furnace. 
The density of the sample was measured using the Archimedes 
drainage method and the theoretical density is calculated by the mixing 
rule. The crystal phases were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD, 
Rigaku Ultima III, Japan) with Cu-K α radiation combined with an 
electron back-scattered diffraction (EBSD). Scanning electron micro -
scopy (SEM, FEI, Quanta FEG250, USA) and transmission electron mi -

microstructures and compositions. To observe the grain boundary dis -

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Microstructure 
Fig. 1 displays the relative density of Ta – Ti alloys prepared by PAS. 
The densification of pure tantalum alloy is only 93% while the densifi -
cation reaches 99.2% with the addition of 2 wt% Ti, indicating that Ti 
can significantly affect the relative density of the alloy. When the 
addition of Ti is 4 wt%, the Ta-4 wt.% Ti alloy shows high densification 
of 99.6%. This result demonstrates that Ti addition is conducive to 
obtaining dense Ta – Ti alloys. 
Fig. 2 shows the XRD patterns of the Ta – Ti alloys with different Ti 
addition. Only the peak of tantalum was observed in the Ta – Ti alloys. 
Interestingly, diffusion peaks of pure tantalum show a clear shift to a 
small angle after sintering and then shift to a large angle with the 
addition of titanium to coincide with the card peak position. From the 
element distribution shown below in Fig. 5 , the Ta matrix contains a 
small quantity of oxygen. According to the work of Stecura [ 26 ] and 
H ¨orz [ 27 ], oxygen with a small atomic size dissolves into Ta lattice, 
resulting in the increase of lattice constant. Thus, it is reasonable to 
assume that the bigger lattice constant in the Ta matrix than that in pure 
Ta is attributable to the solid solution of elements O. With the addition of 
Ti, the diffraction peaks shift to a higher angle, indicating that adding Ti 
could consume the oxygen and inhibit the interstitial solution of O in Ta. 
Fig. 3 shows the SEM images of the sintered Ta – Ti alloys. There are 
many pores in the pure Ta alloy, mainly distributed around the grain 
boundaries ( Fig. 3 (a-a

other is due to the different diffusion coefficients of Ti and Ta, which 
leads to the formation of vacancies and pores in regions where more 
atoms have moved out (Kirkendall effect). The presence of pores de -
teriorates the mechanical properties of the pure Ta. After Ti addition, the 
pores disappear gradually and the alloys become denser ( Fig. 3 (b-c

Fig. 8 shows the fracture morphology of the Ta – Ti alloys. There are 
many pores distributed at the grain boundary and the inner grains which 
reduce the bonding force between the grains leading to the lower 
strength of the pure tantalum sample ( Fig. 8 a). Compared with pure 
tantalum, the Ta – Ti alloy with 1 wt% Ti addition shows more dimples on 
the fracture surface in Fig. 8 b, supporting a ductile fracture. The fracture 
surface of Ta – 2Ti exhibits a combination of ductile fracture and 
transgranular fracture, as illustrated in Fig. 8 (c). The dimples on the 
fracture surface are deep and dense, implying good ductility [ 30 ]. EDS 
analysis of the white box area shows that the transgranular fracture is 
more likely to occur in the Ti-rich region. However, the intergranular 
fracture becomes the main fracture mode when the Ti content increases 
to 4%. The inter-diffusion process will be accelerated when increasing 
the Ti content (as a low melting point phase) and this will lead to grain 
coarsening. 
3.3. phase formation 
Compared with pure tantalum alloy that exhibits low strength and 
intergranular fracture, the Ta – Ti alloys with proper Ti addition show 
obviously improved strength and plasticity. The high strength-plasticity 
is attributable to the good adsorption of Ti to oxygen. Generally, the 
pressed samples with a density of about 50% contain lots of residual 
oxygen, some of which remain in the sample during sintering. During 
sintering, some oxygen is dissolved in Ta [ 31 ], while some react with the 
element Ta to form Ta – O oxide [ 27 ] in the grain boundaries of the Ta 
matrix, both of which cause the embrittlement of Ta and result in the 
intergranular fracture of pure tantalum alloy with poor strength and 
plasticity. After adding Ti, Ti – O oxide is formed priorly during sintering, 
which consumes the residual oxygen in the samples and promotes the 
densification and strength of the Ta – Ti alloys. The good oxygen 
adsorption of Ti to O is illustrated by the reaction thermodynamic cal -
culations, and the main reaction during sintering is shown as follows.

3.4. Strengthening mechanisms 
Ta – 2Ti alloy exhibits high ultimate bending strength of 1345 MPa, 
which is significantly higher than that of Ta – 10W alloy (786 MPa) and 
Ta – 10W-1.5TiC (912 MPa) [ 28 ], suggesting that the addition of Ti 
shows a high strengthening effect. This reinforcement is also confirmed 
by the higher strength and plasticity of Ta – 2Ti as compared with pure Ta 
alloy ( Fig. 7 ). In general, improved strength is accompanied by reduced 
plasticity, in fact, the Ta – 2Ti alloy has enhanced strength and plasticity, 
which is attributable to two reasons. First, TiO
2 
oxide is dispersed in the 
Ta matrix, providing a good dispersion-strengthening effect. To clarify 
the strengthening mechanism, TEM analysis and corresponding EDX 
mapping of Ta – 2Ti alloy after bending deformation were conducted 
( Fig. 10 ). The element distribution of the deformed alloy is in agreement 
with the sintered alloy ( Fig. 10 b). The Ta matrix contains a high density 
of dislocations ( Fig. 10 a and c), while the TiO
2 
oxide shows no 
deformability without obvious dislocations ( Fig. 10 a). The phase inter -
face between Ta and TiO

residual oxygen in the sample was reacted with Ti to generate TiO

Further increasing the Ti addition, the strong adsorption ability of Ti 
to oxygen makes it easy to generate an oxide layer at the Ti agglomer -
ation. On the one hand, the presence of the oxide layer will cut the 
interconnection between the grains of the Ta matrix, and on the other 
hand, there will be a stress gradient due to the difference in linear 
expansion coefficient between the oxide layer and the internal Ti phase 
and Ta matrix. Therefore, it is easy to generate cracks in the phase 
interface, which propagate along the grain boundaries and result in 
brittle fractures ( Fig. 8 d-e). 
4. Conclusions 
The effect of Ti addition on the densification, microstructure, phase 
formation, and mechanical properties of Ta – Ti alloy was investigated. 
The Ta – Ti alloys have high densifications and mainly consist of Ta 
matrix and dispersed TiO
2 
oxide. Ti reacts priorly with the residual 
oxygen to generate TiO
2 
oxide, which prevents the oxygen atom from 
interstitial dissolving to Ta lattice and avoids the formation of brittle 
Ta
2
O
5 
oxide in the Ta grain boundaries. The dispersed TiO
2 
oxide en -
hances the Ta – Ti alloys based on the dispersion-strengthening effect. 
The Ta – 2Ti with proper Ti addition exhibits high ultimate bending 
strength of 1345 MPa and good plasticity of 16%. This work provides a 
method for the development of high-performance Ta-based alloys.
