long-term action of constant and variable loads. These equations establish some relation

It is assumed in this and other equations of state that the change the material under- 
goes during work is connected mainly with the formation of discontinuities within it. To 
account for this, instead of all of the structural parameters we introduce the notion of 
damage ~(m = 0 at the moment of loading, ~ = 1 at fracture). Existing equations do not

because of the need to conduct tests over nearly the entire range of times in question -- 
including long-term tests-- in order to determine these coefficients. Moreover, the physical 
essence of the phenomena under consideration is not considered by this approach. 
When a material works under load at high temperatures, its mechanical properties change 
continuously as a result of the transition of the alloying elements from solid solution in- 
to carbides and the formation of new phases and structures and occurrence of other processes 
(apart from the formation of discontinuities) which lead to a deterioration in its service 
properties. Figuratively speaking, a high-alloy steel (point A on the creep curve in Fig. i) 
is chamged after different periods of work first into a low-alloy steel (point B), then into 
an alloyed steel (point C), etc., and finally, in the stage preceding fracture, becomes a 
law-quality steel. Despite the fact that this is a simplified approach to the matter, it 
has a scientific basis and is supported hy test findings. Let us look at some of these 
findings.

The subdivision of the total damage into time-dependent and nontime-dependent is some-

the ultlm-te strength and elongation of the material after service and in the initial state
