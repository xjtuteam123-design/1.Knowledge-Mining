A B S T R A C T
HfNbTaTiZr is a refractory multi-principal element alloy (RMPEA) that exhibits high strength combined with
good ductility at room temperature. An insufficient understanding of RMPEA tensile behavior, however,
limits their ability to be thermo-mechanically processed and optimized for engineering applications. In
this investigation, uniaxial tensile tests were conducted at room temperature, 800 ◦C, and 1200 ◦C under
vacuum on sheet materials fabricated by hot and cold rolling combined with annealing treatments. At testing
temperatures of 25 ◦C and 1200 ◦C, the HfNbTaTiZr alloy exhibited high tensile ductility (16.0% and 121.0%,
respectively). At 800 ◦C, the HfNbTaTiZr alloy exhibited significantly decreased ductility (2.0%) compared to
room temperature, and coincided with a transition from ductile to intergranular fracture. SEM and TEM studies
revealed precipitation reactions at grain boundaries following thermal exposure and during tensile testing at
800 ◦C, resulting in tantalum and niobium-rich BCC precipitates, and finer-scale hafnium and zirconium-rich
precipitates. After annealing at 800 ◦C for 100 h, nanoindentation revealed soft and stiff tantalum and niobium-
rich BCC precipitates located at grain boundaries. Thermodynamic calculations corroborated the existence of
two BCC phases and predicted an HCP phase to be stable between 703–804 ◦C, which was consistent with
TEM observations. At 1200 ◦C, high tensile ductility resulted from the combined effects of dynamic recovery
and partial dynamic recrystallization. These experimental and computational results underscore a need to
understand the high temperature phase equilibria and complex deformation behaviors of refractory MPEs so
that their microstructure and mechanical performance can be better controlled across the elevated temperature
ranges of interest.
1. Introduction
Refractory multi-principal element alloys (refractory MPEAs or RM-
PEAs) – often referred to as refractory high-entropy alloys – have
generated significant interest recently due to their high strength at
elevated temperatures [ 1]. MPEA design diverges from conventional
structural alloy design wisdom; historically, structural alloys have con-
tained 60–70 atomic percent (at.%) or more of a single element, with
only small additions of other elements for specific property optimiza-
tion [2–4]. MPEAs, however, are often nearly equiatomic, resulting in
no ‘‘base alloy’’ element [ 5–8]. This novel approach to alloy design
offers a vast composition space for exploration beyond the primary in-
terest in equiatomic compositions [8]. Proposed industrial applications
for RMPEAs include nuclear reactor systems and various propulsion sys-
tems: aircraft engine components, rocketry, and hypersonic aircraft [9].
As designs in these application environments push to ever-higher op-
erating temperatures and mechanical requirements, there is a demand

The solid solution HfNbTaTiZr alloy was first developed in 2011 [11]
and subsequently generated significant interest due to its substantial
room temperature ductility, which is critical for components employed
in extreme environments to avoid catastrophic failure. Ductility in
RMPEAs is significant because it can improve the ease of component
fabrication, for instance by enabling a tailored microstructure to be
produced by cold or hot rolling [ 1]. Other processing paths, such as
extrusion or 3D printing will also require some level of tensile ductility.
Additionally, the HfNbTaTiZr alloy possesses a desirable combination
of strength and tensile ductility [ 12,13], though, similar to other body
centered cubic (BCC) metals it experiences dramatic softening with
increasing temperature [14,15].

HfNbTaTiZr is a member of the HfNbTaTiVZr MPE family, which
consistently exhibits good tensile ductility at room temperature [1,7,
16–19], in contrast to Cr, Mo, and W containing materials [8]. Despite
tensile ductility being a strong motivation for RMPEA alloy design, very
few tensile tests have been reported for any RMPEA and the behavior
of these materials in tension across the relevant temperature range is
poorly understood. The most recent compilation of RMPEA mechanical
properties, consisting of records up to the 2020 publication year,
reports over 400 compressive property records and less than 40 tensile
records for 20 unique compositions [8]. Further, with the exception
of tensile properties reported by Chen et al. [20] for HfNbTaTiZr at
temperatures ranging from 300–550 ◦C, RMPEA tensile properties have
only been reported for room temperature and below [21–23]. While the
most recent investigations on RMPEAs focus on room temperature duc-
tility, commercial aircraft components in-service can experience a wide
range of temperatures, from −54 ◦C to 1650 ◦C [24], during operation
and understanding how mechanical properties evolve with temperature
is critical for guiding future development of these materials [25].
Previous investigations have found that additional phases may
evolve at elevated temperatures in HfNbTaTiZr, with reports of phase
separation in the form of various grain boundary precipitation products
at 800 ◦C after different exposure times up to 256 h [1], 100 h [26], and
24 h [27]; BCC(matrix)+BCC(precipitate) [1], BCC(matrix)+HCP(prec-
ipitate) [26], and BCC(matrix)+BCC(precipitate) [27]. However, the
discrepancies in reported crystal structure of these grain boundary
precipitates and their impact on the tensile mechanical behavior of
HfNbTaTiZr are not completely understood [1,26,28].
HfNbTaTiZr serves as an exemplar material for ductile RMPEAs
and enables the investigation of temperature-dependent behaviors of
RMPEAs while the broader compositional space of these materials
continues to be explored. In this investigation, the tensile behavior
of thermomechanically processed HfNbTaTiZr with a recrystallized
microstructure and low interstitial content was examined at room
temperature, 800 ◦C, and 1200 ◦C. Fracture modes were examined post
mortem by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and electron backscat-
ter diffraction (EBSD). High temperature deformation behaviors were
characterized, including dynamic recrystallization and deformation tex-
tures. Additionally, the composition and sequence of precipitation ob-
served at grain boundaries following intermediate temperature expo-
sure was determined by SEM and transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) techniques. The mechanical response of the material annealed at
the intermediate temperature was also probed locally near grain bound-
aries by nanoindentation. Finally, these experimental results were cor-
roborated with phase equilibria predicted by ThermoCalc thermody-
namic software. The results of this investigation highlight the interplay
between phase and microstructural evolution on the HfNbTaTiZr alloy
mechanical response in tension at temperatures that are relevant to its
application.
2. Experimental methods

approximately 300 g button using a non-consumable electrode. The
button was inspected via X-ray radiography to confirm dissolution of
the heavy elements, and then hot rolled into a sheet at 1150 ◦C in air
with a 20 min pre-heat and 10 min re-heats between passes. The sheet

the sheet was conditioned to remove surface cracks and defects and
then vacuum annealed at 1100 ◦C for 1 h. Following annealing, the
HfNbTaTiZr sheet was cold rolled at 3% reduction per pass down to
2.5 mm thick, then reduced by 5% thickness per pass down to 1 mm
thick, with no cracking observed in the sheet. Following cold rolling,
the HfNbTaTiZr sheet was given a final recrystallization (RX) anneal
under vacuum at 1100 ◦C for 1 h. The composition of the HfNbTaTiZr

Tensile tests were performed per specifications of ASTM E8 and
E21 [29,30]. Tensile samples were cut from the annealed HfNbTaTiZr
sheet via electrical discharge machining (EDM) and the recast layer
was mechanically removed prior to tensile testing. The machined ten-
sile bars had a 25.4 mm gauge length with a 6.35 mm wide gauge
section and were machined such that the tensile axis was coincident
with the rolling direction. Tensile tests were performed in a screw-
driven Satec frame with Marshall radiative furnace under 1.33 × 10−2
Pa vacuum. During all tests, an increase in strain rate was imposed
following the yield point from 10 −2 s−1 to 10−1 s−1 as a standard
industry procedure to reduce the operating time of the mechanical
test equipment. The elevated temperature 800 ◦C and 1200 ◦C tests
experienced heating cycles of approximately 6 min each. Macroscale
mechanical properties were determined via crosshead displacement for
all temperatures because use of a strain gauge was prohibited during
testing at elevated temperatures. Elongation values reported for this
investigation in Table 2 were determined by direct measurement of
each sample following tensile testing.
Following tensile testing, the fracture surfaces were observed via
a ThermoFisher Apreo C FEG SEM. EBSD datasets were indexed using
the EMSphInx 0.2 release [35,36]. Subsequent evaluation of the EBSD
datasets was performed using EDAX Orientation Imaging Microscopy
(OIM) Analysis 8 software. A cleanup of each dataset was performed in
OIM, comprised of grain dilation, 1-pixel minimum grain size threshold
due to the observed fine-scale DRX, and 0.1 minimum confidence index.

Nanoindentation was used to determine the hardness and modulus
of regions neighboring and located at grain boundaries in the HfNbTa-
TiZr sample following the 800 ◦C for 100 h annealing treatment. A
Nanomechanics, Inc. iMicro ® Nanoindenter equipped with a 50 mN
load cell and diamond Berkovich tip was used at ambient tempera-
ture. A continuous stiffness method protocol with drift correction was
performed at a target indentation strain rate of 0.1 s−1. The chosen fre-
quency and dynamic oscillating displacement values were 100 Hz and
2 nm, respectively. The drift correction threshold value was 0.1 nm/s.
Two separate arrays were indented to a maximum target indentation
depth of 300 nm with greater than 5 μm spacing between all indents.
From these two arrays, a statistical sampling of 10 indents were selected
from each of the three regions of interest ( Figs. 5 and 7). The depth-
dependent hardness and modulus values for each indent at indentation
depths of 200–300 nm were averaged. This was followed by averaging
the resulting hardness and modulus values for 10 indents in each region
(Fig. 7).
Electron transparent samples for TEM/STEM were prepared via a
focused ion beam (FIB) lift-out technique in an FEI Helios Dualbeam
Nanolab 600. Conventional TEM and STEM with energy dispersive
X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was carried out in a ThermoFisher Talos
G2 200X TEM/STEM with ChemiSTEM EDS and confirmed via Ther-
moFisher Apreo C FEG SEM with EDAX Si-drift EDX detector. X-ray
diffraction was carried out on a Pananalytical Empyrean diffractometer
with Cu k-alpha source.
Thermodynamically stable phases in equiatomic HfNbTaTiZr were
modeled in ThermoCalc using the ThermoCalc — High Entropy Alloys
4 (TCHEA4) database [37]. No phases were suppressed during this cal-
culation. The resulting predictions were evaluated over a temperature
range of 300-2500 ◦C.

3.2. Room temperature microstructure examination
The secondary electron SEM micrograph of the room temperature
fracture surface reveals cup-and-cone fracture surface features (Fig. 3B)
consistent with microvoid coalescence during ductile rupture [ 40].
Inspection of the grain boundaries by TEM in foils extracted within
5 mm of the fracture surface of the post mortem HfNbTaTiZr tensile
bar confirmed the absence of grain boundary precipitation in the
tensile sample tested at room temperature (Fig. A.1). The EBSD dataset
collected from the room temperature sample (Fig. 3E) reveals extensive
gradients in orientation and deformation bands within grains.
3.3. 800 ◦C tensile test microstructure
The secondary electron SEM micrograph of the 800 ◦C fracture sur-
face was obtained following the tensile test performed under vacuum
with a short heating duration of 6 min ( Fig. 3C). The fracture surface
exhibits minimal plastic deformation of the exposed, highly faceted
grains. The EBSD dataset shows equiaxed grains with an approximate
grain size of 100 μm with minor orientation gradients emanating from
grain boundary triple points (Fig. 3F). A FIB lift-out was obtained from
the grip section of the tensile bar, located furthest from the gauge
section, to examine the grain boundary features that were exposed to
temperature but not deformation. A STEM high angle annular dark field
(HAADF) image with complementary EDS data at a grain boundary
(Fig. 4) reveals precipitation at grain boundaries in the form of two
distinct precipitate types; one type is enriched in tantalum and niobium
(BCC-stabilizing elements), while the other is enriched in hafnium and
zirconium (HCP-stabilizing elements).
3.4. Microstructure evolution after 100 h age at 800 ◦C

ducted to evaluate the character and mechanical behavior of the grain
boundary precipitation observed in the tensile tested sample. The back-

volume fraction of intragranular and grain boundary precipitates, both
of which appear bright due to Z-contrast. EDS (Table 3) measurements
indicate that these precipitates are rich in Nb and Ta and depleted
in Hf and Zr. Directly adjacent to the precipitates are solute-depleted
regions rich in Hf, Ti, and Zr, termed the depletion zone. Sections of the

3.5. 1200 ◦C tensile test microstructure
Post mortem investigations of the HfNbTaTiZr tensile sample tested
at 1200 ◦C using SEM and EBSD (Fig. 3A,D,G and Fig. 8) do not reveal
any grain boundary precipitation. Instead, the microstructure exhibits
both unrecrystallized grains with extensive intragranular misorienta-
tions and grain refinement produced by partial DRX along the full
gauge length ( Fig. 8 A–F). The nominally undeformed average grain
diameter is approximately 55 μm and was determined from grains in
the sample grip (Fig. 8G).

collected along the entire gauge length (Fig. 8). Unrecrystallized grains
were partitioned by a grain size (GS) that was specified for each

with texture maps within the standard stereographic triangle (Fig. 8N–
T) reveal that the unrecrystallized grains in region D were the most
textured with preference for the<101> direction. A<101> - oriented
unrecrystallized grain (Fig. 9B,C) exhibits relatively low misorientation
compared to the extensive sub-boundary formation identified in other

4. Discussion

129μm) investigated by Juan et al. [ 31] at room temperature. To our
knowledge, no high temperature tensile investigations have been per-

reported here compare favorably to those reported in compression

4.2. Intermediate temperature ductility debit
While a decrease in yield strength with increasing temperature
is a typical behavior in solid-solution strengthened metals and RM-
PEAs [42], the significant drop in ductility at 800 ◦C and recovery of
ductility at 1200 ◦C motivates a detailed investigation of intermediate
temperature phase equilibria in the HfNbTaTiZr alloy. In contrast to
complete homogenization of the atomic species in the room temper-
ature tensile sample ( Fig. A.1 ), SEM and TEM investigation of the
tensile sample exposed to 800 ◦C under vacuum for only six minutes
reveal precipitation at grain boundaries ( Fig. 4 ). Grain boundaries
provide pathways for more rapid diffusion than through the bulk and
serve as sites for secondary phases to nucleate and subsequently grow.
Two compositionally different precipitates are measured in STEM EDS
located at grain boundaries, and the higher volume fraction of Nb- and
Ta-enriched BCC precipitate type is expected to be associated with the
significantly decreased ductility of HfNbTaTiZr. Occasional secondary
intergranular cracks spanning the length of one or two grains are
observed within 10 mm of the fracture surface in the 800 ◦C sample.
However, these cracks are not associated with brittle cracking of the
grain boundary precipitates.
FIB lamellae removed from the grain boundary region allowed for
post mortem investigation of the grain boundaries of the 800 ◦C tensile
sample. STEM-EDS compositional maps (Fig. 4) reveal phase separation
at grain boundaries that resulted in NbTa-rich precipitates, as well as
smaller amounts of HfZr-rich precipitates. The observation of phase
separation in HfNbTaTiZr at 800 ◦C is supported by thermodynamic
modeling predictions (Fig. 10), with stable coexistence of three separate
phases between 703–804 ◦C; a small 2% volume fraction prediction of
an HCP phase (Table 5), and two body-centered cubic phases that can
be differentiated by their compositions and lattice parameters.
The grain boundary precipitates in the 800 ◦C tensile sample are
less than 100 nm in diameter (Fig. 4), which was prohibitively small for
conclusive analysis with SAED in the TEM used for this investigation.
Therefore, the crystal structure and orientation of the precipitates with
respect to the neighboring grains could not be conclusively determined
directly from the tensile sample. However, a complementary aging
treatment at 800 ◦C for 100 h on a section of HfNbTaTiZr sheet,
presented in Section 3.4 , coarsened the grain boundary precipitates
to a sufficient size for examination via SAED. Based on ChemiSTEM
EDS results and thermodynamic predictions, the phase selection of
coarsened structures following annealing is likely representative of
those observed after tensile testing. This investigation reveals that
they are BCC and incoherent with the neighboring lattice, given misfit
strains of−2 to−3%, which is consistent with behaviors that have been
reported following annealing at 700 ◦C for 2.5 h by Chen et al. [ 28].
The BCC crystal structure of the NbTa-rich regions is expected, since
Nb and Ta both exhibit BCC crystal structures in their pure form and
are completely soluble with each other according to the Nb-Ta binary
phase diagram [43]. Due to the high ductility of pure Nb and Ta and the

The phase precipitation behavior of the alloy can be connected
to the behavior of the associated binary systems. The binary phase
diagrams of the refractory elements have been categorized into six
groups by Natarajan et al. [44], and there are two model behaviors for
the five elements present in equiatomic HfNbTaTiZr: an HCP + BCC
to single phase BCC transition with increasing temperature in X-Y (X
= Hf, Zr, Ti, Y = Nb, Ta) systems and BCC miscibility gaps present in
Nb-Zr and Ta-Zr. The precipitation of both HCP and BCC phases along
the grain boundaries is consistent with both behaviors. In particular,
the HCP + BCC to BCC transition in the Hf-Nb binary system is above
1250 ◦C in Hf-rich materials and the miscibility gap in Ta-Zr can persist
to 1783 ◦C, depending on the composition. Ti can stabilize the BCC
polymorph of Hf and Zr and may account for the small volume fraction
of HCP precipitates.
The persistence of these behaviors in the quinary alloy that are
consistent with model binary phase diagrams has implications for
alloy design of ductile RMPEAs containing high amounts of Hf and
Zr, particularly in the presence of Ta. Many of these alloys demon-
strate extensive room temperature tensile ductility and desirable cold
rolling capability [ 19,45], but none have been tested in tension at
temperatures where diffusion could be sufficient to cause comparable
phase formation and ductility debits. This emphasizes the importance
of investigating properties in tension when diffusion is sufficient for
transformations to progress, i.e. above 800 ◦C for the investigated
alloy, and the importance of determining phase transition boundaries
in these complex materials. As further discussed in Section 4.4 , given
the observed phase equilibria in this alloy, the ductility debit is likely
to persist to at least 1000 ◦C, at which point secondary phases will

To further study the mechanisms responsible for the loss in ductility
at intermediate temperature, nanoindentation was used to locally probe
the mechanical behavior of grain boundary and intragranular regions
(Fig. 7 ). The high volume fraction of BCC precipitate phase (that is
not expected to be inherently brittle at elevated temperature) could
be the cause of the reduction in ductility. Nanoindentation indicates
that the grain boundary BCC precipitates are regions with relatively
low ratios of strength-to-stiffness, a metric that is more representative
of the anisotropic response of a polycrystalline aggregate compared
to single-crystal strength or stiffness [ 48]. These BCC precipitates are
likely to yield more readily compared to the depleted and intermediate
zones located toward the grain interiors, providing pathways along
grain boundaries for the localization of plastic deformation, and local
elevation of stresses that could promote initiation of intergranular
failure. Near the fracture plane, the absence of any brittle secondary
cracks along boundaries or in depleted zones adjacent to boundaries
is indicative of plasticity-induced failure. This precipitation-dependent
phenomenon has been observed in the hot deformation of steel, where
a significant reduction in ductility is observed following tensile testing
at 800 ◦C and 850 ◦C [ 49]. Mintz et al. [ 49] have attributed the
ductility drop to the concentration of strain and microvoid formation
at grain boundaries following an austenite (FCC) to ferrite (BCC) phase
transformation. Fu et al. [50] have modeled grain boundaries as regions
of lower yield strength in comparison to grain interiors, analogous
to the grain boundary regions in this investigation that are shown
by nanoindentation to be soft due to the BCC phase precipitation.
They also reviewed both computational and experimental results and
observe that higher work-hardening rates within grain boundaries as
compared to grain interiors based on preferential dislocation nucleation
and changes in maximum resolved shear stress at grain boundaries,
which could, in turn, result in early intergranular failure.
Interstitials are also a significant source of concern for refractory
materials, but are rarely reported. For example, the miscibility gap
in the Ti-V system is promoted by the presence of oxygen [ 51]. Im-
portant for future investigations into the ductility debit in RMPEAs is
quantification of alloy interstitial content and its impact on observed
phases and their transition temperatures. This is imperative given the
high reactivity of the constituent elements with O and 𝑁 and potential
preferential uptake of O into Hf, Ti, and Zr rich phases [ 52]. In this
investigation, measures were taken to mitigate oxygen contamination
during processing and mechanical testing of the HfNbTaTiZr material.
At 1200 ◦C, the HfNbTaTiZr alloy undergoes an evident restoration
of ductile behavior and exists as a single solid solution BCC phase.
These findings are supported by the phase diagram constructed from
ThermoCalc data ( Fig. 10 ) as well as literature reports, such as the
investigation by Senkov et al. [ 11] into the compressive behavior of
HfNbTaTiZr at 1473 K (1199.85 ◦C). Therefore, the material that was
tensile tested at 1200 ◦C did not experience the same effects of grain
boundary precipitation that impacted the tensile sample at 800 ◦C,
thereby recovering from the ductility debit. The mechanical data and
microstructure investigations discussed here demonstrate the sensi-
tivity of HfNbTaTiZr to evolving phase equilibria with temperature,
with phase separation below 1019 ◦C. Elevated temperature processing

4.3. Elevated temperature tensile deformation
Given that the development of RMPEAs is motivated by structural
applications in operation at temperatures exceeding 1200 ◦C, the per-
formance of the material in this temperature regime is critical. The
behavior of HfNbTaTiZr at 1200 ◦C is complex and involves accumu-
lation of (1) changes in grain shape (2) extensive misorientation and
substructure formation within unrecrystallized grains, including low
angle sub-boundaries, (3) DRX of grains with both low and moderate
misorientations, and (4) development of deformation and RX textures.
Post mortem investigations of the 1200 ◦C tensile sample (Fig. 3D,G)
show a widened distribution in grain sizes relative to those in the
sample tested at 800 ◦C (Fig. 3C,F). This microstructural change is
induced by a combination of dynamic recovery processes, including
accumulation of intragranular misorientation and sub-boundary forma-
tion near unrecrystallized grain boundaries, as well as partial DRX.
Dynamic recovery is enabled by glide and climb of dislocations that
contribute to the development of sub-boundaries and annihilation of
dislocation content [53,54]. In contrast, DRX is a phenomenon associ-
ated with nucleation and growth of new, dislocation-free grains during
plastic deformation [40,54]. DRX is further classified into discontin-
uous and continuous behaviors, both of which are likely operative
under the conditions investigated. Discontinuous DRX corresponds to
an evident nucleation stage followed by growth, whereas continuous
DRX may result from grain boundary impingement or subgrain lattice
rotation [54].
Despite the prevalence of dynamic recovery over DRX in BCC metals
and dilute alloys at elevated temperatures due to their high stacking
fault energies and propensity for slip [54], Eleti et al. [15,39] have
observed partial RX in HfNbTaTiZr following uniaxial compression
testing at 1000 ◦C and 10−3 s−1 along with grain elongation parallel
to the compression axis. In the current investigation, partial DRX
also occurred at a temperature approximately 50 ◦C higher than the
initial hot rolling temperature along with grain elongation parallel
to the uniaxial tensile axis. The as-received (processed and equiaxed)
microstructure exhibited a relatively random distribution of grain ori-
entations and limited texture. It is expected that BCC metals deformed
in compression and tension exhibit <001> and<101> fiber textures,
respectively [55]; Eleti et al. [39] have reported < 001 > texture in
compression. The current investigation observed <101 > deformation
texture in tension within partitioned unrecrystallized grains (Fig. 8)
that is consistent with pure BCC metal literature. It is likely that disloca-
tion slip is highly active in these grains, especially near unrecrystallized
grain boundaries. Significant dynamic hardening outcompetes this dy-
namic recovery within the high area fraction of unrecrystallized grains
along the entire gauge length, as evidenced by the high work-hardening
at large strains (Fig. 2C).
The significant reduction in cross sectional area of the 1200 ◦C
sample gauge prior to failure corresponds to a gradient in strain along
the entire gauge length rather than highly localized necking. The region
of the gauge that experienced the most extreme deformation (Figs. 8A
and 9A) exhibits the most complex recrystallization behaviors, however
the area fraction of recrystallized grains in this region is a maximum of
20.7%. Therefore, even in this region with the highest localization of
deformation, there is a majority area fraction of grains experiencing
hardening without recrystallization until failure, which is also con-
sistent with the high work-hardening at large strains (Fig. 2C). Eleti
et al. [39] have discussed mechanisms for the formation of fine-scale
DRX grain necklace structures at unrecrystallized grain boundaries
during compression of HfNbTaTiZr at 1000 ◦C. Incipient stages of
extremely fine-scale discontinuous DRX with minimal grain growth are
observed in the present investigation (Fig. 9H). The primary < 001 >
and minor < 111 > texture observed for these grains are inconsistent
with the previous finding by Eleti et al. [39] of random DRX texture
distributions in compression. The current investigation also anticipates
continuous DRX behaviors to be active in the 1200 ◦C tensile sample
given the variation in DRX grain sizes and misorientations observed
within some DRX grains.
This continuous DRX process upon deformation to higher strains is
likely preceded by the bulging of unrecrystallized grain boundary seg-
ments, sub-boundary formation, and subsequent sub-division of unre-
crystallized grains. Interpenetration of neighboring grains primes their
boundaries for continuous DRX, which is generally observed at high
temperatures [54]. The region of the gauge that experienced the least
extreme deformation (Fig. 8F) exhibits non-uniform grain boundaries,
indicating some segments of the unrecrystallized grain boundaries were
able to move more readily than others. Two unrecrystallized grains
are highlighted (Fig. 9B,D); one that significantly resisted deforma-
tion and another that developed low angle grain boundaries. Grain
boundary segments are observed to bulge outward in this second grain
(Fig. 9D,E), corresponding to high angular misorientations that likely
serve as locations where sub-boundaries develop.
The observation of complex dynamic recovery, hardening, and par-
tial DRX processes in HfNbTaTiZr at elevated temperatures demon-
strates the need for the current report of tensile tests to build upon pre-
vious literature reports in compression. The extensive microstructural
deformation and resulting texture would pose challenges for in-service
operation at this highest temperature of 1200 ◦C. Clearly, compositions
with a higher resistance to dynamic recovery and recrystallization are
needed.
4.4. Comparison of predicted phase diagram to experimental phases
The phase diagram calculated using ThermoCalc (Fig. 10) shows
good agreement with literature and the characterization and XRD re-
sults presented here. Despite the incomplete reaction, the identification
of two disordered BCC phases and one HCP phase around 800 ◦C
corresponds to the boundary of the three phase region predicted by
CALPHAD. Senkov et al. and Yasuda et al. have observed two BCC
phases at 800 ◦C and no HCP phase [1,27], but the small volume
fraction of HCP phases observed in this investigation indicate they
may be too small to identify without TEM in samples with insufficient
coarsening. Chen et al. has reported observation of all three phases
after annealing at 700 ◦C for 96 h, which lies just below the three
phase regime predicted by CALPHAD [28]. With reference to the tensile
ductility debit, at higher temperatures the transition temperature to
a single BCC solid solution has not been definitively determined, but
is below 1000 ◦C [26,28,56], which is below the CALPHAD predicted
value of 1019.2 ◦C.
The effect of interstitial contents on equilibrium phase behaviors
has not been studied. Interstitial O and𝑁 have significant effects on the
HCP polymorphs of Hf and Zr, elevating the HCP to BCC transition tem-
perature and eventually suppressing the BCC polymorph completely,
and could potentially have an impact on what phases are observed at
equilibrium or during mechanical testing [57] in this investigation. Un-
fortunately, interstitial contents are not frequently reported in literature
and cannot be compared to the low values reported here [9].

5. Conclusions
HfNbTaTiZr serves as a model material for investigation of the
mechanical properties of RMPEAs as new compositional space and high
temperature regimes are identified. HfNbTaTiZr exhibits good strength
and ductility at room temperature, but shows significantly decreased
ductility in tension at 800 ◦C. This ductility drop is associated with
grain boundary precipitation, resulting in intergranular failure under
tensile loading. The precipitates at the grain boundaries consist of two
types: one Ta and Nb-rich BCC precipitate and one Zr and Hf-rich
precipitate. ThermoCalc calculations corroborated the presence of BCC
and HCP precipitates observed in this experiment. At 1200◦C, the alloy
exhibits high ductility due to dynamic recovery and partial DRX of the
microstructure during tensile loading. ThermoCalc calculations predict
that at this temperature, the material exists as a single phase solid
solution BCC alloy. The body of evidence presented in this investigation
indicates a need to control the precipitation and recrystallization be-
haviors with consideration of the potential processing and component
operating temperature ranges when employing RMPEAs.
