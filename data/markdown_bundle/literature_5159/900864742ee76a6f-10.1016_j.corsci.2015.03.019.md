abstract
The high-temperature oxidation resistance of the carbide strengthened nickel-base alloy 602 CA is pro-
vided by the formation of a protective outer Cr 2O3 scale accompanied by internal precipitation of alumina
and dissolution of Cr-rich carbides. The carbide free zone formation has been discussed in the literature to
be a consequence of either chromium depletion or alloy decarburization.
A new dissolution mechanism is proposed in this paper. Carbide dissolution in alloy 602 CA exposed to
oxidizing atmospheres at 1100 /C176C was studied. The alloy microstructure was characterized by means of
SEM/EDX/EPMA and GDOES. The experimental observations were substantiated by CALPHAD based
computations.

1. Introduction
In modern high-temperature applications, e.g., gas burners [1],
heat-exchangers [2], ﬂame tubes and furnaces crucial components
operate under high thermal and mechanical load. The envisaged
candidate construction materials for these applications are
designed to meet these requirements by providing high creep
strength as well as good oxidation/corrosion resistance. The
wrought Ni-base alloys 600 [3], 601 [4], and 602 CA [5] available
on the high-temperature materials market are suitable construc-
tion materials for the mentioned applications. The oxidation/corro-
sion resistance of these alloys relies on the formation of a dense,
adherent and slowly growing chromia scale [6]. The scaling process
leads to a continuous, selective removal of Cr, which results in
compositional and microstructural changes in the alloy subsurface
zone [7–9]. The mentioned chromia-forming Ni-base alloys are
often strengthened by carbide precipitates of the type M
23C6
and/or M 7C3. M is mainly Cr; however, Ni and Fe are known [10]
to substitute Cr on the metal sites and thus have a certain solubility
in M 7C3 and M 23C6 (3–8 at.%, depending on temperature). These
alloys form a carbide free zone (CFZ) beneath the oxide scale
during exposure in an oxidizing environment, in which carbides
are either dissolved or denuded [11–13]. In case of alloy grades
601 and 602 CA the outer chromia scale is formed adjacent to an
internal oxidation zone (IOZ) of Al, which is followed by the CFZ
[14–19]. The dissolution of Cr-rich carbides may have detrimental
consequences for the creep strength of the alloys [20].
Oxidation-induced carbide dissolution in high alloyed steels
and Ni-base alloys is a well-documented experimental phe-
nomenon for which theoretical approaches were proposed. Some
assumptions were expressed about the carbon released from the
carbides during dissolution. Durham et al. [11] speculated on a
decarburization mechanism to describe the depletion of carbides
in the alloy subsurface during 850 /C176C oxidation in pure oxygen of
the model alloy Fe–15Cr–0.5C. The authors presumed that carbon
in the ferritic matrix diffuses towards the surface of the alloy and
oxidizes to form carbon monoxide, which then diffuses outward
through the scale along grain boundaries and submicroscopic
defects in the oxide scale. However, no experimental evidence of
carbon monoxide formation during oxidation was given. More con-
sistent is the assumption of carbon diffusion towards the alloy
interior described in [12,13,17,21,22]. In these papers it has been
generally argued that the reason for subscale carbide dissolution
during protective oxidation is the depletion of Cr [13,20,22]. Due
to the dissolution of carbides induced by the Cr depletion the car-
bon content of the matrix phase increases and a carbon concentra-
tion gradient evolves between the matrix in the CFZ and the matrix

the precipitation of secondary and/or coarsening of existing car-

subsurface zone into the core of the sample, thereby forming
new carbides. The average volume-fraction of the carbides in the
as-received material was about 3%, but after 1400 h and 5470 h
exposure at 1100 /C176C it had increased to 6% and 8%, respectively,
in the center of the specimen.
A consistent thermodynamic–kinetic model of this microstruc-
tural effect occurring in a multi-component high-temperature
alloy is not available so far. Especially, a possible effect of the Al
depletion in the IOZ on the carbide dissolution in alloy 602 CA is
of great interest. It was previously shown [17] with thermody-
namic calculations that the Al depletion as a result of internal oxi-
dation further increases the solubility of carbon in the matrix
phase and hence destabilizes the carbides. However, an elaborate
investigation of the thermodynamic interactions and the inﬂuence
of these thermodynamic changes on the undergoing diffusional
processes was not undertaken in the previous publication. To the
best of the knowledge of the authors, the oxidation driven dis-
solution of carbides has until now not been discussed in terms of
both alloy thermodynamics and diffusion processes.
The aim of the present study is to build up a coupled thermody-
namic–kinetic oxidation model, which allows a quantitative
description of carbide dissolution, CFZ growth kinetics and the
inﬂuence of Al on the CFZ in alloy 602 CA during high-temperature
oxidation. With new experimental observations in combination
with the thermodynamic and kinetic calculations performed in
the present paper, a critical revision of the Cr-depletion concept
used previously to describe carbide dissolution in high tempera-
ture Ni-base alloys has been undertaken.
2. Experimental
The experimental results presented in this study refer to two
types of experiments with the same material, alloy 602 CA [5], car-
ried out in two different institutes. The experiments carried out by
Oel-Wärme-Institut (OWI) refer to exposures in a simulated ﬂue
gas of a burner. Forschungszentrum Jülich (FZJ) carried out expo-
sures of standard corrosion test coupons in a horizontal furnace
in laboratory air. Both institutes used 1100 /C176C as test temperature.
2.1. Materials
Rolled sheets of alloy 602 CA (2 mm thickness) were supplied
by VDM Metals GmbH. The alloys were solution annealed under
usual conditions at 1200 /C176C followed by a heat treatment [5] to
obtain a grain size between 22 lm and 44 lm. The chemical
compositions of the two alloy batches used in the present study
are listed in Table 1 .
The microstructure of the as-received alloy 602 CA consists of a
c-Ni matrix in which Cr-rich carbides are precipitated [15]. These
precipitates were identiﬁed as (Cr ,Ni,Fe)23C6 and (Cr ,Ni,Fe)7C3 as
analyzed in Ref. [17]. According to thermodynamic calculation,
only the M 7C3 is in equilibrium with the c-matrix at the test tem-
perature 1100 /C176C. However, the M 7C3 carbide transforms into
M23C6 upon cooling and is frequently observed in the microscopic
analyses [17]. Other precipitates in the alloy are (Ti,Zr,Y)-carboni-
trides. Based on an analysis of the BSE images, the volume-fraction
of carbide precipitates was found to be 2.9 ± 0.4% in the OWI alloy
batch, and 3.1 ± 0.2% in the FZJ sample material, respectively.
2.2. High temperature exposures
Discontinuous oxidation testing of the OWI specimens was con-
ducted in an electrically heated gas tight furnace at 1100 /C176C. The
gas mixture 2.5O 2–16.4H2O–8.6CO2 (vol.%) balance N 2 was intro-
duced into the furnace with a total volume ﬂow of 97 l/h at room
temperature (RT). At the exposure temperature 1100 /C176C the equi-
librium composition of the atmosphere is not signiﬁcantly differ-
ent from that of the initial gas composition at RT. This
atmosphere corresponds to the ﬂue gas related to the combustion
of natural gas with excess air (air to gas ratio is 1.15).
Rectangular specimens 15 mm /C2 30 mm /C2 2 mm in size were
cut from the sheet material. The surfaces of the samples were
not machined but remained in the as-received condition. Before
exposure the specimens were cleaned in isopropanol and dried in
air.
At the time intervals 700, 1400 and 5470 h samples were cut
from the specimen for metallographic examination. Furthermore,
the experiment was interrupted after 2100, 2800, 3500 and 4460 h.
The FZJ alloy specimens measuring 20 mm /C2 10 mm /C2 2m m
were machined from the delivered 602 CA plate and ground to
1200 grit surface ﬁnish, cleaned in acetone and discontinuously
exposed in laboratory air at 1100 /C176C for up to 1000 h. The oxidation
test was interrupted for weight measurement after 24, 100 and
300 h. At each time interval a sample was removed from the fur-
nace for microstructural analyses.
2.3. Metallographic preparation
Before preparing the metallographic cross sections the oxidized
samples were sputter coated with a thin gold layer to impart the
sample surfaces electrical conductivity. The specimens were subse-
quently electroplated with nickel to obtain a 10–20 lm thick Ni-
layer between the oxide scale and the mounting resin. The plane
of the cross section was aligned perpendicular to the broad surface
of the specimen. The mounted samples were ground to 1200 grit
with SiC grinding papers and subsequently polished with diamond
pastes to 1 lm surface ﬁnish. The ﬁnal polishing step was made
using colloidal SiO 2 slurry.

ﬁles of the elements Cr, Fe, Al and Ni the electron beam was
scanned perpendicular to the sample surface. The measured X-
ray intensities were calibrated by using bulk standards or stan-

2.5. Image analysis
The widths of the IOZ and CFZ in each sample were measured
using image analysis software [23] taking at least 60 counts in a
number of different images. The upper boundary was taken at
the chromia–alloy interface. The lower boundary of the zone was
measured at the position of the deepest precipitate (internal alu-
mina for IOZ or Cr-rich carbide for CFZ) on the measurement line
perpendicular to the scale–alloy interface. The average values were
plotted together with the standard deviation in form of error bars.
The volumes-fractions of the carbides were determined from the
surface area fractions of precipitates in the BSE images. The surface
area fractions of carbides in the two-phase bulk alloy were
assumed to be equal to the respective volume-fractions [24].
2.6. GDOES
Carbon concentrations in the IOZ and the specimen core of the
air exposed sample (FZJ, 1000 h at 1100 /C176C) were determined by
GDOES (Glow Discharge Optical Emission Spectroscopy) using
the HORIBA Jobin–Yvon GD analyzer equipped with a standard
4 mm glow discharge source, a polychromator and a monochro-
matic optical spectrometer. The sputtering conditions were
700 Pa and 60 W. To estimate the carbon concentration in the
IOZ, the GDOES proﬁles were measured by sputtering the oxidized
surface of the specimen. It is well established that reliable quan-
titative GDOES data can be obtained up to a crater depth of
approximately 100 lm (depending on actual alloy system).
Therefore estimation of the carbon content in the specimen core
required a different approach. For this purpose the oxidized
2 mm thick specimen was thinned to about 1 mm by grinding it
with SiC papers. The depth proﬁles were subsequently measured
starting from the ground surface thus representing the center of
the sample. The following procedure was adopted to obtain a
quantitative value for the carbon concentration. The concentra-
tions in the as-received alloy were measured by GDOES. The ratio
of the measured intensities of Ni and C in the as-received alloy was
compared with the ratio of their intensities in the oxidized alloy.
Based on the amount of carbon present in the as-received alloy
known from the chemical analyses ( Table 1 ), the concentration of
carbon in the IOZ and the specimen core was scaled with the ratio
of the measured intensities.
3. Modeling procedure
The coupled thermodynamic–kinetic approach to model the
oxidation induced subscale diffusion and dissolution processes in
the alloy 602 CA was based on the homogenization model pre-
sented by Larsson and Engström [25]. This approach allows the
transformation of a multiphase problem into a single-phase 1D
problem. The sample geometry is discretised into a suitable num-
ber of volume cells. The phase fractions, compositions and chemi-
cal potentials are deﬁned by the equilibrium resulting due to local
element concentrations, temperature and pressure, e.g., in one vol-
ume cell. This assumption of local equilibrium is generally the
basis for virtually all models for multiphase simulations. This
methodology has previously been described by Nijdam and Sloof
[26]. In the present work, the phases c-FCC, M7C3 and M23C6 were
considered for the calculations and diffusion was considered to
occur only in the c-FCC phase.
Successful applications of the homogenization model for diffu-
sion simulations in Ni-base alloys and Ni-base superalloy systems
were described by Nijdam and Sloof [26]. With the homogeniza-
tion approach to model diffusion, the authors simulated the
compositional and phase changes occurring in multiphase alloys
due to the growth of an oxide layer. Additionally, Nijdam and
Sloof [26] evaluated the inﬂuence of alloying additions on the oxi-
dation behavior of MCrAlY coating systems. Larsson and Höglund
[27] successfully used the homogenization model to simulate the
carburisation of two materials, a carbon steel and a Fe–Cr–C alloy.
The model could describe the phase transformation from
a to c
with varying concentration of chromium in the Fe–Cr–C alloy
and the simultaneously occurring carbide precipitation.
For estimating the inﬂuence of the Al depletion in the IOZ on the
diffusion of chromium from the alloy towards the surface, model-
ing of the internal oxidation process is required. Hence, in the cur-
rent work the inﬂuence of internal oxidation was evaluated in
addition to modeling the diffusion processes. The use of an in-
house developed code [28] enabled the setting of additional
boundary conditions within the alloy to model the compositional
changes in the zone of internal oxidation. The homogenization
model was implemented in a FORTRAN program using the
thermodynamic calculation interface of Thermo-Calc [29].
Thermodynamic and kinetic data were taken from the TTNi7 [30]
and MobNi1 databases [31], respectively. The experimentally mea-
sured oxidation kinetics were used to deﬁne the Cr ﬂux as a bound-
ary condition at the oxide-metal interface. The assumption of
symmetry at the center of the sample provided the second spatial
boundary condition of zero ﬂux for all elements. The measured
chemical composition of the alloy in the as-received state was used
as the initial composition at time t = 0. Internal oxidation was
simulated assuming that the velocity of the interface between
the zone of internal oxidation and the alloy is given by the evolu-
tion of the depth of internal oxidation over time. With this bound-
ary condition, a ﬂux of Al, corresponding to the derived Al loss
kinetics, was set at the interface between the IOZ and unaffected
alloy. In each time interval the depth of internal oxidation was
computed and the ﬂux of Al was adjusted accordingly, resulting
in a moving boundary condition in the alloy.
The model provided element concentrations in the alloy matrix
(c-FCC phase), average element concentrations, phase fractions as
a function of time and distance and the evolution of the position of
the interface between the CFZ and the two-phase alloy over time.

5. Discussion
5.1. Thermodynamics of carbide dissolution
As background for elucidating the mechanisms of carbide dis-
solution occurring in a complex multi-component alloy such as
602 CA, the principles of carbide dissolution in an idealized ternary
Ni–Cr–C alloy are discussed ﬁrst. If a NiCr-base alloy containing
carbide precipitates is exposed to an oxidizing environment, two
mechanisms of carbide dissolution in the subsurface zone are
being discussed in literature. For low-pO 2, He–H 2–H2O containing
gases the dominating mechanism for carbide dissolution at high
temperature was found to be decarburization [35–39]. When
exposed to a high-pO 2 gas such as air, two concepts for carbide dis-
solution beneath a growing chromia scale have been discussed in
the literature up to date; decarburization [11] and back diffusion
of carbon triggered by the-Cr depletion as a result of the growth
of external chromia scale. In the latter case, the carbon back diffu-
sion results in additional precipitation of carbides in the core of the
specimen [12,13,17,21,22]. The experimental evidence in the
present work conﬁrms that under the given experimental condi-
tions the carbide re-precipitation mechanism prevails during
high-temperature oxidation of the carbide strengthened alloy
602 CA.
To illustrate the carbide dissolution mechanism in a carbide
containing NiCr-base alloy, the c/c + carbide equilibria in the Ni–
Cr–C system are calculated using Thermo-Calc. The calculated
ternary phase diagram is shown in Fig. 6 together with carbon
isoactivity lines. An isoactivity line is a locus of compositions in
the phase diagram, for which the activity of the selected element
(here carbon) is constant. In the two-phase region the isoactivity
line is equal to the tie-line. The lower isoactivity line in Fig. 6
begins in point A, which corresponds to the Cr and C content in
alloy 602 CA, i.e., 25 and 0.18 wt.%, respectively. This point is
located in the two-phase ﬁeld, in agreement with the experimental
observation that the original alloy microstructure exhibits M
7C3
precipitates in a c-Ni matrix. Based on Thermo-Calc calculations
taking a-Cr and graphite as the reference states for Cr and carbon,
Cr activity in point A equals 0.330 while the carbon activity is
0.042. For the clear explanation of the dissolution mechanism, an
imaginary Cr depletion from 25 to 20 wt.% Cr in the ternary Ni–
Cr–C alloy is assumed. The reaction path representing this Cr
depletion corresponds to the transition from point A to B almost
parallel to the horizontal axis in Fig. 6. If Cr is selectively removed
from the alloy, the concentrations of Ni and C increase accordingly
so that the weight ratio wNi/wC remains constant and the sum of all
concentrations amounts to 100%. Thermodynamic equilibria were
calculated for a number of compositions lying on the depletion
path (red line in Fig. 6 ) and are summarized in Table 2 . The Cr
depletion of the alloy results in an increased carbon solubility
(wmatrix
C ) in the alloy matrix and thus an increased carbon activity
(aC) while the mole-fraction of M 7C3 diminishes with decreasing
Cr content and so does the Cr activity ( aCr). The complete dis-
solution of carbide is predicted to occur once the alloy is depleted
of Cr down to 17 wt.% ( Fig. 6 ).

Carbide dissolution can also be interpreted in terms of carbon
solubility in the alloy matrix. The phase boundary c/c + carbide
in Fig. 6 clearly shows that the carbon solubility in the c-matrix
increases from 0.083 to 0.147 wt.% upon depletion of Cr from 25
to 20 wt.% in the alloy. The corresponding carbide mole-fraction
decreases accordingly from 0.015 to 0.007. The carbon solubility
in the c-matrix of the Cr depleted zone is higher than that in the
c-matrix of the alloy with 25 wt.% Cr. The released carbon will dif-
fuse inwards from the area of high solubility in the subsurface
region to the specimen core where carbon solubility in the alloy
matrix is lower. The back diffusion of carbon will eventually result
in the precipitation of additional carbides in the specimen core in
agreement with experimental observations [19,21].
The mechanism based on the carbon matrix solubility is basi-
cally equal to the dissolution mechanism involving chemical
activities of species. Both interpretations require inward diffusion
of carbon to account for the complete dissolution of carbides in
the Cr depleted subsurface zone. As long as the Cr alloy content
would not decrease beneath 18 wt.%, the CFZ would not form in
the subsurface region unless simultaneous carbon back diffusion
occurs as a result of increased carbon activity in the subsurface
zone due to Cr depletion. According to the thermodynamic calcula-
tions (Table 2), carbon will diffuse back even if the alloy subsurface
is depleted of Cr only by 1 wt.%. To equilibrate such an alloy (Ni–
24Cr–0.182C) with the bulk composition ( aC = 0.042), the carbon
concentration should be decreased to 0.082 wt.% ( Table 2 ). The
alloy Ni–24Cr–0.082C is single-phase ( Fig. 6 ), which implies that
even such a minor Cr depletion will result in the formation of a
CFZ, although the 1 wt.% Cr depletion alone would hardly affect
the amount of carbide in the microstructure ( Table 2 ).
The signiﬁcance of carbon back diffusion for the formation of a
CFZ was recently described for M 6C carbide dissolution during oxi-
dation at 900–1000 /C176C of alloy 625 [40]. In that alloy the dis-
solution of M 6C carbide in the alloy subsurface beneath the
chromia scale was observed although Cr is not the main con-
stituent of the carbide phase, Ni 3Mo3C, and Cr depletion would
be expected not to essentially affect its stability. The reason for
the M 6C dissolution was found to be carbon back diffusion from
the high activity region near the scale–alloy interface towards
the specimen core.

of Al on carbide dissolution will more extensively be illustrated
using thermodynamic–kinetic calculations in the next section.
5.3. Diffusion modeling
The main goal of the thermodynamic–kinetic diffusion model-
ing was to prove a number of assumptions that were made in
the previous sections for interpreting the microstructural phenom-
ena during oxidation of alloy 602 CA. The calculations were
expected to verify: (i) back diffusion of carbon resulting in carbide
enrichment in the core of the specimen; (ii) internal oxidation of Al
promoting back diffusion of carbon and thus carbide dissolution;
(iii) an apparent Cr depletion of the c-matrix in the specimen core
as a result of additional carbide formation.
The calculated Ni, Cr, Fe and Al concentration proﬁles in the c-
Ni matrix of alloy 602 CA after 1000 h oxidation at 1100 /C176C shown
in Fig. 8 a agree well with the EDX measurements from Fig. 4 . The
modeled simultaneous Cr and Al depletion resulted in characteris-
tic diffusion proﬁles of the alloy constituents. Fe and Ni are appar-
ently enriched in the surface-near region due to Cr and Al
consumption. The calculated Cr proﬁle (in the c-matrix) is virtually
uniform in the IOZ and decreases towards the bulk alloy as
observed experimentally ( Fig. 8 a).

cussed in a separate publication [42]. It was concluded that the

6. Conclusions
The presented results have shown that the formation of the
subscale carbide free zone in alloy 602 CA during air or ﬂue gas
exposure at 1100 /C176C cannot be explained only by the occurrence
of oxidation-induced Cr depletion. New experimental and theoreti-
cal evidence based on thermodynamic calculations revealed the
critical role of carbon back diffusion for the oxidation induced dis-
solution of carbides. It is proposed that Cr depletion in the alloy
subsurface increases carbon activity/solubility in the alloy subsur-
face zone, thus promoting back diffusion of carbon towards the
specimen core. Carbide dissolution is thus the consequence of
simultaneous, oxidation driven Cr depletion and inward diffusion
of carbon. Al depletion in the internal oxidation zone imparts an
additional contribution to the carbide dissolution process by
enhancing inward diffusion of carbon. The back diffusion of carbon
leads to the precipitation of additional carbides in the center of the
specimen, which depletes the alloy matrix of Cr in the specimen
core. All microstructural phenomena observed in the present study
were successfully modeled using a new CALPHAD-based
thermodynamic–kinetic approach. The modeling predicted quan-
titatively the element concentration proﬁles, the CFZ growth
kinetics, the inward diffusion of carbon, the carbide enrichment
and Cr depletion of the alloy matrix in the center of the specimen.
The proposed model provides a sound basis for future lifetime
modeling with respect to the chemical degradation of typical, car-
bide containing Ni-base wrought alloys such as 617, 601, or 602 CA
as a result of microstructural changes in these materials during
high temperature exposure.
