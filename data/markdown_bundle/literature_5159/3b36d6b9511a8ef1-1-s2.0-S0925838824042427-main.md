ABSTRACT
Ni-based superalloys exhibit exceptional high-temperature mechanical properties. A specific group, “ Inconel 
superalloys, ” has been a focus of extensive research owing to their ability to withstand high-temperatures, 
making them an inevitable candidate for aerospace applications. Superalloy components are traditionally 
manufactured through casting, forging, and post-machining, leading to material wastage and higher 
manufacturing costs. In this regard, additive manufacturing (AM) is increasingly used for fabricating complex 
shaped Inconel based components for critical aerospace application. AM is particularly advantageous for easy 
fabrication of intricate designs while having high material utilization, and reduced build time, still achieving 
controlled and targeted properties for expensive and difficult-to-machine Inconel superalloy components. 
Nevertheless, the complex layer-by-layer processing technique leads to completely different microstructural 
evolutions with respect to the as-cast counterparts which is also reflected in altered mechanical performance. 
This is particularly crucial considering that Inconel consists of different alloying elements, leading to the for -
mation of multiple phases like γ , γ ′ γ ′ ′ , etc. The current article reports a comprehensive overview of the following 
two topics: (i) commonly practiced AM techniques for fabricating Inconel superalloys and (ii) processing – 
microstructure – mechanical properties correlations for the most studied additively manufactured Inconel su -
peralloys, IN718 , IN625 , IN738LC , and IN939 . Special attention is dedicated in elucidating the influence of heat- 
treatment schedules on both the microstructure and mechanical properties of Inconel superalloys. This extensive 
research would prove beneficial in optimizing the types and processing parameters for AM of Inconel superalloys 
to attain the targeted microstructure, and phases, and also to design the suitable post-manufacturing heat- 
treatment schedule. Such correlation holds significant potential for realizing enhanced mechanical performance 
for additively manufactured Inconel components for industrial applications.
1. Introduction
In the 1940s, Inconel ( IN ) superalloys were developed to facilitate 
the advancement of Whittle Jet Engine for aircraft application [1] . These 
alloys, primarily consisting of Ni and Cr, offer outstanding mechanical 
properties even at elevated temperatures, including a good balance of 
corrosion, oxidation, creep and fatigue resistances even in harsh envi -
ronmental conditions [2 – 4] . Furthermore, contrary to the conventional 
metallic systems, Ni-based superalloys, including Inconel, maintain a 
unique direct relationship between strength and temperature, which is 
commonly termed as strength anomaly [5] . Owing to all these inter -
esting characteristics, IN alloys are predominantly used in applications 
that demand exceptional performance under extreme conditions and 
elevated temperatures, such as in aerospace, gas turbine engines, and 
chemical processing industries, etc.
IN superalloys typically consist of more than ten alloying elements, 
with Ni as the dominant element forming the matrix ( γ matrix), while Cr 
is the second most abundant element along with several other elements 
forming different precipitate and secondary phases [6 – 13] . The γ matrix 
has a face-centered cubic ( FCC ) crystal structure with the space group 
Fm3m (225) [14] . The addition of these elements in proper proportions

2. Necessity for additive manufacturing of Inconel ( AM IN ) 
superalloys
Generally, IN parts are fabricated using casting, forging, and 
machining processes [22] . However, the production cycle often gets 
time-consuming and cost-ineffective due to complex and multiple 
manufacturing steps for intricate shapes. In addition, the machining 
processes involve significant materials wastage, making the components 
further expensive [22] . With the advancement in aerospace technology, 
the shapes and designs of the components that are employed, especially 
in hot sections of the engines, are getting increasingly complicated, 
which makes their fabrication via traditional routes quite challenging 
[39,40] . Furthermore, It is noteworthy that the strength anomaly of IN 
superalloys has made them potential candidates for critical aerospace 
components for elevated temperature applications [41] . However, this 
anomalous trend of increasing strength and hardness with temperature 
acts adversely and poses the following challenges while machining the 
as-cast IN superalloys to the targeted shape [42 – 44] : (i) Increasing work 
hardening rate at par with the strength anomaly – correspondingly, the IN 
superalloy turns harder and stronger as they are worked, which makes it 
extremely difficult to achieve the desired shape and size of the part 
though conventional machining. (ii) High cutting forces – IN is very hard, 
and they are increasingly difficult-to-machine. Hence, the cutting force 
rises to as high as 450 MPa, while temperature increases to ~ 1100 
◦
C. 
These issues result in high tool wear rates, surface damage, increased 
power consumption, short tool life, and high manufacturing costs for 
conventionally manufactured components. (iii) High heat generation – 
machining of IN are associated with significant heat generation, leading 
to thermal damage and dimensional instability of the tools used, and (iv) 
Workpiece deflection – this can lead to dimensional inaccuracies, making 
the fabricated part nonuseful.
Considering these challenges, additively manufactured alloys 
emerge as promising alternatives, and the technique is found suitable for 
fabricating complex components with higher precision out of IN super -
alloys [14,45] . The AM process was developed from the rapid proto -
typing methods in the 1980s that are capable of fabricating 3D structures 
in a layer-by-layer sequence, as opposed to subtractive manufacturing 
technologies [14,22,46 – 48] . This unique feature allows the production 
of complicated geometry parts as per the design, without any need for 
expensive tools such as punches, dies, or casting moulds thereby 
reducing the intermediate processing steps and the scopes for tool 
damage [49] . Overall, AM offers design freedom, dimensional accuracy, 
shorter production cycles, high materials utilization, lower cost, and 
lower emission of harmful gases [50 – 57] , thereby outweighing the 
conventional procedure. In fact, owing to this flexibility in design and 
fabrication capabilities, AM parts are getting attention worldwide and 
have become one of the fastest-growing and most reliable industrial 
techniques [47,58] .
In addition to overcoming the limitations of machining, additively 
manufactured Inconel parts offer several distinct advantages that 
streamline and enhance production efficiency of Inconel superalloys. 
One key benefit is the near-net-shape capability of AM , which allows 
parts to be fabricated close to their final dimensions with minimal ma -
terial wastage, thereby significantly reducing post-processing needs [59, 
60] . Moreover, AM offers lower the buy-to-fly ratio, which is crucial in 
aerospace manufacturing where traditional processes result in high 
material wastage, as most of the initial material is discarded [61] . By 
using AM , this ratio is optimized, ensuring that much of the raw material 
is retained within the final part, and hence substantially reducing the 
production cost making the product economic. AM also enables the 
integration of multiple components into a single piece, effectively 
reducing the number of parts required, which simplifies assembly, 
minimizes potential failure points, and shortens production times [62] .
Collectively, all these benefits of AM allow for a more cost-effective 
and resource-efficient approach for fabricating Inconel components, 
offering valuable alternatives to conventional manufacturing methods. 
Moreover, research into AM -produced components with internal cooling 
channels or optimized structures highlights its potential for more 
effective cooling in high-performance applications like gas turbines and 
aerospace systems [63] . AM enables engineers to design cooling chan -
nels that align with heat load zones, boosting heat transfer and lowering 
weight. Such optimization promotes more even temperature

Advancements in AM technologies have enabled designers to create 
and fabricate complex structures with intricate geometries, unlocking 
new design possibilities. One notable example of this is the lattice 
structure [65 – 67] . Lattice structures are cell structures composed of one 
or more repeating unit cells. Typically, a lattice structure is a porous 
arrangement created by organizing unit cells, and the patterns in this 
structure have a noteworthy impact on its mechanical properties [66,68, 
69] . It is noteworthy that IN superalloys, known for their excellent 
mechanical properties, high corrosion resistance, and high-temperature 
strength, is ideal for high-performance lattice structures. In recent years, 
studying the lattice structures in details for IN superalloys have attracted 
significant attention due to their potential to revolutionize 
high-performance applications, particularly in the aerospace and auto -
motive industries. The lattice structures generated in AM IN superalloys 
leverage the unique properties of the material, such as high-temperature 
stability, corrosion resistance, and exceptional mechanical strength, 
making them ideal for challenging environments. Developing an insight 
on this topic has expanded the understanding and capabilities of these 
materials, which are driven by advancements in AM technologies. 
Recent studies have demonstrated substantial progress in optimizing the 
design and performance of IN lattice structures. A key area of research 
focuses on understanding the mechanical behavior of the lattice struc -
tures formed in AM IN by exploring various designs, unit cell sizes, and 
geometries. Literature studies have identified several critical parameters 
influencing lattice structures, including relative density, structural ge -

distribution within the design space, manufacturing conditions, and 
volume fraction [66,70,71] . These studies emphasize the potential of 
lattice structures in various applications, particularly in aerospace 
lightweight component design. For instance, Trudel et al. [72] achieved

an aircraft bracket using lattice structures optimization technique. These 
examples show how lattice structure optimization can effectively reduce 
weight in aerospace components, contributing to improved fuel effi -
ciency and overall performance. Considering the importance of AM in 
various such aspects, the common methods to fabricate IN superalloys 
are explored further.
3. Routes for additively manufactured Inconel ( AM IN ) 
superalloys
There are seven categories of AM techniques as per the ASTM 52910 
standard [74,75] . A detailed review of over 50 research articles shows 
that the most widely used AM methods for fabricating IN superalloys are 
1. direct energy deposition ( DED ) and 2. powder bed fusion ( PBF ) [14, 
74,76 – 83] . This section will provide basic insights into these two pop -
ular and most widely used AM techniques.
3.1. Direct energy deposition (DED)
DED is defined as an AM process in which focused thermal energy is 
used to fuse materials as they are being deposited [84 – 87] . During the 
process, a component is fabricated by delivering the energy and material 
simultaneously [88] . The schematic of the DED process is shown in 
Fig. 2 .
The raw materials in the form of powder or wire and concentrated 
energy source converge at a central point [89] , as apparent from Fig. 2 . 
The energy density generated at the intersecting point melts the raw 
material in and around that spot leading to melt pool formation [90] .
Typically, thermal energy in the form of laser beam ( LB ), electron 
beam ( EB ), or electric arc ( EA ) is employed during AM . DED is known as 
laser-engineered net shaping ( LENS ) when the laser is used as the energy 
source, electron beam freeform fabrication ( EBF3 ) when electron is used 
as the energy source, and wire arc additive manufacturing ( WAAM ) 
when electric arc is used as the energy source [91] . All these processes 
work based on the same principle but differ in the respective energy 
sources and the form of raw materials used [91] . The procedure is 
usually carried out in a controlled environment with low oxygen levels, 
or occasionally in inert chamber. In case open atmosphere is used for 
fabrication, a shielding gas is passed to prevent contamination of the 
fabricated part with atmospheric gases, like oxygen [92] . The DED 
process has been widely used in the repair, remanufacturing, and 
functional coating of IN-based components.
3.2. Powder bed fusion (PBF)
PBF is a unique AM technique in which a thin layer (typically 20 – 100 
μ m) of powder is spread over the substrate and scanned with a recoater 
roller/scraper, as schematically shown in Fig. 3 . Subsequently, fusion of 
powder materializes through the application of moving focused thermal 
energy, causing them to selectively melt, as per the design [93] . The 
melted powder cools down and solidifies, and the build platform lowers 
by a defined layer thickness. Further layers are added repeatedly and 
fused onto the underlying layers until the desired shape is produced 
[94] .
The thermal energy source in the PBF process could be either a laser 
or an electron beam [95] . When the laser beam is used as an energy 
source, the technique is known as the powder bed fusion-laser beam 
( PBF-LB ). However, the terminology laser-powder bed fusion ( L-PBF ) is 
also equally prevalent and widely acceptable [96] . In most cases, the 
PBF-LB technique uses a continuous wave ( CW ) Yb-fiber laser (Ytter -
bium-doped fiber laser), having a wavelength of 1070 ± 10 nm as a 
source of thermal energy that selectively melts a particular region of the 
powder bed [97] . On the other hand, when electron is used as an energy 
source, the technique is known as powder bed fusion-electron beam 
( PBF-EB ) or most popularly known as electron beam melting ( EBM ) [97, 
98] . In the PBF-EB process, electrons are accelerated from a tungsten 
filament and controlled by magnetic coils. These coils focus the electron

segregation. On the other hand, PBF-EB produces parts with minimal 
residual stress levels [21] .

Among the various AM techniques, PBF-LB has been widely used for the 
fabrication of IN625 [154,163,164] . IN625 , offer significant commercial 
opportunities for AM due to their poor manufacturability by traditional 
methods and their suitability for high-value applications, particularly as 
engineered lattice structures. Research in this area often focuses on 
exploring the mechanical behavior of different lattice designs, unit cell 
sizes, and geometries. For example, Leary et al. [165] has performed a 
comprehensive study to explore how different design parameters influ -
ence the mechanical properties and overall performance of the lattice 
structures of PBF-LB IN625 . The study identifies four critical parameters 
that define the manufacturability of lattice structures: (i) minimum strut 
diameter, (ii) allowable inclination angle, (iii) associated porosity, and 
(iv) pore distribution. Amongst these different parameters, (i) The 
minimum strut diameter refers to the smallest thickness of the struts, 
which are the rod-like elements that form the lattice. This parameter is 
crucial because thinner struts may not fully form, leading to weak points 
in the structure. (ii) The allowable inclination angle is the steepest angle 
at which struts can be fabricated without defects; if the angle is too 
steep, it can result in poor layer adhesion or incomplete strut formation. 
(iii) Porosity refers to the presence of small voids within a material. It 
can help reduce weight, but too much porosity can weaken the structure. 
Researchers quantify the expected porosity levels based on design pa -
rameters and PBF-LB process settings. (iv) Pore distribution describes 
how these voids are spread throughout the lattice structure which 
certainly influences the performance of AM-IN superalloys. While, a 
uniform distribution helps maintaining the mechanical strength, uneven 
distribution can cause stress concentrations and potential failure initi -
ation locations. The study demonstrates that PBF-LB IN625 lattice 
structures exhibit exceptional energy absorption and damage tolerance 
under quasi-static loading conditions both experimentally and numeri -
cally. The research also provides critical insights into how different 
design parameters, such as strut diameter and inclination angle, affect 
the manufacturability and mechanical performance of PBF-LB IN625 
lattice structures. This highlights the potential of these structures for 
applications requiring high energy absorption and damage tolerance, 
paving the way for further optimization of lattice designs in advanced 
engineering applications.

5.4. IN939
Inconel 939 ( IN939 ) is a Ni-Cr alloy that is developed following the 
previous IN superalloy. IN939 offers an outstanding balance of high- 
temperature mechanical performance along with corrosion and oxida -
tion resistance and microstructural stability at temperatures up to 850 
◦
C [7,8,202 – 204] . In fact, IN939 superalloy stands out as one of the 
finest among hot corrosion and oxidation-resistant weldable alloys, with 
desirable mechanical properties [205] . This superalloy distinguishes 
itself with a higher volume fraction of the precipitation hardening phase 
and thus exhibits superior corrosion resistance as compared to the 
IN738LC [206] . IN939 superalloy finds a wide range of applications in 
aircraft engines (turbine blades and exhaust nozzles), power generation 
industries, oil and gas drilling equipment (drill bits), nuclear reactors, 
etc. [203,207] . It is an alloy system with its nominal composition pro -
vided in Table 8 [94,208 – 210] . The alloy shows poor weldability due to 
the higher content of Al and Ti and is susceptible to generating weld 
cracks, as reported in the literature [211] . The grain boundary type 
cracks are noted to initiate from the boundary between the weld metal 
and heat affected zone ( HAZ ) and propagate into the base metal, thereby 
making the superalloy prone to fracture [8,212] . Conventional casting 
techniques are limited with regard to the complexity of components, 
thus limiting the operating range and efficiency. Therefore, novel 
manufacturing processes such as AM techniques, which broaden design 
possibilities, have become a focal point in IN939 research [213] . 
Nevertheless, there are only a few studies in the literature on the 
microstructure and mechanical properties of AM IN939 . All these studies

6. Summary on insights developed
It is noteworthy that Inconel ( IN ) superalloys have gained popularity 
owing to their exceptional high-temperature mechanical performance 
along with significant resistance to oxidation, and corrosion. These 
unique combination of characteristics have made them ideal candidates 
for crucial applications such as turbine blades, combustion chambers, 
and other critical components that are exposed to elevated temperatures 
and mechanical stress. Historically, IN superalloys were primarily 
manufactured through conventional methods, including casting and 
forging. While these processes have produced durable and high- 
performing materials, they come with limitations, particularly in 
terms of producing complex geometries or internal structures. On the 
other hand, through recent progress in the manufacturing methods, it is 
realized that additive manufacturing ( AM ) techniques expand the ho -
rizon for the ease of component fabrication. These techniques, especially 
PBF and DED have revolutionized the production of complex geometries 
of high strength metallic systems that were once difficult or even 
impossible to achieve using traditional manufacturing methods like 
casting, forging, machining. This capability of AM allows for the fabri -
cation of parts with high dimensional accuracy, significant design 
freedom, and reduced material wastage. Certainly, these are the key 
advantages for industries that prioritize efficiency and material perfor -
mance in extreme conditions. As a result, AM is increasingly being uti -
lized for the production of IN superalloys. It is noted through the 
thorough literature survey that AM offers new possibilities by allowing 
the integration of intricate designs such as lattice structures of IN , which 
can reduce the overall weight of a part without sacrificing strength or 
durability. Furthermore, AM processes generally offer shorter lead times 
and lower material costs, enabling manufacturers to rapidly iterate on 
new designs and improve upon existing ones without the long produc -
tion cycles that are typical of traditional methods. Hence, overall pro -
duction time or in other words, component cost reduces, which has a 
huge industrial significance for IN superalloys.
This article presents an in-depth review of the existing literature, 
focusing on the microstructural evolution and mechanical performance 
of four key IN superalloys, namely, IN718, IN625, IN738LC , and IN939 , 
produced through AM techniques. The primary objective of the study is 
to highlight how advanced AM processes, particularly PBF and DED 
techniques, are transforming the fabrication of these superalloys. These 
alloys are critical in industries like aerospace, energy, and defense, 
where high-performance materials capable of enduring extreme envi -
ronments are essential. A detailed survey of the documented research 
highlights several insights regarding the impact of AM processing pa -
rameters and necessity and optimization of subsequent heat treatments 
on microstructure and mechanical properties for the concerned IN 
superalloys: 
• Processing parameters such as laser energy density and scanning 
strategy, greatly impact microstructural evolution including phase 
distribution and porosity generation in AM IN718 superalloys.

in refining the microstructures, eliminating unwanted phases (e.g., 
Laves, δ ), and enhance mechanical properties. Post-processing 
techniques like HIP, thereby reducing residual stresses and 
porosity, is crucial for improving material integrity and performance 
in practical applications.
• Variation in the processing parameters of PBF and DED techniques 
profoundly influence microstructure generation in AM IN625 su -
peralloy, impacting dendritic structures and phase distribution (e.g., 
Laves and δ ). Key heat-treatments like stress relief, solution treat -

7. Future research direction in AM superalloys
The future of AM IN superalloys presents numerous opportunities for 
advancing high-performance materials, especially in industries 
requiring exceptional mechanical properties and performance in 
extreme environments. However, there remain significant research gaps 
that need to be addressed to fully harness the potential of these specially 
manufactured superalloys. Future research in this field should focus on 
several crucial areas, including (i) microstructural optimization, (ii) 
development of lattice structures, (iii) refinement of process parameters, 
(iv) advancements in post-processing techniques, (v) application- 
specific performance testing (vi) powder development, optimization, 
recycling and (vii) in-situ monitoring and real-time AM process control. 
These directions will provide the foundation for addressing current 
challenges and unlocking the full potential of AM superalloys. 
(i) One of the key areas of future research is microstructural opti -
mization and control. The unique thermal conditions during AM 
processes, such as rapid heating and cooling, often result in non- 
equilibrium microstructures that differ from those generated in 
conventionally processed materials. These microstructural char -
acteristics directly influence the mechanical properties of super -
alloys, including their strength, ductility, and resistance to high- 
temperature deformation. To improve the performance of AM 
superalloys, future research should focus on better understanding 
phase formation during solidification, particularly the formation 
of secondary phases and precipitates, which play a critical role in 
strengthening mechanisms. Investigating how varying AM pro -
cess parameters affect phase formation, grain size, and texture 
could enable engineers to design superalloys with tailored mi -
crostructures for specific applications. Research could also focus 
on developing strategies to control grain size and distribution, as 
finer and more uniform grains typically enhance mechanical 
performance, particularly at elevated temperatures.
(ii) Another critical area for future research is the optimization of 
lattice structures in AM superalloys. Lattice structures are a 
defining feature of AM, offering unique opportunities for 
reducing the weight of components while maintaining high 
strength and stiffness. However, much remains to be explored 
regarding how different lattice geometries impact the mechanical 
behavior of superalloys under various loading conditions. While 
there is already some research on the mechanical behavior of 
lattice structures, there is limited work on how lattice designs can 
be optimized for specific superalloys like IN738LC and IN939. 
Further research should aim to develop computational models 
that can predict the performance of different lattice geometries 
under high-temperature and high-stress conditions. This will help 
tailor lattice designs for specific applications, such as aerospace 
components, where lightweight structures with optimized me -
chanical properties are critical.
(iii) Refinement of process parameters is another important avenue 
for future research in AM superalloys. The properties of AM- 
fabricated parts are highly sensitive to the process parameters 
used during manufacturing, including laser power, scanning 
speed, layer thickness, and powder properties. Variations in these 
parameters can result in defects such as porosity, residual 
stresses, and anisotropy, which negatively affect the performance 
of the material. Therefore, future research should focus on opti -
mizing these parameters to minimize defects and ensure the 
consistent production of high-quality components. For example, 
adjusting the laser scanning strategy or powder bed conditions 
could help reduce residual stress and prevent crack formation 
during the build process. Additionally, the development of 
advanced in-situ monitoring techniques could provide real-time 
feedback during the build process, enabling manufacturers to 
detect defects early and make necessary adjustments. This could 
lead to the implementation of closed-loop control systems that 
automatically refine process parameters to ensure optimal part 
quality and consistency.
(iv) Post-processing treatments, particularly heat-treatments, will 
also play a crucial role in the future development of AM super -
alloys. The AB microstructure of AM components is often char -
acterized by high levels of internal stress and non-equilibrium 
phases, which can be detrimental to the material’s performance. 
Heat-treatments are used to relieve these stresses and stabilize the 
microstructure by promoting the precipitation of strengthening 
phases, such as the γ‘ phase in Ni-based superalloys. However, the 
traditional heat-treatments used for cast and wrought superalloys 
may not be optimal for AM IN materials due to their distinct 
thermal histories. Future research should focus on developing 
heat-treatment protocols specifically designed for AM IN super -
alloys to maximize their mechanical properties. For instance, 
multi-stage heat-treatments could be investigated to refine the 
microstructure and improve the distribution of precipitates. 
Surface treatments, such as laser polishing or shot peening, 
should also be explored to improve surface finish and fatigue 
resistance, which are critical for the long-term performance of 
superalloy components in demanding environments.
(v) Application-specific performance testing will be another crucial 
research direction. While many studies have focused on the me -
chanical properties of AM superalloys at room temperature, there 
is a need for more extensive testing under real-world service 
conditions. This includes evaluating the high-temperature prop -
erties of AM IN superalloys, such as creep resistance, oxidation 
behavior, and fatigue life, which are essential for their use in gas 
turbines, jet engines, and other high-temperature applications. In 
particular, the anisotropic behavior of AM parts due to the layer- 
by-layer fabrication process presents a challenge for their use in 
cyclic loading environments. Future research should focus on 
understanding the fatigue crack initiation and propagation 
mechanisms in AM superalloys under both uniaxial and multi -
axial stress states. Additionally, it is important to assess the long- 
term durability of AM components, particularly in aggressive 
environments where corrosion and oxidation can severely 
degrade material performance.
(vi) Further research is also needed in the area of powder develop -
ment and recycling. The quality of the powder used in AM IN 
fabrication has a direct impact on the mechanical properties of 
the final product. Future studies should focus on developing 
powders with optimized particle size distribution, flowability, 
and chemical composition. Research into novel alloying elements 
or grain refiners could lead to improved superalloy powders that 
offer enhanced mechanical properties and oxidation resistance. 
Additionally, the issue of powder recycling is a significant 
concern for the cost-effectiveness of AM processes. Understand -
ing how multiple reuse cycles affect powder properties and the 
quality of the final part will be essential for the sustainable 
adoption of AM technologies in industrial applications.
(vii) Last but not the least, in-situ monitoring and real-time process 
control are promising areas for future research. The ability to 
monitor the AM process in real-time and make adjustments to 
process parameters based on feedback from sensors could 
significantly improve the quality and reliability of AM compo -
nents. Technologies such as thermal imaging, acoustic sensing, 
and X-ray imaging could be integrated into AM machines to 
detect defects as they form, allowing for immediate corrective 
actions. This could also enable the development of closed-loop 
control systems that continuously refine the process parameters
