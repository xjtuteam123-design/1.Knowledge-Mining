Molecular Dynamics Study on Characteristics of 
Misfit Dislocations in Ni-Based Superalloys

and Yoshihiro Tomita 1,d

Keywords: Ni-Based Superalloys, Microstructure, Misfit Dislocation, Interfacial Dislocation Network, 
Molecular Dynamics 
Abstract. By using molecular dynamics simulation, misfit dislocation networks are made on 
semi-coherent interfaces in a laminate structure of Ni and Ni 3Al single crystals. The core structure of 
the networks is discussed in detail, focusing on the different atomic configuration at the interfaces; e.g. 
with or without Al atoms on the Ni 3Al side. It is revealed that the networks can be a source of partial 
dislocation loops under the external loading; however, the loops tend to form immobile wedge-like 
stacking faults, analogous to the stacking fault tetrahedron (SFT), near the interface with Al atoms. On 
the other hand, the loops propagate into both Ni and Ni 3Al phases, from the network dislocations on 
the interface without Al atoms. 
Introduction 
Interfacial dislocation networks can be found in the rafted γ/γ' microstructure of the fourth-generation 
Ni-based superalloys [1]. It is also reported that the mesh spacing of the networks could be correlated 
with the creep tolerance; e.g. the finer mesh, the longer lifetime. We have so far tried to model the 
cutting behavior of the networks by using 3D discrete dislocation dynamics simulation [2]; however, 
there is no knowledge on the short-range or core-level interaction between the "phantom " misfit 
dislocations at the semi-coherent interface and the real ones gliding in the γ matrix. Many molecular 
dynamics (MD) studies, including ours [3], have been devoted only to the coherent interface since the 
lattice misfit is so small that we cannot obtain the semi-coherent interfaces which minimize the strain 
energy of the entire microstructure due to the computational limitation. We have thus tried to make 
semi-coherent interfaces in a periodic simulation cell of Ni and Ni 
3Al bicrystal, by subtracting the 
lattice planes from the Ni 3Al phase. Although the boundary condition does not coincide with that of 
the local part in real superalloys, misfit dislocations are actually formed on the interfaces after the 
initial relaxation. We have then discussed the core structure and motion of misfit dislocations, 
focusing on the presence of Al atoms at the interface. 
Simulation procedure 
The interatomic potential adopted here is the embedded atom method (EAM), of which parameters 
were proposed by Voter [4]. As shown in Fig. 1, the periodic boundary conditions are applied on a 
cuboidal simulation cell of Ni and Ni 
3Al bicrystal to represent an infinite laminate structure. The 
Ni 3Al phase is composed of 49x49x25 unit lattices of the L1 2 structure while the Ni phase 50x50x25 
fcc unit lattices. The lattice parameters for Ni and Ni 3Al, aγ and aγ', are set to 0.350nm and 0.357nm, 
respectively, satisfying 50 aγ=49 aγ' in the initial configuration to avoid the misfit concentration at the 
periodic boundaries. The lattice misfit, δ=( aγ'−aγ)/ aγ, is thus calculated at 0.02, which is one order of 
magnitude larger than that of real superalloys. The laminate structure has two different interfaces, one

Results and discussion 
Core structure in the initial equilibrium. As previously shown in Fig. 1(b), the networks are 
formed as the inscribed lines in the [110] and [ 
110] directions. The line directions coincide with 
experimental observations; however, the net spacing solely depends on the cell size and the number of 
lattice planes [6]. In order to investigate the core structure carefully, Fig. 2 illustrates the atom 
position and migration by arrows, on each (001) layer in the quarter of both interfaces. The origin of 
the arrows indicates the position of atoms after relaxation; the direction and length do the migration 
during relaxation. The thickness of the arrows also shows the magnitude of migration. We can find 
most clear "groove" on the 2nd layer of IF 1, or the (001) plane of Ni 3Al phase without Al atoms. The 
grooving is suppressed at IF 2, or the (001) plane of Ni 3Al phase with Al atoms. Compared to these 
changes in the Ni 3Al side, we cannot find remarkable disorder in the Ni side; however, the atoms near 
the network nodes on the IF 1 migrate along the dislocation lines, as shown in the lower right and 
upper left in Fig. 2(c). Similar migration is also observed in the other nodes on the plane, resulting in 
the clockwise circuit in the entire network. Summarizing these atomic configurations, it is deduced 
that the misfit dislocation has basically the edge character since the lattices expand in the normal 
direction against the dislocation line. It is also suggested that the network nodes on IF 1 involve the 
screw component, from the fact that the atoms migrate parallel to the dislocation line. 
Tensile simulation. Fig. 3(a) shows the stress-strain response under tension. In the simulation, the 
strain is fixed when the stress shows lower value than the previous step, to bring the system to its 
energy minimum (quasi-static loading). Thus the stress rapidly drops to zero at the strain of εzz =0.117, 
after showing the maximum stress of σzz =18.5GPa. Figs. 3(b)~(e) are the internal dislocation structure 
visualized by CNA and Figs. 3(f)~(i) the side view of the simulation cell. The captured points of each 
figure are indicated on the stress-strain curve. The initial morphology of the network is maintained up 
to the strain of 0.075; however, the dislocation core becomes flat and wide along the interface by the 
straining (Fig. 3(c)). Then leading partials nucleate from the networks and propagate into Ni phase (IF 
1) and into Ni 3Al phase (IF 2), as indicated with the arrows in Fig. 3(d). We can also find the stacking

Summary 
For a new insight on the structure and motion of misfit dislocation at the γ/γ' interface in Ni-based 
superalloys, semi-coherent interfaces are made in a laminate structure of Ni and Ni 
3Al, the former has 
an extra plane against the later, by using the molecular dynamics simulation. Misfit dislocation 
networks are actually found on the interfaces with the line directions of the [110] and [ 110], as 
reported in experiments. The Burgers vector of the misfit dislocations is clarified by the atom 
configuration, focusing on the presence of Al atoms at the interface. Tension is then applied normal to 
the interfaces, revealing the fact that the networks can be a source of partial dislocation loops. 
However, there is a remarkable difference in the propagation of the loops, depending on the atomic 
configuration at the interfaces. The loops tend to form immobile wedge-like stacking faults, 
analogous to the stacking fault tetrahedron (SFT), at the interface with Al atoms. On the other hand, 
the loops propagate into both Ni and Ni 3Al phases, from the interface without Al atoms.
