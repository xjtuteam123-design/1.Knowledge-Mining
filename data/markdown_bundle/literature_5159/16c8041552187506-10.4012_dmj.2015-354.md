INTRODUCTION
Ti exhibits good biocompatibility and fine corrosion 
resistance and is used extensively for dental implants 
and dentures in dentistry. However, the strength of 
unalloyed Ti is insufficient for dental prostheses that 
need to have comparatively high strength, such as 
clasps and bridges
1,2). In recent years, narrow-diameter 
dental implants have been developed for use in cases 
where the bone width and height are inadequate as 
well as in minimally invasive treatments. As a result, 
there is a need for developing higher-strength implant 
materials
3,4).
We have developed several experimental binary Ti 
alloys and investigated their properties 5-8). We found 
that several of them are suitable for use in dental 
applications. The tensile strengths, yield strengths, and 
Vickers hardnesses of the Ti-Nb alloys and Ti-Cu alloys 
were higher than those of Ti
5,6). The strengths of both 
Ti-30 mass%Nb (hereafter, “%” stands for “mass%”) and 
Ti-5%Cu were more than twice that of Ti. Further, the 
elongations after fracture for Ti-30%Nb and Ti-5%Cu 
were approximately 10 and 5%, respectively
5,6). On the 
other hand, the mechanisms responsible for the improved 
mechanical properties of Ti-30%Nb and Ti-5%Cu 
differed. The phase transformation from an α-phase to β- 
and ω-phases probably contributed to the improvement 
in the properties of Ti-30%Nb
5), while the solid-solution 
strengthening of the α-Ti phase and the precipitation 
of the microscopic intermetallic compound Ti 2Cu was 
probably responsible for the improved characteristics 
of Ti-5%Cu 6). The yield strengths and elongations of 
the 30%Nb- and 5%Cu-containing alloys satisfied the 
standards for hardened type 4 dental casting gold alloys
9) 
and dental base metal casting alloys 10), suggesting that 
these alloys can be used for partial dentures and clasps. 
Owing to the poor machinability of Ti11), it is difficult 
to cut, grind, and polish Ti dental prostheses. We have 
previously investigated the grindability (i.e., the ease of 
grinding) of the above-described experimental binary 
alloys using SiC wheels
5,12). The grindability of Ti-30%Nb 
was notably higher than that of Ti at low grinding 
speeds
5), while that of Ti-5%Cu was significantly higher 
than that of Ti at high grinding speeds 12). The common 
features of alloys that exhibited good grindability were 
not only their smaller elongations but also the existence 
of a secondary phase, such as an ω-phase or Ti
2Cu, in 
the matrix. The secondary phase probably acts as a local 
brittle zone, in a manner similar to the free-machining 
inclusions in free-machined materials 13). Ternary alloys 
formed by including an alloying element in these binary 
alloys may exhibit good grindability over a wider range 
of grinding speeds. 
According to the equilibrium phase diagram of 
the Ti-Nb system, this system can be classified as one 
with continuous β-phase solid solubility and partial 
miscibility in the α-phase14)(Fig. 1). Alloys with a Nb 
concentration of up to 4% are located within the α-region 
at 400°C, whereas alloys with Nb concentrations of 
4–56% are located within the α+β region 14). The Ti-
Cu system is classified as a system that exhibits an 
eutectoid transformation 14)(Fig. 2). In the case of the 
alloy, the eutectoid point of α-Ti and Ti 2Cu at 7.0% Cu 
is at 790°C, and the range for the eutectoid reaction is 
2.1–39.9% Cu
14). By contrast, the phases present in the 
dental cast alloys Ti-30%Nb and Ti-5%Cu are the β- and 
ω-phases5) and a single α-phase15), respectively, because 
the alloy phases formed as a result of nonequilibrium 
solidification are not always in agreement with the 
equilibrium phase diagram. The Cu-Nb system is an 
immiscible system, since the solid-solubility limit of Nb 
in Cu (α solid solution) is 0.15% and the limit of Cu in Nb

The mechanical properties —tensile strength, yield strength, elongation after fracture, and Vickers hardness— and alloy phases 
of the dental cast alloys Ti-6%Nb-4%Cu, Ti-18%Nb-2%Cu, and Ti-24%Nb-1%Cu were investigated. Ti-6%Nb-4%Cu consisted of a 
single α-phase, while Ti-18%Nb-2%Cu and Ti-24%Nb-1%Cu consisted of α- and β-phases. The tensile strengths, yield strengths, and 
hardnesses of these alloys were higher than those of Ti-5%Cu and Ti-30%Nb; however, their breaking elongations were smaller. 
These differences in the mechanical properties are attributable to solid-solution strengthening or to precipitation strengthening 
by the dual-phase (α+β ) structure. Thus, Ti-Nb-Cu alloys are suitable for use in high-strength dental prostheses, such as implant-
retained superstructures and narrow-diameter implants.

Ti-24%Nb-1%Cu as candidate compositions for use in 
dental applications (Fig. 3). The Ti-Nb-Cu alloys were 
prepared, and their castings were evaluated in terms of 
their microstructures and mechanical properties.
MATERIALS AND METHODS
Preparation of specimens
As mentioned above, Ti-6%Nb-4%Cu (6Nb4Cu), Ti-
18%Nb-2%Cu (18Nb2Cu), and Ti-24%Nb-1%Cu 
(24Nb1Cu) alloys were prepared in this study. The 
desired amounts of Ti sponge (>99.8%, grade S-90, 
Osaka Titanium Technologies, Amagasaki, Japan), 
Nb (>99.9%, H.C. Starck, Goslar, Germany), and Cu 
(>99.99%, Research Institute for Electric and Magnetic 
Materials, Sendai, Japan) were melted in an Ar-arc 
melting furnace (TAM-4S, Tachibana Riko, Sendai, 
Japan) to form the alloy ingots (15 g each). The ingots 
were melted six times in total; they were inverted 
five times to ensure homogeneity, as was done in our 
previous studies
5-8). 
The ingots of each Ti-Nb-Cu alloy were cast into 
testing specimens using a magnesia investment 
(Selevest CB, Selec, Osaka, Japan) in an Ar gas-
pressure dental casting machine (Castmatic-S, Iwatani, 
Osaka, Japan) at 200°C and then bench-cooled. The 
specimens for the X-ray diffraction (XRD) experiments,

X-ray diffractometry and microstructural observations
XRD analyses were performed at room temperature 
using Cu Kα radiation generated at 30 kV and 10 mA 
in an X-ray diffractometer (Miniflex CN2005, Rigaku, 
Tokyo, Japan). In addition to the Ti-Nb-Cu alloys, 
specimens of Ti, Ti-30%Nb (30Nb), and Ti-5%Cu (5Cu), 
which were fabricated in the previous studies
5,6), were 
also analyzed. The peaks in the XRD patterns were 
indexed to an X-ray polycrystalline powder diffraction 
file (PDF-2, JCPDS-ICDD 2004).
The specimen surfaces were polished and etched 
with an etching solution (1.0 mL HF, 4.0 mL HNO
3, and 
300 mL H 2O), and the etched surfaces were observed 
using an optical microscope (PMG3-614U, Olympus, 
Tokyo, Japan). 
Tensile tests
Tensile tests were carried out using a universal testing 
machine (DSS-2000, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at a 
crosshead speed of 0.5 mm min
−1 at room temperature 
(n=6). The ultimate tensile strengths, yield strengths 
(proof strength of 0.2% nonproportional extension), 
and elongations after fracture of the specimens were 
determined. The fractured surfaces were observed using 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-6060, JEOL, 
Tokyo, Japan). 
Hardness tests
The Vickers hardnesses of the specimens were 
determined using a micro Vickers hardness tester (HM-
102, Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan); a load of 1.961 N 
and dwell time of 30 s were used. The tests (n=9) were 
conducted at three randomly chosen points on each 
specimen. 
Statistical analysis
The obtained data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA 
and Scheffé’s statistical tests at a significance level of 
α=0.05.
RESULTS
X-ray diffractometry and metallography
The XRD patterns of the Ti-Nb-Cu alloys, Ti, 30Nb, and 
5Cu are shown in Fig. 4. The peaks in the XRD patterns 
for Ti, 5Cu, and 6Nb4Cu corresponded well with those 
for α-Ti; however, no peaks corresponding to Ti
2Cu were 
found. The peaks for 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu matched 
the characteristic peaks of α-Ti and β-Ti. For 18Nb2Cu, 
the intensities of the α-related peaks were high, while 
those of the β-related peaks were low. For 24Nb1Cu, 
the intensities of the β-related peaks were high, while 
those of the α-related peaks were low. A peak suspected 
to be related to the ω-phase existed at approximately 
80°. However, since only one such peak was observed, 
the presence of the ω-phase could not be confirmed. The 
peaks in the pattern for 30Nb were related to the β- and 
ω-phases, as was also reported previously
5). 
The microstructures of the etched Ti-Nb-Cu alloys 
are shown in Fig. 5. Acicular structures, which are 
characteristic of α-Ti, were observed in the grains of 
6Nb4Cu. However, the intermetallic compound Ti 2Cu 
was not seen. Dendritic structures were observed in 
the cases of 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu. Both light-colored 
structures in Fig. 5b and dark-colored structures in Fig. 
5c are equiaxed dendrites, corresponding to the primary 
crystals. In 18Nb2Cu (Fig. 5b´), precipitated fine 
acicular α phase was observed in the equiaxed grains and 
grain boundaries. In 24Nb1Cu (Fig. 5c´), precipitated fine 
acicular α phase was observed between the dendrites. 
Tensile properties and fractography
The values of the yield strength, tensile strength, and 
elongation to failure for each alloy tested are shown in 
Fig. 6, including the data for Ti, 30Nb, and 5Cu obtained 
in previous studies
5,6). The tensile strengths and yield 
strengths of the Ti-Nb-Cu alloys were significantly 
higher (p<0.01) than those of Ti. The tensile strength 
of 18Nb2Cu (858 MPa) was the highest and was 
approximately 2.5 times higher than that of Ti. The 
tensile strengths of both 6Nb4Cu and 24Nb1Cu were 
greater than 750 MPa and more than twice that of Ti.

DISCUSSION
It has been reported that dental castings of Ti-Nb and 
Ti-Cu alloys consist of the following phases: Ti-Nb 
alloys containing up to 10%Nb contain an α-phase but 
not a β-phase
5); Ti-Cu alloys with 5% Cu contain an 
α-phase; and alloys with more than 10% Cu contain an 
α-phase and Ti
2Cu15). In the present study, the 6Nb4Cu 
specimen showed acicular structures, and XRD analysis 
showed that it consists of an α-phase. Furthermore, 
its microstructure resembled that of 5Cu reported by 
Takada and Okuno
15). Therefore, it was concluded that 
the cast 6Nb4Cu alloy consisted of a single α-phase, 
even though its alloy phases at the equilibrium state are 
unknown. In the case of the microstructure of 6Nb4Cu 
(Fig. 5a), the grains were formed because of an L→β 
transformation, while the acicular structures were 
formed because of a β→α transformation.
Since the 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu alloys have 
greater amounts of Nb and smaller amounts of Cu 
compared to 6Nb-4Cu, we assume that they formed 
microstructures similar to those of Ti-Nb alloys with 
similar amounts of Nb. 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu 
contained dendritic structures (Fig. 5b and c) and 
consisted of α- and β-phases, in keeping with the Ti-
Nb phase diagram. In general, a wider solidification 
range increases the tendency to segregate and form 
dendrites
16). As shown in Fig. 1, for the Ti-Nb system, 
the solidification range is very narrow; however, the 
gradients of both the liquidus and the solidus lines 
are considerably small. Further, the cooling rate of 
dental castings is relatively high. Consequently, the 
composition of Ti-Nb castings may differ greatly, with 
the castings being composed of primary crystals or a 
final solidification part. Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 
2, the addition of Cu to Ti broadens the solidification 
range. Therefore, in Ti-Nb-Cu castings, during the 
L→β transformation, the composition probably varied 
significantly, changing from the primary crystals (Nb-
rich β-phase) to the final solidification part (Nb-lean 
β-phase). The α-phase was precipitated or formed by the 
transformation of the β-phase at temperatures lower 
than 882°C (Fig. 1). 
It should be noted that the microstructures were 
different between the 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu alloys. 
This difference was probably caused by the difference 
of the Nb concentration in β-phase, especially in the 
primary crystals. In 18Nb2Cu, the fine acicular α phase 
precipitated in the grains of β phase or transformed from 
β, which resembled solution-treated and aged Ti-15V-
3Cr-3Al-3Sn alloy
16). In 24Nb1Cu, on the other hand, 
the dendritic equiaxed structures of Nb-rich β were 
retained, and the fine acicular α phase precipitated in 
the Nb-lean β, which was present between the dendrites. 
Although both 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu contain α and β, 
the structure of 18Nb2Cu was the precipitated α phase in 
β phase (mostly α) while that of 24Nb1Cu was composed 
of the retained β crystals (primary crystals) and the 
precipitated α phase in β crystals. The intensity of the 
α-phase peak was strong, and that of the β-phase peak 
was weak in the XRD pattern of 18Nb2Cu. The opposite 
result was obtained for 24Nb1Cu. These results support 
the above-mentioned mechanism.
The strengths of all the Ti-Nb-Cu alloys were 
higher than those of 5Cu and 30Nb. Compared to the 
strengths of the previously reported experimental Ti 
alloys containing 20% elements, namely Ti-20%Au (548 
MPa)
8) and Ti-20%Ag (543 MPa) 6) (because the total 
concentration of the alloying elements in 18Nb2Cu 
was 20%), that of 18Nb2Cu was higher by 1.5 times or 
more. Similarly, the strength of 24Nb1Cu, in which the 
total concentration of the alloying elements was 25%, 
was higher than that of Ti-25%Ag (694 MPa)
7). These 
Ti-Au and Ti-Ag alloys consisted of an α-phase and 
intermetallic compounds such as Ti3Au and Ti2Ag, while 
the alloys 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu consisted of both the 
α-phase and the β-phase. Precipitation strengthening by 
the dual-phase (α+β) structure, as well as solid-solution 
strengthening by the α- and/or β-phases, probably 
contributed to the high strengths of these two alloys. 
Judging from the highest strength value of 18Nb2Cu, 
the precipitated α phase in β structure in this alloy 
mainly contributed to improvement in strength by 
precipitation strengthening. Such Ti alloys could exhibit 
even higher strengths after heat treatment
17). Moreover, 
compared to the Ti-Nb alloys with similar amounts of 
Nb, the strengths of 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu were higher 
than those of Ti-20%Nb (704 MPa) and Ti-25%Nb (696 
MPa)
5), respectively. Cu probably enhanced the strength

of the Ti-Nb alloys through solid-solution strengthening. 
However, the strengths of 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu were 
slightly lower than that of Ti-6Al-4V (930 MPa), which is 
a typical Ti alloy consisting of α- and β-phases
18).
The improved strength of 6Nb4Cu is attributable 
mainly to the solid-solution strengthening of α-Ti. 
The strength of 6Nb4Cu was 1.6 times or more that 
of the experimental α-type alloys in which the total 
concentration of the alloying elements was 10%, namely, 
the alloys Ti-10%Au (443 MPa)
8), Ti-10%Ag (454 MPa)6), 
and Ti-10%Nb (473 MPa) 5). The ternary solid-solution 
was more effective in improving strength than was the 
binary solid-solution. 6Nb4Cu is an excellent α-type 
Ti alloy and similar to high-strength α-type Ti such as 
commercially pure (CP) grade 4 Ti (550–750 MPa)
19) 
and ASTM grade 4 Ti (>550 MPa) 1) in terms of its 
properties. 
Dimples, which are a feature of ductile fractures, 
were observed in all the Ti-Nb-Cu alloys, and, in 
particular, in 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu. However, their 
elongations were not significant. Since pores were 
observed at the fractured surfaces of the castings, 
the porosity probably decreased the elongation and 
strength. The casting pores were likely due to the above-
mentioned wide solidification range. In the case of this 
alloy, wrought specimens are therefore preferable to 
cast ones.
The β-Ti phase of a body-centered cubic structure 
usually has a higher ductility than that of an α-Ti phase 
of a hexagonal close-packed structure
1). However, the 
elongation of 24Nb1Cu (β-rich α+β alloy) did not differ 
significantly from those of the other Ti-Nb-Cu alloys (α 
or α-rich α+β alloy). It is possible that a small ω-phase, 
which is known to be brittle, existed in some of the 
specimens, resulting in the elongation being low and 
having a large variation, even though the ω-phase 
was not identified in 24Nb1Cu using XRD, as only 
one corresponding peak was present. If 24Nb1Cu is 
solution treated and the ω-phase is suppressed, it may 
exhibit higher ductility. The elongation values of the 
Ti-Nb-Cu alloys satisfy the criteria for type 4 and type 
5 metallic materials
2) as well as for dental base metal 
casting alloys10) and are similar to that of cast Ti-6Al-4V 
(approximately 3–5%)20,21). 
Although the strengths of the Ti-Nb-Cu alloys were 
higher than those of 5Cu and 30Nb, the yield strengths 
of the ternary alloys were similar to those of the binary 
alloys. The yield strengths of the Ti-Nb-Cu alloys satisfy 
the ISO 22674 criteria for type 4 metallic materials
2). 
The Ti-Nb-Cu alloys are therefore suitable materials for 
implant-retained superstructures. Further, the Ti-Nb-
Cu alloys can be used for narrow-diameter implants, 
since their strengths match that of the CP grade 4 Ti 
which is the strongest in pure Ti. 
The hardness values could be statistically divided 
into the following four groups: unalloyed (Ti), solution 
hardening of the α-phase (5Cu and 6Nb4Cu), solution 
hardening and precipitation hardening of the α- and 
β-phases (24Nb1Cu and 18Nb2Cu), and solution 
hardening of the β-phase and precipitation hardening 
of the β-phase and ω-phase (30Nb). The hardness 
of 6Nb4Cu was similar to that of hardened type 4 
dental gold casting alloys (237–264)
18). Further, the 
hardnesses of 18Nb2Cu and 24Nb1Cu were similar to 
that of Ti-6Al-4V (320–341)
18,22) but lower than those 
of Co-Cr alloys (350–390) 18). Thus, the Ti-Nb-Cu alloys 
investigated in this study exhibited mechanical 
properties that made them suitable for dental use.
