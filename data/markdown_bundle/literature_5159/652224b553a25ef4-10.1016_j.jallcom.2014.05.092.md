Abstract 
Titanium aluminides are of interest due to their high specific strength and performance up to 750°C. 
Research into high-Nb γ-TiAl based titanium aluminides has shown promising improvements in 
performance by introduction of the σ-Nb2Al phase. However, one current challenge is improving 
mechanical properties at room and elevated temperatures in order to enable their further implementation. 
These properties are closely tied with microstructural refinement, and thus phase evolution and 
microstructural development is the focus of this work. Phase transformation temperatures and stability 
ranges were determined experimentally through DSC analysis of arc melted alloys, compared with 
predictions based upon use of computational thermodynamics tools, and investigated through heat 
treatment of experimental alloys to develop an ultrafine γ+σ microstructure. 
Keywords: Titanium Aluminides; Intermetallics; Microstructure Development; Precipitation; 
Thermodynamic Properties 
1. Introduction 
Ti-Al-Nb alloys have been of interest due to their mechanical properties, which make them appealing for 
use in high temperature aerospace components [1-5]. Alloying additions of Nb, Cr, Mo, Ta, W, Si, and 
Fe have been shown to stabilize the ductile, solid solution β -phase [6-9], which improves machinability 
and high temperature formability [10-12]. Specifically, Nb additions have been shown to enhance the 
mechanical properties of Ti-Al based alloys [2, 13, 14], furthermore increasing the Nb content in Ti-Al-
Nb alloys with 40-50 at% Al results in the formation of the γ-TiAl + σ-Nb2Al microstructure which can 
further improve high temperature mechanical properties [15-19]. Previous studies have revealed that 
modification of the γ + σ microstructure can improve fracture behavior in Ti-Al-Nb alloys with high 
volume fraction σ-phase [17]. It is the aim of this work to understand the effect of Nb concentration in 
the range of 15-25 at% Nb on solid-state transformations and microstructural development in γ+σ alloys.

This composition range is expected to produce microstructures with corresponding σ-phase volume 
fraction from 0.1 to 0.3 of varying topological morphology. 
Currently, γ+α 2 titanium aluminides are used in the compressor and low temperature turbine regions of jet 
engines [3]. In order to improve the high temperature performance of TiAl-based alloys, research has 
been performed to optimize alloy chemistry and microstructure, with two-phase microstructures being 
commonly employed in order to concurrently improve ductility and strength [1, 4, 5, 11, 15, 19-24]. 
Titanium aluminides should exhibit a balance of room temperature ductility, fracture toughness, high 
temperature strength, and creep and oxidation resistance, and these properties can be modified through 
microstructure control [1, 11, 13, 20, 22]. 
Several phases are of current interest for alloy development in the Ti-Al-Nb system, including α 2, β , γ, 
and σ-phases [2, 15, 20, 21, 25, 26]. The β solid solution phase is important in the development of some 
two-phase microstructures in titanium aluminides [21, 27] when retained to room temperature via 
quenching. The γ -TiAl intermetallic phase, which has poor room temperature ductility and fracture 
toughness, is usually present in conjunction with the α 2-Ti3Al phase [1, 11, 20, 28]. Recent research by 
Ebrahimi and coworkers [15, 16, 21, 29, 30] has been conducted on the effect of the σ-Nb2Al phase 
reinforcement on fracture toughness, ductility, and high temperature behavior. The σ-Nb2Al phase is hard 
and brittle, exhibiting no ductility at temperatures up to 700°C even when toughened by a ductile second 
phase [15, 16, 29]. However, research has shown potential for enhancing the toughness of γ+σ titanium 
aluminides through microstructural refinement [19, 30]. Thus to control microstructural evolution, it is of 
upmost importance to understand the effect of alloying on precipitation nucleation and growth behavior. 
This paper focuses on a systematic study of Ti- 45at%Al alloys with Nb, Mo, and Cr additions to reveal 
key chemistry-processing-microstructure relationship for future alloy development.

1.2 Alloy Selection 
In order to achieve a finely dispersed, disconnected γ+σ microstructure through thermal processing, 
thermodynamic calculations were performed using the ThermoCalc software platform [31] . An 
optimized Ti-Al-Nb thermodynamic database developed by Cupid et al [25] was used for all calculations. 
For the alloy series under investigation, compositions were chosen that displayed a high temperature 
single phase (β ) region and a lower-temperature γ+σ phase region that could be produced upon quenching 
and subsequent aging from the β phase [21, 26]. The Al content was fixed to 45 at% Al to enable a wide 
range of composition and phase fraction flexibility, as can be seen by the isopleth and isotherm at 1000°C 
shown in Figure 1. Additionally, 45 at% Al was previously determined to improve environmental 
protection [32] of these alloys. From the calculated phase diagram at 45 at% Al, transformation 
temperatures were predicted for alloys with 15, 20, and 25 at% Nb. 
2. Materials and Methods 
Alloys of composition (49-x)Ti-45Al-xNb-5Cr-1Mo (where x = 15, 20, 25 at%) were prepared via 
vacuum arc melting and re-melting several times to ensure homogeneity. Inductively couple plasma 
(ICP) analysis was conducted to verify all compositions and they were found to be accurate to within 0.4 
at% of the nominal composition for each alloy, summarized in Table 1. 
Samples for differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) of each of the arc melted alloys were cut using a 
low-speed diamond saw to produce 100 – 500 mg samples. DSC a nalysis was conducted using a Setaram 
Setsys Evolution 1750 with DSC 1600 rod, cycling within temperature range of 600-1600°C at 10°C/min. 
Testing was run under ultra-high purity Ar environment, flowing at 20 mL/min, after pumping and 
backfilling with Ar several times. Three DSC cycles were completed after the initial heating to 600°C, 
with the first cycle revealing transformations from the non-equilibrium as-cast state, and the last cycle 
representing equilibrium phase transformation temperatures. DSC results were paired with XRD to 
identify the temperature range of the single-phase β for solution treatment.

Solution treatment of alloys was carried out using a custom built vertical tube furnace with drop 
quenching capability. The system was built around a Centorr CM 1700 Split VTF with an alumina tube, 
vacuum, and gas system for heat treatment of samples in an oxygen gettered flowing Ar environment. 
Samples were heated at a rate of 9°C/min and quenched into agitated water directly from the hot zone of 
the furnace to retain the solution treated microstructure to room temperature. 
DSC was performed on metastable β -phase alloys, and the first heating cycle was used to determine the 
phase transformations from the metastable β -phase. These curves were analyzed to establish the β → γ + 
σ transformation temperature upon heating. Aging of samples to achieve the final γ + σ microstructure 
was accomplished through encapsulation in a quartz tube in Ar environment, heating in an ATS 3150 box 
furnace, and furnace cooling. 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) was conducted on solution treated and aged alloys using an XPert Powder X-
Ray diffractometer, using Cu K-α X-rays, scanning from 20-120° with step size 0.008° and 5.7 sec/step. 
Peak patterns for α2, β, γ, σ, and η were retrieved from the JCPDS database and used for identification of 
phases present at each stage of heat treatment in these alloys. Light optical microscopy (LOM) and 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used for microstructure evaluation and grain size 
measurements. 
3. Results and Discussion 
3.1 Thermal Analysis 
For microstructural control, it is important to understand the precipitation behavior of γ- and σ-phases in 
this alloy system. In order to accomplish this, it is integral to quench the single-phase β to room 
temperature, which can be achieved through sufficiently severe quenching as well as alloying additions 
that alter the thermodynamics and kinetics of the reaction. It has been demonstrated that the addition of 
5at% Cr to a high-Nb TiAlNb alloy significantly stabilizes the β -phase and allows for the retention of β -
phase to room temperature upon quenching [21]. Enhanced β-phase retention results in finer, more

uniform microstructure after aging in the γ + σ region, resulting in enhanced alloy performance [19, 21, 
30]. Molybdenum is alloyed into titanium aluminides due to its β-stabilization behavior as well as its 
slow diffusion rate in this system, which limits the kinetics of phase transformations [24]. These 
additions, along with variation in Nb content, affect the driving force for phase transformations, 
necessitating the measurement of precise transformation temperatures in these quinary alloys via DSC. 
The initial heating in DSC was used to determine the temperatures at which the as-cast microstructure 
transforms into the high temperature β phase and its subsequent melting temperature. Similarly, the first 
DSC heating cycle of the quenched alloy was used to study the γ and σ phase precipitation sequence from 
the metastable β phase. The first exothermic peak upon heating corresponds to the γ-phase nucleation 
while the second is the σ-phase. These transformation points are illustrated in Figure 2, and the results of 
thermal analysis are summarized in Table 2, where they are compared with transformation temperatures 
calculated using ThermoCalc. The multiple endothermic peaks at higher temperatures correspond to the 
dissolution of these two phases and the re-introduction of β-phase upon heating, and the final plateau is 
the single-phase β region. 
From the DSC results it can be seen that temperatures for the transformation sequence upon heating (σ + γ 
→ β + σ + γ → β + γ → β ) increase with increasing Nb content, with the most significant increase in the 
range of 20-25 at% Nb. This trend is consistent when comparing quinary Ti-Al-(Nb,Cr,Mo) alloys with 
the calculated ternary Ti-Al-Nb isopleth section, with the exception of a larger increase between the 
higher Nb content alloys. This can be explained by the β-stabilization effects of Cr and Mo additions as, 
it has been seen that by adding 5 at% Cr to ternary TiAlNb alloys, transformation temperatures are 
depressed by 10-100°C [21]. By comparing the transformation temperatures measured in the DSC 
cycling of the quinary alloy with those predicted by the ternary calculations, as illustrated in Table 2, a 
similar depression in transformation temperatures is seen.

3.2 Microstructure Development 
Of particular interest is the precipitation of the γ + σ microstructure from the metastable β phase upon 
aging. This transformation is not well studied, but the temperature range in which it occurs is important 
to the development of refined microstructures. Heat treatments were designed such that alloys were 
solution treated in the single-phase β region, water quenched to retain metastable β to room temperature, 
and subsequently aged in the γ + σ temperature regime. Analysis of DSC data led to the selection of 
1400, 1450, and 1500°C as solution treatment temperatures, and 1000, 950, and 1125°C as aging 
temperatures for alloys with 15, 20 and 25at% Nb, respectively. All alloys were solution treated for 2 
hours followed by water quench, resulting in microstructures shown in Figure 3. Following this, they 
were aged for one hour followed by furnace cooling to develop the two phase microstructures developed 
illustrated in Figure 4. 
In order to verify the phases present in the temperature regimes of interest, XRD was conducted on 
samples in solution treated state as well as aged state. Peak identification indicates that after solution 
treatment and quenching, the primary phase present is a metastable β phase with some γ phase present. 
After aging, it is seen that the microstructure consists of γ and σ phases, as summarized in Figure 5. 
 
3.3 Phase Transformations 
The temperature regime in which γ + σ nucleation occurs has been determined through DSC, and is seen 
as the region between the two nucleation events and the first dissolution event upon heating of the 
metastable β, as illustrated in Figure 2. Aging at lower temperature within the γ + σ temperature stability 
regime will result in finer microstructure due to increased nucleation rate and slower growth, while higher 
temperature will result in decreased nucleation and faster growth, a trend that has been found to hold in 
similar alloy systems with 22.6at%Nb [30]. The irregular shape of the dissolution peak indicates a double

peak in the alloy with 15% Nb, with γ- and σ-phase dissolution occurring between 1075-1250°C, and as 
Nb content increases the γ + σ microstructure becomes stable to higher temperature. 
Initial aging treatments of the 15Nb alloy at 1000°C show development of an ultrafine microstructure, as 
illustrated in Figure 4. Only γ- and σ-phases are present in the microstructure, as verified with XRD, and 
the scale is greatly refined when compared with alloys where the γ-phase transformation from the β -phase 
is not suppressed, due to less β stabilization upon quenching, or nucleated upon slow heating to the aging 
temperature. Analogous heat treatments conducted on 20Nb and 25Nb alloys show increasing σ-phase 
content, as well as increased microstructural scale in the 25Nb alloy. With more availability of Nb, 
increased amount and growth of σ-phase is seen, and the microstructure transitions to one with a more 
connected σ-phase. 
It has been observed [30, 33] that γ-phase precipitates quickly from the prior β -phase grain boundaries, in 
a widmanstatten morphology that can occur upon quenching or early in subsequent heating during aging. 
It has been experimentally observed that, upon quenching, there is formation of primary γ-phase laths at β 
grain boundaries, as seen in the micrographs in Figure 6. If the microstructure is allowed time to 
transform fully to the γ-phase by slow cooling from β -phase and holding at lower temperature (400-
500°C), σ-phase formation will be suppressed as σ-phase nucleation only occurs from metastable β [27, 
33]. However, if a metastable β-phase is instead quickly heated to the aging temperature, additional γ- 
and σ-phases begin to precipitate from the β-phase and σ-phase will form at the γ/β boundaries, and the 
primary γ-phase begins to grow. The precipitation of σ-phase inhibits the coarsening of γ-laths as well as 
provides additional nucleation sites for γ. The resultant microstructure is one of smaller, equiaxed γ-
grains away from the prior β-grain boundaries, with coarser primary γ-laths which formed at that 
interface. Evidence of this transformation behavior can be seen in the aged microstructures of 15Nb and 
20Nb alloys by observing differences between γ-phase morphology near and away from the prior β-grain 
boundaries. Near these boundaries a coarser acicular morphology is seen, while away from them a very

fine structure is observed, as illustrated in Figure 7. This dual nature of microstructural scale is consistent 
with growth of primary γ-phase at the grain boundaries as well as nucleation of γ-phase within the grains. 
The scale and distribution of σ-phase is indicative of a combination of homogenous and heterogeneous 
nucleation. The finer, evenly distributed, disconnected σ-phase, seen especially in the 15Nb alloy, 
indicates nucleation of the σ-phase directly from the β-phase. It has been observed in samples of 15Nb 
alloy subjected to less severe quench that γ-phase will continue to grow into large laths if unhindered, as 
shown in Figure 8. The nucleation of σ-phase is responsible for retarding the growth of these laths, as 
well as providing additional nucleation sites for γ-phase. Since both γ- and σ-phases only nucleate from β 
phase [27, 33], each one’s presence limits the potential nucleation and growth of the other since they will 
preferentially nucleate at the interface the other has with β -phase [21, 27]. If σ-phase nucleates first or 
concurrently with the γ-phase, only the remaining β between σ-phase particles can transform to γ-phase, 
producing a very fine equiaxed microstructure. In the case where γ-phase nucleates first, as it does with 
slower cooling rate upon quenching, then σ-phase can only nucleate from the remaining β phase, leading 
to a coarser lath-shaped γ + σ microstructure. This microstructural modification via nucleation and 
growth sequence is illustrated in Figure 8, which contrasts the aged microstructures of an alloy that has 
been fully quenched into β-phase to one partially transformed to γ-phase upon quenching. During aging, 
competing nucleation and growth mechanisms are occurring, so the order in which these phases emerge 
plays a very strong role in the development of the alloys’ microstructures. 
4. Conclusions 
Through thermal analysis, heat treatment, and microstructure evaluation of quinary titanium aluminides 
with high Nb concentration (15-25 at%), it has been found that increasing Nb content has several effects 
on phase transformation and microstructure development. Primarily, increasing Nb increases phase 
transformation temperatures for equilibrium phase transformations, suppresses metastable (β → γ+σ ) 
transformation temperatures, increases γ-TiAl formation during quench, as Nb stabilizes all phases to

## Page 11

10 
 
higher temperature, and increases phase fraction of Nb-rich σ-phase. Ultrafine, relatively equiaxed 
microstructure was produced in 15 and 20 at% Nb alloys, but it has been observed that with an increase to 
25 at% Nb, aged microstructures transition to a coarser microstructure with more connected σ-phase due 
to γ-phase formation upon quenching. It has also been demonstrated that microstructural development in 
these alloys is very sensitive to composition and processing parameters, such as heating and cooling rates. 
Cooling rate after solution treatment, and the suppression of transformation to γ-TiAl is vital to the 
modification of γ+σ microstructure in these alloys. Towards the goal of designing a high-Nb titanium 
aluminide with ultrafine, disconnected γ+σ morphology, it has been established that microstructural 
control can be accomplished in alloys containing 15-25at% Nb through targeted chemistry and processing 
controls.
