Highlights for review 
 Microstructures of GBs can be engineered by altering cooling rate of heat-treatments. 
 Lowering cooling rate, more M23X6 phase precipitates on GBs with segregation of B. 
 Lower cooling rate, GBs become more serrated in morphology.

 Weld-crack resistance of Ni-base superalloys can be ameliorated via engineering GBs. 
 
Abstract 
Fusion weld is a portable and economical joining and repairing method of metals. However, 
weld cracks often occur during the fusion weld of Ni-base superalloys, which hinder the 
applications of fusion weld on this kind of materials. In this work, the effects of 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 3

2 
microstructures of grain boundaries (GBs) of the prototype M951 superalloy on its 
weldability were investigated. The precipitated phases, the elemental segregations on GBs, 
and the morphologies of GBs can be largely altered by regulating the cooling rates of 
pre-weld heat treatments. With decreasing the cooling rate, chain-like M23X6 phase 
precipitates along the GBs, accompanying segregations of B, and GBs becomes more 
serrated in morphology. During fusion weld, the engineered GBs in the M951 superalloy 
with a low cooling rate favor the formation of the continuous liquid films on GBs, which 
together with the serrated GB morphology significantly prevents the formation of weld 
cracks. Our findings imply that the weld-crack resistance of the superalloys can be 
ameliorated by engineering GBs. 
 
Key words: Fusion weld; Superalloys; M951; Weld-crack resistance; Grain boundary 
engineering 
 
1. Introduction 
The M951 superalloy with a compositions of 
Ni-9Cr-5Co-5.8Al-3.5W-3Mo-2Nb-0.024B-0.02C (wt.%, Ni: Bal.) is a cast Ni-base 
superalloy, developed by Chinese researchers from Institute of Metal Research for advanced 
vane applications in aircraft engines and industrial gas turbines [1]. The M951 superalloy 
possesses a microstructure of γ particulates embedded in the γ matrix, and the M951 
superalloy shows attractive properties, including a relatively low density, a low cost, high 
incipient melting temperature, good foundry performance, good oxidation resistance at 
temperatures up to 900 °C, and excellent mechanical properties [2]. During manufacture or 
service of vanes, it is indispensable that the parts made of superalloys are required to be 
joined or repaired. Fusion weld, as an economic and portable technique, has been widely 
used in joining and repairing of metals [3,4]. However, like other Ni-base superalloys, the 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 4

3 
joining and repairing of the M951 components by fusion weld remain technically challenging, 
because weld cracks often occur in the fusion zones (FZs) and heat affected zones (HAZs) in 
the weldments [4-11]. This severely restricts the practical applications of fusion weld on 
joining and repairing of Ni-base superalloy vanes. 
According to the previously results on weldments of Ni-base superalloys, weld cracks 
generally form on grain boundaries (GBs) [4,8,12-17]. The relatively lower strength of the 
GBs than that of the interior grains at high temperatures makes GBs become the weak 
regions during welding [8,18-21]. This is the fundamental reason why weld cracks are 
initiated and propagated along GBs. It is well known that microstructures of GBs, such as 
phase precipitates [22,23], element segregation [24,25], and morphologies [26,27], are 
closely related to the property of the superalloys [28-30]. Some previous studies implied that 
the formation of liquid droplets on the straight GBs during welding is the main reason for 
causing the formations of cracks in superalloys [31-33]. However, other studies indicated 
that if the liquid films are continuously distributed along GBs during welding, then the 
superalloys exhibit good weldability [34]. Therefore, the correlation between the 
microstructures of GBs and the weldability of Ni-base superalloys remain obscure, and a 
systematical study on this topic is urgently needed. Furthermore, many previous studies have 
shown that altering cooling rates of the heat treatments can significantly modify the 
microstructure of GBs [29-33]. This provides the possibility to obtain various 
microstructures of GBs and to correlate the microstructures of GBs and the weldability of 
Ni-base supperalloys. 
In this work, we selected the M951 superalloy as a prototype of Ni-base superalloys, and 
investigated how the difference microstructures of GBs affect its weldability. The 
microstructures of GBs, including the precipitated phases, elemental segregations, and 
morphologies, were engineered by regulating the cooling rates of per-weld heat treatments. It

2. Experimental 
The M951 alloy ingots with a composition of 
Ni-9Cr-5Co-5.8Al-3.5W-3Mo-2Nb-0.024B-0.02C (wt.%, Ni: Bal.) were melted in a 
home-made vacuum induction furnace for five times and then cast into the sheets with a 
thickness of 5 mm. The cast sheets were cut into plates with a size of 40 mm×20 mm×4 mm. 
The M951 plates were heat-treated at 1230 °C for two hours and then cooled to room 
temperature with three different ways: (1) the air cooling (AC) with an average cooling rate 
of ~100 °C per minute from 1230 °C to the room temperature; (2) the interrupted furnace 
cooling (IFC) with a first cooling rate of 2 °C per minute from 1230 °C to 900 °C and then 
air cooling with a rate of ~100 °C per minute to the room temperature; and (3) the furnace 
cooling (FC) with a cooling rate of ~2 °C per minutes directly from 1230 °C to the room 
temperature. The cooling rate of the pre-weld treatment gradually decreases from the AC to 
the IFC and FC cooling-ways. For a better clarity, the M951 plates heat-treated by the three 
different cooling ways are also labeled as the AC, IFC and FC samples, respectively. The 
three pre-weld heat-treated plates were fusion welded with the M951 fillers (1.2 mm in 
diameter, cut from the as-cast sheets) by the gas tungsten arc weld (GTAW) technique. 
Identical welding parameters, including a voltage of 12 V, a current of 45 A, a weld speed of 
5 cm per minute, and the pure argon as the shield atmosphere, were used for welding the 
three kinds of M951 samples. 
Transverse sections were cut perpendicular to the welding direction from the welded 
samples for microstructural characterization by means of the scanned electron microscopy 
(SEM, FEI Inspect F50) and the transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI F20). The 
transverse sections were polished and chemically etched with a solution of 50 ml HCl + 40

ml H2O + 10 g CuSO4 + 10 ml H2SO4 before SEM observations. The SEM samples were 
further used for the composition-analysis of the precipitated phases by the electron probe 
micro-analyzer (EPMA, Shimadzu 1610). The picture analysis software “Image pro plus 6.0” 
was used to analyze the area fractions of phases in samples. The time of flight secondary ion 
massive spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) was also used to reveal the element distribution on/near 
GBs. The samples were ion milled for 40 min to minimize the strained layer and

electrolytically thinned in a solution of 10 vol.% perchloric acid + 90 vol.% ethyl alcohol at 
-20 °C. The Thermo-Calc software with a TTNi8 database was used to calculate the 
evolution of the equilibrium phases in the M951 superalloy. 
3. Results 
3.1. Microstructures of the engineered GBs 
The three M951 plates, labeled as AC, IFC or FC, were obtained under three different 
cooling ways of the pre-weld heat treatments. Fig. 1(a)-(c) shows the SEM microstructures 
under a relatively lower magnification of the heat-treated AC, IFC and FC samples. It is clear 
that the GBs in the AC sample are smooth and clean, as shown in Fig. 1(a). With decreasing 
the cooling rate, GBs become serrated and new phases precipitated on GBs, as seen in the 
IFC and FC samples (Fig. 1(b) and (c)). The more detailed microstructures of the AC, IFC 
and FC samples are shown in Fig. 1(d) and (f), respectively. The GBs of the AC sample are 
still clean and smooth under the high magnification, and only a blocky phase located on or 
near GBs can be seen (Fig. 1(d)). Beside the blocky phase, a new chain-like phase is 
precipitated along GBs in the IFC sample (Fig. 1(e)), and the GBs become serrated as 
compared to those in the AC sample. In the FC sample, the blocky phase can still be seen, 
the GBs become more serrated as compared to those in the IFC sample, and the chain-like

The elemental mapping results of B, Cr and Nb in the AC, IFC and FC samples by

brighter contrast of Nb. With decreasing the cooling rate from the AC sample to the IFC and 
FC samples, the contrast of Nb is getting brighter on GBs, suggesting the enrichment of Nb 
in the M23X6 phase on GBs. The SIMS mapping results of the selected three elements (B, Cr 
and Nb) are in line with the EPMA measurements, confirming the blocky phase is the MC 
phase and the chain-like phase on GBs is the M23X6 phase.

The measured average crack length and crack index of the welded AC, IFC, and FC

4. Discussion 
4.1. Engineering GBs by regulating cooling rate of pre-weld heat treatments 
As presented in the section 3.1, the microstructures of GBs have been largely tailored by 
regulating the cooling rates of the pre-weld heat treatments. With decreasing the cooling rate 
of the pre-weld heat treatment, the changes in the microstructures of GBs in the three M951 
samples can be summarized as the following three major features: (i) the amount of the 
chain-like M23X6 phase on GBs increases; (ii) the element B is more prone to segregation on 
GBs; and (iii) the GBs become more serrated in morphology. The underlying mechanisms 
for the three main changes are discussed below. 
In order to understand the precipitation of the chain-like M23X6 phase under a low 
cooling rate, the respective area fractions of the MC phase or the M23X6 phase were 
statistically analyzed from their microstructure of the as heat-treated samples, shown in Fig. 
7. The area fraction of the M23X6 phase monotonically increases from 0% in the AC sample 
to 12%±4% in the IFC sample and 20%±6% in the FC sample, accompanying the decrease in 
the area fraction of the MC phase from 100% in the AC sample to 88% in the IFC sample 
and 80% in the FC sample. The equilibrium phase diagram of the M951 superalloy was 
calculated by Thermal-Calc, as shown in Fig. 8. The major constituent phases in the M951 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 11

10 
superalloy are γ and γ phases, and the MC, M23X6 and M3B2 phases with very small fractions 
exist, see Fig. 8. However, the M3B2 phase is stable only at temperatures higher than 1100 °C. 
In comparison, the M23X6 phase is stable below the 1100 °C, and its fraction increases with 
decreasing the temperature. As the cooling rate decreases from the AC sample to the IFC and 
FC samples, a state closer to the equilibrium state was obtained. Therefore, a lower cooling 
rate promotes the formation of the M23X6 phase and the increase in its area fraction. 
Additionally, previous studies on Ni-base superalloys shown the decomposition process of 
the MC phase into the γ' and the M23X6 phases at low temperatures (below 700 °C) are 
commonly encountered [1,36]. It is evident in Fig. 1(e) and (f) that the precipitation of the 
M23X6 phase was accompanied by the size decreasing of the MC phase and the formation of 
irregular γ' on GBs. Therefore, the decomposed process of the MC phase at low temperatures 
also contributes to the precipitation of the M23X6 phase. 
The decreased cooling rate from the AC sample to the IFC and FC samples also 
contributes to the segregation of B on GBs. According to the Langmuir-McLean segregation 
model [37], if the concentration of the solute in matrix Cm<<1, i.e. a dilute solution, then the 
concentration of the solute element on GBs in equilibrium, CGB, is given by: 
CGB= CmeEb (kT)⁄

where Eb is the averaged bonding energy and k is the Boltzmann constant. Although the 
bonding energy of B in the M951 superalloy is unavailable, the Eb of B with Ni calculated by 
the density-functional cluster model is about -24 eV [38]. Consequently, the concentration of 
B at GBs will increase at low temperatures according to Eq. (1). The final states of the AC 
and the IFC and FC samples are at the room temperature, and this implies that if a state of the 
alloy is closer to its equilibrium state due to a more time for elemental diffusion during 
cooling, such as a state obtained at a low cooling rate, then the concentration of B on GBs is 
higher. Such a mechanism accounts for the observed higher content of B on GBs from the

AC sample to the IFC and FC samples. 
Serration of GBs is another main feature induced by lowering the cooling rate of the 
pre-weld heat treatment. Serrated GBs have been observed in the heat-treated [39,40] or hot 
deformed [41] Ni-base superalloys. In the previous studies, the occurrence of the serrated 
GBs was attributed to the pinning effect of the precipitated phases, including the MC and 
M23C6 phases, on GBs [26,39,42,43]. With decreasing the cooling rate, more chain-like 
M23X6 phase precipitates and more segregation of B occurs on GBs. Additionally, a low 
cooling rate promotes the growth of grains, but the pinning effects by the precipitated M23X6 
phase and the segregation of B cause the serration of GBs. Consequently, the GBs become 
more serrated from the IFC sample to the FC sample, as observed in Fig. 1. 
4.2. Amelioration of weld-crack resistance by engineering GBs 
For a better understanding of the GB microstructural evolution during welding, the 
chain-like phase and the lamellar phases in the welded FC sample were further characterized 
by the EPMA elemental mapping, as shown in Fig. 9. Some region on GBs is enriched of Nb 
and C, suggesting that it is mainly composed of the MC phase, while other region on GBs is 
enriched of Cr, Mo and B, suggesting that it is mainly composed of the M23X6 phase. Both 
regions are mutually connected on GBs, and this implies continuous liquid films form on 
GBs during welding. In the lamellar region (a left-top region in Fig. 9), it is clear all the five 
detected elements (Cr, C, B, Nb and Mo) are enriched in this region, suggesting that a liquid 
phase form there during welding which resolidifies into the lamellar MC and M23X6 phases. 
As discussed in the above section, the microstructure and morphology of GBs can be 
successfully engineered by regulating the cooling rate of pre-weld heat treatments of the 
M951 superalloy, and the engineered GBs lead to the much improved weld-crack resistance. 
The correlation between the various microstructures of GBs and the weldability can be now

## Page 13

established as discussed below. 
Firstly, with decreasing the cooling rate of the pre-weld heat treatment, the chain-like 
M23X6 phase precipitates on GBs and the element B segregates on GBs, both of which 
promote the formation of continuous liquid films with low melting point (see Fig. 8) on GBs 
in FZs and HAZs during welding. As schematically shown in Fig. 10, if the precipitated 
phases are blocky or isolated on GBs, as observed in the AC sample, discontinuous liquid 
films (or say liquid droplets) on GBs will form during welding. Due to the stress 
concentration and weak strength of the discontinuous liquid droplets, weld cracks often 
initiate at these regions and propagate along GBs [44]. This is the fundamental reason for the 
poor weldability of the AC sample. In comparison, if the precipitated phase is in chain-like 
shape with more B segregation, continuous liquid films will form during welding due to the 
continuous distribution of the M23X6 phase with low melting temperatures (see Figs. 6 and 9). 
Unlike the stress concentration and weak strength of the discontinuous liquid films on GBs, 
the formation of continuous liquid films can relieve the stress concentration and avoid the 
formation of cracks, because the grains can locally glide along GBs with the aid of 
continuous liquid films. This is one of the reasons for the improved weld-crack resistance of 
the FC sample compared to the AC sample, as schematically shown in Fig. 10. 
Secondly, the segregation of B on GBs not only promotes the formation of liquid films 
with a low melting temperature, but also reduces the grain boundary energy, γgb, according to 
the adsorption isotherm [25]. With decreasing the cooling rate of pre-weld heat-treatment, a 
higher content of B segregates on GBs. Then the value of γgb decreases from the AC sample 
to the IFC and FC samples. Previous studies shown that a lower GB energy has two 
advantages [45]: (1) it will improve the wettability between the liquid films and the surfaces 
of grains; (2) it benefits the healing capability of the liquid films on GBs. A good wettability 
between liquid films and surfaces promote the spread of the liquid films along GBs, and the 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 14

13 
good continuity of the liquid films are maintained during the local glide of grains along GBs 
during the cooling process of welding cycle. Rappaz et al. [46] proposed a criterion for the 
cohesion of the liquid films between two grains: γgb = 2γsl, where γsl is the interfacial energy 
between the solid grain and the liquid film. The segregation of B on GBs will decrease both 
of γgb and γsl, but γsl decreases faster than γgb because of a higher B content in the liquid phase 
[17]. Therefore, with a high content of B segregated on GBs, the grain boundaries become 
more repulsive due to 2γsl < γgb. As a result, two grains try to avoid the direct contact, and 
then it is energetically favored that a discontinuous liquid film spreads itself along GBs to 
form a continuous liquid film, resulting a self-healing effect. Therefore, the segregation of B 
on GBs is another reason for the ameliorated weld-crack resistance. 
Thirdly, the serration of GBs in the M951 sample with low cooling rates of the 
pre-weld heat treatments also contribute to the improved weld-crack resistance. The main 
underlying mechanisms are attributed to the two reasons. The initiation of weld cracks is 
driven by stress concentrations on GBs, but the serrated GBs benefit a more homogenous 
distribution of stress in the adjacent grains [47], which is good for a better weld-crack 
resistance. Additionally, if tiny cracks form on serrated GBs, the propagation route will be a 
zigzag and much longer path as compared to the straight GBs [48]. The zigzag and long 
cracking route will create large new surfaces, which is actually forbidden by the high-energy 
requirement. This is the third reason of the engineered GBs for the amelioration of 
weld-crack resistance from the AC sample to the IFC and FC samples. 
5. Conclusions 
In summary, the M951 superalloy was selected as a prototype of Ni-base superalloys 
and the weldability of the M951 superalloy with three distinct microstructures of GBs was 
systematically investigated. It is found that the microstructures GBs can be effectively 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 15

14 
engineered by decreasing the cooling rate of the pre-weld heat treatments. With decreasing 
the cooling rate from the AC sample to the IFC and FC samples, the amount of the chain-like 
M23X6 phase on GBs increases, the element B is more prone to segregation on GBs, and the 
GBs become more serrated in morphology. The weld-crack resistance of the M951 
superalloy is much improved from the AC sample to the IFC and FC samples, and the 
microstructures of GBs and the weldability have been correlated. The chain-like M23X6 phase 
on GBs and the segregation of B on GBs promote the formation of continuous liquid films on 
GBs, which relieves the stress concentration on GBs in the FZs and the HAZs, contributing 
the improved weld-crack resistance. The segregation of B on GBs improves the wettability of 
liquid films on GBs and benefits the healing process of the liquid films on GBs, which 
contributes the improved weldability. Furthermore, the serrated GBs benefit a more 
homogenous distribution of stress and high-energy requirement prevents the cracking with a 
long and zigzag route. Thus, the serrated GBs are also beneficial to the improved weldability 
from the AC sample to the IFC and FC samples. These findings imply that the weld-crack 
resistance of the Ni-base superalloys can be ameliorated by appropriately engineering GBs.
