ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Effect of Heat Treatment Temperature
on the Microstructure and Mechanical Properties
of FeCoNiCr 0.6Al0.4 High-Entropy Alloy
Hu Chen, Chenglei Wang, Xiaodu Li, Li Pan, Yatao Zhu, Zhujiang Tan, Mei Huang, Jingya Zhang, Daxiang Li, and Jiayan Huang
Submitted: 19 September 2024 / Revised: 12 November 2024 / Accepted: 23 November 2024 / Published online: 13 March 2025
In this study, the FeCoNiCr0.6Al0.4 high-entropy alloy was prepared using the vacuum arc melting
method, and various aging heat treatments were applied. The effects of different aging temperatures on the
phase structure, microstructure, mechanical properties, and wear properties of high-entropy alloys were
systematically investigated. The results show that the phase structure of the high-entropy alloy transforms
from a simple FCC phase to an FCC + BCC phase with increasing aging temperature. The tensile prop-
erties of high-entropy alloys initially improve and then deteriorate as the aging temperature increases. At
an aging temperature of 700 /C176C, the high-entropy alloy exhibits optimal mechanical properties, with a
tensile strength of approximately 1157 MPa and an elongation at break of about 37%. The FeCoNi-
Cr0.6Al0.4 high-entropy alloy exhibits high wear resistance, with a wear volume of 0.0112 mm
3 under a 3N
load. The wear mechanism is primarily abrasive wear, accompanied by oxidation and stratiﬁed wear.
Keywords aging treatment, high-entropy alloy, mechanical
properties, microstructure, strengthening mechanism
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are a new type of metal
material developed by Cantor (Ref 1) and Y eh (Ref 2) et al.
HEAs are mainly composed of ﬁve or more elements, with each
element/C213s atomic percentage ranging from 5 to 35%, and no
dominant element. HEAs exhibit elevated mixing entropy due
to their multi-element composition. This composition facilitates
an increase in the solubility limits between elements, thereby
preventing atomic ordering and segregation. The high conﬁg-
urational entropy of mixing favors the formation of simple
disordered solid solution structures, such as FCC (face-centered
cubic) phases or BCC (body-centered cubic) phases, over the
formation of intermetallic compounds (Ref 1-8). Among them,
the HEAs with FCC structure have excellent comprehensive
properties, such as excellent corrosion and radiation resistance
along with high ductility (Ref 8–10). However, the HEAs with
FCC structures have low strength despite their excellent
ductility (Ref 11, 12), making it difﬁcult to meet the
performance requirements for current engineering applications.
Therefore, it is urgent to design FCC HEAs with both high
strength and high ductility. The introduction of precipitates in
metallic materials signiﬁcantly enhances alloy strength (Ref 13-
16). The FeCoNiCrAl system of HEAs was developed based on
the FeCoNi base alloy system with FCC structure. The addition
of Cr and Al elements enables the alloys to obtain hard
precipitated phases, signiﬁcantly improving mechanical prop-
erties and wear resistance (Ref 17, 18). For example, Zheng
et al. (Ref 17) prepared CoCrFeNi(Ni3Al)x ( x = 0.25, 0.5,
0.75, 1.0) HEAs by arc melting. The addition of Ni3Al was
regulated to precipitate nano-L12 precipitated phases on the
FCC HEA matrix, improving the alloy /C213s mechanical properties.
As the Ni3Al concentration increased, the alloy /C213s strength and
volume fraction of the nano-L12 phase rose, while its ductility
decreased. The alloy achieves the optimal balance between
ductility and strength when x = 0.75. Research by Wang et al.
(Ref 18) revealed that as the aluminum content increases in
Alx-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni HEAs (from x = 0 to 2.0 molar ratio), the
phase composition changes from pure FCC to a mix of FCC
and BCC, and ﬁnally to pure BCC. The Al0.9CoCrFeNi alloy
exhibits the most reﬁned spinodal structure, with an alternating
dual-phase, resulting in the highest hardness among the series
of alloys. Qi et al. (Ref 19) conducted appropriate cold
deformation, complete recrystallization, and aging heat treat-
ment of (FeCoNiCr)89Ti6Al5 HEA. The introduction and
uniform distribution of precipitates at the grain boundaries of
recrystallization signiﬁcantly increased the alloy /C213s strength
without greatly reducing ductility. Furthermore, multiple stud-
ies have demonstrated that the properties of alloys can be
signiﬁcantly enhanced through simple aging treatments (Ref
20, 21), which can induce precipitates coherently and control
their morphology, size, and volume fraction. Wang et al. (Ref
20) investigated the mechanical behavior of Al0.6CoFeNiCr0.4
HEA after 24 hours of aging at 550-850 /C176 C. As the aging
temperature increases, the alloy /C213s phase structure changes from

FeCoNiCr0.6Al0.4 HEA was chosen as the research object
in this study. We performed appropriate aging treatments to
induce a large number of diffusely distributed precipitates and
investigated the relationship between microstructure and
mechanical properties at different aging temperatures. The goal
was to explain the precipitation behavior and reveal the
underlying mechanism. Subsequently, the alloy with the best
mechanical properties was tested by reciprocating friction and
wear under different loads to reveal the wear mechanism of the
alloy. The aim is to develop new FeCoNiCrAl HEAs with
superior mechanical properties and wear resistance.
2. Experimental
FeCoNiCr0.6Al0.4 HEA was prepared by vacuum arc
melting using raw materials with a purity greater than
99.95%, and argon as the ﬂux gas. The melted FeCoNi-
Cr0.6Al0.4 alloy ingots were homogenized for six hours at
1200 /C176 C, water quenched, and then cold rolled to 66% volume
reduction. The alloy quenched to room temperature using ice
water. To relieve internal stresses and fully recrystallize the cold
rolled sheets, they are recrystallized at 1200 /C176 C for ﬁve
minutes. The recrystallized samples were then heat treated at
600, 700 , 800, and 900 /C176 C for 24 h, followed by water
quenching. For ease of description, these alloys are labeled A-
600, A-700, A-800, and A-900, respectively.
X-ray diffractometry (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance X) was
used to detect and analyze the phase of the high-entropy alloy.
The working voltage was 40 kV , the working current was
110 mA, the scanning speed was 10 /C176 /min, and the measuring
range was 20 to 100 /C176 . The microstructure and wear morphology
of the high-entropy alloys were investigated using scanning
electron microscopy (SEM, FEG Quanta 450). Elemental
changes and distributions on the worn surfaces were examined
with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). The tensile prop-
erties of FeCoNiCr0.6Al0.4 HEA were tested at room temper-
ature using AGX-50KNVD type electronic universal tensile
tester with a strain rate of 1 910
/C0 3 s/C0 1. The HSR-2 M
reciprocating friction and wear testing machine was used to
evaluate the HEA /C213s friction and wear properties, and the
grinding ball was a Si3N4 ball with a diameter of 3 mm.
Reciprocating wear tests were conducted under loads of 3N,
5N, 7N, and 10N, with a friction time of 30 min, a recipro-
cating distance of 5 mm, and a frequency of 300 r/min.

3.2 Tensile Property
Figure 4 shows the tensile engineering stress–strain dia-
grams of FeCoNiCr0.6Al0.4 HEA after different aging treat-
ments. Table 2 shows the tensile properties at room temperature
of the alloys after different aging treatments. The A-600 alloy
exhibits the lowest tensile strength and the highest elongation at
break. The tensile strengths of the alloys increased to varying
degrees with increasing aging temperatures. The tensile
strengths of A-700, A-800, and A-900 alloys were improved
by 457 MPa, 373 MPa, and 150 MPa, respectively, compared
to the A-600 alloy. These three alloys maintained their
plasticity, with elongation at break around 40%. A-700 has a
high tensile strength-elongation product (the product of tensile
strength and elongation at break). The calculated strong
plasticity of the A-700 alloy is 42809 MPa%, an increase of
about 15,000 to 30,000 MPa% compared to typical TRIP steels
(Ref 25, 26). The best combination of high strength and
toughness appears at an aging temperature of 700 /C176 C, making
the A-700 alloy /C213s tensile properties the best.

The tensile strength of the alloy initially increases and then
decreases with rising aging temperatures. The A-600 alloy has
the lowest tensile strength, while the A-700 alloy has the best
tensile strength. This is because when the aging temperature is
600 /C176 C, the L12 phase begins to precipitate inside the grain,
and the B2 phase begins to precipitate at the grain boundary.
The A-600 alloy consists of the FCC phase, the L12 phase and
the B2 phase. However, the volume fractions of both the L12
and B2 phases within the alloy are low and small in size, and
their ability to impede dislocation motion is poor. As a result,
the A-600 alloy has the lowest strength and the best ductility.
As the aging temperature increases, the rod-like B2 phase
begins to appear inside the alloy grains. The number of L12
phases gradually increases, and coarsening occurs. The grain
boundaries are gradually covered by the brittle B2 phase.

remains high at 37%. This may be because the high volume

consistent with the results of the present experiments. The A-

3.3 Wear Behavior
The wear properties of the A-700 alloy are shown in Fig. 7.
The coefﬁcient of friction of HEAs varies between 0.10 and
0.20. The alloy /C213s average coefﬁcient of friction curve is steeper
as the load increases when the load is below 7N. The alloy /C213s
average coefﬁcient of friction curve ﬂattens out when the load
is greater than 7 N. The alloy /C213s average coefﬁcient of friction
curve reaches its minimum value when the load is 10N. The
average coefﬁcient of friction decreases with increasing load.
This is because during the friction process, with the increase in
the load, the coverage area of the oxide layer formed on the
alloy wear surface also gradually expands. This prevents direct
contact between the friction partner and the alloy surface to a
greater extent and effectively reduces material wear (Ref 30-
33). As the load increases, the alloy wear also increases. The
alloy showed the highest wear of 0.0439 mm
3 at a load of 10N.
With the increase in load, the growth rate of alloy wear
gradually levels off.
Figure 8 illustrates the surface morphology of the alloy after
wear under different loads. As shown in the ﬁgure, all wear
surfaces exhibit numerous furrows of varying depths and
widths, along with scattered abrasive debris. This is character-
istic of abrasive wear (Ref 34–37). Additionally, the alloy /C213s
wear surface forms wavy traces. This results from the plastic
deformation of the alloy during friction. Two plastic ﬂows in
the same direction meet two ﬂows in the opposite direction.

Figure 10 illustrates the wear process diagram of the HEA
under different loads. The load can affect the wear behavior of
the alloy by changing the contact area between the friction pair
and the alloy surface. When the load is low, friction occurs only
at some of the contact points, resulting in small abrasive chips
on the alloy wear surfaces. The number and size of contact
points on the wear surface of the alloy increases with the
increase in load. The actual contact area of the friction partner
with the alloy surface increases. With the increase in load, the
wear amount of alloy increases. However, the wear rate
decreases when the load is higher. This may be due to the
formation of an oxide ﬁlm on the surface. The surface oxide
layer prevents direct contact between the friction partner and
the surface of the alloy, which signiﬁcantly reduces the wear of
the alloy (Ref 30-33). As the load increases, the oxidation
degree of the alloy surface becomes higher and higher. This is
because the degree of engagement between the friction pair and
the alloy surface increases with the increase in the load. The
higher the load, the larger the contact point area and the larger
the size of the abrasive chips produced. Higher loads cause the
larger sized chips in the alloy wear surface to break up and form
a large number of smaller sized chips. The smaller sized
abrasive chips are then extruded back into a dense form,
covering the wear surface of the alloy and forming a dense
oxide layer. Additionally, the dense oxide layer acts as a
lubricant, reducing the alloy /C213s wear rate. Consequently, the
alloy achieves the lowest average coefﬁcient of friction and the
highest wear at a 10N load.
