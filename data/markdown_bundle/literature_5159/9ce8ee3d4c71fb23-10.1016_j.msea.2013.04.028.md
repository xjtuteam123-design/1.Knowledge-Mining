abstract
A high-strength Fe85Ni15 alloy with lath martensitic structure was obtained by the self-propagating high-
temperature synthesis technique. After tempering at 500 1C for 2 h, the hardness increased from 3.8 to
5.1 GPa, and the compression yield strength increased from about 1 100 to 1500 MPa without much
sacriﬁce of ductility. The strengthening effect might be attributed to the evolution of lath martensitic
morphology and the reverse martensitic transformation.

1. Introduction
Martensite alloys have attracted great interest because of their
excellent mechanical properties and novel structures [1–4]. Due to
the differences in compositions, cooling rates, or heating proce-
dures, martensite shows different morphologies, such as lath,
lenticular, plate, butter ﬂy etc. [5–7]. Among these structures, lath
martensite steels have been broadly used as engineering materials
due to their high strength combined with good ductility [8,9].
Martensite aging steels represent one category of lath martensite
alloys [3,10]. The use of carbon is restricted in these steels, because
the carbides may precipitate in the grain boundaries and lath
boundaries, which may be detrimental to the steels ' toughness and
weldability [1 1]. Ni, Co, Mo, and Ti are often used in martensite
aging steels to achieve the precipitation hardening effect, because
during the aging process, the precipitation of second phases such
as Ni 3Mo and Ni 3Ti in the matrix and the substructure evolution
could improve the mechanical properties of the aging steels [3].
Researchers often attribute the strengthening effect to the pre-
cipitation of the second phase, but the substructure evolution is
neglected and scarcely investigated. In the present study, the
Fe85Ni15 alloy with lath martensitic structure free from Co, Mo
and Ti was produced. The alloy was tempered from 300 to 800 1C
for 2 h followed by air-cooling, and increases of hardness and
compression strength were achieved without much loss of
ductility after tempering at 500 1C. The effect of tempering on
the strength improvement was studied according to the evolution
of substructures.

3. Results and discussion
The variations of hardness as a function of tempering tempera-
ture for the Fe 85Ni15 alloy are shown in Fig. 1. It can be seen that
the hardness does not change after tempering at 300 and 400 1C
for 2 h; thereafter, it increases by 34% from 3.8 to 5.1 GPa at 500 1C
tempering for 2 h, and after tempering at higher temperatures of
600, 700 and 800 1C, the hardness decreases gradually.
Fig. 2 plots the typical compressive stress –strain curve of the
Fe85Ni15 alloys without and with tempering at 500 1C for 2 h. It is
evident that the alloy exhibits high strength of 1300 MPa before
the tempering process. Impressively, after tempering at 500 1C for
2 h, the compression strength of the alloy increases by 38% from
1300 to 1800 MPa without much loss of ductility, and this is in
accordance with the increase of the hardness from 3.8 to 5.1 GPa.
The tempering at 500 1C for 2 h signi ﬁcantly strengthens the alloy,
which implies that the structure might be changed during the
tempering process.
Fig. 3 shows the typical X-ray diffraction patterns of the original
Fe85Ni15 alloy and the alloy tempered at 500 1C for 2 h. We can see
that the original Fe 85Ni15 alloy mainly consists of body-centered
cubic (bcc) martensite phase ( α′); some retained austenite ( γ)
existing as ﬁlms between the laths cannot be detected by the X-ray
diffraction method due to its low content. After tempering at
500 1C for 2 h, some martensite transforms into austenite, and the
austenite fraction is about 18 vol% according to the calculation
method in Ref. [14]. That is to say some martensite has reversed to
austenite and been retained after tempering at 500 1C, but the
transformation is not dramatic.
The phase transformation temperatures of the alloy are esti-
mated by the differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) as shown in
Fig. 4. The reverse transformation of martensite to austenite begins
at As (521 1C) and almost ﬁnishes at Af (717 1C). On the cooling
procedure, the martensite transformation begins at Ms (289 1C)
and almost ﬁnishes at Mf (228 1C). The reverse transformation of
martensite to austenite does not begin evidently at 500 1C, so after
tempering at 500 1C for 2 h, the basic lath martensite structure
may not change intensively. From the XRD patterns, some mar-
tensite transform into austenite after tempering at 500 1C for 2 h.
We could speculate that the main matrix of lath structure is not
changed, and due to the Ni segregation [15], the reverse transfor-
mation of some martensite may happen at 500 1C, which could be
useful to re ﬁne the structure and strengthen the alloy [16]. While
tempering at higher temperatures of 600, 700 and 800 1C, the
hardness decreases gradually, because the higher temperatures
further induce the massive reverse martensitic transformation,
and higher content of austenite may be retained after tempering
or the lath structure reconstructions, which may decrease the
alloy's hardness [17].

Detailed high resolution transmission electron microscope

Lath martensitic steels are broadly used in the industrial ﬁeld. In
aging steels, tempering is usually conducted to induce the precipita-
tion hardening and recovery of the dislocation cell structure [1 8].I t
has been reported that nickel is necessary for the alloy to maintain
high strength and good toughness, and cobalt can raise the
martensitic transformation temperature (Ms) and lower the solubi-
lity of molybdenum and then the molybdenum will strengthen
the steel with titanium because of the formation of Ni 3 (Mo, Ti)
precipitates in the martensite matrix [3]. In the present study, there
is no such precipitation hardening element of cobalt, molybdenum
and titanium, so the hardening effect mainly originates from the
structure response. Herein, the Al content is 1 .08 at% in the alloy, and
the precipitations of Ni3Al and/or NiAl may be formed, but it is not
observed by the TEM observations. During the lath martensitic
transformation, the prior austenite grain is divided into packets,
blocks, and parallel laths [1 8,19]. Consequently, several types of
internal interfaces are introduced into the alloy and present as
barriers to the slip of dislocations to achieve the strengthening
effects [18]. During the transformation, large local deformation leads
to the formation of high density dislocations within the laths in a
short time, and a large strain is introduced at the same time [20].A s
reported, subgrain boundaries are major obstacles against gliding of
mobile dislocations, and strengthening by subgrain boundaries is
considered to be one of the important strengthening mechanisms
for the martensitic steels [21]. During the tempering process, the
recovery of the lath martensitic structure results in the dislocations'
evolution of annihilation and reconstruction to produce dislocation
cells and/or subgrains in nanometer ranges, which may be one of
the important strengthening mechanisms for the present martensi-
tic steel after tempering [1 8,19,22]. The HRTEM image shows that
some nanocrystallines with different orientations formed in the
material, which may result from the reverse martensitic transforma-
tion. The interfaces of theseﬁne structures may block the movement
of dislocation leading to the increase of the strength[19].A sar e s u l t ,
the strength of the Fe 85Ni15 alloy is evidently improved after
tempering at 500 1Cf o r2h .
