1. Introduction
The pursuit of wear-resistant metallic materials remains vital for 
advanced engineering applications such as aerospace propulsion, energy 
conversion, and high-speed machining, where sliding components are 
often subjected to elevated temperatures and heavy loads [1,2] . Tradi -
tional alloys like 316 stainless steel [3] and 40CrNiMoA steel [4] , 
though widely used, exhibit significant thermal softening and 
oxidation-induced surface degradation above 600
◦
C, undermining their 
long-term reliability under harsh operating conditions. Even Ni [4] , TiAl 
[5] , and Co-Cr [6] based superalloys, which offer superior hot strength 
and oxidation resistance, typically suffer from wear rates as high as 
10

and adverse tribo-oxidation. The governing mechanisms of wear vary 
significantly with temperature: at room to moderate temperatures ( ≤
400

contact, and mild oxidation [7 , 8 ] , while at temperatures above 600
◦
C, 
synergistic effects among plastic flow, diffusion-driven microstructure 
evolution, and accelerated oxidation increasingly control material loss 
[9 , 10] . To meet the growing demand for metallic materials that can 
retain robust anti-wear ability under such extremes, chemically complex 
alloys (CCAs), also referred to as high-entropy alloys, have emerged as 
promising candidates [11 ] . Their multi-principal element compositions, 
high configurational entropy, and sluggish diffusion behavior enable 
CCAs to form stable multi-phase microstructures with tunable deform -
ability and oxidation resistance [12 , 13] .
To engineer CCAs capable of maintaining wear resistance across a 
broad temperature range, several design strategies have been explored. 
One common approach involves incorporating solid lubricants, such as 
soft metals and fluorides, into metallic matrix to reduce interfacial 
adhesion by forming a lubricating tribo-film upon wear [1 , 14] . Another 
strategy entails developing ceramic-reinforced composites, in which 
hard phases such as carbides or borides enhance the load-bearing ca -
pacity and thermal stability [15 , 16] . However, both approaches face 
intrinsic challenges, including limited interfacial compatibility, uneven 
phase distribution, and rapid degradation under dynamic frictional 
loading [17 , 18] . In contrast, tailoring the precipitation behavior of CCAs 
to form in-situ heterostructured microstructures, particularly oxide

deformation mechanisms, dissipate stress over multiple length scales, 
and sustain the dynamic formation of protective tribolayers.
To resolve the inherent trade-offs between deformation tolerance 
and high-temperature wear resistance in CCAs, we propose a dual- 
precipitation strategy that enables the in-situ formation of composi -
tionally and structurally distinct oxide-intermetallic architectures. By 
coupling oxygen-affinitive and intermetallic-forming elements within a 
tailored FCC-based multi-component matrix, this approach promotes 
the controlled precipitation of nanoscale oxides (~31.2 vol%) and 
submicron intermetallics (~6.5 vol%) with hierarchical spatial distri -
bution and lattice-level ordering. Specifically, the alloy design leverages 
the strong oxygen affinity of Cr to promote the formation of an oxygen- 
supersaturated solid solution precursor during mechanical alloying 
(MA) [9] , followed by spark plasma sintering (SPS) to drive the 
sequential precipitation of hierarchical phases [27] . Such dual-scale 
heterogeneities serve not only as mechanical reinforcement sites, but 
also as active regulators of oxygen chemisorption, tribo-layer formation, 
and dynamic subsurface reconstruction under extreme 
thermal-mechanical conditions. As a result, the engineered Ni
