Structural Metals after Exposure to High Temperatures:
Residual Mechanical Properties and Predictions
Yancheng Cai,* Hung Wai Ho, Shuxiang Li, and Martin Mensinger
1. Introduction
Carbon steel (CS), a conventional structural material, is widely
adopted in the construction industry for its high strength-to-
weight ratio, fabrication ﬂexibility, and ease of installation and
transportation.[1,2] Recently, there has been an increasing use
of stainless steel (SS) [3,4] and aluminum alloy (AA) [5,6] in archi-
tectural and structural elements for their exceptional corrosion
resistance, a notable shortcoming of CS. The durability and aes-
thetic appearance of SS [4] and the lightweight and exceptional
formability of AA [6] further explain their increasing adoption
in various structures. As these materials
serve as load-bearing elements of a
structure, their mechanical properties are
crucial in structural design [7,8] and become
critical at high temperatures (e.g., during a
ﬁre) due to the deterioration of their
mechanical properties and creep effects, [9]
particularly the Young ’s modulus, 0.2%
proof stress (yield strength), and ultimate
strength. Recently, the material behavior
of high-strength reinforcement with a yield
strength of 670 N mm /C0 2 at elevated tem-
peratures was investigated as part of a
research project aimed at developing novel
composite columns. [10]
Incipient, growth, burning after ﬂash-
over, and decay are the four key phases
in ﬁre development. After incipient, tem-
perature rises in the second stage and
retains in the third stage, while ﬁnally
declines in the last stage. [11] In the last
stage, the primary concern focuses on
the safety of metal structures and structural
members after being exposed to high tem-
peratures (at post ﬁre conditions). It is cost-
consuming and time-consuming if all the metal structures
exposed to ﬁre are dismantled and replaced by new alternates,
i.e., new metal structural members. On the other hand, directly
reusing or reinforcing these structures raises signi ﬁcant safety
concerns. Therefore, assessing the performance of metal struc-
tures after exposed to ﬁre is crucial. Mechanical properties are
the fundamental prerequisites in evaluating substructures and
structural members, particularly in terms of stiffness and load
resistance. Given the importance of these mechanical properties
after exposed to high temperature (i.e., exposure to high temper-
ature and then cooled down), this assessment provides essential
engineering knowledge for determining structural safety after a
ﬁre.[1– 4] With the increasing use of SS and AAs in construction, it
is crucial to investigate their post ﬁre mechanical properties in
line with those of CS. This provides fundamental yet critical
knowledge of how these metals behave after exposing to high
temperatures (i.e., after a ﬁre), essential for safety design and
assessment.
To support the analysis of the safety of (carbon) steel struc-
tures after their exposure to high temperatures, wide variety
of research works on steel [1,2,12– 19] have been carried out in
the last decades to investigate their post ﬁre mechanical proper-
ties, including the Young ’s modulus, 0.2% proof stress (yield
strength), ultimate strength, and so on. These research works
covered steel with different nominal 0.2% proof stress ranging

Most research works have focused on studying the post ﬁre (after exposure to
high temperatures) mechanical properties of either carbon steel, stainless steel,
or aluminum alloy. There are few studies directly comparing the mechanical
properties of these structural metals after exposure to high temperatures, and
there is no universal predictive equation describing their post ﬁre retention
factors. Herein, mechanical properties of structural metals (i.e., carbon steel,
stainless steel, and aluminum alloy) after exposure to high temperatures are
experimentally investigated. The mechanical properties of these structural metals
are compared and rigorously analyzed. Existing equations are assessed by
comparing their predictions against the corresponding post ﬁre retention factors
of the coupon specimens. A uni ﬁed equation for the post ﬁre mechanical
properties of structural metals is developed, proposing different sets of coef ﬁ-
cients for various structural metals based on the new test data from this study
and the existing literature. The uni ﬁed equation generally provides a safe and
convenient assessment method, enabling practical engineers to determine the
mechanical properties of metal structures after exposure to high temperatures in
terms of Young ’s modulus, 0.2% proof stress (yield strength), and ultimate
strength.

nominal 0.2% proof stresses of 460, [12,14,16] 690,[12,15,16,18,20]
890,[17] and 960 MPa. [2,13,19,20] A recent study by Uszball and
Knobloch[20] found that the mechanical properties of high-
strength steel with nominal 0.2% proof stress up to 960 MPa
in the cooling phase differ from those in the heating phase
and are not reversible due to phase changes of the microstruc-

specimens are ﬁrst heated up to the predetermined temperature
by furnace and then cooled down to the ambient temperature
before performing the tensile test for ﬁnding the necessary
mechanical properties. In the above studies, specimens are
heated to temperatures ranging from relatively low temperature
of 100 °C to high temperature of 1000 °C to study the subsequent
mechanical properties due to the exposure to different high tem-
peratures. In heating up the specimens, majority of studies
increase the temperature inside the furnace in a constant heating
rate (e.g., 15 °C min /C0 1).[2,12– 19] However, in a real ﬁre condition,
temperature should not be increased in a constant rate as pointed
out by Cai and Young. [1] Hence, an ISO 834 standard ﬁre curve
speciﬁed in ref. [21] is followed in their study to control the tem-
perature rise inside the lique ﬁed petroleum gas (LPG) furnace
for replicating the actual situation of a ﬁre.[1] By direct compar-
isons of the test results, they found that the differences between
the retention factors of material mechanical properties obtained
by the specimens heated inside the LPG furnace (following ISO
834 standard ﬁre curve) and electric furnace (with constant
heating rates) under the same post ﬁre temperature were
generally minor.
With regard to the methods of cooling down the test
specimens, research works have focused on air cooling and water
cooling to recreate the different cooling down scenarios after ﬁre
(e.g., steel specimens are cooled down by put into water versus
those cooled down inside furnace after the soaking time).
These studies suggest that, apart from Young ’s modulus, other
mechanical properties (i.e., 0.2% proof stress and ultimate
strength) of the tested steel specimens will signi ﬁcantly change
if they are heated over certain critical temperatures and cooled
down.[13– 17] Such critical temperatures are in a range of
500– 700 °C and depend on the grade of steel, for example,
600 °C for S690, [13] 700 °C for S460, [14] 500 °C for Q690, [15]
and 500 °C for Q890. [17] On the other hand, the effect of elevated
temperatures on the Young ’s modulus of steel is obscure.
In some studies, no or negligible (e.g., 5% decrease [14]) effect
of elevated temperatures is found on the Young ’s modulus,[14– 17]
while signiﬁcant reduction, for example, over 30% reduction for
heating up to 1000 °C, [12] is found in other studies. [1,2,12,13,18,19]
Comparing the air cooling and water cooling of steel specimens,
most of the studies showed that the specimens with water cool-
ing have a higher residual yield strength (0.2% proof stress) and
ultimate strength, but a lower ductility (i.e., a smaller fracture
strain) as compared to those specimens cooled by air. [15– 17]
Such ﬁndings could be explained in terms of microscopic mor-
phology of which different phases of iron (e.g., austenite phase
and martensite phase) are formed due to the different rate of
cooling,[16] where the specimens were cooled down using
ﬁre-ﬁghting foam at post ﬁre condition in order to better
replicate the actual cooling rate in the real case of ﬁre.
These studies [1,2,12– 20] on the mechanical properties were con-
ducted by tensile coupon tests, where a more recent study by
Uszball and Knobloch [20] conducted a comprehensive test pro-
gram on the post ﬁre mechanical properties of normal strength
steel and high-strength steel during the different cooling phases
after exposed to the maximum temperatures, e.g., post ﬁre
mechanical properties at 400, 550, and 700 °C after a maximum
temperature of 900 °C. It is worth noting that recently, the inves-
tigation of steel at post ﬁre condition has moved from material
level by tensile coupon tests to structural component level such
as members with I-section, square hollow section, and circular
hollow section under different loading conditions. [22– 26]
Stainless steel, due to its superior characteristics over CS
(conventional structural steel), is becoming increasingly popular
in various building and civil works as structural load-bearing ele-
ments. Such superior characteristics come from the additional
alloying elements, such as chromium and manganese, which will
also change the stress – strain relationship and mechanical prop-
erties of SS at both ambient and elevated temperatures. [27]
In addition, SS generally has lower emissivity at its surface than
CS, which leads to a delay in temperature rising during heating
stage and takes longer time in cooling phase. To ensure the safety
of SS structure after ﬁre, recently, similar research works have
been conducted to investigate the post ﬁre mechanical properties
in different types of SS, including austenitic SS, [27– 30] ferritic
SS,[28,31,32] and duplex SS. [28,33,34] In general, it was found that
0.2% proof stress of these three types of SS remains unchanged
after exposure to temperature up to 400 – 600 °C and decreases
beyond these temperature levels. [27– 29,31– 34] For ferritic SS, after
the decrease, it is found that the 0.2% proof stress will increase
again as it is heated up to 750 – 900 °C.[28,31,32] This phenomenon
can be explained by the formation of martensite in the ferritic
structure.[31] For SS, elevated temperatures have no signi ﬁcant
inﬂuence on the Young ’s modulus.[27,31,33] Comparing the three
types of SS, austenitic alloy has the best performance in retaining
0.2% proof stresses under elevated temperatures followed by
duplex alloy and then ferritic alloy. [28] In addition to typical
SS, the mechanical properties of stainless-clad bimetallic steel
(i.e., CS cladded with SS) after exposure to high temperatures
were also investigated, and it was found to be similar to the prop-
erties of the corresponding carbon and SS. [35,36]
Apart from CS and SS, attention has increasingly been
focused on different types of AAs due to their increasing usage
in metal structures for their light weight and corrosion
resistance.[37,38] To facilitate the design of structures using
AAs and their safety after ﬁre, research works have been carried
out to study the post ﬁre mechanical properties of different types
of AAs, including 5000 series [39] which alloyed with magnesium,
6000 series[40– 43] which alloyed with magnesium and silicon, and
7000 series[40,44] which alloyed with zinc. In these studies, tensile
coupon tests were also carried out after the specimens are
exposed to prede ﬁned high temperatures and then cooled down.
However, as the AAs have a lower melting point than CS and SS,
the high temperatures adopted in these studies are only up to
550 °C[40– 42] instead of 1000 °C like for steels. [17,18,29,30] It was
found that the effect of post ﬁre condition on the Young ’s modu-
lus is uncertain. Some studies [40,42] suggested that there will be
no, or insigni ﬁcant, change on post ﬁre Young ’s modulus while

In the above studies, it is shown that the mechanical proper-
ties of the structural metals, i.e., CS (conventional structural
steel), SS, and AA, varied after exposure to high temperatures.
In order to predict the retained mechanical properties after
exposed to different high temperatures (elevated temperatures),
various studies have made use of their experimental results to
develop corresponding predictive equations [1,2,12– 19,28,31– 34,40– 44]
for the respective metals. These predictive equations are devel-
oped for the retention factors of the mechanical properties as
compared to the unheated condition for each speci ﬁc metal.
In these proposed equations, the Young ’s modulus, 0.2% proof
stress (yield strength), and ultimate strength are the most con-
sidered mechanical properties[12,12– 19,28,31– 34,40– 44] as these prop-
erties are crucial in evaluating the post ﬁre behavior of metal
structural members and, thus, for the analysis of the safety of
the associated super structures. It should be noted that apart
from these three mechanical properties, predictive equations
were also proposed for the post ﬁre retention factors of ultimate
strain, e.g., refs. [2,17,44], fracture strain, e.g., refs. [41 – 43], and
other levels of stress and strain. [19,44] In the above studies, it was
found that the variation of mechanical properties is different at
different ranges of high-temperature levels. Thus, different pre-
dictive equations were proposed for each of the mechanical prop-
erties (i.e., either Young ’s modulus, 0.2% proof stress, or
ultimate strength) at different range of high-temperature
levels.[1,2,12,13,16,17,19,28,31,32,34,40– 44] For example, predictive equa-
tions of the retention factors of 0.2% proof stress at different
exposed high temperatures are separately proposed from 22 to
300 °C, from 300 to 550 °C, and from 550 to 900 °C. [1]
Moreover, different predictive equations are also proposed due
to different ways of cooling (i.e., air cooling and water
cooling).[14,15,17,19]
In the above literature, the majority of investigations have
focused on studying the post ﬁre mechanical properties of either
CS, SS, or AA, and subsequently, the corresponding predictive
equations for each type of material were proposed. However,
there are few studies that directly compare the post ﬁre mechani-
cal properties of different metals and develop a universal predic-
tive equation that can be used to describe their post ﬁre retention
factors in mechanical properties.
This study aims to ﬁll this gap for a better understanding and
safe prediction of the mechanical properties of structural metals
(i.e., CS, SS, and AA) after exposure to high temperatures. This
article is organized as follows. Section 2 gives the details of metal
specimens fabricated and the procedure of experimental investi-
gation in this study. Post ﬁre mechanical properties of the struc-
tural metals obtained from the tests are discussed with rigorous
analysis in Section 3, where existing prediction equations for the
corresponding metals are assessed in Section 4. In Section 5, a
uniﬁed predictive equation for mechanical properties of struc-
tural metals is developed with different sets of coef ﬁcients pro-
posed for different structural metals based on the new test data in
this study and those from the literature. The uni ﬁed equation
provides a safe and convenient assessment (in a conservative
manner) for mechanical properties of structural metals after
exposure to high temperatures. Finally, the conclusions are pre-
sented in Section 6.
2. Experimental Investigation
2.1. Test Specimens
In this study, test specimens for CS (S275 with nominal yield
strength of 275 MPa), SS (lean duplex, type EN 1.4162), and
AA (types of 6061 and 7075) were prepared for carrying out ten-
sile coupon tests to investigate their mechanical properties after
exposure to high temperatures. The coupons for CS and AA are,
respectively, extracted from the corresponding CS sheet and AA
sheets. The CS sheet has a nominal thickness of 2.0 mm, while
the AA sheets have nominal thickness of 2.0 and 3.2 mm, for
types of 6061-T6 and 7075-T6, respectively. For lean duplex SS
(LDSS) coupons, they were extracted from LDSS rectangular
hollow sections with nominal section size 60 /C2 40 /C2 2 (depth /C2
width /C2 thickness) in mm and square hollow section with nomi-
nal section size 100 /C2 100 /C2 3 in mm. The dimensions of the
coupon specimens were designed according to the requirements
speciﬁed in AS2991 Standard.[45] The measured gauge width and
thickness of the coupon specimens of CS, LDSS, and AA before
and after exposure to high temperatures, including those at
ambient temperature condition (without exposure to high tem-
peratures), are shown in Table 1 .
2.2. Specimen Labeling
The coupon test specimens, as shown in Table 2 and 3, are
labeled to identify the type of metal. In this study, CS, LDSS,
and AA alloy are abbreviated as CS, LDSS, and AA, respectively.
Since there are two types of AA, hence, the aluminum coupon

2.4. Heating and Cooling Methods
2.4.1. General
Metal coupon specimens in this study were heated by LPG fur-
nace and electric furnace. The LPG furnace is able to follow vari-
ous international ﬁre standard curves (e.g., ISO-834 Part-1
standard ﬁre curve[21]) to heat up to 1200 °C. On the other hand,
the electric furnace (Model HT-1200 Split Tube Type High
Temperature Furnace) could be heated up to 1200 °C with
accuracy of 0.1 °C by a series of inside heating elements.
Compared to electric furnace, LPG furnace is able to provide
a higher heating rate.
In the literature, it is found that heating rate (e.g., 10 or
100 °C min/C0 1) has negligible effects on the mechanical proper-
ties of cold-formed high-strength steel after exposure to elevated
temperatures.[46] Also, the study of Huang and Young [31] on the
mechanical properties of ferritic SS (FSS) after being exposed to
elevated temperature suggested that the effect of soaking time is
minimal. Moreover, Cai and Young [1] found that different heat-
ing methods (e.g., using LPG and electric furnace) has negligible
effects for the post ﬁre mechanical properties of thin sheet CS in
different grades and thickness. Hence, the SS specimens in this
study will be heated by LPG furnace following the ISO-834 Part 1
standard ﬁre curve.[21] After heating, the SS specimens will soak
for 15 min at the target peak temperature before cooling down to
the ambient temperature inside the furnace. [1] On the other
hand, CS and AA specimens in this study will be heated by elec-
tric furnace at a heating rate of 15 °C min /C0 1, soak for 20 min and
then cool down to the ambient temperature inside the furnace.

nominal peak temperature, the specimens are soaked for at least
20 min, but generally less than 30 min, and then the electric fur-

down inside furnace until the temperature falls below 50 °C.

4. Mechanical Properties from Test Results
4.1. General
In general, the structural metals CS, LDSS (LDSS-2.0 and LDSS-
3.0), and AA (AA-6061 and AA-7075) behave quite differently
after exposure to different high temperatures that resulted in dif-
ferent residual mechanical properties, including the Young ’s
modulus, 0.2% proof stress, and ultimate strength. Compared
to CS, LDSS is more capable to resist the decline in 0.2% proof
stress ( f0.2) and ultimate strength ( fu) after exposure to high tem-
peratures as, in general, larger retention factors for these
mechanical properties could be found. For AA (AA-6061 and
AA-7075), the deterioration of mechanical properties starts at
a relatively lower temperatures due to its lower melting point
as compared to those of CS and LDSS. Also, the reduction of
retention factors for f0.2 and fu is substantially larger than CS
and LDSS, showing that the mechanical properties of AA are
adversely affected after exposure to high temperatures. The pre-
dictive equations from the literature [33,40,49] for the retention fac-
tors of the similar metals after exposure to high temperatures
were used to assess the retention factors obtained from the exper-
imental program in this study. It was found that these existing
predictions are either very conservative or unconservative in pre-
dicting the retention factors of CS, LDSS, and AA.

Retention factor of ultimate strength ( fu,p/fu,r) for the three types
of structural metals are shown in Figure 12 . In this ﬁgure, it is

temperature as high as possible with a maximum of 1000 °C, and
then soaked and cooled down inside the furnace. The stress –
strain curves of these structural metals were measured, where
their post ﬁre mechanical properties were obtained, including
the Young ’s modulus ( E), 0.2% proof stress ( f0.2), ultimate
strength ( fu), ultimate strain, and fracture strain. Over 500 data
from the literature were collected and compiled together with the
test results in this study. The retention factors of different struc-
tural metals (i.e., CS, SS, and AA) were compared. The predictive
equations from the literature to the corresponding metals were
assessed.
Generally, it was found that the structural metal of SS has
higher retention factors than both CS and AA in mechanical
properties of E, f0.2, and fu after exposure to high temperatures,
while the structural metals of CS has higher retention factors
than AA in these mechanical properties. The existing equations
generally provided unconservative, or unsafe, predictions of the
retention factors for the structural metals. Based on the data sets
from the literature and this study, a uni ﬁed predictive equation
for retention factors of mechanical properties ( E, f0.2, and fu) was
developed, with different sets of coef ﬁcients proposed for differ-
ent structural metals after exposure to high temperatures. It is
shown that the uni ﬁed equation provided a safe and convenient
assessment for mechanical properties of structural metals
(CS, SS, and AA) after exposure to high temperatures. It can
be used by practical engineers to provide a fast and convenient
assessment on the mechanical properties of metal structures
after exposure to ﬁre, in terms of E, f0.2, and fu.
