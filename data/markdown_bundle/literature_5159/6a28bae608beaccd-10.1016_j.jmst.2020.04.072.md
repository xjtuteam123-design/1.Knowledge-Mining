## Page 3

The effect of nitrogen on microstructural evolution and tensile properties of transformation-induced 
plasticity (TRIP) Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 HEAs was investigated. Nitrogen was fully introduced in solid 
solution by pressure-induced melting technique. Nitrogen addition turned the TRIP alloy to a 
twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) alloy, and simultaneously improved the strength and elongation. 
For the nitrogen -doped HEA, the high yield strength is mainly resulted from the friction stress via 
interstitial strengthening eff ect, and the high ductility is originated from retained high strain-
hardening capability via the successive onset of dislocation accumulation and deformation twinning. 
The strain-hardening behavior and microstructural evolution at specified strains were revealed. 
Keywords: High-entropy alloy (HEA); Transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP); Twinning-induced 
plasticity (TWIP); Strength; Ductility

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) , originally proposed in 2004 , are a new class of materials [1, 2]. 
Fundamentally different from conventional alloys which are generally designed based on one major 
element, HEAs usually consist of at least five principal elements with a concentration between 5 at.% 
and 35 at.%, leading to high mixing entropy and a high tendency to form solid solution phase. In view 
of four core effects, i.e. high-entropy, severe lattice distortion, sluggish diffusion and cocktail effects, 
HEAs exhibit attractive properties, such as high strength [3-6], excellent irradiation resistance [7], 
wear resistance [8] and exceptional corrosion resistance [9, 10], and hence have attracted extensive 
attentions over the last decade. Previous studies suggested that HEAs with face-centered cubic (f.c.c.) 
structure have high tensile elongation but limited yield strength, which may hinder the usage for 
engineering applications as structural materials, such as the single-phase CoCrFeMnNi HEA that has 
been extensively studied [11-13]. Recently, a novel Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 (at.%) transformation-induced 
plasticity (TRIP) HEA has been reported by Li et al. [14], which is designed by adjusting the alloy 
composition based on CoCrFeMnNi system and hence the stacking fault energy (SFE) to screen for 
desirable compositions showing the TRIP effect [15]. This innovative multicomponent alloy system 
presents multiple deformation mechanisms including TRIP, twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP), 
dislocation slip and the formation of stacking faults and achieves synchronous improvement of 
strength and ductility compared to CoCrFe MnNi [14]. Such outstanding mechanical performance 
(especially high ductility) makes it a reliable base for further strengthening. 
As an important strengthening mechanism, the effect of interstitial atoms on metallic materials 
has been widely reported [16, 1 7]. O n this basis , interstitial HEA with nominal composition 
Fe49.5Mn30Co10Cr10C0.5 (at.%) was developed, showing a further increase in strength [18]. However, 
the formation of carbides leads to a significant reduction in ductility. Based on the fact that the 
precipitation kinetics of nitride is much slower than that of carbide [19], adding interstitial nitrogen

is a desirable alternation for improving the mechanical performance. Yang et al. [16] reported that the 
FeMnC TWIP steel achieved simultaneous enhancement in both strength and elongation via nitrogen 
addition, which could be attributed to the suppression of plastic instability. This reminds us that the 
addition of nitrogen may have a great influence on the mechanical properties of Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 
HEA. In the present work, we report the effect of nitrogen on tensile behavior of Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 
HEA fabricated by pressurized metallurgy, aimed to disclose the effect of nitrogen on deformation 
mechanism. 
The multicomponent alloy system Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 HEAs doped with 0 at.% and 1.15 at.% 
(corresponding to 0 wt.% and 0.29 wt.%, respectively) nitrogen were produced by pressure-induced 
melting with high purity Fe, Co, Cr and Mn raw materials, and the alloys with 0 at.% and 1.15 at.% 
nitrogen were designated as 0 N and 1 .15N, respectively. The nitrogen content of the HEAs was 
measured by a nitrogen/oxygen analyzer (LECO TC500). In order to avoid the accumulation of errors, 
at least three specimens for each alloy were tested. Fig. 1 shows the variation of weight fraction of 
phases with temperature for 0N and 1.15N HEAs as calculated with Thermo -Calc software . To 
prevent alloy overheating and residual stress -induced cracking during forging, the forging 
temperature should be controlled between (0.65-0.75) Tm and (0.85-0.90) Tm, where Tm is the melting 
point of the alloy [ 20]. As shown in Fig. 1, the melting points of 0N and 1.15N HEAs are 1321 °C 
and 1307 °C, respectively. Therefore, the ingots were hot forged into 100 mm × 30 mm plates within 
the temperature range of 980-1050 °C. The plates were homogenized at 1150 °C for 2 h and water-
quenched. Then rectangular samples with a thickness of 28 mm were hot rolled to 8 mm thickness 
sheets at 1050 °C (about 70% reduction in thickness). In order to ensure that the nitrogen atoms were 
fully dissolved into the matrix after annealing, the annealing temperature should be higher than the 
precipitation temperature of precipitation phase. According to the calculation results, the precipitation

temperature is 923 °C, so the hot-rolled plates were annealed at 950 °C for 40 min followed by water 
quenching. 
Microstructural characterization was performed by using a ZEISS GeminiSEM 300 scanning 
electron microscope (SEM) equipped with electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) and electron 
channeling contrast imaging (ECCI), and a JOEL JEM-2100 transmission electron microscope (TEM). 
The crystalline structures were identified by using a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (XRD) 
with Cu Kα radiation. Dog-bone shaped tensile specimens with the gauge section 15 mm × 4 mm × 
2.5 mm were used for tensile tests by a SANS-CMT 5105 electronic universal tensile testing machine 
with the strain rate of 1×10-3 s-1 at room temperature. 
The morphologies of the 0N and 1.15N HEAs after annealing are summarized in Fig. 2(a) and 
(b), respectively. Fully recrystallized microstructures with annealing twins were observed in both 
samples. The mean grain sizes of 0N and 1.15N HEAs were 18.8 and 14.7 μm, respectively, which 
were estimated measured by the line intercept method including all grain boundaries and twin 
boundaries, showing a slight decrease by nitrogen addition. Furthermore, no nitride was detected in 
1.15N HEA after careful inspection, suggesting that the nitrogen atoms were fully dissolved into the 
matrix in this work , facilitating the benefit from interstitial atoms via solid solution strengthening. 
Fig. 2(c) shows the XRD patterns of the annealed Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 HEAs with 0 at.% and 1.15 at.% 
nitrogen. Diffraction peaks from the f .c.c. lattice dominated in the observed patterns , and no 
significant difference was detected between 0N and 1.15N HEAs. No other peaks were observed even 
in the alloy with 1.15 at.% of nitrogen, demonstrating that both 0N and 1.15N HEAs in the annealed 
state have single phase f.c.c. structure. The lattice parameters of 0N and 1.15N HEAs were calculated 
from the XRD data by JADE 6.0 software, which are 0.3607 and 0.3612 nm, respectively. The solid 
solution of nitrogen element in f .c.c. matrix enhances the lattice distortion [21], thus leading to an

increase of the lattice parameter of the alloys with nitrogen addition. 
Fig. 3(a)-(c) presents the tensile stress -strain curves and strain-hardening curves of the 0N and 
1.15N HEAs, and the squares marked on curves denote the initiation of necking according to the 
Considère criterion. As shown in Fig. 3(a), 1.15N HEA possesses a desired combination of strength 
and ductility with yield strength (YS) of 326 MPa, ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 680 MPa and 
uniform elongation (UE) of 53.57%, achieving simultaneous enhancement of strength and elongation 
compare to 0N HEA, i.e., an increment of 156 MPa in YS and 9.10% in UE. With respect to the 
superior tensile properties of 1.15N HEA, a comparison of YS versus UE with 0N HEA is shown in 
Fig. 3(d). Given that the similar grain sizes of the two alloys and no precipitates in 1.15N HEA, the 
improvement in YS could be attributed to the effect of interstitial nitrogen via solid solution 
strengthening. In this case, nitrogen atoms could be carried along with the dislocations as they move 
through the lattice, resulting in dislocations dragging and hence the increased lattice frictional forces 
[22]. Additionally, the size of the interstitial atoms is substantially smaller than that of the solvent 
atoms in the alloy. In contrast to the undoped reference material , the addition of interstitial atom s 
could produce a higher strain field and cause larger elastic atomic size misfit [23, 24]. The elastic 
interaction between interstitial atoms and dislocations could lead to the lattice distortion of the alloy, 
which ultimately contributes to the enhancement of YS [25]. It is noteworthy that the YS increases 
by 54 MPa per 0.1 wt.% in this work. A similar result has been reported for Fe -15Mn-0.6C-2Cr-xN 
(wt.%) TWIP steels with grain sizes of about 30 μm (measured by excluding the twin boundaries) 
[19], and the YS increases by 47 MPa per 0.1 wt.% nitrogen in their research. Regarding Fe-22Mn-
xC (wt.%) TWIP steels with similar grain sizes, the YS increases by 19 MPa per 0.1 wt.% carbon 
[26]. The above results indicate that nitrogen atom is more effective in improving YS than carbon 
atom, which is most likely contributed by the higher binding enthalpy between nitrogen atoms and

dislocations than that of carbon atoms, leading to a stronger pinning of dislocations [27]. 
Fig. 3(b) shows the true stress-strain curves of the 0N and 1.15N HEAs, from which the strain-
hardening rate curves were calculated, as shown in Fig. 3(c). The strain-hardening rate of the HEAs 
decreases monotonically with increasing strain whether nitrogen is added or not. In the early stage of 
the deformation (with a true strain below 10%), the strain-hardening rate of 0N HEA remains more 
or less constant relative to 1.15N HEA. At this stage, the strain-hardening rate of 0N HEA is about 
700-800 MPa higher than that of 1.15N HEA, and then declines continuously until necking occurs. 
However, a different trend was observed for 1.15N HEA. The strain-hardening rate of 1.15N HEA 
decreases smoothly with strain until the necking point , showing the better retention of strain -
hardening. In th is case, it is necessary to reveal the deformation microstructures to explore 
mechanisms for the s imultaneously enhancement in strength and ductility of this alloy by nitrogen 
addition. 
The microstructural evolutions of the 0N and 1.15N HEAs subjected to tensile deformation by 
10% and 30% were analyzed by XRD and EBSD techniques, as shown in Fig. 4. Although both 
starting alloys possessed single-phase f.c.c. structure, significant differences were observed between 
the 0N and 1.15N HEAs in the evolution of phase composition during deformation. The XRD patterns 
and EBSD phase maps in Fig. 4(a) and (b) indicate that the 0N HEA undergoes a strain-induced 
martensitic phase transformation with the increase of strain. The volume fraction of transformed h.c.p. 
phase calculated by EBSD phase maps increases from 34.3% to 57.2% as t he strain increases from 
10% to 30%, showing prominent TRIP effect in 0N HEA. As for 1.15N HEA, no other phase but f.c.c. 
phase was detected by XRD and EBSD techniques (Fig. 4(c) and ( d)), demonstrating that the 
martensitic transformation was suppressed by nitrogen alloying. This observation indicates that the 
addition of nitrogen may result in an increase in SFE and hence the phase stability, which is similar

## Page 9

to the effect of carbon on this alloy [18]. Moreover, since the stacking faults are considered to act as 
nuclei for the formation of the h.c.p. phase [28], the improvement of SFE can reduce the number of 
nucleation sites of the h.c.p. phase and lead to the suppression of martensitic transformation. 
To further reveal the effect of nitrogen on the deformation behavior of the alloys, detailed 
microstructural characterization by ECCI and TEM techniques were performed at different strain 
levels, as shown in Fig. 5. At a true strain of 10%, h.c.p. plates and a large number of deformation 
twins (DTs) were observed in 0N HEA (Fig. 5(a1)), yet DTs could not be detected by ECCI in 1.15N 
HEA, as shown in Fig. 5(b1). Combined with the aforementioned inhibitory effect of nitrogen on the 
TRIP effect, this result further confirms that nitrogen alloying could increase the SFE of this alloy, 
thus increasing the stress necessary for twin nucleation and suppressing the formation of DTs [29]. In 
the early stage of plastic deformation (with a true strain below 10%), plenty of DTs and h.c.p. plates 
in 0N HEA divide the original grains into smaller subdomains, resulting in dynamic Hall-Petch effect 
[30]. Besides, the interactions between dislocations and the mixture of DTs and h.c.p. plates could 
generate an accumulation of high density of sessile dislocations along the twin boundaries (Fig. 5(a2)) 
and lead to a higher strain-hardening rate compared to 1.15N HEA [31, 32]. At a low strain level of 
10%, t he dislocation structure of 1.15N HEA is mainly characterized by planar slip even if the 
increased SFE by nitrogen addition (Fig. 5(b2)), same as the 0N HEA with relatively low SFE [14], 
which can be attributed to the fact that nitrogen can increase the metallic component of interatomic 
bonds in austenitic alloys [27] and assist the short range ordering (SRO) [33]. During the process of 
plastic deformation, once the SRO is cut by a forward dislocation, a particular lattice plane in SRO 
would become a preferable path for the following dislocations , which could assist the slip planarity 
of dislocations [33]. Hence the formation of SRO caused by nitrogen addition should be responsible 
for dislocation planarity in this alloy. In this case, the mean free path for mobile dislocations would 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 10

be shortened, which assists the efficient accumulation of dislocations [34]. Meanwhile, the increase 
in lattice friction stress caused by the solid-solutionized interstitial atoms is also beneficial to the 
accumulation of dislocations during plastic deformation , which could be c onducive to the 
improvement of strain -hardening capability [23]. Furthermore, Gavriljuk [27] proposed that the 
addition of nitrogen would promote the formation of SRO rather than clusters compared with carbon 
and hence suppress the dynamic strain aging (DSA) effect caused by the interactions between 
dislocations and clusters [35], assisting the homogeneous evolution of plastic strain and delaying the 
nucleation of DTs at the local regions such as grain boundaries, which could be regarded as another 
reason for the suppression of DTs in 1.15N HEA. 
With increasing the strain to 30%, the volume fraction of DTs and h.c.p. plates continues to 
increase significantly in 0N HEA, as shown in Fig. 5(c1) and (c2). As the grains are refined gradually 
by the progressive onset of DTs and h.c.p. plates , higher stresses are required for the nucleation of 
DTs and strain-induced martensitic phase transformation [36, 37], leading to a reduction in the 
formation rate of DTs and h.c.p. plates and thus a continuous drop in strain-hardening rate of 0N HEA 
with increasing strain until necking begins . In 1.15N HEA, DTs were observed and plastic 
deformation was dominated by the dislocation slip and DTs at a strain level of 30%, as shown in Fig. 
5(d1). Multiple twin-twin intersections were formed which leaded to an accumulation of high density 
dislocations at the twin boundaries (Fig. 5(d2)). The blockage of the dislocations propagation by the 
DTs is considered to be responsible for the sustainable retention of strain-hardening in 1.15N HEA 
during plastic deformation, exhibiting an excellent continuous work-hardening capability in contrast 
to 0N HEA. 
In summary, Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 HEAs with 0 and 1.15 at.% nitrogen were produced by the 
pressure-induced melting technique, and nitrogen atoms were fully dissolved in the matrix and no 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 11

precipitate was detected. Nitrogen addition turns the TRIP 0N HEA to a TWIP 1.15N HEA and leads 
to simultaneous enhancement of yield strength and ductility. The early onset of DTs and h.c.p. plates 
resulted in a higher strain-hardening rate in 0N HEA than 1.15N HEA at a tensile strain of 10%. With 
increasing the strain to 30%, t he strain-hardening rate decreased rapidly in 0N HEA due to the 
insufficient formation of DTs and h.c.p. plates, in contrast, the continuous generation of deformation 
twins and accumulation of dislocations sustain a high strain-hardening rate in 1.15N HEA. The 
present work paves a new and accessary way for achieving high yield strength and sustaining high 
ductility via nitrogen addition.
