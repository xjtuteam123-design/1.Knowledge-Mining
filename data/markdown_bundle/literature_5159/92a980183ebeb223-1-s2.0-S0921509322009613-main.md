ABSTRACT 
Nb containing FeCrAl alloys are a new class of promising candidates for accident-tolerant-fuel (ATF) materials 
used in nuclear power plants. The macrostructure, dynamic recrystallization, Laves phase evolution, tensile 
properties, and their correlations of the Nb containing FeCrAl alloy after friction stir welding (FSW) were sys -
tematically investigated at different welding parameters. FSW led to grain refinement and the stir zones (SZs) 
were mainly composed of fine equiaxed grains. The second-phase particles in the base materials (BM) were C14- 
type Fe
2
(Nb,Mo) with a diameter range of 500 – 900 nm. Under low heat input conditions, the majority of the 
Fe
2
(Nb,Mo) Laves phase particles in the SZs were dissolved into the matrix. With heat input increasing, very fine 
Fe
2
(Nb,Mo) Laves phase dispersions and Y
2
O
3 
in the nanoscale were re-precipitated. Microhardness distributions 
of Nb containing FeCrAl alloy joints obtained at different welding parameters exhibited identical “ U-shaped ” 
profiles. The average hardness of the base material (BM) was ~352HV, while that in the stir zone (SZ) was 
reduced to 224-229HV. The joints obtained at 400rpm-80 mm/min held optimal yield strength (389 ± 11 MPa) 
and ultimate tensile strength (492 ± 6 MPa). Micron-scale Fe

of FSW joints were lower than those of the BM. 
1. Introduction 
The Fukushima nuclear accident in 2011 is an unforgettable disaster 
caused by a hydrogen explosion under loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA) 
conditions [ 1 ]. Exploring advanced materials for accident-tolerant-fuel 
(ATF) applications to replace traditional Zr alloys is of particular sig -
nificance in improving the safety of nuclear power plants. A new class of 
alloys, known as nuclear-grade FeCrAl alloys, have shown great promise 
in ATF applications owing to their excellent high-temperature oxidation 
resistance [ 2 , 3 ], exceptional corrosion resistance [ 4 ], low 
radiation-induced swelling [ 5 ], and high tolerance to LOCA conditions 
[ 2 ]. Current efforts are mainly conducted on the optimization of alloy 
design [ 6 – 8 ] and in-reactor performance with tailored microstructure by 
various processing technologies [ 9 – 11 ]. Generation I FeCrAl alloys with 
high Cr content (~17% in wt.) exhibit serious aging hardening and 
irradiation hardening problems due to the formation of Cr-rich α
′
-phase, 
resulting in poor workability and weldability [ 12 ]. Decreasing the 
amount of Cr is an effective way to improve the workability and mitigate 
welding-induced cracking, however, it is inevitable to introduce a 
reduction of oxidation and corrosion resistance. To solve those issues, 
Generation II FeCrAl alloys are developed on the basic chemical 
composition of ~13%Cr and ~4%Al. More recently, the minor addition 
of reactive elements (RE), such as Y, Nb, Mo, etc., to form the Laves 
phase is the next strategy for improving the mechanical properties and 
thermal stability of the FeCrAl alloys [ 13 ].

and technological challenge to improve the mechanical properties of 
FeCrAl alloy joints [ 15 ]. 
Friction stir welding (FSW) is a rapidly developed technology 
featured by the unique solid-state joining. Since there is no melting 
taking place, joints fabricated by FSW can escape from the welding 
defects that may occur by fusion welding, such as voids, cracks, slag 
inclusion, and undercut [ 16 – 19 ]. By the same token, both the peak 
temperature and dwelling time of FSW are reduced, contributing to a 
mitigate the joint softening. More importantly, the microstructure in the 
stir zone (SZ) of the FSW joints is composed of refined equiaxed grains 
due to the severe dynamic recrystallization (DRX), which helps 
strengthen the joint by grain refinement. More recently, it is reported 
that minor alloying addition of Nb to form Fe
2
Nb-type Laves phase can 
effectively improve the thermal stability and mechanical properties of 
FeCrAl alloys in terms of precipitation strengthening [ 7 ]. Hu et al. [ 20 ] 
confirmed the feasibility of FSW in the high-quality joining of primary 
FeCrAl alloy without Nb addition by employing a rotations speed of 
400rpm with a welding speed of 100 mm/min. It is reported that no 
voids and cracks are observed in the friction stir welded FeCrAl alloys 
[ 20 ], shedding some light on the promising application prospect of FSW 
in joining advanced nuclear-grade FeCrAl alloys. Yamamoto et al. [ 13 ] 
suggested that the Nb addition would delay the recrystallization kinetics 
of FeCrAl alloys in comparison with the pure matrix. If the recrystalli -
zation is delayed by the added RE, the microstructure will hold different 
characteristics because it is recrystallization-mediated. However, for the 
precipitates strengthened Nb-containing FeCrAl alloy, microstructure 
evolution and mechanical performance of the FSW joints remain un -
clear. Furthermore, the effect of the welding parameters on the joint 
quality has not been explored. 
In this study, the FeCrAl alloy with Nb doping was prepared as the 
target material. Different rotation and welding speeds were employed to 
reveal the effect of welding parameters on the microstructure and me -
chanical properties of the precipitate strengthened FeCrAl alloy. 
Detailed microstructure characteristics including DRX grains, disloca -
tion distribution, the morphology of precipitates, and their interactions, 
were analyzed and compared. The discoveries in the present study may 
facilitate the application of advanced nuclear-grade FeCrAl alloy in 
nuclear power plants. 
2. Experimental procedure 
2.1. Alloy design and production 
The chemical composition of the Nb-containing FeCrAl alloy under 
investigation was Fe – 13Cr-4.0Al – 1Nb-2.0Mo-0.1Y. The addition of Nb 
can not only enhance the mechanical strength of the FeCrAl alloy but 
also mitigate the irradiation hardening problem [ 7 ]. Minor active 
element Y was added to increase the oxidation resistance under thermal 
cycling conditions [ 21 ]. The weight fraction of Mo was kept at ~2% to 
provide solution hardening for the body-centered cubic (bcc) FeCrAl 
matrix [ 14 ]. First, a 22 kg ingot was cast in a vacuum induction furnace, 
and then it was homogenized at 1100 
◦
C for 2 hh. Afterward, the ingot 
was forged using the residual heat after the previous homogenization 
treatment. Subsequently, it was hot-rolled at 1000 
◦
C, 800 
◦
C, 600 
◦
C, 
and 400 
◦
C to produce plates with a final thickness of 1.7 mm. The total 
thickness reduction was ~80%. After that, the plates were annealed at 
650 
◦
C for 1 hh to activate the microstructure recovery. 
2.2. Friction stir welding 
Fig. 1 a) shows a schematic drawing of the FSW process with a 
detailed indication of the relative positions of components. Argon 
shielding gas was ejected from the guns in front of and behind the tool, 
preventing the top surface of the weld from being oxidized. A conical 
probe tool with a probe length of 1.55 mm was employed to conduct 
FSW. The diameters of the shoulder and probe were 15 mm and 
3.7 – 5.5 mm, respectively. W – Re alloy held excellent strength and 
toughness at high temperatures and thus was used to fabricate the 
conical probe tool. The tilt angle and plunge depth were constant at 2.5
◦
and 0.15 mm, respectively. Our previous exploration of FSW of stainless 
steels indicated that a rotation speed of 400rpm matched with a welding 
speed of 100 mm/min was an optimal parameter [ 20 ]. Motivated by 
this, the welding speed in the present study fluctuated in the range of 
80 – 105 mm/min, and the corresponding rotation speeds were 375rpm 
or 400rpm. For the sake of simplicity, the description of welding pa -
rameters was uniformed into R – W form throughout the whole context, 
where R and W represent rotation and welding speeds, respectively. 
Four groups of welding parameters, including 375rpm-80 mm/min, 
400rpm-80 mm/min, 400rpm-100 mm/min, 400rpm-105 mm/min, 
were used to fabricate joints of Nb-containing FeCrAl alloy.

4. DRX characteristic of Nb containing FeCrAl alloy 
High strain rates, high temperatures, and very large strains inherent 
to FSW and their dependency on welding parameters significantly affect 
the microstructure evolution, and thus the performance of joints [ 24 ]. 
DRX is the intrinsic reason for the complete elimination of the original 
butt interface during FSW, and it is also the dominant mode of the 
microstructure evolution in the SZ. Fig. 4 and Fig. 5 depict the inverse 
pole figure (IPF) and image quality (IQ) of the regions in the SZs ob -
tained at different rotation and welding speeds. The positions of the 
target regions in the SZ have been indicated by rectangles in 
Fig. 2 a)-2d). Notably, the bottom region of the SZ obtained at 
105 mm/min was not analyzed due to the formation of a tunnel defect 
( Fig. 2 d). Based on the EBSD results, Fig. 6 depicts the grain size dis -
tributions with embedded labels of average grain size, and misorienta -
tion angle distributions with embedded marks of high-angle boundaries 
(HAB) fraction. Average grain sizes and number fraction of fine nuclei in 
the SZs are listed in Table 1 . It is seen that regardless of the welding 
parameters, the SZs are mainly composed of fine equiaxed grains owing 
to the complete DRX, and the average grain sizes in the SZs are thought 
to increase in the following order: top region (7.7 – 9.2 μ m), bottom re -
gion (9.3 – 10.7 μ m), and middle region (11.1 – 11.8 μ m). The high strain 
rate in the top region of SZ, as suggested by Arora et al. [ 25 ], caused by 
the severe shear deformation from the shoulder, should be responsible 
for the formation of the finest grains ( Figs. 4 and 6a ). With respect to the 
middle regions of the SZs, relatively coarser grains ( Fig. 5 a – d and 
Fig. 6 b) may be due to a higher peak temperature and a longer dwell 
time herein. Those observations indicate that higher temperature results 
in coarser grains, but a higher strain rate could refine the microstructure. 
Therefore, for the bottom regions of the SZs, where both the strain rate 
and temperature are lower than in other regions, it is understandable 
that the average grain size is moderate compared to those in the top and 
middle regions. When the rotation speed is increased from 375rpm to 
400rpm, the average grain sizes in the top and bottom regions are 
increased as a result of the increasing heat input ( Fig. 4 a – d, Fig. 5 e and f, 
Fig. 6 a and c). With the welding speed accelerating, the grains in the top 
and bottom regions are refined due to the decreased heat input 
( Fig. 4 c – h, Fig. 5 f and g, Fig. 6 a and c). Compared to the prominent 
changes in the top and bottom regions of the SZ, the average grain size in 
the middle region only exhibits a minor fluctuation in the range of 
11.1 – 11.8 μ m. This indicates that the DRX of the SZ near the top and 
bottom surfaces is more sensitive to the change in welding parameters.

6. Conclusions 
(1) Microhardness distributions of Nb containing FeCrAl alloy joints 
obtained at different welding parameters exhibit identical “ U- 
shaped ” profiles. The average hardness of the base material (BM) 
is ~352HV, while that in the stir zone (SZ) is reduced to 224- 
229HV. The joints obtained at 400rpm-80 mm/min hold 
optimal yield strength (389 ± 11 MPa) and ultimate tensile 
strength (492 ± 6 MPa). 
(2) FSW leads to grain refinement and the SZs are mainly composed 
of fine equiaxed grains. Discontinuous dynamic recrystallization 
(DDRX) plays a dominating role in refining the microstructure in 
the top and bottom regions of the SZ; while the formation of 
relatively large grains in the middle of SZ is originated from a 
synergetic effect of the strain rate and thermal field.
