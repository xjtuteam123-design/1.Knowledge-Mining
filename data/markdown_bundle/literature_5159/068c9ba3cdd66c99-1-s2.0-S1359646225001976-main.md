ABSTRACT
The development of high-temperature materials requires a deep understanding of phase transformations gov -
erning microstructural stability and mechanical properties. BCC + B2 superalloys, inspired by the γ / γ ′ micro -
structure in Ni-based superalloys, feature a ductile BCC matrix with coherent B2 precipitates that enhance high- 
temperature strength and creep resistance. This article examines the thermodynamics and phase transformation 
pathways leading to BCC + B2 microstructures in ferritic and refractory superalloys. Their stability depends on 
miscibility gaps, lattice misfit, elastic modulus contrast, and site occupancies. Ferritic superalloys (Fe-Al-based) 
are suited for applications below 1000 
◦
C, while refractory superalloys target extreme temperatures (1300 
◦
C – 1800 
◦
C). However, challenges remain in phase stability, oxidation resistance, and creep performance. 
Strategies for microstructural control through heat treatment, phase-field modeling, and CALPHAD-based design 
are discussed. Enhancing these aspects will optimize BCC + B2 superalloys for aerospace, energy, and defense 
applications, providing a pathway for next-generation materials designed for extreme environments.
Introduction
The performance of high-temperature superalloys is closely tied to 
their microstructure, typically comprising a disordered solid solution 
matrix with a high-volume fraction of an ordered intermetallic phase 
(with a superlattice), forming (semi-) coherent interfaces. This dual- 
phase architecture ensures that the disordered matrix retains ductile 
while the ordered phase provides significant strengthening, resulting in 
superior high-temperature mechanical properties such as creep 
resistance.
Ni-based superalloys achieve exceptional high-temperature strength 
and creep resistance through high fractions of L1 ₂ precipitates in a face- 
centered cubic (FCC) matrix. Analogously, coherent B2 precipitates 
embedded in a body-centered cubic (BCC) matrix have been extensively 
explored for high-temperature applications since the 1950s, particularly 
in Fe-Al and Ni-Al-Ti systems. Over the years, research has expanded 
into other alloy systems, including refractory alloys (such as Ti-Al-X) 
and, more recently, high-entropy alloys (HEAs). The past decade has 
witnessed a surge of interest in refractory-element-based HEAs or re -
fractory complex concentrated alloys (RCCAs) due to their high melting 
points and vast compositional flexibility, affording the possibility of 
microstructural and property optimization.
Inspired by the γ / γ ′ microstructural architecture of Ni-based super -
alloys, a similar design strategy has been explored in BCC-based alloys. 
These alloys can be categorized into ferritic superalloys [ 1 – 3 ] and 
refractory-element-based superalloys, including Cr-based [ 4 – 6 ], 
Nb-based [ 7 , 8 ], W-based [ 9 , 10 ], Ti-based [ 11 , 12 ], and RCCAs [ 13 ]. 
Their application temperatures depend on the solvus temperature of 
intermetallic precipitates, with ferritic superalloys designed for steam 
turbine components where corrosion, oxidation and creep resistance 
( < 1000 
◦
C) are critical, and refractory BCC-B2 alloys considered for 
extreme temperatures (1300 – 1800 
◦
C) in aerospace and energy sectors 
due to their high solidus temperatures.

by the attendant phase transformation pathways (PTPs). Ordering re -

Phase transformation pathways in BCC-B2 superalloys
To understand the formation of a BCC + B2 microstructure, it is 
essential to analyze phase diagrams of relevant alloy systems and asso -
ciated PTPs. Various binary phase diagrams containing both BCC and B2 
exist and depending on whether the B2 ↔ BCC order ↔ disorder transition 
is a first-order or a second-order reaction, the topology of the phase 
diagram can be quite different as shown in Fig. 1 [ 14 ]. In systems with a 
miscibility gap, BCC + B2 microstructures can form via distinct path -
ways: congruent ordering of parent BCC to B2 followed by spinodal 
decomposition of B2 into two B2 phases, one of which disorders to BCC 
(conditional spinodal [ 15 ]), or initial BCC decomposition followed by 
ordering of one of the BCC phases to B2 [ 14 – 17 ].
These diagrams can be represented between a pure BCC component 
A (can contain multiple elements) and an ordered B2 compound (e.g.

regions: a disordered BCC phase and an ordered B2 phase, separated 
by a BCC + B2 two-phase field (for first-order transitions) ( Fig. 1 a), or a 
higher-order transition line (for second-order transitions) ( Fig. 1 b).
A diagram similar to Fig. 1 b, which illustrates a second-order order- 
disorder transition between a BCC phase and a B2 phase, has been 
widely used to describe PTPs in various multicomponent alloy systems, 
including Fe-Al-based and RCCAs. Allen and Cahn [ 15 ] discussed the 
phase transformation mechanisms involving second-order order-dis -
order transitions in a binary Fe-Al system using such a diagram and 
identified a tricritical point where a line of second-order transition ends 
at a miscibility gap ( Fig. 1 b). Hao et al. [ 18 ] presented similar diagrams 
to explain the phase equilibria in FeNiAl and FeNiAlCo systems. Later, 
Naka and Khan [ 19 ] presented similar diagrams to describe their 
experimental observations in multicomponent alloy systems including 
FeNiAl/FeNiAlTi and also multicomponent alloys based alloys on re -
fractory elements. More recently, similar diagrams have been presented 
to explain PTPs in RCCAs [ 13 , 20 , 21 ]. These studies primarily focused on 
second-order transitions, following Allen and Cahn, with first-order re -
actions largely overlooked until the recent work by Kadirvel [ 14 ]. 
Fig. 1 b represents systems where the order-disorder transitions are 
second-order, whereas for systems exhibiting the first-order transitions, 
Fig. 1 a should be referenced [ 14 ].
In addition to the above phase diagrams discussed previously, a 
eutectic BCC-B2 phase diagram is also possible, as seen in Ti-rich Fe-Ti 
alloys where a BCC + B2 two-phase region exists. In contrast, systems like 
Fe-Si and Cu-Zn exhibit phase diagrams with only an order-disorder 
transition line, containing two single-phase regions BCC and B2 re -
gions separated by a higher order-disorder transition line, which inhibits 
the formation of an equilibrium two-phase BCC + B2 two-phase field. To 
achieve a superalloy-like two-phase BCC + B2 microstructure, it is 
essential to analyze the phase diagrams in Fig. 1 and understand the 
relevant PTPs.

combination thereof. The spinodal within the miscibility gap divides the

Ferritic superalloys
Ferritic superalloys are primarily based on Fe-Al-X ternary systems, 
where X include elements such as Ni [ 24 ], Ti [ 25 , 26 ], Co [ 27 , 28 ], V [ 29 ,
30 ], Si [ 31 , 32 ], Ta [ 33 ], or Nb [ 34 , 35 ]. These alloys can form a coherent 
BCC/B2 or B2/L2

observed in Ni-based superalloys. Among these, Fe-Al-Ni system stands 
out for its balanced ductility and creep resistance [ 36 ]. Similar to binary 
Fe-Al [ 15 ], ternary Fe-Ni-Al and quaternary Fe-Ni-Al-Co systems exhibit 
a two-phase region (within the miscibility gap) and a higher-order 
transition line [ 18 , 37 , 38 ], illustrated in a representative 
pseudo-binary diagram shown in Fig. 2 a.
The miscibility gap between the BCC-Fe and B2-NiAl phases enables 
precipitation hardening via coherent B2-ordered NiAl precipitates, 
facilitated by their low lattice mismatch with the matrix (0.2887 nm for 
NiAl and 0.2866 nm for Fe [ 39 ]) allows formation of coherent B2-NiAl 
precipitates. With their high melting point [ 2 , 40 ] and oxidation resis -
tance [ 41 , 42 ], these B2 precipitates are also effectively for high tem -
perature strengthening and can replace carbides, nitrides, and Laves 
phases [ 43 – 45 ].
A representative example, FBB8 (Fe-6.5Al-10Cr-10Ni-3.4Mo-0.25Zr- 
0.005B wt. %), contains ~ 16 – 18 % of coherent B2-NiAl precipitates 
within a BCC Fe matrix, with a ~ 65 nm average radius post heat 
treatment ( Fig. 3 a) [ 2 , 3 , 46 – 48 ]. These precipitates maintain a spherical 
morphology due to low misfit strain and interfacial energy. In FBB8 and 
similar alloys, the B2-in-BCC microstructure dominates due to the high 
BCC fraction ( > 50 wt.% Fe) and stiffer NiAl precipitates. Despite the 
high elastic modulus and low ( < 50 %) fraction of the B2 phase, 
experimental observations [ 49 , 50 ] of co-continuous microstructures in 
such alloys highlight the importance of free-energy landscape in 
microstructural evolution. In the early stages of decomposition, asym -
metry dominates over modulus mismatch and volume fraction, as has 
been demonstrated by a recent phase field simulation study [ 22 ], though 
prolonged annealing may eventually lead to a fully BCC matrix as 
modulus misfit effects take over.

Recent investigations of systems undergoing spinodal decomposition 
suggest that beyond alloy composition, the heat treatment schedule 
could be employed as another degree of freedom to design a rich variety 
of microstructures such as ordered precipitates in a disordered matrix, 
disordered precipitates in an ordered matrix, as well as co-continuous 
mixture of ordered and disordered phases in alloys undergoing spino -
dal assisted PTPs [ 14 , 22 , 23 , 79 – 84 ]. A phase-field simulation study [ 23 ] 
on systems with asymmetric miscibility gaps demonstrated that simply 
switching from isothermal aging to continuous cooling can invert the 
roles of the continuous matrix phase and discrete precipitate phase. This 
behavior is primarily due to the temperature dependence of the phase 
fractions. Owing to the asymmetry of the miscibility gap, one phase 
exhibits a significantly higher volume fraction than the other at elevated 
temperatures. As a result, it becomes the matrix phase during the early 
stages of spinodal decomposition and retains this role throughout the 
continuous cooling process — even though its volume fraction becomes 
smaller at later stages. Additionally, hierarchical microstructures can be 
engineered through two-step isothermal aging in RCCAs, where misci -
bility gap asymmetry determines whether one or both co-existing phases 
undergo further spinodal decomposition, shaping the hierarchical ar -
chitecture. Solution thermodynamics based CALPHAD analysis predicts 
that such hierarchical structures could be tailored in Nb-Zr, Ta-Zr, and 
Nb-Ta-Zr systems through optimized two-step aging treatments [ 23 ].

Stability of BCC þ B2 microstructure
Many RCCAs from the Al-Mo-Nb-Ta-Ti-V-Zr family exhibit a BCC + B2 
superalloy-like microstructure, but they are often metastable, with 
annealing leading to lower symmetry compounds other than BCC and B2 
phases. Due to the large negative enthalpy between group IV elements 
and Al, a sufficient concentration of Y (Mo, Nb, Ta) elements is necessary 
to stabilize a multicomponent B2 compound of the type X
2
AlY, where 
X = Ti/Hf/Zr and Y = Mo/Nb/Ta. Competition between such large nega -
tive enthalpies, and high entropy of mixing of the multicomponent B2 
phase formation, likely stabilizes one or the other phase. In Ti-Al-X 
(X = Nb, Mo, Ta) based systems, ternary B2 phases are more stable 
than the binary Ti-Al, likely due to higher configurational entropy from 
additional elements. However, frequent observation of Al
x
Zr
y 
type 
phases (ordered omega type) in the AlMoNbTaTiVZr family suggests 
their relative stabilization over the multicomponent B2, especially at 
lower temperatures. A recent study on Al
0.25
NbTaTiZr alloy showed a 
stable BCC + B2 microstructure at 1000 
◦
C, transitioning to a three-phase 
BCC + B2 + Al
x
Zr
y 
microstructure at lower temperatures [ 87 ]. This sug -
gests that BCC + B2 stability can be achieved if the configurational en -
tropy of B2 is high enough to overcome the strong negative enthalpy 
favoring Al
x
Zr
y 
phases. This could be promoted at higher temperatures, 
where narrower miscibility gaps limit phase separation, maximizing 
entropy-driven stabilization. Such multicomponent B2 phases, stable at 
high temperatures, could likely result in a more compliant B2 compared 
to the BCC, resulting in an inverted superalloy-like microstructure if the 
B2 volume fraction is sufficiently high [ 23 ]. A recent first-principles 
study showed that the rejection of BCC forming Y elements from an 
X
2
AlY-type B2 phase (X = Ti/Zr, Y = Mo/Nb/Ta) stabilizes Al
x
Zr
y 
type 
ordered omega phases relative to such B2 phases [ 88 ]. Since such 
low-symmetry phases are often predicted in CALPHAD calculations, a 
high-throughput CALPHAD approach could be valuable in optimizing 
RCCA alloy design by identifying stable phase fields and minimizing 
unwanted secondary phases.
Summary and challenges
This article discusses the phase transformation pathways leading to 
BCC + B2 microstructures in ferritic and refractory superalloys, inspired 
by the γ / γ’ microstructure of Ni-based superalloys. Ferritic superalloys, 
based on Fe-Al-X systems, are optimized for applications below 1000 
◦
C, 
while RSAs (including RCCAs) are designed for extreme temperatures 
(1300 
◦
C – 1800 
◦
C). The stability and evolution of the BCC + B2 micro -
structure depend on phase transformations, including ordering re -
actions, spinodal decomposition, and N & G mechanisms. Despite their 
potential, these alloys face challenges in phase stability, oxidation 
resistance, creep performance, and room-temperature ductility.
Some of the key challenges include: 
• Inverted BCC-in-B2 microstructure: Understanding PTPs, especially 
in multicomponent alloys, is crucial for microstructural control.
• Lack of stable B2 compounds: Stabilizing B2 phases in non-Ru RCCAs 
remains a challenge, while Ru-containing RCCAs struggle with 
achieving a homogeneous, high-volume fraction of Ru-X B2 pre -
cipitates in a BCC matrix.
• Low symmetry phase formation: Undesirable phases, such as Al-Zr 
rich ordered omega type phase, can result in debits to mechanical 
properties. High-throughput CALPHAD modeling can potentially 
help design alloy compositions to stabilize miscibility gaps to higher 
temperatures, while suppressing unwanted phases to lower 
temperatures.
• Site occupancies in the ordered B2 and derivative ordered structures: 
Stability of these ordered phases and their impact on the mechanical 
properties is critically dependent on their compositions as well as the 
associated sub-lattice or site occupancies. Therefore, experimentally 
determining the site occupancies and corroborating with first- 
principles DFT based computations is critical.
• Lack of reliable Thermodynamic data: The phase stability of BCC + B2 
microstructures in RSAs is not well-documented in CALPHAD data -
bases, making computational alloy design challenging. Improved 
thermodynamic models integrating first-principles calculations and 
experimental validation are needed to predict RSA compositions 
with stable BCC + B2 microstructures.
