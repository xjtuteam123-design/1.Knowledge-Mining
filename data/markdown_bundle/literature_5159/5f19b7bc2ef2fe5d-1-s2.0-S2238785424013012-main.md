1. Introduction 
Ni-based superalloys with high Co contents, also known as Ni – Co- 
based superalloys, were developed for turbine discs, which generally 
experience a range of temperatures, including lower temperatures at the 
core, intermediate temperatures in the web and higher temperatures at 
the rim [ 1 , 2 ]. Therefore, it is important to understand the mechanical 
responses of the components at the temperatures they experience. 
Tensile deformation research on cast and wrought (C & W) Ni – Co-based 
superalloys revealed that serrated flow occurred as a predominant 
feature during plastic deformation at intermediate temperatures for a 
range of strain rates [ 3 ]. Serrated flow, known as the Portevin-Le 
Ch ˆatelier (PLC) effect, is thought to be associated with dynamic strain 
aging (DSA) [ 4 ]. It is generally believed that the DSA effect is caused by 
the interactions of moveable dislocations and solute atoms, and the 
pinning and unpinning processes correspond to increasing and 
decreasing stages of serrations in the tensile plastic flow curves [ 5 , 6 ]. 
PLC effect is generally thought to occur when the rate of solute diffusion 
is similar to or slightly less than that of dislocation movement. The 
dislocations cannot be pinned if the solute atoms diffuse too slowly, and 
the moveable dislocations cannot be unpinned when the solute atoms 
move too rapidly. As the diffusivities of solute atoms depend on the 
temperature, serrated flow occurs in superalloys deformed within a 
special range of testing temperatures (350 – 550 
◦
C) [ 2 , 5 , 7 ]. Hale et al. 
[ 8 ] noted that for the In718 alloy, lattice diffusion of interstitial C and 
substitutional Cr were responsible for serrations in the 173 – 473 
◦
C and 
523 – 673 
◦
C temperature ranges, respectively. Gopinath et al. [ 4 ] sug -
gested that the PLC effect in Udimet 720Li was caused by the locking of 
mobile dislocations by substitutional alloy elements (Cr, Co, Ti, Al, Mo, 
and W). Beese et al. [ 9 ] reported that PLC did not occur in Inconel 625 
prepared by additive manufacturing (AM-In625) at the temperatures 
and strain rates at which PLC occurs in the conventionally processed 
(CP – In625) material, and the absence of serrations was attributed to the 
unique microstructure derived from AM. 
AM, which is also known as three-dimensional (3D) printing, can be 
used to fabricate components with complex and customized geometries 
and is a technology that could revolutionize manufacturing processes. 
One of the AM techniques uses a laser as a heating source and produces 
parts layer-by-layer with rapid self-cooling and high-temperature gra -
dients. Columnar crystals can generally be prepared with fine dendrites

4. Discussion 
To explain the absence of PLC effect in AM-In625, Beese et al. [ 9 ] 
proposed a new model based on the dislocation arrest theory. It was 
suggested that the secondary phases served as sinks for the carbon at -
mosphere and drained the carbon from the mobile dislocations through 
pipe diffusion when the mobile dislocations were arrested temporarily at 
forest dislocation junctions. Compared with CP-In625, AM-In625 had 
finer carbides as secondary phases and larger contact lengths that 
obstructed moving dislocations. It was proposed that the secondary 
phases in AM-In625 drained carbon even faster than the carbon atmo -
sphere formation at the mobile dislocations, leading to experimentally 
undetectable or no serrations. Unlike In625, which had no serrations 
when fabricated by AM but presented PLC effect in the conventionally 
processed material, the tensile test results showed that PLC effect 
occurred both in AM and C & W 5Co and 23Co. The discrepancy was 
mainly attributable to the different phase constitutions and composi -
tions of the In625 and the Ni – Co-based superalloys investigated in this 
study. Although AM-In625 also exhibited the dendritic structure that 
AM 5Co and 23Co alloys had and CP-In625 showed an equiaxed struc -
ture similar to those of the C & W 5Co and 23Co alloys, it should be 
emphasized that the microstructures of the In625 and Ni – Co-based al -
loys were discrepant. In625 has carbide as the main secondary phase 
and contains C as the main solute atom, and these features are respon -
sible for the PLC effect. However, the 5Co and 23Co alloys had abundant 
γ ′ precipitates as their main secondary phases. Tian [ 23 ] calculated and 
contrasted the activation energies of the dominant solute atoms and 
demonstrated that PLC effect in Ni – Co-based superalloys was mainly

Our previous studies suggested a relationship between the inverse 
PLC effect and the SFs, but the solute/fault interaction mechanisms were 
unclear [ 16 ]. A more detailed illustration can be provided herein by 
analyzing the microstructures and mechanisms of AM alloys ’ PLC effect. 
It is suggested that when tested at low temperatures, type A or B ser -
rations were dominant; mobile dislocations carried unsaturated solutes 
and slid in the matrix until they were blocked by obstacles, leading to the 
stress rise associated with upward serrations. After the dislocations were 
freed from the stress field of the obstacle, the solute atoms gathered 
again, and the concentration of the solute was rebalanced [ 25 ]. At the 
high temperatures at which the type C serrations were dominant, the 
mobile dislocations carried saturated solutes before they were impeded. 
After the unpinning process, the solutes pinned the dislocation imme -
diately to obtain saturation balance again. Therefore, solute concen -
tration at the dislocations played a key role in determining the serration 
types. 
However, McCormick [ 26 ] noted that it is basically impossible to 
produce a sufficient concentration of vacancies enabling the diffusive 
solutes to achieve equal velocities as mobile dislocations. The author 
suggested that the movements of dislocations were discontinuous, and 
that the solute atoms gathered at the dislocations, which were 
obstructed temporally. It should be emphasized that PLC effect occurred 
in the temperature range within which the transformation mechanism 
changed from dislocation sliding to SF formation. Therefore, the 
changing of serrations types could be attributed to the higher temper -
ature that increases the diffusion velocities of the solute atoms and fa -
cilitates the formation of SFs, leading to a dramatic change in the solute 
concentration. Fig. 6 shows that the deformed microstructures of the AM 
5Co alloy ( Fig. 6 (a) ~ (f)) differed significantly with increasing tem -
perature. When tested at temperatures (300 – 350 
◦
C) at which type A 
and type B serrations appeared, dislocation sliding was the main 
deformation mechanism, and the shapes of the secondary γ ′ and tertiary 
γ ′ precipitates were ambiguous due to the shearing effects of the dislo -
cations, while the primary γ ′ precipitates maintained their shapes. In 
addition, large continuous SFs formed across the primary γ ′ precipitates 
and were tangled with the dislocations. It can be deduced that during 
testing at 300 and 350 
◦
C, dislocations with unsaturated solute atoms 
slid in the matrix until they were impeded by obstacles, which resulted 
in the gathering of more solutes and reaching the necessary concentra -
tion for pinning. With the aid of applied stress, the dislocations escaped 
from the solutes and moved until they were impeded by another barrier. 
As the temperature was increased to 400 
◦
C ( Fig. 6 (c) ~ (e)), the for -
mation of SFs was inspired, a number of small dispersed thick SFs ( Fig. 6 
(c)) were observed. Rao et al. [ 27 ] and Smith et al. [ 28 ] found that Cr 
and Co were segregated around the leading partials of the SFs and can 
formed a solute atmosphere to constrain the leading partial in a Ni-based 
superalloy. They proved, by systematic first principles calculations, that 
the solute/fault interactions were driven by the strong negative inter -
action energy between the 2-layered complex SFs. The pipe diffusion 
phenomenon was observed by Legros et al. [ 29 ] via in situ transmission 
electron microscope, and the pipe diffusion velocity was almost three 
orders of magnitude greater than the bulk diffusion velocity. Jiang et al. 
[ 30 ] observed the enrichment of Co and Cr at the SFs in a Ni alloy and 
proposed that Suzuki segregation might be formed through rapid pipe 
diffusion along the dislocation cores of partial dislocations. The influ -
ence of SF on the PLC effect in Ni – Co-based superalloy can be concluded. 
First, the SFs created by the dissociation of dislocations [ 2 ] may decrease 
the density of dislocations and weaken the PLC effect correspondingly. 
Moreover, before gliding and being obstructed by the dislocations, so -
lute atoms far away from the moving dislocations might first gather 
around the dispersed SFs to form a dispersed solute atmosphere. As 
defects, SFs can interact with the dislocations and provide slight resis -
tance to the moving dislocations to extend the waiting time t
w 
for solute 
gathering. At the same time, the solute atoms might be drained from SF 
(microtwins) through pipe diffusion to the dislocations with a high 
diffusion rate for attaining a saturated solution atmosphere. As the 
movement of dislocations and solute atoms are influenced by the pro -
liferation of SFs, the dispersed thick SFs promote the formation of a 
saturated solution atmosphere and the occurrence of type C serrations. 
In addition, the solute concentrations at the dislocations are positively 
associated with the pinning strength, and unpinning requires that the 
applied stress be high enough and equal the sum of the pinning and 
obstructing strengths [ 31 ]. The greater unpinning strength due to the 
saturated solution needs higher applied stress, which corresponds to an 
increased onset strain ε
c
, leading to the occurrence of inverse PLC effect. 
Fig. 6 (d) shows that dislocations were formed in addition to the 
thick SFs in the deformed microstructure. When the SFs between two 
partials inhibit the cross-slip dislocations, the planar dislocation array 
will form as a more uniform structure [ 32 ]. The interactions between SFs 
and dislocations facilitate the formation of a saturated solute atmo -
sphere. In addition, there are many curly dislocations in primary γ ′ 
precipitates. This means that, under thermal activation, the dislocations 
can slide through the phase interface between the γ matrix and the 
primary γ ′ precipitates, which may lead to a more significant unpinning 
effect than the secondary γ ′ and tertiary γ ′ precipitates. Therefore, 
greater applied stress is needed, and this corresponds to the occurrence 
of type C serrations. The dislocations with saturated solutes need more t
w 
to accumulate the energy needed to overcome the precipitates, leading 
to an increased onset strain ε
c 
and the occurrence of the inverse PLC 
effect. As the temperature rose to 450 
◦
C ( Fig. 6 (f)), dislocations piled 
up and formed dislocation arrays that sheared the primary γ ′ precipitates 
and SFs. The velocities of the solutes were high enough to catch up with 
the moving dislocations, resulting in disappearance of the PLC effect. 
It turned out that the increase in Co content did not seriously affect 
the stress decrement values of serrations but increased the temperature 
region of the PLC effect. It was confirmed in previous investigations [ 23 , 
24 ] that increasing the content of Co lowered the SFE and promoted the 
dissociation of gliding perfect dislocations for the formation of SFs and 
twinning [ 32 ]. Compared with the AM 5Co alloy, the deformed micro -
structure of the AM 23Co alloy ( Fig. 6 (g) ~ (l)) had more SFs. Addi -
tionally, an increase in Co content reduced the content of solute Cr and 
increased the formation difficulty of the solute atmosphere, leading to 
an increase in the temperature region of PLC effect [ 16 ]. Furthermore, as 
the temperature region was increased, the obstruction effects of primary 
γ ′ precipitates were weakened for the PLC effect. As shown in Fig. 6 (j), 
the primary γ ′ withstood severe deformation due to the shearing effect of 
dislocations.
