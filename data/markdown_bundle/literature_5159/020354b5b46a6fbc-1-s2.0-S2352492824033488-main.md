ABSTRACT
In the pursuit of enhancing the service temperature capabilities of superalloys, tantalum (Ta) emerges as a 
promising additive for the development of next-generation Ni-base powder metallurgy (PM) superalloys. How -
ever, the current investigation reveals significant variability in the influence of Ta on creep properties, a vari -
ability influenced not only by Ta concentration but also by the intricate interplay among Ta, chromium (Cr), and 
molybdenum (Mo). Ta initiates dynamic splitting of γ ′ phase and induces grain boundary degradation. 
Furthermore, as creep temperature rises or load diminishes, the grain boundary weakening caused by continuous 
σ precipitates in high-Ta alloys emerges as the predominant factor influencing creep life. Ultimately, cracking 
occurs before a substantial portion of grains undergo plastic deformation.
1. Introduction
γ ′ -strengthened nickel-based superalloys, critical materials for aero- 
engines, have been utilized in high-temperature components exposed 
to harsh service conditions. To enhance material temperature capabil -
ities, various metal or non-metal alloying elements have been incorpo -
rated into Ni-based alloys for nearly a century [1 – 3] . Presently, 
commercial turbine disks contain over ten elements in Ni-based super -
alloys. The addition of refractory metals, such as Ta, W, and Mo, 
significantly enhances the creep resistance of superalloys. However, the 
exceedingly high level of alloying presents unprecedented obstacles to 
further processing and fabrication. For example, caution must be exer -
cised with cast superalloys to prevent macro-segregation of elements, 
while cast and wrought superalloys must consider the possibility of 
cogging and forging. Consequently, the high-alloying content in super -
alloys is largely restricted. Nonetheless, the powder metallurgy (PM) 
process of direct hot isostatic pressing (HIP) effectively addresses these 
challenges. Consequently, the study of the action mechanism of various 
refractory metal elements can be more accurately interpreted by P/M 
superalloys, as the influence of casting segregation on properties is 
largely eliminated.
Since the 1990s, Ta has been favored as a critical additive for 
improving the corrosion resistance [4 – 6] and strengthening mechanical 
properties of superalloys, has been favored in cast & wrought superal -
loys [7,8] . In Ni-based superalloys, Ta atoms not only preferentially 
form MC carbides [9] and promote TCP precipitation [9,10] , but also 
influence the properties of the γ ′ phase, as evidenced by numerous 
first-principles studies [11 – 13] . There have been reports on the effect of 
Ta on the microstructure and mechanical properties of superalloys, 
indicating that adding Ta to the alloy can increase tensile strength and 
creep rupture life [14] . However, most attention has been focused on 
Ta-induced casting segregation or on MC and M

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Initial microstructure
The effect of the initial microstructure on the creep properties is 
crucial for polycrystalline superalloys. Therefore, the characteristics of 
the solution heat-treated (SHTed) microstructure of the experimental 
alloys need to be elucidated here. The powder compacts underwent the 
exact same SHT process to ensure that each Ta-containing alloy would 
not have process differences affecting properties. Fig. 2 shows the 
typical microstructure of alloys with different Ta contents. It should be 
noted that the previous particle boundaries (i.e., MC-type carbide par -
ticles) are effectively controlled with the addition of Ta to the alloy. In 
Fig. 2 (a), the carbide particle distribution with powder shape is found in 
the 0Ta alloy, while the carbides are uniformly dispersed within the 
grains in Ta-added alloys (the detailed discussion is in Ref. [9] ). Since 
the same heat treatment process was applied, the mean grain size of each 
experimental alloy is nearly identical (approximately 30 μ m). After the 
same cooling rate and aging treatment, the size of γ ′ precipitates in -
creases slightly with increasing Ta content and tends to split. Some 
butterfly-shaped γ ′ precipitates observed in Ta-added alloys are pre -
sented in Fig. 2 (d – f). Transmission electron microscopy with 
energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (TEM-EDS) obtained secondary γ ′ 
element concentrations in 0, 2.4, and 4.8 Ta alloys, as shown in Fig. 2 (g). 
With the increase in Ta content, the concentrations of Al, Ti, and Nb in 
the secondary γ ′ phase decrease slightly, while the concentrations of W 
and Ta increase slightly. The distribution of MC carbides within the 
grains is observed in Fig. 2 (a – c), and these carbides are highlighted by 
the contrast of the backscattered electron (BSE) imaging in Fig. 2 (h – i). 
After Ta addition, in addition to the obvious increase in the MC volume 
fraction, numerous Ta segregates into the MC carbides, resulting in 
brighter contrasts appearing in the BSE images. Additionally, no other 
precipitates were found at the grain boundaries in SHTed alloys.

degradation of interface is the damage of grain boundary, annealing 
coherent twin boundary and γ / γ ′ phase interface when temperature, 
stress and time act simultaneously. (2) degeneration behavior of the γ ′

4. Conclusion
This study investigates the creep rupture behavior of P/M superal -
loys with varying Ta content, with a focus on microstructural degrada -
tion and potential damage mechanisms. The results indicate that 
damage is primarily driven by the degradation of grain boundaries or 
coherent annealing twin boundaries, instability in the morphology of γ ′ 
particles, and interface damage due to microtwins. The influence of Ta is 
governed by several factors, including element segregation at interfaces, 
the density of annealed twins and microtwins, and its effect on grain 
boundary cohesion. Furthermore, isolated microtwins enriched in Co 
and Cr were observed after creep testing at 800 
◦

gesting that the formation of extended microtwins is strongly associated 
with the applied stress and temperature, as well as the alloy ’ s chemical 
composition.
