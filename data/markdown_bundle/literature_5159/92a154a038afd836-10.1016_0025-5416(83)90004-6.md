SUMMARY 
The properties of two high strength, high 
temperature, high conductivity ingot-based 
copper alloys were examined in detail. These 
are the Cu-O. 06w t. %Mg-O. 63w t. %Cr- 
0.13wt.%Zr (MZC) and the Cu-4.94wt.%Ni- 
2. 40wt. % Ti materials produced as wrought 
ingot-based alloys. Tensile tests at 293 K and 
stress rupture tests at 673 K were performed. 
An oxide-dispersed Cu-Zr-Cr alloy produced 
from fine atomized powders has the same 
stress rupture properties at 673 K as the fully 
heat-treated wrought MZC alloy, and both are 
stronger than the wrought Cu-Ni-Ti alloy. 
The effects of short-time (1 h) annealing to 
1223 K were examined with the interesting 
observation that the Cu-Ni-Ti alloy, after loss 
of hardness due to overaging in the tempera- 
ture interval 6 73-1073 K, rehardens on heat- 
ing above 1073 K and then cooling, by a re- 
solution and aging process. Corn bining water 
quenching from the temperature range 1138- 
1223 K with re-aging at 848-873 K permitted 
recovery of the original fully heat-treated 
room temperature strength and hardness 
properties to full strength and hardness. For 
the MZC alloy, re-solutioning for 1 h at 
1223 K plus aging at 748 K resulted in only a 
partial recovery of strength. The maximum 
electrical conductivity values were measured 
at 42.1 m~ -1 mm -2 (72.5% International 
Annealed Copper Standard (IACS)) and 
28.6 m[2 -1 mm -2 (49% IACS) for the MZC 
and Cu-Ni-Ti alloys respectively in their 
fully recrystallized states. 
1. INTRODUCTION 
High (thermal and electrical) conductivity 
copper alloys with a high strength at room 
temperature and elevated temperatures are in- 
creasingly in demand. Adaptive alloys are 
usually of the precipitation-strengthened 
types and are dilutely alloyed with elements 
of very low solid solubility to preserve high 
levels of conductivity. Some of the commer- 
cial applications for these materials include 
motor commutators, electrical springs, circuit 
breakers and heat exchangers, to name only a 
few. 
The copper alloys of interest are the Cu-Zr, 
Cu-Cr and Cu-Zr-Cr types; they have had 
considerable success in fulfilling the needs of 
the electrical industry. Cu-Cr is the most 
widely used of the high strength, high conduc- 
tivity alloys in the electrical industry because 
of its lower cost [1], its ability to regain a 
high strength and conductivity after being 
heated to brazing temperatures and its capa- 
bility of being strengthened by heat treatment 
alone without the necessity for cold working. 
Cu-Zr alloys are strengthened by a combina- 
tion of cold working and precipitation 
hardening; they have better high temperature 
mechanical properties than do the Cu-Cr 
alloys [2]. Cu-Cr-Zr alloys combine the 
virtues of Cu-Cr and Cu-Zr alloys; the 
ternary alloys respond to both thermo- 
mechanical treatment and aging and they 
yield better mechanical properties at room 
temperature and high temperatures, suffering 
only a slight decrease in electrical conductivity 
[3] due to the combined alloying effects. 
Alloys based on the Cu-Ti system have 
been relatively neglected by the electrical 
industry so far, mainly because of their much 
lower electrical conductivity (15% versus 80% 
International Annealed Copper Standard 
(IACS) for the Cu-Zr, Cu-Cr and Cu-Zr-Cr 
alloys). Nevertheless, Cu-Ti alloys have shown 
room temperature tensile strength values as 
high as 1435 MPa (208 lbf in -2) [4] ; the

2. MATERIALS AND EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS 
The two alloys tested were both supplied 
by AMAX Base Metals Research and Develop- 
ment Inc., Carteret, NJ. The compositions, 
bar sizes, fabrication histories and heat treat- 
ments are listed in Table 1. 
The room temperature hardness and tensile 
strength were measured at 298 K (20 °C); the 
stress rupture tests were performed at 673 K 
(400 °C). For stress rupture testing, constant- 
load tests were used; an argon atmosphere was 
employed to minimize the effect of oxidation 
during the tests. The short-time thermal 
stability of the alloys was measured by the 
variation in room temperature hardness after 
exposure to high temperatures (773-1273 K) 
and by microstructural observations. The 
microstructures of the alloys were determined 
with optical and transmission microscopy as 
needed. 
The electrical conductivity was measured 
using the potential drop method. A d.c. 
power supply, a standard resistor and a micro- 
voltmeter were used in the set-up.

3.2. Thermal stability 
Figure 3 shows a plot of room temperature 
hardness versus annealing temperature (1 h) 
up to 1303 K (1030 °C) for the two alloys. 
Both alloys are stable for short-time exposures 
up to approximately 673 K. Beyond 773 K 
the room temperature hardness values of 
these alloys drop significantly as recovery and 
overaging occur with increasing temperature. 
For the MZC alloy the drop is much more 
rapid and severe than that for the Cu-Ni-Ti 
alloy and continues in a classical overaging 
mode. In contrast the Cu-Ni-Ti alloy starts to 
reharden on air cooling beyond approximately 
1073 K, with hardness values leveling off in 
the 1173-1273 K region at near-peak hard- 
ness values. 
Figure 4 shows the microstructures of the 
two alloys after exposure at 1223 K for I h. 
The grain size of the MZC alloy (Fig. 4(a)) has 
grown considerably after 1 h (from 20-40 pm 
to 150-300 pm); however, the grain size of 
the Cu-Ni-Ti alloy has only grown to 10- 
15 pm (Fig. 4(b)). Second-phase coarsen- 
ing is evident in both alloys; however, the 
much higher volume fraction and thermally 
more stable excess phases in the Cu-Ni-Ti 
alloy prevented severe grain coarsening during 
high temperature annealing. No significant

4. DISCUSSION 
4.1. Cu-Mg-Zr-Cr alloy 
The MZC alloy in the fully heat-treated 
state (MI) has good room temperature 
strength and ductility and excellent stress 
rupture properties at 673 K. Figure 2 com- 
pares the stress rupture values of this alloy 
with those of Cu-0.15wt.%Zr [9] and the 
powder metallurgy ZAC-1 [8] (see Table 2 
for the compositions). The Cu-0.15wt.%Zr 
{ingot alloy) shows an excellent stress rupture 
strength up to fairly long times {about 
10 000 h), but it is doubtful whether the Cu- 
0.15wt.%Zr alloy would have the same 
property recovery capability that the MZC 
alloy has after overheating to 1223-1273 K 
(Cu-Zr alloys are strengthened primarily by 
cold working and show little response to 
aging} [19]. The benefit of adding chromium 
(as in Cu-Zr-Cr and the MZC) should be 
obvious here. 
Figure 2 also compares MZC with ZAC-1 
{Table 2) and shows that their strengths are 
comparable at 673 K. ZAC-1 is a Cu-Zr-Cr- 
type powder metallurgy alloy with a lower 
chromium content than the MZC alloy. This 
powder metallurgy alloy is in fact an oxide- 
dispersion-strengthened alloy with a small

## Page 9

volume content of fine ZrO2 particles formed 
during the gas atomization process and origi- 
nally residing on the surface of the powders. 
It is not known what role the solute ele- 
ment magnesium plays in the MZC alloy in 
terms of mechanical properties. Magnesium is 
added primarily for better control of zir- 
conium additions at low levels by acting as a 
scavenger [ 5] ; however, magnesium was 
shown to reduce the tendency to intercrystal- 
line cracking in Cu-Cr alloys [20]. In the MI 
condition, no intergranular cracking was ob- 
served in stress rupture tests at 673 K for 
times up to 747 h; this was observed by Taplin 
and Collins [21] who worked with a Cu- 
0.3wt.%Cr-0.11wt.%Zr-0.04wt.%Mg alloy. 
They attributed the absence of intergranular 
cracks in the MZC alloy to the strong adhesive 
strength between the "Cu3Zr" precipitates at 
the grain boundaries, which minimized grain 
boundary sliding; however, their alloy also 
has a high grain aspect ratio (about 30) which 
results in less grain boundary sliding. Indeed, 
intergranular cracking (mixed mode failure) 
was found in the present alloy for rupture 
lives greater than 69 h when the alloy was 
additionally solution treated at 1223 K and 
aged (MII); the grain structure after the 
1223 K treatment was highly equiaxed. It 
should be pointed out that a zirconium 
content as low as 0.003 wt.% has been demon- 
strated to have a beneficial effect in stabiliz- 
ing grains and subgrains in Cu-Zr alloys [2]. 
This is not surprising considering that a zir- 
conium atom is 25% larger than a copper 
atom; further, a zirconium grain boundary 
enrichment of the order of 2000 is observed 
for Cu-Zr alloys [22]. The pinning of grain 
and subgrain boundaries by the segregated 
Z irconiumsol-ute in Cu-Zr alloys {s l~{gl~iy 
probable and was suggested by Sohl et al. [23] 
as the cause of the increased softening tem- 
perature in very dilutely alloyed Cu-Zr alloys. 
The segregation of zirconium to the bound- 
aries is expected to have an effect on the grain 
boundary energy; impurities such as sulfur, 
oxygen, phosphorus, antimony etc. are 
known to promote boundary cavitation in 
copper and other metals and alloys [24]. 
Zirconium, acting as a scavenger of some of 
the impurities, would sharply decrease the 
loss of grain boundary energy, thereby 
decreasing the tendency to intercrystalline 
cracking.

4.2. Cu-Ni-Ti alloys 
This alloy can completely recover its room 
temperature strength after high temperature 
overheating, e.g. after brazing or welding, by 
undergoing a simple aging treatment without 
repeating the cold-working step (as with 
Cu-Cr alloys). This unusual behavior of age 
hardening or quenching from the solution 
temperature is confirmed by metallographic 
and transmission electron microscopy 
observations. 
Precipitation on water quenching was also 
observed by Knights and Wilkes [25] while 
studying a Cu-28.85wt.%Ni-2.16wt.%Ti 
alloy. Yakushiji et al. [26] failed to observe 
precipitation on water quenching a {dilute) 
Cu-1.36wt.%Ni-1.14wt.%Ti alloy from 
1273 K; however, quench age hardening was 
observed on a hot-extruded Cu-2wt.%Ni- 
lwt.%Ti powder metallurgy alloy by the 
present authors. Thus specific compositions 
are critical for this quench age-hardening 
phenomenon to take place. 
Phase studies of the Cu-Ni-Ti system by 
other researchers have resulted in the identifi- 
cation of a variety of phases. Knights and 
Wilkes [25] and Maeda and Ishibe [27] each 
identified a second phase in their Cu- 
28.85wt.%Ni-2.16wt.%Ti and Cu-5wt.%Ni- 
1.55wt.%Ti alloys; they reported an Ni3Ti (~?, 
D024) type of ordered h.c.p, structure. 
Dutkiewicz and Litynska [28] detected 7' 
(Ni3Ti) (D022, tetragonal) or 7' (Ni3Ti) 
(L12, f.c.c.) structures, depending on the 
nickel content (Cu-(4.0-4.5)wt.%Ti-(5.0- 
16.0)wt.%Ni). Yakushiji et al. [26] detected 
an intermediate Cu-Ni-Ti precipitate (T 
phase, body-centered tetragonal) in their Cu- 
1.36wt.%Ni-l.l 4wt.%Ti alloy. 
In the current study we identified an 
NisCu5Ti 6 intermediate phase, modified ~? 
(Fig. l(b)); in addition, recent studies [16] 
also indicate an ~ (Ni3Ti) ordered h.c.p. 
structure similar to those observed by Knights 
and Wilkes [25] and Maeda and Ishibe [27]. 
Direct substitution of copper for nickel atoms 
in the Ni3Ti compound seems probable, i.e. 
(Cu, Ni)3Ti; however, the ([Cu] + [Ni])/[Ti] 
molar ratio for the modified ~ phase is only 
2.16. This may suggest that there is a defi- 
ciency of copper and/or nickel atoms in the 
modified ~ particles.

5. CONCLUSIONS AND SUMMARIZING 
REMARKS 
The properties of two high temperature, 
high conductivity copper alloys were 
evaluated. In the solution-treated, cold- 
worked and aged (MI and CI) conditions, 
both alloys are quite strong (the Cu-Ni-Ti 
alloy is stronger than the MZC alloy) at room 
temperature and have excellent ductility. At 
673 K, in stress rupture the MZC alloy is 
significantly stronger than the Cu-Ni-Ti alloy. 
The stress rupture properties at 673 K of the 
MZC alloy are comparable with those of a low 
oxide powder metallurgy oxide-dispersion- 
strengthened Cu-Cr-Zr alloy (ZAC-1). 
The structural stability of the Cu-Ni-Ti 
alloy when heated to very high temperatures

## Page 11

is unusual in that the alloy undergoes aging 
during rapid quenching from the solution 
temperatures. Both alloys are able to recover 
their strength after a simple aging treatment 
when they have been overheated above about 
1173 K. The MZC alloy has much better 
stress rupture properties at 673 K after high 
temperature re-solution treatment and aging 
than the Cu-Ni-Ti alloy after an equivalent 
treatment. For the MZC alloy in its MI condi- 
tion, no intergranular cracking was observed 
in stress rupture tests at 673 K for times up to 
727 h; however, intergranular cracking 
(mixed mode failure) was observed for the 
same alloy in its MII condition for rupture 
lives beyond about 69 h. The Cu-Ni-Ti alloy 
fractured intergranularly (for all heat treat- 
ments studied); as a result, very small fracture 
strains were recorded for this alloy. 
An NisCu~Ti~ phase was identified as one 
of the precipitates in the Cu-Ni-Ti alloy al- 
though the number and types of second 
phases are still undetermined. Elemental 
chromium was detected as one of the precipi- 
tate phases in the MZC alloy; the other pre- 
cipitated phase, Cu3 Zr-Cu4Zr, was present 
but was not properly detected. Work is con- 
tinuing to identify all the phases in both 
alloys for all conditions of heat treatment.
