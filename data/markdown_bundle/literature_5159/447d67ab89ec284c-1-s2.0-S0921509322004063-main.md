ABSTRACT 
We designed and developed a novel ferritic alloy, which reveals excellent high-temperature yield strength 
enhanced by coherent hierarchical precipitates. The composition of designed ferritic alloy was tailored via 
addition of Vanadium into a Fe – Cr – Ni – Al alloy to control the size and volume fractions of coherent B2 – NiAl 
precipitates. The evolutions of microstructures, mechanical properties, and strengthening mechanisms at 973 K 
were examined by the experimental efforts coupled with theoretical approach, such as scanning electron mi -
croscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), X-ray diffraction, compressive mechanical tests at 973 
K, and theoretical (strengthening calculations) approach. It was found that the vanadium results in the reduction 
of the lattice misfit between B2 – NiAl precipitate and matrix and decrease of the precipitate size. Furthermore, a 
hierarchical structure in the precipitate, i.e., nano-scale Fe
23
Zr
6 
phase within the B2 – NiAl precipitate, was 
established in V-containing ferritic alloys, which plays a vital role in enhancing the yield strength at 973 K. 
1. Introduction 
High-temperature properties, such as creep resistance are important 
factors for high-temperature applications. Especially, high strength with 
excellent creep resistance and thermal stability plays a crucial role in 
improving of energy efficiency in the coal-fired power plants [ 1 – 4 ]. The 
enhancement of energy efficiency, in turn, the reduction of emission of 
green-house gas, which mainly lead to a considerable global-warming 
[ 5 , 6 ]. Therefore, there have been extensive efforts to develop the 
high-temperature metallic alloys, such as Ni/Co-based superalloys and 
ferritic/austenitic steels for the coal-fired power [ 7 – 12 ]. Among the 
developed alloys, the ferritic steels have attracted significant attentions, 
owing to their low thermal expansion, good thermal conductivity and 
low cost, as compared to that of the Ni-based superalloys and austenitic 
steels [ 1 , 2 ]. Although the conventional ferritic steels reveal desirable 
strength, which is strengthened by incoherent precipitates [ 13 – 15 ], it is 
still challenge for practical applications due to their microstructural 
instabilities at high-temperatures, such as precipitate-coarsening 
behavior and formation of unexpected phases during long-term expo -
sure to high-temperatures [ 13 – 15 ]. 
To overcome the limitation of microstructural stability at high- 
temperature, many studies have been carried out to provide the new 
design strategies for ferritic alloy, such as an introducing the coherent 
precipitates by tailoring the alloy compositions [ 16 – 19 ]. For instance, 
Fe – Cr – Ni – Al ferritic alloys reinforced by coherent B2 – NiAl precipitates 
in Fe matrix have been studied to improve the mechanical properties at 
high-temperature [ 19 – 22 ], indicating the similar precipitate strength -
ening mechanism of Ni-based superalloys. These developed ferritic al -
loys show nearly zero value of lattice misfit between matrix and B2 – NiAl 
precipitates [ 23 , 24 ], which is a significant beneficial to the micro -
structural stability at high-temperatures (i.e., suppression of precipitates 
coarsening). Moreover, the small degree of lattice misfit is effective to a 
reduction of precipitate size, decreasing the nucleation energy, and thus, 
it results in the efficient enhancement of the strength [ 25 ].

the coherent-precipitate reinforced ferritic alloys, a ‘hierarchical

4.2. Vickers hardness at room temperature 
Considering the results of microstructural evolutions and the Vickers 
hardness at room temperature, Vanadium-added alloys possess nearly 
similar hardness values, which can be elucidated in terms of two view -
points. The first point is the solid solution strengthening from the Va -
nadium element. Vanadium has a relatively low strengthening 
coefficient for solid solution with Fe at room temperature, compared to 
other elements (i.e., C, N, W, Ni and Ti) [ 41 ]. This could be a reason why 
vanadium addition has a limited influence on the mechanical properties 
at room temperature. The second one is formation of the secondary 
precipitate during cooling process. The secondary precipitates were 
identified as a B2 – NiAl from FFT image ( Fig. 5 ), which is often observed 
from the NiAl-precipitation hardened ferritic alloys [ 20 , 29 , 36 ]. Gener -
ally, the nano size (a few nano meter) and fine distribution of the sec -
ondary precipitate leads to a significant strengthening effect at room 
temperature [ 29 , 31 , 42 ], as compared to that of the primary precipitate 
with hundreds nanometer sizes. Thus, the secondary precipitates could 
substantially contribute to the strengthening at room temperature [ 29 , 
31 , 42 ], but this precipitate strengthening mechanism could not function 
properly at high-temperatures, since secondary precipitates tend to be 
dissolved into the matrix at elevated temperatures [ 36 ]. Therefore, it is 
believed that the similar hardness values for Vanadium-added alloys are 
mostly associated with a formation of the secondary precipitate rather 
than primary precipitate formed during aging treatment.

5. Conclusion 
In this study, we developed the Fe – Cr – Ni – Al – V ferritic alloy and 
investigated the microstructural evolutions and mechanical properties 
as a function of vanadium contents in Fe – Cr – Ni – Al ferritic alloys. The 
conclusions are as follows: 
● It is observed that the mean radius of the B2 – NiAl precipitates 
gradually decrease from 56 nm to 30 nm with increasing of the Va -
nadium concentration. The size variation of the B2 – NiAl precipitate 
is related to the lattice misfit between matrix and B2 – NiAl precipi -
tate. Vanadium addition can effectively reduce the lattice misfit 
between B2 – NiAl precipitates and matrix, which gives rise to the 
reduction of precipitate size. 
● Although the B2 – NiAl precipitate size is reduced with increasing of 
the vanadium content, the Vickers hardness results of the V-con -
taining Fe – Cr – Ni – Al ferritic alloys indicate that all alloys possess 
nearly similar values (447 Hv for 0V, 481 Hv for 4V, 480 Hv for 8V 
and 479 Hv for 12V, respectively). The trends of hardness results are 
associated with the formation of secondary B2 – NiAl precipitates. In 
contrast, the results of compressive tests at 973 K show the enhanced 
yield strength from 210 MPa to 401 MPa with decreasing the size of 
the primary B2 – NiAl precipitates. The refined primary B2 – NiAl 
precipitates result from decrease of lattice misfit, which mainly in -
fluences on the mechanical properties at 973 K. 
● Based on the theoretical computation of precipitation strengthening, 
the Orowan dislocation bypassing can be dominant strengthening 
mechanisms for 0V, 4V and 8V alloys. These theoretical calculations 
are in good agreement with experimental values. On the other hand, 
the 12V alloy, which composed of two-phase precipitates, reveals a 
higher experimental value of strengthening than the theoretical 
calculation. The additional strengthening effect results from 
morphology of precipitates, i.e., the formation of hierarchical pre -
cipitates (nano-scale Fe
23
Zr
6 
phase within the B2 – NiAl precipitate), 
effectively enhance the strength in the precipitate-hardened ferritic 
alloys, when dislocation shearing is activated.
