1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are composed of multiple principal ele -
ments mixed in high concentrations [ 1 – 3 ]. Their compositions are 
located near or at the center of the composition space, providing un -
precedented freedom in alloy design. This has led to immense interest in 
HEAs from the scientific community in recent years owing to their 
unique properties and potential for alloy design [ 4 – 6 ]. Typically, HEAs 
are categorized into two groups, namely, the face-centered cubic 
(FCC)-based (CoCrFeNiMn) [ 7 ] and body-centered cubic (BCC)-based 
(WNbMoTaV and TiZrHfNbTa) alloys [ 8 ]. The FCC-based CoCrFeNiMn 
alloy, referred to as the Cantor alloy [ 7 ], exhibits excellent mechanical 
properties at extremely low temperatures [ 9 ]. By contrast, BCC-based 
HEAs, such as NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW, exhibit adequate heat 
resistance and phase stability in the temperature range of 873 – 1273 K 
[ 10 , 11 ]. 
Among the BCC-based HEAs, Al – Co – Cr – Fe – Ni alloys exhibit excel -
lent resistance to heat and corrosion at high temperatures [ 12 – 14 ]. 
Additionally, they have a low thermal expansion coefficient owing to 
their BCC structure, which facilitates their stable performance at high 
temperatures [ 15 , 16 ]. Compared to refractory HEAs such as NbMoTaW 
and VNbMoTaW, Al – Co – Cr – Fe – Ni alloys have a lower operating tem -
perature but are much cheaper and lighter, so they have great potential 
to have a variety of applications. Interestingly, the Al – Co – Cr – Fe – Ni al -
loys exhibit a microstructure similar to that of a conventional superalloy 
[ 17 – 19 ], which comprises γ (disordered FCC A1 structure) and γ ′ (or -
dered FCC L1
2 
structure) phases, while being lighter and more 
cost-effective. The γ ′ phase plays an important role in increasing the 
high-temperature strength [ 20 – 23 ]. However, the phase instability of 
Al – Co – Cr – Fe – Ni alloys at elevated temperatures remains a challenge. 
Therefore, Co-free Al – Cr – Fe – Ni alloys have been increasingly 
researched to enhance phase stability at elevated temperatures [ 24 – 26 ]. 
However, controlling the microstructure of the equiatomic AlCrFeNi 
alloy is difficult [ 25 , 27 ] as it lacks single-phase regions. Thus, designing 
Al – Cr – Fe – Ni alloys with adequate high-temperature phase stability and 
strength is important to use these alloys for high-temperature applica -
tions. This study provides guidelines for designing 
precipitation-hardened Al – Cr – Fe – Ni HEAs that are optimized for 
high-temperature applications.

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Thermodynamic calculations and alloy design 
Thermo-Calc software (version 2022b) was used to calculate the 
thermodynamic phase diagram. The HEA database TCHEA5, which in -
cludes the desired alloying elements, was selected for the analysis. 
Pseudo-binary vertical sections represented the multicomponent ther -
modynamics and phase equilibrium. Optimal chemical composition 
with improved heat resistance was derived from the calculated ther -
modynamic phase diagram. 
2.2. Experiments 
Master alloy buttons (10 g), with nominal compositions of AlCoCr -
FeNi, AlCrFeMnNi, AlCrFeNi and AlCrMnNi (in molar ratios), were 
prepared by arc-melting high-purity ( > 99.9 at%) constituent metals in 
an argon atmosphere. Each alloy button was re-melted five times to 
achieve chemical homogeneity. Cylindrical samples with a diameter of 
5 mm and height of 50 mm were fabricated by suction casting. The 
cylindrical specimens with lengths and diameters of 7.5 and 5 mm, 
respectively, were subjected to compression tests (INSTRON, UT-100E) 
at room-temperature (298 K) at a strain rate of 0.0002/s. Master alloy

3. Results and discussion 
Fig. 1 (a) illustrates a pseudo-binary vertical section of the CoCrFe -
Ni – Al system. According to the calculated phase diagram, the AlCoCr -
FeNi alloy comprised various phases, such as B2, BCC, FCC, and sigma 
( σ ), at elevated temperatures. However, a metastable microstructure 
composed of the BCC and B2 phases was obtained by homogenization at 
1573 K, which existed in the B2 single-phase region; this was followed 
by air cooling. Fig. 1 (b) depicts the BCC precipitates within the B2 
matrix in the form of a cuboidal morphology formed by spinodal 
decomposition. However, as the AlCoCrFeNi alloy comprised a hard B2 
matrix after the homogenization treatment, machining was difficult 
owing to high brittleness (inset in Fig. 1 (b)). Precipitating a soft FCC 
phase to improve the room-temperature ductility lowered the high- 
temperature strength [ 19 ]. The valence electron concentration (VEC) 
was considered to address this problem. According to previous studies 
[ 28 ], alloys with a VEC of less than 6.87 exhibit a stable BCC structure. 
Fig. 1 (c) depicts several Al-containing equiatomic HEAs in terms of their 
VEC values. Among them, the AlCrFeMnNi, AlCrFeNi and AlCrMnNi 
alloys with VEC values less than 6.87 are expected to have a stable BCC 
structure, which was confirmed by the XRD results ( Supplementary 
Fig. 1 ) showing no phase transformation even after 6 h of heat treatment 
at 973 K. Fig. 1 (d) illustrates the results of the room-temperature 
compression test of the AlCoCrFeNi, AlCrFeMnNi, AlCrFeNi and 
AlCrMnNi alloys. These alloys were brittle and difficult to machine into 
tensile specimens (insets in Figs. 1 (b) and Fig. 2 (a)), so compression 
testing was used instead. Among the alloys with VEC values less than 
6.87, the AlCrFeNi alloy exhibited the best strength – elongation combi -
nation (yield strength of 1427 MPa and 24 % elongation) comparable to 
AlCoCrFeNi, which exhibited a yield strength of 1483 MPa and 30 % 
elongation. Thus, the AlCrFeNi alloy was selected as the base alloy for 
designing the novel heat-resistant alloys.

For the 13Cr alloy with excellent strength-ductility balance, high- 
temperature tensile tests were performed at various temperatures be -
tween 723 and 973 K at 50 K intervals to investigate the temperature 
dependence of the yield strength. As indicated in Fig. 4 , the results of the 
high-temperature tensile tests verified that the yield strength of the 13Cr 
alloy was higher than those of the conventional ferritic stainless steel 
and Ti – 6Al – 4V alloys. However, the 13Cr alloy exhibited a relatively 
low allowable operating temperature ( < 923 K) despite a melting point 
of approximately 1700 K, similar to that of the Inconel 718 alloy; this 
was attributed to the softening of the B2 phase [ 35 ], which was 
confirmed by observing that the B2 precipitates of the 13Cr alloy were 
elongated in the tensile direction after the yield point at 823 K ( Sup -

strengthening effect at a higher temperature, the B2 precipitates should 
be replaced by other precipitated phases that can maintain a higher 
strength than the matrix even at 923 K or higher temperatures; the L2

4. Conclusions 
In this study, we developed an approach for enhancing the heat 
resistance of AlCoCrFeNi HEA through microstructural and chemical 
tuning. When designing precipitation-hardened heat-resistant alloys, 
the volume fraction of precipitates, high-temperature phase stability, 
possibility of homogenization treatment, interface coherency, and room- 
temperature machinability should be given priority. Based on the ther -
modynamic phase diagrams and VEC values, we developed a novel B2- 
strengthened Al
13.6
Cr
(x)
Fe
(75.2-x)
Ni
11.2

temperature phase stability and mechanical properties. In addition, the 
high-temperature yield strength could be maximized by adjusting the Cr 
content to reduce the radius of precipitates and interprecipitate spacing. 
Based on the experimental results, the following conclusions were 
drawn. 
1. The Co-free AlCrFeNi alloy with a volume fraction of precipitates of 
~20 % exhibited a low VEC value of less than 6.87, maintaining a 
stable BCC structure below the melting point.
