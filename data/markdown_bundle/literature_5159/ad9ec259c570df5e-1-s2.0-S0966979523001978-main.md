ABSTRACT 
Secondary γ ′ phases are a typical structure in Ni-based single crystal superalloys. The unclear precipitation 
temperature makes it difficult to clarify how secondary γ ′ phases affect properties at different temperatures. 
Especially, it is impossible to determine whether secondary γ ′ phases precipitated during deformation or cooling 
after the high temperature deformation. Compared with the conventional two-step cooling method, the remelting 
method could better determine the precipitation temperature of secondary γ ′ phases in Ni-based single crystal 
superalloys and confirm that they would likely be precipitated at 950 
◦
C – 1000 
◦
C. Therefore, secondary γ ′ phases 
could increase the yield strength of Ni-based single crystal superalloys at room temperature but don ’ t affect that 
at 1100 
◦
C due to the remelting of secondary γ ′ phases. 
1. Introduction 
Ni-based single crystal superalloys have been widely used as turbine 
blades due to their excellent strength, fatigue resistance, and creep 
resistance, as well as resistance to corrosion and oxidation at elevated 
temperatures [ 1 ]. These outstanding mechanical properties are derived 
from high volume fractions of cuboidal L1
2
-ordered intermetallic γ ′ 
phases [ 1 – 3 ]. A bimodal microstructure containing primary and sec -
ondary γ ′ phases is usually formed after full heat treatment [ 4 – 6 ]. In 
general, after primary aging, secondary γ ′ phases are observed in the γ 
matrix during air cooling with a faster cooling rate, while there are no 
secondary γ ′ phases during furnace cooling with a lower cooling rate 
[ 7 – 9 ]. 
Although some studies focused on the effect of secondary γ ′ phases on 
the low temperature properties of superalloys, few studies paid attention 
to their effect on high temperature properties due to their remelting 
temperature was not determined [ 7 , 10 , 11 ]. Xiang et al. [ 12 ] suggested 
that secondary γ ′ phases would be formed during creep testing at 
1100 
◦
C and 137 MPa. However, Cormier et al. [ 13 ] commented that the 
secondary γ ′ phase observed in Xiang et al. ’ s paper did not nucleate 
during their creep tests at 1100 
◦
C but during cooling after the experi -
ments. Unfortunately, they were also unable to exclude the possibility 
that the secondary γ ′ phase could be formed during cooling in Xiang 
et al. ’ s response [ 14 ]. Therefore, it is necessary to clarify the 
precipitation temperature of secondary γ ′ phases. He et al. [ 4 ] suggested 
that secondary γ ′ phases could be removed by decreasing the primary 
aging temperature, which might determine the precipitation tempera -
ture. However, when the primary aging temperature decreases, the 
γ ′ -forming elements in the γ matrix channel may not be enough to cause 
secondary γ ′ phases to precipitate during air cooling. Therefore, the 
precipitation temperature of secondary γ ′ phases could not be deter -
mined by reducing the primary aging temperature. In order to clarify 
these disputes, two schemes were designed to determine the precipita -
tion temperature of secondary γ ′ phases in Ni-based single crystal 
superalloys.

After solution heat treatment, these samples were subjected to the 
primary age heat treatment for 4 h at 1180

different methods, as shown in Fig. 1 (a). In Fig. 1 (b), the sample is 
cooled in air after primary aging, and it is clear that there are numerous 
hyperfine secondary γ ′ phases in the γ matrix. These fine secondary γ ′ 
phases have no regular shape and are clustered together. However, 
when the sample is cooled in the furnace after primary aging, there are 
no secondary γ ′ phases as shown in Fig. 1 (c). Therefore, the formation of 
secondary γ ′ phases is obviously affected by the cooling rate after pri -
mary aging and this phenomenon have been also reported in other 
studies [ 7 , 9 ]. After primary aging, the γ ′ -forming elements (Al and Ta) 
are still supersaturated in the γ matrix channel; hence, the faster the 
cooling rate, the smaller the diffusion time, and the shorter the diffusion 
distance, which is beneficial to the formation of secondary γ ′ phases [ 8 ].

cooling after primary aging at 1180

temperature of secondary γ ′ phases should be below 1180

it is difficult to determine the precipitation temperature by the contin -
uous cooling process. Therefore, a two-step cooling method was adopted 
to determine the precipitation temperature, also known as interrupted 
continuous cooling, which was mostly used to adjust the size distribu -
tion of γ ′ phases in Nickel-based powder metallurgy superalloys [ 15 , 16 ].

cooled sample, they should precipitate during the second step cooling. 
Therefore, the precipitation temperature is close to the lowest first step

Based on this qualitative analysis, during air cooling after primary aging, 
it has the minimum diffusion distance, which could be represented by 
the letter D. However, during the two-step cooling process, the diffusion 
distance became larger, mainly in the first step cooling process, because 
the furnace cooling stage needs a long time and has a higher diffusion 
rate. Hence, compared with air cooling (curve A), the diffusion distance 
for two-step cooling is much longer. For example, the diffusion distance 
of method B is 3.24D, which is 2.24 times larger than that of A. Hence, it 
is obvious that the first step cooling process in the furnace has an 
important effect for the diffusion distance. During the first step cooling 
process, the cooling rate is low and the diffusion distance is larger so that 
the saturation of γ ′ -forming elements in the γ matrix would sharply 
decrease. Therefore, when the sample is first cooled to 1100 
◦

furnace, only a small amount of secondary γ ′ phases could precipitate 
during the subsequent air-cooling process. When the first cooling 
termination temperature further decreases, secondary γ ′ phases no 
longer precipitated. He et al. [ 4 ] also found that the secondary γ ′ phase 
could be removed by decreasing the primary aging temperature, which 
was also due to decreasing the saturation of γ ′ -forming elements in the γ 
matrix. Compared with the two-step cooling method, the saturation of 
γ ′ -forming elements decreases more slowly during continuous 
air-cooling. Hence, during continuous air cooling, when the temperature 
dropped to lower, the saturation of γ ′ -forming elements could still meet 
the minimum level required for secondary γ ′ phase precipitation. How -
ever, for the two-step cooling method, the saturation of γ ′ -forming ele -
ments decreases more quickly during the first step cooling, which results 
in the saturation of γ ′ -forming elements could not meet the minimum 
level required for secondary γ ′ phase precipitation, even at higher first 
cooling termination temperatures. Therefore, the two-step cooling 
method would overestimate the precipitation temperature of secondary 
γ ′ phases, which should precipitate at a lower temperature.

Finally, the effect of secondary γ ′ phases on the tensile properties of 
alloys at different temperatures is shown in Fig. 4 . The sample with room 
temperature-air cooling (RT-AC) represents the tensile properties of the 
sample with secondary γ ′ phases at room temperature, and that with 
room temperature-furnace cooling (RT-FC) represents the sample 
without secondary γ ′ phases. Fig. 4 (a) shows that the yield strength of 
samples with secondary γ ′ phases is larger than that of samples without 
secondary γ ′ phases at room temperature. Compared with sample RT-FC, 
the yield strength of sample RT-AC is approximately 919 MPa, which is 
7.36% higher than that of RT-FC (856 MPa). Different positions on the

there are lots of secondary γ ′ phases in the γ matrix before deformation

4. Conclusion 
In summary, two schemes have been designed to study the precipi -
tation temperature of secondary γ ′ phases in Ni-based single crystal su -
peralloys. The two-step cooling method deduces that the secondary γ ′ 
phase maybe precipitate between 1050 
◦
C and 1100 
◦
C. Due to the low 
cooling rate during the first step cooling process, the precipitation 
temperature is overestimated. The remelting process could better 
determine the precipitation temperature of secondary γ ′ phases and 
confirm that they would likely be precipitated at 950 
◦
C – 1000 
◦
C. 
Therefore, secondary γ ′ phases would increase the yield strength of the 
alloy at room temperature and not affect the yield strength at 1100 
◦
C 
due to the remelting of secondary γ ′ phases.
