ABSTRACT
A new series of high-entropy alloys denoted as Mox(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x, has been designed for aero-engine applications. The study focused on
investigating the microstructural evolution and strengthening mechanisms of the alloys in response to varying Mo content. Results indicate
that the alloys primarily display a BCC crystal structure. Notably, an FCC phase precipitates in the alloy when the Mo content reaches
30%. Furthermore, as the Mo content increases, the strength of the alloys significantly increases while their plasticity diminishes. The room-
temperature yield strength of the alloys shows a substantial increase from 729 MPa (x = 0) to 1202 MPa (x = 25), while the yield strength at
1200○C rises from 62 MPa (x = 0) to 498 MPa (x = 30). Concurrently, the microhardness of the alloys also increases from 247 HV (x = 0)
to 471 HV (x= 30). The observed strengthening mechanisms include solid solution strengthening, fine grain strengthening, and precipitated
phase strengthening.

I. INTRODUCTION
Aero-engines necessitate materials that possess a combination
of traits, including lightweight, high strength, high toughness, and
exceptional heat resistance, in order to endure the extreme condi-
tions of high temperature and high pressure. 1 As the aero-engine
performance continues to advance, the temperatures these materials
must withstand are progressively escalating. Nevertheless, conven-
tional materials such as nickel-based alloys and titanium alloys
are only suitable for temperatures below 1000 ○C. High-entropy
alloys (HEAs) possess many excellent properties, including but
not limited to high strength and hardness, outstanding corrosion
resistance, good high-temperature performance, and exceptional
wear resistance.2–5 Research literature indicates that refractory high-
entropy alloys (RHEAs) demonstrate remarkable specific strength in
the range of 1000–1600 ○C and hold promising prospects for appli-
cations in aerospace and other high-temperature industries. 2,6–9
Among these RHEAs, the Nb–Ta–Ti–Zr system RHEAs are capa-
ble of forming a single BCC structure with similar atomic radius
and electronegativity, as well as low mixing enthalpy, ultimately
exhibiting excellent mechanical properties. 10–13 For example, the
TiZrNbTaMo RHEAs possess a compressive strength of 2600 MPa, a
hardness of 500 HV, and a compression deformation limit exceeding
30%.10
Tseng et al. 14 investigated the influence of Mo, Nb,
Ta, Ti, and Zr on the mechanical properties of equiatomic
Hf–Mo–Nb–Ta–Ti–Zr alloys. Their findings revealed that Mo plays
a significant role in enhancing the high-temperature strength of
the alloy. Specifically, the yield strength of the HfNbTaTiZr alloy
was measured at 295 and 92 MPa when tested at 1000 and 1200 ○C,
respectively. Upon the addition of Mo, the yield strength of the
HfMoNbTaTiZr alloy increased significantly to 814 MPa at 1000○C
and 556 MPa at 1200 ○C.14 This observation aligns with numerous
other studies that have demonstrated the substantial enhancement
of high-temperature strength in HEAs through the incorporation of
Mo into the alloy systems.15–20
Currently, the majority of known RHEAs exhibit high
density.19,21 To alleviate this issue, the content of high-density ele-
ments, such as Ta and Zr, can be reduced, and simultaneously,
the high-temperature yield strength can be effectively adjusted by

II. EXPERIMENTS
In this investigation, a series of Mo x(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x (x = 0,
5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30) alloys with varying compositions were pro-
duced through vacuum arc melting, employing high-purity Mo,
Nb, Ta, Ti, and Zr metal particles with a purity greater than
99.9%. To ensure compositional uniformity, the alloys underwent
a minimum of five remelting cycles under a vacuum pressure of
3 × 10−3 Pa. The physical phases of the alloys were analyzed using a
Rigaku XRD-6100 x-ray diffractometer (XRD) with an x-ray energy
of 8.05 KeV (Cu–K α), and the scanning angle range was from 20 ○
to 90○. Microstructural observations and compositional analyses
were conducted using a Phenom-XL scanning electron microscope
(SEM) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS).
Furthermore, the microhardness of the alloy was measured using an
HV-1000A microhardness tester, applying a load of 500 g for 10 s,
with five tests conducted at 1 mm intervals along a line. Addition-
ally, room temperature compression tests were performed using an
E45-105 electronic universal testing machine with a strain rate of
1 × 10−3/s and a deformation level of 40%. Additionally, room
temperature compression tests were performed using an E45-105
electronic universal testing machine with a strain rate of 1 × 10−3/s
and a deformation degree of 40% on Φ6 mm × 9 mm samples.
Similarly, the high-temperature compression test was performed uti-
lizing the Gleeble-3500 thermal simulation testing machine. The test
parameters included a heating rate of 10 ○C/s, a holding time of
5 min, a vacuum degree of 1 × 10−3 Pa, a strain rate of 1 × 10−3/s,
and a deformation degree of 50%.

the atomic bond length and a subsequent reduction in the lattice
constant.

B. Mechanical properties
Figures 5(a) and 5(b) presents the compressive stress–strain
curves of the as-cast alloys at room temperature and 1200○C, respec-
tively. Meanwhile, Fig. 5(c) illustrates the relationship between the
yield strength of the alloys and the Mo content, and Fig. 5(d) depicts
the microhardness of the alloys. The alloy has a yield strength of
729 MPa and a hardness of 247 HV at room temperature when no
Mo is added. As the Mo content increases, the strength and hardness
of the alloys increase, while their plasticity decreases. Alloys with a
Mo content below 15% exhibit good plasticity, with a compression
strain exceeding 30%. Notably, the yield strength at room temper-
ature peaks at 1400 MPa with a compression strain limit of 16%
when the Mo content is 25%. Furthermore, the hardness reaches its
maximum at 457 HV when the Mo content is 30%, but the result-
ing Mo 30 alloy becomes exceedingly brittle at room temperature,
with a compression strain limit of 5%. This brittleness is attributed
to the precipitation of the FCC phase in the alloy at this compo-
sition, which significantly impedes dislocation movement, leading
to stress concentration and subsequent fracture during compres-
sion. Additionally, Fig. 5(c) shows that the high-temperature yield
strength of the alloys increases from 62 to 498 MPa as the Mo
content increases from 0% to 30%, indicating a significant enhance-
ment in high-temperature strength with the addition of Mo to
the HEAs.
IV. DISCUSSION
A. Phase formation rules
At present, there is a lack of a comprehensive theoretical frame-
work to understand the phase formation mechanism and the factors
influencing the performance of HEAs. The existing understand-
ing is primarily rooted in thermodynamics and the Hume–Rothery
criteria.19 Factors influencing the formation of solid solutions in
alloys include mixing enthalpy ( ΔHmix), mixing entropy ( ΔSmix),
thermodynamic parameters Ω, atomic radius difference ( δ), elec-
tronegativity difference ( Δχ), and valence electron concentration
(VEC).19,23,24 The calculated parameter results for seven alloys and
the theoretical melting point (T m) of these alloys are presented in
Table I.

ple solid solution structure when Ω ≥ 1.1 and δ ≤ 6.6. Meanwhile,

Guo et al.24 suggested that the VEC correlates with the phase struc-
ture of solid solutions in HEAs: VEC < 6.87 tends to form a BCC
solid solution, 6.87 < VEC< 8.0 tends to form a BCC + FCC mixed
solid solution, and VEC ≥ 8.0 tends to form an FCC solid solution.
Based on these criteria and the calculation results in Table I, it can be
inferred that the seven alloys exhibit a single-phase BCC structure,
but the Mo30 alloy actually precipitates an FCC phase.
The data in Table I indicate an initial increase followed by a
decrease in ΔSmix. The increase in alloy ΔSmix leads to greater disor-
der in the alloy system, reduces the free energy of the alloy, causes
a chaotic distribution of different alloy elements in the crystal lat-
tice positions, and inhibits the formation of ordered phases and
phase separation. Additionally, the decrease in ΔHmix to a nega-
tive value with increasing Mo content suggests enhanced binding
forces between alloying elements and an increased tendency to
form intermetallic compounds. Moreover, the increase in Mo con-
tent corresponds to an increase in the atomic radius difference and
electronegativity difference in the alloy. The increase in atomic
radius difference is typically accompanied by lattice distortion, 22
leading to increased strain energy within the crystal and higher inter-
nal energy of the alloy, which is unfavorable for the formation of
the solid solution phase. Furthermore, the heightened electronega-
tivity difference in the alloy facilitates electron gain and loss by alloy
elements, ultimately promoting the formation of intermetallic com-
pounds. These trends collectively contribute to the precipitation of
the FCC phase.

and microstrain ( ε) of the alloys can be estimated using Jade
software.25,26 Although the dislocation density and microstrain

In the case of Mo x(Nb3TaTi3Zr)100−x alloys with a single BCC
solid solution phase, the primary strengthening mechanism is solid
solution strengthening. 22 This occurs due to the lattice distortion
resulting from the interaction between solute atoms and dislo-
cations, which impedes the dislocation movement necessary for
plastic deformation. Furthermore, as depicted in Fig. 6, with the
increase in Mo content, the dislocation density increases and the
subgrain size decreases. The pinning of dislocations prevents dis-
location slip, thereby enhancing the material’s strength. The higher
dislocation density increases the strength of the alloys but reduces
their plasticity.20,22 Additionally, fine-grain strengthening also plays
a role. Figure 6 illustrates that with the increase in Mo content,
the subgrain size decreases. According to the Hall–Petch formula:
σs = σ0 + k/D^1/2, the yield strength of the alloy increases as the
grain size decreases. Moreover, with an increase in Mo content,
the ΔHmix of the alloys becomes more negative, indicating stronger
bonding between the alloying elements, contributing to an overall
improvement in the alloy’s hardness.
At 1200○C, the alloys exhibit not only solid solution strength-
ening and fine grain strengthening observed at room temperature
but also an increase in the T m with the Mo content. This leads
to a decrease in the high-temperature softening effect, contribut-
ing to the enhanced high-temperature yield strength. The Mo 30
alloy becomes highly brittle at room temperature, while at 1200 ○C,
enhanced slip and dislocation mobility between crystals reduce
stress concentration, enabling the alloy to deform uniformly. There-
fore, the FCC precipitated phase is the reason for the sudden
increase in the high-temperature yield strength of the Mo 30 alloy
at 1200○C.
V. CONCLUSIONS
(1) The seven alloys displayed coarse dendrite morphology,
indicating a wide solidification temperature range and the
production of composition segregation. Mo, Nb, and Ta
elements, which possess higher melting points, were found
enriched in the dendrites, while Ti and Zr elements, with
lower melting points, were enriched in the interdendrites.
(2) The seven alloys primarily exhibited a BCC crystal struc-
ture. Specifically, when the Mo content was less than 25%,
the alloys displayed a single BCC structure. However, when
the Mo content reached 30%, the FCC phase (Zr-rich) was
observed to precipitate between the dendrites of the alloy.
With an increase in Mo content, the lattice constant and
subgrain size decreased, while the dislocation density and
microstrain increased.
(3) The strength of the alloys increased with the increase in Mo
content while the plasticity decreased. The room tempera-
ture yield strength of the alloys increased from 729 MPa
(x = 0) to 1202 MPa (x = 25), while the yield strength
at 1200 ○C increased from 62 MPa (x = 0) to 498 MPa
(x = 30). Additionally, the microhardness of the alloys
increased from 247 HV (x = 0) to 471 HV (x = 30).
The strengthening mechanism was mainly attributed to
solid solution strengthening, fine grain strengthening, and
precipitated phase strengthening.
