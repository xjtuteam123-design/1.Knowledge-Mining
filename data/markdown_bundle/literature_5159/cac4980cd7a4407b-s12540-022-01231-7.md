Designing Ternary Laves Phase‑Based Eutectic Alloys CrFeNbx 
with Excellent Strength, Toughness, and Thermal Stability
Yunlong Xue1 · Yuxuan Wang1 · Haohua Sun1 · Na Feng1 · Liang Yuan2
Received: 29 January 2022 / Accepted: 20 April 2022 / Published online: 11 June 2022 
© The Author(s) under exclusive licence to The Korean Institute of Metals and Materials 2022
Abstract
Three ternary Laves phase-based eutectic alloys CrFeNbx (x = 2.0, 2.35, 2.7) were designed successfully by combining the 
binary phase diagrams and thermodynamics calculations. With the increasing Nb content, the solidified microstructures 
evolved from the primary (Cr, Fe)2Nb plus the eutectic (Cr, Fe)2Nb/Nbss in the CrFeNb2.0, via the full eutectic (Cr, Fe)2Nb/
Nbss in the CrFeNb2.35, and finally to the primary Nbss plus the eutectic (Cr, Fe)2Nb/Nbss in the CrFeNb2.7. The full eutec-
tic (Cr, Fe) 2Nb/Nbss of the CrFeNb2.35 was found with outstanding thermal stability, and the lamellar eutectic was quite 
stable even under the heat-treatment of 1273 K/48 h. The compression strength and fracture toughness increased firstly and 
decreased subsequently with the increase of Nb content, which reached their maximums of 2.54 GPa and 18.25 MPa·m 1/2, 
respectively, in the CrFeNb2.35. The excellent combination of compression strength and fracture toughness in the CrFeNbx 
(x = 2.0, 2.35, 2.7) were attributed to the synergistic effects of Laves phase strengthening, interface strengthening, solid 
solution strengthening, alloying toughening and Nbss phase toughening mechanisms.
Keywords Laves phase · Microstructure · Thermal stability · Mechanical properties
1 Introduction
Laves phase Cr2Nb has been attracting much attention of 
academia and industries over the past decades because of its 
high melting points, low density, high strength, and excellent 
creep and corrosion resistance [1 –3]. However, the factual 
engineering applications of Cr2Nb were inhibited signifi-
cantly by its extremely low facture toughness at room tem-
perature, typically around 1.4  MPam1/2 [4, 5].
For improving the fracture toughness of Cr2Nb, 
many efforts have been carried out and the second phase 
toughening was proven to be an effective approach [4 –7]. 
The second phase toughening method is to introduce some 
ductile phases and thereby form the Laves phase matrix com-
posites. The ductile phases can be incorporated by eutectic 
reactions or through solidifying the alloys with proper com-
positions. For instance, the ductile phases Cr solid solution 
(Crss) and Nb solid solution (Nbss) can be introduced based 
on eutectic reactions Cr2Nb-Crss and Cr2Nb-Nbss, and the 
fracture toughness of composites Cr2Nb/Crss and Cr2Nb/
Nbss can reach about 6 MPa⋅ m1/2 [4, 6], at least four time 
larger than single phase Cr2Nb. In similar, by solidifying ter-
nary Laves phase-based composites Cr2Nb–Ti, the fracture 
toughness can be increased to approximately 9.5 MPa⋅m1/2, 
if the volume fraction of Cr2Nb was controlled to be lower 
than 46% [7]. Despite the success of prior efforts in improv-
ing the fracture toughness of Cr2Nb, the overall fracture 
toughness of composites is still not too high to meet the 
requirement of engineering application [8]. Meanwhile, the 
enhancement of fracture toughness presently is always at the 
cost of reducing the strength of composites, and the thermal 
stability of as-fabricated composites was quite inferior [9 ]. 
Therefore, how to effectively toughen Cr2Nb but without 
greatly reducing the strength and thermal stability of com-
posites has become a crucial but unresolved problem.

Designing multiple Laves phase-based eutectic alloys 
sheds light on this problem. By fabricating these alloys, the 
fracture toughness of the brittle Laves phase [10, 11] can be 
improved significantly by the alternative brittle and ductile 
phases. Simultaneously, the high strength can be realized 
by taking advantage of eutectic interface strengthening (i.e., 
the eutectic phase boundary) and severe lattice distortion 
effects [12, 13]. Thus, the trade-off between toughness and 
strength can be alleviated or even avoided. Besides, the ther-
mal stability of multiple Laves phase-based eutectic alloys 
was superior to that of conventional composites because of 
sluggish diffusion effect and low-energy eutectic interfaces 
[11].
Therefore, the concept of multiple eutectic alloys was 
adopted herein to probe the possibility of improving the 
toughness, strength and thermal stability of Laves phase 
 Cr2Nb alloys. In order to design multiple Laves phase-
based eutectic alloys, the eutectic reaction Cr2Nb-Nbss was 
selected, and the remaining work was to find a proper ele-
ment composing eutectic system with Cr or Nb according to 
the rule of multiple eutectic alloy [5]. By referring to current 
binary phase diagrams, element Fe was adopted finally based 
on three following main considerations. Firstly, element Fe 
can form a stable BCC phase with Cr [5 , 11] and compose 
eutectic reaction with Nb [14], thus the CrFe alloy could 
serve as an integrated pseudo-element for eutectic reaction. 
Secondly, the addition of Fe might increase the volume frac-
tion of Laves phase within the eutectic, then the strength of 
eutectic composite could be increased. Thirdly, the radius 
of element Fe is lower than that of element Cr, and the Fe 
within Cr2Nb could increase the atomic free volume of 
deformable αcβ sandwiches and thereby generate the alloy-
ing toughening effect [13, 15]. Based on above considera-
tions, a Laves phase-based eutectic system of CrFeNbx was 
eventually decided, and the detailed process of determin-
ing the exact eutectic point of CrFeNbx was presented in 
Sect. 3.1.
2 Material and Methods
The ternary Laves phase-based eutectic alloys CrFeNb x 
(x = 2.0, 2.35, 2.7, all compositions in this study were 
given in at.% unless otherwise stated) were prepared by 
vacuum non-consumable arc melting from high purity met-
als (> 99.95 wt.%). A little amount of titanium was melted 
before every ingot melting to eliminate the residual oxygen 
and nitrogen. Each ingot was inverted and re-melted for 5 
times to ensure chemical homogeneity. Subsequently, the 
arc-melted ingots were encapsulated in argon backfilled 
quartz ampoule for heat-treatments. The heat-treatments 
were conducted under the conditions of 1273 K/48 h and 
1473 K/48 h, respectively, for evaluating the thermal stabil-
ity of microstructures.
The constitution phases were identified by a Rigaku D/
MAX 2400 x-ray diffractometer with Cu Ka radiation. The 
microstructures were analyzed by a FEI Tecnai G2F20 
S–TWIN scanning electron microscopy (SEM) equipped 
with an energy dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The 
image analysis software (Image J) was applied to measure 
the microstructures (e.g., the eutectic lamellar spacing and 
intermetallic volume fraction).
The compression strength (σ c) was measured from the 
samples with dimensions of ϕ 4 × 6 mm3 by a PT-1036PC 
universal test machine at strain rate of 10–3  s−1. The micro-
hardness (H V) and fracture toughness (K IC) of the alloys 
were evaluated by a micro-hardness tester (HV-1000A) 
with a Vickers intender at room temperature. The test con-
ditions of 10 N/15 s and 20 N/15 s were adopted for the 
measurements of micro-hardness and fracture toughness, 
respectively. For every test, twelve data were obtained to

3 Results and Discussions

ternary Laves phase-based eutectic alloy with the compo-

3.2 Microstructure Characterizations
Fig.  2 shows the XRD patterns of the designed alloys 
 CrFeNbx (x = 2.0, 2.35, 2.7) under the arc-melted (AM) and 
heat-treated (HT) conditions. As shown in Fig.  2a, the AM 
alloys were composed of Laves phase, Nbss, and Fe7Nb6, 
and the Laves phase was detected with three polytypic struc-
tures of the C14, C15, and C36. Under the heat-treatments of 
both 1273 K/48 h and 1473 K/48 h, as indicated in Fig. 2b-c, 
the constitution phases within the CrFeNbx (x = 2.35, 2.7) 
were unchangeable, while the intensity of diffraction peaks 
of phase Fe7Nb6 in the CrFeNb2.0 increased obviously. Thus, 
the thermal stability of the alloy CrFeNb2.0 was inferior to 
that of the alloys CrFeNbx (x = 2.35, 2.7).
Figure  3 shows the microstructures of alloys CrFeNbx 
(x = 2.0, 2.35, 2.7) under different conditions. As shown in 
Fig. 3a–c, the microstructures of AM alloys evolved from 
hypoeutectic, via full eutectic, and finally to hypereutectic 
with increasing Nb content, being consistent with the results 
presented in Fig. 1 and therefore demonstrating the validity 
of eutectic design. Combined with the XRD and the EDS 
analysis, the primary phase in the hypo-eutectic was deter -
mined as ternary Laves phase (Cr, Fe) 2Nb, and that in the 
hyper-eutectic was decided as solid solution Nbss, and the 
eutectic in three alloys was found to be (Cr, Fe) 2Nb/Nbss. 
What to be noticed was that, there was a little amount of 
light-grey phase identified as intermetallic (Cr, Fe) 7Nb6 at 
the margins of eutectic cells in the CrFeNbx (x = 2.35, 2.7). 
The reason is that, the melting point of element Fe is lower 
than that of elements Cr and Nb, and the element Fe segre-
gated preferably at the frontier of solid/liquid interface, lead-
ing to the formation of Fe-rich intermetallic (Cr, Fe)7Nb6 at 
the margins of eutectic cells.

stabilities. As presented in Fig.  3d–f, the microstructures 
of the CrFeNbx (x = 2.35, 2.7) were quite stable, while that

precipitation Nbss in the primary (Cr, Fe)2Nb increased, and

the microstructure of the CrFeNb2.7 became coarsened and

precipitation in the primary Nbss, indicating better thermal

3.3 Mechanical Properties
Fig.  6 shows the fracture toughness and compression 
strength of alloys CrFeNbx (x = 2.0, 2.35, 2.7). With the 
increase of Nb content, the fracture toughness of the arc-
melted alloys increased firstly and decreased subsequently, 
which reached its maximum of 18.25 ± 0.78 MPa·m1/2 in the 
AM-CrFeNb2.35. In similar, the compression strength of the 
arc-melted alloys reached its peak in the AM-CrFeNb 2.35 
and determined as 2.54 ± 0.09 GPa. Therefore, the eutec -
tic CrFeNb2.35 was developed with excellent comprehen-
sive mechanical properties. It has been well known that the 
eutectic lamellar is helpful in strengthening and toughening 
alloys because of its well bonded interfaces [19]. Thus, the 
fracture toughness and compression strength reached their 
maximums in the full eutectic CrFeNb2.35.

properties of three alloys were affected to different degrees 
by heat-treatments. In fact, the fracture toughness of three 
alloys was enhanced by the heat-treatment of 1273 K/48 h

3.4 Toughening and Strengthening Mechanisms
Usually, the increase of toughness always comes at the cost 
of the decrease of strength for conventional alloys, whereas 
the experimental alloys, especially the CrFeNb2.35, were 
manifested with a good combination of strength and tough-
ness. For studying the balanced mechanical properties, the 
underlying toughening and strengthening mechanisms were 
discussed in following sections.
3.4.1 Toughening Mechanisms
As described in Fig. 5, the element Fe segregated preferen-
tially into Laves phase (Cr, Fe)2Nb rather than Nbss, thus the 
toughening effect by alloying Fe on Cr2Nb was introduced. 
For interpreting the alloying toughening effect, the atomic 
structure and associated atomic free volume of the deform-
able αcβ sandwiches in the binary Cr2Nb and ternary (Cr, 
Fe)2Nb was compared in Fig. 8.
In case of binary Cr2Nb, the atomic free volume within 
the deformable αcβ sandwiches was quite limited because 
of the close packed nature of Laves phase. However, by dis-
solving the alloying Fe into Cr2Nb, the atomic free volume 
(marked by yellow bar) was enlarged evidently by the occu-
pancy of Fe onto Cr site [15]. Consequently, the up-down 
movements of atoms in the deformable αcβ sandwiches were 
prompted during the plastic deformation, thereby generating 
the alloying toughening effect on Cr2Nb.
Except for the alloying toughening effect by Fe, the 
toughening effect generated by ductile Nbss also existed.

Figure 12 shows the relationship between the compres -

phase-based alloys [4, 7, 13, 30]. The compressive strength 
increased gradually with the decrease of eutectic lamellar 
spacing, which demonstrated that the eutectic interface was 
helpful in strengthening the alloys.
Attractively, the compressive strength of the AM-
CrFeNb 2.35 was higher than other alloys (i.e., the HT-
CrFeNb2.7 at 1273 K) with similar eutectic lamellar spacing.

with full eutectic, and both phases within the eutectic pre-

fication, and the well bonded interface between eutectic 
phases was reported to helpful in strengthening the alloys 
[19]. In comparison, the primary phases Nbss appeared in 
the HT-CrFeNb2.7 at 1273 K, the interface between the pri-
mary phase and eutectic was thermodynamically unstable 
and detrimental to the strength improvement of the alloys.
4 Conclusions
(1) Three ternary Laves phase-based eutectic alloys 
 CrFeNbx (x = 2.0, 2.35, 2.7) were designed success-
fully by combining the binary phase diagrams and ther-
modynamics calculations.
(2) The solidified microstructures evolved from the pri -
mary (Cr, Fe) 2Nb plus the eutectic (Cr, Fe) 2Nb/Nbss 
in the CrFeNb2.0, via the full eutectic (Cr, Fe)2Nb/Nbss 
in the CrFeNb2.35, and finally to the primary Nbss plus 
the eutectic (Cr, Fe)2Nb/Nbss in the CrFeNb2.7.
(3) The full eutectic (Cr, Fe) 2Nb/Nbss of the CrFeNb2.35 
was found with outstanding thermal stability, and the 
lamellar eutectic was quite stable even under the heat-
treatment of 1273 K/48h.
(4) The compression strength and fracture toughness 
increased firstly and decreased subsequently with the 
increase of Nb content in the CrFeNbx (x = 2.0, 2.35, 
2.7), which reached their maximums of 2.54 GPa and 
18.25 MPa·m1/2, respectively, in the CrFeNb2.35.
(5) The excellent combination of compression strength 
and fracture toughness in the CrFeNbx (x = 2.0, 2.35,
