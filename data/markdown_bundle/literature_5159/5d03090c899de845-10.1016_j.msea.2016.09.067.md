Abstract: Refractory metal alloys such as tungsten and tantalum have long been primary 
materials of use in high temperature applications. The difficulty of processing tungsten and 
tantalum alloys makes the job of economically fabricating components from these alloys 
challenging. Furthermore, despite their intended use at elevated temperatures, limited research 
work has been to done to evaluate mechanical properties of these alloys at elevated temperatures. 
In this work, the mechanical properties of three tantalum alloys, pure Ta, Ta-10vol%W, and Ta-
10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC are studied using a range of mechanical testing procedures. Pure 
tantalum is found to display improved ductility at both room and elevated temperatures in 
comparison to alloyed samples. Addition of both 10vol%W and 1.5vol%TiC addition is found to 
result in comparable high-temperature strength and ductility in comparison to 10vol%W 
addition, and improved hardness and flexural strength at room temperature. XRD and EDS 
analysis paired with microscopy of sintered materials and there fracture surfaces suggest this 
improvement is associated with solid solution strengthening occurring at Ta-W grain boundary 
regions. Analysis of sample fracture surfaces reveals indications of increased sample ductility at 
elevated temperatures for all alloys shown by tearing of tensile bar surfaces near the fracture 
regime and formation of river patterns and other ductile features. 
Introduction 
Metallic alloys of tantalum (Ta) and tungsten (W) have been extensively used for a variety of 
high-temperature applications including nuclear reactor radiation shields1,2 and rocket nozzles3. 
The high melting point and strong d-orbital bonding within these materials allow them to 
maintain strength and hardness at elevated temperatures beyond those of other common metallic 
systems such as nickel, titanium, and aluminum4. Due to their refractory nature, these materials 
cannot be processed using conventional techniques. Generally plasma based techniques such as

arc melting or powder metallurgy routs such as hot isostatic pressing must be used, which require 
large and expensive energy sources, or powder metallurgy routes such as hot isostatic pressing5. 
Multiple studies have been performed investigating the consolidation of refractory metal 
alloys, however most of them have focused on alloying of tungsten with transition metal carbide 
and oxide additives. Despite the excellent mechanical properties of these Ta alloys, studies of 
the mechanical properties and microstructure of tantalum alloys are relatively few in number. 
Microstructural properties of Ta alloys produced by hot pressing has been performed by several 
groups including Kim et al. 6 and Ott et al. 7, which demonstrated powder metallurgy as capable 
of producing Ta alloys with small grain sizes and decent room-temperature mechanical 
properties. Chen et al. performed constitutive modeling of the mechanical behavior of several 
Ta-W alloys produced by arc melting and forging techniques 8. Additional investigations into 
mechanical behavior include work by Briant et al.9 and Harris 10, however these studies fail to 
explore the high-temperature behavior of Ta alloys. 
Field Assisted Sintering Technique (FAST), otherwise known as Spark Plasma Sintering 
(SPS), allows for sintering of materials at significantly reduced temperature and time in 
comparison to traditional sintering technologies, offering the potential for substantial cost 
reductions in production of refractory metal alloys and compounds. The improved sinterability 
of materials using FAST often allows for the consolidation of powders without the need to use 
sintering aids such as nickel and iron in tungsten based materials, many of which serve to reduce 
desired thermal and mechanical properties of material alloys11. Furthermore, the rapid heating 
and cooling rates FAST systems have to offer, significantly reduce undesired grain growth 
during sintering , allowing for production of alloys with improved ductility and strength 12,13,14. 
While some work has been performed investigating properties such as residual stress 15 and 
texture evolution as a function of sintering with hot pressing (HP) or FAST 16, no detailed studies 
have been performed on mechanical properties of Ta alloys formed by FAST 17. In this study, 
the mechanical properties of tantalum alloys formed by FAST were investigated at both room 
and elevated temperatures of 1927 and 2204 ˚C. 
Experimental Procedure 
Tantalum powder supplied by H. C. Starck (+325 low oxygen specification), tungsten powder 
supplied by Global Tungsten and Powders Inc. (M10 Grade), and titanium carbide powder 
supplied by Skyspring Nanomaterials Inc. (TiC 99%, 40 nm) were used for all investigations. 
All powders were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) to determine powder chemistry and 
phase composition. Particle size and morphology of powders was evaluated by scanning 
electron microscopy imaging. XRD was performed using a PanANALYTICAL Empyrean 
Bragg-Brantano X-ray diffractometer with a Cu-Kα source scanning from 5-80° 2θ. MDI JadeTM 
software was used for phase analysis of collected XRD patterns. SEM imaging of powders was 
conducted using a LEO 1530 FESEM with a 0.5 kV accelerating voltage. Materials were mixed 
by rotary ball milling with 3/16” tungsten carbide media in 2000 ml NalgeneTM bottles and then

ball milling for 4 hours at 160 RPM using a rotary ball mill machine. Powders were weighed 
prior to sintering to produce mixtures of pure Ta, Ta-10vol%W, and Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC. 
Tungsten powder was treated at 950 °C for four hours under a hydrogen gas atmosphere prior to 
mixing to reduce the presence of oxygen. 
A 250 ton FCT System GmbH Spark Plasma Sintering Furnace was used for the production of 
all sintered compacts. An image of the FAST system as well as the temperature, pressure, and 
piston displacement profile typical for the tile sintering process are shown in Figure 1. A heating 
rate of 50 °C/min was used to elevate the sample to the final sintering temperature of 2000 °C, 
while pressure was applied at a slow ramp rate of 5 MPa/min prior to increasing temperature 
beyond 400 °C to ensure compaction of powder prior to the start of the sintering process. 
 
Figure 1: (a) Image of 250 ton FAST system used for tile fabrication and (b) Typical sintering curve obtained for 
sintering. Curve taken from Ta-10vol%W sample. Image courtesy of FCT Systeme GmbH. 
Powders were weighed out in quantities corresponding to the theoretical amount required to 
create 101x 101 x 6 mm dimension tiles assuming full powder densification. Powders were then 
quickly transferred to graphite dies to minimize oxygen contamination from exposure to air. Dies 
were loaded into the FAST system and placed under vacuum. Continuous DC current was 
applied to the graphite die to achieve a stable heating rate. Temperature of the die throughout the 
run was monitored through using a pyrometer positioned over the center of the die system. Tiles 
were fabricated using a single-step sintering hold temperature of 2000°C for 15 min and system 
pressure of 35 MPa. A vacuum environment was used for all runs to minimize oxidation and 
prevent the formation of undesired tantalum or titanium hydride and nitride. Following 
completion of the sintering, application of piston pressure onto the die was ceased, and the die 
was allowed to cool to room temperature while maintaining a vacuum environment. Density of 
sintered tiles was measured using the Archimede’s principle method, and compared to theoretical 
density calculated using the rule of mixtures approach in order to obtain a relative density of the 
sintered products.

In order to perform mechanical testing of the material, six tensile test bars were cut from each 
sintered tile using a wire EDM system. Tensile test samples were cut from the center of the 
sample to ensure complete removal of the outer carbide layer. 
Each tensile test sample was then cleaned using sand blasting followed by polishing with 320 
grit sand paper in order to remove machining artifacts and prevent premature failure of test 
samples by surface crack initiation. Tensile test samples were again measured using 
Archimede’s principle to obtain a sample density free of error from the carbide layer. The elastic 
modulus of each tensile test sample was then measured by acoustic impedance measurement 
using an oscilloscope with M1105 ¼” and V1565 ¼" transducers for measurement of 
longitudinal and shear waves through the sample, respectively. Two measurements were made 
on each tensile sample for a total of 12 measurements of elastic modulus for each tile. 
In addition to tensile test samples, 6 mm x 3 mm x 40 mm bend test samples were cut from each 
tile and then gravel tumbled and polished to remove edges and surface defects. Samples were 
then tested in accordance with the ASTM C1161 four-point bend test procedure. An Instron 
5866 testing apparatus was used in all instances. Five samples were tested for each alloy. 
Material produced from regions between tensile test samples was cut, mounted, and polished to a 
final polish of 0.05 μm. Vicker’s hardness testing was performed on polished samples using a 
Vecto V100 Hardness indentation machine with a 1 kg load following ASTM standard E384-11. 
Polished samples were etched using a 2:2:1 ratio HF:HCl:HNO3 mixture and imaged using 
optical microscopy. Grain size was calculated from optical micrographs for each sample using a 
three-line intercept method. 
Tensile samples were tested on high temperature testing rig at the Southern Research Institute 
located in Birmingham, AL. Samples were placed into a specialized graphite die setup designed 
with a graphite pin system capable of performing tensile tests on samples while heating to 
elevated temperatures under an inert gas flow using radiative heating. A mechanical screw 
driven system was used for load application. A diagram of this setup and schematic drawing of 
tensile samples used for room and high-temperature testing is shown in Figure 2.

Samples were tested at room temperature, 1926 °C (3500 °F), and 2204 °C (4000 °F) using a

and SEM imaging was performed face-on and at an angle of 90° from the fracture surface of the 
samples to evaluate sample failure mode and grain structure change. 
Results and Discussion 
Figure 3 shows SEM images of as-received powders and ball-milled powder blends used for tile 
sintering. Starting Ta powder was found to possess an average particle size of approximately 
177.3 µm, with individual Ta particle sizes ranging from 10’s to 100’s of micrometers in size. 
Ball milling with 10vol% W was found to result in similar Ta particle size but resulted in 
agglomeration of the W powder. This produced W agglomerates on the order of several 
micrometers in length as shown in 3(b). Imaging of the Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC powder 
shown in 3(c) shows substantial agglomeration of W powder with TiC lying on Ta particle 
surfaces.

Optical images of polished and etched as-sintered samples are shown in Figure 5. Ta 100% (4a) 
was found to display an average grain size of approximately 50.1±2.5 µm. This indicates the 
presence of multiple grains per Ta particle. Polishing of the Ta-10vol%W sample (4b) resulted 
in substantial relief due to the large difference in hardness between W and Ta. A two-phase 
microstructure was observed consisting of a fully contiguous tungsten-heavy phase surrounding 
a soft Ta-rich phase. Ta regions are approximately the same size as that of the starting powder 
which suggests that each Ta region arose from one Ta particle during sintering. Etching revealed 
a wide range of grain sizes within the sample, with tantalum grain sizes ranging from several to 
hundreds of micrometers in length. As shown by higher magnification imaging in 4(b), Ta-rich 
grains directly contacting the W region are found to be several orders of magnitude smaller than 
grains in the center of the Ta regions. In addition, smaller Ta regions are found to display a more 
refined grain structure than larger Ta regions. It therefore seems likely that these smaller grains 
are a result of interactions between the Ta and W regions, possibly due to diffusion induced grain 
boundary diffusion from W-Ta interdiffusion2 or solute element interactions. Oxygen is a strong 
potential candidate for this phase formation, as previous work has shown preferential oxygen 
segregation into Ta during sintering in W-Ta alloys 18, and Ta is known to readily form 
supersaturated oxide solutions during sintering19. The microstructure of the Ta-10vol%W alloy 
is similar to the Ta-10vol%W-1.5vl%TiC sample (Figure 4c). Ta islands were found to be 
comparable in size between the two samples, as were the size of grains within each Ta region.

X-ray diffraction was performed on pre-sintered powder mixes and as-sintered alloys with results 
shown in Figure 5. Three principle peaks corresponding to the position of Ta are located as 
noted for both powder and sintered samples. Clear W peaks are observed for powders in W 
containing samples in 5(a). These peaks are elevated in intensity in comparison to the expected 
intensity by atomic percent, likely as tungsten is found on top of Ta powder and is acting to 
block x-rays from penetrating the full Ta surface. In the sintered samples in 5(b) the tungsten 
peaks are found to be drastically reduced in intensity and slightly shifted to lower angles. These 
W peaks are found to overlap with the principle tantalum peaks. This combined with the peak 
shift is indicative of alloying between W and Ta within the sample. Small peaks corresponding 
to Ta2C and TiC are also present in the Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC sample, suggesting some 
reaction of TiC with Ta possibly forming either Ta-Ti alloys or a TiOx phase within the material.

Sample density, Vicker’s hardness, and Young’s modulus as measured by Acoustic impedance 
analysis are shown in Figure 6. As shown, densities for all samples were comparable, with pure 
Ta showing the highest relative density of 98.92%. Ta-10%W was found to show the lowest 
relative density. This is likely a result of the difficulty of sintering tungsten powder due to the 
substantially higher melting point of tungsten in comparison to Ta.

oxygen contamination in the sample. Ta is a substantially stronger oxygen getter than tungsten,

Young’s modulus was found to be 178.12 GPa for the pure Ta sample, which agrees with 
previously reported values. Addition of 10vol%W resulted in an increase to a modulus of 192.39 
GPa. The modulus of W is generally reported as approximately 411 GPa. This increase is 
therefore slightly below that which would be expected from a rule of mixtures evaluation which 
produces a modulus of approximately 200 GPA. The difference in values is likely due to the 
slight decrease in density between samples.

In order to evaluate room temperature mechanical performance, four-point bend testing was 
performed on each alloy. Example stress-strain curves and the extracted ultimate flexural 
strength (UFS) of each alloy are shown in Figure 9. Pure Ta was found to be highly ductile to 
the point that the sample was plastically deformed beyond the limits of the testing apparatus. An 
average peak flexural strength (taken as the UFS) of 354.83 MPa prior to contact with the testing 
apparatus was noted for tested samples. Addition of 10vol%W resulted in a dramatic decrease in 
ductility but increase of UFS to 786.14 MPa. Plastic deformation was noted at elevated stresses 
as seen by deviation from linear-elastic behavior prior to failure. This relatively brittle failure is 
expected due as the ductile to brittle transition temperature (DBTT) of polycrystalline tungsten is 
generally seen to be several hundred degrees higher than room temperature, suggesting failure 
occurred as a result of the brittle tungsten phase. The Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC sample 
displayed the least ductility of all samples but highest UFS of 912.27 MPa. This matches well 
with the previously measured increased hardness of the alloy in comparison to the Ta-10vol%W 
sample. The dramatic increase in room temperature strength of the alloy is surprising and 
suggests possible elevated oxygen was present within the sample.

Values extracted from tensile testing of all alloys are shown in Figure 11. Due to the brittle 
failure of Ta-10vol%W and Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC the 0.2% Y.S. was not calculated for 
these alloys at room temperature. Values determined for the elastic modulus of all three alloys at 
room temperature are in good agreement with those found by acoustic measurement, with the 
notable difference beting a slightly higher modulus for Ta-10vol%W as determined by acoustic 
measurement as opposed to tensile testing. Modulus is found to substantially decrease at 
elevated temperatures for all alloys, with the greatest decrease seen for pure Ta with a nearly 
90% decrease occurring between room temperature and 2204 °C. Alloying with tungsten results 
in a substantially higher modulus of 55 GPa versus 12 GPa for Ta at 1927 °C. Testing at 2204 
°C reveals reduced differences in modulus between materials with only a 50% difference in 
modulus between Ta and Ta-10%W. Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC displayed modulus values 
greater than pure Ta but less than Ta-10vol%W for all temperatures. 
Ta-10vol%W exhibited an increase in yield strength by 470% in comparison to pure tantalum at 
1927 °C. Addition of both 10 vol%W and 1.5vol% TiC produced, a slight decrease from W 
addition alone. The greatest improvements from alloying were seen at 2204 °C. Ta-10vol%W 
tungsten displayed a 150% increase in yield strength over pure tantalum, while Ta-10vol%W-
1.5vol%TiC displayed a nearly 240% increase in yield strength. Substantial improvements from 
alloying were also noted in ultimate tensile strength values of tested samples. Ta-10%W 
displayed over a 50% increase in tensile strength at room temperature and 400% increase in 
ultimate tensile strength at both 1927 and 2204 °C. Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC showed over a 
100% improvement in UTS in comparison to pure Ta at room temperature, essentially doubling 
the value seen for 10vol%W addition, and coinciding well with the increased UFS seen for the 
alloy during bend testing. However, Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC displayed reduced UTS in 
comparison Ta-10vol%W alone at both 1927 and 2204 °C. This suggests that while W addition 
increases material strength at all temperatures, titanium carbide addition is most effective when 
used for lower temperature applications (below 1927 °C).

with the formation of channels most easily visible in the 1927 °C surface shown in 12(b). Top

dramatic elongation of the sample surface, coinciding well with the increased ductility at

Conclusions 
Pure Ta, Ta-10vol%W, and Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC alloys with densities exceeding 98% were 
successful synthesized by FAST. Major conclusions can be summarized as follows: 
 Sintering of Ta with 10 vol%W produced a two-phase microstructure composed of Ta 
rich regions surrounded by a continuous tungsten-rich matrix. 
 Addition of 10 vol%W increased hardness Ta by 98%, while addition of 10 vol%W and 
1.5vol%TiC increased hardness by 140%. 
 Pure Ta was found to display highly ductile behavior at room temperature as tested by 
both bend and tensile testing procedures, while Ta-10vol%W and Ta-10vol%W-
1.5vol%TiC displayed relatively brittle failure. 
 Ta-10vol%W and Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC displayed flexural strength and UTS values 
substantially higher than predicted by simple mixture rule approximations, suggesting 
substantial solid solution strengthening occurred in the samples during testing. 
 Ta-10vol%W and Ta-10vol%W-1.5vol%TiC displayed superior strength and ductility in 
comparison to pure Ta at elevated temperatures.
