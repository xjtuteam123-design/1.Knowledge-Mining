ABSTRACT 
The high temperature plasticity of AlCoCrFeMo
0.05
Ni
2 
high entropy alloy (HEA) is significantly enhanced after 
annealing. However, the specific mechanisms are not fully understood. In this study, a polycrystalline HEA model 
with dimensions of 18 nm × 18 nm × 26 nm was used to analyze stress mapping under uniaxial tension through 
Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS). Complemented by experimental results, 
one can see that the decomposition of the FCC phase promotes the formation of nanoscale deformation twins. 
The formation of deformation twinning is most likely one of the primary reasons for the substantial improvement 
in high temperature plasticity. The findings provide valuable insights for controlling the high temperature 
performance of the alloy. 
1. Introduction 
High entropy alloys have surpassed the compositional limits of 
traditional alloys and have shown great potential for high-performance 
applications [1 – 3] . The AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 
HEA is widely recognized for its 
exceptional toughness and strength, which have been extensively 
investigated [4 – 6] . However, its high-temperature strength is not 
significantly outstanding. To enhance its high-temperature strength, our 
previous studies have focused on the incorporation of Mo, Nb, Hf, C 
elements, resulting in the development of the AlCoCrFeMo
0.05
Ni
2 
and 
AlCoCrFeNi
1.9
(Mo, Nb, Hf, C) HEA [7 – 10] . These alloys demonstrates 
improved high-temperature performance, particularly evident in the 
substantial enhancement of high-temperature plasticity after annealing 
at 973 K [9] . Nevertheless, the microstructural mechanisms underlying 
these improvements are still not fully understood. In this study, mo -
lecular dynamics simulations and experimental analysis have revealed 
that annealing-induced decomposition of the FCC phase facilitates the 
emergence of nanoscale deformation twins during the deformation 
process. The nanoscale deformation twins can serve as abundant stress 
relief paths during plastic deformation [11,12] . Therefore, the forma -
tion of nanoscale deformation twins are most likely to be one of the main 
contributing factors to the significant improvement in high-temperature 
plasticity of the alloy. This work provide valuable insights for further 
exploration and optimization of the high-temperature performance of 
the HEAs. 
2. Simulation methods 
In this study, Thermo-Calc software and TCHEA4.2 database were 
utilized for composition calculations in both the as-cast and annealed 
alloys. They have been reliably validated in numerous previous re -
searches [13 – 15] . The Sheil solidification model was employed to 
determine the composition of the as-cast alloy. For the annealed alloy, 
equilibrium phase diagrams were utilized. LAMMPS software was 
employed to simulate the uniaxial tensile behavior [16] . For the uniaxial 
tensile simulations, a polycrystalline simulation cell with dimensions of 
18 nm × 18 nm × 26 nm was used. The cell composed of four random 
grains. Periodic boundary conditions were applied along the x- and z- 
axes. The model underwent energy minimization using the conjugate 
gradient algorithm. Subsequently, a relaxation simulation of 50 ps was 
conducted at a temperature of 973 K. After reaching thermal equilib -
rium, uniaxial tensile simulations were performed along the y-axis while

Fig. 2 presents the results of uniaxial tensile simulation. Fig. 2 a 
shows the stress-strain curves for both the as-cast and annealed alloys, 
one can see that both alloys undergo elastic and plastic deformation 
stages. The as-cast alloy exhibits higher elastic limit, yield strength, and 
elastic modulus compared to the annealed alloy, which is consistent 
with experimental results [9] . Additionally, the as-cast alloy shows a 
pronounced yield drop, indicating significant localized deformation. 
This suggests that the as-cast alloy is more prone to necking and exhibits 
lower ductility. Similarly, a more pronounced fluctuation in the yield 
plateau stage usually indicates limited plastic deformation ability and 
the occurrence of stress concentration in the material. Thus, it can be 
inferred that the annealed alloy has higher elongation compared to the 
as-cast alloy. 
Fig. 2 b-d depict the stress distribution contour maps of the as-cast 
and annealed alloys before and after uniaxial tensile simulation. In 
Fig. 2 b, one can see that the initial stresses are almost zero. Fig. 2 c shows 
higher stress at grain boundaries, primarily attributed to severe distor -
tion during the stretching process. Additionally, layer-like high stress 
regions form within the grains, providing conditions for the formation of 
dislocations and twins. Fig. 2 d reveals higher stress in the phase in -
terfaces, it is primarily attributed to the inhibiting effect of the hard BCC 
phase on dislocation motion. Layer-like stress regions also appear within 
the FCC phase between the BCC phases. Comparing the size of the layer- 
like stress regions in the FCC phase in Fig. 2 c and Fig. 2 d, one can see 
that the annealed alloy exhibits smaller layer-like stress regions. More -
over, the actual grain size of the as-cast alloy is much larger than that 
illustrated in Fig. 2 c due to the limitations of the model size. Conse -
quently, the size of dislocations or twins in the annealed alloy will be 
significantly refined compared to the as-cast alloy.
