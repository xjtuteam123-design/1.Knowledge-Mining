Abstract 
The age-hardening behavior and mechanical properties of the extruded Mg-2Gd-1.2Y-0.5 Zn (at.%) alloy with Zr or Mn additions were 
investigated. The results show that Mn added alloy exhibits more remarkable age-hardening response than Zr added alloy, which is attributed 
to the fact that partitioning of Mn into the β phases, β′ precipitates and long period stacking ordered (LPSO) phases leads to the decrement 
in (Gd + Y) concentrations in the second phases, facilitating the precipitation during aging treatment. Both peak-aged alloys show bimodal 
microstructure comprising the ﬁne DRXed grains with nano-sized β phases pinned at DRXed grain boundaries as well as coarse worked 
grains with strong ﬁber texture. High strength and good thermal stability were obtained in both peak-aged Mn and Zr added alloys. While 
the peak-aged Mn added alloy shows higher strength and superior creep resistance due to its denser β′ precipitates, thin LPSO phases and 
γ′ precipitates and higher area fraction of worked grains with strong ﬁber texture. The 0.2% tensile proof stress and ultimate tensile strength 
of peak-aged Mn added alloy reach up to 454 MPa and 508 MPa, respectively, with elongation of 3.2% at room temperature. The minimum 
creep rate of the peak-aged Mn added alloy at 250 °C / 150 MPa is 2.4 × 10 −8 s −1 , which is superior than previously reported extruded 
Mg-Gd based alloys.

low density, whereas their low strength limit their commer- 
cial applications [1–5] . Rare earth (RE) containing Mg al- 
loys, especially Mg–Gd–Y based alloys have been paid con- 
siderable attentions as light-weight structural materials due to 
their excellent mechanical properties at both room and ele- 
vated temperatures [6–8] . The RE elements such as Gd and 
Y have high solid solubility in α-Mg matrix which decreases

high strength is attributed to the ﬁne dynamic recrystallized

atoms and hinders the formation of 14H LPSO phase [22,23] . 
It is well known that the Fe-contained impurities in the Mg 
alloys deteriorate the mechanical properties. Recently, Chen 
and Pan et al. in Chongqing University have developed a 
novel melt self-purifying method for Mg alloys based on a 
varying-temperature melt treatment [24–26] . The impurity Fe 
content of Mg alloys was signiﬁcantly reduced to as low as 
5 ppm by using this puriﬁcation method [25] . The low-cost 
technology has already achieved large-scale industrial appli- 
cations in China. Besides, it is reported that Mn addition to 
Mg alloys can to some extent transforms the Fe-contained 
impurities into relative harmless compounds so as to improve 
corrosion resistance of Mg alloys [27] , and improves the me- 
chanical properties by reﬁning the grain size of the extruded 
Mg alloys [28,29] . Accordingly, Mn is a potential competent 
alloying element for Mg-RE-Zn alloys. Mn containing Mg–
Gd–Sc and Mg–Y–Sc alloys show high strength and creep re- 
sistance up to 350 °C due to the precipitation of Mn–Gd and 
Mn–Y containing basal precipitates during aging treatment 
[30] . Furthermore, the Mn addition enhances the formation of 
LPSO phases in the solution treated Mg–Gd–Zn alloy [31] . 
The variation of the volume fractions of the LPSO phases 
should inﬂuences the precipitation of the β′ phase, thereby 
affecting the age-hardening response and mechanical proper- 
ties. However, unfortunately, the effect of substituting Mn for 
Zr addition on the age-hardening behavior and mechanical 
properties, especially the creep resistance, of the Mg–Gd–Y–
Zn–Zr alloy has rarely been reported. 
In this study, trace Mn or Zr elements were added to Mg–
Gd–Y–Zn alloy then processed by hot extrusion and aging 
treatment. The microstructure evolution of the peak-aged Mg–
Gd–Y–Zn alloy with Zr or Mn addition was analyzed. The 
age-hardening behavior, tensile properties as well as creep 
resistance at elevated temperatures of the peak-aged alloys 
were investigated. 
2. Experimental procedures 
Mg-2.0Gd-1.2Y-0.5Zn-0.15Mn and Mg-2.0Gd-1.2Y-0.5Zn- 
0.15Zr(at.%) alloy billets with 50 mm in diameter and 250 mm 
in length were fabricated by permanent mould casting. The 
billets were cut and then homogenized at 510 °C for 8 h in a 
Pyrex tube under an Ar atmosphere followed by immediate 
warm water quenching at ∼80 °C. The homogenized samples 
were machined to cylindrical samples with 43 mm in diameter 
and 37 mm in height for extrusion then preheated at 400 °C 
for 5 min prior to the extrusion for homogenizing the tem- 
perature of the samples. Subsequently, indirect extrusion was 
performed at 400 °C with an extrusion ratio of 10:1 and a 
ram speed of 0.1 mm/s. Finally, extruded rods with 13.6 mm 
in diameter and 270 mm in length were produced followed by 
aging treatment at 225 °C for different periods. The peak-aged 
alloys with Zr and Mn additions are denoted as PAZr400 and 
PAMn400, respectively. 
JEOL JSM-7000F ﬁeld-emission scanning electron micro- 
scope (FE-SEM) was used to observe the microstructure of 
the alloys. The samples were sectioned and ground using 
emery papers up to #4000 then mechanical polished and fol- 
lowed by etching in the picric acid. The texture was exam- 
ined by an EDAX-TSL EBSD system operating at 25 kV , and 
the data were analyzed by OIM Analysis software. Thin foils 
for transmission electron microscope (TEM) observation with 
thickness of 0.2 mm were punched into discs with 3 mm in 
diameter and mechanically polished followed by low angle 
ion milling using Gatan precision ion polishing system. TEM 
observations were carried out on a JEOL JEM-2100F operat- 
ing at 200 kV and elemental analysis was performed by the 
Gatan Imaging Filter (GIF) Tridium equipped on the JEOL 
JEM-2100F. The number densities of the precipitates were 
measured by convergent beam electron diffraction (CBED) 
technique on TEM. Firstly, the thicknesses of three observed 
positions were determined accurately by two-beam CBED 
[32] . Based on the scale bar on the TEM bright-ﬁeld image, 
we can calculate the area of the image. Then we counted 
the precipitates in the TEM-BF image taken from these posi- 
tions so that we can get the number density by the following 
equation: 
N = n × 1 
A × t 
where N is the number density of the precipitates, n is the 
number of the precipitates we counted in the obtained BF 
image, A is the area of the BF image and t is the thickness 
of the position we taken BF image calculated based on CBED. 
Hardness was measured by the VMT-7S Vickers hardness 
testing machine with the load of 5 kgf and a loading time 
of 15 s. The specimens for tensile test having a gauge length 
of 30 mm and a diameter of 6 mm and specimens for tensile 
creep test with a gauge length of 50 mm and cross-sectional 
dimension of 10 × 5 mm 2 were cut from the peak-aged al- 
loys. The tensile direction was parallel to the extrusion direc- 
tion (ED). The tensile tests were conducted on a Shimadzu 
Autograph AG-I (50 kN) machine at an initial strain rate of 
1 × 10 −3 s −1 at room temperature (RT), 200 °C and 250 °C, 
respectively. While the tensile creep test was performed using 
an A&D CP-6L-500 machine at temperature of 250 °C and 
an applied stress of 89 MPa and 150 MPa. The temperature 
was maintained at ±2 °C.

Fig. 2 shows the SEM micrographs of the peak-aged al- 
loys observed on the transverse and longitudinal sections. 
Both alloys exhibit typical bimodal microstructures observed 
in extruded Mg alloys comprising ﬁne equiaxed dynami-

grain boundaries in both PAZr400 and PAMn400 alloys, as

The area fractions of the LPSO phases in both PAZr400 and 
PAMn400 alloys were evaluated based on three SEM mi-

3.3. Texture analysis of peak-aged alloys 
EBSD analysis was carried out on the transverse sections 
of the peak-aged alloys to evaluate the texture development. 
The inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) maps presented in Fig. 7 show 
that both peak-aged alloys consist of ﬁne DRXed grains with 
relatively random orientations as well as coarse worked grains 
with basal planes preferentially oriented parallel to ED. The 
average grain sizes of DRXed grains are 1.3 μm and 1.5 μm 
for PAZr400 and PAMn400, respectively and thus no obvious 
grain coarsening occurs during the aging treatment at 225 °C 
(grain sizes for the as-extruded alloys with addition of Mn and 
Zr are 1.4 μm and 1.1 μm, respectively), which results from 
the effective pinning by the dynamically precipitated β phases 
at the DRXed grain boundaries. Moreover, in accordance with 
the SEM micrographs given in Fig. 2 that Mn substitution for 
Zr addition signiﬁcantly reduces the area fraction of DRXed 
grains. Fig. 8 shows the corresponding (0001) and ( 10 ¯1 0) pole 
ﬁgures calculated from the whole regions, DRXed regions and 
worked grains of the peak-aged alloys given in Fig. 7 . Both 
peak-aged alloys exhibit a typical ﬁber texture of extruded Mg 
alloys with < 10 ¯1 0 > //ED [20,21] , that is, the ⟨ 10 ¯1 0⟩ axes of 
the grains parallel to ED. Additionally, the intensities of the 
texture are much weaker in the DRXed regions than those in 
the worked grains. As a result, the PAMn400 shows stronger 
basal texture than PAZr400 due to its lower area fraction of 
DRXed grains with weak ﬁber texture. 
3.4. Mechanical properties of peak-aged alloys 
Fig. 9 shows the tensile properties of the peak-aged alloys 
tested at RT, 100 °C and 250 °C. It can be seen that both 
peak-aged alloys exhibit high tensile strength after peak-aging 
treatment. In comparison with PAZr400, PAMn400 has higher 
0.2% tensile proof stress (TPS) and ultimate tensile strength 
(UTS) at RT with the values of 454 MPa and 508 MPa, re- 
spectively, but lower elongation to failure of 3.2%. The corre- 
sponding discussion on the strengthening mechanisms of the 
peak-aged alloys is given as follows. 
Firstly, it is well known that the solution strengthening 
originates from the elastic interaction between the strain ﬁeld 
of solute atoms and dislocations. Since the Gd and Y have 
larger atomic radius while Zn has a smaller one than that of 
Mg, their saturation in α-Mg matrix results in lattice distortion 
then impede the movement of dislocations [44] . Additionally, 
it is reported that the Gd solute clustering was observed in the 
α-Mg matrix of Mg-0.06Gd (at.%) dilute alloy, which pos- 
sibly acts as an obstacle to the motion of dislocations [45] . 
Furthermore, for Mg–Gd–Zn alloys, Gd and Zn atoms were 
reported to form Gd–Zn dimmers in the α-Mg solid solution 
restricting the mobility of dislocations more effectively than 
those from individual atoms of Gd or Zn [12] . Y and Zn 
atoms can also form dimmers due to the similar atomic ra- 
dius as well as identical mixing enthalpies between Gd–Zn 
and Y–Zn [43] . The equilibrium solid solubility of both Gd 
and Y in α-Mg at 200 °C is ∼0.6 at.% [46] so that, in present 
study, solution strengthening can be expected in both Mn and 
Zr added alloys peak-aged at higher temperature of 225 °C. 
As stated in Section 3.2 that if Mn single addition reduces 
the solid solubility of Gd and Y in α-Mg matrix, Mn sub- 
stitution for Zr addition is supposed to weaken the solution 
strengthening effect of the peak-aged alloy.

Furthermore, ﬁne DRXed grains obtained by hot extrusion

between these phases and dislocations [49] . However, the 
TPS of the PAZr400 and PAMn400 show inverse proportion 
to the area fractions of DRXed regions in these two alloys. 
Fig. 7 shows that ﬁne DRXed grains have almost random

texture with basal planes parallel to ED for both alloys. 
It is known that the basal dislocation slip is the dominant 
deformation mode of Mg alloys, the (0001) < 11 ¯2 0 > Schmid 
factor distribution maps ( Fig. 7 c and d) suggest that Schmid 
factor for basal slip is higher in the DRXed grains than that 
in the worked grains, therefore the basal dislocations are 
readily operative in the ﬁne DRXed grains while difﬁcult to 
be activated in these worked grains when tensile tested along 
ED. As a result, the ﬁne DRXed grains are likely to improve 
the ductility while the elongated worked grains contribute 
to the strength of the alloy [50] . Accordingly, higher area 
fraction of worked grains in PAMn400 accounts for its higher 
strength than PAZr400 in terms of texture strengthening. 
As shown in Figs. 5 and 6 , dense β′ precipitates on the 
prismatic planes along c-axis form during aging treatment 
in both alloys, which are expected to be effective obstacles 
for the motion of basal dislocations and play a key role in 
improving the strength of both peak-aged alloys. However, 
in worked grains, non-basal dislocations, especially prismatic 
⟨ a ⟩ and pyramidal ⟨ c + a ⟩ dislocations may be preferentially 
activated rather than the basal dislocations to accommodate 
the stress during tensile test due to the strong basal texture 
[51] . As a consequence, the β′ precipitates along c-axis of 
the worked grains cannot impede non-basal slip of disloca- 
tions effectively. Fornasini and Manfrinetti [37] investigated 
the strengthening and toughening mechanisms in Mg-Zn-Y 
alloy and found that non-basal dislocations bounded between 
two adjacent γ′ precipitates in Mg-Zn-Y alloy, and γ′ pre- 
cipitates are not sheared along c axis of α-Mg, attributing 
to the fact that basal γ′ precipitates effectively hinder the 
glide of dislocations along prismatic and pyramidal directions. 
Jono et al. [52] also reported that the formation of thin LPSO 
phases and profuse γ′ precipitates from supersaturated α-Mg 
matrix prevent ⟨ c ⟩ component slip effectively, which is desir- 
able for enhancing creep properties. Therefore, in this study, 
the thin LPSO phases and γ′ precipitates on the basal planes 
precipitated in both worked and DRXed grains should effec- 
tively impede the mobility of non-basal dislocations. Since 
Mn substitution for Zr addition enhances the number density 
of the both β′ precipitates, thin LPSO phases and γ′ precip- 
itates, PAMn400 possess greater precipitation strengthening 
effect than PAZr400.

4. Conclusions 
In this study, the effect of substitution of Mn for Zr addi- 
tion on the age-hardening behavior and mechanical properties 
of peak-aged Mg-2.0Gd-1.2Y-0.5 Zn alloy was investigated, 
we can conclude from the results as follows: 
1. In comparison with Zr addition, Mn added alloy exhibits 
more signiﬁcant age-hardening response. Although both 
Mn and Zr atoms partition into the grain boundary β
phases, β′ precipitates and LPSO phases, the Mn parti- 
tioning reduces the (Gd + Y) concentrations in the second 
phases more signiﬁcantly, and then facilitates the aging 
precipitation. As a result, Mn addition results in higher 
number density of β′ precipitates in Mn added alloy than 
that in Zr added alloy. 
2. Both peak-aged Mn and Zr added alloys show bimodal 
microstructure consisting of ﬁne dynamic recrystallized 
(DRXed) grains with random orientations and ﬁne β
phases pinned at grain boundaries as well as coarse worked 
grains with strong basal texture. DRX ratio is higher in Zr 
added alloy than in Mn added alloy. 
3. In comparison with Zr added alloy, Mn added alloy shows 
higher strength at both room and elevated temperatures and 
exhibits superior creep resistance at 250 °C. The 0.2% ten- 
sile proof stress and ultimate tensile strength of peak-aged 
Mn added alloy at room temperature reach 454 MPa and 
508 MPa, respectively, with elongation to failure of 3.2%. 
The minimum creep rate of the peak-aged Mn added alloy 
at 250 °C / 150 MPa is 2.4 × 10 −8 s −1 , which is supe- 
rior than previously reported extruded Mg–Gd based al- 
loys. The higher strength and superior creep resistance are 
mainly due to the precipitation of higher number density 
of β′ precipitates, thin LPSO phases and γ′ precipitates as 
well as higher area fraction of worked grains with strong 
ﬁber texture.
