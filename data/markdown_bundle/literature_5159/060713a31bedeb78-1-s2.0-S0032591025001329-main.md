ABSTRACT
Elevated temperatures coupled with dynamic loads present formidable challenges in advanced applications. 
Using advanced powder metallurgy (PM), CoCrNi, an FCC alloy, was crafted with ultrafine-grained micro -
structure devoid of any second phase, oxides, or precipitates. Despite the ultrafine microstructure, the material 
retains its work hardening at room and elevated temperatures. The yield strength of PM alloy shows a 300 % 
elevation compared to cast alloy at room temperature. Microstructural analysis revealed that mechanical per -
formance stems from the synergistic effect of refined microstructure and nano-domain defects, including stacking 
faults, dislocations, twining, and nano-grains in raw materials. During dynamic deformation, multivariant twins 
evolved to contribute towards strengthening and work hardening. Saliently, the alloy exhibits remarkable high- 
temperature strength and resilience without softening or fracturing. The presence of nano-twins at high tem -
peratures is one of the reasons for the thermal stability of material at elevated temperatures.

1. Introduction
The imperative for materials displaying superior strength at elevated 
temperatures, surpassing the capabilities of conventional heat-resistant 
materials, is pivotal for the progress of future aerospace, nuclear, and 
energy applications. Over the past two decades, research has been 
devoted to multicomponent material systems (MCM), colloquially 
known as high and medium entropy alloys (HEAs and MEAs). Re -
searchers widely recognize MCM as promising structural materials due 
to their outstanding mechanical properties observed at ambient and 
cryogenic temperatures. Exampling here are famous cantor alloy [ 1 ] and 
its subset CoCrNi [ 2 ]. CoCrNi alloys have garnered significant attention 
due to their exceptional mechanical properties, such as remarkable 
fracture toughness [ 3 ] and outshining ductility [ 4 ] particularly at 
cryogenic temperatures. The outstanding mechanical performance of 
the CoCrNi alloy can be attributed primarily to its lower stacking fault 
energy, a characteristic that is further diminished under cryogenic 
conditions. This reduction in stacking fault energy significantly con -
tributes towards introducing abundant twins in the material, thereby 
rendering it particularly well-suited for applications where superior 
strength and resilience are vitally important, especially in environments 
characterized by cryogenic temperatures [ 5 ].
Echoing the demands of cryogenic temperature applications, high- 
temperature scenarios also mandate materials with specialized attri -
butes for strength and plasticity. It is well known that plastic deforma -
tion is a thermally activated process that is invariantly affected by 
temperature and strain rate. The flow stress and yield strength exhibit a 
decreasing trend with an increase in ambient temperature, a common 
observation across various well-known engineering materials such as 
copper [ 6 ], steel [ 7 ], super alloys [ 8 ] and multicomponent materials [ 9 ]. 
Superalloys are renowned as temperature-resistant materials due to 
their inherently higher initial yield strengths, which remain compatible 
with the required strengths at elevated temperatures. MCM are special 
cases since their yield strength is quite low, which proves inadequate for 
high-temperature applications. Meeting the demands of elevated tem -
perature applications necessitates the production of ultrafine-grained 
materials possessing both high strength and stable microstructures. 
MCM are commonly produced by melting and casting that yields coarse- 
grained microstructures. The presence of coarse grains hampers the al -
loy’s overall strengthening characteristics with lower strength. 
Furthermore, MCM produced through induction melting methods face 
processing challenges associated with the high temperatures necessary 
for melting ingots. Additionally, the requirement for multiple melting 
cycles to achieve a uniform composition and minimize elemental 
segregation further complicates the process [ 10 ].
In response to the identified challenge of low strength related to 
casting, many processing methods have emerged and are currently being 
employed to fabricate robust MCM [ 2 , 11 ]. The simplest methods of 
increasing strength rely on a straightforward approach, i.e., refining the 
microstructure. Prevalent approaches for grain refinement often center 
around severe plastic deformation techniques [ 12 ], including high- 
pressure torsion [ 13 ], equal channel angular extrusion [ 14 ], and 
intensive cold rolling [ 15 ]. The SPD methods used for most MCM 
capitalize on these material’s low stacking fault energy (SFE). This low 
SFE favors the formation of deformation twins during intense defor -
mation [ 16 ]. Incorporating twins within coarse grains results in the 
formation of increased interfaces, which act as conventional grain 
boundaries that impede dislocation motion [ 17 ] and increase the ma -
terial’s strength in a classical Hall-Petch way [ 18 , 19 ].
While severe plastic deformation (SPD) methods are well-established 
for enhancing strength, they are often accompanied by a trade-off in 
plasticity and ductility in the material. This compromise arises from the 
intense lattice distortion induced by SPD, which can limit the material’s 
ability to deform plastically and exhibit ductile behavior. To improve 
ductility and plasticity, SPD methods are always followed by thermal 
treatments that can again diminish the strength of materials. The second 
limitation of cold-worked materials is inherent to their unstable 
microstructure at high temperatures. When used at high temperatures 
under severe loading conditions, the highly cold-worked materials are 
prone to instantaneous recrystallization and excessive grain growth. 
This instability limits the applicability of SPD materials for high- 
temperature applications.
To overcome the limitations associated with severe plastic defor -
mation (SPD) methods and to fabricate ultrafine-grained materials with 
minimal intrinsic defects while achieving an optimal combination of 
properties such as increased strength and plasticity, novel processing 
methods are under exploration for high and medium entropy alloys. One 
such method is powder metallurgy (PM), which has evolved as a po -
tential method for fabricating ultrafine-grained materials with superior 
mechanical performance [ 20 , 21 ].
Powder metallurgy utilizes high-energy ball milling (mechanical 
alloying, MA) followed by spark plasma sintering (SPS). Mechanical 
alloying is inherently a stochastic process characterized by a multitude 
of variables. These variables encompass the mill and milling media type, 
ball-to-powder weight ratio, milling time, milling temperature, grinding 
medium velocity, impact frequency, energy transfer efficiency, milling 
atmosphere, powder purity and the nature and quantity of the process 
control agent (PCA). Each of these factors significantly influences the 
characteristics of the milled powder. Mechanical alloying facilitates the 
fracturing and welding of powders, while SPS helps to consolidate the 
powders into densified compacts [ 22 ] in a very short time, hence 
enabling rapid production rates. The shorter heating and holding times 
restrict the grain growth [ 23 ] and facilitate the production of very fine 
and nano-grain materials [ 24 , 25 ]. Pertaining to its salient characteris -
tics, such as a very short production cycle and better final product 
properties, the powder metallurgy method has matured over time, and 
numerous metal-based alloys [ 26 ] ceramics and composites [ 27 ] and 
most recently, HEAs [ 28 ] have been successfully produced using PM.
While powder metallurgy (PM) offers distinct advantages, two sig -
nificant factors may impact the final product quality and processability 
of HEAs by PM. These factors encompass material variables and process 
parameters, specifically those related to milling and sintering processes. 
For example, powder contamination originating from grinding balls and 
milling vials stands out as a primary concern for materials produced 
through mechanical alloying [ 29 ]. Likewise, contamination stemming 
from the surrounding atmosphere during the milling process presents 
another challenge in maintaining the quality of the final product. For 
example, CoCrNi powder fabricated through mechanical alloying 
revealed the formation of numerous oxide phases in the microstructure 
and the presence of iron particles. The occurrence of contamination was 
attributed to the interaction of the powder with air and milling media 
during the ball milling process [ 30 ]. The contamination from the at -
mosphere can be minimized and dealt with using vacuum and/or inert 
gas injection in vials. However, the inevitability of contamination from 
milling media remains a challenge to address.
High strain rate and impact-related applications are prevalent in the 
aerospace, automobile, and defense industries. The impact resistance of 
a material is directly associated with high strength, whereas good 
ductility /plasticity plays a crucial role in energy dissipation [ 31 ]. 
Generally, BCC metals and alloys have superior strengths but offer 
limited ductility, diminishing their scope in energy absorption applica -
tion. On the other hand, metals or alloys with an FCC phase demonstrate 
exceptional ductility but comparatively low strength, specifically at high 
temperatures. While refined and nano-domain microstructures 
contribute to enhanced strength, a notable drawback of grain refinement 
is the diminished work-hardening capacity, reduced plasticity and 
increased susceptibility to microstructural instability at elevated tem -
peratures. These limitations render structures susceptible to premature 
failures. The loss of work-hardening and plasticity is related to the 
saturation of dislocation densities in refined grains [ 32 ], especially 
under high loading rates. Therefore, it is imperative to develop ultra- 
fine-grained/nano-grained materials with extraordinary strength,

The primary objective of this study is twofold. Firstly, it seeks to 
fabricate a material with a fine-grained FCC crystal structure utilizing 
advanced powder metallurgy techniques while meticulously maintain -
ing contamination-free processing conditions. Secondly, this study aims 
to investigate the dynamic response of the fabricated PM material at 
both ambient and elevated temperatures. This is particularly significant 
as the dynamic response of HEAs is scarce [ 33 ] and remains unclear in 
the existing body of knowledge; specifically, no reports comprehend the 
coupled effect of elevated temperatures and dynamic loading for PM 
CoCrNi alloy.
In light of these considerations, we engineered a medium entropy 
alloy using a combination of high-energy ball milling and spark plasma 
sintering. The milling operation utilized lightweight ceramic balls in 
place of traditional metallic balls. The subsequent sintering process 
yielded a pallet characterized by a homogeneous single-phase micro -
structure, devoid of contaminants introduced through either atmo -
spheric exposure or the milling procedures. Moreover, the material 
displayed exceptional mechanical performance compared to its cast 
counterpart, particularly under high strain rate loading conditions. 
Unlike many conventional fine-grained materials that tend to lose their 
work-hardening capability with grain refinement, the PM alloy in the 
current study exhibited outstanding work-hardening properties. More -
over, the alloy in the current investigation showcased exceptional 
elevated temperature dynamic properties comparable to well-known Ni- 
based super alloys and many refractory high entropy alloys. The careful 
selection of milling media, milling duration, and sintering parameters 
emerged as pivotal factors contributing to the material ’ s optimized 
microstructure and enhanced mechanical properties.
2. Material processing and characterization methods
Ni, Cr, and Co powders with a purity of at least 99.5 % and equal 
atomic proportions were used as raw materials for casting and ball 
milling. Cast samples were prepared by vacuum induction levitation 
furnace. For PM material, the powders were first ball milled in a high- 
energy ball mill using steel vials and alumina balls (diameter 2 mm 
and 3 mm) as milling media. The powders and balls (1:2) mixture were 
sealed in the steel vials and filled with argon gas. Mechanical milling 
was carried out in a planetary ball mill for an effective milling time of 10 
h. The rotational speed during milling was fixed at 300 r/min. A 5-min 
cold down interval was implemented every hour throughout the ball 
milling process to prevent abnormal temperature rise in the vial. After 
milling, the powders were transferred to a graphite die with a diameter 
of 50 mm and heated up to 1000 
◦
C under the constant pressure of 40 
MPa using a spark plasma sintering machine. After completion of the 
heating cycle, a final holding time of 15 min was used at constant 
pressure. Finally, the compact product was furnace-cooled to room 
temperature under vacuum. The sintered product was further annealed 
in vacuum at a temperature of 1000 
◦
C for 12 h in a glass tube furnace. 
For the convenience of reading, the sintered-annealed alloy is named as 
“ SA ” in the following sections. The density of the solid samples were 
determined using a Mettler Toledo analytical balance equipped with a 
density determination kit. Afterwards the bulk density is calculated and 
porosity was thereby calculated from the ratio of bulk density to true 
density. Besides, the powder and sintered products have been analyzed 
for C and O contents using LECO-CS-844 and LECO-ONH-836 analyzers, 
respectively.

with a discernible distinction observed in the average values for both

longer melt durations. Melting and casting of powders necessitate tem -

prominent difference is the appearance of intragrain deformation gra -

The three aspects of SA material revealed in microstructure can be 
explained by considering the theoretical background of milling and 
sintering operations. The refined nano-domain grains, after sintering, 
are rooted in the accelerated kinetics of the sintering process in milled 
powder. The high-energy ball milling/mechanical alloying (MA) is 
synchronous to a severe plastic deformation process being carried out at 
high loading rates. This severe deformation process serves two purposes. 
The fracturing of powder by MA generates new high-energy surfaces and 
induces strains within the powder. Secondly, the MA can induce abun -
dant cold work energy in the milled powders in the form of dislocations 
and SF /deformation twins [ 38 ]. When such a high-energy powder is 
heated during sintering, localized heating by joule phenomena occurs 
[ 39 ], resulting in localized melting, recrystallization, and neck forma -
tion among particles. Concurrently, the microstructure evolves at pow -
der surfaces during sintering. It is well known that recrystallization 
occurs preferentially in energetically favorable sites. The recrystalliza -
tion (RCZ) results in the nucleation of stress-free grains in most regions 
by consuming cold work energy. However, due to inhomogeneous 
deformation levels within the milled powder, the level of recrystalliza -
tion is varied. The subsequent annealing after sintering further stimu -
lates the RCZ process, and grains with definite grain boundaries and 
varying sizes are formed ( Fig. 3 b, Fig. 4 d). As seen ( Fig. 4 d), recrystal -
lized nano-grains (marked as DRX grains) are mostly present along pre- 
existing grain boundaries (GB). A plausible reason for this is the stress 
state of a grain boundary (GB). GB is a highly stressed region in the 
matrix, and dynamic recrystallization can occur heterogeneously or 
homogeneously along the pre-existing GBs in a deformed material.
The formation of deformation twins/SF can be explained based on 
stacking fault energy. It is known that materials with low stacking fault 
energy offer more favorable twin nucleation conditions under a high 
strain rate [ 40 ]. As is well known, CoCrNi has a very low SFE in the 
range of 18 ± 4 or 22 ± 4 mJ ⋅ m

[ 41 , 42 ]. Therefore, deformed twins/ 
SFs forming during high-energy ball milling is energetically favorable in 
CoCrNi. In fact, the low SFE favors the dissociation of perfect disloca -
tions into partial dislocations. In refined-grained materials, the move -
ment of partial dislocations is more feasible than perfect dislocations 
[ 43 ]. The dislocation movement partially leaves behind an SF ( Fig. 4 e). 
The lattice distortion by DT/SF results in high energy sites within the 
milled material. These sites are potential regions for recrystallization 
during subsequent sintering and annealing operations. When the milled 
powder is sintered at high temperatures, most of the grains grow in size 
along with the formation of coherent type recrystallized annealing twin 
boundaries ( Fig. 4 c). It is worth mentioning that twin boundaries have 
lower energy than conventional grain boundaries [ 44 , 45 ] and are more 
stable at high temperatures. Therefore, some twins that appear even 
after sintering or annealing may still be deformation twins formed 
during the milling process. This is because nano-twins are stable at 
elevated temperatures and do not disappear [ 46 ].

a higher pace. These dislocations then encounter more frequent obsta -
cles by increased interfaces in fine-grained materials. Due to shorter 
durations under dynamic conditions, the thermal energy effect mini -
mizes; hence, dislocations cannot overcome barriers. Also, the phonon 
drag effect comes into play at high strain rates that affect the mobility of 
dislocations. A higher phonon drag effect always accounts for the 
elevation of yield stress since higher stress is required to cause disloca -
tion mobile for uniform plasticity. Therefore, the flow stress experiences 
an instantaneous and significant increase in UFG materials during a rise 
in strain rate, consequently leading to markedly higher values of SRS.
Another prominent feature is the outstanding work-hardening (WH) 
trend in the SA material, as shown in Fig. 5 c. The work-hardening rate 
for SA is even higher at 5000/s than its cast counterpart at the start of 
deformation. An increase in yield and flow stress while transitioning 
from low to high strain rates is a generic trend seen for many fine- 
grained conventional metals and alloys, such as aluminum alloys and 
ultrafine-grained copper. Simultaneously, work hardening loss is also a 
common phenomenon during grain refinement. Generally, materials 
with fine grains lose their work-hardening ability due to multiple 
mechanisms, including early saturation of dislocation densities [ 50 ] and 
coarsening of dislocation structure. Even under dynamic conditions, 
these materials sometimes show strain softening due to the dominance 
of dynamic recovery or recrystallization [ 51 ]. The increased grain 
boundary area, and hence amplified stress regions, serves as a stimu -
lating factor for elevated recrystallization kinetics. Another factor is the

Generally high temperatures cause prominent strength degradation 
due to factors associated with grain growth, grain boundary diffusion 
and dynamic recovery. Besides, initiation of mechanical twinning re -

deformation temperature delays the onset of twinning due to lowering of 
flow stress and increase in SFE [ 52 ]. The facilitating dislocation anni -
hilation through climb mechanisms at high temperatures also adversely 
affects the work hardening effect. At elevated temperatures, the barrier 
effect of grain boundaries on dislocation motion diminishes due to 
increased thermal activation, enabling dislocations to overcome the 
obstacles posed by grain boundaries. However, the mechanical response 
of materials at elevated temperatures ( Fig. 5 d) points that plasticity and 
strengthening mechanisms still remain active which otherwise are 
overcome by rise in temperature. These mechanisms include the for -
mation of twin/ SFs, retardation of grain coarsening due to sluggish 
diffusion effect of high entropy alloys and balance between dislocation 
generation (due to high strain rate) and annihilation. These deformation 
mechanisms and related microstructural evolution might be responsible 
for the mechanical attributes shown by SA. These experiment findings 
suggest a potential application of sintered PM multicomponent alloys in 
ballistic impact, crash, and armor-piercing areas. The following section 
elaborates on the potential factors and detailed mechanisms for 
strengthening the alloys investigated.

3.3.1. Microstructure evolution at room temperature

dualistic phenomenon, invariably accompanied by microstructural 
changes within the material. A thorough analysis of the deformed ma -
terial ’ s microstructure can elucidate the intrinsic mechanisms governing 
the material ’ s mechanical response. It is widely acknowledged that 
dislocations are the primary deformation mechanism in all metallic 
materials. When the mobility of these dislocations is restricted, the 
materials gain strength and exhibit work hardening. The motion of 
dislocations can be impeded by grain boundaries or other interfaces, 
such as twin boundaries. Although twin boundaries are a special inter -
face type with lower resistance to dislocation motion than conventional 
grain boundaries [ 53 ], they significantly hinder dislocations and cause 
strengthening in a classical Hall-Petch way. These theoretical implica -
tions are well explained by the microstructure evolved after dynamic 
compression. It may be noted that quasi-static microstructures are not 
presented in the current study since the strengthening mechanisms 
primarily comprise dislocation-mediated plasticity, which is common

thickness (~40 nm). Also, there were fewer dislocations around those 
twin boundaries. In contrast, the twins seen in dynamically deformed 
samples are characterized by finer structures ( ≤ 15 nm) and high den -
sity, bent boundaries, and higher dislocation accumulation. High strain 
rates are known to introduce finer-thickness twins with high density in 
materials exhibiting twinning-induced plasticity [ 40 ]. The bending of 
twin boundaries (TBs) is primarily due to the accumulation of disloca -
tions along the TBs and is attributed to the deformation of twins [ 55 ]. 
Hence, it can be asserted that high strain rates induced multiple twin 
variants in the material that helped the material to strengthen in a Hall- 
Petch way. This phenomenon is common in materials with low stacking 
fault energy [ 40 ], in which deformation twins are the main carriers of 
deformation at high strain rates [ 56 ]. At high strain rates, dislocation 
density amplified resulting in clustering, tangling and more frequent 
mutual interaction with other microstructural features such as TB/GB 
and hence dislocation motion may jag. In this case the dislocations 
cannot solely relieve the mechanical loads; hence, twinning becomes 
dominant under high strain rate scenarios.
The second worthy discussion point corners around the work hard -
ening showcased by SA material, as seen in Fig. 5 c. This trend contra -
dicts the results shown by materials with ultrafine microstructure. The 
fine-grained materials generally show poor or no work hardening 
[ 57 ]. The annihilation rate balances the defect generation rate in these 
materials due to the heightened grain boundary area. This scenario is 
more pronounced under high strain rate deformation due to the onset of 
phenomena like dynamic recovery and recrystallization.
The work hardening involves the progression of strain with the 
simultaneous increase in strength. It is well established that plasticity is 
carried mainly by dislocation motion, and strength increases when the 
motion of dislocations is restricted. In a general scenario, the TBs and 
GBs effectively reduce the mean free path of dislocation and impart 
strengthening. However, when dislocations become immobile and 
saturate at a region, the material may experience softening or failures. 
However, no such scenario was observed for SA material. The absence of 
softening or retention of work hardening is only possible when addi -
tional plasticity and strengthening mechanisms are available. These 
mechanisms enable the deformation to progress homogeneously 
without loss of strength. In fact, the evolution of deformation twins 
under dynamic loading serves as secondary plasticity carriers. The 
deformation twins provide extra interfaces to hinder dislocation motion 
and increase the flow stress of the material. Besides restricting disloca -
tion mobility, deformation twins also provide ample regions and glide 
planes for dislocation to move and their storage [ 58 ]. This can 
contribute to continued plastic deformation while maintaining a high 
level of strength, i.e., the so-called work hardening behavior. It should 
also be noted that the second and third-order twins in 5000/s samples 
have tiny thicknesses, and their mutual distance is minimal. As the 
thickness of twins decreases, there is a proportional decrease in the 
distance between them. Experimental findings indicated that reduced 
twin spacing results in increased strength, heightened sensitivity to 
strain rate, improved work-hardening rate, and a greater density of 
mobile dislocations at twin boundaries in nano-twinned metals [ 59 ]. 
The findings in the current work corroborate these reports well. The 
mechanical response of SA material ( Fig. 5 ) shows elevated strength, 
work hardening, and SRS.
Two other aspects that prevail in the microstructure at 5000/s are 
the presence of numerous stacking faults and nano-grains, as highlighted 
in Fig. 6 e & i. Stacking faults are present throughout the matrix and make 
Lomer-Cottrel locks, as marked by blue lines ( Fig. 6 e). The SFs also act as 
an effective barrier to dislocation motion. Besides, SF can facilitate the 
emission of dislocations. These aspects collectively enhance material 
strength and work hardening, as witnessed in the flow curves of the 
material under investigation. It should be noted that twinning is rather 
challenging to commence in materials with ultra-fine and nano-grains 
due to the increased value of resolved shear stress [ 60 , 61 ]. However, 
we have witnessed very fine twins in the microstructure. Also, when 
twinning is suppressed, stacking faults are favorably formed by the glide 
of partial dislocations within the grains [ 62 ]. These findings are clearly 
revealed in the microstructure of material deformed at 5000/s. Nano 
grains can also serve as an effective barrier to hinder dislocation motion. 
The presence of these nano grains, along with dislocation networks, is 
illustrated in Fig. 6 i.

4. Conclusion
In summary, we engineered an ultrafine-grained powder metallurgy 
alloy that surpasses in mechanical performance to coarse-grained 
counterparts and many other conventional and multicomponent alloys 
regarding strength, plasticity at room, and elevated temperatures under 
dynamic loading. Based on the abovementioned results, the powder 
metallurgy route is an effective and efficient method for producing 
multicomponent alloys. Precise selection and control of process pa -
rameters can produce high-quality products without contamination. The 
grain size can be refined to the nanometer range by selecting the proper 
milling time and sintering temperature. Deformation twins can be 
retained in the material even after sintering and annealing operations by 
carefully selecting time and temperature. These twins are much more 
stable at high temperatures due to their low boundary energy and can 
render material to maintain their strength and plasticity at high tem -
peratures. The dynamic response of sintered material is unique by 
exhibiting a robust work-hardening trend, a property that is lost in 
materials with grain refinement. These outstanding mechanical attri -
butes make powder metallurgy alloys a potential candidate for much 
harsher dynamic loading conditions coupled with high temperatures. 
The main conclusions of this study are; 
i. Powder metallurgy comprising high-energy ball milling and high- 
pressure sintering was successfully applied to produce a single- 
phase, contamination-free medium entropy alloy. It can be 
perceived that powder metallurgy can be an effective way to 
produce nano-grained high and medium entropy alloys.
ii. The presence of ultra-fine grains, dislocations, twin networks, 
nano-grains and stacking faults after ball milling and sintering 
resulted in superb mechanical properties of the sintered material.
iii. The material exhibited a superior mechanical response, surpass -
ing both its cast counterparts and numerous other face-centered 
cubic (FCC) medium and high entropy alloys. This exceptional 
performance under dynamic loading resulted from the intricate 
interplay between pre-existing defects in the sintered material 
and defects that evolved during high strain rate deformation.
iv. The high-temperature response of the sintered material is com -
parable to conventional Ni-based superalloys and refractory high 
entropy alloys. Further adjustments to process parameters and 
the application of additional static mechanical deformation 
methods could potentially enhance the performance of the sin -
tered alloy. Additionally, given its remarkable twin formation 
capability at cryogenic temperatures, cryo-milling may be a 
potentially favorable method to embed abundant nano-twins in 
the material. These stable nano-twins can render powder metal -
lurgy CoCrNi alloy a potential candidate and competitor to 
existing high-temperature materials.
