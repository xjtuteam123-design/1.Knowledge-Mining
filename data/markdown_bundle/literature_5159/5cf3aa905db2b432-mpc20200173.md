ABSTRACT
Over the past decade, increased demand for energy generation at affordable rates and in a clean
state has been the driving force for material development for energy generation. In developing
countries, coal-fired thermal power plants are the major source of electricity generation.
However, coal-fired plants induce a higher carbon footprint and contribute to greenhouse
gas emissions. Hence, several strategies have been formulated to mitigate environmental pol-
lution while maintaining high energy output. This review comprehensively presents the historical
material development process that led to an increase in the efficiency of a coal-fired power plant.
Candidate material identification and application, essential alloying elements, and their impact on
service life are comprehensively reviewed. Adapting the ultra-supercritical (USC) and advanced
ultra-supercritical (A-USC) power generation technology requires the development of new alloys
that can withstand harsh operating conditions (700–725°C and 25–35 MPa), which demands the
replacement of conventional materials (ferritic/austenitic steel) with better ones. The candidate
alloys should be evaluated across several key issues such as high-temperature material response
to loading, creep strength, high-temperature oxidation, and component fabricability. The evalu-
ation of creep strength data suggests that nickel-based alloys like Inconel alloy 282 are preferred
because of the high stability of the γ
0 phase. Moreover, at higher temperatures, nickel-based
alloys are preferred over ferritic/austenitic steel alloys because of their superior corrosion resis-
tance. The fireside corrosion and high-temperature oxidation resistance are governed by the
formation of different oxide layers depending on the temperature, pressure, and alloy content.
Furthermore, the weldability of different candidate alloys is discussed in detail, along with the
microstructural evolution in the heat-affected zone. This review also focuses on the cost of con-
structing a greenfield USC/A-USC power plant. Lastly, a perspective on new alloy development
for A-USC/USC application is presented, and the microstructure and property relationships of
these alloys are discussed.

Introduction
Energy is the most vital and indivisible source of human development. Throughout history, humans have endeav-
ored to produce and harness energy for betterment and progress. Thermal energy is one energy form that is being
used directly or in a conserved form. The utilization of thermal energy to generate mechanical work was pioneered
by Thomas Newcomen and James Watt in the 17th century through the discovery of the steam engine. Thereafter,
steam was recognized as a source of mechanical energy, which led to the development of industrial steam generators
in the 19th century.
1 Wood, charcoal, coal, oil, and natural gas were used as boiler fuels in the early stages of
development. The excessive use of wood as fuel led to its scarcity, and as a result, coal emerged as a viable alternative.
Low costs and abundant availability established coal as the prime fuel for steam generation. In the 19th century, after
the invention of the prime mover by Michael Faraday and Joseph Henry (1820), the Werner von Siemens group
developed the first self-exciting dynamo-electric generator. This motivated Thomas Edison (1882) to build a full-
scale central power station by combining mechanical power generation using steam, i.e., steam engine and electric
generation, with dynamo. This was the first milestone of commercial steam-powered electricity generation. Later, in
1884, Sir Charles Parsons led the technology development by inventing the first steam turbine. The capacity of
power generation increased, and they could produce 30,000 kW electricity
2 until the end of the 19th century.
Components such as the economizer and superheater were continuously improved to enhance plant performance,
and with time and technology development, the coal-fired power plant became the prime source of electricity gen-
eration.

material response to loading. In order to circumvent these issues, new materials with improved performance

CANDIDATE MATERIALS FOR USC/A-USC APPLICATIONS
The requirement for power plants to work at critical steam conditions, i.e., higher temperature (higher than 550°
C) and pressure (higher than 22 MPa), includes more durable materials than conventional materials used in
thermal plants. The performance of conventional material degrades with the increase in steam parameters.
The manufacturing defects in conventional materials may have severe effects at critical steam conditions, leading
to catastrophic failures. Generation of high-residual stresses while joining of dissimilar metal, strain-induced
precipitation hardening, and an increase in brittleness because of alloying-element segregation are a few issues
encountered in conventional alloys, which are aggravated in critical steam conditions. Moreover, in the case of
thick components, the occurrence of type IV cracking in the heat-affected zone (HAZ) of the weldment and
hydrogen embrittlement are some issues that deteriorate the service life of the component. Other critical issues,
such as superheater corrosion (fireside corrosion and steam-side oxidation), long-term creep behavior, and ther-
mal fatigue, also affect the service life of the components. There is a continuous change in steam parameters
depending on a particular power generation cycle. The temperature of the water in the economizer is much
lower than the temperature at the inlet of the turbine. This means that material selection depends on the steam
parameters experienced by various components in the power plant. Accordingly, ferritic steel, austenitic steel
alloys, and nickel-based alloys are used for designing such components.

Load distribution in a thermal power plant varies from the no-load condition of the water feeder to the highly
loaded turbine blades. The load continuously varies with the transformation of water into superheated steam and
further condensation into the water at the condenser exit. Such varying loading conditions require the application
of a specific alloy to sustain specific loading conditions. In addition, materials face coupled loading conditions,
and they have to bear structural load because of steam pressure as well as thermal stresses generated because of the
thermal gradient in the system. Moreover, because of intermittent plant operations (startup and shutdown) and
sudden load change, power plant components experience fatigue loading. Quite frequently, power plants have to
operate continuously for months without any break in order to fulfill a surge in electricity demands. Thus, the
material must have structural stability over a period of time. The ability of the material to sustain load fluctuations
and retention of material properties are the prime criteria for material selection.

Weldability of USC/A-USC Candidate Alloys
Weldability is one of the crucial material requirements for application in thermal power plants. In general, a
typical power plant constitutes numerous components fabricated by thousands of welds. The welding operation
is performed on similar or dissimilar materials. The weld could be either single or multipass, depending upon the
design requirements. Not only the microstructural changes that occur during welding are important, but also the
behavior of the welded component during the service life, as it needs to be evaluated for material selection. There
are variations in microstructure inside and near the weld zone because of thermal gradients and solidification of
the weldment, which must be addressed with the help of thermomechanical testing. Hot cracking tests and im-
plant tests are a few weldability assessment tests used to determine the weldability of components, whereas tensile,
creep, and fatigue tests are used to assess the performance of components during service. There are two ways to
evaluate the weldability of a component. The first method employs the use of a replica of the actual product and
subjects it to the same treatment as the real component. The second method is to use a sample from a work
environment and evaluate its response to mechanical treatments.

USC/AUSC Candidate Alloys for Bolting
To facilitate the maintenance process of power plants, along with welded joints, components like turbine casings
are bolted. The critical operating condition of the USC/A-USC plant power plant makes bolt material selection
crucial. In order to sustain high pressure and temperature, bolt material should possess the following
properties
132:
 high resistance to relaxation;
 compatible CTE with flange, elasticated sleeve, and nut;
 high-notched resistance;
 high fracture toughness against variable temper load during erection, starting, and operation;
 high yield point to prevent deformation during preloading;
 resistance to stress-corrosion cracking;
 and microstructural stability over a long service life to prevent any unacceptable reduction in the properties
of the materials because of sustained operating stresses.
At high-temperature applications, the clamping force provided by the bolted joint is highly sensitive to the
relaxation behavior of bolt material. To cope with this, bolts are usually pre-strained to compensate for the re-
laxation. Initially applied elastic pre-strain gradually gets converted into creep strain with the reduction in ef-
fective load on the joint. The relaxed load depends on the initial load applied at room temperature, CTE of the bolt
and flange, creep properties, and thermal loading over a period of time.
133 Thus, the bolt material should possess
sufficiently high resistance to relaxation to provide an adequate clamping load under the influence of high-ther-
mal stresses. Evaluation of mechanical properties, the effect of alloying on high-temperature performance, and
failure analysis of bolted joints are reviewed by researchers.
134–138 Corrosion fatigue, hydrogen embrittlement,
and improper bolt tightening were identified as the cause of failure.
Numerous materials are available for bolting application, like other components dependent upon the oper-
ating-condition bolt material changes. 139 For low-temperature regions, where the operating temperature is below
560°C, 9 –12 % chromium steels are being used, such as Type 422 and 912CrMoV, whereas Nimonic 80A,
Nimonic 85A, Refractaloy 26, and A286 are suitable for operating temperatures less than 620°C. For a higher
operating temperature (>700°C), Nimonic 105, Nimonic 115, Inconel 718, Waspaloy, and Allvac 718Plus are the
most suitable candidate materials. In Europe, 12Cr-MoNiV, 12Cr-MoV, and 11Cr-MoVNbN grades are used as
bolting material, and TF3 (12 Cr-MoVNbW) is used in Japan.
140,141 FENIX700, LTES700R, USC141, and USC800
are some of the recently developed alloys for high-temperature bolted joints. 142
Corrosion/Oxidation of USC/A-USC Candidate Alloys
Working conditions at components like water walls, superheaters, and reheaters are very prone to corrosion. During
operation, tubes encounter both fireside corrosion on the outside and steam oxidation on the inside surface. This
dual degradation of material has a severe effect on product strength at USC/A-USC conditions. At high temper-
atures and pressure, scale formation occurs exponentially, leading to premature failure of components.
143 Scale
formation significantly changes the surface properties of tubes; tube overheating and subsequent bursting may occur
because of the low thermal conductivity of scale. Scale formation primarily depends on alloy type, steam conditions,
i.e., pressure and temperature, and surface microstructure.

Economic Evaluation of a Typical A-USC/USC Power Plant
The cost to utilize USC technology differs between a greenfield project and an upgradation of the existing power
plant system. Technology like the topping cycle can be used in existing power plants to increase the steam temper-
ature sufficiently higher than the super critical (SSC) temperature range.
140 In the case of existing plant upgradation,
the economic feasibility is mostly site-dependent and requires groundwork; thus, for the current discussion, more
emphasis is given to a greenfield USC power plant. Investment, operating, and supporting costs need to be con-
sidered for economic evaluation
163 Engineering, procurement, construction, transmission, and capitalized funding
expenses are covered by investment costs, whereas direct labor costs, direct material costs, manufacturing overhead
costs, marketing costs, and administrative costs are part of the operating expenditure. Supporting costs are expend-
itures that facilitate the functioning of power plants indirectly. Development of research and development centers
and implementation of new government policies, etc., are some of the supporting costs.
To evaluate the cost of the current A-USC power plant, it is admissible to evaluate the cost of the existing USC
power plant. Recently, Li et al.
164 evaluated the cost of a 1,000-MW USC coal-fired power plant operating at 622°C
and 30 MPa. Table 4 shows the cost of the USC power plant, which comprises equipment cost, installation, and
engineering fees.164 It was assumed that plant availability would be 5,500 h/year for a span of 30 years. The coal price
was considered at 4.1 USD/GJ. The annual cost of operation and maintenance was 4 % of the total cost. Furthermore,
it was mentioned that plants have a 0.34-year payback period with a cost of electricity at 0.002 USD/kWh.
In 2008, the Electric Power Research Institute, in the United States, produced an engineering/economic study
comparing the costs and performance of an A-USC power plant with the conventional coal-fired power station.

Material Development and Future Scope
Material development is one of the key factors governing power plant performance. New alloys with better per-
formance are being actively researched. In this regard, adding new solutes to the existing compositions and evalu-
ating the properties such as creep strength, fatigue behavior, weldability, and corrosion resistance is an effective
alloy development strategy. There are existing nickel-based superalloys that can be used to build A-USC power
plants, but the high cost of nickel-based alloys is an impediment to economical power generation. This demands a
significant amount of work to make them viable. There are two solutions to these issues. One is the modification
of the existing low-temperature alloys to make them competent to the harsh environment to such an extent that
utilization of nickel-based alloys will diminish. Another option is the alloying of nickel-based alloys with low-cost
solutes without compromising on the properties.

Summary
Abundant energy production at affordable rates and in the cleanest form has directed the technological develop-
ment in thermal power plants. The use of steam at critical parameters for energy production is the most sig-
nificant way of increasing power plant efficiency; however, it is subjected to the requirement of high-temperature
strength of alloys, their response to the corrosive environment, and fabricability. This requirement of high-per-
formance alloys has driven material development all over the globe. COST, AD700, KOMET 650, and COMTES
700 were a few research programs formulated for material development. Cool Earth-Innovative Technology
Programme led Japan in USC development. To date, Japan has the highest number of working USC power
plants.
11 The end result of the research programs is the identification of candidate alloys for particular compo-
nents. P122 and SAVE12D, etc., are used to fabricate the main steam pipe and header, whereas HR1200 and
TOS110, etc., were used to produce turbine rotors. SUPER304H, 347HFG, NF709, and Sanicro25 are the foremost
austenitic steel alloys being used to fabricate reheater tubes suitable to work up to 650°C. Inconel alloy 740,
Inconel alloy 617, HR6W, and Alloy 230 are candidate alloys to work above 650°C used for turbine blades.
The developed alloys are continuously evaluated by the researchers for their potential applications as power
plant components. Microstructural stability, the effect of alloying elements, the effect of DSA are prime issues
considered for material evaluation. Material weldability and resistance to corrosion are the other crucial issues
studied by different researchers. Type of scale formation, its development, and behavior during service were issues
of concern. During exposure to a corrosive environment, scale formation started with a thin layer of oxide having
a composition of Cr
2O3, MnCr 2O4,M n 7SiO12, FeMn(SiO 4), SiO 2, and Fe 3O4 for steel alloys. Whereas Cr 2O3,
MnCr2O4, and TiO 2 were deposited on nickel-based alloys. This thin layer grows with time to a particular thick-
ness and then gets exfoliated, causing a reduction in thickness, which leads to strength degradation.
