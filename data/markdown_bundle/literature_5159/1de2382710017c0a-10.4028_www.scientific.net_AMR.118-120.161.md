Keywords: Hardfacing, High temperature, Fatigue strength, Failure mode, Life prediction 
Abstract. The main task of this paper was to evaluate the influence of hardfacing technique and 
service temperature on the fatigue properties of heat-resistant steel X45CrSi9-3 coated with Co-based 
alloy Stellite 12. The results of rotating bending fatigue tests showed that at room temperature (RT), 
the fatigue strength of specimens welded by the acetylene gas welding (AGW) was lower than that of 
specimens welded by the plasma transferred arc welding (PTAW). For PTAW specimens, the fatigue 
strength at 500 oC was much higher than that at RT. Two failure modes were presented, one was 
termed as the coating failure mode at RT and the other was termed as coating-interface failure mode at 
500 oC. The fatigue life prediction was conducted by using a modified Murakami’s model. 
Introduction 
The hardfacing coatings, which are commonly used to provide a wear, corrosion and/or erosion 
resistant surface for structural materials, have been investigated extensively. On one hand, some 
studies were contributed to clarify the influence of welding technique [1-5] on the microstructural or 
micromechanical characteristics. On the other hand, some investigations emphasized properties of 
wear and corrosion resistance. Recently, fatigue properties have attracted more and more attention 
[1-3]. It has been shown that the microstructure like the size and population of porosities [4], the 
surface state of coating [4], and the coating thickness [1, 3], significantly influence fatigue properties. 
As for the influence of coating thickness, in some work the fatigue strength increases as the coating 
thickness increases [3], whereas in some studies, it decreases as the coating thickness increases [1]. 
Among these literature studies, however, almost all concentrated on coatings with thickness less 
than 1mm. It has been reported that for some components working in extreme severe condition, the 
thick coating up to 2mm is necessary to improve the wear and corrosion resistance. Moreover, most 
research focused on fatigue properties at RT, whereas fatigue properties at high temperature have 
been rarely investigated. The fracture mechanism at RT and high temperature is generally different, 
thus for some components serving at high temperature a precise understanding of fatigue properties at 
high temperature is indeed critical. 
Therefore, this work concerned fatigue properties of steel hardfaced with thick coating at both 
room temperature (RT) and 500 
oC. The main task was to clarify the influence of hardfacing technique 
and test temperature on fatigue properties of components, to propose the fracture mechanism, and to 
predict the fatigue life of X45CrSi9-3 hardfaced with the thick Stellite 12 coating.

Specimens Preparation and Experimental Procedure 
The Stellite 12 alloy powder was used for hardfacing to modify the surface of X45CrSi9-3 by the 
plasma transferred arc welding (PTAW) and the acetylene gas welding (AGW). The coating thickness 
was 2.5mm. Fatigue tests was conducted under rotating bending condition at 50Hz at RT and 500 
oC, 
and fracture surfaces were observed under a scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with 
energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) to determine the fatigue crack initiation site as well as failure 
modes. 
Results and Discussions 
Microscopic and Static Mechanical Characterizations. The metallurgical structure of the 
coating surface obtained by PTAW and AGW was similar; both can be described as Co-rich dendritic 
structure with a network of carbides in interdendritic regions, as shown in Fig.1. This was in 
accordance with some previous studies on the microstructure of Stellite alloys [5]. It was reported that 
the deposition coating of Stellite alloys contains primary dendrites of Co-rich phase/matrix and 
Cr-carbide rich particles connecting with each other in the interdendritic regions. Besides, the primary 
dendrites of AGW specimens was much smaller than that of PTAW ones. The AGW specimens 
presented “harder” properties with higher Vickers hardness and Young’s modulus, but lower bending 
strength than the PTAW specimens, as presented in Table 1 and 2. The bending strength at 500 
oC was 
much lower than that at RT, which was resulted from the decrease in tensile strength of materials with 
the increase in temperature.

Failure Modes at RT and 500 oC 
According to SEM observations of fractured surfaces in Fig.2, fatigue cracks of all specimens at RT 
initiated from surface or subsurface defects (like voids, inclusion or inclusion cluster), and most 
specimens were multi-site initiated, due to the inevitable welding defects in the extreme thick coating. 
A typical fracture surface of PTAW specimens fractured at RT was shown in Fig.2 (b1) and (b2), 
presenting two initiation sites from surface inclusion clusters and subsurface void, respectively. In 
Fig.2 (b1), elements like Na, Mg, Al, K, Ca, Ti, Cl, S and O were detected. In the center substrate 
region, the river pattern was observed, indicating a brittle fracture during the final stage of the failure 
process. It can be inferred that at RT fatigue cracks initiated and propagated as follows: Fatigue cracks 
first initiated from surface or subsurface defects due to the high stress intensity factor, and then 
propagated from the edge quickly in the brittle coating, next crossed the interface without the 
delamination along the interface, and finally gathered into the center. Specimens failed after the 
cleavage fracture of the center substrate during the final stage. This fracture mode was named 
“coating failure” failure mode at RT. 
The fracture surface at 500 oC is displayed in Fig.2 (c1) and (c2), which show the multi-site 
subsurface initiation from inclusion clusters and delamination along the interface. Along the 
delamination, dimples were observed in both the brittle coating and the substrate. Therefore, some 
conclusions on the fracture mechanism at 500 oC can be obtained: Cracks first initiated at surface or 
subsurface inclusions, and then propagated from these sites. When cracks arrived at the interface, due 
to the relatively lower interface strength at 500 oC, they were arrested by the interface and started to 
propagate along the interface, resulting in the delamination along the interface. This delamination and 
the subsequent fracture process of the substrate exhausted much time, which can be inferred from the 
dimples in both the coating and substrate. This failure mode was named “coating-interface failure” 
mode at 500 oC. 
Based on the understanding of the failure mechanism at RT and 500 oC, it can be inferred that the 
longer fatigue life at 500 oC is mainly because of the relatively lower interface strength at high 
temperature, resulting in the time-consuming process of dimple formation in both the coating and 
substrate. Besides, the temperature influenced the fatigue crack propagation behavior; however, for 
this Stellite 12-X45CrSi9-3 material system this effect is still obscure and needs more research. 
Generally, the influence of temperature on fatigue phenomena is complicated, and is reported as the 
competition result of the increase of the yield strength and fatigue strength with decreasing 
temperature and the increasing effect of dynamic strain aging with increasing test temperature [6].

Conclusions 
(1) Due to the relatively higher propagation rate of fatigue cracks, the fatigue strength of AGW 
specimens at RT was much lower than that of PTAW ones. For PTAW specimens, owing to the 
relatively lower interface strength at high temperature, the fatigue strength at 500oC was higher 
than that at RT. 
(2) Fatigue cracks were all initiated from surface or subsurface defects like inclusions or voids, and 
for most specimens cracks were multisite-initiated. Two fatigue failure modes were proposed for 
the Stellite 12-X45CrSi9-3 system. One was named “coating failure” mode at RT without the 
delamination, and the other was named “coating-interface failure” mode at 500oC with the 
delamination along the interface. 
(3) The modified Murakami’s model, which takes account of the characteristic geometry of 
inclusions, the average Vickers hardness of coating, and the effect of external stress in terms of 
actual fatigue life, was more proper than the Murakami’s model. The fatigue life can be 
determined at a given stress level by this modified model.
