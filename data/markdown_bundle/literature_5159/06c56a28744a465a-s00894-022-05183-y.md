Computational analysis of mechanical behavior and potential energy 
of thermoresponsive copper‑tantalum nanoalloy
Mahesh Kumar Gupta1 · Vinay Panwar2  · R. P . Mahapatra3
Received: 17 December 2021 / Accepted: 3 June 2022 
© The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2022
Abstract
Temperature, strain rate, and defects are important considerations in determining the mechanical properties of materials. The 
mechanical properties of nanocrystalline copper-tantalum (Cu-Ta) alloy are investigated using classical molecular dynamics 
simulation approach in which embedded atom method of potential with periodic boundary conditions in all directions has 
been adopted. Numerical simulation has been performed to predict the mechanical properties of nanocrystalline copper-
tantalum alloy. The virtual tensile test has been conducted at a fixed strain rate and increasing temperature where the discreet 
change in temperature from 50 to 1600 K has been used as a controlling parameter. The strain rate is fixed in the direction of 
the principal crystallographic planes and has not been affected by the change in temperature. The mechanical properties of 
the Cu-Ta nanocrystalline alloy such as yield strength, ultimate strength, and Young’s modulus are observed. Further, simu-
lations are carried out to analyze the vacancy formation energy with vacancy concentration and potential energy response 
at discrete temperatures. Nanocrystalline Cu-Ta alloy is observed to be more susceptible to failure at high temperatures. 
Particularly at 300 K, the strength of nanocrystalline Cu-Ta is 6 GPa which decreases to 4 GPa at 1200 K.

Introduction
Nanocrystalline materials are one of the most advanced 
forms of material in the present time due to their mechanical, 
chemical, and electrical properties. In the last two decades, 
nanocrystalline materials have been an emerging field of 
research and have attracted various researchers due to their 
structural mechanical behavior [1–3]. The mechanical prop-
erties of the materials also vary with changes in size. Based 
on the scale, materials are classified mainly into two cat-
egories, i.e., macro or bulk materials and nanomaterials [4]. 
It is very important to study their properties in a particular 
domain using experimental and numerical techniques. For 
nanoscale materials, various simulation methods can be used 
to study the properties of materials at the molecular level, 
e.g., density functional theory (DFT) [5], Monte Carlo (MC) 
method [6], molecular dynamic (MD) simulation, ab initio 
MD [7], and quantum MC (QMC) [8 ]. There exist limiting 
factors for using the techniques as in the case of DFT, which 
includes the difficulty in describing thermodynamic equi -
librium states of multi-particle systems with non-uniform 
density. MC approach requires large computing times which 
makes it unfeasible to study the dynamic properties with a 
large number of atoms under consideration. Ab initio MD 
and QMC simulation approaches are used for study at small 
length and time scales, only. In the case of ab initio MD, 
the study of the properties can be possible at discrete time 
instants. Molecular dynamics simulation, generally, involves 
solving equations of motion for a system of particles. Newto-
nian, Lagrangian, or Hamiltonian approaches [9] can be used 
to form the equations, all of which rely on microcanonical 
(NVE) statistical ensemble where the number of atoms, vol-
ume, and total energy remain constant. Modified equations

The tensile behavior of nanocrystalline copper was stud-

 CoSb3 and found a linear reduction in ulti-

Dewapriya and Rajapakse [13] investigated the defective 
graphene response with temperature and strain rate by MD 
and observed that vacancy concentration, temperature, and 
strain rate play a vital role in graphene sheet strength. The 
tensile strength of tantalum was investigated by Hahn et al. 
[14] at various strain rates and observed that the strength 
of tantalum increases with increasing strain rate. The study 
considered a strain rate of six order magnitude and combined 
the outcomes with experiments concluding that strength 
depends on nucleation, growth, and coalescence of cracks 
and voids. Ruan et al. [15] used the MD simulation tool 
to investigate the mechanical properties of polycrystalline 
nickel nanowires of different grain sizes at different strain 
rates and temperatures under uniaxial tension. The study 
reported that the stress–strain curve shifts upwards in a 
linear manner upon increasing strain rate and the shape of 
curves follows a similar trend. The study observed that the 
impact of strain rate on the modulus of elasticity is com-
paratively less than the yield stress and with increasing 
temperature, there is an inverse pattern with both Young’s 
modulus as well as yield stress. Ruicheng et al. [16] studied 
the vacancy concentration and consequence of temperature 
on the mechanical properties of γ-TiAl alloy using the MD 
method. The ultimate strain, ultimate stress, and Young’s 
modulus were observed to increase with a decrease in the 
vacancy concentration and temperature. The effect of tem-
perature on the strength was found to have more impact than 
vacancy concentration. The vacancy evolution process was 
however not observed owing to the thermal motion of atoms 
at high temperatures. In addition, potential energy was found 
to be affected by both temperature and vacancy concentra-
tion. A similar kind of study was done by Srinivasan et al. 
[17] on the concentration of tantalum in Cu-Ta alloy where 
the strain rate and temperature were varied to investigate 
the mechanical properties of the alloy. The study observed 
that at high processing temperatures above 973 K grain size 
shifts from nanocrystalline regime to ultra-grained regime 
when tantalum concentration was changed from 5 to 10%.

Methodology
Molecular simulation of this work has been carried out with 
a large-scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simulator 
(LAMMPS) [19]. The potential method used for this simulation 
is the embedded atom method (EAM). EAM potential is used to 
explain the atomic interactions of the molecules and the energy 
method is used to calculate the vacancy formation energy of the 
system. A semi-empirical many-body potential is independent 
of the size of particles while computing the total energy of a sys-
tem [20]. Cohesive energy is the heat of sublimation to divide 
the crystal into individual atoms and is dependent on the grain 
size [21]. Large-scale molecular dynamic simulation is helpful 
to find the laser-driven shock experiments and is used to predict 
the damage behavior [22–24]. In order to prepare 9Cu-Ta crystal, 
the work has been performed with attainable practical surround-
ings via simulation and most of the parameters were taken or 
calculated with reference to the experimental base. For developing 
the crystal structure of copper-tantalum, a composition in atomic 
weight with 90% copper and 10% tantalum has been considered. 
The existence of 9Cu-Ta nanocrystalline alloy has already been 
attested with experiments [25]. However, in order to create a 9Cu-
Ta nanocrystalline alloy, the system unit cells were positioned 
according to the lattice parameter of 9Cu-Ta, taking the lattice 
constant of copper (FCC) as 3.644 Å [25] and that of tantalum 
(BCC) as 3.298 Å [26].

Results and discussion
Temperature dependency of 9Cu‑Ta nanoalloy
The effect of temperature on mechanical properties of 9Cu-
Ta nanoalloy has been studied at different temperatures 
ranging from 50 to 1600 K. The stress–strain curve at cor -
responding temperatures has been shown in Fig.  4. It has 
been realized that the curve follows the same pattern as 
explained by Chen et al. [37]. It can be seen in Fig.  4 that 
ultimate tensile strength and yield strength decrease with 
increasing temperature since the bond strength weakens at 
higher temperatures and thereby fails under lower stress. 
The effect of temperature is low for the elastic deformation 
stage of the stress–strain curve. After a critical value of 
strain, stress increases nonlinearly and at ultimate stress, 
necking starts forming due to reduced binding force in 
comparison to the applied stress. The temperature-induced 
softening in the material can be attributed to high ther -
mal vibrations of atoms at the higher temperature. This 
large amplitude of atomic vibration at high temperatures 
reduces the stiffness. Consequently, the peak length of the 
chemical bond is achieved due to thermal agitations and 
the movement of the atoms becomes easier which leads to 
the failure of nanoalloy. Tensile failure in the regime of 
high strain rate is commonly known as “spall” which is an 
evolution of physical damage generated by a rarefaction 
wave, or combination of waves whose amplitude becomes

Effect of vacancy percentage on vacancy formation 
energy of 9Cu‑Ta nanoalloy
Figure  6 represents the relation between the number of 
removed atoms and the corresponding vacancy formation 
energy. The curve indicates the anomaly at any particular 
concentration of removed atoms and also identifies higher 
resistance to vacancy defect by 9Cu-Ta nanoalloy.
With the increase in the number of deleted atoms, both the final 
energy and vacancy formation energy decrease. However, the fall 
in final energy of the system which is obtained after equilibration 
is far more prominent than the formation energy. It can be seen 
from Fig. 6 that the initial energy of 9Cu-Ta nanoalloy remains 
constant throughout. The final energy of the system is less than 
the initial energy even for a perfect crystal due to the process of 
equilibrium. The process of minimization in molecular dynamics 
results in a stable alloy system than the initial steps to perform 
the required calculations and therefore making the observed dif-
ference between initial and final energy. Subsequently, when the 
stable state is attained by the alloy system it becomes difficult to 
create a further vacancy and hence the formation energy decreases 
drastically as the vacancy concentration is raised. The negative sign 
indicates the amount of energy required to create a vacancy in the 
crystal system. With the loss of atoms, the overall intermolecular 
forces and bonding energy also reduce and result in a reduction in 
the final energy of the alloy system.

due to the thermal softening effect. Some of the key observa-
tions made through this study of thermo-mechanical prop-
erties of 9Cu-Ta nanoalloy having vacancy defects are as 
follows.
• Mechanical properties of nanocrystalline 9Cu-Ta are 
sensitive at higher strain rates. Yield strength and ulti -
mate tensile strength both decrease with increasing 
temperature and follow a nearly-linear relationship with 
temperature. However, Young’s modulus first increases 
with an increase in temperature from 50 to 300 K and 
then decreases with increasing temperature. This varia-
tion could be due to the deformation under the coupling 
effect of high strain rate and lower temperature.
• The vacancy formation energy of the system decreases 
with increasing temperature as well as with vacancy 
percentage, due to softening of material and reduced 
bond strength. This resembles that the thermal effect is 
dominant over vacancy defect. Vacancy percentage does 
not affect the shape of the potential energy curve but it 
decreases the time to reach the peak value and stores the 
lower potential energy before fracture.
• The potential energy of nanoalloy decreases with increas-
ing temperature. This response is found to be due to the 
higher amplitude of vibrations induced in the material 
at higher temperatures. At high temperatures, kinetic 
energy of the atoms increases and simultaneously their 
potential energy decreases.
• The shape of the potential energy curve does not change 
with vacancy concentration but peak time decreases with 
increasing vacancy percentage. It should be due to the 
lower available energy within the defective nanoalloy to 
sustain their integrity. This follows the inverse relation 
between vacancy percentage and potential energy. Con-
sequently, the higher the vacancy percentage lower will 
be the potential energy.
