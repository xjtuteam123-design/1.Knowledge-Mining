Effects of Hf additions on high-temperature mechanical properties of
a directionally solidiﬁed NiAl/Cr(Mo) eutectic alloy
C.Y . Cui∗, J.T. Guo, H.Q. Ye
Institute of Metal Research, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shenyang 110016, China
Received 1 August 2007; received in revised form 17 August 2007; accepted 26 August 2007
Available online 30 August 2007
Abstract
In order to improve the high-temperature mechanical properties of NiAl/Cr(Mo) alloys, the effects of Hf additions on microstructure and
mechanical properties were systemically examined. Two directionally solidiﬁed alloys with composition of Ni-32Al-28Cr-(6−x)Mo-xHf (x = 0.2
and 0.5 at.%, respectively), named as 0.2Hf and 0.5Hf hereafter, were prepared. The Hf additions disturbed the cellular structure. The 0.2Hf alloy
consisted of dendritic structure, while the 0.5Hf alloy had an intercellular structure. In the 0.5Hf alloy, the Ni
2AlHf and Ni16Hf6Si7 precipitates were
also conﬁrmed. The high-temperature strength and brittle-to-ductile transition temperature (BDTT) increased with increasing of Hf additions, due
to the different strengthening mechanism. In contrast, the ductility and creep resistance decreased with increasing of Hf because of the disturbance
of cellular structure.

1. Introduction
Despite an attractive combination of oxidation and thermal–
physical properties, the development of NiAl alloys as replace-
ments for superalloys in turbine blade applications has been
largely limited by its poor fracture toughness at room temper-
ature and low high-temperature strength [1]. Although some
single crystal and polycrystalline alloys with superior creep
strength comparable or better than advanced superalloys have
been developed, these alloys have poor room temperature frac-
ture toughness, which restricts their application [2]. Recently,
the NiAl eutectic systems have been extensively studied,
because these systems have shown the potential to provide a
reasonable balance of creep and toughness properties [3–7].
These alloys have second phases ( /H9251-Cr, /H9251-Mo or Cr(Mo))
that can improve fracture toughness by crack bridging and
blunting. For example, the directional solidiﬁed NiAl-28Cr-
6Mo alloy with a lamellar structure has a fracture toughness
of 21 MPa m
1/2 at room temperature [3]. However, higher

Previous studies have shown that Hf and other Heusler-
forming elements signiﬁcantly increase the high-temperature
strength of NiAl[8–10]. The strength increase is mainly ascribed
to solid solution and precipitate strengthening [8]. Thus, sev-
eral studies have tried to improve the strength of NiAl/Cr(Mo)
system through alloying additions[11–14]. Raj et al.[11] evalu-
ated over 40 alloying addition combination to the NiAl/Cr(Mo)
system. The results showed that many alloying elements even
when added in small amounts effectively destroy the initially
planar microstructure without any signiﬁcant improvement in
the high-temperature strength. For example, Hagihara et al.[12]
investigated the effects of 3% and 5 at.% Ti on microstructure
and mechanical properties of NiAl-31Cr-3Mo alloy and found
a signiﬁcant increase in high-temperature strength accompa-
nied by a large deterioration of room temperature toughness.
We also investigated the effect of 0.5 at.% Hf addition on
microstructure and compressive properties of NiAl-28Cr-6Mo
and found an improvement in high-temperature compressive
strength [13,14]. However, up to now, the effects of Hf addition
on high-temperature mechanical properties, such as strength,
brittle–ductile transition temperature (BDTT) and tensile creep,
were not systemically examined.

The purpose of the paper is to examine the effects of Hf

behaviors, such as compressive strength, BDTT and tensile creep
in order to clarify the effects of Hf addition in NiAl/Cr(Mo)
eutectic alloy.
2. Experimental
The alloying addition was made by substituting for Mo. Prior to directional
solidiﬁcation, two bars of Ni-32Al-28Cr-(6 −x)Mo-xHf ( x = 0.2 and 0.5 at.%)
were prepared by induction melting appropriate amounts of Ni, Al, Cr, Mo and Hf
under an Ar atmosphere (henceforth referred to as the 0.2Hf and 0.5Hf, respec-
tively). The bars were then directionally solidiﬁed (DS) in a Al
2O3–SiO2 mold
through a Bridgman process. The thermal gradient at the liquid/solid interface
was about 70–80 K/cm. The withdrawal rate of the alloys was set to 300 mm/h.
This fast rate is chosen for (1) that the directional solidiﬁcation rates from 25.4
to 254 mm/h have no effect on mechanical properties of NiAl-31Cr-3Mo [15],
and reducing the Si-contamination [16].
Compression, tension, creep and metallography specimens were cut from
the aligned regions of the DS bars by an electrodischarge machining (EDM),
where the long axis of the specimens for mechanical properties tests was par-
allel to the growth direction. The compression specimens with dimension of
5 mm in diameter× 10 mm in length were ground to remove EDM surface dam-
age. Compression tests were performed at nominal strain rates ranging from
2 × 10
−3 to 2 × 10−5 s−1 on a Gleeble 1500 test machine from room tem-
perature to 1373 K. The tensile and creep specimens with a gauge section of
2.5 mm× 2m m× 16 mm were carefully prepared to remove cracks caused by
machining. Tensile tests were performed at nominal strain rates ranging from
10
−2 to 10−4 s−1 on a Shimadzu AG-250KNE testing machine. Elongations
were calibrated from the change in distance between two markers on the gauge
surface after testing. For clarity, the BDTT in this analysis is deﬁned as the lowest
temperature for which 5% tensile ductility could be consistently achieved in the
alloy [17]. Creep tests were performed in air at temperatures ranging from 1255
to 1423 K under initial stress ranges of 30–124 MPa. The creep deformation was
recorded by a dial indicator with all the deformation assumed to occur in the
gauge section.
Microstructural observations were performed in a scanning electron
microscope (SEM) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometer
(EDXS). The fracture surfaces were examined by SEM. Thin foils for transmis-
sion electron microscope (TEM) observations were prepared by an ion-thinning
procedure, and then examined in a JEM2010 high-resolution electron micro-
scope.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructures
Fig. 1 shows the typical microstructures of the alloys
observed on the longitudinal and transverse sections. All
alloys mainly consisted of NiAl and Cr(Mo) phases. Some
minor phases such as Hf-rich precipitate, Heusler (L2
1-ordered
Ni2AlHf) and G-phase (Ni16Hf6Si7) are also observed, as listed
in Table 1. In order to know the Hf partitioning in NiAl and
Cr(Mo), the chemical compositions of constituent phase were
analyzed by EDXS and listed in Table 1. The Hf content in the
Cr(Mo) phase was much lower than that in the NiAl phase, this
means that Hf mainly partitioned to NiAl phase.
The microstructures of the alloys were strongly inﬂuenced
by the Hf additions. According to the previous result [6], cellu-
lar microstructures are formed with the Cr(Mo) and NiAl plate
emanating radially from the cell interior to the cell boundaries
at the growth rate of 300 mm/h. The Hf addition disturbed the
cellular structure of the NiAl/Cr(Mo) eutectic. Speciﬁcally, the
0.2Hf alloy consisted of dendritic structure. The core of dendrites
were composed of NiAl and the interdendritic consisted of NiAl
and Cr(Mo) eutectic. The microstructure of 0.5Hf alloy had an
intercellular structure and the Cr(Mo) phase was not arranged
in well-aligned plate.

3.2. Compression behaviors
Fig. 3 shows the 0.2% compressive yield strength as a func-
tion of temperature. For comparison, the yield strengths of binary
NiAl [19] and NiAl/Cr(Mo) [20] were also included. The Hf-
containing alloys had higher yield strength than binary NiAl and
NiAl/Cr(Mo), the strength increased with the increasing of the
Hf content, showing that Hf was very effective in improving the
strength. For example, at 1373 K, the yield strength of the 0.5Hf
alloy was 300 MPa, which was about 100 MPa higher than that of
the 0.2Hf alloy. In addition, the temperature dependence was dif-
ferent depending on the Hf additions. The 0.2Hf alloy decreased
rapidly with increasing temperature above 1000 K, and showed
almost the same value as the NiAl/Cr(Mo) at 1373 K. In con-
trast, the yield strength of the 0.5Hf alloy still showed higher
stress than that of the 0.2Hf alloy.

3.3. Tensile behaviors
3.3.1. BDTT
Fig. 6(a) shows the true stress–strain curves near the BDTT
at a strain rate of 10
−4 s−1. At 1143 K, the 0.2Hf alloy frac-
tured with slight elongation after yielding, while the 0.5Hf alloy
fractured without yielding and plastic deformation. The yield
stress of the 0.2Hf alloy was higher than the fracture strength
of the 0.5Hf alloy, indicating that the 0.5Hf alloy was brittle. At
1173 K, the two alloys all fractured with appreciable elongation
after yielding, showing that the alloys were ductile at temper-
atures above 1173 K. A map deﬁning the conditions for which
the alloy is ductile or brittle is obtained by plotting strain rate
versus BDTT, as shown inFig. 6(b). Below and to the right of the
line, the alloys exhibited low ductility and fractured in a brittle
manner. Above and to the left of the line, the alloys showed high
ductility. The BDTT increased about 50 K with the increasing
of the Hf addition.

3.3.2. Tensile creep
Fig. 8(a and b) shows the tensile creep curves of the alloys
tested at 1255 K/124 MPa and 1323 K/60 MPa. Each of these
creep curves included negligible primary (transient) creep,
steady-state creep and dominant accelerating creep stage. Com-
parisons of these curves showed that the 0.2Hf alloy had better
creep resistance than the 0.5Hf alloy. For example, at 1323 K and
60 MPa, the steady-state creep rates were 1.8× 10
−7 s−1 for the
0.2Hf alloy and 1.0× 10−6 s−1 for the 0.5Hf alloy, respectively.
Fig. 8(c) plots the steady creep rate as a function of applied stress
at 1323 K. The stress exponents,n, were determined to be about
6 for the 0.2Hf alloy and 3.4 for the 0.5Hf alloy, respectively.
Fig. 9 shows the SEM photos of the metallurgical cross sec-
tion of the failed samples. A number of cracks were observed
away from the fracture surface. These cracks were considered to
be formed and propagate along the weak NiAl/Cr(Mo) interface,
where the Hf-rich and Ni
2AlHf precipitates existed. Debonding
along the NiAl/Cr(Mo) interface also suggested that the NiAl
matrix would be stronger than the NiAl/Cr(Mo) interface due to
the solid solution (Cr, Mo, Hf) and precipitation (Ni
16Hf6Si7,
and Ni2AlHf) strengthening. The similar fracture mode (along
the weak interface) was also observed in multiphase Ni-25Al-
15Cr [21].
Fig. 10 compares the variation of the applied tensile creep
stress with the Larson–Miller parameter (LMP) for the current
and potential NiAl alloys. The common value of 20 for the LMP
constant has been used in the drawing of this ﬁgure. The ﬁgure
showed that the Hf-containing alloys were stronger than binary
NiAl and NiAl-0.5Hf [22]. However, the creep resistance of the
0.2Hf alloy was stronger than that of the 0.5Hf alloy.
It turned out that the microstructures of NiAl/Cr(Mo) alloy
were disturbed by the Hf additions. The 0.2Hf alloy had a den-
dritic structure and the 0.5Hf alloy consisted of intercellular
structure, which was possibly related to eutectic solidiﬁcation
reaction. As reported previously, the solidiﬁcation process of Hf-
containing NiAl/Cr(Mo) alloy can be deduced as follows [17]:
the NiAl and Cr(Mo) eutectic reaction occurred at about 1718 K.
L → NiAl + Cr(Mo) + L1 (2)
Due to the different Hf addition, the 0.2Hf alloy had less
remaining L1 than the 0.5Hf alloy. Thus, the Hf-rich precipi-
tates with simple hexagonal structure would easily be formed in
the 0.2Hf alloy during the following cooling, while the Ni
2AlHf
precipitates with complicated L2 1 structure were formed in
the higher Hf-containing alloy. These precipitates mainly dis-
tributed on the NiAl/Cr(Mo) interface, because the interfacial
energy between NiAl and Cr(Mo) is the highest among the
interfaces (NiAl/Ni
2AlHf, Cr(Mo)/Ni2AlHf) [18]. As a result,
maintaining a lamellar structure could be difﬁcult because any
addition would lower the melting point of the alloy and alter the
eutectic solidiﬁcation reaction.
As to the effects of Hf addition on the mechanical proper-
ties, the high-temperature strength (Fig. 3; Table 3) and BDTT
(Figs. 6 and 7) increased with increasing of the Hf addition. The
strength increase was mainly caused by the different strength-
ening mechanism. Because only small amounts of Hf-rich
precipitate were found in the 0.2Hf alloy (Table 1), the increase
in yield strength was ascribed mainly to the solid solution
strengthening. While the Ni
2AlHf and Ni16Hf6Si7 precipitates
were formed in the 0.5Hf alloy, thus the strength increase in
the 0.5Hf alloy was related to the solid solution and precipi-
tation strengthening. On the other hand, the ductility ( Table 3)
and creep resistance (Figs. 8 and 10) decreased with increasing
of Hf addition because of the disturbance of cellular structure.

As shown in Fig. 9, the higher contents of the Ni 2AlHf phase
in the 0.5Hf alloy made the NiAl/Cr(Mo) interface weaker than
the constituent phases, thus cracks were easily initiated from the
NiAl/Cr(Mo) interface, where lots of Ni
2AlHf precipitates were
distributed. It is, therefore, concluded that microalloying with
Hf was not effective in increasing the high-temperature creep
resistance to the NiAl/Cr(Mo) alloy.
The stress exponents obtained from the compressive tests
(Table 2) were similar to those obtained from the tensile creep
test (Fig. 8(c)), which suggested that mechanical properties are
independent of the speciﬁc testing method.
4. Summary of results
The effects of Hf additions on microstructure and mechan-
ical properties of DS NiAl/Cr(Mo) grown at 300 mm/h were
examined and the following results were drawn:
(1) The 0.2Hf alloy consisted of dendritic structure, while the
0.5Hf alloy had an intercellular structure. In the 0.5Hf alloy,
the Ni
2AlHf and Ni 16Hf6Si7 precipitates were also con-
ﬁrmed.
(2) The high-temperature strength and BDTT increased with
increasing of Hf addition.
(3) The ductility and creep resistance decreased with increas-
ing of Hf addition because of the disturbance of cellular
structure.
