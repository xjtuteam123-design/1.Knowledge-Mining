Introduction
We previously developed “refractory superalloys” (1–5) that are based on platinum-group metals with
fcc and L12 two-phase coherent structures that are potentially useful at ultra-high temperature, at which
Ni-based superalloys cannot be used. From among the platinum-group metals, we selected Ir as the base
material because its melting temperature of 2447°C is higher than that of Ni of 1455°C (6), and because
Ir has an fcc structure, which can be equilibrated with the L1
2 phase of alloy elements.
Preliminary results demonstrated that the strength of Ir-based alloys depends on the precipitate
shape, which is determined by the lattice misfit parameter, d 5 (ap 2 am)/am, where ap and am are
the lattice parameters of the precipitates and the matrix, respectively. Cuboidal precipitates form in
Ir-Nb alloys with a 0.33% lattice misfit, and plate-like precipitates form in Ir-Zr alloys with a 2.19%
lattice misfit. The compressive strength of Ir-Zr alloys with plate-like precipitates at room temperature
and at 1200°C is higher than those of Ir-Nb alloys with cuboidal precipitates (7). Shearing of
dislocations into the precipitates contributes to the strengthening of the Ir-Zr alloy (8).
To better understand the relationship of superalloy strength on the precipitate morphology, we
altered the precipitate morphology by heat-treating Ir-Nb alloys. We obtained three different kinds of
precipitate morphology by heat treatment at 1200, 1500, and 1800°C, and we measured the resulting
compressive strength.
Experimental Procedure
We prepared pure Ir and Ir-Nb binary alloys with nominal compositions of Nb varying from 10 to
25at%. We made 70g button ingots of the alloy by arc melting in an argon atmosphere. According to
Ir-Nb phase diagrams (6), up to 10at% Nb, an fcc single-phase exists in Ir-Nb alloys, and an L1
2 single
phase exists above 24at% Nb. The fcc and L12 two-phase structure exists in the Ir-Nb binary alloys with
compositions between 11 and 24at% Nb.
To understand the microstructure of Ir-Nb alloys, we cut cylindrical samples 3 mm in diameter and
6 mm high from an ingot of the Ir-Nb binary alloy, with fcc and L1 2 two-phase structure, and heat
treated them at 1200, 1500, and 1800°C for 168 hours. Heat treatment at 1200°C was done for samples
encapsulated in quartz tubes filled with argon gas, followed by water quenching. Heat treatment at 1500

Results
Microstructure
Figure 1 shows that dendritic structures were observed in grains of Ir-15at%Nb alloys heat treated at
1200, 1500, and 1800°C. Grain boundaries were not smooth in the samples heat treated at 1200 and
1500°C because grain boundaries were situated along the dendritic structure. Similar dendritic struc-
tures were observed in as-cast samples of the Ir-15at%Nb alloy (7). This shows dendritic segregation
still remained after heat treatment. After heat treatment at 1800°C, however, the dendritic structures
decreased and the grain boundaries became smooth (Fig. 1c). This shows that homogenization
progressed at 1800°C.
Figure 2 shows secondary electron images of these samples, which indicate that fine precipitates
were formed in each dendrite arm. Although the precipitate shape is not clear in the samples heat treated
at 1200 and 1500°C, rectangular precipitates were formed in the samples heat treated at 1800°C.
Therefore, because the precipitates were formed along specific directions, long, wide matrix channels
were observed.

Compressive Strength
Figure 4 shows the temperature dependence of the compressive strength of the alloys. For reference, the
compressive strength of the Ir-15at%Nb alloy heat treated at 1800°C for 72 hours is plotted (9). The
strength of the samples heat treated at 1200°C was about 1200 and 800MPa at room temperature and
1200°C, respectively. Above 1200°C, the strength decreased to about 130MPa at 1800°C. Other
samples heat treated at 1500 and 1800°C showed similar behavior. The strength of the samples heat
treated at 1800°C for 168 hours was lower than those of the samples heated at 1200 and 1500°C. On
the other hand, for the samples heat treated at 1800°C for 72 hours, the strength at room temperature
and 1200°C was equivalent to that of the samples heat treated at 1200°C. Figure 5 shows that in the
samples heated at 1800°C for 72 hours, spherical precipitates with misfit dislocations at the interface,
rather than at cuboidal precipitates, were distributed uniformly in the matrix. Compared with the
strength of the samples heat treated at 1200 and 1500°C, at room temperature the strength of the
samples heat treated at 1500°C was lower than that of the samples heat treated at 1200°C. At 1800°C,
there was no significant effect of the heat-treatment temperature on the compressive strength of the
samples.

Figure 6 shows the 0.2% flow stresses of the Ir-Nb alloys with Nb content varying from 0 to 25at%.
According to standard phase diagrams (6), at 1400°C, the fcc single-phase exists up to 11at% Nb and
the L1
2 single-phase exists above 24at% Nb. The fcc and L12 two-phase structure exists for Nb varying
from 11 to 24at%. Grain sizes of the alloys were about 2200 mm for pure Ir and 50 mm for Ir-10at%
Nb and Ir-25at% Nb alloys. Solid solution hardening was observed in the fcc single-phase alloys,
although we must consider the grain size effect, because the grain size of the Ir-10at% Nb alloy was
smaller than that of pure Ir. By adding more than 10at% Nb, the strength became higher than that of
either the fcc or L1
2 single-phase alloys. This is attributed to precipitation hardening. However,
precipitation hardening was not effective at 1800°C. For reference, in Figure 6 we also show the
strength of the alloys heated at 1800°C for 72 hours. Their strength at testing temperatures was
equivalent to that of the 15at% Nb samples heat treated at 1200°C. However, the strength was lower
than that of the 17 and 20at% Nb samples heat-treated at 1200°C. The strength difference increased with
increasing Nb content.
We estimated the precipitation hardening at room temperature and at 1200°C from our measured
compressive strengths of the alloys. First, we estimated the solid solution hardening of the two-phase
alloys. The contribution of solid-solution hardening to the alloy strength can be estimated by using the
mixture law for composite materials, as a linear combination of the strengths of the fcc matrix and of
the L1
2 precipitates. This is shown by the dotted lines in Fig. 6a. Figure 6a also indicates that the
difference between our measured strength and the strength due to solid-solution hardening is due to
precipitation hardening. Figure 6b indicates that precipitation hardening depends on the precipitate
volume fraction in the Ir-Nb alloys. When the volume fraction of the precipitates was about 50%,
precipitation hardening was maximized. The precipitation hardening was larger at room temperature
than at 1200°C. We did not plot precipitation hardening at 1800°C, because precipitation hardening was
not clearly observed at 1800°C.
Discussion
Precipitate Morphology
The precipitate shape is determined by minimizing the sum of the elastic strain energy of the lattice
misfit between the matrix and precipitate, and of the interfacial energy. In Ni-based superalloys, the
precipitate shape is cuboidal, with cube faces along the ^001& elastically soft directions in both the
matrix and precipitate phases. Although we do not have data for the elastic constants of Ir
3Nb alloys,

similar to Ni-based superalloys, we assumed that in Ir-Nb alloys, Ir 3Nb precipitates are surrounded by
cube faces along the ^001& elastically soft directions.
After heat treatment at 1800°C for 168 hours, cuboidal or rectangular precipitates aligned along the
^001& cube direction. Miyazaki et al. observed that in Ni-15at% Al single-crystals cuboidal precipitates
are not uniformly distributed, but are localized in rows along the ^001& directions (10). They explained
this arrangement using Yamauchi et al’s calculation of the elastic interaction energy between two
spherical particles in highly anisotropic matrices (11). According to Yamauchi et al’s calculation, the
sphere inclusions under strong elastic interactions should be the most stable in the “simple cubic
structure arrangement”. However simple cubic structure arrangements require the precipitates to have
high volume fraction. Miyazaki et al. suggested that in Ni-Al alloys the ideal volume fraction is larger
than 42%. If the volume fraction of precipitates is less than this ideal value, cuboidal precipitates should
be forced to be locally congregated into rows along the ^100& cube directions. The phase diagrams for
Ir-Nb alloys indicate that the volume fraction of the precipitates at 1800°C is about 33%. Thus
precipitates aligned locally along the cube direction. Similar observations were made in Ni-Ti-Al alloys
by Peters et al. (12) and by Murakami et al. (13). Cuboidal
b9-Ni2TiAl precipitates were formed in
arrays along the ^100& directions from the supersaturated b2-NiTi phase. By heat treatment at 1800°C
for 168 hours, the fcc channel size increased. This was caused both by elastic interactions and by
precipitate coarsening.
We observed the fcc phase in cuboidal L1
2 precipitates after heat treatment at 1500°C for 168 hours.
Miyazaki et al. and Doi et al. found that cuboidal L1 2 precipitates in Ni-based alloys split into two
plates, with a progressive increase in size (14,15). They observed a splitting sequence as follows; first
a rod-shaped matrix phase was formed in the center of their cube, then the rod widened along the {100}
planes, and finally this growth reached the side surfaces. The second sequence was similar to the
microstructure we observed (Fig. 3b). The appearance of splitting depends on whether or not the elastic
interaction energy between the plates overcomes the change in surface energy due to splitting. When
precipitates have a low interfacial energy density and a large mismatch with the lattice spacing, the
matrix has a tendency to split. Because the interfacial energy of the L1
2 phase is extremely low (16),
the L12 phase with large lattice misfit tends to split. Although it is not clear from the morphology of
Ir3Nb precipitates in Ir-Nb alloys whether or not splitting occurs, this morphology must be affected by
elastic effects. Another possibility is that these needle-like fcc phases are formed from supersaturated
L1
2 phases during cooling.
Three kinds of precipitate morphology were observed at 1200, 1500, and 1800°C in the Ir-15at%Nb
alloy. We are currently investigating the formation mechanism of the precipitate morphology at each
heat-treatment temperature.
Deformation Behavior
In previous studies, although we tried to observe the dislocation structure in Ir-15at%Nb alloys
deformed at room temperature and at 1200°C, we did not clearly observe evidence of bypass or shearing
mechanisms (8). In this study, the strengths of the samples heat treated at 1200 and 1500°C were almost
equivalent, except for the strength at room temperature. If the alloy deforms by shearing, the strength
of the samples heat treated at 1500°C will be higher than those heat treated at 1200°C, because
dislocations will meet not only coherent interfaces between L1
2 precipitates and the fcc matrix, but also
coherent interfaces from the fcc phase in the L1 2 precipitates. The equivalent strength of both samples
heat treated at 1200 and 1500°C shows that Ir-Nb alloys deform by the bypass mechanism. The
precipitate size of both samples was the same, from 100 to 200 nm. Bypass effects seem to be similar
in both samples. At room temperature, the strength of the sample heated at 1200°C was higher than that
of the sample heated at 1500°C. This is because in the sample heated at 1200°C, fine precipitates

formed during cooling strengthen the alloy. At 1200°C, these cooling precipitates are dissolved in the
matrix, thus there is no large difference between strengths of samples heated at 1200 and 1500°C.
The strengths of samples heat treated at 1800°C for 168 hours were lower than those at lower
heat-treatment temperatures because of large, wide fcc channels. Dislocations do not need to bypass
precipitates and can move even if confined to the fcc matrix. On the other hand, the strength of the
samples heat treated at 1800°C for 72 hours were equivalent to the strengths of the sample heat treated
at 1200°C, because of uniformly distributed 200-nm spherical precipitates.
Precipitation hardening of the samples heated at 1800°C for 72 hours was lower than that of the
samples heated at 1200°C with increasing Nb contents. This is because the coherency of the spherical
precipitates may be lost or spherical precipitates may grow faster and become larger in the alloys with
higher Nb content.
Conclusions
We altered the precipitate morphology by heat treatment of Ir-Nb alloys. The alloy strength depended
on the precipitate morphology. When cuboidal precipitates were localized in rows along the ^001& cube
directions, the strength was lower than that of samples with cuboidal precipitates distributed uniformly,
because of large, wide fcc channels. When plate-like or needle-like fcc phases were formed in cuboidal
L1
2 precipitates, there was no large difference in the strength from that of samples with uniformly
distributed cuboidal precipitates. This shows that Ir-Nb alloys deform by the bypass mechanism.
