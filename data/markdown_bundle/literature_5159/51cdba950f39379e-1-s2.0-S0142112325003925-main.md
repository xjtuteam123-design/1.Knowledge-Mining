ABSTRACT
The development of high-temperature titanium alloys for aerospace propulsion systems requires good thermo -
stability and fatigue resistance under high temperature loading conditions. This study comparatively investigated 
the microstructural characteristics and fatigue performance of Ti-6Al-4Zr-4Mo-2Sn-1W-0.2Si duplex titanium 
alloys fabricated respectively via laser-directed energy deposition additive manufacturing (AM) and conven -
tional forging technologies. The results revealed that the forged titanium alloy contained fine grains with a 
heterogeneously duplex structure consisting of large-size near-spherical primary α and hard β
t 
phases. By 
contrast, the AMed titanium alloy contained columnar grains with an interior homogenous basketweave struc -
ture containing a few unstable high angle boundaries (HABs) and a high ratio of low angle boundaries (LABs). 
Although the room-temperature tensile strengths of two types of alloys were similar (~1100 MPa), the AMed 
titanium alloy suggested superior high temperature mechanical properties with 8 % higher strength retention at 
550 
◦
C than the forged alloy (882 vs. 811 MPa). With an increase of total strain amplitude ( Δ ε
t
), the fatigue life of 
the specimen was prone to decreasing, associated with the declined texture intensity and activity of basal slip 
system of (0 0 0 1) [1 1 2 0]. Under the same Δ ε
t 
value of 0.6 %, the high temperature fatigue lives of the AMed 
alloys were 6 – 7 times longer than the forged counterparts. Fractographic analysis of the fatigue specimens 
revealed that surface slip cracking accompanied by oxide formation served as the predominant fatigue crack 
initiation mechanism, followed by striation patterns observed in crack propagation paths. Compared to the 
forged alloy, the higher fatigue properties of the AMed titanium alloy were mainly attributed to a higher ther -
mostability and optimum microstructure design (fewer HABs and high ratio of LABs) resisting fatigue cracking. 
These findings offer a guide for the fabrication of next-generation titanium alloys requiring simultaneous high- 
temperature strength and fatigue resistance in aerospace applications.
1. Introduction
High-temperature titanium alloys represent a unique class of tita -
nium alloys engineered for sustained operation at temperatures ranges 
from 350 to 600 
◦
C, exhibiting significant microstructural stability, 
exceptional specific strength, enhanced creep durability and superior 
fatigue resistance [ 1 , 2 ]. These characteristics have critical application in 
aerospace propulsion systems, especially in the manufacturing of 
compressor blades, rotor disks, and other thermally loading components 
in gas turbine engines [ 3 ]. When deployed as rotating components in 
high-pressure compressor assemblies, these titanium alloy components 
experience simultaneous thermal exposure and cyclic loading, resulting 
in a high risk of fatigue failure during service. Therefore, optimizing the 
high temperature fatigue resistance of these alloys would not only 
improve service safety but also extend life of components in aerospace 
propulsion systems.

Ti-Al-Mo-Zr-Sn-W-Si (i.e., TC25G) is a series of duplex high- 
temperature titanium alloys, mainly used in high-pressure compres -
sors at temperatures up to 550 
◦
C. Microstructural design and alloy 
composition optimization have emerged as critical strategies for 
enhancing fatigue resistance in these types of high-temperature titanium 
alloys where thermal stability fundamentally governs material behavior 
[ 4 ]. Recent advances in alloy development demonstrated that compo -
sitional modifications could effectively enhance phase stability. Alloying 
titanium with elements, such as W, Mo, Zr and Sn, can enhance its high 
temperature mechanical properties and oxidation resistance. Specially, 
the addition of W and Mo can reduce the self-diffusional rate in the β 
phase limiting dislocation climb and annihilation, thereby improving 
the thermostability of the β phase [ 3 ]. Experimental results obtained by 
He et al. [ 5 ] demonstrated that 4 % Zr element and 2 % Sn addition 
increased α -phase stability under high temperature loading through the 
solid solution strengthening mechanism. Si typically appears in alloys in 
the form of solid solution or silicide. Notably, Si dissolved in high 
temperature titanium alloys was found to promote solid solution 
strengthening, while silicide could strengthen the material through 
precipitation strengthening. Zhuo et al . [ 6 ] alloyed Si elements in tita -
nium alloys, which promoted the formation of Ti
5
Si
3 
nano-size pre -
cipitates, providing Zener pinning forces that simultaneously enhanced 
thermostability and creep resistance.
In addition to alloy composition effect, initial microstructure plays 
crucial role in the thermostability and high-temperature fatigue per -
formance alloys [ 7 , 8 ]. Zhang et al. [ 9 ] studied the effect of initial 
microstructure on the hot deformation behavior of TC25G titanium al -
loys and found that the flow stress of the initial basketweave micro -
structure was greater than that of the equiaxed microstructure at strain 
rates of 0.01 and 0.1 s
 1
. For the deformation mechanism, the lamellar 
primary α phase underwent separation along the α / α boundary, and 
dynamic spheroidization occurred as the boundary separation spher -
oidization phase. Deformation of the specimens with a basketweave 
microstructure was primarily attributed to the distortion of the lamellar 
primary α phase. The secondary α phase present within the β phase 
served to reinforce the material, impeding dislocation movement. By 
contrast, deformation occurred in the equiaxed microstructure speci -
mens in the primary α and β phases, and the alloy exhibited low resis -
tance to deformation due to recrystallization of the β phase [ 9 ]. As 
demonstrated by Doherty et al. [ 10 ], the phase stability of alloys was 
found to be intrinsically linked to grain boundary characteristics. Spe -
cifically, low angle boundaries (LABs, 2 – 15
◦
misorientation) exhibited 
lower boundary energy and higher stable condition, while high angle 
boundaries (HABs, > 15
◦
misorientation) possessed higher boundary 
energy and ~3000 times greater mobility than LABs. The LABs and 
HABs both contributed to fatigue resistance by impeding dislocation slip 
and suppressing crack initiation [ 11 ]. Cui et al. [ 12 ] further demon -
strated that LABs could effectively impede fatigue crack propagation 
and induce crack path deflection during interaction. These results sug -
gested that transforming heterogeneous duplex structures into homo -
geneous microstructures with high LAB density could enhance the high- 
temperature fatigue resistance of commercial titanium alloys.
Although heterostructures have been proposed to enhance tensile 
properties [ 13 ], their effect on improving fatigue performance remains 
unclear. In forged titanium alloys with duplex microstructures, dislo -
cations within the soft primary α phase will preferentially accumulate at 
the α / β phase boundaries, specifically at interfaces between the ductile 
primary α phase and hardened transformed β phase, inducing localized 
stress concentrations and microcrack nucleation at these interfaces [ 14 ]. 
Hu et al. [ 15 ] demonstrated that homogenous basketweave structures 
had a significantly higher fatigue strength than heterogeneously duplex 
structures for high temperature titanium alloys. Nevertheless, the 
mechanistic relationship between microstructural characteristics, espe -
cially boundary characteristics, and the high-temperature fatigue per -
formance of titanium alloys fabricated via different manufacturing 
processes still needs to be determined.
In this study, we employed laser-directed energy deposition (LDED) 
additive manufacturing (AM) technology to fabricate Ti-6Al-4Mo-4Zr- 
2Sn-1W-0.2Si titanium alloys with a homogenous basketweave micro -
structure containing a high ratio of LABs ( Fig. 1 ). A systematic analysis 
on microstructures (boundary characteristics) and 550 
◦
C high- 
temperature fatigue properties was further carried out. Finally, the ef -
fect of microstructure on the high-temperature fatigue properties was 
elucidated.
2. Materials and experimental procedures
The Ti-6Al-4Mo-4Zr-2Sn-1W-0.2Si titanium alloy ingot was melted 
using a 650 kg vacuum consumable furnace in triplicate with the 
chemical compositions shown in Table 1 . After melting, the ingot was 
held at 1100 
◦
C for 4 h, then forged into a rod billet φ 65 mm × 1.5 m in 
size at a temperature of at least 900 
◦
C through repeated upsetting and 
drawing. Then, the φ 65 mm diameter rod was were cut into two parts. 
One part was subjected to double annealing treatment: consisting of 
isothermal treatment at 950 
◦
C for 2 h, air cooling (AC), and subsequent 
annealing at 540 
◦
C for 6 h, under AC. Meanwhile, the other part was 
used to prepare pre-alloyed powders for LDED via the plasma rotating 
electrode method. The prepared powders were screened to ensure that 
the particle diameter was in the range of 53 – 150 μ m. Notably, an in -
crease in oxgen content (~90 ppm) occurred in the pre-alloyed powders 
compared to the ingot. The TA2 titanium plate 250 (length) × 150 
(width) × 15 mm (height) in size served as the base plate, which was 
surface-machined, degreased and dried. In an argon-gas protective 
environment with < 50 ppm oxygen content, bulk alloy specimens 150 
(length) × 50 (width) × 80 mm (height) size was built on the TA2 ti -
tanium plate using LDED technology ( Fig. 1 ). The tensile and fatigue 
specimens were cut along the deposition direction of the AMed bulk and 
length direction of the individually forged rod.
Rod-like tensile smooth specimens M6 × 43 mm in size and a 
working part diameter of 3 mm were prepared and tested according to 
the GB/T 228.2-2021 standard. Room temperature and 550 
◦
C high- 
temperature tensile tests were conducted using a CMT 4304 electronic 
universal testing machine with a heating furnace at a strain rate of 
0.002 s
 1

of three specimens. In addition, standard cylindrical fatigue smooth 
specimens with size of M14 × 72 mm and the working part diameter of 
φ 6 mm were prepared and tested according to the GB/T 15248-2008 
standard. The axis directions of these fatigue specimens were also 
located along the deposition direction of the AMed bulk and the length 
direction of the individually forged rod. Fatigue tests were carried out in 
a strain control mode at a constant load frequency of 0.5 Hz on a Instron 
8801 machine at a temperature of 550 
◦
C. Strain amplitudes of 0.6 %, 
0.7 %, 1.2 %, 1.6 % and 2.0 % were subsequently applied in fatigue 
specimens. The load waveform consisted of a triangular wave, with the 
load method consisting of tension – compression symmetry (strain ratio 
R = 1). Three specimens were tested under each test condition, with 
the fatigue properties of the alloy material given by the intermediate 
values of the three measurements.

3. Results
3.1. Tensile properties
Fig. 2 presents the engineering stress – strain curves of both titanium 
alloys under tensile loading at room temperature and elevated temper -
ature (550 
◦
C). As shown in Fig. 2 A, the conventionally forged alloy 
exhibited an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 1107 ± 8 MPa accom -
panied by 11.0 ± 0.5 % elongation at room temperature, while the 
additively manufactured (AM) alloy exhibited a comparable strength 
(1092 ± 15 MPa) but significantly lower ductility (8.5 ± 0.5 %). 
Notably, this trend reversed at elevated temperatures ( Fig. 2 B). The 
AMed alloy also demonstrated enhanced tensile strength (882 ± 16 
MPa) coupled with improved elongation (16.0 ± 1.0 %) compared to the 
forged counterpart (811 ± 14 MPa and 15.5 ± 0.5 %, respectively). 
These results suggested that both the strength and ductility of the AMed 
alloy were superior to the forged alloy at elevated temperatures.

3.4. Fracture morphologies
The fracture surface morphologies of the titanium alloy specimens 
after 550 
◦
C fatigue testing are shown in Figs. 11 and 12 . Three typical 
zones were observed, namely, crack initiation zone, crack propagation 
zone, and final rupture zone. We noted that crack initiation zones were 
distributed in the circumferential surfaces of the broken specimens with 
multiple-zone cracking characteristics. With an increase in total strain 
amplitude, more crack initiation zones appeared, which was correlated 
with an intensity in surface cracking. On the surface of the broken 
specimens, extrusion ridges and invasion grooves were observed, indi -
cating the surface slip cracking mechanism of fatigue crack initiation. As 
shown in Fig. 12 E and F, many slip characteristics were observed on the 
specimen surfaces, with micro-crack distribution along the radial di -
rection of the specimens. At low total strain amplitude (i.e., Δ ε
t 
= 0.6 
%), as shown in Fig. 12 H, a relatively high content of aluminum and 
oxygen was detected. Therefore, aluminum oxide formed in the local 
broken surface sites compared to surrounding titanium-containing oxide 
films, which could further accelerate crack initiation and propagation in 
the early period of fatigue cracking. In Addition, numerous fatigue 
striations were observed in both the forged and AMed fatigue broken 
specimens ( Fig. 13 ), indicating cyclic plastic blunting and a resharpen -
ing mechanism.
4. Discussion
Among the different processing technologies, high-temperature ti -
tanium alloys exhibit markedly different microstructures and boundary 
characteristics, resulting in variations in high-temperature fatigue

4.1.4. The ratio of LABs
The absolute number evaluation of LABs ( Fig. 5 C and D) suggested 
that the number of LABs in the AMed alloy was lower than the forged 
alloy. In addition, the primary LABs in both alloys were mainly related 
to α lath boundaries ( Figs. 5 and 6 ). Dixit et al. [ 27 ] found that increased 
cooling rates during phase transformation promoted α -lath refinement 
through intensified lattice strain accumulation. Considering the severe 
thermal accumulation effect of high power continuous laser heat input, 
the cooling rate of AM processing was relatively low. Consequently, the 
AMed specimens had coarser α -laths ( Fig. 6 A and B) with reduced 
interfacial density, resulting in decreased LABs amount compared to 
forged counterparts. Notably, normalized LAB frequency analysis pre -
sented an inverse trend, revealing 58.3 % LAB proportions in the AMed 
alloy versus 31.5 % in forged alloy. The result was converse to analyzing 
basing on the absolute number of LABs. Although the absolute number 
of LABs in the AMed alloy was only slightly lower than the forged alloy, 
the total number of boundaries in the AMed alloy was significantly lower 
than the forged alloy, therefore the ratio of LABs in the AMed alloy was 
higher than the forged alloy. The above analysis indicated the aim of 
increasing the ratio of LABs and minimizing the number of HABs in the 
titanium alloy could be achieved via LDED AM technology.

In the high-temperature service condition of 550

AMed alloys was apparently higher than that of the forged alloys 
( Fig. 3 A). This result was related to a lower ε
p 
value for the AMed alloy 
( Fig. 4 F), suggesting a lower plastic damage under cyclic load. This 
lower ε

of the AMed alloy could be attributed to its higher peak stress 
compared to the forged alloy ( Fig. 3 B – F). According to the peak stress- 
cycle curves, although at initial several cycles, the AMed alloy and 
forged alloy possibly had a similar peak stress, where the forged alloy 
showed evident strain softening associated with an increase in fatigue 
cycles, while the AMed alloy showed no obvious strain softening and 
even strain hardening ( Fig. 3 E and F). Consequently, the AMed alloy 
always had a higher peak stress. Microstructure observations after fa -
tigue tests indicated that strain softening in the forged alloy was due to 
its poor microstructural thermostability. Evident coarsening of both α 
and β
t 
grains was observed ( Fig. 5 A, E; Fig. 7 A, B and I, J), and α laths in 
the interior of the β

In comparison, the AMed alloy had high thermostability during high- 
temperature fatigue tests, and neither the size of β

By comparing the process-affected microstructure effect, the high 
temperature fatigue cracking resistance in the forged alloy was lower 
than in the AMed alloy due to the heterogeneous microstructure of the 
forged alloy with a high ratio of HABs. Firstly, the forged alloy exhibited 
significantly finer equiaxed β grains compared to the coarse columnar β 
grains present in the additive manufactured (AMed) alloy. Thus, HABs- 
including β grain boundaries and grain-interior α colony boundary- may 
be more prevalent in the forged alloy. Although numerous HABs in the 
forged alloy could contribute to the high room temperature strength of 
the alloy, it significantly decreased its strength at elevated temperatures 
( Fig. 2 ). This was because the high temperature strength of prior β grain 
boundaries in titanium alloy was usually lower than the strength of the 
matrix phase [ 29 ]. According to the research results of Mazanova et. al. 
[ 30 ], a necessary condition of fatigue crack initiation was the formation 
of extrusion ridges and invasion grooves on the specimen surface. A 
lower high temperature strength of the forged alloy than the AMed alloy 
facilitated the formation of extrusion ridges and invasion grooves. As a 
result, fatigue cracks more easily initiated in the forged alloy than in the 
AMed alloy. Secondly, the forged alloy had a heterogeneous ( α + β
t
) 
microstructure. Microstructure observations following high-

vated the soft α phase due to the role of cyclic loads ( Fig. 16 ), which 
piled up in front of the hard β
t 
phase boundaries, give rise to stress 
concentration and even crack initiation.
5. Conclusions
This study comparatively investigated the Ti-6Al-4Mo-4Zr-2Sn-1W- 
0.2Si titanium alloy fabricated via conventional forging and LDED AM 
technologies. The main findings were summarized as follows. 
The forged titanium alloy exhibited a heterogeneous duplex micro -
structure comprising soft large-sized primary α phases and hard β
t 
phases. By contrast, the AMed titanium alloy had a homogeneous 
basketweave microstructure characterized by significantly fewer 
high-angle grain boundaries (HABs) and a substantially higher pro -
portion of low-angle boundaries (LABs).
