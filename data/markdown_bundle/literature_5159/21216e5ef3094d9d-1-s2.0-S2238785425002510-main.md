ABSTRACT
This study investigates the influence of Cu content on the microstructure and properties of AlCu
x
CrTiV (x = 0.4, 
1.0, 1.7) high-entropy alloys (HEAs) fabricated via vacuum arc melting. Advanced characterization techniques 
were used to analyze the alloy ’ s composition, microstructure, mechanical performance, and corrosion resistance. 
The results show that the alloys primarily consist of BCC, FCC, and HCP phases. With increasing Cu content, the 
HCP phase volume fraction increases, while grain size decreases. Mechanical testing reveals a slight reduction in 
hardness but significant improvements in strength and fracture strain. The AlCu
1.7
CrTiV alloy achieves a 
compressive strength of 1688 MPa, a yield strength of 1443 MPa, and a fracture strain of 17%. Electrochemical 
tests demonstrate that higher Cu content reduces corrosion current density, increases corrosion potential, and 
enhances charge transfer resistance, thereby improving corrosion resistance. Overall, Cu promotes HCP phase 
formation, optimizes the soft-hard phase balance, and enhances ductility while maintaining high strength. 
Furthermore, Cu bonding strength significantly impacts corrosion resistance. These findings provide valuable 
insights for the optimization and practical application of AlCu
x
CrTiV HEAs.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have garnered significant attention in 
the field of metallic materials due to their exceptional mechanical 
properties and superior corrosion resistance, making them highly 
promising for applications in aerospace, energy, and marine engineering 
[ 1 – 6 ]. Unlike conventional alloys, HEAs consist of multiple principal 
elements in near-equimolar ratios, leading to complex alloying effects 
that endow them with remarkable mechanical, thermal, and electro -
chemical properties. The diverse phase structures formed by HEAs, 
driven by their intricate chemical compositions and interactions, play a 
crucial role in determining their microstructure and performance [ 7 – 9 ]. 
By tailoring elemental ratios, researchers can optimize the phase 
composition, crystal structure, and mechanical and electrochemical 
behavior of HEAs [ 10 – 12 ].
The choice of elements is a critical factor influencing the micro -
structure and properties of HEAs [ 13 – 15 ]. Elements such as Al, Ti, Cr, 
and V are often used to reduce density, while Cu is frequently added for 
its ability to promote FCC phase formation [ 16 – 19 ]. Cu acts as a flux that 
enhances plasticity and influences hardness, wear resistance, and 
corrosion resistance. Cai [ 20 ] demonstrated that incorporating Cu im -
proves the wear resistance, hardness, and oxidation resistance of 
FeCoCrNiCu
x 
HEAs. Tong [ 21 ] found that Cu, Co, and Ni promote FCC 
phase formation in AlCuCoCrFeNi HEAs, whereas Al and Cr favor BCC 
phase development. Zhu [ 22 ] reported that Cu ’ s solid-solution 
strengthening mechanism enhances mechanical properties, but exces -
sive Cu leads to Cu-rich FCC phase formation due to its limited solubi -
lity. Furthermore, Cu has been shown to induce pre-solidification liquid 
phase separation, resulting in supersaturated solid solutions and nano -
scale Cu-rich precipitates, thereby improving the strength of CoCrFe -
MoNiCu
x 
HEAs [ 23 – 25 ].
In terms of corrosion resistance, numerous studies have investigated 
the role of Cu in HEAs, particularly in 3.5% NaCl solutions [ 26 – 29 ]. Xi 
[ 30 ] observed that increasing Cu content reduces the pitting resistance 
of Cu-containing stainless steel. Singh [ 31 ] reported that during the 
melting of AlCuCoCrFeNi HEAs, Cu tends to precipitate from the matrix,

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Material preparation
AlCu
x
CrTiV HEAs were fabricated using vacuum arc melting with 
pure metal particles ( ≥ 99.9 wt%). The button ingots, with a diameter of 
approximately 30 mm and a mass of about 40 g, were produced using a 
stepwise melting process. Metals with similar physical properties were 
melted first, followed by the sequential addition of other raw materials 
for remelting [ 32 ]. To ensure homogeneity, each master alloy was 
remelted five times. The vacuum degree of vacuum arc melting is on the 
order of 10 ⁻ ³ Pa to 10 ⁻⁴ Pa, with a power supply of 100 kW.
2.2. Microstructural characterization
Phase identification and crystal structure analysis were performed 
using an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima IV), using Cu K α rays as 
the X-ray source, with a wavelength of 1.5418 Å, a voltage of 40 kV, a 
current of 40 mA. The microstructure, fracture surfaces and post- 
corrosion surfaces were analyzed using a scanning electron micro -
scope (SEM) equipped with an Oxford X-ray energy dispersive spec -
trometer (EDS) operating at 20 kV. Compositional and morphological 
analysis was conducted using a JEOL JAX-8100 electron probe micro -
analyzer (EPMA) with wavelength dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) under 
an accelerating current of 20 nA and a voltage of 15 kV.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Talos F200X) was used for 
phase structure analysis via selected area electron diffraction (SAED) 
and high-resolution imaging at 200 kV. TEM samples were prepared 
using focused ion beam (FIB) techniques, with regions of approximately 
5 × 5 μ m and a thickness of < 50 nm. Fourier and inverse Fourier 
transforms, processed using DigitalMicrograph software, were used to 
extract crystallographic information and calculate dislocation density.
Phase composition, structure, distribution, and grain size were 
further analyzed using a JSM-7900F SEM equipped with an electron 
backscatter diffraction (EBSD) probe operating at 20 kV. EBSD samples 
were prepared using a vibration polishing method.

The EBSD results demonstrate that as Cu content increases, the 
average grain size of the alloys gradually decreases, while the BCC phase

There is a close correlation between mechanical properties, corrosion 
resistance, and grain size. Smaller grain size can bring higher strength to 
alloys. According to the Hall Petch formula, the fine grain strengthening 
effect is significant, and dislocations are obstructed at grain boundaries, 
improving the material ’ s ability to resist deformation. Uniform and fine 
grains contribute to the formation of a denser and more stable passiv -
ation film, reducing the intrusion of corrosive media into channels and 
enhancing corrosion resistance; And the segregation of coarse grains and 
grain boundary impurities can easily become the starting point of 
corrosion.
