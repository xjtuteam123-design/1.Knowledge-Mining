abstract
The coarse-grained Fe e10Ale4Cre4Y2O3ODS nanocomposite (denoted as FeAlOY) has been
developed by the authors and shows promising potential for high-temperature structural
applications at 1000 e1300 /C14 C. Compared to classical ODS alloys, the FeAlOY contains ten
times higher volume fraction of the stable Y 2O3 nanodispersion, which gives the alloy its
high-temperature strength. Furthermore, the high content of Al in the matrix guarantees
excellent oxidation resistance. In practice, one can expect that the FeAlOY is loaded in the
temperature range of 20 e1300
/C14 C due to intermittent device operation. To ensure a safe
operation, it is necessary to determine the tensile strength and ductility of the FeAlOY in
the whole temperature range and detect the dominant mechanisms of strengthening,
plastic deformation, and fracture in the characteristic temperature ranges. Above 1100
/C14 C
the FeAlOY reaches ultimate tensile strength of /C24 100 MPa and plasticity of /C24 1%. However,
in the temperature range of 400 e600 /C14 C, the plasticity can climb above 40%. The achieved
results can also be utilized for the design of the FeAlOY pieces shaping by hot pressing.

1. Introduction
Strengthening of metallic materials by dispersion of ﬁne par-
ticles as precipitates is one of the most effective ways of
improving their mechanical properties [ 1e4]. Majority of al-
loys like steels are strengthened by carbides, nitrides and
carbonitrides M
23C6,M 6C, M 7C3, MX and M 2X, where M de-
notes a metallic element and X represents C or N or their
mixture, and/or by intermetallic compounds such as the
Fe
2(Mo,W) Laves phase [ 5e8]. Austenitic nickel-based super-
alloys are mostly strengthened by ordered g’ precipitates [9].

steels, nanooxides as Y2O3 or mixed YeAleTi based oxides are
dispersed in the matrix [ 10e12].
The interstitial elements such as C and N can be dissolved
easily in austenite and the carbides and nitrides can inten-
sively precipitate after austenite to ferrite transformation, as
the solubility of C and N in ferrite is very low. The heat
treatment signiﬁcantly inﬂuences the precipitate
morphology, and the resultant microstructure and mechani-
cal properties of materials depend signiﬁcantly on the way of
thermomechanical treatment [ 13,14]. In steels, the existence
of precipitates is limited by the temperature of the ferrite to
austenite transformation as in austenite the precipitates
dissolve and the strength drops signiﬁcantly [ 1e3].
To achieve the dispersion strengthening at temperatures
higher than that of typical ferrite to austenite transformation
in steels, austenitic nickel-based superalloys can be consid-
ered. They are strengthened by ordered g
0 precipitates with
usual volume fraction of several tens of % up to 70%. It is
typical for nickel-based superalloys that they keep a rather
high strengths level up to about 800
/C14 C, for which the g0 pre-
cipitates are still chemically and morphologically stable, and
then they lose their strength rather quickly with increasing
temperature due to coarsening or even dissolution of the g’
precipitates [9,15,16].
To achieve the dispersion strengthening in an alloy for a
much wider range of temperatures up to 1300
/C14 C, the
strengthening by oxide dispersion seems to be the only pos-
sibility. The ODS alloys are investigated since 1960 's[ 17e21].
Originally, they were developed for nuclear applications as the
matrix/oxide interfaces are able to absorb He atoms and so the
swelling due to He bubbles formation is signiﬁcantly sup-
pressed [21e23]. As the oxide dispersion in advanced ODS al-
loys is nanosized, typically 5 e20 nm, a rather low volume
fraction of 0.5% is sufﬁcient to an effective strengthening of
the alloy. Moreover, the attractive interaction between the
dislocations and nanooxides leads to speciﬁc properties of the
ODS alloys. Although dislocations easily climb the nanooxide
at high temperatures, they can remain stuck at the back sur-
face of the nanooxides [ 24]. The detachment of dislocations
requires a certain force, which corresponds to the existence of
a threshold stress in high temperature creep, under which the
creep rate drops to immeasurable values [ 25e27]. This gua-
rantees long-term shape stability of the parts loaded below
the threshold even at temperatures quite near to the melting
point of the matrix. It, however, holds true only for stable
coarse-grained ODS alloys, where recrystallization and diffu-
sional creep is effectively suppressed.
It is not possible to dissolve enough O in the liquid or solid
alloy and provoke precipitation of oxides by lowering the
temperature, as it is the case of C and N in steels, because the
oxides are chemically stable up to very high temperatures.
That is why ODS alloys are produced by applying the powder
metallurgy consisting in following steps.
1. The ODS powder is produced by mechanical alloying (MA)
of the input powders or granulates. During the MA the balls
collide, and small particles of powder attached to the ball
surface are forged onto the balls to form growing pro-
trusions. Large protrusions break off as large particles,
which are again gradually crushed to small ones. These
two mutually coupled processes are repeated and the
powder becomes more and more homogenized. In the
early studies, such as e.g. [ 18], it is demonstrated that ﬁne
oxide powder particles, such as TiO
2, MgO or Al2O3 are only
homogeneously dispersed in the matrix during the MA. In
some later studies, such as e.g. [ 28], it is shown that Y
2O3 is
fragmented and homogeneously dispersed in the matrix
during the MA. Recent studies demonstrate that Y 2O3 can
be completely dissolved in the matrix during late stages of
MA and the O and Y atoms are trapped at drastically
multiplied defects as dislocations and vacancies [ 29e32]
and/or form clusters [ 33e35]. The degree of dissolution of
Y
2O3 depends critically on the time and conditions of MA.
The Y 2O3 re-precipitation is then conditioned by a signiﬁ-
cant decrease in defect density. The mechanical properties
and microstructure of resultant ODS steels signiﬁcantly
depend on chemical composition as well as consolidation
and thermomechanical treatment after the MA. In partic-
ular, the ratio of O to stable-oxide-forming elements plays
an important role in volume fraction of the nanooxides and
their stability. Ohtsuka et al. reported that the volume
fraction and chemical composition of nanooxides can be
controlled by the additions of Fe
2O3 or Fe eY intermetallic
[36]. The optimization of the conditions of MA was studied
in detail very recently [ 37].

2. The canned ODS powder can be consolidated by spark
plasma sintering [ 38], hot isostatic pressing [ 39,40] or hot
forming as rolling [ 41e43], pressing [ 44], extrusion
[40,45,46], rotary swaging [47e49], forging [41,50] or by their
combinations [ 51e54]. Spark plasma sintering or hot
isostatic pressing may lead to already completed stable
microstructure with medium-sized grains strengthened by
oxide nanodispersion [ 55]. After consolidation by hot
forming methods applied at signiﬁcantly lower tempera-
tures, the microstructure usually consists of ultra-ﬁne
grains strengthened by nanooxide dispersion and still a
huge density of defects. Such an ODS alloy is hard and
brittle and may contain sufﬁcient stored energy to drive
secondary recrystallization [ 47,56,57].
3. The secondary recrystallization can take place if the
driving force overcomes the pinning of grain boundaries by
the nanodispersoid. In contrast, to achieve a coarse-
grained microstructure, the driving force cannot be too
large. Then only a very small fraction of grains with the
best conditions overcome the pinning effect. They grow to
large sizes on the account of original surrounding ultra-ﬁne
grains before they get in touch with other large, recrystal-
lized grains and the recrystallization is completed [ 58,59].
The grain morphology after secondary recrystallization
can be inﬂuenced by both chemical composition as well as
conditions of hot consolidation [ 58,59].
It is surprising that most studies are devoted to ODS alloys
and their mechanical properties at temperatures of about
800
/C14 C[ 11,60e62] although the strength of cheaper Ni-based
superalloys is often 3 e4 times higher at these temperatures
[16,63e65]. However, the application temperature of 1200 /C14 C
represents an unsurpassable limit for nickel-based superal-
loys [9]. In contrast, the ODS alloys keep a signiﬁcant strength
up to 1300
/C14 C and the ‘crossing’ temperature with nickel-based
superalloys is about 1100 /C14 C. The research dealing with
properties and applicability of ODS alloys at temperatures of
1000e1300 /C14 C is relatively sporadic, see, e.g. [ 66,67].
The Fee10Ale4Cre4Y2O3 (in wt.%) is a creep and oxidation
resistant ODS alloy developed by the authors, and further
denoted as FeAlOY. It has already demonstrated its promising
potential for structural applications in the temperature range
of1100e1300
/C14 C[ 37,47,56,57,59,68e70]. The FeAlOY contains
about ten times more Y 2O3 nanodispersoid (5 vol.%) than
conventional ODS alloys, which justiﬁes calling the FeAlOY a
nanocomposite. The FeAlOY is strengthened by nano-
dispersion of pure Y
2O3, which seems to be the chemically
most stable phase being most resistant against coarsening
[69,71]. Moreover, the FeAlOY has an appreciable resistance
against oxidation up to 1400
/C14 C due to formation of compact
surface layer formed by Al 2O3 and YAG (Al 5Y3O12) grains [ 68].
The primary intended area of use of the FeAlOY are ap-
plications in the temperature range of 1100e1300 /C14 C. However,
the FeAlOY will also be loaded during intermittent device
operation. To guarantee a safe device operation under all
conditions, we aim to determine tensile strength and ductility
of the FeAlOY in the temperature range of 20 e1300
/C14 C. More-
over, the dominant mechanisms of plastic deformation,
strengthening and fracture in each characteristic temperature
range are analyzed in detail.

3.2. Microstructure characterization
Fig. 5 shows results of EBSD analysis of As Received (AR) states
of the FeAlOY, before the tensile tests and Figs. 6e8 show re-
sults of EBSD analysis of specimens after the tensile tests. The
AR states of the same batch used for tensile tests at strain rate
of 10
/C0 3 s/C0 1 and 10/C0 6 s/C0 1 show no signiﬁcant differences in the
grain size and the crystallographic texture. In contrast, the AR
state for the fastest tensile tests at strain rate of 6.6 /C2 10
/C0 2 s/C0 1
is from a different batch of the FeAlOY and exhibits ﬁner
grains, see Fig. 5. We consider this difference as acceptable for
a reliable study. The smaller grain size could be problematic
only in the case of conventional long-term creep testing. The
crystallographic texture in the AR states shows preference of
<001> orientation normal to the rolling direction and a weak
preference of <101> orientation in the rolling direction. The
degree of preferred orientations is given by Multiple of Uni-
form Density (MUD). The MUD value below 4 is usually
considered as not strong, while MUD above 25 can be achieved
in single crystals with low angle boundaries. The lattice
orientation is most important in the rolling direction, as it can
substantially inﬂuence the strength determined by tensile
tests (the rolling and loading direction are the same in this
study). The texture in the rolling direction is only weak for all
AR states and the small differences are considered as
insigniﬁcant.
The grain microstructures of fractured specimens loaded
at rates 10
/C0 6 s/C0 1 and 10 /C0 3 s/C0 1 and different temperatures are
shown in Figs. 6 and 7 . The left part of the specimen corre-
sponds to the loaded specimen in the distance of 2 e3m m
from the fracture, while the right part depicts the fracture
region. At the lowest and highest temperatures, the ductility is
relatively low and thus the grain microstructures of the loaded

have only slightly higher MUD values. The grains become
signiﬁcantly elongated in the loading axis for medium tem-

The changes in the texture due to loading with low ductility (at
the lowest and highest temperatures) can be considered as
insigniﬁcant even in the fracture regions. In contrary, the
specimens with the highest plasticity (at medium tempera-
tures) show strong texture towards <101> orientation in the
loading direction and towards <001> orientation in the
normal to load direction.
In most cases, the fracture of the specimens is trans-
granular, suggesting sufﬁciently strong cohesive strength of
grain boundaries. However, in specimens loaded at highest
temperatures (1000

ing to mixture of transgranular and intergranular, where the
fracture is most probably initiated by decohesion in the grain
boundaries loaded by the highest tensile stress. This gradual
change of fracture mechanism can explain the drop of UTS at
the highest temperatures as well as the drop in ductility.

At the lowest temperatures, one can expect that disloca-
tion climb does not take place, the FeAlOY strengthens
intensively by multiplication of dislocations by slip, which
leads to rather high UTS and moderate plasticity. At medium
temperatures, the dislocation can overcome the nanooxides
by combination of slip and climb and the applied stress is
sufﬁcient to detach dislocations from the back side of nano-
oxides. Then individual dislocations can move to a rather long
distances and can also effectively annihilate (an expressive
recovery is active). This causes a signiﬁcant decrease of
dislocation strengthening and dramatic increase of ductility.
At the highest temperatures (1000
/C14 C) the applied stress is
insufﬁcient to detach the dislocations from the nanooxides
and the grains exhibit nearly no plasticity. Then the cohesive
strength of the grain boundaries becomes the weakest link in
the FeAlOY and its fracture is initiated by the grain boundary
decohesion. One can suppose that the cohesive strength de-
creases linearly with increasing temperature, and it is zero at
the melting point of the FeAlOY (about 1500
/C14 C). Then one can
deduct from Fig. 2 that the strength of the FeAlOY is deter-
mined by the cohesive strength of grain boundaries in the
range 1000 e1500
/C14 C and the fraction of the intergranular
fracture increases with temperature. Consequently, both UTS
and ductility drop with increasing temperature.

Generally, no dynamic recrystallization occurs in the
FeAlOY at any loading conditions even in the fracture region
and the elongation of the grains in the loading direction cor-

To demonstrate the interaction of dislocations with the
nanooxides in the FeAlOY at strain rate of 1 /C2 10
/C0 6 s/C0 1 and
temperature 400 /C14 C, STEM micrographs are presented in Fig. 9.
The majority of the Y 2O3 particles are below 30 nm in size. As
the dislocations move through the matrix, they are trapped by

4. Conclusions
The Fee10Ale4Cre4Y2O3 ODS nanocomposite (FeAlOY) shows
signiﬁcant anomalies in strength and ductility in the tensile
tests carried at temperatures from 20
/C14 C to 1300 /C14 C and strain
rates of 6.6 /C2 10/C0 2 s/C0 1,1 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1 and 1 /C2 10/C0 6 s/C0 1. The
extraordinarily high ductility (up to 50%) is achieved at 600 /C14 C,
500 /C14 C, 400 /C14 C for the mentioned strain rates, respectively.
The top ductility is achieved at combination of 600 /C14 C and
strain rate of 6.6 /C2 10/C0 2 s/C0 1, showing good potential for pur-
poses of manufacturing parts by hot-shaping. The slower
tensile tests are designed to resemble conventional creep
testing. Especially the slowest tensile tests show that the
FeAlOY carries a good combination of strength and plasticity
in the entire temperature range, and thus it is a candidate for
applications where cycles of heating/cooling are expected.
Moreover, the FeAlOY exhibits an extraordinary strength at
temperatures above 1000
/C14 C, where the conventional mate-
rials as Ni-based superalloys fail.
A rather high yield stress and strengthening of the FeAlOY
at low temperatures is due to presence of nanooxides and
dislocation hardening due to the Orowan mechanism, as the
dislocations cannot pass the nanooxides by combination of
climb and slip. The FeAlOY exhibits a transgranular nearly
brittle appearing fracture due to exhausting of the plasticity by
dislocation strengthening.

The signiﬁcantly increased ductility at medium tempera-
tures is caused by an easy dislocation motion across the
dispersion of nanooxides. It is allowed by passing of disloca-
tions around the nanooxides by combination of slip and climb
and its detachment from the nanooxide. Then there is no
reason for a signiﬁcant multiplication of dislocations, and
thus dislocation strengthening, as in the case of the Orowan
mechanism. At such conditions a ductile fracture occurs with
a signiﬁcant necking.
The drop of ultimate tensile strength and ductility of the
FeAlOY at high temperatures is linked to decreasing cohesive
strength of the grain boundaries with increasing temperature.
This phenomenon is accompanied by gradual transition from
transgranular to intergranular fracture with increasing
temperature.
