ABSTRACT
Metal additive manufacturing (AM) promises a revolution with the potential to fabricate more complex, lighter, 
and higher performance components while simplifying supply chains and reducing material waste. However, 
many of the superalloys that are critical to applications requiring superior high-temperature properties are also 
challenging to process using fusion-based AM. The number of publications on this topic has grown significantly 
in the past five years, reflecting a growing interest within industry and academia. This article reviews and 
discusses the challenges associated with powder bed fusion - laser beam (PBF-LB) of γ ′ Ni-based superalloys and 
recent approaches that have enabled their processing. This includes process parameter optimization, alloy 
modification, and heat treatment, all of which have been shown to produce material with reduced defect density. 
Additionally, the properties of γ ′ Ni-based superalloys made with PBF-LB and conventional (cast and wrought) 
processes are compared and the reasons for the observed differences are discussed. Current and future research 
trends are provided based on the current outstanding challenges and prevailing theories in the literature, as well 
as an outlook on the adoption of PBF-LB γ ′ Ni-based superalloys in industry.
1. Introduction
Powder bed fusion – laser beam (PBF-LB) is an additive 
manufacturing (AM) process recognized for having the widest industry 
adoption, and is currently used for serial production of high value 
components in regulated industries such as energy, aerospace, and 
healthcare [1 – 5] . This is attributed to the high technological maturity of 
PBF-LB systems, which have substantially improved over the last decade 
to reach the point where more reliable, high-resolution, and 
high-throughput systems are available that cater to industrial needs [6, 
7] . Alongside the development of PBF-LB systems, significant work has 
also gone into the development and qualification of materials. 
Commercially available powder feedstock of alloys such as AlSi10Mg, 
Ti-6Al-4V, Inconel 625, Inconel 718, CuCrZr, 17 – 4PH, 316 L SS, and 
CoCr are not only widely available from powder manufacturers and 
easily printable, but also widely adopted for end-use applications [8,9] . 
However, the number of alloys qualified by PBF-LB system manufac -
turers pales in comparison to the materials available for subtractive and 
forming processes, which limits the use of PBF-LB in some applications.
One class of materials that has notably been missing from the PBF-LB 
repertoire is the high strength, high temperature-capable γ ′ (gamma 
prime) Ni-based superalloys. The commonly printed Ni-based superal -
loys are either primarily γ " strengthened and therefore limited to in -
termediate temperatures (i.e., Inconel 718 has only a minor strength 
contribution from γ ′ ) or are primarily solid solution strengthened alloys 
with comparatively poor mechanical properties (Inconel 625 and Has -
telloy X). In comparison, γ ′ precipitation strengthened Ni-based super -
alloys are a class of alloys widely used in the power generation, 
aerospace, and even automotive industries for components that must 
withstand temperatures greater than 700 
◦
C. These are critical materials 
used in the hot sections of power and aero turbine engines (in turbine 
blades, vanes, and disks) or in high performance automotive turbo -
chargers (turbine wheels). Although these alloys were typically devel -
oped with casting processes in mind, there is an increasing desire to 
benefit from the capabilities that PBF-LB offers using these industry 
standard, high temperature-capable materials. Unfortunately, these al -
loys are notoriously difficult to weld, and many of the same challenges 
have appeared when attempting to additively manufacture them using 
PBF-LB.

2. Background: PBF-LB, Ni-based superalloys, and cracking
2.1. The PBF-LB Process
PBF-LB is an AM technique that uses a laser to create three- 
dimensional objects directly from 3D computer models. Other com -
mon names include selective laser melting (SLM), laser powder bed 
fusion (LPBF), and direct metal laser melting (DMLM), however the ISO/ 
ASTM standard 52900:2021 refers to the process as PBF-LB. The PBF-LB 
process workflow begins with a model of an object which is first created 
in a design software and is then processed in a build preparation soft -
ware. Build preparation involves properly orienting the model, gener -
ating support structures, slicing the model into thin cross-sections 
typically 20 µ m to 100 µ m in thickness, and assigning process parame -
ters. A machine-readable file is then created with instructions needed for 
the PBF-LB equipment to create the object, including the placement of 
the object within the build chamber, the number of layers and geometry 
of the cross-sections, the laser scan vectors within each cross-section, 
and the parameters associated with each vector. In general, the most 
commonly adjusted parameters include the laser power, laser scan 
speed, hatch spacing (distance between the vectors), layer thickness, 
and pre-heating temperature.
The physical PBF-LB process begins with the spreading of a thin layer 
of powder by a recoater onto a metal plate. As the name implies, the 
process makes use of one or more laser sources to selectively melt and 
consolidate the thin powder layer into a thin cross-section of the object 
being built. Multiple objects can be built on one plate as shown in Fig. 1 , 
requiring that the laser beam consolidate multiple cross-sections on each 
layer of powder. Once the appropriate regions of a layer are exposed by 
the laser, the platform and build plate moves down one layer thickness. 
The recoater then spreads another thin layer of powder on top of the 
previous layer. The laser proceeds to consolidate the second thin cross- 
section of the object or objects being built, and the process repeats itself 
until all of the necessary cross-sections are consolidated across all of the 
layers. In order to obtain the final object, the loose unmelted powder is 
removed (and recycled for future use), followed by removal of the build 
plate from the build chamber and separation of the object or objects 
from the build plate.

2.3. Defect formation during PBF-LB of γ ′ Ni-based superalloys
A significant amount of research has aimed to address the defects 
that form during PBF-LB of γ ′ Ni-based superalloys. These alloys suffer 
from the typical void formation present in other welding and fusion AM 
processes, including lack of fusion or keyhole porosity. Lack of fusion is 
typically the result of insufficient energy input, where incomplete 
melting of the powder feedstock or inadequate overlap between adja -
cent tracks of material occur. On the other hand, keyhole porosities are 
found when excessive energy input is used. This occurs due to instability 
and material vaporization within deep, high aspect ratio melt pools, 
where entrapped gases form spherical pores within the final solidified 
material. Typically, between these two conditions, a process parameter 
window can be identified for most materials where highly dense, mostly 
defect-free material can be printed.
However, some materials – including γ ′ Ni-based superalloys – are 
susceptible to several types of cracking. As a result, the process 
parameter window that allows for a sufficiently defect-free microstruc -
ture becomes much smaller. The four primary cracking mechanisms 
include solidification cracking, liquation cracking, strain age cracking, 
and ductility dip cracking. These mechanisms can occur either in the 
fully solid state – known as cold cracking or solid-state cracking – or 
when liquid is present – known as hot cracking.
Solidification cracking [29,30] is attributed to a combination of 
factors that occur within the mushy zone ( Fig. 4 ) as an alloy cools and 
transitions from a liquid to a solid. Preferential segregation of solute into 
the liquid can create a low freezing point film at subgrain and grain 
boundaries, which solidifies later than the surrounding material. The 
melt pool material experiences solidification- and thermally-induced 
tensile stresses/strains that form due to volume changes during the

In certain situations, the microstructure that forms during solidifi -

enriched liquid between the subgrains solidifies and the volume shrinks, 
voids form that may not be replenished by the liquid phase depending on 
the solidification conditions. Several factors can lead to insufficient void 
backfill, including the very rapid cooling rates experienced during PBF- 
LB, the formation of a viscous or high surface tension liquid due to the 
chemistry changes that arise from solute segregation, the formation of 
solid secondary phases during solidification, or the presence of a high 
solid phase fraction that blocks the liquid ’ s access to the voids. These 
voids, in combination with residual tensile stresses in the solid material, 
can lead to the formation of much larger cracks that propagate towards 
the part ’ s top surface [31,32] .
Similar to solidification cracking, liquation cracking [29] also re -
quires the formation of low melting point phases or solute segregation at 
subgrain or grain boundaries. However, cracking occurs in the prior 
solidified material when adjacent tracks or subsequent layers are 
formed. The previously solidified material is reheated to a point above 
the eutectic temperature but below the liquidus temperature of the 
matrix, causing localized melting in the low melting point phases. The 
local loss in material strength – since a liquid cannot support any loading 
– can combine with tensile stresses to separate the boundaries and form 
cracks. Although liquation and solidification cracking are difficult to 
discern after-the-fact, Ghasemi-Tabasi et al. [31] made use of X-ray 
imaging during the PBF-LB process to observe both cracking mecha -
nisms occurring within a CM247LC alloy.

ductility that accompanies the increase in intragranular γ ′ fraction and

strain age cracking during the PBF-LB process. However, these systems 
are not widespread and face barriers when it comes to industry adop -

resulting in uneconomical batch sizes or restrictive part size limitations, 
and powder reuse challenges that arise from the higher oxygen and ni -
trogen pickup at elevated temperatures ( Section 3.1.6 ).

researchers and industry towards the PBF-LB of γ ′ Ni-based superalloys.

The PBF-LB literature tends to focus on three overarching topics 
when it comes to these alloys: PBF-LB process optimization, post- 
processing (including heat treatment), and alloy modification. A dis -
cussion and analysis of the literature regarding these topics is presented 
in Section 3 . Studies on PBF-LB process optimization typically focus on 
identifying parameters that result in dense material with a limited 
quantity of defects. Heat treatment is typically used to remove defects or 
modify the microstructure to achieve the desired properties. Lastly, alloy 
modification has more recently gained in popularity as an approach to 
facilitate the printing of these alloys, and includes tighter control over 
minor alloying elements, the inclusion of oxide or carbide dispersion 
strengthening particulates, and even the design of completely new γ ′ Ni- 
based superalloys more suited to the PBF-LB process. The increased 
research into alloy modification is likely due to the significant potential 
benefits and increasing industry acceptance of additive-specific mate -
rials, with some commercial solutions available from companies such as 
Alloyed (ABD-900AM and ABD-1000AM), ATI (ATI-1700), Carpenter 
(GP-1100), and Elementum 3D (R80-RAM1).
3. Approaches to PBF-LB of γ′ Ni-based superalloys
3.1. Effect of PBF-LB process parameters on cracking density
A number of studies have been published in literature that reveal the 
influence of the main PBF-LB process parameters on the crack density of 
γ ′ Ni-based superalloys. A correlation matrix analysis between process 
parameters and crack density was performed on those studies, with the 
materials ranging from medium γ ′ fraction alloys such as IN939 to high γ ′ 
alloys such as CM247LC or CMSX-486. The results of this analysis are 
presented in Fig. 5 . In general, the crack density trends for the individual 
primary parameters – power, laser scan speed, and hatch spacing – are in 
agreement across the literature. An increase in power is shown to worsen 
the degree of cracking experienced in these alloys. Laser scan speed was 
clearly found to have a negative correlation, suggesting that a faster 
laser scan speed tends to reduce the extent of cracking. In general, fewer 
studies focused on hatch spacing or layer thickness, often choosing to 
adjust hatch spacing depending on the chosen power and speed, or 
selecting a single layer thickness based on what is commonly used for Ni- 
based superalloys. The studies that varied these parameters and found 
statistically significant results tended to find that hatch spacing and 
layer thickness were both positively correlated to crack density.
Energy densities offer ways to combine the major PBF-LB parameters 
into a single variable that still has physical significance and is often used 
for optimization purposes. One type of energy density used when eval -
uating multi-layer depositions is the volumetric energy density (VED).

the melt pool dimensions are too small to fully overlap with previous

extensive cracking and spherical gas porosity at low scan speeds 
(150 mm/s) for CM247LC printed with high hatch spacing of 105 µ m. 
Short, disconnected cracks began to form above 750 mm/s, which are 
likely to be addressable with hot isostatic pressing ( Section 3.3 ). This 
low crack density and short disconnected crack morphology continued 
up to 1750 mm/s, although lack of fusion began to form at 1250 mm/s 
when the melt pool depth was close to the effective layer thickness. Risse 
[45] identified wider process windows and lower crack densities for 
IN738LC when printing at higher scan speeds (investigating up to 
1000 mm/s), suggesting that there is a benefit to operate at higher scan 
speeds from a manufacturing reliability standpoint. A further increase in 
scan speed to 3200 mm/s was demonstrated in a study by Adegoke et al. 
[59] when using hatch spacings of 20 – 40 µ m.

few studies look at the effects of the PBF-LB process parameters on 
microsegregation.
At the melt-pool scale, Griffiths et al. [68] observed variations in Hf 
segregation within the same melt pool of a CM247LC alloy, which was 
attributed to differences in solidification conditions. These Hf-enriched 
grain boundaries were observed to re-melt when they were re-heated 
using a Gleeble testing apparatus, suggesting they were susceptible to 
liquation cracking. At the part scale, Li et al. [75] observed the influence 
of the repeated thermal cycling during PBF-LB on segregation of Ti and 
Nb in an FGH96 alloy. Layers closer to the substrate plate cool more 
rapidly due to the plate ’ s effect as a heat sink, resulting in less micro -

the build height increases and more heat accumulation occurs, the Laves 
phase that forms is coarser and more continuous in structure. One might 
expect that energy density would have a similar effect – lower energy 
parameters that result in less heat accumulation should lead to less so -
lute segregation.
A study by Xu et al. [76] suggests why the influence of PBF-LB 
process parameters on the extent of microsegregation is not a frequent 
topic of investigation. They experimentally identified enrichment of Ti, 
Nb, Mo, Cr, and C in the intercellular region of a MAD542 alloy, while Ni 
and Al partitioned preferentially to the cell core. As part of their study, 
Xu conducts simulations of solute concentration as a function of the solid 
mass fraction during solidification. A comparison of the expected 
segregation is presented when using a solidification rate of 1 mm/s 
(approximates single crystal casting withdrawal rate) vs. 1000 mm/s 
(approximates PBF-LB laser scan speed). Segregation of solute is only 
slightly different for the major alloying elements, but drastically 
different for minor alloying elements such as Zr and B (for which the 
partitioning coefficient increased by a factor of approximately 32). That 
being said, such notable differences in the segregation of minor alloying 
elements due to different solidification rates are only visible when 
comparing drastically different processes. Xu calculated cell core dis -
tribution coefficients for major and minor alloying elements, and found 
excellent agreement between a wide variety of PBF-LB Ni-based super -
alloys processed with laser scan speeds from 100 mm/s to 1300 mm/s. 
The finding suggests that this laser scan speed range made little differ -
ence in the partitioning of alloying elements, with only carbon showing 
notable scatter in the results.
Xu ’ s findings also clearly show that alloys designed for casting pro -
cess conditions are not always suitable for PBF-LB. Several studies have 
identified these minor alloying elements as possible reasons for solidi -
fication and liquation cracking. The suggested mechanisms include their 
contribution to the formation of low melting point films at grain 
boundaries [77] , as well as their influence on grain boundary and 
solid-liquid interface energies [78] . Modification of minor alloying el -
ements in cast alloys to enable their processing via PBF-LB is a popular 
topic of interest and is discussed further in Section 3.3 .

3.1.3. Texture and grain size
Studies have shown that significant differences in microstructure are 
obtainable by adjusting PBF-LB process parameters. The solidification 
microstructure that forms and remains within a PBF-LB part is influ -
enced by the solidification conditions, melt pool shape, and extent of 
remelting between adjacent tracks or layers. This combination of factors 
results in wildly varying textures or grain morphologies depending on 
process parameters. Li et al. [83] found that dendrites were well aligned 
with the PBF-LB build direction when using a moderate (1000 mm/s) 
laser scan speed, resulting in a strong cubic texture. Higher (1400 mm/s) 
or lower (600 mm/s) scan speeds produced microstructure with weaker 
texture. This was attributed to columnar cellular and dendritic growth in 
the build direction along the bottom of the melt pool, while misoriented 
columnar growth occurred near the top and sides of the melt pool (an 
effect also described by Zhu et al. [84] ). At the slowest scan speed, a 
greater amount of misoriented growth occurred, while at the fastest 
speed the amount of melt pool overlap and remelting was reduced. Both 
of these effects interrupted the epitaxial growth of cells between layers 
in the build direction that was most prominently seen at the moderate 
laser scan speed. A similar effect was observed by Chen et al. [85] , in 
which one set of process parameters resulted in a strong cubic {001} 
< 100 > texture while higher and lower VEDs resulted in a more mis -
aligned texture.
Process parameters can similarly affect the size of the grains formed 
during PBF-LB. Depending on the melt pool size, shape, and overlap, 
dendrites can grow aligned with the build direction and form high aspect 
ratio grains which persist across several layers. This grain structure has a 
lower grain boundary density, and often results in property anisotropy 
that depends on the build orientation ( Section 4.1 ). However, proper 
selection of process parameters can also result in melt pool size, shape, 
and overlap that encourages various competitive grain growth di -
rections. In these scenarios, smaller, more equiaxed grain structures are 
formed and more grain boundaries are present in the material.
The increase in high angle grain boundaries that accompany smaller 
equiaxed grains would intuitively suggest an increase in locations where 
cracking can occur. However, a reduction in grain size has often been 
suggested as one possible solution to solidification cracking and has 
frequently been demonstrated for highly crack-susceptible aluminum 
alloys processed via PBF-LB [86 – 89] . Under the assumption that the 
amount of strain is equivalent in two different samples, the sample with 
the smaller grains and greater number of grain boundaries will have a 
lower amount of strain per boundary [30] . If the amount of strain per 
boundary is kept below a critical value, solidification cracking can be 
avoided. A small grain size can be achieved using inoculants or modi -
fication of the alloy chemistry (discussed in Section 3.3 ), as well as by 
proper selection of process parameters as mentioned above. Grange et al. 
[70] and Fardan et al. [58] achieved grain refinement in IN738LC and 
CM247LC, respectively, with low linear energy density parameters 
(approximately 0.1 J/mm). Alongside a sufficiently small hatch spacing, 
these parameters were able to avoid lack of fusion defects and achieve 
both a small grain size (30 – 60 µ m ) and a small melt pool size 
(100 – 200 µ m), resulting in low crack densities.

approach to obtain crack-free material. Single crystal structures without

3.1.4. Laser source and beam characteristics
In addition to the main process parameters, some literature has 
attempted to modify aspects of the laser source to improve PBF-LB ’ s 
capability to process γ ′ Ni-based superalloys. Unlike the more commonly 
used continuous lasers which emit a constant beam of radiation at a 
constant power level, pulsed lasers emit radiation in discrete intervals 
that result in high peak powers but low average power levels. An early 
publication demonstrating the PBF-LB of a γ ′ Ni-based superalloy – albeit 
the comparatively easy to manufacture Waspaloy – made use of a pulsed 
laser to obtain 99.7 % dense material [91] . Although most PBF-LB sys -
tems nowadays make use of continuous lasers, others have tried to assess 
whether a pulsed laser is beneficial for processing the harder to manu -
facture γ ′ Ni-based superalloys. Rodríguez-Barber et al. [92] had success 
processing IN939, finding a wide processing window with low crack 
densities and few defects of other types. Mu ˜niz-Lerma et al. [93] was 
similarly successful in processing IN738, achieving crack free and highly 
dense material.
A pulsed laser provides more rapid cooling rates in comparison to 
continuous lasers due to the oscillating thermal profile from the inter -
mittent beam exposure and the lower maximum temperatures. This is 
especially useful for manufacturing thin-wall structures and fine fea -
tures that conduct heat poorly [93] . Evidence of the faster cooling rates 
is shown in a study by Guo et al. [94] , who provided a direct comparison 
between pulsed and continuous laser processing of an IN738LC alloy by 
keeping the main process parameters (power, speed, hatch, and layer 
thickness) the same. Samples created with the pulsed laser had a lower 
crack density and a finer microstructure, attributed to the higher cooling 
rates calculated using numerical simulations. However, the faster cool -
ing rate, lower temperature, and increased melt pool liquid viscosity also 
increased the amount of entrapped gas porosity remaining in the final 
material.
Some studies have also looked at modifying the shape of the laser 
beam to improve the processability of γ ′ Ni-based superalloys. Typical 
laser beams display a Gaussian intensity profile, with the maximum 
intensity at the centre and a decreasing intensity towards the edges. This 
results in greater heating of material towards the centre of the melt pool, 
achieving higher temperatures and deeper melt pools. At the same laser 
power, ring or top-hat shaped beam intensity profiles have lower 
maximum temperatures and shallower melt pools. Both of these are 
expected to be beneficial for reducing solidification cracking. Bayol and 
Pallier [95] identified a wide process parameter window for an IN939 
alloy using a top-hat beam profile, and Cloots et al. [77] attributed the 
lower crack density obtained in an IN738LC alloy with a ring beam 
profile to a decrease in the size of the mushy zone because of more 
evenly distributed heating.

formed at the borders due to a stronger horizontal thermal gradient

the cracking susceptibility. Carter attributed the heavy cracking 
observed in the fine grained region at island borders to a ductility dip

Novel scan strategy approaches may help to alleviate the challenges 
associated with varying thermal gradients in typical scan strategies. 
Catchpole-Smith et al. [50] demonstrated the use of continuous fractal

crack density were obtained with the fractal scan patterns. These were 
attributed to reduced stresses from the short vector lengths and more 
uniform temperature distribution as a result of the continuous scanning.
Other novel scanning approaches include multi-laser or multi-pass 
strategies, the first of which is demonstrated by Gerstgrasser et al. 
[98] . A defocused, low power laser is made to follow the primary laser 
used for melting the alloy. The purpose of this offset second laser is to 
reduce the temperature differential between the material being melted 
and the previously deposited tracks. This has the effect of reducing in -
ternal residual stress and reducing part distortion. Hu et al. [99]
demonstrated a multi-pass strategy, in which a consolidated layer was 
scanned a second time after the first scan was completed but before a 
new powder layer is applied. They found that re-scanning the initial 
layer was able to effectively reduce cracking and porosity defects when 
the same or lower energy input parameters were used. These parameters 
resulted in faster cooling rates, which is beneficial for cracking mitiga -
tion. The most effective re-scanning strategy was found to be an island 
approach, which when measured resulted in the lowest dislocation 
density (and therefore residual stress) when compared to bi-directional 
re-scanning strategies. Both Gerstgrasser ’ s and Hu ’ s results are expected 
to have significant implications for strain age cracking mitigation as 
well, since the offset laser or second laser pass acts as a stress relief heat 
treatment with respect to reducing the internal strain. The strain that 
then forms during post-process heat treatment because of γ ′ precipitation 
will not add to an already high PBF-LB process-related strain, reducing 
the likelihood of cracking.

As beneficial as high temperature pre-heat is for reducing or elimi -
nating cracking in γ ′ Ni-based superalloys, there are several downsides to 
the process that limit its use or require careful consideration. From a 
practical standpoint, the industrial use of PBF-LB systems with high 
temperature capabilities is hampered by the low powder re-usability. 
Oxygen and nitrogen react more readily at elevated temperatures, and 
the ideally inert argon environment in PBF-LB systems always contains 
trace amounts of these gases. The powder in the chamber readily picks 
up oxygen and nitrogen at elevated temperatures, rapidly exceeding 
recommended levels after a small number of re-uses. Additionally, PBF- 
LB systems with pre-heating capabilities typically have reduced build 
volumes due to the difficulties in maintaining very high temperatures 
uniformly throughout a large build volume. This limits the size of 
components that can be manufactured. From a metallurgical standpoint, 
higher temperature pre-heating is associated with greater carbide for -
mation – including the formation of continuous carbides rather than 
discrete carbides at grain boundaries – and grain growth [103,106] . 
Whether metallurgical changes are beneficial or detrimental should be 
assessed based on the specific alloy and the intended application. 
Continuous carbide formation may be detrimental to ductility, while 
increased grain size may be beneficial for creep properties at the expense 
of low temperature yield strength.
3.2. Effect of chemistry modification on cracking density
It is common knowledge that conventional alloys were not designed 
with AM processes in mind, and as such may not be suitable for pro -
cessing with techniques such as PBF-LB. Difficulties welding some cast 
alloys – which face similar challenges as PBF-LB – have necessitated the 
development of weldable filler metals. Chemistry modification has also 
been proposed in literature to enable the processing of γ ′ Ni-based su -
peralloys using PBF-LB, which focuses on the role of minor alloying el -
ements and trace elements in cracking [107] . Moreover, the 
development of new alloy chemistries via computation models has also 
been explored [108] , with the primary goal being to improve process -
ability by mitigating crack formation without sacrificing mechanical 
properties.
A significant variable influencing solidification cracking is the so -
lidification temperature range. This range physically manifests as a 
“ mushy zone ” that contains both the solid and liquid phase. In PBF-LB, 
the mushy zone often consists of solid cells or dendritic structures sur -
rounded by liquid enriched in lower melting point elements. In most 
cases, a larger solidification temperature range corresponds to a physi -
cally larger mushy zone which is more susceptible to solidification 
cracking due to higher localized strains and less effective backfilling. In 
order to reduce the size of the mushy zone and the solidification range, 
alloy modification is frequently studied. More specifically, there is a 
drive to reduce the low melting point elements that preferentially 
segregate to the liquid during solidification and decrease the effective 
solidus temperature. However, Ni-based superalloys are composition -
ally complex, such that the type and content of alloying elements may 
vary dramatically in each alloy. Therefore, it is important to consider 
both processability and effects on mechanical properties when evalu -
ating the influence of alloying elements.
3.2.1. Chemistry modification in CM247LC
The alloying element Hf is a prime target for studies on chemistry 
modification due to its influence on solidification range and micro- 
cracking in legacy alloys such as CM247LC [68] . In casting processes, 
the addition of Hf increases the fraction of the intergranular γ / γ ′ eutectic 
phase, which increases the amount of liquid back-filling that heals 
cracks formed between dendrites during solidification. However, solid -
ification conditions (thermal gradients and interface velocities) in 
PBF-LB vary significantly from those typically encountered in superalloy 
casting, leading to differences in micro-segregation ( Section 3.1.2 ). The 
substantially faster solidification and cooling rates occurring in PBF-LB 
significantly reduce the eutectic volume fraction, suggesting that higher 
Hf content may be required. Findings to support this approach were 
presented by Engeli [54] for CM247LC and Yu et al. [109] for IN738LC. 
Both studies concluded that the addition of Hf improves liquid feeding 
through the dendrite/cell network during solidification, enabling the 
back-filling of emerging cracks and reducing the final crack density.
However, most studies have chosen an alternate approach ( Fig. 12 , 
Table 1 ). Often, a substantial increase in Hf concentration is observed in 
the residual liquid towards the terminus of solidification as identified by 
Hu et al. [110] . Enrichment of the residual liquid occurred at very high 
solid fractions (f

= 0.95) and Hf enrichment at crack interfaces was 
observed. Similar findings have encouraged researchers to modify 
cracking susceptibility by removing Hf. The removal of Hf from a 
CM247LC alloy was found by Tang et al. [108] to significantly reduce 
the susceptibility to cracking towards the terminus of solidification 
(0.9 > f

results were obtained through thermodynamic calculations by Griffiths 
et al. [68] and others, who found a reduced solidification range when 
decreasing the Hf concentration and a corresponding drop in cracking 
density during PBF-LB ( Fig. 12 A1, A2, B1, B2).
These results suggest that a composition maxima exists where 
cracking susceptibility is highest [54] . When a small amount of terminal 
liquid forms, grain boundary wetting is minimal, solid bridges form 
across boundaries, and the volume shrinkage is small as the final ter -
minal liquid solidifies. The combination of these factors reduces the 
likelihood of crack formation and propagation. In alloys with greater 
quantities of low melting point elements that encourage eutectic phase 
formation, the amount of terminal liquid increases. This results in 
greater grain boundary wetting and established conditions suitable for 
the formation of solidification cracks. However, if the amount of final 
terminal liquid becomes sufficiently large, and the network forming 
between the dendrites is sufficiently permeable, solidification cracks 
that form can be back-filled and healed with available terminal liquid.
In addition to influencing the amount of terminal liquid during so -
lidification, the addition of Hf improves mechanical properties by 
hardening the γ ′ phase, as well as promoting eutectic γ / γ ′ and blocky 
carbide formation at the grain boundaries [111] . Griffiths et al. [112]
found that reducing the Hf content decreased the γ ′ quantity and the γ ′ 
solvus temperature in a PBF-LB CM247LC. In addition, Hu et al. [110]

3.3. Effect of heat treatment on micro-cracking defects
Although low γ ′ Ni-based superalloys can be processed with PBF-LB 
to form dense, crack-free material, higher γ ′ Ni-based superalloys typi -
cally require post-processing to address internal defects. This is 
frequently done in addition to the other heat treatments that are 
otherwise still required. Hot isostatic pressing (HIP) is usually per -
formed first, in which a high pressure and high temperature environ -
ment are used to soften and compress a material, closing internal 
defects. This is then followed by a solutionizing step to homogenize the 
microstructure, and an aging step that grows the γ ′ strengthening phase 
that nucleated during cooling from the solutionizing step.
Hot isostatic pressing has been shown to successfully heal defects in 
even the most crack-susceptible γ ′ Ni-based superalloys ( Fig. 14 c,d). 
Carter et al. showed the successful closure of cracks in the core of a PBF- 
LB CM247LC sample [148] , but identified surface-connected cracks that 
remained in the alloy after HIP [96] . Several authors have identified this 
surface-connected limitation to HIP when applied to cracking defects, 
and the solution is typically to ensure that cracks are short and isolated 
from each other. Vilanova et al. [149] identified the crack width –

The typical industrial applications of polycrystalline γ ′ Ni-based su -
peralloys requires that their properties and performance be evaluated in 
a wide range of temperatures up to approximately 1000 
◦

commonly studied properties include tensile strength and stress rupture, 
both of which are presented in-depth in the following sections. The 
corrosion and fatigue performance of these alloys is also discussed, 
although there are significantly fewer literature studies that focus on

An attempt was made to only include the mechanical test results of 
crack-free and heat-treated materials, since cracks are detrimental to 
mechanical properties and a proper heat treatment is critical to the 
formation of the γ ′ strengthening phase. Additionally, material in the as- 
built condition is not microstructurally stable at typical operating tem -
peratures, such that as-built mechanical test results would very quickly 
become irrelevant in an end-use application.

heat treatments rather than the manufacturing process itself. How -
ever, many PBF-LB studies in the literature use non-standard heat 
treatments in an attempt to heal defects formed during PBF-LB 
( Section 3.3 ). Differences in heat treatments can result in unimodal 
or bimodal γ ′ distributions, with smaller γ ′ and smaller 
inter-precipitate spacing being more beneficial to yield strength 
[158,161] . Standard heat treatments were also reported to result in 
different γ ′ morphology and evolution when comparing cast and 
PBF-LB IN939 [159] , although the final phase fraction and sizes 
remained similar after a full solution and aging treatment was 
performed.
• Dislocation strengthening occurs as a result of dislocation- 
dislocation interactions via internal stress fields and intersecting 
slip bands, and is dependent on the dislocation density. Similar to a 
strain (work) hardened material, as-built PBF-LB alloys contains 
significant strain and high dislocation densities, which can remain in 
the microstructure if low temperature heat treatments are used 
[160] . Although this strengthening mechanism is less effective at 
elevated temperatures, it can result in comparatively high strength at 
low temperatures. Dislocation strengthening is less significant in cast 
alloys or alloys heat treated at adequately high temperatures that 
facilitate recovery and recrystallization.

• Grain boundary strengthening via a Hall-Petch relation is expected 
to be notably different between the typically small-grained PBF-LB γ ′ 
Ni-based superalloys and their coarse-grained cast counterparts. Fine 
grains result in a higher grain boundary density, which act as barriers 
to dislocation movement and increase the yield stress. The small 
grains formed during the PBF-LB process tend to persist in the final 
heat-treated microstructure even after recrystallization, unless a 
sufficiently high temperature is used to promote grain growth. Even 
when significant grain growth occurs, grain size still tends to be 
smaller in comparison to a cast alloy. As such, grain boundary 
strengthening is expected to contribute to the low temperature 
strength difference observed between PBF-LB and cast alloys.
In addition to unusually high strength at low temperatures, PBF-LB 
alloys also tend to display high ductility at low temperatures. This is 
unexpected, since strength and ductility tradeoffs are frequently 
observed in Ni-based superalloys. Lee et al. [154] attributed the good 
room temperature ductility observed in PBF-LB CM247LC to very fine, 
discrete, and uniformly distributed carbides. When compared to the 
more script-like carbides in the cast material, the fine carbides formed 
during solidification and heat treatment are less brittle and do not 
introduce regions with high stress concentration.
The good low temperature elongation results in PBF-LB γ ′ Ni-based 
superalloys also confirm that there are few cracks or other defects in 
the microstructure resulting from the AM process or heat treatments. 
This is noteworthy as it suggests the low ductility commonly observed at 
elevated temperatures is due to a temperature-dependent embrittlement 
mechanism [189] . High strength γ ′ Ni-based superalloys are observed to 
experience predominantly intergranular failure at elevated tempera -
tures, suggesting that grain boundaries are limiting the ductility of these 
alloys. Many authors have looked to carbide precipitates to explain these 
grain boundary failures. Some studies of high temperature tensile 
deformation have identified interfacial decohesion between aggregated 
carbides and the matrix [187] , and cavity formation in high-stress car -
bide- and eutectic-containing regions [190] . These studies would sug -
gest that grain boundary engineering via improved carbide 
characteristics may be an appropriate avenue to approach the deficiency 
in high temperature ductility. However, Ghoussoub et al. [189] proposes 
that carbide modification is insufficient. They suggest that alloy modi -
fication is required to more closely align the grain boundary to grain 
interior strength ratio that is critical to achieving a ductile alloy at high 
temperatures.
Many of the strengthening- and ductility-improving mechanisms are 
influenced by both PBF-LB process parameters and heat treatments, 
which can result in a significant amount of scatter in the reported 
strength and ductility values for each alloy. For example, grain size is 
strongly influenced by both process parameters and heat treatments, 
while the precipitation of the γ ′ phase is influenced primarily by heat 
treatments. Many studies compare multiple processing routes to eluci -
date these relationships and their influence on mechanical properties. 
Hao et al. [191] found that heat treatments which more effectively 
reduced internal strain in the as-built grains via recovery and recrys -
tallization also achieved a greater amount of room temperature elon -
gation. They also identified that heat treatments resulting in a more 
uniform and denser secondary and tertiary γ ′ precipitate distribution 
improved the strength of an FGH4096 alloy at both low and high tem -
peratures. A key parameter associated with the heat treatment is the 
temperature at which it is performed, with a significant difference in 
microstructure observed at – or slightly below – the γ ′ solvus tempera -
ture [192] . Sub-solvus heat treatments generally result in limited or no 
recrystallization due to undissolved γ ′ and fine discrete carbides pinning 
the as-built grain boundaries. Depending on how far below the solvus 
temperature the heat treatment is performed, the material may also 
retain the sub-grain solidification structure. As a result, the as-built 
columnar microstructure and texture persists, and the primary γ ′ pre -
cipitates that form during heating remain in the structure and limit the 
amount of formed secondary γ ′ . On the other hand super-solvus heat 
treatments dissolve the primary γ ′ and facilitate recrystallization of the 
grain structure. In addition to the precipitation of a more ideally sized 
and distributed secondary γ ′ phase during cooling from the super-solvus 
temperature, different carbide microstructures may form. Atabay et al. 
[192] found that grain boundary carbide films formed due to 
super-solvus heat treatments which reduced ductility significantly 
compared to the fine, distributed carbides arising from sub-solvus heat 
treatment. Super-solvus temperatures also typically result in a more 
equiaxed structure, although grain size anisotropy often remains since 
full recrystallization may not occur.
The grain aspect ratio and texture are key factors that contribute to 
mechanical property anisotropy, which also contributes to the scatter in 
the mechanical property results reported in literature. Fig. 16 plots the

to be elongated and have a strong texture in the vertical direction. 
Atabay et al. [192] found the average grain aspect ratios (height:width) 
in sub-solvus heat treated Rene 41 to be approximately 4:1, which 
decreased to approximately 2:1 after a super-solvus heat treatment 
resulted in a partially recrystallized microstructure. A similar decline in 
the texture intensity was observed. It would therefore be expected that 
anisotropy in properties is reduced after sufficiently high temperature 
heat treatments are performed, however some anisotropy still remains.

Based on the literature results, PBF-LB processed γ ′ Ni-based super -
alloys have a clear ability to outperform conventionally-made alloys at 
low temperatures. The high temperature tensile strengths are generally 
acceptable, but the low ductility is a potential area of concern for 
adoption in end-use applications. Studies have demonstrated the use of 
varying heat treatments to alter the microstructure of these alloys, with 
the goal of improving high temperature mechanical properties at the 
expense of the low temperature properties [183] . Additionally, the use 
of varying PBF-LB process parameters to locally tailor the microstructure 
has also been demonstrated. This allows for the local adjustment of low 
and high temperature mechanical properties as well as property 
anisotropy within a single part [195] . These types of approaches may be 
required to ensure comparable performance between PBF-LB-processed 
alloys and conventional alloys across the full range of typical exposure 
temperatures.

that the specimen with the initially coarser microstructure continued to 
be coarser after recrystallization, and therefore showed stress relaxation 
behaviour more similar – but still inferior – to a cast IN738LC. This can 
be partly attributed to the smaller grain size in PBF-LB IN738LC even 
after recrystallization when compared to the grain size of the typical cast 
counterpart.

properties. Additional contributions to the stress rupture property

larger carbides typically found in the cast alloy. With a reduced grain 
size in PBF-LB alloys and therefore a higher grain boundary density, 
composition modifications in these low carbon alloys may be needed.

optimal to achieving creep resistant carbide distributions and sizes. In 
support of this conclusion, Despr ´es et al. [122] found that a PBF-LB

creep properties. Similarly, due to the formation of grain boundary

5. Conclusion
5.1. Summary of findings
This review focuses on the successes and remaining challenges 
associated with the PBF-LB processing of γ ′ Ni-based superalloys. Four 
main categories are discussed in the context of the available literature, 
including the PBF-LB process, post-processing, alloy chemistry, and 
properties. A brief summary of the findings is provided for each 
category.
The PBF-LB process 
• Studies tend to show that laser power, hatch spacing, and layer 
thickness are positively correlated with increased cracking in these 
alloys, while laser scan speed is negatively correlated. High pre- 
heating temperature is also found to reduce crack density.
• Process parameters and beam shaping both influence the resulting 
melt pool size and shape. In general, studies that are able to achieve 
smaller melt pools and/or larger width-to-depth melt pool ratios 
result in material with reduced crack densities. A small hatch spacing 
and layer thickness also leads to high melt pool overlap, which fa -
cilitates the removal or filling of previous cracks.
• The influence of process parameters on the extent of micro -
segregation is likely limited, and the presence of γ ′ in the as-built 
condition is limited to nanometre-sized precipitates if found at all. 
The initial chemistry of the alloy is likely to have a much larger role 
on the extent of segregation and phase formation than process 
parameters.
• Process parameters and scan strategies have a strong influence on the 
resulting texture and grain size. A reduction in grain size and in -
crease in grain boundary density has been shown beneficial in 
reducing crack density, however the complete elimination of grain 
boundaries via the production of a single crystal also results in crack- 
free material.
Post-processing 
• Hot isostatic pressing (HIP) has been shown to successfully heal 
cracks in γ ′ Ni-based superalloys. However, challenges remain 
addressing surface connected cracks or wider cracks. Some success is 
found by ensuring cracks are short, small, and disconnected from one 
another. Even when successful, small flaws tend to remain in the 
final microstructure as rounded porosity.
Alloy chemistry 
• Modification of alloying elements that segregate to the interdendritic 
region is a common approach to addressing solidification cracking. 
Studies often find that increasing the amount of these elements re -
duces cracking due to improved backfilling during solidification. 
However, studies also find that decreasing the amount of these ele -
ments reduces cracking by reducing the solidification range and 
reducing the amount of terminal liquid during solidification.
• Adjusting the composition in traditionally cast or wrought alloys 
influences the quantity of γ ′ , carbide, and boride particles that are 
beneficial to mechanical properties. Alloy modification must be 
made on a case-by-case basis, and properties evaluated to ensure 
they continue to meet requirements.
• Other approaches to alloy modification include the introduction of 
additives using techniques such as mechanical alloying. These 
generally – but not always – have the effect of decreasing the grain 
size, which reduces the extent of cracking. Other benefits to the in -
clusion of additives include an in-situ modification of the alloy 
chemistry or the reaction of additives with detrimental elements in 
the alloy.
Properties 
• PBF-LB processed γ ′ Ni-based superalloys tend to outperform their 
conventional counterparts in tensile properties at lower tempera -
tures (below 700 
◦
C). At higher temperatures, yield strengths are 
generally acceptable, but lower ductility and lower ultimate tensile 
strengths are observed.
• High temperature creep properties tend to be worse in PBF-LB alloys 
than in the conventional counterparts. This is typically attributed to 
smaller grain sizes and secondary phases such as carbides and bo -
rides which are less effective at pinning grain boundaries. Proper 
alloy modification and fine tuning of heat treatments must be per -
formed to address these issues.
• Property anisotropy between vertically and horizontally built spec -
imens is observed in tensile, stress rupture, and fatigue results. This is 
attributed to elongated grains and grain texture both of which can be 
addressed using proper heat treatment design.
• Differences in corrosion between conventional and PBF-LB γ ′ Ni- 
based superalloys are sometimes observed, attributed to finer 
grains and higher grain boundary densities that facilitate diffusion. 
For this reason, internal oxidation tends to be worse in PBF-LB alloys. 
However, heat treatments can be effective at reducing the grain 
boundary density and achieving results that are comparable to the 
conventionally-made alloys.
5.2. Concluding remarks and future directions
Several industries, including aerospace and power generation, face 
significant challenges in designing and manufacturing components that 
can endure ever-increasing temperature and lifetime requirements. 
Historically, advancements in turbine engine efficiency and perfor -
mance have been achieved by the development of new Ni-based su -
peralloy chemistries and manufacturing processes (i.e., transition from 
poly-crystalline to single-crystal casting).
More recently, AM has enabled the integration of novel components 
into turbine engines: AM-made titanium aluminide blades have been 
adopted for their lighter weight and higher specific strength, while co -
balt chrome cyclonic inducers leverage the geometric complexity 
achievable with AM processes to reduce part count and enhance engine 
performance [229] . However, the adoption of AM in the hottest sections 
of aero and power turbine engines has been limited, despite clear in -
dustry interest. Large companies in the power generation industry such 
as Siemens Energy [41,102,198,230,231] , Rolls Royce [81,153,232, 
233] , and GE Power / Alstom Power (now part of GE Vernova) [54,107, 
195] have published research on PBF-LB γ ′ Ni-based superalloys, while 
components made of PBF-LB γ ′ Ni-based superalloys such as turbine 
rotors with internal cooling channels [40] , first stage gas turbine ring 
segments [41] , and gas turbine blades [234] have been demonstrated. 
Some of these components have been implemented in commercial 
products or are being used in a prototyping capacity to support com -
mercial products. These parts are often high-value and 
geometrically-complex, making them ideal candidates for AM. Howev -
er, broader adoption is primarily hindered by the challenges of reliably 
printing high-strength γ ′ Ni-based superalloys and achieving the desired 
mechanical properties. Overcoming these challenges is crucial for 
further integrating AM into the production of critical turbine engine

The recent surge in publications on the PBF-LB of γ ′ Ni-based su -
peralloys – especially those emphasizing mechanical property charac -
terizations, underscores the growing capabilities of academic and 
industry groups in manufacturing crack-free materials. Numerous so -
lutions have been demonstrated to enable crack-free printing of con -
ventional alloys by modifying material chemistry and optimizing both 
the PBF-LB and heat treatment process parameters. Alloys specifically 
designed for AM have also demonstrated a high degree of manufactur -
ability, typically achieving as-built material devoid of cracks while also 
being less susceptible to strain age cracking during heat treatments. 
Despite these advancements, the adoption of PBF-LB γ ′ Ni-based super -
alloys for the more strenuous and higher temperature applications 
remain limited due to mechanical property challenges. Although low 
and mid-temperature properties generally outperform the conventional 
cast or wrought materials, there is a clear deficit in strength and ductility 
at high temperatures. Current research points to microstructural dif -
ferences between the PBF-LB and conventional alloys as being the cause; 
grain size, shape, and orientation all play a role, as do the size and 
distribution of secondary phases and γ ′ precipitates. To overcome these 
limitations, the research community must focus on developing ap -
proaches that not only ensure crack-free manufacturing but also 
enhance the high-temperature properties of PBF-LB γ ′ Ni-based super -
alloys to meet or exceed those of conventionally-made counterparts. 
This will require further optimization of alloy chemistry, PBF-LB pro -
cesses, and heat treatments.
Until solutions are obtained that allow for comparable microstruc -
tures in PBF-LB and cast conventional alloys, the adoption of PBF-LB γ ′ 
Ni-based superalloys will be hindered by comparisons to the properties 
of their conventionally-made counterparts. In cases where a property 
deficit is present, it will likely be necessary to treat conventional alloys 
made using PBF-LB as if they were new alloys. Extensive testing and 
costs associated with qualifying a “ new ” alloy makes it less likely that 
companies will be willing to replace current components already in 
production where design changes are not easily made, and component 
lifetime requirements are already fixed. However, conventional alloys 
and new additive-specific alloys could be attractive options for inte -
gration into new product launches. Despite this, the highest-performing 
conventionally-made γ ′ Ni-based superalloys currently have no PBF-LB 
equivalent. As familiarity with and qualification of PBF-LB γ ′ Ni-based 
superalloys increases, their applications in maintenance, repair, and 
overhaul (MRO) are likely to become a significant market niche due to 
the economic advantages of lower-volume manufacturing typically 
needed to maintain older, out-of-production equipment.
The aforementioned challenges in the PBF-LB of γ ′ Ni-based super -
alloys have however created numerous R & D opportunities, including 
optimization of alloy chemistries for AM, process parameter optimiza -
tion, heat treatment innovation, microstructural control, simulation and 
modeling of the process for Ni-based superalloys, and sustainability and 
cost reduction through lifecycle assessments. These opportunities are 
being materialized through effective collaboration between industry 
and academia.
