ABSTRACT
An overview of the microstructural and mechanical properties of Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti high entropy alloys (HEAs) at 
high temperatures is presented. With exceptional properties at high temperature and high dissolution temper -
ature of the L1
2 
phase ( γ ʹ ), HEAs exhibit excellent wear resistance, thermal stability, high hardness and strength, 
superior corrosion resistance, and remarkable electrical and magnetic properties, which position them as ideal 
candidates to replace traditional superalloys. Refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) offer excellent mechanical 
properties at high temperatures. However, reducing price and weight without sacrificing mechanical properties 
is an essential requirement for broadening the applications of RHEAs. Among varieties of HEA systems, Al-Co-Cr- 
Fe-Ni-Ti has proven to be an optimum choice for high-temperature applications considering price and weight. 
The development of strength in this alloy system primarily depends on optimizing its chemical components 
regarding various phases and their phase fractions. An increase in Al + Ti content increases the formation of 
Ni
3
(Ti, Al) or γ ʹ and, consequently, the strength of the alloy. Conversely, Cr and Fe must be carefully balanced to 
prevent the formation of brittle phases. Exceeding the limiting values of Co, Cr, and Fe can lead to the formation 
of brittle phases like Heusler , β , η , and σ . In order to achieve better mechanical properties, the optimized [Al + Ti]- 
content should be around 15 – 16 at%. At elevated temperatures, valence electron concentration (VEC) closely 
aligns with the corresponding microstructure, suggesting that VEC can be utilized to predict the microstructure. 
Superior oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures (higher than that of Ni-based superalloys) promotes Al- 
Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti HEAs for high-temperature applications.
1. Introduction
1.1. Traditional alloys
Functional alloy systems are based almost exclusively on a single 
principal metal element for thousands of years. About thirty alloy sys -
tems are commonly used in combinations for the development of science 
and technology [1 – 3] . Creating new alloys with multiple elements is 
somewhat constrained because of the complex formation of interme -
tallic compounds and the requirement of intricate analysis methods 
[3 – 7] . Materials development, or the production of new alloys to over -
come existing challenges, has always been of significant interest to the 
researchers. Retaining the properties of alloys at high temperatures has 
been one such challenge the researchers have always struggled with 
them [8 – 10] . The most commonly used materials for gas turbines in the 
aerospace and energy industries are superalloys [11 – 19] , especially 
Ni-based superalloys. The homologous temperature of superalloys has 
reached its limit, and the current focus is on reducing costs primarily by 
eliminating expensive elements without compromising the mechanical 
properties [20 – 23] . In order to retain excellent mechanical properties at 
high temperatures, superalloys require the presence of L1
2 
( γ ʹ ) cohesive 
precipitates in the γ (FCC) matrix. Unfortunately, the dissolution tem -
perature for the γ ʹ phase of some superalloys like IN718 is less than 
1000 ℃ [24 – 27] . The chemical compositions of superalloys have 
continued to evolve to meet serviceability requirements [28,29] . 
Recently, the addition of Re and Ru to superalloys has reportedly 
enhanced their creep strength [30 – 35] , but at the expense of density and 
cost. Nevertheless, adding heavy elements (Re, W) causes density

1.2. High temperature applications and defects
The efficiency of heat engines increases with increasing temperature, 
regardless of their type. Increasing operating temperature reduces fuel 
consumption, pollution, and operating costs in the power generation 
industry, such as coal and oil [40,41] . Also, increasing operating tem -
perature results in improved performance in the jet engine industry, 
such as heavier load combinations, higher speeds, and longer ranges 
[42,43] . The primary material of engine components, Ni-base superal -
loys, suffers from a lower melting temperature (1300
◦
C) compared to 
the service temperature of 1160 – 1277
◦

essential to design materials that retain their properties at higher tem -
peratures for gas turbine applications. Despite over six decades of 
research, Ni-based superalloys have failed to meet the required prop -
erties for gas turbine applications. Increased operating temperature 
improves efficiency and reduces greenhouse gas emissions in gas turbine 
engines used for aviation and ground power generators, resulting in 
significant economic and environmental impacts [47 – 49] .
1.3. High entropy alloys (HEAs)
HEAs are currently leading the research trend in search of new ma -
terials for such applications. HEAs were proposed in 2004 by Yeh et al. 
[50] and Cantor et al. [51] , classifying them as a new concept of alloys. 
Unlike traditional binary or ternary alloys, HEAs are constituted by the 
addition of five or more elements in equal proportions (between 5 and 
35 at%) [52 – 59] . The superior properties of HEAs include outstanding 
thermal stability [60,61] , high hardness and strength [62,63] , excellent 
wear resistance [64,65] , distinct electrical properties [66] , impressive 
magnetic properties [67] , and high corrosion resistance [68] . Sluggish 
diffusion, high lattice distortion, cocktail effect, and strong nanophase 
formation ability manifest these properties [69 – 72] . Consequently, 
HEAs bear great potential for the application of materials used princi -
pally at high temperatures [73 – 79] . To date, this new alloy concept has 
received significant research [80 – 115] attention and some of such 
popular HEA alloy systems are mentioned here: Al-Co-Cr-Cu-Fe-Ni [86, 
88,90,99] , Al-Co-Cr-Cu-Fe-Ni-B [83] , Al-Cr-Fe-Mn-Ni [92,98,109] , 
Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti [80,81] , Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Mo-Ni [66,67,69,71] and 
W-Nb-Mo-Ta-V [102,108] .

1.5. Overview
Researchers have conducted thousands of studies on HEAs. Based on 
the Science Direct database and density visualization VOS viewer soft -
ware, HEAs, mechanical properties, and microstructure are the most 
used keywords (minimum number of occurrences of a keyword of more 
than 300) for about 1200 articles ( Fig. 1 a). Corrosion resistance, pre -
cipitation strengthening, machine learning, wear resistance, and addi -
tive manufacturing are shown when the minimum number of 
occurrences of a keyword is more than 30 ( Fig. 1 a). However, me -

entropy superalloys keywords are searched ( Fig. 1 b). High-temperature 
alloys, high-temperature strength, precipitation, and superalloys are 
those keywords that are used less than the previously mentioned 
keywords.
The continuous evolution of science has witnessed the gradual 
growth in applications of Ni-based superalloys and ultimately reached a 
point of saturation in their performance, creating a void to develop new 
alloys that offer even greater performance. Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti-based 
HEAs have demonstrated significant potential as a substitute for Ni- 
based superalloys. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the 
microstructure and mechanical behaviour of the Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti alloy 
system is crucial for their development, especially at high temperatures. 
This paper examines the mechanical properties and microstructure of 
Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti HEAs at elevated temperatures and provides an 
overview of the key factors affecting these properties.
2. Mechanical properties and their dependence on composition
2.1. Secondary phase effects
FCC single-phase HEAs have sustainable strength in various tem -
peratures, suitable fracture toughness, excellent wear resistance, and 
high work hardening behavior [135,139 – 143] . However, their lower 
strength at high temperatures compared to Ni-base superalloys dis -
courages their use in engineering applications [144 – 146] . Eliminating 
the bottleneck with the introduction of secondary reinforcing phases has 
been a popular topic of research [132,147 – 161] . Only a few types of 
HEAs can be used for applications above 800 ℃ . Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti alloy 
systems look the most promising. They have an fcc matrix with ordered 
L1
2 
precipitations, which is similar to other Ni-base superalloys [38,40, 
136,162 – 167] . Reports indicate that the Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti HEAs exhibit 
excellent mechanical properties [162] , retain strength at elevated tem -
peratures [163,164] , and exhibit astonishing thermal stability [165, 
166] . There have been encouraging reports about the use of HEAs in 
high-temperature structures [40,136] . The FCC-disordered matrix and 
ordered secondary phases have shown different features. Low stacking 
fault energy (SFE), work hardening, and thermal stability belong to the 
matrix phase, and strengthening at high temperatures belongs to the 
secondary phase (L1

Secondary phases strengthen HEAs by inhibiting dislocation motion, 
refining grain structures, and increasing creep resistance. However, 
their effects are temperature-dependent, necessitating careful tuning to 
balance strength, ductility, and thermal stability. At ambient tempera -
ture, the existence of secondary phases can dramatically impact the 
strength of HEAs using methods including precipitation strengthening, 
load sharing, and grain boundary pinning. At elevated temperatures, the 
role of secondary phases becomes more complex: thermal stability, 
creep resistance, coarsening and instability, and oxidation resistance. 
While secondary phases boost strength, they typically contain flaws, 
such as brittleness, which can reduce ductility and toughness. Further -
more, the inhomogeneous distribution of secondary phases might result 
in stress concentrations and local failure [174 – 177] .
One of the most important factors is deformation mechanisms [131, 
132,147] . The motion and interaction of crystalline lattice faults are part 
of the deformation mechanisms. Dislocations are the main flaws because 
they are the carriers of plastic displacement, which results in plastic 
strain. Major defects include grain boundaries, which interact with or 
generate dislocations, resulting in Hall-Petch strengthening effects 
commonly found in HEAs, and vacancies, which facilitate the diffusion 
of elemental species, hence influencing creep, segregation, and other 
high-temperature properties [163] . The macroscopic features are also

Ductility in FCC HEAs is frequently constrained by necking [52,77] . 
Mechanisms like TWIP or TRIP, which can postpone necking by main -
taining or increasing work hardening rates, can boost both ductility and 
strength through interface hardening. The mechanisms by which 
ductility is improved are unclear; however, in the instance of TWIP, it 
may be attributed to twin barriers that obstruct dislocation movement 
while simultaneously permitting dislocations to glide along their in -
terfaces, thus alleviating stress concentrations. In TRIP, phase trans -
formations that facilitate deformation with minimal misfits are 
advantageous for enhancing ductility [179,180] . BCC HEAs exhibit 
notable differences from FCC HEAs, similar to the distinctions observed 
between simple BCC metals and FCC structures. Screw dislocations 
predominantly influence fundamental strengthening; however, edge 
dislocations may also significantly contribute in certain BCC 
high-entropy alloys [181] . However, is relatively new in comparison to 
current alloys, however it has yet to be established. In some refractory 
HEAs, composition can be modified to destabilize the HCP phase relative 
to BCC, allowing TRIP contributions to be introduced; nonetheless, no 
TWIP effects have been observed in refractory HEAs [182] .

3.2. Designing
Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti HEAs elements are discrete and divided into three 
major groups. Ni and Co are groups that constitute group one. Al and Ti 
with Cr and Fe form groups 2 and 3, respectively. It has been determined 
that 35 at% of group 1 is the minimum content necessary for the stability 
of γ + γ ʹ phases at 800 ℃ . Groups 2 and 3 can have a maximum of 26 and 
53 at% elemental concentration, respectively. Increasing the concen -
tration of elements in group 2 decreases the concentration of elements in 
group 3, although a balance must be restored between them. If the sum 
of the atom fraction of the element in group 2 is negligible ( < 4 at%), γ ʹ
