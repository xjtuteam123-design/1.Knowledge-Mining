abstract
Low-chromium ( <10%Cr) high strength oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) FeCrAl alloys are consid-
ered promising candidates for accident tolerant fuel cladding in light water ﬁssion reactors. These alloys
are designed to combine the bene ﬁcial high temperature mechanical properties of ODS materials with
the exceptionally high temperature oxidation resistance of FeCrAl in comparison to conventional Zr-
containing cladding materials, while also providing good mechanical and aqueous corrosion behavior
under normal light water reactor operating conditions. The initial (1st generation) ODS FeCrAl alloys
combined gas atomized FeCrAl powders with yttria and other oxides for alloying element additions using
the mechanical alloying approach. These alloys exhibited low ductility but excellent high temperature
tensile strength while maintaining good oxidation resistance at temperatures up to 1400
/C14 C. In an
attempt to improve alloy ductility for accident tolerant fuel cladding applications, new low-Cr ODS
FeCrAl alloys with decreased oxygen content were developed with the Zr alloying solute already gas
atomized into the powder prior to mechanical alloying. The resultant Fe-10Cr-6.1Al-0.3Zr þ0.3Y2O3
(106ZY) powders were ball milled for 10, 20, and 40hr followed by consolidation via hot extrusion at
temperatures ranging from 900 to 1050 /C14 C. Increasing the mechanical alloying time decreased the
resultant grain size and improved high temperature tensile properties. Decreasing the extrusion tem-
perature re ﬁned the grain size and subsequently strengthened the ODS FeCrAl 106ZY alloys while
lowering the ductility. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and energy dispersive spec-
troscopy (EDS) demonstrated the Zr solute addition effectively sequestered impurity C and N within the
matrix. The root mean square (RMS) hardening superposition model for yield strength shows good
agreement with experimental results. Errors between predicted and experimental values are discussed
within the scope of processing parameters. These 2nd generation 106ZY alloys show a distinct increase in
alloy ductility without sacri ﬁcing the high temperature tensile properties characteristic of legacy or 1st
generation ODS FeCrAl alloys.

1. Introduction
Since the devastating events at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear
reactor facility in 201 1, research has been aimed at developing
advanced materials that provide enhanced strength and oxidation
resistance in the event of a nuclear accident scenario [ 1,2]. These
accident tolerant fuel (ATF) cladding materials must outperform
existing Zr-based alloys with respect to their oxidation resistance in
steam and their subsequent hydrogen generation in the event of a
design basis or beyond design basis accident, while retaining
acceptable neutronic and corrosion performance under prolonged
normal operating conditions. Furthermore, ATF cladding materials
must exhibit many of the desirable qualities for fuel cladding ap-
plications under normal operating and accident conditions, such as
having good mechanical strength and ductility, chemical compat-
ibility with fuel and coolant selections, both thermal and irradia-
tion stability, a minimal neutron absorption penalty, high thermal
conductivity, and a suf ﬁciently high melting temperature [ 2].
To address these challenges, high strength wrought FeCrAl al-
loys have been developed that exhibit 100 /C2 lower oxidation ki-
netics in high temperature steam than currently used Zr-based
claddings [3e5]. The presence of aluminum in the matrix promotes
the growth of a stable aluminum oxide ﬁlm at high temperatures
while simultaneously allowing for a lower chromium content
(/C20 10%Cr) in the alloys [6]. The lower Cr content is imperative due to
the well-known irradiation induced precipitation of the deleterious
and embrittling Cr-rich
a0 phase that occurs for higher Cr contents
at the ~300 e350 /C14 C operating temperature for fuel cladding in
existing light water reactors [ 7,8]. The higher strength of these
FeCrAl alloys is imperative due to the added neutronic penalty of Fe
in comparison to Zr in the reactor core, which will require a sub-
stantial thinning of existing cladding geometries to maintain ef ﬁ-
cient neutron economy [ 2]. Recent loss of coolant accident burst
experiments on as-fabricated FeCrAl alloys have shown that the
onset of burst for these Fe-based alloys is attributed to exceeding
the high temperature ultimate tensile strength (UTS) for the alloy
[9]. This suggests that further improving the UTS of existing FeCrAl
alloys can prove bene ﬁcial not only for cladding thinning re-
quirements for drop-in replacements for existing reactor assem-
blies, but it may also improve the burst properties of these cladding
tubes in the event of a nuclear accident condition.
To improve the strength of existing FeCrAl alloys, ODS alloys
utilize a high number density of nano-precipitates within the metal
matrix that pin grain boundaries during alloy consolidation, lead-
ing to a re ﬁnement in grain size and a signi ﬁcant increase in both
strength [ 10]. Nanoprecipitates within the as-consolidated alloy
also inhibit dislocation motion and grain boundary sliding through
a Zenner pinning effect, thus improving high temperature me-
chanical properties and creep resistance [ 11]. However, legacy ODS
FeCrAl alloys developed originally for fossil applications such as
MA956 and PM2000 are highly prone to the formation of a0, Cr-rich
precipitates due to their high Cr content (~20%Cr) [ 12e14]. In an
attempt to reduce the precipitation of this Cr-rich phase, low-Cr
(10e12% by weight) ODS FeCrAl alloys have been previously
developed for ﬁssion and fusion applications by milling FeCrAl gas
atomized powder with Y 2O3 and either ZrO 2, TiO 2, or HfO 2 for the
addition of alloying elements Zr, Ti, and Hf [ 15e17]. The resulting
alloys exhibited bene ﬁcial oxidation resistance and a ﬁne-grained
microstructure with corresponding grain sizes ranging from 100
to 500 nm [ 15]. Though these alloys demonstrated exceptional
tensile strength at high temperatures, the corresponding unirra-
diated total deformation ( εtot < 10%) was poor for the alloys at
temperatures characteristic of light water reactor environments
(T < 400/C14 C). The high oxygen content of these 1st generation ODS
FeCrAl alloys was of concern since previous studies on ODS FeCr
alloys have suggested that the oxygen availability in the lattice
strongly in ﬂuences the types of nano-precipitates that can form
during alloy consolidation [ 18,19].
In an attempt to more tightly control the oxygen content and to
fabricate new alloys with better ductility, new 2nd Gen. ODS FeCrAl
alloys with only 10% Cr by weight are being developed at ORNL.
Instead of the addition of alloying elements as oxides during the
mechanical alloying process, FeCrAl powders were procured with
Zr already homogeneously gas atomized into the powder prior to
adding Y2O3 during the ball milling stage. The addition of Zr to the
metal matrix allows for the careful assessment of zirconium's role
in the formation of precipitates in the resulting ODS alloys and also
decreases the amount of oxygen in the ODS FeCrAl lattice. To
optimize the processing methodology for the production of these
ODS FeCrAl alloys, parametric evaluations of the ball milling du-
rations and extrusion temperatures were performed. By varying the
milling time, the impurity content can be balanced with the
effectiveness of Y2O3 incorporation into the lattice. Lastly, the effect
of the extrusion temperature on the mechanical properties of these
alloys is presented with the speci ﬁc goal of optimizing the strength
and ductility of the resulting ODS FeCrAl alloy.
2. Methodology
2.1. Experimental approach
Gas atomized powder from ATI Powder Metals (mesh /C0 100/

10Cr-6.1Al-0.3Zr (all numeric values are in wt. %) was mixed with

The powder mixture was mechanically alloyed in a high energy Zoz
Simoloyer CM01 ball mill in an Ar atmosphere using low-C steel
milling media with a ball to powder ratio of 10:1. The milling was
performed using rotational speeds of 400 rpm/900 rpm for either
10, 20, or 40 h. The resulting Fe-10Cr-6.1Al-0.3Zr þ0.3Y2O3 (106ZY)
ODS FeCrAl powders were packed and degassed for 24 h at 300 /C14 C
in a 4.83 cm (1.9 “) extrusion can. The 106ZY powders were
extruded at 950 /C14 C through a circular 2.22 cm (7/8 ”) die. Further-
more, the 106ZY powder milled for 40hr was also extruded at
temperatures 900 /C14 C, 1000 /C14 C, and 1050 /C14 C to assess the tempera-
ture dependence of microstructural and mechanical properties for
the alloy.
The chemical compositions of the various extruded alloys were
measured using inductively coupled plasma optical emission
spectroscopy (ICP-OES) and combustion analysis by DIRATS. The
results of this compositional analysis are summarized in Table 1 for
each alloy. In comparison to the 1st Gen ODS FeCrAl alloy 125YZ
which had an oxygen content of 1920 ppm by weight [15], all of the
new extruded 106ZY alloys had lower oxygen contents. The 106ZY
alloys extruded at 950 /C14 C after milling for 10hr (1H95C), 20hr
(2H95C), and 40hr (4H95C) show an increasing C content with
increasing milling time. The O and N contents vary somewhat be-
tween specimens, which will be discussed below.
2.2. Mechanical property evaluation
Mechanical property evaluation was performed using an MTS
hydraulic tensile machine using a strain rate of 10 /C0 3 s/C0 1. Sub-sized
(SS-3) tensile specimens with a 7.62 mm gauge length and a cross
section of 1.52 mm /C2 0.762 mm were machined from each 106ZY
sample listed above in Table 1. For consistency, all specimens were
machined with the tensile axis parallel to the alloy extrusion di-
rection. Tensile testing was performed at temperatures varying
from room temperature to 800 /C14 C in air, with at least one specimen
being tested at each respective temperature. Due to the limited

specimens were also procured using the same FEI Quanta 3D dual-

3. Results
3.1. Tensile properties
The summary of tensile data for 106ZY specimens mechanically
alloyed at several milling times is provided in Fig. 1. As mechanical
alloying time increases, ultimate tensile strength (UTS) increases.
This increase in UTS is apparent at all test temperatures between 20
and 800
/C14 C. However, the ductility of the alloy decreases when
milling time increases, which is expected due to the increased alloy
strength. A visual comparison between room temperature UTS and
ductility with increasing milling time is shown in Fig. 2.
One property of interest is the impurity content for each alloy
milled for various times. As Fig. 3 indicates, there is a linear increase
in C uptake in the powder with increasing mechanical alloying
time. The only sources of carbon that exist in the closed mechanical
alloying system are the stainless steel housing, the rotary blade
within the attrition-type ball mill, and the small 5 mm spherical
milling media used in the process. The abrasion between the
milling media and the surrounding housing and rotary blades
during ball milling, coupled with the direct impacts between the
milling media and the sample powder, cause this linear increase of
C in the sample as a function of milling time. This also suggests that
unless the composition of the milling media is changed, this im-
purity is intrinsic to the mechanical alloying process. Nitrogen and
oxygen do not show the same trend, however, and instead remain
roughly constant until longer mechanical alloying times (40 h)
where the amounts of these impurities jump substantially. The only
signiﬁcant source of nitrogen and oxygen is the air environment
outside of the ball milling chamber, which suggests that the
increased 40 h impurity (N and O) contents can be attributed to air
ingress into the milling chamber. This possibility is further sup-
ported by the time-dependency of these impurity increases. Milling
begins with a positive internal chamber pressure of Ar, but if there
exists a small leak in the system the Ar will exit the chamber until
the differential pressure between the inside and outside of the
milling chamber ceases to exist. After equilibrium is established,
some contamination would be possible through air ingress into the

3.2. Microstructure comparisons
To better understand the effects of mechanical alloying and
extrusion temperature on these 106ZY alloys, the as-extruded mi-
crostructures of these alloys were analyzed using scanning electron
microscopy techniques. Backscattering electron (BSE) images of
106ZY alloys milled for increasing times up to 40hr are provided in
Fig. 7 . Both the parallel (longitudinal, “L”) and perpendicular
(transverse, “R”) orientations with respect to the extrusion axis are
shown in the images for each specimen. A clear trend of grain size
reﬁnement is apparent as mechanical alloying time increases from
10 h to 40 h. This re ﬁnement is due to the more homogeneous
incorporation of yttria and subsequent dissolution of Y and O into
the microstructure prior to alloy consolidation. A more homoge-
neous distribution of yttrium and oxygen allows for a higher
number of nanoprecipitate nucleation sites during the high tem-
perature anneal and consequently a higher number density of
precipitates pinning grain boundaries and re ﬁning the ﬁnal grain
size. As shown in Fig. 2, this reduction of grain size correlates well
with the increase of strength and decrease of ductility with longer
ball milling time.

3.3. Precipitation in fabricated alloys
The types of precipitates within the 106ZY alloys are important
to understand due to their integral roles in mechanical strength-
ening and serving as sinks for irradiation-induced defects [ 24]. In
fact, it is well known that the recrystallization temperature for ODS
FeCrAl legacy alloys like MA956 is on the order of 90% of the
melting temperature, which has been attributed to the Zener
pinning by coherent nanoscale precipitates within the matrix [ 25].
For a complete understanding of these 2nd Gen. ODS FeCrAl alloys
it is consequently of interest to see what types of precipitates are
forming during the alloy consolidation step for the 106ZY alloys
fabricated in this work. To aid in this task, scanning transmission
electron microscopy (STEM) was combined with energy dispersive

These results suggest that a variety of nanoprecipitates exist
within these ODS FeCrAl alloys, especially when alloying elements
such as Zr are added to the metal matrix. The APT results in this
work suggest that the smallest nanoprecipitates are comprised
predominantly of Y, Al and O. This is a different result than was
found by Dou and colleagues who asserted that the addition of Zr
shifted the particle types toward ﬁne Y
4Zr3O12 oxides [26]. Instead,
the presence of Y, Al, and O precipitates support the work presented
by Unocic et al. asserting that the nanoprecipitates are a yttrium
aluminum garnet (YAG) type phase [17]. This is an interesting result
because Zr is expected to have a higher af ﬁnity for oxygen in
comparison to aluminum based on the Ellingham diagram within
the temperature range of interest in this study. One possible reason
why the added Zr did not seem to inﬂuence the compositions of the
smallest precipitates may be presence of larger C and N contents for
this series of ODS FeCrAl alloys. The C and N impurity levels were
not explicitly reported by Dou [ 26], but if Zr is in-fact reacting with
interstitial C and N in solution to create a separate distribution of
precipitates, less Zr would be available to compete with Al for the
formation of ternary oxides within the alloys in this study.
Historically, excess C and N impurities within steel lattices has
resulted in the decrease in Charpy V-notch impact energy due to
the formation of Cr-rich carbides and nitrides along grain bound-
aries [27]. The formation of nanoscale Zr carbides and carbonitrides
in our 2nd Gen. ODS FeCrAl as opposed to the formation of larger
deleterious Cr or Al rich carbides or nitrides could improve fracture
toughness and Charpy impact properties, but future studies need to
be performed to better understand the role of Zr in the formation of
these precipitates and their capacity to minimize the deleterious
effects of larger impurity concentrations.

Irrespective of the processing methodology, all 106ZY alloys have
the same composition, so the grain matrix hardening sm compo-
nent can be neglected from the current discussion. Instead, atten-
tion will be paid to the grain boundary, dislocation, and precipitate
components of hardening, which are all integrally related to the
processing methodology. As a general trend, both the modeled and
experimental yield strengths show the clear grain size dependence
of the yield strength, but it can clearly be seen that as the milling
time decreases from 40 h to 10 h, larger errors exist between the
model and the experiment. This larger error can be attributed to the
mechanical alloying dependence of the dislocation density and the
nanoprecipitate population. As the milling time increases, the yttria
added to the gas atomized powder is incorporated through suc-
cessive re-welding and fracturing stages. Dousti et al. tracked the
evolution of the lattice parameter, crystallite size, and powder
morphology as a function of ball milling time for ODS FeCr powder
and found that suf ﬁcient alloying and the transition to this equi-
librium fracturing and re-welding stage does not occur until milling
times greater than 20 h [ 44]. This insuf ﬁcient incorporation of
yttrium and oxygen into the ferritic lattice results in anisotropic
distributions of precipitates throughout the microstructure. In re-
gions where precipitates exist, a higher amount of grain boundary
pinning and dislocation arrestment will be expected, while in areas
without precipitates, dislocations will be able to glide freely.
Consequently, for alloys consolidated after shorter milling times,
pockets of larger grains with fewer dislocations and nano-
precipitates will exist amongst areas of higher precipitate and
dislocation densities associated smaller grains. The heterogeneous
distribution of nanoprecipitates in the 1H95C and 2H95C alloys
decreases the effectiveness of the precipitates for strengthening,
hence lowering the observed dispersed barrier strength as milling
time decreases. Just as the nanoprecipitates are not homogeneously
distributed at lower milling times, as will be the dislocations, which
results in a similar decrease in dislocation forest hardening for
shorter milling durations. Since the model does not account for the
milling time dependence of dislocation density and nano-
precipitate strengthening effects, the model overestimates these
values and consequently the error (model minus experiment) in-
creases as milling time decreases.

5. Conclusions
Fabricated low-Cr (10 wt% Cr) ODS FeCrAl alloys with
~1000e1900 ppm oxygen contents maintain good ductility while
retaining beneﬁcial high temperature strength for accident tolerant
fuel cladding applications. Through the development of these al-
loys, the following conclusions can be drawn from analysis of the
microstructural and mechanical properties:
/C15 The mechanical and microstructural properties of these 106ZY
alloy are highly dependent on the milling time, as milling time
increases from 10 to 40 h, the strength increases and the
ductility decreases due to a more homogeneous dispersion of
nanoprecipitates strengthening the matrix. The results suggest
that 40 h milling time is optimal for the processing of these ODS
FeCrAl alloys, taking into consideration the balance between
grain size re ﬁnement and impurity contents as a function of
time.
/C15 Extrusion temperature is the most efﬁcient means of controlling
the as-fabricated mechanical and microstructural properties at
extrusion temperatures less than 1000
/C14 C. At even higher
extrusion temperatures, losses in strength are associated more
with nanoprecipitate coarsening in addition to losses in Hall-
Petch grain boundary strengthening. To prevent the undesired
coarsening of precipitates at higher temperatures, the extrusion
temperature should be limited to 1000 /C14 C if a single step
isothermal annealing step is used during the extrusion process.
/C15 The role of Zr is apparent in the types of nanoprecipitates that
form within the microstructure during annealing and extrusion.
Zr appears to capture excess C and N in solution, while it has no
substantial effect on the composition or morphology of the
smallest nanoprecipitates enriched in Y, Al, and O.
The microstructure-mechanical property relationships estab-
lished for the low-Cr ODS FeCrAl alloys investigated in this work are
currently being applied towards the fabrication of thin-walled ODS
FeCrAl tubing. In addition, the effect of multi-stage annealing
temperatures on the nucleation and subsequent growth of these
nanoprecipitates is still being investigated for the further optimi-
zation of 106ZY alloy mechanical properties and precipitate dis-
persions for nuclear applications.
