1. Introduction
In recent years, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) [1–17] have pro-
posed a new alloy design perspective based on the conﬁguration
entropy principle of the alloy system, which is considered to be
one of the three breakthroughs in alloying theory in recent decades
[9]. This design perspective breaks the traditional alloy design con-
cept, that is, the alloy is no longer based on a single element, but
uses multi-principle elements as the basic components, showing
the freedom of composition, the diversity of the organization,
and the uniqueness of performance, which shows amazing advan-
tages in the ﬁeld of engineering applications [10–13]. Due to the
special combination of various metal elements, HEAs exhibit four
unique effects: high-entropy effect in thermodynamics; lattice dis-
tortion effect in the structure; sluggish diffusion effect in kinetics,
and the cocktail effects in performance [13]. These unique effects
provide the possibility for HEAs to have good structural stability
under high-temperature conditions. Therefore, in recent years,
HEAs as one of the most promising new superalloys have attracted
great attention [15,16].
It is an important research direction for improving the strength
and toughness of metal materials to ﬁnd effective precipitation
strengthening phase matching with matrix, and the development
of new metal materials with high strength and high toughness is
an eternal topic in the ﬁeld of materials. In magnesium–aluminum
alloys, steel, and superalloys, precipitation strengthening has been
proved to be a very effective strengthening mechanism, and pre-
cipitation strengthening is one of the most effective ways to
increase the strength of metal materials [18,19]. Compared with
traditional alloys, the nature of multi-principal components of
HEAs allows a more extensive selection of precipitates, and the
toughness and ductility of the matrix of the HEAs are more unique,
so the
cʹ phase precipitation strengthened HEAs have great poten-
tial. Recently, precipitation-strengthened HEAs have attracted
widespread attention. A lot of studies have shown that multi-
component precipitation strengthening is the most effective means
to strengthen HEAs [7,20–26]. He et al. [7] obtained Ni3(Al, Ti) type
coherent precipitates with L1 2 structure by adding 2 at.% Ti and 4
at.% Al to the FeCoNiCr-matrix. The tensile strength exceeded
1000 MPa, and the fracture elongation remained close to 40 %.
Yang et al.[20] obtained (Ni, Co, Fe) 3(Ti, Al, Fe) type cʹ strengthened
phase by adding 7 at.% Ti and 7 at.% Al elements into the FeCoNi-
matrix. The yield strength at room temperature exceeded
1000 MPa, the tensile strength was close to 1500 MPa, and the frac-
ture elongation remained about 50 %. Zhao et al. [21] obtained (Ni,
Co, Cr) 3(Ti, Al) type coherent precipitates with L1 2 structure by
adding 3 at.% Al and 3 at.% Ti to the CoNiCr-matrix. Compared with
the single-phase CoNiCr-matrix, the yield strength and tensile
strength increased by 70 % and 44 %, respectively, reaching
750 MPa and 1300 MPa, and the elongation of fracture remained
45 %.
Most HEAs have high structural stability and resistance to high-
temperature softening, which are expected to be applied at high-
temperature. However, most of the precipitation strengthened
HEAs reported so far are still focused on the study of their proper-
ties at room temperature. To broaden the practical application of
HEAs, it is worth exploring the high-temperature application pro-
spects of precipitation-strengthened HEAs. However, the research
results so far show that HEAs do not have good high-
temperature performance [27]. This is mainly restricted by two
factors: (a) The medium-temperature brittleness problem:
although HEAs have very high ductility ability at room tempera-
ture and low temperature, the obvious embrittlement will occur
during the deformation process in the middle-temperature range
(600 /C24 800℃) [28], which will severely limit the use of HEAs under
high-temperature conditions. (b) The high-temperature softening
problem: the composition of HEAs with near/equal atomic ratio
is not conducive to the formation of the optimal composition com-
bination of the cʹ phase, which is often easy to cause the deliberate
pursuit of high entropy and miss the best effect of cʹ phase
strengthening [23,24] so that the material in the high-
temperature service process cannot maintain a high yield strength.
It is difﬁcult to obtain a c0 + c dual-phase structure by preparing
an alloy with an equal atomic ratio and not easy to achieve the pur-
pose of coherent strengthening of the c0 phase by using the tradi-
tional concept of HEAs. Therefore, the concept of HEAs must be
organically combined with the structural characteristics of super-
alloys. Only by controlling the compositional entropy of the c
phase, c0 phase, sublattice (A, B, ... )3(C, D ... ), and the structural
stability of the c0 phase, it is possible to obtain HEAs with good
high-temperature performance, namely high-temperature HEAs.
Therefore, this paper breaks the traditional design concept of HEAs,
takes improving the high-temperature performance of HEAs as the
main research goal, and takes the precipitation strengthening of
the multi-component cʹ phase as the main design idea. In addition,
Ti, V, W, Mo, and other elements of the IVB to VIB group elements
appear relatively low in HEAs, but these elements with similar
properties such as high melting point, excellent heat resistance,
corrosion resistance, and high strength, etc., and are often used
as additives in industrial thermal strength alloys to signiﬁcantly
optimize the comprehensive properties of HEAs [29,30]. In addi-
tion, W, Mo, V, and other elements are usually added to improve
the solid solution strengthening effect and make the alloy obtain
better properties in traditional Ni-based superalloys [31]. Refrac-
tory alloying elements such as W, Mo, V, and other elements are
added to high-temperature HEAs, and the HEAs are strengthened
by using multi-component nanoparticles so that the new type of
alloy can achieve superior mechanical properties at room temper-
ature and high temperature. Explore and develop multi-
component nano-sized
cʹ-phase-strengthened HEAs with high
strength, good ductility, and high-temperature-bearing capacity.
In this study, we choosed (FeCoNi) 88Ti6Al6 as the base alloy,
because the Fe-Co-Ni-Al-Ti multicomponent alloys provide a large
dual-phase region consisting of disordered face-centered cubic
(FCC) and ordered FCC (L1 2) phases, satisfying the requirement
for the formation of precipitation [20]. (1) The selection of 6 at.%
Al and Ti elements are to enable the alloy to form a higher density
L12 precipitates so that the alloy can have better precipitation
strengthening effect; (2) the atomic radii of Al and Ti elements
are larger than those of Fe, Co, and Ni elements, and the addition
of higher content of Al and Ti elements can make the alloy produce
larger lattice distortion, which can also strengthen the alloy to a
certain extent; (3) The content of Fe, Co, and Ni elements is con-
trolled to be the same to make the FCC matrix have good stability
so that the alloy can maintain a stable FCC solid solution structure.
And the different contents of Nb, Hf, Ta, V, W, and Mo refractory
alloy elements were added to the base alloy (FeCoNi) 88Ti6Al6
(Ti6Al6), and the suitable additive elements and contents of refrac-

2. Experimental
19 kinds of HEAs were prepared by vacuum arc melting under
the protection of a high-purity Ar atmosphere, and the chemical
compositions are shown in Table 1 . The purity of all alloys’ raw
materials is /C21 99.95 wt%. At the same time, the free oxygen in
the furnace atmosphere is absorbed by melting high-purity tita-
nium ingot. Each sample was remelted at least 4 times to ensure
the homogeneity of the composition and then placed in a melting
pool with a 5 mm /C2 10 mm /C2 50 mm suction casting mold for suc-
tion molding. To eliminate the microstructure segregation of the
as-cast alloy, all samples were homogenized at 1200 /C176C for 4 hrs
and then by water quenching, and followed by cold rolling with
a rolling volume fraction of 66 %. The cold-rolled sheet was recrys-
tallized at 1200 /C176C for about 10 mins to control the grain size.
Finally, the samples were aged for 4 hrs at 800 /C176C to obtain the pre-
cipitates, and then water quenched. To prevent oxidation of the
samples during the heat treatment, all samples in this study were
vacuum packaged with quartz glass tubes.
The microstructure of the HEAs and the fracture morphology of
the tensile fractured samples were observed using a scanning elec-
tron microscope (SEM, Quanta 450). To carry out SEM observation,
the samples were mechanically polished, and then the samples
were chemically attacked using HCl: HNO 3:H 2O = 2: 1: 1 (in vol-
ume percent, vol.%) mixed solution. The microstructure was char-
acterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos
F200X) equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS).

3.1. Alloy screening
Fig. 1 presents the typical SEM micrographs that taking from six
types of Ti6Al6-based HEAs doped with different alloying elements
including Nb, Hf, Ta, W, Mo, and V. The max doping concentration
for these refractory alloying elements is set at 1.5 at.%. The stan-
dardized heat treatment process is homogenized at 1200 /C176C and
subsequently aging at 800 /C176C for 4 hrs. The Microstructure of HEAs
in this work is listed in Table 2. The criteria for ingredient selection
are whether there are impurities other than the (
c + c0) in the
microstructures after the above-mentioned standardized heat
treatment when the doping concentration is as high as 1.5 at.%.
Based on the above criteria, it is obvious that there are two
groups of results. One group includes the 1.5Nb, 1.5Hf, and 1.5Ta
alloys that containing impurity phases as shown in Fig. 1 a, 1c,
and 1e. Through the EDS mapping analysis, we found that these
impurity phases are the Nb-rich phase, Hf-rich phase, and Ta-rich
phase, respectively (see Supplementary Figures S1, S2 , and S3).
While the remaining type ( Fig. 1 b, 1d, and 1f) are the 1.5Mo,
1.5 W, and 1.5 V alloys that have no impurity phases. Although a
low doping concentration (0.5 and 1 at. %) can obtain a perfect
(c + c’) alloy without other impurity phases (See supplementary
Figure S4 ), very weak strengthening effects may be accompanied.
Thus, 3 target alloys (1.5Mo, 1.5 W, and 1.5 V) were screened from
the 18 candidates after the screening process.

4.1. Phase formation law of HEAs

increase the chaos of the alloys and promote the formation of a
simple solid solution structure [1]. However, the previous studies
indicated that not all alloys with high mixing entropy are com-

which indicates that the high mixing entropy is not the only factor
determining the formation of solid solution in multi-component

4.2. The inﬂuence of W, Mo, and V on the stability and performance of
the L1 2 precipitates phase
According to the previous analysis of tensile properties at room
temperature and high temperature, compared with Ti6Al6 HEAs at
room temperature, the tensile strength and elongation of HEAs
added with 1.5 at.% W, 1.5 at.% Mo, and 1.5 at.% V is improved to
varying degrees, and 1.5 W HEA has the best tensile strength and
fracture elongation, followed by 1.5Mo HEA, and then is the
1.5 V HEA (1.5 W > 1.5Mo > 1.5 V > Ti6Al6). Compared with Ti6Al6
HEA at high temperature, the tensile strength and fracture elonga-
tion of HEAs added with 1.5 at.% W, 1.5 at.% Mo, and 1.5 at.% V is
improved to varying degrees. 1.5 W HEA has the best tensile
strength and fracture elongation, followed by 1.5 V HEA, and then
are the 1.5Mo HEA (1.5 W > 1.5 V > 1.5Mo > Ti6Al6). The addition of
W, Mo, and V into Ti6Al6 HEA can improve the tensile properties
not only at room temperature but also at high temperatures. The
HEAs with excellent strength-ductility combination and high-
temperature properties are obtained. The main reasons are sum-
marized as follows:
(1) The XRD analysis of Fig. 3 shows that all HEAs are composed
of c0/c phases, and no other intermetallic phases are formed, which
ensures that HEAs have good ductility. In addition, the main peaks
of HEAs are all shifted to the left, that is, the angle h decreases.

(2) TEM results show that the structure of the precipitates in

misﬁt between the c0 precipitates and the c matrix, resulting in
lower local elastic strain, so that the HEAs can maintain good duc-
tility while having higher strength.
The HEAs system has a high mixing entropy, the effect on the
decrease of free energy will be further enhanced with the increase
of temperature, which ensures the HEAs can guarantee the stability
of the phase at high temperature [45]. The c’ precipitates sizes of
Ti6Al6, 1.5 W, 1.5Mo, and 1.5 V HEAs are 38 ± 5 nm, 42 ± 10 nm,
36 ± 14 nm, and 40 ± 12 nm, respectively. The c’ precipitates sizes
of the HEAs in this study are all nanometer scales. Different from
nanocrystalline alloys with a single principal element, the high
entropy nanocrystalline alloys have better phase stability, which
can largely avoid the instability of nanocrystalline structure caused
by the precipitation of solute-rich phase, thus improving the ther-
mal stability of the alloy [13].
In addition, these nanometer
c’ precipitates are uniformly dis-
tributed in the c matrix in a coherent relationship, which reduces
the mismatch degree and the distortion energy of the alloy, thus
effectively avoiding the problem of stress concentration between
the precipitates and the matrix. At the same time, these nanoscale
c’ precipitates can play a good role in hindering the movement of
dislocations, thus improving the deformation resistance of the
alloy and increasing the strength of the alloy.
(3) During the tensile creep period, the c matrix has good duc-
tility. Due to the existence of the c matrix plastic phase in the alloy,
it plays a role in coordinating strain and reducing the stress con-
centration, and the c0 precipitates are the strengthening phase of
the alloy, especially the cubic c’ precipitates have a better
strengthening effect. Element distribution studies have shown that
Wi sa c0 strengthening element and is mainly distributed in the c0
phase, but Mo and V can not only strengthen the c matrix but also
strengthen the c0 precipitates, which distribute in both c and c0
phases. Since HEAs added with W, Mo, and V are strengthened,
the high-temperature creep properties of the HEAs can be
improved.

of the L1 2 phase is improved, that’s why the high-temperature

HEAs, the lattice constant of the c0 precipitates in the 1.5Mo HEAs

the c phase increases by 0.0001 nm, and the lattice misﬁt is

of high-temperature tensile properties, indicating that increasing
the lattice constant of the c0 precipitates is beneﬁcial to improve
the high-temperature stability of the c’ phase, thereby improving
the high-temperature tensile properties.
5. Conclusions
In this study, by adding the refractory elements Nb, Hf, Ta, W,
Mo, and V into the classical (FeCoNi) 88Ti6Al6 HEAs, their effects
on the microstructure and properties were carefully studied from
the aspects of the lattice constants, lattice misﬁt, phase stability
and mechanical properties. The strengthening effect and mecha-
nism of W, Mo, and V on HEAs are obtained. The main conclusions
are as follows:
(1) Three doped Ti6V6-based HEAs named 1.5 W, 1.5Mo, and
1.5 V stand out among 18 candidates. All of the three are composed
of c’ precipitates phase (FCC ordered phase, L1 2 structure) and c
matrix phase (FCC disordered structure) without other intermetal-
lic compounds. The
c0 precipitates in 1.5 W, 1.5Mo, and 1.5 V HEAs
are coherently evenly embedded in the c matrix, showing a coher-
ent orientation relationship between the two. The doping element
W is mainly distributed in the c0 phase while the Mo and V occu-
pied the c matrix and the c0 precipitates.
(2) The Ti6Al6, 1.5Mo, and 1.5 V alloys show a negative lattice
misﬁt while the 1.5 W have the positive one, which should be
the main reason for the much better tensile properties of the
1.5 W at high temperature. The Mo-, W- and V-doped Ti6Al6-
based HEAs have a better ductility-strength combination at
room- and high-temperature. The 1.5 W HEA has the best tensile
properties at room- and high-temperature.
