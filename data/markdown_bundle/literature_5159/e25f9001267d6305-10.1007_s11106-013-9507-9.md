SINTERED METALS AND ALLOYS 
EFFECT OF STARTING POWDER PROPERTIES 
ON THE STRUCTURE AND MECHANICAL CHARACTERISTICS 
OF Al–8Cr–1.5Fe ALLOY FOR HIGH-TEMPERATURE APPLICATIONS 
O. D. Neikov,1,2 A. I. Sirko,* N. P. Zakharova,1 
N. A. Efimov,1 G. I. Vasil’eva,1 A. O. Sharovskii,1 
A. V. Samelyuk,1 V. A. Goncharuk,1 and R. K. Ivashchenko1 
UDC 669.715:621.793.7 
A high-temperature Al–8Cr–1.5Fe alloy has been developed for applications in the range from room 
temperature to 300C. The billets are obtained in two ways: using gas-atomized and water-atomized 
powders. Structural analysis shows that particles of the second phase in the water-atomized powders 
are finer and their number in the aluminum matrix is greater than that in the gas-atomized powders. 
The rods extruded from the water-atomized powders have much higher hardness, ultimate strength, 
and yield stress than those from the gas-atomized powders, plas ticity being acceptable at all 
temperatures (20, 190, and 300
C). An additional powder metallurgy operation—pulsed hot 
pressingdoes not increase the strength of billets from either the water-atomized or gas-atomized 
powders. The alloy based on the water-atomized 0–63 m powder shows the maximum strength: 
483, 332, and 261 MPa at 20, 190, and 300C, respectively. 
Keywords: gas-atomized powders, water-atomized pow ders, high-temperature aluminum alloy, 
structure, strength, strain hardening. 
INTRODUCTION 
The development of high-temperature aluminum alloys is necessitated by their applications. It will suffice 
to mention transportation equipment requiring strong, corrosion-resistant, and lightweight materials with high 
performances, construction industry, power engineering, or household appliances. This explains why a great 
number of experimental high-temperature aluminum alloys have been developed in recent years. 
The systems with the highest properties include Al–Fe–Cr(Mn), Al–Cr–Zr(Mn), Al–Fe–V(Mo), and Al–
Fe–Ce. The chromium-based system is noteworthy among th em. The Al–Cr alloys have higher thermal stability at

High-temperature alloys of this nonequilibrium composition can be produced only during rapid melt 
solidification. The AlCr 3Fex alloy ( x = 0.1–3.0 at.%) was produced in [3] from powders by centrifugal melt 
atomization. The highest yield of the quasicrystalline phase was reached at 1 at.% Fe. This phase remained stable 
during holding for 6 h at 350C. The powders were degassed and compacted at the same temperature. 
Melt atomization with inert gas was used to make Al–4Cr–1Fe alloy powders in [4]. The powder was 
compacted in two stages: (i) cold pre ssing and (ii) hot extrusion. The mechan ical properties of the Al–7Cr–1Fe 
alloy are described in [5], where compacts were made from a mixture of 45 µm gas-atomized and 70 nm nanosized 
powders. To promote compaction, the powder mixture was subjected to 10-min pulsed treatment at 150 C. The 
powders were subsequently consolidated at 550 C and 40 MPa for 10 min. The resulting compacts showed the 
following mechanical properties at room temperature: elastic modulus E = 65 GPa, ultimate tensile strength 
UTS = 231 MPa, yield stress YS = 217 MPa, and elongation El = 0.8%. The mechanical properties of the alloy 
improved after treatment at 175C: E = 71 GPa, UTS = 266 MPa, YS = 240 MPa, El = 1.6%. 
The vapor deposition method was used in [6] to synthesize a decagonal quasicrystalline Al–Cr nanopowder 
in a laboratory unit. It was shown that the decagonal quas icrystalline nanopowder transformed into a crystalline one 
at 700C. The vapor-deposited Al–Cr–Fe alloys were studied in [7] at 5 to 9 wt.% Cr and 0.6 to 3.0 at.% Fe. The 
highest strength was exhibited by Al–8.7Cr–1.5Fe [7]. Great amounts of the Al–8.5Cr–1.2Fe alloy powder were 
produced by the vapor deposition method [8]. Vapors of the elements condensed on a collector to form the alloy of 
desired composition. The intermediate product as a 9-mm strip was rolled to a thickness of 1.7 mm. This resulted in 
a superfine fibrous material. 
There are many methods to produce rapidly solidified aluminum alloys. The highest cooling rate (to 
10
8 K/sec) is reached when the melt contacts with metal surf aces (rapidly rotating disks, cooled drums, rollers, etc.) 
that efficiently remove heat. However, these methods are not used commercially and are virtually limited to 
laboratory studies. 
Centrifugal melt atomization produces 0.1–5 mm granules. The drawbacks include a relatively low cooling 
rate (not higher than 103 K/sec) and cumbersome equipment. 
The vapor deposition method is not economical, especially for high-temperature applications requiring 
expensive equipment and much energy to transfer the element into a vapor phase. 
When the melt is atomized with compressed gases, its cooling rate reaches 10 5 
K/sec. This is currently the 
main commercial method of producing aluminum alloy powders (gas-atomized powders). To ensure explosion 
safety, the melt is atomized with compressed inert gases (n itrogen, argon, or air enriched with inert gas), which 
makes the process much more expensive. 
The method of producing water-atomized powders developed at the Frantsevich Institute for Problems of 
Materials Science is safe and ensures a cooling rate of 106 K/sec [9]. 
Based on the above, we chose Al–8Cr–1.5Fe alloy composition for studies. 
Our objective is to examine how the production meth od and properties of starting powders (structure, 
morphology, hardening quasicrystalline phase) influence the structure and mechanical properties of high-
temperature Al–8Fe–1.5Cr alloy billets. 
EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE 
Two methods were used to produce the Al–8Cr–1.5Fe alloy powder to compare their effectiveness: melt 
atomization with inert gas and with water.

compact (25 mm in diameter and 50 mm in height) from the powder pressed at room temperature to 30–40%

The mechanical properties of the billets were determined using an Instron 1246 tensile testing machine. The 
grippers moved at a rate of 1 mm/min, which corresponded to strain rate  = 1.1  10–3 sec–1, the stress–strain curve 
being recorded. The tests were performed at 20, 190, and 300 C. The yield stress, ultimate strength, and elongation 
were determined. The effective part of the test samples was 3 mm in diameter and 15 mm in length. 
The structure was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a Superprobe-733 
microanalyzer. The COMPO mode was used to identif y phases in the alloy. A DRON-2 diffractometer was 
employed for X-ray diffraction. 
The stress–strain curves show the features of the deformation process. To analyze them, we used the 
Ludwig equation derived for single-phase materials that undergo dislocation deformation [10]: 
 = s + NEn, 
where  is the yield stress for true plastic strain E (E = ln (1 + ) and  is the offset plastic strain); s is the offset 
yield stress; N is the strain hardening coefficient; n is the strain hardening index. 
The microhardness of powder particles was measured with a PMT-3 hardness meter at a load of 20 g. The 
powder was fixed with self-hardening acrylic resin in a steel cage. The hardness of the rods and powder billets was 
measured by a Vickers hardness tester at a load of 100 N on the indenter. 
To characterize the thermal stabilit y of the rods, the hardness of the samples was measured after holding 
from 1 to 100 h in a resistance furnace.

According to [11, 12], the quasicrystalline particles in SEM images are always round and some of them are 
pentagram-shaped. The microphotographs of the powders show that particles of the quasicrystalline phase of the 
water-atomized powder are finer and their number is greater than that in the gas-atomized powders in the absence of 
the rod-like phase. 
X-ray diffraction shows that the gas- and water-atomized Al–8Cr–1.5Fe alloy powders represent an 
aluminum matrix and hardening phases consisting of quasic rystals and crystalline intermetallics. The rods produced 
from these powders by pulsed hot pressi ng and hot extrusion have the same ph ase composition. According to [3], 
the quasicrystalline phase in this system may be presented as Al 74Fe12Cr12.5. Figure 3 shows standard X-ray 
diffraction patterns for the Al–8Cr–1.5Fe alloy. 
The extruded rods are fibrous (Fig. 4). The second hardening phase (light) has finer particles in the rods 
made from water-atomized powders (Fig. 4 b) than those from gas-atomized powders (Fig. 4 a). There are oxide 
particles (dark) along the fiber boundar ies; they are oxide films refined duri ng extrusion, which are present on the 
powder particles in accordance with [13]. 
Mechanical Properties of Powders and Billets. The hardness of extruded rods is higher than that of the 
starting powder particles. For example, the water-atomized powder particles (0–63 µm) had 1340 MPa hardness, 
and the rods extruded from this powder at 400 C
 
showed 1500 MPa. This can be explained by the formation of a 
cellular structure in the aluminum matrix and grain refinement during extrusion [14]. 
The hardness of the rods is also higher because th e oxide particles present on the powder particles are 
refined during extrusion and distributed along fibers in the extruded rod, contributing to its hardening [12, 14]. 
It has also been found that the hardness of rods depends on the particle size of both gas-atomized and 
water-atomized powders (Fig. 5). In case of 63–100 µm an d coarser powders, the har dness of rods made from the 
water-atomized powder is higher.

Analysis of Tables 3 and 4 shows that the additional operation (pulsed hot pressing) has no effect on the 
strength of billets, regardless the methods used to produce the starting powders. 
The true stress–true strain curves were used to an alyze differences in the ha rdening of PHP rods from 
water- and gas-atomized powders during tensile tests (Fig. 7) . The strain hardening of both rods proceeds in two 
stages greatly differing in its rate: it is very high at the first stage and slows down at the second stage (Table 5). The 
strain hardening of rods from the wate r-atomized powder is greater at both stages than that of rods from the gas-
atomized powder. 
The strength of rods must depend on the total amount, composition, and particle size of the hardening 
phase. The amount of the hardening phase in a first approximation depends on the doping content (at.%), while its 
composition, particle size, and amount in the matrix are determined by solidification conditions (i.e., powder 
production method) and by the billet production method. The N value increases with a sharp decrease in the free

CONCLUSIONS 
The processes of rapid solidification have opened possibilities for developing the high-temperature Al–
8Cr–1.5Fe alloy to be used in the range from room temperature to 300C. 
The mechanical characteristics of compacts substa ntially depend on the properties of starting powders. 
Particles of the second phase in the wa ter-atomized powders are finer and their number in the aluminum matrix is 
higher than in the gas-atomized powders. 
The extruded rods are fibrous. Oxid e films present on powder particles are refined in the extrusion process 
and distributed along fiber boundaries, providing hardening particles (quasicrystals and intermetallics), promoting 
higher hardness of the rods compared to the starting powder, and retarding recrystallization during annealing and 
heating. 
The rods extruded from the water-atomized powders have much higher hardness, ultimate strength, and 
yield stress at all temperatures (20, 190, and 300 C) than those from the gas-atom ized powders, plasticity being 
acceptable. 
An additional powder metallurgy operation ―pulsed hot pressing has no effect on the strength of billets 
from either the water-atomized or gas-atomize powder. 
Strain hardening proceeds in two stages. Its rate is very high at the first stage and lower at the second; the 
strain hardening of rods from the water-atomized powder is greater at both stages compared to rods from the gas-
atomized powder.
