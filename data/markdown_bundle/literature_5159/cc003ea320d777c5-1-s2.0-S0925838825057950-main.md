ABSTRACT
The drive for higher service temperatures, together with advances in additive manufacturing (AM), motivates 
efforts to improve the high-temperature microstructural stability of TiAl alloys. This study investigates the 
mechanisms by which Hf and Zr additions affect the formation and stability of lamellar structures at 1100 
◦
C, as 
well as their impact on the mechanical properties of a Ti-48Al-2Cr-based alloy (at. %), using a 2Nb-containing 
alloy as a reference. Hf alloying alone refined the lamellar structure and enhanced compressive strength at 
700 
◦
C for as-homogenized TiAl alloy. However, it also intensified Cr segregation, which facilitated the formation 
of coarsened γ phase via the B2 phase during annealing at 1100 
◦
C. Zr alloying produced the finest lamellar 
colonies and spacing in the as-homogenized alloy, yet it promoted an undesirable network-like γ structure prone 
to cracking under load. Notably, the combined Hf + Zr addition mitigated these adverse effects, yielding an 
optimal balance of hardness, high-temperature strength, and structural stability. The exceptional thermal sta -
bility of the Hf- and Zr-co-alloyed TiAl alloy is attributed to a higher equilibrium α -phase fraction at 1100 
◦
C, a 
reduced driving force for phase transformation due to lattice distortion, and decreased diffusion coefficients and 
α ₂ / γ interfacial energy. Although the materials were produced by casting and controlled heat treatments, the 
1100 
◦
C anneal reproduces the thermal environment encountered in AM builds. Accordingly, these mechanistic 
insights and alloying guidelines identified here are directly informative for tailoring TiAl alloys to withstand AM- 
related thermal histories.
1. Introduction
Owing to an attractive combination of low density, high specific 
strength, and favourable high-temperature creep resistance, γ -TiAl al -
loys are regarded as promising candidates for next-generation structural 
components in aerospace and automotive applications. Their mechani -
cal performance depends strongly on achieving a refined α ₂ / γ lamellar 
microstructure (i.e., colony size and lamellar spacing), which improves 
fracture toughness [1] , tensile strength [2] , and creep resistance [3] by 
deflecting cracks and restricting dislocation motion. However, this fine 
lamellar structure is thermodynamically unstable above 800 
◦
C and 
tends to coarsen [4,5] . This markedly degrades creep and fatigue 
resistance, with even minor deterioration (~10 %) to non-lamellar do -
mains causing significant performance losses [6 – 9] . Although 
multi-stage aging treatments have been proposed to stabilize fine 
lamellae [10] , they require tight process control and are inadequate 
under the complex thermal histories inherent to additive manufacturing 
(AM) [11] .
Among AM processes, electron-beam powder bed fusion (PBF-EB) 
offers rapid solidification that suppresses compositional segregation and 
produces fine, relatively homogeneous microstructures in TiAl alloys 
[12] . Nevertheless, the fine as-solidified microstructure inevitably un -
dergoes severe degradation during post-processing because of the pro -
longed high build temperatures (1000 – 1200 
◦
C), resulting in 
microstructural heterogeneity, mechanical property fluctuations along 
the build direction, and limited high-temperature reliability. These is -
sues may persist even after heat treatment [13,14] . Thus, there is a 
critical need for approaches that concurrently achieve lamellar refine -
ment and thermal stability.

Overall, Hf and Zr emerge as particularly promising alloying ele -

room-temperature strength, ductility and the ductile-to-brittle transition 
temperature via lattice distortion and solid-solution strengthening [27, 
28] . Nevertheless, excessive Hf promotes brittle eutectics such as Al ₃ Hf ₂ 
and TiAl ₂ [29] , and the specific role of Zr remains insufficiently clarified 
[30] . Moreover, co-adding Hf and Zr can increase compositional 
complexity and processing challenges. These considerations motivate a 
systematic evaluation of their individual and combined effects, which 
constitutes the focus of the present study.
The present study aims to systematically assess the individual and 
combined roles of Hf and Zr in refining lamellar structures and 
enhancing their stability at 1100 
◦
C (the exact addition levels is not the 
primary focus). Rather than employing AM builds directly, this work 
adopts a cast-and-anneal route to ensure precise thermal control and to 
reduce the material cost associated with multi-composition trials using 
expensive Hf/Zr/Nb powders. Furthermore, the operative strengthening 
mechanisms at room and elevated temperatures are summarized. The 
resulting insights provide a theoretical basis for alloy design and opti -
mization of γ -TiAl alloys, particularly for tailoring compositions to 
withstand AM-relevant thermal histories.
2. Material and methods
2.1. Sample preparation
Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Hf (2Hf), Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Zr (2Zr), Ti-48Al-2Cr-1Hf-1Zr 
(1Hf1Zr) alloys were investigated, using Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb (2 Nb) alloy 
as the reference. All alloys were produced by vacuum arc melting from 
high-purity elements ( > 99.9 wt%) and solidified on a water-cooled 
copper crucible. Each ingot was remelted and flipped at least six times 
to promote chemical homogeneity.
Because alloying additions alter the solidification path and phase 
transformation behavior of TiAl alloys, a common homogenization 
schedule was applied to isolate compositional effects and ensure 
comparability, using the 2 Nb alloy as a process reference. The 2 Nb 
composition lies within the α single-phase field at 1400 
◦
C, enabling a 
fully lamellar microstructure after furnace cooling. Accordingly, as-cast 
ingots were encapsulated in evacuated quartz tubes, homogenized at 
1400 
◦
C for 4 h, and furnace-cooled. Limited surface melting was 
observed for the 2Hf and 1Hf1Zr alloys ( Fig. S1 ), evidenced by a slight 
loss of the original rectangular geometry. The hold time was short and 
no surface oxidation was detected. Therefore, the extent of partial 
melting was minor and did not measurably affect the microstructural 
analyses or the mechanical results.
To assess lamellar stability, specimens were annealed under flowing 
argon at 1100 
◦
C for 48, 96, and 144 h, followed by furnace cooling. The 
choice of 1100 
◦
C reflects the reported build-temperature range of PBF- 
EB [31] . Equilibrium phase diagrams were computed using Thermo-Calc 
software with the TCTI4 database.
2.2. Microstructural characterization
The microstructures of the as-cast, as-homogenized, and as-annealed 
samples were examined using a Zeiss Gemini-300 scanning electron 
microscope (SEM) equipped with secondary electron (SE2) and back -
scattered electron (BSE) detectors, as well as energy dispersive X-ray 
spectroscopy (EDS). Grain boundaries and orientations were charac -
terized by electron back-scattering diffraction (EBSD, Oxford) with a 
step size of 0.1 – 0.2 μ m. EBSD data were analyzed using HKL Channel 5. 
Lamellar colony size and lamellar spacing were quantified with ImageJ. 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a Bruker D8 
diffractometer using Cu-K α radiation over the 20 – 90
◦
(2 θ ), with a 
continuous scan rate of 2 º /min. Phase identification employed Jade 6 
together with the PDF2009 database.
TEM (transmission electron microscopy, Tecnai G2 S-TWIN, 200 kV) 
was used to resolve substructures within the lamellae. HRTEM (high- 
resolution transmission electron microscopy), SAED (selected area 
electron diffraction), FFT (fast Fourier Transform), IFFT (inverse FFT) 
and GPA (geometric phase analysis) were combined to determine crystal 
structure of the relevant phases and to map local strain near lamellar 
boundaries. TEM foils were prepared by mechanical grinding to ~30 μ m 
followed by ion milled with a Gatan 691.
Because α ₂ lamellae act as structural stabilizing agents that help 
preserve lamellar integrity at elevated temperature [32] , the α ₂ lamellar 
spacing was used as an indicator of coarsening. Measurements followed 
the intercept method on ≥ 200 SEM-captured lamellar colonies.
2.3. Mechanical testing
Vickers microhardness measurements was measured at room tem -
perature and 700 ℃ using a Berkovich diamond indenter with a load of 
500 gf and a dwell time of 10 s under a high-purity argon atmosphere. 
Five indentations were performed per condition to obtain the average 
value.
Cylindrical specimens ( Φ 2.5 mm × 5 mm) were cut from the central 
region of the as-homogenized ingots by EDM used for compressive test. 
Prior to testing, the specimen surfaces were ground to 2000-grit using 
SiC paper. Compressive properties were measured on an LE5105 uni -
versal testing machine at 700 ℃ with a nominal strain rate of 1 × 10

s
 1
. Owing to specimen size limitations, an extensometer was not 
employed and only compressive strength was obtained. Three replicate 
tests were conducted for each alloy to ensure data reliability.

3.2. Microstructural stability of the TiAl alloys with different alloying 
elements
The microstructures after 48 h annealing are shown in Fig. 6 . In the 
2 Nb alloy, the degradation initiates preferentially at lamellar colony 
boundaries, where coarse γ grains containing spheroidized α ₂ form

degradation at the colony boundaries manifested as mutual intrusion 
and coarsening ( Fig. 6 c). Inside colonies, well-aligned B2 particles, 
which derived from residual segregation, are surrounded by coarse γ 
phase ( Fig. 6 d). This indicates that B2 formation accelerates local α ₂

To investigate the reason for the superior microstructural stability of 
the 1Hf1Zr alloy, the lamellar colony boundaries in the as-annealed

As shown in Fig. 7 a, the advancing front of coarsening γ lamellae 
invading the colony interior in the 2 Nb alloy is a HAGB, and its pro -

lamellar regions, no ODBs (including LAGBs) were observed. By 
contrast, in the 1Hf1Zr alloy after annealing, no twinning was detected

The lamellar interfaces in the as-annealed 2 Nb and 1Hf1Zr alloys 
were further analyzed by TEM. Fig. 8 a shows a decomposing α ₂ lamellae 
adjacent to a γ lamella in the 2 Nb alloy. Degradation was examined 
both along the lamellae at the tip ( Fig. 8 b-e) and across the lamellae on 
the lamellar plane ( Fig. 8 f-i). The transition region between the α ₂ tip 
and γ ( Fig. 8 b) exhibits fringes parallel to (0001) α ₂ planes, which are 
arranged with a periodicity of three atomic planes ( Fig. 8 c). The 
measured periodicity is 1.415 nm, approximately three times the 
(0001) α ₂ interplanar spacing, consistent with reports of an intermediate 
phase [42] . On the γ side of the interlayer ( Fig. 8 d), no ledges are 
observed. The structural transformation proceeds by dislocation glide on 
every 6th {1 1 1} γ plane, producing stacking faults with a periodicity of 
1.436 nm. On the α ₂ side of the interlayer ( Fig. 8 e), dislocations glide on 
every 6th (01 10) α ₂ plane with a measured periodicity of 1.439 nm. This 
type of stacking fault array resembles that reported by Yu et al. [43] and 
is known to attract Al segregation. A plausible sequence for α ₂ decom -
position is therefore: (i) formation of stacking faults within α ₂ , which 
attract Al to achieve the requisite compositional change; (ii) develop -
ment of the observed intermediate phase and periodic stacking on the γ 
side via dislocation glide; and (iii) gradual ordering into the L1 ₀ - γ phase.

3.3. Mechanical properties of TiAl alloys with different alloying elements
The mechanical properties of the as-homogenized alloys are sum -
marized in Table 2 . All microhardness measurements were performed 
within lamellar regions to specifically assess lamellar hardness. At room 
temperature, lamellar hardness is highest for 2Zr alloy and lowest for the 
2 Nb alloy. It correlates directly with the lamellar spacing, confirming 
the effect of refinement strengthening. Solid solution strengthening is 
also expected, given the larger atomic-radius mismatch of Hf/Zr with Ti 
(0.159/0.160 nm vs. 0.140 nm) relative to Nb (0.148 nm). While the 
hardness of the 2 Nb and 2Hf alloys dropped by 18.1 % and 13.3 % at 
700 
◦

2.9 %. Remarkably, the 1Hf1Zr alloy exhibited a slight hardness in -
crease of 1.2 %. These results indicate that the 2Zr and particularly the 
1Hf1Zr alloys possess an enhanced capacity to resist deformation and 
retain hardness at elevated temperatures.
To probe the origin of this enhanced high-temperature hardness 
retention in the Hf- and Zr-containing alloys, the post-indentation sur -
faces were analyzed. Figs. 10a1-d1 reveal no evidence of precipitation 
within the indented regions, ruling out precipitation strengthening as a 
primary mechanism. Although all indents are surrounded by lamellae, 
the 2 Nb alloy shows microstructural degradation, which likely 
contributed to its reduced hardness. Moreover, numerous pores are 
observed on the 2 Nb indentation surface, which are a result of surface 
oxidation during the test, as indicated by EDS mapping ( Fig. 10 e). A 
repeat high-temperature hardness test on the 2 Nb alloy reproduced the 
original result, thereby excluding test-condition artefacts (see Fig. S8 ). 
This observation is not used to rank oxidation resistance but only to 
describe the local surface condition around the indent under our 
experimental configuration.
XPS analysis was performed to qualitatively characterize the sample 
surface. Ti-O and Al-O bonds were identified in all samples 
(Figs. 10a2 – 10d3). Notably, the 1Hf1Zr alloy was the only sample to 
show distinct peaks from the underlying metallic substrate (Ti-(Ti, Al)), 
suggesting a comparatively thinner oxide layer. his interpretation is 
consistent with the O1s spectra (Figs. 10a4-d4), which show a system -
atic decrease in the O-Ti/O-Al intensity ratio in the order 2 Nb > 2Hf 
> 2Zr > 1Hf1Zr. Considering that a higher O-Ti peak intensity is 
generally associated with a transition from protective Al ₂ O ₃ to less 
protective TiO ₂ [45] , these results qualitatively suggest that the 1Hf1Zr 
alloy experiences less surface degradation at elevated temperature, 
which contributes to its superior hardness retention under our test 
conditions. However, we emphasize that the present XPS data are 
qualitative, and high-temperature microhardness testing is not a stan -
dardized protocol for evaluating oxidation resistance. A comprehensive 
assessment of oxidation behavior and rigorous mechanistic resolution 
will require additional cross-sectional or kinetic studies, which are 
planned for future work.

4.1. Effect of Hf and Zr on the as-homogenized microstructure
According to the calculated phase diagrams ( Fig. 12 ), the severe 
segregation in the 2Hf alloy is attributed to its very wide solidification 
range (217 
◦
C). It provides a large thermodynamic driving force and 
sufficient time for diffusion and segregation of the heavy Hf and Cr 
atoms. The prominent network-like γ and B2 phases in the 2Zr alloy are a 
direct consequence of its solidification and subsequent solid-state 
transformations. The primary α -phase rejects Zr into the liquid, which 
then forms the Zr-rich γ network. During this process, Cr, which is poorly 
soluble in the γ phase, is expelled and partitions to the B2 phase 
( Table S2 ), causing B2 to precipitate within the γ network ( Fig. 1 f). This 
metastable B2 phase later decomposes upon annealing, explaining the 
formation of the clean HAGBs observed in the homogenized state 
( Fig. 3 d). The superior microstructural homogeneity of the 1Hf1Zr alloy 
can thus be understood. Its reduced Zr content inherently suppresses the 
formation of the extensive γ network. Moreover, the uniform distribu -
tion of Hf observed in this alloy ( Table S2 ) suggests a synergistic inter -
action wherein Zr mitigates the strong segregation tendency of Hf.

and the lamellar γ ( Fig. 3 d), together with the shared Blackburn OR with

4.2. Effect of Hf and Zr on the microstructural degradation
The four alloys follow distinct degradation pathways. During 
annealing, the B2 located at lamellar colony boundaries decomposes and 
rejects Cr. Because Cr is less soluble in γ than in the α ₂ phase ( Table S2 ), 
local Cr enrichment hinders γ formation. Accordingly, the 2 Nb and 2Hf 
alloys, which have the lowest B2-phase Cr content (7.3 ± 1.1 % and 8.3 
± 1.3 %), degrade preferentially at colony boundaries through nucle -
ation and growth of equiaxed γ . By contrast, degradation in the 2Zr 
alloy, despite its high B2-phase Cr content (14.1 ± 1.0 %), is governed 
by a crystallographic relationship that facilitates invasion of the 
network-like γ into neighboring colonies. The 1Hf1Zr alloy, which also 
has high B2-Cr (12.6 ± 2.1 %), lacks a strong driver for localized 
degradation and thus evolves more uniformly.
From Fig. 6 i, the lamellar-fraction decay rates are estimated as 
0.7 %/h for the 2 Nb and 2Hf alloys, 0.4 %/h for the 2Zr alloy, and only 
0.3 %/h for the 1Hf1Zr alloy, confirming that the co-addition of Hf and 
Zr is most effective in slowing colony degradation. The rapid degrada -
tion of the 2 Nb alloy is driven by a powerful thermodynamic driving 
force. Its equilibrium structure at the annealing temperature of 1100 
◦
C 
is 100 % γ -phase, providing the maximum impetus for the α ₂ →γ trans -
formation. In addition, the large lattice distortion induced by Hf and Zr 
increases the activation energy for atomic diffusion, thereby kinetically 
hindering the interface migration required for γ coarsening. This kinetic 
suppression is compounded by chemical driving force factors related to 
Cr content, as previously discussed.

After annealing, the 2 Nb alloy contains abundant twins, while the 
1Hf1Zr alloy is twin-free. This indicates that the stacking fault energy 
(SFE) of the 2 Nb alloy is lower than 1Hf1Zr alloy. The different effects 
of these elements on SFE stem from fundamental differences in their 
electronic structure and atomic size. The additional valence electron of 
Nb enhances metallic bonding through hybridization, which is known to 
reduce the SFE [67,68] . Conversely, the large atomic radii of Hf and Zr 
induce significant lattice distortion, which elevates the SFE [67] . The 
interfacial stress state further contributes to this dichotomy. Addition -
ally, the α ₂ / γ interface in the 1Hf1Zr alloy is predominantly under ten -
sile stress ( Fig. 9 i), a condition known to suppress twinning [66] . The 
interface in the 2 Nb alloy, however, exhibits a more complex mixed 
stress state ( Fig. 8 i). Therefore, we conclude that Nb facilitates the α ₂ →γ 
transformation by promoting annealing twins, while the synergistic 
addition of Hf and Zr suppresses this pathway, thereby enhancing 
microstructural stability.
4.3. Implications for high-temperature service and additive manufacturing
Raising the service temperature of TiAl components requires stable 
microstructures that resist lamellar coarsening and equiaxed γ formation 
during prolonged exposure. The present results identify alloying path -
ways that enhance lamellar stability at 1100 
◦
C.
These thermal drivers are intrinsic to PBF-EB, where parts dwell for 
extended periods at ~1000 – 1200 
◦
C during building. Although AM 
builds are not employed, the 1100 
◦
C isothermal annealing used here 
captures the same build plateau at which fine as-solidified lamellae are 
prone to coarsening. The cast-and-anneal platform provides a cost- 
effective, well-controlled surrogate that decouples chemistry-driven ef -
fects from processing artifacts (e.g., scan strategy, layer-wise reheating, 
residual stress) for AM, which is particularly relevant given the cost and 
limited availability of Nb/Hf/Zr-bearing powders. Accordingly, the 
mechanisms established under this controlled annealing are directly 
informative for AM-processed TiAl.
Under this framework, three trends emerge. (i) Hf alone refines 
lamellae but aggravates Cr-assisted B2 →γ coarsening upon annealing, 
(ii) Zr alone yields the finest colony/spacing yet tends to form the un -
desirable network-like γ in as-homogenized TiAl alloy, and (iii) co- 
addition of Hf and Zr mitigates both drawbacks, delivering the most 
stable lamellar architecture and superior high-temperature strength and 
hardness retention. Mechanistically, co-alloying increases the equilib -
rium α fraction at 1100 
◦
C, reduces the transformation driving force, and 
lowers diffusion-controlled degradation kinetics. These insights trans -
late to practical guidance for both service and AM contexts. Practically, 
these trends provide alloy-design guidance for AM: favor combined Hf 
and Zr additions over single-element additions to suppress α ₂ spher -
oidization and coarsening γ during the build; manage β /B2 susceptibility 
by balancing Cr with Hf; and target chemistries that reduce α ₂ / γ mobility 
to restrain colony and lamellar-spacing coarsening during thermal pla -
teaus. While potential AM-specific process ( “ smoke ” , thermal cycling) 
and microstructural factors (banded microstructure, columnar grains, 
massive γ ) may modulate the magnitude of these effects, the mechanistic 
hierarchy outlined here provides a transferable rationale for alloy 
selection resilient to both service conditions and PBF-EB thermal 
histories.
5. Conclusions
The mechanisms by which Nb, Hf, and Zr additions affect the 
microstructure, 1100
◦
C high-temperature stability, and strength of a Ti- 
48Al-2Cr-based alloy were compared and revealed. The main findings 
are as follows: 
1) Hf and Zr additions are more effective than Nb in refining the 
lamellar colony size and spacing. This is attributed to their combined 
effect of the higher density of nucleation sites, larger phase trans -
formation driving force, and greater undercooling, as well as the 
impediment of atomic diffusion by lattice distortion, with Zr being 
the most potent refining agent.
2) In the Hf-containing TiAl alloy, Cr segregation leads to the formation 
of a B2 phase during annealing, which accelerates γ phase coars -
ening. Zr-containing alloy degradation is driven by network- γ inva -
sion, while Hf + Zr co-addition leads to uniform degradation without 
preferential sites.
3) The superior microstructural stability of the Hf-Zr co-alloyed sample 
stems from a combination of (i) a higher equilibrium α -phase frac -
tion, (ii) a reduced α ₂ →γ transformation driving force which relies on 
lattice distortion, and (iii) kinetically suppressed interface migration 
resulting from lower diffusion coefficients and reduced α ₂ / γ inter -
facial energy.
4) Hf primarily enhances compressive strength of as-homogenized TiAl 
while Zr maximizes lamellar hardness. The synergistic co-addition of 
Hf and Zr eliminates detrimental phases, yielding the best strength 
and hardness due to a combination of refinement, solid solution 
strengthening and high-temperature stability.
