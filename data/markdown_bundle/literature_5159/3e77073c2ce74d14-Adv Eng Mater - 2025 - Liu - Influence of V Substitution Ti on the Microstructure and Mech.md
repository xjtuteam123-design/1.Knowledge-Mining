1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), which deviate from traditional alloy
design concepts, have garnered signiﬁcant research interest due to
their high strength, outstanding wear resis-
tance, and superior corrosion resistance.[1– 3]
Among various HEA systems, refractory
high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) stand out for
their high-temperature strength and ther-
mal stability. [4– 6] However, conventional
RHEAs primarily consist of high-density
elements such as W, Ta, Mo, and Nb, result-
ing in excessive density ( >12 g cm/C0 3)t h a t
limits their practical applications. [7,8]
Due to the lightweight design concept, a
novel class of lightweight RHEAs has been
developed by replacing some high-density
elements with low-density elements (e.g.,
Al, Ti, Zr, V), including AlNbTiZr, [9– 11]
AlNbTiV,[12] and CrNbTiZr. [13] These new

In this study, new refractory high-entropy alloys of Al 5Nb20Ti(45/C0 x)Zr30Vx (x = 0,
5, 10, and 15 at.%, described as V0, V5, V10, and V15) are designed by the
strategy of gradient substitution of Ti by V. The effects of V content on the
microstructure and mechanical properties of the alloys are systematically
investigated. The results show that the as-cast alloys all exhibit a single body-
centered cubic (BCC) structure. The introduction of V element signi ﬁcantly
reduces the lattice parameter and induces sever lattice distortion, which
enhances the room-temperature yield strength from 809 MPa for V0 alloy to
1248 MPa for V15 alloy. However, excessive V content (15 at.%) leads to a
reduction in plasticity, with a fracture strain of 29%. High-temperature
compression test (800 °C) shows that the yield strength of V15 alloy (194 MPa)
increases by 104% compared to that of V0 alloy (95 MPa). Microstructure
analysis shows that dynamic recrystallization and dynamic recovery dominate the
softening process at high temperatures.

2.4.2. Mechanical Properties at High Temperature of 800 °C
The compressive stress – strain curves of the cast
Al5Nb20Ti(45/C0 x)Zr30Vx alloys at 800 °C are shown in Figure 8 .
The corresponding compressive mechanical properties at
800 °C of the alloys are listed in Table 5 . The results show that
the yield strength at high temperatures of the alloys decreases
signiﬁcantly relative to the yield strength at room temperature,
suggesting that the alloys are weakly resistant to softening at high
temperatures. Elevated temperatures enhance atomic vibrational
energy, increasing both the frequency and amplitude of atomic
oscillations and facilitating easier passage through the grain
boundary (GB) barrier. [38] Therefore, the observed increase in
softening at higher deformation is due to a reducing interatomic
cohesion and an increasing atomic mobility. Consequently, ther-
mal activation becomes more pronounced at high-temperature
compression, leading to remarkably softening of the alloys
through accelerating dislocation activities and a more signi ﬁcant
number of slip systems.
The high-temperature yield strength of the alloys still
increased with the increase of V element content. At 800 °C,
the yield strength of V0 alloy is only 95 MPa, while the yield
strength of V15 alloy reaches 194 MPa, which is an increase
of 104% compared to that of V0 alloy. At a compressive strain
of 50%, all four alloys still do not fracture and display excellent
plasticity at high temperature. High-temperature compression
experiments demonstrate that V has better high-temperature sta-
bility than Ti.

2.6. High-Temperature Performance
In order to exclude the effect of phase transformation on the
high-temperature compressive of Al 5Nb20Ti(45/C0 x)Zr30Vx
RHEAs, XRD and TEM analyses were performed on V15 alloy
after compression at 800 °C. As shown in Figure 10 , the results
reveal that the V15 alloy maintains a single-phase BCC structure
without any phase transformation occurring during high-
temperature compression, which is consistent with its precom-
pression state.
Figure 11 presents the EBSD results of the
Al5Nb20Ti(45/C0 x)Zr30Vx alloys after 50% strain at 800 °C after
compression. The deformed grains are elongated perpendicular
to the compression direction, exhibiting preferred orientations of
<101> and <001>, as shown in Figure 11a – d. Figure 11e – h
shows the GB maps, in which gray, red, and black lines corre-
spond to low-angle GBs (2° – 10°), medium-angle GBs (10° – 15°),
and high-angle GBs ( >15°), respectively. It can be seen from
Figure 11e– h that there are numerous small recrystallized grains
surrounded by high-angle GBs and medium-angle GBs at the
original GBs of the V0, V5, V10, and V15 alloys, displaying
necklace-like structures. Therefore, dynamic recrystallization
occurred in the four alloys at high temperature. [44] The stress –
strain curves reveal an initial linear increase in ﬂow stress attrib-
uted to work hardening induced by dislocation accumulation in
grains. The softening phenomenon on the stress– strain curves of
high-temperature compression is linked to dynamic recovery and
dynamic recrystallization. [45]
Figure 11e – h also shows that the fraction of recrystallized
grains in the alloys increases with increasing V content, indicat-
ing an enhanced softening phenomenon as V content rises.
Notably, the V15 alloy exhibits a substantially greater increase
in recrystallized grains compared to the V10 alloy, which corre-

stress– strain curve of the V15 alloy (Figure 7). This accelerated
softening phenomenon can be attributed to the formation of
numerous ﬁne recrystallized grains that signi ﬁcantly re ﬁne
the grain structure while increasing the GB density. At elevated
temperatures, these GBs facilitate enhanced sliding and diffu-
sion process, thereby promoting localized plastic deformation
and intensifying softening. [46] Moreover, the high fraction of
dynamic recrystallization grains formed accelerates the rate of
dislocation consumption, which not only diminishes the effec-
tiveness of dislocation strengthening but also fails to compensate
for the dominant softening process.

4. Experimental Section
RHEAs with nominal compositions of Al 5Nb20Ti(45/C0 x)Zr30Vx (x = 0, 5, 10,
15 at.%), herein labeled as V0, V5, V10, and V15, were prepared via vac-
uum arc melting in an argon atmosphere. High-purity raw materials
(>99.9%) were used for the alloys preparation. Prior to the alloy melting,
pure Ti was melted 2– 3 times to remove residual oxygen in the furnace. To
ensure homogeneity and complete melting of the constituents with differ-
ent melting points, the alloy ingots were ﬂipped and remelted at least ﬁve
times. The weight of each ingot is 60 g and the weight loss after melting is
less than 1%, indicating that the actual compositions are consistent with
the nominal values.
The phases in the alloys were analyzed by a PANalytical X ’pert Pro
