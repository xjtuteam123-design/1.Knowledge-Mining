Abstract 
The effect of alloying elements in the substrate on the interdiffusio n processes between a 
NiCoCrAlY-coating and model Ni -base superalloys was studied. Four single -crystal substrates 
Ni-10Cr-8Al-X (wt%, X = 10Co, 9Ta, 6W) were coated with a conventional NiCoCrAlY-coating 
and exposed to air for up to 1000 h at 1050 °C. The microstructural analyses (LOM/SEM/EDX) 
revealed a s trong dependence of the coating degradation rate on the substrate composition. The 
coating applied to the ternary base Ni-10Cr-8Al alloy and to the quaternary Co-containing 
substrate degraded most rapidly while the Ta addition to the substrate effectively suppressed 
interdiffusion. The degradation phenomena such as -NiAl depletion and propagation of the 
secondary reaction zone (SRZ) were i nterpreted in terms of a thermodynamical analysis in 
Thermo-Calc and by using a CALPHAD-based thermodynamic-kinetic diffusion model. 
Keywords: Ni-base alloys, MCrAlY-coatings, oxidation, interdiffusion, diffusion modeling

1. Introduction 
Ni-base superalloys are state-of-the-art construction materials for turbine blades and vanes which 
are exposed to aggressive environments at extremely high temperatures under high mechanical 
loads [1]. Their exceptional high -temperature creep strength relies on an optimized 
microstructure with a high volume fraction (up to approx. 70%) of coherent finely -dispersed 
cuboidal γ'-Ni3(Al, Ti, Ta) precipitates in the γ-Ni matrix [1]. In contrast, the oxidation resistance 
of the modern , highest strength Ni-based superalloys is generally poor [2-5]. The oxidation 
resistance of turbine blades and vanes is thus improved by applying external pro tective coatings 
which are rich in Al and/or Cr [6-9]. The most commonly used protective coatings in the gas 
turbine technology today are p lasma-sprayed overlay MCrAlY -coatings (M=Ni and/or Co). A 
typical microstructure of a MCrAlY consists of a ductile γ-FCC matrix along with a high fraction 
of β-NiAl precipitates that serve as an Al source for retaining protective alumina scale formation 
[10, 11]. Chemical degradation of MCrAlY-coatings during operation is governed by continuous 
dissolution of the β -phase due to Al loss by oxide sca le formation [12-14] and by interdiffusion 
processes between the coating and the subst rate alloy [15-17]. Complete consumption of the β -
phase is often considered the end of the chemical life-time of the coating . Interdiffusion results 
from compositional gradients in the system (e.g. Al,Cr,Co -rich coating and Ni,Ti,W-rich 
substrate alloy) and may have a number of detrimental consequences for the load -bearing 
capacity of the coated component and for the coating adhesion: 
1. Gradual Al and β-phase depletion which eventually results in chemical failure of the 
coating, i.e. formation of less protective oxide phases [18], 
2. Al and Cr ingress into the superalloy result ing in precipitation of undesired TCP-
phases and/or carbide precipitates in the secondary reaction zone (SRZ), i.e. the 
interdiffusion-affected zone between coating and superalloy,

3. Formation of voids at the coating/substrate -interface which may adversely affect 
coating-substrate adherence. 
In order to minimize interdiffusion damage, the coating/substrate combination has to be 
optimized. The complexity of modern superalloys (consisting of up to 14 alloying elements [19]) 
significantly complicates the interpretation and prediction of interdiffusion processes. 
The aim of the present work is to evaluate the influence of alloying elements in the superalloy 
base material on the interdiffusion processes between an MCrAlY -coating and model Ni -10Cr-
8Al-X (wt%, X = 10Co, 9Ta, 6W) substrates during high -temperature exposure. An important 
aspect of the study is the application of thermodynamic equilibrium calculations and diffusion 
modelling in interpreting the experimental observations. The emerging commercial simul ation 
tools such as Thermo-Calc/DICTRA [20, 21], JMatPro [22] as well as similar in-house developed 
simulation approaches already have proven their efficiency in mo deling interdiffusion in 
MCrAlY-coating systems [16, 23-28]. Such calculations may significantly reduce the cost and 
amount of experimental testing required in the coating design and development process.

3. Modeling procedure 
A CALPHAD-based coupled thermodynamic-kinetic approach was applied in the present work to 
interpret the experimental observations. The modelling procedure is based on the 1D 
homogenization approach introduced by Larsson et al. [29] that allows to model multiphase 
diffusion by considering a single phase region with averaged kinetic properties. This method was 
later employed by Nijdam et al. [12] to model oxidation of freestanding MCrAlY-coatings. 
Recently, Pillai et al. [27] successfully applied the homogenization model to simulate the 
degradation of commercial MCrAlY-coated systems. The latter diffusion model was employed in 
the present work. 
The computational program was implemented in a FORTRAN code using the Thermo-Calc 
calculation interface TQ -v.9 [20]. The thermodynamic and kinetic data for equilibrium 
calculations were extracted from the TCNi5 [30] and MOBNi3 [31] databases, respectively. 
Further improvements of the model, such as parallelization and adaptive time-stepping, described 
elsewhere [32], were implemented to increase computation efficiency. 
The chemical composition of the as -received mat erial ( Table 1) was used as the initial 
composition with an error f unction employed at the coating/substrate -interface. Yttrium was not 
included in the calculation du e to its absence in the TCNI5 thermodynamic database. Al loss due 
to oxide formation was derived from experimentally determined oxidation kinetics and set as a 
boundary condition at the oxide/coating -interface. The exclusive formation of alumina (Al 2O3) 
was assumed on the coating surface. A zero -flux boundary condition was applied at the right

4. Results and Discussion 
4.1 General remarks 
The metallographic cross -sections of the coat ed systems after exposure for 100 h ( Fig. 2a) and 
1000 h ( Fig. 2b) at 1050 °C in air show significant differe nces in chemical degradation of the 
coating by simultaneous oxidation and interdiffusion. The main microstructural effects shown in 
Fig. 2 can be summarized as follows: 
1. Formation of the oxidation induced β -phase depletion zone beneath the surface 
oxide scale, 
2. Increasing β-phase depletion with increased exposure time, 
3. Propagation of the secondary γ+β two -phase reaction zone (SRZ) beneath the 
coating/substrate-interface. 
Fig. 2 shows that the β-phase dissolution kinetics can be dramatically changed by a single 
alloying addition in the substrate. The exposure for 1000 h at 1050 °C in air resulted in almost 
complete dissolution of β -phase in the coating in systems S 0 and SCo. In contrast, presence of Ta 
in the alloy significantly retarded the coating degradation and propagation of the SRZ . The 
average volume fraction of remnant β-phase in the coating core of S Ta was measured to be 31.5% 
that is nearly half of the initial β-phase content in the coating (Section 2.3). 
Analysis of the interdiffusion processes in the following s ections is based on the results of 
thermodynamic equilibrium calculations in Thermo-Calc and diffusion modelling using available 
thermodynamic and kinetic data. An adequate interpretation of the obtained results require s 
consideration of the following aspects when comparing the experimental and modeling results: (i) 
variation in coating thickness , which represents the reservoir for Al, Cr and Co ; (ii) accuracy of 
phase fraction measurements by image analysis; (iii) reliability of available thermo dynamic and

4.2 Phase equilibria in Ni-Cr-Al-X system 
Fig. 3a shows a calculated isothermal section of the Ni-rich part of the ternary Ni-Cr-Al system at 
1050 °C. The two-phase γ+γ' microstructure (green) is a basis for high temperature creep strength 
of the Ni-base superalloys [1]. As indicated in Fig. 4, the chosen base Ni-10Cr-8Al model alloy 
(substrate S0) is located in the γ+γ' region with the calculated γ' mole fraction of approximately 
0.55 at 1050 °C. 
The influence of Co on the ternary Ni -Cr-Al system is illustrated using a calculated Ni-Cr-Al 
section of the quaternary system Ni -Cr-Al-10Co (Fig. 3b). In agreement with the data previously 
reported in the literature [11, 41, 42], additi ons of Co significantly expand the two -phase γ+β 
field and/or suppress γ' -Ni3Al. Co is also known to have a strong adverse effect on γ' -phase 
solvus temperature [43]. The calculated mole fraction of γ' -precipitates in S Co (fγ' = 0.41 at 1050 
°C) is substantially lower than that in the base model alloy S0 (fγ' = 0.55 at 1050 °C, Fig. 4). 
According to the calculated quaternary section in Fig. 3c, the addition of 9 wt% Ta suppresses the 
size of the γ+β field in the ternary Ni -Cr-Al section and enlarges the γ+ γ' field. Compared to 
other refractory elements present in common single-crystal nickel-base superalloys (e.g. Mo, W, 
Re) Ta not only acts as a solid solution strengthener in the γ-matrix but also provides extensive 
precipitation strengthening due to its preferentia l partitioning into the γ' -phase [43-45]. The 
calculated mole fraction of γ' -Ni3(Al, Ta) in S Ta at 1050 °C is 0.81. Ta also increases the solvus

4.3 Effect of substrate composition on chemical activities of Al, Cr and Ni 
The chemical interaction between Cr and Al is an important aspect in understanding the influence 
of alloying elements on the thermodynamics in the Ni -Cr-Al system. Addition of Al to a binary 
Ni-Cr alloy is known to substantially increase the activity of Cr (aCr) by reducing its solubility in 
the γ-Ni matrix [17, 46, 47]. A similar effect on Al activity can be observed when Cr is added to a 
Ni-Al alloy. These interactions between Cr and Al will be considered in the further evaluations. 
According to the thermodynamic equilibrium calculations in Thermo-Calc, addition of Co to Ni -
xCr-8Al (x=0, 5, 10 , wt %) at 1050° C results in an increase in aAl (Fig. 5a) as a result of γ' 
dissolution and the release of Al into the γ-matrix. The Cr activity increase induced by the higher 
level of aAl in S Co is compensated by a larger fraction of γ-Ni, in which the Cr solubility is 
substantially higher than in the γ'-phase. Adding Co to Ni-10Cr-8Al does not significantly alter 
the Cr activity (Fig. 6). The equilibrium calculations for Ni -10Cr with a continuous addition of 
Co also showed only a weak effect on the aCr.

The calculated Al activity in S Ta is higher than in the base model alloy Ni-10Cr-8Al (Fig. 5b). 
The addition of Ta to a binary single phase Ni-8Al alloy (0 Cr line) initially slightly increases the

It leads to an increase in the Cr activity, which in turn affects the Al activity (Fig. 6). At lower Cr

5b). The Al and Cr activities in the base alloy Ni -10Cr-8Al thus strongly increase with Ta 
addition (Fig. 6). 
Additions of W to the Ni-10Cr-8Al system increase the Al and Cr activity in a similar manner as 
Ta. However, while Ta participates in the formation of γ' and enlarges the γ+γ'-field (Fig. 3c), W 
stabilizes the γ'-phase mainly by reducing the Al and Cr solubility in the γ -Ni. Hence, the 
decrease in aAl due to the binding of Al in γ'-Ni3Al is less pronounced than in STa (compare 0% 
Cr and 5% Cr curves in Fig. 5b and Fig. 5c). Compared to Ta, the effect of W addition on the Cr 
activity is weaker (Fig. 6), due to a nearly even partitioning of W between γ and γ' at this 
temperature thus providing a higher solubility of Cr in γ' (compare Fig. 3c and Fig. 3d). The 
thermodynamic equilibrium calculations showed a higher Cr content in the γ'-phase of SW than in 
that of STa.

4.5 Mechanisms of observed phenomena 
The experimental results in the present work revealed a significant effect of substrate alloy 
composition on the β-phase dissolution in the coating (Fig. 7a and Fig. 7b) and propagation of the 
secondary reaction zone beneath the coating/substrate-interface (Fig. 7c) during high temperature 
exposure. The calculated profiles in Fig. 7 were obtained by a modelling procedure described in 
Section 3. 
All four coating systems exhibited similar oxidation kinetics, which followed nearly parabolic 
behavior, as determined by measuring the oxide scale thicknesses on metallographic cross -
sections. However, the rate of subscale β-phase dissolution associated with Al consumption as a 
result of oxide formation was found to be different in the systems investigated (Fig. 7a) and to be 
strongly affected by interdiffusion . For instance, after exposure for 300 h at 1050 °C in air, the 
measured subscale β-depletion zone width was 30 .1±2.5 µm in S 0 compared to 18.3±3 .0 µm in 
the Ta-containing system STa. To verify the effect of interdiffusion on subscale β-phase depletion, 
a calculation of the freestanding MCrAlY coating was performed using the procedure described 
in Section 3, thus taking into account only Al loss due to oxide formation. The model predicts a 
near parabolic growth of the subscale β-dissolution zone in the freestanding coating with the zone 
width of 12.5 µm after 300 h at 1050 °C (Fig. 7a), which is more than two times thinner than 
observed for the coated system S0. 
Along with the oxidation-induced Al consumption, the β-NiAl dissolution is related to the Al, Cr 
and Co loss due to interdiffusion between the coating and the substrate. The β-phase depletion in 
the coating applied to S Co after 1000 h at 1050 °C in air was similar to that in the base system S0 
(Fig. 7b), despite a lower aAl and aNi and a very similar aCr gradient between the coating and the 
substrate of the SCo system. This result suggests that the thermodynamic driving forces alone are 
insufficient for interpreting the interdiffusion processes. Apparently, the effect of alloying

additions on the elemental mobilities must be considered. The Co alloyed substrate possessed a 
microstructure with lower γ'-fraction than the other alloys investigated ( Fig. 4). This led to a 
faster transport of the alloying elements in the substrate and a rapid coating degradation despite 
the decreased Al and Ni activity gradients (i.e. smaller driving forces for diffusion). In contrast to 
Co, addition of Ta and W strongly s tabilized the γ'-phase in the Ni -Cr-Al system at 1050 °C 
(Section 4.2). A higher fraction of γ'-phase near the coating/substrate-interface of STa and SW acts 
as a diffusion barrier and, coupled with lower Al, Cr and Ni activity gradients than in S0, 
effectively hinders th e coating degradation . The mobility values in Table 2 also suggest that W 
and especially Ta strongly decrease the mobility of Ni in γ and γ', thus additionally slowing down 
Ni diffusion from the substrate towards the coating. 
The continuous propagation of the secondary reaction zone (SRZ) beneath the coating/substrate -
interface during high temperature exposure was observed in all coating systems ( Fig. 2). The 
SRZ can be characterized by a two-phase microstructure consisting of elongated β -precipitates in 
the γ-Ni matrix which developed in SCo and SW faster than in S0 (Fig. 7c), the zone width s being 
186.3±9.2 µm, 147.1±5.5 µm and 120.4±10.7 µm after 1000 h at 1050 °C in air, respectively. In 
contrast, a propagation depth of only 78.6±10.4 µm was observed for the SRZ in the Ta 
containing system, with β-phase particles found also beneath the two-phase γ+β zone (Fig. 2b). It 
is important to note that the morphology of the SRZ in the S W differed from that in the other 
studied systems (Fig. 2). The reasons for the textured precipitate microstructure of the β -NiAl in 
SW and its possible effect on the diffusion processes fall outside the scope of the present study.

reproduce the main experimentally observed microstructural changes and showed an acceptable 
quantitative agreement with the experiment al results . Fig. 8a compares the measured and

A large discrepancy between the modelling and experimental results was, however, observed in 
the propagation of the SRZ in S Co (Fig. 7c). This overestimation of the SRZ growth rate may be 
related to possible inaccuracies of the thermodynamic and kinetic data [27] or, more likely, to the 
possible influence of the morphology of the two -phase region on the diffusion , a factor which is 
not considered by the model. The effective mobilities in the coating core and in the SRZ of S Co 
(both two-phase γ+β regions, Fig. 2) are treated in the present work by the same homogenization 
function (upper Hashin bound s) despite considerable differences in the morphology of the 
microstructure. Additional simulations using low er Hashin bounds and an average between the 
upper and lower bounds showed a significant effect of the averaging function on the simulated 
coating degradation rate as well as the SRZ width (Fig. 9). 
This difference in the averaging functions can be understood by considering the case, where the 
diffusional velocities in the single phases of a mixture (e.g. γ -Ni and β-NiAl) are significantly 
different (e.g. couple of orders of magnitude) from each other. The effective diffusion in this case 
will be determined by the amount of the phase with the faster diffusion. However, the diffusion 
behavior will be governed by a critical fraction of this phase, under which the overall diffusion 
will be governed by the phase with the slower diffusion kinetics. This threshold is termed as the

distribution of the phases and is difficult to determine for a given real multiphase alloy. A 
practical solution could be to use a combination of the averag ing functions [29], e.g. change from 
upper to lower Hashin bounds at a predefined β-phase fraction. Further investigations are planned 
to evaluate whether the applied homogenization function is indeed the major reason for the 
disagreement with the experiment in case of system SCo. 
 
5. Conclusions 
Four single-crystal Ni-base model alloys, based on the ternary Ni -10Cr-8Al system with 
additions of 10Co, 9Ta or 6W (wt%), coated with a Co-rich NiCoCrAlY-coating were exposed to 
air at 1050 °C up to 1000 h. Chemical degradation of the coating systems studied was found to be 
governed mainly by interdiffusion processes between the coating and the substrate, rather than by 
oxidation-induced Al loss. The experimental and modelling results clearly show that the coating 
degradation rate can be significantly affected by a single all oying element in the substrate. 
Complete (in S 0) and nearly complete (in S Co) dissolut ion of the β -phase in the coating was 
observed after exposure for 1000 h at 1050 °C in air. In contrast, a ddition of W and especially Ta 
to the Ni-10Cr-8Al substrate was found to effectively reduce the coating degradation rate. 
To interpret the interdiffus ion processes, the effect of Co, Ta and W on phase equilibri a in Ni-
10Cr-8Al-X model alloys was discussed with the use of thermodynamic and kinetic data 
available in Thermo -Calc. The considered alloying additions were found to increase the Al and 
Cr activities (minor effect of Co on a Cr) and decrease the Ni activity at 1050 °C in Ni-10Cr-8Al, 
thus reducing the driving force for interdiffusion. The calculations showed that Ta and W 
increase the Al activity by decreasing Al and Cr solubility in the γ-Ni matrix and the Cr solubility

γ'-phase compared to the base ternary Ni-10Cr-8Al alloy. The effect of the alloying additions on 
the effective mobilities of Ni, Cr and Al was related mainly to a difference in the γ'-fraction. In 
contrast to Co, alloying with Ta and W resulted in higher fractions of γ' that led to lower effective 
mobilities of species and, together with reduced activity gradients, slower coating degradation.
