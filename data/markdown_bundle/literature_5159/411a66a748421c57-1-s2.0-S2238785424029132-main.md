ABSTRACT
Misaligned grains present significant challenges in the directional solidification of complex-shaped industrial gas 
turbine (IGTs) blades made from Ni-based superalloys using the Bridgman method. These defects are closely 
associated with the bending of the solid/liquid (S/L) interface isotherm, a phenomenon heavily influenced by its 
relative position to the insulation baffle during solidification. The location of this isotherm is determined by the 
withdrawal rate employed. This study develops a simplified mathematical model that integrates blade and mold 
geometries, processing parameters affecting heat transfer, and the morphology of the liquidus isotherm. The 
model aims to design adaptive strategies for varying the withdrawal rate to control misaligned grain formation 
during directional solidification. By leveraging this theoretical framework, optimal adaptive withdrawal rate 
strategies were automatically generated, effectively flattening the liquidus isotherm and controlling misaligned 
grain formation in both dummy and actual IGT blades. This model, tailored to automatically design adaptive 
withdrawal rate routes, offers a robust strategy for producing misaligned-grain-free IGT blades.
1. Introduction
Due to the elimination of the transversal grain boundaries, the 
directionally solidified (DS) blades of Ni-based superalloys are widely 
used as hot-end components in industrial gas turbines (IGTs) because of 
their greatly improved high-temperature performance [ 1 , 2 ]. In com -
parison to aircraft engine blades, IGT blades are often physically large in 
dimension, which poses significant challenges for the control of 
columnar grain structure during directional solidification [ 3 , 4 ]. Defects 
such as freckles [ 5 – 8 ] and misaligned grains [ 9 – 11 ] form more 
frequently, resulting in a high rejection rate of the blades. Therefore, the 
precise control of these defects is of great importance [ 4 , 5 , 12 ].
Misaligned grains are common defects found in DS industrial gas 
turbine (IGT) blades, where columnar grains have a grain boundary 
deviating by more than 15
◦
from the blade stacking axis [ 13 – 15 ]. The 
presence of misaligned grains can significantly compromise the me -
chanical properties of the blades [ 16 ], leading to decreased strength, 
fatigue resistance, and creep resistance. Additionally, misaligned grains 
can create pathways for crack initiation and propagation, increasing the 
risk of premature failure [ 17 ]. Previous investigations [ 18 – 22 ] have 
shown that the formation of misaligned grains is influenced by the 
morphology of the solid/liquid (S/L) interface isotherm during direc -
tional solidification. A convex S/L interface isotherm causes grains to 
deviate away from the blade ’ s centerline, while a concave interface 
results in grains angling toward the centerline [ 3 ]. A flat S/L interface 
isotherm can eliminate the occurrence of misaligned grains. The 
morphology of the S/L interface isotherm is determined by the location 
of the interface during directional solidification: a convex interface is 
associated with an interface above the cooling zone, while a concave 
interface is located within the cooling zone. The flat interface is posi -
tioned at the transition from the heating to the cooling zone, typically at 
the insulation baffle. Several studies have indicated that the withdrawal 
rate plays a crucial role in determining the position of the S/L interface 
isotherm during directional solidification. A withdrawal rate that is too 
high can lead to the S/L interface isotherm being located in the cooling 
zone [ 23 ], while a rate that is too low can result in the interface being in 
the heating zone [ 24 ]. Proper control of the withdrawal rate is essential 
to ensure that the S/L interface isotherm remains within the insulation 
baffle region. Therefore, designing an appropriate withdrawal rate 
strategy based on the blade and mold geometries and processing

In the industrial production of directionally solidified industrial gas 
turbine (IGT) blades, the conventional approach to determining the 
withdrawal rate route is based on empirical experience, which may not 
consistently ensure universal process control to prevent misaligned 
grain formation. Despite decades of practice, few studies have system -
atically developed an effective theoretical model capable of automati -
cally generating optimal withdrawal rate strategies considering blade 
and mold geometries, as well as processing parameters, for controlling 
misaligned grains in large, complex-shaped blades. This paper proposes 
a physical model that elucidates the occurrence of misaligned grains 
relative to variations in withdrawal rate. By leveraging this model, 
optimal adaptive variable withdrawal rate strategies can be automati -
cally generated for the directional solidification of arbitrarily complex 
blade shapes. Implementing these adaptive strategies can significantly 
reduce misaligned grain occurrences in the final product. Both simula -
tions and experimental results validate the efficacy of the proposed 
model, confirming its ability to successfully mitigate misaligned grain 
formation in directionally solidified dummy and IGT blades. Conse -
quently, this method offers a dependable and practical approach to 
producing misaligned-grain-free IGTs blades.

4.5. Error analysis
Through the method provided in this article, an adaptive variable 
speed withdrawal strategy for controlling the misaligned grain deviation 
of directionally solidified columnar crystals can be derived and vali -
dated in various shapes. However, necessary error analysis needs to be 
declared to ensure the effectiveness of the model. The variable speed 
strategy based on this method may have the following main errors.
First, the compensation method of the model must be based on a 
specific reference plane and a specific direction ( r

), so when selecting 
other reference planes and directions, the obtained variable speed

not have high resolution to adjust the speed at different positions.

Compared to some existing models, a real-time feedback system for

introduced. This may indicate the fault tolerance of the method. 
However, future improvement research based on this method is still 
necessary.
5. Conclusions
In this study, a cost-effective mathematical model incorporating the 
effects of blade and mold geometries, processing parameters on the heat 
transfer, and the morphology of the S/L interface isotherms was devel -
oped to design adaptive variable withdrawal rate strategies for con -
trolling misaligned grains in the directionally solidified blades. The 
model comprehensively utilizes the slope of the isotherm and the height 
difference between the liquidus isotherm and the hot/cold interface to

The model confirms a nearly linear relationship between tilt and 
withdrawal speed, which makes the theoretical basis of variable speed 
control more clear.
Additional compensation in withdrawal rate was integrated into the 
model to minimize the height difference between the liquidus isotherm 
and the hot/cold interface, thereby reducing or restraining the bending 
of the isotherm and making grains grow parallel to the blade stacking 
axis as much as possible.

based on the real-time adjustment of the liquidus isotherm slope for the 
directional solidification of dummy and IGTs blades. The application of 
these designed withdrawal rate strategies resulted in a flattened S/L 
interface isotherm. Simulated grain growths and macrostructure of the 
blades observed in the experiment confirmed that misaligned grains can 
be effectively controlled under the designed withdrawal rate curves.

difference from the liquidus-slope-export strategy. This makes the
