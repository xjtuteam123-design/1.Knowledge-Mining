ABSTRACT
Electron beam melting (EBM) has a lower cooling rate than other additive manufacturing processes (selective
laser melting, laser metal deposition, etc.), so it is suitable for manufacturing titanium aluminide-based inter-
metallics. In this study, a gamma Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy was manufactured using EBM, and its microstructure
and high-temperature creep behaviors were investigated. Conventional Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb was also used for
comparison and analysis. Initial microstructural observation con ﬁrmed that the EBM-built Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb
alloy had a near-gamma (NG) structure, whereas the conventional Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy had a fully lamellar
(FL) structure. Room temperature and high temperature compression tests con ﬁrmed that the Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb
alloy with the NG structure had lower strength in all temperature ranges, and the yield-strength anomaly
phenomenon occurred in both materials. A 750 °C multi-step creep test con ﬁrmed that the EBM-built Ti-48Al-
2Cr-2Nb alloy with lower strength had lower creep resistance as well. Microstructural observation after creep
deformation con ﬁrmed that dislocation movement and mechanical twins were formed dominantly in the creep
deformation of the material with the NG structure. For the material with the FL structure, di ﬀusional creep in
low-stress regions and large deformation at the colony boundary in high-stress regions dominated the creep
deformation. Correlations between the microstructure, strength, and multi-step creep properties were discussed
based on these ﬁndings.
1. Introduction
Intermetallic titanium aluminide based on γ-TiAl has excellent
corrosion resistance, low density, high speci ﬁc strength and stiﬀ ness,
good oxidation and ignition resistance, and high-temperature creep
resistance [ 1– 5]. Owing to such advantages, it is expected to partially
replace Ni-based superalloys in aircraft-engine turbine applications
[6,7]. In particular, the gamma Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy is employed in
the low-pressure turbine blades of aircraft engines, indicating the pos-
sibility of expanding its application [ 8]. However, despite the ad-
vantages of the γ-TiAl alloy, it is di ﬃcult to form complex shapes using
it, owing to the characteristics of intermetallics. Due to this, the lim-
itations in parts design have been causing many di ﬃculties in applying
γ-TiAl to actual parts. In order to overcome these limitations, many
solutions are being suggested, and among them, additive manufacturing
(AM), otherwise known as 3D printing technology, is gaining a lot of
attention.
AM is a technology that controls a part in a layer-by-layer manner
with powder and an energy source according to a computer-aided
design (CAD) model [ 9]. Compared with traditional production tech-
niques (casting → hot or cold working → machining), AM has minimal
material waste, can be used to design complex, sophisticated shapes
without restrictions, and can obtain parts of near net shape [ 10– 12].
Due to such characteristics, it has attracted considerable attention from
researchers for manufacturing parts with the γ-TiAl alloy, which pre-
sents di ﬃculties in shape control. However, γ-TiAl forms cracks due to
heat stress when a method with a high cooling rate, such as selective
laser melting, is applied [ 13– 16]. For this reason, the electron beam
melting (EBM) process, which has a relatively low cooling rate, is
considered to be a suitable method for manufacturing the γ-TiAl alloy.
There are di ﬀerent studies regarding the microstructure, high-tem-
perature mechanical properties, and creep properties of additive man-
ufactured Ti base alloy (e.g., Ti-6Al-4V), but γ-TiAl alloy has been in-
suﬃciently studied [ 17,18]. Recently, Schwerdtfeger et al. [ 19]
performed a microstructural analysis of the Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy
using EBM, and Biamino et al. [ 20] reported the mechanical properties
of this material. Additionally, studies have been conducted to de-
termine the e ﬀects of building direction on microstructural and

2. Experimental method
2.1. Sample preparation
γ-TiAl with a nominal composition of Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy (wt.%)
from ATI Powder Metals (formerly Crucible Research) was used in this
study. The average powder size was 90 μm, and the size distribution
was 45– 150 μm. This powder was used to obtain an additively manu-
factured Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy with EBM. To control interior defects
and residual stress, hot isostatic pressing (HIP) was performed for 4 h at
1260 °C and 170 MPa. A macroscopic image of the manufactured ma-
terial is shown in Fig. 1. No cracks or defects were observed on the
surface, and the size was measured as 77.6 mm (diameter) × 13.7 mm.
The microstructural and property di ﬀerences between the EBM-built Ti-
48Al-2Cr-2Nb and conventional Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb and Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb
alloys manufactured using precision casting and plasma melting were
investigated.
2.2. Microstructure observation
To identify the phase change of the EBM-built Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb
alloy and the conventional Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy, phase analysis was
performed using an X-ray di ﬀractometer (Cu K
α radiation, scan step size
of 0.05°, scan rate of 2°/min, Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan). Here, sections
of the sample perpendicular to the direction of loading polished with
silicon carbide papers (#100 – #2000) and a 1- μm diamond suspension
were analyzed. Then, scanning electron microscopy (SEM, VEGA II
LMU, Tescan, Czech Republic) and ﬁeld-emission SEM (FE-SEM, MYRA
3 XMH, Tescan, Czech Republic) were performed. Additionally, the
material was ground using 0.05-μ m colloidal silica and subjected to
electron backscatter di ﬀraction (EBSD, NordlysNano detector, Oxford,
United Kingdom) analysis for grain orientation and phase analysis.
Electron channeling contrast imaging (ECCI) was conducted at an ac-
celeration voltage of 30 kV with a backscattered electron (BSE) detector
to analyze the creep microstructure. A working distance of 7 mm was
used, with a sample tilt of 6.0°.
2.3. Multi-step high-temperature creep tests
Prior to performing multi-step high-temperature creep tests on the
EBM-built Ti-48Al-Cr-2Nb, temperature-dependent compression tests at
25, 600, 750, 900, and 1050 °C were performed by machining a cy-
lindrical sample with dimensions of
Φ4mm×height 6mm in order to
determine its yield-strength anomaly (YSA). Room- and high-tempera-
ture tests were conducted using MTS-810 equipment until the total
strain was (
εtot) = 0.4, with an initial strain rate of 1 × 10 −3/s. The
compression creep test sample was a 2 mm × 2 mm × 4 mm rectan-
gular parallelepiped, and an RB306-DW direct creep tester was used.
Here, zirconia platens were placed between the sample and the cage. A
constant load was applied during the high-temperature creep test to
induce stress in the range of 150 – 375 MPa at 750 °C (temperature sta-
bility of
±1°C), and a sample was crept in intervals of 86.4 ks.
Compressive strain was determined by measuring the displacement of
the cage in the cold zone using a linear variable di ﬀerential transducer
(LVDT) with a resolution of 0.001 mm. To determine the activation
energies for creep, the creep rates were measured under 225 MPa (EBM-
built, dislocation creep region), 300 MPa (plasma-melted, di ﬀusional
creep region), and 350 MPa (plasma-melted, dislocation creep region).
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructures of EBM-built and conventional Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb
alloys
Fig. 2 shows FE-SEM images of the initial microstructures of the
EBM-built Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy and the conventional Ti-48Al-2Cr-
2Nb alloy. As seen in the low-magni ﬁcation images in Fig. 2(a) and (b),
the EBM sample had a near-gamma (NG) structure, and the conven-
tional sample had a fully lamellar (FL) structure, respectively. In gen-
eral, titanium aluminide can have a variety of microstructures ac-
cording to the cooling rate. Therefore, to identify the microstructural
diﬀerences, the continuous cooling transformation (CCT) diagram
[24,25] shown in Fig. 3 was employed. T
s and T f are the starting and
ﬁnishing temperatures, respectively, of the →+αγα 2 transformation.
The CCT diagram indicates that a variety of microstructural features
can be created according to the cooling rate if solid-state phase trans-
formation is performed from the
α transus. In particular, as the cooling
rateincreases, anNGstructure, ratherthanalamellarstructure, can be
obtained. This is because the di ﬀusion-related phase transformation
changes to di ﬀusion-less phase transformation as the cooling rate in-
creases. Additionally, in the case of Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb, the γ-TiAl and α-
Ti
3Al phases overlap according to the temperature, and so a massive
phase transformation (MT) occurs [ 26]. Thus, an EBM-built material
undergoes a cooling rate of 10 3– 105 K/s, which is suspected to result in
an NG structure owing to the clear MT observed in the CCT diagram
[27]. In the case of plasma melting, an FL structure is assumed to be
formed through lamellar transformation because of the lower cooling
rate.
The EBM-built Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy was con ﬁrmed to have a few
microsized phases, which are suspected of α
2, in some grain bound-
aries, along with an equiaxed γ phase ( Fig. 2(a)). The enlarged image
indicates that these did not exist as the α2 phase, but as an α2-γ lamellar
structure. The α2 ratio was relatively high, allowing the phase to be
identiﬁed as a single phase at a low magni ﬁcation. On the other hand,
in the enlarged image of the plasma-melted Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy
(Fig. 2(b)), a γ-α

colony (average of 267.3 ± 27.5 μm) is observed. The thicknesses of γ
and α2 were measured as 755.0 ± 279.3nm and 247.8 ± 130.6nm,
respectively.

3.2. Temperature-dependent compressive properties and yield-strength
anomaly
Fig. 6 compares the room- and high-temperature compressive yield
strengths of the EBM-built and plasma-melted Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloys.
The average values and standard deviations of the 0.2% o ﬀset yield
strengths at various compression temperatures are presented in Table 1.
As shown in Fig. 6, the plasma-melted Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy exhibited
higher yield strengths than the EBM-built Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy at all
temperatures. In general, the mechanical properties of the gamma TiAl
alloy are highly dependent on the microstructure [ 29]. According to
Kim et al. [ 30], the FL (FG) structure exhibits the most outstanding
mechanical properties. The outstanding mechanical properties of the
FG structure are attributed to the suppression of dislocation movement
by the γ/α interface [ 31]. Thus, an NG structure was formed in the
EBM-built material owing to the high cooling rate, which resulted in
worse mechanical properties compared with the plasma-melted Ti-
48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy with an FL structure.

In the case of pure metals and alloys, as a thermally activated re-
action occurs, the dislocation glide becomes easier and the strength
decreases with the increase in temperature. However, as shown in
Fig. 6, the yield-strength anomaly (YSA) phenomenon, where the yield
strength increases in a speci ﬁc temperature range, was observed in the
EBM-built alloy and the plasma-melted Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy as the
temperature increased. YSA is a unique phenomenon that occurs in a
few materials with an ordered structure and is attributed to dislocation
locking [ 32– 34]. YSA occurred in a Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb alloy manu-
factured with EBM [ 35]. The cause of the YSA is the ordered structure
of TiAl. In the case of the γ-phase, the YSA contributes to the nonplanar
dislocation structure caused by cross slip, i.e., the L1
0 structure, which
is similar to the Kear– Wilsdorf (KW) locking mechanism that occurs in
an L12 structure [36]. KW locking occurs in the process of the antiphase
boundary (APB) and leads to a partial change in the glide plane due to
cross slipping, which occurs when the two APB-coupled 1/
2 < 101 > superpartials are separated into two 1/6 < 112 > Shockley
partials. That is, the strength increases as the mobile dislocation density
decreases due to dislocation locking after the cross slipping of {111} to
the stable {100}. The most signi ﬁcant di ﬀerence between the L1 0 and
L12 structures is that a cross-slipped complex stacking fault (CSF)-
coupled Shockley partial dislocation is present in the more energetically
stable superlattice intrinsic stacking fault (SISF)-coupled pair rather
than in the CSF [ 37]. For the Ti
3Al of the D 019 structure, the thermally
activated rearrangement of edge 2c+a superdislocation forms a dis-
location barrier, resulting in a YSA [ 38]. The YSA occurs at a higher
temperature in Ti
3Al than in TiAl, and the yield-strength increase is also
reported to be greater in Ti 3Al [ 39].

diﬀusion energy of the elements Ti and Al. Despite the signi ﬁcant in-
crease of the stress, the measured activation energies in the dislocation
creep region and di ﬀusional creep region of the plasma-melted sample
were similar, which is attributed to dislocation locking. As previously
described, γ-TiAl forms a nonplanar dislocation structure due to cross
slipping at high temperatures (causing dislocation locking), resulting in
a decrease in dislocation mobility. Thus, even though the applied stress
signiﬁcantly increased as a result of dislocation locking, the activation
energy remained at similar levels.
4. Conclusion
The microstructure and high-temperature creep behavior of a
gamma Ti-48Al-2Cr-Nb alloy manufactured with EBM were in-
vestigated. Based on the results, the following conclusions were drawn.
(1) A massive transformation occurred in the initial microstructure of
the EBM-built sample owing to the fast cooling rate, resulting in a
near-gamma (NG) structure. In contrast, a plasma-melted sample
(conventional alloy) was con ﬁrmed to have a fully lamellar (FL)
structure. It was con ﬁrmed that both materials were composed of γ
and α
2 phases.
(2) Room temperature and high temperature compression tests in-
dicated that the EBM-built sample with the NG structure had lower
strength than the conventional sample at all temperatures.
Additionally, the yield-strength anomaly (YSA) was con ﬁrmed to
occur at 750 °C in both materials.
(3) After the 750 °C high-temperature creep test, the EBM-built sample
with the NG structure had lower creep resistance. This is attributed
to the reduction of the dislocation barrier (e.g., γ/α2 interface) and
the YSA in lamellar α2 being more prominent than that in γ. The
stress exponents ( n) for the EBM-built and plasma-melted samples
were measured as 4.39 and 1.08, respectively, for the di ﬀusional
creep region and 6.87 for the dislocation creep region.
(4) Microstructural observations after deformation con ﬁrmed that the
creep deformation was in ﬂuenced not only by the dislocation
movement, but also by mechanical twins in the case of the EBM-
built sample. The signi ﬁcant increase in n for the plasma-melted
sample is attributed to the large deformation at the colony
boundary.
(5) The activation energies of EBM-built and plasma-melted samples
were measured as 404 kJ/mol and 315 kJ/mol (di ﬀusional creep
region), 310 kJ/mol (dislocation creep region), respectively. These
values are similar to the reported activation energy for creep of a
TiAl-based alloy (300 – 440 kJ/mol).
