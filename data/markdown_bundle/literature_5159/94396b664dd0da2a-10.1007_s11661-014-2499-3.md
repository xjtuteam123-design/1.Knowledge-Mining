Microstructural Stability and Hot Deformation of c–c¢–d Ni-Base
Superalloys
MARTIN DETROIS, RANDOLPH C. HELMINK, and SAMMY TIN
Nickel-base superalloys exhibit excellent high-temperature mechanical and physical properties
and remain the ﬁrst choice for structural components in advanced gas turbine engines for the
aerospace propulsion and power generation applications. In response to the increasing demand
for more eﬃcient solutions and tighter requirements linked to gas turbine technologies, the
properties of nickel-base superalloys can be improved by modiﬁcation of their thermo-
mechanical and/or compositional attributes. Recent investigations have revealed the potential
use of ternary eutectic c–c¢–d Ni-base superalloys in advanced gas turbines due to high tem-
perature mechanical properties that are comparable to state-of-the-art polycrystalline Ni-base
superalloys. With properties largely dependent on microstructural strengthening mechanisms,
both the composition and thermo-mechanical processing parameters of this novel class of alloys
need to be optimized concurrently. The hot deformation characteristics of four c–c¢–d Ni-base
superalloys with varying levels of Nb were evaluated at temperatures and strain rates between
1353 K and 1433 K (1080 /C176 C and 1160 /C176 C) and 0.01 to 0.001/s, respectively. Evidence of dis-
location-based plasticity was observed following deformation at low temperatures and high
strain rates, while high temperatures and low strain rates promoted superplasticity in these
alloys. The extent of the microstructural changes and the magnitude of the cavitation damage
which occurred during deformation was found to vary as a function of the alloy composition.

I. INTRODUCTION
THE unique combination of mechanical and physical
properties exhibited by Ni-base alloys makes them ideal
for use in high performance applications involving
elevated temperatures and corrosive environments.
[1–4]
For applications where service temperatures do not
exceed 1073 K (800 /C176 C), certain classes of polycrystalline
Ni-base superalloys have been engineered to exhibit an
excellent combination of high strength and fatigue
resistance under conditions where creep deformation is
limited.
[5–7] The majority of commercial polycrystalline
nickel-base superalloys consist of a two phase micro-
structure composed of coherent, ordered L12 precipitates
c¢ (Ni3Al), and/or D0 22 c¢¢ (Ni3Nb) surrounded by a
disordered FCC matrix c (Ni). The conventional two
phase microstructure confers high strength and toughness
to the material at elevated temperatures. Alloying addi-
tions enable further control of the properties of the alloy
by providing a combination of solid solution and precip-
itation strengthening. In most instances, additions of Mo
and W are used for solution strengthening of the c matrix
while Ti, Ta, Al, and Nb are intended for strengthening
and control of the volume fraction of the c¢ precipitates,
Cr for resistance to the environment, and minor alloying
additions of B, C, Zr, and Hf assist in controlling the grain
size and grain boundary properties.
[1–4]
For most advanced, high performance gas turbine
engines, the operating temperatures are limited by the
temperature capability of materials used in hot section
structures. In recent years, changes in composition or
adjustments in thermo-mechanical processing parame-
ters have led to modest improvements in the properties
of the material as its intrinsic limitations were
approached. Moreover, many of the compositional
changes made to conventional c–c¢ Ni-base superalloys
do not appear to be capable of achieving the consider-
able improvements in properties required by advanced
gas turbine engine concepts and tend to impair the
formability of the material.
[8] As a result, new concepts
or alloying approaches are needed that provide trans-
formational improvements in properties and enable the
design of advanced gas turbine engine concepts.
Directionally solidiﬁed c–c¢–d Ni-Al-Nb eutectic
alloys were initially evaluated in the 1970s and 1980s
and found to possess excellent high temperature strength
and resistance to creep deformation.
[9–14] High thermal
gradients and slow withdrawal rates during the direc-
tional solidiﬁcation phase promoted the formation of an
in situ composite microstructure comprised of aligned d
(Ni
3Nb) lamellae distributed within a c–c¢ matrix.
Excellent mechanical properties were observed due to
the incorporation of the intermetallic d phase as a
composite reinforcement. However, the large degree of

addition, the combination of mismatch in thermal

between the d phase and c–c¢ matrix resulted in high

render them unsuitable for use at temperatures above

to below 1073 K (800 /C176 C) where creep resistant columnar
or single crystal grain structures are typically not required.
Compared to state-of-the-art, high strength polycrys-
talline Ni-base superalloys, such as Udimet 720 and Rene
95, these polycrystallinec–c¢–d alloys may potentially oﬀer
a number of beneﬁts. High temperature strength can be
enhanced by combining the composite strengthening
associated with the inclusion of relatively high volume
fractions of the intermetallic d phase combined with
signiﬁcant levels of dispersion/precipitate strengthening
from the c¢ precipitates. In its polycrystalline form, the
properties rival those of single crystal Ni-base superalloys
and provide potent levels of Orowan and composite
strengthening in the range of temperature considered.
[1,3,15]
Moreover, the c–c¢–d alloys being proposed as part of this
study possess lower densities and raw materials costs when
compared to a number of commercial high strength
Ni-base superalloys. The majority of advanced powder
processed Ni-base superalloys rely on the additions of W,
Mo, and Ta to provide high levels of strengthening in both
the c and c¢ phases.
[16–20] Not only do these refractory
alloying additions increase the overall density of the alloy,
they also increase the raw materials costs associated with
the alloy. The c–c¢–d alloys investigated as a part of the
present study are based on Ni-Al-Nb or Ni-Al-Nb-Cr
systems and contain limited amounts of expensive refrac-
tory alloying additions. With up to a ~10 pct lower alloy
density, the comparatively higher density normalized
speciﬁc strength may be ideal for producing low cost, high
strength structures for high performance applications.
Recent studies have led to a better understanding of how
alloying additions eﬀect the mechanical properties of
c–c¢–d Ni-base superalloys.
[21–23] While speciﬁc alloying
additions may be used to modify particular attributes of the
alloy, the ability to physically shape and process these
Ni-base superalloys via hot deformation has yet to be
determined. The present investigation was performed in
order to assess the feasibility of forming near net shape
structures with thesec–c¢–d alloys by identifying a range of
deformation temperatures and strain rates amenable for
inducing plastic or superplastic ﬂow. Microstructural
changes such as damage accumulation and the precipita-
tion ofg (Ni
3Al0.5Nb0.5) was quantiﬁed as a function of the
processing parameters and Nb concentration.
II. PROCEDURE
Four experimental Ni-base alloys containing varying
levels of Nb from 8.6 to 15.3 wt pct were used in this
investigation, Table I. Each of the alloys was processed
at ATI Powder Metals in the form of 20 kg billets.
Vacuum induction melting of elemental alloying addi-
tions was used to produce ingots of the speciﬁed
chemistry. Following spray atomization, powder parti-
cles smaller than 53 lm (270 mesh) were collected and
consolidated within mild steel cans via hot isostatic
pressing (HIP). Cylindrical rods measuring 10 mm in
diameter and 100 mm in length were extracted from the
billet using electro-discharge machining (EDM). To
assess the characteristic microstructural changes occur-
ring during hot deformation, the initial microstructure
of the billet in the as-HIP condition was characterized.
Samples were sectioned from the rods using a diamond
saw and prepared using standard metallographic tech-
niques ending with a ﬁnal polish using a 0.05 lm
colloidal silica solution. Following polishing of the
samples, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a
JEOL 5900 in back-scattered electron (BSE) mode at a
voltage of 20 kV was used to characterize the micro-
structure of the as-polished surfaces.
For each of the alloys, metallographic samples were
also prepared from samples deformed at various tem-
peratures and strain rates. A total of 24 cylindrical
samples measuring 15 mm height and 10 mm diameter
were sectioned from the rods for a series of elevated
temperature compression tests designed to simulate
isothermal forming conditions using a Gleeble 3500 sys-
tem. Each alloy was deformed at two strain rates (0.01
and 0.001/s) and three deformation temperatures
[1353 K, 1393 K, and 1433 K (1080 /C176 C, 1120 /C176 C, and
1160 /C176 C)] to span a broad range of isothermal hot
deformation conditions. The ﬁnal strain under com-
pression was set to a constant value of 0.5 for all of the
compression tests. To minimize microstructural changes
occurring prior to deformation, the samples were
rapidly heated to the set temperature in 1 minute and
allowed to thermally soak for 3 minutes prior to
deformation. Three thermocouples were spot welded
onto each compression sample and used to monitor the
temperature of the sample during deformation. Barrel-
ing of the samples during deformation was minimized as
variations of less than ~20 K ( ~20 /C176 C) were measured
along the length of the specimen. During deformation,
graphite was used to minimize friction occurring
between the compression anvil and sample.
Following compression tests, the samples were rapidly
cooled to retain microstructures representative of those
at temperature and sectioned parallel to the deformation
axis. These samples were metallographically prepared
and microstructural characterization was performed
along the geometric centers of the samples where strains
were uniform and constant. Fiducial reference markers
were placed on the sample to permit alignment of the
samples parallel to the deformation axis during charac-
terization in the SEM. This provided a reference
orientation for the determination of the relative orien-
tation distributions of the d phase precipitates. The
corresponding SEM images were then analyzed using
digital image analysis software, GIMP. Lines were
manually drawn over the d phase precipitates to
quantify their relative orientations. After subtraction

C. Characterization of Deformed Microstructures
Since all of the hot deformation tests were performed
with identical parameters to an overall strain of 0.5 for
all of the alloys, metallographic analyses were per-
formed to quantify variations in the microstructures as a
function of alloy composition, deformation tempera-
ture, and strain rate. Figure 5 reveals the microstructure
of RCH49 following deformation at various tempera-
tures and strain rates. Although most of the deformed
specimens exhibited cavitation damage and interfacial
debonding of d phase precipitates, and to a lower extent
of the g phase precipitates following hot deformation,
the extent of damage was found to vary as a function of
the deformation parameters. Following the same behav-
ior as of the ﬂow stresses during deformation, the
amount of damage was found to decrease as the
deformation temperature increased and/or the strain
rate decreased. The magnitude of overall damage was
quantiﬁed for all of the compressed samples, Table IV.
Following deformation at a temperature of 1353 K
(1080 /C176 C) and a strain rate of 0.01/s, the damage area
fraction was ~1.8 pct in RCH53, ~0.8 pct for RCH49,
~0.4 pct for RCH48, and ~0.7 pct for LN8. Interest-
ingly, the overall damage area fraction was found to
decrease with reductions in the initial volume fraction of
d phase for the RCH alloys. However, despite possessing
a microstructure containing no d phase, damage was
observed in LN8 and the level was intermediate between
those in RCH49 and RCH48, Figure 6. Along with
increasing deformation temperatures, reducing the
strain rate from 0.01 to 0.001/s modestly decreased the
magnitude of cavitation damage induced during defor-
mation. For all of the alloys, the combination of high
deformation temperature [1433 K (1160 /C176 C)] and rela-
tively low strain rate (0.001/s) produced no detectable
microstructural damage following a height reduction of
0.5, Figure 5(f) and Table IV. In addition to cavitation
damage and debonding of the d phase precipitates,
cracking of the d precipitates was observed in select
instances during deformation at 1353 K (1080 /C176 C) and
0.01/s, Figure 7(b). Prior to deformation of RCH49, the
d precipitates exhibited a blocky morphology with
straight edges and the g phase precipitates appeared as
elongated, straight rods Figure 7(a). After deformation,
the d precipitates were observed to fracture resulting in
smaller aspect ratios and the
g phase precipitates
appeared to deform plastically. Moreover, alignment
of the d phase precipitates after deformation was
observed in some of the SEM images and the relative
orientation of each precipitate was quantiﬁed, Figure 8.
From an initially random orientation distribution of the
precipitates in the as-HIP condition, the precipitates
were found to orientate in the direction of plastic ﬂow
following deformation. The distribution was found to
depend mainly on the strain rate, Figure 8(a). A
decrease in the uniformity of the distribution was
observed as deformation temperatures decreased, Fig-
ure 8(b), while the volume fraction of d phase was not
found to signiﬁcantly inﬂuence the results, Figure 8(c).
The orientation distribution was found to be most
sensitive to changes in strain rate as higher strain rates
resulted in more pronounced alignment of the d phase
precipitates within the matrix.
Depending on the deformation temperature, micro-
structural changes were observed to occur in the alloys.
Following hot deformation of RCH48 at 1433 K
(1160 /C176 C), the d phase precipitates were observed to
coarsen and the volume fraction of the g phase
precipitates decreased signiﬁcantly when compared to
the as-HIP condition, Figure 9. Despite having a coars-
ened morphology, no measurable change in the overall
volume fraction of the d phase precipitates was detected.
Reducing the deformation temperature and strain rate
to 1353 K (1080 /C176 C) and 0.01/s for RCH48, however,
did not appear to modify the volume fraction or
morphologies of the d and g phase precipitates present.

IV. DISCUSSION
A. d and g Precipitate Phases
Ternary eutectic c–c¢–d Ni-base superalloys have been
shown to exhibit excellent mechanical properties at
temperatures below 1073 K (800 /C176 C) due to a high
volume fraction of intermetallic reinforcement.
[21–23]
This investigation has been conducted in response to
these recent results and was aimed at determining the

d precipitates rotate in conjunction with the grains.

C. Flow Stress Curves
For the RCH series of alloys, signiﬁcant ﬂow soften-
ing was observed during deformation in all cases except
for samples tested at 1433 K (1160 /C176 C) at a strain rate of
0.001/s, Figure 4. In the instances where ﬂow softening
occurred, the peak ﬂow stresses likely corresponded to
fracture of d phase precipitates or damage accumulation
events along the d/c–c¢ interfaces.
[26–28] The ﬂow stress
curves of the RCH alloys at 1433 K (1160 /C176 C) and
0.001/s did not show any appreciable hardening or
softening which was consistent with the occurrence of
superplastic forming and the absence of any observed
microstructural damage. Diﬀerences in the magnitude of
the peak stresses among the RCH alloys could likely be
attributed to the underlying microstructure. The volume
fraction of d phase measured within the microstructures
of RCH 48, 49, and 53 was 20.4, 29.9, and 37.5 pct,
respectively. Although these alloys had similar bulk
chemistries, the morphology of the phases in the RCH
alloys varied signiﬁcantly. The d phase precipitates in
RCH49 and RCH53 were rather large and blocky when
compared to the ﬁner and thinner precipitates observed
in RCH48. As the size of these d phase precipitates were
on the same order as the grain size, load shedding
occurred within the microstructure as the d phase
provided composite reinforcement strengthening as
opposed to precipitation strengthening. With both a
higher overall volume fraction and a physically larger
precipitate size, the degree of composite strengthening
provided by the d phase precipitates in RCH49 and
RCH53 was more potent than RCH48 and resulted in
modestly higher peak ﬂow stresses during deformation
at the temperatures and strain rates investigated.
The ﬂow stress behavior of alloy LN8, however, was
distinctly diﬀerent than that of the RCH series of alloys.
With only g phase present during the high temperature
deformation experiments, the peak ﬂow stresses for LN8
were modest and intermediate in magnitude when
compared to those for the RCH alloys. However, at
1433 K (1160 /C176 C) and 0.001/s the magnitude of the
maximum ﬂow stress for LN8 was 5 to 10 times greater
than that measured for the RCH series of alloys. In all
of the alloys investigated, the g phase tended to form
intragranularly consistent with observations in other eta
containing alloys
[24,29–31] while the d phase precipitates
tended to form preferentially along the grain bound-
aries. This limits the extent and growth of the g phase
precipitates as evidenced by their respective aspect
ratios. Since there were no d phase precipitates in LN8
to break up the continuity of the g phase, the g phase
precipitates were able to grow across low angle grain
boundaries and span across multiple grains as evidenced
by their noticeably higher aspect ratios. This enabled the
g phase to form an interconnected three dimensional
lattice within the microstructure that served to resist
grain boundary sliding and rotation even at 1433 K
(1160 /C176 C) and 0.001/s.

tural variations and the diﬀerences in the g volume
fraction following deformation, the deformation tem-

approach the solvus temperature of the g phase. Starting

V. CONCLUSIONS
The eﬀect of hot deformation on the microstructure
and properties of selected c–c¢–d Ni-base superalloys
was investigated. This study was aimed at quantifying
the ﬂow stresses, the ﬂow behavior and the microstruc-
tural changes occurring during hot deformation across a
broad range of temperatures and strain rates. Based on
the results and observations presented in this study, the
following conclusions can be reported:
1. At deformation temperatures below 1393 K
(1120 /C176 C) and strain rates of 0.01/s, dislocation-based
plasticity mechanisms were responsible for high ﬂow
stresses and comparatively higher levels of micro-
structural damage that occurred during plastic ﬂow.
2. Conversely, deformation at temperatures above
1393 K (1120 /C176 C) at a lower strain rate of 0.001/s,
the ﬂow stresses were signiﬁcantly reduced as grain
boundary sliding and rotation were the primary
deformation mechanisms resulting in a reduced
amount of damage.
3. The d phase precipitates tend to fracture during
deformation at 1353 K (1080 /C176 C) and 0.01/s while
the g phase precipitates deform plastically along
with the matrix.
4. Deformation at temperatures near the c¢ solvus led
to partial dissolution of the c¢ phase thereby return-
ing Al and Nb back into solution and making these
solutes available to contribute to the precipitation
and growth of g phase precipitates.
5. Changes in the volume fraction of g phase precipi-
tates are mainly influenced by the deformation tem-
perature as longer exposures to the deformation
temperature increase the relative variations.
6. A critical deformation temperature, T
C, that corre-
sponds to a deformation at constant volume frac-
tion of g phase precipitates has been identified for
the alloys investigated. Deformation at tempera-
tures above T
C result in a decrease in the volume
fraction of g phase precipitates as they dissolve
while deformation below TC result in an increase in
the volume fraction of g phase precipitates. This
critical deformation temperature varies as a func-
tion of the overall alloy composition.
