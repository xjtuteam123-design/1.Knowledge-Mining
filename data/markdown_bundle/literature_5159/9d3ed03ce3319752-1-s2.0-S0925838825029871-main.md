1. Introduction
Magnesium rare-earths (Mg-REs) alloys are extensively utilized in 
lightweight engineering applications such as aerospace, military arma -
ments, and other critical areas, on account of their superior mechanical 
properties at both room and elevated temperatures [1,2] . The use of 
certain REs, for instance Gd, Nd, and Y, etc. as primary alloying elements 
in Mg alloys have received significant attention due to their remarkable 
solubility and age-hardening responses. Hence, the mechanical proper -
ties of cast Mg-RE alloys can be comparable to the Mg-Al based metal 
matrix composites [3 – 7] . However, for casting of real components by 
Mg alloys, the variations in shape and geometry are complicated, which 
may include and may not limited to: regions with different wall 
thickness, corner regions with different radius, surface and core regions 
of a thick section, welded or repaired layers, and regions with different 
depths in additive manufactured parts. These variations can lead to 
discrepancies in grain size, volume fraction of secondary phases, and the 
tendency of solute segregations due to the differences in cooling rates, 
heat transfer, and solute distribution during solidification. Conse -
quently, the microstructural and compositional differences across 
various regions of the casting result in significant variations in proper -
ties obtained before and after solution treatment. Under this circum -
stance, in order to achieve a rational solid solution effect, it is often 
necessary to increase the temperature or extend the time during solution 
heat treatments. Its disadvantage is that it can lead to abnormal grain 
growth in areas mentioned earlier in the casting component. This

particularly in local areas which require relatively high properties, such 
as the surface region of welding repaired casting or additive manufac -
tured parts, thin walls of variable thickness castings, and small rounded 
corners of complex shaped casting. Additionally, Mg alloys exhibit 
relatively higher Hall-Patch strengthening coefficients than Al alloys 
[8] , indicating the abnormal growth of grains will lead to more signif -
icant decrease in strength. Therefore, it is of great scientific importance 
and engineering significance to achieve microstructural stability, more 
specifically the thermal stability of grain boundaries, in Mg-RE alloy cast 
components during solution treatment.
Mg-RE alloy castings, especially those with large sizes and complex 
structures, are often produced using sand casting techniques. However, 
the relatively low cooling rates associated with sand casting led to the 
formation of coarse grains during solidification, negatively affecting the 
mechanical properties of the alloy. Consequently, grain refinement is 
considered to be an efficient method for improving the strength [9,10]
and ductility [11,12] of sand cast Mg-RE alloys. Among the various grain 
refinement methods, the Mg-Zr master alloy is widely used for Al-free 
Mg alloys and has been applied in commercial alloys such as Mg-Y-Nd 
(WE43, WE54) and Mg-Gd-Y (VW103) series of alloys. For instance, in 
the fabrication of VW103 alloy casting components with relatively large 
scale, Li et al. [13] found that with the increase of Zr content from 0.3 % 
to 0.5 %, the grain size decreased from 129 μ m to 39 μ m continuously. 
However, in the solid solution treatment of large scale castings (e. g., 
castings with a minimum wall thickness of ~3 mm and a maximum wall 
thickness ~130 mm [14] ), the solid solution treatment time is extended 
due to the uneven wall thickness and huge size. Yi et al. [15] demon -
strated that wall thickness variations (10 – 70 mm) in Mg-4Y-3Nd-0.5Zr 
castings amplify grain growth disparities during heat treatment: the 
10 mm sections in the casting showed grain growth from 42 μ m to 48 
μ m, while the 70 mm sections exhibited more obvious growth from 115 
μ m to 138 μ m. Similarly, in semi-continuous casting of Mg-RE ingot, 
grain size and the volume fraction of secondary phase in areas close to 
the edge and near the center are quite different. Zhang et al. [16] found 
that, in semi-continuous casting of Mg-2.85Nd-0.25Zn-0.5Zr (wt%) 
alloy ingot with a diameter of 280 mm, the grain size gradually 
decreased from 82 μ m at the center to 47 μ m at the edge. Wang et al. 
[17] prepared Mg-8Gd-4Y-1Zn-Mn alloy ingot with a diameter of 
315 mm by semi-continuous casting. The average grain sizes at the 
center, 1/2 radius and edge areas of the cast ingot are ~ 306 μ m, ~ 141 
μ m and ~ 102 μ m, respectively; and they are ~ 430 μ m, ~ 196 μ m and ~ 
126 μ m, respectively after solution treatment. The difference in grain 
size between the center and the edge area of the casting becomes more 
obvious, and this difference led to a drop of yield strength from 240 MPa 
at the edge to 189 MPa at the center. In the subsequent solution treat -
ment of such large scale castings, the differences in grain size between 
the edge and center areas will be more apparent and lead to massive 
detriments to strengths.
In addition, fine-grained structures in Mg alloys obtained through 
plastic deformation or additive manufacturing may also lose their grain 
boundary strengthening effect after high temperature solution heat 
treatments. For instance, in the rolled Mg-4Y-3Nd-1.5Al alloy sheets 
[18] , the grain size increased from 18.2 μ m in the rolled state to 37.3 μ m 
after solution treatment at 520 
◦
C for 10 h. Liao et al. [19] found that the 
grain size of laser melting deposited VW103K increased from 19 μ m in 
the deposited state to 158 μ m in the solution treated state, indicating a 
grain coarsening of over 8 times occurred after solution treatment. The 
main reason for this abnormal coarsening of grains is the diffusion 
dominated grain boundary migration rate increases significantly at 
elevated temperatures, and fine grained materials produces a relatively 
large number of interfaces (grain boundaries) which providing more 
paths for diffusion [20] . In summary, abnormal grain growth and grain 
re-coarsening during solution heat treatments are two major factors that 
weaken the grain boundary strengthening contributions in Mg-RE 
alloys.
In recent years, the long period stacking ordered (LPSO) phase has 
been discovered in Mg-RE alloys by alloying with certain non-REs ele -
ments, such as Al [21] , Zn [22,23] , Co [24] and Ni [25] , etc. This phase 
has been shown to enhance the mechanical properties of Mg alloys at 
both room and elevated temperatures [26] . Furthermore, the LPSO 
phase has been reported to inhibit grain boundary migration [27,28] , 
which is expected to improve the thermal stability of grain boundaries in 
Mg-RE alloys. For example, Li et al. [29] found that, in Mg-10Y-1Al 
alloy, the grain size increases slowly from 35 μ m in the as-cast state to 
39 μ m after solution treatment at 520 
◦
C × 72 h, without obvious 
coarsening of the grain size. Deng et al. [30] found that the laser powder 
bed fused Mg-12Gd-2Y-1Zn-0.5Mn alloy with the lamellar LPSO pro -
duced a significant inhibition on grain coarsening during the solution 
treatment. The grain size is nearly unchanged before (~ 4.3 μ m in the 
received state) and after solution treatment (~ 4.7 μ m). Wang et al. [31]
found that the grain size of laser surface re-melted Mg-4Y-3Nd-1.5Al 
alloy almost did not coarse after solution treatment at 545 
◦
C × 30 h, also 
showing excellent thermal stability. The relatively stable grain boundary 
is also reported in the Mg-Gd-Al alloy system [32] . Moreover, by 
alloying with Al in the Mg-RE matrix alloys, the in-situ Al
2
RE particles 
show strong heterogeneous nucleation effect [33,34] , which can effec -
tively refine the grains [35,36] . The grain refining effect of Al
2
RE par -
ticles is also reported can overcome the drawbacks of high burn rate and 
easy settlement in Mg-Zr master alloys [37] . Hence, it is of great 
importance to utilize the grain refinement effect of in-situ Al
2
RE parti -
cles and the inhibition of grain boundary migration by the LPSO phase 
through alloying with Al in the Mg-RE alloys.
In review of the reported literatures, it is apparent that a gap of 
quantitative and systematic study is lacking to perform on the thermal 
stability of grain boundaries in cast Mg-RE alloys, as well as its influence 
to mechanical properties at different temperatures. Based on this, the 
main objectives of the present study are three-fold: First, we will sys -
tematically investigate the impact of Al
2
RE (typically the Al
2
Y in the Mg- 
Y-Al system) phase on the grain boundary thermal stability effects of 
Mg-RE alloys with comparison to conventional Mg-Zr master alloys. The 
optimal range of temperatures and time periods sustaining stable grain 
boundaries will be obtained. Secondly, the tensile strengths at room and 
elevated temperatures of Mg-Y alloys refined by Al
2
Y and Zr and in 
varied heat treatment states will be thoroughly studied. Thirdly, the 
mechanisms governing the thermal stability of grain boundaries and the 
related strengthening mechanisms of the alloys will be discussed 
comparatively. In all, this study aims to provide a comprehensive un -
derstanding of the relationships between grain boundary thermal sta -
bility and mechanical behavior of Mg-RE alloys. The work will offer 
valuable insights for the development of Mg-RE alloys with enhanced 
mechanical properties and improved thermal stability of grain 
boundaries.

aries than the Mg-Y-Zr alloys. The microstructure evolutions and me -
chanical behavior of both alloys were systematically characterized at 
both room and elevated temperatures, and at different heat treatment 
procedures. The underlying mechanisms governing the thermal stability 
of the grain boundaries at high temperatures and the strengthening 
mechanisms in Mg-RE alloys refined by two techniques were discussed 
in detail. The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1) The influence of grain refinement techniques on thermal stability 
of grain boundaries was investigated ranging from 500 
◦
C to 590 
◦
C. The Al
2
Y refined Mg-2.4Y-0.4Al
2
Y (WA-1) alloy retained its 
grain boundary thermal stability at 570 
◦
C, whereas the Mg-2.8Y- 
0.1Zr (WK-1) alloy fails to maintain stability within the studied 
temperature range. The Mg-Y-Al
2
Y alloy demonstrated superior 
thermal stability of grain boundaries than Mg-Y-Zr alloy.
(2) Tensile test at the solution heat-treated state showed that the 
strengths of Al
2
Y refined alloys were identical to those of Zr 
refined alloys at room temperature. However, at elevated tem -
peratures, the yield strength of the Zr refined alloy decreased 
significantly, while the strengths of the Al
2
Y refined alloy 
remained stable. After further thermal exposure heat treatment at 
570 
◦
C for 48 h, the strengths in Al
2
Y refined alloys were much 
higher than those in Zr refined counterparts at both room and 
elevated temperatures. The results proved that the relatively 
good thermal stability of grain boundaries in Al
2
Y refined Mg-RE 
alloy can also enhance the mechanical properties at both room 
and elevated temperatures.
(3) The roles of Al
2
Y particles and the LPSO phase in enhancing the 
thermal stability of grain boundaries were discussed. The for -
mation of the LPSO phase at the initial stage of solution treatment 
obstructs grain boundary migration, thereby imparting thermal 
stability to the grain boundaries during prolonged heat treatment 
at elevated temperatures. The LPSO phase remains stable at 570 
◦
C, while gradually dissolved with further increase of environ -
mental temperature, leading to grain coarsening. Hence, the 
presence of the LPSO phase is analyzed to be the primary 
mechanism for inhibiting grain coarsening.
(4) The strengthening mechanisms for Mg-RE alloy grain refined by 
Al
2
Y and Zr were discussed both in the solution heat treatment 
state and after thermal exposure heat treatment. The contribu -
tions of lattice friction strengthening, solid solution strength -
ening, grain boundary strengthening and particle strengthening 
(from Al
2
Y and LPSO phases) were all considered quantitatively. 
The solid solution strengthening from Y atoms was the primary 
strengthening mechanism for both alloys in the two studied 
states. The difference in grain boundary strengthening was the 
key factor that led to the strength improvements in Al
2
Y refined 
alloys to that of Zr refined counterparts.
