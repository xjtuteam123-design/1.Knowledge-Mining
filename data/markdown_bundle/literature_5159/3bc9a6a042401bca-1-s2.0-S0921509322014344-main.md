ABSTRACT 
Owing to good formability, wrought Al alloys are used in a wide range of industries, including turbochargers in 
automobile engines. The development of Al alloys with increased high-temperature strength is necessary to 
improve the fuel efficiency of engines. In this study, a modified heat-resistant Al – Mg – Zn – Cu – Ni quinary alloy

1. Introduction 
With the rising demand for diesel, high-efficiency combustion with 
high output is required for downsizing the engine and improving fuel 
efficiency to meet the energy-saving trend in the world. While during the 
operation of turbochargers of vehicles, light-weight Al alloy impellers 
are often subjected to high pressure, and the temperature on the intake 
side increases in compressed air [ 1-5 ]. An improvement in 
high-temperature strength (over 200 
◦
C in allowable temperature) of Al 
alloy impellers is necessary to achieve high pressure and temperature on 
the intake side. In turbochargers for automobile engines, the 2618 alloy 
(Al – Cu – Mg – Fe – Ni alloy) [ 6 ] — a common wrought heat-resistant Al 
alloy — is being used as an impeller member inside the compressor. 
However, such conventional alloys cannot be used for structural appli -
cations at service temperatures above 200 
◦
C, as their precipitates (for 
strengthening) with limited volume fractions often coarsen or dissolve 
into the α -Al matrix [ 7 ]. The microstructural instability significantly 
reduces the strength in the high-temperature range (above 200 
◦
C) [ 6 ]. 
Thus, potential heat-resistant alloys would be characterized by 
strengthening by thermodynamically stable phases with high volume 
fractions at elevated temperatures.

wrought-type Al alloys strengthened by stable T-Al

3. Materials and methods 
The nominal composition of the designed alloy was 
Al – 5Mg – 3.5Zn – 2Cu – 2Ni (at %). Each pure metal was weighed into the 
nominal composition and then taken into the alumina pot. These raw 
materials were completely melted and maintained at higher tempera -
tures than 700 
◦
C for 1800 s in a high-frequency induction furnace filled 
with Ar gas. A rod alloy ingot with a diameter of approximately 10 mm 
was cast by pouring the alloy melt into an iron mold; this ingot was used 
for microstructural characterizations. In addition, large-sized ingot rods 
with a diameter of 66 mm were fabricated by the high-frequency in -
duction furnace to prepare the specimens for tensile and creep-rupture 
tests. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP- 
AES) was used to analyze the chemical compositions of these ingots. The 
results of the analysis are shown in Table 1 . The proportions of the alloy 
elements in both ingots were almost the same as the nominal composi -
tion of the designed alloy. These ingots were cut into sample pieces for 
heat treatments. The cut samples were then solution-treated at 480 
◦
C 
(corresponding to the three-phase region of α -Al, T, and Al
3
(Cu, Ni)
2 
phases, as shown in Fig. 1 ) for 24 h, which was followed by water 
quenching. The solution-treated samples were subsequently aged for 
different time periods ranging from 0.1 h to 1000 h at a temperature of 
200 or 300 
◦
C. 
Microstructures of the alloy samples were characterized by scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM: JEOL JSM-6610A) equipped with an energy 
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) system. Thin foil samples prepared by an 
ion slicer (JEOL Ltd., Japan) at 6 V were observed by transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM: JEM-2100HK). The prepared thin samples 
were characterized using scanning transmission electron microscopy 
(STEM)-EDS analyses. The macroscopic constituent phases of the pre -
pared samples were identified via X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement. 
The hardness (HV) values of the heat-treated samples were measured 
using a Vickers indenter at ambient temperature (approximately 20 
◦
C). 
The applied load was 4.9 N and the dwell time was 15 s. The hardness 
tests were carried out within 1800 s after each heat treatment to elim -
inate the effect of natural age hardening. Each sample was subjected to 
more than five tests. From the perspective of application to heat- 
resistant materials, the heat-treated alloy specimens (solution-treated 
at 480 
◦
C for 24 h and pre-aged at 200 
◦
C for 10 h) were used for 
evaluating the mechanical performance at elevated temperatures. 
Standard specimens with a diameter of 4 mm and a gauge length of 20 
mm were used in tensile tests in a temperature range of 20 
◦

under a stress of 105 MPa (lower than a half of the yield strength ( Fig. 9 ), 
corresponding to the low stress level for evaluating the long-term creep

and TEM.
