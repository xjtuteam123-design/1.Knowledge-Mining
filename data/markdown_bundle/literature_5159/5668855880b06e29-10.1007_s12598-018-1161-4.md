Tailoring strength and ductility of high-entropy CrMnFeCoNi
alloy by adding Al
Xin Xian, Zhi-Hong Zhong* , Li-Jing Lin, Zhi-Xiong Zhu, Chang Chen,
Yu-Cheng Wu
Received: 31 May 2017 / Revised: 20 July 2017 / Accepted: 17 September 2018
/C211The Nonferrous Metals Society of China and Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2018
Abstract High-entropy alloys with high strength and
acceptable ductility at both room and elevated temperatures
for high-temperature structural applications are desired. In
this paper, CrMnFeCoNi alloy with outstanding ductility
but low strength was selected as baseline alloy to study the
formation of hard phase to strengthen this ductile alloy by
adding Al into it. The results indicate that there is a phase
structure transition from fcc to bcc when adding enough Al
into CrMnFeCoNi. The yield strength and hardness
increase with the content of Al increasing, due to the for-
mation of hard bcc phase in the ductile CrMnFeCoNi alloy.
The CrMnFeCoNiAl alloy with a bcc structure shows a
high yield strength and adequate ductility at elevated
temperatures. At 400 and 500 /C176C, the yield strength and
ductility of CrMnFeCoNiAl alloy are 975.59 MPa and
6.39% and 989.48 MPa and 9.15%, respectively. An
example of tailoring the strength and ductility of high-en-
tropy alloy is demonstrated in this paper.
Keywords High-entropy alloys; Structure transition;
Strength; Ductility
1 Introduction
Since the equiatomic alloy CrMnFeCoNi was produced [ 1],
the high-entropy alloys (HEAs), named after its high
mixing (or conﬁguration) entropy, have attracted consid-
erable attention in metal materials science, because HEAs
generally exhibit some interesting and unexpected
microstructures and properties [ 2–6]. HEAs contain several
principal components (usually C 5) in approximately
equiatomic proportions and usually exhibit a large mutual
solubility and trend to form a solution-like phase (simple
face-centered cubic (fcc) or body-centered cubic (bcc)
solid solution), instead of complex phases [ 5–9], due to
their high mixing entropy. However, despite effective
forming some stable solid solution through HEA approach,
recent studies have indicated that complex structures con-
taining intermetallic phases could form in HEAs. For
example, hard topologically close-packed (tcp) phases,
such as r and l intermetallic compounds, are reported in
the CoCrFeNiMo system [ 10]. The r phase is also found in
the alloys such as CoFeMnNiV, CoCrMnNiV [ 11],
Al0.5CoCrCuFeNiVx [12] and CoCrFeNiV [ 13]. However,
the exceptional ductility of CoCrFeNi [ 10] HEA (elonga-
tion of * 59%) makes it an ideal candidate baseline alloy
for hard tcp phase precipitation strengthening.
For CrMnFeCoNi alloy [ 1, 5], all of the containing
elements are the fourth-period transition elements and have
similar atomic radii. Similar to CoCrFeNi HEA, this alloy
is a simple fcc phase structure and exhibits outstanding
ductility and fracture toughness at room temperature.
However, the strength of CrMnFeCoNi alloy is relatively
low for structural applications, particularly for high-tem-
perature applications. The important scientiﬁc and techni-
cal viewpoint for structural use of HEAs is mostly
concerned with the question of how to effectively enhance
the strength but without causing considerable embrittle-
ment [ 10]. In this study, therefore, CrMnFeCoNi HEA, a
typical and widely investigated HEA, was selected as our
baseline alloy to study the formation of hard phase to
strengthen this ductile alloy.

Several studies showed that the crystalline structure,
microstructure and mechanical properties strongly depen-
ded on the elements in HEAs and the incorporation of large
atomic radius element into HEAs could cause lattice
expansion, thereby increasing the strength of the alloy
[6, 7, 14, 15]. In the present work, the alloying additions of
Al were added into CrMnFeCoNi. Aluminum has a rela-
tively large atomic radius ( r
Al = 143 pm, rCr = 128 pm)
for solid solution strengthening CrMnFeCoNi matrix.
Furthermore, it is known that Al is readily to form hard
intermetallic phases with Ni, Fe and Co. Also, Al may form
strong bonds with other elements due to energy negative
mixing enthalpy. In addition, it has been reported that
additions of Al stabilize the bcc crystal structure in the
CoCrCuFeNiAl
x and CoCrFeMnNiAl x HEAs [ 16–21] and
gradually increase the strength and hardness of the alloys.
It is also expected that alloying with Al will enhance the
oxidation resistance of the alloy. Hence, in this paper, Al
was selected as a promising element added into CrMnFe-
CoNi HEA for alloy strengthening, in order to acquire high
strength both at room temperature and elevated
temperatures.
2 Experimental
The six-component CrMnFeCoNiAl
x (x = 0, 0.25, 0.50,
0.75 and 1.00, denoted as Al x hereafter) ingots were pre-
pared by vacuum arc melting with high-purity elements
([ 99.9%) under argon atmosphere on a water-cooled
copper mold, and subsequently furnace-cooled. Ingots with
a weight of 30 g were remelted at least ﬁve times to ensure
chemical homogeneity. The crystal structure of as-cast
alloys was analyzed by X-ray diffractometer (XRD,
D/MAX2500 V), ranging from 20 /C176to 100/C176with a speed of
0.2 ( /C176)/C1 min
-1. After polishing mechanically, scanning
electron microscope (SEM, Sigma-Zeiss) and transmission
electron microscope (TEM, Tecnai G2 20) were used for
microstructure analysis, and micro-composition was ana-
lyzed by energy-dispersive spectrometry (EDS, X-MAX
80) equipped with SEM. The fracture morphology of
compressive samples was also examined by SEM operated
at the secondary electron mode. Mechanical properties
were characterized in terms of compression and hardness
tests. Vickers hardness was measured by the 200 HV-5
hardness tester under the load of 1.96 N with the duration
of 15 s at room temperature. The compression tests were
performed using a Shimadzu AG-X testing machine at
room temperature, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800 and 900 /C176C,
respectively. The strain rate was 1.0 mm /C1 min
-1, and the
diameter and height of samples were 3 and 6 mm,
respectively.

alloy and the large atomic size difference of Al with the

size difference in Fig. 2 can also conﬁrm this explanation.
However, with further increasing Al concentration, some
diffraction peaks corresponding to (110) and (211) bcc

lattice constant for bcc structure reduces to 0.2881 nm for
Al1.00 alloy, which is much smaller than fcc solid solution.
Additionally, the relative intensity of the fcc diffraction
peaks reduces, while that of the bcc phase enhances,
indicating that the content of the bcc phase increases and
the fcc peaks decrease and then disappear completely.
Thus, it can be deduced that, low Al content stabilizes fcc
phase. However, when Al content is high, it tends to sta-
bilize bcc structure, which can be attributed to the very
negative mixing enthalpy between Al and the other ele-
ments in CrMnFeCoNiAl
x alloy, as reported in many other
HEAs [ 21–25].

3.3 Mechanical properties
Figure 5 shows the compression stress–strain curves of the
CrMnFeCoNiAlx alloys at room temperature, and the rel-
evant parameters are listed in Table 3. With low addition
of Al, alloys exhibit low yield strength but excellent duc-
tility. For example, the yield strength of Al0.25 and Al0.50
alloys is 286.98 and 340.30 MPa, respectively, and the
ductility values are more than 9.5%. However, as Al con-
tent increases to 0.75, the yield strength increases signiﬁ-
cantly to 1193.52 MPa, while the ductility decreases to
2.72%, due to the increased content of bcc phase and the
enhanced lattice distortion energy [ 21, 24, 25], when the
concentration of Al element increases. In addition, the
yield strength and ductility of equiatomic bcc structure
Al1.00 alloy decrease compared with that of Al0.75 alloy.
The yield strength as a function of Al content is summa-
rized in Fig. 6. Similarly, the hardness also increases with
the increase in Al content, from HV 165.35 (Al0) to HV
497.10 (Al1.00). The enhanced yield strength and hardness
are mainly attributed to the formation of hard bcc phase. It
is known that bcc phases are typically harder than fcc
phases due to the limited numbers of available slip systems
in bcc phases [ 30], while fcc structure has a total of 12 slip
systems.
The fracture surfaces for CrMnFeCoNiAl
0.75 and
CrMnFeCoNiAl specimens at room temperature are shown
in Fig. 7. It is noticed that Al0.75 specimen macroscopi-
cally exhibits a typical shear fracture mode with a shearing
angle of about 45 /C176, while Al1.00 fractures from the middle
with an angle of 90 /C176. As shown in Fig. 7a, the fracture
morphology of Al0.75 is mainly composed of fan-like and
river-like patterns which are the typical fracture charac-
teristics of cleavage fracture under compression. In addi-
tion, some slip features caused by slip separation are also
observed, which may be attributed to the abundant slip
systems of fcc structure. For Al1.00 alloy (Fig. 7b), the
fracture morphology is composed of ﬁsh-bone like and
river-like patterns, which is typical cleavage fracture and
corresponds to the regions consisting of bcc phase.
In order to evaluate the high-temperature mechanical
properties of relatively brittle Al0.75 and Al1.00 alloys,
compression tests were performed for the two alloys at
elevated temperatures, and the compressive stress–strain
curves are shown in Fig. 8. It is interesting to ﬁnd that the
strength and ductility increase when the temperature is
below 500 /C176C. For example, the compressive yield strength
of Al1.00 alloy increases from 858.13 to 989.48 MPa,
when the temperature increases from room temperature to
500 /C176C. However, the strength decreases signiﬁcantly

4 Conclusion
In this study, the effects of Al on the evolution of phase
structure, microstructure, mechanical properties and frac-
ture behavior of high-entropy CrMnFeCoNiAl
x (x =0 ,
0.25, 0.50, 0.75 and 1.00) alloys were investigated. The
CrMnFeCoNiAlx alloy has a fcc phase structure at low Al
content ( x \ 0.50), but the further addition of Al promotes
the formation of bcc phase. Owing to the large atomic size
difference and the negative mixing enthalpy between Al
and other consistent elements, the strength and hardness of
CrMnFeCoNi alloy increase with the increase in Al con-
tent, while the ductility shows an opposite trend. The
strength and ductility of CrMnFeCoNiAl
x alloy could be
tailored by the content of Al. The Al0.75 alloy and Al1.00
show a high yield strength and adequate ductility at ele-
vated temperatures. At 500 /C176C, the yield strength and
ductility of Al1.00 alloy are enhanced to 989.48 MPa and
9.15%, respectively.
