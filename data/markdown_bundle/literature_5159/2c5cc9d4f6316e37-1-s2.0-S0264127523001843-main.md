abstract
Fatigue strength is one of the most important indicators used to evaluate titanium alloy orthopedic
implants. Sufﬁcient fatigue strength can enhance the reliability of orthopedic implants. It is well known
that grain reﬁnement is beneﬁcial to enhancing material strength but against fatigue resistance. This
work has fabricated a triple-phase core–shell micro-nanostructure in a biomedical Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy with
the ultraﬁne grained
a phase stabilized by the nanoscale conjugated b/Ti2Cu shells, of which results in a
high fatigue resistance. The results show that the average grain size of the a, b, and Ti 2Cu phases are
585 nm, 421 nm and 135 nm, respectively. The soft b phases in the conjugated b/Ti2Cu shells can produce
a plastic zone at the crack tip to hinder the propagation of fatigue cracks. The fatigue strength of the core–
shell structured Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy is 750 MPa under the experimental condition of R = -1 and Nf =1 0 7,
which is 36.3 % higher than the widely used Ti6Al4V alloy. The mechanisms for improving the fatigue
properties have also been discussed. These are of great signiﬁcance for the development of a new gener-
ation of high fatigue strength titanium alloys.

nents, which can be processed into artiﬁcial hip joints, knee joints,
etc. These orthopedic load-bearing components are subjected to
alternating loads during daily human activities so they often are
at risk of fatigue failure, leading to costly and painful secondary

The fatigue life of metals is determined by the initiation and
propagation of fatigue cracks. On the one hand, the greater the sta-
tic strength, the more difﬁcult it is to activate slip systems and ini-
tiate fatigue cracks. On the other hand, the higher the plasticity, the
larger the plastic zone area at the crack tip. This is more conducive
to stress relaxation, thus inhibiting the propagation of microcracks
[2]. Based on this, the best way to improve the fatigue performance
is to simultaneously improve the strength and the plasticity of
metals. Grain reﬁnement is recognized as the way in which this
can be achieved. The study of K. Majchrowicz et al. [3] on the
5483 Al-Mg alloy showed that the fatigue strength increases signif-
icantly when the grain size is reﬁned from hundreds of microns to
several microns, and the relationship between the grain size and
fatigue strength conforms approximately to the Hall-Petch for-
mula. However, S. Picak et al. [4] found that when the grain size
is further reﬁned from a few microns to the sub-micron scale,
the improvement of fatigue performance is quite limited, and
sometimes the fatigue performance will even decrease as the grain
size decreases. It can be seen that as the grain size decreases, grain
reﬁnement only marginally improves the fatigue performance.
There are two reasons for this marginal improvement. First,
although grain reﬁnement can improve the static strength of the
material, when the grains are reﬁned to the submicron scale, the
effect of inherent microstructural instability on the mechanical
properties of the material becomes more and more signiﬁcant. P.
Zhao et al. [5] studied how the microstructure of ultraﬁne-
grained pure titanium responded to cyclic loadings and found that
cyclic stress promoted both grain growth and the softening of tita-
nium, eventually leading to the concentration of stress and the ini-
tiation of fatigue microcracks [2,6]. Therefore, grain reﬁnement
only marginally suppresses the initiation of fatigue cracks. Second,
grain reﬁnement might increase the plasticity because of improv-
ing the coordination of plastic deformation, it might also reduce
the plasticity because of reducing the mobility of dislocations.
When the grain size is reﬁned to near the submicron scale, the
effect of the latter gradually exceeds that of the former. R. Liu
et al. [7] found that when the grain size of a Cu-Al alloy decreases
from coarse grains to the nanocrystalline scale, the fatigue strength
ﬁrst increases and then decreases. When the grain size is on the
ultraﬁne scale, the material has the highest fatigue strength. Fur-
ther reﬁnement of the grains thus reduces the plasticity of the
material and accelerates the growth rate of fatigue cracks. Research
done by J. Chen et al. [8] on nanocrystalline super-elastic Ni-Ti
shape memory alloys showed that in the micro-nano scale range,
grain reﬁnement can signiﬁcantly increase the fatigue crack
growth rate d a/dN. Therefore, grain reﬁnement also only margin-
ally suppresses the growth of fatigue cracks. In conclusion, if the
microstructural stability of micro-nanocrystalline titanium alloys
can be improved to inhibit the initiation of fatigue cracks and the
propagation of fatigue cracks through a reasonable structural
design, the scale over which grain reﬁnement will improve fatigue
performance can be expanded, and the fatigue performance of tita-
nium alloys can be greatly improved.
In order to realize this purpose, a triple-phase core–shell micro-
nanostructured Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy (graphical abstract) has been
designed in our previous study [9]. b/Ti
2Cu shells with thicknesses
of tens of nanometers are constructed outside of several equiaxed
a grains to improve the microstructural stability. Meanwhile, the
soft b phases in the b/Ti2Cu shells are used to accommodate the
strain around the crack tips, thus hindering crack growth. Such a
microstructural design is achieved by our newly developed ‘‘eutec-
toid element alloying (E), quenching (Q), and thermal deformation
(D)” EQD strategy, which could not only enable the mass produc-
tion of ultraﬁne-grained metals with high microstructural stability,
but also increase the fatigue behavior of titanium alloys. Moreover,
the EQD strategy is compatible with the conventional low cost hot
rolling or hot forging technologies [9].
Copper is not only an alloying element that is very suitable for
adding to titanium alloys to form a stable ultraﬁne core–shell
microstructure, but also an essential metal ion to the proper func-
tioning of organs and metabolic processes in the human body. In
addition, adding Cu element into titanium alloys has been proved
to have excellent antibacterial and anti-infective properties. [10–
12].
It should be pointed out that we have studied the low cycle fati-
gue properties of Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy in the early stage [13]. In the
previous study, we designed a ﬁne grained Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy with
TRIP effect, which has good plasticity, so it can improve the low
cycle fatigue performance. In this study, the ultraﬁne grained tita-
nium alloy with core–shell structure has a more stable microstruc-
ture and higher strength, so it can improve the high cycle fatigue
performance, which is an extremely important indicator for ortho-
pedic implant components.
In this study, Ti6Al4V5Cu bars with core–shell micro-
nanostructures are fabricated on an industrial scale ( u12
mm /C2 60 m). Then the high cycle fatigue properties of the material
are investigated and compared with the widely used commercial
Ti6Al4V alloy. Finally, the mechanism by which fatigue perfor-
mance is improved is explained, which lays the scientiﬁc founda-
tion for the development of biomedical titanium alloys as
orthopedic implant materials.
2. Materials and experiment
The Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy was melted in a 25 kg furnace using vac-
uum arc remelting. Then, the ingot was kept at 960 /C176C (the b single
phase zone) for one hour and then quenched in salt water in order
to obtain the ultraﬁne nanoscale lath precursors. After that, the
alloy was passed through a hot rolling mill two times at 800 ℃
with a strain rate of 2 s
/C0 1, each time with a deformation of more
than 30 %. Finally, the Ti6Al4V5Cu rolled rod with a diameter of
12 mm was prepared to perform the fatigue experiment. We also
isothermally compressed the ultra-ﬁne nanoscale lath precursor
with a hydraulic press at 800 /C176C with a strain rate of 2 s /C0 1 and pre-
pared a rolled plate with a thickness of 12 mm for the fatigue crack
growth experiment. A commercial Ti6Al4V hot-rolled rod and plate
were used as the control material. Table 1 shows the results of the
chemical composition analysis of both materials.
Uniaxial tensile tests of the Ti6Al4V5Cu and the commercial
Ti6Al4V specimens were performed on a CMT 4304 electronic uni-
versal testing machine at a strain rate of 1 /C2 10-3 s/C0 1. The samples
were machined into standard tensile samples with a diameter of
5 mm and a gauge length of 15 mm. The tensile properties were
given by the average of three samples. The 8800 Mini Tower
Instron fatigue testing machine was used to conduct room temper-
ature high-cycle fatigue (HCF) experiments on the two materials
under stress-controlled uniaxial symmetric tension–compression
cyclic loading (R = -1). The fatigue loading mode was a sinusoidal
wave with a frequency of 30 Hz. The hourglass shaped high cycle
fatigue samples with a total length of 90 mm and a center diameter
of 5 mm were designed according to national standard ASTM E466-
15. The sample surface was polished to a roughness of less than
0.15 lm. In order to determine the fatigue limit of the alloys, at
least 3 specimens were tested under each stress amplitude, and
the stress-cycle (S AN) curves were drawn accordingly. We deter-
mined the stress amplitude corresponding to 10 7 cycles without
fatigue fracture as the fatigue limit in this work, and tested 5 sam-

3. Results
3.1. Initial microstructure and mechanical properties
Optical microscopy (OM) observations show that the
microstructure of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy is uniform, but too ﬁne to
determine the grain size ( Fig. 1 a). TEM bright-ﬁeld (BF) and high
angle annular dark ﬁeld (HAADF) observations reveal ( Fig. 1b) that
there are a large number of ultraﬁne grains below 500 nm in the
alloy. The element mapping observation suggests that the
microstructure is composed of relatively small V-rich phase and
Cu-rich phase grains wrapping around larger Al-rich phase grains.
High resolution-TEM (HRTEM) observations show that these Al-
rich, V-rich and Cu-rich phases correspond to a, b, and Ti 2Cu
phases, respectively. The diffraction pattern shows multiple con-
tinuous closed rings ( Fig. 1 c), indicating that these grains have a
variety of different crystallographic orientations.
EBSD analysis ( Fig. 1 d) indicates that the average grain size of
the a, b, and Ti2Cu phases are 585 nm, 421 nm and 135 nm, respec-
tively, and that the proportions of these phases are 82.5 %, 11.8 %,
and 5.7 %, respectively. The b and Ti 2Cu phases are uniformly and
continuously distributed around the a phases, forming the
expected core–shell microstructure. It can be seen that there is
mainly a {01 1
/C0
0} texture in the a phases, a {0 0 1} texture in the
b phases, and a {1 1 0} texture in the Ti 2Cu phases.
In contrast, OM observations of the Ti6Al4V alloy show that the
grain size is generally between 2 and 5 lm( Fig. 2a). The results of
EBSD analysis ( Fig. 2 b) indicate that the microstructure is com-
posed of a and b phases, where the average grain size of the a
and b phases is 2.75 lm (with a proportion of 90.8 %) and
1.05 lm (with a proportion of 9.2 %), respectively. It can be seen
from the EBSD IPFs that the a phases have a strong {01 1

the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy. Looking at the kernel average misorientation
(KAM) diagram, it can be seen that the micro-strains are mainly
distributed at the grain boundaries.
Tensile experiments were also carried out on the two alloys at
room temperature ( Fig. 3 a), showing that the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy
has achieved a substantial increase in strength without sacriﬁcing
plasticity. Its average elongation is as high as 18 %, and the average
tensile strength reaches 1280 /C6 5 MPa, which is about 30 % higher
than that of the Ti6Al4V alloy. By comparing with other types of a,
(a + b), and b titanium alloys ( Fig. 3 b) [14–28], we found that the
Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy in this study has the best balance of strength and
plasticity.
3.2. Fatigue properties
It can be seen from the S AN curves ( Fig. 4 a) that the fatigue
properties of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy are signiﬁcantly better than that
of the Ti6Al4V alloy. Under the same fatigue life, the fatigue load-
ing stress of the former is much higher than that of the latter, and
the longer the fatigue life is, the more obvious this trend becomes.
The fatigue limit of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy is 750 MPa, which is
36.3 % higher than that of the Ti6Al4V alloy (compared with the
Reference in Fig. 4 b, the fatigue limit of Ti6Al4V alloy is
550 MPa, which is in the normal range). Furthermore, the
Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy in this study also effectively bolstered the previ-
ously marginal effect of grain reﬁnement on the improvement of
fatigue strength ( Fig. 4b) [29–40]. For most titanium alloys (under
the stress ratio R = -1), the fatigue strength ﬁrst increases signiﬁ-
cantly as grains are reﬁned (200
lm＞d＞10 lm), but when grains
are further reﬁned to less than 10 lm, the effect of grain reﬁne-
ment on the improvement of fatigue strength becomes less obvi-
ous (as shown in the grey area in Fig. 4 b), and the fatigue
strength sometimes even decreases with further grain reﬁnement.
The Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy in this study does not follow this trend, and
its fatigue strength is signiﬁcantly higher than other titanium
alloys with similar grain sizes (as shown by the red star in Fig. 4b).

3.4. Fatigue fracture analysis
The fracture of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy was observed using SEM
(Fig. 9 a). The fracture is composed of three areas: the crack initia-
tion area (orange area, accounting for 1.1 %), the crack growth area
(yellow area, accounting for 34.6 %), and the ﬁnal fracture area
(green area, accounting for 64.3 %). The site where the fatigue
cracks begin is located on the surface of the specimen, and there
are many smooth facets in this area. The crack growth area is close
to the initiation site and extends to the surrounding area. In this
area, fatigue striations perpendicular to the crack growth direction
can be observed. The distance between two fatigue striations is the
distance over which fatigue cracks grow under each stress cycle
(fatigue crack growth rate, d a/dN), which increases rapidly as the
stress intensity factor range DK ¼ YDr ﬃﬃﬃﬃﬃﬃpap increases ( Y = 1 is the
geometric factor [33,41], Dr is the stress amplitude, and a is the
length of the fatigue crack) increases. Using this formula, the fati-
gue crack growth rate curve is obtained ( Fig. 10). When the fatigue
crack grows to a certain critical size, which occurs when DK
reaches the fracture toughness KⅠC of the material, the crack rapidly
destabilizes and propagates to form a transient zone. In this area, a
large number of round and shallow dimples can be observed.
The fatigue fracture morphologies of the Ti6Al4V alloy are sim-
ilar to that of Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy ( Fig. 9 b). The differences between
the two materials are mainly reﬂected in the following three
aspects: First, the fatigue crack growth area of the Ti6Al4V alloy
(6.7 %) is smaller than that of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy (34.6 %), while
the fracture area (92.4 %) is larger than that of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy
(64.3 %). Second, according to the crack growth rate curves
(Fig. 10 ), under the same DK value, d a/dN for the Ti6Al4V alloy is
always greater than for the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy. The reason for this
will be explained in detail in Section 4.2. Finally, the dimple diam-
eter of the Ti6Al4V alloy is signiﬁcantly larger than that of the
Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy in the fracture area.
The longitudinal section of the fatigue crack growth area of the
Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy was observed using SEM ( Fig. 11a). It can be seen
that when encountering a/a grain boundaries, a/b phase bound-
aries, a/Ti2Cu phase boundaries, and b/Ti2Cu phase boundaries,
the fatigue cracks will all be deﬂected. When encountering a/b
phase boundaries, the average deﬂection angle is largest (50.84 /C176),
while when encountering a/Ti2Cu phase boundaries, the average
deﬂection angle is smallest (11.63 /C176), indicating that a/b phase
boundaries can effectively hinder the propagation of fatigue cracks,
while a/Ti2Cu phase boundaries provide a much weaker hindrance
to crack propagation. Similarly, for the Ti6Al4V alloy ( Fig. 11 b),
fatigue crack deﬂections occur when the cracks encounter a/a
grain boundaries and a/b phase boundaries, but a/b phase bound-
aries (deﬂection angle 25.35 /C176) have a greater hindering effect on
the fatigue crack growth than a/a grain boundaries (deﬂection
angle 20.38 /C176). In the Ti6Al4V alloy, the crack deﬂection occurs
every 1.85 lm on average, while in the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy, the value
is only 0.97 lm.
4. Discussion
In this study, a micro-nanocrystalline Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy with
ultra-high fatigue strength was successfully prepared. Compared
with the commercial Ti6Al4V alloy, the fatigue strength was
increased by 36.3 %. In the following sections, the mechanism by
which fatigue performance was improved will be explained from
two perspectives: inhibiting fatigue crack initiation and hindering
fatigue crack growth.
4.1. Fatigue crack initiation
According to Fig. 1 and Fig. 5 , the microstructure of the
Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy is ultraﬁne and stable, so fatigue crack initiation
is more difﬁcult in the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy than in the Ti6Al4V alloy.

model of fatigue crack initiation in which they are induced by dis-
location dipoles stacking in front of grain boundaries and calcu-

required for fatigue crack initiation and the grain size D as being
given by the following formula [42]:

From the perspective of stability, although grain reﬁnement can
inhibit the initiation of fatigue cracks, for other titanium alloys
reported in the literature ( Fig. 4 b), when the grain size is reﬁned
to less than 10 lm, the improvement of fatigue strength through
grain reﬁnement becomes marginal. The research results of M.W.
Kapp et al. [43] showed that grain growth and cyclic softening
occur during the cyclic loading process of ultraﬁne-grained pure
Cu and that dislocation accumulation in front of grain boundaries
could lead to local stress concentration and fatigue crack initiation.
In this study, the commercial Ti6Al4V alloy, also undergoes grain
coarsening under cyclic loading ( Fig. 6 a). During the fatigue pro-
cess, due to dynamic recrystallization and boundary migration,
the dislocation density of the Ti6Al4V alloy decreases signiﬁcantly,
resulting in obvious cyclic softening ( Table 2 ), which is consistent
with the results reported by M.W. Kapp et al [43]. From the KAM
diagram ( Fig. 6 d), it can be seen that there are a large number of
dislocations around the grains that exhibit obvious coarsening,
which will promote stress concentration and the initiation of fati-
gue cracks. In contrast, the grain size of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy with
core–shell micro-nanostructures decreased after fatigue. It can be
seen from the results of the experiments ( Table 2) that cyclic soft-
ening did not happen during the fatigue process, and that the dis-
location density and hardness increased. This is because during the
fatigue process, dislocations accumulate in the
a and b phases, and
many low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) are formed. During the
deformation process, the misorientation between the sub-grains
gradually increases, and the LAGBs transform into high-angle grain
boundaries (HAGBs), so the grain size decreases, which in turn
leads to an increase in the hardness of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy. From
the above analysis, we can therefore conclude that the micro-
nanostructured Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy in this study has relatively good
microstructural stability, which is one of the important reasons
why it can effectively inhibit the initiation of fatigue cracks com-
pared to the commercial Ti6Al4V alloy and other ultraﬁne-
grained titanium alloys.

further explained in terms of thermodynamics and kinetics. In

4.2. Fatigue crack growth
According to Fig. 9, we can see that fatigue crack growth is more
difﬁcult and that the crack growth rate is slower for the
Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy. Because the fatigue threshold DKth of the
micro-nanostructured Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy is higher than that of the
Ti6Al4V alloy used for comparison, the growth of fatigue cracks
is more difﬁcult in the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy. For the crack growth rate,
compared with the commercial Ti6Al4V alloy, the micro-
nanocrystalline Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy can effectively reduce the crack
growth rate during the fatigue process. According to the different
slopes of the log(d a/dN)-log

low-speed growth stage (OA, O’A’); stage II, the steady growth
stage (AB, A’B’); stage III, the accelerated growth stage (BC, B’C’).
The good resistance of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy to fatigue crack
growth can be attributed to its lower fatigue crack growth rate
and the larger stress intensity factor range DK in these three stages.

cycles which occur in stage I may be close to or even more than
that of stage II due to the low d a/dN. It is still necessary to clarify

fatigue crack growth resistance during this stage. First, according

propagation to occur in stage I, it needs to meet two conditions:

5. Conclusions
In this study, we successfully prepared a new type of core–shell
micro-nanostructured Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy with an ultra-high fatigue
limit. The main conclusions of this article are as follows:
(1) Through the use of the EQD process, the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy
with a core–shell micro-nanostructure can be prepared,
and the average grain size of the a phases is 585 nm.
(2) The tensile strength of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy is 1286 MPa
and the elongation is as high as 18 %. Its fatigue limit reaches
750 MPa (R = -1, N f =1 0 7), which is 36.3 % higher than the
commercial Ti6Al4V alloy.
(3) The core–shell structure of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy gives it
good microstructural stability and inhibits grain coarsening
and cyclic softening during cyclic loading, thereby effec-
tively inhibiting the initiation of fatigue cracks.
(4) The ultraﬁne grains of the Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy continually
cause the deﬂection of cracks during the propagation pro-
cess. The ﬂexible b phases in the core–shell structure are
conducive to generating a larger plastic zone at the crack
tip, thereby effectively reducing the crack propagation rate
under fatigue loading.
