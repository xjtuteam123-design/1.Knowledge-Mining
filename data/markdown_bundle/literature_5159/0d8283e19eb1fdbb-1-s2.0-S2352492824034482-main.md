ABSTRACT
A new composition of the FeCrAl alloy was developed to fabricate crack-free products possessing excellent tensile 
properties and high-temperature oxidation resistances via laser powder bed fusion. FeCrAl alloys are promising 
materials for high-temperature industrial applications. To achieve a broad range of applications, such as 
accident-tolerant fuel claddings, the development of FeCrAl alloys with high strength and oxidation resistance is 
crucial. In this study, two new types of FeCrAl alloy powder were developed based on its original composition. 
The crack evaluation, microstructure observation, mechanical analysis, and high-temperature oxidation test of 
the fabricated specimens were conducted. The results revealed that solid-state cracking occurred in the speci -
mens obtained using the original composition of the FeCrAl powder. In contrast, cracking was prevented in the 
developed FeCrAl alloys with a lower carbon content. Mechanical tests indicated a substantial improvement in 
the ductility of the as-built specimens fabricated using the developed FeCrAl alloy powders. The low carbon 
content and removal of silicon and manganese elements in the developed FeCrAl alloy considerably improved the 
high-temperature oxidation resistance of the as-built product. The relation between the changes in the carbide 
distribution at grain boundaries, constitution of the surface oxide scale, and improvements in properties due to 
the changes in the alloy composition was explored.
1. Introduction
Iron – chromium – aluminum (FeCrAl) alloys exhibit excellent oxida -
tion resistances at high temperatures, low thermal expansion co -
efficients, and high electrical resistivities. Therefore, they are promising 
materials for high-temperature industrial applications, such as heating 
elements and components for furnaces and structural components for 
nuclear power applications [1 – 3] . Recently, FeCrAl alloys are consid -
ered potential candidate materials for accident-tolerant fuel cladding, as 
replacements for Zr-based alloys used in the fuel claddings of light water 
reactors [4 – 6] . The excellent oxidation resistance of FeCrAl alloys 
originates from the presence of protective scales composed of composite 
oxides, including α -Al

O
3 
and Cr

3
.
To broaden the application realm of FeCrAl alloys in high- 
temperature environments, their strength, oxidation resistance, and 
corrosion resistance should be further improved. The strength and 
oxidation resistance increase with increasing aluminum and chromium 
contents [7] . However, a high aluminum content could increase the 
ductile – brittle transition (DBT) temperature, which may plausibly 
induce cracking issues [8] . Although the corrosion resistance of FeCrAl 
alloys can be considerably increased using a high chromium content, 
this may increase their embrittlement [9] . Generally, aluminum and 
chromium contents are in the range of 3 – 6 and 5 – 18 mass%, respec -
tively. Oxide dispersion – strengthened (ODS) FeCrAl alloys have attrac -
ted attention, as they exhibit enhanced mechanical properties and 
high-temperature corrosion resistance [10 – 13] .
FeCrAl alloys are fabricated via wrought or power metallurgy tech -
niques depending on the desired product form. Additive manufacturing 
(AM) has attracted extensive attention in the metal fabrication industry 
because of its efficient material utilization, ability to fabricate complex 
geometry parts, and higher solidification rate than the conventional 
manufacturing processes [14 – 16] . Among the metal AM technologies, 
powder bed fusion-laser beam (PBF-LB) is regarded the primary choice, 
as it enables high manufacturing precision and excellent part quality.
The fabrication of ODS/FeCrAl alloys via PBF-LB is a cost-effective 
method [17,18] . The Fe-12Cr-6Al alloy fabricated via PBF-LB demon -
strated an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 401 MPa and elongation of 
7.8 % [19,20] . Zr-containing ODS-FeCrAl produced via laser AM

The fabrication of crack-free FeCrAl alloys possessing improved 
mechanical properties via PBF-LB remains a challenge. In this study, two 
new compositions of the FeCrAl alloy with a high chromium content 
were developed to fabricate products featuring high strength, improved 
ductility, and high-temperature oxidation resistance. One type of the 
composition was designed to reduce the carbon content in the FeCrAl 
composition to prevent cracking. This concept was inspired by the crack 
prevention strategy adopted for tool steels that are affected by the car -
bon content [23,24] . To further improve the high-temperature oxida -
tion, the chromium content was increased, and silicon and manganese 
elements were removed from the composition, as manganese adversely 
affects the oxidation resistance [25,26] .
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to develop an 
FeCrAl alloy possessing a high chromium content of 26 mass%. The 
feedstock powder of the three types of the FeCrAl alloy was produced 
using a vacuum gas atomizer. The cracking situations, microstructures, 
mechanical properties, and oxidation resistances of the specimens 
fabricated via PBF-LB were investigated.
2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Materials and process conditions
Three types of FeCrAl alloy powders were obtained in-house via 
vacuum gas atomization. Table 1 lists the compositions and particle size 
distributions (evaluated by a laser diffraction particle size analyzer) of 
the original (Powder A) and modified (Powders B and C) FeCrAl alloy 
powders. Powder B is designed by reducing the carbon content to sup -
press the issues related to cracking during PBF-LB. Powder C is designed 
by eliminating silicon and manganese elements from Powder B to 
improve the high-temperature oxidation resistances. The representative 
morphologies of the powders are provided in the supplementary mate -
rials (Fig. S1).
All the FeCrAl alloy specimens were fabricated using an PBF-LB 
system (EOS M290, EOS GmbH), as previously reported [27,28] . The 
PBF-LB process was conducted under an argon gas flow with an oxygen 
concentration of < 0.1 %. To investigate the crack characteristics in the 
process window, cube-shaped specimens with the dimensions of 
10 × 10 × 10 mm
3 
were obtained using the three types of powders under 
different process parameter conditions. The layer thickness and hatch 
distance were set as 0.04 and 0.11 mm, respectively. The laser power 
and scan speed were 200 – 350 W and 700 – 1500 mm/s, respectively. The 
specimens built from powders A, B, and C were named as “ Specimen A ” 
“ Specimen B ” “ Specimen C ” , respectively. The cross-sections of the 
cube-shaped specimens parallel to the building direction (BD) were 
evaluated to identify the cracks and voids. The mechanical properties 
and high-temperature oxidation resistance of the specimens were 
investigated using a void fraction of < 0.1 % by selecting the PBF-LB 
process parameters from the established process windows.
2.2. Characterization methods
The mirror-polished cross-sections of the cube-shaped specimens 
were analyzed using a digital microscope (Keyence, VHX-6000). Cracks 
were identified at a magnification of × 100. Additionally, the presence 
of cracks was confirmed by evaluating the appearance of the specimens. 
The areal void fraction of the specimens was determined via image 
analysis using the microscope system at a magnification of × 500. Five 
regions were analyzed, and the calculated average value of the void 
fraction was employed as the representative value for each specimen.
The crystal orientation of the specimen cross-section was evaluated 
via electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD, JEOL JSM-7900F) with a step 
size of 2 μ m. The microstructures were characterized in detail via 
scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM; JEOL JEM- 
ARM200F). The STEM foils were prepared using the high-angle grain 
boundaries of the specimens identified via EBSD by employing the 
focused ion beam (FIB) microsampling technique (Hitachi High-Tech 
Corporation FB-2100). The nanoscale elemental distributions were 
identified via elemental mapping through energy-dispersive X-ray 
spectroscopy (EDS).
2.3. Mechanical test
Vickers hardness was measured using a Vickers hardness tester (FV- 
300, FUTURE-TECH) with an indentation load of 10 kg along BD and a 
dwell period of 30 s following the ASTM E92 standard. Five indentations 
were tested at random locations on each specimen, and the average 
value was employed as the representative hardness. The tensile test was 
performed using an INSTRON5982 system at room temperature. The 
tensile direction was perpendicular to BD. The specimens with a gauge 
length of 7.0 mm and cylindrical gauge cross-section diameter of 3.0 mm 
were machined from the built specimens with the dimensions of 
40 × 10 × 10 mm. The tensile test rate was 10 MPa/s before reaching the 
offset yield strength of 0.2 % and was subsequently changed to 5 %/min 
until failure. Three specimens were tested for each type of FeCrAl alloy.
2.4. Oxidation resistance analysis
The oxidation resistance of the specimens was evaluated at 800 ℃ 
and 900 ℃ under atmospheric conditions. The specimens with the di -
mensions of 25 × 25 × 10 mm were machined via wire electric discharge 
machining to a 25 × 25 × 2 mm plate with a 25 × 25 mm plane 
perpendicular to BD. The surfaces were mechanically polished to a grit 
of #1000. After measuring the dimensions and weight, the specimens 
were exposed at the temperatures of 800 ℃ and 900 ℃ for 1000 h in an 
atmospheric furnace. After cooling the furnace to room temperature, the 
specimens were weighed to determine their weight changes during the 
exposure. The mass gain (mg/cm
2
) was calculated based on the weight 
changes utilizing the exposed surface area determined using the di -
mensions and weight differences. Two specimens were exposed to check 
for repeatability for each type of FeCrAl alloy. SUS316L stainless steel 
and nickel-based alloy inconel ® 625 (forged) were used as comparative 
materials in the test conducted at 800 ℃ for 1000 h. The chemical 
composition of SUS316L and inconel ® 625 are presented in supple -
mentary materials ( Table S1 ).

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Evaluation of cracks and void fraction
Table 2 summarizes the characteristics of the cracks and voids in 
FeCrAl alloys. Cracking occurred in all the printed cubes of Specimen A, 
except for the specimen with a high void fraction. Generally, the process 
parameters consisting of a low laser power and high scan speed result in 
a low energy input that cannot achieve the complete fusion of powders, 
leading to high void fractions. The residual stress induced via rapid 
solidification during the PBF-LB process can be alleviated by the voids 
[29] . Therefore, in this study, the occurrence of cracks was prevented 
using a low-energy-input condition. Conversely, no cracks were 
observed in Specimens B and C. Comparing the chemical compositions 
listed in Table 1 , a low carbon content in the feedstock powders can lead 
to a considerable crack reduction, which reveals that the carbon content 
plays an important role in reducing the number of cracks in FeCrAl al -
loys. Fig. 1 presents a typical crack morphology observed for Specimen 
A. The crack was initiated from the edge at a height of 1 – 2 mm and 
extended inward in the horizontal direction. The crack morphology was 
similar to that of tool steel, in which the tensile stress accumulation 
during the PBF-LB process resulted in cracks [23] . For the three types of 
specimens, low void fractions ( < 0.1 %) were obtained for a large range 
of process parameter values in the established process windows. The 
process parameters consisting of a laser power of 325 W and scanning 
speed of 800 mm/s that resulted in a void fraction of < 0.1 % for all the 
powders was employed for printing the specimens used in the me -
chanical and oxidation resistance tests.
3.2. Microstructural characterization
The microstructure characteristics of the cross-section of Specimen A 
are illustrated in Fig. 2 . The inverse pole figure map obtained via EBSD 
( Fig. 2 (a)) demonstrated typical columnar grains that are elongated 
along BD. To further reveal the presence of elemental segregation and 
nanoparticles, dark-field (DF)-STEM was used to evaluate the foils pre -
pared using FIB at the high-angle grain boundary indicated by the black 
dotted line in Fig. 2 (a). Nanoscale precipitates were observed at the 
grain boundaries and intragranular regions ( Fig. 2 (b)). The EDS map -
ping and quantitative analysis results ( Table 3 ) confirmed that the 
precipitates in the intragranular regions were titanium carbides (TiC), 
whereas those at the grain boundaries were TiC and chromium carbides.
Figs. 3 and 4 demonstrate the microstructure evaluation results for 
Specimens B and C. In both the specimens, a few precipitates were noted 
at the grain boundaries, whereas precipitates were not observed in the 
intragranular regions. Furthermore, the precipitates were confirmed as 
TiC via quantitative EDS analysis ( Table 3 ). The TiC precipitate in 
Specimens B showed a lower carbon content than that of Specimens C. 
This may be due to thicker STEM foil and smaller TiC precipitate. EDS 
analysis value of the precipitate changes depending on the foil thickness. 
During STEM-EDS analysis, matrix composition is also counted when the 
precipitate is small. TiC precipitate in Specimen B ( Fig. 3 (h)) was smaller 
than that of Specimen C ( Fig. 4 (h)), therefore the carbon content was 
lower due to the incorporation of the matrix composition. The variations 
in the amount and size of the precipitates between Specimen A and 
Specimen B/C are attributed to the difference in the carbon content of 
the feedstock powders. The results indicated that the higher the carbon 
content in the feedstock powders, the larger the amount of TiC pre -
cipitates in the built specimens.
3.3. Mechanical properties
Fig. 5 depicts the hardness and tensile properties of FeCrAl alloys. 
Among the analyzed samples, Specimen A demonstrated the highest 
hardness of 315 Hv. Specimens B and C exhibited low hardness values of 
254 and 243 Hv, respectively. The results indicated that reducing the 
carbon content in the feedstock powders considerably decreased the 
hardness of FeCrAl alloys. Furthermore, the UTS and 0.2 % offset yield 
strength ( σ
0.2 %
) of Specimens B and C were slightly lower than those of 
Specimen A. The elongation of Specimens B and C were > 10 times 
higher than that of Specimen A. For specimen A, two specimens were not 
available for tensile test due to severe cracks that occurred in the built 
samples. These results demonstrated that the specimens obtained with a 
lower carbon content exhibited a slightly lower hardness and strength; 
however, their ductility was considerably improved.
Compared with Specimen A ( Fig. 2 ), Specimens B and C ( Figs. 3 and 
4 ) demonstrated lower amounts of smaller TiC precipitates, whereas 
chromium carbide precipitates were not detected at the grain boundary. 
The homogeneous microstructure and the reduction of the content of 
solid solution carbon in Specimens B and C led to a considerably 
enhanced ductility, which prevented the cracking. Solid-state cracks 
preferably propagate through carbides in a transcrystalline manner 
[23] . Therefore, the decrease in the amount and size of carbide in 
Specimens B and C contributes to the reduction in the risk of cracking.
Furthermore, FeCrAl alloys comprise body-centered cubic structures, 
which undergo DBT at a temperature range of 100 ℃ – 350 ℃ [2] . The 
thermal stress due to rapid cooling during the PBF-LB process tends to 
result in DBT-induced SSC. Reducing the carbon content in the alloy 
powders decreases the DBT temperature, which results in the suppres -
sion of cracking [30] .

4. Conclusions
Two new types of the FeCrAl alloy powders were designed to prevent 
cracking and improve mechanical properties and oxidation resistance 
based on its original composition. The microstructure, hardness, tensile 
properties, and high-temperature oxidation resistance of the specimens 
fabricated via PBF-LB were investigated, and following conclusions were 
deduced. 
1) SSC was observed in the alloy obtained using the original composi -
tion with a high carbon content in the feedstock powders. However, 
cracking could be prevented by reducing the carbon content in the 
powder composition.
2) A lower carbon content in the feedstock powders led to the formation 
of lesser amount of small TiC precipitates, which decreased the 
hardness and strength while considerably increasing the ductility of 
the alloy. Silicon and manganese elements exerted minor effects on 
mechanical properties.
3) The alloy obtained using feedstock powders that were composed of a 
low carbon content and were devoid of silicon and manganese ele -
ments exhibited an excellent high-temperature resistance, which was 
superior to that of SUS316L stainless steel and nickel-based alloy 
inconel ® 625.
