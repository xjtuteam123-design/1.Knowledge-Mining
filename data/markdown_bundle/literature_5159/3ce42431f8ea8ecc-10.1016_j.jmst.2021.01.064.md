1. Introduction
Molybdenum (Mo) and its alloys have many exotic proper-
ties such as high melting point, high strength, excellent thermal
conductivity and low thermal expansion coefﬁcient, which are
widely used in metallurgy, machinery, chemical, aerospace and
military industries [1–3]. However, Mo possesses a typical body-
centered cubic structure with less sliding system, hence it is usually
accompanied by brittleness and great deformation processing difﬁ-
culty [1,4,5]. Furthermore, low recrystallization temperature, high
ductile-to-brittle transition temperature and inadequate strength
also limit its applications [6–8].
Many researchers
 focus on the dispersion of second phase parti-
cles and the reﬁnement of Mo grains to solve above problems [9,10].
The second phase particles such as carbides (ZrC, TaC, TiC, etc.) and
rare earth oxides (La2O3, Y2O3, ZrO2, etc.) can reﬁne Mo grains and
strengthen Mo matrix [5,11,12]. Compared with carbides, oxides
can be formed in situ with Mo from precursor salts during pow-

extensive attention. Some relevant works have reported that the
larger oxides at grain boundaries (GBs) are more likely to become
the crack source because of their deformation incongruity with Mo
matrix [5,15,16]. Moreover, according to the strengthening mecha-
nisms in the particle-doped Mo alloys, the oxides within Mo grains
are more effective to enhance material strength than that at GBs
in the ﬁnally Mo alloys [17,18]. Speciﬁcally, the oxides within Mo
grains serve as obstacles of dislocations motion while the ones at
GBs have little contribution to strength via the load-bearing effect
[5,19]. Thus one can see that the ﬁner and more the oxide particles
within Mo grains are, the better the mechanical performance of the
ﬁnal alloys are.
Based on the strengthening mechanisms of second particle-
doped alloys, grain boundary strengthening plays a dominant role
in ODS-Mo alloys [5,19], compared with the second phase strength-
ening. On the one hand, the alloys with more GBs have higher
strength and hardness, and better plasticity and toughness based
on ﬁne grain strengthening [20–23]. On the other hand, the high
impurity (O, N, S, etc.) concentration at GBs mainly leads to the
intergranular fracture of bcc metals such as Mo and W [5,24]. On the
premise of ﬁxed impurity content (except O) of raw materials, more

grain boundaries indicate lower impurity concentration and more
uniform impurity distribution [5,11,25]. Although the Mo grains
are reﬁned rapidly after introducing oxide particles, the Mo grains
and oxide particles still remain large size, which is due to their high
sintering temperature (usually above 1800 ◦C) [6,12,26]. The fur-
ther Mo grain reﬁnement needs to reduce sintering temperature,
which is usually accompanied by low relative density, resulting
in the deterioration of mechanical properties. The ultraﬁne pow-
ders can achieve an eminent balance between high relative density
and ﬁne grains when sintered at relative low temperature [27,28].
Therefore, the synthesis of ultraﬁne powders with high sintering
activity has become a research hotspot in recent years.
The conventional preparation of ODS-Mo powders can be
divided into ¨top-down ¨and ¨bottom-up ¨methods. The typical ¨top-
down ¨method is mechanical alloying, which not only introduces
impurity during ball-milling process, but also involves coarse
Mo grains and large oxide particles at GBs in sintered alloys
[5,6,29]. The ¨bottom-up ¨method consisting of sol-gel [25,30,31],
hydrothermal synthesis [1,4,32]and other methods can control the
size, morphology and purity of powders accurately. However, the
obtained ODS-Mo alloys still face large grains in the range from
several microns to several dozens of microns [4,5,12,29]. Thus it is
urgent to explore a new process to synthesize high quality ultraﬁne
ODS-Mo powders, which is more conducive to obtain alloys with
high relative density and ultraﬁne grains [6,12,33].
Fortunately, freeze-drying as a promising approach could be
used to synthesize ODS-Mo composite powders with high sintering
activity. Its basic principle is that the solutes or precipitates stays at
their initial position when the solution or suspension is pre-freezed,
thus achieving uniform doping [34,35]. During the following freeze-
drying process, solutes or precipitates uniformly precipitate at their
initial position during ice sublimation [14,35]. Freeze drying tech-
nology, which has been applied to prepare WC-Co [36], Co2AlO4
[37,38] and other ultraﬁne powders [39,40], could be a potential
candidate to prepare high quality ultraﬁne ODS-Mo powders.
In this work, the freeze drying technology was innovatively
employed to synthesize ultraﬁne Mo-Y2O3 powders, which were
compared with ball-milled Mo-Y2O3 powders. After low temper-
ature sintering, the obtained Mo-Y2O3 alloys possess ﬁnest Mo
grain size, high nearly full density, small oxide size, high hard-
ness and high compressive strength. Furthermore, the mechanism
of puriﬁcation and strengthening were studied based on Y5Mo2O12
particles.
2. Experimental details
2.1. Powder preparation technology
(NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O and Y(NO3)3·6H2O were chosen as raw
materials. Firstly, (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O (Aladdin, purity = 99.9
%) and a small amount of polymer dispersants including
Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP K12, Aladdin, purity = 99.9 %, 2.5 wt%
of molybdate) and Polyethyleneglycol (PEG 6000, Aladdin, purity =
99.9 %, 2.5 wt% of molybdate) were dissolved into deionized water
meanwhile the ultrasound and mechanical stirring are introduced
to intensify the whole process. Next some Y(NO3)3·6H2O salts were
also added into it. After the solution is clariﬁed, the freeze-drying
process was carried out. The suspension was quickly poured into
liquid nitrogen with ultrasonic treatment. After the freeze-dryer
temperature was reduced and stabilized, the pre-frozen suspension
was quickly put into the freeze-dryer and then lyophilized. Finally,
loose powder precursors were obtained after freeze-drying.
The mixed MoO3-Y2O3 powders were obtained after calcina-
tion of the prepared powder precursors at 500 ◦C in the ﬂowing
air atmosphere. Meanwhile, the residual PVP and PEG are removed
during this process. The composite oxides were afterwards ther-
mally reduced in two stage processes (600 ◦C, 2 h and then 750
◦C, 2 h in the ﬂowing H2 atmosphere), thereby obtaining Mo-Y2O3
composite powders.
Using Y2O3 powders (Aladdin, purity = 99.9 %, 20∼50 nm) and
commercial W powders (Aladdin, purity = 99.9 %, 0.6−1 /H9262m) as
raw materials, the ball milling process for the Mo-Y2O3 composite
powders was carried out for comparison. Its speciﬁc process was at
a speed of 300 r/min to work for 20 h in a planetary ball mill with
an argon protection atmosphere and a mass ratio of 10:1 between
powders and milling balls.
2.2. Low temperature sintering details
All powders were pressed into a mold under 20 MPa pressure
with a diameter of about 25 mm, thus obtaining preformed sam-
ples. Subsequently, the pre-formed powders were sintered in an
intermediate frequency
 induction sintering furnace under pure H2
atmosphere at 800 ◦C for 2 h and then 1600 ◦C for 6 h. In order to
state conveniently, the ball-milled and freeze-dried Mo-Y2O3 alloys
was abbreviated as BM-Mo and FD-Mo, respectively.
2.3. Measurement and analysis
The phase composition of Mo-Y2O3 composite powders were
detected by X-ray diffraction (XRD) D/MAX-2500. The microstruc-
ture and morphology of powders and alloys were examined using
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) Hitachi Model No. S 4800
equipped with energy dispersive spectrometers (EDS) and trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM) JEM-2100. The sizes of Mo grain
and oxides were calculated by Nano Measurer 1.2. The density of
samples was measured using the Archimedes’ principle. The inert
gas pulse infrared thermal conductivity method was employed to
measure oxygen contents. The samples were polished and after-
wards subjected to Vickers hardness test by applying a load of 200
gf for 20 s, so that the corresponding subscript of Vickers hardness
should be 0.2. The 20 different positions on the polished surface
were selected during measurement for repeated testing to get an
average hardness value. The cutted specimens for compression test
have a height of 6 mm and a diameter of 3 mm, and the compres-
sion test at room temperature was conducted on the Instron 5982
testing machine at train rate of 0.001 s−1.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. The freeze-dried Mo-Y2O3 composite nanopowders
The XRD pattern presented in Fig. 1 deﬁnitely indicates that
both ball-milled and freeze-dried Mo-Y2O3 composite powders
only contain Mo phase and are free of any peaks corresponding
to impurity phase. In this respect, the whole reduction process is
adequate. The absence of diffracted peaks of Y2O3 phase is due to
the low addition of Y2O3 (only 1 wt%) and the test accuracy of XRD,
which would be further conﬁrmed in subsequent sintered alloys
[14,16].
Fig. 2 reveals the some representative SEM and TEM images
of both ball-milled and freeze-dried Mo-Y2O3 composite pow-
ders. The ball-milled powders in Fig. 2(a) and (b) show an obvious
agglomeration phenomenon, and it is difﬁcult to distinguish the
clear individual grain proﬁle. Speciﬁcally, the impact and friction
of both balls and jar can lead to the plastic deformation and frac-
ture effect, resulting in grain reﬁnement, which is accompanied
by the formation of large particles with irregular shape and hard
agglomeration [18,24,27,41]. These agglomerated large particles
are difﬁcult to be densiﬁed at relative low sintered temperature

In comparison, the freeze-dried powders have relatively clear
grain proﬁles, as shown in Fig. 2(c) and (d). Their grain size is mainly

size is approximate 85 nm. In relevant literatures, La2O3 doped Mo
powders with the size of 1−10 /H9262m were prepared by mechani-
cal ball milling, which is consistent with the large particle size of
our ball-milled powders [29]. Dong et al. synthesized Mo-La2O3
and Mo-Er2O3 composite powders by sol-gel method, and their
average grain size were 1.23 /H9262m and 227 nm, respectively [3].
Similarly, hydrothermal synthesis method was also employed to
prepare Mo-ZrO2 powders with less than 3 /H9262m [43]. The Mo/ZrO2-
Y2O3 powders having a range of 1−5 /H9262m were prepared by Yao
et al. using solid-liquid doping method [43]. For more grain sizes
of ODS-Mo powders reported in the literatures, please refer to the
second column of Table 1. Thus one can see that the freeze-dried
Mo-Y2O3 composite powders in our work occupy smaller grain size,
which implies they possess high sintering activity.
The reason why the freeze-dried powders keep ﬁne size is worth
further exploring. Firstly, molybdate salt reacts with yttrium salt to
realize a co-deposition process of Mo and Y elements in deionized
water. Actually, both (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O and Y(NO3)3·3H2O can
be dissolved into deionized water respectively, but when doped
together they can react and then achieve co-deposition. Consid-
ering that NH4NO3 is soluble in water, and NO3− is not easy
to precipitate with other cations, Y3+ and Mo7O246- can be co-
deposited to achieve uniform element doping in deionized water.
The ultrasonic, stirring and the addition order of raw materials
in our work are crucial to obtain clear solution, resulting in the
uniform dispersion of co-deposition solutes. A lot of steric hin-
drance provided by polymer dispersants prevents the aggregation
and growth of solutes in deionized water. Simultaneously, the
ultrasonic and continuous stirring contributes to the dispersion
of solutes. Second, the suspension is quickly pre-frozen by liquid
nitrogen, and the solute salts are limited at their original position,
and then precipitated at the original position during subsequent
freeze-drying process, thus obtaining powder precursors with high
dispersion and uniform element doping [34,44]. Finally, the poly-
mer will still decompose and leave a lot of voids after calcination,
which further disperse powders [14,27]. All above factors lead to
the ultraﬁne size of freeze-dried Mo-Y2O3 composite powders.
Normally, the powders are ﬁner where Y2O3 particles are abun-
dant and dispersed, and vice versa [18,45]. Thus the freeze-dried
Mo-Y2O3 composite powders should possess dispersed oxide par-
ticles with small size due to the superiority of co-deposition and
freeze-drying, which was also proved in Fig. 3. It is outstanding that
the Y2O3 particles at Mo GBs still remain small size (about 5 nm).
In addition, ultraﬁne powders with large surface area are usually
accompanied by high oxygen content [16,46]. The oxygen easily
reacts with Mo on the surface of Mo nano powders to form a thin
oxide layer, which is well proved in the same group of W powders
[27,46]. The ball-milled powders with multiple defects and residual
stress are highly active, which inclines them to be oxidized more
easily when exposed to air. Speciﬁcally, the measured oxygen con-
tent of ball-milled and freeze-dried powders is 3.41 wt% and 3.06
wt%, respectively. After removing oxygen from Y2O3 phase, the net
content of oxygen impurities is 3.19 wt% and 2.84 wt%, respectively.
Such high content of oxygen impurities will signiﬁcantly deterio-
rate the mechanical properties of the materials, which should be
paid enough attention to.

3.2. The low-temperature sintered Mo-Y2O3 alloys
With the aim of reducing oxygen impurities, the low empera-
ture sintering under hydrogen atmosphere is selected for Mo-Y2O3
composite powders prepared in our work. The measured oxy-
gen content of sintered BM-Mo and FD-Mo is 0.23 wt% and 0.23
wt% respectively, and their corresponding net content of oxygen
impurities are 0.0175 wt% and 0.0175 wt%, respectively (excluding
oxygen in Y2O3 phase). Actually, the preformed samples have low
relative density of approximate 50 %, thus the hydrogen can pene-
trate into them during subsequent sintering process. Accompanied
by the growth of Mo grains and the increase of relative density,
the content of oxygen impurities decreases sharply. The relative
density of BM-Mo and FD-Mo is measured to be 97.5 % and 99.6 %,
respectively. The high density of structure materials usually implies
potential excellent mechanical properties [2,5,11]. Comparing with
that of BM-Mo, the nearly full density of FD-Mo indicates that the
freeze-dried Mo-Y2O3 composite powders possess higher sintered
activity, thus achieving higher density at low sintering temperature
(1600 ◦C).
Both BSE images and SEM images of sintered alloys are observed,
as shown in Fig. 4. The ball-milled Mo-Y2O3 alloys possess an
average grain size of 2.17 /H9262m and the corresponding grain size
distribution mainly concentrate on 1−5 /H9262m. It can be seen from
Fig. 4(a) that many small black gray particles are distributed on the
white matrix and they are enlarged and represented in SEM image
(see Fig. 4(b)). One can see that these oxide particles (marked by
black solid arrow, conﬁrmed by EDS) distribute at Mo GBs with
an average particle size of 250 nm. In relevant literatures about
ball milling technology, Mo-La2O3 alloys with the size of 3−20 /H9262m
was prepared by Chen et al. and its oxide particles have a range
of 100−700 nm [29]. Cui et al. prepared Mo-La2O3-ZrC alloys via
mechanical ball milling, and the corresponding sizes of Mo grain
and second phase particle without mechanical hot working were
10 /H9262m and 3 /H9262m, respectively [6]. While maintaining small oxide
particles (<100 nm), the Mo-La2O3 alloys possess coarse Mo grains
(15−35 /H9262m) [5]. These reported Mo grain size and second phase
particles of ODS-Mo are similar to that in the sintered BM-Mo in
our work.
Fig. 4(c) and (d) shows the BSE and SEM images of sintered FD-
Mo alloys, respectively. The average Mo grain size of FD-Mo alloys is
only 620 nm and its corresponding grains primarily range from 300
nm to 1 /H9262m. In particular, the freeze-dried FD-Mo alloys possess the
ﬁnest Mo grains compared with the available ODS-Mo literatures
[2,4,6,7,25,29]. Take an example of traditional ¨bottom-up ¨process
for preparing ODS-Mo alloys, hydrothermal synthesis was primar-
ily applied to prepare Mo-ZrO2 alloys with grain sizes ranging from
10 to 60 /H9262m [1,2,26,32] meanwhile sol-gel was mainly employed
to prepare Mo-La2O3/Y2O3 alloys with grain sizes ranging from 3 to
5 /H9262m [25,30,31]. More report on the grain sizes of ODS-Mo alloys
in literatures are concluded in Table 1. In comparison, the freeze-
dried Mo-Y2O3 alloys in our work possess the ﬁnest grain size of
620 nm while they still remain a nearly full density (99.6 %). That is
to say, a prominent balance between the ultraﬁne grains and high
relative density are achieved in our prepared Mo-Y2O3 alloys. It
follows that the synthesis of high quality ultraﬁne ODS-Mo pow-
ders with high sintering activity is crucial to this excellent sintered
result. In addition, we can also see from Fig. 4(c) and (d) that only
few oxide particles (marked by black solid arrow in Fig. 4(d)) with
the size of less than 50 nm distribute at Mo GBs, which is partic-
ularly different from the oxide particles distribution in BM-Mo. In
other words, most oxide particles of FD-Mo are dispersed within
Mo grain interior.
To further exploring the oxide particle within Mo grain interior,
both BM-Mo and FD-Mo are characterized by TEM analysis. For BM-
Mo, in addition to many large particles (see Fig. 5(a) and (b)) at
GBs, few particles (see Fig. 5(c)) are distributed within Mo grains.
Moreover, some of them still remain large size (about 300 nm).
Different from BM-Mo, the oxide particles in FD-Mo alloy main-
tain their nanometer scale (mainly <50 nm, few 50−120 nm) and
almost all of them are embedded within Mo grains. The reason for
the oxide distribution of samples is explored. Although Y2O3 par-
ticles are uniformly distributed during ball milling process, they
are clamped at Mo grain edges [19,24,47]. The oxide particles of
ball-milled powders are easy to agglomerate and fuse into larger
particles at Mo GBs during sintering process [18,27]. Some rele-
vant studies showed that Y2O3 size is positively correlated with
W size, which is also applicable to ODS-Mo [13]. Therefore, coarse

stages, it provides the possibility for the formation of Y5Mo2O12.

compared with small Y5Mo2O12 particles, which is contrary to the
results of Fig. 6. Thus it is more reasonable that the Mo atoms dif-
fuse into small Y2O3 particles (<50 nm) to form Y5Mo2O12 at the
sintering stage. But due to the limited diffusion distance of Mo
into Y2O3 particles, Y5Mo2O12 cannot be formed when the size of
Y2O3 particles is large. Furthermore, the formation of Y5Mo2O12

rities nearby Y2O3 particles at the sintering stage, which can purify
Mo matrix, thus improving the comprehensive properties of FD-

direct evidence for the oxygen afﬁnity of oxides within Mo alloys.

4. Conclusions
(1) The novel freeze-drying technology was employed to syn-
thesize ultraﬁne Mo-Y2O3 powders, and the corresponding
average grain size is only 54 nm.
(2) Comparing with available literature about ODS-Mo, the low-
temperature sintered Mo-Y2O3 alloys in our work have the
ﬁnest grain size of 620 nm while maintaining a high density
of 99.6 %.
(3) The oxides remain their small size (mainly <50 nm) within
Mo grains and especially at Mo GBs. When Y2O3 particles are
reﬁned to a certain extent (about < 50 nm), they can react with
Mo matrix to form Y5Mo2O12 particles by absorbing nearby
oxygen impurities, resulting in puriﬁcation and strengthening
of Mo matrix.
(4) The freeze-dried Mo-Y2O3 alloys possess excellent mechanical
properties which mainly involve a high hardness of 487 ± 28
HV0.2, a high yield strength of 902 MPa, a high compressive
strength of 1110 MPa.
