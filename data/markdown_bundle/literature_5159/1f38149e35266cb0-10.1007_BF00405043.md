Correlation between modulus and yield strength in 
Ti-25AI-10Nb-3V-1Mo (a/o) 
D. E. GORDON, S. Y. CHUANG 
Lockheed, Fort Worth Company, Fort Worth, Texas, USA 
In recent years, considerable effort has been made 
to develop advanced titanium aluminide alloys based 
on Ti3A1, referred to as 0~2 (DO19 structure), for 
aircraft turbine engine components and other 
high-temperature applications. These aluminides 
have superior creep strength to much higher tem- 
peratures than do conventional titanium alloys and 
have potential as a low density alternative to Ni-base 
superalloys. Concerns over low temperature ductil- 
ity [1, 2], microstructural stability [3], interstitial 
embrittlement [4], and hot salt stress corrosion 
cracking [5] have limited the application of these 
alloys, however. 
The Ti3Al-base alloys developed to date are 
two-phase systems with compositions based on 
Ti-(23 to 25)A1-(10 to 18)Nb-X(V, Mo, Cr, or 
Ta), where X ranges from nil to a few per cent [6]. 
Over time, various/3-stabilizing additions have been 
added to the oL 2 phase in an effort to improve 
mechanical properties; past efforts have concen- 
trated on room temperature ductility in particular 
[7, 8]. Like conventional oL +/3 Ti alloys, two-phase 
titanium aluminides can be heat treated to a variety 
of microstructures. As reviewed in recent articles 
[6, 9], microstructures in 0L2 alloys include equiaxed 
0~2 + '/3', basketweave, and Widmanst~itten micro- 
structures either with or without primary o~2. The 
second, '/3' phase can be the bcc/3 phase, [6, 10-11], 
the ordered B2 phase [10, 12], or the ordered 
orthorhombic phase [13], depending on alloy sys- 
tems and heat treatments. The metastable c0-type 
phase [6, 10, 14] can also be formed in some alloys 
after quenching. 
The titanium aluminide alloy used for the current 
ultrasonic velocity measurements was of nominal 
composition Ti-25Al-10Nb-3V-1Mo (a/o), re- 
ferred to as Super-oc2. All material was made by 
ingot metallurgy and cross-rolled to sheet thicknes- 
ses ranging from 1.5 mm to 3.2 mm. Five different 
heats of material were produced. All of the sheet 
fabrication steps were conducted by TIMET at their 
Toronto, Ohio facility. A variety of different proces- 
sing variables were used in fabrication of the sheet 
material. All processing was conducted in the 0L2 +/3 
phase field. A typical microstructure of the as- 
received material is shown in Fig. la. As shown, the 
cv2 +/3 process produces elongated primary o{ 2 in a 
transformed /3 matrix. A considerable variation in 
'/3' grain size was observed in the different as- 
received batches. In general, lower processing tem- 
peratures produced finer '/3' grain sizes. After 
elevated temperature exposures of 954 °C/4 h, the

Modulus values for the Ti-25Al-10Nb-3V-1Mo 
(a/o) sheet were determined according to ultrasonic 
techniques. These values were compared to modulus 
values determined from mechanical tensile test 
results. For the ultrasonic tests, both longitudinal 
velocity (VL) and shear velocity (Vs) were 
measured. The ultrasonic velocities, longitudinal 
(VL) and shear (Vs), are functions of shear modulus 
(/0, Lam6 constant (A), and density (p) of the 
medium. Once the longitudinal and shear velocities 
are measured, various modulus can be determined 
using the following equations: 
Shear modulus: ~ = pV 2 (1)

The ultrasonic velocities were measured by using the 
pulse-echo technique with a longitudinal and a shear 
wave transducer and an ultrasonic pulser-receiver 
unit. A digital oscilloscope was used to measure the 
time-of-flight between the echoes of ultrasonic 
waves at the front and back surface of the specimen. 
The velocities were calculated from the time-of- 
flight data and the thickness of the specimens. Those 
velocities were then used to obtain modulus values 
of the specimens from the above equations. The 
velocity and modulus data for the specimens investi- 
gated are summarized in Table I. 
Comparisons were made for modulus values 
obtained from ultrasonic velocity measurements 
with 0.2% yield strengths in the same alloy. These 
results are plotted in Fig. 2. These results indicate 
that for o~2 plus '/3' microstructures, modulus values 
were directly proportional to yield strength, increas- 
ing yield strength corresponding to increasing mod- 
ulus. The correlation was better for heat treatments 
where no orthorhombic phase was observed (Fig. 2).

In the Ti-25Al-10Nb-3V-Mo (a/o) alloy, no 
correlation is seen between modulus and relative 
proportion of '/3' and oc 2 phases. For instance, 
samples with the lowest moduli (89.1 and 89.7 GPa, 
respectively) have quite different microstructures. 
The coupon with the lowest modulus (89.1 GPa) 
contained a relatively small portion of the '/3' phase 
and had relatively low ductility. On the other hand, 
the coupon with a similar modulus (89.7 GPa) had 
fairly high ductility and a large percentage of '/3' 
phase (Table I). Similarly, the specimens showing 
the highest modulus values showed large variations 
in microstructure. 
In conclusion, the main findings of this study on 
the assessment of modulus in Ti-25Al-10Nb-3V- 
1Mo using ultrasonic velocity techniques can be 
summarized as follows: For oL2 plus '/3' microstruc- 
tures, modulus values were directly proportional to 
yield strength: increasing yield strength cor- 
responded to increasing modulus. The correlation is 
better for heat treatments where no orthorhombic 
phase is observed. The ultrasonic parameter which is 
most sensitive to changes in yield strength is the 
shear velocity (Vs). Unlike conventional titanium 
alloys, where it has been demonstrated that the /3 
phase has an intrinsically lower modulus than the o~ 
phase, no such correlation was observed between o~2 
and '/3' in Ti-25Al-10Nb-3V-1Mo. The '/3' phase 
existing as the bcc/3 phase, the ordered B2 phase, or 
the ordered orthorhombic O phase, depending on 
heat treatment, is expected to add to the complexity 
of showing any such type of correlation.
