ABSTRACT
High temperature mechanical properties of diffusion welded Alloy 800H was investigated to fabricate a printed 
circuit heat exchanger (PCHE) for high temperature reactor (HTR) systems. Surface treatment was performed on 
Alloy 800H to transform the solubility product ( K
sp
) to exceed the reaction quotient ( Q ). The surface treatment 
facilitated the grain boundary migration across the interface. The yield strengths exceeded the values described 
in ASME Section III Division 5 Table HBB-I-14.5 up to 760 
◦
C, while the tensile strengths were comparable to 
Table HBB-3225-1 up to 700 
◦
C. At 760 
◦
C, the tensile strength was ~30 MPa lower than the code. The stress-to- 
rupture values exceeded the expected minimum stress-to-rupture values of Alloy 800H described in Table HBB-I- 
14.6C. The ductility of the diffusion weldment acquired from the tensile test was comparable to the as-received 
Alloy 800H. Meanwhile, the formation of the secondary precipitates on the interface during the stress-to-rupture 
test deteriorated the ductility of the diffusion weldment, which induced intergranular fracture.
1. Introduction
High temperature reactor (HTR) systems, such as the very high 
temperature reactor (VHTR) and the molten salt reactor (MSR), have 
been investigated for efficient electricity generation and massive 
hydrogen production using the process heat [ 1 – 7 ]. The outlet temper -
ature of the VHTR is estimated to be the maximum 950 
◦
C, and the heat 
is delivered through the heat exchanger. Therefore, integrity at high 
temperatures is essential for the heat exchanger, and compactness is also 
required to secure a better economy. Hence, printed circuit heat ex -
changers (PCHEs) have been considered a leading candidate for the HTR 
heat exchanger due to their integrity in a wide range of operating con -
ditions and compactness.
Solid-solution strengthened Ni-based superalloys are considered as a 
candidate material due to their excellent high temperature mechanical 
properties and hot corrosion resistance [ 4 – 6 ]. Among the candidate 
materials, Alloy 800H has been deemed a near-term structural material 
for fabricating the steam generator or the intermediate heat exchanger. 
However, the feasibility of the diffusion weldment in the reactor systems 
needs to be validated to conform to the American Society of Mechanical 
Engineers (ASME) Boiler and Pressure Vessel (BPV) Code.
The current guidelines for diffusion welding in the manufacturing of 
PCHEs are outlined in ASME Section IX QW-185. It explains how the 
mechanical properties in the parallel- and through-bond need to comply 
with the original material specifications, and the microstructure needs 
to be free from the discontinuities specified in the code. Meanwhile, no 
standard for diffusion welding has yet to be established in ASME Section 
III Division 5 for High Temperature Reactors. Therefore, the data for 
determining the reduction factor of the diffusion weldment needs to be 
collected in advance for preparing such a standard.
Previous researchers have strived to fabricate a high quality diffusion 
weldment with the candidate materials proposed in the research [ 8 – 18 ]. 
However, the mechanical properties were reported to be deteriorated by 
secondary precipitates, such as Al-rich oxides or Ti-rich carbides, along 
the interface [ 8 – 12 ]. Hong et al. fabricated diffusion weld Alloy 800H at 
the nominal conditions (diffusion welding: 1150 
◦
C/10 MPa/1 h). The 
relative tensile strength and the elongation compared to the as-received 
Alloy 800H were drastically decreased at 650 
◦
C, and the relative tensile 
strength and elongation were ~68 % and ~14 %, respectively [ 9 ]. In 
order to recover the ductility, post-weld heat treatment was imple -
mented [ 8 , 9 , 11 , 15 ]. However, it was difficult to dissolve Al-rich oxides 
and Ti-rich carbides into the matrix. Transient liquid phase (TLP) 
bonding was considered a possible solution because the rejection of the 
solute did not occur before isothermal solidification was finished 
[ 16 – 20 ]. However, brittle phases were formed by melting point de -
pressants, such as B, P, and Si, and these brittle phases induced a drastic 
loss of ductility.

2. Experimental method
2.1. Materials
Table 1 shows the chemical compositions of Alloy 800H. Plates from 
ATI Co. (Heat#: 735400)) were used. The material is classified in ASME 
Section III Division 5; the maximum operating temperature is 760 
◦
C, 
and the permitted design life is up to 300,000 h. It has operating 
experience as a tube material for the steam generator [ 26 – 30 ]. The high 
nickel concentration in the alloy achieves good stress corrosion cracking 
resistance compared to the other austenitic stainless steels.
2.2. Diffusion welding
Table 2 shows the diffusion welding conditions of the experiments. 
Two blocks of diffusion weldment were fabricated for the evaluation. 
Fig. 1 shows the schematics of the diffusion welding processes: 1) A Ni 
increased zone at/near the surface was formed to induce K
sp 
> Q; 2) The 
uniaxial compression at high temperature achieved a monolithic joint; 
and 3) Post-weld heat treatment without compressive stress homoge -
nized the chemical compositions. The details of the surface treatment is 
described in our previous research [ 22 ]. The diffusion welding tem -
perature was below the solid solution annealing temperature. We 
considered the uniaxial pressure to avoid excessive deformation, and the 
deformation along the compressive axis was under 1 %.
2.3. Evaluation methods
We performed a microstructural analysis at/near the interface using 
electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) and scanning electron micro -
scopy (SEM). For the EBSD analysis, the specimen was mechanically 
polished down to 1 μ m and finished with ion milling. The coupon for the 
SEM microscopy was also mechanically polished down to 1 μ m and 
dipped in aqua regia for ~1 min to reveal the microstructure at/near the 
interface. Electron dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) equipped with SEM 
was used to analyze the distribution of the chemical compositions and 
reveal the secondary precipitates.
Cylindrical bar-type specimens with a gauge diameter of 6.0 mm and 
a gauge length of 30.0 mm were used for the mechanical testing, which 
followed the dimensions specified in ASTM: E8 /E8M 21 for the high 
temperature mechanical test. The tensile and stress-to-rupture tests 
complied with the ASTM: E8 /E8M 21 and E21-20. The loading di -
rection was perpendicular to the diffusion welding interface.
An MTS-810 universal material testing machine was applied for 
tensile and strain rate sensitivity tests. The tensile tests were performed 
with a nominal strain rate of 5.55 × 10

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure
Fig. 2 (a) shows a low magnification image of the diffusion welding 
interface characterized with the planar grain boundary. However, the 
high magnification image shows that the grain boundary migrated 
across the interface and a ~30 μ m carbides-free zone at/near the 
interface ( Fig. 2 (b) ). The line profile shows that the thickness of the Ni 
increased zone was ~150 μ m ( Fig. 2 (c) ). The Ni increased zone 
remained due to insufficient surface treatment. The carbide-free zone 
was reported in previous research on diffusion welding of a Ni-based 
alloy with an interlayer containing high Nickel concentration [ 13 – 20 ]. 
The diffused Ni from the interlayer induced K
sp 
> Q , and the formation 
of the carbide-free zone was natural.
Meanwhile, even though the observation of blocky type Ti-rich 
carbides has been reported elsewhere [ 13 , 14 ], we did not expect the 
formation of fine secondary precipitates beside the carbide-free zone 
( Fig. 2 (a) ). EDS results near the interface revealed that a ~3 μ m 
blocky-shaped secondary precipitate was Mg-rich oxide covered with 
Ti-rich carbide ( Fig. 3 (a) ), and ~0.8 μ m fine secondary precipitates 
were Ti-rich carbides ( Fig. 3 (b) ). Considering the diffusion welding 
processes explained the unexpected precipitation of ~0.8 μ m sized 
Ti-rich carbides near the interface.

However, grain coarsening during the diffusion welding process 
degraded the mechanical properties of the weldment compared to the 
as-received material. Since the diffusion welding processes were con -
ducted near the solid-solution heat treatment temperature, grain 
coarsening and the associated mechanical degradation were inevitable. 
Meanwhile, we observed that the planar grain boundary at the interface 
led to a loss of creep ductility. Materials with larger grain sizes are less 
favorable for grain boundary migration across the interface because the 
driving force for grain boundary migration is inversely related to grain 
size. Therefore, further research will be conducted not only to determine 
the optimal diffusion welding conditions (temperature, pressure, time), 
but also to understand the effect of initial grain size on grain boundary 
migration behaviors and its impact on mechanical properties.
