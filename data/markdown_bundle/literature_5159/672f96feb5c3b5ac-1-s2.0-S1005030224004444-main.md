Niobium (Nb) is sensitive to even minute quantities of silicon (Si) solutes, which are known to induce 
pronounced hardening. However, the underlying mechanism for hardening remains elusive since the ef- 
fect of Si solutes on dislocation behavior is unclear. Here, using tensile testing, in-situ microscopy and 
nanomechanical testing, the behavior of dislocations in dilute Nb-Si alloys, containing from 0 at.% to 0.8 
at.% Si, is investigated. We show that the hardness, strength and strain hardening rate increase from two 
to four times, while the uniform elongation in tension only reduces 50 % as the Si content increases. 
Dislocations evolve from complex entangled patterns in Nb to parallel long-straight screw dislocation- 
dominated structures in Nb-Si alloys. In-situ indentation reveals that the origins of the marked harden- 
ing in Nb-Si alloy are the reduction of dislocation mobility and cross-slip propensity. Large densities of 
dislocation debris-superjogs and loops introduced throughout the sample during warm rolling and an- 
nealing are found to provide active internal dislocation sources, which explain the minimal ductility loss 
seen in these Nb-Si alloys. These ﬁndings can help guide the alloy design of high-performance refractory 
materials for extreme temperature applications.

1. Introduction 
Niobium (Nb)-based alloys have high melting points, are rela- 
tively lightweight refractories, and possess a combination of high 
temperature strength and creep-resistance [1–7] . Nevertheless, 
their insuﬃcient strength and toughness at room temperature and 
severe oxidation at elevated temperatures [5] pose a bottleneck 
in their development and application as Nb-based superalloys [6] . 
Among the various methods attempted on Nb-based superalloys, 
alloying emerges as a popular and effective means for strength- 
ening and improving oxidation resistance [ 5 , 6 ]. As one particularly 
light-weight candidate, Nb-Si alloys have received widespread at- 
tention because of their high melting point ( > 1750 °C), relatively 
low density (6.6–7.2 g/cm3 ), oxidation resistance and excellent me- 
chanical properties [8–15] . Alloying with Si solutes enhances the 
oxidation resistance via formation of SiO2 to protect the metal 
from oxidation at elevated temperature [11–15] . At the same time,

glide resistance for dislocations over that of the pure metal, leading 
to enhanced strength and creep-resistance [11] . Further, it has been 
observed that Si solutes double the hardness of pure Nb compared 
to that of Ti solutes [16] and even minor Si ( < 1.0 at.%) additions, 
without formation of precipitates, can improve the strength of Nb 
alloys [17] . The origin of the unusually strong Si solute-induced 
hardening or strengthening in Nb is, however, unclear. 
Alloying in body-centered cubic (BCC) solutions can induce ei- 
ther solid-solution strengthening or softening [18–29] . In BCC met- 
als, solid-solution softening usually occurs at low temperatures 
while solid-solution hardening at elevated temperatures [24] . Plas- 
tic deformation of BCC metals is thermally activated, and the ﬂow 
stress is composed of a thermal component and an athermal com- 
ponent [25] . Solid-solution softening due to alloying decreases the 
thermal component, while solid-solution hardening increases the 
athermal component [25] . For BCC metals, plasticity is controlled 
by screw dislocations, whose glide relies on the thermal-activated 
nucleation and migration of kink pairs [18–29] . Solid-solution soft- 
ening would occur if alloying increased the rate of formation of

kink pairs, while solid-solution hardening is due to the direct inter- 
action between dislocations and solute atoms [25] . Atomistic sim- 
ulations suggest that solute hardening in Nb-Si alloys is associ- 
ated with Si additions causing dislocation glide on the higher order 
{112} plane [29] and increasing the resistance against dislocation 
glide on the {110} plane [30] . For other solutes, such as oxygen, 
for instance, the solute hardening mechanism in Nb can be quite 
complex. A three-body interaction among the oxygen solute, a va- 
cancy, and a screw dislocation has been discovered to play a criti- 
cal role [ 20 , 31 ]. Speciﬁcally, the repulsive interactions between the 
dispersive oxygen solutes and screw dislocations facilitate cross- 
kink formation and copious vacancy emission during screw dis- 
location glide [31] . As another example, the high solute harden- 
ing seen in oxygen-charged Nb and V was attributed to vacancies 
and oxygen solutes that further cluster into oxygen-vacancy com- 
plexes and trap screw dislocations [ 31 , 32 ]. In many of these cases, 
the mechanisms of solid solution hardening in Nb have been based 
largely on atomistic simulations and macroscopic experiments. Yet, 
the dynamic behavior of dislocations is key to understanding so- 
lute softening and hardening in BCC metals [33–36] and to date, 
such observations are lacking and still challenging to obtain. 
In this work, we studied the mechanism of Si solutes hardening 
in Nb-Si alloys. By adding 0–0.8 at.% of Si in Nb, the yield strength, 
strain-hardening rate and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) are si- 
multaneously enhanced without the catastrophic losses in uniform 
elongation that usually accompany alloy strengthening. To unveil 
the strengthening and ductilization mechanisms associated with 
the Si solutes, we employed both macro- and micro-scale exper- 
imental techniques, and focused on the dynamic behavior of dis- 
locations in Nb-Si alloys compared to pure Nb. The analyses re- 
veal that Si solutes reduce dislocation mobility, suppress cross-slip 
propensity, and promote the formation of planar dislocation struc- 
tures, which are the origins of the marked solute hardening. Fur- 
thermore, we posit and provide evidence that suggests the high 
density of dislocation debris-superjogs or loops introduced during 
the warm rolling step involved in sample fabrication, providing a 
homogeneous distribution of dislocation sources, which contribute 
to the ductilization of Nb-Si alloys. 
2. Experimental and simulation methods 
2.1. Preparation of Nb-Si alloys 
Nb-Si alloys with nominal compositions of Nb- x Si ( x = 0.2, 
0.5, 0.8 at.%) were melted using high-purity Si and pure Nb 
(99.999 wt.%) as raw materials. Homogeneous alloy button ingots 
were fabricated by vacuum arc melting with water cooling. The 
weight of each ingot button is 1500 g. In addition to Si solutes, 
there were some trace elements of Al ( < 0.18 at.%) in the three 
Nb-Si ingot. 
Samples for microstructural observation, mechanical test and 
in-situ compression were cut from the center of the Nb-Si alloy 
ingots by electro-discharge machining. Warm rolling at 350 °C fol- 
lowed by a heat treatment was carried out to reﬁne the grain size 
and reduce the initial dislocation density. Heat treatments of Nb- 
0.2Si, Nb-0.5Si and Nb-0.8Si were carried out in a VTL 1700 tube 
furnace, evacuated to a pressure of ∼2 × 10−3 Pa, and then heated 
to 130 0, 150 0, and 150 0 °C for 6, 8, and 8 h, respectively. The sam- 
ples were covered by a pure Nb plate during the heat treatment to 
reduce oxygen charging. The Nb plate was of high purity, had di- 
mensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 2 mm, and had been annealed at 
10 0 0 °C for 3 h in a GSL-1400X tube furnace, followed by furnace 
cooling. The ﬁnal average grain size is about 60 μm for pure Nb 
and 200 μm for Nb-Si alloys. The processing history of pure Nb 
and Nb-Si alloy samples is listed in Table 1 . 
2.2. Microstructural characterization and mechanical tests 
Microstructures of the as-annealed Nb and Nb-Si samples were 
measured using electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) on an FEI 
Helios NanoLab. EBSD samples were prepared using electropolish- 
ing via a solution of 6 % sulfuric acid + 94 % methanol at a voltage 
of 10 V at −35 °C. The dislocation structures were characterized 
using a JEOL 2100F transmission electron microscope (TEM) oper- 
ating at 200 kV. TEM thin foils were prepared ﬁrst by etching using 
a solution of hydroﬂuoric acid (HF):sulfuric acid (H2 SO4 ):nitric acid 
(HNO3 ) = 5:2:2 down to 100 μm. They were then punched into 
disks with a diameter of 3 mm, and further thinned using twin-jet 
electropolishing in a solution of 12.5 % H2 SO4 + 87.5 % CH3 OH at 
a direct voltage of 40 V at −35 °C. 
Micro-Vickers hardness tests were performed on polished pure 
Nb and Nb-Si alloys under a load of 100 gf and holding for 15 s. 
More than 10 tests were repeated for each sample. As-annealed 
Nb and Nb-Si plates were cut into dog-bone specimens with the 
dimensions of 60 mm (length) × 10 mm (width) × 2 mm (thick- 
ness) via electrical discharge machining, then cleaned with ethyl 
alcohol in an ultrasonic cleaner. The gauge sections were ground 
by 80#, 240#, 800# and 2000# SiC abrasive paper in sequence. 
Room-temperature quasi-static bulk tensile tests were conducted 
at a strain rate of 6 × 10−4 s−1 on a SANS CMT4104 universal test- 
ing machine with an extensometer. At least three samples were re- 
peated for each composition. After tensile testing, an SU6600 scan- 
ning electron microscope (SEM) was used to characterize the frac- 
ture surface. In-situ indentation experiments were performed us- 
ing a PicoFemto single-tilt electrical tip holder in a strain-control 
mode with a ﬁxed tip displacement of 6 nm/s (keep a similar 
strain rate) inside a JEOL 2100F TEM. During in-situ mechanical 
loading, real-time videos were recorded by a Gatan 833 charge- 
coupled device camera with a time resolution of 10 frames/s. 
2.3. Molecular dynamic simulations 
Molecular dynamics simulations were performed to analyze 
the formation dislocation debris-superjogs and loops. The molecu- 
lar dynamics simulations utilized the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular 
Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) [37] , combined with a neu- 
ral network potential (NNP) developed using the Atomic Energy 
Network (ænet) package [38] . We employed a single crystal model 
of BCC Nb supplemented with Si, wherein Si atoms were randomly 
substituted for Nb sites up to the desired composition of 0.5 at.%. 
The supercell dimensions were 411.9 ˚A × 188.1 ˚A × 108.6 ˚A along 
[111], [11¯2 ] , and [¯1 10 ] directions, respectively. The screw disloca- 
tion spanned a length corresponding to 142 b ( b : Burgers vector 
length). The boundary conditions included a free surface along the 
[11¯2 ] direction, with periodic boundaries in the dislocation glide 
[¯1 10 ] and Burgers vector [111] directions. For the loading condi- 
tions, a constant strain rate of 1.13 × 108 s−1 was imposed. This 
was achieved by anchoring the bottom 10 ˚A atomic layers and 
driving the upper 10 ˚A layers at a steady velocity in the [111] di- 
rection. The central slab’s temperature, situated between the ﬁxed 
and loading layers, was maintained at a consistent 773 K (500 °C) 
using the Nosé-Hoover thermostat. This temperature was selected 
as an exemplar temperature that also served to accelerate the MD 
computation.

3.2. Si solute-regulated mechanical behavior 
The effect of Si on the mechanical properties is examined via 
hardness and tensile testing. The Vickers hardness of the pure Nb 
and Nb-Si alloys is shown in Fig. 3(a) . Pure Nb has an average 
hardness of 83 HV. After alloying with 0.2 at.% Si, the hardness in- 
creases to 134 HV, which is 1.6 times that of pure Nb. With more 
Si alloying from 0.2 at.% to 0.8 at.%; however, the hardness only 
increases slightly, reaching 159 HV for Nb-0.8Si, since the solution 
limit has already been exceeded and solution hardening exhausted 
at lower at.% Si. Typical tensile engineering stress-strain curves of 
the pure Nb and Nb-Si alloys are shown in Fig. 3(b) . Pure Nb has 
a yield stress of 94 MPa and a total elongation of 60 %, indicat- 
ing low strength and high ductility. With increasing Si content, the 
tensile yield stress increases to 252, 367 and 382 MPa for Nb-0.2Si, 
Nb-0.5Si and Nb-0.8Si, respectively. Even the strongest Nb-0.8Si al- 
loy studied still has a high total elongation of ∼24 %. The ulti- 
mate tensile stress is also enhanced from 224 MPa for pure Nb 
to 513 MPa for the Nb-0.8Si. The tensile true stress–true strain and 
work hardening rate ( /Theta1) curves are plotted in Fig. 3(c) . As shown 
in the inset, Nb-Si exhibits a much higher strain hardening rate 
compared with that of pure Nb. Fig. 3 (d–f) compares the tensile 
data and hardness results. The yield stress and UTS increase with 
Si concentration much like the increases seen in hardness. Notably, 
with increasing Si content the uniform elongation only gradually 
reduces, while the toughness in fact increases at ﬁrst and subse- 
quently decreases ( Fig. 3(f) ). Remarkably, by adding Si solutes, Nb- 
Si alloys show marked strengthening and increases in strain hard- 
ening rate, while only presenting a moderate reduction in uniform 
elongation and toughness.

Nb-0.2Si, Nb-0.5Si and Nb-0.8Si alloys.

To understand the formation mechanism of dislocation debris 
due to dislocation motion in the warm rolling and annealing pro- 
cess depicted in Figs. 5 and 6 , we conducted molecular dynam- 
ics simulations to analyze the dislocation behaviors at 500 °C in 
Nb-Si alloy. As shown in Fig. 7 (a–c), there are only single vacancy 
and interstitial formation within the same simulation timeframe 
in the pure Nb model. Notably, as shown in Fig. 7 (d–f), the glid- 
ing of screw dislocation results in cross-slip, leading to the cre- 
ation of a cross-kink. This ultimately produces a strip-shaped dis- 
location loop, aligning with the observed dislocation debris. When 
the screw dislocation reconnects soon after cross-kink formation, 
a point defect pair consisting of interstitial Nb/Si and a vacancy 
forms adjacent to the Si atom, according to MD simulations. Con- 
versely, if the reconnection of dislocation is protracted, the screw 
dislocation yields a superjog, which takes the form of the strip- 
shaped dislocation loop along the {110} plane. The resultant va- 
cancy and dislocation loop remain intact, not merging with the 
produced interstitial, since the latter can diffuse and be ﬁrmly an- 
chored by the Si atom (with a Si-Interstitial interaction energy of 
−2.1 eV). The simulations conﬁrm that the dislocation debris in 
Figs. 5 and 6 are superjogs and dislocation loops due to the inter- 
action between screw dislocation and Si solutes. The key features 
captured in MD simulations are comparable to the experimental 
observations in Figs. 5 and 6 . 
To uncover the mechanism of Si solute-induced strengthening 
and strain hardening, dislocation structures were characterized in 
pure Nb and Nb-Si alloys after tensile fracture. Fig. 8 (a–d) shows 
images of representative structures, all taken under [110] two- 
beam conditions, as labeled in the top part of each image. In pure 
Nb high densities of dislocations with entangled features were 
observed, which results from interactions between moving dislo- 
cations, cross-slip, and dislocation reactions. The accumulation of 
dislocations and their interactions are known to be the primary 
strain-hardening mechanism in pure Nb. The dislocation structure 
morphologies in the Nb-Si alloys after tensile fracture are com- 
pletely different, as shown in Fig. 8 (b–d). Dislocations are orga- 
nized into numerous long, straight, and parallel screw-character 
dislocation lines periodically arranged on the {110} planes. With 
further tilling the sample, we conﬁrmed that the line defects in 
Fig. 8(c, d) are long straight screw dislocations on {110} plane. In 
Nb-0.2Si, two intersecting directions of screw dislocations are ob- 
served. With increasing Si content, the screw dislocation lines be- 
come straighter, even more aligned, and more concentrated in one 
direction. Fig. 8 (c, d) shows screw dislocation lines in Nb-0.5Si and 
Nb-0.8Si aligned along one main direction. While both the pure Nb 
and Nb-Si alloy samples deformed in tension developed high dis- 
location densities, the Nb-Si alloys developed numerous short dis- 
location segments and tiny dislocation loops. This dislocation mor- 
phology implies a strong interaction between Si solutes and dislo- 
cations during straining. The high density of superjogs and loops 
initially observed in the as-annealed Nb-Si alloys have disappeared 
after tensile deformation, which are likely consumed as internal 
dislocation sources (see discussion section). Clearly, Si solutes have 
a marked effect on deformation dislocation structures.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Interstitial Si solutes and their strengthening effect 
According to the Nb-Si binary phase diagram, Si only has neg- 
ligible solubility in Nb at room temperature [ 1 , 2 ]. Therefore, Si so- 
lutes (with an atomic radius of 0.111 nm) are expected to precipi- 
tate rather than form a solid solution in Nb (with an atomic radius 
of 0.147 nm). However, as done here, it is possible to fabricate a 
solid solution Nb-Si alloy when the Si content is lower than 1.0 
at.% (without too much precipitation), by severe chilling methods, 
such as arc melting with water cooling [17] . According to Bragg’s 
equation, the left-shift of XRD diffraction peaks in Fig. 2 indicates 
that a slight lattice expansion accompanies alloying with Si so- 
lutes. Lattice expansion signiﬁes an interstitial solid solution has 
formed rather than a substitution solid solution (lattice contrac- 
tion). This could partly explain the tremendous solute hardening 
observed here in the Nb-Si alloy. Further, Si solutes likely occupy 
octahedral sites as interstitial atoms in the host BCC Nb because of 
their relatively small atomic radius difference [17] . 
4.2. Effect of Si solutes on the dislocation mobility and strengthening 
For Nb-Si alloys, notable simultaneous enhancements in yield 
stress, strain hardening rate and UTS are observed ( Fig. 3 ). In gen- 
eral, strain hardening in metals is related to dynamic accumulation 
of dislocations and the interactions between moving and trapped 
dislocations. Despite the observed differences in strain hardening, 
no signiﬁcant differences in the total stored dislocation density 
were observed among the pure Nb, Nb-0.2Si, Nb-0.5Si and Nb-0.8Si 
after tensile rupture (see Fig. 8 (a–d)). In contrast, the dislocation 
structure patterns were undeniably distinct. In pure Nb, they were 
tangled, which usually signiﬁes frequent dislocation interactions 
and frequent cross-slip events. This would explain why pure Nb 
achieves the lowest strain hardening rate (see Fig. 3(c) ). The dislo- 
cation structures in the Nb-0.8Si alloy, however, comprise mostly 
long and straight parallel screw dislocations (see Figs. 8 and 10 ). 
This conﬁguration would result from infrequent cross slip events 
and correspond to the highest strain hardening rate ( Fig. 3(c) ). 
Therefore, the strain hardening ability in Nb-Si alloys compared to 
pure Nb is a consequence of their differences in the dynamic char- 
acteristics of the dislocations [ 31 , 32 ].

5. Conclusions 
In this study, the inﬂuence of Si solute additions on the me- 
chanical properties and dislocation behavior of Nb-Si alloys is in- 
vestigated. The main ﬁndings are: 
(1) With increasing Si content from 0 at.% to 0.8 at.%, the hardness, 
strength and strain hardening rate increase two to four times, 
while the uniform elongation only reduces a 50 %. This rela- 
tively small reduction in the strain to failure is not normal for 
solute hardening. 
(2) After testing, the stored intragranular dislocation structures 
change from complex, entangled dislocation patterns in pure 
Nb to long, parallel, and straight screw-dominate arrays in the 
Nb-Si alloys. We propose that these differences arise because 
the Si solute lowered the propensity for cross-slip. 
(3) During in-situ indentation, the edge dislocation velocity de- 
creases proportionally with increasing Si content, whereas the 
screw dislocation velocity abruptly decreases and reaches a 
plateau. We rationalize that the reductions in the mobility 
of both types of dislocations and in the cross-slip propensity 
of the screw dislocations are the origins of the remarkable 
strengthening and strain hardening in Nb-Si alloy. 
(4) The pre-exiting high density of superjogs and loops from the 
rolled and heat-treated material provides internal sources for 
dislocations. Easy activation of numerous dislocation sources 
enabled ductile deformation of the Nb-Si alloys. The segrega- 
tion of Al on superjogs and loops stabilizes these structures, 
thus enhancing the content of Al could serve as a way to in- 
troduce more superjogs and loops in Nb alloys.
