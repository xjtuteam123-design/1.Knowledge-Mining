Additive manufacturing of aluminum alloys has received signiﬁcant attention in the aerospace indus- 
try; however, achieving suﬃcient high strength, especially at elevated temperatures, remains challenging. 
Here, a crack-free and near-full dense Al-1Fe-0.6Cu-1.3Zr alloy was fabricated by the laser powder bed 
fusion (LPBF) technique. The Al-Fe-Cu-Zr alloy exhibits heterogeneous microstructures with two distinct 
zones. One is the so-called coarse-grain zones (CGZs) with an average grain size of 0.95 μm, where (Al, 
Cu)Fe 3 nanoparticles precipitate in the Al matrix and Fe and Cu cosegregate at the grain boundaries (GBs). 
The other is ﬁne-grain zones (FGZs) with an average grain size of 0.45 μm, where an Al 3 Zr nanoparti- 
cle precipitates in each of the α-Al grains (serves as the nuclei), and Fe-rich nanoprecipitates and Fe/Cu 
cosegregation appear at the GBs. As a result, the LPBF Al-Fe-Cu-Zr alloy, with these unique heteroge- 
neous structures, displays high strength at both room temperature and elevated temperatures, e.g., with 
high yield strengths of 500 MPa at room temperature, and 163 MPa at 573 K, both are higher than those 
of additive manufactured Al-based alloys reported thus far. It is suggested that the high strength over 
a wide temperature range of the current LPBF Al alloy is mainly attributed to the combination of the 
precipitation strengthening mechanism and grain-boundary strengthening mechanism.

1. Introduction 
Aluminum alloys have been extensively used in aerospace and 
automotive industries because of their light weight and high spe- 
ciﬁc strength [1] . However, manufacturing Al alloy components 
with complex geometries by traditional subtractive processing is 
encountering serious challenges. To solve this problem, laser pow- 
der bed fusion (LPBF), also known as selective laser melting (SLM), 
has been applied in recent years to manufacture Al-alloy compo- 
nents with weird shapes, which are diﬃcult to manufacture by 
other methods [2–10] . However, manufacturing Al alloys via LPBF 
is challenging owing to their poor processability and high oxi- 
dation tendency [2] . To date, only a few Al alloy systems can 
be reliably manufactured via LPBF, and most of them are near- 
eutectic Al-Si-(Mg) alloys [8 , 9] . Nevertheless, the strength of LPBF 
Al-Si-Mg alloys is not competitive, normally less than 250 MPa at 
room temperature, and rapidly decreases at the evaluated temper-

volved [11–15] . Accordingly, there is an urgent demand to design 
new Al alloys that have good mechanical properties at both room 
and high temperatures and are simultaneously manufacturable by 
LPBF. 
In the past decade, researchers have attempted a variety of 
new compositions; for example, Sc- and Zr-modiﬁed 5xxx al- 
loys (Scalmalloy®) have been developed by Airbus Group Innova- 
tions, which show a promisingly high yield strength of approxi- 
mately 480 MPa at room temperature [16] relying on precipita- 
tion strengthening of the coherent nanosized Al 3 (Sc, Zr) particles 
in the Al matrix [17–20] . Similarly, Jia et al. [21] recently devel- 
oped a new Al-Mn-Mg-Sc-Zr alloy, which exhibits a high yield 
strength of ∼ 460 MPa. Importantly, this alloy can be easily man- 
ufactured by LPBF, making complex components available. In addi- 
tion, a few other high-strength Al alloys have also been for LPBF,

precipitation-strengthened Al-alloys developed in the past exhibit

Al-Ce-Mn alloy that can be easily manufactured by LPBF. This al-

ductility at room temperature ( ∼0%) is not satisfactory. Wang et al.

the Al 13 Fe 4 precipitates displayed a high resistance to coarsening 
at evaluated temperatures. The hardness of the alloy could retain 
85% of the room-temperature hardness after exposure at 573 K for 
100 h. Similarly, the near-eutectic Al-Fe alloy was also prepared 
by LPBF [26–30] . For example, the LPBF Al-2.5Fe binary alloy ex- 
hibited a yield strength of 266 MPa at room temperature and a 
high yield strength of 211 MPa at 573 K, beneﬁting from precipita- 
tion of a large number of nano-sized metastable Al 6 Fe phase in Al 
matrix [28] . 
Based on previous works, there are two major strategies for the 
design of Al-based alloys for LPBF. One is to design new binary 
eutectic alloys, while the other is to add Zr or Sc as a grain re- 
ﬁner and precipitation trigger in conventional LPBF Al alloys. Here, 
we combine the advantages of the eutectic structure and func- 
tionality of Zr, and design a new Al-based alloy, namely, AlFeCuZr, 
which is highly precipitate-strengthened and easily manufactured 
by LPBF. The concept of the alloy design is to utilize nanoprecipita- 
tion strengthening combined with grain boundary segregation. Af- 
ter compositional optimization, a near-eutectic quaternary Al-1Fe- 
0.6Cu-1.3Zr (at.%) alloy was ﬁnally selected. The microstructures in 
the as-built Al alloy were characterized with scanning electron mi- 
croscopy (SEM), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) and trans- 
mission electron microscopy (TEM). The mechanical properties at 
room and elevated temperatures were characterized with tensile 
tests. Theoretical models were applied to analyze the strengthen- 
ing mechanisms. It is demonstrated that this strategy for Al al- 
loy design enables us to achieve high yield strengths of 500 MPa 
at room temperature and 163 MPa at 573 K, both of which are 
superior to those of most other LPBF Al alloys reported thus 
far. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials and LPBF process 
The pre-alloyed Al-1Fe-0.6Cu-1.3Zr (at.%) powder was prepared 
by gas atomization technique, which displays a good spherical 
shape in addition to some satellites ( Fig. 1 (a)). The mean particle 
size used for LPBF process is ∼ 44.3 μm. The real powder com- 
position was measured by X-ray ﬂuorescence spectrometry (XRF, 
EAGLE III, America), and is listed in Table S1 in Supplementary In- 
formation, which is very close to the nominal composition of the 
alloy. Before LPBF processing, the powder was dried at 343 K for 
10 h in a vacuum to improve the ﬂowability. The additive man- 
ufacturing experiments were carried out using an LPBF machine 
(HUSTBMG-I, China) in an Ar atmosphere, equipped with a ﬁber 
laser (wavelength: 1070 nm, diameter of laser spot: 80 μm, maxi- 
mum power: 500 W). The optimized process conditions are: laser 
power of 325 W, scanning speed of 800 mm/s, hatch distance of 
0.06 mm and layer thickness of 0.04 mm. Here, a relatively small 
hatch distance of 0.06 mm was used in order to decrease the 
porosity in the LPBF parts. The alloys were printed on a 5083 Al 
alloy substrate using a cross scanning strategy with the scan di- 
rection rotation of 90 ° between the consecutive layers ( Fig. 1 (b)) 
to reduce residual stress. The porosity was measured with the 
Archimedes principle using the as-cast sample of the same compo- 
sition as the reference. To visualize the pores in the LPBF sample, 
the rod specimens ( φ 2 mm × 4 mm) were further examined by 
X-ray tomography (XRT, FF35, YXLON). The pore size and distribu- 
tion in the specimens were automatically calculated by the Avizo 
Fire software. 
2.2. Microstructural characterization 
After LPBF process, all specimens were cut from the sub- 
strate by wire electro discharge machining (EDM), wet-ground up 
to 30 0 0 grit using SiC paper and then polished to mirror ﬁn- 
ish, followed by ultrasonically cleaned in acetone, washed in dis- 
tilled water, and dried in warm air. Microstructures of the LPBF 
samples were examined using optical microscopy (OM, Leica), X- 
ray diffraction (XRD, XRD-70 0 0SX), scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM, Quanta650 FEG), electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD, 
with a scanning step of 0.1 μm), transmission electron microscopy 
(TEM, Tecnai G2 F30) coupled with energy disperse spectroscopy 
(EDX). For SEM characterization, the as-polished samples were 
etched with the Keller’s etchant (2.5 mL HNO 3 , 1.5 mL HCl, 1 mL 
HF and 95 mL H 2 O) for 40 s to reveal the general morphology. For

2.3. Mechanical properties tests 
The microhardness of the as-built samples was measured using 
a Vickers hardness tester (hvs-10 0 0Z) with a load of 200 g and a 
holding time of 10 s. At least 20 measurements were conducted 
for each sample to ensure data repeatability. For the mechanical 
tests, rectangular dog-bone-sharped tensile specimens with gauge 
dimensions of 12 mm × 2.5 mm × 1.5 mm were cut from as- 
built plates ( Fig. 3 (a)) by electrical discharge cutting. Before test- 
ing, the surface of the sample was ground, polished, and tensile 
tests were performed with a universal mechanical testing machine 
(AG-IC 100 kN) at room temperature (298 K) and two evaluated 
temperatures (473 and 573 K) at a strain rate of 10 −3 s −1 . For the 
high-temperature test, the samples were maintained at the prede- 
ﬁned temperature for at least 10 min to achieve a homogenous 
temperature throughout the specimen prior to the test. At least 
three specimens were tested under each condition. 
2.4. Thermal simulation 
As the microstructure in LPBF AlFeCuZr alloys is strongly de- 
pendent on the thermal proﬁle in the moving melt pool, a ﬁ- 
nite element (FE) model based on a simpliﬁed LPBF process was 
developed to estimate the temperature distribution in a single 
track. Thermal ﬁeld simulation was performed using the ABAQUS 
software by a ﬁnite element method (FEM). Gaussian distributed 
moving heat ﬂux was applied via the ABAQUS subroutine DFLUX. 
The AlFeCuZr alloy served as the substrate with dimensions of 
1 mm × 0.7 mm × 0.35 mm, and a grid cell size of 25 μm and 
a total mesh number of 156,800 were used. C3D8T elements were 
used from the ABAQUS element library for analysis. The thermal 
ﬂux density was applied to the powder layer using the ABAQUS 
subroutine DFLUX. As for the thermophysical data required for FEM 
simulation, the speciﬁc heat capacity ( C p ) of the alloy was mea- 
sured with differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, TA-Q20 0 0) using 
sapphire as the reference and the thermal diffusivity ( α) was mea- 
sured with laser thermal conductivity analyzer (LFA-427, Germany) 
below 550 °C, while the density at room temperature ( ρ0 ) was cal- 
culated based on the Archimedes principle. The thermal conduc- 
tivity and density of the alloy in the liquid state were calculated 
according to the extrapolated method proposed in the literature 
[31–34] . The detailed thermophysical data are summarized in Ta- 
ble S2. 
3. Results 
3.1. Alloy design concept 
The alloy design concept is illustrated in Fig. 2 . The ﬁrst step 
is to select base Al-X binary eutectic alloys, which typically have 
good ﬂowability and low hot cracking tendency due to a relatively 
small freezing range and thus are compatible with LPBF process- 
ing. Considering the low diffusivity and solid-solubility of Fe in Al, 
which are beneﬁcial for high-temperature applications, Al-Fe alloys 
with a eutectic composition (i.e., Al-1Fe, in at.%) and hypereutectic 
compositions (Al-2.5Fe and Al-5.9Fe) were ﬁrst examined. It was 
found that the microhardness of the LPBF Al-Fe alloys increased 
from 95 HV to 260 HV with increasing Fe content, indicating a pro- 
nounced strengthening effect in the Al-Fe systems studied. How- 
ever, the excessive amount of Fe will lead to poor formability for 
additive manufacturing and poor ductility due to the occurrence 
of severe cracking in hypereutectic alloys. Similar thermal cracking 
was also observed in LPBF Al-15Fe binary alloy, which was asso- 
ciated with the appearance of brittle Al 13 Fe 3 intermetallic phases 
along the melt pool boundaries [35] . Therefore, the eutectic Al-1Fe 
alloy is chosen as the base alloy. Second, Cu is added to further 
strengthen the Al-1Fe alloy due to a possible formation of Al 2 Cu 
precipitates in Al matrix after subsequent heat treatment [36] . In 
addition, both Fe and Cu have a low diffusivity in Al grains (see 
Fig. 2 ) [37] , beneﬁting for the obtainment of high-temperature sta- 
bility and strength. To further improve the strength and formabil- 
ity, Zr is added to the Al-Fe-Cu alloy, which acts as a grain reﬁner 
and precipitation trigger. To obtain the most appropriate Zr con- 
tent, a series of Al-Fe-Cu-Zr alloys with different Zr contents (i.e., 
0.3 at.%, 0.6 at.%, 0.8 at.% and 1.3 at.%) were designed, based on the 
assessment of yield strength, grain reﬁnement and printability, the 
optimized Zr content of 1.3 at.% was selected in this study. So the 
ﬁnally designed alloy is Al-1Fe-0.6Cu-1.3Zr. 
3.2. Microstructure analysis 
Fig. 3 (a and b) shows that a near-full dense ( > 99.7%) 
Al1Fe0.6Cu1.3Zr alloy was fabricated by LPBF under the optimized 
energy density (i.e., E = 175 J/m 3 , as measured using the equation 
of E = P / vth [38] , where, P is the laser power, v is the scanning 
speed, h is the hatch spacing and t is the powder layer thickness). 
In addition, the porosity and pore distribution in the LPBF sample 
were measured by XRT. Based on the total volume of pores (59 
pores were quantiﬁed in the sample), the porosity was calculated 
to be 0.04%. Noted that this value is much lower than that mea- 
sured with Archimedes principle (0.3%) due to the limitation of the 
XRT resolution (the resolution of the applied XRT in this work is 
about 5 μm, which means those pores with a size less than 5 μm 
could not be detected). It is found that some large pores with a 
diameter > 100 μm exist in the LPBF samples (Fig. S1 in Supple- 
mentary Information). 
The XRD proﬁle reveals that the dominant phases of the as- 
built alloy consist of α-Al and Al 3 Zr phases ( Fig. 4 (a)). Consid- 
ering that there is no Al 3 Zr phase in the as-received powders, 
so the Al 3 Zr phase is believed to be in-situ precipitated during 
the LPBF process. The DTA curve of the as-built sample exhibits 
only one sharp endothermic peak with an onset temperature of 
879 K ( Fig. 4 (b)), indicating that this Al1Fe0.6Cu1.3Zr alloy is nearly 
at quaternary eutectic composition point [39] . The detailed mi- 
crostructures along building direction of the as-built AlFeCuZr al- 
loy are shown in Figs. 5 and 6 . This Al alloy has two distinct zones: 
coarse-grain zones (CGZs) and ﬁne-grain zones (FGZs) (Fig. S2). The 
CGZs are located inside the molten pools, while the FGZs appear 
at the molten pool boundaries ( Fig. 5 (a)). The reason for the dif- 
ference in grain size at different locations of a molten pool will 
be discussed in the next section. The heterogeneous grain struc- 
tures were further examined by EBSD, and the results are shown in 
Fig. 5 (b). The two zones with different grain sizes are again clearly 
observed, where the average grain size is approximately 0.45 μm 
in FGZs and 0.95 μm in CGZs. In addition, the grains in both zones 
mainly have high angle grain boundaries ( Fig. 5 (c)). 
Noted that the grains in both zones in the present AlFeCuZr al- 
loy are much smaller than those in other LPBF Al alloys [12,20,40–
54] reported previously (see Fig. 5 (d)). Interestingly, equiaxed 
grains were simultaneously obtained in both CGZs and FGZs of the 
LPBF AlFeCuZr alloy, which differs from the microstructure of pre- 
vious LPBF Al alloys that typically have a columnar grain structure 
in the CGZs [21] . This might be associated with the high content 
of Zr (1.3at.%) used in this alloy because it could not only increase 
the density of nucleation sites (i.e., Al 3 Zr), but also reduce the ther-

4. Discussion 
4.1. On the mechanisms of the nanoprecipitation and GB segregation 
To understand how the temperature ﬁeld affects the mi- 
crostructure of the AlFeCuZr alloy in LPBF, ﬁnite element model- 
ing (FEM) simulation was carried out, and the temperature ﬁeld 
proﬁle around a molten pool is shown in Fig. 9 (a). The temper- 
ature at the center in the molten pool is approximately 1505 K, 
but rapidly decays when moving away from the center, where the 
isotherm of 879 K (this is the melting temperature of the AlFe- 
CuZr alloy studied) refers to the bottom of the molten pool. Based 
on the Al-Zr phase diagram, Al 3 Zr is the primary phase during so- 
lidiﬁcation and should initially precipitate at the bottom region of 
the molten pool where the temperature is lower than 1073 K (be- 
low this temperature, Al 3 Zr will be formed [16] ). The primarily 
formed nano-Al 3 Zr particles can then serve as nuclei and subse- 
quently trigger the formation of ﬁne equiaxed α-Al grains when 
the temperature is further decreased below 879 K in the bottom 
region of the molten pool. A similar phenomenon was also ob- 
served in previous works [22] . However, in the molten pool inte- 
rior (i.e., in CGZs), no Al 3 Zr particles were detected, which could 
be associated with the enhanced cooling rate in the interior region 
of the molten pool [55 , 56] . It was reported that, when the cool- 
ing rate is below a threshold value (depending on the Zr content), 
the Al 3 Zr phase with an angular structure could be initially pre- 
cipitated; however, if the cooling rate is higher than this threshold 
value, Al 3 Zr will be totally suppressed due to the restriction of dif- 
fusion of Zr atoms, which allows the formation of only an α-Al 
solid solution (for an Al-Zr binary system) [55] . Based on the FEM 
simulation, the cooling rate at different locations (such as points 
a, b, c, and d, as labeled in Fig. 9 (a)) can be derived from the cor- 
responding temperature-time proﬁles shown in Fig. 9 (b). The cool- 
ing rate increases from 7.4 × 10 6 K/s (point c) at the bottom of 
the melt pool to 1.1 × 10 7 K/s (point a) in the center. It is plau- 
sible that, for the present alloy with 1.3 at.% Zr (corresponding to 
4.44 wt.% Zr), the threshold cooling rate is between 4 × 10 6 K/s 
(the bottom region) and 1 × 10 7 K/s (the interior region) [55] , thus 
Al 3 Zr was suppressed in the interior of the melting pool under a 
high cooling rate. Without Al 3 Zr inoculation, the grain size in this 
region is relatively coarser than that in the bottom of the molten 
pool. 
The current AlFeCuZr alloy, in addition to Zr, also contains Fe 
and Cu. Once the formation of Al 3 Zr is suppressed in the interior 
region of the molten pools, the phase formation of the remaining 
melt will be controlled by the Al-Fe phase diagram (Cu is dissolved 
in Al as Cu has much larger solubility in Al than Fe [57] ). When 
the temperature in the interior region approaches 928 K, a eutec- 
tic reaction with the co-formation of the Al-Zr solid solution and 
metastable (Al, Cu)Fe 3 intermetallic could occur. Note that such a 
eutectic reaction does not give lamella structures, but (Al, Cu)Fe 3 
particles dispersed homogeneously in the Al matrix (Al-Zr solid so- 
lution), mainly due to the high cooling rate ( > 10 6 K/s [58] ) of the 
melt in the LPBF process, which suppresses the growth of the high- 
temperature phase [i.e., (Al, Cu)Fe 3 ]. Since the solidiﬁcation front 
velocity in the top region may be high enough, Cu is easily trapped 
in the (Al, Cu)Fe 3 compound [22] . To clarify the sequence of phase 
formation and the detailed microstructures in the LPBF AlFeCuZr 
alloy, we present a schematic diagram in Fig. 9 (c) , in which the

4.3. On the high strength at elevated temperatures 
The LPBF AlFeCuZr alloy not only exhibits high strength at 
room temperature, but also presents excellent properties at ele- 
vated temperatures, as shown in Fig. 6 (a). The present LPBF AlFe- 
CuZr alloy has yield strengths of 300 MPa at 475 K and 163 MPa 
at 575 K, which are superior to those of other LPBF Al alloys and 
conventional high-strength Al alloys (see Fig. 8 ). At elevated tem- 
peratures, the softening of metal matrix, grain rotation and grain 
boundary (GB) migration are known to be the mechanisms for re- 
duction of yield strength [72] . It has been reported that, for the 
ﬁne-grain Al alloys, the deformation at elevated temperature is 
dominated by grain boundary sliding (GBS), controlled by inter- 
granular dislocation climb and dislocation creep [22] . We believe 
that the deformation of the present LPBF AlFeCuZr alloy (grain size 
of < 1 μm) also follows the same mechanism, i.e., GBS. However,

mechanisms still needs to be investigated in the future, which can 
guide alloy design for improving the strength of current alloys. 
5. Conclusion 
In this work, a novel crack-free and near-full dense AlFe- 
CuZr alloy with superior room-temperature and high-temperature 
strengths was developed via LPBF. The as-built AlFeCuZr alloy ex- 
hibited a heterogeneous microstructure with two distinct zones: 
one was composed of ﬁne grains, while the other consisted of 
coarse grains. In the coarse-grain zones, (Al, Cu)Fe 3 nanoparti- 
cles were precipitated in the grain interior, while Fe and Cu co- 
segregated at the GBs. In the ﬁne-grain zones, an Al 3 Zr parti- 
cle was precipitated inside each of the grains, while Fe and Cu 
cosegregation and a nanosized Fe-rich intermetallic phase were 
formed at the GBs. The yield strength of the as-built AlFeCuZr alloy 
reached as high as 500 MPa at room temperature and 163 MPa at 
573 K, exceeding most conventional LPBF Al-alloys. Such a good 
high-temperature strength is attributed to dual-nanoprecipitate 
strengthening and GB segregations. This work demonstrates a new 
microstructural design strategy by beneﬁcially utilizing the eutectic 
structure and precipitation strengthening effect in Al alloys fabri- 
cated by LPBF.
