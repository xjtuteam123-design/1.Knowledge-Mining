Elasto-plastic deformation in thermal barrier coated superalloys
Nilima Roy a,*, Kersi M. Godiwalla a, Eswarahalli S. Dwarakadasa b, Ashok K. Ray a
a National Metallurgical Laboratory, CAP Division, Jamshedpur 831007, India
b Department of Metallurgy, Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore 560 012, India
Received 14 August 2003; received in revised form 10 March 2004; accepted 3 April 2004
Abstract
The present work highlights some of the results on deformation behaviour in elastic plastic condition under bending, for
miniature test specimens of thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) on Ni-base superalloys. The experimental data was simulated using
ﬁnite element analysis. The analysis indicated concurrence of displacements, for each load case, with the experimental results in the
elastic regime.

1. Introduction
TBCs represent an attractive materials approach of
enhancing the high temperature limits for systems such
as gas turbines, aircraft engines and diesel engines.
Industry and academia in recent years have been ac-
tively pursuing research on evaluating the lifetime and
thermomechanical behaviour of candidate materials in
turbine blades and vanes being subjected to high
mechanical stresses and aggressive environments [1–5].
These blades and vanes are made of superalloys with
ceramic TBCs.
The Ni-base superalloys which have been developed
for this application are made of superalloys having a c
matrix (solid solution based on nickel) strengthened by
both solid solution and precipitation of the c
0-Ni3 (Ti,
Al) phase. The signiﬁcant diﬀerences in properties be-
tween ceramic and metal, as well as the severe temper-
ature gradients applied in such systems, cause thermal
stresses that can lead to cracking, delamination and
ultimately spalling of the coating. In recent years, con-
certed eﬀorts have been made to understand the mech-
anisms of crack initiation and propagation in TBCs
under controlled experimental conditions [1,2,4–8].
The elastic modulus of the bond coat has been sim-
ulated by modifying the existing model for two-layered
composite beam specimen to a three-layered composite
beam specimen [7]. Simulations were carried out using
ANSYS software for all specimens with selected load
conditions in the elastic region of the stress–strain curves
and the outputs of the stresses have been presented and
discussed in Ref. [6].
The aim of the present work is to mathematically
simulate the experimental data for the deformation
curves in elastic plastic condition using beam elements in
FEM analysis and to derive analytical models in the
elastic plastic regime of the deformation curves in TBCs.
In the present analysis only small deﬂections have been
considered.

2.2. Finite element formulation
There are two approaches to the elastic–plastic
analysis of beams: Non-layered approach and layered
approach [8,9]. In the layered approach the beam is
subdivided into a chosen number of layers as shown in
Fig. 1(a). In the ﬁnite element solution it is assumed that
as soon as the stress in the middle of the outer layers
reaches the yield value, then the outer layers become
plastic, while the rest of the layers remain elastic. As the
number of layers is increased, this model provides a
more realistic representation of the gradual spread of
plasticity over the beam cross-section. In the Non-lay-
ered approach, the whole cross-section of the beam is
assumed to become plastic instantaneously when the
bending moment reaches the yield moment.
The dimension and thickness of the TBC specimens
are given in Table 1. The properties of the three layers
are reported in Ref. [7]. These layers are further divided
into number of sub layers. A beam element type is used.
The beam is subjected to four-point bend loading (Fig.
1(b)). A ﬁnite element code has been developed for
numerical calculations.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure
In the oxidized TBC coated samples, due to the high
oxygen diﬀusivity of ZrO 2 at high temperature and the
presence of porosity in the TBC and in the bond coat,
the Al in the bond coat oxidizes resulting in a layer of
Al
2O3 at the TBC/bond coat interface of about 2 lmi n
thickness (Fig. 3). Previous authors [10] have also re-
ported similar observations while studying the oxidation
behaviour of sprayed MCrAlY coatings in air as well as
in helium with 1% oxygen at 1050 /C176 C. The Al
2O3 layer
provides better adhesion of the TBC layer on the bond
coat [11]. Primary carbide precipitation within the
coarsened grains in the substrate and the triple point
junction of the grains marked by secondary carbide
precipitation are evident in Fig. 4(a). The arrow in the
ﬁgure indicates carbide precipitation at the interface of
the bond coat and substrate. The etched (dark) portion
in Fig. 4(b) corresponds to c (austenite) phase and the
cubic precipitates corresponds to the c
0 phase. The
arrow indicates the c0 phase. The c0 phase seems to grow
laterally in a direction parallel to the bond coat after the
aluminium depleted zone.

diﬀerence in the yield stress and the respective load
values in the deformation curves of the as received
specimens is attributed to the diﬀerence in the bond coat
porosity and to the speciﬁed grades of the substrate. As
such, the thickness of the TBC layer has not been con-
sidered in simulating the experimental results. Further-
more, it was observed that the bond coat acts as an
attractive sink for stress relaxation. This prevents the
crack front from propagating into the substrate.
A beam element was chosen for the purpose of sim-
ulation. The element has two degrees of freedom at each
node. Suitable real constants and material properties
were speciﬁed. Suitable values for tangent modulus and
yield stress have been speciﬁed to simulate the plastic
behaviour of the specimen (Table 2). The specimen has
been loaded at two points, which is equidistant from the
two ends. This simulates a perfect bending condition
(four-point bend test).
The simulated results are shown in Table 3. How-
ever, theoretical predictions using analytical formula-
tion and the non-layered approach could approximate
experimental results only for moderate deﬂections be-
yond the elastic limit of the material. Further studies
are necessary to account for the deviations. To over-
come this diﬃculty the layered approach has been
incorporated in the FEM formulation. A ﬁnite element
code has been developed for the same, employing 2-
noded beam elements having two degrees of freedom at
each node.
In the present analysis, FEM formulation based on
both layered and non-layered approach has been used
to validate the experimental data. The results obtained
from both layered and non-layered approach has
been produced in Fig. 6(a)–(d). Predictions are fairly
close to experimental data only in the elastic regime in
the case of non-layered approach. Whereas prediction
based on layered approach is more realistic and able
to validate the experimental data in elastic–plastic re-
gime.
4. Conclusion
The higher yield stress values in the case of oxidized
thermal barrier coated beam specimens compared to
those of the as received ones, were attributed to micro-
structural toughening of the composites.
Analytical as well as FEM formulations were devel-
oped to simulate the experimental results. The analytical
and non-layered FEM formulations could predict the
experimental results reasonably well in the elastic re-
gime. However, for plastic deﬂections, a layered ap-
proach for the ﬁnite element analysis was necessary to
simulate deﬂections of the specimen up to 0.5% strain.
Analysis indicated concurrence of displacements, for
each load case, with the experimental results in the
elastic regime. Reasonable extent of concurrence was
also obtained in the early stages of plastic deformation
of elements. However, minor deviations occurred from
experimental results in the case of large loads.
