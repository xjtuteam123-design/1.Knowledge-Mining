ABSTRACT
Intermediate-temperature embrittlement is a common problem faced by many multi-component alloys. In this 
study, we propose the introduction of nitrogen into a lightweight TiVNb medium-entropy alloy to enhance so -
lution strengthening and induce titanium segregation. Titanium-rich precipitates in turbine blade-like structures 
near the grain boundaries of a body-centred cubic solution matrix yield alloys that exhibit tensile ductility at 
intermediate temperatures. The titanium-rich precipitates dominantly have a face-centred cubic structure 
coupled with a small amount of hexagonal close-packed structure. The size of the titanium-rich phase increases 
with increasing nitrogen content from 0.5 % to 1.5 %, as does the alloy strength. The tensile yield strength of 
TiVNb-N
1.5 
reaches 1247 MPa at room temperature and 623 MPa at 800 
◦
C, with a strain of 10 %. These values 
indicate that the prepared alloy overcomes the ultra-low strain of the base alloy at intermediate temperatures. By 
combining the MRS-Swap algorithm with the density functional theory, the formation mechanism of Ti-rich 
phases is deduced. This study further elucidates the types and pathways of interstitial elements that can 
induce FCC-Ti formation.
1. Introduction
Many groups of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have been developed 
during the last two decades [1,2] . In terms of the phase number, HEAs 
can be categorised into single-phase solid solutions and multi-phase 
alloys. In terms of the crystal structure, they are mostly characterised 
as body-centred cubic (BCC), face-centred cubic (FCC) and combinations 
thereof. According to their density, HEAs can be divided into light and 
refractory high-entropy alloys. By virtue of their outstanding strength at 
high temperatures relative to traditional alloys, most HEAs can be used 
in high-temperature applications [3,4] . Hence, a considerable number of 
studies has focused on the composition design of HEAs and possibility to 
obtain low-density and high-temperature performance HEAs. Light -
weight HEAs commonly contain elements such as Ti, V, Nb, Zr, Al, Co 
and Ni. Combinations of Ti, V, Nb and Zr are typical in the composition 
of lightweight and strong BCC HEAs ( < 7 g/cm
3
) and have been devel -
oped as a basic system [5 – 7] . The tensile strength of TiVNb-Zrx alloys 
can exceed 400 MPa at 800 ℃ by optimising the zirconium content to 
5 at% [8] . However, this group of alloys shows a transition from 
toughness to brittleness between 600
◦
C and 800
◦
C [8,9] . This temper -
ature range represents an important service temperature regime, and 
embrittlement within this range is not conducive to the 
high-temperature use of such materials, resulting in catastrophic failure, 
which causes heavy losses. This ‘intermediate-temperature embrittle -
ment ’ phenomenon was first reported in the FCC-type Al
0.5
CoCrCuFeNi 
wrought HEA [10] . Subsequently, more than ten papers demonstrated 
this problem in different alloys, and the causes of 
intermediate-temperature embrittlement have been analysed [11 – 24] .
Several explanations have been proposed for intermediate- 
temperature embrittlement, most of which are thought to be related to 
oxidation and precipitation-induced stress concentration near grain 
boundaries (GBs) under intermediate temperatures. Consequently, 
measures have been proposed to improve tensile brittleness at inter -
mediate temperatures; these measures are primarily based on the 
improvement of oxidation resistance [18,22] , construction of 
non-equiaxial grain structures [14] or fibre-like structures [23] to hinder

important factor in this embrittlement phenomena, it is not a funda -
mental factor. For example, when the Ni – Co – Fe – Cr – Al – Ti alloy is sub -
jected to tension stress in argon [15] , its strain is increased compared 
with air; however, a large strain reduction relative to low temperatures 
is still observed. Additionally, although stress dispersion strategies are 
applicable to some alloys, they are not universal. In cast and forged 
CrMnFeCoNi FCC HEAs [16] , plastic deterioration occurred at an in -
termediate temperature in both cases. The non-equiaxial structure in 
forged alloys reduces the plastic deterioration temperature from 800 ℃ 
to 650 ℃ but does not prevent the transition from ductility to brittleness.
In recent years, the development of eutectic HEAs or dual-phase al -
loys has led to the use of the combination of hard and soft phases as an 
improved strategy to reduce the stress concentration and enhance the 
strength and toughness synergy of HEAs [25 – 27] . For the formation of 
the dual phase, thermo-mechanical-processing-induced precipitation or 
interstitial-impurity-induced phase transition is usually adopted. The 
latter can be achieved synchronously during material preparation. For 
example, FCC-Ti was induced by C [28] and Al [29] , thereby enhancing 
the compressional strength and toughness of BCC complex concentrated 
alloys. Although the origin of FCC-Ti is explained in literature, the 
reason for the precipitation of a titanium-rich phase instead of vanadium 
or niobium remains unclear.
Herein, with the objective of resolving the intermediate-temperature 
brittleness of BCC type Ti – V – Nb system alloys and simultaneously 
strengthening the synthesised alloys, an FCC-Ti phase was successfully 
induced by trace N content ( ≤ 1.5 at%), which facilitated the formation 
of FCC + BCC dual-phase alloys. The resulting alloy exhibited high 
strength and tensile plasticity at room and elevated temperatures. The 
microstructure, properties, fracture mechanism and formation mecha -
nism of Ti-rich phases were analysed.

2.2. Theoretical calculation
To explore the formation mechanism of the FCC-Ti phase induced by 
nitrogen atoms in the Ti – V – Nb system alloys, the MRS-Swap algorithm 
combined with the density functional theory (DFT) method was used to 
determine the low-energy structure of TiVNb-N systems. Previous 
research has shown that the MRS-Swap algorithm is very effective for 
determining the global minima (GM) of multi-component systems [30] . 
For 3d- or 4d-transition-metal (TM) systems, the Per -
dew – Durke – Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation function and 
double-numerical polarised (DNP) basis set were used for geometric 
optimisation in the DMol
3 
code [31] . Previous studies have shown that 
the PBE function combined with the DNP basis set is effective for 
describing the structural properties of TM systems [32,33] . The opti -
misation convergence criterion is set to 2 × 10
 5 
Hartree for energy 
convergence, 0.005 Å for gradient convergence and 0.004 Å for 
displacement convergence. For TM cluster searching, the spin polar -
isation restriction and symmetry constraints are not used for each ge -
ometry optimisation. Three independent MRS-Swap searches, 
comprising 600 samples with various randomly generated initial con -
figurations, were performed for each molecular cluster. When all 1800 
cluster structures were obtained via MRS-Swap searching, they were 
ranked according to their relative energies, which were calculated ac -
cording to the difference between the global minimum (GM) and local 
minimum (LM) for three separate searches, △ E = E(LM) E(GM). The 
low-energy candidates selected within 6 kcal/mol relative to GM were 
re-optimised via the Orca software package using the Revised 
Perdew-Burke-Erzerhoff (RPBE) function with a different basis set for 
various elements: the TZVP basis set for Ti, V and N and LANL2DZ basis 
set for Nb [34] .

4.3. Reasons for the high alloy strength
Table 1 compares the mechanical properties of nitrogen-containing 
and nitrogen-free TiVNb HEAs, showing that the strength of the 
N
0.5 – 1.5 
alloys continues to increase with the nitrogen content from RT to 
800 ℃ . Considering the N
1.5 
alloy as an example, its strength at RT 
increases by 97 % relative to the N
0 
alloy and by 76 % at 800 ℃ . This 
high strength can be accounted for in two ways: (1) interstitial atom 
strengthening and (2) discontinuous Ti-rich lamellae strengthening. 
Except for a small amount of nitrogen precipitated in Ti-rich phases, 
most nitrogen is dissolved in the BCC matrix, causing lattice distortion. 
As the soluble nitrogen content increases, the lattice distortion increases 
and strengthening effect on the alloy becomes more prominent. There is 
a near-linear relationship between the N content and yield strength. At 
RT, each 0.5 % N content increases the yield strength of the alloy by 
~20 %. Further, lamellae precipitates in the turbine blade-like structure 
near GBs are embedded in the matrix in all directions, thereby increasing 
the intergranular bonding force.
5. Conclusion
TiVNb-Nx (x = 0.5 – 1.5 at% N content) alloys with high strength and 
intermediate-temperature embrittlement resistance were synthesised 
through nitrogen introduction using an arc-melting method. The alloy 
comprises a BCC matrix and turbine blade-like precipitates with mixed 
FCC and HCP structures. The blades are rich in Ti and are predominantly 
distributed near GBs, where they are composed of discontinuous 
lamellae. The size of lamellae in the alloy increases with increasing the 
nitrogen content. Furthermore, the TiVNb-Nx alloy strength is positively 
correlated with the nitrogen content. The turbine blade-like precipitates 
of Ti-rich phases plays a notable role in increasing strain at 800 ℃ 
relative to the nitrogen-free TiVNb alloy. The N
1.5 
alloy exhibits optimal 
performance, with a tensile yield strength of 1247 MPa at RT and 
623 MPa at 800 ℃ as well as a compressive yield strength of 1254 MPa 
at RT and 712 MPa at 800 ℃ . It is also characterised by a tensile strain of 
10 %. The MRS-Swap algorithm combined with DFT reveals the for -
mation mechanism of Ti-rich phases through nitrogen-induced titanium 
enrichment. The high strength of the alloy is attributed to the 
strengthening effect of interstitial nitrogen as well as induced titanium 
enrichment. The intermediate-temperature embrittlement of the TiVNb 
matrix is eliminated by virtue of Ti-rich lamellae, which act as a 
toughness agent for deflecting cracks, resulting in a tolerable strain.
