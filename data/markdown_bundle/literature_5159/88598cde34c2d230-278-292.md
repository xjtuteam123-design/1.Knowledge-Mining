Nitrogen alloying of the multicomponent CoCrFeNiMn alloy: 
processing, microstructure and properties (Review)
E. G. Astafurova, S. V . Astafurov
†elena.g.astafurova@ispms.ru
Institute of Strength Physics and Materials Science, Siberian Branch of Russian Academy of Sciences, Tomsk 634055, Russia
Abstract: In recent 20 years, multicomponent alloys attract much attention of the materials scientists, physics, chemists, 
metallurgist because they open the perspective for obtaining many exceptional properties by both compositional and 
microstructural design. A huge portion of the modern multicomponent alloys started from the well-known Cantor alloy 
(CoCrFeMnNi), which possesses perfect low-temperature ductility and strength, high corrosion resistance. To use it as a 
structural material in the industry, there is a need to improve its strength at elevated temperatures maintaining good low-
temperature properties. Interstitial alloying has been recognized as an effective approach to enhance mechanical properties for 
the Cantor alloys family: it was successfully applied for the CoCrFeMnNi-C systems, but nitrogen-alloying of the Cantor alloy is 
still underinvestigated. The current issues to solve in this research field can be divided into technical (methods of high-nitrogen 
alloys fabrication, development of the thermomechanical treatments to obtain the best combination of the structural and 
functional properties) and physical one (mechanisms of interstitial hardening of the multicomponent alloys, the evaluation of 
the effect of nitrogen on deformation and fracture mechanisms of the alloys, etc.). This review is focused on the recent progress 
on the relationship between nitrogen-assisted hardening mechanisms and mechanical properties of the CoCrFeMnNi family 
alloys. The basic physical problems, including fabrication of the nitrogen-alloys Cantor alloy, solubility of N in CoCrFeMnNi 
system, as well as the influence of nitrogen alloying on strength properties of the CoCrFeMnNi-N alloys have been discussed.
Keywords: high entropy alloy, Cantor alloy, nitrogen, solid solution hardening, nitrides, austenite, microstructure, strength properties, fracture

1. Introduction 
The development of new multicomponent alloys with the 
unique physical and mechanical characteristics that are 
superior to traditional structural and functional materials 
with one or two basic components is one of the urgent 
problems of modern material science and solid-state physics. 
The revolutionary idea about the creating of multicomponent 
high-entropy alloys (HEA) was first formulated by B. Cantor 
[1, 2] and J. Y eh [3,4] in 2004. According to their first concept, 
the HEAs are alloys consisting of five or more components 
in the equiatomic composition or in the interval of 5  to 
35 at.%. The forward idea of HEA composition design was 
formulated on the combination of several key effects: high 
mixing entropy, sluggish diffusion of the alloy components, 
high distortion of the crystal lattice and the synergistic 
“cocktail” effect [2, 5 –10].
Equiatomic Cantor alloy (CoCrFeMnNi) is among the 
amply studied HEAs. Even though the elements that form its 
composition have different types of a crystal lattice, the Cantor 
alloy has a single-phase face-centered cubic (fcc) structure, 
and it possesses excellent ductility and fracture toughness in a 
wide range of temperatures including cryogenic [2, 8,11–18]. 
Despite this, relatively low yield strength and tensile strength 
at elevated temperatures significantly limits application area 
of the Cantor alloy as a structural material. In this regard, 
in the last decade, the much attention of the scientists has 
been paid to finding possible ways to increase the strength 
characteristics of the Cantor alloy without loss of ductility 
and fracture toughness. One of the most attractive methods 
to reach it is to alloy the Cantor alloy with interstitial atoms 
(nitrogen and carbon) [11,17,19 – 21].
In the case of carbon alloying of the Cantor alloy, carbides 
often form in it regardless of the production method and the 
concentration of interstitials in the alloy. According to various 
sources [22 – 27], below the threshold value of 0.5 – 2  at.% 
carbon a solid solution can be formed in the alloy without 
the formation of precipitates. At low carbon concentrations 
(usually about 1 at.%), fine carbide particles and solid solution 
strengthening provides rather high mechanical characteristics 
of the Cantor alloy even in as-solidified state [27 – 30]. 
However, at high carbon concentrations, despite high values 
of the yield strength and tensile stress (comparable to high-
strength steels), large irregularly shaped carbides are formed 
in the alloy, which lead to a catastrophic loss of ductility of 
the Cantor alloy [24, 26, 28, 31, 32]. Unfortunately, the use of 
standard thermomechanical treatments does not allow the 
dissolution of such large carbide particles, which eliminates 
the unique advantages of the Cantor alloy from the point of 
view of a structural material.

Starting from the analysis of the literature data on the effect 
of interstitial atoms on the structure and mechanical properties 
of fcc alloys [33, 34], one suggests that nitrogen alloying has a 
number of advantages over that by carbon atoms. For example, 
steels alloyed with nitrogen are characterized by high strength 
characteristics without a significant loss of ductility and 
toughness, since the addition of nitrogen leads to an increase 
in the concentration of free electrons and, as a consequence, 
increases the metallic nature of interatomic bonds [33, 35]. 
In addition, alloying with nitrogen increases the corrosion 
resistance, fracture toughness, and high-temperature 
properties of the fcc alloys, it causes higher strengthening of 
austenite (fcc austenitic or γ-phase) without the formation of 
coarse precipitates (as in the case of alloying by carbon) [33].
This review is devoted to summarizing the available data 
on the effect of nitrogen alloying of high-entropy Cantor 
alloy on its structure and mechanical properties in order to 
assess the prospects for using this approach and identify the 
main directions for further research in this area.
2. Methods of production of nitrogen-doped 
HEAs and phase diagram CoCrFeMnNi-N
As it was previously reported by M. Y .  He with coauthors 
[20], the synthesis methods of the interstitially-doped HEAs 
can be divided into two main parts: liquid and solid-state 
processing.
Vacuum induction melting is the mostly used liquid-state 
fabrication process of the nitrogen-alloyed CoCrFrNiMn-
based alloys [11,17, 29, 36 – 44]. In this fabrication method, 
nitrogen added as a ferrochrome nitride [36, 37, 39], MnN 
[40, 41] or using high-pressure nitrogen gas [42 – 44]. The 
advantages of the induction melting are short production 
cycle, easy removal of impurities, good alloying effect [20]. 
Nevertheless, poor fluidity, severe composition segregations 
and looseness restrict the obtaining of large-scale ingots of 
the HEAs [20].
Arc melting is an alternative technique, which also 
gives high-quality ingots with microsegregations of the 
components [45, 46]. A mixture of pure metals and Fe 4N in 
a Ti-getter high-purity argon atmosphere [45] or remelting 
in N2 atmosphere [46] was the methods of obtaining of small 
ingots of the nitrogen-doped alloys with nitrogen content 
up to 3.5  at.%. The as-received microstructure: dendritic 
segregations and Cr2N phase at high nitrogen content [45, 46].
The dendritic microstructure is typical of the as-solidified 
HEAs [11,17, 29, 36 – 44]. The examples of the dendritic 
segregations in nitrogen-doped Cantor alloy in comparison 
with those in carbon-alloyed one fabricated by induction 
melting are given in Fig.  1 [29]. Authors of [29] reported

that nitrogen-alloying decreases the tendency to dendritic

But at higher nitrogen content, all authors noted the 
precipitation of Cr2N phase, which seriously reduces ductility

Typical of as-cast HEAs dendritic microstructure and 
Cr2N precipitates can be effectively homogenized by the simple 
thermomechanical treatments including high temperature 
anneal (typically at 1100 –1200°C for 12 – 24 hours), cold or 
hot deformation (rolling, forging) and final solid-solution 
anneal following by a water-quenching [17, 40, 42 – 44]. 
F . Xiong with colleagues [47] applied a friction stir processing 
(FSP) treatment for homogenization of CoCrFeMnNi-N 
alloy (0.9 at.% N). FSP considerably broke down the coarse 
dendritic microstructure of the as-cast material, assisted 
grain refinement and composition homogenization. But 
the processing volume is limited in this treatment, and this 
homogenization method is appropriate for surface treatment, 
obtaining of heterostructures or local processing.
One more progressive method of the liquid-state synthesis 
is a selective laser melting, which was utilized for obtaining 
the dog-bone-shaped specimens of the CrFeCoNiN alloy 
(Fig. 2) [48]. Pre-alloyed powder of the CrFeCoNiN alloy with 
1.5 at.% of nitrogen was used, but the method of its obtaining 
was not described in detail. The additively manufactured 
CrFeCoNiN alloy was composed of a single austenitic (fcc) 
phase with no evidence of segregations or precipitated phases.
Solid-state fabrication of nitrogen-bearing HEAs is 
based on the powder metallurgy routes [49, 50]. I. Moravcik 
et al. [49] obtained a set of HEA powders by ball milling of 
elemental powders with purity over 99.5 %. They milled of 
Co, Cr, Fe, Mn and Ni powders in N 2 and Ar atmospheres 
(for 12, 16 and 24 h), and powders were then consolidated 
by a spark plasma sintering at 1150°C (30  MPa pressure 
and 8  min holding time). In both cases, fully dense bulk 
samples were fabricated, one of them was nitrogen-bearing 
CoCrFeMnNi-N alloy. The examples of the microstructures 
in pure CoCrFeMnNi and CoCrFeMnNi-N alloys obtained 
respectively in Ar and N 2 atmospheres are shown in Fig.  3 
[49]. The authors noted the formation of a coarse-grained 
microstructure in CoCrFeMnNi-N with nitrogen being 
solute in austenitic phase (0.2  wt.%  N) and bounded into 
chromium nitrides. Y .  Xie with coauthors [50] used a ball 
milling and hot pressure sintering: for the preparation of 
CoCrFeMnNi-N0.1 HEA powder they used Fe, Co, Cr, Ni, Mn 
(>99.9  wt.% purity) and FeNCr1-B (65  wt.%  Cr, 9  wt.%  N 
and 25.9 wt.% Fe) powders. The 45 h milled powders were 
sintered at 900°C for 1 h with a constant pressure of 50 MPa 
followed by cooling with a furnace. As-sintered material 
CoCrFeMnNi-N0.1 has very low porosity and complex phase 
composition: austenite, Cr-enriched σ-phase, Cr2N and traces 
of M23C6 carbide [50].
T.  Lindner et al. [51] used a low-temperature gas 
nitrocarburization for the surface interstitial hardening of 
the CrMnFeCoNi and CrFeCoNi alloys (initially arc melted). 
They reported a successful formation of a homogeneous 
diffusion layers with a thickness of 16  μm and graded but 
high microhardness and enhanced wear resistance. This 
method is appropriate for the surface interstitial hardening of 
the HEAs by the formation of a supersaturated solid solution 
of nitrogen (or carbon) in austenitic phase of CoCrFeMnNi 
alloys and for the fabrication of the graded materials.
The methods described above for producing 
multicomponent high-entropy alloys of the Cantor system 
made it possible to obtain bulk materials that are sufficient 
for scientific research, but small enough to talk about their 
industrial production. This is due, among other things, to the 
high cost of alloy components. In this regard, the creation of 
multicomponent films or coatings looks promising. And with 
regard to Cantor's nitrogen-containing alloys, we are talking 
about thin films of multicomponent nitrides [52], which 
cannot be considered as interstitial solid solutions, but only 
as films and coatings on the basis of a CoCrFeMnNiN system. 
High entropy nitride films attract much attention due to the 
satisfactory mechanical properties, excellent anti-oxidation 
performance, corrosion resistance and thermal stability. 
In most works on high-entropy nitrides, a physical vapor 
deposition or PVD method of preparing was applied [52].
For the described above methods of producing nitrogen-
alloyed Cantor alloy, the phase composition of the material 
and the concentration of nitrogen introduced into the solid 
solution of the austenitic phase are well described by phase 
diagrams calculated using the Termocalc software and 
given in some works [20, 37, 53] for CoCrFeMnNi-N alloys 
(Fig. 4 a).
According to the phase diagram predictions [20], the 
highest solubility of N in Cantor alloy is about 0.84  at.% 
(≈0.18  wt.%) at temperature ≈1270°C and it decreases 
drastically with decreasing temperature (Fig. 4 a). M. Y . He et al. 
[20] mentioned that Cr, Mo, W , Mn, and Fe all positively 
influence the solubility of nitrogen, while Ni, Co and Si 
contrarily suppress it. It should be noted that the solubility 
of nitrogen in the equiatomic Cantor alloy has been studied, 
although not in sufficient detail, while for other alloys of 
this system there is critically little data. For non-equiatomic 
compositions enriched with iron, A.  Semenyuk [54] and 
M.  Travisier [53] published phase diagrams that show that

3. Structure and mechanical properties of 
the nitrogen-alloyed CoCrFeMnNi alloys
3.1. Single‑phase (austenitic) Cantor alloy doped 
with nitrogen
As noted above, the predicted solubility of nitrogen in 
Cantor's alloy is limited to 0.84  at.% at a temperature of 
1270°C [20]. At high nitrogen concentrations, a two-phase 
structure is formed, consisting of a γ-based interstitial 
solid solution and nitride particles, predominantly Cr 2N 
phase. One can find a limited number of works in which the 
authors obtained a single-phase structure in the as-solidified 
CoCrFeMnNi-N alloys [37, 46, 47, 55]: 0.5 –1.0 at.% N [37], 
0.9  at.%  N [47], 1.4  at.%  N [55], 3.51  at.%  N [46]. The 
dissolution of more than 3  atomic percent nitrogen in the 
austenitic phase (reported in [46]) seems surprising, but the 
authors of the paper definitely stated that all the nitrogen was 
dissolved in the ingot, and a single-phase state was achieved 
in the as-solidified material. In cast (as-solidified) state for 
all nitrogen concentrations reported in [37, 46, 47, 55], a 
γ  solid solution with a coarse-grained structure is formed 
in the Cantor alloy (Table 1, Fig. 5). In this case, an increase 
in nitrogen concentration is accompanied by an increase in 
the lattice parameter of the γ-phase, which indicates solid 
solution strengthening of the Cantor alloy, and a decrease in 
the average grain size (Table 1).
M. Klimova et al. [37] showed that an increase in nitrogen 
concentration in as-cast CoCrFeMnNi-N alloy from 0.45 to 
0.88 at.% is not accompanied by the formation of secondary 
phases and leads to an increase in the fcc lattice parameter (a) 
from 0.359 to 0.3606 nm. Increase in nitrogen content also 
assists to the decrease in grain size ( d) of the material from 
175 to 165 µm. In [55], alloying the Cantor alloy with 1.4 at.% 
nitrogen leads to an increase in the lattice parameter of the 
alloy in the as-solidified state from 0.3593 nm (alloy without 
nitrogen) to 0.3608  nm (nitrogen-doped one). Chung et 
al. [46] managed to alloy the Cantor alloy with 3.51  at.% 
nitrogen without the formation of secondary phases, which 
led to an increase in the lattice parameter of the γ-phase 
from 0.3586 to 0.3614 nm while reducing the average grain 
size from 88  down to 72  μm. The identified changes in 
nitrogen-assisted lattice expansion and a decrease in grain 
size during interstitial alloying lead to an increase in the 
strength characteristics of the alloy in the as-solidified state, 
while maintaining good ductility characteristics (Table  1). 
In particular, an increase in nitrogen concentration by 
0.43  at.% leads to an increase in the yield strength (YS) 
in 55  MPa and gives value of solid-solution hardening 
ΔYS / ΔC =128  MPa / at.% in [37]. The alloying of Cantor 
alloy by 1 at.% of nitrogen in [47, 55] is accompanied by an 
increase in the YS by about 100 MPa and gives accordingly 
ΔYS / ΔC =100  MPa / at.%. So, for as-solidified interstitially-
hardened alloys there is a problem to calculate the effect of 
solid-solution hardening with high accuracy because of the 
limited number of data and differences between them.
The disadvantages of the interstitial Cantor alloy in 
as-solidified state include coarse grain structure, which 
typically possesses a crystallization texture, and the presence 
of a dendritic microstructure (Fig. 5) [37, 47, 55]. Therefore, 
as-cast materials require homogenizing thermomechanical 
treatments, the consequence of which will be not only the 
homogenization of the chemical composition over the volume 
of the material, but also a decrease in grain size. It should be 
accompanied by an increase in the strength characteristics 
of the alloy along with solid solution strengthening because

Thus, Y .  Han et al [56] compared the mechanical 
characteristics of the equiatomic Cantor alloy and one 
containing 0.52  at.% nitrogen, both alloys have an 
ultrafine-grained (UFG) or fine-grained (FG) structure. 
The original ingots of two alloys were subjected to multi-
stage thermomechanical processing, which consisted of 
homogenization annealing for two hours at temperature 
of 1200°C, hot forging at temperature of 1050 –1150°C 
followed by two-hour annealing at 1100°C, and cold rolling 
to 80 % reduction. To form the ultrafine-grained (grain size 
0.94 – 0.97 µm) and fine-grained (grain size 7.46 – 8.09 µm) 
structures, after rolling alloys were subjected to recrystallization 
annealing with appropriate parameters (5  minutes at 
875°C and 7.5  minutes at 1000°C for the Cantor alloy and 
2 and 30 minutes at 900°C for the N-alloyed samples). The 
homogenized samples of both alloys were characterized by a 
single-phase fcc structure and did not contain nitride particles 
and σ-phase. The authors showed that alloying the Cantor 
alloy with 0.52 at.% nitrogen leads to an increase in the YS, 
and ductility remains high (Table 1). Since the work excludes 
the influence of grain size on the strength characteristics (the 
Hall-Petch effect), we can conclude that the hardening of the 
alloy is caused exclusively by solid solution strengthening by 
nitrogen with the slope ΔYS / ΔC = 335  MPa / at.% for UFG 
state and ΔYS / ΔC = 204 MPa / at.% for FG state. The obtained 
values are fundamentally greater than those given above for 
as-solidified alloys, and it is not clear why they differ so much 
from each other.
A coarse-grained single-phase fcc structures with close

1200°C, cold rolling with 80 % reduction, and final two-
hour recrystallization annealing at 1200°C [17] (Table  1).

A non-standard method of homogenization and 
refinement of the grain structure of Cantor's nitrogen alloy 
(1 at.% nitrogen) was proposed by F . Xiong [47]. The authors 
propose to use the FSP technology for these purposes. In 
particular, it is shown that alloying the Cantor alloy with 
nitrogen and refining the grain structure by FSP technology 
makes it possible to obtain a homogeneous alloy with an 
fcc structure and a grain size of 2.5  μm with high strength 
and ductility (YS = 493  MPa, tensile strength  — 832  MPa, 
elongation to failure — 33 %). This effect is achieved due to 
solid solution strengthening with nitrogen atoms and grain 
refinement during the FSP treatment. The authors reported 
that presence of nitrogen promotes grain refinement during 
the FSP as compared to the interstitial-free alloy by increasing 
the rate of formation of crystallization centers and reducing 
the rate of grain growth during dynamic recrystallization. It 
is surprisingly that quite high values of the YS were obtained 
for Cantor alloys processed using FSP technology [47]. A 
comparison of the data in Table  1 for different alloys with 
similar grain sizes, obtained by different authors, shows 
significantly different strength characteristics in similar alloys 
(Table 1). For example, in [56], a nitrogen-free alloy with a 
grain size of 8 μm has the YS = 260 MPa, while in [47], the 
same alloy with a grain size of 8 μm shows YS-values close to 
400 MPa. A similar deviation can be noted for the nitrogen-
doped alloys. This difference may arise due to the presence 
of tungsten impurities in the alloys processed by FSP , which 
is typical in the case of friction stir welding technology and 
is associated with wear of the processing tool. Apparently, 
tungsten-based particles in this case activate the mechanism 
of dispersion strengthening of the alloy in addition to solid 
solution hardening.

alloy in the temperature range from 77 to 1073 K. Their widely 
recognized work describes that during the initial stages of 
tensile deformation (up to 2 % strain), deformation occurs 
by planar dislocation glide on the normal fcc slip system 
<110>{111}. They observed undissociated and dissociated 
a / 2<110> dislocations, stacking faults and a / 6<112> Shockley 
partials at low strain (up to the 2 %). But at later deformation 
stages (about 20 % strain), they noted nanoscale deformation 
twins after interrupted tests at 77 K, but not in specimens tested 
at room temperature, where plasticity occurred exclusively by 
dislocation slip (with tendency to form a cellular dislocation 
arrangement) [14]. F .  Otto [14] described that deformation 
twinning in low-temperature range (77 K) decreases the mean 
free path of dislocations during tensile testing (“dynamic 
Hall−Petch”), enhances work hardening, prevents the early 
onset of necking instability and assists high ductility of the 
alloy in low-temperature deformation regimes.
M.  Klimova et al. [37] examined the structure 
and tensile mechanical properties of the single phase 
CoCrFeMnNi high entropy alloys doped with different 
amounts of N (0.5 –1.0  at.%) in the as-cast condition, two 
sets of experiments were performed  — at 77  K and room 
temperature. The authors reported that plastic deformation 
of the N-doped alloys at 77 and 293 K was mostly associated 
with planar dislocation slip, and mechanical twinning was 
not revealed even at 77 K [37]. Astafurova et al. [17] studied 
the effect of nitrogen-alloying (0.8, 1.4 and 1.6 at.% N) on the 
microstructure, temperature-dependent strain hardening and 
deformation mechanisms of a homogenized FeMnNiCoCr 
high-entropy alloy under tensile loading in the temperature 
range 77 – 473  K. They confirmed that nitrogen-containing 
alloys possess lower activity of the mechanical twinning 
relative to the interstitial-free Cantor alloy despite their close 
stacking fault energies (about 30  mJ / m2). Dislocation slip 
was confirmed as a dominating deformation mechanism of 
the nitrogen-doped alloys along the whole interval of the 
deformation temperatures and nitrogen concentrations. The 
important result was that in the absence or low twinning 
activity in nitrogen-bearing alloys, their work hardening was 
visibly higher than in interstitial-free Cantor alloy. Therefore, 
nitrogen alloying enhances the tendency to the planar glide, 
and planar dislocation arrays and multipoles facilitate strain 
hardening of the N-doped alloys (Figs. 7 and 8). Analysis of 
the dislocation arrangement testifies the important role of the 
short-range ordering in deformation behavior of interstitially 
hardened Cantor alloy [17, 37, 46]. Y . Han [42] also noted that 
at strains lower than 20 % dislocation slip dominates (tension, 
room temperature), and nitrogen alloying increased the total 
dislocation density and the strain-hardening rate during 
deformation, which could be explained by the interaction 
between nitrogen atoms and dislocations.
The development of martensitic transformations during 
plastic deformation of Cantor alloy or nitrogen alloys based 
on it was not reported till now. Cantor alloy is described as a 
stable austenitic alloy. And the above data on the nitrogen-
assisted increase in SFE suggests that nitrogen alloys can be 
resistant to transformation even under large strain or severe 
plastic deformation.

3.2. Precipitate hardening and the influence of heat 
treatment on the microstructure of the nitrogen‑doped 
Cantor alloy
Depending on the fabrication method and parameters of the 
thermomechanical treatments, Cantor equiatomic alloy may 
have not only a homogeneous fcc structure, but the formation 
of σ-phase particles is possible in its structure [59]. Alloying 
the CoCrFeMnNi alloy with nitrogen, especially with 
concentrations higher than 0.8 at.%, leads to the formation 
of a two-phase structure consisting of an fcc matrix and M2N 
nitride particles with an hcp crystal lattice, mainly based on 
chromium [20, 29, 37, 47, 59]. Nitrides can be formed from 
a melt during the solidification process (at high nitrogen 
concentrations [29, 37]) or precipitate from a supersaturated 
solid solution according to the mechanism of continuous 
decomposition [46]. Due to the lower mixing enthalpy, the 
formation of chromium nitrides is more favorable than the 
formation of the Cr-based σ-phase [46,59].
The formation of second-phase particles in as-solidified 
Cantor alloy doped with nitrogen is noted in [30, 37, 55]. In 
Cantor alloy with 1.6 at.% N [55] or 2.2 at.% N [37], grain-
boundary particles of chromium-based nitrides form, this is 
accompanied by a decrease in the proportion of interdendritic 
layers and grain size. A synergetic effect of solid-solution 
strengthening with nitrogen atoms and dispersion hardening 
due to the formation of nitride particles provides even greater 
strengthening relative to interstitial-free and nitrogen-alloyed 
single-phase alloys (Tables 1 and 2).
Paper of D. E.  Jodi et al. [59] was one of the earliest 
studies devoted to the nitrogen alloying of the Cantor alloy. 
They compared structures and mechanical properties of pure 
CoCrFeMnNi alloy and that doped with 0.95 at.% nitrogen. 
Samples of both alloys were obtained from the ingots using 
thermomechanical treatment: annealing at a temperature 
of 1200°C for 24 hours, cold rolling with reduction of 90 %, 
and subsequent 1 h-annealing at 700°C.  The structure of 
both alloys consisted of the fcc matrix with submicron 
grain size and σ-phase particles (for nitrogen-free alloy) or 
chromium nitrides for the CoCrFeMnNi-N alloy (Table  2). 
The alloy doped with nitrogen has a slightly smaller grain 
size compared to the equiatomic alloy (0.94  μm versus 
0.97  μm). The authors of the work [59] did not estimate 
the particle sizes, but according to the microscopic images 
presented in the work, the sizes of nitrides and the σ-phase 
amounted to be about 100 nm. Together with the specified 
parameters of thermomechanical processing, alloying the 
equiatomic Cantor alloy with 0.95 at.% nitrogen leads to an 
increase in the yield strength and tensile strength by 57 and 
67 MPa, respectively. The work does not analyze the reasons 
for this, however, based on the close size of the particles of 
the second phase and their contents, it can be concluded that 
solid solution strengthening of the austenitic matrix with 
nitrogen atoms gives the main contribution to the increase 
in the mechanical characteristics of the nitrogen-doped alloy 
relative to the UFG Cantor alloy. At the same time, high 
strength characteristics of both UFG alloys, interstitial-free 
and N-doped, proves high efficiency of grain refinement and 
particle strengthening by σ-phase and nitrides.

F .  Xiong et al. [36] fabricated CoCrFeMnNi-1 at.% N 
alloy, homogenized it and processed by cold rolling with 
a series of post-deformation anneals (at 500 – 900°C for 
1  h). Under rolling of Cantor alloy doped with nitrogen, 
deformation develops by dislocation slip in contrast to the 
interstitial-free alloy, in which large portion of twinning and 
shear bands form. The authors suppose that nitrogen alloying 
suppresses deformation twinning in Cantor alloy due to the 
increased stacking fault energy (SFE) of the alloy, which is in 
agreement with data in [17, 37]. Annealing at temperatures 
of 500 – 600°C leads to the partial annihilation of twins in 
the Cantor alloy, while the structure of the nitrogen-bearing 
alloy remains stable [36]. Heat treatments at temperatures 
above 700°C is accompanied by recrystallization in both 
alloys and the formation of chromium nitrides in the 
N-doped alloy. Moreover, regardless of the annealing 
temperature, the grain size in the nitrogen-containing alloy 
is lower than that in the interstitial-free Cantor alloy. On the 
opinion of the authors, this fact is associated with the action 
of a number of mechanisms. First, nitrogen atoms impede 
the movement of dislocations, which leads to an increase 
in the stored strain energy during cold rolling and, as a 
consequence, an increase in the rate of nucleation during 
recrystallization. Secondly, nitrogen increases the SFE, gets 
dislocation glide and dislocation annihilation easier during 
the heat treatment. These factors reduce the driving force 
required for the growth of recrystallized grains in alloys 
with nitrogen [36]. The structural transformations revealed 
for the interstitial-free and nitrogen-doped (1  at.%  N) 
alloys affect their mechanical characteristics. Thus, an 
increase in the annealing temperature leads to a decrease 
in the strength characteristics of the basic Cantor alloy. At 
the same time, the alloy with nitrogen is characterized by 
higher strength regardless of the processing temperature 
and does not exhibit a significant loss of ductility. In this 
case, the greatest increase in the yield strength and tensile 
strength reported for the nitrogen-alloyed Cantor alloy 
annealed at temperatures of 500 and 600°C. However, alloys 
after such heat treatments are characterized by low ductility 
(elongation to failure is less than 5 %). The authors reported 
that the better combination of strength and ductility was 
reached for the nitrogen-alloyed samples after cold rolling 
and annealing at 700°C (YS = 872  MPa, El ≈10 %, Table  2). 
At higher annealing temperatures (800 and 900°C), there is 
a significant decrease in the strength characteristics of the 
alloy with nitrogen while ductility increases [36].
A. Semenyuk et al. [38] and J. Zhang et al. [45] used similar 
approach for the design of the microstructure in Cantor alloys 
(cold rolling and anneal). A. Semenyuk et al. [38] reported 
attractive combinations of mechanical properties in UFG and 
FG heterophase samples of the alloys with 1.0 and 2.0 at.% N 
(Table  2). The best combination of “strength / ductility” was 
revealed in alloy with 2.0 at.% N after rolling and annealing 
at 700°C (grain size 0.7 μm, particles size — 55 nm, particle 
fraction  — 5.4 %): YS = 985  MPa, El =12 %. Contrarily to 
[38], where post-deformation annealing was performed in 
wide temperature interval 700 –1000°C, J.  Zhang et al. [45] 
used a short-term anneal (3 min) at 900°C. As a result, just 
a partially recrystallized microstructure was observed, and 
the estimation of the precipitation hardening is getting a 
non-trivial task. Nevertheless, high strength properties were 
obtained in [45] using a complex multi-mechanism hardening 
(nitrogen solid-solution strengthening, precipitation 
strengthening and grain refinement strengthening): YS is 
slightly above 600 MPa and El about 35 % in alloy with 3 at.% 
of nitrogen.
Thus, the formation of nitride particles in CoCrFeMnNi-N 
nitrogen-doped alloys can give an additional increase 
in their strength characteristics due to the activation of 
the precipitation hardening and particle-related grain 
refinement. The authors of the works [38, 45, 47] noted that 
high strength properties are the result of a complex multi-
mechanisms hardening, but the positive effect of precipitates 
cannot be overestimated as particles influence all material’s 
characteristics: strength, plasticity, ductility, recrystallization, 
deformation mechanism, etc.
4. Deformation mechanisms and mechanical 
properties of the nitrogen-bearing 
three- and four-component alloys
The advantage of Cantor's equiatomic alloy is its unique 
strength characteristics and ductility at cryogenic 
temperatures [2, 8,11–18], while at room temperature 
deformation this material has a low yield strength. 
Deviations from the chemical composition of the classical 
equiatomic Cantor alloy and the exclusion of certain 
chemical elements from it open up a wide opportunity for 
obtaining new materials with unique characteristics [60]. 
Thus, the metastable alloy Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10 is characterized 
by high work hardening and a good combination of strength 
and ductility due to their affinity to phase transformations 
(Transformation-Induced Plasticity, TRIP effect) [61]. 
Low SFE (10 –15  mJ / m2) is responsible for the tendency 
of such alloys to undergo martensitic transformations and 
active development of twinning, which distinguishes them 
from the equiatomic Cantor alloy. Like the Cantor alloy, 
these materials are characterized by a low yield strength at 
room temperature (<250 MPa) [60 – 62]. Therefore, alloying 
with interstitial atoms may be a promising method for 
strengthening such alloys.
Y .  Han et al. [63] studied the Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10 alloy 
doped with 1.15 at.% nitrogen. Specimens were characterized 
by a single-phase fcc structure without nitride particles. 
Alloying with nitrogen led to a decrease in the average grain 
size of the alloy from 18.8 to 14.7 μm and an increase in the 
lattice parameter from 0.3607  to 0.3612  nm. The authors 
showed that alloying with nitrogen leads to a change in the 
mechanism of hardening of the alloy from plasticity induced 
by phase transformations (TRIP effect, Transformation 
Induced Plasticity) to twinning (TWIP effect, Twinning 
Induced Plasticity). This is accompanied by an almost 
twofold increase in the yield strength of the alloy and 
increasing elongation to failure up to 10 %. For the nitrogen-
doped alloy, the high yield strength is mainly resulted from

Z.  He et al. [41] studied the effect of nitrogen alloying 
in a wide range of concentrations (0.5  to 3.2  at.%) on 
the microstructure and mechanical properties of the 
Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 alloy. The author of the work showed that 
an increase in the nitrogen content is accompanied with 
increase in the lattice constant from 3.612  to 3.628  nm, 
and a proportional increase in the yield strength with a 
coefficient of 144  MPa / at.% N was revealed. Using the 
Fe45.9Mn30.4Co10.5Cr10.6N2.6 alloy (with 2.6  at.%  nitrogen), 
the effect of various thermomechanical treatments on 
the structure and mechanical properties of the alloy was 
studied in detail. In particular, heat treatment, consisting 
of hot forging and hot rolling, homogenizing annealing at 
temperature of 1200°C followed by cold rolling with a 60 % 
upset and a short three-minute annealing at a temperature 
of 750°C (below the recrystallization temperature), allows 
one to obtain an alloy with ultra-high strength characteristics 
combined with good ductility. The yield strength of the alloy 
after the specific heat treatment is 1310  MPa (more than 
5  times higher than that in the coarse-grained interstitial-
free alloy), and the elongation to failure is 16.5 %. This unique 
result was obtained due to the combined action of a number 
of mechanisms: solid solution strengthening with nitrogen, 
the formation during deformation of a two-phase structure 
consisting of lamellas of bcc martensite (24  vol.%) in the 
fcc plastic matrix of austenite (this mechanism is similar to 
dispersion hardening), dislocation strengthening due to the 
presence of non-recrystallized austenitic regions, dispersion 
strengthening by nanoparticles of chromium nitrides, grain-
boundary strengthening due to the formation of small 
austenite grains (about 0.8  μm in size) in areas in which 
recrystallization of the material occurred (the Hall-Petch 
effect) and strengthening due to the formation of deformation 
nanotwins.
In [40], Z. He et al. studied the effect of nitrogen alloying 
on the mechanical properties of Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10 alloy at 
cryogenic temperatures. For the studies, alloys without and 
with 1, 1.8 and 2.6 at.% nitrogen were fabricated, subjected 
to thermomechanical treatment similar to that used in 
[41], but the final three-minute annealing was carried 
out at a temperature of 900°C (above the recrystallization 
temperature) to obtain a completely recrystallized structures. 
Authors obtained a series of samples with 0, 1, 1.8  and 
2.6 at.% nitrogen and grain sizes of 6.2, 5.8, 5.3 and 4.5 μm, 
respectively. In this case, the alloy without nitrogen was 
initially (before deformation) characterized by a two-phase 
structure consisting of 37 % ε-martensite and 63 % austenite. 
The nitrogen-bearing samples had a pure austenitic structure. 
Under tension at 77 K, an increase in nitrogen concentration 
from 0 to 2.6 at.% leads to an increase in the yield strength 
of the alloy from 398  to 1323  MPa, the ultimate tensile 
strength varies from 1206  to 1620  MPa, and the value of 
ultimate elongation before rupture decreases from 53 to 11 %. 
Moreover, the alloy with 1 at.% nitrogen is characterized by the 
best combination of strength properties and ductility when 
tested at cryogenic temperature of 77 K: the YS, UTS and El 
are 1078 MPa, 1630 MPa and 33.5 %, respectively. The authors 
of [40] associate this strengthening of the Fe49Mn30Co10Cr10N1 
alloy with a refinement of the structure due to the activation 
of the mechanisms of athermal phase transformations and 
deformation twinning that occur in elastic regime together 
with the increase in lattice friction at 77  K. As the strain 
increases, the dominating deformation mechanism changes 
from phase transformations (up to 11 % strain) to mechanical 
twinning (at higher strains). High values of plasticity are 
associated with the joint contribution from dislocation slip, 
TWIP and TRIP effects. The authors also noted that alloying 
the alloy with nitrogen increases the SFE of the alloy and 
stabilizes the austenitic structure. At cryogenic temperatures, 
the Fe 49Mn30Co10Cr10N1 alloy becomes metastable and the 
TWIP and TRIP effects are activated in it. Refinement of the 
structure due to the action of these effects, coupled with solid 
solution strengthening, leads to significant strengthening 
of the alloy during low-temperature deformation. These 
results directly show the similarity in nitrogen-alloying of 
the stable equiatomic alloys CoCrFeMnNi and metastable 
Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 alloy: nitrogen increases SFE of the 
alloys and suppresses martensitic transformation and 
twinning in them [17, 37, 40]. Because the initial SFE of the 
Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 alloy is lower than that of the Cantor alloy, 
twinning still occurs in the nitrogen-doped alloy.
In [64, 65] authors carried out the joint alloying of the 
Fe50Mn30Co10Cr10 alloy with 0.5  at.% carbon and 1  at.% 
nitrogen. Samples of the Fe 48.5Mn30Co10Cr10C0.5N1 alloy 
were obtained from the original ingots by multi-stage 
thermomechanical processing, consisting of hot forging 
and hot rolling, two-hour homogenization at 1200°C, cold 
rolling with reduction of more than 60 % and final anneal 
in the temperature range 500 – 900°C [64] and 600 – 900°C 
[65]. The authors noted that joint alloying with nitrogen 
and carbon increases the strength characteristics of the 
alloys. At the same time, W .  Zhang et al. [65] obtained the 
N-alloyed samples with fcc structure, completely stable to 
phase transformations, and a small amount of chromium 
carbonitrides (about 50  nm in size) in contrast to the 
interstitial-free alloy: in N-dopes specimens martensite was 
not detected either in the initial samples (homogenized) or 
after their deformation until failure. The best combination of 
strength and ductility (YS = 753  MPa, UTS =1038  MPa and 
El = 48 %) was obtained in the alloy after final annealing at 
700°C. After such heat treatment, a completely recrystallized 
structure with a grain size of about 4 μm is obtained. Above 
mechanical characteristics are significantly higher than 
those of interstitial-free alloy with the similar grain size. 
This allowed the authors [65] to conclude that solid solution 
strengthening with nitrogen and carbon atoms, along with 
the action of the dispersion hardening mechanism (from 
chromium carbonitride nanoparticles), makes a significant 
contribution to increasing the strength characteristics of the 
multicomponent alloys. W . Jiang et al. [64] achieved the best 
combination of strength and ductility after final annealing at 
temperature of 650°C (YS = 984 MPa, El = 20 %). The authors 
emphasize that the main contributions to the strengthening 
of the material are solid solution strengthening by nitrogen 
and carbon atoms, the formation of an ultrafine-grained 
structure in the alloy with a grain size of 910 nm (the Hall-

I. Moravcik and coauthors [39] investigated the possibility 
of increasing the strength characteristics of the medium-

work examines alloys with different grain sizes obtained using 
thermomechanical treatments (homogenizing annealing, hot

nitrogen increases lattice constant of the alloy from 0.3568 to 
0.3572  nm. With comparable grain sizes, the nitrogen-

and similar ductility compared to the N-free alloy: 375 MPa 
versus 282 MPa (grain size of ≈45 μm) and 703 MPa versus 
565 MPa (grain size of 2.6 μm), respectively for N-free and 
0.5 at.% N alloys. The authors of the work attribute this effect 
to strong solid-solution strengthening by nitrogen atoms.
K. S.  Chung et al. [58] compared the effect of alloying 
with carbon and nitrogen on the structure and mechanical 
properties of the FeCrCoNi alloy. They shown that at the 
same concentrations of interstitial atoms (0.5 at.%), C- and 
N-doped alloys have fcc structure, while nitrogen provides 
greater increase in the lattice constant of the fcc phase 
compared to carbon (for interstitial-free alloy a = 0.35729 nm, 
FeCrCoNi-C alloy — a = 0.35752 nm, FeCrCoNi-N alloy — 
a = 0.35771 nm). Alloying with carbon also leads to an increase 
in grain size from 192 to 217 μm, but the nitrogen-bearing 
alloy is characterized by the smallest grain size of 130  μm. 
It is important that the FeCrCoNi-0.5 at.% N alloy does not 
contain nitride particles, but a small amount of chromium-
based carbide particles forms in the FeCrCoNi-0.5  at.%  C 
alloy. As a result, the alloy with nitrogen demonstrates higher 
strength characteristics than that with carbon: alloying the 
FeCrCoNi alloy with 0.5 at.% carbon and nitrogen leads to 
an increase in the compressive YS from 132 MPa to 152 and 
202 MPa, respectively.
Authors of [48] investigate influence of N doping on the 
mechanical properties of FeCrCoNi medium entropy alloy 
produced by selective laser melting. Both FeCrCoNi and 
FeCrCoNiN (with 1.5 at.% of N) were characterized by single 
phase austenitic structure. It was shown alloy doped with 
nitrogen was characterized by finer grain structure (grain size 
in FeCrCoNiN alloy was about 30 % lower than in FeCrCoNi 
one) and higher strength and ductility in comparison 
with nitrogen free alloy (YS = 725  MPa, UTS = 850  MPa 
and El = 45 % for FeCrCoNiN alloy and YS = 630  MPa, 
UTS = 740 MPa and El = 40 % for FeCrCoNi one). It should 
be noted that strength characteristics of FeCrCoNiN alloy 
produced by selective laser melting in [48] is higher than 
in work [58] (725  MPa and 202  MPa, respectively), where 
material was produced using arc melting in argon atmosphere. 
The possible reasons for this are finer grain size (43.5 µm for 
additively manufactured alloy vs. 130 µm for conventionally 
produced one) and higher nitrogen content in the additively-
fabricated alloy (1.5 at.% for [48] and 0.5 at.% for [58]).
In general, the nitrogen-induced changes in the phase 
composition, microstructure and the mechanisms of its 
formation, deformation mechanisms in three- and four-
component alloys are much similar to those obtained for 
the Cantor alloy. At the same time, this direction should be 
developed, since the influence of nitrogen in nonequiatomic 
or medium-entropy alloys is more pronounced due to the fact 
that they often exhibit a tendency to phase transformations. 
Therefore, this direction can be considered as one of the most 
promising for further development.
5. The influence of nitrogen alloying on 
other properties of multicomponent 
CoCrFeMnNi alloys
T. Lindner et al. [51] applied low-temperature nitrocarburization 
for surface hardening of the CrMnFeCoNi and Mn-free 
CrFeCoNi alloys and proved the prospective of the interstitial 
hardening for the material group of high-entropy alloy: an 
interstitially hardened surface diffusion layers improve the wear 
resistance of the alloys without the deterioration in corrosion 
properties. High-entropy nitrides can be considered as a 
limiting case of the formation of “high-entropy alloy-nitrogen” 
compositions. The nitride high entropy films are excellent in 
mechanical, tribological properties, and chemical stabilities 
[52]. Due to the relatively short history of the multicomponent 
alloys and, especially iHEAs, their mechanical properties have 
been mainly studied. Data on other properties are currently 
limited.
6. Summary and perspectives
Starting from the 2004, multicomponent alloys attract much 
attention of the scientists. Despite the fact that deformation 
behavior and deformation mechanisms of the multicomponent 
CoCrFeMnNi system in some manner similar to those in 
austenitic alloys and steels, now we can confidently say that 
multicomponent systems have emerged as a separate class 
of materials, the properties of which in some cases exceed 
the properties of conventional alloys. Two decades is a 
rather short period of time to reveal all the advantages of 
a new class of materials, if only because multicomponent 
alloys provide a huge compositional space for the design of 
structure and properties, which are quite difficult to imagine. 
Interstitial alloys based on multicomponent CoCrFeMnNi 
alloys or other multi-principal-elements systems claim to 
form a separate direction in this area of research. A review 
of the literature on strengthening the family of Cantor 
alloys with nitrogen atoms showed the prospects of the 
chosen direction: alloying with nitrogen makes it possible 
to implement complex strengthening mechanisms in alloys 
and obtain high strength properties in a wide temperature 
range. Among the promising directions for the development 
of multicomponent interstitial alloys, the following should 
be noted:
− Alloys with the complex C, N, and B co-doping.
− N-alloying of the multicomponent alloys with low SFE 
alloy which undergo martensitic transformations.
− Additive manufacturing of the interstitial 
multicomponent alloys.
− Using of the N-alloying for the surface hardening of the 
multi-principal-element alloys, N-doped multicomponent 
coatings and formation of heterostructures in bulk materials 
with gradient nitrogen distribution.
