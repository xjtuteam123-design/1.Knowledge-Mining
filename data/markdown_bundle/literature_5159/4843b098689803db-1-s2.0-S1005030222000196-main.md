Nanocrystalline (NC) W metals and alloys often exhibit higher radiation tolerance and strength than their 
coarse-grained counterparts. However, their thermal stability is low, making it diﬃcult to achieve bulk NC 
W metals and alloys by consolidation using conventional techniques such as pressure-less sintering, hot- 
explosive-compaction sintering, and spark plasma sintering. Here we report the synthesis and mechanical 
properties of bulk NC W 100- x Ti x ( x = 10 at.%–30 at.%) alloys prepared by consolidating mechanically al- 
loyed NC powders under a high-temperature/high-pressure condition. Adding 20 at.%–30 at.% Ti largely 
improves the sinterability of NC W-Ti alloy powders. The room-temperature microhardness and compres- 
sive yield strength of consolidated bulk NC W 80 Ti 20 alloy are ∼ 16.9 and 6.0 GPa, respectively, which 
are mainly caused by grain boundary strengthening and signiﬁcantly higher than those of previously re- 
ported W and W alloys. The ultimate compressive strength of bulk NC W 80 Ti 20 measured between 900 
and 1100 °C deceases with increasing temperature. This behavior can be explained by the activation of 
Rachinger grain boundary sliding. No grain growth is observed in bulk NC W 80 Ti 20 after compression at 
10 0 0 °C. Theoretical calculation suggests that it is the segregation of Ti at grain boundaries that decreases 
the speciﬁc grain boundary free energy and makes the NC W 80 Ti 20 alloy thermodynamically stable.

1. Introduction 
Tungsten and its alloys have been considered as potential 
plasma facing materials (PFMs) for the International Thermonu- 
clear Experimental Reactor because of their high melting temper- 
ature, good thermal conductivity, and low sputtering rates [1–6] . 
Due to the harsh working conditions of irradiation and high tem- 
perature, coarse-grained W can hardly meet the working require- 
ments of PFMs. Thus, much attention has been paid to NC W be- 
cause of the enhanced radiation tolerance and mechanical proper- 
ties [7–11] . These NC W materials are prepared by high-pressure 
torsion [ 7 , 8 ], magnetron deposition [10] , and surface deformation 
by machining [11] . High density grain boundaries are helpful to 
annihilating freely-migrating point defects in irradiated NC mate- 
rials [ 12 , 13 ]. In addition, rapid and eﬃcient dislocation loop ab- 
sorption by the grain boundaries, which is considered to be the 
dominant factor for mass transport to the boundaries during irra-

NC materials [15] . Thus, the thermal stability of NC W should be 
improved. 
Grain growth can be suppressed by both kinetic and thermo- 
dynamic approaches. Kinetically, grain boundaries (GBs) can be 
pinned by second-phase particle pinning (Zener pinning) [16] . The 
second-phase oxide particles, such as Y 2 O 3 , Hf 2 O 3 and La 2 O 3 , 
added into the W matrix are one of very effective method to de- 
crease the grain size and improve the mechanical properties [17–
19] . The addition of 1% Y 2 O 3 into W matrix lowers the average 
grain size from 3.2 to 0.7 μm, and particularly, the hardness of 
W-1%Y 2 O 3 is 6.91 GPa, 30% higher than that of pure W [17] . The 
second-phase particles can also be in-situ formed by adding ap- 
propriate alloying elements. For example, Y, Cr, La, and Ti elements 
have been added into W matrix [20–27] . The Gibbs free energy for 
the formation of oxides with these alloying elements is more nega- 
tive than that for the formation of tungsten oxide [28] . Thus, these 
alloying elements are likely bonded with oxygen, which mainly re-

Thermodynamically, the driving force for grain growth can be 
lowered by the GB segregation of solutes, which in turn reduces 
the speciﬁc GB energy [29–31] . It has been found [32] that the 
segregation of elemental Ti at GBs can largely increase the thermal 
stability of NC W 80 Ti 20 alloy powders. The average grain size of 
mechanical alloyed W 80 Ti 20 powders only slightly increases from 
22 to 24 nm after annealing at 1100 °C under an argon atmosphere 
for one week [32] . The NC W-Ti alloy powders prepared by me- 
chanical alloying [ 25 , 33–35 ] and the W-Ti alloy nanoparticles pre- 
pared by a soft chemical approach [36] have been consolidated by 
pressure-less sintering [ 25 , 33 , 36 ], hot-explosive-compaction sinter- 
ing [34] , and spark plasma sintering (SPS) [35] at a tempera- 
ture of above 10 0 0 °C. However, the consolidated alloys are often 
ultraﬁne-grained [ 25 , 35 ] or even coarse-grained [ 33 , 34 , 36 ]. Thus, 
the hardness of consolidated W-Ti alloys is quite low and located 
between ∼2.3 and 8.9 GPa [ 25 , 33–36 ], which is close to that of 
other W alloys ( ∼ 3 to 8.2 GPa) [37–39] . 
In this work, we report the synthesis of bulk NC W 100- x Ti x 
( x = 10, 20, 30) alloys by mechanical alloying and high- 
temperature/high-pressure consolidation techniques. The grain size 
of consolidated W-Ti alloys ranges from 42 to 81 nm. The micro- 
hardness and compressive strength of consolidated NC W 80 Ti 20 al- 
loy are as high as 16.9 and 6.0 GPa, respectively. Microstructural 
analysis and thermodynamic calculations are performed to under- 
stand the origins of the high thermal stability of NC W-Ti alloys. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Preparation of samples 
Four W 100- x Ti x ( x = 0 at.%, 10 at.%, 20 at.%, and 30 at.%) ma- 
terials were synthesized. The starting materials were W (99.95% 
pure, 3 μm) and Ti (99.99% pure, 48 μm) powders. The W 100- x Ti x 
powders were mechanically alloyed in a SPEX 80 0 0D high-energy 
shaker ball mill (SPEX SamplePrep) with a ball to powder weight 
ratio of about 3:1. Tungsten carbide vial and balls (6.5 mm in di- 
ameter and 2 g in mass) were used for the mechanical alloying 
(MA). After MA for 24 h, the powders were pressed into rods with 
a diameter of 10 mm and a length of 10 mm at 10 MPa. To pre- 
vent the oxidation of the milled powders, the MA and pressing 
were performed in a glove box ﬁlled with argon (99.9999% purity). 
Then, the pressed rods were inserted into pure h-BN crucibles. The 
crucible was mounted in a graphite furnace and compressed in a 
large volume cubic anvil at a selected temperature between 700 
and 1200 °C with a pressure of 4 GPa for 0.5 h. 
2.2. Measurement and analysis method 
The relative density was calculated by dividing the actual den- 
sity by the theoretical density. The actual density of the alloys 
was obtained by averaging the density of three samples measured 
with an Archimedes’ immersion method with an accuracy of ±
0.0 0 01 g/cm 3 . The theoretical density of the alloys was obtained by 
weighting the density of tungsten and titanium based on the com- 
position of the W-Ti alloys. Hardness of the bulk W 100-x Ti x alloys 
was measured using a Vickers microhardness tester (FUTURE-TECH, 
FM-ARS90 0 0) with a load of 9.8 N for 15 s. Ten indentations were 
made on each sample. Room-temperature compression for the bulk 
NC W 80 Ti 20 alloy at a displacement rate of 10 nm/s was performed 
using A Hysitron PI95 TEM (Transmission Electron Microscope) Pi- 
coindenter system inside a FEI Titan environmental TEM operated 
at 300 keV. A nanopillar with a diameter of 240 nm and a length 
of 10 0 0 nm was used for the room-temperature compression test. 
The nanopillar was micro-machined using a FEI helios G4 cx Dual 
Beam focused ion beam (FIB) from the consolidated NC bulk. The 
high-temperature compression for the bulk NC W 80 Ti 20 alloys was 
carried out in a RDL50 electronic creep testing machine (Sinotest 
Equipment Company, China) at a constant strain rate of 10 −3 s −1 
under an atmosphere of air. Cylinders with a diameter of 2 mm 
and a height of 3 mm were used for the high-temperature com- 
pression test. 
The W 100- x Ti x specimens were characterized using a 
D/max-rB/PC X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with CuK α radiation 
( λ=0.15405 nm) at a scanning rate of 2 °/min. For the consoli- 
dated W 80 Ti 20 specimen, the Williamson-Hall equation [40] was 
used to calculate the grain size d and the lattice strain ε, which

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Characterization of mechanically alloyed powder 
Fig. 1 shows the XRD patterns of W 100- x Ti x powders after MA 
for 24 h. A single-phased, BCC-structured solid solution is formed. 
No diffraction peak of Ti can be detected after the MA, suggesting 
that Ti is dissolved into the W lattice. A trace amount of WC impu- 
rities is detected in the pure W powder, which can be attributed to 
WC peeling off from the tungsten carbide vial and balls during the 
MA. However, WC impurities are not detected in W-Ti alloy pow- 
ders. This could be caused by two reasons: (i) Ti is more ductile 
than W. Thus, Ti may be coated on the surface of WC balls and 
WC vial during the MA, resulting in a low content of WC impu- 
rities. (ii) The Gibbs free energy for the formation of TiC at room 
temperature is approx. −180 kJ mol −1 , much negative than that 
of TiC, −40 kJ mol −1 [43] . Thus, Ti can react with WC to form W 
and TiC. The crystallite size of the TiC formed by this solid state 
reaction should be small. In addition, the volume fraction of TiC 
is relatively low. As a result, the presence of TiC cannot be de- 
tected within the resolution of a XRD technique. The average grain 
size, as calculated from the (110) Bragg reﬂection using the Scher- 
rer formula [44] for the W 100- x Ti x is plotted in Fig. 2 . Both pure W 
and W-Ti alloys have an average grain size of 12.5 ± 0.5 nm. The 
lattice parameter was determined using the Nelson-Riley function 
[45] and shown in Fig. 2 . The lattice parameter increases with in- 
creasing Ti content, conﬁrming that Ti is dissolved in the W lattice. 
Note that the atomic radius of Ti is 147 pm, slightly larger than 
that of W, 139 pm. Thus, the lattice parameter of a W(Ti) solid so- 
lution should be larger than that of W. The atomic radius of Ti and 
W differs by only 5.8%, which should result in the formation of a

3.2. Characterization of consolidated bulk W 100-x Ti x 
The relative densities of consolidated W 100- x Ti x as a function of 
sintering temperature are presented in Fig. 3 . The relative density 
increases signiﬁcantly when the sintering temperature is increased 
from 700 to 900 °C. However, the relative density remains almost 
constant when the sintering temperature is increased from 900 to 
1200 °C. At the same sintering temperature, such as 900 °C, in- 
creasing the content of Ti will increase the relative density. How- 
ever, for the Ti content above 20 at.%, the relative density re- 
mains almost constant. This behavior suggests that Ti is beneﬁcial 
for promoting the sinterability. This promotion can be attributed 
to the fact the melting temperature of W 100- x Ti x alloy largely de- 
creases with x when x is between 0 and 20 at.% [46] . The rela- 
tive densities of the bulk NC W 80 Ti 20 and W 70 Ti 30 alloys reaches 
∼ 99.5% when the sintering temperature is 900 °C, indicating that 
adding 20 at.% −30 at.% Ti could largely reduce sintering temper- 
ature. No increase in the relative density of sintered W 100- x Ti x is 
observed when the sintering temperature is above 1100 °C. 
Fig. 4 shows the XRD patterns of W 100- x Ti x consolidated at 
1100 °C. The consolidated W 100- x Ti x specimens are composed of 
only one BCC-structured phase. The full widths at half maximum 
(FWHM) of the diffraction peaks for bulk W-Ti alloys are obviously 
larger than those for bulk pure W. This fact suggests that the grain 
size of W-Ti alloys is smaller than that of pure W.

3.3. Mechanical properties 
Fig. 7 shows the Vickers hardness of W 100- x Ti x sintered at var- 
ious temperatures. The hardness of W 100- x Ti x ﬁrst increases and 
then decreases with increasing sintering temperature. The maxi- 
mum hardness for W, W 90 Ti 10 , W 80 Ti 20 , and W 70 Ti 30 is 17.4 ± 0.5, 
18.1 ± 0.6, 16.9 ± 0.3, and 15.6 ± 0.3 GPa, respectively. The trend 
of hardness curve for the same sintered material can be attributed 
to the competition between relative density and grain size. In the 
low-temperature regime, the increment in hardness caused by the 
increase in relative density is higher than the decrement in hard- 
ness caused by the increase in grain size. However, in the high- 
temperature regime, the relative density does not increase sig- 
niﬁcantly whereas the grains grow signiﬁcantly, leading to the 
downward trend for the hardness curve. The hardness of sintered 
W 80 Ti 20 and W 70 Ti 30 remains almost constant for the sintering 
temperature between 900 and 1200 °C. In addition, the hardness 
of W 70 Ti 30 is slightly lower than that of W 80 Ti 20 . Thus, there is an 
optimum content of Ti that can improve both the thermal stability 
and the mechanical properties of W-Ti alloys. 
Fig. 8 shows the dependence of Vickers hardness and grain size 
on the content of Ti for W 100- x Ti x sintered at 1100 °C. The hard- 
ness ﬁrst increases and then decreases with Ti content, reaching 
the maximum hardness of 16.9 ± 0.3 GPa at 20 at.% Ti. In compar- 
ison, the grain size ﬁrst largely decreases for x = 0 at.% - 10 at.%, 
then slightly decreases for x = 10 at.% - 20 at.%, and ﬁnally slightly 
increases for x = 20 at.% - 30 at.%. The minimum grain size of 
42 ± 13 nm is achieved in W 80 Ti 20 . Table 1 summarizes the grain 
size and hardness of the present W 80 Ti 20 and previously studied 
W and W alloys. Clearly, most of previously studied W and W al- 
loys are ultraﬁne-grained or coarse-grained. The hardness of the 
present NC W 80 Ti 20 is much higher than that of W [47] , W alloys 
[ 37–39 , 48–50 ], and W-Ti alloys [ 25 , 33–36 , 51 ]. The W-0.5wt%TiC is 
nanograined [49] . However, the hardness of NC W-0.5 wt% TiC is 
only 11.6 ± 0.3 GPa, much lower than that (16.9 ± 0.3 GPa) of the 
present NC W 80 Ti 20 .

4. Discussion 
4.1. Free energy of segregation and normalized grain boundary 
energy 
Our experimental results indicate that: (1) the thermal stabil- 
ity of NC W-Ti alloys is signiﬁcantly higher than that of NC W, (2) 
the thermal stability of W-Ti alloys with different contents of Ti is 
different, and (3) Ti is segregated at the GBs of NC W 80 Ti 20 alloy. 
Chookajorn et al. [32] have provided a nanostructure stability map 
for different W-based alloys. In the present study, we calculated 
the change in the free energy of W-Ti alloys due to the segregation 
of solute Ti at GBs in order to understand the thermal stability of 
different NC W-Ti alloys. 
The W-Ti alloys were consolidated under the same pressure 
and temperature. Thus, the content of titanium should be the 
only factor that can change the microstructure of W-Ti alloys. The 
nanograins in pure W will grow at high temperatures due to large 
thermodynamic driving force caused by the high GB energy. For 
binary alloys, the GB energy is closely related to the segregation 
tendency of solute. The free energy of segregation, /Delta1G seg , is com- 
monly used to characterize the segregation tendency of solute in 
GBs. According to model proposed by Wynblatt and Ku [ 69 , 70 ],

The consolidated bulk NC W 80 Ti 20 has exceptional mechanical 
properties at both room temperature and high temperatures. The 
room-temperature microhardness is 16.9 ± 0.3 GPa, signiﬁcantly 
higher than that of previously reported W and W alloys. The com- 
pressive yield strength and ultimate compressive strength mea- 
sured at room temperature are as high as 6.0 and 6.8 GPa, respec-

are mainly caused by GB strengthening. The ultimate compres- 
sive strength measured at 90 0, 10 0 0, and 110 0 °C is 1515, 896, 
and 535 MPa, respectively, signiﬁcantly higher than those of pre- 
viously reported W and W alloys. In addition, the facture strain in- 
creases with increasing temperature. The pronounced decrease in 
the strength with increasing temperature could be caused by the 
activation of Rachinger GB sliding. 
The consolidated bulk NC W 80 Ti 20 has an extremely high struc- 
tural stability against temperature and deformation. After compres- 
sion at 10 0 0 °C, no grain growth is observed. Instead, more Ti el- 
ements are found to segregate at GBs. Theoretical calculation sug- 
gests that it is the segregation of Ti at GBs that decreases the spe- 
ciﬁc GB free energy and makes the NC W 80 Ti 20 alloy thermody- 
namically stable.
