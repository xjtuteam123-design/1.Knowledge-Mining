Abstract
The high temperature tensile properties of a Cu–4.5Ti alloy have been investigated in the temperature range from room temperature (RT) to
550 ◦C in solution treated (ST) and peak-aged (PA) conditions. Yield strength (YS) and tensile strength (TS) in solution treated condition were
found to be independent of test temperature up to 350 ◦C followed by a marginal increase at 450 ◦C and decreased, thereafter up to 550 ◦C.
In situ age hardening during testing at 450 ◦C resulting in the precipitation of ordered, metastable and coherent Cu4Ti, /H9252′phase is attributed
to the increase in strength. Coarsening of /H9252′ precipitate particles at 450 ◦C and precipitation of equilibrium phase /H9252at 550 ◦C decrease the
strengths. In the peak-aged condition, the YS and TS were found to be constant up to 350 ◦C, beyond which they decreased with increasing
test temperature. The decrease in strengths beyond 350 ◦C in the peak-aged alloy is attributed to the coarsening of /H9252′ precipitate particles,
as well as precipitation of equilibrium phase /H9252at 550 ◦C. High temperature tensile properties of Cu–4.5Ti are superior to those of Cu–Be
alloy (C17200). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis of the alloy conﬁrms the presence of metastable and coherent Cu 4Ti, /H9252′
precipitate when tested at 450◦C and both Cu4Ti, /H9252′and equilibrium precipitate Cu3Ti, /H9252at the test temperature of 550◦C in solution treated
as well as peak-aged conditions. Ductile mode of fracture was observed in both solution treated and peak-aged conditions of the alloy tested
at RT as well as at elevated temperatures, i.e. 350, 450 and 550
◦C.

1. Introduction
Cu–Ti alloys have been developed with the aim of sub-
stituting them for Cu–Be alloys. Though Cu–Be alloys are
not toxic by themselves, they do emit invisible toxic fumes
during hot working, heat treatment, machining and pickling
operations due to the toxicity of Be, which causes health
hazards such as serious chronic pulmonary illness [1].A sa
result, these operations demand special safety measures in
order to protect the health of the people working on these
alloys, which make the alloys highly expensive. Therefore,
worldwide research has been aimed at developing a substi-
tute for the noxious and high-priced Cu–Be alloys. Literature
survey indicates that Cu–Ti alloys have very good potential
as substitute for Cu–Be alloys.

2. Experimental
A 30 kg melt of Cu–Ti alloy with aimed Ti content of
4.5 wt% was made in a graphite crucible of STOKES vac-
uum induction melting (VIM) furnace at a vacuum of 4 /H9262m
and cast, after pouring at a temperature of 1230
◦C into a
graphite mould, which was allowed to cool in vacuum in the
mould chamber of the furnace. The ingot was homogenised
at 850
◦C for 24 h and analysed for Ti content. The analysed
composition of the ingot was found to be 4.5 wt% Ti. The
ingot was subsequently hot forged and rolled after soaking
at 850
◦C for 2 h into wrought products (ﬂats and rods) of
suitable size. Round tensile samples of 4 mm gauge diame-
ter and 25 mm gauge length were made from the hot rolled
rods, solution treated and aged for maximum strength. The
details of solution treatment and aging have been reported
elsewhere [7]. These specimens were tested at temperatures
250, 350, 450 and 550
◦C for tensile properties. The times
for the furnace to attain the above temperatures are differ-
ent and were measured as 0.5, 0.75, 1 and 1.5 h, respectively.
However, the tensile specimens were introduced after the fur-
nace attained the test temperature. Further, prior to testing,
the specimens were held at the above temperatures for 30 min
(in situ aging time). All the tensile tests were carried out at a
nominal strain rate of 10
−3 s−1 using an INSTRON univer-
sal testing system. Samples from the grip portion of fractured
tensile specimens were cut, polished, etched and examined
under an optical microscope for microstructure. The fracture
surfaces of the tensile tested specimens were examined under
scanning electron microscope (SEM).
For observation under transmission electron microscope,
thin slices were cut from the grip portion of tensile tested
specimens using low speed ISOMET cutting machine and
then mechanically polished to obtain 40–50 /H9262m thick slices.
Discs of 3 mm diameter punched out from these slices, were
electro-polished in a twin jet electro polisher in a solution of
30 vol% HNO
3 and 70 vol% methanol at −45 ◦C and 20 V .
TEM examination was carried out at 160 kV using a JEOL
200CX transmission electron microscope.

4. Conclusions
Yield and tensile strengths of the solution treated alloy
were found to be independent of temperature up to 350◦C fol-
lowed by a marginal increase at 450◦C and decreased, there-
after. In situ age hardening during testing at 450 ◦C result-
ing in the precipitation of ordered, metastable and coherent
Cu
4Ti, /H9252′phase is attributed to the increase in strength. Coars-
ening of /H9252′ precipitate particles as well as precipitation of
equilibrium phase /H9252decrease the strengths at test tempera-
tures greater than 450 ◦C.
In the peak-aged condition, YS and TS of the alloy
were found to be constant up to 350 ◦C beyond which they
decreased with increasing test temperature up to 550◦C. The
decrease in strengths beyond 350 ◦C in peak-aged alloy is
attributed to the coarsening of/H9252′precipitate particles as well
as precipitation of equilibrium phase /H9252at elevated tempera-
tures.
Elongation and strain hardening exponent n are nearly
constant up to the test temperature of 350 ◦C and decrease
rapidly thereafter, up to 550◦C.
TEM analysis reveals the presence of metastable and
coherent Cu4Ti, /H9252′precipitate when tested at 450◦C and both
Cu4Ti, /H9252′and equilibrium precipitate Cu3Ti, /H9252at the test tem-
perature of 550 ◦C in solution treated as well as peak-aged
conditions.
A ductile mode of fracture was observed in both solution
treated and peak-aged conditions. Size of dimples increased
with increasing test temperature.
