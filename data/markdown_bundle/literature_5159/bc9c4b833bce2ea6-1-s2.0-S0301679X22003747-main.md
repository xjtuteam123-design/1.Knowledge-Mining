1. Introduction 
With the rapid advancement of high technology, the friction and 
wear performances of materials under high temperature conditions have 
received more attention, particularly in the industries of aviation, 
aerospace, and nuclear energy. Some precise components (such as high- 
temperature sealings, and high-temperature bearings) have tribological 
features that are directly connected to the equipment ’ s dependability, 
stability, and durability in severe working conditions. 
In 2004, Yeh et al. [1] proposed a new concept of alloy system with 
multiple main elements, known as high-entropy alloys (HEAs). Four core 
effects (high-entropy effect, lattice distortion effect, hysteretic diffusion 
effect and cocktail effect) make them have better high-temperature 
mechanical properties, heat resistance, high-temperature wear resis -
tance, corrosion resistance and creep resistance than traditional alloys, 
which prompt them have a significant potential application value as 
tribological components at high temperatures [2 – 8] . Therefore, it is 
significant to develop abrasion-resistant high-entropy alloys capable of 
serving stably at high-temperature environment ( ≥ 600 
◦
C), as well as to 
further investigate their friction and wear properties. 
At present, the researches of HEAs most focus on the optimal design 
of compositions and mechanical properties, as well as fracture behaviors 
[9] , while researches on tribological properties, particularly 
high-temperature tribological properties, are still in early stage. Geng 
et al. [10] evaluated the vacuum tribological behaviors of CoCrFeNi 
HEA sliding against Inconel 718 nickel-based superalloy at different 
temperatures ranging from room temperature (RT) to 800 
◦
C. The re -
sults present that the wear rate of CoCrFeNi HEA in vacuum environ -
ment increases as the rise of the operating temperature. In the 
high-temperature vacuum environment, reduction in the 
high-temperature strength of the alloy and inability of forming a 
continuous dense oxide film leads to a dramatic decrease in wear 
resistance. Nguyen et al. [11] investigated the dry friction characteris -

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Preparation 
Commercially available Ti, V, Nb, Ta and W metal powders (30 – 50 
µ m , purity > 99.9%) were selected as raw materials in this study. Three 
types of Ti
x
VNbTaW
y 
RHEAs with different contents of Ti and W ele -
ments (TiVNbTaW0.5, TiVNbTaW and Ti1.5VNbTaW in atomic ratios), 
namely Ti1W0.5, Ti1W1 and Ti1.5W1 alloys, were prepared by powder 
mechanical ball milling mixing and spark plasma sintering. The specific 
preparation steps are as follows: (1) weighing the corresponding mass of 
metal powders according to the atomic ratio; (2) ball milling the 
weighed powders in a WC ball mill tank by using a planetary ball mill 
(QM-QX, Nanjing Nanda Instruments Co., Ltd.) with a ball-to-material 
ratio of 5:1, a rotational speed of 150 rpm and a total grinding time of 
24 h; (3) placing the mixed powders in a graphite mold and subsequently 
sintering in a spark plasma sintering furnace (Shanghai Chenhua Electric 
Furnace Co., Ltd.). The specific sintering process is as follows. First, the 
sintering temperature was raised to 1700 
◦
C with a heating rate of 
100 
◦
C/min and held for 10 min, during which no normal pressure was 
applied to the specimen surface. After that, when the temperature was 
decreased to 1600 
◦
C, a pressure of 35 MPa was applied to the specimen 
and held for 10 min, and finally cooled to room temperature.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Microstructures and compositions 
Fig. 1 shows the XRD analysis patterns of the ball-milled powders 
and sintered samples of Ti1W0.5, Ti1W1 and Ti1.5W1 alloys. It can be 
seen from Fig. 1 a and Fig. 1 b that the powders after mechanical mixing 
present clearly identifiable diffraction peaks of each element, and 
without obvious alloying phenomenon. Fig. 1 c shows that three alloys 
after spark plasma sintering have BCC1 phase, BCC2 phase and Ti par -
ticles, with no other intermetallic compounds. However, the diffraction 
pattern peak positions of the three alloys are slightly different, with the 
BCC phase diffraction peaks of Ti1W1 alloy being shifted to a higher 2 θ 
angle than that of Ti1W0.5 alloy and the BCC phase diffraction peaks of 
Ti1.5W1 alloy being shifted to a lower 2 θ angle than that of Ti1W1 alloy 
( Fig. 1 d). The shift of the diffraction pattern peak positions is related to 
the lattice distortion. Specifically, the addition of Ti with a relatively 
large atomic radius causes an increase in the lattice constant of the BCC 
phase, whereas the addition of W with a relatively small atomic radius 
causes a decrease in the lattice constant of the BCC phase ( Fig. 1 e). 
Fig. 2 shows the SEM-BSE images and the EDS analysis results of the 
three alloys. It can be seen that the distribution of each element in the 
three alloys is relatively uniform over a wide range, and there are no 
obvious defects such as holes and cracks in the materials, with the 
relative density measured by the Archimedes drainage method being 
above 98%. From the SEM-BSE images, three alloys are composed of 
BCC1 phase, BCC2 phase and Ti particles. The gray area is the BCC1 
phase enriched in Ti and Nb elements, which mainly because Ti and Nb 
elements have a complete degree of mutual solubility and tend to solid- 
solve into the same BCC phase lattice [25] . The white area is the BCC2 
phase enriched in Ta and W, and the black area is the Ti particles. During 
sintering, Ti element with a lower melting point has a relatively high 
diffusion coefficient and will performs mass transfer and grain rear -
rangement at the intergranular interfaces [26] . It is well known that the 
Ti element exists in the crystal structure of HCP phase at low tempera -
ture and the phase transition from hexagonal to cubic structure tends to

investigate the microstructure of the cross section of its wear scar, and 
the TEM foil was extracted from the surface of the wear scar along the 
frictional direction by the focused ion beam technology, with a sampling 
depth of 5 µ m (as shown in Fig. 12 a-c, where the area A in Fig. 12 a is the 
sampling location). The cross-sectional image of the wear scar (area B in 
Fig. 12 c) and the corresponding EDS element distribution map show that 
a relatively continuous and dense friction film with a thickness of about 
4 µ m is formed on the surface of the alloy during the friction process. 
Moreover, the friction layer has obvious plastic deformation, and there 
is a clear boundary between the friction layer and the substrate 
( Fig. 13 c). Fig. 13 further analyzes the cross section of the wear scar of 
Ti1.5W1 alloy after friction test at 600
◦
C. It can be seen that the cross 
section of the wear scar is composed of a three-layer structure, which is a 
typical friction-induced microstructure. Obviously, the characteristics of 
the microstructure depend on the distance to the worn surface [43] . This 
is mainly because the high equivalent applied strain and shear strain 
underneath the wear track during sliding friction could induce severe 
plastic deformation, forming plastic deformation layer. Under the effect 
of continuous friction-induced refinement, the surface of the plastic 
deformation layer begins to refine gradually, forming a depth-dependent 
gradient microstructures and an increase in refinement closer to the 
worn surface [44,45] . To be specific, the top layer is a surface oxide 
layer consisting of ultrafine nano-oxide grains (grain size less than 
20 nm). Due to adhesion, the thickness of the surface oxide layer varies 
in the range of 100 – 500 nm. Selected electron diffraction of ultrafine 
grains (inset A in Fig. 13 b) reveals that the surface oxide layer is mainly 
composed of TiO
2
, Nb
2
O
5
, and other small amounts of oxides. The 
subsurface layer is a plastic deformation layer composed of oxide grains 
and alloy matrix, in which some layered slender grains with a layer 
thickness of about 40 nm caused by plastic deformation can be found 
( Fig. 13 b). It is well known that the friction properties of materials are 
related to deformation-induced strain hardening affected by normal 
loads [41] . The microstructure evolution of the friction films is domi -
nated by plastic deformation-induced grain refinement. The ultrafine 
grain structure of the surface layer and the nano-layered crystals of the 
subsurface layer play a role in fine-grained strengthening on the surface 
of the friction films, which can effectively improve the load-bearing 
capacity of the friction films, thereby increasing the wear resistance of 
the alloy. Meanwhile, the plastic deformation layer has a certain buff -
ering effect on the frictional shear force, which can prevent the surface 
oxide layer from falling off, thus reducing abrasive wear. On the other 
hand, the heterostructure induced by friction will further enhance the 
extra subsurface hardening through heterogeneous 
deformation-induced strengthening [46] .

4. Conclusion 
In this work, the microstructure, compressive properties, and high- 
temperature tribological properties of Ti
x
VNbTaW
y 
RHEAs with 
different Ti and W contents were studied. The following conclusions can 
been draw: 
(1) Three types of Ti
x
VNbTaW
y 
RHEAs with different Ti and W 
contents were successfully prepared by the mechanical ball milling and 
spark plasma sintering method. The three alloys are composed of BCC1 
phase, BCC2 phase and Ti particles. 
(2) The three alloys showed considerable compressive strength and 
plasticity, and the fracture mode is a mixture of both ductile and brittle 
fractures. The plasticity of the alloy with lower content of W (Ti1W0.5) 
is relatively good and its plastic strain and fracture strain can be as high 
as 15.8% and 25.7%, respectively. However, with the increase of the 
content of W element, the yield strength and peak strength of the alloy 
are improved, whereas the plasticity is decreased. The yield strength and 
peak compression strength of Ti1.5W1 alloy with a plastic strain of 
approximately 12.2% can be as high as 1375 MPa and 1548 MPa, 
respectively, 
(3) By adjusting Ti and W contents, the wear mechanism of the alloys 
at different temperatures can be changed, thereby affecting their friction 
and wear properties at different temperatures. The main wear mecha -
nisms of all alloys gradually change from abrasive wear at low tem -
perature to oxidative wear and adhesive wear at high temperature. 
(4) All three alloys exhibit the best wear resistance at 600

especially the wear rate of Ti1.5W1 alloy at 600

surface of the friction films, which can effectively improve the bearing 
capacity of the friction films. Meanwhile, the large-sized Ti particles in 
the alloy are dragged to the surface of the alloy, which can promote the 
formation of a dense oxide layer, thus increasing the wear resistance of 
the alloy.
