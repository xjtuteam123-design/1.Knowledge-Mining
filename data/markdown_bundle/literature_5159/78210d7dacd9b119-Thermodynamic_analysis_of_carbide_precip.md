Introduction 
Precipitation-strengthened niobium alloys have shown promise as high-temperature structural materials for 
aerospace applications (1). The achievement of suitable properties and superior creep resistance at service 
conditions requires that the second phase particles remain relatively small, stable, and evenly dispersed in the 
matrix. The key to this development lies in the thermodynamic evolution of the precipitate phase. Charac- 
terization of the various carbide strengthened niobium alloys suggests that a metustable orthorhombic Nb,C 
forms initially during heat treatment that apparently depletes the niobium matrix of carbon supersaturation (2, 
3). The NbzC forms preferentially at gram boundaries and appears as large blocky particles. The equilibrium 
carbide phase however, is quoted to be a zirconium-rich fee monocarbide of the form (Zr,Nb)C who’s specific 
ratio of ZrC-to-P&C varies with aging temperature. The (Zr,Nb)C is observed to be finer and more evenly 
dispersed in the matrix making this form ideal for particle strengthening. These alloys have shown superior 
carbide stability at high temperature with only slight particle coarsening after many thousands of hours ex- 
posure (5,6). 
This paper focuses on the thermodynamic driving force for formation of carbides in a precipitation- 
strengthened tefmctory metal alloy of niobium; Nb- 1 %Zr-0. l%C commercially known as PWC- 11. An ex- 
amination of the free energies of formation of the ditferent carbide phases, their evolution and stability at the 
high temperatures, and a comparison with experimental analysis was made.

Conclusions 
A thermodynamic analysis of particle formation and stability was performed which supports the observed 
transformation of Nb,C to a mixture of ZrC and NbC during heat treatment. Free energy calculations show 
that a maximum NbC concentration (in the mixed carbide particles) exists for a given aging temperature. 
Higher aging/annealing temperatures produce a greater equilibrium NbC content in the particles. The heat 
treatment provided the Nb- 1 Zr-0.1 C alloy was not sufficient to achieve an equilibrium chemistry of the final 
NbC + ZrC carbide mixture.
