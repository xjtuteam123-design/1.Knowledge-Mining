abstract
The effect of complex microalloying by combinative additions of Nb, Sc, Mn, Cr and Mo to the direc-
tionally solidiﬁed Ti–45Al alloys on microstructures and mechanical properties has been examined in
the present study. It is shown that a respective microstructure of fully lamellar for Ti–45Al–3Nb and
Ti–45Al–3Nb–0.5Sc and basket weave type for Ti–45Al–3Nb–2Cr–2Mo–1Mn–0.5Sc were observed. The
high temperature strength of Ti–45Al–3Nb alloy is improved via dispersive strengthening effect of ﬁne
Ti
3 (Al,Sc) dispersoids and solution strengthening effect from the other alloying elements. Enhanced
ductility of Ti–45Al–3Nb alloy was achieved by Cr, Mn and Mo combinative additions.

1. Introduction
During the past decades, numerous investigations have been
concentrated on Ti–Al alloys owing to their high ratios of strength-
to-weight, favorable high temperature strength, and excellent
creep-, oxidation- and corrosion resistance [1,2]. Unfortunately,
the widespread applications in industrial ﬁelds of these alloys
have been limited due to their poor room temperature ductility.
In view of technological approaches, there have been two meth-
ods employed to cope with this main drawback through enhancing
the deformation capability of Ti–Al alloys without deterioration of
their high temperature strength. One is by directional solidiﬁca-
tion (DS) techniques [3–5] to control the orientation of the lamellar
on which the mechanical properties sensitively depend, and the
other approach is complex microalloying for grain reﬁnement and
precipitate pining effects [3,6]. In particular, several previous inves-
tigations indicated that adding a little of Sc to Al [7,8],T i [9] and Mg
[10] alloys yielded uniformly distributed dispersoids or precipitates
with high thermal stability. They are effective deformation barri-
ers at elevated temperatures, and thus can increase the strength
and creep resistance of these alloys. It has been also demonstrated
that Sc additions can enhance the ductility and high temperature
strength of Ti–48Al binary [11] and Ti–40Al–16Nb ternary inter-
metallic alloys [12]. Nevertheless, little attention has been paid to
combining the aforementioned two approaches for examining the

2. Experimental procedures
The alloys in this study were produced from 99.99 wt.% Ti,
99.99 wt.% Al, 99.9 wt.% Nb, 99.9 wt.% Mo, 99.9 wt.% Cr, 99.9 wt.%
Mn and Al-Sc intermediate alloy with a Sc content of 2 wt.%. The
processing details can be found elsewhere [13].
Flat dog-boned specimens were electric-discharge machined
with the tensile loading axis parallel to the withdrawal direction.
All the specimens were mechanically ground to 1500 grit sand
paper and then electropolished. The conventional tensile tests at
high temperatures (800–900
◦C) were carried out at a constant
strain rate of 1.0 × 10−4 s−1 in air on a CSS-3905 multi-functional
electronic relaxation creep testing machine. The tensile strain was
continuously measured using a Linear Variable Differential Trans-
ducer (LVDT) extensometer having a strain resolution of ±0.1 /H9262m.
At least ﬁve specimens were measured for each material at a given
condition.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural characterization

tudinal section for the three DS alloys are shown in Fig. 1 .I t
was indicated that both the Ti–45Al–3Nb and Ti–45Al–3Nb–0.5Sc
alloys exhibited a fully lamellar microstructure. The orientation
of the lamellar was not parallel to, and instead, inclined at an
angle of approximately 45
◦ to the withdrawal direction. Never-
theless, the zigzag boundaries between lamellar colonies were
roughly parallel to the thermal gradient direction. In contrast,
Ti–45Al–3Nb–2Cr–2Mo–1Mn–0.5Sc alloy was characterized by a
basket weave type or vein-like microstructure containing some
equiaxed /H9253phase and the lamellar were miss-aligned. More-

3.2. High temperature deformation
Fig. 2 shows the true-stress versus true-strain curves at
room and high temperatures for the three DS alloys, where
the maximum stress and elongation to failure were also
inserted. It can be found that complex microalloying exerted
a signiﬁcant effect on the tensile strength of Ti–Al alloy.
In general, Ti–45Al–3Nb–2Cr–2Mo–1Mn–0.5Sc alloy exhibited
the highest tensile strength among the three alloys. For
instance, the maximum stress increased from 225 MPa for
Ti–45Al–3Nb, to 249 MPa for Ti–45Al–3Nb–0.5Sc, and to 376 MPa
for Ti–45Al–3Nb–2Cr–2Mo–1Mn–0.5Sc alloy at 850
◦C. The same
trend was true for 900 ◦C. It is also evident that the tensile strength
of ternary Ti–45Al–Nb was improved owing to minor Sc addition.
However, Ti–45Al–3Nb and Ti–45Al–3Nb–0.5Sc alloys, exhibited
little tensile ductility at 850 and 900
◦C, implying that minor Sc
addition cannot contribute to improving deformation capability.
In this case, splitting type fracture immediately occurred after the
stress reached the maximum value without obvious yielding pro-
cess. In contrast, distinct yielding and strain softening was observed
in Ti–45Al–3Nb–2Cr–2Mo–1Mn–0.5Sc alloy subjected to tensile
test at temperatures higher than 850
◦C. In particular, this deforma-
tion feature was more remarkable at 900 ◦C, implying that the alloy
experienced remarkable plastic deformation prior to ﬁnal failure
with an elongation as high as 20.96%.
It is reasonable to deduce that the strength increment was
attributed to precipitation-strengthening of ultraﬁne Ti
3(Al, Sc)
dispersoids and solution-strengthening by Nb, Cr, Mn and Mo. Sim-
ilar effects were also achieved in other Ti–Al alloys containing
minor W, Mo, B, and/or Sc [3,11,18]. The intrinsic lattice structures
of Ti
3Al (ordered hexagonal DO 19-structure) and TiAl (ordered
face-centered tetragonal L1 0 structure) determine the ambient
temperature brittleness of TiAl-based alloys as the motion of dis-
locations is severely limited. It is generally accepted that complex
microalloying is effective to large extent in enhancing the ductility
of Ti–Al-based alloys through improving the inhomogeneous distri-
bution of electron cloud around Ti atoms in the lattice. Under such
a circumstance, the mobility of dislocations can be enhanced. It
is documented that the microalloying elements should occupy the
substitutional positions on Al sublattice and has greater electroneg-
ativities and smaller atomic radii than Ti and/or Al, respectively
[19]. Table 1 presents the atomic radii and electronegativities of the
relevant alloying elements[20,21]. Ti and Al atoms have an identical

4. Conclusions
The microstructure and high temperature tensile
properties of DS Ti–45Al–3Nb, Ti–45Al–3Nb–0.5Sc, and
Ti–45Al–3Nb–2Cr–2Mo–1Mn–0.5Sc have been studied in this
paper. The conclusions were drawn as follows:
(1) Both the Ti–45Al–3Nb and Ti–45Al–3Nb–0.5Sc alloys
presented a full lamellar microstructure whereas the lamellar ori-
entation tended to be inclined with a angle of approximately 45
◦ to
the withdrawal direction. The Ti–45Al–3Nb–2Cr–2Mo–1Mn–0.5Sc
alloy exhibited a basket weave type and lamellar-misaligned
microstructure, band- and vein-like /H9252-Ti phase remained owing
to the /H9252-stabilizing effect of Mo.
(2) Minor Sc addition and complex microalloying of Cr, Mn
and Mo resulted in a prominent increase of the high temperature
strength of Ti–45Al–3Nb alloy, which was attributed to the reﬁne-
ment of interlamellar spacing and dispersive strengthening effect
of ﬁne Ti
3(Al,Sc) dispersoids and solution strengthening effect from
the other alloying elements. Moreover, the deformation capability
of Ti–45Al–3Nb ternary alloy was remarkably enhanced by Cr, Mn
and Mo additions through improving the inhomogeneous distribu-
tion of electron cloud around Ti atoms and increasing the mobility
of dislocations.
