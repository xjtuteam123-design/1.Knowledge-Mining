Crack-Free Welding of a Co-Base Superalloy with High γ’
Precipitate Fraction
Lukas Haußmann,* Bernd Burbaum, Britta Stöhr, Andreas Förner, Lisa Freund,
Mathias Göken, and Steffen Neumeier*
1. Introduction
In the past years, additive manufacturing techniques gained
steadily increasing interest for expensive high-temperature mate-
rials as it allows cost-ef ﬁcient near-net shape manufacturing,
remanufacturing, or individualized repair of components. [1– 3]
For the repair of high-temperature resistant superalloys used
in industrial gas turbines or aerospace engines, laser metal depo-
sition (LMD), also known as laser cladding, is one of the most
suitable methods because of its easy usability and its low and very
local heat input. [1,4– 9] However, due to the high cracking suscep-
tibility of Ni-base superalloys with high γ’ volume fractions,[10– 13]
crack-free welding is limited to alloys that
are only strengthened by solid solution hard-
ening or a low fraction of precipitates, such
as IN718 or IN625. Their good mechanical
properties are restricted to temperatures
below 650 °C. [14– 19] Conventionally, mostly
carbide or solid solution hardened Co-base
alloys are generally considered to be easily
weldable and are used in industrial applica-
tions as welding additives for local repairs,
but offer only low strength. [20,21] Co-base
superalloys with γ/γ’ microstructure have
not been systematically investigated for
their weldability up to now. However, the
ﬁrst results are promising. [22]
Çam and Koçak [4] concluded from a plot
of the Al content over the Ti content that
Ni-base superalloys below the line between
3 wt% Al and 6 wt% Ti are weldable. With a further increase of
the Al or Ti content the alloys show pronounced strain age crack-
ing, mainly due to an increased γ’ volume fraction, which hin-
ders the reduction of residual stresses during the cooling or
reheating of the material. [23] To better compare Ni-base superal-
loys with Co-base superalloys, the plot of weldability from Cam
and Koçak[4] was modiﬁed by including Ta, Nb, and W. Ti as well
as Ta act as strong γ’ forming elements in Ni- [20,24,25] and Co –
base[26] superalloys, Nb is a γ’ former in Ni-base superalloys [20,25]
and W is necessary for the formation of the γ’ phase in Co-base
superalloys.[27] As the partitioning coef ﬁcient of the individual
alloying element strongly depends on the chemical composition
through the interaction with other alloying elements, no multi-
plying factors were used for Ti, Ta, Nb, and W. The resulting
Graph is shown in Figure 1 .
In this weldability plot, the borderline between the readily
weldable and dif ﬁcult to weld line is between 6 at% Al
and 8 at% Ti þ Ta þ Nb þ W for the Ni-base superalloys.
Obviously, the compositions of the Co-base superalloys are far
above the weldability line of Ni-base superalloys, as they
have a signi ﬁcantly higher content of Al. However, Co-base
superalloys usually have a low γ’ solvus and a higher solidus tem-
perature[34] than Ni-base superalloys, which should decrease the
susceptibility for strain age cracking in Co-base superalloys com-
pared to Ni-base superalloys. Furthermore, the γ’ precipitates in
Co-base superalloys usually show a lower deformation resistance,
which should also decrease the strain age cracking susceptibil-
ity.[34,35] An advantage of the higher Al content in the Co-base
superalloys should be an improved oxidation resistance in
addition to the increased γ’ volume fraction. [36,37] However,

Laser cladding of superalloys provides an attractive and cost-ef ﬁcient technique
for near-net shape manufacturing, remanufacturing, or individualized repair of
components. While the weldability of γ’ hardened Ni-base superalloys is limited,
Co-base superalloys should have a lower susceptibility for cracking during
welding due to a lower γ’ solvus temperature and less segregation than Ni-base
superalloys. The weldability of the Co-base superalloy CoWAlloy2Mod and the
resulting mechanical properties at 950 °C are investigated. In the as-welded
condition of CoWAlloy2Mod, a ﬁne dendritic microstructure without forming
cracks and limited porosity is found. After a three-step heat treatment, recrys-
tallized, equiaxed grains and a high γ’ volume fraction are obtained leading to a
very good strength. CoWAlloy2Mod shows a signi ﬁcantly higher strength and
creep strength than the conventional weld ﬁller Co-alloy PWA795.

Besides strain age cracking, cracking in the heat-affected zone
(HAZ) and solidi ﬁcation cracking are further challenges in the
welding of superalloys. Cracking in the HAZ mostly occurs
due to increased γ’ precipitation, undesirable secondary phases,
or localized melting due to segregations. [13,23] Besides the lower
γ’ solvus temperature and lower deformation resistance of γ’pre-
cipitates, Co-base superalloys usually show less segregation than
Ni-base superalloys. [38,39] Therefore the susceptibility for HAZ
cracking should be lower compared to Ni-base superalloys. [23,40]
Solidiﬁcation cracking is caused by residual stresses in the
mushy zone between the liquidus and solidus line and a lack
of residual liquid to ﬁll emerging cavities. [23,40– 42] The solidiﬁca-
tion cracking susceptibility can be reduced by decreasing the
difference between the solidus and liquidus temperature,
increasing the amount, and improving the distribution of the
residual melt in the ﬁnal stage of the solidi ﬁcation.[43– 46] This
can be done by lowering the laser power and scan velocity or
by increasing the preheating temperature.[5,10,23] In certain cases,
crack-free welding of Ni-base superalloys with higher γ’ volume
fractions has been achieved. This, however, requires very precise
control of the local heat input, [47] additive manufacturing by elec-
tron beam melting [48] or by adjusting the chemical composition
with extensive computational effort. [49,50] Crack-free welding was
also achieved by signi ﬁcantly increasing the Boron content,
which, however, leads to a heavily altered microstructure and
a decreased γ’ volume fraction. [51]
A new Co-base alloy was designed intended for structural
applications with good weldability. The composition of this alloy
lies between the γ’ strengthened Co-base wrought alloy
CoWAlloy2[34,52] and the conventional, mainly carbide hardened
Co-base weld ﬁller alloy PWA795 with excellent oxidation proper-
ties.[53] The alloy called CoWAlloy2Mod has in contrast to
CoWAlloy2 no additions of Zr and Si as they form intermetallic
precipitates, especially at grain boundaries, [54– 56] which should
greatly reduce the weldability. Furthermore, The Ni- and W-content
was decreased and the Cr-content was increased since Ni and W
increase the γ’ solvus temperature while Cr decreases it.[57– 59] This
should lead to a generally decreased solvus temperature and there-
fore to a better strain age cracking resistance. CoWAlloy2Mod was
welded and investigated by means of scanning electron microscope
(SEM), transmission electron microscope (TEM), and atom probe
tomography (APT). The mechanical properties were studied with
strain rate jump (SRJ) compression tests and compression creep
tests and compared to the mechanical properties of PWA795
and CoWAlloy2.
2. Experimental Section
The nominal compositions of the investigated Co-base alloys are
given in Table 1. The powder for the laser cladding welding pro-
cess of alloy CoWAlloy2Mod was manufactured by Nanoval
GmbH & Co. KG (Berlin/Germany) by gas atomization and
subsequent sieving.
Cubes with edge lengths of 10 mm were welded on a plate of
Ni-base superalloy Alloy247, using a micro cladding welding pro-
cess at Siemens AG (Berlin/Germany). The reference materials
PWA795 and CoWAlloy2 were cast instead of welded as the cast
specimen were already available and there were no suitable
powders present for these materials. PWA795 was arc-melted
from welding wires (Polymet, West Chester Township/USA)
and CoWAlloy2 was manufactured, cast and hot-rolled at
Vacuumschmelze GmbH & Co. KG (Hanau/Germany).
Further information on the alloy CoWAlloy2 can be found in. [34]
All three materials were heat treated in a 3-step heating pro-
cess at 1230 °C for 2 h, 1080 °C for 2 h, and 870 °C for 20 h. This
heat treatment process was used to investigate the properties
when used as a weld ﬁller material to repair, e.g., turbine blades.
After the heat treatment, the median grain size of CoWAlloy2
was 30 μm with the quartiles being Q0.25 ¼ 19 μm and
Q0.75 ¼ 49 μm and the secondary γ’ volume fraction was deter-
mined to be 45 /C6 1% from SEM images.
This is very similar compared to the secondary γ’ volume frac-
tion of about 42% of CoWAlloy2 after annealing for 2 h at 900 °C
as determined by small angle neutron scattering. [52] PWA795
showed a grain size in the range of hundreds of microns to
millimeters after the 3-step heat treatment and the carbide
structure remained unchanged. However, the formation of an
additional phase could be observed. (Co, Ni)Al precipitates have
been identi ﬁed by EDS measurements corresponding with the
literature.[53]
The microstructure was characterized after a standard metal-
lographic sample preparation using a SEM (Crossbeam 1540
EsB, Zeiss, Oberkochen/Germany) and the grain size was deter-
mined by electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) (Nordlys,
Oxford Instruments, Abingdon/UK). The chemical composition
was probed with a different SEM (Crossbeam 540, Zeiss,
Oberkochen/Germany) by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS) (X-Max N 150, Oxford Instruments, Abingdon/UK). The

3. Results and Discussion
Figure 2 a– d shows the microstructure of CoWAlloy2Mod along
the building direction in the as-welded and fully heat treated
state. The layer architecture of the as-welded condition of
CoWAlloy2Mod, induced by the layer-wise building process,
can be seen in Figure 2a with an average layer height of about
150 μm. Moreover, a typical ﬁne dendritic structure with no
cracks can be noticed in Figure 2b with an average dendrite size
of 4.4 μm. The grain size of the as-welded condition was deter-
mined by EBSD to a median of 23 μm with the quartiles being
Q0.25 ¼ 16 μm and Q0.75 ¼ 44 μm with a strong preferential ori-
entation of the 100 direction parallel to the building direction and
along the edges of the cube.
A high amount of small, spherical carbides with a volume frac-
tion of about 0.5% is also visible in the interdendritic regions in
Figure 2b due to the elevated carbon content of CoWAlloy2Mod
compared to CoWAlloy2. After the three-step heat treatment
CoWAlloy2Mod shows a recrystallized microstructure and the
dendrites have vanished and coarse equiaxed grains are present
(Figure 2c,d). The carbides are not affected by the heat treatment
and remain unchanged in the former interdendritic region. EDS
investigations revealed an increased content of Ta and Hf in
these carbides. The shape and composition of these carbides
indicate an MC type, which was also reported by Belyavin et al [53]
for the alloy PWA795, where the carbides are also mainly
present in the interdendritic regions. The median grain size
rises to 31 μm with the quartiles being Q0.25 ¼ 18 μm and

The porosity in CoWAlloy2Mod is about 1.7% but there are
only a few larger pores and no cracks present. Due to their spher-
ical size and shape, the pores are mainly gas pores generated dur-
ing the powder manufacturing or welding process.[3,15] These gas
pores should not be as crucial for crack initiation as pores with
sharp edges. Additionally, with an optimized welding process,
the porosity can be reduced further.
The SEM analysis of the γ’precipitates in the fully heat treated
condition of CoWAlloy2Mod revealed an even distribution of sec-
ondary γ’ precipitates and no primary γ’ precipitates. The γ’ vol-
ume fraction of the secondary γ’ precipitates for CoWAlloy2Mod
was determined as 42 /C6 1%. The slightly higher γ’ volume
fraction of CoWAlloy2 compared to CoWAlloy2Mod can be
explained by a higher content of the γ’ forming elements W
and Ni in CoWAlloy2 and the higher concentration of Cr in
CoWAlloy2Mod, which reduces the γ’ fraction in Co-base
superalloys.[57,59,62,63]
The results of the TEM and APT investigations of the γ/γ’
microstructure are shown in Figure 3 . The γ’ precipitates in
the TEM dark- ﬁeld image in Figure 3a) exhibit a mean radius
of 27 /C6 19 nm and the diffraction pattern con ﬁrms the L12 struc-
ture of the γ’ phase. It has to be noted that the standard deviation
of the γ’precipitates’radius is relatively high, which could be due
to the elongation and deviation from the ideal spherical shape of
the γ’precipitates induced by the relatively long heat treatment at
870 °C. The γ’ size of CoWAlloy2 ranges between the mean γ’
precipitate radius of 45.2 nm after a heat treatment of 16 h at
900 °C and 19.6 /C6 4.0 nm after a heat treatment of 16 h at
800 °C.[52] This is in relatively good agreement with the interme-
diate heat treatment temperature of 870 °C, the slightly longer
heat treatment duration and the slightly lower concentration
of γ’ forming elements.
The proxigram of a γ/γ’ phase boundary (Figure 3b) and the
chemical compositions ( Table 2 ) obtained from the APT meas-
urements show that elements such as Ni and Al show a strong
partitioning toward the γ’ phase whereas elements such as Co
and Cr show a stronger partitioning to the γ matrix.
Figure 3c reveals that spherical secondary γ’ precipitates as
well as some tertiary γ’ precipitates in the γ channels between
the different secondary γ’ precipitates are present, which are
not shown well in the TEM images due to their small size
and limited volume fraction.
The composition of the γ and γ’ phase determined by APT
are in very good agreement with that in the parent alloy
CoWAlloy2 measured via APT by Freund et al. [64] and ﬁt well
to the concentrations measured by SEM-EDS. Accordingly, the
γ’ volume fraction can also be calculated by the lever rule

In agreement with the SRS compression tests, the creep

Different from the SRJ tests CoWAlloy2 exhibits a higher creep

CoWAlloy2 could be due to sample-related variation but could
also be explained by a slightly higher γ’ volume fraction com-
pared to CoWAlloy2Mod. Furthermore, the grain size distribu-
tion of forged and heat-treated CoWAlloy2 is signi ﬁcantly
smaller with negligible porosity, which also could contribute
to the higher creep strength of CoWAlloy2.
4. Conclusions
In summary, this work shows that high-strength Co-base super-
alloys with a high γ’ precipitate fraction can be welded crack-free
by laser cladding. The as-welded condition of CoWAlloy2Mod
has a porosity of 1.7% and a layered ﬁne dendritic microstruc-
ture. After a 3-step heat treatment optimized for the base mate-
rial CM247, the microstructure of the welded CoWAlloy2Mod is
recrystallized with randomly oriented grains of about 31 μmi n
size. The secondary γ’ volume fraction is 47.5% according to
the APT measurements and 42 /C6 1% according to SEM image
analysis with an average γ’ precipitate radius of 27 /C6 19 nm.
Welded CoWAlloy2Mod has a two times higher strength in
SRJ compression tests and 13.5 times higher compressional
creep strength than the carbide and solute hardened conven-
tional weld ﬁller Co-alloy PWA795. Compared to forged and
heat-treated CoWAlloy2 its strength is similar and the 3.3 times
lower creep strength results presumably from a slightly lower γ’
volume fraction, some porosity, and a larger grain size. These
promising results indicate that Co-base superalloys with high
γ’ precipitate fraction are suitable for additive manufacturing
processes and particularly for techniques like laser metal depo-
sition. In the future, they might therefore be able to replace
PWA795 in some repair applications with high strength needs.
