1. Introduction 
Unlike the traditional alloy with one or two alloying elements as the 
basis, the high entropy alloys (HEAs) contain multi-principal elements 
with a concentration of 5 – 35 at.% [ 1 , 2 ]. The original design concept 
aims to obtain a single-phase solid solution by maximizing the mixing 
entropy. However, with the development of HEAs, the dual-phase so -
lution or a mixture of the single-phase solution and the second phase 
with a complex structure is favored to achieve more excellent mechan -
ical properties [ 3 ]. Featured by the four core effects, namely high- 
entropy effect, sluggish diffusion effect, lattice distortion effect and 
cocktail effect, they possess superior properties compared with tradi -
tional alloys, such as high strength, particularly at high temperatures, 
exceptional hardness and excellent resistance to wear, corrosion and 
oxidation [ 4 , 5 ]. 
To date, numerous studies have been conducted on the single-phase 
HEAs with fcc and bcc structures, such as fcc HEAs Co-Cr-Cu-Fe-Ni, Co- 
Cr-Fe-Ni, Cr-Mn-Fe-Co-Ni, etc. [ 6 – 12 ], and bcc HEAs Nb-Mo-Ta-W, Nb- 
Mo-Ta-V-W and Ta-Nb-Hf-Zr-Ti, etc. [ 13 – 21 ]. However, research on hcp 
HEAs has only emerged in recent years due to the limited number of 
elements with the hcp structure in the periodic table. Researchers ob -
tained the hcp single phase in the alloys consisting of transition metal 
elements, such as CoFeReRu, Co

aiming to provide insights into the influence of the microstructure on the 
mechanical performance of these alloys.

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure 
Fig. 2 presents the XRD patterns of the as-cast Al-Sc-Ti-Zr alloys. The 
constituent phases transition from a single hcp solution to a mixture of 
hcp and bcc phases, and finally to a single bcc solution when the atomic 
percent of Al increases from 5% to 10% to 15%. This suggests that higher 
atomic ratio of Sc and Ti tends to stabilize the hcp structure, while a 
lower ratio promotes the formation of the bcc structure. The uncali -
brated diffraction peaks might be caused by the compound of scandium. 
Fig. 3 shows the backscattered electron images of SEM for the as-cast 
samples, revealing the typical dendritic microstructure. They all 
demonstrate two distinct contrasts, i.e. the light grey within the den -
drites and the dark grey in the inter-dendrite region. Given that only a 
single phase can be identified from the XRD patterns of the Al5 and Al15 
samples, the dark grey contrast that appears a little fuzzy in the SEM 
micrographs may result from the slight segregation of the constituent 
elements. In the case of the Al10 sample, which consists of two phases, 
the SEM micrograph only reveals the bcc phase with segregation, while 
the hcp phase at the nanoscale is not clearly visible, yet confirmed by 
subsequent TEM results. To further investigate the crystal structure of 
the constituent phases and analyze the microstructure at the submicro- 
and nano-scale, TEM images were captured and selected area electron 
diffraction (SAED) was performed. As shown in Fig. 4 (a), the Al5 sample 
shows a predominantly nano-scale structure, consisting solely of the hcp 
phase. The grain is so fine that the neighbor grain is inevitably be 
selected by the selective aperture. Therefore, additional spots appear in 
the SAED image. The high-angle annular dark field (HAADF) image of 
the scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) was given in 
Fig. 5 , along with the EDS element maps of the four elements. These

the HAADF-STEM image of the Al5 sample along with the EDS element 
maps of Al, Sc, Ti and Zr. A comparison with Fig. 5 reveals that the 
microstructure coarsened after annealing, from nearly nano-scale to 
micro-scale, with shorter laths. The element maps indicate that the laths 
consist of the Sc-rich hcp phase and the interspace region comprises the 
Ti-rich hcp phase, with Al and Zr distributing uniformly in the alloy. This 
is because unlike the complete miscibility in the bcc phase for Sc-Zr and 
Ti-Zr systems, Sc and Ti have limited mutual solubility in each other and 
tend to form the hcp_Sc and hcp_Ti phases after annealing. Table 2 lists 
the compositions of constituent phases in the annealed samples, as 
measured by TEM/EDS. For the Al5 sample, Sc and Ti show up in two 
distinct hcp structures. For the Al10 and Al15 samples, the hcp phase 
contains a higher concentration of Sc, while the bcc phase contains a 
higher concentration of Ti. The variation in Al and Zr compositions is not 
as significant as that of Sc and Ti. This reveals that Sc stabilizes the hcp 
phase, while Ti stabilizes the bcc phase.

The annealed Al5 and Al10 alloys possess a similar proportion of the 
dark grey area, comprising approximately 31% and 34% of the total 
area, respectively. This indicates that the ratio of the two phases in these 
alloys, hcpII/hcpI and bcc/hcp, is nearly identical. Yet, as shown in 
Table 6 , hcpI and hcpII exhibits a lattice misfit of 0.035 in the direction 
of a- and b-axis and 0.036 in z-axis, which is less compared to that be -
tween hcp and bcc phases. Based on common knowledge, when the 
lattice misfit is smaller than 0.05, the interface is coherent. This explains 
the higher strength and plasticity observed in the annealed Al5 alloy. On 
the other hand, the dark grey area in the annealed Al15 alloy contributes 
approximately 52% of the total area, indicating a higher fraction of bcc 
phase compared to the annealed Al10 alloy. Yet, the fracture stain still 
decreases. This should be attributed to the coarsened structured of Al15 
alloy since its first-step annealing temperature is 100 K higher than the 
Al10 alloy. According to the literature [ 53 ], a high density of twin 
boundaries can effectively interact with dislocations and impede their 
movement, leading to a significant nanoscale twin strengthening effect. 
It appears that the density of the nanoscale twins formed in the annealed 
Al10 and Al15 alloys is not sufficiently high to have an active effect on 
the mechanical performance. 
As the temperature elevates, the retarding diffusion coefficients were 
supposed to slow down the coarsening of the microstructure, thereby 
maintaining high mechanical properties at high temperatures. It can be 
observed that the samples with high compressive strength at room 
temperature normally maintain high strength at high temperatures. 
However, the plasticity at high temperature does not necessarily inherit 
that at room temperature, and it appears to be closely related to the 
constituent phases. Based on the experimental results of this work, the 
presence of the bcc phase contributes to high plasticity at 873 K.
