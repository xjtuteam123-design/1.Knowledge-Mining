1. Introduction 
The efficient design of metallic components for structural applica -
tions demands high strength and high fracture toughness simulta -
neously. However, conventional metallic alloys face a strength-ductility 
trade-off, limiting weight reduction and efficiency. Recently, high en -
tropy alloys (HEAs), also known as multi-principal element alloys 
(MPEAs), introduced in 2004 [1,2] , have attracted scientific attention 
due to intriguing properties, such as high strength and ductility [3] , 
good corrosion resistance [4] and extraordinary impact or fracture 
toughness [5,6] . For instance, the CoCrFeNiMn single-phase FCC Cantor 
alloy is a well-known example that exhibits high ultimate tensile 
strength (1280 MPa) and ductility (75%) under cryogenic conditions 
and outstanding fracture toughness (⁓200 MPa.m
1/2
) at room and 
cryogenic temperatures [6] . However, the fully recrystallized Cantor 
alloy with equiaxed grains in the range of 4 – 155 µ m exhibits low yield 
strength (170 – 410 MPa) and low ultimate tensile strength (530 – 763 
MPa) with good ductility (39 – 57%) at room temperature [6 – 8] . 
Several strategies, including grain refinement [8,9] , precipitation 
hardening [10,11] , and solid solution hardening [12] have been used to 
increase the strength of this alloy, however, these strengthening mech -
anisms are accompanied by ductility loss. The ductility loss in the Cantor 
alloy due to grain refinement has been related to the deformation 
mechanism [9] . Some of the HEAs showed a significant potential for a 
simultaneous increase in strength and toughness. A 
metastability-engineering strategy, for instance, recently, has been 
suggested to overcome the strength-ductility trade-off in Fe

temperature [13,14] .

3.3. Tensile properties 
Fig. 8 a displays representative engineering tensile stress-strain 
curves for the Co20 and Co50 HEAs at room temperature. As the Co 
content increases from 20 at% to 50 at%, both the alloys ’ yield strength 
and ultimate tensile strength increase from 405 MPa to 502 MPa and 
766 MPa to 1002 MPa, respectively. Additionally, the fracture strain 
(tensile ductility) increases from 40% to 50%. 
Fig. 8 b represents the true stress and strain-hardening rate (d σ /dԑ) 
curves as a function of true strain. The strain-hardening rate curves were 
obtained by calculating the first derivative of the 6th-order polynomial 
functions fitted to the true stress vs. true strain curves. The intersection 
points of the strain-hardening rate and true stress curves both vs. true 
strain represent the plastic instability point of the alloys, where necking 
begins. According to Consid `ere ’ s criterion, necking is predicted to occur 
when d σ / dԑ ≤ σ [44] . The Co50 HEA exhibits necking at higher strains 
than the Co20 HEA, resulting in higher ductility. The strain-hardening 
rate of both HEAs continuously decreases until failure, but their slope 
changes during deformation. The change in slope of the 
strain-hardening rate with strain results from evolving the deformation 
mechanisms during the tensile test, thereby delineating the 
strain-hardening rate curve into distinct stages [45] . The Co20 HEA 
shows three distinct stages of strain-hardening, while the Co50 HEA 
shows four stages. The Co50 HEA exhibits a significantly higher 
strain-hardening rate than the Co20 HEA throughout the plastic defor -
mation process. This indicates that the deformation mechanisms of the 
Co50 alloy have a higher capacity to induce strain hardening, resulting 
in higher ultimate tensile strength and larger uniform deformation 
compared to the Co20 HEA. XRD and TEM analyses in the following 
sections will further elucidate these findings.

4.1. FCC phase stability 
The presence of the HCP phase in both the annealed and deformed 
Co50 samples may be attributed to the metastability of the FCC phase. 
Previous studies have reported that the stability of the FCC phase in high 
entropy alloys, such as Fe
80- x
Mn

Co
10
Cr
10 
[14] , and CoCrFeNiMn [60] 
is relative, and the FCC to HCP transition can occur during cooling from 
the high-temperature single-phase region or mechanical deformation. 
For instance, Zhang et al. [60] observed a polymorphic transition from 
FCC to HCP in the CoCrFeNiMn alloy under hydrostatic compression of 
41 GPa at room temperature. This transition is irreversible and the HCP 
phase retains during decompression, however, the HCP phase almost 
completely transforms to the FCC phase during heating up to 723 K, 
which reveals that the FCC phase is a stable polymorph at higher tem -
peratures, while the HCP structure is more thermodynamically favor -
able at relatively lower temperatures. Thus, the FCC-structured HEA is a 
thermodynamically metastable polymorph attained by kinetic con -
straints, and the thermodynamically favorable HCP structure is rather 
difficult to obtain.

tion mechanism of FCC alloys [45,61] . As the SFE value decreases, the 
deformation mechanism switches from cross slip to planar slip, then to 
deformation twinning, and finally to FCC to HCP strain-induced 
martensitic transformation. In the Fe-Mn-C austenitic steels,

4.3. Mechanisms behind the improved strain hardening rate and ductility 
The strain hardening rate (d σ /d ε ) represents d ρ /d ε . The Co50 HEA 
shows a significantly higher strain-hardening rate than the Co20 HEA 
throughout the plastic deformation process, which reveals that the dis -
locations piled up more rapidly in the Co50 HEA than the Co20 HEA. 
This led to a higher density of dislocations in the Co50 HEA than in Co20 
HEA ( Fig. 11 ). 
During the initial deformation stage, the strain hardening rate for 
both Co20 and Co50 HEAs decreases rapidly due to the increasing rate of 
dislocation annihilation caused by dynamic recovery [18,19] . The Co50 
HEA, which has a lower stacking fault energy (SFE), exhibits a higher 
strain hardening rate during this stage due to reduced dynamic recovery. 
This is attributed to the presence of a high density of annealing twins and 
stacking faults (SFs) ( Figs. 6 and 7 ) as well as different deformation 
mechanisms in Co50 HEA ( Fig. 12 ) compared to Co20 Cantor alloy [55] . 
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations ( Fig. 12 ) reveal 
that the slip character of the Co50 HEA is planar in the later stage 1 (e =
%8) because its low SFE prevents the cross-slip of dislocations. 
Annealing twin boundaries, slip bands, and SFs prevent other disloca -
tion motions, leading to the interaction of dislocations with twin 
boundaries and the formation of pile-ups at the boundaries [50] . In stage 
2, with increasing strain, two sets of SFs or nano-twins form on different 
(111) planes of Co50 alloy, interacting with each other ( Fig. 13 ). 
Additionally, the hexagonal close-packed (HCP) phase forms in only 
some grains ( Fig. 14 ). It has been reported that deformation induced 
HCP laths nucleate through the overlap of SFs on the (111) planes [18] . 
Nano-twins and the HCP phase promote a high strain hardening rate by 
generating extra boundaries acting as barriers to dislocation motion 
[19] . Moreover, replacing the ductile FCC phase with the hard HCP 
phase increases the strain hardening rate [18] . In stage 3, the density of 
nano-twins and HCP phase increases with further increasing strain 
( Fig. 15 ), leading to constant strain rate hardening. The increased vol -
ume fraction of the hard HCP phase and high density of extra boundaries 
as barriers to dislocation motion results in a higher strain hardening rate 
for the Co50 HEA compared to the Co20 HEA, which increases the ul -
timate strength, postpones the onset of necking and increases the 
ductility of the Co50 HEA.

4.4. Other properties of Co50 alloy 
The metastability resulting from tailoring the alloy composition can 
indeed affect other properties of the Co50 alloy such as corrosion 
resistance, weldability, and processing ability. The coexistence of dual- 
phase HCP and FCC microstructures may potentially diminish corrosion 
resistance due to the formation of galvanic microcells. However, it is 
anticipated that the corrosion resistance of the Co50 alloy will be either 
comparable or better than that of the Cantor alloy. This expectation 
arises from the expected low extent of galvanic corrosion in the Co50 
HEA, given the equivalent composition of its FCC and HCP phases, as 
observed in some other metastable HEAs [14,17] . Moreover, the partial 
substitution of Fe and Mn with the more noble element Co enhances the
