ABSTRACT 
Partitioning and diffusion of transition metal solutes can significantly affect the coarsening rate of 
γ' precipitates in Ni -based single crystal superalloys. Using first -principles density -functional 
calculations and atom probe tomography, we investigated the partitioning coefficient and diffusion 
rate of 3d (Ti –Ni), 4d (Zr –Pd), and 5d (Hf –Pt) transition metal solutes. For ternary model 
Ni-based single crystal superalloys, the 3d solutes (except Ti) partition to the matrix phase, 
whereas 4d and 5d solutes (except Ru, Rh, and Ir) prefer γ' precipitates. The existing atom probe 
tomography results are consistent with the calculation results. Across the periodic table, 
middle-row elements have a higher ( lower) diffusion activation energy (diffusion rate) than early 
and late row elements. The band-filling effect that is coupled with electronic structure analysis can 
explain the parabolic behavior of energetics that involve bond breaking. To minimize the 
coarsening rate, we have screened alloying elements that both have a low diffusion rate in the γ 
matrix and partition to the γ' phase. These alloying elements will suppress the coarsening behavior 
of the precipitates and are expected to contribute to the sta bility of precipitates at elevated 
temperature. 
 
Keywords: nickel -based superalloy; partitioning coefficient; diffusion rate; coarsening; 
first-principles; atom probe tomography

1. Introduction. 
Over recent decades, developing temperature capability for turbine blades have required an 
improvement in the creep resistance and the microstructural stability of Ni -based single-crystal 
superalloys. The excellent mechanical properties of Ni -based superalloys at elevated temperature 
are considered to result from their L1 2 strengthening phases. Therefore, the volume fraction, 
morphology, coarsening rate, and chemical composition of the γ' phase at high temperature affects 
the superior performance of the Ni-based superalloys directly. In particular, the diffusion-mediated 
coarsening of the γ' precipitates is very important for the creep properties of superalloys [1-7]. 
A design of the next generation of superalloys with an improved creep resistance and the capability 
to withstand higher operating temperatures is needed to ensure the stability of a γ' phase at elevated 
temperature. To this end, Sluytman et al. [8] developed an Alloy PX5 that provides a relatively high 
volume fraction of pre cipitates with low rates of precipitate coarsening from 1050 to 1200°C. A 
high γ' volume fraction above 1000 °C requires elemental additions to improve the γ' volume 
fractions and the γ' solvus temperature. Based on the classical coarsening theory developed by 
Lifshitz and Slyozov and independently by Wagner collectively known as the LSW theory [9, 10], 
the modified coarsening rate constant k is derived as [11]:

In this work, we performed first-principles calculations to investigate the partition coefficient and 
diffusion rate of alloying elements (3d, 4d, 5d) and the electronic structure was analyzed. 
Simultaneously, the atom probe tomography (APT) experiments were used to verify our calculated 
partitioning coefficients. From the view of inhibiting coarsening of precipitates, we also discussed 
the choice of alloying elements for alloying design.

Cast ingots were prepared by vac uum induction melting. The master ingots were solidified 
directionally using the Bridgman technique (2 mm/min withdrawal rate) to form <001> -oriented 
single crystals. Ternary single crystal alloys were homogenized at 1330 °C for 20 h, and aged at

compositions of the Ni -based alloys investigated in this work are listed in Table 1. The alloy 
compositions were verified by the chemical analysis using inductively cou pled plasma (ICP) 
atomic-emission spectroscopy, which are within the allowable range. 
APT specimens were prepared by cutting 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm × 15 mm rods from the heat -treated 
single crystal bars, before the standard two-step electro-polishing procedures [12]. APT specimens 
were performed in a voltage -pulsed local-electrode atom probe (LEAP 3000 HR), at a specimen 
temperature of ~50 K, a gauge pressure of ~10-8 Pa, a pulse repetition rate of 200 kHz, and a pulse 
voltage fraction of 20%. The detection efficiency of the instrument is ~36%, and the detection rate 
is 0.5%. Subsequent data were analyzed using the IV AS 3.6.8 software, and the compositional 
information was obtained by employing the proximity histogram (proxigram) methodology [13]. 
To delineate the interface between the γ and γ' phases, the isoconcentration surface of 17 at.% Al 
has been used for M2, M4 and M5 alloys, and 15 at.% Al for M7, M8, M9 and M10 alloys. The 
area detected by APT is very large as shown in Figure S1 in supplementary material, and the 
compositions for each phase are accurately measured in sub -volumes which contain at least 1 
million ions. The chemical identities of different ions are determined from their times of flight, 
which are translated into a mass -to-charge-state spectrum (see Figure S2 in supplementary 
material). 
We used density -functional theory (DFT) and implemented the Vienna ab -initio simulation 
package (V ASP) code [14] to investigate the substitutional -formation, vacancy -formation, and 
migration energies. All calculations were performed using the projector augmented -wave method 
[15, 16] and the general gradient approximation in the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof form (GGA-PBE) 
[17]. The minimum plane wave energy cutoff was 350 eV . A first -order Methfessel –Paxton 
smearing method [18] was used with a searing width of SIGMA = 0.2 eV . The convergence 
accuracy of the total energies of electronic self -consistency was 10-5 eV . The lattice parameters of 
γ-Ni and γ'-Ni3Al in the DFT calculations were 3.516 Å and 3. 569 Å, respectively. The supercells 
of γ-Ni and γ'-Ni3Al were 3 × 3 × 3 supercells consisting of 108 lattices: alloying element atoms, 
and a vacancy, or both would substitute depending on the calculation model. A 5 × 5 × 5 
Monkhorst–Pack [19] k-point schem e was used for the Brillouin zone integration. Full ionic 
relaxation was performed until the maximum Hellmann–Feynman force was below 0.02 eV/Å.

Compared with multicomponent alloys [30], W, Mo, and Re partition to the γ' phase in ternary 
model alloys. The reason why Re(or Mo, W) partitioning trend can reverse in the multicomponent 
alloys is that there are many other alloying elements (Ta, W, Mo, Ru , Cr, Co, Hf) added to the 
multicomponent alloy, while Re is the only element in the ternary alloy. Other alloying additions 
would influence the partitioning behavior of alloying elements between the γ and γ' phases. 
Amouyal et al. [31] reported that W prefers to partition strongly to the γ'-phase in a model ternary 
Ni–Al–W alloy, while this behavior is reversed in multicomponent Ni -based alloys. The presence 
of Ru and the higher Co content affect the elemental partitioning of refractory elements and 
improve the microstructural stability [32]. In addition, the similarly reversed partitioning behavior 
of Mo was also found in cobalt base alloys [33, 34] . Murakami et al. [35] suggest that a 
‘competition of partitioning’ occurs among alloying elements in multi -component alloys. This is 
subject to the solid solubility of elements in γ' precipitates at elevated temperature. The elements 
that strongly prefer the γ' phase dissolve first, followed by the other elements. Thereafter, the 
elements that partition weakly t o the γ' are driven to the γ phase. For example, in Ni –Al–Cr–Re 
quaternary alloy [36], the content of Re in γ' phases decreases due to the solubility and the 
competition of Cr. In addition, the solubility of Re in the γ' phases is up to 2 at.% at 1040 °C [37]. 
When Re is added too much, the precipitates can not dissolve extra Re. Thus, the excess of Re 
atoms will go to the γ matrix and sequentially change the partitioning ratio.

3.3 Diffusion rate of transition elements in matrix Ni 
Fig. 5 shows the results for the calculated diffusion activation energy and diffusion rates (at 
1100°C) of the 3d, 4d, and 5d solutes in Ni. Across the transition elements, middle -row elements 
have a higher (lower) diffusion activation energy (diffusion rate) than early and late row elements. 
Fig. 6 shows the interaction energies of a vacancy with 3d, 4d, and 5d solutes in Ni. Positive 
energies (repulsion) mean that it is not easy to form a vacancy near the solute elements compared 
with the host Ni, and negative energies (attraction) imply that it is easy to form a vacancy. For the 
transition metal solutes, the interaction shows a parabolic behavior that reaches a maximum at d4-6 
occupancies in the middle of the row. Across a transition metal series, for the early d solutes, only 
the lower "bonding" parts of the virtual bound states are occupied and the bond strength between 
the solute and the Ni atoms increases gradually, whereas for the later d solutes, the higher 
antibonding states are filled, which lowers the bond strength. Therefore, the parabolic behavior of 
energetics that involves bond breaking (e.g., diffusion energy barrier, solute -vacancy binding 
energy [38]) results fro m the same band -filling effect. The trend in interaction energies between 
the element and vacancy in Fig. 6 is opposite from the trend in the diffusion rate in Fig. 5(b). 
According to the vacancy diffusion mechanism, a vacancy must exist at the first neigh bor of the 
jumping solutes. If the element repulses vacancy, we can believe that the atom that surrounds a 
lattice with a small probability of occurrence of vacancies is not conducive to the diffusion. 
Fig. 7 displays a fair correlation between the diffusi on activation energy of a solute and its radius 
(i.e., radii of ions in 12 -coordinated metals [39]). In general, small solutes diffuse more difficult 
than large ones. Besides the effect of solute radius, the chemical bond effect is also a key factor. A 
stronger bond between the solute and host (Ni) atom, yields an atom with a larger diffusion energy 
barrier that is more difficult to jump. Electronic structure analysis is a good tool for explaining this 
phenomenon. As illustrated in Fig. 8, the repulsive int eraction is attributed to the solute being 
closely integrated with the Ni atoms and changing the charge distribution close to the vacancy; the 
charge-density distribution around the solutes is largely spherical and would yield a high diffusion 
barrier. 
The activation energy of the 4d elements is lower than that of the 5d elements, but the activation 
energy across the 3d elements displays a complex barrier profile. The energy barrier of 3d 
elements in Ni is somewhat larger or less than that of the 4d and 5d elements. A local hollow 
exists in the middle of the 3d row performing spin-polarized. In addition, a similar local hollow as 
shown in Fig. 6 is also found for the interaction energy of a vacancy with transition solutes in Ni. 
This behavior can be attribut ed to the role of magnetic moments of the 3d solutes, which are

Interestingly, we note that the diffusion rate is larger than Ni for Al, Ti, Nb, and Ta and is smaller 
for Co, Mo, Ru, W, and Re. The diffusion rate of elements (Zr, Hf) that strengthen grain 
boundaries is fastest. In addition to Pd, the diffusion rates of platinum group elements, which are 
not used widely in superalloys, are less than that of Ni. A smaller diffusion rate of the 
γ'-partitioning elements in the matrix has a lower coarsening rate of γ' precipitates, and a more 
favorable precipitate stability. By devel oping new compositional domains for Ni -based 
superalloys that maintain a microstructural stability at operating temperature, we can screen the 
alloying elements which the diffusion rate is less than that of Al or even less than that of Ni in the 
periodic table. 
4. Discussion 
A development of creep resistance of nickel -based superalloys requires a relatively high γ' phase 
volume fraction and a low coarsening rate at operating temperature [1, 8]. Precipitate coarsening is 
determined by the interface migration that accompanies solute element diffusion in the matrix. To 
minimize the coarsening rate, an effective alloying strategy is to add elements that both prefer to 
the γ' phase and have a diffusivity that is less than the diffusivity of Al in the matrix. Base d on the 
analysis of Sections 3.1 and 3.3, Pd, Pt, Ta, and W satisfy the above two conditions by screening 
the periodic table. In addition, platinum group metals (PGMs) usually have high melting points 
and a high hot -corrosion resistance, and so the additi on of PGMs is expected to enhance the 
high-temperature strength thermal stability and the oxidation/hot corrosion resistance [41, 42]. 
Pd, Pt, and Ta partition strongly to γ', coupled with a low diffusion rate (<Al), and can also form 
intermetallics (Pd 3Al, Pt 3Al, Ni 3Ta) in a similar way to Ni 3Al. It is thus likely that Ni may 
equilibrate with Pd/Pt to form (Ni,Pd/Pt) 3Al and Al can equilibrate with Ta to form Ni 3(Al,Ta). 
This results indicate that Pd/Pt substitutes for Ni, and Ta substitutes for Al as discussed in Section 
3.1. Therefore Pd, Pt, and Ta may be viewed as γ' phase stabilizers. As we expected, additions of 
Pd, Pt, and Ta favor the stabilization of precipitates against coarsening at an elevated temperature, 
and improve the γ' strength. A reducti on in the coarsening kinetics of precipitates by Pt was also 
observed in the experiments [8, 43], in which the rate constant of the Pt -containing alloys is one

order of magnitude smaller compared with the Pt -free alloys above 1000°C. Pd and Pt have an 
identical crystal structure to Ni and the maximum solubility of Pd and Pt in fcc Ni is 100%. The 
addition of Pd and Pt does not yield a risk of precipitating the TCP phase as refractory elements 
(W, Re, Mo, etc.). However, Pt used in the superalloy is limited in terms of its expense, and, in 
general it would be used to modify the bond coatings (BCs) [44-46]. Pd is a precious metal, but its 
price and density are less than that of Pt, so Pd may offer potential advantages as an addition to the 
superalloys. Although γ'-partitioning Ti and Nb also have large partitioning coefficients and potent 
strengthening effect [47, 48], their diffusion rates are rapid (> Al), and so they are seldom added to 
high-generation single crystal superalloys. 
Ru, Rh, Ir, and Os prefer to reside in the γ matrix coupled with a low diffusion rate (< Ni) and 
thereby stabilize the matrix. In recent years, Ru, as a characteristic element for fourth -generation 
single crystal superalloys, has improved the stability of the single -crystal superallo ys’ 
microstructure with respect to topologically close -packed (TCP) phase precipitation. As the 
slowest rate of diffusion in Ni host in 4d transition metals, Ru additions to the Udimet 720Li 
Ni-based superalloys restrain the coarsening of the γ' phase at 8 50°C conducted by Cui et al [49]. 
Rh and Ir additions have a complete solid solubility with Ni, stabilize the alloy microstructure, and 
suppress TCP precipitation [50, 51]. However, the diffusion rate of Rh is ~20 times that of Ru and 
its density is not much smaller than that of Ru. Although the diffusion rates of Ir and Ru are within 
one order of magnitude, the density and cost of Ir exceed those of Ru. Therefore, the advantage of 
Rh and Ir is not as good as that of Ru. Os has the largest activation energy and the lowest diffusion 
rate in the matrix Ni. However, the diffusion rate of Os is close to that of Re, its density is larger 
than that of Re, and its solubility is smaller than Re, which may result in the TCP phase 
precipitating. Therefore, Os may not display many advantages by its addition to superalloys. 
The diffusivity of Re and W in the Ni matrix is far smaller than that of the other alloying elements in 
the Ni matrix. In multi-component Ni-based alloys, only W exists in a considerable amount in the 
γ' phase (> 1 at.%), the Re content in the γ' phase is small (< 1 at.%) [30]. It may be expected that 
the W and Re contents could be increased in γ' to inhibit coarsening. Indeed, additions can 
increase the preferential partitioning of W and Re to the γ' precipitates [52], which r educe the 
content of refractory elements in the γ phase and may influence precipitate morphology and creep 
properties. The low diffusion rate of W and Re reduces the rate of migration of the phase interface 
and lowers the coarsening rate of precipitates. Moreover, the low content of W and Re in the γ phase 
may reduce the possibility of TCP phase precipitation in the Ni-based alloys. In particular, the low 
cost of W makes it a promising substitute element. The ability to achieve an optimal alloy 
composition is a function of the W and Re contents that are required to maintain high-temperature 
performance. The synergistic effect of W and Re may be responsible for the superior mechanical 
properties of the alloy.

In summary, in this new alloying approach, Pt (Pd) exhibiting both a strong partitioning behavior

5. Conclusion 
By first-principles calculations, we have screened alloying elements that both have a low diffusion 
rate in the matrix phase and partition to the γ' phase. These alloying elements will suppress the 
coarsening behavior of precipitates and are expected to contribute to microstructure stability at 
elevated temperature. 
1. The site occupancy behavior of 3d (Ti –Ni), 4d (Zr –Pd), and 5d (Hf –Pt) transition metal 
elements in L1 2 Ni3Al can be summarized in that the preference for the Al site increases as the 
atomic number increases down a group, and decreases as the d-occupation number increases along 
a period in the periodic table. This behavior is governed by the interactions, i.e., stronger 
Ni-transition-metal interactions relative to Al -transition-metal interactions, with a stronger 
preference for the Al site. 
2. For ternary model Ni-based single crystal superalloys, the 3d solutes (except Ti) partition to the 
matrix phase, whereas 4d and 5d solutes (except Ru, Rh, and Ir) prefer γ' precipitates. The existing 
atom probe tomography results are consistent with the calculation results, whic h proves the 
reliability of our calculations. 
3. Across the periodic table, middle -row elements have a higher (lower) diffusion activation 
energy (diffusion rate) than early- and late-row elements. However, the diffusion rate across the 3d 
elements displays a complex profile because of the influence of magnetism. 
4. For the transition metal solutes, interaction and diffusion activation energies show a parabolic 
behavior that reaches a maximum at d 4-6 occupancies in the middle of the row, and the parabolic 
behavior of energetics that involves bond breaking occurs because of the band -filling effect. The 
physical and chemical effects influencing energies can explain the site and diffusion trends well. 
5. To minimize the coarsening rate, an effective alloying approach is to add elements that both 
prefer to the γ' phase and maintain the diffusivity below the diffusivity of Al in the matrix.

For ternary alloys, almost all 3d solutes partition to the matrix, whereas most 4d and 
5d solutes prefer γ'. 
Across the periodic table, middle-row elements have a higher (lower) diffusion barrier
