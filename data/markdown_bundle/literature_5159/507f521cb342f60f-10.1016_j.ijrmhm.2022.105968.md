tures beyond the capabilities of state-of-the-art Ni-based superalloys.

2. Materials and methods 
The alloys were prepared from high purity (99.9% or better) metals 
into 150 g buttons using a laboratory vacuum arc melter (Retech Inc., 
Ukiah, CA, USA) with a water-cooled copper cavity at an arc current of 
500 – 650 A. Residual nitrogen, oxygen, and carbon were mitigated by 
back-filling the chamber with high purity argon to a pressure of 400 Torr 
after pulling a vacuum better than 5 × 10

copper cavity, the alloys were individually wrapped in Ta foil, placed 
in an alumina crucible, and hot isostatic pressed (HIP) under high-purity 
Ar at 1400 
◦

3.1.2. Microstructural analysis 
Microstructural analysis using SEM shows that the microstructures of 
the HTM, HTW, HTMW and HTN alloys consist of large (~1 mm or 
higher) grains of a high-temperature BCC phase, eutectoidally trans -
formed regions consisting of fine, nanometer-thick lamellae of at least 
two phases inside the grains and at grain boundaries, and non- 
transformed areas inside grains ( Fig. 3 through Fig. 6 ). The 
combination of backscattered electron (BSE) and EDS analyses con -
ducted at high magnifications shows that at least two phases are present 
in the eutectoidally transformed regions ( Fig. 6 , Fig. 7 ). One phase is rich 
in Hf and depleted in Ta, it has a darker Z contrast and, in accord to XRD, 
likely has HCP crystal structure. Another phase is slightly rich in Ta and 
depleted in Hf (relative to the respective alloy composition), has a 
brighter Z contrast and is likely a BCC phase. Dark regions along grain 
boundaries and dark spots or web-like threads inside grains are identi -
fied at lower magnifications ( Figs. 3 a, 4 a, 5 a). Higher magnification 
images of these regions show that they also consist of a lamellar mixture 
of the two phases, and their dark contrast is due to higher volume 
fraction of Hf-rich lamellae ( Figs. 3 b, 4 b and 5 b). It can therefore be 
concluded that Hf was non-homogeneously distributed in these four 
alloys prior to the eutectoid transformation due to thermodynamically 
driven formation of Hf-rich clusters/regions along grain boundaries and 
inside grains. Indeed, the studied alloys have hypo-eutectoid composi -
tions and Hf-rich primary HCP particles should form in the equilibrium

4. Discussion 
4.1. Microstructure 
X-ray and Thermo-Calc analyses agree that HTM, HTMW, HTN and 
HTZ are two-phase alloys consisting of HCP as the major phase (~68%) 
and BCC as a minor phase. There is also an agreement between these two 
methods that the HTC alloy additionally contains a cubic Laves phase in 
the amount of 8.8%, mainly at the expense of the BCC phase. The Laves 
phase is also observed by XRD in the HTW alloy; however, Thermo-Calc 
predicts only HCP and BCC phases in this alloy. The SEM studies could 
not identify the Laves phase either. On the other hand, ~17 vol% of the 
Laves phase were reported earlier, as well as predicted by Thermo-Calc, 
in Hf-21Ta-21 W, together with HCP and BCC phases [ 19 ]. Inability to 
identify the Laves phase by SEM, both in HTW and HTC alloys, can be 
explained if one assume that it is present inside the eutectoid regions in 
the form of fine particles, which dimensions are comparable with the 
dimensions of fine lamellae of the HCP and BCC phases. Low volume 
fraction and fine precipitation do not allow this phase to separate from 
the HCP and BCC lamellae by SEM/EDS. 
Thermo-Calc analyses indicate that the studied alloys have hypo- 
eutectoid compositions ( Fig. 2 ). Therefore, in equilibrium condition 
their microstructure should consist of primary Hf-rich HCP particles 
formed above the eutectoid temperature and an eutectoid mixture of Hf- 
rich HCP and Ta-rich BCC phases between these particles, similar to the 
microstructure observed earlier in the binary Hf – 27Ta alloy [ 19 ] and 
ternary Hf-21Ta-21Mo and Hf-26Ta-5Mo alloys [ 21 ]. The SEM micro -
structural analysis however did not show the presence of the primary 
HCP particles in the studied alloys. Instead, fully transformed grains 
consisting of a eutectoid mixture of fine Hf-rich and Ta-rich lamellae are 
observed in HTC and HTZ alloys ( Fig. 8 ). Only partial eutectoid trans -
formation resulting in the eutectoid regions embedded in a non- 
transformed high-temperature BCC phase is detected in the other four 
alloys, HTM, HTW, HTMW and HTN ( Fig. 3 through Fig. 6 ). The 
disagreement between the thermodynamic calculations and the experi -
mental SEM observations may indicate that the microstructure formed 
in the alloys during cooling after the HIP process at 1400 
◦
C is non- 
equilibrium, and the formation of the equilibrium HCP phase (i.e. pri -
mary Hf-rich particles) above the eutectoid transformation is restricted 
by slow diffusion of the refractory elements [ 21 , 29 ], as well as by weak 
driving force and a narrow temperature range for the BCC to HCP 
transformation ( Table 3 ). The driving force for the equilibrium HCP to 
BCC transformation (i.e. the difference in the Gibbs free energy of BCC 
and HCP phases, Δ G = G

4.3. Oxidation behavior 
Generally, oxidation at high temperatures occurs via two modes: 
diffusion controlled, parabolic oxidation ( n = 0.5) and interface 
controlled, linear oxidation ( n = 1). Protective behavior is associated 
with the diffusion controlled mode such that as time increases, the rate 
of oxidation continuously decreases. Therefore n values approaching 0.5 
are ideal. On the other hand, linear oxidation rates dictate that the 
oxide-scale formation is interface controlled and that the metal contin -
uously and quickly oxidizes. This mode is generally facilitated by 
cracking, delamination, or spallation of the oxide. With this in mind, the 
alloys in this study exhibited interface controlled oxidation, demon -
strating poor performance at 800 
◦

◦

refractory elements are known to react readily with oxygen.

tended to improve the oxidation resistance of the Hf – 27Ta alloy. At 800

inside grains.
