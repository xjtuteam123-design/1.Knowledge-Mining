Effect of Nb Content on Microstructures and Mechanical
Properties of Ti- xNb-2Fe Alloys
Qiang Li , Pu Miao, Junjie Li, Meifeng He, Masaaki Nakai, Mitsuo Niinomi, Akihiko Chiba, Takayoshi Nakano,
Xuyan Liu, Kai Zhou, and Deng Pan
(Submitted March 21, 2019; in revised form July 12, 2019)
β-Type Ti-Nb-based alloys exhibit satisfactory biocompatibility and low Y oung ’s modulus for biomedical
applications. The microstructure and mechanical properties of a series of Ti-(14, 16, 18, 20, 22, 24)Nb-2Fe
alloys fabricated by arc melting were investigated by XRD, optical microscopy, and tensile tests. Both ω and
α″ phases existed in the Ti-14Nb-2Fe alloy, while just a single β phase existed in the other alloys. Twinning
is an important deformation mechanism that causes work hardening and twinning-induced plasticity. It
was found in the Ti-(14, 16, 18, 20)Nb-2Fe alloys and not in the Ti-22Nb-2Fe alloy. The Ti-14Nb-2Fe alloy
exhibited the highest tensile strength and the highest Y oung’s modulus owing to the existence of the ω phase.
The tensile strength decreased gradually from 830 MPa (highest) for the Ti-14Nb-2Fe alloy to 540 MPa
(lowest) for the Ti-24Nb-2Fe alloy with an increase in the Nb content. The Y oung ’s modulus decreased from
90 GPa for the Ti-14Nb-2Fe alloy to 63 GPa for the Ti-22Nb-2Fe alloy and then increased to 71 GPa for the
Ti-24Nb-2Fe alloy. Elongation shows the same trend as the Y oung’s modulus. The Ti-22Nb-2Fe alloy, with a
low Y oung’s modulus of 63 GPa, tensile strength of 570 MPa, and 15% elongation, was found suitable for
biomedical applications. The Ti-20Nb-2Fe alloy also exhibits a high tensile strength, a Y oung ’s modulus
ratio of 9.24 910
−3, and 18% elongation and is thus considered another valuable Ti alloy for biomedical
applications.
Keywords β stability, biomaterials, mechanical properties,
microstructures, Ti-Nb alloys
1. Introduction
Recently, due to the aging population and the concomitant
increase in accidents, replacement of dysfunctional hard tissues
with artiﬁcial instruments such as spinal ﬁxation rods, arti ﬁcial
hip joints, and bone plates has become common (Ref 1). The
currently used biomaterials in these instruments include SUS
316L, Co-Cr alloys, commercially pure Ti (CP-Ti), and Ti-6Al-
4V ELI (extra low interstitial) (Ref 2-5). However, the Y oung’s
moduli of SUS 316L ( /C24 200 GPa), Co-Cr-Mo (200-230 GPa),
CP-Ti (/C24 105 GPa), and Ti-6Al-4V ELI ( /C24 110 GPa) are much
higher than that of a human bone (10-30 GPa), which may lead
to bone atrophy and poor bone remodeling (Ref 6, 7).
Moreover, Ni, Al, and V are widely viewed as high-risk
elements. Therefore, β-type Ti alloys containing non-toxic
elements have been developed due to their low Y oung ’s
modulus, satisfactory corrosion resistance, and great biocom-
patibility (Ref 2, 5, 8). For reducing the cost, researchers have
added moderately inexpensive elements such as Fe, Sn, Cr, and
Mn to the Ti alloys to obtain a good balance between
performance and cost (Ref 9).
Many Ti-Nb-based alloys have been developed due to their
excellent properties for biomaterials, such as Ti-29Nb-13Ta-
4.6Zr (wt.%, TNTZ) (Ref 1), Ti-35Nb-7Zr-5Ta (wt.%) (Ref
10), Ti-24Nb-4Zr-8Sn (wt.%) (Ref 11), Ti-30Nb-6Sn (wt.%)
(Ref 12), and Ti-36Nb-5Zr (wt.%) (Ref 13). Fe is a strong β-
stabilizing element and is bene ﬁcial in retaining the β phase at
room temperature (Ref 14, 15). The atomic radius of Fe
(0.126 nm) is smaller than that of Ti (0.132 nm); thus Fe can
bring solid-solution strengthening to Ti alloys (Ref 16). It is
believed that the addition of Fe to the binary Ti-Nb alloys can
improve their mechanical properties and reduce the cost. Fe has

2. Experimental Procedure
The Ti-(14, 16, 18, 20, 22, 24)Nb-2Fe (at.%, hereafter,
chemical compositions of present alloys are in at.%) alloys
were prepared by arc melting in a high-purity argon atmo-
sphere. Each ingot was inverted and melted 5-6 times to ensure
uniform mixing of the alloying components. The ingots were
homogenized and then solution-treated at 850 °C for 1 h
followed by water quenching. They were cold-rolled under a
reduction ratio of 88% to a sheet with a thickness of 1.4 mm at
room temperature. The sheets were ﬁnally solution-treated at
800 °C for 1 h followed by water quenching. The specimens
were ground after each heat treatment to remove the oxidized
layer on the surface. The phase compositions were analyzed by
a Bruker D8 x-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu-K α radiation
at a voltage of 40 kV and current of 40 mA. The samples for
microstructural observations were mounted in epoxy resin,
ground, mirror-polished, etched in a 5-vol.% hydro ﬂuoric acid
(HF) solution, and observed under an optical microscope. The
samples for electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) were
mirror-polished without etching. The specimens with the gage
part of 12 mm 93m m 91.3 mm for tensile tests were cut by
wire electric discharge machining and measured by a universal
material testing machine at a strain rate of 6.94 910
−4 s−1 with a
ﬁxed strain gauge to ensure the accuracy of the strain
measurement. Three samples were tested for each component,
and the mean and standard deviation were calculated. The
fracture surfaces were observed by Quanta FEG 250 scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) after tensile testing.
3. Results
3.1 Phase Composition
Figure 1 shows the XRD patterns of the Ti-Nb-Fe alloys
after the ﬁnal solution treatment. Some weak peaks corre-
sponding to α″ and ω phases and strong peaks corresponding to
β phase are detected in Ti-14Nb-2Fe. The other alloys only
show single β phase diffraction peaks. This indicates that the β
phase is the most unstable in Ti-14Nb-2Fe. The appearance of
the α″ martensite transformation and a thermal ω phase
transformation usually occurs in Ti alloys with metastable β
phase during quenching from above the β transus temperature
(Ref 2). When the Nb content is increased to 16% or higher, the
β phase becomes stable, and both the transformations are
inhibited during quenching.
Figure 2 shows the XRD patterns of the Ti-Nb-Fe alloys
after the tensile tests. The diffraction peak of the ω phase
becomes unclear owing to deformation and internal stress. The
peak corresponding to α″ phase is also found in Ti-14Nb-2Fe.
Both Ti-16Nb-2Fe and Ti-18Nb-2Fe show very weak peaks at
the same diffraction angle; therefore, it is supposed that a stress-
induced α″ martensite transformation may occur during tension.
There is no change observed in Ti-(20, 22, 24)Nb-2Fe from the
XRD patterns.

3.3 Mechanical Properties
Figure 6 shows the true stress –strain curves of the Ti-Nb-Fe
alloys obtained by tensile tests at room temperature. The Ti-(14,
16, 18)Nb-2Fe alloys do not show clear yielding between
elastic deformation and plastic deformation. The nonlinear
deformation behavior is due to the unstable β phase and stress-
induced α″ martensite transformation (Ref 24, 25). The stress –
strain curves are consistent with XRD results. Residual α″
phase is observed in the Ti-(14, 16, 18)Nb-2Fe alloys, in which
stress-induced α″ martensite transformation appears during
tension. The stress of Ti-(14, 16, 18)Nb-2Fe increases signif-
icantly with the increase in strain during the plastic deformation
stage, indicating strong work hardening. The work hardening
can be attributed to the appearance of twinning, which also
causes the twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) effect (Ref 26).
The Ti-(20, 22, 24)Nb-2Fe alloys exhibit a process of elastic
deformation–yielding–plastic deformation –fracture, and the
slight increase in stress in the plastic deformation section
suggests a weak work hardening owing to the disappearance of
twinning.
The mechanical properties including tensile strength ( σ
b),

(E) were obtained by conducting tensile tests and are shown in
Fig. 7. The tensile strength constantly decreases with increasing
Nb content. The Ti-14Nb-2Fe alloy shows the highest σb of
830 MPa and the highest σ0.2 of 685 MPa owing to the
appearance of the ω phase, which always shows the precipi-
tation strengthening effect. The Ti-(16, 18, 20)Nb-2Fe alloys
exhibit a lower σ

stress at the beginning of plastic deformation in the β phase.

σ0.2 is attributed to work hardening caused by twinning and
diminishes with the decrease in twinning. Therefore, σb
constantly decreases to around 610 MPa with increasing Nb
content to 20 at.%. The Ti-(22, 24)Nb-2Fe alloys show a stable
β phase deformation behavior with weak work hardening. Their
σb is very close to σ0.2. As neither strengthening phase nor
strong hardening mechanism appears in the Ti-(22, 24)Nb-2Fe
alloys, slip easily occurs, develops well, and leads to low σ0.2
and low σb. Although the grain size is re ﬁned with increasing
Nb content, ﬁne-grained strengthening is rarely observed
because the grain size is in the same order of magnitude.
The Ti-14Nb-2Fe alloy with the ω phase shows the highest
Young’s modulus of 90 GPa, because the ω phase always
demonstrates the highest Y oung ’s modulus (Ref 9). The
Y oung’s modulus gradually decreases to its lowest value of
63 GPa (for the Ti-22Nb-2Fe alloy). The Ti-16Nb-2Fe alloy
shows lower Y oung’s modulus than does the Ti-14Nb-2Fe alloy
mainly because of the disappearance of the ω phase. The further
decrease in the Y oung ’s modulus for Ti-(18, 20, 22)Nb-2Fe
alloys is attributed to the enhanced β stability caused by
increasing Nb content. This is consistent with Hon ’s result that
Y oung’s modulus decreased as the wt.% of Nb increased from
34 to 40% (from 21 to 25.6% in at.%) in the Ti-Nb binary
alloys without ω phase precipitation (Ref 27). However,
excessive addition of Nb causes ultra-stable β stability and
leads to an increase in Y oung ’s modulus (Ref 28). This can
explain why the Ti-24Nb-2Fe alloy exhibits a higher Y oung ’s
modulus than does the Ti-22Nb-2Fe alloy.

Figure 8 and 9 shows the fracture surfaces and dimples of
the Ti-Nb-Fe alloys observed by SEM. Necking becomes
remarkable with the increase in Nb based on the low-
magniﬁcation images (Fig. 8). The dimples observed in the
fracture surfaces (Fig. 9) suggest that all the alloys show good
plasticity. Small equiaxed dimples observed in Ti-14Nb-2Fe
and larger equiaxed dimples in Ti-16Nb-2Fe indicate that
uniform deformation is dominant before fracture. The dimples
in Ti-18Nb-2Fe are slightly deformed, and Ti-(20, 22, 24)Nb-
2Fe shows many greatly deformed dimples, suggesting the
appearance of local deformation before fracture. Ti-(20, 22, 24)
Nb-2Fe shows typical dimple fracture surfaces formed by
microvoids coarsening and connecting because some small
voids are found in the fracture surface. The fracture surfaces
observation indicates that plasticity is increased by Nb addition.
However, the elongation shows the same trend with that of
Young’s modulus, which is decreased with Nb increasing from
14 to 22 at.%. It is known that the elongation is closely related
to the microstructure and deformation mechanism of the Ti
alloys (Ref 12, 29). Although the ω phase leads to brittleness
and may reduce the plasticity, it can raise the critical stress for
the slip in the β phase, prevent the long-term movement of a
dislocation, and ﬁnally raise the uniform deformation before
necking. Moreover, it is reported that twinning in β-type Ti
alloys shows the twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) effect
(Ref 26). Ti-14Nb-2Fe with a small amount of ω phase exhibits
a large amount of twinning during tension; therefore, it shows
the largest elongation. The elongation gradually decreases with
Nb content increasing from 16 to 22% owing to the gradual
decrease in twinning in the Ti-Nb-Fe alloys. A similar decrease
in elongation is also found in Ti-22Nb-(0, 1, 2, 3, 4)Cr alloys
for the same reasons, as the Cr content is increased from 0 to 4
at.% (Ref 30). The Ti-(22, 24)Nb-2Fe alloys have stable β
phases without secondary phase precipitation and do not twin
during tension; slip is the primary deformation mechanism. As
Ti-24Nb-2Fe with lower strength is softer than Ti-22Nb-2Fe,
the β phase in Ti-24Nb-2Fe is more easily deformed and has
better synergistic deformation ability. Therefore, Ti-24Nb-2Fe
shows larger elongation ( /C24 20%) than Ti-22Nb-2Fe ( /C24 15%).
4. Discussion
The β stability of Ti alloys strongly in ﬂuences the phase
compositions and deformation mechanisms. In this work, the
Ti-16Nb-2Fe alloy has a single β phase; however, α″ martensite
forms in the Ti-16Nb (Ref 31), Ti-20Nb (Ref 32), and Ti-24Nb
(Ref 33) alloys after solution treatment. This indicates that 2 at.
% Fe addition strongly enhances the stability of the β phase and
effectively inhibits the α″ martensite transformation during
quenching. The Ti-Fe intermetallic compound is not observed
in all Ti-Nb-Fe alloys in this study owing to the small amount
of Fe added. However, 2 at.% Fe also shows effective solid-
solution strengthening. The solutionized Ti-(16, 18, 20, 22)Nb-
2Fe alloys show higher σ

as Zr and Sn. More Ti alloys with high strength and low
modulus could be developed based on the investigated Ti-Nb-
Fe alloys.
5. Conclusion
The microstructures and mechanical properties of Ti-(14, 16,
18, 20, 22, 24)Nb-2Fe alloys were investigated. The following
conclusions can be summarized:
1. Twinning is found in deformed Ti-(14, 16, 18, 20)Nb-
2Fe alloys with metastable β phases and leads to work
hardening and twinning-induced plasticity. The twinning
decreases with increased β stability by Nb addition and
disappears in Ti-22Nb-2Fe.
2. The Ti-14Nb-2Fe alloy exhibits the highest strength, the
highest Y oung’s modulus, and the largest elongation ow-
ing to the precipitation of the ω phase and twinning-in-
duced plasticity. For Ti-(14, 16, 18, 20, 22)Nb-2Fe
alloys, the tensile strength, Y oung ’s modulus, and elonga-
tion gradually decrease with the increase in β stability.
3. Fe shows noticeable solid-solution strengthening in the
studied Ti-Nb alloys. The Ti-20Nb-2Fe and Ti-22Nb-2Fe
alloys show a desirable combination of high tensile
strength, low Y oung ’s modulus, and suitable elongation,
which make them suitable candidates for biomedical
applications.
