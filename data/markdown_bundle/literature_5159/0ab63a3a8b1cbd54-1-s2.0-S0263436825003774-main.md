ABSTRACT
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) exhibit excellent potential for high-temperature applications. However, 
their vast compositional space and the challenge of optimizing multiple properties impede further development. 
To address these challenges, this study integrates high-throughput computation and machine learning to identify 
compositions that meet multiple criteria, including density, strength, ductility, and resistance to high- 
temperature softening. A Bayesian multi-property optimization framework is employed to efficiently enhance 
both compressive yield strength and ductility. After three iterative cycles, the optimized alloys exhibit favorable 
combined yield strength and ductility compared to the RHEAs used for model training. Among these, the

2.2. Surrogate model and Bayesian optimization
A surrogate model was established to predict the compressive yield 
strength and fracture strain of the alloys, and Bayesian optimization was 
employed to efficiently achieve simultaneous enhancement of strength 
and ductility. The compositional space considered in this study was 
restricted to the Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb-Mo-Ta system. The data were collected 
from the experiments and produced using only vacuum arc melting 
under as-cast conditions to minimize the significant effect of the pro -
duction method.
Prior knowledge is a key component of Bayesian optimization, aiding 
in the development of more accurate and effective surrogate models. 
The features used in the surrogate models are listed in Table 2 . Solid 
solution strengthening (SSS) is the dominant strengthening mechanism 
in as-cast HEAs and the contribution of SSS increases with atomic size 
mismatch and modulus mismatch [ 34 ]. In addition to the atomic radius 
difference ( δ R ), the total distortion parameter δ
*

model was included as a feature in the yield strength model [ 35 ]. Short- 
range order (SRO) in HEAs significantly influences dislocation glide 
resistance, glide paths, and stacking fault energy ( SFE ), thereby affecting 
mechanical performance. The formation of SRO is related to lattice 
distortion, bond energy, and electronegativity differences. Therefore, 
features such as δ R , mixing enthalpy ( Δ H
mix

difference ( δ χ ) were employed to represent SRO-related effects [ 36 ]. 
These features are also closely related to phase stability and composition 
[ 37 ]. Moreover, dislocation motion is influenced by stacking fault en -
ergy and surface energy, both of which are strongly correlated with the 
valence electron concentration ( VEC ) [ 38 ]. Thus, VEC was used as a 
representative feature. The shear modulus ( G ), which reflects atomic 
bonding strength, provides information on elastic interactions at the 
macroscopic level [ 35 ]. The Pugh ratio ( P ), which relates the shear 
modulus to the bulk modulus, is an empirical indicator of ductility and 
reflects the relative difficulty of shear versus volumetric deformation 
[ 39 ], and was used in the fracture strain model. Therefore, alloy com -
positions were described using the seven features listed in Table 2 to 
construct the surrogate models.

Bayesian optimization was employed to efficiently enhance alloy 
performance [ 30 ]. A bootstrap sampling approach was applied to the 
training set, generating 1000 subsets by randomly selecting 90 % of the 
data in each iteration. For each subset, models were trained and used to 
predict the yield strength and fracture strain of the candidate compo -

compositions for experimental validation. Based on the predictions from 
the surrogate model and the associated uncertainties, EI is employed to

composition with the highest yield strength and fracture strain) and 
exploration (using the predicted uncertainties to study regions of the 
search space where the model is less accurate) [ 40 ]. The predicted mean 
( μ ) and standard deviation ( σ ) were then used to calculate the EI for

2.3. Experimental procedures
RHEAs compositions were synthesized from high-purity elements 
( > 99.9 wt%) using vacuum arc melting under an Ar atmosphere, with a 
nominal ingot mass of 50 g. Each coupon was flipped and remelted eight 
times to ensure the homogeneity of the alloys.
Phase structures were determined by X-ray diffraction (Miniflex 
600 × ). The microstructure was analyzed using a scanning electron mi -
croscope (SEM, JSM-7001F) and a transmission electron microscope 
(TEM, JEM-2010). TEM samples were prepared by grinding and ion 
thinning (Gatan 691). Room temperature compression tests were per -
formed on the WDW-10E testing machine. High temperature compres -
sion tests were performed on the GLEEBLE 3800 testing machine. The 
dimensions of the cylindrical compressive specimens are Φ 3.0 × 6.0 
mm and Φ 6.0 × 10.0 mm. The cylindrical specimens were obtained 
from the edge of the button ingot. Their compression axis was parallel to 
the bottom surface of the buttons. The test was repeated three times for 
each alloy. The two opposite faces were ground to resemble mirrors 
using SiC sandpaper, ensuring they were parallel to meet compression

3.1. Alloy design and property optimization based on Bayesian 
optimization
The candidates screening process was conducted based on thermo -
dynamic criteria and density constraints. Fig. 2 illustrates the overall 
screening workflow. Starting from the full design space of 346,633 alloy 
compositions, the T
m 
criterion was first applied to ensure resistance to 
high-temperature softening, resulting in 345,737 remaining composi -
tions. Due to the stringent density requirements for aerospace engine 
applications, compositions with a ρ above 8.9 g/cm
3 
were excluded, 
leaving 173,264 candidates. Subsequently, the T
sol 
was constrained to 
be below 1100 
◦
C, and the single-phase region was required to exceed 
800 
◦
C to avoid the formation of brittle phases during service. Finally, 
the solidification range was limited to minimize the risk of solidification 
cracking. After all filtering steps, 6216 candidates met the defined 
criteria.
Before applying Bayesian optimization, a surrogate model was con -
structed. The surrogate model training process includes data prepara -
tion, model selection, feature selection, model construction and 
Bayesian optimization. A dataset containing nominal compositions and 
mechanical properties, including compression yield strength and frac -
ture strain at room temperature, was assembled, consisting of 38 and 37 
instances respectively. The features corresponding to all alloys were 
calculated based on Table 2 , including the alloys collected for modeling 
and the features of the 6216 candidates obtained through the afore -
mentioned high-throughput screening. Based on these data and features, 
model selection was conducted. Fig. 3 (a) shows the performance of the 
three ML algorithms for the yield strength and fracture strain models by 
leave-one-out cross-validation, with RF resulting in the smallest RMSE, 
indicating that the RF algorithm is particularly suitable for this study 
and provides more accurate predictions. To optimize the model, 
hyperparameter optimization was performed, focusing on key parame -
ters such as n_estimators, max_depth, and min_samples_split. For the 
yield strength model, the selected hyperparameter configuration was 
n_estimators = 110, min_samples_split = 2, and max_depth = 6, while for 
the fracture strain model, the configuration was n_estimators = 120, 
min_samples_split = 2, and max_depth = 7. After model selection and 
hyperparameter optimization, the prediction models for yield strength 
and fracture strain were established using the RF algorithm. A com -
parison between the predicted (pred.) and experimental (exp.) values for 
both models is shown in Fig. 3 (b – c). For the testing set, the RMSE are 
59.98 MPa for yield strength and 3.66 % for fracture strain, while the R
2 
for both models are 0.91, indicating excellent predictive performance.
Based on the established yield strength and fracture strain prediction 
models, Bayesian optimization was applied to explore the candidate 
compositional space obtained in the high-throughput screening 
described above. In each iteration, three alloys with high MOEI values 
were selected for mechanical property testing, and the experimental 
results were incorporated into the dataset for subsequent iterations. The 
MOEI values were calculated based on model predictions and associated 
uncertainties. As the iterations progressed, the model accuracy 
improved, uncertainty decreased, and target performance was opti -
mized. Fig. 4 (a) presents the experimental performance of the alloys 
designed through Bayesian optimization. The composition and proper -
ties of each iteration are presented in Section 3.2. For example, “ 1 – 2 ” 
denotes the second alloy selected in the first iteration. A total of three 
optimization iterations were performed in this study. As the iterations 
progressed, both the yield strength and fracture strain of the designed 
alloys increased simultaneously. This demonstrates that Bayesian opti -
mization, with minimal experimental data, is capable of rapidly iden -
tifying the optimal composition of high-performance alloys. 
Furthermore, as the iterations increase, significant improvements in 
performance are observed.

3.3. Microstructure
The XRD patterns of the third iteration RHEAs are shown in Fig. 5 , 
where diffraction peaks corresponding to a body-centered cubic (BCC) 
phase are detected. Fig. 6 displays back-scattered electron (BSE) SEM 
images of the three alloys, revealing a distinct dendritic microstructure. 
The contrast in BSE imaging arises from differences in atomic number, 
with the brighter dendritic regions corresponding to elements of higher 
atomic number. This contrast suggests that high-melting-point elements 
such as Mo and Ta solidify earlier than the lower-melting-point elements 
Ti, Zr, and Hf. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) ( Fig. 6 and Table 3 ) 
analysis confirms significant elemental segregation of Zr, Ta, and Mo, 
while Nb appears relatively uniformly distributed. Evident dendritic 
segregation is observed. As summarized in Table 3 , the dendrite cores 
are enriched in high-melting-point elements Nb, Mo, and Ta, while the 
interdendritic regions are rich in lower-melting-point elements Ti, Zr, 
and Hf.

tures. This is particularly critical for small-data models, where successful 
prediction and screening rely heavily on theoretically guided feature 
selection. To interpret the contribution of individual features, SHapley 
Additive Explanations (SHAP) were employed for model interpretability 
analysis. As shown in Fig. 9 , the SHAP values indicate the positive or 
negative impact of each feature on the predicted yield strength and 
fracture strain. Each point represents a sample, and its position along the 
horizontal axis reflects the magnitude of the SHAP value. A broader 
distribution of SHAP values for a given feature suggests a stronger in -
fluence on yield strength. Features such as the G and t significantly affect 
yield strength, while the Δ H

overall inverse trend between strength and ductility is observed. For 
instance, higher values of G , δ R , δ χ , and VEC contribute positively to 
strength but negatively affect ductility. These relationships are consis -

consistency between model-derived insights and theoretical principles 
underpins the reliability of alloy prediction and optimization.
To further investigate the relationship between elemental composi -
tion and mechanical properties, the optimized ML models were used to 
predict the yield strength and fracture strain of all 346,633 candidate 
alloys, as shown in Fig. 10 . Among the six constituent elements, Mo 
exhibits the most significant influence on mechanical properties. An 
increase in Mo content markedly enhances yield strength but reduces 
ductility, as exemplified by alloys 1 – 3 and 2 – 2. In contrast, lower Mo 
content favors improved ductility but compromises strength, as 
observed in alloy 2 – 1. This trend is not only attributed to Mo ’ s strong 
SSS effect, but also to its high electronegativity and Pugh ratio. As shown 
in Table 5 , increased Mo content leads to greater electronegativity dif -
ferences, which promote the formation of SRO, enhancing both strength 
and toughness [ 50 ]. However, Mo ’ s high Pugh ratio tends to limit 
ductility improvements. Ti generally exhibits intermediate mechanical 
contributions. A moderate amount of Ti helps balance lattice distortion 
in RHEAs, which is beneficial for improving ductility. Excessive Zr 
content appears to adversely affect alloy strength, likely due to Zr ’ s 
relatively low shear modulus. On the other hand, high Hf content con -
tributes positively to ductility. Its low electronegativity increases the 
electronegativity difference, which can promote SRO formation, and its 
low G also favors higher fracture strain. In contrast, high Nb content 
appears detrimental to strength. This may be due to the fact that the 
mixing enthalpies between Nb and most other elements (except Mo) are 
positive, indicating weaker interatomic bonding, which negatively af -
fects strengthening ( Table 6 ). Ta, which has the second-highest shear 
modulus after Mo, contributes positively to strength enhancement when 
present in higher concentrations.

4.2. Deformed microstructure and precipitation strengthening
To further investigate the deformation mechanisms and plasticity of 
the 3 – 2 alloy, its microstructure after 15 % compressive deformation 
was characterized by TEM. As shown in Fig. 11 (a), a large number of 
parallel slip bands are observed, which is a typical feature of planar slip 
behavior. This planar slip behavior originates from the high SFE 
inherent to the BCC crystal structure, which leading to narrow disloca -
tion spread and promoting localized slip along specific slip planes [ 17 ]. 
Fig. 11 (b) reveals slip bands from multiple directions, indicating the 
activation of cross-slip. In Fig. 11 (c – f), numerous short, curved dislo -
cation lines, jogs, Orowan loops, dislocation networks, and dislocation 
tangles are observed, suggesting that, in addition to planar slip, cross- 
slip serves as a supplementary deformation mechanism. Cross-slip- 
induced dislocation interactions facilitate the formation of dense en -
tanglements. In BCC structures, screw dislocations can glide on different 
slip planes when stress is applied, and the availability of multiple slip 
systems enables enhanced plastic deformation capacity [ 52 , 53 ]. The 
intense interactions among dislocations also play a key role. When dis -
locations from different slip systems intersect, they can generate jogs 
and immobilized dislocations, which act as new dislocation sources. The 
activation of local Frank – Read sources leads to continuous emission of 
small dislocation loops, generating new dislocations. These dislocations 
interact, forming complex networks and tangles that further evolve 
under applied stress [ 53 ]. This mechanism helps to mitigate over- 
hardening along a single slip system, thereby improving the alloy ’ s 
work-hardening capability and overall ductility.
The effect of SRO structures is primarily manifested through atomic- 
scale pinning mechanisms. Local compositional fluctuations introduce 
energy barriers that act as short-range obstacles to dislocation motion, 
leading to a wavy or undulating dislocation lines morphology during 
slip, as observed in Fig. 11 (b) and (f). This pinning effect increases the 
local resistance to dislovation motion, requiring dislocations to consume 
more energy to overcome these barriers. Such atomic-level interactions 
enhance the fluctuation of dislocation motion and promote dis -
location – dislocation interactions, thereby contributing to the alloy ’ s 
work-hardening behavior [ 54 , 55 ].
In addition to dislocations, lenticular precipitates were observed in 
the 3 – 2 alloy, as shown in Fig. 12 (a – b). These precipitates exhibit 
lengths of approximately 180 – 420 nm, widths of 7 – 32 nm, and aspect 
ratios ranging from 11 to 26. Structural analysis confirms that the pre -
cipitates possess a hexagonal close-packed (HCP) crystal structure. 
Acting as obstacles to dislocation motion, the precipitates significantly 
impede slip, as evidenced by the locally elevated dislocation density 
around them. Fig. 12 (d) reveals a high density of dislocations in the 
deformed alloy. Dislocation pile-ups and entanglements are clearly 
observed, indicating that the precipitates alter the local stress field by 
blocking dislocation motion. This obstruction induces local stress con -
centrations at the precipitate – matrix interface, increasing the energy 
required for slip and disrupting the continuity of slip bands. This pre -
cipitation strengthening mechanism not only enhances strength by 
hindering dislocation glide, but also contributes to plasticity by regu -
lating dislocation storage and sustaining the alloy ’ s work-hardening 
capability. The precipitation phases require further exploration and 
regulation. Additionally, the grain growth and oxidation resistance of 
alloys in this system at high temperatures also need further 
investigation.
