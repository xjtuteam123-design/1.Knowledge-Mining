1. Introduction 
Zirconium (Zr) and Zr alloys are very interesting structural materials 
used in the nuclear and chemical industries due to their outstanding 
physical and chemical properties, including low neutron absorption 
cross section, excellent irradiation stability, good biocompatibility, and 
superior corrosion resistance [ 1 – 3 ]. In addition to the above advantages, 
metal Zr also has the characteristics of low density, small coefficient of 
expansion, etc. and Zr is very abundant in the earth ’ s crust. These 
characteristics make metal Zr very suitable as a structural material for 
some special fields, such as aerospace structural materials that are very 
sensitive to weight and operate in temperature alternating environ -
ments. However, the strength of conventional Zr alloys generally does 
not exceed 800 MPa, which is unable to meet the requirements for 
structural material strength in other specialized fields such as aerospace 
(the ultimate tensile strength of steel fasteners is generally in the range 
of 1000 – 1300 MPa [ 4 ]). In this context, a new room temperature high 
strength alloy with the nominal composition of Zr – 45Ti – 5Al – 3V(wt%, 
47Zr) have been developed by Liang et al. [ 5 ]. However, compared with 
elevated temperature Ti alloys for aerospace applications, the elevated 
temperature strength or plasticity of 47Zr is still low [ 6 ]. 
The strength of Zr/Ti alloy is largely dependent on the volume 
fraction, morphologies and distribution of α phase. For example, Cao 
et al. [ 7 ] developed a new type of Ti-3Al-5V-5Mo alloy with hierarchical 
submicron α precipitates and nanoscale α ’ precipitates. The combined 
strengthening effect of dislocation accumulation and back stress 
enhancing induced by the hierarchical α / α ’ phases resulted in an ultra- 
high tensile strength of 1585 MPa, while the plastically deformable α 
precipitates helped maintain a moderate plasticity. Devaraj et al. [ 8 ] 
reported that the highest yield strength of ~1650 MPa was achieved in 
Ti185 alloy by a high density of nano-scaled α precipitates in a β matrix. 
However, the dual increase in strength and plasticity can never be 
achieved by merely regulating the phase, but only by making trade-offs 
according to the actual situation. 
Grain refinement is an important strategy to improve the mechanical 
properties of materials [ 9 – 11 ]. However, when the grain size is refined to a 
certain extent, the high-density grain boundaries in the microstructure 
strongly impede dislocation slip. Moreover, the ability of fine/ultra-fine 
grains to store dislocations is very limited, which makes them suscepti -
ble to stress concentration and leads to a significant reduction in plasticity. 
So, how to resolve the inherent contradiction between strength and 
plasticity? Synergistic regulation of phase and microstructure is a

economical to increase the content of nano-scaled α phase by heat 
treatment to improve the strength compared with the preparation of fine-

needs to be achieved by reducing the cooling rate of annealing in the β

grains. In the past, coarse grains were the microstructure that needed to 
be avoided, but recent studies on coarse-grained superplasticity have 
improved this view and also shed some light on our work. It has been well 
accepted for a long time that the fundamental prerequisite for super -
plastic deformation is a relatively-fine equiaxed grain structure with a 
high fraction of high-angle boundaries. However, it has been conclu -

‘non-ideal ’ microstructures such as materials with a large number of 
partially recrystallized microstructures with low angle boundaries 
[ 12 – 14 ], extremely coarse grain materials [ 15 – 17 ] and layered micro -
structures [ 18 , 19 ]. In all such cases, the initial stage of superplastic 
deformation was characterized by the extensive occurrence of dynamic 
recrystallization which converted the “ non-ideal ” initial microstructure 
into the typical fine-grain one. Taking coarse-grained Ti alloy as an 
example, previous studies have found that the superplastic characteris -
tics of coarse-grained Ti alloy are manifested in the rapid proliferation of 
dislocations in the microstructure during deformation, the formation of 
subgrain network, and the initial coarse grains are elongated and refined, 
indicating that dynamic recovery and dynamic recrystallization promote 
the superplastic deformation of coarse-grained Ti alloy [ 20 – 22 ]. 
In summary, the key to improve the elevated temperature perfor -
mance of 47Zr alloy is to increase the content of nano-scaled α phase and 
ensure the occurrence of dynamic recrystallization during deformation. 
The increase of nano-scaled α phase content can be achieved by reducing 
the cooling rate after heat treatment, and the introduction of subgrains is 
an effective way to make the alloy more prone to dynamic recrystalli -
zation in the early stage of plastic deformation [ 23 ]. In the present 
study, a new treatment process, namely, electric field-assisted high 
temperature compression (EFHC), is proposed in order to address the 
poor elevated temperature mechanical properties of 47Zr alloys. 47Zr 
alloy with initial subgrain and millimeter coarse grain was prepared by 
this process. The alloy exhibits excellent elevated temperature me -
chanical properties. 
2. Experimental methods 
2.1. Material 
Sponge Zr (Zr + Hf ≥ 99.5 wt%), sponge Ti (99.7 wt%), industrially 
pure Al (99.5 wt%), and V (99.9 wt%) were used to prepare the 47Zr 
alloy. The alloy was melted three times using a vacuum consumable 
electro-arc furnace to ensure a uniform chemical composition. The ingot 
underwent multiple breakdowns after being held at 1050 
◦

transit temperature for 1.5 h to completely break the coarse grain. The 
ingot was then held at 930

performed, and the ingot was lathed into bars 43 mm in diameter. The 
alloy without EFHC treatment is called as-forged alloy, otherwise it is 
called EFHC-treated alloy. 
2.2. The fabrication process 
A specifically-designed elevated temperature compression mold was 
used for the EFHC treatment in a spark plasma sintering (SPS) machine 
(SPS-3.20 MK-IV; Sumitomo Coal Mining Co., Ltd.). A schematic dia -
gram of the die is shown in Fig. 1 . The operational procedure was as 
follows: first, a cylindrical specimen of the 47Zr alloy with a diameter of 
22 mm and a height of 32 mm was loaded into the mold, which was then 
placed in the discharge plasma sintering equipment. The sintering 
chamber was then evacuated to below 10

4. Discussion 
Generally, coarse grains enhance the plasticity of a material, while 
fine grains enhance the strength [ 35 – 37 ]. The plasticity of coarse grains 
is due to high strain hardening, which is caused by the accumulation of 
defects such as dislocations and twins. In coarse-grained metals, dislo -
cations are generated from traditional dislocation sources such as Frank- 
Read sources [ 38 , 39 ].With the increase in plastic strain, these disloca -
tions effectively accumulate in large particles in the form of dislocation 
cells and subgrain boundaries [ 40 ], as observed in Fig. 11 e. Meanwhile, 
the high strength of fine grains is due to dislocation hardening caused by 
grain boundary strengthening and high dislocation density [ 41 ]. How -
ever, the low ductility of fine grains is also attributed to their small size. 
The smaller the grain size, the more limited is the space for dislocation 
accumulation. During the deformation process, the dislocation density 
easily approaches its saturation level, so the space for further dislocation 
accumulation is limited, thereby reducing the ductility.

deformation in a coarse grained alloy is proposed in Fig. 16 . During the 
hot deformation process, new high-density dislocations (dashed black 
lines) appear in the initial subgrains (dashed green lines) due to the

of the material is derived from the ability to resist plastic deformation, i. 
e., to hinder dislocation slip. The main factors affecting dislocation slip 
are lattice resistance [ 49 – 51 ], interface resistance [ 52 – 55 ], mutual 
entanglement between dislocations (dislocation cells) [ 56 , 57 ] and the

phases in the EFHC sample than in the as-forged sample. In terms of 
crystal structure, hexagonal α ‑ titanium has better strength because it 
has fewer slip systems than body-centered cubic β ‑ titanium [ 62 ]. 
Therefore, more α phase represents higher lattice resistance. The in -
crease of α phase content also represents the increase of α / β phase 
interface, which provides more interface resistance for dislocation slip. 
In addition, the EFHC sample has more LAGB than the as-forged sample, 
which also provides a rich interface resistance. Since dislocation cells 
are formed in both forged and EFHC specimens during deformation, the 
contribution of dislocation cells to the strength improvement of the alloy 
is not considered. In summary, the elevated temperature strength of 
EFHC sample is higher than that of as-forged sample because it has more 
α phase and LAGB than as-forged sample.
