In 2006, a new-ordered L1 2 phase, Co3(Al,W), was discovered that can form
coherently in a face-centered cubic (fcc) A1 Co matrix. Since then, a commu-
nity has developed that is attempting to take these alloys forward into prac-
tical applications in gas turbines. A new candidate polycrystalline Co-Ni c/c¢
superalloy, V208C, is presented that has the nominal composition 36Co-35Ni-
15Cr-10Al-3W-1Ta (at.%). The alloy was produced by conventional powder
metallurgy superalloy methods. After forging, a c¢ fraction of /C2456% and a
secondary c¢ size of 88 nm were obtained, with a grain size of 2.5 lm. The
solvus temperature was 1000 /C176 C. The density was found to be 8.52 g cm
/C03,
which is similar to existing Ni alloys with this level of c¢. The alloy showed the
ﬂow stress anomaly and a yield strength of 920 MPa at room temperature and
820 MPa at 800/C176 C, similar to that of Mar-M247. These values are signiﬁcantly
higher than those found for either conventional solution and carbide-
strengthened Co alloys or the c/c¢ Co superalloys presented in the literature
thus far. The oxidation resistance, with a mass gain of 0.08 mg cm
/C02 in 100 h
at 800 /C176 C, is also comparable with that of existing high-temperature Ni su-
peralloys. These results suggest that Co-based and Co-Ni superalloys may
hold some promise for the future in gas turbine applications.
INTRODUCTION
Nickel-base superalloys are widely used in the hot
section of gas turbines because of their outstanding
balance of high-temperature oxidation and corro-
sion resistance, strength, creep resistance, and fa-
tigue performance. Much academic attention is paid
to alloys for hollow single-crystal blades, which are
produced by Bridgman casting, coated with a ther-
mal barrier coating, and further protected from the
hot gas stream by bleed air ﬁlm cooling. Potentially
of equal engineering importance, however, are
polycrystalline static structures and disk rotors.
These are typically uncoated and, in addition to
creep resistance at the rim, must also have good
strength, fatigue resistance, and toughness. How-
ever, they are only required to sustain rim temper-
atures of around 700–800 /C176 C, as opposed to blades
that operate in a gas stream that is signiﬁcantly
above their melting point. The most modern such
disk alloys are fabricated by powder metallurgy
from inert gas atomized powders.
In common with blade alloys, there is a concern
that the temperature capability of Ni polycrystalline
static structure and disk alloys is reaching a pla-
teau. This puts at risk continuing improvements in
gas turbine operating efﬁciency, which from a
thermodynamic standpoint is limited by the turbine
entry temperature. With global air travel demand
exhibiting long-term growth rates of around 6% p.a.
and concerns over CO
2 emissions and so-called
‘‘peak oil,’’ there is a strong driver for alloys with
enhanced temperature capability that retain a rea-
sonable balance of mechanical properties, oxidation
resistance, and density. Fuel efﬁciency is also a key
differentiator for gas turbine manufacturers in the
market and a strong determinant of airline proﬁt-
ability, to the extent that old aircraft are often re-
tired after only 20 years, long before the end of their
service life.

Therefore, it was fortunate that in 2006, Sato
et al. announced the discovery of a new potential
superalloy system1 in which a ductile intermetallic
cubic phase precipitates coherently from its parent
cubic matrix, based on the newly discovered L1 2
Co3(Al,W) precipitate in an A1 face-centered cubic
(fcc) Co matrix. Since then, several groups world-
wide have been developing this alloy system, in
most instances for blade alloys. The great advantage
of the Co
3(Al,W) system would seem to be the slow
diffusion rate and low solubility of W in the matrix,
limiting coarsening, which might enable greater
endurance at higher temperatures. From a casting
perspective, the freezing range is reduced, as poin-
ted out by Tsunekane et al.,
2 which may be of
interest for very large cast industrial gas turbine
blades and guide vanes. To date, oxidation resis-
tance has proved to be a challenge, although B
seems to have a substantial beneﬁcial effect, as does
Cr.
3
The ﬂow stress anomaly (increasing yield
strength with temperature) has been observed; 4
however, most of the Co superalloys presented to
date have relatively low solvus temperatures com-
pared with Ni superalloys with the same volume
fraction of c¢. In addition, the Co superalloys show
a positive misﬁt d in excess of 0.5%, where

performance generally improves as the misﬁt de-
creases because the concomitant reduction in sur-
face energy reduces the driving force for
coarsening. Retaining a ﬁne c¢ distribution is
important in creep because restricting the c chan-
nel width acts to increase the bowing stress of the
mobile dislocations and thereby strengthen the al-
loy.

As previously mentioned, the deleterious effects
of high misﬁt will be mitigated by the low diffu-
sivity of W in the c. This also has the side effect of
requiring long aging times in many academic
studies to produce c¢ precipitates that are large
enough to image satisfactorily. In industrial
practice, it would be desirable to avoid such
expensive heat treatments, and to retain a ﬁne c¢
distribution.
Over the last several years, we have been
exploring whether viable polycrystalline Co-base
superalloys can be produced, with improved oxida-
tion resistance and mechanical performance com-
pared with conventional Ni superalloys. The current
contribution provides a snapshot of the current
status of this work, using the example of a single
alloy, V208C, that was subject to patent protection
in 2013.

Most of the Co superalloys presented to date 2,4,12
have been targeted at single-crystal turbine blade
applications and have compositions in the region of
Co-10Al-9(W + Ta) (at.%). Such high refractory
metal contents result in an alloy density of more
than 9 g cm
/C03, whereas alloys for disks in particular
usually have densities of<8.5 g cm /C03. Therefore, in
the present case an additional beneﬁt of the intro-
duction of Ni is that it shifts the c¢ stoichiometry to
higher Al contents, allowing a reduction in the
overall alloy density, to 8.52 g cm
/C03 for the present
alloy. For comparison, LSHR13 has a density that is
2% lower, 8.35 g cm /C03.
Meher et al. also found14 that in the Co-Co3(Al,W)
case, signiﬁcant solubility of W in the matrix re-
mained, which has the beneﬁt of allowing for some
solid-solution strengthening of the matrix. In a
similar way, the solubility for Al in Co is signiﬁ-
cantly higher than in Ni, and so Co matrices allow
for density reduction by permitting a greater Al
content than would otherwise be the case.
Therefore, the use of a 1:1 Co/Ni ratio in the
present alloy represents a balance that aims to fulﬁl
the demand for corrosion resistance, density and
matrix strength simultaneously, while retaining the
beneﬁt of having a high refractory metal content in
the c¢ phase, which is the fundamental attraction of
Co-base c/c¢ superalloys over Ni-base superalloys.
The alloy powder was manufactured by vacuum
induction melting and inert gas atomization, and
then consolidated by hot isostatic pressing (HIP) by
ATI Powder Metals (Pittsburgh, PA). It was then
isothermally forged by ATI Ladish Forging (Cud-
ahy, WI). After forging, the material was subjected
to a heat treatment of 1050 /C176 C for 30 min, above the
solvus temperature of 1000 /C176 C.

DISCUSSION AND OUTLOOK
At this stage, together with the work published by
Titus et al., 27–29 Suzuki et al., 4,23,30 Shinagawa
et al.,10,31 and Klein et al.,3,32 the following observa-
tions about the prospects for the development of Co-
Al-W and Co-Ni-Cr-Al-W alloys can be made. The ﬂow
stress anomaly has been observed by several groups
but at similar temperature to those found for Ni-base
superalloys. Minimum creep rates as low as 10
/C08 s/C01
at 900/C176 C and 310 MPa have been reported27,29 for the
single-crystal alloy Co-29Ni-9.8Al-6.3W-2.4Ta-6.4Cr
(at.%), which is similar to the performance of Rene ´
N4, a modern Ni turbine blade alloy.
Oxidation performance similar to that of modern
Ni disk alloys also seems to be attainable; given that
oxidation damage is heavily implicated in low-cycle
fatigue degradation,
33,34 this must be an essential
precondition for the production of fatigue-resistant
alloys. Because density directly impacts weight and
the centrifugal loads that have to be sustained in
service, the high density associated with high W
contents is detrimental, but it seems that alloys can
be produced with similar densities to current high-
temperature alloys for blades, static parts, and disks.
The solvus temperatures attained remain similar,
or even slightly lower, than those for competitor Ni
superalloys, and the freezing ranges are substantially
reduced. Although both these factors have beneﬁts for
processability, a concern would be that the amount of
c¢ present at service temperatures may be limited
compared with that observed at room temperature.
Therefore, higher solvus temperatures are desired.
This will entail addingc¢ formers such as Al, W, Ta, Ti,
and Nb.

CONCLUSION
Results for a polycrystalline Co-Ni superalloy,
V208C, have been presented. The alloy shows good
oxidation resistance, 0.08 mg cm
/C02 mass gain after
100 h at 800 /C176 C, a density of 8.52 g cm /C03,a c¢ solvus
of 1000 /C176 C, and a room-temperature ﬂow stress of
920 MPa. The ﬂow stress anomaly was observed,
with a secondary c¢ fraction of /C2456% and average
size of 88 nm. Substantial partitioning of Co to the c
and Ni to the c¢ was observed (around 2:1 in each
case). The solubility of Cr in the c¢, 4.7 at.%, was
greater than in the case of Ni superalloys. Al
showed similar partitioning to that observed in Ni
superalloys, and substantial solubility for W in thec
(2 at.%) was observed. As in Ni superalloys, ser-
rated grain boundaries and bimodal c/c¢ distribu-
tions could be produced if desired. Although the
present alloy is only an early attempt, it seems that
it may be possible to develop Co-Ni superalloys that
are attractive for gas turbine applications.
