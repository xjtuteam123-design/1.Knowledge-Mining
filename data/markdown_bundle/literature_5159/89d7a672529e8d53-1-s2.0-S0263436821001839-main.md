ABSTRACT 
In this study, the cracking behavior and microstructural, mechanical and thermal characteristics of tung -
sten – rhenium (W – Re) binary alloys fabricated by laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) were investigated. Four bulk 
specimens were prepared by L-PBF: pure W, W – 1%Re, W – 3%Re and W – 10%Re (percentages indicate the mass 
percent of Re). High-density bulk specimens (relative density > 98.0%) were obtained for pure W and W – Re 
alloys under the same laser irradiation conditions. The columnar grains elongated along the building direction 
were gradually refined as the Re content increased. The most remarkable grain refinement was observed for the 
W – 10%Re alloy. Hardness under a high-temperature environment increased with increasing Re content; the 
micro-Vickers hardnesses of pure W and W – 10%Re at 400 
◦
C were 179 ± 4 HV0.1/30 and 281 ± 5 HV0.1/30, 
respectively. Observations with a scanning electron microscope revealed that the 10 mass% Re addition resulted 
in a shorter and narrower crack morphology in comparison with pure W and consequently reduced crack area by 
59%. Furthermore, the anisotropy of the thermal diffusivity was mitigated in the high Re content specimens, 
suggesting that, at high Re content, thermal diffusivity is affected less by cracks than by the effect of Re atoms on 
heat carrier transfer via isotropic scattering. 
1. Introduction 
Additive manufacturing (AM), otherwise known as 3D printing, is a 
layer-by-layer processing technology based on slice data prepared from 
3D computer-aided design (CAD) information. According to the ASTM 
F2792 standard terminology [ 1 ], AM processes fall into seven cate -
gories: binder jetting, directed energy deposition, material extrusion, 
material jetting, powder bed fusion, sheet lamination and vat photo -
lamination. These processes have been used to optimize the design of 
complex components and create new design concepts, as well as to 
improve the functionality of existing designs in various industries, such 
as the automobile, medical, and aerospace industries [ 2 – 5 ]. Research 
into nuclear fusion development has investigated the applicability of 
type 316 L stainless steel [ 6 ] and low-activation ferritic/martensitic 
steel [ 7 – 9 ] prepared using laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) to 
manufacturing components for ITER and test blanket modules (TBMs). 
Furthermore, AM approaches such as binder jetting and L-PBF of SiC 
materials have been studied for nuclear applications [ 10 ]. Thus, AM is 
considered as one of the technologies for manufacturing components for 
nuclear applications. 
Tungsten (W) has several properties that make it a promising 
candidate for plasma-facing materials (PFMs) for use in fusion reactors 
[ 11 ], including a high melting point (3420 
◦
C), high thermal conduc -
tivity (174 W/mK at 27 
◦
C), good strength at high temperatures and 
good neutron irradiation resistance. W components are traditionally 
produced by rolling and swaging processes based on powder metallurgy, 
but recently, the applicability of AM as a new method for processing W 
has been widely investigated. The major AM processes that have been 
applied to pure W are L-PBF [ 12 – 22 ], electron-beam PBF [ 23 ] and 
directed energy deposition (wire and arc AM) [ 24 ]. In particular, there 
are many studies on L-PBF using pure W powder. Pure W bulk specimens 
with relative densities exceeding 98.0% have been prepared by L-PBF, 
by optimizing the laser irradiation conditions [ 15 , 18 , 19 , 21 ]. To achieve 
high density, complete melting of W by laser irradiation cannot be

Research into fusion reactor materials has explored several ap -

its use in PFMs. Alloying [ 27 – 29 ], dispersion strengthening [ 29 – 31 ] and

propagation, by improving thermomechanical properties, but optimi -

W prepared using L-PBF. 
The present study used rhenium (Re), which forms solid solutions 
with W, as the additive element. Because Re is one of the elements 
produced by transmutation of W in a fusion reactor environment, the 
thermal and mechanical properties of W – Re alloys have been studied 
extensively. The solid solution composition range of Re to W is ~26 mass 
%, and the Re content strongly affects the thermal and mechanical 
properties of the alloy [ 34 – 38 ]. The major effects of introducing Re are 
increased high-temperature strength, improved ductility at low tem -
perature and decreased DBTT. A peculiarity of Re addition is that both 
softening and hardening occur, depending on the Re content and test 
temperature. For example, at 162 
◦
C, solid solution softening occurs at 
low Re content ( < 3.3 mass%), while solid solution hardening takes 
place at high Re content ( > 7.7 mass%) [ 39 ]. The solid solution soft -
ening in W – Re alloys is thought to arise from the effect of Re solutes on 
the double-kink nucleation process [ 40 ]. However, it is still unclear 
whether this unique change in mechanical properties due to Re alloying 
is effective in crack suppression in L-PBF W. 
The purpose of the present study is to reduce the crack density in L- 
PBF W by applying the Re alloying approach. In this paper, we describe 
(1) the crystallographic properties and microstructure of W – Re binary 
alloys prepared by L-PBF, (2) their cracking behavior and (3) their 
hardness and thermal diffusivity, and discuss the effects of Re alloying. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Powder material and L-PBF procedures 
Four bulk specimens were prepared in the present study: pure W, 
W – 1%Re, W – 3%Re, and W – 10%Re (in mass%). A L-PBF machine used 
was EOSINT M280 (EOS, Krailling, Germany) and equipped with a 400 
W-class fibre laser (maximum input power: 370 W, wavelength: 1070 
nm and spot diameter: ~0.1 mm). The W – Re binary alloys were fabri -
cated from mixed powder consisting of pure W particles and pure Re 
particles. The mean volume diameter of the W and Re particles were 19 
and 14 μ m, respectively. The chemical compositions of the powder 
particles (pure W and pure Re) reported by the supplier are given in 
Table 1 . The oxygen content in the Re powder was significantly higher 
than that in W powder. The amounts of each powder were adjusted to 
achieve the target composition, and the powders were then blended well 
using a rotary mixer to uniformly disperse the Re particles. Fig. 1 shows, 
as an example, chemical composition maps of a W – 10%Re mixed 
powder. The images were obtained using an electron probe micro- 
analyzer (EPMA; JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The Re particles in the 
mixed powder were uniformly dispersed by adhering to the W particles, 
and the shapes of the Re particles were distorted, suggesting deforma -
tion during the mixing process. The pure W and W – Re bulk specimens 
were prepared by applying the laser irradiation conditions optimized for 
pure W in our previous study [ 21 ] (laser power: 370 W, scan speed: 500 
mm/s, scan distance: 0.09 mm and layer thickness: 0.02 mm) in an Ar 
atmosphere. The oxygen concentration in the process chamber was 
maintained below 0.1% to minimize oxidation. Carbon steel was used 
for the base plate and not preheated during L-PBF. The prepared bulk 
specimens were not post-annealed.

3. Results 
3.1. Phase analysis and microstructure 
Table 1 summarizes the relative densities R
d 
(%) and chemical 
compositions of pure W and W – Re alloys. The relative densities of the 
W – Re bulk specimens were greater than 98.0% and comparable to the 
relative density of the pure W bulk specimen obtained in our previous 
study [ 21 ]. The Re content in the W – Re binary alloys was very close to 
the target composition. The impurity contents in W – 1%Re and W – 3%Re 
were almost the same as those of the pure W bulk, but a slight increase in 
oxygen, nitrogen and carbon concentrations was observed in the W – 10% 
Re specimen. This was attributed to the oxygen present in the Re powder 
( Table 1 ) and not to oxidation during the laser process. Oxygen, nitrogen 
and carbon impurities should be kept at low levels in the final product, 
because their presence in W increases the DBTT [ 43 ]. Future work will 
seek to further reduce the oxygen concentration in the process chamber, 
as well as the impurity content of the raw particles. 
Fig. 3 shows the XRD results of pure W and the W – Re alloys. Figs. 3 
(a) and (b) show the bulk and powder XRD profiles, respectively. The 
W – Re mixed powder diffraction patterns contained both W and Re 
peaks, and the Re peak intensity increased with the proportion of Re 
added to the W powder. By contrast, the only peaks observed in the XRD 
profiles of as-fabricated W – Re alloys were single-phase W peaks ( Fig. 3 
(a)); all of the Re diffraction peaks observed for the powder form dis -
appeared. The disappearance of the Re peaks suggests that W and Re 
formed a solid solution. The black squares in Fig. 4 show the lattice 
constants of the W – Re alloys as a function of the Re content, while the 
red circles show the lattice constants of the stress-relieved W and W – Re 
discs reported by Fujitsuka et al. [ 36 ]. The introduction of Re atoms into 
the W lattice resulted in a decrease in the lattice constant. Although the 
lattice constants of the pure W and W – Re alloys prepared by L-PBF were 
slightly larger than those reported by Fujitsuka et al., the declining trend 
of the lattice constants with increasing Re content (black line in Fig. 4 ) is 
consistent with their observations (red line in Fig. 4 ) [ 36 ]. 
Fig. 5 shows the results of chemical composition analysis of as- 
fabricated L-PBF specimens using EPMA. Composition maps were ob -
tained from the central area of the BSE image. These results confirmed 
that increases in the amount of Re added led to decrease in the intensity 
of the W-characteristic X-rays and increase in the intensity of the Re- 
characteristic X-rays. According to Table 1 , the oxygen content 
increased slightly with increasing Re content, but no significant change 
was observed in the mapping images. As indicated by the circles in Fig. 5 
labelled ‘Area ’ , the mapping analysis of the W – Re alloys revealed local 
enrichment and depletion of Re. Local composition analysis was thus 
performed in the normal solid solution regions (Area 1), and in regions 
with Re enrichment (Area 2). In the W – Re alloys, the Re contents in the 
Area 1 were in good agreement with those shown in Table 1 . However, 
the Re contents of the enrichment regions (Area 2) were 4.3, 15.1 and 
14.2 mass% in W – 1%Re, W – 3%Re and W – 10%Re, respectively. 
Furthermore, the bulk XRD profiles of W – 3%Re and W – 10%Re in Fig. 3 
(a) do not show the formation of intermetallic compounds. These results 
indicate that Re enrichment occurred within the solubility limit (26 
mass%). The Re enrichment in the W – Re alloys showed that the mean 
diffusion distance of Re atoms during rapid melting and solidification by 
laser irradiation was insufficient to achieve a uniform Re distribution at 
the microscopic scale. In L-PBF of W, the cooling rate of the molten 
metal reaches about 5 × 10
6 
K/s [ 25 ]. It is therefore difficult to elimi -
nate Re enrichment solely by optimizing the laser irradiation conditions. 
To address this issue, future work will investigate preparation of W – Re 
bulk specimens using W – Re solid solution powder.

3.2. Cracking behavior 
Fig. 8 shows a BSE images of ion-milled vertical cross-section spec -
imens. Cracks propagating preferentially along the BD were observed at 
high density regardless of the Re content, and a small number of pores 
were also found. The crack width and length gradually decreased as the 
Re content increased. The crack area ratios evaluated by the two- 
threshold treatment of the BSE images were 2.45%, 1.86%, 1.52%, 
and 1.01% for pure W, W – 1%Re, W – 3%Re, and W – 10%Re, respectively; 
it gradually decreased as the Re content increased. It was revealed that 
the 10% Re addition reduced the crack area ratio by 59% in comparison 
with pure W. However, cracks cannot be detected if their width is too 
small to be observed at this magnification. Therefore, the accuracy of the 
crack area ratio thus evaluated depends more strongly on the width than 
the length of the cracks.

4. Discussion 
Cracking in the L-PBF W materials could not be completely sup -
pressed even by the addition of Re up to 10 mass%. Nevertheless, the 
increasing Re content gradually reduced the crack area ratio evaluated 
based on the low magnification BSE image by modifying crack 
morphology. Simultaneously, it was clarified that the microstructure, 
hardness, and thermal characteristics gradually change depending on 
the Re content. In particular, one of the factors that caused the transition 
to shorter and narrower crack morphology is change in grain 
morphology (grain refinement) associated with the Re addition. 
A W – Re melt was formed by heating the W and Re particles simul -
taneously and instantaneously above their melting points (3420 
◦
C and 
3186 
◦
C, respectively) by laser irradiation. When the temperature of the 
molten W – Re dropped rapidly below the melting point, the W – Re so -
lidified into a solid solution through nucleation and grain growth. The 
solidified W – Re layer was then subjected to repeated heat loads by laser 
irradiation to form the upper layers. These heat loads caused remelting 
of the solidified layer or additional grain growth. Suppression of grain 
boundary migration during grain growth by addition of substituted Re 
solute atoms into the W matrix has also been reported [ 44 , 45 ]. There -
fore, the difference in grain size between pure W and W – Re alloys can be 
attributed to the difference in resistance to grain boundary migration. 
Grains elongated along the BD in pure W prepared by L-PBF assist in the 
formation of cracks in the vertical direction [ 25 ]. The grain refinement 
achieved by Re addition should increase the area of grain boundaries 
and consequently reduce the stress driving crack propagation by 
dispersing the force induced by the heat. This is considered to be the 
cause of the reduced crack area ratio observed for W – 10%Re. 
The increasing Re content dramatically reduced the thermal diffu -
sivity. Its reduction rate at RT by 10mass% Re addition was 68% in the Z 
direction and 26% in the XY direction, compared with pure W. The 
decrease in thermal diffusivity induced by the Re addition leads to an 
increase in the time for solidification of the molten metal and subse -
quent cooling, which are expected to have two positive effects on the 
reduction in crack density. First, increased time to wet the surface of 
solidified layer may assist the penetration of molten metal into open 
cracks. The other is the reduction in the temperature gradient across the 
boundary between the molten pool and the surrounding material caused 
by the reduced cooling rate of the solidified layer. In particular, this is 
expected to mitigate thermal stress by local heating of L-PBF [ 48 , 49 ]. 
As reported in our previous paper [ 21 ], high temperature (above 
1900 
◦
C) post-annealing reduces the number density of nanopores via 
grain growth and gas release. The gas retention at the grain boundaries 
weakens the grain boundary bonding [ 26 ]. At high Re content, the so -
lidified layer is exposed to higher temperatures for longer time due to its 
lower thermal conductivity. This should promote gas release and 
improve grain boundary structure, though the nanopores observed at

5. Conclusion 
To clarify the effect of Re alloying on crack suppression, we inves -
tigated the cracking behavior, microstructure, hardness, and thermal 
diffusivity of high-density W – Re binary alloys prepared by L-PBF. The 
following results were obtained: 
1. As the Re content increased, the columnar grains elongated along the 
BD were gradually refined. Particularly remarkable grain refinement 
was observed in W – 10%Re. The average grain diameters in the 
horizontal cross-section in pure W and W – 10%Re were 57 ± 40 and 
36 ± 19 μ m, respectively. 
2. The Re addition led to increase in the nanopore area ratio. Never -
theless, no significant increase in the density of agglomerated 
nanopores at the grain boundaries was observed due to the grain 
refinement by the Re addition. 
3. The increasing Re content resulted in gradual reduction in the length 
and width of cracks. The crack area ratio gradually decreased as the 
Re content increased, and it was 2.45% and 1.01% for pure W and 
W – 10% Re, respectively. The 10 mass% Re addition reduced the 
crack area by 59% in comparison with pure W. 
4. Hardness at high temperatures ( ≧ 600 
◦
C) increased as the Re content 
increased. This indicated that the Re atoms introduced into the W 
matrix functioned as a solid solution hardener at high temperatures. 
On the other hand, at low temperatures (RT), solid solution softening 
was observed for W – 3%Re. 
5. The thermal diffusivity decreased as the Re content increased. 
Thermal anisotropy due to the directional relationship between heat 
flux and the BD was observed. The degree of anisotropy (quantified 
by the anisotropy parameter, D
z
/ D
xy
) decreased as the Re content 
increased. This suggests that at high Re content, cracks play a less 
important role in determining thermal diffusivity than the isotropic 
scattering effect caused by Re atoms on heat carrier transfer.
