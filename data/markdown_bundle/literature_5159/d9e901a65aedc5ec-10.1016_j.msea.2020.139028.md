1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) and medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) al -
ways composed of three or more principal elements with concentrations 
of 5 – 35 at.% [ 1 ]. These HEAs are promising structure material, due to 
their superior combination properties [ 2 ], such as excellent corrosion 
resistance [ 3 , 4 ], high ductility and excellent radiation tolerance [ 5 ]. The 
HEAs with single face-centered-cubic (FCC) structure usually show an 
excellent ductility but relatively low strength [ 6 ]. Similarly, the HEAs 
with single body-centered-cubic (BCC) always display high strength but 
relatively poor ductility [ 7 , 8 ]. Developing advanced materials with a 
combination of high strength and high ductility is significant chal -
lenging for researchers. Consequently, many researchers try to adjust 
microstructure to obtain high strength and ductility in HEAs [ 9 – 12 ]. 
In order to overcome the limitation in engineering application 
caused by the low strength of single-phase FCC HEAs [ 1 ]. The strength of 
these HEAs can enhance through precipitation strengthening and solid 
solution hardening mechanism by adding an element with a large radius 
(Al, Mo, Ti, etc. ) [ 13 – 16 ]. For instance, as for CoCrFeNiMo

4. Discussion 
Fig. 6 Shows the microstructure characterization of the before and 
after deformation samples annealed at 725 
�
C. Fig. 6 a shows the alloy 
without deformation, which includes the recrystallized grains, pre -
cipitates and annealed twins, especially the 9R structure were outlined 
by the red box. Fig. 6 b shows the HRTEM image and SAED pattern of the 
9R structure. This structure comprised of a stacking fault ribbon con -
sisting of a repeating unit of 9{111} atomic layers can act as additional 
barriers to dislocation motion and sources for dislocation multiplication 
[ 26 , 27 ]. Fig. 6 c shows a high density of dislocations and deformation 
twins near the precipitate as the alloy with deformation at 15%. At the 
first stage of stress deformation, the dislocations in the matrix initiate 
and start move under the action of stress [ 28 ]. These dislocations will be 
blocked by the precipitates when move to it [ 29 ]. At the last stage of 
stress rupture, dislocations pile up in front of the precipitates, which 
indicate the precipitate acts as a barrier to dislocation moving to 
enhance the strength of the alloy [ 30 ]. An HRTEM image in Fig. 6 d 
shows dislocation motion was prevented by the boundary of the 
precipitate. 
The strengthening mechanism, such as dislocation strengthening, 
fine-grain strengthening, precipitate strengthening and solution 
strengthening of the conventional alloy also suitable for the HEAs [ 27 , 
31 ]. The high strength and ductility enhanced in this study due to the 
synergetic strengthening effect, including the dislocation strengthening, 
sold-solution strengthening, precipitate strengthening and fine-grain 
strengthening [ 32 ]. For the non-recrystallized structures always 
possessed a high density of dislocation and dislocation cells, these high 
density of dislocation not only enhance the yield strength by dislocation 
forest hardening but also enables a good ductility by the glide of existing 
mobile dislocations [ 33 ]. The recrystallized grains always with small 
sizes, base on the Hall-Petch strengthening effect, these fine grains can 
effectively prevent dislocation moving by increasing the length of the 
grain boundary to enhance the strength of the alloy [ 34 ]. A large 
number of twins are produced during annealing and deformation pro -
cess, and it ’ s important that the extensive and dense deformation 
nano-twins play an important role in alloy deformation process, since 
new interfaces introduced by twinning reduce the dislocation mean free 
path which has been referred as to “ dynamic Hall-Petch ” effect [ 35 ]. As 
shown in Fig. 6 c and d, the boundary of μ -precipitate can effectively act 
as a barrier to dislocation motion, and enhance the strength [ 36 , 37 ].
