Abstract
Alloying non-metallic elements can not only change the structure of alloys, but also tailor the
mechanical and physical properties. The microstructure, mechanical and magnetic properties of
(CoCrFeNi)100-xGex (molar ratio, x= 0 ∼ 33) high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have been systematically
studied in this paper. The experimental results show that the(CoCrFeNi)100-xGex HEAs transform
from a face-centered cubic(FCC) to a body-centered cubic(BCC) solid solution when 10 x  25,
and a hexagonal intermetallic compound(IM) formed at x= 33. Tensile measurement revealed that
the strength and plasticity of the alloy were enhanced simultaneously with Ge addition in the single
FCC region (x  10), a decrease in ductility but sharp increase of strength was found in the alloys when
the BCC phase starts to appear. Theoretical calculations and magnetic hysteresis loop measurements
show that the alloys transform gradually from a paramagnetic to a ferromagnetic state at room
temperature.
1. Introduction
As a new alloy design strategy, the concept of high-entropy alloys(HEAs) with multi-principal elements wasﬁrst
proposed by Yehet al in 2004 [1]. Different from traditional metallurgical theory, HEAs have high mixed
entropy which favors them to form simple solid solution structures, such as FCC, BCC and HCP(hexagonal
close-packed) phases, rather than complex intermetallic compounds. Due to solid solution strengthening and
the intrinsic structural characters, HEAs may display excellent properties, such as high strength[2– 4], high
thermal stability [5– 7], extreme damage-tolerance[8], good wear[9, 10] and corrosion resistance[11, 12], which
greatly promotes the application of high entropy alloys as functional and engineering materials.
As one of the earliest developed systems with a single FCC structure, CoCrFeNi alloys were studied
extensively because of the excellent mechanical properties, such as plasticity and toughness. In contrast, low
strength and hardness also greatly hinder its future development and application prospects. Defects engineering
can be used to improve the mechanical properties of HEAs by doping some other elements with different atomic
radii in the base alloy. The introducing of speciﬁc element brought additional beneﬁts to alloys due to the
‘cocktail effect’. Previous studies have suggested that addition of Sn and Al can enhance the corrosion and
oxidation resistance [13– 15], respectively. In addition, non-metallic elements are also alloyed to HEAs, such as C
and Si [16, 17]. Huang et al [18] reported that addition of C element can effectively enhance the compressive
yield strength and hardness. Huanget al [19] have found that the formation of BCC phase is facilitated by doping
appropriate Si elements, which has a signiﬁcant impact on the wear resistance and micro-hardness of the alloy.
They have also reported the structure of equimolar FeCrCoNiGe HEA, which is a mixture of FCC and BCC
phases. And the Curie temperature of this alloy is well below room temperature. Lamet al [20] and Liu et al [21
]
reported the regulation of mechanical and corrosion properties of CoCrFeNi-based HEAs by Ge and found that
Ge doping (atomic molar ratio of Ge less than 7%) improved the mechanical properties and corrosion resistance
of the alloy. Then, Lizárragaet al [22] used quantum mechanics model to study the effect of Ge element on the

2. Experimental
The HEAs ingots with nominal composition of(CoCrFeNi)100-xGex (0  x  33, at%) were prepared from pure
elements which purity higher than 99.9%(wt%) using arc-melting. The as-cast ingots were remelted four times
for making sure the chemical homogeneity. For convenience, the seven HEAs are named Ge0, Ge5, Ge10, Ge15,
Ge20, Ge25 and Ge33, respectively, and the numbers represent the molar ratio percentage of Ge in the alloys.
The crystal structure of the ingots was examined by x-ray diffractometer(Bruker D8 ADVANCE) with Cu
Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm) for 2θ scan range from 20° to 100°. The microstructure was observed by
scanning electron microscopy(SEM, ZEISS Gemini 300). Electron backscatter diffraction(EBSD) and energy-
dispersive x-ray spectroscopy(EDS) were used to analysis the phase structure and chemical compositions,
respectively. The Vickers hardness(HV, 1000STA) measurements were conducted with a load of 200 g in 10 s
load holding, and the results were calculated from at least 9 individual tests. The tensile samples with a thickness
of 2 mm, a length of 12 mm and a width of 2.2 mm were processed by an electric discharging machining(EDM)
wire cutting. Tensile tests were carried out on a LE5105(Shanghai Lishi Instrument) universal electronic tensile
testing machine with a strain rate of 0.5 mm min
−1 at room temperature. The magnetic hysteresis loop curves
were obtained by a vibrating sample magnetometer(VSM, Quantum Design MPMS3) at an internal magnetic
ﬁeld up to 7 T.

3.2. Micro-hardness and tensile properties
The microhardness of all the alloys was tested with a Vicker hardness tester with a load of 200 g. Figure6(a)
shows the microhardness of(CrFeCoNi)100-xGex (0  x  25) alloys as a function of Ge content. With the x
increases from 0 to 10, the hardness increases from HV0.2 128.1 to HV0.2 184.6. When the BCC phase is formed
at x = 15, the hardness increases rapidly with the increase of Ge content. When x= 25, the transformation is
completed, and the hardness reaches the maximum HV0.2 710.4. The dependence of hardness values of FCC
phase and BCC phase on Ge content were linearlyﬁtted. It is obvious that the increasing rate of hardness value
for the FCC phase at x 10 is far less than that at 15 x  25. It is well known that the hardness of BCC phase is
much higher than that of FCC phase. We predict that the intersection of the twoﬁtted lines as the phase
transition point (about x∼13), which is consistent with the XRD and SEM analyses. When the Ge content
increased to 30 x, the hardness continues to increase, which was caused by the appearance of IM phase. With
the increase of hardness, the sample also became more brittle. As can be seen inﬁgure 4(h), the appearance of
many cracks was caused by the increase of brittleness.
As shown inﬁgure 6(b), the engineering stress-strain curves of the alloy at room temperature were
compared. It can be seen from the stress-strain curve that Ge10 alloy has higher yield strength(YS, 277 MPa),
ultimate tensile strength(UTS, 692 MPa) and elongation (EL, 62.8%) than those of CoCrFeNi-based alloy. We
believe that the improved YS and UTS is mainly due to the addition Ge which enhanced by solid solution
strengthening. However, the ductility of the alloys diminishes and the alloys become brittle when the BCC phase
formed.

3.3. Magnetic properties
The variation of the coercivity(Hc) and saturation magnetization(Ms) of the alloys with different content of Ge
are shown inﬁgure 7. As we know that the CoCrFeNi alloy behaves paramagnetic at room temperature. When x

appearance of BCC phase. With the increase of BCC phase volume fraction in Ge25 alloy, the coercivity(Mc)
value decreases to 28.5 Oe and the saturation(Ms) magnetization increases to 46.1 emu/g(as shown inﬁgure 7).

4. Conclusions
In summary, the phase formation, mechanical and magnetic properties of(CrFeCoNi)100-xGex alloys are
systematically studied.
1. XRD, SEM and EBSD results revealed that (CrFeCoNi)100-xGex alloys transformed from FCC to BCC
structure during 10x25, and nearly a single hexagonal structure was obtained at x= 33. The negative
mixing enthalpy between Ge-(Co,Cr, Fe, Ni) is larger than that of other atomic pairs, which promotes the
above transformation in HEAs.
2. The addition of the Ge element enhanced the FCC phase of YS, UTS and EL. When X = 10, YS, UTS and EL
reach the highest values of 277 MPa, 692 MPa, and 63%, respectively.
3. The hardness of the (CrFeCoNi)100-xGex alloys increases from HV 128.1 (Ge0) to HV 710.4 (Ge25) with the
increase of Ge content. The increase of hardness in single FCC phase(x10) is due to the solution hardening,
and the increase of hardness to the alloys with 10x25 is due to the appearance of BCC phase.
4. The addition of small amount of Ge (x<10) makes the alloys remaining in paramagnetic and FCC structure.
A paramagnetic to ferromagnetic transformation is accompanied by the emergence of the BCC phase. The
saturation of magneticﬁeld strength and coercivity are optimal in pure BCC phase with values of 46.1 emu/g
and 28.5 Oe, respectively.
