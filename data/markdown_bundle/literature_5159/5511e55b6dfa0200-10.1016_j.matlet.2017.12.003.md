Abstract 
In this study, we demonstrate the successful brazing and superior mechanical 
performance of laser brazed Inconel® 718 using a Ni-Mn-Fe-Co-Cu high entropy alloy 
filler metal with a nominal Ni:Cu:Co:Fe:Mn molar ratio of 1:1:1:0.25:1.75. A maximum 
220 MPa shear strength is achieved. The high entropy alloy experiences the hardness 
increases 3-4 times after brazing. If the brazing temperature is higher than 1200 °C, the 
bonding strength suffers due to excessivediffusion between the base metal and the filler

metal. The hardness behavior is attributed to a combination of the cocktail effect and 
lattice distortion induced by brazing. 
Keywords: welding, metals and alloys, laser processing, high entropy alloy, nickel

1. Introduction 
Inconel® 718 (IN718) is a popular nickel superalloy with good high-temperature 
mechanical properties and high corrosion-oxidation resistance [1]. Brazing is an 
attractive joining method for nickel superalloys because of minimal microstructural 
changes compared to welding and application to weld repair. However, few filler metals 
have been explored for IN718 [2, 3]. High entropy alloys (HEAs) are multi-principal 
component alloys with high mixing entropy and contain five or more elements. HEAs are 
attractive materials for various applications due to their unique properties which arise 
from four core effects: high entropy effect, sluggish diffusion kinetics, severe lattice 
distortion (LD), and cocktail effect (CE) [4]. The cocktail effect states that the material 
properties are determined by the constituent elements and the mutual interactions 
between dissimilar elements.. 
In this work, we demonstrate the potential of a new Ni-Mn-Fe-Co-Cu HEAl for 
laser brazing of IN718. We will elucidate the mechanical properties of HEA-brazed 
IN718 and discuss the LD, diffusion kinetics, and CE on the mechanical properties. 
2. Experimental Procedure

## Page 4

The HEA was synthesized by induction melting of nickel, copper, cobalt, iron. 
and manganese powders at a nominal Ni:Cu:Co:Fe:Mn molar ratio of 1:1:1:0.25:1.75. 
The powders were well-mixed and melted with an SP-25VIM vacuum induction melting 
system for 2 minutes, then cooled naturally under vacuum. The solidus (ST) and 
liquidus (LT) temperatures of the HEA are 1080 °C and 1150 °C, respectively.

For laser brazing, IN718 (0.32 mm thick) is cut into 2 mm × 15 mm pieces (Table 
1). The HEA filler metal is cold-rolled to a ~0.4 mm thick piece and placed between two 
Inconel® pieces coated with 601B flux in a lap joint configuration with a 1.5 mm overlap. 
The lap joint rests on a 1 mm thick copper platform. Lap joints were brazed by 
combining two diode lasers (wavelength = 806 nm), one 500 W laser (focal length = 20 
cm) and one 400 W laser (focal length = 10 cm). The integration of two laser heads 
allowed safe and sustainable operation of each head at 200-300 W. A small part of the 
HEA filler metal is directly irradiated for 20 seconds. BNi-2 (82.4% Ni, 7% Cr, 4.5% Si, 
3.1% B, 3% Fe) was also used as a filler metal for comparison. The brazing 
temperature was measured with an Omega SAT-30-12 S-type thermocouple.

It can be determined from the XRD pattern in Fig. 1a that the HEA is a single-phase 
FCC material. The lap joint brazing procedure is shown in Fig. 1b. The HEA material 
has a maximum shear strength using 400 W laser power (220 MPa) which reaches 15 
°C above the HEA LT. Like nearly all brazing materials, the optimal brazing temperature 
for the HEA is slightly above the LT to ensure that the brazing material remains liquid for 
significant metallurgical bonding and diffusion. If the temperature is too low, the limited 
liquidification of brazing materials may result in a shallow diffusion of certain elements 
into the base material and thereby a weak bonding [5]. The brazing temperature of 
samples brazed at 450 W and 500 W is far above the LT which detrimentally affects the 
strength. The fracture surfaces (Fig. 1d-1f) reveal a cleavage fracture surface which is 
common for high strength metals and polycrystalline materials [6]. The fracture surfaces 
of the joints reveal several microcracks which indicates good toughness [7]. EDX 
mapping (Fig. 1g-1i) shows high Cr concentration on the fracture surface which 
increases with laser power. The high Cr concentration in the 450 W fracture surface 
implies that excessive Cr concentration leads to lower strength.

As seen in Fig. 2g, the diffusion zone (DZ) thickness increases with laser power. The 
dramatic increase in DZ thickness at 450 W correlates to the 12% strength decrease. 
This correlation suggests that excessive diffusion is detrimental to the brazing 
performance confirming our previously stated hypothesis. The change in composition 
that occurs because of interdiffusion is another likely cause of the change in shear 
strength and hardness via the CE. In HEAs, the CE constitures atomic-scale 
multielement composite effects and microscale multiphase composite effects. When 
interdiffusion occurs, the alloy composition and mutual interaction between dissimilar 
elements change, thus changing the mechanical properties. For this HEA brazing 
material, Cr is introduced into the lattice and, as previously reported, small element 
concentrations can alter the mechanical properties of HEAs [8]. The XRD pattern after 
brazing (Fig. 2h) also insinuates that the addition of Cr induces additional LD. For 
HEAs, severe LD arises from several different atoms occupying the same lattice. After 
brazing, six atom species occupy the HEA lattice instead of five. Due to the fast heating 
and cooling rates of laser brazing, the severe LD and/or residual stresses increase with 
respect to the as-fabricated HEA. This would cause the diffraction angle and relative 
peak intensity to shift [9]. Limited sub-melting temperature diffusion, slip deformation, 
and other mass transport mechanisms is partially caused by LD, leading to high-
temperature strength, hardness, etc.

According to Fig. 3a-3c, the top half of the joint has greater average hardness than the 
bottom half. Each hardness map also shows a spot with higher hardness than the rest 
of the joint close to the top plate interface. The average hardness before brazing of the 
HEA and IN718 on the Vickers hardness scale is 93 HV and 250 HV, respectively (Fig. 
3d). After brazing the hardness of the HEA more than quadruples compared to the 
initial value. The hardness of IN718 also increases. There is a significant decrease in 
hardness between 350 W (410 HV) and 400 W (304 HV) of the HEA material in contrast 
to the decrease in shear strength that occurs at 450 W. The average IN718 hardness 
experiences a similar hardness decrease between 400 W (351 HV) and 450 W (288 
HV). 
The difference in hardness between the top half and bottom half of the joint (Fig. 3a-3c), 
is likely due to the variable temperature gradient (TG) and cooling rate (CR) in the 
transverse (i.e. perpendicular to the HEA-IN718 interface) and longitudinal directions 
(i.e. parallel to the HEA-IN718 interface). The CR is likely faster when the distance to 
the copper platform decreases due to heat sinking. But the TG is most likely largest in 
the transverse direction. Both the TG and the CR affect the grain nucleation and growth 
rate of the HEA material and the grain size and shape affect the hardness of the 
material. The most probable reason for the large hardness increase in the HEA is the 
changes in LD and CEs induced by Cr atom introduction into the HEA lattice [10]. Unlike 
shear strength, the decrease in hardness at higher brazing temperature is not caused 
by excessive diffusion, but by a change in the CE via the composition change [11]. 
4. Conclusions

## Page 11

The Ni-Mn-Fe-Co-Cu HEA investigated in this paper demonstrates good mechanical 
performance when used as a brazing filler metal for Inconel® 718. Using a brazing 
temperature much greater than the LT results in a loss of shear strength and hardness 
in the brazing material. This loss of strength and hardness is attributed to excessive 
interdiffusion between the filler metal and the base metal and compositional changes in 
the brazing material, respectively [11]. The large increase in hardness after brazing is 
most likely caused by a combination of severe LD and microscale/atomic-scale CEs. 
The current work may open innovative applications, such as turbine blade brazing and 
repair.
