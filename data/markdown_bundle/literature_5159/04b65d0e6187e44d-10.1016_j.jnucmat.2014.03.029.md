Fabricating high performance tungsten alloys through zirconium
micro-alloying and nano-sized yttria dispersion strengthening
R. Liu, Z.M. Xie, T. Hao, Y. Zhou, X.P. Wang, Q.F. Fang ⇑, C.S. Liu
Key Laboratory of Materials Physics, Institute of Solid State Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Hefei 230031, China

abstract
Pure W, W–0.2wt%Zr (WZ), and W–0.2wt%Zr–1.0wt%Y 2O3 (WZY) with a relative density of above 97%
were fabricated by spark-plasma-sintering method. The tensile tests indicated that the WZ and WZY
alloys exhibited a DBTT between 400 and 500 /C176C, about 200 /C176C lower than pure W. The ultimate tensile
strength of WZY alloy at 700 /C176C is 534 MPa, which is 81% and 58% higher than those of WZ alloy
(295 MPa) and pure W (337 MPa), respectively. The grain size of WZY alloy is about 3.2 lm, smaller than
that of WZ alloy and pure W. Besides, at room temperature the fracture strength and hardness of the WZY
alloy is higher than that of pure W. The improved mechanical property of the WZY alloy was suggested to
be originated from the enhanced grain boundaries cohesion by Zr micro-alloying and nano-sized yttria
dispersion strengthening.

1. Introduction
Tungsten and its alloy have attracted extensive interests and al-
ready have been used in many high-temperature applications ow-
ing to their excellent properties such as high melting temperature,
high creep resistance, and high thermal conductivity. Besides,
tungsten has been considered as one of the most promising candi-
dates for plasma facing materials in fusion reactors [1–3], owing to
the high sputtering resistance, low tritium retention, and low ther-
mal expansion coefﬁcient. However, tungsten is known to exhibit
serious embrittlement in several regimes, including room-temper-
ature brittleness, irradiation-induced brittleness and recrystalliza-
tion brittleness [3–5]. The low ductility and irradiation-induced
embrittlement are the most troublesome problems for nuclear en-
ergy application. Therefore, improving the mechanical properties
and irradiation-resistance is a crucial issue for the W based
materials.
Due to the high melting temperature, bulk W is commonly fab-
ricated by powder metallurgy method. However, polycrystalline W
has a high ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) of
about 800 /C176C. A major cause of the low toughness of W alloys is
their sensitivity to some impurities such as O, N and P, which
segregate on the grain boundaries (GBs) and decrease the GBs
cohesion [6–9]. Therefore, methods that modify impurities distri-
butions and strengthen GBs would be effective in improving the
strength and toughness of tungsten. Reducing impurities via
vacuum and zone melting may lower the DBTT of pure tungsten
to about 200 /C176C [10]. However, pure W has low high-temperature
strength and undergoes recrystallization at relatively low temper-
ature ( /C241200 /C176C) which further reduces the fracture toughness
[11]. Reﬁning the grains to obtain ultraﬁne-grained or nano-struc-
tured W through severe plastic deformation could increase GBs
area and decrease the average concentration of impurities at GBs,
and thus improves the toughness of W [12]. Moreover, the large
amount of GBs could serve as effective sinks for irradiation induced
point defects and thus may improve the irradiation-resistance [13–
15]. Nevertheless, the large volume fraction of GBs also leads to an
inherent instability especially under high temperature [16]. Adding
rhenium (Re) could reduce the DBTT to /C24600 /C176C and increase the
recrystallization temperature to 1400 /C176C [11], but would increase
the cost because of the expensive Re. Neutron irradiated W-Re al-
loys also showed drastic radiation-induced embrittlement and
hardening by precipitation [17].
Micro-alloying by adding small amount of active elements, such
as zirconium (Zr) and hafnium (Hf) that have high afﬁnity for
impurities such as O, C, and N and can bind with those impurity
elements to form compounds with high melting temperature
[18,19], may become a promising method to strengthen GBs.
First-principle calculations also indicate that the cohesion effect
of transition metals, such as Zr, Hf, and Re, on GBs in W could sig-
niﬁcantly strengthen the GBs [11]. In one of our previous publica-
tions, W–0.2wt%Zr alloy showed considerable enhancement in the
room-temperature fracture strength and toughness, because the Zr
could capture oxygen in tungsten, form nano-scaled zirconia parti-
cles, and effectively alleviate the detrimental effect of oxygen [20].

Dispersing small amounts of oxide particles, such as La 2O3,
Y2O3, and HfO 2 into W and forming the so-called oxide-disper-
sion-strengthened W (ODS-W), can reﬁne the grains and inhibit
the movement of dislocations and GBs, and thus increase the
high-temperature strength, recrystallization temperature, and
creep resistance [21–26]. Furthermore, the reﬁned microstructure
with abundant grain boundaries and oxide–W interfaces may also
beneﬁt to improving irradiation-resistance [15]. However, the in-
crease of strength in ODS-W often results in the decrease of
ductility.
Combining the advantages of micro-alloying and nano-sized
oxide dispersion strengthening would be a good approach to ob-
tain high performance W and other refractory materials. In this pa-
per, W–0.2wt%Zr–1.0wt%Y2O3 alloy was fabricated through a spark
plasma sintering method by adding tiny micro-alloying element
zirconium and dispersing nano-sized yttria (Y
2O3) particles into
W. For comparison, pure W and W–0.2wt%Zr alloy were fabricated
in a similar way. The microstructure and mechanical properties at
different temperatures were characterized and the reason for the
improvement in the properties of W–0.2wt%Zr–1.0wt%Y 2O3 alloy
was discussed.
2. Experimental details
Pure tungsten, W–0.2wt%Zr (designated as WZ) and
W–0.2wt%Zr–1.0wt%Y2O3 (designated as WZY) samples were fab-
ricated through a powder metallurgy method. Pure W (particle size
500–600 nm, purity >99.9% trace metals basis), ZrH 2 (particle size
10–20 lm, purity >99.9%), and nano-sized Y 2O3 (average particle
size 50 nm, purity >99.99%) powders were used as starting materi-
als. The initial powders of WZY samples contain 98.8 wt%W,
0.2 wt%ZrH2, and 1.0 wt%Y 2O3, while those of WZ samples contain
99.8 wt%W and 0.2 wt%ZrH 2. The powders were ball-milled in a
planetary ball mill for 4 h in argon atmosphere with a rotation
speed of 240 rpm. The ball-to-powder weight ratio was 8:1. Tung-
sten carbide balls and mortars were used to minimize the possible
impurity contamination. The chemical content of tungsten powder
and ball-milling conditions were given elsewhere [20].
The consolidation of the samples was carried out by using spark
plasma sintering equipment (FCT Group, SE-607, Germany). The
temperature and pressure proﬁle of the sintering program in this
study was described elsewhere [20]. The as-prepared powders
were loaded into a graphite mold at ﬁrst and then under a uniaxial
pressure of 47.7 MPa heated to 1800 /C176C by a pulse current (1000–
5000 A) and hold for 2 min. The heating rate at temperatures be-
low and above 1300 /C176C was 100 /C176C/min and 50 /C176C/min, respec-
tively. The cooling rate was 100 /C176C/min. All of the samples were
spark-plasma-sintered following the same sintering program. The
size of the sintered samples was 20 mm in diameter and about
2.0 mm in thickness.
The density of the sintered samples was determined by Archi-
medes’ principle. The theoretical density of the samples was calcu-
lated from the fraction and density of each component. The
densities of tungsten, zirconium, and Y 2O3 were 19.3 g/cm 3,
6.49 g/cm3 and 5.01 g/cm 3, respectively.
For tensile testing, all of the spark-plasma-sintered samples
were cut and polished into dog-bone-shaped samples with a
cross-section of 1.5 /C20.75 mm2 and a working length of 5 mm.
The tensile tests were carried out at various temperatures from
room-temperature to 700 /C176C, using an Instron-5967 machine at a
constant strain rate of 2 /C210/C04 s/C01. The elongations were deter-
mined by measuring the length of fractured samples using vernier
caliper. The hardness was measured at room-temperature using an
Aglient G200 Nanoindenter with a load of 0.43 N and a dwell time
of 10 s.
The fracture surfaces of samples were characterized by ﬁeld-
emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Sirion200, FEI),
and the average grain size of samples was measured based on
over 300 grains in different SEM micrographs. Transmission
electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2000FX) was used to study the

3. Results and discussion
As listed in Table 1, the relative density of all samples is higher
than 97%. Especially the WZY alloy exhibits the highest relative
density of 98.6%, indicating that the addition of Y 2O3 can improve
the sinterability of the sample. In Table 1 , the average grain size
and hardness are also listed and will be discussed in the following
sections.
The engineering stress–strain curves of pure W, WZ, and WZY
alloy at various temperatures are presented in Fig. 1 without cor-
rection of machine-compliance. The tensile strength deduced from
the stress–strain curves and the total elongations determined by
measuring the length of fractured samples using vernier caliper
were listed in Table 2. As shown in Fig. 1a, pure W showed typical
brittle fracture at 600 /C176C, and the fracture strength is only 211 MPa.
When increasing testing temperature to 700 /C176C, pure W showed
obvious ductility with an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of
336.6 MPa and a total elongation of 11.6%, indicating the DBTT of
the spark-plasma-sintered pure W is in the range of 600–700 /C176C.
The ductility could be drastically improved by adding 0.2 wt%
zirconium, as shown in Fig. 1 b and listed in Table 2 . The UTS and
elongation of WZ alloy at 500 /C176C is 373 MPa and 14.0%, respec-
tively. Although the WZ alloy is brittle at 400 /C176C, it still has high
fracture strength of 375 MPa. At 600 /C176C, the UTS of WZ alloy
decreased to 347 MPa while the total elongation increased a little.
At 700 /C176C however, the UTS of WZ alloy decreased to 295 MPa
although the total elongation increased to 16%. These results indi-
cate that the addition of only 0.2 wt%Zr could reduce the DBTT to
400–500 /C176C, which is about 200 /C176C lower than that of pure W, as
well as enhance the UTS at temperatures below 700 /C176C. At 700 /C176C
the UTS of WZ alloy is lower than that of pure W.
The high-temperature strength of W can be obviously im-
proved by adding small amount of oxide particles, such as
nano-sized Y2O3 particles. As shown in Fig. 1 c, the UTS of WZY al-
loy at 700 /C176C is 534 MPa, which is 81% and 58% higher than those
of WZ alloy (295 MPa) and pure W (337 MPa), respectively. The
elongation of WZY alloy at 700 /C176C is 12.4%, which is a little lower
than that of WZ alloy (16%). This indicates that the addition of
Y2O3 increases the strength, but at the same time decreases the
ductility of W–Zr alloy a little. In spite of this, both the uniform
elongation, that is, the strain achieved before the ultimate tensile
stress where the necking instability begins, and the total elonga-
tion to failure have drastically improved in comparison with the
pure W. At 500 /C176C and 600 /C176C, both WZY ( Fig. 1 c) and WZ alloy
(Fig. 1 b) are ductile. Although the elongation of the WZY alloy
is lower than that of WZ alloy, the WZY alloy has a tensile
strength of above 600 MPa, which is much higher than that of
WZ alloy (<400 MPa). Besides, the WZY alloy has a DBTT of
400–500 /C176C, which is about 200 /C176C lower than that of pure W.
These results indicate that WZY alloy not only has superior
high-temperature (700 /C176C) strength and ductility, but also has
more excellent mechanical properties at lower temperature than
pure W.

At room-temperature, the WZY alloy showed improved tensile
properties and hardness than pure W as well, as listed in Tables
1 and 2 . Due to the room-temperature embrittlement, all the ten-
sile samples fractured at a stress level much less than the yield
stress for W alloys. Nevertheless, the WZY alloy and WZ alloy have
high fracture strength of 241 MPa and 265 MPa, respectively,
which are much higher than pure W (154 MPa). As compared with
pure W, the fracture strength of WZY alloy and WZ alloy was ele-
vated by 56% and 72%, respectively. Besides, the WZY alloy also has
the highest room-temperature hardness (5.9 GPa) in comparison
with the values of pure W (5.1 GPa) and WZ alloy (5.3 GPa), as
listed in Table 1 . The hardness was not obviously increased by
adding small amount of Zr, but can be effectively improved by
dispersing small amount of Y 2O3 nano-particles.
The reason for high strength and ductility of the WZY alloy was
analyzed basing on microstructure characterization. Fig. 2 a–c
shows SEM micrographs of tensile fracture surfaces of pure W,
WZ, and WZY alloy at room temperature, which exhibits distinct
differences in grain size and fracture type. All of the samples show
very low porosity and are typical brittle fracture at room-temper-
ature. The fracture micrograph of pure W ( Fig. 2a) mainly consists
of intergranular rupture and very few transgranular rupture, sug-
gesting that the strength of GBs is lower than that of grains. The
weak GBs may become the effective sites of crack initiation and
lead to low fracture strength at room-temperature. While in WZ
(Fig. 2b) and WZY alloy ( Fig. 2c), more transgranular ruptures were
found, suggesting that the cohesion of GBs has been obviously im-
proved. These give reasonable explanation for the higher strength
and toughness of the WZ and WZY alloy than pure W.
At the same time, WZY has the smallest average grain size
(3.2 lm) in comparison with the values of pure W (4.1 lm) and
WZ alloy (4.0 lm), as listed in Table 1 . The result indicates that
small amount of nano-sized Y 2O3 particles added in W could effec-
tively inhibit W grain growth during the high temperature sinter-
ing process. As the WZY alloy showed ductility at higher
temperature above 500 /C176C( Fig. 1 c), the fracture surface of WZY
sample tensile-tested at 500 /C176C (as shown in Fig. 2 d) exhibits the
deformation of tungsten grains, which is obviously different from
those fractured at room-temperature ( Fig. 2 a–c).
Furthermore, the effects of Zr and Y 2O3 addition on the micro-
structure and mechanical properties of tungsten materials were
investigated by using TEM and EDS-mapping. For the WZ alloy,
some nano-sized zirconia particles were found, as shown in
Fig. 3. It is well known that oxygen in W grain boundaries is highly
detrimental. The zirconium dehydrogenated from ZrH 2 can bind
with oxygen in W and form ZrO 2 during the consolidation process
[20,27,28], which would undoubtedly reduce the oxygen concen-
tration in grain boundaries and thus enhance the GBs strength. In
addition, the nano-sized zirconium oxide particles may also pin
dislocations and GBs. Therefore, the improved fracture strength
and toughness at room temperature can be attributed to the cap-
ture of trace impurity oxygen in tungsten by zirconium as well
as to the pin of dislocations and grain boundaries by the dispersed
ZrO2 nano-particles.

4. Conclusions
The pure W, W–0.2wt%Zr (WZ), and W–0.2wt%Zr–1.0wt%Y 2O3
(WZY) alloys were fabricated by spark-plasma-sintering method
from W, ZrH
2, and/or Y 2O3 powders and their mechanical proper-
ties and microstructure were investigated. The results can be con-
cluded as follows:
(1) All samples have a relative density of above 97%, while the
WZY alloy exhibits the highest relative density of 98.6%.
The grain size of WZY alloy is about 3.2 lm, much smaller
than that of WZ alloy and pure W.
(2) The WZ and WZY alloys exhibited a DBTT between 400 and
500 /C176C, which is 200 /C176C lower than pure W. The UTS of WZY
alloy at 700 /C176C is 534 MPa, which is 81% and 58% higher than
those of WZ alloy and pure W, respectively.
(3) Combining with microstructure analysis, the improved
mechanical properties of the WZY alloy was suggested to
be originated from the enhanced GBs cohesion by Zr
micro-alloying and nano-sized yttria dispersion strengthen-
ing, which would provide a possible approach to other high-
performance refractory materials.
