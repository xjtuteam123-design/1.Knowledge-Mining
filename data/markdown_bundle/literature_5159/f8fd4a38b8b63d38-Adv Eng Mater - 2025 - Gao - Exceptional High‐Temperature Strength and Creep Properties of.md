1. Introduction
Ni-based wrought superalloys hold an indispensable role in the
hot sections of aero engines, distinguished by their exceptional
integration of high-temperature strength, weldability, and
deformability.[1– 3] In particular, IN718 alloy is a typical wrought
superalloy applied for turbine disks, fasten-
ers, and other components operated under
650 °C.[4] Rapid aerospace technology prog-
ress necessitates heightened demands on
materials’ temperature endurance. In short,
alloys with better weldability, such as
Haynes 282 alloy [5] and C263 alloy, [6] have
emerged as preferred materials for applica-
tions exceeding 650 °C. Nevertheless, their
high-temperature strength remains a limita-
tion, attributed to the low volume fraction
of γ 0 precipitates. Conversely, alloys with
greater strength, such as IN939 alloy, [7]
are well known to have poor fabricability,
especially in welding. In a pioneering
work, the authors meticulously screened
a novel cast nickel-based superalloy utiliz-
ing high-throughput computations coupled
with rigorous multi-performance criteria
encompassing precipitation content, solidus-
liquidus temperature range, and an innova-
tive weldability assessment metric, etc. This
alloy, labeled as SCR alloy, boasts a remark-
able temperature capability exceeding 800 °C,
complemented by a tensile strength com-
mensurate with IN939, while simultaneously demonstrating excep-
tional weldability, thereby effect ively resolving the long-standing
dilemma between weldability and mechanical performance.[8]
Under the combined effect of temperature and external load,
creep fracture becomes a critical factor that causes structural
failure for superalloys. [9] The deformation mechanism in
γ0-strengthened Ni-based superalloys undergoes intricate complex-
ities during creep, stemming from the intricate interplay between
dislocations and γ 0 precipitates amidst variations in microstruc-
ture, stress, and temperature, that is, shearing process,[10] cooper-
ative climbing, [11,12] and Orowan process. [13,14] Wang et al. [15]
investigated the creep mechanism of Waspaloy alloy at 650 °C/
780 MPa and noticed that the stack faults (SFs) only shear the
γ 0 phase while the γ matrix is unsheared, which contributes to
the creep deformation. Hunfeld et al.[16] analyzed the deformation
mechanisms at different creep stages of C263 alloy. The initial
steep decline in creep rate correlates intimately with a marked aug-
mentation in dislocation density. Throughout the creep process,
with the applied load held constant, the true stress escalates owing
to the cross-sectional diminution of the specimen via plastic defor-
mation, thereby eliciting an acceleration in the creep rate. In
the CoNi-based superalloy ERBOCo-4, the creep deformation is
mainly carried out through the substantial shearing of dislocation

Herein, the tensile and creep properties of a weldable Ni-based wrought
superalloy speci ﬁcally engineered to endure service temperatures surpassing
800 °C have been investigated. The alloy overcomes the dilemma of con ﬂicting
welding and mechanical properties, showcasing remarkable weldability alongside
sustained superior mechanical performance. At ambient temperature, the alloy
boasts a tensile strength of 1390 MPa, while maintaining a commendable
strength of 850 MPa at 800 °C. The creep response under 800 °C/200 MPa
conditions unfolds in three stages, marked by an extended period of steady-state
stage spanning over 210 h. Notably, both the minimum steady-state creep rate
and creep rupture strength surpass those of other conventional wrought,
weldable superalloys, such as Haynes 282. The superior creep resistance is likely
due to the synergistic effect of the Orowan looping and the shearing of stacking
faults. The electron backscattered diffraction analysis shows that during the creep
process of the alloy, the proportion of high Schmid factors increases, allowing
more grains to meet the conditions for dislocation slip and leading to the
acceleration of plastic deformation. After creep, the proportion of twin boundaries
decreases, and the obstacle effect of the alloy on dislocations is signi ﬁcantly
weakened.

3.2. Mechanical Properties
The tensile properties and comparison with other typical weld-
able Ni-wrought superalloys, such as Haynes 282 [5] and C263,[16]
are presented in Figure 2. Intuitively, both the yield strength (YS)
and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of the alloy were higher than
those of the mentioned superalloys. The alloy exhibits room tem-
perature tensile strength and yield strength as high as 1390 and
924 MPa, respectively. The tensile strength at 800 °C showed no
signiﬁcant softening tendency, with the UTS still reaching
850 MPa—nearly twice that of C263 alloy. While most alloys
often sacri ﬁce strength when pursuing excellent weld repair
performance, this alloy successfully balances both properties.
In previous work, we evaluated the weldability of the alloy
through rigorous constrained welding tests, which showed that
the alloy possessed excellent resistance to solidi ﬁcation cracking
tendencies.[8]
The creep strain-time curves for the interest alloy subjected to
temperatures ranging from 800 to 870 °C and constant applied
stresses spanning 200 – 280 MPa are depicted in Figure 3 a.
Each creep test was terminated upon sample fracture, with sam-
ples tested at 800 °C/200 MPa, 800 °C/280 MPa, and 870 °C/
200 MPa experiencing failure after 320 h, 54 h, and 23 h, respec-
tively. It is seen that at a certain temperature, elevated applied
stress correlates directly with an earlier onset of plastic instability
and ultimate rupture. Notably, the creep strain rate serves
as a pivotal metric for assessing superalloy creep behavior.
Figure 3b illustrates the creep strain rate-time curves across
diverse creep conditions. The typical creep process comprises
three distinct stages: decelerated creep stage, steady-state creep
stage, and accelerated creep stage. The steady-state creep stage,
characterized by an approximately constant creep deformation
rate and a linear creep curve, is of paramount importance.
However, specimens often spend a considerable fraction of
their service life in the tertiary stage. For the creep behavior
of 800 °C/200 MPa, the creep rate decreases sharply within
/C25 1.5 h. Subsequently, the alloy transitions into a steady-state
creep stage, attaining a minimum steady-state creep rate of
6.8 /C2 10/C0 9 s/C0 1. Once the steady-state creep stage is completed,
the alloy enters an accelerated creep stage characterized by a
rapid increase in strain rate, ultimately leading to fracture.
Notably, the alloy has a prolonged steady creep stage spanning
210 h, indicative of remarkable creep resistance.

3.3. Fracture Morphology
To further clarify the in ﬂuence of creep condition on creep prop-
erties, the characteristics of creep fracture are investigated by
SEM, as shown in Figure 5. The fracture surfaces of all samples
exhibited a quasi-circular morphology, oriented perpendicular
to the applied stress direction, with no signi ﬁcant necking
observed (Figure 5a – c). Speci ﬁcally, at 800 °C/200 MPa and
870 °C/200 MPa, the creep fracture exhibits several obvious
cleavage facets and shallow dimples, suggesting that the failure
mode is a mix of brittle and ductile fracture, [27] whereas dimples
are signi ﬁcantly reduced at 800 °C/280 MPa. Meanwhile, more
intergranular cracks appear shown in Figure 5e,f. Elevated tem-
perature and stress compromised the alloy ’s intergranular bond-
ing, ultimately leading to grain decohesion and the formation of
voids on the fracture surface. The fundamental mechanism
underpinning the creep fracture in these samples revolves
around the initiation, expansion, and coalescence of creep voids,
which function as preferential pathways for crack propagation,
ultimately culminating in failure. [28] Hence, the formation of
creep voids and the subsequent cracking during creep testing
can be attributed to the failure of the samples.

4. Discussion
4.1. Creep Deformation Mechanisms
A profound comprehension of the underlying mechanisms that
govern material creep failure forms the cornerstone for assessing
creep performance. Subsequently, we will delve into analyzing the
microstructural evolutions exhibited by the alloy throughout distinct
creep stages, aiming to elucidate the operative creep mechanisms.
The subsequent sections provide a detailed characterization of
the alloy ’s behavior across these distinct creep stages. Figure 7
shows the dislocation structures of specimens at different creep
stages. As seen in Figure 7a,b, the deformation in the decelerat-
ing stage manifests as multiple slip bands aligned along the
<001> and <1-11> slip directions while the dislocations are
barely discernible, indicating that the deformation of the lightly
strained specimen is heterogeneous. [29] Figure 7c reveals the
presence of prominent dislocation bowing at Stage 2. The a/2
<110> matrix dislocations traverse the γ matrix, encountering
γ 0 precipitates, where they are unable to penetrate but instead
loop around them, conforming to the Orowan bypass mecha-
nism.[30] The γ 0 precipitates effectively function as barriers to
hinder dislocation mobility. The application of Orowan stress
is necessary to de ﬂect and propagate dislocations around the
γ 0 phase. As evident from Figure 6, an increase in the average
spacing of γ 0 particles, stemming from the coarsening of the
γ 0 phase, presents a viable strategy for enhancing the creep life
of the material in the steady-state creep stage. Hence, the domi-
nant deformation mechanisms, as well as the superb creep resis-
tance in the steady stage, could be ascribed to the Orowan
looping.
In Figure 7d, isolated SFs within γ 0 precipitates are present at
Stage 3. Upon encountering a γ 0 particle, a pair of a/2 <101>
matrix dislocations dissociates, with one partial dislocation enter-
ing the γ 0 precipitate to create an SISF and the other, an a/6
<121> partial dislocation, residing at the γ/γ 0 interface.[29]
The exclusive formation of isolated SFs within γ 0 precipitates pre-
cludes their interaction with mobile dislocations in the γ matrix.
Furthermore, localized shearing within γ 0 precipitates facilitates
strain relief in the matrix, enhancing dislocation mobility
therein. Consequently, these isolated SFs facilitate dislocation
slip in the matrix, thereby compromising creep resistance. As
creep strain intensi ﬁes, increased shearing of γ 0 precipitates
leads to a marked acceleration in the creep rate. [31] In addition
to isolated SFs, the continuous SFs that cut through the γ matrix
and γ 0 but do not span across the entire grain are observed. These
SFs have a few micrometers in width, as shown in Figure 7e.
Additionally, lots of isolated SFs extend in γ 0 precipitates parallel
to the continuous SFs. This mechanism aligns closely with the
model proposed by Viswanathan for the formation of isolated
SFs. The distinction between these mechanisms lies in the
behavior of the a/2 <110> matrix dislocation: at the γ/γ 0 inter-
face, it dissociates to form isolated SFs, whereas within the
matrix, it dissociates to yield extended SFs. Notably, as creep
strain accumulates, these extended SFs undergo diffusion-
mediated reordering, transforming into micro twins. [31,32] As
shown in Figure 7f, deformation-induced micro twins were
observed. These micro twins introduce twin boundaries that
interact with dislocations, effectively shortening the mean free
path of mobile dislocations. [33] This interaction enhances
cross-slip resistance,[34] while the thermally activated reordering
process accompanying micro twin formation concurrently con-
tributes to a reduction in the creep strain rate. [35]
As shown in Figure 8 , dislocations exhibit a curved con ﬁgu-
ration around γ 0 phases, forming distinct dislocation loops.
This observation con ﬁrms that the primary deformation mech-
anism under 800 °C/280 MPa and 870 °C/200 MPa is Orowan
looping.

4.2. Crystallographic Structure
Figure 9 illustrates the coincidence site lattice (CSL) boundary
maps of the specimens before and after creep. Generally, low-
Σ CSL boundaries (with Σ ≤ 27) can reduce lattice mismatch
to a certain extent, while Σ3 boundaries (twin boundaries) act
as barriers to impede the propagation of intragranular cracks,
thereby enhancing the high-temperature fracture toughness of
the alloy. Figure 10 shows the proportion of CSL boundaries
in the alloy before and after creep. Compared with the precreep
state, the proportion of Σ3 twin boundaries decreases after creep,
though the reduction is not signiﬁcant. This phenomenon can be
attributed to two primary factors. During high-temperature
creep, grain reorientation drives the migration of high- Σ bound-
aries, which absorb low- Σ boundaries to minimize system
energy. Concurrently, extended stress application induces the
migration of certain twin boundaries.
Figure 11 presents Schmid factor maps of postcreep specimens.
As a critical parameter characterizing the geometric relationship
between applied stress and slip systems, the Schmid factor spans
0 to 0.5, with values directly proportional to slip system activation
ease — higher factors denote enhanced plastic deformation suscep-
tibility under given stress states. Figure 11d reveals a progressive
increase in the 0.45– 0.5 Schmid factor regime. Relative to 800 °C/

5. Conclusion
The main conclusions are summarized as follows: 1) The novel
weldable wrought superalloy exhibits excellent tensile properties
with yield strength and ultimate tensile strength up to 924 MPa
and 1390 MPa, respectively at RT, and the ultimate tensile
strength of 850 MPa at 800 °C, which are superior to those of
most reported weldable Ni-based wrought superalloy, such as
C263, C264, and Haynes 282. 2) The new alloy offers excellent
creep properties at 800 °C/200 MPa, marked by a lower steady
creep rate of 6.8 /C2 10/C0 9 s/C0 1 and an extended period of steady-
state creep spanning over 210 h, which almost occupied 66%
of the whole life. The superior creep resistance in the steady
creep stage is ascribed to the Orowan looping. Extensive isolated
SFs promote the deformation and decrease creep resistance at
the accelerated creep stage. The creep rupture strength is superior
to C263, Haynes 230, and Haynes 282 alloys, as evaluated by the
Larson– Miller parameter. 3) The steady-state creep rate of the
novel weldable Ni-based wrought superalloy increases signiﬁcantly
with rising creep temperature or applied stress. This variation is
essentially driven by the synergistic effect of crystallographic
orientation evolution and microstructural response, as conﬁrmed
by EBSD characterization and microstructural analysis. Under
elevated temperature or stress, the proportion of high Schmid
factor (0.45– 0.5) grains expands, enabling more grains to activate
the dominant {111} <110> slip system and accelerating
a/2<110> matrix dislocation slip to promote plastic deformation.
Meanwhile, postcreep Σ3 twin boundary proportion decreases —
weakening dislocation obstruction and stress concentration relief
effects—and higher temperatures enhance dislocation thermal
activation energy, activating multidirectional slip to accelerate
creep strain accumulation.
