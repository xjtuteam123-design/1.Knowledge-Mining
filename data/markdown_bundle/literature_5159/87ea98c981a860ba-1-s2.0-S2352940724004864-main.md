ABSTRACT
This study investigates the mechanical properties of an Al-Ni-Cu-Fe alloy fabricated using laser powder bed 
fusion (LPBF) technology, evaluated at temperatures ranging from room temperature to 300
◦
C under various 
strain rates for potential aerospace and automotive applications. Aluminum alloys often face the challenges of 
maintaining high-temperature strength and stability, limiting their use in demanding environments. The results 
demonstrate that the LPBF Al-Ni-Cu-Fe alloy maintains strength above 300 MPa at standard strain rates and 
reaches up to 400 MPa at high strain rates at 300
◦
C, indicating its suitability for lightweight, high-strength 
vehicles and thermal engineering systems. The high-temperature strengthening mechanisms are attributed to 
Al
9
FeNi submicron cellular eutectic walls that impede dislocation motion and precipitation strengthening from 
Al
2
Cu. Fracture analysis reveals that localized brittleness limits elongation to 3% at room temperature, with 
deformation distributed between coarse-grained (CG) and fine-grained (FG) regions. At elevated temperatures 
(300
◦
C), partial homogenization of FG regions reduces melt pool constraints, promoting local deformation. 
Therefore, ductility increases significantly under high strain rates as the dispersed Al
9
FeNi phases become less 
effective in resisting instantaneous stresses, allowing deformation to overcome melt pool boundaries. At extreme 
strain rates during impact testing, twinning becomes the dominant deformation mechanism, contrasting with 
tensile testing. These findings confirm the potential of the LPBF Al-Ni-Cu-Fe alloy as a heat-resistant printed 
aluminum alloy for high-temperature applications and provide valuable insights into fracture mechanisms within 
the bimodal microstructure, guiding future alloy optimization.
1. Introduction
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) is an additive manufacturing (AM) 
technique used to construct complex three-dimensional structures by 
selectively melting pre-laid metal powder with a high-powered laser 
[ 1 – 2 ]. This technology offers advantages, including customization and 
short production cycles. Aluminum alloys have been extensively intro -
duced into the automotive and aerospace engineering fields for their 
cost-effectiveness, exceptional corrosion resistance, recyclability, and 
lightweight properties, making them indispensable in modern engi -
neering solutions [ 3 ]. By leveraging the LPBF process, it is possible to 
fabricate aluminum alloys with a dense, bimodal printed texture that 
exhibits high strength and ductility [ 4 – 5 ]. These materials generally 
surpass the mechanical properties of conventional cast alloys, with their 
ultimate tensile strengths exceeding 500 MPa. Compared to Fe, Ti, and 
other printable alloys, aluminum powders exhibit poor flowability, high 
oxidation tendencies, low laser absorption, and high thermal conduc -
tivity. These challenges affect the final part ’ s density and strength, 
thereby limiting the range of aluminum alloy systems suitable for ad -
ditive manufacturing [ 6 – 8 ]. Furthermore, the literature has indicated 
that LPBF-printed Al alloys exhibit high-temperature instability [ 9 ]. This 
instability is primarily due to the mechanical properties derived from 
the printed texture and the cellular eutectic structure, which tend to 
coarsen or decompose above 200
◦
C, causing a rapid decrease in strength 
[ 10 ]. Therefore, for mid-temperature applications (200-400
◦
C) such as 
engine impellers and pistons, heavier iron and nickel alloys are pri -
marily used, which is not conducive to sustainable development goals 
aimed at energy conservation and carbon reduction [ 11 ]. Therefore, 
developing novel LPBF aluminum alloys for future high-temperature 
applications is essential.

during the solidification process, the LPBF process in aluminum alloys 
predominantly focuses on systems such as Al-10Si-Mg [ 12 – 13 ], Al-Zn 
[ 14 – 15 ], Al-Cu [ 16 ], and Al-Mg-Sc-Zr [ 17 – 18 ]. Through the rapid 
cooling rates afforded by LPBF, these systems enable the alloying ele -
ments to form nanoscale strengthening phases. Notably, the printed 
Al-Zn system exhibits exceptional tensile strength at room temperature. 
However, in alloys such as 7075, the primary strengthening phase, 
MgZn
2

herent η phase under high temperatures, making it challenging to 
maintain high strengths [ 15 , 19 ]. Furthermore, although the Al-Cu sys -
tem meets the demands of high-temperature applications, it is hampered 
by its relatively poor corrosion resistance [ 20 ]. Additionally, systems 
such as AlSi10Mg and Al-Mg-Sc-Zr witness a marked decline in strength 
when subjected to temperatures exceeding 200
◦
C due to the deteriora -
tion of their printed textures. Therefore, it is essential to develop 
aluminum alloys for printing processes designed for high-temperature 
engineering applications. While the newly developed Al-Ce system for 
printed high-temperature applications shows significant tensile strength 
at elevated temperatures, the cost-effectiveness of incorporating Ce is 
relatively low [ 21 ]. Literature indicates that the Al-Al

alloy system [ 23 ], building upon the Al-Ni system as a foundation with 
the addition of Cu and Fe. The literature has revealed that a moderate 
addition of Cu not only enhances the tensile strength and ductility of the 
Al-Ni systems printed via LPBF but also promotes the formation of su -

Given the low solubility of Ni and Fe in Al, casting tends to form 
coarse segregation phases that significantly increase brittleness [ 34 ]. 
While LPBF technology effectively minimizes the size of phases like 
Al
9
FeNi to sub-micron, which enhances mechanical properties, the 
fracture mechanisms in printed Al-Ni and Al-Fe-Ni systems remain 
predominantly brittle. Literature indicates that in LPBF Al-Ni alloy 
systems, rapid cooling accelerates the solidification and growth of 
aluminum grains, which drives a divorced eutectic reaction. This reac -
tion traps additive elements like nickel at the grain boundaries, creating 
a continuous network of cellular eutectic structures [ 35 – 36 ]. Unlike 
conventional aluminum alloys, the cellular eutectic network structures 
produced by LPBF typically have merely sub-micron dimensions. These 
cellular structures are considered highly effective strengthening mech -
anisms in printed aluminum alloys [ 37 – 38 ]. Addressing the aerospace 
and automotive demands for alloys that sustain high strength and 
toughness at elevated temperatures, this study employs LPBF technology 
to develop the Al-4Ni-2Cu-1.4Fe hypoeutectic system. Regarding the 
promising mechanical strength under room temperature demonstrated 
by the higher Fe content (1.4 wt.%) in the LPBF Al-Ni-Cu-Fe system [ 39 ] 
and the potential thermal deformation during T6 treatments of practical 
intricate and thin components, this research focuses on the 
high-temperature mechanical properties of as-printed 1.4Fe. To eluci -
date the influence of strain rates and temperature at elevated tempera -
tures, this study examines the tensile properties of the Al-Ni-Cu-Fe alloy. 
These are also compared with the impact tests from ambient to failure 
temperatures. Further, it delves into the high-temperature strengthening 
mechanisms through comparative analyses of age-hardening treat -
ments. Moreover, addressing the scant research on how variations in 
bimodal microstructure influence different failure mechanisms, this 
study delves into the sub-surface textures at its operational limit of 
300
◦
C. The scope of this study focuses on investigating the 
high-temperature mechanical properties and failure mechanisms of the 
LPBF Al-Ni-Cu-Fe alloy, aiming to provide a foundational understanding 
for designing heat-resistant aluminum alloys by elucidating the influ -
ence of bimodal microstructure and alloying elements on fracture 
mechanisms under extreme applications relevant to aerospace and 
automotive industry. This enhances the alloy ’ s applicability in 
mid-temperature settings and opens new avenues for developing robust, 
heat-resistant aluminum alloys.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Material preparation
The Al-Ni-Cu-Fe alloy used in this study was fabricated from pre- 
alloyed powder provided by Circle Metal Powder Co., Ltd., using elec -
trode induction melting and inert gas atomization. Technical support for 
the laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) process was provided by ANJI 
Technology Co., Ltd. The laser printing parameters are outlined in 
Table 1 , and the alloy ’ s composition is detailed in Table 2 . Fig. 1 (a) 
shows the schematic of the printing process, which features a hatch 
space of 100 μ m and a powder layer thickness of 30 μ m. The pre-alloyed 
powder was examined using a field emission scanning electron micro -
scope (SEM, HITACHI SU-5000, Japan), revealing a predominantly 
spherical appearance with no significant second phases on the surface 
and an average diameter of about 20 μ m. To address directional strength 
issues, samples were printed using a 67.3
◦
rotation angle strategy, as 
illustrated in Fig. 1 (b), to alleviate thermal accumulation and prevent 
keyhole mode deterioration [ 40 ]. Samples were printed on an AA6061 
substrate, with post-print removal through electrical discharge 
machining. Building and side directions are shown in Fig. 1 (c). Fig. 1 (d) 
shows the appearance of as-printed specimens, which exhibit no 
apparent defects and achieve 99% theoretical density as verified by the 
Archimedes method using water. Rockwell hardness testing (Grade F, 
Mitutoyo, Kawasaki-shi, Japan) confirmed that the as-printed specimens 
achieved a hardness of HRF 95-97, indicating superior strength.

3.1. As-printed specimen microstructural characterization
Fig. 2 presents the microstructural morphologies of the as-printed

the coarse-grained (CG) region. In typical printed aluminum alloy sys -

Al-Ni systems, FG regions are less prevalent in a smaller portion, mostly 
appearing at melt pool termini. Research indicates that the strength of 
printed Al-Ni systems derives not only from the fine-grain strengthening 
effect of FG regions but also from the finely distributed cellular eutectic 
boundaries across both CG and FG regions. Additionally, the dark spots 
along the melt pool boundaries in Fig. 2 (b) are attributed to the 
continuous distribution of the Al
9

phase composition, fracture textures, and grain boundary characteris -

Under standard strain rate at room temperature, EBSD analysis reveals 
that the material predominantly undergoes uniform tensile deformation 
throughout CG and FG regions in a synergistic effect, which coopera -
tively bears the applied stress. This cooperative stress distribution across 
the melt pool mitigates localized stress concentrations, preventing strain 
accumulation in vulnerable regions and thereby enhancing overall 
strength and toughness [ 66 ]. The FG and CG regions contribute 
differently under these conditions, where CG provides stability while FG 
offers additional grain boundary strengthening. TEM observations 
further confirm that after aging to 300
◦

homogenization of the FG cellular structure reduce the constraint it 
provides to the melt pool [ 67 ]. This phenomenon explains the different 
tensile behaviors observed in Fig. 7 (a) and 7 (b). At low strain rates

at high temperatures, limiting slip systems and dislocation migration, 
resulting in limited ductility improvement. However, at high strain rates 
( Fig. 7 b), the applied instantaneous outer stress overcomes the con -
straints of the melt pool, especially at high temperatures, where defor -
mation mechanisms are fully activated and partial DRX is also involved 
[ 68 ]. This is evidenced in the KAM and PF analyses, which reveal sig -
nificant dislocation accumulation and grain reorientation, promoting 
overall deformation in the FG region and leading to a substantial in -
crease in elongation. Additionally, during high-temperature deforma -

dislocation propagation, resulting in a slight resilience in strength. It is 
indicated that the observed differences in tensile behavior at low and 
high strain rates can be attributed to the differential activation of 
deformation mechanisms within the melt pool and grain regions.
