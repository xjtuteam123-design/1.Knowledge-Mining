Effect of Carbon Addition on the Strength and Creep
Resistance of FeAl Alloys
R.S. SUNDAR and S.C. DEEVI
We investigated the effect of carbon content (0.05, 0.12, and 0.2 wt pct C) and heat-treatment
temperature (1100 °C and 1300 °C for 2 hours and air cooled) on the tensile and the creep proper-
ties of Fe-24 wt pct Al alloy. The increase of carbon content increased the yield strength without
affecting the tensile ductility of the alloys. Carbon content appears to be beneficial in suppressing
the hydrogen embrittlement at the grain boundary, because the fracture mode changes from predom-
inantly intergranular failure in a low carbon (0.05 wt pct C) alloy to a predominantly transgranular
cleavage failure in a high carbon (0.2 wt pct C) alloy. With the increase of carbon content, the
anomalous yield strength peak shifted to a higher temperature possibly due to the interaction between
carbon and vacancies. Significant improvements were noted in the tensile and the creep properties of
medium (0.12 wt pct C) and high carbon (0.2 wt pct C) alloys after heat treating at 1300 °C. The
improvements in the tensile and the creep properties were attributed to the synergetic effect of retained
vacancies and fine carbide precipitates present in the alloys after 1300 °C heat treatment. However,
the improved strength and creep properties associated with 1300 °C heat treatment were lost when
the heat-treated alloys were further subjected to a vacancy removal annealing. Our results suggest
that the retained vacancies present in the FeAl alloys after high-temperature heat treatment and air
cooling are effective in improving the creep resistance at 700 °C, and yield strength up to 800 °C.
The creep resistance of the present high carbon FeAl alloy is comparable to or better than several
grades commercial heat-resistant Fe-based and Ni-based alloys.
I. INTRODUCTION
INTERMETALLIC alloys based on Fe 3Al and FeAl
are being considered as high-temperature structural materials
due to low raw material cost, low density, high electrical
resistivity, and excellent corrosion resistance in a variety of
aggressive high-temperature gaseous environments.
[1–4] In
particular, FeAl alloys are highly resistant against oxidation,
carburization, and sufidation even at high temperatures
without the necessity of having a high chromium content
as in the case of stainless steels and nickel-based superal-
loys. They can be used as heat-treatment fixtures, furnace
components, resistive heating elements, porous gas-metal
filters, automotive exhaust components, catalytic converters,
and piston valves.
[3,4] It is envisaged that some of the ferritic
steels, austenitic steels, and iron-base and nickel-based super-
alloys will eventually be replaced by FeAl-based alloys
due to their corrosion resistance. The commercial utilization
of FeAl alloys are limited by the low ambient temperature
ductility, rapid drop in strength above 500 °C, and poor
high-temperature creep resistance.
Poor room-temperature ductility of FeAl is understood
to be due to a combination of factors such as moisture-
induced environmental embrittlement, inherent weak grain
boundaries, and embrittlement due to vacancy hardening.
[1,2]
The reaction between aluminum atoms and moisture present
in the air generates atomic hydrogen causing embrittlement
at the crack tip. Moisture-induced embrittlement is shown
to be a function of strain rate, temperature, and test
environment. In addition, the inherent grain boundary weak-
ness leads to poor tensile ductility in FeAl alloys. Addition
of B is shown to increase the cohesive strength of grain
boundaries in FeAl alloys with less than 40 at pct Al due
to segregation of B on the grain boundaries.
[5,6] In alloys
with high Al content, B addition has not been effective,
because these alloys have grain boundaries that are inherently
very weak. High vacancy concentration is retained in FeAl
when cooled even at moderately slow cooling rates from
high temperatures.
[7,8,9] The retained vacancy concentration
increases with the increase of Al content and with the
increase of cooling rate from elevated temperatures. These
thermal vacancies cause considerable strengthening and
decrease ductility, and the ductility can only be improved
by prolonged annealing at low temperatures. Recent inves-
tigations indicate a considerable improvement in ductility
through a variety of thermomechanical treatments or by
alloying with boron.
[1,2,10,11]
The FeAl exhibits a positive temperature dependence of
flow stress at intermediate temperatures and flow stress peaks
between 300 °C and 600 °C.
[1] The alloy composition and strain
rate determine the temperature at which yield stress maxima
are observed. There is a considerable debate in the literature
over the mechanism responsible for the anomalous flow

on the strength and creep resistance of Fe-40 at. pct Al
alloys. In addition to C, the alloying elements B, Zr, Ti,
and Mo are also added and the role of alloying elements
is summarized here briefly. Boron is added to increase
the grain boundary cohesion and to form borides. Carbon
is added to improve the strength by forming carbides and
to improve weldability. Zirconium is added to refine the
grain size and to impart adhesion of the protective oxide
scale to the surface. Elements such as molybdenum,
titanium, and chromium are selected to improve the high-
temperature strength and creep resistance through formation
of carbides or borides. We discuss the microstructures and
tensile and creep properties of FeAl alloys as a function
of carbon content and heat treatments. The tensile and creep
results are compared with those of commercial stainless
steels and nickel- and iron-based superalloys to show that
FeAl alloys could indeed replace several of the existing
commercial alloys.

III. RESULTS
A. Microstructure
Alloy compositions, heat-treatment conditions, and grain
sizes of FeAl alloys are shown in Table I. Irrespective of
the heat-treatment condition, all three alloys exhibited
equiaxed grains (Figure 1). Large grain sizes in the range
of 142 to 174 mm were observed when the alloys were heat
treated at 1300 °C, and the grain sizes were in the range of
90 to 104 mm at 1100 °C heat treatment. Second-phase
precipitates rich in Zr (presumably zirconium boride) and
Ti (complex titanium carbide) were aligned along the
extrusion direction (Figure 2). These streaks of precipitate
structure persisted even after heat treating at 1100 °C.
However, the extent of streaks reduced after 1300 °C heat
treatment.
B. Tensile Properties
The stress-strain curves of all three alloys heat treated
at 1100 °C for 2 hours are shown in Figure 3. The alloys
exhibit smooth yielding followed by strain-hardening
behavior up to 600 °C. At higher temperatures, alloys 1
and 2 exhibit pronounced flow softening (Figures 3(a) and
(b). It is interesting to note that alloy 3 exhibits a sharp
yield point followed by flow softening at temperatures
greater than 500 °C (Figure 3(c)). The room-temperature
yield strength and percent elongation as a function of
carbon content are shown in Figure 4. The room-
temperature yield strength of the alloys increases with
increasing carbon content, while the ductility is not affected
by carbon addition. The variation of 0.2 pct yield strength,
UTS, and percent elongation with temperature are
presented in Figure 5. The yield strength of alloy 1 exhibits
a peak near 400 °C (Figure 5(a)). On the other hand, the
yield strength peak occurs near 600 °C in alloy 3 (Figure
5(c)). A broad plateau in yield stress is observed between
500 °C and 600 °C in the case of alloy 2 (Figure 5b).
The ductility of the alloys increases with increasing
temperature up to 500 °C. The ductility of the three alloys
dropped at 600 °C and a rapid increase in ductility is
observed at higher temperatures. Fracture surfaces of the
alloys tested at room temperature are shown in Figure 6.
Alloy 1 exhibits mostly intergranular fracture (IG) with
very little transgranular cleavage (TG) (Figure 6(a)).
Fracture mode of alloy 2 is similar to alloy 1, except that
the amount of transgranular cleavage is higher than that
in alloy 1 (Figure 6(b)). On the other hand, alloy 3 exhibits
predominantly transgranular cleavage (Figure 6(c)).
Evidence of secondary cracking is also evident in the
fracture surface of alloy 3 (Figure 6(d)).
Tensile properties of the alloys after 1300 °C heat
treatment are summarized in Table II. The effects of
heat-treatment temperature on the yield strength of alloys at
room temperature and at 800 °C are compared in Figure 7.
At room temperature, the yield strength of alloys 1 and 2
increased with an increase in heat-treatment temperature,
while the heat-treatment temperature had very little influence
on the yield strength of alloy 3. High-temperature yield
strength (at 800 °C) of all three alloys increased with heat-
treatment temperature and the increase in yield strength is
significant in alloy 2 with a carbon content of 0.12 wt pct
and alloy 3 with a carbon content of 0.2 wt pct. All the
alloys exhibited a drop in room-temperature ductility when
heat treated at 1300 °C as opposed to 1100 °C. Tensile
properties of alloy 1 after prolonged vacancy annealing treat-
ment at 400 °C are also presented in Table II. The vacancy
annealing treatment enhanced the room-temperature duc-
tility and decreased the yield strength of alloy 1. Fracture
surfaces of alloys heat treated at 1300 °C and tested at room
temperature are shown in Figure 8. Alloy 1 failed pre-
dominantly by intergranular failure, whereas alloys 2 and
3 failed by TG.
C. Creep Properties
Creep studies were carried out on alloy 2 after 1100 °C
and 1300 °C heat treatments. Typical creep curves of alloy
2 tested at 700 °C and 90 MPa are shown in Figure 9. Creep
results of all the alloys tested at 700 °C and 90 MPa are

B. Tensile Properties
1. Room-temperature tensile behavior
Room-temperature yield and fracture behavior of FeAl
is sensitive to Al content, test environment, prior heat
treatment, and presence of minor alloying additions.
[1,2]
Addition of B is shown to be beneficial in strengthening
the gain boundaries of Fe-(35–40) at. pct Al and in increas-
ing the resistance to intergranular fracture. However, the
ductility increase due to B addition is insignificant, since the
B-doped FeAl alloy failed by transgranular cleavage due to
moisture-induced embrittlement. In comparison, B in Ni
3Al
alloys suppresses the environmental embrittlement by seg-
regating on grain boundaries.
[33] In FeAl, the environmen-
tal embrittlement occurs mainly at the cleavage planes, and
therefore, the addition of B is not as effective in mini-
mizing the embrittlement. On the other hand, environmental
embrittlement can be minimized with C addition because
carbides in steel are known to act as hydrogen traps and
to delay embrittlement. Pang and Kumar
[29–31] investigated
the tensile behavior of ternary Fe-40Al-0.6C alloy as a
function of temperature, strain rate, and heat treatments.
When compared to binary FeAl, carbon containing
ternary alloy exhibited better ductility at a given yield stress.
In this section, we discuss and compare tensile results of
our alloys with those of binary Fe-40Al and ternary
Fe-40Al-0.6 C alloys.
At room temperature, all three alloys exhibited smooth
yielding followed by work hardening prior to the final frac-
ture. The absence of a yield point in the present alloys is
understandable due to their coarse grain sizes. A sharp yield
point is observed only in fine-grained FeAl alloys processed
by either power metallurgy or by rapid solidification tech-
niques.
[34] The yield strength of the alloys increased with an
increase of carbon content with no influence on the ductility
of the alloys. Our results agree with the tensile data reported
by Pang and Kumar.
[29,30] Pang and Kumar attributed the
higher strength observed in the as-extruded Fe-40Al-0.6C
alloy (homogenized and extruded at 1000 °C) to the presence
of precipitates and to the vacancy concentration. They argued
that the presence of carbon-vacancy complexes in FeAl (as
in bcc iron) lowers the vacancy migration kinetics and helps

3. Effect of heat treatments
When compared to 1100 °C heat treatment, heat treatment
at 1300 °C resulted in an increase in yield strength both at
room temperature and at 800 °C. An exception to this is
alloy 3, wherein the 1300 °C heat treatment did not change
the yield strength at room temperature. The improvement in
yield strength at room temperature and at 800 °C in the pre-
sent alloys after 1300 °C heat treatment could arise due to
two reasons. Heat treating at 1300 °C results in dissolution
of some of the coarse precipitates as is evident in the
microstructure after 1300 °C heat treatment. The number
of precipitate streaks along the extrusion direction is reduced.
The presence of alloying elements in solution or reprecipi-
tation as fine precipitates during cooling might contribute
to strength through the solid solution or precipitation hard-
ening mechanism. Transmission electron microscope exam-
ination of heat-treated samples is currently under way to
gain better understanding of the microstructure and strength-
ening mechanisms. Strengthening could also be due to the
higher concentration of retained vacancies caused by high-
temperature heat treatment followed by air cooling.
Consistent with the vacancy hardening mechanism, the room-
temperature ductility of the alloys decreased after 1300 °C
heat treatment. Removing the vacancies by prolonged
annealing at 400 °C decreased the strength and improved
the ductility of the alloys. As discussed in the next section,
strengthening due to vacancies is effective even at 800 °C.
The room-temperature fracture mode of alloys 1 and 3 after
1300 °C heat treatment is similar to that observed after
1100 °C heat treatment. On the other hand, the fracture mode
of alloy 2 changed from predominantly intergranular after
1100 °C heat treatment to predominantly transgranular cleav-
age after 1300 °C heat treatment. At present, we do not
understand the reasons for the change in fracture mode in
alloy 2 and are currently investigating those reasons.
C. Creep Properties
The creep results indicate that the creep behavior of FeAl
alloys is dependent on the carbon content and heat-treatment
temperature. Irrespective of the heat-treatment condition,
minimum creep rate decreased and rupture life increased
with an increase of carbon content. Similarly, the creep
resistance of alloys 2 and 3 improved significantly when
the alloys were heat treated at 1300 °C instead of 1100 °C.
However, only marginal improvement in creep strength is
observed when alloy 1 with 0.05 wt pct C is heat treated
at 1300 °C instead of 1100 °C. The creep resistance of
alloy 2 decreased when alloy 2 was further subjected to
an additional heat treatment at 400 °C to remove the
quenched-in vacancies. The above results indicate that
the improvement in creep resistance after 1300 °C heat-
treated samples is due to the combined effect of carbon
and quenched-in vacancies. Maziasz and co-workers
[27,39]
reported a similar improvement in creep resistance after
heat treating a Fe
3Al and a complex Fe-36 Al-0.4 pct C
alloy. The rupture life of Fe3Al (Fe-28Al-5Cr-0.5Nb-0.8Mo-
0.025Zr) also improved after heat treating the alloy at
1150 °C followed by air cooling.
[27] Oil-quenching treat-
ment instead of air cooling further enhanced the creep prop-
erties.
[27] The better creep properties of oil-quenched alloy
are attributed to the dislocation loops, which interact and
intersect the network dislocations giving rise to creep
deformation strength. The enhanced creep resistance of the
oil-quenched sample was lost when the alloy was heat
treated at 750 °C to remove quenched-in vacancies.
[27] In
the case of a complex Fe-36Al-0.4 pct C alloy, a signifi-
cant improvement in the creep resistance was observed
when the alloy was heat treated either at 1200 °C or
1250 °C prior to testing at 600 °C.

V. CONCLUSIONS
We investigated the effects of carbon addition and
heat-treatment temperature on the tensile and creep properties
of Fe-40 at. pct Al (0.2Mo-0.05Zr-0.46Ti-0.02B). The tensile
and creep specimens from extruded rods were heat treated
either at 1100 °C or 1300 °C for 2 hours and air cooled. After
heat treatment, samples were tested in air without removing
the oxide layers formed during the heat treatment. The fol-
lowing conclusions are drawn from the present study.
1. Tensile test results of the alloys heat treated at 1100 °C
show that the addition of carbon to FeAl is effective in
improving the strength without significantly affecting the
ductility. The fracture mode changed from predominantly
intergranular in the low (0.05 wt pct) and medium
(0.12 wt pct) carbon alloys to predominantly transgran-
ular in the high carbon (0.2 wt pct) alloy. The increase
in strength and change in fracture mode with carbon con-
tent is attributed to the presence of carbide precipitates
and their effect in minimizing the hydrogen embrittle-
ment at the grain boundaries.
2. An anomalous yield strength increase is observed at inter-
mediate temperatures in the range of 400 °C to 600 °C.
The temperature corresponding to the yield peak is shifted
to higher temperatures with the increase of carbon content.
The shift in yield peak with carbon content is attributed
to the carbon-vacancy interaction.
3. When compared to 1100 °C heat treatment, heat treating
the alloys at 1300 °C significantly increased the strength
both at room temperature and at 800 °C. The improve-
ment in strength after 1300 °C is attributed to the
combined effect of strengthening due to carbon and
vacancy hardening due to higher retained vacancies
present in the alloys. Consistent with the vacancy
hardening mechanism, the room-temperature ductility
of the alloys decreased after 1300 °C.
4. Increasing the carbon content and annealing at 1300 °C
led to a decrease in the minimum creep rate and increased
the rupture life of the present FeAl alloys. The stress
exponent of the minimum creep rate of high carbon alloy
(0.2 wt. pct C) after 1300 °C heat treatment is close to
16. The observed improvement in creep resistance and
the high stress exponent in the high carbon FeAl alloy
are rationalized by creep strengthening arise from the
presence of fine carbide precipitates and dislocation loops
formed due to condensation of vacancies in the alloy.
5. Retained vacancies formed due to high-temperature
annealing and air cooling are effective in improving the
high-temperature strength (at least up to 800 °C) and
creep resistance (at least up to 700 °C) of FeAl alloys.
6. Creep resistance of high carbon FeAl alloy (0.2 wt. pct)
is comparable to many commercial superalloys such as
IN 625, IN 690, IN 601 and IN 800, and heat-resistant
stainless steels such as 310, 316, and 422.
