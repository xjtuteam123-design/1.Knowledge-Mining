ABSTRACT 
Bearing significant concentrations of high value and critical metals, superalloy scraps are recycled to alleviate the 
current resource shortage. This paper presents a novel method of molten salt electrolysis coupled with a liquid 
cathode (Cd) for the selective nickel extraction from nickel-based superalloys. The method was inspired by the 
high corrosion resistance of superalloys, which makes recovery challenging, and the poor selectivity of existing 
nickel extraction methods. Electrochemical analysis was performed by cyclic voltammetry and linear voltam -
metry. Results indicated that Cr, Fe, and Ni were oxidized sequentially in the superalloy during electrolysis. The 
effects of electrolytic temperature and duration on the dissolution rate of the anode as well as the cathode 
composition were investigated. After electrolysis at an optimal temperature of 650 
◦
C, SEM-EDS and XRD results 
revealed that the surface of the superalloy became porous, and nickel existed as NiF
2 
in the molten salt or as Cd- 
Ni liquid alloy in the cathode. Other superalloy elements, such as iron and chromium, existed in the electrolyte as 
alloy particles or fluorinated salts and were not detected in the liquid cathode. Therefore, the proposed method of 
molten salt electrolysis coupled with liquid cathode exhibited excellent selectivity toward Ni extraction from 
superalloy scraps. 
1. Introduction 
Superalloy is a group of alloys developed for service at elevated 
temperatures where oxidation resistance is frequently required [1] . 
Depending on the main elements constituting the alloy, they can be 
categorized into nickel-based, iron-based, and cobalt-based superalloys. 
The nickel-based superalloy accounts for about 80 % of the superalloy 
production due to its excellent performance, moderate price, and good 
processability. Nickel-based superalloys are primarily used in high- 
pressure turbine blades in aerospace and other large-scale power 
equipment such as nuclear reactors and petrochemical plants [2] . 
Nickel-based superalloys possess the characteristics of good resistance to 
corrosion, thermal and mechanical fatigue, thermal and mechanical 
shock, erosion and creep at elevated temperatures [3] . The excellent 
properties of nickel-based superalloys are often attributed to the addi -
tion of different alloying elements during production, including rare 
earth metals, which account for 3 – 6 wt% of the superalloys. In order to 
meet the working requirements, the most common nickel-based super -
alloys also contain iron (Fe), chromium (Cr), cobalt (Co), titanium (Ti) 
and refractory metals such as tungsten (W), tantalum (Ta), rhenium (Re) 
and molybdenum (Mo) [3 – 5] . After the nickel-based superalloys have 
exhausted their useful life, they can be considered a valuable strategic 
resource due to the significant amounts of valuable metals they are 
composed of [1,6,7] . Recycling of secondary resources is necessitated to 
alleviate the resource shortage and guarantee environmental sustain -
ability [8,9] . 
Methods commonly used to recycle nickel-based superalloy scraps 
include pyrometallurgy, hydrometallurgy, or a combination of both. For 
the pyrometallurgical recycling route, the superalloy scraps with low 
impurity content and discernible alloy composition are re-melted as 
additives with pristine alloying metals to produce the initial superalloy 
ingots [4] . The scrapped parts are pre-treated by acid dissolution or 
mechanical polishing in order to remove the heat-resistant coating (ZrO
2 
or Al
2
O
3
) [10,11] . However, quality control of superalloys becomes 
difficult due to the uncertainty of the composition of the added super -
alloy scrap and high loss of metals (e.g., Re, W, Mo) via volatilization 
during melting, and effective control of the amount of the added su -
peralloy scrap for re-melting is imperative [4] . In general, scraps with a 
high content of impurities are mainly used as an additive in producing 
steel and other alloys [6] , but the loss of valuable metals such as Re and

In the pyro-hydrometallurgical process, high temperature oxidation, 
electrolysis, and molten salt heat treatment were investigated to destroy 
the refractory structure of the superalloys, followed by treatment with 
conventional hydrometallurgical methods [2,17] . In addition, liquid 
metal dealloying has been recently employed for recycling nickel-based 
superalloys [18,19] . Liquid metal dealloying involves mixing metals of 
relatively low melting points (e.g., Mg, Zn) with superalloy scraps to 
enable the selective dissolution of target metals. Subsequently, vacuum 
distillation separates the target metal from the dealloying medium. Thus 
liquid metal dealloying can neutralize the refractory nature of superal -
loys and selectively extract specific metals towards the applications of 
recycling superalloy scraps [4] . However, the selectivity of dealloying 
using liquid Zn was not adequate to produce relatively pure metals. And 
there are safety concerns when using liquid Mg because of the extremely 
reactive nature of liquid Mg.

In the proposed process, electrochemical dissolution of anodic su -
peralloy scraps would result in the enhanced dissolution of metal com -
ponents in ionic form into the molten salt, which subsequently get 
transported to the liquid cathode surface before deposition in metallic 
forms. Nickel metal deposited on the liquid cathode surface would 
dissolve in the cathodic liquid cadmium, while other elements remain 
deposited on the cathode surface. Continuous electrolysis would result 
in gradual enrichment of Ni in liquid cadmium, thereby realizing the 
selective extraction of Ni upon vacuum distillation of the obtained Cd-Ni 
molten alloy. 
Experimental investigation was conducted using the molten salt of 
LiF-NaF-KF (FLiNaK) with superalloy as the consumable anode and 
liquid metal (Cd) as the cathode. A three-electrode system was used to 
study the difference in the decomposition potential of the three main 
metal components (i.e., Ni, Fe, and Cr) in the Inconel 718 superalloy by 
cyclic voltammetry. The effect of electrolysis temperature and electrol -
ysis duration on the selective electrolytic recovery of nickel from the 
superalloy was studied. Furthermore, the chemical composition, 
microstructure, and phases of the obtained products were analyzed, 
aiming at revealing the mechanism of the proposed process. 
2. Materials and methods 
Experiment Materials. — Nickel-based superalloy Inconel 718 used 
in the experiments was provided by Yangguang Special Steel Group Co., 
Ltd, China. LiF, NaF, and KF with a purity of 99 wt% were purchased 
from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Aluminum oxide crucibles 
and tubes were purchased from Suzhou KF Materials Co., Ltd. The purity 
of iron, nickel, and chromium used in the experiments is over 99 wt%. 
Chemical composition of the Inconel 718 superalloy rod used in the 
study is shown in Table 1 . 
Electrochemical reaction vessel and electrodes. — All experiments 
were conducted in a sealed reaction vessel ( Fig. 3 ) heated with a pit-type 
furnace (KJ-A1200J, Zhengzhou Kejia) equipped with a vacuum pump 
and a cooling water circulation system. Residual air inside the reaction 
vessel was first removed using a vacuum pump before heating. Experi -
ments were performed in an inert argon atmosphere (5 N) with a 
continuous argon flow rate of 25 mL/min (after deoxygenation and 
dehydration). The melt and electrodes were placed in an alumina cru -
cible ( φ 50 × 100 mm) which was situated at the bottom and center of 
the reaction vessel. The tip of a K-type thermocouple was kept at the 
same height as the center of the molten salt for accurate temperature 
measurement. A three-electrode electrochemical cell, which consisted of 
a working electrode (WE), a counter electrode (CE) and a reference 
electrode (RE), was used to investigate the electrochemical behaviors. 
The WE was an inert Pt wire with a 0.5 mm diameter or Ni, Cr, Fe, and 
Inconel 718 for different experiments. The CE was a molybdenum rod 
( φ = 4 mm) of 99 wt% purity. A platinum wire ( φ = 0.5 mm) serves as a 
quasi-reference electrode, and the conductor is a spectral purity graphite 
rod ( φ = 6 mm). In the electrolysis process, the Inconel 718 superalloy 
rods ( φ = 10 mm) were used as the consumable anode and the liquid 
cadmium as the cathode. The consumable anode was an Inconel 718 
superalloy rod sheathed with an outer aluminum oxide tube. And only 
the bottom section of the superalloy, measuring 5 mm in height, was 
exposed to the electrolyte ( Fig. 3 ). The exposed part of the Inconel 718 
superalloy rod comprised 50.55 wt% Ni ( Table 1 ), corresponding to a 
mass of 1.8 g Ni. The cathode was liquid cadmium (h = 8 mm) at the 
bottom of the crucible, using a molybdenum rod sheathed in an 
aluminum oxide tube as the electrical conductor. 
Electrochemical techniques. — An electrochemical workstation 
(Squidtat Plus, Admiral, USA) controlled by the SUI v2.0 beta software 
package was employed to carry out all electrochemical measurements. 
The linear voltammetry and cyclic voltammetry were used to study the 
electrolytic characteristics of the Inconel 718 superalloy.

3.3. Effect of electrolysis temperature and time on anodic dissolution 
Fig. 6 and Table 2 confirm that nickel enrichment in cadmium can be 
achieved by electrolysis of superalloys in the FLiNaK system. However, 
at the start of the electrolysis process, there were hardly any nickel ions 
available in the molten salt for reduction. Thus, alkali metal ions in the 
molten salt can be reduced at the cathode, which is the reason for the 
presence of potassium in Area 4. The alkali metal ions were reduced, 
resulting in the chemical composition of the molten salt deviating from 
the ternary eutectic point. The resultant increase in the melting point 
and viscosity hindered the diffusion of metal ions when the system was 
not at an eutectic point. Therefore, a small amount of NiF
2 
was added to 
the control experiment to reduce the occurrence of side reactions during 
the initial electrolysis stage. The quantification of the electrolysis rate 
based on the mass loss of the superalloy anode was not accurate as the 
residual superalloy adhered to the fluoride salts, which are impossible to 
completely remove after electrolysis. Instead, the anode was cut longi -
tudinally into two exact halves, and the volume of the anode that had 
been dissolved was calculated based on the measurement of the di -
mensions of the longitudinal section and by assuming volumetric sym -
metry of the residual alloy.

3.4. Analysis of liquid cathode after electrolysis 
Chemical analysis was conducted on the liquid cathodes obtained 
from different electrolysis times. Fig. 12 shows the change in the con -
centrations of Ni, Fe, and Cr in the liquid cathode as a function of 
electrolysis duration. The nickel content in the liquid cathode shows a 
steady increase within the first 5 h of electrolysis, followed by a sharp 
increase afterward ( Fig. 12 ). Nickel was more difficult to electrolyze 
than other metals in superalloys due to its corrosion resistance nature, 
hence the concentration of nickel ions in the electrolyte was low in the 
early stage of electrolysis, resulting a very slow increase in the acuc -
mulation of Ni in the liquid cathode. On the other hand, Fe and Cr were 
almost nonexistent in the liquid cathode, indicating that cadmium has 
excellent selectivity and solubility for nickel . However, the nickel con -
tent in the cathode was far less than the amount of nickel present in the 
anode and electrolyte at the beginning of electrolysis. There are three 
possible reasons for the low Ni content in the cathode. 
First of all, Fig. 6 a showed that the superalloy anode gradually 
developed a porous Ni-rich outer layer during electrolysis. Cr, Fe, and 
other elements in the superalloy were preferentially electrolyzed, which 
may have caused the nickel-rich metal layer to peel off and fall into the 
molten salt as small nickel-rich alloy particles. These alloy particles 
tended to become suspended in the molten salt and did not reach the 
liquid cathode. Fig. 13 shows the molten salt (electrolyte) near the 
anode and cathode after electrolysis, and some points were randomly 
selected for EDS analysis with the result shown in Table 3 . The SEM 
micrograph of the electrolyte near the anode after electrolysis for 0.5 h 
showed the dispersion of Ni-rich alloy particles ( Fig. 13 a ), and the 
particles ( Point 1 ) had a nickel content of more than 70 wt% ( Table 3 ). 
This contributed to the low Ni content in the cathode. 
Secondly, the viscosity of the electrolyte near the anode has likely 
increased due to the electrochemical dissolution of the anode forming 
respective metal ions. The resultant increase in viscosity hindered the 
diffusion of metal ions to the cathode. Fig. 13 b-d shows the morphology 
of the solidified molten salt near the anode after electrolysis for 10 h, 
suggesting the presence of large quantities of crystalline particles 
measuring < 10 μ m in size. Table 3 shows that these crystalline particles 
are mainly composed of K, Ni, and F, which are presumed to be NiF
2
-KF. 
Therefore, fluoride crystalline particles with a high melting point were 
concentrated around the anode, which led to an increase in the viscocity 
of the electrolyte and hindered the diffusion of nickel ions to the 
cathode.

near the liquid cathode to form the Cd-Ni alloy. The SEM micrograph of 
the electrolyte near the liquid cathode after electrolysis for 10 h shows a 
large number of reticulated and granular metals ( Fig. 13 e -h). EDS 
analysis on Point 3 shows that the main components of these metals 
were cadmium and nickel ( Table 3 ). The formation of this structure may

4. Conclusions 
This paper presented a proof-of-concept for the enhanced selective 
recovery of nickel from Ni-based superalloy scraps. Nickel was selec -
tively dissolved into the liquid cathode cadmium and existed at room 
temperature as Cd
5
Ni by FLiNaK molten salt electrolysis at 650 
◦
C with 
Inconel 718 superalloy as the consumable anode. Linear sweep vol -
tammetry confirmed that FLiNaK can be applied as a molten salt me -
dium for Inconel 718 superalloy electrolysis. Cyclic voltammetry and 
EDS showed that Cr, Fe, and Ni dissolved into the molten salt sequen -
tially during electrolysis. The electrolysis experiments show that the 
optimum temperature for electrolytic nickel recovery is 650 
◦
C. The 
nickel content in the cathode increased significantly after 8 h of elec -
trolysis at 650 
◦
C compared to 5 h. After iron and chromium were 
preferentially dissolved during electrolysis, a porous nickel-rich outer 
layer was formed at the surface of the superalloy anode. The relatively 
low recovery of Ni was likely caused by the detachment of nickel-rich 
alloy particles from the superalloy, the formation of particles with 
high melting points near the anode that inhibited ionic diffusion, and the 
volatilization of cadmium that suspended the Cd-Ni alloy above the 
cathode. Furthermore, the experimental results show that cadmium as a 
liquid cathode has excellent selectivity and solubility for nickel. 
Conversely, cadmium exhibited minimal solubility for other metals such 
as iron and chromium. This method achieves excellent nickel separation 
compared to other processes for nickel-based superalloys, and can be 
potentially employed to treat other nickel-based alloy scraps.
