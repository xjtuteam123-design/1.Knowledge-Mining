Lattice Misfit Predictions via the Gaussian Process Regression 
for Ni‑Based Single Crystal Superalloys
Yun Zhang1  · Xiaojie Xu1
Received: 17 July 2020 / Accepted: 14 September 2020 
© The Korean Institute of Metals and Materials 2020
Abstract
Ni-based single crystal superalloys exhibit superb mechanical strength, particularly, creep resistance at elevated temperature. 
The unique microstructure, which is consisted of 
/u1D6FE and /u1D6FE/uni2032.var phases, is a major factor that determines the mechanical behavior of 
these alloys. The lattice misfit between the two phases is of particular interest in understanding and predicting the deforma-
tion mechanism. The measurement of the lattice misfit by advanced analytical instruments is costly and difficult. In current 
study, we develop the Gaussian process regression model to predict lattice misfits for Ni-based single crystal superalloys 
based on chemical composition, temperature, and two morphological indicators. The model is highly stable and accurate 
and promising as a fast, robust, and low-cost tool for lattice misfit estimations.
Keywords Ni-based single crystal superalloys · Lattice mismatch · Lattice constant · Machine learning
1 Introduction
Nickel-based single crystal superalloys (Ni-SXs) exhibit 
superior mechanical strength, particularly, the excellent 
high temperature creep strength. The high creep resistance 
of these alloys makes them great candidates for the manu-
facturing of turbines, heat exchangers, and nuclear reactors 
[1–10].
The superb high temperature creep resistance results from 
the absence of any harmful grain boundaries and the precipi-
tation of a high-volume fraction ( 
∼ 70%) of the long-range 
ordered L1 2 /u1D6FE/uni2032.var phase. The ordered 1 2 /u1D6FE/uni2032.var phase appears as 
cubes that are coherently embedded in a face-centered-cubic 
solid solution 
/u1D6FE matrix [11]. The lattice misfit, which is a 
result of different partitioning behavior of alloying elements 
in the two phases, is closely related to the movements and 
interactions of dislocations and thus creep properties of the 
Ni-SX [12–14]. For example, elements such as Ti, Al, and 
Ta, prefer to partition to the 
/u1D6FE/uni2032.var phase, while those such as Cr, 
Co, nad Mo, prefer to partition to the /u1D6FE phase. Due to 
different sizes of the atoms, the /u1D6FE/uni2032.var phase usually has a larger 
lattice constant than the /u1D6FE phase, which leads to a positive 
lattice misfit at room temperature. The natural lattice misfit 
is defined as:

prevents the

location network at the /u1D6FE//u1D6FE/uni2032.var interface consists of a/2<110> 
and a<100> dislocations, which benefits the mechanical 
behavior during the isothermal creep [20].

Because of the significant correlation between the lattice 
misfit and mechanical behavior, the lattice misfit value has 
been one of the important parameters in mechanical behavior 
modeling [21, 22]. Generally, the lattice misfit can be meas-
ured by advanced electron microscopy and diffraction tech-
niques [23–25]. In situ X-ray diffraction (XRD), convergent 
beam electron diffraction (CBED), and transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM) with electron energy loss spectroscopy 
(EELS) are among the popular methods to obtain lattice mis-
fit values at different temperature. However, these analyti-
cal methods are highly resource-intensive, time-consuming, 
and thus expensive as the sample preparation, instrumenta-
tion, and technical skills required to perform measurements 
are immense [26– 30]. Meanwhile, empirical models have 
been developed to calculate and predict the lattice misfit. 
Composition-based models are among the basic ones, which 
correlate the lattice constant changes in both phases with 
element partitioning [31]. Besides, molecular dynamics 
and density-function-theory-based models are developed to 
study the thermodynamic and structural properties of super-
alloys [32–35]. But these approaches still require significant 
amount of inputs and computational power. As these models 
are built based on ideal and equilibrium conditions, morpho-
logical conditions are not considered, which, however, affect 
the measured lattice misfit values [36, 37].
Data driven approaches have been used to model impor -
tant physical parameters and materials performance in 
diverse systems [38–59]. In the current study, we develop the 
Gaussian process regression model to predict lattice misfits 
based on chemical composition, temperature, and morpho-
logical indicators for Ni-based single crystal superalloys. 
The model manifests highly accurate and stable performance 
and is thus promising as a fast, robust, and low-cost tool for 
lattice misfit estimations.

and creep properties. But as the number of the alloying ele-
ments increases significantly, the effect of each element is 
difficult to interpret, due to synergistic effects of all elements 
on the interdiffusion and co-segregation in both phases. By 
changing the composition of the alloy, the lattice constant of 
each phase changes and so does the eventual lattice misfit.
In our study, the GPR model is able to recognize the 
influences of up to 13 elements on the lattice misfit, which 
matches well with experimental results. For example, the 
lattice misfit of ExAl7 is negative but that of IN792DS is 
positive at room temperature [24]. The sign difference is due 
to the increase of lattice constant of the 
/u1D6FE-phase in ExAl7 
while maintaining the lattice constant of /u1D6FE/uni2032.var-phase in both 
samples nearly the same. As Re mainly enriches in /u1D6FE-phase, 
its addition solely increases the /u1D6FE-phase constant. How -
ever, the situation becomes more complicated when both 
Re and Ir are added to the alloys. Without the Re addition, 
CMSX-6, ExAl9, and IN792DS show positive lattice misfits 
at room temperature, which are expected when compared 
with ExAl7 and CMSX-4. When both Re and Ir are added 
in ExAl10, however, a positive lattice misfit is observed, 
which seemingly contradicts the assumption that Re and Ir 
addition should enlarge the lattice constant of 
/u1D6FE-phase and 
render the lattice misfit negative. This can be explained by 
the reduction in Cr content in ExAl10, as the low Cr con-
tent could partially or fully compensate the increase of the 
/u1D6FE
-phase constant [24]. This example illustrates the importance 
of taking into account the combined effects of the addition or 
reduction of multiple elements, which may be time-consum-
ing and resource-intensive from an experimental approach. 
Thus, the GPR model is built to recognize the pattern, and is 
useful in fast predictions of lattice misfits for samples with 
complicated composition variations.
The temperature effect is also considered in the model 
development as the thermal expansion, partition, and seg-
regation of both phases vary differently with temperature 
changes, which along with composition, influence the sign, 
absolute value, and evolving trend of the lattice misfit. Posi-
tive values of lattice misfits at room temperature in samples 
CMSX-6, ExAl10, ExAl9, and IN792DS change to negative 
at high temperature, i.e. 1100 
◦C . The negative magnitude 
of the lattice misfit of CMSX-4 increases with increasing 
temperature. It is also observed that the variations in the 
lattice misfit with temperature are more pronounced in all 
Re-free alloys. The model also recognize the temperature 
effect on AM1 samples [ 5]. The GPR model is capable of 
learning the pattern of the non-monotonic change in the lat-
tice misfit over a wide temperature range for each sample, 
and recognizes the effect of the Re addition as well.
The lattice misfits at different locations and specimen 
forms (bulk or foil) can vary within the same sample. It was 
observed in samples CMSX-4, SRR99, and SC16 that the 
absolute value of the lattice misfit reaches the maximum at 
the dendrite center due to the segregation of refractory ele-
ments in the matrix in the dendrites [61]. It is important to 
include these features in the model development, as it would 
be useful to characterize and compare the lattice misfit at the 
/u1D6FE//u1D6FE/uni2032.var interface or at the dendrite center.
4.5 Parsimony
As aforementioned that a relatively small sample size is 
employed for model building, the model parsimony is of 
interest to examine. In Table 5, we compare our final model, 
M2, with sixteen different models, each of which remov -
ing one of the sixteen predictors in M2. It can be seen that 
for the overall case, removing one of Ni, Al, Cr, Mo, Ru, 
Ta, W, Hf, Nb, Ti, Ir, and the specimen thickness from M2 
only slightly decreases model performance. Removing Co or 
Re slightly increases average performance gap between the 
training and validation sub-samples, i.e. slightly decreased 
stabilities. The two most important predictors under our 
GPR modeling framework are the temperature and dendrite 
location, removing of which leads to significantly decreased 
model performance. Therefore, in model implementations, 
it is important to ensure availabities of the temperature and 
dendrite location values, which are also critical parameters 
when applying the model to practical situations for inter -
mediate and high temperature creep property evaluations. 
This is one of the simplest approaches to access the model 
parsimony. Removing multiple predictors together from M2 
and re-conducting similar analysis here might be of interest 
as well for model parsimony evaluations.
5 Conclusion
We develop the Gaussian process model to predict lattice 
misfits based on chemical composition, temperature, and 
morphological parameters for Ni-based single crystal super-
alloys (Ni-SXs). The model is simple, accurate, and stable, 
and thus is promising as a fast, robust, and low-cost tool 
for lattice misfit estimations. It applies to a wide range of 
Ni-SXs with various composition at room temperature and 
elevated temperature. As the model includes two morpho -
logical parameters, the sample thickness, and the dendrite 
location as descriptors, predicted lattice misfit values could 
be with more practical applications. Additionally, the model 
could be used to help design the composition of Ni-SXs 
with desired lattice misfit values based on the requirement 
on service temperature.
