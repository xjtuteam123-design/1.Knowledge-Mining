Abstract 
The Nbss/Nb3Si-Nb5Si3 alloys exhibit good high-temperature strength and creep resistance, 
and are candidate high-temperature materials for hypersonic aerospace applications . However, 
their poor oxidation resistance necessitates the application of protective coatings.The present study 
examines the comparative cyclic oxidation behavior of uncoated and Fe-Cr modified slurry silicide 
coated Nb-18.7Si, Nb -18.7Si-5Ti and Nb-18.7Si-5Zr (in at.%) alloys in air at temperatures 
between 800-1400°C. The uncoated samples showed extensive loose -scale formation from the 
initial stages of oxidation. On the other hand, the protective multi-phase oxide scale formed on the 
coating surface prevented dimensional degradation for at least 5-7 h at high temperatures of 
1400°C. 
 
Keywords: A. intermetallics; A. silicide coatings; B. thermal cycling; C. oxidation; C. high 
temperature corrosion 
 
1. Introduction 
The near eutectic Nb-Si based alloys possessing Nbss (Nb-Si solid solution)/Nb3Si-Nb5Si3 
microstructure are potential high-temperature materials for high-altitude and hypersonic 
applications. These alloys exhibit a combination of promising properties: low density, high melting 
temperatures and excellent creep resistance [1-3]. The Nbss phase provides the room temperature 
toughness, whereas the silicide phase imparts high temperature strength and creep resistance [4]. 
However, the poor oxidation resistance of both the constituent phases imposes severe limitations 
on the high temperature use of these alloys [3]. Alloying additions of Ti, Sn, Cr, Al and Mo are 
reported to improve the inherent oxidation resistance of these alloys [5-9], but not to significant 
extents [1]. Therefore, application of protective surface coatings is an alternate approach for 
increasing the high-temperature oxidation resistance of the Nb-Si alloys, as typically done for the 
Ni-based superalloy turbine engine components [10-14]. 
Silicide-based coatings provide significant oxidation resistance to the Nb-based alloys [11-
21]. Knittel et al. have demonstrated the effective oxidation resistance provided by Nb4Fe4Si7 pack 
silicide coatings applied on Nb and Nb -Si composites during oxidation in air at 1100 °C [13,14]. 
Slurry Fe-Cr modified silicide coatings were shown to provide good resis tance to the Nb -based

C103 (Nb-10Hf-1Ti-0.7Zr-0.5Ta-0.5W, in wt.% ) alloy during cyclic oxidation in air at 
temperatures until 1500°C [18]. The oxidation protection of the silicide coatings is ascribed to the 
formation of protective silica scale , which spreads on the surface as well as heals the coating 
cracks; thereby preventing the direct contact of oxygen with the substrate and improving the 
overall oxidation resistance at high temperatures. Additions of Fe and Cr further enhance the 
oxidation resistance of silicide coatings by the formation of Fe - and Cr-oxides along with silica 
[15-18]. Besides, the chemical compatibity of the silicide coatings with that of the substrate Nb-Si 
alloys can be beneficial in preventing interdiffusion of Nb and Si across the coating/substrate 
interface and the associated microstructural instability as well as the formation of deleterious 
topolologically close packed (TCP) phases during thermal exposure . Therefore, the silicide 
coatings hold the potential for application on Nb -Si alloys [13,14]. How ever, studies on the 
effectiveness of alloyed silicide coatings against oxidation of the near eutectic Nb -Si alloys are 
limited in the open literature. 
The present study examines the cyclic oxidation behavior of Fe-Cr-modified slurry silicide 
coated Nb-18.7Si, Nb-18.7Si-5Ti and Nb-18.7Si-5Zr alloys in air over the temperature range 800-
1400°C. The alloys of Nb-18.7Si containing Ti and Zr were of particular interest for study because 
of their improved fracture toughness [ 22,23] and improved kinetics of the eutectoid 
transformation, i.e. Nb3Si Nbss + α-Nb5Si3 [24], respectively, and promising engineering 
relevance. The slurry method, by virtue of its ability to generate uniform coatings and being a 
relatively economical and environmentally benign process, was adopted for the application of the 
coating. It is demonstrated that the oxidation resistance of the above alloys can be improved 
substantially by the application of the slurry Fe-Cr-modified silicide coating. The coating is 
indispensable for realizing the potential use of the alloys at high-temperatures in air. 
 
2. Experimental details 
 About 80 mm diameter and 8 mm thick disks having compositions of Nb-18.7Si, Nb -
18.7Si-5Ti and Nb-18.7Si-5Zr (in at.%) were prepared from high purity Nb (99.9%), Si (99.999%), 
Ti (99.99%) and Zr (99.98%) powders using the non-consumable vacuum arc process. Cuboidal 
coupons having dimensions of 12×10×2 (in mm) were excised by the wire-EDM process. The 
coupons were acid pickled followed by cleaning in acetone. Some of the samples were coated with 
a Fe-Cr-modified silicide coating by the slurry method [18,19]. In this process, elemental powders 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

## Page 5

of high purity Si, Fe and Cr were mixed in the ratio of 60:20:20 (wt.%) in a suitable liquid medium 
to form a slurry, which was subsequently applied on the sample surfaces by brushing. The samples 
were diffusion heat treated in vacuum at 1400°C for 2h to effect interdiffusion between the Fe, Cr, 
Si contained in the slurry and Nb from the substrate, which was essential for the formation of the 
diffusion silicide coating . Further details on th e slurry coating process can be found elsewhere 
[18,19]. 
The samples were subjected to cyclic oxidation in air at 800 , 1200 and 1400°C in an 
automated thermal cycling furnace. The low oxidation temperature of 800°C enabled the 
evaluation of effectiveness of the coating against pesting damage of the samples [13, 25-28]. Each 
cycle comprised soaking the samples at the test temperature for 15 min. and cooling outside of the 
furnace for 15 min. The soaking time was counted only after the samples attained the oxidation 
test temperature, which was monitored using a thermocouple placed in proximity to the samples 
on the sample loading stage. The weight of the samples was recorded after each cycle of oxidation. 
The weight of any loose scales/oxide powders which got detached from the sample surface was 
not considered, and only the weight of the net intact sample was measured. The specific change in 
the nett weight, i.e. the change in weight per unit initial surface area of the samples, was calculated 
after each cycle. For assessing the oxidation behavior, thermo-gravimetric plots, i.e. the change in 
specific weight of the samples with respect to the duration of oxidation, were generated. The 
various phases and the microstructure were characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), electron probe-
microanalysis (EPMA), and electron back scattered diffraction (EBSD) techniques. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Microstructure of the coated alloys 
The cross-section microstructure of the coating deposited on the various alloys is shown in 
Figs. 1(a-c). For all the alloys, the coating showed similar microstructure and its thickness varied 
in the range 2 50-300 µm. Several through-thickness cracks were observed in the coating cross -
section (Fig. 1), which occurred due to the thermal stresses generated during cooling of the coating-
substrate ensemble from the diffusion heat treatment temperature. The silicide coating experienced 
tensile residual stresses because of its higher CTE (i.e. 7.3-11.7 µm.°C-1) than that of the substrate 
alloys (i.e. 7-9 µm.°C-1) [19,21]. The coating being brittle was un-accommodative to the tensile 
thermal stresses and undergoes cracking [19,21]. Such thermal stress induced cracks are typical of

silicide coatings formed on Nb, W and Mo alloys [18, 29-34]. However, the cracks were confined 
to within the thickness of the coating and did not penetrate the substrate (Fig. 1). 
Based on the phase contrast in the back scattered electron micrographs , the coating 
microstructure can be classified into three distinct regions: i.e. outer layer, intermediate layer, and 
inter diffusion zone (IDZ). The linear quantitative analysis, i.e. chemical composition, measured 
across the coating thickness is shown in Fig. 2a. The outer layer of the coating contained 33 at .% 
Nb, 67 at.% Si a nd minor amounts of Fe and Cr (1 -2 at.%). The atom ratio of 1:2 for Nb:Si 
indicated that the outer layer contained the NbSi 2 phase, which was also confirmed from the X-
ray diffaction peaks corresponding to the unoxidized coated samples (Fig. 3). The intermediate 
layer contained lower amount of Nb, Si and higher content of Fe, Cr than that of the outer layer. 
The Nb and Si content in the intermediate layer was 23 and 45 at.% respectively, whereas that for 
Fe and Cr varied between 10-20 at.% and 10-25 at.%, respectively (Fig. 2a).The IDZ exhibited an 
increase in Nb and concomitant decrease in the Si, Fe and Cr content (Fig. 2a). Besides the 
relatively different compositions, the grain structure in each of the coating layers was also 
significantly different. The EBSD analyses confirmed that t he outer layer of the coatings was 
primarily constituted of the NbSi 2 phase and had an equiaxed grain structure , as typically shown 
in Fig. 4. On the other hand, t he intermediate layer contained complex silicid e phases of Nb, Fe 
and Cr, such as Fe3Nb4Si5, CrFe3Nb3Si6 Fe4Nb4Si7, and exhibited a columnar grain structure (Fig. 
4). The IDZ, in turn had two distinct layers comprising of fine equiaxed NbSi2 and Nb5Si3 phases, 
respectively (Fig. 4). The above phase constitution and grain structure of the coating was consistent 
with that of the coating formed on the Nb-10 wt.% Hf alloy, C103, as reported in our recent study 
[18,19,21]. 
The substrate in all the coated alloys primarily exhibited a two -phase microstructure. For 
the Nb-18.7Si and the Nb -18.7Si-5Ti alloys, the bright and gray phases corresponded to the Nb 
solid solution (Nbss) and Nb3Si, respectively, and their relative phase percentages were about 28.7 
and 68.5%. Some minor amount of the α-Nb5Si3 phase (2.8%) was also present in both the above 
alloys, as evident from the EBSD phase map (Fig. 5). The α-Nb5Si3 phase was not expected in the 
as-cast Nb -18.7Si and Nb-18.7Si-5Ti alloys and had , however, formed due to the eutectoid 
transformation of minor amounts of Nb 3Si phase during the coating formation heat treatment at 
1400°C. The formation of the α-Nb5Si3 phase in the above alloys was consistent with the literature 
reports in dicating that the eutectoid decom position of the Nb 3Si phase is indeed promoted by

3.2. Oxidation behavior 
 As mentioned earlier, the samples were cyclically oxidized in air at 800, 1200 and 1400°C, 
and each cycle comprised soaking the samples at the oxidation test temperature for 15 min. 
followed by their cooling outside the furnace for 15 min. The typical temperature profile of the 
samples during one cycle of oxidation is shown in Fig. 7a. All the uncoated samples, irrespective 
of some containing Ti/Zr, exhibited rapid formation of loose oxide scales and dimension al 
degradation from the initial stages of oxidation at all temperatures, as shown in the insets 1 -3 in 
Fig. 7b. The oxide scale s detached from the sample surfaces an d were non -protective in nature . 
Higher oxidation temperature caused greater scale formation and catastrophic dimensional 
degradation in the uncoated samples. Besides, the oxides formed on the surface reacted and got 
stuck with the crucibles during oxidation at temperatures of 1400°C. Therefore, the weight change 
of the uncoated samples could not be measured without ambuguity. However, the formation of the 
loose oxide scales and their detachment from the surface (insets 1-3 in Fig. 7b) were indicative of 
loss in the net weight of the samples. 
Unlike that of the uncoated samples, a ll t he coated samples exhibited weight gain and 
dimensional integrity at all temperatures (Fig. 7b and insets 4-9). The weight change plots for the 
coated alloys showed three distinct regimes: (i) initial parabolic weight gain, (ii) subsequent steady 
weight gain, and (iii) rapid weight loss with further thermal exposure (Fig. 7b). The value for the 
parabolic oxidation rate constant (kp) was calculated using the weight change data over the regime 
corresponding to the initial parabolic weight gain, i.e. until the onset of the regime showing steady 
weight gain. At any given oxidation temperature, the kp values were similar for all the coated alloys 
(Table 1). However, the kp values increased by about an order of magnitude with the increase in 
oxidation temperature from 800 to 1 200°C (Table 1 ). With further increase in the oxidation 
temperature above 1200°C, the rate of increase in kp was low, i.e. the kp at 1400°C was about 4-5 
times higher than that at 1200°C (Table 1). The maximum weight gain for the coated samples also

increased with the increase in oxidation test temperature (Fig. 7b). The oxidation life of the sample 
can be estimated as the duration over which the sample weight change remained positive. Exc ept 
for the coated Nb-18.7Si-5Zr sample, which showed weight loss after 120 min. at 1400°C (due to 
sticking with the crucible) as compared to its longer oxidation life of 360 min. at 1200°C and 305 
min. at 800°C, all the other coated samples registered an increase in the oxidation life. For instance, 
the oxidation life for the Nb -18.7Si sample was 305, 345 and 405 min during oxidation at 800, 
1200 and 1400°C respectively, and the corresponding value for the Nb -18.7Si-5Ti sample was 
240, 345 and 435 min (Fig. 7b). 
The general trend of increase in the kp values, weight gain, and oxidation life indicated that 
the coating was more protective against oxidation at higher t emperatures. The resistance aga inst 
oxidation was achieved by the formation of a protective oxide scale on the surface [18,19,37,38]. 
The parabolic oxidation kinetics suggested that the superficial oxide scale served as an effective 
barrier against the inward diffusion of oxygen from the ambient to the oxide -scale/coating 
interface, which thereby lowered the oxidation rate substantially [ 18,19,37,38]. The regime of 
steady weight gain was a manifestation of the lowering of the overall rate of weight gain in the 
samples, which ensued due to the competition between the parabolic growth of the oxide scale and 
the volatilization loss of Si and Cr from the oxide scale in the form of SiO and CrO 3 vapors, as 
reported previously [18-20,37,38,39,40]. The onset of weight loss in the coated samples, observed 
after ~300 min. of oxidation (Fig. 7b), occured due to the cracking and spallation of the oxide scale 
under the influence of thermal stresses arising during the repititive heating and cooling oxidation 
test cycles, as evident from the surface morphology shown in Fig. 8. It needs to be emphasized 
that despite the weight loss, there was n either catastrophic degradation nor any profuse scale 
formation in the oxidized coated samples . Rather, the coated samples appeared glossy and their 
dimensions remained intact over the entire duration of oxidation testing for 300-450 min, as shown 
in the inset s 4-9 in Fig. 7b. The glossy lustre of the surface was more prominent for the coated 
samples oxidized at higher temperatures. The similarity in the kp values for the coated Nb-18.7Si, 
Nb-18.7Si-5Ti and Nb-18.7Si-5Zr samples at the respective oxidation test temperatures indicated 
that the presence of Ti and Zr in the alloy did not have any effect on the oxidation characteristics 
of the coating (Table. 1).

3.5. Phases constituting the oxide scale 
The oxide layer formed on the coating surface was dense and had a multi-phase constitution 
across its thickness, as evident from the magnified image of the cross -section of the oxide scale 
(Fig.14). The elemental X-ray maps indicated that the dark phase (region 1 in Fig.14) was that of 
silica, whereas the bright phase (region 2 in Fig. 14) contained oxide of Nb. The silica and niobia 
phases formed by oxidation of the outer NbSi2 phase layer of the coating. The elemental maps also 
indicated that the bright phase (region 2 in Fig. 14) contained oxides of Fe and Cr along with oxide 
of Nb ( Fig. 1 4). A careful examination of the oxide scale also indicated the formation of an 
innermost continuous thin layer of Cr -oxide (region 3 in Fig. 14). However, no complex oxides 
containing Nb, Fe and Cr were detected from the XRD measurements (Fig. 3), which suggested 
that Fe and Cr were present in solid solution in Nb 2O5. Based on the free energy of formation (-
Gf°) values and the reported stability of oxides, it can be expected that oxidation of the Nb, Si, 
Cr and Fe containing phases in the coating resulted in the formation of the respective Nb2O5, SiO2, 
Cr2O3 and Fe2O3 phases. The -Gf° over the temperature range of 800-1400°C for the above oxides 
are as follows: Nb2O5 (1175-1420 kJ.mol -1), SiO2 (610-712 kJ.mol -1), Cr2O3 (692-843 kJ.mol-1) 
and Fe 2O3 (389-537 kJ.mol-1) [43]. The Nb 2O5 is an ionic oxide containing positively charged 
anion vacancies in its crystal lattice [ 44], and the formation of substitutional solid solution was 
plausible because of the similar radius of Fe3+(0.69 Å) [45], Cr3+(0.75 Å) [46] and Nb5+ ions (0.78 
Å) [47]. Besides their similar ionic radius , the Fe 3+, Cr 3+and Nb 5+ ions exhibit octahedral

3.6. Mechanism of oxidation protection 
The beneficial effect of the coating in preventing oxidation and dimensional degradation 
of the samples can be ascribed to the formation of the protective multi -phase oxide scale which 
served as a barrier against the inward diffusion of oxygen and thereby reduced the overall oxidation 
of the substrate (Fig. 14). The activation energy (Q) associated with the cyclic oxidation in air of 
the coated samples was calclulated using the Arrhenius equation, i.e. ln kp = A exp(-Q/RT); where 
A, R and T denote the pre-exponential constant, universal gas constant, and oxidation tes t 
temperatures in K [39,40,42]. The values of Q for all the three coated alloys we re similar in the 
range of 60 -70 kJ.mol-1 (Table 2 ), and correspond to the energy required for the diffusion of 
oxygen through the multi-phase oxide scale during cyclic oxidation. 
The formation of silica is profuse above temperatures of 1000 °C and s luggish at lower 
temperatures [ 18,37,49,50]. This aspect was also evident from the comparison of surface 
morphologies which showed relatively high amount of silica formation at the higher temperature 
of 1400°C (Figs. 9,10 and 11) than that at the lower temperature of 800°C (Fig.8). Concurrently, 
the formation of Nb-, Cr- and Fe-oxides was spontaneous at all temperatures, which was typically 
corroborated by the presence of the inner continuous layer of chromium-rich oxide just above the 
coating surface (region 3 in Fig. 14). The presence of Cr and Fe in the oxide layer (Figs. 14(d,e)) 
also suggested that during the exposure at high temperatures, the outward diffusion of Fe and Cr 
occurred from the intermediate layer of the coating to the outer layer , which was otherwise 
constituted predominantly of the single phase NbSi 2 in the un -oxidized coating (Fig. 4). Along 
with the Nb2O5 and SiO2, the Fe- and Cr-oxides in the oxide scale also contributed to the oxidation 
resistance. 
As alluded to earlier, the weight loss occuring at the later stages of thermal cycling can be 
ascribed to the cracking and spallation of the protective oxide scale due to stresses generated during 
thermal cycling (Fig. 7b). The Pilling Bedworth (PB) ratio for the Nb2O5 and SiO2 is reported to

growth rate, which induces stresses in the oxide scale and its spallation [ 42,49,50]. On the other

at low temperatures below 1000°C, the rate of silica formation is low [18,37,49,50]. Therefore, the 
dense outer oxide scale formed at the low temperature of 800°C, being predominantly constituted 
of the Nb2O5 phase (Fig. 8) underwent early cracking and spallation, which caused lowering of the 
oxidation life ( Fig. 7b). On the other hand, at higher oxidation temperatures above 1000°C, the 
oxide scale contained more of the compliant silica phase (Figs. 9,10 and 11), which contributed to 
the better retention and spallation resistance of the oxide scale, and hence improved the oxidation 
life of the coated samples (Fig. 7b). 
Despite the eventual spallation of the oxide scale, its reformation can be expected to 
continue until the entire coating thickness was consumed and exhausted. It was evident from Fig. 
12 that the coating remained intact and contributed to the formation of the oxide scale over the 
entire duration of the oxidation tests. The above aspect was also evident from the fact that despite 
the weight loss beyond about 300 min of oxidation, there was no dimensional degradation in the 
coated samples (insets 4-9 in Fig. 7b). Besides, the coatings were compositionally compatible with 
the substrate alloys and , hence, no deleterious topologically close packed phases formed at the 
coating-substrate interface during the high temperature oxidation exposure (Fig. 12). Therefore, 
the present study has demonstrated that the Fe-Cr-modified silicide coating can provide significant 
oxidation resistance to the near -eutectic Nb -Si alloys and prevent their oxid ation-induced 
dimensional degradation at high temperatures, which otherwise sever ely limits their high -
temperature use irrespective of alloying with oxidation inhibitors such as Ti and Zr. 
 
4. Conclusions 
The cyclic oxidation behavior of uncoated and Fe-Cr-modified slurry silicide coated Nb -
18.7Si, Nb -18.7Si-5Ti, Nb -18.7Si-5Zr (in at.%) alloys are evaluated in air at 800, 1200 and 
1400°C. All the uncoated alloys exhibited rapid oxidation and severe dimensional degradation, 
which was manifested by profu se scale formation from the very initial stages of oxidation 
exposure. On the other hand, the Fe, Cr modified silicide coating imparted good oxidation 
resistance and prevented degradation in the alloys until 5 -7 h even at higher temperatures of 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

## Page 14

1400°C. The protection against oxidation is ascribed to t he protective multi-phase oxide scale, 
which formed on the coating surface as well as healed the cracks . The oxide scale served as a 
barrier against the inward diffusion of oxygen from the ambient , which lowered the overall 
oxidation rate. The present Fe-Cr-modified silicide coating holds significance for the use of the 
near-eutectic Nb -Si alloys in strategic high -temperature applications involving oxidizing 
environments.
