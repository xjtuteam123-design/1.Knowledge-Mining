abstract
The effects of strain rate and forming temperature on the deformation behaviors of typical high-strength
alloy steel were investigated by uniaxial hot compression tests with the temperature range of 850–
1150 /C176C and strain rates of 1–50 s /C0 1. Based on the experimental results, the Johnson–Cook and Zerilli–
Armstrong models were established for the studied alloy steel. However, the comparisons between the
measured and predicted ﬂow stress by Johnson–Cook and Zerilli–Armstrong models indicate some signif-
icant deviations in most loading conditions. This is because these two models overlook the coupled
effects of strain rate–temperature–strain. Therefore, a combined Johnson–Cook and Zerilli–Armstrong
(JC–ZA) model was developed to describe the relationship of the ﬂow stress, strain rate and forming tem-
perature. Results show that the stress–strain values predicted by the proposed model well agree with
experimental ones, which conﬁrmed that the combined JC–ZA model gives an accurate and precise esti-
mate of the ﬂow stress for the studied typical high-strength alloy steel.

1. Introduction
During the hot forming process of metals and alloys, the mate-
rials are subjected to complex time, strain, strain rate, and temper-
ature histories in industrial forming processes. So, material ﬂow
behaviors during hot formation process is often complex. The
understanding of metals and alloys behavior at hot deformation
condition has a great importance for designers of metal forming
processes. Many researches have been carried out to study ﬂow
behaviors of alloy steels during hot deformation and both experi-
mental and theoretical approaches have been employed to predict
dynamic recrystallization, recovery kinetics and their effect on
ﬂow behavior [1–5]. Ji et al. [4] developed an artiﬁcial neural net-
work (ANN) model to predict the hot deformation behavior of the
ultrahigh strength steel of Aermet100 and their results show that
the ANN model can efﬁciently and accurately predict hot deforma-
tion behavior of Aermet100. Yue et al. [5] developed a set of math-
ematical models to describe the evolution of grain size during
dynamic recrystallization (DRX), metadynamic recrystallization
(MDRX), static recrystallization (SRX), and grain growth process
after recrystallization, respectively, for GCr15 steel in hot working
process. Based on metallurgical factors, some various constitutive
equations of materials were developed from the experimentally
measured data to describe the sensitivity of the ﬂow stress to the
strain, strain rate and forming temperatures in commercial hot
working applications [6–12]. The high-temperature ﬂow stress in
a Ti-modiﬁed austenitic stainless steel and modiﬁed 9Cr–1Mo
(P91) ferritic steel were predicted by the constitutive equations,
which considering the effects of strain on material constants
[6,7]. Predictions of the other two models are in good agreement
with the experimental data. Khamei and Dehghani [9,10] investi-
gated the hot deformation behaviors of 60Nitinol (Ni60–Ti40 wt.%)
alloy and developed constitutive equations to model the hot defor-
mation behaviors of 60Nitinol. Lin et al. [11] proposed a revised
model to describe the relationships of the ﬂow stress, strain rate
and temperature of 42CrMo steel at elevated temperatures by
compensation of strain and strain rate. Also, based on the classical
stress-dislocation relation and the kinematics of the dynamic
recrystallization, the ﬂow stress constitutive equations of the work
hardening-dynamical recovery period and dynamical recrystalliza-
tion period were established for 42CrMo steel, respectively [12].
Although the great progresses in development of material con-
stitutive models have been made, the physical models are still not
advanced enough to account for the whole complexity of the dy-
namic response of metals. In this study, the effects of strain rate
and forming temperature on the compressive deformation charac-
teristics of a typical high-strength alloy steel were investigated by
uniaxial hot compression tests. A combined Johnson–Cook and
Zerilli–Armstrong model for typical high-strength alloy steel over
a wide range of temperature and strain rate was developed to
describe the relationship of the ﬂow stress, strain rate and forming
temperature.

2. Experiments and results
A typical high-strength alloy steel with chemical composition
(wt.%) of: 0.450C–0.280Si–0.960Cr – 0.630Mn–0.190Mo–0.016P–
0.012S–0.014Cu-(bal.)Fe, was used for the experiments. Cylindrical
specimens were 10 mm in diameter and 12 mm in height. In order
to minimize the frictions between the specimens and die during
hot deformation, the ﬂat ends of the specimen were recessed to a
depth of 0.1 mm deep to entrap the lubricant of graphite mixed
with machine oil. The hot compression tests were performed on
Gleeble-1500 thermo-simulation machine in the four different
temperatures (850 /C176C, 950 /C176C, 1050 /C176C and 1150 /C176C), three different
strain rates (1 s
/C0 1,10 s/C0 1 and 50 s /C0 1). As shown in Fig. 1, the exper-
imental procedure for hot compression tests are the specimens
were heated to 1200 /C176C at a heating rate of 10 /C176Cs /C0 1, held for
5 min and cooled at 10 /C176Cs /C0 1 to the forming temperature, held
for 1 min to eliminate thermal gradients. Then, the specimens were
compressed at the selected constant temperature and rapidly
quenched with water.
Typical true stress–strain curves obtained from hot compres-
sion tests of the studied alloy steel are shown in Fig. 2 . It can be
found that the effects of the forming temperature and strain rate
on the ﬂow stress are signiﬁcant for all the tested conditions. The
deformation resistance increases with the decrease of deformation
temperatures for a given strain rate and decreases with the de-
crease of strain rates for a given deformation temperature. The ef-
fects of the deformation temperature and strain rate on ﬂow stress
can be explained by the terms of dynamic recrystallization and dis-
location mechanism. The deformation mechanism associated with
the isothermal compression test is a thermally activated process.
3. Constitutive equations for predicting ﬂow stress
The stress–strain data obtained from compression tests under
different strain rates and temperatures can be used to determine
the material constants of constitutive equations. Amongst the
empirical and semi-empirical models, Johnson–Cook (JC) [13–16]
and Zerilli–Armstrong (ZA) [17–20] models were successfully used
for a variety of materials with different ranges of deformation tem-
perature and strain rate.

4. Results and discussion
4.1. Johnson–Cook (JC) model
According to the original JC model, 850 /C176C is taken as reference
temperature (minimum temperature of test matrix) and 1.0 s /C0 1 the
reference strain rate. A, which is the yield stress at reference tem-
perature and reference strain rate, can be measured as 102.60 MPa.
Based on the measured stress–strain data, the parameters of JC
model (shown in Table 1 ) can be easily evaluated by the similar
method to reference [23]. The comparisons between the measured
and predicted results by JC model of the studied alloy steel are
shown in Fig. 4. Obviously, the prediction can be accepted only un-
der the reference temperature and reference strain rate. When the
difference between the testing strain rate and reference strain rate
increases, the accuracy of the prediction decreases. Also, when the
difference between the testing temperature and reference temper-
ature increases, the error of the predictions increases. i.e., a signif-
icant deviation in prediction indicates that JC model cannot
adequately represent the high-temperature ﬂow behaviors of the
studied alloy steel. This is because JC model assumes that thermal
softening, strain rate hardening and strain hardening are three
independent phenomena and can be isolated from each other.
Actually, the coupled effects of temperatures, strain rates and tem-
perature on the ﬂow behaviors of the alloy steel should be consid-
ered [24].

5. Conclusions
The deformation characteristics of a typical high-strength alloy
steel have been investigated by means of the compression test over
a practical range of temperatures and strain rates. Based on exper-
imental stress–strain data, a combined JC–ZA model is proposed to
predict the ﬂow stress. Comparisons between the experimental
and predicted results were carried out and conﬁrmed that the
developed constitutive equations can presented an accurate and
precise estimate of the ﬂow stress for the studied typical high-
strength alloy steel. This is because the developed combined JC–ZA
model not only consider the yield and strain hardening phenome-
non, but also the coupled effects of the temperature and strain rate
on the ﬂow behaviors of the alloy steel. However, there are some
signiﬁcant deviations in predicted results by JC and ZA models in
most of the loading conditions.
