Abstract 
The presence of precipitate phases M23C6 (M = Cr, Fe, Mo, or W) plays a significant role in 
the mechanical strength of Ni-based superalloys. The crystal structure of these complex 
carbides consists of 116 atoms in the cubic cell of an fcc lattice. Here we report the 
investigation of the electronic structure, interatomic bonding and mechanical properties for 
four binary phases of M23C6, and M23N6, and six ternary phases of M123-x M2xC6. Based on 
the results of our comprehensive calculations and the evaluation of a quantum mechanical 
metric called the total bond order density (TBOD), we conclude that Fe tends to make the 
intermetallic structure less stable whereas W makes the crystal more compressible. We trace 
the root of these observations to the size of the metal ions and the complex interatomic 
bonding between C atoms and the metal d electrons. In addition, we correlate the TBOD with 
calculated mechanical properties leading to confirmation that Cr 23C6 is the most stable 
precipitate phase with superior overall physical properties. On the other hand, replacing C 
with N in these crystals does not result in discernable differences in their properties. Our 
detailed investigation sheds much light on the use of carbides precipitates as a stabilization 
strategy for Ni-based superalloys. 
Keywords:, Electronic structure, mechanical properties, total bond order density, precipitate carbides and nitrides.

1. Introduction 
The desire for corrosion -resistant alloys for various high 
temperature applications led to the development of stainless 
steels in the 20 th century. Over the course of subsequent 
decades various stainless steel “su peralloy” improvements 
were manufactured [1]. Other alloys that are used above 
540°C (e.g., nickel -, iron-nickel and cobalt -based alloys) are 
also regarded as superalloys [1-5]. Superalloys fulfill a critical 
need for high strength at high temperature for a wide variety 
of structural application because of their superior mechanical 
properties. For example, having adequate creep resistance at 
elevated temperatures is an important requirement so that 
power plants can maintain a high level of thermal efficiency 
and minimize emission of CO 2 and other hazardous gases [6, 
7]. The development of improved alloys is also important in 
other applications such as in liquid boilers, thermal and 
nuclear power generators, chemical and petroleum refinement 
plants, etc. [8]. 
 
The base microstructure of a superalloy is typically 
characterized by the presence of a polycrystalline face 
centered cubic (fcc) matrix phase comprising a mixture of 
transition metal (TM) elements such as nickel, cobalt, or iron. 
The mechanical strength of the fcc-based matrix depends on a 
number of other strengthening phases such as crystalline 
precipitates that are added/produced both within the grain 
structure and at the grain boundaries (GBs) [1, 4, 9]. Ni-based 
superalloys include the following to v arying degrees: (1)  
phase (fcc ordered Ni) along with precipitate phases such as ’ 
(fcc ordered Ni 3Al) within the matrix phase; (2) the fcc 
carbides M23C6, where M stands for a transition metal such as 
Cr, Mo, W, Fe, or their mixtures) located at the GB [10]; and 
(3) ’’ [11], an ordered body centered tetragonal (bct) phase 
Ni3Nb. The amount, morphology, and distribution of these 
precipitates plays a significant role in controlling and 
enhancing the mechanical properties and oxidation resis tance 
of superalloys, especially the Ni -based ones [12-15]. The 
strength of the alloys is usually derived from the precipitate 
phases ’ and ’’[4, 5, 9], while the creep resistance is heavily 
influenced by the carbide phases at the GBs [10, 16]. The alloy 
is also strengthened by the grain structure and the 
discontinuous precipitation of lamellar M 23C6 crystallites at 
the GBs [17-19] [20]. The formation of M 23C6 begins with 
combination of carbon atoms with vacancy clusters [21]. The 
M23C6 precipitates in the GB can be in the form of globules, 
platelets, lamella, and cells [1]. The inter facial interaction 
between the GB precipitates and the  phase helps govern the 
coarsening process, subsequently the creep behavior, and then 
the rupture life of the alloy [19, 22]. Additionally, the M 23C6 
carbides may provide strengthening through the formation of 
discrete particles at the GB which could delay GB sliding 
during creep resulting in an increase of the creep -rupture life 
[1]. On the other hand, non -homogeneous distribution of 
M23C6 precipitates are also connected to intergranular 
corrosion leading to a decrease in ductility, toughness [20], 
and creep properties [23, 24]. 
 
A variety of methods or alloying strategies have been 
implemented to control the stability and morphology of the 
M23C6 carbides. For example, altering the steel chemistry by 
adding stabilizers or nitrogen can reduce the formation of 
M23C6 [25, 26]. Replacing even a small amount of C by N has 
been shown to reduce the carbides with undesired 
morphologies because N is not soluble in M 23C6. However, 
because M 23N6 can exist in N -stabilized austenitic stainless 
steels [27], it is certainly of interest to see if the M 23N6 phase 
shows behavior similar to M 23C6 in terms of its mechanical 
properties. To our knowledge, there has not been any study 
that performs a one -to-one comparison between these two 
precipitate phases. More broadly, understanding these 
precipitates is not only important for the development of 
superalloys, but it is also helpful for planetary researchers and 
geophysicist who wish to understand the forma tion of 
meteorites and minerals [28, 29]. 
 
Crystalline M23C6 has been studied extensively with a focus 
on micr ostructural characterization [30-34]. There are a 
limited number of ab initio theoretical calculations on M23C6. 
Previous work examines the fundamental stability of binary 
and ternary carbides M23C6 [35] and γ-(Fe, M) 23C6 [36], and 
another study examines the coherent interfaces between an fcc 
metallic matrix and Cr 23C6 [37]. There are also a wide range 
of crystallographic studies examining the binary mixture of 
M1 and M2 (M123-xM2xC6) wherein the C is replaced by B or 
P [38-40] or N [26]. The mechanical properties in some of 
these carbides are reported by [41]. There are also electronic 
structure and mechanical properties studies on some of the 
M23C6 carbides [42]. Nevertheless, fundamental studies that 
focus on the elastic properties of the M23C6 carbides as well as 
the relationship between mechanical properties and the 
electronic structure and interatomic bonding are unfortunately 
still missing. Moreover, there has been no comparative study 
between M23C6 carbides and M23N6 nitrides. In this paper, we 
report the results of extensive first -principles calculations of 
the electronic structure, inter atomic bonding, mechanical 
properties for both binary carbides M 23C6 and nitrides M 23N6 
(where M = Cr, Fe, Mo, and W). In addition, we also report 
our analysis on the fundamental properties of the ternary 
variants of these carbides M123-xM2xC6 where (M1, M2) is the 
combination of two metals among Cr, Fe, W, and Mo. In the 
following, we first discuss the complex crystal structure of 
M23C6 in section II. In section III, the computational methods 
used for the calculation are discussed. The main results are 
presented and discussed in section IV including a comparison

2. M23C6 phases

base superalloys, Cr 23C6 is the most prominent phase first 
discovered in 1933 [43]. Its crystal structure was reported by

structure as Cr 92C24 in this work. Fig. 1 depicts the 
cuboctahedra of C (gray) atoms with light blue outline and

3. Computational Methods 
We used two computational packages in this work. The 
Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [45] and the 
Orthogonal Linear Combination of Atomic Orbitals 
(OLCAO) method [46]. Both are based on density functional 
theory (DFT). For structural optimization and elastic 
properties calculations, we used VASP. The PAW -PBE 
potential [47] with generalized gradient approximation (GGA) 
for the exchange correlation potential was adopted. We used a 
relatively high energy cutoff of 600 eV, with electronic and 
ionic force convergence se t at 10 -6 eV and 10 -4 eV/Å 
respectively with 2×2×2 k-point mesh. For elastic properties 
using VASP, we used the stress (σ j) and strain (ε i) response 
analysis scheme [48, 49] on the fully relaxed structure and 
applied a small strain ε (± 0.25%) to obtain the elastic 
coefficients Cij and compliance tensor Sij (i, j = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6). 
From the calculated Cij, other mechanical properties such as 
the bulk modulus (K), shear modulus (G), Poisson’s ratio (η), 
and Young’s modulus ( E) were obtained using the Voight -
Reuss-Hill (VRH) polycrystalline approximation [50, 51]. 
 
For electronic structure and interatomic bonding, we us ed 
the in -house developed OLCAO method with structures 
obtained from VASP relaxation as input. In the OLCAO 
method, atomic orbitals are used in the basis expansion. The 
economic use of the basis set in OLCAO with the VASP 
optimized structure has been succe ssfully used in electronic 
properties calculations for bulk metallic glass [52] , 
biomolecules [53-56], glasses [57, 58] and other complex 
systems [59-61]. In OLCAO, a full basis (FB) is used for the 
self-consistent potential, total density of states (TDOS) and 
partial DOS. A more localized minimal basis (MB) is used for 
the calculation of bond order (BO) values or the overlap 
population 𝜌𝛼𝛽 between any pair of atoms ( α , β) based on 
Mulliken population analysis [62, 63].

4. Results and Discussion 
4.1 Electronic Structure 
Detailed ab initio electronic structure calculations on 
complex precipitate crystals such as M 23C6 can be very 
insightful. The calculated total density of states (TDOS) and 
atom-resolved partial density of states (PDOS) for all binary 
and ternary carbides are shown in Figs. 2 and 3 respectively. 
The zero of the energy is set at the Fermi level ( EF). The 
occupied portion of the DOS (< 0.0 eV) consists of two 
segments. The segment below approximately -12 eV reflects 
the strong M-C bonding while the bands within the region of 
approximately -7 to 0 eV are derived from the 3d or 4d orbitals 
of the TMs. The TDOS at the Fermi level, N(EF), shows the 
presence of a deep valley in each case except for Fe 23C6. The 
magnitude of N(EF) and its atomic composition is important 
for transport properties (e.g., electrical conductivity and the 
electronic contribution to thermal conductivity) in metallic 
system because they represent, qualitatively, the availability 
of charge carriers at the Fermi surface and their ability to 
transition to conducting states. The atomic contribution to 
N(EF) can be obtained from the PDOS. Lower N(EF) values 
tend to imply a deeper valley at EF. However, the 
interpretation of the depth of the valley at EF is also related to 
the details of the TDOS near the minimum. A minimum in 
N(EF) has been conventionally used as a qualitative measure 
to estimate the stability of a crystal [64] and this seems to be

eliminate the impact of Fe. These results suggest that the 
addition of Fe to a binary carbide may lead to reduced stability 
of the precipitate phase whereas the mixing of larger TMs such 
as W and Mo will have the opposite effect. The dynamic 
interplay of different atomic mixing in the replacement of Cr 
by other TMs can be traced to a slight shift of EF in the PDOS 
related to the d-orbitals of the metal in a rather complicated

Fig. 3(d) is that in Fe46W46C24, the C-related peak close to -12

upper portion of the DOS from W-3d orbitals. The C-W bonds 
have a significantly different distribution compared to the 
other systems because C is very small in size whereas W is the 
largest atom of the four TMs studied. S uch information can 
only be revealed by detailed calculation of the electronic 
structures. 
4.2 Interatomic bonding 
The M 23C6 carbides tend to have strong, short covalent 
bonds between the metal and carbon atoms (M -C). They also 
have metallic bonding between TMs which is very different 
from covalent or ionic bonding in insulators. Proper 
description of multi -atom metallic bondin g is highly 
controversial because all the nearby atoms (including those 
beyond the NN atoms) that form a metallic pair contribute to 
the overall bonding. The OLCAO method described in Section 
III can naturally account for this multi -atomic bonding since 
atomic orbitals are used in the basis expansion instead of 
plane-waves. The BO vs. bond length (BL) plots for binary 
and ternary carbides are shown in Fig. 4 and Fig. 5 
respectively. It should be emphasized that the BLs referenced 
here are the distances of separation between two atomic pairs, 
but that the BO values reflect the quantum mechanical 
influence of the other atoms (See Eq. (1)).

5. Conclusions 
In this article, we have presented detailed results on the 
electronic structure, interatomic bonding and mechanical 
properties of precipitate M 23C6 crystals and their ternary 
mixtures with four different transition metal elements Cr, Fe, 
Mo, and W. Similar calculations on binary M 23N6 nitrides 
were also carried out and compared to the binary carbides. Our 
study provides the following important conclusions: 
(1) We show the presence of a deep valley , indicating 
stability of the crystal , in the DOS at the Fermi level along 
with low values for N(EF) in these crystals except for M = Fe. 
(2) The Fe-containing binary carbides and nitrides are less 
stable among the precipitate crystals due to weaker Fe -Fe 
bonding compared to the other M-M bonds. 
(3) The elastic modulus and the mechanical parameters of 
the four binary and six ternary carbides and four binary 
nitrides are calculated and analyzed in detail in relation to their 
electronic structure and bonding. Most importantly, we 
provide the evidence of a positive correlation between 
electronic structure (N(EF)), TBOD, and mechanical 
properties (especially the G/K ratio) in these complex crystals.
