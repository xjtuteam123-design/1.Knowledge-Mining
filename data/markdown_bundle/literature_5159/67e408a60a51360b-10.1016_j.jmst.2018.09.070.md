The good combination of mechanical and tribological properties for self -lubricating 
materials is crucial. In this work, novel self -lubricating Fe-16.4Mn-4.8Ni-9.9Al-xC 
(wt.%) steels containing graphite phase were fabricated using mechanical alloying and 
spark plasma sintering. The compositions of the steels were designed by using 
thermodynamic calculation, and the eff ect of carbon addition on the microstructure was 
further investigated. The steel possesses high hardness of 621 HV , high yield strength of 
1437 MPa and good fracture toughness at room temperature. The yield strengths are still 
above 600 MPa at 600 °C. The tribological behavior and mechanical properties from room 
temperature to 800 ºC were studied, and the wear mechanisms at elevated temperatures 
were discussed. The steel ha s a stable friction coefficient of 0.4 and wear rate in a 
magnitude of 10-6mm3/N·m below 600 ºC. The good tribological properties of the steels 
were mainly attributed to the high hardness, lubrication of graphite and stable surface 
oxide layer.

1. Introduction 
Self-lubricating materials are important when serving under extreme condition instead of 
conventional oil lubricating systems, especially in high temperature environment [1-4]. Metal 
matrix self -lubricating materials are one of the most important materials due to their excellent 
mechanical properties. In recent years, a number of metal matrix self -lubricating materials have 
been successfully developed using powder metallurgy process, spark plasma sintering 
(SPS)[4-6]. It was reported that the SPS can avoid the reaction between the matrix and lubricants 
due to the rapid sintering process [5,6]. However, adding lubricant leads to the decrease of 
mechanical properties, which may cause serious engineering failure [7,8]. Therefor e, it is still a 
great challenge on balancing the mechanical and wear properties of the self -lubricating 
materials. 
The matrix phases offer the basic mechanical properties of the self -lubricating materials. 
Co-based and Ni -based alloys, due to the good com bination of mechanical properties and 
thermal stability, have been widely used as matrix materials [9-11]. Recently, high entropy alloys 
(HEA) have been chosen to be the matrix of self -lubricating materials due to their simple 
structures, which avoid the de trimental phases[12-14]. However, the high cost and decreasing of 
mechanical properties due to the addition lubricants to the alloys are still not satisfactory [15]. 
Therefore, developing a new candidate matrix alloy with both low cost and promising 
mechanical properties is necessary. 
Developing of novel Fe -based alloy, such as high entropy steels and ultra -strong steel 
materials have received substantial attention [16]. These steels have excellent mechanical 
properties which in part far exceed those of the known alloys. Replacing the current materials 
with these Fe -based materials can greatly cut the cost of the matrix alloy without sacrificing the 
mechanical properties [17]. For designing self -lubricating Fe -based materials, the alloying 
elements are careful ly selected. Expensive metal elements such as Co, Ti are avoided [15], also, 
strong carbide -formation elements such as Cr, W are not used in order to preserve the graphite 
phase, which is designed to be the lubricant [18,19]. Among the Fe -based materials, the

Fe-Mn-Ni-Al alloys are very promising [20-23]. The alloying of Ni and Al can promote the 
formation of the B 2 intermetallic compound, which enhances the mechanical properties and 
thermal stability. Thus, Fe -Mn-Ni-Al alloys can be considered as the matrix of self -lubricating

The aim of this work is to design a self -lubricating steel containing graphite phase by 
using powder metallurgy method. The composition of the matrix alloy was chosen from the 
Fe-Mn-Ni-Al syst em. The microstructural evolution, and the mechanical and wear properties 
were studied. 
 
2. Experimental 
The high entropy steel was prepared by high purity Fe, Mn, Ni, and Al powder with the 
nominal compositi on of Fe -16.4Mn-4.8Ni-9.9Al (wt %). The powder after milling for different 
time was examined using X -ray diffraction with a Cu Kα target at 40 kV and 30 mA. The 
ball-milled powder for 25 h was used. The compositions of Fe-16.4Mn-4.8Ni-9.9Al-xC, (x= 0, 
1, 2, 3 and 4 wt %) were used in this study. For convenience, they are denoted as C 0, C1, C2, C3, 
and C 4, respectively. The powder was placed into a stainless vial with stainless balls, and the 
ball to powder ratio was 10:1. In order to prevent the welding of powder in the vial, a process 
control agent o f 2 wt% stearic acid was added. The vial was then assembled on a high -energy 
milling equipment with a speed of 250 rpm. The mixtures of milled powder and carbon were 
filled into the graphite die with a diameter of 30 mm. SPS (HPD 25/3) was conducted with a 
vacuum of 1 × 10-3 Pa. The powder was heated from room temperature to 1373 K at a heating 
rate of 100 K /min and held for 15 min, followed by furnace cooling. The actual C content of 
the steels after SPS was determined by the fusion method on a LECO C ana lyzer (Table 1). 
The microstructures of the steels were observed by using a scanning electron microscope 
(SEM, Quanta FEG 250) equipped with an Energy Dispersive X -ray (EDX) analyses. The 
density was determined by the Archimedes method, and the relative density was measured by 
the imaging analyses under an optical microscope (Leica -DM3 XL). The micro -hardness was 
measured by a HV -Vicker’s hardness instrument using a load of 0.3 N. Samples with a gage 
size of 6 mm × 9 mm were prepared for the compressive t ests on Gleeble -3180 with a strain 
rate of 1.0 × 10 -3 s-1. The fracture toughness of the samples was determined by three -point 
bending tests using single edge notch binding (SENB) according to ASTM E399 -12.

The dry friction and wear tests from room temper ature to 800 ºC were carried out on a 
rotational ball-on-disk high temperature tribometer (HT -1000, China) in air. The 
Fe-16.4Mn-4.8Ni-9.9Al-xC alloys were tested in a disk size of 30 mm × 5 mm. The counterface 
ball is Si 3N4 with a size of 6 mm. The rotati ng testing parameters, including diameter, sliding 
speed, loads, testing time and sliding distance were 4 mm, 0.28 m/s, 20 N, 30 min, 504 m,

(NT9100, Veeco). The microstructures of the worn surfaces were examined by using SEM, 
EDS and a micro-beam XRD diffractometer (Rigaku Rapid IIR). 
3. Results 
3.1. Phase diagram calculation 
Thermo-Calc program together with a validated database based on the CALPHAD has 
been applied for compositional design in the complex multi -phase systems [24,25]. For 
self-lubricating Fe -based materials, Fe, Mn, Ni, Al and C elements were selected. Based on the 
fact that Mn, Ni and Al could form continuous solid solutions with Fe after mechanical 
alloying [26,27], the Fe-16.4Mn-4.8Ni-9.9Al-xC steels were taken as the Fe -based alloy, and the 
preliminary calculations were then based on the Steels/Fe -Alloys datab ase. The presence of 
graphite phase with the increasing of carbon addition can be expected by the calculation, as 
shown in Fig. 1. 
At the sintering temperature of 1373 K, the graphite phase appears a bove the content of C 
of 1.6 wt%. According to the content of carbon after sintering ( Table 1), the phase compositions 
of C0, C1 and C 2 are BCC and FCC, while those of C 3 and C4 are BCC, FCC and graphite. The 
carbide phase may form during the cooling. As the result of the rapid cooling rate after the SPS, 
the graphite phase can be remained to room temperature [6]. 
3.2. Microstructural characterization 
Fig. 2 shows the XRD patterns of the as -milled powder and the as -sintered steels. As shown 
in Fig. 2(a). At a milling time of 15 h, the Al element disappears completely, suggesting that Al 
has dissolve d into the Fe lattice (BCC structured) due to the rapid diffusion [28]. A complete 
dissolution of Mn is very difficult as a result of its high hardness [29]. Therefore, the diffraction 
peak of Mn can still be observ ed after 35 h milling. As shown in Fig. 2(b), the phase structures

of sintered steels fit well with the calculation in Fig. 1. A strong FCC and a weak BCC -structure 
peaks can be observed in C 0, suggesting that the phase transformation has taken place durin g 
SPS. The peaks of AlFe 3Cx carbides appear in the sintered steels. Only small peaks of graphite

Fig. 3 shows microstructures of the Fe-16.4Mn-4.8Ni-9.9Al-xC steels. The C 3 steel of high

be found in the microstructure. The graphite phase can be remained due to incomplete diffusion 
between the carbon and the matrix during the rapid SPS process [5,6]. 
According to the XRD analyses (Fig. 2 ) and microstructures (Fig. 3 ), the experimental 
phase composition fit well with the calculation. T he content of the carbon in C0 is determined 
to be 0.28 wt% ( Table 1 ). As there is no carbon added in C 0, the C may come from the 
processing control agent, st earic acid . According to the phase diagram based on the CALPHAD 
results ( Fig. 1), the max imum solubility of carbon in the alloy at room temperature is 0.87%, 
which is far above 0.28%. Combining the results of phase diagram, XRD and microstructure of 
C0, it is clear that all the carbon is in solid solute. With the addition of C, the carbide phase 
(AlFe3Cx) in C 1 and C 2 steels can be observed ( Fig. 2 (b)). C exist s as both interstitial element 
and carbides. With the further increasing of carbon , excessive C is supersaturated and graphite 
starts to precipitate in C 3 and C 4 steels, as shown in Fig. 1. Take the C 3 alloy for example , 
sintered at 1373 K , the phase is composed of FCC, BCC and Graphite. In the cooling process, a 
generation of carbide could be observed around 1100 K. The phase composition is the same as 
the RT XRD result. This is stem med from the fast cooling speed of the SPS process, preventing 
segregation from the phase formed at elevated temperature. 
The microstructure and composition of C 3 steel are shown in Fig. 4. Three types of phases 
with different contrasts can be observed, which are marked by white arrow and designated as 
phases I, II and III. Phase I is the main phase of the steel. Phase II distributes in the grains of 
phase I while phase III disperses homogeneously. According to the compositional mappings, 
phases I, II and III are Fe -rich, (Ni, Al)-rich and C -rich, respectively. 
3.3. Mechanical properties 
Compressive curves of C 0 and C 3 steels under room temperature and that of C 3 at elevated 
temperatures are shown in Fig. 5. Both C 0 and C3 steels show obvious work hardening and good

ductility. The addition of C apparently increases the yield (~ 400MPa) and compressive 
strength (~ 300 MPa) without significant decrease of the ductility. C 3 deformed at 400 °C 
shows continuous strengthening after yiel ding at 803 MPa. At 600 and 800 °C, a rapid decline 
in yield stress and apparent softening is occurred. 
It has been widely reported that the B 2-structure precipitation in Fe -Ni-Mn-Al alloys can 
lead to an increase in strength but a decrease in ductility [21-23]. When Fe 36Ni18Mn33Al13 was 
treated under 1000 K for 72 h upon annealing, the size of B 2 intermetallics can increased from 
139 nm to 409 nm [21]，the growth of B2 phase actually is very slow . In this study, the testing 
temperature is lower than 1000 K (673 -873 K), and the exposure time is rather short, it can be 
inferred that the B2 phase in the alloy should be stable enough . 
B2 intermetallics is a common precipitation in the Fe -Mn-Ni-Al alloys, the hard B 2 phase is 
acted as a strengthening phase at low temperature to obstacle the motion of dislocation. 
However, the lack of plastic ity in the B 2 phase will lead to workhardening [21-23]. 
According to compression curves of C 3 steels at elevated temperatures, the softening ha s 
replaced the continues strengthening between 400 and 600 ºC, and the strength drops sharply at 
800 ºC. At the same time, the COF has increased quickly at 800 ºC. Therefore, the steels are 
suitable to be applied in the temperature s ranging from 400 to 600 ºC. 
3.4. Wear behavior 
The wear properties of the steels were studied . The coefficient of friction (COF) against the 
sliding time and wear properties are illustrated in Fig. 6. It can be seen that all the steels exhibit 
steady COF curves a t room temperature. Comparing to C 0, C1 and C 2, the COFs of C 3 and C4 
decrease obviously. This is mainly due to the presence of graphite phase. The wear rates of the 
steels tested at room temperature are in the magnitude of 10 -5mm3/N·m. The decrease of the 
wear rate of C 1 and C 2 is due to the solid solution strengthening of carbon, which leads to an 
increase in the hardness. H owever, the increase of wear rates of C 3 and C4 is due to the presence 
of graphite phase, which leads to the weakening of the matrix and decrease of hardness. 
The COFs of C 3 tested at elevated temperatures are shown in Fig. 6(c). With the 
temperature increasing, the COF firstly decreases and then increases. The COF at 200 ºC 
slightly decreases to 0.36. The COF s at 400 ºC and room temper ature are similar. Further 
increasing the temperature to 600 ºC, the COF becomes unstable, but still maintains around 0.4. 
When the temperature reaches 800 ºC, the COF increases sharply and unstable. The wear rates 
tested at high temperatures decrease dram atically compared to those at room temperature.

The worn surfaces of the C 3 steels in the temperature s ranging from 200 to 800 °C are 
shown in Fig. 7 . The worn surfaces of C 3 at high temperatures are discontinuous. The worn 
surfaces tested at 200 and 400 ºC are similar showing a mixture of adhesive and abrasive wear

In order to further understand the wear resistance of C 3 steel at high temperatures, the 
typical w orn tracks and profilometer traces across the wear tracks are shown in Fig. 8. The 
track after tests at 800 °C is broader than those tested at low temperatures. This is consistent 
with the morphology of the worn su rfaces ( Fig. 7). Meanwhile, the depths of the tracks of 
various temperatures are also different. At 800 °C, a deeper wear track can be seen than those 
of other temperatures. There is significant pil e-up of materials on the edges of the worn track 
due to plastic deformation at high temperatures. 
The EDS results ( Table 2 ) show that the O contents on the dark regions of the worn 
surfaces are obviously higher than those of the light regions (Region 1 vs 2; 4 vs 3; 5 vs 6), and 
the C contents are lower at the O -rich regions. This indicates that the oxidation wear occurred. 
The overall increasing of O contents with the temperature indicates a more serious oxidation at 
high temperatures. The decreasing of C content may reduce the lubricity of the graphite. Also, 
there are debris attached on the worn surfaces, indicating that the wear mechanism at high 
temperatures is the combination of abrasive and adhesive wear. After testing at 800 °C, the 
content of Ni and Al decrease obviou sly compared to the other temperatures. This indicates that 
Ni and Al have mostly been oxidized in the surface. A small amount of Si from the counterpart 
Si3N4 balls is found on all the surface of the steels. This also shows that the adhesive wear has 
occurred. 
Fig. 9 shows the micro -beam XRD analyses of the C 3 steel after being tested at different 
temperatures. The regions correspond to Fig. 7. Al2O3 is found at low temperature s, while other 
types of oxides such as (Fe, Mn) 2O3, and (Ni, Al) intermetallic compound are found at high 
temperatures. With the increasing of temperature, the peak of C can rarely be observed, 
indicating a severe oxidation.

The hardness -density diagram including Fe -based alloys and the steels of this work is 
shown in Fig. 10. The alloys are Fe -Mn steel[30,31], cast iron [32], ultrahigh carbon steel [33,34], and 
self-lubricating alloys [4-6]. The steels in this work has a lower de nsity and higher hardness 
(6.42-6.82 g/cm 3, 566-658 HV) than conventional steels . The density and mechanical properties 
of C0 and C 3 steels are listed in Table 3. The density of C 0 and C 3 steel is 6.82 and 6.49 g/cm3, 
respectively. The density decreases with the addition of carbon. The low density of the steel is 
benefited from the additions of elements , such as Al, C, and the high hardness derives from the 
fine microstructures. 
For commonplace, the addition of lubricating phase in the self -lubricating materials 
decreases the mechanical properties, especially in fracture toughness [5,6,35]. However, 
comparing to self -lubricating HEAs ( Table 3), the steels in this work have better mechanical 
properties. The good properties are mainly derived from the Fe -Mn-Ni-Al matrix alloy. 
Therefore, powder metallurgical Fe -Mn-Ni-Al-C steels are very promising compared to other 
self-lubricating materials. 
4.2. Worn mechanism at elevated temperatures 
For self -lubricating materials, high temperature (HT) wear resistance and lubricity are 
important properties. The F e-Mn-Ni-Al-graphite material has superior wear resistance at HT 
compared to other self -lubricating materials under the same testing conditions [5,6]. The good 
performance of the C 3 steel are explained as follows. 
With the increasing of testing temperature s, the oxidation of the steel becomes severe. The 
decrease of carbon indicates that the graphite has been oxidized, which may reduce the 
lubrication and lead to an increase of COF. However, the C 3 at elevated temperatures keeps a 
stable COF of 0.4, because th e formation of oxidation products (iron oxides) on the worn 
surfaces play an important role in the wearing process [35,36]. 
The C 3 steel also possesses relatively low wear rates in the order of 10 -6 mm3/N·m at 
elevated temperatures, while the wear rate is i n the order of 10 -5 mm3/N·m at room temperature. 
The wear rate decreases from room temperature to 400 ºC and increases above 400 ºC. At low 
temperatures, the protection of oxides is crucial to the wear resistance. According to results of 
microbeam XRD (Fig. 9 ), it can be found that the oxidation layers mainly consist of Al 2O3 at 
200 ºC, and the Al 2O3 can enhance the hardness [37]. The Al 2O3 reduces the contact between the 
counterpart and the matrix, thus prevented further peeling off of the matrix and im proves the

wear resistance. The (Ni , Al) intermetallic compound was also found on the worn surfaces, 
which can also offer high hardness and enhance the wear resistance of the steel. 
Further increasing the temperature softens the microstructure and decreases the strength of 
the C 3 steel, resulting in an increase of the wear rate. At 600 and 800 ºC, (Fe, Mn) 2O3 and 
Fe3O4 were formed. These oxides have low hardness and poor wear resistance. The higher the 
testing temperature, the more iron oxides were formed and peeled off, leading to the increase of 
the wear rates. 
The formation of oxides at various temperatures can be explained by Ellingham diagram 
and Gibbs free energy. The position of the reaction line on the diagram shows the stability of 
the oxide as a function of temperature. In the alloy compositions, Al is closer to the bottom of 
the diagram than Mn and Fe, so it is more active and its oxide (Al 2O3) is more stable. The 
Gibbs free energy (∆ G) presents the thermodynamic driving force that makes a reaction occur. 
The lower ∆ G of the oxides (Al 2O3<Mn2O3<Fe3O4<Fe2O3) tends to formed at lower 
temperatures. To sum up, the effect of the surface oxides on the wear resistance and the 
lubricating behavior are mainly determined by the types of oxides. Owing to lubrication of 
graphite and stable surface oxides, t he COF of the alloy ha s no noticeable change from 400 to 
600 ºC , the wear loss remains in the magnitude of 10 -6 mm3/N·m. Furthermore, the density of 
the C3 alloy is 6.49 g/cm 3, lower than most of self -lubricating materials due to the addition of C 
and Al. Therefore, the materials can be promising in applications in high temperature and high 
load areas, for example, the braking systems and heavily sealing systems. 
5. Conclusions 
(1) Fe-16.4Mn-4.8Ni-9.9Al-xC self-lubricating steels containing graphite have been 
successfully designed and fabricated. 
(2) The phase compositions of Fe-16.4Mn-4.8Ni-9.9Al-xC steel are of FCC, BCC, Carbide and 
graphite. The presence of graphite in the microstruc ture is due to the rapid cooling of SPS 
process, and the supersaturated C in the matrix. 
(3) The Fe-16.4Mn-4.8Ni-9.9Al-3C steel possesses high hardness of 621 HV, high yield 
strength of 1437 MPa and good fracture toughness at room temperature. The yield strengths are 
still above 600 MPa at 600 °C. The good mechanical properties are derived from the 
Fe-Mn-Ni-Al matrix. The me chanical properties are better than most reported HEA 
self-lubricating materials.

matrix. The COFs are stable at 0.4, and the wear rates are in the order of 10 -6 mm3/N·m at 
elevated temperatures. The improvement in wear resistance comparing to room temperature is
