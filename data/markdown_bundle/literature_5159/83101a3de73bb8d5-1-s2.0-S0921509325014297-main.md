ABSTRACT
Balancing lightweight, high strength, and good ductility is a major challenge for structural metals. In this study, 
we applied a series of mechanical-thermal treatments to a new Ti-V-Al-Zr-Nb lightweight refractory complex 
concentrated alloy (LRCCA) and systematically examined its microstructure and mechanical properties. The 
introduction of the precipitates effectively blocks dislocation movement, causing dislocations to pile up at phase 
interfaces, and thus increasing alloy ’ s strength. Meanwhile, the spacing between precipitates allows some dis -
locations to keep moving, which reduces stress concentration and helps maintain good ductility. By introducing 
these precipitates, the alloy achieves a high yield strength of 1100 MPa while still reaching ~15 % fracture 
strain. This balance between strength and ductility outperforms many reported RCCAs. The precipitates also 
enhance work hardening and promote more uniform deformation. Our findings offer a practical way to develop 
lightweight, strong, and ductile alloys for advanced applications.
1. Introduction
Metal structural materials are the cornerstone of modern industries 
such as aviation, aerospace, automobile manufacturing and energy 
production. The rapid development of modern industry requires more 
and more stringent performance of metal structural materials, and the 
traditional alloy design method is gradually difficult to meet the needs of 
its use. In 2004, Yeh et al. [ 1 ] and Cantor et al. [ 2 ] creatively proposed 
an alloy design strategy based on multiple principal metal elements with 
equal or nearly equal atomic ratio, which broadened the idea of new 
material design. In recent years, in the process of exploring the wide 
compositional space of complex concentrated alloys (CCAs) and opti -
mizing their mechanical properties, a class of CCAs with BCC structures 
composed of refractory elements namely refractory CCAs (RCCAs) has 
emerged [ 3 – 6 ]. These RCCAs have attracted much attention due to their 
outstanding phase stability, excellent mechanical strength and good 
heat softening resistance, and even demonstrate high-temperature 
strength superior to that of conventional Ni-based superalloys [ 7 , 8 ]. 
Unfortunately, most of the reported RCCAs usually have high densities 
( > 10 g/cm

which greatly limit their further engineering applications.

with low density and good ductility at room temperature, and one of the 
methods is to add lightweight elements (e.g., Al, Ti, etc.) to RCCAs 
[ 9 – 14 ]. Through rational compositional design and thermo-mechanical

4. Discussion
4.1. Formation of deformation bands and their contribution to ductility
The EBSD fracture analysis ( Fig. 9 a – g) reveals abundant slip traces 
and kink bands in the Re alloy ’ s fracture surface. During initial tensile 
deformation, partial dislocation sources become activated, generating a 
small number of dislocations. As strain accumulates, continuous dislo -
cation multiplication and pile-up form dense dislocation networks. 
These dislocations undergo collective motion and interactions, ulti -
mately developing into slip bands with significantly higher dislocation 
density than surrounding areas ( Fig. 9 ). And the multicomponent nature 
of the studied alloys introduces substantial lattice distortion due to the 
diverse principal elements with high concentrations and large atomic 
size mismatches. This strongly impedes dislocation slip, causing pile-ups 
on {110} and/or {112} slip systems. The resulting local stress fields 
drive screw dislocations to rearrange through cross-slip, forming dislo -
cation walls. With progressive deformation, these walls evolve into well- 
defined substructures, forcing the lattice to undergo shear rotations of

During tensile deformation, the {112} < 111 > slip systems are prefer -

resulting in a characteristic 45

sile direction. Under high stress conditions, positive-negative disloca -
tion pairs with [ 111] slip vectors align along two parallel planes 
perpendicular to the (1-12) slip plane, effectively minimizing strain and 
alleviating stress concentration. The applied stress then forces rotation 
of these dislocation planes, causing localized bending of slip planes. This 
rotation terminates when encountering defects or other planar disloca -
tions, forming stable kink band boundaries. In fact, this deformation 
mechanism represents the alloy ’ s self-adaptive response to 
accommodate heterogeneous plastic strain, where localized lattice 
rotation serves to dissipate accumulated strain energy.

This increases dislocation storage density, allowing the alloy to maintain 
high strength while achieving better strength-ductility balance. Addi -

generating numerous kink bands to mitigate strain localization. These 
kink boundaries further absorb excess dislocations, slowing down 
dislocation multiplication and improving strain accommodation capac -

uously adapt by nucleating new kink bands to redistribute strain away 
from crack tips, significantly enhancing fracture resistance [ 51 ]. From a 
microstructural heterogeneity perspective, grains with kink bands can 
be considered “ hard domains ” , while those without act as “ soft do -
mains ” . The differing plastic deformability between these domains may 
introduce additional hetero-deformation induced (HDI) strengthening 
[ 50 , 52 ]. A schematic illustration of kink band formation and its mech -

collectively lead to the predominant precipitation of Ti-Al phases while 
preventing Al-Zr phase formation. The Ti-Al precipitation is primarily 
driven by three synergistic factors, (1) strongly negative mixing 
enthalpy, (2) rapid Al diffusion kinetics, and (3) high Ti concentration, 
which together facilitate the formation of thermodynamically stable Ti- 
Al phases.

5. Conclusions
In summary, in this work, we achieved an optimal strength-ductility 
balance in the Ti
66.8
V
11.2
Al
9.6
Zr
6.2
Nb
6.2 
LRCCA through a series of 
thermo-mechanical treatments. Simultaneously, we systematically 
investigated the alloy ’ s microstructural evolution, mechanical proper -
ties, deformation behavior, and strengthening mechanisms. Based on 
our research findings and analysis, the following conclusions can be 
drawn. 
(1) Peak aging treatment increased the alloy ’ s strength from 1000 
MPa to 1100 MPa, a 10 % improvement, while maintaining a 
fracture strain of ~15 %, achieving an excellent strength-ductility 
trade-off.
(2) The introduction of precipitates enhanced the alloy ’ s work 
hardening capability and uniform deformation ability. These 
precipitates strongly impede dislocation motion, thereby 
improving strength. Meanwhile, the inter-precipitate spacing 
serves as channels for dislocation movement, effectively relieving 
stress concentration at BCC/HCP interfaces and maintaining good 
ductility. However, due to their relatively large size (micrometer- 
scale), the precipitates ’ contribution to strength remains limited, 
making solid solution strengthening the dominant strengthening 
mechanism.
(3) Although Al-Zr pairs have the most negative mixing enthalpy, 
compositional and kinetic constraints lead to Ti-Al phase domi -
nated precipitation while preventing Al-Zr phase formation. The 
Ti-Al precipitation is primarily driven by three factors, strongly 
negative mixing enthalpy + rapid Al diffusion + high Ti con -
centration, resulting in stable Ti-Al phase formation.
