Oxidation resistance: One barrier to moving beyond Ni-base superalloys
B.A. Pint∗, J.R. DiStefano, I.G. Wright
Metals and Ceramics Division, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak Ridge, TN 37831-6156, USA
Received in revised form 19 September 2005; accepted 19 September 2005
Abstract
The implementation of new high-temperature materials is often hampered by their lack of oxidation or environmental resistance. This failing is
one of the strongest barriers to moving beyond Ni-base superalloys for many commercial applications. In practice, usable high-temperature alloys
have at least reasonable oxidation resistance, but the current generation of single-crystal Ni-base superalloys has sufﬁcient oxidation resistance that
optimized versions can be used without a metallic bond coating and only an oxygen-transparent ceramic coating for thermal protection. The material
development process often centers around mechanical properties, while oxidation resistance, along with other realities, is given minor attention .
For many applications, the assumption that an oxidation-resistant coating can be used to protect a substrate is seriously ﬂawed, as coatings often
do not provide sufﬁcient reliability for critical components. Examples of oxidation problems are given for currently used materials and materials
classes with critical oxidation resistance problems.

1. Introduction
The development of new and better high-temperature alloys
has been an important topic in materials science for more
than 50 years. One driving force has been the demanding per-
formance needs of turbine engines for aircraft [1]. However,
while the maximum-temperature capability has risen signiﬁ-
cantly over this time period, recent progress in this area has
been limited. The increase in turbine inlet temperature over the
past 15 years has been accomplished mainly by the advent of
internally cooled components and thermal barrier coatings with
only a minor increase in the temperature capabilities of Ni-
base superalloys [1]. It is widely recognized that superalloys
have nearly reached their maximum-use temperatures, which
are limited by their solidus (melting) temperatures ( Fig. 1 a).
Therefore, there is a great deal of interest in other systems that
have higher melting points, particularly refractory metals and
precious metals. The purpose of this paper is to discuss the
critical importance of oxidation resistance in the development
of high-temperature alloys, including the practical implications
of optimized second-generation Ni-base superalloys, which are
sufﬁciently oxidation-resistant to be able to operate with only
a ceramic thermal protection coating [3]. Oxidation resistance

3. Deﬁning high-temperature oxidation resistance
To discuss high-temperature oxidation resistance, it is ﬁrst
necessary to brieﬂy deﬁne this concept (of course, a more exten-
sive treatment can be found in several text books[6–8]). “High”
is a relative term, but for oxidation resistance, a good deﬁni-
tion is over 550
◦C. Below this temperature, iron forms a fairly
adherent surface oxide, but above this temperature, FeO forms
and the oxidation rate increases rapidly. Oxidation is used as a
general reaction term since in most cases oxygen is the most
abundant oxidant and, therefore, is most widely discussed. Sim-
ilar concepts apply for sulfur, nitrogen and other such species.
Since most oxides are more thermodynamically stable than met-
als, the issue is notif oxidation will occur, but the kinetics of the
reaction (deep space is one of the few places, where oxidation is
not an issue; however, even Mars has an oxidizing atmosphere
of ∼600 Pa (6 mbar) of mostly CO
2 [9]).
There is no practical strategy for preventing high-temperature
oxidation. In the best case, so-called oxygen barriers still allow
some ﬂux of oxygen to diffuse and to react with the substrate,
and in realistic cases, they eventually crack or otherwise degrade,
allowing oxygen ingress. Therefore, the only practical long-term

4. The limitation of coatings
Often the implicit suggestion with materials development
is that a new high strength material can be coated with an
oxidation-resistant material for applications at high tempera-
ture. Even more fanciful is the suggestion that the coating will
be a barrier to oxidation (it does need to be sufﬁciently dense
to prevent direct oxygen ingress to the substrate). As discussed
above, high-temperature oxidation is minimized and controlled,
not prevented. Coatings are not, and have never been, prime
reliant. This means that the system is not solely reliant on coat-
ing performance to reach its service life. Critical components
must perform reasonably without their coatings. Therefore, a
material that would undergo rapid or catastrophic oxidation if
the oxidation-resistant coating failed could not be used in most
commercial applications. Rocket engines, where the mission is
short and human life is not necessarily at risk, are one exception.
Even if an oxidation-resistant coating were sufﬁciently reli-
able, fabricable and inexpensive, there are several fundamental
issues to consider: (1) there must be a good thermal expansion
match between coating and substrate, otherwise cracking [34]
or other degradation could occur. (2) There must be good chem-
ical compatibility between the coating and substrate, otherwise
undesirable phases could form. (3) From a mechanical proper-
ties standpoint, most oxidation-resistant coating compositions
have relatively low high-temperature strength, and therefore, can
have problems with fatigue or deformation during service that
could limit their durability. (4) Finally, there can be a long-term
problem of interdiffusion between the coating and substrate, par-
ticularly the loss of the protective scale-forming element to the
substrate. As with bulk materials, if the coating content of Al, Cr
or Si drops below the critical level, the coating will cease to be
protective. This is an acute problem for the long-term durabil-
ity of aluminide coatings at high temperatures due to the rapid
diffusion of Al in most Fe- and Ni-base alloys.

5. Examples of the important role of oxidation
resistance
A few examples are provided to illustrate the importance
of oxidation resistance in materials for high-temperature appli-
cations. With developmental materials, or those with limited
commercial attention, it is sometimes difﬁcult to obtain com-
plete information and references.
5.1. Second-generation Ni-base single-crystal superalloys
Typically, superalloys in the hottest section of a turbine are
protected by thermal barrier coatings (TBCs) consisting of an
inner metallic or “bond” coating rich in aluminum for oxida-
tion resistance and a ceramic top coating with a low thermal
conductivity (e.g., yttria-stabilized zirconia), which provides
no oxidation resistance itself, as it is essentially transparent to
oxygen [45]. The second generation of single-crystal superal-
loys (with ≈7 at.% Cr and 13 at.% Al) exhibits good uncoated
oxidation resistance. Examples are alloys, such as Ren ´e N5,
PW A1484 and CMSX-4. Issues, such as reactive element addi-
tions (Hf and Y), and sulfur and carbon impurities [18,46,47],
have been intensively studied and resulted in nearly optimal
behavior for these NiCrAl-type alloys[16]. The oxidation resis-
tance of some optimized alloys in this class (e.g., PW A1487
and Ren ´e N5+, both containing Y) is good enough that they
can be directly coated with the ceramic layer and do not even
require oxidation-resistant bond coatings in jet engine service
[3,48]. Recent papers in the literature describe experiments with
no bond coat TBC systems [49,50] and trade publications and
company presentations allude to the commercial use of such
systems [48,51]. This is truly an exceptional development for a
creep-resistant superalloy to be sufﬁciently oxidation-resistant
to perform without an oxidation-resistant metallic coating. A
turbine blade without a bond coating presumably would have
lower fabrication costs, less weight and avoid problems associ-
ated with fatigue or deformation of the weaker metallic coating
[52].
5.2. Subsequent generation Ni-base single-crystal
superalloys
The third generation of single-crystal superalloys (e.g., Ren´e
N6 and CMSX-10) contain 6 wt.% Re, compared to 3% in the
second generation. However, the concurrent reduction in the Cr
and Al contents has resulted in a major decrease in the oxidation
resistance of this class of alloys [53], even when coated [44].
Previous suggestions that Re was beneﬁcial to oxidation resis-
tance have not proven correct, and actually, the opposite is true
(e.g., Fig. 6). It is likely that the oxidation beneﬁt attributed to
adding Re in the second-generation superalloys had to do with
the fact that Re replaced elements (such as Ta and Ti) that were
even more detrimental to oxidation resistance.
Fourth-generation superalloys with Re and Ru additions face
equal or greater problems, with both elements being potentially
detrimental to oxidation resistance (e.g., Fig. 6). Initial coating
problems with this next generation of single-crystal superalloys
(sometimes denoted MX) may lead to reformulations. However,
little public information is available.

to the presence of water vapor. Whereas ceramics are gener-
ally thought to be oxidation-resistant, critical problems with
silica volatilization have been observed for Si-based ceramics

[54–57]. These materials have been considered for combustor
liners and hot section turbine components for many years. How-
ever, when advanced processing and compositing techniques
improved mechanical properties to the point where engine test-
ing was warranted, recession problems were encountered due
to volatilization in the presence of high water vapor pressures
[58,59]. This problem has proven to be a signiﬁcant deterrent
to the use of these materials in combustion environments. A
new class of environmental barrier coatings (EBCs) has been
developed in an attempt to reduce this problem by inhibiting the
interaction of water vapor with the silica scale[60]. While some
EBCs have been effective, they have the same basic problems
of any coating; thus, maintaining the integrity of these ceramic
materials represents a signiﬁcant problem for moving parts or
any application where the EBC may crack under tensile loading
and allow water vapor to reach the underlying material.
5.4. Refractory metals
Perhaps, the holy grail for developing high-temperature mate-
rials is improving the extremely poor high-temperature oxida-
tion resistance of refractory metals to enable these high melting
temperature materials with relatively good high-temperature
strength to be used in a wider range of applications. A great
deal of research currently is being directed to this problem
[33,61–64], but much larger programs in the 1950s and 1960s
were unable to make much progress [65]. The oxidation behav-
ior of refractory metals and their alloys is a complex function of
temperature and pressure [7]. However, the oxidation problems
fall into two fundamentally different categories. The ﬁrst is par-
ticular to those metals with high oxygen solubility, such as Nb,
Ta and V . For these refractory metal alloys, determining a reac-
tion rate does not sufﬁciently describe their oxidation behavior.

phases (e.g., NbAl
3 and MoSi2) are formed [72,73]. However,
recent progress has been made on the development of oxidation-
resistant Mo–Si–B alloys [61,62,64]. The addition of low levels
of B and Si allow the formation of a boro-silicate scale, which
can be protective in certain temperature ranges. However, the
stability of this type of scale will be questionable in combus-
tion gases (Table 2). Further breakthroughs will be required to
develop these materials to the point, where they can be consid-
ered for commercial applications.
5.5. Precious metal-based alloys
Brieﬂy, if the cost and availability of Pt, Rh or Ir were
not a sufﬁcient deterrent, the problem of evaporation of their
oxides [74,75] in ﬂowing gas environments should make the
prospects for this class of alloy very limited. As with refrac-
tory metal alloys, the addition of sufﬁcient Al, Cr or Si to form
protective scales results in the formation of brittle intermetallic
phases.
6. Summary
For high temperature, commercial applications, oxidation
issues become increasingly important in the development of
durable high strength materials. The literature is full of exam-
ples where oxidation resistance issues were largely ignored,

and materials were investigated, and even developed, with little
chance of commercial success due to unsolvable durability
problems. The inferred strategy of protecting new alloys with
oxidation-resistant coatings is unlikely to be successful for most
long-term commercial applications, which make up the bulk of
the need. More emphasis is needed on oxidation resistance early
in the development process in order to focus resources on the
most attractive candidates. Fundamental studies on the oxidation
behavior of refractory metal alloys are needed to complement
the other development work in this area.
