ABSTRACT
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), as an emerging advanced metal manufacturing technology, alters the micro -
structural characteristics of Mg alloys through its unique non-equilibrium solidification effects, thereby influ -
encing their mechanical responses, especially at high temperatures. In this context, this study characterizes the 
microstructure and temperature-dependent quasi-static tensile behavior of laser powder bed fused (LPBFed) 
WE43 alloy. The inherent remelting and thermal cycling processes of LPBF lead to the in-situ precipitation of β ′ , 
β
1
, and β phases, as well as the formation of a bimodal grain structure, resulting in unique high-temperature 
mechanical properties. High-temperature tensile tests show that the LPBFed WE43 alloy maintains good 
strength at 200 – 250
◦
C, but its tensile strength significantly decreases at 300
◦
C. Additionally, the LPBFed WE43 
alloy exhibits anomalous elongation at 250
◦
C. EBSD technology was used to systematically further reveal the 
unique deformation mechanisms of the LPBFed WE43 alloy. The results indicate that the presence of various 
dispersed in-situ precipitates, which act as nucleation sites for recrystallization, leads to recrystallization being 
the primary deformation mode of the LPBFed WE43 alloy during high-temperature tensile testing at 200 – 300
◦
C. 
Simultaneously, the existence of these dispersed precipitates also restricts the formation of twins. During this 
process, both discontinuous dynamic recrystallization (DDRX) and continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX) 
occur simultaneously, with DDRX becoming dominant as the temperature increases. Moreover, significant stress 
concentration is observed only at 250
◦
C, which is responsible for the anomalous elongation and the activation of 
pyramidal II < c + a > slip. At 300
◦
C, the grain boundary strength of fine grains significantly decreases, leading to 
pronounced grain boundary sliding (GBS). GBS greatly alleviates stress concentration but also significantly re -
duces strength. Different deformation mechanisms ultimately lead to different failure behaviors of LPBFed WE43 
alloy. At 200
◦
C, failure predominantly originates from microcrack propagation induced by elemental segregation 
at melt pool boundaries. At this critical temperature of 250
◦
C, the insufficient activation of DRX behavior and 
GBS creates a ’ mechanistic gap ’ , resulting in untimely strain accommodation near oxides. When the temperature 
reaches 300
◦
C, a fundamental transition in crack propagation mode occurs due to significantly enhanced dy -
namic recrystallization processes, with intergranular cracking becoming the predominant failure mechanism. 
This work reveals the temperature-dependent deformation and fracture behaviors of the LPBFed WE43 alloy and 
provides new insights for its application in high-temperature environments.

1. Introduction
Magnesium (Mg) alloys have tremendous potential for lightweight 
applications in aerospace, mechanical transportation, and other in -
dustries due to their low density, high specific strength, and recyclability 
[1] . Moreover, as advances in lightweight design and economic con -
siderations continue to accelerate, a growing number of applications 
(such as, engine housings, gearboxes used in 200 – 300
◦
C) demand 
components that simultaneously fulfill stringent requirements for 
reduced mass and high-temperature strength [2] . However, the poor 
high-temperature performance of Mg alloys has limited their utilization 
in high-temperature environments [3] . To address this limitation, re -
searchers have developed various high-strength Mg alloys and 
manufacturing processes [4] . The advancement of additive 
manufacturing technologies, particularly laser powder bed fusion 
(LPBF), has enabled the progressive development of high-performance 
geometrically complex magnesium alloy components through its 
unique manufacturing mechanisms characterized by rapid solidification 
and layer-by-layer deposition [5,6] .
The LPBF technology, characterized by extremely rapid cooling 
rates, results in complex non-equilibrium solidification effects, causing 
laser powder bed fused (LPBFed) alloys to deviate from equilibrium 
solidification conditions. However, the layer-by-layer processing 
approach of LPBF, coupled with localized heating, cooling, and thermal 
cycling, produce unique microstructures and mechanical properties that 
significantly differ from the counterparts fabricated via traditional 
manufacturing methods [7,8] . For example, Deng et al. [9] created a fine 
equiaxed structure in a laser powder bed fused (LPBFed) 
Mg-10Gd-3Y-1Zn-0.4Zr alloy with an average grain size of only 
4.1 ± 0.5 µ m. Esmaily et al. [10] further observed a strong basal texture 
in LPBFed Mg alloys, with most c-axes of grains nearly parallel to the 
building direction (BD). Sun et al. [11] reported that the high thermal 
stresses generated during the LPBF process led to a high dislocation 
density, contributing to the increased alloy strength. Furthermore, B ¨ar 
et al. [7] discovered various types of segregation behaviors and in-situ 
precipitation phases in the LPBFed WE43 alloy. These findings high -
light the potential of LPBF technology to produce alloys with unique 
microstructures and superior mechanical properties compared to their 
traditionally fabricated counterparts.
However, some researchers have underscored that segregation, fine 
grains, dislocation pile-up, and thermal stress concentration induced by 
additive manufacturing can not only lead to unexpected failure behav -
iors but also exacerbate the softening behavior at high temperatures
[12 – 14] . Several researchers have investigated the high-temperature 
mechanical behavior of LPBFed alloys to lay the foundation for prac -
tical high-temperature applications. Plotkowski et al. [15] observed that 
melt pool boundaries in LPBFed Al-Ce-Mn alloys acted as metallurgical 
notches, localizing strain and significantly reducing the 
high-temperature creep life. Xi et al. [16] discovered that uneven 
elemental segregation caused by LPBF led to a bimodal size distribution 
of carbides in the LPBFed GH3230 superalloy, resulting in the reduced 
ductility. Targeted heat treatment was utilized to adjust the micro -
structure of GH3230 alloy, thereby successfully improving both strength 
and ductility. In-situ studies have provided valuable insights into the 
deformation mechanisms of LPBFed alloys. Wang et al. [17] used in-situ 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to investigate the high-temperature 
fatigue behavior of additively manufactured Al-Si10 Mg alloys, identi -
fying lack of fusion regions as primary contributors to crack propaga -
tion. Jiang et al. [18] employed in-situ electron backscattered 
diffraction (EBSD) technology to examine the LPBFed Al-Mn-Sc alloy, 
uncovering the effects of grain orientation, grain size, and aspect ratio 
on grain rotation behavior. Geng et al. [19] investigated the tensile 
behavior of 5183 Al alloy under various strain rates and temperatures. 
They found that dynamic recovery dominated the deformation at in -
termediate temperatures (20
◦
C~200
◦
C), while DRX prevailed at higher 
temperatures (300
◦
C~500
◦
C). These findings not only reveal the unique 
deformation and failure behaviors of additively manufactured 
high-performance alloys but also provide a valuable practical founda -
tion for their practical applications. Additionally, they offer valuable 
theoretical insights for further guiding improvements in additive 
manufacturing processes. In recent years, LPBF has been successfully 
employed to fabricate high-strength Mg alloys, including 
Mg-10Gd-3Y-1Zn-0.4Zr [9] , Mg-Gd-Zr [20] , WE43 [7,10,21,22] , and 
Mg-11Gd-2Zn-0.4Zr [23] alloys. While extensive research has focused 
on room-temperature properties and microstructure of additively man -
ufactured Mg alloys, significant gaps remain in understanding their 
high-temperature behavior, particularly thermal stability, deformation 
mechanisms, and fracture resistance under thermomechanical condi -
tions. This research deficiency substantially hinders the reliable 
deployment of LPBFed Mg alloys in demanding applications such as 
aerospace thermal components, automotive powertrain systems, and 
energy conversion devices where service temperatures routinely exceed 
200
◦
C. Accordingly, an in-depth and accurate understanding of the 
deformation mechanisms and failure behavior of LPBFed Mg alloys 
under high-temperature loading conditions will serve as a critical 
foundation for their future applications in high-temperature 
environments.
WE43 alloy is regarded as one of the most successful commercial Mg 
alloys that have been successfully applied in aerospace components due 
to its stable intermetallic phases [24,25] . The addition of rare earth el -
ements enables WE43 alloy in the Mg-Y-Nd system to develop unique 
microstructural characteristics during solidification or deformation 
processes that differ significantly from those observed in conventional 
rare-earth-free magnesium alloys (particularly manifesting as retarda -
tion of dynamic recrystallization, deformation and shear banding, 
twinning, and so on) [26,27] . These structural modifications profoundly 
influence the deformation mechanisms and failure behaviors under 
service conditions. Furthermore, the LPBF introduces additional 
complexity to the microstructural evolution of alloys, creating hierar -
chical architectures that deviate from those formed through conven -
tional processing routes [8] . Recent studies have investigated the unique 
characteristics of WE43 alloys fabricated through LPBF. Yin et al. [28]
reported that the LPBFed WE43 alloy exhibited a distinctive bimodal 
grain structure, attributed to remelting and thermal cycling during the 
LPBF process. Esmaily et al. [10] noted the presence of numerous 
Y-Zr-containing oxides in LPBFed WE43 alloys, while B ¨ar et al. [7]
observed that the consumption of Y by Y-Zr oxides resulted in pre -
cipitates that deviated from those expected based on traditional Mg-Y 
and Mg-Y-Nd phase diagrams. These unique microstructure features, 
induced by the non-equilibrium solidification effects of LPBF, are likely 
to introduce novel mechanisms for high-temperature deformation and 
fracture behavior compared to traditionally manufactured alloys. At 
present, the room temperature performance of Mg alloys produced by 
LPBF has been evaluated [29] , but there are few researchers who have 
revealed their high-temperature performance, deformation, and failure 
behavior in LPBFed Mg alloys, especially for Mg rare earth alloys.
The aim of this study focuses on elucidating the deformation and 
failure mechanisms of LPBFed WE43 alloy under elevated temperature 
conditions. Through systematic high-temperature tensile testing, the 
temperature-dependent evolution of mechanical characteristics was 
quantitatively established. Notably, the LPBFed WE43 alloy exhibited 
distinctive mechanical responses, manifesting as anomalous elongation 
behavior at 250
◦
C followed by significant performance deterioration at 
300
◦
C. To unravel the underlying deformation mechanisms, EBSD 
analysis was meticulously performed on 5 %-strain samples and frac -
tured samples at varied temperatures. Through comprehensive charac -
terization, critical correlations between microstructural evolution and 
macroscopic failure patterns were established, ultimately revealing the 
fundamental origins of the unique thermomechanical behavior in 
LPBFed WE43 alloy. This research not only provides practical guidance 
for optimizing additive manufacturing processes of high-strength heat- 
resistant Mg alloys but also establishes a theoretical basis for enhancing

3. Results and discussions
3.1. Microstructure and mechanical properties of LPBFed WE43 Mg 
alloys
Fig. 2 presents the macro and microstructural morphologies of the as- 
deposited WE43 alloy. The macro-optical image ( Fig. 2 a) reveals typical 
melt pool characteristics and low porosity. Figs. 2 b and 2 c depict the 
microstructural features. BSE images can clearly distinguish the distri -
bution patterns of rare earth (RE) and Mg elements. RE-rich regions 
appear bright and are predominantly located within the melt pools and 
at grain boundaries, while Mg-rich regions appear dark and are mainly 
found at the melt pool boundaries and within the star-shaped areas. 
Also, larger RE-rich precipitates and oxides are distributed in the as- 
deposited WE43 alloy. TEM analyses ( Figs. 2 d- 2 h) provide further in -
sights into the internal morphology of precipitates and corresponding 
diffraction patterns. Figs. 2 d- 2 g reveal the presence of plate-like and 
block-like phases within the as-deposited alloy. Based on the diffraction 
patterns, the block-like β phase (tetragonal, a = b = 1.47 nm, c =
1.04 nm) exhibits a certain alignment along the c-axis, consistent with 
previous studies [7] . The plate-like β
1 
phase (fcc, a = 0.74 nm) is 
distributed among the block-like phase [2,21] . Additionally, the spher -
ical β
1 
phase is also observed at the grain boundaries similar to the 
research investigated by Fang and B ¨ar et al. [7,21] . It is noteworthy that 
the precipitates observed in the as-deposited alloy correspond to char -
acteristic phases of Mg-Y-Nd system alloys at different aging stages [31] . 
This phenomenon is intrinsically associated with the unique 
thermo-kinetic conditions inherent to the LPBF process. The ultrahigh 
solidification rates of LPBF restrict sufficient solute diffusion, resulting 
in the formation of highly supersaturated solid solutions within the α -Mg 
matrix. During layer-by-layer deposition, recurrent thermal cycling ex -
erts in-situ aging effects on previously deposited layers, driving 
continuous precipitation of metastable phases. More significantly, the 
inherent high cooling rates of LPBF introduce substantial residual 
stresses that induce localized dislocation multiplication [32] . These 
high-density dislocations serve dual functions in subsequent thermal 
cycles: acting as accelerated solute diffusion pathways and providing 
heterogeneous nucleation sites, synergistically promoting dynamic 
coarsening and phase transformations of precipitates. Consequently, the 
as-deposited alloy exhibits multiscale precipitate distributions with 
diverse morphologies. A flocculent-like phase rich in Y, Zr, and O ele -
ments is also detected ( Figs. 2 h and 2 i). The matrix exhibits varying 
contrast ( Fig. 2 d), with darker regions characteristics of the β ′ phase [33, 
34] . Both β ′ and β
1 
phases are known to enhance strength in Mg-Y-Nd 
alloys by impeding dislocation movement [31] . Previous research

It has been reported that the DRX is delayed at lower temperatures
[39] . In the as-deposited alloy, in-situ precipitated phases at grain 
boundaries and within grains restrict dislocation movement, making it 
difficult for dislocations generated by deformation to annihilate through 
slip. These effects reduce the dynamic softening effect and increase 
recrystallization nucleation sites, leading to grain refinement. Higher 
temperatures increase grain boundary energy, promote dislocation 
movement, weaken the work-hardening ability, and accelerate grain 
boundary migration, resulting in slightly larger fine grain sizes. The 
texture intensity decreases gradually at 200

temperatures. During deformation, Mg alloys typically coordinate strain 
through DRX, twinning, and GBS [40 – 42] . In the traditional casting 
process, both Han et al. [40] and Xu et al. [43] found that twinning and 
DRX are the primary deformation mechanisms during high-temperature 
deformation in Mg alloys. In this study, however, almost no twinning 
was observed. The formation of precipitates can significantly increase 
the critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) for twinning, thereby inhibiting 
twinning formation [44] . In addition, the precipitates can serve as 
nucleation sites for recrystallization, thereby alleviating stress concen -
tration during the deformation process, which in turn suppresses the 
formation of twinning. This phenomenon is common in Mg-RE alloys 
[45,46] . At 300

become the new deformation coordination mechanism between fine

Schmid factor (SF) is a crucial parameter describing the deformation 
mechanism under stress and is generally related to grain orientation and 
the stress direction [49] . The SF can be calculated using Eq.1 : 
SF = cos φ cos λ (1)
where φ is the angle between the sliding plane and the applied stress 
direction, and λ is the angle between the sliding direction and the 
applied stress direction. When the applied stress ( σ ) satisfies the con -
dition of Eq.2, a specific slip system can be activated. 
σ = CRSS / SF (2) 
For Mg alloys, the CRSS for basal slip is significantly lower than that 
for non-basal slip at room temperature, making basal slip the primary 
deformation mode. However, Srinivasarao et al. [54] indicated that 
higher temperatures decrease the CRSS for non-basal slip, resulting in 
the increased activity of non-basal slip. The SF is a crucial parameter 
that reflects the ease of slip system activation, with higher values indi -
cating a greater likelihood of slip system initiation [55] . To elucidate the 
evolution of slip systems during deformation, the SF for each sample was 
statistically analyzed following the method processed by Yuan et al. 
[56] . In this method, we quantified the SF of different slip systems for 
each grain in various samples. These were represented using different 
colors based on the maximum SF, serving as an indicator of their 
preferred deformation mode ( Fig. 10 ). This work mainly considered 
common slip systems in Mg alloys, including basal < a > slip, prismatic 
< a > slip, pyramidal I < a > slip, and pyramidal II < c + a > slip [49,57] .
