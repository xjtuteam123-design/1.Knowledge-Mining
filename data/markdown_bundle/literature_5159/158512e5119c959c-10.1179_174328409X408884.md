Influence of carbides on the strength and
toughness of a niobium alloy
R. Ding * and I. P. Jones
The mechanical behaviour of Nb35Ti6Al5Cr8V (at.-%) containing different C contents (0, 1 and
10 at.-%) has been investigated. The addition of 1 at.-%C does not increase the strength at room
or high temperature, whereas adding 10 at.-%C improves significantly the strength at both room
and high temperature. Adding 1 at.-%C slightly increases the fracture toughness; aging the same
alloy for 9 days also results in a slight improvement in the toughness. A 10 at.-%C addition,
however, leads to a dramatic decrease in the toughness. The variation in the toughness is linearly
related to the proportion of cleavage as modified by the carbide.
Keywords: Nb–Ti–Al alloy, Carbon, Strength, Fracture toughness
Introduction
The continual drive to increase the efﬁciency and to
reduce the emissions associated with the next generation
of areoengines demands the use of superior grades of
heat resistant alloys for higher service temperatures.
Currently, Ni based superalloys are mature technology
and enjoy widespread use in jet and turbine engines.
However, their operation temperature, 1423 K, is 90%
of their melting point, which is a limitation for their
further use at extreme high temperatures in the next
generation of engines. Several structural materials, such
as intermetallics, refractory alloys, ceramics and C/C
composites, have been suggested over the past decade as
replacements for Ni based superalloys. Among them,
Nb based alloys have been studied widely, because,
although these alloys need to be coated, Nb has a
high melting point (2741 K) and a relatively low density,
as well as advantages in respect of alloying and
manufacturing.
The strength of Nb decreases substantially at tem-
peratures above 1200 K but may be improved by
various approaches, including solid solution strengthen-
ing with Mo, Hf, Ta and W,
1–3 and dispersion or
composite strengthening with intermetallic compounds
such as Nb
3Al and Nb 5Si,4,5 as well as with carbide. 6–9
Another major problem in the development of Nb based
superalloys is their low ductility at room temperature. A
recently developed b-Nb–Ti–Al based alloy shows good
ductility at room temperature and reasonable strength at
high temperature.
10 The alloy is only solid solution
strengthened and therefore, there appears to be a good
possibility of strengthening it further by introducing
precipitates or secondary particles at the expense of
losing some room temperature ductility.
As a potential structural material designed for service
at elevated temperature, it has not only a good high
temperature strength but a reasonable room tempera-
ture toughness. The present study, therefore, evaluates
the high temperature strengths and room temperature
fracture toughnesses of Nb–Ti–Al alloys with different
C contents.

Results and analysis
Microstructures
The microstructures of the Nb based alloys with
different C contents are shown in Fig. 1. To understand
the effect of forging plus annealing on the microstruc-
tures of the alloys, as cast microstructures are also
presented in Fig. 1. The backscattered electron images
of the 0C alloy indicate that light elements segregate to
the interdendritic regions in the as cast condition,
whereas after forging plus annealing such segregation
was eliminated (Fig. 1 a and d). Figure 1 b and e shows
that after forging and annealing, the distribution of
carbides in the 1C alloy is more homogeneous and that
the needle like carbides have been broken into several
smaller particles. After forging plus annealing, the
amount of dendritic carbide in the 10C alloy is decreased
and the precipitation of grain boundary carbides
becomes more obvious (Fig. 1 c and f). Two types of
carbides (primary and secondary) were observed in the
10C alloy, whereas only small (secondary) carbides were
formed in the 1C alloy. TEM study showed that in the as

(Ti,Nb)

Mechanical response at room temperature
Table 1 shows the hardness and yield stress of the

the high volume fraction of carbide in the 10C alloy.
Figure 3 shows an indentation in the 10C alloy. The
matrix Nbss and the primary carbide show different
behaviours during penetration of the hardness indenter.
As seen in Fig. 3 b, in which a tip of the indentation
locates accurately on a carbide, cracks initiate within the
carbide (arrow in Fig. 3 b). In the case of the indentation

within the Nbss area. However, in the carbide phases
around the indentation, cracks have been produced

carbide is brittle, whereas the Nbss deforms plastically at
room temperature. Tensile testing demonstrated that the

analyse the contributions to strengthening of several
mechanisms deriving from the addition of carbide.

Fracture toughness
Two failure modes predominated in all the tests: ductile
dimple and cleavage. The former represents a classical,
relatively high energy failure mode; cleavage is a low
energy failure mode. Fractographic examination reveals
that the proportion of dimple/cleavage differs between
the different alloys. Also, the addition of C affects the
fracture toughness of the alloys. The addition of
1 at.-%C slightly improves the toughness, which could
be contributed by the small carbides ( ,1 mm) in the 1C
alloy providing sites for void nucleation and therefore
decreasing the number of cleavage facets and improving
the proportion of dimples. Aging for 9 days led to a
slight increase in the toughness. This could be attributed
to aging making the carbide distribution uniform,
decreasing the amount of banded carbides and thus
inhibiting the formation of cleavage strips. It is
noteworthy that secondary cracks were observed in the
1C and aged 1C alloys (Fig. 7 a). The formation of
secondary cracks could improve the toughness. Kim
et al.
14 observed that the addition of suitable amounts of
carbide ( ,5%) improved the toughness of Nb–5Mo–
15W. They found that the presence of carbide particles
hinders straight crack propagation and eventually leads
to crack deﬂection and branching, and thus improves
the fracture toughness.
The addition of 10 at.-%C resulted in the formation of
many primary carbides ( y12 vol.-%). Such large
carbides promote brittle fracture (Fig. 8 c). In other
words, large carbides provide many available sites for
crack initiation and propagation. It should be noted that
the high strength of the 10C alloy will reduce the size of
the plastic zone at a crack tip, thereby enhancing
cleavage fracture and leading to a decrease in fracture
toughness. Thus, the 10C alloy displays the lowest
toughness. The presence of large carbides is known to
have a detrimental effect on the fracture properties of
alloys. Some models

Implications for optimum C content
The mechanical properties of the alloys as a function of
C content are shown in Fig. 14, which shows that adding
1 at.-%C does not improve the strength, slightly impairs
the ductility, but increases the toughness; a 10 at.-%C
addition dramatically decreases the ductility and the
toughness, but signiﬁcantly improves the strength. These
mean that there is an optimum C content, which
represents a compromise between strength and ductility,
depending on the exact application. This value will
probably lie between 3 and 5 at.-%.
A signiﬁcant improvement to these alloys would
probably result from decreasing the size of the carbides.

Conclusions
After forging plus annealing, the segregation of light
elements to the interdendritic region was eliminated. The
distribution of carbides in the 1C alloy is more
homogeneous and the needle like carbides were broken
into several smaller particles. The amount of dendritic
carbide in the 10C alloy is decreased.
The addition of 1 at.-%C does not increase the
strength at room and high temperature due to the low
volume fraction and the relatively large size of the
carbides, whereas adding 10 at.-%C clearly improves
the strength at both room and high temperatures. The
increase in room temperature strength induced by
adding 10 at.-%C results from load transfer from the
matrix to the carbide.
Adding 1 at.-%C slightly increases the toughness,
which could be contributed by small carbides ( ,1 mm)
in the 1C alloy providing sites for void nucleation and
therefore decreasing the number of shear regions and
improving the proportion of dimples. Aging for 9 days
resulted in a slight improvement in the toughness
because aging made the carbide distribution uniform
and decreased the number of banded carbides, thus
inhibiting the formation of shear strips. A 10 at.-%C
addition, however, led to the formation of many large
brittle primary carbides, thus dramatically decreasing
the toughness.
