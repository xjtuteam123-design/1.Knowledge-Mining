ABSTRACT 
TaW refractory alloys with excellent high-temperature mechanical behaviors up to 2000 ℃ have been intensively 
researched recently. Such performances herald great potential in industrial applications. In this study, we 
manufactured a Ta10W refractory alloy using selective electron beam melting with elemental TaW powder. The 
as-received microstructure shows high chemical homogeneity, and the obtained hardness values are 295 ± 3 HV 
and 293 ± 3 HV for XOY and XOZ planes, respectively. The yield strength of the compression tests can reach 664 
± 8 MPa. The yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and strain after fracturing of samples normal to the build 
direction of the as-deposited Ta10W refractory alloy are 750 ± 5 MPa, 764 ± 4 MPa, and 6.05 ± 2.34, respectively. 
This study comprehensively investigated the microstructure and mechanical behaviors of the additively manu -
factured Ta10W refractory alloy, which can provide considerable insight into additive manufacturing of high- 
melting point refractory alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Refractory metals, such as Tungsten, Tantalum, Molybdenum, and 
Niobium are widely applied in the aerospace industry [1,2] . Because of 
their excellent high-temperature mechanical behaviors, refractory al -
loys gain more attention in the research field [3,4] . In these commonly 
seen refractory metals, tantalum with good ductility, corrosion resis -
tance, and considerable biocompatibility attracts researchers ’ notice. 
Moreover, the 2996 ℃ melting point makes tantalum a significant 
constituent of alloys that can be applied to high temperatures up to 2000 
℃ , see TaW refractory alloys [5,6] . These brilliant high-temperature 
behaviors provide TaW refractory alloys numerous chances to be 
high-profile candidates for fabricating components utilized in the nu -
clear reactor and aerospace industry. Conventional methods such as cast 
and powder metallurgy can be costly and time-consuming for fabri -
cating refractory alloys before entering the market. Additive 
manufacturing is a computer-aided design technology that fabricates 
parts layer-by-layer to create precise geometry, complex shapes, and 
high efficiency. This technology is commonly used in the aerospace, 
automotive, and medical industries. Nowadays, the use of additive 
manufacturing technologies for producing refractory alloy parts has 
become prevalent in the research field due to the shortened working 
time before utilization [7 – 11] . 
Several additive manufacturing methods with unique advantages/ 
disadvantages have been used to create refractory alloy samples/com -
ponents. Livescu and colleagues [12] utilized direct metal laser sintering 
formed tantalum and investigated the reduction of the porosity in the 
as-deposited microstructure. The results suggest that parameter opti -
mization can help reduce porosity and influence the formation of 
texture. Song and coworkers [13] attempted to improve the perfor -
mance of selective laser-melted tantalum to meet requirements in 
medicine and industry. They deposited tantalum and obtained a high 
density of over 99 %, and the resulting ultimate tensile strength is 
706 MPa accompanied by an elongation of 32 %. Other research about 
additive manufacturing, see electron beam powder-bed fusion of 
tantalum also reported a high density of 99 % with an ultimate tensile 
strength of 368 MPa as well as an elongation of over 31 % [14] . All the 
abovementioned works suggest that additive manufacturing is a 
powerful tool in fabricating tantalum with high quality and mechanical 
performance. Other works on pure tantalum are also focused on the 
tailoring of microstructure and enhancing strength [15,16] . Tantalum, 
like other body-cubic-centered (BCC) metals, is seriously influenced by 
impurities and alloying elements. For instance, alloys like Ta2.5 W, 
Ta5W, and Ta10W alloys [17] . In these tantalum alloys, Ta10W, as a 
widely researched and commercially utilized refractory alloy with good 
ductility-strength synergy at room and elevated temperatures is notable

In Fig. 4 , the microstructure of the as-deposited Ta10W refractory 
alloy is comprehensively characterized. Fig. 4 (a) shows the columnar 
grains along the build direction. This may not only be attributed to the 
large thermal gradient in the selective electron beam melting. To melt Ta 
and W, which have high melting points, the energy input in the melt 
pool needs to raise the temperature to at least 3410 ℃ . As a result, a 
slower scanning rate of the electron beam is required during the melting 
process of these refractory metals. In the present study, it was found that 
using a higher energy input does not necessarily result in a larger melt 
pool for metals with higher melting points. This is because metals with 
higher melting points require more energy input to melt compared to 
alloys with lower melting points such as titanium alloys. However, using 
a higher energy input leads to increased temperature in the melting 
pool, resulting in larger thermal gradients in the Ta10W refractory alloy 
after deposition [27] . Therefore, there are many columnar grains in a 
plane parallel to the build direction. However, the rapid cooling rate 
during selective electron beam melting also restricts columnar grains 
from growing too large. Fig. 4 (b) shows grains of the plane normal to the 
build direction which has grains with irregular shapes. The mean grain 
diameters of the grains observed from planes parallel and normal to the 
build direction are 19.07 μ m and 15.64 μ m, respectively. It has been 
reflected in Fig. 4 (e), and a strong texture with a direction of < 001 > can 
be noticed. This correlates well with the existence of large thermal 
gradients during Ta10W refractory alloy deposition as mentioned.

3.2. Mechanical properties 
3.2.1. Vicker ’ s hardness 
The randomly tested hardness values are shown in Table 1 . To 
compare, the hardness of planes XOZ and XOY ( Fig. 2 (a)) are presented. 
Notably, the hardness values of the XOZ plane are all larger than 280 HV 
and smaller than 300 HV. And hardness values of the XOY plane are 
similar, ranging from 280 HV to 300 HV. The calculated mean values 
with standard deviation are 295 ± 3 HV and 293 ± 4 HV for XOZ and XOY 
planes, respectively. This means the two planes have similar resistance 
to loads. Moreover, the obtained hardness values of the two planes are 
significantly larger than other work [23] . 
3.2.2. Compression performance 
Although tantalum and tantalum alloys have good ductility in 
deformation before fracture [16 – 18,23] , however, compression tests on 
the selective electron beam melted TaW refractory are rare. Herein, the 
engineering stress-strain curves of the as-fabricated Ta10W refractory 
alloy are shown in Fig. 6 . In Fig. 6 (a), the smaller size samples ( ϕ 4 × 6, 
mm) have curves with similar shapes, and the mean compressive yield 
strength is 664 ± 8 MPa. Notably, samples in Fig. 6 (a) cannot be com -
pressed to fracture, they simply deform to be shorter with a larger 
diameter. A similar phenomenon can also be observed in Fig. 6 (b). From 
the engineering stress-strain curves, it can be calculated that the mean 
compressive yield stress of the as-fabricated Ta10W refractory alloy is 
727 ± 3 MPa. These larger size samples ( ϕ 6 × 9, mm) still deform without 
any fracture trend, which suggests that the as-fabricated Ta10W re -
fractory alloy has good compressive ductility. Compared to other works 
[6,18 – 20] , the as-built Ta10W refractory alloy here has substantially 
improved compressive yield strength, which is 100 MPa higher than 
other works. Ta and W have similar atomic radii, and they form infinite 
solid solutions. Different forming methods, such as cast and additive 
manufacturing, cannot change the interactions between atoms. How -
ever, the residual stresses of the additively manufactured parts some -
times make a difference [27,28] . It has been studied that the residual 
stresses are common in electron beam melting processes [29,30] . The 
already reported results indicate that the mechanical performance of the 
additively manufactured parts correlates to the residual stresses gener -
ated [31] , and such a relation generally exists in laser and electron beam 
melting processes [32,33] . It can be concluded that large thermal gra -
dients cause significant residual stress in Ta10W alloy, thereby 
improving compressive strength. Additionally, the stresses may be 
exacerbated by rapid cooling during additive manufacturing. Thus, the 
additively manufactured Ta10W alloy has a stronger compressive yield 
strength than previously reported TaW alloys using other manufacturing

4.2. Mechanical behaviors 
Ta10W refractory alloy is a substitutional solid solution, which 
suggests that there is a weak interaction between atoms to cause severe 
lattice distortion, and thus cannot strongly influence the microhardness, 
compressive behaviors, and tensile behaviors. Moreover, to clarify the 
influence of the grain boundaries on the mechanical behaviors of the as- 
built Ta10W refractory alloy, microhardness values obtained at different 
sites are provided ( Fig. 9 ). There are indents inside and at the grain 
boundaries with similar hardness values, which indicates a negligible 
influence from the grain boundaries. 
However, as discussed above, the as-built Ta10W refractory alloy 
exhibits significantly enhanced strength compared to the TaW refractory 
alloy manufactured by other methods. Therefore, there must be another 
way to strengthen Ta10W refractory fabricated by additive 
manufacturing. It has been found that residual stresses present in alloys 
manufactured through additive processes may significantly impact their 
mechanical properties [27] . In this study, to obtain a direct under -
standing of the residual stresses in the additively manufactured Ta10W 
refractory alloy, a self-deformation sheet was cut from the upper surface 
of the as-built Ta10W refractory alloy (as shown in Fig. 10 (a) and (b)). 
The sheet had a thickness of less than 0.31 mm, a length of 34 mm, and 
the highest point of the bow was 1.37 mm. Fig. 10 (c) is the process of the 
sheet from being cut out of the sample to being curved as a bow. sig -
nificant residual stresses are likely generated in Ta10W refractory alloy 
during selective electron beam melting, which cannot be eliminated by 
heat treatment from thermal cycles [15] . Research has shown that re -
sidual stress accounts for 50 – 80 % of the yield strength of 
laser-free-formed materials and 5 – 10 % of the ultimate tensile strength 
of electron beam melted materials [32,33] . According to research that 
used a 3D finite element model to study stress profiles in electron beam 
melting, the stress in as-built Ni-based superalloys can reach 600 MPa 
[41] . The Ta10W refractory alloy as-fabricated has compressive stress in 
the upper layer, resulting in tensile stress in the underlying metal [42] . 
The Ta10W refractory alloy undergoes hardening due to pre-existing 
stresses, which enhances its mechanical performance when exposed to 
applied loads. 
Understandably, enhancement in strength or ductility at room tem -
perature may always have underlying dislocation-based mechanisms.

Additionally, residual stresses in additively manufactured alloys can be 
partially relieved by generating dislocations [43 – 45] . Hence, the role of 
dislocations in strengthening the additively manufactured Ta10W re -
fractory alloy is of great importance. In detail, edge and screw disloca -
tions with different characters have their dominating conditions 
according to their temperature dependence, respectively. It has been 
studied that screw dislocations a/2 < 111 > on plane {110} or {112} 
sometimes dominate the strength of alloys with BCC [46,47] . Simula -
tions suggest that screw dislocations are prevalent in TaW refractory 
alloys at room temperature, which strengthens BCC refractory alloys, 
including relatively dilute alloys [48 – 50] . Local chemical fluctuations 
and solute atoms in metals play a key role in altering their mechanical 
properties [50,51] . 
It is only now that we can observe evidence of the strengthening 
effects, which can be seen in the dislocations and substructures formed 
by dislocations in Fig. 11 . In Fig. 11 (a), dislocations are randomly 
distributed and can glide with a considerable mean free path, thereby 
contributing to the ductility. In Fig. 11 (b), there are many glide traces of 
the dislocations that crossed through the boundaries of the substructures 
in the microstructure. Hence, before compression and tensile deforma -
tion, there were already interactions between dislocations and sub -
structure boundaries that significantly increased the difficulty for 
dislocations to glide. Moreover, numerous dislocation tangles ( Fig. 11 
(c)) and the formation of dislocation walls ( Fig. 11 (d)) impeded the 
dislocation from moving any farther [52 – 54] . It is notable in Fig. 11 (d) 
that a dislocation is pressed to cross the dislocation wall. It can be 
inferred that in Fig. 7 (a), the strength rapidly increased due to a shorter 
deformation duration, resulting in fewer dislocations being employed.

5. Conclusion 
In summary, TaW powder was melted using selective electron beam 
melting to form a Ta10W alloy with good homogeneity and mechanical 
properties. The detailed conclusions are listed below, 
(1) Ta10W refractory alloy manufactured by selective electron beam 
melting has a homogeneous microstructure with uniformly 
spread Ta and W elements. 
(2) The as-fabricated Ta10W refractory alloy has a compressive yield 
strength of 727 ± 3 MPa, hardness values of 295 ± 3 HV (XOZ 
plane), and 293 ± 4 HV (XOY plane). 
(3) The tensile test results show that the yield strength, ultimate 
tensile strength, and ductility are 750 ± 5 MPa, 764 ± 4 MPa, and 
6.05 ± 2.34, respectively. 
(4) Residual stresses dominate dislocation-based strengthening of as- 
built Ta10W alloy compared to other TaW alloys.
