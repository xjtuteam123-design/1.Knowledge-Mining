ABSTRACT 
Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys are designed for extreme conditions due to their superior corrosion and 
creep resistance properties. However, these pose challenges in the subsequent recycling after reaching their end- 
of-life. Molten magnesium (Mg) can rapidly corrode the stable spent nickel-based superalloys and selectively 
dissolve nickel (Ni). This waste-free process represents an effective method for recycling spent superalloys and 
accomplishing metal regeneration. This study investigates the mechanism of selectively dissolving Ni from DD5, 
a nickel-based single-crystal superalloy, by optimizing process temperature, time, and Mg content in an inert 
atmosphere. Vacuum distillation was employed to separate the resulting Mg, residual superalloy (i.e., the ma -
terial left post-extraction), and Ni-rich alloy (i.e., the metal product selectively extracted). The findings revealed 
that the residual superalloy after selective Ni dissolution is characterized by a porous skeleton structure with pore 
sizes predominantly ranging from 2 to 30 nm and a low compressive strength which is 1/10 of the original DD5 
superalloy. 
1. Introduction 
Superalloys refers to a group of alloys developed for service at 
elevated temperatures where oxidation resistance is frequently required 
[ 1 ]. Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys which are a typical type of 
superalloys exhibit superior resistance to corrosion, thermal and me -
chanical shock, thermal and mechanical fatigue, erosion, and creep at 
elevated temperatures, compared to polycrystalline nickel-based su -
peralloys [ 2 – 4 ]. Hence, nickel-based single-crystal superalloys are pri -
marily employed for high-pressure turbine blades in aerospace and other 
large-scale power equipment such as nuclear reactors and petrochemical 
plants [ 5 – 7 ]. Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys have a complex 
composition, incorporating nickel (Ni), iron (Fe), chromium (Cr), cobalt 
(Co), aluminum (Al), titanium (Ti), and refractory metals including 
tungsten (W), tantalum (Ta), rhenium (Re), and molybdenum (Mo) [ 8 , 
9 ]. The manufacture, processing, and use of nickel-based single-crystal 
high-temperature alloys generate a substantial volume of spent super -
alloys. Recycling of these spent superalloys not only helps mitigate 
environmental contamination, but also tackles the issue of the scarcity of 
mineral resources [ 10 , 11 ]. However, the distinctive characteristics 
inherent to spent nickel-based single-crystal superalloys render their 
recycling a formidable challenge, and nowadays only 10 – 15% recycling 
rate has been achieved [ 12 , 13 ]. 
Presently, recycling of larger-sized spent superalloys predominantly 
employs a pyrometallurgical process. This approach subjects the spent 
superalloys to the temperatures above the melting point, followed by 
metal recovery via oxidation de-inclusion, vacuum refining and other 
required steps to produce the new master alloys of superalloys [ 14 , 15 ]. 
However, ensuring the quality control of the new master alloy presents a 
formidable challenge due to the inherent uncertainty surrounding the 
composition of the introduced superalloys scrap. Furthermore, essential 
metals such as Re, W and Mo in superalloys are susceptible to oxidation 
during melting, and the high volatility of the oxides leads to substantial 
losses of high-value metals [ 4 , 16 ]. Because of these challenges, the py -
rometallurgical treatment of spent superalloys has not been widely 
adopted, and the hydrometallurgy-based approach is currently the 
preferred process when reclaiming spent superalloys. The hydrometal -
lurgical process includes chemical dissolution and electrochemical

sulfuric acid, aqua regia, or organic acids to dissolve the constituent 
elements of superalloys [ 19 , 20 ]. Electrochemical dissolution enhances 
the leaching process by employing the superalloy as the consumable 
anode. Next, chemical precipitation, electrolytic deposition, organic 
solvent extraction, ion exchange, and other methods are employed to 
extract the refined metals or compounds from the pregnant solution [ 20 , 
21 ]. The application of the wet process is constrained by several factors, 
such as low leaching efficiency, substantial discharge of effluent, and 
intricate nature of subsequent separation procedures. These challenges 
are primarily attributed to the high corrosion resistance of superalloys. It 
is worth mentioning that a hybrid pyro- and hydro-metallurgical process 
has been reported for reclaiming spent superalloys [ 13 , 22 ]. In this 
approach, the constituent metallic elements of the spent superalloys are 
initially converted to sulfides, oxides, or intermetallic compounds via a 
high temperature process. Given the compromised strength and hard -
ness of superalloys after the high temperature treatment, conventional 
hydrometallurgical methods can then be used [ 12 , 18 ]. While this hybrid 
method mitigates the inefficiency associated with the pure wet process, 
substantial reagent consumption and intricate procedural complexities 
are reported. 
Liquid Metal Dealloying (LMD), an etching method that exploits the 
distinctive solubility of each alloy component in the liquid metal, is 
typically applied to produce nano-porous metals/alloys such as Ti, Mg, 
high-entropy alloys, and heterogeneous composites with promising 
properties [ 23 – 26 ]. When applying the LMD process to the recycling of 
spent superalloys, the principal component of the spent superalloys, i.e., 
Ni, can be selectively dissolved by molten Mg. In a previous study, to 
tackle the difficulties associated with recycling superalloy, we proposed 
a novel process for the selective extraction of Ni from the Ni-based su -
peralloy (Inconel 718) by employing molten Mg as the dealloy medium, 
followed by vacuum distillation [ 27 ]. 
As shown in the Mg – Ni phase diagram ( Fig. 1 ) [ 28 ], the solubility of 
metallic Ni in the molten Mg at 800 
◦
C is quite high, 31.5 at%. Likewise, 
other metals including Co and Al also have high solubilities in the 
molten Mg at 800 
◦
C, 17.2 at% for Co, and 100 at% for Al. In contrast, Fe 
and Mo are almost insoluble in the molten Mg [ 28 , 29 ]. The phase 
diagrams (by the FTlite database in FactSage 7.1 [ 28 ]) indicate that W, 
Mo, Ta, and Hf exhibit negligible solubility in molten Mg at 800 
◦
C. It is 
possible to use Mg as dealloying medium to achieve selective extraction 
of certain elements from spent nickel-based single-crystal superalloys. 
Preliminary experimental results show that when using Mg as dealloying 
medium, Ni is gradually enriched to the liquid metal, forming a molten 
Mg – Ni alloy. Since Mg is quite volatile compared with other metals (i.e., 
vapor pressure of Mg is orders of magnitude higher than other metals), 
Mg could be easily removed from the obtained molten Mg – Ni alloy by 
evaporation upon heating and vacuum. 
To ascertain the applicability of the LMD process in a broader 
context, specifically for the recycling of spent nickel-based superalloys, 
this study focuses on the more stable and higher-value Ni-based single- 
crystal superalloys (i.e., DD5, a typical nickel-based single-crystal su -
peralloy). To enhance the mass transfer during the dealloying process, 
electromagnetic induction heating was applied. The effects of time, 
temperature, and the mass ratio of superalloy to Mg on the Ni extraction 
rate from DD5 were investigated. The mechanical properties of the re -
sidual superalloy under optimal conditions were also evaluated.

As seen in Fig. 3 b, the contents of Ta, Re, and W in the residual su -
peralloy are slightly enriched compared to the original DD5 superalloy. 
This can be attributed to the selective dissolution of Ni, accompanied by 
minor amounts of Al and Co by the molten Mg. With the dissolution of Ni 
into the molten Mg, the Mg-rich channels within the superalloy were 
formed. The molten Mg within these channels continued to dissolve the 
surrounding Ni, gradually extending the presence of Mg-rich channels 
throughout the interior of the superalloy. Driven by the concentration 
gradients, the diffusion of Ni and minor amounts of Al and Co within the 
superalloy occurs along these channels towards the exterior utilizing the 
molten Mg as solvent. Meanwhile, the concentrations of Ta, Re, and W 
gradually decrease when approaching the residual superalloy/Mg 
interface; the concentrations of these elements in the Mg alloy 
approached zero, which confirms that the refractory metals do not 
dissolve in the molten Mg. This phenomenon may arise from two factors. 
Primarily, the substantial difference in atomic radius between Mg (R

their solubility, as indicated by Hume-Rothery rules when the difference 
in atomic radius exceeds 15% [ 32 – 34 ]. Secondly, the solubility is further

W and Re, which are more likely to form stable intermediate compounds

Fig. 3 c displays the solubility of metals existing in DD5 superalloy 
within the molten Mg at varying temperatures, calculated from the 
FTlite database in FactSage 7.1 [ 28 , 29 ] with the ratio of Mg to super -
alloy at 10:1. The results show that Ni, Co, Re, and Al can be completely 
dissolved in the molten Mg above 800 
◦

( Fig. 3 b) show that the migration of Co and Al from the DD5 superalloy 
to the molten Mg was rather limited, possibly due to the slow kinetics 
involving their dissolution from their original phases in the superalloy to 
the molten Mg. Meanwhile EDS results show that Re failed to diffuse into 
the molten Mg, which is inconsistent with the thermodynamic calcula -
tions shown in Fig. 3 b. Besides the significant difference in atomic radius 
and electronegativity between Re and Mg, diffusion coupling in -
vestigations revealed that Re exhibits notably retarded diffusion 
compared to other solute elements such as Ta, W, displaying a diffusion 
rate an order of magnitude lower than W [ 36 , 37 ]. 
In Fig. 3 a and d , Cr is enriched at the boundary between the residual 
superalloy and the Mg alloy. It is known that Cr acting as a solid solution 
strengthening element in superalloys typically precipitates as γ -phase. 
Due to the solubility of Cr in Ni, the molten Mg – Ni within the Mg-rich 
channel dissolves a portion of Cr, forming a ternary low-melting-point 
Mg – Ni – Cr melt. The Ni and Cr within the molten Mg diffuse outward 
driven by the concentration gradient. Upon reaching the boundary be -
tween the superalloy and molten Mg, the higher Mg content in the melt 
causes Ni to diffuse further away from the superalloy. Consequently, this 
results in a significant decrease of Ni content in the ternary Mg – Ni – Cr 
melt. Simultaneously, the solubility of Cr in the Mg – Ni – Cr melt exceeds 
saturation, causing its subsequent precipitation at the boundaries. 
Fig. 3 d illustrates that the Ni and Co concentrations were marginally 
higher within the Cr-rich boundary layer than the exterior. This means 
that the formation of the Cr-rich layer may hinder the diffusion of Ni and 
Co to the outside of the superalloy, which may be a factor limiting the Ni 
extraction rate.

concentration gradient of Ni, i.e., the Ni concentration in the molten Mg

postulated that by appropriately raising the position of the superalloy in 
the crucible, the contact surface between the superalloy and the molten 
Mg rich in Ni can be reduced during vacuum treatment, which will be 
helpful in preventing or minimizing the Ni back-diffusion.

3.4. Microstructure and mechanical properties of the residual superalloy 
Residual superalloy, rich in high-value metals, necessitate the 
employment of a hydrometallurgical process for their further separation 
and purification. The microstructure and material properties of residual 
superalloy under the optimal condition were thoroughly characterized 
to establish its enhanced suitability for subsequent hydrometallurgical 
processing compared to pristine counterparts. The results of BET in 
Fig. 6 a show that the pore size of the sample was concentrated in the 
range of 2 – 30 nm in diameter. This indicates that micropores and 
mesopores were mainly formed in the residual superalloy after deal -
loying. The N
2 
isothermal adsorption and desorption curves, presented 
in Fig. 6 b, show an H3-type hysteresis loop. The absence of a distinct 
saturated adsorption plateau in the figure indicates that the pore 
structure of the residual superalloy was highly irregular. The types of 
pores reflected by H3-type include flat plate slit structures, cracks, and 
wedge-shaped structure, etc [ 38 ]. The pores may be formed by the slit 
channels in the residual superalloy during dealloying and cracks pro -
duced by the different shrinkage stresses of each phase upon cooling. 
Fig. 6 c illustrates the load and compressive strain of the residual 
superalloy. The results show that the compressive strength of the re -
sidual superalloy is reduced to about 1/10 of the original DD5 superalloy 
after dealloying. Mechanical testing proves that the residual superalloy 
can be easily crushed, which is beneficial to the subsequent recovery of 
Re and other valuable metals. With the significantly reduced mechanical 
strength, the residual superalloy can be more easily crushed into smaller 
particle size powder with a short milling time. Fig. 6 d exhibits the 
particle size analysis of the residual superalloy after dealloying under 
ball milling for a short time of 3 min using a frequency of 1200 Hz. The 
powder exhibits an average particle size of 23.52 μ m, with a particle size 
distribution predominantly ranging from 5 to 100 μ m.
