Abstract
As next generation ultra-high temperature structural materials, the balance between high-temperature strength and room-temperature
toughness of the Nb-base in-situ composites is the most important issue. This paper investigated the mechanical behavior of Nb–10W–10Si–xB
(x = 0, 0.1, 1 and 2). Materials were prepared by arc-melting and annealed at 1870 K for 100 h. The microstructures of the Nb–10W–10Si–xB
alloys were primary Nb solid solution (NbSS) and a eutectic of NbSS/Nb5Si3 (5–3 silicide). At 0.1 and 1 mol% B contents, Vickers hardness and
strength at room temperature were decreased. But those were increased at 2 mol% B. All the alloys have more than 7% compressive ductility at
room temperature. B improves both the room-temperature toughness (K
Q) and the high-temperature strength (σmax). TheKQ and the σmax of the
Nb–10W–10Si alloy at 1670 K are 8.2 MPa m1/2 and 400 MPa, respectively, and are 8.8 MPa m1/2 and 470 MPa in the Nb–10W–10Si–2B alloy.
With two-phase NbSS/Nb5Si3 microstructures, the compressive damage at high temperature was dominated by debonding of the interfaces
between the primary NbSS and the 5–3 silicide, while the fracture mode at room temperature is transgranular, controlled by the primary NbSS
cleavage.

1. Introduction
With a relatively high melting point of about 2743 K and
room-temperature ductility, Nb is one of the most promis-
ing refractory metals for future high-temperature structural
materials. Approaches to improving the high-temperature
strength and the room-temperature toughness of Nb have
involved solid solution strengthening or forming in-situ
composites with Nb and intermetallics such as silicides
[1–17] and aluminides [18–21]. The Nb–Si binary with
equilibrium phases of primary Nb solid solution (Nb
SS)
and Nb 5Si3 silicide has the most potential for strength at
elevated temperatures. In these phases, the Nb SS offers
room-temperature ductility, and the silicides supply the

As structural materials, conﬂict between strengthening
and toughening still exists in the Nb-base in-situ compos-
ites. How to achieve a balance between the high-temperature
strength and the room-temperature toughness of the Nb-
base in-situ composites is the most important issue. In pre-
vious work, the Nb–10Mo–10W–10Ti–18Si alloy [15,16] is
a potential candidate from the viewpoint of the strength at
elevated temperatures; however, its poor ductility (less than
1%) at room temperature may be a barrier to aerospace appli-
cations. In order to modify the room-temperature plasticity
and toughness of the Nb–10Mo–10W–10Ti–18Si the solid
solution strengthening elements Mo and Ti were deleted and
the volume fraction of the silicide phase was reduced by
reducing the Si content. High-temperature strength was uti-
lized by micro-alloying with B. Thus, Nb–10W–10Si–B al-
loys with low B contents were studied.
2. Experimental procedures
The composition of Nb–10W–10Si is used as base and
the Nb was further replaced by 0, 0.1, 1 and 2 mol% boron.
Elements with 99.95 mass% or higher purity were used.
Button ingots were prepared by arc-melting in 1 atm argon
ﬁve or six times to homogenize the compositions through-
out the ingots and were then arc-melted again two to three
times to cast the samples into bars. The bars were ﬁnally
annealed at 1870 K under vacuum for 100 h, which ensured
the equilibrium microstructures. By those technologies, the
ﬁnal oxygen contents of the materials are ∼40 mass ppm.
The microstructures were observed using a scanning elec-
tron microscope (SEM), and the composition of each phase
was analyzed using energy-dispersion X-ray spectroscopy
(EDX) in the SEM. Vickers hardness ( HV) was measured
under an applied load of 9.8 N. Compressive ductility (ε
max)
is deﬁned as the strain corresponding to the specimen shat-
ter in the compressive stress-strain curve. Notched fracture
toughness K
Q at room temperature was measured using a
three-point-bending test. The three-point-bending specimen
is 30 mm in length, 6 and 3 mm in width and thickness, re-
spectively. A notch was introduced up to a/w = 0.5( a:
notch length, w: specimen width) by electrical discharge ma-
chining with a wire diameter of 0.1 mm. Compression tests
were conducted at room temperature, 1470 and 1670 K in
vacuum at an initial strain rate of 1 × 10
−4 s−1. The dimen-
sions of the compression test specimens were 2 mm × 2m m
in cross-section and 5 mm in length, and all surfaces of the
samples were polished using a 6 /H9262m diamond polish prior
to testing. Because less than 0.3% weight of the materials
was lost during arc-melting and annealing, we assumed that
the actual bulk compositions of these alloys were the nom-
inal compositions designed. To investigate the crack propa-
gation path, the mechanical testing machine was turned off,
and the partially fractured three-point-bend samples were
unloaded. Finally, the crack propagation and fracture mode
at room temperature, and the compressive damage at high
temperature were observed by SEM fractography.
3. Results and discussions
3.1. Microstructures and compositions of each phase
Typical SEM microstructures (back-scattered electron im-
ages) of the annealed samples are shown inFig. 1. The bright
phase is the Nb-base solid solution (referred to as Nb
SS here-
after) and the dark phase is the (Nb, W)5Si3 silicide (referred
to as 5–3 silicide hereafter). The Nb–10W–10Si– xB alloys
consist of primary Nb SS dendrites and a ﬁne scale eutectic
of NbSS/5–3 silicide. The Nb SS particles in the eutectic are
very small compared with the primary dendrites. At small
nominal B content, B is soluble mainly in the 5–3 silicide
as shown in the Nb–Si–B ternary diagram [18]. No evident
difference in the microstructures of the Nb–10W–10Si– xB
alloys is found when the B content is changed. It is evident
that the Nb–10W–10Si–xB alloys contain the large primary
dentritical Nb
SS and ﬁne secondary NbSS particles uniformly
distributed in the 5–3 silicide matrix. The volume fraction of
the primary Nb
SS measured by statistical quantitative met-
allographic analysis is about 70%.
EDX analysis revealed that the Si concentration in the
NbSS is less than 1% and the W in the 5–3 silicide is negli-
gibly small. W tends to concentrate in the NbSS and is about
13 mol%. This W content is consistent with results calcu-
lated from the actual volume fraction of the Nb
SS.

In general, increases in strength accompany decreases in
toughness in engineering materials. However, it seems that
the reduction in room-temperature toughness and increase
in high-temperature strength in the Nb–10W–10Si alloy can
be avoided by the B addition. According to the Nb–Si–B
ternary diagram, B solidiﬁes in the silicide at the current
B concentrations and does not change the characteristics of
the SEM microstructure of the Nb–10W–10Si. Based on the
solid solution strengthening principle, more solidiﬁed B in
the silicide undoubtedly obstructs the movement of disloca-
tions, thereby, improves the capability of the strain hardening
and strength of the Nb–10W–10Si at the high temperature.
But the toughening mechanism of B on the Nb–10W–10Si
is still unknown. Since the interface plays a key role in both
the room-temperature fracture and high-temperature dam-
age, B is considered to cause an improvement in toughness
at room temperature and in strength at elevated temperatures
by changing the behavior of the interface. More investiga-
tions, such as the effects of B on the dislocation structure
and the interaction of B with the dislocation and the interface
during deformation, etc. should be done in the future. These
may reveal the strengthening and toughening mechanisms
by B addition, and guide the optimization of compositions
and mechanical properties of the Nb–W–Si–B alloys.
4. Conclusions
In Nb–10W–10Si– xB alloys, the following conclusions
can be made about the microstructure and the mechanical
response at room- and high-temperatures:
