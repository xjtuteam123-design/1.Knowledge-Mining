1. Introduction
The breakthrough of the high-temperature strength of structural
materials will help to promote the revolutionary development of
aviation and aerospace industry. [1] At present, those alloys used
for high-temperature structural parts are still mainly Ni-based
superalloys, such as Haynes 230, Inconel 718, and MAR-M
247.[2– 4] However, restricted by the properties of the principal ele-
ment, the melting points of currently developed Ni-based super-
alloys are about 1300 °C, and the operating temperature should
not exceed 780 °C. [5] The most direct way to increase the melting
point of an alloy is undoubtedly to intro-
duce the metals with high melting points,
especially those refractory metals, such as
vanadium (V), tungsten (W), niobium
(Nb), zirconium (Zr), molybdenum (Mo),
hafnium (Hf ), and tantalum (Ta). Thus,
one potential strategy is to develop refrac-
tory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs).
Coincidentally, the concept of high-entropy
alloys was ﬁrst creatively proposed by
Cantor[6] and Yeh[7] in 2004, which provides
an entirely new direction for the ﬁeld of
metallurgy and alloys. [8] Senkov[9,10] ﬁrst
designed and investigated two kinds of
RHEAs with a single-phase body-centered-
cubic (BCC) structure: NbMoTaW and
VNbMoTaW, which have great high-
temperature mechanical properties.
However, so many BCC RHEAs have
been found; they are brittle with poor ductility at room
temperature.[11,12] If the ductility cannot be improved, it will
make it dif ﬁcult in an extensive industrial application. [13– 15]
To improve the ductility of RHEAs, one of the effective strate-
gies is triggering the transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP),
from BCC to hexagonal-close-packed-structured high-entropy
alloys.[16] Huang[16] triggered the TRIP phenomenon by lowering
the Ta content in TaHf ZrTi system. The designed Ta 0.4Hf ZrTi
alloy obtained 27% fracture strain, but the yield strength inevita-
bly decreased by about 73.3%.
Besides, many strategies have selected the Hf element to
improve the ductility. [17– 19] considered three RHEAs designed
principles and designed the single-phase BCC HfNbTaTiV alloy,
which exhibited a high compressive yield strength of about
1350 MPa and over 45% compressive ductility.
However, the Hf element is much expensive, which can sig-
niﬁcantly limit the potential industrial application. Therefore,
reducing the cost by substituting elements and designing ingre-
dients is necessary. Based on the classical WNbMoTa, several
researchers have found that V instead of W can show good
mechanical properties and ductility. [20– 23] Yao[20] reported that
VNbMoTa RHEAs can exhibit 1.5 GPa yield strength with
21% compressive strain at room temperature. Yin [21] found that
V element can be the prime element to strengthen high-entropy
alloys. V xNbMoTa RHEAs with V concentrations of 0 – 25 at%
have also been studied, and the testing results illustrated that
the fracture strain of the equimolar alloy is over 25% at room

To design metal materials that combine high-temperature strength with room-
temperature ductility, three kinds of body-centered-cubic (BCC) refractory high-
entropy alloys (RHEAs) with different Mo element contents are screened and
prepared by combining several parameters. The room-temperature ductility and
excellent high-temperature (1500 °C) mechanical properties are investigated.
Combined with the microstructure test, the characteristic of fracture and
deformation is also discussed. RHEAs studied herein have great compressive
yield strength approximately ranging from 1324.5 to 1424.4 MPa with excellent
compressive ductility ranging from 20.7% to 44.2% at room temperature. At
1500 °C, the yield strengths of three RHEAs are over 335.47 MPa at 10 /C0 3 s/C0 1
strain rate, which is rarely found in traditional metals materials. The excellent
mechanical properties of these three RHEAs at both room and high temperature
as a promising material are bene ﬁcial to future high-temperature application.

As for the principles of the high-entropy alloy design, several
parameters are important, including mixing entropy
(ΔSmix),[24,25] enthalpy of mixing (ΔHmix),[25,26] Gibbs free energy
(ΔGmix),[27] the atomic size difference (δ%),[28,29] electronegativity
difference ( Δχ),[30] and valence electron concentration (VEC). [31]
Generally speaking, ΔGmix determines the stability of the alloy
system, and if ΔSmix is much higher than the entropy required
to form intermetallic compounds, the alloy system tends to form
simple solid solutions. [32] For RHEAs, the melting point of the
alloy ( Tm) is also an important parameter. A too high melting
point will make preparation more dif ﬁcult and the distribution
of elements more nonuniform. Recently, the relationship among
the average values of the shear modulus mismatch ( δηave),
average atomic radius mismatch, and tensile yield strength
has been already reported.[30] There is a positive linear correlation
between δηave and strength, where the strength increases the
δηave increases.
Based on our previous study,[23] we tried to design and prepare
new VNbTaMo x RHEAs with excellent high-temperature
mechanical properties and room-temperature ductility by com-
bining the six parameters of ΔHmix, ΔSmix, Tm, VEC, δ%, and
δηave. Those ﬁve parameters were used to determine the content
range of Mo element in VNbTaMo x system. Then, vacuum arc
melting method was selected to prepare as-cast alloys button
ingots. The in ﬂuences of the Mo contents on mechanical prop-
erties and microstructures were also investigated. This study
explored the strategy of RHEAs design on high-temperature
applications.
2. Experimental Section
The as-cast alloys button ingots were prepared with high-purity
(>99.9 at%) metal blocks or particles using vacuum arc melting
in water-cooled copper crucibles under argon atmosphere. The
pseudobinary phase diagram for adjusting Mo element was cal-
culated using Thermo Calc. The TCHEA1 database was selected
and used, which contained 15 elements commonly used in high-
entropy alloys, including 105 binary alloys and 200 ternary alloy
phase diagrams.
Room-temperature mechanical properties were tested by a
universal testing machine (SUNS UTM5105X). The strain rate
was 10 /C0 3 s/C0 1. The size of compressed test samples was
φ4 /C2 6 mm. In order to ensure the accuracy of the experimental
results and reduce the test error caused by the difference of sam-
ples, three samples were tested for each alloy. The mechanical
properties at different high temperatures were tested by thermal
simulation testing machine (Gleeble-3500). The size of high-
temperature compressed test samples was φ8 /C2 12 mm. The
platinum– rhodium thermocouple was welded to the surface of
the sample before the test to synchronously measure the surface
temperature of the sample. The sample was heated to 1500 °C at
the heating rate of 500 °C min /C0 1, it was kept at this temperature
for 1 min to uniformly heat the sample, and then it began to
compress. Two strain rates were selected, 10 /C0 3 and 10 /C0 2 s/C0 1,
and the default deformation was 70%. After high-temperature
compression, the cylindrical specimens with φ8 /C2 12 mm were
deformed into drum specimens, and the ﬁnal drum specimens
were collected for further analysis.
As for characterizations, the phase of as-cast alloys was ana-
lyzed by X-Ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance) using
Cu kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). The scanning range was from
20° to 100° with scanning rate at 10° min /C0 1. The microstructures
of as-cast alloys were imaged by a scanning electron microscope
(SEM) equipped with electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD)
detector. The as-cast alloy samples were sanded on 400#, 600#,
800#, 1000#, 1500#, and 2000# sandpaper in turn, and then the
surface to be tested was sanded by argon-ion polishing until there
were no obvious scratches and the surface stress was removed.
The test step size was 4 μm, and the test area was
1600 /C2 1600 μm. Figure 1 shows the sample cutting method after
high-temperature compression. 1.5 mm slices were cut parallel
to the compression direction in the drum specimens. After
the surfaces of the samples were sanded and argon ion polished,
crystallographic orientations were characterized. The test step
size was 1 μm, and the test area was 500 /C2 500 μm.

3.2. Microstructure of VNbTaMo x RHEAs
XRD was employed to identify the phase structure for
VNbTaMox alloys, as shown in Figure 4. These three alloys have
a single BCC phase, which are consistent with the Thermo-Calc
calculation results. It indicates that a small range of variation in
Mo element content cannot affect their BCC phase directly.
According to Bragg ’s law and the mixing calculation principle
of disordered solid-solution alloys, both actual lattice constant
(aXRD) and theoretical lattice constant (a ROM) can be obtained
as listed in Table 1. It can be found that the differences between
aXRD and aROM are quite small, which means that the three alloys
follow the mixing principle. It can further prove that the
component phase of three alloys belongs to the disordered
solid-solution phase.
Figure 5 shows the inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) maps from Y-axis
and corresponding grain size distributions of Mo 0.8,M o 1.0,a n d
Mo1.2 alloys. For as-cast samples, the grain orientation distribu-
tions are random. The average grain sizes of Mo 0.8,M o 1.0,a n d
Mo1.2 alloys are 123, 149, and 162 μm, respectively. During the
solidiﬁcation process, the grain size is closely related to the active
nucleation particles. When the undercooling in the solution
exceeds the critical undercooling of free formation, nucleation par-
ticles can become active nucleation particles and grow into grains;
on the contrary, it inhibits the growth of nucleation particles.
Undercooling in the melt is formed due to the different solidi ﬁ-
cation sequences during the solidiﬁcation process, resulting from
different melting points of different elements. An increase of
Mo content leads to a narrowing of the solid – liquid phase zone
(as shown in Figure 1), which weakens segregation and reduces
the undercooling of the components in the solution during solidi-
ﬁcation, making it more difﬁcult to reach the critical undercooling
for activating nucleation particles, resulting in a decrease in active
nucleation particles and the formation of coarser grains.

We also observed the fracture surface of Mo 0.8,M o 1.0, and
Mo1.2 alloys after compression deformation by means of SEM,
as shown in Figure 6b – d. The fracture morphologies of three
alloys show transgranular fracture. The typical features of ductile
fracture such as dimples are almost invisible. A large number of
tearing edges, cleavage steps, and river patterns can be observed.
In general, tear edges and cleavage steps can be classi ﬁed as
quasicleavage faults, which lie between brittle and ductile frac-
ture modes. This shows that the RHEAs of this component
system are not absolute brittle fracture, but begin to show ductile

3.4. Mechanical Properties at 1500 °C High Temperature
Figure 7a,b shows the true stress– strain curves of compression at
1500 °C high temperature with 10 /C0 3 and 10 /C0 2 s/C0 1, respectively.
All three alloys at both strain rates present the typical strain-
softening feature. At strain rate of 10 /C0 3 s/C0 1, the yield strength
of three RHEAs ranges from 335.47 to 412.57 MPa. When the
strain rate increases, at 10 /C0 2 s/C0 1, the corresponding yield
strengths of three RHEAs range from 433.79 to 577.87 MPa,
and the yield strength increases by 40.13% at most.
Dynamic recrystallization (DRX) is a common phenomenon
during hot deformation of alloys, especially at high temperature.
The relationship between work hardening rate and strain/ ﬂow
stress leads to the critical stress and critical strain at the emer-
gence of DRX (Figure S1 and S2, Supporting Information). The
newly designed alloy, due to the different alloying elements, will
affect its stacking fault energy and then change its recrystallization
mode. DRX is an important mechanism of microstructure evolu-
tion of metal materials. The work hardening rate is pretty high at
the initial stage of hot deformation, but when the strain reaches a
critical strain value, the work hardening rate drops sharply due to
the start-up of DRX. Table 2 shows the results of critical stresses/
strains at different strain rates. From Table 2, it can be found that
the critical stress decreases with the decrease of strain rate. This
indicates that the alloy is more prone to DRX under low strain rate
loading. Noticeably, with the increase of strain rate, the critical
strain rate lags correspondingly.

different strain rates at 1500 °C. Figure 8a represents the EBSD
results of Mo 0.8 alloy at 10 /C0 3 s/C0 1 strain rate. It can be found that
the initial large grains are elongated perpendicular to the com-
pression direction. A large number of small sized grains occur
along the initial grain boundary, forming a chain structure,
which is a typical partial DRX microstructure. [39,40] A similar
microstructure with a smaller recrystallized grain size is shown
in Figure 8b for the Mo 0.8 alloy at a strain rate of 10 /C0 2 s/C0 1. The
same phenomenon can be found between Figure 8c – f, namely,
with the increase of strain rate deformed at 1500 °C, the recrystal-
lized grain size decreases. It is also can see from Figure 8 that the
average DRX grain sizes decrease with the increase of Mo ele-
ment content when deformed at different strain rates at
1500 °C. Table 3 shows the average recrystallized grain sizes
of the three alloys deformed under different strain rates at
1500 °C. For example, the average DRX grain sizes of Mo 0.8 alloy
at 10 /C0 3 and at 10 /C0 2 s/C0 1 strain rate are about 10.19 and 4.96 μm,
respectively, shown the decreasing of average DRX grain sizes by
about 51.32%. The average DRX grain sizes of three alloys

4. Conclusion
In this work, a series of BCC RHEAs were designed and studied,
and three of them were screened, prepared, and veriﬁed by room-
and high-temperature experiments. They have compressive yield
strength approximately ranging from 1324.5 to 1424.4 MPa with
compressive fracture strain ranging from 20.7% to 44.2% at
room temperature. The fracture surface can be classi ﬁed as qua-
sicleavage faults, which is between brittle and ductile fracture
modes. At 1500 °C, the yield strength of three RHEAs is over
335.47 MPa at 10 /C0 3 s/C0 1 strain rate, which is rarely found in tra-
ditional metals materials. Besides, with the increase of Mo ele-
ment content in alloy ingots, the high-temperature yield strength
increases synchronously. According to the microstructure, the
typical rheological softening characteristics exhibited by
stress– strain curves at 1500 °C were caused by DRX. This work
has successfully designed and prepared RHEAs with excellent
room- and high-temperature mechanical properties, which cast
new light on high-temperature-application metals materials.
