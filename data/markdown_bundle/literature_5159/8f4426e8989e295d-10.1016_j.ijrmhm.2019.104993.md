Abstract 
Two bulk Mo-Si-B based alloys ( Mo-12Si-10B and Mo -26Nb-12Si-10B (at.%), 
abbreviated as 0Nb and 26Nb alloy respectively ) were fabricated by mechanical 
alloying and then hot pressing. Comparative studies were carried out on the 
densification behavior, microstructure, room-temperature fracture toughness, elevated 
temperature compression and oxidation resistance of these two alloys . The results 
showed that alloy 0Nb was composed of (Mo), Mo3Si and Mo5SiB2, while alloy 26Nb 
was free of Mo3Si and had higher (Mo) content and a little Nb5Si3. Compared to the 
alloy 0Nb, alloy 26Nb presented better compactibility , higher room-temperature 
fracture toughness (8.84 ± 0.17 vs. 6.77 ± 0.20 MPa·m1/2) and elevated temperature 
compression strength (851.7 ± 11.7 vs. 644.2 ± 10.2 MP a) but worse oxidation 
resistance. 
Keywords: Mo-Nb-Si-B alloy; densification behavior; mechanical property; oxidation 
resistance 
1. Introduction 
The key for improving the performance of gas turbine engines, which are widely 
used in the fields of power generation and aerospace, is to increase the turbine inlet 
temperature [1]. This drives the quest for inventing and discovering new ultrahigh 
temperature structural materials . As the most widely-used turbine blade materials, 
Ni-based superalloys can function at temperatures nearing 1150 oC, close to the upper

limit restriction of their melting points. Although applying highly refined cooling 
systems and thermal barrier coatings in the blades can help them bear higher gas 
temperatures to some extent, this leads to large fuel efficiency losses of the engine [2]. 
As a result, it is urgent to develop new ultrahigh temperature structural materials with 
the capability operating at temperatures beyond that of Ni-based superalloys. In the 
past few decades, Mo -Si-B based alloys, as promising candidates for Ni-based 
superalloys, have drawn much attention due to their high melting points, excellent 
elevated temperature strength and oxidation resistance [3-4]. According to the 
Mo-Si-B phase diagram, Mo -Si-B alloys with the potential for ultrahigh temperature 
structural materials should be located in the Mo solid solution ( (Mo)), Mo 3Si and 
Mo5SiB2 three-phase region, where the (Mo) toughens the alloys and the intermetallic 
phases afford elevated temperature strength and oxidation resistance [4]. However, 
the catastrophical oxidation behavior of Mo 3Si due to its high Mo content [5-7] and 
weaker elevated temperature strength than Mo 5SiB2 and Mo 5Si3 [8, 9] make it an 
undesired phase for Mo-Si-B alloys. Many researches show that alloying elements Nb, 
Ta and W can destabilize Mo3Si phase [10, 1 1]. Owing to its lower density, 
substitution of some Mo by Nb can reduce the density of a Mo-Si-B alloy, which is 
encouraging for its application in the field of aerospace. However, the oxidation 
product of Nb, Nb 2O5, is thought to be de trimental to the formation of a protective 
borosilicate scale of Mo-Si-B alloy during oxidation [12, 13]. Therefore, it is of great 
importance to determine the minimum addition of Nb with the capability of 
suppressing Mo3Si phase. In previous work [14], the critical value was determined to 
be between 24 and 26 at.% for Mo -xNb-12Si-10B (at.%, hereafter all compositions 
are given in at.% unless otherwise stated ) alloys fabricated by mechanical alloying 
(MA), cold pressing and then pressureless sintering . Great changes of the alloys ’ 
microstructure were observed along with the suppression of Mo 3Si, which certainly 
affected the alloy properties [14]. Thus, in the present paper , Mo-12Si-10B and 
Mo3Si-free Mo-26Nb-12Si-10B bulk alloys were fabricated by MA and then hot 
pressing (HP) and the densification behavior, microstructure, mechanical properties 
including room-temperature fracture toughness and elevated temperature compression

2. Experimental procedures 
MA followed by HP was used to fabricate bulk Mo -Si-B based alloys with the 
compositions of Mo -12Si-10B and Mo -26Nb-12Si-10B ( abbreviated as 0Nb and 
26Nb alloy respectively). Elemental powders with purities of 99% , or better , were 
used as raw materials. Powder mixtures weighed according to the desired 
compositions were milled in a high energy planetary ball mill for 30 h under an Ar 
atmosphere using hardened stainless steel jars and balls. The milling speed and ball to 
powder ratio were fixed at 400 rpm and 15:1. Consolidation of the MAed powders 
was performed using a self-designed HP sintering furnace. To release the gas impurity 
in powders as much as possible, the target pressure of 40 MPa was not applied until 
the temperature reached to 1000 oC. During this period, the molecular pump had been 
working to pump out the gas impurity . Afterwards, along with the holding of 40 MPa 
pressure, the temperature was increased from 1000 oC to 1600 oC with a heating rate 
of 10 oC/min and then soaking was done for 2 h. Finally, cylindrical billets with fixed 
diameters of 30 mm were fabricated. During the HP process, the displacement of the 
upper punch, namely the shrinkage of the powder compact, was recorded by a grating 
ruler (Renishaw, RGH 25) with an accuracy of 1 m. To eliminate the disturbance of 
thermal expansion from HP sets on the displacement, an additional calibration 
experiment was conducted under the same sintering process, but without the powders. 
To reveal the phase transformation during sintering, HP processes performed fully in 
accordance with the procedures for fabricating bulk alloys were ceased when the 
temperatures were heated to the selected values ( 1150 oC, 1257 oC and 1340 oC, 
derived from the densification curves as shown in Fig. 1). 
Cubic oxidation specimens with dimensions about 6 × 6 × 6 mm3 were cut from 
the sintered billets using electro-discharge machining (EDM). After being ground 
using SiC abrasive papers down to 1200 grit, these cubic specimens were cleaned in 
an ultrasonic ethanol bath for 15 min and then dried for oxidation tests . Oxidation 
tests were conducted in a chamber electric furnace at 1300 oC in static air for 1 h, 5 h 
and 10 h. The specimen mass changes before and after oxidation were measured using

compositional analyses were performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, 
Tescan MIRA3) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectrum analyzer ( EDS, 
Inca X-sight). To determine the composition of every phase, at least five similar areas 
were analysed, and then an average was taken. Quantitative metallographic analys es 
were performed using Image-Pro Plus (IPP) software.

Fig. 1 shows the densification processes of alloys 0Nb and 26Nb beyond 1000 oC 
at 40 MPa pressure. Both alloys exhibited sigmoidal relative density curves, similar to 
the densification behavior of most single-phase sintered materials [17-20]. Thus, after 
an initial slow densification the densification rate increased significantly, followed by 
a decrease. However, the densification rate curve s presented significant fluctuation s 
during the rapid densification stage. In the ranges of these fluctuations, alloys 0Nb 
and 26Nb had the minimum densification rates at 1340 oC and 1257 oC respectively . 
As shown in Fig. 2, XRD analyses of alloys 0Nb and 26Nb fabricated by interrupted 
HP processes showed the gradual formation of the intermetallic phases : Mo3Si, 
Mo5Si3 and Mo5SiB2. These strong intermetallic phases were believed to strengthen 
the billets and decreased the densification rate. Afterwards, increased sintering 
temperature softened the particle s and enhanced the particle surface diffusion to 
release the frictional force, promoting the particle deformation and sliding [21]. As a 
result, the densification rate sped up again. As shown in Fig. 2, alloy 26Nb needed a 
lower temperature for the obvious formation of intermetallic phases than alloy 0Nb , 
for example, when heated to 1257 oC, obvious diffraction peaks for Mo3Si and 
Mo5SiB2 could be observed in alloy 26Nb , while only extremely weak peaks belong 
to Mo 3Si and Mo 5Si3 could be found in alloy 0Nb . The easier formation of 
intermetallic phases during heating in alloy 26Nb was responsible for its earlier 
appearance of the fluctuation of the densification rate during the rapid densification 
stage. Addition of less than 30 at.% Nb to Mo -12Si-10B powders [14] helped to 
accelerate the MA process , and thus promoted the formation of intermetallic phases 
during the following sintering process. 
After applying 40 MPa pressure at 1000 oC, alloy 26Nb had a lower relative 
density (41.5%) than alloy 0Nb (48.4%). This was attributed to its higher particle

3.2 Microstructure of the as-HPed alloys 
Fig. 3 shows the XRD spectra of the MAed powders and as -HPed alloys and 
back-scattered-electron (BSE) images of the microstructures of the as -HPed alloys 
0Nb and 26Nb. Fig. 3(a) shows obvious Nb diffraction peaks, implying its existence 
after 30 h milling. Table 1 shows the EDX results of the phases in Fig. 3 (c) and (d) . 
Due to the extremely small phase size, EDX analyses of a phase also included signals 
from the surrounding phases. As a result, EDX results were just used to further 
confirm the phases detected by XRD analyses . Although EDX could not measure B 
content exactly, the identification of Mo 3Si and Mo 5SiB2 was achieved by their 
obvious difference in Si contents. For the as-HPed alloys, XRD, SEM-BSE and EDX 
analyses showed that alloy 0Nb comprised of (Mo), Mo 3Si and Mo 5SiB2, and alloy 
26Nb comprised of (Mo), Mo5SiB2 and a little Nb5Si3. Here, addition of 26 at.% Nb 
to Mo-12Si-10B alloy succeeded in suppressing the formation of Mo3Si. Based on the 
Mo-Nb-Si-B quaternary phase diagram [ 22], Nb 5Si3 phase was excluded for alloy 
26Nb. However, as shown in Fig. 3(a), obvious Nb diffraction peaks implied its

existence in the 30 h mill ed powder particles . Different from smelting processes, 
elements showed lower diffusion ability during sintering . As a result, during HP 
process of alloy 26Nb, it was hard for all residual Nb to disso lve into (Mo) or 
Mo5SiB2 by solid diffusion , and then Nb5Si3 formed in some Nb-rich regions. For 
alloy 26Nb, EDX results and the obvious shifts to lower 2  angles of the diffraction

For simplicity , Mo5SiB2 and Mo 3Si or Nb5Si3 are together referred to as 
intermetallics due to the marginal contrast between them. Alloy 0Nb had an obvious 
intermetallic matrix containing isolated (Mo) islands and residual pores. Intuitively , 
compared to the alloy 0Nb, (Mo) in alloy 26Nb had a better continuity, although it 
still had an island-like morphology. 
Table 2 shows the apparent densities measured using Archimedes’ method and 
area fractions of constituent phases and pores counted by IPP software used on several 
SEM-BSE images of the two alloys. Alloy 26Nb had a porosity of 0.2%, while alloy 
0Nb had a much higher porosity of 2.5%. This was consistent with the 
above-mentioned densification behavior of two alloys. As expected, compared to the 
alloy 0Nb, alloy 26Nb had an obvious reduction o f the apparent density , which was 
beneficial to its wide application in aerospace field. Alloy 26Nb had a higher (Mo) 
content than alloy 0Nb, due to the suppression of Mo3Si by the 26 at.% Nb addition. 
3.3 Mechanical properties 
3.3.1 Room-temperature fracture toughness 
Table 3 shows the room-temperature fracture toughness K Q values of alloys 0Nb 
and 26Nb. Alloy 26Nb exhibited significantly improved (increased by about 31%) 
average K Q than alloy 0Nb. Fig. 4 shows the typical s econdary-electron (SE) SEM 
fractographs for SENB specimens of alloys 0Nb and 26Nb. The dominant fr acture 
mechanism of alloy 0Nb was intergranular fracture. Many residual closed pores were 
at the grain boundaries (indicated by red arrows in Fig . 4(a)), which induced stress 
concentration at these sites. Also, the segregation of impurities, especially oxygen, at 
the grain boundaries would also weaken their cohesion [23, 2 4]. These two 
mechanisms resulted in a high incidence of intergranular fracture (confirmed by the 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT

appearance of large amount of sintering gr ains in Fig. 4(a) ) and a lower fracture 
toughness of alloy 0Nb. As shown in Fig. 4(b), the incidence of transgranular fracture 
increased significantly in alloy 26Nb (indicated by blue arrows in Fig . 4(b)). Its 
almost fully densif ied microstructure and increasing content of ductile (Mo) was 
responsible for this behavior. Higher resistance for crack propagation through grain 
interiors increased the toughness of this alloy [25]. 
3.3.2 Elevated temperature compression properties 
Table 4 shows the peak stress values of alloys 0Nb and 26Nb compressed at 
1300 oC and a strain rate of 5 × 10-3 s-1. Alloy 26Nb exhibited significantly improved 
(increased by about 32%) average peak stress than alloy 0Nb. 
Fig. 5 shows the SEM-BSE images of the microstructure at the center of vertical 
sections of alloys 0Nb and 26Nb after compression. As shown by the black arrows, 
the compression direction is almost parallel to the height direction of the image. The 
(Mo) was significantly flattened along the direction perpendicular to the compression 
axis in both alloys. Sturm et al. [26] found the tensile yield strength of Mo-1.7Si alloy 
(comprised of (Mo) and a little Mo 3Si) tested at 1093 oC/2.2 × 10-3 s-1 was 275 MPa 
(just one available data ). Obviously , (Mo) was easily deformed in the current 
compression tests. Intermetallic phases , including Mo3Si, Mo5SiB2 and Nb 5Si3, were 
also rearranged along this direction. However, their granular appearance, as outlined 
by the red dashed line s, was still similar to that in as-HPed alloys. This indicates that 
no obvious bulk deformation occurred in these intermetallic phases. This is consistent 
with their excellent elevated temperature strengths. For example, the [100] -oriented 
Mo3Si monocrystal has a yield strength of 700 MPa (just one available data) when 
compressed at 1325 oC/1 × 10-3 s-1 [27]. The [021] -oriented Mo5SiB2 monocrystal 
yields at 650 MPa (just one available data) when compressed at 1 500 oC/1 × 10-5 s-1 
[28]. As a result, the deformation mechanism of these strong intermetallic phases 
should be boundary sliding. Finally, it can be inferred that the main deformation 
mechanisms for these two alloys were bulk deformation of (Mo) and the boundary 
sliding of intermetallic phases. As indicated by the white arrows in Fig. 5, 
microcracks mainly distributed in the phase boundaries can be found. The formations

As (Mo) was the softest constituent in both alloys, its deformation behavior is the 
controlling factor for the elevated temperature strength of the two alloys. Based on 
above-mentioned deformation mechanism, the deformation behavior of (Mo) was 
mainly affected by two factors : intrinsic strength and the deformation constraint from 
the surrounding strong intermetallic phases. In alloy 26Nb, many Nb atoms were 
introduced into the Mo crystal lattice, which strengthened the (Mo) intrinsically by 
solution strengthening. Chan et al. [29] studied the plastically constraint of ductile 
phases by stro ng intermetallic phases and found that the amount of constraint on 
ductile phases was exacerbated by their decreasing size, as the mean free path 
between two intermetallic phases became shorter. The linear intercept method was 
used to measure the phase size of (Mo) in the two alloys. For the as -HPed alloys, the 
(Mo) phases were 1.7 ± 0.5 m and 1.2 ± 0.3 m for alloys 0Nb and 26Nb 
respectively . After deformation, (Mo) bands in alloy 26Nb were much thinner 
thickness than in alloy 0Nb ( 1.4 ± 0.3 vs. 0.8 ± 0.2 m). Thus, although alloy 26Nb 
had a higher (Mo) content, the accompanying decrease in its phase size increased its 
constraint. Different from the intactness of the deformed (Mo) phases in alloy 0Nb, 
some microcracks, indicated by red arrows in Fig. 5(b), were observed in the interiors 
of the deformed (Mo) in alloy 26Nb. This showed that (Mo) in alloy 26Nb had greater 
resistance to deformation due to Nb solution strengthening and larger constraint. Also, 
substitution of Mo 3Si by stronger Mo 5SiB2 and maybe the solution strengthening of 
Nb in intermetallic phases (further verification was needed) contributed to 
strengthening alloy 26Nb. Finally, compared with alloy 0Nb, alloy 26Nb had a 
significantly improved elevated temperature compression strength.

Fig. 7 shows the SEM-BSE images and corresponding XRD and EDX results 
conducted on the scale surfaces of alloys 0Nb and 26Nb after oxidation for 5h at 1300 
oC. A SiO2 scale was observed to cover the surface of alloy 0Nb after 5 h oxidation 
completely. Subsequent analyses on the cross section of the scale showed a MoO 2 
layer beneath the SiO2 outer layer. The obvious MoO 2 diffraction peaks in the XRD 
spectrum indicated a thin outer SiO2 layer. However, not only SiO2, identified by the 
EDX result, but also plenty of Nb 2O5 was found on the scale surface of alloy 26Nb. 
Many channels were observed on the scale surface. 
Fig. 8 shows SEM-BSE images of cross-sections of the scales of alloys 0Nb and 
26Nb oxidized at 1300 oC for 5 h. Table 5 shows the corresponding chemical 
compositions of various phases in Fig. 8. The scale of alloy 0Nb presents a three-layer 
structure (a continuous and compact SiO2 outer layer, a MoO 2 middle layer and 
interior oxidation zone (IOZ)) with a total thickness of about 75 ± 3 m. The 
formation of the protective SiO2 outer layer as reactions (1) -(3) restrained the inward 
diffusion of oxygen and resulted in the decrease of oxygen partial pressure to just 
being able to oxidize Mo to MoO 2 as reactions (4)-(6) rather than stable MoO 3 [31]. 
With increased oxidation depth, the oxygen partial p ressure continued to be reduced 
to just being able to oxidize the more active Si and B elements, giving the IOZ [32].

As shown in the enlarged view of IOZ (Fig. 8(a1)), (Mo) islands were still present, 
and the appearance was similar to the as -HPed alloy . EDX analyses conducted in 
areas surrounding these (Mo) islands, that is, the sites of original intermetallic phases,

partial oxidation of Mo3Si and Mo5SiB2 according to reactions (7) and (8). 
As shown in Fig. 8(b), the scale of alloy 26Nb after oxidation at 1300 oC for 5 h 
had a thickness of 1.6 ± 0.1 mm, and possessed a three -layer structure: loosened 
Nb2O5 outer layer, SiO2+Nb2O5 middle layer with many channels and Nb 2O5+MoO3 
inner layer. There is a much higher coefficient of oxygen diffusion in Nb2O5 than that 
in silica glass : the coefficient of oxygen diffu sion in Nb 2O5 (8.7 × 10-11 cm2/s) is 
almost 1000 times higher than that in silica glass (1.8 × 10-14 cm2/s) at 1000 oC [12]. 
Thus, unlike SiO2, the Nb2O5 itself could not restrain the inward diffusion of oxygen 
effectively , and then failed in supplying sufficient oxidation resistance. Also, channels 
in the scale acted as fast pipes for inward diffusion of oxygen to accelerate the 
oxidation of matrix alloy. Moreover, different from MoO 3, which evaporated beyond 
750 oC rapidly [32], the formation of this non -volatile Nb2O5 certainly prevented the 
SiO2 for covering the alloy completely. Finally, alloy 26Nb had very poor oxidation 
resistance at 1300 oC. To improve the oxidation resistance of the Nb macroalloyed 
Mo-Si-B based alloys, further work should be carried out to increase the Si addition 
or fabricate oxidation resistant coating by pack cementation process. 
4 Conclusions 
Bulk alloys with the compositions of Mo -12Si-10B (0Nb) and 
Mo-26Nb-12Si-10B (26Nb) were successfully fabricated by MA and HP processes. 
The constituent phases of alloy 0Nb were (Mo), Mo3Si and Mo5SiB2. However, alloy 
26Nb was free of Mo3Si and had higher (Mo) content and a little Nb5Si3. Unlike the 
lower relative density of alloy 0Nb (95.8%), alloy 26Nb had an almost completely 
densified microstructure. Due to its higher relative density and larger amount of 
ductile (Mo), alloy 26Nb had a significantly improved fracture toughness than alloy 
0Nb (8.84 ± 0.17 vs. 6.77 ± 0.20 MPa·m1/2). As a result of the solution strengthening 
effect of Nb and more significant constraint of the strong intermetallic phases on the

complete coverage of SiO 2 scale, alloy 26Nb presents very poor oxidation resistance 
at 1300 oC.
