1. Introduction
With the development of aerospace and marine industries, excellent 
heat-softening resistance has been required for the materials used in 
turbine engine components [ 1 – 6 ], such as blades and rotors, in order to 
achieve higher work efficiency [ 7 , 8 ]. Since conventional high- 
temperature alloys can no longer meet these demands, thus the devel -
opment of novel alloy systems with excellent high-temperature perfor -
mance is extremely urgent. Refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) have 
shown excellent heat-softening resistance at elevated temperature 
[ 9 – 11 ], but the specific strength of RHEAs is relatively low due to the 
high density of the constituent elements [ 12 ]. Our previous work has 
developed novel MoNbVTa
x 
RHEAs with good high-temperature 
strength and heat-softening resistance [ 13 ]. The evolution of micro -
structure and properties at different Ta contents has been investigated in 
detail. It is found that MoNbVTa
0.5 
RHEAs exhibited a typical dendritic 
structure, with Ta and Mo preferentially accumulated in the dendritic 
region. The alloy exhibited the optimal high-temperature performance 
among MoNbVTa
x 
RHEAs, which showed a yield strength up to 697 MPa 
and a plastic strain over 35.4 % when deformed at 1200 
◦
C. However, 
similar to other RHEAs, the density of MoNbVTa
0.5 
is high (~9.65 g/ 
cm
3
). Thus, it is essential to further decrease the density of the alloy.
Al is generally applied to decrease the density of RHEAs [ 14 – 21 ]. For 
example, Bhardwaj et al. [ 15 ] reported that doping Al into TiZrNbHf 
alloys resulted in a significant improvement in the mechanical proper -
ties. The compressive yield strength was increased from 310 MPa to 
1245 MPa and the density was reduced from 8.681 g/cm
3 
to 7.785 g/ 
cm
3

increased from 1228 MPa to 1391 MPa. The large differences in atomic 
size between doped Al and other elements should facilitate the solid 
solution strengthening effects, which introduces a more severe lattice 
distortion and subsequently higher strength and hardness [ 17 – 19 ]. 
Furthermore, during the high-temperature deformation, such strong 
bonding should also help to improve the heat-softening resistance [ 20 ].
In the present study, Al has been added into the previous MoN -
bVTa
0.5

specific strength. The low-density, high-strength MoNbVTa

properties with various Al content were investigated in detail.

2.2. Experimental details
The alloy ingots of these four compositions were prepared using 
vacuum arc melting in a water-cooled copper hearth under an argon 
atmosphere. Prior to melting, the raw materials (Mo, Nb, Ta, V, and Al) 
with a purity of 99.99 % were mechanically ground to remove the sur -
face oxidation and then ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol for 3 min. 
High-purity Ti was employed as a degassing substance to remove re -
sidual oxygen in the vacuum chamber. In order to ensure chemical ho -
mogeneity, the button ingots were remelted for six times.
Rectangular specimens with dimensions of 5 × 5 × 10 mm were 
machined from the button ingots. All surfaces were mechanically pol -
ished to a mirror-like finish. The microstructure were characterized 
using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD-6000, Shimadzu) with Cu-K α radi -
ation and a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Nova NanoSEM 450) 
equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS) and a back -
scatter electron (BSE) detector. Elemental micro-segregation was char -
acterized by electron probe micro-analysis using an EPMA-8050G 
operating at an accelerating voltage of 15 keV. Vickers microhardness

Figur 1 presents the XRD patterns of all four alloys, which reveals 
that all samples exhibit a similar monolithic BCC solid solution phase. 
With increasing Al content, the diffraction peaks gradually shift to the 
right side, indicating a decrease in the lattice constant due to the solid 
solution of Al. Fig. 2 shows the backscattered electron images of these 
four alloys. It can be seen that the specimens with different Al contents 
exhibit a similar typical dendritic microstructure, consisting of two 
parts: the preferentially formed dendritic region (bright area) and the 
subsequently formed inter-dendritic region (dark area). It is noted that 
the average size of dendrites firstly decreases and then increases with 
higher Al content. On the one hand, the addition of Al should increase 
the number of nuclei during the solidification process, leading to a 
reduction in dendritic size, which is consistent with the experimental 
results from previous studies [ 26 ]. On the other hand, the mixing 
enthalpy between Al and other refractory elements is very negative, thus 
a high Al content should enhance the binding effects between elements 
in the melt. As a result, the degree of supercooling during solidification 
decreases and the solidification time increases, giving rise to a contin -
uous growth of the dendritic microstructure [ 27 ]. The secondary den -
dritic spacing of the above alloys was also measured, which were 31.7 
μ m in Al0.3, 27.3 μ m in Al0.5, 25.1 μ m in Al0.7 and 21.6 μ m in Al1.
Since the melting point and density of the constituent elements vary 
a lot in the present alloy systems, the elemental segregation should be 
considered. Fig. 3 shows the elemental distribution of Al0.5 analyzed by

3.2. Mechanical properties at ambient temperature
The hardness of Al0.3, Al0.5, Al0.7 and Al1 were measured to be 
619.7 HV, 649.2 HV, 654.6 HV and 670.7 HV, respectively, which in -
dicates that doping with Al can improve the hardness in MoNbVTa alloy 
system. Such phenomenon may be attributed to the decreased degree of 
elemental segregation and the secondary dendritic spacing with 
increasing Al addition. Fig. 5 (a) illustrates the true compressive stress- 
strain curves of MoNbVTa
0.5
Al
x 
alloys at ambient temperature. It is 
noted that all samples exhibit high strength but limited plasticity. Fig. 5
(b) presents the yield strength and plastic strain of the samples with 
various Al contents. It can be seen that the yield strength is improved as 
Al content increases, but the plastic strain shows the opposite trend. The 
addition of Al promotes the lattice distortion of the alloy, leading to a 
significant improvement in strength. The more severe lattice distortion 
should interact with the dislocation stress field, commonly known as 
elastic mismatching. Displaced solute atoms act as the sources of 
spherically symmetric stress fields, generating tensile stresses (such as 
vacancy defects) or compressive stresses depending on the size of the 
solute atoms compared to the solvent ones. When dislocation motion is 
hindered by the distortion region created by solute atoms, external en -
ergy is required to sustain dislocation movement [ 21 , 29 ]. Moreover, on 
the one hand, the mixing enthalpies between Al and each refractory 
elements are more negative compared with the values between these 
refractory elements themselves, which indicates a stronger bonding 
between the atomic pairs in Al-containing alloys [ 30 ]. On the other 
hand, the interaction between Al and refractory metals with p and 
d hybridization orbitals enhances the directional bonding within the 
hard-yet-brittle BCC structure, resulting in difficult activation of spiral 
dislocations and subsequent low ductility at ambient temperature. These 
findings indicate that doping with Al degrades the plasticity at ambient
