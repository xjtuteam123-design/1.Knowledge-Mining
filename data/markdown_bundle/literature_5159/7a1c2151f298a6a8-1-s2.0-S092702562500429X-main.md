 A B S T R A C T
This paper investigates the relationship between microstructural features and small-angle scattering (SAS) 
patterns in Ni-based superalloys using a combined phase-field and SAS simulation approach coupled with mi-
crostructure analyses. The simulated SAS patterns accurately capture key experimental observations previously 
reported in the literature, including the time-dependent transition from circular to square-shaped precipitates 
and the development of anisotropic SAS patterns. Importantly, our analysis reveals the correlations between 
characteristic length scales extracted from SAS profiles and microstructural descriptors, such as precipitate 
size and inter-precipitate distance. These findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the link between 
SAS profiles and microstructure evolution in Ni-based superalloys, offering valuable insights for materials 
characterization and design.1
1. Introduction
High-performing metallic alloys designed to operate at high temper-
atures, such as nickel-based superalloys for instance, exhibit excellent 
thermo-mechanical properties due to precipitation strengthening. In 
these alloys, the second-phase particles are generally precipitated in 
the solid-solution matrix to promote microstructural stability and en-
dow the material with superior properties at high temperatures. The 
physical properties of these alloys are strongly influenced by the crys-
tallographic character, the volume fraction, the shape, and the spatial 
and size distributions of the precipitates, all of which stem from the 
kinetics of precipitation [1,2]. This kinetics is influenced by both the 
precipitate/matrix interfacial energy and the elastic interaction energy 
between precipitates [3–7]. 
Many experimental and modeling techniques have been developed 
to study precipitation kinetics in metallic alloys, see for instance [8] for 
a comprehensive review. Of particular interest, Small-Angle Scattering 
(SAS) is a non-destructive experimental technique, allowing for in-
situ and real-time measurements of those kinetics without the need of 
sample preparation, simplifying the experimental process. This tech-
nique is based on measuring fluctuations of the scattering factor of 
a sample. Both structural and spatial information can be obtained 
from the scattering intensity in the reciprocal space. SAS has been

on simplified models and approximations to interpret the complex and 
high dimensional patterns; these models are used to extract low-order 
microstructural measures such as interparticle interference, especially 
for high volume fraction precipitates [20]. 
Various models, often simple, are routinely used to extract the 
characteristic lengths of microstructures (e.g. the average precipitate 
size or the microstructure wavelength) from the scattering intensity 
𝐼(𝐪,𝑡 ). One such model is based on Porod’s law [21] for the analysis 
of 1D SAS spectra. Porod’s law describes the asymptotic behavior 
of the scattering intensity 𝐼(𝑞) for large scattering wave numbers 𝑞
and is used to characterize surface properties, i.e. sharp vs. diffuse 
interfaces [22,23], roughness or curvature [18]. For instance, for a 
sharp interface, Porod’s law predicts that the scattering intensity scales 
as 𝐼(𝑞) ∝ 𝑆Porod𝑞−4 for large 𝑞, where 𝑆Porod is the average surface area 
of the precipitates. Conversely, for rough surfaces, if the roughness can 
be described by a fractal dimension 𝑑 between 2 and 3, then Porod’s law 
predicts that the scattering intensity scales as 𝐼(𝑞) ∝ 𝑆Porod𝑞−6−𝑑 [24]. 
Similarly, another commonly used method for analyzing SAS patterns is 
the Guinier model [9]. This model is used to evaluate the mean radius 
of gyration of the precipitates by fitting the scattering intensity to an 
equation of the form: ln [𝐼(𝑞,𝑡 )] = ln( 𝐼0) − (𝑅2
𝑔∕3)𝑞2, where 𝑅𝑔 is the 
radius of gyration. Such analysis is typically done only for the low-𝑞
portion of the 1D SAS spectrum. Linear (Guinier) scaling suggests that 
the system is essentially monodispersed. Finally, so-called Krakty plots, 
which are generally used for studying macromolecules and polymers, 
less so for metals, plot the scattering intensity as 𝐼(𝑞) ×𝑞2 vs. 𝑞 (instead 
of simply 𝐼(𝑞) vs. 𝑞). This representation divides out the decay of the 
scattering intensity. By analyzing the shape and features of the Kratky 
plot, researchers can infer important information on the microstructure. 
For instance, for aluminum (Al) alloys, it was used to identify the 
volume fraction of precipitates [13,14,25]. 
These models and their interpretations are based on various as-
sumptions, e.g. uniformly distributed precipitates or the absence of 
long-range correlations, but they are often used outside the domain 
of validity of such approximations [11,15,26]. Additionally, when ex-
ercised, these models make use of simplifications and idealization of 
the microstructure as made up of elementary-shaped precipitates (e.g.
dilute spheres, perfect rods or platelets) to extract microstructural 
characteristic lengths. To remedy such approximation, various strate-
gies combining simulated diffraction with mesoscale simulations have 
been proposed to compare directly the simulated scattering intensity 
to a microstructure analysis [14,15,27,28]. For example, Gille [27] 
presented a linear simulation model for interpreting SAS experiments 
of random two-phase systems, focusing on the distribution laws of 
random distances between phase interfaces. Duchêne et al. [29] studied 
multi-scale aggregates of nanoparticles (as in alumina catalyst supports) 
with 1D SAS spectra. Scattered intensities were analyzed and charac-
terized through form and structure factors or by means of multi-scale 
Boolean models of spheroids. Welborn and Detsi [30] characterized the 
morphological features of nanoporous materials, such as the pore size 
distribution, pore curvature, and specific surface area, using different 
mathematical, idealized models. Schmidt-Rohr [28] presented an effi-
cient numerical approach based on the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) 
algorithm, which was implemented to compute 1D SAS spectra of 2D 
or three-dimensional (3D) idealized microstructures. He applied this 
method to systems of long parallel particles at high densities, which 
are simple to treat by this method but not by competing approaches. 
Schmidt-Rohr and Chen [31] have later on simulated SAS curves for 
spherical-cluster material models, polymer and network structures, and 
analyzed the origin of correlation peaks on SAS curves for these specific 
microstructures. For two- or three-phase materials simply modeled with 
Gaussian Random Fields (GRFs), length scale parameters and volume 
fractions were predicted with accuracy from 1D SAS simulated with a 
fast FFT-GPU method [32] using a machine-learning-based framework. 
For more complicated microstructures, some of the examples are using 
mesoscale methods such as the phase-field method [33] to generate 
statistics on the microstructural features and compare those statistics 
with experimental in-situ SAXS measurements [34,35]. 
To complement these studies, phase-field simulations [36–41] are 
commonly used to generate realistic shapes and spatial distributions of 
𝛾 ′ precipitates in the 𝛾 matrix during isothermal conditions and study 
the evolution of precipitate size, shape (aspect ratios) and deviation 
from cuboidal shape of 𝛾 ′ precipitates [37,38,42]. However, such stud-
ies are rarely combined with SAS analysis of those microstructures, 
rather they are analyzed with statistical descriptors of the microstruc-
ture. Nguyen et al. [35] used phase-field simulation to study the evo-
lution of a synthetic 𝛾/𝛾 ′ microstructure under a tensile load and ana-
lyzed 2D and 3D moment invariants of these microstructures. Sudbrack 
et al. [43] focused their study on the spacing between the precipitates 
and pair correlation functions. Similarly, 2-point correlation functions 
with reduce-order models like PCA (Principal Component Analysis) 
were extracted by Yabansu et al. [44] from phase-field simulations. 
For Ni-based superalloys, the linkages between process variables (aging 
time and aging temperature) and higher-order representations of the 
microstructure were provided [45]. 
Our work here is intended to link SAS analysis to phase-field simu-
lations for a more comprehensive analysis of the microstructure and 
extend these types of studies to more complex microstructures seen 
during precipitation kinetics in Ni-based superalloys. The phase-field 
model utilized is based on the Kim-Kim-Suzuki (KKS) [46] interface 
model with four order parameters related to antiphase boundaries and 
anisotropic (cubic) and heterogeneous elasticity. We performed both 
2D and 3D simulations of precipitation kinetics by varying interfacial 
energy and elastic properties to generate various times series of coars-
ened and rafted microstructures with different precipitates characteris-
tics in terms of sizes and morphologies. To keep the analysis tractable, 
we limited our phase-field model to elastic rafted microstructures (with 
negative misfit strain and heterogeneous anisotropic elasticity) as re-
ported for example in [47] and we did not consider creep loading. 
From the generated microstructures, we simulated 1D and 2D SAS 
patterns with Schmidt-Rohr FFT-based approach [28] to study the time 
evolutive features of the generated Ni-based superalloy microstructure 
and their relation to the evolution of the SAS patterns. The results 
on time-dependent correlation peaks were analyzed in light of spatial 
microstructure statistics and metrics [48,49]. We studied both the 
morphology and spatial arrangements effects in these alloys and the 
linkages between SAS profiles and different microstructure features, 
like precipitate size distributions or sharp vs. diffuse interface. We 
also performed PCA to gain further insights into the fundamental 
microstructural fingerprints encoded in SAS patterns.

While the above results show correlations between the scattering 
features of the SAS patterns with some microstructural features in 
terms of precipitate morphology and spatial correlations, the analyzed 
features are not necessarily unique nor do they correspond to only one 
specific structure. For instance, in the case of mesoporous materials, 
Gommes [71] showed that different stochastic models of microstruc-
tures can actually fit the same SAS experimental data, because a 
matching correlation function does not unambiguously define a unique 
microstructure. It was also shown that different multiscale Boolean 
models of spheroids can share very similar SAS curves [29], as well 
as different poly-dispersed spherical or ellipsoidal precipitates [72]. 
Additionally, the 2D SAS data exhibits pronounced anisotropy (see 
e.g. Fig. 4) as evident from the morphological analysis in Figs. 5–7. This 
anisotropic behavior significantly complicates the interpretation of 1D 
scattering intensity profiles 𝐼(𝑞), which fail to fully capture the complex 
information encoded in the correlation peaks or unique microstruc-
tural features beyond simple correlation lengths. The anisotropy also 
affects the FWHM measurements of the 1D scattering intensities in Fig. 
8(b), resulting in broad SAS profiles. To accurately account for these 
anisotropic effects, an orientation-dependent analysis of 𝐼(𝑞) would be 
necessary to reconcile these effects. While the impact of anisotropy 
would be minimal for Cases 1 through 4 (since they all present a uni-
form and isotropic distribution of precipitates), it becomes particularly 
pronounced in Case 5 (due to the formation of rafted precipitates), 
highlighting the importance of considering anisotropy in diffraction 
data analysis for complex microstructures.

example in Figs. 1 and 2. Hence, a Porod’s law was obtained from the 
slope of the scattering intensity 𝐼(𝑞) at high 𝑞 in Fig. 5. Considering

better characterized in the plot: 𝐼(𝑞) ×𝑞4 (in logarithmic scale) vs. 𝑞2, 
see Fig. 13(b), is due to the presence of a diffuse interface causing 
a depletion of high-angle scattering [22–24,73]. Fig. 13(c) shows the 
effect of the diffuse interface of the phase-field model on 2D scattering 
patterns. The depletion of high-angle scattering is also observed in all 
the directions of the (𝑞⟨10⟩, 𝑞⟨01⟩)–plane. Finally, Fig. 13(d) shows the 
angular dependence of the curvature parameter 𝑆SAS with the angle 𝛩
accounting for the diffuse interface. While clear differences are seen 
in the 1D and 2D scattering intensity for high 𝑞, we observe that the 
angular dependence is almost unchanged when comparing the sharp 
vs. diffuse interface cases, with a maximum approaching 0.8 at 𝛩 ≈ 50 ◦. 
This result leads to the conclusion that using sharp or diffuse interface 
has very little influence on studying the precipitate morphology using 
𝑆SAS. 
4.5. Connection with SAXS/SANS experiments
Fig. 14 shows experimental 2D-SAXS spectra and TEM images from 
Paris et al. [18] and Fratzl [11] for Ni-based alloy systems for both 
cuboid and rafted microstructures. A visual comparison between these 
experimental SAXS patterns and the simulated patterns for the same 
types of microstructures displayed in Fig. 4 shows a strong qualita-
tive agreement between the two. For instance, change of the aspect 
ratio and loss of the cubic symmetry for 2D patterns for the rafted 
microstructures is correctly captured by the simulated SAXS patterns. 
However, due to several experimental factors, some characteristics of 
the experimental SAXS/SANS patterns differ from the simulated ones. 
The finite 𝑞-range of the experimental instrument and its resolution 
limits due to instrument configuration (resolution smearing), beamstop, 
limited size and irreducible pixel size of detectors, and the presence 
of incoherent scattering and background noise all convolve into the 
SAXS data to produce complex scattering profiles that are not directly 
comparable to the idealized, simulated SAXS patterns. The simulation-
based data analysis approaches presented here could be improved 
to address these real-world experimental challenges by incorporating 
realistic instrumental effects into the simulation workflow. This adap-
tation could involve integrating resolution functions and background 
corrections to modify the idealized simulated SAS patterns, effectively 
mimicking experimental conditions.
5. Conclusions
This paper examines the usage of SAS patterns for the quantifica-
tion of a comprehensive list of microstructural features in Ni-based 
superalloys using a combined phase-field, SAS simulation approach, 
and microstructure analysis. The simulated SAS patterns obtained from 
the phase-field model accurately captured key experimental observa-
tions reported in the literature, including the time-dependent transition 
from circular to square-shaped precipitates and the development of 
anisotropic SAS patterns with a specific symmetries, reflecting the 
microstructure’s symmetry. Our analysis revealed a strong correlation 
between characteristic length scales extracted from SAS profiles and mi-
crostructural descriptors, such as precipitate size and inter-precipitate 
distance, obtained from the autocorrelation function analysis and the 
Hoshen–Kopelman algorithm. PCA analysis on the 2D scattering in-
tensities showed the ability of SAS patterns to uniquely differentiate 
precipitation kinetics. This study also demonstrated the sensitivity of 
SAS analysis to the presence of diffuse interfaces, highlighting the need 
to account for this factor in interpreting SAS data. These findings pro-
vide a comprehensive understanding of the link between SAS profiles
