Abstract 
The aim of this work is ﬁrstly to optimize T6 heat-treatment of low-pressure sand-cast Mg–10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy, and then systematically 
investigate the mechanical behavior of the T6-treated alloy from room temperature to 300 °C. It turned out that the optimum T6 heat-treatments 
for the tested alloy are 525 °C × 12 h + 225 °C × 14 h and 525 °C × 12 h + 250 °C × 12 h which integrated age-hardening and tensile properties 
into account, respectively. The strength of the T6-treated alloy indicates obvious anomalous temperature dependence from room temperature 
to 300 °C, namely both ultimate tensile strength and yield strength of the tested alloy ﬁrstly increase with tensile temperature, and then 
decrease as temperature increases further. Elongation increased with temperature monotonously. The tensile fracture mode of the tested alloy 
changes from transgranular fracture to intergranular fracture with the increasing of test temperature.

1. Introduction 
Demand for weight reduction of automobile and aerospace 
industries are growing into the world hotspot recently, magne- 
sium alloy is becoming one of the crucial engineering mate- 
rials on account of its low density, high elastic modulus, high 
speciﬁc strength and stiffness [1] . Rare earth metals (RE), 
which has been testiﬁed are the most effective elements to 
enhance the strength performance of Mg [2–4] . The strength 
of these Mg–RE alloys is higher than conventional Mg and Al 
alloys which is achieved mainly by precipitation hardening. 
Mg–Gd–Y system, what the previous researches have indi- 
cated, is one of the most promising options because of its 
rapid age-hardening response [5–9] , higher room temperature 
speciﬁc strength and better creep resistance than conventional 
Mg and Al alloys, including WE43 and WE54 [10–14] . Based 
on these results in Refs. [5–14] , the Mg–10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr al-

2. Experimental procedures 
The Mg–10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr (GW103K, wt.%) were produced 
with pure metal Mg and Mg–25Gd (wt.%), Mg–25Y (wt.%), 
Mg–30Zr (wt.%) master alloys. The details of the process 
were as follows. Firstly, pure metal Mg was melted in a high- 
frequency induction furnace with appropriate magnesium cov- 
ering ﬂux. Then Mg–25Y and Mg–25Gd master alloys were 
added into the Mg melt when the melt temperature reached 
730–750 °C followed by manual stirring for 2 min. The melt 
was subsequently held at 780 °C for 40 min to allow the RE 
to completely dissolve. The preheated Mg–30Zr master alloy 
was added at ∼ 800 °C. After it was puriﬁed by 1.5% reﬁn- 
ing ﬂux, the melt was then held at 780 °C for 40 min and was 
low pressure cast in sand molds of Ø3 0 mm × 200 mm [1] . 
The actual chemical composition of the alloy (Mg–9.80Gd–
3.36Y–0.42Zr (wt.%)) was determined by an inductively cou- 
pled plasma analyzer (PerkinElmer, Plasma-400). 
On the basic of the experimental results in the Refs. 
[15–17] , the solid-solution temperature for the tested spec- 
imens was determined to be at 525 °C and the solid-solution 
time was 12 h. The air-cooling treatment was carried out on 
the tested specimens after solid-solution. Subsequently, the 
specimens were artiﬁcially aged respectively at 225 °C and 
250 °C for different time in aging furnace. The hardness test 
of the T6 treated specimens was taken on Vickers hardness 
(HV) testing, which using 49 N load and holding time of 15 s 
to check the aging response. And then the tensile behavior 
at temperature range from 25 °C to 300 °C of the optimal 
T6-treated alloys was measured. 
The tensile specimens were machined with the load axes 
along the cast direction (CD) of the sand-cast bars. Tensile 
properties of the magnesium alloys were performedat the ini- 
tial strain rate of 5 × 10 −4 s −1 on Zwick/Roell Z020 tensile 
machine at different temperatures with cylindrical specimen 
with a marked dimension of 33 mm gauge length and 5 mm 
diameter. 
The microstructure of the tested alloys was analyzed by 
optical microscopy (OM, Zeiss Axio Observer A1). Phase 
composition was characterized by X-ray diffraction using 
Ni-ﬁltered Cu-K-alpha radiation at the scan rate of 4 °/min. 
The morphology of phase and corresponding diffraction pat- 
tern was detected by transmission electron microscope (TEM, 
JEOL2100F). The fracture surfaces of the tensile specimens 
were observed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI 
QUANTA FEG 250).

3.3. Fractography 
Fig. 6 shows the tensile fracture surfaces of the T6 treated 
GW103K alloy tested at different temperatures. It is noticed 
that there are many tearing ridges and cleavage planes in 
the tensile fracture surfaces of the peak-aged (225 °C × 14 h) 
specimens which tested at room temperature. Likewise, a few 
dimples and microporouses are perceived. As a consequence, 
the fracture mode of the peak-aged (225 °C × 14 h) alloy at 
ambient temperature is transgranular quasi-cleavage fracture. 
On account of the ductility improvement of the alloy, there 
are less cleavage planes and more grain boundaries and dim- 
ples are observed in the fracture surface of the specimens 
tested at 200 °C. The fracture surface of the specimens tested 
at 300 °C, by contrast, is primarily made up of massive grain 
boundaries and a few long tear ridges, exhibiting distinct in- 
tergranular fracture mechanism. That is, the fracture mode 
of the peak-aged GW103K alloy changes from transgranular 
fracture to intergranular fracture, with the increase of tensile 
temperature. 
Fig. 7 shows the microstructure of the fracture surface pro- 
ﬁles of the peak-aged GW103K alloy tested at different tem- 
peratures. After tensile testing at different temperatures, there 
are secondary crack morphologies in the entire specimens. 
The tensile specimens tested at room temperature showed dis- 
tinctly transcrystalline secondary cracks (as shown in Fig. 7 a) 
which indicated that the fracture mode of the T6 treated alloy 
was primarily transcrystalline fracture at ambient temperature. 
With increasing the test temperature, intergranular secondary 
cracks come to emerge in the fracture of the specimens tested 
at 200 °C (showed in Fig. 7 b) which indicated the trend of 
transforming to intergranular fracture mechanism in terms of 
fracture mode. It is consistent with the above-mentioned frac- 
ture surface analysis. In the microstructure of the tensile frac- 
ture surface proﬁles of the specimens tested at 300 °C (shown 
in Fig. 7 c), there are cracks even small cavities observed on 
grain boundaries in the vertical direction of the tensile direc- 
tion. Along grain boundaries, the secondary cracks are pri- 
marily formed which indicated the fracture mode of the al- 
loys tested at 300 °C is intergranular fracture. Furthermore, 
ﬁne twins are found in the entire specimens. In the hcp mag- 
nesium alloy, twinning is a crucial deformation mechanism, 
in which, at room temperature, ﬁve independent slip systems 
required for arbitrary shape changes are not easily activated 
[21] . The secondary cracks are in signiﬁcant connection with 
twinning in the study. The microcracks can initiate and ex- 
pand along the twin and form a transgranular crack.

extra slip planes is likely to one of reasons on the anoma-

increased. Nonetheless, up to now, there are few studies on 
the anomalous strength behavior of UTS and YS. As a re- 
sult, the mechanism of anomalous strength behavior is the 
focus point of future research which is studied by such as 
calculating stacked faults and observing the evolution of pre- 
cipitated phase under different tensile temperatures with high- 
resolution transmission electron microscopy etc. 
5. Conclusions 
This study ﬁrstly optimized the T6 heat treatment of low- 
pressure sand-cast Mg–10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy, and then sys- 
tematically studied the mechanical behavior of the T6-treated 
alloy from room temperature to 300 °C. The results obtained 
are summarized below: 
(1) The optimum T6 heat treatments with air-cooling pro- 
cess for the Mg–10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy are 525 °C ×
12 h + 225 °C × 14 h and 525 °C × 12 h + 250 °C × 12 h 
under different aging temperatures. 
(2) The T6 treated Mg–10Gd–3Y–0.5Zr alloy shows signif- 
icant anomalous strength behavior from room temper- 
ature to 300 °C, that is, both UTS and YS ﬁrstly in- 
crease with the increase of tensile temperature and then 
decrease when the tensile temperature increases further. 
Comparatively, the elongation increased monotonously 
with increasing the tensile temperature. 
(3) The fracture mode for the T6 treated Mg–10Gd–3Y–
