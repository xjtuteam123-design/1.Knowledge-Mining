ABSTRACT
The microstructure and mechanical properties of 35Co-34Cr-24Ni-7Mo medium entropy alloy, known as MP35N, 
were investigated at different cryogenic temperatures (77 K and 195 K) and compared with those at room 
temperature (295 K). The microstructural features at the mentioned three distinct temperatures were charac -
terized by X-Ray diffraction, electron back skater diffraction analysis and transmission electron microscopy. It 
was found that, compared to room temperature, cryogenic temperatures significantly increased the yield strength 
and tensile strength from 330 MPa and 1300 MPa at 295 K to 711 MPa and 1890 MPa at 77 K, respectively, 
without a noticeable change in elongation. Microstructural investigation revealed that lower deformation tem -
peratures resulted in higher dislocation density, higher stacking fault probability, more mechanical twins, and a 
more homogeneous dislocation structure. Up to a strain of 0.4, no sign of fcc to hcp phase transformation was 
observed in EBSD, TEM images, or SAED patterns, even at 77 K. The higher work hardening due to mechanical 
twinning, increased dislocation density, and lower stacking fault energy, in addition to a more homogeneous 
dislocation structure, postponed the formation of geometric instabilities and maintained a ductile fracture 
mechanism at cryogenic temperatures.
1. Introduction
Medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) have been considered an advanced 
group of alloys due to their attractive mechanical properties, including 
high strength and ductility across a wide temperature range, from 
cryogenic to elevated temperatures [1 – 5] . The enhancement of strength, 
elongation, and fracture toughness at cryogenic temperatures has been 
reported for different HEAs, owing to the formation of mechanical twins 
[6 – 11] . In addition to the role of twinning as a deformation mechanism, 
twin boundaries act as barriers for dislocation glide. The presence of 
twins in the microstructure, therefore, leads to increase strain hardening 
rate, dislocation storage capacity, postponement of plastic instability, 
and improved ductility. The propensity for twinning strongly depends 
on stacking fault energy (SFE) [6,12] , temperature [10,13,14] , grain 
size [12,15,16] , strain rate [17,18] , and strain path [19] .
Among various MEAs, Co-Cr-Ni is reported to have notable me -
chanical properties at cryogenic temperatures [13,20] . Superior 
strength in this alloy can be achieved by fine-tuning the formation of 
different microstructural features, such as a high density of grain 
boundaries, dislocation cells, stacking faults, mechanical twins, and 
Lomer – Cottrell locks [1,3,21,22] . These features can be affected by 
chemical composition. It has been stated that SFE tends to decrease with 
an increase in the Cr and Co content in the alloy [23,24] . Moreover, high 
Cr concentrations and a high Ni/Co ratio can increase lattice distortion 
[24] , which improves strength. According to Wang et al. [24] , the 
non-equiatomic 45Cr-20Co-35Ni alloy exhibits 13 % higher cryogenic 
ductility compared to the equiatomic Cr-Co-Ni alloy. Coury et al. [25]
developed a 45Cr-27.5-Co-27.5Ni alloy with a 50 % improvement in 
yield strength while maintaining the same ductility compared to the 
equiatomic Cr-Co-Ni alloy. Deng et al. [23] proposed two novel non -
equiatomic 50Co-25Cr-25Ni and 40Co-30Cr-30Ni alloys with fcc to hcp 
transformation induced plasticity (TRIP) capability, resulting in higher 
ductility. Enhancing lattice distortion by adding other alloying elements 
such as W [26] , Mo [2,21,27,28] , Ce [29] , Al [30] , and Fe [31] has been 
applied as another strategy for improving the strength and ductility of 
CoCrNi alloys. It has been stated that the addition of Mo up to 5 at%

The 35Co-34Cr-24Ni-7Mo alloy, also known as the MP35N alloy, is 
well known for its various medical and electromagnetic applications as 
an important alloy of the CoNiCrMo group [34,35] . The mechanical 
properties and microstructural features of this alloy have been investi -
gated in a wide range of temperatures. It has been observed that the flow 
stress of this alloy can be subjected to flow softening, due to the 
occurrence of dynamic recrystallization at high temperatures [36] , flow 
serration, because of dynamic strain aging at moderate temperatures 
[36] , and high strain hardening at low temperatures [37 – 40] .
While there are some reports on the relation of the high strain 
hardening rate of this alloy to the formation of plate-like features, i.e. 
mechanical twins and hexagonal martensite [39,40] , more recent

twinning, multiple twinning, de-twinning, and in the necked region, 
nano-grains formed from broken twins, but neither diffraction or 
morphological evidence of martensite phase was detected. Recently the 
ratio of sample thickness to grain size or feature size at cryogenic tem -
perature of this alloy has been investigated by Zhu et. al. [44] . It has 
been found that reduction in feature size and deformation temperature 
by promoting mechanical twinning improve the plasticity and strength. 
It was reported by Cheng et al. [28] that as the tensile strain reaches to 
0.25, stacking faults appear and afterwards, deformation twins and 
dislocation-twin interactions were observed, especially for higher values 
of Mo and larger grain sizes. Therefore, the microstructure character -
ization of this alloy at room temperature deformation is still challenging. 
Moreover, not much information has been provided about the me -
chanical behavior and microstructure evolution of this alloy at cryo -
genic temperatures. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the 
tensile properties and microstructure of the mentioned alloy at different 
cryogenic temperatures and compare them with room temperature.

3. Results
3.1. Mechanical properties
The true stress-strain curves of the CoNiCrMo alloy tested at 77 K, 
195 K, and 295 K are shown in Fig. 1 a. As seen, compared to room 
temperature, the cryogenic temperatures lead to a remarkable increase 
in yield and ultimate strength without disturbing the plasticity of the 
studied alloy. According to this figure, as the tensile temperature de -
creases, the yield strength increases from ~330 MPa to ~711 MPa, and 
the ultimate tensile strength increases from 1301 MPa to 1890 MPa. 
Meanwhile, the tensile ductility (strain to fracture) did not change 
significantly. As depicted in Fig. 1 b, the strain hardening increased with 
reducing temperature. The higher work hardening can be accompanied 
by a transition from dislocation glide as the dominant deformation 
mechanism at room temperature to mechanical twinning, phase trans -
formation and dislocation glide at cryogenic temperatures. Higher work 
hardening due to the formation of mechanical twinning, and a homo -
geneous dislocation structure resulting from lower dynamic recovery at 
cryogenic temperature, can postpone the formation of geometric in -
stabilities or necking, and hence improve the elongation.
As shown in Fig. 2 a, the initial annealed microstructure is covered by 
large grains. In such grains, containing low dislocation density, a long 
mean free path is ahead of dislocations to glide. Therefore, at low 
temperatures and at the initiation of deformation, where the activation 
of secondary slip systems and dynamic recovery are limited, planar slip 
is dominant. Fig. 2 b shows the microstructure of the sample deformed at 
195 K from an area parallel to the tensile direction, which was electro- 
etched, then deformed to the strain of 0.07. Comparing this micro -
structure with the initial microstructure reveals the occurrence of planar 
slip to the mentioned strain, resulting in straight slip lines. Straight slip 
lines also convey a homogeneous deformation within the grain due to 
uniform orientation within each grain.
In other words, in coarse microstructure, deforming at low temper -
atures, the strain hardening resulting from the interaction of dislocations 
and dislocation pile-up behind the obstacles (boundaries) will be post -
poned. As a result, an easy glide region with a low strain hardening rate

3.4. Analytical transmission electron microscopy
To further study the deformation behavior of the mentioned alloy at 
different temperatures, the microstructure of the deformed samples at a 
certain distance from the fracture surface was examined by TEM ( Fig. 7 ). 
Fig. 7 a-b represents the bright-field TEM micrographs of the sample 
deformed at room temperature, i.e. 295 K, in two magnifications. Based 
on the fact that the initial grains are large, only the areas that represent 
all the microstructural features are presented in the micrographs. In 
these microstructures, lots of dislocations are apparent, indicating the 
deformation mechanism is mainly dislocation sliding at room temper -
ature. Considering the amount of strain, i.e., 0.4, it is reasonable that the 
planar arrays of dislocations observed at strains of 0.05 – 0.2 [39,53] , are 
not apparent. Instead, as a result of the activation of several slip systems, 
interacting dislocations together and obstacles, and dynamic recovery, 
dislocation tangles and dislocation walls are formed (shown in images). 
In addition to dislocations, some very thin deformation features can be 
found in the microstructure, which, as mentioned, could be deformation 
twins, stacking faults, or even planar slip, all of which are observed in 
low SFE alloys.
Reducing the deformation temperature from 295 K to 195 K resulted 
in higher numbers of deformation features ( Fig. 7 c-d), which are thicker 
than those observed in 295 K. Although the number of deformation 
features is increased, dislocation structure are still evident in the 
microstructure. As can be seen in Fig. 7 c, the dislocation pile-up behind 
the boundaries of initial annealing twins (AT) acting as the obstacle led

the activation of the second deformation mechanism at this tempera -
ture, i.e., twinning. Deformation twins by introducing new interfaces 
and barriers into the microstructure, reduce the mean free path of dis -

3.5. Fractography
Fig. 8 represents the fractographs of tensile samples tensioned at 
different temperatures. Based on SEM images of the central region of the 
fractured area ( Fig. 8 a-c), the fracture mechanism in all of the samples is 
a ductile fracture, i.e., formation of dimples followed by dimple coa -
lescence. According to the fractured cross sections ( Fig. 8 d-f), it seems 
that the reduction in the area of the sample deformed at room temper -
ature is more than the others. In other words, lower macroscopic 
necking appears with deformation at cryogenic temperatures. The lack 
of necking is also evident in the flow stress curve of sample deformed at 
77 K ( Fig. 1 a), where it suddenly falls to fracture. As mentioned previ -
ously, higher strain hardening due to the activation of mechanical 
twining, lower recovery rate, and higher dislocation density at this 
temperature, postpones the plastic instability until the fracture finally 
occurs by dimples coalesces.( Fig. 9 )
4. Discussion
As was seen, deformation at low temperatures resulted in higher 
yield strength and strain hardening without changing ductility. It is 
known that yield stress is associated with the stress required to move the 
first dislocations, i.e. Peierles-Nabarro stress. This stress, in turn, de -
pends on temperature. By reducing temperature, dislocation width be -
comes narrow, and as a result, critical stress for gliding dislocation rises. 
Therefore, the yield stress becomes higher at cryogenic temperatures. 
The two main parameters that can hinder cross-slip and intensify planar 
slip are low SFE and high Peierls stress of slip systems [51] . Reducing 
temperature not only increases the critical resolved shear stress for 
dislocation glides, but also decreases the SFE. Therefore, dynamic re -
covery, associated with dislocation cross-slip and climb, is restricted at 
lower temperatures. Due to limited recovery, deformation at cryogenic 
temperatures will result in a higher dislocation density, and therefore a 
higher strain hardening rate. In addition to higher dislocation density, it 
has been stated that a relatively homogeneous network of dislocations 
will be formed at cryogenic temperatures [10] . Homogeneous distribu -
tion of dislocation can improve elongation by postponing crack and void 
nucleation from the dislocation piled-up area. Shifting peaks to the left, 
decreasing the FWHM in XRD results, and a more uniform distribution of 
KAM in EBSD support the more homogeneous deformation at cryogenic 
temperatures.
To verify the reduction of SFE by decreasing temperature, the value 
of SFP for individual samples was calculated from XRD results. It has 
been mentioned that stacking fault energy (SFE) and SFP have a 
converse relation [52] . In other words, the higher obtained SFP indicates 
a lower SFE of alloy. As can be seen in Fig. 7 , for different medium and 
high entropy alloys, SFP is increased by reducing temperature. It means 
that SFE rises with temperature, as reported by other researchers [10] . 
Based on the fact that the critical resolved shear stress for twinning is a 
direct function of SFE [11] , decreasing temperature by reducing SFE 
promotes the formation of mechanical twinning during deformation. 
Therefore, it is expected to observe the mechanical twins in

5. Conclusions
In the present study, the tensile properties of the MP35N medium- 
entropy alloy at cryogenic temperatures were investigated and 
compared with the mechanical properties of the alloy at room temper -
ature. It was observed that compared with room temperature, cryogenic 
temperatures lead to a remarkable increase in yield strength, ultimate 
tensile strength, strain hardening, and toughness without disturbing 
plasticity due to postponing necking. According to KAM maps and XRD 
results, decreasing deformation temperature resulted in a higher dislo -
cation density, which is more uniformly distributed in the microstruc -
ture, and a higher stacking fault probability. Although mechanical twins 
and dislocations were observed in the microstructure at all investigated 
temperatures, and both dislocation glide and mechanical twinning are 
active to accommodate plastic strain, the role of twinning becomes more 
pronounced with decreasing temperature. Moreover, no hcp phase, 
generated from the strain-induced transformation of fcc, was detected in 
EBSD, TEM images, or SAED patterns, even at 77 K. According to the 
fractured cross sections, the fracture mechanism in all samples is a 
ductile fracture.
