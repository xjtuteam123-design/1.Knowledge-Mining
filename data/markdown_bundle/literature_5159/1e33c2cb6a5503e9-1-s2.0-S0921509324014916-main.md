ABSTRACT
Herein, Ti – Nb – Zr – Mn alloys were prepared by ball milling and spark plasma sintering. The effect of residual 
carbon on the microstructure, phase composition, and mechanical properties of the as-prepared Ti – Nb – Zr – Mn 
alloys was systematically studied. The incorporation of stearic acid led to the presence of residual carbon, 
thereby resulting in the emergence of the FCC phase, encompassing both FCC Ti and carbon-deficient Ti
2
C. 
Subsequently, the ultimate tensile strength of the Ti – Nb – Zr – Mn alloys increased from 889 ± 13 MPa to 1166 ±
13 MPa, while the plasticity significantly decreased from 23.6 % to 3.8 %. Meanwhile, the elastic modulus and 
hardness exhibited an increase in correlation with higher stearic acid content. The Ti
2
C phase demonstrates 
significantly higher hardness and modulus compared to FCC Ti; however, it is brittle and carries the risk of 
reducing ductility and leading to brittle fracture. To achieve a synergistic match of low elastic modulus, high 
strength, and preferable ductility for metastable β Ti, careful regulation of the carbon content in the Ti alloys is 
essential.
1. Introduction
Titanium (Ti) and its alloys are widely used in aeronautics, astro -
nautics, biomedical, and other engineering applications due to their 
high strength-to-mass ratio and excellent corrosion resistance [ 1 – 3 ]. The 
Elastic modulus of Ti alloys is high, which leads to the “ stress shielding ” 
effect when utilized as an implantable material. The Elastic modulus of 
Ti alloys can be effectively reduced by adding an appropriate number of 
β -stabilizing elements and through suitable thermomechanical pro -
cessing [ 4 – 6 ]. Ti - Nb - based alloys were developed in recent research, 
which have low Elastic modulus, high strength, excellent corrosion 
resistance, and preferable biocompatibility [ 7 , 8 ]. The binary Ti – 38Nb 
alloy exhibits a low modulus of 56 GPa following the processes of cold 
rolling and annealing [ 7 ]. Traditional casting alloys often have the 
disadvantages of uniform distribution and coarse grain size, which can 
be overcome by powder metallurgy (PM) [ 9 – 11 ].
However, the majority of β Ti alloys produced using PM techniques 
have consistently suffered from embrittlement induced by impurity 
contamination. The main sources of carbon contamination usually 
include ball milling and the sintering process [ 12 , 13 ]. Mechanical 
alloying (MA) is a powder preparation technology that involves sub -
jecting metal or alloy powder to prolonged and intense impact and 
collision between powder particles and grinding balls in a high-energy 
ball mill [ 14 ]. This process typically includes the addition of process 
control agents (PCA) to prevent cold welding but unavoidably in -
troduces carbon, which may greatly affect the microstructure and/or 
mechanical properties of Ti alloys. Bolokang et al. [ 15 ] prepared pure Ti 
alloys by ball milling with and without the addition of stearic acid (SA) 
and sintering and found that adding SA can yield the formation of the 
metastable FCC phase, improving the hardness. Nouri et al. [ 16 ] 
investigated the effect of different amounts and types of PCA on the 
porous structure and mechanical properties of Ti – 16Sn – 4Nb alloys and 
found that adding different PCA content led to a different pore 
morphology and porosity. Moreover, the hardness of the sintered tita -
nium alloys using the powders with the addition of ethylene 
bis-stearamide (EBS) were significantly higher than those of SA. Jiang

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Alloy preparation
Ti (~45 μ m), Nb (~15 μ m), Zr (~45 μ m), and Mn (~45 μ m) 
elemental powders with a weight composition of Ti – 24Nb – 4Zr – 2.5Mn 
(abbreviated as TNZM, total mass 100 g) are used as raw materials. All 
the powders were purchased from Changsha Tianjiu Metal Materials 
with a purity higher than 99.9 wt%. The powders were accurately 
weighed and then placed in a stainless-steel tank under an argon at -
mosphere together with a stainless-steel ball of 10 mm diameter. The 
weight ratio of balls to powder was set at 10:1. Ball milling was carried 
out using a high-energy planetary ball mill (QM-QX4) with a rotational 
speed of 400 r/min for 20 h. To prevent cold welding during the ball 
milling process, stearic acid (SA) with a different content (1, 1.5, 2 wt%) 
was added into the TNZM powders as a process control agent (PCA) in 
the glove box, respectively. Another control group without stearic acid 
was set up, and the ball milling speed was controlled to be 300 r/min, 
with a ball milling time of 12 h to obtain the high powder yield rate. The 
milled TNZM powder was loaded into graphite molds and then sintered 
using SPS (LABOX-350) at 30 MPa uniaxial pressure. To avoid a reaction 
between the powder and graphite, a thin layer of carbon paper was 
applied between the mold and the powder. Cylindrical samples with a 
diameter of 20 mm and a height of about 8 mm were obtained after 
sintering. In this work, the control group TNZM alloy without stearic 
acid is named as SA0, and TNZM alloys with different content SA (1, 1.5, 
2 wt%) are named as SA1, SA1.5, and SA2, respectively, according to the 
addition amount of SA.
2.2. Materials characterization
The density of the sintered TNZM series alloys was measured based 
on the Archimedes method. The phase analysis was carried out by an X- 
ray diffractometer (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance) equipped with Cu K α ra -
diation. The bulk samples were rough ground with silicon carbide 
sandpaper of different grits and then polished with a finer-grained silica 
suspension (W2.5). A mixture of 1 % HF and 3 % HNO
3

prepared to etch the alloy samples prior to metallographic observation 
using an optical microscope (OM). The microstructure and chemical 
composition were determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, 
ZEISS Sigma 300) and energy dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS). The 
carbon content of the sintered alloys was analyzed using a carbon/sulfur 
analyzer (LECO CS844). Microstructure and selected area electron 
diffraction (SAED) were also visualized by an EDS-equipped spherical 
aberration-corrected electron microscope (AC-STEM, FEI Themis Z) 
with an accelerating voltage of 300 kV.

echo material characterization system (UMS-100). The hardness of the 
samples was determined using a Vickers indenter (MHVD-50AP) with an 
applied load of 200 g. Seven indentations were tested for each sample, 
and the average value was taken after removing the minimum and 
maximum values to ensure the accuracy of the test results. The tensile

4. Discussions
4.1. Effect of residual carbon on the formation of FCC phase
SA is a fatty acid that is fully saturated and has a chain of 18 carbon 
atoms. Consequently, a large amount of carbon may be retained in the 
alloy when SA is used as a PCA. The carbon content of SA1 is larger than 
0.5 wt% (0.83 wt%), and it demonstrates a notable increase with the 
higher SA addition ( Fig. 4 ). Based on prior theoretical calculations, it is 
anticipated that FCC Ti will emerge as the most stable phase when the 
carbon content is elevated, subsequently resulting in the BCC-FCC 
allotropic transformation of Ti [ 17 ]. Thus, the FCC Ti phase formed 
accordingly.
Simultaneously, titanium carbides can also be precipitated. The lat -
tice parameters of the FCC phases of our TNZM alloys are around 4.31 Å 
as calculated by XRD patterns in Fig. 3 . The values fall in the lattice 
parameters range of TiCx measured for C/Ti ratios in earlier works [ 32 ]. 
The formation of the TiCx phase is due to the limited solubility of C in 
the Ti matrix ( < 0.05 wt%) [ 33 ]. During the SPS sintering process, a 
solid-state reaction between excessive carbon and TNZM alloy matrix 
results in the formation of TiCx precipitates. Ti
2
C and TiC are much 
more stable than other titanium carbide compounds; TiC normally exists 
over the composition range of TiCx (0.48 – 1), while the Ti
2
C phase tends 
to exist in relatively low carbon concentrations [ 34 ]. In conjunction with 
the XRD and TEM results, the TiCx phase could be identified as Ti
2
C. 
Given that Nb and Mn are expected to lead to a decrease in the solubility 
of carbon in Ti alloys [ 35 , 36 ]. Thus, coarse Ti
2
C would form close to the 
β grain boundaries, in line with the microstructural observation.
Hence, it can be affirmed that the residual carbon, when SA is 
employed as the PCA, induces the formation of the FCC phase in TNZM 
alloys. This phase encompasses both the FCC Ti solid solution phase and 
the Ti
2
C phase.

very excellent mechanical properties, which can significantly improve 
the mechanical properties of Ti alloys. As the carbon content rises, the 
volume fraction of the FCC phase increases gradually, thus leading to a

The current investigation demonstrates the FCC phase consists of the

5. Conclusions
TNZM alloys were prepared using ball milling with or without stearic 
acid as a process control agent, followed by subsequent SPS sintering. 
The effect of residual carbon on the microstructure, phase composition, 
and mechanical properties of the as-prepared Ti alloys was systemati -
cally studied. The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1) The addition of SA resulted in the residual carbon, which caused 
the emergence of the FCC phase that encompasses both the FCC 
Ti and the carbon-deficient Ti
2
C.
(2) The ultimate tensile strength rose from 889 ± 13 MPa (SA0) to 
1166 ± 13 MPa (SA2), accompanied by a reduction in plasticity 
from 23.6 % (SA0) to 3.8 % (SA2). As the stearic acid content 
increases, the Elastic modulus and hardness of Ti alloys also 
increase.
(3) Among various strength mechanisms, the primary enhancement 
in strength can be attributed to the load transfer strengthening of 
the FCC phase.
(4) To achieve a synergistic match of low elastic modulus, high 
strength, and preferable ductility for metastable β Ti, the carbon 
content of Ti alloys should be carefully regulated to obtain the 
appropriate amount of FCC Ti and simultaneously avoid the 
excessive generation of Ti
2
C.
