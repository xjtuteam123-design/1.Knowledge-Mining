ABSTRACT
Topologically close-packed phases (TCPs) are key limitations of the development of new generations of super -
alloys needed for high-efficiency, low-CO
2
-emitting advanced gas turbines. Their nucleation and growth remain 
puzzles in the two-phase microstructure ( γ / γ ′ ) of superalloys. Here, we find that the TCP precipitation is not a 
simple thermal activation process instead being significantly affected by plastic activity in superalloys. Atomic- 
resolved analysis via scanning transmission electron microscopy and atomic probe tomography reveals a diffu -
sion highway across the γ / γ ′ microstructure with the shear of superlattice stacking faults, leading to discontin -
uous nucleation and scatter-gather-merge growth of σ phase. Such pathway is energetically favorable according 
to first-principles calculations compared to ordinary case without fault, to induce large growth momentum of 
TCPs in a “ straight-across ” pattern. Our findings can be generalized to other TCPs, such as μ and P, which are 
usually reaction products of the σ phase, generating new insights into the nucleation and growth of TCPs in 
superalloys.
1. Introduction
Topologically close-packed phases (TCPs) represent a class of com -
plex intermetallics widely found in advanced metallic alloys such as 
high-alloy steels, Ni- and Co-based superalloys, and refractory high en -
tropy metals [ 1 – 5 ]. Their precipitation usually reduces microstructure 
stability and alloy performance. For modern superalloys [ 6 – 8 ], TCPs are 
especially prone to precipitate since appreciable amounts of refractory 
elements ( Re , W, Mo, and Ta) are added to the alloys, and they operate at 
extremely high temperatures. It was expected [ 9 ] that the knowledge of 
TCPs might lead to the design of new alloys with reduced dependency on 
refractory elements. Understanding of TCP formation mechanisms is 
critical to optimal alloy design and the development of the next gener -
ation of superalloys.
It is widely accepted that the formation of TCPs is sensitive to its 
coherence with matrix phase, and the particles nucleate on planes with 
low-energy coherent interface with matrix [ 10 – 12 ] and grow along di -
rections with a minimum of interface energy and lattice distortion en -
ergy [ 13 – 16 ]. In addition, the fact that TCPs precipitate in a matrix of 
two phases with distinct structures and compositions, γ and γ ′ of the 
superalloy [ 5 ], makes their formation much more complex than in a 
single-phase solution. Several critical questions about their nucleation 
and growth kinetics remain unresolved. The first issue concerns the 
nucleation site. It was thought that TCPs nucleated in the γ phase since 
the selective partition of refractory elements in this region should create 
a higher supersaturation than in the γ ′ phase [ 17 ]. However, TCPs 
usually formed initially in γ ′ in some alloy systems [ 18 , 19 ]. The second is 
about the growth pattern. Regardless of the nucleation site, TCPs grow 
in a “ straight-across ” pattern, not confined in γ or γ ′ but easily across 
multilayered γ / γ ′ structures, to develop morphologies like needle or

the driving force to overcome the energy barrier presented by the

In this work, we use Cs-corrected scanning transmission electron 
microscopy (STEM) and atomic probe tomography (APT) to reveal the 
nucleation and growth of a σ phase during creep in a modern fourth- 
generation single-crystal superalloy. The σ phase is well known to be 
the most common TCP and a meta-stable intermediate for other coun -
terparts such as the μ and P phases [ 9 , 20 ]. We discover a new mecha -
nism that promotes the nucleation and “ straight-across ” growth of TCPs 
with the shear of the planar defects (superlattice stacking faults) across 
the γ / γ ′ structures. The faults are one of the main sources of the plasticity 
of superalloys [ 21 – 23 ]. They can easily move across multilayered γ / γ ′ 
structures and assist solute diffusion through the γ / γ ′ interface, thereby 
fostering the σ phase precipitation in a pattern of discontinuous nucle -
ation and scatter-gather-merge growth along the faults. Compared to the 
ordinary case of a fault-free crystal, we also employ first-principles 
density functional theory (DFT) calculations to confirm and explain 
the energetic advantages of this precipitation behavior dependent on 
fault shearing, with which we have developed a new kinetic model. Our 
findings generate new insights into the nucleation and growth of TCPs in 
superalloys by showing that the TCP precipitation is strongly affected by 
plastic activities rather than a simple thermal activation process, which 
are highly instructive for future superalloy designs.

3.2. Precipitation of the σ phase along faults
To understand the effects of these plastic activities on the σ phase 
precipitation, we perform detailed microstructure analysis on the 0.7 % 
L-H creep sample. The HAADF-STEM image in 〈 110 〉 zone axis in Fig. 3 a 
shows that the intermittent σ particles are connected by a bright, 
straight ribbon, forming an alternated microstructure along the particle 
growth direction (see the inset). The atomic-resolved analysis in 
Fig. 3 b – d, referring to the enlarged 1 – 3 areas in Fig. 3 a, reveals the 
superlattice extrinsic stacking fault (SESF) for the ribbon extending from 
inside of the σ phase into the γ ′ phase matrix. The relevant EDS mapping 
in Fig. 3 e displays that the fault ahead of the σ phase is segregated with 
Cr, Co, Mo, and Re , while the γ ′ -rich element Al is depleted. These spe -
cific solute segregations account for the brighter Z-contrast intensity of 
the fault.
Quantitative assessment by the APT measurements on the same 0.7 
% creep sample is depicted in Fig. 4 . The fault that extends out of σ phase 
can be distinguished from APT reconstruction of an isocompositional 
surface of 2.5 at.% Cr ( Fig. 4 a). The 2D projection of the fault region is 
shown in Fig. 4 b, referring to the box in Fig. 4 a, and shows the promi -
nently high concentrations of Cr. The combined 1D compositional 
analysis for line 1 in Fig. 4 c shows that the fault region is enriched by 
approximately 5 % Co, 2 % Cr, 1 % Mo, and 1 % Re (at.%) compared to 
the surrounding γ ′ matrix (left in Fig. 4 d), and depleted in 4 % Al and 3 % 
Ni (right in Fig. 4 d). Another 1D analysis across the σ /matrix interface 
(line 2 in Fig. 4 c) exhibits ~70 % atomic proportion of Re , Mo, Cr, and 
Co in the σ phase, among which the Re content is near 30 % (left in 
Fig. 4 e), whereas Al and Ni are significantly depleted in the σ phase 
(right in Fig. 4 e). For each compositional profile, at least 2 sets of data 
points were collected from the original APT data. The profiles were 
drawn by the average value of as-collected data points, and the error 
bars are the variance of each data point. Thus, the compositional profiles 
in Fig. 4 d and e are representative to fully resolve the variation of 
concentrations at the stacking fault region.
The detailed compositions of the σ phase as measured by APT are 
shown in Table 1 . Apart from Re and Mo, the concentrations of other two 
refractory elements W and Ta are extremely low in the σ phase, although 
these two elements are also the common TCP-forming elements [ 33 ]. It 
maybe that W and Ta are not prone to segregate at fault, and they take 
part in the formation of TCPs through mainly thermal diffusion, which is 
restricted at this low temperature, especially the two elements diffuse 
slowly [ 34 ]. Nevertheless, although specific solute contents differ, the 
segregation of Re , Mo, Cr, and Co and depletion of Al and Ni result in a 
local chemistry at the fault consistent with that of the σ phase. Consid -
ering the intermittent morphology of the σ phase and the alternated 
microstructure ( Fig. 3 a), the fault would serve as the precursor to assist 
the σ phase precipitation.

in a slow, diffusion-controlled process at an extremely small 
under-saturation level. These conditions are not met by TCP formation 
in superalloys where the ‘solution ’ is a two-phase microstructure ( γ and 
γ ′ ), which suffers dynamic evolutions at high temperatures (and stresses) 
and interacts with TCPs to create a more complex precipitation envi -
ronment. A typical example is that the TCP formation is strongly affected 
by stress [ 47 – 49 ]; the types, morphologies, and compositions of TCPs 
vary with the magnitude, direction, and loading model of the stress. Yet 
the physics underlying this phenomenon remains largely unknown. 
Some recent studies [ 50 – 52 ] reported that the TCP formation is highly 
localized to the regions with intensified dislocation activities. One 
explanation is that dislocation motion assists the redistribution of solute 
elements, thereby governing the subsequent precipitation process. Such 
a scenario has already been discovered in another typical structure 
evolution in the superalloys, the rafting process during 
high-temperature creep [ 53 – 56 ]. Our investigation provides firm evi -
dence to show that the σ phase precipitation is not a simple thermal 
activation process instead to be significantly affected by specific plastic 
activity of stacking fault shearing (SESF). With the kinetic models 
described in Fig. 11 , we now discuss the nucleation and growth of the σ 
phase, with and without fault.
Our first-principles studies support that, in a pure Ni-Al system, the γ ′ 
phase is more energetically favorable to nucleate the σ phase both with 
and without the fault. But considering its higher supersaturation level 
for alloying additions, the γ phase could offer more opportunities for the 
σ phase to nucleate. In Fig. 11 a, nucleation without a fault is depicted as 
a random process in the γ and γ ′ phases. Fault shearing significantly 
decreases the formation energy of σ in γ and γ ′ . Since the planar defects 
will not change the coherent relationships at the interface, one possible 
reason is that the ESF/SESF transforms the local stacking from the FCC 
type to the hexagonal close-packed (HCP) type, and the latter creates 
more energetically preferred pathways for the transition to TCPs [ 57 ]. 
Additionally, the priority of γ ′ phase in the nucleating σ phase is 
remarkably increased with the SESF. This is due to the γ ′ / σ lattice 
mismatch being smaller than γ / σ , and hence, the γ ′ phase has a lower 
nucleation barrier. More importantly, the segregation of Re , Mo, Cr, and 
Co leads to the local chemistry at the SESF being close to the γ phase, 
which diminishes the effects of the supersaturation levels. As the 
consequence, the σ phase nucleates predominantly at the SESF in the γ ′ 
phase ( Fig. 11 b). Depending on whether the local concentration reaches 
a threshold value, the nucleation is discontinuous along the fault, 
leading to the intermittent morphology observed.
As the growth of particles is diffusion-controlled, the fault shearing 
with TCP-formers ( Re , Mo, Cr, and Co) redistributed in a wide range 
promotes the growth of the σ phase. Another HAADF-STEM analysis of 
an SESF across γ / γ ′ interface after 16.6 % L-H creep shows pronounced 
Cr, Co, Re and Mo segregations at the intersection of the SESF and the 
γ / γ ′ interface ( Fig. 12 a and b). Relevant EDS line-scan analysis of the 
SESF on both γ side and γ ′ side, referring to arrows 1 and 2 in Fig. 12 a, 
reveals that the enrichment of Cr, Co, Re and Mo already existed before 
the fault entered γ ′ (see Fig. 12 c and d). Similar to the APT, the 
compositional profiles in Fig. 12 c and d were obtained by the average of 
at least 2 sets of data points and the error bars are added by calculating 
the variance of each data point, which make the data representative. 
Along the fault the Cr and Co concentrations on γ ′ side are comparable to 
ones on γ side, but the Re and Mo concentrations are lower on γ ′ , which is 
attributed to the lower diffusion rate of Re and Mo. These imply that 
fault shearing generates a channel for diffusion across the γ / γ ′ interface.
One possible explanation is that the fault breaks the highly ordered 
lattice of γ ′ and lowers the diffusion barrier presented by ordered- 
disordered γ / γ ′ interface. The fault can easily pass through multilay -
ered γ / γ ′ structures and shares the same {111} habit planes with the σ 
phase, which determines the ease of growth of the σ phase along the 
fault. Therefore, the σ particles can grow quickly and merge into a large 
plate ( Fig. 11 b) once they nucleate extensively at the fault. If viewed 
edge-on, the σ plates would display a rod-like morphology parallel to the 
fault, as that shown in Fig. 3 a. The growth of the σ phase in thickness 
direction should be relatively confined compared with one along the 
fault plane. It expects that with extension of growth time the rod-like 
precipitates should further develop into a large, needle-like 
morphology. Such a scenario can explain the large growth momentum 
of the TCPs and the “ straight-across ” growth pattern. We also notice the 
segregation is overall reduced at the fault ahead of the σ phase in 
comparison to the one before σ phase precipitation ( Figs. 4 d and 6 b). 
This is clearly manifested by the increase of | Δ | of Re , Mo, Cr, and Co 
(SF1 vs. SF2 in supplementary Table S1). Since the larger | Δ | the farther 
the compositions to bulk γ , the high supersaturation of these elements at 
stacking fault decreases with the σ phase precipitation. This effect is 
especially prominent for Re and Cr, which are the most important for -
mers of the σ phase in this work. It strongly suggests that the growth of σ 
depletes the forming elements at the fault. In the absence of a fault, 
although the σ particles can nucleate in both γ and γ ′ phases, the 
disparity in composition makes the γ phase slightly preferable ( Fig. 11 a). 
In either region, the nucleation and growth of the σ phase is severely 
suppressed without fault shearing.

sensitive to temperature and stress, an important aspect is to clarify the 
interaction between different deformation defects and TCPs. In our case, 
a specific planar defect, SESF, significantly promotes the σ phase

dislocation motion inevitably changes the kinetic conditions, thus being 
expected to affect TCP precipitation. The fact is that TCPs are more 
easily formed at the regions with intensified dislocation activity 
[ 50 – 52 ]. These strongly hint that the various deformation defects would 
serve as nucleation site of TCPs and motion and interaction of these 
defects would promote the growth of TCPs.
Among those common TCPs in superalloys ( σ , μ and P), the σ phase 
was considered to nucleate first since the good fit between the σ and 
matrix results in the low formation energy. However, the decreased 
stability of the σ phase at high temperatures makes it prone to transform 
to the other counterparts [ 9 ]. The phase transformation of TCPs is 
diffusion-controlled, usually proceeded along specific crystallographic 
relationships between parent and new phases and assisted by the for -
mation and motion of defects within the phases to gain energetical ad -
vantages [ 59 ]. In our study, deformation defects should not change the 
precipitation sequence of TCPs, but accelerate the formation of a specific 
σ phase. Although the low temperature is not favorable for the formation 
of μ and P, it expects that the phase transformation from σ to μ or P 
should be accelerated with defects. At higher temperatures, μ and P

The high cost and high alloy density make those alloys less competitive 
in engineering applications. According to our findings, future superalloy 
designs may limit TCP precipitation by controlling the plastic activities 
in the alloy. The first is to use new strengthening elements to replace 
those TCP-forming elements, especially Re . These elements should also 
form solute atmosphere to drag dislocation motion, but not accelerate 
the TCP nucleation at defects. The second is that alloying to improve the 
intrinsic strength of the γ ′ phase as well as its resistance to dislocation 
shearing. This aims to limit mass flow with the motion of superpartials 
into γ ′ , retarding the growth of TCPs.
5. Conclusion
In summary, we have proposed a new kinetic model that correlates 
the formation of the σ phase with the shear of planar defects ESF/SESF 
during low temperature creep. An SESF passing through γ / γ ′ structures 
results in the segregation of specific solutes of Re , Mo, Cr, and Co, 
significantly lowering the formation energy of the σ phase. The γ ′ phase 
has more potential to nucleate the σ phase, and the SESF can magnify the 
energic advantages compared to the γ phase. The “ straight-across ” 
growth benefits from the promoted solutes diffusion along the fault and 
the scatter-gather-merge type of growth of the σ particles. These findings 
reveal that the σ phase formation is not a simple thermal activation 
process instead being strongly affected by plastic activities in the alloy. 
Since the σ phase is usually a meta-stable intermediate, this new kinetic 
model has the potential to be generalized to other TCPs, shedding light 
on our understanding of the nucleation and growth of these complex 
intermetallics. The design of the next generations of superalloys should 
fully exploit the relationships between deformation defects and TCPs. 
Alloying strategy that reduces the segregation of TCP-forming elements 
at defects or restricts the mass flow along with the shear of γ ′ should be 
helpful. These strategies to confine the nucleation and growth of TCPs by 
controlling the plastic activities are different from those employed in 
previous alloy designs, which utilize extensive Ru additions to meet 
minor increase of refractory elements, making those alloys less 
competitive in engineering applications.
