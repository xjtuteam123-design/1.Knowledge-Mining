Abstract
This study delved into the influence of room temperature rolling on an FeCoCrNiMn high-entropy alloy. Initially, the alloy 
was synthesized via vacuum induction melting and subsequently homogenized at 1100 °C for a duration of 6 hours. Cold 
rolling, with thickness reduction of up to 85%, significantly altered the material’s microstructure. It led to increased disloca-
tion density, elongation of initial grains in the rolling direction, formation of shear bands, and twinning within the grains. 
As deformation intensified, the alloy exhibited enhanced mechanical properties. Specifically, hardness, yield strength, and 
ultimate strength rose, while elongation decreased. Remarkably, with an 85% thickness reduction, the tensile strength reached 
1268 MPa—approximately 2.7 times higher than that of the as-cast counterpart. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) examinations of the fracture surface revealed a transition from ductile-to-brittle fracture mode as thickness reduction 
occurred.
Keywords FeCoCrNiMn high-entropy alloy · rolling · microstructure · strain hardening behavior · tensile properties
Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) represent a fascinating class 
of metallic materials, characterized by exceptional proper -
ties. These alloys exhibit high ductility, impressive strength, 
remarkable high-temperature resistance, superior thermal 
phase stability, and excellent corrosion and wear resist-
ance—making them ideal for structural applications [1 –4]. 
HEAs consist of five or more alloying elements, each con-
tributing distinct features. Notably, these elements introduce 
lattice distortion, high configurational entropy, the cocktail 
effect, and sluggish diffusion, all of which significantly 
impact the final mechanical behaviors and microstructure [5, 
6]. The concept of high configurational entropy plays a piv-
otal role. By reducing the free energy required for stability 
and solid solution phase formation (such as FCC, BCC, and 
FCC + BCC), it shapes the alloy’s behavior. Simultaneously, 
it acts as a gatekeeper, inhibiting the stability of intermetallic 
compounds and phase separation [7–9].
Numerous studies have demonstrated that both alloying 
methods and post-processing techniques can effectively 
modify the microstructure of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) 
and enhance their mechanical properties. For instance, cold 
rolling of FeCoCrNiMn HEA has been shown to increase 
strength while reducing ductility [10]. In a related study, 
Shabani et al. [8] investigated the impact of room tempera-
ture rolling on the mechanical behavior and microstructure 
of FeCuCrMnNi HEA. They observed that increased defor-
mation led to higher tensile strength but decreased ductil-
ity—a consequence of heightened dislocation density and 
the emergence of new grain boundaries. Furthermore, Wang 
et al. [11] demonstrated that an 84% reduction in thickness 
through cold rolling remarkably elevated the yield strength 
of HEAs, surpassing conventional alloys like steels, mag-
nesium, and aluminum. Similarly, Hou et al. [12] strength-
ened the Al0.25CoCrFeNi HEA alloy by 90% cold rolling, 
achieving a tensile strength 2.8 times greater than its as-
cast counterpart, albeit with reduced elongation. Notably, 
the deformation mechanism during cold rolling involved 
nanotwinned deformation. The influence of cold rolling 
extended to the NbHfTaTiZr refractory alloy, as investi-
gated by Zherebtsov et al. Zherebtsov et al. [13]. Their find-
ings revealed that cold rolling induced kinking, increased 
dislocation density, and elongated shear bands with a fine

Materials and Experimental Procedure
In this study, we explore the microstructure and composi-
tion of an FeCoCrNiMn high-entropy alloy (HEA) through 
a series of controlled processes. The initial as-cast ingot 
underwent several critical steps to enhance its properties. 
The FeCoCrNiMn ingot, in its as-cast state, was melted using 
an induction furnace. Subsequently, it was cast into a graph-
ite mold under a vacuum of 10−3 bar. The starting material 
consisted of elements with a purity exceeding 99.5%. To 
minimize segregation, the ingot (with the dimensions of 
100 × 35 × 35  mm3) was remelted three times. Wire electri-
cal discharge machining (WEDM) was employed to cut the 
ingot into slabs. These slabs measured 70 × 20 × 3  mm3. The 
slabs were then cold-rolled at room temperature. Reduction 
levels of 5–10% per pass were applied. This process resulted 
in plates with thickness reductions of 35%, 45%, 75%, and 
85%.
Phase analysis and lattice parameter determination were 
performed using a Philips X’PERT diffractometer. Cu Kα 
radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) was utilized. Specimens were ground 
and polished along the rolling direction. Philips XI30 SEM 
was used for detailed microstructural analysis. The as-cast 
ingot’s chemical composition was obtained through energy-
dispersive x-ray spectrometry (EDX). For (light microscopy) 
LM and SEM analysis, all specimens were etched in a solu-
tion of 15% CH3COOH + 10% HNO3 + 10% HCL.
To assess the mechanical properties of our specimens, we 
conducted tension tests using a universal testing machine 
(Hounsfield H25KS) at a strain rate of 10− 3 S−1. Prior to 
these tests, we meticulously prepared thin plates with vary-
ing thickness reductions. These plates were machined into 
dog-bone shapes, featuring a width of 2.5 mm and a gauge 
length of 10 mm, using wire electrical discharge machin-
ing (WEDM). Following the tensile tests, we examined the 
fracture surface of the specimens using a scanning elec-
tron microscope (SEM). The fracture mechanisms were 
further analyzed using ImageJ software. Additionally, we 
determined the hardness values of the rolled specimens 
by employing a hardness tester. A load of 100 grams was 
applied for 15 seconds during this assessment. All tests were 
meticulously repeated four times, and the average results 
were meticulously reported.

Hardness and Tensile Behavior
Figure  6a illustrates the tensile stress-strain curves of the 
studied alloy under various processing conditions, spe-
cifically  homogenized  and  cold-rolled. Additionally, 
Fig. 6b presents the changes in yield strength (σ YS), ulti-
mate tensile strength (σ UTS), and elongation with differ -
ent thickness reductions during cold rolling. As depicted 
in Fig. 6, the homogenized specimen exhibited remarkable 
plasticity, demonstrating an elongation of 62%. However, 
its yield strength and tensile strength were measured to 
be 197 MPa and 474 MPa, respectively. Previous reports on 
numerous as-c HEAs have also highlighted excellent elonga-
tion [11, 12]. According to Fig. 6b, it is evident that reducing 
the thickness of the alloy led to a substantial enhancement 
in both yield strength and tensile strength. However, this 
improvement could be realized at the expense of decreased 
elongation, as also corroborated by other researchers [8, 11, 
23].
The rolled specimen, with a thickness reduction of 35%, 
demonstrated an excellent balance between yield strength, 
ultimate strength, and elongation, which were measured 
to be 799 MPa, 846 MPa, and 21%, respectively. Simi-
larly, a cold rolling reduction of 45% resulted in a yield 
strength of 929 MPa, tensile strength of 1036 MPa, and 
elongation of 17%. Moreover, when the thickness reduction 
reached 85%, both the yield and ultimate strength exhibited 
a significant increase of approximately 620% and 270%, 
respectively, compared to the homogenized alloy. However, 
the elongation of the alloy decreased to 10% after reach-
ing 85% deformation. Consequently, cold rolling was proven 
to have a substantial impact on optimizing the strength and 
elongation characteristics of the studied alloy. The combina-
tion of high strength and elongation makes HE alloys highly 
suitable for various industrial applications.
Figure  7 illustrates the relationship between hardness 
variation and thickness reduction. As depicted, the average 
hardness of the alloy increased from 144 HV under homog-
enized conditions as the thickness reduction progressed. 
Notably, a significant enhancement of 330% in hardness was 
observed after achieving 85% thickness reduction, compared 
to the initial homogenized state. This behavior could be 
attributed to the accumulation of dislocation density result-
ing from work hardening during the cold rolling process, as 
reported by others [8, 12].

Conclusion
The objective of this study was to investigate the impact of 
cold deformation on microstructural changes and variations 
in mechanical properties in an equiatomic FeCoCrNiMn 
HEA. To achieve this, we subjected the alloy to arc melting, 
as-casting, homogenization, and room-temperature rolling 
with varying thickness reductions. We then examined the 
alloy’s microstructure evolution and mechanical behav -
ior under different conditions. Additionally, we measured 
microstrain, crystallite size, and dislocation density to com-
pare mechanical responses with microstructural characteris-
tics. The following key conclusions can be drawn:
1. The homogenized sample consisted of a single FCC 
solid solution phase with a coarse-grained structure 
(185 ± 12 µm). This alloy exhibited a yield strength of 
197 MPa, an ultimate tensile strength of 474 MPa, and 
a remarkable elongation of 62%.
2. No new phases formed during cold rolling. Furthermore, 
a linear relationship existed between hardness and ten-
sile strength. After an 85% reduction in thickness, we 
observed a significant increase in yield strength (approx-
imately 16%), tensile strength (about 37%), and hardness 
(around 33%). However, this improvement came at the 
cost of reduced elongation (approximately 17.1%).
3. Our analysis of strengthening mechanisms confirmed 
that dislocation strengthening played a crucial role in 
enhancing the alloy’s strength during thickness reduc-
tion.
4. The fracture mechanism in the studied alloy transitioned 
from a highly ductile fracture to a ductile-brittle fracture 
after 75% deformation.
