Abstract 
The effect of the manufacturing process (additive manufacturing and forging) of IN -718 Ni -based 
superalloys was studied isothermally at 600, 700 and 800 °C for 48 h in Ar and in air. The microstructure 
of the Cr 2O3 scale depended on the manufacturing process. Oxygen was soaked in the bulk of the 
additive manufactured IN-718 and outward cation diffusion was blocked. Diffusion of oxygen occurred 
through the grain boundaries of the forged material but cation outward diffusion was not arrested. The 
oxide scales grown in the forged IN -718 appeared less compact than those grown on the additive 
manufactured as shown by hydrogen charging experiments. 
Keywords: oxidation; high temperature; IN-718 superalloy; oxide scales; hydrogen charging 
1. Introduction 
Because of its very adequate mechanical resistance and weldability, Inconel 718 (IN-718) represented 
about 45 % of wrought and 25 % of cast Ni -based superalloys in 1992 [ 1] and is thus employed in a 
wide variety of applications and different environments from cryogenic to high temperatures. At high 
temperatures, the mechanical degradation of small -grained IN-718 is known to occur by oxidation 
assisted crack growth [2]. Most of the oxidation studies refer to very high temperatures [3] and/or to 
very long exposure times [ 4]. Chromia (Cr 2O3) is reported to be the predominant oxide scale [ 3,4]. 
Trindade et al. also found intergranular oxidation of their 70-µm sized IN-718 after 140 h of exposures 
to air at 1000 °C [5]. In addition, Geng et al. indicated that a duplex oxide layer grew at 1100 °C [6]. The 
outermost scale was composed of Nb-containing Cr2O3 oxide while the inner scale was made of Al2O3. 
A third internal layer rich in Ti and Nb carbides appeared in the substrate as a result of the outward 
diffusion of Cr. Under cyclic conditions and 750 °C, Al-hatab et al. found major outward diffusion along 
the grain boundaries of 25 -µm sized IN-718 resulting in a few Cr oxide nodules containing Ti and Mn 
[7]. With increasing temperature, the alloy also underwent intergranular oxidation and a three-layered 
oxide scale. TiO2 and MnCr2O4 grew at the scale/gas interface, Cr2O3 composed the inner layer, and Nb 
and Ti oxides at the scale/substrate interface. Dressler et al. revealed that the chromia scale formed 
majorly through grain boundary diffusion at 800 °C [8]. In addition, the long exposures (4000 h) at 800 
°C allowed Ti to diffuse through the chromia scale and the (Nb,Ti)C to decompose at the surface 
resulting in bulky Nb2O5. Such Nb2O5 protrusions were also found over a vermicular microstructure of 
Cr2O3 at just 700 °C [9]. It thus derives that IN-718 is particularly sensitive to the oxidation temperature. 
However, its oxidation behaviour is not well documented in the open literatu re at moderate 
temperatures and relatively short times. 
In addition to the complexity of the oxide scales formed on “conventional” IN -718, this superalloy 
material is the object of recent investigations on its fabricability by additive manufacturing (AM) a nd 
on the derived microstructure and mechanical properties [ 10-12]. During the direct laser deposition 
AM process itself, Cr2O3 and traces of Al2O3 formed as a consequence of the oxygen impurities in the 
Ar gas employed [13]. The low oxygen partial pressur e employed during manufacturing impeded the 
formation of other oxides like TiO 2 or Nb2O5. However, to date, the only study dedicated to the very 
oxidation kinetics and mechanisms of AM IN -718 was proposed by Jia and Gu [ 14]. They fabricated 
three IN -718 by selective laser melting (SLM) with different laser energies that were subsequently 
oxidized in still air at 850 °C for 100 h. They reported that increasing the density of the microstructure 
resulted in more parabolic oxidation behaviour and less intergran ular oxidation of the substrate. In 
contrast, Cr diffused significantly provoking its depletion under the Cr 2O3 scale in the less dense 
substrates. As opposed to the oxidation studies in “conventional” IN-718, neither Ni and Fe containing 
spinels nor Ti and Nb oxides were found in the scales. 
However, none of these works established any clear relationship of the oxidation behaviour with the 
initial microstructure of IN-718. Therefore, this paper intends to unveil the oxidation mechanisms at 
relatively low temperatures (600 to 800 °C) of IN-718 and short times (48 h) all by considering different 
fabrication methods, hence their microstructures. To elucidate the growth mechanisms both Ar and

2. Experimental Procedure 
Three types of IN -718 were kindly provided by the Ariane and Safran Groups (France) without any 
specification of the fabrication method except that conventional IN -718 “C” (bar of 10.8 mm of 
diameter) and “C2” (turbine ring) had been casted, forged and annealed while the additive 
manufactured IN-718 “AM” had been produced by selective laser melting and subsequent annealing. 
All the materials were sectioned to different sizes of samples d epending on the purpose of 
investigation. The surfaces were ground with SiC emery paper till grade P4000 for the oxidation tests. 
Additional final polishing with Struers -OPS was carried out for the analyses of the cross -sections. 
Oxidation was performed in a Setaram TGA-92 apparatus (10-6 g accurate). Before the introduction of 
the oxidizing gas at 600, 700 and 800 °C, the chamber was systematically purged and heated at 
50°C/min with Ar to avoid significant oxidation. Then, either synthetic air (<3 vpm H2O) or Ar (<2 vpm 
O2; <3 vpm H 2O) were introduced for 48 h. The thickness of the samples was 200 µm thus avoiding 
edge effects on the oxidation. 
The characterization of the as -received and of the oxidized materials was undertaken by optical 
microscopy (LEICA DMRM), scanning electron microscopy (FEI Quanta 200F) coupled to an EDAX 
detector for the EDS analyses and to EBSD (EDAX/TSL OIM), transmission electron microscopy (JEOL 
JEM-2011 at 200 kV). For the preparation of the TEM specimens, the samples with 3 mm of diameter 
were thinned by mechanical polishing till about 100 µm. Then, the surface of interest was covered with 
a Parafilm film while the back was electrochemically thinned at 10 V with a double jet of a solution 
composed of 450 mL CH 3OH, 100 mL of C 6H14O2 and 50 mL of HClO 4 at -15 °C in a Tenupol -5 device. 
The impurity amount of oxygen and nitrogen in the alloy samples was conducted by thermal 
desorption spectrometry (TDS) in a Jobin Yvon Horiba EMGA-620W apparatus ( 1.5 wppm accurate). 
In addition, X-ray diffraction was performed with CuKα radiation in a Bruker AXS D8 Advance. 
The compactness of the oxide scales was indirectly determined through hydrogen charging the 
oxidized samples in a three-electrode cell. The cell consisted in the sample as the cathode, a Pt grid as 
the counter -electrode and the reference electrode of Hg 2SO4 saturated in K 2SO4. The whole was 
immersed in a 0.1 M NaOH solution deaerated by continuous bubbling of Ar upon the application of a 
cathodic current of 10 mA/cm 2 according to our previous works [e.g. 15] The quantification of 
hydrogen was performed by TDS (Jobin Yvon Horiba EMGA-621W 0.1 wppm accurate.

3.2 High temperature behaviour of C2 and AM at 600, 700 and 800 °C 
 
Except for the grain size and the shape and distribution of -Ni3Nb, the C2 and AM types of IN -718 
were considered equivalent from the metallurgical point of view. Therefore, they were oxidized in air 
and in Ar at 600, 700 and 800 °C for 48 h. The specific mass gains are plotted against time in Figures 6a 
and 6b for the C2 and the AM materials, respectively. In both materials, the higher the temperature, 
the greater the specific mass gains. At 600 and 700 °C, the differences between both materials and 
atmospheres are considered as negligible when considering the very low values of specific mass gain. 
However, when the temperature was increased to 800 °C, the materials underwent a significant 
oxygen uptake compared to the lowest temperatures. The mass gain of the C2 material was similar in 
Ar and in air. However, the oxygen uptake was about 1.5 times greater in Ar than in air in the AM 
superalloy. 
(b) 
 
 
Assuming parabolic oxidation behaviour for this material [ 8], the instantaneous k p values were 
determined and plotted against the inverse of temperature in the Arrhenius graph of Figure 7. This 
allowed to calculate the activation energies gathered in Table 4, which also includes a comparison with 
data from the literature [4,7,18]. Our log(kp) data were higher than those obtained by Gulbransen and 
Andrew for pure Cr2O3 scales grown in Cr between 800 and 950°C in pure O 2 [18] but appeared to be 
in line with those of E ngland and Virkar at their lowest oxidation temperature (900°C) [ 4]. In Ar, the 
activation energies of the C2 (154 kJ mol-1) and AM (151 kJ mol-1) materials were very similar to those 
of Gulbransen and Andrew (150 kJ mol -1) [18]. In air, the values were hi gher (165 kJ mol -1 for C2 and 
200 kJ mol-1 for AM) than in Ar and are similar to those reported by Al-hatab et al. for continuous Cr2O3 
scales (173 kJ mol -1) [7]. In contrast, Al -hatab found that the activation energies were lower (117 kJ 
mol-1) when the Cr2O3 layers were discontinuous [ 7] while England and Virkar found much higher 
values with very compact scales Cr2O3 grown under isothermal conditions in air at 800-1100°C [4]. This 
suggests that both the C2 and AM IN-718 grew rather continuous oxide scales. 
 
 
At this stage, it was thus difficult to rationalize whether there was either a potential effect of the 
impurities contained in the alloys on the oxidation or that the different mass gains were ruled by a 
grain boundary effect (or a combination of both). 
 
As for the role of impurities (Table 3), the TDS analyses highlighted much greater N (165 wppm) and O 
(176 wppm) contents in the AM alloy than in the C2 (72 and 5 wppm, respectively for N and O). The 
evaluation of the inclusions by SEM/EDS revealed that only the AM material had some tiny inclusions 
of Al2O3 and of TiN but not the C2 material. However, since the content of N and O was very low and/or 
they were trapped as Al2O3 and of TiN in both materials, their contribution to the overall specific mass 
gain was considered to be negligible.

3.4 Compactness of the oxide scales through hydrogen charging 
 
The sensitivity to hydrogen penetration of the unoxidized and the oxidized materials was employed as 
an indirect means to evaluate the compactness of the materials and of the scales. Hydrogen was 
chosen instead of oxygen because of its smaller size and less reactivity with the materials so that just 
the adsorption and absorption mechanisms of penetration are considered. The tests were only 
performed in the C2 and AM materials because of their closer metallurgical features. Table 6 gathers 
the hydrogen content before hydrogen charging and after the electrochemical charging to foster the 
penetration of hydrogen. 
 
 
Within uncertainty limits, the hydrogen content in IN -718 C2 and AM are equivalent in all conditions 
before the electrochemical charging. Compared to the as-received materials, the content of hydrogen 
increases after oxidation probably because of the water vapour (about 3 vpm) contained in the bottle 
of synthetic air. After polishing of the surface, the hydrogen content dropped down to about the as -
received condition, which is indicative of the trapping of hydrogen in the scale. 
 
After the electroc hemical charging, the hydrogen content sharply increased. In the as -received 
materials the hydrogen content was 3-folded in the C2 but was almost 15 times greater in the AM one. 
This strongly supports that the AM material is far more permeable than the C2 substrate. After 
oxidation, the opposite occurs, i.e. the scale grown in AM retains less hydrogen than that of the C2 
material. Compared to the as-received conditions before charging, it clearly appears that the hydrogen 
content is 9 times greater in AM wh ile it is only about 4 times in C2. After polishing, the hydrogen 
contents are again similar to those of the as -received substrates, i.e. hydrogen gets trapped in the 
oxide scales. Therefore, the oxide scale of C2 soaks more H than that of AM as can be thus considered 
as less compact. 
 
4. Discussion 
 
4.1 As received 
 
Inconel 718 is hardened by precipitating coherently the body centred tetragonal γ″ (Ni 3Nb) and the 
face centred cubic γ′ Ni3 (Al,Ti), in particular the former. In addition, it contains minute amounts of the 
orthorhombic -Ni3Nb to control the grain size by precipitating at the grain boundaries, ideally in 
globular form. Therefore, after casting, the precise control of the forging parameters (temperature, 
applied force and strain rate) are paramount to fabricate the billet and/or the component with the 
desired microstructural features, hence the mechanical properties [ 16,19,20]. In most 
thermomechanical treatments the idea is to forge first above the solvus line of the -phase then hyper-
quenching from 950°C [21]. Two other successive tempering treatments of 8 h at 718 °C and 620 °C 
are then applied to precipitate respectively, the hardening phases γ’ -Ni3(Ti,Al) and γ’’-Ni3Nb [21]. An

additional heat treatment can be applied to control the size and dis tribution of the δ-Ni3Nb phase by 
heating in air and final water quench [16]. The temperature achieved by this final heating determines 
the microstructure of the δ -phase. At 880 °C intragranular precipitates are obtained while at 900 °C 
the δ-phase appears inter- and intragranular. In contrast, globular precipitates are obtained at 1000 °C 
[16]. Here, it was not possible to observe the γ’’ -Ni3Nb in the C material while it clearly contained 
needle-like intergranular δ -Ni3Nb precipitates in addition to Nb car bides. This indicates that the 
annealing treatment was probably too long and dissolved the strengthening γ’’-Ni3Nb. In contrast, the 
globular δ-Ni3Nb precipitates and the reduced presence of carbides suggest that a final temperature 
at or above 1000 °C was carried out for relatively short times that allowed to maintain the γ’’-Ni3Nb in 
both the C2 and the AM materials. The different grain size may just be related to a standard 
thermomechanical treatment in IN -718 C (bar) and a high strength processing in IN-718C2 (turbine 
ring). Obviously, the torn grain size of the IN-718 AM was derived from the SLM processing with a rapid 
quench and a subsequent annealing that did not fully anneal the microstructure. The very few 
inclusions of Ca found in this material could arise from the additives sometimes employed to increase 
the flowability of the powders [22]. Therefore, we considered that the C2 (ring) and the AM materials 
were relatively equivalent from the metallurgical perspective compared to the C (bar). 
 
 
 
 
 
4.2 Oxidation mechanisms 
 
The potential role of inclusions is disregarded in this discussion since the N and O contents were very 
low in the C2 material while they were trapped respectively, as TiN and Al2O3 inclusions and still their 
frequency was negligible before oxidation. 
 
The type of manufacturing process (forge or additive manufacturing) of IN-718 does not seem to have 
a significant impact on the overall oxidation behaviour after 48 h of exposure at 800 °C in air and in Ar. 
Indeed, the oxidation is mainly governed by a parabolic growth, for which counter-current diffusion of 
oxygen and chromium by grain boundary diffusion has been reported for Cr 2O3 scales [23]. Also, the 
activation energies of oxidation of both the coarse grained C2 and the additive manu factured (Fig.7, 
Table 4) are quite in line with those of Refs. [7,18] thereby suggesting the growth of continuous oxide 
scales. However, the manner the scales grow and stabilize differs between the three types of IN -718. 
It has been shown that both forged C and C2 materials continuously decrease their k p constants with 
oxidation and do not stabilize after 48 h (Fig. 8 and Fig.9) following a classic mechanism whereby the 
mass gain is directly proportional to the establishment of the scale and is then supers eded by solid 
state diffusion through such scale [24]. In contrast, the AM IN-718 displays constant instantaneous kp 
values all along the oxidation time but could not calculated upon the first 10 h of oxidation. Therefore, 
it seems that the protective properties of the scale do not evolve with time once it has been formed. 
 
These apparently inconsistent results can be explained when considering the hydrogen charging 
experiments (Table 6), where it has been demonstrated that the AM material can soak much gr eater 
amounts of hydrogen than the forged C2 one. With an analogous reasoning for the oxygen contained 
in the air or in Ar bottles (water vapour is disregarded), one can presume that part of the overall oxygen 
uptake of the specific mass gains of Figures 8 and 9 is made by the material without being used to grow 
a scale. According to Lenglet et al. a mixed scale of Cr2O3, NiCr2O4, Fe2O3 grows in IN-718 in the first 60 
min in air at 800 °C [ 25], which evolves to Cr 2O3 with time. Here, it appears that a conco mitant 
mechanism of outward diffusion of Cr and inward diffusion of oxygen takes place in the AM during the 
first stages of oxidation as well. This is supported by the Cr denuded area in the X-ray maps (Figure 12), 
the presence of internal oxidation (Figure 11e, 11f) and the absence of hydrogen in the material after

polishing the oxidized AM samples (Table 6). The penetration of oxygen in the AM substrate was also 
observed by Jia and Gu after oxidation of their three SLM AM IN -718 materials [14]. In contrast, the 
oxygen uptake with time would help to densify the scale. This hypothesis can be also supported by the 
absence of Nb or Ti oxides at the surface of the scale in contrast to the forged materials (Figure 10). As 
a result, the Cr2O3 scale grown in the AM material displays equiaxed grains under the TEM (Figure 13c) 
and neither the calculated kp values nor the theoretical scale thickness are consistent with the actual 
values (Table 5). This interpretation would be in line with the fact that the outward dif fusion of Cr 
through the grain boundaries is more marked in the Ar atmosphere than in air because the P O2 is 
insufficient to establish an even Cr 2O3 scale at the very initial stages. The absence of any other oxide 
but Cr2O3 may be explained because of the fact that Nb and Ti are initially trapped as Ni3Nb, -Ni3Nb 
and carbides and their content in the alloy, i.e. their chemical potentials for oxidation should be lower 
than that of Cr. As such, the external zone of the substrate was again denuded of Cr (not shown) but 
less oxygen had penetrated into the bulk. 
 
In the forged materials, the major differences in oxidation behaviour arise from the smaller grain size 
and the greater content in δ-Ni3Nb phase in the IN-718C than in the C2 one. Like in the AM materia l, 
the development of the Cr2O3 seems to occur rapidly in the C material according to the equiaxed TEM 
microstructure of the Cr2O3 scale (Fig. 13a). This results from the rapid nucleation of the oxides at the 
high energy regions like the many grain boundaries in the alloy and through the deformed areas upon 
polishing (and probably by the random >29 grain boundaries too) (Fig. 10a and 10b) that provide 
continuous supply of cations for oxidation to continue. Therefore, the instantaneous kp values keep on 
decreasing with time but do not stabilize after 48 h of exposure (Fig. 8b and 9b). Oxidation is 
particularly prominent at the grain boundaries where the -Ni3Nb precipitated resulted in a ridged 
morphology, in particular in air (Fig. 10a and 10b). As with the AM material, there is Nb and Ti 
enrichment and Cr depletion at the substrate/scale interface. With time, outward diffusion of Ti and 
of Nb occurs, as also observed by other authors whether in cyclic [7] or in isothermal [8] conditions in 
the same temperature range. In addition, the significant presence of Ti, Mo and Nb carbides in IN-718C 
compared to the IN-718C2 material contribute to the greater specific mass gain. The oxidation of the 
NbC into “tower-like” Nb2O5 was already reported by Dressler et al. [8]. Here, the protrusions include 
blocky Nb2O5 mostly at the grain boundaries so that they occur by oxidation of the -Ni3Nb phase or 
by forming clusters of hexagonal prisms of TiO 2 in greater extent in air than in Ar, and in C compared 
to C2 (Fig. 10 a-d). Since the scales grew by a mixed internal oxygen and external metal diffusion, some 
internal oxidation of Al is also observed but to a much lesser extent than in the AM material. 
 
As for the coarse grained IN -718C2 material, there are many similarities w ith the other materials 
(outward Cr diffusion into the scale, Cr depletion and Nb enrichment at the substrate/scale and some 
internal oxidation of Al). However, the (near) absence of needle -like -Ni3Nb precipitates at the 
beginning of oxidation and the mu ch greater grain size of IN -718C2 makes the oxidation mechanism 
turn into an almost completely typical outward diffusion reported for this material [26] resulting in the 
columnar grains of Cr2O3 observed by TEM (Fig. 13b). This often results in concentration of cavities at 
the scale/metal interface [26] that we could not observe experimentally. In any case, the C2 material 
soaked more hydrogen than AM (Table 6). Therefore, either the scale was less compact or the cavities 
contributed the enhanced adsorption of hydrogen. The surface of the C2 material also developed less 
clusters of Ti and of Nb oxides than in the fine-grained C material probably because the latter contained 
more carbides at the surface in the as -received condition than the C2 (Fig. 11c and 1 1d). However, it 
was noted that the microstructure of the C2 substrate had developed needle-like -Ni3Nb phases after 
48 h of exposure at 800 °C, which may thus change the oxidation behaviour for prolonged exposures 
(Fig. 11c and 11d). 
 
In summary, the results observed here for the three types of IN -718 are very much in line with the 
internal oxidation clearly highlighted by Trindade et al. at 1000°C of their 70µm-grained IN-718 [5] and

with the layers formed in the 25 -µm grained IN -718 oxidised cyclical ly between 750 and 950°C [ 7]. 
However, Al-hatab et al. found outer TiO2-MnCr2O4 and internal Nb and Ti oxides at the Cr2O3/substrate 
interface that we never found. 
 
As for the lower temperatures (600 and 700 °C), the specific mass variations were significantly low and 
in agreement with the findings of Greene and Fincrock [ 3]. These authors reported that for 
temperatures equal or lower than 700 °C at least 24 h of oxidation were needed for the oxidation to 
convert from transient to parabolic but the kinetics were too slow to measure them accurately. 
Therefore, they considered IN-718 as passivated although we have observed the onset of oxidation at 
700 °C through the grain boundaries (figure not shown). 
 
5. Conclusion 
 
The characterisation and high temperatur e oxidation behaviour of three IN -718 superalloys were 
studied here: C (casted and forged, supplied as bar), C2 (casted and forged, supplied as a turbine ring) 
and AM (additive manufactured by SLM supplied as chunk). Their chemical composition was very 
similar but the microstructure differed significantly. C and C2 had homogeneous grain shapes but their 
sizes were very different (about 10 µm for C, about 140 µm for C2). The grain shape of AM was very 
heterogeneous and intermediate size of about 50 µm. The content of high energetic grain boundaries 
of AM was also intermediate compared to C2 (highest) and C (lowest). IN-718C displayed the greatest 
content of δ-Ni3Nb precipitates (needle or globular). 
 
In spite of the metallurgical differences of the three (C, C2 and AM) IN -718, the oxidation kinetics 
increased with temperature from 600 to 800 °C both in air and in Ar. After 48h of exposure, the oxide 
scales were very thin (about 0.7 µm) in all the substrates whether in Ar or in air. The oxidation kinetics 
of the large-grained IN-718C2 were similar in air and in Ar. In contrast, the small -grained C material 
and the porous AM substrate underwent greater mass gains in Ar than in air. This was ascribed to O 
inward diffusion through the grain boundaries and defects a s demonstrated through the hydrogen 
charging experiments. The scales grew by major Cr outward diffusion, which caused a Cr depletion in 
the substrate. Over the Cr2O3 scale, some Ti and Nb oxide protuberances. They were particularly Nb2O5-
rich in IN-718C due to the greatest content of δ-Ni3Nb. The scales had either columnar (C2), equiaxed 
(AM) or mixed (C) microstructures. The outwardly grown columnar scales of C2 appeared more 
permeable to hydrogen, hence less compact than those grown in the equiaxed AM IN-718.
