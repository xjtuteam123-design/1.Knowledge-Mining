Abstract 
 
γ-γ coatings are envisaged replacing the traditional -(Ni,Pt)Al coatings in thermal barrier 
coating (TBC) systems. In a first, the tensile properties of a freestanding 35 µm thick γ-γ 
coating are evaluated by microtensile testing and compared with th ose of 100 µm thick 
standalone -(Ni,Pt)Al coatings. The coatings exhibit stark differences in tensile behavior. The 
γ-γ coating exhibits a decrease in strength with increase in temperature, except for the increase 
in strengthening at ~700°C. It is stronger than -(Ni,Pt)Al up to 850°C and relatively more 
ductile until 750°C. However, its ductility at higher temperatures is severely restricted to ~0.2% 
by intergranular fracture, unlike that of the -(Ni,Pt)Al. Implications of tensile properties on 
rumpling are discussed. 
Keywords: γ-γ coating; Pt-aluminide coating; micro tension test; thermal barrier coating ; 
rumpling; superalloy 
1. Introduction 
In the recent times, oxidation resistant diffusion γ -γ type bond coats are being 
developed for application on Ni-base superalloy components operating in the hot sections of 
gas turbine engines [1-9]. These coatings are constituted of the γ-Ni and γ-Ni3Al phases, are 
enriched in Pt (30-35 at.%), and contain low Al (15-20 at.%) [3-9]. The oxidation resistance is 
derived from the protective adherent alumina layer that forms on the surface during thermal 
exposure in air/oxidizing environments. The presence of Pt enhances the oxidation resistance 
further by i mproving the spallation resistance of the alumina scale [4-7]. Besides good 
oxidation resistance, the γ-γ coatings also exhibit good microstructural stability during thermal 
exposure. By virtue of its γ-γ phase constitution and Al content similar to that of the superalloy 
substrate, interdiffusion of Ni and Al between the coating and the substrate and the associated 
dynamic phase changes in the coating can be greatly reduced. As a consequence, the γ-γ 
coatings are expected to show increased rumpling resistance as compared to their -(Ni,Pt)Al 
counterparts which contain much higher amounts of Al (30-60 at.%) [10-13], undergo dynamic

phase changes including   to +γ and reversible   L10 martensite, and exhibit

(Ni,Pt)Al coatings in TBC systems [1-9,18,19]. 
The diffusion coatings form an integral part of the substrate and the inherent mechanical 
properties of the coating affect the mechanical properties of coated substrate [20]. For instance, 
brittle fracture in the -(Ni,Pt)Al coatings imparts low strain tolerance to the coated 
components at low temperatures (RT-700˚C) whereas ductile failure in the coating improves 
the tensile properties of the substrate at higher temperatures [20-23]. - coating, on the other 
hand, shows a contrasting behavior. While the coating is relatively ductile at RT, it fails by 
intergranular cleavage at 870°C, causing a concomitant decrease in the tensile ductility of the 
substrate [ 24]. The above contrasting tensile behavior of the coatings warrants for a 
comparative evaluation of the ir representative tensile properties which can be achieved by 
testing them in freestanding condition, i.e. without any attached substrate. 
In the recent past, the inherent mechanical properties of the freestanding β-(Ni,Pt)Al 
coatings have been evaluated using the microtensile [21,22] and bending techniques [ 25]. 
However, the mechanical properties of freestanding - coatings have not been reported in the 
open literature. Therefore, the present study examines the tensile behavior of a freestanding -
 coating using the microtensile test method in the temperature range of RT -1100°C. The 
tensile properties are compared with that of the freestanding β-(Ni,Pt)Al coatings [21] and 
implications on rumpling characteristics discussed. 
2. Experimental details 
The γ-γ coating was formed on 0.5 mm thick strips of d irectionally solidified (DS) 
CM-247LC superalloy. The nominal composition of the alloy is (in wt.%) Co-9.2, Cr-8.1, W-
9.5, Al-5.6, Ta-3.2, Hf-1.5, Ti-0.7, Zr-0.015, Mo-0.5, B-0.15, C-0.07, balance Ni. The strips 
were grit blasted at a low pressure of 0.2 bar and electroplated with a 5 µm thick Pt layer. A 
subsequent vacuum diffusion treatment (1080°C for 4 h) ensured interdiffusion between the Pt 
layer and substrate, leading to formation of the γ-γ coating [26].

Flat freestanding coating microtensile samples having a gage length of 2 mm, gage 
width of 0.5 mm, fillet radius of 0.5 mm and an overall length of 8 mm [21], were excised from 
the coated strips by wire -electrical discharge machining (EDM) . A low current and low feed 
rate was used for preventing damag e to the edges of the samples while machining. Each cut 
out sample was polished from one side using a Tripod Polisher, which contained three precision 
micrometers around a stub holder. The sample was glued on the stub and the sample surface 
levelled with respect to the feet of the three micrometers. Subsequently, each of the 
micrometers was retracted by equal extents and the sample was wet-polished on SiC abrasive 
papers of grade 600, 800, 1000 and 1500, in that sequence. The micrometers ensured flat 
polishing of the sample and enabled direct measurement of the extent of reduction in sample 
thickness. Final polishing of the samples was done on diamond lapping films of grade 3, 1 and 
0.5 µm in that sequence. Polishing was continued til l the sample thickness was equal to the 
final coating thickness . Using the above approach, 35 µm thick freestanding γ -γ coating 
microsamples were fabricated for the present study. Subsequently, Pt markers were deposited 
on the sample gage length using a Quanta 200 -3D focused io n beam (FIB) machine. These 
markers enabled the in-situ measurement of strain in the gage during tensile testing using a 
non-contact video extensometer. The above methods were also adopted for the preparation of 
100 µm thick freestanding β -(Ni,Pt)Al coatin g samples and subsequent evaluation of their 
tensile properties, as reported in our earlier study [21]. 
The freestanding coating samples were tested in a 500 N Walterbai -Ag microtensile 
tester. Slotted grips were used to hold the fragile samples during the test [21]. Tensile testing 
was performed at temperatures ranging between RT-1100°C in air at a nominal strain rate of 
10-3 s-1. For the high temperature tests , the microsample was resistively heated and the gage 
temperature monitored using a two -color rat io in frared radiation (IR) pyrometer with an 
accuracy of  10°C. The strain in the gage was measured using a video extensometer [ 21]. 
About three-five samples were tested at any temperature. Microstructure and fracture surface 
were studied using a Quanta 400 scanning electron microscope (SEM) operating at 20 kV.

3.2 Tensile test results 
 Figure 2(a) shows the engineering stress (s)-strain (e) plots for the freestanding γ-γ 
coating at various temperatures. The coating exhibited yielding at RT and the values for elastic 
modulus (E), yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) values were ~145 GPa, 
720 MPa and 850 MPa, respectively. Since the overall strain to failure in the coatings was low, 
the YS was measured as the stress corresponding to the deviation in the slope of the s -e plot 
from linearity by 10% [21]. The value for E decrea sed in the range of 145 -105 GPa with 
increase in temperature from RT to 1100°C. The value s of YS and UTS also decreas ed with 
increase in temperature except at 700°C, where an increase in strength was observed (Figs. 2(a-
c)). For instance, the YS decreased from 660 to 247 MPa between RT-650°C. It then increased 
to 535 MPa at 700°C and subsequently decreased to 67 MPa at 1100°C (Fig. 2b). The UTS 
also exhibited a similar trend with increase in temperature ( Figs. 2(a,c)). The ductility, i.e. 
plastic strain to failure, was very limited with a maximum value of about 0.2% at 1100°C (Figs. 
2(a,d)). 
3.3 Fracture surface features 
After fracture at RT, the coating surface exhibited a combination of cleavage facets and 
dimples (Fig. 3a)). Based on t he back scattered electron (BSE) image , it was confirmed that 
cleavage fracture occurred in the bright (γ phase whereas dimples had formed in the gray (γ) 
phase [24]. The respective formation of dimples and cleavage facets suggested that the γ phase 
was more ductile than that of the γ phase at RT. For the samples tested at 400°C, the 
γexhibited faceted cabbage -leaf cleavage features whereas the γ failed by ductile dimple 
voiding (Fig. 3b). However, unlike that at RT (Fig. 3a), intergranular separation occurred along 
the interfaces of the γcleavage face ts and the du ctile γ matrix (Fig. 3b). The extent of 
intergranular cleavage fracture in the coating increased with increase in temperature until

4. Discussion 
The overall tensile behavior of the γ -γ coating will be affected by the inherent tensile 
characteristics of the constituent γ and γ  phases. Since the γ phase of the coating contained 
elements from the superalloy substrate in solid solution, the variation of its inherent tensile 
properties with tempera ture can be assumed comparable to that of typical single -phase γ 
superalloy such as Ni-625. Similar to the monotonic decrease in YS from 660 to 85 MPa with 
increase in temperature from RT to 1000°C in Ni-625 [27], the strength of γ phase of the coating 
can be expected to undergo a monotonic decrease with increase in temperature . On the other 
hand, the γ phase of the coating can be expected to show anomalous strengthening as observed 
in polycrystalline bulk γ-Ni3Al [28-30]. It has been reported that the 0.2% flow stress of bulk 
γ-Ni3Al increases from 240 MPa at RT to 640 MPa at 870°C. Further increase in temperature 
to 1000°C leads to a decrease in flow stress to 530 MPa. It has been well-established that the 
thermally activated cross-slip of superdislocations from octahedral to cube slip planes in the γ 
phase results in the formation of immobile Kear-Wilsdorf (K-W) locks which raise the overall 
flow stress and impart anomalous strengthening at intermediate temperatures [28-30]. From 
the above discussion, it can be concluded that in the approximate temperature range RT-900°C, 
while strengthening of the γ phase of the coating occurs with increasing temperature, the γ 
phase undergoes weakening. This leads to incompatibility in the strength of these phases and 
consequent de-cohesion along the γ-γ interface at high temperatures, as typically seen in Figs. 
3(b,c). Furthermore, the embrittlement of the γ  phase boundaries with atmospheric oxygen 
during the high temperature tensile tests could also contribute t o intergranular cleavage 
cracking and concomitant loss of ductility, as reported for bulk γ-Ni3Al [30]. Therefore, the 
above aspects cause increase in intergranular cleavage fracture of the coating with increase in 
temperature between RT-900°C (Figs. 3(a-c)). Above 900°C, the failure of both γ and γ phases

The extent of strengthening and the temperature corresponding to the peak flow 
strength in γ-γ alloys are sensitive to the phase constitution [31,32]. The study by Beardmore 
et al. [ 31] indicates that a decrease in γ phase content reduces the extent of anomalous 
strengthening as well as the temperature associated with peak hardening in γ-γ alloys. For 
instance, the flow stress increased by ~400 MPa, i.e. from 240 MPa at RT to a maximum of 
641 MPa at 870°C, for an alloy containing 100 vol.% of γ phase. On the other hand, for alloys 
containing 80, 60 and 40 vol.% of γ phase, a strength increase of 260, 85 and 55 MPa, 
respectively, was observed. The temperature corresponding to the peak strength was 850, 800 
and 700°C, respectively [31]. The extent of strengthening with increase in temperature is 
negligible for the alloys containing less than 20 vol.% of γ phase [31]. As mentioned earlier, 
the present γ-γ coating contained about 40 vol. % of γ phase and exhibited an increase in 
strength in the vicinity of 700°C (Fig. 2b). This is similar to the temperature value reported by 
Beardmore et al. for the bulk γ-γ alloy containing 40 vol.% of γ phase [31]. Thus, the above 
observation suggests that the strengthening in the γ-γ coating with increase in temperature can 
be associated with the inherent deformation of the constituent γ phase and the formation of K-
W locks [28-32]. The decrease in strength of the coating at further higher temperatures is 
caused by thermally assissted glide of dislocations on the cube planes in the γ phase [28-32] 
as well as weakening of the γ phase. It is interesting indeed to note that strengthening imparted 
by the γ phase with increase in temperature, in bulk γ-γ alloys and Ni-base superalloys [28-
33], is also realized at the micron-scale dimensions such as in the present 35 µm thick γ-γ 
coating. 
As mentioned earlier, the γ-γ bond coats are being considered as alternatives to the 
traditional β-(Ni,Pt)Al coatings in advanced TBC systems [1-9,18,19]. Therefore, it is pertinent 
to compare their tensile behavior. The tensile properties of freestanding high activity and low 
activity β-(Ni,Pt)Al coating s have been reported by Alam et al. [ 21] and Pan et al. [ 22], 
respectively. Both the types of β-(Ni,Pt)Al coatings exhibit the following features: linear stress-
strain (i.e. brittle) response and failure by brittle transgranular cleavage with negligible ductility 
below ~700°C (Figs. 2d and 4), a distinct brittle-to-ductile-transition-temperature (BDTT) and 
onset of yielding in the range of 650-750°C, and ductile failure at higher temperatures [21,22].

yielding (Fig. 2a) and is relatively more ductile between RT-750°C (Fig. 2d). However, the γ-

ductility of ~0.2% at higher temperatures (Fig. 2d) and does not exhibit a BDTT , which is 
unlike that for the β-(Ni,Pt)Al coating (Figs. 2d and 4b) [21] . Furthermore, the γ-γ coating 
does not show post -UTS elongation even at 1000°C and beyond (Fig. 2d). Such behavior of 
the γ-γ coating is in striking contrast to that of the high activity β-(Ni,Pt)Al coating which 
shows appreciable post-UTS elongation (Fig. 4b), undergoes dynamic recrystallization [34], 
and exhibits extensive ductility (nearly 2%) beyond 900°C (Figs.2d and 4b) [21]. Increase in 
temperature causes a decrease in strength for the γ-γ coating, except for the increase in strength 
at 700°C (Fig. 2b). β-(Ni,Pt)Al coatings, on the other hand, show a marginal increase in fracture 
stress between RT-750°C and the YS decreases as the temperature increases further (Figs. 2b,c) 
[21,22]. On the whole , the γ-γ coating i s stronger than its β-(Ni,Pt)Al counterpart in the 
temperature range RT-850°C and weaker at higher temperatures (Figs. 2(b,c)). The value of E 
for the γ-γ and the β-(Ni,Pt)Al coatings vary in the similar range 150 -110 GPa with increase 
in temperature from RT to 1100°C. 
The stre ngth of the bond coat determines i ts resistance to rumpling under thermal 
cycling conditions which in tur n affects the durability of the TBC system [16]. Stresses in a 
TBC system can be caused by mismatch in coefficient of thermal expansion among its 
constituents (locked-in thermal stresses), dynamic phase changes (transformation stresses), and 
complex external loads [16]. Finite element simulations by Evans et al. indicate that the bond 
coat experiences high accumulative stresses (~1 GPa) during cooling of the TBC under cyclic 
heating and cooling [16]. Therefore, weaker bond coats would deform and rumple, causing

detachment of the ceramic layer in the TBC. Higher strength of the bond coat, on the other 
hand, would enable it to undergo lower extent of rumpling and consequently lead to higher 
TBC durability. Therefore, the relatively higher strength of the γ -γ coating than that of the 
typical β-(Ni,Pt)Al coatings at lower temperatures (RT-900°C) suggests that the γ-γ bond coat 
would have a better rumpling resistance. However, the intergranular fracture and re stricted 
ductility in the γ-γ bond coat as compared to the β-(Ni,Pt)Al coatings at high temperatures 
(Fig. 2d) can be a critical factor limiting the use of γ-γ bond coat in advanced TBC systems. 
According to the Ni -Pt-Al ternary phase diagram reported by Hayashi et al. [ 35], increasing 
the Pt a nd Al contents shall expand the γ phase field and is crucial for the development of 
advanced γ-γ bond coats possessing high strength and superior rumpling as well as oxidation 
resistance. 
 
5. Conclusions 
In summary, the tensile properties of a 35 µm thick freestanding γ-γ coating have been 
evaluated between RT-1100˚C and compared with that of conventional β-(Ni,Pt)Al bond coats. 
The γ-γ coating exhibits decrease in strength with increase in temperature, except for the 
increase in YS and UTS at 700°C. The strength of the coating is higher than that of β-(Ni,Pt)Al 
in the temperature range of RT -900°C and lower at higher temperatures (>900°C). It exhibits 
yielding and is relatively more ductile than the brittle β-(Ni,Pt)Al at lower temperatures (RT-
750°C). Ho wever, its ductility at higher temperatures is severely limited to ~0.2% by 
intergranular fracture when compared to the extensively ductile β-(Ni,Pt)Al coating. By virtue 
of its higher strength at low temperatures, the rumpling resistance of the γ-γ coating is expected 
to be better than that of the β-(Ni,Pt)Al coating.

Highlights 
 In a first, microtensile testing of freestanding γ-γ(35 µm thick) coating has been carried 
out between RT-1100˚C. 
 Tensile properties for the freestanding γ-γcoating are significantly different than that 
of traditional β-(Ni,Pt)Al coating, suggesting different rumpling behavior. 
 The γ-γcoating exhibits increase in strength at ~700˚C and is stronger than β-(Ni,Pt)Al 
coating until 850˚C. 
 The γ-γ coating is relatively more ductile than that of the brittle β-(Ni,Pt)Al coating up 
to 750°C. 
 Beyond 750°C, the ductility of γ-γ coating is severely restricted by intergranular 
fracture whereas the β-(Ni,Pt)Al coating exhibits appreciable ductility.
