ABSTRACT
Low angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) form during directional solidification of Ni-base single crystal superalloys
and extend over distances of one to several average dendrite spacings (mm scale). Their influence on the su-
peralloys ’ behavior has often been overlooked. In the present work we use the high-resolution rotation vector
base line electron back scatter diffraction (RVB-EBSD) technique to locate LAGBs, and focused ion beam (FIB)
micromachining to prepare specimens for investigations by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM)
and atom probe tomography (APT). STEM confirms that LAGBs consists of fine dislocation networks. APT pro-
vides experimental evidence for the segregation of Rhenium (Re) to these LAGBs in the as-cast state. RVB-EBSD
results show that during a multi-step post cast heat treatment, irregular shaped LAGBs straighten out and move
over distances of the order of several 10 µ m. Considering this LAGB movement, and in the light of previous
reports (He et al., 2020), we propose that Re segregates to LAGBs during Bridgman processing, redissolves during
the high temperature ( > 1300
◦
C) solution phase, allowing LAGB migration, and then resegregates during the
medium temperature ( < 1000
◦
C) precipitation phase of the multi-step heat treatment, which immobilizes the
LAGBs during creep loading in the 1000
◦
C temperature range. The finding of the present study shades a new light
on the behavior of Re and LAGBs in Ni-base single crystal superalloys.
1. Introduction
Highly alloyed single crystal Ni-base superalloys (SXs) like ERBO/1
(CMSX-4 type) [1] are key materials for first stage turbine blades in
modern gas turbines [2 – 6] , where they must withstand mechanical
loads at high temperatures. They are produced as technical single
crystals in an ingot metallurgy processing route which involves direc-
tional solidification in a Bridgman type process [3,5 – 7] . SXs have
complex compositions and contain up to 10 alloying elements. During
solidification, these elements tend to partition between dendritic and
interdendritic regions on a length scale of several hundred micrometers.
The resulting heterogeneous alloy composition can be homogenized by
solution heat treatments. Several examples of this type of
homogenization can be found in the literature (e.g. Fig. 5 in [1] and
Fig. 6 in [8] ). Fig. 1 provides an electron micro probe analysis (EMPA)
distribution map of Re that illustrates an example of the chemically
heterogeneous microstructure in the alloy investigated in the present
work. Beside this, even in single phase materials, the growth of single
crystals is accompanied by the formation of defects, including grown-in
dislocations and crystal mosaicity, where small ideal single crystalline
regions are separated by low angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) [9 – 12] .
The topic of crystal mosaicity in directionally solidified technical SXs
has recently received new attention e.g. [13 – 16] . It has been shown how
the evolution of the dendrite structure leads to the formation of LAGBs,
which separate groups containing up to a few equally oriented dendrites
from each other, e.g. [13 – 16] . Fig. 2 a shows an example for such a

tion, and we explore, whether Re-segregation can be detected in the
as-cast material state. We also investigate whether certain features of
LAGBs are affected by a typical post cast heat treatment, as this point is
not clear at present.

2.3. Chemical analysis on the meso and micro scale
The larger scale element distribution map shown in Fig. 1 was ob-
tained using a CAMECA SXFiveFE EPMA, equipped with a field emission
gun operating at 20 kV and a probe current close to 75 nA. Quantitative
concentration data were obtained using the X-Phi procedure from Merlet
et al. [32] . Fine scale chemical analysis was performed using atom probe
tomography (APT). APT specimens were prepared using a FEI Helios
Nanolab 600i following the in-plane lift out protocol described in [33] .
Based on RVB-EBSD analysis, regions of interest containing a LAGB were
lifted out, and specimens were sharpened such that their main axis is
perpendicular to the direction of two LAGB segments, associated with
misorientation angles close to 5
◦
. APT was performed on a LEAP 5000
XR from Cameca Instruments Inc., at a base temperature of 50 K by
applying a laser energy of 45 pJ and a pulse repetition rate of 125 kHz,
with a target detection rate was set at 5 ions every 1000 pulses. The
collected data was analyzed using the software AP Suite 6.1 from
Cameca.
2.4. Transmission electron microscopy
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed in a FEI
TECNAI G2 F20 TEM operating at 200 kV in scanning transmission
electron microscopy (STEM) mode. STEM diffraction contrast imaging
(STEM-DCI) data were captured by a high angle annular dark field
(HAADF) detector working at camera length of 300 cm. Good contrast of
all microstructural features of interest were obtained capturing STEM
images in a multi-beam condition with the [001] pole of the
single crystalline foil region oriented parallel to the electron beam [34] .
Thin foils were FIB micromachined from locations with LAGBs, identi-
fied by RV-EBSD (see previous section). The FIB of type FEI Helios G4
was operated 30 kV. Preparation artifacts were kept at a minimum
using ion polishing in a Fischione NanoMill 1040. Ion polishing was
carried out for two to four hours at 2 keV for + 15 and 10
◦
polishing
tilt angles in 30 minutes intervals. A beam energy of 0.5 keV was chosen

The findings discussed so far raise the question, how the LAGB-Re-
segregation which has been detected by He et al. [21] after the stan-
dard multi-step heat treatment is related to the segregation results
observed directly after single crystal Bridgman processing. In this
context the observation presented in the RVB-EBSD mappings in Fig. 7
are important. There is clear evidence that LAGBs evolve from the
as-cast state ( Fig. 7 a, c and e) to their final configuration after the
multi-step heat treatment ( Fig. 7 b, d and f). Most importantly, LAGB
migration is observed over distances as large as 40 µ m ( Fig. 7 e and f). We
assume that this process is driven by the reduction of LAGB energy. The
segregation of atoms to internal interfaces including subgrain bound-
aries and the effect of this segregation on interface mobility has been a
topic for materials research for several decades [38 – 48] and is still
ongoing. Grain boundary migration is defined as the movement of a
grain boundary perpendicular to the tangent plane. The elementary
process of grain boundary migration is the diffusion of one atom or a
group of atoms from the shrinking grain to the growing grain. Boundary
migration strongly depends on the crystallographic characters of the
boundary, on the mobility of atoms along the boundary, on temperature
and internal stresses. There is general agreement on the fact that
segregation slows down grain boundary mobility. In Fig. 7 we provide
microstructural evidence for the movement of a LAGB which occurs
during the multi-step heat treatment. This has to be discussed in the
context of the segregation data presented in Fig. 13 . It seems reasonable
to assume that there are different stages of Re-segregation to LAGBs.
There is a segregation of Re to LAGBs during the slow Bridgman pro-
cessing which characterizes the formation of the dendrite substructure
in Ni-base superalloys [13] . LAGBs are formed during the solidification
stage of the Bridgman process, when slightly-misoriented dendrites
collide, as shown in Figs. 4 and 5 by slice and view tomography. In this
context it is worth to note that the two pole figures representing dendrite

work is required to find out at what temperature Re segregates to LAGBs
and to take a closer look at the effect of the dislocation network spacings
and the associated subgrain misorientations on Re-segregation.
The present work has shown that LAGBs form during solidification,
Figs. 4, 5 and 6 . They represent dislocation networks which are made of
geometrically necessary dislocations [55,56] which accommodate the
misorientation between two neighboring subgrains, as well as possible
solidification stresses. LAGBs adopt their final features during the
multi-step heat treatment (5 rotational and 3 translational parameters)
where boundaries straighten out and minimize the overall strain energy.
It has been suggested that geometrically necessary dislocations
contribute to creep strength [57] , which is in line with the knitting
scenario which was recently proposed by Parsa et al. [20] . They sug-
gested that the dislocation networks which enwrap γ ʼ -particles accom-
modate the misfit between the γ - and the γ ʼ -phase and that coupled
knitting in and knitting out reactions, which involve climb, are rate
controlling processes during high temperature and low stress creep.
LAGBs also represent dislocations networks and are subjected to knitting
processes during high temperature plasticity. Thus, it is reasonable to
assume that they provide an additional contribution to creep strength.
Further work is required to study this aspect.
5. Summary and conclusions
In the present work we investigate low angle grain boundaries
(LAGBs) which account for the crystal mosaicity in single crystal Ni-base
superalloys (SXs). During solidification, LAGBs form in interdendritic
regions through growth of dendrites with slightly different crystal ori-
entations. Each dendrite grows into its own < 001 > direction as could be
documented using slice and view tomography in combination with high
resolution EBSD. LAGBs consist of fine dislocation networks which
represent geometrically necessary dislocations. Re segregates to LAGBs
during Bridgman processing, while cooling down after solidification.
LAGBs smoothen out during the multi-step heat treatment, a process
which involves interface migration. This suggests that during the ho-
mogenization state of the multi-step heat treatment, Re and also γ ʼ -
particles re-dissolve, allowing interface migration. Then, during subse-
quent cooling, or, during the precipitation stage of the multi-step heat
treatment, Re re-segregates to LAGBs. The results obtained in the present
work suggest that SX-LAGBs play a similar role as the fine dislocation
networks which enwrap γ’ -particles. Further work is required to find out
to what extend SX-LAGBs contribute to creep strength.
