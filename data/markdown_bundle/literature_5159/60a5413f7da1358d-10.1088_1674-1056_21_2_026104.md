Abstract
The status of searches for physics beyond the Standard Model is reviewed, in the light of the
data from the Large Hadron Collider runs at 7 and 8 TeV centre-of-mass energy.

1. Introduction
This is perhaps the most exciting time for fundamental physics
for over a century. By 1900, the world-view of classical
physics, founded on a Gallilean understanding of space
and time, a Newtonian picture of dynamics and Maxwell’s
description of electromagnetism was so solidly veriﬁed that
it was possible to imagine that no new physics remained to be
discovered. However, this view did not survive for long. The
discovery of new effects such as radioactivity, null results like
the Michelson–Morley experiment and theoretical problems
like the instability of the planetary atom model, led ultimately
to a non-classical picture including quantum mechanics and
relativity as completely new effects. Naturally enough, these
effects appeared in the regimes where classical physics had
never been tested: very small energies, and very high relative
velocities. This led to a more open view of physics, in which
new perspectives could be expected to open up as the reach of
experiments increased.
We are in a similar situation today. There are new results:
compelling evidence for dark matter and dark energy from
astrophysics, as well as the recent discovery of the Higgs
boson. There are interesting null results: all searches for
supersymmetry (SUSY), extra space dimensions, technicolour
and other exotics have so far proved negative, in spite of
very persuasive arguments that some new phenomena should
appear at the TeV energy scale. There are also unsolved
theoretical problems, one of which is the hierarchy problem:
how can a scalar Higgs boson have a light mass? So although
the Standard Model (SM) appears to be as solid as classical
physics once claimed, it faces challenges which it seems
unable to address. The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) will
continue to extend its reach above the TeV scale with future
running, and it is likely that end of the SM may come into
sight, just as the Higgs discovery completes it.
Hence it is fair to claim that this is the most exciting
time for physics since the overthrow of the classical
picture: something new is anticipated, but we have no clear
understanding of what form it might take. The ﬁeld is open
for new ideas and new experimental results.
Before the LHC began its run, there was an impressive
theoretical ediﬁce built on the success of the SM. The
difﬁculty in stabilizing the Higgs mass at the weak scale
was dealt with by SUSY [ 1–9], cancelling the divergent
contributions to the Higgs mass [ 10–13] with new diagrams
involving the super-partners of the known ﬁelds [ 14–19].
SUSY models could easily supply suitable weakly interacting
massive particles consistent with the observations of dark
matter [ 20, 21]. They also integrated well into grand uniﬁed
theories, giving long proton lifetimes [ 18, 22–29]. Extra
space dimensions could also be used to cure the Higgs
mass instability, and taken together with SUSY they gave all
the ingredients for superstring theories, which could ﬁnally
attempt to model quantum gravity and produce theories of
everything.
The lack of evidence for any non-standard model
particles so far at the LHC casts serious doubt over this
attractive scenario. The expectation that new physics should
be found in the TeV range is based to a large part on
naturalness, the idea that the physics of the Universe
should not depend on unlikely random relationships between
parameters. This concept is now hotly debated.

in its favour. The dark matter problem is neatly addressed
by a lightest-SUSY particle (LSP), if a symmetry exists to
prevent it from decaying to SM particles. The contribution

Since the divergence in the Higgs mass is dominated
by loops involving top quarks, it is not necessary for all the
SUSY particles to be light: a spectrum involving a light LSP
and a light stop quark can produce acceptable results, even
if most other SUSY particles are beyond the reach of the
LHC [30, 31]. The sensitivity of the LHC depends on the class
of particles being sought, with the third-generation squarks
being harder to detect than ﬁrst generation or gluinos. This
means that the current LHC limits do not eliminate some
viable SUSY spectra. Constrained models, which are driven
by top-down parameter sets fare less well. For example, the
minimal supersymmetric standard model (MSSM) prediction
of degenerate squark masses struggles with the existing
experimental results.
Strong constraints on SUSY result from generic searches
for strong production of SUSY states, such as the A TLAS
zero-lepton search [ 32]. It is based on the idea that escaping
LSPs will create missing energy, and that the highest rate
production processes will be strong production of squarks and
gluinos. The discriminating variable is meff (incl.) which sums
the transverse momenta ( pT) of all jets with pT > 40 GeV
together with the missing energy.

Since the mass limits on ﬁrst and second-generation
squarks are now well above 1 TeV , attention has turned
to the third generation. As noted above, the hierarchy
problem can be resolved as long as the dominant loop
diagrams coupling the top quark to the Higgs are cancelled
by stop loops. It is therefore possible to postulate spectra
with light stop (and perhaps sbottom) quarks. Such spectra
normally also contain light gauginos, and a gluino which
is not too heavy. They evade generic searches because the
third-generation squarks have low production cross-sections,
as do the weakly produced gauginos. The mass limits
inferred from the LHC data are currently compatible with
solutions to the hierarchy problem without invoking large
ﬁne-tuning.

There are still escape routes for SUSY without simply
retreating to higher mass scales at the cost of higher levels
of ﬁne-tuning. The assumption that ﬁnal states contain

the detectors, leading to weaker limits. In extreme cases,
with very small mass splittings, the ﬁnal decay to the LSP
can be very slow, leading to a pseudo-stable state which is

in models of new physics at the TeV scale. These include
high mass vector bosons, excitations of SM particles in
composite models, spectra of new bound states in technicolour
models, leptoquarks, fourth-generation quarks and leptons,

When searching for exotic phenomena, the expectations
are strongly model dependent, and so it is wise to consider
signatures which would be expected to occur in a wide range
of cases. The invariant masses of lepton pairs and of jet
pairs are both sensitive to the presence of narrow resonances,
as well as kinematic features generated by heavy particle
decay chains. They are therefore studied very carefully.
Figure 10 shows results from A TLAS on the invariant
mass of electron–positron pairs [ 44] and from CMS on the
invariant mass of dijets [45]. The data show smooth behaviour
consistent with the SM out to 1 TeV in the dilepton case, and
as far as 3.5 TeV for the dijets. These results therefore present
a challenge to model builders across the whole range of new
physics.
4. Conclusions
The results from the LHC runs to date are a tribute to the
skills of the experimental teams and the staff running the
accelerator. The physics of the TeV region is beginning to be
revealed, but so far remains consistent with the SM, in spite of
all the hints that new physics should be within reach. However,
to produce really deﬁnitive tests, data at higher centre-of-mass
energy is required, and the LHC will soon produce it. Each
Symposium speaker was asked to present three questions
for consideration. Looking at the state of the data, and the
theoretical understanding today, I ﬁnd the following questions
to be the most pressing:
• What is dark matter, and how can we detect it in
the laboratory? This is a challenge primarily to the
experimentalists, at the LHC, at underground laboratories
and in astrophysics.
• Is naturalness a good guide when constructing theories
of new physics? This is perhaps a philosophical question,
but it is of increasing concern to theorists, as the level
of ﬁne-tuning needed to make SUSY compatible with
observation becomes large.
• Do we have solid foundations for our theories? We
apply Occam’s razor to our problems, and assume,
for example, a simple dark sector, often with a single
WIMP . We rely on the CPT theorem, Lorentz invariance
and time-independent fundamental constants. Perhaps
somewhere in our most basic assumptions is the crack
in the foundations which will ultimately bring down the
structure of the SM.
