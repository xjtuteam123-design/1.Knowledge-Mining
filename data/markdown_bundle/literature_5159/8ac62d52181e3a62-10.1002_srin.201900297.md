Influence of tempering temperature on the microstructure and mechanical 
properties of a Cr-Ni-Mo alloyed steel for rock drill applications 
Ming-Chun Zhaoa, Tuguldur Unenbayara, Ying-Chao Zhaoa*, Chao Liua, Yan Tiana, Dengfeng Yina*, Andrej Atrensb 
a, School of Materials Science and Engineering, Central South University, Changsha 410083, P.R. China. 
b, School of mechanical and Mining Engineering, University of Queensland, Brisbane, Qld4072, Australia 
* Corresponding: Dr. Dengfeng Yin: 206191@csu.edu.cn, Ying-Chao Zhao: zhaoyc99@csu.edu.cn. 
 
 
Abstract: This research investigated the mechanical properties of a Cr-Ni-Mo alloyed rock 
drill steel tempered at different temperatures from 1 50 oC to 550 oC. The highest strength, 
hardness and impact energy occurred when the steel was tempered at 180 oC. Strength, 
hardness and impact energy decreased with increasing tempering temperature from 180 oC to 
400 oC. For tempering temperatures higher than 400 oC, the steel exhibited: (i) an increase in 
impact energy; (ii) an increase in strength and hardness when tempered at 420 oC, 450 oC 
and 480 oC, and (iii) a gradual decrease in strength and hardness when tempered at higher 
than 480 oC. The mechanism of t he changes of impact energy and hardness wit h tempering 
temperature are proposed. 
Keywords: Cr-Ni-Mo alloyed Steel; Rock drill; Tempering; Impact toughness; Hardness.

1. Introduction 
The rock drill industry has developed rapidly in recent years. Rock drill steel requires 
appropriate strength and hardness, high surface wear resistance, and good toughness [1-4]. 
From the point of view of production technology , these properties can be ach ieved through 
an optimized heat treatment [5-7]. 23CrNi3Mo, a Cr -Ni-Mo steel , is widely used as rock 
drill steel at present [4], which can be heat treated to obtain a good combination of strength 
and toughness by austeni zing, quenching and tempering , because it contains the alloy ing 
elements such as Cr, Ni and Mo, which promote high hardenability [ 8-12]. However, 
23CrNi3Mo rock drill tools often suffer early failure, such as brittle failure or fatigue failure 
even though there has been relatively little wea r [4, 11]. Brittle failure greatly decreases the 
service life of rock drill tools, lowers the efficiency of rock drill ing, and causes large 
economic costs to production. These early failures are largely related to heat treatment . Too 
high or too low hardne ss for rock drill tools is obtained due to improper heat treatment. Too 
high hardness easily leads to brittle fracture while too low hardness easily causes early wear 
[11,13]. Tempering is an important part of the heat treatment, which is used (i) to obtain the 
expected microstructure having a good combination of strength and toughness, and (ii) to 
eliminate quenching stresses. In this type of rock drill steel containing Cr, Ni, and Mo such 
as 23CrNi3Mo, tempering is a critical factor affecting their mechan ical properties, because 
these alloy ing elements may cause temper brittleness in a certain range of tempering 
temperatures [14-15]. Although 23CrNi3Mo steel has been widely used in rock drill tools, 
its heat treatment, in t he rock drill industry, is typically limited to a certain specific

Blanks, 110 mm in diameter and 60 mm in length , were cut from the as-rolled round 
bar, with a size of 110 mm in diameter and 7.0 m in length. Heat treatment was carried out in 
a laborator y furnace. The blanks were heated from room temperature to 830 oC at 
approximately 18 oC /s, held at this temperature for 30 min, heated to 920 oC, held at 920 oC 
for 360 min, furnace-cooled to 830 oC in about 60 min, and oil quenched to room 
temperature. This heat treatment process for quenching is derived from the heat treatment 
process of this steel in industrial production [4]. These quenched blanks were tempered at 
150 oC, 180 oC, 200 oC, 350 oC, 400 oC, 420 oC, 450 oC, 480 oC, 510 oC and 550 oC for 240 
min followed by air cooling.

Specimens for Charpy impact test s and tensile tests were cut from the middle of the 
heat treatment blanks in the direction transverse to the rolling direction. Charpy impact tests 
were conducted at room temperature with standard V-notched specimens (10 mm × 10 mm ×

3. Results and discussion 
Fig. 1 shows the SEM micrographs of the steel tempered at 180 oC, 200 oC, 350 oC, 400 
oC. The microstructure of the steel tempered at 180 oC was mainly composed of tempered 
martensite. Some parallel martensite packets with a width of about 20 μm seemed to localize 
in the prior austenite grain boundaries with irregular shapes plus a preferred orientation. The 
prior austenite grain boundary network had completely disappeared. T here w ere some 
retained austenite grains between the martensite laths (Fig. 1a) . For the tempering 
temperature at 200 oC, the microstructure was still dominated by the tempered martensite. 
The main characteristics were similar to that produced by tempering at 180 oC (Fig. 1b). The 
diffraction peak of retained austenite was in X -ray diffraction (XRD) pattern embedded in 
Fig. 1b, in which the “●” peaks represented -Fe, the other peaks were from -Fe. When the 
tempering temperature was 350 oC, the martensite laths were less clear than those produced 
by tempering at 180 oC and 200 oC. The microstructure comprised both tempered martensite 
and tempered troostite, which implie d the transformation from tempered martensite to 
tempered troostite (Fig. 1c). The diffraction peak of retained austenite was not in the XRD

It is well known that m ost alloyed steels containing Cr and Ni show temper brittleness 
[14-15], because these two elements generally cause impurity elements such as P and S to 
segregate around prior austenite grain boundaries and thus promote temper brittleness. The 
experimental steel contains 1.28% Cr, 3.01% Ni, 0.018% P and 0.004% S as listed in Table 1. 
Temper brittleness in the experimental steel is substantiated by Fig. 2b, in which the impact 
energy after tempering at 4 00 oC was lowest. Although Mo coexisting with Cr and Mn are 
believed to restrain this temper brittleness [16-19], this effect is limited because the content 
of Mo was just 0.26% as listed in Table 1. Therefore, the temper brittleness tendency is 
substantial from 350 oC to 420 oC as depicted in Fig. 2 b for the experimental steel. As a 
consequent, tempering temperature must keep away from this temperature range. 
As stated above, the highest impact energy (53 J) and concomitantly the highest 
hardness (HRC45) after tempering at 180 oC were obtained for 23CrNi3Mo. This was 
attributed to the microstructure, which consisted of tempered martensite as shown in Fig. 1.

microstructure was mainly composed of some parallel tempered martensite laths and some 
fine precipitates were observed. The high hardness of lath martensite is derived from solid 
solution strengthening caused by carbon (C) atoms, and numerous dislocations produced by 
martensite phase transformation [20]. Furthermore, because 23CrNi3Mo is a low carbon 
steel with a high Ms temperature, sufficient carbon mobility can cause cementite 
precipitation within the martentite [ 21]. These microstructural characteristics substantiated 
the highest hardness after tempering at 180 oC. Furthermore, substructure in this low carbon 
martensite (0.22 wt.% C, as shown in Table 1) was also attributed to the highest toughness 
after tempering at 180 oC. It is reported that t his kind of substructure with inhomogeneous 
dislocation distribution has relatively -low-density dislocation areas that provide movement 
space to dislocations, thereby greatly alleviating stress concentration s and improving 
toughness [20-22]. On the other hand, 23CrNi3Mo has much Ni (3.01 wt.%), Si (0.26 wt.%) 
and Mn (0.72 wt.%, as shown in Table 1) , which promote s austenitic stabilization and 
increases the amount of retained austenite. As demonstrated in Fig. 1b, the diffraction peak 
of r etained austenite was found in XRD pattern, indicating that retained austenite was 
evidently presented in microstructure. The retained austenite was attributed to good 
toughness [ 23], and so additional impact energy was caused for the experimental steel 
tempered at 180 oC.

gradual decrease accompanied by a significant decrease in toughness, as shown in Fig. 2b, 
even though the microstructure was still tempered lath martensite, as shown in Figs. 1b and 
1c. Fig. 3c shows the TEM micrographs of the experimental steel tempered at 350 oC, in 
which more and coarser precipitates were observed compared to those tempered at 180 oC as

4. Conclusions 
1. The highest hardness (HRC 45) and impact energy (53 J) were obtained by austenization, 
oil quenching and tempering at 180 oC. 
2. The temper brittleness tendency was substantial from 350 oC to 420 oC, and tempering 
temperature must keep away from this temperature range. 
3. For tempering temperature higher than 400 oC, the impact energy increased gradually, the 
hardness firstly increased for tempering temperatures between 420 oC and 480 oC, and then 
gradually decreased . Good combinations of hardness and toughness were obtained by 
austenization, oil quenching and tempering at 450~480 oC.
