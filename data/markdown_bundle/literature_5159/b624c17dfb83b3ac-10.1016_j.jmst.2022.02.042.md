In this study, the stress relaxation (SR) behaviors of a Ti-6Al-4V alloy pre-loaded from elastic to plastic 
regions and corresponding strength evolution mechanisms at different temperatures were systematically 
studied. In order to quantitatively analyze the detailed deformation and strength evolution mechanisms 
during the whole SR tests, which is composed of the loading stage and subsequent SR stage, the evolu- 
tions of α/ β structures and dislocations have been identiﬁed by a series of microstructural observations, 
i.e., scanning electron microscopy (SEM), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), high resolution trans- 
mission electron microscopy (HRTEM), energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). A great quantity of entan- 
gled dislocations in α phase introduced by the plastic loading at temperatures below 800 °C promotes 
the emergence of SR behavior with a higher creep rate, leading to the much higher SR level with larger 
pre-load levels. Diffusion is signiﬁcantly enhanced by dislocations accumulated at interfaces with higher 
temperatures ( > 800 °C), contributing to a similar SR phenomenon under different initial strain levels. 
Apparent strength loss has been observed after SR with high temperatures or pre-loaded to the plastic 
region, e.g., 94 MPa loss for 800 °C, pre-loaded with a stain of 10% and SR for 2400 s. The strength loss 
mainly comes from the loading stage where distorted and fragmented β layers occur. The subsequent SR 
stage facilitates interfacial diffusion and results in a higher fraction of granular β phases, leading to a 
further decrease in yield strength (YS). This study enhances the understanding on the deformation and 
strength evolution mechanisms of titanium alloys with lamellar structures in the whole SR process, pro- 
viding a physical foundation for optimizing the processing parameters for manufacturing titanium alloy 
components with high accuracy and performance.

1. Introduction 
Hot forming technology has become a research hotspot with the 
increase of the proportion of titanium alloy with complex struc- 
tures in the aviation industry. Due to the good fracture tough- 
ness and high fatigue life of extruded titanium alloys developed 
recently [ 1 , 2 ], utilizing the combined processes of new hot bend- 
ing and machining with complex section proﬁles to manufacture 
curved structural parts is becoming a promising technology, es- 
pecially for large thin-walled structural products in the aerospace 
industry, such as reinforcement frames in the wing, fuselage and 
hatch door. A new hot bending process has been proposed which

of complex thermomechanical processing and SR time on strength.

In recent years, there are many reports about the thermal de- 
formation behavior and microstructure evolution of titanium al- 
loys. It is widely accepted that at high temperatures and large 
plastic deformation (strain > 50%) conditions, the two-phase mi- 
crostructure titanium alloys show a ﬂow softening phenomenon, 
due to the microstructure changes, including spheroidization of α
lamella, change of texture orientation, and ﬂow localization at a 
high strain rate [8–10] . In addition, the SR phenomenon of tita- 
nium alloy from elastic strain to permanent plastic strain at high 
temperatures has also attracted extensive attention [ 11 , 12 ]. Accord- 
ing to previous studies, the SR behavior is signiﬁcantly affected by 
temperatures and initial strain levels. Luo et al. [13] have reported 
that the creep mechanism of Ti-6Al-4V alloy with equiaxed struc- 
ture at 660 °C/10% (temperature/initial strain) is mainly grain ro- 
tation. The grain size decreases due to grain rotation and dislo- 
cation movement, which is conducive to the reduction of thresh- 
old stress (minimum stable stress in SR curve) after SR. Different 
microstructures show different creep resistance. The titanium al- 
loy with lamellar microstructure has high creep resistance at high 
temperatures, followed by bimodal and equiaxed microstructure 
[14] . When the temperature in the β-transus is cooled quickly, 
grains, colonies, and α phases of different sizes in the lamellar 
structure are gradually formed [15] . Gu et al. [16] have reported 
that dislocations will be pinned by α/ β interface in the SR pro- 
cess of plastic strain. Small α lamellar spacing will shorten the slip 
distance and improve the creep resistance. However, current stud- 
ies mainly focus on understanding the spheroidization and texture 
orientation changes of α phase during severe plastic deformation 
and SR conditions. Related studies considering the deformation be- 
havior at medium deformation levels (elastic to plastic regions) are 
rarely reported yet. 
High temperature deformation, including SR, may change the 
microstructures of the materials, which could then affect the 
mechanical properties [17–20] . Callegari et al. [21] have studied 
the behavior of severe plastic deformation (strain > 50%) of β- 
metastable titanium alloy (Ti-5553) at 950 °C (above β-transus 
temperature), and concluded that the poor mechanical properties 
are caused by the uneven substructure during deformation at high 
strain rate. Chong et al. [ 22 , 23 ] have obtained the bi-lamellar mi- 
crostructure in Ti–6Al–4V alloy with reﬁned colony sizes by a 
novel through- β-transus processing (1040 °C/60% + 940 °C/60%) 
followed by intercritical annealing (910 °C/20 min). Both tensile 
strength and ductility are signiﬁcantly improved due to the reduc- 
tion of the colony size. In the process of high temperature large 
plastic deformation (such as forging), the α lamellae will not only 
fragment due to local shear, but also deﬂect toward the direction of 
metal ﬂow [24] . The separation and coarsening of α phase caused 
by plastic deformation (25%–130%) and high temperature (820–
860 °C) will gradually spheroidize into uniform equiaxed grains 
in the subsequent heat treatment process, which leads to the im- 
provement of YS, tensile strength and elongation at room tempera- 
ture [25] . Fan et al. [26] have reported a nano/ultraﬁne-trimorphic 
structure composed of equiaxed α, irregularly fragmented α and 
α layers in Ti-5553 alloy after plastic compression deformation 
(strain > 70%) at 600 °C. These ﬁne and even nanostructures signif- 
icantly improve the strength and plasticity. The above studies have 
shown that the main reason for the loss of interfacial strengthen- 
ing is the fragmentation of the defective α phase. The effects of 
heat treatment and large plastic deformation on the mechanical 
properties of titanium alloys have been widely reported. However, 
the effects of medium deformation level in both elastic and plas- 
tic regions during SR of lamellar microstructure on the mechanical 
properties are rarely reported. 
In this study, the SR behavior, microstructure and subsequent 
YS evolution of Ti-6Al-4V in the elastic and plastic regions at 
700–850 °C were investigated. SR tests with various strain levels 
were conducted to investigate their effects on SR characteristics 
and corresponding YS. Systematic microstructural observations, e.g. 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM), electron backscatter diffrac- 
tion (EBSD), and high resolution transmission electron microscopy 
(HRTEM), were performed to examine the evolution of detailed mi- 
crostructures during the tests. With the assistance of related the- 
oretical calculations and analysis, detailed formation and strength 
evolution mechanisms under different tests have been proposed. 
This work enables a deeper understanding of the macro/micro 
mechanism of titanium alloy with lamellar microstructure during 
high temperature SR, and meanwhile provides detailed information 
for the SR related manufacturing process planning and optimiza- 
tion for curved structural components of titanium alloys. 
2. Experiments 
2.1. As-received material 
The as-received material used in this study is Ti-6Al-4V ex- 
truded proﬁle with a fully lamellar microstructure (Widmanstät- 
ten) provided by BaoTi Group (China). The thermomechanical his- 
tory of the material is shown schematically in the left part of 
Fig. 1 (a): the billet was extruded at 1050 °C, air-cooled to 750 °C 
for 2 h annealing treatment, followed by furnace cooling to room 
temperature. It has been reported that the processed material gen- 
erally consists of α colonies with different orientations, in which 
thin β layers appear between adjacent α lamellae [ 27 , 28 ]. 
2.2. SR and uniaxial tensile tests 
SR tests and subsequent tensile tests were performed in the as- 
received material, as illustrated in the right part of Fig. 1 (a). The 
samples used for both SR and tensile tests were cut from the ex- 
truded proﬁle by electrical discharge machining along the ED, as 
shown in Fig. 1 (b). 
For SR tests, the specimen was heated to the designated tem- 
perature and held in a furnace for 2 min to achieve a uniform tem- 
perature distribution. The heated specimen was then ﬁrstly loaded 
to the predetermined strain level under a quasi-static condition 
with a load speed of 0.08 mm/s and then held for a speciﬁc time 
for SR, after which, the sample was cooled to 400 °C in the fur- 
nace, and ﬁnally air-cooled to room temperature. Tensile tests at 
room temperature were then performed after SR tests to obtain 
the strength of the specimens, with a strain rate of 5 × 10 −4 s −1 . 
The SR and tensile tests were performed on a DDL-100 me- 
chanical testing machine. The specimens were coated with high- 
temperature antioxidants and three pairs of thermocouple wires 
were attached to the specimen (bottom, center and top positions) 
to record the temperatures during tests and temperature difference 
is within ±2 °C. The strains for SR were measured by an Epsilon- 
3549 high temperature extensometer, while a CBY1-25-2.5 exten- 
someter was applied to measure the strain during tensile tests. 
To avoid the signiﬁcant change in microstructures during de- 
formation, the temperatures used for the SR process are usually 
controlled below the β-transus temperature ( T β = 995 °C for Ti- 
6Al-4V). Hence, four temperatures below T β, i.e.700, 750, 800 and 
850 °C, were selected to investigate the detailed effect of tem- 
perature on the deformation and strength of the alloy during SR 
[ 29 , 30 ]. Three sets of time durations for SR process, i.e., 0 s, 1200, 
and 2400 s, were used for investigation in this study. 
The tensile curves of the as-received material at four temper- 
atures determine the yield points (0.2% offsetting) for the proper 
initial strain levels of SR tests. The two initial strain levels in the

2.3. Microstructure characterization 
To reveal the detailed deformation and strength evolution 
mechanisms in the microstructural side during SR of the alloy, var- 
ious observation tests were conducted. SEM was performed to ob- 
serve the evolution of microstructure, EBSD was conducted to ana- 
lyze the average geometrically necessary dislocation (GND) density 
and the misorientation of lamellae after different conditions, while 
the dislocation morphology and element diffusion behavior were 
characterized by TEM and EDS. The coherence relationship of the 
two-phase interface was observed by HRTEM. 
The samples to be characterized were cut from the gauge length 
of dog bone specimens along the ED. The same samples were 
used for SEM and EBSD under a JSF-7900F in this study. The sam- 
ples were mechanically ground, and electropolished in a 6%HClO 4 : 
34%C 4 H 9 OH: 60%CH 3 OH mixed solution at −25 °C and 20 V. Mate- 
rial image processing and automatic reconstruction (MIPAR) soft- 
ware was used to identify and calculate the average percentage 
and width of phases from SEM images with the same magniﬁca- 
tion taken from ﬁve random areas. Standard EBSD working con- 
ditions, i.e., 20 kV acceleration voltage with 15 mm working dis- 
tance, were used and the orientation data were collected by using 
0.2 μm step size. The GND density from four different regions with 
the same magniﬁcation was statistically calculated by the GND cal- 
culation module in AZtecCrystal software, in which the maximum 
core size was set to 5 × 5, the maximum misorientation angle was 
5 °, and the Berger vector was 0.3 nm. The scanning surfaces of all 
samples for microscopic characterization were ED-transverse direc- 
tion (ED-TD) planes. The foils were prepared by a twin-jet thinning 
technique using 6%HClO 4 : 34%C 4 H 9 OH: 60%CH 3 OH mixed solution 
at −30 °C and 45 V. The TEM/HRTEM and EDS were performed on 
a TECNAI G2 F30. 
3. Results 
3.1. SR with different strain levels and temperatures 
Fig. 2 shows the SR results with different initial strain lev- 
els at the test temperatures. All SR curves have a logarithmic de- 
creasing trend and can be divided into two stages: the ﬁrst rapid 
stress decreasing stage, and the second stable stress stage. The 
same trend of the SR curve has been explained by the competi- 
tion between dislocation propagation and dislocation recovery [6] .

The stable level of stress during SR can be approximately treated 
as the threshold stress of the material under the tested conditions 
[ 31 , 32 ]. The threshold stress after SR in the temperature range of 
700-750 °C is usually maintained at a high value, for example, the 
threshold stresses for 700 °C/0.5%, 700 °C/1.5% and 700 °C/10% con- 
ditions are as high as 32, 20, and 21 MPa, respectively. While the 
threshold stress after SR in the temperature range of 800-850 °C 
tends to lower, for example, the threshold stress corresponding 
to 80 0 °C/0.5%, 80 0 °C/1.5%, and 80 0 °C/10% conditions are only 
5, 7 and 6.9 MPa, respectively. The results show that the maxi- 
mum variation range of threshold stresses reaches a low level after 
2400 s at the test temperatures, about 10, 5, 4 and 3 MPa at 700, 
750, 800 and 850 °C, respectively. At 700 and 750 °C ( Fig. 2 (a)), the 
more signiﬁcant slope of SR curves with larger threshold stresses is 
observed with increasing initial strains in the elastic region, while 
an opposite trend of threshold stress is observed in the plastic re- 
gion. The higher the initial strain level, the lower the threshold 
stress. However, at 800 and 850 °C ( Fig. 2 (a)), all SR curves at dif- 
ferent initial strain levels show almost the same trend and thresh- 
old stress. 
To further compare the effects of elastic and plastic initial 
strains on SR behavior at different temperatures, relaxed stress per- 
centage, deﬁned as the ratio of the total relaxed stress to the ini- 
tial stress, is used and plotted in Fig. 2 (b). All curves show a fast- 
increasing trend in the elastic region and the increasing speed de- 
celerates after entering the plastic region. Meanwhile, the temper- 
ature also plays an important role in affecting the relaxation speed. 
The curves at 800 °C/800 s and 800 °C/2400 s have a horizon- 
tal trend, which indicates that the stress caused by initial loading 
reaches the threshold limit faster at higher temperatures. The dif- 
ferent SR behaviors could suggest distinct SR mechanisms at dif- 
ferent strain levels (elastic and plastic regions) and different tem- 
peratures investigated comparatively lower temperatures (700 and 
750 °C) and higher temperatures (800 and 850 °C). Typical temper- 
atures in the two temperature ranges, 700 and 800 °C are selected 
for comparison in the following study for further investigation. 
3.2. YS evolution during SR 
The development of YS at room temperature during SR of Ti- 
6Al-4V under different initial strain and temperature conditions is 
shown in Fig. 3 . The strength is only minorly affected by SR in the 
elastic region, but it decreases obviously when the initial strain en- 
ters the plastic region at lower temperatures. It should be noted 
that the YS loss is contributed by two stages of the tests, including 
initial loading and subsequent SR stages, as shown in Fig. 3 (b). The 
percentage is calculated by the YS loss at the current stage divided 
by the YS of as-received material. Most of the YS loss comes from 
the loading stage (the drop in YS from the as-received level to the 
value at 0 s) rather than the subsequent SR (the drop of YS after 
the SR stage). The strength in the two stages decreases by 67 MPa 
(7.7%) and 27 MPa (3.1%), respectively at 800 °C/10%. Temperature 
plays a key role in the loading stage. For example, only a 3 MPa 
(0.3%) variation of YS occurs after loading at 700 °C/0.5%, while it 
becomes 33 MPa (3.8%) at 800 °C/0.5%. The initial strain level ef- 
fect cannot be ignored. The YS loss at 800 °C/10% is nearly twice 
that at 800 °C/0.5%. Previous studies have shown that the strength 
of titanium alloy was determined by the lamellar microstructure 
[33] , indicating that the morphology of the two-phase structure 
may change during the loading and SR stages at the test tempera- 
ture and initial strain range considered in this study. The lower YS 
decrease in the SR stage is likely due to the distinct contributions 
of various strengthening mechanisms under different conditions. 
3.3. Phase changes during SR 
Obvious differences can be observed in the SR behavior of 
both elastic and plastic regions in the temperature range of 700–
750 °C, rather than in the range of 800–850 °C. Therefore, it 
is necessary to select two strain levels (0.5% and 10%) in these 
two temperature ranges to explain the phenomenon of SR behav- 
ior. In addition, the strength loss after elastic SR in the temper- 
ature range of 700–750 °C is almost negligible, which promotes 
the abandonment of cases in the elastic region. It is easy to con- 
clude from Fig. 3 (b) that the strength loss mainly comes from the 
loading stage. It is not appropriate to design microscopic cases 
at different times in the SR stage. In summary, the initial ma- 
terials, 700 °C/10%/2400 s, 800 °C/0.5%/0 s, 800 °C/0.5%/2400 s, 
800 °C/10%/0 s and 800 °C/10%/2400 s were selected. Fig. 4 shows 
the SEM results of phase distributions in the material under dif- 
ferent test conditions. The microstructure of the as-received ma- 
terial consists of α phase and β phase in Fig. 4 (a), similar to the 
results reported previously [15] , consisting of differently-oriented 
colonies with parallel α lamellae and β layers in a single grain. The 
β phases partly fragment after SR at 700 °C/10%/2400 s when com- 
pared with the as-received material ( Fig. 4 (b)). Similar fragmented 
β phases have been reported by Zherebtsov who believed that the 
penetration of α phase directly through the transverse boundary 
of the β phase resulted in pinch-off of the β layers [34] . At higher 
temperatures, more fragmented β phases, even with some granule 
shapes, can be observed after loading, as shown in Fig. 4 (c, e) un- 
der 800 °C/0.5% and 800 °C/10% conditions. In addition, apparent

3.4. Variations of GND and misorientation during SR 
In general, the local misorientations could be characterized by 
the kernel average misorientation (KAM) based on EBSD measure- 
ments, as shown in Fig. 6 (a). GND map in Fig. 6 (b) is calculated 
based on the average local misorientations of different regions in 
the experimental results. It can be seen that the hot spots on both 
KAM map and GND map agree well. This is reasonable as GND 
is used to accommodate the misorientation across the geometri- 
cally conﬁned crystals. A low-density of dislocations (800 °C/0.5%) 
introduced by the initial strain in the elastic region experience 
a slight drop after SR. Higher initial strain levels in the loading 
stage promote the accumulation of density dislocations (e.g. 700 
and 800 °C/10% cases in Fig. 6 (c)). In the subsequent SR stage, the 
high-density dislocations propagate initially. The accumulation and 
recovery of dislocations compete mutually during SR stage, which 
leads to the saturation of dislocation density. Finally, a decreasing 
trend of dislocation density at the end of SR is observed. 
High density dislocations and local orientation changes occur 
almost simultaneously. The relationships between the initial strain 
level and the orientation are shown in Fig. 7 . The blue and black 
lines denote low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) (2 ° < LAGBs < 
10 °), and high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs) (HAGBs > 10 °), re- 
spectively. With increasing initial strain levels, the LAGBs in the 
colonies with the same orientation are denser ( Fig. 7 (c)). Many 
LAGBs are formed in the interior of the colony where the β layers 
are distorted, which implies that dislocation activities are the cause 
of β distortion. Point-to-point (PTP) is the lattice misorientation 
between adjacent phases, which can reﬂect the local deformation 
of the colonies. Point-to-origin (PTO) is the cumulative misorienta- 
tion, which implies the distorted level of α phase in the line direc- 
tion caused by initial loading. The cumulative misorientation from 
the PTO along the AB direction is calculated up to 1 ° and 2.25 °, re- 
spectively ( Fig. 7 (d, e)). Similar misorientation indicates that the β
layers with parallel characteristics can still be seen in the colony. 
However, the cumulative misorientation of the β layer has reached 
7.6 ° when the initial strain increases to 10% ( Fig. 7 (f)). The disloca- 
tion densities at the SR in Fig. 6 and cumulative misorientation in 
Fig. 7 are directly proportional to the initial strain level. The local 
concentrated stress caused by accumulated dislocations related to 
thermomechanical processing increases the misorientation [ 28 , 34 ], 
which may be one of the reasons for strength loss. The relation- 
ship among dislocation, misorientation caused by initial strain in 
elastic or plastic regions, and strength will be discussed in detail 
in Section 4.3 . 
3.5. Element diffusion during SR 
Fig. 8 shows the elemental distributions of Al and V between 
the α lamellae and the β layers. The line scan of the element con- 
tent in the β layer and adjacent α phases is presented in Fig. 9 . 
Compared with the α and β phases in Fig. 8 (a, b), the interfaces 
of the two phases in Fig. 8 (c) become indistinguishable. It is re- 
ported that Al and V, stable elements of α phase and β phase, are 
enriched in the two phases, respectively [ 35 , 36 ]. With increasing 
strain level, it seems that Al prefers to diffuse into β phase, and 
V in β phase can diffuse into α phase, leading to a diffused α/ β
interface as seen in Fig. 8 (c). A line scan in steps of 1 nm was 
performed along the selected α/ β interface. The scanning curves 
in Fig. 9 (a, b) clearly prove that the V distribution is nonuniform. 
There is a peak in the center of β phase, i.e., V enrichment, and a 
lower concentration at the interface. The peak values of Al and V in 
the β lath without SR are approximately 25% and 2.5% ( Fig. 9 (a)). 
Although the SR in the elastic region still retains a clear two-phase 
structure, V in the β lath decreases to 18.28% and Al increases to 
4.42% ( Fig. 9 (b)). It is obvious from the results in Fig. 9 (c) that 
there are no peaks in the curves of Al and V. The free energy of 
the system decreases with the gradual transformation between α
phase and β phase at a higher temperature, which leads to the 
variation of Al and V chemical potential in α and β phases [35] . 
4. Discussion 
4.1. Dislocation and diffusion evolution during SR 
Previous studies have reported that SR mechanisms are highly 
dependent on temperatures [37–39] . For example, the phase tran- 
sition from α phase to β phase at high temperatures, the growth 
of β phase into coarse α phase, and the dislocation climb are con- 
sidered as the main reasons for the difference in the SR behavior 
of lamellar structure [ 33 , 40 ]. Although the dislocation activities de- 
veloped by different initial strains are similar at 70 0 and 80 0 °C in 
the experimental results of Section 3 , the diffusion activities during 
SR at lower and higher temperatures are totally different. Hence 
it can be postulated that the interactions between dislocation and 
diffusion mechanisms are the cause of the difference in the SR be- 
havior of lamellar-structured titanium alloys.

affecting the SR behavior at 700–750 °C [ 41 , 42 ]. The HRTEM

high level of SR, they will lead to signiﬁcant deterioration of me-

ufacturing processes utilizing SR, the process temperature should 
then be carefully selected according to the strain levels. For ex- 
ample, when the initial strain slightly exceeds the yield point at 
approximately 700 °C, the relaxation percentage of approximately 
90% can be obtained in 120 0–180 0 s, and the severe YS loss can be 
avoided after the hot bending process. When a larger initial strain 
cannot be avoided, the strain can be reasonably distributed by in- 
creasing the cross-sectional area of the extruded proﬁle, ensuring 
the strength of the parts. 
5. Conclusions 
The different SR behaviors, YS evolutions and corresponding 
mechanisms of extruded Ti-6Al-4V alloy with lamellar microstruc- 
tures under various stress levels (elastic and plastic regions) and 
temperatures have been investigated through a series of SR tests, 
SEM, EBSD, EDS and TEM observations. The following conclusions 
can be drawn: 
(1) The higher dislocation density introduced by initial plastic 
strain than that by elastic strain at 700–750 °C contributes to 
larger SR rates. The threshold stresses at 0.5%, 1.5% and 10% 
for 700 °C are 32, 20 and 21 MPa, respectively, and the max- 
imum variation range of threshold stress exceeds 10 MPa. The

enhanced diffusion activities at 800–850 °C eliminate the differ- 
ences from dislocation densities with elastic and plastic strains. 
The threshold stresses at 0.5%, 1.5% and 10% for 800 °C are 5, 
7 and 6.9 MPa, respectively, and the maximum variation range 
is within 4 MPa.Sparse and parallel dislocations are generated 
in the α phase after elastic loading. While after plastic loading, 
both entangled dislocations in the α phase and dislocations at 
the α/ β interface are present, assisting the subsequent SR. The 
stress concentration promoted by dislocation at interfaces leads 
to the β layers with an increased misorientation (more than 
7 °). At high temperatures, with increasing initial strain levels, 
the movement of high-density dislocations facilitates the en- 
hancement of the diffusion creep mechanism. The contribution 
of the two mechanisms promotes the emergence of fragmented 
β phase after loading and the indistinguishable β phase after 
SR. 
(2) Apparent YS deterioration in the whole SR process has been ob- 
served. Most of YS deterioration (about 7.7%) occurs after load- 
ing, due to distortion and fragmentation of lamellar microstruc- 
ture, especially when loaded to plastic region. Subsequent SR 
can enhance the fragmentation and granulation of β phases, 
resulting in a continuous decrease in the YS of the alloy. The 
higher temperatures and larger dislocation densities both lead 
to a more apparent diffusion and higher strength loss in the 
material during SR. 
(3) A reasonable range of process parameters, such as relaxation at 
the initial strain near the yield point at about 700 °C/1200 s, 
for the manufacture of the curved structural part of Ti-6Al- 
4V titanium alloy with SR process can be given, achiev- 
ing approximately 90% relaxation percentage with minor YS 
deterioration.
