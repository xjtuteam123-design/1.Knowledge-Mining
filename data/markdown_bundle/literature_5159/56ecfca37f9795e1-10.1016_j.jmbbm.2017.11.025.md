ABSTRACT
The eﬀ ects of thermomechanical treatment on the microstructure and mechanical properties of a newly de-
veloped β titanium alloy, i.e., Ti –28 Nb–35.4Zr (wt%, hereafter denoted Ti –Nb–Zr) were investigated. The as-
cast Ti –Nb–Zr alloy was subjected to solution treatment at 890 °C for 1 h, after which its thickness was reduced
by 20%, 56%, 76%, and 86% via cold rolling. Results indicated that annealing at 890 °C for 1 h after cold rolling
at a thickness reduction ratio of 86% resulted in a phase transformation from the stress-induced α” and ω into β,
leading to a recrystallization of a uniform single β phase. The recrystallized Ti –Nb–Zr alloy exhibited a tensile
strength of 633 MPa, Young's modulus of 63 GPa, and elongation at rupture of 13%, respectively. The cold rolled
specimens showed a higher Young's modulus than that of the recrystallized specimen due to the stress-induced ω
phase. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that ω, α” and β phases co-existed in the
microstructure of the cold-rolled specimens. Electron backscatter di ﬀraction analysis revealed that the de-
formation mechanisms during thermomechanical processing included kink bands, {332} < 113 > twins and
shear bands; and the predominant deformation mechanism depended on the extent of CR deformation.
1. Introduction
Metallic biomaterials have the oldest history when compared with
other biomaterials ( Niinomi, 2002), and stainless steels were the ﬁrst
metals used in orthopedics in 1926 ( Nouri and Wen, 2015 ). Commer-
cially pure titanium (CP-Ti) and titanium alloys are currently preferred
as orthopedic implant materials due to their excellent biocompatibility
(Geetha et al., 2009), high corrosion resistance ( Niinomi, 1998 ), and
relatively low Young's modulus ( Long and Rack, 1998 ). Titanium alloys
can be used as hip- and knee- joint prostheses, bone plates, screws, and
nails for fractures in the ﬁeld of orthopedics ( Destefani, 1990). The
superior mechanical, physical, and biological performances of CP-Ti
and titanium alloys in comparison with other metallic materials play an
important role in making them one of the most attractive materials for
biomedical applications ( Niinomi, 1998; Rack and Qazi, 2006). One of
the most attractive β-type Ti alloys developed for biomedical applica-
tions is Ti-13 Nb-13Zr (wt%). The Ti-13Nb-13Zr alloy, developed by
Davidson and Kovacs ( Davidson and Kovacs, 1992 ), has been used for
orthopedic and dental implant applications due to its mechanical and
biological compatibilities ( Davidson et al., 1994; Geetha et al., 2001 ).
Ti-13Nb-13Zr showed a wide range of mechanical properties after
various thermomechanical processes, with Young's modulus and yield
strength being in the range of 79– 84 GPa, and 836 –908 MPa, respec-
tively ( Niinomi, 1998 ). New Ti-Nb-Zr alloys with di ﬀerent concentra-
tions of niobium and zirconium for biomedical applications were also
reported by di ﬀerent research groups and their Young's modulus and
yield strength ranged from 59 to 75 GPa and 345– 810 MPa, respec-
tively ( Ning et al., 2010; Ozan et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2013).
The d-electron design method has been extensively used by re-
searchers working on β and metastable β titanium alloys and their
mechanical properties ( Biesiekierski et al., 2016, 2017; Kuroda et al.,
1998; Lin et al., 2016; Matsugi et al., 2010; Ozan et al., 2015; Saito
et al., 2003; Zhang et al., 2011). Our previous studies ( Lin et al., 2016;
Ozan et al., 2015 ) have demonstrated that new β titanium alloys de-
signed by using the delectron design method ( Morinaga et al., 1988 )
combined with the molybdenum equivalence (Mo
eq)( Bania, 1994) and
electron-to-atom ratio (e/a) ( Boyer et al., 1994 ) approaches showed an
excellent combination of mechanical properties for biomedical appli-
cations.

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Materials preparation
A cold crucible levitation melting (CCLM) method, which presents
an advantage in terms of enabling homogenous melting between al-
loying elements with large melting point di ﬀerences ( Matsugi et al.,
2010), was used for the preparation of the as-cast alloy. An ingot with a
nominal composition of Ti –28 Nb–35.4Zr (wt%, hereafter denoted
Ti–Nb–Zr) was re-melted 5 times in order to guarantee chemical
homogeneity. The as-cast Ti –Nb–Zr alloy ingot was subjected to solu-
tion treatment at 890 °C for 1 h to exclude micro-segregation, followed
by quenching in water. This group of specimens is denoted ST. In the
next stage, the thickness of the ST alloys was reduced progressively by
20%, 56%, 76%, and 86% via CR. ST alloy specimens with their
thickness reduced by 20%, 56%, 76%, and 86% are denoted CR-1, CR-
2, CR-3, and CR-4, respectively. In addition, CR-4 specimens were an-
nealed at 890 °C for 1 h for recrystallization and this group of specimens
is denoted CRST. Fig. 1 shows the processing steps of the Ti –Nb–Zr alloy
specimens schematically.
2.2. Microstructural characterization
For X-ray di ﬀraction (XRD) analysis, microstructural observation,
electron backscatter di ﬀraction (EBSD) analysis and micro-hardness
measurement, disc specimens with diameter 8 mm and thickness 2 mm
were used. These specimens were cut by electrical discharge machining
(EDM) and ground progressively via 200, 320, 1200, 2400, and 4000
grit SiC grinding papers, followed by mirror polishing using a colloidal
silica. XRD analysis of the specimens was carried out over a 2 θ range
from 20° to 90° at a scanning rate of 0.5°/s with a step size of 0.02°
using a Cu K α radiation source (D8 Advance, Bruker). Micro-hardness
of the specimens was measured using a 300 g force and held for 15 s.
Ten diﬀerent
measurements were performed and the average value was
calculated. For optical microstructural observation, specimens were
etched with Kroll solution consisting of 4% HF, 6% HNO
3, and 90%
H2O. EBSD analysis of the specimens was carried out using a ﬁeld-
emission scanning electron microscope (FEI Nova NanoSEM).
For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis, discs with
3 mm diameter and 400 µm thick were cut from the bulk specimens by
EDM. The discs were then manually ground progressively via 600,
1200, and 2400 grit SiC grinding papers down to 100 µm thick. The
thin foil specimens were mechanically dimpled using a dimple grinder
(Gatan Inc., Model 656) to 20 µm thickness using a 9 µm diamond
paste, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in pure ethanol solvent for 180 s.
Argon ion milling of the dimpled specimens was then performed using
an ion-slicer (JEOL, 09100IS). Specimens were ion-milled for ~1.5 h
until holes appeared at the center. TEM analysis was performed on a
LaB6 transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of
200 kV (JEOL 2010).
2.3. Mechanical property testing
Tensile tests were carried out in accordance with the procedures
listed in ASTM standard E8/E8M ( ASTM, 2016 ). The specimens were
cut in the CR direction by EDM. Both surfaces of the specimens were
progressively ground via 200, 320, 1200, and 2400 grit SiC grinding
paper. The tests were performed at a strain rate of 8 × 10 −4 s−1 at
room temperature. Averages of the mechanical property values were
obtained from 5 tensile specimens for each condition. The yield
strengths of the specimens were calculated using the 0.2% o ﬀset
method, whereas the Young's modulus values were calculated using the
slope of the linear part of the stress –strain curves. The total energy
absorbed in the course of tensile deformation until fracture occurrence
is deﬁ ned as toughness, while the energy absorbed during elastic de-
formation until elastic limit is deﬁ ned as the modulus of resilience, i.e.,
elastic energy ( Callister, 2007 ). The elastic energy of the specimens is
represented by the area under the elastic region in their stress –strain
curves, whereas the toughness is represented by the total area under-
neath the tensile curves. A ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscope
(SEM, ZEISS SUPRA 40 VP) was used to examine the fracture surfaces of
the specimens after tensile tests.
2.3.1. Statistical analysis
Mechanical properties of the Ti –Nb–Zr alloy specimens were ana-
lyzed via one-way ANOVA ( ρ < 0.05). CP-Ti (grade 2), used as a re-
ference group for the evaluation of the tensile mechanical properties,
was tested under the same conditions as the Ti –Nb–Zr alloy specimens.
In addition, Tukey's HSD post hoc test was performed to determine the
signiﬁcant di ﬀerence between groups and, at ρ < 0.05, the di ﬀerences
among the specimens were accepted as statistically signi ﬁcant.

The XRD patterns of the Ti –Nb–Zr alloy specimens after di ﬀerent
thermo-mechanical processes are given in Fig. 3. It can be seen that all
the specimens exhibited (200)
β peaks. However, the ST and CRST
specimens exhibited peaks attributable to only a β phase, as shown in
Figs. 3a and 3f, while the cold rolled specimens exhibited not only
(200)β peaks, but also (200) α” peaks ( Fig. 3b-e). This con ﬁrmed the
formation of a stress-induced α” during CR. Metastable β-type titanium
alloys, which present a martensite start temperature below room tem-
perature, exhibit martensitic transformation upon applying external
loads, resulting in the formation of α” (Hanada et al., 2013; Matsumoto
et al., 2007 ), or ω (Nakai et al., 2011; Zhao et al., 2011a, 2011b, 2012 ),
or a combination of both of them ( Yang et al., 2010). The formation of
stress-induced α” and/or ω following CR process of a β type alloy is
linked to its electronic parameters ( Lin et al., 2016; Ozan et al., 2015 ),
e/a ratio ( Ikehata et al., 2004) and Mo eq (Weiss and Semiatin, 1998 ).
When considering the metastability of a β-type titanium alloy, it is of
importance to consider the Moeq ( Bania, 1994), and d-electron para-
meters (Morinaga et al., 1988 ) and e/a ratio ( Boyer et al., 1994 ). If the
Moeq value of the alloy is higher than 8 ( Weiss and Semiatin, 1998 ) and
e/a ratio higher than 4.2 ( Ikeheta et al., 2004), it is then possible for a
β-type titanium alloy to remain in stable state when quenched from a
temperature above the β-transus temperature without martensitic
transformation phases ( Zhou et al., 2004). The Mo eq value of the Ti-Nb-
Zr alloy investigated in this study is 7.84 ( Ozan et al., 2015 ), slightly
less than that of critical value; however, the Ti-Nb-Zr alloy is evaluated
to be a metastable β-type titanium alloy as stress-induced α” was found
to exist in the microstructure upon applying CR.

3.3. Micro-hardness
The micro-hardness values of the Ti –Nb–Zr alloy specimens after
various thermo-mechanical processes are given in Fig. 8. The average
micro-hardness values of CR-1, CR-2, CR-3, CR-4, and CRST specimens
are 262 ± 9 HV, 275 ± 8 HV, 280 ± 6 HV, 287 ± 8 HV, and 251 ± 9
HV, respectively. It can be seen that the micro-hardness of the speci-
mens is in the order: CR-4 > CR-3 > CR-2 > CR-1 > CRST. The CR-4
specimen, with a CR reduction ratio of 86%, exhibited the highest
micro-hardness of 287 HV. The CRST specimen showed a lower micro-
hardness compared to the cold rolled specimens due to its recrystallized
β phase microstructure. The increase in hardness of cold rolled speci-
mens is attributed to the ﬁner grains, increasing dislocation density and
tangles, and stress-induced ω phase.
3.4. Tensile properties
Fig. 9 shows the tensile stress –strain curves of the Ti –Nb–Zr alloy
specimens after di ﬀerent thermomechanical processes. The tensile
strength, yield strength, and elongation at rupture values of the
Ti–Nb–Zr alloy specimens are presented in Fig. 10 . The average tensile
strengths are 470, 763, 776, 857, 941, and 633 MPa, and the yield
strengths are 350, 708, 717, 785, 890, and 611 MPa for CP-Ti, CR-1,
CR-2, CR-3, CR-4, and CRST, respectively. After CR, the tensile strength
of all alloy specimens increased while the elongation at rupture de-
creased. Further, the change in tensile strength and elongation at rup-
ture is attributed to increased dislocation density, dislocation tangles
and the formation of the stress-induced ω phase. Notably, ω particles
are evaluated to be e ﬀective in increasing the mechanical strength of β-
type titanium alloys as reported elsewhere ( Lin et al., 2016; Ozan et al.,
2017; Ping, 2014).
The thermo-mechanically processed Ti –Nb–Zr alloys can be listed in
decreasing order with regard to their tensile strength and yield
strength: CR-4 > CR-3 > CR-2 > CR-1 > CRST. The average elonga-

21%, 8%, 5%, 5%, 4%, and 13%, respectively. The CRST specimen
showed the highest elongation and CR-4 specimen showed the lowest
elongation. It can be seen that CR-4 showed the highest tensile strength
and yield strength, and both the yield strength and tensile strength of
the specimens increased with an increase in the CR reduction ratio,
while the elongation at rupture decreased with the CR reduction ratio.

deformation during tensile testing. On the other hand, the CRST spe-

3.5. E ﬀects of deformation products on Young's modulus
In case a texture rather than (200) α” is developed during deforma-
tion of metastable β-type titanium alloys, it is then possible to observe
an increase in Young's modulus ( Matsumoto et al., 2007). Although a
(200)α″ texture was observed in the cold rolled specimens ( Fig. 3b-e), it
should be noted that the CRST specimen exhibited a Young's modulus
lower than that of the cold rolled specimens ( Fig. 11 ). Here, it can be
explained that the formation of the stress-induced α” and ω phases
signiﬁcantly a ﬀected the Young's modulus of the thermomechanically
processed Ti-Nb-Zr alloy. Taking into consideration that stress-induced
ω exhibits higher elastic modulus than those of α” and β phases ( Hao
et al., 2003; Niinomi et al., 2002 ), the increase in Young's modulus of
cold rolled specimens can be assumed to be originated from the stress-
induced ω phase. TEM observations con ﬁrmed a stress-induced ω in the
cold rolled specimens. Further, the e ﬀect of stress-induced ω can be
considered exceeding that of the stress-induced α″ on the Young's
modulus, resulting in an increase in the Young's modulus of cold rolled
specimens.
It has been reported that Zr and Sn elements inhibit the formation of
athermal ω and α” metastable phases in β-type titanium alloys ( Hao
et al., 2006 ). For binary Ti-Nb alloys, the minimum addition of Nb
required to stabilize the β-phase is 38% ( Hu et al., 2008 ), while the Ti-
Nb-Zr alloy in this study contains 28% Nb; its content of 35.4% Zr re-
duced the amount of Nb required to maintain β phase stability. As long
as the formation of metastable phases can be suppressed during
quenching with a careful selection of the alloying elements in the de-
sign of the β-type titanium alloys, it is possible to obtain β-type tita-
nium alloys with low Young's modulus ( Hao et al., 2007). In this study,

4. Conclusions
In this study, the e ﬀects of thermomechanical processes on the
mechanical properties of a Ti –Nb–Zr alloy were investigated. The ten-
sile strength of the alloy increased with an increase in the cold rolling
ratio, whereas the ductility, i.e., the elongation at rupture decreased.
The Ti –Nb–Zr alloy after cold rolling at a thickness reduction ratio of
86% followed by annealing exhibited lower Young's modulus than that
of the cold rolled specimens, and yet retained high mechanical strength
and ductility.
The deformation mechanisms during thermomechanical processing
included kink bands, {332} < 113 > twins, formation of stress-induced
α” and ω and shear bands. Notably, the predominant deformation
mechanism depended on the extent of CR deformation.
The Ti –Nb–Zr alloy specimens after thermo-mechanical processing
exhibited Young's modulus values ranging from 63 to 79 GPa. Phases of
β, and stress-induced ω and α” were detected in the microstructure of
cold rolled specimens. The cold rolled specimens exhibited higher
Young's modulus than that of the recrystallized specimen, resulted from
the stress-induced ω phase. TEM observations con ﬁrmed a stress-in-
duced ω via {332} < 113 > twinning during cold deformation.
The Ti –Nb–Zr alloy after various thermo-mechanical processing
showed average tensile strengths from 633 to 941 MPa, elastic ad-
missible strains from 0.90% to 1.27%, and elongation at rupture from
4% to 13%. The CRST specimen exhibited the highest elongation at
rupture (13%) and the lowest Young's modulus (63 GPa). The Ti –Nb–Zr
alloy is promising for biomedical applications due to its composing of
entirely biocompatible alloying elements and excellent mechanical
properties.
