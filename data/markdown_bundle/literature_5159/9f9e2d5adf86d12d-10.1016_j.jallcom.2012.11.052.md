abstract
The effect of Mo on the microstructure and mechanical properties of Nb–Ti–C–B multiphase alloy was
investigated. The alloy without Mo contains the primary Nb solid solution (Nbss) dendrites and interden-
dritic eutectic network containing Nbss, (Nb,Ti)C and (Nb,Ti)B. In Mo-added alloys, the solubility of Mo is
found in Nbss and (Nb,Ti)B. Mo addition reﬁnes the primary Nbss dendrites and promotes the coarsening
of boride phase, but does not obviously change the volume fraction of primary Nbss and total secondary
particles. The hardness, compression properties and fracture toughness were evaluated to determine the
strengthening and toughening effect of Mo-added alloys at room temperature. The results indicate that
the hardness and compressive yield strength increase with increasing Mo content, but Mo addition
decreases the room temperature fracture toughness.

1. Introduction
The efﬁciency improvement and thrust increment of next gen-
eration advanced turbine engines required to develop new high
temperature structural materials, which are operating at the high-
er temperature to replace the conventional materials such as Ni-
based superalloys [1,2]. Among these materials, refractory metals
have attracting considerable attention due to their superior prop-
erties at temperatures above the maximum operating temperature
limit of Ni-based alloys. Speciﬁcally, niobium has a high melting
point (2741 K), which is about 1000 K higher than that of Ni, and
a relatively low density (8.57 g/cm 3) comparing with Ni or any
other refractory metals such as Mo, W and Ta [3,4]. Its alloys are
more highly potential in the improvement of high-temperature
strength over Ni-based alloys [2,5]. Thus, Nb-based alloys have
been widely studied as a preferred candidate in high temperature
application [3,5–7].
The mechanical properties of Nb-based alloys have been exper-
imentally studied in the past several years. Solid solution strength-
ening with W, Mo and Hf was employed to effectively improve the
strength of Nb-based alloys [4,8,9]. Secondary phases such as
Nb5Si3,N b 3Si, Nb 3Al and Cr 2Nb [5,10,11], as well as with various
carbides [12,13], could also strengthen Nb-based alloy. Generally,
in order to obtain the better strengthening effect of Nb, the addi-
tion of solid solution strengthening element is also necessary when
introducing secondary strengthening phases. From the references
[9,14], Mo addition in Nb-based alloys strengthened by the sili-
cides can raise the melting point and inhibit the formation of inter-
mediate Nb 3Si phase to strongly stabilize Nb 5Si3, which also
indicated its positive role in increasing the elastic modulus, hard-
ness and compressive yield strength at room and high tempera-
tures. Alloying with Mo in Nb–Ti–C and Nb–Zr–C alloys also
signiﬁcantly improved the compressive yield strength of these al-
loys from room temperature to elevated temperatures [4,15].
Simultaneously, the high room temperature fracture toughness is
also required for the real application of Nb-based alloys, as well
as the high strength [11]. Kim et al. [16] have demonstrated that
5% Mo addition in the near-eutectic Nb–Si alloy increases the room
temperature fracture toughness from 4.5 to 9–15 MPa m 1/2 be-
cause of the coarsening of Nbss thickness in the Mo-added alloys.
In addition, the results by Ref. [17] show that the introduced car-
bide in Nb–5Mo–15W alloy increases the room temperature frac-
ture toughness from 7.8 to 10 MPa m 1/2.
Carbide and boride of niobium possess some excellent proper-
ties such as high melting point, high strength, high thermal and
electrical conductivity, and chemical stability [18]. Therefore, sin-
gle (Nb,Ti)C or (Nb,Ti)B phase is considered as the ideal candidate
phase to enhance room and high temperature mechanical proper-
ties of Nb-based alloys [4,13,19]. In the present study, the hybrid
phases of (Nb,Ti)B and (Nb,Ti)C were introduced into the Nb–Ti–
C–B alloy. Moreover, different Mo concentrations were also added
into Nb–Ti–C–B alloy. The effects of Mo on the microstructure,
phase stability, hardness, strength and fracture toughness of this
multiphase Nb-based alloy were also explored in details.

2. Experimental procedure
The alloys with nominal composition of Nb20Ti4C4B xMo ( x: 0, 5, 10 and
15 at.%), which are termed as 0Mo, 5Mo, 10Mo and 15Mo alloys, respectively, were
produced using the arc-melting technology under an argon atmosphere. The weight
of each ingot was 50 g. Raw materials used in this study were 99.99 wt.% Nb,
99.99 wt.% Ti, 99.9 wt.% Mo, 99.85 wt.% C and 99.9 wt.% B
4C. The ingots were re-
melted ﬁve times to ensure the chemical composition homogeneity.
The samples were cut from as-prepared ingots by electron discharge machining
for further phase identiﬁcation and mechanical testing. X-ray diffraction (XRD)
analysis was performed to identify the phase constitutions via Rigaku D/max-
2200 diffractometer with Cu-k
a radiation. Microstructural characterization was
identiﬁed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4300N) with energy
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) and transmission electron microscope (TEM,
Tecnai G2 F30). The volume fractions of primary phase and total secondary particles
of the alloys were measured by point counting method with assistance of an image
analyzer using the recorded SEM images. Vickers hardness was evaluated via Model
HV-1000 microhardness tester with a test load of 9.8 N and a load-dwell time of
10 s. Compression properties were assessed by Instron 5500R testing machine at
room temperature at an initial strain rate of 2.78 /C2 10
/C0 4 s/C0 1, and the size of com-
pression specimens was 3 /C2 3 mm cross-section and 6 mm height. The room tem-
perature fracture toughness ( KQ) was evaluated using a single-edge notched
beam test on Instron 5569 testing machine. The size of fracture toughness measure-
ment sample was 22 mm length, 4 mm width and 2 mm thickness. A notch was
introduced up to a/w = 0.5 (a, notch length; w, specimen width) by electron dis-
charge machining. The tests were executed with 16 mm span at an initial crosshead
speed of 0.1 mm min
/C0 1 in air. The fracture micrograph was observed by a scanning
electron microscope (SEM, FEI Quanta 200F).
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural characterization
The XRD patterns of the as-cast alloys are shown in Fig. 1 .I n
each alloy, three phases are identiﬁed, which are assigned to the
body-centered cubic W-type Nb solid solution (Nbss), the face-cen-
tered cubic NaCl-type (Nb,Ti)C carbide and the orthorhombic CrB-
type NbB boride. It indicates that Mo addition does not induce any
new phase and not cause any change in the crystal structure types
of constituent phases. Moreover, according to SEM–EDS results as
listed in Table 1, in the case of Nb-based alloy without Mo (Sample
0Mo), Ti presents in the Nbss, (Nb,Ti)C and NbB. When Mo is added
in the Nb-based alloys, Mo is also found in the Nbss and NbB,
whose percentage increases with increasing its nominal content
in the raw materials. However, Mo was not detected in (Nb,Ti)C
carbide here, which is consistent with previous reports about
Mo-added Nb–Ti–C [20] and Nb–Ti–Si–C [21] alloys. In this paper,
the NbB with Ti in solid solution is referred to as (Nb,Ti)B in 0Mo
alloy, and the NbB with Ti and Mo in solid solution is referred to
as (Nb,Ti,Mo)B in Mo-added alloys.
Fig. 2 shows the typical SEM backscattered electron (BSE)
micrographs of the alloys. The white, gray and black parts are in-
volved in the surface micrograph. In combination with XRD and
EDS results, the white one is assigned to be the primary Nbss den-
drites, the gray part is (Nb,Ti)B boride and black part is (Nb,Ti)C
carbide in the as-cast 0Mo alloy. The hybrid (Nb,Ti)B boride and
(Nb,Ti)C carbide are formed in the interdendritic eutectic network
around the primary Nbss ( Fig. 2a). In the Mo-added alloys ( Fig. 2b–
d), the gray (Nb,Ti,Mo)B boride and black (Nb,Ti)C carbide are also
existent in the interdendritic eutectic around the white primary
Nbss. When 5 and 10 at.% Mo are added in the alloys ( Fig. 2 b and
c), the eutectic network around Nbss dendrites becomes ﬁner. As
the nominal Mo content increases up to 15 at.% ( Fig. 2 d), the net-
work-shape eutectic almost disappears, while the intermittent
(Nb,Ti)C and (Nb,Ti,Mo)B hybrid phases are distributed in the
interdendritic region. Moreover, the Mo addition gives rise to the
reﬁned Nbss dendrites, however, the coarsened boride. Table 2
shows the volume fractions of primary Nbss and total secondary
particles in Nb-based alloys. Image analysis shows that Mo addi-
tion does not obviously change the volume fraction (11–12%) of to-
tal secondary particles in the matrix.
Fig. 3 a shows a bright ﬁeld TEM image of (Nb,Ti)C and
(Nb,Ti,Mo)B in the Nbss matrix of 10Mo alloy. It can be clearly seen
that the interface between Nbss/(Nb,Ti)C and Nbss/(Nb,Ti,Mo)B can
be easily recognized, indicative of no interfacial reaction or inter-
diffusion. (Nb,Ti)C and (Nb,Ti,Mo)B phases respectively demon-
strate the near-equiaxed and lamellar shapes. Fig. 3 b–d shows
the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns of local
micrographs. Diffraction pattern in Fig. 3b can be indexed in terms
of [0 0 1] zone axis of the body-centered cubic Nbss. The diffraction
pattern of (Nb,Ti)C was taken in the [ 112] zone of a face-centered
cubic structure ( Fig. 3c), and the diffraction pattern of (Nb,Ti,Mo)B
was recorded along the [5 3 1] zone of a orthorhombic structure
(Fig. 3 d). The lattice parameter of face-centered cubic (Nb,Ti)C is
a = 0.4351 nm, and (Nb,Ti,Mo)B has the lattice parameter of
a = 0.3269 nm, b = 0.8647 nm, c = 0.3136 nm.

The microstructure evolution can be attributed to the solidiﬁca-
tion during the fabrication process using arc-melting method. As
stated above, the solidiﬁed microstructure of 0Mo alloy consists
of the large Nbss dendrites and interdendritic eutectic containing
Nbss, (Nb,Ti)C and (Nb,Ti)B. Based on earlier report [22], there oc-
curs the eutectic reaction in the Nb–B–C system: L M (Nb) + Nb2-
C + NbB. Furthermore, the reported results provide the evidence
that Ti addition causes Nb 2C to change into (Nb,Ti)C in Nb–Ti–C
alloys, and Ti could be dissolved in NbB phase [13,23,24]. Thus,
the solidiﬁcation path in 0Mo alloy could be described that the
coarse Nbss dendrites preferred to solidify through L ? Nbss + L1,
followed by the eutectic reaction L 1 ? Nbss + (Nb,Ti)C + (Nb,Ti)B.
As a result, the eutectic mixture formed in the interdendritic re-
gion. Similarly, when the Mo was added, solidiﬁcation abode by
the path: L ? Nbss(Mo) + L1 ? Nbss(Mo) + (Nb,Ti)C + (Nb,Ti,Mo)B.
However, Mo addition reﬁned the primary Nbss dendrites and
interdendritic eutectic while coarsened the boride. As conﬁrmed
by SEM–EDS results, the solid-soluted Mo was found in Nbss and
boride phases. Therefore, the presence of Mo might affect the dif-
fusion of Nb, Ti, C and B in the liquid and changed the solidiﬁcation
rate. During the solidiﬁcation, Mo concentration in the alloy might
promote the nucleation of primary Nbss to reﬁne its size. Further,
Mo addition also might promote the precipitation of secondary
boride via modiﬁcation of the solubility of boron in Nb and/or of
the growth kinetics of precipitation of the boride, so as to increase
the boride size in Mo-added alloys. As B and C content is un-
changed in the Nb-based alloys, the interdendritic eutectic reﬁning
may be related to primary Nbss reﬁning.
3.2. Mechanical properties
In order to evaluate the hardening effect by adding Mo, the
Vickers hardness (HV) measurements were carried out on all sam-
ples. The values of Vickers hardness for these alloys are listed in Ta-
ble 3 . It is clear that all alloys with Mo have the higher HV values
than 0Mo alloy, and the hardness is gradually increased with Mo
increasing from 5 to 15 at.%. Here, the hardness of 0Mo alloy was
HV 304, and increased to the maximum hardness value (HV 462)
after 15 at.% Mo addition. The hardness is increased by 51.8%. It
can be concluded that more amount of Mo obviously hardens the
Nb–Ti–C–B alloy. Stephens and Witzke [25] investigated the hard-
ness behavior of binary Nb alloys after adding various elements
and pointed out that the atomic size misﬁt played a dominant role
in increasing hardness of binary Nb alloys. The hardness increase
may be attributed to large misﬁt (5.81%) between Mo and Nb
[26] in the present study.

Room temperature fracture toughness ( KQ) was tested using the
notched three-point-bending samples, as shown in Table 3 . It can
be found from Table 3 that the fracture toughness decreases with
Mo concentration increasing, and the KQ value decreased from
18.6 MPa m1/2 of 0Mo alloy to 9.6 MPa m 1/2 of 15Mo alloy, but still
higher than Mo-added Nb40Ti40C alloys (3.1–3.7 MPa m 1/2) [29].

the room temperature fracture toughness of Nb-based alloys. As
well known, in order to improve the room temperature fracture
toughness, one of the most useful approaches is to incorporate a
ductile-phase in brittle intermetallic compounds. The ductile
phase toughening has been studied in many secondary phase
strengthened Nb-based alloys [5,11,14]. Chan and Davidson [30]

the carbide and boride are the brittle phases, and Nbss is the ductile

in Nb matrix and forms the solid solution Nbss [14,20]. As a result,
Nbss is strengthened, but its ductility is lowered down with Mo
increasing. Mo addition could enhance the plastic constraint in
the ductile Nbss phase to decrease the toughness of Mo-added al-
loys from 5Mo to 15Mo alloy. Moreover, Chan pointed out that
the small ductile phase and coarse secondary particle can decrease
the toughness [30,31]. As seen from microstructure, the primary
ductile Nbss is reﬁned and boride particles is coarsened by Mo
adding, which may also be harmful to the fracture resistance of
Mo-added alloys. The detailed SEM observations on the fracture
surfaces of different Mo-added alloys after bending tests were
carried out in micro-scales, as shown in Fig. 5 . In each alloy, the
Nbss fractures with a transgranular cleavage mode, and some river
patterns and tiny tear ridges are also observed. It indicates that the
Nbss obviously underwent the plastic deformation, and ﬁnal failure
took place in a ductile manner. The carbide and boride phases show
the ﬂat fracture surfaces, whose fracture is a brittle cleavage model.
For these alloys containing the ductile Nbss and brittle carbide and
boride phases, the different mechanical properties among these
phases can lead to the large mismatch on interfaces, and the discon-
tinuous deformation easily occur on Nbss/carbide and Nbss/boride
interfaces. The interface separation was also frequently found on
the fracture surfaces. In summary, the fracture process of the alloys
consists of Nbss/carbide and Nbss/boride interfaces separating,
plastic deformation of Nbss grains, brittle cleavage of carbide and
boride phases.
4. Conclusions
(1) 0Mo alloy consists of Nbss, (Nb,Ti)C and (Nb,Ti)B phases, and
Mo-added alloys consists of Nbss, (Nb,Ti)C and (Nb,Ti,Mo)B
phases. The microstructure of 0Mo alloy is composed of
the primary Nbss dendrites and interdendritic eutectic net-
work containing Nbss, (Nb,Ti)C and (Nb,Ti)B. Mo addition
reﬁnes the primary Nbss dendrites and promotes the coars-
ening of boride phase, but does not obviously change the
volume fraction of primary Nbss and total secondary
particles.
(2) The hardness and compressive yield strength of the alloys
were improved signiﬁcantly with increasing Mo content
from 5 to 15 at.% at room temperature. The improvement
