ABSTRACT 
The method of adding the initial elements greatly affects the structure and mechanical properties of a tungsten 
alloy. In this study, the 90(W
–
20Ta) – Ni – Fe alloy was prepared by the direct addition of elements and W
–
Ta 
pre-alloying. The element diffusion and phase transformation behavior under different states are discussed. The 
matrix phase to segregate into lumpy Ni
3
Ta and Fe
2
Ta phases after sintering by directly adding tantalum, which 
deteriorated the structure of the alloy. The pre-alloyed group first formed body-centered cubic W
–
Ta solid 
solution with fine particles through mechanical milling. During the sintering process, the tantalum element 
gradually precipitated to form the acicular Ni

Through W

fracture, which increased the strength of the alloy by 130%. 
1. Introduction 
Tungsten is widely used in industrial production, defense technol -
ogy, and other fields due to its high melting point (3695.15 K), high 
density (19.3 g/cm
3
), high hardness, and good chemical stability [ 1 – 3 ]. 
However, pure tungsten has intrinsic brittleness due to its low disloca -
tion mobility at room temperature, high sliding resistance, and poor 
inter-crystalline bonding force [ 3 – 5 ]. Although the intergranular brit -
tleness of tungsten can be improved by combining tungsten with soft 
nickel – iron phase, the intrinsic brittleness of tungsten limits the 
improvement of the mechanical properties of tungsten alloys [ 6 , 7 ]. 
Tantalum is a transition element with high density, melting point, and 
plasticity [ 8 ]. The lattice parameters of tantalum are similar to those of 
tungsten [ 9 , 10 ]. It can be dissolved in tungsten. Meanwhile, the calcu -
lation results show that there is an ordered phase in the W
–
Ta solid 
solution, which can improve the ductility of tungsten [ 11 – 13 ]. There -
fore, tantalum is regarded as a potential element that can improve the 
intrinsic brittleness of tungsten. 
Years of research have shown that the method of adding alloying 
elements has a great impact on the structure and properties of tungsten 
alloys [ 14 , 15 ]. For example, Kiran et al. found that the addition of Re 
through the pre-alloying method can effectively improve the uneven 
structure caused by direct addition [ 16 ]. Early research by Bose et al. 
reported that the direct addition of tantalum would form an 
intermediate phase in the tungsten alloy [ 17 ]. However, the sintering 
diffusion process and phase formation have not been studied in detail. In 
our previous study, we also found that the mixing enthalpy of tantalum 
and the matrix is lower than that of tungsten, and it is more inclined to 
combine with the matrix [ 18 ]. Therefore, the initial method of adding 
tantalum will have a greater impact on subsequent sintering and diffu -
sion. At present, there are few study reports on the influence of tantalum 
addition on the structure and properties of tungsten alloys, especially on 
the addition of high tantalum content ( > 10 wt%) [ 19 ]. 
Based on these reports, the current study first prepared W
–
Ta solid 
solution particles by pre-alloying. The innovation was using W
–
Ta solid 
solution particles as the core and low melting point nickel – iron sintering 
to prepare the 90(W
–
20Ta) – 7Ni – 3Fe alloy. Simultaneously, the 72 
W – 18Ta – 7Ni – 3Fe alloy was prepared by direct mixing and sintering of 
W, Ta, Ni, and Fe elements. The effect of the initial state of the two 
tantalum additions on the microstructure and properties of the alloy was 
studied. The effect of pre-alloying on alloy phase transformation and 
element diffusion was explored. In addition, the relationship between 
the alloy structure and the failure mechanism was analyzed to provide 
experimental guidance for the preparation of the new high-strength 
tungsten alloy.

2. Materials and methods 
The physical properties and manufacturers of powder raw materials 
(tungsten, tantalum, nickel, and iron) used in this study are shown in 
Table 1 . The experimental samples are divided into two groups, A 
(mixing) and B (milling), which were prepared differently. 
In Group A, four kinds of powders were directly prepared according 
to the ratio of W:Ta:Ni:Fe = 72:18:7:3. In Group B, W – 20wt.%Ta solid 
solution powder was prepared by pre-alloying. The pre-alloying process 
adopted the omnidirectional planetary ball mill produced by Changsha 
Tianchuang Powder Technology Co., Ltd. The equipment can make 
planetary rotational and overturning movements in a 360
◦
space. Its 
ability to move in multiple dimensions and directions means the degree 
of disordered movement of the grinding balls and materials is improved. 
Cemented carbide ball-milling beads and cemented carbide ball-milling 
tank were used for ball milling for 60 h. The ball-milling speed was 300 
rpm and the ball material ratio was 10:1. Anhydrous ethanol was used as 
a ball milling medium. After powder loading, we vacuumed the ball- 
milling tank, filled it with high-purity argon, and repeated this three 
times. The ball-milled powder was dried in a high vacuum drying oven 
to obtain W – 20 wt% Ta solid solution powder. The processes of powder 
loading, powder taking, and drying were conducted in an argon gas 
glove box to prevent powder oxidation. Next, with W
–
Ta solid solution 
powder as the raw material, Ni and Fe powder were added according to 
the ratio W – Ta:Ni:Fe = 90:7:3 (research shows that controlling Ni:Fe =
7:3 can avoid the formation of intermetallic compounds to improve the 
microstructure and properties) [ 20 ]. 
Subsequently, Group A and B powders were mixed for 6 h by a three- 
dimensional mixer filled with argon and vacuumized. In a rubber 
sheath, a round cake-shaped compact (about 100 g) underwent cold 
isostatic pressing at 250 MPa. A high-vacuum sintering furnace 
(VVP – 60, Shanghai Hao Yue Furnace Technology Co., Ltd.) was used at 
1450 
◦
C for 90 min. Two types of alloy blocks, A and B, were obtained. 
The powder particles were analyzed by a laser particle size analyzer 
(Malvin Mastersizer 3000). The powder and alloy phases were analyzed 
by an X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) diffractometer (Bruker Advance 
D8). The microstructure of the powder and alloy was analyzed with a 
scanning electron microscope (SEM, FEI Quanta 250 FEG). The distri -
bution of alloying elements was characterized by an electron probe 
microanalyzer (JXB-8230). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) 
samples were prepared by processing the alloy with a double-beam 
microscope (Helios ™ G4 PFIA HXe DualBeam ™ ). The microstructure 
of the powder and alloy was characterized with a transmission electron 
microscope (FEI Titan G2 60 – 300). The tensile strength of the alloy was 
tested using a double-column desktop material testing machine (Ins -
tron ® 3369). The Vickers hardness of the alloy was tested by a micro 
indentation hardness tester (Buehler ® MicroMet 5104). 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Powder characterization 
The morphology and particle size of the original W and Ta powders 
and the ball-milled W
–
20Ta powder are shown in Fig. 1 . The particle 
sizes of raw powder W and Ta differ greatly. The average particle size of 
W is 6.42 μ m, and it shows a polyhedron-shaped structure. The average 
particle size of Ta particles is 29.9 μ m, and it is irregularly massive. The 
powder is refined into a lamellar structure by omni-directional planetary 
ball milling for 60 h. The particle size was homogeneous with an average 
particle size of 4.33 μ m. The homogenization of particle size shows that 
W and Ta powders have been uniformly mixed by ball milling. 
The XRD comparison between directly mixed powder and ball-milled 
powder is shown in Fig. 2 . The diffraction peak of Ta powder essentially 
disappears after ball milling, and there are only four main peaks of W. 
However, a new peak with relatively low intensity was formed on the 
left of the original Ta peak. The peak was determined to be a tantalum 
oxide peak using a powder diffraction file card. This showed that, after 
60 h ball milling, Ta had essentially been absorbed as a solid solution in 
the W lattice, and the disappearance of the Ta peak also proves that it 
had become a fully solid solution. The main peak shift is not evident 
because of the error caused by the broadening of the main peak pro -
duced by the grain refinement. Therefore, the microstructure and lattice 
changes of the powder were further analyzed by TEM. 
Fig. 3 demonstrates the TEM microstructure of ball-milled W
–
20Ta 
powder. From Fig. 3 (b), it can be seen that the large lamellar powder in 
Fig. 1 (c) is an aggregate of several fine particles. These fine oval lamellar 
particles have a particle size of approximately 200 nm. This is formed by 
repeated crushing and calendering under the huge mechanical energy of 
ball milling. The high surface energy of nanoparticles and the charge 
between the particles lead to firm agglomeration. This shows that a 
single particle is not composed of a single grain but several fine grains. 
From a microscopic point of view, the severe plastic deformation in 
the process of high-energy ball milling leads to an increasing dislocation 
density in metal crystals. When the dislocation density reaches a certain 
level, the dislocations will be multilateralized to form subcrystals. 
Subsequently, the subgrain boundary evolved into a large angle grain 
boundary, resulting in grain refinement [ 21 – 23 ]. At the same time, 
diffusion solid solution was also realized between elements in this pro -
cess. Simultaneously, from the energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS) surface scan analysis results, there was no enrichment of W and Ta 
on the large agglomerated particles. This shows that W and Ta powders 
have achieved alloying after 60 h of omni-directional ball milling. 
3.2. Alloy microstructure 
3.2.1. Densification

eutectic with Ni and reduce the sintering temperature of the alloy [ 18 ]. 
Therefore, sintering at 1450 
◦

liquid phase formation. The densification degree of the compact and 
sintered block is shown in Fig. 4 . It can be seen that although the pre- 
alloyed powder has poor formability, the degree of sintering and 
densification is significantly better than that of the mixed powder. The 
pre-alloyed powder reached a density of 98.5% after sintering. The in -
crease in the degree of densification is related to the original powder 
particle size and the sintering diffusion reaction. The microstructure is 
further analyzed below.

tungsten alloy directly added with tantalum is large and uneven. In 
Group B, the grain size of the tungsten alloy added by pre-alloying is 
small, and the microstructure distribution of each phase is relatively 
uniform. The phase analysis of XRD in Fig. 6 shows that the central peak 
of W in Groups A and B alloys retains high strength. However, there is

The two alloys have obvious differences in microstructure and phase

3.3. Mechanical properties and failure behavior 
The tensile strength and hardness of the two alloys are shown in 
Fig. 11 . The addition of Ta through pre-alloying greatly improves the 
performance of the directly added alloy. The improvement in mechan -
ical properties is related to the improvement in alloy microstructure. 
From the fracture structure of the alloy ( Fig. 12 .a), the failure modes of 
the Group A alloy are mainly the interface fracture between the tungsten 
particles and the brittle fracture of the matrix phase. Transgranular 
fractures of tungsten particles with higher fracture strength occur less 
frequently. Many cracks at the interface between the matrix phase and 
the tungsten particles were found from the fracture ( Fig. 12 .c). This is 
due to the transformation of the matrix phase composition, which 
considerably weakens the interfacial bonding force between it and the 
tungsten particles. It is also found that the fracture of the matrix phase is 
a brittle cleavage fracture and not a ductile-tearing one. This is because 
the Ni
3
Ta and Fe
2
Ta phases were both harder and more brittle than the γ 
 (Ni,Fe) phase. The deterioration of the performance of the matrix 
phase and the weakening of the interface bond led to the poor overall 
performance of the alloy. 
The failure mode of the Group B alloy is dominated by the 
transcrystalline fracture of the tungsten particles and the ductile tear of 
the binder phase, accompanied by a small amount of oxide particles and 
the matrix phase interface fracture. It can be seen from the fracture 
structure that several tungsten particles have transcrystalline fracture 
cleavage lines ( Fig. 12 .b). The dispersive distribution of acicular pre -
cipitates can also be found at the tear fracture of the matrix phase 
( Fig. 12 .d). These nano-precipitated phases hinder the propagation of 
cracks, cause the fracture to appear up and down, and enhance the 
strength of the matrix phase. Simultaneously, the interface between the 
matrix phase and the tungsten particles is firmly bonded, so that the 
failure occurs in the stronger tungsten particles and the alloy properties 
are improved. Therefore, adding Ta through pre-alloying significantly 
improves the alloy strength. In addition to interface bonding, the in -
crease in the overall hardness of the alloy is also affected by the changes 
in the composition of each phase. Therefore, nanoindentation experi -
ment analysis was conducted on each phase of the alloy. 
The results of nanoindentation experiments shown in Fig. 13 and 
Table 4 indicate that the matrix phase of the directly mixed Group A has 
a substantial increase in hardness due to the transformation of the phase 
composition. In particular, the hardness of the Ni
3

18.8 GPa. The modulus and indentation depth are significantly reduced.

hardness modulus [ 29 ], fracture toughness is proportional to elastic 
modulus and inversely proportional to hardness. Therefore, the brittle -

increased, resulting in brittle fracture with lower strength. The matrix 
phase of the pre-alloyed Group B has a certain increase in hardness 
compared with the traditional γ (Ni,Fe) phase because of the dispersed

4. Conclusions 
In this study, a 90(W
–
20Ta) – Ni – Fe alloy was prepared by the 
following two methods: pre-alloying and direct powder mixing. The 
effect of different initial methods of adding tantalum on the alloy phase 
formation and element diffusion was studied. The relationship between 
the alloy structure and failure mechanism was analyzed. The conclu -
sions are as follows: 
1. Through omni-directional high-energy ball milling at 60 h and 300 
rpm, a uniform W
–
20Ta alloy powder with fine particles is formed. 
2. The directly added tantalum destroys the matrix phase of the tung -
sten alloy, causing the elements to segregate to form Fe
2
Ta and Ni
3
Ta 
brittle phases. Simultaneously, the tantalum-rich liquid phase limits 
the dissolution – precipitation process of tungsten particles and de -
teriorates the uniformity of the structure. 
3. The Ta added by pre-alloying gradually precipitated from the tung -
sten particles during the sintering process, forming a nanoscale 
acicular Ni
3
Ta phase. This improves the uniformity of the micro -
structure and increases the density of the alloy. 
4. The failure mode of the direct addition alloy is mainly the fracture of 
the weaker tungsten particle and the matrix phase interface. The 
acicular precipitation phase formed by the pre-alloying alloy 
strengthens the binder phase. The failure mode is transformed into 
the ductile tearing of the trans-crystalline cleavage of the tungsten 
particles and the matrix phase, which increases the tensile strength 
of the alloy by 130%.
