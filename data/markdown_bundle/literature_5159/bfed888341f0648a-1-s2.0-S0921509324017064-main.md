1. Introduction
High entropy alloys (HEAs), also known as multi-principal element 
alloys (MPEAs), are a type of multi-component compound whose higher 
configurational entropy inhibits the formation of brittle intermetallic 
compounds, making them more prone to condense into simple solid 
solutions [ 1 – 3 ]. In addition, high-entropy alloys have high thermal 
stability and high temperature oxidation resistance, and can achieve 
extremely high temperature strength, as well as corrosion resistance and 
other characteristics [ 4 – 6 ]. As a new type of alloy that has developed in 
the past decade, its almost infinite design space provides possibilities for 
the demand for new materials in various industries.
The concept of refractory high-entropy alloys (also named as re -
fractory multi-principal element alloys) was first proposed by Senkov of 
the US Air Force Laboratory in 2010 [ 7 , 8 ]. The two earliest reported 
NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW refractory high-entropy alloys with a single 
BCC structure have extremely excellent high temperature softening 
resistance, and can even have a yield strength of over 400 MPa at a test 
temperature of 1600 
◦
C. Their potential service space far exceeds that of 
traditional Ni-based superalloys ( < 1050 
◦
C) [ 9 – 11 ], demonstrating a 
considerable potential application. However, the density of more than 
12 g/cm
3 
and the brittleness at ambient temperature seriously limit the 
application of this RMPEA in the fields of new lightweight structural 
materials. To break through the aforementioned bottleneck, Senkov 
et al. [ 12 , 13 ] designed a novel refractory multi-principal element 
HfNbTaTiZr alloy in subsequent studies, which has a compressive yield 
strength of up to 929 MPa at room temperature. During the plastic 
deformation stage, even if the compressive strain exceeds 50 %, the 
strength continues to increase at an almost constant rate without frac -
ture. Although the density has decreased to about 10 g/cm
3
, there is still 
a long way to go before reaching the standard for lightweight alloys. 
Therefore, based on the “ cocktail effect ” , later researchers believed that 
choosing lighter elements (such as Al, Ti, etc.) for alloying was the 
primary choice to reduce the overall density of multi-principal element

as large atomic size differences between constituent elements and mix -

precipitation of a large number of structurally complex intermetallic 
compounds or intermediate phases inside the alloy, deteriorating its

large amount of Laves intermetallic compounds in alloys was not 
conducive to the balance between strength and plasticity. On the con -
trary, the presence of an appropriate amount of Laves phase can actually 
have a balancing effect on strength and plasticity. Thus, from the 
perspective of the selection, design, and development of structural ma -
terials, the selection of elements, the phase composition of alloys, and 
the trade-off of strength and plasticity are the issues that researchers 
need to face.
The hard and brittle Al-Zr intermetallic compound phase is an 
important means to enhance the compressive properties of Al-Nb-Ti-V- 
Zr RMPEAs, but it may also be one of the reasons why few researchers 
have studied the tensile properties. From another perspective, can the 
precipitation of Al-Zr intermetallic compounds in Al-Nb-Ti-V-Zr alloy be 
suppressed during the design process to optimize the microstructure and 
meet the needs of multi angle research? In previous studies, we found 
that an increase in Ti content increased the volume fraction of the BCC 
matrix phase and inhibited the precipitation of the second phase [ 18 ]. 
Therefore, Al-Nb-Ti-V-Zr alloys with high Ti content form a micro -
structure with a single-phase BCC structure, which have excellent 
strength and plasticity combination [ 17 ]. Furthermore, the V element 
has a unique enhancement effect in both FCC and BCC families [ 26 ]. In 
BCC-based MPEAs, the addition of V not only increases strength but also 
improves ductility compared to V-free alloys [ 26 ]. Therefore, alloy 
optimization can focus on the impact of changes in the concentration 
level of V. Based on the above reasons, a series of low-density

this phenomenon is due to the fact that the secondary diffraction spots

transmission spots will interfere on the object mirror plane, and then 
form interference fringes. In other words, The Moir ´e fringes are the 
interference fringe between the transmission beam and the nearby sec -
ondary diffraction beam. This phenomenon is relatively common in the 
electron microscope observation of metals [ 28 , 29 ]. The increase of grain 
boundary width will inevitably have a certain impact on the properties 
of the MPEAs. The structure at the phase interface was revealed by using

When dislocations encounter grain boundaries, they hinder the 
movement of dislocations [ 34 ]. In this way, dislocations will accumulate 
near grain boundaries, causing strain hardening of the material and 
resulting in better plasticity. At the coherent phase interface, disloca -
tions can achieve dislocation crossing and sliding by relaxing the elastic 
strain energy caused by the coherent phase at the interface [ 40 ]. In 
addition, the coherent interface is also conducive to the long-range slip 
of dislocations, thereby improving the strength and plasticity of the 
material [ 40 ]. Dislocations can pass through non-coherent interfaces, 
but due to poor lattice matching and weak interface bonding strength, 
the interaction at the interface is very weak. Therefore, the case of dis -
locations passing through non coherent interfaces is relatively rare [ 41 ]. 
Lattice distortion and stress concentration hinder the movement of 
dislocations, reducing the material ’ s plastic deformability. In addition, 
the high-energy state at non coherent interfaces can also lead to inter -
face instability, which can easily cause interface roughness and phase 
separation, further reducing the performance of the alloy. Based on the 
above analysis, we present the tensile deformation mechanisms of
