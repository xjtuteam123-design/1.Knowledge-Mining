ABSTRACT
High-entropy alloys have long faced a key challenge in their industrial applications: balancing relatively low 
strength with high plasticity. To address this, our study fabricated Fe55Cr25Co10Ni10-TiC composites by laser 
melting deposition, incorporating a non-equiatomic Fe55Cr25Co10Ni10 high-entropy alloy with a high pro -
portion (7 wt%) of TiC. Compared to Fe55Cr25Co10Ni10 high-entropy alloy, the composites exhibit a phase 
structure comprising a face-centered cubic solid solution and a TiC phase. Detailed characterization results 
indicate that the composites exhibit smaller average grain size and a more dispersed grain growth direction. The 
room temperature yield strength and tensile strength increased from 203 MPa to 279 MPa (improved 37.4 %) 
and 542 MPa to 673 MPa (improved 24.2 %), respectively, while maintaining a fairly high plasticity. These 
enhancements result from the synergistic contributions of effects of Hall-Petch strengthening, dislocation 
strengthening, dispersion strengthening, and load transfer effect strengthening. The improved wear resistance of 
high-entropy alloy composites is mainly due to the hard TiC particles, which prevent the matrix from wearing out 
due to prolonged load-bearing. The findings provide theoretical guidance for the fabrication of high-strength, 
high-ductility high-entropy alloy composites by laser melting deposition manufacturing technology.
1. Introduction
Before the 20th century, scientists designing new alloys generally 
followed one principle: first, using 1 – 2 metal elements as the primary 
components; second, minor additions of other metallic elements to tailor 
the alloy composition [1,2] . Traditional alloy systems have increasingly 
obvious limitations. This is especially true in aerospace and other fields, 
where higher performance requirements are needed. In order to meet 
higher performance requirements, scientists are continually striving to 
explore [3] . The definition of multi-component alloys was first intro -
duced by Prof Ye in the 1990s, and high-entropy alloys gradually 
emerged during the process [4,5] . In recent years, high entropy alloys 
have attracted more and more scholars ’ attention and research interests 
due to their exceptional mechanical properties, wear resistance and 
thermal stability under extreme environments [6] . However, low yield 
strength limits their widespread application in industry [7] . Therefore, 
in order to improve their strength, some scholars have added some metal 
elements with large atomic radii (such as Co, Cr and Ti, etc.) into 
high-entropy alloys [8,9] . These elements are not only capable of 
inducing lattice distortion to some extent but also promoting the for -
mation of new phases [10,11] . However, the high costs of Co and Ti, 
along with the scarcity of Co in the Earth ’ s crust greatly limit their in -
dustrial applications [12] . In addition, ceramic powders are relatively 
inexpensive. Another strengthening approach involves the addition of 
ceramic particles (TiC, TiB
2
, NbC, Al

et al.) into high-entropy alloys, 
which act as additional nucleation points for the grains, refining the 
grains and improving the mechanical properties of alloys [13,14] .
There are several traditional methods for manufacturing high en -
tropy alloy composites, including mechanical alloying, powder metal -
lurgy and casting [15] . However, traditional manufacturing methods for 
high-entropy alloys have significant limitations: Mechanical alloying 
has a long production cycle and risks of impurity mixing [16] ; Powder 
metallurgy easily allows air ingress, reducing workpiece fatigue life 
[17] ; Casting typically requires large heat input, and its slow cooling 
rates tend to generate coarse grains [18] . At the same time, severe 
elemental segregation is prone to the formation of brittle phases, which 
in turn affects the mechanical properties [19] .

manufacturing technology is considered to be one of the most promising 
technologies of the 21st century. Laser additive manufacturing tech -
nologies are typically classified into two types: laser melting deposition 
(LMD) and selective laser melting (SLM). Both technologies rely on the 
high energy density of the laser beam to achieve rapid melting and so -

more efficient method to fabricate high-entropy alloy composites with 
ceramic particles [21] .
Currently, research on high-entropy alloy composites has made 
notable advancements. Gao et al. [22] found that adding 30 % TiC 
effectively improved the hardness and wear resistance of the matrix. 
Zhang et al. [23] used laser melting deposition to fabricate 
TiC/WC-reinforced high-entropy alloy composites, showing that both 
ceramic particles significantly improved the mechanical properties. 
Amar et al. [24] prepared different proportions of TiC/high-entropy 
alloy composites, showing that the mechanical properties improved as 
the TiC proportion increased. Yan et al. [25] found that the addition of 
4 wt%. TiC could successfully inhibit crack formation in FeCrAlY 
high-entropy alloy and could improve its mechanical properties. Wang 
et al. [26] successfully fabricated 5 wt% TiB2-reinforced CoCrFeMnNi 
high-entropy alloy composites via DED technique. Their results showed 
that adding TiB2 could refine the microstructure. Compared with the 
high-entropy alloy matrix, the tensile strength increased by 42 % and the 
friction coefficient decreased by 22.4 % [27] .

attention due to its light weight (4.93 g/cm

[28] . Currently, it serves as a reinforcing phase in the field of materials 
science.

[28,29] . To further enhance the yield strength of the material, 
Fe55Cr25Co10Ni10-TiC composites with a high percentage (7 wt%) of 
ceramic particles addition is fabricated by LMD and conducts an 
in-depth study on the effects of a high percentage (7 wt%) of TiC on the 
microstructure, properties and strengthening mechanisms of the matrix.

growth of columnar crystals along the building direction [29] . Laser 
melting deposition is a layer-by-layer build-up process, and the molten 
pool moves with the heat source. When the lower-layer molten pool 
solidifies, heat is conducted through the solidified lower layer. At the 
same time, the upper layer continues to be heated. This creates an 
obvious temperature gradient along the building direction. According to 
the research by Osman et al., the grains tend to grow along the direction 
of the maximum temperature gradient, forming a columnar crystal 
structure [30] . The image quality map ( Fig. 6 c) indicates that in T0, 
24.6 % of the grain boundaries are high-angle, while 75.4 % are 
low-angle, with an average grain size of 138 μ m. The Kernel average 
misorientation ( Fig. 6 d) is used to characterize the dislocation density 
and local strain in the crystal. It is found that T0 has a higher dislocation 
density, likely due to the rapid cooling inherent in the laser melting 
deposition process, which may lead to residual stresses in the alloy [31] . 
The inverse pole figure in Fig. 6 e shows that the grains in T0 have an 
obvious preferred orientation along the (001) direction. This observa -
tion aligns with the microstructure analysis presented in previous 
studies. According to the research by Wang et al., in the pole figure, the 
larger the area occupied by blue, the more obvious the preferred 
orientation; conversely, the worse it is. The highest percentage of blue 
areas in the (001) pole figure of Fig. 6 f further confirms that the grains in 
T0 grow along the (001) direction [28] .
Fig. 7 shows the microstructure along the building direction of T7. It 
can be found that T7 fabricated by LMD consist of columnar dendrites 
(red dashed line), cellular structure (yellow dashed line) and TiC 
particles (green arrow), as observed via OM and SEM [32] . Previous 
studies [28,31] have shown that the interfacial energy between TiC 
ceramic particles and the matrix is much lower than the grain boundary 
energy within the high-entropy alloy, which provides favorable condi -
tions for the nucleation of grains. In contrast, TiC particles have 
extremely high thermal conductivity properties, which increase the 
temperature gradient around them in the molten pool and inhibits 
dendritic grain growth, resulting in the formation of cellular structures 
[30] . The size and shape of the grains are primarily influenced by two 
factors: the temperature gradient (G) and the growth rate (R) [33] . 
Typically, a larger G combined with a lower R facilitates the develop -
ment of columnar crystals, while a smaller G and a faster R favor the 
development of equiaxed crystals. The addition of TiC increases the laser 
absorption of the high-entropy alloy composites, which decreases the 
melt pool ’ s G; it also provides numerous nucleation sites for grains and 
inhibits their continuous growth [34] . The TiC particles exert a pinning 
effect on the grain boundaries, hindering their movement and thus 
contributing to the refinement of the grains [35] . As the ratio of G/R 
decreases, the microstructure of high-entropy alloy composites gradu -
ally transitions from columnar crystals to columnar dendrites and 
cellular structures, which further enhances the properties of the 
material.

3.3. Friction and wear properties
To further gain an understanding of the effect of TiC ceramic parti -
cles on high entropy alloys, we compare the friction and wear properties 
of T0 and T7 fabricated by LMD at room temperature. Fig. 9 shows the 
macroscopic morphology, three-dimensional morphology, coefficient of 
friction curve and depth profile of T0 and T7 after room temperature 
friction and wear tests. Fig. 9 (a – b), show no obvious cracks in either the 
T0 or T7 after room temperature friction wear tests. The 3D morphology 
of the friction wear track is captured by depth-of-field microscope as 
shown in Fig. 9 (c – d). It is well known that the change of different colors 
in the 3D morphology represents the change of wear depth. The 3D 
morphology reveals that T0 experienced more severe wear during 
sliding after the reciprocal friction and wear test, which is further 
confirmed by the depth profile plot in Fig. 9 (f). As shown in the friction 
coefficient curves in Fig. 9 (e) that the friction coefficients of both T0 and 
T7 experienced a sharp increase and then a decrease. This behavior is 
attributed to the unstable contact between the friction pair and the 
samples at the start of the friction and wear test resulting in a sharp 
increase in the coefficient of friction. When the friction wear test has 
been carried out for a period of time, the friction wear will gradually 
stabilize [40] . After stabilization, the friction coefficient of T7 is notably 
lower than that of the T0 sample. This further suggests that the incor -
poration of TiC ceramic particles significantly enhances the wear resis -
tance of the matrix. According to the research by Zhang et al., ceramic 
particles themselves have high hardness and good wear resistance, 
adding it to the matrix further enhances the hardness of the matrix. Hard 
ceramic particles can withstand most of the external loads, preventing 
wear of the matrix due to prolonged loading. During the deformation 
process of the material, the movement of dislocations is the main cause 
of plastic deformation [40] . As shown in previous studies, ceramic 
particles are diffusely distributed in the matrix, which hinders disloca -
tion motion, thereby enhancing the hardness and wear resistance of the 
matrix [41] .

two samples. As shown in Fig. 10 (a) and Fig. 10 (c), the wear surface of 
T0 exhibits numerous furrows, spalling, debris and attached abrasive 
particles, which is typical of adhesive wear and abrasive wear [41] . The 
low hardness of T0 causes local spalling pits when the abrasive head

4. Conclusion
In this study, we successfully fabricated 7 wt% TiC-reinforced 
Fe55Cr25Co10Ni10 high-entropy alloy composites (HEACS) with 
excellent mechanical properties and wear resistance by LMD. The main 
conclusions are summarized as follows:
1. Defect-free HEACS reinforced with 7 wt% TiC ceramic particles was 
fabricated by LMD.
2. The 7 wt% TiC-reinforced Fe55Cr25Co10Ni10 HEACS consists of a 
face-centered cubic phase and TiC particles, which are uniformly 
distributed in the matrix. The incorporation of TiC alters the 
preferred grain growth orientation and promotes grain refinement.
3. The addition of 7 wt% TiC particles enhanced the yield strength and 
tensile strength of the matrix from 203 MPa and 542 MPa to 
279 MPa and 672 MPa respectively. This enhancement can be 
attributed to the combined effects of Hall-Petch strengthening, 
dislocation strengthening, dispersion strengthening, and load- 
transfer effect strengthening.
4. The HEACS exhibited superior wear resistance, primarily due to the 
high hardness of the TiC ceramic particles, which can withstand most 
external loads and prevent matrix wear under prolonged loading.
5. The high-entropy alloy composites with the addition of 7 wt% TiC 
exhibits good strength. It is expected to be applied in the field of 
aero-engine combustion chambers.
