1. Introduction
With the development of aerospace industry, the performance
requirement of the high-temperature materials has drawn much
attention. Compared with conventional Ni-based or Co-based
superalloys, Nb-based superalloys and their composites are consid-
ered to be novel high-temperature structural materials [1–3]. As the
nature of refractory metal, pure Nb has relatively low density and
high melting temperature (2468 /C176C), as well as excellent ductility
at room temperature [4]. However, the oxidation resistance seri-
ously aggravates with increasing the working temperature [5].I n
addition, continuous disastrous oxidation caused by oxygen embrit-
tlement takes place even at 600 /C176C for pure Nb [6]. This disastrous
oxidation behavior is referred as the ‘‘pesting’’ oxidation and
Nb2O5 phase forms as the main oxide production during oxidation
[7]. The high Pilling–Bedworth Ratio (PBR) between Nb 2O5 and Nb
(2.74) results in the internal stress between the interface of the
Nb matrix and the oxide layer, which in turn causes the formation
of the uncompacted oxide layers and the continuous exfoliation of
the outer oxide layers [8]. This poor oxidation resistance behavior
at high temperature has been the main bottleneck for further devel-
opment and application of the pure Nb. To resolve this problem, it is
documented that the oxidation resistance can be improved by solid
solution of Ti, Si, Al, Cr or Hf [9,10]. By doping these elements into
pure Nb, an alternative oxidation kinetic process occurs and the
oxide product with smaller PBR value forms [11,12], which will
make contribution to the protection for the matrix.
On the other hand, Nb-based alloys enhanced by secondary com-
pound phases (such as (Nb and ti)C [13,14], (Nb, Zr)C [15,16],N b
3Al
[17,18] and Nb5Si3 [19]) are much developed recently. By introduc-
ing carbides (such as NbC, TiC) into the Nb matrix, the grain growth
of the alloy is restricted and much smaller grains can be obtained
[20]. It should be also noted that the oxidation resistance is possibly
improved through this grain size controlling. However, there are
very few experimental studies devoted to the oxidation behavior
of Nb-based multi-phase system, which consists of the refractory
solid solution phase and the carbide phase.
A detailed understanding of the oxidation mechanism of Nb–Ti–
C alloys needs an undoubted urgent, which will provide a basis for
the further development and application of this and related alloy
systems. The purpose of this paper is to comprehensively investi-
gate the oxidation behaviors of Nb-based multi-phase system
and this will help to better design novel Nb-based superalloys with
promising oxidation resistance at high working temperatures.
2. Experimental details
2.1. Selection of the alloys
In developing new superalloys, several major aspects such as
low temperature fracture toughness, high temperature strength,
creep resistance and oxidation resistance should be considered.
The Nb–Ti alloy was selected as the matrix, as it provides a basis
for developing microstructure of the Niobium solid solution (Nbss)
[20]. In the latter materials, tensile ductility and fracture resistance
at ambient temperature are provided by the Nbss. As reported in
Refs. [18,19], the Ti additions can increase the oxidation resistance.

2.2. Experimental procedure
Pure Nb with high purity, Ti sponge and TiC powder were used as
raw materials. The starting materials were cleaned by acidwashing
and water scrubbing. The alloys were prepared using nonconsum-
able arc-melting furnace under a positive pressure of high-purity
Ar and casted into buttons. The buttons were melted ﬁve times to
ensure compositional homogeneity. The buttons were cut into sam-
ples with dimension of 4 mm /C2 4m m /C2 4 mm using wire electrical
discharge machines (WEDMs) for high temperature oxidation tests.
The surfaces of each sample were polished using #600 silicon
carbide abrasive papers, then were cleaned using ultrasonic for
ten minutes.
The test method of the high temperature oxidation experiment
adopts the way of circular oxidation [12,21]. The circular oxidation
meant that one test sample was weighted after each deﬁnite oxida-
tion time, and then put into the electrical resistance furnace to con-
tinue the oxidation experiment [19,21]. The weight gain was
measured using electronic analytical balance, with measurement
accuracy of 0.1 mg. The weight of the sample was carefully mea-
sured before and after each period of oxidation, and the measured
weight includes the mass of exfoliated oxide layer. The phases of
the samples were analyzed using X-ray diffraction analyzer (XRD,
Rigaku D/max-RB). The microstructure was characterized using
scanning electron microscope (SEM, S-4700 equipped with energy
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS)).
3. Results and discussions
3.1. Microstructural analysis
Fig. 1 shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns obtained from
the Nb–21Ti–4C, Nb–35Ti–4C, Nb–9Ti–8C and Nb–25Ti–8C alloys.
For these samples, some sharp crystalline diffraction peaks can be
seen and these peaks can be indexed to be a body-centered-cubic
(bcc) structured (Nbss) phase (PDF: 35-0789) and a face-cen-
tered-cubic ( fcc) structured (Nb, Ti)C phase (PDF: 47-1418). Espe-
cially, other sharp peaks belonged to a hexagonal structured
Nb2C phase (PDF: 15-0127) can also be detected for the Nb–9Ti–
8C alloy, which suggests a different microstructure.
Fig. 2 shows the SEM back scattered electron (BSE) images of
the as-cast Nb–Ti–C alloys. As conﬁrmed by the XRD, a two-phase
like microstructure consisting of Nbss and carbide was observed
for each alloy. However, it should be mentioned that, it is difﬁcult
to directly clarify the Nb 2C phase and the (Nb, Ti)C phase for the
Nb–9Ti–8C alloy (Figs. 1c and 2c), due to their similar composition.
In order to further determine the carbides, the EDS analysis was
performed. As shown in Table 2, a solid solution nature can be ob-
tained for these two carbides. A relatively higher Nb content and a
lower content of Ti (higher average atomic number) of Point 2 re-
sult in a relatively brighter contrast, and this phase can be deter-
mined to be the Nb 2C phase. The result is corresponding to the
Ref. [14], Ti has little solubility in Nb 2C phase, on the other hand
NbC makes with TiC a wide continuous band of solid solutions in
Nb–Ti–C composition. So, the phase with relatively darker contrast
(Point 1) should be the (Nb, Ti)C phase, because of the lower aver-
age atomic number. It should be also noted that the content of C is
much higher ( /C24 65 at.%), which is not consistent with the composi-
tion. This is probably the result of contamination in the SEM during
collection of the spectra.
For Fig. 2 a–c, the Nbss exhibits a coarse dendritic morphology,
indicating that the matrix precipitates primarily and a eutectic
mixture of carbide and Nbss phases forms from the interdendritic
liquid. The comparisons of Fig. 2a and b suggest that the grain size
increases with increasing the addition of Ti element. Also, more
carbides precipitate as the fraction of C increases ( Fig. 2 c). For
the Nb–25Ti–8C alloy ( Fig. 2 d), the large-sized carbide (the dash
circle) is seen, which suggests that this carbide forms primarily
during solidiﬁcation, followed by the precipitation of the Nbss ma-
trix and the eutectic mixture of the carbide and the Nbss phase. It
is also of interest to note that the grain reﬁnement can be achieved
by doping C into the Nb matrix ( Fig. 2 d).
3.2. Oxidation resistance
Fig. 3 shows the mass gain curves of the circular oxidation at
800 /C176C. As can be seen, the mass gain increases with increasing
the oxidation time and this relationship between the weight gain
and the time follows an approximate parabolic reaction equation
[22,23]. The average mass gain, for Nb–21Ti–4C, Nb–35Ti–4C and
Nb–25Ti–8C alloys, is 0.18 mg/cm
2, 0.34 mg/cm 2, 0.56 mg/cm 2.
The values of oxidation reaction rate constant based on the weight
gain per unit area measurements are similar for the three alloys,
and the Nb–25Ti–8C alloy demonstrates more outstanding oxida-
tion resistance than the other samples. Based on the parabolic
reaction equation and mass gain rate, the results suggest that the
rate of oxidation decreases gradually with increasing the time,
namely the matrix alloy can be protected effectively by the oxi-
dized layer when the working temperature is at 800 /C176C. However,
for Nb–9Ti–8C alloy, the average mass gain is 3.8 mg/cm 2, which
result from the formation of non-protective oxidation layer. These
results show that the oxidation resistance is signiﬁcantly improved
by doping Ti elements into Nb–Ti–C alloy. However, an inverse

of oxygen into the matrix signiﬁcantly decrease owing to the reac-
tion of Ti and O, during which a compacted oxide layer can be ob-
tained attributing to the formation of TiO
2.

4. Conclusions
Based on the microstructure and oxidation behavior analysis of
Nb–(21, 35)Ti–4C and Nb–(9, 25)Ti–8C alloys, the following con-
clusions can be drawn:
1. The Nb–21Ti–4C, Nb–35Ti–4C and Nb–9Ti–8C alloy consist of
the primary Nbss matrix and the eutectic structure of carbide
and Nbss. The carbides of (Nb, Ti)C and Nb 2C are detected only
in Nb–9Ti–8C alloy. On the other hand, the Nb–25Ti–8C alloy
consists of primary carbide of (Nb, Ti)C, secondary Nbss matrix
and the eutectic structure of (Nb, Ti)C and Nbss. Furthermore,
the grain reﬁnement is achieved in Nb–25Ti–8C alloy.
2. In the range between 800 and 1000 /C176C, the oxide layer are a mix-
ture of Nb 2O5, NbO2 and TiO2 in Nb–Ti–C alloy system. The oxi-
dation resistance improves with increasing the Ti element, but
aggravates with increasing the oxidation temperature. The
Nb–25Ti–8C alloy exhibits excellent oxidation resistance at
800 /C176C due to a compacted TiO 2 enrichment protective layer
obtained, which attributes to the grain reﬁnement and the
formation of the compacted oxide layer.
3. Ti element diffuses toward the reaction interface and reacts
with oxygen primarily during oxidation process at high temper-
ature, which causes the decreasing of solubility and diffusion
rate of oxygen into the Nbss matrix. The oxide mechanism of
carbide is the reaction formed C dissolving into metal oxide
and desorption of CO and CO 2, which is responsible to the
cracks forming in the oxide layer.
