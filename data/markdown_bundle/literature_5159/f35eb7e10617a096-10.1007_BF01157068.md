The main difficulties in conventional argon-arc welding methods of high-temperature nickel-base alloys are 
caused by their elevated capacity for crack formation and theoimpossibility in some cases of obtaining joints 
with a strength equal to the strength of the base metal. Welding such materials by beam methods (laser and 
electron-beam) presents special interest. This paper presents the results of an investigation of welded joints 
of high-temperature alloys obtained by laser welding.

welding sheets of weakly age-hardening high-temperature

An investigation of the weld and the near-weld zone has 
shown that in all cases the penetration was full both in weld- 
ing on the base metal and in butt welding of two sheets. The 
welding rates used provided well-formed joints without inter- 
nal and external defects. The welds had a uniform fine-flake 
structure on both sides of the sheets; some specimens were 
notched. A typical macrostructure of a welded joint is shown 
in Fig. 2. 
Using laser welding at slow rates (50 and 70 m/h) we ob- 
tained joints with a shape close to that formed by conven- 
tional welding methods. As the welding rate was increased to 
v w = 130 m/h and higher, the welds began to acquire a wine- 
glass shape (Fig. 2). This can be explained as follows: with 
increase in welding rate, the volume source of heat that 
shapes the weld is changed to a linear source. With a linear 
source of heat a gas-vapor channel appears in the constriction 
of the "wine glass." 
The penetration depth, the width of the welded joint, and 
the zone of thermal effect (ZTE) depend exponentially on 
the welding rate for v w < 100 m/h (with an increase in the 
welding rate these parameters decrease substantially). For 
v w > 100 m/h these parameters are minimum and do not 
change any more (Table 3). Compared to argon-arc welding 
the ZTE decreases by a factor of 5 - 7, which is associated 
with the considerable decrease in the time during which the 
metal stays in the near-joint zone (NJZ) in the region of sub- 
solidus temperatures [1, 2].

With increase in the rate of laser welding the dendritic 
structure has a tendency to disintegrate (Fig. 4). It becomes 
more dispersed, which is caused by a smaller zone of concen- 
tration cooling. We should also note the substantial segrega- 
tion inhomogeneity of the metal of the welded joints. In ac- 
cordance with the data of [2, 3] titanium and tungsten tend to 
a more intense segregation in the investigated alloys. Tita- 
nium enriches interaxial spacings and depletes the axes of 
dendrites. In crystallization occurring in laser welding we did 
not encounter features of dispersion segregation of the 
)"-phase in light primary axes of dendrites. This seems to be 
connected with the depletion and the rather rapid cooling of 
the solid metal, which provides selective hardening of the 
solid solution. It has been established that in welding high- 
temperature nickel alloys by conventional methods the con- 
centration of the alloying elements (Ti, C, Si, Mn, A1) along 
dendritic axes does not depend on the thermal parameters of 
the weldpool and the conditions of cooling from tempera- 
tures below the solidus. The chemical composition of the 
axes remains invariable; only the distribution of the alloying 
elements in the interdendrite spacings changes. The distribu- 
tion of the elements depends on the region of the weld (from 
periphery to the center). For a transcrystallite structure the 
proportion of the alloying elements in dendritic axes and in- 
teraxial spacings decreases from the periphery of the weld to 
its center and tends to 1. This is even more typical for laser 
welding, because in this case the diffusion processes leveling 
the metal composition are suppressed by the short time of 
crystallization. It seems that the properties of the metal of the 
weld change with the methods and regimes of welding de- 
pending on the mutual orientation of dendrites and the inter- 
dendrite segregation. 
In the zone of thermal effect the welding regime mostly 
determines the welding time and the degree of dissolution of 
the ),'-phase in the zone. For argon-arc welding, on the con- 
trary, the most important processes are the grain growth, the 
recrystallization, the migration of grain boundaries, and the 
formation of the secondary structure [1].

growth and recrystallization in the ZTE do not occur in either 
of the investigated specimens. This indicates that the rate of 
welding does not affect the grain size in the ZTE. The zone 
of thermal effect is only weakly defined even in intense pick- 
ling of specimens of alloys VZhl01 and steel VZhl02 
(Fig. 6a, b), which is associated with selective homogeniza- 
tion of the solid solution and a quite complete dissolution of 
the strengthening ,/~ec-phase over grain boundaries. In grain 
bodies this phase does not dissolve. Since the time during 
which the metal of the near-weld zone in laser welding stays 
in the temperature range of the dissolution of the strengthen- 
ing phase is considerably shorter and the phase does not have 
enough time to dissolve completely, the possibility of the ap- 
pearance of cracks due to phase presence in this zone is 
rather low. We did not detect fusion over the nearby grain

The microhardness over the cross section of the welded 
joints remains virtually unchanged. 
We could not determine the saturation of the metal with 
nitrogen and oxygen metallographically, because the 
amounts of carbonitrides and oxides changed very little over 
the cross section of the metal. 
A comparative analysis shows that in conventional ar- 
gon-arc welding the width of the near-weld zone is 5- 7 
times greater than after laser welding. In addition, argon-arc 
welding is inevitably accompanied by grain growth and local 
fusion of grain boundaries, which does not occur in laser 
welding of high-temperature alloys. 
CONCLUSIONS 
1. The use of laser welding at a rate of 50 - 170 m/h for 
thin sheets of weakly age-hardening high-temperature nickel 
alloys provides high-quality defectless welds and makes it 
possible to considerably decrease the length of the zone of 
thermal effect by eliminating nondesirable structural trans- 
formations and grain growth in this region. 
2. The use of helium in laser welding reliably protects 
the liquid and solid metal from hydrogen and nitrogen of the 
air despite the high contents of chromium, titanium, and alu- 
minum in the alloys.
