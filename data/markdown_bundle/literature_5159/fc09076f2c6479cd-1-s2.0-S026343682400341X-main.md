1. Introduction
Niobium alloys are renowned for their high melting point, excellent
specific strength, superior plasticity, and outstanding machinability and
weldability. As a highly significant high-temperature material, it has
been widely used in aerospace fields such as rocket nozzles, jet aircraft
heat exchanger tubes, and nuclear reactors [ 1 – 7 ]. Its typical operating
temperature range is between 1200
◦
C and 1400
◦
C. However, the
strength of pure niobium alloys rapidly declines at temperatures above
1000
◦
C, posing challenges for their high-temperature applications [ 8 ].
To overcome this challenge, researchers have added high melting point
elements such as W, Mo, Ta, Zr to enhance the niobium alloy through
solid solution strengthening. This processing method has paved new
ways for improving the performance of niobium alloys [ 9 – 12 ]. For
instance, FS-85 (Nb-11 W-27.5Ta) and WC3009 (Nb-30Hf-9 W) show an
increase in yield strength by approximately 0.2 GPa and 0.3 GPa at
1200
◦
C, demonstrating significant strengthening effects [ 13 ]. However,
refractory high-entropy alloys containing Mo and W elements exhibit
poor room-temperature plasticity. Similarly, Nb-containing NbMoTaW
and NbMoTaWV refractory high-entropy alloys have high yield
strengths of above 400 MPa at 1600
◦
C, but only ~2 % compressive
plasticity at room temperature [ 14 , 15 ].
Second phases such as silicide and carbides have been introduced to
enhance the strength of the niobium alloy through second-phase
strengthening [ 17 – 21 ]. However, introducing small amount of car-
bides shows limited strength improvement, such as Nb-1Zr-0.1C alloys,
which has a yield strength of only 0.2 GPa at 1300
◦
C [ 17 , 18 ]. Increasing
the second-phase content shows a high strength enhancement, for
example, the Nb-10Mo-10Ti-18Si and Nb-10Mo-15 W-10Ti-18Si alloys
have high yield strengths of 0.55 GPa and 0.65 GPa at 1500
◦
C,
respectively [ 19 ]. Unfortunatly, their room-temperature plasticity is
limited with a compressive fracture strain of only 1 %, showing a

4.2. Effects of fine grain strengthening and interfacial strengthening
Generally, increasing the strength by adding alloying elements and
carbide phases results in an expense of plasticity. However, the
Nb

0.5

The phenomenon of elemental segregation is influenced by the dif-
ferences in melting points between the constituent elements. The doping
of high-melting point elements exacerbates these differences, leading to
an enhancement of non-equilibrium solidification phenomena, which is
not conducive to improving the plasticity of the alloy. Mo and Tungsten
W have higher melting points and larger atomic diffusion rates, resulting
in a greater difference in melting points between the constituent ele-
ments. This causes both the start temperature (Tc) and the end tem-
perature (Ts) of phase transformations to increase, enhancing the
stability of phase formation. Moreover, for those alloy systems with
small melting point differences, it is conducive to promoting the uni-
formity of the organization. As illustrated in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 , the den-
dritic spacing of the Nb
