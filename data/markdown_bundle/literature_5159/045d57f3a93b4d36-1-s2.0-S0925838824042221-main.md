1. Introduction
Molybdenum-based alloys with high melting points ( > 2000 
◦
C), 
moderate costs, good machinability, and sustainability have been 
considered as promising substitutes for extensively used nickel-based 
superalloys in aero-engines to meet the requirements of increasing 
operating temperature [1 – 5] . However, there are two long-standing 
obstacles towards practical applications — inadequate mechanical per -
formance and high-temperature oxidation resistance. The former could 
be improved by doping ceramic nanoparticles to achieve fine-grain and 
dispersion strengthening [6 – 10] . Regarding the latter issue, Mo-Si-B 
alloys have been developed to suppress the pesting oxidation caused 
by MoO
3 
evaporation at intermediate temperatures, by promoting the 
formation of a protective borosilicate scale on alloy surfaces when 
exposed to oxygen [11 – 13] .
Mo-rich Mo-Si-B alloys with phase constitution of α -Mo + Mo
3
Si +
Mo
5
SiB
2

mechanical performance and oxidation resistance [14] . However, 
achieving a practical level of improvement in both mechanical proper -
ties and oxidation resistance remains a challenging task. One crucial 
strategy for enhancing these properties in advanced Mo-Si-B alloys is the 
incorporation of second-phase particles. Li et al. [15] notably enhanced 
fracture toughness and compression strength through the combination 
of liquid-liquid doping, mechanical alloying and hot pressing sintering. 
In the case of the Mo-12Si-8.5B alloy, Li & Wang et al. [16 – 23] devel -
oped a bimodal α -Mo grain structure consisting of both micrometer and 
sub-micrometer grains using mechanical alloying and incorporating

introduced to mitigate impurity O element segregation, thereby 
reducing grain boundary (GB) defects, purifying GBs, and improving GB 
cohesion. The combination of GB strengthening and the formation of 
dense (Hf, Y)-containing silicate layers results in superior mechanical 
and oxidation properties.

Si-B ternary systems to regulate the phase constitution [28] . Yoshimi 
et al. [29 – 33] carried out thorough investigations on the phase equi -
libria, microstructure, and room/high-temperature mechanical proper -

characteristics when incorporating second-phase particles. Although 
Mo-Si-B-Ti alloys prepared by arc melting have been systematically 
investigated, there are few works on Mo-Si-B-Ti alloys fabricated via 
powder sintering. Due to the distinct physical processes involved in 
these two fabrication methods, several unaddressed issues persist. This 
study focuses on four Ti-added Mo-rich alloys, Mo-12Si-8B- x Ti ( x = 10,

the phase constitution, microstructure, room-temperature mechanical

2. Experimental procedures
This study focused on alloys with two phase constitutions: α -Mo +
Mo
3
Si + Mo
5
SiB

and α -Mo + Mo
5

+ Ti

of Ti in Mo-rich alloys is limited to a maximum of 40 %. Following this 
criterion, alloy samples with nominal compositions of Mo-12Si-8B- x Ti 
(at%, x = 10, 20, 30, 40), hereafter referred to as 10Ti, 20Ti, 30Ti and 
40Ti alloy, respectively, were prepared by mixing Mo, Si, B, and Ti 
powders with purities of 99.95 %, 99.99 %, 99.9 %, and 99.9 % wt%, 
and particle sizes of 3 – 5 μ m, 3 – 5 μ m, 0.5 – 2 μ m, and 1 – 3 μ m, respec -
tively. Powders were provided by Zhongnuo Advanced Material Tech -
nology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Initially, the mixed powders were 
milled in a planetary mill at a speed of 300 rpm with powder-to-ball 
volume ratio of 1:1. The ball mill was rotated forward and reversed 
for 5 h respectively under an argon protective atmosphere to obtain a 
homogeneous mixture. Subsequently, the mixed powders were placed in 
a graphite mold, and were then put into a vacuum hot-pressing furnace 
(CXZD-50, Chenxinweike). The furnace operating parameters see else -
where for details [46] . Finally, the sintered samples underwent 
annealing at 1600 
◦
C in vacuum for 3 h to relieve residual stress.
X-ray diffraction (XRD-6100, Shimadzu) with Cu-K α radiation 
( λ = 1.5406 Å) was used for the phase identification of unoxidized alloys 
(2 θ - θ measurement mode) and oxide scales (thin film grazing incidence 
XRD), operating at a scanning speed of 1 
◦
/min and counting interval of 
0.02 
◦
. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS GeminiSEM 300) 
equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Oxford 
Xplore) detector and electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD, Oxford 
C-Nano) detector was used to surface morphology observation, semi- 
quantitative element analysis and grain size/distribution characteriza -
tion. The EBSD scanning step size was set as 0.15 μ m, misorientation 
angle θ > 15
◦
was used to distinguish the GBs. Specimens for EBSD un -
derwent initial mechanical grinding and polishing with SiC sandpapers 
of varying grit sizes (120, 240, 400, 800, 1000, and 1200), followed by 
polishing with 9 μ m, 3 μ m, and 50 nm SiO
2 
suspensions, and concluding 
with argon ion milling under liquid nitrogen cooling. Transmission 
electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai F20) was utilized to characterize 
grains and phase interfaces, the specimens were prepared by ion-beam 
thinning. Subsequent post-processing of XRD, EBSD and TEM results 
were carried out using Jade 9, Channel 5 and Gatan Digital Micrograph 
software, respectively.
Thermodynamic modeling was used to construct the isothermal 
phase diagram of Mo-Si-Ti ternary system and theoretical phase 
constitution of (80- x )Mo-12Si-8B- x Ti quaternary systems. The calcula -
tion was performed employing CALPHAD approach [47,48] , with Gibbs 
energy functions of pure elements as input (sourced from NIST Ther -
modynamic Database [49,50] ). Further information on the thermody -
namic modeling can be found elsewhere [51 – 56] .
The sample density was measured using the Archimedes suspension- 
wire method (MH-300A, Arip Instruments). Microhardness was assessed 
by indentation testing with a Vickers hardness tester (HV-1000A, 
Huayin) under loads of 0.5 and 1 kgf for 15 s. The final value was 
determined as the average of five indentation tests, sufficient spacing 
between indentation locations was ensured to prevent interference. 
Young ’ s elastic modulus and Poisson ’ s ratio were measured using an 
ultrasonic material characterization system (UMS-100, TECLAB) on 
specimens sized 5 × 5 × 3 mm. These measurements relied on the 
constitutive relation between these two parameters with the shear and 
longitudinal wave velocities in isotropic solids. The room-temperature 
flexural strength and fracture toughness were determined via 
unnotched and single-edge notched three-point bending (SENB) tests on 
specimens sized 25 × 3 × 5 mm, with a pre-existing crack of 2.5 mm depth 
at the beam center specially for SENB tests. Formulas for calculating 
flexural strength and fracture toughness can be found in Refs. [32,37] . 
Each alloy underwent two independent measurements for three-point 
bending tests, each of other tests mentioned above were conducted 5 
times.
Simultaneous thermal analysis (TGA/DSC; PerkinElmer, STA 6000) 
was performed in an air atmosphere to assess the thermal stability and 
decomposition behavior of alloys. The analysis involved differential 
scanning calorimetry (DSC), thermogravimetric (TG) and derivative 
thermogravimetric (DTG) profiles as functions of temperature ranging 
from 30 to 1400 
◦
C, with a heating rate of 10 
◦
C/min. Cyclic oxidation 
experiments were conducted in a high-temperature oxidation furnace 
(CXYH-4, Chenxinweike) at 800, 1000, and 1200 
◦
C for 20 h. The 
specimens were placed into the furnace once the temperature reached to 
a stable range. Every 5 h, the specimens were taken out, cooled, and 
weighed using an electronic balance with a precision of 0.1 mg. Sub -
sequently, the specimens were placed back into the furnace, and these 
steps were repeated until the completion of the oxidation experiment.
