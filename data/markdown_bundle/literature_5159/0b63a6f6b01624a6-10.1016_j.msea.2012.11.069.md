abstract
Ti–50Pt (all compositions in atomic %) alloy exhibits phase transformation from B2 phase having bcc
crystal structure to orthorhombic martensitic (B19) phase above 1200 K. However, it exhibits a partial
shape memory effect due to its low critical stress for slip deformation compared with the stress
required for martensitic transformation. In order to improve the shape memory properties and strength
of Ti–50Pt alloys at high temperature, this study examined the effects of partial substitution (e.g. 5 at%)
of Ti with group IV (e.g. Zr) transition metals and the effects of partial substitution of Pt with platinum
group (e.g. Ru) transition metals. The compressive strength and shape memory properties at high
temperature were improved by partial substitution of Ti with Zr as well as by partial substitution of Pt
with Ru in Ti–50Pt alloys: e.g., 1468 MPa for TiPt–Zr, 712 MPa for TiPt–Ru and 485 MPa for TiPt alloys.
Furthermore, Ti–50Pt–5Zr alloy exhibited much higher strength and better shape memory effect than
Ti–45Pt–5Ru alloy.

1. Introduction
The shape memory effect is related to diffusionless solid phase
transformation from parent austenite phase to thermo-elastic
twinned martensite upon quenching, detwinned martensite upon
mechanical loading and reverse phase transformation from mar-
tensite to parent austenite phase upon heating [1,2]. The shape
memory effect results from non-linear strain recovery upon
heating and is used for biomedical and engineering applications
[3,4]. However, most of the shape memory alloys currently used
for such applications are limited to temperatures below 373 K due
to their low transformation temperatures below 373 K, low
resistance to high-temperature oxidation, low long-term stability
and low critical stress for slip deformation at high temperatures
compared to the stress required for thermo-elastic martensitic
transformation [5–8].
Recently, non-allergic and non-toxic
b type titanium based
shape memory alloys have attracted attention for biomedical and
engineering applications [9–13]. However, many of these alloys
cannot be used for high-temperature applications due to the
precipitation of isothermal o phase having a hexagonal structure
and/or isothermal a phase having a hexagonal closed packed
structure upon heating above 373 K, resulting in a decrease in
fracture strain and shape memory properties [14–17]. Near-
equiatomic TiNi, which is known as nitinol, is a well-known
titanium based shape memory alloy due to its excellent shape
memory and superelastic properties [18–21]. However, most
applications of nitinol at present are also below 373 K due to its
low transformation temperature [22,23].
Recently, Ti–Ni– X shape memory alloys have been studied for
high-temperature (above 373 K) applications such as gas turbines,
automobiles, rocket and aero engines, and thermal power plants
[24,27]. Ti–Ni–Zr and Ti–Ni–Hf high-temperature shape memory
alloys (HTSMA) with average martensite ﬁnish temperature ( Mf)
of around 400 K and average austenite ﬁnish temperature ( Af)o f
around 530 K have attracted interest mainly due to the relatively
low cost of the raw materials and relatively high transformation
temperatures compared to binary Ti–Ni alloys [25–27]. Miyazaki–
Kim’s research group recently reported Ti–Ni–Hf–Nb [27], Ti–Ta–
Sn [28], Ti–Ta–Al [29] and Ti–Ni–Pd–Cu [30] alloys for high-
temperature material applications due to their high transforma-
tion temperatures, thermal stability and good cold workability
compared to binary Ti–Ni shape memory alloys. However, the
austenite ﬁnish temperature ( Af) of these alloys remained below
873 K, and hence their potential for high-temperature material
applications, remained below 873 K [27–30]. Pt and Pd have also
been shown to be effective for increasing the transformation
temperatures of Ti–Ni shape memory alloys [31,32].
Our research group has recently reported equiatomic Ti–Pt and
near-equiatomic Ti–Pt–Ir based alloys for high-temperature

and equiatomic Ti–Au [39,40] shape memory alloys, equiatomic
Ti–Pt based alloys also exhibit phase transformation from B2
phase having ordered bcc crystal structure to thermo-elastic
martensitic phase upon quenching. However, equiatomic Ti–Pt
based alloys exhibit very low ( /C24 11%) shape memory effect due to
the low critical stress for slip deformation compared to the stress
required for martensitic transformation [35,37]. One great advan-
tage of equiatomic Ti–Pt based shape memory alloys is their very
high (above 1300 K) austenite ﬁnish temperature [35,37] com-
pared to equiatomic Ti–Ni based shape memory alloys (below
373 K) [22,23] and near-equiatomic Ti–Au based shape memory
alloys (below 900 K) [39]. Therefore, equiatomic Ti–Pt based
alloys could be used for much higher-temperature shape memory
material applications if their shape memory properties can be
enhanced by increasing the critical stress for slip deformation at
high temperature.
Compared to b titanium shape memory alloys that generally
transform to isothermal o (hexagonal) and/or a (hexagonal
closed packed) phase precipitation upon heating and thus suffer
deterioration of their shape memory properties and fracture
strain [14–17], equiatomic Ti–Pt based alloys do not transform
to such o and/or a phase precipitation upon heating [33–38].
However, equiatomic Ti–Pt alloys have low strength when
mechanically tested at high temperature (above 1000 K) [35].
Increasing the critical stress for slip deformation will improve the
shape memory properties as well as low cycle fatigue properties
of Ti–Pt alloys as low cycle fatigue is a strain based parameter
[41,42]. Increasing the stress for slip deformation will also
improve the strength as well as high cycle fatigue (high cycle
fatigue is a stress based parameter) properties [41–43] of Ti–Pt
high-temperature shape memory alloys [35,37].
Zr belongs to the same group as titanium (group IV) in the
periodic table, and the number of valence electrons for each group
IV transition metal element is the same (four valence electrons).
However, the atomic radius of Zr (0.155 nm) is larger than that of
Ti (0.140 nm), so solid solution strengthening is expected due to
the generation of compressive stresses by the partial substitution
of Ti with Zr atoms having large atomic radii [44]. One objective
of this study was to improve the strength and shape memory
properties of Ti–50Pt alloys at high temperature over 1000 K by
partial (5 at% in this study) substitution of Ti with the group IV
transition metal Zr in expectation of an increase in critical stress
for slip deformation related to solid solution strengthening effect.

refer to six metallic elements clustered together in the periodic table.
These elements are all transition metals, lying in the d-block

Ruthenium (Ru) has lower density (12.4 g/cm 3) than platinum

partial substitution (5 at%) of Pt with Ru, which also belongs to PGMs
in terms of high-temperature mechanical and shape memory proper-
ties and phase transformation temperatures of Ti–50Pt alloys.
For shape memory alloys, there is a close relation between
critical stress for slip deformation, critical stress for martensitic
transformation/martensite reorientation and shape memory effect.
For good shape memory effect, the critical stress for slip deformation
should be higher than the stress req uired for martensitic transfor-
mation/martensite reorientation; otherwise shape recovery will be
adversely affected as more irreversible deformation will be induced
by dislocation slip [47–49]. The critical stress for slip deformation
decreases with increasing temperature and if it becomes lower than
the stress required for martensitic transformation/martensite reor-
ientation, the shape memory properties will drastically decrease due
to the occurrence of plastic defor mation. Negligible shape memory
effect and single yielding of Ti–50Pt alloy at 1183 K below the
martensite ﬁnish temperature are related to low critical stress for
slip deformation compared to the stress required for martensite
reorientation of B19 orthorhombic martensite, as only B19 marten-
sitic phase was analyzed at room temperature [35]. The objective
qof this study was to investigate if we can improve the high-
temperature mechanical and shape memory properties of Ti–50Pt
alloy by increasing the critical stress for slip deformation due to
partial substitution of Ti and Pt with Zr and Ru, respectively.
2. Experimental procedures
Ti–50Pt–5Zr (at%) and Ti–45Pt–5Ru (at%) alloy ingots were
prepared by the argon arc melting method using high purity
(99.9%) Ti, Pt and Ru metals. After arc melting, these alloy ingots
were sealed in a silica tube with a small amount of argon gas and
solution-treated at 1523 K for 10.8 ks. This solution-treatment
temperature was in the B2 (austenitic) phase region, well above
the austenite ﬁnish temperature and well below the melting
temperature of equiatomic Ti–Pt alloys [37]. Then, the alloy ingots
were ice–water quenched for diffusionless martensitic phase
transformation. Plate samples were sliced using a wheel cutting
machine from the solution-treated alloy ingot for XRD and
microstructure observation. To determine the transformation
temperatures, small pieces measuring 2 /C2 2 /C2 0.8 mm were cut
from button ingots of Ti–50Pt–5Zr and Ti–45Pt–5Ru alloys using a
cutting wheel. The phase transformation temperatures were
investigated using differential thermal analysis (DTA) between
873–1373 K at a heating and cooling rate of 0.17 K/s using a
Shimadzu DTA-50 machine.
For XRD analysis, sliced samples were mechanically polished
before XRD measurement. X-ray diffraction analysis was con-
ducted at room temperature (298 K) from 20–90 1 in 2
y using a
Rigaku TTR-III X-ray diffractometer equipped with Cu Ka radia-
tion. For microstructural analysis, the sliced samples were
mounted using thermosetting polymeric powder and mechani-
cally polished using standard grinding/polishing procedures.
Sputter etching (dry etching) was used for Ti–50Pt–5Zr and
Ti–45Pt–5Ru alloys using a glow discharge optical emission
spectrometer (GD-OES) using Ar and N 2 gas mixture as sputter
etching agent. Conditions of 200 Pa pressure, 20 W power and
30 s sputtering time were used for the dry sputter etching
process. Optical micrographs were then taken of the sputter-
etched Ti–50Pt–5Zr and Ti–45Pt–5Ru developed alloys.
To measure the shape memory effect of the Ti–50Pt–5Zr and
Ti–45Pt–5Ru developed alloys, rectangular samples measuring
2.5 /C2 2.5 /C2 5 mm were cut from the solution-treated alloy ingots
using a wheel cutting machine. These samples were then com-
pressed at 5% applied strain at a temperature of 50 K below the
martensite ﬁnish temperature using a Shimadzu AG–X compres-
sion test system. Cyclic loading–unloading compression tests
were conducted once the desired temperature had stabilized.
After the compression tests, samples were heated at 1523 K for
3.6 ks under vacuum. The different experimental steps used for
measuring the shape memory effect are shown schematically in
Fig. 1 . Shape memory strain was measured by comparing the
length of the samples before deformation, after applying 5% strain
and after heat treatment. For mechanical testing, cyclic loading–
unloading compression tests were conducted at an applied strain
of 20% at a test temperature of 50 K below the martensite ﬁnish
temperature and 50 K above the austenite ﬁnish temperature

3.2. Phase analysis
Fig. 3 presents optical micrographsof sputter-etched Ti–50Pt–5Zr
and Ti–45Pt–5Ru developed alloys; only the martensitic phase
structure was analyzed. These results matched the DTA result given
in Fig. 2 where the martensite ﬁnish temperatures of these alloys
were well above room temperature. Fig. 3 clearly indicates that the
martensite plates are twin oriented for Ti–50Pt–5Zr alloy, showing
that this martensite appeared as a self-accommodating twinned
martensitic structure, which is characteristic of thermo-elastic mar-
tensite in shape memory alloys [38,50]. However, twinning was not
so clear in most of the martensite plates for Ti–45Pt–5Ru alloy,
although some twinned oriented plates were seen.
XRD results at room temperature are presented in Fig. 4 . Only
orthorhombic martensitic (B19) phase was detected for Ti–50Pt–
5Zr and Ti–45Pt–5Ru alloys at room temperature. The XRD results
are in accordance with the differential thermal analysis (DTA)
results in Fig. 2 and optical micrographs in Fig. 3 , where only
martensitic phase was detected at room temperature.
3.3. High-temperature mechanical properties
The stress–strain curves of the cyclic loading–unloading com-
pression tests at high temperatures (50 K below the martensite
ﬁnish temperature given in Table 1 ) of Ti–50Pt–5Zr and Ti–45Pt–
5Ru alloys are shown in Fig. 5. Stress was applied until the applied
strain reached 20% and then the stress was removed. Stress for
the rearrangement of martensite variants (stress for detwinned
martensite) was evaluated by drawing a line parallel to the elastic
region of the stress–strain curve and then the stress correspond-
ing to the deviation from the elastic region of the stress–stress
curve was taken as the stress required for martensite reorienta-
tion during loading. Both alloys exhibited double (two stage)
yielding phenomenon, which is characteristic of shape memory
alloys. As shown in Figs. 2–4 , there is only martensitic phase at
room temperature, so the ﬁrst yielding corresponds to a change
from twinned martensite to detwinned martensite upon loading
and the second yielding is related to permanent deformation due
to slip [51–53]. Our research group has already reported that
Ti–50Pt alloy does not exhibit the double yielding phenomenon,
indicating slip as the dominant deformation mechanism [35].

4. Conclusions
The effect of partial substitution of Ti with Zr and of Pt with
platinum group metal Ru was investigated. The strength of
martensitic phase as well as B2 phase was improved by partial
substitution of Ti and Pt with Zr and Ru, respectively. However,
compressive strength values of Ti–50Pt–5Zr and Ti–45Pt–5Ru
alloys in the B2 phase region were still much lower than those
in the martensitic phase region. Shape memory properties were
also improved by partial substitution of Ti and Pt with Zr and Ru,
respectively. However, partial substitution of Ti with Zr was
found to be much more effective than that of Pt with Ru due to
more effective solid solution strengthening effect. The compres-
sive strength at 50 K below Mf of Ti–50Pt–5Zr (1468 MPa) and
Ti–45Pt–5Ru (711 MPa) was much higher than that of Ti–50Pt
(485 MPa) alloy. It is concluded that these Ti–50Pt–5Zr and
Ti–45Pt–5Ru developed alloys have potential for high-temperature
material applications.
