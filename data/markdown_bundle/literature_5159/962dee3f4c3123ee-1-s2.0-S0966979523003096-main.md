1. Introduction 
High/medium entropy alloys (H/MEAs) are a class of materials that 
consist of multiple elements in equal or near-equal atomic proportions 
[ 1 – 6 ]. The alloy design of such a vast composition space results in the 
possibility of creating metallic materials with exceptional properties 
[ 7 – 9 ]. However, the composition of equal or near-equal atomic pro -
portions also obviously increases the raw material costs, which limits 
their widespread applications. Recently, low-cost ferrous 
medium-entropy alloys (Fe-MEAs) with excellent cryogenic ultimate 
tensile strength (UTS) have been developed by scientists [ 10 – 16 ]. 
However, insufficient room temperature yield strength (YS) of Fe-MEAs 
remains a crucial dilemma [ 17 – 19 ]. Therefore, tailoring Fe-MEA alloys 
with good cost-performance balance has become one of the challenging 
issues. 
The design of heterogenous structures through deformation and 
partial recrystallization, as well as the resulting hetero-deformation- 
induced (HDI) strengthening, has been introduced into Fe-MEAs [ 16 , 
18 , 20 – 22 ]. For example, the Fe

alloys have low service safety at room temperature. The 
transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) effect, including FCC to BCC 
transformation, or FCC to HCP transformation, is important for the 
higher cryogenic work hardening extent of Fe-MEAs [ 25 – 29 ]. The TRIP

(SFE), which are affected by alloy composition and temperature

FCC matrix phase. Meanwhile, the SFE decreases as temperature de -
creases. The synergism of decreased phase stability and lowering SFE 
usually introduce TRIP effect in Fe-MEAs during cryogenic deformation 
[ 34 , 35 ]. However, the Fe-MEAs with high YS and work hardening extent

To further characterize the microstructure of the current specimen 
before tensile testing, we carried out the TEM observations. Bright-field 
TEM image reveals that the specimen has obvious dual heterogeneous 
structures consisting with the partial recrystallization matrix and het -
erogeneously distributed nanoprecipitates (indicated by while circles in 
Fig. 3 a). The recrystallized region contains equiaxed grains with an 
average grain size of ~142 nm, and the volume fraction of the L1
2

recrystallization regions ( Fig. 3 b). The atomic percentage of Ni and Al is 
near 3, which means that the precipitates should be a NiAl-type inter -

[001] zone axis suggest that the precipitates have an ordered FCC phase 
structure with L1

non-recrystallized regions are mainly composed of high-density tangled 
dislocations ( Fig. 3 a), which are formed during cold rolling and persist 
after short annealing. Some fine nano-precipitates can also be observed 
in the non-recrystallized regions ( Fig. 3 a). Precipitation and recrystal -
lization compete with each other during annealing. The precipitation of 
the nano-precipitates consumes some of the energy under the same heat 
treatment conditions, resulting in a lower recrystallization driving force. 
Here, the precipitate has a dual influence on the evolution of disloca -
tions during both recrystallization and deformation. First, precipitation 
and recrystallization can occur simultaneously during low-temperature 
annealing. The competition between the two can restrain dislocation 
reconfiguration, resulting in the refinement of recrystallized grains [ 54 ]. 
On the other hand, the precipitate can be effective pinning dislocation 
and increasing deformation resistance. This results in an increase in the 
strength of materials while maintaining a good plasticity. In addition, a 
large number of annealing twins can also be observed in the recrystal -
lized regions ( Fig. 3 f). As we know, the twin, firstly, itself can tune the 
mechanical properties, and secondly, it means a low SFE of matrix 
phases [ 32 ]. 
4. Discussion 
Based on the above structural analyses, the high yield strength of the 
current alloy is closely related to its dual heterogeneous structures. First, 
the partial recrystallization matrix can contribute to the hetero- 
deformation-induced (HDI) strengthening effect due to the mutual 
constraint between the non-recrystallized regions and the recrystallized 
regions during deformation [ 24 , 55 ]. Second, the high-density nano -
precipitates also provide a strong precipitation strengthening. There -
fore, the superior room temperature mechanical response of the current 
specimen should be the result of the synergism of multiple mechanisms, 
which can be described by a simple linear rule as follows:

As we can see, the initial microstructure of current specimens in -
cludes high-density prestored dislocations and nanoprecipitates 
( Fig. 2 a), but an obvious yield platform followed by a prominent strain 
hardening stage with increasing strain, can be observed on the stress- 
strain curves ( Fig. 1 a), which is consistent with the deformation 
behavior of other Fe-MEAs and some TRIP steels when deforming at 77K 
[ 57 – 59 ]. To better elucidate the deformation mechanism, we performed 
EBSD analyses on the deformed samples by interrupting tensile tests. 
Fig. 5 shows the phase volume fraction of the tensile specimen with 
different engineering strains. The initial structure of the alloy is 
composed of FCC phase (95.8 vol %) and BCC phase (4.2 vol %) ( Fig. 5 a), 
which is consistent with the XRD results ( Fig. 2 ). When the engineering 
strain is 5 % (~ lower yield point), about 17 % of the FCC phase is 
transformed into the BCC phase ( Fig. 5 b). When the strain is 10 % (~ the 
end of yield platform), ~65.2 % of the FCC phase is transformed into the 
BCC phase ( Fig. 5 c). The volume fraction of BCC phase continues to 
increase with the increase of strain, and almost all of them become BCC 
phase (~96 %) after fracture ( Fig. 5 d – f). Obviously, the addition of Ti 
and Al produces a second phase with Ni while reducing the Ni content in 
the matrix, introducing precipitation strengthening while decreasing 
FCC stability in the present alloy. This accelerates the FCC → BCC 
martensitic transformation, which enhances both strength and work 
hardening extent through the TRIP effect at room temperature. 
In addition, the accelerated martensitic transformation should also 
relate to the localized partial recrystallization structures. Previous 
research has revealed that the martensitic transformation occurs pref -
erentially at defects [ 14 , 43 ]. This is due to the fact that defects not only 
supply nucleation sites for phase transformation, but they can also be 
exploited as an energy source, thus reducing the nucleation barrier. 
Therefore, the high-density prestored dislocations in the initial structure 
also accelerate the martensitic transformation. Here, it is important to 
note that the highly localized prestored dislocations make the 
martensitic transformation first activating in the non-recrystallized re -
gions, resulting in typical non-uniform deformation behavior across the 
length of the whole yield platform stage ( Fig. 1 a), corresponding to stage 
II. The MEA demonstrates obvious yield drop after reaching the upper 
yield point (960 MPa), followed by a long yield platform. It suggests that 
the yield drop phenomenon is due to the lack of mobile dislocations

As we know, the plasticity of metals depends on the multiplication 
and evolution of dislocations. In the present specimens, the interplay 
between the non-recrystallized regions and the recrystallized regions 
during deformation forms a multiaxial stress state, which can promote 
the spatial multiplication of dislocations [ 54 , 62 , 63 ]. As can be seen from 
Fig. 5 e, the fractured specimen consists of 96.1 % BCC phase and 3.9 % 
FCC phase, and the interface between the FCC phase and the newly 
formed BCC phase plays an additional obstacle to dislocation motion. In 
addition, the nano-precipitates act also as additional obstacles to 
dislocation motion. Fig. 7 displays the TEM images of the sample after 
fracture. As can be seen that the high-density dislocations and dislocated 
cellular structures occur around the newly formed BCC phases ( Fig. 7 b 
and c). It indicates a prominent multiplication and evolution of dislo -
cations with the increase of strain, which is also beneficial to work 
hardening extent. The nano-precipitates also are found around in the 
region with more dislocations, indicating that the nano-precipitates 
participates in the tuning of deformation by interacting with the dislo -
cation motion ( Fig. 7 a). 
Based on the analyses mentioned above, the addition of Ti and Al 
produces the nanoprecipitates, introducing precipitation strengthening 
while decreasing FCC stability in the present alloy, which contributes to 
a continuous martensitic transformation from FCC to BCC and a conse -
quent TRIP effect during whole plastic deformation. The dynamic syn -
ergism of multiple mechanisms, including the TRIP effect, HDI 
strengthening, and precipitation strengthening, leads to an ultra-high 
ultimate tensile strength and work hardening extent at room 
temperature.
