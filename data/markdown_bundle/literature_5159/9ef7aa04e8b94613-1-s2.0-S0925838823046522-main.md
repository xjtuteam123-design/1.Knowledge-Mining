1. Introduction 
There is increasing need for materials that can survive extreme 
temperatures for extended periods to enable more efficient turbine jet 
engines and hypersonic vehicles technologies [1,2] . Legacy structural 
superalloys based on iron, nickel, or cobalt remain the workhorse 
high-temperature metals [2 – 6] . They are widely used for their oxidation 
resistance, formability, and toughness [7] . However, they are limited to 
applications below 1000 
◦
C because microstructural instabilities lead to 
an unacceptable drop in mechanical strength at higher temperatures 
[8 – 12] . To overcome this limitation, ultra-high-temperature ceramics 
and ceramic-matrix composites are in development [1,13] , but their 
brittleness remains a bottleneck for engineering, machining, and use in 
complex-shaped components. To meet the needs of rapidly evolving 
technological demand, it is imperative that new metallic alloys are 
designed to maintain strength and structural integrity at temperatures 
beyond 1000 
◦
C [14 – 16] . 
Body-centered cubic (BCC) refractory multi-principal element alloys 
(RMPEAs) are a class of metals that shows great potential for high- 
temperature applications due to their remarkable solid-solution 
strengthening and temperature stability [17 – 20] . Derived from the 
concept of high entropy alloys (HEAs) [21,22] , equiatomic or 
near-equiatomic compositions tend to form single disordered phases 
with improved solid-solution strengthening [23] . In addition, recent 
studies of deformation mechanisms in BCC RMPEAs have highlighted 
the importance of non-screw dislocations [24,25] to achieve high 
strength beyond the current superalloy regime. To further identify 
ultra-strong, stable, and lightweight RMPEAs, Elder et al. [26,27] pro -
posed a departure from the conventional equiatomic RMPEA design rule 
by optimizing for BCC phase stability and maximization of yield 
strength. With BCC RMPEAs showing the desired high strength and 
solid-solution strengthening, it is therefore paramount to investigate the

Since key alloy properties such as strength, ductility, texture, fatigue, 
fracture, corrosion, and electrical conductivity are influenced by den -
dritic microstructure, understanding the dendrites formed by the 
CrMoNbV is important. Surface/interfacial energies which are energet -
ically favored in certain growth directions in metals are anisotropic. The 
crystallographic facets along the 〈 100 〉 cube-edge are the preferred 
growth direction in most cubic metals because they form the minimum 
energy shape in parallel and opposite directions to the heat flow [30] . 
This has previously been confirmed in FCC CrCoMnFeNi (Cantor alloy) 
by the combination of phase field simulation and in-situ observation 
using three-dimensional time-resolved tomography and 
three-dimensional-x-ray diffraction [31] . To examine the dendrite 
morphological features with crystallographic direction of the underlying 
body centered cubic (BCC) crystal structure and confirm orthogonality, 
EBSD was used to index the crystallographic orientation of the alloy. 
Fig. 2 (a-b) shows the inverse pole figure (IPF) and image quality (IQ) 
maps collected during the EBSD scan. Hough Transform-based indexing 
of diffraction patterns from five points randomly selected on a single 
dendrite stem and arm gave the bands shown in Fig. 2 (c). The indexed 
bands corresponding to symmetrical equivalents of hkl indices allow for 
easy identification of angular relationships and orthogonality. Fig. 2 (c) 
shows multiple bands, but the (200) and (020) bands of the 〈 100 〉
cube-edge are the only directions parallel to the reference dendrite stem 
and arms. The dendrite stem and branches show special angular relation 
that reflect orthogonality along the six 〈 100 〉 cube-edge direction. Sim -
ilarities in dendrite morphology and orientation between the three 
samples point to similar cooling and solidification rates.
