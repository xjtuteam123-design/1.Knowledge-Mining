Abstract
This study investigates the tensile properties and high-temperature wear behavior of laser-directed energy deposition (LDED) prepared 
 CoCrFe0.5Ni1.5Nb0.2 high-entropy alloy (HEA). Molecular dynamics simulations reveal Shockley dislocations dominate the deformation mechanism 
during stretching. Experiments demonstrate outstanding room temperature mechanical properties: 746.6 MPa tensile strength and 28.9% elonga-
tion. At 800°C, the alloy shows superior wear resistance with low friction coefficient (0.47) and wear rate (13.4 × 10–6  mm3/N·m), attributed to high-
temperature oxide layer formation. The combined atomic-scale simulation and experimental analysis provide fundamental insights for applying HEAs 
in high-temperature tribological environments, highlighting their potential for extreme condition engineering applications.
Introduction
Laser-Directed Energy Deposition (LDED) is an advanced 
additive manufacturing technology that enables the rapid proto-
typing of complex geometries and the preparation of high-per-
formance materials.[1–4] This technology utilizes a high-energy 
laser beam to melt and deposit metal powder layer by layer, 
forming dense metallic components. LDED is characterized 
by high energy density, rapid cooling rates, and precise con -
trol, which allow for effective regulation of the microstructure 
and mechanical properties of alloys. Consequently, the use of 
LDED for the fabrication of alloys not only facilitates their 
rapid formation but also enhances their mechanical properties 
and wear resistance through optimized process parameters. 
High-Entropy Alloys (HEAs), [5–7] as a novel class of multi-
principal-element alloy materials, have garnered significant 
attention in recent years due to their unique compositional 
design and superior mechanical properties. Unlike traditional 
alloys, HEAs consist of five or more principal elements in equi-
molar or nearly equimolar proportions[8] and exhibit character-
istics such as the high-entropy effect, lattice distortion effect,[9] 
sluggish diffusion effect, and "cocktail" effect. [10] These fea-
tures enable HEAs to demonstrate exceptional performance 
in extreme environments, including high-temperature, high-
strength, and high-wear-resistance conditions. [11–13]
CoCrFeNi-based HEAs have attracted considerable atten-
tion owing to their remarkable room temperature plastic -
ity,[14–16] similar atomic radii, and compatibility with the 
face-centered cubic (FCC) structure, which collectively pro-
mote the formation of stable solid solutions. The integration 
of high-melting-point elements such as cobalt (Co) and nickel 
(Ni), combined with chromium’s (Cr) excellent antioxidant 
properties, ensures that these alloys maintain their strength 
and creep resistance at elevated temperatures. Moreover, the 
incorporation of niobium (Nb) into CoCrFeNi-based HEAs not 
only induces significant lattice distortion through atomic size 
mismatches,[17–19] thereby enhancing solid solution strengthen-
ing, but also facilitates the formation of the Laves phase dur -
ing LDED processes, achieving precipitation strengthening and 
markedly improving tensile strength. However, the study of 
high-temperature friction and wear properties of HEAs should 
not be neglected as well, especially in high-temperature envi-
ronments,[20–22] where the friction and wear behaviors of the 
materials have a direct impact on their service life and reli -
ability. The high-temperature friction and wear performance is 
not only related to the mechanical properties such as hardness 
and toughness of the material, but also affected by a variety of 
factors such as surface oxidation, phase transformation, and 
lubrication conditions. [23–27] Therefore, it is of great signifi-
cance to systematically study the friction and wear performance 
of high-entropy alloys at different temperatures and to explore 
the correlation between them and their microstructures, in order 
to optimize the design of the materials and to expand their 
application fields.
In summary, the aim of this paper is to prepare 
 CoCrFe0.5Ni1.5Nb0.2 HEA by LDED technology and to study 
their tensile properties using molecular dynamics simulation, as 
well as their friction and wear properties at different tempera-
tures. Through systematic experimental and simulation studies,

Experimental method
LDED technique was used to prepare CoCrFe 0.5Ni1.5Nb0.2 
HEA. The raw materials were high-purity (≥ 99.5%) Fe, Ni, 
Co, Cr, and Nb metal powders, which were mixed according 
to the proportion of the desired compositions, and the shape of 
the powders and the distribution of the surface elements were 
shown in Fig. S1. The parameters of the LDED process were 
as follows: the laser power was 1000 W, the scanning speed 
was 15 mm/s, the energy density calculated is 22.22 J/mm 2, 
the powder feeding rate was 4 g/min, and the protective gas 
was 99.999% high-purity argon. The deposited samples will 
be wire-cut into dog-bone-shaped specimens measuring 34 
mm × 10 mm × 1.5 mm and friction wear specimens measuring 
20 mm × 10 mm × 1.5 mm for subsequent testing and analysis.
The composition of the phases was analyzed by X-ray dif-
fraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance) with Cu–Kα radiation 
(λ = 0.154 nm). The scanning range was from 30° to 100° 
with a scanning rate of 4°/min. PA53MET optical microscope 
(OM), scanning electron microscope (SEM, JEOL JSM-
7800F) equipped with X-ray energy dispersive spectrometer 
(EDS) were used to investigate the microstructural characteri-
zations, powder shape, fractography, and chemical composi-
tion distribution of the samples. Electron backscatter diffrac -
tion (EBSD) was employed to analyze grain orientation and 
dislocation density. The experimental conditions included an 
acceleration voltage of 20 kV , a step size of 0.9 μm, an area 
size of 765 μm × 600 μm, and a magnification of 100 × . The 
tensile experiments were carried out at room temperature using 
a universal materials testing machine (Instron 5967) with a 
strain rate of 1.2 × 10–3 s−1. The tensile properties were further 
verified by molecular dynamics simulations, and the atomic 
model of CoCrFe0.5Ni1.5Nb0.2 HEA was constructed by using 
the LAMMPS software, which was used to simulate uniax-
ial tensile process, and the force field was selected from the 
embedded atom method (EAM) was chosen for the force field, 
the strain rate was set to 1 × 1010 s−1, and the temperature was 
set to 300 K.
Microhardness was measured using a Vickers hardness 
tester (HV-1000) with a load force of 1.96 N applied for 10 s. 
To minimize experimental error, 15 measurements were taken 
at different locations along the building direction. The blocky 
alloy was subjected to pretreatment through dissolution in the 
reactor, and the elemental composition of the alloy was subse-
quently analyzed using ICP-MS (Agilent 7800). The high-tem-
perature friction and wear experiments were conducted using 
a ball-disk friction and wear tester (model: HT-1000), and the 
abrasive material was Si3N4 ceramic balls with a diameter of 6 
mm. The experimental conditions were: load of 1000 N, slid-
ing time of 20 min, and temperatures of 25°C,200°C, 400°C, 
600°C, and 800°C. The wear rate was calculated by measuring 
the wear volume, which was analyzed by a white-light interfer-
ometer (Zygo NewView 9000) for three-dimensional morphol-
ogy. The wear surface morphology was observed by SEM and 
the elemental distribution in the wear area was analyzed using 
an energy spectrometer (EDS).
Microstructure of the HEA
Figure 1(a1) shows the XRD pattern of the CoCrFe0.5Ni1.5Nb0.2 
alloy. The analyses of the XRD spectra indicated that the LDED 
sample have a single FCC crystalline structure. The phase and 
phase percentage of the HEA through equilibrium solidifica -
tion were predicted using Thermo-Calc software with TCHEA3 
database. It shows that the alloy has FCC accompanied by other 
phases, such as Laves and sigma phases, and the presence of 
Laves phases was also confirmed in subsequent SEM micro-
graphs (point by white arrow). To explore the grain morphol-
ogy evolution, the metallographic structures of BD and SD 
direction of the sample were observed by OM and SEM. Fig-
ure 1(b) represents the cross-section zone of the LDED sample 
perpendicular to the Y direction, which shows that the direc-
tion of columnar grain extends toward both sides. During the 
LDED process, the growth mode of dendrites is influenced by 
multiple factors, including heat flow and temperature gradient. 
The heat transfer within the molten pool primarily occurs along 
the building direction and the laser scanning direction, caus-
ing the main dendrite stem to tend to grow along the direction 
of maximum heat flow (i.e., perpendicular to the boundary of 
the molten pool). The backscattered electron image reveals the 
distribution of the higher ordinal number Nb (white regions). 
The microstructure along SD direction of the wall-shape HEA 
is presented in Fig. 1 (c), which is almost filled with dendritic 
grains and cellular grains. During the process of LDED, the 
microstructure will be influenced by the high-speed inert gas. 
After melting, the smaller droplets have a high cooling rate 
and form cellular crystals. The larger droplets have difficulty 
in dissipating heat and solidify due to high temperature, and the 
cellular crystals will transform into dendritic crystals.
The SEM mapping and point locations are presented in 
Fig. 2, which found that Fe, Co, and Ni elements are uniformly 
distributed, there is local segregation of Nb element and local 
absence of Cr element in the Nb-enriched region. The differ -
ences in element content can be depicted in both Fig. 2 (a) and 
Table S1. The mixing enthalpy constitutes the crucial factor 
for diffusion and distribution of Nb element, mixing enthalpy 
of Nb with Cr, Fe, Co, and Ni is − 7 kJ/mol, − 16 kJ/mol, − 25 
kJ/mol, and − 30 kJ/mol, respectively. When the alloy under -
goes melting and subsequent solidification, Nb possessing the 
highest melting point, will solidify first. Cr and Nb exhibit the 
most positive mixing enthalpy, coupled with the lowest bind-
ing energy between them, which precludes the formation of a 
stable compound. There is in accordance with the Laves phases 
predicted by the phase diagram.

Mechanical property and tensile 
simulation
The mechanical properties of CoCrFe 0.5Ni1.5Nb0.2 HEA, 
including microhardness and tensile at room temperature, were 
studied. The average microhardness of the alloy is 223.3 ± 7 
 HV0.2. The dimensions of the tensile specimen are illustrated 
in Fig. 3(a1). At ambient temperature, the yield strength of the 
alloy is 367.1 MPa, the ultimate tensile strength is 746.6 MPa, 
and the elongation to failure is 28.9%. Figure 3(a2) depicts the 
macroscopic morphology of the tensile fracture, which exhibits 
an irregular surface and a non-linear crack propagation path, 
indicative of ductile fracture. Figure 3 (a3) presents the SEM 
mapping analysis of the tensile fracture surface, revealing the 
enrichment of Nb at the tear ridges. The presence of Laves 
phase and Nb segregation, as observed in Fig. 2 (a), suggests 
that the Nb-rich secondary phase may serve as the origin of 
crack initiation during tensile deformation. Tensile tests inves-
tigate the macroscopic mechanical behavior of materials under 
uniaxial loading conditions. LAMMPS molecular dynam-
ics (MD) simulations elucidate the microscopic deformation 
mechanisms at the atomic scale: when the simulation system 
is subjected to strain, dislocations facilitate plastic deforma -
tion through nucleation, slip, and proliferation. The explana -
tory capability of dislocation theory for macroscopic plasticity 
can be validated using LAMMPS. Furthermore, the limita-
tion of experimental methods, which cannot directly observe 
defect motion, is addressed by visualizing the dynamic evolu-
tion of the dislocation network, thereby offering mechanistic 
insights for the design of material strengthening and toughening 
strategies.

Wear behavior at various temperature
The coefficient of friction (COF) curves at various temperature 
are illustrated in Fig. 4(a). In the initial phase of friction system 
operation, the integration of alloy materials significantly impacts 
the dynamics of interfacial interactions. Specifically, the inher-
ent properties of the alloy, including its high surface hardness 
and chemical reactivity, induce transient incompatibilities within 
the friction pair assembly. Consequently, the friction coefficient 
curve demonstrates characteristic non-linear growth during this 
period. As the alloy is continually abraded by the friction pair, 
its surface becomes smoother, leading to a more stable friction 
coefficient. Throughout the friction process, the microscopic 
irregularities and impurities on the contact surfaces are gradually 
worn away, and changes in the surface microstructure directly 
influence the magnitude of the friction force, resulting in fluc-
tuations in the friction curve. As the temperature rises, the COF 
of the alloy progressively diminishes, indicating a reduction in 
both wear and energy loss. The average friction coefficient of 
each temperature is calculated and shown in Fig. 4(b), which is 
0.715 (RT), 0.722 (200°C), 0.621 (400°C), 0.484 (600°C), and 
0.466 (800°C), respectively. Alloys exhibit the highest coefficient 
of friction at 200°C, maybe due to the increased propensity for 
micro-convexities on the surface of material to form interlocking 
joints at this temperature. This necessitates greater force to over-
come these joints, thereby enhancing the coefficient of friction. 
As the temperature rises further, the micro-convexities soften, 
resulting in a reduction in the friction coefficient. Figure 4(c) 
displays the cross-sections of wear tracks. At 600°C and 800°C, 
the depth and width of the abrasion mark are significantly lower 
than other temperatures.

The complex effects under different temperatures are 
investigated by the corresponding worn surface morphol-
ogies as shown in Fig. S4. It can be seen that Fig. S4(a) 
shows a rotating high-temperature friction and wear device 
to study the friction and wear of alloys at different tempera-
tures by varying the temperature of the heating furnace. Fig. 
S4(b) – (f) shows the surface morphology of the alloys at dif-
ferent temperatures, and it can be clearly seen that the alloys 
are subjected to three types of wear mechanisms, which are 
abrasive, adhesive, and oxidative wear, respectively. Abra -
sive wear is a common wear mechanism, in which the fric -
tion partner continuously cuts the alloy during the rotation 
process, thus generating a large number of abrasive particles. 
Although the surface of the alloy is carefully polished, micro-
scopic unevenness still exists, and metal adhesion can occur 
locally on the contact surfaces of the friction pair during 
rotational friction. Oxidative wear is a chemical–mechani -
cal synergistic degradation mechanism of materials under 
the combined influence of high-temperature friction. This 
process can be categorized into three critical stages: First, 
when the temperature in the heating environment surpasses 
the critical threshold for material oxidation (typically above 
400°C), the base metal reacts with oxygen to form a surface 
oxide layer. Second, during the relative motion of the fric-
tion pair, the shear stress induced by micro-protrusions leads 
to cracking and localized spalling of the brittle oxide film, 
thereby exposing fresh metallic surfaces. Finally, the exposed 
reactive metal undergoes rapid secondary oxidation in the 
high-temperature environment, and the newly formed oxide 
layer re-enters the wear cycle.
From the content and distribution of oxygen in the surface 
spectrum, it can be found that the change of oxygen content 
and the change of average coefficient of friction are consist-
ent with the changes of 6.40 at.% (RT), 4.02 at.% (200°C), 
19.31 at.% (400°C), 39.06 at.% (600°C), 47.26 at.% (800°C), 
respectively. The lower the content of oxygen, the higher the 
average coefficient of friction, which indicates that at high 
temperatures, the oxidized film of the alloy acts as lubrication 
during friction and reduces direct contact between friction 
partner and the alloy which reduces the coefficient of friction. 
At 200°C atmosphere, the oxide film is not the main factor 
affecting the coefficient of friction, and the oxygen at this 
time may be increasing the surface roughness through the 
formation of oxide particles, resulting in a higher coefficient 
of friction.
Conclusion
In this work, LDED technology was used to prepare 
 CoCrFe0.5Ni1.5Nb0.2 HEA free of metallurgical defects, and the 
microstructure, mechanical properties, and friction and wear 
properties of the alloy at different temperatures were investi-
gated, and the main conclusions are as follows:
(1) The CoCrFe0.5Ni1.5Nb0.2 HEA produced via LDED exhib-
its a predominantly single FCC crystalline structure with 
secondary Laves phases (e.g., Fe2Nb). The microstruc-
ture exhibited Nb segregation and Cr depletion in Nb-rich 
regions are attributed to differences in mixing enthalpy 
and binding energy, influencing phase formation and 
elemental distribution.
(2) The alloy demonstrates excellent mechanical properties 
at room temperature, including a yield strength of 367.1 
MPa, ultimate tensile strength of 746.6 MPa, and elonga-
tion of 28.9%. Ductile fracture behavior is observed, with 
Nb-enriched secondary phases acting as crack initiation 
during tensile deformation.
(3) Plastic deformation in the alloy is governed by Shockley 
partial dislocations (Burgers vector 1/6 < 112 >), stacking 
faults, and twins. Dislocations primarily accumulate at 
low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs), with grain bound-
ary deformation and dislocation interactions dominating 
polycrystalline plasticity.
(4) The wear resistance improves at elevated temperatures 
(600 – 800°C) due to thermal softening resistance and the 
formation of a lubricating oxide layer. Wear mechanisms 
transition from abrasive and adhesive wear (RT ~ 400°C) 
to oxidative wear (600– 800°C), with the lowest wear rate 
(13.1 × 10–6 mm3/N·m) observed at 600°C, correlating 
with enhanced oxide film stability.
