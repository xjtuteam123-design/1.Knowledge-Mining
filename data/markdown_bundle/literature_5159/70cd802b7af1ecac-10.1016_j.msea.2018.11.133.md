ABSTRACT 
Two low-carbon 9-Cr ferritic-martensitic steels were designed with the aim of decreasing M23C6 and maintaining or 
increasing MX phase fraction . A low -carbon (LC) alloy and a low-carbon, zero-niobium (0Nb) alloy were 
fabricated, their designs based upon the P92 alloy system . Solutionizing temperatures to maximize V and Nb in 
solution while avoiding δ -ferrite were determined to be 1170 °C for the P92 alloy and 1050 °C for LC and 0Nb, 
significantly lower than predicted using ThermoCalc® modelling. As was intended, the M23C6 phase fraction was 
reduced for LC and 0Nb alloys after both heat treating and aging relative to the base P92 alloy , as determined by 
wide angle x-ray scattering (WAXS) analysis. Dislocation density measurements from x-ray line broadening in P92 
and LC suggest these alloys had more stable dislocation substructures than 0Nb at lower temperature and shorter 
time aging conditions. While LC exhibited lower microhardness than P92 at room temperature, the tensile properties 
were comparable at 650 °C, suggesting that elevated temperature strength can be achieved with lower carbon 
contents. Aging studies showed that P92 had a more stable microstructure for higher temperature and longer time 
aging conditions. The P92 alloy also had a longer stress rupture life, implying that the M 23C6 precipitate contribution 
to thermal stability is important. Evidence of Z -phase was discovered for the LC alloy aged 10,000 h at 650 °C, 
corresponding to decreased strength and increased ductility. Overall, the stress rupture lives of the modified heat-
treatment variations of P92 and LC compare favorably to literature values for 9% Cr steels with conventional heat 
treatments.

I. INTRODUCTION 
Conventionally processed 9Cr ferritic/martensitic ( F/M) steels are a cost -effective solution for structural 
power plant applications, including the nuclear industry where irradiation effects are an additional consideration. A 
long-standing goal is increasing the creep rupture temperature from 620 to 650 °C for the “standard life ” of 
100,000 h at 100 MPa [1]. In the normalized and tempered condition, the strength of F/M steels comes from 
dislocation density and boundary strengthening in the martensitic microstructure, M23C6 [M: Cr23, Fe21Mo2]

precipitation at lath boundaries , dispersion strengthening by MX [M :V,Nb; X: N,C] precipitates, and solid solution 
strengthening with tungsten (W) and molybdenum (Mo) [2]. Prominent mechanisms of strength degradation during 
creep e xposure include coarsening of precipitates, dissolution of carbonitrides and precipitation of a Z -phase that 
consumes solute and existing MX precipitates, and dislocation recovery. Coarsening occurs at lower rates for MX 
precipitates than M 23C6 precipitates [2-3]. Thus, it was posited that the rate of strength reduc tion at elevated 
temperatures may be reduced by maintaining MX precipitation and decreasing M 23C6 precipitation. 
One promising route to improve creep resistance may be a reduction in carbon as shown by Taneike et 
al. [5], who performed creep rupture testing at 650 °C for several Fe–9Cr–3W–3Co–0.2V–0.06Nb–0.05N alloys 
with carbon level variations. Time for rupture increased at carbon levels below 0.02 wt%, which was attributed to a 
decrease in M 23C6 precipitation leading to stabilization of martensite structure by more coarsening resistant MX 
precipitates. ThermoCalc ® analysis reveal ed that M23C6 phase fract ion decreases with decreasing carbon content 
while the MX phase fraction is nearly constant, since MX precipitates are predominantly nitrides. Taneike et al. [5] 
showed that reducing carbon allows fine MX precipitates rather than M 23C6 precipitates to form at boundaries [6], 
and c reep rupture properties improve because MX precipitates are more coarsening resistant , pinning boundaries 
more effectively during elevated temperature creep. 
The objective of the current paper is to analyze precipitate characteristics and stability of modified P92 
steels, both in as -heat treated and thermally aged conditions, developed through computational simulations with the 
objective of producing microstructures with smaller M23C6 fractions and greater MX fractions than conventional P92 
steel [7]. The alloy design approach and results are discussed, heat treatment design is presented, and resultant 
microstructures are examined through TEM and WAXS/SAXS characterization of precipitation . The alloy design, 
processing, and microstructure are then related to room temperature hardness and elevated temperature tensile and 
creep properties.

strategy is distinctly different than that employed by Taneike et al. [4], who used cobalt (Co ) to stabilize austenite. 
Co is also known to inhibit thermal recovery processes [10]. Thus, the current study isolates the influence of 
precipitation with respect to thermal recovery since the alloy design strategy does not include additional elements 
that mitigate recovery . One designed alloy, designated the LC alloy, has 0.01 wt.% C with austenite stabilized by 
increased Mn and an addition of N to e nhance MX precipitation. A low -carbon, zero-niobium (0Nb) alloy was also 
designed to assess effects of reduced Nb on strength levels and a proposed influence of Nb accelerating precipitation 
of undesired Z-phase [11]. Vanadium was augmented in this alloy to offset the decrease in MX phase fraction that 
resulted from elimination of niobium. Overall, modifications to the low carbon (LC) alloy included nominal 
composition changes to 0.01 wt% C, 1.4 wt% Mn, and 0.06 wt% N. The 0Nb alloy design shared these changes and 
also had the additional removal of Nb to 0 wt% and increased V from 0.20 to 0.25 wt%. The 0Nb alloy was 
expected to have a similar amount of MX precipitation at equilibrium as the LC alloy over the temperature range of 
interest [7]. 
During heat treating, both solutionizing and austenitization considerations are important in achieving a 
fully martensitic microstructure with maximum precipitation strengthening potential upon subsequen t tempering and 
aging. To achieve a fully martensitic microstructure, the alloy must be fully austenitized before cooling to room 
temperature. The criteria for selecting the solutionizing temperature was to dissolve the maximum amount of 
precipitate-forming elements while avoiding the δ -ferrite phase , since the Fe 2W Laves phase preferentially 
precipitates at δ-ferrite/martensite boundaries during elevated temperature exposure , leading to cavities that result in 
decreased creep resistance [12]. Increasing the amount of V in the low Nb alloy results in l ess solute V at 
solutionizing temperatures. Undissolved V results in coarser MX precipitates that do not contribute significantly to 
strengthening or microstructure control. A nominal 0.05 wt% increase in V was predicted to maintain or increase the 
amount of fine MX precipitation based on ThermoCalc® modeling for a solutionizing temperature of 1050 °C, which 
is conventionally used to heat treat this class of ferritic-martensitic steels. 
 
III. EXPERIMENTAL METHODOLOGY 
Alloy Fabrication and Heat Treatment Design 
Experimental alloys were fabricated at the National Energy Technology Laboratory. High nitrogen levels 
were targeted in the LC and 0Nb alloys both to offset a reduction in austenite stability and to promote fine 
precipitation [7]. ThermoCalc® was used to ascertain the likelihood of solutionizing a high qu antity of nitrogen [13]. 
Figure 1 shows the solubility of nitrogen in the liquid steel for the P92 and LC alloys using the TCFE7 database. 
The label “GAS” indicates the predicted weight percent N at the melt temperatures available as nitrogen gas. The 
results were interpreted to suggest potential difficulty avoiding porosity after solidification using nitrogen levels in 
excess of 0.04 wt%, though the designed alloys call for a nitrogen content of 0.06 wt%.

Determination of Phase Transformation Temperatures 
Phase transformation temperatures were determined through a combination of optical microscopy and 
dilatometry. Two dilatometry runs were performed and averaged for each alloy. Cylin drical dilatometry specimens, 
3 mm in diameter and 10 mm in length, were induction heated to 750 °C over the course of 10 min and held for 
thermal equalization for an additional 10 min before being heated to 1000 °C at 5 °C/min. An additional run 
included heating the specimens to 1250 °C in an attempt to determine the temperature where austenite transforms to 
δ-ferrite, but no dilatometric changes associated with phase transformation were detected in the region where 
δ-ferrite was indicated by ThermoCalc®. As such , optical microscopy in conjunction with Vickers microhardness 
was utilized to identify the temperature where austenite begins to transition to δ -ferrite, as this phase was to be 
avoided for improved creep resistance. Samples were polished to 1 μm diamond , and Vickers microhardness was 
conducted at 500 g load with a 10 s dwell time; 30 indents were taken from 6 disparate sections of the coupons. 
Microscopic analysis was conducted on these samples after they were etched with Villela’s Reagent (1% picric aci d, 
5% HCl, 94% ethanol).

Mechanical Testing 
Mechanical property evaluation included creep rupture testing and high-temperature tensile testing of 
normalized and tempered specimens before and after aging. All specimens were machined with lengths parallel to 
the rolling direction. For high-temperature tensile testing, a temperature of 650 °C was chosen for consistency with 
creep testing and with the goal operating temperature for this alloy. A quasi -static true strain rate of 0.001 s-1 was 
selected. Displacement was measured with an extensometer placed outside the closed-vacuum tensile apparatus; 
thus, compliance corrections were applied. The geometry for the tensile and creep specimens is shown in Figure 3; 
samples were subsized due to material constraints. 
Couplers to test the specimens in the creep frames were machined from Inconel 718 for high -temperature 
creep resistance. A stress of 150 MPa was used at a temperature of 650 °C for all creep tests. The lever arm of the 
creep machine applies a 20x mechanical advantage to the weight added to the weight -bearing side of the machine. 
Thus, with a creep sample cross-sectional diameter of 4.78 mm (0.188 in), 13.6 kg (30 lbs) was loaded onto the 
mechanically advantaged lever arm to apply 150 MPa.

IV. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Effects of Initial Processing on Microstructure and Microhardness 
Phase transformation temperatures obtained from the dilatometry exper iments and ThermoCalc ® 
predictions are given in Table 5. The measured values of A 1, A 3, and M s were repeatable to within 5 °C for each 
alloy. As predicted in ThermoCalc®, the A1 temperatures for the designed alloys (LC and 0Nb) are lower than the A 1 
temperature for the P92 alloy. All of the examined alloys have A 3 temperatures very close to 900 °C. As C, N, and 
Mn are strong austenite stabiliz ers, the increase d Mn and N in the LC and 0Nb alloys provided a single phase

significantly lower than the measured values of A1. However, the The rmoCalc® predictions for the A 3 temperatures 
were much closer to the experimentally measured values, with the P92 alloy showing the only significant deviation 
from the ThermoCalc ® prediction for A 3. The M s measured for the designed alloys is higher than th e P92 alloys, 
which could be due to the lower carbon level, and may increase the likelihood of auto-tempering in LC and 0Nb.

Vickers microhardness is plotted for the as received, as normalized, and as normalized and tempered 
conditions in Figure 5; the solutionizing temperature was 1050 °C for all normalized conditions. It should be noted 
that the designed alloys have a lower hardness in the as received and normalized conditions before tempering . 
Several factors could contribute to this decreased microhardness. Less carbon in the designed alloys yields less solid 
solution strengthening than in P92 . In a similar sense, the LC alloy may be softer than 0Nb due to lesser nitrogen 
content. The presence of δ-ferrite in the 0Nb could also lead to decreased microhardness. Upon tempering, hardness 
decreases as tempering temperature increases. While the designed alloys were significantly softer in the as received 
and as normalized conditions, this difference is much less after tempering, demonstrating that the designed alloys 
have more resistance to softening during tempering. While LC was softer in the as-received condition, LC becomes 
harder than 0Nb upon tempering. One possibility is that more precipitation occurs in LC than in 0Nb, in contrast to 
ThermoCalc equilibrium predictions.

Correlating Microhardness to Material Properties as a Function of Aging Conditions 
The Larson -Miller Parameter (LMP) is often used to represent time/ temperature equivalence to help 
understand the dependence of mechanical properties on aging conditions. Equation 4 presents the parameters 
involved in calculating the LMP, with temperature in Kelvin and time in hours. 
 (4) 
Figure 10a shows a Larson -Miller plot of microhardness (HV 0.500) versus LMP for all of the aging 
conditions of each of the alloys, combined. Two stages were identified: Stage (I) consists of a fairly c onstant 
microhardness with secondary hardening noted in all three alloys at LMP values between approximately 18,500 and 
19,500. In Stage II, microhardness decreases rapidly at rates of 0.062, 0.092, and 0.041 HV0.500/LMP for P92, LC, 
and 0Nb, respectively. P92 and LC exhibit similar trends, with Stage II for both alloys beginning at a Larson -Miller 
Parameter of approximately 21,500. The 0Nb alloy begins to soften at a lower LMP of approximately 20,200, 
although it softens at a lower rate than either P92 or LC. These hardness results indicate that the P92 and LC alloys 
have enhanced thermal stability compared to the 0Nb alloy at lower combinations of aging temperature and time , 
possibly owing to the presence of δ -ferrite in the 0Nb condition . At higher values of LMP, i.e. higher temperature 
and time combinations, the hardness of LC degrades significantly more than the P92 alloy, implying there is a role 
of M 23C6 carbides in maintaining strength in this regime. The lower slope noted for the 0Nb alloy at high LMP 
values possibly reflects microstructure evolution beneficial in engineering extended elevated temperature life , as 
increased stability is one critical performance metric. 
Figure 10b shows microhardness plotted vs. the square root of dislocation density; dislocation density 
appears to be a clear correlative for strength in these alloys. Strength of martensite is largely controlled by 
dislocation density, even in highly tempered conditions [20]. However, the plot indicates that other strengthening 
mechanisms must be operative as the hardness for P92 is usually higher than the other alloys for a give n dislocation 
density. This result again implies that there is an important strengthening role of M 23C6 carbides. M23C6 precipitates 
likely provide precipitation strengthening. They also can assist in resisting recovery, i.e. preserving the lath 
boundaries in the martensitic microstructure. Overall, promoting MX over M23C6 precipitation, was not sufficient to 
achieve a high hardness and maintain thermal stability compared to the P92 alloy with higher amounts of M 23C6.

Tensile engineering stress versus str ain curves for P92, LC, and 0Nb as HT at room temperature and at 
650 °C are given in Figure 11. Tensile engineering stress versus strain curves for P92, LC, and 0Nb aged for 2,000; 
5,000; and 10,000 h at 650 °C are given in Figure 12a, Figure 12b, and Figure 12c, respectively. Tensile results at 
650 °C for P92 and LC are nearly indistinguishable as heat treated and upon aging up to 2,000 h despite significant 
differences in room temperature tensile properties and Vickers microhardness. The tensile properties at elevated 
temperature imply d ifferences in elevated temperature and room temperature strengthening mechanisms. For 
example, short range barriers to dislocation slip are less effective at elevated temperature, though possible 
differences in short range barriers between the P92 and LC alloys are not immediately obvious. 
The as HT condition exhibits by far the highest yield strength in every alloy. Yield strength initially falls 
precipitously after 2,000 h aging for P92 and 0Nb and changes much less during longer aging. After 10,000 h aging, 
yield strength is halved in the LC alloy while total elongation is nearly tripled compared to the as HT condition. The 
large decrease in strength of LC at the longest aging condition could be associated with the precipitation of Z -phase 
noted in the WAXS results. Z-phase forms from MX precipitates but proceeds to coarsen rapidly after 
transformation, thus depleting strengthening from both fine precipitates and from solid solution contributions [11]. 
This behavior is depicted more precisely in Figure 13, which shows yield and tensile strength and reduction in area 
(RA) at 650 °C for P92, LC, and 0Nb as a function of aging time at 650 °C. Reduction in area increases slightly as 
YS decreases, up to 10,000 h. The higher strength of the P92 condition again supports the importance of M 23C6 for 
elevated temperature strength and microstructural stability. Promoting MX precipitation in the designed alloys did 
not compensate for the reduction of M 23C6. The 0Nb alloy shows lower dislocation density, which can partially 
explain its lesser strength. Upon aging, the importance of higher phase fraction of M23C6 as the LC alloy 
microhardness decreases even while the dislocation density matches or exceeds that of P92.

V. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS 
1. The amount of M 23C6 precipitation was significantly higher in the P92 condition than the LC and 0Nb 
conditions as intended by the alloy design. 0Nb also showed evidence of δ -ferrite despite phase modeling 
predicting the onset of δ-ferrite formation at higher temperatures. 
2. The designed alloys have lower microhardness than the P92 condition . At elevated temperatures up to 650 °C, 
the LC condition has comparable tensile properties to the P92 condition even after 2000 to 5000 hours of aging, 
which suggests that elevated temper ature strength can indeed be achieved with lower carbon contents. 
However, the higher thermal stability of P92 results in higher strengths after longer aging times at 650 °C.
