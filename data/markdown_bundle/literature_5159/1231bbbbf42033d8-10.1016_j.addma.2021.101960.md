1. Introduction 
The increasing demands for lower cost and more environmentally 
friendly operating conditions in the aeronautical industry lead to a 
constant search for better performing lightweight materials. The mate -
rial composition and processing routes need to be considered together 
since the improvements in performance often require parts with better 
material properties built into more complex design geometries. In this 
scenario, additive manufacturing (AM) techniques are gaining consid -
erable attention recently because it allows ample freedom of design 
[1 – 4] . Although some Al alloys have been produced by these techniques, 
often, these alloys are conventional alloys, which were not designed to 
match the specific processing conditions inherent of the AM processes 
[1,4] . 
A particularly challenging development for this industry is the design 
of high-temperature Al alloys. Since Al has a low melting point of about 
660 
◦
C, diffusion is already very significant above 200 
◦
C, because 
talking about mechanical properties as a function of temperature, 200 
◦

means 473 K, which corresponds to about 50% of the absolute melting 
temperature. This yields enough thermal energy to overcome short- 
range deformation barriers and grain and precipitate coarsening in 
most conventional alloys, leading to negligible strength above this 
temperature [5] . Promising alloys for these applications usually involve 
mixtures of fine dispersoids that are hard and thermally stable, for 
example, one of the best performing alloys is the commercial 8009 alloy 
produced via melt spinning and subsequent hot extrusion [6] . It consists 
of fine intermetallics and quasicrystals embedded on a face-centered 
cubic ( α -Al) matrix [7] .

Icosahedral quasicrystalline (i-QC) phases are typically hard and 
brittle at room temperature due to the difficulty of dislocation move -
ment in the quasiperiodic lattice [8,9] . However, when used as rein -
forcement phases in metallic composites, i-QCs allow significant 
improvement in mechanical properties [9] . Icosahedral quasicrystalline 
phases have been found in several Al-based alloys [10 – 14] and, when 
combined with a ductile α -Al matrix, typically results in high-strength 
and moderate ductility composites [8] . The Al-Fe-Cr-Ti system is one 
example, where nanometric i-QC with spherical morphology forms in a 
dendritic α -Al matrix. These composites present high strength and good 
thermal stability at high temperatures [15] . The i-QC phase in these 
Al-based systems is metastable and typically needs cooling rates in the 
order of 10
3
– 10
6 
K/s to precipitate [16,17] . As a consequence, quasi -
crystalline Al-based alloys are generally processed by rapid solidifica -
tion methods such as copper mold suction casting [18] , melt spinning 
[19] , laser surface remelting [14,17] , or gas atomization [20,21] and are 
subsequently consolidated via hot extrusion [22] . Thus, the 8009 alloy 
discussed previously fits in this category. However, specimens prepared 
by these processes are limited in terms of dimensions and geometry, 
which restricts their applicability. In addition, the process of hot 
extrusion/consolidation may also promote grain and particle coarsening 
and decomposition of the icosahedral quasicrystalline phase during 
heating, which may reduce the strength and hardness of these alloys 
[22 – 24] . 
The possibility of employing the AM technique of Selective Laser 
Melting (SLM) for processing various Al-based alloys has been intensely 
explored in recent years. The most frequently processed Al-based alloys 
to date are conventional weldable Al-Si [25,26] , and Al-Si-Mg alloys 
[27 – 29] , which are well suited for SLM. There is a large window for the 
development of SLM-optimal alloys to exploit the unique particularities 
of this technique to design better performing materials. 
This approach is particularly attractive for producing near net- 
shaped components with customizable geometries, dimensions, and 
properties without resorting to molds, tools, or dies. By SLM, parts are 
fabricated layer by layer, using a laser to selectively melt a powder bed 
[25,28] . Due to the small melt pool and the short interaction between 
powder and laser beam, high cooling rates (10
3
– 10
5 
K/s) can be ach -
ieved [30 – 33] , resulting in a complex and ultrafine microstructure or 
even in the formation of metastable phases. Consequently, SLM is 
particularly interesting for the processing of quasicrystal-containing 
Al-based alloys. 
Kang et al. [34] have processed a mixture of pure aluminum and 
quasicrystal-forming Al-Cu-Fe-Cr powders by SLM. In this case, the QC 
phase is stable, and the authors have obtained a composite structure of 
α -Al and QCs with different sizes. They have observed that as laser 
power increases, the QC particle size increases from hundreds of nano -
meters to several micrometers [34] . Even though multiple microcracks 
formed in the sample, the material exhibited a relatively high wear 
resistance. These results indicate that this route is promising, but the 
optimal composition and processing parameters are yet to be found.

The size and distribution of dispersed phases can affect the me -
chanical properties, especially considering systems containing hard, 
brittle, plate-like particles in a ductile matrix [59] . As known, the i-QC 
particles have an extremely hard and brittle nature [9,60] . In this study, 
a properly disperse, very fine, and mostly spherical particles of the i-QC 
phase (about 200 – 500 nm) was obtained (as seen in the STEM images). 
As reported by Kimura et al. [49] , there is a clear tendency for the 
mechanical strength to increase with decreasing particle size of the i-QC 
phase [49] for a constant volume fraction, this means a higher number of 
smaller obstacles and an increase in the boundary area between the 
particles and the matrix. Precipitates hinder the movement of the dis -
locations by forcing them to either shear weak obstacles or bypass 
stronger ones (Orowan strengthening) [12] , alternatively the dislocation 
can move out of the slip plane for instance by climb, which can happen 
at high temperatures. In all cases, the stress required for a dislocation to 
propagate is increased, thus raising the yield strength of the material. 
However, i-QC particle shearing has never been observed in the litera -
ture [23,61] , this could be due to the unique crystal structure of the 
iQCs, being typically incoherent, and the shearing mechanism is more 
common in coherent precipitates. Hence, at low temperatures, Orowan 
strengthening is expected to be the main active particle strengthening 
mechanism in the present alloy, and the Orowan mechanism has the 
characteristic behavior of increasing the strength for smaller precipitate 
sizes. Therefore, the high cooling rate imposed by SLM makes it an ideal 
processing route for such material, given the exceptional mechanical 
behavior of such material. At 400
◦

since climb is expected, the yield stress should be strain-rate dependent. 
Thus, for comparison with other conventional aluminum alloys, a 
similar loading condition was used.
