of low angle boundaries in Ni–base superalloys

Abstract
An experimental study coupled with a local thermal undercooling model has been used to discriminate between possible contributions to the
evolution of misorientation producing low angle grain boundaries during solidiﬁcation in the Ni–base superalloys, CMSX4 and CMSX10N; these
include: (a) successive dendrite branching, (b) dendrite bending during steady state growth and (c) thermal-undercooling driven transient dendrite
growth kinetics. While extensive dendrite branching/steady state growth led to an average spread of misorientation≈2.3
◦ and was random in nature,
enhanced growth kinetics accompanying non-steady state conditions produced anaccumulated misorientation up to 6◦; the latter was suggested to
potentially include plastic deformation of the dendrite stems within the mushy zone.

1. Introduction
Turbine blades in single crystal form are routinely cast from
Ni–base superalloys with principal advantages arising from
enhanced creep ductility and minimisation of cyclic stresses
during thermal cycling by orienting the elastically soft ⟨001 ⟩
orientation parallel to the maximum load[1]. Not much empha-
sis has been directed towards addressing secondary grains that
form low/medium angle boundaries with the primary single
crystal; the primary focus being on freckles and geometrically
undercooled related defects. Two distinct observations in the
directional solidiﬁcation of Ni–base superalloy turbine blades
and nozzle guide vanes formed the impetus for this investiga-
tion.
i. In vanes ( Fig. 1a), the primary dendrite front splits into two
envelopes, which grow independently before subsequently
re-joining along the length of the nozzle guide vane. Small
deviations in the orientations of each branch can lead to a
low angle grain boundary when the two branches rejoin.

ii. In turbine blades (Fig. 1b), the primary front suddenly widens
at a geometrical discontinuity, such as a platform where the
secondary arms grow into undercooled liquid where tertiary
and quaternary arms also develop. Subsequent intersection
of the original and secondary growth fronts can also result in
low angle grain boundaries.
In cases (i) and (ii), three distinct solidiﬁcation phenomena occur
(Fig. 1a and b). (a) Branching of the primary dendrite front (i) and
(ii) into independent growth segments, (b) continued growth of
the split fronts (i) under steady state growth conditions governed
by the constitutional undercooling ahead of the dendrite tips
and (c) growth of the secondary front (ii) into an increasingly
thermally undercooled melt leading therefore to non-steady state
dendrite tip growth.
A number of previous investigations aimed at understanding
the evolution of low angle boundaries can be classiﬁed with
respect to the physical mechanisms outlined below.
A. Dendrite adjustment and branching during steady state
growth.

order arms occurs when there is an expanding cross-section,

B. Non-steady state dendrite growth.
An increasing local undercooling ahead of the dendrite

and thermal gradients, Bellet [5] has shown that there is
an associated greater total mosaicity, i.e. formation of sub-
grains observed in the microstructure. The formation of low
angle boundaries has been shown to occur when a secondary
front having developed by growth into a highly undercooled
liquid at geometrical discontinuities, subsequently re-joins
with the primary envelope; this typically occurs at a turbine
blade platform [2,6]. In neither of these previous experimen-
tal investigations was it possible to unequivocally identify
the contributions of: (a) branching, (b) sustained steady state
growth and (c) rapidly increasingly non-steady state dendrite
growth in producing misorientations. Dendrite branching is
interwoven with the mechanism(s) of growth. Fabietti and
Trivedi [7] have shown that the evolution of misorienta-
tion during solidiﬁcation could arise from solute inhomo-
geneities, which produce stress that lead to the formation of
grain boundaries or dislocations at the solid/liquid interface.
However, this phenomenon is restricted to facetted crystals
where the stress generated at the perturbed solid/liquid inter-
face is relaxed through the formation of grain boundaries.
FCC metals however are characterised by low entropy of
fusion and not facetted. Ding and Tewari [8] have demon-
strated through quenched directional solidiﬁcation experi-
ments in the Pb–SB (FCC) system that side branch evolution
is along ⟨001 ⟩ (four-fold symmetry) and is independent
of rate and directionality of heat extraction. This has been
conﬁrmed for the case of Ni–base superalloys as well [9].
Therefore, the generation of misorientation must be related
to the response of the fast growing ⟨001 ⟩to the local solid-
iﬁcation conditions accompanying branching and growth.
The objective of this study is subsequently two-fold: Aim 1:
identify the maximum range of misorientation arising from
repeated successive branching by precluding growth kinet-
ics and Aim 2: quantitatively characterise the response of
dendrite growth tip kinetics in producing an accumulated
misorientation.

Therefore, we suggest the possibility ofplastic deformation that

force experienced by these off-axial stems causing them to bend.

this effect. Rather, the central theme of this investigation was
to clearly delineate the roles of dendrite branching, steady state
and unsteady state growth in the formation of sub-grains and
determining the average misorientation that could be associated
with these individual phenomena.
5. Conclusions
1. An experimental investigation to delineate the roles of: (a)
cross-section motivated dendrite branching, (b) sustained
steady state growth and (c) unsteady state growth in the for-
mation of a low angle boundary has been explored.
2. Cross-section motivated branching and steady state growth
under constant undercooling produced an average misorien-
tation ≈2
◦.
3. The magnitude and random nature of such misorientation was
consistent with that related to the thermo-mechanical stress
driven plastic deformation accompanying /H9253
′precipitation in
the solid-state.
4. Advancing dendrite stems in an increasingly thermally
undercooled liquid (non-steady state growth) produced an
accumulated misorientation during branching/growth. Mis-
orientations up to 6
◦ were observed in the case of successive
branching.
5. The increased misorientation and its monotonic increase dur-
ing unsteady state conditions were suggested to possibly arise
from plastic deformation of the dendrite stems within the
mushy zone.
