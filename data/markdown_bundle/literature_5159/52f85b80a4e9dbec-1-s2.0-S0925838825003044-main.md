ABSTRACT
Structural materials for fusion reactors need exhibit both high radiation resistance and high-temperature me -
chanical properties, while also meeting low activation requirements due to nuclear waste management con -
straints. High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are recognized for their outstanding overall performance and are of 
significant interest in the field of nuclear materials. However, many HEAs contain non-low-activation elements, 
highlighting the need for the development of reduced-activation high-entropy alloys (RAHEAs) with excellent 
radiation resistance and mechanical properties. This study investigates a previously developed BCC-phase 
RAHEA FeMnCrTi
0.1 
that demonstrates good mechanical properties at room temperature, exceeding those of 
current low-activation materials and most HEAs. Here, the focus of the research is on the high-temperature 
mechanical properties and radiation resistance of this alloy. The alloy exhibits a yield strength of over 
1000 MPa and a fracture strain exceeding 25 % at 650 
◦
C. After irradiation with hydrogen ions up to 100 dpa, the 
material shows no significant segregation or dislocation loops. The average bubble size is less than 2 nm, and the 
bubble density and swelling rate is lower than that of CLAM (China Low Activation Martensitic) steel under 
similar irradiation conditions. Additionally, the irradiation hardening rate of this alloy is lower compared to 
common HEAs. This research demonstrates that the RAHEA possesses both excellent high-temperature me -
chanical performance and strong radiation resistance, indicating its potential for application in fusion reactors 
and other high-temperature, high-radiation environments.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys, are a new type of metallic material composed of 
multiple elements that form single-phase or dual-phase alloys in 
equiatomic or non-equiatomic proportions [1] . These alloys not only 
exhibit excellent mechanical properties but also possess good corrosion 
resistance, high-temperature stability, and irradiation resistance [1,2] . 
HEAs with outstanding comprehensive properties have been attracting 
increasing attention and interest in the field of nuclear materials [3,4] . 
To meet the low activation requirements for structural materials in 
fusion reactors, a class of materials known as reduced-activation 
high-entropy alloys has been developed in recent years [5] . These 
high-entropy alloys, composed of low-activation metal elements such as 
Fe, Cr, Mn, Ti, V, Y, W, and Ta, primarily exhibit a BCC phase and often 
demonstrate a hard-brittle mechanical behavior [6 – 8] . Given that the 
primary concerns for nuclear structural materials are mechanical 
properties and radiation resistance, designing a reduced-activation 
high-entropy alloy that possesses both excellent mechanical properties 
and radiation resistance is currently a key focus in the research of nu -
clear high-entropy alloys [5,9,10] .
However, there is currently limited research on the high-temperature 
mechanical performance and irradiation resistance of RAHEAs [5] . Most 
studies still focus on alloy composition design and room temperature 
mechanical properties [5] , [11 – 19] . Wu et al. developed a alloy

2.1. Alloy preparation
The FeMnCrTi

repeatedly purged with high-purity argon gas, and a molten titanium 
ingot was employed to further adsorb oxygen. Each alloy ingot under -
went five cycles of remelting, during which electromagnetic stirring was 
applied to enhance compositional homogeneity. The details of contents 
of the FeMnCrTi
0.1 
alloy was shown in Table 1 . The post-melted ingots 
were then cut into samples of various sizes using electrical discharge 
machining for microstructural characterization and performance 
testing. To facilitate comparison with low-activation high-entropy al -
loys, a typical candidate structural material for fusion reactors, reduced- 
activation ferritic/martensitic (RAFM) steel was selected. The CLAM 
steel was provided by the Institute of Nuclear Energy Safety Technology, 
Chinese Academy of Sciences, and its specific composition and pro -
cessing details can be found in reference [40] . The CLAM steel used here 
underwent standard heat treatment (The hot-rolled samples were heated 
at 1000 ℃ for 40 minutes and then water-quenched, followed by a hold 
at 750 ℃ for 90 minutes and air-cooled), and various samples were 
prepared by machining the CLAM steel to the same dimensions as those 
of the FeMnCrTi

0.1

2.2. Irradiation testing
Proton irradiation was employed to assess the radiation resistance of 
the FeMnCrTi
0.1 
alloy. Typically, the evaluation of materials ’ radiation 
resistance is often conducted using ion irradiation as a substitute for 
neutron irradiation, and heavy ion accelerators are frequently utilized 
for irradiation testing. Additionally, structural materials in fusion re -
actors are subjected not only to neutron irradiation but also to the in -
fluence of the gas atoms for helium and tritium, which can result in the 
formation of irradiation defects such as dislocation loops, precipitates, 
voids, and bubbles within the material [33 – 38] . These defects can affect 
the physical properties of the material. Therefore, in this study, proton 
irradiation was applied to evaluate the radiation resistance of the alloy. 
Moreover, the main factors influencing the gas ion irradiation of mate -
rials include flux and temperature. Different irradiation conditions can 
lead to the formation of irradiation defects with varying characteristics, 
such as defect type, density, size, and so on, which may further impact 
the material ’ s properties. High-flux hydrogen ion irradiation at room 
temperature was employed here to study the swelling behavior and

to the casting microstructure of the alloy. The dendritic BCC2 phase

banded microstructure created by grain compression deformation. This 
synergy between the soft and hard phases explains the high strength and 
good ductility observed at room temperature. This dual-phase defor -

stainless steels, which also exhibit both high strength and excellent

good resistance to irradiation hardening, making them suitable for use 
as structural materials in nuclear applications.
For nuclear structural materials used in advanced reactors, in addi -
tion to high-temperature strength and plasticity, resistance to high- 
temperature creep and low-temperature embrittlement are also among 
the most critical properties of concern [5,7,56] . However, research on 
these two mechanical properties of advanced alloys is still relatively 
limited. For advanced nuclear structural materials, in addition to 
focusing on the fundamental mechanical properties such as strength and 
plasticity of these alloys, future research should also address compre -
hensive mechanical performance aspects such as high-temperature 
creep and low-temperature embrittlement. Furthermore, studies on 
the microstructural mechanisms should explore how composition design 
optimization influences these other mechanical properties. In addition, 
due to the scarcity of neutron irradiation resources, the use of heavy-ion 
irradiation as a substitute for neutron irradiation to study material 
damage behavior has become a common research method. However, the 
effects of neutron irradiation and the complex radiation environment 
that materials face during reactor service must also be considered [57] . 
Beyond heavy-ion irradiation, it is important to investigate the impact of 
gas-ion irradiation, such as hydrogen and helium ion irradiation, on 
alloy materials. In the future, research should continue to explore the 
effects of synergistic heavy-ion and hydrogen-helium ion irradiation on 
the microstructure and mechanical properties of alloys.
