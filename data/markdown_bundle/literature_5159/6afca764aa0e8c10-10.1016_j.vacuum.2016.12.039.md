abstract
In the present work, the combination of mechanical alloying (MA) and spark plasma sintering (SPS) has
been utilized to improve the hardness and strength of the Fe-20Mn-11Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy dis-
playing good corrosion resistance. The mechanically alloyed powders consisted of a primary body-center
cubic (BCC) phase and a small amount of face-center cubic (FCC) phase, however, the sintered alloy was
composed of
g matrix, k-carbide ((Fe, Mn) 3AlCx)a n dC r 7C3 carbide. In addition, nanoscale twins pre-
sented in some g grains after SPS. Vickers hardness of the sintered Fe-20Mn-11Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy
with ultra-ﬁne grains (UFGs) is 705 HV, which is markedly higher than that of the as-cast Fe-20Mn-11Al-
1.8C-5Cr counterparts. The compressive yield strength of the sintered samples at room temperature (RT)
and at 873 K are 2339 MPa and 980 MPa, respectively, which are both signi ﬁcantly higher than those of
their as-cast counterparts, suggesting that the ultra- ﬁne grained Fe-20Mn-11Al-1.8C-5Cr alloy can be
potentially used in corrosion-wear conditions at elevated temperatures.

1. Introduction
Fe-Mn-Al-C quaternary alloys with high contents of Mn and Al
have been extensively studied for high temperature oxidation
resistance applications [1,2] and corrosion resistance applications
[3,4]. Fe-Mn-Al-C quaternary alloys also exhibit a satis ﬁed combi-
nation of high strength, fracture toughness and microstructural
stability over a wide range of temperatures [5e8], good ductility at
room temperature [9,10], low cost and easy accessibility to the
recycle system [7], and have been primarily developed for cavita-
tion erosion applications [1 1]. Typically, for the Fe-Mn-Al-C qua-
ternary alloys, Cr was found to be able to improve the corrosion
resistance [12e14],the high temperature oxidation resistance [15]
and the wear resistance [16]. Therefore, the Fe-Mn-Al-C-Cr qui-
nary alloy system has been considered as a more promising ma-
terial for corrosion-wear applications than Fe-Mn-Al-C quaternary
alloys and has been received considerable attentions [1 7].
Normally, high hardness and high strength are crucial to the
components used in corrosion-wear conditions especially at
elevated temperature because high hardness and strength can
avoid large plastic deformation and effectively support the tribo-
oxide layer. Therefore, the explored Fe-Mn-Al-C-Cr quinary alloys
with desirable chemical composition and microstructure should
have a combination of excellent corrosion resistance, high hard-
ness, and high strength, especially at elevated temperatures. In
general, alloying elements and processing routes have remarkable
inﬂuence on the phase composition, microstructure and mechan-
ical properties of alloys. Chen et al. [18] found that the micro-
structure within the a transforms from a þ B2 to a þ DO3 when x
value increases from 3.0 to 7.0 in Fe-9Al-30Mn-0.6C- xCr quinary
alloys, and the microstructure within the g transforms from g to
g þ k when x value increases from 0.6 to 1.2 in Fe-9Al-30Mn-xC-yCr
quinary alloys. Suto et al. [19] found that the hardness increases
from 431 HV to 500 HV when x value increases from 1 1 to 14 in Fe-
20Mn-xAl-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloys, and increases from 384 HV to
457 HV when x value increases from 1.0 to 1.8 in Fe-20Mn-13Al-xC-
5Cr quinary alloys. Li et al. [20] found that the addition of Al raises
the solvus of both inter- and intra-granular k phases signi ﬁcantly,
while the addition of Mn only causes slight reduction of the solvus
of both phases. Inspection of the literature [19,21e26] associated
with the effects of processing routes on the phase composition,
microstructure and mechanical properties of Fe-Mn-Al-C(-Cr) al-
loys reveals that the researchers are focusing on the casting fol-
lowed by various heat treatments. For instance, solution treatments

introducing precipitation hardening and/or work hardening. Cold

corrosion-wear applications at elevated temperatures because
hardness and strength of these Fe-Mn-Al-C(-Cr) alloys often
signiﬁcantly degenerate with increasing temperature.
Additionally, alloys can readily achieve signi ﬁcant enhance-
ments of the hardness and strength via reﬁning grains, especially
by introducing ultra- ﬁne grains or nanosized grains. Mechanical
alloying (MA) followed by consolidation is a widely used processing
route to acquire ultra- ﬁne grains or even nanosized grains with
good homogeneity [27]. In addition, spark plasma sintering (SPS)
can rapidly consolidate powders into bulk materials with ﬁne
grains and high density by applying pressure and passing electric
pulse current at relatively low sintering temperatures [28]. Hence,
this work explores the possibility of producing UFG Fe-Mn-Al-C-Cr
quinary alloys which could exhibit high hardness and high strength
using the MA process followed by SPS.
In view of the above ﬁndings, the objective of the present work
is to design and fabricate an Fe-Mn-Al-C-Cr quinary alloy with high
hardness and high strength, which could be potentially used as
corrosion-wear materials at elevated temperatures. Firstly, the Fe-
20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy possessing excellent compre-
hensive mechanical properties including hardness, tensile strength,
yield strength and elongation [19], was selected as the target alloy.
Subsequently, the target alloy was produced by the combination of
MA and SPS. Accordingly, microstructure and phase evolution of
the MA powders and the SPS sintered alloy were studied in detail.
Vickers hardness and compressive strength of the sintered alloy
were also investigated.
2. Experimental details
Elemental powders of Fe, Mn, Al and Cr with high purity of
99.9 wt% and particles size <45 mm, and C (graphite) with high
purity of 99.9 wt% and particles size < 600 mm were used as starting
materials. Elemental powders were ﬁrstly blended with the nom-
inal composition of Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr (wt.%). Then, 100 g of
the blend elemental powders and 1000 g of WC grinding balls of
5 mm in diameter were packed in a stainless steel vessel. A high
energy planetary ball mill (QM-3SP4) was used at 300 rpm, with an
ON time of 60 min followed by an OFF time of 10 min. Under an
argon atmosphere, the powders were wet milled for 5 h by adding
cyclohexane as process control agent (PCA) after 45 h of dry milling.
After being dried, the powders were passed through a 200
mm sieve
to eliminate larger particles which could affect the following
experiments.
Energy disperse spectroscopy (EDS) analysis shows that the 50 h
milled powders contains 62.02%Fe, 19.42%Mn, 10.59%Al, 3.24%C,
4.73%Cr (wt.%, EDS with a thick-window detector is limited to
detect the elements of atomic number of 1 1 or above, therefore,
carbon cannot be examined accurately by this method), which is
similar to that of the starting powder mixture. The sieved powder
of 16 g was placed in a cylindrical graphite die with an inner
diameter of 20.2 mm, and then sintered by SPS (Dr. Sinter 825,
Sumitomo Coal Mining Co. Ltd., Japan). The die temperature Td was
monitored using a pyrometer focused into a hole Ø 5 mm /C2 10 mm.
In order to reduce radiation losses, the die was covered by graphite
felt with a thickness of ~5 mm. To make sample extraction easier, a
graphite foil with a thickness of ~0.2 mm was used to cover the
inner surfaces of the die. The sintering schedule is as follows: the
mechanically alloyed powders were heated to 1323 K within
15 min, and then heated to 1373 K within 5 min before being
soaked for 5 min at 1373 K under a constant pressure of 50 MPa in
vacuum (residual cell pressure < 8 Pa).
Phase and microstructure evolution of the MA powders and
sintered samples were analyzed by a Bruker D8 ADVANCE X-ray
diffractometer (XRD) with a Cu Ka radiation, a scanning electron
microscopy (Phenom proX, Phenom-World B.V., Netherlands) us-
ing back scattered electron image (BSE) compositional mode, and a
scanning electron microscopy (Zeiss Supra 40, Carl Zeiss NTS
GmbH, Germany) using second electron image (SE) compositional
mode. Thin foil specimens were prepared by mechanical thinning
followed by ion milling at room temperature and was analyzed by a
transmission electron microscopy (TEM, TECNAI G2 S-TWIN F20,
FEI, USA) with selected area electron diffraction (SAED) analysis. To
investigate the reaction path for the formation of the ﬁnal bulk
alloy, thermal behavior of the powder mixture was measured by a
differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, STA-409, NETZSCH, Ger-
many) at a heating rate of 10 K/min from RT to 1523 K. The as-
heated powder was also analyzed by using XRD.
Specimens were cut from the sintered bulk alloys using elec-
trical discharge machining. Compression test specimens are cylin-
drical with the dimension of Ø3 mm /C2 4.5 mm. Vickers hardness
was measured on the polished surfaces of specimens with a load of
300 gf with duration of 10 s using a Digital Micro Hardness Tester
HVS-1000 Vickers hardness instrument. The reported hardness
value is an average of at least 10 measurements. Room temperature
compression tests were performed on an INSTRON 5569 testing
system at a constant strain rate of 1 /C2 10
/C0 3 s/C0 1 according to GB/T
7314-2005 [29], whereas compression tests at elevated tempera-
ture were performed on an INSTRON 5500R testing machine. Each
data point is an average of at least three samples.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. XRD analysis and microstructure during the MA process
Fig. 1 shows the XRD patterns of the mechanically alloyed
powders with different milling time. It is obvious that the initial
blended powders (0 h) include diffraction patterns of all the pure
elements. After 5 h of milling, drastic decrement of diffraction in-
tensity is observed. The C element peak disappeared completely
and diffraction peaks of Al element can hardly be observed, sug-
gesting that C and Al may have gradually dissolved into the BCC
structured Fe lattice. According to Chen et al. [30], rapid dissolution
of Al could be associated with its lower melting point than other
elements. In contrast, the early disappearance of diffraction peaks
of C may result from its smallest atomic radius in the alloy system,
attributing to that the small C atoms are more easily to occupy the
interstitial positions between the solvent or host atoms to form a
solid solution. After 15 h of milling, Al element peaks have dis-
appeared completely and diffraction peaks of Mn and Cr elements
can hardly be observed, which suggested that Mn and Cr may have
gradually dissolved. As milling time increases to 30 h, peak
broadening is more obvious and all the elemental peaks completely
disappear. Instead, main peaks corresponding to a BCC structure
and a FCC structure can be identi ﬁed, indicating that a primary BCC
supersaturated solid solution and a FCC supersaturated solid solu-
tion are formed. After 45 h of milling, the diffraction peaks exhibit
no evident change. However, as the milling time extends to 50 h
(5 h wet milling after 45 h dry milling), the diffraction peaks exhibit
a minor broadening and a evident increment of diffraction in-
tensity, which can be attributed to grain re ﬁnement and an incre-
ment in lattice strain [31].
Fig. 2 shows SEM images of the blended powders and me-
chanical alloyed powders of Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy
after various milling time. In Fig. 2a, the spherical particles were the
Al particles and the others were Fe, Mn, C and Cr particles with
irregular shape. After 5 h of milling ( Fig. 2 b), it is dif ﬁcult to
distinguish spherical Al particles from the other elements particles
while the particle surface were non uniform, which suggests that
both fracturing and welding operates at this milling time. As shown
in Fig. 2 c, further milling (15 h) leads to the particles showing
similar morphology, but displaying different particle sizes, After
30 h of milling ( Fig. 2 d), powder particles tend to show uniform
particle size. After 45 h of milling ( Fig. 2 e), powders have similar
morphology to those of the 30 h milled powders, but exhibiting an
evident increase in particle size, indicating the occurrence of
agglomeration. After an additional 5 h of wet milling ( Fig. 2 f, after
50 h of milling), an evident decrease in particle size occurred,
indicating that wet milling can certainly reduce particle size.
3.2. Phase evolution and microstructure after SPS
The XRD pattern of the Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy
after SPS at 1373 K is also shown in Fig. 1. To differentiate the initial
FCC phase during the MA process from the new FCC phase formed
during the SPS process, the new FCC phase is termed as
g. Evidently,
the alloy consolidated after SPS contains not only a g phase with
FCC structure, but also a k-carbide with a L1 2-type structure and a
Cr7C3 carbide with an orthorhombic crystal structure. However, the
50 h milled powders are composed of a primary BCC phase and a
small amount of FCC phase, suggesting that phase transformation
took place during SPS. Note that the diffraction peaks of the
consolidated alloy are a bit sharper than those of 50 h milled
powders, which is due to the grain growth and the decrease of
lattice strain during SPS.
Fig. 3 illustrates second electron (SE) and back-scattered
electron (BSE) images of the bulk Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary
alloy consolidated by SPS at 1373 K. As shown in Fig. 3 a, SE image
reveals that the microstructure of the alloy consists of two
distinctive areas, and a small amount of porosity can be seen. The
phase showing dark grey contrast and light grey contrast are typical
g þ k microstructure with a few microns in size. Clearly, the light
grey contrast phases are likely to be the main phase because of a
higher volume fraction. BSE images ( Fig. 3b and Fig. 3c) show that
some dark grey and black particles were formed within the light
grey matrix and grain boundaries. Most of these particles might be
numerous nanoscale phases, and a small minority of particles
might be porosity as seen in Fig. 3a. In summary, the bulk Fe-20Mn-
1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy consists of at least three phases, which
is consistent with the XRD results as shown in Fig. 1.
The bright-ﬁeld TEM image and corresponding SAED patterns of
the bulk Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy are shown in Fig. 4 .

due to the short soaking time during SPS. In order to con ﬁrm the
chemical composition of these phases, EDS/TEM was performed to

(marked as a-w). Results of the chemical composition analysis are

transition of FCC / g0 þ Cr7C3 occurring in the present study is a
common precipitation behavior which has ever been observed in
as-aged Fe-28.3Mn-8.7Al-1C-5.5Cr alloy [21] and Fe-30Mn-9Al-xC-

mol) is lower than that of Mn 7C3 (e(9.1 ± 1.3) kJ/mol) [34], ac-
counting for the precipitation of Cr 7C3 instead of Mn7C3. Taking the
formation mechanism of k-carbide into consideration, spinodal
reaction (g / g0 þ k) [7,20,32,35,36] and decomposition of meta-
stable austenite into ferrite and k-carbide ( g / a þ k) could occur
[19,37]. Obviously, k-carbide precipitate in the present work was
formed by spinodal reaction rather than decomposition, because no
ferrite was observed. It has been concluded that k-carbide precip-
itation requires the diffusion of both C and Al in austenite, and no k-
carbide precipitation can be observed if the chemical driving force
for the precipitation is insufﬁcient with a deﬁciency of either Al or C
[38]. Increasing Al contents can apparently increase the driving
force for the precipitation of inter- and intra-granular phases [20].
Moreover, the content of C in the k phase is higher than that in the g
matrix, suggesting that C content can also in ﬂuence the precipita-
tion of the k phase. As the ﬁne k carbides grew within the austenite
matrix, a heterogeneous precipitation started to occur on austenite
grain boundaries resulting in the typical k þ g/k microstructure
with coarse k-carbides on austenite grain boundaries and ﬁne k-
carbides within austenite matrix as shown in Fig. 3 . These results
are similar to that reported by Wang et al. in the Fe-30Mn-9Al-2C
alloy [32].
As the sintering temperature increases to 973 K, the diffraction
peaks are almost identical to those of the bulk alloy consolidated at
873 K. When being sintered at 1073 K, the diffraction peaks of Cr7C3
carbide exhibit no evident change, however, the relative intensities
of BCC decrease signiﬁcantly, whereas the relative intensities of the
k-carbide and g phase increase signiﬁcantly, indicating that BCC /
g / g0 þ k occurred. The BCC / g phase evolution between 973
and 1073 K could be attributed to the metastable state of the BCC
supersaturated solid solution formed after 50 h MA, as well as the
release of lattice strain at higher temperature. This phase trans-
formation during SPS is also associated with reordering due to the
annihilation of defects generated from serve plastic deformation
during MA [39]. When the sintering temperature increases to
1173 K, BCC phase has completely transformed into g phase.
Meanwhile, the relative intensities of k-carbide diffraction peaks
become stronger than those of k-carbide diffraction peaks at 1073K,
but weaker than those of k-carbide diffraction peaks at 1373 K,
suggesting that the precipitation of k-carbide is still proceeding
during sintering at 1 173e1373 K.
3.4. Mechanical properties
Fig. 9 shows the compressive stress-strain curves of the sintered
Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy at different temperatures. At
room temperature, the alloy underwent 5% plastic deformation
prior to fracture and compressive yield strength is 2681 MPa. The
mechanical properties of Fe-20Mn-1 1.5Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy
and some typical Fe-Mn-Al-C(-Cr) alloys are listed elaborately in
Table 2. The listed Fe-Mn-Al-C(-Cr) alloys are prepared by casting
(arc-melting) except the Fe-20Mn-1 1.5Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy
investigated in this work. Obviously, the bulk Fe-20Mn-1 1.5Al-1.8C-
5Cr quinary alloy fabricated by MA and SPS exhibits the highest
hardness and yield strength of the Fe-Mn-Al-C(-Cr) alloys listed in
Table 2 . The average Vickers hardness of the Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-
5Cr quinary alloy synthesized via MA and SPS is 705 HV, which is
markedly higher than those of the Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr casting
by any subsequent treatments reported by Sutou et al. [19]. The
compressive yield strength of the compact at room temperature is
2339 MPa, which is signi ﬁcantly higher than the as-cast Fe-25Mn-
15Al-10C with a yield strength of ~1 150 MPa[8]. As the temperature
increases up to 873 K, the compressive yield strength of the
compact decreases gradually from 2339 MPa to 980 MPa. However,
the alloy show excellent compressive plastic deformability, and it
kept unbroken after 50% plastic deformation, and 980 MPa is still
higher than the Fe-25Mn-15Al-10C casting (780 MPa) at 873 K.A t
1073 K, the compressive yield strength of the compact decreases
signiﬁcantly to 152 MPa. However, it still kept unbroken after 50%
plastic deformation. The high hardness and high strength of Fe-
20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy is primarily attributed to grain-
boundary strengthening of the ultra- ﬁned g grains as well as
precipitation-hardening associated with k-carbide and Cr 7C3 car-
bide. Hence, the bulk Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr alloy prepared by MA
and SPS exhibiting high Vickers hardness and high yield strength at
room temperature and at elevated temperatures is strongly
encouraged for further study in the corrosion-wear resistant.
4. Conclusions
High hardness and high strength Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr qui-
nary alloy with ultra- ﬁne grains has been successfully synthesized
by MA and SPS. A primary BCC structured supersaturated solid
solution and a minor FCC structured supersaturated solid solution
are evidently observed after MA. However, the decomposition re-
action FCC /
g0 þ k and FCC / g0 þ Cr7C3 took place at relatively
low temperature and then BCC / g / g0 þ k phase evolution took
place at higher temperatures during SPS. The EDS/TEM analysis
results conﬁrmed that the alloy consisted of a Mn-Fe-rich g phase, a
Mn-Al-C-rich k-carbide ((Fe, Mn)3AlCx), a Cr7C3 carbide, and a trace
amount of Al 2O3 phase. The high content of Al and C play a critical
role on the precipitation of k-carbide. The Vickers hardness of the
sintered Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr quinary alloy is 705 HV, which is
much higher than that of all the reported Fe-20Mn-1 1Al-1.8C-5Cr
