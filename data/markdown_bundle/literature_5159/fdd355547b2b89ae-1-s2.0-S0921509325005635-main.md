ABSTRACT
Ruthenium (Ru) is often added to Ti alloys to enhance corrosion resistance, and due to its inherently high 
hardness, dilute Ti-Ru alloys could have both high strength and excellent corrosion resistance, which are 
appealing properties for load-bearing biomedical applications. However, limited knowledge exists regarding the 
microstructures, mechanical properties, and deformation mechanisms of Ti-Ru alloys. In this study, a Ti-1.5Ru at. 
% alloy with a duplex microstructure consisting of α blocks embedded within a matrix containing α ′ martensites 
and retained β was prepared. Microstructural analyses confirmed that both α blocks and α ′ martensites main -
tained the classical Burgers orientation relationship with the retained β . Nanoindentation experiments demon -
strated higher hardness in the α blocks than in the matrix regions. Room-temperature tensile tests revealed 
excellent mechanical properties, including Young ’ s modulus of 98 GPa, yield strength of 987 MPa, ultimate 
tensile strength of 1237 MPa, and significant strain hardening, resulting in 5.7 % uniform elongation. The high 
strength of this Ti-1.5Ru alloy can be attributed to the refined duplex microstructure, while the dislocations in 
the α blocks, along with stress-induced α ′ and α ″ martensites in the retained β , contributed to the observed strong 
strain hardening. This study sheds light on the potential load-bearing biomedical applications of high-strength 
dilute Ti-Ru alloys, offering microstructural robustness and promising mechanical performance.
1. Introduction
Titanium (Ti) and its alloys have gained significant attention in the 
aerospace, chemical, and biomedical industries due to their high specific 
strengths and excellent corrosion resistance [ 1 ]. In biomedical applica -
tions, particularly for implants and prosthetics, desirable properties 
include good biocompatibility, high strength, low modulus, and 
enhanced corrosion and wear resistance. Currently, pure Ti and 
Ti-6Al-4V wt% alloy are the most commonly used Ti alloys for such 
applications, with Ti-6Al-4V favored for load-bearing implants like hip 
heads and femoral knees replacements due to its superior mechanical 
properties [ 2 , 3 ]. However, Ti-6Al-4V has lower corrosion resistance 
than pure Ti, which can lead to the release of V and Al ions [ 4 ], both of 
which are toxic and potentially harmful to the human body [ 5 ]. 
Therefore, developing high-strength Ti alloys with non-toxic elements 
and improved corrosion resistance is crucial for enhancing implant 
biocompatibility by decreasing both the host response and materials 
degradation in the body environment [ 6 ].
To address these challenges, researchers have explored various 
alloying elements to design novel Ti alloys and to modify microstruc -
tures and optimize the properties of commercial Ti and Ti alloys. 
Ruthenium (Ru), a platinum group metal, stands out due to its high 
hardness, melting point, abrasion resistance, corrosion resistance, and 
catalytic activity, rending it valuable in electronic, chemical, and 
biomedical fields [ 7 ]. Trace Ru additions to Ti and Ti alloys (including 
Ti-6Al-4V wt% alloy ) can greatly improve their corrosion resistance 
[ 8 – 12 ] via mechanisms like “ cathode modification ” and oxide film sta -
bilization [ 8 ]. Dilute Ti-Ru alloys, such as Ti-0.1Ru wt% [ 8 , 10 ] and 
Ti-(0.07 – 0.55) Ru wt% [ 12 ], have already shown improved corrosion 
resistance compared to pure Ti. Moreover, due to its high hardness (for 
instance, Young ’ s modulus is 447 GPa for Ru and 116 GPa for Ti [ 13 ]), 
Ru is commonly utilized as a hardener in many alloys [ 7 , 14 ]. Ti-Ru al -
loys have also shown significantly higher hardness than pure Ti [ 15 ]. 
The above suggest that dilute Ti-Ru alloys could achieve both enhanced

considered as biocompatible and non-toxic [ 5 ]. Studies have confirmed 
the excellent biocompatibility of Ru-containing alloys; for instance, a 
Zr-1Ru wt% alloy exhibited good cytocompatibility [ 16 ], while 
Ti-20Nb-(0.5, 1.0, 1.5)Ru at.% alloys significantly increased cell pro -

Ti-Ru alloys a promising alternative to Ti-6Al-4V for load-bearing

However, very limited research on the mechanical properties and 
deformation mechanisms of Ti-Ru alloys may hinder their processing 
and application. According to the Ru-Ti binary phase diagram [ 18 , 19 ], 
Ru acts as a β stabilizer by lowering the transformation temperature 
between the body-centered cubic (BCC) β and hexagonal-close packed 
(HCP) α phases in Ti alloys. Hence, dilute Ti-Ru alloys will likely have 
near α or α + β microstructures. The mechanical properties of these 
dual-phase Ti alloys are strongly influenced by the phase composition, 
proportion, and grain size of α and β phases [ 20 , 21 ]. In which the α 
phase plays an important role in ensuring the tensile ductility of these 
alloys by providing the mean free path for dislocation slip [ 21 – 23 ]. In 
addition to the plastic contribution from α , the slip resistance variation 
in the α / β interfaces results in the strengthening effect observed in 
dual-phase Ti alloys [ 24 ]. Meanwhile, higher cooling rates can refine the 
microstructures [ 22 , 23 , 25 – 27 ], and at sufficiently high rates, the β → α ’ 
(HCP) martensitic transformation occurs [ 22 , 23 , 26 , 28 ]. In general, α ′ 
martensites are characterized by high strength and low ductility; an 
increase in cooling rate typically leads to increased hardness [ 29 , 30 ] 
and strength [ 29 ] but reduced elongation due to the refined structure 
and formation of α ′ martensites in these Ti alloys [ 22 , 29 ].
The α and α ′ martensites favor plasticity and strength in Ti alloys, 
respectively. Therefore, a method has been proposed to maintain the α 
fraction and promote the formation of α ′ martensites to achieve both 
enhanced strength and large ductility in α + β dual-phase Ti alloys. 
Recent research on Ti alloys with α and β + α ′ duplex microstructure has 
shown a combination of high strength and ductility. This performance is 
attributed to multiple deformation mechanisms. For instance, reor -
ientation of α ′ martensites has been reported to enhance both strength 
and ductility in Ti-Al-Fe-Si duplex alloys [ 31 ]. In addition to the dislo -
cation activity in the α phase, stress-induced martensites (SIM) of the α ″ 
phase were observed in the retained β phase of Ti – 5Al – 7.5V wt% [ 32 ] 
and Ti-3Nb-13Ta-2Zr wt% alloys [ 33 ]. Similarly, a low-cost 
Ti-2.8Cr-4.5Zr-5.2Al wt% alloy with an α and β + α ′ duplex micro -
structure demonstrated remarkable mechanical properties due to the 
hierarchical coherent α ′ / β phase boundaries [ 34 – 36 ].
Given these insights, Ti-Ru alloys could achieve enhanced mechan -
ical properties through developing an α and β + α ′ duplex microstruc -
ture. In this study, a Ti-1.5Ru at. % (Ti-1.5Ru hereafter) alloy with a 
duplex microstructure of α and β + α ′ was prepared. The microstructures, 
mechanical properties, and deformation mechanisms of the Ti-1.5Ru 
alloy were systematically investigated. Considering the limited 
research on Ti-Ru alloys, the findings of this study are significant and 
can provide valuable insights into the processing and applications of Ti- 
Ru alloys.
2. Materials & Methods
The Ti-1.5Ru button ingot with a diameter of 50 mm and a height 25 
mm was arc-melted using 99.99 % pure Ti and 99.95 % pure Ru metals. 
The ingot was homogenized at 1373 K for 20 h in an Ar atmosphere and 
water-quenched, then hot-rolled at 1173 K to a final thickness of 10 mm 
and air-cooled. The rolled plate was cut into sheets after removing the 
oxide surfaces on a milling machine. These sheets were sealed in quartz 
tubes filled with Ar gas and solution-treated at 1173 K ( β region [ 18 , 19 ]) 
for 10 min, followed by water quenching to obtain the water-quenched 
(WQ) samples for the present study.
For tensile testing, plate-like samples with gauge dimensions of 3 ×
16 × 2 mm were cut using an electric discharge machine, and the 
surfaces were carefully grounded. Tensile tests were conducted on a 
universal tensile testing machine equipped with an extensometer. A

Obviously, the α blocks are harder than the surrounding matrix ( α ′ 
martensites and retained β ). The hardness differences likely stem from 
the intrinsic mechanical difference between the HCP- α and BCC- β pha -
ses, as observed in nanoindentation results on Ti-6Al-4V alloy [ 41 ]. 
Despite α ′ martensites are generally regarded as hard phase in Ti alloys, 
the relatively soft β phase lowers the overall matrix hardness. Addi -
tionally, the α ′ martensite itself may also be softer than the α blocks, as 
reported in a Ti-12Nb wt% alloy, where fully α ′ -martensitic samples 
exhibit a lower Young ’ s modulus than the α + β two-phase samples [ 23 ]. 
This is consistent with the fact that α phase is generally utilized to 
enhance the strength of the α + β two-phase Ti alloys compared to the 
single-phase β Ti alloys [ 42 ]. Thus, in the present Ti-Ru alloy, the micro 
α blocks function as hard phases within the transformed matrix.

3.3. Deformation mechanisms
The plastic deformation products of the Ti-1.5Ru alloy were exam -

tured sample, which comprises four sets of diffraction peaks. A 
comparison with the XRD peaks of the WQ sample shown in Fig. 1 (b) 
indicates that the peak corresponding to α -1 ( α blocks) remains the most 
prominent in the fractured sample, whereas the intensity of the peaks 
associated with α -2 ( α ′ martensites) increases significantly after defor -

difference in the XRD peaks obtained before and after deformation. 
Furthermore, a new diffraction peak originating from the orthorhombic 
α ″ martensites is observed. Thus, the XRD result of the fractured sample 
suggests that during plastic deformation, the α blocks undergo negligible 
variations, whereas the α ′ martensites grow, and new α ″ martensites 
emerge.
A representative nanoindentation load-depth curve from Fig. 5 (a and 
b) is shown in Fig. 7 (b), highlighting the pop-in phenomenon (i.e.,

3.4. Origin of high strength and strain hardening of Ti-1.5Ru alloy
The EBSD ( Fig. 2 ) and TEM ( Fig. 3 ) results present the ultrafine α +
( β + α ′ ) duplex microstructure, with microscale α , nanoscale α ′ and β . In 
addition to their intrinsic hard characteristics, the fine α blocks share 
both phase and chemical boundaries with the matrix ( β + α ′ ) areas, which 
can confine the plastic activity within both the α and matrix areas. 
Meanwhile, the ultrafine martensitic network isolates each β grain and 
results in dense α ′ / β phase boundaries, which hinders slip propagation 
from β to α ’ . Similar strengthening mechanisms were observed in a 
water-quenched Ti-2.8Cr-4.5Zr-5.2Al wt% [ 34 – 36 ] and a 
Ti-3Nb-13Ta-2Zr wt% alloy [ 33 ] with duplex microstructure. Therefore, 
higher stress is required to initiate macroscopic yielding due to the 
well-known Hall-Petch relationship between yield strength and grain 
sizes. In addition to the refined microstructure, solid-solution strength -
ening effect of Ru also plays a crucial role in enhancing the overall 
strength of this Ti-Ru alloy.
The XRD ( Fig. 7 ) and TEM ( Fig. 8 ) investigations clearly show that 
during the plastic deformation of the Ti-1.5Ru alloy, abundant disloca -
tions form within α blocks, facilitating plastic deformation in the hard α . 
The SIM α ′ and α ″ were produced from the β phase. The β→ α ′ and β→ α ″ 
martensitic transformations contribute to the so-called transformation- 
induced plasticity (TRIP) effects in the matrix ( β + α ′ ) region of this Ti- 
1.5Ru alloy. The abundant < a > - and < c > -type dislocations ensure the 
plastic deformation of α blocks, whereas the β→ α ′ and β→ α ″ martensitic 
transformations contribute to the TRIP effects in the retained β . This is 
consistent with the results of a Ti – 5Al – 7.5V wt% [ 32 ] and 
Ti-3Nb-13Ta-2Zr wt% alloy [ 33 ] with a similar duplex microstructure, 
which underwent β→ α ″ martensitic transformation. Collectively, the

4. Conclusions
In this study, a dilute Ti-1.5Ru alloy was prepared and subjected to 
comprehensive microstructural analyses and mechanical tests. The key 
findings and conclusions are summarized below. 
(1) The water-quenched Ti-1.5Ru alloy exhibits a duplex micro -
structure consisting of α blocks embedded within a matrix of α ′ 
martensites and retained β . TEM and EBSD analyses revealed that 
both α blocks and α ′ martensite maintain the classical Burgers 
orientation relationships with the retained β .
(2) Nanoindentation tests revealed that the α blocks are harder than 
the surrounding matrix regions. Room-temperature tensile 
testing showed that the Ti-1.5Ru alloy possesses Young ’ s 
modulus of 98 GPa, high yield strength (987 MPa) and ultimate 
tensile strength (1237 MPa), and strong strain hardening 
throughout a uniform elongation of 5.7 %.
(3) Detailed investigations into the deformed microstructures 
revealed the presence of < a > and < c > dislocations within the α 
blocks, indicating their plastic deformation capability. Addi -
tionally, stress-induced α ′ and α ″ martensites were observed. 
These contributed to significant strain hardening in the alloy.
(4) Overall, this study highlights the potential of the novel Ti-1.5Ru 
alloy for load-bearing biomedical applications, emphasizing its 
microstructural robustness, remarkable mechanical properties 
due to strong strain hardening.
