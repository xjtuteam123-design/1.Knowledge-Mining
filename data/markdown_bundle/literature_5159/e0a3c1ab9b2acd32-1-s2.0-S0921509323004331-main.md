ABSTRACT 
In the present work, a Ti – 48Al – 2Cr – 2Nb alloy prepared by selective electron beam melting (SEBM) and post- 
treated by forging at a small strain was investigated. The results show that the microstructure of the as-built 
TiAl alloys exhibited an alternating layer of coarse single γ grains and fine γ + α
2 
grains configuration along 
the building direction. After forging at a small strain, the fraction of the coarsely grained region decreases, and 
substantial fine sub-grains form in the fine-grained region. Additionally, fractions of the α
2 
phase and α
2
/ γ 
lamellar colonies increase. These factors lead to an increase in the tensile strength of alloys. Meanwhile, the low- 
angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) in fine sub-grains and the substantial mobile dislocations in coarse γ grains are 
favorable for improving the ductility of alloys. The formation of deformation twins in the γ grains facilitates the 
simultaneous improvement of strength and ductility. In addition, the anisotropy of tensile properties is reduced 
after small-strain forging, which can be attributed to the reduction in fraction of the coarse γ grains, and the 
increase in the fractions of the α
2 
phase and α
2
/ γ lamellar structure. Therefore, small-strain forging was verified 
as an effective post-treatment method for the improvement of tensile strength and ductility of SEBM prepared 
TiAl alloys at high temperatures. 
1. Introduction 
As an innovative high-temperature structural material, TiAl based 
alloys exhibit the advantages of lightweight, high specific strength, and 
good creep and oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures. These 
attractive properties make them the most promising candidates to 
replace the heavier nickel-based superalloys in aero-engines [ 1 – 3 ]. For 
example, the TiAl4822 alloy (Ti – 48Al – 2Nb – 2Cr, at. %) has been used as 
turbine blade material in commercial aircraft engines [ 4 , 5 ]. In the last 
decades, precision casting and hot isothermal forging were the two main 
methods for preparation of TiAl components [ 6 – 8 ]. In recent years, 
electron beam melting (EBM) has been considered as an advanced 
method for preparing brittle TiAl based materials [ 9 – 11 ]. 
Although additive manufacturing has shown significant advantages 
in component preparation, there exist inevitable defects, i.e., pores in 
the printed products that require further post-treatment. The most 
frequently used post-treatment is hot isostatic pressing (HIP), but the 
long duration at high temperatures during HIP can coarsen the grains 
and thus degrade the mechanical properties of the alloys [ 12 – 14 ]. 
Considering that forging can effectively refine the microstructures and 
improve the mechanical properties of TiAl alloys [ 15 , 16 ], a combined 
technique that samples after produced by additive manufacturing are 
then post-treated by forging, has attracted great research attention. 
Sizova et al. [ 17 ] found that after hot compression, the internal pores in 
the additive manufactured Ti-43.5Al – 4Nb – 1Mo-0.1B alloy almost dis -
appeared, and the microstructure was significantly refined, i.e., the 
grain size was about three times smaller than that after HIP. The study of 
Maurya et al. [ 18 ] showed that compared to the built Ti – 6Al – 4V

For small-strain deformation, the accumulation, entangle and slip of 
dislocations lead to the formation of dislocation walls (DWs). These DWs 
constitute sub-grain boundaries, forming a large number of fine sub- 
grains with an average size of 200 – 300 nm. Compared to HAGBs, 
LAGBs show better strain compatibility between grains, thereby 
improving the ductility of alloys. Besides, LAGBs provide ideal pathways 
for dislocations slip across boundaries and avoid stress concentration at 
grain boundaries [ 34 ]. 
Apart from the increase in the proportion of LAGBs, sufficiently high 
mobility of dislocations in γ grains also have a great influence on the 
tensile ductility of TiAl alloys. It is widely accepted that dislocation 
multiplication in pure metals and disordered alloys occurs primarily 
through the operation of Frank-Read or Bardeen-Herring sources. For 
TiAl alloys, multiplication of ordinary dislocations with the Burgers 
vector b = 1/2 < 110] is the most frequently observed dislocations in the 
γ phase. Fig. 13 (a) demonstrates substantial dislocations slip in a single γ 
grain under the zone axis of BF = [101]
γ
. Some dislocation lines were 
bent, dislocation interactions occurred, and dislocation jogs were 
formed, as shown in Fig. 13 (b). It means that the dislocations underwent 
cross-slip and climb during the small-strain forging process. In the 
process of high-temperature deformation, additional strain accommo -
dation is accomplished by climb of ordinary dislocations. The climbing 
process relax the requirement for independent slip systems, which is 
certainly beneficial for the ductility of TiAl alloys [ 35 ]. Moreover, 
multiplication of these dislocations can therefore take place through the 
stress-driven cross-slip or climb [ 36 ].

4.3. Fracture behavior of high-temperature tensile 
The microstructure of the as-built and forged TiAl alloys exhibited an 
alternating layer configuration along the building direction, i. e., layers 
consisting of coarse single γ grains and fine (majority γ phase + α
2 
phase) 
grains, respectively. Therefore, the different loading directions inevi -
tably lead to different crack initiation and propagation modes, which 
eventually determine the anisotropy of tensile properties [ 37 ]. For most 
engineering structural materials, microcracks readily nucleate from the 
interface of phases, grains or between matrix and precipitates [ 25 , 38 ]. 
Also, when the tensile direction is parallel to the building direction (the 
L direction), the interfaces between FG and CG regions provide potential 
nucleation sites of microcracks. Therefore, microcracks can easily con -
nect with each other and propagate, resulting in low strength and 
elongation in the L direction. However, in the T direction, the cracks are 
mainly generated and interconnected in the coarse-grained regions, thus 
resulting in a very tortuous crack path. In the forged specimens at a small 
strain, the α
2 
phase grows and forms α
2
/ γ lamellar colonies. The coarse γ 
grains are almost invisible near the fracture. During high-temperature 
tensile, the propagation of cracks is strongly blocked to the α
2 
phase 
and lamellar structure. Meanwhile, the random orientation of lamellar 
colonies will deflect these cracks more frequently [ 37 ]. Thereby the 
fracture resistance is considerably increased, which results in the 
weakened anisotropy of tensile properties after small-strain forging.
