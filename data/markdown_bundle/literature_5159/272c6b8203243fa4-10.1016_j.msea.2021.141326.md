ABSTRACT 
Metastable β -Ti alloys are attractive for additive manufacturing as they offer high strength and toughness, with 
the ability to readily tailor properties through control of the phase fraction and morphology of precipitate phases. 
However, during additive manufacturing processes thermal cycling associated with repetitive layer-by-layer 
deposition can potentially promote unintended β -phase decomposition and in-situ precipitation of other pha -
ses. This work investigates the stability of the β -phase during high heat input (1648 J/mm) Wire Arc Additive 
Manufacturing (WAAM) of Ti – 3Al – 8V – 6Cr – 4Mo – 4Zr (Beta-C). Thermocouples and pyrometers to first used to 
measure the thermal environment during WAAM, and SEM, XRD and TEM characterisation techniques are used 
to investigate the microstructure of the as-built alloy. Despite observing large thermal excursions during WAAM 
well above the temperatures necessary to precipitate α -phase, no evidence of β -phase decomposition was found 
during WAAM. A Time-Temperature-Transformation (TTT) diagram is used to show that the cooling rates are too 
fast and the cumulative thermal exposures are too short to decompose the β -phase during WAAM. Consequently, 
the alloy retains the bcc β -phase which gives the alloy moderate strength (tensile strength ~748 MPa) and good 
ductility (~20%) during WAAM. 
1. Introduction 
Metastable β -Ti alloys are promising candidates for structural ap -
plications due to their higher strength and improved combinations of 
toughness and fatigue resistance compared to α and α + β class Ti alloys 
[ 1 ]. However, wide-scale adoption of metastable β -Ti alloys is still 
limited owing to their higher cost (due to expensive alloying elements) 
and greater difficulties in processing these alloys via traditional means 
[ 1 – 4 ]. Additive Manufacturing (AM) promises to overcome these limi -
tations as higher material efficiency reduces net material costs (due to 
reduced waste AM processing expenses are largely alloy independent) 
[ 5 , 6 ] and the metastable β -Ti alloys are amenable to AM processing 
methods [ 7 , 8 ]. 
The majority of recent publications have been devoted to studying 
AM of α and α + β Ti alloys such as Ti – 6Al – 4V [ 9 , 10 ], with relatively little 
attention given to metastable β -Ti alloys fabricated by AM [ 7 , 8 , 11 – 15 ]. 
This may in part be due to the complicated thermal history of AM pro -
cesses in conjunction with the complex phase behaviours involving 
various equilibrium and metastable phases (e.g. β
′
, ω , α , α ") that form in 
metastable β -Ti alloys. For example, recent reports on an AM metastable 
β -Ti alloy revealed the co-existence of secondary α , ω and α " phases in 
the as-deposited state with unusual heterogeneous phase distributions 
and atypical phase morphologies which impacted mechanical and 
corrosion properties [ 16 , 17 ]. The metastable β -Ti alloys are sensitive to 
thermal conditions, such as cooling rate, ageing temperature and dwell 
time [ 18 , 19 ], which poses significant challenges to the control of 
microstructure and phase composition during AM. 
The purpose of this research is to demonstrate that a single β -phase 
state can be attained in an as-deposited metastable β -Ti alloy through 
appropriate alloy selection and knowledge of the thermal conditions 
during processing. A single β -phase state provides greater opportunity 
for post-deposition processing as it offers enhanced deformability and 
permits controlled microstructure tailoring through direct ageing heat 
treatments. To demonstrate the approach, the relatively low cooling rate

2. Materials and methods 
Beta-C Ø 1mm wire (composition determined by ICP-AES and LECO 
combustion was 3.89 wt% Al, 7.49 wt% V, 5.5 wt% Cr, 3.78 wt% Mo, 
3.53 wt% Zr, 0.07 wt% O, 0.02 wt% N) was deposited on Ti – 6Al – 4V 
substrate using a Gas Tungsten Arc Welder with a constant current of 
125 Amp, average voltage ~ 11V, wire feed rate of 1.5 m/min, depo -
sition speed of 50 mm/min and electrode-substrate distance of 5 mm 
under localised Argon gas (Purity: 99.999%) shielding at 20 L/min. 
Ti – 6Al – 4V substrate is widely available and previous work has shown 
that little chemical interaction occurs when used as a substrate for 
depositing Beta-C [ 21 ]. Although vaporisation of some elements in ti -
tanium alloys can occur during additive manufacturing processes [ 22 ], 
our previous work found that there is no significant change in chemistry 
for the primary alloy elements in Beta-C produced by WAAM compared 
to the original wire feedstock [ 21 , 23 ]. However, like other additive 
manufacturing processes, a slight pickup in oxygen typically up to 300 
ppm can occur in titanium produced by WAAM [ 24 ]. 
To understand the temperature cycling experienced during the 
WAAM process, a combination of pyrometers and embedded thermo -
couples were used during deposition in order to create an accurate re -
cord of temperature and duration. It was necessary to employ a non- 
contact pyrometer to measure the temperature in the top layer 
because the temperatures experienced during melting titanium exceed 
the melting point of conventional thermocouples. However, after several 
layers are established (solidified), traditional thermocouples can be 
embedded to collect additional temperature data as new layers are 
added. A non-contact IR pyrometer was used to measure the tempera -
ture of each layer along the centreline of the build during deposition 
(details about the pyrometer calibration and set up are available else -
where [ 21 ]). After the initial three layers were deposited, a Ø 1 mm hole 
was drilled into the side of the second layer having already experienced 
2 thermal cycles, i.e. one on deposition and one from reheating as a 
subsequent layer was deposited prior to the thermocouple insertion, at a 
distance 6 mm below the top surface (refer to Fig. 1 ) and a B-type 
thermocouple was embedded to measure the temperature at the cen -
treline of the build. Thermally conductive paste was used to ensure good 
thermal contact between the thermocouple and the component. Thus, 
real time temperature measurements were collected in the same X and Y 
reference planes but varying Z as each subsequent layer was deposited. It 
was not possible to embed a thermocouple in the layer immediately 
below the currently deposited layer because the temperatures experi -
enced were too high and caused the thermocouple to melt. A 9-layer 
high, single bead width wall was fabricated with approximate di -
mensions 70*14*34 mm (length (x)*width (y)* height (z)). The sample 
reference frame is given in Fig. 1 . 
Sample preparation for macro/micro-structure observation was 
performed using standard metallographic procedures. The grain size 
was measured using the linear intercept method [ 25 ]. Samples were 
examined using SEM (Hitachi SU3500) and phase constituents were 
characterised by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku Smartlab), with a 9 kW 
Cu rotating anode source, operated at 45 kV and 200 mA. TEM foils were 
prepared using the Focused Ion Beam (FIB) technique and examined on 
a Hitachi HT7700 TEM at 120 kV. A thin slice of the FIB specimen (either 
from Layer 1 or Layer 8) was firstly milled from the bulk with an ion 
beam current in the range of 3nA – 30nA at the acceleration voltage of 30 
kV. The post thinning was carried out at lower ion beam current (0.3 nA 
down to 30 pA) to avoid damaging each side of the FIB specimen or 
changing its microstructure [ 26 ], and the specimen was progressively

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Thermal cycling during the WAAM process 
Fig. 2 shows the typical thermal cycling experienced during WAAM 
for deposition of 7 consecutive layers measured via embedded thermo -
couple and the top layer measured by the pyrometer. It was not possible 
to measure the 2nd thermal cycle with the thermocouple due to its close 
proximity to the electric arc which caused melting of the thermocouple 
sheath. As expected, there is a progressive decrease in the peak tem -
perature as new layers are deposited with the peak temperature of each 
new layer approximately 10 – 17% lower than the last. There is potential 
for secondary phases (of α , β’ or ω ) to precipitate during prolonged 
exposure at temperatures below the β -transus. From Fig. 2 it is clear that 
the first 5 thermal cycles involve peak temperatures that exceed the 
β -transus (730 
◦
C) [ 19 ]. This could mean that any secondary phases that 
might occur during formation of the first 5 layers are potentially dis -
solved as the next cycle heats back into the β -transus. However, it is 
important to note that conditions are far from equilibrium and it is 
uncertain whether the diffusion kinetics are favourable for this to occur, 
given that the 5th layer only experiences ~30 s above the β -transus 
which is not very long for dissolution of metastable phases (if present). 
After 5 thermal cycles, the next three additional cycles (cycles 6 – 8) 
reach peak temperatures that are known to be effective for causing 
precipitation hardening of this alloy.

3.3. Mechanical properties 
Hardness testing revealed no significant variation across the layers 
( Fig. 4 a). In general, a substantial hardness increase is ascribed to 
α -phase precipitation upon ageing of β -Ti alloys. Metastable phases (e.g. 
ω -phase, nanoscale α -phase) may contribute around a 15 – 90% increase 
in hardness compared to the solution-treated or quenched conditions 
[ 38 , 39 ]. Previous work has shown that Beta-C produced by WAAM 
markedly hardens with α -precipitation from ageing [ 23 ]. The average 
hardness of the top layer and layer 8 are 301.5 HV and 300.5 HV, 
respectively. This further supports the microstructural analysis that 
as-built Beta-C is thermally stable during the WAAM processing and 
retains a single β -Ti phase structure. The β -phase gives the alloy very 
good tensile ductility (exceeding 20%) and moderate strength of 739 ±
17 MPa ( Fig. 4 b). In single phase β -alloys, Hall-Petch strengthening is 
important and the strength level achieved here is lower than wrought 
Beta-C on account of the difference in grain size (in this case average ~ 
745 μ m vs typical recrystallised wrought grain size of ~60 μ m). Schmidt 
et al. [ 40 ] reports that wrought Beta-C (in the solution treated condition 
with a grain size of 62 μ m) typically has a UTS of 794 MPa and elon -
gation of 25%. Hence it is particularly important to control the grain size 
during additive manufacturing of single β -Ti phase alloys. Madikizela 
et al. recently demonstrated that Beta-C produced by high cooling rate 
SLM, which produced a columnar grains approximately 20 μ m in length 
and 4 – 10 μ m in width, achieves higher strength levels (UTS 845.1 ± 1.0 
MPa; Elongation: 30.5 ± 3) [ 41 ]. 
3.4. Phase stability of Beta-C during additive manufacturing 
The absence of phase transformation of Beta-C during the WAAM 
process can be understood from the perspective of the Time- 
Temperature-Transformation (TTT) diagram for the Beta-C alloy (as 
shown in Fig. 5 , redrawn from Ref. [ 42 ]). The cumulative thermal 
exposure time relative to the specific heat exposure for layer 8 is also 
indicated in Fig. 5 which follows an approximate thermal sequence 
identified by the points a-d. Referring to the TTT diagram, the closest 
point for WAAM Beta-C to follow the decomposition sequence β → β + β
′
→ β + β
′
+ α → β + α is the point ‘a ’ , at a temperature of 550 
◦
C. For this 
decomposition to occur, the temperature within the layer must be 
maintained for almost 7 min (408s). Similarly, at the lower temperature 
of 450 
◦
C, β decomposition is expected to occur only beyond 14 min 
(840s) exposure. The thermocouple measurements show that while 
these temperatures are achieved during the thermal cycling associated 
with build-up of new layers, the exposure time is too short for 
β -decomposition to occur in practice during WAAM. Therefore, despite 
all layers undergoing thermal cycling during WAAM, the dynamic low 
temperature intrinsic heat-treatment (thermal exposure with each new 
layer) was insufficient to reach the critical ageing time necessary for 
β -decomposition into secondary phases. 
It is worth mentioning that metastable β -Ti alloys are a wide class of 
alloys but by definition require that no α
′
, martensite or α precipitation 
forms during quenching from the β -phase field [ 42 ]. Beta-C has suffi -
cient β -phase stability to depress α
′
formation, and the incorporation of 
sluggish diffusing elements (e.g. V, Mo, Cr) assist to restrict α precipi -
tation by retarding precipitation kinetics. The high β -stability alloys 
have excellent potential for additive manufacturing. Other metastable 
β -Ti alloys which have higher MoE values than Beta-C (such as 
Ti – 12Mo – 6Zr – 2Fe and Ti – 13V – 11Cr – 3Al) are similarly expected to be 
insensitive to thermal cycling conditions during WAAM processing. The 
WAAM process studied here has higher heat input (1648 J/mm) and 
longer heat exposure time than other AM processes. In turn, the higher 
cooling rate AM processes (such as SLM or LENS) should also provide a 
production pathway for processing such β -Ti alloys without in-situ phase 
decomposition (higher cooling rates will inevitably pass the β + β’ nose, 
further left on the TTT diagram in Fig. 5 compared to that indicated for 
WAAM). However, alloys with lower content of slower diffusing ele -
ments may be susceptible to β -phase decomposition as the noses of the 
relative curves on the TTT diagram will shift to the left, and this requires 
ongoing investigation. The effect of preheated build platforms, espe -
cially in electron beam powder bed fusion (EB-PBF) AM processes may 
also promote β -phase decomposition. The production of other meta -
stable alloys with lower MoE, such as Ti – 24Nb – 4Zr – 8Sn (MoE ~ 6.7) 
was found to consist of a single β -phase when produced by SLM (due to 
the high cooling rates and low build platform temperature) but the same 
alloy produced by EB-PBF with a heated build platform caused α -phase 
nucleation in the as-fabricated part [ 11 , 12 , 43 – 45 ]. 
4. Conclusions 
The stability of the β -phase in a metastable β -Ti alloy 
(Ti – 3Al – 8V – 6Cr – 4Mo – 4Zr, also known as Beta-C) was studied under a 
high heat input (1648 J/mm) Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing 
(WAAM) process. The thermal history within a layer was measured as 
multiple new layers were deposited. Despite experiencing repeated 
thermal cycling at temperatures sufficient for precipitation of α and/or 
other metastable phases ( ω or β’ ), no evidence of β -phase decomposition
