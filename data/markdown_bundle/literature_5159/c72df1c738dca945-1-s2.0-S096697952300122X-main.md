1. Introduction 
Refractory high-entropy alloy (RHEA) usually contains refractory 
metals such as Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta, Cr, Mo and W. These metals have 
high melting points and good mechanical properties at high tempera -
tures, forming RHEA with the main phase BCC also owns high melting 
points and good mechanical properties [ 1 – 6 ]. There are also elements 
containing Fe, Co and Ni to form high-entropy alloys with FCC as the 
main phase, which also present good properties at high temperatures 
[ 7 – 10 ]. RHEA are considered as potential new generation materials for 
high temperature applications such as aero engines, nuclear reactors and 
gas turbines. The phase stability of RHEA at high temperatures is 
important for applications at high temperatures, and RHEA with a single 
phase in the as-cast state will have a second phase precipitated after heat 
treatment to change the properties of the alloy [ 11 – 15 ], and some will 
undergo an order-disorder transformation to generate an ordered B2 
phase [ 16 , 17 ], and the most of RHEA will continue to maintain a simple 
phase structure in the as-cast state. The study of RHEA thermal stability 
and kinetics of the generated phases can control the microstructure and 
phases of RHEA which provide desirable mechanical properties under 
the range of intended using temperature, thus enabling 
application-driven performance optimization [ 18 , 19 ]. 
Compared to conventional refractory alloys, the oxidation rate of 
RHEAs could reduce significantly and the high degree of alloying slow 
down the oxygen diffusion rate. Moreover, Al, Cr, etc. such elements that 
RHEAs contained form protective oxide films to inhibit the oxidation of 
other refractory elements [ 20 – 22 ]. The oxidation kinetics of the RHEA
