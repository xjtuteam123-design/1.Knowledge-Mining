Abstract
A Ti–5.4Al–6.4Zr–6.2Sn–0.4Mo–1.6W–0.4Nb–3.2Ta–0.5Si alloy is designed following cluster formula approach that 
achieves a strength level of 1 GPa at 600 ℃ in the as-cast state, superior to any existing high-temperature Ti alloys. Its com-
position is formulated by 17 basic units, α-{[Al-Ti12](AlTi2)}12 + β-{[Al-Ti12Zr2](Mo0.125Nb0.125Ta0.5W0.25Sn1.5Si0.5)}5, each 
unit covering a nearest-neighbor cluster plus a few next-neighbor glue atoms. This design is on the basis of the composition 
formula of Ti65 alloy, with an enhanced β stability via more Zr, Mo, Nb, Ta, W, Sn, and Si co-alloying. Upon copper-mold 
pour casting, this alloy shows a good microstructure stability. In tensile testing below at 650  ℃, its α plates thickness is 
nearly at the same level of 0.2 μm, which is much smaller than 0.7–0.8 μm of Ti65 at the same condition. The changes in 
volume fraction of β phase are increased by 86%, much less than by 105% in Ti65. Its room-temperature strength reaches the 
ultra-high-strength level, with an ultimate tensile strength of 1328 MPa and a yield strength of 1117 MPa, with a moderate 
elongation of 3.8%. At 600 ℃, its ultimate tensile strength of 1017 MPa and yield strength of 936 MPa are superior to those 
of any existing high-temperature Ti alloys, with an elongation of 7.2%. At 650  ℃, its ultimate tensile strength of 848 MPa 
still maintains a high level.

1 Introduction
High-temperature titanium alloys have the comparative 
advantages of low density, good corrosion resistance, and 
high specific strength, making them desirable for aero-
space industries, such as compressor blades, blade disks, 
and engine casings [1 –4]. In a stringent environment, the 
high-temperature Ti alloys are beneficial to improve the 
thrust-to-weight ratio of the engine and the maneuverability 
of the aircraft [5]. After nearly 70 years of extensive inves-
tigations, their service temperatures have been greatly 
improved from 300 to 600  ℃ [2, 6–10]. Predominantly, 
Ti–Al–Sn–Zr–Mo–Si near-α system is considered suitable 
for 600  ℃ service, as represented by BT36 [6 ], Ti6242S 
[7], MI834 [ 8], Ti1100 [ 9], and Ti600 [10 ]. Their high-
temperature strengths reach 850 MPa at 600  ℃. Extensive 
efforts have been devoted to increasing the service tempera-
ture above 600  ℃ [11–13] via multi-element alloying, as 
marked by near-α Ti65 [11] containing many β -stabilizers 
such as Ta, W, Mo and Nb, and by near-α 7715D [13] fea-
turing rare-earth Ce alloying. However, the major draw -
backs of these multi-element Ti alloys are their insufficient 
tensile strengths, usually less than 800 MPa at 650  ℃, and 
less than 1200 MPa at room-temperature, plus poor elon -
gations of 2–5%. To further improve the strengths at high 
temperatures, impurity control [14], minor alloying [15], 
rolling and forging processes [16, 17], and heat treatment 
[18] are widely conducted. The basket-weave microstruc-
ture, consisting of lamellar α and remnant β, is preferred for 
high-temperature titanium alloys [1 , 10, 13], in view of its 
balanced performances, such as excellent fracture toughness

and creep resistance. In fact, all popular high-temperature 
Ti alloys feature basket-weave microstructure containing 
two solid-solution phases, hexagonal close-packed-based α 
phases as the matrix and a small amount (2–10 vol.%) of 
body-centered-cubic-based β as the reinforced phases [10]. 
The key to the microstructure stability of high-temperature 
Ti alloys lies in controlling the β phase stabilities. This is 
because the β phases have higher self-diffusion coefficients 
than α-phases and tend to form brittle compounds under 
long-time heating conditions [11, 19]. Precisely controlling 
the β phase stability and its content decides the performance 
of high-temperature Ti alloys.
To design such an alloy with good β phase stability and 
appropriate content, the key issue is to find the composi-
tion origin of Ti alloys. Our team has developed a so-called 
cluster-plus-glue-atom model [ 20, 21], a new structural 
tool stressing the importance of nearest- and next-neighbor 
ordering. A solid solution can be expressed in the cluster 
formula as [cluster](glue atoms), consisting of a nearest-
neighbor cluster plus a few next-neighbor glue atoms. This 
unit could be used to precisely control the phases stability 
and their content, and is also recognized as the composition 
gene of the material [22]. Up to now, the cluster formula 
approach has been proved effectively useful in designing 
many industrial alloys [23, 24]. In a previous work [25], the 
composition of widely used α + β dual-phase Ti–6Al–4V is 
interpreted as α-{[Al-Ti12](AlTi2)}12 + β-{[Al-Ti14](V2Ti)}5, 
consisting of twelve α-Ti units and five β-Ti ones, based on 
the measured phase compositions [26, 27]. The details are 
presented in the following section. This formula provides 
the basis for the composition optimization of various Ti 
alloys: by varying the ratio of the α and β units and alloy -
ing them separately, different Ti alloys can be formulated, 
which offers great potential for developing high-perfor -
mance Ti alloys. Then, various high-strength Ti alloys [28] 
and high-temperature Ti alloys [29, 30] have been explored 
on the basis of Ti–6Al–4V composition formula. Ti65 is a 
newly developed high-temperature near-α Ti alloy, mainly 
for use at 600–650  ℃, with the β phase transition point of 
(1050 ± 15) ℃, exhibiting a balanced high-temperature per-
formances, but its β phases is readily decomposed [11, 17, 
31]. To achieve an excellent high-temperature performance, 
the anticipated Ti alloy should maintain the composition 
formula of Ti65 composed of twelve α-Ti units and five β-Ti 
ones; its β part should also be stabilized via multi-element 
co-alloying within the framework of the T65 formula.
In the following, the design of an ultra-strong high-
temperature Ti–5.4Al–6.4Zr–6.2Sn–0.4Mo–1.6W–0.
4Nb–3.2Ta–0.5Si alloy, based on composition formula of 
Ti65, is first detailed. In the as-cast state, its tensile strengths 
are superior to conventional wrought-state high-temperature 
Ti alloys such as Ti65, Ti1100, and IMI834, achieving an 
ultra-high-strength level of 1300 MPa at room temperature 
and 1 GPa at 600 ℃.
2 Composition Design Using the Cluster 
Formula Approach
2.1 Cluster‑plus‑glue‑atom Model
Common industrial alloys are mostly based on solid 
solutions. The problem for the alloy design is especially 
complicated by solid solutions presenting chemical short-
range orders [ 32, 33]. Therefore, the accurate modeling 
of chemical short-range ordering in solid solution is the 
prerequisite for alloy composition design. Dong et al. [ 20, 
21] proposed a cluster-plus-glue-atom model to describe 
the short-range-order structures. This model identifies a 
structural unit as the composition carrier of the material, 
which covers a nearest-neighbor cluster plus a few next-
neighbor glue atoms. This chemical unit is then formulated 
as [cluster] (glue atoms), where the square-bracketed part 
[cluster] refers specifically to the nearest-neighbor coor -
dination polyhedron and is matched with the parenthesis 
part (glue atoms) positioned at the next-neighbor sites.
The existence of such a cluster-plus-glue-atom struc -
tural unit was theoretically proven using Friedel oscil-
lations theorem [21, 22, 29]. This structure unit is also 
regarded as the composition gene of the material [22].

enters the center site of the β formula. The atomic occupa-
tions in the cluster formula are mainly determined by the 
enthalpy of mixing (∆H ) between the solute and solvent 
Ti elements [25]. Al shows strong interactions with Ti, 
ΔHTi-Al = −30 kJ/mol [ 34], and enters the center site of 
the units. Zr is Ti-like a weak β -stabilizer and shows a 
zero enthalpy of mixing with Ti, and replaces Ti in the 
shell position in the β -Ti unit. Mo, Nb, W, and Ta are 
strong β-stabilizers and show weak interactions with Ti 
(ΔHTi-Mo = −4 kJ/mol, ΔHTi-Nb = 2 kJ/mol, ΔHTi-W = −6 kJ/
mol, ΔHTi-Ta = 1 kJ/mol). They are then placed at the glue 
sites in the β -Ti unit. Sn and Si are weak β -stabilizers and 
show smaller atomic radii than solvent Ti, and placed at 
the glue sites in the β-Ti unit. Furthermore, the α composi-
tion in integer form is idealized as [Al-Ti 12](AlTi2) in the 
cluster formula on the basis of the measured phases com-
position of Ti–6Al–4V [25], for its composition is nearly 
independent of the annealing temperature [26, 27]. In the 
α formula, the positions of glue atoms only contain Al and 
Ti. Thus, those β stabilizers, such as Mo, Nb, W, Ta, Sn, 
and Si, are do not arranged in the positions of glue atoms 
in the α phase.
Among them, Ti–6Al–4V, Ti65, IMI834, Ti1100, TA29, 
and TA33 are characterized by more β units, being 5, rela-
tive to 2–4 of TA15, Ti-6242S, TA35, IMI685, and BT8. 
Though 7715D, Ti750, BT36, BT25, and BT25Y have fewer 
β units, they contain more β stabilizers. It is then anticipated 
that high-temperature strengths can be achieved via proper 
matching of the number and the stability of the β units.

First, the α and β units still satisfy the same ratio of 12:5 
as Ti65 and the widely used Ti–6Al–4V to ensure a good 
plasticity. As shown in Table 1, all typical high-temperature 
Ti alloys contain a certain β units (2–6) for a sufficient plas-
ticity. To achieve a good high-temperature performance, the 
targeted alloy should be near-α type, mainly in a limited β 
units (seems the maximum number of 5) in favor of a good 
high-temperature strength.
Second, the initial β -unit [Al-Ti 13.03Zr0.97](Mo0.08Si0.4
Nb0.1Ta0.32W0.14Sn0.96Ti1) is transformed to [Al-Ti 12Zr2]
(Mo0.125Nb0.125Ta0.5W0.25Sn1.5Si0.5) via more Zr, Mo, Nb, 
Ta, W, Sn, and Si co-alloying to improve the β structural 
stability just like typical high-temperature Ti alloys BT36, 
BT25, and BT25Y, and the maximum β stability corre-
sponds to the full occupation of the three glue sites. The 
content of those β elements in the β -Ti unit is arranged on 
the basis of the following considerations. Sn and Si have the 
same valance electron concentration (VEC) of 4 and belong 
to the same category. Similar to Ti65 alloy, the total content 
of Sn and Si in β-Ti unit is set to 2, where the integer atomic 
number is 0.5 for Si and 1.5 for Sn. Ta is conducive to the 
improvement of high-temperature stability of Ti alloys, 
and thus its content increased from 0.32 to 0.5. With refer -
ence to the classical high-temperature IMI834 alloy, both 
the content of Mo and Nb is 0.125, in an equimolar ratio 
to keep the maximum entropy of mixing. W occupies the 
remaining glue atom sites with the number of 0.25. When 
Zr content is 2 in the β -Ti units [35], the Ti–Al–V–Zr qua-
ternary alloy shows balanced tensile strength. Therefore, the 
Zr content for the current resultant alloy is increased from 
0.97 to 2. The resultant composition formula is α-{[Al-Ti12]

alloy were divided into coarser α grain (length  ˃ 30 μm)

improve the mechanical performances of Ti5666.

5 Conclusions
A high-temperature Ti5666 (Ti–5.4Al–6.4Zr–6.2Sn–0.4M
o–1.6W–0.4Nb–3.2Ta–0.5Si, wt%) alloy is designed fol-
lowing the cluster formula α-{[Al-Ti12](AlTi2)}12 + β-{[Al-
Ti12Zr2](Mo0.125Nb0.125Ta0.5W0.25Sn1.5Si0.5)}5 and prepared 
by vacuum copper-mold arc melting. Its composition is 
modified from that of popular Ti65 with enhanced β sta-
bility via more Zr, Mo, Nb, Ta, W, Sn and Si co-alloying. 
Upon copper-mold pour casting, this alloy shows a good 
microstructure stability. In tensile testing below at 650  ℃, 
its α plates thickness is nearly at the same level of 0.2 μm, 
which is much smaller than 0.7–0.8 μm of Ti65 at the same 
condition. The changes in volume fraction of β phase are 
increased by 86%, much less than by 105% in Ti65. Its 
room-temperature strength reaches the ultra-high-strength 
level, with an UTS of 1328 MPa and a YS of 1117 MPa, 
with a moderate elongation of 3.8%. At 600  ℃, its UTS of 
1017 MPa and YS of 936 MPa is superior to those of any 
existing high-temperature Ti alloys, with an elongation of 
7.2%. At 650  ℃, its UTS of 848 MPa and YS of 812 MPa 
still maintains a high level.
