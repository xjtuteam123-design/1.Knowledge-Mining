1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are considered as promising structural 
materials with great potential for applications in space, aerospace and 
nuclear industries due to their high strength, good ductility, exceptional 
cryogenic toughness and excellent corrosion resistance [ 1 – 6 ]. However, 
the generally inferior high-temperature mechanical performance 
(especially tensile properties) has become the Achilles ’ heel of HEAs 
[ 7 – 9 ], hindering their application towards hostile environments. Among 
various HEAs, face-centred cubic (FCC) HEAs show relatively low 
strengths even at ambient temperatures and thus are not qualified for 
high-temperature application [ 7,8 ]. The rapidly reduced lattice friction 
stress with increased temperature causes pronounced decrease in 
strength for this type of HEAs [ 7 ]. Twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) 
and transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) have been recently intro -
duced in HEAs through alloy design and were found to be beneficial to 
the strength-ductility synergy of the Fe-Mn-Co-Cr-based HEAs at 
ambient and lower temperatures [ 10 – 12 ]. However, the TWIP 
mechanism becomes ineffective at elevated temperatures due to 
increased dynamic recovery, reduced work hardening and the suppres -
sion of deformation twinning [ 13 ]. Strengthening via martensitic 
transformation for the TRIP HEAs would be curtailed once the temper -
ature exceeds the martensite start temperature near 600 K [ 13 ]. Eutectic 
HEAs tend to exhibit ultrahigh strengths at low temperatures but their 
strength will be significantly compromised at elevated temperatures due 
to softening of body-centred cubic (BCC) or B2 lamellae and FCC/B2 
phase boundaries [ 14 – 16 ]. The microstructural instability constitutes 
another barrier for acquisition of good high-temperature mechanical 
properties for a number of HEAs [ 17 – 20 ]. BCC HEAs, especially those 
refractory BCC HEAs demonstrate good high-temperature compression 
properties but their tensile properties are rather poor [ 21-25 ]. To 
enhance the high-temperature mechanical properties of HEAs, recently, 
Jia et al. [ 16 ] employed B microalloying to strengthen the FCC/B2 phase 
boundaries of a eutectic HEA, which considerably enhances the tensile 
strengths at 700

While alloy design and compositional modification may improve the 
high-temperature mechanical properties of HEAs, advanced 
manufacturing technologies such as additive manufacturing (AM) may 
be also promising in enhancing the mechanical properties of HEAs at 
both ambient and elevated temperatures due to its unique processing 
characteristics. The extremely rapid cooling and solidification rates 
upon AM can significantly refine microstructure of various HEAs that 
are beneficial to their mechanical properties. Moreover, with AM, a 
number of novel microstructural features can be developed in different 
HEAs, leading to pronounced strengthening effects. For example, 
numerous nano-sized cellular structures and high-density dislocations 
were formed in AM-processed FCC HEAs and precipitate-strengthened 
HEAs [ 28 – 31 ], which substantially enhances the tensile properties of 
these alloys at ambient or lower temperatures. Nanoscale eutectic 
lamellar structure was produced in eutectic HEAs through AM [ 14 , 32, 
33 ], which significantly elevates the tensile properties at room tem -
perature. Dislocation-precipitate skeleton architecture was created in an 
AM-processed precipitate-strengthened HEA, leading to acquisition of 
an ultra-high tensile strength of ~ 1.8 GPa together with the maximum 
elongation (EL) of ~ 16 % [ 30 ]. Strip-shaped L1

number of other AM-processed precipitate-strengthened HEAs [ 35 – 39 ]. 
However, reports on the high-temperature mechanical behaviour of 
AM-processed HEAs are relatively lacking. How the microstructure

• The l-PBF processed (CoCrNi)
94
(TiAl)
6

and numerous ultrafine cellular structures with cell boundaries 
decorated by Al

◦

◦

• Ageing at or above 600 
◦

the sizes of the precipitates increasing constantly with increased 
temperature but the area fraction increases first to the highest level 
at 800 
◦

◦

• Ageing treatment at or above 650 
◦
C results in the formation of σ 
precipitates along grain boundaries, the area fraction of which in -
creases continuously with increased ageing temperature.
• The as-printed samples exhibit high yield strength and excellent 
ductility at room temperature. Ageing treatment significantly en -
hances the yield strength and ultimate tensile strength.
• The samples containing cellular structures and coarsened γ ′ (aged at 
700 
◦

and display unprecedented tensile strengths at high temperatures 
due to the γ ′ strengthening and the cell boundaries decorated by 
Al
2
O
3 
nanoparticles which have acted as strong barriers for dislo -
cation motion.
• At room temperature, the sample deforms mainly by dislocation slip 
and formation of stacking faults and at high temperatures, the 
deformation becomes increasingly planar as a higher density of 
stacking faults form while twinning has been activated.
