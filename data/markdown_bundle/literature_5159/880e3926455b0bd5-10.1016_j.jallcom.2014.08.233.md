abstract
Here, we investigate a new type of kink structure that is found at c/c0 interfaces in nickel-based single
crystal superalloys. We studied these structures at the atomic and elemental level using aberration cor-
rected high-angle annular dark-ﬁeld scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). The
core of the dislocation absorbs high-Z elements (i.e., Co and Re) that adopt hexagonal arrangements,
and it extrudes elements (i.e., Ni and Al) that adopt face centered cubic (fcc) structures. High-Z elements
(i.e., Ta and W) and Cr, which is a low-Z element, are stabilized in body centered cubic (bcc) arrange-
ments; Cr tends to behave like Re. High-Z elements, which migrate and adopt a hexagonal structure,
induce kink formation at
c/c0 interfaces. This process must be analyzed to fully understand the kinetics
and dynamics of creep in nickel-based single crystal superalloys.

1. Introduction
Superalloys containing a mixture of c and c0 phases exhibit a
maximum creep rupture life when they are comprised of /C24 65%
c0. At high temperatures and under low stress, such alloys were
determined to have yield strengths of 0.2% [1,2]. These properties
make nickel-based superalloys ideal materials for a variety of
applications, including the construction of gas turbine blades in
the aerospace industry [3]. High melting point elements, such as
Ta, W, Mo and Re, are often added to solid solutions of such alloys
to strengthen the c matrix. However, when nickel-based superal-
loys are composed of excessive amounts of such refractory ele-
ments, instabilities are promoted. These instabilities can lead to
the formation of deleterious topologically close-packed (TCP)
phases when the superalloy is exposed to elevated temperatures
[4–8]. Furthermore, these TCP phases can serve as damage accu-
mulation sites that initiate or accelerate crack propagation, which
ultimately cause the creep strength of the material to decrease
[9,10].
The c/c0 interfacial structure, which is affected by lattice misﬁt
and coherent strain, plays a signiﬁcant role in determining the
mechanical properties of this class of alloys. Many studies have
attempted to elucidate the interfacial structure of nickel-based
superalloys, especially their elemental distributions, which are
generally quite pronounced [11–15]. When these c/c0 interfaces
are enriched with heavy elements, their displacement is retarded
during creep. To the best of our knowledge, there have been only
a few detailed reports describing the ﬁne structure of c/c0 inter-
faces in nickel-based single crystal superalloys.
In this letter, we investigate how and why kink structures form
at c/c0 interfaces in nickel-based single crystal superalloys during
creep. The structure and elemental composition of the kink struc-
tures were analyzed using an aberration corrected high-angle
annular detector dark-ﬁeld scanning transmission electron micro-
scope (HAADF-STEM) coupled to an energy dispersive X-ray (EDX)
spectrometer.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Composition characterization of the c/c0 interfaces in crept nickel-
based superalloy
Fig. 1(a) shows a HAADF microscopy image of the c/c0 interfaces
in a nickel-based superalloy after a creep test was performed. The
alloying elements segregate differently in the c and c0 phases, lead-
ing to the varying contrast observed on either side of the interface.
Fig. 1 (b)–(h) are elemental maps of the region shown in Fig. 1 (a)
for Ni, Al, Ta, Cr, Co, Re and W, respectively. As described in the lit-
erature [3,17,18], Ni, Al and Ta mainly partition into the c0 phase,
while Cr, Co and Re mainly partition into the c phase. W shows
no preferentially partitioning. Heavy atoms with large atomic radii
(i.e., Cr, Co and especially Re) are enriched near the interfaces, as
evidenced by the high contrast observed in Fig. 1 (e)–(g). Creep is
controlled by elemental diffusion [15], and the Re in the c phase
is particularly effective at hindering the diffusion of the c0 forming
elements into the c phase and inhibiting diffusion controlled pro-
cesses during creep [19–22]. According to both experimental and
theoretical work, Re has the lowest diffusion coefﬁcient of all the
alloying elements [23,24]. Thus, during cooling, enrichments can
form that can retard creep by slowing down elemental diffusion.
3.2. Formation of kink structures
High contrast regions are also observed at the c/c0 interfaces, as
shown in Fig. 2 (a), indicative of high concentrations of heavy ele-
ments. During creep, kink structures appear at the c/c0 interfaces
in the single crystal superalloys; only a few publications have
investigated this phenomenon. The kink structures inside the black
rectangle ( Fig. 2 (a)) were analyzed using HAADF-STEM ( Fig. 2 (b)
and (c)). The apexes of these two kinks are composed of a compar-
atively high concentration of heavy elements. Higher contrast
areas ( /C24 2–3 nm in size) are observed in Fig. 2 (b). Such kinks may
form when the alloying elements diffuse between c and c0 during
creep. In addition, dislocation cores were found at the apex of the
kinks (black rectangles 1 and 2, Fig. 2 (c)). Fig. 2 (d) shows an
enlarged image of a representative dislocation; by drawing a local
Burgers circuit, its Burgers vector was identiﬁed to be a/2h110 i.
The a/2h110 i dislocation shown in Fig. 2 (d) originates from the c
phase, glides into the c0 phase and pins near the c/c0 interface. Dis-
locations glide into the softer c-matrix phase after they are gener-
ated. The formation of interfacial dislocation networks can prohibit
or retard dislocation pile-ups at c/c0 interfaces during creep. Addi-
tionally, these networks can effectively strengthen the c/c0 inter-
face and act as barriers to the movement of dislocations through
the c0 phase; few dislocations can glide across c/c0 interfacial dis-
location networks. Thus, we propose that the observation of such
kinks stems from the structural nature of the dislocation core.
3.3. Composition characterization of the apexes of kink structures
As mentioned previously, the displacement of interfaces is
caused by the diffusion of the alloying elements between the c
and c0 phases during creep. However, more work needs to be per-
formed to determine the compositions of the kink structures in
Fig. 2 (b) and (c), especially the compositions of the clusters at the
apexes. These clusters of heavy atoms slow down the displacement
of the interfaces during creep, retarding the process. The enrich-
ment of heavy elements near dislocation cores may strengthen
the high temperature performance of superalloys by hindering dis-
location movement. An area that included two kink structures near
an interface was selected, and the distribution of alloying elements
in this region was analyzed (Fig. 3(a)). The corresponding elemental
maps of Ni, Al, Ta, Cr, Co, Re and W are displayed in Fig. 3 (b)–(h),
respectively. The segregation behavior of these alloying elements
is consistent with the trends observed in composition distribution
(Fig. 1(b)–(h)). The mapping image in Fig. 3(g) indicates local clus-
tering of Re (clusters /C24 2–3 nm in size); Re is enriched in these areas
and tends to form clusters due to its low diffusivity [25,26]. The for-
mation of kink structures depends on the nature of the dislocation
core structure. At the apexes of the kink structures, near the core
of the dislocation, Re was enriched. Because Re atoms exchange
with vacancies as the dislocations move, Re atoms could act as high
diffusion energy barriers in these lattices [24]. Further, the presence
of Re atoms near dislocation cores could pin dislocations and ham-
per their ability to climb and glide during creep, thus improving the
creep resistance of superalloys [15]. The formation and appearance

3.4. Formation mechanism of the kink structures at the c/c0 interfaces
A schematic of the likely mechanism of formation of kink struc-
tures at c/c0 interfaces during creep deformation is shown in Fig. 4.
In general, the interfacial dislocations form square dislocation
networks, which efﬁciently stabilize the c/c0 interface ( Fig. 4 (a)).
Then, the interfacial networks become unstable, and the disloca-
tion segment bows out, especially if has a large spacing, and moves
causing creep to accelerate [27]. Thus, the dislocation core struc-
ture is easily observed parallel to the bow-like dislocation
segments. Moreover, the bow-like dislocation segment is embed-
ded in the c0 phase, and the dislocation core structure stays in
the c0 phase ( Fig. 4 (b)). A large number of heavy elements

present near the interface are displayed in yellow). As mentioned
previously, creep is controlled by elemental diffusion [15]. A rela-
tively high concentration of Re exists near the c/c0 interface
because most of the Re atoms tend to diffuse to the core region
of the dislocation [28] (Fig. 4(c)). During diffusion, Re atoms switch
places with Al atoms in the c0 phase [29,30] as they travel from
between the interface and the dislocation core because they have
lower activation energies for diffusion near the dislocation core
[31]. The diffusion path forged by the Re atoms is comprised of
the c phase as a result of the atomic interactions that occur with
such large-scale substitutions ( Fig. 4 (d)). At the apexes of inter-
faces near dislocation cores, clusters enriched in heavy elements,
including Cr, Co and Re, exert a pinning force that stops the move-
ment of dislocations, retards the displacement of the c/c0 interface
during creep, and thus improves the creep resistance of
superalloys.
4. Conclusions
In summary, the kink structures at the c/c0 interfaces of nickel-
based single crystal superalloys were observed for the ﬁrst time
using HAADF-STEM. EDX was used to determine the elemental
compositions of the apexes of the kink structures. The dislocation
core absorbs high-Z elements (Co and Re), which adopt a hexago-
nal structure, but extrudes Ni and Al, which adopt a face-centered
cubic (fcc) structure. High-Z elements (Ta and W) are stabilized in a
bcc arrangement, while Cr, a low-Z element that adopts a bcc
arrangement, tends to behave like Re. Re clusters can be found at
the apexes of kinks. Because of such trends, kink structures typi-
cally are generated at c/c0 interfaces. These interfaces could play
a role in retarding their displacement and could exert a pinning
force that stops the movement of dislocations during creep.
