abstract
In this study, c-TiAl based alloys of composition Ti-46.5Al-% xTa (x = 0.8, 4 and 8 at.%) were fabricated by
mechanical alloying and consolidated by spark plasma sintering. The main objective of the study was to
evaluate the inﬂuence Ta contents had on strengthening effect of the c-TiAl based alloy. X-ray diffractom-
etry (XRD) at room and high temperatures and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses were used
to characterise the sintered alloys and fractured surfaces. Compression tests measured mechanical prop-
erties from 25 to 1000 /C176C. The results show that the addition of 0.8 at.% Ta increased the compressive
strength of the c-TiAl alloy from 980 N.mm /C0 2 (at 25 /C176C) to 1280 N.mm /C0 2 (at 850 /C176C). At 1000 /C176C, the
strength of the alloys increased with increasing Ta additions. At 850 /C176C and higher, the mode of failure
of all the sintered alloys was by intergranular fracture.

1. Introduction
Weight-saving materials are constantly ﬁnding use in the aero-
space and automobile industries. Some of such materials are the
TiAl based intermetallic alloys. They have a low density, high melt-
ing point of 1460 /C176C, high elastic moduli, low diffusion coefﬁcient,
good corrosion and oxidation behaviour and are ductile at their
service temperatures while also maintaining good structural sta-
bility [1].
A major challenge associated with c-TiAl based alloys, despite
their attractive properties, is low ductility at ambient tempera-
tures. Over the years to date, a lot of efforts have and are chan-
nelled in improving the limited room temperature ductility of
TiAl alloys. Some include (i) microalloying to form ternary, quater-
nary or quinary alloys [2,3] (ii) grain reﬁnement through the pow-
der metallurgy processing route or by rapid solidiﬁcation [4,5], (iii)
modiﬁcation of the morphology, volume fraction and distribution
of constituent phases by heat treatment [6,7].
In recent years, the efﬁcacy of Nb additions ( /C20 10 at.%) to TiAl
based alloys has received signiﬁcant attention. It has proven to
enhance room temperature ductility and high-temperature
strength [8,9] but with decreased amounts of Al by shifting the a
phase boundary to the left [10]. The addition of Nb and other
BCC metals have been reported to modify the crystal structure of
TiAl alloys to a more symmetric cubic lattice to attain the mini-
mum number of slip systems required for plasticity thus increasing
ductility [11–13].
An equal replacement of Nb is the use of Ta. Their positions on
the periodic table suggest that the chemical behaviours of both ele-
ments are alike. Ta has an atomic radius of 2.22 Å comparable to
2.18 Å for Nb. In addition, as a marked advantage, Ta has a high
melting temperature (3017 /C176C), thus presents the avenue of
increasing the service temperature of TiAl based alloys. Lately,
the low diffusivity of Ta in TiAl alloys which favours massive cM
(TiAl) transformation over lamellar formation at low cooling rates
is greatly desirable and depended on for the fabrication of the new
air-hardenable alloys [14]. The air-hardenable alloys have been
reported to exhibit good mechanical properties [15] and offer

2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Alloy synthesis
Elemental powders Ti (<45 mm, 99% purity), Al (7–15 mm, 99.5%
purity), Ta ( /C25 2 mm), Cr (<45 mm) and Nb (<45 mm, 99.8% purity)
were supplied by LGC Industrial Analytical, South Africa. The Al
and Nb powders were milled with a 2 wt% stearic acid process con-
trol agent (PCA) while Ti and Cr were milled with ethanol as PCA. A
Retsch PM 400 high energy ball mill equipment with a 250 ml zir-
conia vial and grinding balls of 1 mm diameter was used in milling
the individual elemental powders at a speed of 350 rpm. Al powder
was milled for 6 h whereas Nb, Ti and Cr were milled for 4 h each.
The ball-to-powder mass ratio (BPR) used for all the milling pro-
cess was 5:1. Weighed compositions of Ti-46.5Al- xTa ( x = 0.8, 4, 8
at.%) alloys and a 48-2-2 (Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb at.%) reference alloy
were loaded into a 500 ml plastic container and then placed in a
Turbula
/C210shaker-mixer for 4 h. The mixed compositions were
mechanically alloyed using a Retsch PM 100 ball mill employing
a 500 ml tungsten carbide vial and stainless steel grinding balls
of diameters 10,15 and 20 mm. For each alloy, the weighed stearic
acid used was 6 wt% of the total powder mass charge. Mechanical
alloying was done for 6 h for each composition at a speed of
350 rpm and a BPR of 10:1. The FCT H-HP D25 hybrid SPS equip-
ment (FCT Systeme GmbH, Germany) was used in consolidating
and sintering of the alloys. The process was carried out in a vacuum
atmosphere, making use of a graphite die of inner diameter
100 mm, graphite punches and graphite foils -which were used
to line the ﬂat ends of the die [17]. With a heating rate of 200 /C176C.
min/C0 1, an applied pressure of 32 MPa and a dwell time of 10 mins,
sintering was done at 1150 /C176C.
2.2. High-temperature x-ray diffractometry analysis (HTXRD)
The Rigaku x-ray diffractometer, coupled with a pro-
grammable temperature controller PTC-30 was used to obtain
the HTXRD data of the sintered alloys (TiAl- xTa). The experiments
were carried out in a vacuum with the aid of a turbo-molecular
pump controller. The thickness of each sintered sample was about
1.3 mm. Calibration of the high-temperature stage was done
using alumina. For each test, the sample was placed on an alu-
mina plate and then mounted on a platinum cavity which served
as both a sample stage and heating element. A Pt/Pt-Rh thermo-
couple welded to the bottom of the stage was used in measuring
the temperature. In a continuous mode and using a nickel ﬁltered
Cu-Ka radiation source (50 kV and 300 mA), HTXRD data were
collected between the 2 h range of 5–90 /C176with scanning step size
and rate of 0.02 /C176and 4 /C176min/C0 1 respectively. With a heating rate of
20 /C176C.min/C0 1, the data were attained at temperatures ranging from
400–1000 /C176C and a soak time of 20 mins was carried out at each
temperature. Analysis of the acquired HTXRD data was done
using the PDXL 2 software.
2.3. High-temperature compression tests
The as-sintered alloys were sectioned into dimensions of
3 /C2 3 /C2 6 mm each. The compression tests were carried out in air
at four temperatures, i.e. room temperature (RT), 550 /C176C, 850 /C176C
and 1000 /C176C at a strain rate of 0.1 mm.min /C0 1 using the Shimadzu
Autograph AG-X 250 kN compression machine. At each tempera-
ture, the samples were soaked for 15 mins before compressing
with a starting load of 30 N.
3. Results and discussions
3.1. Microstructure development
Fig. 1 exempliﬁes the polished surface morphology of the as-
sintered alloys using Ti-46.5Al-4Ta (at.%) alloy. The bright contrast
constituted mostly of Al and O, whereas the dark contrast was pre-
dominant of Ti and Al. From the SEM analysis, the bright phase can
be concluded to be a-Al2O3 and the dark phase of a2-Ti3Al and c-
TiAl. Similar polished surface morphologies have been reported
by Li et al. [18].
The SEM backscattered micrographs of the as-sintered alloys
are shown in Fig. 2 . The microstructures of the sintered alloys
were inhomogeneous. The EDX spot analysis ( Fig. 3 a) conﬁrmed
the primary phases in the reference alloy were the a2-Ti3Al (grey
contrast), a2 þ c (white contrast) and a-Al2O3 (black contrast). In
Ti-46.5Al-0.8Ta (at.%) alloy ( Fig. 3 b), the c-TiAl phase was the
main phase of a dark grey colour. For alloys with 4 and 8 Ta
(at.%), a2 þ c phase was the predominant phase ( Fig. 3 c and d).
The microstructural inhomogeneity may be attributed to the slow
diffusivity of Ta and the presence of high oxygen contents in the
alloys. Oxygen as a a-stabilizing element is known to dissolve
preferentially in the a-phase. Hence, during solidiﬁcation, the
high oxygen contents expanded the a-phase ﬁeld and raised the
eutectoid temperature of the alloys [19,20]. Additionally, upon
cooling, oxygen favoured the chemical ordering of a ! a2 owing
to the signiﬁcant oxygen solubility difference between the a2
and c phases.
The formation of a-Al2O3 in the alloys may be explained from
two perspectives. The high-energy mechanical alloying process
may account for the appreciable increase in oxygen content [21],
which consequently resulted in an in situ reaction culminating in
the formation of a-Al2O3 [18]. Secondly, the saturation of oxygen
in the a-phase led to the precipitation of a-Al2O3. When the ratio
of Ti/Al is /C21 1 or Ti/Al < 1 with high oxygen content, the amount

3.2. HTXRD analysis
HTXRD patterns from RT up to 1000 /C176C of the reference alloy, Ti-
46.5Al-0.8Ta, Ti-46.5Al-4Ta and Ti-46.5Al-8Ta (at.%) alloys are
shown in Fig. 4 . Noticeably, for the reference alloy in Fig. 4 (a), at
46/C176(2h) above 650 /C176C a new a2 phase emerged. Also, with increas-
ing temperature, the a2 phase at 58 /C176(2h) slightly increased in
intensity. At 68 /C176(2h) from RT, the c and a-Al2O3 phases continu-
ously decreased in intensity as temperature increased. The pres-
ence of the two phases at this position is consistent with the
report by Kawabata et al. [22]. The authors asserted that some of
the
c-TiAl grains could be covered by a layer of a-Al2O3 phase
which possibly resulted from the segregation of oxygen to the
grain boundaries leading to continuous precipitation of a-Al2O3
phase on the grain boundaries. Moreover, the intensity of the c
and a2 peak at about 84 /C176(2h) gradually decreased with increasing
temperature. For Ti-46.5Al-0.8Ta, the a2 phase at 47 /C176(2h), the a2
and TaAl peak at 68 /C176(2h), the c peak at 76 /C176(2h) and the c, a2
and TaAl peak at 81 /C176(2h) all slightly increased in intensity with
increasing temperature, as seen in Fig. 4 (b). Similarly, in Fig. 4 (c)
in the case of Ti-46.5Al-4Ta, a marginal increase in the intensity
of the a2 peak at about 68 /C176(2h) as temperature increased was
observed. On the other hand, the intensity of the c peak at 46 /C176
(2h), a2 peak at 48 /C176(2h) and a2 and TaAl peak at 66 /C176(2h) height-
ened as temperature increased for the Ti-46.5Al-8Ta alloy
(Fig. 4 d). Also, a c phase emerged at about 80 /C176(2h) from 650 to
1000 /C176C and accordingly increased in intensity as temperature
increased.
The HTXRD analysis of all the alloys conﬁrmed their high-
temperature stability from RT to 1000 /C176C and thus could strongly
serve as options for high-temperature applications.
3.3. High-temperature compression behaviour
The high-temperature compression behaviours of the as-
sintered alloys are depicted in Fig. 5 . The addition of Ta led
to an increase in the strength of the binary Ti-46.5Al alloys
compared to the reference alloy both at RT and at elevated
temperatures. The alloy with 0.8 at.% Ta addition exhibited
the highest strength at RT, 550 /C176C and 850 /C176C. At 550 /C176C and
850 /C176C, the strength of the alloys even though higher than the
reference alloy, decreased with increasing Ta contents. However,
at 1000 /C176C, the contrary was observed. The strength of the
alloys increased with increasing Ta additions. The Ti-46.5Al-
8Ta alloy had the highest strength, followed by Ti-46.5Al-4Ta
and Ti-46.5Al-0.8Ta alloys. Ductility and plastic deformation
were observed at 850 /C176C and 1000 /C176C for the as-sintered alloys.
As known and from literature, as ductility of materials enhance
their brittleness decrease [23]. This was clearly seen in Fig. 5 (d)
for the as-sintered alloys at 1000 /C176C. Ta is a solid solution
strengthener with a high melting temperature and low diffusiv-
ity in TiAl alloys and therefore strongly interact with dislocation
leading to the strengthening effect and improved ductility as
seen at 850 and 1000 /C176C [24]. Additionally, it has been reported
that the addition of Ta can modify the interfacial structure and
especially reduce the stacking fault energy in TiAl alloys leading
to strength retention at high-temperature and improved
ductility [25].

1. The chemical compositions and microstructures of all the fabri-
cated alloys were inhomogeneous. The predominant phases
formed included the c-TiAl, a2-Ti3Al, TaAl and a-Al2O3.
2. The appearance of a new a2 phase and the decrease of existing c

3. All the sintered alloys at RT and 550 /C176C exhibited a brittle mode
of failure under compressive stresses. Ti-46.5Al-0.8Ta exhibited
the highest strength, from RT to 850 /C176C. At 1000 /C176C, the strength
of the alloys increased with increasing Ta contents. Also, all the
alloys exhibited compressive plasticity at elevated tempera-
tures except for Ti-46.5Al-8Ta alloy at 850 /C176C.
4. Furthermore, SEM analysis of the fracture surfaces revealed that
microvoids formation and growth, as well as crack initiation
and propagation particularly at the segregated regions of the
alloys’ microstructures, led to the failure of the alloys.
