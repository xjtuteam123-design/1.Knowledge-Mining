Cryogenic Temperature Dependence of the 
Yield Strength of High-Strength Alloys 
The effect of cryogenic temperatures (from 78 F to —423 F) on the yield strength of 
twenty alloys was studied. Experimental results prove that they do not conform to 
any of the following theories: Hollomon and Zener's, Cottrell andBilby's, or Fisher's. 
However, all the plottings in loge-logc scale of yield strength versus absolute cryogenic 
temperatures of these alloys fall on straight lines which are governed by one single general 
equation, <ry = bT~m. From the Cottrell's dislocation theory on yielding and Fisher's 
equation of activation energy in forming a dislocation loop, the same type of equation 
of yield strength versus temperature as expressed by the empirical ones can be derived 
theoretically. The empirical equations are very useful in predicting yield strengths at 
any cryogenic temperature within or slightly out of the test range for which data were 
available. Some limited yield strength data at elevated temperatures for a few
 alloys were 
studied for comparison. It was observed the general equation for yield strength versus 
cryogenic
 temperatures holds valid for stainless steels but not so well for titanium, 
nickel, and aluminum alloys at elevated temperatures. However, no conclusion can be 
drawn until further detailed studies at elevated temperatures are made.

Theory 
As mentioned
 previously, Dorn, et al., suggested that the flow 
stress is a function of the instantaneous values of structure, strain 
rate, and temperature. Therefore the failure of the mechanical 
equation of state must be attributed to the fact that structural 
changes attending plastic deformation depend not only on strain 
but temperature and strain rate as well. Dorn, et al. [11] again 
said, "In the language of dislocation theory, this means that the 
patterns of dislocations arising from plastic deformation depend 
on the temperature and strain rate as well as the extent of the 
deformation." 
Now it
 seems that Cottrell and Bilby's dislocation theory on 
yielding has taken these aspects into consideration. Besides, 
their theoretical curve is comparatively closer to the general 
pattern of the curves plotted from Figs. 5 to 8. Therefore it is 
logical to look into this theory thoroughly and hope some-
thing will be derived from it to explain the pattern of the ex-
perimental curves shown from Figs. 5 to 8. However, before 
Cottrell and Bilby's theory can be adopted, it has to be as-
sumed that their yielding mechanism is applicable to all of the 
alloys under study in this paper. This seems a reasonable 
assumption.

pinned by adsorbed carbon, nitrogen, and other solute atoms. 
Yielding occurs when dislocations are freed from their adsorbed 
solute atoms with the aid of thermal energy fluctuations. Based 
on this theory, they analyzed a detailed model of solute-atom
