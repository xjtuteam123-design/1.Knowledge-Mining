ABSTRACT
Refractory multi-principal element alloys (RMPEAs) have gained interest recently due to their superior properties
at elevated temperatures, including outstanding yield and ultimate strengths, high thermal conductivity, and
resistance to creep. RMPEAs can be designed to exhibit a wide range of properties by tailoring their composition.
However, the vast chemical design space makes brute-force experimental screening inefficient and costly. In this
work, we follow a closed-loop, iterative computational/experimental screening approach that combines
computational alloy design methodologies with high-throughput synthesis and characterization tools to explore
the vast RMPEAs space and design new RMPEAs that satisfy multiple objectives and constraints. In particular, we
targeted compositions with yield strengths higher than 50 MPa at 2000
◦
C, W content of more than 30 at.% for
high-temperature strength and operability up to 2000
◦
C, narrow solidification range for additive manufactur-
ability, competitive ductility metrics, among other property constraints. We evaluated the mechanical properties
and microstructure of 58 alloys designed in 5 batches, in both as-cast and homogenized conditions, synthesized
using vacuum arc melting, utilizing scanning electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, Vickers microhardness,
nanoindentation, and high-temperature compression testing. Based on the microhardness screening experiments
in each batch, the best-performing alloys were selected for scale-up. High-temperature compression at 1800
◦
C
was performed in these alloys, demonstrating that the designed alloys exhibit up to five times higher yield
strength than a pure tungsten benchmark. We conclude that W-containing RMPEAs designed in this study merit
further consideration for next-generation structural materials for ultra-high temperature applications.
1. Introduction
1.1. State-of-the-art in refractory alloys for high-temperature applications
The search for novel structural materials capable of withstanding
ultra-high operating temperatures is critical for numerous applications,
particularly those in aerospace applications (e.g., liquid rocket engines,
advanced propulsion systems, and hypersonic leading edges, to name a
few). Materials used in these applications must satisfy multiple design
constraints, including a high strength-to-weight ratio, the ability to
operate at elevated temperatures, and manufacturability into complex
geometries [ 1 – 3 ]. Refractory metals and their alloys have been the
primary choice for extreme high-temperature applications due to their
high melting points and desirable mechanical properties at elevated
temperatures [ 4 – 7 ].

More recently, there has been a considerable effort in developing
refractory multi-principal element alloys (RMPEAs) for high-

high-entropy alloys (HEAs) at the beginning of the 2000s, leading to the
discovery of novel alloy compositions with unprecedented properties.
The concept of HEAs was first introduced in 2004 [ 31 , 32 ], with mate-
rials consisting of 5 or more principal alloying elements, concentrations
ranging from 5 to 35 at.%. The main motivation behind the HEA concept
is to explore unexplored regions of multi-dimensional alloy spaces [ 33 ]
with particular interest in alloys with single-phase solid solutions
resulting from high configurational entropy rather than brittle inter-
metallic compounds [ 34 ]. While historically, the properties of HEAs
have been attributed to their supposedly high configurational entropy,
severe lattice distortion, cocktail effect, and sluggish diffusion [ 35 ], the
only unquestionable attribute of this alloy space is its vastness.
MPEAs, also called complex concentrated alloys (CCAs), are a further
generalization of HEAs without restrictions on the concentration, a
minimum number of principal elements present, or configurational en-
tropy. MPEAs represent materials without a single dominant element in
the composition and are comprised of 3 or more principal elements in
contrast to the traditionally engineered alloys with a single dominant
element, such as Ni-based superalloys or steels. Within this field,
RMPEAs have emerged as a promising subset, offering exceptional me-
chanical and physical properties specifically tunable for high-
temperature applications [ 34 – 44 ] that not only surpass those of con-
ventional alloys but also retain the design flexibility that is intrinsic to
MPEAs.

1.2. Fabrication challenges for refractory alloys and additive
manufacturing of refractory alloys
A major obstacle to the deployment of refractory alloys lies in the
formidable fabrication challenges posed by their conflicting re-
quirements. Specifically, the inherent high strength of these alloys at
elevated temperatures renders traditional hot-working fabrication
methods impractical [ 66 – 68 ]. The constraints posed by heat transfer
and oxidation confine the process of hot working to temperatures that
are either equivalent to or less than the intended service temperatures,
resulting in a core paradox wherein the fabrication necessitates
achieving low strength and considerable ductility while also maintain-
ing exceedingly high strength for operational use under comparable
temperature conditions [ 67 ]. Because of these limitations, additive
manufacturing (AM) is seen as an emerging technology that can expand
the applicability of these materials by reducing materials usage, allow-
ing for geometric intricacy, and surpassing the need for conventional
processing steps [ 67 , 69 – 71 ]. Compared to the sheer amount of work on
AM of (relatively) easy-to-print engineering alloys, such as stainless
steels and titanium alloys, there is limited work on AM of refractory
alloys [ 72 – 77 ].
Much of the literature on AM of refractory materials is on the pro-
cessing of pure tungsten [ 73 , 78 – 83 ], likely owing to the interest in the
nuclear industry as a promising candidate for plasma-facing materials in
fusion reactors with its high melting point, low sputtering yield and high
plasma sputtering erosion resistance [ 84 – 89 ]. In general, although
high-density parts were achieved during AM of pure W or Mo and their
alloys, in many cases, microcracks were inevitable due to the inherent
high ductile to brittle transition temperature (DBTT) of these alloys,

1.3. Objectives and approach of the present work
Based on the literature above, we aim to co-design W-containing
RMPEAs for high-temperature performance and suitability for AM using
the W-Nb-Ta-Mo-V-Hf- Re elemental pallet. In our previous work [ 102 ],
we emphasized the importance of co-designing alloys for performance
and AM simultaneously, rather than designing for performance first and
then adjusting processing conditions for printability.
Regarding the elemental pallet, Nb, Ta, Re , and Hf were chosen as
they have been shown to improve the ductility of RMPEAs [ 103 – 105 ].
Mo was selected as it has been shown to have strengthening effects in
RMPEAs [ 40 ]. V was selected as it was shown to be an optimal
strengthener for BCC RMPEAs [ 106 ]. However, since V readily boils at
the melting temperature of W, it is challenging to use it as a solid
solution-strengthening element in BCC RMPEAs. We have shown that if
W is alloyed with other elements first then followed by V, this will cause
melting point depression of the alloyed material [ 107 ] thereby enabling
the incorporation of V in W-containing RMPEAs.
A primary goal of this work is to design printable W-containing
RMPEAs. We frame the co-design of W-containing RMPEAs for high-
temperature performance and amenability to AM as an iterative,
(mostly) composition-agnostic, constraint-satisfaction problem. In a
composition-agnostic alloy design scheme, we avoid making
composition-based prescriptions before conducting investigatory cal-
culations. Our aim is to design W-containing RMPEAs with at least 30 at.
% tungsten (W), without imposing other composition-based restrictions.
This still leaves a vast chemical space to design within e.g., 35,756
candidate alloys if the W-Nb-Ta-Mo-V-Hf- Re alloy space is grid sampled
at 5 at. % considering binary to quinary alloys and then filtered for alloys
with at least W ≥ 30 at.%.
A constraint-satisfaction alloy design scheme involves filtering a pool
of candidate alloys down to a set of feasible options — those meeting
specified property constraints. In this work, the property constraints
concern phase stability, yield strength at 2000
◦
C, room temperature
ductility, favorable solidification sequences, and amenability to AM.
It should be noted that in addition to these property constraints, we
also treat the W constraint (i.e., a minimum amount of W in the alloy) as
a filter. In this work, we apply the union of filters to design spaces
simultaneously. Although this approach is less computationally effi-
cient, it allows us to investigate the effects of other alloying elements on
alloy properties. This method helps us learn about chemistry-property
trends within the alloy spaces explored in this work. These chemistry-
property insights are presented in the Results and Discussion section.
An iterative alloy design scheme consists of several batches of syn-
thesis, characterization, and analysis, with each batch offering insights
about alloy space. Bayesian optimization has become a popular
approach to closed-loop experimental campaigns and the design of ex-
periments [ 108 ]. Bayesian optimization is a technique that uses prior
knowledge and probabilistic models to efficiently explore and optimize
complex design spaces by guiding the selection of new experiments
based on expected improvement [ 108 ]. However, an iterative alloy
design scheme does not need to strictly follow a closed-loop Bayesian
optimization method; instead, adjustments to the design scheme can be
made based on lessons learned throughout each batch.
Throughout this campaign, we conducted 4 batches of constraint-
satisfaction design using different acquisition policies in each batch. In
each batch, alloys were down-selected for synthesis and characterization
based on different policies that account for strength, ductility, and
printability. In each batch, mechanical properties were rapidly assessed.
This rapid assessment of properties then decided which alloys were
scaled up for high-temperature testing. In the 5th and final batch, we
“ close the loop ” with respect to HT yield strength, using Bayesian
optimization to select the alloys that are most likely to improve the
strength-ductility-printability Pareto-front (in the direction of yield
strength). In total, 58 novel RMPEAs were designed in 5 batches.

The Maresca-Curtin model was used to screen for yield strength in
BT1 – BT4. In BT5, we corrected the Maresca-Curtin model in a Bayesian
manner. Specifically, using HT compression data acquired from previous
batches and data available in the literature [ 114 ], we corrected the
Maresca-Curtin model using hierarchical Gaussian Process (GP) regres-
sion with informative priors.
There are two aspects to this modeling approach: informative priors
and hierarchical GP regression with proxy experiments. We first address
informative priors for GP regression. A GP regressor is a Bayesian non-
parametric regression model that exploits structured correlations
among training data [ 115 ]. Recall that the Bayes rule provides a pos-
terior given data, a prior, and a likelihood. GP regression is a method of
extending the Bayes rule to functions [ 115 ]. In our case, the
Maresca-Curtin model acts as our prior prediction for
temperature-dependent yield strength i.e., in the absence of data, we
rely on the Maresca-Curtin model. The prior prediction is updated after
having observed data, yielding a posterior prediction (e.g., the trained
GP regressor). In our case, we trained the GP regressor whose prior is the
Maresca-Curtin model with experimental yield strength measurements,
yielding a posterior prediction that is informed by both a physics-based
model and experiment. For chemistries (and temperatures) that are
similar to what the model has been trained with, the GP regressor will
provide predictions that are similar to the training data. For chemistries
(or temperatures) for which there is sparse training data, the model will
default to the prior e.g., the physics-based Maresca-Curtin model. In
previous work [ 44 ], we demonstrated this method enables safer
physics-based extrapolation of temperature-dependent yield strength.
We now address the hierarchical GP regression with proxy experi-
ments. In addition to informative priors, our yield strength model in-
corporates proxy hardness experiments. Vickers hardness (HV) and yield
strength (YS) are related by the Tabor relationship (e.g., 9.91 × HV/3
[MPa] ≈ YS [MPa]). Hierarchical GP regression can exploit this linear
correlation between HV and YS. Hierarchical GP regression uses a GP
trained on HV to predict HV for new candidate alloys. These predicted
HV values are used as a feature for a GP regressor trained on YS. The
quality of the HV prediction improves as more HV experiments are
provided to the model. Likewise, if the HV GP is equipped with an
informative prior, this will also improve the HV predictions. As HV
predictions improve, the predictions for YS also improve [ 44 , 116 ].
In the present case, the HV GP regressor was trained on HV data
acquired from previous batches and literature [ 114 ]. The HV GP model
is also equipped with the Maresca-Curtin-Tabor model as an informative
prior. The Maresca-Curtin-Tabor model is merely the YS prediction from
the Maresca-Curtin model ( Eqs. (1-4) ) converted to a HV prediction via
the Tabor relationship. In summary, to predict yield strength we first
build a model for GP regressor that is equipped with the
Maresca-Curtin-Tabor model as a prior. The prior is updated with
experimental HV data from this campaign and from the literature. This
HV prediction is then used as an input for a GP regressor for YS. This YS
regressor is equipped with the Maresca-Curtin model as a prior. The
prior is updated with experimental YS from this campaign and from the
literature [ 114 ]. The code, benchmarking results, and comprehensive
explanation for the data-corrected HV-informed Maresca-Curtin model
for YS can be found in Ref [ 44 ].
Regarding ductility, although decreasing the DBTT has been shown
to decrease cracking in W-based alloys [ 117 ], DBTT is a difficult
ductility metric to estimate as it is dependent on many factors such as
grain size and interstitial oxygen impurity content [ 118 ]. Therefore,
instead of relying on DBTT as a design criterion, we opted to use a suite
of easier-to-query, commonly used ductility indicators: Pugh Ratio,
Cauchy Pressure, and VEC [ 119 ]. Each of these ductility indicators
captures a different aspect/mechanism of ductility. The Pugh Ratio is
defined as the ratio of the bulk modulus and shear modulus and captures
the competition between plastic deformation (shear modulus, G) and
fracture strength (bulk modulus B) [ 110 ]. The Cauchy Pressure is an
indicator of metallic bond directionality [ 120 ]. A positive Cauchy
Pressure is an indication of non-direction bonds (ductile) whereas a
negative Cauchy Pressure is an indication of directional bonds (brittle).
Qi and Chrzan [ 121 ] showed that lowering the VEC can shift the Fermi
level of the alloy, which in turn can induce a Jahn-Teller distortion that
causes shear instability which is fundamentally responsible for ductility.
While the VEC is defined as the compositionally weighted average

[ 119 ], the Pugh Ratio and Cauchy Pressure are derived from elastic
constants (see Eqs. (6) and (7) ) which are most often calculated using
density functional theory (DFT). DFT calculations are prohibitively
expensive and are not suited for high-throughput alloy design. However,
in other works [ 43 , 110 ] we have demonstrated that ROM

In sequential BO, the hyperparameters ( θ ) associated with the GP
objective model (in this case GP-based yield strength model) are set by
expert opinion or are tuned such that θ maximizes the likelihood of the
model. However, there is no guarantee that the selected hyperparameter
set θ is appropriate for the entire design space. Setting θ makes a strong
assumption about the underlying shape and behavior of the function
being modeled. To avoid this assumption, BBO is often used [ 108 , 138 ].
Batch BO circumvents any strong assumptions about θ by not
committing to a single θ but instead considering a batch of θ s. If the
batch size is n, then n different θ s will be selected, yielding n slightly
different GP-based models for yield strength. These n slightly different
GP-based models are used to query an acquisition function, which in our
case is the commonly used Expected Hypervolume Improvement
(EHVI), as defined in Eq. (17) . See the Supplementary Materials for
more information and codes concerning Batch BO.

4. Results and discussions
The computational and experimental results from this (mostly)
composition-agnostic, iterative, constraint satisfaction design campaign
are organized as follows: In Section 4.1 , the results of the constraint
satisfaction design scheme are presented. Specifically, we investigate the
effect of the applied constraints on the down-selected alloy space. We
investigate chemistry-property correlations such as why certain com-
positions are likely to fail specific constraints and the effect of individual
alloying elements on various properties. Furthermore, we investigate
property-property correlations and identify various trade-offs and syn-
ergies among the properties considered in this design scheme. In Section
4.2 , the as-cast characterization results of the 58 alloys designed in this
work are presented. Specifically, results from the microstructural
investigation, phase identification, and mechanical responses (i.e.,
Vickers hardness and nanoindentation elastic modulus) are presented.
Section 4.3 presents the same analysis for the 58 alloys in the dendrite-

data analysis is conducted; high-temperature mechanical properties
obtained from the compression experiments and lessons learned
throughout the alloy design campaign are presented.
4.1. Results of constraint-satisfaction design scheme
The design formulation used in BTs 1 – 5 is a composition-agnostic
constraint satisfaction scheme [ 43 , 107 ]. This design scheme consists
of querying relevant properties for all candidate designs and discarding
candidates whose properties violate the constraints listed in Section 2.2 .
The alloys that meet the union of these constraints are deemed to be
‘feasible ’ and thus merit further study and down-selection. The indi-
vidual effect of each property constraint is demonstrated in Fig. 5 .
Fig. 5 consists of 9 uniform manifold approximation and projections
(UMAPs) [ 140 ] of the RMPEA design space. We use UMAP to project the
7-element RMPEA space to 2 dimensions. When a composition space is
grid sampled and projected to 2 dimensions, the resulting UMAP can be
interpreted as a flattened Gibbs hyper-tetrahedron [ 43 ]. These UMAPs
are read like a Gibbs ternary diagram and provide an ‘overhead view ’ of
the RMPEA design space. Each dot in a UMAP represents an alloy. Alloys
that are mapped closer to the corners of the UMAP are richer in a
particular element. Alloys that are mapped on the edges connecting 2
corners are binary combinations of the 2 elements on the corners. Alloys
with higher configurational entropy (e.g., HEAs) are plotted in the
central regions of the UMAP. Alloys colored grey fail a certain
constraint. For feasible alloys, those colored red exhibit properties close
to the constraint, while alloys colored blue greatly surpass the
constraint. This dimensionality reduction helps visualize how certain
filters affect certain regions in the RMPEA design space. More details on
this visualization technique can be found in Refs. [ 43 , 107 , 141 ].

this constraint. This is reasonable as V and Hf have the lowest melting
points of all the elements in this design space. Furthermore, alloys that
have narrow solidification ranges (blue dots) tend to be closer to the
corners and edges of the composition space. This is reasonable as the
solidification range should decrease with decreasing alloying
complexity. Fig. 5 b shows the strictest solidus temperature constraint

There are limitations to visualizing constraint satisfaction using
UMAPs. For example, UMAPs are susceptible to overcrowding, meaning

compositions can only provide a qualitative relationship between alloys.

such projections. Furthermore, the appearance of the filter can be
affected by the location of corners chosen for each element. For these
reasons, UMAPs alone are not sufficient to visualize the relationship
between chemistry and properties.

4.2. Characterization of designed alloy compositions in as-cast condition
Most of the studies in the literature on RMPEAs [ 42 , 45 , 48 , 50 , 119 ,
143 – 157 ] rely on the properties measured in as-cast conditions con-
sisting of casting defects and elemental segregations, which might
degrade the mechanical properties of the alloys. In addition, most of
as-cast RMPEAs feature composite microstructures including at the
minimum two different BCC phases due to elemental segregation, and

Lessons Learned Concerning Campaign Methodology: In reflecting on
the execution of this alloy discovery campaign, we deem it important to
share insights and areas for improvement to contribute to emerging
initiatives toward enhancing closed-loop data-driven alloy discovery
campaigns.
One self-identified deficiency in our framework pertained to the
timely and reliable acquisition of high-temperature yield strength data.
A major design consideration in this work was to identify alloys that
have yield strengths greater than 50 MPa at 2000
◦
C, yet our testing
capabilities were limited to compressive testing at 1800
◦
C. Further-
more, the time required for these tests posed a bottleneck in the
campaign. Instead of delaying progress, we opted to continue the
campaign using alternative down-selection methods other than Bayesian
optimization as high-temperature yield strength data was not immedi-
ately available to close the design loop. Therefore, in BTs 1 – 4 alloys
were selected using various acquisition functions that targeted a variety
of strong, ductile, and printable alloys. By BT5 high-temperature YS data
was available for alloys designed in the previous batches and therefore
BT5 makes use of Batch Bayesian optimization to down-select alloys for
synthesis.

strength modeling methodology between batches, aside from updating
the model with more experimental data. Unfortunately, during BT1 – 4,
we had not yet finalized the development of the machine learning

Regarding the constraint satisfaction aspect of the campaign, ideally,

A key lesson learned is that alloy design efforts should be cautious
when upgrading modeling methods during closed-loop campaigns.
Ideally, a closed-loop experimental campaign should have immutable
modeling methodologies and evolve only based on experimental results.
In light of these shortcomings, we would like to refer readers to the
two works [ 193 , 194 ] that specifically address what an ideal closed-loop
experimental alloy design campaign should resemble. These works are
informed by the institutional knowledge and experience gained from the
present design campaign. While these works do not specifically address
RMPEAs, these frameworks are chemistry-agnostic and can be applied to
any alloy family.
Because the goal of this work was to design a refractory alloy for
high-temperature operation and printability, the elegance of the method
leveraged to achieve this was not our top priority. Rather, we took a
pragmatic approach to alloy design throughout this campaign, allowing
the problem formulation to change according to evolving attitudes to-
ward constraints, increasing modeling capabilities, and expert metal-
lurgical opinion. This is at odds with rigid approaches where constraints
and objectives are immutable once the campaign has started. Bayesian
optimization is one such approach as it does not provide a formal
statistics-informed method to update attitudes toward constraints and
objectives. A potential solution to strike a balance between the flexibility
of heuristic expert-informed approaches and the explainability and
optimality of statistics-based Bayesian approaches is to frame the alloy
discovery campaign not as a search in composition space but instead as a
search in the problem formulation space.
In Ref. [ 195 ] the authors propose a novel Bayesian optimization
framework that is robust to evolving attitudes and preferences. The
framework seeks to formalize the changing of the design problem being
solved i.e., seeks to formalize the iterative updating of objectives, con-
straints, and acquisition functions throughout a closed-loop Bayesian
campaign. In our experience, such a method would be ideal for
closed-loop experimental alloy design campaigns as often researchers
want to update the alloy design methodology in light of having observed
data from previous batches, as was the case in this campaign. Instead of
researchers explicitly choosing what constraints to relax/restrict and
what objectives to prioritize/deprioritize, a statistics-based acquisition
function that is informed by the preferences of the researcher is used to
alter constraints and objectives. In this way, preferences about the
design methodology can be encoded in the design methodology itself.
More details on this proposed method are available in [ 195 ].
5. Summary and conclusions
The design of novel structural materials for ultra-high temperature
applications cannot be myopic, however to date in the literature, many
works on the design of RMPEAs address a few properties. Furthermore,
fewer of these have made consideration for L-PBF or other AM pro-
cessing routes. In this work, we propose an alloy design framework that
is composition-agnostic, and aware of multiple constraints and objec-
tives. We first conduct a high-throughput (HTP), (mostly) composition
agnostic, factorial exploration of the RMPEA space, simultaneously
balancing multiple performance constraints to design a printable
RMPEA capable of operating at 2000
◦
C continuously. Various con-
straints and utility functions were used to iteratively design over 5
batches. Even when the least restrictive set of constraints relevant to
ultra-high-temperature applications (BT2, Table 3 ) was applied to the
41,496 alloys of the W-Nb-Ta-Mo-V-Hf space explored in this work, only
3.5 % of the alloy space remains feasible. This affirms the adage [ 43 ],
that states that when application-relevant constraints are considered
during alloy design, “ the high entropy alloy space is not as big as we
think it is. ”
The 58 alloys designed throughout this work were synthesized with
vacuum arc melting and characterized using electron microscopy (SEM/
EDS), X-ray diffraction, Vickers microhardness, nanoindentation and
high-temperature compression with down-selected candidates. This
campaign generated a large amount of experimental and computational
data. However, the key findings from these data concerning the 58
synthesized alloys are as follows:
• The alloy design campaign converged on NbW-rich alloys, TaW-rich
alloys, and alloys with no constituent element exceeding 50 at. %,
indicating that these subregions of the RMPEA space may contain
strong, ductile, printable refractory alloys. These compositions, their
predicted properties, and their measured properties are provided in
the Supplemental Material .
• The total TaW content plays a significant role in the elemental
segregation observed in as-cast conditions. Alloys with a higher total
TaW content ( > 85 at.%) were found to be dendrite-free without a
need for homogenization heat treatments. The increase in the
amount of constituent elements other than Ta and W considered in
the design space increases the elemental segregation.
• Synthesized alloy compositions were designed to have a single BCC
phase using CALPHAD; the phase identification analysis via XRD
confirmed that the synthesized RMPEAs exhibit single phase BCC in
both as-cast (peak-broadening is observed due to dendritic micro-
structure in some cases) and after homogenization treatments. The
measured lattice parameters were found to be in close agreement
with the rule-of-mixture approximations, indicating the formation of
solid solutions.
• Regarding yield strength, of the 13 alloys tested in compression at
1800
◦
C, all exhibited compressive yield strengths above 160 MPa at
1800
◦
C outperforming pure Tungsten tested under the same strain
rate and temperature. Overall, Hf-added alloys showed the most
outstanding performance in strength and ductility combined.
Furthermore, we demonstrate that the Maresca-Curtin model still
acts as a lower bound estimate for compressive yield strength, even
at 1800
◦
C. These findings indicate that these alloys likely have a
compressive yield strength greater than 50 MPa at 2000
◦
C, however,
this has yet to be validated.
In addition to these key findings, we have learned several lessons that
further merit future alloy design campaigns:
• Dendritic microstructure observed in many alloys (42/58) were
successfully eliminated through homogenization heat treatments at
2000
◦
C for 28 h. Thermo-Calc DICTRA diffusion simulations
centered around the compositions of the dendritic and interdendritic
regions, and the maximum dendrite arm spacing are sufficient to
predict the temperature and time required to homogenize RMPEAs.
Based on the DICTRA simulations, the time required to fully ho-
mogenize the microstructure at 1600
◦
C and 1800
◦
C is usually
infeasible ( > 100 h), and higher temperatures are needed.
• Compositionally weighted variance in melting temperatures ( δ T
ROM
)
was found to be highly correlated with the degree of micro-
segregation in as-cast RMPEAs. The conventional microsegregation
indicators, the equilibrium ( Δ T
Equil
) and Scheil ( Δ T

) solidifica-

Regarding the design methodology of any future works, ideally, the
campaign would be closed-loop in every batch, use the same selection
policy in every batch, have minimal changes to the constraint and ob-
jectives, and use the same acquisition policy to down-select alloys for
synthesis and then to further select alloys for scale-up. In future design
campaigns where printability is investigated experimentally with single-

defects as per Eqs. (13-15) . In this way, the number of single-track ex-
periments required to characterize the printability of candidate alloys is
minimized. Once all candidates are successfully printed or determined
to be unprintable, the mechanical properties of as-printed specimens can
be characterized and compared.
