ABSTRACT 
To study temperature and rate-dependent behaviors of molybdenum-rhenium (Mo – Re) alloys, compressive tests 
at variable temperatures and strain rates were conducted. After the same machining and thermal annealing 
processes, fully recrystallization happens in Mo and Mo – Re alloys with low Re content (5 wt %), while recovery 
occurs in Mo – Re alloys with high Re content (14 wt% and 42 wt %). The strength increases as Re content rises at 
all temperatures (300 
◦
C, 600 
◦
C and 1200 
◦
C), meaning sufficient strengthening effect of Re. The rate-dependent 
behaviors, however, show different trends between Mo and Mo – Re alloys. Positive strain rate sensitivity is 
detected in Mo and low Re Mo – Re alloys in all temperatures, which is consistent to other research. While, near 
zero strain rate sensitivity is found in high Re Mo – Re alloys at 600 
◦
C. Such an unusual phenomenon may be 
contributed to high stored dislocation density during mechanical processes. The stored dislocations are activated 
at a proper temperature to accommodate plastic flow, which weakens the influence of strain rate on strength. 
1. Introduction 
High structural stability in molybdenum makes it widely applied in 
harsh environment, such as aerospace [ 1 ] and nuclear reactors [ 2 ]. 
Single BCC phase maintains in Mo from room temperature to melting 
point [ 3 ]. Although many advantages in Mo, lacking ductility at room 
temperature limits machinability and practical application [ 4 ]. Re 
alloying is one of the most effective methods to improve processed and 
mechanical properties due to decreased ductility to brittle transition 
temperature in Mo – Re alloy [ 5 , 6 ]. The ductile to brittle transition 
temperature decreases from near room temperature in Mo to lower than 
liquid nitrogen temperature ( 180 
◦
C) in Mo – Re alloys with 47.5 wt% 
Re [ 6 ]. 
High strength and low ductility in BCC metals are contributed to non- 
planar core of screw dislocations [ 7 ]. “ Rhenium effect ” in refractory 
metals is first found in Geach ’ s and Hughes ’ research in 1955 [ 8 ]. In 
theory, adding Re to Mo could induce non-planar core transfer to planar 
core that causes solid solution softening [ 9 ]. Re effect in experimental 
results, however, depends on various factors, including Re content [ 10 ], 
stress state [ 11 ], processing methods [ 12 ], and temperature [ 13 ]. 
Leichtfried et al. reported that the room-temperature tensile ductility of 
recrystallized Mo – Re remains nearly constant as Re ranging from 10 wt 
% to 41 wt% [ 10 , 11 ]. The ductility of cold-worked Mo-47.5Re is 
enhanced by testing at liquid nitrogen temperatures [ 13 ]. 
Although a lot of research have been made in Mo – Re alloys, some 
questions, such as temperature and rate-dependent behavior, are still 
not clear. To clarify this problem, compressive tests at varied tempera -
ture and strain rates are considered. The compressive stress-strain en -
ergy provides a research basis for studying the high temperature 
deformation and dynamic recrystallization properties in Mo [ 14 ]. 
Compression texts are widely used in molybdenum alloys to study me -
chanical responses at various temperatures in many researches [ 15 , 16 ].

Mo and Mo5Re behavior positive strain rate sensitivity at all tem -

increasing strain rate or reducing temperature could decrease the

strain rate sensitivity is closely related to purity [ 22 ]. The probability of

and Mo5Re is relatively low compared to Mo14Re and Mo42Re, because 
Mo and Mo5Re are fully recrystallized. As strain rate exceeds the rate of 
dislocation movement and increment, strengthening happens at high 
strain rate in Mo and Mo5Re. However, a lot of sub-grains made by high

high density stored dislocations may be activated to mobile dislocations

compression conducted at 300

in Mo and Mo5Re, and leads recovery in Mo14Re and Mo42Re after the 
same annealing processes. In general, Mo – Re alloys with high Re con -
tent possess excellent properties, including high thermal stability, 
strength and hardening with strain rates. Peak compressive stresses 
reduce as temperature rises in Mo and Mo – Re alloys. While, strength -
ening effect of Re on Mo is justified at all test conditions. Microstructural 
stability in Mo – Re alloys is effectively enhanced via adding Re, espe -
cially at high temperature (1200 
◦
C). In most cases, an increase in strain 
rate leads to an increase in flow stresses. However, an unusual behavior 
is that Mo14Re and Mo42Re show strain rate independence at 600 
◦
C. 
Such a phenomenon may be mainly attributed to high density of stored 
dislocation that activated to mobile dislocations at a proper tempera -
ture. Furthermore, the migration of grain boundaries become slower in 
Mo14Re and Mo42Re than in Mo and Mo5Re.
