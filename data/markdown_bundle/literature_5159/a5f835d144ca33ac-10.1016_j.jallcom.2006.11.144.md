The microstructure and properties of Ti–Mo–Nb
alloys for biomedical application
L.J. Xu∗, Y .Y . Chen, Zh.G. Liu, F.T. Kong
School of Materials Science and Engineering, Harbin Institute of Technology, Harbin 150001, PR China
Received 23 May 2006; received in revised form 16 November 2006; accepted 16 November 2006
Available online 22 December 2006
Abstract
A group of Ti–10Mo–nNb alloys (n = 3, 7, 10) were prepared using arc melting and graphite mould casting. When n = 3, the as-prepared alloy
had a composite microstructure containing an equiaxed /H9252-Ti phase dispersed in a /H9251-Ti matrix. When n = 7 and 10, the as-prepared alloys had
microstructure only containing equiaxed /H9252-Ti phase. The equiaxed crystal grain of Ti–10Mo–10Nb alloy was obviously ﬁner. These new alloys
exhibited high Vickers hardness values of 394–441 HV , high compression strength of 1717–1918 MPa, high yield strength of 1404–1854 MPa,
and low Young’s modulus in the range of 24.7–28.4 GPa, together with large plastic strains. The bending strength of Ti–Mo–Nb alloys was in the
range of 701–1489 MPa. The combination of high strength and low elastic modulus of Ti–Mo–Nb alloys offer potential advantages in biomedical
applications.

1. Introduction
Since, the population ratio of the aged people is rapidly grow-
ing, the amount of usage of instruments for replacing failed
hard tissues such as artiﬁcial hip joints, dental implants, etc. is
increasing among the aged people. Metallic biomaterials such
as stainless steels, Co based alloys, titanium and its alloys are
the most suitable for replacing failed hard tissue up to now.
Recently, titanium alloys are getting much attention for biomate-
rials because they have excellent speciﬁc strength and corrosion
resistance, no allergic problems and the best biocompatibility
among metallic biomaterials [1–4]. Commercial pure titanium,
Ti–6Al–4V and Ti–6Al–4V ELI have basically been developed
for structural materials although they are still widely used as
representative titanium alloys for implant materials [5]. But, V
exhibits high cytotoxicity and Al may induce senile dementia
[6–7]. Therefore, V free /H9251+ /H9252type alloys such as Ti–6Al–7Nb
and Ti–5Al–2.5Fe and V and Al free /H9251+ /H9252type alloys com-
posed of non-toxic elements like Ti–15Sn–4Nb–2Ta–0.2Pd and
Ti–15Zr–4Nb–4Ta–0.2Pd have been developed [5]. Low mod-

are required to be much more similar to that of bone. The/H9252type

In general, a biomedical titanium alloys should have low den-

a low modulus, a large room-temperature plasticity and good
casting properties. By either modifying the available titanium
alloys or exploring new compositions, one can achieve better
performance of the titanium alloys in terms of their biomedical
and mechanical properties or workability[9]. Nb, Ta, Zr, Mo and
Sn are selected as safe alloying elements to titanium, which are
judged to be non-toxic and non-allergic [4]. The isomorphous
stabilizers such as Mo, Nb and Ta can improve strength, as well
as plasticity [9,10].
In this paper, we discuss a group of new Ti–Mo–Nb alloys.
The new alloys have advantages in their very high strength and
low elastic modulus, as well as large plasticity. They may there-
fore have potential in biomedical applications.

Since, cast pores could form in Ti–Mo–Nb alloys, the bending

The Ti–Mo–Nb alloys exhibited a typical brittleness fracture
behavior. The bending strength of Ti–10Mo–3Nb alloy was
701 MPa, that of Ti–10Mo–7Nb alloy was 818 MPa and that
of Ti–10Mo–10Nb was 1489 MPa. The bending strength of
Ti–10Mo–10Nb was higher than that of c.p.Ti (884 MPa) and
lower than that of Ti–6Al–4V (1860 MPa)[10]. For an easy com-
parison, all the compressive test data and the bending strength
values for the three alloys are listed in Table 1.
For the as-cast Ti–Mo–Nb alloys, the compressive strength
and yield strength reduced with increasing Nb content. The plas-
tic strain and Young’s modulus improved. It is well known that
Mo and Nb are /H9252-phase stabilizer. By adding such elements,
the primary solidiﬁcation can be shifted from the /H9251case to the
/H9252case. With increasing Mo and Nb contents, the /H9252-Ti phase
increased [9]. Due to the high plasticity of the /H9252-phase struc-
ture and the low elastic modulus of the /H9252-type titanium alloys
[10,11], the Ti–10Mo–10Nb alloy has the lowest elastic mod-
ulus and the highest plasticity. The isomorphous stabilizers of
Mo and Nb can improve strength, as well as plasticity [9,10].
Furthermore, the strength and plasticity is a relation to the
microstructure of Ti–Mo–Nb alloys. The ﬁner microstructure
of the Ti–10Mo–10Nb is propitious to the properties improve-
ment [9]. Since, cast pores can form in the alloys, the tensile
plasticity, which is very sensitive to the cast ﬂaws, will be lower
than that observed in compression tests. In applications under
large tensile stresses, the casting methods for the present alloy
need to be improved to avoid the cast defects. More work is
needed in this direction and is beyond the scope of this report
[9].

Fig. 6shows SEM micrographs of the fractured surfaces of the
specimens after compression testing of the Ti–Mo–Nb alloys.
The cleavage planes, shear cracks and trace of the crack propa-
gation through the ﬁne grain structures could be detected on the
fracture surfaces of Ti–10Mo–3Nb and Ti–10Mo–7Nb alloys

Because the plasticity of the alloy was the highest, the com-
pression specimen did not rupture (Fig. 6 (c)).
Fig. 7 shows SEM micrographs of the fractured surfaces of
the specimens after bending testing of the Ti–Mo–Nb alloys. In
both cases the fracture was clearly affected by the crystal grain
size. Fig. 7(a) was the fracture micrograph of the Ti–10Mo–3Nb
alloy and the cleavage-like morphology showing the river pat-
tern and fan pattern that are characters of many brittle materials
that dominated the fracture surface. The fan pattern, ﬂake pat-
tern and river patterns could be seen on the fracture surface of
Ti–10Mo–7Nb alloy ( Fig. 7 (b)). Some microcracks could be
seen in Fig. 7 (a) and (b), which are denoted by black arrows.
Secondary dimple, obvious long facets and microcracks were
observed on the fracture surface of Ti–10Mo–10Nb alloy (Fig. 7
(c)), which are denoted by black arrows.
4. Conclusions
With addition of Mo and Nb, equiaxed microstructure had
been achieved. The equiaxed /H9252-phase and the /H9251-phase matrix
could be observed in Ti–10Mo–3Nb alloy. For Ti–10Mo–7Nb
and Ti–10Mo–10Nb alloys, the equiaxed /H9252-phase became the
dominant phase.
All the Ti–Mo–Nb alloys had much higher Vickers hardness
values (394–441 HV). Ti–Mo–Nb alloys exhibited high com-

The very low elastic modulus of Ti–Mo–Nb alloys (the Young’s

Such combined properties, make these new alloys promising
candidates as biomedical materials.
The fracture surfaces of the compression specimens of
Ti–10Mo–3Nb and Ti–10Mo–7Nb alloys indicated some cleav-
age planes and cracks, and the compression specimen of
Ti–10Mo–10Nb alloy did not rupture. The Ti–Mo–Nb alloys
exhibited a typical brittleness fracture behavior. The fan pat-
tern, river pattern and microcracks could be seen in the fracture
surfaces of the bending specimens of Ti–Mo–Nb alloys.
