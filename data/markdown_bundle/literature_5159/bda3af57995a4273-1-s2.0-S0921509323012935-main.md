ABSTRACT 
The subcritical crack growth behavior under sustained loading and its microscopic mechanism in < 100 > ori -
ented single crystal (SC) Ni-based superalloys subjected to a relatively low temperature condition (500 
◦
C) are 
critically investigated. An evident time-dependent crack growth (TDCG) phenomenon is observed even at such a 
temperature condition, but its rate (d a /d t ) is significantly small and weakly dependent on the crack length, i.e. 
the stress intensity ( K ) value. The cross-sectional observation of a crack tip region by electron microscopy (SEM/ 
TEM), together with the conventional fractography, reveals the following points: the macroscopic crack path 
shows pronounced branching influenced by the {100} planes; most dislocations in the γ’ phase near the crack tip 
are rectilinearly aligned and locked on the {100} planes; the crack tip dislocations provide the microscopically 
preferable oxidation paths. These points strongly suggest that the dislocation-mediated micro-oxidation process 
plays a major role in the observed anisotropic TDCG mechanism that eventually leads to the anomalous d a /d t - K 
property. 
1. Introduction 
The growing demand for the higher temperature operation of gas 
turbine generators has been supported by the use of high-performance 
heat-resistant alloys as their blade materials. Single crystalline (SC) 
nickel (Ni)-based superalloys have become the key blade materials for 
today ’ s commercial generators owing to their superior creep resistance 
[ 1 , 2 ]. In order to ensure the safe operation of the generators, it is pivotal 
to predict the failure life of the blades subjected to various mechanical 
and/or thermal conditions. In practice, however, this is a rather difficult 
task as it is not possible to directly monitor blade damage evolution 
during the operation period. Instead, an out-of-service inspection con -
firms the existence of various subcritical cracks at various blade posi -
tions. The microscopic characterization of such cracks together with the 
simulation of local stress/temperature history would thus be the first 
step to resolve the issue. At the same time, it is also important to 
experimentally simulate the subcritical cracks under designated condi -
tions and elucidate their properties, which will strongly facilitate the 
characterization process. 
Recently, subcritical cracks (several to 10 mm in length) were found 
in some parts of SC turbine blades exposed to a relatively low temper -
ature condition ( ≈ 500 
◦
C). What intrigued us most was that this turbine, 
which was one of the newest (therefore high-efficient) power genera -
tors, had not experienced severe on/off sequences during its operation 
period of far over 5000 h. The cracks found were, therefore, very likely 
to be caused under a long-term sustained load condition unlike those 
found in conventional turbines frequently subjected to severe on/off 
sequences. Moreover, the apparent growth rate of those cracks was 
estimated to be significantly small (on the order of 10
 10 
m/s or less). 
Such a phenomenon per se was quite a unique one, which strongly 
motivated the authors to pursue the time-dependent crack growth 
(TDCG) properties in Ni-based SC superalloys, particularly in the low 
temperature regime. 
As with other materials for high-temperature use, studies on the 
fracture of SC superalloys were initiated in connection with their creep 
deformation. An early study by Nathal and Ebert [ 3 ] showed that cracks 
nucleate from pre-existing defects (micropores) in a SC superalloy 
(NASAIR100) during the tertiary creep stage under a relatively

2. Materials and methods 
Two types of SC alloys (A and B) are used in this study. Table 1 lists 
their chemical composition. Alloy “ A ” is a typical second-generation SC

matrix phase. The Re free alloy “ B ” is a modified version of alloy A: the 
absence of the beneficial effect of Re at high temperature regime [ 12 , 13 ]

Δ K values of 28/36 MPa m
1/2

The specimen temperature was kept at 500 
◦

3.1. Crack growth behavior 
Fig. 2 shows the results of sustained load tests. Both alloys clearly 
show TDCG behavior in 500 
◦
C as shown in Fig. 2 (a). The TDCG rate 
(d a /d t ) of alloy B is higher than alloy A until the end of the test. This is 
represented in Fig. 2 (b) with regards to K value. The TDCG rate of alloy 
A, which repeats ups/downs in the 10

dependent on K value within the present test range. Note that the test of 
alloy A was interrupted at 4500 h ( K = 46 MPa m
1/2

specimen was statically fractured to reveal the cracked plane for a 
fractographic analysis. The TDCG rate of alloy B, on the other hand,

10

3.2. Microscopic observations 
3.2.1. Alloy A 
Fig. 3 compares the cross-section (at mid-thickness position) of an 
interrupted crack and the cracked surface of alloy A. The main crack 
path for TDCG region is basically normal to the tensile axis (i.e. mode I) 
while it also shows an eminent branching almost parallel to the tensile 
axis, see Fig. 3 (a). The fractography shown in Fig. 3 (b) reveals that the 
overall TDCG region extends in fan shape and most of its front line still 
resides inside the specimen. The magnified image, showing the area of 
similar K level as Fig. 3 (a), also reveals the existence of subcracks that 
form cuboidal facets. It appears that such facets are formed as the result 
of the cuboidal branching shown in Fig. 3 (a). Note that {100}-oriented 
facets observed in creep fractured SRR99 at 750 
◦

formed in combination with pre-existing micropores [ 5 , 6 ], which should 
be distinguished from the present phenomenon.

4. Discussion 
The TDCG in alloys A and B at this temperature (500 
◦
C) has been 
shown to accompany no microstructural damages such as the formation 
of twin-induced shear bands, the precipitation of topologically close- 
packed (TCP) phase, micro-voids in the γ channels and dendrite 
boundary cracking, all of which cooperatively contribute to the TDCG at 
a severer condition (900 
◦
C), see Ref. [ 11 ]. The present crack tip region 
would rather be in the primary creep state (i.e. low temperature/high 
stress condition) if the usual deformation classification were applied. 
Unlike other studies [ 16 – 18 ], however, scarcely any planar defects 
(stacking faults) are found to extend in the γ’ phase: highly populated 
yet discernible dislocations reside instead. These dislocations, along 
with those in the γ matrix, may carry the creep strain around the crack 
tip but not likely to cause fully plastic rupturing. In other words, the 
main process of the current TDCG is expected to be dimensionally much 
more limited. 
An alternative (and probably the most viable) crack tip mechanism is 
the thermal oxidation followed by brittle oxide failure. An estimation on 
the oxide film formation of pure (unalloyed) bulk Ni using reported data 
[ 19 ] yields 20 nm/s under 500 
◦

The distribution of oxygen at the crack tip region shown in Fig. 5 (b) 
suggests that the oxidation in the γ ′ phase does not proceed homoge -
nously. It is strongly affected by the distribution of dislocations in the γ ′ 
phase. The trace of these dislocations is similar to those imaged in Fig. 5 
(a) and 7 . In general, a dislocation (or in a specific terminology, 
superdislocation) on the {111} plane of an ordered alloy with the L1
2 
structure is normally dissociated into two separate superpartials with a 
planer defect (anti-phase boundary; APB) in between. Since the APB 
energy on the {111} plane is significantly high, a superdislocation of the 
screw type tends to cross-slip onto the {100} plane having a very low 
APB energy at the expense of its mobility. This type of locking 
mechanism, termed “ K – W lock ” after Kear and Wilsdorf [ 21 ], has been 
studied in terms of the yield stress anomalies of L1
2 
ordered alloys as 
reviewed in Refs. [ 22 – 24 ]. The present rectilinear screw dislocations 
apparently oriented to the < 100 > / < 110 > directions in the γ ′ phase can 
thus be attributed to the locked ones on the {100} planes. These sessile 
dislocations effectively provide short circuits for oxygen diffusion in the 
γ ′ phase and as a result the main crack path along the {100} planes. Here 
we show an additional dislocation observation in Fig. 9 . Note that this 
image was taken at a remote position (c.a. 8 μ m from the crack) for a 
better visibility plus a group of dislocations having a certain Burgers 
vector was made invisible with the shown diffraction condition. It 
confirms some segments connecting different K – W locks, which is 
similar to those reported in literatures [ 23 , 25 – 27 ]. These segments may 
also provide winding pathway for γ’ oxidation although they seem to be 
less frequent than the locked segments. Concerning the γ matrix phase, 
its oxidation may also be assisted by the normal (i.e. glissile) disloca -
tions while its impact on the overall crack path is probably minor owing 
to channel width shortness.

TDCG pattern shown in Fig. 10 (b) represents the vertical pattern which 
is parallel to the (010) plane. No stress components (tensile or shear) 
should exist on this plane if a simple uncracked bar specimen were 
subjected to the [100] tension. In the present case, however, stress field 
at the crack tip is more complex: both tensile and shear components exist 
on the (010) plane. Once a small embryonic subcrack exceeds a critical 
dimension, it will start growing along the (010) plane driven by its own 
crack tip stress intensity, K . Note that the local K value at the vertical 
subcrack tip is speculated to be smaller than that at the main mode I 
crack [ 28 ]. The vertical subcrack, therefore, will eventually be arrested 
unless it undergoes another horizontal deflection and acquires a larger 
mode I driving force. Or, in a rare situation where such a regain occurs in 
an extreme fashion, a subcrack can even turn to the main crack that 
leaves cuboidal facets on the cracked surface ( Fig. 3 (b)). 
Our discussions based on the local oxidation model in relation to 
micro slip modes have so far elucidated the observed TDCG to some 
extent, particularly from a phenomenological viewpoint. The different 
TDCG rates of alloys A and B ( Fig. 2 (b)) may then be partly attributed to 
their local susceptibility to oxidation that depends on factors like 
chemical composition of γ / γ ′ phases, dislocation core width in relation 
to the chemical composition and slip mode (dislocation distribution and 
density) in the γ’ phase.

weak dependency of d a /d t to the applied K level apparently goes against 
such theory. In fact, a similar phenomenon is well known for the TDCG 
of metals in corrosive environments, i.e. stress corrosion cracking (SCC). 
The plateau behavior in the d a /d t - K diagram (so-called stage II regime) 
of SCC has been correlated to the emergence of crack branching, see e.g. 
refs [ 30 – 32 ]. From a mechanical viewpoint, such a phenomenon has 
been explained by focusing on the local K property of a bifurcated crack 
[ 28 ]. After losing K value by a branching event, each branched crack 
gradually grows again and restore its own local (microscopic) K value 
until it is lost again by the next branching. When such a discrete crack 
growth is viewed macroscopically, average growth rate appears to stay 
rather constant despite the total crack length monotonically increases. 
The above concept thus helps providing a reasonable explanation on the 
weak dependency of TDCG rate on K in the present observations where 
frequent branching accompanies. The interesting analogy mentioned 
here, to the best of our knowledge, has never been reported in studies 
related to SC superalloys. The strongly anisotropic TDCG behavior 
encountered in this material will be simulated provided that the key 
factors (i.e. local dislocation density, dislocation-assisted oxygen diffu -
sion, stress-induced oxide film growth and its failure criterion) are 
appropriately considered. 
Before closing this paper, we would like to add a brief note on the 
effect of creep. Although our TEM observations did not suggest any 
definitive creep-related dislocation structures, we still cannot 
completely exclude the possible creep effect on the TDCG behavior 
shown in Fig. 2 . If the absence of Re in alloy B markedly enhances the 
self-diffusivity in the γ matrix phase and hence the dislocation activity 
therein, it may accelerate TDCG when compared to alloy A. The validity 
of such an effect at the present low temperature (500 
◦
C) is however 
unclear, which should be further discussed with carefully evaluated 
creep data of the present materials. 
5. Summary 
The subcritical crack growth behavior and its microscopic 
mechanism in < 100 > oriented single crystal (SC) Ni-based superalloys 
exposed to a relatively low temperature condition (500 
◦
C) in air were 
critically investigated. In addition to the conventional fractography, the 
cross-section of crack tip region obtained from an interrupted test 
specimen was also analyzed in detail by SEM/TEM methods. The main 
results obtained are summarized as follows. 
(1) When subjected to an initial stress intensity factor ( K ) of 40 MPa 
m
1/2
, time-dependent crack growth (TDCG) was clearly observed 
in two alloy types, A and B, having different chemical composi -
tions. The TDCG rate (d a /d t ) of both alloys were found to be 
significantly small (not more than the order of 10
 10 
m/s) and 
weakly dependent on the crack length, i.e. the K value. 
(2) The macroscopic TDCG paths showed significant branching. They 
were mainly influenced by the {100} planes, forming even a 
vertical branching.
