ABSTRACT 
Owing to the limited available slip systems and the weak bonding force at grain boundaries, the 
ductility of intermetallic alloys is usually poor at room temperature and brittle fracture occurs 
readily. This study fabricated Ni-Co-Fe–Al-Ti chemically complex intermetallic alloys (CCIMAs) using 
the laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) method, which has outstanding mechanical properties. 
Furthermore, the effects of hot isostatic pressing (HIP) and heat treatment (HT) on the 
microstructure and mechanical properties of the as-built Ni-Co-Fe–Al-Ti were investigated. The as- 
built samples illustrated L1
2
-based matrix decorated by nanoscale B2 phase. High-density cell 
structures composed of a dislocation network can also be identified. The as-built CCIMA showed 
excellent yield strength of 1030.67 ± 13.00 MPa, tensile strength of 1426.80 ± 8.30 MPa and 
elongation of 11.63 ± 0.68%. A disordered interface can be formed through tailored post-treatment. 
In this way, the yield strength decreased slightly and tensile strength remained at a similar level, 
but elongation increased 1.7 times compared to the as-built alloy. The current study illustrated a 
feasible approach to prepare a strong yet ductile intermetallic alloy through additive manufacturing.

Introduction
As compared with solid-solution alloys, intermetallic 
alloys demonstrate unique physico-chemical properties, 
including high strength and hardness, excellent high- 
temperature resistance, good corrosion resistance, and 
electrical conductivity. Consequently, they are widely 
used in aerospace, automotive, electronic devices and 
other fields [ 1–3 ]. Nevertheless, owing to the limited 
available slip systems and the weak bonding force at 
grain boundaries, intermetallic alloys exhibit poor duct -
ility at room temperature and are prone to brittle frac -
ture [ 1 , 4–6 ]. Further optimisation of traditional 
intermetallic compounds with a simple composition is 
difficult to further enhance the properties. Recently, a 
new design concept of the multi-component alloys pro -
vides a promising strategy to overcome the thorny 
problem [ 7 ]. For example, face-centred cubic (FCC) 
multi-component alloys, such as CoCrFeNi and CoCrFe -
NiMn alloys, demonstrate good plasticity and unique 
strength-ductility synergy at cryogenic temperatures 
[ 8–10 ]. Such multi-principal component design 
concept can be implemented into the design of interme -
tallic alloys; consequently, chemically complex interme -
tallic alloys (CCIMAs, also referred to as multi- 
component intermetallic alloys or high-entropy interme -
tallic alloys) came into being. Yang et al. [ 2 ] studied the 
strength-plasticity mechanism of the Ni

the same time, the co-segregation of elements at grain

With the continuous development of aerospace tech -
nology, the demand for complex-shaped parts used in 
high-temperature environments has increased dramati -
cally [ 14 ]. Laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF), a leading 
additive manufacturing technology, enables the fabrica -
tion of complex geometries without design constraints 
while offering advantages such as material efficiency 
and cost reduction, making it a widely adopted solution 
in aerospace engineering. For example, the lattice struc -
ture of L-PBFed Inconel 718 nickel-based superalloy 
demonstrates good mechanical properties and can be 
used for preparing lightweight support structures, sand -
wich panels and high-temperature filters in aerospace, 
automotive industry and other fields [ 15 ]. It should be 
noted that, to retain a highly ordered structure, 
CCIMAs often contain high Al and Ti contents, which 
are regarded as ordered L12-stabilizing elements [ 1 ]. 
However, when the content of Al and Ti exceeds 4.5 
wt.%, the alloys are classified as ‘difficult to weld’ 
alloys [ 14 ]. Since additive manufacturing process experi -
ences the ‘repeated welding process’, so how to control 
the number of defects in the additive manufacturing 
process is a critical issue. Currently, hot isostatic pressing 
(HIP) is an effective method to improve internal defects 
of alloys. Its working principle is that under the 
combined high temperature and high pressure, the 
material is uniformly pressed in all directions, thereby 
increasing the density of the material [ 14 , 16 ]. Even for 
parts with complex geometric shapes such as lattice 
structures, HIP can effectively improve the density 
while maintaining the original shape of the parts [ 17 , 
18 ]. Additionally, the L-PBF process achieves the 
highest dimensional resolution among metal additive 
manufacturing technologies, as evidenced by its capa -
bility to fabricate features with sub-100 μm accuracy, 
surpassing both laser metal deposition (LMD) and elec -
tron beam selective melting (EBSM) [ 19 , 20 ]. This 
superior resolution stems from the synergistic effect of 
a finely focused laser spot and the inherent stability of 
the powder bed fusion mechanism, which collectively 
enable the precise fabrication of intricate geometries, 
such as components with internal cooling channels [ 21 ].

Therefore, in the present study, quinary chemically

by L-PBF. The effects of various post-treatments (e.g. HIP 
and heat treatment [HT]) on the defects and microstruc -
ture evolution of the L-PBFed alloy were investigated. 
We carefully examined the relationship between proces -
sing, microstructure, interfacial segregation, mechanical 
properties and deformation mechanisms, with the aim 
of improving the comprehensive mechanical properties 
of the L-PBFed CCIMA.

Pores and defects analyses
Since the essence of the L-PBF manufacturing process is 
similar to that of overlay welding, the weldability of the 
alloy is evaluated before L-PBF manufacturing. Generally 
speaking, when the Al + Ti composition exceeds 4.5 
wt.%, the weldability is poor [ 25 , 26 ]. However, to main -
tain the intermetallic matrix, the Al and Ti content reach 
a high value for inducing intermetallic phase formation 
in the current work, exceeding 10 wt.%. As shown in 
Figure 3 (a), although the L-PBF processing parameters 
are fully optimised, the intrinsic L-PBF thermal cycle 
with fast heating and cooling process inevitably 
induces minor cracks in the as-built sample. HIP served 
as a post-treatment process for crack healing without 
modifying the initial alloy composition. After HIP, no 
cracks were observed in the HIP sample ( Figure 3 (b,c)), 
and the number of pores was significantly reduced, 
which suggests that the HIP can effectively inhibit the 
defects.

sample
In Figure 8 , it can be seen that after HIP, the matrix phases 
are decorated with some isolated precipitates. Similar pre -
cipitates can also be seen in HIP + HT sample, indicating 
that the precipitates will not dissolve into the matrix 
upon HT at 1200℃. SEM-EDS analysis was performed 
on the precipitates, and the results are shown in Figure 
8 (b). It can be seen that the precipitates witness the 
enrichment of the Al element. According to our previous 
XRD patterns ( Figure 4 ), these precipitates are identified 
as Al-rich B2 phase. It is worth noting that there is 
different elemental enrichment in the B2 phase of the 
as-built specimen compared with those in the HIP and 
HIP + HT specimens. Elements Al and Ti can occupy the 
same sub-lattice in the B2-type crystal structure. Com -
bined with the research of Yang et al. [ 2 ], the B2 phase 
can be identified as the (Ni, Co, Fe)(Al, Ti, Fe) phase. In 
the as-built specimen, due to the rapid heating and 
cooling characteristics, the Ti-rich B2 phase formed as a 
result of the non-equilibrium state. However, after HIP, 
elements were redistributed and moved towards the 
equilibrium state, B2 phase appeared to be Al-rich.
To determine the effects of post-treatment on the 
grain structure, EBSD analysis was performed on the 
HIP and HIP + HT specimens. As shown in Figure 9 , 
columnar crystals growing along the building direction 
are observed in all three specimens. We speculate that 
the HIP fails to trigger grain recrystallisation within the 
as-built CCIMA; therefore, the grain morphology 
remains columnar after HIP. The average grain size of 
the samples is measured on the XY plane, and the 
average grain size of the as-built, HIP and HIP + HT speci -
mens is 21.91, 21.58 and 22.85 μm, respectively, indicat -
ing almost no grain growth after HIP and HT. All the 
above results suggest superior microstructural stability 
of the L1
2
-type intermetallic-based matrix. It should be 
noted that the grain size of the matrix phase does not 
change after HIP and HT; however, we notice significant 
B2 phase growth during HT. As shown in Figure 9 , B2 
particles can be clearly identified in HIP and HIP + HT 
samples via EBSD, which can be attributed to the 
notable particle coarsening during post-treatment. 
Moreover, the B2 phase mainly located along the high- 
angle grain boundaries (HAGBs), which can be possibly 
attributed to the evolved localised chemical compo -
sition and fast solute diffusion at the grain boundaries.

Tensile properties
Figure 13 (a) illustrates the typical stress–strain curves and

Discussion
Evolution of microstructure in the as-built, HIP 
and HIP + HT specimens
After careful parameter optimisation, we still could not 
fully eliminate cracks in the as-built CCIMA. Therefore, 
we utilised HIP to control the defects within the as- 
built CCIMA, resulting in greatly reduced cracks and 
holes after HIP. Moreover, through carefully tuned 
cooling rate upon post-treatment, the specimens also 
exhibited distinctively different grain morphology and 
grain boundary segregation profiles. The altered grain 
morphology and grain boundary segregation have a 
critical effect on the mechanical properties of the 
CCIMA [ 2 , 5 ], which prompted a detailed investigation 
into the microstructure and segregation profiles 
( Figure 11 ).
In the as-built specimen, the main structural charac -
teristics are the uniform distribution of cellular structure 
and the B2 precipitates at the cell boundary. The L-PBF 
process features rapid heating and cooling, in general, 
the cooling rate of L-PBF technology can reach as high 
as 10

rate and a large degree of supercooling, elements do 
not have enough time to diffuse fully during the

the alloy is governed by the temperature gradient (G) 
and solidification rate (R). In the L-PBF process, the 
growth of primary dendrites is promoted, while the 
very short solidification time inhibits the formation of sec -
ondary dendrites, resulting in the formation of nanoscale 
cellular structures [ 41 , 42 ]. TEM also shows that there is a 
network dislocation structure in L-PBFed Ni-Co-Fe–Al-Ti 
CCIMA, which is similar to cellular substructures observed 
in SEM, indicating that the formation of this substructure 
is related to dislocation entanglement. Furthermore, the 
rapid cooling rate causes the microscopic segregation 
of solute atoms before solidification, so the distribution 
of Ti elements along the cell boundary is observed in 
the as-built specimen. Additionally, the temperature gra -
dient direction during the L-PBF process is along the 
building direction, leading to preferential grain growth 
along this direction. Owing to the layer-by-layer proces -
sing characteristics, the energy input of the laser 
enables dendrites to grow through multiple molten 
pools, forming columnar crystals. The temperature gradi -
ent difference between the molten pool boundary and 
the molten pool centre also causes the columnar crystal 
morphology to deviate from the building direction by a 
certain angle [ 43 ].
After HIP, the defects in the alloy almost disappear, 
and the dislocation density also decreases significantly. 
However, cellular structures remain inside both the HIP 
and HIP + HT specimens, and the shape of the grains 
hardly changes, indicating that no recrystallisation 
occurs at 1200℃. In addition, due to the fast diffusion

HAGB grows significantly and is distributed in the form

Evolution of mechanical properties in the as- 
built, HIP and HIP + HT samples
The Ni-Co-Fe–Al-Ti CCIMA prepared by L-PBF exhibits a 
high yield strength, primarily attributed to the L-PBF pro -
cess’s ability to generate high-density dislocations. 
However, the room temperature brittleness of the inter -
metallic compound and the rapid heating and cooling 
process characteristics of the L-PBF process lead to 
cracks inside the as-built specimen. Therefore, this 
study employs the HIP process to mitigate defects 
such as cracks and pores in the alloy.
Driven by the reduced interfacial energy, the Fe and 
Co elements in the alloy tend to segregate to the grain 
boundaries to minimise the system’s free energy. Slow 
cooling after HIP in the HIP specimen, however, 
weakens this segregation tendency, reducing Fe and 
Co accumulation at HAGBs and inhibiting the L1

-to- 
FCC phase transformation. For the HIP + HT specimen, 
rapid cooling after HT promotes Fe and Co segregation 
towards the HAGBs, promoting the L1

transformation. To investigate the role of segregation- 
induced disorder in plastic deformation, systematic 
TEM observations were conducted on the HIP and HIP 
+ HT specimens. As shown in Figure 16 (a), dislocations 
in the HIP specimen are blocked by the grain bound -

HAGBs. In contrast, the HIP + HT specimen exhibits 
extensive dislocation migration across adjacent HAGBs 
( Figure 16 (c)). This suggests that segregation-induced 
grain boundary disordering enhances dislocation mobi -
lity, thereby improving the tensile ductility. Furthermore,

In summary, the as-built Ni-Co-Fe–Al-Ti CCIMA, fabri -
cated via L-PBF, witnesses high-density dislocation cells 
formation, resulting in high yield strength (1030 MPa). 
However, due to ultra-fast heating and cooling 
characteristics in the L-PBF process, notable cracks can 
be found among the as-printed specimens, whose pres -

tuned out that HIP post-processing successfully elimin -

structure, whereas HIP + HT further induces Fe/Co segre -

boundaries, enhancing dislocation mobility and signifi -
cantly enhancing the tensile ductility. This ductility 
improvement correlates with a shift from intergranular 
fracture (HIP) to dimpled fracture (HIP + HT), which is 
governed by the localised grain boundary chemistry. 
Ultimately, the synergistic L-PBF-induced dislocation 
cell, HIP-driven defect healing and HT-mediated grain 
boundary disordering contribute to the CCIMA’s 
strength-ductility synergy.
Conclusions
This study focuses on L-PBF-fabricated Ni-Co-Fe–Al-Ti 
CCIMA and investigates the effects of different post- 
treatments on the microstructural evolution and mech -
anical properties of the alloy. The main conclusions are 
as follows: 
(1) The as-built Ni-Co-Fe–Al-Ti CCIMAs witness minor 
crack formation due to the high Al and Ti content 
of the alloy and the unique fast heating and 
cooling process of L-PBF. After HIP, the cracks in 
the alloy were closed, indicating that HIP can effec -
tively reduce the number of defects in L-PBFed Ni- 
Co-Fe–Al-Ti CCIMA.
(2) Both the HIP and HIP + HT specimens exhibit the L1
2 
matrix, minor B2 phase and FCC phase. Notably, the 
HIP + HT specimen demonstrates stronger Fe and Co 
grain boundary segregation and a distinct order-to- 
disorder transition, whereas the HIP specimen 
retained an ordered grain boundary structure due 
to constrained elemental segregation.
(3) Owing to the formation of high-density dislocation 
cells, the as-built specimen demonstrates higher 
yield strength than HIP and HIP + HT specimens, 
reaching a yield strength of 1030.67 ± 13.00 MPa 
and an ultimate tensile strength of 1426.80 ± 8.30 
MPa. The HIP + HT specimen achieved an elongation 
of 20.17 ± 1.98%, 1.7 times higher than that of the 
as-built specimen. This improvement can be 
ascribed to the grain boundary order-to-disorder 
transition, which facilitates dislocation movement 
across adjacent grains and reduces stress 
concentration.
(4) The fracture surfaces of the HIP specimen show a 
large number of intergranular tearing edges,

trast, the HIP + HT specimen exhibits abundant
