ABSTRACT
Additive manufacturing (AM) of nickel-based superalloys has been widely 
researched for its immense potential in the aerospace industry for producing 
near-net complex geometries for elevated temperature applications. This review 
critically examines the microstructural evolution during the different AM pro-
cesses and its impact on creep deformation behaviour. Critical insights drawn 
from various AM Ni-base superalloys IN718, IN625, IN738LC, CM247LC, and 
CMSX-4 are presented to establish common trends and distinct behaviour vis-à-
vis cast/wrought counterparts. The intrinsic microstructural features developed 
during the AM process, such as columnar grain morphology, strong < 001 > crys-
tallographic texture, chemical and crystal microsegregation, and fine cellular dis-
location networks, result in a creep behaviour deviating from the conventionally 
processed counterparts. Despite promising creep resistance in vertical build speci-
mens, the precipitation hardenable Ni-base superalloys exhibit a strong orienta-
tion dependence, with several AM microstructural features contributing to the 
creep anisotropy. A critical understanding from this review can be translated 
into tailoring AM processes and post-processing strategies to enhance the creep 
resistance in additively manufactured Ni-base superalloys.

Due to their exceptional creep and oxidation resist-
ance, Ni-base superalloys have been the workhorse 
materials for various high-temperature components. 
In modern aircraft engines, they account for approxi-
mately 50% of the total weight [1 , 2]. Traditionally, 
these components are fabricated using primary (cast-
ing) and secondary processes like hot forging and/or 
rolling, followed by extensive machining to achieve 
the final shape. However, these conventional fabri-
cation methods limit the development of novel and 
more efficient structural designs, and new superalloy 
compositions that could enhance the turbine engine 
efficiency [3, 4].
Additive manufacturing (AM) has emerged as a 
promising technique to overcome the shortcomings of 
conventional manufacturing, enabling near-net-shape 
fabrication and tailoring microstructures for site-spe-
cific properties [5 , 6]. Over the past decade, signifi-
cant efforts have been made to develop AM methods, 
optimizing processing conditions to achieve a sound 
product [7 –12] and establishing the structure–prop-
erty relationships at both room and elevated tem-
peratures [13– 19]. Microstructural evolution during 
AM is highly dependent on alloy composition and 
the specific AM technique utilized. Consequently, the 
post processing (heat treatment) schedules are tailored 
to optimize the performance of AM materials.
Several articles have reviewed the current state of 
AM for Ni-base superalloys and their properties [20, 
21]. For instance, reviews by Sanchez et al. Adomako 
et al. Raza et al. and Zheng et al. examined the impact 
of processing parameters on the mechanical proper-
ties, analysing the structure–property relationships 
in as-built and heat-treated conditions, primarily in 
the quasi-static regime, predominantly tested at room 
temperature [ 22–25]. However, only a few studies 
have addressed the elevated temperature deforma-
tion behaviour of these materials. While Bryndza et al. 
reviewed the creep behaviour of AM IN718 (a widely 
studied Ni-base superalloy), their work did not fully 
account for the AM-specific microstructural features, 
such as grain morphology, crystallographic texture, 
and process-induced chemical heterogeneities, that 
significantly influence creep deformation [26].
Each Ni-base superalloy exhibits a distinct micro-
structural evolution during AM because of its alloy 
chemistry and microstructure, which necessitates the 
understanding of creep mechanisms on a case-by-
case basis. Despite the growing number of experi-
mental creep studies in recent years, a comprehensive 
understanding that connects processing, microstruc-
ture, and creep deformation behaviour across dif-
ferent AM-fabricated Ni-base superalloys is absent 
in the literature. This review aims to fill this gap by 
systematically analysing the interplay between alloy-
specific AM-induced microstructural characteris-
tics and creep behaviour. It provides a comparative 
perspective across a few Ni-base superalloys and 
AM technologies and identifies key microstructural 
parameters influencing creep behaviour. Also, we aim 
to highlight unresolved challenges and opportunities 
for future research in designing AM superalloys for 
high-temperature applications.
This article is organized as follows: “Microstructure 
and its evolution in AM Ni-base superalloys” Section 
provides an overview of the AM techniques used 
for Ni-base superalloys and the influence of process 
parameters on the formation of distinct microstruc -
tural features, such as cellular structures, elemental 
segregation, crystallographic texture, and phase distri-
bution. "Creep behaviour of AM Ni-base superalloys" 
Section provides a critical review of the creep behav-
iour and deformation mechanisms in AM Ni-base 
superalloys, drawing comparisons with convention-
ally processed counterparts. It also examines the role 
of AM-induced microstructures on creep behaviour. 
The article concludes with key insights and future 
directions for advancing research and development 
in this field.
Microstructure and its evolution in AM 
Ni‑base superalloys
Among the published research articles on additive 
manufacturing of Ni-base superalloys, nearly 98% 
have focused on AM processes using powder bed 
fusion (PBF) or directed energy deposition (DED). It 
is very well established that variation in AM process 
parameters significantly affects the thermal gradient 
and solidification rate [27– 30], thereby governing the 
macro and microstructural evolution in the as-built 
condition [31–36]. For instance, laser-based DED oper-
ates at significantly higher power levels, 300–5000 W, 
than other methods [37, 38]. Also, it often utilizes 
unidirectional and longer scan vectors with a higher 
deposition rate; together, this results in a narrow zone 
of heat accumulation and higher residual stresses 
[39]. In contrast, laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) uses 
100–1000 W and employs finer layer thicknesses and

Grain morphology and crystallographic texture 
in the as‑built condition
The grain morphology and texture characteristics of 
the AM superalloys are significantly influenced by the 
AM process, energy density, scanning strategy, and 
layer thickness. While the following discussion pre-
sents the microstructural observations on IN718, simi-
lar trends are observed in the other superalloys despite 
differences in the chemistry and phase constituents.
AM processes
Distinct microstructures are observed across the dif-
ferent AM processes for a given Ni-base superalloy.

Scanning strategy
Among several process parameters, scanning strategy 
is one of the most extensively studied yet less under-

Elemental microsegregation characteristics 
in AM‑processed Ni‑base superalloys
Microsegregation in AM-processed alloys is a conse-
quence of rapid and non-equilibrium solidification [68, 
69]. The high cooling rates (103–106 K·s−1) limit the dif-
fusion of solute elements in the solid phase, resulting 
in their rejection into the remaining liquid [70]. This 
leads to localized chemical heterogeneities at the sub-
micron scale, observed as element-rich or element-
depleted zones between cells or dendrites [71, 72]. The 
degree of segregation of a solute is characterized by its 
equilibrium partition coefficient (k). Table 1 illustrates 
the equilibrium partition coefficient for major alloying 
elements of nickel-based superalloys in nickel.
Elements with a low partition coefficient (k < 1) tend 
to segregate to interdendritic regions more promi-
nently during solidification. Figure 4 illustrates the 
spatial distribution of key solute elements in various 
LPBF-processed superalloys, as revealed by scan-
ning transmission electron microscopy coupled with 
energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (STEM-EDS). 
The respective alloys were arranged in decreasing 
order of /u1D6FE/uni2032.var strengthening phase fraction, starting with 
CM247LC (Fig. 4a) at the top, having V /u1D6FE/uni2032.var ~ 60% fol-
lowed by IN939 (Fig. 4b) with V /u1D6FE/uni2032.var ~ 33% and IN718 
(Fig. 4c) with V /u1D6FE/uni2032.var < 10%. These STEM-EDS elemental 
mapping closely aligns with the trends predicted by 
the equilibrium partition coefficient (k) values pre-
sented in Table 1. Elements with low partition coeffi-
cients, such as Nb, Mo, Ti, and Al, exhibit pronounced 
segregation along the interdendritic regions, whereas 
elements like Fe, Cr, and Co are notably depleted. It 
is noted that CM247LC shows significant Al segrega-
tion, suggesting that a critical level of supersaturation 
is required for Al to partition into the interdendritic 
regions during solidification. This segregation behav-
iour is consistently observed across various rapid 
solidification additive manufacturing processes, 
including LDED and EPBF [75– 77]. Such segregation 
frequently leads to the formation of brittle intermetal-
lic or topologically close-packed (TCP) phases or the 
Laves phase. These phases are responsible for dete-
riorating mechanical properties (creep and fatigue), 
particularly under elevated temperature service, by 
acting as crack initiation sites or by depleting the sur-
rounding matrix of strengthening elements [78– 80].
However, the elemental fraction and spatial distri-
bution of the segregated elements differ depending 
on the processing conditions. Further, this microseg-
regation can be controlled by varying scan speed in 
LPBF, thus controlling the solidification rate, and con-
sequently refining the cellular structure [84– 87]. This 
leads to reduced cell spacing and indicates suppressed 
TCP phase formation under faster cooling. However, 
such suppression is only partial and does not fully 
eliminate segregation effects. A tailored high-temper-
ature homogenization/solution heat treatment is still 
essential for complete dissolution of these elements.

Influence of heat treatment on microstructural 
evolution
The majority of the Ni-base superalloys intended 
for high-temperature structural applications rely on 
precipitation strengthening. Therefore, except for 
EPBF, which benefits from in-situ heat treatment due 
to elevated powder bed temperatures, all other AM 
processes require dedicated post-build heat treat-
ments [100, 101]. These heat treatments are critically 
necessary to dissolve both chemical and crystal seg -
regants formed during rapid solidification, as out-
lined in “Elemental microsegregation characteris -
tics in AM-processed Ni-base superalloys” Section. 
Among these, crystal segregants such as TCP phases/
Laves phase entrap the primary solute required for the 
precipitation of strengthening precipitates. The pres-
ence of these TCP/Laves phases not only reduces the 
alloy’s strength but also introduces brittle phases that 
severely deteriorate creep and fatigue behaviour. Con-
sequently, an optimized heat treatment is necessary to 
eliminate these undesirable phases, achieve chemical 
homogenization, and promote a uniform distribution 
of strengthening precipitates throughout the micro-
structure. Building on the insights gained and issues 
discussed in “Grain morphology and crystallographic 
texture in the as- built condition’’ and “Elemental 
microsegregation characteristics in AM-processed Ni-
base superalloys’’ Sections, it is evident that the as-
built microstructure varies significantly depending on 
the AM process and the specific parameters employed. 
This variation directly influences the response of the 
material to subsequent heat treatment, which will be 
discussed in the following section.

Creep behaviour of Ni‑base superalloys
Creep behaviour in Ni-base superalloys is strongly 
influenced by temperature, applied stress, and 
microstructural characteristics [109– 111]. Moham-
med–Langdon deformation mechanism maps (Ashby 
maps) highlight that, at intermediate to high tem-
peratures and stresses, dislocation-mediated mecha-
nisms (dislocation glide and climb), often referred 
to as power law creep, are dominant [112]. As stress 
decreases and the temperature increases, in the range 
of 0.6–0.9 Tₘ (where T ₘ is the melting temperature), 
diffusional creep mechanisms (Nabarro–Herring and 
Coble creep) become predominant [113, 114]. Further-
more, in polycrystalline materials, fine grains can sig-
nificantly contribute to the creep strain through grain 
boundary sliding [115, 116]. The transition between 
creep regimes reflects the underlying competition 
between dislocation activity and atomistic diffusion 
pathways, which are sensitive to the specific thermal, 
mechanical, and microstructural conditions. Over-
all, these creep mechanisms substantially govern the 
primary and secondary stages of creep, while the dam-
age mechanisms leading to failure control the tertiary 
stage [117].
Ni-base superalloys designed for intermediate- to 
high-temperature applications (~ 1100 °C) derive their 
strength from coherent, ordered /u1D6FE/uni2032.var or /u1D6FE/uni2032.var/uni2032.var precipitates 
[118, 119]. Significant efforts are made in optimizing 
the chemical composition to achieve the optimum 
phase fraction while maintaining a stable microstruc-
ture to attain the superior creep resistance. Nonethe-
less, with an increase in the precipitation fraction, the 
creep behaviour of nickel-based superalloys is more 
strongly influenced by their grain morphology.
The creep characteristics of nickel-based superal-
loys can be classified based on their grain structure 
as: polycrystalline (PC), directionally solidified (DS), 
and single-crystal (SX) forms [120, 121]. Extensive 
research has been devoted to understanding the creep 
mechanisms in a wide range of superalloys with dif-
ferent grain morphology. In polycrystalline alloys, the 
presence of randomly oriented grain boundaries aids 
grain boundary sliding and diffusion, thereby accel-
erating creep [122–124]. Directionally solidified alloys 
mitigate these effects due to preferential alignment of 
grains along the primary stress axis, thereby reducing 
grain boundary fraction and particularly transverse 
boundaries and improving creep resistance [125–127]. 
Furthermore, single-crystal alloys eliminate grain 
boundaries altogether, offering superior creep resist-
ance by removing the weakest link for deformation 
and failure.
It is important to note that creep deformation 
behaviour is extremely sensitive to initial microstruc-
ture (before subjecting to creep). Specifically, under-
standing developed from “Grain morphology and 
crystallographic texture in the as-built condition’’ and 
“Influence of heat treatment on microstructural evolu-
tion’’ Sections, the AM-processed superalloys exhibit 
a heterogeneous microstructure compared to the con-
ventional alloys. Consequently, the conventional creep 
models and mechanistic interpretations developed for 
wrought or cast Ni-base superalloys may not necessar-

Influence of crystallographic texture on creep behaviour
The crystallographic texture formed during the AM 
process plays a critical role in dictating their aniso-
tropic creep behaviour. As discussed earlier, the 
directional solidification inherent to the AM process 
typically results in elongated columnar grains with 
preferred crystallographic orientations often aligning 
⟨001⟩ directions parallel to the build axis (as discussed 
in “Grain morphology and crystallographic texture in 
the as-built condition” Section).
It can be difficult to produce materials with different 
texture strengths while keeping the other microstruc-
tural features constant. As a result, being a polycrystal-
line material, the relative contribution of the crystallo-
graphic texture compared to the other factors, such as 
grain morphology, is still not fully understood. How-
ever, one way this has been analysed is by comparing 
the alloys produced through different AM techniques.
Figure 11 illustrates the influence of crystallographic 
texture and loading direction on the creep rupture 
behaviour of IN738LC fabricated by LPBF and EPBF 
processes. The EPBF IN738LC exhibits significantly 
strong < 001 > texture (Fig. 11d: ~ 10 mud) compared to 
the LPBF, which is relatively low (Fig. 11b: ~ 1.5 mud). 
This difference is attributed to the distinct thermal his-
tories and solidification conditions inherent to each 
process, as detailed in “Microstructure and its evolu-
tion in AM Ni-base superalloys” Section. The time to 
rupture as a function of applied stress for specimens 
tested along the horizontal direction (HD), diagonal 
direction (DD), and vertical direction (VD) is shown 
in Fig. 11a, c for LPBF and EPBF, respectively. Similar 
to observations in IN718, the DD specimens consist-
ently exhibit the shortest rupture life, while the VD 
specimens show superior creep rupture life as seen 
elsewhere. In contrast, HD specimens show an inter-
mediate behaviour between VD and DD. This trend is 
consistently observed across both processing routes, 
highlighting the dominant role of grain orientation 
and texture in governing creep resistance irrespective 
of the additive manufacturing technique employed.
The influence of texture on mechanical behaviour 
is often assessed using orientation-dependent param-
eters such as the Schmid factor and Taylor factor. In 
strongly textured microstructures, grains oriented 
with low Schmid factor are expected to exhibit lim-
ited slip system activation under applied stress, result-
ing in restricted deformation, causing localized stress 
concentrations and reduced ability to accommodate 
deformation.
The Schmid factor distribution maps for HD, DD, 
and VD built specimens (Fig. 11e) show a heterogene-
ous distribution, with alternating regions of high and 
low values across adjacent grains. This suggests poten-
tial for the development of hot spots of stress concen-
tration and strain incompatibility at grain interfaces, 
which could lead to premature failure. In contrast, the 
VD specimens showed a more uniform Schmid factor 
distribution, indicating more homogeneous deforma-
tion under creep loading. Further, Kernel Average 
Misorientation (KAM) maps (Fig. 11f). reveal local-
ized strain accumulation, particularly along trans-
verse grain boundaries that separate grains of harder 
(low Schmid factor) and softer (high Schmid factor) 
orientations. This heterogeneity was absent in the VD 
specimens, which showed uniform strain distribution, 
superior creep rupture life, and ductility.

Influence of microsegregation on creep behaviour
As discussed in “Elemental microsegregation charac-
teristics in AM-processed Ni-base superalloys” Sec-
tion, microsegregation is a critical microstructural 
feature present in AM materials. In the as-built state, 
chemical segregation can significantly influence the 
subsequent phase evolution during heat treatment, as 
illustrated in Fig. 13. For instance, in the case of as-
built LPBF IN718 subjected directly to double ageing, 
bypassing solution treatment to attain higher strength 
for specific applications, the resulting microstructure 
exhibits a heterogeneous distribution of strengthen-
ing precipitates, as shown in Fig. 13a. In particular, 
the solute-rich interdendritic regions facilitate the 
dense formation of /u1D6FE/uni2032.var precipitates, whereas the solute-
depleted dendritic cores remain largely free of such 
phases, leading to the formation of precipitate-free 
zones (PFZs).
To reduce the effects of elemental segregation and 
associated mechanical heterogeneity, typically, a solu-
tion treatment is performed prior to ageing. For IN718, 
the commonly applied solution treatment condition 
(1080 °C for 1 h) often falls short of achieving com-
plete homogenization, particularly in LPBF-processed 
superalloys. Figure 13b illustrates the PFZ formation 
adjacent to the grain boundary, shown along with the 
EDS elemental mapping. The solution-treated micro-
structures still exhibit chemical heterogeneity, espe-
cially along grain boundaries where slow-diffusing 
solutes (e.g. Nb, Mo) tend to accumulate. Adjacent 
to these enriched boundaries, solute-depleted zones 
persist (Nb depletion), giving rise to PFZs (the zones 
devoid of /u1D6FE/uni2032.var/uni2032.var precipitates). It is worth noting that these 
features are not widely observed in their convention-
ally manufactured counterparts.
In addition to the direct consequences, such as 
microsegregation, the solute distribution established 
during the LPBF process also plays a critical role in 
governing carbide precipitation behaviour. The rapid 
solidification inherent to LPBF leads to the formation 
of extremely fine MC-type carbides, often below 10 nm 
in the as-built condition, which remain relatively 
small even after high-temperature heat treatments. 
These carbides, being closely spaced and enriched in 
elements like Nb, Ti, and Ta, tend to nucleate pref-
erentially near grain boundaries. This local enrich-
ment facilitates carbide growth at the expense of the 
surrounding /u1D6FE/uni2032.var-forming solutes, resulting in solute 
depletion zones adjacent to the grain boundaries as 
illustrated in Fig. 13c. Consequently, /u1D6FE/uni2032.var precipitation is 
suppressed in these regions, giving rise to pronounced 
PFZ formation, which is detrimental to the creep rup-
ture life. In contrast, conventional manufacturing 
routes like casting typically result in coarser, more 
widely spaced carbides (on the order of 5–50 µm), 
which are less likely to induce such severe solute 
redistribution or PFZ formation along grain bounda-
ries, thus benefiting the creep rupture behaviour.

ronmental effects during creep exposure have also 
been shown to significantly impact material perfor -
mance, particularly by reducing creep ductility. Pre-
mature failure can be triggered by oxidation or other 
environmental interactions at elevated temperatures, 
especially along grain boundaries, which serve as easy 
diffusion pathways for reactive species. Such degra-

across various alloy systems and are often associated 
with embrittlement phenomena. Therefore, environ-
mental degradation must be recognized as a critical 
contributing factor in creep damage evolution, along-
side intrinsic microstructural features.
Role of substructure and micro‑mechanisms on creep 
behaviour
The substructure/ cellular networks formed during 
the AM processing are shown to have a critical influ-
ence on the deformation behaviour at elevated tem-
peratures. While some alloys during supersolvus heat 
treatments exhibit extensive recrystallization, leading 
to the dissolution of these networks, others retain rem-
nants of the as-built substructure. However, a defini-
tive understanding remains elusive due to the com-
plex interplay of multiple microstructural features/
constituents and deformation mechanisms.
A study concentrating specifically on this aspect 
has been performed by Liu et al. in LPBF IN718 [137] 
where the heat treatment was tailored to retain the 
substructural remnants to understand their influence 
on creep deformation. Figure 17 illustrates the creep

ent microstructures were produced, one with the con-

of δ phase around and within the grains (referred to 
as HTA). Another heat treatment (HTB) was specifi -

erentially precipitating /u1D6FE/uni2032.var/uni2032.var (Ni3Nb) precipitates along

tion of /u1D6FE/uni2032.var/uni2032.var was attributed to heterogenous nucleation

These substructures and their associated precipita -

thereby suppressing creep strain and enhancing creep 
resistance in the HTB condition (Fig. 17d). Besides the 
improvement in creep resistance (lower minimum 
creep rate), these substructures and segregation also 
prevent the crack propagation by creating a tortuous 
crack path. This crack deflection mechanism delays 
failure, thereby contributing to the overall improve-
ment in rupture life [159]. Similar observations and 
conclusions are drawn in other studies, strengthening 
this understanding [16, 89, 159]. However, the same 
features that improve resistance to deformation also 
reduce the material’s capacity to accommodate strain, 
thereby limiting creep ductility. Thus, while the cel-
lular structure strengthens the alloy against creep, it 
introduces a trade-off between strength and ductility.

Summary and conclusions
This review presents a critical assessment of the fac -
tors influencing microstructures and their consequent 
influence on creep behaviour in additively manufac-
tured Ni-base superalloys. Based on the reviewed 
literature and comparative microstructural analyses, 
the following microstructural factors have emerged 
as key contributors to creep performance in these AM 
Ni-base superalloys.
1. Grain morphology It has a notable effect on creep 
anisotropy, especially in precipitate hardened 
superalloys. Reportedly, vertical build (VB: load-
ing along the build direction) has given better rup-
ture life than horizontal build (HB: loading per-
pendicular to the build direction) and even better 
than wrought alloys. The lower rupture life in HB 
is attributed to a higher fraction of transverse grain 
boundaries, which act as the crack initiation sites, 
followed by rapid coalescence along the straight 
path, accelerating the failure.
2. Crystallographic texture Retention of 
strong < 001 > fibre texture impacts the alignment 
of slip systems with respect to loading direction, 
therefore dictating the overall plastic deformation 
behaviour even at high temperatures. Orientation-
dependent deformation becomes prominent, as 
Taylor factor and Schmid factor analyses reveal a 
heterogeneous distribution of hard- and soft-ori -
ented grains. This mismatch in local deformation 
capability leads to strain incompatibility at grain 
interfaces, promoting localized stress accumula-
tion, cavitation, and ultimately creep failure.
3. Microsegregation Improper homogenization results 
in localized variations in chemical composition, 
which can influence the precipitation behaviour,

promoting the formation of brittle phases along 
grain boundaries and precipitate-free zones (PFZ) 
adjacent to grain boundaries or interdendritic 
regions. These phases/regions often act as stress 
concentrators, thereby accelerating damage accu-
mulation and promoting premature creep failure.
4. Grain boundary particles and inclusions Inclusions 
originating from powder contamination (impu-
rities) or processing (oxygen) are unequivocally 
detrimental. They reduce grain boundary cohe-
sion, lower boundary energy, and serve as prefer-
ential sites for void formation and crack initiation, 
whereas the presence of grain boundary particles 
plays a dual role in creep deformation. In some 
cases, second-phase particles such as carbides or 
/u1D6FF-phase precipitates effectively impede boundary 
sliding by forming serrated boundaries. This has 
shown a significant improvement in creep resist-
ance in alloys like HX and IN718. However, exces-
sive or continuous particle networks can also act 
as stress concentrators and crack nucleation sites, 
especially when they are present along the grain 
boundaries.
5. Substructure/subgrains and micro‑mechanisms The 
cellular substructures formed during additive 
manufacturing can effectively impede disloca-
tion motion, thereby enhancing creep resistance. 
In medium to high /u1D6FE/uni2032.var-strengthened alloys such as 
IN738LC and CMSX-4, the dislocation–precipi-
tate interactions largely resemble those in con-
ventionally processed counterparts. However, a 
distinct advantage of AM processing is observed 
in EPBF-processed CMSX-4, where refined initial 
/u1D6FE/uni2032.var precipitates exhibit slower coarsening compared 
to Bridgman-grown samples. This refined micro-
structure contributes to improved creep ductility 
and rupture resistance, emphasizing the critical 
role of initial microstructure in controlling creep.
Future perspectives and directions 
towards creep studies on AM Ni‑base 
superalloys
1. Despite significant progress in understanding 
creep behaviour in additively manufactured 
superalloys in the last five years, the majority of 
investigations have predominantly focused on 
alloys such as IN718 and IN625, primarily due to 
their good weldability. In contrast, better creep-
resistant yet AM processing-challenging alloys like 
IN939 and CM247LC have received comparatively 
limited attention, which warrants detailed creep 
studies on these AM superalloys.
2. A major limitation in the current literature is the 
predominance of short-term or interrupted creep 
tests. While these studies provide valuable insights 
into early-stage deformation, they often overlook 
the progressive microstructural changes that occur 
under prolonged thermal exposure. Future work 
must focus on long-duration creep testing to cap-
ture time-dependent degradation mechanisms 
such as precipitate coarsening, grain boundary 
phase evolution, and damage accumulation. This 
will be critical for developing reliable lifetime pre-
dictions of AM components in high-temperature 
creep environments.
3. Another unexplored but promising area lies in 
the study of functionally graded microstructures, 
a unique advantage offered by AM. Investigating 
creep behaviour across microstructural gradients, 
such as varying grain morphology, texture, and 
precipitate distributions within a single compo-
nent, could open new pathways for site-specific 
tailoring of microstructure in demanding high-
temperature structural applications.
4. Recent findings (Demeneghi et al.) show that 
build interruptions during AM, particularly in 
DED, can introduce localized porosity that affects 
mechanical integrity [167]. While restart protocols 
may prevent tensile degradation, their impact on 
creep performance remains unclear. Future studies 
should focus on understanding how such process 
anomalies influence creep resistance and develop-
ing strategies to ensure build continuity and integ-
rity under service conditions.
