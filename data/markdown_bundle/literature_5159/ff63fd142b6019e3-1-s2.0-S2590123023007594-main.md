1. Introduction 
Magnesium alloys are known for their lightweight, high specific 
strength, and stiffness [ 1 – 3 ]. Magnesium alloys have the potential to 
revolutionize the aviation and automotive industries. Significant weight 
reduction can be achieved by replacing heavier metallic materials and 
alloys with magnesium, leading to increased fuel efficiency and reduced 
emissions [ 4 – 8 ]. The use of magnesium alloys in vehicle parts such as 
engines, transmissions, and suspension systems can improve perfor -
mance and durability, while also decreasing the carbon footprint. 
Similarly, in aviation, the use of magnesium alloys can lead to lighter 
aircraft, higher speeds, and lower fuel consumption, ultimately resulting 
in lower operational costs and a more sustainable industry. The rise in 
magnesium use in recent years has been significantly fueled by the 
automotive industry. Magnesium and other lightweight materials offer a 
way to combat the propensity for new feature inclusion to raise vehicle 
weight. A lighter overall vehicle has better handling, acceleration, 
braking, and fuel efficiency, which reduces costs while increasing per -
formance. The use of magnesium castings in the automobile industry has 
grown significantly during the last two decades. Low elevated temper -
ature mechanical strength and poor corrosion resistance have been 
identified as two main limitations for the usage of magnesium alloys in

2. Processing and fabrication of ZA alloys 
Magnesium alloys can be utilized in wrought and cast forms, just like 
other common metals. However, the bulk is used in pressure die cast -
ings, and less than 5 % of it is used for wrought applications. This is 
partly because of magnesium ’ s exceptional casting abilities, which make 
it possible to create sophisticated components with thin walls and 
intricate cavities.

Magnesium has outstanding castability and is the world ’ s lightest metal 
that can be die-cast [ 46 ]. Therefore, compared to die-cast aluminum 
alloys, magnesium die castings can be produced with less draft, closer 
tolerances, complex shapes, higher speeds, and greater volumes from a 
well-designed die. High-pressure die casting is used to manufacture the 
bulk of applications for magnesium alloys. This is because a large pro -
duction volume at minimal manufacturing costs is feasible. Because 
liquid magnesium does not have the same attraction for iron as molten 
aluminum, magnesium alloys can be melted and retained in crucibles 
made of ferrous materials. It is the standard procedure to melt, cast, and 
handle liquid magnesium in steel crucibles and tools. Electrical furnaces 
with steel lids and melt transfer mechanisms (a mechanical pump or a 
heated transfer tube) are frequently used in modern casting operations. 
Semi-solid forming technology, according to Xu et al. [ 47 ], was 
initially proposed in the 1970s, when semi-solid slurry was made using a 
mechanical stirring approach. SSM casting eliminates a lot of the 
drawbacks associated with high-pressure die casting. This processing 
technique comes in the forms of thixomolding, rheocasting, thixocast -
ing, and thixoforming. Low porosity levels, intricate shape formation, 
and extended die life are all made possible by semi-solid metal (SSM) 
forming. It uses a semi-solid slurry with a higher viscosity and lower heat 
content than the liquid form of the same alloy. As a result, cooling speeds 
can be accelerated, thermal efficiency can be improved, and die life can 
be extended. Lower quantities of confined gases are produced during 
filling because of the increased viscosity and reduced turbulent flow. 
The high cost of the required feedstock is a drawback of this approach. 
2.2. Melt protection during processing 
Unless care is taken to shield its surface from oxidation, molten 
magnesium tends to oxidize and burn. Aluminum alloys often produce a 
continuous, impermeable oxide layer on the liquid bath, preventing 
further oxidation, whereas magnesium alloys typically develop a loose, 
porous oxide coating on the surface of the molten metal. As a result, 
oxygen can get through the oxide ’ s surface and support burning un -
derneath. As a result, it ’ s crucial to use a flux or a protective gas cover to 
shelter and protect the molten alloy from oxygen [ 48 – 50 ]. There are 
essentially two types of melt protection systems for magnesium alloys: 
flux and fluxless systems. The introduction of fluxless melting methods, 
based on the utilization of an air/SF6 mixture to prevent the burning of 
molten magnesium, significantly lowers the high melting costs that are 
brought on by the high recycling ratio [ 51 ]. The mixture of air and sulfur 
hexafluoride being a potent greenhouse gas, the use of air/SF6 melt 
protection is being discouraged due to concerns about its impact on the 
environment [ 52 ]. As a result, researchers have suggested melt protec -
tion alternatives that are more environmentally friendly, such as using 
nitrogen or vacuum melting [ 53 , 54 ]. Furthermore, reducing or elimi -
nating the usage of flux will lower environmental issues and, conse -
quently, pollution control expenses. Mg – Zn – Al alloys are grain refined 
by superheating or by a carbon inoculation technique after being 
degassed with chlorine (metal chloride flux). Flux is widely applied to 
the melt ’ s surface to stop it from oxidizing and burning. To encourage 
the settling of oxides and other unwanted insoluble impurities, fluxes are 
frequently used to refine the melt. The flux cover is kept in place 
throughout the melting procedures and is only taken off shortly before

Manganese is a second element that is alloyed with Mg – Al, 
Mg – Al – Zn, and Mg – Zn – Al alloys in order to minimize the amount of iron 
and other heavy metal impurities and convert them into intermetallic

concentration of magnesium in the alloy [ 57 ]. This phenomenon is 
important to consider in the manufacturing of Mg – Al – Zn alloy, as it can 
affect the properties and performance of the final product. It is therefore 
crucial to control the aluminum concentration in the alloy to ensure the 
desired magnesium concentration and properties. 
The corrosion resistance and other properties of Mg – Al-based alloys 
are adversely affected by certain impurity elements, particularly iron, 
copper, and nickel [ 55 ]. These elements need to be maintained at

corrosion resistance [ 39 , 55 ]. As a result, manganese is usually added to 
magnesium alloys, usually in amounts between 0.2 and 0.4 wt%.

by zirconium and aluminum obstructs the refining process.

alloys 
Applications for previously developed magnesium alloys (Mg – Al – Si 
and Mg – Al-RE) in elevated temperature casting are limited due to their 
poor die castability or higher cost [ 66 ]. There are extremely few mag -
nesium alloys that are suitable for casting applications and have good 
creep resistance, adequate castability, and reasonable costs. Hot crack -
ing/tearing involves the undesirable formation of irregular shrinkage 
cracks in metal castings during the solidification and cooling of metals 
and their alloys in the mould/die cavity. Most metals experience this 
phenomenon, also known as solidification cracking. Hot cracking hap -
pens as a result of inadequate liquid metal to fill the gaps between 
molten metal that solidifies as a result of shrinkage strains and thermal 
stresses during solidification [ 67 ]. Alloy composition, particle size, 
freezing range, and eutectic phase percentage are significant influencing 
factors [ 68 ]. Hot cracking commonly occurs in pressure die-cast mag -
nesium alloys with zinc levels above 2 wt% [ 55 ]. However, reports claim 
that alloys with a higher zinc percentage are less prone to hot cracking 
[ 27 ]. The relationship between the upper range for hot cracking and

5. Progress in the development of Mg – Zn – Al (ZA) alloys 
Researchers have been working on developing magnesium-zinc- 
aluminum (ZA) alloys with improved high-temperature mechanical 
properties and creep resistance. Despite their limited commercial use, 
the demand for magnesium-based alloy components in the automobile 
industry has been steadily growing, which has led to sustained research 
in this area. This section presents a comprehensive summary of all the 
ZA alloys developed since researchers began to study ZA alloys, 
including a detailed analysis of the effects of Zn/Al mass ratio and 
concentration, processing techniques, and quaternary alloying on their 
structure, creep resistance, and mechanical performance. The study has 
revealed that some of the newly developed ZA alloys exhibit superior 
mechanical properties and creep resistance at high temperatures, mak -
ing them ideal for use in the automobile industry.

6. Conclusion 
This comprehensive review paper delves into the development of 
Mg – Zn – Al alloys and analyzes the effects of composition and incorpo -
rating different quaternary microalloying elements on the creep defor -
mation resistance, elevated temperature mechanical properties, and 
microstructural characteristics of the ZA alloys. Advanced processing 
techniques implemented on the ZA alloys such as ECAE (Equal Channel 
Angular Extrusion), self-inoculation, extrusion, and ageing were found 
to have a positive influence on the phase constituents, mechanical 
properties, and creep resistance of the ZA alloys. Notably, quaternary 
additions of calcium, tin, and rare earth metals were reported to be 
effective in improving the phases, elevated temperature mechanical 
properties, and resistance to creep deformation of these alloys. Creep 
resistance was enhanced with the addition of calcium in concentrations 
of 0.3 – 0.6 wt%, resulting in creep rates ranging from 0.75 to 3.75 %. A 
comprehensive analysis of microstructure and phases revealed that the 
block τ -Mg
32
(Al,Zn)
49 
ternary phase has more potential to improve 
creep resistance and high-temperature mechanical performance of 
various ZA alloy compositions. The ternary phase ’ s unique microstruc -
ture and composition contribute to increased strength and durability, 
making it a desirable phase for enhancing the performance of ZA alloys 
in high-temperature applications. The review provides a detailed anal -
