1. Introduction
Ti–Al–Nb alloys, based on Ti3Al (/H92512), form the basis for advanced
high temperature titanium alloys [1–5]. While Ti alloys containing
Al and Nb in the range of 20–25 at.% and 1 1–25 at.%, respec-
tively, have been studied extensively, very little information about
mechanical properties of alloys containing lower Al and Nb, i.e. Al in
the range of 15–20 at.% and Nb in the range 4–12 at.% alloys is avail-
able in the literature. Alloys in this range assume importance since
lower Al content could improve fracture toughness and lower Nb
content could minimise the segregation effects in addition to reduc-
tion in cost of the alloy. However, reduced Nb content is known to
decrease the stability of the /H9252phase making it susceptible to vari-
ous metastable phase transformations like /H9252to martensite ( /H9251

/H9252to omega to occur during quenching or under the inﬂuence of
stress. In fact, Ti–Nb alloys containing 14–28 at.% Nb are known to
undergo partial stress-induced martensitic transformation (SIMT)
[6] resulting in a two-phase microstructure prior to the onset of
plastic deformation.
It is well known that in single-phase polycrystalline metallic
alloys, the yield strength (/ESC

There have been some efforts to apply the H–P relation, initially
developed for single-phase alloys, to alloys containing two ductile
phases such as various two-phase steels [9–27], Ti alloys [28–30]
and other alloys [31–39]. However, in nearly all the cases, the grain

addressed these issues and proposed a modiﬁed Hall–Petch rela-
tionship for two-phase alloys, which is found to be valid in several
materials.
Interestingly, while the applicability of Hall–Petch relation-
ship in two-phase alloys has been well studied, the same is not
the case for alloys which start off as single-phase alloys and
undergo stress-induced martensitic transformation during tensile
testing prior to the onset of plastic deformation. A few studies on
alloys undergoing stress-induced transformation report that stress
required to trigger the transformation (trigger stress) follows H–P
equation [41–43]. However, it should be noted here that the stress-
induced transformation occurs during elastic deformation of the
parent phase and thus the trigger stress does not represent the
yield strength for the alloy. Therefore three such Ti–Al–Nb alloys

Two models [52] have evolved for explaining the Hall–Petch
dependence of yield strength on grain size in single-phase alloys.
The same interpretation can be considered to be valid in two-phase
alloys with a caveat that in addition to the grain boundaries, the
phase boundaries also play the same role. The friction stress is a
measure of the overall resistance of lattice to the dislocation move-
ment conﬁned in an isolated grain and its interpretation remains
the same in both the models, whereas the connotation of k varies
with the model. The ﬁrst model is based on the concept that a
dislocation source emits dislocations on the glide plane result-
ing in a dislocation pile-up at the grain boundary, which causes
a stress concentration at the tip of the dislocation pile-up. If the
stress concentration exceeds a critical value, other glide planes in
adjoining grain get activated and the plastic deformation is car-
ried over. In coarse grains, the length of the dislocation pile-ups is
longer resulting in greater stress concentration, and thus a lower
applied stress value is required for yielding. The unpinning con-
stant, k, gives a measure of the pile-up stress that is required to
cause yielding in the neighbouring grain. The dislocation pile-up
model is usually favoured [53] to explain grain boundary strength-
ening in alloys, which show planar slip. The second model, on
the other hand, avoids pile-up of dislocations at grain boundaries
and instead, focuses on the inﬂuence of grain size on dislocation
density. This model, also known as the forest-hardening model,
assumes that additional ﬂow stress component has its origin in
the extra dislocations stored due to the presence of grain bound-
aries. These extra dislocations produce the grain size dependence
of strength through their contribution to the total dislocation den-
sity.
