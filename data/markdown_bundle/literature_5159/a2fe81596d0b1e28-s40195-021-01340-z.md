Abstract
The effects of laser shock processing (LSP) and warm laser shock processing (WLSP) on the microstructure of surface 
hardening layer and high-cycle fatigue performance at room temperature and high temperature (600 °C) of IN718 alloy were 
investigated. It has been revealed that the grain refined hardening layer with greater residual compression stresses, higher 
fraction of coincidence site lattice (CSL) boundaries and dislocation densities was formed in WLSP-treated alloy than in 
LSP-treated alloys. Moreover, microtwins included γ″ phase/high density dislocation complex was found in the surface of 
WLSP-treated alloy. These characters caused the significant enhancement of the medium value fatigue strength of WLSP-
treated alloy at room temperature and elevated temperature. Apparently, the microtwins included γ″ phase/high density 
dislocation complex formed in the surface hardening layer of LSP-treated alloy has more complicated steric structure and 
more stable at elevated temperature than γ″ phase/low density dislocation complex formed in LSP-treated alloy, leading to 
the slow recovery process. Therefore, the surface hardening layer in the WLSP-treated alloy remained more ideal strengthen-
ing effect under high-cycle fatigue at elevated temperature than that in LSP-treated alloy. This resulted in the much longer 
fatigue crack initiation incubation and longer high-cycle life of WLSP-treated IN718 alloy under cycling load at 600 ℃ . 
This discovery provides a new cognition of fatigue resistance by WLSP treatment of precipitation strengthening superalloy.
Keywords IN718 superalloy · Warm laser shock processing (WLSP) · Microstructure · Fatigue · Residual stress
1 Introduction
IN718 superalloy, as a typical Ni-Cr-Fe-based superalloy 
strengthened by the precipitates of Ni3Nb (γ″) and Ni3(Al, 
Ti)(γʹ), can be applied safely at temperature ranging from 
-235 to 650 °C for a long duration [1 ]. The usage of IN718 
superalloy has occupied over 50% among all the superal-
loys and it has been utilized in aerospace, nuclear power, 
and transportation fields [2]. In particular, IN718 superalloy 
dominates a vital role in turbine rotor and its surrounding hot 
components in modern high-performance aero-engine [ 3], 
such as turbine disks, turbine rings and engine casings [4 ], 
which have been widely used in a variety of models of tur -
bine power units [5]. However, with increasing requirement 
of high-performance aero-engine properties, crucial hot 
components are required to service at higher temperature 
and more complex dynamic stress conditions [6 ]. Accord-
ingly, it is challenging for the existing mature alloy system 
with extensive applications [7 ]. In recent years, the analy -
sis from the accidents of many aero-engine failure [8 ] and 
numerous research results of material science shows that 
cyclic alternating load [ 9, 10] is the major reason of the 
fatigue damage and failure of the hot components [11].
In order to meet with the requirement of the aero-engine 
key components in the harsh environment [12], a lot of 
efforts have been made persistently to explore the novel 
alloys systems to service safely at higher temperature and 
more severe stress [13, 14]. Nevertheless, the universal 
alloys including IN718 superalloy are extensively used and 
highly consumed. Moreover, these alloys have advantages of 
stable microstructures and properties, owning super service

Additionally, the dynamic service property of the materi-

fatigue performance and extend fatigue service life without

and its particular advantages, laser shock processing (LSP) 
has been paid more and more attention from researchers 
and customer companies. This technology plays a dominant 
role in the fatigue resistance of ultra-high strength steels 
[18], superalloys [19], titanium alloys [20], and aviation 
aluminum alloys [21].
Higher compressive residual stress and the entanglement 
of high density dislocations [22, 23] are regarded to be the 
main reason and mechanism for improving the fatigue per -
formance of the materials [24]. However, as far as the super-
alloy serviced at elevated temperature for long duration is 
concerned, the recovery and interaction will take place in 
the dislocations and substructures, causing the weakness or 
disappearance of the compressive residual stress in LSP-
treated alloy. All these effects will reduce or even lose the 
fatigue resistance fabricating effect on the high temperature 
structural materials after LSP treatment. Currently, there is 
a substantially different cognition whether the high tempera-
ture fatigue increasing amplitude performance of superalloys 
and fatigue resistance manufacturing effect at high tempera-
ture after WLSP treatment still exist [23, 25], as reviewed 
from the results of LSP fatigue resistance manufacturing in 
some reports. In recent years, gradually developed thermally 
assisted warm laser shocking processing (WLSP) [26] has 
been recognized to be very advantageous surface fatigue 
resistance fabrication technology [27] due to its usage of 
coupling interaction between temperature and strong shock 
fields [28]. Nonetheless, the mechanism on the dislocation 
reaction and residual stress variation on the surface of the 
WLSP-treated superalloy at high servicing temperature has 
seldom been reported and the recognition has not been in 
good agreement yet. Unfortunately, the wide application for 
WLSP technology in the high temperature fatigue resist-
ance of structural superalloy is restricted. Therefore, it is of 
great significance to investigate the effect of WLSP on the 
surface morphology, substructures, and fatigue performance 
of IN718 superalloy.
In the present work, IN718 superalloy was surface treated 
with LSP and WLSP technologies. The microstructure evo-
lution and morphology of surface strengthening layer for 
WLSP-treated alloy was investigated in comparison with 
both untreated and LSP-treated alloys. The mechanism of 
the higher temperature fatigue strength for WLSP-treated 
alloy was discussed by combining the difference in high tem-
perature fatigue performance, crack initiation and propaga-
tion in untreated and LSP-treated alloys.
2 Material and Experimental
2.1 Material
IN718 superalloy plate with the thickness of 5 mm was pre-
pared via double vacuum melting, forging and hot rolling 
(finishing rolling temperature: 980 °C). The chemical com-
positions of the alloy are listed in Table 1. Prior to LSP and 
WLSP treatments, the alloy was subjected to the heat treat-
ment at 1020 °C for 1 h followed by air cooling and 620 °C 
for 8 h followed by air cooling consecutively.
2.2 LSP and WLSP Treatment
LSP and WLSP treatments were conducted on nanosecond 
pulsed Nd: YAG laser (SGR-60, Beamtech Optronics, Co. 
Ltd, China) after heat treated IN718 superalloy plate was 
ground and polished. LSP treatment was performed under 
the following parameters, the laser pulse duration of 15 ns, 
laser spot diameter of 2.5 mm, power density of 9.55 GW/
cm2, overlapping rate of 60%, absorbing layer materials 
of black polyvinyl chloride (PVC) tape with thickness of 
0.13 mm, and water confinement layer of 2 mm. Based on 
the LSP treatment, the assistance of environment tempera-
ture 300 °C was introduced in WLSP treatment, highly pure 
aluminum foil with the thickness of 0.10 mm was used as 
absorbing layer, the silica glass with thickness of 2 mm was 
used as confinement layer, and the other parameters are as 
same as those for LSP treatment.

3 Results
3.1 Effects of LSP and WLSP Treatments 
on the Surface Layer Microstructure of IN718 
Superalloy
IN718 superalloy exhibits good comprehensive mechani-
cal properties below 650 °C. Figure  1 shows the morphol-
ogy of heat treated IN718 superalloy. It can be seen that the 
matrix is equiaxed γ phase with the average grain size of 
76.58 ± 1.56 μm (Fig. 1a). There exists a small amount of 
Nb(C, N) phase in the γ matrix. There are well-dispersed 
tiny precipitates, mostly assigned to be a large amount γ″ 
phase and a few γʹ phase in the grain. The long axis of γ″ 
phase shows certain orientation correlated with the γ matrix 
(Fig. 1b). Because the low finish rolling temperature was 
applied to satisfy the requirement of the properties in practi-
cal application, short rod-like δ phase precipitates at the high 
angle grain boundary of the partial γ matrix. Free γ″ phase 
precipitation zone with a width of 100 nm surrounding the 
δ phase is visible at the grain boundary, where only γʹ phase 
is present.
The evolution of the microstructure of the heat treated 
IN718 superalloy after pre-optimized LSP and WLSP treat-
ments was traced. Figure  2 exhibits the inverse pole fig-
ure (IPF) for the surface layer of IN718 superalloys before 
and after LSP and WLSP treatments. It can be seen that 
severe plastic deformation takes place on the surface of 
IN718 superalloy and the grains crush under compressive 
cyclic load from the strong explosion shock. It is evident 
that the grain in the surface layer significantly is refined 
after LSP and WLSP treatments. The average grain size is 
reduced down to 29.28 ± 2.36 μm for LSP-treated alloy and 
26.28 ± 2.89 μm for WLSP-treated alloy, respectively.
Surface laser shock strengthening can induce the severe 
plastic deformation in the surface layer of the metallic mate-
rials [29]. At the same time, laser-induced surface plasma 
shock wave produces high speed shock pressure with a value 
of 4.59]03 MPa [30, 31]and shock strain rate of 106  s−1 [32] 
because of the explosive shock. As for the Ni-based super -
alloys with large amounts of precipitation strengthening 
phases, the large and rapid plastic strain accumulation will 
cause the intense interactions between the strengthening 
phases and dislocations [33].
Figure  3 illustrates the morphologies of the γ″ phase 
and dislocation configuration in the surface layer of 
LSP- and WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys. Obviously, 
γ″ phase is still disk-shaped, and its fraction and size 
on the surface hardening layer do not change after the

3.2 Hardness Distribution Along the Depth in LSP 
and WLSP‑treated IN718 Superalloy
The grain refinement, the strong entanglement between the 
strengthening phase and dislocation will lead to the surface 
layer hardening of surface treated IN718 superalloy. Figure 4 
depicts the distribution of hardness from the surface to inner 
site of both LSP- and WLSP-treated base IN718 superal-

3.3 Effects of LSP and WLSP Treatments 
on the High‑Cycle Fatigue Property at Both 
Room Temperature and 600 °C
Generally, the formation of surface hardening layer will 
play a positive role in improving the mechanical prop-
erties of IN718 superalloy at both room temperature 
and elevated temperature. Quasistatic tensile tests of 
LSP- and WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys were per -
formed at both room temperature and 650 °C. It has been 
found that both the yielding strength and ultimate ten-
sile strength of LSP-treated IN718 superalloy at room 
temperature and 650 °C are slightly increased, compared 
with untreated IN718 superalloy. The yield strength of 
the untreated IN718 superalloy after LSP- treatment is 
increased from 1185.16±21.25 MPa to 1256.31±15.86 
MPa at room temperature, and from 1109.68±18.02 MPa 
to 1175.74±12.98 MPa at 650 °C, with the enhancement 
of 5.99% and 5.95%, respectively. While the elongation 
at room temperature and 650 °C is mildly declined. In 
comparison with LSP-treated alloy, the yield strength and 
ultimate tensile strength for WLSP-treated alloy at room 
temperature and 650 °C are a little increased. The yield 
strength at room temperature and 650 °C for WLSP-treated 
alloy is 1271.29 ± 15.42 MPa and 1194.28 ± 16.69 MPa, 
increased by 7.26% and 7.66%, in comparison with the 
untreated alloy, respectively. It discloses that WLSP treat-
ment does not significantly affect the room temperature 
and high temperature tensile strengths of IN718 superalloy 
in spite of the formation of high surface hardness and deep 
surface hardening layer due to the intense entanglement 
of γ″ phase/high density dislocation complex on the sur -
face layer. It is because that the hardening does not take 
place in the whole base alloy plate through the surface 
strengthening treatment, only in the thin surface hardening 
layer depth of 0.856 ± 0.052 mm and 0.937 ± 0.020 mm for 
LSP- and WLSP-treated alloys. Evidently, WLSP treat-
ment shows no remarkable advantages over LSP- treatment 
even though the quasistatic tensile strength of the alloy 
at room temperature and elevated temperature is slightly 
enhanced.
Furthermore, the effect of WLSP on the high-cycle 
fatigue property at both room temperature and 600 °C for 
WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys was investigated. The 
room temperature and elevated temperature fatigue S –N 
curves of LSP- and WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys are 
shown in Fig.  5. Apparently, the high-cycle fatigue prop -
erties at room temperature and 600 °C are improved after 
the treatments, while the strength of WLSP-treated IN718 
superalloy is higher than that of LSP-treated IN718 superal-
loy. WLSP treatment shows a more distinct effect on improv-
ing the high-cycle fatigue property than that LSP treatment. 
The high-cycle medium value fatigue strength for WLSP-
treated IN718 superalloy reaches 877.76 ± 11.26 MPa, 
whose values are increased by 17.98% for the LSP-treated 
IN718 superalloy and 42.12% for WLSP-treated samples, 
compared with that of untreated IN718 superalloy. It should 
be noticed that the medium value fatigue strength of LSP-
treated IN718 superalloy at 600 °C is close to the value of 
untreated IN718 superalloy, indicating that the contribution 
of LSP treatment to the high temperature fatigue strength 
is not obvious. However, the high-cycle medium value 
fatigue strength at 600 °C for the IN718 superalloy after 
WLSP treatment with assistance of temperature (300 °C) 
is 672.97 ± 16.21 MPa, increased by 28.73% in comparison 
with the untreated alloy. This value is obviously higher than 
that for LSP-treated IN718 superalloy (14.77%), meaning

4 Discussion
4.1 Effects of LSP and WLSP Treatments 
on the Initiation and Propagation Behaviors 
of the Fatigue Crack
The SEM images of the initiation and stable state propaga-
tion at the initial stage of the fatigue cracks for LSP and 
WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys under stress amplitude 
of 950 MPa are displayed in Fig.  6. The cracks for the 
untreated, LSP-treated and WLSP-treated IN 718 alloys 
under same stress amplitude (950 MPa) initiate from sur -
face layer or subsurface layer, where a large amount of 
fatigue protrusions and micro-tearing ridges can be found 
on the main crack surface. The blocky Nb(C, N) precipi-
tates in surface layer or subsurface layer are easily crushed 
due to the stress concentration, resulting in the penetra-
tion of the micro-cracks into the surrounded grains and 
the initiation of fatigue crack source vertical to loading 
direction under the cyclic loading. In comparison with 
untreated IN718 superalloy, the fluctuation of the cracks 
path is more remarkable because of the difference in the 
crystal orientation in the fatigue crack source of LSP- and 
WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys, which closely correlates 
with the refined grains on the surface hardened layers after 
LSP- and WLSP treatments [34].
It is worth to note that the interconnection of micro-
cracks around blocky Nb(C, N) phases close to the surface 
penetrating into the matrix of LSP- and WLSP-treated 
IN718 superalloys is greatly retarded. This suggests that 
LSP- and WLSP treatments not only refine the grains in the 
surface hardening layer but also impede the initiation of the 
fatigue crack by the surface compressive residual stress, high 
dislocation density and the formation of the precipitates/
dislocation complex.
Under the same stress amplitude (950 MPa), the fatigue 
striations vertical to the main crack propagation direction 
appear on the fracture surface at the stable state propaga-
tion stage for the untreated, LSP-treated and WLSP-treated 
IN718 superalloys. As compared with untreated IN718 
superalloy, the stable state propagation after the initiation 
of the fatigue cracks occurs in the surface shock harden-
ing layers of LSP- and WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys. 
Meanwhile, the distance between the fatigue striations of 
LSP- and WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys is decreased, 
while it is smaller for WLSP-treated IN718 superalloy than 
that for LSP-treated IN718 superalloy. It implies that the 
fatigue crack propagation in the surface hardening layer of

4.2 Surface Residual Stress of LSP 
and WLSP‑treated IN718 Superalloy
The entanglement of the dislocations and increased sub-
structure density in the surface hardening layer of WLSP-
treated IN718 superalloy are not the most important fac-
tors in increasing the strength and improving the high-cycle 
fatigue performance. Moreover, the entanglement of the 
dislocations and the increment of substructure density in 
the surface hardening layers of WLPS treated alloy can lose 
their positive roles in enhancing the fatigue strength, which 
is ascribed from the dislocation reaction and substructure 
evolution at elevated temperature under long duration and 
low cycle loading process in the low load range at 600 °C. 
There should be some other factors dominating the key role 
in increasing the medium value fatigue strength at 600 °C 
after WLSP treatment.
The surface residual stress analysis from XRD pat-
terns and the corresponding values for untreated, LSP- and 
WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys are presented in Fig.  7. It 
is evident that the surface compressive stress occurred with 
the precipitation of γ″ and γʹ after solution treatment and 
double aging treatment. The XRD patterns of γ matrix for 
LSP- and WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys shift obviously 
to the high diffraction angle side, manifesting the increased 
surface residual stress of the alloys. Moreover, the surface 
compressive residual stress for WLSP-treated IN718 super-
alloy reaches −713.52 ± 104.67 MPa, which is increased by 
166.42%, much higher than that of LSP-treated IN78 alloy 
(117.91%). The existence of sufficient surface compression 
stress in a structural material is an essential prerequisite for 
guaranteeing the high fatigue resistance of the material. The 
high surface compressive residual stress will be helpful to 
balance the tensile stress between the surface and subsur -
face, contributing to the improvement of the fatigue resist-
ance and delay fatigue crack initiation in the surface layer.
Figure 8 shows orientation imaging microscopy (OIM) 
grain boundary reconstruction map and kernel average mis-
orientation (KAM) map of the surface hardening layer for 
untreated, LSP- and WLSP-treated IN718 superalloys. As for 
untreated IN718 superalloy, the concentration of the coinci-
dence site lattice (CSL) boundaries is low, where the grain 
boundary is mainly composed of high angle grain boundary 
and a small amount of Σ3 twin boundaries (Fig.  8a). After 
the respective LSP and WLSP treatments, the grains in the 
surface hardening layer are refined and the number of grain 
boundary is increased. The constituent of the grain boundary 
is also changed, the fraction of CSL boundaries is increased, 
and the increasing ratio of the Σ3 twin boundary is increased 
significantly. Especially, the remarkable enhancement of the 
CSL boundaries ratio in the surface hardening layer takes 
place in WLSP-treated IN718 superalloy.
The grain refinement, dislocation entanglement, and 
CSL boundaries increment in the surface layer definitely 
correlate with the characteristics of the stress distribu-
tion after the surface explosion shock process. The stress

4.3 Microtwins Phenomenon Near and Within 
γ″ Phase in LSP‑ and WLSP‑treated IN718 
Superalloys

ment, formation of γ″ phase/high density dislocation com-
plex and the different reduction level in the lattice constants 
of matrix and strengthening phase due to the compressive 
residual stress in WLSP-treated alloys. These effects suggest 
that the shock strengthened plastic strain allocation distri -
bution in the surface hardening layer is changed by WLSP 
treatment with the assisted warm (300 °C), compared with 
LSP treatment. Figure  9 exhibits the TEM image of γ″

surface hardening layer of IN718 superalloy after WLSP

(Fig. 9a). It can be seen from its corresponding dark field 
image of γ″ phase that a lot of tiny microtwins are present 
within γ″ phase, whose microtwins boundary is parallel and 
shows an orientation relationship with the long axis of γ″ 
phase (shown by blue arrows in Fig. 9b).
From HRTEM images in Fig.  9c and d, it can be deter -
mined that a lot of microtwins are formed near the inter -
face of γ/γ″ after WLSP treatment. One end of the micro-
twins crosses the γ″ phase, the other end terminates in the 
high density dislocation entanglement region nearby the γ″ 
phase in γ matrix (shown by red arrows in Fig.  9c). While 
this phenomenon is absent in the surface hardening lay -
ers of untreated and LSP-treated IN718 superalloys. This 
particular structure of microtwins within γ ″ phase/high

treated IN718 superalloy. This extraordinary structure is

Differently from room temperature, the atoms of WLSP-

the greater expansion value of γ matrix also results from the 
rapid temperature increasing of the alloy surface due to the 
coupling fields of temperature and shocking stress caused by 
the shock explosion when the alloy is subjected to WLSP 
treatment.

5 Conclusions
1. Surface hardening layers with the thickness of 0.835–
0.986 mm in LSP- and WLSP-treated IN718 superal-
loys were produced, by the grain refining. Meanwhile, 
compressive residual stress was present in the surface 
hardening layers of the surface treated alloys. The 
lattice constants of γ″ phase, γ matrix in the surface 
hardening layers of WLSP-treated IN718 superalloy 
were decreased while the CSL boundary fraction was 
increased, as compared with LSP-treated IN718 super -
alloy. Moreover, the dislocation density in the surface 
hardening layer of WLSP-treated IN718 superalloy was 
higher than that of LSP-treated IN718 superalloy. These 
high density dislocations and substructures mainly dis-
tributed around γ″ phase, forming γ″ phase/high den-
sity dislocation complex. During the WLSP treatment, 
the grain refinement, remarkable compressive residual 
stress, high CSL boundary fraction, and well-dispersed 
γ″ phase/high density dislocation complex contributed 
the excellent surface strengthening effects to IN718 
superalloy.
2. The high-cycle fatigue performances at room temper -
ature and 600 °C for LSP- and WLSP-treated IN718 
superalloys were improved. The high-cycle medium 
value fatigue strength of WLSP-treated IN718 super -
alloy was 42.12%, 28.73% higher than those of LSP-
treated IN718 superalloy at room temperature and 
600 °C, respectively. Compared with LSP-treated IN718 
superalloy, WLSP-treated IN718 superalloy showed bet-
ter properties, while the room temperature high-cycle
