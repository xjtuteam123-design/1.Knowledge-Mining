 Introduction 
 Materials design is critical to achieving target performance, 
reliability, durability, and manufacturability of industrial com-
ponents and products. Effi cient and robust materials design 
and development methodologies have been sought for faster 
discovery, qualifi cation, implementation, and industrialization 
of new materials. Under the framework of Integrated Computa-
tional Materials Engineering (ICME), 
 1 there have been numerous 
efforts to develop computational capabilities and tools to design 
materials and predict material performance, as represented by 
the Materials Genome Initiative (MGI). 
 2 Recent advancements 
in computational power and analytical capability have enabled 
the application of machine learning (ML) in materials design, 
as well as physics-based material models at various length 
scales. In this article, examples of successful applications of 
computational tools, both data-driven ML and physics-based 
models, in alloy design and optimization are described using 
single-crystal (SX) Ni-based superalloys as a model system. 
 SX Ni-based superalloys are commonly used for high-
temperature applications in aircraft engines and land-based 
power-generation gas turbines. 
 3 – 5 Typical SX Ni-based super-
alloys contain multiple alloying elements, including Co, Cr, 
Mo, W, Re, Al, Ta, Ti, Hf, C, and B, and consist of a γ -Ni solid-
solution matrix phase and γ ′ -Ni 
3 Al strengthening precipitates with 
cuboidal morphology. There are multiple property requirements, 
including mechanical, environmental, and physical properties, 
as well as processability, manufacturability, and cost ( Table I ) 
that need to be taken into consideration in alloy design. 
 Traditionally, experimental candidate alloy compositions 
were selected based on empirical rules and simple regressions 
built for a few primary property requirements. Multiple iterations 
of alloy compositions were typically required to identify the fi nal 
composition that meets the desired combination of properties. 
Ideally, alloy property models and a multi-objective optimization 
process would guide us to quickly reach the best solution, if there 
are property models capable of providing accurate predictions. 
However, many of the properties have complex interactions and 
dependencies on various factors, and it is diffi cult to predict the 
properties directly from alloy compositions. 
 For example, sustained-peak low-cycle fatigue (SPLCF) 
resistance, which is one of the important properties for SX 
superalloys, includes elements of fatigue, creep, and envi-
ronmental resistances. 
 6 , 7 Creep resistance itself has been also 
known to be dependent on multiple compositional and micro-
structural factors, such as fraction and morphology of the 
strengthening γ ′ precipitates, compositions of phases, lattice 
parameter misfi t between the γ and γ ′ phases, coarsening 
kinetics of the precipitates, and thermal stability of the phases 
 Application of computational tools 
in alloy design 
 Akane Suzuki , Chen Shen , and Natarajan Chennimalai Kumar 
 Alloy design is critical to achieving the target performance of industrial components 
and products. In designing new alloys, there are multiple property requirements, including 
mechanical, environmental, and physical properties, as well as manufacturability and 
processability. Computational models and tools to predict properties from alloy compositions 
and to optimize compositions for multiple objectives are essential in enabling effi cient, 
robust alloy design. Data-driven property models by machine learning (ML) are particularly 
useful in predicting physical properties with relatively simple dependence on composition, 
and in predicting complex properties that are too diffi cult for a physics-based model to 
achieve with desirable accuracy. In this article, we describe examples of ML applications to 
model coeffi cient of thermal expansion, creep and fatigue resistance in designing Ni-based 
superalloys, and optimization methodologies. We also discuss physics-based microstructure 
models that have been developed for optimizing heat-treatment conditions to achieve desired 
microstructures.

Instead of building ideal physics-based models that 
capture all of the underlying physics in the complex, high-
dimensional composition space, a more realistic approach 
is to build alternative models that use (1) merit indices based 
on scientific principles
8 or (2) ML methods if sufficient, 
reliable, and consistent data are available. ML models have 
advantages in predicting the following two types of properties. 
The first is properties with relatively simple dependence on alloy 
composition. This includes physical properties such as thermal 
expansion, density, and modulus. The second type is proper -
ties that are too complex to accurately describe using physical 
parameters, which include creep and SPLCF resistance.
ML for materials property modeling
The General Electric Company (GE) has a long history in 
developing SX superalloys.
5,9 All of this experience has resulted 
in a rich database of alloy compositions and various properties 
that lend themselves to be readily exercised with advanced 
analytic techniques and ML methods. By combining the inter-
nal GE data with additional data collected from the published 
literature, hundreds of data points can be used to build data-
driven models. However, it should be noted that this is not an 
ideal data set from an ML perspective. The size of the data set 
is relatively small, and the key input factors, including alloy 
compositions, were not sampled in the most ideal way – either 
as factorial design of experiments (DOEs) or the Latin hyper-
cube sampling (LHS)-based design – but rather, following 
empirical decisions to sample certain regions in the overall 
design space.
Modeling composition-dependent properties—
Coefficient of thermal expansion
The coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of a material 
is one of the basic properties that lacks a universal physical 
model. The design of some of the low CTE alloys has been 
guided by discovered mechanisms such as the Invar effect.
10 In 
alloy design, CTE has been typically treated as a composition- 
dependent property, and there have been different approaches 
in seeking the composition–CTE relationship. For example, the 
underpinning physical mechanisms have been explored with 
first-principles simulations.
11–13 Data-driven methods, such 
as linear regression, 14,15 or 
more sophisticated models 
such as neural networks
16 
have also been adopted. 
In this section, application 
of ML to model CTE and 
optimize composition in a 
high-dimensional compo-
sition space of interest is 
described.
Current ML toolkits, such as Scikit-learn,
17 make it easy for 
one to exercise multiple regression algorithms. Multiple mod-
els can be combined to further build a metamodel (an ensemble 
model) that is even better in performance. Figure 1a illustrates 
a typical workflow of ML that consists of two sequential steps: 
(1) build a model and (2) search composition space with the 
model. High-order models require regularization to combat 
model overfitting that significantly degrades model accuracy 
in prediction than in training. Usually, input data are randomly 
split into a training set and a test set. Only the former is used 
to train models and tune hyperparameters, and the test set is 
applied to evaluate performance of the final model. When input 
data are relatively small, K-fold cross-validation
17 is used during 
tuning to minimize prediction errors. This procedure is often 
applied to multiple models, of which the best performing 
model (i.e., with the lowest error) is selected as the final one. 
Alternatively, one may instead choose several top-performing 
models and use them to build a metamodel as the final model. 
The latter approach was taken to build the CTE model 
(Figure 1b). A total of 16 models in the Scikit-learn package 
were trained in parallel, and six top-performing models, includ-
ing KNeighborsRegressor and RandomForestRegressor were 
down-selected to train a StackingRegressor model,
18 which was 
taken as the final model. An overlay plot of the test set shows 
good match between the actual and predicted values.
To identify alloy compositions that meet a specific design 
criterion for CTE, a set of 10
6 compositions was generated 
in a SX superalloy composition space by optimized LHS. 
In this example, screening with a CTE criterion yielded 10
3 com-
positions. Additional screening criteria on other properties and 
parameters can be included in this step to narrow down composi-
tions. To further reduce the number of compositions to a realistic 
number, similar data points were grouped using K-means 
clustering, and each group was replaced by a respective centroid. 
Visualizing data points in high-dimensional space is challeng-
ing. Principal component analysis
17 is a convenient tool to reduce 
dimensions. Figure 1c shows the projections of the data points that 
meet the CTE criteria and the centroids of the 10 clustered groups 
on a plane defined by the two leading principal components.

the model. The BHM can also provide variance-based global 
sensitivity information for the creep resistance. Figure 2c shows

19,20 One key observation 
is that the creep resistance is composed of a larger fraction of 
higher-order interactions, which was expected due to the com-
plex interactions among the alloying elements, parameters, and 
properties used as inputs. The sensitivity analysis also shows 
contributions of each input and interaction, which helps in cross 
validating the model and the physics understanding.
The BHM creep can be used as one of the inputs to build a 
more complex hierarchical model for SPLCF resistance. By 
building separate models for SPLCF life at 1800°F (1255 K) 
and 2000°F (1366 K), alloy chemistry optimization was per -
formed to identify design space(s) that can result in the maxi-
mization of SPLCF resistance at both temperatures. The 
results from the global optimization are plotted in Figure 3, 
showing pareto fronts of solutions optimizing both objec-
tives. The optimization was performed at four different lev-
els of Re content, which is one of the known key contributors 
to the high-temperature capability of SX Ni-superalloys. 
Each data point corresponds to one alloy composition. The 
global optimization methods are useful for optimizing alloy 
compositions for multiple property requirements and design 
constraints (such as heat-treatment window, environmen-
tal resistance, and physical properties) and for performing 
tradeoff analyses between competing property requirements, 
such as Re content and raw material cost.

Future outlook
In this article, we have described powerful data-driven meth-
odologies for modeling highly nonlinear properties of superal-
loys and for utilizing the models to discover new alloys with 
desired combinations of properties. For further improving the 
performance of the models (i.e., reducing uncertainty and error 
in predictions), the following two areas of improvement need 
to be addressed: (1) advancement of data-driven modeling 
methodologies and ML algorithms, and (2) enhancement of 
the input database in terms of quality and volume.
The input data sets currently available for SX superalloy 
design are often not ideal for ML due to the relatively small 
size and insufficient (e.g., nonuniform) sampling in the com-
position space. In addition, among the experimentally col-
lected data points, scatter and noise are inevitable, especially 
in mechanical property data. Methodologies for curing the 
imperfection of the data set will greatly benefit model training 
quality and prediction capability. On the other hand, incorpo-
rating physics understanding or parameters as model inputs to 
build a physics-informed data-driven model will help a model 
to better tolerate data errors. Sensitivity analyses in the BHMs 
are useful for identifying the need for physics-based param -
eter input to complement the model.
For enhancing the databases, BHM models can be used 
in a reverse way to guide database maturation strategies by 
identifying design spaces with high uncertainties. Efficient 
methodologies to obtain more reliable, consistent property 
measurement data in wide ranges of composition spaces 
are also required. One useful approach is high-throughput
