Abstract
Future increases in gas turbine performance will require numerical algorithms capable of predicting the deformation
and rupture of blade materials at elevated temperatures. The current generation of Ni-based superalloys requires
constitutive models for rate-dependent plastic deformation of single-crystal turbine blades. Ni-based superalloys show a
complicated relationship between temperature and slip on octahedral or cubic slip systems, including softening. A three-
dimensional (3-D) ﬁnite deformation algorithm is presented for ﬁnite element analysis (FEA) of stress and strain
histories in anisotropic elastic materials with rate-dependent slip on speciﬁc, multiple slip systems. The FEA includes
multiplicative decomposition of the deformation gradients, Hencky strain tensor, and Green–Naghdi stress rate with
temperature-dependent crystal slip relations to compute ﬁnite deformation, slip induced rotations and material soft-
ening. Deformation predictions of Ni-based superalloys are compared with experimental measurements and analytical
solutions. Good agreement is found, especially when the specimen-to-specimen variations are considered. Ó 2000

1. Introduction
The spectacular increases in gas turbine performance
achieved over the preceding decades are due in no small
measure to the development of advanced superalloys.
Materials and processing developments have allowed
higher operating temperatures by raising the creep re-
sistance at elevated temperatures. Currently available
Ni-based single-crystal alloys allow operation at ho-
mologous temperatures ( T=T

Greater opportunities will be realized by making better
predictions of the material behavior under the operating
conditions. This chieﬂy means developing constitutive
models and mathematical techniques for numerical
analysis of turbine components.
Numerical analysis of turbine components requires
algorithms for transient, three-dimensional (3-D) stress

crystal components. The material description uses the

2.4. Iterative solution strategy
The crystal slip equations predict the shear strain rate
for a given shear stress. If the stress history is known it is
quite simple to compute the strain change and disloca-
tion density growth with time. On the other hand, if the
strain history is known, determining the stress and dis-
location density requires the solution of a set of coupled
nonlinear equations. In multiaxial problems each stress
component is further coupled to all the other compo-
nents. In standard displacement-based FEA, the con-
stitutive routines must take this total strain and compute
the stress history subject to the constraints describing
the material behavior. The constitutive routines there-
fore must simultaneously ﬁnd the roots of six, coupled,
highly nonlinear equations.
The constitutive algorithm allows explicit integration
by assigning 0 for the integration factor a. In this case,
the stress is determined only from the state at the be-
ginning of the step. Explicit integration schemes have
been successfully used but they do place restrictions on
the size of each step. If the steps are too large, numerical
instabilities may occur. In practice, the maximum size
for stable integration can be extremely small.
Implicit integration ( a> 0) allows the stable inte-
gration of larger steps. This requires the iterative de-
termination of six stress components; i.e., the roots of
six, coupled systems of nonlinear equations. As dis-
cussed in Ref. [16], there are no good, general methods
for solving systems of nonlinear equations. A combi-
nation of iterative schemes with adaptive subincremen-
tation has been adopted. Stress components are treated
separately. A combined bisection/Brent scheme is used
ﬁrst. If this is not sucient, the iterative process switches
to a 1-D Newton scheme. If this is still not adequate,
then the step is subdivided.
The iterative scheme involves taking candidate values
of the stress at the end of the step, computing the error
in the candidate, selecting a new candidate and repeating
the process. To select each new candidate, it was as-
sumed that each stress component can be treated sepa-

2.5. Accuracy and stability
Unconditionally, stable numerical integration
schemes are preferred although numerical stability does
not necessarily guarantee accuracy. A speciﬁc test to
demonstrate the performance of the algorithm was ini-
tiated. A single element was elastically loaded in one step
to create an initial uniaxial stress of 450 MPa at 950 °C.
In subsequent steps, no further axial displacements were
permitted. The rate-dependent plasticity causes the
uniaxial stress to relax exponentially with time. Strictly
speaking, this is only a test of the constitutive model
since there is no deformation after the elastic step.
Stresses and strain rates decrease rapidly making im-
plicit integration of the crystallographic slip equations
essential. Fig. 1 shows the computed axial stresses ob-
tained. At 500 h, there is no more than a 10 MPa dif-
ference in the stresses computed with 100 steps and with
two steps.
3. Comparisons with experiment
Strain measurements taken from the uniaxial stress
experiments in Ref. [1] of rod-shaped, single-crystal
specimens of SRR99 were examined. Comparisons were
made between the measured strains, the analytical so-
lution and the FEA results. Additional tests compared
larger, multielement FEA results with strain measure-
ments for two cases having di/C128erent axis orientations
and loads. A ﬁnal example shows the results of addi-
tional multielement FEA tests with di/C128erent tempera-
tures and orientations. When comparing numerical
predictions to experimental measurements one must
bear in mind that considerable scatter in test results is
usually the rule in creep tests. Certainly, small di/C128er-
ences in chemistry and processing and thus the initial
microstructure can a/C128ect the deformation. However,
large di/C128erences are frequently seen even for multiple
specimens taken from the same heat. Measurements on
single-crystal samples of CMSX4, a similar Ni-based
superalloy, taken from the same bar can show a factor
of 2 di/C128erence in the deformation [19]. Misorientation
errors of as little as 10 ° can a/C128ect the creep deformation
by a factor of 4 [19]. In the previous stress relaxation
case, the deformation was ﬁxed and plastic slip relaxed
the stresses. The converse of this is a case of constant
stress and changing deformation. Uniaxial stress tests
are described in which stresses remain constant and the
deformation is computed. Softening of the material
due to plastic slip accelerates the deformation rate.

3.1. Step size e/C128ects
Implicit integration schemes are favored because they
promote stability. There is no guarantee of accuracy
however. Increasing step sizes will still a/C128ect the accu-
racy.
Depending on the orientation of the specimen, the
shear stress on a given slip system must lie between zero
and half the tensile stress. There is no possible orienta-
tion which could result in a shear stress greater than this
on any slip system. Fig. 2 shows the results of the
comparisons of the uniaxial strains for a single-element
specimen, uniaxially loaded to 450 MPa at 850 °C in the
Æ001 æ direction. The largest possible shear stress on any
slip system could not exceed 225 MPa. Therefore, at this
temperature, for an arbitrarily oriented specimen, slip
on cubic systems would tend to be much greater than on
the octahedral systems for the same shear stress. How-
ever, by loading in the Æ001 æ direction, slip would only
be possible on the less active octahedral systems. This
makes a good test case because any errors in the stress or
rotation will tend to cause additional slip on the more
active cubic systems. Thus, errors will manifest them-
selves strongly. The measurements in Ref. [1] are only
the plastic deformation and do not include the elastic
deformation.
Comparing the analytical solution to the measured
values establishes an upper limit to the accuracy ex-
pected in any numerical results. First of all, numerical
integration always shows some di/C128erences with analyti-
cal solutions. Second, the constitutive algorithm used in
the FEA computations assumes that an average stress,
constant in the interval, can be used with the analytical
solution to compute incremental strains and dislocation
density growth. If the analytical solution is a poor ap-
proximation to the measurements, then numerical re-
sults cannot be better.
The analytical solution shows somewhat lower plastic
strains than were measured. This can only be the result
of the numerical process used to compute the material
parameters in Ref. [1]. The results of a numerical inte-
gration scheme in Ref. [1] are in closer agreement with
experiment than that of the analytical solution. Without
the analytical solution, some curve ﬁtting, or minimi-
zation procedure must have been used. This has created
minor errors that were compensated to some extent in
the numerical integration scheme in Ref. [1].
The single element FEA results shown in Figs. 1 and
2, for di/C128erent sizes of time steps, used the same mesh
and applied conditions. The integration parameter a was
0.5 in all cases. As larger time steps are used, the FEA
results become progressively further from the measured
and analytical values. Increasing the step size results in a
spurious sti/C128ening and an underestimate of the strain. It
would appear that the steps must be small enough that
the uniaxial strain in any step is not more than 0.002.
The incremental shear strain on any slip system must
therefore be kept less than 0.0006 for these conditions.
These are the minimum requirements. The comparisons
are much better if incremental uniaxial strains are never
larger than 0.001 (incremental shear strain on any slip
system not more than 0.0003).

crystal orientation and the nominal orientation. Mis-

values. The step size tests show that the numerical in-
tegration of the stress history is sensitive to the step size.

ditions also contribute to the di/C128erences. Deformation
frequently leads to a geometric softening as the slip
planes rotate into more favorable orientations [6]. The
rotations will however be a/C128ected by the length and di-
ameter of the specimen as well as the rigidity of the test
apparatus. Without a detailed description of the speci-

to simulate these aspects. Given these factors, the pre-
dictions are in good agreement with measurements.
6. Conclusions
(1) The constitutive equations of McLean et al. [1,2]
for single-crystal plasticity of SRR99 have been imple-
mented in a 3-D, ﬁnite deformation FEA code. The
formulation allows rotations and deformations for stress
histories at ﬁxed temperatures. A mixed explicit/implicit
temporal integration scheme is used. Computation of an
elastic-creep sti/C128ness matrix is included.
(2) The relations proposed by McLean et al. [1] can
be integrated to produce analytical relations for strain
and dislocation density if the temperature and stress are
constant. These have been modiﬁed to eliminate the
need for the Runge–Kutta integration schemes in the
constitutive model.
(3) The temporal integration scheme performs best
when steps are kept as small as possible. The automatic
subincrementation scheme, while e/C128ective even for large
time steps, can require excessive numbers of substeps
and may underestimate the deformation. As a general
rule many smaller steps will run faster and give better
results than fewer large steps.
(4) Comparisons of computed deformation with an-
alytical and experimental measurements are in good
agreement.
