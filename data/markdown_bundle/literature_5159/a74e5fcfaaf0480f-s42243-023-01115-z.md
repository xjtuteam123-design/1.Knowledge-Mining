Abstract
The effects of V on microstructure, room temperature, and high-temperature (350 /C176C) mechanical properties of Al–5Cu–
1.5Ni alloy were investigated. The results show that Al–5Cu–1.5Ni– xV alloys mainly comprise a-Al, Al2Cu, Al3(Ni, Cu)2,
Al3Ni, Al7Cu4Ni, and Al 10V phases. The addition of V can signally reﬁne a-Al dendritic structure, which improves the
mechanical properties of the as-cast alloys at room and high temperatures. After T6 heat treatment (540 /C176C/12 h/
WC ?170 /C176C/8 h/AC), a-Al, network skeleton structure Al 3(Ni, Cu)2, and Al 10V phases all show coarsening, while the
ﬁne nano-h0-Al2Cu precipitated from the matrix and dispersed distributed. The introduction of V promotes the quantity of
precipitated h0-Al2Cu particles. The ultimate tensile strength of the heat-treated alloys at room and high temperatures is
greatly improved compared to that of the as-cast alloy. The highest high-temperature ultimate tensile strength of the alloys
with V is 111.8 MPa, 21.5% higher than that of the base alloy. The analysis shows that the improvement of high-
temperature mechanical properties after heat treatment is mainly due to the introduction of V, increasing the precipitated
quantity of nano- h
0-Al2Cu particles and improving its thermal stability. The dispersion strengthening effect of h0-Al2Cu
particles exceeds the weakening effect of other adverse microstructures on the mechanical properties.

1 Introduction
The heat-resistant aluminium alloy boasts many advan-
tages, including high speciﬁc strength at high temperatures,
oxidation resistance, creep resistance, and thermal con-
ductivity. Additionally, it is relatively affordable to man-
ufacture. Consequently, it is often used to produce heat-
resistant parts for automobiles, aerospace, military equip-
ment, ships, and other applications [ 1–7]. However, tradi-
tional heat-resistant aluminium alloys have a service
temperature limit of approximately 250 /C176C[ 8], which
cannot partially replace the expensive titanium alloy
application in 300–400 /C176C or more, limiting their appli-
cations in many high-temperature ﬁelds. It is due to the
limited thermal stability of the high-temperature strength-
ening phase in aluminium alloy, which quickly dissolves or
coarsens at high temperatures and loses its strengthening
effect [2, 9–11]. To improve the mechanical properties of
aluminium alloys at high temperatures, it is necessary to
introduce a ﬁne dispersion strengthening phase with high
strength and high thermal stability into the aluminium alloy
matrix or modify the thermal stability of the existing high-
temperature strengthening phase.
The Al–Cu alloy possesses excellent strength, heat
resistance, and machining abilities. Research indicates that
the Al–Cu casting alloy is a highly favourable aluminium
alloy system for heat-resistant applications (between 250
and 350 /C176C) due to its impressive performance in both
room and elevated temperatures [ 12, 13]. As is known to
all, the main strengthening phase is the nanoscale h

This skeleton structure with a high melting point and high
thermal stability can effectively nail the grain boundary,
prevent the grain boundary sliding or grain rotation under
high-temperature load, and thus, improve the high-tem-
perature mechanical properties of the alloy. However, this
eutectic phase is generally a brittle aluminide. Excessive
production will seriously damage the Room temperature
plasticity and strength of aluminium alloy, so that the
amount of this element should be appropriately controlled.
Li et al. [ 15] studied the different contributions of various
heat-resistant phases formed by nickel in Al–Cu alloys to
the high-temperature properties of the alloys. The research
shows that d-Al

high-temperature mechanical properties. At the same time,
previous studies have proved that introducing Sc, Ti, Mn,
Zr, Nb, and other elements into the Al–Cu alloy will pro-
mote the precipitation quantity of h
0-Al2Cu and improve its
thermal stability at high temperatures. The main reason for
improving thermal stability is that these elements segregate
at the precipitation-matrix interface, reducing the interface
energy [ 33–38]. Shyam et al. [ 36] also found that the
segregation of Mn and Zr to h
0-Al2Cu precipitation-matrix
interface can modify the thermal stability of h0-Al2Cu,
indicating that these transition metals are beneﬁcial to
improving the high-temperature performance of Al–Cu
alloys.
According to the existing reports, adding V improves the
performance of aluminium alloy at room temperature. For
example, Meng et al. [ 39] showed that adding V to Al–Cu
alloy helps improve the room temperature mechanical
properties of the as-cast and heat-treated alloys. The ulti-
mate tensile strength at room temperature after heat treat-
ment is 12.3% higher than that without V. V will reﬁne the
grains of the matrix of the as-cast alloy. However, when the
content of V is too high, the sizeable Al
10V phase forms,
which is easy to produce stress concentration, making the
mechanical properties worse [ 40]. Wang et al. [ 41] sys-
tematically studied the grain reﬁnement effect of peritectic
forming solutes (V, Zr) and eutectic forming solutes (Cu,
Mg, and Si) on pure Al. After adding peritectic solute, the
grain size is reﬁned, while the grain size is slightly reduced
after adding eutectic solute. Shin et al. [ 38] used density
functional theory (DFT) based on the ﬁrst principles to
study the effect of elements like V on the segregation
behaviour of the h
0-Al2Cu/Al and calculated the segrega-
tion energy of the interface. It was found that the intro-
duction of V could segregate at the h0-Al2Cu/Al interface
and reduce the interface energy, improving thermal sta-
bility of the phase at high temperatures. However, this is
only a theoretical calculation, and the actual composition
of the alloy is generally more complex, which is worth
further conﬁrmation by practical experimental methods.
In this paper, the effect of V on microstructure and
mechanical properties of Al–Cu–Ni alloy at room tem-
perature and high temperature and its mechanism was
studied experimentally. The alloys were prepared by
ordinary melting casting and heat treatment processes. The
inﬂuence of V on the thermal stability of high-temperature
reinforced phases in the presence of Ni and Cu was dis-
cussed, laying a foundation for developing new heat-re-
sistant aluminium alloys.

2.2 Melting and casting
The melting experiment was conducted in a medium-fre-
quency induction furnace (model: M.M.F.00008). First, the
pure aluminium block was heated and melted in a graphite
crucible with a coating on the inside wall. The aluminium
block was completely melted, followed by slagging off.
Then, the melt temperature was measured using a K-type
portable handheld thermocouple by dipping it into the melt.
The melt temperature was controlled at about 760 /C176Cb y
adjusting the heated power. After that, the Al–50Cu master
alloy was added to the melt, adding the covering agent.
After holding for 7–8 min, slagging-off was carried out
again, and then, Al–10Ni and Al–10V master alloys were
added into the melt in sequence, followed by adding the
covering agent again. After holding for about 4–5 min,
slagging-off was carried out again. Afterwards, the graphite
agitator rod was inserted into the melt and mechanically
stirred at a rate of 300 r/min for 2 min. Then, the melt
temperature was lowered to about 740 /C176C for degassing
and slagging off. Finally, the melt was poured into a steel
mould with a preheating temperature of about 200 /C176C,
followed by demolding after solidiﬁcation and cooling. The
size of the steel mould is shown in Fig. 1.
2.3 Heat treatment and heat exposure
T6 heat treatment was carried out for all the as-cast alloys,
including solution, quenching, and ageing. The specimens
were heated in a Kington electric furnace (YYX1200-40
JINDUN) at 540 /C176C for 12 h and then, quenched in cold
water at room temperature. The specimens were removed
from the cold water after 10 min, followed by drying. The
ageing temperature and time are 170 /C176C and 8 h, respec-
tively. The cooling method is air cooling. The heat expo-
sure was carried out at 350 /C176C for 12 h for the
V-containing and V-free alloys to investigate the effect of
V on the thermal stability of h
0-Al2Cu.
2.4 Tensile tests
Tensile tests at room and high temperature (350 /C176C) were
conducted on a WDW3100 microcomputer-controlled
electronic universal testing machine. The tensile rate was
set at 0.5 mm/min for both room and high-temperature
tensile tests. The images and dimensions of the tensile
specimen are shown in Fig. 2. High-temperature tensile
specimens were heated to 350 /C176C and held for 20 min
before loading. The elongations (El) of the specimens after
fracture were measured manually with a vernier calliper
(accuracy is 0.01 mm).
2.5 Metallography observation
In this experiment, the phase analysis of the as-cast and
heat-treated alloy specimens was carried out using a
MiniFlex600 X-ray diffractometer. The scanning speed is 6
(/C176)/min, and the scanning angle range is 20 /C176to 80 /C176. The
microstructure and phase composition of the alloys were
analysed by a Phenom scanning electron microscope
(SEM) equipped with an energy spectrometer.

conclusion is consistent with the conclusion reported in
some literature [ 39, 43]. It is generally believed that peri-
tectic solute V plays a role in grain reﬁnement through two
mechanisms. On the one hand, V solutes segregate at the
front of the solid–liquid interface and inhibit the growth of
a-Al grain; on the other hand, V solutes, as a heteroge-

believe that V has extremely low solid solubility and
thermal diffusivity in the aluminium matrix, resulting in
most of the V solutes not being absorbed by the solidiﬁed
matrix but being pushed out in the solidiﬁcation front.
They move slowly at the solid–liquid interface front. They
are easy to enrich and cluster, which provides a heteroge-
neous nucleating agent for the nucleation of aluminium
grains, and ﬁnally improves the nucleation rate and pro-
motes grain reﬁnement. When the addition of V increased
to 0.45 wt.%, the coarse blocky Al

EDS composition analysis of each phase in Table 2 and
Fig. 5, it can be seen that tiny amounts V is also dissolved
in other second phases. The introduction of V has no
noticeable effect on the morphology and quantity of other
second phases.

The addition of V improves the tensile properties of the
Al–5Cu–1.5Ni alloy at both room temperature and high
temperature to a certain extent. It not only improves the
ultimate tensile strength and yield strength at room tem-
perature but also improves the tensile plasticity at room
temperature. Generally, mechanical properties improve-
ment after alloying element addition includes solution
strengthening, precipitation strengthening, ﬁne grain
strengthening, and so on. In this experiment, after intro-
ducing a small amount of V, it was found that a small
amount of V dissolved in the matrix (about 0.1–0.2 wt.%),
so that it should play a speciﬁc solution-strengthening
effect. However, the limit solid solubility of transitional
metal element V in aluminium is very low (only 0.56%). Its
solid solubility will be even lower at room temperature, so
that the solid solution strengthening effect caused by V is
relatively weak. The massive Al
10V phase appears when
the amount of V is increased, but this is a coarse and brittle
phase, which often damages the mechanical properties of
the matrix [ 44]. As seen from the microstructure pho-
tographs in Fig. 3, the introduction of V reﬁnes the a-Al
dendrite structure or grain size to varying degrees.
According to the Hall–Petch formula [45], grain reﬁnement
can improve the strength and plasticity of the material
synchronously, which is consistent with the experimental
results in this work. Therefore, the main reason for the
improvement of mechanical properties at room temperature
of the as-cast alloy in this experiment is the reﬁning effect
of V on a-Al grains. Although the grain reﬁnement effect
of the alloy adding 0.6 wt.% V is almost the same as that of
the alloy adding 0.45 wt.% V, the mechanical properties of
the former are signiﬁcantly reduced, indicating that too
much V leads to the formation of coarser and brittler Al
10V
that damages the mechanical properties of the alloy.
Adding V does not affect the morphology and quantity of
other second phases (Al
3(Ni, Cu)2,A l3Ni, Al7Cu4Ni). The
most obvious effect is the grain reﬁnement of the a-Al
matrix. However, generally speaking, grain reﬁnement is
not conducive to the high-temperature mechanical prop-
erties of the alloy. However, the experimental results show
that grain reﬁnement also improves the high-temperature
mechanical properties of the alloy. In our analysis, the
high-temperature tensile test was conducted under 350 /C176C,
and most of the equal strength temperature (Te) of alu-
minium alloy is above 450 /C176C[ 46]. Therefore, the grain
boundary strength of the alloy is greater than the in-grain
strength below 350 /C176C, which indicates that the grain
boundary can strengthen the matrix, and the number of
grain boundaries increases after grain reﬁnement, thus
improving the overall high-temperature mechanical prop-
erties of the alloy.

diffusion during ageing from the metastable supersaturated
solid solution. The precipitated h0 is often coherent with the
matrix. In order to increase the precipitated quantity of h0-
Al2Cu particles in the matrix, it is necessary to promote the
nucleation of the h0-Al2Cu phase during the artiﬁcial age-
ing process to increase the nucleation rate of this phase. In
order to increase the nucleation rate, it is necessary to
increase the number of atomic clusters of Al and Cu in the
ageing process. When the size of these atomic clusters
reaches a speciﬁc size (critical nucleus radius), they will
nucleate and grow up. The high melting point transition
metal V in aluminium alloy has very low thermal diffu-

of Al and Cu atomic clusters, resulting in a low nucleation
rate of h
0-Al2Cu and low precipitation quantity of h0-Al2Cu,
as shown in the high-magniﬁcation photograph inserted in

4 Conclusions
1. The as-cast and heat-treated Al–5Cu–1.5Ni alloys are
mainly composed of a-Al, Al2Cu, Al3(Ni, Cu)2, and a
small amount of Al 3Ni, Al 7Cu4Ni, and Al 10V. In the
as-cast alloy, adding V signiﬁcantly reﬁnes the den-
drite structure of a-Al, and the dendrite or grain size of
a-Al decreases more signiﬁcantly with the increase in
V. The reticular ( h0-Al2Cu ? a-Al) eutectic structure
is distributed along the grain boundaries of the primary
a-Al grains in the as-cast alloy. The network skeleton
structure and ﬁshbone Al 3(Ni, Cu) 2 phase are contin-
uously distributed along a-Al interdendritic or grain
boundaries. After T6 heat treatment, a-Al dendrites,
Al3(Ni, Cu) 2 phase, and Al 10V phase show obvious
coarsening. Fine nanocrystalline h0-Al2Cu particles are
dispersed from the matrix, and adding V promotes the
number of h
0-Al2Cu particles precipitated after heat
treatment.

tensile strength and yield strength of as-cast alloy
initially increase and then, decrease as the V content
increases. The ultimate tensile strength at room
