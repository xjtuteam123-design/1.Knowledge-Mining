ABSTRACT 
In this work, the potential of Nb addition to improve the mechanical performance of a new Ni – 26W – 6Cr-based 
superalloy designed for molten salt reactor (MSR) was studied. To this purpose, the evolution of the micro -
structure and the tensile properties of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr alloy with different Nb contents have been systematically 
investigated, with special attention to the solidification process. Nb addition in the Ni – 26W – 6Cr alloy causes 
constitutional undercooling and refines the grains of the as-cast alloys. In the following hot rolling and annealing 
treatment, the average grain size of Nb-bearing alloys remains smaller than that of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr alloy. Nb 
addition in the Ni – 26W – 6Cr alloy does not change the type of M
6
C carbides but increases the number of them, 
refining the grains of the alloys further by hindering grain boundary migration. When tensiled at room tem -
perature (RT), the strength of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys increases with Nb content due to the effects of solid 
solution strengthening, grain refinement and increased carbides number. Conversely, the elongation of the 
Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys maintains a semblance of uniformity, as the deleterious effect engendered by the 
amplified number of carbides counterbalances the enhancement in plasticity, facilitated by a reduction in grain 
size and an elevated proportion of twin boundaries. The effect of Nb addition on the strength and EL of the alloys 
at 850 
◦
C is similar to that at RT. The strength of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys increases with Nb content, and the 
EL is roughly equal, except for the decreased EL of the alloy with 4 wt% Nb addition. Nb addition slows down 
dynamic recrystallization in the plastic deformation stage of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys, and it is completely 
inhibited in the alloy with 4 wt% Nb content. 
1. Introduction 
The molten salt reactor (MSR) distinguishes itself from alternative 
fission reactors by employing molten salts, serving concurrently as 
coolants and fuel [ 1 – 3 ]. The intrinsic properties of molten salt, including 
a modest melting point, an elevated boiling point, and a subdued vapor 
pressure, confer upon the MSR numerous technical merits, such as 
excellent thermal conductivities and inherent safety. However, these 
attributes also pose formidable challenges to the performance of the 
structural alloy [ 1 , 2 , 4 ]. Crucial oxidation-resistant alloying elements in 
nickel-based superalloys, such as Cr, Al and Si, exhibit a pronounced 
susceptibility to corrosion when exposed to molten salts. Consequently, 
their concentrations in the structural alloy designated for MSR appli -
cations are meticulously regulated [ 5 – 8 ]. Hastelloy N, a 
Ni – 17Mo – 7Cr-based superalloy developed by Oak Ridge National Lab -
oratory (ORNL), stands as the sole mature structural alloy for MSR, 
attributable to its triumphant service in the Molten Salt Reactor Exper -
iment (MSRE) for an approximate duration of four years [ 9 , 10 ]. The 
maximum operating temperature of Hastelloy N is 704 
◦
C, above which 
the mechanical properties of Hastelloy N become insufficient [ 9 ]. 
Nevertheless, in pursuit of attaining more efficacious energy conversion 
paradigms, such as high-temperature hydrogen production, it becomes 
imperative to elevate the operational temperature of the MSR to exceed 
800 
◦
C [ 11 ].

Numerous studies have been conducted to improve the service 
temperature of the structural alloy for MSR [ 12 – 19 ], which can be 
summarized into two paths. One approach is to clad Ni or other 
corrosion-resistant coatings on high-strength superalloys like Inconel 
617 to obtain excellent corrosion resistance [ 15 ]. The alternative is to 
improve the high-temperature mechanical properties of the Hastelloy N 
alloy through composition optimization [ 16 – 19 ], and one new 
Ni-(26 – 28)W – 6Cr-based superalloy (Chinese alloy designation: 
GH3539) has been designed in this way. The Ni-(26 – 28)W – 6Cr alloy is 
proposed by replacing the primary solid solution element Mo in Has -
telloy N by W [ 17 – 19 ]. Our previous work shows that the Ni-(26 – 28) 
W – 6Cr alloy possesses superior mechanical properties than Hastelloy N 
due to the better solid solution strengthening effect of W than Mo [ 17 , 
19 ]. In addition, the Ni-(26 – 28)W – 6Cr alloy is less susceptible to being 
corroded by molten salts than Hastelloy N alloy as the Gibbs formation 
energy of W to form fluoride is larger than that of Mo [ 20 , 21 ]. Besides, 
the Ni-(26 – 28)W – 6Cr alloy exhibits better resistance to ion irradiation 
than the Hastelloy N alloy because the defect migration barriers and 
defect formation energy are larger in the Ni-(26 – 28)W – 6Cr alloy [ 22 ]. 
Then, the effects of W content on the microstructure and tensile prop -
erties of Ni-xW-6Cr alloys were systematically investigated to determine 
that the optimal W content should be 27.7 – 34 wt% in terms of me -
chanical properties [ 19 ]. However, when the content of W exceeds 25 wt 
%, excessive W will react with Cr

To further improve the mechanical properties of the Ni-(26 – 28) 
W – 6Cr alloy, elements like Nb and Ta with excellent solid solution 
strengthening effects and large solid solubility can be added to the alloy 
[ 24 ]. In fact, many researchers have demonstrated that an appropriate 
amount of Nb addition improves the mechanical properties of 
nickel-based superalloys. Christofdou et al. found that Nb addition in the 
powder metallurgy RR1000 superalloy caused a higher γ ′ volume frac -
tion, finer tertiary γ ′ distribution and increased anti-phase-boundary 
energy of γ ′ , resulting in improved tensile and creep properties of the 
alloy [ 25 ]. Detrois et al. found that the creep life of Inconel alloy 725 
was improved through additions of Nb or Ta as they promoted the 
precipitation of γ ′ ′ phase and grain boundary phases [ 26 ]. Zhang et al. 
added 4.9 wt% and 8.9 wt% Nb in the K4169 superalloy, the results 
show that Nb addition did not change the number of carbides but the 
number of Laves, δ and γ ′ ′ phase, leading to improved ultimate tensile 
strength [ 27 ]. Liu et al. modified the Ni – 17Mo – 7Cr-based superalloy 
with 2 wt% Nb and the strength of the alloy was improved at RT and

ment and nano twins strengthening [ 28 ]. Besides, addition of a proper 
amount of Nb is beneficial to the high-temperature oxidation resistance 
of nickel-based superalloys [ 29 , 30 ]. Moreover, Nb is added to modify 
Hastelloy N alloy to improve the corrosion resistance to molten fluoride 
salts containing UF
4 
and tellurium [ 31 – 33 ] and resistance to irradiation 
[ 34 ]. Considering the abovementioned considerations, adding Nb in the 
Ni-(26 – 28)W – 6Cr alloy may potentially resolve the dilemma wherein 
mechanical properties and high-temperature oxidation resistance 
impose antithetical demands on the Tungsten (W) content. 
Given that the microstructure and mechanical properties of a su -
peralloy are intrinsically sensitive to its alloy composition, the 
Ni – 26W – 6Cr-based alloys with Nb additions ranging from 0 to 4 wt% 
were prepared. The effects of Nb contents on the carbides, grain 
boundary characteristics, and tensile properties of a Ni – 26W – 6Cr-based 
superalloy are systematically investigated in this work. The corre -
sponding mechanism is discussed in detail.

3.2. Grain boundary character distribution 
As shown in Fig. 1 , a large number of annealing twins can be 
observed in the microstructures of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys. Since 
twins in fcc alloys play a critical role in the mechanical properties, the 
characteristics of the grain boundaries in the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys 
were measured by EBSD, and the result was analyzed by Aztec software. 
As illustrated in Fig. 5 , Σ 3 type grain boundaries and random grain 
boundaries each account for about half of total grain boundaries, and the 
fraction of Σ 3 type grain boundaries increases with Nb content. The 
increased Σ 3 type grain boundaries are caused by the decrease of 
stacking fault energy (SFE) with Nb content in the Nickel-based super -
alloys [ 38 – 40 ].

4. Discussion 
It is known that the mechanical properties of alloys are influenced by 
the microstructure which depends on their compositions. In this work, 
addition of Nb in the Ni – 26W – 6Cr alloy causes lattice expansion, grain 
refinement, increase of carbide number and strain hardening, and the 
tensile properties are therefore improved. The mechanisms concerning 
the relationship among Nb content, microstructure and tensile proper -
ties are discussed in detail. 
4.1. Effects of Nb addition on the microstructure 
Nb addition in the Ni – 26W – 6Cr alloy causes obvious refinement of 
the γ matrix and an increase of Σ 3 type grain boundaries and the number 
of carbides. As shown in Fig. 9 , the solidification temperature of the 
alloys drops with Nb addition. The constitutional undercooling can 
promote the nucleation of γ phase and M
6
C carbides and result in the 
refinement of grains. It is confirmed by the as-cast microstructures of the 
0Nb, 3Nb and 4Nb alloys in Fig. 10 . The as-cast microstructures of the 
0Nb, 3Nb and 4Nb alloys consist of refined grains, columnar grains and

4.2. Contributions of microstrutures to the mechanical properties 
4.2.1. Contributions of microstrutures to the RT mechanical properties 
The contributions of solid solute, grain refinement and carbides to 
the increased yield strength of the experimental alloys Ni – 26W – 6Cr and 
Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb were semi-quantitied as follows. It should be noted 
that the calculations may deviate from the actual results because the 
equations are mainly based on unitary- or binary-alloy systems. 
(1) Solid solution strengthening: The atomic radius of Nb (0.148 nm) 
is much larger than that of Ni (0.125 nm) [ 24 ]. Nb addition in the 
Ni – 26W – 6Cr alloy produces lattice distortion in the γ matrix ( Fig. 4 ), 
and the corresponding long-range stress field will hinder the movement

It is well known that an increase in strength is generally achieved at 
the expense of ductility in alloys [ 56 ]. However, Nb addition in this 
work leads to grain refinement and an increased proportion of twin 
boundaries in the Ni – 26W – 6Cr alloy. Grain refinement not only im -
proves the strength of alloys but also the ductility. The smaller average 
grain size of the alloy means more grains per unit volume, leading to less 
strain in each grain when the alloy is stressed. So the deformation in the 
alloy with finer grains is more uniform, and the formation and expansion 
of cracks is hindered. The increased twin boundaries caused by the 
decreased SFE with Nb content can also improve the strength and 
ductility of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys simultaneously, as they can 
impede the migration of dislocations and resist the formation and 
expansion of cracks at the same time. With a combined effect of grain 
refinement and an increased proportion of twin boundaries, the elon -
gation of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys is improved with Nb content. 
However, the elongation of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys just keeps about 
54 %. This is because the carbides can act as nucleation sites of cracks 
due to the different strains between them and the γ matrix caused by the 
hardness misfit. As Nb promotes the precipitation of carbides, the 
improvement effect in elongation brought by grain refinement and the 
increased proportion of twin boundaries is offset.

grain boundaries, twin boundaries and carbides. The solid solution 
atoms, grain boundaries, proportion of twin boundaries and the number 
of carbides increase with Nb content in the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys, 
resulting in improved strength.

strain curves of the Ni – 26W – 6Cr-xNb alloys at 850

strain softening of the alloys. When tested at RT, the deformation of the

plastic deformation stage, and strain hardening can be observed in

solution atoms, grain boundaries, twin boundaries and carbides through 
thermal activation. As shown in Fig. 6 c, the stress of the 0Nb alloy de -

dicates the occurrence of dynamic recrystallization (DRX) in the plastic 
deformation stage. Whether DRX takes place during deformation de -
pends on factors such as temperature, the initial grain size, concentra -
tion of solutes, stacking fault energy and second phase particles [ 57 , 58 ]. 
Generally, alloys with low stacking fault energy, such as nickel-based 
superalloys, tend to occur DRX when applied to stress at elevated tem -
peratures [ 58 ]. The result in our previous work also demonstrates that 
the temperature of 850 
◦
C is enough to trigger DRX in the 0Nb alloy 
[ 42 ]. In addition, DRX is easier to happen in alloys with finer grains as 
grain boundaries are preferred sites for nucleation. However, the 
increased number of solutes and carbides can inhibit DRX by hindering 
boundary motion [ 58 ]. Through the combined effect of these factors, 
DRX slows down in the 1Nb, 2Nb and 3Nb alloys and is completely 
inhibited in the 4Nb alloy, and it is the reason why the EL of the 4Nb 
alloy is lower than the other alloys. 
5. Conclusion 
The evolution of microstructure and mechanical properties of a 
Ni – 26W – 6Cr-based superalloy with Nb addition has been systematically 
investigated in this work. The major conclusion can be drawn as follows: 
(1) Nb addition decreases the solidification temperature of the 
Ni – 26W – 6Cr superalloy, promoting the nucleation of γ phase and 
refining the grains. In the following hot rolling and annealing 
treatment, the average grain size of Nb-bearing alloys remains 
smaller than that of the 0Nb alloy due to the finer initial as-cast
