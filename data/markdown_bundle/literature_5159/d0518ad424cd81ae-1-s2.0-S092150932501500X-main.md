A B S T R A C T
GRX-810 is a novel Multi-Principal Element Alloy (MPEA) belonging to the NiCoCr family, primarily strength-
ened through the Oxide Dispersion Strengthening (ODS) mechanism. This alloy demonstrates high tensile
strength and exceptional creep resistance, coupled with significant oxidation resistance, while maintaining
remarkable ductility for applications in extreme-temperature environments. Additive manufacturing enabled
the development of this alloy by the dispersion of the nano-scale𝑌2𝑂3 throughout the microstructure. However,
meeting industrial and production demands requires comprehensive characterization of the microstructure,
mechanical and thermophysical properties, as well as variations in geometry and machine parameters. This
study investigates the size and temperature-dependent mechanical properties and deformation mechanisms of
GRX-810, focusing on the relation between its pristine (pre-deformed) microstructural features and mechanical
behavior for elevated temperatures up to 1148 ◦C. As a medium entropy alloy, GRX-810 benefits from
an atomic scale strengthening mechanism and precipitates in the form of carbides and nano-scale yttria
oxides, resulting in desirable properties for extreme environment applications. A size-dependent variation in
porosity volume, with higher porosity in thinner GRX-810 samples is observed. However, no evidence of size-
dependent variation in mechanical properties was identified. Fracture surface analysis revealed deformation
twinning through different temperatures, particularly during the later stages of plastic deformation, serving
as a strengthening mechanism by delaying necking and fracture. At 537 ◦C, the Portevin-Le Chatelier (PLC)
effect was observed and attributed to lattice distortion impeding dislocation movement. Overall, quasi-static
tensile testing demonstrated a progressive linear decrease in yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength
(UTS) with increasing temperature, while elongation remained consistently above 30% up to 871 ◦C for all
thicknesses.
1. Introduction
The concepts of Multi-Principal Element Alloys (MPEAs) and High-
Entropy Alloys (HEAs) were first introduced in 2004 by Cantor et al. [1]
and Yeh et al. [2], making HEAs a relatively recent innovation in mate-
rials science. HEAs are defined [2] as ‘‘alloys that may contain principal
elements with concentrations of each element ranging between 35 and
5 at.-%’’. Two key characteristics of HEAs are their high configurational
entropy and distorted lattice structures. The increased configurational
entropy, combined with the variation in atomic sizes within the crystal
lattice, could promote solid-solution strengthening [3]. Since their in-
troduction in 2004, extensive research has been conducted on HEAs and
their counterpart, Medium-Entropy Alloys (MEAs) [4–22]. Research

The deformation mechanisms of MEAs and HEAs have been exten-
sively investigated and compared across studies. These alloys’ desirable
mechanical properties are often attributed to their atomic-scale de-
formation mechanisms [32]. The MEA strengthening mechanism is
reported to be the combination of hierarchical microstructural fea-
tures such as solid-solution strengthening, low stacking fault energy,
and nano-scale mechanical twinning [13,15,25,28,30,33]. Laplanche
et al. [11] characterized the deformation mechanism of CrCoNi MEA
under tensile testing at cryogenic and ambient temperatures to be dislo-
cation glide at the early stages of plasticity and nano-twinning as a later
stage deformation mechanism responsible for higher strain hardening
rate and improved mechanical properties. Miao et al. [32] also ob-
served the deformation substructure under tensile testing at cryogenic
and ambient conditions to predominantly feature planar dislocation
slip and the dissociation of dislocations into stacking faults at lower
strain levels. As the strain increased, additional substructures emerged,
including nano-twins and a newly formed phase comprising hexagonal
close-packed (HCP) lamellae. Lastly, Feng et al. [34] proposed that
a CoCrNi MEA alloy, which is pre-deformed with high density of
deformation twins, displays an exceptional resistance to crack growth.
High fracture toughness is also an important property of such alloy
families [35]. Studies summarized above highlight that the defor-
mation mechanisms of MEAs and HEAs involve multiple sequential
processes.
An important characteristic behavior occurring during certain
medium-range temperatures and strain rates is serrated plastic flow
in solid-solute-strengthened-alloys [19,36,37]. This serrated plastic
flow in tensile stress–strain curves is attributed to the Portevin-Le
Chatelier (PLC) effect and is a well documented phenomenon in many
solid solute strengthened alloys, such as nickel superalloys [38–40].
HEAs and MEAs exhibit serrated flow behavior during deformation
at specific temperature and strain-rate regimes. This phenomenon,
characterized by stress drops or ‘‘serrations’’ in stress–strain curves,
arises from the dynamic interaction of dislocations with solute atoms,
leading to intermittent pinning and unpinning. Unlike conventional
alloys, the solute-rich matrices of MEAs and HEAs enhance dislocation
pinning through faster healing rates, resulting in distinctive serration
patterns [36].
Considering the literature regarding strengthening mechanism of
MEAs and the impact of additional alloying elements that further
enhance the overall performance of these alloys, it becomes evident
that MEAs possess significant potential for tailored applications. Smith
et al. [41] developed an ODS NiCoCr alloy using Laser Powder Bed
Fusion (L-PBF), where NiCoCr base metal powder is coated with less
than 1 wt% nano-scale 𝑌2𝑂3 particles through a high-energy mixing
technique. Using the same acoustic resonance mixing method, GRX-810
was developed by NASA Glenn Research Center, an ODS alloy designed
to withstand extreme temperatures above 810 ◦C [42]. This alloy
demonstrates notably improved mechanical properties in terms of creep
and tensile properties compared with NiCoCr and NiCoCr ODS MEA and
similar alloys from the same family. These enhancements are attributed
to the alloy’s optimized microstructure including uniform dispersoid
distribution and stable carbides along grain boundaries. The presence
of stacking fault tetrahedra, which act as an inhibitor to dislocation
movement is also another characteristic strengthening mechanism of
this alloy.

GRX-810 is designed for extreme-temperature applications, bridging
the gap between traditional superalloys and refractory materials. Its
targeted applications include turbine blisks, high-temperature mechan-
ical testing tools, rocket engine injectors and nozzles, heat exchangers,
and environments demanding exceptional high-temperature ductility,
creep, and oxidation resistance [43,44]. Although this alloy holds
great promise, there remains a lack of extensive studies on its defor-
mation mechanism and the influence of microstructural features on
mechanical properties at different temperatures and varying geometric
thicknesses.
This study investigates the microstructure and underlying defor-
mation mechanisms of AM GRX-810 by conducting quasi-static tensile
tests at ambient and elevated temperatures (up to 1148 ◦C) in thin-wall
samples with thicknesses varying from 1 mm to 2.5 mm. The method-
ology and characterization methods are described in Section 2. The
discussion on the pre-deformed state of the GRX-810 samples, referred
to as ‘‘pristine’’ throughout the study, is presented in Section 3.1.
Further discussion on the mechanical properties, mechanical response
and deformation mechanisms is provided in Section 3.2, Section 3.3
and Section 3.4, respectively.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Manufacturing parameters and heat treatment
GRX-810 tensile test thin-wall samples were fabricated in two condi-
tions of non-heat treated and post-processed using hot isostatic pressing
(HIP). The HIP parameters were 1163 ◦C for 3–4 h at 100 MPa, follow-
ing standard nickel-based superalloys reported in the ASTM-3301. The
chemical composition of GRX-810 is shown in Table 1 and is consistent
with data reported in [42]. The specific printing parameters are also
provided in Table 2. Thin-wall samples tested at ambient temperature
(Fig. 1) were designed in accordance with the ASTM E8 standard,
whereas the same geometry of thin-wall samples intended for elevated
temperature tests featured extended grip sections to accommodate the
high-temperature test setup. Neither the as-built nor HIP thin-wall
samples underwent any machining or surface treatment prior to testing.
2.2. X-ray computed tomography
X-ray Computed Tomography (XCT) was used to investigate the
porosity distribution and volume of the tensile test samples and to
evaluate the impact of the HIP process on varying sample thicknesses.
For each microstructure (as-built and HIP), one sample with thicknesses
1 mm,1.5 mm,2 mm and2.5 mm was scanned using a Zeiss Xradia Versa
520 Micro-XCT system with a voltage of 140 k V, 21 W power and a
resolution of6.31 μm.

2.3. Surface topography
Surface analysis was conducted in this study for two specific rea-
sons: (1) to determine if surface topography is size-dependent and (2)
to accurately calculate the load-bearing cross-sectional area of tensile
test samples. The surface topography of the specimens was measured
on a Keyence VHX-1000 laser microscope using a 20× magnification
lens and at a working distance of 3.1mm in wide-field focus variation
scan mode.
To obtain statistically significant surface measurements, two mea-
surements with an area of5.9 mm2 were scanned in both the front and
back of three samples for each thickness and for each heat treatment
condition. The measurements were taken in accordance with the sur-
face roughness ISO 25178 standard, employing a Gaussian filter with a
5μm S-filter a0.8μm L-filter. The accuracy of the adjusted cross-sectional
area was validated by removing the surface topography of ambient HIP
samples with 600-grit silicon carbide grinding paper.
2.4. Microscopy
Samples were sectioned from the test samples and prepared for
microscopy by grinding with silicon carbide grinding paper from 400
grit to 600 grit to 800 grit (15 μm), followed by sequential polishing
with glycol diamond suspensions ranging from 9 μm to 3 μm, and a
final polish with 0.05 μm alumina paste. To eliminate residual surface
deformation caused by the grinding and polishing steps, the samples
were vibratory polished with0.02 μm colloidal silica for 24 h.
The material’s microstructure was analyzed using a TESCAN Mira
3 field emission Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) equipped with
an OXFORD Symmetry S2 high-speed Electron Backscatter Diffraction
(EBSD) detector and an OXFORD Energy-dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy
(EDS) detector. EBSD, EDS, and secondary electron (SE) imaging were
performed with an acceleration voltage of 15 k V. High-resolution SE
images were captured with a beam intensity of 15, which was increased
to 20 for EBSD and EDS analysis. For scan areas larger than500 μm by
750 μm, a step size of2 μm was used, while a smaller step size of0.2 μm
was applied to smaller regions. Texture analysis of the EBSD data was
performed using AZtec Crystal software [45].
2.5. Tensile testing
Quasi-static tensile tests were conducted on samples at ambient
temperature and high temperatures 537 ◦C (1000 F), 871 ◦C (1600
F), 1093 ◦C (2000 F) and 1148 ◦C (2100 F). The tests were conducted
using an Instron 8801 servo-hydraulic load frame under displacement-
control, with an approximate strain rate of10−3 s−1. The strain values
were calculated from displacement readings collected using Epsilon
model 3542 and 3549 contact extensometers for ambient and high
temperature tests, respectively.
Samples were brought to testing temperature using AMTECO model
350-3Z three-zone furnace with a heating rate of 20 ◦C/min. To ensure
accurate distribution of the temperature throughout the gauge section
of the samples, temperature profiling was conducted using two R-type
thermocouples at both ends of the gauge section of the high tempera-
ture samples. The temperature distribution was controlled within±3 ◦C
of the designated temperature between both measured points of the
gauge section. To ensure repeatability of the results, three tests were
conducted for each testing case. The high temperature tensile test setup
can be found in [46].
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Pristine state
3.1.1. Porosity analysis
Initial XCT observations of bulk defects show a high volume of
porosity and microcracks elongated in the build direction present in
the as-built test samples for all thicknesses. HIP processing was found
to successfully reduce internal porosity and microcracks in treated
samples by approximately 90% relative to the as-built porosity volume.
However, it was not as effective in closing surface-connected or near-
surface porosity, consistent with expectations for the HIP process in
AM parts [47]. Fig. 2 provides a representative image of 1 mm as-
built and HIP samples, highlighting the porosity distribution and the
effectiveness of the HIP process.
Further porosity analysis reveals the volume of porosity and inter-
nal microcracks increase with decreasing sample thickness in as-built
samples. In turn, HIP samples show significantly lower porosity volume
without such observable trend (Fig. 3). Size-dependent porosity volume
was previously reported for L-PBF GRCop-42 [48], where the porosity
volume percentage increased as the thickness decreased, from 0.27%
for2.0 mm samples to 1.07% for0.7 mm samples.
The porosity volume was found to be size-dependent in the as-built
samples. This observation suggests that bulk laser parameters for GRX-
810 may need to be further optimized when printing thin-walled or fine
features to better address the change in the associated thermal history.
Despite these defects, the density of the as-built material was measured
to be over 99.8% for all samples.

3.2. Mechanical properties
The stress–strain curves from as-built and HIP GRX-810 samples
with the varying thicknesses are shown in Fig. 9. As depicted in
Fig. 9(a) and Fig. 9(b), 𝑆𝑧 cross-section adjusted stress–strain curves
display negligible thickness dependence, except subtle thickness and
temperature driven variation in elongation. This observation is con-
sistent with the findings in [53], which states low porosity volume
(approximately below 1%) has been reported to have a diminished
effect on such mechanical properties compared with microstructural
effect for AM alloys such as Ti-6Al-4 V [54] and Inconel 718 [55].
Therefore, the analysis of mechanical properties is conducted exclu-
sively using stress–strain curves based on 𝑆𝑧-adjusted cross-sectional
areas.
The GRX-810 temperature-dependent mechanical properties are
presented in Table 4. Due to the close agreement among stress–strain
curves across all thicknesses, the mechanical properties in terms of
Young’s modulus (E), yield strength (YS), and ultimate tensile strength
(UTS) for both as-built and HIP conditions were averaged over the
1 mm to 2.5 mm samples. A progressive decrease in UTS and YS is
observed with increasing temperature in both as-built and HIP samples,
as presented in Fig. 10(a) and Fig. 10(b), respectively. Additionally,
an approximate 11% reduction in microhardness was measured in HIP
samples across all thicknesses, decreasing from 257.6 ± 11.2 HV in
the as-built condition to 230.5± 8.2 HV after HIP, which supports the
observed reduction in YS. The evolution of Young’s modulus under am-
bient and elevated temperature conditions is shown in Fig. 11(a). HIP
samples exhibit consistently higher Young’s modulus values compared
to their as-built counterparts, which is typically expected following
HIP post-processing due to the elimination of internal porosity [56].
In the present study, the increase in Young’s modulus in HIP samples is
attributed to reduced porosity and the healing of microcracks initially
presented in the as-built condition.
Since elongation shows both temperature and thickness dependent
behavior, the separated elongation values are presented in Table 5.
Across all thicknesses and testing temperatures, HIP process consis-
tently improves elongation relative to the as-built condition, with the
largest influence evident at ambient temperature, for approximately 7–
8 percentage-point increases at1 mm and1.5 mm. Elongation generally
decreases with increasing test temperature, showing a gradual decline
up to 871 ◦C followed by a more rapid reduction between 871 ◦C
and 1148 ◦C. As-built specimens decrease from approximately 30%–
39% at ambient temperature to 17%–25% at 1148 ◦C, while HIP
specimens drop from approximately 37%–42% to 19%–23% over the
same temperature range. Thickness exerts a secondary but noticeable
influence, where elongation tends to be higher in thicker than in
thinner samples, which is more pronounced at lower temperatures.
Overall, HIP processing provides a robust ductility benefit that persists,

3.3.2. Serrated plastic flow
Serrated plastic flow, also known as the PLC effect, is characterized
by a series of load drops and recoveries that occur at the onset of plas-
ticity. The critical strain associated with this phenomenon is influenced
by both the strain rate and temperature [36]. This behavior, illustrated
in Fig. 16, is evident in the GRX-810 as-built and HIP stress–strain
curves obtained from quasi-static tensile tests conducted at 537 ◦C.
The critical strain at which this mechanism is activated is observed to
be approximately 0.01 ± 0.005 and does not start immediately after
the deviation from the linear zone of the stress–strain curves. Both as-
built and HIP GRX-810 samples exhibit a similar PLC effect through

Extensive studies on the PLC effect generally agree that the pinning
and unpinning of dislocations by obstacles in the microstructure is the
primary mechanism driving this phenomenon [36,46,65–67]. Among
the mentioned studies, June et al. [46] investigated the initiation and
transformation of the PLC effect through elevated temperature tensile
testing from 250 ◦C to 650 ◦C. A notable finding was that the PLC effect
exhibited size-dependency, influencing its formation and characteris-
tics. Carroll et al. [36] proposed a model that provides insights into this
behavior specifically for MEAs and HEAs. During plastic deformation,
dislocations within the crystal - referred to as ‘‘weak spots’’ in [36] -
begin to slip when local stress exceeds the critical stress for dislocation
movement. The stress drops observed during serrations are attributed
to the slip or unpinning of dislocations from the high concentration
of solute atoms, a characteristic feature of MEAs and HEAs. The sub-
sequent recovery, referred to as healing or re-strengthening, is driven
by the rearrangement of Interstitial solute atoms around the arrested
dislocations, leading to their re-pinning. This repetitive cycle of slip, un-
pinning, and re-pinning results in the serrated plastic flow characteristic
of the PLC effect. The temperature dependence of the PLC effect can be
attributed to the ability of surrounding solute atoms to pin and unpin
dislocations within this temperature sensitive range. At temperatures
below the range where the PLC effect is observed, dislocations are
insufficiently pinned by surrounding atoms, leading to the absence of
serrations. Conversely, at temperatures above this range, stress–strain
curves become smooth and free of serrations due to increased thermal
fluctuations that disrupt the pinning mechanism [36].
The serrated plastic flow observed in GRX-810 at 537 ◦C
underscores the intricate interplay between dislocations and solute
atoms inherent to the PLC effect. The critical strain of approximately
0.01± 0.005 highlights the conditions under which the pinning and un-
pinning mechanisms activate, aligning with the temperature-sensitive
behavior reported in the literature. The pronounced serrations in the
stress–strain curve reflect the cyclic processes of dislocation slip, unpin-
ning, and re-pinning, driven by localized stress fields and solute atom
dynamics.

the propagation of internal microcracks, and delaying failure. The role

ductility in NiCoCr MEAs, it is hypothesized that the reduced ductility

sighted deformation twinning at these elevated temperatures.

microstructure exhibits heavily ⟨001⟩-oriented columnar grains aligned
parallel to the build direction. As deformation progresses, the texture
evolves to display a combination of ⟨001⟩ and ⟨111⟩ fiber texture.

are oriented parallel to the load direction, thus, no significant effect
was noticed during tensile testing. This observation is consistent with
the findings in [69], where microcracks that are aligned parallel to the
load direction experience reduced tensile stress perpendicular to their
propagation plane, thereby limiting their influence on the material’s
ductility. The segregation of alloying elements near microcracks in the
pristine state of as-built GRX-810 samples, along with the presence of
coarsened carbides and precipitates at the grain boundaries of HIP sam-
ples, suggests a shared failure mechanism. This mechanism is believed
to be initiated and dominated by weak grain boundary cracking, as
shown in Fig. 22.
4. Summary
GRX-810 is a novel alloy for extreme environment applications,
however, a thorough investigation of the deformation mechanisms is
necessary to ensure the performance of GRX-810 L-PBF components.
This study investigates the critical deformation mechanisms in as-built
and HIP microstructures for varying elevated temperatures. The key
findings of this study are as listed below:
• The volume of porosity in GRX-810 samples is size-dependent,
suggesting bulk laser parameters are not optimized for thin-
walled or fine part features. These defects did not result in dis-
tinguished influence on the tensile mechanical properties such
as the YS and UTS of as-built and HIP samples tested for both
ambient and elevated temperatures in the build direction. While
size effects have been commonly observed in pre-existing AM
alloys [46,48,50], this work has shown the quasi-static tensile
behavior is not dictated by specimen thickness, irrespective of
testing temperature.
• YS and UTS experience constant decrease with the increase of
quasi-static tensile testing temperature, while elongation remains
stable at testing temperatures up to 871 ◦C and begins decreasing
above this temperature.
