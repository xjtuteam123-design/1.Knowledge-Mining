Tensile Strength of Zr–Ti Binary Alloy*
ZHOU Yun-Kai(周云凯), JING Ran(景然), MA Ming-Zhen(马明臻), LIU Ri-Ping(刘日平)**
State Key Laboratory of Metastable Materials Science and Technology, Yanshan University, Qinhuangdao 066004
(Received 2 September 2013)
Zr–Ti binary alloys are prepared using a nonconsumable tungsten electrode under Ti-gettered inert atmosphere
(argon). Microstructures are observed mainly as𝛼 phase using x-ray diffraction. A tensile test is performed to
investigate the tensile strength of a series of Zr–Ti binary alloys at room temperature. The findings indicate that
increasing Ti concentration results in an initial increase (<50at% of Ti) and then a decrease in tensile strength.
The Zr55Ti45 (at%) component exhibits the maximum tensile strength of 1216.68MPa, which is much higher
than that of pure Ti (increased by approximately 200%) or pure Zr (increased by approximately 100%). The
potential mechanisms for the remarkable tensile strength are solid solution strengthening and grain refinement.

Pure Zr and its alloys, such as Zr–Nb [1−5] and
Zr–Sn,[2] are extensively used in medical, chemical,
and nuclear industries because of their good bio-
compatibility, preeminent corrosion resistance, and
small capture cross section for thermal neutrons.[6−11]
However, scant attention has been given to their
strength and strengthening mechanisms. Zr-based
bulk metallic glass shows considerable improvements
in strength, but its potential application is restricted
by fragility, difficult fabrication into different shapes,
and so on.[12,13] Alloying is an effective method to
strengthen materials. Most alloying elements, such
as Sn, C and O, may form compounds with Zr. Ti
is an appropriate alloying element for Zr. Zr and Ti
belong to the IVB group in the periodic table and
have similar chemical characteristics. In the equilib-
rium phase transformation diagram of Zr–Ti, the bi-
nary alloy shows a solid solution alloy in both high (𝛽
phase, body-centered cubic structure) and low tem-
perature (𝛼 phase, hexagonal close-packed structure)
ranges.[14] Furthermore, pure Ti and its alloys have
low densities, high strength-to-weight ratios, and bet-
ter elasticities.[15−20]
A considerable improvement in tensile strength is
observed experimentally by alloying. A function is ob-
tained, which represents the relationship between the
tensile strength of the alloys and their compositions.
Based on this alloy system, Zr–Ti binary alloy is im-
portant for scientific research. The current study on
Zr–Tibinaryalloywillprovidetheoreticalreferenceon
the relationship between alloy composition and prop-
erties in the alloy system.
The prepared alloys contained pure Zr, 90Zr, 80Zr,
60Zr, 55Zr, 50Zr, 45Zr, 40Zr, 30Zr, 20Zr, and 10Zr,
as well as pure Ti (the composition of alloys is indi-
cated in mole percent hereafter). All ingots of Zr–Ti
binary alloy were prepared by arc melting a mixture
of pure Zr (99.7 wt%) and Ti (99.9 wt%) with a non-
consumable tungsten electrode under a Ti-gettered in-
ert atmosphere (argon). All ingots were converted and
re-melted to ensure compositional homogeneity.
Then, the ingots were cut into tensile test sheet
and normal sheet specimens using an electric spark
cutting machine near the center of the ingot. Prior
to the tests, all samples were polished to reduce the
effect of electric spark cutting. Conventional x-ray
diffraction(XRD)wasusedtoidentifythephaseofthe
specimens. Microstructure was analyzed under opti-
cal microscopy. The sheet specimens were prepared
for metallographic analysis using a standard metallo-
graphic procedure, followed by etching in a mixture
of hydrofluoric acid, nitric acid, and water (5:10:85 by
volume). Tensile tests were performed at room tem-
peratureusinganInstron5982tensiletestingmachine.
Figure 1 shows the XRD patterns of the Zr–Ti bi-
nary alloys. All diffraction peaks match those of the
𝛼 phase. Therefore, the Zr–Ti alloy crystal structures
are mainly 𝛼 phase. The diffraction peaks tend to
shift to low angle when Zr concentration increases.
The equilibrium phase transformation diagram of Zr–
Ti shows that the 𝛽-phase region expands as the Ti
concentration increases. However, no obvious𝛽-phase
diffraction peaks are found in the XRD results when
the Ti concentration increases (Fig.1). Banerjee et
al.[21] and Hatt et al.[22] studied a Zr–Ti binary alloy
and determined the factors for 𝛽 retention. The al-
loy was contaminated with interstitial alloy elements,
such as oxygen and nitrogen. Moreover, the addition
of 𝛽-stabilizing elements maintained the 𝛽 phase at
room temperature.

Figure 3 shows the optical micrographs of the Zr–
Tibinaryalloy. Themicrostructureevolvesalongwith
the change in alloy composition. The increase in Zr
concentration from<50at% results in thinner lamellar
𝛼 phase. Then, the lamellar𝛼 phase becomes thicker
again as the Zr concentration continues to increase.
The microstructure evolution trend is due to more Ti
(<50at%), resulting in an enlarged 𝛽-phase region.
Moreover, Ti reaches the crystal surface, which in-
hibits the 𝛼 phase growth during the cooling phase
transformation.
Figure 3 also shows the “basket weave” microstruc-
ture. In the 𝛽–𝛼 phase transformation, the orienta-
tions between the two phases are as follows:
{0001}𝛼//{110}𝛽,
{1120}𝛼//{111}𝛽.
At least 12 different orientations exist between the
𝛽 and 𝛼 phases. A number of possible orientations
result in repeated choices for𝛼-phase growth, which
finally forms a “basket weave” microstructure.[23,24]

Figure 4 demonstrates the tensile test results. The
alloys exhibit good tensile strength. Adding a small
amount of Ti leads to remarkable improvement in
the alloy tensile strength. Adding more Ti further
improves the tensile strength of the alloys. The
55Zr component exhibits the experimental maximum
strength of 1216.68MPa, with ductility in terms of
elongation reaching 7.12%.
The improved strength is due to the solid solution
strengthening and grain refinement. Lattice distor-
tion is the main strengthening mechanism of solid so-
lution strengthening. Figures 2(b) and 2(c) indicate

the increasing lattice distortion. The stronger solid
solution strengthening results in larger lattice distor-
tion. Solid solution strengthening causes lattice dis-
tortion, which increases the resistance to dislocation
movement. This phenomenon makes slip more diffi-
cult, thereby improving the alloy strength. Figure 3
demonstrates the relationship between alloy compo-
sition and grain size. According to the Hall–Petch
formula, fine grain strengthening will increase alloy
yield strength.[25] The inset in Fig.4 shows the true
stress–strain curve for Zr–Ti alloy. An analysis of the
results in Figs.3 and 4 show that the smaller grain
has higher yield strength. Grain refinement increases
grain boundary area, which brings the retarding ef-
fect on the dislocation glide, resulting in improved
strength.
The curve in Fig.4 shows the experimental varia-
tion by numerical analysis and fitting tensile test re-
sults. The corresponding function is as follows:
𝜎 = 0.0003𝑥3− 0.2715𝑥2 + 26.9617𝑥 + 496.0612, (1)
where 𝜎 is the strength of the alloys and𝑥 is the Zr
content (at%) in the alloys.
The curve fits well in Fig.4 (the maximum devi-
ation is approximately 90MPa). The curve visually
expresses the alloy property variation and provides a
reference to obtain the proper alloy composition with
goodproperties. Thecurvealsosuggeststhatthealloy
strength increases initially with an increase in Zr con-
centration (before 50at% of Zr), and then decreases.
This result is similar to that of Kobayashiet al.[26] Ac-
cording to Eq.(1), the compound of Zr–Ti alloys with
maximum strength is 54.6Zr alloys.
According to the experimental results, Zr–Ti bi-
nary alloys with 𝛼 phase exhibit remarkable ten-
sile strength because of solid solution strengthening
and grain refinement. Compared with Zr-based bulk
metallic glass, the ductility in terms of elongation
reaches 7.12%, with 1216.68MPa tensile strength, as
exhibited by the 55Zr alloy. The alloy has good po-
tential as a structural material.
