ABSTRACT 
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) has great potential in the manufacturing of difficult-to-cut Ni-based superalloys 
with complex geometries. The approach endows the alloys with multi-scale microstructure hierarchy, while its 
formation mechanism and contribution to mechanical properties are poorly understood. In this work, the hi -
erarchical architecture in LPBF-fabricated K418 Ni-based superalloys, in terms of 3D interlocked grains, low- 
angle grain boundaries (LAGBs), cellular structure, inter-cellular segregation, and nano-sized γ
′
precipitates 
and carbides, is comprehensively investigated. The formation of cellular structure is governed by the rapid so -
lidification condition, and the 3D interlocked grain structure is associated with the cell growth behavior regu -
lated by locally varied temperature gradient in the melt pool. The LPBF K418 alloys show a higher tensile 
strength but a slightly lower ductility at both room temperature and 600 
◦
C than their conventional cast 
counterparts. The superior strength of LPBF alloys is dominantly attributed to the strengthening effect of 
interlocked grain boundaries and cellular microstructure. This work demonstrates the capability of LPBF to 
manufacture Ni-based superalloys with hierarchical microstructures and high performance for the application of 
hot-section gas turbine components. 
1. Introduction 
K418 superalloys, a category of γ
′
-precipitation hardened Ni-based 
superalloy developed in China (analogous to IN713C alloys in the US), 
are widely used to fabricate hot-section components ( e.g. , low pressure 
blades and vanes) of gas turbine engines [ 1 ], due to their excellent 
mechanical properties and oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures 
[ 2 ]. Such high-temperature performances are relevant to complex 
chemical compositions with high concentrations of solute elements, and 
a dual phase microstructure consisting of a high volume fraction of L1
2 
ordered γ
′
-Ni
3
(Al, Ti) precipitates inside the disordered γ austenite 
matrix [ 3 ]. In practice, the repair or complete replacement of Ni-based 
superalloy components is always demanded [ 4 ], as they suffer from 
various in-service damages, including creep deformation, fatigue 
cracking, corrosion, and oxidation [ 5 ]. Moreover, γ
′
precipitation 
hardened superalloys are classified as difficult-to-cut materials through 
conventional machining methods due to their intrinsically high hardness 
and strength [ 6 ]. Therefore, novel technologies with the feasibility of 
shaping superalloys into complex geometries using cost-effective pro -
cesses are urgently required [ 7 ]. 
In the last few decades, the repair and restoration of Ni-based su -
peralloy components based on additive manufacturing (AM) technolo -
gies have attracted extensive interest [ 8 ]. LPBF, one of the most effective 
AM methods, offers great advantages of near-net shaping complex 
components [ 9 ]. During this process, a laser beam with sub-millimeter 
spot size scans along the pre-planned routes and selectively fuses the 
powder bed over a base metal or a previously deposited layer [ 10 ].

microstructure with length scales spanning several orders of magnitude, 
including melt pool boundaries, high and low angle grain boundaries, 
cellular walls, dislocation cells, and segregated elements. In particular, 
they proposed that the cellular structure, analogous to high angle grain 
boundary (HAGB), exhibits a Hall-Petch typed strengthening behavior. 
Despite the aroused controversy about this estimation [ 14 , 15 ], Liu et al. 
[ 16 ] demonstrated that the cellular structure is a dominant contributor 
to the strength-ductility synergy in LPBF-fabricated 316L stainless steels. 
Thus, understanding the formation mechanism of hierarchical ar -
chitecture and the interrelations between microstructural hierarchy and 
mechanical behavior is critical for the development of high-performance 
structural materials via LPBF approach. Nevertheless, these issues still 
remain poorly understood for most LPBF-fabricated superalloys. Li et al. 
[ 17 ] observed the hierarchical architecture in a Ni-based superalloy 
through laser direct energy deposition, whereas its contribution to me -
chanical behavior is unclear. Mohsin Raza et al. [ 18 ] explored the 
optimal LPBF process parameters for crack-free IN713C alloys and Zhao 
et al. [ 19 ] verified that the mechanical properties of LPBF IN713C alloys 
are superior to those of casting counterparts, while they did not pay 
much attention to the hierarchical architecture and the strengthening 
mechanism of LPBF IN713C alloys. Additionally, during LPBF process, 
the maximum heat flow direction, antiparallel to the building direction, 
facilitates the formation of columnar grains with a strong texture [ 20 ], 
which renders the mechanical behavior of LPBF alloys anisotropic [ 21 ]. 
As a consequence, a detailed characterization of grain structure is 
essential to unravel the underlying mechanism of mechanical perfor -
mance. However, the visualization of grain structures in the literature is 
almost based on 2D electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) method, 
which cannot capture complex 3D grain geometries. 
In this study, the hierarchical architecture of LPBF-fabricated K418 
superalloys was comprehensively studied by using multi-scale charac -
terization methods and the formation mechanism was discussed. 
Particularly, 3D EBSD technique was implemented for the visualization 
of irregular 3D grain structures that arise from complex solidification 
conditions and thermal history. Ultimately, the role of hierarchical ar -
chitecture on the mechanical behavior and deformation mechanism at 
both ambient temperature and 600 
◦
C was investigated.

The grain structure in XY plane was further characterized by using 
2D EBSD method. Fig. 3 a shows the obtained inverse pole figure map

4.1. The formation mechanism of hierarchical architecture 
4.1.1. The formation of cellular structure

Thermodynamic calculation by using the nickel based superalloy

According to traditional solidification theory, the transition of the 
solidification behavior from a planar to a cellular or to a dendritic 
morphology is dependent on the G / R ratio at the liquid/solid interface, 
where G is the temperature gradient and R is the growth rate [ 26 ]. It has 
been pointed out that cellular growth mode prevails if the criterion G / R 
> Δ T / D is met, where Δ T is the undercooling and D is the diffusion 
coefficient [ 39 ]. A sufficient large G / R ratio can be readily achieved 
during LPBF process, and the cellular structure is prevalent in various 
FCC alloys fabricated through LPBF [ 33 , 40 ]. Consequently, the LPBF 
process in this study supplies the thermodynamic conditions for the 
formation of cellular structure. 
The formation of cell structure during the solidification process of 
LPBF K418 alloys can be described as follows. At ~1344 
◦
C, the solidi -
fication begins with the formation of primary γ phase cells. Meanwhile, 
the cell growth is accompanied by significant element partitioning, that 
is, carbide forming elements ( i.e. , Ti, Nb, Mo, Cr and C) are rejected into 
the inter-cellular liquid. It should be noted that all the carbide forming 
elements have the melting points higher than nickel. This circumstance 
is similar to the criteria for the formation of a cellular structure proposed 
by Prashanth and Eckert [ 41 ]. Then the nucleation of nano-sized 
MC-type carbides from the inter-cellular liquid takes place near a tem -
perature of 1313 
◦
C. The solidification terminates with a eutectic reac -
tion L → γ + MC (~1277 
◦
C). After solidification, γ
′
phases precipitate 
from the supersaturated γ matrix when cooling down below the γ
′
solvus 
(~1158 
◦
C). Moreover, the combination of thermal stresses and phase 
transformation stresses during repetitive heating and cooling processes 
will stimulate dislocation nucleation in the inter-cellular region and cell 
interior ( Fig. 4 a).

local variation in the temperature gradient. Accordingly, the epitaxial 
grain broadens across not only multiple in-layer melt tracks but also

growth direction is locally changed. Such a grain growth mode will help

beneficial to the mechanical performance [ 42 , 43 ].

4.2. The contribution of hierarchical architecture to deformation behavior 
TEM investigation of the fractured samples was performed to unravel 
the deformation mechanisms at different temperatures. In the RT frac -
tured sample, dislocation tangles at the cell boundaries but relatively 
less dislocations within the cells ( Fig. 10 a and b) are visible. The for -
mation of this dislocation pattern is relevant to the pre-existing nano- 
sized MC-type carbides that act as a stationary impediment to disloca -
tion motion. Besides, dislocation bow-out within the cells is observed, 
where γ
′
precipitates serve as pinning points (marked by orange tri -
angles) against dislocation motion ( Fig. 10 b). Additional evidence of the 
interaction between γ
′
precipitates and dislocations is the paired dislo -
cations (denoted by blue arrows) as a result of dislocation cutting in the 
γ
′
precipitates. Basically, a leading dislocation cutting in a γ
′
precipitate 
will create an anti-phase boundary (APB); then a trailing dislocation 
with the same Burgers vector is required to remove APB and preserve the 
ordered structure of the γ
′
precipitate. 
For the 600 
◦
C fractured sample, a higher density of dislocations is 
introduced at the cell boundaries and within the cells. In some regions, 
for example the upper-right corner of Fig. 10 c, a considerable number of 
dislocations are trapped in the cell, demonstrating a remarkable dislo -
cation storage capability of cellular structures. The LAGBs ( Fig. 10 d), 
originating from the LPBF process induced thermal stresses and the 
slight deflection of cell growth direction, also act as obstacles to mobile 
dislocations. The bowed and paired dislocation features ( Fig. 10 e – f) 
signify the interaction between γ
′
precipitate and dislocations, similar to 
those found in the RT fractured sample. Occasionally, a stacking fault 
(SF) connected with a pair of dislocations is present in Fig. 10 f. The 
formation of SF, resulting from the dissociation of dislocations, is a 
common deformation mechanism at mid-temperature (600 – 800 
◦
C) for 
γ
′
precipitation hardened superalloys [ 45 ]. 
As interpreted in Fig. 10 g, the contribution of hierarchical archi -
tecture to the deformation mechanism and mechanical properties 
spanning from nano-, micro-, to macro-levels is discussed. At the 
beginning of plastic deformation, dislocations nucleate firstly within the 
cells, but their motion is extensively inhibited by the pre-existing γ
′
precipitates and the inter-cellular carbides. The γ
′
precipitates impede 
dislocation motion and create APBs when they are sheared by disloca -
tions. The finely dispersed nano-sized carbides with intrinsically high 
hardness are a strong barrier of dislocation transmission across the cell 
boundaries. The slightly varied orientations from cell to cell and the 
inter-cellular nano-sized carbides make the cellular structure a stable 
barrier to hinder dislocation motion at both RT and 600 
◦
C. Meanwhile, 
the synergetic effect of 3D grain interlock, irregular grain boundaries, 
and profuse LAGBs leads to an enhanced strength. However, because the 
inter-cellular carbides are too stiff to permit dislocations to pass through 
cells, dislocation tangles and local stress concentration at the incoherent 
carbide/matrix interface are responsible for the sacrifice of ductility. 
This is the main reason why LPBF samples exhibit slightly lower elon -
gations than the conventional cast counterparts ( Fig. 5 b). Benefited from

5. Conclusions 
To gain a better insight into the underlying formation mechanism of 
hierarchical microstructure and its contribution to mechanical behavior 
of LPBF-fabricated K418 Ni-based superalloys, the multi-scale micro -
structure, together with the mechanical properties and deformation 
mechanisms at different temperatures have been investigated. Several 
key conclusions can be drawn from this study: 
(1) The LPBF-fabricated superalloys show pronounced microstruc -
tural hierarchy, including 3D interlocked grains with strong cube 
texture, substantial LAGBs, sub-micro-sized cellular structure 
with inter-cellular element segregation, and nano-sized γ
′
pre -
cipitates and MC-type carbides. 
(2) Under a sufficiently large G / R ratio condition during LPBF, cell 
structure is formed with cell walls decorated by nano-sized MC- 
type carbides. After solidification, numerous coherent γ
′
phases 
in size of ~20 nm precipitate from the supersaturated γ matrix. 
(3) The formation of 3D interlocked grains is associated with the 
growth behavior of cells, which is governed by the locally varied 
temperature gradient directions in the melt pool. 
(4) The LPBF K418 alloys have a tensile strength of 1078 MPa and a 
tensile elongation of 7.1% at RT, while these values decrease to 
946 MPa and 6.3% at 600 
◦
C, respectively. Despite a reduced 
ductility, the strength of LPBF K418 alloys is superior to that of 
the conventional cast counterparts, which is mainly attributed to 
the hierarchical architecture, especially the cellular structure and 
3D interlocked grains. 
(5) Under tensile strains at both RT and 600 
◦
C, dislocations are 
blocked by γ
′
precipitates and inter-cellular carbides, that is, 
completely confined inside the cell structures. Besides, SFs rele -
vant to dissociated dislocations are induced by tensile strains at 
600 
◦
C.
