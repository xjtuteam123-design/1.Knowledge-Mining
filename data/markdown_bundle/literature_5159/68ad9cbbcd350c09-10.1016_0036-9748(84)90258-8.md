Introduction 
Rapid solidification from the melt can extend the solid solubility of elements which other- 
wise show negligible solid solutions at equilibrium in aluminum. This has opened a new avenue 
to advanced alloy design by forming a larger volume fraction of thermally stable precipitates. 
Thursfield and Stowell [I] made splat-particulate of Ai-8wt%Fe and obtained, after compaction 
and extrusion, UTS of 572 MPa, YS of 503 MPa and elongation of 5% at 293 K; the corresponding 
values at 573 K were 234 MPa, 214 MPa and 11.5%. To further increase the thermal stability of 
iron aluminide precipitates, the ternary system of AI-Fe-X (X being Cr, Nb, Hf, Mo, Ti, Si, W, 
Zr, V and rare earth elements) has been studied extensively. To date, two alloy systems, 
AI-8wt%Fe-2wt%Mo by Pratt and Whitney [2] and Ai-8wt%Fe-3.4wt%Ce by Alcoa [3], are superior 
amon~ the other experimental alloy systems. The former demonstrates UTS of 228 MPa and elonga- 
tion of 10% at 616 K and the latter UTS of 179 MPa and elongation of 7%, both failing to meet 
the 616K AFWAL goal of 276 MPa UTS [4]. This goal, representing approximately I00 K improvement 
in temperature capability over existing alloys, is related to aerospace structural component 
requirements for airframe and engine propulsion system application at service temperatures 
between 394 and 616K. 
The objectives of the present work are: (a) to produce substantial proportions of 
microeutectic microstructure, an optically featureless region that has solidified essentially 
without macroscopic solute partitioning during solidification; and (b) to provide high 
temperature-high strength aluminium alloys which can meet the AFWAL goals. These objectives 
have subsequently been met by incorporating a large volume fraction of thermally stable precipi- 
tates in the alloy in a finely and homogeneously distributed manner during P/M thermomechanical 
processing. Among various alloy systems studied only the results for AI-Fe-V alloys are 
reported here. 
Experimental 
A series of AI-Fe-V ternary alloys (Fe = 8 to 14 wt%, V = i to 3.5 wt%) were melt-spun into 
thin ribbon (25 to 40 ~m thickness and 6 to 25 mm width) using an Allied proprietary casting 
technique [5]. Briefly, approximately 2.7 kE of high purity elements were induction-melted in 
a quartz crucible, and the molten alloy was both jet-cast into a narrow ribbon of 30 to 40 ~m 
thickness and 6 mm width, and planer-flow-cast into a ribbon of 25 to 30 ~m thickness and 25 m~ 
width. Special precautions were adapted to protect the melt stream from oxidation and to elimi- 
nate the turbulence associated with casting, specific details of which are presented elsewhere 
[5]. 
Cast ribbons were then mechanically pulverized into powder. Approximately 2.7 Kg of 
screened powders (-60 + 250 mesh size with a log-normal size distribution) was compacted and 
extruded to a 9.5 mm by 56 mm rectangular bar (18:1 extrusion ratio) at 658 to 672 K. 
MicroVickers hardness measurements were conducted on as-cast and heat-treated ribbons while 
Rockwell hardness measurements were made on the extruded bar. The mlcroVickers hardness data 
obtained for heat-treated ribbons were used for determining consolidation conditions (time and 
temperature). Also conventional Archimedeian density measurements were made for extruded 
materials. Transmission electron microscopy was carried out on as-cast, heat-treated ribbons

Results and Discussion 
Depicted in FIG. i are optical microstructures of as-cast ribbon cross-sections obtained 
from three different wheel speeds, typically In an Al-13.9wt%Fe-2.2wt%V alloy. When jet cast at 
a low surface velocity the resultant microstructure has a heavily dendritic morphology with 
large coarse precipitates similar to that described by Jones [6] as "zone B." An increase in 
the relative surface velocity of casting results in two distinct mlcrostructures "zone A + zone B", 
the "zone A" being only on the wheel side. At high casting speeds the mlcrostructure is all 
"zone A." This optically featureless "zone A" morphology is predominantly a microeutectlc 
mlcrostructure (see also FIG. 2). This mlcro-structure is comprised of two regions: The back- 
ground is a uniform cellular microcrystalline network of aluminum solid solution (containing 
Fe and V) with fine binary intermetalllcs, at the interfaces, which are less than about 5-10 nm 
in size and are composed of aluminum and transition elements. 
To determine precipitation hardening response mlcroVickers hardness measurements were 
carrled'out on the heat-treated ribbons. Typical results are shown in FIG. 3 for 
Al-13.9wt%Fe-2.2wt%V and Al-ll.8wt%Fe-2.6wt%V alloys which were cast into both "zone A" and 
"'zone B" mlcrostructures. It is clearly seen that the material with "zone A" structure sustains 
higher hardness levels in the temperature range presently studied. Microstructural changes 
associated with isochronal annealing for 3600 s are shown in FIGS. 4 (a)-4(d). The as-cast 
mlcroeutectlc structure inherited from the high speed quenching remains essentially unchanged up 
to 623 K (FIGS. 4(a) and 4(b)). At 673 K the mierostructure begins to coarsen with some disso- 
lution of the metastable binary intermetallics (FIG. 4 (c)). At 723 K (FIG. 4(d)) the 
mlcrostructure is aluminum solid solution (with Fe and V) plus fine polygonal shaped equilibrium 
dispersoids approximately 0.05 - 0.2 ~m in size. 
FIGURE 5 (a) shows optical mlcrographs of three faces of a vacuum hot pressed slug extruded 
at an 18:1 extrusion ratio after soaking for 3600 s at 658 K exhibiting a homogeneous structure. 
FIG. 5 (b) shows a SEM micrograph of a sample etched with bromine to exhibit finely distri- 
buted precipitates. The average size of these precipitates is about |0 times larger than that 
observed in the ribbon heated at almost the same temperature (refer to FIG. 4 (c)), but is close 
to that found in the ribbon heated to a temperature some 100 K higher (refer to FIG. 4(d)). The 
coarser structure may be due to either adiabatic heating or strain enhanced coarsening during 
extrusion. The measured density of this particular alloy is 3018 kg/m 3 and Rockwell B hardness 
is 93.8. 
Depicted in FIG. 6 are the results of tensile testing for AI-13.9 wt%Fe-2.2 wt%V obtained 
from two different starting microstruetures "A" and "A+B". It is noted here that regardless of 
the starting mlerostructure the strength of this alloy clearly surpasses the AFWAL goal in the 
temperature range from ambient to 616 K and there is a marked difference between the two 
materials of different starting mlcrostruetures, especially with respect to ductility. Despite 
adequate strength~ the material with "A+B" structure reveals rather poor ductility at lower tem- 
peratures which is inadequate for the structural applications intended. Dynamic Young's modulus 
measurements were carried out on an Al-13.9wt%Fe-2.2wt%V alloy made from the totally "A" 
microstructue, vacuum-hot-pressed at 623 K and extruded at 658 K with an extrusion ratio of 18:1 
at selected temperatures. This alloy exhibits an elastic modulus at room temperature approxima- 
tely 40% higher than that of conventional aluminum alloys and retains its high modulus at ele- 
vated temperatures (96.5 GPa at room temperature, 91.0 GPa at 403 K, 86.2 GPa at 474 K ad 75.8 
GPa at 639 K).
