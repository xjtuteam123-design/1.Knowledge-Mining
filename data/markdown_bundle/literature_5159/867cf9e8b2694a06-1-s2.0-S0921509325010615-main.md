1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have emerged as a transformative class 
of materials that deviate from traditional alloying principles by incor -
porating multiple principal elements in near-equiatomic or high-molar 
ratios [ 1 , 2 ]. Unlike conventional alloys that rely on one or two domi -
nant elements with minor additions of others, HEAs form complex solid 
solutions that exhibit remarkable structural stability, enhanced me -
chanical properties, and exceptional resistance to extreme environments 
[ 3 – 5 ]. Among these, refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) stand out 
due to their superior thermal stability and high melting points, making 
them prime candidates for applications in extreme conditions such as 
those found in aerospace propulsion, nuclear reactors, and advanced 
energy systems [ 6 – 9 ].
RHEAs, characterized by their incorporation of refractory elements 
such as molybdenum (Mo), niobium (Nb), tantalum (Ta), titanium (Ti), 
and zirconium (Zr), have demonstrated significant promise as next- 
generation materials for high-temperature applications [ 10 , 11 ]. These 
alloys typically exhibit body-centered cubic (BCC) structures, which 
offer high strength and thermal resistance, but often suffer from limited 
plasticity at room temperature [ 7 , 12 ]. This trade-off between strength 
and plasticity has driven extensive research efforts aimed at optimizing 
their mechanical properties by modifying their phase composition, 
refining microstructures, and leveraging strengthening mechanisms 
such as solid-solution hardening, precipitation strengthening, and grain 
boundary engineering [ 13 , 14 ].
The development of RHEAs is particularly significant in the quest to 
surpass the performance limitations of conventional superalloys, 
including nickel-based and Nb-based alloys [ 7 , 15 ]. While these tradi -
tional materials have long been used in high-temperature environments, 
they suffer from creep deformation, oxidation susceptibility, and phase 
instability at extreme temperatures [ 15 ]. In contrast, RHEAs provide a

the challenge remains in overcoming the inherent brittleness of 
BCC-based structures and optimizing their high-temperature mechanical 
performance for real-world applications [ 16 ]. One of the most promising 
approaches to improving the mechanical properties of RHEAs is the 
introduction of ordered phases that coexist with the disordered BCC 
matrix [ 7 , 17 , 18 ]. This approach is inspired by the microstructural 
design of nickel-based superalloys, which derive their exceptional 
strength from the γ / γ ′ phase structure [ 12 , 18 – 20 ]. In these materials, a 
face-centered cubic (FCC) γ matrix provides plasticity, while the ordered 
L1

introducing ordered B2 phases within a disordered BCC matrix, effec -
tively enhancing strength without severely compromising plasticity [ 12 ,
17 , 18 , 21 ].
Aluminum (Al) plays a crucial role in stabilizing the B2 phase, as it 
exhibits strong chemical interactions with Ti and Zr, leading to the 
formation of nanoscale ordered precipitates [ 17 , 18 , 22 ]. These pre -
cipitates hinder dislocation movement, providing a substantial 
strengthening effect [ 17 ]. The presence of the B2 phase enhances me -
chanical performance through multiple mechanisms, including precip -
itation hardening, lattice distortion effects, and improved thermal 
stability [ 17 , 18 , 23 ]. However, excessive ordering can lead to embrit -
tlement, as it reduces the availability of independent slip systems 
necessary for plastic deformation [ 18 , 23 , 24 ]. Consequently, careful 
control of the volume fraction and distribution of B2 precipitates is 
essential to achieving a balance between strength and plasticity.
In addition to the B2 phase, Laves phases can form in Al- and chro -
mium (Cr)-containing RHEAs [ 25 , 26 ]. The Laves phase, characterized 
by its hexagonal close-packed (HCP) structure, is a topologically 
close-packed (TCP) intermetallic compound known for its high hardness 
and excellent thermal stability [ 26 ]. Cr, due to its small atomic radius, 
readily forms Cr
2
X-type Laves phases (where X = Ti, Zr, Nb, Hf, or Ta), 
which contribute to strengthening the alloy by acting as hard rein -
forcement particles [ 26 , 27 ]. However, similar to the B2 phase, excessive 
formation of Laves phases can lead to brittleness, particularly when 
these phases aggregate at grain boundaries and act as crack initiation 
sites [ 26 ]. The challenge, therefore, lies in optimizing the formation of 
both ordered phases to harness their benefits while mitigating their 
potential drawbacks.
While previous studies on RHEAs have primarily focused on single- 
phase BCC alloys, such as Mo

high-temperature strength but poor plasticity at room temperature [ 12 ,

thermal stability remains underexplored. Most prior research has been 
directed at reducing intermetallic compounds at grain boundaries to 
improve plasticity rather than leveraging these compounds for 
strengthening [ 12 , 28 ]. By intentionally incorporating ordered B2 and 
Laves phases, it is possible to achieve a synergy between strength and 
plasticity, providing a pathway toward the development of more robust 
high-entropy alloys.

behavior at both room and elevated temperatures. Advanced charac -

of next-generation RHEAs with tailored microstructures optimized for 
extreme environments. In doing so, this research contributes to the 
broader field of high-entropy alloy development, offering a viable 
strategy for overcoming the trade-off between strength and plasticity in 
BCC-based systems.

Cr, on the other hand, adds complexity to the alloy system. Cr is 
inherently a BCC element and can readily dissolve into the disordered 
BCC matrix, contributing to solid-solution strengthening when present

equilibrium and non-equilibrium solidification conditions [ 52 – 54 ].
The mechanism of ordered phase formation is closely tied to the 
atomic size mismatch and lattice distortion effects present in these al -
loys. Al and Cr possess smaller atomic radii (143 p.m. for Al and 129 p. 
m. for Cr) compared to Nb (147 p.m.), Ti (147 p.m.), and Zr (160 p.m.) 
[ 55 ]. When Al and Cr are introduced into the BCC matrix, they cause 
significant lattice distortions due to size differences, which is reflected in 
the XRD patterns as a shift of BCC peaks toward higher diffraction an -
gles. This shift indicates a reduction in lattice constants, a direct result of 
incorporating smaller atoms into the lattice. Initially, these smaller 
atoms dissolve into the matrix, enhancing solid-solution strengthening 
[ 56 ]. As their concentration increases, the chemical driving force for 
phase separation also increases, leading to the nucleation of ordered B2 
and Laves phases.
At low levels of Al and Cr addition (x = 0.2 to 0.4), Cr and Al pre -
dominantly remain in solid solution, with only limited formation of 
Laves phase particles observed along interdendritic regions. At these 
concentrations, the disordered BCC matrix remains the primary phase, 
with the mechanical properties of the alloy benefiting from solid- 
solution and limited precipitation strengthening. However, as Al and 
Cr content increases beyond x = 0.4, the formation of B2 and Laves 
phases becomes more pronounced. At x = 0.6, the alloy achieves an 
optimal distribution of these ordered phases, as confirmed by electron 
microscopy and XRD data. TEM reveals that B2 precipitates are uni -
formly distributed as nanoscale particles within the matrix, while Laves 
phases are predominantly located in interdendritic regions, where they 
act as reinforcing particles that contribute to load-bearing capacity 
during deformation.
The grain size evolution observed in the alloys also supports the 
formation of these ordered phases. The addition of Al and Cr signifi -
cantly refines the grain size, reducing it from an average of 87.9 μ m in

Furthermore, EBSD analysis shows that these phases contribute to an 
increase in dislocation density, particularly within the BCC regions, 
which in turn enhances the strength of the alloy via dislocation 
strengthening mechanisms.
Another critical factor in understanding the formation of ordered 
phases is the mixing entropy of the system [ 47 ]. High-entropy alloys are 
characterized by a high configurational entropy that stabilizes disor -
dered solid solutions. However, when specific elements like Al and Cr 
are introduced, they alter the local chemical ordering tendencies and 
reduce the overall configurational entropy, especially if they strongly 
prefer to form compounds. This explains why ordered phases emerge 
despite the general tendency for entropy to favor disordered structures 
in HEAs. Essentially, the enthalpic driving force for forming stable 
intermetallic compounds (e.g., B2 and Laves phases) outweighs the 
entropy-stabilizing effect at certain compositions.

Interestingly, while these ordered phases significantly enhance the 
strength of the alloys, their impact on plasticity depends strongly on 
temperature. At room temperature, the presence of a high volume 
fraction of B2 and Laves phases leads to brittleness, as dislocations are 
unable to bypass these hard obstacles, resulting in premature crack 
initiation and propagation [ 12 , 28 ]. However, at elevated temperatures 
such as 1000 
◦
C, thermal activation mechanisms enable dislocations to 
overcome some of these barriers, partially restoring plasticity. The 
increased temperature reduces the critical resolved shear stress (CRSS) 
required for dislocation glide and climb, allowing dislocations to bypass 
ordered precipitates through thermally activated processes such as 
cross-slip and climb-assisted Orowan bypassing. In particular, screw 
dislocations, which are prevalent in BCC structures, can cross-slip more 
easily at high temperatures, facilitating more uniform deformation and 
reducing the likelihood of localized stress concentrations that could lead 
to fracture.
Moreover, the cross-slip behavior of screw dislocations in B2 phases 
is especially relevant under high-temperature conditions [ 72 , 73 ]. As the 
temperature rises, screw dislocations can switch glide planes, enabling 
them to circumvent obstacles such as ordered precipitates and grain 
boundaries [ 74 ]. This process is crucial for retaining some plastic 
deformability in an otherwise strengthened alloy. Cross-slip reduces the 
accumulation of dislocations at phase boundaries, thereby minimizing 
stress concentrations and delaying the onset of crack formation. 
Consequently, the ordered-disordered phase synergy that may embrittle 
the alloy at room temperature becomes advantageous at elevated tem -
peratures, as it provides a strong yet deformable microstructure capable 
of withstanding high mechanical loads without catastrophic failure.
Another key aspect of the high-temperature strengthening mecha -
nisms is the synergistic interaction between different types of ordered 
phases and the disordered matrix [ 12 ]. The disordered BCC matrix re -
mains relatively soft and ductile even at elevated temperatures, 
providing a continuous phase that can accommodate plastic deforma -
tion. At the same time, the ordered B2 and Laves phases contribute to 
load-bearing and impede dislocation motion, thus enhancing strength 
[ 12 , 75 ]. This combination allows the alloy to achieve a unique balance 
of high strength and moderate plasticity that is essential for 
high-temperature structural materials. Notably, the careful control of 
the volume fraction, distribution, and size of these phases ensures that 
the alloy maintains sufficient deformability without sacrificing strength.
