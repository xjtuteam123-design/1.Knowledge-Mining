1. Introduction 33 
TiAl-based alloys, as a new generation of high-temp erature structural materials 34 
in the aerospace and automotive industries, have re ceived significant attention due to 35 
their attractive properties, such as lightweight, high modulus and elevated temperature 36 
performances [1–3]. However, the thermal processing windows of traditional ( γ+α2) 37 
TiAl alloys are narrow and the application of TiAl alloys is limited [4]. TiAl alloys 38 
containing the β phase show much better hot workability than ( γ+α2) TiAl alloys, even 39 
enabling the production of TiAl components using ne ar conventional hot-working 40 
processes and equipment [5,6]. This increases the e conomical feasibility of hot 41 
processing for the manufacturing of TiAl components. 42 
Recently, a series of β phase containing TiAl alloys has been developed, s uch as 43 
Ti-43.5Al-4Nb-1Mo-0.1B (TNM alloy) [7,8], Ti-42Al-1 0V [9], Ti-42Al-5Mn [10,11] 44 
and Ti-43Al-9V-0.3Y [12]. In general, the mechanica l properties of TiAl alloys are 45 
determined by their chemical composition and micros tructure. Studies [13–15] have 46 
shown that the fine-grained lamellar microstructure has the best balance between 47 
room temperature ductility and high temperature str ength. The microstructures and 48 
mechanical properties of TNM alloy have been report ed in Refs. [16,17]. The nearly 49 
lamellar TNM alloy microstructure exhibits a limite d ductility of ~1% of plastic 50 
elongation to fracture. The tensile properties of T i-42Al-10V alloys, with 51 
microstructure consisting of 70% fine lamellar colonies and 30% B2 and γ grains, was 52 
reported in Ref. [9]. Although its tensile strength is as high as 1334 MPa, the poor 53 
elongation was only 0.35% at room temperature. Simi lar to conventional ( γ+α2) TiAl 54 
alloys, a major challenge for β phase containing TiAl alloys is how to improve 55 
room-temperature ductility. 56 
For β phase containing TiAl alloys, the influence of the B2 (B2 is 57 
low-temperature ordered β) phase on the room temperature ductility is a cont roversial 58 
topic. Several works reported that the presence of the B2 phase in TiAl alloys 59 
deteriorated room temperature ductility [18,19], because it is a hard phase at room 60 
temperature. However, other works indicated that B2 phase containing TiAl alloys 61 
exhibited high room-temperature ductility [20]. Therefore, further research on how B2 62 
phase affects the room-temperature ductility of TiAl alloys is necessary 63 
In this study, a V- and Cr-stabilized TiAl alloy, T i-42Al-6V-1Cr (at.%), was 64 
fabricated, and an ( α+β) phase region extrusion process was adopted to opt imize its 65

2. Materials and methods 70 
The nominal composition of the TiAl alloy used in t he present study was 71 
Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr (at.%). The alloy was melted three t imes in a vacuum consumable 72 
electrode arc furnace. The parent materials used in the alloy were Ti sponge(99.9%), 73 
purity Al(99.99%), Al-V inter-alloy(the composition was 56.58% V , 42.98% Al, 0.18% 74 
Fe, 0.24 Si and 0.02% O) and granular Cr(99.9%).The final ingot size was ⌀210 × 75 
650mm. Billets of ⌀190 × 200 mm were cut from the ingot by turning mac hining and 76 
then canned in stainless steel. After heating to 12 50°C with a holding time of 3h, the 77 
canned billets were hot extruded with an extrusion ratio of 10:1 and annealed at 78 
900°C for 5h. Afterwards, the stainless steel was removed from the TiAl billets. 79 
The tensile tests of the TiAl alloy were conducted from 25 to 800°C on a MTS 80 
testing machine with an initial strain rate of 5×10 −4 s −1. Three tensile samples were 81 
tested for each test condition and the average value is reported. The yield strength (YS) 82 
was determined as the 0.2% offset stress. The speci mens were taken from the 83 
extruded billet with the tensile direction parallel to the extrusion direction. The 84 
specimens with gauge size of Φ3×15mm were prepared by electric discharge 85 
machining and polishing with 1200 sand paper. The n ano-hardness measurements of 86 
the microstructural constituents ( γ phase, B2 phase and γ/α2 lamellar structure) in 87 
as-extruded alloy were carried out on a nanoindente r XP (Agilent Technologies), 88 
using a Berkovich diamond indenter. For each consti tuent, 10 indentations were 89 
performed to obtain acceptable statistical hardness values. The samples for the 90 
nano-hardness test were electrolytically polished. 91 
The microstructures were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) 92 
with a back-scattered electron (BSE) detector and t ransmission electron microscopy 93 
(TEM). Phase analyses were conducted by X-ray diffr action (XRD). Samples for 94 
SEM-BSE observation were prepared by mechanical pol ishing. The foils for TEM 95

3.1. Microstructural analysis 111 
Figure 1 shows the as-cast microstructure of the Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr alloy. As shown 112 
in Figure 1(a), the microstructure is mainly compos ed of lamellae and a mixture of γ 113 
and B2 grains. These grains are distributed in the inner lamellar colony and colony 114 
boundaries. Figure 1(b) is a high magnification of the lamellar colony, the 115 
morphology and position of the γ/α2 lamellae and γ and B2 grains can be clearly 116 
observed. These γ and B2 grain mixtures are produced by the disconti nuous reaction 117 
of β/B2 →γ in the solidification and annealing processes [19]. 118 
In order to estimate the microstructure and phase s tate at the beginning of hot 119 
extrusion, the sample, which was treated at 1250°C for 3 h and then water cooled to 120 
preserve the high temperature state, was analyzed using SEM and XRD. Figure2 gives 121 
the SEM-BSE image of the microstructure at the begi nning of hot extrusion shows 122 
that it consists of only the α and β phases, without γ phase. According to our previous 123 
study on this alloy, the γ phase will completely dissolve when the temperatur e is 124 
higher than 1200°C. Figure 3 shows the XRD patterns of the as-cast, the beginning of 125 
hot extrusion and as-extruded states. 126 
Figure 4 shows the microstructure of the Ti-42Al-6V -1Cr alloy after the ( α+β) 127 
phase extrusion process. The microstructure consist s of fine-grained lamellar colonies 128 
and γ and B2 grains. The γ/α2 lamellar structure is the result of the γ lamellae 129 
precipitating from the high temperature α grains during the cooling process. At the 130 
same time, γ grains along the B2 grains are formed as the γ phase precipitates during 131 
the cooling from the high temperature β phase. 132

Figure 6 shows the tensile properties of the as-ext ruded Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr alloy at 155 
different temperatures. As shown in Figure 6(a), th e room temperature tensile curves 156 
of the three samples indicate that the tensile prop erties are very stable. The ultimate 157

more than 860 MPa, with δ up to 34%. According to the previous research [26,27], the 161 
brittle-to-ductile transition temperature (BDTT) of TiAl alloys is the temperature at 162 
which the plastic strain reaches 7.5%. The as-extru ded material has a BDTT less than 163

Compared with other β phase containing TiAl alloys, the present alloy ha s the 165 
better tensile properties, as shown in Table 2. The strength and ductility of TiAl alloys 166 
are mainly controlled by microstructural features [ 28–31]. Lamellar spacing and 167 
colony size are the key parameters in determining t he yield strength. The relationship 168 
between yield strength, lamellar colony size and sp acing can be approximately 169 
described by the Hall-Petch relation. The current s tudy reveals that the lamellar 170 
colony size and lamellar spacing were refined signi ficantly by hot extrusion in the 171 
(α+β) phase region. As is well known, microstructural refinement and homogenization 172 
are beneficial to ductility. It was considered that the high lamellar fraction, fine 173 
lamellar colony size and fine lamellar spacing in t he as-extruded alloy may be 174 
responsible for the high strength and improved ductility. 175 
The nano-hardness values of the B2 phase, γ phase and α2/γ lamellar colony in 176 
the as-extruded Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr alloy are shown in F igure 7. The evaluated hardness 177 
values were 5.3, 4.7 and 3.9GPa for the B2 phase, α2/γ lamellar and γ phase, 178 
respectively. The B2 phase was harder than the γ phase. Compared with the other TiAl 179 
alloys, the hardness difference between the B2 phas e and the γ phase is the smallest 180 
(in Figure 7(b)). The more similar hardness values are conducive to the coordination 181 
of deformation between the B2 and γ phases. Additionally, the hardness of the B2 182 
phase (5.3GPa) in the present alloy was much lower than the 7.2GPa for the 183 
Ti–43.5Al–4Nb–1Mo–0.1B alloy [32] and 8.5GPa for th e Ti–43Al–4Nb–2Mo–0.5B 184 
alloy [19]. The hardness of the B2 phase is highly dependent on β stabilizers. V and 185 
Cr considerably reduce the hardness of the B2 phase . The reduced hardness of the B2 186 
phase may be beneficial to the room temperature duc tility of TiAl alloys. The room 187 
temperature deformation mechanism of the B2 phase h as been discussed in detail in 188 
section3.3. The tensile ductility is not only affec ted by the microstructural features, 189 
but also by the alloying elements. Based on Refs.[3 3,34], V and Cr addition enhance 190 
the tensile ductility of TiAl alloys at room temper ature. The addition of Cr atoms to 191 
TiAl results in the increase of the electron densit y in bulk and grain boundaries of the 192 
alloy and produces a good effect on room temperatur e ductility [35]. Thus, the 193 
chemical composition of the Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr alloy ma y be one reason why the 194 
present alloy has better tensile ductility at room temperature than other TiAl alloys. 195

3.3. Deformation mechanisms 198 
For γ-TiAl based alloys, the tensile deformation mainly depends on the γ phase. 199 
Dislocations and twins were widely observed in both the globular γ grains and γ laths, 200 
while the α2 phase did not seem to deform, as shown in Figure 8 . As α2 is less 201 
deformable, high constraint stresses may develop in α2, which easily excites the 202 
fracture stress. Thus, the tensile elongation is la rgely governed by the volume fraction 203 
and distribution of α2, as described in the literature [38]. It can be se en from Table 1, 204 
the α2 lamellar width is much thinner than γ width in the as-extruded Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr 205 
alloy. The volume fraction of the α2 lamellae is also lower than that of γ lamellae in 206 
lamellar colony. This would be beneficial to tensil e deformation. A number of studies 207 
have analyzed the deformation structure of the γ phase [39–41]. It has been claimed 208 
that the γ phase deforms both by glide of slip systems, invol ving ordinary dislocations 209 
1/2<110] and superdislocations ((0 1/g33641) or 1/2<11 2/g3364]), and twinning 1/6<112]{111} 210 
produced by glide of ordered twinning. Figures 8(c) and (d) show an example, taken 211 
from different diffraction conditions. The high den sity of dislocations observed is 212 
visible with g=(111) and invisible with g=(002). Th ese are mainly ordinary 213 
dislocations having a Burgers vector 1/2<110], gliding on plane ( 1/g336411). There are some 214

Figure 10 shows dislocations in one of the TEM spec imens. The dislocations 225 
were imaged under different diffraction ( g) conditions. Table 3 summarizes the 226 
electron beam axes, diffraction g values, and corresponding dislocation contrast 227 
observed. It was determined that both dislocations A and B in Figure 10 are the <110> 228 
type. Trace analysis indicates that the slip plane is {111}. Thus, the slip system is 229 
{111}<110>. The presence of {111}<110> slip modes in the B2 phase would provide 230 
five independent slip systems [42], the {111}<110> slip system satisfies the von 231 
Mises criterion of five independent slip systems th at are necessary for a polycrystal to 232 
undergo a general homogeneous strain. Thus, the {111}<110> slip in Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr 233 
alloy appears to be a key contributor to the high d uctility. According to the literature 234 
[43], the slip of dislocations with <110> Burgers v ectors can reduce stress 235 
concentrations that could otherwise lead to prematu re fracture. An interesting feature 236 
of B2 intermetallic compounds is the wide variety o f deformation modes that may 237 
occur during straining. The <001>, <110>, <111> typ e dislocation were directly 238 
observed by TEM examinations [42–47]. The operation of a certain slip mode 239 
strongly depends on the alloy composition and defor mation conditions. The <001> 240 
type dislocation was the dominant deformation mode for NiAl [47]. The <001> and 241 
<111> dislocations were present in YAg and DyCu [45 ,46], The <110> type 242 
dislocations were observed in RuAl [42]. For CoTi [ 43], the <001> type dislocations 243 
were activated at low stresses. However, when sampl es deformed beyond a transition 244 
in the strain hardening behavior, the <110> type di slocations were activated. The 245 
questions remain about how the B2 phase forms <110> type dislocations in the 246 
Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr alloy, and further research is necessary. 247

The deformation twins in γ grains and dislocations in the B2 grains can be se en 250 
in Figure 11. It is noteworthy that the dislocation emission in the B2 grain occurs 251 
when the grain boundary is impinged by twins of adj acent gamma phase. The red 252 
arrows in Figure 11 indicate the intersection posit ions. Due to the deformation twins 253 
of the γ phase, a stress concentration is produced at the grain boundaries of the B2 and 254 
γ grains. The stress activates the dislocations in t he B2 phase and the stress 255 
concentration at the grain boundaries is released. This process avoids intergranular 256 
cracking due to the excessive stress concentration at the B2 and γ grain boundaries. 257 
This indicates that the B2 phase has a good deforma tion capacity and the B2 phase 258 
can coordinate the deformation of the γ phase. This result also provides a reasonable 259 
explanation for the high ductility of the as-extrud ed Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr alloy. In 260 
summary, it appears that the V and Cr-rich B2 phase has a sufficient number of slip 261 
systems available to make it more ductile. 262 
Based on the above analysis, the deformation abilit y of γ, B2 and α2 in 263 
Ti-42Al-6V-1Cr alloy are in order: γ ˃ B2 ˃ α2. On loading, the γ phase can deform 264 
more easily. Then, the strain hardening will occur with increasing strain and this will 265 
increase the flow stress necessary to continue to d eform the γ phase. Eventually this 266 
flow stress will be sufficiently high to start to d eform the B2 phase. In this study, the 267 
difference between the hardness of the B2 phase and the γ phase is lower. The more 268 
similar hardness values are conducive to the coordi nation of deformation between B2 269 
and γ phases. Therefore, the improved room temperature d uctility exhibited by our 270 
present alloy comes from two aspects. On the one ha nd, the microstructure of the 271 
as-extruded alloy was fine, uniform and it has the appropriate phase contents. On the 272 
other hand, due to the addition of V and Cr element s, the γ and B2 phases are more 273 
deformable. 274

Highlights 
A novel TiAl alloy with excellent tensile properties was manufactured by extrusion. 
The as-extruded alloy demonstrated a good combination of high strength and ductility. 
The dislocation structures of B2 phase grains were investigated in detail. 
The B2 phase was beneficial to the room-temperature ductility of the present alloy.
