ABSTRACT
AerMet100 (A100) ultra-high strength steel is renowned for its exceptional fracture strength and toughness, and 
favored in various fields due to its wide application temperature range. Unfortunately, even A100 ultra-high 
strength steel will still be damaged during prolonged high-temperature service, resulting in performance 
degradation. In this work, laser cladding technology was used to repair A100 ultra-high strength steel, and 
excellent repair results were obtained. The key innovation lies in the use of new FeCrCoNiAlTi dual-phase high- 
entropy alloy and Y
2
O
3 
particles repair materials, which makes the repaired layer obtain excellent medium-low 
temperature performance. The microstructure evolution, high temperature deformation behavior, and 
strengthening mechanism of the repaired specimens were studied in detail by high temperature compression 
experiments in the temperature range of 300 – 1073 K. Notably, the repaired specimens exhibit excellent me -
chanical properties, significant strain hardening, and good microstructural stability at 300 – 873 K. Interestingly, 
the short-range ordered L12 phase and Y
2
O
3 
particle induce a large number of deformation twins, similar to the 
twinning mechanism of Ni-based superalloys. The high strength is mainly attributed to the multiple interactions, 
such as dislocation-Y
2
O
3
, dislocation-stacking faults, dislocation-lomer cottrell locks, and dislocation- 
deformation twins, endowing the repaired specimen with continuous strain hardening capability.
1. Introduction
AerMet100 (A100) is an ultra-high strength martensitic steel, 
renowned for its exceptional fracture strength and toughness [ 1 , 2 ]. This 
alloy is highly favored in various critical fields due to its comprehensive 
excellent properties, particularly in applications such as aircraft landing 
gear, jet engine shafts, and key load-bearing structural components of 
nuclear reactors [ 3 , 4 ]. Additionally, the A100 alloy maintains excellent 
mechanical properties and stability even in high-temperature environ -
ments ranging from 673 K to 873 K [ 5 , 6 ]. Unfortunately, these com -
ponents are prone to damage during prolonged service, resulting in a 
decline in performance [ 7 , 8 ]. Given the high cost of replacing new 
components, it is particularly crucial to effectively repair damaged A100 
alloy parts [ 9 ].
Laser cladding technology, as an advanced surface repair process, 
has become an ideal choice for repairing damaged parts due to its 
advantages of having a small heat-affected zone and good metallurgical 
bonding [ 10 , 11 ]. This technology restores the geometric shape and 
surface properties of components by adding cladding material to the 
damaged areas and rapidly melting it using the high thermal energy of a 
laser beam [ 12 , 13 ]. Aditya et al. utilized laser cladding repair technol -
ogy (LCR) to achieve defect-free repair of A100 steel with a narrow heat- 
affected zone [ 11 ]. The repair zone, composed of quenched martensite 
and residual austenite, exhibits significantly higher microhardness and 
mechanical properties than the substrate [ 14 ]. It is worth noting that 
current research focuses on addressing the applicability of laser cladding 
technology for the repair of high-strength steel, but there is limited in -
formation available regarding the raw materials used for the repair [ 15 ].
Duplex high-entropy alloys (DP-HEAs) are typically composed of five 
or more elements in near-equiatomic ratios, forming solid solutions or 
ordered phases with high entropy, thereby providing high strength and 
good high-temperature stability [ 16 , 17 ]. FeCoCrNiAlTi is a typical DP-

3.3. Microstructure in the A100 substrate regions after deformation at 
873 K
To further clarify the microstructural characteristics of the A100 
substrate region after deformation at 873 K, TEM was used to analyze 
the deformed specimen. Fig. 6 (a and b) show that the A100 substrate, 
after deformation at 873 K, retains a lath-martensitic microstructure 
accompanied by dense dislocation tangles; the lath thickness is 
approximately 50 nm. A small number of ~35 nm nanoparticles are 
dispersed within the matrix ( Fig. 6 f). Consistent with previous studies, 
the high contents of C, Co, Cr and Ni in A100 favor the formation of the 
nanoscale carbides [ 7 , 8 ], so the particles can be identified as MC car -
bides. The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern from region 
I, recorded along the [011] zone axis ( Fig. 6 (c)), exhibits only BCC re -
flections without any additional phases, confirming that no phase 
transformation occurred during deformation at 873 K. As illustrated in 
Fig. 6 (d), dislocation entanglements preferentially develop at lath 
boundaries and in the vicinity of MC particles. Previous work has 
demonstrated that both martensite interfaces and MC particles strongly 
impede dislocation motion, thereby elevating the yield strength [ 9 ]. 
Notably, numerous stacking faults (SFs) are observed adjacent to the MC 
particles, as shown in Figs. 6 (e and f). It is therefore proposed that, 
during deformation at 873 K, MC particles first act as barriers to dislo -
cation glide via Orowan looping or particle-shearing, generating local -
ized stress concentrations [ 3 , 4 ]. Subsequent interactions between 
dislocations and the martensite laths/MC particles promote the forma -
tion of SFs, ultimately imparting to A100 a high yield strength and 
pronounced strain-hardening rate.

As per the analysis presented, Fig. 9 illustrates the mechanism of 
microstructural evolution in the LCR-specimen during the compression 
deformation at 873 K. Figs. 9 (a and b) depict the initial microstructure of 
the LCR-sample and the microstructure after deformation at 873 K,

process, the A100 substrate and LCR layer regions were explained 
respectively. In the A100 substrate region, the microstructure after high 
temperature deformation is still M, but the number and volume of MC 
particles increase [ 2 ]. This indicates that the microstructure of A100 
substrate has good stability at 873 K. Under the influence of high tem -
perature, MC particles will precipitate and grow extra. In the LCR-layer 
region, the microstructure is still BCC and FCC phases, and it has good 
stability after deformation at 873 K. Furthermore, a variety of defor -
mation microstructure at 873 K are activated, such as dislocation bands, 
stacking faults (SFs), Lomer-Cottrell locks (L-C locks), and deformation 
twins (DTs) [ 40 ], as depicted in Fig. 9 (b).
4.2. Multiple strengthening mechanism of the LCR-specimen deformed at 
873 K
To understand the potential mechanism of strain hardening ability 
and large compressive plasticity in LCR-layer region, the dynamic evo -
lution of microstructure under different strains was studied. Fig. 10
shows the microstructure of SFs and L-C locks after various strains at 
873 K. At the strain of ~5 % ( Figs. 10 (a1 and a2)), the dislocation 
slipping is activated in the appearance of SFs on the slip planes. Upon 
further straining to approximately 15 %, as depicted in Figs. 10 (b1 and 
b2), the density of SFs (indicated by arrows) increases significantly, 
exceeding that observed at the 5 % strain. With strain continues to in -
crease, the plane slip deformation is triggered, which is characterized by 
the obvious high density of SFs, along with numerous SF intersections 
(L-C locks), as illustrated in Figs. 10 (c1 and c2).
Therefore, L-C locks may not be very high in the early stage of plastic 
deformation, which lead to low strain hardening ability in the early 
stage of plastic strain. As the deformation progresses, the density of the 
SF network and the L-C locks will increase. The effectiveness of L-C locks 
in strain hardening comes from their ability to accumulate dislocations 
from two aspects [ 41 ]: on the one hand, when two dislocations meet to 
produce an L-C locks, it stabilizes the SFs network by fixing the

4.3. The short-range ordered L12 particle phase leads to a high hardening 
rate
The microstructure of the LCR-specimen was examined to compre -
hend how the deformed microstructure forms, after deformation at 873 
K. Fig. 12 (a) shows DF image of the LCR-layer after deformation at 873 
K. There are high-density dislocation tangles and deformation bands in 
the FCC region of the LCR-layer. Figs. 12 (b and c) show the BF image of 
dislocation bands, in which the dislocation interaction is obvious, 
showing the operation of multiple slip systems. In addition, a large 
number of L12 phases are embedded in the deformation band [ 49 ], as 
shown in Fig. 12 (c). Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that the L12 
phase particles provide the deformation resistance of the LCR-specimen 
[ 50 ]. Previous studies have shown that the activation of SF, l-C locks and 
DTs leads to the continuous strain hardening ability of the two-phase

deformation, the change of back stress and compressive stress

to additional work hardening.

5. Conclusions

by laser cladding technology using a new combination of FeCrCoNiAlTi 
HEA + Y
2
O

particles as repair materials. The microstructure evolution, 
high temperature deformation behavior and strengthening mechanism 
of the repaired samples were studied in detail by high temperature 
compression experiments at 300 K, 673 K, 873 K, and 1073 K. The key 
findings are presented as follows: 
(1) Excellent mechanical properties and significant strain hardening 
phenomenon: The LCR-specimen exhibits excellent mechanical 
properties and significant strain hardening phenomenon. At 300 
K, its ultimate compressive strength is as high as 2364 MPa. Even 
at 1073 K, the ultimate compressive strength of the LCR-specimen 
remains above 691 MPa, despite the strain softening 
phenomenon.

of the LCR-specimen, the microstructure characteristic remain 
almost unchanged after deformeation at 873 K, indicating that it 
has good thermal stability. More interestingly, deformation mi -
crostructures were observed after deformation at 873 K, such as 
stacking faults, Lomer-Cottrell locks, and deformation twins in 
the LCR layer.
