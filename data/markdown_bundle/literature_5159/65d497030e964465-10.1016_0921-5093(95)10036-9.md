Abstract 
Niobium aluminide (Nb,Al) base intermetallic compounds are potential candidates for applications at temperatures above the 
operating temperatures of currently used superalloys, provided their low-temperature ductility and fracture toughness and 
high-temperature oxidation resistance arr: improved. Two-phase (Nb,,/Nb,Al) materials with different volume fractions depending 
on the aluminium content were prepared by ingot metallurgy followed by isothermal forging and heat treatment. The addition of 
tantalum (about 10 mol%) has no influence on the phase content, volume fraction and grain size. The compressive strength of 
both the binary and ternary alloys investigated depends strongly on the aluminium content. Increasing the volume fraction of Nb,, 
phase decreases the strength at all temperatures tested. The addition of tantalum improves the high-temperature strength of 
Nb,,/Nb,Al alloys. 
Key~orn’s: Nb,AI; Ingot metallurgy; Microstructure; Tantalum addition; Microhardness; High-temperature strength 
1. Introduction 
The demand for high performance structural materi- 
als at operating temperatures higher than 1000 “C in 
advanced aerospace propulsion systems has led to the 
development of many intermetallic compounds based 
on refractory metals. Of these Nb,Al is one of the most 
promising because of its high melting point, low density 
and good high-temperature strength [I]. Single-phase 
Nb,Al with a complex Al5 structure is found in the 
composition range between 21 and 23 mol% Al at 1600 
“C in the binary Nb-Al phase diagram [2,3]. Though 
this complex structure is responsible for the good high 
temperature strength, it limits the number of slip sys- 
tems and causes low ductility [2]. However, it exists 
with a bee niobium-rich solid solution (Nb,,) over a 
wide composition range between about 9 and 18 mol% 
Al and offers the possibility of manufacturing in situ 
composites. The “ductile-phase toughening” of brittle 
intermetallic phase may improve the ductility and frac- 
ture toughness. Mechanical properties of two-phase 
materials have been investigated recently [l-7]. The 
compressive yield strength of niobium aluminides pre- 
pared by powder metallurgy (PM) techniques was

Single-phase Nb,Al possessed an extremely high 
strength at high-temperatures. Increasing the amount of 
Nb,, phase decreased considerably the high-temperature 
strength. The ductile-brittle transition temperature 
(DBTT) also decreased with increasing amount of Nb,, 
phase. The inclusion of Nb,, improved the fracture 
toughness and fatigue crack growth behaviour at room 
temperature [6,7]. The improvement in low-temperature 
ductility and toughness by incorporation of a ductile 
phase has been achieved at the cost of high-temperature 
strength. So, it is necessary to improve the low-temper- 
ature ductility and fracture toughness without sacrific- 
ing the high-temperature strength. 
Ternary alloying of refractory intermetallics was re- 
ported by Shah and Anton [8]. Nb,Al has a very wide 
alloying potential with good solubility for many of the 
refractory and metalloid elements. Additions of vana: 
dium and chromium were found to give tough mi- 
crostructures [8]. The effects of alloying additions on 
the mechanical properties of conventional niobium al- 
loys were widely investigated [g-12]. Tungsten and 
molybdenum additions cause significant strengthening 
but degrade the low-temperature ductility of niobium

Powder metallurgy (PM) techniques, including the 
infiltration process developed for manufacturing super- 
conducting Nb,Al wires, were successfully employed in 
the preparation of bulk niobium aluminides [4,5]. How- 
ever, Nb,Al alloys fabricated by the above processes 
contained oxide particles such as alumina in spite of 
processing in argon. In addition, the impurity level of 
the materials processed by PM techniques tends to be 
high, and therefore the mechanical properties may be 
significantly influenced in a similar manner to conven- 
tional niobium-base alloys [14]. Recently, the “clad- 
chip extrusion process” was also employed in the 
preparation of Nb,Al [15]. This process consists of clad 
rolling niobium and aluminium plates, cutting the clad 
sheet into pieces, filing up the pieces in a container, 
extruding the billet, rolling the extruded bar and diffu- 
sion annealing. Though this resulted in an “alumina- 
free” material, the process itself is very complex. In 
addition, it is difficult to prepare bulk samples for 
evaluation of the mechanical properties by the above 
mentiond processes. 
In the present study, the microstructure, hardness 
and strength of binary and tantalum alloyed two-phase 
Nb,,/Nb,Al with different aluminium contents were in- 
vestigated using samples prepared by ingot metallurgy 
(IM). Strengths at low and high temperatures of the 
binary and tantalum added Nb,,/Nb,Al are reported. 
2. Test materials and experimental procedure 
Binary niobium aluminides with aluminium content 
ranging from 16 to 25 mol% Al and ternary materials 
with 16-25 mol% Al and 10 mol% Ta were used for the 
present investigations. These aluminium contents will 
result in two-phase materials with different volume 
percentages of Nb,, and Al5 phases (considering the 
loss of aluminium during melting). The tantalum con- 
tent was limited to 10 mol% since more than this will 
increase the density of the alloy and may cause signifi- 
cant solid solution hardening at room temperature. 
High purity niobium and aluminium were melted using 
a non-consumable tungsten electrode in a water cooled 
copper hearth into 40 mm diameter by 8 mm thick 
buttons: The buttons were flipped and remelted three 
times for homogeneity. For preparing ternary materials, 
tantalum was added to the Nb-Al buttons and 
remelted twice since melting of all the three elements 
together resulted in higher aluminium loss and inhomo- 
geneous distribution of aluminium. The buttons were 
cut into pieces using a diamond cutter and remelted 
into rectangular bars of dimensions 55 mm x 25 
mm x 14 mm (thickness). Though remelting caused 
considerable loss of aluminium (about 3-4 mol% in 
binary and 5-6 mol% in ternary materials) homoge- 
neous distribution of aluminium and tantalum was 
obtained. 
The cast materials were homogenization treated at 
1900 “C for 3 h in the case of binary and for 5 h in the 
case of ternary material in a vacuum of better than 
4 x 10 -4 Pa. The homogenization treated block was 
isothermally forged at low strain rates at 1600 “C to 
approximately 50% of its original height in a vacuum of 
better than 4 x 10m4 Pa. Isothermally forged materials 
were annealed at 1600 “C for 60 h in a vacuum of about 
3 x 10m4 Pa. The chemical compositions of the an- 
nealed samples are given in Table 1. Samples for mi- 
crostructural analysis were prepared from different 
parts of the annealed material. Specimens were mechan- 
ically polished up to 1500 grade Sic emery paper and 
lapped using lapping tape up to 2000 grade. The sam- 
ples were electropolished using 10% HzS04 in methanol 
at - 46 “C and 18 mA. Electropolished samples were 
chemically etched using HNO,, HF and H,O in the 
ratio 1: 1: 10. The microstructures were characterized by 
a scanning electron microscope using the backscattered 
electron mode which provides contrast dependent pri- 
marily on the atomic number and the proportion of 
elements present in the phase. X-ray diffraction of the 
annealed samples was performed to identify the phases. 
Chemical compositions of the constituent phases were 
measured using electron probe micro-analysis (EPMA). 
Compression tests were carried out using annealed 
specimens of dimensions 2 mm x 2 mm x 5 mm. Tests 
were performed at an initial strain rate of 1.67 x low4

The present experimental investigations show clearly 
that the addition of a small amount of tantalum (about 
10 mol%) to niobium aluminide retains the softness of 
the Nb,, phase (and retains the ductility) at room 
temperature and improves the high-temperature 
strength of the Nb,,/Nb,Al alloys which are most 
promising for structural applications. No significant 
solid solution strengthening of the tantalum alloyed 
Nb,, phase at room temperature (Fig. 3) is beneficial in 
retaining the low-temperature ductility of the ductile 
phase and thereby that of the composites. At present 
the influence of ternary alloying on the mechanical 
properties of niobium aluminide base intermetallic 
compounds has not been investigated. However, con- 
siderable work has been carried out on conventional 
niobium alloys [g-12]. Generally, it is accepted that the 
addition of tantalum (about 10 mol%) to niobium does 
not result in solid solution hardening although it in- 
creases the melting point and density of the alloy [lo]. 
The present experimental results show also that the 
addition of a small amount (10 mol%) of tantalum 
causes no significant change in the hardness values of 
Nb,,, although a larger amount of tantalum causes 
considerable solid solution hardening [17]. Solid solu- 
tion strengthening effects in bee metals have been inves-

Saito et al. [IQ have found that the addition of tanta- 
lum to Nb,Al single-phase alloys prepared through the 
clad-chip extrusion process improves the high-tempera- 
ture strength. The present results are in good agreement 
with their results. Further experiments are needed to 
identify the mechanisms causing the increased high- 
temperature strength of single-phase (A15) Nb-Al-Ta 
intermetallic compounds. 
The dependence of composition on the yield stress of 
Nb,AI alloys prepared by PM techniques has been 
reported by Murayama and coworkers [4,5]. They also 
reported the decrease in yield stress due to the presence 
of Nb,,. These results are in agreement with the present 
results. The presence of the low strength Nb,, phase 
decreases the strength of Nb,,/Nb,Al. Kumagai [I91 has 
observed that the dislocation density in Nb,Al alloys 
deformed at high temperatures is considerably higher in 
the Nb,AI grains adjacent to an Nb,, grain. Dislocation 
multiplication may occur easily at Nb,,-Nb,Al 
boundaries, thereby reducing the strength of the two- 
phase alloys. 
5. Conclusions 
The microstructure of the Nb,,/Nb,Al alloys depends 
strongly on the aluminium content. Two-phase alloys 
with different volume fractions of. ductile Nb,, phase 
and brittle Al5 phase are obtained. Microstructural 
factors such as phase content, grain size, morphology, 
etc. are not affected by the addition of tantalum of 
about 10 mol%. 
The strength of Nb,,/Nb,Al alloys depends strongly 
on the volume fraction of Nb,, phase. Increasing the 
volume fraction of Nb,, phase decreases the strength at 
all temperatures. The room-temperature hardness of the 
solid solutions depends strongly on the aluminium con- 
tent. The addition of tantalum causes no significant 
change in the room temperature hardness of solid solu- 
tions. 
The addition of tantalum improves the strength of 
Nb,,/Nb,Al.
