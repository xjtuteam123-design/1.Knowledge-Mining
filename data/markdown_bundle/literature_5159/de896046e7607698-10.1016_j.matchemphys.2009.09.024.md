abstract
The high temperature oxidation behavior of HVOF sprayed ceria added NiCrAlY coatings on superalloys
has been studied. Oxidation kinetics of the bare and coated superalloys in air at 900 ◦ C under cyclic
conditions was investigated. X-ray diffraction, ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscope with energy
dispersive spectroscopy and X-ray mapping were used to analyse the scales formed on the surface of
the oxidised samples. The NiCrAlY–0.4 wt.% CeO
2 coated specimen showed negligible microspalling of
the scales. The incorporation of ceria in NiCrAlY powder has contributed to the development of adherent
oxide scale, in the coating, at elevated temperature and provided the better oxidation resistance.

1. Introduction
An ever increasing demand on high performance of superal-
loys makes the surface coatings more favorable than ever before
as they offer cost-effective ways to combat degradation resulting
from mechanisms such as wear, oxidation, corrosion, or failure
under an excessive head load without sacriﬁcing the bulk proper-
ties of the component materials [1]. Many components operating
at high temperature are coated or surface treated to achieve the
required properties. Coatings are generally applied to provide oxi-
dation, corrosion or thermal protection depending on the nature of
the operating environment and thermal loads to be endured [2].I t
is important to understand the nature of all types of environmen-
tal degradation of metals and alloys as vividly as possible so that
preventive measures against metal loss and failures can be econom-
ically devised to ensure safety and reliability in the use of metallic
components [3]. Cheruvu et al. [4] reported that the replacement
cost of the hot-section components alone can exceed 35% of the
cost of a new unit of a gas turbine. Pennefather and Boone [5] have
shown in their studies that the success of a coating in a high tem-
perature application is measured by its ability to remain in place,
to resist oxidation, and also to avoid cracking.
The oxidation of alloys depends on the rates at which anion and
cation transport could occur through the crystal lattice or along
the grain boundaries in the oxide [6]. The oxidation behavior is
characterized by the inward diffusion of species from gaseous envi-
ronments; oxygen being one of the important species. It causes loss

or cobalt based alloys are not sufﬁciently protective above 500 ◦C.
Hence, these alloys are alloyed with chromium, aluminum or silicon
to form more protective oxides (scales) of chromia, alumina or sil-

stoichiometric to maintain low ionic transport, stress free at oper-
ating high temperatures to reduce scale failure, adherent, free of
defects like pores and cracks to prevent short circuit transport of
reactants [8], homogeneous and stable as possible to constitute a
good diffusion barrier [9]. As the interactions of materials with the
environment begin at the surfaces, surfaces play a crucial role in
many physical phenomena [10].
The decrease in the oxidation rate and improvement in the
adherence of scales are due to prevention of void formation at the
alloy–oxide interface and to structure of the scales. The addition
of reactive elements also reduces the segregation of impurities at
the oxide–alloy interface which otherwise degrades the adherence
of oxide scales [11]. The adherence of scale is very important in
service conditions, where alloys are subjected to thermal cycles of
heating and cooling. An addition of small percentage of active ele-
ments to the high temperature alloys has a beneﬁcial effect on the
properties of the protective scales for providing enhanced oxidation
and corrosion resistance under isothermal and cyclic conditions
[12–14]. DeMasi-Marcin and Gupta [15] reported that the metallic
coating systems such as diffusion aluminides and MCrAlY overlay
coating with active element additives (Si, Hf and Y) exhibit higher
oxidation resistance. Coatings with rare earth additives are basi-
cally designed to form a thin tenacious uniform aluminum oxide
layer, which acts as an effective barrier to prevent oxidation and
deterioration of the substrate alloys. Transport processes in grow-
ing scales are of importance primarily because they determine the

High velocity oxy-fuel (HVOF) processes belong to the family of
thermal spraying technologies and are capable of producing coat-
ings with lower porosity, higher hardness, superior bond strength
and less decarburization than many of the other thermal spray-
ing methods [17–19]. The main advantage of HVOF process is the
shorter residence time in the ﬂame and the higher kinetic energy
of the particles impacting. This produces a dense coating with less
degradation of the powder during spraying [20].
High temperature oxidation behavior of HVOF sprayed NiCrAlY
coatings with ceria addition on Ni- and Fe-based superalloys is not
subjected to a detailed study so far. Degradation by oxidation is
one of the main failure modes of hot-section components in gas
turbines, so an understanding of the oxidation resistance is very
necessary for superalloys [21]. Therefore, the high temperature oxi-
dation behavior of HVOF sprayed NiCrAlY–0.4 wt.% CeO
2 coatings
on Ni- and Fe-based superalloys at 900 ◦C, under cyclic conditions,
has been studied in the present work. The oxidation studies under
cyclic condition constitute a realistic approach for understanding
corrosion of superalloys as reported in the literature [22]. The oxi-
dized specimens were analysed by various analytical techniques
such as XRD, FESEM/EDS and X-ray mapping to elucidate the cor-
rosion mechanisms of coated and uncoated superalloys.
2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Substrate materials and coating formulation
The superalloys substrates viz. Superni 76, Superni 750 and Superfer 800 were
procured from M/s Mishra Dhatu Nigam Limited, Hyderabad, India, in the form of
rolled sheets. The chemical compositions of the superalloys are given in Table 1 .
Each specimen measuring approximately 20 mm × 15 mm× 5 mm was cut from the
rolled sheet. The specimens were polished and grit blasted (grit 20) with alumina
powder prior to HVOF coating.
A commercially available NiCrAlY powder of chemical composition
Ni–22.1Cr–10.4Al–1.01Y (wt.%) supplied by M/S H.C. Starck, Germany was
used in this study. An additive CeO
2 (0.4 wt.%) with 99.99% purity was added in
to NiCrAlY powder. Hipojet-2100 HVOF thermal spray system using liqueﬁed
petroleum gas (LPG) fuel has been used to develop the coatings on the three
different superalloys. The particle size of the NiCrAlY powder ranges between 1 and
20 /H9262m and the ceria powder size ranges between 1 and 3 /H9262m. The FESEM image of
the coating powder (Fig. 1a) indicates the spherical morphology of the particles and
the polished surface ( Fig. 1b) of the coating consists of ﬂat splats with varying ceria
composition along the splat boundaries. The spray parameters used for depositing
the coatings are given in Table 2 . All the process parameters were kept constant
throughout the coating process. The specimens were cooled with compressed air
jets during and after spraying.
2.2. Oxidation studies under cyclic conditions
Cyclic oxidation studies were performed in air environment at 900
◦ C for 100
cycles. Each cycle consists of 1 h heating at 900◦ C followed by 20 min cooling to room
temperature. The specimens were subjected to weight change measurements at the
end of each cycle with the help of electronic balance Model CB-120 (Contech, India)
having a sensitivity of 1 mg. The spalled scale if any was also included during the
weight change measurements. The physical conditions of the exposed specimens
such as changes in the color, spallation, disintegration of the coating, cracks in the
surface etc., if any, were observed visually from the macrographs. Weight change
measurement with time was made to determine the kinetics of oxidation. The scale
formed on the surface of the oxidised samples was characterised by various ana-
lytical techniques such as X-ray diffraction (XRD) and FESEM (FEI company, Quanta
200F). X-ray diffraction patterns of the samples were obtained by a Bruker AXS D-8
Advance Diffractometer (Germany) with Cu K /H9251radiation and nickel ﬁlter at 30 mA
under a voltage of 40 kV. The specimens were scanned with a scanning speed of
2

directly on the diffraction pattern. These‘ d’ values were then used for identiﬁcation
of various phases with the help of JCPDS data cards. The genesis software was used to
analyse the compositions (wt.%) of the oxide scale formed after oxidising at 900
◦ C.
X-ray mapping analysis was used to analyse the cross-sectional details.

4. Discussion
The high temperature oxidation process has followed a diffu-
sion controlled mechanism at 900 ◦C. It is noticed from the visual
observation that the scale formed on the surface of the coating was
uniform and free from cracks. The green color of the oxide scale
indicates the presence of NiO which is similar to the ﬁndings of
Bornstein et al. [23], Sidhu [24] and Mahesh et al. [25,26]. In case of
bare superalloys, the initial rate of oxidation was high which grad-
ually levels off with the formation of oxide layers. It was clearly
seen that the bare superalloys deviated from the parabolic law.
Bare Superni 750 showed the maximum weight gain but the mini-
mum was shown by bare Superfer 800. In case of NiCrAlY–0.4 wt.%
CeO
2 coated superalloys, the rapid weight gain of the coated alloys
was observed only during initial cycles of oxidation study and it
may be attributed to the rapid formation of oxides at the coating-
splat boundaries as well as due to the penetration of the oxidizing
species along the splat boundaries/open pores. However, the coat-

into the inner portion of the coating gets slowed down once the

oxidation behavior even with the prolonged exposure time. Zhang

Addition of fractional amount of ceria into NiCrAlY coating
enhances its oxidation resistance by improving the scale adhesion.
Streaks of cerium oxide formed along the splat boundaries preclude
the oxidizing species getting into the coating and subsequently into
the substrate alloy.

NiO + Cr2O3 = NiCr2O4 (i)
The formation of spinels on the top scale may further enhance
the oxidation resistance due to their much smaller diffusion coef-
ﬁcients of the cations and anions than those in their parent oxides
[32].
The surface EDS analysis revealed the presence of cerium oxide
in scale in a minor amount. This cerium oxide helps in better adhe-
sion of the oxide scale with the coating when exposed at 900
◦C. The
top surface of the scale consists of oxides of aluminum, chromium
and nickel. It is observed from the surface-scale morphology that
the splats are rich in nickel and the region around the splats con-
sists of higher percentage of aluminum and chromium. It is noticed
from the X-ray mapping analysis that the coating is partially oxi-
dised and oxygen has not penetrated into the substrate superalloys.
This indicates the protective nature of the coating in the given envi-
ronment at elevated temperature. The formation of cerium oxide
along the intersplat regions in the top surface of the scale and in
the cross-section might have attributed to the better adhesion of
the oxide scale to the coating and thus enhanced the performance
of the coating at elevated temperature. It is well known in the lit-
erature [8] that small amount of ceria present in the oxide scale of
base alloys helps in the better adhesion of the oxide scale and bet-
ter oxidation resistance of base alloys. The use of reactive elements,
especially rare earths (RE) to improve high temperature oxidation
resistance of chromia and alumina forming alloys is well known.
The improvements are in the form of reduced oxidation rates and
increased scale adhesion. An addition of low levels of certain oxy-
gen reactive elements such as rare earths to alloys used in high
temperature oxidative atmospheres has been known to reduce oxi-
dation rates and increase scale adhesion [33]. Papaiacovou et al.
[34] have reported that the addition of oxygen-active elements
such as Ce and Y or their oxide dispersions may further improve
the oxidation resistance of base alloys under isothermal or cyclic
conditions through the following effects: (i) selective oxidation of
Cr and the formation of a continuous, ﬁne-grained Cr
2O3 scale; (ii)
enhancement of alloy oxide adhesion; (iii) reduction of the growth
rate of the oxide scale through a change in transport mechanism.
Whittle and Stringer [35] have observed that the adhesion between
scale and alloy is markedly improved by the addition of rare earth
elements, other reactive metals, or dispersions of stable oxides, and
this increases the alloy’s resistance to thermal cycling exposure. The
oxides of aluminum and chromium have formed along the splat
boundaries in all the cases and might have clogged the diffusion
of the oxidising species into the substrate superalloy. Transport
of some alloying elements has been observed from the substrate
into the coating which is also supplemented by the X-ray map-
ping analysis. Presence of oxides of Fe, and Mn on the top surface
of the scale and along the intersplat boundaries indicates diffu-
sion of these elements from the substrate into the coating. The
compositional gradient may be the driving force for the transport
of alloying elements from the substrate into coatings. The prob-
able oxidation mechanism for the NiCrAlY–0.4 wt.% CeO
2 coated
Superni 750 exposed to air at 900 ◦C for 100 cycles is shown in
Fig. 7 . During the initial period of exposure, oxygen penetrates
into the coating through the open pores and splat boundaries. As
the oxidation process continues, the top surface of the oxidised
scale consists of oxides of nickel, chromium, aluminum and spinel
of nickel and chromium. Oxides of aluminum and chromium are
formed along the splat boundaries, during the course of the oxida-
tion study, which prevents the permeation of the oxidising species
into the coating and substrate superalloy. Streaks of cerium oxide
are found along the splat boundaries, which assist in enhancing
the oxidation resistance of coatings. A band of aluminum oxide
is formed along the coating–substrate interface, acting as a bar-
rier for the oxidizing species to enter the substrate superalloy.
Alloying elements such as iron, manganese have diffused from the
substrate into the coating during the high temperature oxidation
study.

5. Conclusions
The high temperature oxidation behavior of HVOF sprayed
NiCrAlY–0.4 wt% CeO 2 coating on Ni- and Fe-based superalloys
have been studied and the following conclusions were made.
1. The HVOF spraying process has been successfully used to deposit
the NiCrAlY–0.4 wt.% CeO 2 using LPG as a fuel gas on Ni- and
Fe-based superalloys. The scale formed on the surface of the
coating was intact without any sign of cracks. There was only
superﬁcial microspalling of the scale during the course of the
study.
2. The weight gain of the coated superalloys was less compared
to that of the bare superalloys. NiCrAlY–0.4 wt.% CeO
2 coatings
lead to a reduction in weight of about 41% in case if Superni 76,
whereas 43% and 39% for Superni 750 and Superfer 800 respec-
tively.
3. The NiCrAlY–0.4 wt.% CeO
2 coated superalloys followed the
parabolic rate law after exposing at 900 ◦C for 100 cycles. In
case of bare superalloys, slight deviations from parabolic rate
law were observed.
4. The scales formed on the surface of the coating after 100 cycles
of exposure consist of mainly oxides of chromium, aluminum,
nickel and spinel of nickel and chromium. Oxides of iron, man-
ganese and titanium are also present in the surface scale in
smaller amounts. It must have formed by the diffusion of these
elements from the substrate to the top scale through the inter-
splat regions.
5. The formation of aluminum oxide, chromium oxide, nickel oxide
and spinel of nickel and chromium has provided better protec-
tion to the substrate superalloys. The formations of these oxides
are conﬁrmed by XRD, SEM/EDS and X-ray mapping analysis. The
presence of cerium oxide in the oxide scale might have helped
the scale to be intact with the coatings as revealed by EDS and
X-ray mapping analysis.
