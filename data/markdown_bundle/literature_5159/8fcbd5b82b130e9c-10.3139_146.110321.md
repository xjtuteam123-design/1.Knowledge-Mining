Creep strength of a binary Al 62Ti38 alloy
Dedicated to Professor Dr. Monika Feller-Kniepmeier on the occasion of her 80 th birthday
Al-rich Ti – Al alloys, as compared to Ti-rich c-TiAl-based
alloys, offer an additional reduction in density of 20 %, bet-
ter oxidation resistance and sufficient strength at high tem-
peratures. High temperature creep of a binary Al 62Ti28 al-
loy was studied in compression in the temperature range
between 1 173 K and 1 323 K in air. It is shown that the al-
loy exhibits quite reasonable creep resistance at 1 173 K,
especially in view of its low density of around 3.8 g cm
–3 .
Stress exponents calculated as the slope n = D log (strain
rate)/D log (stress) = 4 were found to be relatively constant
for the temperature and stress regime investigated. This in-
dicates that dislocation climb may be the rate controlling
creep mechanism. The values of the activation energies for
creep for the as-cast and the annealed Al 62Ti38 material co-
incides well with those found in the literature for interdiffu-
sion of Al in c-TiAl.
Keywords: TiAl alloys; Creep
1. Introduction
In previous decades Ti-rich TiAl-based intermetallic alloys
have been developed successfully as materials for high tem-
perature structural applications because of their low density
and high specific strength. Favourable creep properties
have been achieved for alloys with lamellar microstructures
of c-TiAl and the Ti-rich a2-phase [1, 2]. TiAl alloy sys-
tems with increased aluminium content are expected to re-
sult in a significant additional weight reduction up to 20 %
and a higher oxidation resistance, due to the formation of
an adherent Al
2O3 scale [3]. Furthermore, Al-rich TiAl al-
loys may offer adequate strength at high temperatures,
which would enable them to be used at significantly higher
temperatures than Ti-rich c-TiAl-based alloys. During in-
vestigations of the phase equilibria in the Al-rich part of
the Ti-Al system, Fig. 1, Palm et al. [4 – 6] found that lamel-
lar c-TiAl + r-Al
2Ti microstructures can be generated by
means of heat treatment. Likewise, the mechanical proper-
ties of these alloys would benefit from the formation of la-
mellar microstructure. Based on these findings an elaborate
investigation has been started which aims at the production
and detailed characterisation of two-phase microstructures
of c-TiAl + r-Al 2Ti and a subsequent evaluation of the re-
sulting mechanical properties.
2. Experimental procedure
Centrifugal casting with Induction Skull Melting technol-
ogy has shown high potential for manufacturing c-TiAl
components [7]. Hence, test bars of Al 62Ti38 were produced
with this technology by ACCESS in Aachen. In general, the
alloy showed good melting and casting behaviour. Never-
theless, some pores were found by non-destructive X-ray
inspection in the centre line of the test bars in the as-cast
condition, probably caused by uncontrolled solidification
initiated by a high thermal gradient. The microstructure of
the alloy in the as-cast state and after annealing was studied
by light-optical, scanning (SEM) and transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses.
Chemical analysis by inductively coupled plasma-optical
emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) was used to compare
the as-cast composition with our target composition. The

3. Results
3.1. Microstructure
Optical microscopy showed a single-phase microstructure
with several small precipitates of oxides located on the
grain boundaries (Fig. 2). XRD analysis confirmed a single
phase c-TiAl microstructure which would contradict the re-
cently assessed phase diagram, Fig. 1. In-depth characteri-
sation of the microstructure by combined TEM imaging
techniques reveals a complex microstructure of the
Al62Ti38 alloy in the as-cast condition. There are two differ-
ent orientations of slabs of the metastable h-Al 2Ti phase in
the c-TiAl matrix: [001] ( c-TiAl) || [001] (h-Al 2Ti) and
[100] ( c-TiAl) || [100] (h-Al 2Ti) or [100] ( c-TiAl) || [010]
(h-Al2Ti), respectively1 [8]. The slabs of h-TiAl 2 are bound
by {301} faces while the c-TiAl slabs in between are bound
by faces of type {101} (Fig. 3), which is the equivalent ori-
entation regarding the crystallographic interrelationship be-
tween these two phases [9]. The interfaces between both
h-Al2Ti versions are {310} twin surfaces [8]. Correspond-
ing TEM diffraction patterns and dark field images are
shown in Fig. 3a to c. Different regions in the sample con-
tain mainly precipitates of one of the two orientation do-
main variants of h-Al 2Ti (Fig. 3 a, c, top and bottom area).
The investigation also revealed domains of the metastable
Al5Ti3 phase inside the c-TiAl matrix in addition to the
h-Al2Ti phase. Corresponding dark field images and dif-
fraction patterns are shown in Fig. 3b and c, respectively.
After heat treatment at 1223 K for 50 h and water
quenching [4] the microstructure of Al 62Ti38 was trans-
formed to a relatively fine lamellar microstructure of the
two phases (Fig. 4), which were expected to form in accor-
dance with the phase diagram, Fig. 1. The microstructure
is composed of a c-TiAl matrix containing fine lamellae of
r-Al2Ti on (001) faces exhibiting the orientation relation-
ship [001] ( c-TiAl) || [001] (r-Al 2Ti) and <100] ( c-TiAl) ||
<100] (r-Al2Ti), as is known from investigations on similar
materials [4].

3.2. Creep behaviour
Compression tests were carried out at three different strain
rates, namely 10 –3 s –1 ,1 0 –4 s –1 and 10 –5 s –1 until a con-
stant stress level was reached for each strain rate (which is
considered here as steady-state). At lower strain rates com-
pressive creep tests at constant true stress were carried out
instead of subjecting one specimen to three different strain
rates. The minimum creep rate obtained from the constant
true stress tests and the steady state stress obtained from
the constant strain rate tests were plotted in a double-loga-
rithmic representation. In Fig. 5 the stress dependence of
the creep rate of Al
60Ti40 [14] and Al 62Ti38 in the as-cast
and the annealed condition is shown for 1 273 K and
1 323 K. As expected, the creep rates decrease with de-
creasing temperature as well as with a reduction in stress.
Compared with the creep results for the nearly lamellar
Al60Ti40 alloy [14] it is obvious that both the as-cast and
the annealed Al 62Ti38 alloy exhibit better creep resistance
up to 1 323 K. There is no significant difference in the creep
behaviour of the as-cast and the annealed condition for both
alloys. It should be mentioned that the high Al content of
this alloys also provides very good oxidation resistance
which made testing in air possible.
4. Discussion
It should be noted first that during the creep experiment a
phase transformation (h-Al
2Ti to r-Al 2Ti) occurs together
with a massive reorganisation of the microstructure in sam-
ples not pre-annealed. However, even though the microstruc-
tures of all tested conditions are substantially different, the
creep strength does not reflect this microstructural variation:
with a value of approximately n = 4 the stress exponents
were found to be relatively constant in the temperature and
stress regime investigated. This value is within the range of
n = 3 – 6 which is predicted by various creep models avail-
able in the literature [15 – 18] and indicates that dislocation
climb may be the rate controlling creep mechanism.

multiple linear regression, additional compressive creep
data were obtained at 1173 and 1223 K (not shown in
Fig. 5) and plotted together with those obtained at 1 273
and 1 323 K as the logarithm of the minimum creep rate
normalised with respect to the temperature vs. the stress

The values obtained in our analysis for both the stress ex-
ponent as well as the activation energy of creep are in good
agreement with those of Al-rich single phase c-TiAl [19] as
well as of fully lamellar Ti-rich TiAl alloys [20] where in-
terface related creep phenomena as well as the degradation
of the microstructure due to recrystallisation and phase
transformation determine the creep strength and the activa-
tion energy. In contrast, the microstructure of the present al-
loys appears to be stable during high temperature exposure
but in a temperature range up to 200 K higher than that of
the above mentioned alloys [19, 20].
The Al
62Ti38 alloy shows the same deformed microstruc-
ture for the as-cast and the annealed condition with ex-
tended low angle grain boundary formations and disloca-
tions in globular r-Al 2Ti and c-TiAl. (The latter phase
contains fine precipitates of Al 5Ti3 formed on cooling in
the testing machine after finishing the compression test.)
Furthermore, the investigation revealed areas of lamellar
r-Al2Ti + c-TiAl microstructure. Here the dislocations
were found nearly exclusively in the c-TiAl phase. Corre-
sponding TEM images are shown in Fig. 6a and b, respec-
tively. The results of the TEM investigation are a plausible
explanation for the nearly similar creep behaviour for the
as-cast and annealed condition of the alloy.

5. Conclusions
High temperature creep of a binary Al 62Ti38 alloy in the as-
cast condition and after annealing (which produced fully la-
mellar c-TiAl + r-Al 2Ti microstructures) was investigated,
and the following results were obtained:
(a) The binary alloy exhibits reasonable creep resistance up
to 1323 K, especially with respect to its low density of
around 3.8 g cm –3 . The rather high Al content of the al-
loy provided very good oxidation resistance which en-
abled testing in air.
(b) Stress exponents n = 4 indicate that dislocation climb
may be the rate controlling creep mechanism in both mi-
crostructures. However, the activation energy for creep
of the as-cast materials and the annealed Al 62Ti38 mate-
rial coincides well with the values for interdiffusion of
Al in c-TiAl found in the literature.
