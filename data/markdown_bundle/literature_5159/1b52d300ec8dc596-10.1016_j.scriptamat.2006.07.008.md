New Ni–Co-base disk superalloys with higher strength and
creep resistance
Y. Gu, * H. Harada, C. Cui, D. Ping, A. Sato and J. Fujioka
High Temperature Materials Center, National Institute for Materials Science, 1-2-1 Sengen, Tsukuba, Ibaraki 305-0047, Japan
Received 16 May 2006; revised 4 July 2006; accepted 6 July 2006
Available online 2 August 2006
A new kind of Ni–Co-base superalloys with a c/c0 two-phase structure has been proposed recently for turbine disk applications.
Some of these Ni–Co-base superalloys are assessed by tensile tests. The results show that the Ni–Co-base alloys can be processed by
cheap cast and wrought route and show superior tensile strength at temperatures up to 750 /C176 C and higher creep resistances up to
725 /C176 C than those of the UDIMET 720 LI alloy.

Keywords: Rolling; Ni–Co-base superalloy; Tensile strength; Creep
Higher engine performance can be achieved by high-
er turbine inlet gas temperatures and compression ra-
tios, which mainly depend on the development of the
new materials with higher temperature capabilities for
the key components such as turbine disks and blades
[1,2]. Turbine disks are machined from forgings of Ni-
base superalloy because of their excellent properties at
elevated temperatures. A typical Ni-base disk alloy con-
tains 0–20 weight per cent (wt.%) Co and up to 5 wt.%
Ti; the processing route can involve either the casting
and wrought (C&W) or powder metallurgy (P/M) hot
forming methods [3–6]. The C&W method is one of
the earliest production techniques and still commonly
used in the overall production of turbine components
because of its low cost and processing beneﬁts. How-
ever, the C&W method can not be applied to some
modern disk alloys that contain high volume fractions
of gamma prime ( c
0) phase and refractory elements.
Therefore, the latest Ni-base disk alloys, like alloy
RR1000 developed by the Rolls-Royce Company [7]
and alloy ME3 developed by a joint contract eﬀort be-
tween GE Aircraft Engines, Pratt&Whitney and NASA
Glenn Research Center, all use powder metallurgy (P/
M) processing routes; good tensile strengths and creep
properties at temperatures up to 720 /C176 C have been ob-
tained [8]. However, the requirement of clean powder
and subsequent thermo-mechanical processing steps
limit the application of the P/M route.
Recently, we proposed a new concept for designing
superalloy for aircraft and land-based turbine disk appli-
cations, as indicated in Figure 1 . Two kinds of c/c
0
two-phase superalloys, Ni-base superalloy and Co–Ti
superalloys, are combined together to develop a new
disk alloy system – the TMW alloy, which can be pro-
cessed by the C&W route and can achieve high strengths
especially in the temperature region 600–750 /C176 C.
In a previous study, new alloys were based on an
UDIMET 720LI, also known as U720LI (UDIMET is
a Registered Trade Mark of alloys developed and
patented by the Special Metals Corporation), and a
Co-18at.% Ti alloys. Those new Ni–Co-base superalloys
are designated TMW-1 to -7 ( Fig. 2 ). In these TMW
alloys, the contents of Co and Ti are higher than in nor-
mal commercial disk alloys, i.e., Co content varies from
22 wt.% to 31 wt.% and Ti content varies from 5.1 wt.%
to 7.4 wt.% [9]. Increasing amounts of Co may improve
phase stability and lower the gamma prime solvus tem-
perature, which allows a large processing temperature
range and reduce the stresses induced by controlled
cooling and quenching [10–12]. Ti is a strong gamma
prime former and readily substitutes for Al in the gam-
ma prime phase for anti-phase boundary strengthening
[13,14]. The preliminary results based on compression
tests of the TMW alloys indicate that this design concept
is promising and can lead to further alloy design [9].

In the present study, the tensile strength and creep
resistance are examined for some TMW alloys that only
contain the c/c0 two-phase structure. The nominal chem-
ical compositions of tested TMW alloys are listed in
Table 1 . U720LI alloy is referred to as TMW-1 alloy
for comparison. The volume fractions of c0 are calcu-
lated to be 45% in the TMW-1 and the TMW-4 alloys,
and 48.7% in the TMW-2 alloy by Thermo-Calc
software.
For three TMW alloys in Table 1 , 50-kg ingots were
cast and then hot rolled at the Special Metals Corpora-
tion. The hot rolling was conducted smoothly at about
1100 /C176 C and only small cracks were found at the sides
of some rolled plates. The ﬁnal thickness of hot rolled
plates with a rolling reduction of 75% was 12.7 mm.
The rolled plates were heat treated at 1100 /C176 C/4 h fol-
lowed by air cooling (AC), and then aging at 650 /C176 C/
24 h/AC + 760 /C176 C/16 h/AC. All tested specimens with
a2 2m m · 4 mm diameter cylindrical gauge section were
cut from the heat-treated plates. For microstructure
characterization, samples cut from the heat treated
plates were polished and etched in a solution of Marble
reagent (10 g CuSO 4 +5 0m lH C l+5 0m lH 2O).
The microstructures of the alloys investigated by
scanning electron microscopy are shown in Figure 3 .
The mean grain sizes determined by the linear-intercept
method for all heat treated alloys were about 10 lm. All
tested TMW alloys were characterized to exhibit c/c
0
two-phase structure consisting of large primary c0 parti-
cles, with sizes varying between 0.5 and 1.7 lm( Fig.
3a–c), interspersed with ﬁne c0 particles, typically less
than 0.1 lm, formed upon aging or cooling from the
aging treatment ( Fig. 3 d). The morphology and size of
these c0 precipitates are almost the same for all tested
alloys ( Fig. 3 ). The composition analysis of the phases
in the tested alloys are measured from selected volume
analysis by three-dimensional atom probe; results reveal
that the c
0 phase existed as (Ni,Co) 3(Ti,Al), and added
cobalt preferentially segregate to the c and titanium par-
tition to the c0 phase. Increasing titanium in the TMW-2
and TMW-4 compared to TMW-1 results in an increase
of the Ti content in the major c0 phases.

Another critical property that must be considered
is creep resistance of new disk superalloys. Figure 4
shows tensile creep curves of the tested alloys at
650 /C176 C and a constant load of 628 MPa. Each of these
creep curves included a primary (transient) creep stage
and a near steady-state (viscous) creep stage. For the
selected testing time, i.e., less than 1100 h, none of the
tested alloys exhibited the signature of tertiary creep.
Comparisons of these curves indicated that the TMW-
2 alloy had the best resistance against primary creep as
illustrated by the lowest creep strain for the ﬁrst 300 h.
Time to 0.2% creep strain was about 810 h for the
TMW-1 alloy (U720LI) and was over 1000 h for the
other tested TMW alloys. It is noted that 1000-h creep
strain was only about 0.02% for the TMW-2 alloy.
Creep curves obtained at 725 /C176 C and 630 MPa are
shown in Figure 4 b. Time to 0.2% creep strain was
about 10 h for TMW-1 alloy (U720LI), 62 h for
TMW-2 alloy and 127 h for TMW-4 alloy. So there is
a factor of 12 improvement in creep resistance of
TMW-4 alloys compared with TMW-1 alloys (U720
LI) at such high temperature and stress (see Fig. 5 ).
Comparing TMW alloys to U720LI and ME3 [8] by a
Larson–Miller plot as shown in Figure 6 , the TMW-2
and TMW-4 alloys fall in-between the alloys U720LI
and ME3 with a temperature gain of about 32 /C176 Ca t
630 MPa as compared to U720LI superalloy.
As expected, the TMW alloys show superior tensile
strength at temperatures up to 725 /C176 C than that of
U720LI alloy while possessing good fabrication ability.
Because tested TMW alloys have a c/c
0 two-phase struc-
ture and similar grain size, the superior strength of
TMW-2 and TMW-4 alloys compared with that of
TMW-1 alloy can be attributed to solid solution
strengthening and precipitate hardening. It has been re-
ported that cobalt content has little direct inﬂuence on
the tensile properties of a Ni-base disk alloy Udimet
700 [12]. Meanwhile, the contents of main solid solution
hardening elements, like Mo and W, are lower in the
TMW-2 and TMW-4 alloys than in TMW-1 alloy.
Therefore, the strength improvement may be due solely
to the precipitate hardening. Comparisons of the tensile
properties of the TMW alloys with the same volume
fraction of c
0 precipitate (TMW-1 and TMW-4) suggest
that the precipitates, (Ni,Co) 3(Al,Ti), in Ni–Co-base
alloys may supply more strength hardening eﬀect with
an increase of the Ti content within the precipitates
[13]. However, with the increase in the volume fraction
of c

points. Perhaps the software we used to calculate the
volume fraction can not give the right value because
