ABSTRACT 
CoCrFeNiCuSn high-entropy alloy and Cu foam composite interlayer was used as a filler for the brazing of SiC 
ceramics and Zr. The microstructure and mechanical properties of the brazed joint at room temperature and high 
temperatures as well as the brazing mechanism were systematically investigated. The microstructure is adjusted 
by controlling the brazing temperature. The main phases in the joint were identified at different brazing tem -
peratures to be a high-entropy alloy phase, α -Zr (s, s), Zr
2
Cu, (Zr, Sn) and Zr(Cr, Fe)
2 
Laves phase. The joint 
brazed at 1040 
◦
C for 20 min exhibited a maximum shear strength of 221 MPa at room temperature and an 
average shear strength of 207 MPa at 600 
◦
C. The room temperature and high temperature-strength obtained 
here are much higher than those obtained for joints brazed using a conventional filler. Owing to the high-entropy 
effect, the joint matrix is mainly composed of solid solution phase, which improves the strength and thermal 
stability of the joint. The existence of the hard Zr(Fe, Cr)
2 
Laves phase and the soft α -Zr (s, s) phase in the joint 
significantly improves its strength and plasticity. 
1. Introduction 
Zirconium (Zr) and Zr alloys are widely used as cladding materials 
for the fuel rods of water-cooled nuclear reactors due to their unique 
properties such as excellent corrosion resistance, good mechanical 
properties and low thermal neutron capture cross-section [ 1 – 3 ]. How -
ever, in a loss-of-coolant accident, Zr alloy claddings react with 
high-temperature steam through an oxidative reaction, releasing 
hydrogen gas [ 4 , 5 ], which is an enormous safety hazard. Thus, new 
cladding materials must be developed to avoid major accidents. SiC 
ceramics are a promising potential substitute for Zr in atomic-energy 
applications due to their chemical stability, corrosion and 
abrasion-resistance, radiation resistance and excellent high-temperature 
mechanical properties [ 6 , 7 ]. They also have a lower thermal neutron 
absorption cross-section than Zr alloys and a very low reactivity with 
high-temperature steam [ 8 ]. Thus, SiC could be the cladding material for 
a new generation of nuclear reactors. However, SiC is expensive and has 
poor processability [ 9 ]. Therefore, Zr and SiC can be combined to obtain 
the advantages of both materials. 
So far, the welding methods of ceramics and metals mainly include: 
brazing [ 10 , 11 ], diffusion welding [ 12 ], electron beam welding [ 13 ] 
and laser welding [ 14 ]. Among these, brazing is a simple, convenient 
and economic technique that can effectively solve the problem of 
low-temperature joining of ceramics. The type of the brazing filler is 
important as it controls the mechanical properties of the brazing joint 
because it affects the substrate wetting, interface reaction and residual 
stresses [ 15 , 16 ]. Conventionally, AgCuTi, Ni-based, and Ti-based active 
fillers are the most widely used fillers. Shi et al. investigated the 
microstructure and mechanical properties of ZrC – SiC ceramics and TC4 
alloys brazed using traditional Ag – Cu – Ti fillers. They found that Ti re -
acts with ZrC to form TiC on the ceramic side and reacts with Cu to form 
a series of Cu – Ti intermetallic compounds (IMC) in the brazing seam due 
to the strong affinity between Cu and Ti. Cu – Ti IMC and TiC increased 
the hardness and brittleness of the brazing seam, increasing the residual 
stress and causing the properties of the brazed joint to deteriorate [ 17 ]. 
Qi et al. brazed SiC ceramics using a Ni – Ti filler, and the results indi -
cated that the Ti
2
Ni intermetallic compound was the major component 
in the central area of the brazed joint. Due to the mismatch in the co -
efficient of thermal expansion (CTE) of Ti
2
Ni and the SiC ceramics, a 
large stress concentration is generated at the joint, hindering the 
improvement of the brazed-joint strength [ 18 ].

It has been reported that CoFeCrNiCu HEAs have a simple solid-

has a lower melting point, which can reduce the solidus and liquidus

metal and improve the fluidity of the filler metal phase [ 22 , 23 ]. To 
reduce their melting point and improve the wettability on the ceramic 
surface, Sn is added to obtain CoCrFeNiCuSn HEA. In this work, a 
CoCrFeNiCuSn HEA was used as a brazing filler in the brazing process of 
a SiC ceramic and Zr. Due to the mismatch of CTE, there is usually a large 
residual stress in the joint. To reduce the residual stresses in the joint, Cu 
foam is added to the CoCrFeNiCuSn HEA to form a brazing composite 
filler. As a special structural material, Cu foam has a unique 3D porous 
structure and good plastic deformation ability. It is expected to be used 
to reduce the thermal residual stress of the joint [ 24 , 25 ]. Its special 
structure makes the distribution of Cu foam in the microstructure of the 
joint relatively uniform. In addition, even if the Cu foam dissolves in the 
joint, it can also play a role in stress relief [ 26 ]. The effects of the brazing 
temperature on the interfacial microstructure, interfacial reaction 
mechanism and mechanical properties of the brazed joints were inves -
tigated. In addition, the high-temperature shear strength of the brazed 
joints was measured.

3. Results and discussion 
Fig. 2 a shows the microstructure of the CoCrFeNiCuSn alloy. Four 
phases are identified in the HEA alloy ( Fig. 2 b): a small amount of black 
phase, ξ ; grey phase, ω ; dendritic light black phase, φ and white phase, π . 
Table 1 shows the EDS results, which indicate that phase φ is a high- 
entropy face centred cubic (FCC) phase composed of Co, Fe, Cr and 
Ni. Phases ω , π and ξ are considered as Cu (s, s), Sn (s, s) and Cr (s, s), 
respectively. Liquid-phase separation typically depends on the positive 
enthalpies of mixing between Cu, Sn and other elements [ 27 ]. Table 2 
shows the mixing enthalpies between Co, Fe, Cr, Cu and Sn elements 
[ 28 ], based on which it can be concluded that the binding forces of Cu 
and Sn with Co, Cr and Fe are weak. Thus, Cu and Sn cannot thermo -
dynamically mix with Co, Cr and Fe, and hence, the Cu and Sn elements 
are separated from the (Co, Cr, Fe, Ni) matrix. Therefore, it is easier to 
form an FCC solid solution from Co, Cr, Fe and Ni, which is in line with 
the results obtained in previous studies [ 29 ]. However, the mixing en -
thalpies between Cu and Sn are positive; thus, it is easier to form Cu (s, s) 
and Sn (s, s). This result is in agreement with previous reports [ 27 ]. In 
addition, Cr and Fe elements are more likely to segregate in HEA alloys 
[ 30 ]. Therefore, some of the elemental Cr segregated in the region near 
phase ξ to form Cr (s, s). Fig. 2 c shows the XRD results of HEA, which 
further confirms that it mainly consists of four mixed phases: CoFeCrNi, 
Sn (s, s), Cu (s, s) and Cr (s, s). Fig. 2 d demonstrates the DSC curve of the

2

Fe, Cr, Ni, Cu and Zr in the filler solidifies into a solid solution (HEAP)

Sn and Zr with the lowest melting points use the Zr
2

nucleation particle, grow in a hexagonal shape and form a (Zr, Sn) solid 
solution. The joint structure eventually forms five phases.
