TiAl/IN718 dissimilar brazing with TiZrNiCuCo high-entropy
filler metal: phase characterization and fractography
Dariush Kokabi1 & Ali Kaflou1
Received: 28 May 2020 / Accepted: 19 January 2021
# International Institute of Welding 2021
Abstract
This paper aims at understanding the microstructural evolution during the dissimilar brazing ofγ-TiAl intermetallic to a Ni-based
superalloy by using a novel high-entropy quinary amorphous TiZrNiCuCo filler alloy. Three distinct zones were characterized in
the joint region: reaction layer adjacent to theγ-TiAl, diffusion zone in the IN718 side, and centerline solidified zone. Ti-Ni (Cu)-
Al intermetallic compounds were formed in the reaction layer. αʹ (Cr, Fe) and γ (Fe, Ni) have precipitated in the γ-Ni matrix of
the IN718 substrate discontinuously. The middle TiNi phase solidified isothermally during bonding. The maximum shear
strength of 396 MPa achieved at 1020 °C for 40-min bonding, and the high temperature strength was 67% of the room
temperature strength.
Keywords TiAl intermetallic . High-entropy filler alloy . Ni-based superalloy . Brazing . Microstructure evolution . Phase
transformation
1 Introduction
The bonding of dissimilar materials can incorporate the mate-
rials with different functions into a hybrid structure to cover
more features for wider applications. Considering the attrac-
tive characteristics of excellent high temperature mechanical
properties, high fatigue resistance, high specific strength, and
low density, the TiAl-based intermetallics have been regarded
the most promising materials to alternate the Ni-based super-
alloys in the temperature range of 650 –750 °C, with the ben-
efit of a weight saving of 50% [ 1–5]. The joining of TiAl to
metallic materials has posed a significant challenge to the
application of this alloy in the industry. Thus, picking up a
suitable joining technique is indispensable for the practical
implementation of the TiAl in high temperature structural ap-
plications [6].
The joining of TiAl-based intermetallics to Ni-based super-
alloys has always deals a great challenge because of the major
differences between the two alloys and the poor weldability of
themselves. The TiAl alloys are difficult to weld with conven-
tional methods because of fusion crack sensitivity [7]. Brazing
[8, 9] is one of the proper techniques which has been used for
joining of TiAl/Ni-based superalloys joints with more reliable
features.
The selection of filler metal plays a key role in the brazing
of dissimilar TiAl/Ni-based superalloys to obtain a high effi-
cient joint. Dong et al. utilized two different amorphous alloys
as the filler metal, a Zr-Al-Ni-Co [ 8] and a Fe-Co-Ni-Si-B
[10]. The author has reported a dissimilar transient liquid
phase joint by using a Ni-based Ni-Cr-Fe-Si-B [ 11], Ni-
based Ni-Si-B, and pure copper [ 12] as the filler metals in
previous works. Ren et al. investigated on the dissimilar
Ti3Al/Ni-based superalloy brazed joint via using two distinct
AuNi [9]a n dA g C u P d[13] filler metals. Amorphous Ti-Zr-
Cu-Ni-Co alloy also was used in TiAl [14] and TiAl/high Nb-
containing TiAl [15]j o i n t s .

great attentions in the last few years of researches. The term
“high entropy” was first proposed by Yeh et al. [16] and refers
to alloys composed of five or more principal elements in equi-
molar ratios or concentration of each element being between
35 and 5 at.%. The great feature of these alloys is that they

2 Materials and experiments
2.1 Materials
The chemical compositions of the selected TiAl-based al-
loy and Ni-based superalloy are written in Table 1.
Titanium and aluminum with high purity (99.97 and 99.7
wt%, respectively) were used for producing TiAl interme-
tallic compound. Alloying and casting in 2 × 10 × 30 mm
mold were carried out in a vacuum arc remelting (VAR)
furnace, and the ingot was remelted for 4 times. The as-
cast ingots were placed in the quartz capsule filled with
the high purity (99.999%) Argon and inserted in the tube
furnace and has been homogenized at 1100 °C for 24 h. The
Ni-based IN718 superalloy was used in the solution treated
standard heat treatment condition.
The amorphous high-entropy filler alloy was prepared by
two steps: Firstly, an ingot of Ti
34 Zr 26 Ni 18 Cu 15 Co 7
was fabricated by VAR furnace with the mixture of pure
metals (purities above 99.9 %) under the Argon-protective
atmosphere, four times remelting repetition for reaching
higher homogeneous composition were exerted. Secondly,
the ribbon-shaped filler metal was fabricated using a Melt
Spin Furnace with a high speed rotating copper wheel ( V =
1800 round per minute). The thickness of the obtained ribbons
was 78 ± 4 μm. The thermal behavior of the ribbon was
examined by utilizing the differential scanning calorimeter
(DSC). The heating rate was 15 °C/min from room tempera-
ture. Considering the DSC curve, the glass transition temper-
ature (T
g), crystallization temperature (T x), solidus tempera-
ture (T s ), and liquidus temperature (T l )o ft h e
Ti34Zr26Cu15Ni18Co7 are 508 °C, 549 °C, 891 °C, and 922
°C, respectively. Figure 1 shows the DSC curve of the amor-
phous ribbon.
2.2 Experiments
The samples were sandwiched with the amorphous ribbon as
the filler metal. The size of the samples was 2 × 10 × 10 mm,
and before bonding, the two substrates and the filler metal
were ultrasonically cleaned in acetone. The bonding experi-
ments were conducted at the temperature of 1020 °C for 10
and 40 min and 950 °C for 40-min holding time. During the
bonding experiment, the vacuum was kept at 6 × 10
−3 Pa, and
the heating rate was 10 °C/min.
After the brazing, the microstructure of phases formed
in the bond region and semi-quantitative chemical analy-
sis of phases formed in the joint region were studied by
using the scanning electron microscope (SEM) MiraII
TESCAN, equipped to an EDS analyzer system. The mi-
crohardness Vickers test was used to evaluate the hard-
ness of different zones. The room and high temperature
shear tests were implemented using a testing machine
(SANTAM 250).

3 Results
3.1 Microstructural evolution during brazing process
Figure 2 shows the typical BSE image of the
TiAl/TiZrNiCuCo/IN718. The whole joint area could be di-
vided into three distinct zones: a reaction layer (RL) near to
the γ-TiAl substrate, a diffusion zone (DZ) adjacent to the
IN718 substrate, and a solidified zone (SZ) at the middle of
the joint. As can be seen in Fig.2, the width of the bond region
is 115 μm, which in comparison with the initial thickness of
the filler metal (78 μm) shows a strong interaction (including
dissolution, diffusion, and r eaction) between substrates
alloying elements and molten filler metal occurred during
brazing that leads to widening of the joint region from the
initial filler alloy thickness.
It is of note that both the element pairs of Ti and Zr and Cu
and Ni are not only chemically compatible (similar crystal
structure and atomic radii) but also completely soluble in each
other [18]. It means that Zr and Cu in a certain sense can be
regarded as Ti and Ni, respectively.
Figure 3 shows the detailed micrographs of phases formed
in the joint region, and the EDS analysis results of all the spots
in Fig. 3 have been given in Table 2. The featured microstruc-
tures in each zone have been formed due to atomic diffusion
during the brazing (especially during initial melting of the
filler metal), solidification, and solid-state precipitation. At
the onset of brazing, intense dissolution of the TiAl and Ni-
based IN718 substrates was occurred into the molten Ti-Zr-
Ni-Cu-Co filler, and the diffusion of Al, Nb, Fe, Cr, and Ni
(alloying elements of substrates) from the base materials into
the molten pool was driven by the concentration gradient.
Consequently, the liquid zone grew wider and led to the in-
creasing the width of the brazed joint than in comparison with
the thickness of the initial filler metal.
3.1.1 Reaction layer adjacent to the γ-TiAl
The dissolution of the TiAl substrate readily started into the
molten filler metal, and due to the high tendency of Ti, Ni
(Cu), and Al to form compounds, the rejection of Zr and Co
occurred. Considering the Al-Ni(Cu)-Ti ternary phase dia-
grams [ 19, 20], the reaction between molten filler enriched
with Ni and Cu, and the γ-TiAl base alloy leads to the forma-
tion of two phase region consisted of α-Ti and τ
2 phases (L+
γ→α-Ti+τ2-TiCu(Ni)Al) at the bonding temperature. The
phases in this region were saturated with Ti, Al, and Cu
(Ni). Consequently, the high levels of Al could be existed in
the α-Ti at the bonding temperature according to Ti-Al phase
diagram [19].
As a result, during the cooling stage, the α
2 phase formed
discontinuously from a solid-state transformation in the τ2
matrix (Fig. 3(a), spot A and B). The solid-state feature of
the α2 formation was reported by Shiue et al. [ 21] and ob-
served at the RL/TiAl substrate in other studies [ 14, 22]. The
remained molten alloy reacted with a portion of τ2 matrix and
led to the formation of a triple phase layer (spot C in Fig.3(a))
according to the ternary reaction (L+ τ2→τ1-Ti(Cu, Ni)2Al+
τ3-Ti2(Cu, Ni)Al5), and the stability of the triple phase region
at lower temperatures could be validated using the 850 °C
isothermal section of Al-Cu-Ti ternary phase diagram [23].
3.1.2 Diffusion zone adjacent to the IN718
In the Ni-based superalloy substrate side, during the heating
stage, by reaching the solidus temperature of the filler metal,
two distinct in situ Ni-based (γ) and Cr-based (αʹ)p h a s e sh a v e
precipitated in the solid-stateγ-Ni matrix of the substrate (spot
K); this microstructure only has been reported by Dong et al.
[10] in dissimilar TiAl/Ni-based superalloy. According to the
morphology of these precipitates and by considering isother-
mal sections of the Ni-Cr-Fe ternary phase diagram at the
bonding temperature and lower temperatures, one can con-
clude that these phases have been formed during the holding
time at the bonding temperature [ 19, 24]. The size of the
discontinuous lamellas is lesser than 2 μm and has been
shown in Fig. 4. Ti, Cu, and Zr elements diffused into the
substrate from the composition of filler alloy, and no
partitioning tendency for these elements was detected due to
the equal amounts of them that have been distributed in the
two phases. From the EDS analysis of αʹ (Cr, Fe) and γ (Fe,
Ni) phases, it can be inferred that Mo has had more tendency
to dissolve in the αʹ (Cr, Fe) phase, on the other side, Nb has
partitioned into the γ (Fe, Ni) phase during the phases
formation.

3.1.3 Solidification zone
At the bonding temperature, the high tendency of Ti and Al to
form intermetallic compounds with Ni and Cu led to dissolu-
tion of Ti and Al from the base TiAl substrate, and diffusion of
Cu and Ni from the molten filler alloy into the reaction zone.
On the other hand, the liquid adjacent to the reaction zone
enriched with Zr and Ti. According to Ti-Cu and Ti-Ni phase
diagrams [19], the reduction of Cu and Ni and the increase of
Ti alongside with Zr enrichment in the liquid phase led to an
increase in melting temperature of the liquid phase. So, the
remaining liquid started to solidify isothermally (during hold-
ing time at the bonding temperature) and formed a continuous
isothermal solidified layer which has enriched with Ti and Zr
elements (spot D).
Another isothermal solidification has occurred on the
IN718 side. Because of the low solubility of Zr in Ni, the
amount of Zr that has diffused into the IN718 substrate is
much lower than the other elements presented in the molten
filler composition. As a cons equence, the molten filler
enriched with Zr and led to the liquid temperature increment
and isothermal solidification of a Zr-rich solid solution layer
(spot H). Moreover, due to the dissolution of substrates
alloying elements into the molten filler, the excess amounts
of them rejected and have been solidified as the (Ni, Cu)
3(Ti,
Zr) phase at the bonding temperature isothermally (spot I).

4 Discussion
4.1 Diffusion and joint evolution
Figure 7 shows the X-ray composition line scan across the
joint region and the initial concentrations of elements in
the filler metal and substrate compositions. The Co and Zr
diffusion behavior is seen to be similar. In other words,
they distributed toward the two substrates uniformly, but
due to low solubility of Zr in Ni a Zr-rich layer has been
solidified at the SZ/DZ interface. In return, it is observ-
able that the Cu and Ni have had high tendencies to form
different compounds with TiAl substrate elements.
The reason for high concentration of Ti in the joint region
near to the TiAl and Ni in proximity of the IN718 superalloy is
dissolution of Ti and Ni from the TiAl and Ni-based sub-
strates, respectively. During the holding time, Fe and Cr ele-
ments dissolved from the Ni-based superalloy substrate into
the molten filler metal due to the composition gradient. The
element Al shows high diffusivity at the bonding temperature

In order to describe the joining process intuitively, a sim-
plified schematic diagram of the microstructure evolution is
proposed based on the discussed results analysis, as displayed
in Fig. 8. When the temperature reaches to the liquidus tem-
perature of the filler alloy, the Ti-Zr-Ni-Cu-Co starts to melt,
and then the base metals partially dissolve into the molten
filler metal. Due to the concentration gradient, filler alloy el-
ements (Zr, Cu, Co) diffuse towards the two substrates. Ni and
Ti elements mainly diffuse to the γ-TiAl and the IN718 sub-
strates, respectively (Fig. 8a).
During the holding time at the bonding temperature due to
the diffusion and dissolution, some metallurgical reactions
occur, and some new phases and reaction or diffusion layers
are being formed as indicated in Fig. 8b. By prolonging the
dwell time at the bonding temperature, Ti-Al-Ni(Cu) interme-
tallic phases are formed in the TiAl-side reaction layer. Some
lamellar Fe-, Ni-, and Cr-based phases precipitate in the dif-
fusion zone, and TiNi solid solution contains some of alloying
elements dissolved from substrates, which solidify at the joint
centerline. Finally, during on-cooling stage, α
2 precipitations
form in the τ2 matrix, and (Ti, Zr)2(Ni, Cu) phase precipitates
in SZ and DZ (Fig. 8c) discontinuously.
4.2 Hardness distribution
Figure 9 shows the hardness profile of the bonded sample with
the TiZrNiCuCo high-entropy filler metal. Generally, it can be
seen that the hardness of the joint region phases is higher than
that of substrates due to intermetallics and high alloying solid
solutions. The highest amount of the hardness is pertinent to
the isothermally solidified Zr-rich layer, and excluding this
layer, the hardness of remaining phases is approximately in
the same level. The hardness profile of the joint is more uni-
form in comparison with other studies [ 9, 10].
4.3 Shear strength and fractography
Figure 10 shows the shear strength of the TiAl/IN718 brazed
joints as a function of brazing process parameters. The maxi-
mum strength is offered by the joint at 1020 °C for 40-min
holding time due to the high portion of isothermally solidified
zones. The strength of this joint is higher than earlier investi-
gations on dissimilar TiAl/Ni-based superalloys joints [8, 10].
On the other hand, by decreasing of the time or temperature of
the bonding process, because of the presence of the athermally
solidified intermetallic phases that are detrimental for the me-
chanical properties, the shear strength of these joints are lower
than that of the joint at 1020 °C for 40 min.

The weak point of the joints bonded at lower holding times
or temperatures is the formation of a continuous Ti 2Ni-based
intermetallic compound which has been formed adjacent to
the DZ of the IN718 side that is too brittle and reduced the
mechanical response of the joints as it was reported in the
previous studies [ 14, 22, 26, 27]. Moreover, it was demon-
strated that by increasing the bonding time and temperature,
the amount of the (Ti, Zr)
2(Ni, Cu) decreased drastically, and
the rest changed to the discontinuous phase type that is less
detrimental for the mechanical properties.
Fracture analysis was utilized by means of studying the
fracture surfaces SEM. Figure 11 shows the fracture path of
the joints, schematically. Two different fracture paths were
indicated in the brazed joints. The major phase presented at
the fractured surface of the joint bonded at higher holding time
(1020 °C for 40 min) is high containing Zr-Ti solidified layer
adjacent to the RL of the TiAl side (Fig. 12a), with some
regions with chemical compositions near to the reaction layer
phases. Furthermore, by considering the fracture path indicat-
ed in Fig. 12b, it is observable that the fracture has occurred
adjacent to DZ of the IN718 side due to the formation of
continuous (Ti, Zr)
2(Ni, Cu) brittle phase in the brazed joint
fabricated at lower holding times (1020 °C for 10 min).
Figure 12 shows typical fracture surfaces of the joints.
Regarding to the micrographs, it can be concluded that the
fracture behavior of the joints is brittle-dominated due to the
presence of the brittle fracture appearances on the surfaces.
Cleavage facets (CF) and cleavage planes (CP) were observed
in the fracture surfaces as the cleavage fracture features.
To sum up, the fracture behavior of the joints was brittle-
dominant regardless of the brazing process parameters.
Moreover, the difference was in the location of the fracture
occurrence which was shown in the Fig.11. Actually it can be
inferred that by increasing the bonding temperature or holding
time, the weak point of the brazed joints changed from Ni-based
superalloy side of the bond region to the TiAl side of the joint.
Furthermore, the high temperature shear test was conduct-
ed on the joint with the best room temperature strength, and it
was concluded that the high temperature strength of the joint is
67% than that of the room temperature strength of the joint.
5 Conclusions
The TiAl/IN718 brazed joint was fabricated by using a novel
high-entropy TiZrNiCuCo amorphous quinary filler alloy.
The key microstructural features of the joint are as follows:
(i) Ti-Ni (Cu)-Al intermetallic compounds in the reaction lay-
er of TiAl side, (ii) αʹ (Cr, Fe) and γ (Fe, Ni) discontinuous
precipitations in the γ-Ni matrix of the IN718 substrate, and
