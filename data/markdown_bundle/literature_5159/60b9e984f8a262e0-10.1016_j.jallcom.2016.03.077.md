abstract
The demand for enhanced material performance and ef ﬁciency in high-temperature applications has
motivated the development of novel Co-based superalloys. Co-Al-W-based superalloys with the g/g0
structure have suitable properties for use at high temperature and are good candidates for replacing Ni-
based superalloys in some applications. The current generation of Co-Al-W-based alloys has superior
wear, corrosion, and oxidation resistance. The poor precipitation strengthening of these alloys compared
to that of Ni-based superalloys can be overcome by designing the alloying system to promote
g0-Co3(Al,
W) precipitation. In this work, the compositions of the selected Co-Al-W-based alloys were chosen
because they improve the precipitation strengthening of the alloys. Starting from powders, the materials
were fabricated by mechanical alloying (MA) and subsequently consolidated by ﬁeld assisted hot
pressing (FAHP). The prepared Co-Al-W superalloys have a large amount of well-dispersed nano-
precipitates in their microstructures and have nearly full density. The effects of the MA and consolidation
process (FAHP) conditions on the microstructures and mechanical properties of selected samples are
determined by comparing the samples.

1. Introduction
Material failure at high temperatures is one of the most signif-
icant limitations in many engineering systems. Ni-based superal-
loys constitute the primary family of metallic materials used in
high-temperature applications because effective precipitation
strengthening of a ﬁne coherent ordered phase (g0) dispersed in the
FCC Ni matrix (g) can be achieved [1]. This type of microstructure is
referred to as the g/g0 structure. The g0 ordered phase has an L1 2
structure [2] and therefore exhibits an A 3B stoichiometry, as in
Ni3(Al,Ti). Traditionally, Co-based superalloys used in high-
temperature applications are formed by the precipitation of sec-
ondary carbides, or they consist of solid solutions of refractory
metals [3]. At high temperatures, these alloys are inferior to Ni-
based superalloys due to carbide coarsening or dissolution into
the matrix, thereby causing the mechanical properties to deterio-
rate. Although the g/g0 phase microstructure can be obtained for
some Co-based binary alloys (e.g., Co-Ti), the phase stability of the
g0 structure is low, resulting in inferior high-temperature
properties.
In 2006, Sato et al. [4] studied the Co-Al-W ternary phase dia-
gram and discovered an intermetallic phase with the A 3B stoichi-
ometry, Co3(Al,W), which is stable at high temperatures and has an
L12 structure. The existence of this intermetallic phase demon-
strates the possibility of obtaining Co-based g/g0 superalloys that
exhibit satisfactory properties at high temperatures by designing
the microstructure and applying an appropriate heat treatment, as
in the case of the Ni-based superalloys.
Since 2006, much research has focused on developing this new
family of alloys [5e12] but conventional ingot metallurgy, which
requires careful control to avoid defects such as macrosegregation,
microsegregation, porosity, solidi ﬁcation shrinkage and second-
phase inclusions, is used to fabricate them. Additionally, the cost
of machining and thermomechanical processing is high [13].
These drawbacks can be overcome by using a near net shaping
technology, such as powder metallurgy (PM), which enables the
production of nearly full density products with a minimal amount
of material. To understand the potential of this fabrication route,

fabricate Co-Al-W-based alloys for high-temperature applications.
The PM stages, which included MA to produce the powder and
FAHP to consolidate it, were optimized to improve the precipitation
strengthening and room-temperature mechanical properties of the
Co-Al-W alloys.
2. Experimental procedure
A Co-based alloyed powder with a composition of Co-8Al-12.5W
(at.%) was obtained by mechanical alloying in a planetary ball mill
(Planetary Pulverisette 6,FRITSCH, Oberstein, Germany) using the
parameters presented in Table 1. Raw Co, Al and W powders were
high-energy milled for 30 h in an Ar atmosphere. Because this
process affects the composition, grain size and strengthening
mechanisms, it can be considered to be the most important in
terms of the ﬁnal composition of the processed alloys. To evaluate
the effect of mass transfer from the milling media on the phase
evolution during milling, the ﬁnal microstructure and the me-
chanical properties, two materials, a stainless steel (SS, 316L) and a
hard metal (HM, Co-WC type), were employed for the tandem
grinding balls and bowls. To minimize carbon contamination, a PCA
(processing control agent) was not used with the hard metal
grinding tools.
The MA powders were packed in a graphite die to manufacture
discs that were 10 mm in diameter and 5 mm thick. The samples
were pressed using a Gleeble 3800 simulator (Dynamic Systems,
Inc., DSI) under vacuum (10-5 Torr) at 1000, 1 100 and 1250 /C14 C for
10 min. The applied pressure was 50 MPa, and the heating and
cooling rates were 100 /C14 C/min and approximately g 3 /C14 C/min,
respectively. The compact surface was cleaned by grinding with
emery paper to achieve a mirror ﬁnish, and then the density was
measured by the Archimedes method with water as the liquid
medium.
To assess the phase evolution with milling time and identify the
phases after consolidation, the samples were characterized by X-
ray diffraction (XRD). The lattice parameters, crystallite sizes and
internal strain values were calculated from the X-ray diffraction
patterns using the Williamson-Hall method.
The amount of interstitial elements present in the MA powders
and consolidated samples due to contamination from the milling
media was measured by mass spectrometry using LECO CS200 (for
C and S) and LECO TC500 (for O and N) glow discharge spectrom-
eters. A minimum of three measurements were performed to
obtain the standard deviation. The chemical composition was
determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spec-
troscopy (ICP-OES).

The SEM analysis of the MA powders con ﬁrms the phases
identiﬁed by XRD (Fig. 2). The white precipitates are undissolved W
(BCC), and the Co matrix is the g phase (FCC). In the case of the Co-
8-12.5-SS powders, a dark gray area is detected, which was deter-
mined to be an Fe-rich zone by EDS analysis. This element is a
contaminant from the steel milling media.
The amount of interstitial elements present during the devel-
opment of the ﬁnal microstructure also plays an important role in
the overall process ( Table 3). The presence of O, S and N cannot be
avoided because the milling bowls are not completely sealed during
milling. Leaks in the Ar atmosphere during the MA process
contribute to an increase in the oxygen partial pressure, enabling
the incorporation of an additional interstitial element into the
material. Moreover, the high reactivity of the powder during the
milling process should be considered. Because of the effects of
carbon on the material properties, the carbon level should also be
controlled. The carbon source varies depending on the milling
media. For Co-8-12.5-SS, the use of a PCA increases the C content,
whereas for Co-8-12.5-HM, C is transferred from the hard material
of the milling tools in smaller amounts during MA.
3.2. Effect of the FAHP temperature on the microstructure
Fig. 3 shows the XRD patterns of the samples pressed at 1000,
1 100 and 1250
/C14 C. Due to the different milling media and consoli-
dation temperatures, different precipitate phases are formed. If MA
leads to Fe enrichment (Co-8-12.5-SS), a new Fe2W2C carbide and
complex Co 6W6C carbide are formed. The formation of these car-
bides is possible because the carbon in the samples combines after
milling. The Co-8-12.5-HM material consists of different phases
because it has a different ﬁnal MA powder composition.
The effect of the FAHP temperature on precipitation strength-
ening can be analyzed by comparing the structural parameters of
the materials obtained at different FAHP temperatures using
different MA powders. For the Co-8-12.5-SS alloys, the highest
sintering temperature results in a sharper g peak, indicating an
increase in the crystallite size ( Table 4 ). The Co-8-12.5-SS alloy
crystallites grow with increasing temperature, and thus, the pre-
cipitation pining is less effective. The change in the unit cell
parameter with the consolidation temperature demonstrates the
effect of the Fe content on the Co-8-12.5-SS samples.
To measure the amount of Fe in the Co-8-12.5-SS alloys, a
quantitative chemical analysis was performed using ICP-OES. The
results show a high level of contamination from the stainless steel
bowl and balls (26 wt.% Fe, 6.5 wt.% Cr and 3.4 wt.% Ni; the presence
of these alloying elements in the materials obtained using the HM
grinding media is negligible, Table 5), indicating that the initial Co-
8Al-12.5W composition is fully modi ﬁed. Consequently, the FCC Co
phase is more stable; therefore, its mechanical response is different.
For the Co-8-12.5-HM alloys, the Co, Al and W contents change
depending on the area selected for analysis and the precipitates
that are close to it, which is advantageous due to the chemical
compatibility of the precipitates with the processed powders with
the processed powders.
The microstructures of both alloys are consistent with the
phases detected by XRD. The consolidated samples have two well-
deﬁned areas ( Fig. 4); the area with white precipitates consists of a
g matrix with dispersed carbides (Co-8-12.5-SS: Co 6W6C and
Fe2W2C, Co-8-12.5-HM: Co 6W6C). The morphology and distribu-
tion of the free precipitates (those surrounding the former parti-
cles) suggest that a liquid phase forms during the consolidation
process ( Fig. 5 ). Based on the Al eCo binary phase diagram and in
accordance with [15], a peritectic reaction can occur close to the
consolidation temperature, explaining the possible presence of a
liquid phase during sintering.
For Co-8-12.5-SS, the temperature at which the complex car-
bides are formed affects their morphology and distribution. As
shown in Fig. 3 (left), the carbide peaks become sharper and nar-
rower as the consolidation temperature increases. Furthermore, the
Co-8-12.5-HM precipitates appear to be ﬁner than those in alloys
with higher amounts of g stabilizers; therefore, it the ﬁnal micro-
structure depends on the carbon content, alloying system and
processing temperature. The Co-8-12.5-HM microstructure should
result in a harder material.

presence of Fe and carbide formation detected by XRD ( Fig. 5 ).
Three types of precipitates are identi ﬁed: type I: irregular black

Moreover, the FIB analysis reveals that two types of precipitates

3.3. Mechanical properties of the processed alloys
The densi ﬁcation of the samples determines the mechanical
response of the consolidated material. Fig. 8 shows the inﬂuence of
the starting powder composition on the density. The FAHP tem-
perature does not affect the Co-8-12.5-SS material density, but the
porosity of the Co-8-12.5-HM materials decreases with increasing
FAHP temperature.
As expected, the material hardness and microhardness results
(HV30 and HV1, respectively, Fig. 9 ) follow the same trend as the
densiﬁcation results, which demonstrates the importance of the
porosity in terms of the mechanical response.
To further analyze the mechanical properties of the alloys, the
compressive strength was measured at room temperature ( Fig. 10).
The Co-8-12.5-SS specimens, which have a softer matrix and fewer
precipitates, exhibit the highest ductility and toughness with a
relatively high compressive strength (1.25 GPa). However, the
hardness and precipitate density of the Co-8-12.5-HM materials
result in a brittle response without plastic deformation. Never-
theless, the compressive strength of these materials exceeds 2 GPa.
In comparison, Zhang et al. [17] achieved a maximum compressive
strength of nearly 1.1 GPa for Co-ODS under the same testing
conditions.
4. Conclusions
In this work, a Co-based superalloy with good mechanical
properties at room temperature was fabricated using the PM
technique (mechanical alloying and ﬁeld assisted hot pressing).
Mechanical alloying is demonstrated to be an ef ﬁcient way to
fabricate the alloys, but the milling time and grinding media must
