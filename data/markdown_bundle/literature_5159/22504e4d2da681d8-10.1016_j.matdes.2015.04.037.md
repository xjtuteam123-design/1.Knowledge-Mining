abstract
A novel Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy was prepared by combined processes of direct-chill semi-continuous
casting, heat-treatment and hot-extrusion. The alloy has good mechanical properties at room tempera-
ture due to the formation of profuse nano-spaced basal plane stacking faults (SFs). The ultimate tensile
strength (UTS), tensile yield strength (TYS) and elongation are 358 MPa, 253 MPa and 20%, respectively.
The Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy with SFs also obtains better damping capacity compared with Mg–8Er alloy.
The damping mechanism of Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy with SFs cannot be generally included in traditional
G–L theory. Inﬂuences of SFs to mechanical properties and damping capacity have been discussed, which
may provide a new perspective for developing magnesium alloys with both high damping and mechan-
ical properties.

1. Introduction
Magnesium (Mg) alloys are regarded as the promising struc-
tural metallic materials owing to their low density and high speci-
ﬁc strength. Moreover, pure Mg also has the best damping capacity
among various metallic materials and it may be ﬁt for dynamic
mechanical systems such as spacecrafts, speaker diaphragms and
acoustic cable shielding, but unfortunately, the strength of pure
Mg is far from desired level in engineering. Therefore, plenty of
work has been done to try to obtain both high damping and
mechanical properties through alloying treatment. However, gen-
erally speaking, in the cases of alloys the strength and damping
are incompatible, that is, Mg alloys with high strength generally
exhibit low damping capacities [1–4].
The traditional view about damping capacity of Mg alloys con-
forms to Granato–Lücke (G–L) theory, i.e. dislocation damping
mechanism [5–7]. According to G–L theory, the damping capacity
of Mg is only related to the movement of basal dislocation, which
relies on internal friction caused by vibration stress. Generally, in
high damping alloys dislocations are relatively free to vibrate.
While nowadays including solid solution [8], precipitation [9]
and grain size reﬁnement, all the available methods for strengthen-
ing Mg alloys is based on the pinning of dislocation [10,11]. It can
be concluded that in the high strength alloys movements of dislo-
cation are restrained by internal defects and boundaries [12].
Obviously, dislocations have opposite effects on the mechanical
and damping properties, and a contradiction exists in the combina-
tion of high damping capacity and high strength properties in Mg
alloys according to the traditional theory. Therefore, an extensive
application of Mg alloys as antivibration structural components is
restricted to a great extent. Recently, a Mg–Cu–Mn alloy was pre-
pared by Nishiyama et al. [13] using the powder metallurgy (PM)
technique. Compared with pure Mg the alloy has a better damping
capacity and an adequate strength. More importantly, a new mul-
tiple damping mechanism which is different from the traditional
G–L theory is revealed. It demonstrates that combining high damp-
ing capacity and high strength in one Mg alloy is completely pos-
sible according to the new damping mechanism.
As one important kind of Mg alloys, Mg–RE–Zn alloys (RE/Zn
weight ratio > 1, RE = Y, Gd, Tb, Dy, Ho, Er, Tm) have attracted great
attentions recently due to their enhanced properties by long period
stacking ordered (LPSO) structure. The LPSO phase is an ordered
structure in which the main components are RE and Zn atoms
and they are placed periodically in the Mg basal planes [14,15].

Conﬁrmed by plenty of researches, the LPSO phase could promote
mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, damping capacity and
other properties of Mg alloys [16–20]. Wang et al. [20] have ever
investigated the mechanical properties and damping capacity of
Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloys with varied secondary phases, including
MgZn2,M g 3Y2Zn3,M g 3YZn6 and Mg 12YZn (LPSO phase), and they
found that LPSO phase could enhance mechanical properties and
simultaneously optimize their damping capacities. During the
study of LPSO structure, there are also a few ﬁndings about stack-
ing faults (SFs) in as-cast, as-extruded or heat-treated Mg–RE–Zn
alloys. It is generally acknowledged that the SFs are the early stage
of LPSO structure formation [21–23]. However, few reports about
the effect of SFs on both damping capacity and mechanical proper-
ties of Mg alloys have been referred to up to now.
In current work, a new Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn (wt.%) alloy rein-
forced by profuse SFs was prepared based on Mg–8Er alloy, in
which Gd was added to replace half of Er and 1 wt.% Zn was added
to form a novel SFs structure. The microstructure, mechanical and
damping properties of the alloy with SFs were investigated, and it
hopes to contribute to the lightweight material design of Mg alloy
for antivibration and noise-reduction applications.

To make further investigation of the structures, EBSD data of
Mg–8Er and Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloys are shown in Figs. 4–6 . The
inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) maps ( Fig. 4) indicate that both the as-ex-

relatively uniform equiaxed grains, and the difference is that the
grains are reﬁned obviously along with the appearance of SFs in
the Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy. As shown in Fig. 5 , both the Mg–8Er
and Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloys exhibit typical RE texture component
with basal plane deviating from the extrusion direction.
Furthermore, it should be noted that the latter exhibits much
weaker basal texture than the former due to alloying with addi-
tional Gd and Zn. From Fig. 6 (a) it can be seen that both alloys
are mainly composed of large angle grain boundaries. The appear-

and may not be an effective factor to change mechanical properties
and damping capacity of the as-extruded alloys. It is important to
note the fraction distribution of Schmid factor for basal slip along
the extrusion direction (ED) of the two alloys, as shown in
Fig. 6 (b). The basal slip Schmid factors of Mg–8Er alloy tend to

Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy with SFs exhibits a relatively equal distri-
bution and its average value is 0.26001. Such a change of Schmid
factors from 0.34777 to 0.26001 would be an important factor
affecting the alloy performance.
3.2. Mechanical properties
Fig. 7 (a) and (b) shows the tensile properties of as-extruded
alloys at room temperature (RT). A superhigh ductility is exhibited
by the as-extruded Mg–8Er binary alloy and the elongation can
reach 44%, which is almost 5 times of that of the as-extruded pure
Mg. However, a relatively low tensile strength is also obtained on
the Mg–8Er alloy, whose tensile yield strength (TYS) is 153 MPa
and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) is 260 MPa. Compared with
Mg–8Er alloy, the strength of Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy is improved
markedly and the TYS and UTS are increased to 253 MPa and
358 MPa, respectively. A following order of the tensile strength is
shown in the three materials, that is Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn > Mg–
8Er > pure Mg. In addition, it should be noted that the ductility of
Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy still remains at a high level and the elonga-
tion can reach 20%. Similarly, as shown in Fig. 7 (c) and (d) also

Some researchers have reported that the damping capacity is
sensitive to the change of Schmid factor. The pure Mg demon-
strated the best damping capacity when Schmid factor reached
the maximum (0.447) and it almost lose the damping capacity
while Schmid factor was only 0.065 [27]. So lower Schmid factor
of Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn (0.26 vs. 0.35 for Mg–8Er alloy) will surely
weaken the damping capacity theoretically. Moreover, as strong
pitting center of dislocation, grain boundary (or ﬁne grain size)
can promote mechanical properties, and its negative effect on
damping capacity is also widely accepted according to G–L theory
[28]. Considering the above forecast of Schmid factor and grain
boundary, the damping capacity of Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy is
assumed to be lower than that of Mg–8Er alloy. However, the cur-
rent experimental results demonstrate that the former has the bet-
ter damping capacity than the latter. Therefore, it is easy to deduce
that the formation of SFs in Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy is not only
beneﬁt for mechanical properties but also damping capacity at
room temperature. According to the G–L theory [5–7], damping
capacities of Mg alloys are inﬂuenced by the maximum strain
amplitude and mainly originate in the movement of dislocations.
Solute atoms and nodes of dislocation networks on the basal plane
impose restrictions on dislocations, which are forced to consume
energy to perform vibration. To obtain high damping capacities,
the dislocations between the pining centers must be relatively free
to vibrate in terms of this theory. However, dislocation motion is
purposefully obstructed by internal defects and boundaries in tra-
ditional design of strength reinforcement. Consequently, the
mechanical properties and damping capacities in Mg–4Er–4Gd–
1Zn alloy may not follow the classic G–L theory, implying the pos-
itive effects of SFs. Therefore, further discussion about G–L theory
is needed to verify whether there really exists a new mechanism
involved in the effect of SFs on the damping capacity. Fig. 9 shows
the G–L plot of the Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy and a linear relation-
ship is roughly established, demonstrating that the damping
behavior may still have some relation to the normal dislocation
damping mechanism, but not only depend on it.
It has been reported that in many shape memory alloys with
excellent damping capacities, the origin of the high damping
capacities is associated not only with the presence and the hys-
teretic mobility of interphase and intervariant interfaces, but also
with the internal defects within martensite variants such as dislo-
cations, twin structures and SFs [29–31]. As mentioned above, the
special structure of SFs introduces a large amount of interface and
dislocation/cluster interaction [24], which should have large effect
on damping capacity, and in turn may play an important role in
damping mechanism besides movement of dislocation. Though
SFs structure suppresses movement of dislocation, it may also
cause interface decline or static hysteresis, as the same inﬂuence
of thermoelastic martensite twin structure in Mn–Cu or Ti–Ni
alloys [29–31]. Furthermore, more recently, Wang et al. reported
the LPSO structure in Mg–Cu–Mn–Zn–Y [32] and Mg–Zn–Y–Zr
[33] alloys had the function in improving mechanical properties
and meanwhile retaining high damping capacities and they called
it temporarily as ‘‘LPSO mechanism’’. With respect to the alloys
with SFs, there might be also a new damping mechanism related
to the unique structure of SFs as LPSO which cannot be generally
included in traditional G–L theory. However, whether the so-called
new mechanism or the ‘‘LPSO mechanism’’ both still need further
study in more detail in the future. Anyway, the present study

4. Conclusions
In the present study, a new Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn (wt.%) alloy was
fabricated by combined processes of direct-chill semi-continuous
casting, heat-treatment and hot-extrusion, and its microstructures,
mechanical and damping properties were investigated in detail.
The main results are summarized as follows.
(1) The microstructure of as-extruded Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy is
mainly composed of ﬁne equiaxed grains with high number
density of nano-spaced SFs, and meanwhile, the basal tex-
ture of this extruded alloy is weaker compared with com-
mon wrought Mg alloys.
(2) The as-extruded Mg–4Er–4Gd–1Zn alloy with SFs exhibits
high strength and ductility, and its UTS, TYS and elongation
are 358 MPa, 253 MPa and 20% at RT, respectively.
Furthermore, this alloy also shows higher damping capacity
than Mg–8Er alloy.
(3) The damping mechanism of the alloy with SFs is not in
agreement with G–L theory. The present study may open a
new perspective for developing magnesium alloys with high
strength and damping capacity.
