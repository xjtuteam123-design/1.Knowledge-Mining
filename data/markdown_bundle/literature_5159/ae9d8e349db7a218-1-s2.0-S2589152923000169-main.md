ABSTRACT 
Refractory alloys which retain high strength above 1000 
◦
C and, at the same time, have excellent malleability, 
are in high demand for high temperature structural applications. Here we report the microstructure and me -
chanical properties of an equiatomic MoReW alloy, which has demanding combination of strength and ductility 
in the temperature range of 25 
◦
C to 1500 
◦
C. The alloy was prepared by arc melting followed by hot isostatic 
pressing at 1400 
◦
C, 207 MPa for 5 h and it has a single-phase BCC structure with the average grain size of ~170 
μ m. The alloy yield stress is relatively low (555 MPa) at 25 
◦
C, but it has weak temperature dependence resulting 
in remarkably good yield stress values at high temperatures, e.g. 402 MPa at 1000 
◦
C and 247 MPa at 1400 
◦
C. 
The alloy shows strong strain hardening at 25 – 1000 
◦
C, with the true flow stress tripled at 25 
◦
C and doubled at 
1000 
◦
C after true strain of 0.4. The microstructural analysis of the deformed specimens reveals extensive 
deformation twinning activity at the beginning of deformation and activation of other deformation modes at later 
deformation stages in this temperature range. This is unusual behavior as generally activation of twinning occurs 
at high stresses, after extensive dislocation activity, and deformation twinning in refractory alloys has never been 
reported above 400 
◦
C. To understand and explain the observed behaviors of the alloy, a detailed analysis of 
strengthening mechanisms associated with screw and edge dislocation glide, as well as with twin nucleation and 
propagation, has been conducted and reasonable qualitative models of yielding, strain hardening and ductility of 
the studied MoReW alloy have been proposed. 
1. Introduction 
Refractory alloys and especially refractory complex concentrated 
alloys (RCCAs) are promising structural materials for use at tempera -
tures much beyond advanced Ni-based superalloys [ 1 , 2 ]. These mate -
rials can retain their strength and hardness at temperatures up to about 
0.6 – 0.7 of their absolute melting temperature T
m 
[3] . For example, 
RCCAs based on the Mo – Nb – Ta – W system can have T
m 
in the range from 
2673 K to 3273 K (2400 – 3000 
◦
C), and these alloys can retain their 
strength up to ~1603 – 2290 K (1330 – 2017 
◦
C). At these temperatures, 
Ni-based superalloys are already soft or even molten. 
Among refractory alloys, Mo – W based alloys have unique combina -
tion of high melting point, high strength at elevated temperatures, low 
coefficient of thermal expansion, high electrical and thermal conduc -
tivities, high corrosion resistance against many molten metals and 
glasses, and high tolerance to radiation damage. This makes Mo – W al -
loys of materials of choice for such aerospace applications as reaction 
control system thrusters, nuclear thermal propulsion (NTP) fuel clad, 
hypersonic wing leading edges, converging-diverging nozzles and power 
conversion systems [4] . For example, Mo-W alloys with T
m 
≥ 3000 K are 
considered for a rocket NTP reactor chamber in a proposed crewed 
mission to Mars [ 5 , 6 ]. Unfortunately, the applications of Mo – W alloys 
are limited by brittleness below brittle-to-ductile transition temperature 
( T
BDT
), which is often between 200 
◦
C and 800 
◦
C for these alloys [ 7 , 8 ]. 
T
BDT 
usually increases with increasing the amount of interstitial impu -
rities. The interstitial elements have been suggested to cause brittleness 
of these alloys (a) by segregating at grain boundaries and promoting 
inter-granular rupture and crack initiation and (b) by inhibiting dislo -
cation mobility and reducing the resistance to crack propagation [ 9 , 10 ].

3.1. HIP ’ d microstructure

4. Discussion

MoReW alloy has an essentially single-phase BCC crystal structure. A 
tiny enrichment in Re, possibly in the form of precipitates, was identified 
by SEM at some grain boundaries. It is likely that this Re enrichment 
strengthen grain boundaries and prohibit inter-granular cracking typical 
to Mo and W alloys [9] . The experimental data are in disagreement with 
the calculated equilibrium phase diagram for this alloy, which predicts a 
continuous increase in the volume fraction of a sigma phase with 
decreasing temperature below 1400 
◦
C ( Fig. 16a ). The disagreement can 
be explained by limited diffusion kinetics of the alloying elements

4.2. Yield stress for deformation twinning in MoReW 
Our experimental observations indicate that (112)[11 1]-type twin -
ning occurs during deformation of MoReW in the temperature range of 
25 
◦

◦
C and at the stress levels much smaller than the calcu -
lated critical stress for the screw dislocation motion. Taking into account 
that the yield stress for twinning has weak temperature dependence [ 15 , 
33 , 34 ] and noting that the temperature dependence of the yield stress 
for MoReW is also weak, one may suggest that deformation twinning 
controls the plastic onset in this alloy.

While deforming by mechanical twin shear, MoReW shows consid -

the yield stress ( Fig. 3 ). The strain hardening rate n = dln( σ )/d ε is

Different mechanisms can contribute to the observed strain hard -
ening at 25 – 1200 
◦
C. At the beginning of deformation strain hardening 
is likely caused by increasing difficulty for deformation twins to 
nucleate. Indeed, in accord to our calculations, screw dislocations are 
completely immobile during yielding while very mobile edge disloca -
tions annihilate or form pile-ups near grain boundaries. Under the 
applied stress part of a screw dislocation non-planar core transforms to 
the three-layer twin nucleus form. The nucleus needs to thicken to the 
critical height on the (112) plane to propagate [40] . This transformation 
stress is lower than the stress to move screw dislocations with the 
original core and corresponds to the observed yield stress. At this stress, 
only small part of screw dislocation transforms its core to the n-layer 
twin nucleus form while the rest of dislocation remains non-transformed 
and immobile. These observations have been made recently in large 
scale atomistic simulations for a beta-Ti alloy [42] . The remaining part 
of the screw dislocation that has not transformed at the yield stress will 
require higher stress to split into partials, form twins and thus contribute 
to further deformation process. As a result, the effective density of 
original screw dislocations that transforms their core to a 3-layer twin 
nucleus and take part in the deformation process will increase with 
increasing the applied stress. 
Mechanical twinning itself can cause hardening due to fragmentation 
of the original grains and thus a decrease in the effective slip length 
(Hall – Petch effect for slip) and effective shear length for crossing twins 
(Hall-Petch effect for shear) [ 34 , 43 ]. The analysis of available literature 
data conducted by Meyers et al. [34] shows that, for the same BCC 
metals, the Hall – Petch slope for twinning (38 – 125 MPa.mm
0.5
) is 4 to 9 
times higher than that for slip (3.5 – 33 MPa.mm
0.5
), indicating stronger 
strain hardening from twins than from dislocations due to a decrease in 
the effective grain size. Detailed studies by Mahajan on Mo – 35%Re [ 25 , 
40 ] however indicate that coherent twin boundaries are weak obstacles 
to dislocations and intersecting twins and thus the contribution to 
Hall – Petch effect from the twin boundaries should be weak. On the other 
hand, our results show that during extended deformation many former 
twin boundaries lose their coincident site lattice (CSL Σ 3) configuration 
and, therefore, during extended deformation they may behave as ordi -
nary grain boundaries. It is therefore expected that an increase in the 
number of mechanical twins and thus a decrease in the effective grain 
size during deformation of MoReW should cause stronger strengthening 
effect at smaller stresses where screw dislocations inactive and 
strengthening is controlled by an increase in the twin shear stress, but 
strain hardening should considerably slow down after the flow stress 
approaches and exceeds the critical stress for screw dislocation motion. 
Our experimental results seem to support this suggested strengthening 
scenario. 
Additional strain hardening can be caused by an increase in the 
immobile dislocation density. Our microstructure analysis shows that 
many twins propagate slowly, they do not propagate through the entire 
grain, but stop their movement inside the grain interior. It is expected 
that local stress concentrations formed in the front of the moving twin 
are sufficient to nucleate and activate dislocation sources. During this 
multiplication process, highly mobile edge dislocations accommodate 
the twin shear while screw dislocations move only a small distance and 
increase the density of forest dislocations. The validity of this 
strengthening mechanism is supported by the metallographic observa -
tions of high screw dislocation density near twin boundaries at the 
stress/strain levels below the critical values for screw dislocation mo -
tion, i.e. at stress/strain conditions in which screw dislocations are 
considered to be immobile. After the applied stress reaches a critical 
value and all the accumulated screw dislocations become mobile, strain 
hardening decreases considerably and extensive shear occurs, which, at 
low temperatures, may lead to strain localization and failure. This ex -
plains why the true peak stress achieved during deformation of MoReW 
is only slightly higher than the critical stress for activation of screw 
dislocation mobility and why the temperature dependences of these two 
stresses are similar. 
Finally, twin-induced strain hardening can also be caused by an in -
crease in the yield stress in the twinned regions due to crystallographic 
re-orientation and/or formation of sessile dislocations inside the twins 
[44 – 46] . The strengthening effect from this mechanism will increase 
with increasing the number of “ poorly ” oriented twins and it may also 
contribute to the Hall-Petch effect described above.

by interstitial elements which (a) segregate at grain boundaries, weaken

considerably impede dislocation mobility, thus reducing the plastic zone 
in front of the propagating crack, which results in intragranular cleavage

negative effects caused by the interstitial elements, which results in 
improving ductility and decreasing brittle-to-ductile transition temper -

micro-segregate at grain boundaries ( Fig. 2 ). As no cracks are found at 
grain boundaries of the heavily deformed alloy, one can suggest that the 
enrichment of grain boundaries with Re has beneficial effect on grain 
boundary strength of MoReW, although understanding the detailed 
mechanism of the Re-induced grain-boundary strengthening requires 
additional efforts, and this is out of the scope of the objectives of the 
current paper. Secondly, Re promotes an additional deformation 
mechanism, mechanical twinning, by decreasing the yield stress for twin 
shear in MoReW to rather low levels of ~400 – 550 MPa. These values are 
much smaller than those required to initiate slip of screw dislocations 
and, therefore, much less elastic energy is stored in the compression 
sample when the plastic deformation starts. Therefore, there is much less 
probability for the elastic energy aided crack nucleation and propaga -
tion. Local elastic stresses formed in the front of the propagating twin 
should be dissipated by dislocation slip. Due to high critical stress for 
screw dislocation movement in the studied alloy, it is unlikely that screw 
dislocations participate in the elastic energy dissipation. On the other 
hand, edge dislocations are estimated to have high mobility in MoReW. 
One can suggest that the local stresses in front of the moving twin are 
sufficient for dislocation multiplications (operating of different type of 
dislocation sources). The edge components of the formed loops glide 
rapidly to accommodate twin shear and dissipate the elastic energy, 
while screw components are moving much smaller distances and are 
collected near twin boundaries. This scenario is in agreement with 
SEM/EBSD and TEM observations of high density of screw dislocations 
near twin boundaries. At the applied stresses larger than the critical 
screw dislocation stress, both edge and screw components accommodate 
the twin propagation. Gliding of screw dislocations accumulated near 
twin boundaries create characteristic shear bands adjacent to the twin 
boundaries, as it can be seen, e.g., in Fig. 11 .
