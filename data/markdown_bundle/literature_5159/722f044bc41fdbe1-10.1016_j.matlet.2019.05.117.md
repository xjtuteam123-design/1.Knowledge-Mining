Abstract
The mechanical properties of dehydrogenated Ti-55 titanium alloy after superplastic 
deformation at its maximum service temperature of 550 °C were investigated through tensile 
experiment. It was found that reasonable hydrogen content can significantly improve the strength 
at 550℃ of dehydrogenated Ti-55 alloy after superplastic tension. Compared with as-received one, 
the yield strength and ultimate tensile strength of 0.5wt%H-dehydrogenated specimen are 
increased by 35.6% and 33.9%, respectively. This results from the microstructural refinement and 
dislocation strengthening by thermohydrogen processing. 
Keywords: Ti-55 alloy; Thermohydrogen processing; Mechanical properties; Superplastic 
deformation
1. Introduction 
In recent years, many investigations have been focused on hydrogen as a temporary alloying 
element to refine the microstructure and enhance mechanical properties of titanium alloys [1-4], 
which is called thermohydrogen processing (THP) [5-7]. Ti-55 titanium alloy with a nominal 
composition of Ti-5.5Al-3.5Sn-3.0Zr-1.5Mo-0.4Ta-0.4Nb-0.3Si (wt.%) is a near-alpha titanium 
alloy and the maximum service temperature of Ti-55 titanium alloy is 550 °C. This alloy shows 
desirable mechanical properties and excellent high-temperature creep resistance [8]. It is a 
candidate material for the structural parts of advanced aerocrafts [9]. The superplastic deformation 
characteristics of Ti-55 titanium alloy were widely studied, such as tensile deformation behavior at 
elevated temperatures, microstructure evolution and recrystallization mechanism [10-12]. 
Although the investigations on superplastic properties of metals and alloys have been extensively

carried out, few studies are focused on the effect of hydrogen addition on mechanical properties of 
titanium alloy after superplastic deformation. Actually, hydrogen has two sides in titanium alloys. 
On the one hand, the reasonable hydrogen content can improve the hot workability of titanium 
alloys [13-15]. The optimum superplastic tensile temperature of Ti-55 alloy with 0.1wt%H is 
reduced by about 125 ℃ [13, 14]. The superplastic deformation domain in Ti600 titanium alloy 
with 0.16wt%H occurs at lower deformation temperature and higher strain rate than those of as-
received one [15]. On the other hand, the hydrogen content in titanium alloys should be strictly 
restricted in industrial applications to avoid hydrogen embrittlement [16]. Therefore, in order to 
get a closer reference to engineering applications of superplastic forming by hydrogen addition, 
the mechanical properties of dehydrogenated Ti-55 titanium alloy after superplastic deformation at 
its maximum service temperature of 550°C were studied. It helps to improve the application of 
THP technique in superplastic forming of titanium alloys.
2. Experimental
Ti-55 alloy in sheet form with a thickness of 2mm was supplied by Institute of Metal 
Research in China. The specimens were hydrogenated at 750 ℃ by holding in a pure hydrogen 
environment for 1h followed by air-cooling to room temperature. The hydrogen contents in the 
range of 0~0.5wt% were obtained by controlling the charging time. The actual hydrogen content 
was determined by weighing the specimen before and after hydrogenation through an analytic 
balance with an accuracy of 0.01mg. 
The superplastic tension tests of as-received alloy specimens and hydrogenated ones with 
0.1wt%H, 0.3wt%H and 0.5wt%H were first carried out on Zwick Z050 universal testing machine 
at 825℃ with an initial strain rate of 10 -3s-1. All the specimens were stretched to an elongation of 
about 100%. And then the tension was ceased. After superplastic deformation, the stretched 
samples were dehydrogenated and all residual hydrogen contents of Ti-55 are less than 
0.0125wt%, which is a threshold value of hydrogen content for safe use in Ti-55 titanium alloy 
following the GB/T 3620.1-2016 standard [17]. It should be mentioned above that the maximum 
service temperature of Ti-55 titanium alloy is 550°C. Therefore, the dehydrogenated specimens 
were subjected to tensile testing at 550 ℃ with an initial strain rate of 10 -2s-1. Each tension under 
the same condition was repeated with a minimum of three samples to ensure the repeatability and 
reproducibility. The hydrogenated and dehydrogenated Ti-55 alloys after high-temperature tension

3. Experimental results and discussion
The high-temperature strength and elongation (EL) at 550 ℃ of tensile samples after 
superplastic deformation is shown in Fig.1. With the increase of hydrogen content, the yield 
strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of dehydrogenated Ti-55 alloys are both 
improved. Compared with the as-received alloy, YS and UTS of 0.5H-dehydrogenated specimen 
are increased by 35.6% and 33.9%, respectively. Meanwhile, the elongation firstly almost 
maintains unchanged and then decreases with increasing hydrogen content. Actually, low 
hydrogen content of no more than 0.1wt%H results in relatively less effect on high-temperature 
mechanical properties. When the hydrogen content is relatively high, the strength increase and 
elongation decrease level off with further increasing hydrogen content.
SEM microstructures of as-received and dehydrogenated Ti-55 alloys after high-temperature 
tension at 550 ℃ are observed in Fig.2. The content of β phase in as-received alloy is relatively 
low, which randomly distributes in α phase matrix. Fig.2 (b) shows that the 0.1H-dehydrogenated 
specimen is composed by equiaxed α phase and intergranular β phase. Furthermore, the β phase 
distributes uniformly at the grain boundaries of α phase, which contributes to the compatible 
deformation due to the good plasticity of β phase. The 0.3H-dehydrogenated sample reveals a 
large number of acicular α phases and a small amount of intergranular β phase in Fig.2 (c). The 
acicular α phases increase grain boundary area, which results in dislocation pile-up and 
deformation resistance increase. Therefore, a higher strength and less plasticity is obtained. With 
further increasing hydrogen content, the content of acicular α phases decreases and β phase 
uniformly disperses in α phase matrix as shown in Fig.2 (d). As the degree of uniform dispersion 
increases, more α/β phase interfaces impede the movement of dislocations. The dislocations

accumulate at grain boundaries, which severely inhabits their movement and results in the 
dislocation strengthening effect. Furthermore, finer microstructure exists in 0.5H-dehydrogenated 
specimen compared with 0.3H-dehydrogenated one. The microstructures are easily refined due to 
the transformation and recrystallization during hydrogen absorption and desorption process [18]. 
Therefore, the 0.5H-dehydrogenated specimen indicates the highest strength and the worst 
plasticity. 
Fig.3 indicates EBSD of as-received and 0.5H-dehydrogenated Ti-55 alloy after high-
temperature tension at 550 ℃. The average grain size of 0.5H-dehydrogenated alloy is 
approximately 0.5µm in Fig.3(b), which is much smaller than that of as-received alloy in Fig.3(a). 
Many studies have shown that the THP treatment can refine grain microstructure. According to 
the Hall-Petch relationship [19, 20], a smaller grain size can lead to a higher strength. Additionally, 
the grain refinement can prominently increase grain boundary area. Actually, the grain boundary 
pinning effect hinders further dislocation movement [21]. The dislocation strengthening effect can 
be also improved. Consequently, the strength of 0.5H-dehydrogenated sample is significantly 
increased in comparison with as-received alloy. 
TEM observations of as-received and 0.5H-dehydrogenated specimens after high- 
temperature tension are shown in Fig.4. The dislocation density in 0.5H-dehydrogenated sample is 
much higher than that in as-received one. Meanwhile, the interaction and tangling of dislocations 
cause greater resistance to dislocation movement. According to the dislocation strengthening 
theory, more dislocation density plays an important role in strength increase. Meanwhile, it 
usually leads to the elongation decrease [22]. Therefore, the strength of 0.5H-dehydrogenated 
specimen obviously arises while the elongation reduces.
4. Conclusions
The high-temperature (550 ℃) mechanical properties of dehydrogenated Ti-55 alloy after 
superplastic deformation were studied and the following conclusions can be drawn:
(1) With the increase of hydrogen content, the strength at 550 ℃ of Ti-55 alloy after 
superplastic deformation and dehydrogenation are improved. Compared with the as-
received alloy, the yield and ultimate tensile strengths of 0.5H-dehydrogenated specimen 
are increased by 35.6% and 33.9%, respectively.
