ABSTRACT 
Designing a novel microstructure with higher strength-ductility than lamellar microstructure 
(LM), which has been considered the most valuable engineering application in the service tem -
perature range, is desired for lightweight TiAl alloys. Our prior publication reported a three-phase 
tri-modal (T-T) structure for Ti-43.5Al-4Nb-1Mo-0.1B(.at%) with superior mechanical properties 
than LM at room temperature (Ref: Acta Mater. 225 (2022) 117,585). However, its strength- 
ductility at working temperature has yet to be fully exploited. This work fills the much-needed 
gap. The qualitative and quantitative optimizations indicate that the T-T structure ’ s yield 
strength increases first and then decreases with increasing pearlitic-like microstructure (PM, 
which consist of three-phase ( α
2
, γ and β
o 
phase) with clear orientation relationships that form in 
a lamellar/cellular manner) content while the ductility keeps rising. When the T-T structure 
contains ~20 % PMs, it exhibits a yield strength about 130MPa higher than LM coupling with 
doubled ductility at 750 
◦
C. PMs can induce a prominent twinning induced plasticity and 
dislocation jog dragging (TWIP & DJD) effect, resulting in better strength-ductility below 700
◦
C; 
above this temperature, the TWIP & DJD effect significantly plasticizes the alloys. The higher 
strength of the T-T structure at 750 
◦
C is caused by hetero-deformation-induced strengthening 
between LMs and PMs, which cannot be offset by the plasticizing caused by PM when the volume 
fraction of PMs is less than 50 %. This study demonstrates that introducing an appropriate 
amount of plasticizing structures (similar to PM) with excellent work hardening capacity is a 
promising strategy for enhancing the strength-ductility synergy of TiAl and possibly other brittle 
materials.

1. Introduction 
Intermetallics with ordered phase structures constitute a class of structural materials that bridge the gap between ordinary metals 
and hard ceramics. TiAl, a promising lightweight intermetallic, is currently employed to replace heavier Ni-based superalloys for 
aerospace components in the temperature range of 650 – 850 
◦
C ( Chen et al., 2016 ; Clemens and Mayer, 2013 ). The attractive ad -
vantages of TiAl alloys include low density, good oxidation resistance, high creep resistance and strength up to high temperatures 
( Appel et al., 2016 ; Bewlay et al., 2016 ). All these superiorities render their applications with a weight reduction of as much as ~50 % 
compared to their counterpart Ni-based superalloys, effectively facilitating environmental protection and energy conservation with 
almost no compromise on the in-service mechanical performance. For example, using the lighter TiAl alloys in the aerospace industry 
significantly reduce fuel consumption and, thereby, the emissions of CO
2 
and NO
χ 
( Clemens and Mayer, 2013 ; Neogi and Janisch, 
2021 ). GE Aviation and MTU Aero Engines have implemented the Ti – 48Al – 2Nb – 2Cr (all in .at% across the paper unless otherwise 
mentioned) and forged Ti-43.5Al-4Nb-1Mo-0.1B (TNM) alloys in the turbine blades of GEnx ™ (Boeing 787) and A320 engines, 
respectively ( Bewlay et al., 2013 , 2016 ; Zhu et al., 2010 ). However, large-scale engineering applications of TiAl intermetallics still 
require an optimization of microstructure and mechanical performance in the service temperature range. 
Therefore, achieving higher strength and better ductility at working temperatures for TiAl alloys is much needed. Up to now, three 
approaches have been devoted to improving the high-temperature mechanical performance: (1) Manufacturing the polysynthetic 
twinned TiAl single crystals with 0
◦
lamellar orientation. This single crystal exhibits remarkable failure strength and superior creep 
resistance at 900 
◦
C when the external loading is parallel to the lamellar orientation ( Chen et al., 2016 , 2021 ), far better than that of 
polycrystalline TiAl alloys. However, because of the strong anisotropy, it is unsuitable for rotor parts, such as the engine blade. (2) 
Alloying with refractory metal elements, such as the Nb, Mo, W, Ta, etc., to provide solution strengthening can endow TiAl with higher 
strength ( Clemens and Mayer, 2013 ; Imayev et al., 2021 ). These alloying induce a majority of ordered β
o 
phases in TiAl, which severely 
degrades the room temperature ductility and gives rise to a microstructural instability at service temperature. (3) Adding Si, C and N to 
develop hard second-phase particle which effectively impedes the dislocation gliding and enhances the alloys ’ service performance 
( Genc and Unal, 2022 ; Kastenhuber et al., 2018 ). These methods to increase strength often reduce ductility. In recent decades, 
microstructural engineering to form gradient nanostructures ( Fang et al., 2011 ; Wei et al., 2014 ), nanotwinned grains ( Liang et al., 
2022 ), and heterogeneous structures ( Ma and Zhu, 2017 ; Park et al., 2018 ; Wu and Zhu, 2017 ), simultaneously enhanced strength and 
ductility of metals. Among them, developing a heterostructure in TiAl alloy has inherent advantage due to the ability to control 
complex phase composition and the phase transition between phases. The heterostructure can induce a hetero-deformation-induced 
strain-hardening/strengthening, improving both strength and ductility. 
Due to the low SFE of the major γ phase in TiAl intermetallics, deformation twins can be developed easily during plastic defor -
mation. Mechanical twinning (a deformation mechanism that competes with dislocation slip) was effective in breaking strength- 
ductility trade-off via the TWIP effect ( Palomares-García et al., 2017 ; Zhang et al., 2022 ). This effect has also been well demon -
strated in TiAl intermetallics ( Hao et al., 2017 ; Liu et al., 2021 ; Sun et al., 2023 ). The extensive formation of deformation twins 
improves the dislocation storage capacity by regulating the mean free path of dislocations, and thereby sustaining strain hardening to 
larger strains ( Guo et al., 2021 ). It also enables a remarkable dynamic Hall-Patch effect, endowing alloys higher strength. Remarkably, 
deformation twins are more likely to be activated in TiAl intermetallics during straining at elevated temperatures ( Appel et al., 2016 ), 
which may produce a more noticeable TWIP effect. However, this hypothesis still needs to be verified in TiAl. It will be vital to reveal 
how the TWIP effect affects the mechanical performance of TiAl alloys at working temperature. This knowledge will facilitate their 
broader applications. 
Due to the extreme absence of strain hardening of LM, it exhibits the highest strength but undergoes catastrophic failure imme -
diately after the elastic or yielding stage during tensile deformation ( Selvarajou et al., 2022 ). The lack of strain hardening suggests an 
extremely low defect storage capacity, which also causes the strength of TiAl alloy not to be fully exploited. It may be possible to 
enhance the strength-ductility by designing a novel microstructure that couple the hetero-deformation-induced strength -
ening/hardening and the TWIP effect. Based on this microstructural design philosophy, our prior work introduced PM to develop T-T 
and T-B structures via decomposing LM using cellular reaction ( Zheng et al., 2022 ). The designed T-T structure significantly improved 
the alloy ’ s strength while doubling the ductility at RT compared to the LM ( Zheng et al., 2022 ). However, as an alloy for 
high-temperature applications, it is more important to further optimize the microstructure with improved mechanical properties at 
working temperature. 
The present work performs a microstructural optimization with improved mechanical performance of the designed T-T structure at 
working temperature, addressing the urgent need to enhance the strength/ductility of TiAl intermetallics for aerospace applications. A 
solution aging heat treatment process with a variable aging temperatures and soaking time was devised to control the volume fraction 
of PMs. Surprisingly, this optimized T-T structure exhibits far better strength-ductility matching than LM at 750 
◦
C, which was never 
reported for TiAl alloys. The correlation among mechanical performance, microstructure, and deformation mechanisms was clarified 
in detail via microscopic characterization. Our investigation may open up a new way to synchronously improve the strength and 
ductility of TiAl intermetallics.

2.1. Preparation of materials 
The TiAl alloy selected in this study is the so-called TNM alloy with the nominal composition of Ti – 43.5Al – 4Nb – 1Mo – 0.1B (at.%). 
Upper service temperature of it is 750 
◦

microstructure. 
2.3. Microstructural characterization 
The microstructural features of LM, T-T (T-T1 and T-T2), and T-B structures are characterized via scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) in backscattered electron (BSE) mode and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The samples for SEM examination were 
prepared by standard metallurgical procedures. First, these samples were polished with SiC abrasive paper from 80# to 3000#. After 
that, they were electrochemical polished for approximately 40s in a solution of 30ml of perchloric acid, 210ml of butanol, and 390ml of 
methanol at 25V and 0

distance of 10 mm. To reveal the deformation mechanism of the three microstructures during the tensile process, the deformed samples 
were sliced perpendicular to the tensile axis via electron discharge machining. Moreover, interrupted T-B samples at 4 % and 10 % 
strain were used to explore the evolution of the microstructure and deformation mechanism as a function of strain. These thin foils 
were thinned to electron transparency via a conventional twin-jet polishing method with a solution consisting of 5 % perchloric acid, 
30 % n ‑ butyl alcohol and 60 % alcohol at 20
◦

field-emission transmission electron microscope (FEI Talos F200X TEM) operating at an accelerating voltage of 200kV.

3.2. Tensile properties of various structures 
The baseline strength and ductility are essential indicators for the engineering application of structural materials. Fig. 4 (a) shows 
the mechanical performance of the optimized T-T structure and LM at various temperatures, indicating that the T-T structure possesses 
higher YS and better ductility than LM from room temperature (25 
◦
C) to 750 
◦
C. To the best of the authors ’ knowledge, this work is the 
first to report on a novel microstructure that possesses much higher strength and better ductility than the LM at elevated temperatures. 
Therefore, the T-T structure shows its superiority over the LM. When the tensile tests were performed at 800 
◦
C, the T-T structure 
exhibited lower YS than that of the LM despite a dramatic improvement in ductility. Retaining better strength and ductility matching is 
essential for the press-forming and energy-absorption capability to improve crashworthiness in the service process ( Gao et al., 2021 ). 
Our experiments indicated that the optimal T-T structure containing 20 – 25 % PMs exhibited the best strength-ductility matching at 
elevated temperature; when its PMs are 55 – 70 %, the room temperature strength-ductility matching is best. Our quantitative opti -
mization gives clear instruction for the microstructural design to improve the comprehensive strength-ductility of TiAl intermetallics 
significantly. 
Fig. 4 (b) shows the mechanical performance of the LM, T-T1, T-T2 and T-B structures tested at 750
◦
C, which strongly depends on 
the PMs ’ volume fraction. As the proportion of PMs increases, the yield strength (YS) increases first and then decreases, while the 
ductility increases rapidly. The inset in Fig. 4 (b) indicates that T-T1 exhibits ~130MPa higher YS and UTS than LM while doubling the 
ductility. The UTS of T-T2 is still comparable to that of LM and has a 3-fold enhancement in ductility. When the PM volume fraction in 
the T-T structure exceeds that of T-T2, its strength is lower than that of LM, whereas the ductility increases rapidly. In particular, the 
UTS of T-B is ~100MPa lower than that of LM, yet its ductility is nine times that of LM. Thus, tuning the proportion of PM in the T-T 
structure can effectively optimize its mechanical properties.

3.3. Deformation mechanism of the LM 
Fig. 5 illustrates typical TEM, HRTEM, and FFT patterns of the deformed LM at 750
◦
C. Dislocation networks are observed in 
relatively thicker γ lamellae (interlamellar spacing > 80nm) but are almost absent in thinner γ laths, as presented in Fig. 5 (a). These 
dislocations and dislocation networks accumulated at the α
2
/ γ phase interface. Moreover, these γ lamellae are also characterized by 
blade-shaped bundles that are at angles of 60
◦
to the α
2
/ γ interface. At first glance, they resemble SFs. Hsiung et al. considered them 
twin embryos ( Hsiung et al., 2002 ); however, there are no diffraction pattern of the twin symmetry or HRTEM image to prove this. 
Fig. 5 (c) shows an HRTEM micrograph of C region where contains blade-shaped bundles. A coherent and serrated interface is observed 
between the bundle and matrix, suggesting that the misfit between them is elastically taken up. FFT patterns of the matrix and bundles 
(I and III regions in Fig. 5 (c)) present the < 01 1] γ and < 1 10 > γ zone axes, respectively, as presented in Fig. 5 (d) and (f). Therefore, the 
blade-shaped bundle shares the same lattice structure with the γ matrix, which is the different oriented γ variant. The angle between 
< 01 1] and < 1 10 > is 120
◦
, indicating that the bundle is a product of the 120
◦
rotation fault of the γ matrix along the < 111 > γ zone axis. 
Hereafter, the blade-shaped γ bundle is denoted as the γ’ variant. Moreover, the FFT pattern of area II(contains the γ matrix and γ’ 
variants) shown in Fig. 5 (e) does not show any evidence of twin diffraction spot. The orientation relationship between γ / γ’ follows 
< 01 1] γ // < 1 10 > γ’ , (1 11) γ // (111) γ’ . The local development of γ’ variants within the lamellar γ matrix during tensile deformation is 
rarely reported. The large elastic strain energy presented at γ / γ’ interface accommodates the misfit compensating defects. Such misfit 
accommodation naturally generates high constraint stress during plastic deformation. 
For those thinner γ laths with typical interlamellar spacing less than 80nm, no dislocations or γ’ variants developed after tensile 
tests, indicating a different deformation mechanism. Fig. 6 shows the HRTEM micrographs of deformed thinner γ laths, revealing a

4.2. Low ductility of LM 
It is well-known that the LM in TiAl intermetallics exhibits the worst ductility, which poses severe risks to engineering applications 
( Zhu and Wu, 2023 ). Three reasons are responsible for this: 
(1) Deformation incompatibility of adjacent α
2
/ γ lamellar domains. As shown in Fig. 2 (a), the adjacent domains of LM display 
different lamellar orientations, resulting in the incompatibility of deformation behavior. Mismatched deformation induces local 
stress concentration and development of micro-cracks, and thus the poor ductility of LM between domains ( Edwards et al., 
2019 ). 
(2) Cleavage fracture is induced by α
2 
lamellae. It is extremely difficult to induce slip in the ordered α 2 lamellae ( Appel et al., 2016 ). 
When high levels of stress are localized at the α
2
/ γ interface, the α
2 
lamellae are prone to cleavage fracture ( Liang et al., 2022 ). 
The interrupted dislocations slip and propagation of γ’ variants at α
2
/ γ boundaries presented in Fig. 5 (a) suggest severe stress 
concentration in front of α
2
/ γ interfaces, which can easily induce cleavage fracture of α
2 
lamellae, leading to low ductility. 
(3) The limited space of nanoscale lamellae made accumulating crystal defects difficult ( Li et al., 2010 ), resulting in a non-durable 
work hardening behavior; as a result, it is difficult for LM to maintain the tensile deformation to a larger strain. 
Moreover, when the lamellar spacing of LM is less than 80nm, the activation of dislocation and γ’ variants become difficult. The 
constraint stress accumulated at lamellar boundaries is accommodated by forming LPSO structures parallel to the lamellar interface, 
such as 9R and 6H. Studies in Mg alloys have demonstrated that the LPSO structure can significantly enhance the strength and creep 
resistance ( Li et al., 2017 ; Mayama et al., 2022 ). Our investigation demonstrated that forming of multiple LPSO bands could greatly 
enhance ductility without compromising yield strength in TiAl alloy ( Zheng et al., 2023 a). Nevertheless, no multiple LPSO bands were 
observed in the deformed LM of this work; the small amount of LPSO structure does not have much impact on the improvement of 
ductility.

4.4. Back stress strengthening in the T-T structure 
T-T is the classical heterostructure due to the different deformability of LM and PM. The interaction between heterostructures can 
cause hetero-deformation strengthening/hardening during deformation, producing mechanical performance exceeding the rule-of- 
mixtures prediction ( Chou et al., 2022 ; Li et al., 2020 a; Ma and Wu, 2019 ). As shown in Fig. 4 (b), the T-B structure exhibits a YS 
~150MPa lower than LM at 750 
◦
C; upon tensile straining, the PM starts to deform first and, subsequently, are constrained by the 
stronger LM around them. The restraint effect converts uniaxial tensile stress into a complex two-dimensional stress, rendering more 
even deformation ( Wu et al., 2015 a; Yang et al., 2018 ). In order to accommodate the disparity of deformation, high-density GNDs were 
blocked at the LM and PM interfaces, as shown in Fig. 15 (a, b). The generation of GNDs reflects an increase in dislocation density; 
further inducing an isotropic and kinematic strain hardening of the T-T structure ( Fribourg et al., 2011 ). GNDs accumulation produces 
long-range back stress in the PM, increasing the strength of PM by the time the whole specimen is yielded ( Wu et al., 2014 ; Zhu and 
Wu, 2023 ); in turn, forward stress was induced in the LM, as illustrated by Fig. 15 (c). The forward stress promoted the deformation of 
LM, which have the same effect as the applied stress in driving dislocation slip. For the softer PM, the back stress was accommodated by 
applying external stress, which induced further plastic straining. Due to the mechanical incompatibility of the two structures, the back 
stress strengthening remains until the end of stretching ( Liang et al., 2018 ). The back stress can be quantified using the loading ‒

4.6. A strategy for improving the strength and ductility of TiAl alloy 
In general, the extreme lack of work-hardening leads to the low ductility of TiAl alloys, which also restricts the effective play of 
their intrinsic strength. Introducing a microstructure with excellent dislocation storage capacity to TiAl alloy may be an effective 
strategy to improve its strength and ductility simultaneously. Our present and previous work has advanced this strategy by introducing 
the PM into the LM ( Zheng et al., 2022 ). This strategy has also been successfully used to solve the intractable problem of high brit -
tleness of nanocrystalline metals ( Yinmin Wang, 2022 ), Ni
3
Al intermetallics ( Yang et al., 2020 ), nano-lamellar materials ( Fan et al., 
2020 ), and precipitation-hardening high-entropy ( Liang et al., 2018 ). The benefits of this strategy are twofold: (1) Improving the 
work-hardening capacity of the whole alloy, which not only contributes to the improvement of ductility but also gives full play to its 
strength; (2) Generating high back stress strengthening effect. The challenge is to find a structure with deformation capacity 
compatible with TiAl alloys from the vast design space. 
5. Conclusions 
In this work, the T-T structure and its high-temperature tensile properties were optimized by tuning the PM content using the 
solution and aging heat-treatment process. Compared with the mechanical properties of LM which was always considered to be the 
most valuable in engineering applications in the past, it was found that the optimized T-T structure exhibited much higher strength and 
ductility than LM. Based on the different deformation mechanisms and morphology of LM and PM, the underlying mechanisms for the 
improved mechanical properties of the T-T structure were revealed. The following conclusions can be drawn: 
(1) Increasing the aging temperature promotes cellular reaction rate and increases PM volume fraction (PM is an indispensable part 
of the T-T structure) in the T-T structure. 
(2) The deformation mechanisms of LM exhibit a strong dependence on lamellar spacing. When the lamellar width is less than 
80nm, the development of LPSO structures parallel to the lamellar interface dominates the plastic deformation of LM. Above 
80nm, the plastic deformation is performed by dislocation slip and forming of γ’ variants. The nanoscale lamellar spacing of LM 
makes accumulating crystal defects relatively difficult, resulting in a non-durable work hardening rate and low ductility.
