Development of Ni – Mn-based alloys
for the fast epitaxial braze-repair
of single-crystalline nickel-based superalloys
Dedicated to Professor Dr. Monika Feller-Kniepmeier on the occasion of her 80 th birthday
Diffusion brazing is a widely-used technology for the repair
of cracks in hot section turbine components, mostly fabri-
cated from nickel-based superalloys. However, the filling
of wide cracks in the range of 100–300 lm is difficult since
the precipitation of brittle secondary phases, which are
formed by the conventionally used melting point depres-
sants B and Si, leads to deteriorating mechanical properties.
Therefore, new Ni – Mn-based braze alloys were developed
which allow a very fast epitaxial healing of particularly
wide cracks in single-crystalline components. As B and Si
are replaced by Mn, the repair process can be significantly
shortened since the epitaxial solidification is not comple-
tely controlled by diffusion but can also be controlled by
cooling. Ni – Mn-based systems enhanced by Al, Cr and Ti
were investigated. In this work an improved brazing cycle
for the minimization of porosity within the braze gap as
well as an enhanced heat treatment, which produces a c/c’
microstructure very similar to the parent material, are pre-
sented. Results from tensile tests at room temperature and
at 900 8C conducted on 300 lm gap width samples are dis-
cussed.
Keywords: High temperature brazing; Epitaxial solidifica-
tion; Nickel-based superalloys; Single-crystals
1. Introduction
With regard to a cost effective life extension of turbine
components, repair of damaged components instead of re-
placement is of special interest [1]. This is due to the fact,
that on the one hand single-crystalline components are very
expensive, due to their complicated fabrication; on the
other hand the fraction of expensive alloying elements such
as Re and Ru has increased during recent years, since both
elements improve the creep strength of nickel-based super-
alloys and, therefore, allow applications at higher tempera-
tures [2 – 4].
Diffusion brazing has been used for many years for the
repair of cracks in components made from nickel-based
superalloys. Originally, the technology was developed by
Duvall et al. for the joining of complex shaped parts, fabri-
cated from nickel-base superalloys [5]. The advantage of
this so-called TLP-bonding (transient liquid phase bonding)
is, that in contrast to solid state diffusion bonding technolo-
gies, high bonding pressure is not necessary. If sufficient
hold times at the bonding temperature are applied, the braze
gaps can be homogenized both in composition and struc-
ture, producing bond strengths equivalent to the base mate-
rial [5].
TLP-bonding has also been recognized as an applicable
technology for the repair of narrow cracks: a braze system
similar to the base material, which is enhanced by a fast dif-

fusing melting-point depressant (MPD) such as B, is ap-
plied to the cracks. During the brazing cycle, as the brazing
temperatures exceeds the liquidus temperature of the braze
alloy, the filler liquefies and flows into the crack. In case
of narrow gaps in the range of 50 – 100 lm, this happens
due to capillary attraction [4]. As the MPD starts to diffuse
into the surrounding base material, a diffusion controlled,
isothermal solidification starts. If a single-crystalline mate-
rial is at hand, heterogeneous nucleation at the gap faces of
the single-crystalline material occurs. Therefore, epitaxial
solidification can be achieved, which means that no grain
boundaries are produced within the braze gap [5 – 12].
For the repair of wide cracks in the range of 100 –
300 lm, which are typically found on industrial gas turbine
components [13], another brazing technology has been de-
veloped by Demo and Ferrigno [14]. This so-called ADH-
process (activated diffusion healing) uses a superalloy
powder (additive filler), mixed with the braze filler metal
and an organic brazing binder, which allows easier applica-
tion of the metal powder mixture. The additive filler pro-
vides three basic functions: it acts as 1) a source for the cap-
illary forces to draw the molten braze alloy, 2) a gap retainer
during brazing and 3) an additional sink for the MPDs B
and/or Si [4].
The last function in particular is very important, since the
solubility of B and Si in Ni is very poor. Therefore, com-
plete diffusion of these elements into the base material and
the filler material, respectively, is essential to avoid the for-
mation of brittle intermetallic phases within the braze gaps.
Since both systems, Ni – B as well as Ni – Si, are eutectic
ones, MPD-concentrations above the solubility limit in-
volve solidification terminating with the eutectic reaction.
This results in a poly-crystalline microstructure with brittle
phases in the center of the braze gap. Even with the use of
ADH, the filling of wide cracks is not economically feasi-
ble, since hold times of several days are necessary to pro-
vide sufficient diffusion of the MPDs [15 – 19].
To achieve a significant shortening of the hold times
which are required for the wide gap repair, new braze alloys
have been developed [20 – 22]. The approach is the use of
Ni – Mn-based alloying systems, which are completely mis-
cible. In this case, the formation of secondary phases, ser-
ving as nucleation sites for stray grains, is completely
avoided. The results of several brazing experiments by ap-
plication of Ni – Mn-based systems are demonstrated in
Ref. [20]. The epitaxial repair of wide gaps was achieved
within brazing times up to 100 times shorter compared to
conventional braze alloys. With regard to appropriate
high-temperature stability and sufficient oxidation and cor-
rosion resistance, the braze alloys were enhanced by Cr and
Al, acting as c solid-solution hardeners, oxide-film formers
(Al
2O3,C r 2O3) and c’formers (Al) [2, 23]. Ti was identi-
fied as an appropriate secondary MPD, due to its additional
c’stabilizing effect [21].

2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Variation of heat treatment
Samples fabricated from the second generation single-crys-
talline nickel-based superalloy René N5 were used in line
with earlier brazing experiments with Ni – Mn-systems, de-
scribed in Refs. [20 – 22]. 300 lm wide gaps perpendicular
to the [001] direction of the single-crystal were introduced
using a cutting machine with a 300 lm wide cutting blade.
It was deduced from thermodynamic simulations, described
in Ref. [22], that the amount of Al and Ti has to be re-
stricted in order to avoid the formation of the intermetallic
phases NiAl and g, since both phases lead to a decreased
amount of c’and thus reduce the high temperature stability.
As the c’solvus temperature is slightly decreased by in-
creasing amounts of Cr, the amount of Cr was limited to
5 wt.%. The selected braze alloys are listed in Table 1.
Small billets of each braze alloy were produced in a va-
cuum arc furnace. These billets were cut into thin bars
which were placed on the gaps of the single-crystalline
material. Previously, the corresponding solidus and liquid-
us temperatures were measured by means of DSC (differen-
tial scanning calorimetry), applying a heating rate of
10 K min
–1 .
As can be seen in Fig. 3 and Table 1, the liquidus tem-
peratures of alloys No. 1 and 4 are very similar, therefore,
it is concluded that the reduction of Mn by 5 wt.% can be
compensated by 3 wt.% of Ti. Relatively high melting
points were measured for braze alloys No. 2 and 3. Surpris-
ingly, the liquidus temperature of alloy No. 3 is slightly
higher than that of alloy No. 2, even though the Ti content
is higher. Furthermore, the melting interval is somewhat
smaller, therefore, the solidus temperature of alloy No. 3 is

To improve the c/c’microstructure, the heat treatment
after the brazing cycle was varied. The corresponding heat
treatment cycles are depicted in Fig. 4. Initially, 1 h solu-
tion annealing at 1 080 8C followed by 2 h precipitation
hardening at 850 8C were selected (HT 1) for all braze al-
loys. In this work longer heat treatment cycles at higher
temperatures were used to provide a complete c’solution:
the temperature for the solution annealing was increased to
1 1408C, which was held for 6 h; afterwards, precipitation
hardening was conducted for 4 h at 1 000 8C, 1 010 8C and
1 0208C, respectively.
2.2. Tensile tests
Tensile tests at 900 8C were carried out on brazed samples,
manufactured from the first generation single-crystalline
nickel-based superalloy PWA 1483. All tensile specimens
were prepared in the same way: plates, as indicated by the
dashed line in Fig. 5, were cut into with the help of a
300 lm wide cutting disc. The plates were filled with braze
alloy No. 1 (Table 1) according to brazing cycle 1 (Fig. 1).
After brazing the excess braze alloy material on the top
and bottom side was ground off and the enhanced heat treat-
ment cycle No. 2 (Fig. 4) was then applied. Finally, the geo-
metry of the tensile samples was eroded. This relatively
complex sample preparation was chosen to provide an opti-
mal filling of the central area of the plates and thus the
cross-section of the tensile samples.
All tensile tests were performed with a hydraulic testing
machine using a crosshead speed of 0.93 mm min
–1 . The
temperature was controlled continuously by a Ni/Cr – Ni-
thermocouple fixed near the braze gap. During the test tem-
perature, load, displacement and strain were recorded and
written to a data file. A high-temperature extensometer with
two ceramic arms held by a constant pressure on the sample
surface was used for the strain measurement. Local strain
measurement within the braze gap could not be realized;
only the global strain, measured over a length of 25 mm,
could be recorded. The testing equipment prior to heating
is depicted in Fig. 6.
2.3. Variation of brazing cycle
As a high amount of porosity within the braze gaps was ob-
served in earlier brazing experiments, the brazing cycle was
modified. Originally, the temperature was lowered from
1 2008C to 1 000 8C within 2 h, whereas it was observed in
earlier brazing experiments that epitaxial solidification
could also be achieved in shorter brazing times [20]. How-
ever, in order to provide better filling of the samples, which
means that pores occurring as a result of shrinking during
solidification have to be filled, longer brazing times are ne-
cessary. It was expected that the formation of pores could
be avoided by an additional hold time above the liquidus
temperature of the braze alloy.
Therefore, further brazing experiments on tensile sam-
ples were conducted by applying braze alloy No. 1 and
brazing cycle 2 (Fig. 7) in combination with the enhanced
heat treatment cycle No. 2 (Fig. 2). During cooling a tem-
perature of 1 150 8C was held constantly for 1 h to allow re-
sidual liquid material to flow into the pores. The tensile
samples brazed according to the new brazing cycle were
tested at room temperature. The fracture surfaces were
compared to those of the high temperature tensile speci-
mens, which had been filled by applying the old brazing
cycle.

3. Results
3.1. Variation of heat treatment
To evaluate the effect of the modified heat treatment, all
samples were analyzed by means of scanning electron mi-
croscopy (SEM). To reveal the c/c’microstructure, molyb-
dic acid etchant was used for metallographic preparation.
Figure 8 shows the microstructures of the samples sub-
jected to heat treatment 1 (Fig. 4, HT 1). All figures show
sections from the center of the brazed gaps.
A microstructure composed of two phases is clear in all
four braze alloys. The brighter precipitates were identified
as c’by means of EDX-analysis (element dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy), however, the typical cubic shape of the c’
precipitates was not observed. Furthermore, the etching be-
havior of the base material and the braze gap is different:
usually, molybdic-acid etchant dissolves the c’ phase,
which is visible in the base material (Fig. 1d). However,
within the brazed gaps, the matrix has been dissolved. Ac-
cording to Thermo-Calc-simulations [22], the amount of c’
increases with higher concentrations of Ti; additionally,
the c’precipitates become coarser, which can also be ob-
served for decreasing amounts of Mn.
Figure 9 shows the microstructures resulting from the en-
hanced heat treatment cycle. Particulary in case of higher
amounts of Ti, the size of the c’precipitates is much smaller
compared to the samples which have been subjected to heat
treatment 1 (Fig. 8). An almost cubic precipitate morphol-
ogy can be observed. Some long pearl-cord-like precipi-
tates are visible which were identified as c’phase. The rea-
son for this precipitate morphology has not yet been
investigated, however, no significant influence on the me-
chanical behavior is expected.
3.2. Tensile tests
Tensile tests were conducted with six samples at 900 8C. An
average tensile strength of 344 MPa was calculated. The
fracture surface of the tensile samples was analyzed by
means of SEM. One half of each sample was mounted in
cross-section for metallographic preparation. These sam-
ples were ground off to their half thickness in order to ex-
amine the center of the fractured gaps. Afterwards, they
were polished and etched with molybdic acid etchant
(Fig. 10).
It can be seen from the fracture surface that a very high
amount of freely solidified surface formed and this oc-
curred in case of all samples tested. Therefore, the effective
cross-section was greatly reduced meaning that the real ten-
sile strength could not be measured, due to the high amount
of porosity. The flat fracture surface indicates relatively
brittle fracture behavior, however, the dimples in the small
fracture surfaces on top of the dendrites (Fig. 10b) suggest
higher ductility. The results of a cross-section polish are
shown in Fig. 10c. From this perspective it is difficult to
distinguish between fracture surface and original dendrite
surface. However, it is assumed that the shape of the surface
is predominantly formed by the original dendrite growth
during solidification, while the amount of real fracture sur-
face is estimated to be relatively small. In each case, the
bonding to the base material was very stable; failure within
the bonding zone was not observed.

3.3. Variation of brazing cycle
To evaluate the influence of the enhanced brazing cycle
(Fig. 7), six further tensile specimens were fabricated ac-
cording to cycle 2 in combination with the longer heat treat-
ment (Fig. 4). First, three samples were tested at room tem-
perature; an average tensile strength of 675 MPa was
measured. One of these fracture surfaces is shown in
Fig. 11. For all three fracture surfaces it was observed that
the additional hold time at 1 150 8C leads to significantly
improved filling of the gaps, however, a small amount of
porosity at the top side is still left. Therefore, to eliminate
all effects of porosity, the upper half of the other three sam-
ples was ground off. Thus, the porous area was completely
removed in order to measure the intrinsic strength of the
braze alloy. In this case, the tensile strength was much high-
er, indicating that the room temperature strength of the
braze alloy is in principal on the level of the base material:
an average tensile strength of 917 MPa was recorded. One
of the corresponding fracture surfaces is shown in Fig. 12.
It can be seen that some scattered pores are still present,
however, the amount of porosity relative to the whole
cross-section is very small.
4. Discussion
In Ref. [20] Ni – Mn-based braze alloys were described
which provide epitaxial solidification in significantly short-
er brazing times compared to conventional TLP- and ADH-
technologies. This offers the opportunity of brazing particu-
larly wide gaps in economically justifiable times without
the formation of brittle secondary phases.
Many authors have investigated diffusion brazing tech-
nologies with B and Si free braze alloys. Dinkel et al. ex-
amined Ge as alternative MPD [24]. PWA 1483 and René
N5 with gaps of 200 lm were used: in case of a complete
epitaxial solidification, tensile strengths in the range of
95 % of PWA 1483 and 92 % of René N5 were achieved.
However, hold times of 48 h at 1 200 8C were necessary
to provide complete epitaxial solidification, due to the
slow diffusion of Ge in Ni. If shorter hold times were ap-
plied, only 40 % of the reference material’s strength were
measured.
Miglietti and Du Toit replaced B and Si by Hf and Zr;
wide-gap samples (1.5 mm) fabricated from the poly-crys-
talline nickel-base material IN 738 were filled using an
ADH-process, where MarM247 powder was used as an ad-
ditive filler material [13]. Very dense joints were produced
within 12 h at 1 238 8C followed by a solution heat treat-
ment (1 232 8C/4 h) and a HIP (hot isostatically pressed)
heat treatment at 1 080 8C and 172.5 MPa for 4 h. The ten-
sile strength of the Zr-containing alloys tested at 870 8C,
reached 94 % of MarM247 (790 MPa); the Hf-containing
alloys reached 68 %. However, if the samples were pre-
pared without the solution heat treatment and without the
HIP cycle, tensile strengths of only 61 % (Zr) and 43 %
(Hf) of MarM247 at 870 8C were achieved.
In contrast to the eutectic systems (Ni – B, Ni – Ge, Ni –
Hf, Ni – Si, Ni – Zr) the system Ni – Mn has the advantage
that Mn is almost completely soluble in Ni (Fig. 13). There-
fore, single phase solidification occurs even in the case of
very fast quenching, no secondary phases can precipitate
during solidification. Furthermore, a very small melting in-
terval exists which provides sufficiently high re-melting
temperatures of the brazed gaps. This is a very important
aspect since most of the Mn remains within the braze gap
after cooling. By application of the Ni – Mn-based systems,
gaps in the range of 300 lm could be closed epitaxially

within only 10 min [20]. This is due to the fact that in con-
trast to the MPDs mentioned above, no diffusion of Mn
has to be awaited. The other MPDs have the problem that
either their solubility in Ni is extremely low (B) or rela-
tively high amounts are necessary for an effective melting
point reduction. Therefore, MPD concentrations above the
solubility are necessary, which means that single phase soli-
dification cannot be provided without diffusion of the
MPD.
Cr-, Al- and Ti-containing systems were described in
Refs. [21, 22]. While in Ref. [20] Si was used as a second-
ary MPD to reduce the amount of Mn, in Refs. [21, 22] Si
was replaced by Ti. In addition to its melting point depres-
sing and c’stabilizing effect in Ni, Ti improves the wett-
ability of the braze alloys, which is also utilized in the case
of active braze alloys for producing ceramic/ceramic- or
ceramic/metal-joints [26, 27]. In contrast to the Ni – Mn-sys-
tem,
Ni – Ti and Ni – Al are eutectic systems. Therefore, one
could expect that the epitaxial solidification is disturbed by
the addition of these elements. However, compared to B, in
both cases the solubility in Ni is much larger. In the Ni – Al-
system c’(Ni3ðAl; TiÞ) precipitates out of c solid-solution.
This is the main reason for the excellent high temperature
properties of nickel-based superalloys [23]. The situation
in the Ni–Ti-system is similar: for higher amounts of Ti,
the precipitation of the TiNi 3 phase is predicted, however,
due to the high solubility of Ti, this occurs at relatively
low temperatures. In contrast to B, single phase solidifica-
tion occurs, therefore, secondary phases, which serve as nu-
cleation sites for stray grains, cannot form during solidifica-
tion; the epitaxial solidification is not disturbed.
Next, the influence of enhanced heat treatment was in-
vestigated. It was found that in contrast to the former heat
treatment a microstructure very similar to the base material
was produced: a large amount of fine, cubic c’precipitates
were observed. The improvement of the microstructure is
due to the fact that the temperature, which was initially se-
lected for the solution annealing, was below the c’solvus
temperature. Therefore, a bi-disperse microstructure con-
sisting of coarse primary c’and fine secondary c’formed.
Since the c’ solvus temperature increases with higher
amounts of Ti and lower amounts of Mn, the primary c’pre-
cipitations become larger from alloy No. 1 to No. 4. As the
temperature for the solution annealing is increased to
1 1408C, c’is completely dissolved in all alloys. The preci-
pitation morphology still becomes coarser in the case of
more Ti and less Mn, however the effect is less pronounced
than before. Shorter hold times would probably also have
been applicable, however, it was seen in earlier experiments
that better homogenization takes place in case of longer
hold times.
In addition to the variation of the heat treatment the braz-
ing cycle was modified. Although the epitaxial solidifica-
tion principally requires only very short brazing times
[20], the relatively high porosity observed in the tensile
tests reveals that an appropriate filling particularly of the
wide gaps is difficult within these short times. Therefore, a
hold time above the solidus temperature of the braze alloy
(1 1508C for alloy No. 1) was attached to the brazing cycle,
resulting in a reduction in porosity. It is concluded that dur-
ing this additional hold time, where a small amount of liq-
uid phase still exists, pores forming during solidification
are closed. As the temperature is lowered, the solidification
starts from the gap faces while a liquid layer enriched with
Mn remains in the center of the braze gap. This residual liq-
uid layer solidifies until its solidus temperature is reached,
accompanied by significant shrinkage induced by the phase
transformation. As a result, porosity occurs predominantly
in the center of the gaps.
It is assumed that this effect is less pronounced in B-con-
taining systems since completely diffusion controlled soli-
dification is expected. After the extremely long hold times
described above, the gaps are completely homogenized
and B-enrichment in the gap center does not occur in those
systems. As described in Refs. [11, 13], HIP’ing is also an
effective method for the avoidance of porosity. However,
since the process is very expensive, in this work achieving
appropriate filling by modifying the brazing cycle was at-
tempted. It has to be noted that in spite of the additional
hold time the Ni – Mn-process is still much faster than the
other processes. It is planned to include one or two addi-
tional hold times at 1 130 8C and 1 110 8C in order to pro-
vide complete filling.
The enhanced heat treatment discussed above not only
influences the microstructure but also the filling of the gaps.
As the temperature for the solution annealing is above the
solidus temperature of the braze alloy (Table 1, No. 1), it
is assumed that partial re-melting of the gaps occurs. This
is predominantly located in the gap center where higher lev-
els of Mn are present. During 6 h hold time at 1 140 8C the
spare braze material on top of the sample also partially liq-
uefies, thus pores can be filled. Additionally, more Mn is
able to diffuse into the surrounding solid material resulting
in better homogenization, so the effect of shrinkage within
the gap center can be minimized. It is presumed that the re-
sidual pore concentration at the top of the samples is caused
by a lack of braze material. Therefore, it is planned for
further experiments to put new braze material on the gaps
prior to the heat treatment.
The evaluation of the high temperature tensile tests re-
vealed very low tensile strength at 900 8C. However, due
to the high porosity, these tests can only be used for a rough
estimation of the internal strength of the braze alloys. As-
suming that only about 50 % of the cross-section was filled,
the measured strength of 344 MPa can be doubled. How-
ever, it has to be noted that this estimate represents the ab-
solute minimum of the real strength, since notching effects
resulting from the pores were not considered. Therefore, it
is expected that the real strength is significantly higher than
about 700 MPa.
This estimate was confirmed by the room temperature
experiments: In the case of the three samples with the pore
concentration on the top side, an average tensile strength
of 675 MPa was recorded, while the ground samples
reached an ultimate tensile strength of 917 MPa, which is
about 83 % of the base material’s strength (PWA 1483:
UTS(RT) & 1 100 MPa). Regarding the ground samples,
the porosity was almost eliminated, however, a small notch-
ing effect resulting from the scattered pores still occurs. Al-
together, it is concluded that the internal strength of the
Ni – Mn-alloys is principally on the level of nickel-based
superalloys. Currently, samples for high temperature tensile
tests, LCF (low cycle fatigue) and stress rupture tests, are
being fabricated. For these samples it is also planned to
grind off the porous areas to measure the intrinsic strength
of the base material.

5. Conclusions
The epitaxial brazing of single-crystalline nickel-based
superalloys with Ni – Mn-based alloys was demonstrated.
In particular the influence of modified heat treatments and
brazing cycles on the microstructure and the filling of the
gaps was investigated. Therefore, enhanced braze alloys
containing Cr, Al and Ti were used. The following conclu-
sions can be drawn:
1. An additional hold time during the brazing cycle, which
is above the solidus temperature of the braze alloy, leads
to improved filling of the gaps. Although the time re-
quired for the epitaxial solidification is extremely short
in the case of the Ni – Mn-based systems, additional
hold times during brazing are necessary in order to pro-
vide appropriate filling of the wide gaps of 300 lm.
2. The addition of an appropriate heat treatment is very im-
portant for the quality of the brazed gaps: on the one
hand the microstructure is improved significantly since
high amounts of very fine and cubic c’precipitates are
produced. On the other hand the heat treatment leads to
better filling of the samples since partial re-melting of
the braze material occurs.
3. Tensile tests were carried out at 900 8C and at room tem-
perature. Due to the high porosity within the samples
low tensile strengths were achieved, however, the ten-
sile samples tested at room temperature, which were
prepared according to the improved brazing cycle, con-
firmed that the internal strength of the braze alloys is
on the level of nickel-based superalloys.
4. Further brazing cycles with additional hold times above
the solidus temperature of the braze alloy will be carried
out. The internal strength of the braze alloys will be in-
vestigated through further mechanical testing.
