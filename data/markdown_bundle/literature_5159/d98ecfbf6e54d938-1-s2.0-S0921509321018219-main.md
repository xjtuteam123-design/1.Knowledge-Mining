ABSTRACT 
Preparing tungsten heavy alloys with high strength and toughness by laser-directed energy deposition (LDED) is 
challenging. Here, we obtained high-performance LDED-fabricated 90W – 7Ni – 3Fe (90W) alloys through two 
optimized post-heat treatments. The results show that the 90W sample achieves a superior combination of the 
ultimate tensile strength (UTS: 821 ± 26 MPa) and ductility (17.46 ± 2.93%) after the high-temperature and 
long-time heat treatment, which are comparable to or even better than those of 90W alloys processed by powder 
metallurgy methods. The superior ductility is attributed to the improvement of the matrix-phase coordinated 
deformability, the enhancement of the W-M interfacial bonding strength, and the involvement of W particles in 
the coordinated deformation during tensile testing. In addition, the cycle-short-time heat treatment can simul -
taneously improve the UTS and elongation of the as-fabricated sample. The significant improvements in UTS 
(834 MPa → 870 MPa) and elongation (3.44% → 6.96%) are mainly related to the increase of W particles 
cleavage, the improvement of W-M interfacial bonding strength, and the reduction of porosity in the micro -
structure. This work provides a theoretical guide for improving the mechanical properties of tungsten alloys 
manufactured by additive manufacturing (AM) technology, which can be beneficial for expanding the applica -
tion of AM technology in the field of tungsten alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Tungsten heavy alloys (WHAs) are typical two-phase composites 
consisting of tungsten particle phase (W) as the main phase (85 – 98 wt% 
W) and other low melting point phases as the binder phase (e.g., Ni, Fe, 
Cu, etc.) [ 1 ]. Because of an outstanding combination of high strength, 
low thermal expansion, excellent ray absorption ability, and thermal 
conductivity, WHAs are extensively applied in the civil, defense, and 
space industries, such as counterweights, radiation shields, kinetic en -
ergy penetrators, and gyroscope rotors [ 2 , 3 ]. Owing to the large dif -
ferences in melting point and density between W particles and the 
binder phases, WHAs are commonly processed using traditional powder 
metallurgy (PM) methods, which primarily include solid-state sintering 
and liquid-phase sintering as forming mechanisms [ 4 , 5 ]. However, 
traditional PM methods also have some shortcomings, such as complex 
processes, poor flexibility, and difficulty in manufacturing complex and 
large-sized parts. 
In recent years, laser-based additive manufacturing (AM) technology 
has shown great potential in the field of material processing due to its 
short processing period, high material utilization, and personalized 
customization [ 6 – 9 ]. For instance, a large number of metal parts, such as 
titanium alloys [ 10 – 12 ], nickel-based alloys [ 13 , 14 ], aluminum-based 
alloys [ 15 , 16 ] have been successfully manufactured using AM technol -
ogy. Currently, there are two main representative technologies for AM 
processes, including laser-directed energy deposition (LDED) and laser 
powder bed fusion (LPBF). For the LDED process, it allows the building 
of large-scale components with high fabrication speed. Moreover, it can 
be used for other applications, such as repairing and re-manufacturing.

Compared to LDED, LPBF is characterized by its high manufacturing 
precision and is usually used for net-forming small and medium-sized 
parts [ 17 ]. A successful LPBF depends on powder spreadability. Poor 
powder spreadability can cause porosity in the LPBF-manufactured 
parts, which consequently deteriorates their mechanical properties [ 18 ]. 
Reviewing previous researches, discussions on utilizing AM tech -
nology to manufacture tungsten alloys are extensive [ 19 – 25 ]. Alja ˇz 
Ivekovi ´c et al. [ 26 ] successfully manufactured high density ( > 95%) 
90W – 7Ni – 3Fe alloy by LPBF, showing high ultimate tensile strength 
(UTS: 872 ± 30 MPa) with elongation less than 1.0%. Li et al. [ 27 ] 
optimized LDED parameters and obtained a near-full density ( ≥ 99%) 
90W – 7Ni – 3Fe sample with UTS of 1121 MPa and poor elongation 
( < 1%). Zhou et al. [ 28 ] fabricated ultra-high strength 50W – Ni – Fe (UTS: 
1120 MPa) and 75W – Ni – Fe (UTS: 1316 MPa) alloys using LDED, and the 
elongation was about 4% and 6%, respectively. Wang et al. [ 29 ] fabri -
cated a dense 90W – 7Ni – 3Fe alloy with UTS of 1037 MPa and elongation 
of 3.5% through LDED. These efforts have shown enormous potential of 
AM technology for manufacturing high-strength tungsten alloys. The 
high strength is attributed to the refined microstructure, the high W 
content in the matrix phase, and the presence of W dendrites. However, 
it is also noteworthy that AM-manufactured WHAs exhibit a common 
deficiency of poor ductility, manifested as elongations of approximately 
5% or less. This fatal weakness restricts the further application of these 
AM-manufactured WHAs, which urgently need further improvements. 
Post-heat treatments are known to be classical optimization methods 
for relieving residual stress, tailoring microstructure and obtaining 
excellent mechanical properties of AM-manufactured parts [ 30 – 32 ]. To 
date, several researchers have investigated the effects of post-heat 
treatments on PM-processed tungsten alloys [ 33 – 35 ]. G. Prabhu et al. 
[ 36 ] obtained high-performance 92W – 5Ni – 3Co alloy through cyclic 
heat treatment (CHT). The CHT-treated samples obtained fine W pre -
cipitates in the matrix phase, and exhibited superior tensile as well as 
impact properties. U. Ravi Kiran et al. [ 37 ] studied the effect of aging 
heat treatment (700 
◦
C for 1 h, 2 h, and 3 h) on 90W – 6Ni – 2Fe – 2Co alloy. 
The heat-treated samples showed lower W – W contiguity, less inter -
granular failure, and higher ductility. Li et al. [ 38 ] achieved excellent 
tensile properties of 97W – 2Ni – 1Fe alloy through heat treatments 
(600 
◦

◦

◦

and 120 min), which is mainly attributed to the enhancement of 
W-matrix interfacial strength and the growth of W grains. For 
PM-processed WHAs, post-heat treatments are performed to: 1) reduce 
W – W contiguity (W – W interfaces are preferential void/crack nucleation 
sites); 2) increase the solid solution of W in the matrix phase (solid so -
lution strengthening); 3) remove hydrogen (sintering is usually per -
formed in hydrogen atmosphere); 4) suppress intermetallics formation 
[ 37 ]. However, for AM-manufactured tungsten alloys, the deposition 
process with domain-by-domain (e.g., point-by-point, line-by-line, and 
layer-by-layer) localized forming characteristics takes place in a small 
melt pool featuring strong convection and ultrafast cooling rate, which is 
markedly different from PM-processed WHAs [ 17 , 39 ]. So far, the 
influencing mechanisms of post-heat treatments on the microstructure 
and mechanical properties of AM-manufactured WHAs maintain to be 
underexplored and wait for further elucidation. To meet the increasing 
requirements of modern industrial components, it is of great theoretical 
and practical significance to explore post-AM heat treatments to achieve 
high-strength and high-toughness WHAs. 
In this work, we explored and established two optimized post-heat 
treatments for LDED-fabricated 90W – 7Ni – 3Fe (90W) alloys. The 
microstructure evolution, corresponding mechanical properties, and 
tensile fracture behavior of LDED-fabricated 90W alloys before and after 
heat treatments were investigated and compared in detail. The purpose 
is to obtain high-performance LDED-fabricated 90W alloys, which can 
reach the level of traditional PM-processed WHAs.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Microstructure 
Fig. 4 shows the typical microstructure of the 90W alloys in as- 
fabricated and heat-treated conditions (HT1 & HT2). As shown in 
Fig. 4 (a, d, g), all of which are composed of subspherical W particles 
(bright phase) and the continuous matrix phase (dark phase). Differ -
ently, the W particles of the heat-treated samples (HT1 & HT2) are more 
closely spaced compared to the as-fabricated sample. Moreover, it is 
further observed that the heat-treated samples (HT1 & HT2) exhibit low 
porosity and smooth-surface W grains, which are significantly different 
from the as-fabricated sample, as shown in Fig. 4 (b, c, f, i). Besides, the 
LDED + HT1 sample shows more W – W interfaces and some irregular 
large-sized W particles (defined as re-grown W), as shown in Fig. 4 (e and 
f). 
More detailed microstructural parameters (W-grains size, W-parti -
cles volume fraction, W – W contiguity) of the as-fabricated and heat- 
treated samples are presented in Fig. 5 (d – f) and listed in Table 3 . It 
can be seen that the LDED sample exhibits a relatively small W-grains 
size of 21.29 μ m, a W-particles volume fraction of 75.13 ± 0.17%, and a 
W – W contiguity of 0.17 ± 0.01. Relative to the LDED sample, the LDED 
+ HT1 sample shows a remarkable increase in W-grains size (28.81 μ m), 
W-particles volume fraction (84.80 ± 0.62%), and W – W contiguity 
(0.33 ± 0.03). However, for the LDED + HT2 sample, the W-particles 
volume fraction (85.82 ± 0.59%) displays a significant increase, but the 
W – W contiguity (0.21 ± 0.01) and W grain size (21.68 μ m) change little. 
Further EBSD analysis was performed to understand the morphology 
and orientation of W grains in the LDED, LDED + HT1 and LDED + HT2 
samples. Fig. 5 (b) shows the coarser W grains structure of the LDED +
HT1 sample, while Fig. 5 (a) and (c) present the finer W grains of the 
LDED and LDED + HT2 samples. Additionally, it is noteworthy that the 
W grains of all three samples are randomly oriented and grow towards 
various crystalline orientation without obvious texture features, which 
is supported by the pole figures in Fig. 6 (a – c). The maximum texture 
distribution densities of W grains in the LDED, LDED + HT1, and LDED 
+ HT2 samples are 2.24, 2.95, and 2.42, respectively, indicating the 
absence of preferred orientation, which is similar to the textures re -
ported in other WHAs in the literature [ 45 , 46 ]. This indicates that the 
two optimized post-heat treatments have no significant effect on W 
grains orientations of the as-fabricated sample. 
3.2. W content in the matrix phase 
The W content in the matrix phase has an important influence on the 
mechanical properties of WHAs. High solid-solution W in the matrix 
phase can have a solid solution strengthening effect on it [ 29 ]. As shown 
in Fig. 7 , 30 points in different areas of each sample were scanned with 
EDS to obtain more accurate values of the W content in the matrix phase. 
It can be found that the LDED sample has the highest W content in the 
matrix phase (30.40 ± 0.40%), followed by the LDED + HT2 sample 
(28.09 ± 0.28%) and the LDED + HT1 sample (22.62 ± 0.58%).

3.4. Mechanical properties 
3.4.1. Nano-hardness 
The hardness values of the matrix phase (M) and individual W par -
ticles (W) of the three samples are shown in Fig. 8 and listed in Table 4 . 
Compared to the LDED samples (W: 7.63 ± 0.18 GPa; M: 6.32 ± 0.13 
GPa), the LDED + HT1 samples shows lower hardness values (W: 6.41 ±
0.35 GPa; M: 5.61 ± 0.06 GPa), while the LDED + HT2 sample (W: 7.46 
± 0.25 GPa; M: 6.21 ± 0.15 GPa) is not significantly different. The 
hardness values of the matrix phase and W particles are mainly related 
to the W content in the matrix phase (solid solution strengthening effect) 
and W-grains size (Hall-Petch relationship). The unique forming char -
acteristics of the LDED process produce small-sized W grains and a 
highly supersaturated solid solution of W in the matrix phase, which 
effectively increases grain refinement strengthening and solid solution 
strengthening, leading to the high hardness values [ 46 ].

3.5. Heat-treated samples: tensile fracture behavior 
It is known that WHAs consist of four fracture modes [ 56 ]: W par -
ticles cleavage (W), W – W inter-particle fracture (W – W), W 
particles-matrix interface separation (W-M), and matrix phase tearing 
(M). During the tensile test, the crack source preferentially generates at 
the W – W interface and extends along the W – W interface, followed by 
plastic deformation of the relatively soft matrix phase, which is domi -
nantly accommodated by dislocation motion [ 57 ]. As the tensile load 
increases, dislocations aggregate at the W-M interface via slipping, 
leading to stress concentration at the W-M interface. If the W-M inter -
facial bonding strength is strong, the load will drive the deformation of 
W particles. And cracks always nucleate at the periphery of W particles 
with relatively small deformation and then extend along the interior of 
W particles to form W particles cleavage. In contrast, the cracks formed 
at the W-M interface will expand and cause the W-M interfacial sepa -
ration [ 58 ]. In general, high strength and ductility can be achieved if 
there is a large fraction of W particles cleavage and matrix phase tearing. 
To further understand the effects of two optimized post-heat treatments 
on the tensile properties of the LDED-fabricated 90W samples, the ten -
sile fractures and the tensile fracture cross-sectional surfaces were 
observed.

fracture form, as indicated by the red arrow in Fig. 10 (f). A large amount

4. Conclusions 
In summary, post-heat treatments can be judiciously used to tailor 
the microstructure and enhance the final mechanical properties of the 
LDED-fabricated WHAs. Different microstructures and various combi -
nations of strength and ductility can be achieved through different heat 
treatments. The high-temperature and long-time heat treatment is a 
highly effective route in improving the ductility of the LDED-fabricated 
WHAs, but it will cause a slight reduction in strength due to W-grains 
coarsening and the precipitation of solid-solution W in the matrix phase. 
In contrast, the cycle-short-time heat treatment shows a considerable 
possibility to obtain high-strength LDED-fabricated WHAs. Surely, 
compared to conventional PM-processed WHAs, the HT2 process re -
quires further optimization (e.g., number of cycles, holding time) to 
achieve a better elongation ( ≥ 10%).
