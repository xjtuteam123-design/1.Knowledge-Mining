Abstract
Conventional high-strength Mg-RE-based wrought alloys usually contain a high amount of RE solutes, which largely 
increases the alloy cost and thus restricts their adoptions. In this work, we developed a low-RE-containing Mg-3Sm-1Nd-
0.6Zn-0.4Zr alloy by hot extrusion with low extrusion ratio, which shows a high tensile yield strength (TYS) of 435 MPa 
and a satisfactory elongation of 5.6% at room temperature, outperforming most Mg-Gd-Y-based extrusion alloys with RE 
contents of 12 wt% at least. Outstanding high-temperature strength, such as the TYS of 280 MPa at 200 °C and 251 MPa 
at 250 °C, is also obtained in this alloy. The alloy presented a typical bimodal grain structure including coarse hot-worked 
grains with a strong texture and fine recrystallized grains with random orientations. Also, abundant Mg3RE particles were 
mostly introduced in hot-worked grains and at recrystallized grain boundaries by dynamic precipitation during extrusion. 
Consequently, the high strength of this alloy is principally attributed to the combined hardening effect of numerous Mg3RE 
particles, fine recrystallized grains, and strongly textural hot-worked grains, rather than the ultra-strong age-hardening effect 
in traditional high RE-alloyed Mg alloys.
Keyword High strength; Mg-RE alloys; Dynamic precipitation; Heat resistance
1 Introduction
Mg alloys have a huge potential in the fields of aerospace, 
automobile, and electronics owing to the lightest structural 
metallic materials. In contrast to counterpart Al alloys, 
however, the absolute strength of Mg alloys is not enough 
so that their adoption in terms of structural components is 
very limited [1–3]. Considerable efforts have been executed 
to develop high-strength Mg alloys in the last decades. And 
numerous studies show that Mg-RE (RE = rare earth) alloys 
are the most promising high-strength Mg alloy systems 
mostly due to their potential for ultra-strong precipitation 
hardening [4 –7]. For example, a coarse-grained Mg-13Gd 
(wt%) binary alloy exhibited an ultrahigh tensile yield 
strength (TYS) of ~ 458 MPa after conventional forging 
coupled with ageing, in which ageing-induced TYS 
increment is over 170 MPa [5 ]. Yu et al. [6 ] developed a 
high-strength Mg-11.7Gd-4.9Y-0.3Zr extrusion alloy with 
a TYS of ~ 500 MPa, which basically exceeds all Mg-RE 
alloys prepared by traditional extrusions. Li et  al. [7 ] 
used hot extrusion, warm-rolling and ageing fabricated 
a Mg-15Gd alloy with a TYS of ~ 504 MPa. The alloy’s 
strengthening derives from amounts of dynamical and ageing 
precipitates, fine recrystallized grain, and stacking faults. It 
is remarkable that these high-strength Mg alloys normally 
contain large amounts of heavy RE solutes characterized 
by a high solid solution in Mg. The high concentration of 
heavy RE elements in Mg can produce a high density of

The development of high-strength Mg-RE alloys with 
low-content REs is extremely challenging because a decrease 
in RE amounts largely cuts down the age-hardening response 
which is the predominant strengthening component for them 
[4]. Although there were few studies on low-RE-based Mg 
alloys, recent works have shown that Sm as one of the light 
REs has a significant strengthening function in Mg [2 , 
8–10]. During low-temperature extrusion, large amounts of 
dynamic precipitations are readily occurred in Mg-Sm-based 
alloys due to Sm having high diffusivity and low solubility in 
Mg [2, 9]. As a result, Mg-Sm-based alloys can achieve high 
strength by a high level of dynamic precipitation hardening, 
instead of the strong age-hardening effect dominated in those 
Mg alloys with lots of heavy REs [1, 5]. These results indi-
cate that adjusting dynamic precipitation and texture enables 
high strength in low RE content of Mg alloys. For instance, 
Zhang et al. [9] reported ultra-strong Mg-3.5Sm-2Yb-0.6Zn-
0.4Zr extrusion alloy, with the TYS of near 450 MPa outper-
forming most high-RE-alloyed Mg-RE alloys, in which both 
strong texture and abundant dynamic precipitations account 
for high strength. High-strength Mg-4Sm-0.6Zn-0.4Zr alloy 
extruded at low temperature obtains TYS of 458 MPa after 
ageing, while the elongation is so limited [2 ]. Nd-like Sm 
is also a light RE element, and Nd-containing Mg alloys 
have been verified to enable high strength over 350 MPa 
by conventional extrusion. As an example, the extruded 
Mg-3Nd-0.3Zn-0.5Zr alloy exhibits a superior strength-
ductility balance, with TYS of 360 MPa and elongation of 
14.6% [11]. At present, most Nd-containing Mg alloys usu-
ally act as cast Mg alloys at present, such as Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr 
(ZM6) and Mg-Y-Nd-Zr (WE43 and WE54), whereas a few 
studies focus on the associated wrought alloys [4 ]. Finally, 
Zn is often adopted as micro-alloying elements in Mg-RE 
alloys to enhance substantially ageing hardening response 
and improve the ductility [1, 2, 9].
In this work, a low-RE-alloyed Mg-3Sm-1Nd-0.6Zn-
0.4Zr alloy was designed, in which trace Zn is to modify 
precipitates and Zr can refine as-cast matrix. Co-addition of 
Sm and Nd with similar properties aims to increase dynamic 
precipitation during extrusion since various RE elements in 
Mg can reduce the solubility of each other to alter precipita-
tion kinetics [4]. Hot extrusion with a small extrusion ratio 
was adopted to construct a high proportion of highly tex-
tural hot-worked grains, which contributes to strong texture-
hardening effect in this alloy. As expected, as-extrusion alloy 
obtains a high TYS of 435 MPa and an acceptable ductility 
of 5.6% after ageing. Thus, the aim of this work is to explore 
the underlying mechanisms responsible for high strength. 
The strength at room and high temperatures is also compared 
with other typical Mg-RE extrusion alloys, which allows for 
a better insight into the high strength in this alloy.
2 Experimental
The ingots of Mg-3Sm-1Nd-0.6Zn-0.4Zr alloy with 90 mm 
in diameter were produced by semi-continuous casting. Its 
practical composition was measured to be Mg-3.42Sm-
0.87Nd-0.62Zn-0.28Zr (in wt%) by an inductivity coupled 
plasma atomic emission spectroscopy. The ingots were 
homogenized at 520 °C for 12 h and then quenched into 
hot water of ~ 80 °C. As-homogenized ingots were machined 
into cylindrical bars with a diameter of 80 mm and a height 
of 100 nm. Hot extrusion was made at 320 °C with an extru-
sion ratio of ~ 6:1 at a ram speed of 0.3 mm/s. Before extru-
sion, the cylindrical bars were preheated at 320 °C for 1 h. 
After extrusion, the extruded bars were immediately cooled 
to water. Finally, some extruded bars were aged at 200 °C 
for 35 h for further increasing the strength.
Tensile properties of both as-extruded and as-aged alloys 
were evaluated using a Shimadzu AG–X plus test machine. 
Tensile sample has a diameter of 5 mm and a gauge length 
of 30 mm. The initial strain rate is set to 0.001/s, and at 
least three samples are tested for each test. The axis of 
tensile samples is parallel to the extrusion direction (ED). 
High-temperature tension tests for as-aged samples were 
carried out at 200 °C and 250 °C, and the samples were pre-
heated at tensile temperature for 20 min before tension. The 
microstructure characterization was carried out by an opti-
cal microscope (OM Olympus GX71), a scanning electron 
microscope (SEM, FEI Nova 400) equipped with electron 
backscattered diffraction (EBSD) detector, and a transmis-
sion electron microscopy (TEM, Philips CM20). OM sam-
ples were etched in a solution of 97 ml ethanol and 3 ml 
nitric acid before observation. TEM samples were prepared 
by an ion beam thinner (Gatan 691) thinning foils with 3 mm 
in diameter and 30–50 μm in thickness (parameters: 4.6 kV, 
90 min, liquid nitrogen cooling). EBSD samples were pre-
pared by mechanical grinding and Argon ion polishing, and 
the EBSD data were analysed using Aztec Crystal software. 
The texture was analysed by an Oxford HKL Channel 5 elec-
tron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) detector equipping 
in a FEI Nova 400 SEM operating at 40 kV along with the 
measured step of 0.15 μm.

3.2 Grain Structure and Texture
Figure 2a shows an OM image of as-homogenized sample. 
The average grain size of as-homogenized sample is ~ 50 μm, 
and a few undissolved coarse compounds are still retained 
at grain boundaries. Figure  2b shows an OM image of 
as-extruded sample, revealing that dynamic recrystallization 
partly occurred during hot extrusion. Thus, the bimodal 
grain structure was generated, which is composed of 
coarse hot-worked grains elongated parallel to the ED and 
fine recrystallized grains. The area fraction of dynamic 
recrystallization was estimated to be only ~ 30%, which is 
possibly related to a relatively low extrusion temperature 
and a small extrusion ratio [12]. Figure  2c shows the high-
magnification inverse pole figure (IPF) map, where the 
bimodal grain structure is clearly presented. It is seen that 
recrystallized grains are basically equiaxed and have a mean 
average grain size of ~ 1 μm. The grain orientation spread 
(GOS) map (Fig.  2d) reflects the level of lattice distortion 
with grains, and a larger GOS value means severe lattice 
distortion and high dislocation density. Significantly, 
recrystallized grains have low GOS values, whereas 
dramatical lattice distortion occurred in hot-worked grains 
due to high GOS values [13].
According to GOS values, IPFs of recrystallized and hot-
worked regions were separated to explore the texture feature, 
as indicated in Fig. 2e-g1. As-extruded sample has a typical 
basal fibre texture of the < 10–10 > direction of most grains 
parallel to the ED (Fig. 2e and e1). Hot-worked grains only 
have a single texture component of < 10–10 > //ED; thus, its 
texture intensity is also the largest (Fig.  2f and f1). Conse-
quently, the texture of as-extruded sample is overwhelmingly 
controlled by a high fraction of hot-worked grains. On the 
contrary, recrystallized grains have random orientations with 
a rather low texture intensity (Fig. 2g and g1). The multiple 
texture components are observed, such as non-basal texture 
of < 0001 > //ED and < 10–11 > //ED, except for conven-
tional < 10–10 > //ED. Note that the formation of non-basal 
texture components has been reported to be linked with RE 
solutes, which can cut down basal texture intensity [14]. In 
this work, however, the weakening response of recrystal-
lization textures is not noticeable, mainly owing to a low 
recrystallization fraction.
Texture can influence the deformation modes of Mg alloy; 
thus, the Schmid factor (SF) of different slip systems is 
calculated, as shown in Fig. 3. The average SF value for basal 
slip systems is very low, ~ 0.18 (Fig.  3a and d), suggesting 
that basal < a > slips are in hard orientation along the ED. 
While the average SF value of prismatic < a > (Fig. 3b and e) 
and pyramidal < c + a > slips (Fig. 3c and f) is larger than that 
of basal < a > slips, the plastic deformation of as-extruded 
alloy could be dominated by prismatic < a > slip due to the 
largest SF value as the loading direction is parallel to the 
ED. Considering the higher critical resolved shear stress 
(CRSS) of non-basal systems than basal slip, a strong texture

3.3 Deformation‑Induced Dynamic Precipitation
Figure 4a shows a backscattered SEM image of as-extruded 
sample, in which large amounts of particles are observed 
in the matrix. There are several coarse particle bands 
distributed along the ED, which should be evolved from 
undissolved coarse compounds that were crushed down 
during hot extrusion [6 ]. High-magnification SEM image 
(Fig. 4b) indicates that a high density of fine particles was 
formed in hot-worked grains. Further TEM images (Fig. 4c) 
illustrate that fine particles also existed in recrystallized 
grains, but their density is lower compared to hot-worked 
grains. During hot extrusion, dynamic precipitation 
inevitably occurred due to high temperature and high 
strain, so these fine particles in the matrix were produced 
by dynamic precipitation [ 15]. These fine particles in the 
matrix have an average size of ~ 60 nm. In addition, a lot 
of residual dislocations were retained in hot-worked grains 
(Fig.  4d), but almost not in recrystallized grains because 
dynamic recrystallization nucleation consumes strain-
induced dislocations [9].
Figure  5 shows bright-field TEM images along with 
the associated selected area electron diffraction (SAED) 
patterns of some typical particles. Firstly, several coarse 
particles with a size of over 1 μm are observed and were 
confirmed as the Mg41RE5 phase with a body-centre 
tetragonal structure (a = 1.477  nm and c = 1.032  nm 
[8]), according to the SAED pattern (Fig.  5a), which 
should originate from the undissolved secondary phases 
in as-homogenized sample. It is noticed that many fine 
recrystallized grains marked by red circles are found 
around such coarse particles. Generally, coarse particles 
larger than 1 μm can facilitate the dynamic recrystallization 
via the particle-stimulated nucleation (PSN) mechanism 
during extrusion, which also favours the formation of the 
randomized texture [1, 6]. Then, there are some submicron 
particles with a size of ~ 200 − 400 nm, and they are 
identified as the Mg3RE phase with a face-centred cubic 
structure (a = 0.73 nm [2 ]) based on the SAED pattern 
analysis (Fig.  5b). During the extrusion, the undissolved 
phases were broken and partial fragments of them evolved 
into the isolated particles. The introduction of dislocations 
in these isolated particles boosts the diffusion of solutes, 
leading to them being thermally unstable; thereby, their 
sharp boundary becomes smooth by the spheroidization 
response [6 ]. As a result, due to the solute diffusion and 
deformation, the isolated fragments eventually evolve 
into the smooth boundary of Mg3RE particles. Finally, 
some fine particles precipitated at grain boundaries still

3.4 Sub‑Grain Boundary and Residual Dislocation
Figure 7a shows the grain boundary misorientation maps. 
It is significant that hot-worked grains would be composed 
of a lot of low angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) with the 
misorientation angle range of 2º − 15º, whereas a few 
LAGBs have existed in recrystallized grains. Bright-field 
TEM images in Fig.  7b and c show a high density of sub-
grain lamellae with a thickness of ~ 150 − 350 nm in hot-
worked grains, and their boundaries are parallel to the 
basal plane trace. It should be noted that the nucleation of 
dynamic recrystallization at grain boundary and interior 
occurred at roughly the same time [18]. Deformation-

to assemble the LAGBs for sustaining system energy 
equilibrium. However, the grain boundary can offer a larger 
driving force for the transition of LAGBs to HAGBs (high 
angle grain boundaries) compared to grain interior; thus, 
dynamic recrystallization is preferentially produced at grain 
boundaries [18]. As illustrated in Fig.  7c, some dynamic 
precipitates were precipitated at LAGBs, which would make 
LAGBs keep low misorientations by blocking them to abort

g = [10, 11] and g = [0002], but their contrast is slightly

these observed dislocations principally are < c + a > -type,

favours ductility improvement.

microstructure of the peak-aged sample. It is shown that 
recrystallized grains exhibit a good heat stability, almost 
no grain growth after peak ageing (Fig.  8a), which should 
be attributed to the substantial dynamic precipitates at 
grain boundaries [4 ]. In addition, there is also no obvious 
precipitate growth in both recrystallized and hot-worked 
regions after ageing, indicating that Mg3RE phases have 
the high thermal resistance (Fig.  8a and b). In addition,

However, those mobile < c + a > dislocations primordially

Further ageing enhances the strength of as-extruded alloy, 
and the ageing-induced increment in TYS is 45 MPa. It must 
be pointed out that the yield strength increment in our alloy 
is much lower than that of those high RE-containing Mg 
alloys (RE > 12 wt%), such as > 100 MPa in Mg-Gd-based 
alloys [5, 6]. This is mainly due to a low density of precipi-
tates formed in grains during ageing, since the present alloy 
has a low RE content and during extrusion dynamic precipi-
tation consumes large amounts of solutes. As expected, low-
density precipitates are observed in as-aged matrix (Fig. 8c). 
These precipitates have ~ 200 − 500 nm in length and less 
than 1 nm in thickness. The SAED pattern shows the weak 
contrast of streaks along the c -axis of the Mg matrix, with 
a strong indicator of the formation of basal precipitates 
(Fig.  8d). Such the diffraction character was observed in 
those aged Mg-Sm-Zn [ 10] and Mg-Gd-Zn [24] alloys, 
which is often induced by basal γ-series (mainly γ’’ and γ’) 
precipitates. Nie et al. [24] reported that γ’ phase often has 
a large aspect ratio of over ~ 200:1; thus, most precipitates 
are γ’ phase. Figure  8e is the HRTEM image of γ’ phase, 
exhibiting that the γ’ phase is coherent with the Mg matrix. 
In addition, the ordered Guinier–Preston (GP) zones could 
be formed in as-aged matrix, as evidenced by the HRTEM 
image along with FFT patterns (Fig.  8f). It is observed that 
such an ordered GP zone has a monolayer structure on the 
basal plane and is coherent with the Mg matrix, in agree-
ment with previous reports [25]. It is worth noting that the 
hardening effect of basal precipitates in Mg-RE alloys is 
limited, compared to prismatic precipitates, which should 
be responsible for the low yield increments of as-extruded

4 Discussion
4.1 Dynamic Precipitation
As previously mentioned, as-extruded alloy displays a 
bimodal grain structure consisting of fine recrystallized 
grains and coarse hot-worked grains. A high density of 
dynamic precipitates is uniformly distributed in hot-worked 
grains, while only a few precipitates are randomly formed in 
recrystallized grains. This phenomenon may be attributed to 
differences in precipitation kinetics [15]. It has been shown 
that during extrusion large amounts of strain-induced defects 
such as dislocations and vacancies would be produced pref-
erentially in the mantle zone of initial grains, which offers 
the effective driving force for dynamic recrystallization and 
dynamic precipitation [ 26]. Thus, the combined reaction 
of dynamic precipitation and recrystallization near grain 
boundaries is generated, which leads to nearly dislocation-
free new grains and fine precipitates at new grain bounda-
ries. Note that two processes are competitive because the 
nucleation and growth of dynamic recrystallization con-
sumed stored energies which is also necessary for dynamic 
precipitation [2 ]. The transition process from LAGBs to 
HAGBs and absorbing dislocation towards the boundary is 
also accompanied by the solute redistribution, which gives 
rise to solute segregation towards grain boundaries [5 , 14]. 
Eventually, an increasing number of solutes would accumu-
late at around grain boundaries so that dynamic precipitation 
of Mg3RE phases occurred here. These fine Mg3RE precipi-
tates residing at grain boundaries will prevent the formation 
of lattice curvature necessary for nucleation and suppress 
the growth of new recrystallized grains, which is crucial in 
refining grains [16]. In such circumstances, a few dynamic 
precipitations occurred in recrystallized grains due to a low 
concentration of solutes, even though more solute diffusion 
as extrusion continues.
In contrast, abundant dynamic precipitation occurred 
in the coarse hot-worked grains during hot extrusion. The 
precipitation distribution was basically homogeneous. 
Hot-worked regions are primarily derived from those ini-
tial grains with a hard orientation in which the activation 
of dislocation slips is more difficult than that in the soft 
orientation of grains, resulting in stored dislocations in hot-
worked regions [18]. As for small extrusion ratio and low 
temperature in this work, the accumulated dislocations in 
hot-worked grains are too limited to enable dynamic recrys-
tallization, and thus they rearrange to configure LAGBs or 
isolated dislocation segments. It is known that strain-induced 
dislocations can act as heterogeneous nucleation sites via 
reducing the barriers for the precipitate nucleation, thereby 
boosting the diffusion kinetics for the precipitate growth [15, 
26]. This situation is analogous to pre-deformation prior to 
ageing to introduce a mass of dislocations which increases 
the nucleation density of precipitates and thus motivates 
the plentiful precipitation [4 ]. As a result, large amounts of 
 Mg3RE phases were formed in hot-worked grains by strain-
induced dynamic precipitation.
4.2 Responsible Mechanisms for the High Strength
The above-mentioned microstructure characterization 
indicated that the high strength displayed by the present 
alloys is mostly associated with the following four aspects: 
(i) abundant Mg3RE precipitates, (ii) fine recrystallized 
grains, (iii) strong texture, and (iii) high-density dislocations. 
Though texture can strengthen the alloy, it is generally not 
considered as an independent hardening mechanism. A 
higher intensity of basal texture provides more strengthening 
effect by boosting the efficiency of other strengthening 
mechanisms such as grain-boundary and dislocation 
strengthening [19]. Hereinafter, the major contributions to 
the yield strength of alloys will be quantified.

uted in hot-worked grains of as-extruded alloys, which is 
expected to produce a large precipitation strengthening

4.3 Comparison with other Mg‑RE Extrusion Alloys
In order to compare the strength of this alloy with some 
typical Mg-RE extrusion alloys reported previously [31–37], 
their mechanical properties are summarized in Table 1. It is 
apparent that the present alloy exhibits a higher TYS than 
that of most high-RE-alloyed Mg extrusion alloys with 
RE content of 12 wt% at least, such as only ~ 373 MPa in 
a T5-treated Mg-11Gd-4.5Y-1Nd-1.5Zn-0.5Zr alloy [34], 
although this alloy only contains 4 wt% RE solutes. This 
may be attributed to the microstructure difference which 
can be determined by extrusion process and the nature of 
RE solute. Both Sm and Nd have a low solubility in the 
Mg matrix; thus, they can easily create Mg-RE phases by 
dynamic precipitation during hot extrusion [26]. And low-
temperature extrusion lowers the atomic diffusivity and thus 
reduces the rate of precipitate growth [15]. In contrast, both 
Gd and Y have a high solubility in the Mg matrix, and a 
higher extrusion temperature of over 350 °C is required for 
those high-RE-alloyed Mg-Gd-Y-based alloys [1 , 4, 6, 7]. 
Therefore, our alloy can obtain a stronger hardening effect of 
dynamic precipitation. In addition, both lower temperature 
and small extrusion ratios facilitate the formation of strong 
deformation texture and fine recrystallized grains [12]. As a 
result, the higher TYS displayed in our alloy than high-RE-
alloyed Mg-Gd-Y-based alloys is mainly attributed to more 
dynamic precipitations, stronger texture, and finer recrystal-
lized grains.

In terms of high-temperature strength, our alloy is 
comparable to partial high-RE-alloyed Mg-RE-based 
alloys, such as the traditional WE54 alloy [31], the long-
period stacking ordered (LPSO) structure hardened 
 Mg97 Y2Cu 1 alloy [32], and homogeneous-grained 
Mg-8Gd-2Y-1Nd-0.3Zn-0.6Zr alloy [36]. It is known that 
the high-temperature strength of Mg-RE alloys heavily 
depends on the RE concentration, which is mainly because 
[17]: (a) RE solutes are of a stronger tendency to develop 
local clusters in the Mg matrix, which can induce the stable 
solute hardening effect at high temperatures; (b) Mg-RE 
phases often are thermally stable, and thus increasing 
Mg-RE phases enhances the high-temperature strength; 
and (c) partial RE ( i.e. Y, Gd, Er, Dy, etc.) elements 
coupled with Zn/Cu/Co form the long-period stacking 
ordered (LPSO) structure which is highly thermostable. 
For this, we ascribe the superior high-temperature strength 
in this alloy to high-density dynamic precipitations of 
 Mg3RE phases with thermal stability [33]. In addition, the 
grain structure could affect the high-temperature strength. 
Fully recrystallized Mg-10Gd-3Y-0.5Zr alloy has a TYS 
of only 190 MPa at 250 °C, much less than 251 MPa in 
our alloy, although it contains a high density of prismatic 
precipitates and profuse RE solutes in the matrix [37]. 
Consequently, constructing bimodal grain structure is also 
crucial for enhancing high-temperature strength in Mg-RE 
alloys, as evidenced by Su et al. report [38].
5 Conclusion
In summary, a high-strength Mg-3Sm-1Nd-0.6Zn-0.4Zr 
alloy was processed by a hot extrusion with a small extru-
sion ratio, which exhibits a high TYS of 435 MPa and an 
acceptable elongation of 5.6%. And this alloy also achieves 
superior high-temperature strength, such as the TYS of 
280 MPa at 200 °C and 251 MPa at 250 °C, outperform-
ing most high-RE-alloyed Mg-RE-based alloys. The alloy 
characterizes a typical bimodal grain structure consisting 
of coarse hot-worked grains with a strong texture and fine 
recrystallized grains with random orientations. A high 
density of Mg3RE phases was dynamically precipitated in 
the Mg matrix during extrusion, mostly distributed in hot-
worked grains, thereby producing a strong precipitation 
hardening response. The precipitation of Mg3RE phases 
at grain boundaries favours the formation of fine grains 
with a mean size of ~ 1 μm via the pinning effect, which 
endows the substantial refinement strengthening. After 
further ageing, basal γ-series phases were introduced in 
the matrix, but their number density is very limited due to 
low content of RE solutes. As a result, the high strength 
in this alloy is primarily associated with the bimodal grain 
structure involving fine grain and strong texture as well as 
with the large amounts of dynamic precipitates, instead of 
age-hardening in conventional high RE-alloyed Mg alloys. 
These results provide an important reference for develop-
ing high-strength Mg alloys with low RE contents.
