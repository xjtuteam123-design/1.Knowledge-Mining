In this work, the nanometer-scale mass ﬂow coupled to dislocation processes near the γ/ γ′ -interface 
during high temperature and low stress creep of a model Ni-base single crystal superalloy is investigated. 
In the early creep stages, the dislocation networks in the γ-phase at γ/ γ′ -interfaces attract γ-stabilizing 
elements like Cr, Co and in particular Re, resulting in compositional gradients close to the interface. At 
larger strains, where dislocations frequently cut into the γ′ -phase, this local interfacial enrichment in 
these elements is no longer observed. The cutting dislocations take part of the segregated atoms away, 
whilst the remaining atoms are released and diffuse back into the γ-channels.

Rafting and the subsequent topological inversion, are well- 
known microstructural instabilities which occur during high tem- 
perature and low stress (T > 900 °C, σ < 300 MPa) [001] tensile 
creep of Ni-base single crystal superalloys (SXs) [1] . The microme- 
chanics, thermodynamics and kinetics of rafting have been exten- 
sively studied in the past decades [2–25] . Pollock et al. [2] sug- 
gested that mass ﬂow is essential and mainly involves crossed 
ﬂuxes of γ′ -stabilizing elements (e.g., Al, Ti, Ta, Ni) from the hori- 
zontal channels to the vertical channels and of the γ matrix com- 
ponents (e.g., Cr, Co, W) in the opposite direction. Carry et al. and 
Zhu et al. [3,4] pointed out that this solute transport can be re- 
lated to the climb of the γ-channel network dislocations close to 
the γ/ γ′ -interfaces. Kolbe et al. [5] concluded and proposed three 
mechanisms regarding the role of interfacial dislocations during 
rafting: (i) They provide pipe diffusion paths from one γ′ -surface 
to another; (ii) their stress ﬁelds can affect local thermodynamic 
potentials and thus the driving force for rafting; and (iii) dislo- 
cation climb is associated with the transport of solute elements. 
These conclusions are in good agreement with an earlier ﬁnding 
[22] where it was shown that the dissolution rate of the γ′ -phase 
is proportional to the density of dislocations in the interfacial dis- 
location networks.

However, so far no hard evidence for such dislocation- 
associated diffusion process was provided. Not until recently, by 
using state-of-the-art atom probe tomography (APT), Kontis et al. 
[26] and Wu et al. [27,28] characterized solute decoration at mul- 
tiple dislocations (including the interfacial ones) in γ′ in crept Ni- 
base SXs. They showed that these dislocations carry γ-stabilizing 
solutes into γ′ . Yet, how local atomic mass ﬂow is associated with 
the evolution of interfacial dislocation networks during creep re- 
mains an open question. 
The present work aims at clarifying this point by studying 
atomic-scale mass ﬂow near the γ/ γ′ -interfaces at different stages 
of creep and of the associated microstructural evolution. Atom 
probe tomography (APT) was employed to study the local solute 
concentrations across γ/ γ′ -interfaces. Our results clearly reveal the 
pivotal role of interfacial dislocations under the applied high tem- 
perature low stress creep condition. They ﬁrst account for an en- 
richment of atoms at the interfacial dislocations which later fades 
out because these cut into the γ′ -phase and drag part of the seg- 
regated solutes along with them, whilst those left behind back dif- 
fuse into the γ channels. This ﬁnding suggests that the strength- 
ening effect of Re through interaction with interfacial dislocations 
changes greatly as creep strain accumulates.

It has been shown that in the early stages of low tempera- 
ture and high stress creep regular dislocation networks form [14] . 
The dynamic reconstruction of such networks in the γ-channels 
at γ/ γ′ -interfaces is promoted by glide of dislocation segments 
through extended γ-channels [5,14,32] , and is completed at the 
minimum creep rate [14] . It seems reasonable to assume that these 
regular, low-energy dislocation arrays attract Re in order to mini- 
mize [38] the overall strain energy. This is in good agreement with 
results from Buﬃere et al. [24,25] who suggested that elements 
like Cr and Co, with a tendency to partition to the γ-channels, 
are attracted to the interface dislocations during rafting. Here, after 
0.9% creep strain, we observe Co, Cr and Re enrichment at the in- 
terface which is compensated by diffusion of Al and Ni away from 
the interface. The strong Re enrichment in the close vicinity of the 
γ/ γ′ -interface in the early stages of creep can be rationalized on 
the basis of the presence of an interfacial dislocation network. And 
while network dislocations are not static, the low creep rate near 
the minimum suggests that their movements are slow and Re in 
the atmosphere can follow them. This is in agreement with recent 
results showcasing that Re segregates to interface misﬁt disloca- 
tions under a load-free high-temperature aging condition [38] . 
In the later stages of high temperature and low stress creep, the 
ongoing formation of the dislocation networks is hampered as the 
long γ-channels vanish and the microstructure becomes more ir- 
regular, leading to topological inversion. It has also been shown on 
several occasions as well as in the present work in Figs. 1 e and f, 
that dislocation segments from dislocation networks can cut into 
the γ′ -phase. These high-temperature cutting processes start to 
signiﬁcantly contribute to the overall creep process after the mini- 
mum creep rate [32,39,40] . Most importantly they directly result in 
a decrease in the number of dislocations in the interface networks. 
As a consequence, solutes trapped at the interface, as observed in 
the 0.9% strained state, lose their loci for segregation and hence 
diffuse back into the channel, decreasing segregation levels in the 
interface’s vicinity. This explains why Re only segregates at the in- 
terfacial grooves (which form because of the presence of interface 
dislocations [41] ) after 23.3% creep strain, as shown in Supplemen- 
tary Fig. S1. 
Moreover, cutting dislocations can drag Re atoms along with 
them into the γ′ -phase, where equilibrium Re-levels increase 
with increasing temperatures [30] . For low temperatures and high 
stresses, Wu et al. [27,28] suggested that the cutting dislocations 
drag solutes from the γ-channel interface regions into the γ’- 
phase. It seems reasonable to assume that a similar effect occurs 
at higher temperatures and lower stresses, where the nature of 
the cutting process results in the formation of a [001] super dis- 
location (two super partials with different a/2 < 110 > Burgers vec- 
tor shear the γ′ -phase in a combined process of glide and climb 
[39,40,42] ). This suggestion is supported by the APT results pre- 
sented in Fig. 3 , from the specimen deformed to 23.3% strain. 
Fig. 3 a shows Ni (green) within an APT reconstruction compris- 
ing only the γ′ -phase. A golden iso-composition surface highlights 
regions containing more than 5 at.% Cr, thereby revealing segre- 
gation to defects based on previous results [27,33] . Figs. 3 b and c 
present close-up views of the structure from two viewing direc- 
tions. The observed features (of linear and planar nature) can be 
attributed to the super partial and the associated faulted region. 
Fig. 3 d plots the corresponding 2D elemental maps of Cr, Re, Co, 
and W, while Fig. 3 e and f show the 1D composition proﬁles mea- 
sured along the arrows labelled e and f in Fig. 3 b. The 2D maps as 
well as the 1D proﬁles show similar depletions of Ni (by ~7 at.%) 
and Al (by ~5 at.%) as well as enrichments in Co (by ~7 at.%), Cr 
(by ~8 at.%) and Re (by ~1 at.%) compared to the surrounding γ′ - 
phase. Although more detailed TEM observations are needed to re- 
veal the full nature of the super dislocation in the γ′ -phase, these 
results suggest that the γ′ -dislocation which was observed after 
23.3% creep strain features more than 10 at.% Cr, 14 at.% Co and 
1 at.% Re in its atmosphere. 
To our knowledge, this atomic-scale mass ﬂow has not been 
reported before. It holds particularly for Re, which is one of the 
most expensive and crucial alloying elements for Ni-base superal- 
loys. During the early stages of creep, Re is attracted to the in- 
terfacial dislocation networks and thus strengthens the material 
through (i) stabilizing the network conﬁguration against disloca- 
tion cutting, and by (ii) blocking pipe diffusion paths thus slowing 
down rafting kinetics. However, in the later stages of creep, where 
γ′ -phase cutting frequently occurs, those trapped Re atoms accom- 
pany the cutting dislocations into the γ′ -phase or they diffuse back 
into the γ-channel to establish chemical equilibrium. This explains 
the loss of interface Re atoms in the later stages of creep, after the 
global creep rate minimum. In the long run, Re cannot impede the 
microstructural degradation towards topological inversion. 
In summary, we show that the atomic-scale mass ﬂow near the 
γ/ γ′ -interfaces during high temperature creep of a model Ni-base 
SX ERBO1 changes during creep, as sketched in Fig. 4 . Before 0.9% 
strain, channel dislocations glide through the extended γ-channels 
and deposit segments at the γ/ γ′ -interfaces, Fig. 4 a. This results 
in the formation of networks which also contribute to an energet- 
ically favorable scenario by decreasing the overall strain energy in 
view of the coherency stresses associated with the misﬁt. For Cr, 
Co and Re, segments of these interface dislocation networks rep- 
resent energetically attractive segregation sites. As they segregate 
to the networks, concentration gradients build up, which were de- 
tected in the present work after 0.9% strain. In particular, higher 
Re compositions are observed close to the γ/ γ′ -interfaces. Fig. 4 b 
schematically illustrates, how Re atoms decorate the segments of 
an interface dislocation network in the creep stages where regu- 
lar networks evolve. While this is not unexpected, why and when

they move back into the channel. The evolution of the nature of 
this nano-scale ﬂuxes during creep has so far not been reported in 
the literatures.
