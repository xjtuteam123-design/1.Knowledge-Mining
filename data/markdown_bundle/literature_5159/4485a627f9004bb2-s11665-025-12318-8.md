ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Revealing the Underlying Mechanism of Mechanical
Properties Regulation in a Dual-Phase FeCrNi Medium
Entropy Alloy via Thermomechanical Treatment
Xiaolan Liu, Yan Sun, Yuejuan Huang, Yan Wang, and Ziyuan Chen
Submitted: 5 April 2025 / Revised: 27 June 2025 / Accepted: 10 July 2025
This study investigates the evolution of microstructure and mechanical properties in a cold-rolled dual-
phase Fe40Cr40Ni20 medium entropy alloy consisting of face-centered cubic (FCC) and body-centered cubic
(BCC) phases, following annealing at different temperatures. The solid solution state alloy encompasses the
FCC phase within the BCC phase. After cold rolling and annealing at various temperatures, the same
microstructural transformation that all alloys undergo can be summarized as follows. The original BCC
phase transforms into a non-recrystallized BCC phase with a different chemical composition, accompanied
by short strip-shaped FCC phases and long strip-shaped recrystallized BCC phases. The ﬁnal evolved
structure of the original FCC phase consists of an FCC matrix with Cr-rich BCC particles. The strength of
the alloy decreases with increasing annealing temperature, primarily inﬂuenced by the volume fractions of
FCC and BCC phases, residual dislocation strengthening post-annealing, grain reﬁnement, and back stress
arising from the heterogeneous structure. Ductility is mainly determined by the continuity of the soft region.
These ﬁndings offer valuable insights and technical considerations for the design of dual-phase alloys with
enhanced properties.
Keywords ductility, heterostructure, medium entropy alloy,
strength
1. Introduction
High entropy alloys (HEAs) and medium entropy alloys
(MEAs), also known as multi-component alloys, have garnered
signiﬁcant attention due to their excellent properties and
promising applications (Ref 1-3). Initially, the focus was on
achieving single solid solution structures, speciﬁcally the face-
centered cubic (FCC) phase or body-centered cubic (BCC)
phases. However, single FCC HEAs exhibit high ductility but
low strength, while single BCC HEAs demonstrate high
strength but poor deformation ability (Ref 4-7). Numerous
efforts have been made to improve the strength of FCC HEAs/
MEAs and enhance the deformation ability of BCC HEAs/
MEAs. For FCC HEAs/MEAs, various methods have been
employed to bolster strength (Ref 8), such as grain reﬁnement
(Ref 5, 9), precipitation strengthening (Ref 10-12), partial
recrystallization structure (Ref 13, 14), heterogeneous grain
structure (Ref 15, 16), and crystal defects (Ref 17). Similarly,
endeavors have been devoted to improving the ductility of BCC
HEAs/MEAs, including valence electron number regulation
(Ref 18), sample size reduction (Ref 19), and phase-transfor-
mation introduction (Ref 20). Notably, designing an FCC/BCC
dual-phase structure and regulating the structure proves to be a
straightforward and effective approach to achieving outstanding
mechanical properties for HEAs/MEAs.
In recent years, several FCC/BCC dual-phase HEAs have
been designed and investigated. Among them, eutectic high
entropy alloys (EHEAs) have received extensive attention
owing to their excellent mechanical properties and castability
(Ref 21-24). Lu et al. (Ref 21) proposed the concept of EHEA
and successfully fabricated AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 EHEA with an
FCC(L12)/BCC(B2) lamellar structure. This alloy exhibited an
exceptional combination of strength and ductility in its as-cast
condition. Zhao et al. (Ref 22) delved into the underlying
strengthening and deformation mechanisms of AlCoCrFeNi 2.1
EHEA, revealing that good ductility mainly arises from
dislocation slip and stacking faults (SFs) in the FCC phase.
Whereas the BCC phase exhibited high strength, further
enhanced by Cr-rich precipitation. The excellent mechanical
properties were mainly due to the coordination between the soft
FCC phase and hard BCC phase. Additionally, some quaternary
EHEAs with an FCC(L1
2)/BCC(B2) lamellar structure also
demonstrated excellent mechanical properties in the as-cast
condition (Ref 23, 24). Apart from lamellar-structured EHEAs,
FCC/BCC dual-phase HEAs characterized by dendritic struc-
tures have also been reported (Ref 25-30).
The mechanical properties of FCC/BCC dual-phase alloys
are closely linked to various factors, including phase volume
fraction, morphology, distribution, chemical composition, and
grain size. In recent years, the ‘‘cold rolling + annealing’’
technique has been widely used to optimize the structure and

mechanical property regulation. We hope that our ﬁndings can
provide reference for the design and fabrication of dual-phase
alloys with exceptional properties.
2. Material and Methods
Five alloy ingots were prepared by arc-melting in an argon
atmosphere, with a nominal composition of Fe 40Cr40Ni20
(at.%). The raw metals used had a purity of 99.99 wt%. To
ensure homogeneity, each ingot was re-melted ﬁve times.
Solution treatment was conducted at 1200 /C176 C for 24 h,
followed by water-quenching (WQ). One ingot was utilized
for investigating the microstructure in the solid solution state.
Alloy sheets with a thickness of 6 mm were cut from the
remaining ingots and cold-rolled to a thickness of 1.8 mm,
resulting in a 70% reduction. Subsequently, the cold-rolled
samples were annealed at temperatures of 750 /C176 C, 800 /C176 C, 850
/C176 C, and 900 /C176 C for 1 h, followed by WQ. The corresponding
alloys were labeled as A75, A80, A85, and A90, respectively.
The processing history of different alloys is shown in Fig. 1.
Phase identiﬁcation was performed using a D/max-rB x-ray
diffractometer (XRD) with Cu Ka radiation. Scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)
observations were conducted by Tescan MIRA3 and ZEISS-
sigma instruments, respectively. The SEM and EBSD samples
with the size of 5 9 5 9 3m m
3 were mechanically polished
using SiC papers ranging from 600-grit to 2000-grit. Subse-
quently, electro-polishing was carried out in a mixed solution of
93% C
2H5OH + 7% HClO 4 at 25 V for 10-20 s. To distinguish
between the two phases, the SEM and EBSD samples were
etched using a mixed solution of HCl:HNO
3:H2O = 3:1:4.
EBSD scans were acquired with an accelerating voltage of 20
KV and a step size of 0.4 lm. Transmission electron
microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai F30) was employed to charac-
terize the microstructures of different alloys. TEM specimens
with a size of U3m m 9 50 lm were thinned using ion milling.
Energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) was used to obtain the
chemical composition of the phases. The dog-bone-shaped
tensile specimens had a gauge length of 10 mm, a width of
2.5 mm, and a thickness of 1.5 mm. Tensile tests were
performed using an Instron 5565 machine equipped with an
extensometer, with a strain rate of 1 9 10
/C03/s.

4. Discussion
4.1 Microstructure Evolution
The MEA underwent a complex structural transformation
following cold rolling and annealing treatment, which can be
summarized in terms of phase transformation and recrystal-
lization. (1) Phase transformation: After annealing treatment,
new BCC particles formed within the original FCC phase, and
short strip-shaped FCC phases formed within the original BCC
phase. Chemical composition analysis in Table 3 revealed that
the strip-shaped FCC phase in R1 had the same chemical
composition as the FCC matrix in R2, while the BCC matrix in
R1 and BCC particles in R2 shared the same composition.
However, the morphologies of the new FCC and BCC phases
differed signiﬁcantly. This can be explained from the perspec-
tive of nucleation and growth. Massive dislocations formed
within the deformed FCC and BCC grains due to the cold
rolling process, providing favorable nucleation sites for the new
phases with low activation energy (Ref 36). Therefore, the Cr-
rich BCC particles and the short stripe-shaped FCC phases
nucleated at these dislocations. The growth of the new phases
was controlled by atom diffusion, aided by the pipe diffusion
provided by dislocations. The densiﬁcation of BCC lattice was
smaller than that of FCC, allowing diffusing atoms more room
to migrate to vacancies. Consequently, the BCC phase exhib-
ited a lower atomic migration barrier and a larger diffusion
coefﬁcient. As a result, the growth rate of the new FCC phase
within the original BCC phase was higher compared to the
growth of Cr-rich particles in the original FCC phase. Thus, the

4.2 Strength and Ductility
In FCC/BCC dual-phase alloys, the activation of slip system
was more easier for FCC phase at elastic deformation stage
(Ref 40). A planar slip of 1/2 < 110 > dislocations on {111}
planes ﬁrst appeared in FCC phase (Ref 41, 42). The
dislocations accumulated at the FCC/BCC phase boundaries
in FCC phase. However, the slip system was difﬁcult to activate
due to the high critical shear stress in BCC phase. Dislocation
transmission terminated at the phase boundary and the defor-
mation of FCC phase was constrained by the hard BCC phase
(Ref 32, 43). The back stress generated by dislocation pile-up
made it difﬁcult for the movement of subsequent dislocations in
FCC phase, then improving the strength of soft FCC phase. FU
et al. have proved that BCC phase still remains in elastic
deformation stage even FCC phase has entered plastic defor-
mation (Ref 40). It indicates that the phase boundary of soft
FCC phase and hard BCC phase was the most critical factor for
the enhancement of YS. After the annealing treatment, all
alloys consisted of two regions, namely R1 and R2. In R1, the
short strip-shaped FCC phase and the recrystallized BCC grains
embedded in non-recrystallized BCC matrix. R2 consisted of
recrystallized FCC grains and non-recrystallized FCC grains.
Cr-rich BCC particles formed in recrystallized FCC grains. In
R1, the short stripe-shaped FCC phase was surrounded by non-
recrystallized BCC phase. As the external stress gradually
increases, the slip systems in the strip-shaped FCC phase were
activated at low stress level, and the slipping dislocations will
accumulate at the phase boundary. It is more difﬁcult to activate
dislocation slip for non-recrystallized BCC phase due to the
existence of residual dislocations. The stronger constraint on
the deformation of short strip-shaped FCC phase causes an
increase in strength. The deformation structures of R1 region in
A90 alloy at two strains are shown in Fig. 10. The dislocation
density in the strip-shaped FCC phase increased signiﬁcantly
with the increasing strain. Additionally, it can be clearly
observed that the glide dislocations accumulated at the phase
boundary in the strip-shaped FCC phase. Some representative
positions were marked with white arrows. This demonstrates
the presence of back stress in the strip-shaped FCC phase.
Figure 11 shows the evolution of back stress in A90 alloy. It
can be observed that the back stress increases with the rise of
true strain, exhibiting a rapid initial growth followed by a
slower increase at later stage. This indicates that back stress
strengthening serves as one of the primary sources of work
hardening in the alloy. This is consistent with the ﬁndings
reported for some dual-phase MPEAs (Ref 44, 45). In R2,
dislocation slip ﬁrst appeared in recrystallized FCC grains.
Figure 12 shows the deformation structures of R2 region in
A90 alloy at different strains. It can be observed that the
dislocation density at the grain boundary and phase boundary at
both strains is signiﬁcantly higher than that in other regions.
Some representative positions were marked with white arrows
and circle. It indicates that grain boundary and Cr-rich particle
are the main barriers for slip dislocations. Moreover, the
dislocation density in non-recrystallized FCC grains was even
higher than that in non-recrystallized BCC grains, as Fig. 8(a3),
(b3) and (c3) shows. It is difﬁcult to slip for dislocations in non-
recrystallized FCC grains owing to the strong dislocation
strengthening effect. The non-recrystallized FCC grain, which
could be regarded as a hard phase, improving the strength
through suppressing the deformation of contiguous recrystal-
lized FCC grains. Hence one can see that, boundaries were the
key factors in improving the strength of annealed alloys,
including the boundaries of soft and hard phase (regions) and
grain boundary. As the annealing temperature increases, the
volume fraction of non-recrystallized FCC grains decreased and
the size of short strip-shaped FCC phase increased, resulting in
a decrease in boundary area. The grain size of recrystallized
FCC phase increased with the increasing annealing tempera-
tures, as shown in Fig. 8(a2)-(d2). All these structure changes
can lead to the reduction of the strength. The Cr-rich BCC
particles had a little change in size and volume fraction in
recrystallized grains, thus the change in strength caused by Cr-
rich BCC particles was small.
Notably, the alloys A75, A80, and A85 exhibit similar EL
ranging from 4.5 to 6.1%, but EL suddenly increases to 21.5%
at an annealing temperature of 900 /C176 C, rather than exhibiting a
gradual change like strength. The deformation ability of the two
regions differs. In R1, the strip-shaped FCC phase was
embedded within the non-recrystallized BCC matrix. As
mentioned earlier, the strip-shaped FCC phase in R1 displayed
good deformation ability through dislocation slip, as depicted
in Fig. 10. However, activating dislocation movement in the
non-recrystallized BCC matrix is challenging due to the
hindrance caused by the high density of retained dislocations
after deformation. As a result, the coordination between the
strip-shaped FCC phase and the non-recrystallized BCC matrix
in R1 is poor. The continuous presence of the hard BCC matrix
around the strip-shaped FCC phase hindered its deformation
ability. Figure 12 illustrates the deformation structure of R2,
where the high density of dislocations indicates that the
recrystallized FCC phase possesses good deformation ability.
Meanwhile, non-recrystallized FCC grains disappeared at 900
/C176 C, resulting in good deformation ability in R2. Thus, R1 and
R2 can be treated as the hard and soft regions, respectively. At
lower annealing temperatures, the soft R2 was surrounded by
hard R1, therefore, the deformation of R2 was fully constrained

Based on above analyses, the mechanical properties of
annealed alloy could be regulated by adjusting the annealing
temperature. On the one hand, the strength of annealed alloy
depended on the boundary area. The boundary area of short
stripe-shaped FCC phase and non-recrystallized BCC phase
increased with the decrease of stripe-shaped FCC phase size.
The short-stripe FCC phase was the product of BCC ﬁ FCC

annealing time was not conducive to atomic diffusion, then
forming FCC phase with small size in R1. Meanwhile, the high
volume fraction of non-recrystallized FCC grains could be
obtained under the same process condition. On the other hand,
regulating the continuity of soft R2 emerged as a crucial factor
for the ductility of the MEA. However, a high annealing
temperature was desired. More work should be done to ﬁnd the
optimal annealing process parameter in order to obtain
excellent matching of strength and ductility.
5. Conclusions
In summary, we examined the microstructure and tensile
properties of a cold-rolled Fe 40Cr40Ni20 MEA at different
annealing temperatures. By investigating the relationship
between strength, ductility, and microstructure evolution, we
have drawn the following key conclusions:
1. Cold rolling and subsequent annealing lead to a complex
evolution of microstructure. The original BCC phase
transforms into a structure composed of a non-recrystal-
lized BCC matrix, a short strip-shaped FCC phase, and a
long strip-shaped recrystallized BCC phase. The original
FCC phase undergoes partial recrystallization at tempera-
tures of 750 /C176 C, 800 /C176 C, and 850 /C176 C, and full recrystal-
lization at 900 /C176 C, resulting in the formation of an FCC
matrix and Cr-rich BCC particles.
