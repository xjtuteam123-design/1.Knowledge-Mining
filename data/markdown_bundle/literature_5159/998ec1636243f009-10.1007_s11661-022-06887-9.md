Effect of Microstructure, Strain Rate, and Elevated
Temperature on the Compression Property
of Fe–Co–Ni–Cr–Zr Alloy
P.C. ZHANG, B. ZHAI, and H.P. WANG
Eutectic high-entropy alloys with FCC solid solution phase and hard Laves phase can be used as
potential structural materials to meet the service conditions from room temperature to elevated
temperature. In this work, a series of FeCoNiCrZr 0.5 alloy rods with diﬀerent diameters ( F2,
F3, and F5 mm) prepared by vacuum suction casting were applied to investigate the eﬀects of
microstructure and strain rate on compression properties at room temperature, as well as the
microstructure evolution and deformation behavior at high temperature. With the decrease of
the sample diameter, in addition to the signiﬁcant reﬁnement of the lamellar eutectic in the
solidiﬁed microstructure, the alloy also undergoes a transformation from regular eutectic to
dendritic Laves phase plus eutectic microstructure. Moreover, the deformation behavior of the
alloy at diﬀerent strain rates was discussed based on the cross-sectional microstructure and
fracture-surface morphology of the compressed samples. The alloy samples obtained the
maximum compressive strengths of 2173 MPa at the strain rate of 10
–4/s. Also, the instability of
the lamellar eutectic and the precipitation of Ni 10Zr7 phase occurred in the alloy sample after
annealing above 1073 K. Finally, combined with ﬁnite element simulation and microscopic
transmission analysis, it is proved that the inhomogeneous microstructure of the deformed alloy
under high-temperature compression consists of the deformation region of bending lamellar or
shear instability and the spheroidized recrystallization region. This alloy exhibits excellent
high-temperature performance due to the coordinated ﬁne microstructure and the large number
of stacking faults present in the deformation. In summary, this work will provide new insights
and guidance for the design and application of gradient microstructure with dual-phase and
structural high-entropy alloys.

I. INTRODUCTION
THE mechanical properties and service performance
of high-entropy alloys have been a research focus since
they were developed. [1,2] Under normal circumstances,
researchers design single-phase or dual-phase alloys with
diﬀerent crystal structures by adjusting the type and
proportion of the elements, so as to meet and optimize
the performance of the material under complicated
working conditions.
[3–6] As a strengthening phase in
structural materials, intermetallic compounds (such as
Ti3Al, Nb 2Ni, Ni 5Zr, Laves, etc.) endow the alloy with
high strength, hardness, and creep resistance. [7–10] How-
ever, its own inherent defects such as grain boundary
brittleness limit its application as a structural
material.
[11,12]
In order to satisfy the balance between the strength
and plasticity, a dual-phase high-entropy alloy with
coupled soft phase and hard phase was designed and
studied in depth. Gangireddy et al .
[13] found that the

eﬀect of the dual-phase nanostructure and the near
equiaxed crystals in the alloy not only inhibits the
initiation of cracks, but also eﬀectively optimizes the
tensile properties by the formation of intragranular and
grain boundary back stress. Cao et al .

II. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE
The FeCoNiCrZr
0.5 eutectic high-entropy alloy ingots
were prepared using an arc melting device ﬁlled with
argon protection. The raw materials are made of
high-purity ( ‡ 99.95 pct) metal elemental Fe, Co, Ni,
Cr, Zr in proportion. Each ingot was repeatedly melted
several times and overheated before being sucked into a
water-cooled copper mold, so as to obtain the cylindri-
cal rod with diameters of F2, F3, and F5 mm. The
structural characterization of the studied EHEA was
identiﬁed by X-ray diﬀraction (XRD, Broke) with a Co
target ( k = 0.17 nm). The solidiﬁed, annealed, and
deformed microstructures of the samples involved in
this work were determined by a scanning electron
microscope (SEM, FEI Verios G4) equipped with an
energy spectrometer (EDS) and a high-resolution trans-
mission electron microscope (HRTEM, FEI Talos
F200X).
The sample with a diameter of 2 mm was selected and
packaged in a quartz tube backﬁlled with argon. Then, the
quartz tube was put in a high-temperature resistance
furnace of model X5 for annealing experiment, in order to
compare with the as-cast microstructure. After reaching
the established temperature (973 K, 1073 K, and 1173 K)
at a heating rate of 5 K/min, the temperature was kept for
3 hours and then cooled to room temperature in the
furnace. The as-cast alloy rods with diﬀerent diameters
were cut into samples with a height-to-diameter ratio of
2:1 for subsequent compression tests. Firstly, under the
room temperature of 273 K, compression experiments
were performed on alloy samples with diﬀerent diameters
at the strain rates of 10
–2,1 0 –3, and 10 –4/s. In the
isothermal deformation experiment, the F2 sample was
placed in the middle of the upper and lower pressure
plates and heated to 875 K, 925 K, 975 K, and 1075 K.
The sample was kept at the test temperature for 10 min-
utes to obtain a uniform temperature distribution, after
that the compression experiment was carried out at a
strain rate of 10
–3/s. Since the sample would not fracture
during the high-temperature compression, the experiment
was ended when the stress just increases after the strain
softening. The compression test under the same condi-
tions was repeated three times to ensure the reliability of
the data.
The thermal compression process of the alloy was
realized by ﬁnite element simulation. The strain rate of
high-temperature deformation was controlled by the set
stroke speed and time. The simulation adopted 2-D
axisymmetric and isothermal conditions. The mechan-
ical performance data required in the simulation were
obtained by the above-mentioned universal testing
machine (INSTRON 5969), while other physical param-
eters were calculated by the mixed criteria.

III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
A. Phase Constitution and Microstructure
Characterization
The phase constitution and microstructure of a
material are closely related to its composition and
solidiﬁcation conditions. Figure 1 illustrates the XRD
patterns of the as-cast samples with diﬀerent diameters.
The result shows that all samples are composed of FCC
and Laves phases. Moreover, with the decrease of the
diameter, changes in the height and width of the
diﬀraction peaks imply that the solidiﬁed microstructure
of the samples may have evolved. Simultaneously,
Figure 2 depicts the solidiﬁed microstructure evolution
under diﬀerent suction casting conditions. It can be seen
that the solidiﬁed microstructure of F5 and F3 samples
changes from irregular eutectic in the surface
layer ﬁ primary phase + lamellar eutectic in the tran-
sition layer ﬁ fully regular eutectic in the interior. On
the surface of the rod, due to the chilling eﬀect of the
copper mold, the increasing cooling rate causes the alloy
to produce an irregular eutectic microstructure after
solidiﬁcation. The smaller the sample diameter, the
higher the cooling rate during solidiﬁcation. The
non-equilibrium eﬀect caused by diﬀerent cooling rates
changes the eutectic morphology of the alloy after
solidiﬁcation. Combining the EDS analysis with the
X-ray diﬀraction in Figure 1, it can be determined that
the primary phase is the (Zr, Ni)-rich Laves phase which
is easily corroded by aqua regia, and the other is the (Fe,
Cr)-rich FCC phase with excellent corrosion resistance.
However, with the further increase of the cooling rate,
the solidiﬁed microstructure of F2 sample manifests a
large number of primary Laves phase and lamellar
eutectic between dendrites. During rapid solidiﬁcation,
the growth of eutectic-dendrite is aﬀected by the eﬀects
of non-equilibrium interface dynamics and non-equilib-
rium solute diﬀusion. With the increase of cooling rate,
the eutectic point will deviate from the equilibrium
position. Simultaneously, the solute distribution coeﬃ-
cient and the liquidus slope also change. The actual
solidiﬁcation of the eutectic alloy starts below the
equilibrium point due to the eﬀect of kinetic undercool-
ing under rapid solidiﬁcation condition. At the cooling
rate of 2 mm sample, the kinetic undercooling of
non-equilibrium solidiﬁcation in this alloy exceeds the
required undercooling for Laves phase crystallization,
resulting in a dendrite-eutectic microstructure. As the
leading phase, Laves phase nucleates and grows in the
melt ﬁrstly, while the remaining liquid phase generates
the eutectic microstructure of coupled growth of Laves
phase and FCC phase, as displayed in Figures 2(a)
through (e). Similarly, Figure 2(f) proves that the
lamellar eutectic thickness of the alloy is positively
correlated with the size of the solidiﬁed sample. Obvi-
ously, the diameter of the rod is reduced from 5 to
2 mm, the size of the lamellar eutectic ( k

from 90 nm ( F5) to approximate 45 nm ( F2), such as
shown in Figure 2(f). Also, there is little diﬀerence in
thickness between FCC phase and Laves phase under
the same cooling rate.

analyzed by the model of Jackson and Hunt. [30,31] The
growth of lamellar eutectic is determined by the solute
diﬀusion and thermal diﬀusion at the liquid–solid
interface. The diﬀerence in composition makes the
lamellar sizes of two-phase distinct, and the lamellar
spacing both decrease with increasing cooling rate.

C. Strain Rate Sensitivity and Fracture Behavior
For the alloy studied, F5a n d F3 samples all exhibit a
positive correlation between compressive strength and
strain rate. The strength enhances with the increasing
strain rate. This positive strain rate sensitivity was also
found in high-temperature tests of CoCrFeNiTa
[22]
EHEA as well as in room and cryogenic temperature
compression of metastable V 10Cr10Fe45Co35 high-en-
tropy alloy. [34] The strain rate hardening behavior can
be explained by dislocation strengthening. However,
from the stress–strain curve, it can be found that the F2
sample abnormally reaches the maximum compressive
strength and strain at a strain rate of 10 –4/s, exhibiting
2173 MPa and 12.7 pct, respectively, while the strength
is lowest at high strain rate of 10
–2/s.
To explain the strength-dependent strain rate behav-
ior of diﬀerent samples during compression, microscopic
analyses of cross-sections and fracture surfaces were
performed on alloys at varying compression rates.
Fracture of materials is caused by the initiation and
propagation of cracks when stresses are applied.
According to the SEM characterization, the solidiﬁed
microstructures of F5 and F3 samples are both com-
posed of external irregular eutectic and internal lamellar
eutectic. Under the action of stress, cracks are initiated
in the eutectic matrix and propagate along the direction
of the maximum shear force or the interface of the
lamellar eutectic. The fracture mode is quasi-cleavage
fracture with the transgranular and intergranular com-
bination, according to Figures 5(a) through (d).

smaller. Also, this sample also exhibits fractures com-

D. Annealed Microstructure Stability
At high temperature, the microstructure of eutectic
alloy may change, which will aﬀect the mechanical
properties of the materials. Therefore, it is necessary to
study the microstructure stability at diﬀerent annealing
temperatures. Figure 6 displays the microstructure evo-
lution of the alloy after annealing at 973 K, 1073 K, and
1173 K. Evidently, the microstructure of the sample
after 973 K annealing is basically the same as the as-cast
of the F2 sample. It can be seen that the morphology,
volume fraction, and size of the dendritic Laves phase
and the interdendritic eutectic display little change, as
shown in Figures 6(a) and (b). However, the eutectic
structure undergoes some interesting changes in its
microstructure after annealing at 1073 K and 1173 K.
Speciﬁcally, the primary arm (parallel lamella) of the
dendritic eutectic did not widen, while the branch
(curved or helical eutectic microstructure) signiﬁcantly
decomposed and coarsened with the increasing temper-
ature. Also, another new phase formation was observed
in the dendrites and around the Laves phase in the
coarsened eutectic, as exhibited in Figures 6(c) and (d).
In order to analyze the microstructure evolution of the
alloy after annealing and to conﬁrm the new phase, the
TEM characterization of the alloy under the annealing
condition of 1073 K was carried out. It can be observed
that the primary phase and the same phase in the
eutectic matrix are rich in Ni and Zr elements, while the
other phase in the matrix is rich in Fe and Cr elements,
as presented in Figure 7(a). It is worth noting that
diﬀerent regions of the primary phase in the HADDF
image exhibit distinct color contrast, which may be due
to diﬀerent crystal orientations or structural transfor-
mation of the Laves phase. Combined with the SAED in
Figures 7(b) through (d), it is conﬁrmed that in addition
to the remaining Laves phase in the primary phase after
annealing, the precipitated Ni
10Zr7 phase with orthog-
onal lattice is also demonstrated on the [1 20] and [2 13]
orientations. Also, after annealing at 1073 K for this
alloy, there are a lot of stacking faults in Ni 10Zr7 phase.
The basic feature of the stacking fault in diﬀraction
image is the light and dark fringes parallel to the plane
of stacking fault. The HRTEM image in Figure 7(b)
shows that atomic staggered arrangements have
occurred in the Ni
10Zr7 phase, thereby changing the
original stacking order, which proves the formation of
stacking faults. Combined with the spots connected by
the diﬀraction fringes in the corresponding electron
diﬀraction, it is determined that the stacking fault is
produced on the (002) plane. The phase transition stress
and thermal stress during the annealing can be released
by atomic plane slip, which leads to the easy formation
of stacking faults in the close-packed plane (002) of the
Ni
10Zr7 phase. Among the ﬁve constituent elements of
the alloy, under the action of thermal diﬀusion, Ni and
Zr elements are easily segregated to form Ni 10Zr7
intermetallic compounds because Ni and Zr exhibit the
largest atomic size diﬀerence. Similar experimental
phenomena were also found in CoFeNi 2V0.5Nb0.75
EHEA.[17] In the paper, Li et al . pointed out that the
microstructure of this alloy spheroidizes and coarsens
with the increase of annealing temperature, and the
stable NbNi
4 intermetallic compounds precipitate from
Fe2Nb-type Laves phase when the annealing tempera-
ture is higher than 873 K.
To sum up, the microstructure of the alloy after
annealing at 973 K is relatively stable. However, when
the annealing temperatures are 1073 and 1173 K, edge
globularization, terminal matter migration, and discon-
tinuous coarsening of grain boundaries caused the
instability of the eutectic lamellar. The change of
eutectic morphology and the precipitation of Ni
10Zr7
phase will have a signiﬁcant impact on the microscopic
deformation mechanism and macroscopic mechanical
performance of the alloy.

IV. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, FeCoNiCrZr
0.5 EHEA with diﬀerent
diameters was prepared by suction casting. The solid-
iﬁed, annealed, and deformed microstructure evolution
as well as deformation behavior at room and elevated
temperatures were systematically evaluated. The major
results are summarized as follows:
(1) Dependence of microstructure on cooling rate:
With the decrease of sample diameter (increase
of cooling rate), in addition to the significant
refinement of the lamellar eutectic in the solidified
microstructure, the alloy shows a transformation
from regular eutectic microstructure to dendritic
Laves phase + eutectic.

primary phase shows the largest compressive

12.7 pct, respectively. The yield and compressive
