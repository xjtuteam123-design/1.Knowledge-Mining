 Abstract 
 The microstructure evolution and mechanical prop erties were investigated in a 
multi-precipitation Ni-Co base superalloy system with different chemical compositions (wt. %) of 
Ni 
30 -Co 33 -C0.02 (1#) and Ni 28.5 -Co 34.5 -C0.01 (2#). The interaction of multi-precipitates and its 
influence on the creep behavior were studied in cor relation with the evolution of deformed 
microstructure. The multi-precipitates mainly consi st of γ′ -Ni 3Al phase, β-NiAl phase, carbides, 
and η phase. After a long term aging, both of the β and γ′ phase possess a strong thermal stability 
in the two superalloys, while the number density of η precipitates has increased a lot (volume 
fraction: 0.02%-0.21%) in the 2# Ni-Co alloys compa red with that in the 1# Ni-Co alloys. The 
room temperature mechanical properties of both Ni-C o alloys almost remain stable such as 
ultimate tensile strength (1260-1280 MPa) and total elongation (22-24%) even after a long aging 
time of 1500 h. However, different from the stress- free aging process, the γ′ precipitate in the 2# 
Ni-Co alloy shows a higher rafting rate compared wi th that in the 1# Ni-Co alloy after the creep 
(903 K, 450 MPa) for 500 h. Thus, the 2# Ni-Co allo y exhibits a significantly higher creep strain 
rate and an advanced accelerating creep stage than the 1# Ni-Co alloy. The differences of the creep

1. Introduction 
Ni base superalloys have attracted a lot of attention for the structural application on aerospace, 
compressor, and turbine systems due to the low coef ficient of thermal expansion, high strength 
and good resistance to stress accelerated grain bou ndary oxidation [1, 2]. Their superior 
mechanical performance is always associated with th e major precipitating phase in a disordered γ 
matrix, such as L1 2-type ordered γ′ -Ni 3Al precipitates[3], incoherent β-NiAl phase[4], MC, M 23 C6, 
or M 6C carbides[5]. The coherent γ′ phase imparts precipitation strengthening while th e 
introduction of the β phase mainly contributes to the resistance of stre ss corrosion cracking[6]. 
Moreover, the precipitation of carbides during inte rmediate temperature aging or creep also has a 
strong impact on the creep behavior, as it gives ri se to increased resistance to dislocation 
movement [7] and consumes the alloying elements fro m the matrix and other precipitates. In 
decades, various alloying element[8] such as Cr, Ti, Mo, Nb, Fe, and B has been added to achieve 
an excellent combination of physical, mechanical an d corrosion/oxidation properties, which 
inevitably makes the phase composition and mechanic al properties more complicated. Thus, the 
microstructure optimization of the constituent phas es has always been a crucial problem for 
designing an excellent high-temperature mechanical response of superalloys. 
Recently, a lot of studies in superalloys have focused on precipitation behavior when varying 
the temperature, time, and stress. Numerous simulat ions[9] and experiments[10] have provided 
important insights into the effect of aging temperature on the precipitation kinetics and coarsening 
behavior of the γ′ phase based on the diffusion-limited model. It is demonstrated that the 
coherency loss[11] between γ′ precipitate and the matrix is crucial to the rafti ng behavior of

superalloys, while appropriate element segregation at the γ/γ′ interface can slow down the 
coarsening kinetics by reducing the lattice mismatch[12]. Wu et al. [13] found that the change of β 
phase morphology may deteriorate the high temperatu re mechanical properties during the 
annealing process. Moreover, the nucleation of the γ′′ -Ni 3Nb was found to be enhanced by 
external stress during rapid cooling in Inconel 718 [14]. However, little attention has been focused 
on the interaction of multi-precipitation behaviors and its influence on the high temperature 
properties such as creep, fatigue, and oxidation be havior. Firstly, the inter-diffusion of the main 
alloying elements in the austenite matrix is compli cated for studying precipitation reactions. 
Secondly, there is an interactive transformation te ndency of different precipitates, for example, 
high temperature aging above 650 ℃ can result in the acceleration of transformation from γ″ phase 
to orthorhombic δ phase[15]. Furthermore, the roles of different pre cipitates on the mechanical 
properties are various even contrary. The formation of either M 23 C6 carbides or coarse grain 
boundary γ′ precipitates can induce serrated grain boundaries[ 16] associated with longer creep 
rupture time while a large amount of plate-like η phase precipitates[15] are likely to be 
detrimental. 
On the other hand, the creep mechanisms are closely correlated with the evolution of 
precipitates, and more importantly, the interaction s of dislocations, stacking faults, and 
micro-twinning with precipitates. Firstly, the mult iple size distributions[17] of γ′ phases are 
utilized in polycrystalline superalloys to improve the ductility and creep performance by the 
dislocation shear or Orwan mechanisms. The lattice misfit[18] of γ/γ′ interface seems to be an 
important factor in determining the rate-limiting step for creep behavior, and it is directly linked to 
the diffusivity of solute species to these lattice defects. The deformation modes in Ni-base 
superalloys are still not fully understood at vario us temperatures. The creep deformation always 
results in the shearing of γ′ phase [19] by the formation of super-lattice stack ing faults, which 
relieves the build-up of dislocation content within the γ channels. Recent investigations[20] have 
found that the dominant deformation mechanisms change from super-dislocation shearing into the 
γ′ phases to dislocation climbing over the rafted γ′ precipitates when the creeping stage evolves 
from the steady-state to the accelerated-sate. Indeed, the creep behavior at elevated temperature is 
generally characterized by two major factors[21]: ( i) directional coarsening of γ′ precipitates and 
(ii) the γ/γ′ interfacial dislocation network, while few works e mphasize on the interplay between

[22], which could be affected by the addition of some alloying element, plays an important role in 
the dislocation mobility and the overall creep prop erties [23] of Ni-Co base superalloys. With the 
decrease of SFE, the wavy slip will be gradually replaced by planar-slip and the stacking fault and

which could largely improve the tensile strength an d creep life. Thus, the influence of SFE on the 
creep properties is also crucial in the multi-precipitation hardened superalloys. 
 The work aims at investigating the relationshi p between the microstructure evolution of 
multi-precipitate (including γ′ -Ni 3Al phase, β-NiAl phase, carbides, and η phase) and the 
mechanical properties (creep). Compared to the existing literature [11-14], little attention has been 
focused on the interaction of multi-precipitates an d its influence on the creep behavior. Many 
publications only focus on the effect of a single p recipitate on the creep properties. Moreover, the 
previous studies [21] indicated that the creep resi stance of Ni-Co based superalloys mainly 
depends on some microstructural factors, including the volume fraction, particle size and 
distribution of the γ′ phase. However, the influence of other precipitates such as β-NiAl phase and 
carbides on the creep deformation mechanisms has be en rarely studied when considering the 
complicated chemical compositions. Thus, in the pre sent study, a multi-precipitation Ni-Co base 
superalloy system was studied. Two alloys of Ni 30 -Co 33 -C0.02 and Ni 28.5 -Co 34.5 -C0.01 (wt. %) were 
chosen to alter the solid solvus temperatures of γ′ and β phase and the precipitation kinetics of 
carbides. A different initial microstructure constitution of multi-precipitates was designed to reveal 
their influences on the overall mechanical response s. Meanwhile, the creep mechanisms are 
discussed based on the evolution of deformed micros tructure and the correlated stacking fault 
energy, which provided a guide for the design of hi gh performance precipitation-hardened Ni-Co 
base superalloys. 
 
2. Experimental procedures 
The present study focused on two Ni-Co base superal loys (1# and 2#), the detailed chemical 
compositions measured by Atomic Absorption Spectrometer (AAS) were given in Table 1. For 
both tested alloys, 20 kg ingots were cast using va cuum induction melting (VIM). These ingots

were then hot extruded into 35 mm bars with an extr usion ratio of 12:1 at about 1433 K. A 
three-step heat treatment was conducted in the hot worked flat. Firstly, the cast material was 
annealed at 1393 K for 1 h for homogenization (air cooling). Then, the annealed material was aged 
at 1116 K for 4 h and air-cooled. Finally, it was a ged at 991 K for 8 h and furnace cooling at 550 
K/h to 894 K, held for 8 h at this temperature foll owed by air cooling to room temperature. The 
latter two procedure was called β aging and γ′ aging, respectively, which precipitates the 
corresponding secondary phase in the γ matrix. To evaluate the microstructure stability, the 
as-received (AR) state was isothermal exposed at 90 3 K for different aging time of 100 h, 500 h, 
1000 h, and 1500 h, respectively, which were defined as the AG states. 
Detailed microstructure evolution was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM TESCAN) and transmission e lectron microscopy (TEM JEM 2100) 
with energy Disperse Spectroscopy (Oxford, EDS). SE M samples were mechanical polished and 
then etched by electrolytic phosphoric acid. TEM sa mples were punched to a Φ3 mm disk, 
mechanically thinned to 50 μm, and followed by double-jet electro-polishing usi ng a mixed 
solution of HClO 4:C 2H5OH = 1:4 with a direct voltage of 50 V at 253 K. Te nsile tests at room 
temperature were carried out on samples (the gage section 16 mm * 5 mm * 1.5 mm) with the long 
axes parallel to the rolling direction in a Zwick ( BTC-T1-FR020 TN.A50) universal testing 
machine with a strain rate of 0.5 mm/min. Vickers h ardness measurements were conducted under 
a 1000 g applied load and a dwell time of 15 s, and the average hardness values were measured 
from eight different measurements. A 450 MPa monotonic tensile creep test was performed at 903 
K using an INSTRON stress rig to observe the creep behavior and corresponding microstructure 
evolution. The dimensions of creep specimens are sh own in Fig. 1. To ensure the data reliability, 
At least two and three specimens were tested under each condition for creep and tensile 
experiments, respectively.

3.2 The evolution of multi-precipitation during creep at intermediate temperature 
 The creep properties under 450 MPa at 903 K an d corresponding creep rate curves of 1#- 
and 2# Ni-Co superalloys are shown in Fig. 7. Altho ugh the room temperature tensile properties 
and the overall microstructure constitutions of both superalloys are similar, their creep stress-strain 
curves are totally different. Firstly, the 2# Ni-Co alloy exhibits a significantly higher creep strain 
than the 1# Ni-Co alloy under an equivalent creep t ime (Fig. 7a). Meanwhile, the creep strain of 
the 2# Ni-Co alloy exhibits a rapidly increasing rate especially after creeping for 1000 h, while the 
plastic strain in the 1# Ni-Co alloy shows a stable rising tendency all over the creeping stage. 
Moreover, the curves of 2# Ni-Co superalloys can be divided into three remarkable stages: a very 
short primary decelerating stage, followed by a lon g steady-state creep stage and an accelerating 
stage where the strain rate almost increases linear ly with strain. However, the second stage 
occupies the most proportion of creep life of the 1 # Ni-Co superalloy and the accelerating stage 
does not occur even until 1500 h. Also, the strain rate of the 1# Ni-Co alloy in the steady-state 
creep stage (Fig. 7b) is much lower than that of th e 2# Ni-Co alloy. This phenomenon may be 
closely related to the interaction between the defo rmed substructure and the existing 
multi-precipitates of such Ni-Co superalloys.

Two types of γ′ phases can be defined: large irregular (20-50 nm) and small spherical (3-10 nm).

with that in the 1# Ni-Co alloy. This phenomenon ca n be attributed to the acceleration of γ′ phase

As is all known, the γ′ phase is the major strengthening phase in superall oys. Thus, the 
mechanical properties of both Ni-Co alloys almost r emain unchanged even that it experiences a 
very long-term tempering due to the high thermal st ability of γ′ phase. A little increase in yield 
strength of 2# Ni-Co alloys can be attributed to the precipitation of the η phase after aging for 500 
h (Fig. 4d). Moreover, there is a large difference of the creep behavior between the two 
superalloys, though they show a similar microstructure constitution in the initial AR state. The 2# 
Ni-Co alloy exhibits a significantly higher creep s train than the 1# Ni-Co alloy at an equivalent 
aging time, and the steady-state stage of the 1# Ni -Co alloy occupies the most proportion of creep 
life. The creep properties of such Ni-Co superalloy s are determined by a couple of factors: the 
precipitation strengthening by γ′ phase, η phase, and the formation of carbides[27]. In both 
superalloys, the interaction between dislocations a nd γ′ precipitates depends on the critical 
dimension of the precipitates, that is, large irreg ular precipitates by looping or climbing while 
small spherical precipitates by shearing[28]. Meanw hile, the nucleation and growth of the

Furthermore, the SFE is also an important intrinsic parameter [29] and is closely connected 
with the deformation mechanism, such as cross-slip, stacking fault, and deformation twinning. In 
this work, the tuning of SFE value is achieved by the minor addition of Co and C conte nt to 
replace Ni in the Ni-Co base superalloys. Firstly, the SFE values of the matrix in the 1# and 2# 
Ni-Co base superalloys at room temperatures are cal culated to be 36.9 and 42.1 mJ/m 2 based on 
the change of Gibbs free energies [30, 31], respectively. Under the creep stress, the decomposition 
of dislocations exhibits distinct features from the stress-free conditions. The lower SFE results in 
lower interfacial energy, thus leading to a large amount of stacking fault which can assume a large 
portion of strain in the 1# Ni-Co alloy. On the con trary, the generation of stacking fault is 
suppressed in the high SFE alloy, while large amoun ts of piling-ups of perfect dislocations are 
observed in the γ′ /γ interfaces of the 2# Ni-Co alloy, which also contr ibutes to the weakening of 
creep resistance. 
Thus, the origin of lower creep resistance of the 2 # Ni-Co alloy can be revealed based on 
the following aspects: (i) During the steady-state creep stage, a high rafting rate of γ′ phase leads 
to a change of deformation mechanism from dislocati on gliding to dislocation climbing over the 
rafted γ′ phase, thus contributing to a lower strengthening degree than that in the 1# Ni-Co alloy. 
Moreover, a high SFE of the matrix results in a str ong stress concentration and dislocation 
network formation in the γ/γ′ interfaces during the acceleration stage (Fig. 10d ), while the 
generation of lots of stacking fault can assume a large portion of strain in the creep deformation of 
the 1# Ni-Co alloy. (ii) Although the volumetric sh rinkage[32] due to the precipitation of the η 
phase can decrease the creep strain rate, the inter action between the dislocations and the coarse η 
phase inevitably results in a localized strain heterogeneity, thus leading to an accelerated creep. In 
contrast, sustainable precipitation hardening from spherical MC carbides can delay the steady 
stage to a very long creep life until 1500 h in the 1# Ni-Co alloy. 
Besides, the 2# Ni-Co alloy shows a higher oxidatio n resistance than the 1# Ni-Co alloy 
(not provided in this study) during high temperatur e aging, which is mainly related to the slight

5. Conclusions 
In this work, a multi-precipitation Ni-Co base supe ralloy system was studied with different 
chemical compositions and microstructure constituti ons. The tensile and creep tests were 
conducted in correlation with the microstructure ev olution of multi-precipitates, including 
γ′ -Ni 
3Al phase, β-NiAl phase, carbides, and η phase. Moreover, the creep mechanisms were 
evaluated by the interaction of the dislocations wi th various precipitates during different creep 
stages. Main conclusions could be drawn: 
(1) During aging at 903 K, the β phase possesses a strong structure stability in bo th superalloys. 
However, the number density of η precipitates has increased a lot in 2# Ni-Co alloy s 
compared with that in the 1# Ni-Co alloys after a l ong term aging. The γ′ phase also shows a 
high thermal stability in both superalloys, and slo w growth of spherical (3-10 nm) and 
irregular phase (20-50 nm) dominates the long term aging process of the 1# and 2# Ni-Co 
alloys, respectively. However, different from the stress-free aging process, the γ′ precipitate in 
the 2# Ni-Co alloy shows a high rafting rate after creep compared with that in the 1# Ni-Co 
alloy. 
(2) Due to a high microstructure stability, the mechani cal properties of both Ni-Co alloys almost 
remain unchanged such as ultimate tensile strength (1260-1280 MPa) and total elongation 
(22-24%), even that it experiences a very long term tempering. A little increase in yield 
strength of the 2# Ni-Co alloy can be attributed to the precipitation of η phase after aging for 
500 h. 
(3) The creep process can be divided into three stages: a very short primary decelerating stage, 
followed by a long steady-state creep stage and an accelerating stage where the strain rate 
almost increases linearly with strain. The second s tage occupies the most proportion of creep 
life of the 1# Ni-Co superalloy and the acceleratin g stage does not occur even until 1500 h. 
However, the 2# Ni-Co alloy exhibits a significantl y higher creep strain than the 1# Ni-Co 
alloy at an equivalent creep time.
