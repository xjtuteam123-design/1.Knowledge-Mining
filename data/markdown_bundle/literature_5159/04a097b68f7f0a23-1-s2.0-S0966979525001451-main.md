1. Introduction
With the rapid development of the aerospace and nuclear energy 
industries, the demand for high-performance materials is increasing. For 
instance, the structural materials used in nuclear fusion reactors should 
have exceptional high-temperature mechanical properties, irradiation 
resistance and oxidation resistance simultaneously [ 1 ]. In recent de -
cades, nickel-based superalloys have been the primary high-temperature 
structural materials due to their outstanding properties. However, their 
average operating temperature is only 1050 
◦
C, which significantly re -
stricts their applications in extremely high-temperature environments 
[ 2 ]. Tungsten alloys possess advantages such as a high melting point and 
excellent thermal conductivity, making them promising candidates for 
the first wall of nuclear fusion reactors [ 3 ]. Nevertheless, the tungsten 
alloys still face many challenges, including poor neutron irradiation 
resistance and recrystallization brittleness [ 4 ]. Recently, the develop -
ment of high entropy alloys (HEAs) offers a novel approach for exploring 
high-performance materials [ 5 ]. The W-containing refractory high en -
tropy alloys (REHAs) have then garnered significant attention due to 
their exceptional mechanical properties and structural stability at 
elevated temperatures [ 6 – 10 ].
Compared to traditional alloys, RHEAs can demonstrate high 
strength at elevated temperatures, resulting from the solution 
strengthening and sluggish diffusion of refractory elements [ 11 – 13 ]. For 
example, the NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW RHEAs demonstrated 
remarkable resistance to high-temperature softening, along with supe -
rior high-temperature mechanical properties [ 14 ]. However, these 
RHEAs usually exhibited limited plasticity at room temperature, despite 
their exceptional strength at elevated temperatures. For instance, Zhang 
et al. reported the Al
