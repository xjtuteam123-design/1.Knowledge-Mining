1. Introduction
The development of advanced structural materials with superior 
high-temperature performance is essential for applications in aerospace, 
energy generation, and high-temperature industrial systems [ 1-3 ]. 
However, conventional Fe-based and Ni-based superalloys are 
approaching their performance limits, rendering them inadequate for 
increasingly stringent high-temperature applications [ 4 , 5 ]. These alloys 
are prone to grain coarsening, phase instability, and precipitate disso -
lution at elevated temperatures, which collectively lead to significant 
mechanical degradation [ 6-8 ]. For example, in Ni-Fe-based alloys under 
tensile loading above 700 
◦
C, microcracks often initiate around M
23
C
6 
carbides at grain boundaries. These cracks can progressively link up to 
form secondary cracks, resulting in intergranular fracture and a sub -
stantial loss of mechanical integrity [ 9 ]. Additionally, Fe-based and 
Ni-based alloys tend to form unstable oxide scales in oxidative envi -
ronments, promoting oxygen ingress and corrosion during service 
[ 10-12 ]. Therefore, developing alloys with outstanding mechanical 
properties and oxidation resistance at high temperatures remains a 
significant challenge.
High entropy alloys (HEAs), a novel class of materials with innova -
tive compositional designs, offer unique advantages in terms of me -
chanical properties, thermal stability, and corrosion resistance [ 13-15 ]. 
Unlike traditional alloys, HEAs are composed of four or more principal 
elements in near-equiatomic proportions [ 16-18 ]. Despite significant 
advancements in optimizing HEAs through compositional optimization 
and thermomechanical processing, their performance remains limited in 
certain areas [ 19-21 ]. For instance, face-centered cubic (FCC) HEAs such 
as CoCrNi and CoCrFeNi exhibit excellent ductility at room temperature 
due to their multiple slip systems [ 22-24 ]. However, the relatively low 
yield strength ( σ
y
) limits the applications in both ambient and 
high-temperature environments [ 2 , 25 ]. Precipitation strengthening has 
recently emerged as a promising approach to overcome this limitation 
by enhancing strength across a broad temperature range [ 26-29 ]. By

deformation and high-temperature strength of HEAs can be significantly 
improved, demonstrating great potential for high-temperature alloy 
design [ 30 , 31 ]. The high-temperature performance of 
precipitation-strengthened HEAs is critically influenced by the type, 
distribution, size, and stability of the precipitates [ 32 , 33 ]. Among the 
commonly strengthening phases, B2 and L1

promising high temperature potential due to the stable semi-coherent or

[ 31 ] introduced a high density of B2 precipitates into a FeNiCoAl-based 
HEA through thermomechanical processing, achieving an excellent 
combination of strength and ductility from ambient to high tempera -

oxidation resistance of a CoCrFeNi-based HEA by incorporating L1

However, B2 phases tend to soften rapidly at high temperatures, leading 
to a notable decline in strength [ 30 , 31 ]. Additionally, L1

HEAs often suffer from intermediate-temperature embrittlement, 
particularly under high-stress conditions [ 36 ]. These limitations high -
light the ongoing challenge of developing HEAs that simultaneously 
exhibit superior strength-ductility synergy and high-temperature 
performance.
For high-temperature applications, excellent oxidation resistance is 
critical, as surface oxidation at elevated temperatures can lead to pre -
mature material failure [ 3 , 37 ]. The complex elemental composition and 
synergistic effects in HEAs can significantly enhance their oxidation 
resistance [ 35 ]. For instance, Sheikh et al. [ 38 ] demonstrated that HEAs 
containing Cr and Al elements stable oxide scales at high temperatures, 
effectively protecting the underlying matrix and substantially improving 
oxidation resistance. The formation of B2 and L1
2 
precipitates, often 
facilitated by the addition of Al, is commonly employed to further 
enhance oxidation performance [ 39 , 40 ]. For example, Ding et al. [ 37 ] 
introduced a high density of L1
2 
precipitates in a FeCoNiCr-based HEA 
by adding trace amounts of Al and Ti. The results showed that increasing 
Al content promoted the formation of a continuous Al
2
O
3 
layer beneath 
the Cr
2
O

scale, effectively inhibiting the diffusion of oxygen ions and 
metal cations, thereby significantly enhancing oxidation resistance. 
However, the role of Ti element, which is closely associated with L1
2 
precipitates, remains controversial. While some studies suggest that Ti 
may hinder Al
2
O
3 
formation and increase vacancy concentrations in 
Cr
2
O

diffusion of oxygen ions, ultimately reducing oxidation resistance [ 35 ,
41 ]. In contrast, other studies indicate that Ti can positively influence 
oxidation resistance, improving it to some extent [ 42 , 43 ]. These con -
flicting findings underscore the importance of precisely controlling Al 
and Ti content to tailor precipitate type and distribution for optimal 
oxidation and mechanical performance. Achieving a synergistic balance 
between superior high-temperature mechanical properties and oxida -
tion resistance remains a significant challenge. Furthermore, oxidation 
processes involve atomic-scale charge transfer and energy trans -
formations, which cannot be fully elucidated through experimental 
methods alone [ 44 , 45 ]. First-principles calculations provide a powerful 
tool for predicting the migration energy barrier of oxygen atoms on the 
surface of HEAs, and electron transfer processes at the atomic level [ 46 ,
47 ], offering deeper insights into the oxidation mechanisms of alloys.
In this study, we systematically investigated the influence of B2 and 
L1
2 
precipitates on the high-temperature performance of CoCrFeNi- 
based HEAs by adjusting Al and Ti content. The microstructural evolu -
tion, thermal stability, and mechanical properties at high-temperature 
conditions were thoroughly analyzed. Scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were employed to 
observe and analyze the oxidation behavior and structural stability. 
Room-temperature and high-temperature tensile tests were conducted 
to evaluate the mechanical properties of HEAs strengthened by different 
types of precipitates. Furthermore, first-principles calculations were 
performed to investigate the oxidation mechanisms at the atomic scale. 
This work offers theoretical and experimental insights to support the 
design of high-performance alloys for extreme service environments.

2.5. First-principles calculations
All calculations in this study were performed using density func -
tional theory (DFT) with the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method 
[ 48 ] to generate pseudopotentials, as implemented in the Vienna Ab 
initio Simulation Package (VASP) [ 49 , 50 ]. The structures of the HEAs 
were generated using the Alloy Theoretic Automated Toolkit (ATAT) 
and modeled using the special quasi-random structure (SQS) approach 
[ 51 , 52 ]. The exchange-correlation functional was treated within the 
generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the 
Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) form [ 53 ]. Monkhorst-Pack grids were 
employed for k-point sampling, and ionic relaxations were optimized 
using the conjugate gradient method. A plane-wave basis set with a 
cutoff energy of 400 eV was utilized for all calculations. The conver -
gence criteria for energy and atomic forces were set to 10
-6 
eV and 10
-2 
eV/Å, respectively. To eliminate periodic interactions, a vacuum layer of 
10 Å was introduced along the z-direction in the surface models. Tran -
sition states were investigated using the climbing image nudged elastic 
band (CI-NEB) method [ 54 , 55 ] to determine the minimum energy path 
and corresponding diffusion barriers, with transition states confirmed 
through frequency calculations.
