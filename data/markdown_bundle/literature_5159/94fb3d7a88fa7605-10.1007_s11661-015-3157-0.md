Maintaining High Strength at High Temperature
in a Mg-Y-Zn-Gd Alloy by Heat Treatments
and Severe Deformation Processing
GERARDO GARCE´S, MARI´A A. MUN˜ OZ-MORRIS, DAVID G. MORRIS,
PABLO PEREZ, and PALOMA ADEVA
The evolution of mechanical properties between room temperature and 573 K (300 /C176 C) has been
studied in a Mg-Y-Zn-Gd alloy under diﬀerent heat treated and thermomechanically processed
conditions. The diﬀerent distributions of long period stacking ordered phase and precipitation of
lamellar Gd-rich phase, modiﬁed by severe deformation processing, lead to strengths ranging
between 112 and 175 MPa at 573 K (300 /C176 C). Microstructures have been evaluated under
diﬀerent processing conditions and comparison made with a similar alloy without Gd previously
studied. Although the alloys can achieve similar room temperature strengths, only the
Gd-containing alloy can retain suﬃcient high strength at temperatures above 473 K (200 /C176 C).
This is due to the strengthening eﬀect of the lamellar Gd-rich precipitates that are broken and
redistributed during the severe deformation processing.

I. INTRODUCTION
MG alloys containing yttrium and diﬀerent rare
earth additions have been extensively studied in the past
decade with the aim of improving their mechanical
strength and creep properties up to temperatures of
523 K (250 /C176 C).[1–9] More recently there has been
considerable interest in the development of magnesium
alloys containing yttrium or rare earth elements leading
to the formation of LPSO (Long Period Ordered
Structure) phases. [10–16] Some of these alloys, produced
by casting and extrusion, have successfully reached yield
strengths of about 350 MPa at room temperature
decreasing substantially at 473 K (200 /C176 C).[17–19] The
high strength of these alloys is due to their complex
microstructures produced by the extrusion process. The
LPSO phase is deformed and fractured, appearing
elongated along the extrusion direction, while the Mg
matrix has a bimodal grain structure, with some areas of
dynamically recrystallized grains (DRX) and others of
coarse, deformed, and textured grains. At room tem-
perature, the LPSO phase and non-recrystallized Mg
grains contribute to enhance the mechanical strength
while the randomly oriented DRX Mg grains contribute
to improve ductility. [17,20,21] The volume fraction and
size of DRX grains can be modiﬁed by the thermome-
chanical processing [20,21] leading to changes in mechan-
ical properties.
Severe deformation by equal channel angular pressing
(ECAP) was recently used to process a Mg-Y-Zn alloy
at several temperatures and led to volume fractions of
DRX grains ranging from 25 to 75 pct with grain sizes
between 600 nm and 1.1 lm. For the best microstruc-
tural conditions, yield stresses over 400 MPa were
obtained at room temperature. [21] While grain reﬁne-
ment is very eﬀective for increasing room temperature
strength, retaining strength at high temperature implies
that grain growth is avoided, [22–24] either by precipitat-
ing ﬁne particles or by breaking down larger ones during
processing. The addition of gadolinium to Mg-Y-Zn
alloys can be used for this purpose since this element has
been shown to form LPSO phase in Mg-Gd-Zn alloys by
heat treatments of cast alloys. [25,26] The addition of
gadolinium promotes the formation of ﬁne lamellar
precipitates preserving the LPSO phase. The LPSO
formed during heat treatments in Mg-Gd-Zn alloys has
a 14H crystallographic structure and can exhibit a
stacking fault (SF)-like morphology, a plate shape or a
block shape, depending on aging temperature and
time.[26] It has been reported that the plate-shaped
LPSO phase and/or the solute-segregated SF that
precipitate coherently in the a-Mg matrix before extru-
sion can enhance high temperature strength as it
promotes the retention of non-recrystallized grains with
strong ﬁber texture with the basal plane parallel to the
extrusion direction. [27] On the other hand, if the ﬁne
plates are broken down into ﬁne particles during
thermomechanical processing, they may help nucleate
and retain ﬁne grain sizes in the DRX zones and,
consequently, help retain high strength at high
temperature.

II. EXPERIMENTAL
The material studied had a composition (atomic
percent) of 96pctMg-2pctY-1pctZn-1pctGd, designated
WZ72Gd1. (Atomic percentage will be used to indicate
chemical composition throughout this publication.)
Comparison will be made with a Gd-free version of
composition 97pctMg-2pctY-1pctZn, designated WZ72.
Both alloys were prepared by melting in an electric
furnace under a protective salt ﬂux using high purity
elemental Mg and Zn and Mg-Y and Mg-Gd master
alloys. Ingots were cast into a steel mold of diameter
42 mm, suitable for extrusion.
A variety of heat treatments and conditions for
extrusion and severe plastic deformation processing by
ECAP was employed, with conditions and material
terminology summarized in Table I. As-cast WZ72Gd1
samples were extruded at 623 K (350 /C176 C) at a ram speed
of 30 mm/min and reduction ratio of 18:1 (designated
R18, total strain e
T = 2.9). Some R18-extruded samples
were given homogenization treatments of 8 hours at
808 K (535 /C176 C) in a magnesia bed and cooled either by
an oil quench (designated R18+TT-Q) or by a slow
furnace cool at 10 K/min (designated state
R18+TT-SC). Other cast samples were given the same
homogenization treatment, followed by slow cooling,
before extrusion at 623 K (350 /C176 C) with an extrusion
ratio of 4:1 (designated TT+R4, total strain e

Such extrusion produced bars of diameter 20 mm which
could be processed by ECAP, at 573 K (300 /C176 C), up to
four and eight passes, designated TT+R4+4Ep and
TT+R4+8Ep, respectively. The ECAP was carried out
in a hydraulic machine using a circular cross section die
of diameter 20 mm with die angle of 118 deg, producing
a true strain of 0.7 per pass, using route A (without
rotation between successive passes). The standard press-
ing speed used was 20 mm/min with a total cycle time
(preheating and pressing) of 10 minutes. Following each
ECAP pass, the heated split-die was opened hydrauli-
cally for rapid sample removal and water quenching.
WZ72Gd1 material processed in such ways will be
compared with WZ72 processed similarly, see Table I.

C. Inﬂuence of Microstructure on Room and High
Temperature Strength
The most important diﬀerences in microstructures
between the two alloys can be seen to be associated with
the fraction of LPSO phase precipitated out in the form
of thin plates in WZ72Gd1 alloy during the homoge-
nizing annealing treatment and slow cooling prior to
extrusion or ECAP processing, see Figure 1(b). These
thin plates accelerate the recrystallization process by
providing more interfaces for the nucleation of new
grains, see Figure 2(c), and their fracture into ﬁne
particles, about 150 nm in size, during ECAP processing
contributes to an increase of the fraction recrystallized
and a reduction in size of the ﬁne DRX grains compared
to those in WZ72 alloy. These various microstructural
aspects are expected to contribute to an increase in room
temperature strength and also help maintain strength at
high temperatures.
The mechanical property data shown in Table II
conﬁrm that at room temperature not only the yield
stress but also the UTS and tensile ductility are generally
higher in WZ72Gd1 than in WZ72 alloy, although the
diﬀerence is often not very large. Notably, when
considering material given 8 ECAP passes, alloy WZ72
has no tensile ductility while the Gd-containing alloy has
a ductility of 4.5 pct. This is believed to be related to the
larger volume fraction of ﬁne recrystallized grains in this
material, 62 pct, as compared to 50 pct in WZ72.
[18]
After four ECAP passes, the Gd-containing alloy has a
larger fraction of recrystallized grains (49 pct, as com-
pared to 38 pct) and higher yield stress and UTS values,
with similar ductilities achieved in both cases. The
higher strength is expected to be related to the smaller
grain size of the recrystallized regions (420 nm, as
compared to 620 nm), and also to the contribution of
ﬁne particles produced by fracture of the ﬁne LPSO
plates.

Comparison between the two alloys in Figures 6 and
7 helps understand the eﬀect that the precipitation and
fracture of the LPSO plates during processing has on
room and high temperature strength of the materials.
After processing by eight ECAP passes, both alloys have
similar yield stress at room temperature, see Figure 6(b).
While both materials have a similar amount of recrys-
tallization, alloy WZ72 has a much larger contribution
from the matrix with its deformed grains, see Figure 7,
even though it has no ﬁne particle strengthening. This
suggests that the greater matrix strength arises from the
diﬃculty of deforming the large textured grains and this
creates a higher dislocation density during severe defor-
mation. The yield stress values of the Gd-free alloy in
this state decrease much faster between 473 K and
573 K (200 /C176 C and 300 /C176 C) than in the Gd-containing
alloy, see Figure 6(b), which suggests that faster recov-
ery is taking place in the deformed grains. Thus, the
WZ72Gd1 alloy retains a high strength of 375 MPa at
473 K (200 /C176 C), which decreases to 112 MPa at 573 K
(300 /C176 C), as compared with strengths of 260 and
50 MPa for the WZ72 alloy. Although a discussion of
the possible high temperature deformation mechanisms
responsible for the diﬀerent strengths of the two alloys
at high temperatures is outside the scope of the present
study, it is evident that the ﬁne (150 nm) particles
present in the Gd-containing alloy are responsible for
the strength retention, representing obstacles which
hinder dislocation motion and recovery.
In addition to their direct role in strengthening, the
ﬁne particles clearly help also to inhibit grain growth
(see Figures 3(d), 4(b) and (c)), both during processing
and during high temperature testing. For example, the
recrystallized zones in the Gd-containing alloy after
ECAP have grain sizes of 0.41 lm initially, which
change only slightly, to 0.55 and 0.7 lm after
mechanical testing at 473 K and 573 K (200 /C176 C and
300 /C176 C), respectively. On the other hand, grains are
somewhat coarser in the WZ72 alloy after equivalent
processing, changing from 0.6 lm after ECAP to 1.2 lm
for testing at 573 K (300 /C176 C), which presumably con-
tributes to the decrease in yield stress at high temper-
ature of this material. It is also worth noting that the
higher volume fraction of the LPSO blocky phase in the
Gd-free alloy does little to improve its high temperature
strength, conﬁrming that it is the ﬁnely fractured
particles that are important for strengthening. Finally,
while ECAP processing is the best for achieving high
room temperature strength, for both alloys it is the R4
extruded state which retains the best strength at the
highest temperatures.
IV. CONCLUSIONS
Severe deformation processing improves substantially
the room temperature strength of both Mg-base alloys
examined here, but is associated with signiﬁcant strength
loss at high temperature as recovery and recrystalliza-
tion occur.
A homogenization treatment before processing has no
eﬀect on room temperature strength, but allows strength
retention at high temperature, especially at 573 K
(300 /C176 C).
The volume fraction of coarse blocky LPSO phase
contributes only a small amount to the yield stress, while
the fracturing of ﬁne plate precipitates leads to an
increase in the volume fraction of DRX regions and a
reduction of its grain size. The ﬁne particles serve to
improve strength in both the DRX regions (by retaining
ﬁne grain size) and the unrecrystallized matrix (by
particle hardening). Such particles lead to good hard-
ening in the low temperature range [room temperature
to 473 K (200 /C176 C)] but are not suﬃcient to retain
strength at 573 K (300 /C176 C). In the Gd-free alloy, which
has no precipitates, strength of material processed by
ECAP falls rapidly at high temperatures.
The best strength at high temperatures is shown by
material extruded to the R4 state, where particles
fractured to an intermediate size appear to be the best
to delay recovery in the deformed unrecrystallized
regions.
