1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are a new class of multicomponent solid 
solution (SS) alloys that have gained attention in recent years. Such 
alloys were initially defined as those alloys containing five or more 
principal elements in equal or nearly equal atomic ratio, and each has a 
concentration from 5 at% to 35 at%. Another definition of HEAs was also 
proposed, which says that the alloys can be known as HEAs if their 
configurational entropies are higher than 1.5R and it is much higher 
than traditional alloys in the liquid state of in the random SS state (R is 
the gas constant). The idea is that it is possible to stabilize a single-phase 
solid solution by reducing Gibbs free energy, avoiding the formation of 
undesirable intermetallic phases [ 1 – 10 ]. Because of their excellent 
properties, HEAs are suitable for numerous industrial applications. 
In particular, FeCoNiCrMn-series alloys have attracted much 
attention for their prominent mechanical properties at high tempera -
tures [ 11 – 13 ]. However, the density of FeCoNiCrMn-series HEAs is 
approximately 8.1 g/cm
3
. Because of the demand for energy conserva -
tion from transportation industries, the development of novel light -
weight HEAs with high specific strength (strength/density) has become 
a trending topic within the communities of materials science and engi -
neering [ 14 – 22 ]. Nevertheless, lightweight HEAs are less investigated, 
because of challenges such as limited numbers of light, nontoxic, and 
inexpensive elements. In recent years, most designs of lightweight HEAs 
have contained Al or Ti in high concentrations to result in lower density 
and a multiple-phase microstructure containing SS and intermetallic 
phases. Only a few compositions can form single-phase SSs [ 7 , 9 , 11 , 13 , 
15 ]. 
Although some reported lightweight HEAs exhibit outstanding me -
chanical properties, such alloys typically still possess higher density

2.2. Computational methodologies 
A phase diagram is a foundation of basic material research and serves 
as a road map for material design and process optimization because it is 
the starting point for the manipulation of processing variables to attain 
desired microstructures. Phase diagrams are primarily composed after 
experimentation. Although this approach is both feasible and necessary 
for determining the phase equilibria of binaries and of ternaries over 
limited compositional regions, it is nearly impossible to obtain phase 
diagrams of ternary or higher order systems over wide ranges of com -
positions and temperatures. To assist in the design of multicomponent 
alloys, it is necessary to utilize the CALPHAD approach to obtain the 
phase distribution for a multicomponent system before experimentation 
[ 32 , 33 ]. 
In the CALPHAD approach, the parameters of thermodynamic 
models are obtained for the Gibbs energies of the constituent phases in 
lower order systems — binaries and ternaries — on the basis of known 
thermodynamic and phase equilibrium data. We can then obtain the 
Gibbs energies of multicomponent alloy phases from those of the lower 
order systems through extrapolation. These Gibbs energy values enable 
us to calculate reliable multicomponent phase diagrams. Experimental 
studies are then required only for confirmatory purposes and not for the 
drawing of full diagrams. The basic premise of modeling is that the 
thermodynamic description of a higher order system, that is, one with 
more than three components, can be obtained through extrapolation 
from those of lower order systems, usually binaries and ternaries. The 
database applied in this study was verified using six binaries and some 
parts of ternary systems; no quaternary phases were included. In this 
study, the available thermodynamic parameters of six binary systems 
(Ti – Al, Ti – Cr, Ti – Nb, Al – Cr, Al – Nb, and Cr – Nb) [ 34 – 39 ] were adopted. 
Ternary interaction parameters of Ti – Al – Cr [ 40 ] and Ti – Al – Nb [ 41 ] 
were included in this quaternary database. 
The solution phases, that is, liquid, BCC ( β Ti), face-centered cubic 
(FCC), and hexagonal close-packed (HCP) ( α Ti), were described by a 
substitutional solution model, and the excess Gibbs energy of the solu -
tion phases was described by the Redlich – Kister – Muggianu polynomial 
[ 42 ]. For the intermetallic compounds, we applied a simple line com -
pound or sublattice model. These models and their interaction param -
eters were mainly extrapolated from subbinary and subternary systems.

2.3. Microstructure characterization 
The samples were characterized through X-ray diffraction (XRD, 
Bruker D2) for phase identification by using Cu K α radiation. The 
morphology of the polished samples after etching was examined using 
an optical microscope (OM, Olympus TH4-100) and electron backscatter 
diffraction (EBSD, Oxford AztechHKL, Channel 5) to observe the grain 
structure and confirm the single-phase state. Then, the grain size was 
calculated using the average grain intercept method. Moreover, scan -
ning electron microscopy (UHR FE-SEM, Hitachi SU8220) coupled with 
energy-dispersive spectrometry was conducted to examine the phase 
structure and analyze the chemical composition of each alloy. High- 
resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JOEL JEM 2100 
and FEI EO Tecnai F20 G2 field-emission transmission electron micro -
scope operated at 200 keV) was conducted to characterize the interior 
microstructure of each grain. A dual-beam focused ion beam system (FEI 
Versa 3D FEG FIB, operated at 30 kV) was used to prepare the TEM 
samples from the interior part of the as-cast alloys, with special care to 
minimize the ion damage to the foil TEM samples.
