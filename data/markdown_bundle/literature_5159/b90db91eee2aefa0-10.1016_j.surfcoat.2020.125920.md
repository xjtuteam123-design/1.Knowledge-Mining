ABSTRACT
Phase and element composition, microhardness, adhesive strength, wear resistance and microstructure of the
coatings and underlying layers formed on WC-Co hard alloy using Nb and Mo vacuum-arc sources were studied.
It is shown that the inﬂ uence of ion beams with energy of 1 keV created by Nb and Mo vacuum-arc sources for at
least 1 –2 min allows the formation of carbide coatings based on embedded metals and alloy elements with
(Nb,W)C0.7,M o2C and Co 6W6C2 phases. The synthesis of carbide layers occurred by ion exposure by Nb or Mo at
a surface temperature of the hard alloy of at least 1300 °C, controlled by an optical thermograph. Reducing of Nb
and Mo ion exposure time down to 30 s does not allow heating of samples surface to temperatures above 700 °C,
as a result, the formation of carbide layers on the alloy surface does not occur. The plasma deposition of NbC or
MoC coatings on sublayers created by preliminary Nb or Mo ion treatment accompanied by heating of the sample
surface up to 1300° С forms carbide layered system with hardness of 60 GPa, adhesive strength of 120 –140 N and
2–3-fold lower volume wear compared to NbC and MoC plasma coatings deposited on a hard alloy without
preliminary treatment.
1. Introduction
To increase the wear resistance of cutting carbide tools used in
various ﬁelds of industry, the possibilities of new types of hard coatings
having a multiphase composition, a multilayer structure, the nano-
crystalline structure of the entire coating or its layers are actively stu-
died. Using various vacuum deposition methods, nanostructured coat-
ings were synthesized, consisting of crystallites of TiN, ZrN, (Zr,Al,Cr)N
nitride ceramics and others with sizes < 10 nm, which are separated by
disordered or amorphous nanolayers of carbon, Si 3N4, etc. [ 1–5].
Multilayer coatings with nanocrystalline structure are also formed from
such nanocomposites. Such coatings acquire high hardness values
(≥40 GPa). The increase in hardness of multilayer coatings with na-
nocrystalline structure is due to the size of nanocrystallites and the
optimal ratio of the volume fraction of interphase boundaries between
them. Also, an increase in the hardness of such coatings is a ﬀected by
internal stresses in them.
It is known that increasing the wear resistance of a coated material
in practical applications is determined not only by the hardness of the
coating. Mechanical stresses (internal, residual) in the coating, re-
sistance to cracking, adhesion strength of the coating to the substrate,
as well as interlayer, intergranular strength, etc. have a great inﬂ uence
on the wear resistance [ 4,5].
One of the advantages of the vacuum-arc method in relation to other
methods of vacuum deposition of coatings is a high degree of ionization
of the arc plasma and the possibility of exposure to ionized plasma on
the surface of the samples providing cleaning, heating and the forma-
tion of a metal sublayer to ensure adhesion before subsequent plasma
deposition of the coating [ 6–9]. It should be noted that coatings that
improve the operational properties of cutting woodworking carbide
tools should have a special set of properties: low friction coe ﬃcient,
high hardness, adhesion, oxidative and chemical resistance to interac-
tion with oxygen and components of wood-containing materials
[10,11]. To implement a complex of these properties, a layered coating
architecture can be used [ 2–4,11,12]. The possibility of using oxide
chromium, aluminum, or other types of antifriction and chemically
resistant layers as upper coating layer providing a low coe ﬃcient of dry
friction and oxidation resistance is studied [ 10–12]. The main coating
layer with adhesive transition layers should provide resistance to de-
formation, cracking, wear. To strengthen the wood-cutting tool, it is
possible to use coatings made of carbide ceramics, which are not widely
used in metalworking due to an increase in the friction coe ﬃcient

It was shown [ 13] that new types of composite hard alloys based on
niobium and tungsten carbide can signi ﬁcantly increase the tool wear
resistance compared to the known alloys based on tungsten carbide and
cobalt. It is also known that carbide coatings of transition metals have
high hardness, chemical inertness, and thermal stability [ 14].
Promising coatings for increasing of carbide wood-cutting tool wear
resistance could be high-hard multilayer thermostable carbide coatings
formed in a single technological cycle of vacuum-arc ion bombardment
and plasma deposition. Studies of the mechanical properties of niobium
carbide coatings formed by the vacuum-arc method on a WC carbide
tool showed the possibility of creating layered carbide coatings having
a hardness of up to 50– 60 GPa [ 15]. The hardness of these coatings
exceeds the hardness of various types of nitride coatings with a nano-
crystalline structure, layered coating structure those formed by the
vacuum-arc method, but without creating special thick hard sublayers
on the surface [ 16–20]. Insuﬃcient attention is paid to the e ﬀect of the
formation of special hard sublayers between WC-Co hard alloys and
coatings. Usually, the preliminary sublayers created before applying the
main coating are made in the form of soft metal “binder” layers or in
the form of a metal layer/nitride layer system [ 7,16–20]. It was shown
in the works [ 21,22] that heating of a hard alloy surface under the
inﬂuence of electron or ion beams creates solid hardening surface layers
in a hard alloy. This can occur as a result of melting or as a result of the
diﬀusion interaction of WC and Co carbide grains at lower exposure
temperatures. Cathode-arc sources of refractory metals in the ion
bombardment mode allow the creation of ion ﬂuxes su ﬃcient to heat
samples in a wide temperature range, depending on the modes of use
and exposure time [ 7]. Molybdenum, like niobium, has a high chemical
aﬃnity for carbon; their carbides can form solid solutions with WC
[23]. It is assumed that the use of vacuum-arc sources of Mo and Nb in
the ion bombardment mode will allow one to reach the di ﬀusion in-
teraction temperatures of WC carbide grains with Co to form a har-
dened layer. At the same time, ion implantation of Nb or Mo into the
heated layer will occur. This will stimulate the formation of new types
of carbide phases that increase the hardness of the formed layer. The
high temperature of alloy samples before the plasma deposition of
carbide coatings will reduce the level of residual and internal stresses of
these coatings. High hardness plasma coatings of molybdenum and
niobium carbides will be deposited on the solid surface layer created by
the ionic and temperature e ﬀects. As a result, a layered system of re-
fractory carbide coatings with high mechanical and adhesive properties
on a hard alloy will be formed. Thus, the aim of the work was to study
the phase and element composition, microhardness, adhesive strength,
wear resistance and microstructure of a system of layered refractory
carbide coatings formed with hard sublayers on a WC-Co hard alloy
using vacuum-arc sources of Mo or Nb in the modes of ion bombard-
ment and plasma deposition.
2. Experimental details
The hard alloy on which the coatings were applied was an industrial
design of the knives of a woodworking tool. A feature of the hard alloy
is
a low Co content (3 at.%), WC grain size is in the range of 0.2 –0.8 μm.
The hard alloy has a hardness of 17 GPa. The process of forming layered
carbide coatings consisted of two steps. First step is ion bombardment
for 1 and 2 min with an energy of 1 keV using Nb, Mo cathode-arc
sources on a VU-2MBS installation. The second step is the coating de-
position, was made at a methane pressure of 10 –1 Pa for 5 min. The bias
was – 200 V. Cathode arc current was 180 А for Nb and Mo. The density
of the ion ﬂux on the samples using the Nb cathode was 1.8 –2.0 mA; for
the Mo cathode; 1.7 –2.2 mA.
To increase the temperature of the samples during coating forma-
tion, the tool samples were placed at a small distance from the center of
the cathodes; the special fastening of the samples reducing the heat
removal from them to the sample holder was used. The temperature
ﬁelds on the surface of the samples during niobium and molybdenum
ion bombardment and the subsequent coating deposition were mon-
itored with a high-temperature thermograph [ 24]. In the temperature
measurement range of 800– 1700 °C, the thermograph ensured high
accuracy of temperature measurement, the error did not exceed 5 °C.
The time interval for setting the thermograph on the surface of the
samples under study took no > 10 s. Experimental measurements
showed that the decrease in the recorded temperature values due to
absorption of radiation by the window of the vacuum chamber is
180–210 °C. This corrective was further taken into account when
measuring the surface temperature of carbide tools.
The study of the phase composition of the coatings was carried out
with glancing incidence of X-rays on the surface with angle of 5°. The
microstresses in the formed coatings were determined by X-ray dif-
fraction studies using the Holder-Wagner technique.
To study the changes in the microstructure and concentration of
elements in the coating and in underlying layers of the hard alloy, the
cross–section patterns were made. SEM- images of cross –section pat-
terns surface were obtained using a scanning electron microscope (LEO
1455 VP). Element composition of cross-section patterns surface was
determined by X-ray microanalysis. The microhardness of the samples
was measured by the Knupp and Vickers methods on a Wilson
Instruments 402MVD instrument at load of 1 N.
Scratch tests were carried out with 0.5 mm radius diamond in-
denter. The scratch length was about 20 mm. The track after scratching
was analyzed using an optical microscope to determine the minimum
load at which cracks or peeled pieces of coatings became noticeable in
the track. The determined load value corresponds to the critical load of
the beginning of peeling of the coating.
To determine the wear resistance of the formed coatings, tribolo-
gical tests and determination of the speci ﬁc volumetric wear were
carried out. The tests were carried out on a scratch tester with a re-
ciprocating movement of a diamond indenter sample with a 0.5 mm
rounding on the surface under a load of 20 N, 10 mm long at a speed of
20 mm/min for 30 min. The wear track formed on the tool surface was
measured by a pro ﬁlometer (Mahr Surf SD26) and then the average
speciﬁc volumetric wear was determined from pro ﬁlograms in di ﬀerent
places of wear track.
We introduce the designations of studied samples depending on the
type of vacuum arc source, the time of ion treatment, the presence or
absence of subsequent plasma deposition by an arc source. For ion
treatment only with an Nb or Mo arc source for 1 min – Nb_1 or Mo_1
respectively; 2 min – Nb_2 or Mo_2. For subsequent plasma deposition
and preliminary ion treatment of Nb or Mo for 0.5 min – Nb_C or Mo_C,
1 min – Nb_1C
or Mo_1C, 2 min – Nb_2C or Mo_2C.

Niobium ion exposure with 1 and 2 min forms a layer containing

layer has a darker gray contrast compared to the WC ( Fig. 2, а, с), this
layer is indicated in the ﬁgures by the letter B. The tungsten content in

crystalline grains in the layer. Therefore, the layer has a nanocrystalline
structure.
A feature of the SEM image of the subsequent layer of the hard alloy
is increase of the area of dark spots in which the cobalt content is
greater than the average cobalt content in the initial alloy ( Figs. 2, 3 ).
The area of bright spots corresponding to WC grains decreases. This
layer in the ﬁgures is indicated by the letter C. The element composition
of the cross-section patterns indicates an increase in the cobalt content
in this layer ( Fig. 3).
In the case of Mo ions bombardment for 1 and 2 min the thickness of
the surface layer is smaller compared to Nb bombardment. This layer is
indicated in the ﬁgures by the letter B. This di ﬀerence is especially
noticeable when comparing the e ﬀects of both ion types within 1 min. It
is assumed that the decrease in the thickness of the surface layers of
molybdenum created by ion exposure is due to the higher ionic density
of the cathode-arc source Mo compared to the source Nb. As a result of
the use of the Mo source, a predominance of ion surface sputter over
deposition and ion imlantation. The thicknesses and compositions of
the cobalt-rich layers formed by the action of Mo ions are similar to
those of Nb. The same heating temperatures during Nb and Mo ions
exposure for 1 and 2 min are the main reason for the similarity of the
composition and thickness of the sublayers enriched by cobalt.

Table 1 shows the mechanical properties and microstresses of
layered carbide coatings on a hard alloy depending on the time of ion

plasma deposition. The hardness of samples corresponding only to the
long-term ionic bombardment by Nb and Mo vacuum-arc sources

The Table 1 for comparison presents data for samples Nb_C and
Mo_C for which the pre-ion treatment time did not exceed 30 s. The
hardness of these plasma carbide coatings without specially formed
sublayers is in the range of 40– 45 GPa, the thickness is not < 3 μm.
High microstress values for these coatings (4 –7 GPa) determine their
high hardness, however, the ﬂaking load and wear are inferior to
layered coatings formed with longer times of ion bombardment by arc
sources.
Note that the values of the mechanical properties for Nb_C and Mo_C
samples with a short ion treatment time ( Table 1) are similar to the
mechanical properties of nitride coatings formed by the vacuum-arc
method. For example, the results of work on the formation of NbN on a
hard alloy show the hardness of the created coatings in the range of
20–40 GPa, depending on the composition of the nitride phases [ 18].
The adhesion of the hardest coatings was poor. SEM-images of cross-
section patterns surface have shown the absence of sublayers for de-
posited coatings. Vacuum-arc single-phase NbC, NbN coatings produced
using ﬁltered cathodic arc plasma deposition processes achieve a
hardness of 40 GPa [ 15]. The paper [ 19] shows an increase in the
hardness of or multiphase coatings of (Nb,Ti)N, (Nb,Zr)N up to 50 GPa
compared to single-phase coatings of the Nb eN system, having a
hardness in the range of 34– 40 GPa. In these articles, the relationship
between the hardness values of the coatings and the level of macros-
tresses in them is shown. In the studies described above, the tempera-
ture of the samples during coating deposition was in the range of
400–580 °C.
The microstresses for layered carbide coatings formed using longer
ion treatment by arc sources and plasma deposition do not exceed
1.5 GPa ( Table 1 ). In our opinion, a decrease in the magnitude of mi-
crostresses for layered carbide coatings in comparison with single-layer
carbide coatings on a hard alloy occurs as a result of the inﬂ uence of a
longer ion treatment and a higher temperature during their formation.

4. Conclusions
Carbide layered coatings with high hardness, adhesion and wear
resistance are formed on WC-Co hard alloy using vacuum-arc tech-
nology.
It is shown that the in ﬂuence of ion beams with energy of 1 keV
created by Nb and Mo vacuum-arc sources operating at arc currents of
180 A for at least 1– 2 min allows the formation of carbide coatings
based on embedded metals and alloy elements with (Nb,W)C 0.7,M o 2C
and Co 6W6C2 phases. The thickness of the (Nb,W)C 0.7 and Mo 2C coat-
ings does not exceed one micron. This occurs under the in ﬂuence of
increase in the samples surface temperature up to 1300° С, which sti-
mulates the di ﬀusion interaction of embedded Nb and Mo ions, and Co
with WC alloy carbides. Reducing of Nb and Mo ion exposure time
down to 30 s does not allow heating of samples surface to temperatures
above 700 °C. Thus the formation of carbide coatings similar to those
described above is not detected.
