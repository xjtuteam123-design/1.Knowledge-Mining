ABSTRACT 
Refractory alloys with their brilliant high-temperature tolerance earn a place for applications in extremely harsh 
environments. Recent development in additive manufacturing technologies speeds up these alloys ’ design and 
forming processes before entering the market. However, the desire for better microstructures and superior high- 
temperature mechanical behaviors has never been fed up. As was wished, in this study, an additively manu -
factured WTaRe alloy with compressive strength, ultimate compressive strength, and strain of 356 ± 15 MPa, 
731 ± 15 MPa, and > 40% at 1600 
◦
C is herein developed. Composition characterization after deformation of the 
as-deposited WTaRe alloy suggests the super high thermal stability of this alloy. Considering the excellent high- 
temperature mechanical behaviors, the printable WTaRe refractory alloy may serve as an alternative in a high- 
temperature environment. 
1. Introduction 
Refractory alloys are well known for their superb thermal resistance 
at elevated temperatures [ 1 – 3 ], thereby are constantly applied in fields 
such as aerospace, nuclear reactors, and other power-generation sectors 
[ 4 , 5 ]. Demands of the potential technological progress in these afore -
mentioned fields regard refractory alloys as attractive candidates for 
fulfilling their future requirements in materials. Since refractory alloys 
intensively resist further machining with their high hardness and 
intrinsic brittle nature, thereby exerting impediments upon the popu -
larization of these conventional manufacturing methods, see casting and 
powder metallurgy. Tremendous efforts with high efficiency have been 
paid to fabricating these refractory alloys, particularly additive 
manufacturing of components demanding complex geometries and 
flexible designs [ 6 , 7 ]. 
In recent years, progress in developing new refractory alloys with 
strong thermal resistance inspired the arrival of the landmark WMo -
TaNb and WMoTaNbV multi-principal element refractory alloys (MPE -
RAs) [ 2 ]. However, limited by the intrinsic brittleness at room 
temperature, further industrialization of these alloys will naturally be 
confined to a small scale [ 8 – 10 ]. Attempts to alter the intrinsic brittle -
ness and promote the manufacturing efficiency of these alloys pave the 
way for combining alloying and 3D printing in product customization 
[ 11 – 13 ]. Hitherto, almost all the additively manufactured WMoTaNb 
and WMoTaNb-based MPERAs performed poorly in plastic deformation 
at ambient temperature, which demonstrated that the formation of re -
fractory alloys with complex geometries is not readily applicable for 
practical applications. To date, searching for printable refractory alloys 
with both excellent high-temperature and room-temperature mechani -
cal behaviors remains challenging.

4. Discussion 
On the basis of these microstructural features, there must follow an 
underlying mechanism that is critical to the strong thermal resistance of 
the as-deposited WTaRe alloy at 1600 
◦
C. As was discovered in the 
microstructure of the as-deposited WTaRe alloy, cellular structures 
consisting of dislocations and solutes can play an important role in 
deformation at high temperatures. Therefore, the unified hindering 
walls established by the previously formed and well-organized disloca -
tion boundaries and solutes, pose overwhelmingly robust barriers to the 
intrinsically hard diffusion as well as climbing and movement of newly 
generated or already existing dislocations. As was experimentally veri -
fied in BCC refractory alloys, nonscrew character dislocations dominate 
the high-temperature strength [ 35 – 37 ]. Nevertheless, dynamic recovery 
under such high-temperature can substantially annihilate some newly 
generated dislocations, leaving defects-free regions where sub- 
structures cannot reach. Obviously, these defects-free regions in 
conjunction with the dislocations liberated from the cellular structures, 
generated in deformation, and dislocation climbing, consistently pro -
longed the stage and enlarged the spread range for dislocation move -
ment, which eventually account for increased strain in plastic 
deformation. To further uncover the underlying mechanism of defor -
mation at such high temperatures, a diffusion-controlled influential 
factor cannot be ignored. Investigation of the diffusion behavior during 
deformation suggests a ~ 0.5 – 0.6T

controlling deformation occurs [ 38 ]. The as-calculated ~0.5 – 0.6T

The initial dynamic equilibrium in a stable hardening rate consists of

strengthening mechanism. Microstructural observation envisioned that 
deformed cellular structures and these nearby dislocations are evidence 
for the ever-existed strengthening process during the deformation of the 
as-deposited WTaRe alloy ( Figs. 5 (d) and 5 (e)). 
Yin and coworkers simulated dislocation-short range ordering in -
teractions to clarify how it enhances high-temperature behaviors of 
WMoTaNb refractory alloy [ 41 ], which inspires a mechanism through 
impeding dislocation motion. Recent research on the creep behavior of 
single-phase nickel ‑ cobalt ‑ chromium alloys brings the diffusion- 
inhibiting mechanism into remaining high-temperature strength with 
the nanosized grain boundary networks [ 42 ], and convinces that the 
strengthening effect was enhanced by dislocation-grain boundary net -
works interaction. The microstructure of the as-deformed WTaRe alloy is 
shown in Fig. 8 (a). Different from many refractory alloys, WTaRe alloy 
has a strong thermal resistance at 1600 
◦

structure exhibits severely distorted cellular structures with dislocations 
moving around them. There stand many cellular structures that resist 
high-temperature diffusion and dislocation campaigns. The uniaxial 
loads applied under elevated temperatures appear to introduce strong 
texture to the microstructure, which deformed the grains and compelled 
the sub-structures (cellular structures) to experience a severe alteration 
in shape ( Fig. 5 (d) and Fig. 8 (b)). Subsequently, the dislocations and 
cellular structures divorced in the end to cause even larger strain 
hardening when approaching the strain of 50%. The dislocations 
repelled from the cellular structures glide and climb under driving force 
from the remained high-temperature regime and loads ( Fig. 5 (e) and 
Fig. 8 (c)). The presence of dislocation tangles and densification also 
strengthened the BCC matrix ( Fig. 8 (d)).
