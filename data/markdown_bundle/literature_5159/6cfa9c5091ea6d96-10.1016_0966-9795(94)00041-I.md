Tensile tests were conducted at temperatures ranging from 145-1123 K on four 
different FeAl alloys, containing 40,43,45, and 48 at.% Al, each doped with 0.12 
at.% B. The alloys were initially heat treated to obtain a relatively large grain size 
(~200 pm), after which they were given a long, low-temperature anneal (673 K 
for 5 d), to minimize, respectively, the effects of grain boundary strengthening 
and thermal vacancies on the measured yield strengths. Each alloy displayed bcc- 
type behavior at low temperatures (yield strength decreasing with increasing 
temperature), followed by a strength anomaly at intermediate temperatures (yield 
strength increasing with increasing temperature), and a sharp drop in yield 
strength at elevated temperatures (beyond the anomalous strength peak). Thermal 
vacancies that are generated during the hold time at the test temperature may 
contribute to the production of the strength anomaly. In specimens not given the 
vacancy-minimizing anneal, quenched-in vacancies were found to substantially 
increase low-temperature strength, thereby masking the yield strength anomaly. 
As the Al concentration of FeAl increased, the prominence of the yield strength 
anomaly decreased. Ductility also exhibited a peak at elevated temperatures, first 
increasing with temperature until it reached a maximum value and then decreasing 
with further increases in temperature. The peak in ductility occured at lower 
temperatures as the Al content increased. The fracture mode in all four alloys was 
mixed (intergranular + transgranular) at cryogenic temperatures, predominantly 
intergranular at around room temperature, dimpled rupture at peak ductility, and 
intergranular cavitation at elevated temperatures where the ductility dropped. 
Key words: FeAl, boron-doping, yield strength anomaly, thermal vacancies, 
fracture mode 
INTRODUCTION 
Ordered intermetallics based on FeAl offer many 
desirable properties. Along with a low cost, these 
alloys possess a reasonably high specific modulus 
and strength-to-weight ratio and display good fati- 
gue resistance. 1,2 The FeAl structure exists over a 
wide composition range, approximately 36-50 at.% 
Al,* and retains its B2 structure to relatively high 
temperatures.3 These characteristics, along with its 
superior oxidation and corrosion resistance,4 make 
FeAl an attractive candidate for intermediate tem- 
perature applications in harsh environments. 
However, polycrystalline FeAl alloys, as well as 
single crystals, exhibit brittleness at room tempera- 
ture which limits their use. Recently, Liu et aLe7

the room-temperature mechanical properties of

small amount of B to Fe-40Al increases its ductility 
and increases the amount of transgranular fracture. 
Later, Liu and George7 found using Auger electron 
spectroscopy that boron did, in fact, segregate to 
the grain boundaries, providing a possible mechan- 
ism for the observed grain-boundary strengthening. 
The elevated-temperature tensile behavior of

arc melted and drop cast into copper chill molds.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Yield strength 
The dependence of yield strength on test temperature 
for the four alloys is shown in Fig. 2. As shown in 
Fig. 2(a), the Fe-40Al-0*12B alloy exhibited a 
rapid decrease in yield strength from -430 MPa 
at 146 K to -280 MPa at 473 K, consistent with 
the trend expected for a bee-based structure. 
Above 473 K, the yield strength showed an anom- 
alous increase with increasing temperature to a 
peak of -415 MPa at 823 K. Beyond the peak 
temperature, the yield strength declined sharply. 
These results are broadly similar to the trends 
observed by Xiao and Baker’* in their study 
of large-grained (-250 pm) polycrystalline B-free 
Fe-40Al which had similiar processing conditions 
and thermal histories as the alloys in the present 
study. They showed that the yield strength of 
B-free Fe-40Al first decreased with increasing 
temperature from room temperature to -500 K, 
followed by an anomalous increase with increasing 
temperature to a peak of -325 MPa at 675 K, and 
a sharp decrease thereafter. Despite the overall 
similarity, however, there are three features that 
distinguish the present B-doped alloy from the 
B-free alloy of Xiao and Baker: (a) B-doped Fe- 
40Al displays a higher yield strength throughout 
the temperature range tested, (b) its peak yield 
strength occurs at a higher temperature (~150 K 
higher than in B-free Fe-40Al), and (c) its peak 
is broader than that of B-free Fe-40Al. Since the 
grain sizes, processing conditions, and thermal 
histories of the alloys are similiar, it appears that 
the difference between the two studies is related to 
the presence of boron. 
The temperature dependencies of the yield 
strengths of Fe-43Al-0*12B and Fe-45Al-0.12B 
are shown in Figs 2 (b) and (c). As was the case in 
Fe-40Al-O.l2B, yield strength first decreased with 
increasing temperature, then increased anom- 
alously with increasing temperature to a peak 
value, and finally decreased sharply with further 
increases in temperature beyond the peak. Since 
the anomalous yield strength peak is quite broad, 
and there is considerable uncertainty in defining a 
‘peak temperature’, we can only say that the peak 
occurs somewhere between 800 and 900 K in all 
three alloys, and that there does not appear to be a 
systematic variation with Al concentration between 
40 and 45%. Similarly, the value of the peak stress 
also does not change much with Al concentration, 
falling between 400 and 450 MPa for Al con- 
centrations between 40 and 45%. The valley stress, 
on the other hand, increases from 280 to 340 MPa 
as the Al concentration is increased from 40 to 
45%, making the strength anomaly appear less 
prominent as the stoichiometric composition is 
approached.

needed to understand the detailed mechanisms

the dependence on Al concentration and how it 
compares with that in the B-free FeAl alloys.

The foregoing raises an obvious question: can 
the strength anomaly itself be due to vacancy 
hardening? If one starts with relatively vacancy- 
free FeAl, then a specimen that is tested at the peak 
temperature will have a higher vacancy concentra- 
tion than one that is tested at room temperature 
(because of the excess thermal vacancies generated 
during heat-up to the test temperature). Can this 
higher vacancy concentration be responsible for 
the higher strength at the peak temperature (i.e. for 
the strength anomaly)? Figure 3 hints at this pos- 
sibility: while the excess vacancies in specimens not 
given the long-term anneal result in significant 
strengthening at around room temperature, they 
also increase the strength at the peak temperature. 
In other words, if present in sufficient quantity, 
vacancies can increase not only low-temperature 
strength, but also the strength at around the peak 
temperature. It is not apparent whether enough 
excess vacancies are in fact generated at the peak 
temperature (relative to the vacancy concentration 
already present in specimens given the long-term 
anneal) to account for the strength anomaly. 
Clearly, additional experiments are needed in 
which well-annealed specimens are rapidly heated 
to a temperature in the vicinity of the strength 
anomaly, held for varying lengths of time at this 
temperature, and then tensile tested. If the yield 
strength is found to increase monotonically as a 
function of increasing hold time, it would provide 
strong evidence in support of our proposed 
mechanism in which vacancies are responsible for 
the strength anomaly in FeAl. We are currently in 
the process of performing such experiments at the 
peak temperature and temperatures in the vicinity 
of the strength anomaly in FeAl+B alloys. 
Another approach is to perform tests using a dif- 
ferent sequence of temperatures (instead of begin- 
ning the tests from room temperature, start at a 
higher temperature than the test temperature, e.g. 
hold at 1273 K for 1 h, cool relatively fast to the 
peak temperature, and then pull to fracture). We 
attempted such experiments; however, the slow 
heat transfer in vacuum only allowed for slow 
cooling to the test temperature. 
Several other possible sources for the peak have 
been discussed in the literature. One such source 
is slip transition. Slip in FeAl has been found 
to undergo a change from < 1 1 1 > type at low 
temperatures to < 100 > type at higher tempera- 
tures.25,28-30 A study by Yoshimi et al.19 revealed 
that the transition temperature coincided with the 
peak temperature of yield strength in single crystal 
FeAl alloys. Based on this observation, and their 
other observation that dislocations with non- 
< 111 > burgers vectors are sometimes seen in 
FeAl at temperatures below the peak temperature, 
Yoshimi and Hanada3’ proposed, as a mechanism 
for the strength anomaly, the decomposition of 
< 1 1 1 > dislocations into < 100 > and < 110 > 
segments, which then act as pinning points against 
< 111 > slip. The density of these pinning points is 
assumed to increase with increasing temperature, 
presumably because the decomposition becomes 
likelier at higher temperatures, producing an 
anomalous increase in yield strength with increas- 
ing temperature. This anomalous increase in yield 
strength with increasing temperature continues 
until - at the peak temperature - < 100 > slip is 
favored over < 111 > slip, at which point yield 
strength begins to decrease with temperature (in 
the conventional way). A potential difficulty with 
this scenario is the observation by Mendiratta et 
aL30 that the slip transition temperature increased 
with decreasing Al content in FeAl. Furthermore, 
Umakoshi and Yamaguchi28 found that the slip 
transition temperature decreased with decreasing 
Al content. Both studies seem to suggest that the 
temperature of the yield strength peak should 
depend on Al concentration, but exactly how Al

Other possible physical sources for the positive 
temperature dependence of the yield strength 
include anti-phase boundary (APB)-dragging and 
cross-slip pinning (CSP).32-34 In the CSP mode1,35 
< 111 > dislocations are assumed to cross slip 
from the { 110) to the (112) plane, where they 
become pinned. A possible driving force for such 
cross slip is the lowering of APB energy that might 
occur upon moving from the primary slip plane to 
the cross-slip plane. However, Fu and Yoo~~ cal- 
culated that the APB energy was lower on the pri- 
mary { 1 lo} plane than on the cross-slip { 112) 
plane, suggesting that a lowering of the APB 
energy cannot be the driving force for cross slip in 
FeAl. In B-doped FeAl, it is conceivable that B 
segregates to the APBs and changes the relative 
magnitudes of the APB energies on { 110) and 
(112). However, since the strength anomaly is seen 
also in B-free FeA1,18T20 any explanation that relies 
on B segregation alone cannot be generally applic- 
able. A second possible driving force for cross slip 
arises from the so-called torque term in the force 
couplet model employing anisotropic elastic con- 
stants.37 However, the elastic constants of FeAl are 
such that the torque term also does not provide 
much of a driving force for cross slip.36 It would 
seem, therefore, that the cross-slip pinning model 
has significant difficulties in explaining the strength 
anomaly in FeAl. Yoo et aZ.33 concluded that the 
APB-dragging mechanism, which is based on dif- 
ferences in atomic order in APBs on different 
planes, would be the more likely explanation for 
the strength anomaly in B2 intermetallics. In this 
connection, it is worth noting that B can segregate 
to the APBs, lower their energies, and increase the 
APB-dragging force, making B-doped FeAl stron- 
ger than B-free FeAl, as shown earlier. Never- 
theless, it is not clear how the APB-dragging 
mechanism can explain the observed orientation 
dependence of the strength anomaly.19 
Yet another mechanism has been proposed, 
based on the climb dissociation of < 111 > dis- 
locations gliding on (1 lo} planes.38-43 The climb 
dissociated segments are presumed to pin the 
gliding < 111 > dislocations, and their density is 
thought to increase with temperature, resulting 
in an increase in yield strength with temperature. 
The anomalous increase in yield strength continues 
until at the peak temperature there is a change in 
the slip direction from < 111 > to < 100 > , where- 
upon yield strength begins to decrease. This 
mechanism, too, suffers from the drawback that it 
has difficulty explaining the orientation dependence 
of the critical resolved shear stress.44 Clearly, 
additional work is needed to come up with a model 
for the strength anomaly that takes into account all 
the experimental observations, including the orien- 
tation dependence of the strength anomaly, the 
lack of a compositional dependence of the peak 
temperature on Fe/Al ratio and the strengthening 
as well as the shift in the peak temperature due to 
small boron additions. 
Ductility 
The elongation to failure of Fe-40Al-0.12B 
demonstrated little change from 145 K to 473 K 
and then increased sharply with temperature dis- 
playing a peak of 45% at -873 K, see Fig. 4(a). 
With further increases in temperature, the ductility 
declined rapidly. A similar decrease in ductility at 
elevated temperatures has also been reported by 
Gaydosh et aLlo in a study of powder-processed 
Fe-40Al and was attributed to the influence of 
diffusion-assisted mechanisms, such as grain 
boundary cavitation, which become operative at 
higher temperatures. In the same study, B addi- 
tions to Fe-40Al containing C, Zr, or Hf were 
found to increase the tendency for grain boundary 
cavitation at elevated temperatures. Figure 5 
depicts a typical fracture sequence in all the alloys 
as the test temperature was increased. The fracture 
was mixed mode (intergranular + transgranular) 
at 145 K, Fig. 5(a). At room temperature, the 
fracture mode changed to predominantly inter- 
granular (IG) fracture, Fig. 5(b). The intergranular 
areas of the fracture had a ‘rough’ appearance. At 
low magnification (100x), these areas were remi- 
niscent of wavy slip lines, while at high magnifica- 
tion (1500x), the areas appeared to be faceted. At 
823 K (peak in yield strength) the fracture was 
transgranular (TG) with the formation of micro- 
voids, Fig. 5(c). At 873 K (peak in ductility) 
extensive dimples and microvoid coalescence were 
evident on the fracture surface, Fig. 5(d). As the 
temperature was increased to 973 K, ductility 
dropped, and the fracture changed to mainly IG 
with grain boundary cavitation apparent, see Fig. 
5(e). At 1123 K, the fracture remained mainly IG 
with some apparent areas of TG fracture; grain 
boundary cavitation was also present, Fig. 5(f).

CONCLUSIONS 
An anomalous peak in the yield strength was 
observed in all four of the Fe-Al + B alloys studied. 
Each alloy displayed bee-type behavior at low 
temperatures and a positive temperature depen- 
dence of yield strength at intermediate temperatures. 
In contrast with B-free Fe-40Al studied by Xiao 
and Baker:” (a) B-doped Fe-40Al displays a 
higher yield strength throughout the temperature 
range tested, (b) its peak yield strength occurs at a 
higher temperature (- 150 K higher than in B-free

B-free Fe-40Al. Quenched-in vacancies increase 
the strength of the alloy at low temperatures 
thereby masking the yield strength anomaly. The 
yield strength anomaly in well-annealed specimens 
may be due to the rapid increase in vacancy con- 
centration with test temperature. However, further 
investigation is required to determine the role of 
vacancies on the production of the peak. The duc- 
tility of each alloy increased with temperature to a 
maximum and then declined rapidly with further 
increases in temperature. Along with this decrease 
in ductility, the fracture mode of the alloys chan- 
ged from transgranular microvoid coalescence to 
intergranular cavitation. As the Al concentration 
of FeAl increased (i.e. as the stoichiometric com- 
position was approached) the prominence of the 
peak in yield strength decreased. Also, the tem- 
perature at which a maximum in ductility occurred 
decreased as the Al content was increased. Further 
testing of B-free and B-doped alloys is required to 
determine the physical source of the strength 
anomaly, as well as the effects of B.
