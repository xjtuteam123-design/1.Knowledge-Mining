Structure and High-Temperature Oxidation of Ti-Al-Nb and Ti-Al-Ta 
Intermetallics 
Dalibor Vojtěch, Tomáš Popela 
Department of Metals and Corrosion Engineering, Institute of Chemical Technology, Prague, 
Technická 5, 166 28 Prague 6, Czech Republic 
Dalibor.Vojtech@vscht.cz 
Keywords: TiAl alloy, intermetallic, oxidation, high-temperature. 
Abstract. Ti-Al based intermetallics are prospective high-temperature materials showing low 
weight combined with a relatively high strength, high creep resistance and good oxidation resistance 
at high temperatures. Beside Ti and Al, these materials commonly contain other additives modifying 
their properties. In the present work, structure and oxidation resistance of two Ti-Al-Nb and Ti-Al-
Ta alloys are studied. The alloys are prepared by vacuum arc melting and oxidation is conducted in 
air at 800-1000°C. It is found that there are significant differences in the structure depending on the 
ternary additive. There are also differences in oxidation behavior and these differences are discussed 
in relation to oxidation mechanism. 
Introduction 
TiAl intermetallics are attractive materials for high-temperature applications in aerospace and power 
industry, due to their low weight, good creep and oxidation resistance at high temperatures. Pure Ti-
Al intermetallics, however, suffer from the room temperature brittleness and poor oxidation 
resistance. Therefore, various ternary and other alloying elements, such as V, Nb, Cr, Mn, W, Ta, 
Mo, Si, B, C and other, have been used to improve the room temperature ductility, strength and to 
suppress high-temperature oxidation. 
The reason for the low oxidation resistance of binary alloys is that mixtures of rutile and alumina 
formed during oxidation show insufficient protective effect. For a good oxidation resistance, a 
compact alumina sub-layer in scales would be needed. In a number of studies, niobium has been 
shown to be very effective in this way [1]. Its positive effect is generally explained in terms of the 
doping effect and increase of aluminum activity and diffusivity. The doping effect refers to a 
reduction of O vacancy concentration in rutile lattice which are responsible for oxygen diffusion, 
due to the higher valency of Nb than of Ti. The increase of aluminum activity and diffusion rate 
results in the formation of a compact protective alumina layer in the scales. 
Because of the similarity between the chemical behavior of Nb and Ta given by their positions in 
the Periodic Table, one may expect that tantalum shows a similar effect on high-temperature 
oxidation as niobium. To our knowledge, there are only a few studies concerned with the influence 
of Ta, however, these studies do not provide clear and generally accepted conclusions. For these 
reasons, detailed studies concerned with the behavior of both alloying elements during the high-
temperature oxidation are still needed, particularly, because these elements represent the main 
additives in the latest generations of TiAl based alloys which are under development at present [2]. 
Therefore, the present work compares the influence of Nb- and Ta-additions on the high-
temperature cyclic oxidation of a TiAl alloy at 1000°C. 
Experiment 
Two alloys with chemical compositions in at. % Ti-45.2Al-7.2Nb (hereafter denoted as Nb) and Ti-
44.8Al-6.6Ta (denoted as Ta) were investigated in our study. The alloys were prepared by arc 
melting of pure metals (99.99 %) in a water cooled copper crystallizer under a high-purity helium

Ingots of 20 ×10 ×80 mm in size were then cut by spark-machining into rectangular samples with 
dimensions of 10 ×10 ×5 mm for oxidation experiments. Prior to oxidation, surface of samples was 
ground on SiC papers, then polished by a 1 µm diamond paste and finally washed and dried. Cyclic 
oxidation in air was conducted in a resistance furnace at 1000°C and total oxidation time was 380 
hours. Oxidation kinetics was measured as weight gain versus oxidation time. Surface and cross-
section of as-cast and oxidized samples were examined by various techniques including light 
microscopy (LM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy- and wave-dispersion spectrometry 
(EDS, WDS), x-ray diffraction (XRD), x-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF), glow discharge 
spectrometry profiling (GDS). 
Results and discussion 
Fig.1 presents SEM micrographs of the investigated alloys. Although Al concentrations in both 
alloys are similar, the as-cast structures significantly differ. In the Nb alloy, see Fig.1a, there are 
large grains composed of alternating lamellae of α
2-Ti 3Al and γ-TiAl. It is estimated from XRD 
pattern (not shown) that volume fractions of Ti 3Al and TiAl phases are 26 and 74 %, respectively. 
Lamellar structure of TiAl intermetallics is generally considered as beneficial in relation to 
mechanical properties. Structure of the Ta alloy shown in Fig.1b is quite different. It is composed of 
grains (dark) having a lamellar sub-structure consisting of α2-Ti 3Al and γ-TiAl phases, similarly to 
the previous one. However, there are also large regions (light) composed of massive γ phase. The 
diffusionless massive transformation of high-temperature α to highly faulted γ phase commonly 
occurs on rapid cooling which suppresses diffusion, i.e. decomposition of parent α to a mixture of α 
and γ. Some ternary additives to TiAl based alloys, especially tantalum, have been shown to support 
the massive transformation even at relatively low cooling rates [2]. It is attributable to a slow 
diffusivity of Ta in Ti-Al phases. For the Nb alloy, the massive γ is not observed, because niobium 
is believed to possess a higher diffusion coefficient in Ti-Al phases than tantalum [2].

Fig.2 presents oxidation kinetics of both alloys at 1000°C expressed as a function of weight gain 
and time. One can see that the weight of both alloys rapidly reduces during the whole oxidation 
period, due to intensive oxide spallation. By comparing both alloys, one can observe that the 
spallation rate is higher for the Nb alloy than for its Ta-containing counterpart. The positive 
influence of tantalum on the oxide adherence is also shown in Fig.3 where surfaces of both alloys 
after oxidation at 1000°C are presented. These micrographs provide a good contrast between 
external oxide (white) and internal part of scales (dark). They illustrate that on the Nb alloy there are

spalled off oxides and surface of oxidized samples were 
examined by XRD. XRD patterns for the formers are

Due to the absence of separate Ta- or Nb-oxides, it can 
be assumed that these metals substitute titanium atoms 
in the rutile lattice [3]. 
Phases present in a region beneath the external scales 
differ from the spalled off oxides. On both alloys, there 
are gamma TiAl phase originating from the substrate, 
remaining oxides (TiO 2 and Al 2O3), and also ternary 
Ti 2AlN nitride. Neither Nb- nor Ta-containing phase is 
detected, indicating that these additives dissolve in 
titania, in nitride and in the base intermetallic phase. N-
enrichment in a sub-scale zone was also evidenced by 
EDS and WDS chemical profiling of the oxidized alloys (not shown). Regarding the Ti-Al-O-N 
phase diagram [4], reactions of Ti and Al with oxygen are preferred over nitriding during oxidation 
of Ti-Al alloys in air. After oxygen is consumed and its activity decreases under a certain value, 
situation becomes favorable for a reaction of metallic elements with nitrogen to form nitrides, as is 
observed beneath the oxide scales. Nitrides may represent additional barrier for penetration of 
oxidizing species towards the metal.

It is known that the rate of high-temperature oxidation of TiAl based intermetallics depends 
mainly on protective properties of oxide scales on the surface. In case of a pure Ti-Al alloy, a 
mixture of TiO 
2 and Al 2O3 oxides is usually formed showing a poor protection against further 
oxidation. The reason is that titania represents a good path for penetrating oxygen towards the 
metallic substrate. On the other hand, diffusion of oxygen through alumina is extremely slow, so 
that an increase in alumina content in scales may enhance protective effect. In order to improve

However, there is still a question why 
tantalum surprisingly exhibits a better 
protection at 1000°C than niobium (Fig.2, 
3), despite their similarity. This difference 
could be related to thermodynamic behavior 
of Ta and Nb during oxidation and 
nitriding. By comparing the Gibbs energy of 
formation of Ta 
2O5 and Nb 2O5 oxides, 
being -1413 and -1256 kJ/mol at 1200°C, 
respectively [5], one can see that the former 
possesses a higher thermodynamic stability. 
The Gibbs energy of formation is closely 
related to the strength (energy) of chemical 
bonds, so that Ta-O bonds may be 
considered as stronger than Nb-O ones. 
Similar situation occurs in case of nitrides. 
Tantalum nitride TaN is thermodynamically 
more stable than niobium nitride NbN 
which is also supported by melting points of these substances, being above 3000°C and 2570°C, 
respectively [5]. It may be anticipated that interactions of Ta with O and N are stronger than those of 
Nb during oxidation and nitriding. As a result, oxygen and nitrogen diffusion through scales 
towards the substrate may proceed more slowly for the Ta alloy. Subsequently, the Ta-alloy shows a 
lower oxidation rate and a lower tendency for scale spallation. 
Summary 
In the present work oxidation kinetics of Ti-45.2Al-7.2Nb and Ti-44.8Al-6.6Ta alloys at 1000°C are 
compared. It was found that the latter shows a higher oxidation resistance than the former. This is 
explained in relation to oxidation mechanism and thermodynamic behavior of both additives.
