1. Introduction
Recent advances in the aerospace and natural gas industries have 
increased the demand for alloys with exceptional cryogenic mechanical 
properties. To achieve high strength at cryogenic temperatures while 
avoiding unstable deformation, the strategy of transformation-induced 
plasticity (TRIP) has gained significant attention in both academic and 
industrial fields. In particular, the metastable face-centered cubic (FCC) 
structure has demonstrated outstanding mechanical properties by un -
dergoing a hard martensitic transformation during deformation [ 1 – 3 ]. 
This transformation effectively enhances strain hardening and delays 
the onset of unstable deformation [ 4 ]. To maximize the TRIP effect and 
achieve excellent strain hardenability, a single FCC structure is initially 
designed. However, its inherently low yield strength often restricts its 
practical engineering application in industries [ 1 – 4 ]. Therefore, addi -
tional microstructural engineering is required to enhance the yield 
strength of FCC alloys.
Precipitation hardening is an effective method for improving yield 
strength by hindering dislocation movement [ 5 , 6 ]. Among 
precipitation-hardened alloys, the coherent L1
2 
phase in an FCC matrix, 
widely used to strengthen Ni-based superalloys, provides excellent me -
chanical performance [ 6 , 7 ]. Excellent mechanical properties can be 
achieved in the L1
2
-precipitated alloys containing high contents of Ni 
and Co [ 8 , 9 ]. To obtain excellent mechanical properties in

4. Discussion
4.1. Development of the hierarchical heterostructure
The formation of the hierarchical heterostructure can be attributed 
to differences in recrystallization kinetics between grain boundaries and 
grain interiors. The high dislocation density acts as a driving force for 
recrystallization. In this alloy, the deformation induced by hot rolling, 
unlike cold rolling, does not reach saturation due to dynamic recovery 
and recrystallization during the process. During deformation, disloca -
tions glide and accumulate predominantly at grain boundaries (but not 
fully saturated). This unsaturated deformation state creates a hetero -
geneous driving force for recrystallization, leading to variations be -
tween grain boundaries and grain interiors [ 56 ]. The highly deformed 
state near the grain boundaries induces selective recrystallization and 
the formation of shell grains, characterized by low GND density and fine 
grain size. Additionally, precipitates at grain boundaries are reported to 
contribute to growth accident phenomena and the formation of annealing 
twins, further influencing distinct recrystallization [ 57 ]. Annealing 
twins form as a result of changes in boundary velocity when a moving 
grain boundary is pinned and subsequently released by precipitates, as 
shown in Supplementary Fig. 6 [ 57 ]. The significant accumulation of 
dislocations at the twin boundaries leads to bending and distortion, 
causing them to shift away from their original twinning orientation 
[ 58 ]. Therefore, the twin boundaries lose their twinning characteristics 
and can be transformed into high-angle grain boundaries with random 
orientations, consequently forming a harmonic structure.
The absence of the C14 Laves phase in this alloy, confirmed by SEM 
and XRD analyses, is inconsistent with the thermodynamic predictions. 
This inconsistency can be attributed to the limited reliability and 
availability of thermodynamic databases for high- and medium-entropy 
alloy systems.
4.2. Phase transformation induced by the hierarchical heterostructure
The hierarchical heterostructure shows local TRIP behavior due to 
the preferred martensitic transformation at grain boundaries in the A- 
DHet alloy. Figs. 6 and 7 show the deformed microstructure of the 
current aged sample during cryogenic tensile deformation at equivalent 
plastic strain levels of 10 %, 20 %, and 30 %. The initial microstructure 
of the A-DHet consisted of a fully FCC phase ( Supplementary Fig. 1 and 
2 ). During the deformation at cryogenic temperature, some grains are 
transformed into martensite, as shown in Fig. 7 . The local strain distri -
bution along a given distance was estimated through the FEM simulation 
( Supplementary Fig. 7 ), and the fracture sites were aligned with real 
tests. Supplementary Fig. 8 displays the tensile curves obtained by the 
FEM simulation alongside the experimental tensile curves. The image 
quality (IQ) maps are provided in Supplementary Fig. 9 to more clearly 
suggest the microstructural morphology of the samples. The IQ maps 
reveal the presence of lath martensite and deformation twins in the 
specimen.
In the A-Hom sample, up to a strain level of 30 %, only a small 
amount of martensite is observed at the grain boundaries ( Fig. 6 ), 
showing limited TRIP behavior and implying that TRIP is not the pri -
mary deformation mechanism at this strain level for this sample. In 
contrast, the A-DHet alloy exhibits distinct TRIP behavior ( Fig. 7 ). 
During deformation, an increased presence of martensite is observed 
along the grain boundaries. This indicates that the dual-graded hetero -
structure influences metastability near the grain boundaries, promoting 
selective TRIP behavior. This unique deformation behavior enables 
hard-shell grains, characterized by fine grain size and nano-rod pre -
cipitates, to be further strengthened through a martensitic trans -
formation during cryogenic deformation. This phenomenon can be

can still undergo martensitic transformation, particularly during defor -
mation at cryogenic temperatures [ 60 – 62 ]. At lower temperatures, the 
thermodynamic stability of the austenite phase decreases, thereby 
increasing the chemical driving force for martensitic transformation. As 
the temperature approaches the martensite start (Ms) temperature, the 
metastable austenite more readily transforms into martensite under 
deformation. In addition, the significantly increased flow stress at 
cryogenic temperatures provides a higher applied stress, which further 
promotes the martensitic transformation.
The kinetic path for the nucleation and evolution of martensite is 
highly affected by grain size and dislocation density [ 63 ]. The present 
alloy exhibits significant microstructural heterogeneity, and the factors 
influencing FCC stability are discussed in this section.
First, grain refinement is known to increase apparent stacking fault 
energy (SFE), increasing the energy required for phase transformation 
and hindering TRIP behavior [ 63 ]. However, it has been recently 
revealed that in ultrafine grain structure, the martensite forms on the 
grain boundaries with rapid kinetics [ 63 – 65 ]. Second, dislocations play 
complex roles in TRIP behavior. The high density of dislocations in the 
matrix obstructs deformation-induced martensitic transformation, as 
partial dislocation glide requires additional energy to bypass entangled 
dislocations [ 66 ]. The pile-up of dislocations at the grain boundary, as 
shown in Fig. 2f2 , produces stress concentration and promotes 
martensite nucleation at the boundary [ 67 ]. Furthermore, the glissile 
interface between austenite and martensite facilitates martensite growth 
[ 68 ]. The high dislocation density impedes the motion of the glissile 
interface for martensite propagation [ 66 ]. Consequently, the high 
dislocation density in the core grains enhances FCC stability, suppress -
ing TRIP ability, while the stress concentration due to dislocation pile-up 
at shell grains promotes phase transformation at the shell/core bound -
aries. The relatively low dislocation density in shell grains due to local 
recrystallization favors martensite propagation in these domains. Third, 
the formation of local DP regions alters the chemical composition, 
thereby affecting metastability. 3D reconstruction via atom probe to -
mography (APT) was conducted on DP in shell grains ( Fig. 8 ). The 
representative elements (Fe, Cr, Ni, and Ti) in the reconstruction are 
shown in Fig. 8 a, and all elements from the APT results are presented in 
Supplementary Fig. 10 . DP formation depletes FCC-stabilizing elements 
such as Ni and Al from the matrix, which affects metastability through 
changes in the chemical composition. After DP formation, the matrix 
composition changes to Fe68.0-Ni15.5-Al2.2-Nb0.4-Ti0.7-Cr12.9 (at%).
The Gibbs free energy difference between FCC → BCC, Δ G 
(FCC → BCC), and FCC → HCP, Δ G(FCC → HCP), in the A-DHet sample, 
under conditions with and without DP formation, is presented in Fig. 9 . 
The existence of DP formation significantly reduces FCC stability across 
the temperature range. Therefore, in the present alloy, the heteroge -
neous distribution of dislocations and DP formation contribute to TRIP 
behavior in the shell grains.
4.4. Synergistic hardening from microstructural heterogeneity
The superior mechanical properties of the A-DHet sample arise from 
its unique hierarchical heterogeneous microstructure. During the 
deformation of heterostructured materials, extra GNDs are generated 
and accumulate at the interface to compensate for the plastic in -
compatibility between hard and soft domains [ 69 – 72 ]. These accumu -
lated GNDs at the domain boundaries hinder dislocation mobility and 
produce long-range backstress, i.e., hetero-deformation-induced (HDI) 
strengthening [ 73 ], which contributes directly to mechanical strength -

properties, loading-unloading-reloading (LUR) tests were conducted on

This hierarchical structure meets the design criteria for an ideal 
heterostructure, fulfilling the requirements for heterostructure engi -
neering. During the deformation of heterostructured materials, GNDs 
accumulate, contributing to HDI hardening. Thus, designing structures 
that promote greater GND accumulation is crucial for enhancing HDI 
hardening in heterostructured materials. Three design factors for 
achieving an efficient heterostructure can be considered [ 75 – 77 ]: (1) 
high interface density, (2) significant mechanical differences between 
the domains, and (3) a consistent heterostructure.
First, a large number of interfaces between hard and soft domains 
should be introduced to achieve substantial GND accumulation. In this 
work, the serrated grain boundaries and the emergence of shell grains 
contribute to the increased interfaces for GND accumulation (as shown 
in Fig. 10 c). Second, a significant strength disparity between the do -
mains is necessary to facilitate greater GND accumulation through 
considerable strain partitioning. The fine-grain size of the shell grains 
enhances the domain strength, according to the Hall-Petch relationship 
[ 78 ]. Furthermore, localized DP precipitation within the shell region 
strengthens the shell grains, resulting in a greater strength difference 
between the domains. Lastly, in a typical heterostructure, the soft do -
mains exhibit higher strain hardening than the hard domains, which 
reduces the mechanical contrast and weakens the synergistic hardening 
effect during deformation. Therefore, developing a consistent hetero -
structure is critical. The TRIP behavior in the shell grains ’ hard domains 
contributes to the consistent heterostructure, i.e., a dynamic hetero -
