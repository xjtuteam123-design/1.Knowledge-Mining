Introduction
The development and commercial application of a range of Nickel (Ni) based superalloys for use as
single crystals has contributed significantly to the continued improvement of gas-turbine efficiency
through increased operating temperature. But the melting temperature of Ni of 1455°C has prevented
its application to higher temperatures. For applications at higher temperatures, Iridium (Ir) is one of six
platinum group metals, and is characterized by excellent oxidation and corrosion resistance and a
relatively high melting temperature of 2447°C (1). Ir-0.3%W alloys are used as the primary contain-
ment in space-based power systems and for general-purpose heat sources (2). Iridium is also used to
make solar thrusters, which is one of the most important items in Solar Thermal Propulsion (3).
We have systematically investigated many Ir-based alloys (4) and named them “Refractory Super-
alloys”(5) because of the existence of coherent structures, similar to those in Ni-based superalloys. Our
preliminary results show that these refractory superalloys are of superior strength to Ni-based super-
alloys at temperatures higher than 1400°C. However, intergrandar fracture in Ir-based binary alloys is
a serious problem in commercial applications (6). Other problems are high cost and high density. High
density is a problem because for a given part size, the weight of the part increases. For space
applications weight must be minimized to keep payload size as large as possible, and in gas-turbine
applications weight must be minimized to minimize rotational forces. One method for reducing density,
cost, and intergrandar fracture is to add Ni (6).
To improve the performance of Ir-based alloys, we propose alloys designed by combining Ir- and
Ni-based alloys because Ni-based alloys are ductile, have a relatively low density (about 8.5g/cm
3,
compared to 22g/cm3 for Ir), and low cost. The objective is to combine the high-temperature strength
of Ir-based alloys with the good ductility, low density, and low cost of Ni-based alloys. If the fcc and
L1
2 phases with coherent structure form in both alloys, we expect that the fcc and L1 2 two-phase
regions of the Ir- and Ni-based alloys will connect with each other at the interface of the Ir-based and
Ni-based alloys. The coherency of the fcc/L1
2 phase is very important for quaternary alloys to achieve
high strength at high temperature. We chose the Ir-Ta alloy from the available Ir-based binary alloys
because the strength of the Ir-Ta binary alloy is high at high temperature (over 700 MPa at 1200°C) and
because Ta has a solid solution hardening effect in Ni-based alloys. For the Ni alloy, we chose the Ni-Al

Experimental Procedures
The Quaternary Ir-Ta-Ni-Al Alloys Were Prepared by Mixing Ir-15 at. % Ta and Ni-16.8at.
% Al with fcc and L12 two-phase structures, in proportions as shown in Table 1. To determine the phase
in each alloy, samples were heat-treated at either 1300°C or 1400°C for 168 hrs in a vacuum furnace.
The phase structures of alloys was then analyzed by X-ray diffraction, the microstructures were
observed by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and the phase composition was analyzed by a
Energy Dispersive Analysis X-ray Spectroscopy (EDAX) system attached to the scanning electron
microscope SEM.
Samples 3 mm in diameter and 6 mm long were cut from ingots of the alloys and used for
compression tests. Tensile tests have not been carried out because of the high cost of the alloys. The
temperature of the samples was maintained at 1400°C for 168 hrs before the compression tests. The
initial strain rate was 4 3 10
23/s. The compression tests were done at 1200°C after the samples were
equilibrated to 1200°C for 10 minutes.

The compressive strength of quaternary alloys increased with increasing Ir-Ta alloy content. The yield

Discussion
The Partitioning of Elements
The quaternary Ir-Ta-Ni-Al alloys were prepared by two sorts of Ir- and Ni-based binary alloys. In order
to understand the distribution of elements in quaternary alloys, the comparison of Ir-based with
Ni-based alloys is discussed. In generation, we found similar alloy element distributions in Ir-based and
Ni-based superalloys. For example, in the Ni-based superalloys, Ta is not only a solid solution
hardening element, enlarging the lattice parameter, but most of the Ta is a constituent of the L1
2
phase-Ni3(Al,Ti) (g9) and increased the dissolve temperature of g9. The partitioning of Ta between g9
and g phases was g9: g 5 1: 0.05 (8). Murakami et. al. (9,10) investigated the partitioning of elements
to each phase and the site occupancy of atoms in the g9 phase in Ni-based single crystal superalloys.
They indicated that Ta and Al preferentially partition into theg9 phase and that Ir has a slight preference
to partition into the g phase. In our quaternary alloys, Ta also has a strong preference to partition into
the L12 (Ir3Ta) phase as a forming element of the L1 2 (Ir3Ta). The partitioning of Ta between g9 and
g was g9: g 5 1: 0.1;0.3. Difference of the proportion of Ta in g matrix between Ni-based superalloys
and quaternary Ir-based alloys can be explained by considering the binary diagram of Ni-Ta and Ir-Ta.
The solubility of Ta in Ni and Ir is about 3 and 11 at.%, respectively (11). The solubility of Ta in
Ir-based solid solutions is larger than in Ni-based solid solutions. For Al, as an element of the Ni
3Al-L12
phase the partitioning of Al into the Ni 3Al-L12 phase is stronger than into the fcc phase, although the
composition of Ni 3Al in quaternary Ir-Ta-Ni-Al alloy is not clear. Al preferentially partitions into the
fcc phase in alloys A and C, while most of the Al formed the B2 (IrAl) phase in alloy B. The partitioning
of Ir behaves differently in the quaternary Ir-Ta-Ni-Al alloys than in the Ni-based superalloys; Ir

The Interaction of Elements
As mentioned above, the object of this study is to combine the high-temperature strength of Ir-based
alloys with the good ductility, low density, and low cost of Ni-based alloys
The results of compression tests indicated that the yield strength increased with increase of the
amount of Ir-based alloy, although the B2 structure existed in alloy B with a 50% Ir-based alloy. The
results can be explained in the view of the interaction of elements. In general, the strength of quaternary
alloys can be estimated by the low mixture in strength of composite materials, as a linear combination
of the two kinds of binary alloys. When Ir and Ta are added into Ni-based binary alloy (for example
alloy A), Ir and Ta are refractory elements, they must be contributed to solid solution hardening of either
phase and increase the melting temperature of quaternary alloys compared with that of Ni-based binary
alloys. The same thing must be happening to Ir-based binary alloy. When Ni and Al are added into
Ir-based binary alloys (for example alloy C), Ni and Al also cause solid solution hardening of either
phase. However, they decrease the melting temperature of the quaternary alloy compared with that of
Ir-based alloys. As a result, the strengths of quaternary alloys at high temperature are located between
that of Ni-based alloy and Ir-based alloys.
On the other hand, a larger (over 100
mm in size) L12 phase existed in our samples (see Fig. 2). The
larger L12 phase was not resolved from as cast sample by heat treatment even up to 1400°C and the
secondary precipitated particles were also larger (over 1 mm in size) and incoherent. We did not do our
best to control the microstructure at the first study. This means that if the size and distribution of L1 2
phases were controlled well, we could obtain the fcc/L1 2 coherent structure and will achieve good
strength and ductility.
From the above discussion, we see that quaternary alloys are promising ultra-high temperature
materials. The fcc and L1 2 two-phase regions, which connected by two kinds of fcc/L1 2 two-phase
regions (fcc/Ir3Nb or fcc/Ni3Al), exists between Ir-based and Ni-based alloys if the alloy composition
is changed. We can choose alloy compositions based on the properties required for each application. For
example, when we need ultra-high temperature materials (i.e., above 1800°C) and low density is not
required, we can use alloys with a high proportion of Ir-based alloys. When we need materials with low
density and ultra-high temperature capability is not required, we can use alloys with high proportion of
Ni-based alloys.
Conclusions
We proposed quaternary refractory superalloys from two binary alloys, which were composed of
fcc/L12 coherent structures. To prepare different quaternary refractory superalloys, we mixed Ir-Ta and
Ni-Al binary alloys in different proportions. We investigated the microstructure and compressive
properties of quaternary alloys. The results indicate that a three-phase equilibrium exists in the alloys
including 25% and 75% Ir-based alloys, consisting of two kinds of L1
2 structures (Ir3Ta and Ni 3Al
types) and the fcc structure. A four-phase equilibrium exists in the alloy including 50% Ir-based alloy
after heated at 1300°C for 168 hrs, consisting of two kinds of L1
2, fcc, and B2 structures. At 1200°C
these alloys exhibited higher yield stress and compressive strains than that of Ni-based super alloys
during the compressive tests.
