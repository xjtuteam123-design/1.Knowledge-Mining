Niobium alloys have found extensive application in industries, such as aerospace, nuclear reactor, and 
emerging electronic technologies, owing to their high melting point, low density, and remarkable forma- 
bility. Nevertheless, they still fall short in terms of comprehensive strength, toughness, and thermal 
stability when subjected to complex impacts and/or torsional forces during service. Here, a dual-phase 
(BCC/FCC) Nb alloy with attractive mechanical properties and thermal stability was designed by tuning 
stable element C in the Nb-BCC matrix assisted by hot deformation and aging processes. Our ﬁndings 
reveal that the formation of discontinuous carbides at the grain boundary promotes the phase trans- 
formation of the matrix from BCC to FCC (K-S orientation relationship), resulting in the formation of 
FCC thin layers and nano particles. This unique conﬁguration hinders the slipping of dislocations during 
deformation and impedes the degeneration of microstructures during the thermal cycling process from 
200 °C to 900 °C. Moreover, the discontinuous carbides at GBs provide channels to transfer dislocations 
between various phases and/or grains, which results in attractive mechanical properties and thermal sta- 
bility. The ultimate tensile strength, yield strength, elongation, and elasticity modulus of the designed Nb 
alloy reach impressive values of 790.5 MPa, 436.5 MPa, 39.1 %, and 63.5 MPa, respectively. These obser- 
vations provide guidelines for designing dual-phase Nb alloys with remarkable strength, toughness, and 
thermal stability for aerospace applications by tuning the stabilizing element C in the Nb-BCC matrix.

1. Introduction 
Compared with most other refractory alloys, Nb alloys have be- 
come preferred materials in the aerospace, nuclear reactor, and 
new electronic industries, because of their high melting point, low 
density, and remarkable formability [ 1 , 2 ]. However, currently, the 
Nb alloys employed in aerospace mainly consist of a BCC sin- 
gle phase. It does not satisfy the requirements of toughness and 
thermal stability owing to the limited slip system and dislocation 
movement resistance [ 3 , 4 ]. With the increase in the ﬂight speed, 
thrust-to-weight ratio, and frequency of heating and cooling cy- 
cles, there is an urgent need to design and develop Nb alloys with 
considerable strength, plasticity, and thermal stability [ 5 , 6 ]. Many

high-entropy Nb alloys such as Nb20 Ni20 Ti20 Co20 Zr20 , Nb-Ta-V, and 
double-precipitation Nb alloys. These have substantially improved 
the properties of Nb alloys in terms of density, preparation process, 
and mechanical properties. However, these alloys are still ineffec- 
tive for engineering applications [ 5 , 8 ]. Therefore, strength, plastic- 
ity, and stability are the three determining factors that prevent Nb 
alloys from becoming a signiﬁcant structural material for highly- 
advanced aerospace technologies involving ultra-high service tem- 
peratures above 1200 °C [6] .

chanical properties of alloys by increasing the dislocation resis-

to BCC [11] . This has improved the strength and ductility. In par- 
ticular, the introduction of multislip FCC nanoparticles or coherent 
multiple interfaces in BCC, such as FCC/BCC, FCC/L12 and BCC/B2 
in Fe26 Co25 Ni20 Cu15 Al13.1 Ga0.9 [12] , can increase the phase inter- 
face effectively, thus improving hinderance of grain growth and the 
dislocation migration at lower stress levers, while permitting dis- 
location transmission at relative higher stress levers. This would 
result in considerable strength and plasticity. The Nb-BCC phase 
can also transition into the Nb-FCC phase at elevated temperatures 
( > 900 °C) [13] . Nevertheless, owing to the high formation-energy 
of Nb-FCC at room temperature (FCC: 0.320 eV/atom) compared 
with that of the Nb-BCC phase (BCC: 0 eV/atom) [14] , Nb-FCC com- 
pletely transforms into the Nb-BCC phase after preparation. This 
results in a low thermal stability [15] . Therefore, the stabilization 
of the Nb-FCC phase with good strengthening and toughening ef- 
fects on the Nb-BCC matrix is attractive but challenging. 
In addition, niobium alloys exhibiting dimensional stability dur- 
ing thermal cycling are also important in aerospace applications. 
The structural instability of the Nb alloy is likely to result in slight 
variations in the inclination angle, increased wind resistance and 
local heat ﬂow density, reduced reliability, and other potential 
problems during the long-term synergy of heat, ﬂow, and force in 
service. These severely hinder the safe and reliable use of aircrafts 
[16] . In recent years, it has been observed that the commonly used 
carbide-strengthening phases generally consist of NbCx with vary- 
ing Nb/C ratios. Moreover, certain NbCx phases are unstable during 
thermal cycling, thereby resulting in severe variations in the di- 
mensions and mechanical properties of the Nb matrix. These con- 
straints limit the broad application of Nb alloys in the aerospace 
industry. Therefore, the research on a synergistic enhancement of 
the strength, plasticity, and thermal stability of Nb alloys is of sci- 
entiﬁc and engineering signiﬁcance. 
Here, we determined that C plays an important role in tuning 
the BCC phase transition as a stabilizing element for the Nb-BCC 
phase. Moreover, we designed a dual-phase Nb alloy with remark- 
able strength, plasticity, and thermal stability using a novel two- 
step process. In addition, the mechanisms of the remarkable prop- 
erties of the alloy were discussed based on its special microstruc- 
tures, i.e. the coexistence of the discontinuous nano carbide at the 
grain boundary (GB) and nano Nb-FCC phase around the carbides 
and in the Nb-BCC matrix. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Preparation and processing of alloys 
An ingot with a Nb87.5 W7.0 Mo2.5 Zr1.5 C1.5 (at. %) chemical com- 
position was cast into a graphite mold via vacuum arc melting. The 
as-cast ingot was homogenised at 1800 °C for 2 h under vacuum. 
An as-cast ingot with dimensions of 20 × 20 × 40 mm was then 
cut into block specimens with dimensions of 10 × 10 × 40 mm 
(indicated as Raw-Nb). The austenisation was ﬁrst carried out in 
a muﬄe furnace at 1200 °C. Then, the samples were hot rolled at 
1200 °C with 60 % thickness reduction and quenched in water at 
room temperature. The quenched samples were aged at 1350 °C for 
0–5 h. Thereby, the samples NDA0, NDA1, NDA2, NDA3, NDA4, and 
NDA5 were obtained. Electrolytic extraction [17] was used to sepa- 
rate the precipitates from the alloy matrix and neutron diffraction 
was utilized to determine the phases of both separately. 
2.2. Testing of performance 
The Vickers hardness of each sample was evaluated by av- 
eraging over ﬁve locations using a computerised microhardness 
tester (200HBVS-30) at a load of 15 N for a dwell time of 15 s. 
A microcomputer-controlled electronic universal testing machine 
(MTS E45.105) was used for uniaxial tensile tests at room tem- 
perature at a strain rate of 1 × 10–3 s–1 . Tensile specimens with 
an overall length of 40 mm, scale length of 25 mm, scale width 
of 4 mm, and thickness of 2 mm were removed from the center 
of the hot-rolling plate. Three samples of each alloy were tested 
to ensure reproducibility. Using the ZRPY-1700 microstrain testing 
device, samples with different stabilization treatments were heated 
and cooled at temperatures from 200 to 900 °C, and thermal strain 
response curves were obtained simultaneously. 
2.3. Processing and preparation of samples 
The scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Sirion 200, Amer- 
ica) samples were prepared by mechanical polishing and chem- 
ical etching. The etching solution was as follows: 1HF: 25HNO3 
(68 vol. %): 25H2 O. The electron back scattering diffraction 
(EBSD) samples were polished using a Hitachi IM40 0 0 and low- 
temperature focused ion-beam cutting polishing system. Elec- 
trolytic polishing was performed using anhydrous magnesium per- 
chlorate and a methanol solution. The EBSD measurements were 
performed at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV with a scan step 
size of 0.07–0.1 μm by using an OIMXM4 EBSD testing system. In 
order to eliminate orientation noise, the lower limit boundary mis- 
orientation cut-off was set as 2 ° According to the ASTM standard 
(ASTM E2627-13), the grains were deﬁned as the group of points 
that have the same misorientation in the EBSD map [18] . The HR- 
TEM samples were prepared using a two-beam shear system (FIB. 
Helios Nanolab G3 UC). 
2.4. Characterization and analysis 
The phase components of the samples during preparation and 
processing were analysed by neutron diffraction using the angle 
dispersive strain scanner STRESS-SPEC at the FRM II high-ﬂux neu- 
tron source [19] on the Scientiﬁc Research Dog Instrument Test- 
ing Platform of Fudan University. A carbon and sulfur elemental 
analyser (CS744) and an X-ray ﬂuorescence spectrometer (Axios 
mAX) were used to determine the C content of the matrix after 
the different treatments (the samples were extracted electrolyti- 
cally to remove the carbide). The precipitate distribution and ten- 
sile fracture morphology of the samples were analysed using ﬁeld- 
emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Sirion 200, USA). 
An electron probe microanalyzer equipped with a spectrometer 
(EPMA, JeolJXA-8230, Japan) was used to analyze the point, line, 
and mapping energy spectra of the elements. EBSD was employed 
to analyze the recrystallisation, phase distribution, grain size dis- 
tribution, textures, strain, and orientation distribution using the 
47 ° Gaussian-smoothed harmonic-level expansion method of TSL 
OIM analysis software (HKL Nordlys Max and OIMXM4). The EBSD 
tests were performed at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV with a 
scan step size of 0.07–0.1 μm. The grain size was measured along 
one ﬁxed direction and obtained by averaging hundreds of grains. 
A spherical-aberration electron microscope (Titan G2 60-300) was 
used for bright-ﬁeld and high-resolution TEM imaging. 
2.5. Modeling, calculation, and simulation 
The TCTI2 database of Therm-Calc software was used to cal- 
culate the phase diagram of the carbide (NbCx ) in the Nb-W- 
Mo-Zr-C alloys (nominal composition Nb87.5 W7.0 Mo2.5 Zr1.5 C1.5 , at.% 
( Fig. 1 (a)). The crystal structure was modelled at each stage using 
Materials Studio and VESTA. SolidWorks mechanical design soft- 
ware was used to model the deformation. ANSYS ﬁnite element 
software was used to simulate and analyze the distribution of 
strain during alloy deformation.

convergence of the total energy. In the various calculations, geo- 
metric optimization was ﬁrst performed. This was followed by the 
calculations of the free energy and density of states. 
3. Results 
3.1. Alloy design 
The carbides in the Nb87.5 W7.0 Mo2.5 Zr1.5 C1.5 (at.%) alloy exist in 
the form of M2 C, MC, Nb5 C3 , and Nb4 C3 (M contains Nb and Zr). 
This was calculated from the phase transition diagram for carbides 
in Fig. 1 (a). To obtain the stable single-phase carbide (Nb4 C3 ) at a 
high temperature, the processing temperature needs to be higher 
than ∼1160 °C ( Fig. 1 (a)). Carbide precipitation under heat treat- 
ment leads to a decrease in interstitial C in the matrix, which in- 
creases the BCC-free energy ( Fig. 1 (b)), thereby leading to instabil- 
ity and volume shrinkage of BCC–Nb [20] . This, in turn, is likely to 
induce a partial volume-expanding phase transition from BCC–Nb 
( V = 36.52 ˚A3 , two atoms per cell) to FCC–Nb ( V = 75.33 ˚A3 , four 
atoms per cell). The phase transformation (two BCC cells to one 
FCC cell) is accompanied by a 3.1 vol. % volume expansion. This 
can compensate for the volume contraction induced by the reduc- 
tion in interstitial C atoms. Based on ﬁrst-principles calculations, 
the increase in the FCC–Nb phase facilitated the reduction of the 
matrix free energy, which in turn will stimulate the further forma- 
tion of the FCC–Nb phase. Until 10.5 % FCC–Nb, the matrix energy 
attained a minimum ( Fig. 1 (c)). 
In conjunction with the above calculation, the austenitizing pro- 
cess ( Fig. 1 (d)) (i.e., 1200 °C for 2 h followed by hot rolling for 
60 %) was employed ﬁrst. This process introduced a large number 
of strain and carbide nucleation sites into the Nb matrix. This, in 
turn, promoted the precipitation of stable carbide (Nb4 C3 ) from the 
matrix and induced a partial phase transformation from BCC–Nb to 
Nb-FCC. Then, the alloy was aged at 1350 °C for 1–5 h to tune the 
conﬁguration of the carbide and dual-phase microstructures of the 
Nb alloys ( Fig. 1 (d)). 
3.2. Optimization of deformation process 
First, the microstructure, precipitation distribution, and com- 
position during the deformation of Raw-Nb were analysed. Con- 
sidering that the distribution of the strains introduced by hot 
rolling signiﬁcantly affects the conﬁguration of a stable dual-phase 
structure [21] , ﬁnite element simulation was employed to analyze 
the strains within the alloy after hot rolling ( Fig. 2 (a)). Notably, 
for simpliﬁcation purposes, the rod-shaped model was employed 
( Fig. 2 (a)). Thus, the sample in the center (purple-boxed area in 
Fig. 2 (a)) was selected for the subsequent studies. As presented in 
Fig. 2 (b), only a marginal amount of precipitate was detected in 
the raw Nb. However, numerous void defects (e.g. craters) formed 
at the GB.

3.4. Characterization of meso–structure 
After deformation, extensive defects, unstable carbides and 
phases were produced. This increased the strength but signiﬁcantly 
reduced the plasticity and stability of the alloy. Here, the alloy was 
aged at 1350 °C for 1–5 h (indicated as NDA1-NDA5, respectively) 
to optimize the precipitates and matrix ( Fig. 1 (a)). Numerous de- 
fects were detected in the Raw-Nb sample ( Fig. 5 (a)). According to 
the neutron diffraction results ( Fig. 5 (g)), the Raw-Nb alloy con- 
tained single-phase BCC and unstable Nb2 C. After the hot-rolling 
deformation (NDA0), the carbides were transformed into Nb5 C3 
( Fig. 5 (g)). After aging at 1350 °C for 1 h, the carbides increased 
signiﬁcantly in the GB ( Fig. 5 (b)), and the phase transformed into 
Nb4 C3 with the formation of the new FCC phase ( Fig. 5 (g)). 
As the aging time increased to 2 h ( Fig. 5 (c)), carbides precipi- 
tated along the GB, and numerous ﬁne precipitates were observed 
in the grains. With an increase in the aging time (3 h, Fig. 5 (d)), 
the carbides in the GB were distributed in a discontinuous chain. 
As a weak region in the alloy, the GB is generally the fracture 
initiation zone, and discontinuous carbides help improve its me- 
chanical properties ( Fig. 3 (h)). After aging for 4 h ( Fig. 5 (e)), the 
carbides precipitated as non-uniform needle-like dendrites along 
the GB. This signiﬁcantly weakened the GB [59] . As the aging 
time increased to 5 h, the dendrites combined with each other to 
form coarser dendrites (Fig. S2. This severely reduced the plastic- 
ity and stability of the alloy. Fig. 5 (f) shows that NDA3 formed a 
GB carbide network skeleton structure. In conclusion, the carbides 
evolved from Nb2 C to Nb4 C3 and increased gradually from Raw- 
Nb to NDA5. The C content of the matrix also decreased with the 
increase in aging time. 
Notably, the formation of NbCx resulted in the reduction of in- 
terstitial C atoms [60] and lattice relaxation in the adjacent matrix. 
Meanwhile, with an increase in NbCx at the GB ( Fig. 5 (a–e)), the 
diffraction intensity of the FCC phase also increased signiﬁcantly 
( Fig. 5 (g)). Lattice expansion during BCC phase transition to FCC 
can marginally compensate (3.1 vol.%) for the lattice relaxation. 
Therefore, precipitation of NbCx is supposed to be a key factor in 
inducing phase transformation of the Nb matrix from BCC to FCC. 
This was also demonstrated by the DOS analysis ( Fig. 5 (h)). In the 
alloy containing 10.5 % FCC, the decrease in DOS pseudogap val- 
ues (inset of Fig. 5 (h)) below the Fermi level indicated a gradual 
increase in the formation of covalent bonds between NbCx . This 
facilitated lattice expansion through the FCC transition to compen- 
sate for the lattice shrinkage caused by the precipitation of inter-

4. Discussion 
4.1. Formation of special microstructures 
A high-performance Nb alloy (NDA3, Fig. 3 ) was designed using 
theoretical calculations and experimental optimization. Compared 
with the single-phase Nb-BCC alloy, the strength, plasticity, and 
thermal stability of the NDA3 alloy were improved signiﬁcantly 
( Fig. 4 ). This occurred owing to their unique microstructures (Figs. 
5(d, f) and 7(d-i)), i.e. discontinuously distributed carbide GBs net- 
work skeletal and BCC + FCC dual-phase grains (Figs. 7(d-i) and 6(j- 
m)). 
Calculations ( Fig. 1 (b, c)) showed that the C content is closely 
related to the thermodynamic stability of the BCC phase ( Fig. 1 (b)). 
That is, the FCC phase increases with decreasing C in the matrix. 
Therefore, the regulation of C in the matrix is likely to tune the 
phase and properties of the alloy ( Fig. 1 (a–c)). Based on these re- 
sults, hot rolling combined with subsequent aging was employed 
to optimize the microstructures and properties of the Nb alloys. 
The Raw-Nb sample was ﬁrst hot-rolled at 1200 °C for 60 % 
( Figs. 2 and S1) to tune the C content in the matrix and obtain a 
single stable carbide ( Fig. 1 (a)). This promoted the precipitation of 
Nb4 C3 at the GB and inside the grains ( Figs. 2 and 5 (g)). Simultane- 
ously, the C content in the matrix decreased signiﬁcantly compared 
with that before deformation (inset in Fig. 2 (c)). Hot rolling can

introduce high strain concentrations near the GBs, break and re-

within the grains [67] . This promotes the precipitation of carbides 
during the subsequent aging process ( Fig. 5 (b–e)), thereby inducing 
a martensitic phase transformation of the BCC near the carbides in 
the grain and at the GB. In addition, casting defects can be mit- 
igated by hot rolling ( Fig. 2 (b,d)). Combined with the phase dia- 
gram calculations of the carbide ( Fig. 1 (a)), the hot-rolled sample 
was aged at 1350 °C to induce the gradual precipitation of highly- 
pure and stable Nb4 C3 and reduce the interstitial C atoms in the 
BCC matrix. This resulted in lattice relaxation and instability of the 
Nb-BCC phase [68] as well as marginal variations in the DOS pseu-

treatments induced the transformation of the BCC around the car- 
bide into FCC with a volume expansion of ∼3.1 vol.% ( Fig. 7 (h, i)). 
This is supposed to compensate for most of the lattice contraction 
induced by the reduction in interstitial C atoms in the matrix. The 
entire microstructural evolution process during the preparation is 
shown in Fig. 8 . 
To this end, we conducted extensive experiments by verifying 
various experimental parameters in pursuit of the optimal sam- 
ple (NDA3). For example, we explored a range of rolling deforma- 
tions, including 0, 30 %, 60 %, and 90 %, to tune both grain size 
and distribution of carbides formed during the subsequent anneal- 
ing process. Among them, 0 and 60 % deformed microstructures 
are shown in Fig. 2 . The 30 and 90 % deformed microstructures 
are shown in Fig. S1. Our ﬁndings reveal that the most uniform 
distribution of carbides and grains within the matrix was achieved 
with a 60 % deformation. For the annealing temperature, the phase 
composition and stability of various carbides between 0 and 1600 
°C were calculated in Fig. 1 (a). The result shows that 1350 °C is 
the optimal annealing temperature to induce the formation of the 
stabilized carbides (Nb4 C3 ). Thereby, 1350 °C is employed as the 
annealing temperature. In addition, annealing process was also op- 
timized by verifying heating duration at 1350 °C, namely, 0–5 h. 
Correspondingly, we carefully analyzed the mechanical properties 
and microstructures of 6 samples after annealing, i.e., the sample 
NDA0-NDA5. Therefore, the best sample (NDA3) in the manuscript 
was obtained by optimizing various experimental parameters. 
Additionally, theoretical calculation ( Fig. 1 (b, c)) and experi- 
mental analysis ( Fig. 5 ) were carried out to analyze the effect 
of carbon content on the formation of the ideal dual-phase mi- 
crostructure. Speciﬁcally, the C content was mainly tuned by vary- 
ing annealing duration at 1350 °C following the rolling deforma- 
tion. Initially, the C content in Raw-Nb is ∼1.5 at.%. With increasing 
annealing duration, interstitial C atoms gradually precipitated from 
the Nb-BCC matrix with the formation of carbides. This causes a 
decrease in the content of stabilizing element C in the Nb-BCC ma- 
trix, i.e., decrease of the stabilization of the Nb-BCC phase (Fig. 
1(b)), resulting in phase transformation from BCC to FCC. For ex- 
ample, after heating at 1350 °C for 3 h, the C content in the ma- 
trix is only 0.05 %, Nb-BCC is close to the most unstable state, and 
the dual-phase BCC-FCC microstructure was obtained ( Fig. 5 (d, f)). 
Therefore, phase transformation is closely associated with the con- 
tent of C in the Nb-BCC matrix. 
In conclusion, we introduced uniform nucleation sites, such as 
defects, are introduced into the matrix by 60 % hot rolling defor- 
mation. During the subsequent heat treatment, interstitial C atoms 
precipitated from the Nb-BCC matrix, resulting in a reduction in 
volume and an increase in the free energy of the Nb-BCC matrix 
( Fig. 1 (b)). Meanwhile, the unstable Nb-BCC phase gradually trans- 
formed into Nb-FCC, causing a volume expansion of 3.1 vol.%. This 
transformation effectively counterbalances the volume contraction 
induced by the precipitation of interstitial-C atoms in the matrix, 
thus maintaining a stable dual-phase structure consisting of both 
BCC and FCC phases. In addition, we ﬁnd when the Nb-FCC con- 
tent reaches 10.49 %, this dual-phase structure is at its most stable 
( Fig. 1 (c)). 
4.2. Strengthening and toughening 
The remarkable mechanical properties and thermal stability of 
the NDA3 samples are mainly associated with their unique mi- 
crostructures, i.e. the dual-phase conﬁguration and discontinuous 
carbide network skeleton at the GB. The effect of the BCC + FCC 
dual-phase structures on the mechanical properties can be sum- 
marised as follows: (1) The dual-phase structure can hinder dislo- 
cations through an increased phase interface and thereby, increase 
the strength [ 4 , 69 ]. (2) The K–S orientation relationship between 
the BCC matrix and FCC phase (including a ∼6 nm thin layer be- 
tween the carbides GB and BCC matrix and ∼2–3 nm nano par- 
ticles in the matrix ( Fig. 7 (e, h, i))) facilitates the slipping of dis- 
locations within the grains between various phases during defor- 
mation. This results in an increase in plasticity [70] . (3) The in- 
terfaces between various phases, including the Nb4 C3 mesh layer, 
FCC thin layer ( ∼6 nm), and the Nb-BCC matrix facilitates the nu- 
cleation and multiplication of dislocations. This also improves plas- 
ticity [ 71 , 72 ]. (4) In addition, the phase transition relaxes the local 
stresses and generates plastic slip. This reduces premature failure 
and thus, improves ductility. The hardening response associated 
with the phase transition retards the extension of microcracks, 
thereby improving the alloy deformation resistance [73] . (5) Be- 
cause of the decomposition of single-phase BCC into a dual-phase 
with limited growth, the average grain size is reﬁned to ∼5 μm 
( Fig. 6 (i)). As a result, a large amount of GB is introduced into the 
alloy, and the strength of the alloy is improved based on the Hall 
Patch and grain boundary strengthening effects [74] . This also en- 
ables the dynamic behavior of the carbide to be controlled by the 
process and thus the grain size to be controlled. Several previous 
studies have also shown that the BCC + FCC dual-phase structure 
can be employed to improve the mechanical properties of alloys 
[ 75 , 76 ]. 
The discontinuous Nb4 C3 at the GB ( Fig. 7 (d, g)) can hinder 
grain growth and transfer the local deformation of the matrix to 
the entire Nb4 C3 pinning network [77] . As shown in Figs. 6 (j–m) 
and 7 (e–i), after the aging at 1350 °C, a large number of nano FCC

This veriﬁes that C is a stabilizing element of the Nb-BCC phase. C

carbide network skeleton functions as a constrained reinforcement 
for the nail migration of GBs, hinders grain growth during aging 
and recrystallization, and transforms the local deformation of the 
matrix into the deformation of the entire pinning network [ 77 , 80 ]. 
Consequently, the average grain size is reﬁned gradually ( Fig. 6 (h, 
i)). 
4.3. Thermal stability 
Studies of various alloys have observed that carbides generally 
precipitate at heterogeneous sites such as GBs, phase boundaries, 
internal defects, strain concentrations, and dislocations [ 81 , 82 ]. The 
kinetic explanation is that these heterogeneous sites contribute a 
large amount of energy to nucleation and a high diffusion rate of 
atoms during growth [83] . Here, the carbides tend to precipitate in 
GBs and in the grains (Figs. 6 (j–m) and 7 (e, f)). However, tuning 
carbide at GBs is challenging. The reason is that stresses tend to 
concentrate at GBs, thereby causing the non-uniform precipitation. 
This severely reduces their stability [84] . 
In single-phase Nb-BCC ( Figs. 2 (b) and 7 (a)), other structural 
defects such as dislocations, vacancies, and craters may function as 
nucleation sites to form unstable primary carbide phases. However, 
the particle size and distribution of the second phase are diﬃcult 
to control because of its tendency to coarsen and undergo phase 
changes at high temperatures [85] . Before the experiment, a region 
with a uniform strain distribution during hot rolling was selected 
as the experimental sample by ﬁnite element simulation ( Fig. 2 (a)). 
In this way, both the work-hardening capacity of the GB and the 
strain in the grain are increased, leading to the simultaneous pre- 
cipitation of carbides at the GB and in the grain. Additionally, by 
controlling the aging temperature and time, the carbides nucleated 
discontinuously at the GB and were dispersed in the grain. 
Meanwhile, nano Nb-FCC (3.1 vol.% volume expansion com- 
pared with that of the Nb-BCC structure) was generally distributed 
near carbides ( Figs. 6 (j–m) and 7 (e)). As discussed above, this is 
associated with a decrease in the interstitial C atoms around the 
carbides. That is the formation of a volume-expansive FCC phase 
around the carbides prevents the diffusion of interstitial C-poor 
atoms, thus hindering the growth of the carbides. In addition, the 
3.1 vol.% volume expansion of the BCC to FCC transformation com- 
pensated for the lattice relaxation caused by the reduction in in- 
terstitial C. This maintained the lattice stability of the matrix. Fur- 
thermore, the localized deformation was transformed into a defor- 
mation of the entire network skeleton and increased the stabil- 
ity of the alloy. Moreover, the matrix was almost completely de- 
pleted of C and attained a steady state. The diffusion rate of the 
component elements affects the kinetics and determines the phase 
composition of the carbides [ 83 , 86 ]. At 1350 °C, the C diffusivity is 
higher than that at lower temperatures. Moreover, combined with 
the phase diagram in Fig. 1 (a), C-rich Nb4 C3 may form more conve- 
niently than C-poor Nb5 C3 and Nb2 C ( Fig. 5 (g)). Ultimately, the car- 
bide was stabilised as Nb4 C3 after aging 3 h ( Figs. 5 (g) and 7 (g)). 
Therefore, the phase composition of the carbide was mainly con- 
trolled by the heat-treatment temperature and aging time. 
5. Conclusions 
In this work, a strengthening and toughening Nb dual-phase al- 
loy was designed and developed by tuning the phase conﬁgura- 
tions based on phase diagram calculations, hot-rolling deformation, 
and heat treatment. The key results are summarised below: 
(1) The phase diagram calculations revealed that above 1160 °C, 
the carbides existed as a single phase of Nb4 C3 . First- 
principles calculations showed that the stability of the BCC 
matrix was closely related to the concentration of C in the 
substrate. 
(2) After 60 % hot rolling deformation at 1200 °C, and a sub- 
sequent aging treatment at 1350 °C for 3 h, the Nb alloy 
(NDA3) presented remarkable mechanical properties. That is, 
the hardness, ultimate tensile strength, yield strength, elon- 
gation, and modulus of elasticity were 345.8 HV, 790.5 MPa, 
436.5 MPa, 39.1 %, and 63.5 MPa, respectively. In addition, 
the NDA3 alloy exhibited an attractive thermal stability dur- 
ing the thermal strain response experiment from 200 °C to 
900 °C. This was owing to the formation of carbide parti- 
cles with a discontinuous network skeleton at the GB and 
the depletion of the stabilizing element C of the BCC matrix. 
(3) The attractive mechanical properties of the designed Nb al- 
loys were associated with the formation of special phase 
conﬁgurations. The hot-rolling process assisted by heat 
treatment facilitated the precipitation of discontinuous sta- 
ble Nb4 C3 particles at the GB. This resulted in the depletion 
of C around the GB and lattice relaxation of the BCC ma- 
trix. Correspondingly, this promoted the phase transforma- 
tion of the Nb-BCC matrix to FCC (3.1 vol.% volume expan- 
sion) around the carbides. This compensated for the lattice 
contraction induced by the depletion of C in the matrix. 
(4) Remarkable orientation relationships, i.e., the K-S relation- 
ship between the nano FCCs and BCC matrix, not only hinder 
the dislocation movement by phase interface but also allow 
the dislocation sliding. Moreover, the formation of carbides 
at the GBs and in the grains can hinder the movement of 
dislocations. The discontinuous conﬁguration of the carbides 
at the GB provides channels for dislocation movement across 
the GBs Eq. (1) .
