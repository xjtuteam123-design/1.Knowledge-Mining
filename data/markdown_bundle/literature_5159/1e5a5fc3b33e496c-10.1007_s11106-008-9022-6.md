II. CONNECTION BETWEEN MECHANICAL 
PROPERTIES AND STRUCTURE

The experimental dependences of strength, plastic properties, hardness, and fracture toughness on 
sintering or pressing temperature for ultrafine alloy WC–41 wt.% Co are presented. The alloy 
densifies in solid phase and temperature varies from 950 to 1250
o
C. The dependences of mechanical 
properties are extreme, excepting fracture toughness. The properties reach their maximum values at 
1050 to 1150
o
C depending on the type of testing. Fracture toughness continuously increases with 
densification temperature. The highest values of some properties are reached after additional solid-
phase annealing. The mechanical properties of ultrafine high-cobalt alloy samples are assessed with 
the use of structural parameters and empirical equations established for standard hard WC–Co 
alloys sintered in liquid phase. The calculat ed and experimental values of properties differ: 
transverse rupture strength, fracture toughness, and yield strength show higher values, while 
hardness and compressive strength have lower values as compared with calculated ones.
Keywords: tungsten carbide, cobalt, so lid-phase densification, ultraf ine structure, mechanical 
properties. 
The paper [1] focused on the densification and structural evolution of nonstandard hard alloy VK40 
produced over a wide temperature range (950–1250 °C) by unconventional consolidation of powder billets, and 
compared the mechanical properties of the composite and hard industrial alloys intended to perform under cyclic 
shock loads. It was shown that test ultrafine alloy VK40 had high transverse rupture strength, yield stress, and 
fracture toughness and possessed hardness and fracture ener gy commensurable with shockproof hard alloys with 
smaller content of the metal phase. The test alloy also fund amentally differs from standard hard alloys in that it is 
produced by solid-phase densification. In this regard, of interest is to analyze how the mechanical properties behave 
depending on the temperature of solid-phase densificatio n and to check how structural parameters influence the 
mechanical properties of hard alloys sintered in liquid phase. The objective of this paper is to treat these issues. 
Ultrafine powder mixture WC–41 wt.% Co was made by intensive ball milling and densification in vacuum 
at 950–1250°C with specific energy 1.6–2.0 kJ/cm 3. The experimental procedure and sizes of the test samples are 
detailed in the paper [1].

Of interest is to compare the main mechanical properties of test ultrafine alloy VK40 and of standard alloys 
and to assess the level of properties based on their available empirical dependences on structural parameters. 
Noteworthy is transverse rupture strength, which is equa l to 3000–3500 MPa for experimental samples (Fig. 1a). 
This value is higher by a factor of 1.5 to 2 than the transv erse rupture strength of coarse high-cobalt alloys sintered 
in a liquid phase and, in essence, is close to or even agr ees with the ultimate values of transverse rupture strength 
for hard WC–Co alloys. Undoubtedly, this level of strength ow es to the ultrafine structure or, more specifically, to 
the thin layers of metal phase, formed in the alloy despite a very large amount of the meta l phase. It is difficult to 
predict the level of strength since there are no universally recognized theoretical and empirical dependences of 
transverse rupture strength on structural parameters. Nevertheless, it is easy to compare it with the strength of 
standard hard alloys using the set structural para meter, which is the mean thickness of cobalt layer LCo or ratio 
LCo2/LWC, based on the experimental dependences of strength on these structural parameters from [2, 3]. The 
comparison shows that the transverse rupt ure strength of the ultrafin e alloy with high binder content is greater than 
that of hard standard alloys, LCo or LCo2/LWC being equal. If the values of transverse rupture strength for alloys 
from [2, 3] are still relevant, our result indicates that, first, there is no un ique connection between the transverse 
rupture strength and these structural parameters and, second , there are reserves for increasing the transverse rupture 
strength of alloys with high content of a metal phase. 
In bending testing, the experimental alloy may show high strength (3000–3500 MPa) both in case of very 
low contiguity (alloy VK40) and very high one (alloy VK15). It is concluded therefore that the carbide phase 
contiguity either does not influence th e strength of alloys in bending testing or its influence is overridden by other 
factors, for example, by the strength of phase boundarie s, which may be weakened in solid-phase sintering. 
However, it is hard to believe that an alloy with weakened phase boundaries would show transverse rupture strength 
of 3500 MPa. Regardless of the carbide pha se contiguity, this level rather reflec ts the strength in the detachment of 
cobalt alone (or solid solution of tungsten and carbon in cobalt). According to V. A. Ivensen [4], this level 
corresponds to 3300 MPa, i.e., to the strength of cobalt whiskers, which fracture without plastic deformation. 
Hence, the transverse rupture strength of hard alloys over a wide range of tungsten carbide contents is determined 
by the strength in the rupture of thin cobalt layers, which have the same tensile strength as whiskers do. The 
densification of this alloy above 1200°C promotes rapid coarsening of carbide grains, thickening of the cobalt layer, 
and accumulation of both grains and binder. This facilitate s cobalt plastic deformation by shear and decreases the 
strength of cobalt and alloy as a whole. 
To understand the mechanism by which transverse rupture strength of hard alloys changes, fracture 
surfaces of different samples should be examined. Analysis of fractured samples at small magnification shows that 
large fracture origins are absent almost in all cases, i. e., no large pores, large carbide particles, or phase 
accumulations are found on fractured su rfaces. There are small structural def ects which are difficult to identify and 
which do not seem to substantially decrease the transver se rupture strength. Fracture origins were not found on 
samples pressed at the lowest temperature (950 °C). This seems to result from th e weakest structural elements, 
which are phase boundaries and inter-carbide boundaries; they are quite extensive in ultr afine structure. Examining 
the fracture surface at high magnification (Fig. 2) shows that the alloy mainly co nsists of ultrafine particles, which 
are not very distinct. The fracture surface seems to repr esent the cobalt phase covering ultrafine tungsten carbide 
particles. The structure of alloy VK15M (Fig. 2d) shown for reference has more distin ct carbide particles. In 
addition, the fracture surfaces of alloys VK40 and VK15M have different macroreliefs. The fracture surface of alloy 
VK40 has large alternating valleys and ridges, which are evidenced by light and dark areas in the photograph and 
which are especially well seen at low magnification. This indicates that a crack propagates along quite a tortuous 
path when VK40 sample fractures.

rapidly increases with densification temperature. Fract ure toughness increases more intensively between 1050 and 
1150°C than between 1150 and 1350 °C. This behavior of fracture toughness can be explained as follows: two 
factors influence KIc at low densification temperatures: strength of boundaries and thickness of cobalt layer; 
and only one factor at higher temperatures: thickness of c obalt layers. The linear connection between the fracture

Fracture toughness and transverse rupture strength permit assessing the critical size of conditional structural 
defect that initiates fracture depending on the pressing temperature of samples (Fig. 3). The size of the critical 
structural defect was calculated by the formula from [5]: 
ac = (KIc/σb)2/ 1.21π. (3)
The calculated size of the critical structural defect varies from 10 to 70 μm depending on production procedure (Fig. 
3). The smallest size is typical of samples pressed at 1200 °C. There is a reason for that since tungsten carbide 
particles actively grow together [10, 11] at this temper ature and the fracture toughness of samples decreases. The 
largest critical sizes of conditional defects are observed in sintered samples and those produced at the lowest 
temperature. The latter should be specially explained as fracture of samples with lower fracture toughness is 
initiated by a larger origin than in alloys with higher fracture toughness. This may be because the inherent strength 
of the samples pressed at 950 °C is quite high and may keep a longer crack from catastrophic propagation. 
Nevertheless, this assumption should be additionally verified. 
Analyzing the fracture toughness shows that only the layer thickness does not unambiguously determine the 
level of this characteristic. The binder volume content is a major factor, whic h in most cases is not considered in 
assessing one or another property. In this regard, of interest is to analyze the hardness of samples (Fig. 1c) since 
there are empirical dependences for hardness as well, whic h connect hardness with the mean layer thickness. For 
example, the dependence from [12]: 
HV = 990 LCo–0.2. (4) 
Substituting the mean thickness of the cobalt layer, for example, for the sample pressed at 1150 °C [1, Table 1] into 
this formula, we obtain hardness of 11.6 GPa. The hardness of the experimental alloy is equal to 7.7 GPa. The lower 
hardness of the experimental alloy indicates that one structural parameter is insufficient to assess and predict the 
hardness of alloys over a wide range of binder contents.

The yield stress in the Marsh formula was determined in compression tests since the flow property of 
material is best evident in this type of testing (Fig. 1d). This value was quite high for the experimental alloy. It was 
higher by a factor of 1.5 to 2 than the yield stress of standard hard alloys with smaller binder content. To assess the 
yield stress of hard alloys using structural parameters, two approaches are employed: one is based on the matrix 
binder structure for all hard alloys and the other is based on the skeleton structure of carbide and cobalt phases. To 
assess the yield stress of the experimental alloy, the matrix approach, i.e., Kreimer’s strength theory [15] based on 
dispersion hardening, is of great interest. However, Krei mer’s theory offers no dependence that connects the yield 
strength simultaneously with the binder volume content and mean size of tungsten carbide particles. In addition, 
experimental dependences of the yield strength on the bind er volume content and mean size of particles are limited 
to alloys with VCo = 37 vol.% and DWC ≥ 1.4 μm. Therefore, Ivensen’s formula [16] was taken as dependence for 
assessment based on the skeleton structure of alloys on the assumption that the yield strength of the alloy is 
determined by the yield strength of the carbide skeleton: 
σt(WC–Co) = σt(WC) ⋅ CWC. (7)

The compressive yield stress and compressive strength differ, according to Kreimer [15], by a constant that 
does not depend on binder content and so they are described by similar dependences that connect the compressive 
strength with the binder volume content or mean size of tungsten carbide particles (Fig. 1d). As change in the binder 
content and size of tungsten carbide particles affects th e mean binder layer thickness, there were attempts 
previously to connect the compressive strength with the mean layer thickness. In particular, the paper [18] presented 
the dependence between σc and LCo in semilogarithmic coordinates that insignificantly differed from the linear 
dependence for hard alloys with binder content from 6 to 24 wt.%. The linear approximation of the data from [19] 
results in the following expression: 
σc (MPa) = –1138ln LCo (µm) + 2635, (9) 
which was used to calculate the compressive strength for the experimental alloy.

bear in mind that the smaller the mean size of carbide phase particles, the higher the binder volume content, the 
mean binder layer thickness being equal in different hard alloys. To assess the mechanical properties of hard alloys 
with different binder content and mean size of carbide part icles, at least three structural parameters should be 
considered: phase volume content, mean linear phase size, and contiguity of hard particles. It seems that the last 
parameter may be neglected in a number of cases. 
While there have been different attempts to establis h connection with structural parameters for strength 
characteristics of WC–Co alloys, there are practically no dependences for plastic strain which would show how 
structural parameters influence plasticity. Hence, we will re strict our discussion of the experimental alloy plasticity 
to the comparison of its values for different alloys (Fig. 1e ). Note that the plasticity of experimental alloy VK40 is 
commensurable with that of coarse hard alloys containi ng 20 wt.% of binder. In other words, ultrafine alloy 
containing 41 wt.% of binder does not exhibit high plasticity but has high fracture energy considering its plastic 
strain and compressive strength (Fig. 1f). In addition, the highest plasticity is shown by samples pressed at quite a 
low temperature of 1050 °C, and increase in densification temperature decreases plasticity but increases the mean 
binder layer thickness, increases density, and improves boundaries. This experimental dependence permits an 
assumption that phase boundary sliding (given weak bounda ries) and mutual movement of carbide particles (given 
low porosity) can contribute to the total plastic strain for ultrafine alloy. However, this assumption should be 
verified especially since the annealing of samples pressed at 1150°C also ensures high plasticity. 
Therefore, analysis of the mechanic al properties of experimental ultrafine alloy VK40 shows that it differs 
from standard hard alloys and is not governed by the structure–property dependences established for them. On the 
one hand, it is attributed to the unambiguous influence of individual structural parameters on properties and, on the 
other hand, to the fact that alloy with very high binder content (55 vol.%) may show other dependences resulting 
from the large volume of the metal phase in the composite. 
CONCLUSIONS 
The experimental dependences of the mechanical properties of ultrafine alloy WC–41 wt.% Co on 
densification temperature are mainly extreme. They reach their maximum at pressing temperatures between 1050 
and 1150°C. The only exception is fracture toughness, which continuously increases with densification temperature. 
The highest transverse rupture strength (3500 MPa) and fracture toughness (35.8 MPa ⋅ m1/2) are reached after 
solid-phase annealing of samples preliminary pressed at 1050 to 1150°C. 
The mechanical properties of the ultrafine high-coba lt alloy in most cases differ from those assessed using 
available empirical dependences established for standard hard WC–Co alloys. The transverse rupture strength, 
fracture toughness, and yield stress show higher values and hardness and compressive strength show lower values 
than the calculated ones.
