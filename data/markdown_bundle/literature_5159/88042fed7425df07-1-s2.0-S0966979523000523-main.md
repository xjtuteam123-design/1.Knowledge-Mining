1. Introduction 
Reactor structural materials are served in extreme environments for 
a long time, such as high temperatures, high doses of irradiation and 
strong oxidative corrosion. Traditional alloys are failed to meet the 
needs of the next generation of advanced nuclear reactors in service, 
becoming a challenge limiting the development of the nuclear materials 
[ 1 ]. The cladding, as the core of the nuclear reactor, is the first safety 
barrier. The materials for conventional pressure water reactor cladding 
are mainly zirconium alloys, such as Zr2, Zr4, E635, Zirlo and M5 [ 2 – 4 ]. 
However, as the working conditions of the fourth-generation nuclear 
reactor are more stringent, the properties of traditional zirconium alloys 
can no longer meet the application requirements. Thus, the development 
of new materials is very urgent. 
High entropy alloys (HEAs) are a new type of alloys developed in 
recent years, usually consisting of at least five main elements, each of 
which has an atomic ratio of 5 – 35%. In HEA systems, good properties, 
such as high strength [ 5 , 6 ], good plasticity [ 7 , 8 ], irradiation resistance 
[ 9 , 10 ], corrosion resistance [ 11 , 12 ] and high temperature resistance 
[ 13 , 14 ] can be obtained through rational alloy design due to high 
structural entropy, slow diffusion, lattice distortion and cocktail effect, 
thus attracting extensive attention from many researchers [ 15 – 17 ]. 
Using the concept of HEA, several HEAs based on refractory elements 
(Ti, Zr, Hf, V, Nb, Ta, Cr, Mo and W) with or without the addition of 
non-refractory elements such as Al, Si, Co and Ni for modulation have 
been recently reported and are known as RHEAs [ 18 ]. These RHEAs are 
capable of exhibiting high-temperature strengths comparable to those of 
nickel-based high-temperature alloys [ 18 ]. In addition, considering that 
RHEAs are resistant to high temperatures, irradiation, oxidation and 
corrosion, RHEAs open up new possibilities for development in nuclear 
applications. In recent years, RHEAs have become a hot issue for current 
research. For example, alloys of the NbTaV-(Ti, W, Mo) system with a 
disordered body-centered cubic (BCC) structure have been proposed as 
high-temperature materials with high strength, high plasticity and 
oxidation resistance [ 19 , 20 ]. Among them, the TiVNbTa alloy shows 
excellent compressive mechanical properties at room temperature ( σ

3.1. Phase composition analysis 
The X-ray diffraction patterns of the as-cast Ta5 and V5 RHEAs are 
shown in Fig. 1 . The two alloys have a single BCC phase. The lattice 
constants of the Ta5 and V5 RHEAs are 0.3307 nm and 0.3302 nm, 
respectively. 
3.2. Microstructure analysis 
Fig. 2 a-b shows the inverse pole figure (IPF) and grain size distri -
bution of the cast Ta5 and V5 alloys. The IPF images show that the grain 
orientation distribution of both alloys is random without texture. Both 
alloys mainly consist of equiaxed grains. Fig. 2 c-d shows the grain size 
distribution of the Ta5 and V5 alloys with average sizes of ~49 μ m and 
~53 μ m, respectively. 
Fig. 3 (a) and (b) show the as-cast microstructures of the Ta5 and V5 
RHEAs. The microstructures of both alloys are typical of dendritic 
structures with grey and black regions corresponding to the dendritic 
region (DR) and interdendritic region (IR), respectively. Their chemical 
compositions are shown in the insets of Fig. 3 a and (b). Fig. 3(c – h) and 
(i-n) show the mapping of the elemental distribution between the DR 
and IR. For the Ta5 RHEA, the high melting point Nb and Ta elements 
are enriched in the DR. While the low melting point Zr and Al elements 
are enriched in the IR, and Ti element is almost uniformly distributed in 
the DR and IR. The area fraction of the DR is approximately 58 ± 6%. 
For the V5 RHEA, the Nb element is enriched in the DR, the Zr and Al 
elements are enriched in the IR, and Ti and V elements are almost uni -
formly distributed in the DR and IR. The area fraction of the DR is 
estimated to be 52 ± 5%. Differences in elemental distribution between 
the DR and IR may arise from the differences in the melting points and 
enthalpies of mixing of the elements [ 25 , 35 ]. The enthalpy of mixing 
between the elements in this alloy system is shown in Table 1 . The 
enthalpy of mixing between Zr and Al is 44 kJ/mol, which is the 
minimum value in this alloy system, indicating that Zr and Al are more 
easily bonded together during solidification than the other element pairs 
[ 36 ]. Thus in both alloys, Zr and Al form solutions with the other ele -
ments and when solidification begins, Ti, Nb and Ta solidify first to form 
dendrites. While Ti/Nb/Ta with Zr are less compatible, making the 
Zr – Al low melting point solution be repulsive to interdendrites. As a 
result, the DR is rich in Ti, Nb and Ta, while Zr and Al separate in the IR 
after solidification, showing a typical dendritic microstructure.

3.3. Microhardness 
The Vickers microhardness of the cast Ta5 and V5 alloys are 311 ± 3 
HV and 314 ± 5 HV, respectively. There is a large difference in the 
atomic radii of Ta and V elements with the same atomic percentages 
( Table 2 ), but there is almost no difference in the microhardness of the 
two alloys, indicating that the hardness can be attributed to solid solu -
tion strengthening rather than lattice distortion [ 38 , 39 ]. 
3.4. Tensile properties and fracture mechanisms 
Fig. 6 shows the engineering tensile stress-strain curves of the Ta5 
and V5 RHEAs at room temperature. The cast Ta5 RHEA has a yield 
strength of ~850 MPa with an elongation of ~25%. The cast V5 RHEA 
has a yield strength of ~848 MPa with an elongation of ~16%. The 
tensile yield strength and plasticity of the cast Ta5 and V5 alloys with the 
materials in nuclear engineering applications and RHEAs are compared 
in Fig. 7 . It can be seen that the tensile yield strengths of the Ta5 and V5 
RHEAs are much higher than those structural materials of nuclear fission 
reactor. In addition, they also possess excellent overall mechanical 
properties compared to other RHEAs with tensile plasticity. 
Fig. 8 shows the fracture morphologies of the cast Ta5 and V5 
RHEAs. It can be observed that both of the alloys fracture as a slip 
separation, occurring obvious plastic deformation. The fracture surfaces 
are mainly covered by plastic tear ridges and large-sized dimples. 
Numerous micro-pores were also observed in the slip band during 
deformation, which absorbed the energy during deformation [ 52 ], 
enabling the Ta5 and V5 RHEAs possess good plasticity.

4.2.2. Nanoprecipitate strengthening 
Recently, various studies have demonstrated that the mechanical 
properties of alloys can be effectively improved by controlled chemical 
short-range ordering [ 79 , 80 ]. However, several studies [ 81 , 82 ] have 
shown that the arrangement of atoms in HEAs may not be ideally 
disordered due to the complex interactions between the constituent el -
ements and the diversity of atomic sizes. Under casting conditions, 
diffusivity or the corresponding decrease in the rate of rapid cooling of 
the melting temperature leads to the formation of disordered structures 
or structures with undetectable order [ 83 – 85 ]. In most Al-containing 
RHEAs, the ordered B2 phase usually exists in the form of the 
cuboidal nanoprecipitates [ 86 ], which do not contribute significantly to 
the strength improvement. When the B2 nanoprecipitates is dispersed in 
the matrix, the strength of the alloy will be significantly improved [ 32 ]. 
HRTEM images and FFT images are given in Fig. 4 . Fig. 4 (f, e) and 
(m, n) show that the B2 nanoprecipitates are diffusely distributed in the 
BCC matrix. For nanoprecipitate strengthening, dislocation bypassing 
mechanism usually occurs when the size of the nanoprecipitate exceeds 
a critical value or when the interface is not coherent with the matrix. 
However, since the nanoprecipitate is sufficiently small and coherent in 
the Ta5 and V5 RHEAs, the dislocation shear mechanism will dominate 
[ 87 , 88 ]. For the cast Ta5 and V5 RHEAs, the strength due to the dislo -
cation shear mechanism can be expressed as [ 89 ]:
