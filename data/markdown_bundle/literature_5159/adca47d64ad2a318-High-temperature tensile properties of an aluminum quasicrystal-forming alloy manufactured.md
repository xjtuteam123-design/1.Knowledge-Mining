ABSTRACT 
Normalized tensile samples of Al – 4Fe – 3Cr – 2Ti (wt.%) alloy were successfully fabricated by laser powder bed 
fusion (LPBF) additive manufacturing. The alloy ’ s tensile properties and microstructure were investigated at 
room and high-temperature (up to 500 
◦
C). This Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy exhibits exceptional thermal stability and 
outstanding mechanical performance at temperatures of up to 500 
◦
C. A particularly interesting feature is the 
alloy ’ s yield strength values, which reach ~300 MPa at 300 
◦
C and ~180 MPa at 400 
◦
C. The outstanding 
mechanical behavior of this alloy at high temperatures is attributed to the in situ precipitation of ultrafine 
icosahedral quasicrystalline (i-QC) particles endowed with high thermal stability. These values are notably 
higher than those achieved for A319, 6061-T6 and Al-8%wt.Cu – Mg – Ag cast alloys – three of the most common 
heat-resistant Al alloys, and higher than commercially available Al-based alloys for LPBF. Based on hardness 
measurements and the bimodal composite microstructure, we propose a model to predict this alloy ’ s yield 
strength. This model shows a good agreement with the experimental results and can be used to maximize the 
alloy ’ s tensile strength. This work describes a lightweight aluminum alloy with outstanding high temperature 
tensile strength within a wide range of temperatures, in addition to being additively processable and affordable. 
1. Introduction 
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) is a highly auspicious layer-by-layer 
additive manufacturing (AM) technology for producing metallic com -
ponents for different industrial purposes, including the automotive, 
aerospace, biomedicinal and electronics sectors [ 1 , 2 ]. Unlike the con -
ventional subtractive manufacturing routes, the layer-by-layer nature of 
the LPBF process allows the manufacturing of complex and lighter parts 
without requiring expensive molds, dies or tooling. Furthermore, 
traditional complex structured components that have to be joined 
together by welding or fasteners from several individual parts can now 
be consolidated into a single piece by the LPBF process [ 3 ]. Another 
advantage is the extremely high cooling rates (10
4
– 10
7 
K/s) achieved by 
the LPBF process [ 4 – 7 ]. The ultrafast and highly localized interaction 
between the laser beam and the powder bed, and the complex periodic 
thermal cycling history enable the production of unprecedented mi -
crostructures and properties that are not achievable via conventional 
manufacturing routes. 
Nonetheless, the number of Al-based alloys that can be successfully 
processed by LPBF into robust structural parts with promising me -
chanical performance is still limited [ 8 ]. So far, only low-performance 
conventional weldable Al-Si [ 9 , 10 ] and Al – Si – Mg alloys [ 11 – 13 ] 
exhibit reliable behavior when additively manufactured at affordable 
costs. Nonetheless, the mechanical resistance of these laser powder bed 
fusion manufactured AlSi10Mg-based alloys (hereinafter referred to as 
LPBFed alloys) decreases at temperatures above 150 
◦
C [ 13 – 15 ]. On the 
other hand, because of the effects of non-equilibrium melting and so -
lidification and intrinsic heat treatment involved in LPBF, high-strength 
age-hardenable Al-based alloys (7 xxx and 2 xxx series) often result in 
undesirable microstructures with large columnar grains and a strong 
tendency to crack [ 16 ]. Additionally, the vacuum environment of LPBF 
processing provides suitable conditions for alloying elements (Zn, Mg, 
and Li) to evaporate. Because these alloying elements are age-hardening 
phase formers, their evaporation decreases the high precipitation

reported the preferential evaporation of certain alloying elements, 
which inevitably leads to a deviation from the alloy ’ s nominal chemical 
composition while simultaneously altering the its final properties. The 
high reactivity of Al with oxygen and the high reflectivity of aluminum 
powder are also challenges associated with the LPBF processing of Al

The literature [ 18 ] discusses the development of numerous 
high-temperature Al-based alloys (e.g., Al – Ce – Mn, Al – Ni – Fe, Al – Mg – Si 
( – Cu) – Sc – Zr) suitable for additive manufacturing. High-temperature 
Al-based alloys currently manufactured additively are divided into 
two main categories, those using expensive elements such as Sc [ 19 – 22 ] 
and Zr [ 23 – 25 ], which render these alloys extremely expensive, and Al 
alloy composites containing dispersed non-reactive ceramic particles. In 
the latter case, the different thermal and mechanical properties of the Al 
matrix and dispersed particles, as well as their distribution/ -
agglomeration, must be taken into account, since these factors will likely 
determine the alloy ’ s mechanical strength. 
Al-based quasicrystalline (QC) alloys have been developed with 
microstructures of nano to micrometer-sized icosahedral quasicrystal -
line (i-QC) particles embedded in an α -Al matrix [ 26 – 29 ]. The high 
thermal stability of i-QC phases maintains the high strength of Al-based 
i-QC alloys at elevated temperatures [ 28 ]. Due to its hard and brittle 
characteristics at room temperature, its dislocation movement through 
the quasiperiodic lattice of the i-QC phase is very difficult [ 30 , 31 ]. 
However, when combined with a ductile metallic matrix, i-QC phases 
are excellent reinforcements [ 30 , 32 – 37 ]. Nevertheless, the precipitation 
of the i-QC phases in a matrix requires high cooling rates (~10
3
– 10
6 
K/s) [ 38 , 39 ] during the solidification process. This explains why 
Al-based QC alloys are conventionally processed by rapid solidification 
methods such as melt spinning [ 28 ], copper mold suction casting [ 40 ], 
laser surface remelting [ 36 , 39 ], and gas atomization [ 41 , 42 ] with sub -
sequent consolidation via hot extrusion [ 43 ]. Bulk Al-based QC alloys 
with complex geometries, however, are needed for commercial appli -
cations. According to the literature, Al – Fe – Cr – Ti 
icosahedral-quasicrystalline-former alloys present high thermal stability 
of their i-QC phase (430 
◦
C), allied to significant hardness and tensile 
strength within a temperature range of room temperature to 350 
◦
C [ 28 , 
44 ]. Due to the metastability of the i-QC phase formed in Al – Fe – Cr – Ti 
alloys, quasicrystalline approximant phases have also been reported as 
precipitate/reinforcement phases [ 28 , 31 , 44 – 46 ]. 
For so long, physical metallurgy has recognized the relationship 
between microstructure and mechanical properties. Hardness is a me -
chanical property directly related to the metal ’ s microstructure. Due to 
its nondestructive (or semi-destructive) nature, associated with easy 
measurement, hardness testing has always been a very attractive 
method to estimate other mechanical properties of metallic materials 
[ 47 – 53 ]. Over the years, researchers have endeavored to model the yield 
strength in aluminum alloys based on microstructural evolution and 
hardness [ 54 – 56 ]. Because the microstructure of alloys depends on the 
processing method, their yield strength should be modeled considering 
its relationship with process variables. It is well known that in predicting 
the mechanical properties of composite materials, the content and 
properties of each constituent should be considered individually. In our 
previous paper [ 57 ], we demonstrated that LPBFed Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloys 
have a bimodal microstructure with different properties. Due to the 
complexity of phase formation and their small sizes, it is very difficult to 
quantify the fractions of each phase contained in Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloys 
processed by LPBF. However, because the center and border/HAZ 
(heat-affected zone) of these alloys are clearly distinguishable and their 
micromechanical properties can be measured [ 57 ], it would be useful to 
model the their macromechanical properties using the two 
aforementioned items of information, i.e., content of center and border 
and their individual micromechanical properties. 
In this study, we investigate the tensile properties of LPBF-processed 
Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy at room and high temperatures (up to 500 
◦
C). Using 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and differential scanning 
calorimetry (DSC) the microstructure that ensures the strength of the 
alloy at high temperatures was identified. The mechanical properties of 
this alloy were compared with those of heat-resistant cast Al alloys 
(A319, 6061, and Al-8%wt.Cu – Mg – Ag) and Al-based alloys commer -
cially available for LPBF. The results of these experiments indicate the 
potential use of Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloys as novel heat-resistant Al alloys 
suitable for additive manufacturing by LPBF. Based on hardness mea -
surements and the bimodal composite microstructure of this material, 
we propose a model to predict the yield strength of this LPBFed 
Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy. 
2. Experimental and methods 
2.1. Sample preparation 
The Al – 4Fe – 3Cr – 2Ti (wt.%) ingots were subjected to gas atomization 
(PSI Hermiga 75/5VI) using argon under 40 bar pressure. The powder 
production and characterization details are provided elsewhere [ 57 ]. 
The gas-to-melt mass flow ratio (GMR) used here was 1.7. An SLM 
machine (AconityMINI, Germany) equipped with an ytterbium fiber 
laser was employed to produce cylindrical samples 7 mm in diameter 
and 44.5 mm in height. Argon was used as a protective gas to prevent the 
powder from oxidizing. The residual oxygen content was kept below 
100 ppm during processing. All the samples were produced with a layer 
thickness (t) of 30 μ m. In our previous LPBF optimization studies [ 57 ], 
we investigated the behavior of Al – 4Fe – 3Cr – 2Ti (wt.%) powder (P1) 
during processing. In that study, the parameters used to achieve the 
densest samples were as follows: a laser power (P) of 150 W, hatch 
distance (h) of 102 μ m, and laser scanning speed (v) of 600 mm/s. A 
laser spot size of 60 μ m and a one-way (unidirectional) scanning strategy 
were used. For each layer, the scanning vectors were rotated by 79
◦
. The 
image-measured relative densities of the samples manufactured by the 
LPBF process with the optimized parameters exceeded 99%, indicating 
the occasional presence of pores inside their structure. The samples were 
produced on a pure Al substrate with 0
◦
, 45
◦
, and 90
◦
orientation with 
respect to the building direction. Before tensile testing, the cylinders 
were machined according to the German standard DIN 50125:2009-07 
(Type B test pieces). The samples before and after machining are 
depicted in the Supplementary material, Fig. 1 . The chemical composi -
tion of the ingot, powder, and LPBFed samples was determined using a 
microwave-induced plasma optical emission spectrometer – MIP-OES 
(4200 MP-AES, Agilent Technologies, Melbourne, Australia) equipped 
with an N2 generator (Model 4107, Agilent Technologies), and a 
five-channel peristaltic pump was used in all the experiments. Because 
the alloy was produced with recycled material, it contains traces of Mg 
and Mn. The instrumental operating conditions were selected for the

4. Discussion 
4.1. Microstructural characterization of the LPBFed Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy 
The development of the Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy composite microstructure 
was favored by the rapid solidification that occurs in the LPBF process. 
As can be seen in Fig. 1 a), a bimodal microstructure was formed in the 
center and border regions of the laser tracks. Due to the higher cooling 
rates at the center of the melt pool [ 68 ], a large number of fine bright QC 
particles were distributed within the α -Al matrix ( Fig. 1 a) and b) ), 
whereas coarser particles were localized along the melt pool 
border/heat-affected zone (HAZ) ( Fig. 2 a)-c)). Because of the local 
thermal gradients between the center and the border of the melt pool, 
the second phase particle distribution was heterogeneous. Particularly 
at the melt pool borders, the cooling rates were found to be lower, since 
the overlapping regions between two adjacent laser tracks extend the 
time spent at higher temperatures [ 69 ]. Therefore, not only are big QC 
particles present in the border of the melt pool, but also i-QC approx -
imants and Fe-rich phases, as proven by STEM images, SADP 
( Fig. 2 b)-h)) and XRD diffractogram ( Fig. 3 ). 
One of the contributions of Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy fabricated via LPBF is 
the formation of primary QC particles, which precipitate before the α -Al 
phase during solidification, thereby acting as an effective barrier against 
α -Al phase directional growth. The refinement of α -Al grains associated 
with the presence of fine, spherical, well-dispersed QC phases can 
greatly enhance the mechanical properties of the material. The SAED 
pattern in Fig. 1 d) confirms the presence of the five-fold symmetry axis 
characteristic of icosahedral quasicrystals, which are formed by 
nonconventional structural units [ 61 ]. Hard, brittle, incoherent i-QC 
particles [ 31 , 70 ] act as a barrier to dislocation movements, thus 
increasing the material ’ s yield strength. Moreover, according to the 
literature [ 71 ], there is a clear tendency for mechanical strength to in -
crease as the size of dispersed QC phases decreases. As demonstrated in 
our earlier work [ 57 ], the microhardness in the center of the melt pool is 
much higher than at its borders, which is attributed to the high content 
of ultra-fine i-QC phases in the center of the melt pool. Thus, the LPBFed 
Al – Fe – Cr – Ti microstructure is interpreted as a composite, and the final 
mechanical properties of this material depend on the content and indi -
vidual characteristics of each microstructural component. 
It should be noted that the microstructure obtained in this work did 
not exhibit undesirable coarse columnar grains, which typically solidify 
along the thermal gradient towards the top-center of the melt pool in Al- 
based alloys processed by LPBF [ 5 , 72 – 75 ]. Instead, a more equiaxed 
microstructure was obtained, which strongly favors the isotropy of 
LPBFed parts. The results suggest precipitation of the i-QC phase is 
essential to prevent the growth of columnar grains and enable the for -
mation of equiaxed grains.

5. Conclusion 
In this study, normalized tensile samples of Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy were 
successfully manufactured via laser powder bed fusion (LPBF). The 
tensile properties at room and high temperatures (up to 500 
◦
C) and the 
microstructure were studied. In addition, a model for predicting the 
yield strength of LPBFed Al – Fe – Cr – Ti was proposed. 
The following main conclusions can be drawn: 
(1) A bimodal microstructure consisting of i-QC particles of different 
sizes embedded in an α -Al was generated in situ during the LPBF 
of Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy. The center of the melt pool exhibited a high 
content of ultra-fine spherical i-QC phases well distributed 
throughout a dendritic α -Al matrix. The borders/HAZ showed a 
coarser microstructure, albeit still fine, with lower content of i- 
QC phases, which are overall larger than those in the center of the 
melt pool. QC approximant (Al

(2) The LPBFed Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy exhibits outstanding thermal sta -
bility and excellent mechanical performance at temperatures of 
up to 500 
◦
C. Yield strength values of ~300 MPa and ~180 MPa 
were reached at 300 
◦

significantly higher than those attained by A319, 6061-T6 and Al- 
8%wt.Cu – Mg – Ag cast alloys – three of the most common heat- 
resistant Al alloys. The levels of high-temperature strength ach -
ieved here were also higher than those exhibited by ceramic 
particle-reinforced aluminum alloys or even alloys containing 
costly elements such as Sc and Zr. The outstanding mechanical 
behavior of LPBF Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy is attributed to the high 
thermal stability of ultrafine i-QC particles that precipitate in situ 
during processing. 
(3) A model for predicting the yield strength of LPBFed Al – Fe – Cr – Ti 
is proposed here based on the resulting composite microstructure 
and its Vickers microhardness, which depend on the LPBF process 
parameters applied in the production of samples. 
(4) The proposed model shows excellent agreement with the exper -
imental results obtained in this work, indicating that the yield 
strength of LPBFed Al – Fe – Cr – Ti can be accurately predicted 
based on optical microscopy images (to measure the content of 
each region (center and border/HAZ)) and on Vickers micro -
hardness tests (to measure Meyer ’ s hardness coefficient). 
(5) This paper introduces a practical methodology for predicting and 
optimizing the mechanical properties of the metastable 
Al – Fe – Cr – Ti alloy produced via LPBF. The alloy is characterized 
by two distinct structural regions: the heat-affected zone located 
at the border of the molten pool, which typically displays a 
coarser and partially decomposed structure, and the remaining 
region (center of the molten pool). Optical microscopy enables 
clear differentiation between these two regions, which also 
exhibit unique properties. By incorporating the fraction and 
micromechanical properties of these distinct regions, a model is 
presented to forecast the mechanical properties of this alloy. This 
methodology can be readily applied to other types of alloys with 
similar characteristics.
