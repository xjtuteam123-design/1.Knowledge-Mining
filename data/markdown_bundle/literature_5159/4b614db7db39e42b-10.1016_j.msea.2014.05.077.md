Abstract 
Four quinary Fe-Al-Mo-Ti-B and Fe-Al-W-Ti-B alloys based on Fe 3Al produced by vacuum 
induction melting were investigated regarding their microstructure and mechanical properties. 
The microstructures consist of a Fe 3Al matrix with complex borides along grain boundaries. 
Compared to other Fe 3Al-based alloys the present alloys show favourably low ductile-to-
brittle transition temperatures (BDTT) and a markedly high creep strength at 650 °C. The 
latter is most pronounced for an alloy whose strength, be sides strengthening by solid solution 
hardening through Mo and by the boride precipitates, is further enhanced by stabilising the 
D03-ordered structure up to higher temperatures.

1. Introduction 
 Fe-Al based alloys are well known for their excellent corrosion resistance at high 
temperatures and are therefore considered as potential structural materials [1-3]. Moreover, 
they have lower densities compared to steels or iron. However, poor strength at high 
temperatures and low ductility at room temperature limit their application yet. Hence, the 
properties of Fe-Al based alloys have to be improved by alloying and respective strategies 
have been reviewed [4-9]. 
 The designation Fe
3Al-based alloys is frequently used for Fe-Al alloys with about 25 at.% Al, 
though the intermetallic phase Fe 3Al with D0 3 order is only stable below 545 °C. At higher 
temperatures these alloys show B2 order or are disordered A2. It has been shown that addition 
of B and Ti to Fe 3Al-based alloys can lead to the formation of TiB 2, which significantly 
improves strength at elevated temperatures without de terioration of the ductility at ambient 
temperatures [10-12]. The strengthening effect is due to the formation of fine TiB 2 
precipitates at the grain boundaries of the Fe 3Al matrix. In addition, the TiB 2 precipitates are 
highly resistant to coarsening which results in effective pinning of the grain boundaries thus 
inhibiting grain growth of the Fe3Al matrix at high temperatures [7,11-13]. 
 Alloying with Mo increases the wet corrosion resistance of Fe-Al alloys because it has the 
strongest passivation effect in acidic conditions [14,15]. It also increases the critical 
temperature cT of the D03 /g316 B2 transition [16-20]. Stabilisation of D03 order combined with 
notable solid solution hardening increases the strength of the Fe-Al-based alloys [8]. W has a 
low solid solubility in Fe-Al alloys. However, it is one of the most effective elements to 
improve the strength of Fe
3Al-based alloys through solid solution hardening [21]. 
 The aim of the present study was to examine the combined effect of Ti + B and Mo or W on 
the microstructure and the mechanical properties of Fe 3Al-based alloys, i.e. to investigate 
whether TiB 2 or other borides form, whether they contribute to strengthening at high 
temperatures and if W and Mo preferentially enter into the Fe-Al matrix to improve their 
properties.

2. Experimental 
Four quinary alloys were prepared from high purity Fe (99.98 wt. %), Al (99.99 
wt. %), Mo (99.99 wt. %), W (99.99 wt. %), B (99.99 wt. %) and Ti (99.99 wt. %) by vacuum 
induction melting under argon. A cold copper mould with Ø 18 mm was used for casting. One 
part of each alloy was annealed at 1000 °C for 100 hours and cooled slowly under argon in 
the furnace. The nominal compositions are listed in Table 1. The actual compositions 
established by wet chemical analysis are in good agreement with the nominal compositions 
(see Table 1). 
 Slices of the alloys were cut by electrical discharge machining (EDM). For light 
optical microscopy (LOM), the samples were first grinded up to 4000 grit, and then polished 
with 3 /g541m and 1 /g541m diamond spray and oxide polishing suspension (OPS), and finally etched 
with Ti2 solution (68 vol.% glycerine, 16 vol.% 70% HNO
3, 16 vol.% 40% HF). For 
observations of polished samples in the scanning electron microscope (SEM) a JEOL JSM-
6490 operated at 15-25 kV was used. 
Chemical compositions of individual phases were established by wavelength-
dispersive spectrometry (WDS) carried out on an electron probe micro analyser (EPMA) 
JEOL JXA-8100. The instrument was operated at 15 kV, 20 nA and the pure elements were 
used as standards. 
Crystal structures of the phases were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) of 
metallographic sections. On a Seifert Type ID 3003 the 2/g537 range from 20 ° to 125 ° was 
scanned using Co-K/g302
1 radiation (/g540=1.7890 nm). Lattice parameters were refined by the least-
square fitting software LCLSQ 8.5 [22]. 
Differential thermal analysis (DTA) was performed on a Setaram SETSY-18 DTA. 
Experiments were carried out in alumina crucibles under an argon atmosphere at a heating 
rate of 20 K/min. The accuracy of the temperature calibration is ±1 K. The reproducibility of 
the results was checked by repeated measurements of the same samples for heating and 
cooling. 
Microhardness was measured by using a LECO M-400-H1 machine. A Vickers 
indenter with 300 g load was applied with an indentation time of 15 s. For compressive stress-
strain tests, samples of 5 × 5 × 10 mm
3 in size were cut by EDM. Tests were performed in air 
between room temperature and 1000 °C at a constant strain rate of 10 -4 s-1 using a universal 
spindle-driven testing machine from SCHENCK TREBEL. The same machine was employed 
to examine the brittle-to-ductile transition temperatures (BDTT) by four-point bending tests. 
Samples for the bending tests with dimensions of 3 × 6 × 18 mm
3 were cut along the length of

3.1 Phases and microstructures 
All as-cast and annealed alloys have been examined by LOM and SEM. Figure 1 
shows the microstructures of the alloys containing Mo in the as-cast and annealed condition. 
The as-cast alloys show a Fe
3Al matrix and a eutectic consisting of Fe 3Al and a boride. After 
1000°C /100 h annealing the eutectics coarsened and the boride is distributed 
inhomogeneously along grain boundaries. The alloy with the higher Mo content (Fe-25.4Al-
4.2Mo-0.5Ti-1.2B; all compositions in at.%) exhibits a larger volume fraction of eutectic 
microstructure and a larger volume fraction of boride in the as-cast and heat-treated condition, 
respectively. 
Figure 2 shows SEM-BSE micrographs of the alloys containing W. The 
microstructures resemble those of the Mo containing alloys in that a Fe
3Al matrix with a 
eutectic are observed in the as-cast condition. After annealing at 1000 °C for 100 h, again a 
boride is found inhomogeneously distributed along the Fe 3Al grain boundaries. Also, as in 
case of the Mo containing alloys, the alloy with the higher W content exhibits the larger 
volume fraction of eutectic in the as-cast condition and the higher volume fraction of boride 
after heat treating. 
While the eutectics are too fine-scaled in the as-cast alloys to be analysed by EPMA, 
all phases have been analysed in the heat treated alloys (Tables 2, 3). The Fe and Al content in 
the Fe3Al matrix is higher than those of the bulk chemical compositions (Table 1), while Mo, 
W, and Ti are depleted in the Fe 3Al matrix with respect to the bulk chemical composition. 
The content of B in the Fe 3Al matrix is below the detection limit of 10 wt. ppm. Table 3 
shows the compositions of the second phases in the ann ealed alloys. Apparently, in all alloys 
the second phase is a Fe-Mo-Ti or Fe-W-Ti boride, which only contain minor amounts of Al.

cast condition and after annealing 1000 °C/100 h. Two phases can be clearly identified. Fe 3Al 
(the matrix) is D0 3 ordered. It is noted, that due to the texture within the metallographic 
section some reflections are missing, e.g., (220) and (440) are missing in the XRD of the

3.2. Mechanical properties 
Table 7 presents the microhardness of the Fe 3Al matrix of the four alloys before and 
after annealing and that of binary Fe3Al [26]. It shows that annealing at 1000 °C for 100 h did 
not affect the microhardness. Compared to binary Fe 3Al, the present alloying additions show 
no marked effect on the microhardness. 
Figure 6 shows the compressive 0.2% yield stress of the annealed alloys as a function of 
temperature. At room temperature the compressive 0.2% yield stress of the Mo containing 
alloys is about the same as for binary Fe-26Al [27] while the yield stresses for the W 
containing alloys are about 100 MPa higher. All alloys show the so called “yield stress 
anomaly” (YSA), i.e. an increase of the yield stress at intermediate temperatures. The 
maximum of the YSA for the Mo containing alloys is at about 500 to 550 °C, i.e. at about the 
same temperature than for binary Fe-26Al [27], while the YSAs for the W containing alloys 
are at about 450 to 500 °C. It is noted that at least one of the alloys (Fe-25.2Al-0.5W-0.5Ti-
1.1B) shows a two-fold YSA, which had already been observed before in case of some Fe-Al-
Ti alloys [28]. It is also noted that the present results are further evidence that there is no 
relation between the YSA and the D0
3 /g316 B2 transformation [27], i.e. while the temperature 
of the YSA is more or less not affected by the present alloying elements, 03 2DB
CT /g108 increases 
by up to 115 °C. At temperatures above the YSA the yield stresses of the investigated alloys 
are markedly higher than those of binary Fe-26Al [27] and are comparable to those of other 
Fe3Al-based alloys strengthened by boride precipitates [11]. 
Four-point bending tests are a standard technique to evaluate the brittle-to-ductile 
transition temperature (BDTT) of alloys [29]. In this study, all annealed alloys were tested in 
the “as-cut” condition and after polishing. The results of Risanti et al. [29] showed that Fe-Al 
alloys with Al contents between 20 and 30 at.% Al are ductile at about 100 – 150 °C. 
However, actually alloys in this composition range do not show a marked BDTT. Instead, the 
transition from brittle to ductile behaviour occurs over a temperature range of a couple of 
100 °C. Such an extended tra nsition range is also observed in case of all present alloys 
(Figures 7, 8).
 
Figure 7a shows the fracture strain (referring to the outer fibre in four-point bending) 
as a function of temperature for the alloys with addition of Mo, binary Fe-25Al [29] and 
ternary Fe-26Al-5Mo [30]. The alloy Fe-25.5Al-2.1Mo-0.5Ti-1.2B is ductile at 100 °C 
(Figure 7a), which is lower compared to Fe-25Al (175 °C) [29]. Because of a limited number 
of samples, the temperature where Fe-25.4Al-4.2Mo-0.5Ti-1.2B becomes fully ductile could 
not be evaluated. However, as this temperature is 500 °C for the “as-cut” samples (see below), 
it can be estimated to be at about 400 °C in the polished condition. This is considerably lower 
compared to ternary Fe-26Al-5Mo, which only becomes ductile at 600 °C [30]. Figure 7b

## Page 8

shows the fracture strain behaviour of the alloys with addition of W and that of quaternary Fe-
26Al-0.75W-0.75B [11]. Again, all alloys show no marked BDTT but become ductile after a 
transition range of about 200 °C. The three alloys are ductile at 200 – 250 °C, i.e. only slightly 
higher than binary Fe-25Al (175 °C) [29]. The results of the bending tests are remarkable in 
that usually ternary and higher-order additions to Fe-Al alloys result in a pronounced increase 
of the BDTT [31]. Thereby the present results confirm that boron effectively inhibits a 
marked increase of the BDTT in higher order Fe-Al-X-… alloys [11]. 
This is remarkable as all investigated alloys show boride precipitates along grain 
boundaries. Alloying with boron is effective in enhancing the ductility of a number of 
intermetallic alloys, but according to common understanding only as long as it is in solid 
solution [32]. For Fe-Al alloys with Al contents of about 40 at.% Al Fraczkiewicz et al. found 
that boron in solid solution is enriched at the grain boundaries [33, 34]. First principles 
calculations suggest that the strong Fe-B interactions lead to an improved cohesion of the 
grains [35]. However, the present results show that at least in Fe
3Al based alloys boron may 
also be effective in enhancing ductility when it forms boride precipitates, in accordance with 
previous observations on such alloys [11]. This behaviour is quite different from that of other 
intermetallic alloys, where it has been in general observed that the beneficial effect of boron is 
not any longer effective when precipitates form along grain boundaries [32]. From the present 
investigation it cannot be concluded what the beneficial effect of boron/borides in the present 
alloys is, e.g. whether it may reduce the sensitivity of Fe
3Al for environmental embrittlement. 
Figure 8 shows the temperature dependence of the fracture strain of the unpolished 
(“as-cut”) alloys. In this condition the temperatures where the alloys become ductile are about 
100 °C higher compared to the polished samples. That the unpolished samples, i.e. those with 
higher surface roughness, always become ductile at higher temperatures than the polished 
samples hints to notch sensitivity of the investigated alloys, comparable to binary Fe3Al alloys 
[36]. 
Compressive creep testing was conducted at 650 °C. Figure 9 shows the stress 
dependence of the secondary creep rate of the investigated alloys. The creep resistance of the 
alloy with 4.2 at.% Mo is markedly higher than those of the other three investigated alloys. 
The difference between this alloy and the others is that the Fe-Al matrix is in Fe-25.4Al-
4.2Mo-0.5Ti-1.2B is D0 3-ordered at 650 °C while in the other alloys it has B2 order at this 
temperature (Figure 5). Hence, the excellent creep resistance of Fe-25.4Al-4.2Mo-0.5Ti-1.2B 
at 650 °C can be related to the D0 3-order, which leads to much higher strength of the Fe-Al 
matrix as in case of B2 order [37].

From the slopes of the plots the stress exponent n is derived according to Norton’s 
power law (Figure 9). All alloys show similar values for the stress exponent n, ranging from 
3.8 to 4.5. Values for n of 3 - 5 are typically observed for Fe-Al alloys and suggest that creep 
is controlled by climb and glide of dislocations [38]. It is noted that for Fe-25.2Al-0.5W-
0.5Ti-1.1B a change in the stress exponent is observed after about 400 h, when 
n increases to 
a value of 9.6. Such high values suggest that creep is controlled by precipitates or fine 
particles. Actually, post mortem investigation of the crept sample revealed that during creep at 
650 °C a new phase precipitated along the grain boundaries (Figure 10), but the size and 
volume fraction of this phase are too small for further identification. All oth er alloys showed 
no changes in the microstructure and composition of the phases after creep. 
Figure 11 shows the comparison of the creep behaviour of various Fe 3Al-based alloys 
at 650 °C. The present alloys show a favourable creep strength compared to other Fe 3Al-
based alloys strengthened by borides [11] or carbides [39-41] (data extrapolated from 675 °C 
in [39] and data interpolated from 600 and 700 °C in [40]). It seems that the combined effect 
of strengthening by complex borides and solid solution hardening of the Fe-Al matrix by Mo 
or W is quite effective. This is in accordance with a previous study on Fe 3Al-based alloys 
strengthened by boride precipitates, where Fe -26Al-0.75W-0.75B showed the highest creep 
resistance [11]. However, compared to P92, an advanced iron based (Fe-9 wt.% Cr-
WMoVCN) alloy for steam turbine applications, the alloys which are only strengthened by 
precipitates and solid solution hardening show an inferior creep strength [42]. But when in 
addition the order of the Fe-Al matrix is increased from B2 to D0 3, in case of Fe-25.4Al-
4.2Mo-0.5Ti-1.2B a creep strength is achieved, which even surpasses that of P92 at 650 °C.

Summary and conclusions 
 
Fe
3Al-based alloys with additions of Ti, B and Mo or W were investigated with respect to 
structural characterisation and mechanical tests, which include microhardness tests at room 
temperature, compression tests between room temperature and 1000 °C, four-point bending 
tests as well as creep tests at 650 °C. 
 Following important conclusions are drawn: 
(1) All investigated alloys show a microstructure that consists of a Fe
3Al matrix with 
complex borides along the grain boundaries. In the Mo-containing alloys the boride is of 
Mo
2FeB2-type, while in the W-containing alloys a complex boride possibly of Cr 23C6-
type is observed.

DTA analysis shows that the additions of Mo (+Ti) and W (+Ti) increase the critical 
temperatures 22B A
CT /g108and 32DO B
CT /g108. Addition of W has a more pronounced effect on increasing 
22B A
CT /g108 than addition of the same amount of Mo, though they have about the same atomic 
radii. This disagrees with the theory that the main reason for the increase of the critical 
transition temperatures should be the atomic size effect [19]. 
(2) The microhardness tests confirm the pronounced solid solution hardening of Fe3Al by W. 
All alloys show a pronounced yield stress anomaly (YSA), at the same or a slightly lower 
temperature than binary Fe-26Al. Because the transition temperature 32 DO B
CT /g108increases by 
addition of Mo (+Ti) and W (+Ti) compared to binary Fe-26Al, this gives further evidence 
that there is no relation between the YSA and 32DO B
CT /g108, as has been repeatedly claimed [19, 
43-46]. 
(3) All investigated alloys pass from brittle to ductile behaviour within a larger temperature 
range instead of showing a marked BDTT. Compared to other Fe-Al-X-… alloys, all 
investigated alloys become ductile at low temperatures, comparable or even lower than 
binary Fe-Al alloys of the same Al content. 
(4) While the creep strength at 650 °C of three of the four investigated alloys resembles that 
of Fe
3Al-based alloys strengthened by borides or carbides reported in the literature before 
[11, 39-41], the creep strength of the fourth alloy is considerably higher. This is related to 
the fact that through alloying with Mo the Fe-Al matrix is D03-ordered at this temperature./g3
