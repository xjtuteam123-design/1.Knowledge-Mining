We have studied the large-scale plasticity assisted topological inversion in a Ni-based superalloy after 
creep at 850 °C. Multi-scale characterization from the micro- to the near-atomic scale was used to un- 
ravel the processes governing topological inversion of a Ni-based single crystal superalloy and reconstruct 
the resulting three-dimensional microstructure. A plasticity assisted redistribution of interacting solutes 
mechanism, where shearing dislocations mass-transport solutes, is proposed to explain the transition 
from a rafted to intricately interconnected (inverted) structure. The interplay between microstructural 
evolution and mechanical behavior is discussed in terms of applied stress and microstructural features, 
relating atomic-scale processes to macro-scale behavior.

Ni-based single-crystal superalloys are state-of-the-art materi- 
als that are used for turbine blades of aero-engines, which are 
typically subjected to high loads at high temperatures and corro- 
sive environments [1 , 2] . These alloys derive their outstanding high- 
temperature properties from the unique microstructure consisting 
of a high volume fraction of discreet Ni 3 Al γ ’ precipitates uni- 
formly dispersed in γ matrix [3] . Dissolution of these precipitates 
during service can have a signiﬁcant impact on their mechanical 
response, with the remaining volume fraction controlling the over- 
all creep performance [4 , 5] . Hence, understanding the evolution of 
the γ ’ precipitates that often takes place during deformation at 
elevated temperatures is of utmost importance for safety-critical 
components. 
For instance, upon accumulation of a critical value of strain, 
the γ ’ precipitates undergo concurrent partial dissolution and di- 
rectional coarsening (rafting) [6 , 7] . The rafting process is largely 
governed by the superposition of the applied stress and inter- 
nal coherency stress arising from the lattice misﬁt between the 
two phases, where a positive misﬁt results in rafts oriented par-

allel to the stress axis (P-type rafting) and a negative misﬁt re- 
sults in perpendicular rafts (N-type) under tensile loading. Kon- 
tis et al. [8] have shown that the transport of solutes via dislo- 
cation pipe-diffusion drives this microstructural evolution through 
the so called plasticity assisted redistribution of interacting solutes 
(PARIS) mechanism [9] . Recently, it was shown that the segregation 
of solutes to dislocations is also controlled by the applied strain 
rate [10] –a s the strain rate increases, the level of Re segregation 
to dislocations decreases in a single-crystal Ni-based superalloy. 
Hence, it is important to consider the inﬂuence of the strain rate 
in understanding deformation mechanisms in superalloys based on 
the PARIS mechanism. 
It is often observed that further strain accumulation beyond 
the rafting stage can result in topological inversion of the mi- 
crostructure [6 , 11 ] . In this case, the γ phase essentially becomes 
the precipitates embedded in a matrix of γ ’. This is the so-called 
topological inversion and occurs only in alloys (and at tem- 
peratures) where the γ ’ volume fraction higher than 50%, and 
especially at low applied stresses [1 1] . High temperature exposure 
in the absence of external stress does not result in topological 
inversion [1 1] , implying that it is related to the presence of defects 
generated during creep deformation. Although the exact atomic-

scale processes that drive this inversion and the resulting three- 
dimensional structure remain unclear, this phase transformation 
has direct consequences for the creep resistance of the alloy, as its 
occurrence is reported to coincides with strain accumulation accel- 
eration, i.e. transition to tertiary creep [1 1–14] . As such, topological 
inversion is considered deleterious and is often the subject of dif- 
ferent damage accumulation and creep life prediction models [13] . 
Epishin et al. [12] proposed that the driving force for the inversion 
is the surface diffusion, along the γ - γ ’ interface, of γ ’ elements 
originating from raft-tip dissolution to areas of high concentration 
of dislocations in the γ phase, resulting in γ′ junctions pene- 
trating the γ matrix. However, this has not been experimentally 
veriﬁed and the atomic-scale processes remain unknown. Another 
important question yet to be answered is whether topological 
inversion drives the massive precipitate shearing leading to failure 
by transferring the load to the γ ’ precipitates, or whether shearing 
of γ ’ drives the topological inversion transformation, and how 
does the applied stress inﬂuence the overall process. Unraveling 
the atomic-level processes that govern topological inversion and 
elucidating its true microstructural underpinnings will allow for 
better property models and enable an informed alloy design that 
aims to suppress this phase transformation. 
Here, we propose a mechanism via plasticity assisted redis- 
tribution of solutes occurring during creep in a nickel-based su- 
peralloy at 850 °C, that accounts for the topological inversion. 
In order to evaluate the effect of strain rate on the redistribu- 
tion of solutes, we performed creep tests at different stress levels. 
Three-dimensional microstructural characterization uncovered the 
complex and inter-connected γ - γ ’ structure. Meanwhile, electron 
channeling contrast imaging (ECCI) and atom probe tomography 
(APT) revealed the high level of dislocation activity and segregation 
of solutes to such faults, respectively. The effect of such segregation 
to lattice defects is discussed in the perspective of how solute ele- 
ments are transported on a macro-scale during the creep process, 
leading to the observed microstructural evolution. These ﬁndings 
highlight the interplay between microstructural evolution and me- 
chanical behavior that needs to be accounted for when designing 
new alloys and/or life prediction models. 
Creep specimens were prepared from [001]-oriented single- 
crystal MC2 bars subjected to a standard heat treatment [4] . The 
creep tests were performed under isothermal creep conditions at 
850 °C at 650 and 400 MPa. After rupture, the samples were sec- 
tioned such that all observations and analyses can be made normal 
to the (010) surfaces and ~3 mm away from the fracture surface. 
Scanning electron microscope (SEM) observations were made us- 
ing a Thermo-Fisher Scientiﬁc Apreo operating at 1 kV and 50 pA 
for the γ - γ ’ microstructure, while a Zeiss Merlin SEM operating at 
30 kV and 2.0 nA was used to observe near-surface (up to ~13 nm 
in depth [15] ) dislocation structure. A FEI Helios Nanolabs 600i fo- 
cused ion beam (FIB) was used to for serial sectioning and imaging 
of a 6 μm × 28 μm area using a 50 nm step size. The image set 
was analyzed and reconstructed with the image processing soft- 
ware MIPAR TM [16] , using a custom-developed recipe. Several APT 
specimens from the 850 °C/650 MPa condition were prepared by 
FIB using the protocol outlined in Ref. [17] . APT was conducted us- 
ing a CAMECA LEAP® 50 0 0XR and running conditions set as: base 
temperature of −233 °C (40 K), 30 pJ laser pulse energy, pulse rep- 
etition rate of 200 kHz, and evaporation rate of up to 1.5%. 
The creep behavior of MC2 at 850 °C and 650 MPa and 400 MPa 
is represented by the creep strain vs. time and strain-rate vs. strain 
curves shown in Fig. 1 a and b, respectively. Both samples ruptured 
at similar strains, ~15%, but the creep rupture time at 400 MPa was 
~82 times longer than that at 650 MPa – 1896 h vs 23 h. In this re- 
gard, the strain rate at the 650 MPa condition was more than two 
orders of magnitude higher than that of the 400 MPa condition 
for the entire strain range. Fig. 1 c and d shows the near fracture- 
surface microstructures in the dendrite cores of the two conditions. 
At 650 MPa, the initial cuboidal microstructure is more or less pre- 
served, Fig. 1 c, although upon closer inspection it can be seen that 
the precipitates have started connecting suggesting that γ ’ raft- 
ing has begun – inset of Fig. 1 c. At 400 MPa, the microstructural 
degradation is more advanced, showing irregular N-type rafts with 
a high degree of γ termination points, a telltale sign of topological 
inversion [1 1 , 12] , as shown in Fig. 1 d. The inset shows the wavy 
nature of the raft and channel surfaces, which has led to connec- 
tion of adjacent rafts at different points. Although the two samples 
have different γ / γ ’ microstructures, it should be pointed out that 
the active deformation mechanisms are almost the same –g l i d e 
of a/2 < 110 > { 111 } matrix dislocations. At 650 MPa, the cuboidal γ ’ 
precipitates are mainly circumvented by gliding/climbing disloca- 
tions along the γ / γ ’ interfaces in the vertical γ channels [7 , 18] , 
and are also occasionally sheared by isolated dislocations through 
a stacking fault cutting mechanism (i.e. < 112 > { 111 } type glide). At 
low applied stress (i.e. 400 MPa), most of the initial creep deforma- 
tion is still ensured by < 110 > { 111 } matrix type slip and interfacial 
climb, but the longer duration of the test allows for the forma- 
tion of interfacial networks, leading to γ ’ rafting. Once rafting is 
complete as in the case at 400 MPa, the closure of the vertical γ
channels and interconnections between rafts necessitates that the 
γ ’ rafts are sheared for further deformation [18 , 19] . Next, we fo- 
cus on the 850 °C 400 MPa condition in order to gain insights into 
the mechanism driving this microstructural evolution, where the 
micro- and substructural characterization was primarily carried out 
for the dendrite cores as the interdendritic area is more irregular. 
To gain further insight into the complex structure, serial sec- 
tioning and imaging enabled three-dimensional reconstruction of 
the microstructure. Each phase has been extracted from the main 
reconstruction and the corresponding structures are presented in 
Fig. 2 a and b for the γ (34.5 vol%) and γ ’ (65.5 vol%) phases, re- 
spectively, (videos provided as Supplementary material). Each in- 
dividual reconstruction was rendered such that every discreet pre- 
cipitate is assigned a different color, and it can be thus seen that 
each phase is represented mainly by a single precipitate with a 
very complex interconnected structure. This is in contrast to the 
conventional view that during topological inversion the γ ’ phase 
becomes the matrix containing discreet γ precipitates [1 1 , 14] , a 
conﬁguration that is often used for modeling studies [13] . Indeed, 
Epishin et al. [12] also suggested that the γ phase is intercon- 
nected after SEM observations of deeply-etched microstructures. 
This means that no speciﬁc phase can be considered as the ma- 
trix/precipitate anymore. The resulting complex structure which 
has a higher amount of interfacial area compared to a rafted struc- 
ture, also implies that a minimization of the total interfacial energy 
is not the driving force behind topological inversion. 
Microstructural evolution is governed by the atomic scale pro- 
cesses that occur during thermal and stress exposure, hence ECCI 
and APT were used to interrogate the lattice defect and solute re-

zone ~3 mm away from the fracture surface. The ECC image in

trast, in the γ and γ ’ phases. Fig. 3 a also shows the well-developed 
interfacial dislocation network as evidenced by the intense bright 
contrast at the interfaces of rafts. Interestingly, this structure per- 
sists at points of adjacent raft coalescence, as shown in the inset, 
where the red dashed-lines are offset from the interfaces for easier 
visualization. A three-dimensional reconstruction of an APT speci-

Al atoms and a set of 7 at% Cr isocomposition surfaces. These fea- 
tures represent regions of relatively higher Cr content that can be 
associated with segregation to dislocation and fault structures in 
the γ ’ phase of Ni- and Co-based superalloys [8 , 10 , 20–24] ]. A one-

The microstructural evolution of the γ - γ ’ microstructure dur- 
ing creep has a complex relationship with deformation. Kontis 
et al. [8] explained the sub-structural activity leading to the for- 
mation of rafts during creep of MC2. Here, we focus on the evo- 
lution from the post-rafted microstructure up to topological inver- 
sion at failure, and schematically illustrate this process in Fig. 4 . 
After the minimum strain rate is reached the rafting process is 
more or less complete and the interfacial dislocation network is 
formed for isothermal creep conditions [7 , 18 , 25 , 26] , as depicted 
in Fig 4 a. To accommodate further deformation, dislocations (par- 
tials or perfect depending on the creep conditions) must shear into 
the γ ’ rafts, forming an anti-phase boundary (APB) or a stacking 
fault, as shown in Fig. 4 b. To simplify the schematic, the interfa- 
cial network is omitted in the subsequent steps, but its interac- 
tion with incoming matrix dislocations is considered as the source 
of the dislocations shearing the γ ’. Different studies on Ni- and 
Co-based superalloys have shown that these dislocations in the 
γ ’ exhibit regions of segregation of γ -solutes - primarily of Cr 
and Co [8 , 22 , 27–31] , in agreement with the APT results shown in 
Fig. 3 . The transport of these solutes by the shearing dislocations 
creates regions either depleted of γ -solutes at their origination 
points or enriched in solutes at the opposite interface, as shown 
in Fig. 4 c. These local γ -solute enrichment/depletion regions cause 
the formation/dissolution of γ phase by altering locally the solu- 
bility and consequently the γ - γ ’ equilibrium, respectively, Fig 4 d. 
Upon many such shearing events, the rafts start to exhibit wavy 
interfaces –a s shown in Fig. 1 d –a n d eventually interconnect, 
Fig. 4 e, forming the complex structure shown in Fig. 4 f (locally) 
and Fig. 2 (globally) via the PARIS mechanism for redistribution of 
solutes. He et al. [30] showed that when this process is highly lo- 
calized as in the case of slip planes, the formation of { 111 } rafts 
along the slip bands ensues. As shown in Fig. 3 a, this process can 
also “trap” the interfacial dislocation network between two con- 
necting rafts. This can have direct consequences on the mechanical 
properties as it will promote a high number of γ ’ shearing events

Another important aspect to consider is the fault velocity, as 
it has been linked to the level of solute enrichment at the dislo- 
cation core [10 , 34] . The higher the applied stress, the faster the 
strain rate (and dislocation velocity) and consequently there will 
be a lower level of solute transport. As shown in Fig 1 , the creep 
samples for both stress levels reach the same strain level, how- 
ever, the strain rate of the 650 MPa condition is two orders of 
magnitude higher (and correspondingly represents a faster defect 
velocity) compared to that of the 400 MPa sample. Hence, topo- 
logical inversion does not take place at 850 °C/650 MPa, where the 
lower level of solute transport reduces the microstructural evolu- 
tion kinetics, and the presence of vertical γ channels suppresses 
shearing of the γ ’ precipitates. Our observations show that the re- 
distribution of solutes via the PARIS mechanism can inﬂuence dra- 
matically the mechanical performance of superalloys. It becomes 
apparent that the interplay between time-dependent mechanical 
behavior and microstructural evolution is complex and governed 
by solute partitioning to lattice defects for a given defect velocity 
(as dictated by stress level) and subsequent mass transport. Thus, 
controlling the redistribution of solutes can allow us to design su- 
peralloys with enhanced properties. Finally, this mechanism will be 
operative for most, if not all, superalloys subjected to creep, how- 
ever, whenever the γ ’ volume fraction is more than 50%, the close 
proximity of rafts results in a more complex microstructure that is 
viewed as a topological inversion. On that note, since the interden- 
dritic areas deform relatively more easily [35] and have higher γ ’ 
volume fractions, the degree of microstructural degradation would 
be higher in those areas compared to the dendritic cores. This has 
been experimentally conﬁrmed for CMSX4 by Schwalbe et al. [26] , 
who showed that the interdendiric regions exhibited an earlier in- 
version than the dendrite cores. 
In summary, we have related the near-atomic-scale processes to 
the macro-scale behavior aiming to understand the topological in- 
version of the single-crystal nickel-based superalloy after creep at 
850 °C. The three dimensional microstructural characterization re- 
vealed that upon inversion, the two phases develop into complex 
interconnected structures, where no phase can be considered the 
matrix any longer. APT showed segregation of γ solutes to disloca- 
tions in the γ ’ phase, which can be transported via shearing, and 
thereby drive the microstructural evolution. During coalescence of 
adjacent rafts, interfacial dislocations can become trapped in the 
γ ’ phase and promote additional shearing. The applied stress plays 
an important role in this process as it determines the fault ve- 
locity, which has been shown to inﬂuence the segregation level, 
and therefore governs the degree of microstructural evolution. It 
is apparent that the redistribution of solutes controls microstruc- 
tural alterations in superalloys during creep and it should be taken 
into consideration when designing new alloys and or developing 
microstructural evolution and mechanical behavior models.
