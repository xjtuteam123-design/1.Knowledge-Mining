Abstract
Single-phase Fe–Al–Ti alloys with the Heusler-type L2 1 structure and two-phase L21 Fe–Al–Ti alloys with MgZn 2-type Laves phase or
Mn23Th6-typet2 phase precipitates were studied with respect to hardness at room temperature, compressive 0.2% yield stress at 20–11008C,
brittle-to-ductile transition temperature (BDTT), creep resistance at 800 and 1000 8C and oxidation resistance at 20–1000 8C. At high
temperatures the L21 Fe–Al–Ti alloys show considerable strength and creep resistance which are superior to other iron aluminide alloys.
Alloys with not too high Ti and Al contents exhibit a yield stress anomaly with a maximum at temperatures as high as 7508C. BDTT ranges
between 675 and 900 8C. Oxidation at 900 8C is controlled by parabolic scale growth.

1. Introduction
Iron aluminides are promising for applications as
structural high-temperature alloys because of their out-
standing high-temperature corrosion resistance [1–4] .
Moreover, they offer a density advantage compared to
other iron-base materials such as cast iron and steels. A
sufﬁcient strength and creep resistance are vital properties
for high temperature applications. In this respect iron
aluminides need to be improved by proper alloying or
precipitation of second phases and appropriate strategies for
iron aluminides have been discussed in some detail [5–9] .
The intermetallic compound Fe
3Al with D0 3 structure
has been extensively studied and there are several
reviews on its properties [7,10–15] . In addition to its
good corrosion resistance in oxidising and sulﬁdising
environments [1–4,11] ,F e
3Al is considered for structural
applications because it shows a higher strength than
comparable iron alloys [11]. In view of structural
applications, its low ductility at ambient temperatures
and the loss of strength above the critical temperature T
c
for the D0 3 $ B2 transition are major concerns [7,11,12].
It has been shown that the low ductility of the iron
aluminides at ambient temperatures is not an intrinsic
property, but is caused by environmental embrittlement
[7,16–18] . Sufﬁcient ductility in Fe
3Al can be obtained
by alloying, especially with Cr [19–21] together with
increasing the Al-content from 25 to 28 at.% [14,22].
Efforts to enhance strength at elevated temperatures have
been less successful up to now. The problem is connected
with the limited stability of the D0
3 structure which is stable
only up to the critical temperature Tc of about 550 8C for
binary Fe–Al alloys with 25 at.% Al. Tc decreases
progressively as the Al content deviates from 25 at.% Al.
Elements have been looked for which increase T
c:
Molybdenum, titanium and silicon indeed increase Tc
[23–25] , and an improved high-temperature strength of
the iron aluminides has been found [14,26,27].
In Fe3Al (D03) Al occupies the position 4(a) at 0 0 0 in
the unit cell and Fe is on the positions 4(b) 1/2 1/2 1/2 and
8(c) 1/4 1/4 1/4. The substitution of Fe by a third element on
the 4(b) position yields the L2
1 structure of the Heusler
phase, i.e. the D0 3 structure can be regarded as the binary
limiting case of the L21 structure [28] as both structures are
structural isotypes (Pearson symbol cF16). Athanassiadis
et al. showed that e.g. titanium preferentially substitutes for
Fe on the 4(b) position[29]. In the present work, D03 is only

Phase equilibria in the Fe–Al–Ti system have been well
established in previous studies [28,30–34] . Ohnuma et al.
[28] characterised the stability of the L21 structure along the
lines Fe3Al–Fe 2TiAl and FeAl–Fe 2TiAl. They showed that
there is complete solid solubility between binary Fe 3Al and
stoichiometric Fe 2TiAl. The substitution of Fe by Ti
increases the critical temperature Tc for the D03/L21 $ B2
transition progressively from 550 8C in the binary Fe–Al
system up to a maximum of 1215 8C at about the
stoichiometric composition of Fe 2TiAl. This means that in
isothermal sections above 550 8C the L21 structure is stable
in an island-like composition range which extends from the
stoichiometric composition Fe–25Al–25Ti to lower Ti
contents, below which B2 is found. At 800 8C the lower
limit is between about 5 and 12.5 at.% Ti, the Ti content
increasing with increasing Al content, while at 1000 8C this
limit is between about 12 and 15 at.% Ti [31,32]. The
extension of the stability range of the L2
1 structure as well as
the respective multi-phase equilibria at 900 8C are shown in
the isothermal section of the ternary phase diagram inFig. 1.
In addition to possible strengthening by increasing the
stability of the L2 1 structure to higher temperatures, i.e. by
Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al with L2 1 structure, and apart from solid–
solution strengthening, there is the possibility of precipi-
tation of second phases, especially a hexagonal Laves phase
with C14 structure, for achieving further strengthening.
According to the established isothermal sections at 800, 900,
and 1000 8C the two-phase equilibrium L2
1 Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al þ
Laves phase is stable within a considerable composition
range and the compositions of the coexisting phases change
only little with varying temperature between 800 and
1000 8C [31–33] . For a detailed review of the phase
equilibria in the Fe–Al–Ti system the reader is referred to
the assessments by Raghavan [35,36].
The aim of the present study is the evaluation of basic
properties of L2
1-ordered Fe–Al–Ti alloys. Several single-
phase alloys and two-phase alloys with ﬁxed volume
fractions of second phases (after equilibration heat treat-
ment below the solidus) have been produced. Properties
have been studied with respect to variations of alloy
composition, state of atomic ordering in the alloy matrix,
i.e. L2
1 or B2 structure, and precipitates.
2. Experimental
Fifteen alloys ( Table 1 ) each with a mass of about
400–600 g were produced from the pure elements by
induction melting under argon. Rods of about 22 mm
diameter and 200 mm length were cast by pouring the melt
into cold copper moulds. In addition, 13 alloys from a
previous study on the phase equilibria in the Fe–Al–Ti
system [31], which were produced by levitation melting,
were used for complementary hardness tests.
The actual compositions of the alloys were established
by electron probe microanalysis (EPMA) by measuring ﬁve
grids of 225 points each on the as-cast alloys and then
calculating the average for the 1125 points. Otherwise
conditions for EPMA were the same as used before for the
measurement of Fe–Ti–Al alloys [33]. Table 1 reveals that
actual compositions are quite close to the nominal
compositions.
Samples for metallography, mechanical and oxidation
testing were cut from the rods by electrical discharge
machining (EDM). The microstructures of the as-cast
samples were studied by light optical microscopy after
etching the samples with ‘Ti2’ solution (68 ml glycerine,
16 ml 70%-HNO
3 and 16 ml 40%-HF).
Hardness measurements were performed using a Fischer-
scope H100 unit with a 1368 Vickers indenter [37–39] . The
continuous recording of the indentation depth as a function
of the applied load during loading and unloading provided
data for Young’s modulus, universal hardness HU (from the
indentation depth under load), plastic hardness HV (from
the indentation depth after unloading, corresponding to
Vickers hardness) and ratio of plastic and elastic work RPE.
In addition, conventional Vickers hardness tests to obtain
HV
0.2 data were performed on alloys from a previous study
as mentioned above.
For compressive yield stress and creep tests specimens of
5 £ 5 £ 10 mm3 were cut by EDM with subsequent parallel-
polishing of the end surfaces. Yield stresses were deter-

3.3. 0.2% yield stress
Fig. 4a and b show the compressive yield stress as a
function of temperature for the alloys 1–5 (group I) and for
a binary Fe-30 at.% Al alloy [40] for comparison. The most
prominent feature in Fig. 4a is the anomalous temperature
dependence of the ﬂow stress. It is noted that above 800 8C
the highest ﬂow stresses are shown by alloys 1 and 2
according to Fig. 4b. Alloy 5 shows a deviating behaviour,
i.e. there are higher yield stresses up to 600 8C, while above
600 8C markedly lower yield stresses are found compared to
those of alloys 1–4.
Although alloys 1 and 1A have the same nominal
composition, the as-cast alloy 1A exhibits much higher
yield stresses at temperatures below 700 8C than the heat-
treated alloy 1 as illustrated by Fig. 5 . This difference is
attributed to a higher volume fraction of Laves
phase in the as-cast state (see Section 4.1 and Section
4.6). In addition, Fig. 5 exempliﬁes the effect of a heat
treatment at 400 8C on the 0.2% yield stress. The yield
stress at 400 8C decreases with increasing prior annealing
time.
Compared to the single-phase alloys, the ﬂow stresses
of the two-phase alloys cover a much wider range ( Fig. 6a
and b ). A pronounced yield stress anomaly has been
observed only for the two-phase alloys 6 and 7, i.e. those

4.1. Microstructure
The alloy compositions were chosen according to the

alloys, the microstructures ( Fig. 2 ) of which differed in
some cases considerably from those expected from the
isothermal section in Fig. 1. This is caused by considerable
differences in the compositions of the Laves phase anda (a

the details the eutectic trough between the areas of primary

The differences between the microstructures of the as-

This alloy with nearly eutectic composition shows a eutectic

eutectics or few Laves phase. The amount of residual

the eutectic composition. All phases reported in Table 1

re-investigation of the Fe–Al–Ti liquidus surface [34].
The microstructures according to equilibrium at
800–1000 8C, which are indicated in Table 1 , can be

temperatures. Indeed the constituent phases of alloys 1, 2
and 12, which were heat-treated at 1000 8C for 96 h after
solidiﬁcation, were found to correspond to equilibrium at
1000 8C. Any testing of the alloys at elevated temperatures
may lead to changes in their microstructures if the initial

C14 Laves phase as majority component in the initial as-cast

dissolve quickly at the employed high testing temperatures.

4.3. Brittle-to-ductile transition temperature (BDTT)
The observed BDTT values are in the range of
650–900 8C and are increased by heat-treating the as-cast

BDTT range of 600–900 8C was also found by Eumann[46]

7Mo6 or

amounts of Laves phase or (Fe,Al)12Zr precipitates [47]. For
Fe3AlC-base alloys BDTT was found to increase from 600
to 1000 8C with increasing carbon content [48].
4.4. Effect of order on yield stress
An anomalous temperature dependence of the yield
stress with peak temperatures of about 700 8C is indicated
most clearly by the data for the heat-treated alloys 1 and 2
(Fig. 4a ), which are single-phase with the L2
1 structure
(Table 1 ). The other L2 1 alloys 3 and 4 with few Laves
phase precipitates again show peak temperatures of 700 and
750 8C, respectively. However, for the corresponding binary
Fe3Al alloys with the analogous D0 3 structure the ﬂow
stress maximum is between 450 and 550 8C [7] as is
illustrated by the data for the Fe-30at.%Al alloy of Hartig
et al. [40] in Fig. 5. An Fe-26at.%Al-4at.% Ti alloy with L21
structure shows a peak temperature of about 550 8C [49]
whereas the peak temperature for an Fe-25at.%Al-10at.% Ti
alloy is about 600 8C and for an Fe-25at.%Al-15at.% Ti
about 800 8C [41,50]. Obviously the peak temperature
increases with increasing Ti content.
In the past the yield stress anomaly of Fe
3Al alloys was
related to the partial reduction of long-range order just
below the D0
3(L21)/B2 transition temperature Tc since the
anomalous yield stress peak was observed in the vicinity of
T
c and the peak was raised to higher temperatures as Tc was
increased by ternary alloying additions, e.g. [10,26,51].
More recent investigations ruled out a direct relation
between T

supported by the present ﬁndings as Tc for the D03(L21)/B2
transition [28] is up to 570 8C higher than the observed yield
stress peak temperatures. In particular Tc for alloy 1A is
about 1190 8C while the yield stress peak is observed at
about 625 8C.
The yield stress at room temperature of the L2 1
Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al alloys 1–5 ( Fig. 4a ) as well as the hardness
at room temperature ( Fig. 3a) inreases with decreasing Ti
content (alloys 1–3 and 4–5) and increasing Al content
(alloys 3–5). A similar effect, i.e. increasing compressive
yield strength with decreasing Ti content has been reported
also for Fe
3Al-base alloys containing 0 to 15 at.% Ti [41,
49]. A contrasting effect, i.e. a decreasing tensile strength
and increasing elongation with decreasing Ti content was
reported in the past [1] for Fe–Al–Ti alloys with less than
20 at.% Al and only little Ti contents up to 3 at.%, i.e. alloys
with disordered A2 structure.
The stoichiometric composition for L2
1 Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al is
given by the formula Fe 2TiAl. Thus a decreasing Ti
content and an increasing Al content for the sequence of
alloys 1-5 means an increasing deviation from stoichi-
ometry with increasing constitutional disorder, i.e. an
increasing concentration of constitutional point defects.
This is supported by the composition dependence of the
critical temperature T
c for the B2/L2 1 transition, which
decreases from about 1200 8C (alloy 1) to 900 8C (alloy 5)
[28]. The variation of the concentration of thermal point
defects (vacancies and antistructure atoms) with compo-
sition may be less signiﬁcant for these alloys since the
temperature of melting varies only by less than 100 8C for
these alloys [34]. It is well known that the degree of order
has a strong effect on mechanical properties, e.g. [53], and
in particular the ﬂow stress of binary Fe
3Al increases with
decreasing degree of order [51,54]. This variation is caused
by the constitutional point defects, which are immobile at
low temperatures and act as obstacles to dislocation
movement (see e.g. [55]). It is noted that for B2-ordered
FeAl alloys the hardening effect of thermal vacancies has
been veriﬁed in much detail (e.g. [56–59] ). Accordingly
the increase of the yield stress of alloys 1-5 at room
temperature with decreasing Ti content and increasing Al

4.5. Effect of second phases on yield stress
The alloys 6–12 with additional second phases, which
were tested in the as-cast state ( Table 1 ), show a more
complex behaviour ( Fig. 6a ). The yield stress at room
temperature of alloy 6 with L2
1 Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al matrix is
higher than those of alloys 2–4 with similar Ti contents.
This is attributed to the hardening effect of the residual
eutectics with Laves phase along the grain boundaries
forming a hard skeleton. The yield stress of alloy 7 at room
temperature is much higher than that of alloy 6 by nearly a
factor of 2 since its matrix is the harder Laves phase at room
temperature. This Laves phase matrix in alloy 7 is not stable
at higher temperatures, i.e. it transforms to an L2
1
Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al matrix with residual Laves phase, the faster
the higher the temperature. Indeed, the yield stress of alloy 7
steeply decreases with rising temperature to approach the
yield stress of alloy 6 at 400 8C. Alloys 6 and 7 then exhibit
a ﬂow stress anomaly with a peak temperature of 600 8C
which is lower than those for alloys 1–4 with similar Ti
contents and which may be attributed to the lower Al
contents. Above 600 8C alloys 6 and 7 show equal yield
stresses which decrease with increasing temperature to
reach the yield stresses of alloys 1–4 at 1000 8C( Figs. 4b
and 6b ). Obviously the effects of matrix disorder and
precipitate hardening in alloys 6 and 7 compensate each
other at such high temperatures.
A similar reasoning applies to the yield stress variation of
alloys 8–12 in Fig. 6a and b . The decrease in the yield
strength of alloys 8–10 between 500 and 600 8C may be
attributed to the beginning dissolution of the Laves phase.
The subsequent increase in yield strength for alloys 8 and 10
could be caused by a yield strength anomaly, though this is
much less pronounced for alloy 10 than for alloys 6 and 7
with comparable Ti but lower Al contents.
A closer analysis of the data necessitates a detailed
knowledge of the distribution of phases and its evolution
during testing.
4.6. Effect of heat treatment on the 0.2% yield stress
Fig. 5 shows that the annealing of alloy 1A at 400 8C
prior to testing decreases the yield stress with increasing
annealing time. The as-cast microstructure of this alloy
comprised primary dendrites of Laves phase within an L2
1
Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al matrix which contains some additional
precipitates of Laves phase. This explains the very high
yield stress of the as-cast alloy compared with the heat
treated alloy 1 with the same composition. The Laves phase
dendrites as well as the particles in the matrix in alloy 1A
show clear signs of coagulation, presumably because
according to the nominal composition of the alloy the
primary dendrites should have been surrounded by a ﬁne-
scaled eutectic which apparently has already transformed
partly at high temperatures during cooling. The heat-
treatment temperature of 400 8C is obviously too low to
affect the microstructure to a large extent. Therefore the
reason for the decrease in yield stress could be due to factors
which might have affected the strength of the L2
1
Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al matrix, such as redistribution of impurities
such as carbon, changes in the anti-phase boundary (APB)
structure or decrease in short-range order (SRO) and the
concentration of vacancies, which are all mechanisms that
are effective at 400 8C [54,60–62] . For B2-ordered FeAl
alloys it has been demonstrated that a low temperature
anneal results in a decrease in yield stress due to an
annihilation of quenched-in vacancies [63–65] .
A comparable effect is shown by the data of Stein et al.
[49] for a binary Fe-26Al (at.%) alloy where a decrease in
the yield stress for annealed samples is observed if tested at
400 8C, i.e. at a temperature below the maximum of the
yield stress anomaly. In contrast, a lesser effect of the heat
treatment was observed at the maximum of the yield stress
anomaly and at higher temperatures according to the data of
Ref. [49].

5. Conclusions
The L2 1 structure, the ternary equivalent of the binary
D03-structure, is stabilised by titanium at much higher
temperatures compared with the binary Fe3Al compound. In
view of possible alloy developments, the mechanical
properties such as hardness, RPE, 0.2% yield stress,
BDTT and creep resistance have been screened for single-
phase and two-phase L2
1 Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al-base Fe–Al–Ti
alloys.
Compared to other iron aluminide-base alloys, the
present Fe–Al–Ti alloys show a higher yield stress and
creep resistance at high temperatures up to 1100 8C. The
high yield stress of the single-phase alloys is due to an
increased peak temperature of the yield stress anomaly
which is at about 750 8C, i.e. it is about 150–300 8C higher
than those for binary D0
3 alloys and other ternary L2 1
alloys. As the critical temperatures Tc for the L2 1/B2
transition for all present alloys are still much higher
than those for the yield stress anomaly, there is no direct
relation between the L2
1/B2 transition and the yield stress
anomaly.
Deviations from stoichiometry decrease the atomic order
in the L2 1 structure with increasing concentration of
constitutional point defects, which increase the yield stress
at room temperature and decrease the yield stress at high
temperatures as well as the creep resistance. Second phases,
which are the Laves phase or the
t2 phase in the L2 1
Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al-base alloys, produce additional hardening
effects, which diminish with rising temperature above
800 8C.
The observed BDTT values range between 675 and
900 8C and are similar to those for other iron aluminide-base
alloys. No marked difference in the BDTT was shown by the
single-phase and two-phase alloys. The BDTT did not vary
distinctly with varying composition though the RPE data
indicate an ductility increase when approaching the
stoichiometric composition of Fe
2TiAl.
Parabolic scale growth has been observed for the
oxidation of the present Fe–Al–Ti alloys. The parabolic
rate constant for the most oxidation resistant alloy is at
the upper edge of the scatter band for Al
2O3-forming
alloys.
In view of the present results, the Fe–Al–Ti alloys on
the base of the L2 1-ordered Fe2(Fe,Ti)Al are promising for
further development for high-temperature applications due
to their improved strength at high temperatures in
combination with a high oxidation resistance. The proper-
ties of these alloys, like those of other Fe–Al alloys, are
controlled sensitively not only by aluminium and/or
titanium content, but also by heat treatments which
control the phase formation, atomic order in the phases
and all other aspects of microstructure. This opens a wide
range of possibilities for optimising L2
1-ordered Fe–Al–
Ti alloys with respect to strength, ductility and oxidation
resistance.
