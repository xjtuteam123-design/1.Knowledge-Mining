Effect of Zr on creep deformation behaviors of PM 
Ni‑based superalloys
Jian Jia1,2, Haopeng Zhang1,2,*, Ting Y an1,2, Qiong Hou1,2, Xiaokun Li1,2, and Yiwen Zhang1,2 
1 High Temperature Material Research Institute, Central Iron and Steel Research Institute, Beijing 100081, China
2 Gaona Aero Material Co., Ltd, Beijing 100081, China
ABSTRACT
In this study, the creep tests of three PM Ni-based superalloys with different Zr 
contents were carried out at 700 °C/780 MPa, 750 °C/550 MPa and 800 °C/400 MPa, 
respectively. The effect of Zr on creep deformation behaviors was studied by OM, 
SEM, EBSD and AC-STEM. The results show that the addition of appropriate 
Zr (0.1 wt.%) inhibits crack initiation and premature cracking, prolongates the 
creep rupture time, reduces the minimum creep rate, and increases the propor-
tion of steady-state creep duration. However, excessive Zr content (0.5 wt.%) 
can promote the continuous precipitation of μ, σ, and Ni7Zr2 phases at the grain 
boundaries, which is easy to become a crack source and reduce the creep per -
formance. In general, the alloy with 0.1Zr addition has high creep strength, low 
tendency of intergranular cracking at high temperature, and the best overall creep 
performance. This study provides a theoretical basis for the appropriate addition 
of Zr in PM Ni-based superalloys and provides some ideas for the optimization 
of alloy composition and design of novel PM Ni-based superalloys.
Introduction
The powder metallurgy (PM) process limits the com-
position segregation caused by high alloying to micro-
sized powder particles, resulting in dense, uniform, 
and fine-grained billets that help minimize macro-
scopic segregation and cracking tendency, achieving 
a good balance of tensile, creep, and fatigue proper-
ties. Therefore, PM Ni-based superalloys have become 
the preferred material for turbine disks and baffles of 
advanced aero engine [1–4].
In the 1950s, due to the reaction between the melt 
pool and the crucible during melting process, Zr unin-
tentionally entered Ni-based superalloys, significantly 
improving the hot workability and high-temperature 
creep strength [5 –7]. Subsequently, Zr was intention-
ally added into Ni-based superalloys as a typical grain 
boundary strengthening element to improve the com-
prehensive performance [8 –13]. Since the birth of PM 
Ni-based superalloys in the 1960s, Zr has been widely 
added to various alloys with content ranging from 
0.03% to 0.10%. In the early days, it was thought that

Experimental
The actual composition of three experimental PM Ni-
based superalloys with different Zr contents is listed 
in Table 1. The alloys were prepared by the following 
processes: vacuum induction melting (VIM) master 
alloys, powders producing by electrode induction gas 
atomization (EIGA) without crucible, powders sieving 
to obtain 0 ~ 63 μm alloy powders, hot isostatic press-

(HT) with solution treatment temperature of 1160 °C, 
stabilization treatment temperature of 871 °C, and 
aging treatment temperature of 760 °C. The creep test 
conditions include 700 °C /780 MPa, 750 °C /550 MPa, 
and 800 °C /400 MPa. The creep temperature range of 
700 °C  ~ 800 °C covers the operating temperature of 
this PM Ni-based superalloys, and the creep stresses 
are determined to ensure typical creep curves can 
be obtained and the experimental cost is reasonable. 
The creep samples have cylindrical gauge sections of 
φ5 × 25 mm.
The microstructure characterization was performed 
using Olympus GX71 optical microscope (OM), JEOL 
JSM-7200F field emission scanning electron micro-
scope (FE-SEM) equipped with Oxford Symmetry S2

 an angular resolution of 0.05° operated at 5 ~ 20 kV,

mission electron microscope (AC-STEM) equipped

X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detectors operated at 300 kV. 
OM samples were prepared by chemical etching with 
adding Zr could improve the creep strength at high 
temperature because Zr helped to desulfurize and 
form Zr, thus purifying the grain boundary. How-
ever, further study shows that Zr can also significantly 
change the grain boundary precipitates and delay the 
microstructure evolution process of microcracks. Add-
ing a small amount of Zr can significantly change the 
grain boundary state and play an important role in 
changing the creep fracture mode.
Some previous studies have reported the effect of Zr 
on creep properties. Research on the creep properties 
of Ni-20%Cr solid solution-strengthened superalloys 
under vacuum and atmospheric conditions [14– 16] 
showed that the addition of Zr significantly improved 
the creep strength. The creep rupture time and elon-
gation after fracture increased with the increase of 
Zr content, while the minimum creep rate decreased 
with the increase of Zr content. Zr promoted dynamic 
recrystallization near the creep fracture surface, 
slowed down the growth of voids, and changed the 
fracture mode from intergranular brittle fracture to 
intergranular and transgranular mixed ductile frac-
ture. Studies on precipitation-strengthened Ni-based 
superalloys [17 –19] also showed that an appropriate 
amount of Zr improved the grain boundary state and 
increased the creep rupture time, while an excess of 
Zr formed harmful phases at the grain boundary and 
reduced the creep rupture time. Research on ATI -
718Plus superalloy found that Zr increased the grain 
boundary cohesion and strength, promoted the forma-
tion of Zr-rich MC carbides, and increased the number 
of microtwins, thereby improving the high-tempera-
ture creep strength [20]. When the Zr content in the 
CM247LC superalloy increased from 0.1% to 0.4%, the 
appearance of the low melting point NiZr phase led to 
a decrease in the creep rupture time [21]. The optimal 
content and effect mechanism of Zr varied among dif-
ferent alloy systems.
However, the effect of Zr on creep deformation 
behaviors, especially its mechanisms, is still unclear. 
Therefore, this study focuses on three experimental 
alloys with only Zr content changes and systematically 
investigates the effects of Zr on creep deformation

Creep curves and creep properties
A typical creep curve of PM Ni-based superalloys can 
be divided into three stages according to the varia-
tion of creep rate: (I) the deceleration stage, (II) the 
steady-state stage, and (III) the acceleration stage. The 
stage where the derivative of strain (i.e., creep rate) 
is approximately constant is divided into the steady-
state stage [23]. Figure 2 shows the creep curves (indi-
cated by black curves) and creep rate curves (indicated 
by blue curves) of experimental alloys after dividing 
into three stages. It can be seen that the duration of 
deceleration stage is relatively short, while the dura-
tion of the acceleration stage is relatively long due to 
the high damage tolerance of the experimental alloys. 
It is worth noting that there are no curves of 0.5Zr 
alloy after 800 ℃/400 MPa creep because it fractures 
during the process of creep stress loading.
For the convenience of comparing the effects of Zr 
on the creep rupture time and creep rate, the creep 
curves and creep rate curves of alloys with different 
Zr contents under the same creep conditions were 
plotted together, as shown in Fig. 3. Furthermore, the 
creep rupture time (t r), minimum creep rate (  ̇𝜀 ), pro-

obtained, as shown in Table 3. Under creep conditions

Discussion
The grain boundaries of HT alloys with different Zr 
contents were characterized using EBSD. The grain 
boundaries with the grain misorientation angle more 
than 10° and annealing twin boundaries (A-TB) 
with the grain misorientation angle about 60° were 
marked in black and red, respectively, and the length 
proportion of A-TB to total boundaries was marked 
in the upper right corner, as shown in Fig. 11. It can 
be observed that Zr significantly reduces the propor-
tion of A-TB, from 19.6% in 0Zr alloy to 8.6% in 0.5Zr 
alloy. This is because Zr is a strong MC-type carbides 
forming element, which promotes the precipitation of 
MC-type carbides (as shown in Table 2) and improves 
their stability [25], and stable MC-type carbides help 
to hinder the nucleation and growth of A-TB [26].

By using JMat Pro 12 software, the variation of 
stacking fault energy with temperature for alloys 
with different Zr contents was calculated, as shown 
in Fig. 12. It can be seen that the stacking fault energy 
increases with the increase in temperature, and within 
the entire temperature range, the increase of Zr con-
tent reduces the stacking fault energy. The reduction 
of the stacking fault energy helps to increase the den-
sity of stacking faults, which effectively increases the 
resistance of dislocation motion during creep defor-
mation, therefore improving the creep strength of the 
alloy [28].
In addition, the lattice mismatch of the γ/γ′ phase 
also affects the creep properties. A higher lattice mis-
match of the γ/γ′ phase promotes the formation of a 
denser dislocation network around the interface of the 
γ and γ′ phases, which helps to hinder the extension 
of stacking faults and the looping of dislocations [29]. 
Therefore, by increasing the lattice mismatch of the 
γ/γ′ phase, Zr helps to improve the creep performance.
However, it is worth noting that during the creep 
process, the increase of Zr content promotes the pre-
cipitation of various phases, which also has a signifi-
cant effect on the creep strength. The characterization 
of the grain boundary precipitates of 0.5Zr alloy after 
800 °C /400 MPa creep is shown in Fig. 13. Through 
TEM-EDS mapping, it can be found that although 
the precipitates are all rich in Mo, some are signifi-
cantly rich in W, while others are significantly rich 
in Cr, indicating the existence of two types of grain 
boundary precipitates. Firstly, SAED was performed 
on the precipitates rich in W (indicated by red circle 
1), and the diffraction spot was shown as SAED-1, 
which was identified as the μ phase along the [  212 ] 
zone axis (ZA). Then, SAED was performed on the 
precipitates rich in Cr (indicated by red circle 2), and 
the diffraction spot was shown as SAED-2, which 
was identified as the σ phase along the [  113 ] zone 
axis. Therefore, after 800 °C/400 MPa creep, the 0.5Zr 
alloy precipitates numerous μ and σ phases at grain 
boundaries.
Moreover, blocky Zr-rich phases were also 
observed in 0.5Zr alloy after 800 °C /400 MPa creep, 
as shown in Fig. 14. SAED was performed on the 
Zr-rich phases (indicated by the red circle), which 
was identified as the Ni7Zr2 phase along the [  010 ] 
zone axis. Therefore, after 800 °C /400 MPa creep, 
the 0.5Zr alloy also precipitates numerous Ni7Zr2 
phases. The addition of Zr promotes the precipita-
tion of μ, σ, and Ni7Zr2 phases, which is consistent 
with the results in Table 2.
The no-coherent μ, σ, and Ni7Zr2 phases have 
weak bonding with the γ matrix and are prone to 
become crack sources under high temperature and 
stress [30, 31], as shown in Fig. 15. It can be seen that 
cracks propagate along grain boundaries filled with 
numerous precipitates, which significantly reduces 
the creep strength. Therefore, due to the promotion 
of no-coherent precipitates, excessive Zr (0.5Zr) 
reduces the creep performance of alloys and leads 
to early fracture during 800 °C/400 MPa creep, as 
mentioned in 3.2 and 3.3 sections.
In general, Zr changes the creep deformation 
behaviors of the alloy by influencing the propor-
tion of A-TB, stacking fault energy, lattice mis-
match and grain boundary precipitates. Under the

discussed, and the following main conclusions were

(4) Zr improves creep performance by reducing 
the proportion of annealing twin boundaries to 
reduce dislocation accumulation and by reducing 
the stacking fault energy and increasing the lat-
tice mismatch of γ/γ ′ phases to increase the move-
ment resistance of the dislocation during creep. 
However, excessive Zr content will promote 
the continuous precipitation of μ, σ and Ni7Zr2 
phases at the grain boundaries, which is easy to 
become a crack source and reduce the creep per-
formance.
(5) When the Zr content is moderate (0.1Zr), the 
alloy has a relatively long creep rupture time, a 
small minimum creep rate, and a large propor-
tion of steady-state creep duration. The alloy with 
0.1Zr also has high service temperature and creep 
strength, and the tendency of intergranular crack-
ing is not obvious at high temperature, resulting 
in the best overall creep performance.
