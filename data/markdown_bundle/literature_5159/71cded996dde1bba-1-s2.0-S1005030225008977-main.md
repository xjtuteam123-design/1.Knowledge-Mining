This study demonstrates how high-pressure thermal treatment achieves a breakthrough in the trade-off
relation between strength and ductility in Ti-55511 alloy, providing critical insights for aerospace materi- 
als design. When Ti-55511 alloy was processed at 5 GPa across temperatures (room temperature, 700 °C, 
900 °C), temperature-dependent gradient microstructures were formed and governed by stress hetero- 
geneity. This heterogeneity arises from friction constraints induced by high-pressure stress at the con- 
tacting surfaces. The surface region exhibited stress concentration with higher hardness values, while the 
center region displayed a lower stress state with lower hardness values. A gradient distribution of the 
shear bands was induced at room temperature, with shear band-assisted phase transformation occurring 
in the surface region. Comparing the α-phase distributions emerging in the specimens treated in single- 
phase and dual-phase regions, the stress was found to promote α precipitation for the former one and 
suppress α precipitation for the latter one in the surface region. Notably, a unique α-phase fragmenta- 
tion mechanism was identiﬁed in high-temperature-high-pressure treatment, with the nucleation of the 
β phase within the α phase, induced by α-to- β phase transformation. When the interior β phase grew 
up to connect the α/ β interfaces on both sides of the α precipitates, the fragmentation process was com- 
pleted. Besides, dynamic recrystallization occurred in the center of the specimen treated by high-pressure 
treatment in the single-phase region with limited α precipitation. This led to a notable enhancement in 
the mechanical properties, with plasticity increased by over 11 % and strength increased by ∼160 MPa 
compared with the original solid-solution state. These ﬁndings demonstrate the uniqueness of the high- 
pressure thermal treatment process and its potential as a preparation technique for titanium alloys with 
high-strength and high-ductility.

1. Introduction 
The pursuit of high-performance materials for industrial appli- 
cations requires synergistic optimization of strength and ductil- 
ity [ 1 ]. However, it is inevitable for conventional thermomechan- 
ical processing routes to enhance alloy strength at the cost of los- 
ing plasticity [ 2 , 3 ]. Recent breakthroughs in synthesizing super- 
hard materials have propelled high-pressure treatment into the 
forefront of materials engineering innovations, garnering signiﬁ- 
cant attention among industrial and academic researchers [ 4 , 5 ]. 
Tang et al. [ 6 ] reported that a eutectic high entropy alloy with 
initial strong-yet-brittle properties was signiﬁcantly enhanced by 
high-pressure treatment to attain an ultimate tensile strength of

tural evolution and mechanical behaviors of titanium alloys treated

Ti-5Al-5Mo-5V-1Fe-1Cr alloy, abbreviated as Ti-55511 alloy, 
serves as a promising candidate material for aerospace load- 
bearing components due to its exceptional speciﬁc strength and 
light weight [ 10 , 11 ]. However, practical application of Ti-55511 al- 
loy tends to face challenges due to its limited ductility at room 
temperature (RT), which limits its applicability under complex ser- 
vice conditions [ 12 , 13 ]. In consideration of the superiority of high- 
pressure treatment, it is meaningful to introduce this technology 
into the fabrication process to break the strength-ductility trade- 
off of Ti-55511 alloy. Under mechanical stress, various microstruc- 
tural evolutions can occur in Ti alloys, including dynamic recrys- 
tallization (DRX), phase transformation with the precipitation of 
α phase and other metastable phases, and deformation structures 
[ 14 –16 ]. In general, the structural evolution during practical de- 
formation processes involves the coexistence of multiple transfor- 
mation mechanisms, operating through synergistic and competi- 
tive pathways. Li et al. [ 17 ] proposed a novel design strategy for 
high-strength titanium alloys: by initially introducing nano-scaled 
kink bands through a combined cold forging and rolling process, 
multi-scale α precipitates were obtained within and outside the 
kink bands, resulting in simultaneous improvement of the strength 
and ductility in Ti-V alloys. In addition, microscale shear bands 
were introduced by Zhang et al. [ 18 ] in Ti-Al alloy, accompanied 
by the preferential nucleation of DRX in shear bands and nan- 
otwins activated by shear deformation. To comprehensively under- 
stand the coexisting transformation mechanisms during the high- 
pressure treatment process, in-depth and multiscale experimental 
investigations are essential. 
In this work, high-pressure treatments were adopted at differ- 
ent temperatures to strengthen the Ti-55511 alloy and improve 
the ductility simultaneously. The effects of high-pressure stress 
at different temperatures on the structural evolution and me- 
chanical properties were systematically investigated and character- 
ized. Based on the characterization and testing results, the related 
mechanisms, particularly DRX and phase transformation mecha- 
nisms, and their effects on the mechanical responses were thor- 
oughly discussed. This work provides a new strategy to break the 
strength-ductility trade-off for titanium alloys by a combination of 
high-pressure and high-temperature treatments. 
2. Experimental 
The raw material, purchased from the market, was an as-cast 
metastable Ti-55511 alloy with the actual composition of Ti-5.01Al- 
4.74Mo-4.75V-1.08Fe −1.12Cr (wt. %). Its β-transus temperature, 
measured via metallography, was ∼870 °C. A pure β-phase mi- 
crostructure was obtained through solution treatment at 900 °C 
for 30 min and subsequent water quenching. Standard specimens 
were prepared, with a size of /Phi110 mm × 17 mm. As depicted 
in Fig. 1 (a), the specimen was mounted and located at the spe- 
ciﬁc position of the self-designed hexahedron-anvil-press appara- 
tus. The specimen was heated to target temperatures including 
RT, 700 and 900 °C, under a pressure of 5 GPa and held for 1 h. 
The obtained specimens were named as HP-RT, HP-DT and HP- 
ST, according to the target temperatures at RT, dual-phase region 
temperature and single-phase region temperature. To better under- 
stand the microstructural evolution during high-pressure process- 
ing, annealing at 700 and 900 °C for 1 h without high pressure was 
adopted following solution treatment. The specimens were named 
as DT and ST, respectively. 
To understand the microstructural features of the specimens, a 
systematic group of characterization and analytical techniques was 
employed, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron mi- 
croscopy (SEM), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and trans- 
mission electron microscopy (TEM). The analyses were performed 
using a Dmax 2500VB machine with Cu Kα radiation, an FEI He- 
lios Nano Lab G3 UC SEM with integrated EBSD capabilities, and an

3.2.1. Microstructural evolution induced by high-pressure treatment 
at room temperature 
The microstructure of the HP-RT specimen at different locations 
can be seen in Fig. 4 . In the center region, a group of parallel shear 
bands is observed in Fig. 4 (a) and 4(b). In the transition Ⅰ , Ⅱ and 
Ⅲ regions ( Fig. 4 (c–h)), the number of the shear bands increases

Fig. 4 (i) and (j). The gradient distribution of the shear bands with

EBSD characterizations have been adopted to analyze the shear

Further microstructural characterizations of the shear bands in 
the HP-RT specimen are presented in Fig. 6 . Fig. 6 (a) shows the 
bright ﬁeld TEM (BF-TEM) image of a shear band with a width of 
∼50 nm in the center region. It can be seen in a high-resolution

a width of ∼160 nm was observed to be further sheared by dis- 
location slip, as shown in Fig. 6 (c). The shear band is fragmented 
into stepped segments, as evidenced by the rotation and separa- 
tion of the diffraction points in the corresponding selected area 
electron diffraction (SAED) pattern inserted in Fig. 6 (c). Addition- 
ally, the enlarged view of the area marked by a yellow square, 
shows that a small number of ﬁne precipitates exist and the cor- 
responding SAED pattern conﬁrms these precipitates as marten- 
site α′′ phase, in accordance with the XRD result in Fig. 3 . From 
Fig. 6 (d), it can be found that a series of dislocations pile up at the 
boundaries of the shear band. Fig. 6 (e) displays the dark ﬁeld TEM 
(DF-TEM) image of Fig. 6 (d), revealing extensive ω phase precipi-

shear band boundary shows that it is parallel to the (112) plane,

conditions. Considering the preparation of the HP-RT specimen at 
RT and the EDS results showing no element diffusion, the ω phase 
generated in the HP-RT specimen was classiﬁed into stress-induced 
ω phase in present work.

3.2.3. Microstructural evolution induced by high-pressure treatment 
at dual-phase region temperature 
The microstructure of the HP-DT specimen at different locations 
can be seen in Fig. 10 , showing α phase with higher density com- 
pared with the HP-ST specimen. Besides, unlike the microstruc- 
ture of the DT specimen, where the α phase precipitates uniformly 
from the matrix, the distribution of α phase is inhomogeneous in 
the HP-DT specimen. Under the action of high pressure, the mi- 
crostructure has also been divided into stripes by the continuous 
precipitation of the α phase along the shear direction, as shown 
in Fig. 10 (a), (c), (e), (g) and (i). The density of the α phase is 
higher at the boundaries, compared with the interior of the stripes. 
However, different from the microstructure of the HP-ST specimen, 
the precipitation of the α phase decreases obviously from the cen- 
ter region to the surface region. In the center region, the α phase 
precipitated in different directions, as shown in Fig. 10 (b). As the 
characterized region gradually approaches the surface, the length- 
diameter ratio of the α phase increases signiﬁcantly ( Fig. 10 (d), (f) 
and (h)). And α phase almost grew along the same direction from 
the boundaries to the interior of the stripes, as shown in Fig. 10 (h). 
In the surface region, further decreased α phase density results 
in precipitation-free zones (PFZs) within stripes ( Fig. 10 (j)). This 
phenomenon indicates that high pressure results in inverse phase 
transformation from α to β in the dual-phase region. 
Fig. 11 shows the results obtained by TEM characterization of 
the HP-DT specimen. Fig. 11 (a) reveals that β phases nucleated 
within α phases and grew to connect α phase boundaries in 
the center region, resulting in α phase fragmentation arrowed in 
Fig. 11 (b). Comparing the α phase in the center region ( Fig. 11 (c)) 
and surface region ( Fig. 11 (d) and (e)), it can be seen that there 
is a dramatic increase both in the distribution density and length- 
diameter ratio of the α phase. Besides, the deﬂection can also oc- 
cur at the stripe boundaries with the continuous precipitation of 
the α phase, as shown in Fig. 11 (d). Fig. 11 (f) shows an obvious PFZ 
without α precipitates, a feature conﬁrmed by the inserted SAED 
pattern and consistent with the characterization result in Fig. 10 (j). 
3.3. Room-temperature mechanical responses after high-pressure 
treatments 
The hardness values of high-pressure-treated specimens were 
tested from one surface to the other, with the results shown 
in Fig. 12 (a). Hardness curves decrease from both upper and 
lower surface regions toward the central region, demonstrating a 
symmetrical distribution. For the HP-RT specimens, the hardness 
reaches 343.7 HV in the surface region but decreases to 268.8 HV 
in the center region, with a difference of 74.9 HV. Similarly, the HP- 
ST and HP-DT specimens also have signiﬁcant hardness gradients 
from the surface region to the center region, as shown in Fig. 12 (a). 
This symmetrical hardness distribution and gradient variation can 
be attributed to the input of symmetrical high-pressure stress, 
which induced the gradient microstructure discussed before and 
consequently affected the mechanical properties of the specimens.

generally follow the order HVHP-RT < HVHP-ST < HVHP-DT , a trend pri- 
marily correlated with the precipitation density of the α phase. 
Specimens with higher α phase density tend to exhibit higher 
hardness, resulting in the highest hardness of 462.5 HV in the sur- 
face region of HP-DT specimens. Notably, the hardness of the HP-

4. Discussion

4.2. Effect of high-pressure stress on phase transformation at high 
temperature 
4.2.1. Precipitation and variant selection of α phase in the HP-ST 
specimen 
Similar to the gradient density-distributed shear bands in the 
HP-RT specimen, gradient density-distributed α phase was also ob- 
served in the HP-ST specimen. And the percentage of α phase in- 
creases progressively from the center region to the surface region. 
However, the microstructure of the ST specimen consists of a single 
β phase with no α-phase precipitation. The existence of α phase 
with a gradient density distribution in the HP-ST specimen indi- 
cates that the high-pressure stress has a promoting effect on the 
β→ α phase transformation. The occurrence of β→ α transforma- 
tion in metastable β titanium alloys fundamentally relies on the 
coupling of shear-dominated displacive mechanisms and thermally 
activated diffusive processes [ 42 ]. The action of high stress can dra- 
matically enhance the kinetics of the shear-driven displacive pro- 
cess, while concurrent high temperature can facilitate atomic diffu- 
sion through increased vacancy mobility, thereby fulﬁlling both the 
thermodynamic and kinetic requirements for phase transformation. 
Notably, in the center region of the HP-ST specimen, limited α pre- 
cipitates coexist with DRX grains aligning along the shear direc- 
tion. However, in other regions, abundant α precipitates occurred 
with the disappearance of DRX grains. The microstructural evolu- 
tion reveals a competition between phase transformation and DRX 
mechanisms, driven by stress requirements. Results indicate that 
DRX preferentially consumes nucleation sites and energy within 
the β-phase matrix. 
In general, orientation relationship (OR) between α and β
phases conforms to Burgers orientation (OR): (0 0 01)α//(110)β and 
[11 ¯2 0]α//[111]β [ 49 ]. According to the Burgers OR maintained 
with β-phase matrix, twelve equivalent α variants may theoret- 
ically form. However, only four α variants were observed in the 
surface region of the HP-ST specimen in the present work, in- 
dicating the presence of a variant selection phenomenon [ 50 ]. 
The variant selection phenomenon suggests that localized stress 
state and anisotropic plastic deformation preferentially stabilize 
speciﬁc crystallographic orientations during phase transformation 
[ 42 ]. Crystallographic analysis reveals that among the twelve possi- 
ble α variants, ﬁve distinct orientation relationships exist between 
every two variants [ 51 ]. In the four observed variants, two speciﬁc 
relationships were identiﬁed: variant I and II, as well as variant III 
and IV, demonstrate a 10.5 ° rotation about the [0 0 01] axis, while 
other variant combinations exhibit the 90 °/[7 10 17 0] relationship. 
Under applied high-pressure stress, variant IV preferentially nucle- 
ated and grew along the shear direction, partitioning the β matrix 
into stripes. Within these stripes, four α-phase variants I–IV dis- 
play a random spatial distribution. Differences in the selection and 
precipitation conditions of α variants across regions indicate that

precipitation environment inﬂuences variant selection. Notably, the 
crystallographic characteristics of both the β grain boundaries [ 52 ]

tion. For instance, different α variants exhibit various degrees of 
elastic self-accommodation when contacting with each other, and 
the interfacial energies vary among different α variants when in-

4.2.2. Suppression of α-phase precipitation with a certain sequence 
in the HP-DT specimen 
The HP-DT specimen demonstrates a distinct gradient distribu- 
tion of α-phase precipitation compared to the DT specimen, as 
shown in Fig. 13 (c). The precipitation of α phase decreases pro- 
gressively from the center region to the surface region in the HP- 
DT, as shown in Fig. 10 , even with a PFZ observed in the surface 
region ( Fig. 10 (j)). The microstructural evolution also reveals a re- 
duction in the diversity of growth orientations of α phase. Unlike 
the α phase in the central region, which precipitates with vari- 
ous growth directions ( Fig. 10 (b), (d) and (f)), the α phase around 
the surface region appears as continuous α-phase bands along the 
shear direction and adjacent α precipitates ( Fig. 10 (h) and (j)). 
With a similar morphology to shear bands, the formation of 
α-phase bands arose through a sequential mechanism, involving 
shear-assisted preferential nucleation of α phase along the shear 
direction, followed by stress-driven coalescence of the adjacent nu- 
clei via directional growth. The formation mechanism of α-phase 
bands is similar to that of the grain boundary α phase [ 11 ]. It is 
known that Widmanstätten α precipitates usually occur with the 
existence of grain boundary α phase, because the grain boundary 
α phase can act as nucleation sites for Widmanstätten α to grow 
into β grains [ 55 ]. The precipitation behavior of α precipitates ad- 
jacent to the α-phase bands shares mechanistic similarities with 
the Widmanstätten α formation near the grain boundary α phase, 
resulting in the preferred nucleation and growth of α precipitates 
adjacent to α-phase bands, compared to the other α precipitates 
far from α-phase bands. Therefore, the precipitation of α phase in 
the HP-DT specimen follows a sequential order: initial formation 
of α-phase bands along the shear direction, subsequent growth of 
the Widmanstätten α phase nucleated from α-phase bands, and ﬁ- 
nal inﬁlling of intragranular α precipitates in the stripes divided by 
α-phase bands. 
The gradient distribution of α phase arises not only from se- 
quential nucleation kinetics but also from the stress gradient effect. 
Unlike the stress-promoted β→ α transformation observed in the 
HP-ST specimen, the precipitation of α phase was suppressed by 
high-pressure stress in the surface region of the HP-DT specimen. 
The formed PFZ likely results from the synergistic interplay of the 
two stress components: (i) intensiﬁed local stress ﬁeld generated 
by high-density precipitation of the acicular α phase, and (ii) the 
pre-existing complex state with high stress. Under high-pressure 
conditions, frictional constraints between the specimen surface and 
the press apparatus induce a gradient stress distribution, with the 
stress concentration in the surface region decreasing toward the 
center. This gradient stress ﬁeld, combined with the stress gener- 
ated by the dense acicular α-phase precipitation, creates a local- 
ized stress superposition in the surface area. When the cumulative 
stress exceeds the critical threshold for phase transformation sup- 
pression, it triggers mechanical stabilization of the β-phase ma- 
trix, effectively blocking subsequent α-phase nucleation [ 42 ]. This 
process is further exacerbated by the inverse phase transformation 
( α→ β) induced by high pressure in the dual-phase region. Addi- 
tionally, the high-pressure environment alters the precipitation se- 
quence: instead of the uniform α-phase distribution seen in the 
static heat-treated DT specimens, the surface region experiences 
suppressed α-phase nucleation kinetics due to the enhanced β- 
phase stability. The combined effects of stress superposition, in- 
verse phase transformation, and inhibited precipitation kinetics re- 
sult in the formation of a PFZ at the surface, where α-phase pre- 
cipitation is completely blocked. 
4.2.3. Fragmentation mechanism of α phase related to α→ β
transformation 
During the thermo-mechanical processing of titanium alloys, 
the lamellar α phase frequently undergoes dynamic fragmenta- 
tion and spheroidization behaviors driven by strain localization 
and interfacial energy minimization [ 56 , 57 ]. This phenomenon 
involves the progressive breakup of elongated α lamellae into 
equiaxed particles through dislocation-mediated boundary split- 
ting and diffusion-controlled curvature-driven spheroidization [ 57 ]. 
Current understanding of lamellar α fragmentation mechanisms 
includes several established models: boundary splitting, edge 
spheroidization, lath shearing, and continuous dynamic recrystal- 
lization [ 58 , 59 ]. These mechanisms can be classiﬁed into two cate- 
gories: (i) interfacial fragmentation via solute diffusion-mediated 
segregation of β-stabilizing elements along the α/ β boundaries 
[ 60 ], and (ii) dislocation-mediated direct interfacial reconﬁgura- 
tion within α phase independent of β phase [ 61 ]. However, dif- 
ferent from the above mechanisms, a new mechanism is identiﬁed 
in this work comprising three stages, as shown in Figs. 9 (g–i). In 
stage Ⅰ , β-phase nucleation occurs within α phase via α→ β trans- 
formation, accompanied by the formation of grooves at the α/ β
interfaces through β-phase penetration ( Fig. 9 (g)). In stage Ⅱ , β
phase further grows to connect previously formed grooves or di- 
rectly bridges adjacent α/ β interfaces via thermally activated diffu- 
sion. In stage Ⅲ , when β phase within α phase has fully intercon- 
nected with the β matrix on both sides of α phase, the fragmenta- 
tion process of the original α phase is completed. The microstruc- 
tural evolution of α-phase fragmentation process involving three 
stages was actually driven by the synergistic effect of β-phase nu- 
cleation mechanisms, which combines α→ β phase transformation 
and interfacial instability principle inherent to the boundary split- 
ting model. 
In metastable β titanium alloys, β-to- α phase transforma- 
tion typically dominates within the dual-phase region temperature 
range [ 62 , 63 ]. However, in this work, β-phase nucleation mech- 
anism occurred under a high-pressure stress state in the dual- 
phase region, revealing the existence of the reverse α→ β phase 
transformation. Phase transformation fundamentally represents a 
transition between two distinct energy systems. From a thermody- 
namic perspective, such transitions require suﬃcient driving force. 
In a strain-free condition, the driving force originates primarily 
from the free energy difference between phases induced by tem- 
perature variations. However, during thermomechanical processing, 
deformation-induced stresses may supply additional driving force 
through mechanical work input. In addition, diffusion kinetics and 
redistribution characteristics of solute elements under deformation 
conditions can alter the two-phase equilibrium state compared to 
static thermal treatments, potentially accelerating or suppressing 
the phase transformation depending on stress-strain ﬁeld condi- 
tions [ 42 ]. For example, Koike et al. [ 64 ] observed stress-induced 
Fe redistribution between α and β phases during tensile deforma- 
tion of Ti-5.5Al-1Fe alloy at 1050–1200 K. This elemental redistri- 
bution altered phase stability, with the free energy of α phase in- 
creasing and that of β phase decreasing, thereby promoting the 
α→ β transformation.

Under the effect of high temperature and high stress, the re-

Hall-Petch boundary strengthening mechanism, resulting in supe- 
rior hardness compared to other specimens in Fig. 12 (a). However, 
the excessive precipitation of α phase can induce early-stage brit-

from the center to the surface regions, accompanied by a pro-

and 11 . This microstructural evolution enhanced dislocation pin- 
ning eﬃciency through geometrically necessary dislocation accu- 
mulation at elongated α-phase interfaces, thereby contributing to 
the observed hardness gradient. 
The gradient hardness distribution in the HP-ST specimen pri- 
marily originates from the gradient distribution of α-phase density. 
Concurrently, pronounced thermal effects induce extensive dislo- 
cation annihilation during the processing, partially counteracting 
the hardening contributions from applied mechanical stress. This 
thermo-mechanical coupling results in limited hardness enhance- 
ment over the HP-RT specimen, even with a similar hardness value 
compared to the HP-RT specimen observed in the transition Ⅱ re- 
gion. Within the transition Ⅱ region (near the center region), the 
HP-ST specimen enhanced mechanical properties through syner- 
gistic grain reﬁnement strengthening and moderate α-phase pre- 
cipitation enhancement. In contrast, the HP-RT specimens relied 
solely on dislocation hardening, characterized by shear band for- 
mation and consequent grain reﬁnement. This fundamental differ- 
ence explains the superior hardness and a combination of strength 
( ∼862.8 MPa) and plasticity ( ∼21.6 %) for the HP-ST specimen, 
compared to the HP-RT and the ST specimens in the center re- 
gion. From the transition Ⅱ to the surface regions, the HP-ST ex- 
hibits obvious hardness elevation due to markedly increased α- 
phase density. In contrast, the modest hardening effect in the HP- 
RT specimen resulted from the dense distribution and interaction 
of the shear bands and shear-band-induced phase transformation. 
While the comparable hardness in the transition Ⅱ region suggests 
the equivalence between α-phase strengthening and shear band- 
related hardening. 
It is worth noting that a ﬂow serration was observed on the 
tensile curve of the HP-ST specimen. The occurrence of ﬂow serra- 
tion in stress-strain curves is closely associated with the Portevin- 
Le Chatelier (PLC) effect [ 66 ]. This phenomenon typically manifests 
during dynamic strain aging, arising from interactions between so- 
lute atoms and moving dislocations, which cause periodic pin- 
ning and unpinning of dislocations [ 66 ]. For the HP-ST specimen 
prepared via high-pressure treatment in the single-phase region, 
a supersaturated metastable β solid solution was formed. During 
room-temperature tensile testing, supersaturated solute atoms can 
diffuse along high-density dislocations [ 67 ] and interact with mov- 
ing dislocations, resulting in periodic pinning and unpinning. This 
dynamic interaction may be the reason for inducing ﬂow serration 
in the stress-strain curve. 
Besides, comparing the mechanical properties of the center re- 
gion in the HP-RT and original specimens, the high-pressure treat- 
ment brought a signiﬁcant increase in the plasticity from ∼8.8 % 
for the original specimen to ∼23.9 % for the HP-RT specimen. 
It should be noted that the original solid-solution treated spec- 
imen was obtained by water quenching from a single-phase re- 
gion, resulting in the supersaturated metastable β solid solution. 
While the supersaturated solute atoms tend to cause lattice dis- 
tortion and further increase the resistance to dislocation move- 
ment, resulting in diﬃculty in initiating slip. Besides, supersatu- 
rated metastable β solid solutions are also prone to stress-induced 
phase transformation during room-temperature tensile tests, form- 
ing brittle metastable phases that compromise the ductility of 
the specimen. In contrast, the HP-RT specimen retains residual 
compressive stress from high-pressure processing, which can ef- 
fectively inhibit crack propagation. Furthermore, the formation of 
shear bands also contributes to grain reﬁnement, which enhances 
ductility by increasing the number of grain boundaries that act 
as obstacles to dislocation motion and promote uniform deforma- 
tion. The reﬁned grains, resulting from the fragmentation of origi- 
nal grains through dislocation interactions, create a larger volume 
of grain boundaries that allow for more distributed plastic defor- 
mation across the microstructure, reducing localized stress concen- 
trations and enabling the material to sustain greater strain before 
fracture. Additionally, the high density of the dislocations accumu- 
lated at shear band interfaces serves as active sources for further 
plastic deformation, facilitating dislocation emission and slip trans- 
fer between adjacent grains. Consequently, the HP-RT specimen ex- 
hibits a signiﬁcant improvement in ductility compared to the orig- 
inal as-solution treated specimen. 
5. Conclusions 
In this work, the microstructural evolution and phase transfor- 
mation under different temperatures were investigated in high- 
pressure treated Ti-55511 alloy, with the following conclusions 
drawn. 
(1) Temperature-dependent gradient microstructures and me- 
chanical properties are governed by non-uniform stress dis- 
tribution. Gradient hardness values were caused by the 
synergistic effect of high-pressure treatment and the fric- 
tion constraint on the contact surface. Various gradient 
microstructures, including an increasing number of shear 
bands, a growing precipitation of α phase and a decreas- 
ing trend of α precipitation were obtained for the speci- 
mens high-pressure-treated at RT, single-phase region and 
dual-phase region temperatures, respectively, indicating the 
gradient distribution of the stress. Besides, DRX dominated 
the microstructure with limited α precipitation in the cen- 
tral region of the specimens treated in the single-phase re- 
gion, resulting in simultaneous improvements in strength 
and plasticity compared with the original solid-solution 
specimens. The signiﬁcant development in mechanical prop- 
erties demonstrates an enormous potential of the high- 
temperature-high-pressure technology. 
(2) Various roles of stress exist in phase transformation during 
high-pressure treatment at different temperatures. At RT, a 
promoting effect of the stress emerged in the precipitation 
of stress-induced α′′ martensite adjacent to shear bands and 
ω phase within the shear bands, indicating the existence of 
a shear band-assisted phase transformation mechanism. An 
increasing precipitation of α phase from the center to the 
surface regions in the HP-ST specimen also indicates stress- 
induced facilitation of β→ α transformation. In contrast, the 
precipitation of α phase in the HP-DT specimen was sup- 
pressed by the high-pressure stress. 
(3) A special fragmentation mechanism of α phase occurred 
through β-phase nucleation within α phase. During high- 
pressure treatment at high temperature, β phase nucleated 
by α→ β transformation within the α phase, accompanied 
by the formation of grooves at the α/ β interfaces. With the 
growth of β phase, it can connect the formed grooves or 
adjacent α/ β interfaces via thermally activated diffusion, re- 
sulting in the ﬁnal fragmentation of α phase. This process, 
driven by α→ β phase transformation and interfacial insta-
