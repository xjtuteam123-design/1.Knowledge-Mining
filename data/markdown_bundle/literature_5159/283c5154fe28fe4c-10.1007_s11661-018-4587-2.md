MC Carbide Characterization in High Refractory
Content Powder-Processed Ni-Based Superalloys
STOICHKO ANTONOV , WEI CHEN, JIAJIE HUO, QIANG FENG,
DIETER ISHEIM, DAVID N. SEIDMAN, EUGENE SUN, and SAMMY TIN
Carbide precipitates in Ni-based superalloys are considered to be desirable phases that can
contribute to improving high-temperature properties as well as aid in microstructural reﬁnement
of the material; however, they can also serve as crack initiation sites during fatigue. To date,
most of the knowledge pertaining to carbide formation has originated from assessments of cast
and wrought Ni-based superalloys. As powder-processed Ni-based superalloys are becoming
increasingly widespread, understanding the diﬀerent mechanisms by which they form becomes
increasingly important. Detailed characterization of MC carbides present in two experimental
high Nb-content powder-processed Ni-based superalloys revealed that Hf additions aﬀect the
resultant carbide morphologies. This morphology diﬀerence was attributed to a higher
magnitude of elastic strain energy along the interface associated with Hf being soluble in the
MC carbide lattice. The composition of the MC carbides was studied through atom probe
tomography and consisted of a complex carbonitride core, which was rich in Nb and with slight
Hf segregation, surrounded by an Nb carbide shell. The characterization results of the
segregation behavior of Hf in the MC carbides and the subsequent inﬂuence on their
morphology were compared to density functional theory calculations and found to be in good
agreement, suggesting that computational modeling can successfully be used to tailor carbide
features.

I. INTRODUCTION
NICKEL-BASED superalloys are used as the mate-
rials for high-temperature components in modern gas
turbine engines due to their high strength along with
considerable creep and fatigue resistance even when
exposed to corrosive environments, high stress levels,
and elevated temperatures. [1–9] The superior high-tem-
perature properties of Ni-based superalloys can largely
be attributed to the unique microstructure consisting of
ordered L1 2 intermetallic precipitates, c¢ (Ni3Al), dis-
tributed coherently within a disordered FCC A1 matrix,
c (Ni), that provide substantial order and coherency
type strengthening by inhibiting dislocation motion
during plastic deformation. The composition of
Ni-based superalloys tends to incorporate a variety of
elemental additions, each focused at improving a certain
property. For example, refractory alloying elements,
such as W, Mo, Cr and Co, in modern polycrystalline
Ni-based superalloys serve to strengthen the c matrix
phase through solid solution strengthening, while con-
centrations of c¢-forming Al, Ti, Nb, and Ta are utilized
to produce an optimized volume fraction of the c¢
strengthening phase as they will preferentially partition
to the c¢ phase. The c¢-forming elements also serve to
lower the antiphase boundary energy as well as to
strengthen the precipitate’s resistance to dislocation
shear.[10] Moreover, elevated concentrations of these
elements may induce partitioning to the c phase that can
act as potent solid solution strengthener. [11–14] In addi-
tion, certain refractory elements can bond with various
trace elements, such as C, N, and O, to produce
carbides, nitrides and oxides, which could be either
detrimental or beneﬁcial to the mechanical properties
depending on the type of compound that forms. [15]

Carbides in Ni-based superalloys are typically desired
microstructural features as they can contribute to the
strengthening of the c matrix by inhibiting dislocation
motion, when present intragranularly, as well as pin
grain boundaries and help in structural reﬁnement
during fabrication and processing of the material, when
dispersed through the grain boundaries.
[16,17] More
importantly, however, they can improve high-tempera-
ture strength and creep by inhibiting slip in the grain
boundaries and preventing grain boundary sliding.
[18,19]
Carbides can also serve as sites for fatigue crack
initiation[20,21] and recently Kontis et al .[22] studied the
eﬀect of surface carbide oxidation. Their results show
that due to the volume expansion of the oxidizing
carbide, high geometrically necessary dislocation
(GND) density regions form surrounding the carbide,
which leads to c¢ dissolution and matrix recrystalliza-
tion. As a result, the oxidized carbide, carbide pores,
and softer recrystallized matrix can lead to a signiﬁcant
crack nucleation life reduction, hence the size, type, and
fraction must be controlled and optimized.
[23] In some
cases, MC carbides can undergo a solid-state transfor-
mation and decompose to form Cr-rich M 23C6 carbides
and/or W-rich M 6C carbides. [16,24,25] When present as
an alloying addition, Nb has been shown to retard the
MC decomposition reaction in Ni-based superalloys.
The thermodynamic stability of MC carbides arranged
in the order of most to least stable is TaC > NbC >
TiC > VC, where Nb is approximately equivalent to
tantalum in stabilizing the MC carbides.
[26] Hf, which
has been shown to have solubility in the c¢ phase, is
often added in trace amounts to Ni-based superalloys
due to its positive eﬀects on grain boundary cohesion,
leading to improved properties. [18,27–30] It has also been
observed to partition to and stabilize the MC carbides in
cast Ni-based superalloys and exert a signiﬁcant inﬂu-
ence on the composition and morphology, stabilizing
the blocky morphology with respect to the Chi-
nese-script shape or detrimental needle.
[31–33]
Despite many commercial Ni-based superalloys
actively utilizing MC carbides for their positive eﬀect
on the structural reﬁnement, as well as high-temperature
creep, limited literature exists on the eﬀect of Hf on the
carbide composition and morphology in powder-pro-
cessed Ni-based superalloys. In order to further under-
standing of how to improve the mechanical properties
based on the inﬂuence of MC carbides, it is important to
study how minor alloying additions can change carbide
morphology and chemistry. The aim of this study is to
elucidate the eﬀect of minor Hf additions on the
chemistry and morphology of MC carbides of two high
Nb-content powder-process superalloys through the use
of advanced characterization techniques. In addition,
density functional theory calculations are evaluated as
means for predicting carbide behavior based on minimal
experimental data.
II. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
The compositions of the alloys used in this study,
RRHT2 and RRHT4, are presented in Table I. The
levels of Cr, Co, Mo, Ta, W and C are nominally
identical for both alloys and fall within the ranges
speciﬁed in Table I. In addition, RRHT4 has been
doped with Hf, in order to elucidate its eﬀects on the
MC carbides. Both experimental alloys were fabricated
using nominally identical powder metallurgy processing
parameters, where spray atomized powder was sieved,
canned, and consolidated via hot isostatic pressing
(HIP). Following densiﬁcation to form a ~ 10 kg billet,
the material was isothermally forged at 1373 K
(1100 /C176 C) to form a pancake. Specimens were then
excised from the forged pancake and solution heat
treated at 1443 K (1170 /C176 C) for 1 hour, followed by a
controlled cool at 1 K/s (1 /C176 C/s), and aged at 1123 K
(850 /C176 C) for 4 hours in a MTI KSL-1500X muﬄe
furnace.
Metallographic samples were prepared using standard
grinding and polishing techniques to a ﬁnal polishing
using a solution of 0.06 lm colloidal silica and observed
under a JEOL JSM-6701F ﬁeld emission scanning
electron microscope (FESEM) in backscatter mode with
an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a probe current of
8 nA. Images of the microstructure were taken at 2000 9
in diﬀerent regions of each sample in order to obtain a
good representation of the carbide distribution inside
the grains, as well as at the grain boundaries, and
ImageJ
[34] was used to quantify the maximum feret size
of the MC carbides in each alloy. To study the MC
carbide structure, thin foils for transmission electron
microscopy (TEM) observation were prepared by
punching 3 mm disks from the bulk and manually
grinding them to 50 lm. The disks were subsequently
thinned in a twin-jet electropolisher with a solution of 7
pct Perchloric acid and 93 pct ethanol, at 248 K
(/C0 25 /C176 C) and 40 V. A FEI Tecnai F30 transmission
electron microscope operated at 300 kV was used for
observation, selective area diﬀraction (SAD), and
energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS).
In addition, electrolytic extraction of the minor
phases was performed using a solution of 10 pct HCl
and 1 pct tartaric acid in methanol at room temperature,
where an Ag plate was used as the cathode and the
anode consisted of a Pt wire spot-welded to the sample.

IV. DISCUSSION
Carbide phases in superalloys may signiﬁcantly
impact their properties, as they contribute to pinning
of grain boundaries during thermal—mechanical pro-
cessing, prohibiting extensive grain growth, as well as
preventing grain boundary sliding during creep condi-
tions.
[16,47] However, carbides can also serve as crack
initiation sites and drastically lower the fatigue life of
Ni-based superalloy components. [20,24] The primary MC
carbides of two high Nb content c–c¢ Ni-based super-
alloys were investigated in order to better understand
their microstructural characteristics. Although these two
alloys have very similar nominal compositions, Table I,
the MC carbide distributions are distinctly diﬀerent.
RRHT2 exhibits larger MC carbides, Table II, mean-
while the number density of MC particles in RRHT4 is
four times higher. The higher content of carbides can be
beneﬁcial for creep by providing additional barriers to
dislocation motion; however, it also provides a higher
number of potential crack initiation sites. In addition,

The calculations of bulk NbC and HfC show both

/C0 0.94 eV/atom, respectively, suggesting a stronger

Table II, explaining the increase in the MC lattice

The morphology of a precipitate in equilibrium with
its environment is determined by the surface energy of
diﬀerent facets of the precipitate, which strictly speaking
should correlate with the interfacial energy of the
precipitate and matrix. Surface calculations were per-
formed to investigate the surface segregation tendency
of solute Hf atoms, as well as the surface energy of the
carbides. The surface calculations use slab models with a
slab thickness of at least 15 A ˚ and a vacuum layer of 12
A˚ . The dimensions of the slab supercells were ﬁxed at
the equilibrium lattice constant of bulk NbC, but all
atoms were allowed to relax until forces on each atom
fall below 0.01 eV/A ˚ . The surface segregation energy
was deﬁned as the energy diﬀerence between the slabs
with the solute atom in the surface and in the middle of
the slab.
[53] A negative surface segregation energy
indicates a thermodynamic preference for the solute
atom to occupy the surface instead of the bulk sites. The
surface segregation energy of Hf atoms to the (100) and
Nb-terminated (111) surface of NbC is 0.23 eV/atom
and 0.46 eV/atom. The positive values suggest an
energetic preference for Hf to stay in the bulk. The
result is consistent with the APT results in Figure 3,
where Hf has an elevated level in the carbonitride core,
as compared to the carbide shell. The tendency of Hf
atoms not to segregate to the NbC surfaces can be
understood from the formation enthalpy calculations.
Since the magnitude of the binding energies between Hf
atoms and C is higher than that for Nb atoms and C ( i.e.
HfC has more negative formation enthalpy), locating a
Hf atom in the surface layer requires breaking energet-
ically favorable Hf-C bonds, preventing the surface
segregation of Hf atoms. Additional calculations also
indicate the trend of positive surface segregation energy
does change with the coverage of segregated surface Hf
atoms, with a slightly diﬀerent lattice constant, e.g.
using the lattice constant of HfC for the NbC slab, with
the existence of an adsorbed Ni layer and even in a NbN
host. Assuming a coherent lattice within the precipitates,
the existence of Hf atoms in the bulk creates a size
mismatch between the HfC core and the NbC shell,
which requires considerably more strain energy with the
increase of the precipitate size. Therefore, the sizes of
(Nb, Hf)C precipitates in RRHT4 are generally smaller
than the NbC precipitates in RRHT2.

V. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
Based on the results and observations presented in
this study, the following conclusions can be reported:
1. The presence of Hf in the structure of MC carbides
increases the MC/matrix lattice difference, changes
the surface energy, and causes a cubic MC carbide
morphology, rather than a blocky one.
2. With the absence of Ti, a complex MCN core
forms, consisting of various transition metals,
primarily NbN, on which the MC carbide forms.
The distribution of Hf remains fairly constant
throughout the carbide, while Ta substitutes for
Nb as the MCN transitions to the MC.
3. Thermodynamic databases predict MC carbide
compositions with limited solubilities for Zr and
Ta, compared to those measured by APT and
TEM-EDS. The Ta content of the carbide was
observed to increase as the MC carbide coarsens,
substituting for Nb.
4. DFT calculations can successfully be used in order
to accurately determine the segregation behavior of
solute atoms in MC carbides, as well as carbide
shape.
