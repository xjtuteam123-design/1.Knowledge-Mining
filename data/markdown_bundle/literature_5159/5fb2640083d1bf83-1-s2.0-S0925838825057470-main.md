ABSTRACT
Titanium alloys, leveraging advantages such as high strength and low density, are widely applied in aerospace, 
automotive, medical, and other fields, but they face challenges including significant processing difficulty, per -
formance degradation during long-term high-temperature service, and insufficient wear resistance. In the present 
work, the effects of Si and Y co-doping on the microstructure and mechanical properties of Ti-6Al-4V alloys were 
investigated. The experimental results showed that the addition of Si and Y promoted the formation of β -phase ， 
facilitated the nucleation of yttrium oxides at dimples, and altered the tensile fracture mode. Mechanical 
property tests of the alloy show that Si-Y doping exerts a significant optimization effect on the strength and 
toughness of the Ti64 alloy. It not only increases the compressive strength from the original 528 MPa to 711 MPa, 
but also raises the elongation from 10.1 % to 13.1 %, which not only verifies the synergistic enhancement effect 
of this doping scheme on strength and toughness, but also provides key support for the regulation of alloy 
properties.
1. Introduction
Titanium, an important structural metal developed in the middle of 
the 20th century, is mainly used in the aerospace industry owing to its 
advantageous properties including high strength, low density, high heat 
resistance, good corrosion resistance and good toughness. In recent 
years, significant advancements in titanium alloys have expanded their 
applications to industries such as automotive [1] , marine, petrochem -
ical, and medical fields [2] . When titanium alloy is used at high tem -
peratures for a long time, it is prone to changes in its microstructure and 
degradation of its properties, shortening its service life. Moreover, due 
to its relatively low hardness, titanium alloy often fails to meet the re -
quirements for wear resistance in applications that involve high friction 
and wear.
In the field of titanium alloy research, Qingmiao Hu et al. [3] . 
discovered that elements such as Al, Ga, Ge, and Si can promote the 
formation of ordered phases in titanium alloys. Among them [4] , Al and 
Ga act as α - stabilizing elements, while Si tends to form silicide particles 
to strengthen the metal matrix [5] . Kim et al. [6] believed that the in -
crease in the tensile strength of silicon-containing alloys benefits from 
the modification of the microstructure by the Si solid solution within a 
certain range(0 – 0.25 wt%). Evidently, silicon has a promising prospect 
in improving various properties of alloys.
Rare earth elements have the reputation of ‘industrial vitamins ’ , and 
the addition of trace rare earth elements in alloys can change the 
microstructure of the material, thus improving the performance of alloys 
to a large extent. For instance, the addition of rare earth oxides such as

confirmed that the addition of small amounts of rare earth elements or 
compounds can effectively optimize the microstructure and properties 
of titanium alloys [11] . The mechanisms by which rare earth elements 
influence titanium alloys can be categorized into two main [12] : (1) 
acting as surface-active agents to increase nucleation rates, reduce sur -
face tension [13] , and impede grain growth [14] ; (2) reacting with 
impurity elements to form precipitates at grain boundaries, thereby 
purifying the matrix and hindering dislocation movement [15] . In 
addition, scandium micro-alloying has been proven to improve the creep 
performance and service life of titanium alloys [16] . However, there is 
relatively little research on the influence of rare earth yttrium on the 
mechanical properties of silicon-containing titanium alloys.
Existing studies have shown that the effect of yttrium (Y) in different 
titanium (Ti)-based alloy systems exhibits significant matrix depen -
dence: In Ti-Al based alloys (e.g., Ti-48Al), Y achieves significant grain 
refinement by inhibiting grain growth [17] . Combining grain refinement 
strengthening and second-phase strengthening, it enhances the micro -
hardness and compressive strength of the alloy, with the compressive 
strength reaching a maximum of 2229 MPa at a Y content of 0.5 at%. 
However, excessive Y induces the formation of a reticular brittle Al ₃ Y 
phase, leading to a decrease in alloy strength, and the fracture mecha -
nism is dominated by brittle fracture. In Ti-Al-Mo-V series alloys (e.g., 
Ti-4Al-3Mo-1V), low Y content can refine as-cast β grains [18] . After 
thermomechanical processing, the dispersed Y ₂ O ₃ particles formed in 
the alloy can pin grain boundaries to inhibit grain growth, reduce the 
flow stress during superplastic deformation, and the inhibitory effect is 
more prominent at low strain rates. In biomedical β -type Ti-Nb-Zr alloys, 
Y exhibits a dual role: on one hand, it can refine grains and purify oxygen 
elements in the matrix to improve toughness; on the other hand, 
excessively large Y particle size or excessive Y content will increase the 
alloy porosity and cause performance degradation [19] . Only when Y 
exists in a small-sized form with an appropriate content (approximately 
0.3 wt%) can the fracture toughness of the alloy be effectively improved 
[20] . In Ti-6Al-4V alloys, Y realizes grain refinement through constitu -
tional supercooling, heterogeneous nucleation of Y ₂ O ₃ particles, and 
grain boundary pinning. The refinement effect becomes more significant 
after laser remelting, making Y a potential high-efficiency grain refiner 
for the additive manufacturing of this alloy. For TC4 titanium alloys, 
melting experiments with Y contents (mass fraction) of 0.3 %, 0.6 %, and 
0.9 % have shown that the density, hardness, strength, and plasticity of 
the alloy first increase and then decrease with the increase of Y content 
[21] . The hardness reaches the highest when the Y content is 0.3 %, 
while the density, strength, and plasticity achieve the optimal compre -
hensive performance when the Y content is 0.6 %. This is attributed to 
the refinement of grain size and optimization of the alloy ’ s micro -
structure by an appropriate amount of Y.
Despite extensive research on the property regulation effect of Y in 
Ti-based alloys, systematic comparative analysis is still lacking 
regarding the differences in its action mechanisms across different 
matrices, the synergistic working mechanisms with other elements, and 
process adaptability. In particular, no clear guidance has been estab -
lished for Y doping strategies. Based on this, the present study takes 
Ti64-based alloys as the research object to investigate the regulation 
mechanism of Si-Y co-doping on their microstructure and properties, 
aiming to provide theoretical support and experimental basis for the 
composition design and property customization of Ti64-based alloys.
2. Experimental materials and methods
2.1. Sample preparation
High-purity Ti, Al, V, Si, and Y (purity ≥ 99.99 wt%) were weighed 
using a high-precision electronic balance. For the purpose of preparing 
ingots with a dimension of 70 × 35 × 10 mm³ , the Ti64 – 0.25Si- x Y alloys 
were fabricated via arc melting on a water-cooled copper tray. This 
melting process was conducted with a non-consumable tungsten 
electrode under the protection of a high-purity argon atmosphere. The 
raw materials were loaded into the crucible in ascending order of their 
melting points. To enhance the compositional and microstructural ho -
mogeneity of the alloys, the samples were flipped and remelted five 
times. After the melting process, the final ingots were weighed, and the 
melting loss was determined to be less than 1 wt%.
2.2. Experimental methods
Specimens for X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) characterization, tensile testing, and compression 
testing were extracted from the ingot interior using electrical discharge 
machining (EDM) wire cutting. The tensile specimens were designed 
according to the schematic in Fig. 1 , while compression specimens were 
fabricated as cubic (4 × 4 × 8 mm³) and cylindrical ( ϕ 5 × 2 mm³) 
geometries.
Before testing, the surfaces of sample were mechanically ground and 
polished with 1200# SiC paper. Compression tests were carried out 
using a compressor at a strain rate of 1 mm/min under a force pressure 
of 150 N. Tensile tests were carried out on a LD26.105 universal me -
chanical testing machine with a strain rate of 0.001 s

3.3.2. Compressibility
Fig. 6 shows the results of the room-temperature compressive 
property test of the alloy. It can be seen from the figure that the alloy 
exhibits a strengthening response related to its composition. The 
maximum compressive strength gradually increases from 1601 MPa 
(Ti64 + 0.25 wt% Si + 0.5 wt% Y) to 1665 MPa (Ti64 + 0.25 wt% Si +
0.6 wt% Y), and then decreases slightly, reaching 1578 MPa when the 
composition is Ti64 + 0.25 wt% Si + 0.7 wt% Y.
Meanwhile, the plastic deformation capacity also shows a similar 
trend. When the composition is Ti64 + 0.25 wt%Si + 0.6 wt% Y, the 
strain reaches 33.2 %, which is 15.7 % higher than that of the matrix 
alloy (28.7 %). The corresponding strains of Ti64 + 0.25 wt%Si 
+ 0.5 wt% Y and 0.25 wt%Si + 0.7 wt% Y reach 31 % ( + 8 %) and 
32.2 % ( + 12.2 %) respectively. Therefore, the alloy with the addition of 
Ti64 + 0.25 wt%Si + 0.6 wt% Y has the best comprehensive mechanical 
properties.
In particular, after adding the Y element, the formation of the β -Ti 
and Y ₂ O ₃ phases is promoted. The increase in the β -Ti phase helps to 
improve the plasticity and strength of the matrix alloy. At the same time, 
the generated rare-earth phase Y ₂ O ₃ helps to reduce the oxygen con -
centration in the matrix, thus achieving the effect of purifying the alloy.

lattice mismatch. These high-density dislocations interact through the 
strain field in the deformation zone to trigger micropore offset. As 
described in the literature [24] , this increase in the offset dislocation 
density will ultimately lead to micropore nucleation.

In the EBSD maps of the Ti64 alloy, the colored regions exhibit a 
continuous elongated or lamellar-interlaced morphology, with narrow 
color bands extending directionally; the colors are concentrated in a 
limited number of high-frequency hues. This microstructural charac -
teristic indicates that the α -phase grains are elongated along a specific 
direction under the effect of phase transformation, thereby forming a 
strong texture. Consequently, the Ti64 alloy exhibits significant 
mechanical anisotropy, typically characterized by high strength along 
the processing direction but poor ductility in the transverse direction. 
Although the boundaries of the colored regions are dominated by light- 
colored thin strips, grain boundary statistical analysis ( Fig. 11 (c)) 
confirms that these boundaries are predominantly high-angle grain 
boundaries-a phenomenon attributed to the fact that abrupt color 
changes in EBSD maps correspond to high-angle grain boundaries. 
Nevertheless, the continuous elongated grain morphology implies 
insufficient grain boundary segmentation, resulting in a microstructure 
dominated by large grains and strong texture.
For the Ti64 – 0.25Si-0.6Y alloy, the addition of Si and Y elements 
modifies the EBSD microstructural characteristics through two key 
mechanisms: (1) second-phase pinning: Y precipitates as Y ₂ O ₃ nano -
particles at grain boundaries, which act as "pinning sites" to impede 
grain boundary migration and thus inhibit grain growth; (2) recrystal -
lization promotion: Si reduces the recrystallization activation energy or 
facilitates dynamic recovery via solid solution strengthening, thereby 
breaking deformed grains into fine recrystallized grains. These effects 
collectively lead to shortened and discontinuous colored regions in the 
EBSD map, with a more randomized color distribution that indicates 
significant texture weakening. Additionally, the number of dark-colored 
bands (a signature of high-angle grain boundaries) at the boundaries of 
colored regions increases, forming a "fine-grain + multi-grain-bound -
ary" microstructure.

The refinement of room-temperature α phases, in essence, is a chain 
reaction triggered by Y-induced primary β grain refinement: When Ti64

significantly improving the nucleation density of α phases. Dense

grain count, and morphological characteristics between Y-free and Y-

4. Conclusion
In this paper, the influence of different rare earth Y contents on the 
microstructure and properties of silicon-containing titanium alloys is 
investigated, and the main research results are as follows: 
(1) The addition of Y element changes the tensile fracture mode of 
the alloys through micropore nucleation at dimple. The alloy 
with 0.25 wt%Si + 0.6 wt% Y addition has optimal mechanical 
properties, with the tensile strength and elongation 689 MPa and 
13.1 %, respectively; and the compressive strength and defor -
mation were 1665 MPa and 33.2 %, respectively.
(2) In Ti64 alloy, Y refines primary β grains via the combined 
mechanism of "grain boundary segregation and second-phase 
pinning". This process not only enhances the nucleation density 
of α phases but also restricts their growth space, thereby enabling 
the refinement of α phases. Furthermore, the characterization 
results of α -phase texture and grain boundary misorientation 
further consolidate the correlation between "Y-induced β grain 
refinement and subsequent α -phase refinement" from the 
perspective of micro-orientation.
(3) The rare earth element Y in the alloy mainly exists in the form of 
yttrium oxide (Y ₂ O ₃ ). The formation of this oxide can effectively 
reduce the oxygen concentration in the alloy matrix, thereby 
playing a role in purifying the matrix. Furthermore, Y ₂ O ₃ particles 
preferentially precipitate at the α -phase grain boundaries and 
hinder the migration of α -phase grain boundaries. The Si element 
reduces the recrystallization activation energy of the α -phase
