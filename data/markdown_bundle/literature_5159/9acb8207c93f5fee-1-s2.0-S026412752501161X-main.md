ABSTRACT
Laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) is poised to revolutionize the manufacturing of high-value metallic materials, 
allowing for intricate, geometrically complex designs while minimizing material waste. The primary challenge 
lies in formulating alloys compatible with L-PBF that also maintain properties suitable for the demanding con -
ditions encountered in energy, space, and nuclear applications. We introduce a category of high strength, defect- 
resistant octonary CoNi-based high entropy superalloy (CoNi-HESA), comprising roughly equal parts of Co and 
Ni, along with Cr, Al, V, Ti, Ta, and W. This alloy exhibits as-printed tensile strength exceeding 1 GPa and tensile 
ductility exceeding 30 % at room temperature. Furthermore, compression tests demonstrate that the as-printed 
parts maintain a yield strength of about 1 GPa at room temperature up to 700 
◦
C, which decreases to 0.9 GPa and 
0.7 GPa as the test temperature reaches 800 
◦
C and 900 
◦
C, respectively. With a careful combination of laser 
powder and scan speed, the developed HESA is well-suited for crack-resistant, high-density component pro -
duction through L-PBF. Alloy design principles are elucidated through CALPHAD calculations based on the high 
entropy alloy (HEA) database, including the structure and properties of L-PBF processed CoNi-HESA.
1. Introduction
The aerospace sector has long recognized the critical importance of 
increasing the maximum operating temperature of aircraft engines to 
enhance engine efficiency [ 1 ]. Significant efforts have been devoted to 
the development of advanced metallic and intermetallic materials with 
exceptional performance capabilities [ 2 – 4 ]. Among these materials, Ni- 
based superalloys have stood at the forefront for decades due to their 
remarkable high-temperature properties. These alloys are renowned for 
their ability to undergo γ’ -Ni
3
(Al,Ti) precipitation hardening, which is 
coupled with solid solution strengthening, carbide hardening, and the 
ability to produce directionally solidified and single crystal components. 
As a result, Ni-based superalloys exhibit exceptional resistance to creep 
deformation, making them a state-of-the-art choice for demanding 
aerospace applications [ 5 , 6 ].
Traditional Co-based superalloys have historically been limited to 
low- stress components due to their lower mechanical properties at high 
temperatures than Ni-based superalloys. These alloys are typically 
strengthened by solid-solution hardening and carbide precipitation, 
providing good corrosion and oxidation resistance [ 7 , 8 ]. However, the 
use of traditional Co-based superalloys has been restricted in high-stress 
applications [ 9 ]. Nevertheless, a breakthrough occurred when Sato et al. 
[ 10 ] demonstrated that Co-based superalloys could also achieve signif -
icant strengthening through the precipitation of γ’– Co
3
(Al,W) with an 
L1
2 
crystal structure. This discovery has sparked increasing interest in 
research on Co-based superalloys and their potential for wider appli -
cation [ 10 – 14 ]. The exploration of CoNi-based superalloys as a novel 
research direction emerged from the concept of combining the strengths 
of two different superalloy families, both characterized by γ’ precipita -
tion hardening. This synergistic approach aims to achieve exceptional 
performance at high temperatures, facilitated by an increased γ’ solvus 
temperature [ 15 – 17 ].
Together with the need to increase the maximum operating tem -
perature of aircraft engines, there has been a growing interest in 
obtaining near-net shape defect-free complex-shaped components, 
especially through additive manufacturing (AM) technologies. Laser

manufacturing technique, in which the microstructure can be engi -
neered through L-PBF process and post heat treatments [ 18 – 22 ]. How -
ever, rapid cooling can introduce defects such as cracks and pores, which 
significantly affect the mechanical properties and performance of the 
final product [ 23 – 26 ].
Among the materials extensively studied by the AM community, Ni- 
based superalloys stand out for their remarkable high-temperature 
properties. Regarding printability, these alloys can be classified into 
two categories including weldable and non-weldable Ni superalloys. The 
differentiation lies in the volume fraction of the γ’ phase, a crucial 
precipitate that imparts superior high-temperature properties to these 
alloys [ 18 ]. Weldable Ni superalloys have a γ’ volume fraction of less 
than 40 %, while non-weldable Ni superalloys has a γ’ volume fraction 
between 40 % and 80 %. The chemical composition of these alloys varies 
primarily in terms of the amount of Ti and Al present, as these elements 
play a vital role in γ’ formation [ 27 – 31 ].
To mitigate issues regarding non-weldable Ni superalloys, several 
strategies have been developed to optimize the L-PBF process and 
improve the quality of the AM parts. For example, controlling the laser 
power and scan speed can help to reduce the temperature gradient and 
cooling rate, leading to a more homogeneous microstructure and fewer 
defects [ 32 ]. Preheating the powder bed and reducing the layer thick -
ness can also improve the thermal and mechanical properties of the AM 
parts [ 33 – 36 ]. Currently, there is a significant interest in newly devel -
oped CoNi-based superalloys due to their higher volume fraction of γ’ 
phase and their ability to provide a narrow freezing range and good hot 
processing window. These characteristics make the CoNi-based alloys 
potential candidates for overcoming the drawbacks of Ni-based super -
alloys [ 11 , 32 ].
Summarizing the research progress on the 3D printing of HEAs, 
particularly the FCC + L1
2 
series, reveals significant advancements. 
Studies have demonstrated that AM techniques such as L-PBF, can 
effectively produce HEAs with desirable properties, including high 
strength and ductility. The FCC + L1
2 
series, known for its excellent 
mechanical properties and thermal stability, has been successfully 
fabricated using 3D printing, showcasing refined microstructures and 
enhanced performance [ 37 – 40 ]. Continued research in this area focuses 
on optimizing processing parameters, understanding microstructural 
evolution, and improving the mechanical properties to further expand 
the applications of these advanced materials in various industries.
Murray et al. [ 15 ] conducted pioneering work in the processing of 
CoNi-based superalloys in 2020. Their research focused on the devel -
opment of a defect-resistant Co-36.4Ni-13.2Al-1 W-3.5Ta-6Cr (at. %) 
alloy, known as SB-CoNi-10, specifically designed for metal AM. To 
enhance grain boundary strengthening, Small amounts of C, B and Y 
were added to the alloy composition to improve grain boundary 
strength. Using electron beam powder bed fusion (EB-PBF) and L-PBF, 
the SB-CoNi-10 parts were successfully produced. Notably, the alloy 
demonstrated excellent printability, and careful control of the solvus 
and solidus temperature range (also known as freeing range, Δ T =
52 
◦
C) helped prevent cracking during the fusion-based AM processes. 
The mechanical properties of as-build and post processed alloys were 
remarkable, with ultimate tensile strength (UTS) exceeding 1.1 GPa and 
tensile ductility greater than 13 % at room temperature. This study 
highlighted the potential of CoNi-based superalloys for AM applications, 
particularly in achieving a high-volume fraction of γ’ precipitates of 
about 70 % and demonstrating low susceptibility to cracking defects. In 
another work, Murray et al. [ 41 ] successfully manufactured an SB-CoNi- 
10 and a higher carbon variant (SB-CoNi-10C) using the L-PBF process. 
They revealed that chemical microsegregation was less pronounced in 
the L-PBF fabricated SB-CoNi-10 compared to other fabrication 
methods, and different heat treatments affected the microstructure and 
mechanical properties, including recrystallization, grain structure 
retention, and the precipitation of γ’ phase, ultimately influencing the 
tensile ductility and strength at different temperatures. More recently, 
Raeker et al. [ 42 ] investigated the impact of minor alloying elements on 
the solidification cracking susceptibility (SCS) of CoNi-based superalloys 
during the L-PBF process. Specifically, the addition of B and C to the SB- 
CoNi-10 alloy was studied. The results revealed that even a small in -
crease in B content rendered the alloy highly susceptible to cracking 
during L-PBF processing.
Based on the reported data, it becomes evident that the development 
of CoNi-based superalloys through AM technologies is still in its early 
stages. These studies highlight the need for further exploration in 
various aspects, such as (1) investigating different chemical composi -
tions, (2) understanding the effects of process parameters, and (3) 
employing innovative alloy design strategies. By delving deeper into 
these areas, it is possible to unravel the intricate properties and potential 
applications of CoNi-based superalloys, paving the way for advance -
ments in AM and the realization of their full potential. Consequently, 
extensive efforts and continued research are necessary to push the 
boundaries and unlock the transformative capabilities of CoNi-based 
superalloys in the field of advanced manufacturing.
The present study focuses on investigating the AM processing of a 
CoNi-based high entropy superalloy (CoNi-HESA) using the L-PBF pro -
cess. A Design of Experiment (DOE) approach based on Response Sur -
face Methodology (RSM) was used to gain a comprehensive 
understanding of the process. This approach allowed for the systematic 
study of the effects of L-PBF input parameters on important factors such 
as densification, microstructural features, presence of defects, and 
microhardness. Furthermore, the mechanical properties of the optimal 
sample were evaluated at both room and elevated temperatures. 
Through the integration of experimental and computational techniques, 
this study aims to offer valuable insights into the processability and 
properties of L-PBF fabricated CoNi-HESA, thereby contributing to the 
advancement of AM of superalloys.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Alloy design
Following the introduction of L1
2 
strengthened Co-based superalloys 
in 2006 [ 10 ], the task of selecting an optimal chemical composition and 
comprehending the impact of alloying elements on microstructural 
features and high-temperature properties poses a significant challenge. 
Our previous investigation [ 43 – 46 ], centered on novel Co – or CoNi- 
based superalloys, we proposed a hypothesis concerning the correla -
tion between the entropy of mixing and the solvus temperature of γ’ . We 
have demonstrated that an increase in the entropy of mixing has a 
positive influence on increasing the γ’ solvus temperature in CoNi-based 
superalloys. Our study integrated the thermodynamic principles of high 
entropy alloys (HEAs) core effects into CoNi-based superalloy to design 
and develop CoNi-based HESA. Our designed HESA, characterized by a 
chemical composition of Co-35Ni-8Al-4Ti-4 V-2Ta-2 W-9Cr (all 
composition values are given in at. %) exhibited an entropy of mixing 
equal to 1.568R (where R is the universal gas constant), a γ’ solvus 
temperature of 1165 
◦
C, and a γ’ area fraction of ≈ 68 % after aging at 
900 
◦
C for 24 h. Notably, in the alloy design, we endeavored to narrow 
the freezing range to 62 
◦
C, enhancing the AM processability and miti -
gating solidification susceptibility in the developed CoNi-HESA. In this 
context, our objective is to expand our understanding of powder pro -
duction through gas-atomization and assess the AM processability using 
the L-PBF process for the recently formulated HESA. Additionally, we 
seek to validate the results of thermodynamical calculations using the 
calculation of phase diagrams (CALPHAD) approach on the AM- 
processed samples. For thermodynamic calculations, the commercially 
available TCHEA6 database in ThermoCalc 2022a software (Solna, 
Sweden) was employed to compute isopleth phase diagrams, mass molar 
fractions, and solidification curves, utilizing both the Lever rule for 
equilibrium states and the Gulliver-Scheil classic model for non- 
equilibrium (NE) states.

2.2. The L-PBF processing
Pre-alloyed CoNi-HESA powder was produced via argon gas atomi -
zation by CEIT, San Sebasti ´an, Spain. The as-received feedstock was 
sieved to obtain a powder with a particle size distribution between 20 
and 63 µ m . A Renishaw AM400 with Ar flow was then used to process 
the sieved powder. To investigate the printability of the developed 
powder and determine the optimal parameters for producing high- 
density parts using L-PBF, a series of experiments were conducted 
based on the available information on L-PBF of CoNi-based superalloys 
[ 41 ]. The input parameters considered for this study were laser power 
(LP) and scanning speed (SS), while relative density (RD) was chosen as 
the output response variable. To establish the experimental design, LP 
was varied at five levels ranging from 110 to 190 W, and SS was varied at 
four levels ranging from 550 to 850 mm/s. Initially, a full factorial 
design layout was considered, resulting in 20 experimental conditions 
for optimization. Subsequently, fourteen experiments were selected as a 
reference to eliminate conditions with volumetric energy density (VED 
= LP / ( SS × LP × LT )) greater than 90J/mm
3

3

2.3. Characterizations
The as-received powder was subjected to comprehensive character -
ization to evaluate its particle size distribution using laser diffraction, 
chemical composition, morphology and microstructure analysis. Field- 
emission gun scanning electron microscopy (FEG-SEM) was performed 
using an Apreo 2S microscope from ThermoFisher Scientific, equipped 
with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and electron backscattered 
diffraction (EBSD) Oxford Instruments detectors. As-printed coupons 
with a dimension of 5 mm × 7 mm × 5 mm (for optimization) were 
characterized in terms of RD. The RD was determined using an image 
processing method. The coupons were cut on plane XZ, which is parallel 
to the building direction (BD). The section surface of the coupons was 
subsequently ground using griding papers up to 2000 grit size and pol -
ished using 1 µ m diamond paste. For each sample, 10 micrographs were 
captured at 100 × magnification using an Olympus BX51 optical mi -
croscope (OM). The RD values were then calculated using ImageJ soft -
ware [ 47 – 49 ]. To quantify defects, mainly pores, micro-computed 
tomography ( μ -CT) was used to collect data from optimized sample 
using a Zeiss Merotom 899 under 85 kV and 47 mA and final spatial 
resolution of 5 μ m/pixel. Post-process and visualization of the μ -CT data 
was carried out using the VGSTUDIO software. Additionally, the OM 
images were utilized to determine the crack density and crack count. 
The crack density represents the total length of recognized cracks per 
unit area, while the crack count indicates the total number of recognized 
cracks per unit area [ 30 , 50 ]. Furthermore, Vickers microhardness tests 
were conducted using a load of 1 kg and a dwell time of 10 s. An average 
of five measurements per coupon was reported for each experimental 
condition.
The XZ surface was polished using 40 nm silica oxide polishing 
suspension (OPS) following the hardness testing to prepare the samples 
for microstructure analysis using SEM, EDS, and EBSD techniques. EBSD 
analysis was conducted using a voltage of 20 kV and a current ranging 
from 2.7-3.2 kV with a step size of 500 nm. Additionally, the sample 
surfaces were chemically etched with Kalling ’ s No. 2 solution to enhance 
the visibility of melt pools and grains. Both OM and SEM micrographs 
were captured to provide a comprehensive understanding of the 
microstructural features and defects. To analyze the XY microstructure, 
coupon number 4 (see Table 1 ) was cut on a plane perpendicular to the 
building direction. The same procedure as the XZ plane was repeated for 
this plane as well. OM, SEM, and EBSD analyses were carried out to 
examine the microstructure of the sample on the XY plane. Moreover, 
the XY plane microstructure was investigated at nano-scale through 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) employing a FEI Talos F200x 
microscope operating at a voltage of 200 kV. TEM lamellae were ac -
quired using the trenching-and-lift-out method with a focused ion beam 
(FIB) on a FEI Helios NanoLab 600i FEGSEM operating at 30 kV.

condition were printed for evaluation of mechanical properties. The

3.2. L-PBF process optimization
3.2.1. Linear analysis of parameter effects on relative density and defects
The experimental design layout of the L-PBF process is presented in 
Table 1 . In Fig. 2a , RD is plotted as a function of LP and SS, assuming a 
linear relationship between output response and input parameters. An 
evident optimum-like trend is observed, where four different combina -
tions (150 W, 850 mm/s; 170 W, 850 mm/s; 190 W, 850 mm/s; 150 W,

4. Discussion
The discussion section includes two key aspects: 1) evaluating the 
performance of the fabricated HESA via the L-PBF process, using both 
calculated phase diagrams and acquired experimental results, and 2) 
understanding the relationship between microstructure and resultant 
mechanical properties.
4.1. CALPHAD calculations
In our prior study [ 43 ], the CoNi-HESA was precisely design and 
developed with a focus on maximizing configurational entropy, using 
Thermocalc software along with a Ni-based superalloys database. This 
design methodology was subsequently validated through casting and 
heat treatment experiments. In the present context, we aim to assess the 
efficacy of Thermocalc in predicting potential phase formations during 
the L-PBF process. Fig. 8 represents the performed phase diagram cal -
culations using the Thermocalc software which is conducted using the 
HEAs database. In Fig. 8 a and b, which are calculated using Lever rule 
(equilibrium state) it is evident that at 35 at.% Ni, the dashed line 
crosses the β phase region. A comparative analysis with the Ni-based 
superalloys database [ 43 ] reveals that the HEAs database offers more 
accurate predictions regarding the formation of the β phase. Notably, 
Fig. 8b confirms the formation of β phase, although the volume fraction 
of the β phase is negligible.
Segregation during AM processes in superalloys is a common chal -
lenge, but Co-based superalloys exhibiting lower levels of segregation 
during solidification compared to Ni-based superalloys [ 68 ]. Fig. 8c il -
lustrates the solidification pathway of the developed HESA using the 
classic Scheil solidification model, where it becomes apparent that the 
formation of the β phase is more likely. According to Fig. 4c , the for -
mation of the β phase depends on the cooling rate, which is related to LP. 
At lower LP (lower cooling rate, Sample 6), the formation of the β phase 
is more likely, but with an increase in LP (higher cooling rate, Samples 4 
and 11), the amount of β phase decreases. In total, the measured amount 
of β phase is less than 1 %, aligning well with phase diagram 
calculations.
In a recent examination of the L-PBF processed Co-Ni-Al-W alloy in 
its as-built state [ 69 ], the study reported the formation of the β phase, 
originating from W-deficient regions, acting as potential crack nucle -
ation sites. It is noteworthy to consider that the Co-Ni-Al-W powder 
source differed, employing elemental powder mixed via low-energy ball 
milling, which could have a more pronounced influence on segregation 
in the as-built state. However, after undergoing homogenization, the 
observed W segregation completely dissolved in the matrix, resulting in 
the attainment of a random solid solution. Another study focusing on 
PBF processed SB-CoNi-10 alloys [ 15 ] revealed a less pronounced solute 
segregation, indicating enhanced resistance to cracking. This highlights 
the importance of post-processing treatments in mitigating segregation-

4.2. Manufacturability of CoNi-based superalloys with a high γ ′ content
Raising the operating temperature of engines is key to improving 
performance, but it requires materials that can retain strength and resist 
degradation under extreme conditions. Ni-based superalloys have 
excelled in this role for decades, thanks to their γ ′ precipitation 
strengthening and exceptional creep resistance, yet their high γ ′ content 
makes AM challenging due to cracking and other defects [ 18 , 23 ]. Recent 
breakthroughs in CoNi-based superalloys, combining the favorable at -
tributes of both Co – and Ni-based systems, offer a promising alternative 
[ 11 , 15 , 32 , 41 , 42 ]. These alloys exhibit high γ ′ volume fractions, narrow 
freezing ranges, and improved hot-processing windows, making them 
attractive for AM of complex, near-net-shape components. To maximize 
manufacturability and performance of CoNi-based superalloys as a new 
solid-solution – strengthened alloys, proactive measures are necessary to 
minimize common defects such as pores and cracking.
It is crucial to avoid LoF defects since their irregular shape can act as 
stress concentrators, leading to a degradation of mechanical properties 
[ 48 , 73 ]. The main reason for the formation of these defects is believed to 
be lower LP (see Fig. 2b ). In such cases, the laser beam does not possess 
sufficient energy to fully melt consecutive layers and ensure material 
continuity. Interestingly, when both SS and LP are increased while 
maintaining a constant VED (i.e., constant LP/SS ratio), the presence of 
these defects is reduced. This observation suggests that higher energy 
input (leading to higher volumetric energy density) during the printing 
process helps mitigate the formation of LoF defects. In this context, it is 
challenging to establish a general threshold for VED since the same VED 
value (e.g., 62.5 J/mm
3
) can be achieved through different combina -
tions of SS and LP, resulting in either the formation or the absence of LoF 
defects. However, when a fixed value of SS (e.g., 550 mm/s) is main -
tained and LP (and thus VED) is increased, a threshold for VED can be 
identified. For instance, above a VED value of ≥ 85 J/mm
3
, this type of 
defect has not been observed [ 51 ]. Furthermore, when larger particles 
are present in the powder bed, coupled with low values of LP, they can 
contribute to the formation of irregular shape pore defects. These larger 
particles act as a barrier, preventing the smaller particles from melting 
properly and inhibiting the re-melting of the top of the previously so -
lidified layer. This issue, which is caused by shadowing effect [ 74 , 75 ], 
leads to a discontinuity in the printed structure. This observation em -
phasizes the significance and impact of the starting powder on the 
overall quality of components produced via the L-PBF process [ 76 ].

segregations or TCP phases. Tensile properties and corresponding frac -

crystal structure. Ti and Al are particularly noteworthy as they are prone

cracking during the process, especially when their concentration 
exceeds 6 wt% [ 80 – 82 ]. The addition of Ni to Co-based superalloys 
proves beneficial, enhancing the thermal stability of γ’ and facilitating 
the incorporation of additional alloying elements such as Cr, Ta, and V to 
further elevate the γ’ solvus temperature and enhance high-temperature 
performance. Moreover, the increase of configurational entropy, 
coupled with phenomena such as sluggish diffusion and the cocktail 
effect, serves as an additional rationale to postpone or prevent the for -
mation of undesirable phases, particularly at elevated temperatures.

5. Conclusions
In summary, the selection of an octonary CoNi-HESA (Co-35Ni-8Al- 
4Ti-4 V-2Ta-2 W-9Cr) for L-PBF processing was informed by CALPHAD- 
based thermodynamic predictions with the aim of developing an alloy 
with a narrow freezing range to minimize cracking and solidification 
susceptibility and achieve a potentially high γ’ volume fraction after 
ageing treatment.
Using a combination of design of experiment coupled with process 
map derived from various laser power and scan speed combinations, 
crack-free parts were successfully produced with a remarkable relative 
density exceeding 99.9 %.
Microstructure analysis revealed the presence of sub-micron size 
grains with a solid solution γ phase in the as-built specimens due to high 
temperature gradient and rapid cooling during L-PBF without pre- 
heating.
The as-fabricated parts demonstrated remarkable tensile strength 
and ductility of ~1 GPa and ~30 %, respectively, at room temperature.
Compression tests further indicated sustained yield strength of about 
1 GPa at room temperature up to 700 
◦
C, gradually decreasing to 0.9 
GPa and 0.7 GPa at 800 
◦

◦

of CoNi-based superalloys by means of thermodynamic predictions 
based on mixing entropy allows to substantially improve the properties
