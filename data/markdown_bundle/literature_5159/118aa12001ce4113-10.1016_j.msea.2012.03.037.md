1. Introduction
With the development of aerospace industry, higher require-
ment for mechanical properties and serve temperature of titanium
alloys has been proposed. In order to further improve high-
temperature strength of titanium alloys, ceramic reinforcements
with high elastic modulus and high strength were incorporated
into titanium-alloy matrices [1–4]. Much work has conﬁrmed that
titanium matrix composites (TMCs) exhibit high strength both at
room and high temperatures and excellent creep resistance [5–7].
Among the discontinuous ceramic reinforcements used in TMCs,
TiC has been considered as a very suitable reinforcement, mainly
owing to its excellent properties and good compatibility with tita-
nium matrices [8,9]. In the past few decades, powder metallurgy
(PM) [10,11] and combustion assisted cast (CAC) [12,13] have been
widely used to fabricate in situ TMCs. Compared with PM process,
CAC technology, exhibiting signiﬁcant advantages such as uniform
distribution of reinforcements, high compactness and good purity,
large scale production, simplicity, low cost and ﬂexibility, have
attracted great attention [9,14].
Previous researches on tensile properties of TMCs are mainly
concentrated in the temperature range from room temperature to
700 ◦C [15–17]. Higher temperature tensile properties should be
studied in order to enhance the serve temperature. An investigation
conducted by de Castro et al. [16] in TiC/Ti composites with TiC vol-
ume fraction lower than 10% shown that ultimate tensile strengths

In this paper, TiC/Ti-alloy matrix composites with 5, 10 and
15 vol.% TiC manufactured using CAC technique were tested at
700 ◦C, 800 ◦C and 900 ◦C. The main purpose of present work is to
investigate the inﬂuence of TiC volume fraction and test tempera-
ture on tensile strength.
2.
 Experimental procedure
In this experiment, an induced skull melting furnace (ISM)
was used to fabricate Ti–6Al–3Sn–3.5Zr–0.4Mo–0.75Nb–0.35Si
matrix composites reinforced with 5 and 10 vol.% TiC, referred
to as TMC1 and TMC2, respectively. In addition, 15 vol.%
TiC/Ti–6Al–3Sn–3.5Zr–0.4Mo composite (referred to as TMC3) was
prepared by medium frequency induction melting furnace. Choos-
ing the two different matrix compositions is based on the following
two reasons. First, increasing TiC volume fraction will inevitably
degrade the tensile elongation at room temperature. Decreasing
the content of alloying elements can make TMC3 possess certain
room-temperature ductility since matrix ductility can be enhanced
correspondingly. Second, it is well known that the contribution
of Nb and Si on high-temperature tensile strengths of titanium
alloys is not signiﬁcant. In addition, the contents of Nb and Si
are very low. For preparing these composites, the raw materials

Olympus optical microscopy (OM) and scanning electron

specimens with gauge length of 20 mm and cross section of
4 mm × 2 mm were cut by electric discharging from the castings
and carried out on Instron 5500R testing machine at a strain rate of
0.5 mm/min at 700 ◦C, 800 ◦C and 900 ◦C. In order to ensure repro-
ducibility, three samples were tested at each condition. Fracture
surfaces were observed by SEM.
3.
 Results
3.1. Phase identiﬁcation and microstructure
Fig. 1 shows the XRD patterns of TMC2. It can be observed that
the present phases are /H9251-Ti and TiC. The XRD analysis proves that
TMCs reinforced with TiC can be fabricated by in situ technique
utilizing the reaction between Ti and C. Similar results have been
reported by Ma et al. in TiC/Ti1100 composite [19].
Fig. 2 shows the microstructural characteristics of TiC in these
composites. It can be seen that reinforcements are uniformly dis-
tributed in the matrix macroscopically. TiC grows in long strip
shape in TMC1 (Fig. 1a), which is eutectic TiC. In TMC2, primary
TiC exhibits equiaxed or near-equiaxed shape with sizes mainly in
the range from 5 /H9262m to 10 /H9262m, while eutectic TiC shows short rod
shape, as shown in Fig. 1(b). Primary TiC accounts for the major-
ity of the reinforcements. With the increase in TiC volume fraction,

3.3. Fractography
In order to further investigate the deformation behavior of
matrix and the load-bearing effect of TiC as well as fracture mech-
anisms at high temperatures, fracture surfaces and longitudinal
sections just below the fracture surfaces of the failed composites
were examined. The SEM micrographs of the fracture surfaces of
TMC1, TMC2 and TMC3 at 700 ◦C and 800 ◦C are showed in Fig. 5.
It can be seen from Fig. 5(a) that TMC1 exhibits a brittle cleavage
fracture mode at 700 ◦C. Cracked TiC can almost not be identiﬁed in
Fig. 5(a). However, strip-like TiC in TMC1 was broken into several
pieces during tensile deformation, which is observed in the lon-
gitudinal section (Fig. 6a). This is due to the mismatch between

TiC and matrix strain. The fracture surfaces of TMC2 and TMC3
are composed of the cleavage planes and tearing ridges or dim-
ples (Fig. 5b and c), indicating a mixed ductile and brittle fracture
mode at 700 ◦C. It should be noticed that the number of dimples in
Fig. 5(b) is more than that in Fig. 5(c), reﬂecting that the matrix of
TMC2 experienced larger plastic deformation at 700 ◦C. Evidently,
the failure of all composites is initiated by TiC fracture followed by
the ductile damage of the matrix at 700 ◦C [21,22].
As tensile temperature increases to 800 ◦C, fracture surfaces of
all composites show dimple characteristic (Fig. 5a–f), showing that
the composites all fractured in a ductile manner and void nucle-
ation, growth and coalescence cause the fracture of TMC1, TMC2
and TMC3. Additionally, few TiC pulled-out can be found, showing
that a large amount of plastic deformation of the matrix exceeds
TiC. These observations suggest that load-bearing effect of TiC at
800 ◦C is remarkably weakened compared to that at 700 ◦C since
the interfacial strength is signiﬁcantly low as a result of matrix soft-
ening. Razaghian et al. [23] obtained the similar result in 15 vol.%
SiC/7075Al composites at high temperatures.
At 800 ◦C, TiC with strip shape in TMC1 displays similar fracture
characteristic with that at 700 ◦C and were arranged in the deforma-
tion direction in the longitudinal section (Fig. 6b), whereas majority
of primary TiC particles with equiaxed morphology in TMC2 did not
fracture after the failure of the composite, as illustrated in Fig. 6(c).
4.
 Discussion
The understanding of stress–strain characteristic and fracture
mechanisms of the composites at high temperatures can provide
useful insight into matrix softening characteristic and the strength-
ening mechanisms [24]. It is apparent that the capacity of the
composites to resistant plastic deformation is the most impor-
tant factor above 700 ◦C (Fig. 4), determining the high-temperature
tensile strength. Taking into account that matrix dominates the
plastic deformation of the composites, improving the resistance
to matrix deformation is vital, which is affected by the addition
of TiC.
With respect to the strengthening factors in composites, it
is well established that the strengthening effect of C is mainly
attributed to two factors: one is the solid solution of C in matrix;
the other is load-bearing mechanism of TiC particles [19,25]. In
addition, the enhanced strength of matrix due to an improve-
ment in the dislocation density of matrix is a more important
strengthening effect [26]. Solid solution strengthening is almost
not dependent on tensile temperature [19]. Load-bearing effect
of reinforcement and matrix strengthening effect are dependent
on each other. In general, the higher the matrix strength, the
more obvious the load-bearing effect of reinforcement is; vice
versa. Moreover, load-bearing effect of reinforcements is restricted
by the interfacial strength between matrix and reinforcement
in TMCs [17].
It can be deduced from Fig. 6 that strip-like TiC can impede
the plastic ﬂow of the matrix more prominently than that with
equiaxed shape. In other words, load-bearing effect of TiC with
strip shape in TMC1 is more evident, although tensile tempera-
ture is higher than 700 ◦C, when comparing with that of TiC with
equiaxed shape in TMC2. Hence, strip-like TiC makes larger contri-
bution on tensile strength than that with equiaxed shape. This help
in explaining the small discrepancy in UTSs of TMC1 and TMC2
above 700 ◦C. Additionally, in our experiment, as TMC1 was heat-
treated in /H9252 + TiC phase ﬁeld, strip-like TiC was of granulation and
the UTS of TMC1 at 800 ◦C is 244.1 ± 4.2 MPa, reduced signiﬁcantly
compared to the as-cast UTS (Table 1). This provides another evi-
dence to conﬁrm that the contribution of TiC with strip shape on

When TiC all exhibits equiaxed or near-equiaxed shape in TMC2
and TMC3 (Fig. 2), increasing TiC volume fraction leads to the
enhancement in the matrix strength as a result of the increase in
the dislocation density of the matrix [27]. Arsenault et al. [28] also
indicated that dislocation density increases in the matrix with an
increase in reinforcement volume fraction in SiC/1100Al compos-
ites and thus, yield stresses are improved. According to the results in
Fig. 5(e) and (f), load-bearing effect of TiC in TMC2 and TMC3 above
800 ◦C is believed to be limited due to very low interfacial strength.
Thus, matrix dominates the tensile strength and the enhancement
in UTSs of TMC3 is mainly attributed to the higher matrix strength
above 800 ◦C. At 700 ◦C, since TMC3 exhibits relatively small dim-
ples, the capacity of matrix in TMC3 to resistant plastic deformation
should be stronger than that in TMC2. Accordingly, load-bearing
effect
 of TiC in TMC3 is more obvious than that in TMC2. All of these
contribute to the enhancement in UTS of TMC3 at 700 ◦C. Therefore,
it can be concluded that increasing TiC volume fraction can enhance
high-temperature tensile strength.
Based on the variation of elongations with tensile temperature
(Table 1), it is reasonable to believe that matrix softening is more
and more serious with increasing test temperature. It is therefore
expected that the capacity of matrix to resistant plastic deformation
is weakened, i.e. matrix strength is reduced as a result of dislocation
climb and recovery process directly decreasing dislocation den-
sity [23]. In addition, matrix softening can decrease load-bearing
effect of TiC. The two factors attribute to the degradation in UTSs
of all composites as the tensile temperature increases. It should
be noted that the decrease in matrix strength is the dominant fac-
tor. Hence, linear decrease in UTSs of all composites above 700 ◦C
reﬂects decreasing tendency of matrix strength in this temperature

5. Conclusion
Tensile strengths of in situ TiC/Ti-alloy matrix composites above
700 ◦C were experimentally investigated in the present work. The
following conclusions can be drawn:
1. Ultimate tensile strengths degrade approximately 170 MPa per
100 ◦C increase in tensile temperature in all composites, while
elongation increases faster and faster above 700 ◦C.
2. There exists small difference in the ultimate tensile strength of
the composites with 5 and 10 vol.% TiC due to the discrepancy in
load-bearing effect of strip-like TiC and TiC with equiaxed shape.
With further increase in TiC volume fraction, ultimate tensile
strength is enhanced and the increase is about 50 MPa at each
temperature.
3. The
 failure of all composites is initiated by the fracture of TiC
followed by ductile damage of matrix at 700 ◦C, whereas above
800 ◦C, void nucleation, growth and coalescence result in the
failure of all composites.
