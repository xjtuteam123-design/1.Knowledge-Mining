Abstract
In an effort to impro ve the high-temperature mechanical properties of FeAl, we in vestigated the effects of alloying addition and
heat treatment temperature on the tensile and creep properties of Fe /C1/40at.% Al. Alloying of Fe /C1/40Al with titanium, molybdenum,
zirconium, boron and carbon, and heat treating at 1300 8C resulted in significant improvement in strength and creep resistance due
to solid solution and precipitation hardening mechanisms. The strength and the creep resistance of FeAl alloys at 700 8C is better
than several grades of stainless steels, superalloys, Fe 3Al alloys and an ODS-FeAl alloy.

1. Introduction
The intermetallic compound FeAl exists with an
ordered B2 structure up to the melting temperature,
and is stable in the composition range 36 /C1
/50 at.%
aluminum [1]. FeAl alloys are promising as structural
materials for a wide variety of high-temperature struc-
tural applications in view of their attracti ve properties
such as low cost, low density, good wear resistance and
excellent high-temperature corrosion resistance. In ad-
dition, it exhibits high electrical resisti vity [2,3] and can
be made ferromagnetic [4 /C1
/6] under certain conditions.
Mechanical properties of FeAl are sensiti ve to the
aluminum content and useful aluminum content is
found to be in the range 38 /C1
/42 at.%. A unique feature
of FeAl is that it generates a large number of thermal
vacancies, and the vacancy concentration increases with
increasing aluminum content [7 /C1/12]. Heat treatment
temperature and cooling rate determine the magnitude
of the vacancy concentration. As will be discussed later,
vacancy concentration profoundly affects the mechan-
ical properties (ductility, strength and creep resistance).
Understanding the generation of vacancies and ability
to control and tailor the vacancy hardening may help in
attaining the desired mechanical properties. General
overviews on the recent progress in de veloping alloys
based on FeAl intermetallic can be found in the papers
by Baker and Munroe [1], Liu et al. [13], Dee vi et al.
[14], Stoloff et al. [15] and Maziasz et al. [16].
It is en visaged that some of the ferritic steels,
austenitic steels, and iron-based superalloys will e ven-
tually be replaced by FeAl [13 /C1/16]. Howe ver, wide
commercial usage of FeAl is limited due to its low
ambient temperature ductility, and poor strength and
creep resistance abo ve 600 8C. Poor room-temperature
ductility of FeAl is belie ved to be due to a combination
of moisture-induced embrittlement, inherently weak
grain boundaries, and vacancy hardening [1,13].I n
recent years, considerable enhancement in ductility has
been achieved by a variety of thermo-mechanical treat-
ments and by alloying with boron [13]. Despite their
high yield strength at ambient temperatures, iron
aluminides ha ve poor strength and creep resistance at
elevated temperatures. This may be attributed to the
open B2 structure, which enhances diffusion-controlled
creep at temperatures higher than about 0.4 on the
homologous temperature scale. The creep strength of

2. Experimental
The nominal compositions of the two representati ve
alloys are shown in Table 1 and are denoted by their
melt numbers. Composition of alloy 912 is designed to
improve the strength and creep resistance through solid
solution hardening due to elements like molybdenum,
titanium and zirconium. In alloy 787, carbon content is
increased to form carbides (by combining with strong
carbide formers like titanium) and the alloy deri ves its
strength through precipitation hardening. Each alloy of
approximately 7 kg in weight was prepared by arc
melting under argon atmosphere and was drop-cast into
a water-cooled copper mold. The alloys were canned in
a mild-steel container and extruded at the selected
extrusion temperature ( Table 1) with an extrusion ratio
of 21:1. Microstructures, precipitates and fracture
surfaces of the alloys were characterized by scanning
electron microscopy (JEOL 35 C) equipped with an
EDAX detector.
Tensile and creep tests were carried out on round
specimens with a gauge length of 25 mm and a gauge
diameter of 5.7 mm according to ASTM standard E8M.
The specimens were machined from the extruded rods
with the tensile axis lying along the extrusion direction.
The tensile and creep samples were heat-treated at
1100 8C for 2 h and air-cooled (AC) to room tempera-
ture. In addition, alloy 787 was heat-treated at 1300 8C
for 2 h and AC. Tensile specimens were tested in the
oxidized state, since the oxide layer is beneficial in
minimizing the en vironmental embrittlement due to
hydrogen. The tensile tests were carried out on an
MTS 810 machine in the temperature range 20 /C1
/800 8C
at a nominal strain rate of 5 /C29/10/C284 s/C281. Three samples
were tested at each test temperature to obtain reliable
tensile results. The specimens were heated to the

furnace and soaked for 15 min prior to testing at high
temperatures with an accuracy of 9
/2 8C. The load vs.
cross-head displacements were digitally acquired to
obtain 0.2% yield stress, ultimate tensile strength, and

required for replacement of superalloys at operating
temperatures at least up to 700 8C.
Our tensile and creep results of FeAl alloys as well as
Reimann and Sauthoff’s [43] suggest that retained
vacancies formed due to high-temperature annealing
and air-cooling are effecti ve in impro ving the high-
temperature strength (at least up to 800 8C), and the
creep resistance (at least up to 700 8C) of FeAl alloys.
The improvement in the high-temperature strength and
the creep resistance of FeAl due to retained vacancies is
found to increase with carbon content [37]. There are
opportunities to further impro ve the high-temperature
strength of FeAl by combining the vacancy hardening
with other conventional strengthening mechanisms such
as solid solution and precipitation hardening. For
example, high-temperature strength and the creep re-
sistance of FeAl can be further impro ved by combining
strengthening due to solid solution hardening (by
varying the amount of titanium, molybdenum and
zirconium additions), with precipitation and vacancy-
hardening (by optimizing the carbon content and heat
treatment). A more detailed account on the effect of
aluminum content and transition alloying additions on
the tensile and creep properties, and their implication on
alloy design will be presented soon [21,44].
4. Summary
Two FeAl alloys, strengthened by solid solution
(molybdenum, zirconium and titanium) and precipita-
tion (complex carbides) hardening mechanisms, are
characterized for their high-temperature strength and
creep properties. Interestingly, both the alloys show
significant impro vement in strength and creep resis-
tance. High-temperature mechanical properties, namely
the yield strength and the creep resistance of the present
FeAl alloys meet or exceed many of the con ventional
Fe-based alloys. The abo ve alloys ha ve the potential to
serve at operating temperatures of up to 700 8C.
