ABSTRACT 
The molybdenum ‑ titanium ‑ zirconium ‑ carbon alloy TZM is one of the few molybdenum alloys that can be pro -
cessed crack-free in laser powder bed fusion (LPBF). However, the parts have a coarse-grained, columnar 
microstructure comprising epitaxially grown grains, and their strength is limited by residual porosity. Conse -
quently, the mechanical properties do not reach the values of their conventionally powder-metallurgically 
produced pendants. In this work, the conventional alloy composition of TZM was adapted to the unique pro -
cessing conditions in laser powder bed fusion by increasing the carbon content, and the effects on the micro -
structure and mechanical properties were investigated. 
Increasing the carbon content to 2.3 at.% yielded a significantly refined and isotropic microstructure. In 
addition, C led to the formation of a cellular subgrain structure consisting of (Mo, Ti) cells 0.4 ± 0.1 μ m in size 
surrounded by a closed network of ternary (Mo, Ti) carbide. Residual oxygen impurities in C-modified TZM were 
partly dissolved in the ternary (Mo, Ti) carbide network and partly bound by Zr as nanometer-sized ZrO
2 
par -
ticles. These two mechanisms for binding oxygen minimized oxygen segregation — a major issue limiting the 
grain boundary strength in Mo and Mo alloys — and thus purified and strengthened the grain boundaries. The 
mechanical strength increase due to the increase in carbon content was 50% (0.5 at.% C vs 2.3 at.% C). At 
elevated test temperatures of 800 
◦
C and 1200 
◦
C, TZM-2.3 at.% C exceeded the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) 
of conventionally produced TZM by 24% and 16%, respectively. 
1. Introduction 
Titanium ‑ zirconium ‑ molybdenum (TZM) is a molybdenum alloy 
that is optimized for high-temperature applications and is produced 
mainly by powder metallurgy. Addition of 0.5 wt% titanium, 0.08 wt% 
zirconium and typically 0.01 – 0.04 wt% carbon ( ≙ 1 at.% Ti, 0.084 at.% 
Zr and 0.008 – 0.032 at.% C) can produce materials in which most of the 
titanium is dissolved in the molybdenum (Mo) matrix, and a high 
number of fine carbides — i.e., molybdenum, titanium and ternary (Mo, 
Ti) carbides — and zirconium oxide ZrO
2 
are homogeneously distributed 
mainly on the grain and sub-grain boundaries [ 1 – 3 ]. At elevated tem -
peratures, the carbide and oxide particles inhibit grain growth and 
improve creep resistance [ 1 , 3 , 4 ]. The applications of TZM components 
include forging tools, rotating anodes in X-ray tubes, radiation shields, 
extrusion dies and boring bars [ 3 , 5 ]. 
Additive manufacturing through laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 
provides an alternative to the established powder metallurgical (PM) 
manufacturing route for TZM components. LPBF offers higher flexibility 
in shaping and thus component functionalization. Solidification during 
LPBF occurs with a steep temperature gradient G = 10
3
– 10
6 
K/m, a high 
cooling rate of 10
3

6 
K/s and a high solidification rate, R, which is 
close to the typical scan speed of ~10
3 
mm/s [ 6 , 7 ]. Microstructural 
development mechanisms in LPBF are fundamentally different from 
those in PM manufacturing. The processing of molybdenum and mo -
lybdenum alloys in LPBF is a particular challenge, because they are 
prone to intergranular cracking, owing to the processing conditions in 
LPBF and the low fracture toughness of molybdenum. This low fracture 
toughness has its origin in the limited mobility of the dislocations, owing 
to the high share of covalent bonds and the resultant high stacking fault 
energy [ 8 , 9 ], and by segregated oxygen impurities that significantly

The author ’ s previous work [ 12 ] has shown that LPBF TZM can be 
produced crack-free with high density. The microstructure of LPBF TZM 
shows molybdenum grains with titanium in solid solution, as well as Mo 
carbides and ternary (Mo, Ti) carbides finely distributed in the grain 
interior. In LPBF TZM, in contrast to PM TZM, the grain boundaries are 
free of particles and segregations. Oxygen impurities are bound as ZrO
2 
and are found dissolved in ternary (Mo, Ti) carbides. The grain boundary 
strength is therefore not adversely affected, and transgranular fracture 
behavior at room temperature and enhanced strength are observed. 
However, regarding the microstructure in LPBF TZM, an insufficient 
quantity of a grain-refining alloy component during additive processing 
yields a coarse-grained, textured microstructure with epitaxially grown 
grains in the building direction [ 12 ], which is similar to that observed in 
pure molybdenum [ 13 – 15 ]. This microstructure leads to anisotropic 
component properties, which limit flexibility in shaping and the po -
tential degree of functionalization for specific applications. Beyond 
anisotropy, a coarse-grained microstructure in molybdenum results in 
lower yield strength, ductility and fracture toughness at room temper -
ature than those in fine-grained molybdenum. 
Various alloying strategies for grain refinement in additive 
manufacturing of molybdenum have been discussed [ 16 – 21 ], and the 
addition of carbon appears to be the most effective strategy in this 
respect. Carbon triggers a lag in the growth rate during solidification, 
owing to solute rejection at the solid/liquid interface, thereby increasing 
the thermal undercooling of the melt and resulting in new grain for -
mation [ 18 ]. Braun et al. [ 18 ] showed that the grain size and aspect ratio 
decrease significantly with increasing carbon content, and crack sus -
ceptibility and porosity simultaneously decrease. Beyond microstruc -
tural refinement and defect suppression, increased carbon content might 
have an additional beneficial effect for TZM because of the high volume 
fraction of carbides that are formed. Carbides retard the grain boundary 
migration at elevated temperatures and increase the high-temperature 
strength of molybdenum and molybdenum alloys. 
The aim of this work was to investigate the effect of increased carbon 
content in LPBF manufactured TZM on the microstructure and the me -
chanical properties at room temperature and elevated temperatures. 
2. Experimental procedure 
This study used two spherical, pre-alloyed, powders ( Fig. 1 ), with 
modification of the nominal alloying element content of TZM of 0.5 wt% 
Ti, 0.08 wt% Zr and typically 0.01 – 0.04 wt% C ( ≙ 1 at.% Ti, 0.084 at.% 
Zr, 0.008 – 0.032 at.% C) as follows. Powder 1 contained a slightly higher 
carbon content of 0.5 at.% and is denoted TZM-0.5 at.% C. Powder 2 
contained a substantially higher carbon content of 2.3 at.% and is 
denoted TZM-2.3 at.% C. Both had a metallic purity of > 99.9%, were 
produced by gas atomization and were subsequently fractioned by 
sieving with a mesh size of 63 μ m. The physical powder properties were 
characterized with a Malvern Mastersizer 3000 laser diffraction particle 
size analyzer and a measuring funnel according to ASTM B527 for tap 
and bulk density. The chemical properties of both powders and all LPBF 
manufactured specimens were characterized with a LECO CS - 230 in -
strument (combustion method) for the carbon; a LECO TC - 500 in -
strument (carrier gas hot extraction) for oxygen content, hydrogen and 
nitrogen content; and a Thermo Scientific ICAP 600 Duo inductively 
coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer for titanium and zirco -
nium content. Table 1 shows the physical (see also Fig. 1 c) and chemical 
properties of both powders. Both powders were processed by LPBF to 
produce specimens of which microstructure (scanning electron micro -
scopy (SEM) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analyses) and 
bending strength were determined. On the basis of the data, TZM-2.3 at. 
% C was selected to investigate the microstructure at high resolution and 
to build tensile bar specimens in different orientations for high- 
temperature tensile tests. 
LPBF manufacturing of specimens was performed using an Aconi -
tyLAB equipped with a 400 W, 1064 nm Yb:YAG fiber laser and optional 
substrate plate heating. The oxygen level in the building chamber was 
kept below 20 ppmv for all experiments by using argon shielding gas. All 
specimens were manufactured on a pure molybdenum substrate plate. 
Optimized LPBF process parameters for specimens with the highest 
density were determined in a pilot study. The linear energy density 
(LED) was calculated as LED = P/v, where P is laser power, and v is 
scanning speed. The substrate plate was kept at a temperature of 800 
◦
C 
during the building process. The scanning strategy details are presented 
in Table 2 . For both alloys, cubic specimens of 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm 
were manufactured and used for density measurements and micro -
structural characterization, and cuboid specimens of 35 mm × 6 mm ×
6 mm (length × width × height, height parallel to the LPBF building 
direction) were manufactured and used for bending strength tests. 
Furthermore, for TZM-2.3 at.% C, cylindrical specimens with Ø 10 mm 
and 60 mm height for tensile testing were fabricated in vertical (length 
parallel to the LPBF building direction) and horizontal (length perpen -
dicular to the LPBF building direction) orientation in the same manner.

setting the substrate plate temperature to 1200

3. Results

the carbon content of TZM significantly affected the grain morphology, 
size and texture, and consequently the mechanical properties. Compared 
with 0.1 at.% in [ 12 ] and 0.5 at.%, a carbon content of 2.3 at.% 
decreased the grain size by a factor of 2 and decreased grain elongation 
along the building direction. The grain size decrease was accompanied 
by a significant decrease in the texture prevalence and a change in the 
preferred direction, from 4.3 in the < 001 > direction for TZM-0.5 at.% C 
to 1.8 for < 111 > direction for TZM-2.3 at.% C. 
A decrease in texture prevalence accompanied by grain refinement 
with increasing C content has also been observed in a prior study [ 18 ] in 
a Mo
–
C alloy. The grain refining effect of C in Mo has been investigated 
in [ 17 , 18 , 22 ] and was attributed to increased thermal supercooling at 
the solid/liquid interface, owing to an increased lag of the S/L interface 
to the isothermal of the solidus temperature caused by the solute 
rejection effect of carbon [ 18 , 23 , 24 ]. The degree of grain refinement 
observed in this study was higher than that reported for pure molyb -
denum alloyed with carbon [ 18 ]. This difference was due to 1 at.% ti -
tanium and 0.1 at.% zirconium in the base alloy TZM used herein. 
Zirconium is likely to contribute to grain refinement in a similar manner 
to carbon through the effect of solute rejection and the associated in -
crease in thermal supercooling. In the case of titanium, a substantial 
solute rejection effect is unlikely, because titanium is fully soluble in the 
molybdenum solid. Nevertheless, alloying elements with full solubility 
in the base element also have a mild grain refining effect, as reported for 
tantalum in molybdenum [ 19 ] or tantalum in tungsten [ 25 ]. In the 
present case, titanium might have contributed to the observed grain 
refinement with a twofold effect. First, titanium decreases the diffusion 
rate of molybdenum, thereby increasing the lag of the S/L interface to 
the isothermal of the solidus temperature leading to higher thermal 
supercooling [ 23 , 24 ]. Second, the presence of a sufficient amount of Ti

The microstructure in TZM-2.3 at.% C consists of fine grains with a 
cellular substructure and therefore substantially differs from that of non- 
modified TZM, in which no sub-grain structure is observed [ 12 ]. 
Elemental analyses revealed cells rich in molybdenum and titanium; an 
intercellular phase rich in Mo, Ti, C and O; and nanometer-sized parti -
cles rich in Zr and O located at or near the interface. The phases found in 
the present study are analogous to those of unmodified TZM [ 12 ], with 
clear differences in the phase proportions. The cells in TZM-2.3 at.% C 
consisted of molybdenum with titanium in solid solution surrounded by 
intercellular ternary (Mo, Ti) carbides containing dissolved oxygen, with 
nanometer-sized ZrO
2 
particles located in the interface region between 
carbide and cells. 
No segregated oxygen impurities were found on grain boundaries. 
This finding is of utmost importance and is a basic prerequisite for 
meaningful investigation of the mechanical properties of these mate -
rials, because grain boundary strength-reducing segregated oxygen im -
purities are a major cause of crack formation in LPBF [ 10 ]. Two 
mechanisms ensure that the grain boundaries remain free of oxygen: the 
formation of ZrO
2 
nanoparticles and the dissolution of oxygen in the 
ternary (Mo, Ti) carbide. The effect of oxygen dissolution in ortho -
rhombic Mo
2
C and its effectiveness in grain boundary purification were 
first described in reference [ 22 ] for Mo
–
C, and have been found to also 
apply to ternary (Mo, Ti) carbides of TZM [ 12 ]. This effect can be 
optimally exploited with TZM-2.3 at.% C, because the ternary carbide 
network is narrow and closed (mean cell size 0.4 ± 0.1 μ m), thereby 
minimizing the diffusion length for oxygen impurities. 
The results of the bending strength test in Fig. 3 b indicated that 
additional carbon content positively influenced the mechanical 
strength. Increasing the carbon content from 0.5 at.% to 2.3 at.% led to a 
50% increase in bending strength, from 666 ± 360 MPa to 998 ± 287 
MPa. Comparison with the results for standard TZM with 0.1 at.% C of 
490 ± 170 MPa [ 12 ] confirmed this trend. The main reasons for this 
finding were likely to be the increased grain refinement with increasing 
carbon content, as well as the efficient grain boundary purification by 
the narrow, closed network of oxygen-gettering ternary (Mo, Ti) car -
bides. It should be noted that the standard deviations of the bending test 
results for TZM-0.5 at.% C and TZM-2.3 at.% C are relatively large, 
despite the number of specimens of N = 7. The same was found in [ 12 ] 
for unmodified LPBF TZM and was attributed to the fact that the residual 
pores present in the specimens have a critical size for the room tem -
perature bending test and thus their occurrence and position in the 
tested volume decisively influence the measured strength. It can there -
fore be assumed that this effect is also responsible for the large deviation 
of bending strength values in TZM-0.5 at.% C and TZM-2.3 at.% C. 
The tensile test results in Fig. 7 a and b, in addition to the three-point 
bending test results, yielded the following conclusions. First, the results 
of vertically and horizontally oriented samples showed only minor dif -
ferences, thus revealing that the fine-grained, non-textured TZM-2.3 at. 
% C samples had nearly isotropic tensile behavior. Second, these small 
variations in strength are pronounced differently at different test tem -
peratures. These variations were greater for TZM-2.3 at.% C at 23 
◦
C, 
which is below the DBTT, as manifested by fracture in the elastic range. 
Pores exceeding a critical size have a fracture-inducing notch effect, 
owing to the low fracture toughness of TZM [ 12 ] and the here investi -
gated C-modified TZM at this temperature. In addition to pore size, pore 
shape influences the notch effect. Pores elongated transverse to the test 
direction, such as the lack of fusion pores in Fig. 2 , are particularly 
disadvantageous in vertically oriented specimens and may explain the 
lower tensile strength of vertically than horizontally oriented samples.

• With unmodified TZM and TZM-0.5 at.% C, epitaxial grain growth
