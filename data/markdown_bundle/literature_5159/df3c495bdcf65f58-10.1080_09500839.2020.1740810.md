ABSTRACT
A lightweight high-entropy Al 24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 alloy
has been e ﬀectively developed by sonication-assisted
physical blending of micro-nanometre size particles
followed by high-temperature sintering. A blending process
homogenised the elemental micro-nanometre particles,
which were well bonded by a solid-state di ﬀusion process,
and these developed into multicomponent intermetallic
compounds in the sintered alloy microstructure. The
resulting Al
24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 alloy a very high strength
measured in terms of hardness compared to the value
obtained by compression testing.

1. Introduction
Conceived just barely more than a decade ago [1,2], multi-element metallic alloys
have attracted considerable attention owing to their unique microstructural fea-
tures associated with exceptional properties compared to the those of traditional
alloys containing a single principal element [3–9]. The alloys are generally known
as high-entropy alloys (HEAs), baseless alloys, concentrated solid-solution
alloys, multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs), or compositionally complex
alloys. They are characterised by high entropy, severe lattice distortion, very slug-
gish di ﬀusion and the so-called cocktail e ﬀect [ 10]. Complex and concentrated
phases in high-entropy alloys experience severe lattice distortion, more severe
than that experienced by single-element-dominated traditional alloys, owing to
the di ﬀerent atomic size of the constituent elements. Lattice distortion induces
uncertainty in the atomic lattice position leading to excessive con ﬁgurational
entropy [ 4,11]. This results in a reduction in X-ray peak intensities during
phase identiﬁcation, improvement in mechanical properties, and reductions in
thermal and electrical conductivities. Owing to the complex nature of the

atomic arrangements inside high-entropy alloys, the activation energy for substi-

diﬀusion, which enables the formation of nanocrystals in the cast [ 14] and
annealed [ 15] high-entropy alloys. The presence of unusual compositions is
allowed under the classic Hume-Rothary rule. The consequential microstructure
in high-entropy alloys has a synergistic e ﬀect on the resulting properties, as evi-
denced by their unpredictable and extraordinary behaviour that has been
reported. The majority of high-entropy alloys are usually processed [ 16] using
arc melting and the powder metallurgy route. There is a recent comprehensive
review by Miracle and Senkov [ 5] of high-entropy alloys, which are dominated
by transition metals (e.g. superalloys), refractory metal alloys and very few
light-metal alloys. Compared to the development of superalloys and refractory
alloys, there has been very little focus on the development of lightweight
alloys. Mechanical alloying, a common powder-metallurgy route, involves
repeated cold welding, fracturing and re-cold welding, followed by compression
and sintering of the blended elements or pre-alloyed metal powders to produce
equilibrium and non-equilibrium alloys [ 17,18]. Despite having a relatively
higher degree of porosity, the mechanical alloyed powder metallurgy processed
can contain either a homogeneous or bimodal distribution of alloying elements
with superior mechanical properties [ 18,19]. However, the advent of economical
mass production technology for nanometre-size metal powder has brought a new
perspective in alloy powder processing that excludes mechanical alloying [ 20].
Considering the minimally explored lightweight high-entropy alloy develop-
ment, the current work is aimed at the development of high-entropy alloys in the
ﬁeld of widely used aluminium-silicon alloys. The study explores the develop-
ment of powder-processed equiatomic aluminium-silicon-copper-titanium-
nickel high-entropy alloys using a blend-press-sinter powder metallurgy proces-
sing route. The focus is to study the e ﬀect of processing parameters on the evol-
ution of microstructures and the resulting mechanical properties of the
developed high-entropy alloys.
2. Materials and methods
2.1 . Materials and processing
In this study, pure aluminium particles (30 μm size with 99.5+% purity) were
blended with copper (25 nm size with 99.8% purity), silicon (100 nm with
99% purity), titanium (40 –60 nm size with 99.9+% purity) and nickel (20 nm
size with 99.9+% purity) particles. Aluminium was procured from Alpha Chemi-
cals, USA, and the copper, silicon, titanium and nickel particles were procured
from Skyspring Nanomaterials Inc., USA.
Equiatomic amounts of aluminium, copper, titanium and nickel, with a 3.25
atomic percentage of silicon particles (Al
24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2), were added

together in acetone containedin a glass beaker. A physical blending of the met-
allic elements was done using a probe sonicator (VC-50, Sonics) for ten minutes.
The physically blended high-entropy alloy suspension was kept in an oven at 60°
C for 12 h to evaporate the suspension media. This was followed by subsequent
compaction into preforms of 10 mm height and 10 mm diameter, at a pressure
of 450 MPa using a uniaxial hydraulic press. The preforms were wrapped in alu-
minium foil and subjected to a two-step sintering process in an inert argon
atmosphere in a resistance heated tube furnace (MTI GSL-1700X, MTI corpor-
ation, USA). During the sintering process of the Al
24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2
compact, the ﬁrst step was at 500°C for 4.5 h followed by a second step at
600°C for another 4.5 h, followed by furnace cooling.
2.2. Microstructural characterisation
The microstructure of a sintered Al 24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 alloy was investi-
gated to study the homogeneity in composition of distribution patterns in
solid-state solutions and intermetallic compounds. A ﬁeld-emission scanning
electron microscope (FESEM) (Quanta 250 FEG, FEI, Czech Republic) equipped
with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) was used for this purpose.
A sintered Al
24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 alloy was exposed to Cu K α radiation
(λ =1 .5418 Å) in an automated X-ray di ﬀractometer (Bruker-AXS D8
Advance −40kv/40Ma) to identify the phases evolved in the non-equilibrium
high-entropy alloy. The specimen was scanned at a rate of 0.02° s −1 between
the di ﬀraction angles of 20° to 100°. Bragg angles and the corresponding
inter-planer spacing from the X-ray di ﬀraction spectrum were matched with
the standard values reported in the JCPDS card [ 21] to identify the phases,
including aluminium, silicon, copper, titanium, nickel and any other possible
intermetallics that could have formed from these elements in the alloy. X-ray
diﬀraction was conducted on an unsintered Al
24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2
compact for reference purposes.
Room-temperature DC electrical conductivity of the sintered Al24.2Si3.2Cu24.2-
Ti24.2Ni24.2 alloy was measured to understand the extent of the intermetallic for-
mation by using a four-in-line probe DC electrical conductivity measuring
instrument (Scienti ﬁc Equipments, India) using methodology described else-
where [ 22]. The DC electrical conductivity of an unsintered Al
24.2Si3.2Cu24.2-
Ti24.2Ni24.2 compact was measured for reference purposes.
2.3. Mechanical characterisation
The mechanical properties of the Al 24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 alloy were evalu-
ated in terms of microhardness and compressive behaviour. Hardness values
were measured on metallurgically polished samples using a load of 0.3 kg and
a dwell time of 15 s on a Beuhler MMT-3 automatic digital microhardness

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Macrostructural characteristics
The macrostructural characterisation of an ultrasonically blended non-equili-
brium Al 24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 alloy in a compact and sintered form did
not reveal any apparent physical defects, measured in terms of dimensional
deformation, cracks and visible oxidation marks. There was no sign of a diﬀ u-
sional bond between the aluminium foil and the alloy during the sintering
process.
3.2. Microstructural characteristics
The microstructural characterisation of the sintered Al 24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2
alloy revealed that (see Figure 1) the micrometer-size aluminium and the nano-
metre-size silicon, copper, titanium and nickel elemental particles were dis-
persed quite homogeneously am during the sonication-assisted blending
process. However, the presence of some large patches of distinctive copper
and nickel particle clusters suggests that the blending parameters, speci ﬁcally
the blending duration, used in this study were apparently inadequate to break
a few of the initial copper and nickel nanoparticles clusters. It has to be men-
tioned that the nanoparticles have a relatively higher tendency for agglomeration
[23–25].
According to the Hume-Rothary rule, the mutual solubility among the binary
formation of the added elements is extremely limited, except in the case of
copper and nickel [ 26] at the processing temperature used in this study. In
addition, the homogeneous distribution pattern of the elemental nanoparticles
in the blended Al
24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 alloy compact was not present in
the typical diﬀusion-couple form during the sintering process. Hence, the sinter-
ing process resulted in the formation of multicomponent intermetallics in the
evolved microstructure, instead of a solid solution, as can be seen in the X-ray
diﬀraction spectrum (see Figure 2). Interestingly, the 2 θ position for the XRD
peaks identi ﬁed in the case of the sintered alloy coincided with the 2 θ position
for elements identiﬁed in the unsintered compact. This was apparently

3.3. Mechanical characteristics
Microhardness measurement on the sintered Al 24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 alloy
revealed a signi ﬁcantly large value of 1118 ± 132 MPa. This high hardness
value could be attributed to the formation of multicomponent intermetallics
in the sintered microstructure, as intermetallic compounds are typically much
stronger than their elemental counterparts even when in solid-solution form.
The relatively large standard deviation in the hardness values measured diagon-
ally across the polished surface (see Figure 4a) apparently arises from the pres-
ence of inhomogeneity in the microstructure, with the presence of metal element
clusters, as shown in Figure 1a. The variation could have also been a ﬀected by
the presence of porosity, which is unavoidable in powder-processed sintered
structures [27]. The results of the compression failure test of the sintered Al

4. Conclusions
In conclusion, the traditional physical blending of micro-nanometre size par-
ticles using sonication, followed by sintering, is e ﬀective in processing light-
weight Al
24.2Si3.2Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 high-entropy alloys. Solid-state di ﬀusion in
the well-dispersed non-equilibrium elemental mixture led to the development
of strong bonding among the added particles and the formation of intermetallic
compounds. The powder-processed lightweight non-equilibrium Al
24.2Si3.2-
Cu24.2Ti24.2Ni24.2 high-entropy alloys with multicomponent intermetallic com-
pounds achieved a very high degree of strength measured in terms of
hardness, which was relatively low when measured using a compression test.
