1. Introduction 
The utilization of high-performance lightweight aluminum alloys 
plays an integral role in the current industrial material selection for the 
aerospace and transportation industries. ( Drits and Vovnyanko, 2010 ). 
Traditional manufacturing methods have limitations in forming light -
weight and complex precision components due to processing difficulties, 
time constraints, and high costs. In contrast, laser additive 
manufacturing (AM) offers significant processing advantages ( Tempel -
man et al., 2014 ). In addition to the advantages in complex shape 
forming of AM, the higher solidification rate (10
3

8 
K/s) during laser 
AM solidification can effectively refine the grains and improve the solid 
solubility of elements, enabling higher mechanical properties of AM 
sample ( Niu et al., 2023 ), which has great potential in lightweight ap -
plications of Al alloys. Direct metal deposition (DED) is a 
near-net-shaping technology based on a powder-fed AM process with 
free-moving powder nozzles and a relatively large forming chamber, 
enabling the effective manufacture of parts with complex shapes and 
large dimensions ( Mazumder et al., 1999 ). At present, DED technology is 
mainly used in forming stainless steel and titanium alloy components. 
The application of aluminum alloy in DED is limited by the high laser 
reflectivity, easy oxidation and high thermal conductivity of Al alloy. As 
a result, the development of new systems for AM Al alloys is regarded as 
necessary, especially the heat-resistant Al alloys. Although the tradi -
tional AM 4xxx and 7xxx aluminum alloys can achieve the evaluation 
standard of "light weight & high strength" at ambient temperature ser -
vice, it is difficult to continue this superiority above 200 ℃ ( Jia et al., 
2019 ). Hence, the development of heat-resistant Al alloys corresponding 
to high-temperature service conditions is necessary. 
The design principle of heat-resistant aluminum alloys follows a 
simple idea: introduce a high melting point phase to ensure that enough 
heat-resistant phases are available to strengthen the alloy at high tem -
peratures. Several researchers have investigated entirely new AM Al 
alloys systems with this goal. These alloys are based primarily on near- 
eutectic system, including Al-Mn ( Geng et al., 2022 ), Al-Fe ( Wang et al., 
2020 ), Al-Cu ( Schuster et al., 2021 ), Al-Ni ( Ding et al., 2023 ), and 
Al-RE-TM, where RE is a rare earth metal and TM is a transition metal. 
Cr is a promising TM element that can be used as a dispersion forming 
agent for grain refinement in Al alloys ( Mikhailovskaya et al., 2014 ).

3. Results 
3.1. Single track morphologies 
Fig. 2 displays the cross-sectional morphologies corresponding to 20 
single tracks, which were deposited on a 5083 Al-alloy substrate. The 
horizontal coordinates represent laser power, while the vertical co -
ordinates represent scanning speeds with all other parameters fixed as 
constants. The deposited layers are all relatively dense and crack-free. 
The fusion line between the deposited layer and the base material is 
curved and shows good metallurgical characteristics. 
The size of the deposited layer grows as the laser power increases, 
and the influence of laser power on the thickness is greater. This is 
because the width of a single deposit depends mainly on the size of the 
molten pool. With the increase of laser power, the laser energy acting on 
the substrate and the range of molten pools generated also increase. The 
greater the area of the melt pool, the more powder that can penetrate the 
interior of the melt pool, increasing the height of the deposited layer. 
The change of laser power not only affects the size of the deposited layer, 
but also affects the interaction between the laser spot and the powder 
and substrate. When the laser power is less than 1200 W, the heat input 
and energy density of the laser are low, resulting in a small or non- 
existent deposition pool. As the laser density increases, the number 
and size of pores in the sediment also increase. Due to the increased laser 
energy density, there is an enhanced volatilization of Mn elements in the 
alloy powder, which prevents gas from escaping in time to form pores 
inside the melt pool. 
During the DED processing, the scanning speed primarily influences 
the energy input from the spot to the melt pool, the heating time, and the 
powder utilization ( Li et al., 2022 ). In general, the faster the scanning 
speed, the shorter the laser heating period on the powder and the sub -
strate, and the lower the powder utilization. Conversely, slowing down 
the scanning speed increases the powder utilization. The shorter the 
heating time, the lower the powder usage; conversely, reducing the

With the increase of scanning speed, the height and width of the 
sedimentary layer decreased simultaneously, but the size of the sedi -
mentary layer stabilized when the scanning speed reached 800 mm/ 
min. This is due to the fact that the effect of changing the scanning speed 
on the melting pool is mostly reflected in two aspects during the laser 
additive manufacturing process. First, when the scanning speed in -
creases, the substrate is impacted by the laser heat source in unit time, 
and the heating area reduces, resulting in a smaller melting pool. Sec -
ond, when the scanning speed increases, the amount of powder entering 
the molten tank per unit time drops, resulting in a decrease in deposition 
layer height. 
The non-smooth morphology of the melt pool at power below 
1600 W due to droplet splashing. The collapse of the cavity near the 
bottom of the molten pool causes particles to erupt from the surface, 
resulting in a splash. Typically, sputtering occurs due to the presence of a 
large pressure gradient or recoil pressure caused by a vapor plasma 
plume, helping to stabilize the melt pool during the DED process, but 
resulting in the formation of surface defects or an increase in surface 
roughness. 
In order to assess the processability of single tracks, the cross- 
sectional dimensions (aspect ratio, dilution rate), cross-sectional 
morphology, surface morphology and defects are usually used as 
criteria. The aspect ratio is B/H and the dilution rate is h/(h + H) of a 
single melt pool, where H is the height, B the width and h the depth. 
Fig. 3 shows the aspect ratio and dilution rate of single tracks processed 
at various parameters. Previous studies have shown that the aspect ratio 
reflects the strength of the metallurgical combination between the 
deposited layer and substrate, as well as the flatness of the scan channel 
during deposition ( K ¨ohler et al., 2021 ). Besides, dilution rate is an 
important index to evaluate the influence of parameters on the inherent 
properties of the deposited layer and the strength of the metallurgical 
combination between the deposited layer and the substrate ( Dutta, 
2022 ). Combined with the single-pass light microscopic morphology, it 
is concluded that the deposition layer is better formed when B/H ≥ 6 and 
0.4 < the dilution rate < 0.6. The forming parameters, laser powder 
1600 W and scanning speed 600 mm/min, are selected by the optical 
microscopy images to deposit large-size samples. 
3.2. As-fabricated microstructures 
In Fig. 4 , the cross-sectional microstructure characteristics of the 
DEDed Al-Cr-Sc-Zr samples are presented. Good processability can be 
confirmed from the optical microscopy (OM) image of single-pass multi- 
layer structure in Fig. 4 a. In addition, the OM image and electron 
channeling contrast image (ECCI) display the microstructure features of 
the DEDed block sample deposited at optimal parameters ( Fig. 4 b, c), 
demonstrating the absence of solidification cracks or other metallurgical 
defects. Apparently, the microstructure is heterogeneous and consists of 
two different morphological distributions, whose arrangement reflects 
the geometry of the melt pool. A large number of coarsened and short 
second phase particles are present in the melt pool boundary (MPB), 
with a rosette morphology ranging from approximately 5 to 10 µ m in 
diameter. While elongated particles are present inside melt pool, with an 
acicular structure ranging from approximately 10 to 25 µ m in length. 
Unlike the reported equiaxed-columnar bimodal grain structure of a 
DEDed Al-Mg alloy, a fully equiaxed grain structure was obtained in the 
DEDed Al-Cr-Sc-Zr alloy, as confirmed by the EBSD inverse pole figure 
(IPF) in Fig. 4 d. 
The EBSD map implied in Fig. 5 a reveals homogeneous grain size of 
the DEDed sample. Further EBSD statistic studies found that most grains 
have a size in the range of 6 – 10 µ m, with the average grain size of 7 µ m 
( Fig. 5 b). The distribution of grain boundary orientation angles reveals 
that the samples do not possess a specific orientation. As shown in 
Fig. 5 c, most of orientation angles are more than 15
◦
, proving the ex -
istence of high-angle grain boundaries. It is believed the high-angle 
boundaries can offer more nucleation sites for the second phase. The 
{001} {101} and {111} pole figure indicated the these equiaxed grains 
have a random grain orientation, with a maximum value of 1.122. 
The ECCI image ( Fig. 6 a) displays the periodic band structure 
stacked layer by layer. The distinctive structure is composed of alter -
nating rosette-shaped and acicular particles along the building direction, 
creating a contrast between dark and bright regions. The morphological 
features of the precipitated particles allow for identification of the melt 
pool inner and melt pool boundary as separate entities, although the 
boundary between them is indistinct. It becomes apparent that slender 
particles are formed inside the melt pool, while coarsened particles are 
formed at the melt pool boundary. This is the consequence of the layer- 
by-layer nature for DED deposition. When printing the next layer, the 
upper boundary of the melt pool is re-heated, while the short heating 
process leads to coarsening of the primary particles distributed at this 
boundary. In addition, due to the low growth rate at the melt pool 
boundary, the primary particles have sufficient time to grow and 
coarsen. In contrast, the cooling rate inside the melt pool is far faster and

4.4. Future design strategies for heat-resistant AM Al alloys 
Traditional heat-resistant aluminum alloys are designed with a 
composition (Cu/Ni/Si/Fe) and microalloying elements (Zr/Ag/V/Sc) 
to enhance high-temperature strength ( Xue et al., 2023 ). However, the 
improvement of high-temperature performance faces several challenges, 
primarily attributed to three common issues: instability of the reinforced 
phase at elevated temperatures, lack of controllability in heat-resistant 
phase configuration, and mismatch between the heat-resistant phase 
and matrix. For example, the θ phase undergoes severe coarsening at 
high temperatures after aging strengthening in Al-Cu alloys, resulting in 
insufficient thermal stability ( Andre et al., 2021 ). Meanwhile, the δ 
phase is randomly distributed in the Al-Si system and does not match 
with the matrix as a heat-resistant phase, leading to an inability to 
maintain high-temperature strength ( Gao et al., 2020 ). To design 
heat-resistant aluminum alloys with high reliability, three design prin -
ciples are proposed here: ① generation of nanoscale heat-resistant par -
ticles; ② controllable heat-resistant phase configuration; ③ refinement of 
the matrix grain. 
The formation of thermally stable nanoprecipitates within the matrix 
is a fundamental aspect in the compositional design of high heat- 
resistance Al alloy. Nano-precipitated phases offer even greater advan -
tages in the microstructure design of aluminum-based alloys. On one 
hand, a smaller loss in plastic toughness can be traded for a more sig -
nificant precipitation strengthening effect. On the other hand, the in -
dustry has developed mature ideas for constructing microstructures. The 
dispersed nanoprecipitates, with interparticle spacing ranging from a 
few to tens of nanometers, will exert a strong dislocation pinning effect 
through either the shear mechanism ( Smallman and Ngan, 1963 ) or 
Orowan mechanism ( Zhu and Starke, 1999 ), thereby significantly 
enhancing the strength of alloys. Clearly, assuming the same size dis -
tribution, an increased volume fraction of precipitated phase can yield a 
more pronounced precipitation strengthening effect. The same principle 
applies to a more finely dispersed and diffuse distribution of precipitated 
phases with the same volume fraction, which contributes to a greater 
strengthening effect. This design principle also holds true for 
high-strength aluminum alloys, but even high strength cannot 
completely compensate for their weakness in terms of heat resistance. 
The most crucial approach to achieving this objective is to minimize the 
significant reduction in the volume fraction of precipitated phases at 
high temperatures (dissolution) and the rapid increase in phase spacing 
(roughening). Therefore, for "heat-resistant Al alloys" where precipita -
tion enhancement is a fundamental consideration, the corresponding 
strategy is to stabilize precipitated phases, which serves as the focal 
point of this paper. 
For controlling the heat-resistant configuration of particles, several 
strategies can be employed, including solute bias at the precipitated 
phase interface, co-lattice interlocking at the interface of multilayered 
tissues, high-concentration vacancy compositing, and interstitial 
ordering of precipitated phases. Ostwald (1901) Ripening is the primary 
mechanism for microstructure destabilization at high temperatures. The 
interfacial solute atom bias can significantly impede precipitated pha -
se/matrix interface migration and enhance microstructure stability, 
thereby enabling aluminum alloy materials to overcome their original 
service temperature limitations. Prof. Schuh ’ s team conducted a 
comprehensive review and analysis of the potential trends in interfacial 
polymerization for transformational applications ( Chookajorn et al., 
2012 ). Pursuing strong interfacial coalescence is a worthwhile

ultra-high thermal stability, interfacial cohesion itself is the optimal 
means of stabilizing interfacial cohesion. Recently, researchers have
