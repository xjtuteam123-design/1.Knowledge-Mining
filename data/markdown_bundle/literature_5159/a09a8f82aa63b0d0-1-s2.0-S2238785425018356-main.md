ABSTRACT
TiAl alloy, as known for its low density and high-temperature strength, contributes significantly to enhance the 
thrust-to-weight ratio and improve fuel economy. However, its broader application is limited by insufficient 
strength, creep resistance and oxidation resistance at elevated temperatures. Ceramic particles, leveraging their 
inherent high melting points, exceptional hardness, and superior Young ’ s modulus, demonstrate transformative 
potential in comprehensively upgrading the high-temperature performance of TiAl alloys. Recent investigations 
have demonstrated that ceramic particle-reinforced TiAl matrix composites possess excellent overall perfor -
mance. A comprehensive overview is provided in this paper on recent developments regarding the elevated- 
temperature properties of ceramic particle-reinforced TiAl composites, focusing on: ceramic/TiAl matrix in -
terfaces, effect of ceramic particles on the microstructure, high-temperature strength and ductility, creep 
behavior, and oxidation resistance behavior. Finally, the limitations faced by ceramic particle reinforced TiAl 
composites and their future development directions were summarized. The work aims to provide references for 
developing high-performance lightweight TiAl composites.
1. Introduction
Aerospace engines are required to utilize nickel-based superalloys for 
turbine blade fabrication. However, they possess a significant drawback 
of high density, typically ranging between 8 and 9 g/cm
3 
[ 1 , 2 ]. Weight 
reduction proves crucial for decreasing engine fuel consumption and 
mitigating greenhouse gas emissions. For instance, a one-pound reduc -
tion in aircraft weight translates to annual fuel savings of 11,000 gallons 
for airline operators [ 3 – 5 ]. TiAl alloys are lightweight intermetallic 
compounds characterized by their inherent advantages, such as low 
density, high specific strength, and superior oxidation resistance at in -

strengths that are similar to or surpass those of nickel-based superalloys 
[ 10 – 12 ]. TiAl alloys are presently being effectively used in the 
production of turbine wheels [ 13 ], low-pressure turbine blades for 
aircraft engine [ 14 – 16 ] and racing engine components [ 12 , 17 ] et al. 
However, the broader adoption of conventional TiAl alloys is hindered 
by intrinsic limitations such as insufficient room-temperature ductility 
[ 18 ], rapid strength degradation above 800

3. Preparation methods of ceramic particle-reinforced TiAl 
composites
The performance advantages and disadvantages of ceramic particle- 
reinforced TiAl composites are strongly influenced by the type, 
morphology, size, and distribution of reinforcement particles within the 
matrix, which in turn are profoundly influenced by fabrication pro -
cesses. This section will focus on three mainstream preparation methods 
(casting, powder metallurgy, and additive manufacturing), and provide 
a detailed analysis of their corresponding reinforcement particle types 
and distribution characteristics.
3.1. Casting
The casting method typically involves melting TiAl alloys via vac -
uum induction melting or arc melting, followed by incorporating 
ceramic reinforcement particles into the molten bath. After thorough 
stirring, it is poured into molds for solidification. Due to the high melting 
point and high reactivity of molten TiAl alloys, casting must be con -
ducted under vacuum or inert atmosphere to prevent oxidation. This 
process is characterized by single-step melting and forming, offering 
relative operational simplicity. However, it suffers from slow cooling

3.2. Powder metallurgy
Powder metallurgy is a solid-state processing technique that fabri -
cates composites through powder mixing, pressing, and sintering. 
Throughout this process, the matrix metal never melts extensively, 
thereby avoiding severe segregation and interfacial reactions inherent in 
melt-casting methods, while enabling composite fabrication at lower 
temperatures [ 77 , 78 ]. Since the powder particles themselves are small, 
the sintered microstructure is usually finer and more uniform than the 
cast state, with a smaller grain size, so the material properties are stable 
and isotropic [ 79 ]. Of course, powder metallurgy also has its 
shortcomings, such as easy oxidation of powder during the preparation 
process, residual pores left by insufficient sintering, and high large-scale 
production costs [ 80 ].
In powder metallurgy, it is easier to achieve a uniform distribution of 
ceramic particle reinforcements in the matrix [ 78 ]. The distribution of 
reinforcements particles in powder metallurgy composites depends 
mainly on the uniformity of powder mixing and the movement and re -
action of particles during sintering. If the powder mixing process is 
sufficient, ceramic particles will be randomly mixed between metal 
matrix powders [ 81 ]. The pressing and sintering process basically re -
tains this mixing state, so the sintered composites have a relatively 
uniform distribution of reinforcement particles throughout the volume 
from a macroscopic and statistical point of view, and there will not be 
obvious composition differences between the upper and lower parts due 
to gravity as in casting [ 82 ]. Despite macroscopic uniformity, particle 
agglomeration may still occur at the microscopic level if the powder 
mixing process is not appropriate. In particular, nano powders tend to 
agglomerate into agglomerates. During sintering, particles may be 
enriched on grain boundaries [ 83 ]. In-situ endogenous methods can 
avoid agglomeration of foreign particles, thereby providing better uni -
formity and performance. For example, Guo et al. [ 84 ] prepared nano- 
and micron-sized Ti
2
AlC reinforced TiAl composites by adding 
nano-carbon powder. The nanoparticles were mostly distributed at the 
γ / α
2 
layer interface, and the micron-sized particles were blocky and 
distributed in the matrix and the blocky γ phase boundary. The number 
and size of particles increased with the increase of carbon content, and 
the particles precipitated more fully and were more evenly distributed 
after heat treatment.
3.3. Additive manufacturing
Additive manufacturing, an innovative fabrication technique that 
constructs complex components through layer-by-layer material depo -
sition, offers solutions for developing TiAl composites [ 22 , 85 – 87 ]. Its 
core advantage lies in the rapid solidification rates that simultaneously 
refine matrix grains and inhibit coarsening of ceramic reinforcements, 
while enabling in-situ synthesis and homogeneous distribution of rein -
forcing phases through controlled reactions [ 85 , 88 , 89 ]. Yang et al. [ 68 ] 
added TiB
2 
when preparing TiAl by additive manufacturing. TiB
2 
pre -
cipitates in TiAl in the form of needle-like strips with sizes ranging from 
nanometers to micrometers, evenly distributed at grain boundaries and 
in lamellar sheets, and increases in both quantity and size as the content 
increases. Kan et al. [ 90 ] also fabricated TiC/TiAl composites via addi -
tive manufacturing, achieving uniform distribution of nano-TiC particles 
free from agglomeration and aggregation.

5. Microstructural characteristics of TiAl composites
A strong relationship exists between the microstructure and the 
resulting properties of the material. The incorporation of ceramic re -

The high-temperature strengthening mechanisms in ceramic 
particle-reinforced TiAl composites are not entirely independent. They 
exhibit inherent interconnections, with potential overlaps in certain 
mechanistic contributions, which often collectively results in elevated 
theoretically calculated values than experimentally measured. Grain 
refinement strengthening and thermal mismatch strengthening can be 
considered complementary mechanisms. The incorporation of ceramic 
particles not only promotes grain refinement but also increases dislo -
cation density. Higher dislocation density interactions with more grain 
boundaries, making the material more strength at elevated tempera -
tures. Simultaneously, elevated dislocation density facilitates twin for -
mation, thus promoting twinning strengthening. Simultaneously, the 
non-ideal homogeneous distribution of ceramic particles within TiAl 
composites also contributes to the overestimation in theoretical pre -
dictions. In the application, an optimal strengthening effect can be 
achieved with a properly controlled ceramic particle content, but 
excessive loading may induce particle agglomeration and exacerbate 
interfacial defects, which will correspondingly reduce the fracture 
toughness and ductility of the material. Therefore, in actual design, it is 
necessary to comprehensively consider the particle type, size, content 
and distribution state to achieve the optimal strengthening effect.
6.3. Toughening mechanisms
The inherent brittleness of TiAl alloys stems from limited dislocation 
slip systems, resulting in exceptionally low fracture toughness at room 
temperature [ 136 , 157 ]. Although ductility improves at elevated tem -
peratures, it still difficult to meet the requirements for resistance to 
crack propagation under higher-temperature conditions [ 158 ]. The 
toughening mechanism of ceramic particle reinforced TiAl composites is 
the result of multiple synergistic effects. Firstly, the addition of ceramic 
particles reduces the α
2
/ γ lamellar spacing. Fine α
2
/ γ lamellae can 
support and coordinate plastic deformation during deformation, thereby 
enhancing the toughness [ 136 ]. In addition, ceramic particles can act as 
an obstacle to crack propagation, causing cracks to bypass particles or 
deflect along interfaces, thereby increasing the crack propagation path. 
It takes additional energy for cracks to turn or branch on the propagation 
path, thereby improving fracture toughness [ 83 , 159 ]. When the cracks 
extend around the reinforcing particles, the particles may still be 
embedded between the two walls of the cracks, playing the role of 
bridging the load. These bridging particles hinder the opening of the 
crack surface by bearing part of the tensile stress, and higher stress is 
required for the crack to continue to extend [ 128 ]. In addition, traces of 
particles being pulled out of the fracture surface can often be observed in 
the fracture [ 117 , 126 ], which is conducive to improving toughness. Li 
et al. [ 118 ] also found that Ti

8.3. Oxidation resistance mechanisms of TiAl composites
The formation of Al
2
O
3 
is thermodynamically favored over TiO
2
, as 
evidenced by the lower Gibbs free energy of Al-O reactions compared to 
Ti-O systems in TiAl [ 188 , 198 ]. However, the evolution of oxide layer is 
simultaneously governed by the diffusion kinetics of Ti, Al, and O. The 
significantly higher diffusion rate of Ti, results in a TiO
2
-dominated 
outer layer with coarse, porous microstructures that degrade oxidation 
resistance [ 188 , 199 , 200 ]. As was shown in the previous section, the 
presence of ceramic particles promotes the formation of a dense Al
2
O
3 
layer in the oxide layer, preventing inward oxygen flow and improving 
the oxidation resistance of TiAl composites. Furthermore, the ceramic 
particles restrict both the outward diffusion of Ti and the inward flow of 
O, thereby synergistically enhancing the TiAl composites oxidation 
resistance [ 190 ]. Ceramic particles enhance the oxidation resistance by 
promoting grain refinement. ( Fig. 10 ). Primarily, the reduced grain size 
increases grain boundary density, which facilitates the formation of 
Al
2
O
3 
oxide pegs at these regions, which in turn strengthens the adhe -
sion between the oxide layer and TiAl matrix [ 40 ]. Additionally, the 
mismatch in coefficients of thermal expansion between the oxide layer 
and TiAl matrix typically induces crack initiation. However, the refined 
grain microstructure enhances plastic deformation capability, effec -
tively reducing thermal stresses at the oxide/matrix interface [ 40 ,
201 – 203 ]. This results in stronger interfacial bonding and significantly 
improves resistance to spallation.
Different types of ceramic particles reduce oxide layer thickness, 
decrease oxidation weight gain, and enhance oxidation resistance by 
altering the composition of oxidation products, improving the densifi -
cation of oxide films, and increasing the stability of the oxide layer. 
Additionally, different types of ceramic reinforcement particles exhibit 
their own unique mechanisms. TiB
2 
primarily relies on refining the 
microstructure to promote the formation of a dense Al
2
O
3

significantly enhance the adhesion of the oxide film. The primary role of

2

2

and improving 
oxide scale quality. This includes enhancing scale density and stability, 
retarding O and Ti diffusion, and strengthening oxide/substrate inter -

reinforcement particles and oxidation resistance performance. The 
oxidation resistance mechanisms of different types of ceramic particles 
are also highly interrelated. Reinforcement phases containing active 
elements (Si, Y) directly enhance the oxidation resistance of the matrix 
by altering oxidation reaction products and kinetics, while highly stable 
or high-hardness reinforcements (TiB

grains and reduce thermal stress).

9.2. Current limitations
Although ceramic particle reinforcement has brought significant 
improvements, TiAl composites still face several limitations. Interfacial 
Compatibility: Achieving optimal bonding at the ceramic/TiAl inter -
face remains challenging. Poor bonding may result in debonding or 
crack formation under mechanical or thermal loading. Particle 
Agglomeration: Excessive or uneven distribution of ceramic particles 
may lead to stress concentration, thereby compromising mechanical 
properties. Trade-off between brittleness and toughness: Although 
ceramic particles can improve strength and resistance to creep, they may 
also reduce fracture toughness and ductility, especially at room tem -
perature. Processing Limitations : TiAl composites exhibit poor hot 
workability, requiring elevated processing temperatures that results in 
high manufacturing costs.
9.3. Future perspectives
To overcome current limitations and further advance TiAl composite 
development, several research trends are emerging. Develop smart 
ceramic reinforcements with dynamic responsiveness, such as 
temperature/stress-activated ceramics and shape-memory ceramics, to 
achieve service-condition-adaptive toughening. Relying on atomic-level 
precise control to deposit single-atom interface coating on the surface of 
ceramic particles and femtosecond laser additive manufacturing to 
suppress segregation to solve the problem of ceramics reinforcement 
distribution and interface bonding. Design TiAl composites with 
dynamically reconfigurable ceramic-matrix interfaces that evolve with 
time and service conditions, enabling self-healing capabilities and 
extended service life during operation.
