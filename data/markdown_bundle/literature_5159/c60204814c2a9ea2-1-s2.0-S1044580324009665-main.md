ABSTRACT
W-Re-HfC alloys have been extensively applied in the field of high-temperature structural parts. To ensure high 
temperature reliability, it is essential to enhance creep resistance ability. In this study, the microstructures of 
forged W-Re-HfC alloys were adjusted using different heat treatment processes. The corresponding tensile creep 
properties were also measured at 2000 
◦
C with 40 MPa. Furthermore, a possible creep fracture mechanism was 
also explored. The forged W-Re-HfC sample annealed for 2 h exhibited the best creep performances with a 
steady-state creep rate of 3.28 × 10
 6 
and a creep life of 5.6 h. Generally, a larger grain size indicates a lower 
steady-state creep rate; however, the precipitation of carbides at the grain boundaries (GBs) deteriorates the 
bonding strength of interfaces, thus reversing the former trend. The dominant creep mechanism is diffusion 
creep, in which the voids nucleate perpendicular to the GBs, grow and connect into cracks, ultimately leading to 
fractures.
1. Introduction
Tungsten alloys are key high temperature structural material for 
components served in aerospace and nuclear fields because of their high 
melting point, high temperature strength and good corrosion resistance 
ability [ 1 – 4 ]. The solution softening of Re element, namely “ Re effect ” , 
and the second phase strengthening of HfC associated with subsequent 
thermo-mechanical processing can significantly improve the high tem -
perature strength of tungsten alloys [ 5 – 7 ]. Therefore, the W-Re-HfC 
alloy is the most advantageous candidate material when the tempera -
ture exceeds the limiting service temperature of nickel-based superal -
loys, such as emitters of space power systems (operating at a 
temperature close to 1900 K) and hydrazine arc thrusters with a working 
temperature capability of up to 2000 
◦
C [ 8 ]. Moreover, numerous 
studies have reported its room and high temperature strengths as well as 
the underlying strengthening mechanism thoroughly [ 9 , 10 ]. Under 
actual working conditions, a metal undergoes a continuously slow 
deformation, a process known as creep, when stressed at high temper -
atures, which leads to material failure damage and costs billions of 
dollars for repair in advanced devices [ 11 ]. Consequently, creep per -
formance parameters represent crucial indicators for the design and 
assessment of high-temperature structural parts [ 12 – 15 ]. However, 
owing to the limitations of available equipment and the high cost of 
testing, there has been relatively limited research on ultra-high- 
temperature creep.
Witzke [ 16 ] reported that the addition of HfC significantly enhanced 
the creep resistance of tungsten ‑ rhenium alloys, surpassing the effects of 
ThO
2

such as stress exponent, activation energy, but did not elucidate the 
relationship between the microstructure and creep properties [ 17 ].
In contrast to the Hall-Petch effect at room temperature, grain 
boundary (GB) exhibits viscosity at high temperatures ( ≥ 0.5 T

atomic diffusivity in GB is several orders of magnitude larger than that in

creep resistance ability deteriorates with smaller grain sizes [ 20,21 ]. 
However, it is unclear whether the change in microstructure affects the 
creep properties during high temperature service, and there are few 
studies. Therefore, it is of paramount importance and immediate ne -
cessity to investigate the creep behavior of W-Re-HfC alloys at ultrahigh 
temperatures and the influence of microstructure variation on creep 
properties.

3.2. Creep results
The creep curves of various W-Re-HfC alloys are shown in Fig. 5 (a), 
and the transient creep rate curves obtained from the first-order differ -
ential of creep curves are displayed in Fig. 5 (b). In general, the defor -
mation rate is significantly high in the early stage of creep because the 
deformation resistance ability is low, which is called the first stage. 
Subsequently, due to the work hardening, the creep rate gradually de -
creases, and the dynamic recovery rate is accelerated. When work 
hardening and dynamic recovery reach a dynamic equilibrium, the 
creep speed remains constant, known as the steady-state creep stage or 
second stage. The formation of creep voids leads to localized stress 
concentration, and the necking of the specimen increases the effective 
stress, accelerating the creep rate, which is generally called the third 
stage of creep, commonly referred to the creep damage stage [ 25 ]. It is 
evident that the creep rate of A-2 h initially decreases, followed by the 
appearance of a steady state segment. In contrast, the remaining speci -
mens do not clearly exhibit a steady-state creep phase, making it chal -
lenging to discern the second and third creep stages.
In order to determine a mechanical steady-state, the idea inflection 
point has been adopted from the work by Kassnera and Perez-Prado 
[ 26 ]. Hence, the inflection points of the second and third creep stages 
(represented by the bule pentagram) is designated as the transient

3.3. Creep deformation and fracture mechanisms
The fracture morphology was further captured, as depicted in Fig. 6 . 
The creep region fracture of all samples shows a grain-twisted 
morphology without fracture dimple that are similar to the Class І 
solid solutions [ 28 ]. Curved slip lines can be observed, which are slip 
bands of cross-slip indicating activation of multiple slip systems, as 
indicated by the blue arrows [ 6 ]. Slip is a result of complete dislocation 
motion, indicating that the dislocation sliding is one of the mechanisms 
contributing to creep. Traces of dislocation climbing second phase par -
ticles can also be observed, as indicated by the red arrows. These second 
phase particles can be effective barriers to dislocation motion, which is 
beneficial for improving high temperature performances [ 29 ].
The fracture morphology away from the deformation zone is mostly 
intergranular with a small amount of transgranular fracture. The 
diameter of the sample in this region is coarser than the creep defor -
mation zone and the creep stress suffered is lower than that in the center, 
which belongs to the high temperature mode with low stress. Owing to 
the different vacancy concentrations on the tensile and compressive 
stress faces, the vacancy concentration gradient causes vacancy diffu -
sion while the atomic diffusion generated in the direction opposite to the 
vacancy flow leads to grain deformation. When the vacancy concen -
tration at the GBs reaches a certain level, the fracture occurs. Therefore, 
the creep mechanism is dominated by the diffusion at GBs. While, in the 
middle region, the stress is higher, resulting in dislocation slip and the 
corresponding interaction with the second phase. As can be seen from 
Fig. 7 , a relative sliding deformation occurs at GBs. Grain boundary 
sliding must be coupled with plastic deformation of the GB holes 
resulting from GB steps and interfacial undulations [ 22 ], which is origin 
for the torsional grain shape of the fracture. Consequently, the creep 
deformation mechanism consists of two parts: including the plastic 
deformation of grains and the deformation of GB.

In summary, grain deformation under creep stress primarily involves 
two key processes. On the one hand, the plastic deformation occurs 
within the grains due to dislocation creep. On the other hand, the 
deformation is predominantly localized at GBs, where GB diffusion is the 
dominant creep mechanism with GB sliding and grain rotation as sec -
ondary auxiliary mechanisms. Creep voids can nucleate at various sites, 
including inclusions, second phase particles, GB ridges and trident GB 
junctions [22]. The stress concentration caused by the vacancy diffusion 
and the obstruction of the slip band at GBs is the main reason for the void 
nucleation. Once the voids nucleate, the grains undergo localized GB 
splitting, cracking interactions and bridging, ultimately leading to 
fracture. The creep mechanism is illustrated in Fig. 10 .
4. Conclusions
(1) The A-2 h exhibits the most favorable creep properties with a 
creep life of 5.6 h and a steady-state creep rate of 3.28 × 10
 6
.
(2) The grain size strongly affects the steady-state creep rate. With 
increased grain size, the steady-state creep rate decreases, but the 
precipitation phase at the grain boundary deteriorates the creep 
performance.
(3) The primary creep mechanism of W-Re-HfC alloy at 2000 
◦
C with 
40 MPa is grain boundary diffusion. The creep cavity nucleates 
and grow perpendicular to the grain boundaries, subsequently 
connecting into cracks, which ultimately cause fracture.
