1. Introduction 
Multicomponent high-entropy alloys (HEAs) with outstanding me -
chanical properties, such as a high strength, high fracture toughness, 
high wear resistance, and good corrosion resistance, have been widely 
developed in the past two decades [ 1 – 5 ]. Among them, body-centered 
cubic (BCC) refractory HEAs (RHEAs) particularly have excellent resis -
tance to softening at elevated temperatures [ 4 – 6 ]. Some of them are 
considered to replace nickel-based superalloys for use in even 
higher-temperature environments [ 4 , 6 ]. Owing to the local chemical 
ordering that causes local potential hills and valleys, kinks of screw 
dislocations are spontaneously formed at the energy valleys [ 7 ]. The 
strength of BCC HEAs will then be dominated by the difficulty of kink 
movement than traditional kink pair nucleation [ 7 ]. Also, because of 
severe lattice distortions to cause the rugged twisting motion of dislo -
cations, the movement of edge dislocations is slowed down to the speed 
close to that of screw dislocations [ 8 ]. In addition, the intersection of 
kinks produces immobile cross kinks, hence contributing towards the 
high-temperature softening resistance of BCC HEAs [ 7 – 10 ]. As exam -
ples, the early developed WTaMoNbV and WTaMoNb RHEAs presented 
outstanding high-temperature softening resistance, with a yield strength 
of near 500 MPa at 1600 
◦
C. However, they suffer from the drawbacks of 
high density and low ductility at room temperature [ 11 , 12 ]. A series of 
HfNbTaTiZr RHEAs with much improved ductility were thereafter 
developed but lost the advantage of high-temperature strength [ 13 – 16 ]. 
In recent years, for obtaining both a high-temperature strength and 
plastic deformability, heterogeneous HEAs, including gradient, precip -
itation, dual-phase, layered, eutectic structures, etc., have been inten -
sively developed [ 17 – 25 ]. With the combination of soft and hard zones 
and the discontinuity of plastic strain, the strength could be improved, 
and the ductility could partly be retained [ 17 – 23 ]. For lowering the 
density, Al has been especially added in RHEAs [ 26 , 27 ]. Because of the 
large negative mixing enthalpies of Al with refractory metals [ 28 ], 
aluminide-based intermetallic compounds with an ordered B2 or Laves 
structure were formed [ 29 – 39 ]. These hard phase can strengthen the 
alloys but however are detrimental to ductility at an excessive content.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Microstructure and phase formation 
Fig. 1 first presents the microstructures, crystal structures and 
chemical compositions of as-cast Al
x
RHEAs samples. In the SEM images 
in Fig. 1 (a) , a primary dendritic structure (white) and later solidified 
interdendritic regions (red) upon solidification were observed. As 
determined by using the software ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) 
and presented in Table 2 , the fraction of the dendritic regions was all 
about 25 %. As the Al content increased, the size of the dendrite arms 
decreased from about 10 to 4 μ m, owing to two possible factors: (1) more 
nucleation sites were generated for the liquid-to-solid phase trans -
formation, and (2) a second phase formed in the interdendritic regions 
inhibited the growth of the dendrites or the coarsening of the arms. As 
revealed by the rising diffraction peaks of the other phase from the solid- 
solution BCC matrix in the XRD patterns in Fig. 1 (b) , an increasing 
amount of the second phase (very possibly the hexagonal close-packed 
(HCP) C14 Laves phase) was formed. As given in Table 1 , with an 
increased Al content, the lattice constant of the BCC matrix gradually 
decreased not only because of the smaller atom size of Al than the re -
fractory metals but also the large negative mixing enthalpies of Al with 
these refractory metals. The strong potential field around the Al atoms 
was expected to cause partial covalent-like bonds and a shortened 
interatomic distance [ 47 ]. The experimentally measured lattice pa -
rameters were thus close to the theoretically predicted values using the 
rule of mixtures. A slight split of diffraction peaks of the BCC phase was 
also noticed, attributable to the compositional deviation in the dendritic 
and interdendritic regions stated below and thus a small difference in 
lattice constants. 
The EDS elemental mappings in Fig. 1 (c) and Supplementary Fig. S1 , 
and the approximate chemical compositions of different phase regions in 
Supplementary Tables S1 – S4 , indicate the compositional deviation in 
the dendritic and interdendritic regions of the Al
x
RHEAs samples. In 
addition, the EBSD crystallographic orientation (IPF) and phase maps in 
Fig. 2 suggest the formation of a second phase from the BCC matrix. 
Basically, while Ti and V were uniformly distributed, the dendrites were 
rich in (but not limited to) high-melting-point Mo, Nb and Ta, and the 
interdendrites were rich in Hf and Zr. The segregation of mutually sol -

It is noticed that the nanoindenting hardness of all the individual 
phases was basically higher than the overall Vickers hardness of the 
alloys due to the size effect of the indentations [ 49 ]. The measured 
hardness basically increases with decreasing size of indentation owing to 
the nucleation of dislocations (particularly the geometrically necessary 
dislocations) within the plastic zone [ 49 ]. In general, the nanoindenting 
hardness is higher than the Vickers hardness by 10 – 30 % [ 50 ]. In these 
Al-added alloys particularly with a higher Al content, the hardness of the 
interdendrites was even higher, but the modulus of the interdendrites 
was contrarily lower than that of the dendrites. The Al-(Hf,V,Zr)-rich 
C14 Laves precipitates in the interdendritic regions is a relatively hard 
intermetallic compound that will contribute towards high composite 
strengthening to the Al-added alloys. However, its constituent elements 
are inherently less stiff than Mo, Nb and Ta [ 49 , 50 ], which will result in 
the lower modulus of the interdendrites than that of the dendrites as 
observed.

grain boundaries, no pinning of dislocations by the precipitates to resist 
softening was observed. 
Much differently, as suggested by Fig. 6 (c) and (d), the deformation 
of the Al
0.5
RHEA with more second phase was dominated by the inter -
action of dislocations with the precipitates than simply by the motion of 
dislocations in the BCC matrix. Owing to a strain discontinuity and a 
large strain mismatch between the two phases, a very large number of 
dislocations were expected to initiate from the phase boundaries 
[ 56 – 58 ]. But, they would soon be pinned by and piled up at the 
boundaries, contributing to the high strength of the alloy. The very large 
amount of fine twisted dislocations were observed to form a severely 
tangled dislocation forest in the matrix of both the room-temperature 
and 600 
◦
C deformed Al
0.5
RHEA. Their intensive intersection possibly 
produced many immobile cross kinks [ 7 – 10 ], which was believed to be 
an additional mechanism for the high softening resistance of the alloy at 
elevated temperatures. According to the literature [ 59 – 61 ], the mobility 
of screw and edge dislocation was suggested to mediate the plastic 
deformation at room temperature and elevated temperatures, respec -
tively. Owing to the rugged energy landscape in BCC RHEAs, screw 
dislocations might form kinks at energy valleys [ 59 ]. The intersection of 
kinks on different slip planes would then form stuck cross kinks to 
decelerate the motion of screw dislocations that dominated the defor -
mation at room temperature. At elevated temperatures, cross kinks 
might be eliminated owing to the diffusion of vacancies. However, the 
wavy motion would markedly lower the speed of edge dislocations that 
alternatively mediated the deformation at high temperatures [ 59 – 61 ]. 
For certain, the detailed geometry (such as the types, Burgers vectors 
and slip planes) of the dislocations and the exact mechanisms for 
yielding the high softening resistance are worth further investigations. 
Additionally, although the accumulation of dislocations at the phase 
boundaries led to work hardening [ 59 – 61 ], however, an excessive 
accumulation would result in an unconscionable stress concentration, 
and some dislocations could even pass through the precipitates to leave 
slip traces, which would thereafter cause the brittle fracture of the alloy 
in the latter stage. 
4. Conclusions 
In this study, Al

0.5
NbTa
0.5
TiVZr dual-phase RHEAs with 
different amounts of Al were developed. During the casting and solidi -
fication of the Al
x
RHEAs samples, high-melting-point refractory ele -
ments formed (Mo,Nb,Ta)-rich BCC dendrites of high hardness before 
the freezing of relatively low-melting-point (Hf,Zr)-rich interdendrites. 
With a higher Al content, a larger fraction of hard, network-like Al-(Hf, 
V,Zr)-rich C14 Laves second phase was formed along the interdendrites. 
While the hard precipitates enhanced the yield strength and softening 
resistance, they lowered the plastic deformability of the alloys. At a 
proper Al content of x = 0.5, the Al
0.5
RHEA sample exhibited promising 
softening resistance at 600 
◦
C and considerable plasticity. Unidirectional 
planar gliding of relatively linear dislocations dominated the plastic 
deformation of the alloy with a low Al content at 600 
◦
C, causing a 
marked decrease in strength. With a high Al content, a large number of 
dislocations were generated from and piled up at the second phase 
boundaries, forming a severely tangled dislocation forest in the matrix 
and contributing towards the high softening resistance. An appropriate 
Al content needs to be considered for optimizing the mechanical per -
formance of dual-phase Al
x
RHEAs.
