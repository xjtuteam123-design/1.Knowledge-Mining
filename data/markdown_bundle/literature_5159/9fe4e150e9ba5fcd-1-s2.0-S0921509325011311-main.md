ABSTRACT
The utilization of revert cast superalloys presents significant advantages in environmental protection and pro -
duction efficiency enhancement, which contain large amounts of nitrogen. This study comprehensively inves -
tigated the nitrogen-induced effects on microstructural characteristics and creep performance in Ni-based cast 
superalloys, with focus on microsegregation and inclusion evolution. Experimental results demonstrated that 
increasing nitrogen content from 4 ppm to 55 ppm led to progressive intensification of elemental segregation. A 
direct correlation between nitrogen concentration and nitride precipitation was revealed: higher nitrogen levels 
promote both increased number density and dimensional growth of nitride particles. First-principles calculations 
demonstrated that TiN inclusions preferentially form during solidification and serve as heterogeneous nucleation 
cores, thereby refining the grain structure. Increased nitrogen content reduced the volume fractions of MC 
carbides, γ / γ ′ eutectics, and γ ′ precipitates. A MC carbide morphological transition from rod-like to blocky 
configurations was observed as N content rose, accompanied by increased number density and reduced particle 
dimensions. Creep tests demonstrated an inverse relationship between nitrogen content and mechanical prop -
erties, with average creep life decreasing from 49.63 h (4 ppm N) to 23.33 h (55 ppm N). These findings provide 
critical insights into the application of revert superalloys and sustainable manufacturing in high-temperature 
component production.
1. Introduction
With the continuous advancement in aero-engine design, the de -
mand for higher thrust-to-weight ratios has intensified, placing stricter 
requirements on the high-temperature mechanical properties of critical 
components [ 1 – 3 ]. Superalloys are widely used in these hot-section 
components because of the outstanding service performance in 
high-temperature environments [ 4 – 7 ]. Nickel-based casting superalloys 
have dual-phase structures of γ matrix and γ ′ precipitation, which are 
stable at high temperatures and display excellent mechanical properties 
and ductility at extreme temperatures through the precipitation 
strengthening mechanism [ 8 – 11 ]. In addition, nickel-based casting su -
peralloys have outstanding molding capability for complex-shaped 
parts, so they have gradually become a hot spot in the research of 
high-temperature structural materials [ 12 , 13 ]. The casting production 
process of complex components generates many non-compliant scrap 
castings. Considering the high raw material cost of nickel-based super -
alloys and the scarcity of raw material sources, recycling and reusing 
these revert superalloys can significantly reduce production costs, 
improve production efficiency, and realize environmental protection 
[ 14 – 16 ].
However, the revert superalloys contain substantial nitrogen and 
inclusions [ 17 , 18 ], necessitating systematic investigation into the ef -
fects of nitrogen on microstructural evolution and properties of cast 
superalloys. As a common impurity element in nickel-based superalloys, 
nitrogen forms strong bonds with Ti, Mo, Ta, and V, making it chal -
lenging to eliminate during melting [ 19 , 20 ]. Nitrogen significantly 
affected the microstructure of superalloys [ 21 ]. Liu et al. [ 22 ] observed

Nitrogen exerts complex effects on alloy mechanical properties 
through distinct mechanisms. On the one hand, as a typical interstitial 
strengthening element in face-centered cubic alloys, controlled nitrogen 
additions enhance strength via lattice distortion [ 30 – 33 ]. Song et al. 
[ 34 ] achieved joint enhancement of tensile strength and ductility after 
introducing and dissolving a small amount of N atoms in FeCoNiCr al -
loys. Concurrently, nitride-induced lattice expansion mitigates the vol -
ume shrinkage during solidification, effectively reducing microporosity 
in single-crystal superalloys and abnormally enhancing creep resistance 
[ 35 , 36 ]. On the other hand, the nitrides formed in the superalloys after 
N addition had sharp edges that could be sites of stress concentration 
and crack initiation, increasing the fatigue crack growth rate [ 37 ]. 
Moreover, the mechanical properties of cast superalloys, such as tensile 
and stress rupture properties, were degraded by the increase in N con -
tent [ 38 ].
While nitrogen behavior in superalloys has been extensively studied 
[ 39 – 41 ], the mechanisms of nitrogen in polycrystalline cast superalloys 
remain incompletely revealed. Therefore, it is of great importance to 
study the effect of nitrogen on polycrystalline cast superalloys. In this 
study, we selected a low-cost and low-density cast polycrystalline su -
peralloy enriched with nitride-forming elements: Ti, Al, Cr, Mo, and V. 
Systematic investigations were carried out for the effect of nitrogen on 
the inclusions, microsegregation, microstructure, and creep properties 
of polycrystalline cast superalloys. This study aimed to adequately 
reveal the role of nitrogen in polycrystalline cast superalloys, providing 
a basis for the optimization of revert superalloys, and the green pro -
duction of nickel-based superalloy castings.
2. Experiment
2.1. Materials preparation
The cast polycrystalline superalloys studied here had the following 
composition (wt%): 9Cr, 10Co, 0.17C, 3Mo, 0.75V, 4.4Ti, 5.25Al, bal -
ance Ni. High-purity raw materials were first vacuum arc-melted under 
argon atmosphere to produce master-alloy ingots. To systematically 
control nitrogen content, different amounts of CrN were introduced into 
the melt. Four N content gradients were initially designed: 0, 10, 20, and 
50 ppm. However, due to residual N in the raw materials and N losses 
during melting, the final measured N concentrations were 4 ppm (N4), 7 
ppm (N7), 19 ppm (N19), and 55 ppm (N55). The alloys were subse -
quently cast into round bars, with each specimen named according to its 
nitrogen concentration.

reduced the area fraction of MC carbides.

nickel-based superalloys. Fine nitride particles acted as obstacles to

mismatch between the inclusions and the matrix, hindering deformation 
coordination under stress. Consequently, interfacial debonding between 
inclusions and the matrix readily occurred, leading to the formation of 
pores [ 58 ]. Moreover, large inclusions with sharp edges tended to 
induce localized stress concentrations. To investigate the stress con -

precipitated first during solidification. These early-formed nitride in -

increased nucleation density refined the grain structure of cast super -

During creep deformation, MC carbides served as effective barriers to 
dislocation motion and pinned grain boundaries [ 62 ]. Consequently, the 
MC carbide content exerted a significant influence on the creep prop -
erties of superalloys. As evident from Fig. 3 (a), the area fraction of MC 
carbides in the experimental alloy progressively decreased with higher 
nitrogen content. This reduction in MC carbide content directly 
contributed to the degraded creep performance observed in high-N 
alloys.
As shown in Fig. 11 , γ ′ precipitates effectively restricted dislocation 
motion within the matrix channels, leading to dislocation plugging 
around the γ ′ precipitates. Furthermore, the formation of dislocation 
networks due to high dislocation density significantly hindered further 
dislocation movement [ 63 ]. However, nitrogen addition reduced the 
available Ti content in the alloy through TiN formation. Since Ti is a key 
constituent of γ ′ precipitates, the precipitation strengthening effect was 
weakened in high-N alloys. This reduction in precipitation strengthening 
diminished the resistance to dislocation motion, ultimately deterio -
rating the creep properties.
As massive dislocations accumulated around the γ ′ phase, a fraction 
of them would cut into the γ ′ precipitation via the shear mechanism. 
Consequently, the resistance to dislocation cut-in at the γ’ / γ interface 
played a critical role in determining the creep properties of nickel-based 
superalloys. This resistance primarily depended on the antiphase 
boundary (APB) energy of the γ ′ phase — higher APB energy made 
dislocation cutting into γ ′ precipitation less favorable [ 64 ]. Baither et al. 
[ 65 ] reported that reducing the Ti/Al ratio in Ni-based superalloys from 
1.28 to 0.22 lowered the APB energy from 258 to 350 mJ/m

exhibited more dislocations cutting into the γ ′ phase.
5. Conclusion
The effects of nitrogen content on the formation of inclusions, 
elemental segregation, microstructure evolution, and high-temperature 
creep properties in nickel-based polycrystalline cast superalloys were 
systematically investigated. The main conclusions are as follows. 
(1) Nitrogen addition widened the solidification freezing range, 
which exacerbated micro-segregation of alloying elements, 
particularly Ti and V.
(2) The solubility of nitrogen in the nickel-based cast superalloys was 
approximately 2.85 ppm, with excess nitrogen forming nitrides. 
Higher nitrogen content increased both the number density of 
nitrides and the proportion of large-sized nitride particles.
(3) Increased nitrogen content promoted TiN formation, consuming 
Ti and consequently reducing the volume fractions of MC car -
bides, γ / γ ′ eutectics, and γ ′ precipitates. The nitrides acted as 
heterogeneous nucleation sites for MC carbides, increasing the 
number density while decreasing the average size. During MC 
carbide growth, compositional and thermal field variations 
caused a morphological transition from rod-shaped to blocky 
structures.
(4) Higher nitrogen content degraded creep properties through 
multiple mechanisms. Increased nitride particle density provided 
more crack initiation sites, while nitride-induced grain refine -
ment introduced more grain boundaries that created additional 
crack propagation paths. The combined reduction of γ ′ phase 
fraction and antiphase boundary energy weakened dislocation 
motion resistance.
(5) For applications of revert superalloy castings, nitrogen content 
should be controlled below 7 ppm to achieve an optimal balance 
between economic viability, environmental considerations, and 
the mechanical performance.
