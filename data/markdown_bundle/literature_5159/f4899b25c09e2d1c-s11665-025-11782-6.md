Microstructure and Mechanical Properties of NiAlCrFeMo
High-Entropy Alloy Sheet under Different Hot-Rolling
Parameters
Xinyu Wang, Yuanming Huo, Zhijun Wang, Zhenrong Yan, Wenhan Yu, Yue Sun, Hao Chen, and Anqi Jiang
Submitted: 12 March 2025 / Revised: 19 June 2025 / Accepted: 11 July 2025
This study investigates the hot rolling of a newly developed NiAlCrFeMo high-entropy alloy, which
demonstrates excellent high-temperature mechanical properties but is prone to cracking due to its unique
microstructure. Hot-rolling experiments were conducted with varying rolling temperatures (1000-1100 /C176C),
rolling speeds (300-900 r/min), and reduction amounts (5-20%) using a double-stick hot- and cold-rolling
mill. Results show that as the reduction amount increases, the grain size reﬁnes, and the alloy’s mechanical
properties are optimized at a 20% reduction, with tensile strength increasing by 40.6% (966.1 MPa) and
elongation by 14.8% (3.1%) compared to the cast state. At a 5% reduction, the FCC phase volume fraction
is highest (66%), leading to the best ductility due to maximum crystal randomness. As rolling speed
increases, grain size decreases, and the FCC phase volume fraction increases. At 900 r/min, tensile strength
improves by 26.1% (866.5 MPa) compared to the cast state. The increase in rolling temperature leads to
larger grain sizes, and at 1100 /C176C, a reverse phase transformation occurs, increasing the BCC phase volume
fraction. Compared with the as-cast state, the specimen that has undergone 1000 /C176C rolling treatment
exhibits signiﬁcantly enhanced tensile strength and elongation in the subsequent room-temperature tensile
test, with the tensile strength increasing by 13.5% to 779.8 MPa, and the elongation increasing by 11.1 to
3%.

properties, NiAlCrFeMo high-entropy alloy,
superalloys
1. Introduction
The rapid development of industries such as aerospace,
aircraft, and automobiles has raised higher demands for the
comprehensive performance of materials (Ref 1, 2). High-
entropy alloys are a new type of alloy material, composed of
ﬁve or more principal elements with atomic concentrations that
are roughly similar, and each element’s content ranges from 5
to 35%. Although high-entropy alloys contain multiple com-
ponents, they do not form complex phases; they typically
consist of relatively simple face-centered cubic (FCC) or body-
centered cubic (BCC) solid solution phases (Ref 3, 4). Research
shows that FCC phase high-entropy alloys generally exhibit
good plasticity and toughness, but lower strength (Ref 5). BCC
phase high-entropy alloys, on the other hand, exhibit higher
strength but lower toughness (Ref 6). To achieve large-scale
production of high-entropy alloys, the production of sheet
materials becomes one of the key steps. High-entropy alloy
sheets can not only meet the demand for high-performance
materials in ﬁelds such as aerospace, automotive, and energy,
but also provide better processing performance and structural
stability in practical applications.
Hot rolling, as an efﬁcient processing technique, not only
signiﬁcantly improves the microstructure of high-entropy alloys
through grain reﬁnement, texture optimization, and phase
transformation, but also enhances their mechanical properties
(Ref 7, 8).
Y uan Li (Ref7) et al. demonstrated that hot rolling of a BCC
WMoTaV refractory high-entropy alloy (RHEA) induces the
formation of a W-enriched nanolayer, which signiﬁcantly alters
grain orientation and texture. This process results in dominant
<001>//ND and <111>//ND textures while suppressing
high-angle grain orientations. Under large rolling reductions,
the strong basal texture and activated non-basal slip work
synergistically to enhance both yield strength and plasticity.
Notably, the sample with a 60% reduction achieves an ultrahigh
yield strength of 1524 MPa and a plasticity of 8.1%. These
ﬁndings offer valuable insights for further optimizing the
mechanical properties of RHEAs. Zhe Liu et al. (Ref 9)
demonstrated that thermomechanical processing can enhance
the strength and ductility of small-size AlCoCrFeNi2.1, but its
effectiveness for large-size variants remains uncertain. Cold
rolling and warm rolling are ineffective for large-size
AlCoCrFeNi2.1 due to high deformation resistance and casting
defects. In contrast, hot rolling successfully deforms the large-
size alloy, yielding tensile properties on par with those from
cold rolling. This breakthrough facilitates the engineering
application of AlCoCrFeNi2.1. Wang et al. (Ref 10) demon-

were attributed to grain reﬁnement and dislocation strengthen-

strength, lightweight metallic materials.
However, the surface quality of the material is crucial during
the hot-rolling process, as it signiﬁcantly affects the processing
and mechanical properties (Ref 15, 16). Hiroshi Utsunomiya
et al. (Ref 17) investigated the deformation behavior of oxide
scale on low-carbon steel during the hot-rolling process,
revealing a variety of complex defects that may occur,
including uniform deformation, cracking, fragmentation,
wedge-shaped deformation, and V-shaped cracks. Shengjie
Wu et al. (Ref 18) examined the formation of serrated edge
cracks in high-grade non-oriented electrical steel (NOES)
during hot rolling. They found that these cracks are closely
linked to abnormal grain growth at the slab edges, which is
primarily caused by slab bulging during continuous casting and
exacerbated by the abnormal growth of columnar grains during
high-temperature reheating. Zhang Longchao et al. (Ref 19)
studied the upwarping defects on the surface of 780DH steel
hot-rolled coils. They found that edge defects were caused by
transverse cracks at the slab corners, while rib defects were due
to dense subsurface cracks. These issues were linked to the
high-aluminum steel composition and process parameters like
casting speed and secondary cooling intensity. Lei Zhi Liang
et al. (Ref 20) studied edge warping and hole defects in Q235B
and SPHC steel hot-rolled coils. They found that these defects
were mainly caused by FeS precipitation at grain boundaries,
which weakened intergranular bond strength and induced
cracking during hot rolling. This was related to a low Mn/S
ratio in the steel composition, favoring FeS over MnS
formation. Increasing the Mn/S ratio to above 20 could
signiﬁcantly reduce these defects and improve surface quality.
The study focuses on a newly developed NiAlCrFeMo high-
entropy alloy, which exhibits excellent high-temperature per-
formance but is prone to cracking due to its unique microstruc-
tural features. Given that this is a newly developed alloy, no
prior rolling experiments have been conducted on it. Therefore,
investigating its hot-rolling process is essential to understand its
behavior under deformation conditions. By conducting rolling
experiments under different conditions, including temperatures
(1000, 1050, 1100 /C176C), rotational speeds (300, 600, 900 r/min),
and reductions (5, 10, 15, 20%), the inﬂuence of the rolling
process on the microstructure and mechanical properties of the
alloy was examined. This research is particularly important as it
can provide critical insights into optimizing the processing
parameters to enhance the alloy’s performance and mitigate
cracking issues. The alloy, composed of nickel, aluminum,
chromium, iron, and molybdenum, demonstrates the diversity
and synergistic effects of high-entropy alloys. It not only
enhances high-temperature stability and oxidation resistance,
providing a foundation for applications in extreme conditions,
but also exhibits higher economic potential without the use of
precious metals, making it a promising subject for further
research.
2. Experimental Procedure
2.1 As-Cast NiAlCrFeMo High-Entropy Alloy
Using a non-consumable vacuum arc furnace, bulk Ni, Al,
Cr, Fe, and Mo metals with a purity of no less than 99.9% were
successfully melted to prepare NiAlCrFeMo high-entropy alloy
ingots. To ensure chemical homogeneity, the alloy ingots were
melted at least four times on both sides and subsequently
suction-cast into a water-cooled copper mold. The speciﬁc
ingredients are shown in Table 1. Eight alloy sheets with
dimensions of 72 9 50 9 13 mm were cut from the alloy ingot
using a wire-cutting device and subjected to hot-rolling
experiments using a twin-roll mill (model 250-280). The
XRD analysis results in Fig. 1(b) indicate that the NiAlCrFeMo
high-entropy alloy is primarily composed of FCC and BCC
phases. SEM observation shows that these two phases are
alternately distributed within the material.

3.2 Mechanical Properties of High-Entropy Alloys
3.2.1 Inﬂuence of Reduction Amounts on Phases. As
shown in Fig. 5, after hot-rolling deformation with different
reductions, the microstructure of the NiAlCrFeMo high-entropy
alloy still consists of FCC and BCC phases, which is consistent
with the phase composition of the as-cast microstructure. This
indicates that no new phases were formed during the hot-rolling
deformation processes.
Combining Fig. 6 and 7, it can be observed that as the
reduction percentage increases from 5 to 20%, the percentage
of the FCC structure ﬁrst decreases and then increases,
dropping from 66 to 53.9%, and then rising to 60.1%, whereas
the percentage of the BCC structure ﬁrst increases and then
decreases, rising from 34 to 46.1%, and then falling to 39.9%.
This can be attributed to the fact that, during the hot-rolling
process, increasing the reduction amount simultaneously alters
both the deformation and thermodynamic conditions of the
material. When the reduction amount is between 5 and 15%,
the accumulation of deformation energy and the associated
thermal effects may collectively promote the formation of the
BCC phase. However, when the reduction amount reaches
20%, the excessive deformation energy and elevated temper-
ature may destabilize the BCC phase, thereby reducing its
volume fraction (Ref 23-27).

3.3 Inﬂuence of Rotational Speeds on the Microstructure
and Mechanical Properties of High-Entropy Alloys
3.3.1 Inﬂuence of Rolling Speeds on Phases. According
to the XRD analysis results, as shown in Fig. 11, after rolling
treatment at different speeds, the NiAlCrFeMo high-entropy
alloy still maintains a dual-phase composition of FCC and BCC
phases, with no formation of other new phases. This indicates
that under different rolling speeds, the phase structure of the
alloy is relatively stable.
Combining the analysis of Figs. 12 and 13, it can be
observed that as the rolling speed increases, the volume fraction
of the FCC phase increases from 46.8 at HR300 to 67% at
HR900, while the volume fraction of the BCC phase decreases
from 53.2 at HR300 to 33% at HR900. This indicates that a
higher rolling speed is conducive to the formation of the FCC
phase and simultaneously reduces the proportion of the BCC
phase. This is because, at high rotational speeds, the strain rate
increases, and the material’s deformation energy may not be
sufﬁcient to support the stable existence of the BCC phase,
instead favoring the formation of the lower-energy FCC phase.
This change in energy balance may lead to the transformation
from the BCC phase to the FCC phase, thereby increasing the
volume fraction of the FCC phase. Additionally, the high-strain
rate may lead to more dislocation accumulation and stress
concentration, promoting the formation of the FCC phase. This
is because the low stacking fault energy of the FCC structure
makes it more likely to deform through slip under high-strain
conditions. Moreover, as shown in Table 5, with the increase in
rotational speed, the grain size decreases, which may favor the
stability of the FCC phase, as ﬁne-grained structures can reduce
grain boundary energy, thereby enhancing the relative stability
of the FCC phase (Ref 35, 36).

3.4 Inﬂuence of Temperatures on the Microstructure
and Mechanical Properties of High-Entropy Alloys
3.4.1 Inﬂuence of Rolling Temperatures on Phases. Fig-
ure 17 shows that after rolling treatment of the NiAlCrFeMo
high-entropy alloy at different temperatures, the phase compo-
sition of the alloy remains unchanged, still mainly composed of
FCC and BCC two crystal structures, and no new phase
formation was observed. This indicates that although the
change of rolling temperature may affect the microstructure and
mechanical properties of the alloy, within the selected temper-
ature range (1000, 1050, and 1100 /C176C), the phase stability of
the NiAlCrFeMo high-entropy alloy is of goodness, and no
new phase transformation or precipitation occurs.
By comparing the data from Figs. 18 and 19, it is evident
that the volume fractions of the FCC and BCC phases exhibit
contrasting behaviors with increasing hot-rolling temperature.
At 1000 /C176C (HR1000), the FCC phase accounts for 64.2% of
the volume, while the BCC phase constitutes 35.8%. As the
temperature rises to 1050 /C176C (HR5), the FCC phase volume
fraction increases to 66%, whereas the BCC phase decreases to
34%. However, at 1100 /C176C (HR1100), the FCC phase volume
fraction drops to 59.1%, and the BCC phase volume fraction
rebounds to 40.9%.
In the temperature range of 1000-1050 /C176C, it is observed
that the volume fraction of the FCC phase increases with rising
temperature, while the volume fraction of the BCC phase
decreases correspondingly. This phenomenon indicates that
within this temperature range, the FCC phase exhibits higher
thermodynamic stability and lower Gibbs free energy, making it
more favorable and prominent in the microstructure (Ref 49,
50). However, when the temperature reaches 1100 /C176C, the
volume fraction of the BCC phase increases, while that of the
FCC phase decreases. This may indicate that a reverse phase
transformation has occurred in the high-entropy alloy under this
temperature condition. The FCC phase, due to its reduced
thermal stability caused by the increase in temperature,
redissolves into the BCC matrix, while the BCC phase
reprecipitates at high temperature (Ref 35, 51-53).

and reduction amount) on the NiAlCrFeMo high-entropy
alloy, validating the feasibility of the adopted hot-rolling
process. Although a ‘‘warpage’’ phenomenon occurred
at a 20% reduction amount, there was no edge cracking
overall, indicating that the alloy exhibits good macro-
