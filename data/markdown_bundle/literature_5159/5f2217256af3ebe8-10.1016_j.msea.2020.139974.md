1. Introduction 
The medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) – a new alloy design strategy – 
contain three to four elements in equimolar or near-equimolar constit -
uents, which is similar to the high-entropy alloys (HEAs). They have 
raised wide attention in research communities owing to their promising 
mechanical behavior [ 1 – 4 ]. Compared with conventional alloys, their 
superior properties do not result from a single dominant component, 
such as iron in steel, nickel in superalloy and WC in cemented carbide, 
but rather entropically stabilized from the synergistic effects of equi -
molar multiple elements with the potential for unique excellent com -
binations of mechanical properties [ 4 – 6 ]. 
Recently, intensive studies have shown that the lattice defects 
significantly contributed to strengthening and toughening of MEAs 
[ 7 – 11 ]. Besides having very low stacking fault energy, the MEAs usually 
present face-centred cubic (f.c.c) or hexagonal-closed pack (h.c.p) lattice 
structures. Thus, the most commonly observed lattice defects, i.e. dis -
locations, staking faults (SFs), Lomer-Cottrell locks (LCs), nanotwins, 
and phase transformation, tend to form readily during materials pro -
cessing or mechanical deformation [ 8 – 10 ]. To some extent, the excellent 
strength-ductility in these alloys with such lattice defects probably re -
sults from the strain hardening capacity that has been initiated by in -
dividual or multiple strengthening mechanisms. For instance, the 
CoCrFeNi alloy enhanced the formation of nanotwin boundaries and 
then the blockage of dislocation glide during straining, which delivered 
the promising strength/elongation 1.2 GPa/13.6% [ 9 ]. Moreover, the 
high Mn content in some MEAs/HEAs could promote the 
transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) effect, which contributed to 
improving mechanical properties [ 11 – 13 ]. Compared with its perfect 
single-phase counterpart, an appropriate combination of lattice defects 
led to better strength-ductility synergy in the Fe

Moreover, previous studies have shown that HEAs/MEAs containing 
Cr, Mn, Fe, and Co atoms exhibit strong temperature dependence of SF 
energy [ 14 , 15 ]. The cryogenic temperature corresponds to the small SF 
energy. Usually, the small SF energy facilitates abundant activities of 
Shockley partial dislocations, and, meanwhile, promotes the formation 
of high-density defects (such as SFs, shear bands and nanotwins) [ 16 ]. 
Plastic deformation can be dominated by motion of Shockley partial 
dislocations and full dislocations, deformation twinning, and even f.c.c 
→ h.c.p phase transformation at lower temperatures [ 17 – 21 ]. Depend -

dislocations, twins, phase transformation and grain refinement can 
accommodate plastic flow [ 16 – 21 ]. The CrMnFeCoNi and Fe

4. Conclusions 
In summary, a large quantity of different defects (i.e. nanoparticles, 
dislocations, deformation twins, stacking faults, Lomer-Cottrell locks 
(LCs), h.c.p. bands, and amorphous bands) were intentionally engi -
neered in the Co
23
Cr
23
Ni
23
Mn
31 
medium-entropy alloy by different 
thermomechanical treatments. Compared with the as-cast single f.c.c 
phase counterpart having deficient defects, the defects-enriched 
Co
23
Cr
23
Ni
23
Mn
31 
alloy exhibited superior mechanical properties at 
room and cryogenic temperatures. At 298 K, the defects-enriched alloy 
showed yield stress of 0.74 GPa, ultimate tensile stress of 1.26 GPa and 
fracture strain of 43.9%. Moreover, the yield stress, ultimate tensile 
stress and fracture strain were increased to 1.18 GPa, 1.71 GPa and 
65.4% at 77 K, respectively. These tensile properties are otherwise 
inaccessible for their defects-deficient counterparts (400 MPa, 570 MPa 
and 64.4%) at 298 K. The enhanced mechanical properties are mainly 
attributed to the interactions of LCs and/or nanoparticles with mobile 
dislocations, similar to Orowan ’ s strengthening mechanism. Meanwhile, 
numerous nanostructures (i.e. nanotwins, h.c.p lamellae and amorphous 
bands) formed during tensile deformation at different strain levels. 
These nanostructures simultaneously generated massive new interfaces 
with their surrounding f.c.c matrix. Further, these nanostructures and 
new interfaces not only accommodate plastic flow, but also serve as 
strong barriers for mobile dislocations, resulting in the strengthening 
and toughing of materials.
