ABSTRACT 
The effects of different crystal forms of zirconia (m-ZrO
2
, t-ZrO
2
, c-ZrO
2
) on the microstructure and properties of 
tungsten alloys were investigated using pure tungsten (PW) and tungsten alloys strengthened with different 
crystal forms of zirconia. The microstructure and compressive and tensile properties of the four metals were 
comprehensively compared. The results show that the microstructure and properties of tungsten alloys 
strengthened by zirconia with different crystal forms are remarkably similar, which is attributed to the high 
similarity of the morphology and distribution of zirconia in tungsten alloys. Meanwhile, the properties of 
tungsten alloys were significantly improved compared with PW. The ultimate compressive strength and failure 
strain at room temperature increased by 50% and 97% respectively, and the ultimate compressive strength at 
high temperature increased by 15 – 30% compared with PW. The characterization of the rolled tungsten alloy 
shows that the addition of zirconia can reduce the formation of shear bands, increase the texture strength, and 
make the texture type, which is mainly composed of {111} 〈 110 〉 and {001} 〈 100 〉 , more uniform. The high- 
temperature tensile results show that the ultimate tensile strength of the tungsten alloy is approximately 30% 
higher than that of PW at 1000 
◦
C, and the dimples are dense and small compared with PW. 
1. Introduction 
Tungsten has a high melting point, high density, outstanding radia -
tion absorption capacity and high-temperature mechanical properties. It 
is an irreplaceable key material in extreme environments, such as those 
with ultrahigh temperatures and strong radiation. Tungsten is widely 
used in high-temperature heating devices, high-end medical treatment, 
weapons and equipment, large-scale scientific engineering, aerospace 
engineering and other fields [ 1 – 5 ]. However, the application of pure 
tungsten in high-end fields is limited due to its low temperature brit -
tleness, recrystallization brittleness and irradiation brittleness [ 6 , 7 ]. A 
previous report [ 8 ] mentioned that from the perspective of material 
design, second phase strengthening is an effective means to improve the 
properties of tungsten alloys [ 9 , 10 ]. However, the second phase in the 
report is mostly rare earth oxides, such as Y
2
O
3
, La
2
O
3

2

years, our team has developed and reported a series of ceramic oxide 
(Al
2
O
3

2

which are also excellent in performance. For example, the compressive 
strength of the W-0.2 wt% Al
2

3 
plate prepared by Wang et al. [ 16 ] 
reached 2224 MPa, 37.2% higher than that of pure tungsten. Cui et al. 
[ 17 ] found that the addition of zirconia can reduce the generation and 
growth of dislocation rings in tungsten alloys under gold ion irradiation, 
and increase the radiation resistance of tungsten alloys. In short, both 
mechanical properties and irradiation properties can be comparable to 
the properties of tungsten alloys strengthened by rare earth oxides, 
which is expected to replace these rare and expensive rare earth re -
sources and save national strategic resources. 
Zirconia ceramics have the advantages of a high melting point, high 
hardness and high temperature stability. As the reinforcing phase of 
strengthened tungsten alloy, we have also made various reports in the 
early stage. For example, Li et al. [ 18 ] used a wet chemical method to 
prepare c-ZrO
2 
strengthened tungsten alloy and studied and compared 
the properties of its sintered, spin-forged and annealed tungsten alloys. 
Zhao et al. [ 8 ] reported the hot deformation behavior of tungsten alloys 
strengthened by m-ZrO
2 
with different contents. However, only one

addition, the grain sizes of WT1 and WC1 are also significantly refined 
compared with PW. The specific statistical distribution of grain size is 
shown in Fig. 3 e, showing a normal distribution. The average grain size 
of the WT1 and WC1 grains is very close to approximately 5 μ m, which is 
significantly smaller than the 15 μ m of PW. The specific statistical results 
of the average grain size are shown in Fig. 3 f. After calculation, the grain 
sizes of WT1 and WC1 are reduced by 61.6% and 61.1% compared with 
PW, respectively. Similarly, the statistical results of the relative density 
of WT1 and WC1 are shown in Fig. 3 g. The results show that their 
relative density has increased from 95.6% of PW to approximately 98%. 
According to previous research and analysis results, t-ZrO
2 
and c-ZrO
2 
also improve the sintering activity of tungsten alloys and significantly 
refine tungsten grains, and the air between tungsten grains is more likely 
to repel during the sintering process. According to the statistical results 
of hardness in Fig. 3 h, the hardness of WT1 and WC1 is 391.08 HV and 
385.38 HV, which are 17.97% and 16.25% higher than that of PW, 
respectively. The comparison of grain refinement, density, hardness and 
other basic properties shows that different crystal forms of zirconia have 
almost the same effect on the tungsten alloy, and the action and 
strengthening mechanism are the same. 
3.3. Compressive tests and analysis of compression fracture morphology 
of sintered PW, WM1, WT1 and WC1 
In addition to the basic properties, the compression properties at 
room temperature were also compared. To clearly show the comparison 
effect, the compression curves at room temperature, ultimate compres -
sive strength, strain-to-failure and other data of WT1 and WC1 are 
compared with the data of WM1 and PW previously studied [ 8 ], as 
shown in Fig. 4 a, b and c. From Fig. 4 a, the trend and change rule of the 
room temperature compression curves of WT1 and WC1 are highly 
similar to those of WM1. Similar to the results of WM1, when the peak 
stress is passed, small drop points can be observed on the compression 
curves of WT1 and WC1, which are marked with green and red arrows 
respectively, indicating that cracks begin to occur during compression. 
With continuous compression until complete fracture, their complete 
fracture strain also reached an amazing 60%. The statistical results in 
Fig. 4 b show that the ultimate compressive strength of WT1 and WC1 
increased by 46.06% and 47.39%, respectively compared with PW, 
which is slightly lower than that of WM1 with an increase of 51.94% but 
at the same level. The strain-to-failure point is the corresponding strain 
point when the stress equals the ultimate compressive strength. Fig. 4 c 
shows that the strain-to-failure of the three zirconia doped tungsten 
alloys is very similar, approximately 33%, which is approximately 97% 
higher than that of PW.

reported, pure tungsten shows intergranular fracture, and there are

3.9. A discussion on the reasons for the similar effect of zirconia- 
strengthened tungsten alloys with different crystal forms 
As shown in Fig. 10 , a schematic diagram showing the refinement of 
tungsten grains by zirconia addition and its effect on crack extension is 
shown. Fig. 10 a shows PW with a relatively large grain size, and crack 
extension is characterized by intergranular fracture. Fig. 10 b shows that 
regardless of which crystal form of zirconia is doped, the tungsten grains 
can be significantly refined, and at the same time, the crack extension 
mode is changed from the original intergranular fracture to the mixed 
fracture mode of intergranular and transgranular fracture, thus 
improving the mechanical properties of the tungsten alloy. 
However, it is worth noting that the properties of zirconia- 
strengthened tungsten alloys with different crystal forms are similar in 
many aspects. What is the cause? Before discussing this issue, we need to 
determine whether zirconia in tungsten alloys has the three crystal 
forms we set. It is worth affirming that in the performance test at room 
temperature, the crystal forms of three zirconia of tungsten alloy are 
determined, which are the crystal forms we set, which have been veri -
fied by transmission and other means. However, in the test at high 
temperature, once the temperature exceeds the phase transition tem -
perature (1170 
◦
C) of monoclinic zirconia, its crystal form will change 
into the tetragonal phase. At present, there is no method to stabilize the 
monoclinic phase to high temperatures reported in the literature. 
However, the tetragonal and cubic phases doped with the yttrium oxide 
stabilizer can maintain the stability of the crystal form over the whole 
temperature range, which has been confirmed in a previous study on the 
calcination behavior of zirconia [ 31 ]. Therefore, it can be confirmed 
that at least two kinds of crystalline zirconia-strengthened tungsten 
alloy properties are compared in the high-temperature experiment. 
Based on this conclusion, the properties of tungsten alloys at room 
temperature and high temperature are still close to each other. First, it 
can be speculated that the morphology and distribution of zirconia with 
different crystal forms in the tungsten matrix are highly consistent. 
Second, as shown in Fig. 10 c, in theory, different interface structures 
may be formed between zirconia with three crystal forms and the 
tungsten matrix, forming different interface bonding strengths, thus 
changing the overall mechanical properties. However, from the 
macroscopic performance results, we can infer that the different inter -
face energies caused by the crystal form difference are weak compared 
with the fracture energy between the whole tungsten matrix, which 
leads to the approximate improvement of the zirconia crystal form for 
the macroscopic performance of the tungsten alloy.
