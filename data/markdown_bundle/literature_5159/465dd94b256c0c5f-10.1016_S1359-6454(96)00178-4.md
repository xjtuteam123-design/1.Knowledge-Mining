Abstract-Fe-Al alloys containing slightly less than 25% Al have a two-phase mixture of disordered and 
DO,-ordered phases at temperatures somewhat below 550°C. The microstructure of such alloys is similar 
to that of Ni-base y-y’ superalloys, well known for their good high temperature strength and creep 
resistance. Analysis of the high temperature strength of the two-phase FeeAl alloys suggests that their 
high temperature strength is good under conditions of slow strain rate or creep. Their deformation 
behaviour differs from that of the y-y’ superalloys, however, since it is relatively easier to shear the ordered 
particles than to bow dislocations down channels between the particles. The relatively low ductility of these 
materials seems to be related to the localization of shear by the cutting of particles rather than to 
environmental sensitivity, as suggested for FelAl alloys. Copyright 0 1997 Acta MetaNurgica Inc. 
1. INTRODUCTION 
Iron aluminide alloys containing about 2430% Al 
(throughout this report alloy compositions are given 
as atomic per cent) are known to have the DO? 
structure at temperatures below about 550°C with 
the B2 ordered structure at slightly higher tempera- 
tures, eventually disordering at higher temperatures 
still [l]. Alloys with slightly lower Al contents, in the 
range of about 20-24%, have a two-phase (disor- 
dered b.c.c. + DO, ordered) mixture at temperatures 
below about 400-550°C [ 11. A recent examination [2] 
of such two-phase mixtures, called a-a” mixtures in 
analogy to the y-y’ mixture that makes up the 
Ni-base superalloys, shows that the volume fraction, 
size and morphology of the ordered regions can be 
adjusted by controlling the annealing temperature 
and time for an alloy of defined Al content in the 
binary Fe-Al system. Further flexibility of micro- 
structural choice is available in ternary alloys, such as 
Fe-Al-% alloys [2], where it becomes possible to 
obtain a given volume fraction of ordered phase at 
any desired temperature by adjustment of Al and Si 
contents. 
Very little work has been done to examine the 
mechanical properties of such two-phase alloys, 
which may be expected to show good high 
temperature, especially creep, properties, as is found 
for the two-phase Ni-base superalloys. One study [3] 
examined the age hardening behaviour of an Fe-24% 
Al alloy as this was annealed at temperatures near 
500°C to induce precipitation and coarsening of DO3 
ordered particles but was restricted, however, to 
examinations of room temperature properties and did 
not consider behaviour at high temperatures. At high 
temperatures, the ordered particles can act as barriers 
to dislocation motion, leading to Orowan-type 
hardening when the particles are not cut by the 
dislocations or a different extent of hardening when 
the particles can be sheared by the dislocations. Such 
classical hardening models apply specifically to 
materials where the volume fraction of the particle 
phase is fairly low and dislocations encounter 
particles essentially one at a time. For the case where 
the volume fraction of ordered precipitate is very 
high, as for modern y-y’ superalloys, dislocations are 
often unable to cut the particles and are forced to 
travel down the narrow matrix channels defined by 
neighbouring particles [4]. It is of interest then to 
determine the high temperature properties of 
two-phase CI--CI” alloys and to examine the behaviour 
of dislocations as a function of the type of 
microstructure, i.e. the dislocation behaviour as 
depending on the volume fraction and size of ordered 
regions and on the separation of ordered particles or 
on the size of disordered channels separating the 
ordered regions. 
The examination of the properties of such iron 
aluminides of low Al content is of particular interest 
because of the possibility of finding better ductility 
than for the fully ordered Fe,Al or FeAl alloys. Such 
iron aluminides show fairly low ductilities, which has 
been related, at least in part, to environmental attack 
by water vapour at the tip of a crack [5]. Based on 
such ideas, it has been shown that disordered Fe-Al 
alloys with about 16%Al can show higher ductility 
and appear less sensitive to the environmental attack 
phenomenon [6. 71. The evidence of environmental

2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS 
The work discussed here has been carried out using 
a binary FeA1 alloy of composition 22.7% Al 
(atomic percent), provided in cast formt and 
homogenized by an anneal of 96 h at 1000°C. 
Samples were machined for mechanical testing after 
giving various heat treatments to ensure a variety of 
ordered microstructures. The development of micro- 
structure as heat treatments are varied has been 
discussed in detail elsewhere [2], and examples of 
typical structures will be shown later. Essentially, the 
homogeneous material was given a standard disor- 
dering treatment of 1 h at 800°C and then annealed 
to provide a fairly coarse, stabilized ordered structure 
at the same temperature as for later mechanical 
testing. At temperatures above about 500°C the 
material is fully disordered, and a 1 h treatment at the 
desired temperature, followed by a water quench, was 
used to stabilize the structure at these temperatures. 
At temperatures between about 350°C and 500°C the 
material is a two-phase CI--O~” mixture and samples 
were annealed for 10 days at the desired temperature 
to provide a stable state of order for subsequent 
testing with a coarse microstructure. This fairly 
coarse microstructure was chosen to limit the extent 
of subsequent changes taking place on heating before 
or during mechanical testing, as well as to provide, as 
for the y-y’ superalloys, a structure resistant to 
dislocation motion or cutting during high tempera- 
ture testing. At temperatures below 350°C the 
material is fully DOs ordered and samples were given 
initial ordering treatments of 10 days at 400°C 
followed by 10 days each at 35O”C, 300°C and 200°C 
stopping at the temperature where mechanical testing 
will later be carried out. 
tMateria1 was kindly supplied by Dr S. Naka of ONERA, 
Paris, France. 
Mechanical testing was carried out in compression 
over a wide range of temperatures and using a range 
of applied strain rates at each temperature. For such 
tests, the samples were loaded under argon for 
testing, and heated for a total of about 30 min before 
testing at the desired temperature. In addition, to 
examine the ductility of the two-phase materials, 
tensile testing was carried out at a strain rate of about 
8 x 10m5 SK’ in laboratory air. In some cases, tensile 
testing was carried out, also in laboratory air, over a 
range of strain rates from near 1O-6 s-l to near 
10-l s-‘, to examine the influence on strength and 
ductility. The gauge lengths of samples for tensile 
testing were carefully ground using fine abrasive 
papers before electropolishing. A few measurements 
of fracture toughness were also made, using notched 
bend samples of standard sample proportions (notch 
length, sample width, height and length). 
Microstructural analysis was carried out using 
X-ray diffraction and Transmission Electron Mi- 
croscopy (TEM) methods, to determine the fraction 
of ordered phase, its long range order parameter, and 
ordered particle dimensions-these aspects are 
reported elsewhere [2] and more specific details are 
not given here. The deformation microstructure was 
examined by TEM using thin foil samples prepared 
from compression samples, deformed about 2%, by 
fine diamond saw cutting and electropolishing using 
a twin-jet unit operating at 8 V and -30°C with a 
mixture of one part nitric acid in four parts of 
methanol. Microscopy was carried out using a Philips 
CM12 instrument operating at 120 kV. General 
observations of dislocation structures were made by 
bright field and weak beam imaging using fundamen- 
tal 400 or 220 imaging vectors (referred to the DO3 
ordered lattice), with the weak beam condition near 
g:4g used. Both grown-in and shear-produced 
antiphase boundaries or ordered particle sizes were 
examined by dark field imaging using a 111 or 200 
superlattice vector, corresponding to the DO3 and B2 
ordered lattices, respectively (it may be recalled that 
imaging using the DOs diffraction vector allows APB 
faults in both the DOs lattice and the B2 lattice to be 
observed, while imaging using the B2 diffraction 
vector shows only the APB faults of the B2 lattice). 
Fracture surfaces were examined by scanning 
electron microscopy using a Cambridge Stereoscan 
360 instrument operated in the secondary electron 
mode.

3.3. Studies of fracture surfaces and dislocation 
structures 
Typical fracture surfaces of tensile samples are 
illustrated in Fig. 6. In all cases it is clear that failure 
has occurred by rather brittle cleavage, with the 
crystallographic facets seen to be smooth apart from 
the river lines running across them. There was no 
evidence of any grain boundary influence on failure, 
with many of the grain boundaries leading to no 
visible disruption of the fracture surface. The grain 
size was about 100-200 pm and many of the 
macroscopic fracture facets seen clearly crossed grain 
boundaries without any significant change in 
appearance.

materials examined here is generally a smooth yield

5. CONCLUSIONS 
The mechanical properties of two-phase CI + a” 
(A2 + DOJ) Fe,Al materials have been studied at 
room and high temperatures, looking particularly at 
room temperature ductility and high temperature 
strength. Good strength is observed at intermediate 
temperatures when testing over a range of strain 
rates, due to the ordered cl” particles acting as 
obstacles to dislocation motion, and it is predicted 
that the creep behaviour should be excellent. 
For all test temperatures examined, it is easier for 
dislocations to cut the ordered particles than to bow 
between. There are two reasons for this: first, the 
structural scale of the materials examined is too fine 
and the spacing between the ordered particles is too 
small (of the order of 2-100 nm here); second, it is 
fairly easy to shear the particles because of the low 
APB energy, particularly as partial disordering of the 
ordered regions occurs at intermediate temperatures. 
In this respect a B2 or DO, ordered alloy, disordering 
by a second order phase change, is less suitable than 
a LIZ ordered alloy, disordering by a first order phase 
change, since partial disordering sets in at lower 
temperatures. 
At low to intermediate temperatures (300-350°C) 
the ordered particles are sheared initially by coupled 
pairs of l/4(11 1) dislocations and only later, as 
observed by TEM, do the dislocation pairs decouple 
to produce single dislocations. 
At intermediate to high temperatures (40&45O”C) 
the ordered particles are sheared by single l/4( 111) 
dislocations from the start. It is initially difficult to 
move such dislocations, trailing APB faults as they 
move, but easier to move them later, after disordering 
of the slip plane has occurred. This process leads to 
large upper yield point effects and serrations on the 
stress-strain curves during deformation. 
The shear localization which occurs due to the slip 
plane disordering leads to low overall ductility for 
these materials. 
It should be possible to improve the ductility of 
such two-phase materials by maintaining the 
dislocations coupled together as pairs by increasing 
the B2 order, without considering the DO, order. 
Such modification is not possible in the binary FeAl 
system, but is perhaps possible in ternary Fe-Al-X 
systems.
