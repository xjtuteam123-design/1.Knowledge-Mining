1. Introduction
Developing alloys with excellent strength-ductility synergy at low 
temperatures is of significant importance for the development of energy 
storage and transportation in low-temperature environments [1,2] . 
However, traditional alloys exhibit poor toughness under 
low-temperature conditions [3 – 6] . In recent years, multi-principal 
element alloys, specifically high-entropy alloys (HEAs), have attracted 
considerable interest due to their outstanding performance in physical 
and chemical properties [7 – 9] . In particular, face-centered cubic (FCC) 
structured HEAs have garnered attention for their exceptional 
room-temperature and low-temperature ductility [10 – 12] . Neverthe -
less, their yield strength (YS) still requires further enhancement.
To address these issues, numerous strengthening strategies have 
been proposed to improve the yield strength of FCC HEAs. However, 
methods such as grain refinement, precipitation strengthening, defect 
strengthening, solid solution strengthening, and short-range order 
strengthening often lead to significant reductions in ductility or limited 
strengthening effects [7,13 – 16] . Currently, heterogeneous structure 
engineering is recognized as an effective strategy to synergistically 
enhance both strength and ductility [17 – 19] . However, the heteroge -
neous structure engineering strategy typically necessitates the intro -
duction of coarse-scale domains, which makes it challenging to 
maximize strengthening effects [20,21] . Additionally, metastable 
structure engineering strategy can effectively enhance the strain hard -
ening ability of alloys by inducing significant strain-induced martensitic 
transformation (SIMT), thereby synergistically improving strength and 
ductility [22,23] . Nevertheless, metastable structure engineering often 
requires the addition of large amounts of expensive alloying elements 
such as Co, Ni, V, Al, and Ti [24 – 29,59,60] , hindering industrial appli -
cation due to high alloy costs. Therefore, developing HEAs with excel -
lent low-temperature yield strength at liquid nitrogen temperatures 
(77 K) and good ductility, while ensuring economic viability, remains a 
significant challenge.

4.2. Strain hardening mechanisms
Our alloy exhibits good strength and ductility at room temperature 
(298 K) as well as at low temperatures (173 K and 77 K). Particularly, at 
77 K, a synergistic enhancement of strength and uniform elongation is 
achieved, primarily attributed to its excellent strain hardening ability. 
As shown in Fig. 4 b, the strain hardening rate curves do not exhibit a 
monotonic decrease but instead go through stages: an initial decrease 
(stage 1), followed by an increase (stage 2), and then a subsequent 
decrease (stage 3), until necking occurs.
Firstly, for alloys tested at 298 K and 173 K ( Fig. 4 b), in stage 1, the 
rapid decrease in the strain hardening rate curve is attributed to the 
alloy undergoing elastic-plastic transition [55] . In stage 2, instead of 
continuing to decrease, the strain hardening rate curve gradually in -
creases due to continued deformation, which elicits further micro -
structural responses in the alloy. Specifically, with ongoing deformation, 
activation of slip bands, movement of dislocation, and subsequent 
activation of stacking faults (SFs) and deformation twins (DTs), leading 
to strain-induced martensitic transformation (SIMT, FCC → BCC 
martensitic phase transformation), coordinating plastic deformation, 
which can be verified by Fig. 6 and Fig. 7 a-c. In order to further deter -
mine that SIMT occurred at stage 2, we performed EBSD on the micro -
structure of the alloy at the YS point and the UTS point, using the 
specimen tested at 173 K as an example. The result shows that the vol -
ume fraction of the BCC phase at the UTS point increased sharply, which 
suggests that a significant FCC → BCC martensitic phase transformation 
occurred (shown in Supplementary Fig. S4 ). Additionally, the syner -
gistic strengthening and toughening effects of the bimodal and 
dual-phase dual-heterostructure with nanoscale precipitates ((Fe, 
Ni)
2
SiTi) contribute to a high work hardening rate while avoiding pre -
mature strain localization and necking. Although we were unable to 
clearly capture the interaction of dislocations with the 
nano-precipitations due to technical reasons, we will overcome this 
problem in our future work. Indeed, the promotion of heterostructure 
and composite toughness nano-precipitations with respect to strain 
hardening can be identified in related reports [17,21,31] . In stage 3, the

4.3. Temperature sensitivity of strain-hardening ability
Another accompanying question arises: why does the alloy demon -
strate superior strain hardening capability at lower deformation tem -
peratures (especially at 77 K)? It has been reported that deformation 
twinning occurs when the stacking fault energy (SFE) of FCC HEAs is 
between 15 and 45 mJ/m
2 
[7] . We observed deformation twins (DTs) 
and stacking faults (SFs) in our alloy deformed at room temperature 
(298 K), where SFs typically act as embryos for deformation twins [9] . 
This indicates that our alloy has moderate SFE. Moreover, since SFE 
exhibits temperature dependence, generally decreasing with decreasing 
temperature [57,58] , our alloy exhibits lower SFE during deformation at 
77 K, significantly promoting SFs formation. This explains why dense SF 
networks and L-Cs are observed during deformation at 77 K rather than 
at higher temperatures (298 K or 173 K). Additionally, the formation of 
stacking faults and L-Cs effectively suppresses strain localization at 
lower temperatures (77 K) during deformation, allowing sustained SIMT 
and thereby demonstrating excellent strain hardening ability.
4.4. Temperature insensitivity of yield strength
Although temperature-dependent changes in the stacking fault en -
ergy (SFE) had an impact on the strain-hardening ability of our alloys, 
especially at 77 K for tensile deformation, the alloy exhibited excellent 
strain-hardening ability, specifically achieving a significant synergistic 
increase in ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and uniform elongation 
(UEL) (UTS and UEL increased from 1151 MPa and 22 % at 298 K to 
1531 MPa and 28 % at 77 K, respectively). However, it is worth noting 
that although the yield strength (YS) of our alloys increased slightly with 
decreasing tensile temperature (yield strength increased from 1007 MPa 
at 298 K to 1072 MPa at 173 K to 1095 MPa at 77 K). However, the 
increase in yield strength at reduced tensile temperature to 173/77 K is 
very limited compared to that reported in literature [29,58] . It suggests 
that the yield strength of our alloy is less sensitive to tensile temperature. 
This phenomenon is mainly related to the fact that (1) the SFE of our 
alloys is not sufficiently low, resulting in the inability of the elastic 
deformation stage to efficiently form a large number of SFs / nano-twins 
/ martensite for grain refinement and inducing the dynamic Hall-Petch 
effect ( Supplementary Fig. S4 ) [58,61] , and thus reducing the defor -
mation temperature could not enhance the yield strength by effectively 
promoting the above mentioned substructures; (2) our alloy has a high 
volume fraction of the BCC phase before the tensile test (34 %, Fig. 2 c). 
It has been reported [62] that an increase in the BCC phase share tends 
to lead to an inhomogeneous distribution of stress at grain boundaries 
and grain interiors, and the stress tends to be easily concentrated at the 
FCC/BCC phase interface, which contributes to the increase in yield 
strength. However, as the deformation temperature is lowered to 77 K, 
due to the temperature-dependent increase in lattice friction stresses 
[58] , stress localization at the FCC/BCC grain boundaries becomes more 
pronounced, leading to preferential plastic deformation of neighboring 
FCC grains [62] , leading to a preemptive onset of yield, and thus 
lowering the tensile temperature is not able to achieve significant in -
creases in yield strength through homogeneous deformation as in the 
case of the FCC or near-FCC phase alloys. Therefore, the yield strength of 
our alloys is weakly dependent on temperature.
It is also because of the above two aspects that our alloys have good 
strain-hardening ability for deformation at low temperatures of 77 K. 
Firstly, due to the insufficiently low SFE and high FCC phase stability of 
our alloys, the activation of SFs / nano-twins / BCC martensite is 
delayed, which promotes the strain-hardening ability. Secondly, due to 
the presence of the FCC + BCC dual-phase heterostructure, the role of 
heterogeneous deformation-induced hardening (HDI) also promotes the 
improvement of strain hardening capacity. In addition, with the syner -
gistic effect of other favorable microstructures (shown in Section 4.2 for 
details), our alloys exhibit excellent low-temperature strain-hardening 
ability.
