Sintering and Joining of Ni-Based Superalloys via
FAST for Turbine Disc Applications
CHARIS I. LIN, SEBASTIAN J. NIUMAN, ANIL K. KULKARNI, DEREK S. KING,
JOGENDER SINGH, and NAMIKO YAMAMOTO
Turbine discs are currently made of nickel-based superalloys, known for their high strength and
creep resistance at high temperatures. Turbine discs of dual microstructure, each tailored for
diﬀerent functions, allow for performance improvement and weight savings, but current
methods of joining dissimilar nickel-based superalloys, such as friction welding, exhibit a
heat-aﬀected zone (HAZ) and localized melting at the interface, leading to weak bonding. Here,
we demonstrate that ﬁeld-assisted sintering technology (FAST) can be eﬀective in sintering
CM247LC powder to high relative density and in joining two dissimilar superalloys, CM247LC
and Inconel 718 (IN718), by diﬀusion bonding without forming a heat-aﬀected zone. The
subscale tensile testing of this FAST-joined specimen resulted in failure through both the
bonding zone and IN718; the yield strength ( ~ 348 MPa) is slighter higher than that of
FAST-processed IN718, conﬁrming successful joining by FAST.

I. INTRODUCTION
THE commercial and DoD sectors aim to double the
thrust-to-weight ratio of aerospace turbine engines, and
one technology currently pursued to increase the
thrust-to-weight ratio is a one-piece rotating turbine
system composed of turbine airfoils (single crystal,
directionally solidiﬁed, or equiaxed) connected to a
turbine disc. The whole assembly, a blisk, is made of
nickel (Ni)-based superalloy. Expected weight savings
by elimination of mechanical ﬁr-tree connectors is ~ 30
pct, leading to an increase in rotational speed and
pressure ratio per stage, and thus thrust-to-weight
ratio.
[1] In addition, elimination of the mechanical joint
can reduce vibration and associated fatigue. [2] There are
two challenges that must be addressed to achieve this
goal. First, the approach to joining airfoils with the
turbine disc must be developed to minimize any strength
penalty. Second, high-temperature properties must be
maintained. Single crystal airfoils are typically operated
at temperatures up to 1400 /C176 C.
[3] This high operating
temperature will have an impact on the operating
temperature of the turbine disc rim. Typically, the
operating temperature of a turbine disc is about 600 /C176 C
to 750 /C176 C, and the outer rim temperature may increase
with a blisk conﬁguration. Therefore, the operating
temperature and other requirements of the outer rim of
turbine discs must be addressed.
There are two objectives in this research paper, to
explore the joining of Ni-based superalloys (without any
strength penalty) and to improve the operating temper-
ature capability of the outer rim of turbine discs, which
is associated with other requirements. The outer rim
must have good creep resistance, which is associated
with large grain size, whereas the core must have high
fatigue strength and toughness, which are associated
with small grain size, as shown in Figure 1.
[4] This
illustrates that a turbine disc should ideally have a dual
microstructure. Limited research has been conducted in
the development of dual microstructure turbine discs by
suitable preferential heat treatment, i.e., heating the
outer rim to achieve large grain size and cooling the core
to retain a smaller-grained microstructure.
[5–8] The
shortcoming of this approach is that the chemical
composition of the entire disc remains the same, and
thus the outer rim of the disc will not have the needed
high thermal stability. This high thermal stability
requirement needs to be addressed.
Joining of dissimilar parts to form dual microstruc-
ture has previously been attempted by gradient heat/
energy treatment
[5–8] and by solid-state joining, but so
far resulted in limited microstructure variation,

eﬀective solid-state joining method in which compo-

in a linear or rotated manner
[9]; the kinetic energy and

far limited to aluminum alloys. [14] CMT deposition, or
cladding, of Inconel 718 (IN718) was demonstrated for
repair and coatings.
[15,16] The resulting HAZ was much
smaller ( ~ 0.5 mm) than that of traditional welding due
to the lower process temperature, but CMT resulted in
brittle Laves and niobium carbide phases in the
deposited material.
[15
The emerging theme of this research paper is to
explore an alternative approach that will oﬀer more
ﬂexibility in the development of dual microstructure
turbine discs with tailored high thermal stability by
utilizing solid-state joining—a one step process as shown
in Figure 2. For this eﬀort, ﬁeld-assisted sintering
technology (FAST) was considered. FAST is also
known by various names including spark plasma sin-
tering (SPS),
[17] plasma-assisted sintering (PAS), [18] and
pulsed electric current sintering (PECS). [19] Unlike
friction welding and CMT, FAST can potentially
achieve solid-state joining with strong interfaces of
minimum HAZ. FAST enables rapid heating rates (up
to ~ 1000 /C176 C/min) with eﬃcient volumetric Joule heating
by ﬂowing a high current ( ~ 1 to 10 kA) through the
samples in addition to axial pressure application.
[20] An
increased diﬀusion rate is uniquely achieved with pulsed
voltage discharge between powders that can promote
localized melting and rapid solidiﬁcation at their inter-
faces,
[21,22] leading to high densities. [20] With such high
diﬀusion rate, FAST requires lower temperatures and
shorter processing times than conventional sintering
methods, minimizing alteration of original microstruc-
tures within solid parts and mitigating grain growth
within powder-sintered parts.
[23,24] Diﬀusion bonding
between two components, or even functional grading, [25]
using FAST has been previously demonstrated. Joined
materials included metal alloys (titanium, tungsten,
copper, iron-based superalloy) and ceramics (silicon
carbide) and resulted in high bond strength
[26,27] and no
HAZ.[26,28–31] The diﬀusion bonding was also engi-
neered by inserting ﬁllers [27,31–33] or by component
doping.[34
In this work, FAST is investigated as a new method to
join Ni-based superalloy parts of diﬀerent microstruc-
tures: powder-sintered CM247LC with a large-grained
structure is used for the rim and a solid IN718 plate is
used for the core as illustrated in Figure 1. First,
microstructures (such as diﬀusion degree and grain
growth) and mechanical properties of powder-sintered
CM247LC using FAST are evaluated with varying
sintering conditions. A small volume fraction of HfC
was added and powder-sintered with the CM247LC to
increase the rim’s thermal stability without degrading
mechanical performance. Additives to Ni-based super-
alloys have been eﬀective to inhibit grain growth and
thus to increase hardness
[35] and to further increase
thermal stability. [36] The addition of Hf [37–41] (< ~ 1w t
pct) and associated precipitation of HfC [42–45] in
Ni-based superalloys have previously been shown to
increase ductility, creep resistance at high temperatures,
and even low-cycle fatigue resistance. Hf is often a
preferred additive over HfC due to its lower melting
temperature, thermodynamic stability, and higher dif-
fusion rate. While the focus of this paper is on HfC
additives, sintering with Hf additives is currently under-
way. Second, CM247LC powder is simultaneously
sintered and joined to a solid Inconel 718 plate using
FAST (see Figure 2). The mechanical properties of the
powder-sintered CM247LC, the FAST-processed solid
Inconel 718 plate, and their joining interface will be
evaluated in relation to their microstructures.

tensile tests at room temperature are summarized in
Table III, and the exemplary stress–strain plots are
given in the Appendix (Figure A2). First, the eﬀect of
HfC additives on the strength of powder-sintered

fractured surfaces revealed the causes of this loss of
ductility (Figure 14). Without HfC additives, under
loading, CM247LC grains ﬁrst elongated, then partially

eﬀective diﬀusion. With HfC additives, much less
elongation and porosity was observed, and intergranu-
lar crack propagation dominated, indicating weak grain
boundaries. This test result conﬁrms the minimum HfC
diﬀusion observed in Figure 10.

IV. DISCUSSION
CM247LC specimens were successfully powder-sin-
tered to > 99 pct density with minimal grain growth
using FAST. After sintering using FAST with 45 MPa
applied pressure at 1250 /C176 C, a room-temperature tensile
strength of 1347 ± 35 MPa (8 tensile specimens) was
achieved. This is a higher tensile strength than that of
previous CM247LC tensile strengths reported in litera-
ture
[57]; conventionally cast CM247LC after solution
treatment (1260 /C176 C for 2 hours) and aging (1079 /C176 C for 4
hours followed by 871 /C176 C for 20 hours) previously
resulted in a room-temperature tensile strength of 950
MPa while directionally solidiﬁed CM247LC that
underwent the same heat treatment resulted in a tensile
strength of 1170 MPa.
[57] The higher strength of
FAST-sintered CM247LC is associated with the ﬁne-
grained microstructure. It is important to mention here
that the starting powder particle size was ~ 100 lm.
After FAST sintering at 1250 /C176 C, the average grain size
was 23 lm, about four times smaller than the starting
particle size. Therefore, it was determined that during
compaction and sintering, the powder experienced
signiﬁcant deformation and recrystallization of new
grains in the short sintering cycle time, contributing to
smaller grains than the original powder particle size. As
a result, the ﬁne-grained FAST-sintered CM247LC
exhibited higher tensile strength than the cast and
directionally solidiﬁed alloy. It can be concluded that
FAST has the potential for fabrication of ﬁne-grained
Ni-superalloys from powder without the need for
melting; FAST may also be a more time-eﬀective
alternative to conventional heat treatments. However,
understanding of the resulting high-temperature prop-
erties as well as post-fabrication heat treatments of the
FAST-sintered material is needed.
Although a sintering temperature of 1250 /C176 C resulted
in excellent tensile strength, localized porosity was still
present at the edges of the sintered specimens. Thus, a
two-step sintering condition was introduced to decrease
the porosity at the specimen disc edge; higher temper-
ature increased ductility, and lower pressure allowed for
grain growth. In the future, specimen inhomogeneity
needs to be further inspected in terms of grain size and
phases, and their relations with resulting properties.
FAST co-sintering of CM247LC and HfC powders
was attempted to provide the desired microstructures of
CM247LC micro-grains with HfC phase at the grain
boundaries for increased creep resistance and ductility.
The short sintering time ( < 15 min) of FAST did not
alter the CM247LC phases, and HfC powders exhibited
a short diﬀusion range (very small interface layer of <30
nm) with the sintering condition of 1250 /C176 C and 45
MPa. The resulting microstructure as seen in
Figure 10(a) showed spherical shapes of the starting
powder, suggesting that HfC additions partially pre-
vented dynamic recrystallization and grain boundary
diﬀusion. As the amount of HfC added in this study
increased the amount of HfC that may precipitate out of
CM247LC, it is likely that the solid-solubility threshold
was surpassed. This is indeed supported by the retention
of HfC and the absence of any excess HfC dissolving
into the alloy during FAST processing, resulting in a

FAST joining of powder-sintered CM247LC material
and solid IN718 plate was successfully achieved; an
elemental diﬀusion zone ( ~ 10s of lm in length) of
gradual element transition, with some Hf-rich precipi-

parts, and the strength of joined specimens was mea-
sured to be as strong as FAST-processed IN718.
Ni-based superalloys joined using linear friction weld-

[11,12] have also been shown to exhibit tensile strength
comparable with that of the weaker component. Unlike
linear friction welding, the FAST process is not sensitive
to crystal orientation. More inspection is necessary to
evaluate element and phase transition across the bond-
line using TEM/EDS.
Meanwhile, the IN718 plate microstructure changed
grain growth and precipitate dissolution after FAST
joining, and its hardness and strength were degraded.
These property changes can be explained by the
microstructure change of IN718 during the FAST
process. Solution treatment above ~ 1000 /C176 C leads to
dissolution of the d phase,
[58,59] and solution treatment
at 1270 /C176 C has been shown to result in incipient melting
and dissolution of carbides at the prior particle bound-
aries,
[59,60] leading to large grain growth. Incipient
melting may occur at temperatures as low as 1160
/C176 C.[61] Thus, the high temperatures during the FAST
process (up to 1225 /C176 C) may have resulted in such
incipient melting and dissolution of carbides. This is
conﬁrmed through microstructural inspection; the
IN718 grain size increased from ~ 15 to ~ 310 lm (see
Figure 12(a)), and niobium carbide (NbC) and chro-

IN718,52–54] were observed to dissolve (see
Figure 12(b)). With fewer precipitates, ductility
increased and thus hardness decreased. Meanwhile, the
FCC phases of both c¢ phases and d phase[52,54–56] were
maintained after the FAST process. A post-annealing

V. CONCLUSION
In this work, the capability to sinter and join a
Ni-based superalloy using FAST was experimentally
evaluated. In summary:
/C15FAST was utilized to sinter CM247LC powders to
high relative density ( > 99 pct), and mechanical
properties exceeding the literature value (236 GPa
stiffness, 395 HV hardness, and 1347 MPa ultimate
tensile strength) were observed with sintering param-
eters of 45 MPa, 1250 /C176 C, 15 min.
/C15FAST-sintered CM247LC exhibited a much smaller
grain size ( ~ 16 to 40 lm) than the powder particle
size ( ~ 105 to 149 lm).
/C15HfC additives to CM247LC resulted in porosity
reduction and higher hardness, with a small interface
layer between the two materials (<30 nm). However,
HfC addition resulted in lower tensile strength and
ductility.
/C15FAST joining of a powder-sintered CM247LC and
solid IN718 plate was successfully demonstrated. A
sharp material interface exhibiting a small elemental
diffusion zone was observed; no heat-affected zone
(HAZ) was observed. Under tensile testing, the
joined specimen fractured through the IN718 and
bonded interface, and tensile strength was compa-
rable to that of a FAST-heat-treated IN718 plate.
