Achieving high yield strength (250-270 MPa) at elevated temperatures (250 °C and above) for precip- 
itation strengthened aluminium-copper (Al-Cu) alloys still remains a challenge for the alloy designers. 
This paper presents a systematic progression of the microstructure evolution in a binary Al-0.3Hf, ternary 
Al-0.3Hf-0.3Si and quaternary Al-2Cu-0.3Hf-0.3Si (in at%) alloys. A quantitative understanding of the role 
that Hf and Si plays in promoting ordered precipitates and the metastable Al-Cu intermetallic compounds 
θ"/ θ’ has been attempted. The initial microstructure is guided by the initial precipitation of primary 
phases, triggering the formation of Hf-rich dendrites and ﬁnally, the Si/Cu segregation at boundaries. 
Ageing of binary Al-Hf alloy results in precipitation of L1 2 ordered phase via discontinuous (cellular) 
process. Si, however, promotes a continuous precipitation process, resulting in the formation of coher- 
ent and spherical L1 2 ordered phase in the matrix. The role of these ordered precipitates in inﬂuencing 
the precipitation of θ"/ θ’ compounds in quaternary copper containing alloy was explored in detail, in- 
cluding their coarsening and mechanical properties at elevated temperatures. The compact morphology 
developed in the alloy results in yield strength of 445 ± 8 MPa at room temperature (25 °C). The yield 
strength evolves through the interaction of L1 2 and θ"/ θ’ ordered phases with the α-Al matrix. The coars- 
ening of θ’ phase was slowed down due to Hf segregation at a semi-coherent interface and led to a 
thermally stable microstructure that resulted in yield strength of 256 ± 8 MPa at 250 °C.

1. Introduction 
Since its discovery, precipitation strengthened aluminium- 
copper (Al-Cu) alloys have played an important role in aircraft and 
automobile industries [1–2] . The formation of metastable phases 
like θ"/ θ’ plates during heat treatment has resulted in alloys with 
a high strength-to-weight ratio [3] and hence, widely used for 
energy-conversion and transportation sectors [4–9] . However, the 
alloys suffer from inadequate thermal stability above 200 °C. The 
strengthening phases coarsen rapidly, resulting in a rapid reduc- 
tion in strength, limiting their applications [10–12] . Therefore, ex- 
tensive attempts are being currently made to improve the high- 
temperature (20 0-30 0 °C) capabilities of these alloys. 
Principal approaches to enhance the performance of existing al- 
loys include reducing the coarsening rate of θ"/ θ’ plates and in- 
troducing additional thermally-stable dispersoids in the Al matrix.

The addition of Mn with Zr can act as an effective stabilizer for 
θ’ plates [13–16] . Shyam et al. [13] demonstrated a higher degree 
of Mn segregation at semi-coherent interfaces than at coherent in- 
terfaces. In contrast, Zr content was higher at the corner (the re- 
gion between coherent and semi-coherent interfaces). This resulted 
in the thermal stability of precipitates up to 350 °C. Bahl et al. 
[14] showed that these classes of alloys were stable for 50 0 0 h 
at 300 °C and reported a yield strength of 190 MPa, measured at 
room temperature. Here, yield strength was not directly obtained, 
however the microhardness was measured at room temperature 
after thermal exposure and converted to a yield strength using a 
conversion factor from ref. [17] . The segregation of Mn at coherent 
and semi-coherent interfaces delays the coarsening of θ’, extends 
the time for Zr/Ti to reach the interface, and forms an L1 2 type or- 
dered structure [ 14 , 16 ]. Segregation of Mg and Sc at θ’ plate/matrix 
interface also promotes effective stabilization of plates at 200 °C, 
resulting in thermal stability for up to 10 0 0 h [18] . 
Apart from the issue of stabilizing θ’ plates, the formation of 
thermally stable ordered precipitates gained particular attention in 
the past few decades. The addition of transition elements (Sc, Zr, 
Ti, V etc.) and rare-earth elements (Y, Er, Yb) in aluminium results

in stable Al 3 Sc/Al 3 Er or metastable Al 3 (Zr,Ti,V) L1 2 ordered precip- 
itates. It offers a promising solution for developing creep-resistant 
high-strength Al-based alloys, often called aluminium superalloys 
[19–20] . The nanometre size ordered precipitates coarsen slowly at 
the operating temperature of 20 0-30 0 °C. However, above 300 °C 
the precipitates containing Sc and Er coarsen rapidly due to the 
higher diffusivity of these elements, restricting their use for high- 
temperature applications [21] . Group IV transition elements (Ti, Zr, 
Hf) are slow diffusers in Al [19] . The combined addition of Sc and 
Zr improves the recrystallization resistance and mechanical prop- 
erties such as yield strength and fatigue strength of the Al alloys 
[22–25] . The addition of Si further accelerates the ageing response 
and results in a more reﬁned and homogeneous distribution of L1 2 
ordered Al 3 Zr precipitates [26–29] . The atom probe analysis of L1 2 
ordered precipitate in Al-0.06 Sc-0.06 Zr-0.06 Si (at%) alloy reveals 
the presence of Si at the core and Zr at the interface of the ordered 
precipitate, exhibiting (Al,Si) 3 (Sc,Zr) type stoichiometry [27] . How- 
ever, the overall increment in the strength due to these ordered 
precipitates is lower than existing commercial Al alloys [ 4 , 30,31 ]. 
Combining the ordered precipitates and reducing the coarsen- 
ing of the strengthening phase like θ’ via segregation can result 
in an improvement in the performance of the alloys. Thus, a com- 
bined addition of transition elements like Zr, Nb or Hf and Cu to 
aluminium offers a possibility for developing high-strength high- 
temperature alloys [32–40] . The presence of these ordered precipi- 
tates in the matrix also enhances the precipitation kinetics of θ" 
and θ’ plates [41–42] . Yang et al. reported a ∼40% reduction in 
θ’ plate length and ∼80% increment in the number density of θ’ 
plates after 0.18 at% Sc addition to Al-1.08 at% Cu alloy aged at 250 
°C for eight hours [43] . Aiming high-temperature strength, Maki- 
neni et al. designed a three-stage heat treatment in Al-Cu-Zr-Nb 
alloys that offers promising results in terms of strength [44] . Au- 
thors claimed a yield strength of ∼460 MPa and ∼250 MPa at room 
temperature and 250 °C, respectively, owing to its unique compos- 
ite microstructure. Mondol et al. followed it by adding Zr and Nb 
in 2219 alloy, enabling a similar microstructure with improved me- 
chanical properties [41] . The slow diffusivity of Zr and its ability to 
form metastable L1 2 ordered Al 3 Zr that reﬁnes the θ’ plates was 
proposed as the main reason for the improvement of thermal sta- 
bility and mechanical properties at higher temperatures. Despite 
the above reported advantages of synergistic coupling of ordered 
precipitates like Al 3 Sc or Al 3 Er or Al 3 Zr and θ’ plates in the α-Al 
matrix in Al-Cu alloys, less effort is devoted to exploring Al alloys 
containing Al 3 Hf precipitates. The diffusivity of Hf in aluminium 
is the lowest among elements in this group [19] . Hf addition has 
several advantages over Sc and Zr, such as lower density and sim- 
ilar chemical properties and is worth exploring in the wake of de- 
veloping a coarsening resistant aluminium alloy with better high- 
temperature strength. 
This paper reports a detailed and systematic study of alu- 
minium alloys containing Hf. In binary Al-Hf alloy, ageing of cast 
alloy yields discontinuous precipitation of L1 2 ordered Al 3 Hf phase. 
Adding Si suppress discontinuous precipitation and promotes con- 
tinuous precipitation of the ordered phase. We have synthesized 
Al-Cu-Hf-Si alloy, which on heat treatment yield microstructure 
containing multiple phases of L1 2 / θ"/ θ’ and explore its room and 
elevated temperature tensile properties. 
2. Experimental methods 
2.1. Alloy preparations and heat treatment 
Four alloys with nominal compositions of Al-0.3Hf, Al-0.3Hf- 
0.3Si, Al-2Cu-0.3Si, and Al-2Cu-0.3Hf-0.3Si (all in at%) were arc 
melted on a water-cooled copper hearth under a high purity ar- 
gon atmosphere using 99.99% pure constituent elements (Sigma 
Aldrich). The melted buttons were remelted 3-4 times to obtain a 
homogeneous composition. These were further cast into 6 mm (di- 
ameter) × 70 mm (height) rods (for microstructural studies) and 4 
mm (thick) × 10 mm (wide) × 70 mm (height) slabs (for tensile 
measurements) using water chilled split copper mould in a vac- 
uum suction casting unit. The compositions, provided in Table 1 , 
were obtained by scanning large areas using an electron probe 
micro-analyzer (JEOL, JXA-8530F) equipped with both energy and 
wavelength-dispersive spectrometer (EDS and WDS) and a ﬁeld 
emission gun (FEG). 
The Al-Hf, Al-Hf-Si and Al-Cu-Hf-Si cast rods/slabs were sub- 
jected to isothermal ageing at 400 °C. The alloys were water 
quenched, and Vickers micro-hardness tests were performed to 
monitor the ageing effect between each ageing step. The Al-Hf-Si 
cast alloy was additionally aged at 450 °C. After the initial ageing 
treatment, the Al-Cu-Hf-Si cast alloy was brieﬂy kept at 530 °C to 
dissolve segregated copper and subsequently aged at 190 °C [44] . 
Each heat treatment step was terminated by water quenching. 
2.2. Microstructural Characterization 
The microstructural characterization of cast and heat-treated 
alloys was conducted in the scanning electron microscope (SEM) 
equipped with a W source and operated at 20 kV in backscat- 
tered mode (SEM Quanta 200, Thermo Fischer Scientiﬁc). A stan- 
dard metallographic polishing procedure has been adopted. The 
specimens were polished on SiC papers (from 800 to 30 0 0 grades), 
followed by cloth polishing with 0.25 μm diamond paste to achieve 
a mirror ﬁnish. The grain sizes of all alloys were measured by Elec- 
tron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) analysis using a dual-beam 
SEM/FIB system (Helios NanoLAB 600i, Thermo Fischer Scientiﬁc). 
The diffraction studies and precipitate evolution during age- 
ing was quantitatively characterized using a transmission elec- 
tron microscope (TEM) equipped with LaB 6 ﬁlament and oper- 
ated at 200 kV (TecnaiTM T20-ST, Thermo Fischer Scientiﬁc). The 
atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark-ﬁeld scanning transmis- 
sion electron micrographs (HAADF STEM) were obtained using a 
ﬁeld emission microscope (TITAN Themis G2 60-300, Thermo Fis- 
cher Scientiﬁc) operating at 300 kV. The composition of precipi- 
tates (spherical and plates) was obtained by EDS elemental map- 
ping using a super-X detector in STEM nanoprobe mode in TITAN. 
The number density and diameter of the spherical precipitates 
were estimated by averaging data from more than 250 precipitates 
acquired from different grains. HAADF-STEM images were used to 
estimate the length/thickness of θ" and θ’ plates. The foil thick- 
ness was estimated by capturing convergent beam electron diffrac- 
tion (CBED) patterns for volume fraction estimations. Specimens 
for TEM studies were prepared by cutting 3 mm disc from aged 
samples and mechanically polished up to ∼25 μm using a spec- 
imen disc grinder (Model 160 Specimen grinder, Fischione make) 
to obtain uniform thickness. It was followed by ion milling using a 
precision ion polishing system (PIPS II, GATAN) to obtain electron 
transparent specimens. 
The atomic-scale compositions for different precipitates were 
determined by atom probe tomography (APT) using a LEAP TM 
50 0 0XR (Cameca). APT needles were prepared in a dual-beam FIB 
(Scios, Thermo Fischer Scientiﬁc) and adopting a new procedure 
published elsewhere [45] . The specimens for APT were run in laser 
mode at 25 K with a pulse repetition rate of 120 kHz and a laser 
pulse energy of 60 pJ with a target detection rate of 0.5%. The data 
reconstruction was performed using the IVAS TM 3.8.4 software.

3. Results 
In Al-Hf binary diagram, the equilibrium solubility of Hf in alu- 
minium is 0.18 at% [46] . The ordered intermetallic Al 3 Hf initially 
precipitates in the liquid on cooling. It undergoes a peritectic re- 
action with the melt ( ∼99.93 at%) to form α-Al with Hf solubility 
of ∼ 0.19 at% at 662 °C [45] . The solubility of Hf reduces with a 
decrease in temperature. It has negligible solubility at room tem- 
perature. However, non-equilibrium solidiﬁcation can increase the 
solubility, thereby increasing the volume fraction of the Hf com- 
pound in aluminium alloys. In the present study, we suction cast 
all our alloys in a copper mould under identical conditions that 
enhance the rate of solidiﬁcation.

3.2. Ageing of Al-Hf alloys 
3.2.1. Al-Hf and Al-Hf-Si alloy 
Fig. 2 (a) shows the Vickers micro-hardness number (VHN) as a 
function of isothermal ageing time for Al-Hf binary alloy at 400 
°C and Al-Hf-Si ternary alloy at 400 and 450 °C. The hardness of 
Al-Hf alloy increases gradually from 31 ± 2 VHN at 1 h to 49 ±
2 VHN after 100 h of ageing. For Al-Hf-Si alloy aged at 400 °C, 
the microhardness changes from 42 ± 1 VHN at 1 h to a steady- 
state value of 63 ± 3 VHN after 10 h. At 450 °C, the peak hardness

at 400 °C for 30 h. The selected area diffraction (SAD) pattern

3.2.2. Ageing of Al-Cu-Hf-Si alloy 
The Al-Cu-Hf-Si cast alloy was aged in three stages, as men- 
tioned in Section 2.1 . Fig. 4 (a) shows the plot of the microhard- 
ness variation with the time of the cast alloy directly aged at 400 
°C. The hardness increases from 81 ± 4 VHN at 2 h to 89 ± 3 
VHN after 10 h and after that, remains stable. Fig. 4 (b) shows a 
dark ﬁeld microstructure using a ¯1 01 superlattice reﬂection from a 
sample aged for 10 h. The inset shows the SAD pattern taken along 
the [010] Al zone axis. The regions depleted with precipitates mark 
the inter-cellular region. Fig. 4 (c) shows the coherent, spherical, 
nanometre-sized ordered precipitates distributed uniformly within 
the cells. The histogram of precipitate size distribution can be ﬁt- 
ted to a log-normal distribution ( Fig. 4 (d)). The mean diameter of 
the precipitates was 8 ± 2 nm. 
After ageing at 400 °C, the alloy was brieﬂy solution treated at 
530 °C for 15 min to dissolve copper segregates. The temperature 
was chosen below the Al-Cu eutectic point to avoid incipient melt- 
ing of metastable Cu-rich phases present at the cell boundaries. 
The evolution of microhardness with time is shown in Fig. 5 (a). Ta- 
ble T2 summarizes the compositions measured in the cellular and 
inter-cellular regions after solution treatment. Fig. 5 (b) exhibits the 
centered bright-ﬁeld micrograph along [001] Al zone axis showing 
the precipitation in the inter-cellular region during solution treat- 
ment. However, the number density of the precipitates was low. 
Fig. 5 (c) shows a high-magniﬁcation dark-ﬁeld micrograph with 
110 superlattice reﬂection near [001] Al revealing the presence of 
L1 2 ordered spherical precipitates with a mean size of 11 ± 4 nm 
within the cells ( Fig.5 (d)). Elemental maps using WDS of Al, Cu, Hf 
and Si after 15 min of solution treatment suggest that the Cu and 
Si were distributed homogeneously in the α-Al matrix. In contrast, 
Hf is preferentially partitioned to the cells (supplementary Fig. S5). 
The solution treated Al-Cu-Hf-Si alloy was further aged at 190 
°C to precipitate θ" and θ’ plates in the matrix. The ageing curve 
is shown in Fig. 6 and compared with that obtained for the Al-Cu- 
Si alloy. The peak hardness achieved for Al-Cu-Hf-Si alloy was 141 
± 3 VHN at 10 h. In contrast, the Al-Cu-Si alloy reaches a peak

alloy shows better performance, ∼15% higher hardness than Al-Cu- 
Si alloy. On further ageing at 190 °C, the hardness for Al-Cu-Hf-Si 
alloy drops moderately and stabilizes after 30 h, whereas Al-Cu-Si 
alloy shows a continuous drop in hardness. The measured hardness

and 95 ± 2 VHN, respectively.

4.2. Evolution of precipitates during ageing 
4.2.1. The ordered L1 2 precipitates 
The suction casting of Al-Hf binary alloys resulted in super- 
saturation of Hf in the dendritic regions. Similar supersaturations 
were reported earlier during the rapid solidiﬁcation of binary Al- 
Hf alloys [53–54] . Ageing at 400 °C, discontinuous precipitation of 
metastable L1 2 ordered Al 3 Hf phase was observed ( Fig. 2 (c)) in Al- 
0.3Hf alloy. Similar precipitation of Al 3 Hf phase were reported in 
rapidly solidiﬁed Al-Hf alloys when aged at 450 °C [53–54] . Hori 
et al. also observed discontinuous precipitation after ageing at 450 
°C (10 0 0 h) in an Al-6 wt% Hf alloy produced by splat quenching 
[55] . Nes and Biladal reported similar discontinuous precipitates 
in rapidly solidiﬁed Al-Zr alloys and termed them as fan-shaped 
precipitates [56] . Discontinuous precipitation dominates because of 
its low energy barrier compared to continuous precipitation. The 
moving grain boundary aids in reducing the Hf concentration by 
discontinuous precipitation from highly supersaturated Al-Hf alloy 
during ageing.

The minor addition of Si ( ∼0.3 at%) effectively inhibits the grain 
boundary reaction or discontinuous precipitation and stimulates 
the continuous precipitation reaction. After ageing at 400 °C, the 
homogeneous distribution of ordered spherical precipitates was 
observed in Al-Hf-Si alloy ( Fig. 2 (e)). STEM-EDS analysis indicates 
that the L1 2 precipitates were richer in Si than the α-Al matrix. 
Si is known to promote the precipitation kinetics of L1 2 phase 
[27] . Hori et al. observed that the addition of Si accelerates the 
precipitation kinetics of metastable Al 3 Hf precipitates [57] . Subse- 
quently, Hallem et al. studied the effect of Si on Al-Hf alloys but 
did not observe the uniform or dense precipitation [58] . However, 
a new phase called the ’S’ phase with stoichiometry close to Al- 
HfSi was reported. The formation of the S phase occurred when 
Si content in an alloy was above 0.15 wt% ( ∼0.14 at%). Such inter- 
metallic phases were not observed in the present study (Si ∼ 0.3 
at%). Michi et al. studied the effect of Si/Fe additions on Al-0.08Zr- 
0.08Hf-0.045Er at% alloy and found that the Si accelerates the age- 
ing response of L1 2 ordered precipitates [59] . The APT results show 
the precipitates contain ∼0.90 at% of Si and ∼4.5 at% of Hf, leading 
to a stoichiometry (Al,Si) 3 (Zr,Hf,Er) [59] . 
The present study indicates the promotion of continuous pre- 
cipitation accompanied by the suppression of discontinuous pre- 
cipitation due to Si addition. It could be attributed to the following 
factors 
i. The nucleation of discontinuous precipitation is governed by 
the boundary reaction and utilizes the energy from the bound- 
ary. The WDS analysis (Fig. S1) shows the segregation of Si at 
the inter-dendritic boundaries in Al-Hf-Si alloy. Segregation of 
Si results in the reduction of inter-dendritic boundary energy. 
It raises the critical energy required for discontinuous precipita- 
tion and thermodynamically inhibits the precipitation reaction. 
ii. Another possible factor that may be happening is the cluster- 
ing of Si atoms. Water quenching from 400-450 °C helps to 
trap vacancies in the matrix. The possibility of solute cluster- 
ing around quenched-in vacancies was documented in Al-Mg-Si 
and Al-Cu-Mg alloys during the early stages of decomposition 
by performing positron annihilation lifetime spectroscopy [60–
61] . Although a signiﬁcant part of Si goes at the inter-dendritic 
boundary, Si is still present in the appreciable quantity in the 
Al matrix. The measured concentration of Si in the dendritic 
region is close to ∼0.1 at% obtained by WDS point analysis. 
Therefore, there exists a possibility of forming Si-vacancy clus- 
ters during the early stages of ageing treatment that could later 
act as the nucleation sites for (Al,Si) 3 Hf precipitates. The argu- 
ment is supported by the ﬁrst-principles calculation performed 
by Peng et al. that showed the Si-Si-Va clusters have favourable 
binding energy (0.14 eV) in aluminium [62] . A similar effect was 
reported in Al-Sc-Er-Zr-Si alloy, where Si stimulates the precipi- 
tation of Al 3 (Zr,Sc,Er) precipitates and in Al-Mg-Sc alloys, where 
Mg helps to nucleate Al 3 Sc precipitates [26] . Nasim et al. also 
observed the high diffusivity of Si results in the formation of 
GP zones that act as potential sites for the precursors of the 
spherical nano precipitates [63] . 
4.2.2. Synergistic effect of (Al,Si) 3 Hf and θ "/ θ ’ plates 
The addition of Cu to Al-Hf-Si alloy resulted in an equiaxed 
grain structure with an average grain size of ∼9 μm. The concen- 
tration of Hf in the dendritic cells was higher due to the higher 
cooling rates achieved during suction casting. Ageing at 400 °C 
results in the formation of dense and continuous precipitation of 
metastable L1 2 ordered (Al,Si) 3 Hf precipitates. Solution treatment, 
followed by ageing, results in the dissolution of Cu into the α-Al 
matrix. The solution treatment at 530 °C increases the Cu concen- 
tration by 0.43 at% in the matrix. Further, the ordered precipitates 
coarsen by ∼3 nm within 15 min of solution treatment. A similar 
observation was also reported by Makenini et al. for Al 3 Zr precipi- 
tates in Al-Cu-Nb-Zr alloy [44] and Mondal et al. for Al 3 (Sc,Zr) pre- 
cipitates in 2219ScZr alloys [64] . On ageing at 190 °C, θ"/ θ’ plates 
precipitate in the matrix. The (Al,Si) 3 Hf precipitates remain un- 
affected during this step of ageing. Therefore, this step involves 
the nucleation and growth of plate-shaped precipitates only. The 
prior presence of these ordered precipitates remarkably increases 
the number density and reduces the diameter of plates during age- 
ing at 190 °C. L1 2 ordered precipitates in the matrix suppress the 
homogenous nucleation of plates and concomitantly enhance the 
heterogeneous nucleation. The high number density of the order 
of 9.5 × 10 18 and 2.9 × 10 18 per m 3 were observed for θ" and 
θ’ plate-precipitates, respectively in the peak-aged Al-Cu-Hf-Si al- 
loy. The number density of θ’ plates in Al-2.5 wt% Cu alloy was

4.2.3. The θ ’ plates and its coarsening kinetics 
In Al-Cu-Hf-Si alloy, the ageing results in precipitation of θ’ 
plates which are major contributors to the strength of the al- 
loy both at room and elevated temperatures and presented in 
section 3.3 . The coarsening kinetics of these plates, therefore, play 
an important role, and the presence of Hf and Si is expected to 
inﬂuence such a process. In the following, we examine the coars- 
ening kinetics of Al-2Cu-0.3Hf-0.3Si alloys at 250 °C and the role 
of Hf and Si.

above 200 °C was one of Al-Cu alloys’ major challenges for in- 
tended industrial applications at elevated temperatures [69] . The θ’ 
plates in binary Al-Cu alloys transform to the equilibrium θ phase 
at elevated temperatures ( > 250 °C) and deteriorate the strength of 
the alloy [70–71] . The present study reveals the θ’ plates in Al-Cu- 
Hf-Si peak-aged alloys were thermally stable during exposure at 
250 °C and exhibited negligible coarsening compared to Al-Cu-Si 
and binary Al-Cu alloys ( Table 3 ). The EDS composition analysis of 
θ’ plate in Al-Cu-Si alloys exposed for 100 h revealed the partition 
of Si to the θ’ plate. Therefore, the delay in coarsening of θ’ plate 
in Al-Cu-Si alloys compared to Al-Cu alloys could be due to the 
partitioning of Si to the θ’ plate. In Al-Cu-Hf-Si alloys, both Hf and 
Si partitioning to the θ’ plate was detected. The dramatic reduc- 
tion in coarsening kinetics of θ’ plate in Al-Cu-Hf-Si alloys (refer 
to Fig. 13 ) could be attributed to the partitioning of low diffusing 
Hf element along with Si.

In the result section we have observed bright spots at the semi- 
coherent interface of the θ’ plates in the Al-Cu-Hf-Si peak-aged al- 
loys exposed at 250 °C for 500 h ( Fig. 11 ). The intensity of the 
HAADF column is proportional to the square of atomic number Z 
[48] . Hence it may be taken as evidence of Hf segregation. How- 
ever, recently several studies dealt with segregation of solutes at 
the interface of matrix and θ’ plate at high temperatures [ 14 , 16 , 49- 
51 , 72-73 ]. Chisolm et al. [49] have provided compelling arguments 
that bright spots at the semi-coherent interface are due to ﬁlled 
copper column in the alloy Al-Cu-Zr-Mn-Sc. All the evidences and 
simulations presented are acquired looking at the semi-coherent 
interface at [110] direction. No such bright spot due to copper col- 
umn has been reported when image is taken along [100] direc- 
tion. Since Hf is much heavier atoms, we expect it to show up with 
bright intensity irrespective of how we look at it. Thus, our result 
may indicate presence of Hf atoms. Their presence at the semi- 
coherent interface can act as a kinetic barrier due to its low diffu- 
sivity in the α-Al matrix and may hinder the coarsening along the 
length of the θ’ plate. We note that the lengthening rate have re- 
duced drastically, almost ∼65 times compared to Al-Cu alloys, then 
the thickening rate ( ∼31 times). Shin et al. performed the density 
functional theory (DFT) calculations of the interfacial segregation 
energies of various solutes segregating at θ’ plate/Al-matrix coher- 
ent and semi-coherent interfaces. Fig. 11 (b) summarises the results 
for Cu, Hf, and Si segregation energies relative to their segregation 
at different positions from the semi-coherent interface (0 being 
the interface). Hf shows favourable segregation behaviour at the 
semi-coherent interface, conﬁrming the HAADF-STEM observations 
in the present study (refer to Fig. 11 (a)). Si also tends to segre- 
gate near semi-coherent interface but being next to Al, but it was 
diﬃcult to detect in the Z-contrast STEM micrograph. The segrega- 
tion of Hf at the semi-coherent interface lowers the interfacial en- 
ergy and reduces the lengthening rate of θ’ plates (refer to Eq. 2 ). 
A similar observation was made in Al-Cu-Mn-Zr alloys exposed at 
300 °C for 200 h, where Mn segregation at semi-coherent inter- 
faces was considered to lower the interfacial energy and restrict θ’ 
plates against coarsening [13] . Jiang estimated the reduction in in- 
terfacial energy due to Mn segregation at semi-coherent interface 
by 72.6 mJ/m 2 after exposure at 280 °C for 24 h for Al-Cu-Sc-Zr- 
Mn alloys [51] . Transition elements like Sc or Zr were reported to 
segregate at coherent interface of θ’ plates [ 16 , 37 , 74 , 75 ] affects the 
thickening rate by decreasing interfacial energy ( ∼0.18 at% Zr seg- 
regation reduces interfacial energy by 3.52 mJ/m 2 ). However, the 
present study reports the possibility of segregation of Hf at semi- 
coherent interface, that can act as an effective barrier to the coars- 
ening of θ’ plates. 
The solute vacancy interactions also played a signiﬁcant role in 
coarsening to happen [76] . The Cu atoms have to migrate through 
Al matrix from one plate to another, which requires vacancies 
to be available in the matrix. Hf and Si strongly bind with va- 
cancy, resulting in the depletion of free vacancies available for Cu 
[62] . First-principles calculations showed the Si-Si-Va clusters have 
higher binding energy (0.14 eV) than the Cu-Si-Va cluster (0.07 eV) 
and Cu-Cu-Va cluster (0.06 eV) [62] . The solute atoms (Hf or Si) 
that reach the plate interface also bring vacancies. These vacancies 
were readily absorbed by the θ’ plate coherent interface, resulting 
in the binding of solute atoms at the interface [76] . Thus, the pres- 
ence of these slow diffusing solute atoms acts as a kinetic barrier 
due to the solute drag effect and delays the coarsening of plates 
[13] . 
Therefore, the improved thermal stability of Al-Cu-Hf-Si and Al- 
Cu-Si peak-aged alloys during exposure at 250 °C can be described 
by the delay in coarsening of θ’ plates due to the partitioning of 
solutes (Hf and Si) to the θ’ plate, segregation of Hf atoms at the 
semi-coherent interface of θ’ plate, and the vacancy solute bind- 
ing in the Al matrix. The thermal stability of the microstructure in 
Al-Cu-Hf-Si peak-aged alloys was also reﬂected in the tensile mea- 
surements carried out at 250 °C. The yield strength of the Al-Cu- 
Hf-Si peak-aged alloy at 250 °C was 256 ± 8 MPa, 60% higher than 
that reported for commercial 2219-T851 alloys (refer to Fig. 12 (d)). 
In a recent work by Li et al. [77] the existence of Si along with 
Sc inside θ’ plates reported to effectively reduces coarsening at 
300 °C and enhances strength of Al-Si-Cu-Sc alloys. Chen et al. 
[74] showed by ﬁrst principle calculations that the segregation of 
Zr (or Sc) enhances the fracture resistance of interface resulting 
in strengthening of interfaces. Since, Zr and Hf has similar chem- 
ical behaviour, it can be safely assumed that segregation of Hf 
enhances the interface strengthening which positively affects the 
overall strengthening of alloy.

5. Summary 
1. The suction casting of binary Al-0.3 Hf alloy results in the su- 
persaturation of Hf during casting. The addition of Si and then 
Cu reﬁne the microstructure. Cu segregates at the cell bound- 
aries during casting. 
2. Ageing of alloys at 400 °C resulted in the precipitation of L1 2 
ordered (Al,Si) 3 Hf metastable phase. In binary Al-Hf alloys, the 
grain-boundary assisted discontinuous precipitation occurs. The 
addition of Si promotes continuous precipitation of coherent 
L1 2 ordered precipitates in the α-Al matrix. EDS line analy- 
sis reveals the strong partitioning of Si to the L1 2 ordered 
precipitates. The APT analysis reveals the stoichiometry to be 
(Al,Si) 3 Hf. 
3. Al-Cu-Hf-Si alloys were subjected to multistage heat treatment. 
The ﬁrst step, ageing at 400 °C, results in the formation of L1 2 
ordered (Al,Si) 3 Hf precipitates in the α-Al matrix within cells. 
The average size of the ordered precipitate was 8 nm. Subse- 
quently, the aged alloy was treated at 530 °C to dissolve the 
excess Cu in the matrix without affecting the (Al,Si) 3 Hf ordered 
precipitates. An increase in the average diameter from 8 to 11 
nm of L1 2 ordered precipitates were observed during this stage. 
The last step, ageing at 190 °C, results in the precipitation of θ" 
and θ’ plates. The TEM and APT results show the heterogeneous 
nucleation of θ" and θ’ plates on pre-existing L1 2 ordered pre- 
cipitates. The composition analysis from APT reveals the parti- 
tioning of Hf and Si to the θ" and θ’ plates. The presence of 
slow diffusing Hf atoms slowed the growth of plates and aided 
in stabilizing the microstructure. 
4. Synergistic coupling of (Al,Si) 3 Hf precipitates and θ"/ θ’ plates 
improved room temperature tensile properties due to the 
enhanced precipitate strengthening effect. The theoretical 
strength estimated due to the formation of L1 2 ordered 
(Al,Si) 3 Hf precipitates, θ" and θ’ plates in the matrix was ∼ 323 
MPa. 
5. The microstructure developed in the present study improves 
the coarsening resistance of the θ’ plates during thermal expo- 
sure at 250 °C. The higher thermal stability of the microstruc- 
ture was reﬂected in the tensile properties measured at 250 °C. 
HAADF-STEM EDS analysis shows the partitioning of Hf and Si 
to the plate precipitates. The presence of Hf and Si solute in 
the matrix affects the Cu diffusion, which aids in the delay of 
coarsening. The observed segregation of Hf atoms at the semi- 
coherent interfaces results in the decrease of the interfacial en- 
ergy and thus, resists the θ’ plates against lengthening. 
6. Orowan strengthening was the primary strength mechanism. 
The remaining strengthening was due to stress-free transforma- 
tion strains (SFTS), solid solution and grain boundary.
