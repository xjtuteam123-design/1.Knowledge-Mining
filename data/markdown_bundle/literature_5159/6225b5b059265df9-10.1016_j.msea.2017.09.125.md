Abstract 
This paper studied the role of bimodal -grained st ructure in strengths and 
asymmetry behaviors of Mg-15Gd-1Zn-0.4Zr (wt.%) alloys under the as-extruded, 
compressive-yielded and fractured conditions . The bimodal -grained structure 
consisting of fine recrystallized grains and coarse un -recrystallized grains f ormed 
when the alloys were extruded with relatively low temperature. Compared with the 
equiaxed structure, the bimodal -grained structure po ssessed higher tensile strength 
attributed to the fine recrystallized grains plus the strong and hard un-recrystallized 
texture with its basal planes nearly parallel to the loading direction . However, the 
bimodal-grained structure weakened the ultimate compressive strength, because the 
un-recrystallized texture is favorable for tensile twining in the compressive test . 
Eventually, the aged sample with the bimodal -grained structure exhibited ultra -high 
tensile yield strength and ultimate tensile strength of 465 MPa and 524 MPa ,

1. Introduction 
Magnesium (Mg) alloys with excellent specific strength s are promising 
lightweight structural material in automobile and aircraft industries [1-3]. However, 
their relatively weak strengths are bottlenecks restricting the widespread industrial 
applications. This situation makes it pretty urgent to improve the mechanical 
performance of the Mg alloys for the wide applications [4-6]. In the past few years , a 
series of high-strength Mg-Gd(-Zn) alloys have been systematically developed by 
taking advantages of the strong precipitation hardening e ffect of Gd, due to t he very 
sharp decrease in equilibrium solid solubility of gadolinium (Gd) in Mg (from 23.5 
wt.% at the eutectic temperature 548 °C to only 5.0 wt.% at 250 °C and 3.8 wt.% at 
200 °C ) [7-11]. Besides, the minor Zn addition can further strengthen the alloys 
through introducing basal precipitates and long period stacking ordered (LPSO) 
structure [2, 10, 12, 13]. 
Hot extrusion is widely applied in producing Mg components with higher 
strength and better ductility [13-18], because the process can greatly refine grains and 
reduce casting defects by dynamic recrystallization (DRX) [14, 16, 19] . The yield 
asymmetry is another important factor in determining the application environment of 
the wrought Mg components, besides the strength and ductility. Particularly, low

## Page 4

asymmetry is effective in widening their industry applications. The wrought Mg -RE 
(including Mg -Gd) based alloys totally show much lower compressive-tensile yield 
asymmetries generally less than 1.2 [16, 20] due to their weak er textures than 
traditional Mg-Al-Zn series alloys with compressive-tensile yield asymmetries above 
1.6 [20-23]. 
Furthermore, both the strength and yield asymmetr y of the Mg -Gd based alloys 
are greatly associated with extruded microstructure which is sensitive to the extrusion 
parameters, especially the temperature. For example s, if an alloy is extruded at 
temperature beyond the required one for DRX , there will be equiaxed extruded 
structure a nd a progressive increase in the grain size during the grain growth step 
[24-26]; and oppositely, if the temperature is not high enough, only part of the grains 
will recrystallize and the bimodal-grained structure consisting of fine 
dynamic-recrystallized (DRXed) grains and coarse un -dynamic-recrystallized 
(unDRXed) grains will form [16, 27, 28] . These two cases may lead to different 
mechanical performances including strength, ductility and yield asymmetries. 
In some cases, t he bimodal-grained structure is one of the main contributors for 
the excellent mechanical properties. For instance, a high-strength 
Mg-10Gd-5.66Y-1.62Zn-0.65Zr (wt.%) alloy wi th the obvious bimodal-grained 
structure was developed via indirect extrusion and ageing to have the tensile yield and 
ultimate strengths of 473 MPa and 542 MPa, respectively [16]. Meanwhile, the 
bimodal-grained structure is likely to decrease the asymmetry of the Mg -Gd based 
alloys as the Mg-10Gd-5.66Y-1.62Zn-0.65Zr (wt.%) alloy’s asymmetry (1.11) is quite 
low even in the Mg -Gd based alloys and the reason was suggested to be absence of 
twins. However, it lacked enough explanations why the twining did not occur in the 
coarse grains with strong textures [16]. 
Although the bimodal -grained structure shows positive effects on the s trength 
and yield asymmetry , only a few researches and few detailed characterizations have 
been done on the bimodal -grained structure in the Mg -Gd-Zn series alloys, especially 
during mechanical tests. The strengthening and decreasing asymmetry mechanisms o f 
the bimodal -grained structure have not been clear yet until now. Hence, the

## Page 5

microstructure evolution of the bimodal -grained structure in the Mg -Gd-Zn series 
alloys need more detailed investigations. 
In our present work, we changed the extrusion temperatu res to get different 
extruded microstructures (from the bimodal -grained structure to the equiaxed 
structure) in the Mg -Gd-Zn-Zr alloys. The microstructures, including the twining, of 
the samples under the as-extruded, compressive-yielded and fractured conditions were 
characterized by the electron back scattered diffraction (EBSD) to reveal the 
microstructure evolution of the extruded samples during the deformations. The 
twining occurred mainly after the yield points in the bimodal -grained structure, which 
makes the twining little affect yielding and leads to quite low yield asymmetries. 
Besides, the present results help us well establish the relationships between the 
extrusion parameters, microstructures and mechanical properties. 
2. Experimental procedure 
An alloy ingot with a nominal composition of Mg-15Gd-1Zn-0.4Zr (wt.%) was 
produced with raw materials of pure Mg, pure Zn, Mg -87 wt.% Gd and Mg -30 wt.% 
Zr master alloys. The alloy was melt in an electric resistance furnace permeated with 
the shielding gas mixed with 99 vol.% CO 2 and 1 vol.% SF 6 and then cast into a s teel 
mold with dimensions refe rred to [29]. Then the as -cast ingots were solution -treated 
at 500 °C for 2 h and then at 520°C for 12 h, followed by air cooling, which decreased 
the area ratio of the secondary compounds to only 3.7% [29]. Before extrusion, the 
solution-treated billets were machined into cylinders with 60 mm in diameter and 80 
mm in height. After that, all the billets were preheated at ~490 °C for ~1 h and the n 
extruded with an extrusion ratio of about 9:1 and a ram speed of 2 mm·s -1. The 
extruded bars have stick-shape with a diameter of about 20 mm. Table 1 shows the 
detailed extrusion parameters of the samples. To control the actual extrusion 
temperature, t he die temperature s varied from low temperature (LT , ~320 °C) to 
middle temperature (MT, ~370 °C) and then to high temperature (HT, ~420 °C). The 
higher die temperature means the higher actual extrusion temperature as all the other 
extrusion parameters wer e nearly the same. For clarification, the three groups of the 
samples were denoted as the LT, MT and HT samples, respectively.

The specimens for microstructure observation were mechanical ground, polished 
and then etched in the acetic-picral solution (4.2 g picric acid, 10 ml acetic acid, 80 ml 
ethyl alcohol and 10 ml water). After that, the samples were observed on the scanning 
electron microscope (SEM, FEI Nova NanoSEM 230 with a backscatter electron 
(BSE) detector). The electron back -scattering diffracti on (EBSD) data were also 
acquired using the FEI Nova NanoSEM 230 microscope. T he data were analyzed 
using the Oxford Channel 5 software to get the orientation maps, Schmid factor maps, 
inverse pole figures (IPF), Schmid factor distributions, crystal orientations , 
misorientation angles and recrystallized distributions. 
To make use of the precipitation strengthening, part of the as-extruded samples 
were aged in an oil bath at 200 °C, and t he ageing time for the as -extruded samples 
was the time when the hardness reached the peaks. The Vickers hardness distribution 
on the longitude section of the extruded bar was tested using a HVS -30P digital 
metallic Vickers hardness tester and the test parameters were 49 N load and 15 s 
dwelling time . The Vickers hardness values are the averages of at least five 
measurements. 
To reveal the effects o f the bimodal -grains and equiaxed grains on the 
mechanical properties , the as-extruded and aged LT, MT and HT samples were 
tensile-stretched and compressed, and the index “yield asymmetry” was calculated by 
dividing the compressive yield strength (CYS) by the tensile yield strength (TYS) , 
namely, the compressive -tensile yield asymmetry . Billets were electrical discharge 
wire c ut into tensile specimens and compressive cylinders. The tensile specimens 
(dimensions with gauge section 10 mm in length, 3.5 mm in width and 2 mm in 
thickness, referred to [11, 29]) were tensile tested using a Zwick/Roell Z100 machine 
equipped with an extensometer at a strain rate of ~0.001 s -1 (a constant tensile speed 
of 0.5 mm·min-1). The compressive cylinders (dimensions with 5 mm in diameter and 
10 mm in height ) were compressed on a Zwick/Roell Z20 machine at a strain rate of 
~0.001 s-1 (a constant compressive speed of 0.5 mm·min -1). The loading directions in 
both the tensile and compressive tests were parallel to the extrusion direction. As the 
extensometer could not be applied on the compressive tests , the original co mpressive

strains obtai ned from the crossbeam contained both the strains of the samples and 
machine holder. Hence, we fixed the compressive strains of the samples based on the 
corresponding tensile curves, as the Young modulus of each sample should be nearly 
the same in both tensile and compressive conditions [25]. The tensile yield strength 
(TYS, offset = 0.2%), ultimate tensile strength (UTS), tensile elongation (TEL), 
compressive yield strength (CYS, offset = 0.2%), ultimate compressive strength (UCS) 
and compressive elongation (CEL) were averaged from at least three parallel tests. 
3. Results 
3.1 As-extruded Microstructures 
The cast ing microstructures and phase constitutions of the Mg-Gd-Zn series 
alloys have been well documented in [11, 29, 30] . The lamellar and blocky phases 
which have been proved to be the LPSO and (Mg, Zn)3Gd phases in the literature [29]. 
The rod-like particles are quite similar to the reported Zn-Zr compounds [31-33]. Fig. 
1 shows the BSE -SEM micrographs o f the as-extruded LT, MT and HT samples 
observed along the directions parallel and normal to the extrusion direction. The main 
changes among the three groups of the samples lie in the morphologies and 
distributions of the grains and the LPSO structure. 
First, both the MT and HT samples were nearly fully recrystallized and show the 
equiaxed structure, while the LT sample has obvious bimodal -grained structure 
consisting of very fine DRXed grains and relatively coarse unDRXed grains 
lengthened along the extrusion direction . The similar bimodal-grained structure has 
been reported in Mg alloys [16, 27, 29] . With increasing extrusion temperatures and 
recrystallization degrees (i.e. from the LT to the MT, and then to the HT samples), the 
samples witnessed a progressive increase in the DRXed grain size during the grain 
growth step. The LT sample has t he finest grains because the grain growth of the 
DRXed grains were suppressed by the large ratio of the unDRXed grains [16, 28, 29], 
while the HT sample has the coarsest DRXed g rains whose grain sizes are relatively 
difficult to control due to quite aggressive growth [24-26]. 
Secondly, the lamellar LPSO structure in the present Mg -Gd-Zn-Zr alloy also 
exhibited its evolutions. In the LT sample, the LPSO structure occupied the unDRXed

## Page 8

grains (Fig. 1a and 1b), and the DRXed grains are clear inside viewed along the 
extrusion direction (Fig. 1b). In the MT sample , most of the DRXed grains are also 
very clear inside (Fig. 1c and 1d ), proving that most of the LPSO structure actually 
dissolved during the recrystallization, as reported in the literature [29]. Furthermore, 
the ratio of the DRXed grains containing the LPSO structure greatly increased in the 
HT sample. Meanwhile, different from the LPSO structure ben t in the unDRXed 
grains of the LT sample, the LPSO structure in th e DRXed grains of the HT sample is 
quite straight (Fig. 1e and 1f). The comparison indicates that the LPSO structure in 
the HT sample formed in the grain growth step after the recrystallization step. In other 
words, the LPSO structure dissolved during the recrystallization step and precipitated 
again in the following grain growth step. 
Fig. 2 shows the EBSD orientation maps and IPF of the as-extruded LT, MT and 
HT samples , with the schematic diagram of the observation plane inserted at the 
bottom. The grain distributions in the orientation maps match well with those shown 
in the BSE -SEM micrographs in Fig. 1. The average grain size of the fine grains in 
the LT sample is only 1.6 μm , while those in the MT and HT samples are 2.5 μm and 
4.1 μm, respectively, which means that the higher extrusion temperature actually 
resulted in coarser DRXed grains. 
Besides the grain size, the samples also have r emarkably different textures 
according to the IPF in Fig. 2 . The LT sample has the strongest textu re with an 
intensity of 9.61 in the three samples , which show a typical fiber texture (i.e., c -axes 
vertical to the extrusion direction) of wrought Mg alloys. When it comes to the fully 
recrystallized MT sample in Fig. 2b , the texture is quite weak with an intensity of 
only 1.96. However, the orientations (c -axes) of the equiaxed grains still have a 
tendency to be parallel to the extrusion direction , which is so-called rare-earth texture 
in the literature [34]. The HT sample has similar orientations but a stronger intensity 
(4.96) compared with the MT sample. The comparison ind icates that the c-axes of the 
DRXed grains tend to be parallel to the extrusion direction in the grain growth step. In 
fact, similar textures have also be found in other fully recrystallized Mg-Gd-Y-Zr 
alloys [34]. Hence, the texture of the as -recrystallized grains in the Mg -Gd based

## Page 9

alloys is very weak but becomes stronger with increasing grain growth degree. In 
other words, the equaixed structure has a preferred orientation parallel to the extrusion 
direction. 
Fig. 2 also provides the corresponding Schmid factor maps and distributions , in 
which a darker color means a lower Schmid factor and a harder grain orientation for 
the basal slips. As the basal slips are predominant among all the slip systems in Mg 
alloys, especially at room temperature [35], low Schmid factors are closely associated 
with strong room temperature strength ening. The average Schmid factors of the 
as-extruded LT, MT and HT samples along the extrusion direction are 0.23, 0.30 and 
0.27, respectively. Hence, the textures in the LT and HT samples provid ed stronger 
texture strengthening effects than that in the MT sample. Meanwhile, according to the 
Schmid factor maps in Fig. 2a, the texture of the unDRXed grains in the LT sample 
are relatively hard for the basal slips. 
To further clarify the bimodal -grained structure, the microstructure of the LT 
sample was separated based on the grain size. Fig. 3a and Fig. 3b show the EBSD 
orientation maps, recrystallized maps and IPF of the coarse and fine grains, 
respectively. The red color standing for the deformed g rains accounts for 97% in the 
recrystallized map in Fig. 3a. Hence, the coarse grains are mainly unDRXed grains.

3.2 Age Hardening Response 
Fig. 4 illustrates the age hardening curves of the LT, MT and HT samples , and 
the corresponding key features were summarized in Table 2 . The curve of the 
un-extruded sample which was preheated at ~490 °C for 1 h after the solution 
treatment was also shown in Fig. 4 for comparison , as it represents how much 
precipitate hardening capacity the alloy has before the extrusion. It can be seen that 
the Vickers hardness of the prehe ated sample, increased from 85 HV to 126 HV after 
being aged at 200 °C for 64 h, with an increment of 41 HV. After the LT, MT and HT 
extrusions, the as-extruded hardness increased to 103 HV, 100 HV and 98 HV, 
respectively, mainly because of the grain refin ement. The aged LT, MT and HT 
samples (at 200 °C for 32 h ) exhibited relatively higher Vickers hardness of 129 H V, 
134 HV and 137 HV. However, their peak hardness increments decreased to 26 HV, 
34 HV and 39 HV, respectively. The smaller increments of the hardness indicate that 
the dynamic precipitation occurred during the extrusions and the precipitation amount 
increased with decreasing extrusion temperature. Meanwhile, the precipitation process 
was accelerated by the extrusions as the time to reach p eak hardness was shortened 
from 64 h to 32 h, whatever the extrusion temperature is.

3.4 Tensile-stretched and Compressed Microstructures 
Fig. 6 shows the BSE-SEM micrographs of the tensile -stretched and compressed 
to fractured LT and HT samples , and it can be seen that twins marked by the red 
broken lines appeared in the unDRXed grains because coarse grains are helpful for 
the twining [38, 39]. Fig. 7 illustrates the orientat ions of the grains containing twins 
and the misorientation angles between the twins and matrix grains , in which the 
orientations of the unDRXed grains are found to be roughly normal to the extrusion 
direction. To be mainly noticed, the twins appear ing in the compressed LT sample 
exhibited more quantity and wider sizes than those in the tensile-stretched LT sample. 
This difference is ascribed to the different formation thresholds between the tensile 
and compressive twins. In the deformed LT sample, the tensile tests during which the 
c-axes of the unDRXed grains were compressed will result in the compressive twins 
with misorientation angles of ~56° [40-42] (Fig. 7a), while the compressive tests will

## Page 13

result in the tensile twins with misorientation angles of ~86° [40-42] (Fig. 7b). In Mg 
cells, the tensile twins form much more easily than the compressive twins [38, 43, 44]. 
Therefore, it is reasonable that the more and wider twins appear in the compressed LT 
sample, and t he twins finally resulted in lower UCS than UTS under both the 
as-extruded and aged conditions as the twins themselves can result in crack nucleation 
[11, 45] . However, the CYS and TYS of the LT samples are quite close, which 
indicates the twins affected little on the yield strengths. 
To further clarify the role of the twining on the mechanical properties, the 
as-extruded LT samples were unloaded at different compressive plastics strains and 
the BSE-SEM microstructures are shown in Fig. 8. Few twins can be observed in the 
LT samples compressed to plastic strains of 0.05%, 0.11% and 0.25% as shown in Fig. 
8a, b and c. However, a large number of twins appeared in the unDRXed grains of the 
LT sample compressed to fracture as shown in Fig. 8d. This implies that the tensile 
twining mainly occurred after the yield point (0.2% plastic strain). Considering the 
texture unfavorable for the tensile twining in the tensile t est [46] and the close CYS 
and TYS of the LT samples , few twins is expected to form in the LT sample when 
tensile-stretched t o the yield point, either. Furthermore, because precipitation goes 
against twining [11], the twins should not form before the yield point in the aged LT 
sample, either. Therefore, it is reasonable to suggest that twining nearly did not 
happen until the LT samples were compressive-yielded, and affected little on the TYS 
or CYS. The fact that the twining occurred only after the yield point is important for 
explaining the relationship between the bimodal -grained structure and the mechanical 
properties including the strengths and yield asymmetry, which will be detailed 
discussed in Section 4. 
As for the HT samples, the majority of the twins are the tensile twins after both 
the tensile and compressive tests considering the misorientation angles of ~86° (Fig. 
7c and 8d ), b ecause the ten sile twining occur s more easily than the compressive 
twining [38, 43, 44]. According to both the basal planes marked in blue lines in Fig. 6 
judged by the LPSO structure [47, 48] and the s chematic Mg cells shown in Fig. 7c 
and d, the c-axes of the matrix grains containing the twins are about parallel to the

## Page 14

tensile loading direction while about normal to the compressive loading direction, 
which are favorable for the tensile twining in the respective tests [46]. Namely, the 
twining occurred in the grains with suitable orientations in the HT samples . 
Considering the textures shown in Fig. 2b and 2c, the MT and HT sample have more 
grains whose orientations align parallel rather than normal to the loading directions, 
which makes the tensile twining form more easily in the tensile test than in the 
compressive test. It is responsible for the weaker UTS than the UCS of the HT and 
MT samples. Meanwhile, the stronger texture in the HT samples widened the gaps in 
the UTS and UCS than the MT samples. 
4. Discussion 
4.1 Evolution of twining in the bimodal-grained structure during the mechanical 
tests 
As been provided in Fig. 3a and Fig. 7, the unDRXed grains have strong textures 
favorable for the tensile twining during the compressive tests. However, the twining 
did not occur before the compressive yield point but strongly occurred in the coarse 
unDRXed grains before the fracture, as shown in Fig. 8. Considering the very random 
texture of the DRXed grains ( Fig. 3 b) compared with the unDRXed grains’ strong 
texture ( Fig. 3 a) unfavorable for the basal slips, the basal slips should active more 
easily in the fine DRXe d grains than in the coarse unDRXed grains. Therefore, the 
fine DRXed grains in the bimodal -grained structure undertake the major deformation 
firstly. Meanwhile, the basal slips have much lower critical resolved shear stress 
(CRSS) than that of the tensile twining [49, 50]. Considering the very small grain size 
and random texture of the DRXed grains, basal slips in the DRXed grains can be more 
easily activated than the twining in the unDRXed grains, which makes the fine 
DRXed grains have a higher priority over the unDRXed grains on undertaking the 
deformation by basal slips instead of twining . Furthermore, as the fine DRXed grains 
can undertake the major deformation firstly, the unDRXed grains take little duty to 
deform and few twins will form at small deformation degree. The above fact can be 
proved by the reported phenomenon that t he twining even did not obviously occur in 
the extruded Mg -10Gd-5.66Y-1.62Zn-0.65Zr (wt.%) sample compressed to fracture

## Page 15

[16]. However, when the basal slips in the DRXed grains cannot satisfy the increasing 
deformation, the unDRXed grains will have to take part of the deformation duty. As 
the basal slips are hard to be active in the unDRXed grains because of the texture 
(c-axes) normal to the loading direction as shown in Fig. 3a, twining will be forced to 
happen in the unDRXed grains, which leads to the results shown in Fig. 7a, 8b and 9d. 
If the twining occurs at the beginnin g of the mechanical tests, the twining leads 
to subsequent deformation and brings the yield point (0.2% in plastic strain) forward 
in strength. Meanwhile, the twins can be the crack nucleation and degrade the strength 
and ductility [11, 16, 45, 51] . Hence, the twining, if existing, will str ongly weaken 
both the yield and ultimate strength s and elongation. The fact that the twining 
occurred only after the yield points is quite important for the effects of the 
bimodal-grained structure on the mechanical property and yield asymmetr y, which 
will be discussed in Section 4.2 and 4.3, respectively. 
4.2 The role of the bimodal-grained structure in the mechanical properties 
The bimodal -grained structure consists of the fine DRXed grains with very 
random textures and the coarse unDRXed grains with rela tively strong texture s. The 
quite fine DRXed grains can certainly provide a strong grain boundary strengthening 
effect on both the tensile and compressive properties , and their quite random textures 
can minimize the difference in the tensile and compressiv e properties. However, the 
effects of the unDRXed grains are much more complicated lying in three aspects. 
Firstly, the coarse size of the unDRXed grains normally decreases the mechanical 
properties according to the Hall -patch relationship [52, 53] . Secondly, the ir strong 
textures which are hard for the basal slips can strengthen the samples as the loading 
directions are parallel to the extrusion direction. Thirdly, according to the favorable 
loading directions for tensile and compressive twining [46], the strong textures of the 
unDRXed grains are favorable for the tensile twins in the compressive tests. 
Therefore, the effects of unDRXed grains on the mechanical properties need to be 
discussed in details. 
During the compressive tests, it was observed from Fig. 8 that the tensile twining 
did not occur before the yield point . Thus, the CYS is mainly affected by the grain

## Page 16

size and texture instead of the twining. In terms of the as-extruded samples as shown 
in Table 3, the texture intensities and CYS values are both in the following orders: 
MT < HT < LT. The HT samples with the coarser grains and the stronger textures 
exhibited stronger CYS than the MT samples, which means the hard texture 
overweighed the coarse grains. Therefore, i t is reasonable to sugge st that the hard 
texture in the LT sample is a more significant contribution to the strength than the 
grain size . With the help from the quite fine DRXed grains, the bimodal -grained 
structure strengthen ed the properties more effecti vely than the equiaxed structure. 
However, after the yield points in the compressive tests, the twins present more easily 
because the bimodal -grained structure is more favorable to the occurrence of the 
tensile twining due to the textures of the unDRXed gr ains. I n this situation , the 
DRXed and unDRXed grains p erform oppositely on the compressive strength , and 
twining becomes the most decisive factor among the three main factors (i.e. grain size, 
texture and twining). Therefore, the bimodal-grained structure became harmful to the 
compressive strength as a result of a large number of twins , and the LT sample 
exhibited the weakest UCS. 
If no twining occurs during the tensile tests, the advantages of the strong and 
hard textures of the unDRXed grains will overweigh the disadvantages of their larger 
grain size. Combined with the strengthening effect of the fine DRXed grains, the 
bimodal-grained structure is also beneficial to the tensile yield strength. In contrast, if 
the twining occurs, the negative effects of the twins on the mechanical properties are 
closely associated with the number and sizes of the twins . The texture orientations 
made the twins in the tensile-stretched LT and HT samples to be the compressive and 
tensile twins, respectively, which suggests the twining occurred more easily in the 
equiaxed structure than in the bimodal -grained structure as the tensile twin s form 
more easily than the compressive twin s [38, 43, 44] . Hence, there must be more 
tensile twins in the equiaxed structure when the compressive twins appear in the 
bimodal-grained samples. The more twins are more harmful to the strength of the 
equiaxed sample. Combining the less twins with the grain boundaries strengthening of 
the fine DRXed grains and the texture strengthening of the unDRXed grains, the

## Page 17

bimodal-grained structure performs well than the equiaxed structure in the tensile 
tests, which resulted in the LT samples’ highest UTS of 423 MPa and 524 MPa in the 
samples under both the as-extruded and aged conditions. 
Meanwhile, the texture favorable or unfavorab le for the twining in the 
mechanical tests should also be responsible for the differences in the elongations. The 
reason why the aged LT sample exhib ited the highest TEL but the lowest CEL is that 
its texture is unfavorable for the tensile twining in the tensile test but favorable in the 
compressive test, as the twins can degrade ductility by providing crack nucleation [11, 
16, 45, 51] . Besides, the as -extruded LT sample has the lowest TEL is suggest to be 
caused by the most precipitates forming in the ageing treatment in the LT, MT and HT 
samples, according to Fig. 4 and Table 2. 
Table 4 compares the microstructures and tensile properties between the present 
studied Mg-15Gd-1Zn-0.4Zr (wt. %) alloy and some reported high-strength wrought 
Mg alloys [16, 17, 28, 54] . Note that compressive properties were not compared as 
data are rarely reported in references . It is shown that the bimodal-grained alloys 
generally possess superior tensile properties to the equia xed alloys [16, 28] , even 
though the latter ones have higher concentrations of alloy elements or more complex 
producing process such as cold rolling before the ageing treatment [17, 54]. Besides, 
the bimodal -grained alloys generally have better ten sile ductility than the equiaxed 
alloys. Hence, producing Mg alloys with the bimodal-grained structure is an effective 
way to develop high tensile strength Mg alloys. The effects of the bimodal -grained 
structure may strongly depend on the grain distributions including the grain size and 
unDRXed ratio. However, how to refine the DRXed grain size and control the ratio of 
the un DRXed grains to optimize the bimodal -grained structure still needs further 
investigations. 
In short, t he above discussions reveal the relationships between the 
microstructures and strengths , which can guide prepare components for different 
application purposes by simply adjusting the extrusion parameters. For example, 
when the component faces stronger tension than compression, low extrusion 
temperature, ratio and speed unfavorable for the DRX [24-26] can be employed to

## Page 18

produce the bimodal-grained structure with stronger tensile strength. In contrast, when 
the sample needs to be resistant to compression, relatively high extrusion temperature, 
ratio and speed may be better choices because the prepared equiaxed structure has 
stronger compressive strengths. 
4.3 The role of the bimodal-grained structure in the asymmetry behaviors 
In the general opinion, homogeneous structure with weak texture is usually 
associated with a low yield asymmetry [20]. However, the bimodal -grained structure 
(i.e. the LT samples) in this work shows a considerable posit ive effect on decreasing 
the yield asymmetry as illustrated in Table 3. The main reason is that few twins form 
before the yield points in the mechanical tests. When the plastic strain performed to 
the LT sample with the bimodal-grained structure is small (e.g. 0.25% in Fig. 8c), the 
very fine DRXed grains can firstly undertake the deformation task by slips as 
discussed in Section 4.1 . The same performances in the tensile and compressive tests 
is generated by the relatively weak textures of the as -recrystallized grains. However, 
when the fine DRXed grains cannot satisfy the plastic strain, the unDRXed grains will 
be forced to deform and the twining will occur (Fig. 8d), as also discussed in Section 
4.1. The twining behaviors can also explain the stronger UTS than UCS of the LT 
samples, while their TYS and CYS are nearly the same, as the yield strengths are 
mainly affected by the slips and rotations of the fine DRXed grains rather than the 
twining. Hence, the quite low yield asymmetries resulted from the fine DRXed grains 
with weak textures in the bimodal-grained structure. 
When it comes to the equiaxed structure, the textures increased the yield 
asymmetries. The textures of the MT and HT samples shown in Fig. 2b and c are 
more favorable for the tensile twining in the tensile test than in the compressive test. 
Hence, both the MT and HT samples have stronger CYS than TYS and the relatively 
high compressive-tensile yield asymmetries as provided in Table 3 . The hi gher 
compressive-tensile yield asymmetries of the HT samples whose as -extruded texture 
intensity is 4.38 compared with 1.96 of the MT sample are another strong evidence 
for the influence of the textures. Besides, t he coarser grains of the HT samples which

## Page 19

is also helpful for the form ation of the twins [38, 39] can further enhance the texture 
strengthening effects. 
The reported high-strength Mg-10Gd-5.66Y-1.62Zn-0.65Zr (wt.%) alloy with the 
bimodal-grained structure has CYS and TYS of 419 MPa and 400 MPa, respectively, 
under the as -extruded condition, and 524 MPa and 473 MPa , respectively, under the 
aged condition [16]. The compressive-tensile yield asymmetries (1.05 and 1.11) are 
also lower than those of the equiaxed -grained MT and HT sam ples in this work, 
which also proves the decreasing yield asymmetry effect of the bimodal -grained 
structure. Hence, the bimodal -grained structure can effectively decrease yield 
asymmetry than the equiaxed structure , ma inly due to the quit e fine DRXed grains 
undertaking deformation firstly by slips . Considering the alloys in the present work 
and literature [16] with the bimodal -grained structures exhibited low but different 
yield asymmetries, th e effects of the bimodal -grained structure on the yield 
asymmetries is suggested to be associated with the ratio and size of the unDRXed and 
DRXed grains. The relationship needs further investigation in future researches. 
5. Conclusions 
The Mg-15Gd-1Zn-0.4Zr (wt.%) alloy was extruded with low temperature (LT), 
middle temperature (MT) and high temperature (HT), respectively. The LT sample has 
the bimodal-grained structure consisting of both coarse and fine grains, while the 
other two samples are equiaxed. Th e coarse and fine grains in the bimodal -grained 
structure were separately characterized and analyzed by EBSD. The tensile and 
compressive properties with the loading directions parallel to the extrusion direction 
were tested . And the compressive-yielded and fracture d microstructures of the 
bimodal-grained structure are characterized to reveal the effects of the 
bimodal-grained structure on the mechanical strength and compressive-tensile yield 
asymmetry. The following conclusions were obtained: 
(1) The bimod al-grained structure resulted in stronger tensile yield strength 
(TYS) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) due to the strong texture strengthening of 
the unDRXed grains and grain boundary strengthening of the fine DRXed grains. 
However, the bimodal -grained structure weakened the ultimate compressive strength

## Page 20

(UCS), because the unDRXed texture is favorable for the tensile twining in the 
compressive tests. 
(2) Both the as-extruded and aged LT samples with bimodal -grained structure 
exhibited the lowest compressive-tensile yield asymmetries of 0.98~0.99 compared to 
the equiaxed samples, which is attributed to that the twining was suppressed in the 
bimodal-grained structure before the yield points . Although the texture of the 
unDRXed grains was favo rable for the tensile twining in the compressive tests, the 
fine DRXed grains have a priority over the unDRXed grains on undertaking the 
deformation by basal slips instead of twining , which suppressed twining before the 
yield points in both the tensile and compressive tests. 
 (3) The aged LT sample with the bimodal-grained structure exhibited ultra-high 
TYS of 465 MPa and UTS of 524 MPa, respectively, while the aged HT sample with 
the equiaxed structure has high CYS of 500 MPa and UCS of 520 MPa, respectivel y. 
The mechanical properties strongly depend on the extrusion temperature, which 
allows us to produce Mg alloys components for different application purposes by 
simply adjusting the temperature and obtaining objective microstructure.
