High creep strength, dispersion-strengthened iron aluminide
prepared by multidirectional high-strain forging

Abstract
A high-temperature forging technique was used to impart very high-strain to a cast iron aluminide containing a coarse distribution of
borides. The resulting material retains a coarse grain size with a reﬁned dispersion of ﬁne boride particles. This material shows reasonable
room temperature ductility with excellent creep strength up to temperatures >700 /C176C. The high-temperature strength is analysed by con-
sidering the material as a composite of hard cell walls and dispersion-strengthened near-cell regions, together with soft cell interiors.

1. Introduction
Iron aluminides have been intensively examined as poten-
tial structural materials in engineering systems, such as in
power generation, where good oxidation/corrosion resis-
tance combined with acceptable strength and ductility can
make these alloys interesting [1–4]. Despite years of research
activity, the application of such materials remains elusive,
however, essentially because room temperature ductility
and creep resistance at high-temperatures are insuﬃcient.
Ductility is low for alloys containing large amounts of alu-
minium and can be somewhat improved by microstructure
control, especially by grain reﬁnement [4–7]. Creep strength
has been examined in many alloys which have been strength-
ened by the incorporation of carbides [8–10], borides [11] or
intermetallic particles [12–17,9,18–22], as ﬁne precipitates
[12–14] or as-cast coarser particles [15–17,9,18–22], and also
by additional ordering [23] or phase decomposition [24,25].
Improvements in creep strength have been shown to be lim-
ited owing to insuﬃcient thermal stability of precipitates or
to phase decompositions during extended high-temperature
exposure [10,12–14,24,25], or owing to the coarse nature of
as-cast second phases arranged as a brittle network
[11,16,18,19]. Some attempts have been made to reﬁne such
coarse as-cast particle dispersions by thermomechanical
working (e.g., Ref. [26]), but these attempts have not proved
to be especially successful.
Microstructural reﬁnement by severe plastic deforma-
tion has been extensively studied over the past two decades
[27,28], but this has mostly concentrated on reﬁnement of
the alloy grain size using techniques such as high-pressure
torsion testing or equal channel angular pressing. Multiax-
ial warm pressing or forging has also been attempted as a
processing method for imposing relatively large strains
[29,30], again with the intention of reﬁning the matrix
microstructure. Matrix grain reﬁnement can lead to
increased ductility and room temperature strengthening
[31], but this is not particularly interesting for high-temper-
ature creep strength. A further problem is the diﬃculty of
processing iron aluminides, owing to their high strength
and relatively low ductility. Second-phase particles can be
expected to fracture and reﬁne during severe plastic defor-
mation (e.g., Refs. [32,33]), but very high-strains are
required for extensive particle fragmentation and for
achieving relatively homogeneous particle distribution [34].

The present study examines the eﬀects of very high-
strains imposed on iron aluminide by repeated forging car-
ried out at high-temperature. The high-temperature is
essential to ensure that forging loads are suitably low and
the material is ductile enough to avoid cracking. High-tem-
perature ensures that dynamic recovery and recrystalliza-
tion occur during processing, and a large grain size is
retained, desirable for good creep resistance [35,36]. The
function of the high imposed strain is to ensure fragmenta-
tion and dispersion of the as-cast cellular arrangement of
stable second-phase particles and generate a microstructure
as similar as possible to that obtained by mechanical alloy-
ing after subsequent annealing for grain coarsening [35,36].
The use of high-strain forging on bulk material would also
imply that material fabrication costs would not approach
the prohibitive values associated with mechanical alloying
powder metallurgical methods.
2. Experimental details
The material used was induction melted from pure ele-
ments in alumina crucibles under Ar atmosphere. Parallel-
epiped samples of width and thickness 25 mm and length
50 mm were obtained by drop casting into a Cu mould.
Alloys had a typical columnar grain structure from the out-
side to near the centre, where a small region of equiaxed
grain structure formed. The grain width of the columnar-
grained region was /C24 200–300 lm, similar to the grain size
in the equiaxed–grained region, with a ﬁner cellular segre-
gation structure inside. The nominal alloy composition was
Fe–28%Al–5%Cr–1%Nb–2%B (at.%) with metal contents
conﬁrmed by large area energy dispersive spectroscopy
(EDS) carried out in a scanning electron microscope.
Forging was carried out at 1150 /C176C, following tests at
1000 /C176C and 1100 /C176C which showed immediate fracture or
eventual fracture at increasing strain levels. Forging was
carried out using a high-speed press which imposed a strain
of /C24 0.1 per blow in /C24 1 s (an initial piston speed of
3m ms
/C0 1, slowing to a stop at the imposed strain). An ini-
tial height reduction of 0.3 was imposed (with three blows),
which led to a width increase of /C24 0.15 and a length
increase of the same amount. The sample was quickly
rotated 90 /C176and the procedure repeated (the new height
reduced by 0.3 in three blows), leaving the sample with a
square cross section of slightly reduced size. At the end
of this cycle, the sample had accumulated an equivalent
(von Mises) strain of 2 /C2 0.36 (=0.72), while the ﬁnal
cross-sectional area reduction was half this amount. This
procedure was repeated many times, until accumulated
strains of four and six were achieved, when the samples
were long bars of small square cross section. The accumu-
lation of strain during the forging procedure involves the
superposition of some redundant or reversed straining,
and in this sense is similar to equal channel angular press-
ing where following passes modify or reverse some previ-
ous strains. When the sample had cooled signiﬁcantly
during the forging processing (after two to three forging
cycles), it was returned to the furnace and reheated to
1150 /C176C for a total time of 10 min. At the end of straining,
the sample bars were reheated a ﬁnal time, again for 10 min
at 1150 /C176C, to ensure recrystallization, and plunged into a
sand bath to ensure slow cooling. Sample bars are referred
to as forged-4 or forged-6 throughout this paper, indicating
the total accumulated forging strain. Recovery of the
deformation microstructure and recrystallization occur
quickly at this temperature, presumably during the forging
process itself as well as during any preheating and subse-
quent heating, and essentially the same grain size was
found in all materials forged to strains >2, as discussed
later.
Samples were machined from the forged bars for tensile
testing at room temperature, compression testing from
room temperature to 700 /C176C, and for constant stress tensile
creep testing. For tensile and compression testing, an initial
strain rate of 2.8 /C2 10
/C0 4 s/C0 1 was used. Some of the high-
temperature compression tests were repeated, with the
strain rate jumping between this value and 2.8 /C2 10
/C0 5

operating. Details of the creep testing procedure have been
presented elsewhere [32] and involved testing on a machine
with an Andrade lever arm to maintain constant tensile
stress, with samples preheated for a total time of 1 h at
the test temperature before loading. Creep strain and strain
rate were monitored continuously by a data acquisition
system. Some tests were allowed to continue to sample fail-
ure, while others were stopped after reaching the minimum
creep rate for microstructural examination of dislocations
and particle arrangements. For these tests, the furnace
was removed, and the sample cooled quickly under load,
reaching temperatures <500 /C176C (where dislocation recovery
becomes very slow) within 2–3 min.
Samples for structural examinations were prepared by
twin-jet electropolishing using a 20% perchloric acid in
methanol mixture at /C0 30 /C176C and /C24 30 V. Microstructures
were examined by SEM using a JEOL 6500 instrument
and by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) using a
JEOL 2010 instrument, both equipped with EDS systems
for local chemical analysis.
3. Results
3.1. Microstructural evolution during forging
The as-cast material has a columnar grain structure
/C24 200–300 lm wide containing a cellular segregation struc-

/C24 30 lm, and showed a weak h110 i texture along the bar
axis. A very similar grain size and texture was found after
forging to strains >2, with the forged-4 and forged-6
materials being almost identical in this respect. This grain

3.3. Microstructural analysis
Typical starting microstructures examined by optical
microscopy and SEM were presented earlier. More detailed
information on deformation processes and the role of sec-
ond-phase particles was obtained by TEM examination of
samples cooled quickly after creep deformation to the min-
imum creep rate.

second-phase particles identiﬁed as Nb
5B6 and Nb 3B4
phase, as described earlier. These particles have been
fragmented into two types of sizes, /C24 0.2–0.7 lm and
/C24 40–80 nm, as illustrated in Figs. 7 and 8 . The original seg-
regation cell walls have been transformed into boundaries
of relatively low misorientation, as indicated by the similar-

decorated with the 0.2–0.7 lm particle fragments (see

the coarse particles lying on the boundaries, ﬁner particles

close to the boundary, as illustrated in Fig. 7 . Such ﬁne

and the ﬁner fragments nearby are clearly the consequence

present as-cast material are seen to have inferior creep

creep strengths to mechanically-alloyed Fe–40Al [35] (they
are slightly inferior) and to precipitation hardened

also be remembered that the very strong and highly alloyed
iron aluminides, e.g., Fe–Al–Ti alloys [23], generally show
very poor ductility in the cast state, which makes their
acceptance for engineering use uncertain. As a ﬁnal point,
the relatively coarse boride particles in the present materi-
als will show good thermal stability at high-temperatures
(they have already been subjected to nearly 1 h at
1150 /C176C during processing) and can be expected to retain
their creep properties after very long exposure at high-tem-
peratures, better than the precipitate hardening alloys, for
example those containing Nb, Zr or carbides, which have
ﬁner but less stable particles [3,12–14,47].
The present study has shown that very heavy deforma-
tion introduced by multiple forging passes at high-temper-
ature in two orthogonal directions has led to a reﬁnement
of the second-phase particles and a slight tendency to redis-
tribute and homogenize throughout the material. In view
of the redundant or partially reversed strain introduced
to the material, the total strain imposed is signiﬁcantly lar-
ger than the overall shape change, somewhat analogous to
other methods of severe plastic deformation, such as equal
channel angular pressing. Such reﬁnement and redistribu-
tion of second-phase particles has led to a signiﬁcant
improvement in the high-temperature creep strength of
the material. The selection of boride phase has been impor-
tant, since the chemical components required to form the
boride are soluble in the liquid state (in contrast to more
stable oxide phases) but very poorly soluble in the solid
state (in contrast to precipitation-hardening solute such
as C, Nb, Ta, Zr) and therefore show excellent thermal sta-
bility under the relevant temperatures where creep is car-
ried out. The boride particles are distributed in a cellular
arrangement in the as-cast material and could be somewhat
reﬁned beyond that found for the present starting material
by increasing the solidiﬁcation rate. However, much hea-
vier working by forging would be required to improve fur-
ther the reﬁnement and homogenization of the boride
particles over that obtained in the present investigation.
This is diﬃcult to carry out unless a larger starting ingot
size than here (several centimetres) or much smaller ﬁnal
bar dimensions than here (several millimetres) can be
accepted.
5. Conclusions
A novel way of processing an iron aluminide alloy con-
taining a cellular distribution of second-phase boride parti-
cles was used to impose a very high forging strain at very
high-temperatures. The high forging temperature is neces-
sary to avoid cracking during forging, but also ensures that
recrystallization takes place to leave a moderate grain size,
as required for good creep strength at high-temperature.
The high forging strain imposed leads to an elongation of
the cellular distribution of particles, but also the reﬁnement
of particles by their fracture, and some slight improvement
in the homogeneity of distribution of these particles.
