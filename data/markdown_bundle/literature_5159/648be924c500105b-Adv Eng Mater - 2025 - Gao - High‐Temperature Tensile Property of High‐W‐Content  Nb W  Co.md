1. Introduction
TiAl-based alloys have garnered signi ﬁcant
interest for high-temperature applications
in the aerospace, automotive, and nuclear
power industries, due to their low density,
good oxidation resistance, good corrosion
resistance, and high strength properties
at elevated temperatures as well as excellent
radiation damage resistance and low neu-
tron activation. [1–7] As a high-temperature
structural material, reasonable alloying
method is the basic means to improve its
mechanical property. [8–10]
For improving the creep/rupture
property of TiAl-based alloys, (Nb,W)
co-alloying is a better method. Ye et al. [11]
prepared the Ti-45Al-8.5Nb-0.2W-0.2B-
0.02Y alloy to study the creep/rupture prop-
erty. The results showed that the creep/
rupture life reached about 130 h at
800 °C–200 MPa. Zhang et al. [12] prepared
the Ti-44Al-8Nb-0.2W-0.2B-0.1Y alloy and
tested its creep/rupture property. The results showed that the
alloy had a creep/rupture life of 240 h at 750 °C –300 MPa. Xu
et al. [13] prepared the Ti-46Al-7Nb-0.4W-0.6Cr-0.1B alloy and
tested its creep/rupture property at 760 °C –275 MPa. The ﬁnd-
ings demonstrated that the alloy exhibits a creep/rupture life
of up to 408 h. The results also indicated that high Nb resulted
in twinning and twin intersection. At the same time, some stud-
ies of Zhu et al. [14,15] showed that the addition of W leads to
dynamic precipitation of beta phase and strengthening during
long-term creep. In our previous studies, we also investigated
the rupture property of (Nb,W) co-alloying TiAl-based alloys
and found that the high-W-content (Nb,W) co-alloying alloy
has good rupture property under low stress. At the same time,
the effects of high-W-content and low-W-content on the rupture
property were also studied. [16–18]
Under ideal conditions, the tensile force modes of alloys
can be divided into two categories. One is the constant stress
condition, generally corresponding to the tensile creep/rupture
property as discussed above. Its crack failure mechanism can
be expressed by formula GI = 1/B·( ∂Ue/ ∂a)F, where GI is the
driving force of crack propagation, Ue is the elastic strain energy,
a is the crack length, and B is the thickness of the crack body.
The other is the constant tensile rate condition, generally

To investigate whether high-W-content (Nb,W) co-alloying TiAl-based alloys have
better high-temperature tensile property at different tensile rates, the Ti-44Al-
4Nb-1W-0.1B alloy (high W) is designed and prepared. Meanwhile, the Ti-44Al-
7.2Nb-0.2W-0.1B alloy (low W) and the Ti-44Al-8Nb-0.1B alloy (pure Nb) are also
prepared for comparative analysis. The tensile property is tested at 800 °C. The
microstructure evolution and fracture surface are studied. Finally, the two (Nb,W)
co-alloying alloys exhibit higher ultimate tensile strength than the pure Nb
alloying alloy at different tensile rates. The Ti-44Al-4Nb-1W-0.1B alloy shows
higher ultimate tensile strength than the Ti-44Al-7.2Nb-0.2W-0.1B alloy at low
tensile rate, but demonstrates lower ultimate tensile strength at high tensile rate.
As W content increases, the alloy ’s grain size decreases, enhancing the ﬁne-grain
effect. Combined Nb and W elements also contribute to solid solution
strengthening, while the B2 phase improves stress coordination. These factors
lead to better dislocation strengthening, so that the ultimate tensile strength of
the two (Nb,W) co-alloying alloys is higher than that of the pure Nb alloying alloy.
Under different tensile rates, the three alloys exhibit a combination of trans-
lamella fracture and trans-granular cleavage fracture modes.

In fact, there have been many studies on the mechanical
property of TiAl-based alloys under constant tensile rate, i.e.,
the tensile property. For example, Li et al. [22] fabricated a near-
net-shaped Ti-45Al-7Nb-0.3W alloy, demonstrating satisfactory
tensile property at 850 °C, with an ultimate tensile strength
measuring 390 MPa. Xu et al. [23] developed the Ti-46Al-7Nb-
0.6Cr-0.4W-0.1B alloy. At 800 °C, it exhibited outstanding
high-temperature tensile property, with an ultimate tensile
strength of 648 MPa and an elongation of 5.73%. Zhang et al. [24]
prepared the forged Ti-44Al-8Nb-0.2W-0.2B-0.1Y alloy and tested
tensile property. The results showed that the yield strength can
reach about 500 MPa at 800 °C.
From the current studies, we can ﬁnd that the high-
temperature tensile property of low-W-content TiAl-based alloys
was commonly studied and they are generally better. Since the
(Nb,W) co-alloying alloys with high-W-content have tensile
creep/rupture property (i.e., under the constant stress condition),
what will be the tensile property of these alloys (i.e., under the
constant tensile rate condition)? This remains to be studied.
Moreover, the current researchers pay more attention to the
tensile property of the alloys at a single constant strain rate.
In the actual stress process of the alloys, there is likely to be a
change in the strain rate. Thus, it is also valuable to investigate
the tensile property of the alloys at different tensile rates.
In view of above, this article mainly focuses on the high-
temperature tensile property of (Nb,W) co-alloying TiAl-based
alloys with high-W-content under different tensile rates. We
continue to use the Ti-44Al-4Nb-1W-0.1B alloy (high-W-content
(Nb,W) co-alloying TiAl-based alloy) as a model alloy. The
Ti-44Al-8Nb-0.1B alloy (pure Nb alloying TiAl-based alloy) and
the Ti-44Al-7.2Nb-0.2W-0.1B alloy (low W content (Nb,W)
co-alloying TiAl-based alloy) are used for comparative analysis.
The microstructure evolution and fracture surface are investi-
gated. And the reasons for the change in tensile property are
explored and discussed.
2. Experimental Section
Three cylindrical master ingots, each having a diameter of
90 mm and a height of 450 mm, were produced using the
vacuum consumable melting method. They were composed of
Ti-44Al-8Nb-0.1B (at%), Ti-44Al-4Nb-1W-0.1B (at%), and
Ti-44Al-7.2Nb-0.2W-0.1B (at%). The chemical analysis had veri-
ﬁed that the actual chemical composition of the three master
ingots complies with the speciﬁed criteria. According to the inﬂu-
ence of W content and subsequent convenience for readers
to distinguish, they were named TAN alloy, TANW þ alloy,
TANW alloy, respectively. The sample was prepared for tensile
testing using wire electrical discharge machining, with dimen-
sions speciﬁed as follows: length 7 mm, width 4 mm, and thick-
ness 2 mm. The tensile testing was completed by the universal
testing machine E45.305 at 800 °C in air. During the testing,
different tensile rates were used: 0.1, 0.5, and 1.0 mm min /C0 1.
To ensure experimental accuracy, each condition was subjected
to more than two tensile testing.
Microstructure was studied by scanning electron microscopy
(SEM), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and transmission
electron microscopy (TEM). For SEM analysis, the samples were
manually polished using 2000# SiC sandpaper. The etching pro-
cess lasted 14 s, with the etching solution composed of 19% HF
(hydroﬂuoric acid), 2% HNO 3 (nitric acid), and 79% H 2O (water)
by volume. For EBSD analysis, the samples were electropolished
using a solution consisting of 59% methanol, 34% butyl alcohol,
and 7% perchloric acid by volume. The electropolishing was per-
formed at /C0 24 °C and 30 V for 46 s. The step size is 0.53 μm, and
the relationship between the X 0 direction and the stretching
direction of the EBSD system is parallel. For TEM, the thin foils
were initially cut to a thickness of 500 μm and then further
ground down to ≈50 μm. Following this, discs with a diameter
of 3 mm were punched out from the center of these foils. Finally,
the foils with a diameter of 3 mm underwent twin jet electropo-
lishing. Etching liquid, etching temperature, and voltage of twin
jet electropolishing were the same as that of electropolishing.
Average grain size was determined using the intercept method,
with measurements taken at least 6 times under each condition.
The B2 phase content in the initial microstructure of the three
master alloys was quanti ﬁed using Image-Pro-Plus 6.0 software,
with measurements conducted a minimum of 6 times.
3. Results and Discussion
Figure 1 shows the initial microstructure of the three master
alloys. Figure 1a,b depicts the microstructure of the TAN alloy,
characterized by a near-lamellar structure. This structure con-
sists of ( α2 þ γ) lamella colonies and a minor presence of B2
phase, which constitutes ≈2.34% of the total composition. The
average lamella colony size measures 235.6 μm. After adding
W element, the two (Nb,W) co-alloying alloys still exhibit a
near-lamellar microstructure, composed by ( α2 þ γ) lamella col-
onies, blocky γ phase and B2 phase. As depicted in Figure 1f,
blocky B2 phase is present within the lamella colony, the phe-
nomenon referred to as α-segregation,[25] because the lamellar
directions on both sides are the same marked by the dotted
yellow line. Blocky B2 phase is found along the colony boundary,
the phenomenon known as β-segregation,[25] because the lamel-
lar directions on both sides have a certain angle. According to
Figure 1e,f, the TANW þ alloy exhibits a signi ﬁcant amount of
B2 phase, comprising ≈10.73% of the total composition. The
average lamella colony size measures 99.2 μm. As depicted in
Figure 1c,d, the TANW alloy exhibits a medium amount of
B2 phase with the content about 7.33%. The average lamella col-
ony size measures 133.8 μm. With an increase in W content,
there is an observed increase in B2 phase. Simultaneously, there
is a decrease in the average size of the lamella colony.

matrix due to more defects, and hence ﬁne grains may lead to
reduced strengthening. However, the presence of more B2 pre-
cipitates in ﬁne-grained alloys can effectively hinder dislocation
motion, although B2 may be softer than the matrix, and thus
improve the strengthening, especially at low strain rates and dur-
ing creep. [33] However, high strain rates can increase the stress
concentration at the B2 phase, and thus reduce the ﬁne-grain
strength. The so-called ﬁne-grain effect is the abovementioned
situation, i.e., “low strain rate ! longer stretching time ! more
dislocation proliferation ! stronger dislocation strengthening !
increased ultimate tensile strength ”. And the ﬁne-grain effect is
mainly determined by the grain size of the original as-cast
alloy.[16] Similarly, compared with TANW alloy, the TANWþ alloy
has smaller grain size and higher ultimate tensile strength. At
high tensile rate, the stress concentration alleviated by ﬁne
grain strengthening increases the plasticity of the two (Nb,W)
co-alloying alloys. In addition, the ﬁne-grain effect helps to
reduce the risk of stress concentration and local fracture, thus
indirectly improving the ultimate tensile strength of the material.
At the same time, Nb and W in the alloy may further improve the
strength through other strengthening mechanisms (such as solid
solution strengthening). So the ultimate tensile strength of the
two (Nb,W) co-alloying alloys is better than that of the pure
Nb alloying alloy. As shown in Figure 3i, it can be found that
there are many holes and cracks at the B2 phase, indicating that
the stress concentration induced by high tensile rate at the B2
phase is easy to cause cracking. [16,17] Because the TANW þ alloy
contains a higher proportion of the B2 phase, its ultimate tensile
strength is lower compared to that of the TANW alloy at high
tensile rate. In addition, when the strain rate is further increased
to 1 mm min /C0 1, the dislocation propagation is inhibited, the
strain hardening effect is enhanced, and the strength of the
whole alloy is improved. Although the B2 phase may still be
the initial source of cracks, the crack propagation is limited,
resulting in an increase in the strength of the TANW þ alloy.[16,23]

trans-lamella and trans-granular cleavage fractures, across differ-

the typical dimple morphology indicates a ductile fracture mode,

signiﬁcant pores can be seen, they may be induced by the stress

4. Conclusion
In this article, the tensile property of (Nb,W) co-alloying TiAl-
based alloys and pure Nb alloying TiAl-based alloy at different
tensile rates are tested, and the characteristics of high-W-content
(Nb,W) co-alloying TiAl-based alloy are compared and analyzed.
The following conclusions are drawn: 1) The two (Nb,W)
co-alloying alloys have higher ultimate tensile strength than pure
Nb alloying alloy at different tensile rates. Due to the addition of
W element, Nb and W work together and the two (Nb,W)
co-alloying alloys have the solid solution strengthening effect.
2) For the two (Nb,W) co-alloying alloys, at 0.1 mm min /C0 1 tensile
rate, the Ti-44Al-4Nb-1W-0.1B alloy achieved an ultimate tensile
strength of 502 MPa, whereas at 1.0 mm min /C0 1, this ultimate
tensile strength reduced to 434.7 MPa. In comparison, the
Ti-44Al-7.2Nb-0.2W-0.1B alloy exhibited an ultimate tensile
strength of 487.4 MPa at 0.1 mm min /C0 1 and 448.4 MPa at
1.0 mm min/C0 1. 3) Because of the ﬁne-grain effect and the coor-
dination of B2 phase, two (Nb,W) co-alloying alloys have higher
ultimate tensile strength than pure Nb alloying alloy, which
means longer tensile time, resulting in dislocation proliferation
and dislocation strengthening effect. This is also the reason why
Ti-44Al-4Nb-1W-0.1B alloy exhibits higher ultimate tensile
strength compared to Ti-44Al-7.2Nb-0.2W-0.1B alloy. 4) At high
tensile rate, because of the higher proportion of the B2 phase in
the Ti-44Al-4Nb-1W-0.1B alloy, stress concentration in B2 phase
is easy to cause cracking. As a result, the Ti-44Al-4Nb-1W-0.1B
alloy demonstrates a lower ultimate tensile strength than the
Ti-44Al-7.2Nb-0.2W-0.1B alloy.
