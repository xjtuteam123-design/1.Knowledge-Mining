1. Introduction
The demand for structural materials capable of sustaining excep -
tional strength and ductility over a broad temperature range, particu -
larly down to cryogenic regimes, is rapidly increasing in sectors such as 
liquefied gas storage, aerospace propulsion, and deep-sea exploration. 
Conventional alloys, as well as some of the more recently developed 
ones, often suffer from an inherent strength-ductility trade-off and 
pronounced temperature sensitivity. These shortcomings limit their 
applicability under extreme or fluctuating thermal conditions, which 
require more appropriate alloys [1 – 7] . Metastable high-entropy alloys 
(HEAs) have emerged as a transformative class of materials due to their 
ability to exploit transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) to overcome 
the strength-ductility trade-off [8 – 13] . While the vast majority of 
TRIP-enabled HEA studies focus on the FCC → HCP ( ε -martensite) 
pathway, the FCC → BCC ( α ′ -martensite) transformation — herein referred 
to as the BCC-TRIP effect — offers superior strengthening potential and 
work-hardening capability [9,14] . However, this phase transformation 
in Fe-based HEAs is generally activated only at cryogenic temperatures, 
resulting in limited applicability at ambient conditions [15] .
Recent advances have shown that Si alloying in metastable Fe-based 
HEAs reduces the stacking fault energy (SFE) and enhances the driving 
force for the FCC → BCC martensitic phase transformation, thereby 
activating a strong BCC-TRIP effect even at room temperature [16 – 22] . 
While the tensile behavior of Si-containing HEAs has been systemati -
cally investigated at ambient conditions, their cryogenic mechanical 
response and the influence of Si content on α ′ -martensite stability and 
strength remain underexplored. In particular, the role of α ′ -martensite 
intrinsic strength — highly sensitive to temperature — indicating the 
cryogenic performance of these alloys has not been quantitatively

4. Results and discussion
Fig. 2 shows the XRD patterns of all selected HEAs in their initial 
microstructure, revealing single-phase austenitic structures with no 
detectable presence of other phases, such as α ’ -martensite or 
ε -martensite. Thus, even at a high Si content of 6 at%, the microstructure 
remained single-phase, as determined by XRD analysis. Notably, 
achieving a single-phase microstructure before tensile deformation is 
essential for attaining exceptional ductility in metastable alloys [9] .
Fig. 3 (a-d) present the tensile stress-strain curves of the studied HEAs 
analyzed at both room and cryogenic temperatures. It can be observed 
that reducing the temperature from room to cryogenic temperature 
significantly increased the yield stress (YS) and work-hardening capa -
bility of the fabricated HEAs. This ultimately led to remarkable UTS and 
provided favorable mechanical properties in Si-containing HEAs at 
cryogenic temperature. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of these 
HEAs at both room and cryogenic temperatures revealed insights 
regarding the stability of austenite and the effects of Si addition. In this 
context, a summary of the mechanical properties is presented in Fig. 3 (e- 
g). It can be seen that the addition of Si resulted in an increase in YS at 
both temperatures due to the solid solution strengthening effect (see 
Fig. 3 e). Additionally, a comparison between Figs. 3 e and 3 f shows that 
Si had a significantly greater impact on the UTS at both room and 
cryogenic temperatures compared to YS. It is also evident that the 
incorporation of Si has enhanced the UTS, with the 6Si alloy exhibiting 
the highest stress level at both room and cryogenic temperatures, a topic 
that will be explored further.

It is also important to note that, in addition to the quantity of 
α ’ -martensite, its morphology (lath-type or plate-type) can significantly 
influence the mechanical properties of metastable alloys. Previous 
studies on Fe-C and Fe-Ni alloys, as well as high-strength low-alloy 
steels, have shown that the fracture toughness and overall mechanical 
performance are strongly influenced by the morphology of α ’ -martensite 
[35 – 38] . Lath-type α ’ -martensite, with its fine and homogeneous 
microstructure, contributes to improved fracture toughness. In contrast, 
plate-type α ’ -martensite, characterized by a coarse and heterogeneous 
microstructure, markedly decreases fracture toughness [39,40] . How -
ever, as shown in Fig. 5 , no meaningful difference was observed in the 
morphology of α ’ -martensite formed in the base and 6Si alloys.
Regarding the 6Si alloy, it is noteworthy that while the volume 
fraction of α ’ -martensite formed after tensile deformation at both room 
and cryogenic temperatures remained approximately consistent, the 
increase in the work-hardening rate was pronounced at the cryogenic 
temperature ( Fig. 4 ). This sharp rise in the work-hardening rate may be 
attributed to the enhanced strength of α ’ -martensite at lower tempera -
tures. As previously mentioned in the literature, the strength of the BCC 
structure (same crystal structure as α ’ -martensite) significantly increases 
with decreasing temperature [34] . It is crucial to note that the strength 
of the BCC phase exhibits significantly greater sensitivity to the defor -
mation temperature compared to the strength of the FCC phase [34,41] . 
This heightened sensitivity can be leveraged through the use of meta -
stable alloys that exhibit the BCC TRIP effect during deformation, 
resulting in the attainment of exceptionally high UTS at cryogenic 
temperatures. It is noteworthy that YS, where the austenite with an FCC 
structure plays a pivotal role, exhibited a difference of ~300 MPa be -
tween room and cryogenic temperatures. In contrast, the difference in 
UTS, in which α ’ -martensite is predominant, was considerably more 
pronounced between these two states (~700 MPa). Therefore, it can be 
concluded that the TRIP effect at cryogenic temperature induced sub -
stantial strength and work-hardening capacity, which is reflected in the 
results shown in Fig. 4 d .
