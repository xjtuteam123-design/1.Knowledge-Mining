1. Introduction
The emergence of complex-concentrated alloys (CCAs), also referred 
to as high-entropy and medium-entropy alloys, has opened up a far- 
reaching compositional space for developing alloys with novel and 
desired microstructures and properties [1 – 7] . Unlike traditional alloys 
with only one or two principal components, CCAs mix together three or 
more principal elements at equal or non-equal molar proportion [2,4] . 
With properly tunning the compositions and microstructures, CCAs have 
been demonstrated to exhibit unexpected and superior properties, e.g., 
excellent strength-ductility combination at both ambient and cryogenic 
conditions [8 – 10] , optimum balance between mechanical and soft 
magnetic properties [11] , good wear resistance [12] and corrosion 
resistance [13,14] . Nowadays, face-centered cubic (FCC) based Co-free 
CCAs, as a new category of CCAs, have gained more and more atten -
tions not only due to their good properties but also because their lower 
costs as compared to Co-doped CCAs [5,14 – 22] . Meanwhile, several 
strengthening mechanisms including solid solution [23,24] , 
grain-refinement [25] , TWIP and TRIP [14] , and precipitation [5,16,22, 
26 – 28] have been successfully used to enhance Co-free CCAs. Among 
these strategies, precipitation strengthening is consider as one efficient 
strategy for achieving excellent mechanical properties of alloys.
Additionally, partial recrystallization has emerged as a promising 
strategy to enhance the mechanical properties of CCAs [19,29 – 33] , 
including the newly developed Co-free CCAs [19,31] . Typical examples 
are the intermediate temperature annealing of the cold-rolled

Owing to the spatially differing recrystallization kinetics of the 
pre-deformed materials [30,33] , unique heterogeneous microstructures 
composed of recrystallized and non-recrystallized grains with different 
grain size are formed after heat treatments. Additionally, the growth 
rate of grains is inhibited due to the sluggish diffusion effect of CCAs [7] , 
which favors the formation of the partially recrystallized microstruc -
tures. The recrystallized and non-recrystallized constituents have 
different strengthening and deformation mechanisms, synergistically 
giving rise to good strength-ductility balances of the resultant alloys 
[31 – 33] . However, recrystallization is generally known to deteriorate 
strength of the pre-deformed alloys [19,31 – 34] . Thus, various

3.2. Mechanical responses at room temperature
Engineering tension stress-strain curves of the CR and PR samples are 
shown in Fig. 5 a. For comparation, tensile properties of the precipitated 
sample with fully recrystallized coarse grains [22] , hereafter denoted as 
FR sample, were also shown here. The detailed mechanical properties 
are summarized in Table S2 . Marked enhancement in mechanical 
properties by fabricating the partially recrystallized microstructure are 
evident. The CR sample has a high yield strength (YS) of 1160 MPa and 
an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 1205 MPa with a low total 
elongation (TE) of about 3.6 %. However, the PR sample exhibits a high 
YS of 1208 MPa, a high UTS of 1431 MPa and a TE of 21.2 %, signifi -

3.3. Microstructure evolution during deformation
In order to clarify the mechanism of the significantly improved 
strength-ductility combination of the PR sample (see Fig. 5 a), micro -
structures at true strains of about 5 % and 15 %, were investigated by 
TEM. The typical deformed microstructures are shown in Fig. 6 . In the 
case of RDG, at the true strain of 5 %, as shown in Fig. 6 a, deformation 
occurs mainly by dislocation planar slip along primary {111} slip planes, 
accompanying with significant amounts of tangled dislocations. With 
increasing strain ( Fig. 6 b), deformation-induced microbands were 
observed in RDGs, similar with the observations in FCC-alloys [47 – 49] . 
The progressive development of microbands, which together with their 
domain boundaries are the structure are composed of GNDs, leads to 
continuous strain hardening during straining and results in excellent 
strength-ductility balance [49] . Differing from RDG
S
, the main defor -
mation mode in SRXGs consisting of the recrystallized FGs and UFGs is 
wavy slip ( Fig. 6 c - f). The observed difference in dislocation sub -
structures during tension could be attributed to the grain size effect. 
Generally, the existence of ordered-precipitates is believed to be bene -
ficial for planar slip [50,51] , whereas grain refinement inhibits the 
occurrence of planar slips [47] . In the initial tensile stage, dislocation 
tangles and local clusters were found in both FGs ( Fig. 6 c) and UFGs 
( Fig. 6 e), and a significant number of dislocations accumulated nearby 
the FG/UFG interface were observed, as demonstrated in Fig. S5a . In 
addition, as shown in Fig. 6 e and S4c, numerous dislocations accumulate 
at annealing twin boundaries, demonstrating the effective inhibiting of 
annealing twins on dislocation motion. At the true strain of 15 %, 
dislocation cell structures were observed in FGs ( Fig. 6 d), but there are 
numerous dislocations and localized dislocation clusters formed in 
UFGs.

plastic deformation during plastic deformation [39,50] . In view of 
this, the hetero-yielding process prior to the global yielding of the pre -
sent PH-HG CCA is expected to be happened. During straining, the 
relative softer grains preferentially yield and deform plastically. How -
ever, they cannot deform freely, due to the restraint by the relatively 
stronger one, which are still in elastic state. As a result, mobile dislo -
cations pile-up in soft grains against hetero-interfaces, plastic in -
compatibility takes place, leading to strain gradient [40] . This local 
strain gradient results in a long-range internal stress, i.e. HDI stress, and 
makes dislocations difficult to move in soft grains until the plastically 
deformation occurs in neighboring domains [49] . Ultimately, such hi -
erarchical constraint enables the softer grains stronger to sustain higher 
flow stress, leading to the so-called synergetic HDI strengthening and 
hardening, and thus contribute to the mechanical properties of the 
present PR sample.
Here, LUR tests were performed to explore the evolution of HDI 
stress upon tensile deformation. As shown in Fig. 8 a and b, LUR curves of 
both PR and FR show hysteresis loops, which are the evidence of Bau -
schinger effect due to the HDI hardening [51] . The width of hysteresis 
loops of the PR sample, as shown in Fig. 8 b, is significantly larger than 
that of the FR sample, indicating the stronger HDI hardening in PR 
sample [51] . It is worth noting that at a given strain the stress of the PR 
sample is much higher than of FR. Following the method of Slone et al. 
[33] , the hysteresis effects of the PR and FR samples are compared at the 
same stress level, as shown in Fig. 8 c. It is clear that even at the same 
stress level, the HDI effect of the PR sample is larger than that of the FR 
sample, demonstrating the extraordinary HDI effect of the PR sample. 
Fig. 8 d shows the dynamic evolution of HDI stress along with the true 
strain in the range of 1.5 – 15 %. It is evident that the PR sample manifest 
the highest HDI stress at a given strain, suggesting that HDI stress plays a 
critical role on the strain hardening of the PR sample. Moreover, as for 
the PR sample, HDI stress increases rapidly with strain up to 6 %, which 
is just the up-turn region of strain-hardening rate of the PR sample (see 
Fig. 5 b). This indicates that the efficiency of HDI stress on strain hard -
ening is most effective at the early strain stage.
