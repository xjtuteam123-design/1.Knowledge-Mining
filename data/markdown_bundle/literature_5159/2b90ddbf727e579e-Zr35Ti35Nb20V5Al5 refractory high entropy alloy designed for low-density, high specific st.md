Refractory high entropy alloys have gained signiﬁcant interest over the past decade as promising candidates 
for high-strength applications, particularly at high temperatures. However, achieving ductility and workability 
at room temperature remains a challenge for large-scale manufacturing and applications. This study explores 
the design and characterisation of a novel RHEA with low density, high ductility, and high strength at room 
temperature. High-throughput screening and experimental validation identiﬁed a non-equiatomic composition, 
Zr35Ti35Nb20V5Al5 (at%), which exhibits a room-temperature yield strength of 1030 MPa, 11% tensile strain 
to failure, and a low density of 6 g/cm3. The alloy’s grain size was reﬁned to <20 μm through rolling and 
recrystallisation, bypassing traditional high-temperature homogenisation while avoiding microsegregation. The 
tailored Zr35Ti35Nb20V5Al5 RHEA demonstrates a new design approach and processing route, opening applications 
in next-generation nuclear and aerospace technologies.
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have emerged as a promising class of 
materials due to their exceptional mechanical properties and multifunc-
tional performance, including superior resistance to extreme environ-
ments with reports of outstanding high temperature strength, wear and 
irradiation resistance [1–3]. Refractory High Entropy Alloys (RHEAs) 
have further demonstrated excellent mechanical performance at high 
temperatures, and achieving high dynamic strength through stress-
induced microstructural phase transformations, as well as outstanding 
irradiation resistance with minimal defect formation [4–6]. Although 
HEAs display impressive high temperature performance, a consistent 
diﬃculty, in particular RHEAs, has been navigating the trade-oﬀ be-
tween ductility and high strength materials -w i t h the majority having 
minimal ductility at room temperature [7]. The vast compositional land-
scape of HEAs leaves unexplored phase space that opens up the potential 
for discovery of new materials with as-yet unknown properties that may 
yield high performance capabilities. Due to their atomic complexity, 
there is often diﬃculty in producing intrinsically ductile alloys, with 
many RHEAs demonstrating brittle behaviour at room temperature es-
pecially with as-cast material containing defects and residual stress from 
the casting process. Understanding the deformation mechanisms within

Across most RHEAs there are, however, limitations in acquiring 
room-temperature ductility, in particular with elements such as W, 
Cr, and Mo, which often contribute towards increased brittle be-
haviour [16–18]. Consequently, alloy selection has become a major 
forefront of HEA research to develop and better understand how to 
navigate the enlarged design space and ﬁne tune alloy compositions 
for speciﬁc applications. It is widely accepted that to achieve a body 
centred-cubic (bcc) HEA, it must have a valence electron concentration 
(VEC), VEC <6.87 [19]. In 2014, Qi and Chrzan proposed that the in-
trinsic ductility of a bcc refractory alloy can be predicted using VEC [11].

In the current study, we demonstrate that designing for low density and 
high ductility, RHEAs can achieve rapid thermomechanical processing 
to reﬁne grain size through cold rolling and recrystallisation as well as 
high speciﬁc yield strength as a result of the reﬁned grains.
The Alloy Search And Predict (ASAP) code [24] was used to screen 
for single-phase high temperature stability alloys and two four-element 
equimolar alloys were identiﬁed VNbTiZr, VNbCrMo as promising can-
didates [25,26]. From here, a series of combination alloys were assessed 
within the CrMnMoNbTiVZr system with minor additions of Al (Al 𝜖0, 
5at%) using an additional screening tool. The modelling of the combi-
nations of alloys across the refractory elements consisted of a custom 
Python script that generates all possible alloy combinations in a given 
boundary and assigns values to the individual alloys using designated 
calculations, in this case, VEC and density.
A 400 g ingot of Zr35Ti35Nb20V5Al5 (at%) alloy was synthesised us-
ing elements of high purity (99.9%) melted in an evacuated and argon 
backﬁlled arc melter Arcast -A r c 200. The as-cast ingots were cut into 
even plates of approximately 5 mm thick and rolled at room temperature 
down to a thickness of 0.5 mm (90% reduction), in increments of ap-
proximately 5% reduction per pass. The plates were sectioned, wrapped 
in Mo foil and encapsulated in silica glass tubes, evacuated, backﬁlled 
with Ar gas and annealed at these respective temperatures and times 
(800 ◦C 30 mins, 1hr – 900 ◦C 5 mins, 15 mins, 30 mins, 1 hr) and water 
quenched. The Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Electron Backscat-
ter Diﬀraction (EBSD) and Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) 
images were all acquired using a TF Apreo 2S HiVac SEM operated at 20 
keV. The samples were ground ﬂat to 4000 grit with SiC grit paper fol-
lowed by 0.02 μm colloidal silica for 30 minutes. Diﬀraction data were 
obtained using Synchotron X-ray Diﬀraction (SXRD) at Diamond Light 
Source, Harwell Campus, UK. The heat-treated samples were cut down 
into SSJ-3 sample specimen dimensions (5 x 1.2 x 0.5 mm gage length, 
width and thickness respectively [27]) using wire Electrical Discharge 
Machining and ground to 800 grit using SiC papers. The SSJ-3 samples 
were tested using an Instron 6800 series load frame with a 5 kN load cell 
including a DIC set-up with a Touptek camera equipped with 16-160x 
magniﬁcation to accurately measure the strain.

This criteria identiﬁes 46 alloys from which 31 alloys have a bcc 
phase prediction (VEC <6.87) and as displayed in Fig. 1, a signiﬁcant 
number of these alloys are comprised entirely of refractory elements: 
Nb, Ti, V, Zr, Mo, Cr. With the consideration of these identiﬁed equimo-
lar alloys, a new screening tool was developed to iterate through all 
possible 4-element and 5-element alloy combinations of a given set of 
elements and identify the trend of VEC and density within the given sys-
tem. The given elements were restricted to between 5at% and 35at% in 
intervals of 5at%. In Fig. 1, the refractory based elements selected were 
Ti, Zr, V, Nb, Cr, Mn and Mo -all of which were consistently identiﬁed 
within the ASAP model. The densest refractory elements Hf, Ta, and W 
were omitted from the calculation due to the elements being redundant 
when selecting for low-density alloys. Al was included with quantities of 
0at% or 5at% as literature suggests that Al in a solid solution can result 
in increased lattice distortion that impedes dislocation movement -pro-
moting the strength of the alloy but in turn reducing ductility [28–32]. 
This correlation between alloying additions of Al into RHEAs to improve 
strength but reduces ductility is worth noting that the initial correlation 
between VEC and ductility may only be true amongst the refractory el-
ements themselves. This suggests that a more accurate explanation for 
intrinsic ductility amongst RHEAs may be additionally related to the 
atomic displacement of the atoms within the alloy and not just their 
electron conﬁguration. New parameters to better predict ductility have 
been attempted to combine previous work to achieve a more reﬁned 
predictive model of RHEA ductility, however it has not shown deﬁni-
tive improvements to previous criterion and was therefore not used in 
this study [22,33].

shown by the shape of the alloy distribution, the low-density bound-

Four alloys were identiﬁed with the lowest VEC of 4.2, listed from 
top to bottom as: Zr35Ti35Nb25−𝑥V𝑥Al5, 𝑥 𝜖5, 10, 15, 20. With four al-
loys down-selected, CALculation of PHAse Diagrams (CALPHAD) predic-
tions were made to identify the phases. Shown in Fig. 2, the predictions 
indicate a single-phase bcc region for the Nb20 (Zr35Ti35Nb20V5Al5) al-
loy, however when increasing the V quantities this single-phase region 
disappears and a stronger formation of C14 laves is predicted. For this 
reason, the Nb20 alloy was selected for further analysis instead of the 
less dense Nb5 (Zr35Ti35Nb5V20Al5) alloy.
The selected alloy shows incredible room temperature ductility al-
lowing the sample to be cold rolled 𝜖= 90% from the as-cast state. This 
super-formable capability enables rapid thermo-mechanical processing. 
Where a signiﬁcant number of RHEAs require long homogenisation heat 
treatments, this alloy instead enables the removal of conventional ho-
mogenisation treatments through the process of cold rolling to signiﬁ-
cant reductions and ﬂash annealing. Due to the signiﬁcant density of dis-
locations within the alloy at such high cold roll reductions, the atoms can 
diﬀuse along dislocations at much faster rates when annealed [15]. This 
provides an eﬀective means of alloy processing that reduces processing 
time and improves cost eﬃciency. This may also enable microstructure 
tuning by varying grain size via recrystallisation with diﬀerent temper-
atures and annealing times as shown in Fig. 3.
Fig. 2 displays an SEM micrograph and SXRD of the alloy, which 
has been cold rolled 𝜖=90% and ﬂash annealed at 900 ◦C for 5 min-
utes. Following this processing the alloy forms a relatively small-grained 
equiaxed microstructure (16.6 ±8.6 μm) that is both homogenous and 
single-phase bcc. The CALPHAD predictions indicate a minor presence 
of C14 Laves phase at 900 ◦C, however from both SEM investigation 
and SXRD analysis, there was no clear indication of any presence of 
C14 Laves. This could be due to insuﬃcient time of 5 mins at 900 ◦C to 
form detectable peaks or the CALPHAD predictions have overestimated 
the phase stability of C14 Laves at 900 ◦C.
The cold roll capability of this alloy allows for ﬁne tuning the grain 
size through recrystallisation as shown in Fig. 3reducing the grain size 
to ∼16 μm exploiting the Hall-Petch relation to achieve more favourable 
mechanical properties. Utilising this method, the alloy is able to be sin-
gle phase and fully recrystallised within 5 minutes at 900 ◦C from a cold 
rolled sample. The increased number density of dislocations and grain 
boundaries as well as the stored energy within the dislocations, allows 
for a greater driving force for recrystallisation and an increased num-
ber of nucleation sites for the grains to grow from enabling these rapid 
recrystallisation times. Additionally, reduced annealing times may mit-
igate the introduction of the predicted C14 Laves phase to form along 
the grain boundaries that may lead to a reduction in ductility although 
there was no evidence of the presence of C14 Laves phase.
The recrystallised RHEAs showed excellent tensile strength and duc-
tility with the strongest (800 ◦C 30 mins) having a 0.2% proof yield 
strength of 1030 MPa and the most ductile (900 ◦C 5 mins) failing at 
11.6% as shown in Fig. 4. Note that the failure point indicates a point 
of signiﬁcant necking as opposed to a clean break, where the tests con-
tinued to signiﬁcantly decrease in strength for another 1-2% strain and, 
although it was not quantitatively tested, it may suggest increased crack 
propagation resistance and fracture toughness due its resistance to a 
clean tensile break. Additionally, it was expected that a reduction in 
grain size would result in an increased number density of grain bound-
aries that can act as barriers to dislocation motion, creating a pile-up 
of dislocations requiring more signiﬁcant stress applied for their move-
ment and thus increasing tensile strength [34].
The increased ductility of the 900 ◦C 5 mins alloy in comparison to 
the longer annealing times could indicate that there is residual strain 
which may contribute to slight elevations in work hardening to extend 
the uniform elongation region beyond that of the longer recrystallisa-
tion times. However, with the relative minor increase in ductility, small 
tensile specimens, and EBSD of the alloy pre and post-mortem it would 
suggest that this ductility improvement is not signiﬁcant enough to sup-
port a substantial diﬀerence in deformation mechanisms between the 
samples and/or minor inclusions of a C14 Laves phase forming. This 
seems to be supported by the fractography imaging as all the recrys-
tallised conditions display similar ductile dimple deformation behaviour 
of approximately 10 μm with no evidence of crack propagation from the 
centre or boundaries of the dimples that would be expected if the C14 
Laves phase was present. The cold rolled sample consisting of inclusions 
of ﬂat faceted regions and shallow dimple formation which is congruent 
to the expected behaviour derived from the recrystallised conditions of 
deeper dimples and signiﬁcantly fewer regions of ﬂat facets and shear-
ing.

By designing for low density and ductility, Zr35Ti35Nb20V5Al5 un-
dergoes grain size reﬁnement via cold rolling, promoting additional 
strengthening compared to other ductile RHEAs, as shown in Fig. 5. 
Using larger quantities of low-density elements such as Ti, the al-
loy achieves exceptionally high speciﬁc yield strength. In literature, 
ductile RHEAs contain large quantities of Group IV elements (Ti, Zr, 
Hf) and smaller additions of Group V elements (V, Nb, Ta); how-
ever, many alloys suﬀer in speciﬁc yield strength due to Hf and Ta 
additions. Although Hf and Ta may oﬀer some improved ductility 
and high temperature performance, it signiﬁcantly reduces the speciﬁc 
yield strength due to their densities (13.31 and 16.69 g/cm3 respec-
tively [58]).
In conclusion, a high-throughput computational screening tool was 
implemented to identify RHEAs with low-density and high ductility 
potential. The combined use of this iterative screening tool and pre-
vious code ‘ASAP’, assisted in identifying the Zr-Ti-Nb-V-Al system and 
speciﬁc composition, Zr35Ti35Nb20V5Al5, predicted to oﬀer signiﬁcant 
room temperature strength, ductility and low density of 6 g/cm3. The 
alloy was produced by arc melting, deformation processing alongside re-
crystallisation treatments achieving exceptional performance compared 
to other literature RHEAs especially the 800 ◦C 30 mins annealed alloy 
with a speciﬁc yield strength of 172 kPa m3/kg. The variations in re-
crystallised grain sizes highlight its tunable nature, as well as its ability 
to bypass high temperature homogenisation heat treatments. It leaves 
this alloy as a great prospect for high speciﬁc yield strength applications 
in nuclear and aerospace.
