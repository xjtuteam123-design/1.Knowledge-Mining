The mechanical behavior of metastable β Ti alloys can be controlled through heat treatments. Thus, 
the relationship between the precipitation of α phase and the mechanical properties of these alloys is 
of special interest. In this work, the microstructure evolution of Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy during aging heat 
treatments was evaluated using optical microscopy, scanning electron microscopy and X-ray diffraction. 
Moreover, Vickers hardness and elastic modulus were measured as a function of aging time. Finally, the 
ultimate strength and ductility of the alloy aged at 500 °C was assessed by tensile tests. In comparison 
to a Ti-30Nb alloy, the addition of Fe lowered the β-transus temperature, decreased the martensite start 
temperature to a value below room temperature, increased the precipitation temperature and reduced 
the dissolution temperature of ω phase, and lastly, decreased the α phase precipitation temperature. 
Low heating rates enabled isothermal ω phase precipitation and growth, providing favorable conditions 
for α phase precipitation and increasing the amount of α phase precipitates. Compared to the solution 
heat-treated and water-quenched condition, aging heat-treated Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy presented higher 
Vickers hardness and mechanical strength, without significant loss of ductility.
Keywords: Ti alloys, biomaterial, thermomechanical treatment, Vickers hardness, elastic modulus.
1. Introduction
Metastable β Ti alloys are widely used in the manufacturing 
of load-bearing components for aerospace and medical 
applications
1-4
. Besides their excellent corrosion resistance 
and improved biocompatibility, the extensive use of Ti 
alloys is strongly based on their mechanical behavior, 
which results from Ti allotropy combined with a range of 
alloying elements and suitable processing routes. Upon 
heating, the α phase (hcp) of CP Ti is transformed into 
the β phase (bcc) at 882.5 °C (for pure Ti), also known as 
β-transus temperature. The addition of alloying elements and 
application of proper thermomechanical processing allows 
for tailoring of phase transformations and hence, control of 
the mechanical behavior
5,6
.
The addition of Nb to Ti has been investigated because 
it lowers the β-transus temperature, resulting in full β phase 
stabilization at room temperature and also reducing the 
elastic modulus
7-9
. Furthermore, Nb is highly biocompatible, 
enabling its alloys to be employed as biomaterials
10
. Fe is 
another interesting Ti alloying element. Previous reports 
suggest that the addition of Fe to the Ti-Nb system refines 
the α phase precipitates
11
, stabilizes the β phase
12
, decreases 
the elastic modulus
7
, and may also reduce its cost. Therefore, 
Ti-Nb-Fe alloys can potentially be used in the manufacture 
of devices for biomedical implants.
In addition to their low elastic modulus, Ti alloys 
designed for use in hard tissue implants must possess both 
high mechanical strength and high ductility, and one way 
of achieving this goal is to control the precipitation of α 
phase in the β phase matrix
13
. The mechanical behavior of β 
Ti alloys can be tailored by subjecting solution heat-treated 
samples to aging heat treatments, inducing the precipitation 
of α phase, which is harder than β phase. Upon aging, the 
decomposition of metastable β phase and precipitation of α 
phase are complex phenomena that involve the dissolution 
of the athermal ω phase, precipitation and dissolution of 
isothermal ω, and finally, precipitation of α phase. Typically, 
α phase precipitation is based on heterogeneous nucleation 
assisted by microstructural heterogeneities, such as grain 
boundaries, β/β′ interfaces resulting from phase separation, 
and eventually, particles of ω phase dispersed in the β 
phase
14
. Nucleation, which involves ω phase acting as 
nucleation sites, plays a key role in α phase precipitation 
and its distribution in the β matrix
15,16
. It is known that 
under favorable conditions, isothermal ω phase dissolution 
can result in a microstructure consisting of highly refined 
and well dispersed α precipitates. The mechanism involved 
in ω-assisted nucleation of α phase is based on the direct 
transformation of ω phase into α phase or on solute partitioning 
in the β phase close to ω particles
14
. In a recent report, results 
obtained by an aging heat treatment of Ti-30Nb alloy carried 
out at temperatures close to 400 °C indicated that isothermal 
ω phase, albeit metastable, remained in the microstructure for 
long periods without α phase precipitation, which is in good 
accordance with the temperature vs. time map suggested by

Ivasishin et al.17. When the aging temperature was increased, 
dissolution of isothermal ω phase and α phase precipitation 
took place rapidly18. Alpha phase precipitation based on the 
ω phase assisted mechanism depends on the misfit between 
ω/β phases14,15. It is well established in the literature that, in 
high misfit systems, the ledges between ω/β phases act as 
nucleation sites for the α phase, but the precise mechanism 
in low misfit systems (Ti-Nb, for instance) is not well 
known19. However, regardless of misfit, ω phase precipitates 
contribute to increase the driving force for α precipitation 
in Ti alloys14,16,20.
An additional factor that strongly affects α phase precipitation 
in highly stabilized β Ti alloys is the heating rate applied to 
reach the final aging heat treatment temperature17,21. This is 
due to the prolongation of the holding time in the ω phase 
formation temperature zone, generating a different number 
of nucleation sites for α phase precipitation. The effects of 
the heating rate on α phase precipitation were investigated 
previously17,22 and indicated that the application of low 
heating rates followed by subsequent aging could provide 
a fine and well-dispersed α+β microstructure, with an 
interesting strength-ductility balance.
Therefore, the aim of this investigation was to describe 
the aging response in the Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy in the β quenched 
condition in order to optimize fine α phase precipitates in the 
β phase matrix. Efforts are focused on determining the effect 
of heating rates at different final aging temperatures and aging 
holding times. Lastly, correlations between microstructure 
and mechanical properties were also investigated.
2. Experimental Procedure
The Ti-30Nb-3Fe (wt.%) alloy was produced by voltaic 
arc melting using non-consumable tungsten electrodes and 
a water-cooled copper crucible under a highly pure argon 
atmosphere. High-purity Ti, Nb and Fe elements were used as 
raw materials and compositional homogeneity was achieved 
by remelting and flipping the ingots several times. This was 
followed by heat-treating the samples at 1000 ºC for 24 h 
with subsequent water quenching. The resulting ingots were 
cold-rolled in multiple passes to decrease their thickness 
by up to 70% without intermediate annealing, thereby 
obtaining 2 mm thick plates. These plates were annealed 
at 700, 800, 900 and 1000ºC for 1 h and water-quenched 
in order to reduce the grain size. The samples’ chemical 
compositions were measured using the X-ray fluorescence 
(XRF) technique (Shimadzu 7000 EDX-7000). The contents 
of interstitial elements (O and N) were measured using a 
Leco TC400 analyzer.
A Kroll etching solution (6 mL of HNO 3, 3 mL of HF 
and 91 mL of H 2O) was employed in the metallographic 
preparation. The samples’ microstructure was investigated 
by optical microscopy (OM) using a Olympus BX60M 
microscope, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a 
Zeiss EVO 15 microscope, and the crystalline phase was 
identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a PANalytical 
X’Pert diffractometer equipped with a Pixel X-ray detector, 
operating at 40 kV and 30 mA, with CuKα radiation, 
λ = 0.15406 nm.
To evaluate the β-transus temperature, recrystallized 
samples were heated to the β phase field in an argon atmosphere. 
To monitor their actual temperature, thermocouples were 
inserted into the samples and an iterative procedure was 
employed to measure the β-transus temperature: samples 
were heat treated at a certain temperature, from 569 to 593 ºC, 
for 15 min, after which they were water quenched. Their 
microstructure was then characterized by the method described 
in the previous paragraph. Whenever α precipitates were 
found in the β matrix, the procedure was repeated at a higher 
solution temperature. This procedure was carried out until 
the correct β-transus temperature was found and performed 
three times to take into account possible experimental errors.
Next, the samples were sealed in quartz tubes under an 
argon atmosphere and heat-treated at 400 and 500ºC, with 
subsequent water quenching. In order to reach the aging 
heat treatment temperature, three different heating rates 
(1, 10 and 30 ºC/min) were employed.
Vickers hardness was measured using a Buehler hardness 
tester with a 1 kgf load applied for 10 s. The average hardness of 
each sample was determined from 10 measurements. The elastic 
modulus was measured using the pulse-echo acoustic emission 
technique with a Panametrics-NDT 5072PR pulse receiver 
equipped with transducers operating at 5 MHz. Tensile tests 
were performed in an MTS 810 universal testing machine, 
with a strain rate of 1 mm/min. The samples were prepared 
by water jet cutting and machining, as shown in Figure 1.

According to the Ti-Nb phase diagram 24, the 
β-transus temperature of the Ti-30Nb alloy is 
approximately 614 ºC. The addition of Fe reduces the β-transus 
temperature, and according to theoretical calculations25, is 
approximately 589 ºC for Ti-30Nb-3Fe. This theoretical 
temperature was entirely consistent with that measured 
in this study (between 585 and 593 ºC). Since aging heat 
treatments should be performed at temperatures below the 
β-transus temperature, the aging temperatures selected 
were 400 and 500 ºC.
The initial condition of the samples for all the aging heat 
treatments was the solution heat treatment followed by water 
quenching, which produced microstructures composed of β 
and athermal ω phases, identified in a previous paper 11 by 
XRD and TEM due to small volume fraction and nanometric 
size of the athermal ω phase. The Vickers hardness and elastic 
modulus of the Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy after β solution heat 
treatment followed by rapid quenching were measured and 
were found to be 259 ± 3 HV and 83 ± 2 GPa, respectively.
Optimization of the aging heat treatment temperature 
was also based on data available on phase transformation 
temperatures of the Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy7,11,26. It is known that 
adding Fe to Ti-30Nb alloy results in an increase in the β phase 
stability and a decrease in the martensite start temperature, 
eventually shifting it to values below room temperature. As a 
result, no evidence of martensite decomposition was detected 
in the thermal analysis of Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy carried out 
previously11. The corresponding DSC curve also showed the 
exothermic reaction β→ω occurring at approximately 340 ºC 
in Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy and the dissolution of this metastable 
phase at temperatures close to 430 ºC. For comparison, 
the DSC results from Ti-30Nb alloy 11 revealed that β→ω 
occurred near 300 ºC and the dissolution of ω phase at 
around 460 ºC. Therefore, the addition of Fe to the Ti-Nb alloys 
increased the ω phase starting temperature and decreased ω 
phase dissolution temperatures, decreasing the temperature 
range in which ω phase is present in this system. The addition 
of Fe also decreased the α phase precipitation temperature, 
as expected, since Fe is a β stabilizer11.
Initially, solution heat-treated and water quenched 
Ti-30Nb-3Fe samples were aged at 400 ºC, applying 
different heating rates (1, 10 and 30 ºC/min) up to the final 
aging temperature. For each heating rate applied, samples 
were aged at 400 ºC for 10 min, 1 h, 4 h, and 8 h. It was 
observed that Vickers hardness and elastic modulus measured 
in samples heated at 10 and 30 °C/min did not present 
differences when compared to the β solution heat-treated 
and quenched samples. This meant that the heating rates 
applied here hindered isothermal ω phase precipitation. 
The application of a lower heating rate (1 °C/min) resulted 
in Vickers hardness and elastic modulus values that increased 
with the aging holding time, as shown in Figure 4.
Figure 5 shows the X-ray patterns of Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy 
aged to 400 ºC at a heating rate of 1 ºC/min for 10 min, 1 h, 
4 h and 8 h. The nanometric ω phase was detected by XRD 
after only 8 h of holding time when its volume fraction was 
appreciable. Precipitation of isothermal ω phase probably 
started earlier, during a shorter aging holding time, but its 
low volume fraction prevented its detection by XRD. Despite 
the fact that only β phase peaks were detected in some of 
the XRD patterns, higher values of hardness suggest that 
the heating rate applied (1 °C/min) provided favorable 
conditions for the formation and growth of isothermal ω 
phase. The XRD patterns of the Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy samples 
aged at 400 ºC, applying heating rates of 10 and 30 ºC/min, 
showed only β phase peaks.

The phase transformation mechanism of α phase 
precipitation in Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy is influenced by several 
factors. According to Ivasishin et al.17, β phase decomposition 
during an aging heat treatment causes competing ω and α phase 
precipitation mechanisms. Heating rate, aging temperature 
and holding time are essential to define the sequence of 
precipitation in continuous heating and isothermal aging.

In addition to Vickers hardness tests, the effects of the 
aging heat treatment conditions on mechanical behavior 
were investigated by analyzing tensile tests results. Samples 
subjected to aging at 500 ºC for 30 min, applying a heating 
rate of 1 ºC/min, were chosen because of their fine and 
homogeneously distributed α phase precipitation in the 
β phase matrix. The samples reached an ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS) of 765 MPa and 19% of elongation (EL). 
A solution heat-treated and water quenched Ti-30Nb-3Fe 
alloy presented an ultimate tensile strength close to 660 MPa 
with 18% of elongation11. The aging heat treatment performed 
in this study increased the mechanical strength without loss 
of ductility. The high density of fine α precipitates acts as 
an obstacle to dislocation motion in the β phase, providing 
increasing of hardness and mechanical strength29.

4. Conclusions
Based on the aforementioned results, it was concluded 
that the addition of Fe to Ti-30Nb alloy lowered the β-transus 
temperature to between 585 and 593 oC. It also decreased 
the martensite start temperature to below room temperature, 
increased the precipitation temperature and lowered the 
dissolution temperature of the ω phase, and lastly, decreased 
the α phase precipitation temperature. Compared to different 
solution temperatures, 700 oC was found to be highly effective 
in obtaining small grain sizes.
The results of this study also confirmed that the heating 
rate to the aging temperature is of paramount importance. 
Low heating rates enable isothermal ω precipitation and growth, 
providing favorable conditions for α phase precipitation. 
High heating rates made ω precipitation more difficult, 
hindering α phase nucleation. As the final aging temperature 
was increased from 400 to 500 oC, ω-assisted precipitation 
of α phase in Ti-30Nb-3Fe became easier. Applying a final 
aging temperature of 500 oC and a 1 ºC/min heating rate 
led to more intense ω phase precipitation, and therefore, a 
higher density of fine and dispersed α phase precipitates. 
The parameters of the aging heat treatment also interfered in 
the resulting Vickers hardness and elastic modulus values.
Compared to the solution heat-treated and water quenching 
condition, the aging heat treatment of Ti-30Nb-3Fe alloy 
led to higher Vickers hardness and mechanical strength, 
without loss of ductility.
