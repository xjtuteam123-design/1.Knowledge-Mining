Abstract
In the past decade, multi-principal element high-entropy alloys (referred to as high-entropy alloys, HEAs) are an emerging 
alloy material, which has been developed rapidly and has become a research hotspot in the field of metal materials. It breaks 
the alloy design concept of one or two principal elements in traditional alloys. It is composed of five or more principal ele-
ments, and the atomic percentage (at.%) of each element is greater than 5% but not more than 35%. The high-entropy effect 
caused by the increase of alloy principal elements makes the crystals easy form body-centered cubic or face-centered cubic 
structures, and may be accompanied by intergranular compounds and nanocrystals, to achieve solid solution strengthening, 
precipitation strengthening, and dispersion strengthening. The optimized design of alloy composition can make HEAs exhibit 
much better than traditional alloys such as high-strength steel, stainless steel, copper-nickel alloy, and nickel-based superal-
loy in terms of high strength, high hardness, high-temperature oxidation resistance, and corrosion resistance. At present, 
refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) containing high-melting refractory metal elements have excellent room temperature 
and high-temperature properties, and their potential high-temperature application value has attracted widespread attention 
in the high-temperature field. This article reviews the research status and preparation methods of RHEAs and analyzes the 
microstructure in each system and then summarizes the various properties of RHEAs, including high strength, wear resist-
ance, high-temperature oxidation resistance, corrosion resistance, etc., and the common property tuning methods of RHEAs 
are explained, and the existing main strengthening and toughening mechanisms of RHEAs are revealed. This knowledge 
will help the on-demand design of RHEAs, which is a crucial trend in future development. Finally, the development and 
application prospects of RHEAs are prospected to guide future research.

particular structure of HEAs, it is easy to perfectly com-

HEAs is not only frontier but also has academic research 
value and broad application prospects.

1.2 Definition of HEAs
There are two definitions of HEAs: one is based on com-
position and the other is based on entropy. Therefore, there 
must be an alloy system that only satisfies one of the two. 
It is controversial whether this type of alloy can be called 
a high-entropy alloy. [21]. But so far, scholars generally 
believe that it is not necessary to strictly follow the defini-
tion of HEAs because a more extensive composition range 
is conducive to discovering more new alloys with excellent 
properties [11, 22].
1.2.1 Composition‑Based Definition
Yeh et al. [15] first defined HEAs as "alloys composed of five 
or more major elements with equal molar ratios." Afterward, 
the requirement for equimolar concentration was reduced, 
and the definition was extended to "alloys composed of 5 or 
more major elements, and the mole fraction of each major 
element is between 5 and 35%. In other cases, In the case of 
essential elements, the content of each minor element needs 
to be less than 5%", which greatly expands the composition 
range of HEAs [23– 25], and can also make better use of 
the addition of trace alloying elements to control various 
properties of HEAs, such as high strength, wear resistance, 
high-temperature oxidation resistance, corrosion resistance, 
etc. Therefore, the definition of high-entropy alloy based on

in the study of refractory metal HEAs. And because it can 
maintain high strength at 1600 °C, it immediately attracted 
wide attention from researchers in the high-temperature field

added alloy elements, more and more alloy systems related

has gradually expanded from refractory high-entropy alloy

refractory high-entropy alloy systems based on transition 
metal elements and supplemented by other elements (non-
refractory metal elements and non-metal elements).
Recent studies have shown that due to the inherent 
"cocktail effect" of HEAs, the types and contents of alloy -
ing elements in RHEAs have extraordinary effects on phase 
stability, composition uniformity, and microstructure, thus 
affecting the comprehensive properties of RHEAs. At the 
same time, due to the inherent "lattice distortion effect" of 
HEAs, the addition of alloying elements will affect the origi-
nal lattice of the RHEAs. The first is to enter the gap position 
of RHEAs, the second is to replace the atoms on the original 
lattice of RHEAs, and the third is to form the precipita-
tion phase/dispersion phase. These effects will change the 
toughening mechanism of RHEAs. The alloying elements 
are the "carrier" and "coordinator" of various comprehen-
sive properties and mechanical properties of RHEAs. So that 
many RHEAs have excellent comprehensive properties after 
element regulation. Therefore, it will always be an indis -
pensability research direction in the future on the element 
regulation of RHEAs. Therefore, this paper reviewed the 
research status and preparation methods of RHEAs from 
the perspective of alloy elements and analyzed the micro-
structure of each system. Then, various properties of RHEAs 
were summarized, including high strength, wear resistance, 
high-temperature oxidation resistance, and corrosion resist-
ance. The commonly used performance control methods of 
RHEAs were expounded, and the existing main strengthen-

2 Preparation of RHEAs

and the significant differences in melting point between

to cause microscopic changes. Macro-segregation (usually 
in the form of dendritic structure), pores, and solidification 
defects such as residual stress [32, 33] bring numerous chal-
lenges to preparing RHEAs. As shown in Fig.  5, the meth-
ods commonly used to prepare refractory high-entropy alloy 
blocks include arc melting [31, 34–37], induction melting 
[37–39], laser melting [40], spark plasma sintering [41–43], 
etc. Among them, arc melting is currently the most widely

application fields of HEAs.
3 Phase Selection of RHEAs
3.1 Reported Microstructure of RHEAs
Our current collection of HEA systems seem to indicate a 
rule: RHEAs synthesized from pure transitional refractory 
elements will only exhibit a BCC phase. According to exist-
ing reports, although some of these alloy systems may have 
two or more structures, they are still BCC structures. If these 
alloys want to precipitate the second phase of other crystal 
structures, other non-refractory metal elements, such as Cr, 
Al, Si, C, etc., need to be added.
In this paper, the series of HEAs with refractory metal 
elements as the principal component and some non-refrac-
tory metal elements are called RHEAs. Therefore, as listed 
in Table 1, according to the phase structure, RHEAs systems 
can be roughly divided into three categories:
(1) A solid solution RHEA with a single-phase BCC struc-
ture;
(2) It is a RHEA system in which the second-phase inter -
metallic compound (IM) is precipitated on the BCC 
solid solution matrix, which mainly includes the BCC/
B2 coherent precipitation strengthening phase and the 
Laves precipitation strengthening phase.
(3) Other strengthening phases precipitated on the BCC 
matrix, such as HCP [ 20, 52] AlxZr5 [53, 54], M5Si3 
[55–57], etc.
Senkov et al. [27, 58, 59] studied NbTaMoW, VNbTa-
MoW, TaNbHfZrTi alloys, and the results showed that they 
are all a single BCC solid solution phase. Wu et al. prepared 
HfNbTiZr alloy [60], Tian et al. studied TiZrNbMoVx alloy 
[61], and Huang et al. prepared Ti35Zr27.5Hf27.5Nb5Ta5 alloy 
which also have the same structure [62].
Because the Cr element has a high mixing enthalpy 
with other refractory elements, the addition of the Cr ele-
ment will increase the enthalpy value of the alloy system 
and causes the instability of the BCC solid solution phase. 
 NbCrMo0.5Ta0.5TiZr [63], CrNbTaTiZr, CrNbTiZr, and 
CrNbTiVZr alloys [64] will also have the formation of Laves 
phase or other solid solution phases, but the primary phase 
is still the BCC solid solution phase.
To adjust the properties or reduce the overall density of 
RHEAs, other non-refractory metal elements such as Al or 
non-metallic elements such as Si are also added to RHEAs. 
These alloys are still based on the BCC solid solution phase 
as the primary phase. For example, with the increase in Al 
element content in AlxHfNbTaTiZr RHEA, a small amount 
of new rich-(Al, Hf, Zr) phases will be formed in the alloy.
Yurchenko et al. annealed AlNbTiV at 1200 °C for 24 h, 
and the alloy obtained has a B2 ordered single-phase struc-
ture.AlNbTiVZrx RHEA is acquired after alloying with the 
Zr element. The study found that the order of the B2 phase 
in this RHEA has changed, and the Al3Zr5 phase is precipi-
tated. [65].
Liu et al. [ 57] synthesized a simple HfMo0.5NbTiV0.5 
alloy with a disordered BCC structure by induction levita -
tion melting. They found that multi-component silicides (Hf, 
Nb, Ti)5Si3 were generated inside RHEA by adding the Si 
element. This structure significantly improves the hardness 
and strength but reduces the malleability of the alloy.

4 Properties of RHEAs
4.1 Mechanical Properties
4.1.1 Room Temperature Strength
The crystal structure of the RHEAs matrix usually shows a 
BCC structure. RHEAs are similar to many BCC structural 
metals and alloys and lack ductility at room temperature 
(except for HfNbTaTiZr and some of its derivative alloys) 
[76, 77]. But RHEAs have higher strength than most metals 
and alloys. It shows obvious brittleness [78]. The RHEAs

4.4 Oxidation Resistance
It is well known that superalloys need to meet tremendously 
harsh conditions to be applied in high-temperature fields 
such as aerospace engines. Such materials need to have 
excellent high-temperature stability, a particular strength, 
and formidable high-temperature oxidation resistance. High-
temperature oxidation, also known as high-temperature cor-
rosion, is a process in which elements in the material react 
with the surrounding gas phase or other condensed matter 
at a high temperature according to thermodynamic and 
kinetic factors, resulting in their destruction [100]. Usually, 
the oxide film is formed on the metal surface during the 
oxidation process of the alloy. However, only in the oxide 
film with stable chemical properties, denseness, and fewer 
defects, a trivial difference in thermophysical parameters 
with the matrix and good combination can hinder the further 
oxidation of the alloy and protect the matrix [101, 102].
Due to the characteristics of refractory metal elements, 
the high-temperature oxidation resistance of RHEAs is not 
ideal. Compared with nickel-based superalloy Inconel 718 
[100, 103], most RHEAs have been highly combustion at 
high temperatures, and some have been completely com-
bustion at 1300 °C, especially for HEAs containing only 
refractory metals.

5 Mechanical properties of RHEAs 
controlled by alloying elements
The demand and pursuit for materials with high strength, 
high ductility, and high thermal stability have never disap-
peared in practical applications and scientific research. The 
design starting point of RHEAs is to develop new high-tem-
perature materials. At high temperatures, the contribution 
of entropy dominates thermodynamics, so this new metal 
material has high phase stability and sizable mechanical 
strength. At the same time, because it contains many kinds 
of refractory elements, this material has a good application 
prospect at high temperatures. Therefore, the research on the 
properties of RHEAs mainly focuses on hardness, compres-
sion properties at room temperature and high temperature, 
and high-temperature oxidation resistance. The properties 
of the alloy are closely related to the types and contents of 
the elements. Adding or removing elements in RHEAs can 
sizably change the balance between strength and plasticity, 
because the addition or substitution of alloy elements can 
affect the microstructure and phase composition of the alloy, 
thus affecting the mechanical properties.
These RHEAs use nine refractory metal elements (Cr, 
Hf, Mo, Nb, Ti, Ta, V, W, and Zr), and these can also con-
tain other metal elements, such as Al, Si, Co, and Ni. These 
added elements can reduce the density of the alloy (the 
density range is 13.8–5.6 g/cm 3). These elements also can 
form compounds to enhance alloys and affect other proper -
ties such as hardness and environmental resistance. At the 
same time, the hardness and wear resistance of RHEAs can 
be remarkably improved by introducing non-metallic ele-
ments such as C, N, B, and Si into the high-entropy alloy. In 
the beginning, considerable research has been carried out 
to study the RHEAs with single-phase solid solutions. With 
the deepening of research, the introduction of other non-
refractory metal elements often produces other phases, so 
people gradually turn a lot of work to two-phase and mul-
tiphase RHEAs. Existing studies have confirmed that these 
ordered solid solutions and intermetallic compounds can 
further enhance the RHEAs.
Based on many previous studies, this chapter summarizes 
the effect of alloy element regulation on RHEAs. The influ-
ence of alloy elements on wear resistance, corrosion resist-
ance, and oxidation resistance is described in the anterior 
part. There is not much repetition here, but only the descrip-
tion of strength and plasticity in mechanical properties.
The strength of metal originates from the binding force 
between atoms. There are two ways to strengthen metal 
materials. One is to improve the binding force between 
alloy atoms, improve its notional strength, and produce 
intact crystals without defects. Another strengthening way 
is to introduce many crystal defects in the crystal, such as 
dislocations and point defects. These crystal defects hinder 
the movement of dislocations, which will also significantly 
improve the strength of the metal. The traditional strengthen-
ing methods of metal materials are also suitable for RHEAs, 
and the strengthening degree of RHEAs is higher due to the 
slow diffusion effect and lattice distortion effect. At pre -
sent, most research is to use alloying element regulation to 
achieve strengthening. The main functions of alloying ele-
ments are as follows:
(1) Solid solution strengthening effect of alloy elements.
(2) Alloy elements promote the precipitation of new phases 
to strengthen RHEAs.
(3) The reinforcement compound formed by alloying ele-
ments was introduced to strengthen RHEAs.
(4) Introducing non-metallic elements to form high-entropy 
ceramics.
(5) Other strengthening methods, such as phase transfor -
mation, induced plasticity, etc.
5.1 Solid Solution Strengthening and Toughening
The principle of solid solution strengthening is to integrate 
metal or non-metallic atoms different from the matrix mate-
rial into the lattice gap or node of the matrix material (usu-
ally in the way of alloying). These will change the local 
lattice of the matrix to produce lattice distortion, resulting 
in lattice mismatch, thereby increasing the deformation 
resistance of the matrix metal [ 125, 126]. The solid solu -
tion strengthening effect of alloy elements on the matrix 
depends on the differences between solute atoms and solvent 
atoms in size, elastic properties, electrical properties, and 
other physical and chemical properties. And the solid solu-
tion strengthening is mainly realized through the interaction 
between solute atoms and dislocations [127, 128]. Compared 
with traditional refractory metals and alloys, RHEAs with 
multi-component solid solutions have no solute or solvent, 
and each element can be regarded as a solute atom. The 
difference in the atomic size of different principal elements 
will cause a severe lattice distortion effect. And the stress 
field caused by lattice distortion will hinder the dislocation 
movement, even playing a pinning role. These increase the 
yield strength of the alloy to some extent. The effect of solid 
solution strengthening can be further improved by further 
adding appropriate alloying elements into the single-phase 
solid solution RHEAs matrix.
The solid solution strengthening of traditional metals 
has two ways: replacement solid solution strengthening and 
interstitial solid solution strengthening. Usually, the radius 
difference between solute atoms and solvent atoms is used

they are divided into two categories according to their solid

(1) The replacement solid solution strengthening of 
matrix alloy is to replace the original “solute” atoms

caused by replacement solute atoms in solid solution

interaction before and after solute replacement. There-

solution in RHEAs is not significant.
(2) The interstitial solid solution strengthening of matrix

radius at the interstitial position of high-entropy solid 
solution, such as H, C, B, N, O, etc. Since the gap 
atomic radius is usually heavier than the size of the 
lattice gap, a large lattice distortion can be generated 
in the high-entropy alloy, so the strengthening effect on 
the high-entropy alloy is significantly higher than that 
of the displacement solid solution strengthening.
5.1.1 Application of Solid Solution Strengthening
Solid solution strengthening is the main strengthening 
mechanism of BCC RHEAs. Lin et al. [129] selected the 
Al element to improve the strength of high toughness BCC 
HfNbTaTiZr alloy. And it also can reduce the density of 
the RHEA. The obtained AlxHfNbTaTiZr RHEA (x = 0, 0.3, 
0.5, 0.75, and 1.0) maintained a single BCC solid solution 
in the as-cast state and revealed a typical dendritic struc-
ture. The addition of Al increased the compressive yield 
strength from 1073 ± 6 MPa to 1489 ± 7 MPa, but the duc-
tility decreased. The increase in yield strength is due to the 
solid solution strengthening caused by the increase in lattice 
distortion and the enhancement of bonding between Al and 
transition metal. Wang et al. [130] also studied the effect 
of Al content (x = 0–1.5) on the compressive properties of 
HfNbZrTi RHEA. The addition of Al did not change the 
phase composition of the overall BCC structure, but when 
x > 0.75, there was an orderly BCC phase in the alloy. There-
fore, the compressive yield strength increased rapidly, and 
the compressive strain decreased rapidly accordingly. These 
confirm the positive effect of Al content on its hardness, and 
Al can also reduce the density of the alloy. Therefore, the Al 
element plays an important role in RHEAs materials with 
lightweight and high strength.
As mentioned above, many RHEAs can still maintain 
good microstructure stability at high temperatures; for 
example, Senkov et al. first reported NbMoTaW and VNb-
MoTaW alloys [ 31]. It can be seen that for RHEAs with 
bcc structure, only solid solution strengthening can play a 
very significant strengthening effect at high temperatures.

5.2 Second‑Phase Strengthening and Toughening
In metal materials, it is one of the most commonly used 
strengthening methods to improve the yield strength by hin-
dering the dislocation movement through the second-phase 
particles. It is widely used in steel, aluminum-based, copper-
based, and superalloy materials, and it is a very effective 
strengthening mechanism at room temperature and high 
temperature. There are two forms of the second-phase rein-
forcement: one is precipitation strengthening (also known 
as precipitation strengthening or aging strengthening). By 
adding alloying elements that make up the reinforcement, 
the second-phase reinforcement is precipitated from the 
supersaturated solid solution matrix of RHEAs. The surface 
of the reinforcement particles is clean and pollution-free. 
The matrix and reinforcement are coherent or semi-coherent, 
with good wettability and high interfacial bonding strength. 
When heated to a high temperature, the particles can be dis-
solved in the alloy. The other is dispersion strengthening. By 
direct addition (through internal oxidation or powder metal-
lurgy, etc.) or one-time precipitation of the second-phase 
reinforcement in a non-coherent relationship with the matrix 
to produce the strengthening effect, the surface of the formed 
reinforcement particles is affected to varying degrees adhe-
sion of impurities. Because the matrix and reinforcement 
are in a non-coherent relationship, the interface bonding

5.2.2 Dispersion Strengthening
According to the above classification, dispersion strengthen-
ing can be divided into two categories: directly adding the 
second-phase enhanced particles and non-coherently pre-

5.2.2.1 Direct Addition of  Second‑Phase Reinforce ‑
ment There are two ways to add reinforcement directly 
to the RHEAs. One is to prepare composite materials by 
mixing the alloyed RHEAs powder with the reinforcement 
powder or block [167]. However, this method is trouble-
some to operate and rarely studied. The other way is to 
make composite materials by alloying the reinforcement 
powder or preform with the powder or block of HEAs ele-
ments under certain conditions. To increase the binding 
ability between the reinforcement and the matrix, a coating 
containing matrix elements is usually attached to the sur -
face of the reinforcement. Then, the treated reinforcement is 
added to the HEAs matrix. This method is relatively simple 
and has been widely used in the strengthening of ordinary 
HEAs. However, the plasticity of the alloy is often seriously 
reduced due to the non-coherent relationship between the 
added second-phase particles and the matrix, and it is trou-
blesome to match the strength and plasticity. Therefore, it is 
rarely reported in brittle RHEAs.
Yang et al. [168] achieved excellent HEAs strengthen-
ing and toughening by directly adding the second phase. 
They proposed that the high-density ductile multi-compo-
nent intermetallic compound nanoparticles (MCINPs) can 
be introduced into the high-entropy alloy system in a con-
trollable manner to achieve the second-phase strengthen-
ing [168]. Tensile test at ambient temperature shows that 
the (FeCoNi) 86-Al7Ti7 (Al7Ti7) and (FeCoNi) 86-Al8Ti6 
(Al8Ti6) alloys strengthened with MCINPs showed excel -
lent strengthening and toughening effects. These are differ -
ent from the traditional intermetallic compound-induced 
embrittlement. Among them, Al7Ti7 with 15 GPa tensile 
strength and up to 50% ductility is the best. It provides a 
good insight into strengthening and toughening by directly 
introducing the second phase.

Reinforcement Some non-coherent precipitation phases 
often appear in RHEAs, among which Laves phase inter -
metallic compounds are the most common. These non-
coherently brittle phases precipitate at the grain boundaries, 
which has serious damage to the mechanical properties of 
alloys, especially at high temperatures [64, 169–171]. The 
addition of Al, Cr, and V elements can promote the for -
mation of Laves phase and improve the high-temperature 
strength of the alloy. Al and Cr elements can also improve 
the oxidation resistance and reduce the density of the 
alloy due to their low density and the formation of com-
pact oxide film at high temperature, which plays an impor -
tant role in the field of high temperature [38]. Yurchenko 
et al. introduced the Al element into RHEAs. AlNbTiVZrx 
(x = 0,0.1,0.25,0.5,1.0,1.5) alloys were prepared by arc 
melting, and studied its microstructure and mechanical 
properties [53]. The results show that when the Zr content 
is 0, the microstructure is a single-phase B2 structure. With 
the increase in Zr content (x = 0.1, 0.25), the order degree 
of the B2 phase changed, and the Zr5Al3 phase gradually 
began to precipitate in the alloy. When the phase content 
was 1% and 5%, the compressive yield strength of the alloy 
increased, but the plasticity remained at 3.7%. When the Zr 
content increases to x = 0.5, Laves phase begins to precipi-
tate in the alloy, and the phase content is about 10%. When 
the Zr content increases to x = 1.5, the total content of Laves

5.2.3 Second‑Phase Strengthening Mechanism
It can be seen from the above that after adding certain non-
refractory metal elements and undergoing heat treatment, 
RHEAs often form the coherent or non-coherent (rarely 
reported semi-coherent) second phase in the matrix. These 
second phases change the simple BCC structure of the 
alloy. These precipitates interact with dislocation, consume 
the energy of dislocation movement, hinder dislocation 
movement, and produce the second-phase strengthening 
effect. Usually, the second-phase particles can be divided 
into deformable and non-deformable. These two types of 
particles interact with dislocations in different ways. And 
the strengthening paths are also divergent [179].

nanometer scale and is coherent with the matrix, the disloca-

is called the dislocation cutting mechanism. The interface 
between these particles and the matrix can form a lower 
coherent phase boundary, which is more stable at high tem-
peratures. The precipitated phase precipitates in the matrix.

5.4 Transformation Induced Plasticity 
Strengthening and Toughening
The development of high-strength and high-toughness mate-
rials has always been a hot topic in material research. How-
ever, most traditional strengthening methods will reduce 
plasticity when improving strength. RHEAs continue this 
trend. The alloys have the strength that traditional alloys are 
difficult to compare. And these also have extremely poor 
plasticity. In recent years, it has been considered that the 
phase transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) has been 
an effective method to overcome the strength extensibility 
tradeoff [206– 211]. TRIP has been confirmed in various 
metastable alloys, for example, TRIP steel [209, 212–214] 
and metastable titanium alloys [210, 215, 216]. Nowadays, 
the concept of HEAs is extended to non-equiatomic com-
ponents, which greatly broadens the alloy design window 
and makes the application of TRIP in HEAs possible. TRIP 
was first applied by Li et al. [206] in common HEAs sys-
tems such as Fe80−xMnxCo10Cr10 (at.%). Its basic idea is to 
destroy the stability of the high-temperature FCC phase and 
the stacking fault energy by reducing the manganese con-
tent, to promote the TRIP in the deformation process, and 
realize the synchronous improvement of strength and plas-
ticity. The experimental results show that with the decrease 
in Mn content, the deformation of the alloy changes from 
dislocation slip to twin deformation. When the Mn content 
decreases to 35 (at.%), the hexagonal martensite phase is 
formed during the tensile process. The alloy with Mn = 30 
(at.%) has BCC + HCP dual-phase structure, and the increase 
in HCP content during the deformation process makes the 
alloy obtain excellent strength and plasticity.
Based on the successful application of TRIP in ordinary 
HEAs, Huang et al. [52] used brittle BCC TaHfZrTi as a 
model material to reduce the thermal stability of the high-
temperature phase and the mechanical stability of the room 
temperature phase by metastable engineering. In the alloy, 
reduce the content of the BCC phase stabilizing element Ta 
to reduce the stability of the BCC phase. The dual-phase 
structure with staggered HCP laths in the BCC matrix was 
obtained, as shown in Figs.  19 and 20. The results show 
that with the decrease in Ta content, the strength of the 
alloy only slightly decreases under the combined effect of 
phase transformation strengthening and interface strength-
ening generated by the dual-phase structure. However, the 
introduction of the TRIP effect promotes the crystal interior 
deformation, increases the plasticity of the material, and at

7 Problems and Research Direction 
of RHEAs
Many conventional BCC phase refractory alloys have com-
mon problems: poor room temperature ductility, poor oxida-
tion resistance, and high density. And these make them dif-
ficult to process, limiting the application of alloys. However, 
RHEAs synthesized by a variety of refractory metal elements 
still face these problems. Therefore, it has become the unani-
mous expectation of all RHEAs researchers that how to make 
breakthroughs in optimizing oxidation resistance, reducing 
alloy density, improving room temperature ductility, and pro-
cessing ability.
As a new concept of alloy design, high-entropy alloy breaks 
away from the category of traditional alloy design. The design 
idea of HEAs covers the broad untapped composition space 
in the central area of the multivariate alloy phase diagram. 
Nowadays, the research and development of RHEAs focus 
on performance optimization (matching a variety of excellent 
properties, such as high-temperature resistance (high-temper-
ature strength, oxidation resistance) and room temperature 
plasticity), deformation and strengthening mechanism, opti-
mization of the heat treatment process, and processing tech-
nology, and development of RHEAs with high-temperature 
adaptability. However, most of these studies are focused on 
element regulation, which is still too one-sided for the whole 
research field and broad composition space of RHEAs and 
more in-depth design and research are needed in the future. 
To improve the development speed of alloys and reduce the 
development cost, researchers began to gradually introduce 
“material genome engineering” into the design and research of 
RHEAs and achieved good results. It is believed that the pro-
portion of “material genome engineering” will become larger 
and larger in the future. However, it is undeniable that due to 
the inherent “cocktail effect” of HEAs, many RHEAs have 
obtained excellent comprehensive properties through trace 
element regulation, which will continue to be a significant 
research direction in element regulation.
8 Prospects and Future Opportunities
HEA is a new alloy design idea. The simple phase composi-
tion and excellent comprehensive performance make HEAs 
have good development potential. RHEAs with refractory 
metals as the main component are superior to the traditional 
refractory metals and alloys in property and may exceed 
the nickel-based superalloy. Therefore, it provides a broad 
alloy development idea for the development of new high-
temperature and high-strength alloys. Any components 
using nickel-based superalloys are potential applications 
of RHEAs, such as high-temperature components of aero-
engine, heat exchanger piping, chemical processing industry, 
and superalloy components in the military industry. These 
can also replace traditional refractory metals and alloys. And 
these are applied to heat-resistant materials of liquid rocket 
engine nozzle, nuclear reactor, and a high-temperature fur -
nace. In addition, RHEAs containing more elements have 
excellent room temperature strength and hardness and have 
a broader range of applications [237–239]. It can be used as 
an excellent structural material and wear-resistant material 
at room temperature to be made into cutting tools, abrasive 
tools, etc. Although many studies have focused on RHEAs, 
since they contain a variety of elements with different con-
tents and have a wide range of composition space, we have 
little understanding of the basis of RHEAs, which also pro-
vides unlimited possibilities for the discovery and develop-
ment of new alloys. Based on the existing RHEA materials 
and the reported literature, there are several future evolution 
prospects and opportunities are proposed.
8.1 Theory of Phase Formation
The composition elements of RHEAs are complex, and at 
the same time, additional alloying elements are introduced. 
The research scope of the RHEAs has expanded from the ini-
tial single-phase solid solution alloy with equal molar ratio 
to multi-component dual-phase and multiphase alloys, and 
then to complex component alloys. At present, the design 
of RHEAs is mainly based on the trial and error method of 
basic alloy systems, and there is no scientific theory of alloy 
element selection. And it is troublesome to obtain the multi-
variate phase diagram as traditional alloys. So far, there is no 
scientific crystallization solidification theory or solid-phase 
transformation theory to guide the RHEAs design. Although 
many scholars have designed different series of HEAs with 
theoretical guidance from the fields of mixing enthalpy, 
mean atomic radius difference, and valence electron con -
centration, these theoretical bases have not been recognized 
uniformly in the academic circle. The resultful guidance of 
phase formation theory is essential for novel RHEAs with 
specific structural properties in future development, which 
have high academic value and need to be solved urgently.

The average melting point of each element in RHEAs 
is high, and it is often impossible to prepare bulk alloy

materials with uniform structure and composition and no

the advantages, such as uniform composition, fine structure, 
and dispersed distribution of the second phase, etc. And 
these are prospective to shine in the preparation of RHEAs. 
At the same time, the preparation of high melting points 
and HEAs by laser 3D printing technology may become an 
effective method for preparing HEAs with the high melting

on the modification of RHEAs after forming is progressing 
slowly. To make the structure of RHEAs controllable and 
apply it to industrial production and life as soon as possible, 
it is necessary for the majority of scientific researchers to 
further study its forming mechanism, explore more efficient 
and inexpensive preparation techniques, and form a set of 
relatively complete basic theory.
8.3 Study on the Properties of Alloys
The four primary effects of HEAs are still the chief theo-
retical basis for studying their structure and properties. 
The high-entropy effect and slow diffusion effect can sig-
nificantly affect the structure of the alloy. And the lattice 
distortion effect and the cocktail effect of HEAs can effec-
tively control the properties. Most of the RHEAs currently 
publicly reported have poor plasticity and toughness at room 
temperature, high density, and poor high-temperature oxi -
dation resistance. Therefore, how to develop an alloy sys-
tem with excellent comprehensive properties (such as low 
density, high strength, certain room temperature plasticity 
and toughness, and oxidation resistance, etc.) will be the 
development direction of RHEAs. Many current types of 
researches can solve one of the problems, but it is difficult 
to do both. It is expected that the alloy structure can be con-
trolled by adjusting the alloy element composition and alloy 
modification, thereby improving the brittleness-density-oxi-
dation resistance matching problem of RHEAs. Nowadays, 
it is more limited to the regulation of a single alloy element. 
There are few reports on the coordinated adjustment and 
control of multiple alloy elements, and the research on non-
metallic elements on RHEAs is relatively single, especially 
the B element. At the same time, the discovery of TRIP in 
RHEAs has promoted the research process of alloy structure 
regulation. We can also boldly propose to achieve twinning 
induced plasticity (TWIP) effect in RHEAs, BCC–FCC 
two-phase solid solution structure, and the more popular 
heterostructures in ordinary HEAs, etc. In addition, the 
research on alloy wears resistance and corrosion resistance, 
etc. It is limited to tests at room temperature, and there are 
few studies on fatigue resistance and radiation resistance at 
higher temperatures. In the future, it is necessary to study 
the microstructure and structure of the alloy to reveal the 
formation mechanism of the microstructure. And it is needed 
to clarify the relevant mechanism of strength and toughness, 
corrosion resistance, and oxidation resistance of the alloy.
8.4 Exploration of Strengthening and Toughening 
Mechanisms
The analysis of the above strengthening and toughening 
mechanism shows that the primary plastic deformation 
mechanism of RHEAs is dislocation slip, and the stress-
induced phase transformation mechanism is also found. The 
plastic deformation mechanism is affected by alloy com-
position, deformation conditions, and other factors. From 
the perspective of fundamental physics, the deformation 
and strengthening mechanisms found in RHEAs and even 
in the whole HEAs system are generally similar to those of 
traditional principal component alloys. However, the crys-
tal lattice of HEAs containing most primary elements with 
different atomic sizes and chemical properties is seriously 
distorted. In this distorted lattice, the generation, interaction, 
and movements of various crystal defects, such as vacancies, 
dislocations, stacking faults, and crystal boundaries, should 
reasonably be different from those in traditional alloys. But 
no convincing correlation study has been fully and undis-
putedly revealed, which may be an important direction for 
future fundamental research. In addition to the solid solution 
strengthening mechanism, other strengthening mechanisms 
have not been proved to be different from the traditional 
alloy strengthening mechanism. It is frustrating that the solid 
solution strengthening model is a one-sided, and more in-
depth consideration of lattice distortion, elastic mismatch, 
and local chemical order is needed. At present, the related 
strengthening and toughening mechanisms of RHEAs need 
to be studied in depth. Examination of the strengthening 
mechanism, multi-mechanism, multi-structure, cross-scale, 
and high dispersion are the problems that should be con-
sidered. The focus is to realize that RHEAs still have good 
plasticity and toughness while maintaining high strength. At 
the same time, the high-strength and high-plastic behavior 
of some RHEAs with low stacking fault energy and how to 
widely realize it in RHEAs need to be clarified. The diversi-
fication of application potential of high strength, toughness, 
and HEAs is enough to bring enormous economic benefits 
to society. The study of RHEAs with high strength and 
toughness not only has frontier academic value but also has 
immeasurable economic benefits and strategic significance.
