Effect of Alloy Preheating on the Mechanical Properties
of As-Cast Co-Cr-Mo-C Alloys
C. MONTERO-OCAMPO, M. TALAVERA, and H. LOPEZ
The effect of various alloy preheatings followed by full solid solution treatments on the resultant
strength and ductility of as-cast Co-Cr-Mo-C alloys was investigated. Three preheating temperatures
were evaluated: 815 7C, 950 7C, and 1100 7C for 4 hours and then solid solution treated at 1225 7C
for 4 hours. Tensile and compressive tests were carried out on the heat-treated alloys. It was found
that the strength and ductility of the heat-treated alloys exhibited signi®cant improvements over the
as-cast condition. In particular, optimum ductility of the heat-treated alloys and alloy strength were
promoted by preheating at 815 7C. A relatively ®ne grained structure coupled with a uniform dis-
tribution of second-phase particles promoted homogeneous plastic deformation in the bulk. Fracto-
graphic observations indicated that the exhibited ductility was associated with the development of
numerous plastic bands combined with band interlockings. Alloy preheats at 950 7C and 1100 7C
prior to solutionizing lead to inferior strength and ductility. Although preheating at 1100 7C led to
slight improvements, in both cases, the fracture path was dominated by the presence of continuous
carbide ®lms surrounding the dendritic grains. Hence, less than optimum combinations of strength
and ductility were achieved by the heat treatments at the higher temperatures.
I. INTRODUCTION
INVESTMENT castings made out of Co-Cr-Mo alloys
have been widely employed in the manufacture of surgical
prostheses due to their high corrosion resistance and ade-
quate mechanical properties.[1] However, in the as-cast con-
dition, preferential distributions of extensive interdendritic
and grain boundary carbides signi®cantly effect the alloy
ductility. Thermal processing has been commonly used in
Co alloys to modify the potential carbide precipitation re-
actions. Various carbide microstructures [2] can be produced
which in turn might lead to improved alloy strength. In
particular, the development of stacking faults and twins
during alloy heat treating seems to play a key role in the
resultant carbide distribution and morphology. [2]
The work of Rajan and Van der Sande[3] shows that aging
of as-cast or wrought ASTM-F75 Co alloys promotes the
formation of fcc stacking faults as well as a martensitic
transformation from fcc to a heavily faulted hcp phase. In
their work, preferential precipitation was found at intrinsic
stacking faults (ISFs), which manifested as intragranular
striations. This was further con®rmed by Taylor and Wa-
terhouse,
[4] who found that aging between 650 7C and 1150
7C a previously solutionized Co alloy resulted in prefer-
ential precipitation of M 23C6, carbides at intragranular stri-
ations. Apparently, the observed striations consisted of
densely packed ISFs, which became copious at lower aging
temperatures. Aging at elevated temperatures where the sta-
bility of the fcc matrix is signi®cantly improved reduces
the extent of intragranular striations. At the higher temper-
atures, the developed striations are relatively coarse and
discontinuous, which in turn affects the active precipitation
reactions. In particular, Taylor and Waterhouse found that

II. EXPERIMENTAL
Table I shows the chemical composition of the experi-
mental alloy used in this work. An ASTM-F75 cobalt-based
alloy was induction melted under vacuum and cast under
an argon atmosphere at 1450 7C in a mold preheated at 800
7C. The molds employed where cylindrical of 18-cm length
by 1.27-cm diameter. In addition, during alloy melting, the
carbon contents were adjusted to ®nal values of 0.29 and
0.35 pct. The specimens used for mechanical testing were
machined from the cylindrical bars obtained by investment
casting. The alloys were tested in the as-cast condition and
after heat treating. The heat treatments consisted of alloy
preheating at a given temperature, with some specimens
solutionized at 1225 7C. In both cases, the heating time was
®xed at 4 hours and the samples were protected from sig-
ni®cant oxidation by a graphite coating. Each heat treat-
ment was followed by water quenching at room
temperature. Three preheating temperatures were chosen
using as a reference the time-temperature-precipitation di-
agram available for this alloy system.
[2]
Alloy preheating treatments were aimed at inducing three
different carbide morphologies: ( a) precipitation at intra-
granular striations, as well as a grain boundaries with la-
mellar structure at 815 7C, (b) a transition to blocky-type
morphology by preheating at 950 7C, and ( c) precipitate
coarsening and spheroidization at 1100 7C. The solution-
izing temperature exceeded the required minimum (1210 7C
for .1 hour) suggested to ensure satisfactory results, [9] but
it was low enough to avoid incipient grain boundary melt-
ing.
Tension and compression tests were carried out in both
as-cast and heat-treated alloys. The ASTM E8-69 standard
was employed for tension testing, whereas cylindrical spec-
imens of 9-mm length by 6-mm diameter were used for
compressive straining. Both tests were carried out at a de-
formation rate of 1.5 mm zmin
21 using a 15 ton Instron test-
ing machine model 4206. A minimum of two test
specimens were tested for a given heat treatment and car-
bon content. This was followed by Rockwell C hardness
measurements of the alloys in the as-cast condition and af-
ter heat treating. Finally, optical microscopy and scanning
electron microscopy (SEM) of the tested specimens were
employed for fractographic and microstructural determina-
tions. Electrolytic etching with a chromic acid solution was
used to reveal the microstructure. Finally, quantitative de-
terminations of grain and carbide sizes were carried out in
an OMNIMET image analyzer.

IV. DISCUSSION
For both carbon contents, alloy preheating at 815 7C pro-
duced a ®ne carbide precipitate and ®ne grained structure
with a relatively large volume fraction of precipitates con-
®ned to the grain boundaries. The obtained microstructures
were in agreement with those reported in a previous work.
[2]
Furthermore, preheating at 815 7C led to relatively large
stacking fault densities coupled with intragranular stria-
tions. These striations became apparent after preferential
precipitation of carbides on the stacking faults. The precip-
itation reactions can be attributed to preferential solute dif-
fusion to the faulted regions, as indicated by Taylor and
Waterhouse.
[2] In their work, they suggested that Cr and Mo
solutes diffuse preferentially at stacking faults due to the
local hexagonal crystal structure. Also, carbide precipitation
is favored at ISF hcp Co {0001} planes, which are nearly
identical to M 23C6 {111} planes. Alloy preheating at 950
7C induced a transition in the precipitate morphology, prob-
ably as a result of a sharp reduction in the stacking fault
density. Taylor and Waterhouse [4] found that at or above
these temperatures, there is a change in the active nuclea-
tion and growth mechanisms responsible for the carbide
precipitation reactions. Under these conditions, nucleation

In the present work, the contribution of solid solution
strengthening can be assumed to be the same for any of the
heat treatments employed. Hence, the main differences in
alloy strengthening are related to variations in microstruc-
tural features such as the location and size of precipitates,
as well as to grain coarsening. The results of this work
indicate that the ductility and strength of as-cast Co-based
alloys are signi®cantly improved by proper selection of the
heat treating cycle. In particular, alloy preheating at 815 7C
gives rise to optimum properties as it promotes the devel-
opment of a ®ne precipitate distribution coupled with a ®ne
grained structure. Accordingly, the in¯uence of the den-
dritic structure on the exhibited properties was greatly min-
imized, and homogeneous deformation in the alloy bulk
was prevalent.

sy) exhibited by preheating at
815 7C can be explained by the expected dependence on
grain size d (sy a 1/d1/2) and precipitate spacing l (sy a 1/l).
This, in turn, suggests that relatively homogeneous stress
buildups might develop in the matrix and across grain
boundaries. Consequently, inhomogeneous stress localiza-
tions by band intersections at grain boundaries are greatly
minimized. This is particularly important in these alloys,
where most dislocations are easily dissociated and their in-

localization.
[12] In addition, it seems that the relatively ®ne

In 0.35 pct C alloys, the increase in the volume fraction
of carbides provided alloy strengthening through interac-
tions of the carbides with the bulk dislocations. This ex-
plains the mixed mode of fracture in which signi®cant
ductile dimples were developed on the fracture surfaces
(Figure 10(a)). Although, important improvements in alloy
strength and ductility were found by preheating at 815 7C,
in other preheats, the resultant properties were less than
optimum. In the alloys preheated at 950 7C and 1100 7C,
the exhibited properties can be related to appreciable car-
bide and grain coarsening. From the experimental outcome,
it can be inferred that continuous grain boundary carbide
®lms provide brittle paths for fracture, explaining the ex-
hibited fracture modes (Figures 9(b) and (c)). In addition,
the lack of signi®cant plastic deformation bands indicates
that the presence of coarse grains and coarse particles se-
riously inhibits dislocation dissociation reactions as well as
the motion of extended dislocations. Hence, it is expected
that the semicontinuous brittle phase and the dendritic
structure both dominate in the exhibited properties. More-
over, the lack of signi®cant precipitation in the bulk cou-
pled with coarse grained structures can result in severe
strain localization. Accordingly, any buildup of stress in-
compatibilities at the grain boundaries will be expected to
trigger a crack along the weak path constituted mainly by
continuous carbide ®lms.
Although alloy preheating at 1100 7C did not give rise
to optimum strength, it provided better ductility and
strength than the as-cast counterparts, or the alloys pre-
heated at 950 7C. This is probably due to the modi®cation
in carbide morphology by the active interfacial spheroidi-
zation mechanisms, which took place at 1225 7C. This in
turn seems to reduce the extent of serrations exhibited by
the carbide interfaces. As a result, the expected stress in-
compatibilities might be signi®cantly reduced. Alterna-
tively, plastic deformation in the form of bands or dimples
(Figure 10(a)) might contribute to the relaxation of stress
buildups. This is particularly apparent from the maximum
compressive strength and ductility achieved by the alloy
preheated at 1100 7C (0.29 pct C). The effect of carbon
alone, within the 0.29 to 0.35 pct C range, does not seem

Finally, a martensitic transformation induced by plastic
straining has been suggested to occur in Co-based alloys
due to their low stacking fault energies.
[13] However, strain-
induced martensitic transformations are limited to low car-
bon alloys.
[14,15] Apparently, at the solid solution
temperatures, carbon goes in solid solution in the fcc ma-
trix. Under these conditions, these alloys seem to ``lose''
their ability to promote the nucleation of
ε-martensite em-
bryos.[13] The formation of martensitic embryos by a fault-

carbon is the main element entering solid solution, it is
likely that its presence at ISF intersections would hinder
the development of the appropriate dislocation con®gura-
tions for the formation of stable embryos. Therefore, even
when a strain-induced martensitic transformation might be
active, it is likely that it might only provide a small con-
tribution to the total alloy strength and ductility.
V. CONCLUSIONS
Optical microscopy observations indicated that Co-Cr-
Mo-C alloys fabricated by investment castings exhibited
large microstructural defects, which included interdendritic
carbides, solute segregation, relatively large grains, and po-

The tensile properties of the heat-treated alloys exhibited
signi®cant improvements in ductility and strength when
compared with the as-cast counterparts. The main effect of
alloy preheating was manifested as a removal of the exten-

breakup of the dendritic grain structure. This led to the
development of a homogeneous equiaxial grained structure
and the consequent improvement in strength and ductility.
