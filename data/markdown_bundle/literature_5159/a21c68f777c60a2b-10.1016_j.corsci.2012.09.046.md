abstract
Isothermal oxidation was carried out on c0-strengthened Co–Al–W–B–Ni and Co–Al–W–B–Si superalloys
at 800, 900, and 1000 /C176C. Ni-addition to Co–Al–W–B alloys leads to an absence of B within the inner oxide
layer and at the grain boundaries, as indicated by ToF-SIMS. As a result, also inner Al 2O3-formation is
inhibited, which leads to inferior oxidation properties compared to Ni-free alloys. In contrast, Si-addition
enhances formation of protective Al 2O3 at 900 and 1000 /C176C due to enhanced selective Al-oxidation. Si-
containing phases at the oxide/alloy interface, at the grain boundaries, and within precipitates are of sub-
ordinate importance but may further improve oxidation resistance.

1. Introduction
Conventional corrosion resistant Co-base superalloys exhibit
poor mechanical properties at high temperatures compared to c0-
strengthened Ni-base superalloys [1]. Therefore, these alloys are
used in mechanically lower loaded parts of gas turbines, aircraft
engines, or in corrosive environments [2,3]. Recently, Co-base al-
loys of the system Co–Al–W were developed, showing high tem-
perature strength values comparable to conventional Ni-base
superalloys, due to the strengthening effect of a stable ternary
Co3(Al,W) compound with the L1 2 structure, which precipitates
as a cuboidal submicron phase ( c0-phase) embedded in the fcc c-
Co solid solution phase [4]. This is similar to the strengthening of
Ni-base superalloys. However, the high temperature oxidation
behaviour of the new Co-base alloys has to be improved consider-
ably. So far, very little research [5] has been carried out in this ﬁeld
compared to Ni-base superalloys [6–10]. Therefore, Klein et al.
[11,12] systematically explored the oxidation resistance and mech-
anisms in air for c0 -strengthened Co–Al–W superalloys at different
temperatures (800 and 900 /C176C) and studied the inﬂuence of differ-
ent alloying elements, such as B or Cr. Depending on the composi-
tion of the alloy as well as on the oxidation time and temperature,
the formation of protective inner Al 2O3 and Cr-rich scales can be
observed, in addition to cobalt oxides and oxides containing
mainly Co, Al, and W. However, Cr leads to a decrease of c0-stability
[12]. In addition to the already reported improved mechanical
properties of polycrystalline B-containing superalloys [1,13–15],
Klein et al. [11,12] revealed highly improved oxidation resistance
and oxide layer adhesion due to the addition of a sufﬁcient amount
of B (0.12 at.%). Si has been reported to improve the oxidation
resistance of e.g. Ni- and Fe-base superalloys at elevated tempera-
tures (T > 900 /C176C) because of SiO 2 formation [16,17], and due to the
beneﬁcial effect of Si on the formation of protective Al 2O3 [18].
However, up to now no research has been carried out for Si-con-
taining c0-strengthened Co-base superalloys.
Shinagawa et al. [19] reported that the c0-phase in the Co–Al–W
system widens with increasing Ni-content. This may allow for
higher contents of additional alloying elements, such as Cr, without
decreasing the stability of the c0-phase [12]. In addition, Differen-
tial Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) measurements revealed that the
c0-solvus temperature increases with Ni-content [19]. However,
no literature addressing the effect of Ni on high temperature oxida-
tion of this new class of materials can be found.
In the present study, the high temperature oxidation properties
of the novel c0-strengthened Co–Al–W–B–Ni and Co–Al–W–B–Si
alloys were explored at temperatures of 800, 900, and 1000 /C176Ci n
air. In addition to thermogravimetry, characterisation of the oxide
layer morphology and chemical composition was carried out by
means of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy disper-
sive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Furthermore, the inﬂuence of high
temperature oxidation on the distribution of Si and B in the alloy
matrix (especially at grain boundaries and within precipitates)
and the oxide layer was of interest. Therefore, time-of-ﬂight

2. Experimental
The experimental superalloys of this work were manufactured,
prepared, and investigated similarly to the B- and Cr-containing al-
loys of previous studies [11,12]. In this regard, the alloys were vac-
uum arc melted as ingots from commercially pure metals. This was
performed by ‘‘Hauner Metallische Werkstoffe’’ (HMW, Röttenbach,
Germany). The compositions of the investigated alloys and the uti-
lised abbreviations are given in Table 1 . In order to obtain the de-
sired c/c0 -microstructure, the as-cast alloys were solution heat
treated at 1300 /C176C for 12 h in argon atmosphere and subsequently
aged at 900 /C176C for 200 h in air. After cutting samples from the ingot
(with a dimension of approximately 20 mm /C115 mm/C12 mm), they
were wet ground using SiC paper up to grit 1200. Subsequent pol-
ishing, using lubricant and diamond suspensions up to a grain size
of 1 lm, was performed in order to remove residual scratches on
the sample surface. Isothermal oxidation was carried out in air at
800, 900, and 1000 /C176C for 500 h in a vertical tube furnace. The spec-
imens were attached to a microbalance (accuracy ±0.01 mg) by
means of a heat resistant freely suspended platinum or NiCr wire
and introduced into the vertical furnace after the set temperature
was reached. A constant temperature, which corresponds to the
set temperature of the furnace control, was solely present in a nar-
row area within the tube. Therefore, samples were always sus-
pended at this speciﬁc height of the tube in order to assure the
desired oxidation temperatures. Mass change (in mg/cm
2) was
determined simultaneously with increasing oxidation time by ther-
mogravimetry (TGA). Reproducibility of the curves was checked by
performing three different oxidation experiments on one alloy sys-
tem at a speciﬁc temperature. In the present work, representative
oxidation curves are presented in the corresponding ﬁgures. The
surface area of the samples was measured prior to oxidation in or-
der to provide comparability of the results. After ﬁnishing the oxi-
dation experiments, samples were immediately removed from the
vertical furnace and cooled off at room temperature. The morphol-
ogy of formed oxide layers and the c/c0-microstructure were inves-
tigated by SEM (Jeol JSM 6400, FE-SEM Hitachi S-4800, and FEI
Quanta 3D FEG) of prepared cross-sections, using the BSE (back-
scattered electron) mode. In order to calculate the mean oxide layer
thickness (and the standard deviation), at least 15 measurements
were carried out using SEM micrographs and image processing pro-
gramme ‘‘ImageJ’’. EDX (Oxford Instruments Inc.) measurements
were carried out in order to determine the atomic composition
across the cross-sections. Glow Discharge Optical Emission Spec-
troscopy (GDOES, RF GD Proﬁler, HORIBA Jobin Yvon GmbH) was
applied in order to conﬁrm the B-content of the alloys in Table 1 ,
since B is a light element and not detectable by EDX. Positive and
negative static ToF-SIMS (ION-TOF GmbH Münster, TOF.SIMS 5
spectrometer) allowed for determination of the B- and Si-distribu-
tion by means of ToF-SIMS mappings. The samples were irradiated
with a pulsed 25 keV Bi + liquid–metal ion beam. Signals were iden-
tiﬁed according to their accurate mass and isotopic pattern in the
high mass resolution mode ( m/Dm > 8000 at 29Si) and chemical
maps were recorded in high lateral (and low mass) resolution
mode. A lateral resolution of 200–300 nm and a sampling depth
of /C2410–20 Å is achieved with this setting. Images were recorded
in positive and negative polarity and care was taken not to exceed
the static limit of 1013 ions cm/C02. The brightest pixel corresponds to
the largest number of counts for the respective fragment (raging
from 2 to 109 counts).

3.2. Inﬂuence of Si-addition
Thermogravimetric results of the isothermally oxidised 0.12B
[11] and 0.12B2Si alloys (500 h at 800, 900, and 1000 /C176C in air)
are presented in Fig. 5 . Oxidation of the Si-containing alloy at
800 /C176C results in inferior properties, characterised by lower oxida-
tion resistance and continuous mass gain, compared to the Si-free
alloy. However, oxidation of the 0.12B2Si alloy at 900 and 1000 /C176C
leads to considerably higher oxidation resistance and therefore
much thinner oxide layers compared to the 0.12B alloy (see also
Table 2 ). In Fig. 6 , EDX mappings of the oxidised 0.12B2Si alloy
(500 h at 800 /C176C (a + b), 900 /C176C (c + d), and 1000 /C176C (e + f) in air) re-
veal that Si accumulates, similar to W and B [11,12], within the in-
ner oxide layer and at the grain boundaries. Comparing the
oxidation at 800 and 900 /C176C, higher amounts of Al can be detected
at the oxide/alloy interface after oxidation at 900 /C176C, very likely
forming protective inner Al 2O3. After oxidation at 1000 /C176C, there
are still several Al-rich zones visible. However, scale spalling
becomes an issue after removing the samples from the oxidation
furnace (see crack in Fig. 6 e and f).
In Fig. 7, an SEM micrograph (BSE mode) and EDX mappings of a
grain boundary in the oxidised Si-containing alloy (500 h at 900 /C176C
in air) are presented. In contrast to the Ni-containing alloys, pro-
nounced grain boundary precipitates can be detected in the Si-
containing sample. A high Co- and a low W-content indicate the
presence of a c-Co region close to the grain boundary, i.e. lack of
the c0-phase, whereas Co- and Al-rich precipitates (most likely
CoAl [20]), but also W- and Si-rich phases accumulate at the grain
boundary.
Since Si detection by means of EDX may be problematic due to
peak overlapping of Si and W, additional ToF-SIMS investigations
were carried out for the oxidised 0.12B2Si alloy (500 h at 900 /C176C).
In Fig. 8, ToF-SIMS mappings of the Si distribution near the sample
surface (a) and at the grain boundaries (b) are shown. The results
conﬁrm the presence of Si accumulations at the oxide/alloy inter-
face, at grain boundaries, and within precipitates.
4. Discussion
Supplementary SEM-studies ( Fig. 9 ) indicate that the addition
of Si and Ni to 0.12B alloys results in very similar c/c0-microstruc-
tures, characterised by a cuboidal c0-phase and a high c0-volume
fraction within the c-Co phase. However, Ni and Si addition to
0.12B alloys leads to highly different oxidation resistances and
grain boundary precipitation phenomena. It is known that the for-
mation of ductile B-containing intermetallic phases leads to im-
proved grain boundary cohesive strength [21] and therefore
better mechanical properties of B-containing high temperature al-
loys [1,13–15,23]. Klein et al. [12] attributes an improved oxide
layer adhesion on the investigated 0.12B alloys to the formation
of borides within the inner oxide layer. Similar mechanisms as dis-
cussed in Ref. [21] could be operative in our present study. The
present ﬁndings show that the addition of Ni to the 0.12B alloy
leads to an absence of B at the grain boundaries and in the inner
oxide layer. This is very likely one of the reasons for the observed
scale spalling on 0.12B18Ni alloys (oxidised for 500 h at 800 /C176Ci n
air), due to lower cohesive strengths between the oxide layer and
the alloy. The lack of B in the inner oxide layer and at the grain
boundaries may be explained by the higher solubility of B within
the alloy matrix due to the addition of Ni. In addition, it has been
reported that B enhances inner Al 2O3 formation and therefore con-
siderably increases oxidation resistance [12]. In case of Ni-contain-
ing alloys, this effect is suppressed which leads to an absence of a
continuous and protective inner Al 2O3 layer at the oxide/alloy
interface (see Fig. 2 ). Observed Al-accumulations within the inner

not exceed 1 at.% in order to avoid embrittlement and the forma-

superalloys may then be further improved by adding elements,
such as Si, which on the one hand form stable and protective oxi-

the c/c0-microstructure, such as Cr [12].
The investigation of the Si-containing 0.12B2Si alloy shows that
Si has a strong inﬂuence on the oxidation properties. In addition to
the formation of SiO 2 [16,17] and other Si-containing phases, it en-
hances the generation of protective inner Al 2O3 [18] at the oxide/
alloy interface (see Figs. 5 and 7 ) at elevated temperatures (900
and 1000 /C176C), which leads to improved oxidation resistance
(Fig. 4 ). The beneﬁcial effect of Si on inner Al 2O3 formation is ex-
pected to be the dominant factor for improved oxidation resistance
of the Si-containing alloys. Even though the 0.12B2Si alloy has a
lower Al-content that the 0.12B alloy (see Table 2 ), improvements
are considerable, which suggests that Si enhances selective oxida-
tion of Al. In addition, Si-rich precipitates in the alloy matrix and at
the grain boundaries (Figs. 6 and 7) may also increase the oxidation
resistance, since those phases may act as diffusion barriers. How-
ever, these phases are expected to decrease the mechanical proper-
ties of the alloy. Decreased creep strength at 850 /C176C was already
reported for novel B-free Co-base superalloys as a result of Si addi-
tion [23]. However, it would be of interest to investigate the
mechanical behaviour also on Si- and B-containing samples, such
as the 0.12B2Si alloy of this study, in order to determine the effect

5. Conclusions
Isothermal oxidation in air was carried out on different c0-
strengthened Co–Al–W–B–Ni and Co–Al–W–B–Si superalloys at
800, 900, and 1000 /C176C. Ni-addition to Co–Al–W–B alloys leads to
an absence of B in the inner oxide layer and at the grain bound-
aries. Therefore, the formation of protective inner Al 2O3 is inhibited
as well, which results in lower oxidation resistances compared to
the Ni-free alloys. However, the different Ni-containing alloys
(0.12B9Ni and 0.12B18Ni) show comparable properties. In con-
trast, Si-addition to the Co–Al–W–B alloy enhances the formation
of protective inner Al 2O3 at temperatures above 900 /C176C. Si-contain-
ing phases at the oxide/alloy interface, at the grain boundaries, and
within precipitates may further improve the oxidation resistance
but are expected to decrease the mechanical properties of the al-
loy. Nevertheless, the effect of Si on Al 2O3 formation is the domi-
nating factor for improved oxidation resistance.
