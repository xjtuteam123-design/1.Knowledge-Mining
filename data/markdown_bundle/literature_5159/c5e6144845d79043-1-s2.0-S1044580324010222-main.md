guidance on structural stability. This study aimed to propose an element-addition method to create thermally

1. Introduction
Metallic materials with excellent mechanical properties at elevated 
temperatures are essential for various industrial applications, such as 
aerospace and high-temperature reactors [ 1 ]. Refractory high-entropy 
alloys (RHEAs), comprising elements with high melting points, 
evolved from the high-entropy alloy (HEA) community and have pro -
vided many novel alloys with excellent high-temperature properties 
[ 2 – 6 ]. Most reported RHEAs exhibit a single solid – solution structure in 
the as-cast state. The high configurational entropy of HEAs helps sta -
bilize the solid – solution phase over intermetallic compounds (IMCs) at 
high temperatures [ 7 ].
The single-phase structure in RHEAs generally provides higher 
softening resistance and operation temperatures than multiphase al -
loys [ 8 ]. However, although some RHEAs show structural stability at 
room temperature and above 1000 
◦
C, recent studies indicate that the 
effects of high configurational entropy on structural stability at 
intermediate temperatures were questionable, with IMCs formed in 
some single-phase RHEAs after annealing [ 9 , 10 ]. For example, Schuh 
et al. reported that the solid – solution phase of TiZrHfNbTa RHEA 
decomposed into a multiphase alloy, including an NbTa-rich body- 
centered cubic (BCC) phase and a ZrHf-rich phase [ 11 ]. The type and 
structure of the secondary phase also changed under different heat 
treatment conditions [ 11 ]. The phase decomposition in RHEAs occurred 
in a short time [ 12 , 13 ]. Chen et al. obtained similar results in 
TiZrHfNbTa RHEA after annealing at 700 
◦

heat-treated Al

Microstructure changes are often accompanied by significant shifts

for structural materials. Although obtaining an alloy with a stable phase 
structure and mechanical properties is vital to a potential engineering 
alloy, only a few studies have focused on the stability of phase structure 
or mechanical properties of the RHEAs. Factors affecting the phase

Besides stable microstructures, improving alloy performance at high 
temperatures is vital in the metallic community. Understanding the 
reasons behind the high-temperature softening of alloys can aid in 
improving their properties. Although significant high-temperature me -

decomposition is a primary cause for reduced high-temperature strength 
in unstable RHEAs [ 16 ]. However, the understanding of microstructure 
evolution and deformation mechanisms of a stable single-phase RHEA at 
intermediate temperatures is insufficient. Therefore, the second primary 
aim of this study was to investigate the microstructure evolution and 
deformation mechanisms in detail to provide further insights into 
improving the high-temperature mechanical properties of stable alloys.

phase-diagram calculations, to help obtain alloys with stable micro -

4.2. Deformation mechanisms
A continuous softening phenomenon was observed in the AH600, 
AH800, and AH1000 alloys, which sharply contrasted with the behavior 
of the ACMo7 alloy deformed at room temperature ( Fig. 6 a). The com -
bination of XRD and EBSD revealed that the dislocation motion was the 
primary deformation medium during room-temperature deformation. At 
600 
◦
C, both dislocation motion and GBS contributed significantly to the 
deformation process. Besides, DRX occurred during high-temperature 
deformation at 1000 
◦
C, leading to distinct mechanical properties in 
the alloy.
Fig. 14 presents schematic diagram illustrating deformation mech -
anisms across temperatures to help understand their differences. The 
deformation process was categorized into three stages. Initially, the 
activation of certain dislocations led to increased stress in the alloy 
(stage 1; Fig. 14 a, d, and g). Then, diverse response mechanisms 
occurred during deformation at various temperatures. Dislocations were 
activated and moved along the slip plane until they encountered GB 
during room-temperature deformation (stage 2; Fig. 14 h). Fig. 10 shows 
the accumulation of substantial dislocations at the GB. The dislocations 
interacted during movement, resulting in the disappearance of some 
dislocations even when the total number of dislocations continued to 
rise. As deformation progressed, more dislocations were activated, 
moving along the same slip plane (stage 3, Fig. 14 i). This strong inter -
action between dislocations led to increased compressive strength.
In stage 2, distinctions among the three deformation mechanisms 
became apparent. At 600 
◦
C, enhanced dislocation mobility enabled 
dislocations to overcome obstacles with reduced stress, facilitating 
plastic deformation (stage 2 of AH600, Fig. 14 e). The high temperature

5. Conclusions
The phase-diagram calculation approach was employed to develop 
RHEAs with stable microstructures. This method identified the optimal 
ranges for elemental composition in the AlMoNbTiHf alloy system to 
achieve stability. An alloy within these specified ranges was prepared 
and heat-treated over a wide temperature range for 100 h to validate the 
effectiveness of this method. The microstructure evolution and me -
chanical properties of the long-time annealed alloys were investigated. 
The deformation mechanisms were also discussed. The following con -
clusions were drawn: 
(1) Simple phase-diagram calculations provided a straightforward 
element-addition method for alloy design. The recommended 
elemental ranges for a stable microstructure in the AlMoNbTiHf 
system were 100 ≥ (NbTi) > 65; 33 ≥ Mo ≥ 0; 20 ≥ Hf ≥ 0; and 5 
≥ Al ≥ 0. The Mo7 alloy with composition within these specified 
ranges maintained a single-phase structure after annealing for 
100 h at 600 
◦
C, 700 
◦
C, 800 
◦

◦
C.
(2) The alloy annealed for 100 h demonstrated robust mechanical 
properties. The tensile yield strength ( σ
0 . 2
) and fracture elonga -
tion at room temperature for the A800 alloy were 832 MPa and 
11 %, respectively, whereas the compressive true PS at 800 
◦
C 
was 484 MPa. These properties were comparable to those typi -
cally observed in as-cast RHEAs, with similar results for the A600, 
A800, and A1000 alloys.
(3) Phase structure stability was closely related to alloy composition. 
Nb, Ti, and Mo favored the formation of a solid solution with a 
BCC structure at a temperature above 600 
◦
C, and hence were 
highly recommended for RHEA design. The Al content should be 
strictly controlled in BCC-structured RHEAs, as it tends to form 
IMCs with other elements at intermediate temperatures.
(4) The excellent mechanical properties were primarily attributed to 
dislocation motion. Dislocation pile-up and strong interactions 
contributed to continuous strength enhancement at room tem -
perature, whereas GBS and DRX were observed in specimens 
deformed at high temperatures. GBS and DRX played vital roles in 
promoting dislocation annihilation and reducing dislocation in -
teractions, thereby reducing flow stress during high-temperature 
deformation.
