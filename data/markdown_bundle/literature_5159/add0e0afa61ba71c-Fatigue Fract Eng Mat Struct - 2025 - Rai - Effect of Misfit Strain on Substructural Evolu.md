1 | Introduction
Directionally solidified (DS) nickel- based superalloys are 
widely used in turbine rotor blades due to their excellent high- 
temperature mechanical strength and oxidation resistance. 
During operation, these blades experience significant thermal 
fluctuations, which induce low- cycle fatigue (LCF) deformation 
[1–3]. The microstructure of these alloys contains ordered L1 2 
γ′ cuboidal precipitates, which are coherently distributed in a 
face- centered cubic (FCC) γ matrix [ 4–8]. The L1 2 γ′ precipi -
tates impede dislocation motion in the matrix, contributing to 
the elevated temperature strength of the alloys. Moreover, due 
to variation in chemical composition, the γ and γ′ phases exhibit 
different lattice constants, which introduce misfit strain at the 
γ/γ′ interfaces [2, 9].
Due to the misfit strain, misfit stresses of several hundred 
megapascals are generated, which significantly influence the 
evolution of the dislocation substructure during fatigue defor -
mation. Previous studies have demonstrated that under high- 
temperature LCF/creep deformation of Ni- based superalloys, 
dislocation networks form at the γ /γ′ interfaces, modifying the 
fatigue/creep performance of the alloy [2, 10, 11]. However, very 
little research has been conducted to investigate the effect of 
misfit strain on dislocation network configurations and their in -
fluence on the overall fatigue deformation behavior of the alloy 
under relevant service conditions.
Therefore, in this work, the role of misfit strain in the evolution 
of dislocation substructures during LCF deformation of a DS 
Ni- based superalloy was studied. It has been demonstrated how 
misfit strain modifies substructural evolution and, consequently, 
the fatigue performance of the alloy. To the best of my knowl -
edge, no such studies have been conducted on this alloy system. 
This research offers a unique opportunity to assess the intrinsic 
fatigue behavior of this alloy under realistic service conditions.
2 | Experimental Procedure
The CM 247 DS LC alloy used in this study was received as rods 
measuring 14 mm in diameter and 103 mm in length. The rods 
underwent homogenization at 1243°C for 8 h, followed by gas 
fan quenching (GFQ). Aging was carried out in two stages: first 
at 1080°C for 4 h, and then at 870°C for 20 h, with GFQ after 
each stage. The fully heat- treated rods are hereafter referred to 
as AR [ 2]. The composition (wt.%) of the alloy is 9.28 Co, 5.56 
Al, 8.0 Cr, 9.31 Co, 0.5 Mo, 9.5 W, 0.739 Ti, 1.421 Hf, 0.0179 B, 
0.011 Zr, 3.22 Ta, 0.07 C, and balance is Ni. The LCF test speci -
mens were prepared in accordance with ASTM E- 606 [12] and 
had the following specifications: gauge length of 18 mm, diam -
eter of 6 ± 0.1 mm, and surface roughness less than 0.2 μm. The 
LCF tests were conducted at a strain rate ( ε) of 0.001 s−1 with 
a strain ratio ( R) of 0, using a triangular waveform. A few ma -
chined specimens were exposed to thermal cycling for 500 h 
(HT500 specimens). The heat- treatment cycle involved heating 
the samples at 850°C for 2 h, followed by air cooling for 30 min, 
and a total of 250 such cycles were applied to the H500 samples. 
A few DS rods were subjected to the same thermal cycling as 
described previously, and LCF samples (HTR500) were subse -
quently machined from these rods. It should be noted that both 
the HT500 and HTR500 samples were subjected to identical 
thermal cycling conditions. For HT500, thermal cycling was 
applied directly to the machined LCF specimens, whereas for

The substructures of AR, HTR500, and HT500 fatigue- tested

croscope” (TEM) (JEOL- 2200- FS). For TEM analysis, foils mea -

the loading direction. The foil thickness was further reduced to 
25–40 μm using silicon emery paper and then perforated using 
a twin- jet electro- polisher. Twin- jet polishing was done in 6% 
HClO4 and 94% CH 3OH at − 25°C and 26 V. The misfit strain 
(δ) between the γ ′ and γ phases was calculated using a “Rigaku 
Smart Lab HR- XRD equipped with Johansson optics,” on a sam-
ple sectioned along the longitudinal axis of the loading direction.
3 | Results
Figure 1a depicts fatigue lives ( Nf) of AR, HTR500, and HT500 
specimens at 750°C and Δε/2 of 0.8%. It can be seen in Figure 1a 
that the N f is minimum for H500 specimens. The cyclic stress 
response curves of the DS alloy at 750°C and Δε/2 of 0.8% in AR, 
HTR500, and HT500 conditions are shown in Figure  1b. The 
alloy in AR and HT500 conditions shows cyclic hardening for 
the first 3–5 cycles; after that, softening is observed, whereas in 
HTR500 condition, cyclic hardening is observed up to 70–80 cy-
cles following softening.
The lattice misfit ( δ) of γ /γ′ was calculated using the Wilkens 
and Eckert method [2, 13].
The 2θ value for 200 asymmetric peaks was chosen for the anal -
ysis (Figure  2a–e) [3, 9 ]. The δ values calculated for the AR, 
HT500, and HTR500 and HT500 LCF- tested samples are shown 
in Figure  2f. The magnitude of δ values depicted in Figure  2f 
increases in the following sequence: AR → (750°C; HTR500; 
Δε/2 = 0.8%) → ΗΤ500 → (750°C; HT500; Δ ε/2 = 0.8%).
Figure 3a–i depicts the TEM micrograph of AR alloy (Figure 3a) 
and deformation substructures of AR (Figure  3b,c), HTR500 
(Figure  3d–f), and HT500 (Figure  3g–i) specimens fatigued at 
750°C for Δε/2 of 0.58%, respectively. It is observed in Figure  3a 
that the cuboid- ordered γ′ precipitates (confirmed by the super 
lattice reflections in SADP shown in inset of Figure  3a) are uni-
formly distributed in the γ - matrix phase. The substructure of 
the AR sample tested at Δ ε/2 of 0.8% consists of minimal dislo -
cation activities in the γ - matrix channels and shearing of the γ ′ 
by stacking faults (SFs). In contrast, the HTR500 and HT500 fa -
tigued samples show intense dislocation activities in the matrix 
channels, and the γ′ is sheared by APB- coupled dislocation par-
tials. Additionally, in these samples, dislocation networks are 
observed at the γ /γ′ interfaces. One of the striking differences 
in the dislocation substructures of HTR500 and HT500 samples 
is the nature of the dislocation network. In the HTR500 sample, 
the dislocation network has diamond- shaped morphology, while 
in the HT500 sample, a hexagonal network is formed.
The misfit stresses, combined with the applied cyclic loading, led 
to extensive dislocation movement. One of the primary driving 
forces behind such movements is the relaxation of strain fields 
induced by lattice misfit. Moreover, different types of disloca -
tions, that is, edge and screw, are associated with varying strain 
fields and are therefore expected to move differently under the

An a/2[−110] edge dislocation, oriented along the [11–2] direc -
tion, glides on the (111) plane of the γ matrix toward the γ /γ′ 
interface to alleviate the misfit strain (Figure 4a). Further move-
ment of these dislocations is hindered, allowing them to move 
only through climb mechanisms. After climbing from the (100) 
γ/γ′ interface, they may resume gliding on a different (111) plane 
(Figure 4a) [14].
Unlike edge dislocations, screw dislocations do not relax the 
normal strain field induced by lattice misfit, as they generate 
only shear stresses. However, the movement of screw disloca -
tions can produce 60° mixed dislocations, which help relieve 
the misfit strains. Figure  4b shows that a screw dislocation 
transforms into a U- shape, containing two parallel 60° mixed 
dislocations [14]. These mixed dislocations at the γ /γ′ interface 
facilitate the relaxation of misfit strain. The movement of both 
edge and screw dislocations leaves 60° dislocations at the γ/γ′ 
interface, promoting the network formation.
4 | Discussion
Past studies on DS Ni- based superalloys have corroborated that 
the LCF properties are dictated by the nature of dislocation sub -
structures, which are strongly affected by the γ′ size, morphol -
ogy, and distribution within the γ matrix [ 10, 15]. Furthermore, 
most of these studies were focused on isothermal exposures. The 
role of thermal cycling, specifically in discerning microstruc -
tural changes and their influence on dislocation substructures 
and LCF performance, has not been fully explored. This study 
addresses that gap by examining the effects of prior thermal cy -
cling under two distinct sample conditions, that is, HT500 and 
HTR500, to establish a clear relationship between substructural 
evolution and the deterioration of fatigue properties in the alloy.

0.687 ± 0.011 μm in the HT500 samples, without any change in

The increase in precipitate size and chemical partitioning of 
constituent elements between the γ and γ′ phases induced lattice 
misfit in the alloy and is a key factor contributing to the deteri -
oration of fatigue life (Figure  1a) in thermally cycled samples, 
with a more pronounced decrease observed in HT500 samples 
compared to HTR500 samples. The higher negative γ /γ′ misfit 
led to the formation of different dislocation network configu -
rations (Figure  3), generating deformation heterogeneity and 
consequently reducing fatigue life. Moreover, the increased 
negative misfit strain altered the network configuration from 
a diamond- pyramid shape to a hexagonal one (Figure  3e,f,h,i), 
reducing the resistance to dislocation shear in the γ′ precipitates 
and thereby weakening interfacial strengthening. This behav -
ior is evidenced by the increased number of antiphase boundary 
(APB)–coupled dislocation partials observed in HT500- fatigued 
samples (Figure 3g).

the fatigue deformation behavior of the alloy, it is important to 
comprehend the mechanisms behind their formation. These dis-

The a/2<110> dislocations glide in the matrix and form 60° 
mixed dislocations at the γ /γ′ interfaces, leading to the forma -
tion of the <110> diamond- shaped networks. These mixed dis -
locations further transform into a /2<100> edge dislocations via 
climb/cross- slip processes at the γ /γ′ interfaces, leading to the 
formation of hexagonal dislocation networks [14].
The driving force for such glides stems from the interaction of 
misfit strain and the dislocations, facilitating the relaxation of 
the misfit stresses.
The aforementioned analysis revealed that the increase in mis -
fit strain led to the formation of different dislocation configu -
rations, which promote strain localization and thereby degrade 
the LCF properties of the alloy.
5 | Conclusions
The following conclusions have been drawn from this study:
 I. An increase in misfit strain leads to dislocation movement 
toward the γ/γ′ interfaces via sweep gliding and cross- slip 
gliding, resulting in different dislocation network configu -
rations that degrade the fatigue performance of the alloy.
 II. The decrease in fatigue life and the divergence in associ -
ated fatigue behavior are attributed to change in disloca -
tion configurations.
