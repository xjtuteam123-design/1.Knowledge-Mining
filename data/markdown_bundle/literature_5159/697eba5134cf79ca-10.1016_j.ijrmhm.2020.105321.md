ABSTRACT
Microstructure and mechanical properties of ﬁve Nb alloys containing 8 to 17 at.% Mo, 8 or 35 at.% Zr, up to
7 at.% Ti, up to 2 at.% Al and up to 2 at.% Cr are reported. These alloys have been developed to replace heavy,
expensive and di ﬃcult to process commercial Nb alloys, such as C-3009, for use at temperatures up to 1600 °C.
The density of the alloys is in the range from 7.6 to 8.6 g/cm 3. The alloys have a BCC matrix phase, and they also
contain small amounts of secondary phases, which are rich in Zr and have BCC, FCC, hexagonal or monoclinic
crystal structures depending on the concentration of other alloying elements, including oxygen and nitrogen. In
the temperature range from 25 °C to 1600 °C, the alloys with a smaller amount of Zr are ductile and have higher
speciﬁc strength than C-3009. The alloy containing 35 at.% Zr is stronger, but less ductile, than other alloys at
temperatures up to 600 °C; however, it loses the strength rapidly at higher temperatures and becomes softer than
other alloys at T > 1000 °C. The possible strengthening mechanisms responsible for the observed temperature
dependence of the yield stress of the alloys are also discussed.
1. Introduction
Future airspace applications require advanced metallic materials for
use at temperatures beyond 1000 °C. Refractory metals and alloys, with
their melting points exceeding 1800 °C, good high-temperature strength
and creep resistance, high thermal conductivity and low coe ﬃcient of
thermal expansion are considered for such applications [ 1–3]. Un-
fortunately, many of the currently available refractory alloys, especially
W and Mo based alloys, su ﬀer brittle-to-ductile transition above room
temperature, have inferior oxidation resistance above ~800 °C and
have high density. Among the currently available refractory alloys, only
Nb alloys are ductile at room temperature and may have density below
10 g/cm
3. For example, C-103 (Nb-5.4Hf-2.0Ti-0.7Zr-0.3Ta-0.3W, by
at.%), which is one of the most commonly used Nb alloys, has the
density of 8.86 g/cm
3 and its excellent cold formability enables making
complex structures by closed die forging using tool steels [ 4]. Un-
fortunately, C-103 has relatively low strength (< 150 MPa) at
T ≥ 1000 °C. Another commercial Nb alloy, C-3009 (Nb-20Hf-6W), has
much improved high temperature strength (e.g. yield strength of
397 MPa and 388 MPa at 1000 °C and 1200 °C [ 5]), but it has higher
density (10.3 g/cm
3), more di ﬃcult to process, and much more ex-
pensive than C-103.
Several Nb alloys containing Mo and Ti have recently been reported,
which do not contain heavy and expensive Hf [ 5]. The density of the
reported alloys is in the range from 7.70 to 8.03 g/cm 3, the alloys have
shown good ductility at room temperature and their strengths at tem-
peratures from 20 °C to 1200° are superior to C-103. Unfortunately, at
T ≥ 600 °C, the Nb-Mo-Ti alloys were not as strong as C-3009. In this
work, microstructure and mechanical properties of new Nb alloys
containing Mo, Zr, Ti, Al and/or Cr are reported, some of which have a
combination of good room temperature ductility and high temperature
strength, superior to both C-103 and C-3009.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure of the studied alloys
According to X-ray di ﬀraction, the three-component Nb-Mo-Zr al-
loys, NMZ1 and NMZ2, and a commercial C-3009 are single-phase BCC
structures with the lattice parameters a = 330.5 pm, 329.5 pm, and
334.2 pm, respectively ( Fig. 1a, Fig. 1b, Fig. 1f). Small X-ray di ﬀraction
peaks from minor secondary phases are present in NMZ3 ( Fig. 1c),
NMZ4 ( Fig. 1d) and NMZ5 ( Fig. 1e). In NMZ3 these peaks can be
identiﬁed as an unknown hexagonal phase with c/a = 1.413, or an FCC
phase with a = 587.5 pm; in NMZ4 as a metastable hexagonal AlZr 3
–rich phase (c/a = 0.859) [ 6] and in NMZ5 as a hexagonal Zr-rich
phase (c/a = 1.613). The phases and their lattice parameters identi ﬁed
by the X-ray di ﬀraction in the studied alloys are summarized in Table 2.
The microstructure of the studied alloys is shown in Fig. 2 and
Fig. 3. Fig. 2 is a set of low magni ﬁcation images, which illustrates the
grain structure and grain size, while Fig. 3 is a collection of higher
magniﬁcation images, which illustrates retaining dendritic structure
and/or secondary phases present at grain boundaries and inside grains.
The average grain size (diameter) in NMZ1 is ~220 μm. Increasing the
concentration of Mo from 12.7% in NMZ1 to 17.1% in NMZ2 or adding
5% Ti in NMZ3 decreases the grain size to 160 μm or 137 μm, re-
spectively. The NMZ4 alloy, which contains Mo and Zr in the amounts
similar to NMZ1 but also additionally contains small amounts of Al and
Cr, has the most coarsened grain structure, with the average grain size
of ~300 μm. The alloy NMZ5, which has high concentration of Zr, has
the ﬁnest grain size of ~30 μm( Table 2). No subgrains or micro-seg-
regation of the alloying elements are observed in NMZ1 and NMZ4
alloys, and grains in these two alloys have even contrasts ( Fig. 2 a, d).
On the other hand, grains in NMZ2, NMZ3 and NMZ5 alloys are divided
in ﬁner subgrains with second-phase precipitates located at subgrain
boundaries ( Fig. 2 b, c, e).
Higher magni ﬁcation images reveal secondary phases in all ﬁve
alloys ( Fig. 3). One type of the secondary phases is present in the form
of needle-like precipitates inside grains (alloys NMZ1, NMZ2, NMZ3
and NMZ4, Fig. 3 a–d) or near subgrain boundaries (NMZ5, Fig. 3 e–f).
The second type is present in the form of gray-color (as seen on BSE
images), irregular-shaped or plate-like precipitates, located mainly at
GBs, in alloys NMZ2, NMZ3 and NMZ5 ( Fig. 3b, c, f). The third type is
present in the form of dark-color, irregular shaped precipitates at GBs
(NMZ1-NMZ3, NMZ5, Fig. 3 a–c, e) and inside grains (NMZ2, NMZ3
and NMZ5, Fig. 3 b, c, f). These dark precipitates are often bordering
with gray precipitates ( Fig. 3b, c, f). The irregular-shaped precipitates
are absent in NMZ4.
Combined EDS and EBSD analyses ( Fig. 4, Fig. 5 ) showed that the
matrix of the studied alloys is a BCC phase with the composition similar
to
the composition of the respective alloy. The needle-like precipitates
are rich in Zr ( Fig. 4 , Fig. 5) and have BCC (NMZ1-NMZ3), hexagonal
(NMZ3-NMZ5) or FCC (NMZ5) crystal structures. The gray-color, plate-
like and irregular-shaped precipitates (they are present in NMZ2, NMZ3
and NMZ5) are rich in Zr, Ti and N or Zr and O, they have an FCC
crystal structure (Fig. 4a, Fig. 5a) and are identi ﬁed as (Ti,Zr)N or ZrO.
Finally, the dark-color, irregular-shaped precipitates in NMZ2, NMZ3
and NMZ5 are found to be ZrO 2 with a monoclinic (P2 1/c) crystal
structure (Fig. 4). The volume fractions of secondary phase precipitates
are estimated from SEM images to be 1.0 ± 0.5%, 2.1 ± 0.5%,
2.8 ± 0.5%, < 1% and 7.3 ± 0.6% for NMZ1, NMZ2, NMZ3, NMZ4
and NMZ5, respectively.

3.5.1. Estimation of strengthening mechanisms operating at room
temperature
The room temperature yield stress of an alloy can be described as
the sum of solid-solution strengthening Δσss, grain-boundary (GB)

3.5.2. Temperature dependence of the yield stress
All the studied alloys, except NMZ5, show a rapid yield stress de-
crease with increasing temperature from 25 °C to 600 °C and a weak
temperature dependence in the temperature range from 600 °C to
1200 °C. Such behavior is typical to refractory alloys, which plastic
deformation in this temperature range occurs by thermally activated
glide of screw dislocations [ 20,24,27,30,31]. NMZ5 shows similar
temperature dependence of the yield stress in the range from 25 °C to
600 °C. However, at temperatures between 600 °C and 800 °C this alloy
experiences very rapid yield stress drop, by ~520 MPa, which is likely
due to dissolution of the second-phase precipitates and losing pre-
cipitation strengthening contribution. A rapid decrease in the yield
stress of all the studied alloys above ~1200 °C is likely due to the ac-
tivation of di ﬀusion-controlling mechanisms [ 32,33].
To analyze the temperature dependence of the yield stress in the Nb-
Mo-Zr-based BCC solid solution alloys, we use recently developed Rao-
Suzuki solid solution strengthening models based on screw dislocation
glide controlled by migration and collision of kinks [ 14,31,33–35].
According to these strengthening mechanisms, kinks are always present
on screw dislocations in highly concentrated BCC alloys due to inter-
actions with solute atoms. In the low-temperature regime (LT,

Analysis of the experimental data in the framework of strengthening
mechanisms indicates that solid solution strengthening of the alloys is
controlled by screw dislocation glide through the Peierls barriers
modiﬁed by solute atoms. The solute-controlled dislocation glide may
include such thermally activated processes as kinking over character-
istic length scale, kink migration, cross-kink unpinning and, at high
temperatures, di ﬀusion-induced jog dragging. Good agreement of the
proposed solution strengthening mechanisms with the temperature
dependence of the yield stress of the studied alloys was found.
