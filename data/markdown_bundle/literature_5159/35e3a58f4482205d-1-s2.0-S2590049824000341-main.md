ABSTRACT 
The development of Refractory High Entropy Alloys (RHEAs) for Additive Manufacturing has been advancing 
rapidly in recent years, buoyed by the triumph of RHEAs generated using traditional techniques in various 
applications. RHEAs, a recent category of materials possessing extraordinary high-temperature attributes, have 
only lately emerged. However, owing to their intricate microstructure, the production of RHEAs employing 
conventional methods such as casting and forging poses a challenge. On the other hand, Additive Manufacturing 
(AM) has exhibited significant potential for creating RHEAs due to its ability to generate intricate microstructures 
and complex geometries with minimal material wastage. The fundamentals of AM and RHEAs are introduced at 
the outset, in conjunction with the unique attributes of 3D-printed RHEA products, such as their enhanced 
strength and unique microstructure. The techniques for producing RHEA powders, including atomization and 
mechanical alloying, as well as the characteristics of the powder, are then elucidated. In this review article, we 
will provide a comprehensive overview of RHEAs produced using Additive Manufacturing (AM). We will 
examine the most innovative AM methods presently available for producing RHEAs, their microstructure, at -
tributes, and potential applications. 
1. Introduction 
The emergence of RHEAs is intricately connected to two elements, 
precisely, the refractory metals and the high entropy alloys (HEAs). The 
concepts of "high-entropy alloy" and "multi-principal alloy," which 
upended the design of conventional alloys and reinvented the process of 
designing alloys, were initially put forth in 2004 by Yeh and Cantor et al. 
[ 1 , 2 ]. The majority of HEAs are made up of five or more different types 
of elements. Additionally, the quaternary alloy is sometimes denoted as 
HEAs, wherein the elements are skillfully merged with atomic ratios that 
are either identical or strikingly similar [ 3 , 4 ]. Refractory metal alloys 
possess a broad spectrum of prospective applications due to their 
elevated density, exceptional thermal conductivity and 
high-temperature capabilities, remarkable robustness and toughness, 
and minimal sputtering yield and hydrogen interactions. Illustrative 
examples encompass plasma-facing components designed for fusion 
reactors, fusion targets, armor penetrators, and nuclear space power and 
propulsion. The utilization of structural alloys, specifically the RHEAs, 
renowned for their elevated melting points, is highly advantageous in 
demanding situations. The term ’ refractory metals ’ has been defined in 
various ways, but an exceptional melting point is a common require -
ment for their inclusion. Some definitions specify a melting point above

Additive Manufacturing (AM), in contrast to conventional produc -

chanical alloying, offers unparalleled freedom in geometric design and 
accelerated prototyping capabilities across numerous applications 
[ 10 – 13 ]. In contrast to standard metal complexes, high entropy can help 
promote a unique phase structure and significant lattice deformation 
(strain). These substances consist of several metallic elements in pro -
portions that are either equal or very close to equal, and they possess a 
structure of a disordered solid solution with an increased configura -
tional entropy [ 14 – 16 ].

remarkable qualities, such as aerospace and defense, energy production

2. Development processes of RHEA powders for AM 
Currently, RHEA powders are created by mechanical alloying [ 34 ], 
gas/water atomization, and Plasma rotating electrode techniques. At -
omization and Plasma rotating electrodes can make spherical powder 
particles. In contrast, mechanical alloying creates irregular-shaped 
powder particles that must be treated to reach high levels of sphericity 
for optimal printability. The process parameters in AM are also influ -
enced by powders ’ thermal parameters (specific heat, thermal conduc -
tivity, latent heat, and expansion coefficient) and optical parameters 
(absorption, reflection, and transmission). Therefore, creating printable 
RHEA powders requires a thorough understanding of such features. 
2.1. Mechanical alloying 
In a high-energy ball mill, particles are repeatedly cold welded and 
fractured as part of the mechanical alloying (MA) process. This method 
is frequently used to make RHEA powders and allows for the creation of 
uniform powder combinations. The procedure typically entails mixing 
the appropriate components in the correct ratios and milling the mixture 
for a predetermined time to produce a uniform powder. 
According to Fig. 2 , elemental powders split and fuse during the 
milling process to create RHEA powder particles as a result of the 
spinning of the grinding bowl (see Fig. 1 ) [ 35,36 ]. Extreme hydrostatic 
compression causes elemental powder particles to be trapped between 
colliding grinding balls and undergo significant plastic deformation 
before shattering. Table 1 and Table S1 summarize the different RHEAs 
with different processing parameters for making mechanical powders. 
The milling procedure involves repeated cold welding and fracture, 
which results in ongoing particle refining. Powder particles with a 
steady-state size distribution and evenly distributed components are 
produced when the repetitions of welding and fracturing are balanced 
( Fig. 2 ). The mechanical alloying process offers several key advantages, 
including an improved ability to dissolve solids, process materials at 
room temperature, and a uniform distribution of elements. A critical

Through the repeated process of cold welding and crushing, the me -

sizeable free volume creates numerous diffusion channels for solid-state 
alloying. The milling duration is a critical alloying parameter that affects 
the phases, crystal sizes, and lattice stresses of RHEA powder particles.

creating pre-alloyed powders for DED and PBF. It uses high pressure

is said that printable RHEA powders have been created using water and 
gas atomization. The most widely used technique for producing fine

make contact with an atomizer, fragmenting the stream into droplets.

3.2. Laser cladding 
Laser cladding (LC) can create a variety of surface alloys and com -

The determination of quality and performance in DED and PBF 
products relies upon the attributes of the powder utilized. Inadequate 
powder quality can instigate deficiencies in printed items, including but 
not limited to porosity, cracks, inclusions, and suboptimal surface 
roughness. It has been suggested that RHEA powder particles possess 
high mixing entropies exceeding 1.5R. RHEA powders are manufactured 
using mechanical alloying, gas atomization, and water atomization 
techniques. Spherical powder particles can be generated through gas 
and water atomization, whereas mechanical alloying engenders powder 
particles of irregular shape that necessitate treatment to attain elevated 
levels of sphericity for optimal printability. To enhance the printability 
of these powders, a diverse range of post-processing techniques and 
treatments can be utilized. Several prevailing methods include sieving, 
screening, sintering, surface coating, post-annealing, powder blending, 
and chemical treatment. 
3.3. Powder bed fusion (PBF) 
One of the most popular additive manufacturing (AM) processes for 
creating RHEAs is powder bed fusion (PBF). Powder bed fusion (PBF) 
methods primarily come in two types: selective laser melting (SLM) and 
electron beam melting (EBM). In the SLM method, a laser beam uses a 
3D computer model to melt and fuse metal powdered particles layer by 
layer selectively. The powder bed is heated and melted by the laser ra -
diation, which hardens when the energy source advances to the 
following layer. PBF is a well-known additive manufacturing method for 
metals, alloys, and occasionally ceramics. In this method, the powder is 
put in layers that range in thickness from 20 to 150 μ m, and then the 
targeted area is selectively melted using a laser beam (either a contin -
uous or pulsed wave). Build layer thickness, laser power, scan speed, and 
hatch spacing are crucial factors affecting how well materials are built. 
These variables affect the processing conditions ’ volumetric energy 
density. Another essential factor that affects how much laser energy is 
linked to the powder layer is laser absorptivity. Notably, PBF is 
frequently carried out in an argon environment with oxygen concen -
trations between tens and hundreds of parts per million (ppm).

3.6. Directed energy deposition (DED) 
DED is another popular AM technique utilized for fabricating RHEAs. 
This method involves the utilization of a high-energy heat source, such 
as a laser or electron beam, to selectively melt a feedstock material, 
which is usually in the form of a wire or powder. The final product is 
constructed by adding layer upon layer of the melted material in a 
controlled manner [ 82 , 93 ]. Metals undergo a process of melting sub -
sequent to deposition in the form of powders or wires, utilizing a laser, 
electron beam, or electric arc. Several terms, such as laser metal depo -

employed to delineate the DED process based on melted powders.

powder flow, as illustrated in Fig. 7 . The powder can fuse in a melt pool 
that flows in tandem with the laser when the laser and powder flow ’ s 
focal points are aligned for deposition. DED is often carried out in an 
argon-filled inert atmosphere where the oxygen content can be lowered

both RHEA powders and various immaculate elemental powders that are 
supplied simultaneously through numerous hoppers. Through the opti -

elemental powders onto a substrate. RHEA melt pools, the foundation

the solidification. The qualities of the printed goods are greatly influ -
enced by the process parameters in DED, such as laser power, scanning

6. Potential application of RHEAs 
A valuable technology for producing RHEA items, AM has recently 
made strides in powder development, printing methods, and printed 
product performance, as detailed in the preceding sections. Due to their 
precise microstructures, printed RHEA items exhibit exceptional quali -
ties as compared to products made using traditional production methods 
because of the rapid solidification in DED and PBF. The use of 3D 
printing for HEAs enables the creation of lightweight, customizable, and 
non-assembly-required goods with a variety of material, design, and 
manufacturing freedoms. RHEAs, which may be created via additive 
manufacturing (AM), have the potential to be used in a variety of ap -
plications. In this section, we will talk about some of the potential uses 
for RHEAs made with AM. 
The aerospace industry has a lot of promise for AM RHEA goods since 
there is a growing need for lightweight materials. On the basis of ele -
ments with lower densities, such as Al, Mg, Be, and Ti, lightweight 
RHEAs are still being developed. The majority of the RHEAs are 
immiscible with other transition metals, including Cr, Fe, and Cu, which 
frequently combine to create intermetallic compounds, making it chal -
lenging to manufacture lightweight RHEAs with a single solid solution

their high strength, corrosion resistance, and high-temperature stability.

storage, and biomedicine. Beyond the highlighted applications, AM of

7. Conclusion and future prospects of AM with RHEAs 
A brand-new class of materials called RHEAs is being created for its 
high-temperature capabilities, corrosion resistance, and mechanical 
strength. Explosive progress has been attained in AM for RHEAs, which 
has been motivated by the promising future of HEAs and the distinctive 
advantages of 3D printing techniques. In this article, the latest ad -
vancements in AM for RHEAS have been meticulously analyzed and 
evaluated in terms of their powder creation, printing processes, phases, 
crystal properties, mechanical attributes, functionalities, and potential 
applications. The production of RHEA powders typically involves so -
phisticated techniques such as atomization and mechanical alloying. 
The capability of RHEA powders to be printed majorly depends upon the 
morphologies, particle size distributions, and element distributions of 
the powders created. The DED and PBF (SLM and EBM) methods are 
predominantly employed for printing RHEA products from pre -
developed powders as they offer the advantage of quick solidification 
and the ability to generate intricate geometries. Furthermore, DED can 
print RHEA products by using unsullied elemental powders from various 
powder-feeding channels, thus avoiding the time-consuming develop -
ment of RHEA powders, which is not feasible in PBF.

for printing applications.

and enable the fabrication of single parts in small numbers are just a few 
benefits that additive manufacturing (AM) techniques provide for the 
creation of RHEAs. To enhance the quality of printed RHEA products, 
printing procedures can be developed and improved. In order to reduce 
cracks and deformation, printing techniques like binder jetting and 
material extrusion can be used to prevent the thermal stresses present in

8. Research gaps and challenges for realizing the full potential 
of RHEAs 
Even though RHEAs can be produced using additive manufacturing 
(AM), there are still a number of issues that need to be resolved. Some of 
the unmet needs for AM of RHEAs include: 
• Limited options for materials: The variety of RHEAs that can be 
manufactured with AM is still constrained when compared to con -
ventional production processes. This is due to the fact that not all 
RHEAs can be processed using the present AM procedures, necessi -
tating the creation of novel materials and processing methods. 
Generally speaking, the target alloys ’ range of applications can be 
increased by adding new alloying elements to existing alloys, which 
can produce new alloy types with distinct microstructures and at -
tributes. Mainly composed of Cr, Nb, Mo, Ti, Al, V, Zr, W, and Ta 
components are the currently produced RHEAs. Elements Ni or Sc 
have also been added to RHEAs in a number of reports. In light of the 
general characteristic, low-density metallic elements such as Li, Mg, 
Y, and light rare-earth (i.e., Cerium group) elements can be included. 
Furthermore, as previously mentioned by Huang et al. [ 177 ], the 
inclusion of nonmetallic elements like B, C, N, or Si in RHEAs may 
enhance their mechanical properties. 
• Process optimization : Rapid heat cycling in laser technology pro -
vides RHEAs with distinct microscopic characteristics like ultra-fine 
grains, regular element distribution, and distinctive precipitated 
phases, which enhances RHEAs ’ mechanical strength. The variation 
in the powder element content affects these microscopic features, 
which manifest as variations in the size and form of the grains and 
the composition of the precipitation phase. These variations may also 
have an impact on the properties of RHEAs. However, RHEAs man -
ufactured by SLM and LMD frequently have defects such as unmelted 
particles, pores, and cracks. In fact, SLM-made RHEAs can even show 
micro flaws like localized exfoliation at grain boundaries. On the 
other hand, because LC RHEAs with low coating thickness will 
generate less heat effect, fewer flaws will develop in LC-fabricated 
RHEAs. Defects have an impact on RHEA strength and may poten -
tially be the cause of failure in conditions that are corrosive or 
vibrate. The goal of several investigations has been to reduce the 
number of defects that are generated in laser-fabricated RHEAs by 
increasing energy input or adding specific powder constituents. They 
serve as a guide for enhancing the forming quality of RHEAs pro -
duced using laser technologies. 
• Microstructural control : RHEAs with dual- or multi-phase struc -
tures have a fatal drawback in that they are relatively weak, 
particularly in the case of those with brittle precipitates or bcc_B2 
matrix. Knowing the mechanisms underlying RHEAs ’ room- 
temperature toughening would be beneficial. Furthermore, the 
precipitated Laves phase is widely thought to improve the overall 
mechanical characteristics of RHEAs; however, specific processes for 
strengthening and toughening at elevated temperatures are still un -
known. Therefore, it is necessary to learn more about the mecha -
nisms of toughening and strengthening in lightweight RHEAs by 
combining atomistic simulations (such as molecular dynamic simu -
lations) with multi-scale characterization techniques. This will allow 
for the acquisition of comprehensive mechanical properties. 
• Surface quality : For parts composed of RHEAs created using AM, 
surface quality is essential. Rough surfaces and residual stresses are 
examples of inherent characteristics that might alter the mechanical 
qualities and surface smoothness of RHEAs manufactured by additive 
manufacturing. Post-processing methods such as machining and 
polishing are used to improve the quality of the surface. These 
techniques do have certain disadvantages, though, such they are
