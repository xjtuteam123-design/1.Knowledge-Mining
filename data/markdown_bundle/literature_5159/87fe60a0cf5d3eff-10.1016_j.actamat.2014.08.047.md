Abstract—A new approach to the design of Ni-base polycrystalline superalloys is proposed. In this approach, we assume that the creep–rupture char-
acteristics of a superalloy are mostly determined by the strength of interatomic bonding at grain boundaries (GBs) and in the bulk of c matrix. The
ideal work of separation, W sep , of a GB is used as a fundamental thermodynamic quantity that controls the mechanical strength of an interface,
whereas the partial cohesive energy, v, of an alloy component serves to characterize its contribution into the strength of the bulk. Using the R5
(2 1 0)[1 0 0] symmetric tilt GB as a representative high-angle GB in Ni, we calculate W sep ; v, and GB segregation energies, Eseg , for the complete
set of 4d and 5d transition metal impurities, to which we add B (a typical microalloying addition), S and Bi (notoriously known as harmful impurities
in Ni-base superalloys). The purpose of the analysis is to identify the elements that demonstrate a high tendency to segregate to GBs, have positive
(preferably high) partial cohesive energies in the bulk, and have positive impact on W sep of GBs. We refer to these elements as low-alloying additions.
Our study reveals Zr, Hf, Nb, Ta and B as the most promising low-alloying additions. Our next step is to introduce the elements found in the ﬁrst step
into a new powder metallurgy (P/M) Ni-base superalloy. The results of the subsequent testing conﬁrm that the newly created P/M superalloy indeed
demonstrates superior mechanical properties at high temperatures compared to the existing Russian P/M alloy EP741NP.

1. Introduction
Ni-base superalloys (NBS) are key materials used for the
manufacturing of aerospace engines [1]. Some critical parts
of gas turbines, e.g. turbine disks, are produced from poly-
crystalline NBS by means of powder metallurgy (P/M),
which is one of the most eﬃcient technologies for preparing
“net–shape” turbine disks with ﬁne polycrystalline struc-
ture and reduced macrosegregation [2].
In terms of creep resistance, grain boundaries (GBs) are
the “weakest” elements of a polycrystalline alloy. Indeed,
GBs are normally characterized by enhanced diﬀusivity
[3], which favors creep under loads at high temperature
(particularly in ﬁne-grained NBS). It is well known that
one of the most important factors aﬀecting the properties
of GBs is the chemical composition. The chemical compo-
sition of GBs diﬀers from the average chemical composition
of an alloy due to segregation of certain alloying elements
and impurities to GBs. Several surfactant elements, referred
to as “grain boundary elements ” [2], can enrich GBs. GB
elements, in turn, can be divided into “useful” microalloy-
ing additions and “harmful” impurities. It is considered
that “useful” microalloying additions, such as boron, segre-
gate to GBs and increase the work of GB separation [2].
“Harmful” impurities also segregate to GBs, but reduce
the alloy’s cohesive strength.
Thus, to assess the impact of the chemical elements on
the state of GBs in alloys, one should, ﬁrst, investigate their
ability to segregate to a GB and, second, determine their
eﬀect on the cohesive strength of GBs. The second is a more
diﬃcult task. Indeed, the experimental determination of the
cohesive strength of GBs is a complex problem that has
rarely been the subject of an experimental study. A theoret-
ical solution to this problem has been proposed by Rice,
Thomson and Wang [4,5]. The Rice–Thomson–Wang
approach is centered around the ideal work of separation
(of a GB), W

Ab initio calculations based on the density functional
theory (DFT) have been the most useful tool for investiga-
tion of GB segregation and embrittlement, especially dur-
ing the last decade (see Ref. [6] for a recent review). Ni
GBs are no exception. Perhaps the most theoretically stud-
ied element in this context is sulfur [7–12]. S is predicted to
readily segregate to Ni GBs and cause the embrittlement of
the latter, in perfect agreement with experiment [13,14].
Systematic studies of metal and non-metal sp impurities
in Ni [9,12] suggest that most of them weaken Ni GBs, with
the exception of Be, B and Si, which have a strengthening
inﬂuence, whereas C, Al and P have little or no eﬀect. Cal-
culations of transition metals are scarcer. Sanyal et al. [11]
predict that Hf has a strengthening eﬀect, and according to
Young et al. [8], Cr and Nb slightly strengthen GB, whereas
Zr shows a small embrittling potency. The latter, however,
disagrees with the prediction of a phenomenological model
proposed by Geng et al. [15].
In this paper we undertake a systematic investigation of
the eﬀect of transition metal impurities on a representative
GB in Ni. Thus, our study is complementary to those of sp
impurities in Ni by Yamaguchi et al. [9] and by Vsˇianska´
and Sˇ ob [12] as it considers the complete set of 4 d
(Y–Ag) and 5 d metals (La–Au), to which we add Cr, B, S
and Bi. We further show how these data can be used to
select the most eﬃcient alloying elements in terms of GB
resistance to decohesion ( “low-alloying additions ” con-
cept). Finally, the proposed “low-alloying additions ” con-
cept is tested in an experimental study of a new P/M NBS.
2. Theory
We would like to ascertain a few physical quantities that
are unambiguously deﬁned and can be employed to charac-
terize the individual contributions of each alloy component
to the overall strength of the polycrystalline material. It
would be also beneﬁcial if these quantities are straightfor-
ward and reasonably quick to calculate ab initio.
Recently it was suggested [16] that the partial molar
cohesive energy of impurity atoms could be used to
characterize the cohesion in the bulk. Indeed, an excellent
correlation between impurity cohesive energies and high-
temperature properties of single-crystal NBS was found
(see Section 2.2). Encouraged by this ﬁnding, in the present
study we shall also use the partial contribution of alloying
elements to the ideal work of GB separation as a suitable
quantity to characterize cohesion at the GB of polycrystals.
To these, we add a third quantity, namely the energy of
impurity segregation to GB, as it is important that the
impurity in question is present at GBs in thermodynamic
equilibrium (see Section 2.1).
2.1. Segregation energies and the work of GB separation
The ideal work of separation, W sep , is a fundamental
thermodynamic quantity that controls the mechanical
strength of an interface [4,5]. In essence, the work of sepa-
ration characterizes the resistance of a GB to decohesion
and therefore is relevant to our analysis.
In the interface thermodynamics, there are two similar
quantities responsible for the interface decohesion: the
work of adhesion, W ad , and the work of separation, W sep
[17]. The former is deﬁned as the reversible work required
to cleave a GB into two free surfaces (FSs) and assumes
that full thermodynamic equilibrium is maintained during
the decohesion:

3. Computational details
The spin-polarized calculations in this work are per-
formed using the projector augmented wave method
[23,24] as implemented in the Vienna ab initio Simulation
Package (VASP) [25]. Exchange–correlation eﬀects are
treated within the PBE ansatz [26] of the generalized gradi-
ent approximation (GGA). The convergence criteria for
total energy and forces are chosen as 10 /C0 5 eV and
9 /C2 10/C0 3 eV A˚ /C0 1, respectively. Ionic relaxation is included
in all types of simulations.
The R5 (2 1 0)[1 0 0] tilted symmetric GB is selected as a
representative high-angle GB in NBS as its energy falls in
the midrange of special GBs in pure Ni [4,5]. The equilib-
rium structure of this boundary shown in Fig. 1 coincides
with the structure used in other studies [10,11]. The GB is
modeled as a supercell containing 19 atomic layers of Ni
(4 atoms per layer, 76 atoms per cell) and represents a single
cell doubled along the [1 0 0] direction. The slabs are sepa-
rated with 8 A˚ of vacuum suﬃcient to suppress interactions
between periodic images [11]. In our 0.25 ML impurity
excess simulations, a single impurity atom either replaces
one of four equivalent host atoms in the GB plane
(substitutional segregation) or is inserted between Ni atoms
(interstitial segregation). Both substitutional and interstitial
sites are indicated in Fig. 1 with white spheres. For calcula-
tions of impurity atoms at the (2 1 0) surface we employ the
same atomic conﬁguration except the GB is removed and
we use only substitutional sites (see Fig. 1 ). The 4 /C2 4 /C2 2
Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh [27] is used in all slab
calculations.
The impurity energies in Ni bulk are obtained in a
4 /C2 4 /C2 4 fcc supercell (64 atomic sites) using the
6 /C2 6 /C2 6 k-point mesh. The native crystal structures of
impurities, required for cohesive energy calculations (see
Section 2.3.3), are selected according to Refs. [22,28],
namely hcp, bcc or fcc for transition metals, A16 ( a-S struc-
ture) for sulfur, A7 (As structure) for bismuth, and boron is
calculated in its a-rhombohedral crystal structure. A plane
wave energy cutoﬀ of 270 eV is used throughout. Increasing
the cutoﬀ to 320 eV for boron impurity (boron is the most
sensitive to the cutoﬀ element in our study) changes the seg-
regation energies by less than 3 meV per atom and the GB
strengthening energy by less than 2 meV per atom.
4. Calculation results and discussion
Below we shall present results for a series of impurities
in fcc Ni: in the bulk, at the free (2 1 0) surface and at the
R5 (2 1 0) [1 0 0] symmetric tilt GB. Our aim is to ﬁnd ele-
ments, the segregation of which to the GB would be most
beneﬁcial for the alloy strength. In order to make our
search more systematic, our impurity “database” includes
all transition metals from the fourth and ﬁfth row of the
periodic table since many of these elements are used in
NBS as alloying additions. To the complete set of 4 d
(Y–Ag) and 5 d metals (La–Au), we also add S and Bi
(impurities known to have a deteriorating eﬀect on Ni
GBs), as well as Cr and B (another two elements very pop-
ular in the NBS context).

4.3. Low-alloying additions
We return now to the main question of the study,
namely which elements appear most promising as alloying
additions to P/M NBS and therefore could be classiﬁed
as low-alloying additions? As discussed in Section 2, the
impurity in question will be selected using the following
three criteria: (i) large negative g; (ii) large positive v; and
(iii) large negative E
gb
seg .
A speciﬁc feature of P/M alloys is the fact that they have
smaller grains and hence larger GB area. Therefore among
the above three criteria, condition (ii) appears less impor-
tant in P/M NBS than in cast NBS. In addition, examina-
tion of Fig. 2 reveals a decent correlation between GB
strengthening energies, g, and (minus) partial cohesive ener-
gies in Ni bulk, ð/C0 vÞ, shown in Fig. 2 c and d, respectively.
In particular, most impurities that strengthen GB (i.e. have
g < 0) also strengthen Ni matrix (i.e. v > 0Þ. Cr and Pt,
which appear exceptions from this rule, have both v and
g rather small in magnitude, and therefore are unlikely to
be selected as promising additions anyway.
Thus, we concentrate on conditions (i) and (iii). In Fig. 3
we replot data in Fig. 2 a and c as a map Egb
seg vs. g. Ideally,
we would like to have elements with both Egb
seg and g being
large and negative, i.e. elements residing in the bottom left
corner of the map. However, we observe that (apart from
Os and, to certain extent, Tc, to which we return shortly)
elements on the map tend to arrange themselves in a “diag-
onal” pattern: elements which strengthen GBs most (large
negative g) have a moderate segregation energy E
gb
seg, and
elements which are most likely to be found at GBs
(large negative Egb
seg ) exhibit moderate GB strengthening
propensity.
Thus, to progress one needs to decide on the relative
importance of criteria (i) and (iii): is one of them a priority
or both are equally desirable, or, perhaps, some linear com-
bination of them must be used? The choice must be dictated
by particular details of the alloy in question, such as the
alloy manufacturing process and expected working condi-
tions. In our case we assume that alloys have had suﬃcient
time to attain the equilibrium segregation, if not during
their preparation then during their exploitation at high
temperatures. Given that segregation in multicomponent
metallic alloys is predominantly substitutional, the avail-
able segregation sites should therefore be occupied in accor-
dance with the impurity’s Egb
seg : elements with large negative
g might be displaced from GB sites by elements having
more negative Egb
seg . Hence, we select criterion (iii) as a pri-
ority over criterion (i).
In Fig. 3 (or, equivalently, in Fig. 2 a) one can see that
the elements with the most negative Egb
seg are Bi, S, B, Zr,
Nb, Tc, Ag, Hf, Ta, Os and Au. Out of these, Bi, S, Ag
and Au have positive g and must be avoided. Tc and Os
look rather promising since their Egb
seg and g are large and
negative. However, these elements are in practice not used
as alloying additions in NBS due to the health hazards they
pose (Tc is radioactive; Os forms the volatile and highly
poisonous oxide OsO
4). We are left, therefore, with the
set Zr, Hf, Nb, Ta and B, which are the low-alloying addi-
tions arising from our study.
5. Experiment
5.1. Chemistry of interfaces in Ni-base alloys
Experimental data on the segregation of transition met-
als to NBS interfaces (GBs and c=c0 phase boundaries) are
scarce. Nevertheless, we would like to highlight a few exam-
ples relevant to our study.
Blavette et al. [34] used electron microscopy and atom
probe techniques to investigate GB microchemistry in the
Ni-base superalloy Astroloy (alloying system Ni–Co–Cr–
Mo–Al–Ti–Zr–B–C). Atom probe tomography revealed
the presence of B, Mo and Cr at GBs, which agrees well
with our theoretical predictions. Complex phases contain-
ing Zr, C and S were also observed at GBs; no segregation
of pure Zr was detected. Note the authors’ suggestion that
GB enrichment by B, Cr and Mo should be controlled by
equilibrium segregation; the same assumption is made in
the present study.
The N18 Ni-base superalloy (alloying system Ni–Co–
Cr–Mo–Al–Ti–Hf–Zr–B–C) investigated in Ref. [35] was
fabricated by P/M. Transmission electron microscopy and
atom probe tomography study were combined in order to
characterize the structural type (high angle) and chemical
composition of a GB under investigation. It was also
observed that B, Mo and Cr enrich the GB, whereas no seg-
regation of Zr was found.

More results on the inﬂuence of small additions of the
“grain boundary elements ”, e.g. B, C, Zr, on the perfor-
mance of Ni-base superalloys can be found in literature
(see, for instance, Ref. [2] for review). However, the role
played by small additions remains controversial.
To summarize, the GB segregation behavior of alloying
elements in NBS is not yet fully established. Existing exper-
imental data agree on some elements, but disagree on oth-
ers. Even less is known regarding the eﬀect of these alloying
elements on the performance of P/M NBS. Therefore, in
the manufacture of a new P/M alloy described below, the
selection of the alloying system was based mainly on our
theoretical results in Section 2. Namely, a small amount
of the low-alloying additions identiﬁed in Section 4.3 (Zr,
Hf, Nb, Ta and B) was introduced into the new test alloy,
NGK-6.
5.2. Composition design
The theoretical results of Sections 2–4 were used for the
design of a new NBS fabricated by P/M [40–42]. A remark-
able feature of P/M NBS in the compacted state is their rel-
atively “ﬁne-grained” microstructure as compared to cast
alloys [43]. This is why GBs in these alloys play a particu-
larly important role in such processes as fatigue and creep
at elevated temperature. GB cohesive strength beneﬁts most
from alloying elements having large negative segregation
energy E
gb
seg and GB strengthening energy g. We refer to this
selection method (selection according to the values of the
segregation energy and of the GB strengthening energy)
as the concept of low-alloying additions.
The concept of low-alloying additions was used along
with the “traditional” criteria for NBS design to determine
the chemical composition of a new P/M NBS [41].I n
accordance with the aforementioned theoretical results,
we designed the new alloy, NGK-6, by adding a set of
low-alloying additions of transition metals (Zr, Hf, Nb,
Ta) and B into a P/M alloy, the composition of which
meets the following requirements:
(i) The c0-phase solvus temperature (T solvus
c0 ) has to be
nearly the same as in the cast alloy GS6U [44]
(type MAR M200).
(ii) The amount of the c0-forming alloying elements
in the new P/M alloy, in particular the sum
Al + Ti + Nb, has to provide approximately 60
vol.% of the c
0-phase, V ðc0Þ, similar to the cast
alloy GS6U.
(iii) The diﬀerence between the lattice parameters
of c-matrix and c0-phase (value of misﬁt
d ¼ ac /C0 ac0 ) has to be controlled in a special
way. As shown in earlier work of one of the
authors [45–47], a small positive value of param-
eter d for c/c0 interfaces in NBS is a beneﬁcial fac-
tor for (acceptable) creep–rupture behavior.
(iv) The new alloy has to have a low propensity to
form topologically close-packed phases (esti-
mated via parameter M
d in the New PHACOMP
method as described in Ref. [43]).
The GBs of the new NGK-6 alloy were strengthened by
adding a “package” of alloying elements (Zr, Hf, Nb, Ta
and B) chosen in accordance with our theoretical predic-
tions described in Section 4. Note that these elements have
already been used in diﬀerent combinations in a number of
NBS, proving their eﬃciency as alloying additions [2].
The test sample of NGK-6 alloy containing the package
of selected GB-strengthening alloying additions and meet-
ing the above requirements, has, in accordance with the
patent [42], the following chemical composition: Ni–(8–
10) W–(4–5) Cr–(11–13) Co–(3–5) Mo–(2–2.5) Ti–(4.5–5)
Al–[(1.5–2) Nb–(1–2) Ta–0.5Hf–0.01Zr–0.015B]–(0.01Ce,
0.01La, 0.01Y) (wt.%). The exact amount of each of the
low-alloying additions (shown in square brackets) was
determined experimentally. For instance, the amount of
Zr is limited to 0.01 wt.% due to the high risk of the forma-
tion of a Zr-rich phase at a GB. We emphasize again that
alloy NGK-6 is intended for P/M manufacturing.
5.3. Alloy preparation
NGK-6 test alloy electrodes have been melted in a high-
vacuum induction furnace (LEYBOLD-HERAEUS). Pre-
pared electrodes were sprayed in the UCR-4 installation
(PREP technology) at 14,000 rounds min
/C0 1 in Ar and He
atmosphere. Obtained granules were magnetically sepa-
rated in the KRP-2 installation in Ar atmosphere. Electro-
static separation was carried out in a protective Ar and He
atmosphere.
The granules were then compacted using hot isostatic
pressing (HIP) in Ar atmosphere at 1180–1250 /C176C, at
160 MPa for 3 h. After HIP, the samples of the new alloy
were cut out of the compacted powder, quenched (in air)
from the c
0 solvus temperature, and subjected to a two-step
ageing.

6. Conclusions
We investigate the inﬂuence of 4 d and 5 d transition
metal alloying elements as well as of B, Bi, S and Cr on
the GB strength in Ni within the framework of the Rice–
Thomson–Wang approach. The results of the study are
combined with the low-alloying addition concept also pro-
posed in this paper. The concept aims at improving the
mechanical properties of a P/M NBS by adjusting its chem-
ical composition.
Based on the theoretical work reported herein, a set of
low-alloying additions (Zr, Hf, Nb, Ta and B) is identiﬁed
and subsequently used to design a new test P/M alloy,
NGK-6. The microstructure of the new alloy reveals an
enrichment of the GB areas with chemical elements having
