ABSTRACT 
The exceptional mechanical properties of face-centered cubic (FCC) concentrated solid solution (CSS) alloys 
generally correlate various deformation mechanisms, such as deformation induced nano twins and/or hexagonal 
close packed (HCP) phases, which is normally determined by stacking fault energy (SFE). To systemically change 
the SFE but keep the influence of atomic size and modulus mismatches minimum, a series of NiCo-based alloys 
are designed by adjusting the Ni/Co ratio. All alloys are fabricated to be single phase FCC with grain size 
carefully controlled to be ~60 μ m. Deformation substructures after tensile tests over temperature ranging from 
77 K to 1073 K are carried out to reveal the effect of SFE on mechanical properties and deformation mechanism. 
SFE has little effect on yield strength (YS) at all test temperature, but do affect the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) 
and elongation to fracture (EF) of the alloys. At temperature lower than 873 K, it seems that the lower the SFE, 
the higher the UTS. At 1073 K, there is no obvious trend for UTS and EF, indicating that SFE has little effect on 
deformation behavior of alloys at high temperature. Formation of deformation nano twin/HCP depends on not 
only the SFE of the alloys but also the temperature. As testing temperature and/or SFE of the alloys decreases, 
deformation induced nano twin/HCP are favorable and become dominant deformation modes for the FCC CSS 
alloys, resulting in a combination of high strength and good plasticity due to the great work hardening capacity. 
1. Introduction 
Face-centered cubic (FCC) solid solution (SS) alloys, where foreign 
solute atoms are completely dissolved and randomly distributed in the 
solvent lattice, are the most important metallic materials for structural 
application, especially those with a high concentration of solute ele -
ments [ 1 – 3 ]. For example, austenitic stainless steels [ 4 – 8 ], Ni/Co-based 
superalloys [ 3 , 9 , 10 ], and newly developed high/medium entropy alloys 
(H/MEAs) [ 11 – 17 ] are essentially all FCC concentrated solid solution 
(CSS) alloys. FCC CSS alloys normally possess outstanding thermody -
namic stability [ 18 ], excellent machinability [ 19 ], and easy processing 
steps no matter the abundance of alloy types. Moreover, another most 
promising advantage of those alloys is their extraordinarily excellent 
mechanical properties from cryogenic to elevated temperatures [ 20 ], 
making them great choices for extreme service conditions. For example,

The exceptional mechanical properties generally result from various 
deformation modes [ 21 ], which can enhance the strength and plasticity 
of the alloy. In FCC CSS alloys, deformation mechanisms are closely 
related to stacking fault energy (SFE) [ 22 ]. For alloys with high SFE, the 
deformation generally originates from dislocation movement [ 23 ], 
resulting in low strain hardening capacity. For the alloys with low SFE 
(such as Hastelloy X and CoCrFeMnNi alloy), nano twin will form during 
deformation [ 9 , 24 , 25 ]. The interaction between dislocation and nano 
twin improves the strain hardening capacity and the necking instability 
is postponed to higher strains, which is called twin-induced plasticity

further reduced, phase transformations, such as FCC to ε -martensite 
(hexagonal close packed, HCP), may occur during the deformation in

so-called transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) [ 27 – 30 ]. The inter -

alloy strengthening and toughening. 
However, current researches on the effect of SFE on the deformation 
mechanisms are based on different alloy systems and the compositions 
of materials are rather complicated, thus various alloying elements 
would introduce atomic size and modulus mismatches, which also affect 
mechanical properties and deformation behaviors [ 31 ]. Furthermore, 
mechanical properties and deformation behaviors are also closely 
related to microstructures, such as the grain size. Therefore, it is 
essentially necessary to carefully design a series of model alloys with 
systematically changing the SFE and meanwhile controlling their mi -
crostructures for the purpose to reveal the intrinsic effect of SFE on the 
mechanical properties and deformation mechanism of FCC CSS alloys. 
The atomic radii of Ni and Co are 0.1243 nm and 0.1250 nm, while 
their shear moduli are 76 GPa and 82 GPa, respectively [ 3 ]. The dif -
ference in atomic radii and moduli of Ni and Co is almost negligible. 
Moreover, increasing the concentration of Co can effectively alter the 
SFE of Ni – Co binary FCC SS alloys from positive to negative [ 22 , 32 , 33 ]. 
Therefore, a series of NiCo-based alloys are designed and fabricated. By 
adjusting the Ni/Co ratio, the SFE can be systematically changed but 
keep the influence of the atomic size and modulus mismatches mini -
mum. Microstructures with the same grain size are obtained by thermal 
mechanical processing in order to exclude the effect of grain refinement 
strengthening. The aim of the current work is to systematically study the 
SFE effects on intrinsic mechanical properties and deformation behav -
iors of FCC CSS alloys over a wide temperature range, with the focus on 
the SFE effect on the formation of dislocation, nano twin and HCP phase, 
which might be beneficial to design or optimize new or next generation 
FCC CSS alloys. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Material preparation 
Four NiCo-based alloys with different Ni/Co atomic ratios, whose 
nominal compositions are shown in Supplementary Table S1 , were 
named NC1, NC2, NC3 and NC4, respectively. The model alloys were 
prepared in a water-cooled copper hearth under an argon atmosphere by 
the method of arc melting Ni, Co, Fe, Mo, Cr (purity > 99.95 wt%). The 
alloy ingots were turned over and remelted at least five times to ensure 
uniform mixing of the elements. The melts were cast into a water-cooled 
copper mold with a size of 12.5 × 12.5 × 120 mm
3
, and cuboid-shaped 
ingots were obtained after cooling. The weight loss of the alloy produced 
by casting was within 0.2% to ensure that the actual composition of the 
alloy was close to the nominal composition, which was confirmed by 
energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) quantification ( Supplementary 
Table S1 ). The as-cast ingots were then homogenized at 1473 K for 9 h 
followed by water cooling. The homogenized cuboid ingots were cold 
rolled in the longitudinal direction until the total thickness was reduced 
by ~80%. Then recrystallization was carried out under different con -
ditions (NC1: 1453 K/1 h, NC2: 1398 K/1 h, NC3: 1493 K/1 h, NC4: 
1493 K/1 h) to ensure alloys having the same grain sizes.

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure, crystal/elastic properties, and SFE of the four alloys 
A series of FCC CSS model alloys with systematically changing the 
SFE are fabricated to reveal the intrinsic effect of SFE on the mechanical 
properties and deformation mechanisms of FCC CSS alloys. To minimize 
the effect of grain size on mechanical properties, same grain sizes are 
required for all four CSS alloys, which can be achieved by carefully 
adjusting their recrystallization temperature and time. Fig. 1 is the mi -
crostructures and XRD patterns of the four model alloys with system -
atically changed Ni/Co ratios. Clearly each alloy exhibits a uniform 
equiaxed grain structure with grain size of ~60 μ m ( Fig. 1 a – d). EDS 
analysis are also used to examine elemental distribution in the alloys. 
One example is shown in the inset of Fig. 1 b, which is elemental maps of 
NC2 alloy captured from the area indicated by dash rectangle in Fig. 1 b, 
indicating that all elements Ni, Co, Fe, Cr and Mo are uniformly 
distributed inside the alloy in SEM-EDS resolution. XRD patterns 
( Fig. 1 e) demonstrate that all the four alloys are single-phase FCC 
structure. Lattice parameters are calculated to be 0.360 nm for all four 
alloys, indicating that changing Ni/Co ratio almost does not change the 
atomic spacing of the FCC lattice. Ultrasonic method is used to measure 
the sound velocity inside the alloys. In combination with the specific 
weight, the shear modulus of the alloys can be determined and the re -
sults are listed in Supplementary Table S2 . All four alloys have same 
shear moduli of 89 – 90 GPa, indicating that tuning Ni/Co ratio almost 
does not change the modulus mismatch of the alloys. 
To experimentally confirm the idea that SFE of FCC CSS alloys can be 
systematically changed by tuning the Ni/Co ratio in model alloys, SFEs 
are calculated (Eqs. (2) – (4 )) by measuring the width (d) between two 
partial dislocations and the angle ( θ ) between the Burgers vector of the 
full dislocation and the dislocation line. Fig. 2 a displays a TEM-WBDF 
image of extended dislocations in NC1 alloy showing the extended 
width of a full dislocation (d) and the corresponding angle ( θ ). Values of 
d and θ are measured and summarized in Fig. 2 b. Thus, according to Eqs. 
(2) – (4 ), SFEs of the four alloys are estimated to be 32 ± 4 mJ m

3.2. Tensile properties 
Fig. 3 presents the engineering stress strain curves at four test tem -
peratures from 77 K to 1073 K. It is obvious that tensile properties and 
deformation behaviors depend on both composition of the alloy and 
temperature, because stress strain curves of the four FCC CSS alloys at 
the same temperature and each alloy at different temperatures are 
different. However, there are still some similarities for the curves of the 
alloys at the same temperature. At temperatures ranging from 77 K to 
873 K, all alloys exhibit continuous work hardening. When ultimate 
tensile strength (UTS) has been reached, the alloys quickly fracture after 
a slight decrease of engineering stress, indicating negligible necking. At 
1073 K, when strain is less than 20%, curves of the four alloys are 
similar. Stresses for NC1 and NC2 alloys reach maximum when strain is 
about 20%, while that for NC3 and NC4 alloys reach maximum when 
strain is about 30%. After maximum stresses are reached, stresses of the 
four alloys continuously decrease until fracture, which is often observed 
in other FCC CSS alloys, such as Hastelloy X [ 9 ]. 
YS, UTS and EF of four alloys as a function of temperature are plotted 
in Fig. 4 . Obviously, YS and UTS of the four alloys decrease with 
increasing temperature, as shown in Fig. 4 a & b. The EF of NC1, NC2 and 
NC3 alloys also decreases with increasing temperature. But for NC4, 
which has the lowest SFE, the EF deceases at 77 K, although it still 
reaches ~77% with compared to the EF at RT (~107%). At each tem -
perature, YS of the four alloys are almost the same ( Fig. 4 a), while UTS 
and EF are slightly different for the four alloys, indicating that SFE has 
limited effect on the YS but affects deformation behaviors of alloys. At 
77, 293 and 873 K, it seems that the lower the SFE, the higher the UTS 
( Fig. 4 b). For example, NC4 alloy with the lowest SFE achieves the 
highest UTS among the four alloys. In addition, EF also increases with 
decreasing SFE at 77, 293 and 873 K for all alloys, except that NC4 alloy 
exhibits the relatively low EF at 77 K with compared to that at RT. At

3.3.1. At 77 K 
As shown in BF-STEM images ( Fig. 5 a), in all alloys fractured at 77 K, 
there are large amounts of straight strips, which might be related to 
dislocation slip band or deformation twin or phase transformation. 
Further characterization demonstrates that most of these strips are 
deformation twins in the alloys with relatively high SFE. For example, 
the SAED pattern ( Fig. 5 b) and dark field TEM image ( Fig. 5 c) of NC1 
alloy, which are captured from the same grain of NC1 alloy shown in 
Fig. 5 a, clearly reveal the existence of deformation twins in tensile 
fractured specimens. High resolution HAADF-STEM image ( Fig. 5 d) 
further determines that thickness of these twins is only several nano -
meters. However, for the NC3 alloy with relatively low SFE, a small 
amount of HCP phase with thickness only about 3 nm can also be found 
together with nano twins ( Fig. 5 e), although there are only diffraction 
spots of matrix and deformation twins in the SAED pattern, which might 
be due to extremely small amount of HCP phase. In contrast to the other 
three alloys, in the NC4 alloy with the lowest SFE, SAED pattern ( Fig. 5 f) 
and dark field images ( Fig. 5 g) demonstrate that there are high density 
of both deformation nano twins and HCP phase in the tensile fractured 
specimens. Moreover, at early deformation stage of NC4 alloy (e.g., at 
plastic strain of only 10%), besides dislocations ( Fig. 5 h), a few defor -
mation nano twin/HCP are already to be observed ( Fig. 5 i). 
SEM image can capture information in relatively large area with 
comparison to TEM. BSE technique with electron channeling contrast 
has been proved to be suitable for directly distinguishing dislocations 
from deformation twin/HCP [ 38 – 43 ]. Therefore, samples with different 
plastic strains have been investigated by using SEM-BSE mode to reveal 
the evolution of deformation substructures, specifically the stress/strain 
conditions of the initial occurrence of nano twin/HCP in all four alloys 
and the results are shown in Fig. 6 . Large amounts of lamellae with sharp 
borders are observed in four alloys for all samples after tensile strain 
above 50%. EBSD including IPF and phase distribution map ( Supple -
mentary Figs. S1a and S1b ) are used to confirm that those lamellae 
observed in BSE images ( Fig. 6 ) are indeed corresponding to nano 
twin/HCP. Moreover, for different alloys, lamellae are observed at 
different plastic strain levels, which is ~10%, 18%, 25% and 50% for 
NC4, NC3, NC2 and NC1 alloys, respectively. 
However, BSE technique is not able to distinguish nano twin from 
HCP phase, and density of nano twin/HCP at low strain levels is too low 
to be distinguished by SEM due to the limitation of resolution. There -
fore, TEM observations of alloys with different plastic strains in Fig. 6 
are further used to determine the deformation substructures. If defor -
mation nano twin or HCP phase is observed, the corresponding BSE 
image in Fig. 6 is marked by red or blue dot. It seems that initiation of 
nano twin/HCP is significantly influenced by SFE. Deformation twin -

3.3.2. At 293 K 
Different from that at 77 K, for the NC1 alloy with relatively high 
SFE, only dislocations ( Fig. 7 a) are observed during deformation. Fig. 7 a 
shows dislocations in the fractured NC1 alloy. SAED pattern (inset in 
Fig. 7 ) further demonstrates that there is no deformation twin/HCP. For 
the other three alloys, deformation nano twins are observed after frac -
ture. For example, Fig. 7 b & c shows dark field images and corre -
sponding SAED patterns of deformation twins in NC3 and NC4 alloys, 
respectively. However, high resolution HAADF-STEM characterization 
( Fig. 7 d) reveals that, beside deformation twins, there is also nano HCP 
phase accompanying with nano twin in the NC4 alloy. In combination of 
TEM characterization, SEM-BSE images ( Fig. 8 ) demonstrate that 
deformation twinning initiates in NC2, NC3 and NC4 alloys at 50%, 45% 
and 35% strain, respectively. Therefore, the lower the SFE, the lower 
plastic strain is required for deformation twinning. In addition, nano 
HCP phase is only observed in NC4 alloy when strain is higher than 50%. 
3.3.3. At 873 & 1073 K 
When temperature increases to 873 K, nano HCP phase has not been 
found in all alloys ( Fig. 9 ), but nano twins can still be found in alloys 
with relatively low SFE at strains higher than 70% (NC3 alloy) and 50% 
(NC4 alloy). Dark field images and corresponding SAED patterns clearly 
show the deformation twins in fractured NC3 and NC4 alloys ( Supple -
mentary Fig. S2 ). With temperature continuously increasing to 1073 K, 
lamellae have not been observed in all alloys after fracture ( Fig. 10 ), 
which indicates that both deformation twinning and phase trans -
formation do not occur at 1073 K in all four alloys.

5. Summary and conclusions 
To investigate the effect of SFE on the formation of nano twin/HCP 
during deformation in FCC CSS alloys, a series of NiCo-based alloys with 
different Ni/Co ratios are designed to systematically change SFEs of the 
alloys. Tensile tests are carried out at temperatures ranging from 77 K to 
1073 K, and deformation substructures are investigated from atomic to 
micrometer scales to explore the effect of SFE on the mechanical prop -
erties and deformation behaviors of FCC CSS alloys over a wide range of 
temperatures. The following conclusions can be drawn: 
1. By changing the Ni/Co ratios, four single phase FCC CSS alloys are 
obtained. The SFEs of NC1, NC2, NC3 and NC4 alloys are 32 ± 4, 20 
± 3, 13 ± 2 and 8 ± 2 mJ m
 2
, respectively. Other parameters that 
would influence mechanical properties, such as grain size, crystal 
structure, atomic size and modulus mismatches, are the same. 
2. SFE has little effect on YS at all test temperatures, but do affect the 
UTS and EF of the alloys. At 77, 293 and 873 K, it seems that the 
lower the SFE, the higher the UTS at each temperature. In addition, 
EF also increases with decreasing SFE for NC1, NC2 and NC3 alloys, 
except for NC4 at 77 K. Although NC4 alloy has the lowest SFE, the 
EF slightly deceases at 77 K (~77%) with compared to the EF at RT 
(~107%). 
3. At 1073 K, there is no obvious trend for UTS and EF of the four alloys, 
indicating that SFE has little effect on deformation behavior of alloys 
at high temperature. 
4. Formation of deformation nano twin/HCP depends on not only the 
SFE of the alloy but also the temperature. As testing temperature 
and/or SFE of the alloys decreases, deformation twinning and phase 
transformation are favorable and dominant deformation modes for 
the FCC CSS alloys, results in a combination of high strength and 
good plasticity due to the great work hardening capacity. 
5. The temperature effect on the formation of nano twin/HCP appears 
not due to relatively low critical stress to activate deformation 
twinning/phase transformation at low temperature. As temperature 
decreases, the strength of alloy increases, and the critical stress 
required for activating deformation twinning/phase transformation 
can be relatively easy achieved.
