Beyond Ni-Based SuperalloysResearch Summary
 In this study, the microstructure of 
precipitation-strengthened platinum-
based superalloys after homogenization 
heat treatment and various cooling 
regimes was investigated. The study 
focused on the lattice misﬁ t between 
the γ matrix and γ′ precipitate phase as 
well as the precipitate morphologies. 
Water quenching led to a γ single-phase 
structure, whereas air cooling resulted 
in the precipitation of 200 nm sized γ′ 
cubes with 30% volume fraction. Further 
investigations found that chromium 
in Pt-Al-Cr-Ni alloys had a strong γ′ 
promoting effect.
INTRODUCTION
 Nickel-based superalloys are applied 
in gas turbines as blade and disc materi-
als. To increase the thermodynamic 
efficiency of these turbines, higher 
operating temperatures are needed. 
However, despite their continuing 
advancements, this seems to be dif-
ficult to achieve with nickel-based 
superalloys, which already operate up 
to temperatures of about 1,100°C at the 
blade surface.
1 In this context, platinum 
alloys are of increasing interest for 
very high temperature applications.
2 
They are envisaged to have potential for 
thermally high loaded, but non-rotating 
parts and as construction elements 
with superior oxidation and corrosion 
resistance in chemical industry.
 The goal is to copy the successful 
precipitation-strengthening mechanism 
of nickel-based superalloys to a new 
group of high-temperature alloys based 
on platinum. Research groups in South 
Africa,
3–10 Germany,3,4,11–13 and Japan2,13 
are investigating superalloys based on 
the Pt-Al system. These alloys, having 
a face-centered cubic (fcc) matrix 
with strengthening L1
2-ordered and 
coherently embedded Pt3Al precipitates,

MICROSCOPY RESULTS
 The secondary electron micrographs 
in Figure 2 show the γ′ phase as dark-
gray particles surrounded by brighter 
matrix. The bright fringes are a result 
of the so-called edge effect, due to the 
preferential etching of the γ′ phase 
in comparison to the platinum-rich 
solid solution by the aqueous potassium 
cyanide (KCN) electrolyte.
 After the homogenization of 
Pt
78.5Al12.5Cr3Ni6 at 1,500°C for 12 h in 
argon and at different cooling rates, 
two-phase microstructures were found 
in each sample (Figure 2). Small 
precipitates with a volume fraction of 
about 3% were observed in the water-
quenched sample (Figure 2a). After 
cooling in air, evenly distributed 
cuboidal-shaped precipitates of about 
200 nm diameter and a volume fraction 
of about 30% were found (Figure 2b). 
Image analysis of the furnace-cooled 
sample found a precipitate volume 
fraction of about 34% (see Figures 2c 
and d). A bimodal precipitate-size 
distribution with small (≤ 200 nm) and 
large precipitates (about 3,000 nm) was 
observed. The large particles formed by 
coarsening and agglomeration of many 
small ones. Due to the small precipitate 
sizes, the chemical compositions of 
matrix and precipitates after water 
quenching (Figure 2a) and air cooling 
(Figure 2b) could not be determined 
properly by energy dispersive x-ray 
analysis (EDX). After furnace cooling, 
the chemical composition of the larger 
precipitates in Pt
78.5Al12.5Cr3Ni6 was 20.0 
at.% Al, 1.9 Cr, 3.8 Ni, and Pt balance.
 In order to examine the inﬂ uence of

Aluminum contents of about 12 at.%

were measured in both alloys after 
homogenization heat treatment by 
means of EDX.
 Pt
82Al12Ni6 (Figure 3a) shows evenly 
distributed cuboidal-shaped precipitates 
of about 100 nm diameter. The precipi-
tate volume fraction of this alloy was 
determined to be 22%. Increasing the 
chromium content to 6 at.% leads to 
an increase of the precipitate volume 
fraction to 50% and to a maximum 
precipitate size of about 500 nm (Figure 
3b). The precipitates in Pt
76Al12Cr6Ni6 
are apparently more spherical.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES
 Platinum-based alloys with nominal and actual compositions given in Table A were arc 
melted under argon to button ingots of about 3 g. Each sample was turned and re-melted ﬁ ve 
times to promote homogeneity. The chemical compositions of the specimens were measured 
by energy-dispersive x-ray analysis.
 The differences between nominal and actual compositions (Table A) are predominantly 
a result of aluminum evaporation during arc melting. The alloys were named according to 
their nominal composition in atomic percent. Homogenization treatments were performed 
at 1,500°C for 12 h, 1,500°C for 6 h, and 1,510°C for 6 h, respectively, under continuous 
argon gas ﬂ ow. The samples were cooled by water quenching with at least 300 K/s, in air with 
about 2.5 K/s, and by controlled linear furnace cooling with 0.1 K/s.

DISCUSSION
 Investigations in the Pt-Al-Cr-Ni 
system showed that an alloy with 11 
at.% Al, 3 Cr, 7 Ni, and Pt balance 
is single-phase after water quenching 
from 1,450°C,
11 whereas the otherwise 
similar alloy Pt 77Al13Cr3Ni7 with an 
aluminum content of about 13 at.% was 
difﬁ cult to solutionize at 1,500°C.
12 
After water quenching from 1,500°C, 
this alloy disclosed a ﬁ ne distribution 
of γ′ particles with a volume fraction of 
about 30%.
12 The alloy composition 77 
at.% Pt, 13 Al, 3 Cr, 7 Ni may therefore 
lie entirely in the miscibility gap (i.e., 
this alloy can not be heat treated in a 
single-phase ﬁ eld). In order to clarify 
the situation, systematic heat-treatment 
tests on alloys with an intermediate 
aluminum content of 12.5 at.% were 
attempted.
 The alloy Pt
78.5Al12.5Cr3Ni6 can be 
made almost single-phase by water 
quenching from 1,500 °C. Special care 
was taken to ensure that the small sample 
did not cool down during unloading 
from the furnace. Air cooling was 
insufﬁ cient to suppress γ′ precipitation 
in Pt
78.5Al12.5Cr3Ni6. Instead, partially 
aligned and evenly distributed γ′ cubes 
of about 200 nm edge length formed. 
The γ′ distribution resembles that of 
Pt
77Al13Cr3Ni7 a fter water quenching 
from 1,500 °C.12 W enderoth et al. 12 
suppose that the particles precipitate 
during unloading the small sample from 
the furnace before water quenching. In 
order to suppress precipitation, high 
cooling rates at high temperatures 
are required. This seems especially 
important at high solution heat-treatment 
temperatures if the composition of 
the Pt-Al-Cr-Ni alloy is very close 
to the boundary of the γ single-phase 
field because of rapidly increasing 
supersaturation upon cooling (see 
Figure 1). Coarse γ′ particles in alloy 
Pt
78.5 Al12.5 Cr3Ni6 after slow furnace 
cooling from 1,500 °C indicate the 
importance of the cooling rates to 
control particle size distribution and 
morphology.

CONCLUSIONS
 Dendritic as-cast structures of 
Pt
78.5 Al12.5 Cr3Ni6, Pt 82Al12Ni6, and 
Pt76Al12Cr6Ni6 could be homogenized 
by heat treatment at 1,500–1,510 °C. 
Formation of Pt 3Al precipitates was 
almost completely suppressed in 
Pt78.5Al12.5Cr3Ni6 after homogenization 
for 12 h at 1,500 °C and subsequent 
water quenching. Air cooling leads to 
homogeneous distributions of Pt
3Al 
particles with 200 nm average edge 
lengths and a volume fraction of about 
30%. The same alloy shows a bimodal 
particle distribution with a coarse particle 
fraction after furnace cooling from 
1,500°C. The entire γ′ volume fraction 
was 34%. Increasing the chromium 
content to 6 at.% leads to average 
edge lengths of 500 nm and a volume 
fraction of 50% in Pt
76Al12Cr6Ni6 after 
homogenization for 6 h at 1,500 °C 
+ 6 h at 1,510 °C in argon followed 
by air cooling. For the investigated 
platinum-based superalloys, controlled 
air cooling after solution heat treatment 
is sufﬁ cient to provoke a microstructure 
similar to nickel-based superalloys. 
Time-consuming ageing heat treatments 
may be omitted. X-ray diffraction 
measurements show that the Pt
3Al phase 
in Pt78.5Al12.5Cr3Ni6 and Pt76Al12Cr6Ni6 is 
L12-ordered at room temperature.
