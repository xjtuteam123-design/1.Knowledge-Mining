Alumina forming Ni-base superalloys are essential due to their oxidation resistance at elevated tempera- 
tures. A two-step procedure allows to assess the outward growth of the oxide scale from the resulting ox- 
ide ridges that form at 1100 °C and cap the alumina grain boundaries. Employing undoped 50Ni50Al (at%) 
as reference, the impact of reactive elements on the diffusion processes, here Zr and Hf, is quantiﬁed us- 
ing atom probe tomography. Unexpectedly, we ﬁnd that up to one monolayer of Ni may co-decorate the 
alumina grain boundaries. Additionally, a decrease in Al-, and Ni-diffusivity of two orders of magnitude is 
observed owing to the reactive element effect. We employ density functional theory calculations to bet- 
ter understand the role of aliovalent cations, here Ni(II), Zr(IV), and Hf(IV) in the α−alumina scale. The 
calculations show that Ni may not only decorate the alumina grain boundaries but also facilitates trans- 
port of electrons as well as oxygen vacancies. Thereby oxide scale growth becomes enhanced. In turn, the 
dual impact of reactive elements, i.e. to annihilate oxygen vacancies and to remove impurity states in the 
band gap, explains the reduced scale growth rate.

1. Introduction 
Alumina forming high temperature Ni- and Fe-base superal- 
loys play important roles in turbine technology for power genera- 
tion and aero-engine applications. Besides mechanical strength, the 
good oxidation resistance of this class of materials at high temper- 
atures is ensured by the formation of a stable, continuous, slow- 
growing, dense and well adherent alumina scale that results from 
the selective oxidation of aluminum [1–6] . The slow growth of this 
oxide scale is maintained by grain boundary diffusion, the lattice 
self-diffusion at elevated temperatures of both O and Al [7] be- 
ing suppressed in absence of lattice defects. At high temperatures, 
thermally grown alumina is predominantly inward growing with 
only a minor concurrent outward growing component, see e.g. [8–
16] . 
Small changes in the elemental composition of alumina form- 
ing alloys, including minor dopants such as Zr, Hf and Y (so called 
“reactive elements” (REs), dramatically affect scale performance, as 
ﬁrst observed in 1937 by Pfeil [17] . Indeed, at temperatures above 
1100 °C and under oxidizing conditions, the presence of REs in the 
alloy has been shown to control scale growth rate as well as scale-

actually referring to different effects under different conditions has 
recently been reviewed [20,21] . Hence, what controls the impact of 
REs on diffusional ﬂuxes in alumina grain boundaries [13] remains 
a highly relevant topic in high temperature oxidation. 
Here, we shed new light on the impacts of REs on oxide scale 
growth on NiAl alloys by employing a method that registers the 
minor outward growing alumina scale component. While the study 
of effective inward diffusion of oxygen by isotope labeling is quite 
straightforward [8–11] such a method cannot be used in the case 
of aluminum, having only one stable isotope ( 27 Al). The only ra-

order to assess the extent of outward diffusion of elements, the 
outermost rough and small grained fraction of the thermally grown 
oxide scale is removed while retaining the robust α-alumina part, 
after which the samples are re-oxidized. The cross-section area of 
the resulting oxide ridges formed above oxide GB is used to quan- 
tify the outward diffusion of metal ions (see Nychka et al. [23] and 
Yang et al. [24] ) using high angle annular dark ﬁeld scanning trans- 
mission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM).

2. Experimental procedures 
Stoichiometric NiAl alloys, with and without small additions 
of Hf or Zr were investigated. Their compositions, as determined 
by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP- 
AES), are listed in Table 1 . The alloys were oxidized, as introduced 
in [25] , in the following process of three steps sketched in Fig. 1 : 
I. 1st exposure in O 2 at 1100 °C for 100 h resulting in a roughly 1 
to 2 μm thick oxide layer, having an outer region with relatively 
small-grained alumina, with Zr or Hf oxide precipitates as well 
as voids, and an inner layer with larger Al 2 O 3 -oxide grains. 
II. Smoothing of the outer oxide surface, achieved either by me- 
chanical polishing or polishing using a focused ion beam, FIB, 
for easy identiﬁcation of outward diffusion after the subsequent 
oxidation. 
III. 2nd exposure in O 2 at 1100 °C for 10 h. This re-oxidation, while 
mainly causing inward growth of the oxide, also leads to the 
formation of small ridges on top of the grain boundaries, GBs, 
due to the outward diffusion of metal ions. 
For the mechanical smoothing, polishing paper P2400 and dia- 
mond sprays with decreasing grain sizes of 15, 3, 1 and 0.25 μm 
were used. A gradient in oxide thickness was achieved by applying 
more pressure to one side of the sample. Prior to milling, a thin 
(100 nm) ﬁlm of Au or Ag was deposited by magnetron sputtering 
both to protect and to mark the oxide surface. This is required to 
image the original oxide surface (now Ag/oxide interface) in APT, 
because a layer of electron or ion beam deposited Pt contains C 
(and Ga), which not only results in a higher fracture rate but also 
an uneven atom density in the reconstructed 3D atom maps. For 
the FIB preparation a Tescan (Xe-FIB) or a Versa 3D (Ga-FIB) from 
FEI were used to mill circle shaped patterns into the oxide with 
depths between 0.3 to 2 μm. All the three surface smoothing meth- 
ods were applied for investigation of outward ﬂux of Al ions using 
SEM and TEM analyses [25] while only mechanical polishing was 
used for APT quantiﬁcation of the outward ﬂux of other metal ions. 
To identify the position of ridges after the 2nd exposure, for 
preparation of samples containing areas of interest for both elec- 
tron microscopy and APT analyses, scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) in the Versa 3D was used. Following this step, thin lamellae 
containing the ridges were produced using a focused Ga ion beam. 
The TEM/STEM-lamellae were attached to a Cu-grid and thinned 
down to a thickness of less than 100 nm. High-angle annular dark- 
ﬁeld scanning transmission electron microscopy, HAADF-STEM, im- 
ages and EDS maps were obtained in a Titan 80–300 and a Tecnai 
T20 from FEI. 
APT tips containing GBs from ridge areas were also prepared in 
the Versa 3D (see supplementary). These samples were prepared 
by annular milling of small pieces of lamella attached to Cameca 
ﬂat top posts (with Pt) using an ion beam from the top. In the ﬁnal 
step, the tip was thinned down until only a very thin layer of the 
protective Ag or Au-coating was left using ion beam acceleration 
voltage of 5 kV or less. If successful, the ﬁnal tip contained a ridge 
with GB covered by Ag. 
Atom probe analyses were performed with a local electrode 
atom probe (LEAP) 30 0 0 HR with a green laser at a frequency of 
100 kHz with laser pulse energies of 200 pJ at a set temperature of 
30 K and at KIT with a LEAP 40 0 0X HR with UV-laser, laser pulse 
energies of 20 pJ at 30 K. The data was reconstructed and subse- 
quently evaluated with the Cameca IVAS 3.6.14 software. 
3. Modelling considerations 
Modelling alumina with the purpose of extracting properties 
relevant to thermal growth must consider two complementary per- 
spectives, i.e. the scale acting (i) as an increasingly more eﬃcient 
barrier for oxidation and (ii) comprising a medium for transport. 
Indeed, the history of the scaling process may to a signiﬁcant ex- 
tent be explored by inspecting the fossilized scale morphology.

To the extent that an electrochemical understanding is applica- 
ble for describing the scale growth [28] , electron conductivity from 
the anodic alloy/oxide interface to the cathodic gas/oxide interface 
and complementary outward diffusion of Al(III) and inward diffu- 
sion of O 2 − are simultaneously required to maintain the oxidation 
of aluminum, albeit at a decelerating speed owing to the increas- 
ing scale thickness. 
A major reason for the alumina scales being slow growing is 
associated with bulk alumina comprising a large band gap insu- 
lator. The relevant electron transport across the scale utilizes de- 
fects offering impurity states in the band gap [29] . By virtue of the 
stability of the corundum structure, such defects are understood 
to segregate to the alumina GBs during thermal growth. Extended 
temperature dependent GB regions, as deﬁned by the so-called GB 
width, are understood to constitute “high-ways” for both electronic 
and ionic transport [30] . A particularly striking phenomenological 
manifestation of this understanding comprises the GB density and 
width dependent scaling rates on alumina forming alloys, see e.g. 
[31,32] . 
Albeit structure-dependent, generic impact on transport may be 
ascribed to any GB i.e. acting temperature dependent source of 
point defects into the extended GB region irrespective of their ori- 
gins in the GB, being either point-like or extended [33] . 
A ranking among GB structures is indeed inferred. Take the 
boundary between two grains to be deﬁned by the number n of 
their coincidence sites. Then, among the manifold of remaining 
atomic degrees of freedom for each such /Sigma1n GB, the most repre- 
sentative defect structures may be identiﬁed based on some crite- 
ria. Subsequently, the ability of the inherent GB associated defect 
structures of the obtained GBs to act sources of point defects in 
vicinity may be quantiﬁed. 
The purpose of the present study, however, is not to provide 
such a ranking among different high- or low-angle GBs. Rather, 
possible generic impact of REs on the ability of GBs with inherent 
point-like defects to act source of point defects into the extended 
GB region is formulated and implications thereof discussed. 
Because our interest is ﬁrstly of principal nature, here and in 
previous studies [21,34,35] a procedure has been adopted that re- 
duces the structural ambiguity of alumina GBs and minimizes the 
corresponding interface energy. Thus, a chemically intuitive pro- 
cedure is employed as initiated by bringing hydroxide terminated 
semi-inﬁnite oxide lamella together to form hydrogen bonds across 
the interfaces. Subsequently, protons are gradually exchanged for 
cations of choice, thereby maintaining the charge neutrality while 
ensuring low surface energies of the grain boundaries. 
By this procedure, reminiscent of a sintering process, a quasi- 
amorphous fusion zone is achieved that in turn allows any generic 
differential properties to emerge, e.g. properties of aliovalent ions 
that are based on “near-sightedness” rather than unique long-range 
orders implied by speciﬁc GB misorientations, to be subject to de- 
tailed analysis. Thus, the stability of the resulting GB emerges from

3.1. Computational details 
The spin polarized DFT calculations in this study were per- 
formed using the CASTEP code within the Materials studio 6.0 
suite [37] . The generalized gradient approximation functional as 
developed by Perdew, Burke and Ernzerhof (GGA-PBE) [38] was 
employed for all calculations. Non-local Vanderbildt ultra soft 
pseudopotentials [39] have been used to describe core electrons 
together with a 550 eV energy cut-off and the Brillouin zone 
was sampled with 0.04 ˚A −1 k-points separation [40] employing a 
3 ×3 ×2 sampling mesh. The convergence criteria included 10 −3 ˚A 
(displacement) and 10 −5 eV/atom (energy). 
Use of DFT must face the fact that the universal density func- 
tional is not available to us. Rather, the ab initio GGA-PBE density 
functional [38] was employed, building on its robustness, its ex- 
cellent track record as well as its well-known shortcomings. Here, 
rather than absolute numbers, the present study relies on cancel- 
lation of errors. Indeed, it is the energetics for reshaping the alio- 
valent metal oxides upon accommodation in the intergranular in- 
terface, that is computed by DFT. The actual oxidation enthalpy is 
taken from experiment. Also, when estimating the temperature up

To quantify the outward diffusion of other metal ions neither 
SEM EDS nor STEM EDS are accurate enough. Instead, APT was 
used, offering 3D analyses with nearly atomic resolution. How- 
ever, due to a combination of high applied mechanical stress on 
the APT samples, over 10 GPa [41] and the fact that interfaces (es- 
pecially between oxide and metal) are mechanically weak links in 
the structure, the APT specimen often fail prematurely. This limits 
the success rate of APT analyses of oxides (estimated to be below 
30%) which already suffers from a low FIB tip preparation success 
rate (less than 20%). The latter results from SEM resolving neither 
GB, ridge nor oxide-metal interface reliably in the ﬁnal step of the 
preparation. Due to these diﬃculties, from about 70 APT tips pre- 
pared by FIB, four APT runs were successful i.e. the analyzed vol- 
ume contained a suﬃciently large GB region to be statistically re- 
liable. Furthermore, a couple of short runs with premature break 
down of the tip, just after the GB appeared in the analysis in addi- 
tion to several analyses in which a GB was not present have been 
acquired.

4.3. Modelling 
Proof of concept as to (1) the viability of transient Ni(II) decora- 
tion of alumina grain boundaries and (2) impact to enhance oxide 
growth, is provided by means of DFT. 
4.3.1. Energetics 
A 2 ×2 ×1supercell of α-alumina that includes a nominal stack- 
ing fault, previously utilized for studying RE(III) as well as RE(IV) 
decoration [21] , is resorted to, see Fig. 2 . The energetics for Ni(II) 
incorporation in said model GB is summarized in Table 2 . 
In consideration of the fact that Ni in presence of Al in NiAl or 
NiAl(RE) contradicts thermodynamic equilibrium, any Ni(II) decora- 
tion of alumina grain boundaries must emerge from kinetic consid-

5. Discussion 
In this study we emphasize the kinetic aspects of oxidation. 
While the process must be thermodynamically spontaneous, the 
degree of spontaneity must be subordinate to the local oxidation 
conditions. A less spontaneous event may thus take precedence 
over a more thermodynamically favorable one.one. 
In this context, the observed Ni(II) decoration of alumina grain 
boundaries in thermally grown oxide on NiAl(s) alloys offer a par- 
ticularly dramatic contradiction to predictions based on equilib- 
rium data. Evidently kinetic in origin, the observed sub-monolayer 
coating of the alumina grains results from the rapid oxidation of Al 
combined with the high activity of Ni in NiAl. Thus, intermittent 
local Al depletion during oxidation causes Ni to oxidize at lower 
oxygen activities than would be expected from the Ni/NiO equilib- 
rium. 
Here, support for this emerging take is provided by the ob- 
served preference for Ni(II) as well as RE(IV) to decorate alumina

5.1. Experiment and modelling considerations 
The experimental methodology employed here to quantify out- 
ward transport of cations builds on the validity of the assumption 
that the protruding oxide ridges do indeed reﬂect cation diffusion 
from the alloy. 
Mechanical polishing induces defects into the oxide thereby 
altering aluminum activity in the scale. The alternation will un- 
doubtedly entail a redistribution of aluminum via GBs since they 
provide fast diffusion paths. The time to restore aluminum activity 
corresponds to the time to restore the growth stress in the newly 
formed oxide due to countercurrent diffusion of oxygen and alu- 
minum. Tolpygo and Clarke [43] demonstrated that growth stresses 
are restored within minutes of re-oxidation. Therefore the alu- 
minum activity reaches equilibrium very early on in our experi- 
ment, which mandates the conclusion that the mechanical smooth- 
ing procedure has only a minor effect on the obtained results. Sur- 
face smoothing using ion milling by Ga + or Xe + , which was ap- 
plied solely for investigations of aluminum outward diffusion [25] , 
should not have a large inﬂuence on the analysis of Al outward 
diffusion, either. The created ion damage should be in nm range 
e.g. the observed amorphization zone in Si for Ga + accelerated by 
30 kV is 22 nm and 12 nm for Xe + [44] . At high temperatures 
substantial recovery takes place relatively fast (see [45] ). In fact, 
the effect of ion irradiation on ﬂux is smaller than for mechanical 
polishing as can be seen in Fig. 4 . 
Several factors are responsible for the resulting spread of the 
outward ﬂux J , see Fig. 4 , thus rendering the calculated outward 
ﬂuxes order of magnitude estimates. Indeed, errors in the mea- 
surements of the ridge dimensions are less important (account to 
less than 20% for the worst cases) as compared to 
I. ambiguity of thicknesses, and actual vertical grain boundary 
lengths, see Fig. 1 . 
II. occurrence of second phase precipitates or voids in some GBs 
that impact the diffusion ( Fig. 3 and [25] ) 
III. ignoring the crystallography aspects; high and low angle GBs 
are known to affect ion transport differently [46,47] 
IV. relying on 2D-cross sections while the ridge growth is 3- 
dimensional. This may mask strong lateral differences in thick- 
ness that will contribute to the oxide growth. 
5.2. Impacts of Ni, Zr and Hf in grain boundaries on grain boundary 
transport 
The outward ﬂuxes of Ni, Zr and Hf were calculated from their 
quantitative segregation and the ridge height. The results for the 
different samples are displayed in Fig. 4 using a double logarith- 
mic plot of the ﬂux J against the thickness of the oxide, h . As ex- 
pected, the aliovalent doping levels have signiﬁcant impacts on the 
outward diffusion of Al. In undoped samples, the outward diffusion 
is two orders of magnitude larger compared to Zr- and Hf-doped 
samples [25] . 
From modelling considerations, it is noted that for large 
bandgap insulators such as alumina, the electron conductivity re- 
quired to sustain the cathode process –h e r e O 2 reduction at the 
outer oxide surface –i s facilitated by impurity states in the band 
gap ( Fig. 8 ). These impurity states are associated with both oxy- 
gen vacancies and Ni(II) 3d states [21,29,35] , see Fig. 9 . It is fur- 
ther noted that oxygen vacancies greatly facilitate oxygen mobility 
and consequently also the inward growing component of the scal- 
ing process. The Ni(II) decoration of alumina grain boundaries sup- 
ports both types of impurity states owing to the electroneutrality 
requirement, this leading to the observed enhanced oxide growth. 
The decoration of RE(IV) on the other hand expels oxygen vacan- 
cies from the GB and removes the mentioned impurity states in 
the band gap. Because doping NiAl(s) with RE(IV) displays virtu- 
ally exclusive preference of RE(IV) in the alumina grain boundaries 
an overall slow-down of the oxide scale growth rate is observed. 
That also applies to the small outward growing fraction owing to 
outward diffusion of Al. 
It is thus surprising to ﬁnd Ni(II) co-segregation with RE(IV) to 
alumina GBs in case of Hf-doped NiAl, since RE(IV) and Ni(II) are 
expected to be mutually excluding owing to the former expelling 
and the latter attracting oxygen vacancies. Despite the limited data 
available, the experiments indicate this to be valid for Zr(IV), but 
evidently not for Hf(IV). An explanation could be found in their ox- 
ides exhibiting somewhat different stabilities, thus rendering Zr(IV) 
and Hf(IV) different speciation in the alumina GBs, cf. [5] . From 
this a greater tendency for HfO 2 than of ZrO 2 to form precipitates 
in thermally grown alumina may be inferred. Thus, the apparent 
co-existence of Ni and Hf in the same alumina grain boundaries 
can be taken to reﬂect interpenetrating domains of sub-monolayer 
of Ni(II) and nominal sub-nano-granular Hf(IV)O 2 . 
5.3. On comparative cation mobility 
From the GB ﬂux the GB diffusivity for cations can be calculated 
by ﬁtting the data to the expected from Fick’s 1st law, J x = -Dc/h . 
Taking the concentrations of the different constituting elements to 
be equal the average composition of the alloy, it becomes possible 
to roughly estimate the diffusivity despite the spread of the data 
for Al outward diffusion and only a few data points available for 
the other cations. This can, in the case for the Al diffusion be per- 
formed by ﬁtting Fick’s 1st law using the χ2 -method to the slope 
( Fig. 4 ) or using the individual data points, see Table 3 . The qualita- 
tive nature of this analysis is stressed, as these concentrations may 
deviate from the nominal alloy composition, in particular close to 
the alloy/oxide interface due to the oxide growth. 
The mobility of Ni in the GB comes out to be the lowest despite 
its segregation to the GB being comparable to that of Hf and Zr. 
The latter is owing to the much higher Ni content compared to REs 
in the alloy. The addition of RE reduces the diffusivity of Al and 
Ni by two orders of magnitude, with the mobility of Ni generally 
being two to three orders of magnitude smaller than that of the 
other elements. 
Besides attenuating both the inward and the outward growth 
components of the alumina scale, it is tempting to assume the dis- 
tributions of RE and Ni to reﬂect those of passive tracers (PT) for 
the outward diffusion of Al(III) in the alumina GB. The formal ba- 
sis of this hypothesis is to assume a common chemical potential 
drive in the low-gradient approximation, i.e. that for oxidation of 
aluminum by oxygen to form alumina. 
Intuitively, and supported by experiment, the PT hypothesis 
would constitute a better model for Zr(IV) and Hf(IV) than for 
Ni(II). Thus, the lower apparent diffusivities of the two RE elements 
by an order of magnitude as compared to Al(III) could be traced to 
the impacts of the higher oxidation states and ionic radii deviating 
from that of Al(III).

5.4. On grain boundary coverages 
The segregation values for the minor outward diffusing el- 
ements detected by APT were ~13 atoms/nm 2 for Ni, ~1.5 
atoms/nm 2 for Hf and ~2.5 atoms/nm 2 for Zr at most. In compar- 
ison, a monolayer of either element would be 10–20 atoms/nm 2 
depending on the type of grain boundary. The coverages that are 
considered in the DFT modelling are in 8–10 atoms/nm 2 range in 
case of both Ni and RE. While this is the relevant in case of Ni, it is 
four times greater than the coverages reported for RE. Yet, from the 
latter it is inferred that oxygen vacancies are suppressed in vicinity 
of RE(IV) ions. Besides low oxygen vacancy mobility, also absence 
of local electronic impurity states renders electron mobility low, 
both acting to suppress oxide scale growth. 
6. Conclusions 
APT and SEM may be combined for the study the outward ﬂux 
of cations through alumina scales. Employing oxide ridges on the 
outer surface as guides, these techniques can be eﬃciently utilized 
to study transport processes in alumina GBs. In addition, the ridges 
may be utilized for quantitative assessment of the minor outward 
growing fraction of the net scale growth process associated with 
the outward diffusion of cations. This coupled with comparative 
exposures and measurements on RE doped and undoped samples 
allowed the study of how REs, residing in the alloy prior to oxida- 
tion, impact scale growth. 
Interestingly, the coverage of segregated Ni at the alumina GBs 
on NiAl reaches ~15 atoms/nm ² in the absence of any REs, which is 
compatible with what is required to complete an atomic layer. The 
dramatic impact of Zr and Hf doping on the Al and Ni diffusion 
through thermally grown alumina include: 
I. The diffusivity of Ni comes out 2 orders of magnitude lower 
than both Al and REs. 
II. The ﬂuxes of both Al and Ni are heavily dependent on the pres- 
ence of REs reducing the outward diffusion rates of the former 
by 2 to 3 orders of magnitude. 
This dramatic change in transport properties is in line with 1st 
principles modelling, which shows that 75% and 100% Ni decora- 
tions come out slightly spontaneous, while co-decoration by e.g. 
75% Ni and 25% Al already becomes decisively favorable. Thus, the 
plausibility of Ni decoration of alumina grain boundaries of ther- 
mally grown alumina is validated and consistent with the experi- 
mental ﬁndings. Moreover, said Ni(II) decoration is associated with 
presence of oxygen vacancies V O that in turn facilitate O 2 − diffu- 
sion. Moreover, corresponding impurity states in the band gap, of 
V O as well as Ni 3d origins, facilitate the electron transport nec- 
essary to sustain the O 2 reduction process at the oxide/gas inter- 
face [29] . Consequently, by replacing nominal NiO with nominal 
RE(IV)O 2 in the GB model, properties beneﬁcial to both ionic and 
electronic transport are lost in addition to the suppressing effect 
of selected REs themselves, explaining why undoped NiAl exhibits 
signiﬁcantly poorer oxidation resistance. The incorporation of se- 
lected REs into GB being more spontaneous and faster than Ni also 
suppresses any following incorporation of the latter. This is due to 
the strong oxygen aﬃnity of REs [21,29] , disallowing the required 
Vo presence needed for Ni(II) incorporation.
