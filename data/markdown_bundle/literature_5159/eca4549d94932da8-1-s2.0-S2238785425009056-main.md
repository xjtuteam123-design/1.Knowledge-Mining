ABSTRACT
The preparation of bulk nanocrystalline (NC) tungsten alloys with excellent thermal stability and ultrahigh 
strength at elevated temperatures is a challenge. In this study, bulk NC W-3at%Y (W – 3Y) with an average grain 
size of 16 ± 4 nm was successfully prepared via mechanical alloying and high-pressure/high-temperature sin -
tering. The NC W – 3Y alloy not only has ultrahigh Vickers hardness of 22.0 ± 0.3 GPa - which is close to the 
hardness of ceramic tungsten carbide - but also can maintain high hardness of 16.0 ± 0.2 GPa and a small grain 
size of 37 ± 12 nm after annealing at 1300 
◦
C for 50 h. The compressive strength of NC W – 3Y at 900, 1000, and 
1100 
◦
C are as high as 1116, 571, and 345 MPa, respectively, much higher than those of previously reported 
tungsten and tungsten alloys. Doping Y into W increases the GB diffusion activation energy, lowers the GB 
diffusion coefficient and in turn the GB sliding rate, and thus increasing the strength at elevated temperatures. 
Partial alloying Y element segregated at grain boundaries (GBs) reduces the GB energy, leading to an excellent 
thermal stability, as revealed by our experimental observation and theoretical calculation. The utilization of high 
pressure has direct and indirect effects on lowering the grain growth rate of W – 3Y during sintering: directly 
reducing diffusion coefficient, indirectly enhancing the plastic deformation, thus shortening the atomic diffusion 
distance required to fill the gaps between powder particles and leading to a lower sintering temperature.
1. Introduction
As the potential material used as the plasma-facing material in fusion 
reactors, tungsten and tungsten alloys have attracted the interest of 
many researchers [ 1 , 2 ]. Tungsten possesses high melting point 
(3410 
◦
C), low coefficient of thermal expansion (4.5 μ m K
 1 
at 25 
◦
C), 
good thermal conductivity (173 W/m ⋅ K
 1

◦

yield, low hydrogen retention, and excellent high-temperature strength 
[ 3 – 5 ]. Given these excellent properties, tungsten alloys are widely used 
in aerospace, military, and nuclear industries [ 6 – 8 ]. However, tungsten 
and tungsten alloys also have such disadvantages as room-temperature 
embrittlement and low ductile-brittle transition temperature, which 
greatly limit their application [ 9 , 10 ]. Coarse-grained (CG) tungsten and 
tungsten alloys materials always possess low strength and suffer from 
plasma irradiation induced performance degradation. Refining their 
grains to nanoscale could be one of the most promising strategies to 
solve these problems. Previous studies [ 11 – 13 ] have shown that, when 
the grain size of tungsten and tungsten alloys is refined to nanoscale, 
their mechanical properties could be improved unprecedentedly.
At present, the preparation methods of NC materials can be roughly 
classified into two types. One is to directly prepare NC materials by 
introducing or acting external energy on the matrix material, causing 
phase transition or microstructure transformation of the matrix mate -
rial. Common methods include inert gas condensation [ 14 ], magnetron 
sputtering [ 15 ], high-pressure torsion [ 16 ], and electrodeposition [ 17 ]. 
However, these methods have many drawbacks, such as difficulty in 
preparing large-sized samples by electrodeposition [ 17 ] and difficulty in 
obtaining uniform nanocrystals by high-pressure torsion [ 16 ]. The other 
belongs to the preparation of NC materials using the powder metallurgy 
method. The specific process is to first prepare NC powder particles by 
means of ball milling or chemical methods, and then consolidate or 
sinter the NC particles into bulk NC materials. The methods for pre -
paring nanopowder particles include mechanical alloying (MA), 
self-propagating high-temperature synthesis, and wet chemical method.

The sintering techniques include high temperature and high pressure 
sintering [ 11 ], spark plasma sintering (SPS) [ 18 ], pulse current assisted 
sintering [ 19 ], and high frequency induction sintering [ 20 ]. The com -

grain growth, so the bulk materials obtained by SPS [ 18 ], pulse current

2. Experimental
2.1. Material synthesis and preparation
The NC W and W-(2-5 at%) Y powder precursors were prepared 
through high-energy ball milling. The starting materials were W powder 
(99.95 % pure, 1 – 5 μ m, purchased from Aladdin) and Y powder (99.99 
% pure, 425 μ m, purchased from Alfa Aesar). For simplicity, the W-2at% 
Y, W-3at% Y, W-4at% Y and W-5at% Y alloys are named W – 2Y, W – 3Y, 
W – 4Y and W – 5Y, respectively. The raw materials were first sealed in a 
tungsten carbide (WC) vial containing WC balls in an argon atmosphere 
and then mechanically alloyed in a high-energy ball milling system 
(SPEX Sample Prep 8000, USA) at 1420 r/min for 24 h. The diameter of 
the WC balls was 8 mm whereas the ball-to-powder weight ratio was 2:1. 
After ball-milling for 24 h, the powders with a weight of ~10 g were 
pressed into rods with a diameter of 10 mm and a length of 10 mm using 
a tablet press at 10 MPa. To prevent the oxidation, the ball milling and 
pressing were performed in a glove box (Etelux Lab2000, China) with 
argon atmosphere (99.9999 % pure). Then, the pressed rods were 
inserted into a pure h-BN crucible, which was then mounted in a 
graphite furnace and compressed in a large-volume cubic anvil (CS – 1B, 
Guilin, China) at a pressure of 4 GPa. The pressure was kept for 0.5 h at 
1000 
◦
C. The sintering temperature for W was between 1100 and 
1300 
◦
C. Finally, the consolidated specimens were slowly cooled to 
room temperature within a cooling time of 10 min. For comparison, the 
above pressed rods were also sintered using a SPS technique using a 
sintering system (SPS, LABOXTM-110, Sinter Land Inc., Japan) for 10 
min under a pressure of 40 MPa between 1100 and 1300 
◦
C. The SPS- 
sintered specimens were furnace cooled to room temperature.
