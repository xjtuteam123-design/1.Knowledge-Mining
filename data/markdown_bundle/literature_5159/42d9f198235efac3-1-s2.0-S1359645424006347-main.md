ABSTRACT
Mechanically alloyed (MA) oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) IN9052 Al alloy exhibits excellent strength both
at ambient and high temperatures (up to 300
◦
C). The limited ductility at ambient temperatures, stemming
primarily from the inherent microstructure, impede its practical appeal in structural applications. The present
study signifies a maiden effort to additively manufacture MA-ODS IN9052 alloy using solid-state additive friction
stir deposition (AFSD) process which has added advantages of uniform particle dispersion, severe particle
breakdown, and a recrystallized microstructure. This study indicates a remarkable transition from the initial
strain softening behavior in the as received alloy to significant work hardening in the AFSD condition, while
maintaining satisfactory yield strength of ~380 MPa at ambient temperature highlighting a favorable strength-
ductility synergy. Importantly, exceptional thermal stability has been achieved in the AFSD alloy by systemat-
ically optimizing the process parameters to engineer microstructural heterogeneity. The AFSD alloy demon-
strated an impressive high temperature strength, with ~22 % and ~49 % increase in yield strength as compared
to forged alloy at 240
◦
C and 340
◦
C, respectively. Enhancing high temperature strength in aluminum alloys has
been a long standing goal and the current results outperform other commercially available high temperature Al
alloys. Excellent thermal stability in the AFSD processed alloy is attributed to the nano-scale carbon-rich cluster
formation around oxides and carbides in the matrix. These particles restrict grain boundary sliding and are more
effective in dislocation-particle interaction. The findings are supported by detailed TEM and atom probe to-
mography analysis.
1. Introduction
Aluminum (Al) alloys are widely employed in aerospace and auto-
motive industries due to their favorable attributes, including low
weight, remarkable corrosion resistance, and good thermal conductivity
[ 1 ]. High-temperature applications such as engine compressors and heat
exchangers require Al alloys to maintain elevated temperature strength.
The need for enhanced strength of Al alloys at high temperatures has
resulted in a demand for thermally stable microstructure. Oxide
dispersion strengthened (ODS) alloys address this need for materials
with elevated temperature strength (~350 to 550 MPa), as they contain
large numbers of thermally stable oxide particles that pin the disloca-
tions and thereby restrict their motion [ 2 – 5 ].
The IN9052 alloy, an ODS Al alloy, was initially developed to
enhance creep resistance for high-temperature aerospace applications.
Irmann [ 6 ] initially prepared the alloy through ball milling of Al powder
with an 8 % oxygen (O) atmosphere, causing the surface oxide-layer of
the powder to crush, disperse, and consolidate in the Al-matrix and thus
lead to the assimilation of O into the fabricated alloy. Building on this,
Bufferd and Grant [ 7 ] judiciously superimposed solid solution
strengthening mechanisms upon the milled Al powders, subsequently
harnessing ODS Al-Mg compositions. Furthermore, Jangg and Kutner
[ 8 ] incorporated carbon during ball-milling of Al powders, which
resulted in a fine dispersion of A1

dated Al alloy. Hence, the alloy showcased remarkable tensile strength
of 545 MPa at ambient temperature along with limited ductility [ 9 ].

Dispersoid distribution significantly impacts the performance of ODS
alloys, especially for high-temperature applications. Conventional
powder metallurgy techniques, such as MA, are prone to microstructural
imperfections such as uneven mixing, contamination, and the genera-
tion of relatively large particles. Traditional post-processing methods
like hot rolling or extrusion offer benefits such as grain refinement and
eliminate the microcracks. However, such approaches add multiple
steps to processing, and often result in the alignment of oxide particles,
leading to an undesirable microstructural texture that causes anisotropy
and material property deterioration [ 12 ]. Nevertheless, post-processing
of MA alloys through thermo-mechanical processing offers the advan-
tage of effectively breaking down particles and ensuring uniform
dispersion throughout the material. Achieving control over particle
dispersion and grain size uniformity can potentially be accomplished in
the as-fabricated alloy through process parameter optimization during
thermo-mechanical processing. Friction stir processing (FSP) as one of
the thermomechanical process was widely used for the ODS alloys
[ 13 – 17 ]. Wang et al. [ 18 ] reported an increase in mechanical strength
by 16 % in MA956 alloy due to grain refinement achieved through oxide
dispersion. However, a resultant particle coarsening was also reported
due to thermal conditions which prevail during FSP. Conversely,
Ni-based alloy MA 754 depicted a reduction in strength after FSP
because of particle agglomeration [ 19 ]. Recently, additive friction stir
deposition (AFSD) has gained tremendous attention due to its ability to
fabricate Al alloy structures with high build rates. The AFSD technique
represents an innovative process under the domain of additive
manufacturing (AM) clubbed with the solid-state processing capabilities
of FSP, thereby enabling fabrication of near-net shaped components
with unprecedented efficiency and defect-free builds [ 20 ]. By inte-
grating process variables such as tool rotational rate (RPM), traverse
speed (mm/min), and material feed rate (mm/min) in AFSD, a broader
processing window can be attained [ 21 – 23 ]. By eliminating several
intricate processing steps, AFSD offers an alternative manufacturing
route, which results in a wrought-like recrystallized microstructure,
leading to enhanced properties that distinguish it from traditional
fusion-based AM techniques as well from powder metallurgy (PM)
routes [ 20 ]. Notably, as a high-temperature process, AFSD requires
controlled plastic flow of material to maintain the integrity of the
structural component during deposition. Thus, a most important attri-
bute of the AFSD process is the requirement of good flowability of ma-
terial at such processing temperatures.
The present investigation combines the attributes of AFSD process
and the benefit of superplastic properties of IN9052 at high tempera-
tures to produce a defect-free alloy deposit. A novel approach for opti-
mization of process parameter was adopted based on the high
temperature deformation behavior of the as received alloy. The impact
of AFSD processing conditions on the microstructure and mechanical
properties of the given ODS alloy was investigated, with the aim of
evaluating the feasibility of utilizing AFSD for the fabrication of such
alloys. From the application point of view, high temperature perfor-
mance of AFSD processed alloy was evaluated using high temperature
tensile testing. A detailed microscopic investigation coupled with
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and atom probe tomography
(APT) was performed to determine the potential causes of enhanced
mechanical properties at both room and high temperatures.
2. Materials and methods
The alloy investigated in the present study is Al-4Mg-0.5O-1.1C
(~wt.%) alloy, commercially available as IN9052, procured from
IncoMAP company. Initially the alloy was manufactured through the PM
route and was received in the form of extruded billets. Feed rods for
AFSD were prepared from the billet. The feed rods having a length of
143 mm and a square cross-sectional area of 9.52 × 9.52 mm
2
were
machined using the KENT wire electric discharge machine (EDM), as
depicted in Fig. 1 (c).
AFSD was carried out on MELD-B8 ® machine using an H13 tool steel
having an outer diameter of 38.1 mm. The tool had 4 teardrop-shaped
protrusions on the shoulder to ensure mixing of material and breaking
of the surface oxide layer during each layer deposition ( Fig. 1 (a2)). The
details of the process were presented in our previous study by Agrawal
et al. [ 22 ]. Graphite spray was used to lubricate the feedstock rods
during AFSD. The feedstock rods were fed vertically down through the
hollow spindle and rotating tool ( Fig. 1 (a2)) by the actuator, which gets
plasticized beneath the tool shoulder at high temperature by the com-
bined action of frictional heating and severe plastic deformation. The
full layer-by-layer deposition process in AFSD is divided into three
stages, as illustrated in Fig. 1 (b). Stage 1 is the dwell stage, where the
contact friction between the AFSD tool shoulder surface and the sub-
strate material assists in heat generation. During this stage, the feed rods
are slowly pushed against the substrate through the AFSD tool gradually
heating the rods due to contact friction which subsequently plasticizes
the feed rod material. Plasticized material then extrudes and fills the gap
between the rotating tool and the substrate. As the material starts to
plasticize and extrude out of the AFSD tool, Stage 2 initiates with the
transverse of the tool as per the computer-generated G-code which shear
deposits the layer onto the substrate plate as shown in Fig. 1 (b). Once a
single layer deposition is completed, the tool is raised to the new layer
height defined by the layer thickness and continues the specified tool
path, thus completing the layer-by-layer deposition. The final stage of
the AFSD process is Stage 3, where the tool retracts after completing the
G-code ( Fig. 1 (b)). The details of the process were presented in our
previous study by Mishra et al. [ 20 ]. A commercially available AA 6061
T6 alloy substrate plate was used for the deposition process. The depo-
sition was done utilizing different combinations of tool rotational rate
( ω ) , traverse speed (v), and actuator feed rate while maintaining a
constant thickness of 1 mm.
To monitor temperature variations during AFSD of IN9052 alloy over
the set of parameters used, during the processing ( Fig. 1 (d)), thermo-
couples were inserted carefully into the holes of diameter 0.75 mm,
which were drilled 2.5 mm below the surface of the substrate plate using
a Tormach PCNC 440 milling machine. A K type thermocouple with 0.5
mm diameter lead wire was used for temperature measurement, and an
Omega multichannel digital data acquisition module was used for data
acquisition at a temporal resolution of 0.025 s.
Uniaxial quasi-static tensile tests were conducted using a 2.5 kN load
capacity, custom-made, computer-controlled micro-tensile tester. Dog
bone-shaped miniature tensile samples of gauge length 5 mm, 1.25 mm
width and 1.2 mm thickness were machined precisely using EDM. The
samples were then polished to a surface finish of 1 µ m to remove surface
irregularities. The samples were extracted from the plane perpendicular
to the build direction ( Fig. 1 (d)). The tensile tests were performed at
room temperature (RT) at an initial strain rate of 10
– 3
per sec . Three
samples were tested for each condition to ensure statistical significance
and data accuracy. For high-temperature tensile tests, an ATS series
3210 split tube furnace with maximum operation temperature of 700
◦
C
was used.
Comprehensive multiscale microstructural characterization was
performed to gain insights on the microstructural evolution of the AFSD
deposited alloy. Samples for microscopy were also extracted from the

overall microstructural characterization was performed using scanning

3.3. Microstructural characterization
Detailed microstructural characterization was restricted to samples
S2 and S3 owing to their better, defect free build. Fig. 3 shows the in-
verse pole figure (IPF) maps and the SEM-BSE micrographs of the AR,
and as-deposited S2 and S3 conditions (along LD, Fig. 1 (e)). The IPF map
of the AR sample in Fig. 3 (a1) illustrates a notable presence of fine
grains, having an average grain size of 0.5 ± 0.3 µ m, alongside larger
grains with an average size of 2.5 ± 0.1 µ m . Such an observation con-
firms a bimodal microstructure, a conclusion further substantiated by
the grain size distribution map depicted in Fig. 3 (a2). The processed S2
and S3 samples exhibit recrystallized microstructure characterized by
fine grains with average grain size of about 1.7 ± 0.6 µ m and 1.3 ± 0.7
µ m , respectively. Notably, the average grain size of S2 and S3 samples is
larger (~70 % and 60 % for S2 and S3, respectively) than the average
grain size of fine grains in the AR condition ( Fig. 3 (b1), (c1)). Of crucial
importance is highlighting that grain size comparison specifically em-
phasizes the fine grains, given the significantly low fraction of coarse
grains in the AR condition. Additionally, while comparing S2 and S3
samples, S2 displayed relatively coarser grains as compared to the S3
sample ( Fig. 3 (b1) and 3 (c1)). S2 depicted a notable 23% increase in
grain size as compared to S3 ( Fig. 3 (c1)). Additionally, both the S2 and
S3 samples display a monomodal grain size distribution.
In the SEM image of the AR sample as shown in Fig. 3 (a3), coarse
constituent particles with an average size of about 0.7 ± 0.01 µ m were
observed. The average particle size in both S2 and S3 samples exhibited
finer particle size of 0.4 ± 0.02 µ m. The particle size reduction indicate
that the coarse particles were fragmented during the AFSD process.
Moreover, in AR sample, the particles were mainly observed at the grain
boundaries ( Fig. 3 (a3)) whereas such particles were uniformly dispersed
in as-deposited S2 and S3 condition ( Fig. 3 (b3, c3). Additionally, the
volume fraction of particles is higher after AFSD in comparison with the
AR condition ( Fig. 3 (a3), 3 (b3), 3 (c3)). The volume fraction of the
particles in the AR sample is 0.0134 ± 0.005, while that of S2 and S3 is
0.0192 ± 0.006 and 0.0154 ± 0.002, respectively.

tion ( Fig. 1 (e)), microscopy on the cross-section of the AFSD processed
S2 and S3 samples was conducted, as depicted in Fig. 3 (d, e). Fig. 3

bottom regions of the AFSD processed S2 and S3 samples, respectively.
The IPF maps reveal the presence of fine, recrystallized grains with
equiaxed morphology in both S2 and S3 samples throughout the build.
The average grain size along the build for S2 and S3 samples are sum-

size along the build direction was observed in both the samples which
indicates a uniform microstructure within the entire build. Additionally,
SEM images from the top, middle, and bottom regions of both the AFSD
processed S2 and S3 conditions are shown in Fig. 3 (d4 – d6) and 3 (e4-
e6). The average constituent particle size and volume fraction from top
to bottom in both S2 and S3 samples are summarized in Table 2 . Esti-

in the build in both S2 and S3 conditions, pole figures (PF) extracted
from the EBSD data, shown in Fig. 3 (d7 – d9) and 3 (d7 – d9), were

Thus, the AFSD ODS alloy showed a fully recrystallized grain structure
with nearly uniform grain size distribution, consistent constituent par-

in both AFSD builds. Notably, to maintain consistency throughout the
discussion, 1.7 ± 0.6 µ m and 1.3 ± 0.7 µ m are taken as the average grain
sizes for further calculations.

distinct conditions (AR, S2 and S3) were investigated by TEM imaging as
shown in Fig. 4 . The low-magnification image of the AR sample reveals

and white arrows in Fig. 4 (a2). The average size of the rod shaped

contaminated with light grey lamellar type features distributed

5. Conclusion
The present study successfully demonstrated the feasibility of AFSD
process to manufacture MA-ODS IN9052 alloy, emphasizing the novelty
of the research. The AFSD process driven microstructural engineering
resulted into superior mechanical properties in the AFSD processed alloy
amenable for both room and high temperature applications. The key
findings of the study are:
1. The novel parametric optimization of the AFSD process facilitated
the creation of an optimized process window, resulting in improved
mechanical properties for the MA-ODS IN9052 alloy.
2. The AFSD process dynamics transformed the bimodal, nanocrystal-
line grain structure in as received condition into a monomodal, fully
recrystallized, ultrafine grain structure in AFSD condition.
3. The high temperature and strain rates prevailed in AFSD process
resulted in shear induced fragmentation of coarse carbide (Al
4
C
3
)
particles and strain induced agglomeration of fragmented carbide
and C rich phases around fine oxide (MgO) particles. Such nanoscale
agglomerated particles were found to pin the GBs inhibiting grain
coarsening. Additionally, the unagglomerated oxide particles were
observed to be pin the dislocations.
4. The AFSD processed alloy demonstrated enhanced work hardening
with holding a satisfactory strength of ~380 MPa at room temper-
ature, achieving a strength-ductility synergy which is attributed to
dislocation pinning by the particles and dislocation-dislocation
interaction within the grains. The critical grain size for transition
from insignificant strain softening in AR alloy to strain hardening in
IN9052 alloy was determined to be within 400 – 500 nm.
5. High-temperature tensile tests demonstrated exceptional strength in
the AFSD condition, primarily attributed to nanoscale agglomerates
of oxide- carbide particles. These clusters effectively hindered grain
boundary sliding, while the unagglomerated oxide particles facili-
tated more efficient dislocation-particle interaction with
dislocations.
