ABSTRACT
FeCrCuMnNi multiphase high entropy alloy was cold-rolled to 85% reduction and annealed at di ﬀerent tem-
peratures. Recrystallization behavior of the alloy was investigated using XRD, SEM-EBSD, hardness, micro-
hardness, and tensile tests. The results revealed variation in phase distribution due to annealing and earlier
recrystallization of the FCC1 phase. Recrystallization of FCC1 and FCC2 phases initiated at 873 and 1073 K,
respectively, and a fully recrystallized microstructure was seen after 1273 K. Deformation texture components
eliminated slowly with increase in annealing temperature, and D and Cube components formed as a result of
recrystallization. Faster decrease in FCC1 microhardness was seen due to earlier recrystallization. In addition, an
excellent combination of strength and elongation was achieved in the partially recrystallized samples compared
to conventional alloys. The strength was a ﬀected more after annealing at temperatures higher than 1073 K.
Fracture behavior of FCC2 phase changed from a brittle fracture to a ductile fracture with increase in annealing
temperature; however, FCC1 phase revealed a ductile fracture even at low annealing temperatures.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are multicomponent equiatomic or
near- equiatomic alloys, consisting of at least ﬁve elements with
amounts in the range of 5 –35 at. %. There has been growing interest in
HEAs in recent years for their superior mechanical properties, such as
high strength, large strain-hardening capabilities, high fracture tough-
ness, and so on. [ 1–3]. According to Boltzmann's hypothesis, based on
the regular solution model, HEAs revealed high entropy of mixing in
liquid state or regular solution state [ 3,4]. Therefore, HEAs with special
composition, may consist of simple solid solution structures such as
face-centered cubic (FCC), body-centered cubic (BCC), and/or hex-
agonal closed-pack (HCP) instead of ordered phases or complex inter-
metallics [ 4–6]. However, the presence of ordered phases or inter-
metallics in the microstructure of di ﬀerent HEA systems were reported,
and it was seen that these phases signi ﬁcantly a ﬀects their properties
[1,3,7,8].
Hitherto, most of the studies carried out on HEAs have assessed as-
cast or homogenized materials [ 4,9–12]. However, similar to conven-
tional alloys, there is a great possibility of microstructural control
through thermo-mechanical processing (TMP) in HEAs in order to im-
prove their properties [ 1,2]. Nevertheless, this ﬁeld has recently at-
tracted the attention of researchers working on di ﬀerent parameters
and di ﬀerent alloying systems [ 7,13,14].
It has been reported that HEAs reveal resistance to grain growth and
maintain their deformed microstructure at higher temperatures, com-
pared to traditional alloys [ 1,15,16]. According to Guo et al. [ 13],
Al

grain boundary energy decreases due to the strain energy related to
severe lattice distortion in HEAs; therefore, the driving force for re-
crystallization decreases.
Moreover, a meaningful combination of tensile strength and elon-
gation for partially-recrystallized and recrystallized HEAs has been re-
ported [ 1,7,14,17]. Mostly, researchers have reported that the tem-
perature eﬀect evidently inﬂuences tensile strength and have reported a
linear decline in strength with increasing temperature [ 18]. Baker et al.
[1] showed > 800 MPa tensile strength with 20% elongation in re-
crystallized FeNiMnAlCr alloy. The dual phase nature of the alloy, ﬁne
grain size, and solution hardening e ﬀect were claimed to be the main
reasons for these properties [ 1]. Additionally, Park et al. [ 2] have
shown that the microhardness of the CoCrCuFeNi alloy decreases with

2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Processing
Using vacuum induction melting and high purity (> 99.85%) raw
materials, the FeCrCuMnNi high entropy alloy was produced. The ele-
ments were cleaned with acetone before charging into the crucible. The
melting chamber was maintained at a vacuum level of 1 × 10 −3 Pa,
and then, ﬁlled with high purity Argon gas to achieve 1 atm pressure.
The vacuum-ﬁlling cycle was repeated twice. The alloy was then casted
into a graphite mold and to ensure the compositional homogeneity, the
ingots were remelted twice. Then, 3 mm thick samples were cut from
the as-cast ingot and heat treated for 4 h at 1273 K. Cold-rolling to 85%
thickness reduction was conducted on the samples, and in order to
study the static recrystallization of the samples di ﬀerent annealing re-
gimes with di ﬀerent annealing temperatures (873 K, 973 K, 1073 K,
1173 K and 1273 K) were applied to the samples for 1 h.
2.2. Characterization
The crystal structure of the HEA was investigated using a PHILIPS X-
ray di ﬀractometer with Cu K
α (λ = 0.15406 nm) radiation. The phase
percentage and residual microstrain of the samples subjected to dif-
ferent annealing temperatures were determined through analyzing the
XRD patterns via the Rietveld software [Materials Analysis Using
Diﬀraction (MAUD)] [ 19]. The Rietveld method includes minimizing
the diﬀerence between a crystallographic model and experimental data,
via a least squares re ﬁnement. Such complicated modelling of the
height, width and position of Bragg re ﬂections in an X-ray di ﬀraction
pattern is directly related to the crystal structure of each studied phase
[20–22]. The MAUD software works based on the Rietveld method. This
software is an upgraded version of full X-ray pattern ﬁtting program,
LS1 [ 23], and has been used in di ﬀerent investigations to characterize
microstructural parameters including lattice parameter, phase percen-
tage, crystallite size, and residual microstrain [ 19–22,24]. Using a SEM
(PHILIPS FEI XLF30) equipped with an EBSD attachment, the micro-
structure of the samples was observed and their texture was measured.
Scan step size of 0.1 –0.5 μm was used for the annealed samples. Ad-
ditionally, chemical compositions of the individual phases were char-
acterized using SEM-EDS. At least 10 EDS points were conducted on
each phase.
In addition, Vickers hardness test was used to evaluate the hardness
of the alloy. Tests were performed under a load of 10 kg and within time
of 30 s. Additionally, using an automatic hardness measurement ma-
chine (Qness Q10A), Vickers microhardness test was performed on the
samples under a load of 5 g, within a time of 10 s, and selecting 3 dif-
ferent phases. Tensile test samples were machined from the annealed
sheets by electrical discharge machining (EDM). The gauge width and
length of the tensile specimens were 2.5 and 6.4 mm, respectively. The
tensile tests were performed using a universal testing machine (Make:
Model: Houns ﬁeld H25KS).

4. Discussion
As shown in Fig. 4a, recrystallization of the alloy initiates at 873 K
through nucleation in FCC1 phase. Grain growth occurs through in-
creasing annealing temperature, and the nucleation of recrystallized
grains of FCC2 phase initiates at 1073 K as shown in Fig. 4c. Grain
growth then continues in both phases with increasing annealing tem-
perature. Generally, proceeding through the typical nucleation and
grain growth stages is a characteristic of discontinuous recrystalliza-
tion, which occurs during recrystallization in low SFE material [ 25].
The FeCrCuMnNi alloy was considered as a low to medium SFE alloy in
a recent research by Shabani et al. [ 26]. It was seen that re-
crystallization in FCC1 phase initiates at a lower temperature compared
to FCC2 phase. Generally, a greater amount of deformation and strain
leads to more stored energy and more driving force for the re-
crystallization [ 1,25]. As shown in Fig. 3, the amount of residual mi-
crostrain after 85% reduction in thickness in FCC1 is almost 3 times
greater than FCC2 phase, which can be the main reason for the earlier
recrystallization of this phase. It is important to point out that higher
amount of microstrain in FCC1 phase is due to more deformation ex-
perienced by this phase during cold-rolling [ 26].
Fig. 6 illustrates that a low grain growth occurs in the alloy and,
even after annealing for 60 min at 1273 K, the grain size was < 7 μm.
This observation is in good agreement with the ﬁnding of other re-
searchers conducted in HEAs [ 13,14,27,28]. Sluggish di ﬀusion eﬀect in
HEAs is reported as the main reason for the low grain growth of this
alloy [ 13,27,28]. Otto et al. [ 27] reported that sluggish di ﬀusion is
responsible for the high activation energy for grain growth in HEAs. In
addition, Tsai et al. [ 28] argued that the 3 parameters of solution
hardening in HEAs, Suzuki's interaction, and low concentration of
equilibrium vacancy lead to low grain growth in HEAs. Solution hard-
ening in HEAs inhibits the slip or cross-slip of dislocations. Suzuki's
interaction in the whole-solute matrix leads to reduction in the stacking
fault energy, and consequently, increased spacing or stacking fault
between paired Shockley partial dislocations, thus making cross-slip
more di ﬃcult. Low concentration of equilibrium vacancy reduces the
activity of dislocation climb since vacancies are less important in in-
creasing the con ﬁguration mixing entropy in high-entropy alloys [ 28].
Moreover, the multiphase characteristic of the alloy is also e ﬀective on
the
grain growth. Growth or the recrystallized grains in one phase can
be limited to the interface of the other phase due to lamellar distribu-
tion of the phases. This observation is in good agreement with that of
Baker et al. [ 1], who have reported the role of the second phase in low
grain growth in FeNiMnAlCr HEA.
As shown in Fig. 7a, Brass, Rt-G, and S1 are the main texture
components of the sample annealed at 873 K. These components are the
deformation texture components of this alloy, which remain stable at
this temperature [ 26]. Generally, Brass is known as the main texture
component of the highly deformed low to medium SFE material
[15,25,26]. Increasing annealing time led to elimination of deformation
texture and formation of new components such as Cube and D. It has
been reported that the D component originates in the formation of
annealing twins [ 16]. Furthermore, the origin of the Cube texture could
be either nucleation of grains with a particular orientation or growth of
grains of speci ﬁc orientations [ 25]. However, alloying elements and
impurities can strongly decrease the Cube texture intensity [ 5,25].

As revealed in Figs. 10 and 11 , tensile strength of the alloy decreases
with increasing of the annealing temperature, with almost the same
trend that was seen in hardness. However, it seems that at lower tem-
peratures the strength declines at a lower rate in comparison to higher
temperatures. In addition, a signi ﬁcant increase occurs in elongation to
fracture at temperatures higher than 1073 K. Generally, the decrease in
strength and enhancement in elongation is attributed to recovery and
recrystallization [ 13,18,25]. However, the FeCrCuMnNi alloy seems to
preserve its strength up to higher temperatures compared to other
traditional alloys [ 29–31] in which < 10% decrease in strength was
seen after annealing at 873 and 973 K. Dini et al. [ 31] reported > 40
and 45% reduction in tensile strength of a TWIP steel after annealing at
873 and 973 K, respectively. In addition, according to Purcek et al.
[30], > 55% reduction in UTS occurs in interstitial free steel after an-
nealing at 873 K for 60 min. The ﬁrst reason for this phenomenon is the
higher strength of the HEAs in comparison to conventional alloys, due
to the higher degree of solid solution hardening from the increased
number of elements [ 32]. Furthermore, maintaining the deformed
structure up to higher temperatures and late recrystallization of the
HEAs is also another reason [ 13,28], which will be discussed more. Otto
et al. [ 27] reported that Cu based solid solutions, which were deformed
by cold rolling, and subsequently, annealed at temperatures lower than
the recrystallization temperature (FCC1 here), reveals extra hardening
due to solute segregation to stacking faults and short range ordering.
Fig. 4a and b illustrate that at lower annealing temperatures, FCC1
recrystallized through nucleation of new sub-micron grains, while
FCC2, which is the major phase (see Fig. 2), maintained its deformed
microstructure. Partially recrystallized microstructure is reported to be
an e ﬀective parameter on high strength of di ﬀerent alloys [ 7,31].
As revealed in Section 2, annealing behavior of the alloy can be
divided into two di ﬀerent parts. First, hardness, UTS, and YS of the
alloy
showed a slight decrease after annealing up to 973 K, and then, a
signiﬁcant drop in these properties was also seen with increase in an-
nealing temperature. The exact opposite behavior was found in elon-
gation to fracture. The ﬁrst part is a result of recrystallization in FCC1
phase, and the second part is attributed to the recrystallization in FCC2
and grain growth in both phases. Stress relaxation and dislocation an-
nihilation induced by the recovery and recrystallization process are the
main reasons for the drop in strength and increase in elongation
[18,25]. As depicted in Fig. 3, residual microstrain declines remarkably
with increase in annealing temperature and results in reduced strength.
Decrease in dislocation density and lack of interactions between dis-
locations lead to a decrease in strength and increase in elongation
[13,25]. In addition, replacement of severe deformed structure by un-
distorted equiaxed grains formed during recrystallization is also re-
sponsible for the variation in mechanical properties [ 13]. During an-
nealing, climb, slip, and cross-slip of the dislocation lead to the
annihilation of dislocation [ 25]. Furthermore, misorientation of new
grains increases due to the joining of dislocation to the grain boundary
of recrystallized grains. Consequently, recrystallized microstructure
forms consist of equiaxed grain with HAGBs [ 25]. Fig. 4 clearly illus-
trates that with increase in annealing temperature the amount of LAGBs

completely recrystallize or nearly recrystallize (> 95%) in one hour as
recrystallization temperature [ 13,25], for the FeCrCuMnNi alloy, re-
crystallization temperature is about 1223 K. Using the rule of mixture,
the melting point of the FeCrCuMnNi alloy is about 1720 K, therefore,
recrystallization temperature is 0.71 T

Tm for pure metals [ 25]. This phenomenon is also reported in other
HEAs [ 13,28,33,34]. Tsai et al. [ 28] reported that large solution
hardening, low stacking fault energy, and low equilibrium concentra-
tion of vacancies are the main causes of the resistance of HEAs to re-
crystallization. In addition, sluggish di ﬀusion renders grain boundary
migration slow and the movement of dislocation di ﬃcult [ 28,35].
Furthermore, according to Bhattacharjee et al. [ 5], dislocation, and
grain boundary energy decline due to the strain energy related to severe
lattice distortion in HEAs; therefore, the driving force for re-
crystallization decreases.
As shown in Fig. 12, the samples annealed at lower temperatures
revealed a bimodal fracture, where fracture of FCC1 phase is ductile
and a brittle fracture occurs in FCC2 phase. Generally, ductile fracture
is characterized by crack growth with excessive plastic deformation,
slow tearing of the metal, and formation of rough fracture surface with
equiaxed, deep dimples [ 36,37]. Formation of dimples and symptom of
plastic deformation are obvious during fracture of FCC1 phase in
Fig. 12 a to d. However, dimple size and depth seem to increase with
increase in annealing temperature. Recrystallization in FCC1 phase at
low temperatures (see Fig. 4) is the main reason for ductile fracture of
this phase; however, increase in annealing temperature leads to pro-
ceeding of recrystallization and softening of this phase, which results in
larger dimples in size and depth. Furthermore, smooth surface without
formation of dimples, low amount of plastic deformation, cleavage fa-
cets, and striations [ 26,36,38], which are the symptoms of the brittle
fracture, are evident in FCC2 phase after annealing at temperatures up
to 1173 K, (shown by yellow arrows). As illustarated in Figs. 3 and 4,
FCC2 phase revealed a resistance to recrystallization up to 1173 K.
Therefore, maintaining of the deformed microstructure in FCC2 phase is
the main reason for this fracture mechanism. However, after full re-
crystallization of the alloy at 1273 K, a completely ductile fracture with
large dimples in size and depth in both FCC phases is presented in
Fig. 12e. Additionally, polyhedral shape particles may act as preferred
nucleation sites for microvoids [ 39], and fracture during tensile test.
5. Conclusions
In the present study, the microstructure, texture, and mechanical
properties of a cold-rolled and annealed FeCrCuMnNi alloy were in-
vestigated using di ﬀerent tests. Samples were cold rolled to 85%
thickness reduction and annealed for 60 min at di ﬀerent temperatures.
The following conclusions were extracted:
1. Although no phase transformation occurred during annealing, dis-
tribution of the phases changed.
2. The fast elimination of residual microstrain in FCC1 phase led to
earlier recrystallization of this phase compared to FCC2 phase.
3. Deformation texture components weakened as the annealing tem-
perature increased. D and Cube texture were the major components
of the recrystallized samples.
4. Microhardness of the phases declined with increase in annealing
temperature, and hardness; however, the alloy preserved its strength
compared to conventional alloys. The nature of HEA, partial re-
crystallization, and the multiphase characteristic of the alloy were
the main reasons for this phenomenon.
5. As the elongation of the alloy increased with increase in annealing
temperature, fracture behavior of the alloy changed to a ductile
fracture. However, FCC2 phase resisted to softening more than FCC1
phase.
