Abstract
Tensile tests were carried out using a Ti–49at.%Al alloy in vacuum, air or ﬂowing hydrogen gas at room temperature, 473 K
and 573 K, before or after the specimens were exposed to a high temperature hydrogen gas. With increasing test temperature, in
vacuum, the unexposed specimen tends to decrease the yield strength, elongation and work-hardening rate, whereas the exposed
specimen exhibits the decrease in yield strength and an increase in elongation and work-hardening rate. The specimen tested in
a ﬂowing hydrogen gas at room temperature exhibits an increase in the yield strength and work-hardening rate, but drastically
decreases the elongation, in comparison with those of the unexposed specimens tested in vacuum. The hydrogen, whether it exists
inside or outside the specimen, results in a reduction in elongation from room temperature to 573 K. A small amount of hydrogen
introduced during plastic deformation in a ﬂowing hydrogen gas also affects their tensile properties. The higher yield strength and
work-hardening rate in the exposed specimens is associated with the dislocation pinning. © 2002 Elsevier Science B.V. All rights

1. Introduction
Titanium–aluminum intermetallics are of interest as
candidate materials in aircraft engines and structures
because of their attractive properties, such as light
weight, high strength and modulus, and good creep
strength and oxidation resistance at high temperatures
[1]. However, their limited ductility and fracture tough-
ness at ambient temperatures restrict their applications.
Environment embrittlement is an important factor for
the poor ductility of these alloys, and now it is well
accepted that hydrogen plays a main role in environ-
ment embrittlement. When tested in air or in a hydro-
gen gas, TiAl base alloys exhibit lower ductility than
they do in vacuum, and the reduction in ductility in
hydrogen gas is greater than that in air [2–4]. However,
the yield stress is insensitive to the test environment.
The addition of alloying elements and the microstruc-
ture control can affect and change the sensitivity of
these alloys to the test environment [5]. After TiAl
alloys are exposed to a high temperature hydrogen gas,
the compression yield strength increases due to the
formation of hydrides [6], while the critical stress inten-
sity factor decreases with increasing hydrogen concen-
tration [7]. A model has been proposed to describe the
dependence of tensile elongation on strain rate and
hydrogen pressure, and the hydrogen-induced environ-
ment embrittlement is interpreted phenomenologically
[8].
Because of the limited tensile ductility of TiAl alloys,
many studies on work-hardening of these alloys have
been carried out through compression tests. It has been
shown that the yield strength and the work-hardening
of two-phase /p97
2 + /p103TiAl alloys increase with increasing
volume fraction of the lamellar /p972 + /p103constituent [9].
When the test temperature is lower than 673 K, the
work-hardening of a TiAl base alloy is insensitive to
temperature, but it can be dynamically recovered above
673 K [10]. The addition of alloying elements is proven

to cause an increase in the work-hardening due to solid
solution hardening [11 –13]. The work-hardening of
TiAl base alloys is thought to be derived from long-
range elastic interactions between dislocations on paral-
lel and oblique slip planes as well as from dislocation
dipoles and debris defects, which are trailed and termi-
nated at jogs in screw dislocations and can be overcome
with the aid of thermal activation [14,15]. No study has
been reported on the effect of hydrogen on the work-
hardening of TiAl base alloys. It has been shown
previously that in Ti-H alloys, hydrogen in solid solu-
tion state produces signi ﬁcant softening of the /p97phase
and (/p97+ /p98) phase mixture, while it induces hardening of
the /p98phase [16]. The hydrogen-induced softening is also
observed in /p97-Ti [17]. The softening or hardening pro-
cess caused by hydrogen is mostly attributed to the
interactions between hydrogen and dislocations, rather
than to the effect of hydrogen on lattice friction [18,19].
In the present work, the yielding and work-hardening
of a Ti –49at.%Al alloy have been studied in tensile tests
at temperatures from 295 to 573 K in different test
environments before or after the specimens were ex-
posed to a hydrogen gas at 1073 K.
2. Experimental procedures
AT i –49Al alloy ingot (chemical composition in mass
%: Al 35.1, C 0.01, Si 0.01, Mn 0.02, Fe 0.04, Ni 0.02,
O 0.020, N 0.002, H 0.0003, Ti bal.) was prepared by
vacuum arc melting, hot-isostatic-pressed at 1473 K
under an argon pressure of 120 MPa for 3 h and then
homogenized at 1473 K for 24 h, followed by isothermal
forging at 1473 K to a thickness reduction of approx.
80%. Tensile specimens with a gauge section of 15×4×
1.5 mm were cut from the forged alloy, wrapped with
tantalum foil, and then heat treated in a high purity
argon gas at 1573 K for 2 h, followed by cooling in the
argon gas. A dual-phase microstructure with equiaxed /p103
grains and /p103//p97
2 laths was obtained as shown in Fig. 1.
Before tensile tests, all specimens were electro-pol-
ished in order to minimize the inﬂuence of scratches and
damage in the surfaces on tensile properties of the
specimens. Some specimens were exposed to a ﬂowing
hydrogen gas of 0.1 MPa at 1073 K for 3 h followed by
furnace-cooling in the hydrogen gas, and then some of
the hydrogen-exposed specimens were reheated at 823 K
for 1.5 h in a high purity argon gas to remove hydrogen.
The hydrogen content in the specimens was estimated
from a weight change before and after thermal desorp-
tion (TD) analysis up to 1273 K using an MSQ-150A
quadruple mass spectrometer (Ulvac Japan, Ltd.). The
heating rate of TD analysis was 200 K/h. X-ray observa-
tion was carried out to determine whether or not hy-
drides formed during the hydrogen exposure.
Thereafter, the unexposed specimens and the exposed
specimens are designated as the specimens before and
after the hydrogen exposure, respectively.
Tensile tests were carried out at a nominal strain rate
of 2 ×10
−4 s−1 using an Instron-type testing
machine at temperatures from room temperature to 573
K, and each specimen was held for 30 min in a given
environment at a given temperature before they were
loaded. Tensile test conditions are listed in Table 1, and
the 0.2% offset stress, plastic elongation to failure, and
work-hardening rate at 0.5% plastic strain were mea-
sured. For each condition, three samples were tested,
and the average of these three values for each tensile
property was adopted. The fracture surfaces of the
specimens were examined by scanning electron mi-
croscopy (SEM), and the dislocation structures near the
fracture surface of specimens after a tensile test were
investigated by transmission electron microscopy
(TEM).

4. Discussion
The experimental results indicate that the tensile
properties of the Ti –49Al alloy are sensitive to the test
environment. The presence of hydrogen, whether the
hydrogen exists inside or outside the specimens, in-
creases the yield strength and work-hardening rate at
0.5% plastic strain (Figs. 3 and 4), and decreases the
tensile ductility at each test temperature (Fig. 5). The
facts that the tensile properties were recovered to some
extent after the hydrogen content was decreased by
reheating the exposed specimens clearly suggest that the
hydrogen-induced embrittlement of the Ti –49Al alloy
should be caused by solute hydrogen.

suggest that there is a difference in the process of
hydrogen-induced embrittlement between the exposed
specimens tested in vacuum and the unexposed ones
tested in a hydrogen gas.
It is believed that the work-hardening of TiAl-based
alloys is ascribed to long-range elastic interactions of
dislocations [20]. The positive temperature dependence
of the work-hardening rate in the exposed specimens is
considered to be caused by the interaction of hydrogen
and dislocations. As shown in Fig. 7, the higher the test
temperature the more dislocations are pinned, which
suggests that the formation of ﬁne precipitates might
become more facilitated with increasing temperature,
and the precipitates would impede the dislocation mo-
tion. This is responsible for the embrittlement of the
exposed specimen.
On the other hand, when the unexposed specimens
were tested in a ﬂowing hydrogen gas, hydrogen could
be absorbed through the fresh surface, which was
formed by plastic deformation. Although the hydrogen
content in the specimens is very small and hardly
observed after fracture (Table 2), a small amount of
hydrogen permeating through the localized parts of the
surface into the specimen heavily affects the mechanical
properties. It is reported that in nickel single crystals
the absorbed hydrogen might reduce the stress to gener-
ate dislocations near surface [21] and facilitate the local
plastic deformation. Meanwhile, it is reported that the
ﬂow stress of an austenitic stainless steel (21Cr –6Ni–
9Mn) increases at temperature from 77 to 298 K by
hydrogen charging [22]. It is also known that the ﬂow
stress of iron increases or decreases at a low tempera-
ture as well as 200 K by hydrogen charging, depending
on the purity of the iron [23]. Thus, the present result
might be associated with the interaction between the
solute hydrogen and dislocation, resulting in the in-
crease in the yield strength and work-hardening rate for
the unexposed specimen. However, the amount of hy-
drogen permeating through the fresh surface is so small
and localized that hydride might not be formed and
cause the dislocation pinning, although the temperature
increases from room temperature to 573 K. Thus, the
work-hardening rate decreases with increasing
temperature.
When tested in air at room temperature, the unex-
posed specimens exhibited a similar reduction in ductil-
ity for the exposed ones tested in vacuum, and similar
yield strength and work-hardening rate at 0.5% plastic
strain for the unexposed ones tested in vacuum (Figs.
3–5). These facts indicate that the unexposed specimens
were not affected by air (moisture in air) at an early
stage of plastic deformation, but heavily in ﬂuenced
after a large plastic deformation. When the specimen is
tensile tested in air, hydrogen is considered to be intro-
duced by the chemical reaction between moisture in air
and fresh surface and permeates into the specimen
through the fresh surface [24]. Thus, in an early stage of
plastic deformation (0.2 –0.5% plastic strain), hydrogen
hardly permeates into the specimen, probably because
of the limited and localized fresh surfaces. At a larger
deformation stage, however, hydrogen would suf ﬁ-
ciently permeate into the specimens, because of the
copious fresh surfaces, thereby leading to the reduction
of ductility similar level to that for the exposed speci-
men tested in vacuum. Oxygen in air might also play a
certain role in inhibiting hydrogen permeation into the
specimen, since TiAl based alloys are known to exhibit
no embrittlement in dry oxygen [24,25].
5. Conclusion
The yielding and work-hardening behavior of a Ti –
49Al alloy was investigated in vacuum and in a ﬂowing
hydrogen gas from room temperature to 573 K using
tensile specimens exposed to and those unexposed to a
hydrogen gas at 1073 K, and the fracture surfaces and
dislocation structures after deformation were examined.
The results obtained are as follows. in vacuum, the
yield strength, work-hardening rate at 0.5% plastic
strain and elongation of the unexposed specimens tend
to decrease with increasing temperature; when tensile-
tested in a hydrogen gas, the unexposed specimens
exhibited an increase in the yield strength and work-
hardening rate, and the decrease in elongation at each
test temperature, compared with those tested in vac-
uum; when tensile-tested in vacuum, the exposed speci-
mens exhibited an increase in the yield strength and
work-hardening rate, and the decrease in elongation at
each test temperature, compared with the unexposed
specimens tested in vacuum. The work-hardening rate
of the exposed specimens increased with increasing test
temperature; after the exposed specimens were reheated
and hydrogen was removed, the tensile properties could
be recovered to some extent.
These results clearly suggest that the presence of
hydrogen, whether hydrogen exists inside, or outside
the specimen, results in a reduction in the elongation of
Ti–49Al from room temperature to 573 K. The hydro-
gen introduced during plastic deformation also affects
the tensile properties. The high yield strength and
work-hardening rate in the exposed specimens is associ-
ated with the dislocation pinning.
