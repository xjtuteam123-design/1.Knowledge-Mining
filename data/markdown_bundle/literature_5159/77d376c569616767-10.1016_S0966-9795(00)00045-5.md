Abstract
Current more or less progressed developments on the base of intermetallic phases usually aim at new materials with the highest
possible strength, creep resistance and oxidation resistance at the highest possible temperature and tolerable brittleness at lower
temperatures for structural applications at high temperatures. Intermetallic alloys o/C128er advantageous possibilities for reaching these
aims by appropriate combination of phases and optimisation of phase distribution. This is exempliﬁed with respect to strength and
creep resistance by recent studies on NiAl alloys with strengthening Laves phase and multiphase TiAl alloys. The beneﬁcial e/C128ects
of additional softening phases on deformability and toughness are demonstrated by the results of recent studies on Laves phase
alloys with disordered Fe–Al phase, NiAl alloys with disorderd Ni–Fe phase and partially transformed martensitic NiAl alloys.
Mechanisms and problems are discussed and perspectives are outlined. # 2000 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

1. Introduction
Manifold developments of new structural alloys based
on intermetallic phases — in particular the aluminides
of titanium, nickel and iron — have greatly progressed
in the recent past [1, 2]. Intermetallic alloys are attractive
because of high strength at all temperatures, which,
however, is usually accompanied by brittleness at lower
temperatures. The aim always is to increase the strength
with a shift of the possible service temperature to higher
temperatures and to reduce the brittleness at lower
temperatures. Higher strength can easily be obtained by
adding additional hard phases to the alloy in question.
However, this usually leads to increased brittleness at
low temperatures and increased brittle-to-ductile transition
temperature (BDTT). A ductilization of brittle inter-
metallic phases has been possible only in rare exceptional
cases, and the outstanding example is the ductilization
of Ni
3Al by the boron e/C128ect[3]. Usually a ‘‘ductilization’’
can only be achieved by addition of a second ductile
phase, which is sometimes termed ductile phase tough-
ening. In any case the task always is optimisation of the
ratio of high temperature strength and low temperature
ductility by variation of phase selection and phase dis-
tribution.
The present report overviews recent work on various
intermetallic alloy systems, which are studied for clarifying
the relation between microstructure and properties and
exploring possibilities of alloy development.
2. Intermetallic alloys with strengthening phases
As conventional disordered alloys, intermetallic alloys
can be strengthened by second phases which may be
introduced as dispersoids or precipitates and which may
be distributed discontinuously as discrete particles or
continuously as ﬁbres, lamellae, grain boundary layers
etc. The various strengthening e/C128ects were discussed
recently with respect to nickel aluminide alloys, and it
was noted that even soft precipitate particles can produce
strengthening [4].

3. Intermetallic alloys with softening phases
3.1. Laves phase+(Fe–Al)
The ternary system Fe–Al–Ta allows the formation of
the hard and brittle ternary Laves phase Ta(Fe 0.5+x,
Al0.5ÿx)2 with x 4 0.5 with the hexagonal C14 structure
in close analogy to the systems Ni–Al–Ta and Ni–Al–
Nb [31]. Accordingly, the Laves phase can be combined
with various binary Fe–Al phases with di/C128erent ductilities:
the intermetallic phases FeAl with B2 structure and Fe
3Al
with D03 structure and the disordered Fe-Al phase with
A2 (bcc) structure depending on x, i.e. the Al content [32].
The mechanical properties of the resulting two-phase
alloys have been studied in an exploratory way [32]. The
data indicated a decreasing yield strength and increasing
ductility with decreasing Al content corresponding to
decreasing atomic order in the Fe–Al phase. Of parti-
cular interest in view of alloy softening are the two-phase
alloys with about 10 at.% Al or less which contain soft
disordered Fe–Al besides the Laves phase. The reduction
of the Ta content of these alloys reduces the volume
fraction of Laves phase which reduces the BDTT from
about 1200
C to about 700C for 50 vol.% Laves phase
and down to room temperature for 23 vol.% Laves
phase according to the data in Fig. 2. Likewise the yield
stress at room temperature decreases from about 850 to
500 MPa with volume fraction of Laves phase decrease
from 50 to 23% according to data in [32], whereas the
yield stress at 800
C is practically constant in this
volume fraction range according to the data in Fig. 1.
Thus such two-phase TaFeAl alloys o/C128er attractive
possibilities for materials development since the adjustment
of the Ta content for adjusting the volume fraction of the
brittle Laves phase and the adjustment of the Al content
for adjusting the properties of the softening Fe–Al phase
allow the optimisation of properties with respect to the
particular application. It is noted that such alloys can be
prepared by ingot metallurgy without any problems,
textured material was obtained by directional solidiﬁcation
[33], and sheet material was formed by hot rolling [34].
3.2. NiAl + (Fe–Ni)
Various possibilities for intermetallic alloy softening
are o/C128ered by the ternary Ni–Al–Fe system [35]. The
best-known example is the combination of the nickel
aluminide Ni
3Al — the well-known /C130 phase — with the
disordered /C13–Ni–Al phase which is the base of the
familiar Ni-base superalloys.
The other nickel aluminide NiAl is of interest for the
development of high-temperature alloys because of its
high melting temperature and excellent oxidation resis-
tance, but it is brittle at room temperature [1]. The
brittleness of NiAl can be relieved by combining it with
both disordered bcc a-Fe–Al and fcc g-Fe–Ni [35]. NiAl
+ g-Fe–Ni alloys have been studied with respect to the
transition from brittle behaviour to ductile behaviour. It
was found that a volume fraction of at least 25% ductile
g-Fe–Ni was necessary for improving the room tem-
perature ductility of these two-phase NiAl alloys [4,35].
The transition from brittle behaviour to ductile beha-
viour was e/C128ected by the transition from a continuous
distribution of the ductile phase as thin layers on the
NiAl grain boundaries to a discontinuous distribution
of the ductile phase as particulate grains between the
NiAl grains. The room temperature ductility was further
improved by thermomechanical treatments, which pro-
duced a duplex phase distribution with particulate ductile
phase.
The attractive combination of strength and ductility
of these NiAl–Fe alloys initiated an alloy development,
which made use of alloying with Cr and microalloying
with Hf in order to improve the corrosion resistance
without losing the advantageous mechanical properties
[35,36]. This was achieved by adding up to 8 at.% Cr
and 0.03 at.% Hf, which necessitated a balanced reduc-
tion of the Fe and Ni content, whereas the Al content
had to be kept constant. The resulting alloy with 10%
tensile ductility at room temperature was found to be
most promising because of its advantageous mechanical
behaviour at low and high temperatures in combination
with its excellent high-temperature corrosion resistance.
The optimised alloy, which contains about 30 wt.% Fe,
10 wt.% Al, 8 wt.% Cr and 0.1 wt.% Hf (balance Ni)
and is designated Nicrofer 6009 Al10, has been pro-
duced as hot-rolled sheet material with dimensions up
to 900350040 mm

or sulphur makes Nicrofer 6009 Al10 promising for

manifold investigations of intermetallic phases and alloys
have shown that strength increases can be achieved with-
out problems by the familiar means of alloy strength-
ening which have been made use of in various alloy
developments. The present discussion with reference to
intermetallic alloys on the base of NiAl and TiAl has
shown that signiﬁcant strength increases can be achieved
by adding strengthening phases in sucient quantity.
Besides volume fraction of strengthening phases, the
distribution of strengthening phases is important, i.e. a
ﬁne dispersion of a phase further increases the strength
and the creep resistance at high temperatures.
However, a high strength is usually correlated with a
low deformability. A ductilization of brittle inter-
metallic phases was achieved only in rare exceptional
cases. In the general case, one has to rely on ductile
phase toughening, i.e. on addition of soft ductile phases.
The present discussion with reference to intermetallic
alloys on the base of a Laves phase and NiAl has shown
that the brittle-to-ductile transition temperature can
indeed be reduced from about 1200
C in the case of the
Laves phase to room temperature by adjusting the
volume fraction of soft phase. A volume fraction of
about 25% soft disordered Fe–Al phase was sucient
for achieving deformability at room temperature in the
cases of Laves phase with Fe–Al and NiAl with Fe–Ni.
In the third case of NiAl with stress-induced martensite
an improved toughness was only observed for two-
phase NiAl, i.e. for partially transformed material with
the two phases B2 NiAl and NiAl martensite.
There are many intermetallic phases, which are pro-
mising for high-temperature applications because of
high strength and high melting temperature, besides the
classic aluminides of iron, nickel and titanium. These
other phases can be combined with soft phases by
proper alloying. However, the basic knowledge of the
respective alloy systems is insucient for estimating the
potential. Thus, more fundamental work on the respective
multinary phase diagrams as well as the constitution and
properties of the phases involved are necessary for devel-
oping new intermetallic alloys with highest possible
strength, creep resistance and oxidation resistance at highest
possible temperature and tolerable brittleness at lower tem-
peratures for structural applications at high temperatures.
