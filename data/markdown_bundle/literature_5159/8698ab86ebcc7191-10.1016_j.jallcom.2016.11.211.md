Effect of B2-ordered phase on the deformation behavior of Ti-Mo-Al
alloys at elevated temperature

abstract
The microstructures and mechanical properties of as-cast Ti-Mo-Al alloys with various compositions
were systematically investigated in this study, aiming to explore the potentials of B2-ordered phase ( b2)
in high temperature applications. According to the experimental results, these as-cast Ti-Mo-Al alloys
were mainly composed of
b2 phase and contained a large number of dislocation walls and sub-grain
boundaries. Overall they exhibited a high level of compressive strength at 1073 K, with the maximum
value approaching ~879 MPa. The impressive mechanical properties of b2-dominating Ti-Mo-Al alloys are
related to the excellent stability of B2 structure and extensive dislocation entanglements at elevated
temperature.
© 2016 Elsevier B.V. All rights reserved.1. Introduction
The alloys developed on the basis of Ti-Al system have great
potentials in aerospace applications because of their low density,
high strength and good corrosion resistance [1e3]. Compared with
conventional Ti alloys, the phase transitions induced by alloying
into Ti-Al base alloys are more complicated since the established
disorder/order transformation along solidiﬁcation might be altered
and new ordered phases could be formed. Among the novel or-
dered phases reported in Ti-Al-X ternary systems, the b2 phase
(indicated as b0 in some literature) with B2 structure is a very
common one and has been widely observed, especially when X
represents refractory elements such as Nb, Mo, Cr, W, Ta and V
[4e6].
The signi ﬁcance of
b2 phase for the development of Ti-Al base
alloys mainly lies in the improvement of mechanical properties. It is
well known that the attractive high temperature strength of these
alloys generally arises from the stiff intermetallic compounds (e.g.
g-TiAl (L10) and a2-Ti3Al (D0 19)), which, on the other hand, bring
about the limited room temperature ductility and inadequate
workability. The b2 phase, being able to deform with multiple slip
systems, has been recognized as a critical toughening phase to a2
phase. For example, Gogia et al. demonstrated that the room
temperature ductility of Ti-1 1Nb-24Al alloy characterized with
a2þb2 dual phases was up to 6 e10% while there was almost no
ductility of single a2 alloys [7]. Recent studies further pointed out
that an even enhanced deformability could be achieved in the al-
loys composed of b2þO (Ti 2AlNb, orthorhombic) or a2þb2þO
phases, e.g. Ti-27Nb -22Al [8] and Ti-23Nb-22Al [9e11 ]. Moreover,
b2 phase has been employed to strengthen ductile b phase (A2).
Naka et al. made numerous efforts in constructing bþb2 micro-
structure for Ti-Al base ternary and quaternary alloys used at
elevated temperatures, as inspired by the classic g-Ni matrix þg0-
Ni3Al structure in nickel-based superalloys [12].
As a “b stabilizer” approximately four times stronger than
niobium, molybdenum exhibits impressive capability in stabilizing
the cubic structure for Ti-Al base alloys. The b2 (B2) phase in Ti-Mo-
Al system is usually denoted as Ti2MoAl, but it has been observed in
a wide composition range including stoichiometric one (Ti 50M-
o25Al25) and non-stoichiometric ones (Ti 45Mo5Al50,T i 54Mo20Al26
and Ti50Mo12.5Al37.5, etc.) [13e15]. Previous works on the Ti-Mo-Al
alloys dominated by B2 structure focused on their ordering and site
occupancy behavior [15e20]. For example, Chen and Jones reported
the ﬂexible occupancy of Mo atoms in either sublattice by exam-
ining characteristic X-ray intensities ratios of three Ti-Mo-Al B2
alloys [19]. Based on the Rietveld re ﬁnement of obtained X-ray and
neutron diffraction data, Singh et al. further divided the site occu-
pancies in B2 phases of non-stoichiometric Ti-Mo-Al alloys into two
groups. Assuming Ti atoms occupied A sites while Al and Mo
occupied B sites, they suggested that excess Ti atoms occupied B

effect of b2 on the deformation behavior of Ti-Mo-Al alloys is
essentially necessary. In this work, we investigate the microstruc-
ture and mechanical properties of Ti-Mo-Al alloys with various
chemical compositions, aiming to provide insights into the alloy
design strategy for Ti-Al base alloys.
1.1. Experimental procedure
Pure Ti (99.999%), Mo (99.99%) and Al (99.95%) were selected as
starting materials to fabricate Ti-Mo-Al alloys with various nominal
compositions (as listed in Table 1 ). Unless mentioned otherwise,
the compositions appeared in this work are in atomic percent. The
samples were prepared by conventional arc melting technique in
Argon atmosphere. All the ingots were ﬂipped over and re-melt for
at least ﬁve times to ensure chemical homogeneity.
The ﬁne-polished bulk samples were examined using a Bruker
D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu K
a radiation to
identify constituent phases in the as-cast Ti-Mo-Al alloys. Their
microstructures were characterized on a JEOL JSM-7800F scanning
electron microscope (SEM) under back-scattered electron (BSE)
imaging mode. The detailed chemical composition of constituent
phases was determined by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS)
on a Zeiss Ultra 55 SEM. We further examined their microstructure
and lattice structure by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on
a JEOL JEM-ARM 200F microscope operated at 200 kV.
The hardness of investigated alloys was measured by Vickers
hardness indenter at a load of 0.5 kg. Compression tests were
conducted on an Instron 5982 machine at 1073 K. The compression
specimens with the dimension of about 2 /C2 2x4 mm were electron
discharge machined from the ingots. For each alloy, at least three
compression specimens were tested to ensure the repeatability. All
the compression tests were performed at a constant strain rate _ε of
2.1 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1.
2. Results and discussion
Fig. 1 shows the XRD spectra of as-cast Ti-Mo-Al alloys investi-
gated in this study. It is clearly seen that these Ti-Mo-Al alloys
exhibited characteristic re ﬂections of body-centered cubic (BCC)
structure. With increasing concentration of Mo, the re ﬂections
(100) and (210), which are associated with B2-ordered structure (b2
phase), become visible. However, in Fig. 1 the superlattice re-
ﬂections (e.g. (210)) are not so conspicuous for the strongly ordered
composition, Ti 50Mo25Al25. These weak re ﬂections of B2 super-
lattice agree with the observation from Singh et al., who suggested
that the neutron diffraction would be more suitable than X-ray
diffraction in inspecting the superlattice re ﬂections of Ti-25Al-
25Mo alloy because of the negative scattering amplitude of tita-
nium [15]. For ternary B2 intermetallic compounds, the strong
fundamental re ﬂections from low index planes such as (1 10) are
able to mask the superlattice re ﬂections to a large extent during
XRD measurements. When the bulk XRD sample is aligned with
weakened (1 10) reﬂection, the superlattice re ﬂections will appear,
as shown by the Ti 50Mo35Al15 and Ti59Mo22Al19 diffraction patterns
in Fig. 1. Based on the location of all (100) and (210) re ﬂections in
Fig. 1 , we calculated the lattice parameter of b2 phase to be
~0.318 nm, in consistence with the values reported by Bohm [23]
and Singh [18]. The dominating role of b2 phase in our Ti-Mo-Al
alloys has also been con ﬁrmed by TEM. Fig. 2 a and c are repre-
sentative bright- ﬁeld TEM micrographs of Ti 50Mo35Al15 and
Ti50Mo25Al25 alloys, respectively, with their corresponding
diffraction patterns displayed in Fig. 2 b and d, where the super-
lattice spots were detected (as indicated by arrows). The prevalence
of b2 phase might be induced by the high rate of ordering trans-
formation in Ti-Mo-Al ternary system. According to the differential
thermal analysis on Ti 50Mo25Al25 alloy from Das [24], the ordering
reaction occurred around 1691 K, below which the ordered phase
was more stable.
Fig. 3 a is a representative BSE-SEM image illustrating the
microstructure of Ti59Mo22Al19 alloy. As seen from the ﬁgure, there
is limited compositional variation all through this sample. In
contrast, a markedly compositional variation was observed in
Ti50Mo35Al15 sample, as reﬂected by the strong image contrast in its
BSE-SEM micrograph ( Fig. 3 b). The related EDS analysis indicates
that the dark areas in Fig. 3 b is featured with a relatively high
content of Ti (~Ti 60.0Mo21.3Al18.7). Nevertheless, TEM examinations
on both dark and bright areas show B2 diffraction patterns. In other
words, B2-ordered structure could be easily formed in Ti-Mo-Al
alloys. It seems that the increasing addition of Mo is prone to
enhance the segregation in Ti-Mo-Al alloys and consequently to
induce such compositional variations. We attempted to conduct
annealing treatments on these Ti-Mo-Al alloys at 1473 K but failed

It is noted that there are a great deal of dislocations walls and
subgrain boundaries existing in the as-cast Ti-Mo-Al alloys ( Fig. 2a
and c). A speci ﬁc investigation of these internal defects was carried
out by HRTEM. Fig. 2e illustrates the atomic arrangement around a
typical internal defect, revealing the presence of misorientation.
This kind of misorientation is obviously manifested in the corre-
sponding fast Fourier transformation (FFT) pattern ( Fig. 2 f). The
subgrain boundaries probably arise from the solid-state trans-
formation from A2-disordered structure to B2-ordered structure
rather than liquid to solid transformation since the L /b/b2 so-
lidiﬁcation route is commonly obeyed by the Ti-enriched Ti-Mo-Al
alloys. The high density subgrain boundaries potentially act as
barriers for gliding dislocations in deforming process and ﬁnally
lead to the strengthening in Ti-Mo-Al alloys, which will be dis-
cussed in following. Unfortunately, anti-phase boundaries (APBs),
which are usually deemed as a straightforward evidence of solid-
state ordering transformation, have not been observed here. It
had been reported that the growth rate of anti-phase domains
(APDs) in B2 phases of Ti-Al-X (X ¼ Nb, Ta, Mo) systems is
extremely high [24]. As a result, the anti-phase boundaries could
disappear quickly during cooling of casting.

To evaluate the high temperature mechanical properties of
these Ti-Mo-Al alloys, compression tests were performed at 1073 K.
Fig. 5 illustrates the true stress-strain curves of each alloy. Overall,
these as-cast Ti-Mo-Al alloys exhibited impressive high tempera-
ture strength, which could be up to ~879 MPa, as obtained in
Ti50Mo35Al15 sample. Except the stoichiometry composition
(Ti50Mo25Al25), the yield strength of our Ti-Mo-Al alloys
(640e750 MPa) has greatly surpassed other reported B2 phase
dominating Ti-Al-X alloys such as Ti-Nb-Al (~400 MPa) [22], Ti-V-Al
(~100 MPa) [18] and Ti-Zr-Al (~450 MPa at _ε ¼ 3 /C2 10/C0 4s/C0 1) [25] at
this temperature. The excellent mechanical strength of Ti-Mo-Al
alloys largely resulted from the outstanding stability of B2-
ordered phase at high temperatures. It is well known that the B2
phases in Ti-Nb-Al (i.e. ~Ti 2NbAl), Ti-Zr-Al (i.e. Ti 2ZrAl), Ti-V-Al (i.e.
Ti5VAl2) systems have the tendency of decomposing into O phase or
u phase when they are subjected to external loading at elevated

Another feature worth noting in Fig. 5 is the strain hardening
behavior of as-cast Ti-Mo-Al alloys. Learned from Fig. 5 ,T i 50M-
o35Al15,T i 59Mo22Al19 and Ti 50Mo25Al25 alloys displayed conspicu-
ous strain hardening at 1073 K. In particular, Ti 50Mo25Al25 alloy
presented an early yielding at ~387 MPa but subsequently experi-
enced considerably strong strain hardening in plastic deformation
region. By following the classic power-law equation of describing
strain hardening, the strain hardening exponents are calculated to
be ~0.15 for Ti 50Mo35Al15 and ~0.46 for Ti 50Mo25Al25, respectively
(Fig. 7). It is the ﬁrst time that the strong strain hardening has been
observed in Ti-Al-X system at such a high temperature. The
different stress-strain responses between Ti 50Mo25Al25 and other
tested Ti-Mo-Al alloys ( Fig. 5 ) could be partially explained by the
inﬂuence of stoichiometry on the mechanical behavior of B2
intermetallic compounds. Due to the low concentration of va-
cancies and antisite defects in the stoichiometric composition, the
strengthening effects arising from these defects could hardly be
exerted [26,27]. Hence, the yield strength generally exhibits
minima at this composition, as re ﬂected by the early yielding of
Ti
50Mo25Al25 sample on Fig. 5 . The subsequent strain hardening is
associated with the interaction between dislocations and solute
atoms. For our Ti-Mo-Al alloys, the impeding effects on moving
dislocations from enormous sub-grain boundaries should be also
remarkable in deforming process. To better understand the
strengthening mechanisms, the microstructural changes in Ti-Mo-
Al alloys after compression were carefully examined. Fig. 8 is a
bright-ﬁeld TEM micrograph revealing the microstructure of an as-
cast Ti 50Mo25Al25 alloy compressed to the plastic strain of ~5% at
1073 K, from which the extensive dislocation entanglements can be
clearly seen. According to the absence of strain hardening in
Ti59Mo9Al32 and Ti62Mo14Al24 alloys shown in Fig. 5, the amount of
Mo is supposed to play a critical role in strengthening Ti-Mo-Al
alloys at elevated temperatures. When the concentration of Mo is
below 15 at.%, there would be limited strain hardening effect.
Moreover, the variation in mechanical response of the Ti-Mo-Al
alloys might also be related to the degree of ordering and site oc-
cupancy. However, it is extremely dif ﬁcult to estimate the detailed
ordering degrees or predict the effect of site occupancy on the
dislocation motions at elevated temperatures based on present
experimental data.
With regards to the excellent phase stability and distinguished
compressive strength identi ﬁed in the b2-dominating Ti-Mo-Al
alloys, great potentials in high temperature applications can be
expected for this alloy system. In order to guarantee the strength-
ening effects of B2-ordered phase, suf ﬁcient Mo concentration
should be included in alloy design.
3. Conclusions
The as-cast Ti-Mo-Al alloys investigated in this study is domi-
nated by B2-ordered phase ( b2). These alloys exhibit a high level of
compressive strength, which is up to ~879 MPa at 1073 K. The
impressive mechanical properties of b2-dominating Ti-Mo-Al alloys
at elevated temperature are related to the excellent stability of B2
structure and extensive dislocation entanglements occurred during
deforming process.
