Mechanical and hardness evaluations of
Fe–18Cr–18Mn alloys
J. Rawers * and N. Duttlinger
A series of Fe–18Cr–18Mn–N–C alloys was produced and evaluated to determine their
mechanical and hardness properties. In one group, different levels of nitrogen and carbon were
added to a base composition of Fe–18Cr–18Mn. The base Fe–18Cr–18Mn alloy had a fcc–bcc
duplex microstructure. The addition of nitrogen and carbon stabilised the fcc phase. These alloys
had increasing room temperature hardness, strength, and failure energy with increasing
interstitial concentrations. At cryogenic and elevated temperatures, these alloys had increasing
hardness (and by implication, increased strength) with increasing interstitial concentrations. In a
second group, different levels of nitrogen and carbon were added to the base Fe–18Cr–18Mn
composition to which minor solid solution additions of silicon, molybdenum, and nickel had been
added. Minor alloy additions made only nominal improvement to mechanical and hardness
properties. Nitrogen remained interstitial. However, these minor solid solution additions reduced
carbon solubility resulting in the formation of M
23C6 precipitates that, while increasing alloy
hardness and strength, greatly reduced fracture toughness. The present study suggests that
replacing nickel with manganese in stainless steels results not only in more economical alloys but
also in alloys with higher strength and hardness.
Keywords: Nitrogen, Carbon, Manganese, Stainless steels microstructure, Tensile, Hardness
Introduction
With increasing uncertainty of the future cost of nickel,
materials engineers are reevaluating their pervasive use of
nickel based stainless steels. There are currently several
grades of manganese based stainless steels that can
substitute for nickel based stainless steels.
1 These
manganese based stainless alloys generally have superior
strength2 and higher wear resistance 3,4 than nickel rich
stainless steel. Manganese based stainless alloys gain
much of their engineering advantage over nickel stainless
steels from their increased interstitial solubility, especially
for nitrogen.
5,6 Interstitial nitrogen as high as 0 ?5 wt-%
can be obtained during atmospheric melting of Fe–18Cr–
18Mn.1,5 Recent research studies have shown that, with
sufﬁcient nitrogen addition, carbon can also be added to
increase the interstitial concentration,
7–10 further increas-
ing mechanical properties such as strength, hardness,
failure energy and wear.
The present study evaluates mechanical and hardness
properties of Fe–18Cr–18Mn stainless alloys as a
function of nitrogen and carbon concentration and
examines the effects that minor solute solution additions
of nickel, molybdenum, and silicon have on Fe–18Cr–
18Mn–N–C alloys. Total nitrogen and carbon concen-
tration vary from 0 ?0t o .1?0 wt-%. Room temperature
tensile properties were measured. Hardness measure-
ments, generally a good indicator of steel strength, were
obtained at room temperature, cryogenic (63 and
195 K), and elevated temperatures (675 to 1025 K) to
evaluate these alloys over a wide range of potential
engineering applications.
Experimental
Six 1?0 kg ingots were produced:
(i) ND-1: base compositions of Fe–18Cr–18Mn
(ii) ND-2: base composition with nitrogen
(iii) ND-3: base composition with nitrogen and
carbon, plus a series of three compositions to
which minor solute concentrations of 2 wt-%Ni,
1 wt-%Mo and 0 ?3 wt-%Mo were alloyed
(iv) ND-4: base composition with minor solute
addition with nitrogen added
(v) ND-5, ND-6: base composition with minor
solute addition with nitrogen and two different
carbon concentrations.
Three alloys were made from 99% pure elemental powder
compacts (ND-1, ND-2, ND-3). Three were remelted
from commercial alloy (Carpenter 18–18 plus) to which
carbon was added (ND-4, ND-5, ND-6). Compacts were
melted in controlled gas environments (argon: ND-1, or
nitrogen: ND-2 thru ND-6) at one atmosphere pressure.
Subsequent to melting, ingots were homogenised at
1425 K for 12 h, furnace cooled, then hot worked at
1425 K in 25–30% reductions into ,3 mm thick sheets.

Results and analysis
Mechanical properties such as strength, ductility and
hardness are a function of chemistry, microstructure and
processing. The alloys in the present study all have
similar base composition: Fe–18Cr–18Mn. As a result of
similar hot working and annealing, the alloys have very
similar grain size, ,50 mm. The base alloy Fe–18Cr–
18Mn had the duplex, fcc–bcc microstructure (ND-1).
The addition of nitrogen (ND-2) and nitrogen plus
carbon (ND-3) stabilised the fcc phase as predicted by
the Schaefﬂer formalism,
11 remaining interstitial without
forming precipitates. Minor alloy additions of 2 wt-
%Ni, 1 wt-%Mo and 0 ?3 wt-%Si went into the fcc
matrix as solid solutions (ND-4, ND-5, ND-6). Nitrogen
additions to these minor addition alloys remained
interstitial (ND-4), whereas nitrogen plus carbon addi-
tions resulted in the nitrogen remaining interstitial and
the carbon forming a uniform distribution of 10–20 mm
M
23C6 precipitates (ND-5, ND-6). 12
Increasing nitrogen and carbon interstitial concentra-
tion expanded the fcc lattice d-spacing, increased the
room temperature strength, ductility, energy to failure,
and hardness properties. The tensile properties of the
precipitation free alloys, i.e. yield and ultimate strength,
failure strain, and failure energy increased with increas-
ing interstitial nitrogen (ND-2) and nitrogen plus carbon
(ND-3) concentrations. For alloys that contained similar
interstitial nitrogen concentrations (ND-2, ND-4) there
was solid solution strengthening. However, the addition
of carbon to these alloys resulted in formation of the
M23C6 precipitate, decreased yield strength, ductility
and, thus, failure energy (ND-4, ND-5, NC-6) . Alloys
ND-5 and ND-6 had nearly identical base and minor
alloy chemistry, nitrogen concentration, and fcc lattice
d-spacing (i.e. identical interstitial concentration). The
higher carbon concentration in ND-6 resulted in
increase in carbide precipitate concentration density,

similar hardness, whereas, at cryogenic temperatures,
the duplex phase alloys had the highest hardness.
As mentioned at the beginning of the manuscript, one
of the principal reasons for the present study was to
evaluate manganese based stainless steels for possible
substitution of or replacement for nickel based stainless
steels, such as SS304, 310, 316, 347. Comparing the Fe–
18Cr–18Mn alloys in the present study to commercial
Fe–18Cr–8Ni (300 series) alloys show the alloys in the
study have higher yield ( >600 versus (250 MPa) and
tensile strengths (1000 versus 550 MPa), greater elonga-
