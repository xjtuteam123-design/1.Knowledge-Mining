Alloys containing dislocation bundles exhibit optimal plasticity in tensile tests.
1. Introduction
Developing high-entropy alloys (HEAs) has disrupted the traditional 
concept of alloy design, which typically involves combining only one or 
a few major elements [ 1 , 2 ]. By incorporating multiple principal ele -
ments, HEAs not only broaden the compositional range of alloys but also 
create solid solution systems with distinctive structural characteristics 
that facilitate superior overall performance. This innovative design 
approach has attracted extensive attention from the materials science 
community over the past few decades [ 3 – 8 ]. Since it was first proposed 
in 2004, HEAs have emerged over the past decade as materials with 
exceptional properties that significantly outperform conventional alloys 
[ 9 – 14 ]. Driven by this advanced design concept, high-temperature 
resistant HEAs incorporating low-density, high-melting-point elements 
are emerging as a prominent research focus [ 15 – 19 ] (see Table 1 ).
According to the requirements of the national industry in recent 
years, lightweight high-temperature resistant HEAs have become the 
hotspot of research. They have received wide attention because of their 
excellent properties, such as high strength, excellent oxidation resis -
tance, and good corrosion resistance, and researchers are exploring the 
potential applications of these alloys in advanced engineering materials, 
especially in the ductility and specific yield strength of the performance 
of the outstanding [ 13 , 20 – 24 ]. So far, a series of lightweight 
high-temperature resistant alloy systems have been developed, such as

Specifically, it was found that the microstructure of the alloys can be 
effectively optimized by controlling the Al-induced B2 phase ordering, 
thereby overcoming the trade-off between strength and ductility com -
mon at room temperature [ 31 ]. Therefore, an in-depth investigation of 
the formation mechanism of Al-induced B2 ordered phases in light -
weight high-temperature resistant HEAs and their specific effects on 
tensile properties is of great scientific significance and application 
prospects. This research direction not only helps to reveal the influence 
law of the B2 phase on the mechanical properties of alloys but also may 
provide a new theoretical basis and practical guidance for the design of 
high-performance materials, especially in improving the balance of 
plasticity and strength of alloys, with potential innovative 
breakthroughs.
To meet the urgent demand for lightweight, high-specific-strength 
materials in the aerospace industry, researchers have developed a 
Ti – Zr – Nb – V – Al alloy system, representing a breakthrough in light -
weight high-performance alloys. Compared to conventional lightweight 
alloys, the Ti – Zr – Nb – V – Al system exhibits significant advantages in 
density, strength, thermal stability, and long-term high-temperature 
performance. A key highlight is its low density, achieved by incorpo -
rating Al, which not only reduces mass but also enhances processability 
and corrosion resistance. Moreover, this alloy demonstrates exceptional 
thermal stability due to the strategic addition of high-melting-point el -
ements (Ti, Zr, Nb, V), effectively stabilizing the B2 phase even at high 
Al content [ 32 ]. Unlike traditional Al-containing HEAs, which often 
suffer from phase instability and decomposition at elevated tempera -
tures, the Ti – Zr – Nb – V – Al system maintains balanced strength and 
ductility with minimal density, outperforming other Al-bearing HEAs in 
aerospace and high-temperature applications [ 33 , 34 ]. Its optimized 
composition prevents performance degradation under thermal cycling 
or prolonged heat exposure, offering superior mechanical properties and 
long-term stability, thereby addressing a critical limitation of conven -
tional alloys.

2.1. Sample preparation
To prepare the Ti

99.99 wt% were selected as raw materials. The alloy samples were 
fabricated using a water-cooled copper crucible vacuum non- 
consumable arc melting furnace. Before melting, the melting chamber 
and copper molds were thoroughly cleaned with anhydrous ethanol to 
ensure a high level of cleanliness in the experimental environment. 
During the charging process, the elements were placed in the copper 
molds in order of their melting points, from lowest to highest. To further 
enhance the cleanliness of the melting environment, high-purity Ti 
ingots were added early in the melting process to adsorb and remove 
oxygen from the chamber during their complete melting. The melting 
system was then evacuated to 5 Pa using a mechanical pump, and a 
molecular pump was used to further reduce the pressure to 3 × 10
 3 
Pa. 
This pumping process was repeated thrice to thoroughly remove im -
purity gases from the chamber. Finally, vacuum arc melting was carried 
out in the water-cooled copper crucible, with electromagnetic stirring to 
improve melting uniformity. At least six remelts were performed per 
ingot to ensure the uniform distribution of alloy composition.

2.3. Tensile test
To systematically evaluate the quasi-static tensile properties of the 
prepared alloys, ingots were electrospark machined (EDM, DZ703) to 
extract standard dog-bone tensile specimens. The specimens had a gauge 
length of 12 mm, a gauge width of 2.5 mm, and a thickness of 2 mm, 
meeting the requirements for standard tensile testing. Before testing, the 
specimen surfaces were carefully treated to ensure a high-quality surface 
condition. SiC sandpaper was used progressively, starting from 240 grit 
and finishing with 2000 grit, to effectively remove surface impurities 
and create a smooth, defect-free surface. Tensile tests were performed 
utilizing a universal testing machine with the A10 standard extensom -
eter (ETM 105D) at room temperature with a quasi-static strain rate of 
0.72 mm/min. To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the re -
sults, at least three independent tensile tests were performed for each 
alloy composition.

4.5. Effect of dislocations produced by different contents of B2 phase on 
plasticity
To better understand the mechanism by which B2 phase content 
affects dislocation behavior and its impact on plasticity, a schematic 
diagram was created to illustrate the process more clearly. Fig. 10 de -
picts the changes in dislocation structure at different B2 phase contents. 
The results show that when the alloy contains only a trace amount of B2 
phase, the dislocations are primarily arranged relatively parallelly. This 
dislocation structure helps to elongate the atomic planes of the crystal, 
creating a lattice arrangement that is more favorable for plastic defor -
mation. The presence of parallel dislocations facilitates dislocation slip, 
reducing dislocation resistance and significantly enhancing the alloy ’ s 
plasticity and ductility [ 31 ]. However, as the B2 phase content in -
creases, the dislocation structure becomes more disordered. A large 
amount of B2 phase leads to chaotic dislocation arrangements at the 
atomic level, forming complex ring dislocations. This disordered struc -
ture not only reduces the slip capacity of dislocations but can also cause 
local lattice distortions and stress concentrations, ultimately diminish -
ing the material ’ s plasticity and deformability. Specifically, the disor -
dered arrangement of dislocations leads to local shrinkage of atomic 
planes, which increases the brittleness of the crystal structure and hin -
ders plastic deformation under external forces. Additionally, when only 
a trace amount of B2 phase is present, the dislocation walls maintain a 
relatively parallel structure, which helps preserve the orderly arrange -
ment of atoms, further improving the alloy ’ s ductility. In contrast, when 
the B2 phase content is higher, the chaotic distribution of dislocation 
walls complicates the interaction forces between atoms. This leads to 
localized tightening at the atomic level, as well as crossing and entan -
glement of dislocations, which inhibits plastic flow. These findings 
highlight the significant role that B2 phase content plays in influencing 
dislocation behavior and plastic deformation in the alloy. Proper regu -
lation of B2 phase content can therefore optimize the plasticity and 
mechanical properties of the material.
To explain the above phenomena, the microstructural changes 
within the grains can be further analyzed by integrating the results 
shown in ( Fig. 2 (i) and (j)) and ( Fig. 5 (e) and (f)) for the Al0.5 alloy, in 
which the BCC phase coexists with a trace amount of B2 phase after 
tensile deformation. In this alloy, a distinct structure of dislocation 
bundles, oriented parallel to each other, is formed after tensile defor -
mation. The intertwining of individual dislocation threads creates these 
bundles through single-slip motion, reducing the dislocation energy [ 31 ,
58 ]. When these dislocation bundles encounter grain boundaries, they 
typically interact with each other in an intertwined manner, forming 
strong and stable dislocation walls. The formation of dislocation walls 
significantly impacts the plasticity of the material and enhances the 
alloy ’ s plastic deformability. During stretching, the dislocation walls not 
only increase the hindrance effect of grain boundaries but also promote 
further plastic deformation by effectively localizing the slip behavior of 
dislocations. Specifically, the synergistic effect of dislocation bundles 
and dislocation walls facilitates the interaction between grain bound -
aries, leading to more complex plastic behavior during the deformation 
of the alloy system. As seen in Fig. 9 (e – f), the dislocation bundles act as 
local stress concentrators during the tensile process, contributing to the 
further slip and distribution of dislocations, which enhances the mate -
rial ’ s deformation capability. Although the dislocation bundles partially 
fracture or rearrange during stretching, their presence still helps delay 
macroscopic rupture. It provides additional plastic deformation paths, 
thereby enhancing the fracture resistance of the material at high strains. 
This suggests that dislocation bundles positively contribute to the plastic 
behavior of the alloy, improving its overall plasticity. Further analysis of 
Fig. 8 reveals that a trace amount of B2 phase primarily drives the 
generation of an appropriate amount of dislocations. In the Al0.5 alloy, 
the trace amount of B2 phase not only promotes the initial generation of 
dislocations but also triggers the formation of dislocation bundles 
through its interaction with the matrix. These dislocation bundles evolve 
during subsequent deformation, forming dislocation wall structures as 
they interact with grain boundaries. Through this process, the B2 phase 
plays a key reinforcing role in the alloy ’ s plastic deformation, contrib -
uting to a better understanding of the material ’ s mechanical behavior at 
the microscopic scale. Additionally, the B2 phase increases dislocation 
density and promotes further dislocation movement and obstruction 
through dislocation bunching and grain boundary interactions. This 
leads to a higher resistance to deformation during plastic deformation.

contents. The study explores the formation of a trace amount of B2 phase 
induced by Al elements. It examines how these B2 phase promote the 
formation of dislocation bundles, thereby significantly enhancing the 
strength-plasticity synergy of the alloys. The main conclusions are as 
follows. 
(1) In the studied alloy system, Al content is crucial for B2 phase 
formation and stability. With increasing Al, the BCC phase 
transforms to a BCC-B2 coexistence phase. In Al0.5 and Al0.7 
alloys, the B2 phase amount rises progressively: only a trace 
amount of B2 phase in Al0.5, but a significant quantity in Al0.7, 
showing Al content promotes B2 phase formation.
(2) In the studied alloy system, a trace amount of B2 phase forms in 
the Al0.5 alloy. It promotes appropriate dislocation generation, 
forming parallel dislocation bundles. These bundles do single-slip 
motion, reducing dislocation energy. Originating from inter -
twined dislocation lines, they interact with grain boundaries to 
form stable walls. These walls regulate strain and deformation 
during plastic deformation, helping the alloy distribute stresses, 
reduce stress concentration, and enhance plasticity.
(3) Molecular dynamics simulations show that among alloys with 
only BCC phase and those with varying B2 phase amounts, alloys 
with a trace amount of B2 phase had exceptional tensile plas -
ticity. Dislocation analysis found appropriate dislocations 
generated in these alloys, tending to arrange parallelly into 
bundle structures. Further tensile simulations confirmed alloys 
with dislocation bundles had superior deformation properties, 
with bundle formation enhancing plastic deformation.
