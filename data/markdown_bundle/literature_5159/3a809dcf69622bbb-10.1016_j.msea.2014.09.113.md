abstract
Effect of aging treatment on the microstructure, creep resistance, and high temperature mechanical
properties of the Mg –6Zn–3Cu alloy (ZC63) with La- and Ce-rich rare earth additions was investigated by
impression creep and shear punch tests (SPT). Impression creep tests were performed in the
temperature range 423 –498 K and under punching stress in the range 150 –700 MPa for dwell times
up to 3600 s. The ultimate shear strength (USS) was measured by the SPT in the temperature range 298 –
498 K. The results showed that aging treatment increased the shear strength of the cast alloys, whereas
it had detrimental effects on the creep strength of materials. These observations were discussed on the
basis of the microstructural features and phase constituents of the investigated alloys in the as-cast and
aged conditions.

1. Introduction
Magnesium alloys are light materials that have recently received
a great deal of attention due to their excellent combination of
superior castability, good corrosion resistance and acceptable
mechanical properties. However, poor mechanical properties at
elevated temperatures, especially creep resistance, have restricted
their uses [1–3]. This has been attributed to the low thermal
stability of their microstructural constituents, which cannot with-
stand the imposed high temperatures [4–6].T oa d d r e s st h e s e
disadvantages, attempts have been made to develop new Mg base
alloys having structural stability at high temperatures. Among many
possibilities, alloys based on Mg –Zn–Cu system are of particular
interest. These alloys exhibit relatively high ductility and high
strength, due to the grain re ﬁnement caused by Cu and Zn [7,8].
Further needs for better performance of Mg –Zn–Cu alloys
necessitate the use of other alloying elements such as rare earth
(RE) elements, which can incorporate creep resistance and micro-
structural stability into the base Mg –Zn–Cu alloys. RE metals are
known for their positive effects on the high temperature proper-
ties of the Mg alloys. They combine with the Mg matrix and/or
other alloying elements in the alloy and form thermally stable
intermetallics [9–11 ]. The bene ﬁcial in ﬂuences of Ce-rich misch-
metal on the high temperature tensile behavior of the Mg –6Zn–
3Cu (ZC63) alloy have been examined [12]. It was demonstrated
that RE addition up to 1 wt%, signiﬁcantly increases high temperature
mechanical properties of the alloy by the formation of thermally
stable Mg 12RE and MgRE compounds [12]. Recently, the effect of
La- and Ce-rich misch-metal addition on the creep resistance[13]and
mechanical properties [14] of the cast ZC63 alloy has been investi-
gated. It has been shown that RE-containing alloys exhibit enhanced
creep resistance, mainly due to the formation of a grain boundary
network of thermally stable phases, which can effectively inhibit grain
boundary migration and sliding during the creep process [13].
In another work, it has been shown that addition of 2 wt% Ce-rich
misch-metal to a cast Mg –4Zn–0.5Ca alloy signi ﬁcantly improved
creep resistance by forming thermally stable (MgZn) 12RE and
Mg17RE2 particles [15]. A recent study showed that the choice of RE
elements does have signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the creep resistance of
die-cast Mg–RE alloys, with the Mg–Nd alloys being the most creep-
resistant, followed by the Mg –Ce and Mg –La alloys [16].I tw a s
demonstrated that when dislocation creep is the dominant creep
mechanism, the level of RE solute supersaturated in theα-Mg matrix
appears to be a key factor in ﬂuencing creep resistance. In another
study, it has been shown that small additions of Zn to Mg –Nd-based
alloys reduces the steady-state creep rate of these alloys by an order
of magnitude, due to formation of a high number density of
homogeneously distributed, highly reﬁned, precipitates within the
α-Mg matrix which are expected to impede dislocation motion [1 7].

based on the Mg –Zn system is age hardening. According to the
Mg–Zn binary phase diagram [18], the solubility of Zn in the α-Mg
decreases from 6.2 wt% at the eutectic transformation temperature
of 613 K to less than 1.6 wt% at room temperature. This means that

The effect of aging treatment on the microstructure, creep
behavior and high temperature mechanical properties of Mg –6Zn–
3Cu alloy with La- and Ce-rich rare earth additions has not been
studied before. Therefore, it is the aim of this work to study these
effects by means of the miniature testing techniques of impression
creep and shear punch tests. These tests have proved to yield
reliable data on the creep and mechanical behavior of materials
[20,21].
2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Materials and processing
The three alloys used in this investigation were based on ZC63
(Mg–6.0 wt% Zn –2.7 wt% Cu –0.4 wt% Mn) with separate additions
of 1 wt% RE (La) and 1 wt% RE (Ce). RE (La) and RE (Ce)
represented, respectively, the lanthanum-rich and cerium-rich
misch-metals, the chemical composition of which is given in
Table 1 . The details of melting, alloying, and casting processes
have been described elsewhere [14]. The cast slabs with the
dimensions of 12 mm /C2 20 mm /C2 180 mm were cut into slices of
3m m /C2 12 mm /C2 20 mm using an electrodischarge wire-cut
machine for structural characterization, impression creep and
mechanical testing.
To study the effect of precipitation hardening on the mechan-
ical behavior of the material, some of the samples were solution
treated at 713 K for 8 h in a resistance furnace under an argon
atmosphere, quenched in cold water followed by an arti ﬁcial aging
at 473 K for 16 h, in accordance with the recommended T6
treatment for the ZC63 alloy [22]. Both cast and aged samples
were studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to examine
the microstructure of alloys. The specimens were polished with
0.3-μm alumina paste, followed by polishing on an abrasive-free
microcloth. Etching was carried out using an acetic picral solution
(5 ml acetic acid þ6 g picric acid þ10 ml H 2Oþ100 ml ethanol) at

2.2. Impression creep tests
Impression creep tests were performed using an MTS universal
testing machine equipped with a three-zone split furnace to carry
out constant-load impression tests in the air atmosphere [23].
A ﬂat-ended cylindrical punch of 2 mm diameter was mounted in
a holder positioned in the center of the vertical loading bar. The
specimen was located on an anvil below the loading bar, and the
assembly of the specimen and indenter was accommodated by the
split furnace. The tests were performed in the temperature range
423–498 K, and under punch stress in the range 150 –700 MPa for
dwell times up to 3600 s. After application of the load, the
impression depth was measured automatically as a function of
time by the machine; the data were acquired by a computer. This
testing arrangement made it possible to control the load with an
accuracy of 7 1 N and impression depth recording resolution of
71 μm.
2.3. Shear punch tests
The strength of the investigated alloys was assessed by the
shear punch test (SPT). The 1 mm thick slices of the materials were
ground to a thickness of 0.7 mm, from which disks of 10 mm in
diameter were punched for the SPT. The details of the testing
arrangement are explained elsewhere [24] and will only be brie ﬂy
described here. All shear punch tests were performed in the
temperature range of 298 –498 K using a screw driven MTS
material testing system. Tests were run with a load cell of 20 kN
capacity and at a constant cross-head speed of 0.25 mm min /C0 1.
After application of the load, the applied load P was measured
automatically as a function of punch displacement; the data were
acquired by a computer so as to determine the shear stress of the
tested materials using the relationship

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural observations
Fig. 1 a–c shows the SEM microstructures of the investigated
alloys in the as-cast condition. As can be seen, both of the base
material and the RE-containing alloys exhibit dendritic cast
structures with interphases at interdendritic regions. It is notice-
able that the volume fraction of the secondary phases increases
with RE additions; the effect being more pronounced for the Ce-
rich RE addition, for which a grain boundary interphase network is
formed. It is also obvious that RE additions have effectively re ﬁned
the grain size of the base alloy. Average grain sizes of ZC63, ZC63 –
1RE (La) and ZC63 –1RE (Ce) alloys are about 93, 60, and 45 μm,
respectively. It seems that RE (Ce) has a stronger effect than RE (La)
on the grain re ﬁnement of the base alloy. The mechanism of grain
reﬁnement has been discussed in detail in our previous investiga-
tion [13]. The relatively high-magni ﬁcation SEM micrographs of
the as-cast alloys are shown in Fig. 1d–f. It is evident that the grain
boundary second phase has a lamellar eutectic morphology in
alloys ZC63 and ZC63 –1RE (La). According to EDX analysis, the
result of which is given in Table 2, this lamellar eutectic structure
shows the composition of Mg 37.4Zn55.2Cu7.4, which corresponds to
the Mg(Zn,Cu) 2 compound. In addition to this phase, another
Mg–Zn–Cu compound with a composition of Mg 54.9Zn38.8Cu6.3,
which represents the Mg(Zn,Cu) phase is also present in the form
of small particles. It is to be noted that in the ZC63 –1RE (Ce) alloy,
the morphology of this phase changes to an irregular bulky
eutectic ( Fig. 1 c and f) at grain boundaries, substituting most of
the lamellar eutectic structure. The bulky rare earth-containing
particles have the composition of Mg 89.6RE6.8Zn2.1Cu1.5, indicative
of the Mg 12RE compound precipitated as bright spots on the
eutectic structure at grain boundaries. In alloy ZC63 –1RE (Ce),
some sparse spherical Mg 57.2RE39.1Zn2.4Cu1.3 particles correspond-
ing to the MgRE intermetallic appear as isolated particles inside
the grains ( Fig. 1c and f).

based alloys, the precipitation behavior as well as the crystal
structure and composition of phases formed during aging have not

precipitates, XRD analyses were performed on specimens and the
results are shown in Fig. 3. As can be seen, α-Mg, Mg(Zn,Cu) and Mg

though these precipitates are unresolvable in SEM microstructures.
Examination of the XRD patterns for the RE-containing alloys shows
some new peaks corresponding to Mg 12RE and MgRE phases. It can
also be inferred that, the intensity of Mg 12RE and MgRE peaks is
increased by the aging process which is in accord with the SEM
observations. Therefore, it can be concluded that the main effect of
aging treatment is on the change of the microstructure of the cast
alloys from a continuous grain boundary interphase network
structure to a discontinuous precipitation along grain boundaries.

Comparing the effect of aging treatment on creep resistance
and shear strength of the investigated alloys, it is deduced that
while aging has signi ﬁcantly improved the shear strength of the
cast materials, it has no or even an adverse effect on creep
strength. Such contradictions between effects of aging treatment
on mechanical properties and creep resistance have been reported
in the literature. Wenwen et al. [29] investigated the tensile
properties and creep behavior of Mg –Al based alloys with Ca
and RE additions. They reported that aging treatment resulted in
signiﬁcant increase of strength, whereas the creep resistance of
the alloys in aged condition was reduced, as compared to the as-
cast condition. Detrimental effect of aging on creep resistance has
also been reported for an Mg –Al–Si (AS21) magnesium alloy
modiﬁed by RE addition, while the creep strength of the as-cast
materials were higher than the heat treated materials [30]. The
study of the qualitative model for creep of AZ91D magnesium alloy
has also indicated that aged specimens exhibited higher creep
rates than the as-cast specimens [31].
The observed differences in the creep and shear deformation
responses of the materials to aging treatment can be associated
with the microstructural features and phase constituents of the
investigated alloys. As mentioned earlier, aging treatment
increases the volume fractions of eutectic phases and thermally
stable Mg –RE compounds. Furthermore, the continuous grain
boundary network structure is fragmented, and discontinuous
participates are formed along grain boundaries. As published by
the authors elsewhere [14], the more continuous the grain
boundary network structure, the greater the achieved creep
strength for similar alloys. It is well established that continuous
grain boundary networks are effective in improving creep resis-
tance by inhibiting grain boundary migration and grain boundary
sliding during diffusional creep [32]. Accordingly, it can be con-
cluded that when this network structure is broken as a result of
heat treatment, the creep strength of the investigated alloys is

leading to an adverse effect on the shear strength of the material
[14]. However, discontinuous morphology of interphases along
grain boundaries and the higher volume fraction of the thermally
stable Mg –RE compounds might have both contributed to the
increase of strength after aging treatment.
4. Conclusions
Effect of aging treatment on the microstructure, creep behavior,
and high temperature mechanical properties of ZC63 alloy with
La- and Ce-rich rare earth additions was investigated. The
obtained conclusions are summarized as follows:
(1) Aging process increased the volume fractions of eutectic
phases and thermally stable Mg –RE compounds. Moreover,
the continuous grain boundary network structure was broken
and discontinuous participates were formed along grain
boundaries.
(2) The creep strength of the investigated alloys was deteriorated
as a result of heat treatment, mainly due to the fragmentation
of the grain boundary network structure of the cast materials
upon aging.
(3) Aging signi ﬁcantly improved the shear strength of the cast
materials, mainly due to the formation of discontinuous net-
work structure of the eutectic phases along grain boundaries
and the increment in the number density of thermally stable
Mg–RE compounds.
