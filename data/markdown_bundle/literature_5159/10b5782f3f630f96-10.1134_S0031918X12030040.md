† INTRODUCTION 
In conventionally applied alloys based on the
Fe⎯ Cr–Co system there occurs decomposition of
high-temperature homogeneous solid solution with
the formation of a modulated structure consisting of
α1 (Co-enriched) and α2 (Cr-enriched) regions.
Hereinafter, for the brevity sake we will call these
regions the α1 and α2 phases. The decomposition is
accompanied by alloy strengthening, however, the
strength level reached is not high; for instance, the
yield stress and the ultimate strength of the well-
known alloy Arnokrome III (Arnold Magnetics T ech-
nologies) are not higher than 800 MPa.
In [1] the alloys based on the Fe–Cr–Co system
were studied, which are characterized by high strength
and required reserve of plasticity in order to prevent
brittle character of fracture. The strengthening is
achieved by alloying with tungsten and by cold defor-
mation preceding precipitation hardening. It has been
demonstrated that the strengthening of alloys with
22% Cr and 15% Co strongly depends on the degree of
cold deformation with a maximum near 60% both
after deformation and, particularly , after aging. It has
been established [2], that in cold-rolled specimens of
these alloys the decomposition into 
α1 and α2 phases
occurs without the formation of a modulated structure
and that the process proceeds deeper in comparison
with the specimens aged directly after quenching from
the region of homogeneous solid solution. Moreover,
in these alloys there is readily formed a disperse
η' phase enriched in tungsten, whereas its formation
during alloy aging directly after quenching from

strengthening [3].
High strength properties are also characteristic of
the Fe–Cr–Co–W– Ga alloys, in which Cr and Co
contents are lower than the boundary of the miscibility
gap for the 
α solid solution (12–20% Cr and 12–
14% Co) [4]. In these alloys in the course of postdefor-
mationl low-temperature annealing there occurs a
decomposition of the homogeneous solid solution
with the precipitation of disperse particles of the
η' phase. The content of the weakly magnetic α2 phase
decreases sharply with decreasing chromium content
in the alloy .
Since the cold deformation, which precedes aging,
influences the alloy strengthening, it was of interest to
study its role in the creation of the high-strength state.
T o this end, in this work we studied the properties and
structure of alloys Fe–15Cr–13Co–8W–0.5Ga and
Fe–22Cr–15Co–9W–0.5Ga after treatments result-
ing in high strength.

achieve the high-strength state in alloys with different contents of chromium and cobalt.

2. Results of X-ray Diffraction Studies
Figure 1 displays X-ray diffraction patterns
obtained on (a) the specimens of the Fe–22Gr–
15Co–9W–0.5Ga alloy aged after reduction to 60%,
and specimens of the Fe–15Cr–13Co–8W–0.5Ga
alloy aged after cold rolling to (b) 60 and (c) 80%. The
X-ray diffraction patterns contain peaks of one
α phase and weak peaks of the tungsten-rich η' phase
marked with arrows. A similar situation was observed
earlier for the alloys containing 
α1 and α2 phases in
[2], where it was shown that both these phases are
inhomogeneous in terms of composition, which leads
to a spread of the magnitudes of the lattice parameters,
which in turn leads to a broadening of diffraction lines.
Since the difference in the lattice parameters of the
α1-(Fe–Co) and α2-(Fe–Cr) phases is not large, the
peaks in the diffraction patterns are not resolved but
merge into one broad peak. The (110) peaks of the
α phase are weak, whereas the (200) peaks are rather
intense. This means that the specimens after cold roll-
ing are strongly texturized (planes {100} of the α phase
are parallel to the rolling plane), and the degree of tex-
ture slightly increases with increasing degree of defor-
mation. The diffraction pattern (a) contains traces of
the (111) peak of fcc Fe, and the diffraction patterns
(b, c) are quite similar.

The particles of the

obstacles for the motion of dislocations and contribute
to the value

alloy . The high difference in the strength properties of
the two alloys after equal deformation by 60% and
aging can be attributed to the influence of the signifi-
cantly larger amount of the 
α2 phase in the high-chro-
mium alloy . The main factors that determine the
deceleration of moving dislocations in alloys with
coherent precipitates are summarized in [9]. In the
alloy under consideration, the moving dislocations,
probably , can interact with the field of elastic stresses
that exist around the precipitates of the 
α2 phase,
which presumably are higher, the higher the difference
in the lattice parameters of the adjacent regions. An
additional elastic interaction between the dislocation
and the precipitate can be related to the difference in
the elasticity moduli of the matrix and the deposition.
The data given in [10] show that elastic moduli in pure
chromium are higher than in pure iron by a factor of
1.5; it can be assumed that the 
α2 phase, which is
strongly enriched in chromium, also has higher values
of the elastic moduli than the 
α1 phase. The strengthen-
ing due to these factors can be significant. For instance,
in single crystals of the Fe–28Cr–10Co–2Mo alloy the
critical shear stress for slip increases from 260 to 550–
600 MPa upon the alloy separation into the 
α1 and
α2 phases [11]. Presumably , the retardation of disloca-
tions by precipitates of the α2 phase is also responsible
for the value of the yield stress which has a value of
about 800 MPa in the aforementioned alloy Arnok-
rome 111. The low content of the 
α2phase in our low-
chromium alloy , probably , provides only insignificant
contribution to the alloy strengthening.
In [2] it has been established that cold rolling
before aging leads to a more deep decomposition into
the 
α1 and α2phases in comparison with the specimens
aged directly after quenching from the homogeneous
solid solution, that is, promotes increase in the differ-
ence of compositions and lattice parameters of the
phases. As a consequence, deformation is also favor-
able for increasing alloy strength. In particular, Kir-
illov [11] showed that an increase in the degree of sep-
aration in the spinodal alloy Fe–28Cr–10Co–2Mo
leads to an increase in the critical shear stress for slip.
In order to significantly increase the strength prop-
erties of the low-chromium alloy , it is required to
increase the strain before aging to 70–80%; at that,
there occurs a simultaneous decrease in the relative
elongation. Meanwhile, the phase composition in the
specimens of this alloy aged after reduction to 60 or
80% is the same, according to the data of X-ray dif-
fraction and Mössbauer studies: the specimens are
three-phase (
α1 + α2 + η'), and there are no grounds to
assume that the compositions of the phase differ sig-
nificantly .

CONCLUSIONS 
Therefore, the strengthening of the alloys consid-
ered is affected by the existence of disperse precipitates
of the 
η' phase, α1 and α2 phases, subgrain boundaries,
and, probably , lattice defects. Cold plastic deforma-
tion preceding low-temperature aging, affects all these
structural factors; it promotes precipitation of the
η' phase, leads to a division of matrix grains into sub-
grains, creates high amounts of defects.
The contents of the α1 and α2 phases is determined
by the alloy composition; in the high-chromium alloy
the content of the α2 phase is three times higher. This
provides sufficiently high strength properties of this
alloy . Due to a low content of the 
α2 phase in the low-
chromium alloy , its yield stress after reduction to 60%
and aging is not high; it is therefore required to create
additional barriers for the deceleration of moving dis-
locations, such as, e.g., subgrain boundaries, which
can be created in high amount as a result of increase in
the degree of cold deformation to 70–80%.
Deformation preceding aging promotes deeper
decomposition into the 
α1 and α2 phases (i.e., leads to
an increase in the difference in the compositions and
lattice parameters of the phases). This is also favorable
for an increase in the alloy strength.
