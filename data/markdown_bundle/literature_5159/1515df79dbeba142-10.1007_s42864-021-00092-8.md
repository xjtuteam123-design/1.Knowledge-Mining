Abstract
The extreme environment in a fusion reactor, namely high thermal load and intense energetic particles, requires the mate-
rials to possess high strength and good ductility at high temperature in combination with excellent radiation resistance. 
Conventional metal tungsten (W) and its alloy cannot satisfy these rigorous requirements, but the discovery of the W-based 
high-entropy alloys (HEAs) with outstanding properties sheds light on the developments of structural materials. Unique 
properties of some of these alloys make them promising candidates for engineering applications in fusion reactor beyond 
conventional W and its alloys. In particular, their strengthening-toughening mechanism has also aroused wide concern. 
Here, the design, microstructure, mechanical properties and irradiation performance of W-based HEAs are reviewed, and 
their future prospects are outlined.

1 Introduction
Key components in fusion reactors, such as the diver -
tor or the plasma-facing materials (PFMs), must oper -
ate at high temperature (≥ 1000 K) for long durations 
(≥ 107 s), but without failure or extensive erosion while 
exposed to large plasma heat and an intense mixture of 
ionized and energetic neutral species of hydrogen isotopes 
(D and T), helium (He) ash (fluxes > 1024  m−2·s−1) and 
neutrons [1 ]. Therefore, they are required to have out-
standing properties including low activation, high melting 
point, good thermomechanical properties, low sputter ero-
sion, and lower tritium retention/codeposition. To realize 
these high performances, innovative candidate materials 
including C, Be, and W for PFM have been developed 
for many years: Be for the first wall of the main reac-
tor chamber and W for the divertor area. However, there 
exists Be depletion, and also He nanobubble formation, 
which strongly affects deuterium retention and release 
when exposed to D-He plasma [2 , 3]. W metal is utilized 
as the primary PFM in the International Thermonuclear 
Experimental Reactor (ITER) divertor and a full W first 
wall has been proposed to the next-generation devices 
such as China Fusion Engineering Test Reactor (CFETR) 
due to its high thermal conductivity, high melting point, 
low tritium retention, and low erosion rate [4 ].
Although W metal is one of the innovative candidates 
owing to its unique properties above, several issues should 
be addressed to its further engineering application. The most 
important one is its low fracture toughness characterized by 
brittle transgranular and intergranular failure regimes, which 
severely restrict the useful operating temperature window 
and also create a range of fabrication difficulties [2]. Another 
crucial issue is blistering at moderate temperature (< 800 K) 
by D and He and the formation of pits, holes, and bubbles 
by He at higher temperature (≥ 1600 K) [1]. The formation 
mechanisms that underpin these phenomena are not well 
understood, but is largely attributed to the accumulation of 
diffusing D and He in extended defects. Also, at the slightly 
low temperature ranging from 1250 to 1600 K, the formation 
of nanometer-scale bubbles is observed in W exposed to the 
He plasma. Importantly, at larger He ion fluences, which 
are close to ITER working conditions, exposed surfaces

Many efforts have been undertaken to design novel 
W-based materials, such as W–X alloys (X = Re, K, Ta, V, 
Cr, Mo, Ti) and dispersion strengthened W-based materi-
als [5, 6]. They show better irradiation resistances than W 
metal due to the fine grain boundaries and interfaces acting 
as sinks for the radiation-induced defects. For example, the 
addition of potassium in W brings in nanosized potassium 
bubble to pin grain boundary and dislocation, resulting in 
improved toughness and strength simultaneously [2 ]. Dis-
persion strengthened W-based materials, such as W–TiC 
and W–Y2O3 with the particle addition (0.2–2.0 wt%) have 
been produced with enhancement in hardness and stabil-
ity but no decrease in thermal conductivity using standard 
powder metallurgy (PM) method and the spark plasma 
sintering (SPS) method [6 ]. However, detailed analyses of 
conventional binary alloys revealed several constraints as 
well [5]. For example, some W-based binary alloys showed 
brittle behavior and certain alloys, such as W–Re and W–Os 
underwent irradiation-induced embrittlement. The presence 
of metastable phases has also been found to deteriorate the 
mechanical properties of W–Ti and W–V binary alloys.
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) provide a transformative 
opportunity to design materials that are custom-tailored to 
the distinct needs of a given application, thereby shifting 
the paradigm “from applying the material you have to engi-
neer the material you need” [7 ]. Refractory HEAs (RHEA) 
were first introduced in 2010 and immediately attracted great 
attention due to their ability to retain high strength up to 
1873 K [8]. The first two RHEAs are based on five refrac-
tory elements (Mo, Nb, Ta, V, and W), but subsequent alloys 
have been drawn from a broader palette of nine elements in 
Group IV (Ti, Zr, and Hf), Group V (V, Nb, and Ta), and 
Group VI (Cr, Mo, and W), sometimes with additions of 
nonrefractory metals such as Al, Si, Co and Ni. In 2018, 
Lu et al. [ 9] reported a new single-phase body-centered 
cubic (BCC) structured Ti2ZrHfV0.5Mo0.2 RHEA possess-
ing excellent irradiation resistance, i.e., negligible amounts 
of irradiation hardening and abnormal lattice constant 
reduction after helium-ion irradiation, which is completely 
different from conventional alloys. This is the first time to 
report the abnormal X-ray diffraction (XRD) phenomenon 
of metallic alloys with almost no hardening after irradia-
tion. Very recently, a BCC-type WTaCrV RHEA with neg-
ligible irradiation hardening was developed by Los Alamos 
National Laboratory [1 ]. These excellent properties make 
W-based HEAs promising candidates as core components 
in the next-generation nuclear reactors.
In this work, we compiled ca. 129 RHEA, 156 eutec-
tic/near-eutectic alloys, W-based disordered solid solu-
tion HEAs (WDSSHEAs, 39) and W-based eutectic HEAs 
(WEHEAs, 16) published in open literature since 2010 
[10–35] (see Table 1). First, we introduce the design method 
and the microstructure of WDSSHEA and WEHEA, then 
review the recent developments on the mechanical properties 
and irradiation performance, and finally point to the future 
trends.

2.1 Design of WDSSHEA
Since the discovery of HEA in 2004, many efforts have been 
put forward on the rational design of disordered HEA with 
a single phase (face-centered cubic (FCC) or BCC). Hume-
Rothery (H-R) criterion is an important theoretical basis in 
physical metallurgy. It describes the effects of atom size, 
crystal structure, valence electron concentration (VEC), and 
electronegativity on the phase formation [36]. Zhang et al. 
[37] extended the H-R to the HEA, and proposed that it 
was the difference in the atomic radius (δ), mixing enthalpy 
(∆Hmix), and mixing entropy (∆Smix) that affected the phase 
formation of HEAs. Based on these basic thermodynamic 
parameters, many criteria have been proposed [38–41]. For 
example, Zhang et al. [37] proposed that the disordered HEA

2.2 Design of WEHEA

3 Microstructure of WDSSHEA and WEHEA
3.1 Typical solidification microstructure 
of WDSSHEA
The typical solidification microstructure of HEA is dendrite 
[53], cellular structure [54] and eutectic structure [55]. Most 
of the WDSSHEAs are BCC-type solid solutions and/or 
BCC + IM, while the others are FCC-type solid solutions 
[29] and their mixture, e.g. WFeNiMo (BCC + FCC + µ) 
[56]. The microstructure is determined by the synergy and 
competitive effect of thermal undercooling and constitu-
tional undercooling. The effect of thermal undercooling 
on solidification reveals that the primary phase morphol-
ogy changes from dendrite to columnar dendrite then to 
dendrite + grain morphology with respect to an increase in 
undercooling [30]. The other is constitutional undercooling, 
caused by chemical composition, which leads to the for -
mation of dendritic structures. The stronger the undercool-
ing effect is, the larger the primary dendrite is. Therefore, 
the change of undercooling effect through varying chemi-
cal composition is an important method to tune the micro-
structure. For example, the increase in the Cr concentration 
will lead to the transition from cellular structure to dendrite 
structure [54].
In general, there exists element segregation in as-cast 
HEAs with a heavy element rich in dendrite arms (da) and 
lighter elements rich in interdendritic regions (idr). The 
difference in melting temperature between constituents 
encourages W to solidify first in the da, ultimately forcing 
lighter elements, such as Mo, V, and Nb into the idr. The 
observed micro-segregation of the constituents is a mani-
festation of a non-equilibrium solidification of the alloys 
within the temperature range between the liquidus and soli-
dus temperatures, so that homogeneous distribution of the 
alloying elements in the growing solid phases is kinetically 
restricted. The level of micro-segregation of the alloying 
elements is quantitatively described by a partition coefficient 
[57], K = Cda/Cidr, where C da is the average concentrations 
in the centers of dendrite arms and C idr is the average con-
centrations in interdendritic regions. Electron probe micro-
analysis (EPMA) evidences that the K of Nb, Mo and V is 
less than 1.0 and the K of Ta is nearly 1.0, but the K of W is 
greater than 1.0 (1.3–1.82), as shown in Fig. 3. It should be 
noted that the K is sensitive to the alloy system. The level 
of the element segregation increases with an expansion of 
the liquidus–solidus temperature range, which is generally 
associated with an increase in the difference in the melting 
temperature of the constituent elements, and an increase in 
the solidification rate. It can be seen that the segregation 
of an element i increases with an increase in the difference

3.2 Microstructure of WEHEA
Casting of HEAs, particularly at a kilogram scale is quite 
often a challenge since most HEAs do not possess good 
castability and exhibit considerable chemical inhomoge-
neity in directly cast alloys, which retard their industrial 
applications. Interestingly, eutectic alloys are the most 
widely used casting alloys in industries due to the intrinsic 
two main features [58]: near-equilibrium microstructure 
and good castability. Moreover, the eutectic reaction is an 
isothermal transformation without a solidification-temper -
ature range; thus, both the segregation and shrinkage cav -
ity can be alleviated. EHEAs, as a sub-group of HEAs, are 
becoming a new research hotspot in the metallic materials 
community due to their similar features to eutectic alloys. 
Some of the EHEAs have shown promising potentials 
for industrial applications [58– 60]. In fact, the eutectic 
growth mechanisms are also affected by the undercooling 
effect, but it is much more complicated, which involves 
the kinetic undercooling, curvature undercooling, consti-
tutional undercooling, and thermal undercooling. Their 
detailed analyses are beyond the scope of this work, which 
can be found in ref [10].
Lu et al. [59] first proposed to use the eutectic alloy con-
cept to design HEAs, aiming at good castability of HEAs. 
Then they proposed to design the eutectic alloys with a mix-
ture of soft fcc and hard bcc phases, to achieve the balance 
of the high fracture strength and great ductility. In 2019, 
they directly casted a 2.5 kg CoCrFeNiNb0.45 EHEA [12]. 
Since then, more and more researchers have been attracted 
by the advantages of EHEAs and their great potential in 
industrial applications, and have dedicated to the research on 
EHEAs [58]. EHEAs exhibit fine and uniform regular lamel-
lar microstructures with lamella widths ranging from a few 
microns to tens of nanometer. In general, EHEAs composed 
of BCC/B2 or BCC/IM phase are brittle and have no tensile 
plasticity, while some EHEAs composed of soft and hard 
phases, like FCC/BCC (or B2), show excellent mechanical 
properties.
As aforementioned, only two WEHEA systems, i.e. 
 Co2MoxNi2VWx (x = 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.5, and 1.75) and 
 Ni30Co30Cr10Fe10Al18W2 have been reported. The micro-
structure of the former consists of a primary BCC solid solu-
tion phase plus eutectic microstructure, which is a mixture 
of FCC solid solution phase and µ phase, as shown in Fig. 4, 
while that of latter is a mixture of FCC- and BCC-type solid 
solution, as shown in Fig.  5. The Co2MoxNi2VWx with 
x = 0.5 and 0.6 alloys exhibited a typical near-eutectic micro-
structure, where the eutectic phase showed a lamellar mor -
phology, while a fully eutectic microstructure was obtained 
for x = 0.8. With further increasing of Mo and W contents 
(x ≥ 1.0), the microstructure changed to a primary BCC 
phase plus secondary phase and eutectic microstructure for 
x = 1.0 and 1.5, and finally to a primary phase plus eutectic 
microstructure for the x = 1.75. The Ni30Co30Cr10Fe10Al18W2 
alloy showed a hierarchical microstructure with fine regu-
lar lamellar eutectic and coarse irregular eutectic. Further 
characterization by high-resolution transmission electron 
microscope (HR-TEM) revealed that the interface between 
FCC and B2 phase was rough at atomic scale, which was the 
typical feature of non-faceted eutectic growth.

4.1 Strengthening–toughening mechanism 
in W‑based HEAs
The understanding of the strengthening–toughening mech-
anism is important to design W-based HEAs with high 
strength and good ductility used for the fusion reactor. The 
general mechanisms include solid solution strengthen-
ing–toughening [63], grain refinement [62], (namely grain 
boundary strengthening governed by the Hall−Petch rela-
tionship), interstitial strengthening (O [64], N [65], B [66]), 
secondary phases strengthening (namely, Orowan strength-
ening), twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) [67], transfor -
mation-induced plasticity (TRIP) [68, 69], eutectic micro-
structure [70] and heat treatment [71]. Unlike the traditional 
alloys, these mechanisms are more pronounced in HEA due 
to the significant chemical inhomogeneity and severe lattice 
distortion. For example, the ordered O interstitial complexes 
lead to the unprecedented enhancement of both strength and 
ductility in TiZrHfNb HEA. The tensile strength is enhanced 
(by ca. 48%) and ductility is substantially improved (by ca. 
95%) by doping with 2.0 atomic percent oxygen. Here it 
should be noted that only several mechanisms, namely solid 
solution strengthening, grain boundary strengthening, sec-
ondary phase strengthening, eutectic microstructure and 
TWIP, have been reported on W-based HEAs. In the follow-
ing part, we will review strengthening–toughening mecha -
nisms. First of all, we introduce the solid solution strength-
ening–toughening. The typical BCC-type W-based HEA is

Ti will increase the room temperature ductility of the HEAs

TaW HEA, and the yield strength increases from 996 MPa of 
the NbMoTaW HEA to 1455 MPa of the TiNbMoTaW HEA 
[62, 71]. This is attributed to the segregation of ductile ele-
ments such as Ti and Nb at the inter-dendrite regions, which 
favors the accommodation of the increased strain. Here it is 
worth noting that strengthening in HEAs is associated with

distance of W and impede the slip. Although first-principles 
calculations reveal the effect of alloying elements on GSFE, 
no experiment has confirmed these findings.

5 Irradiation resistance of W and WDSSHEA
W in a fusion reactor will be exposed to a neutron damage 
of hundreds of displacement per atom (dpa) for an antici-
pated 3–5 year lifetime, which can result in lattice defects, 
namely, point defect, dislocation loop rafting and dislocation 
decoration [76]. These lattice defects will further cause the 
mechanical, thermal properties degradation, the diffusion 
and retention of hydrogen and its isotopes. Understanding 
the fundamental aspects of these defects and their effects on 
properties assists in evaluating irradiation damage of nuclear 
materials. However, most of the evaluations rely on TEM 
observations and hardness tests after irradiation.
Atwani et al. [76] examined the dislocation raft forma-
tion in W irradiated with different dpa rates at room tem-
perature (RT) and high temperature (1050 K) to different 
doses. Their observations confirmed the mechanism previ-
ously suggested by Wen et al. [77], which explained raft 
formation due to loop glide, cluster–cluster and grown-in 
dislocation cluster interaction with subsequent Burgers 
vector rotation. Similar irradiation studies on nanocrystal-
line W showed that these materials were more resistant to 
raft formation at RT irradiations [76], as shown in Fig.  8. 
Wei et al. [78, 79] reported enhanced ductility in ultrafine 
(100–500 nm) and nanocrystalline (ca. 100 nm) W based on 
compression tests. To further assess the mechanical proper-
ties of nanocrystalline tungsten before and after irradiation, 
nanoindentation, micropillar compression and cantilever 
bending tests were performed on nanocrystalline tungsten 
films (grain size less than 35 nm) prior to and post-irradia-
tion at high temperature (1050 K) with heavy ions [80]. The 
results demonstrate an increased change in area (enhanced 
ductility under compressive stresses) when compared with 
large-grained tungsten in the compression tests. However, 
the force–displacement curves generated from the cantilever 
tests showed brittle fracture of nanocrystalline tungsten. No 
differences were observed between the unirradiated and the 
irradiated nanocrystalline W. It is evident that compression-
only tests do not provide a true judgement about the ductility 
of the samples.
The improved irradiation tolerance in both FCC and BCC 
HEAs has been observed [80–84]. The chemical complexity 
in HEAs can lead to a variation in the thermodynamic and 
kinetic properties of defects that might modify the micro-
structure evolution under irradiation [80]. The amount of 
irradiation-induced defects and defect properties is related 
to electron and phonon mean free paths and dissipation 
mechanisms that could be tuned in these alloys by varying 
their composition. Also, the sluggish mobility of dislocation 
loops is identified as the main mechanism leading to irra-
diation tolerance of Ni-based FCC HEAs [81]. Theoretical 
calculations reveal that the equilibrium vacancy concentra-
tions of HEAs are larger than that in pure metals and simple 
binary alloys [85]. Hence, it seems that the original existed 
high concentration of vacancy defects in HEA can effec-
tively capture the helium atoms, limiting thus the formation 
of intergranular helium bubbles and restraining the irradi-
ation-induced embrittlement, as shown in Fig.  9, which is

Here it should be mentioned that among the numer -
ous reported methods of fabricating HEAs, most HEAs 
have been made by arc melting (AM). However, this route 
is suitable only at the lab-scale because of the very high 
melting temperatures of the constituents. Additionally, 
further heat treatment is necessary to homogenize the 
microstructure. However, powder metallurgical process 
of mechanical alloying (MA) process can readily fabri-
cate HEA powders having homogeneous morphologies 
using simple and precise composition control. A subse-
quent consolidation process using SPS is typically used 
to make bulk high-density HEAs, which can provide 
ultrafine grains with excellent microstructural homogene-
ity and improved strength and hardness compared with 
those of HEAs fabricated by AM [86]. During the MA 
process, the alloy elements will dissolve into the matrix. 
It shows that the alloying sequence of the constituents is 
related to the melting point of the component elements; an 
element with a lower melting point has a higher intrinsic 
diffusion coefficient in the solid state. Generally, an ele-
ment with a higher melting point would possess stronger 
atomic bonding, leading to a lower diffusion coefficient. 
When the alloy components have similar melting points, 
such as Mo and Nb, the alloying rate is proportional to the 
brittleness of the pure elements. Here it should be noted 
that during the MA as the milling time is increased, the 
alloy behavior continuously changes from polycrystalline 
to nanocrystalline and ultimately to amorphous [87]. If 
the processing parameters are not well controlled, there 
will be incomplete alloying, resulting in deterioration of 
irradiation resistance. Irradiation tests reveal that the inter -
face between the incomplete alloying and the matrix is 
very weak, which will cause the swelling of the HEAs, as 
shown in Fig.  10.
6 Summary and prospects
W-based HEAs are based on the multi-principal-element 
concept, and the study of which fills in the blanks of the 
total HEAs research, such as some fundamental under -
standings. In this work, we review the design, microstruc-
ture, mechanical properties and irradiation performance 
of WDSSHEA and WEHEA, in particular the strengthen-
ing–toughening mechanisms. Compared with traditional W 
metal and W-based alloys, the microstructure and mechan-
ical properties of W-based HEA can be ameliorated in a 
wide range, although only a few strengthening–toughening 
mechanisms have been reported. However, this field is still 
in its incipient stage, and there are still some problems and 
opportunities involved in further research, in particular for 
the engineering application in the fusion field due to the 
more rigorous requirements in DEMO than that in ITER 
[88]. We hope this communication may be beneficial to 
guide the main researches and development directions of 
W-based PFMs for future fusion facilities.
The first and most important is the high-throughput 
design of alloys with multiple targets based on data mining 
in combination with machine learning. To date, the high-
throughput design of alloys with target property-based 
machine learning method has been successfully evidenced 
on BMGs [42], optimization of thermal conductivity of 
 UO2-based composites and eutectic alloy design based 
on big data [89]. Although the W-based HEA shows a 
good combination of mechanical properties and irradiation 
resistance, it is still a great challenge to synthesis W-based 
HEA with multiple properties, such as high thermal con-
ductivity and high irradiation resistance, which may be 
resolved by W-based EHEA in combination with the state-
of-the-art machine learning technique. Recently, Zhang 
et al. [90] have tried multi-task learning (MTL), aiming 
to improve the performance of multiple related learning 
tasks, which probably provide an effective way to predicate 
multi-targets of the design of WDSSHEAs and WEHEAs.
Second, the thermal conductivities of the HEAs are 
much lower than those of their as-cast bulk states due 
to the strong phonon- and electron-point defect scatter -
ing by point defects and boundaries [91]. Recently, Reza 
et al. [92] measured the thermal diffusivity of tungsten 
exposed to different levels of 20 meV self-ion irradiation. 
They found that low 3.2 × 10−4 dpa will cause a measur -
able reduction in thermal diffusivity, while higher doses 
of 0.1 dpa and above, up to 10 dpa, will further deteriorate 
the thermal diffusivity by 55% from the pristine value at 
room temperature. We have tested several W-based HEAs 
prepared by HIP, such as WVCr (20.2 W·m−1·K−1), WVTa 
(32.4 W·m −1·K−1), WVCrRe (33.0 W·m −1·K−1), WVTiTa 
(31.9 W·m −1·K−1) and WVTiTaRe (21.1 W·m −1·K−1), of 
which the thermal conductivities are only about 1/9–1/6 
of W (174 W·m −1·K−1). So the adjustment of components 
of the new W-based HEAs is required to consider thermal 
conductivity besides phase stability, mechanical property 
and irradiation resistance.

Academy of Engineering Physics 
with a major in Nuclear Fuel 
Cycle and Materials. From 2016 
to 2017, he worked as a post-
doctor at the Royal Institute of 
Technology in Sweden, with his 
co-supervisor Levente Vitos. His 
research currently focuses on the 
design of fusion/fission struc-
tural and functional materials, 
serves as the chief of the ITER 
talent project of the Ministry of 
Science and Technology, and 
presides one innovation fund of 
the Chinese Academy of Engineering Physics.
