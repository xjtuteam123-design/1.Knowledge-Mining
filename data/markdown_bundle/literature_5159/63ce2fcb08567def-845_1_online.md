ABSTRACT
The mechanical properties comprising the stress-strain characteristics under uniaxial tensile deformation, the acoustic properties from
mechanical resonance spectroscopy and — in parallel — the microstructural evolution during deformation of a nonequiatomic high-entropy
alloy (HEA) Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10 have been studied in a wide temperature range, including ultralow temperatures down to 0.5 K. In the tem-
perature range 300 to 4.2 K, a strong temperature dependence of the tensile strength occurs, hinting at the thermally activated nature of
plastic deformation. Within the range of extremely low temperatures (4.2 –0.5 K), however, the alloy exhibits anomalies of the yield strength,
as well as discontinuous plasticity. Over the whole temperature range, the dynamic Young ’s modulus of tensile deformed samples shows a
reduction of absolute values compared to those of the undeformed ones, and at temperatures < 30 K a change of the temperature depen-
dence from almost linear to power-law type. At all temperatures down to 0.5 K, the alloy ’s plasticity stays as high as 50% as a consequence
of a deformation driven martensitic phase transformation from fcc to hcp lattice (TRIP effect). Considering the ultralow deformation tem-
peratures, the tensile strength reaches record values of 1513 MPa at 4.2 K, and still of 1274 MPa at 0.5 K, each being paired with significant
strain hardening. These results suggest the HEA Fe
50Mn30Co10Cr10 as a promising structural material for use in cryogenic environments
down to extremely low temperatures.

1. INTRODUCTION
The use of metals and alloys at cryogenic temperatures has
significant limitations due to the phenomenon of low-temperature
brittleness. This reduces the number of alloys suitable for cryogenic
applications such as materials for space and nuclear technology,
for superconducting devices, nuclear reactors, aircraft construction,
for liquid storage tanks and others. At present, traditional alloys are
used for these purposes, e.g., fcc steel 12X18H10T, which has suffi-
ciently high strength and plasticity at low temperatures due to large
deformation hardening. However, the number of such alloys, which
at cryogenic temperatures reach strengths around 1 GPa and high
plasticity, is extremely small.
1,2 Therefore, the emergence of a
fundamentally new class of materials, multi-element high entropy
alloys (HEA)3,4 with adjustable structure and improved physicome-
chanical properties, is highly welcome. Due to the large number of
different elements in equiatomic concentration, HEAs exhibit a high
entropy of mixing and reduced Gibbs free energy. In most cases, the
structures of HEAs correspond to simple crystalline lattices: face-
centered cubic (fcc), body-centered cubic (bcc), hexagonal-packed
(hcp), which helps to improve their physico-mechanical properties
compared to traditional alloys.
In recent years, however, studies have shown that the maximum
configuration entropy is not the only factor that causes the improved
mechanical properties of the HEAs.
5–11 Multicomponent alloys were
developed with structures connected with nonequiatomic ratio of ele-
ments, as well as HEAs with numbers of elements less than five

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS OF RESEARCH
Nonequiatomic high-entropy alloy Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10 (mass
percents: Fe = 50.31458, Mn = 29.69738, Co = 10.61901, Cr = 9.36903)
was obtained by arc melting of elements at low pressure in a high
purity argon atmosphere inside the cooled water copper plate.
The purity of the alloying elements was above 99.9 at. %. After that
the alloy was cast in rectangular mold size of 5 × 12 × 50 mm and
annealed in pure argon at T = 1000 °C during 24 hours. The forma-
tion of the initial structural state in the samples for further tensile
tests consisted of the following: a) the ingot was rolled to a thick-
ness of ≈ 0.4 mm at room temperature with a reduction of 10 –15%
in one pass; b) after deformation ≈ 50% intermediate annealing
was carried out in an argon atmosphere at 1000 °C for 1 hour.
Samples for tensile tests with size of 16 × 3 × 0.4 mm were cut by an
electric spark machine, after that they were grinded and polished.
The final annealing of the samples was carried out at 1000 ° С
for 10 min in a quartz ampoule filled with pure argon, followed
by air cooling. The samples were deformed by uniaxial tension in
the temperature range 0.5 –300 K with a strain rate of 1 ⋅10
−4 s−1.
Liquid He 3 (T = 0.5 K), liquid He 4 (T = 4.2 K) and liquid N 2
(T = 77 K) were used to obtain cryogenic temperatures.
Intermediate temperatures in the range of 77 –300 K were obtained
by cooling the samples with N 2 vapor, and 4.2–77 K with He 4 vapor.
Phase composition and microstructure of the alloy were studied
by x-ray diffraction analysis (using a Bruker D8 Discover diffractometer
with monochromatic Cu-K
α and Co-Kα radiation) and electron micros-
copy (using a scanning electron microscope Zeiss Supra 55 VP with
attachment for electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) analysis —
Oxford Instruments). The size of diffraction area was 0.5 × 0.5 mm.
The place for obtaining diffraction patterns and electron microscopic
observations for samples in the initial state was chosen in the center of
the gauge length of the samples, andfor deformed samples at a distance
of 1 mm from the neck of the destroyed samples.
Acoustic studies were performed by mechanical resonance spec-
troscopy method. This method is based on the study of linear bending
oscillations of a cantilever sample — a thin plate operated at small

3.2. Mechanical characteristics of the alloy in the
temperature range 300–0.5 K
The engineering stress-strain curves and true strain hardening
curves are shown in Figs. 3(a) and 3(b). The hardening rate curves
are calculated from the corresponding stress-strain curves Fig. 3(b).
It can be seen [ Fig. 3(a) ] that with a temperature decrease
from 300 to 4.2 K, a significant increase in both the yield strength
σ
0.2 and ultimate strength σU occurs, which is typical of thermally
activated plastic deformation. 37–40 So, at 2 K, σ0.2 equals 606 MPa,
and the ultimate strength σU reaches 1513 MPa, in which both
values are almost 3 times higher than the corresponding values
measured at 295 K.
At the same time, the plasticity of the alloy over the entire tem-
perature range remains at a high level ∼ 50% engineering strain, and
at T = 0.5 K it increases to 54% engineering strain. As seen from
Fig. 3(b), with all temperatures, the deformation curves have a staged
character. At the first deformation stage, a strong decrease of strain
hardening rate θ is observed, which is associated with (i) the gradual
involvement of grains in the process of plastic deformation, due
to their different initial orientation, and (ii) due the inhomoge-
neous distribution of internal stresses as sources of nucleation of
dislocations.
41,42
In the second stage, when the strain (stress) increases, the σ–ε
curves have a region with a weaker θ(ε) dependence. This stage in
this HEA, as in other classic fcc alloys, 43 is typically characterized
by linear strain hardening, which corresponds to nearly constant
value of θ. In HEAs the deformation at this stage is explained
by many hardening mechanisms, including solid solution
hardening, grain boundary hardening, and hardening due to
deformation-induced phase transitions.
16,43,44
As seen in Fig. 4 within the temperature interval between 4.2
and 0.5 K, the thermally activated character of the temperature
dependence of the yield strength appears to be violated, by showing
an anomalous decrease from 606 to 536 MPa. A similar anomaly of
the σ
0.2(T) dependence was observed earlier, both in a number of
traditional fcc alloys, 45,46 and in some HEAs. 27,47

To explain this ultralow-temperature anomaly, the operation
of three different mechanisms of dislocation motion through local
barriers must be considered: those of inertial, quantum, and
quantum-inertial
47–51 motion. Note that in the case of the domi-
nance of the quantum mechanism, the yield strength has an athe-
rmal character, which was not observed in the alloy investigated
here. Thus the anomaly of the yield strength is best explained by
the inertial motion of dislocations,
52–55 with the following details:
within this ultralow temperature range, where the contribution of
thermal fluctuations to the dislocation motion is very small, the
viscous resistance of dislocations during their movement between
localized obstacles becomes low.
56 Under these conditions, disloca-
tions overcome localized barriers by inertial motion without the
promotion of thermal fluctuations, which, in turn, leads to a
decrease in yield strength with decreasing temperature.
The temperature dependence of the ultimate strength is
similar to the behavior of the yield strength ( Fig. 5 ); namely, when
the test temperature decreases from 300 to 4.2 K an increase in the
ultimate strength is observed, but with further temperature decrease
to 0.5 K also the ultimate strength decreases. The maximum value
of the ultimate strength was recorded at a temperature of 4.2 K and
was equal to 1513 MPa. From Fig. 5 it can be seen that with
decreasing temperature, the difference between the values of σ
0.2
and σU increases, which reflects the increase of strain hardening
with decreasing temperature.
At the same time, as can be seen from Fig. 5 , the value of
uniform elongation in the temperature range of 300 –4.2 K is practi-
cally independent of temperature and is equal to ∼ 50%. With the
further decrease in temperature from 4.2 to ultralow temperature
0.5 K, this quantity even increases up to about ∼ 60% engineering
strain.

3.3. Discontinuous flow at temperatures of 4.2 and 0.5 K
Approaching deformation temperatures of 4.2 K and below,
the deformation curves change their character ( Fig. 6 ), i.e., from a
smooth flow at higher temperatures to serrated (discontinuous)
flow at the low(est) temperatures.
It should be noted that discontinuous flow at low temperatures
is characteristic of many metals and alloys.
57 In traditional fcc and
hcp alloys, the stress jumps on the tension curve reflect the slip
localization during the process of deformation of the specimens.
We can assume that in high-entropy alloys low-temperature dis-
continuous flow has the same nature as in traditional fcc and hcp
alloys, namely, is determined by the combined action of two inter-
related processes: dislocation governed ones
45,46 and thermally
activated58–60 ones.

The average flow stress increases with increasing deformation.
Statistical analyses showed that the process of jump-like deforma-

changes in the distribution of the jump amplitudes along the defor-
mation curves.
Considering the deformation curves taken at 4.2 K ( Fig. 6 ),
they also show stress jumps. Below deformations of about ≈ 0.15,
segments of macroscopically smooth plastic flow appear between
individual stress jumps. The distribution of jump amplitude is
almost uniform; however, with increasing deformation, the average
jump amplitude increases. In addition, a maximum appears within
the distribution density of the jump amplitudes, which shifts
towards higher values with increasing deformation. Near the ulti-
mate strength, the amplitude of the stress jumps reaches 100 MPa.
At 0.5 K, the deformation curves showed stress jumps, which
start immediately after reaching the yield strength, and continue for
all the deformation curve left ( Fig. 6). The average amplitude of the
jumps increases with increasing deformation; between adjacent
jumps, however, no macroscopically smooth plastic flow was
observed. A maximum occurs within the distribution of the jump
amplitudes, which with increasing deformation shifts to the region
of large amplitudes. Near the ultimate strength, the amplitude of
the stress jumps reaches 100 –120 MPa.

When the temperature decreases from 4.2 to 0.5 K, the
number of jumps on the deformation curves increases by a factor
of 1.4 –1.9. The stress jumps are due to the correlated collective
motion of dislocation avalanches. The maximum in the distribution
of the jump amplitudes indicates the existence of a characteristic
scale of localized plastic deformation. This scale is determined by
the density and strength of the barriers that impede the movement
of dislocations and cause their accumulations. The shift of the
maximum with increasing deformation to the region of large
amplitudes indicates an increase in the characteristic scale of local-
ized plastic deformation; this increase indicates an increase of the
rigidity of deformation induced defects, i.e., dislocation barriers –
probably caused by their size and their stress field. Changes of
deformation curves with decreasing temperature, and the patterns
of jump-like deformation can be explained by a combination of
several interconnected processes: (i) of a change in the defect
structure and arrangement during deformation, (ii) of an increase
in inertial effects during the movement of dislocations, and ( iii) of
a change in local heating in the regions of motion of dislocation
avalanches. A special feature of the material under investigation is

3.4. Elastic characteristics of Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10 alloy
in coarse-grained state
The dynamic elasticity modulus E(T) of the alloy in the initial
state and after tensile deformation up to engineering strains of 50%
has been investigated in a wide range of temperatures ( Fig. 7 ).
It is found that E(T) decreases monotonically, both in the initial
(in the temperature range 4.2 –320 K) and in the deformed state
(in the temperature range 4.2 –410 K). In the initial state, E(T)
decreases by 11% from 246 GPa at 4.2 K to 204 GPa at 410 K, and
in the deformed state by 9% from 180 GPa at 4.2 K to 159 GPa at
320 K. The transition from the initial state to the deformed one
changes the temperature dependence of E(T) from an almost linear
to a power-law dependence, and reduces the absolute values of the
modulus by about 20% in the entire temperature range studied.
From the thermodynamic point of view, the change in E(T)
during a structural phase transition is associated with a stepwise
change in the internal energy of the alloy during the formation of a
new phase.
67 This and also the generally smaller value of E(T)i n
the deformed state can, at least qualitatively, be ascribed to the
growth of the deformation induced hcp phase (see Table I ).
Note that similar changes in the temperature dependence of E(T)
were observed during structural-phase transitions in classical alloys
like shape memory alloys
68 and stainless steels, 69 as well as in the
high-entropy alloy Al 0.5CoCrCuFeNi.70
4. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, for the case of the nonequiatomic HEA TRIP
alloy Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10, we studied the effect of temperature on
the mechanical properties and microstructural evolution under
tension at a constant rate, at very low deformation temperatures
down to 0.5 K. It is found that the mechanical properties of this
alloy at ultralow temperatures are not worse than those of the alloy
at deformation at room temperature.
The following conclusions can be drawn:
(1) It was found that, due to the fcc → hcp phase transformation
from the induced by tensile plastic deformation, in the entire
investigated temperature range from 0.5 to 300 K record high
values of strength are reached while significant plasticity with
marked strain hardening was maintained: ( σ
0.2 ∼ 606 MPa,
σU ∼ 1513 MPa, engineering strain ε ∼ 50% at 4.2 K and
σ0.2 ∼ 536 MPa, σU ∼ 1274 MPa, engineering strain ε ∼ 50%
at 0.5 K).
(2) It was found that the fraction of the hcp phase in the deformed
samples only slightly depends on the deformation temperature
(94.0% at 300 K, 97.3% at 77 K, 97.6% at 4.2 K). This means
that already at 300 K almost the maximum fraction of the hcp
phase appears.
(3) Below about 300 K, the yield strength σ
0.2 shows a strong tem-
perature dependence, which can be related to the thermally
activated nature of plastic deformation.
(4) In the ultralow temperature range of 4.2 –0.5 K, an anomalous
decrease of the yield strength σ
0.2 with decreasing temperature
was found, indicating a change in the plasticity mechanism
from thermally activated to inertial dislocation motion; more-
over, in this temperature range, the nature of the plastic
deformation is unstable manifesting as stress jumps in the
deformation curves.
(5) It was found that the patterns of jump-like deformation are
governed by a combination of dislocation dynamics and
thermal activation processes. Statistical analysis showed the
presence of a maximum in the distribution density of the
jumps, amplitudes, indicating the existence of a characteristic
quantity of localized plastic deformation.
(6) Acoustic studies of the coarse-grained state demonstrated that
plastic deformation changes the temperature dependence of the
dynamic Young’s modulus from an almost linear to a stepwise
one, and also reduces its absolute values in the entire tempera-
ture range.
(7) The results of the study allow strongly recommend the HEA
Fe
50Mn30Co10Cr10 for use at extremely low temperatures.
Thanks to the occurrence of TRIP mechanism, and the high
solid solution hardening of high-entropy alloys, outstanding
mechanical properties can be achieved in cryogenic applications.
