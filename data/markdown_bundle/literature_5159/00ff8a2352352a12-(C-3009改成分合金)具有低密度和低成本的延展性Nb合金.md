abstract
Several Nb-based alloys containing Ti, Mo and W were produced and their microstructure, phase
composition and mechanical properties in the temperature range of 23 e1200 /C14 C were reported and
compared with a high-strength commercial Nb alloy C-3009, which contains Hf and W alloying ele-
ments. Replacement of Hf with Ti and W with Mo led to alloys with considerably reduced density and
cost, as well as with reduced micro-segregation of the alloying elements. The strengthening effect from
W was higher than from Mo at temperatures above ~600
/C14 C and more amount of Mo was required to
compensate the strength decrease. The strengthening effects from Hf and Ti were comparable at tem-
peratures up to 1000
/C14 C; however, at 1200 /C14 C the effect from Hf was superior. It was concluded that the
NbeMoeTi system can be used as a baseline for the development of high strength, high-temperature
alloys using alloying with other elements.

1. Introduction
Nb-based refractory alloys currently used in some high-
temperature engine applications contain expensive and dense
alloying elements [1]. For example, C-103, which is one of the most
commonly used medium-strength Nb alloys, contains (by wt.%) 10
Hf, 0.5 Ta, 0.5 W, 0.7 Zr and 1 Ti; and a high strength C-3009 con-
tains 30 Hf and 9 W. Hf, Ta and Zr are expensive, costing approxi-
mately $1200, $290 and $150 per kilogram, respectively, and Hf, W
and Ta have high density of, respectively, 13.21, 16.65 and 19.25 g/
cm3 (Table 1). Moreover, these alloys have poor oxidation resistance
above 600 /C14 C. It would therefore be bene ﬁcial to develop less
expensive and lower density Nb alloys, which would have struc-
tural properties, such as high temperature strength and oxidation
resistance, equivalent or better than those of the currently used
commercial Nb alloys. There has been extensive research on Nb
alloys containing Si, whose main goal is to improve both high
temperature strength and oxidation resistance [ 2e6]; however,
NbeSi alloys are generally brittle at temperatures /C20 1000 /C14 C and
they have not found practical use yet. Refractory complex concen-
trated alloys (RCCAs) is another promising direction of research
resulting in developing several multi-principal element alloys
(MPEAs) with attractive high temperature mechanical properties
and improved oxidation resistance [ 7]. The main challenge is the
lack of experimental data, as well as valid theoretical models, on
thermodynamic, mechanical and physical properties of these
complex MPEA systems. RCCAs experience slow kinetics of phase
transformations and may contain metastable phases. Thus exten-
sive studies are required to identify equilibrium phase diagrams,
kinetics of phase transformations and phase stability in these alloys
in the time-temperature-stress conditions of practical interest
[8,9].
In this work, we used conventional methods for alloy develop-
ment and produced several Nb-based alloys, which contain Mo, W
and Ti. A high-strength commercial Nb alloy C-3009 was used as a
baseline material and the new alloys were produced by replacing
heavy W and Hf with lower density Mo and Ti, respectively. Mo
belongs to the same group (VI) of the periodic table as W and Ti to
the same group (IV) as Hf. In addition to decreasing density, Ti is
known to noticeably improve oxidation resistance of Nb [ 12,13].
However, Mo and Ti have lower melting temperatures than W and
Hf. In addition, Mo has smaller elastic moduli than W but Ti has
higher elastic moduli than Hf. Because the strength of the alloys
generally increases with increasing in melting temperature and
elastic moduli, replacement of W with Mo may reduce high tem-
perature strength relative to C-3009, while the effect from
replacement of Hf with Ti is not straightforward. Thus, the effect of
the concentration of the alloying elements on high temperature
properties has also been studied in this work.

2. Experimental procedures
A C-3009 alloy was received from ATI Wah-Chang, Inc. in the
form of an as-cast ingot. Nb eWeTi (W1), Nb eMoeTi (M1, M2, M3
and M4) and NbeMoeWeTi (MW1) alloys were prepared from high
purity (99.9% or higher) metals by vacuum arc melting. The
elemental compositions of the alloys were determined using X-ray
energy dispersive spectroscopy and are shown in Table 2. The alloy
density was measured using a helium pycnometer, with the accu-
racy of ±0.005 g/cm
3. Vickers microhardness was determined as an
average value of 10 measurements using a diamond pyramid applied
to a polished surface of the alloy sample at 500 g load for 10 s. X-ray
diffraction was conducted using Cu K a1 radiation in the 2 q range of
10e120/C14 . Microstructure and chemical composition were studied
using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with back-
scatter electron and energy dispersive spectroscopy detectors.
Compression mechanical tests were conducted at 23 /C14 C, 600 /C14 C,
800 /C14 C, 1000 /C14 C and 1200 /C14 C at a constant ram speed of 0.008 mm/s
that corresponded to the initial strain rate of 0.001 s/C0 1. Compression
specimens had a squared cross-section of 5 mm /C2 5 mm and the
height of 8 mm. Special grinding tools were used to make the sam-
ples with parallel load surfaces and right-angle edge surfaces. Room
temperature tests were conducted in air and high-temperature tests
were conducted in vacuum of ~2 10 /C0 3 Pa. The temperature setting
for high-temperature compression testing consisted of heating at
50 /C14 C/min to a test temperature, holding at the temperature for
15 min, deformation with 50 e55% height reduction (or until frac-
ture, if it occurred ﬁrst) and cooling at the initial cooling rate of
10 0/C14 C/min or the rate of furnace cooling, whichever was slower. To
calculate a true sample displacement during loading, the following
procedure was used. At a given testing temperature, the response of
the loading system (machine frame, load cell, dies, etc.), without a
sample, was ﬁrst measured by registering displacement Dlm as a
function of the applied load P. Then a sample was placed between the
dies and the displacement Dlsys for the system with the sample was
registered as a function of the applied load P at the same tempera-
ture and ram speed. After that, at every registered P i values, the
sample upset values Dli (Pi) were calculated as Dl (Pi) ¼ Dlsys (Pi) e
Dlm (Pi). The true strain wasﬁnally calculated as ln (1- Dl/lo) and true
stress was calculated as P (1- Dl/lo)/Ao, where lo and Ao are the initial
height and initial cross-section area of the sample. Room tempera-
ture deformation and elastic behavior of the samples was addi-
tionally recorded using Vic-Gage software by Correlated Solutions
[14] and good agreement between these two methods was found.
The room temperature elastic modulus values reported in this work
were measured using Vic-Gauge software.

tion, up to ~0.1e0.18 true strain; then strain softening and/or steady

ductility was observed in C-3009 at 1200 /C14 C( Fig. 4 d). Visual
observation of the tested C-3009 samples showed that this loss in
compressive ductility was due to strain localization in a single
~45/C14 -inclined shear band and fracturing along this band. Such
behavior indicates deformation instability of C-3009 at 1200 /C14 C.
Fine-grained structure and multiple cracks were observed inside
this shear band. It is likely that formation of ﬁne-grained structure
within the shear band was responsible for strain localization and
cracking. The ﬁne-grain structure initiated extensive grain bound-
ary sliding, which, however, was not yet accommodated by dislo-
cations and diffusion inside grains, and this resulted in crack
formation at triple junctions and along grain boundaries. By
growing and merging, these micro-cracks led to macroscopic
fracture. Obviously, the strain hardening capability of the alloy is
not sufﬁcient to initiate plastic deformation on 5 independent slip
systems needed for homogeneous shape change.

3.2.4. Improving speci ﬁc strength
Although the yield stress of C-3009 was superior to the devel-
opmental alloys W1, M1, M2 and M3 at T /C21 600 /C14 C, its speciﬁc yield
stress, due to higher density, lagged up to 1000 /C14 C( Table 6, Fig. 5).
For example, at room temperature, s0.2/r of C-3009 and M1 are the
same ( ¼65.5 MPa cm3/g) and much smaller than those of W1, M2
and M3. At 600 /C14 C, s0.2/r of C-3009 is similar to M2 but smaller than
that of M3 and W1. At 800 /C14 C, s0.2/r of C-3009 is almost similar to
that of M3; but at T /C21 1000 /C14 C, C-3009 became superior to these
three Mo-containing alloys. At the same time, s0.2/r of W1 is su-
perior to M1, M2, M3 and C-3009 ate temperatures up to 1000 /C14 C.
The speci ﬁc yield stress values of M4 and MW1 are considerably
higher than those of all other studied alloys, including C-3009. It is
apparent that solution strengthening of Nb by Mo is noticeably
weaker than by W. Alloying with higher amount of Mo is required
to achieve the strengthening effect similar to W. At the same time,
Ti provides stronger strengthening effect than Hf at room temper-
ature; however, the advantage is lost above 800 /C14 C.

The produced alloys, except MW1, had single-phase BCC crystal
structures. MW1 had small fraction of Ti-rich precipitates located at
grain boundaries. While chemical micro-segregation was clearly
observed in the cast C-3009, replacement of Hf with Ti and W with
Mo led to more homogeneous distribution of the alloying elements
in the developmental alloys. In Mo-containing alloys, the average
grain size decreased with an increase in the amount of Mo.
The densities of the produced Nb alloys were considerably
smaller than the density of C-3009 (10.3 g/cm 3). Alloy M4 had the
smallest density ( ¼7.70 g/cm3) and MW1 had the largest density
(¼8.94 g cm3) among the reported alloys.
W-containing alloys (C-3009, W1 and MW1) were stronger than
Mo-containing alloys at T ¼ 600e1200 /C14 C. With increasing tem-
perature in this temperature range, Mo-containing alloys lost their
strength quicker than W-containing alloys.
Alloying with Hf had bene ﬁcial effect on high-temperature
strength relative to alloying with Ti; however, the effect from Ti
was stronger at room temperature. On the other hand, alloying
with Ti reduced the alloy density and increased speci ﬁc strength at
higher temperatures.
At all the studied temperatures, the strength of Mo-containing
alloys increased almost linearly with increasing Mo concentration
evidencing solid-solution strengthening.
Atomistic simulation based on Suzuki's model can satisfactory
predict yield stress of the Nb refractory alloys at temperatures
/C20 1000 /C14 C, but it fails at higher temperatures, when the diffusion-
controlled processes start to dominate.
Due to reduced density, the room temperature speciﬁc strengths
of the developmental alloys were superior to that of C-3009. Spe-
ciﬁc strengths of M4 and MW1 were superior to C-3009 and all
other reported alloys at all the studied temperatures.
