Abstract
In this study, novel metastable Ti–(9–13)Nb–7Sn–(2–6)Mo alloys were developed using Bo–Md method. The Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys exhibited two 
types of structure: β and α" + β. Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys had high bending strength (1145–1255 MPa). Also, Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys presented low elastic 
modulus (53–63 GPa). Furthermore, the moduli of resilience of Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys were as high as 4.05–7.99 MJ/m3, which is significantly higher 
than those of commercially pure Ti (1.16 MJ/m 3) and Ti–25Nb–8Sn (3.02 MJ/m3). The novel metastable Ti–11Nb–7Sn–5Mo alloy developed in this 
research exhibited the greatest value of the modulus of resilience, which has the potential to be used for biomedical implant applications.
Introduction
316L stainless steel (316L SS), cobalt-chromium-molybdenum 
(Co–Cr–Mo) alloy, commercially pure titanium (c.p. Ti), and Ti 
alloys are often used as biometals.[1] Among them, 316L SS has 
good mechanical properties and processing characteristics, but 
its abrasion and corrosion resistance are worse than the other 
alloys. Also, Ni ions can cause allergic reactions in human 
body and decrease the biocompatibility of implant materials.[2] 
The corrosion resistance and biocompatibility of Co–Cr–Mo 
alloy are better than those of 316L SS, but its excessively high 
elastic modulus may induce the stress shielding effect. C.p. 
Ti and Ti alloys are widely used in biomedical fields, exhib-
iting good specific strength, biocompatibility, and corrosion 
resistance.[1,3,4]
At present, the most representative Ti alloy for biomedi-
cal applications is Ti–6Al–4V .[4] Compared with 316L SS and 
Co–Cr–Mo alloy, the Ti–6Al-4V with extra-low interstitial 
(ELI) has a good specific strength, lower elastic modulus, and 
better corrosion resistance. However, the release of aluminum 
(Al) and vanadium (V) ions generated from the Ti–6Al–4V 
ELI can induce an adverse effect on the human body. [5] Fur-
thermore, the elastic modulus of Ti–6Al–4V ELI (110 GPa) 
is much higher than that of human bone (≤ 30 GPa), thus the 
stress shielding effect is still unavoidable. [6]
Ti–Nb-based alloys have been widely developed for bio-
medical applications due to their low elastic modulus. [7,8] The 
metastable design concept introduced in Ti alloy is expected to 
obtain a lower elastic modulus, such as Ti–29Nb–13Ta–4.6Zr,[9] 
Ti–24Nb–4Zr–7.9Sn,[10] and Ti–12Mo–6Zr–2Fe.[11] Bond order 
and metal d-orbital (Bo–Md) method are often used to predict 
the phase stability of Ti alloys. [12,13] The Bo and Md values 
of Ti alloys are calculated based on the Bo and Md values of 
their constituent elements. The formula of Bo and Md values 
are shown as follows[14]: Bo = ΣXi(Bo)i, Md = ΣXi(Md)i, where 
Xi is the atomic fraction of the alloy element ith component in 
the alloy, and (Bo)i and (Md)i are the Bo and Md values for the 
ith component, respectively. In the current study, the positions 
of the Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys on the Bo–Md diagram are shown 
in Fig. 1.
Ho et al.[15] added Fe element to Ti–5Cr alloy and developed 
β-type Ti–5Cr–3Fe alloy. The bending strength of Ti-5Cr–3Fe 
(2200 MPa) was increased about 70% compared with Ti–5Cr 
(1300 MPa) under the contribution of solid solution strength-
ening of Fe element. Through the addition of 3 wt% Fe in the 
Ti–5Cr alloy, the structure of the alloy was transformed from 
α’/β/ω to a single β phase, resulting in a decrease in the elas-
tic modulus from 120 to 85 GPa. Furthermore, Ti–5Nb–3Mo 
(α’) had a higher bending strength (1675 MPa) and a lower 
elastic modulus (79 GPa) than those of Ti–5Nb (α ’).[16] In our 
previous study, a metastable β Ti–25Nb–8Sn alloy was devel-
oped, exhibiting a low elastic modulus (52 GPa).[17] However, 
Ti–25Nb–8Sn shows an insufficient yield strength (560 MPa) 
and contains greater amounts of high-cost Nb element. In recent 
years, powder injection molding,[18] powder metallurgy,[19] and 
3D printing[20] have been widely used to reduce manufacturing 
costs. However, there is another straightforward approach to 
reduce costs by replacing expensive elements (such as Nb) with

In this study, several quaternary Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys were 
developed by adjusting the percentage of alloying element of 
Ti–25Nb–8Sn and the substitution of some amount of Nb with 
Mo element. The additions of Mo can increase the mechanical 
strength of the alloy through the solid solution strengthening 
effect and reduce the cost of the alloy. Furthermore, the Bo–Md 
method was used to adjust the compositions of alloying ele-
ments of Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo to achieve a metastable β structure 
for the purpose of obtaining a lower elastic modulus. Under 
the above alloy design, it can be expected that the alloy can 
have both a higher yield strength and a lower elastic modulus. 
The microstructures, phase structures, and mechanical proper-
ties of Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys were examined in this study and 
compared with Ti–25Nb–8Sn and c.p. Ti.
Experimental
The quaternary Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys were designed for this 
study, including Ti–9Nb–7Sn–4Mo, Ti–9Nb–7Sn–6Mo, 
Ti–11Nb–7Sn–3Mo, Ti–11Nb–7Sn–5Mo, Ti–13Nb–7Sn–2Mo, 
and Ti–13Nb–7Sn–4Mo. The Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys were pre-
pared with a commercial arc-melting vacuum pressure cast -
ing system (A-028, DAWN SHINE, Taiwan) by using Ti (99.7 
wt% pure), Nb (99.95 wt% pure), Sn (99.9 wt% pure), and Mo 
(99.95 wt% pure). All the Ti alloy ingots were melted more 
than five times for homogenizing the constituent elements. The 
proposed Ti alloys were compared with c.p. Ti (Grade 1) and 
Ti–25Nb–8Sn. The surfaces of all alloys were mechanically 
polished according to a standard metallographic procedure to a 
final level of 0.06 μm SiO2 polishing suspension. After cleaning 
the surface of the samples with deionized water, the samples 
were soaked in alcohol and cleaned using an ultrasonic cleaner. 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) and three-point bending tests were 
performed on the polished alloys. The alloys were etched in a 
solution of deionized water, nitric acid, and hydrofluoric acid in 
an 80:15:5 volume ratio for optical microscopy (OM).
Chemical compositions of as-cast Ti alloys were exam-
ined through scanning electron microscopy (SEM; 6330, 
JEOL, Japan) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS). The acceleration voltage was 15 kV for SEM–EDS; 
the scanning time for analysis was 3 min. At least three sam-
ples of each Ti alloy were analyzed to ensure the reliability 
and accuracy of the test results. The measured compositions 
of the as-cast Ti alloys obtained through EDS-SEM are pre-
sented in Table I. Phase analysis was conducted through XRD 
(D8-Advance, Bruker, Germany) with Cu–Kα radiation at 
40 kV , 40 mA, 2θ = 30°–80°, a scanning speed of 4°/min, 
and a step size of 0.02°/step. The phase volume fractions of 
as-cast Ti alloys were calculated using the XRD patterns.
Three-point bending tests were adopted to evaluate the 
mechanical properties of all alloys in this study because 
implants are often subjected to flexural (bending) stresses 
instead of tensile loads during the movement of the human 
body from a clinical practice viewpoint. [21,22] Three-point 
bending tests were performed using a desktop mechani-
cal tester (HT-2102AP, Hung-Ta Instrument, Taiwan) with 
a 30-mm span length at a crosshead speed rate of 0.5 mm/
min, maximum deflection of 8 mm, and maximum deflection 
angle of 56.1°. The bending strength was calculated using 
the following equation [23]: σB = 3PL/2bh2 , where σB is the

span length (mm), b is the sample width (mm), and h is the 
sample thickness (mm). The dimensions of the samples were 
L = 30 mm, b = 5 mm, and h = 0.9 mm. The yield strength was

Δδ is the increase in deflection (mm) in the middle of the 
sample. At least five samples were measured for each kind 
of Ti alloy, and the average values and standard deviations 
were calculated for the Ti alloys. The recovery angle for all 
Ti alloys was measured from the changes in the deflection

Results and discussion
Phase and structure
The XRD patterns of Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys are shown 
in Fig. 2 (a). Ti–9Nb–7Sn–4Mo, Ti–9Nb–7Sn–6Mo, 
Ti–11Nb–7Sn–5Mo, and Ti–13Nb–7Sn–4Mo presented 
a single β phase (BCC structure). Ti–11Nb–7Sn–3Mo 
and Ti–13Nb–7Sn–2Mo exhibit a dual-phase α " (hexago-
nal) + β structure and the volume fractions of α " phase in 
Ti–11Nb–7Sn–3Mo and Ti–13Nb–7Sn–2Mo were 80 and 70%, 
respectively. The occurrence of α " phase in the two alloys is 
because their alloy compositions contain fewer β-stabilizing 
elements (for example, Mo), which result in the alloys failing 
to reach a single β phase. According to the “Mo equivalent”,[25] 
Mo is a strong β-stabilizing element, much higher than Nb and 
Sn.
The optical metallographic images of the Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo 
alloys are shown in Fig. 2 (b–g). All the quaternary Ti alloys 
showed a dendritic structure, which is a typical microstruc-
tural feature of as-cast alloy. Also, the equiaxed β grains 
were observed in the metallographic photos of the β -phase 
Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys. A small amount of fine needle-
like α" phase was also observed in the dual-phase (α " + β) 
Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys. Ho et al.[26] indicated that the equiaxed 
crystals and fine needle-like precipitates in the Ti alloys are the 
characteristics of β and α" phase, respectively. The results of 
metallographic examinations were in line with the inferences 
of XRD analysis.
Mechanical properties
The bending strengths and yield strengths (1145–1255 and 
700–975 MPa, respectively) of the Ti–(9–13)Nb–7Sn–(2–6)Mo 
alloys were significantly greater than those of Ti–25Nb–8Sn 
(962 and 560 MPa, respectively) and c.p. Ti (705 and 480 MPa, 
respectively) [Fig. 3 (a–b)], which may be attributed to the 
solid solution strengthening effect caused by the discrepancy 
in atomic radius and percentages of Nb and Mo.[24] Among all 
the quaternary alloys, the α " + β Ti–13Nb–7Sn–2Mo had the 
greatest bending strength (1255 MPa), which can attributed to 
the phase boundaries hindering the dislocation motion. [27,28] 
However, the yield strength is more crucial than the bend-
ing strength because the plastic deformation of the implant 
in the human body was not permitted. Ti–11Nb–7Sn–5Mo 
with β phase has the greatest yield strength (975 MPa), which 
was 74% and 103% higher than Ti–25Nb–8Sn and c.p. Ti, 
respectively.
The elastic moduli and elastic recovery angles of the 
Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys, Ti–25Nb–8Sn, and c.p. Ti are displayed 
in Fig. 3(c–d). The elastic moduli of Ti–(9–13)Nb–7Sn-(2–6)
Mo (53–63 GPa) was significantly lower than that of c.p. Ti (99 
GPa) and was similar to that of Ti–25Nb–8Sn (52 GPa). Among 
all the Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys, Ti–13Nb–7Sn–4Mo had the low-
est elastic modulus (53 GPa), which may be attributed to its 
metastable β structure. The metastable β -phase Ti alloy can 
exhibit a lower elastic modulus than Ti alloys with α or α + β 
phase.[29] The elastic recovery angles of the Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo 
alloys (14.8–32.6°) were much greater than that of c.p. Ti (2.7°) 
and similar to that of Ti–25Nb–8Sn (19°). Among all the qua-
ternary alloys, Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys with a single β phase had 
greater elastic recovery angles than the Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys 
with α" + β phase, which is due to the phase boundaries decreas-
ing the ability of elastic deformation of the alloy.[30,31]
Nunes et al.[13] developed the metastable Ti–29Nb–2Mo–6Zr 
and Ti–24Nb–4Mo–3Zr alloys with high hardness values (227 
and 235 HV , respectively) and low elastic moduli (93 and 97 
GPa, respectively) using the Bo–Md theory. They indicated that 
the alloys can reach the metastable state and obtain low elastic 
modulus when the Bo–Md values of alloys are located between 
the Ms and Mf lines. In the present study, the elastic moduli of 
the six alloys were lower than those of the Ti–29Nb–2Mo–6Zr 
and Ti–24Nb–4Mo–3Zr. In fact, although Ti–13Nb–7Sn–4Mo 
was located to the right of the M f line on the Bo–Md dia-
gram (Fig. 1 ), the alloy exhibited a single β phase and low 
elastic modulus (53 GPa). Similarly, Hao et al. [10] developed 
Ti–24Nb–4Zr–7.9Sn with a single β phase and low elastic 
modulus (42 GPa), which was also located on the right side of 
the Mf line (Bo: 2.825 and Md: 2.443) (Fig. 1). Therefore, even 
if the Bo–Md theory is helpful for the design of metastable 
Ti alloys, the Bo–Md value that enables the alloy to have the 
lowest elastic modulus in various alloy systems still needs to 
be verified by experiments.
Among the alloys with α" + β structure, the bending 
strength and yield strength of Ti–13Nb–7Sn–2Mo were 
greater than that of Ti–11Nb–7Sn–3Mo. Also, the elas-
tic modulus of Ti–13Nb–7Sn–2Mo was higher than that of 
Ti–11Nb–7Sn–3Mo. The above results may be attributed to 
the higher volume fractions of α" phase in Ti–11Nb–7Sn–3Mo 
than in Ti–13Nb–7Sn–2Mo. For various phases of Ti alloys, 
the α" phase has the lowest elastic modulus. From this perspec-
tive, the effect of phase structure on the mechanical properties 
of Ti alloys seems to be more pronounced than solid solution 
strengthening.

potential as an implant can be evaluated by the modulus of 
resilience, which is the measure of a material’s ability to 
store and release elastic strain energy. [32] Furthermore, the 
low elastic modulus of an implant can provide protection

Conclusions
In this study, six new metastable Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys 
were developed by adding Mo element to the Ti–25Nb–8Sn 
alloy proposed by our research group previously. Also, the 
Bo-Md theory was used to adjust the alloy composition. Six 
Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys exhibited two types of phases, namely 
β and α" + β. The Ti–9Nb–7Sn–4Mo, Ti–9Nb–7Sn–6Mo, 
Ti–11Nb–7Sn–5Mo, and Ti–13Nb–7Sn–4Mo alloys presented 
a single β structure, which contains more β -stabilizing ele-
ments. Besides, Ti–11Nb–7Sn–3Mo and Ti–13Nb–7Sn–2Mo 
exhibited an α" + β dual-phase structure. The bending strengths 
(1145–1255 MPa) of Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys were 19–30% higher 
than that of Ti–25Nb–8Sn. The quaternary Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloy 
system designed by metastability theory exhibited the feature 
of low elastic modulus. Furthermore, the values of the modulus 
of resilience of Ti–Nb–Sn–Mo alloys were significantly higher 
than those of c.p. Ti and Ti–25Nb–8Sn. Moreover, the meta-
stable β-type Ti–11Nb–7Sn–5Mo had the greatest value of the 
modulus of resilience (7.99 MJ/m3), which may be considered 
as a potential candidate for bioimplant applications.
