Abstract 
Intermediate temperature embrittlement is a general phenomenon in Ni-based superalloys and 
is frequently attributed to the equi-cohesive temperature. In this paper, the correlation between 
intermediate temperature embrittlement and equi-coh esive temperature is studied by the 
tensile ductility of high-purity Ni and binary Ni(B i) alloy. Elevated-temperature tensile 
ductility of the Ni(Bi) alloy shows evident interme diate temperature embrittlement with 
minimum ductility between 700°C and 750°C, while th at of high purity Ni does not. 
Room-temperature tensile ductility of Ni(Bi) alloy reveals clear minimum after a critical time 
of isothermal pre-annealing at 650°C or 750°C. The fracture morphologies of tensile samples 
are well consistent with the changes of their corre sponding tensile ductility. Based on these 
results and reported results of Ni-based superalloy s, it is clear that equi-cohesive temperature 
is insufficient to be a general interpretation for intermediate temperature embrittlement. 
Keywords: Ni alloys; Intermediate temperature embrittlement; Equi-cohesive temperature; 
Tensile test;

1. Introduction 
Ni-based superalloys are widely used in aviation, a erospace, petroleum and chemical 
industry because of the good high temperature stren gth, oxidation resistance, fatigue 
properties and fracture toughness [1-11]. At the sa me time, the tensile tests of Ni-based 
superalloys at different temperatures manifest a ge neral phenomenon that the ductility 
(elongation at fracture or reduction in area) at in termediate temperature is much lower than 
those at low and high temperatures [1,2,3,12-17], a nd the fracture morphologies tested at the 
intermediate temperature range are intergranular [1 8]. This phenomenon is known as the 
intermediate temperature embrittlement (ITE). 
The ITE was explained firstly by the concept of equ i-cohesive temperature (ECT) [19]. 
Fig. 1 is the schematic illustration of the ECT. It is considered that the strengths of metals are 
composed of the grain interior (GI) strength and gr ain boundary (GB) strength. At low 
temperature, the GB strength is greater than the GI strength. With the increase in temperature, 
both strengths decrease. Because the atoms in GBs a re more unstable and GBs have more 
defects, the decrease in GB strength is fast. At a certain temperature, TE, the strengths of GI 
and GB are equal. This temperature is defined as the ECT. If the temperature increases further, 
the GB strength will be lower than the GI strength. It can be concluded that the fracture 
morphology is transgranular when a sample is fractu red at a temperature lower than the ECT 
and is intergranular when the temperature is higher than the ECT. Naturally, polycrystalline 
metals must possess the ECT. 
After the concept of the ECT was proposed, it was w idely used to explain the ITE 
[20-23]. It is considered that at low temperature, both GB strength and GI strength are higher

and the GB strength is even higher than the GI stre ngth. Thus, the tensile deformation makes 
dislocation density and consequent stress concentra tion in GIs increase, causing cracks to 
initiate inside GIs and propagate transgranularly. Finally, high ductility is obtained after 
fracture. At intermediate temperature, dislocations formed during deformation accumulate 
near GBs and give rise to stress concentration at G Bs. The stress concentration will lead to 
crack initiation at GBs since the GB strength decre ases at intermediate temperature range. 
This results in rapid propagation of cracks along GBs and causes low ductility. As a result, the 
fracture morphology is intergranular. If the temper ature is high, the stress concentration at 
GBs can be relaxed by coordination deformation of G I and GB, thus leading to few initiation 
and slow propagation of cracks. Consequently, high ductility is obtained. 
However, experiments also revealed that the ITE doe s not exist in polycrystalline 
high-purity Ni [24,25]. This fact causes the doubt of the interpretation of ECT to ITE since 
polycrystalline materials possess ECT intrinsically . In this case, the present work is carried 
out to verify the validity of the interpretation of ECT for ITE by performing tensile tests of 
high-purity Ni and binary Ni(Bi). The results will provide essential bases to understand the 
nature of ITE and are helpful to improve the service reliability of Ni-based superalloys. 
 
2. Experimental 
The tensile samples of high-purity Ni (the total co ncentration of impurities is less than 1 
wt ppm) were cut from the as-delivered sheets by sp ark erosion. The cross-section was 2 mm 
× 2 mm and the gauge length was 6 mm. The samples w as sealed in evacuated quartz capsule 
(6×10-3 Pa) and subjected to heat treatments at 1000 °C/0.5 h. They were quenched into iced

water as quickly as possible after the quartz capsules were broken. 
The Ni(Bi) alloy with 25 wt ppm Bi was vacuum melte d by mixing high-purity Ni with 
pure Bi. After melting, Ni(Bi) alloy was deformed i nto a bar of 4 mm diameter by a hammer 
machine at room temperature. After deformation, Ni( Bi) alloy was heat-treated by 455 °C/1h 
+ 600 °C/1h + 1000 °C/0.5h and quenched into iced w ater at once. The tensile samples of 
Ni(Bi) alloy with dimensions of 2 mm in diameter and 6 mm in gauge length were made from 
the quenched alloy. 
Elevated-temperature tensile tests were performed i n air by an INSTRON-1195 machine 
equipped with a furnace. The strain rate is 10-2 s-1 and the temperature range is from 400 °C to 
850 °C. All the elevated-temperature tensile sample s were kept isochronally at the test 
temperature for 45 minutes before testing. The elon gation at fracture is used to quantify the 
ductility. Six independent samples of Ni(Bi) alloy and three independent samples of 
high-purity Ni were measured in order to ensure the accuracy of the data. Henceforth, 
elevated-temperature tensile ductility after isochr onal heat treatment in air is designated as 
isochronal ductility. 
For the room-temperature tensile tests, the tensile samples of Ni(Bi) alloy were first 
sealed in evacuated quartz capsule and subjected to isothermal annealing at 650 °C and 
750 °C, respectively. After heat treatment, samples were quenched into iced water as quickly 
as possible after the quartz capsules were broken. Tensile tests were then performed at room 
temperature with strain rate of 10 -2 s -1. Three independent samples were used to get the 
average ductility. Henceforth, room-temperature ten sile ductility after isothermal heat 
treatment in vacuum capsulation is designated as isothermal ductility.

3. Results 
Fig. 2 illustrates the isochronal ductility of high -purity Ni and Ni(Bi) alloy at different 
testing temperature. It is seen from Fig. 2(a) that the isochronal ductility of the high-purity Ni 
increases continuously from 60.22% at 400 °C to 81. 67% at 750 °C with the increase in 
temperature, and then decreases slowly over 800 °C and finally reaches 72.67% at 850 °C. 
Thus, ITE does not exist in high-purity Ni. Fig. 2( b) shows that the isochronal ductility of 
Ni(Bi) alloy decreases first with the increase in t emperature from 46.45% at 400 °C to 
26.92% at 750 °C and then increases rapidly until 5 7.75% at 825 °C. Therefore, Ni(Bi) alloy 
shows evident ITE. Besides, the ductility of high p urity Ni is higher than that of Ni(Bi) alloy 
in the whole temperature range. 
Fig. 3 presents the fracture morphologies of high-p urity Ni after isochronal heat 
treatment and elevated-temperature tensile tests. C learly, necking takes place in all tensile 
samples, as already shown in Fig. 2(a). For the sam ple tested at 400 °C, the fracture 
morphology shows necking and dimples in its outer a nd inner regions, respectively. For the 
samples tested at 750 °C and 850 °C, the fracture m orphologies show a "pyramid" structures, 
demonstrating that the cross-sectional area of high -purity Ni sample necked to almost a point 
before rupture. This stands for excellent ductility of high-purity Ni. The variation in necking 
is well consistent with the behavior of isochronal ductility in Fig. 2(a). 
Fig. 4 shows the fracture morphologies of Ni(Bi) al loy after isochronal heat treatment

4. Discussions 
From Fig. 2(b) it is obvious that Ni(Bi) alloy has ITE. According to the interpretation of 
ECT, cracks propagate by normal transgranular mode at low temperature, thus causing a high 
ductility. At intermediate temperature, cracks init iate at GBs and propagate along GBs. As a 
result, low ductility is obtained and the fracture morphology is intergranular. At high 
temperature, coordination deformation between GI an d GB leads to high ductility. As shown 
in Fig. 4, the Ni(Bi) samples display ductile fract ure at 400 °C, intergranular fracture at 
750 °C, and ductile fracture at 825 °C. Therefore, the changes of isochronal ductility and 
fracture morphology are consistent to the interpretation of ECT. 
However, Fig. 2(a) confirms that the ITE does not e xist in high-purity Ni. As known, 
high-purity Ni is polycrystalline and ECT must oper ate in it. Furthermore, ITE of high-purity 
Ni must emerge. This deduction contradicts directly to the facts that high-purity Ni has not 
ITE. It is notable that the fracture morphologies o f high-purity Ni show high ductility in the 
whole temperature range, which is inconsistent to E CT. In this case, it can be concluded that 
ECT is not an intrinsic factor for ITE. 
Fig. 5 illustrates that the isothermal ductility of Ni(Bi) alloy at 650 °C and 750 °C 
decreases first and then increases with the increas e in isothermal annealing time. As shown in 
Fig. 6 and Fig. 7, the fracture morphologies of Ni( Bi) alloy after isothermal heat treatment at 
650 °C and 750 °C first reveals apparent necking an d ductile fracture, then intergranular 
fracture, and finally ductile fracture, which are w ell consistent with the changes of isothermal 
ductility shown in Fig. 5. As known, the isothermal ductility at a certain temperature should

be constant if the embrittlement is caused by ECT. Therefore, the ECT cannot explain the 
changes of the isothermal ductility although it see ms to be consistent to the isochronal 
ductility of Ni(Bi) alloy (i.e. ITE). 
The interpretation of ECT contradicts to not only t he experimental results of high-purity 
Ni and Ni(Bi) alloy (the isothermal ductility) but also those of Ni-based single crystal 
superalloys. The ductility of Ni-based single cryst al superalloys PWA 1484 [26], DD3 [27], 
CMSX-4 [28], and CMSX-10 [29] were proven experimen tally to reveal ITE, too. For 
example, the tensile property of PWA 1484 in Fig. 8 [26] displays that the ductility decreases 
from 17.6% with increasing temperature to 12% aroun d 600 °C and then increases rapidly to 
32% around 1100 °C. This indicates an obvious ITE. However, single crystal superalloys have 
not the ECT just because of the absence of GBs. Thu s, the interpretation of ECT is not valid 
for the ITE in single crystal superalloys. 
Furthermore, several experiments [30-33] have confi rmed that the ITE of Ni-based 
superalloys disappear under the full heat treated c onditions. For instance, the influence of 
thermal exposure on the elongation at fracture of D Z951 superalloy was studied by Xia et al 
[33]. In their experiment, the DZ951 superalloy was heat treated before long-term thermal 
exposure. The procedure for the standard heat treatment (SHT) was: 1220 °C/4h, air cooling + 
1050 °C/4h, air cooling + 870 °C/24h, air cooling. The alloy was then isothermally aged at 
900 °C for 100 h, 500 h, 1000 h, 2000 h and cooled in air. As shown in the Fig. 9 [33], the 
DZ951 superalloy shows evident ITE after standard h eat treatment (SHT). However, the ITE 
vanished after the isothermal exposures at 900 °C. Clearly, the DZ951 superalloy is 
polycrystalline and ECT still operate in it even af ter long-time isothermal exposure. Hence,

the ECT cannot be responsible for the disappearance of ITE. 
Based on the above experiments and discussions, it can be concluded that the ITE and 
ECT do not have an inherent relationship and ECT ca nnot be a general interpretation to ITE 
of Ni alloys. Besides, the results of high-purity N i and Ni(Bi) alloy indicate the ITE is caused 
by harmful impurities. So, the study on the ITE sho uld be further conducted from the 
viewpoint of impurity effect. 
 
5. Conclusion 
Elevated-temperature tensile ductility of the Ni(Bi ) alloy shows evident ITE with 
minimum ductility between 700 °C and 750 °C, while that of high-purity Ni does not. 
Room-temperature tensile ductility of Ni(Bi) alloy reveals an obvious minimum after 50 h or 
45 min after isothermal treatment at 650 0C or 750 0C. Fracture morphologies of tensile 
samples are well consistent with the changes of their corresponding tensile ductility. 
Based on these results and reported results of Ni-b ased superalloys, the interpretation of 
ECT is confirmed to not be a general interpretation to the ITE of Ni alloys. The nature of ITE 
needs further research on the impurity effect.
