High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have gained attention for their exceptional mechanical properties across a 
wide temperature range. At the same time, the alloys with sustained high strength and toughness across 
temperatures remain scarce. This study focuses on a Ni-rich HEA, Ni44 Fe19 Co16 Cr9 V9 Al3 , tailored specif- 
ically for elevated temperature applications. The alloy exhibits outstanding strength-ductility synergy at 
room temperature, low temperature (77 K), and high temperatures (873 and 1073 K). Its ultimate ten- 
sile strengths are about 1.59 GPa, 1.28 GPa, 1.03 GPa, and 619 MPa, respectively at 77, 293, 873, and 
1073 K, accompanied by excellent ductility of 44.7 %, 33.6 %, 35.1 %, and 13.7 %, respectively. The excep- 
tional mechanical behavior is realized by deliberate intervention reinforcement mechanisms at various 
temperatures, including back stress, dislocation, coherent precipitates, stacking faults, and TWIP effects. 
Furthermore, phase ﬁeld simulation indicated that, besides the TWIP effect caused by the lower stack- 
ing fault energy, the subsequent deformation mechanisms, including the breakage of the wider twins 
into nanotwins to relieve the stress concentration, and the non-turning transfer of intracrystalline dis- 
locations by the nanotwin boundaries, are important reasons for the high strength and high ductility of 
the alloy at 77 K. Conversely, at ambient temperatures, deformation is governed by hetero-deformation- 
induced hardening, driven primarily by the accumulation of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs). 
At elevated temperatures (873–1073 K), dynamic interactions between precipitates and dislocations dom- 
inate, involving mechanisms such as the Portevin-Le Chatelier effect and precipitate bypass (e.g., Orowan 
looping), with stacking faults further contributing to strengthening. This study presents a comprehensive 
understanding of the deformation mechanisms and can provide an eﬃcient pathway for further enhance- 
ment of the alloy for broad temperature applications.

1. Introduction 
The rapid advancement of modern technology has driven a 
growing demand for structural materials that exhibit exceptional 
strength and toughness over a broad temperature range, partic- 
ularly in critical ﬁelds such as nuclear energy, aerospace, and 
chemical engineering [ 1 , 2 ]. Traditional alloys, though often opti- 
mized for speciﬁc service environments, tend to lose their me- 
chanical integrity under wide temperature ﬂuctuations [ 3 , 4 ]. In 
contrast, high-entropy alloys (HEAs), developed in recent decades,

have shown great promise due to their outstanding mechanical 
performance, as well as superior corrosion, fatigue, and radiation 
resistance [ 5 , 6 ]. These properties are primarily attributed to the 
core effects of HEAs: high-entropy effect, sluggish diffusion, lat- 
tice distortion, and cocktail effect, which arise from their multi- 
principal element compositions and deviation from the classical 
Gibbs phase rule [ 7 ]. The remarkable tunability of HEAs offers 
fascinating potential for developing materials with unprecedented 
mechanical properties for critical applications under extreme con- 
ditions. For instance, face-centered cubic (FCC) HEAs composed of 
transition metal elements exhibit extraordinary strength-ductility 
synergy at ambient to cryogenic temperatures, resulting in ex- 
cellent fracture toughness. In contrast, some body-centered cubic

(BCC) refractory HEAs demonstrate ultra-high strength at elevated 
temperatures. However, the optimization of HEA properties has 
largely been achieved within a narrow temperature range, either 
at low or high temperatures [ 3 , 8 ]. Therefore, enhancing the me- 
chanical performance of HEAs across a wide range of service tem- 
peratures is of particular interest for their potential applications in 
outer space exploration and other extreme environments. 
Similar to traditional alloys, HEAs can be reinforced through 
methods like grain reﬁnement [ 9 , 10 ], interstitial solid solution 
[ 11 , 12 ], precipitation of phases [ 4 , 13 ], twinning-induced plasticity 
(TWIP) [ 14 , 15 ], or transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) [ 16 , 17 ]. 
TWIP and TRIP phenomena often result in simultaneous improve- 
ments in strength and toughness, primarily inﬂuenced by the in- 
trinsic property of stacking fault energy (SFE). Lower SFE facil- 
itates the transition from slip to TWIP, while further reduction 
triggers TRIP [ 14 , 15 ]. Therefore, activating TWIP or TRIP effects is 
more likely to occur at lower temperatures, as the stacking fault 
energy decreases with temperature. However, at elevated tem- 
peratures, it becomes challenging due to dynamic recovery pro- 
cesses, which inhibit the required shear stresses for TWIP ef- 
fect initiation. Recent research has also highlighted the impor- 
tance of alternative strengthening mechanisms such as heteroge- 
neous structure [ 18 , 19 ]. Heterogeneous structure strengthening can 
be achieved through partial recrystallization, rapid sintering, me- 
chanical surface treatment, or secondary annealing [ 20 ], which 
aims to overcome the strength-ductility trade-off in HEAs and 
prevent premature necking at low or room temperatures. How- 
ever, at high temperatures, the heterogeneous structure may di- 
minish due to continuous grain growth, potentially leading to re- 
duced strengthening eﬃcacy. Nickel-based alloys have been fa- 
vored for high-temperature applications due to their stable mi- 
crostructure, which does not undergo allotropic transformations 
from room temperature to elevated temperatures, resulting in ex- 
ceptional high-temperature mechanical properties [ 8 ]. Therefore, 
the effectiveness of individual strengthening mechanisms is typi- 
cally conﬁned to speciﬁc temperature ranges. By utilizing the broad 
compositional space of HEAs, adjusting the alloy composition to re- 
duce SFE and introduce TWIP/TRIP effects at lower temperatures, 
adding higher nickel content to enhance high-temperature per- 
formance, and incorporating mechanisms such as heterogeneous 
structure strengthening and precipitate phase strengthening to im- 
prove room-temperature strength, outstanding mechanical proper- 
ties can be achieved across a broad temperature spectrum, from 
cryogenic to elevated temperatures. Compared to traditional Ni- 
based superalloys, the primary advantage of Ni-rich HEAs lies in 
their broad compositional space. This enables the retention of the 
high-temperature strength characteristic of Ni-based superalloys 
while allowing signiﬁcant adjustments to other components to re- 
duce SFE, thereby achieving metastable engineering strengthen- 
ing. Additionally, this compositional ﬂexibility is beneﬁcial for op- 
timizing precipitate phases. These differences confer unique per- 
formance to our HEA in extreme environments ranging from cryo- 
genic to high temperatures. 
In this study, we have designed a nickel-rich HEA with the 
composition Ni44 Fe19 Co16 Cr9 V9 Al3 , the alloy exhibits low stacking 
fault energy at room temperature. The alloy composition is ob- 
tained through a series of screening steps. Detailed procedures 
and criteria are provided in the supplementary materials. As a 
result, a nickel-rich HEA featuring low SFE and coherent pre- 
cipitates was successfully obtained. Through precisely controlled 
heat treatment, a heterogeneous structure is obtained, capitaliz- 
ing on the strengths of HEAs. The resulting alloy demonstrates su- 
perior strength-ductility combinations not only at room tempera- 
ture but also at low temperatures (77 K) and high temperatures 
(873 and 1073 K). Additionally, the observation of obvious hys- 
teresis loops in the low-temperature and room-temperature cyclic 
loading–unloading curves provides robust evidence of the presence 
of back stress strengthening. This research aﬃrms the feasibility of 
employing multiple strengthening mechanisms to achieve a wide 
temperature range, high-strength, and high-toughness HEAs, pre- 
senting novel avenues for the development of such alloys. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Alloy preparation 
A mixed raw material composed of metal particles with a purity 
of 99.99 % Ni, Fe, Co, Cr, V, and Al was synthesized using a vacuum 
arc-melting furnace under a high-purity argon atmosphere. The re- 
sulting ingots underwent a minimum of ﬁve melting cycles and 
were subsequently air-cooled in a copper mold at 25 °C. The initial 
ingots weighed approximately 50 g and had dimensions of roughly 
30 mm in diameter and 15 mm in thickness. To eliminate elemen- 
tal segregation during solidiﬁcation, the alloys were then homoge- 
nized at 10 0 0 °C for 4 h. Following this, a 70 % thickness reduction 
cold rolling was performed, followed by static recrystallization at 
800 °C for 30 min. All heat treatments were conducted in a con- 
trolled environment, with water quenching to room temperature. 
2.2. Mechanical characterization 
The experimental process for testing the tensile properties of 
Ni44 Fe19 Co16 Cr9 V9 Al3 HEA at different tem perature conditions is 
detailed as follows: Dog bone-shaped plate specimens were pre- 
pared using electrical discharge machining, with varying sizes suit- 
able for speciﬁc temperature tests. Specimens for room tempera- 
ture (293 K) testing had dimensions of 14 mm length and a cross- 
section of 3 mm ×1 mm, while specimens for low temperature 
(77 K) testing measured 8 mm × 2 mm × 1.2 mm. To ensure re- 
liability, the specimens’ surfaces were meticulously polished using 
SiC paper up to 30 0 0 grit to minimize defects. Room temperature 
tensile tests were conducted using a UTM5305H 100 kN testing 
machine with strain gauge control at a strain rate of 1.0 × 10−3 
s−1 . For both low-temperature and high-temperature tests, a WDS- 
50 testing machine with a capacity of 100 kN was utilized, main- 
taining an engineering strain rate of 1.0 × 10−3 s−1 . Standard- 
sized specimens and a calibrated load sensor ensured accurate 
force measurements throughout the process, minimizing concerns 
regarding experimental errors. In the case of low-temperature ten- 
sile tests, the samples were fully immersed in liquid nitrogen at 
77 K during the stretching process. For high-temperature tensile 
tests, the specimens were placed in a high-temperature furnace 
with argon protection and raised to the target temperature at a 
heating rate of 10 K/s. After a 10-minute dwell time, the specimens 
were subjected to tension until fracture. A laser extensometer was 
employed for high-temperature strain measurement. To ensure re- 
producibility, three tests were conducted for each condition. 
Cyclic loading-unloading tensile tests were conducted at 77 
and 293 K to investigate the presence of back stress in the 
Ni44 Fe19 Co16 Cr9 V9 Al3 alloy. The gauge dimensions for these spec- 
imens are 10 mm × 2 mm × (1.0–1.2) mm. The initial unloading 
point is selected at the engineering strain of 2 % with subsequent 
cyclic strain controlled with intervals of 5 %. Each unloading point 
terminates at a load of 20 N. The cyclic tensile test persists until 
a fracture occurs. Throughout the experiment, the strain rates of 
loading and unloading are precisely controlled at 10−3 and 10−2 
s−1 , respectively.

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure 
Fig. 1 shows the microstructural analysis of the 
Ni44 Fe19 Co16 Cr9 V9 Al3 alloy obtained from the SEM/EBSD analysis. 
The representative inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) for the investigated 
HEAs is shown in Fig. 1 (a). As observed, the alloy is not fully re- 
crystallized, containing not only equiaxed grains but also deformed 
grains interspersed among them. The distributions of deformed 
grains, recrystallized grains, and substrctured grains can be seen in 
Fig. 1 (b). The recrystallized grain is conﬁrmed by the average grain 
orientation lower than 2 °, while the deformed grain is conﬁrmed 
by the average grain orientation higher than 6 °, between which 
is the substructured grain. Combined with Fig. 1 (c), the band 
contrast map featuring recrystallized grains in a light gray hue, 
it can be clearly seen that the recrystallization process is indeed 
incomplete. Fig. 1 (d) is a kernel average misorientation (KAM) map 
of the high-entropy alloy. The deformed grains appear to have 
larger kernel average misorientation angles of < 0.3 ° than the 
recrystallized grains at < 0.1 °. The kernel average misorientation 
angle measures for the local lattice tilt, e.g., caused by a dislocation 
defect, and thus is considered related to the dislocation density 
[ 28 ]. This observation suggests a higher dislocation density in the 
deformed grains than in the recrystallized grains. The proportion 
statistics of these grains are depicted in the bar chart in Fig. 1 (e), 
the ratio of recrystallized grains is 46 %, with substructured grains 
comprising 12 %, while the proportion of the original deformed 
grains stands at 42 %, this signiﬁes that the alloy features a 
partially recrystallized heterogeneous microstructure. The EDS 
mapping of the element distribution shown in Fig. 1 (f), preliminar- 
ily conﬁrms that the alloy does not exhibit signiﬁcant precipitate 
phase particles. A more detailed microstructure characterization 
was carried out using TEM, as shown in Fig. 2 .

3.2. Tensile properties across an extensive temperature spectrum 
Tensile mechanical properties of the Ni44 Fe19 Co16 Cr9 V9 Al3 HEA 
were evaluated over a broad temperature range, within a range of 
77 K to 1073 K. The engineering stress–strain curves, as depicted in 
Fig. 3 (a), indicate that at ambient temperature, the alloy achieves 
a tensile strength of approximately 1.28 GPa (1279 ±13 MPa) and 
demonstrates a ductility of around 33.6 % ±0.15 %, showcasing 
a notable combination of high strength and ductility. At a re- 
duced temperature of 77 K, the alloy’s tensile strength rises to 
roughly 1.6 GPa (1593 ±16 MPa), accompanied by an unusual in- 
crease in ductility to about 44.7 % ±0.5 %. Consistent with these 
high-strength ﬁgures, the alloy exhibits signiﬁcant strain hard- 
ening, reaching rates above 4.5 GPa at 77 K and 4 GPa at 
293 K, as illustrated in Fig. 3 (b). At higher temperatures, the 
Ni44 Fe19 Co16 Cr9 V9 Al3 HEA retains considerable tensile strength, 
with values surpassing 1 GPa (1032 ±20 MPa) at 873 K and main- 
taining 619 ±15 MPa at 1073 K. 
Fig. 3 (b) illustrates a distinct strain-hardening behavior of the 
alloy during deformation at 77 K. A notable increase in the strain- 
hardening rate is observed at approximately 7.5 %, followed by 
a gradual decrease compared to other curves. The curve also 
exhibits ﬂuctuations at approximately 10 %, 15 %, 22.5 %, and 
30 %, suggesting the activation of different deformation mecha- 
nisms from the initial one. Such a shift signiﬁcantly impacts on 
the alloy’s behavior, which will be further discussed in subsequent 
sections. 
To emphasize the substantial improvement in the performance 
of this HEA by multiple strengthening methods, other recently de- 
veloped structural alloys, such as ﬁbers [ 29–31 ], heterogeneous 
structures [ 32–35 ], precipitation-hardened alloys [ 36 , 37 ], single- 
phase HEAs [ 38 , 39 ], eutectic HEAs [ 40 , 41 ], TRIP steels [ 42 ], and 
austenitic steels [ 43 ] were compared with this HEA in terms of 
their tensile properties at 77 K, as shown in Fig. 4 (a). In the 
meantime, the mechanical properties of this HEA at 293 K were 
compared with those of cold-worked and nano-crystallized al- 
loys [ 39 , 44–46 ], TRIP steels [ 47 ], heterogeneous structured al- 
loys [ 48 ], multiple structured alloys [ 47 , 49 , 50 ], ﬁne-grained [ 51 , 52 ] 
and ultraﬁne-grained alloys [ 53 ], dual-phases and coarse-grained 
[ 38 , 39 , 49 , 54 ] alloys for the mechanical properties at 1073 K, 
we compared it with the current mainstream nickel-based high- 
temperature alloys [ 55–60 ], titanium alloys [ 61 ], steels [ 62–65 ], 
and other bulk high-temperature HEAs [ 51 ], as shown in Fig. 4 (b 
and c). The comparison results show that the mechanical proper- 
ties of this HEA at 77 and 293 K are superior to those of the cur- 
rent mainstream alloys, and the mechanical properties at 1073 K 
are comparable to those of nickel-based high-temperature alloys, 
indicating that the comprehensive utilization of composition ad- 
justment, microstructure control, and speciﬁc processing technol- 
ogy to enable the alloy to have different strengthening methods 
at different temperatures is an effective method to achieve cross- 
temperature mechanical properties.

To understand the high yield strength and pronounced strain hard-

stresses on ﬂow stress, loading-unloading-reloading (LUR) tests 
were conducted across different strain-hardening stages to explore 
the Bauschinger effect. Notably, at both 77 and 293 K, the HEA 
showed distinctive hysteresis loops in each LUR cycle, even near 
the yield point, as depicted in Fig. 5 (a and b), indicating a signiﬁ- 
cant Bauschinger effect. The reverse plastic strains for the HEAs at 
77 and 293 K initially increase to approximately 25 % and 15 %, 
respectively, and then diminish with further straining, demonstrat- 
ing the Bauschinger effect’s prominence during most strain phases. 
At 77 K, the near-yield-point effective stress and back stress in the 
alloy are considerably higher (447 and 812 MPa, respectively) than 
those at 293 K (305 and 684 MPa), as shown in Fig. 5 (c). Fig. 5 (d) 
reveals that the back stress contributes 70 % to the true stress near 
the yield point at 77 K, highlighting its role in the alloy’s high 
yield strength at this temperature. The alloy at 77 K experiences 
concurrent increases in back stress and effective stress up to their 
peak values of 1422 and 683 MPa, respectively, until failure. In con- 
trast, the effective stress of the HEA at 293 K continuously rises 
from 305 MPa to 684 MPa, while the back stress initially peaks at 
906 MPa at 14 % strain and then reduces to 788 MPa before frac- 
ture. The distribution of stress between back stress and effective 
stress is further illustrated in Fig. 5 (d). 
When the heterogeneous FCC HEAs were subjected to stress, 
the substructured grains yielded ﬁrst, followed by adjacent unde- 
formed recrystallized grains [ 45 ]. Due to the constraint imposed by 
the ﬁne recrystallized grains surrounding the substructured grains, 
dislocations in the former are impeded, leading to the formation of 
long-range back stress. This back stress hinders dislocation move- 
ment, effectively enhancing the yield strength of the substructured 
grains. Additionally, the higher critical stress required for yielding 
in deformed grains generates back stress within adjacent recrystal- 
lized grains, further bolstering the overall yield strength of the het- 
erogeneous material. This combination of factors contributes to the 
material’s high ductility and strain-hardening capability, preventing 
strain localization and promoting overall mechanical stability.

The grain sizes of both substructured and recrystallized grains 
show signiﬁcant variation, which requires considering both lower 
and upper limits to determine the extreme values for Hall–Petch 
strengthening. As shown in Table 1 , the calculated critical stress 
values for individual grains reveal that substructured grains exhibit 
critical stress values within the lower range, remaining below the 
yield strength. While some recrystallized grains have critical stress 
values that overlap with the entire range of substructured grains, 
the initial dislocation density in substructured grains is roughly ten 
times higher than in recrystallized grains. This means that dislo- 
cation slip occurs more easily in substructured grains, even when 
the critical stresses are similar, as the existing dislocations provide 
more nucleation sites for dislocation multiplication [ 45 ]. 
When the substructured grains begin to yield, stress concentra- 
tions develop in the neighboring undeformed recrystallized grains, 
causing them to undergo plastic deformation as well. This leads 
to the yielding of the entire sample. The ﬁne recrystallized grains 
surrounding the substructured grains exert a constraining effect, 
causing dislocations in the substructured grains to accumulate and 
become obstructed at the grain boundaries. This accumulation 
generates long-range back stress, which hinders dislocation mo- 
tion within the substructured grains, thus increasing their yield 
strength. Similarly, back stress also forms in the recrystallized 
grains adjacent to the deformed regions, as the yield stress in the 
deformed grains is much higher than in the recrystallized grains. 
Consequently, this back stress further elevates the yield strength 
of the heterogeneous HEA.

At 15 % strain, twin formation begins to occur in the sub- 
structured grains ( Fig. 7 (c)), which signiﬁcantly enhances strain 
hardening in heterogeneous structures [ 45 , 71 ]. Typically, twinning 
happens in grains with low dislocation density, where twin nu- 
cleation precedes dislocation slip. However, in high-entropy al- 
loys, deformation twins can form in grains with high dislocation 
densities due to the increased twinning stress in these materi- 
als [ 45 ]. The higher twinning stress in HEAs is likely a result of 
their higher critical resolved shear stress compared to conventional 
alloys [ 45 , 72 ]. For twinning to occur, a suﬃciently high disloca- 
tion density is required to achieve strain hardening before reaching 
the critical twinning stress. Therefore, the formation of deforma- 
tion twins in the substructured grains, combined with back-stress 
hardening, leads to an increase in the strain hardening rate at this 
stage. 
When the strain increases to 22.5 %, dislocations accumulate at 
the grain boundaries between deformed and recrystallized grains, 
initiating slip within the recrystallized grains. This slip increases 
the dislocation density inside the recrystallized grains, leading to 
an increase in internal stress. Consequently, a signiﬁcant number of 
deformation twins begin to form to accommodate the strain. These 
twins serve to redistribute the strain and reduce internal stress. 
The dislocations generated by the slip accumulate within the de- 
formation twins, further enhancing the strain hardening rate [ 72 ]. 
At a strain of 30 %, the deformation mechanisms in the HEA un- 
dergo signiﬁcant changes. On one hand, the increased nucleation 
of dislocations at the grain boundaries between deformed and 
recrystallized grains activates a dislocation slip-dominated plastic 
deformation mechanism. This leads to the formation of numer- 
ous slip bands within the recrystallized grains [ 70 ]. On the other 
hand, twinning activity becomes more pronounced within the re- 
crystallized grains, increasing twin density and their interweaving. 
The dense nanostructure of these twins effectively obstructs the 
movement of dislocations, thereby further enhancing the strain- 
hardening effect [ 73 ]. 
As the stress increases to fracture, TEM images in Fig. 7 (f) re- 
veal that the dislocation accumulation and entanglement within 
the deformation twins are heterogeneous. The dislocation tangling 
exhibits a locally concentrated morphology, which partitions the 
twins into regions with high dislocation density and regions with 
low dislocation density. This leads to the destruction and frag- 
mentation of the twin structure. The fragmentation of deformation 
twins can, on one hand, potentially enhance the back stress in the 
alloy, thereby maintaining a relatively stable back stress in the HEA 
before fracture. On the other hand, it can simultaneously improve 
both the strength and ductility of the material [ 74 ]. This dual ef- 
fect will be discussed in greater detail in the subsequent CPF sim- 
ulations.

is closely related to the width of the twin, and the energy bar-

formation of polycrystalline systems containing twins under uniax- 
ial tensile loading. However, there are several key differences be- 
tween the simulation conditions and actual experiments. The PFC 
method assumes a two-dimensional triangular lattice and simpli-

actual experiments, deformation is three-dimensional and local-

erogeneity, and sample geometry. Additionally, the simulation does 
not account for factors such as thermal activation, strain rate ef- 
fects, or stress state evolution, which are crucial in the actual de- 
formation process. Despite these limitations, the phase-ﬁeld crystal 
method is still able to capture twin behavior and theoretically re- 
produce complex phenomena, such as the formation of nanoscale 
twins, which are observed in experiments. 
5. Conclusions 
Through strategic compositional design and microstructural en- 
gineering, we developed an FCC HEA with exceptional mechani- 
cal performance spanning cryogenic to elevated temperatures (77–
1073 K). By modulating SFE, introducing coherent γ′ precipitates, 
and incorporating nickel as a stabilizing element alongside a con- 
trolled partial recrystallization process, we achieved a hierarchi- 
cal microstructure that activates distinct deformation mechanisms 
across varying temperature regimes. 
(1) At cryogenic temperatures (77 K), deformation progresses 
through a sequence of strain-dependent mechanisms: initial 
dislocation slip transitions to dislocation pile-ups at grain 
boundaries, generating back-stress hardening. Subsequent strain 
accumulation triggers deformation twinning within substruc- 
tured grains, which evolves into twin fragmentation at higher 
strains. This hierarchical twin-dislocation interaction enhances 
strain hardening while preserving ductility, yielding a synergis- 
tic strength-ductility combination. 
(2) At ambient temperature (293 K), hetero-deformation-induced 
hardening dominates, driven by GND accumulation at hetero- 
geneous interfaces. This mechanism sustains a high strain- 
hardening rate, effectively suppressing plastic instabilities and 
enabling extended uniform elongation. 
(3) Under elevated temperatures (873 K to 1073 K), dynamic 
precipitation-dislocation interactions govern deformation. At 
873 K, the Portevin-Le Chatelier effect emerges due to γ′ 
precipitate-mediated dislocation pinning. At 1073 K, partial dis- 
location recovery and precipitate bypass mechanisms coexist, 
while stacking faults persist as supplementary strengthening 
features. 
This work demonstrates that a compositionally and struc- 
turally graded HEA design, incorporating SFE optimization, 
nanoscale precipitation, and heterogeneous grain structures, fa- 
cilitates temperature-adaptive activation of complementary defor- 
mation mechanisms. The synergy of dislocation slip, twinning, 
hetero-deformation-induced hardening, and precipitate-dislocation 
interactions creates a multi-mechanistic framework that ensures 
temperature-independent mechanical robustness. This approach 
offers a strategy for designing advanced metallic alloys that main- 
tain a balanced combination of strength, ductility, and thermal sta- 
bility in extreme service conditions.
