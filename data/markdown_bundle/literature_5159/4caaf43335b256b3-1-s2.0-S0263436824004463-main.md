ABSTRACT
TiC-reinforced Mo – Si – B alloys (designated as MoSiBTiC alloys) exhibit great potential for application in the
aerospace industry, due to their high melting point, moderate density and attractive high-temperature strength
and creep resistance. Nevertheless, further material design and industrialization requires more systematic un-
derstanding of the phase diagram and solidification paths of Mo – Mo
5
SiB
2
– TiC pseudo-ternary system. In this
study, an advanced ultra-high temperature thermal analysis method employing blackbody radiation and an
electromagnetic levitation technique for in situ observation of the solidification process, were utilized to
experimentally investigate the solidification process and reaction temperature of alloys in the vicinity of the
Mo – Mo
5
SiB
2
– TiC ternary eutectic point. On the basis of the experimental results, three vertical sections of phase
diagram at 9 mol% Mo
5
SiB
2
, 10 mol% TiC, and 70 mol% Mo
ss
isopleths in the Mo – Mo
5
SiB
2
– TiC pseudo-ternary
system were established. The liquidus surface projection on the Mo – Mo
5
SiB
2
– TiC system has also been recon-
structed, with the addition of a new Mo
2
B primary phase region and a larger Mo
5
SiB
2
primary phase region than
previously reported.
1. Introduction
Refractory metal-based alloys exhibit strong potential as high-
temperature structural materials for some heat components in such
high-performance power generation or air transportation because of the
very high melting point [ 1 – 6 ]. TiC-reinforced Mo – Si – B alloys, desig-
nated as MoSiBTiC alloys, are a promising candidate which is attributed
to their impressive high-temperature strength and creep resistance
[ 7 – 10 ], as well as good room-temperature fracture toughness [ 11 ]. In
this material, the incorporation of TiC has been demonstrated to
enhance both the high-temperature strength and the room-temperature
fracture toughness [ 8 – 14 ]. This is achieved through the formation of a
fine Mo
ss
(molybdenum solid solution) – Mo
5
SiB
2
(T
2
) – TiC ternary
eutectic microstructure, which exhibits a continuous Mo
ss
phase [ 11 ].
Nevertheless, it is still unclear how TiC is involved in the solidification
process and influences microstructure formation. Previous reports
indicated that the microstructure of the MoSiBTiC alloys is the result of
numerous solidification steps in which ternary and/or quaternary
eutectic reactions are involved, and can change extensively under varied
compositions and heat treatment conditions [ 9 , 10 , 15 , 16 ]. Therefore, for
the further optimization and industrialization of the MoSiBTiC alloys, it
is necessary to determine basic data including solidification pathways
and phase diagrams.
Because of the ultra-high temperature point ( > 1900
◦
C) and
complicated solidification process of the MoSiBTiC alloys, the phase
diagram is difficult to investigate using differential thermal analysis
(DTA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), which are commonly
used for high-accuracy thermal analysis at temperatures ≤ 1600
◦
C. An
advanced ultra-high temperature thermal analysis method employing
blackbody radiation was developed by Fukuyama et al. [ 16 ] and re-
ported that the solidification process of MoSiBTiC alloys can be ther-
mally analyzed in the temperature range of 1084 – 1953
◦
C with a
maximum error of 5
◦
C [ 16 ]. In this method, a graphite sample holder
with a blackbody cavity was designed and fabricated, and the blackbody
radiation emitted from the cavity was measured by a radiation pyrom-
eter to determine the sample temperature using Planck ’ s law.

2. Experimental procedure
Three groups of 11 different MoSiBTiC alloys were produced by non-
consumable arc-melting method in an argon atmosphere. High purity
Mo rod (99.99 wt%), Si chip (99.99 wt%), B chip (99.5 wt%) and TiC
powder (99 wt%, 2 – 5 μ m) were used as starting materials. Before
melting the samples, pure titanium was melted to remove residual O
2
and N
2
impurities in the chamber. Each button ingot was flipped over
and remelted more than five times to ensure homogeneity. Table 1
summarizes the detailed nominal compositions of the alloys. Fig. 1 il-

5

surface projection reported in previous work [ 16 ]. Three groups are
located on the isopleth of 9 mol% Mo
5

Ultra-high temperature thermal analysis was performed in a radio
frequency induction heating furnace (RF furnace). The scheme is shown
in Fig. 2 (a). Samples were contained into a CaO-stabilized ZrO
2
crucible
with a blackbody cavity and then heated and cooled at a rate of 10
◦
C/
min in the RF furnace under an Ar flow at 10 mL/min. Before cooling,
each sample was held above the liquidus temperature for 30 min to
ensure a homogeneous melting. The heating and cooling cycle was
repeated more than three times to ensure reproducibility. The ultra-high
temperature analyzer was equipped with upper and lower radiation
pyrometers: the upper pyrometer monitored radiation from the black-
body cavity to measure the sample temperature T
s
, and the lower py-
rometer monitored the susceptor temperature T
r
for furnace
temperature control and reference. The thermal analysis apparatus was
carefully calibrated using the melting point of pure copper [ 21 ], the
eutectic temperatures of ruthenium – carbon [ 22 ] and nickel – carbon
samples [ 22 ]. The accuracy of the temperature measurements was
estimated to be within ± 0.4 % over the temperature range of
1084.62 – 1953
◦
C. Additional information regarding the principle of
ultra-high temperature thermal analysis can be found in the work of
Fukuyama et al. [ 16 ].
To in situ observe the solidification process of the melt as well as the
microstructure variation under different thermal histories, the EML
technique was used in this work. Fig. 2 (b) shows the scheme of the EML
apparatus. To prevent the oxidation of samples during test, the chamber
was initially evacuated to 10
 2
Pa and then filled with Ar – 5 vol% H
2
gas
at a rate of 3 L/min. The sample was levitated by applying an alternating
electric current to the levitation coil and then melted by induction
heating with an additional heating laser (power of 8 – 140 W, wavelength
of 807 nm). A 10 T static magnetic field was also applied to suppress
both translational motion and convection of sample droplet. The droplet
was held at 2200
◦
C for 75 s to ensure complete melting, and was rapidly
solidified by switching off the heating laser and then blowing He gas.
The sample droplet was recorded during rapid cooling using a high-
resolution charge-coupled device (CCD) camera (Mikrotron, MC1310)
with 512 × 512 pixels at 60 frames per second. The multiple light
emissions from the alloy melt during solidification due to the release of
latent heat (recalescence) were carefully identified. A monochromatic
pyrometer (IMPAC Pyrometers: spectral range 1.45 – 1.8 μ m, tempera-
ture range 450 – 2500
◦
C) was used to measure the sample temperature.
Calibration of this pyrometer has been previously conducted at the
liquidus temperature of the MoSiBTiC alloys. In addition to the rapid
cooling test, the sample droplet was also held for 10 min at the tem-
perature immediately following the recalescence peak was observed.
This holding period enables different phase to grow and contributes to
identifying the solidification path comprehensively. The solidified
microstructure was then characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker
D2 Phaser) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL JCM-5700)
equipped with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX).

3.2. Solidification behaviour of MoSiBTiC alloys by EML
In this section, the solidification process of MoSiBTiC alloys was
investigated using the EML technique. During the solidification process,

4. Discussion
During the cooling process, when a solidification reaction occurs, the
accompanying release of latent heat appears as an inflection point in the
cooling curve of thermal analysis and as a transient emission of light
(recalescence) in the EML in situ observation test. However, if two or
more reactions are close in temperature, or if the latent heat released is
minimal due to their small fraction, the inflection point and the reca-
lescence may not perfectly coincide or accurately represent all reactions.
Considering these factors, the solidification paths of the MoSiBTiC alloys
are discussed according to inflection points, recalescences and solidified
microstructure observations. In addition, since no intermetallic com-
pound is formed by the reaction between Si, B and TiC, this means that
