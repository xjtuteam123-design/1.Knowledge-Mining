1. Introduction 
The Zr-2.5 wt% Nb alloy is used for pressure 
tubes [I] in CANDU* power reactors. Here we report 
on the creep at 573 K and 723 K of quenched and 
aged Zr-2.5 wt% Nb alloy as a function of: 
(i) solution temperature in the range 1073 to 
1273 K, 
(ii) oxygen concentration in the range 170 to 
1150 ppm, 
(iii) aging at 673 K or 773 K. 
The effects of cooling rate from the solution temper- 
ature and grain size of the prior beta-phase on creep 
and hardness changes during testing are discussed. A 
qualitative correlation of microstructure and creep 
behaviour is presented.

2. The Zr-2.5 wt% Nb alloy 
The (a! + 0) phase region (fig. 1) for low-oxygen 
(100 ppm) Zr-2.5 wt% Nb has been reported to lie 
between about 880 and 1100 K [2-41. Oxygen is an 
effective o-stabilizer; 0.10 wt% (1000 ppm) in solu- 
tion raises the /I-transus by about 70 K [4]. 
On rapid quenching from temperatures above 
1070 K the bee fl-Zr phase transforms by a martensi- 
tic transformation to the cph tr’ phase with the form 
of the martensite determined by the quenching rate. 
It has been shown [5] that cooling an alloy containing 
2.5 wt% Nb and 0.1 wt% 0, at rates > 770 K . s-l 
results in transformation of the SZr to microtwinned 
(Y’ plates, whereas for cooling rates in the range about 
510-770 K . s-l the transformation is by slip rather 
than twinning. The untwinned ct’ plates, which have 
a high dislocation density, tend to be somewhat 
smaller than the twinned plates.

3. Experimental 
Specimens were taken from 12.7 mm diameter 
rods of Zr-2.5 wt% Nb. The niobium and oxygen 
analyses of these alloys and the P-transus tempera- 
tures are given in table 1. 
The heat-treatments were carried out in evacuated 
quartz capsules on rod sections prior to machining 
them into creep specimens. All specimens other than 
those described in subsect. 4.4 were held 0.25 h at 
the solution temperature and quenched into water at 
a cooling rate of about 2000 K . s-l. Based on Wil- 
liams and Gilbert’s [S] correlations between micro- 
structures and hardness, the quenched specimens were 
judged acceptable for testing if their Vickers hardness 
fell within + 10 points of the mean value expected for 
twinned martensitic structures from the work of 
Kench et al. [4] and Williams and Gilbert [5]. Other 
details of the heat-treatments are described in sect. 4. 
The creep tests were carried out in Instron con; 
stant-load creep machines under an argon atmosphere. 
The specimen gauge was 25.4 mm long and 4.07 mm 
diameter. The strain was measured with dial gauges of 
5 X 10M4 mm sensitivity. The specimens were kept at 
the test temperature for about 20 h prior to loading and 
were maintained at that temperature to * 1.5 K dur- 
ing the test.

The difference in the creep behaviour of specimens 
aged at 673 K compared to those aged at 773 K may 
be explained on a model proposed by McLean [24]. 
For a constant volume fraction of second phase he 
suggested a three-stage dependence of creep rate on 
particle size. At very small particle sizes McLean pre- 
dicts that the creep rate will decrease with increasing 
particle size. In this range the dislocations are assumed 
to move by pushing the particles ahead of them. 
With larger, closely spaced particles where the dis- 
locations can no longer push the particles ahead and 
have not got the space to bow around, the dislocations 
must either cut or climb over the particles before they 
can glide. Under these circumstances the creep rate 
will be unaffected by particle growth on aging so long 
as dislocations cannot loop between particles. How- 
ever, as the particles continue to grow eventually dis- 
location bowing becomes the controlling creep mech- 
anism. If the rate of creation of dislocations at a source 
does not change appreciably on aging, it can be shown 
that the creep rate should then be proportional to d2, 
where d = diameter of particles.
