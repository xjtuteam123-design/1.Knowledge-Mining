Abstract
As cast FeCrCuMnNi high entropy alloys were heat treated at different temperatures and 
times. Structure and mechanical properties of the heat treated alloy were studied using X-Ray 
diffraction, tensile test, hardness test and scanning electron microscopy. It has been found 
that the FeCrCuMnNi has thermal stability in mechanical properties almost up to 600 °C. 
Nevertheless, the alloy revealed an unusual behavior after heat treating at higher 
temperatures. A sharp drop of the tensile strength, strain to fracture and work hardening 
capability occurred at temperatures between 700 °C and 800 °C due to formation of a hard σ 
phase in the alloy. The hardness follows a plateau in the 700-800 °C range and then softening 
continues, together with a decrease in strength and increase in ductility, due to dissolution of 
the hard σ phase and decreased amount of BCC phase.
Keywords: FeCrCuMnNi high entropy alloy, Heat treatment, Mechanical properties, Strain 
hardening
1. Introduction
In recent years, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) with multiple principal elements in equimolar or 
near-equimolar ratios, has been developed by Yeh et al. [1]. These alloys are defined as 
alloys with 5 to 13 major elements, where the molar ratio of each element is from 5 to 35 at% 
[2, 3]. According to Boltzmann’s hypothesis based on the regular solution model, HEAs 
revealed high entropy of mixing in liquid state or regular solution state [1, 4]. Therefore, 
these alloys mostly consist of simple solid solution structures such as face-centered cubic

(FCC), body-centered cubic (BCC) and/or hexagonal closed-pack (HCP) instead of ordered 
phases or complex intermetallics [1, 5, 6]. Therefore, HEAs systems exhibit excellent high-
temperature behavior, mechanical properties, and corrosion resistance, and they are 
promising materials for industrial applications [7-10].
In the past few years, a large numbers of studies have been reported on investigation of 
mechanical properties of single-phase alloys [11-13]. Basically, single-phase HEAs have 
been found difficult to reach a reasonable balance between strength and (tensile) ductility 
[14]. Therefore, a mixture of these phases could lead to an interesting compromise between 
hardness and ductility. In addition, few research has been conducted to optimize the 
mechanical properties of dual phase HEAs [14-16]. Recently, FeCrCuMnNi alloy was 
reported as a new HEA with mixture of FCC and BCC phases; however; did not go through a 
sufficient investigation of its different properties [16, 17].
The annealing process is also very important in improving the microstructure and properties 
of the alloys [18]. It has been reported that different properties of the HEAs are directly 
affected by annealing treatments [7, 18-20]. Zhang et al. [19] and Wen et al. [7] in different 
researches reported that annealing of the CoCrFeNiCuAl HEA leads to transformation of 
BCC phase to FCC and reduction in compressive strength. It was reported by Zhuang et al. 
[18] that increasing the annealing temperature, reduced the compressive strength of the 
FeCoNiCuAl HEA, while increasing the annealing time up to 13 h at 1093 K enhanced it. 
Precipitation of a FCC phase in the alloy structure was the main reason for this enhancement 
[18]. Precipitation and hardness increase during annealing was also reported in other HEA 
systems [8, 9, 20].
In this paper, mechanical properties of the FeCrCuMnNi high entropy alloy following 
different heat treatment regimes were investigated. The produced as cast alloy was heat

The strain hardening behavior was analyzed, and fracture mechanisms were studied using

2. Experimental procedure
Alloy ingots with nominal composition of equiatomic FeCrCuMnNi were produced by 
vacuum induction melting process. The casting was done using pure (more than 99.85%) raw 
materials, under 1 atm of high-purity argon atmosphere. In addition, the ingots were remelted 
to ensure the compositional homogeneity. Specimens with dimensions of 100 mm × 10 mm × 
1 mm were cut from the as cast ingot and heat treated in different regimes. Samples were heat 
treated at temperatures of 500 °C, 600 °C, 700 °C, 800 °C, 900 °C and 1000 °C for 4 hours to 
evaluate the effects of heat treatment temperature. In addition, samples were heat treated for 
1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 hours at the temperature of 800 °C in order to investigate the effects of heat 
treatment time.
Using a PHILIPS X-ray diffractometer (XRD) with Cu Kα (λ = 0.15406 nm) diffraction 
generated at 40 kV and 30 mA, the crystal structure was investigated. In order to study the 
variations of mechanical properties of the produced alloy via different heat treatment 
regimes, tensile tests were carried out on the samples. The tensile test samples were machined 
by EDM from the heat treated sheets. The gauge width and length of the tensile test samples 
were 2.5 and 6.4 mm, respectively. The tensile tests were performed on a Hounsﬁeld H25KS 
testing machine. In addition, variation of the hardness was studied using Vickers hardness 
tests, under a load of 10kg and time of 30 seconds. Eight hardness tests were averaged on 
each sample. Fracture surfaces of the tensile samples were analyzed using a scanning electron 
microscope (SEM) and an optical microscope (OM).

3. Result and discussion
Fig. 1 reveals the XRD patterns of the as cast and heat treated alloys at different 
temperatures. In addition, XRD patterns of the heat treated samples at 800°C, at different 
times, are shown in Fig. 2. The following comments apply:
1. The as cast structure of the alloy consists of three different phases, 2 FCC phases and 
1 BCC.
2. By increasing the heat treatment temperature, the peak intensity of the FCC1 and 
FCC2 phases decrease and increase, respectively.
3. Increasing the heat treatment temperature leads to the progressive dissolution of 
almost all BCC phase content in the alloy.
4. A σ phase with composition Cr 5Fe6Mn8 forms during heat treatment at 800 °C; it 
dissolves at higher temperature.
5. Heat treatment time beyond 1h does have a strong impact on the structure, at 800 °C. 
However, both FCC1 and BCC phases volume fractions are reduced.
6. The σ phase remains stable in time at 800 °C.
Fig. 3 presents the microstructures of the as cast and heat treated FeCrCuMnNi alloy at 
different temperatures. BCC phase could be detected as dendrite regions (see Fig. 3a). The 
gray regions around the dendrites are marked as FCC2 phase, and the matrix of the alloy 
(interdendrite regions) is named FCC1 phase. The alloy dendritic structure is almost stable 
after heat treatment at temperatures up to 800 °C (Figs. 3a to c). However, the alloy structure 
entirely changes when reaching 1000 °C. Fig. 3c shows that at 800 °C, the growth of the 
FCC2 phase and dissolution of the BCC phase occurred. At 800 °C the FCC2 phase starts 
growing around the dendrite regions (BCC phase). After heat treatment at 1000 °C, the

dendritic structure disappears. Dendrite regions (BCC phase) dissolve into the alloy matrix 
and the FCC2 phase spherodizes.
Fig. 4a depicts the tensile stress strain curves of the as cast and heat treated FeCrCuMnNi 
alloy, at different temperatures for 4 hours. It can be seen that, for heat treatment 
temperatures below 600 °C, the alloy keeps similar mechanical properties. For higher 
temperatures, these properties are considerably reduced. This behavior has been noted in 
other HEAs as well [5, 7, 9, 10, 18-20].
Variations of the yield strength (σY) ultimate tensile strength (σUTS) and strain to failure of the 
FeCrCuMnNi alloy as a function of the heat treatment temperature are shown in Fig. 4b. At 
800 °C, σY, σUTS and strain to failure decrease to 530 MPa, 678 MPa and 4.2 %, which 
represents, compared to the as cast sample, a 15%, 29% and a 71 % decrease, respectively. At 
higher temperatures, ductility increases again, while the strength follows a kind of plateau 
before going down. The abnormal behavior observed at 800 °C can be related to the 
following factors:
a. Formation of the σ phase. This phase with composition of Cr 5Fe6Mn8 forms in the in a 
temperature interval close to 800 °C. The σ phase has a tetragonal structure with 
lattice constants of a = 9.09 Å and c = 9.99 Å and results from the transformation of 
the BCC phase [8]. It is known to be very hard (~1300 HV) [8], and can be associated 
to early failure, during the tensile test since this phase forms in small size and can 
impedes dislocation motion. 
b. Dissolving of the BCC phase. Generally, BCC structured HEAs have higher strength 
and reduced ductility, compared to FCC structured HEAs [2, 21]. Therefore, 
decreasing the BCC phase amount leads to a softer material. Solution hardening in the 
BCC structure is stronger, and this might be related to the difference in coordination

number (8), compared to the FCC case (12), leading to a higher fraction of pairs with 
unlike atoms, and therefore to a higher lattice distortion [4]. Besides, (110) planes in 
BCC structures are known to have a smaller interplanar spacing and higher lattice 
friction for dislocation motion, compared to (111) planes in FCC structures [7, 22].
c. Grain growth and stress relieve [23]. Generally, grain growth and stress relieve occur 
in as cast metallic samples due to high temperature heat treatment.
Enhancement of the strain to failure at heat treatment temperatures higher than 800 °C is 
attributed to the dissolution of the σ phase, decrease of the BCC phase volume fraction, and 
softening of the alloy due to heat treatment at high temperature, which was explained as grain 
growth and stress relieve. The latter two phenomena decrease the alloy strength, whereas the 
disappearance of the hard σ phase has the opposite effect, and is responsible for the slight 
strength increase observed at 900 °C.
Fig. 5a reveals the tensile stress strain curves of the as cast and heat treated FeCrCuMnNi 
alloy, considering a temperature of 800 °C, and different heat treatment times. Increasing the 
heat treatment time has little effects on the alloy mechanical properties, which is well 
reflected in Fig. 5b showing σY, σUTS and strain to failure as a function of time. The 3% 
decrease in σY and σUTS resulting from the increase in heat treatment time from 1 to 16 hours 
is associated to the reduction in BCC phase amount, evidenced in Fig. 2, and to the softening 
of the alloy due to high temperature heat treatment.
Variation of the hardness of FeCrCuMnNi via different heat treatment temperatures and times 
are shown in Fig. 6. Fig. 6a shows a plateau between 600 and 800 °C, which is attributed to 
formation of the σ phase.

The variation of hardness with heat treatment time given in Fig. 6b is connected to the 
dissolution of the BCC phase and the softening of the alloy due to heat treatment at high 
temperature, as already discussed for Figs. 4 and 5. 
Comparing Figs. 4b and 5b to Fig. 6 reveals that the hardness and Y.S. curves match well; 
however, for heat treated sample at 900 °C for 4 hours and heat treated samples for 1 and 2 
hours at 800 °C, hardness and Y.S. reveals different variations. These phenomena could be 
explained as follow:
a) Based on XRD patterns in Fig. 1, at temperature 900 °C the σ phase dissolves into the 
alloy matrix. It was explained that the presence of this phase can increase the hardness and 
decrease the strength. Therefore, σ phase dissolution at 900 °C leads to decrease in hardness 
and increase in strength.
b) Heat treatment of the alloy at 800 °C for 1 and 2 hours leads to formation of σ phase as 
shown in XRD patterns (see Fig. 2); therefore, the hardness of the alloy increases where the 
strength decreases. However, by increasing heat treatment time the effect of high temperature 
heat treatment leads to reducing in σ phase effects on hardness and strength.

in Fig. 8. The strain hardening behavior of the alloy is shown to depend on heat treatment 
temperature and time. The as cast sample (Fig. 8a) exhibits two regimes. In the first step, 
softer phase (FCC1) deform plastically and the harder phases are in the elastic-plastic regime 
[27, 28], which leads to a low strain hardening rate. In this step the FCC1 phase carry most of 
the deformation, while transferring load to the two other phases. In the second step, both FCC 
phases deform plastically while, BCC phase deformation is elastic, which results in a more 
non-homogeneous deformation in the microstructure, with therefore an increased production 
of geometrically necessary dislocations, leading to an increased strain hardening rate 
compared to the first step [27, 28]. 
As can be seen in Figs. 8b and c, almost all the samples reveal the same strain hardening 
behavior with two different steps; however, the rate of hardening seems to vary with heat 
treatment. Fig. 8b illustrates that by increasing heat treatment temperature the rate of 
hardening decreases in both steps. As explained in previous sections, it could be related to 
dissolution of BCC phase, growth of softer phases, Grain growth and stress relaxation. 
Furthermore, formation of σ phase led to embrittlement of the sample heat treated at 800 °C 
and decrease in amount of plastic deformation and hardening. The third step in sample heat 
treated at 1000 °C is related to necking during the tensile test, and reveals that no strain

The strain hardening behavior of the alloy as a function of the heat treatment time at 800 °C, 
is shown in Fig. 8c. The two-steps behavior shown earlier prevails for all heat treatment 
times; however, a decrease in strain hardening rate can be seen by increasing heat treatment 
time. The fluctuations of the very hard σ phase, and other BCC and FCC phases shown in 
Fig. 2 explain the changes in stress level, but do not change the overall behavior.
In order to study the fracture mechanisms of the alloy after heat treatment, the fracture 
surfaces were studied using SEM and OM. Fig. 9 depicts the micrographs after failure. As 
cast sample reveals a mostly ductile fracture. This is usually evidenced by crack growth with 
excessive plastic deformation, slow tearing of the metal and formation of rough fracture 
surface with equiaxed, deep dimples [29, 30]. In Fig. 9a, equiaxed and deep dimples are 
formed in the fracture surface of the as cast sample. The fracture surface of the sample heat 
treated at 600 °C illustrates almost the same mechanism; nevertheless, cracks propagate 
between FCC and BCC phases (Dendrite regions), along the contact surfaces of the FCC and 
BCC phases, as marked in Fig. 9b. After heat treatment at 800 °C, Fig. 9c shows a brittle 
fracture, i.e. a smooth surface without formation of dimples, low amount of plastic 
deformation, cleavage facets and striations [31]. This variation in the fracture mechanism is 
due to the formation of the hard σ phase. After heat treatment at 1000 °C, a completely 
ductile fracture is recovered, as seen in Fig. 9d, with large amount of plastic deformation and 
equiaxed, deep dimples formed on the rough fracture surface.
4. Conclusions

## Page 12

ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
11
The FeCrCuMnNi high entropy alloy was heat treated in different regimes in order to study 
the effects of heat treatment temperature and heat treatment time on the mechanical 
properties of the alloy. Using XRD, SEM, tensile and hardness tests, variations in structure 
and mechanical properties of the alloy were studied. The following conclusions could be 
drawn:
1. Dendritic structure of the alloy remained stable almost up to 800 °C; however, 
dendritic regions (BCC phase) dissolved into the alloy matrix at higher temperatures. 
Furthermore, FCC2 phase spherodized around the remained BCC particles.
2. Tensile strength is reduced by increasing the heat treatment temperature, due to 
dissolution of the BCC phase and softening (grain growth and stress relieve) of the 
alloy. A remarkable decrease in σUTS and strain to failure is attributed to formation of 
the σ (Cr5Fe6Mn8) hard phase, in a temperature interval close to 800 °C.
3. The strain hardening behavior of the alloy before / after heat treatment revealed two 
different regimes, which are distinguished by the way plastic strain is distributed in 
the different phases. 
4. Fractography results revealed that the alloy has an almost ductile fracture in the as 
cast state, and after a 600 °C heat treatment. The alloy becomes brittle after heat 
treatment at 800 °C, but recovers full ductility after heat treatment at 1000 °C.
