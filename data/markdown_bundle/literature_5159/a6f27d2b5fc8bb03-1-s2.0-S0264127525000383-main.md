ABSTRACT
Friction stir welding (FSW) of high temperature materials such as ferrous alloys, Ti-alloys, and Ni-alloys is 
challenging due to the high costs, short tool lives, and limited availability of tool materials. Commercially 
available cermets such as WC/Co are relatively cost-effective, yet their performance is largely compromised by 
subpar high temperature properties of the Co binder. To address this issue, an integrated computational materials 
engineering (ICME) approach was applied to design a WC/HEA cermet with high entropy alloy (HEA) binder for 
better mechanical properties and high temperature performance. The WC/HEA cermet was fabricated by powder 
metallurgy route using mechanical alloying and mixing followed by spark plasma sintering (SPS). The SPS- 
sintered WC/HEA cermet possessed good room temperature hardness-fracture toughness synergy and hot 
hardness property. FSW tools were fabricated using the WC/HEA material and the tools were benchmarked 
against a high strength low alloy grade 50 (HSLA-50) steel. The tools performed with no observed chemical 
interaction with the workpiece material. The HSLA-50 material showed improved microstructure, hardness, and 
tensile properties post FSW. This study is the first proof-of-concept for the design, fabrication, testing, and 
evaluation of a novel cermet FSW tool material with low-cost and high-performance synergy for high temper -
ature FSW application.
1. Introduction
Friction stir welding (FSW) was invented in 1991 at The Welding 
Institute (TWI) in the United Kingdom. It has been over three decades 
since this technology disrupted the world of welding by offering an 
attractive alternative to fusion welding [1] . The initial years of research 
on FSW were dedicated to joining various Al and Mg alloys, mainly for 
the automotive and aerospace sectors [2] . Interest in FSW of high- 
temperature materials such as ferrous alloys, Ti-alloys, and Ni-alloys 
picked up in the late 1990s/early 2000s after the success seen with Al 
and Mg alloys [3 – 5] . Since then, the major limiting factor for FSW of 
high temperature materials has revolved around tool materials that 
could sustain properties in adverse environments. The key 
considerations for the high temperature tool materials have been: (i) the 
tool material should be chemically inert to the workpiece material and 
the welding atmosphere, (ii) the tool material should have a good 
combination of strength, fracture toughness, and fatigue properties at 
both room temperature and elevated temperatures, (iii) the tool material 
should have good wear resistance, and (iv) the tool material should have 
microstructural and mechanical stability over a wide range of temper -
atures to resist cracking, creep, and thermal fatigue during the FSW 
process [6] . Other considerations include cost, machineability, and 
reusability. So far, various tool materials have been developed and 
tested for high temperature FSW [7] , including polycrystalline cubic 
boron nitride (PCBN) [8,9] , tungsten alloys (W-Re, W-Re-HfC, W-La, W-

Among ferrous alloys, high-strength low-alloy (HSLA) grade steels 
are an important class of materials that are extensively used in the 
shipbuilding industry [21 – 23] . HSLA steels were originally developed to 
improve the strength-to-weight ratio of low-carbon steels through 
microalloying additions, thus the HSLA steels provide a combination of 
high strength, ductility, toughness, formability, improved corrosion 
resistance, weldability, and superior impact toughness at low tempera -
tures [24] . HSLA steels are typically produced in the form of a bar or 
sheet using thermomechanical controlled processing (TMCP), where the 
material is subjected to multiple hot rolling passes followed by 
controlled cooling [25] .
Joining of HSLA steels is inevitable due to their wide structural ap -
plications in the shipbuilding sector. Several fusion welding processes 
have been employed to join HSLA steels, however, there are some 
inherent disadvantages of fusion welding of HSLA steels such as: (i) 
considerable loss of strength and toughness in the heat affected zone 
(HAZ); the HAZ is also vulnerable to hydrogen embrittlement, (ii) a high 
tensile residual stress, resulting from rapid, localized heating and cool -
ing, can lead to accelerated cracking behavior, and (iii) the high tem -
peratures associated with fusion welding can lead to growth of prior 
austenite grains which result in coarse ferrite and bainite structures 
inside the weld nugget leading to loss of strength [26 – 30] . FSW can be 
utilized to join HSLA steels while overcoming the inherent disadvan -
tages of fusion welding. The first attempt of joining steels using FSW was 
published in 1998 [8] . The advances since then have mainly been 
through improved tool materials selection and tool design. The use of 
WC [31] , WC/Co [32] , W-Re [8,11,24] , and PCBN [8,11,33] tools with 
various tool geometries have been reported for joining HSLA steels. But 
none of them could provide the synergy of low-cost and high- 
performance that can make the process adaptable. Fig. 1 presents a 
representative cost-performance plot of existing tool materials and in -

2. Materials and methods
2.1. ICME-based modeling
ICME-based modeling was carried out using Thermo-Calc 2023a 
software (Thermo-Calc Software AB, Sweden) and QuesTek ’ s pro -
prietary ICMD ® software platform [43] . The softwares run on the 
CALculation of PHAse Diagram (CALPHAD) methodology and utilize 
specific thermodynamic databases to predict the microstructures and 
properties as a function of composition, temperature, and pressure, for 
multicomponent systems corresponding to real materials. The thermal 
stability of the binder and the compatibility of the binder and the 
ceramic phase over the application temperature range were evaluated 
using the software. TCHEA6 and TCHEA7 material database was used 
for HEA and WC/HEA design, and TCFE11 material database was used 
for HSLA-50 continuous cooling transformation (CCT) diagram 
simulation.

2.5. ICME based binder and cermet design
WC/Co cermets have been the legacy materials used for various 
applications such as cutting, grinding, and polishing [45] . However, the 
cobalt binder is prone to transform from FCC to HCP (a more brittle 
phase) during FSW application which makes the WC/Co material un -
suitable for high-temperature FSW application [34] . The design phi -
losophy of the HEA was to improve the properties of the binder in ways 
that benefit the cermet for FSW application, while avoiding the addition 
of expensive elements. With Co as the first element on the canvas, equal 
amounts of Fe and Ni were added to form stable the FCC CoFeNi alloy as 
the base for HEA, which was expected to have a better strength than Co. 
Fe and Ni additions to a WC/Co cermet also led to the improvement in 
the fracture toughness property [46] . To further increase melting tem -
perature and high temperature resistance, Cr was added, which also 
improves oxidation resistance [47] . Mo was added to suppress the for -
mation of Cr

2.7. WC/HEA cermet tool characterization and testing
The as-machined tool fabricated according to the schematics shown 
in Fig. 3 was characterized to evaluate the sinter bond between the D2M 
alloy shank and the sintered WC/HEA cermet. Fig. 8 (a) shows the di -
mensions of all the tool features. Fig. 8 (b) shows the tool secured inside 
a tool holder with the sinter bond region highlighted. Fig. 8 (c) shows a 
low-magnification BSE micrograph of the sinter bond region which 
indicated a sound bond throughout the mating surface. The SEM and 
EDS characterization of the sinter bond region, from Fig. 8 (c) and (d), 
respectively, indicated a good bonding between the D2M alloy shank 
and the WC/HEA cermet.
Two identical tools were fabricated using the aforementioned steps 
for testing. The first tool was used to make a series of FSW plunge tests to 
evaluate the response of the tool. The reason for subjecting the tool to 
repeated plunge cycles is that the tool usually experiences the maximum 
axial load (or forge force) and spindle torque during this stage as the 
workpiece is not hot enough for easy material flow before the shoulder 
fully engages with the workpiece and provides sufficient heat [1] , this 
step is typically when brittle tool materials would fail. The harsh con -
ditions imposed on the tool during repeated plunge cycles can be used to 
evaluate the facture toughness of the tool materials, and the sinter bond 
between the WC/HEA cermet and D2M alloy shank. A total of 20 plunge 
cycles were carried out during this test. Fig. 9 summarizes the plunge 
tests that were carried out on the first tool.
The tool pin profile underwent a noticeable shape change upon 
subsequent plunge cycles moving from the original geometry to a “ self- 
optimized ” geometry as seen in Fig. 9 (a). The self-optimization process 
reduces the forces and torques acting on the tool and is a function of the 
tool material, workpiece material, and the process parameters used 
[53] . The approximate pin profiles of the initial and final stages of the 
tool are schematically represented in Fig. 9 (c). The peak force and 
spindle torque recorded for the 1st and the 20th plunge cycles were 
6340 N and 18 N.m, and 5612 N and 17 N.m, respectively, as seen from 
the plot in Fig. 9 (d). There was a drop of about 11.5 % in the peak force 
and a drop of 5.5 % in the spindle torque as the result of the tool profile 
self-optimization. Thus, the WC/HEA cermet undergoes plastic defor -
mation and avoids catastrophic failure. This behavior of the WC/HEA 
cermet establishes the utility of this cermet for FSW application. The 
self-optimized tool geometry can serve as a guideline while designing 
the tool geometry of subsequent tools to reduce the forces and torques 
experienced by the tool.

3. Conclusions
A WC/HEA cermet was designed using ICME-based alloy design 
approach and synthesized with mechanical alloying, mixing, and spark 
plasma sintering. Two FSW tools were fabricated with the WC/HEA 
material and tested using HSLA-50 steel. This study forms the first proof- 
of-concept for the design, fabrication, testing, and evaluation of novel 
cermet FSW tool materials geared towards low-cost high-performance 
synergy. Below are the salient findings of this study:
