ABSTRACT 
Further application of protective coatings to superalloys is limited by the microstructure evolution of coating/ 
superalloy interfacial area due to the significant interdiffusion of various elements. Here, a trade-off between 
oxidation resistance and microstructural stability at 1100 
◦
C is made for a high-Mo single-crystal superalloy 
coated by NiAlHfZr or NiCrAlYSi. The complex mechanisms of Al and Cr affecting the protective oxide scale 
formation, interdiffusion behaviors and deleterious Mo-rich phases precipitation have been uncovered. An 
efficient route utilizing thermodynamic calculation and kinetics simulation has also been established to evaluate 
the coating-associated microstructural stability of superalloys and to tailor coating compositions further. 
1. Introduction 
Nickel-based superalloys are widely applied in aero-engine turbine 
blades and vanes. Coatings with mechanical and chemical compatibility 
are widely used, in order to achieve comprehensive performances with 
excellent mechanical properties and oxidation/corrosion resistances of 
superalloys [1,2] . Al and Cr have been considered to be the key elements 
for improving the oxidation and corrosion resistance of superalloys. The 
formation of a stable and dense α -Al
2
O
3 
oxide scales is a good guarantee 
to protect the alloy substrate from oxidation, while Cr plays an impor -
tant role in promoting the formation of early external aluminum oxide 
scales [3] . Therefore, MCrAlY (M = Ni, Co, Ni and Co) and NiAl coatings 
are frequently used as bond coatings and thermal barrier coatings. 
However, there are significant differences in the chemical composi -
tion, phase constitutions and microstructure between superalloys and 
coatings. Therefore, element interdiffusion often occurs inevitably at the 
interface between coating and alloy substrate, driven by the chemical 
potential gradient, temperature gradient and stress gradient during 
service. An interdiffusion zone (IDZ) beneath the coating/substrate 
interface usually forms as a result [4] . Moreover, the lower solubilities of 
refractory elements Mo, Re and W in γ’ phases lead to the precipitation 
of Topologically Close-Packed Phases (TCP) and the formation of a 
secondary reaction zone (SRZ) [5,6] . The near surface γ / γ’ microstruc -
ture of the alloy will be also broken when new phases form, which de -
teriorates the long-term microstructural stability of superalloy and its 
mechanical properties eventually [7 – 12] . 
So it is important to control the interdiffusion behavior and micro -
structural evolution at the coating/substrate interface to alleviate the 
degradation of interfacial γ / γ’ microstructure. Recent studies have 
shown that the compositions of both coating and alloy substrate have 
important influences on the microstructural evolution at the interface. 
Galiullin et al. [13] designed four single-crystal model alloys 
Ni-10Cr-8Al-X (wt%, X = 0, 10Co, 9Ta, 6 W) and found that the addition 
of W or Ta to Ni-10Cr-8Al substrate was effective in reducing the 
depletion rate of β -NiAl in the MCrAlY coatings during the exposure at 
1050 
◦
C. Moreover, Ta addition to Ni-base single-crystal superalloys 
increased the volume fraction of γ
′
phase at the NiCrAlYSi coat -
ing/substrate interface, which has a significant inhibitory effect on the 
growth of IDZ, but it promoted the precipitation of TCP phases in the 
alloy substrate [14] . A study by Salam et al. [15] also found that Re and 
Al elements could increase the content of β -phase in the Co-rich and 
Ni-rich MCrAlY coatings and reduce the diffusion rate and oxidation rate 
of Al, leading to a longer lifetime of the coatings.

3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure of the as-deposited coatings 
In order to comparatively analyze the microstructure evolution in 
the coatings and at the coating/substrate interface before and after 
isothermal oxidation, we first observed and analyzed the as-deposited 
coating microstructure. According to the XRD patterns of the coating 
after multi-arc ion plating preparation in Fig. 1 , the NiAlHfZr coating 
consists of γ and γ
′
phases. The as-deposited NiCrAlYSi coating consists 
of γ / γ’ and β -NiAl/ α -Cr phases from XRD pattern. According to other 
published results, the wide diffraction peaks indicate that the γ / γ’ or β / α 
exist as amorphous or nano-crystalline phases in the as-deposited 
NiCrAlYSi coating [32,41] . The γ’ or α phase cannot be distinguished

addition of these reactive elements did not change the phase composi -

The microstructure morphologies (backscattered electron images) 
and composition distributions of the coating/substrate cross-sections 
obtained by EDS are shown in Fig. 2 . The thickness of the NiAlHfZr

approximately 85 wt%, 12 wt% and 1.6 wt% respectively. However, the 
Cr content is comparable to that in the alloy substrate, which suggests 
the diffusion of Cr from alloy to coating during the preparation process. 
For the NiCrAlYSi coating with a thickness of ~30 µ m, the contents of 
Ni, Al and Cr are approximately 66 wt%, 11 wt% and 22 wt% respec -
tively. According to the EDS composition analysis results ( Fig. 2 (c, d)), 
the main composition differences between the two coatings (except the 
reactive elements Si, Y, Hf, Zr) are mainly in the contents of Al and Cr. 
The higher Cr content leads to the phase constitution in the coatings

4. Discussion 
4.1. Interdiffusion and microstructural evolution at coating/substrate 
interface 
Based on the EDS composition analysis results in Fig. 10 , we further 
carried out composition-related chemical potential calculations to 
obtain the diffusion trends of the major elements. The formation of IDZ 
and SRZ under the coating is attributed to the interdiffusion of elements 
between coating and substrate [46 – 48] . The calculation results show 
that the higher chemical potentials of Al and Cr in the NiCrAlYSi coating 
make them diffuse to the alloy substrate (as shown in Fig. (11) ). The 
increases of the Al and Cr contents in the SRZ also lead to the trans -
formation from γ / γ
′
two phases to single γ
′
phase in the substrate. The 
underlying reactions occur as follows [49,50] :

In general, the outward diffusion of Ni from the substrate to the 
coating causes the formation of SRZ [9,51] . The refractory elements 
such as Mo and Re in the alloy substrate usually have lower solubilities 
in γ
′
than in γ [52,53] . Therefore, a large number of spherical and 
rod-shaped Mo-rich precipitates form due to the element interdiffusion 
mentioned above. Moreover, due to the less outward diffusion of Mo 
from alloy substrate to the NiCrAlYSi coating compared with that to the 
NiAlHfZr coating, the precipitation of Mo-rich phase is further promoted 
by the higher content of Mo left near the substrate. On the contrary to 
the NiCrAlYSi case, the diffusion of Cr is negligible in the NiAlHfZr 
coating due to its low chemical potentials gradient ( Fig. 11 ). Never -
theless, similar diffusion of Al from coating to alloy substrate still occur 
to form a IDZ in the NiAlHfZr/IC21 system, while the amount of this 
diffusion is significantly lower than that in the NiCrAlYSi/IC21. 
Approximately 10 wt% Al still presents in the NiAlHfZr coating after 
100 h of isothermal oxidation at 1100
◦
C, in spite of its similar 
as-deposited Al content to that in the NiCrAlYSi coating ( Fig. 10 ). These 
results indicate that the diffusion of Cr not only promotes the precipi -
tation in IDZ and SRZ but also has an important influence on the 
diffusion of Al. Moreover, the diffusion direction of Ni in NiAlHfZr/IC21 
system is also opposite to that of NiCrAlYSi/IC21. The higher content of 
Ni in the NiAlHfZr coating (83 wt%) than that in the alloy substrate 
(77 wt%) is considered to account for the absence of SRZ.

5. Conclusion 
In order to tailor a bond coating for the Ni
3
Al-based single-crystal 
alloy IC21, experiments and simulations have been carried out 
comprehensively in this work. Due to the composition features of high 
Mo and low Re in IC21, special considerations should be taken on the 
damage of substrate caused by coating during service, in addition to its 
oxidation resistance. To achieve this goal, we chose two typical coatings 
as candidates and paid a close attention on the interdiffusion behavior 
and microstructural evolution at the coating/substrate interface during 
isothermal oxidation at 1100
◦
C. The experimental results show that the 
average oxidation rate of the NiAlHfZr coating was larger than that of 
the NiCrAlYSi coating. The higher Al activity in the NiCrAlYSi coating 
results in the formation of a continuous dense Al
2
O
3 
layer in the later 
stages of oxidation. However, the higher chemical potential of Ni in the 
NiAlHfZr coating and the increased external diffusion of Mo from alloy 
substrate prevented the precipitations of TCP phases during isothermal 
oxidation at 1100
◦
C. Further simulation results indicate that the con -
tent and rate of Al and Cr diffusing from coating into alloy significantly 
affects the phase transformation at the coating/substrate interface. The 
driving forces for the precipitation of Mo-rich phases at the interface also 
change with the Al and Cr contents nearby, and various Mo-rich phases 
with different structures may precipitate at the same time. Fortunately, 
when Al < 5 wt% and Cr < 20 wt%, no Mo-rich precipitate forms for the 
Ni
3
Al-based single-crystal alloy IC21 studied in this work based on 
thermodynamics calculations. Moreover, the activity gradient of Al 
across the interface can be mitigated by lower Cr content obviously, 
although the higher Mo content increase the Al activity on the alloy side. 
Without sacrificing the oxidation resistance of coating, it is preferable to 
design coatings with a lower Cr content ≤ 20 wt% and an appropriate Al 
content of 8 – 10 wt% in order to maintain the microstructural stability of 
Ni
3
Al-based single-crystal alloy IC21.
