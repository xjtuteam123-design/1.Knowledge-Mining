ABSTRACT
Co-based superalloys are of great interest in the aeronautical and aerospace industry due to their superior 
thermal performance compared to Ni-based superalloys. This work explores the influence of elemental additions 
(Ni, Al, Cr, Si, and Ti) on the critical temperatures (liquidus, solidus, solvus, and eutectic) and microstructural 
features of several Co systems fabricated by vacuum induction melting until the final Co-30Ni-10Al-4Cr-2Si-2Ti 
(wt%) composition was obtained. Differential Scanning Calorimetry results indicated high thermal stability 
concerning the γ’ phase formation. Si and Ti additions suppressed the eutectic presence in the proposed su -
peralloy, which is desirable to avoid the incipient melting of components. Instead, the present work exhibits a 
low-density Co-based superalloy conformed of a continuous γ / β microstructure with γ’ precipitating uniformly. 
These characteristics make the Co-30Ni-10Al-4Cr-2Si and Co-30Ni-10Al-4Cr-2Si-2Ti superalloys promising 
candidates to be subjected to high temperatures in comparison with current Co-based superalloys.
1. Introduction
Turbine blades and disks are frequently made from nickel-based 
superalloys [1] . Nevertheless, cobalt-based superalloys have recently 
shown superior high-temperature strength related to these components 
[2] . This enhancement is achieved due to the γ - γ’ microstructure, which 
improves the operational temperature of hot section components in 
aero-engines and land-based gas turbines [3] . Therefore, this opportu -
nity has spurred increasing research in cobalt-based superalloys. In the 
past, the Co-Al-W system [4] was a key reference for controlling the γ - γ’ 
microstructure. This situation sparked interest in studying more alloying 
elements, as well as their effects on the microstructure-property re -
lationships. Nonetheless, there is still no formal classification of 
cobalt-based superalloys based on chemical composition.
Machine learning in combination with thermodynamic databases are 
helpful in the design of compositions for cobalt-based superalloys. For 
instance, Yu et al. [5] identified six suitable compositions validated for 
alloy manufacturing that include tungsten (W). In contrast, Ruan et al. 
[6] incorporated density reduction criteria into their algorithm, 
resulting in compositions without W. As noted, this method aids in the 
design of multicomponent alloys, yet it still requires validation on the 
microstructural effects of each alloying element. Furthermore, 
computational-based multicomponent diagrams may not always accu -
rately predict the resulting phases [7] . Key criteria for the development 
of cobalt-based superalloys are avoiding the formation of undesirable 
Topologically Closed-Packed (TCP) phases, high γ’ -solvus temperature 
and low density [8,9] . Therefore, the selection of alloying elements is 
fundamental.
From the experimental perspective, some elements used in cobalt- 
based superalloys have notable characteristics. Nickel (Ni) is essential 
for γ’ stabilization [10] . Aluminum (Al) and chromium (Cr) are crucial 
for improving oxidation resistance, an uppermost property of these 
materials [11] . Therefore, the Co-Ni-Al ternary phase diagram may be 
useful as a first approximation for designing alloy composition, specif -
ically to control phase transformations in the as-cast condition. 
Although no other elements are strictly mandatory, some of them 
enhance the γ’ precipitation. For example, tungsten (W), tantalum (Ta) 
and titanium (Ti) are commonly reported as γ’ formers, but copious W

detailed microstructural monitoring.
2. Materials and methods
The Co-based alloys were fabricated by adding high-purity elements, 
one by one, into a vacuum induction furnace until a composition of Co- 
30Ni-10Al-4Cr-2Si-2Ti (wt%) was obtained. To avoid oxidation, three 
vacuum cycles were performed before melting. Then, the liquid melt 
was degassed for about 5 min. Finally, casting was performed into a 1 cm 
thick × 10 cm long × 5 cm wide Cu-mold coupled with a water-cooling 
system, reaching a cooling rate of 278 K/s [20] . Samples were analyzed 
in a TA Instrument Q600 calorimeter to quantify the liquidus, solidus, 
solvus and eutectic temperatures. Phase identification was carried out 
on a D5000 Siemens X – Ray Diffractometer (XRD) using a λ

7600 F Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) coupled with an Energy 
Dispersive Spectrometer (EDS). Density measurements were performed 
by water displacement according to Archimedes ’ principle.

Finally, Fig. 4 (e) shows that the addition of Ti to the Co-5 alloy preserves 
the same phases as in the Co-4 alloy, although a greater IR volume 
fraction is observed. This allows to balance the volume fractions of the β 
and γ phases in the Co-5 alloy concerning the Co-4 alloy.
Prior work with similar microstructures to those of the Co-4 and Co-5 
alloys [44] indicated that an increase in the volume fraction of the γ 
phase was favorable to improve the ductility of Co-Ni-Al-based alloys. 
The mechanism of a balanced dual γ and β microstructure that enables γ’ 
precipitation in the AlCoCrFeNi system was reviewed by B. Gwalani 
et al. [45] . They founded the FCC structure to provide high ductility and, 
simultaneously, the BCC structure to provide high strength, being 
mandatory for the industrial performance of high-temperature 
components.
In contrast, X. Gu et al. [46] researched the influence of Ti addition in 
an AlCoCrFeNi system. When Ti concentration approached the equia -
tomic ratio, a loss of ductility occurred since the volume fraction of the 
TCP σ phase increased. Substantial evidence from A.S. Wilson [47]
verifies that TCP phases strongly assist crack initiation and propagation 
among γ’ precipitates in superalloy components. Furthermore, the 
non-equiatomic AlCoCrNiSi alloy of T.M. Butler et al. [48] , with less 
than 9 at% Si keeps the γ + β + γ’ + β’ microstructure, avoids the for -
mation of TCP phases, and reports convenient high-temperature oxida -
tion performance. Comparatively, the Co-5 alloy achieves a similar 
microstructure with just 4.43 at% Si, which then allows the incorpora -
tion of Ti, a recognized γ’ former according to L. Shi et al. [49] . That is, 
the concentration of Si and Ti in multicomponent alloys affects the 
precipitation control in the current dual FCC and BCC as-cast 
microstructures.
The above-mentioned FCC and BCC mixture in the microstructure 
and the absence of TCP precipitates are indicators of favorable me -
chanical behavior. Hence, the high-temperature stability of the γ’ phase, 
supported by DSC characterization, indicates that the Co-4 and Co-5 
alloys have great application potential for high-temperature 
components.
3.5. Alloying elements distribution in the Co-5 alloy
Fig. 5 presents the EDS mapping of the Co-5 alloy to study its 
elemental distribution. Fig. 5 (b) and 5 (e) shows that Co and Cr tend to 
enrich the IR. Ni is mainly distributed along the dendrites, and a slight 
decrease in its intensity is observed in the IR, as indicated by the inset in 
Fig. 5 (c). Al enrichment is observed in Fig. 5 (d) in a similar way to Ni. 
From points (1), (2), and (3) in Fig. 4 (e), it was observed that both Ti and 
Si diffused into the liquid as solidification progressed. However, Fig. 5 (f) 
shows that some interfaces were depleted in Si, while Fig. 5 (g) identifies 
that Ti tends to enrich at the interphase, as indicated by the arrows. 
Thus, it is considered that Ti enrichment at the interphase promotes an 
increase of Co, Cr and Si concentration at the IR.
Besides, Si and Ti have been reported to increase their concentration 
at the IR and to facilitate the diffusion of other elements such as Al, Co, 
Cr, and Ni, as demonstrated in the work of X. Zhuang et al. [50] . This 
behavior is in line with EDS observations discussed in Fig. 4 . Therefore, a 
small concentration of Si and Ti heavily promotes the transformation of 
FCC dendrites to a more balanced FCC and BCC microstructure by Al, Co 
and Cr distribution.
