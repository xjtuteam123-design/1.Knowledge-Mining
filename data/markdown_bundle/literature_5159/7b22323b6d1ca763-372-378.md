Properties of the Zr-doped CrCoMnFeNi high-entropy alloy after 
high-pressure torsion
A. S. Gornakova†1, A. V . Korneva2, B. B. Straumal1, N. S. Afonikova1, G. S. Davdian1, 
V . I. Orlov1, A. V . Druzhinin1, J. Dutta Majumdar3
†alenahas@issp.ac.ru
1Osipyan Institute of Solid State Physics of the Russian Academy of Sciences, Chernogolovka 142432, Russia
2Scientific Research Institute of Materials for Agrotechnology, Cotton Growing, Seed Production and Agrotechnology, 
Salar 100140, Uzbekistan
3Indian Institute of Technology, Kharagpur 721302, India
Abstract: The single-phase CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy (HEA) possesses a face-centered cubic lattice. Its strength and 
ductility at room and elevated temperatures are insufficient to compete with stainless steels or titanium- and nickel-based 
alloys. However, it is possible to increase its strength by adding dispersion hardening oxide, nitride or carbide particles. Such 
particles being inside the metallic matrix grains can impede the movement of dislocations. In this work, a study of the HEA 
Cantor alloy CoCrFeMnNi with the addition of 1 wt.% zirconium was carried out. Zirconium forms fine oxide particles in 
the alloy and does not dissolve in the matrix. It is shown that the alloy is thermally stable in the temperature range from 
500 to 1000°C, its phase composition and lattice parameter do not change. The microhardness of the material also varies 
slightly depending on the heat treatment. A slight increase in hardness from 154 HV in the as-cast state to 172 HV occurs 
after annealing at 750°C. The three-point bending tests showed an increase in Y oung’s modulus from 109 to 166 MPa with 
an increase in the annealing temperature from 500 to 1000°C. The conditional tensile strength has a minimum of 380 MPa 
in the sample annealed at 750°C. The sample of CoCrFeMnNi alloy + 1 wt.% Zr subjected to high pressure torsion showed a 
significant increase in hardness compared to the initial sample, almost 3 times, and the conditional tensile strength increased 
by almost 6 times. However, the phase composition and lattice parameter of studied alloy have not changed.
Keywords: high-entropy alloys, high-pressure torsion, single-phase material, stable structure, oxide particles

1. Introduction
In 2004, Cantor and co-authors published an article [1] 
on the five-component alloy Fe20Cr20Mn20Ni20Co20. In 
this paper they presented data on the phase and structural 
changes of this HEA when adding the sixth component. 
Subsequently, alloys with five or more components, in 
equiatomic proportions, became known as high-entropy 
alloys (HEA) or alloys without principal component [2]. 
To improve the mechanical and physical properties of the 
Cantor alloy, the influence of various alloying elements was 
studied, such as Zr [3], Ti [4 – 5], Nb [6], and a number of 
elements. The introduction of carbon and nitrogen into the 
HEA alloy leads to an increase in yield strength and tensile 
strength [7 – 9], while the authors note a decrease in plasticity. 
To date, it is known that the “improved” Cantor alloy can 
be used as coatings [10 –13]. It turned out that the strength 
and ductility of a single-phase face-centered cubic Cantor 
CoCrFeMnNi HEA at room and elevated temperatures are 
insufficient to compete with stainless steel alloys or titanium 
and nickel-based alloys [14]. However, these HEA possess 
the remarkable corrosion resistance, exceptional ductility 
and fracture toughness (or crack resistance) [15 –16]. One of 
the most common strategies for alloying the CoCrFeMnNi 
alloy to increase strength is dispersion hardening based 
on oxide, nitride or carbide particles inside a metal grain. 
Such particles can prevent the movement of dislocations, 
like for example those of chromium and hafnium oxides 
[16 –17]. Addition of 1 to 3 wt.% of HfO 2 oxide in Cantor 
alloy increases its microhardness to 500  HV [16]. Of 
interest are also works that consider the production, phase 
formation, phase transformation, as well as the mechanisms 
of HEA hardening [18]. The material with the highest 
addition of HfO 2 showed the highest yield strength during 
compression. Thus, Lee and co-authors [17] succeeded to 
increase the hardness of CoCrFeNi films by adding of 1.5 % 
Cr2O3 particles. In another work by Wu and co-authors [19], 
molybdenum was added to the Cantor alloy. As a result,

amount of molybdenum. However, the coefficients of dry

strengthened the CoCrFeNi alloy with carbide particles. 
Nanoscale carbide particles (NbC) were inside the grain, and

strength and yield strength increased to 732 and 911 MPa,

to the CoCrFeNi alloy increase the yield strength three 
times to 650 MPa [21]. The CoCrFeMnNi alloy has a good 
potential of application in the engineering field in the form 
of coatings, for example for gray cast iron, due to its high 
corrosion resistance. The additional component zirconium 
selected by us has high ductility and corrosion resistance. 
Zr should not only strengthen the HEA but also keep its 
reliable ductility. We investigated here also the resistance of 
the CoCrFeMnNi alloy to heat treatment and the effects of 
high-pressure torsion (HPT) on the material, as well as its 
hardness, Y oung’s modulus and conditional yield strength. 
The use of the HPT technique in the work makes it possible 
to change the properties of the material such as hardness, 
strength, plasticity, etc., without radically changing the 
phase composition and structure of the HEA alloy, as an 
example [22].
2. Materials and methods
The high-entropy alloy Cr19.08Mn18.11Fe19.71Co21.54 
Ni20.70+1 wt.% Zr (subsequent designations in the article 
CrMnFeCoNiZr) was smelted as a cylindrical ingot 
with a diameter of 10  mm in an induction furnace in an 
atmosphere of pure argon. It was produced from pure metals 
like zirconium (99.98 wt.%), manganese (99.97 wt.%), 
cobalt (99.9  wt.%), nickel (99.97  wt.%), iron (99.97  wt.%), 
chromium (99.99  wt.%) and zirconium (99.99  wt.%). The 
micrographs of the microstructure and component analysis 
were carried out on scanning electron microscopes Tescan 
Vega TS 5130 MM equipped with an energy dispersion 
spectrometer LINK (Oxford Instruments) (as-cast state) and 
FEI dual beam Versa 3D HighVac (after annealing). 0.9 mm 
thick discs were cut off from the ingot with electrospark 
erosion. Image processing and analysis was performed in 
the Atlas program, the software for the Tescan scanning 
electron microscope (Czech Republic). After removing the 
surface riveted layer with sanding paper, the samples were 
annealed in evacuated quartz ampoules, the initial residual 
pressure in which was about 4 ×10−3 Pa. The annealing of the 
samples was carried out in high-temperature furnaces SNOL 
6,7 / 1300 at 500°C (192 h), 750°C (24 h) and 1000°C (5 h). 
One initial sample (0.7  mm thick) was subjected to high-
pressure torsion (HPT) at room temperature, 5 revolutions 
of the plunger under pressure of 6 GPa at a rotation speed of 
1 revolution per minute, in a Bridgman anvil-type installation 
using computer control manufactured by W . Clement GmbH, 
Lang, Austria. After the HPT, the thickness of the sample was 
0.35 mm. The microhardness was measured with ITV-1-AM 
device, equipped with an MC-5.3 camera with LOMO-
Microsystems MCview software. The average microhardness 
values were based on measurements of 10  indenter prints 
at a load of 100  g. Three points bending tests of HEA 
samples were carried out on a testing machine for structural 
materials UTS 111.2-50 (TestSystems LLC, Ivanovo, Russia) 
at room temperature. X-ray diffraction patterns were 
obtained using Rigaku Smartlab X-ray diffractometer under 
Cu-Kα radiation. They were used for the structural and phase 
analysis of the samples. The analysis and calculation of the 
lattice parameters was performed using the PowderCell 
2.4  program. (PowderCell for Windows. Version 2.4. 
08.03.2000, Werner Kraus and Gert Nolze, BAM, Berlin). In 
order to obtain the composition contrast between different 
phases in the sample, the backscattered electron signal 
(BSE mode) was chosen for the SEM images. For study of 
the fracture surface fractography, the secondary electron 
signal (SE mode) was used. More demanding examinations 
of the microstructure were carried out by means of a 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) with a TECNAI 
G2 FEG super TWIN (200 kV) instrument (manufactured 
by FEI, Hillsborough, OR, USA) equipped with the EDS 
spectrometer produced by EDAX (AMETEK, Inc., Berwyn, 
PA, USA). For the production of TEM thin foils, the twin-jet 
polishing was performed using a D2 electrolyte in a Struers 
machine (Cleveland, OH, USA). The focused ion beam (FIB) 
technique was applied by means of FIB Quanta 3D, TECNAI 
FEG microscopy (30  kV) for the preparation of thin foils 
of deformed material. HAADF analysis performed on foil 
prepared by the FIB.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure and phase composition of the 
initial alloy
A part was cut off from the manufactured alloy ingot, on 
which component analysis (Fig. 1a) and X-ray phase analysis 
(Fig.  1b) was performed. The material under study is a 
single-phase face-centered cubic (FCC) solid solution with a 
lattice parameter a = 0.3597 nm. Using X-ray phase analysis, 
we were unable to observe the peaks of second phase. It 
is visible in Fig.  1a as the white dots / areas enriched with 
zirconium (Table 1). Table 1 shows the average composition 
based on data on five points each for the matrix (it appears 
dark-gray in Fig. 1a) and particles (they appear light-gray in 
Fig. 1a). The area proportion of light-gray particles is about 
1–1.5 %. The analysis of light particles showed the presence 
of oxygen in them and how we determined the composition 
is close to ZrO 2. And although pure zirconium was added 
in the process of making the ingot, and not its oxide, most 
likely its oxidation occurred during smelting. It turns out 
that such amount is insufficient to obtain visible XRD peaks 
in the diffraction patterns (Fig. 1b).
3.2. The microstructure of the annealed alloy
The microstructure of the annealed alloy does not differ 
much from the microstructure of the initial as-cast alloy, as 
evidenced by the SEM micrographs (Fig.  2 a – c) and XRD 
patterns (Fig. 2 d). Fig. 2 d shows that the relative intensity 
of the peaks is changing. It indicates the texturing of the 
samples during annealing. The prolonged annealing at 500°C 
leads to the formation of undesirable ZrO 2-based oxides

3.3. The microstructure of the alloy after HPT 
treatment
After HPT treatment, the SEM micrographs of the alloy 
(Fig. 3 a) and the phase composition of the sample (Fig. 3 b) 
did not change much compared to the initial state (Fig. 1). 
This indicates not only the thermal stability of the structure, 
but also that the severe plastic deformation could not destroy 
hard zirconium oxide particles.
HAADF analysis performed on foil (Fig. 4) prepared by 
the FIB method showed that the microstructure of the alloy 
is more complex. In addition to zirconium oxide particles, 
there are particles enriched with chromium (Fig.  4 d) and 
manganese (Fig. 4 f), Co, Cr, Fe are evenly distributed in the 
matrix.

The results of the three-point bending tests and the 
microhardness values are presented in Table 2. From 
Table  2 and Fig.  6, it can be seen that pre-annealing has 
little influence on the conditional yield strength (σ 0.2). In 
the same time, there is a significant increase in the elasticity 
modulus E of the material. The E value increases by about 
3 times after annealing at 1000°C compared to the initial state 
of the sample. Elasticity strongly depends on the structure. 
However, X-ray diffraction and phase analysis showed no 
changes in the phase composition of the samples. Moreover, 
the immutability of the lattice parameters indicates no 
changes in the component composition of the phase. Thus, 
the most logical reason for such significant changes in the 
elasticity of the alloy may be the reorientation of the grains 
during annealing and quenching (Fig. 2 d). The samples are 
single-phase, while highly textured, the two main peaks for 
the (111) and (200) planes change their intensity depending 
on the annealing temperature. In approximately equal 
proportions, the combination of grains of the directions 
[111] and [200] gives us the highest modulus of elasticity. 
Thus, it can be assumed that the most logical reason for the 
change in the elasticity value of the alloy is a change in the 
orientation of the grains during annealing and quenching. 
But this explanation does not work with the HPT material. 
This is the assumption of the authors of this article, the alloy 
is multicomponent, although single-phase, but it is difficult 
to predict its behavior, since the distribution of elements in 
the lattice of FCC is unknown and may locally differ in the 
volume of the material.
It can be seen from the data in the Table  2 that HPT 
processing of the alloy led to an increase in σ0.2 by 7 times, and 
E by 3 times. An increase in the strength of the material can 
be due to many reasons, for example, dislocation hardening 
due to an increase in dislocation density (Bochvar-Odinga 
curve), and / or grain boundary hardening due to a decrease in 
grain sizes and an increase in the proportion of intergranular 
boundaries in the volume of the material (Hall-Petch law). 
We calculated the size of scattering coherence regions (SCR) 
for the investigated alloy. SCR was equal to 80  nm (initial 
state), 85 nm (500°C), 180 nm (750°C), 210 nm (1000°C) and 
20 nm (after HPT). The direct correlation between SCR and 
the obtained values of E and σ0.2 has not been observed.
4. Conclusions
1. The CrMnFeCoNi Cantor alloy hardened with 
Zr-based oxides showed stable thermal stability, its phase 
composition and lattice parameters did not change after 
annealing. At the same time, a correlation was found between 
the microhardness, the average particle size and their 
proportion in the total area.
2. High-pressure torsion treatment of the alloy did 
not change the phase composition of the sample, the grain 
size decreased by 4  times from 80  to 20  nm. However, the 
mechanical properties of the material changed dramatically. 
Instead of plastic behavior, the material became brittle-
plastic, and its microhardness increased 3 times.
