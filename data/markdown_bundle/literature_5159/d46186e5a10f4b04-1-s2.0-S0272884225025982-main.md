1. Introduction
Metal matrix composites (MMCs) are advanced materials formed by 
incorporating reinforcing phases (e.g., particles, whiskers, or fibers) into 
a metallic or alloy matrix [ 1 – 3 ]. Compared to conventional alloys, 
MMCs exhibit superior physical and mechanical properties, including 
enhanced wear resistance, reduced coefficients of thermal expansion, 
and exceptional strength-to-weight ratios, making them highly suitable 
for meeting the increasing demands of modern industrial applications 
[ 4 , 5 ]. However, traditional alloy systems are constrained by the Gibbs 
phase rule, which limits the number of principal elements to ternary or 
quaternary compositions, thereby restricting their performance en -
hancements. Additionally, the significant mismatch in thermal expan -
sion coefficients between ceramic reinforcements and metallic matrices, 
combined with insufficient interfacial bonding strength, often leads to 
compromised overall performance of MMCs [ 6 ]. High-entropy alloys 
(HEAs), as a novel class of materials, provide a promising solution to 
these challenges due to their unique multi-principal element composi -
tions and exceptional properties, such as improved wettability and 
enhanced retention of reinforcement particles [ 7 , 8 ]. Leveraging these 
advantages, researchers have proposed a new paradigm: utilizing HEAs 
as the matrix material and incorporating particle reinforcements to 
fabricate high-entropy alloy matrix composites (HEAMCs). This 
approach not only addresses the limitations of traditional MMCs but also 
opens up new possibilities for designing materials with tailored prop -
erties for advanced engineering applications.
HEAs with a single-phase face-centered cubic (FCC) solid solution 
structure typically exhibit excellent ductility. However, their strength 
and wear resistance are often limited due to the relatively soft nature of 
the FCC structure. The incorporation of hard ceramic particles into the 
HEA matrix has been shown to significantly enhance the strength and 
wear resistance of the composite material, thereby broadening its

and 27.6 nm, respectively, and then increased to 50.9 nm and 29.7 nm. 
Similarly, the lattice strains first increased to 0.28 % and 0.33 %, 
respectively, and then decreased to 0.18 % and 0.31 %.
The enhancement of the diffraction peak intensity after sintering is 
mainly attributed to the grain growth or alignment in specific directions 
during the sintering process, resulting in the formation of preferred 
orientations (such as the (111) preferred orientation for the FCC phase 
and the (110) preferred orientation for the BCC phase), which relatively 
enhances the diffraction intensity of certain crystal planes. The lattice 
constants of the FCC and BCC phases in the powder state are usually 
higher than those in the sintered state because other atoms or groups 
adsorbed during ball - milling occupy certain space and cause lattice 
expansion. During sintering, these adsorbed substances are released, 
resulting in a reduction in lattice constants. As the sintering temperature 
increases, the solid solubility of Ti elements with larger atomic radii in 
the matrix increases, causing a slight increase in the lattice constants of 
the FCC and BCC phases.
The change in the grain size of the sintered composite is closely 
related to the presence of the second - phase particles (TiB
2
@Ti). At 
lower sintering temperatures, these particles effectively pin the grain 
boundaries, inhibiting grain growth and resulting in smaller grain sizes. 
When the sintering temperature rises to a certain extent, the second - 
phase particles may dissolve or coarsen, thereby weakening their 
pinning effect on the grain boundaries. Consequently, the grains begin to 
grow rapidly, leading to an increase in grain size. The lattice strain of the 
composite shows a negative correlation with the grain size. A decrease in 
the grain size significantly increases the grain boundary area, where 
atoms are arranged irregularly, inducing a large number of lattice dis -
tortions and thus increasing the overall lattice strain.

Similar to the formation of intermetallic compounds at the solder joint 
interface, in our EHEAMCs, the diffusion of Ti atoms at the interface 
forms a solid solution zone ( Figs. 5 – 7 ). This solid solution zone affects 
the stress distribution around the interface. The high negative mixing 
enthalpies of Ti with elements such as Ni, Al, and Co not only promote 
elemental diffusion but also change the stress state of the surrounding 
matrix. When an external load is applied, the stress concentration at the 
interface between the reinforcement and the matrix is affected by the 
structure and properties of this solid solution zone. If the interface 
bonding is strong and the solid solution zone has an appropriate 
composition and structure, it can effectively transfer stress, reduce stress 
concentration, and improve the overall mechanical properties of the 
composite. Conversely, if there are defects or inhomogeneities in the 
interface or the solid solution zone, stress concentration will be aggra -
vated, which may lead to premature failure of the material. Therefore, 
high temperature sintering promotes the diffusion of Ti in the solid so -
lution zone, improves the compositional uniformity of the interface or 
the solid solution zone, reduces stress concentration, and thus effectively 
and synergistically enhances its strength and plasticity.
