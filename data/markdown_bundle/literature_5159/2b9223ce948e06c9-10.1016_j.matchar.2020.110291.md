ABSTRACT
Eﬀects of annealing temperature and cooling medium on the hexagonal close-packed (HCP) phase fraction and
mechanical properties of a novel Co 35Cr25Fe37.5Ni2.5 alloy are investigated. The uniformly initial grain size and
diﬀerent HCP phase fractions are obtained via the same annealing temperature and di ﬀerent cooling mediums.
The HCP phase area fraction is doubled as the cooling medium of water is replaced by liquid nitrogen, and the
main reason is the rapid increase of nucleation sites of liquid nitrogen cooling. HCP laminates with di ﬀerent
orientations intersect within grains. The yield strength and ultimate tensile strength of Co 35Cr25Fe37.5Ni2.5 alloys
annealing at 900 °C with liquid nitrogen quenching are ~482 MPa and ~1079 MPa, respectively. Contributions
of the FCC phase and HCP phase to yield strength are discussed. The results indicate the combination of an-
nealing temperature and cooling medium is an eﬃcient manipulation method to adjust the HCP phase fraction
and mechanical properties of dual phase high entropy alloy.
1. Introduction
Multiple principal element alloys, referred to as high entropy alloys
(HEAs) or medium-entropy alloys, have attracted extensive attention
since the ﬁrst discovery over a decade ago [ 1–4]. The CoCrFeMnNi
alloy with a single face-centered cubic (FCC) phase is one of the most
thoroughly studied HEAs, which is found to have excellent tensile
strengths and ductility, especially at the cryogenic temperature,
opening a door to structural components used in extreme condition [ 5].
A transition from planar-slip dislocation activity to twinning induced
plasticity (TWIP) with the decrease of temperature occurs, owing to the
lower stacking fault energy (SFE) at the cryogenic temperature.
Recently, the metastability-engineering strategy has been proposed
to introduce the transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) e ﬀect into
HEAs [ 6–18]. Rather than focusing on phase stabilization and single-
phase formation, ductile multi-phase con ﬁgurations are designed to
overcome strength-ductility trade-o ﬀ, which is important future re-
search directions. Li et al. reported a series of TRIP-dual phase (DP)-
HEAs, such as Co
10Cr10Fe80-xMnx [11], Co 20Cr20Fe40-xMn20Nix [10].
Based on the simulation results of Ab initio calculations, enhanced
hexagonal close-packed (HCP) phase stability with reduced Ni con-
centration is bene ﬁcial for the appearance of TRIP phenomena in HEAs
systems [ 10]. Co
20Cr20Fe34Mn20Ni6 HEA shows the TRIP-DP e ﬀect and
exhibits higher ultimate tensile strength and strain-hardening ability
compared to the corresponding CoCrFeMnNi alloy [ 10]. The e ﬀects of
annealing time on the HCP phase fraction and mechanical properties of
Fe
50Mn30Co10Cr10 DP-HEAs are investigated, both strength and ducti-
lity of HEAs with a similar HCP phase fractions increase with the de-
crease of average grain size, owing to the improvement of the stability
of the FCC matrix [ 11]. Su Jing et al. designed a hierarchical micro-
structure of DP-HEAs via tempering and annealing process to achieve a
superior yield strength, which is two times higher than that of fully
recrystallized microstructure, due to the pre-existing nano-twins and
nano-sized grains [ 19,20]. Meanwhile, the in ﬂuence of compositional
inhomogeneity [21], interstitial atom [ 22], friction stir processing [ 23]
and deformation driven bidirectional transformation [ 24] on mechan-
ical behavior of the dual-phase high-entropy alloy are investigated re-
cently. However, there is a lack of the e ﬀect of cooling mediums on the
HCP phase fraction and the discussion of contributions of the FCC phase
and HCP phase to the yield strength.

2. Experiment details
The alloys with a nominal composition of Co 35Cr25Fe37.5Ni2.5 (at.%)
were produced by a vacuum arc melting furnace with a water-cooled
copper mold using pure metals (purity higher than 99.9 wt%). Melting
was performed under a high-purity argon atmosphere. The ingots were
re-melted at least ﬁve times to improve chemical homogeneity. The
obtained button ingots were homogenized at 1200 °C for 5 h followed
by water-quenching. Then, the samples were cold rolled to a thickness
reduction ratio of 65%. Subsequently, the rolled sheets were annealed
at di ﬀerent temperatures (from 600 °C to 900 °C) for 60 min, respec-
tively, followed by water-quenching or liquid nitrogen quenching. The
samples were kept at cooling mediums for 10 min.
The microstructures of the samples in di ﬀerent heat treatment
conditions were analyzed by multiple methods. Electron backscatter
diﬀraction (EBSD) analysis was carried out using a JEOL 7100F scan-
ning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with a TSL OIM data col-
lection software. Prior to the above EBSD measurements, the samples
were ﬁrst ground using silicon carbide paper from 400 to 3000 gran-
ulation. Then samples were electrically polished in a solution of 20%
HClO
4 and 80% C 2H5OH at 25 V for 50 s. The conventional mean-
linear-intercept method was used to determine the representative grain
size. At least three ﬁelds and 400 grains each ﬁeld were analyzed to
determine the average grain size. The value of grain size refers to the
size of the whole matrix grain which may contain HCP laminate. X-ray
diﬀraction (XRD) measurements were conducted using a Bruker D8
DISCOVER equipped with Cu K
α1 radiation ( λ = 0.15418 nm) oper-
ating at 40 kV and 40 mA between 30° and 110° (2 θ) with a step size of
3°/min. The microstructures of samples were examined using a JEOL
7100F SEM. The composition and elemental distribution of samples
were investigated using a JEOL 8530F Electron Probe Microanalysis
(EPMA) measurements.
The dog-bone-shaped tensile specimens with a gauge length of
10 mm, a width of 5 mm, and a thickness of 1 mm were machined by
electrical discharge machining (EDM). Room temperature tensile tests
were carried out using a SHIMADZU AGS universal electronic tensile
tester with a nominal strain rate of 0.001/s. Three specimens were
tensile-tested for each condition to con ﬁrm reproducibility.
3. Results
3.1. Microstructure of Co
35Cr25Fe37.5Ni2.5 alloys during annealing
The bulk composition of the used alloy was determined by an EPMA
analysis, and concentrations of Co, Cr, Fe and Ni elements are 35.2 at.
%, 24.8 at.%, 37.7 at.% and 2.3 at.%, respectively, which is close to the
nominal compositions. Fig. 1 shows typical microstructures of the
Co35Cr25Fe37.5Ni2.5 alloys after cold rolling and annealing at di ﬀerent
temperatures for 60 min. At the annealing temperature of 600 °C, re-
crystallization behavior is di ﬃcult to be found ( Fig. 1(a)). For the
sample annealing at 650 °C, recrystallized grains can be observed in
region B, and the microstructure consists of > 50% recrystallized grains
and minor non-recrystallized grains (region A), as shown in the
Fig. 1(b). The sample annealing at 700 °C is fully recrystallized with an
average grain size of ~4.7 μm( Fig. 1(c)). Note that the value of grain
size refers to the whole matrix grain before the HCP phase is thermally
induced. The variations and distributions of grain size with increasing
annealing temperature are shown in Fig. 2. With further increasing the
annealing temperature to 800 °C and 900 °C, the average grain size
increases to 6.9 μm and 13.4 μm, respectively ( Fig. 1(d) –(e)). Accord-
ingly, the average grain size of the sample annealed at 900 °C with
liquid nitrogen quenching (LNQ) is 13.8 μm( Fig. 1(f)), which is almost
the same with the samples annealed at 900 °C with water quenching
(WQ).
Fig. 3(a) shows the XRD patterns of Co
35Cr25Fe37.5Ni2.5 alloys at
diﬀerent annealing temperatures of 650 °C and 800 °C. Increasing an-
nealing temperature leads to changes in the di ﬀraction intensities, in-
dicating a variation in phase volume fractions. The HCP phase fraction
of samples annealed at 800 °C is higher than that of samples annealed at
650 °C. Further, EPMA maps of the Co 35Cr25Fe37.5Ni2.5 alloys annealed
at 800 °C are shown in Fig. 3(b). The EPMA maps show that there is no
apparent elemental partitioning in the dual-phase structure and all
elements (Co, Cr, Fe and Ni) are uniformly distributed.
The EBSD inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) maps and phase maps in Fig. 4
show the microstructure evolution of the Co 35Cr25Fe37.5Ni2.5 alloys

fraction, number and thickness of the HCP laminate is varying, as
shown in Fig. 5 . The HCP phase area fraction gradually increases with
the increase of annealing temperature. Interestingly, as the cooling
medium of water is replaced by liquid nitrogen, the HCP phase area
fraction is doubled. With the increase of the annealing temperature

minate increases from 0.85 μm to 1.47 μm and 2.68 μm, respectively.

is an obvious di ﬀerence compared with the samples with WQ. There is

4. Discussion
4.1. E ﬀects of annealing conditions on thermally induced HCP phase
fraction
TRIP-DP-HEAs attract high attention via metastability engineering
strategy [6]. Non-equal molar HEAs with Low SFE could be designed by
tuning the constituent elements, such as lowering the Ni content or
increasing the Co content, which is bene ﬁcial to obtain the TRIP-dual
phase HEAs [ 10,12,13]. According to the FCC-HCP transformation
model proposed by Mahajan [ 27], two perfect dislocations split into
faulted pairs and product three Shockley partials on every second {111}
plane, which grow into one another and form the HCP phase. The
stacking faults are also nuclei for the formation of the HCP phase which
are con ﬁrmed via in-situ tensile testing combining electron channeling
contrast imaging (ECCI) and EBSD techniques [ 28]. For the equal molar
CoCrFeNi HEAs, the annealing microstructure contains a single FCC
phase and annealing twins. With the decrease of Ni content, SFE of
HEAs decreases, and the HCP phase was thermally induced during the
water quenching [ 25]. Grain size is an important in ﬂuence factor for
the fraction of the HCP phase [ 29,30]. First, grain boundaries have been
known as the prime nucleating sites for the HCP phase, and an increase
of grain size will decrease the density of nucleating sites due to the
decrease of the grain boundary area. On the other hand, the growth of
the HCP phase within a grain is stopped by grain boundary, and an
increase of grain size is bene ﬁcial to the growth of the HCP phase. Li
et al. investigated the e ﬀect of annealing time on the thermally induced
HCP phase fraction and found that the HCP phase fraction increases
with the increase of annealing time and the growth of grains. However,
the variation of the HCP phase fraction was not monotonic, due to the
combined mechanism of grain boundary [ 11].
In this work, the number and thickness of HCP laminates in the
Co
35Cr25Fe37.5Ni2.5 alloys with the di ﬀerent annealing conditions are
counted, as shown in Fig. 5. The thickness represents the growth of HCP
laminates, and the number represents the nucleation density of the HCP
phase. The thickness of HCP laminate increases with the increase of
annealing temperature, which results from the decrease of resistance of
grain boundaries, owing to the increase of grain size. However, the
nucleation density of the HCP phase sharply declines, due to the de-
crease of the grain boundary area. The fraction of the HCP phase rises
slowly with the increase of annealing temperature due to the variation
of the grain size and the increase of HCP phase size.
The uniformly initial grain size and di ﬀerent HCP phase fractions
are obtained via the same annealing temperature and di ﬀerent cooling
mediums. The area fraction of the HCP phase of DP-HEAs increases
rapidly via liquid nitrogen cooling compared with water cooling. A lot
of HCP phase laminates with di ﬀerent lengths and thickness intersect
within grains, as shown in Fig. 7. According to the mechanism proposed
by Olson and Cohen [ 30], the density of nucleation sites in the mate-

4.2. Contributions of FCC phase and HCP phase to yield strength

DP-HEAs with fully recrystallized grains are about 200-400 MPa and
600-900 MPa, respectively. The yield strength and ultimate tensile

1000–1100 MPa, which are higher than that of reported TRIP-DP-HEAs

eﬀect of the alloying elements Co and Cr. Meanwhile, the increase of Co
and Fe content and decrease of Ni content ensure the lower SFE
