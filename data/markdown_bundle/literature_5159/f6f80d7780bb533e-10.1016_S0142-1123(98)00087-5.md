Abstract
Fatigue in the polycrystalline Ni-base superalloys is discussed, with particular emphasis on modelling the mechanism at the grain
and sub-grain scale. The importance of Ni-base superalloys as aeroengine materials is summarised, and indications are given as to
where modelling offers potential for further advancements. In the experimental section, there are three parts. The ﬁrst identiﬁes
how at the microstructural scale, the instantaneous rate of fatigue crack growth is governed by the crack path selection mechanism.
The second part describes a statistical analysis of i) crack arrests, ii) instantaneous crack growth rates and iii) and their dependence
on linear elastic fracture mechanics parameters. It is shown that all fatigue crack growth events occurring at the microstructural
scale can be represented by a few statistical distributions. Thirdly, an initial analysis of the underlying slip band and dislocation
mechanism of conventional fatigue in Ni-base superalloys is presented. Crown Copyright Ó 1999. Published by Elsevier Science
Ltd. All rights reserved.
1. Introduction
Ni-base superalloys were ﬁrst introduced to aeroeng-
ines as turbine-disc materials to allow turbine tempera-
tures and stresses to be raised [1,2]. The introduction
and continuous improvement of these alloys has enabled
considerably higher thrust-to-weight and fuel efﬁciency
to be attained. As a percentage of engine weight, general
Ni-base and other superalloy applications have risen
from about 10% to about 30%. They include single crys-
tal blades, high temperature discs, combustion chambers
and nozzle guide vanes [3].
Their microstructures are composed of a face-centred
cubic matrix g (Ni stacked on Ni plus Fe) [4,5], with
large additions of alloying elements that give solution
hardening, corrosion resistance and a low stacking fault
energy. g9 (~1/3 mm) and g0 (~1/20 mm) precipitates are
randomly interspersed throughout the g matrix and at
grain boundaries; it is these that make the lattice planes
highly resistant to slip band formation at high tempera-
ture. The components were originally cast and forged.
Later the hot isostatic pressing (HIP) powder route was
developed for polycrystalline Astroloy, IN100, Rene 95,

RR1000 etc.[6] The HIP route produces higher strength

Developments in ceramics and refractory metal alloys
have failed to produce materials which are useful in the

continuing upward trend in alloy performance justiﬁes
the quest for further improvements.
Empiricism and experimental trial-and-error are useful
alloy advancement strategies because alloys and liﬁng
methods can still be improved without having to under-
stand fully the underlying materials mechanisms. How-
ever, such approaches are limited by experimental error
and by the consequential cost of multiple specimen and
component testing, especially in regimes involving crack
initiation, minor cycling and HCF. This paper describes
a model of fatigue on the microstructural scale and
makes a few generalised fatigue liﬁng predictions that
would hardly be viable to verify by empirical analysis
of conventional specimen test results.

Considering the large volume of published research
on fatigue at the grain scale upwards ( .0.2 mm) and on
the dislocation scale (< nm), there is comparatively little
on the mechanism of fatigue between these two extremes
of scale. At this intermediate scale, high magniﬁcation
SEM studies show that the dislocation process causing
slip band failure can do so where dislocations move most
freely [10] (mode II crack initiation). Thus, slip band
crack initiation starts at the centre of a grain. However,
some dislocation models assume simply that the rate of
fatigue crack growth depends solely on the distance of
the crack tip from the approached grain boundary, using
an argument based on the trapping of dislocations at
grain boundaries [11]. Results are presented later in this
paper showing that this relationship is not observed in
the superalloys. Interesting TEM studies of single crystal
SRR99 material show that when the slip bands ahead of
a mode I crack tip cannot accommodate the dislocation
movements, the crack path suddenly switches from
,001. mode I type to octahedral mode II type [12]. In
summary, fatigue at the nanometre-to-micron scale is not
so well understood and it deserves further study, as it
offers potential in the development of fatigue liﬁng mod-
els that perform well in regimes that current empirical
liﬁng methods are less effective.
Increased performance and decreased life cycle cost
will be ever present targets, for future aircraft. Alloy
research will continue to play a major role, as discussed.
Equally proﬁtable is liﬁng modelling research, which
continues to produce: i) improved component design
practice to enhance performance while maintaining
safety and ii) reduced life-cycle cost-of-ownership eg.
safe methods of component life extension, safer liﬁng
procedures, reduced fabrication costs, etc.
As 2/3 of the cyclic life of a typical component con-
sists of crack initiation and short crack growth, these are
especially signiﬁcant regimes for liﬁng research. Paris
(i.e. d a/dN=CDK

1.1. Effect of temperature and environment on fatigue
in Ni-base superalloys
The results presented in this paper are characteristic
of the stress-controlled fatigue mechanism (below
400°C) and of the time-independent non-creep compo-
nent of crack growth (f .1 Hz) in the temperature range
of about 400–650°C. In the broader context, some effects
of temperature on Ni-base superalloy behaviour are as
follows.
Below about 400°C (dependent on material) the dam-
age mechanism in Ni-base superalloy components is
primarily time-independent (ie. independent of load
cycle frequency). An air environment is observed to
enhance the rate of fatigue crack growth, in a time-inde-
pendent manner [15]. This shows that mechanical move-
ment of slip bands is necessary to drive the oxygen or
hydrogen ions into the slip bands to where they can
enhance the fatigue mechanism. Both during short crack
growth and approaching threshold, a predominantly
facetted fracture surface is formed, whereas at suf-
ﬁciently high DK a predominantly striated fracture sur-
face is observed.
Between about 400 and 650 °C (again dependent on
material) the damage mechanism can include creep,
fatigue and oxidation. For example, crack initiation in
IN718 is quantiﬁable principally in terms of equations
for creep exhaustion. However, after the crack initiation
stage, cracks can grow much faster in an air environment
than they can in a high vacuum. Published studies for
many Ni-base alloys: Waspaloy [16], Astroloy [17,18],
IN718 [19–21], Rene95 [22] etc. show that at high vac-
uum the observed crack growth rates approach low
values which are nearly time-independent (ie. fatigue
dominated). During crack propagation ( a.0.38 mm
radius), creep partially relaxes the local stress cycle at
the local stress concentration feature, but otherwise the
crack growth rate in IN718 is controlled by a modiﬁed
LEFM parameter K rather than the creep energy para-
meter C*. Extensive published experimental studies
show that the damage rate is dependent on the time spent
at high load and upon the loading rate of the stress cycle.
Fracture surfaces grown at the time-independent rate are
predominantly transgranular [23]. As the cyclic fre-
quency is decreased, the typical proportion of the frac-

1.2. Effect of grain structure
In the lower temperature band, that is, below about
400°C, a decreased grain size in Waspaloy and Astroloy
is observed to result in improved short fatigue crack
growth resistance and in poorer long crack growth resist-
ance [25–27]. The effect in the short-crack regime is
thought to be the result of increased crack deﬂection (see
later in paper) and the effect in the long crack regime is
thought to be due to reduced roughness induced closure.
In the middle temperature band, it is observed that a
smaller grain size usually promotes more rapid inter-
granular fracture, because the grain boundary density is
higher. In some cases, decreasing the grain size has been
linked to a lower boron and zirconium concentration at
grain boundaries [22]. Both elements are thought to
enhance grain boundary properties and their dilution
may therefore also be partly responsible for more rapid
intergranular fatigue crack propagation observed in the
ﬁne grain superalloys.
1.3. Quantiﬁcation of fatigue life and the effect of
data smoothing artifacts
During short-crack growth, instantaneous crack
growth rates over short cyclic intervals are extremely
variable. Accurate measurement of an average crack
growth rate curve spanning the whole short-crack growth
regime requires the growth curves from at least 30 +
specimens. This costly exercise has been undertaken for
relatively few materials. To life components, the SN
curve based approach is used, as it requires fewer
expensive component tests. An advantage of the SN
curve approach is that ‘N’ is taken as the integral of the
life over the whole short crack regime, and therefore this
‘N’ value is subject to considerably less scatter than an
instantaneous crack growth rate d a/dN.
Published crack growth data are often presented in
smoothed form [28,29], and many crack length measure-
ments are taken at large cyclic intervals. Although the
reason for smoothing is typically to highlight total life or
average behaviours or to simplify; an insufﬁcient sample
could give a false average curve and therefore sub-
sequent analysis may prove to be more complex. Fur-
thermore, considerable information about the scatter and
the microstructural mechanism can be lost when data
is smoothed.
Similarly, a limitation of the P.D. and eddy current
methods of crack measurement is that they do not give
the exact position of crack fronts. They contain an
inherent form of smoothing because they measure aver-
age crack radii. In this study, growing cracks and slip
bands were observed at the specimen top surface and via
3D mapping (involving sectioning). These observational
methods enable crack growth rate curves and the respect-
ive crack path to be correlated. It was predicted that
measurement of crack growth over short intervals of
fatigue cycles would reveal new information about the
mechanism of fatigue, and this led to the following
experimental approach.

of progressively ﬁner fracture-surface faces. During

Characteristics (1–7) show that fatigue damage consists
of two types: i) the irreversible strain exhaustion and
cleavage of systems of slip bands during crack advance
and ii) the establishment of new cleavage directions dur-
ing crack arrest. At high DK more slip systems are likely
to be involved in the crack advance process than at low.
In polycrystalline material, cleavage of the slip sys-
tems that are operating ahead of a given short-crack tip
(at any one instant) only allows a crack to extend a lim-
ited distance. For the crack to extend further, new slip
systems must be activated. This aspect can be observed
as threshold is approached, when a crack grows as far
as the active slip systems allow and then stops because
no new slip systems have formed.
The active slip systems have a vital effect of dissipat-
ing the stress intensity away from the crack tip. If this
did not occur, the material would be brittle because mod-
erate loading could build up a stress singularity at the
crack tip of sufﬁcient intensity to cause the material to
unzip.
In theory, the uniformity of the hardening precipitate
distribution within individual grains should be beneﬁcial
for 2 reasons: i) to give a higher maximum resolved
shear stress to form slip bands (ie. so that there are few
weak spots due to regions of sparse hardening
precipitate) and ii) uniformity is likely to encourage slip
bands to form in greater numbers (where they do form)
thus spreading the strain accumulation over as many slip
bands as possible.

The observed cracks in the Waspaloy specimens acted
independently while they were separated by at least a
whole grain. Otherwise, they coalesced at a rate
approaching the rate of crack growth in single crystal
material (under similar conditions). The observed rapid
surge in the observed crack growth rate immediately fol-
lowing coalescence suggests that the cracks then rapidly
returned to their original semi-circular shape.
6. Slip band and dislocation mechanism of fatigue
Slip band formation is the ﬁrst stage of the pure
fatigue mechanism in a Ni-base superalloy. Sub-
sequently, a fatigue crack advances at regions of
maximum dislocation activity within the active slip
bands. This can be observed at a slip band crack
initiation site, by tracing back the river lines within the
initiation grain. That is, fatigue in surface grains begins
at a site located away from grain boundaries and towards
the original surface [10]. Conversely, crack arrests occur
at high angle grain boundaries because these sites can
delay/retard slip band activity. TEM observations of Fe-
2wt%Si, for example, show dislocations piled up at a
grain boundary [30–32]. This two stage mechanism is
also considered to contribute to the propagation of
larger cracks.
Some authors have reasoned that fatigue crack
advance is caused by irreversibility of slip [33,34]. That
is, slip cannot be perfectly reversible, otherwise an
unloading strain could cancel the respective loading
strain, such that a fatigue crack could not advance. This
raises the question of what happens after irreversibility
has developed. A consequence is that the stress concen-
trations are dissipated less effectively by the slip bands.
Meanwhile, the stress ﬁeld may not be sufﬁciently high
to break bonds. Thus, slip is encouraged to occur in new
lattice planes that lie in close proximity to the original
slipped lattice plane, forming the distinctive intense slip
bands.[5] Also, high resolution observations indicate that
Fe-2%Si behaves in a similar manner, as they show that
a dense mesh of trapped dislocations occurs immediately
ahead of a specimen crack tip [30].
Extreme positive and negative stresses normal to the
slip plane can occur around the dislocations. For
example, these may occur at tight loop dislocations that
have attracted vacancies to form stacking faults [35]. It
is hypothesized, cycling causes the build-up of the dense
dislocation mesh ahead of the crack tip (as described in
the preceding paragraph) until in places, stresses exceed
the bond strength of the material. Then a ligament of the
material becomes unstable and fails. During the instant
of failure, part of the stored dislocation energy is
released. Some of this energy goes into increasing the
surface area of the material required for crack advance.
That the plane-normal stresses are tensile in some
regions, may explain why rewelding of the ligament
does not occur immediately after it has failed.
At the macroscopic level, ligament failure is perceived
as fatigue crack growth. At high DK, the fatigue process
in Ni-base superalloys might also be enhanced by plastic
displacement of material around the crack tip. (Other
mechanisms occur in other materials. If the slip process
forms extrusions and intrusions, for example, the forma-
tion of intrusions can be a contributory pure fatigue
mechanism).
Sub-surface fatigue proves that prevention of reweld-
ing by corrosion is not an essential condition for fatigue
to occur.
During fatigue, progressive ligament failure exposes
new microstructure to higher stress, thus new slip bands
form and so on.
The above hypothesis seems to be consistent with
observations, irrespective of the blend of dislocation
mechanisms that are operative in a speciﬁc superalloy.
These issues and detailed modelling are being explored
further in the author’s ongoing research.

7. Prediction of macroscopic fatigue
This section gives a brief summary of how the obser-
vations given in this paper are used as a basis for the

## Page 12

404 A.D. Boyd-Lee /International Journal of Fatigue 21 (1999) 393–405
microstructural model of fatigue described in the separ-
ate paper [14].
At time zero, the model represents an inﬁnite popu-
lation of cracks of a given size. Crack size is character-
ised by a single measurement of the centre point of the
crack initiation site to a deﬁned point on the crack front.
Time is then advanced in increments of fatigue cycles.
The likelihood that an individual crack grows a dis-
tance ‘x’ during this time step is given by the probability
density versus instantaneous crack growth rate relation-
ship for the particular value of DK. For those cracks that
are arrested, the arrest distribution is used to decide how
long each arrest will last for. Experimentally determined
distributions for the probability of a crack becoming
arrested are used to decide when cracks become arrested.
The model predicts correctly that for a constant ampli-
tude stress cycle, LCF fatigue lives are distributed
according to a lognormal probability density function, to
a good approximation. Using input data from threshold
studies, it predicts that HCF lives are also distributed
approximately according to a lognormal probability den-
sity function. Owing to high scatter it is normally
impracticable to measure HCF life distributions directly
(subject to constant Ds loading). This is therefore a sig-
niﬁcant prediction. A relatively low scatter factor of
about 2 was predicted (i.e. ratio in life between the –3s
points on the failure distribution), instead of a conserva-
tive value of 4 which is typically used in the liﬁng of
fracture critical parts. The cause of this relatively low
value is the low sample size (equal to one), whereas the
value of 4 is based on over 50 specimen tests (DERA
and Rolls).
Prediction of minor-cycle fatigue is another regime
which can be problematic to characterise empirically due
to low erratic growth rates and high scatter. It would be
of interest to exploit the capabilities of the above model
in this regime also.
8. Conclusions
1. Crack arrests occurred only at points on the crack
front where the direction of crack growth was
deﬂected. Thus the more tortuous the path that the
advancing crack must take, the greater the short
fatigue growth resistance of the material. It follows
that the more persistent arrests occurred at high angle
corrugated grain boundaries.
2. The likelihood of a deﬂection event causing an arrest
decreased with increasing DK.
3. Both reduction of grain size and increasing the irregu-
larity of grain boundaries can enhance the short crack
growth resistance of a Ni-base superalloy.
4. During facet formation the respective crack front sec-
tions grew at high rates, comparable to those occur-
ring in single crystal material under similar con-
ditions.
5. Facet edges that ran in the direction of crack growth
did not retard fatigue crack growth.
6. Long crack threshold was due to the persistences of
crack arrests tending to inﬁnite numbers of cycles.
However, long crack threshold was observed to not
affect any growing section of the crack front. Cracks
stopped growing at threshold because eventually the
whole crack front got pinned by crack arrests.
7. The statistics of crack growth and arrest have been
characterised in detail (Fig. 11).
8. Successive small increments of crack growth were
observed to be statistically independent.
9. Conclusions 1–9 allow the development of a micro-
structural model of fatigue capable of quantitative
macroscopic liﬁng predictions.
10.Uniformity of g9 hardening precipitate distribution
would enhance fatigue endurance.
11.In the discussion, a dislocation mechanism of fatigue
failure is predicted.
12.Statistical modelling of the ‘microstructural’ mech-
anism of fatigue has been developed to enhance pre-
dictions of HCF (high cycle fatigue) lives. Theoreti-
cally, the approach should offer similar potential for
fatigue crack growth regimes involving signiﬁcant
scatter.
The results presented in this paper are principally for
Waspaloy. Separate studies of AP1 and U720 by the
author have shown similar qualitative behaviour to that
of Waspaloy.
