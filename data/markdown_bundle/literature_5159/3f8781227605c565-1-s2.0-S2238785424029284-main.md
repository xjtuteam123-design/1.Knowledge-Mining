ABSTRACT
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are known for their distinctive microstructural features, outstanding performance, 
and potential applications, establishing them as novel metallic materials. Studies indicate that face-centered 
cubic HEAs generally offer increased strength and toughness at lower temperatures, suitable for cryogenic ap -
plications. This work on (FeCoNi)
86
Al
7
Ti
7 
HEA fabricated using powder bed fusion (PBF) studies the phase 
composition, microstructure, and mechanical properties at both room and cryogenic temperatures (298 K and 77 
K). The PBF HEA exhibits a hierarchical microstructure with columnar grains, Ti-enriched cellular substructures 
entangled with high-density dislocations, and L21 nanoprecipitates, contributing to an excellent strength- 
ductility combination at room temperature. Notably, as the temperature decreases from 298 K to 77 K, both 
strength and ductility increase, with a higher yield strength of ~1.0 GPa, ultimate tensile strength of ~1.55 GPa, 
and ductility of ~42%. Dislocation strengthening is dominant at both room and cryogenic temperatures, with 
dislocation slip as the primary deformation mechanism at 298 K and a combination of dislocation slips and 
stacking faults at 77 K.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), a unique class of metallic materials 
primarily composed of solid solutions with multiple elements present in 
equiatomic or near-equiatomic ratios, were first introduced in 2004 by 
Yeh et al. [ 1 ] and Cantor et al. [ 2 ]. The discovery of such a single-phase 
solid solution with multiple principal elements has shattered traditional 
notions of phase formation in metallic materials, significantly expanding 
the compositional design norms and space for metallic materials [ 3 ]. As 
expected, HEAs have garnered increasing attention among researchers 
since their inception. Generally, the HEAs exhibit four core effects [ 4 – 7 ]: 
high entropy of mixing, sluggish diffusion, severe lattice distortion, and 
cocktail effects. Recent studies have revealed that HEAs exhibit excep -
tional mechanical properties, including high strength, ductility, tough -
ness, excellent wear, and fatigue resistance, both at ambient and 
high/cryogenic temperatures [ 8 – 12 ]. These superior properties, sur -
passing those of the traditional materials, have deepened the under -
standing of mechanisms operating in these HEAs and broadened their 
prospects for engineering applications [ 13 , 14 ].
Predominantly, HEAs are fabricated either through casting (such as 
vacuum arc melting) or through conventional powder metallurgical 
techniques [ 15 – 17 ]. However, due to the presence of multiple elements 
with varied melting points, the casting process often introduces 
elemental segregation (apart from other typical casting defects), leading

Reports on HEAs mostly focus on their mechanical and corrosion 
properties at room temperature [ 34 – 36 ]. However, metallic materials 
with superior mechanical properties at both ambient and cryogenic 
temperatures are in high demand for modern engineering applications, 
especially in fields such as deep-space exploration, low-temperature 
superconductors, and the gas industry [ 37 , 38 ]. Some traditional 
metals typically exhibit brittleness (especially materials with a BCC 
phase) as the temperature decreases, significantly impacting their sta -
bility and reliability in the working environment [ 39 – 41 ]. Recent re -
ports indicate that FCC -based HEAs become stronger and more ductile at 
lower temperatures [ 42 , 43 ]. The TWIP and TRIP effects have been 
demonstrated as the dominant deformation modes at low temperatures, 
thereby enhancing the work-hardening rate of these HEAs and 
consequently delaying the onset of necking [ 44 – 46 ]. Even though re -
ports on the mechanical properties of HEAs in cryogenic conditions are 
emerging, most studies focus on HEAs fabricated using traditional 
methods, primarily Cantor alloys. Since the materials fabricated by the 
PBF process offer a fine microstructure with high-density dislocations 
(internal defects) [ 47 , 48 ], their performance under cryogenic applica -
tions will be interesting and not many reports exist on the properties of 
the PBF-processed HEAs under cryogenic applications.
Accordingly, in this work, the microstructure, tensile properties 
(room and cryogenic temperatures), and deformation mechanisms of the 
PBF (FeCoNi)86Al7Ti7 HEA were investigated. The microstructure of 
the PBF HEA shows a hierarchical characteristic. The tensile properties 
were tested at both room and cryogenic temperatures. The deformation 
mechanisms operating at both room and cryogenic temperatures were 
elucidated to provide new insights into the development of high- 
performance HEAs for cryogenic applications using AM.

significant temperature gradient on the surface of the melt pool, and 
rapid solidification primarily occurs at the edges or bottom of the melt 
pool. When the laser scans a new powder layer, it partially remelts the 
previously solidified layer. These partially remelted grains provide 
nucleation sites for the rapid nucleation of new columnar grains and 
grow epitaxially [ 57 , 58 ].

The EBSD observations indicate that dislocation slip is the primary 
deformation mechanism for the samples tested at both 298 K and 77 K, 
respectively.
Unlike the samples deformed at 298 K, nanoscale stacking faults 
(SFs) can be observed along the grain boundaries and the vicinity of the 
L2
1
/matrix interface in addition to the high-density dislocations ( Fig. 13
(a and b)). Typically, the SFs can effectively impede the glide of dislo -
cations on slip planes spanning across SFs [ 85 ]. The presence of these 
SFs favors plastic deformation (ductility), as slip dislocations trans -
mitted through SFs result in significant strain hardening effects [ 99 ]. 
Apart from the typical paralleled SFs ( Fig. 13 (c)), there are also inter -
secting SFs forming Lomer-Cottrell (L-C) locks ( Fig. 13 (d)). The L-C locks 
become barriers impeding dislocation motion, and hence the 
strain-hardening effect was effectively improved due to their dislocation 
storage capability [ 62 , 88 ]. Thus, the deformation mechanism operating 
at 77 K differs from that of the sample tested at 298 K. Moreover, the 
transition in the deformation mechanism is closely related to the 
stacking fault energy (SFE) [ 100 ]. Previous reports have indicated that 
with decrease in the SFE, the deformation process sequentially involves 
dislocation slip, stacking faults, twinning (TWIP), and phase trans -
formation (TRIP) [ 37 , 101 ].
Since the SFE is temperature-dependent, lower temperatures corre -
spond to lower SFE, generating SFs at cryogenic temperature, further 
enhancing the strain-hardening capability [ 102 ]. As mentioned earlier, 
SFs tend to occur along the cellular boundaries and around the L2
1 
nano-precipitates, since these areas experience local stress concentra -
tion due to the accumulation of massive dislocations. During deforma -
tion at 77 K, more dislocations are generated, leading to higher local 
stresses exceeding the critical stress required for the activation of the 
SFs. Therefore, the formation of SFs serves as another source of strain 
hardening to achieve continuous and stable plastic deformation [ 103 ,
104 ]. Hence, the lower SFE at 77 K results in more pronounced local 
stress concentrations near the cellular boundaries and nanoprecipitates, 
where dislocations are accumulated. This local stress exceeds the critical 
stress required for SF nucleation, enabling the cooperative action of 
dislocations and SFs during deformation. Furthermore, the intersecting 
SFs lead to the formation of L-C locks, which act as effective barriers to 
dislocation motion, enhancing strain hardening and delaying necking.

5. Conclusion
The PBF-fabricated (FeCoNi)
86
Al
7
Ti
7 
HEA was characterized using 
various methods to investigation its phase composition, elemental dis -
tributions, microstructures, and mechanical behavior at both ambient 
and cryogenic temperatures. The strengthening and deformation 
mechanisms were also examined. The main conclusions are: 
(1) Due to significant temperature gradients and high cooling rates 
during PBF, the as-printed samples exhibit a hierarchical struc -
ture with columnar grains, Ti-enriched cellular substructures 
entangled with high-density dislocations, and L2
1 
nano -
precipitates uniformly distributed at the cellular boundaries.
(2) The PBF-processed HEA exhibits excellent strength-ductility 
synergy at both ambient and cryogenic temperatures. The me -
chanical properties vary with temperature; as testing temperature 
decreases from 298 K to 77 K, YS increases from ~730 MPa to 
~1030 MPa, UTS increases from ~1000 MPa to ~1550 MPa, and 
ductility increases from ~34 % to ~42 %.
(3) High YS is due to combined effects of grain boundary strength -
ening, dislocation strengthening, and precipitation hardening, 
with dislocation strengthening being dominant. The cellular 
substructures hinder dislocation movement by trapping them at 
their boundaries, thereby enhancing the YS of the material.
(4) The deformation mechanisms are temperature-dependent, with 
significant changes attributed to the variation in stacking fault 
energy (SFE) as a function of temperature. At 298 K, dislocation 
slip dominates plastic deformation. However, at 77 K, the 
reduced SFE facilitates the activation of nano-SFs and the for -
mation of intersecting SFs (L-C locks), which impede dislocation 
motion and enhance strain hardening. These mechanisms 
collectively lead to superior tensile strength and ductility at 
cryogenic temperatures.
