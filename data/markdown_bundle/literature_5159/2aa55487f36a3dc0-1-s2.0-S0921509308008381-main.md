abstract
The mechanical behaviour of Nb 35Ti6Al5Cr8V (at.%) containing different C contents (0, 1, 10 at.%) has
been investigated. Both conventional constant strain rate deformation and compressive creep tests have
been performed and the deformation microstructures have been examined using transmission electron
microscopy (TEM). At room temperature all three alloys deform primarily by slip, with some mechanical
twinning. Deformation at higher temperatures occurs by a combination of dislocation glide and climb pro-
cesses, giving more homogeneous microstructures. Most of the dislocations in the matrix of these alloys
have Burgers vector b = 1/2<1 1 1> although some short segments with b = <1 0 0> were also observed
where two dislocations with b = 1/2<1 1 1> interact during high-temperature deformation. Deformation
of carbide at high temperatures occurs primarily by{111 }<1 1 0> slip. The presence of extensive carbides
resulted in a substantial increase in yield strength at room and high temperatures and improved creep
resistance.

1. Introduction
To increase the efﬁciency and to reduce the emissions associ-
ated with the next generation of aeroengines, advanced structural
materials for applications at temperatures above the maximum
operating temperature of conventional high temperature Ni-base
superalloys [1,2] have been sought. Several structural materials,
such as intermetallics, refractory alloys, ceramics and C/C compos-
ites, have been suggested over the past decade as replacements
for Ni-based superalloys. Among them, Nb-based alloys have been
studied widely, because, although these alloys need to be coated,
Nb has a high melting point (2741 K) and a relatively low density,
as well as advantages in respect of alloying and manufacturing.
The strength of Nb decreases substantially at temperatures
above 1200 K but may be improved by various approaches, includ-
ing solid solution strengthening with Mo, Hf, Ta and W [3–5], and
dispersion or composite strengthening with intermetallic com-
pounds such as Nb
3Al and Nb 5Si [6,7], as well as with carbide
[8–11]. Another major problem in the development of Nb-based
superalloys is their low ductility at room temperature. A recently
developed /H9252Nb–Ti–Al-based alloy shows good ductility at room
temperature and reasonable strength at high temperature [12].
The alloy is only solid solution strengthened and, therefore, there

As a potential structural material designed for service at
elevated temperature, strength and creep resistance are very
important. This study, therefore, evaluates the mechanical
behaviour and deformation mechanisms of Nb–Ti–Al alloys with
different C contents.
2. Experimental procedure
Eight hundred grams buttons of alloys with nominal compo-
sition of Nb–35Ti–6Al–5Cr–8V–xC( x: 0, 1 and 10 at.%), which are
referred to as 0C, 1C and 10C alloys, were prepared in a Retech
transferred-arc plasma melter on a water-cooled hearth under a
positive pressure of Ar. High purity Nb, Ti, Al, Cr, V and TiC powder
were used as the starting materials. The mean size of the TiC pow-
der particle was 5 /H9262m according to the supplier speciﬁcation. The
buttons were remelted ﬁve times to promote chemical homogene-
ity, and were forged at 1100
◦C at a strain rate of 5× 10−3/s, followed
by annealing for 1 h at 1100◦C to provide samples for assessment
of mechanical properties and microstructure.
Compression tests at room temperature were carried
out on a Zwick 1484 universal mechanical testing machine
with a strain rate of 3 × 10
−3/s. Specimens with dimensions
4.5 mm× 4.5 mm× 9.0 mm were cut using an electro-discharge
machine (EDM).

3. Results and analysis
3.1. Mechanical behaviour
3.1.1. Constant strain rate deformation
All three alloys exhibited a good compression ductility even at
room temperature where no obvious cracking was observed after
15% plastic deformation. The value of yield stress measured for each
alloy at testing temperatures of 20, 800 and 1000
◦C are presented in
Table 1. The yield stress decreased with increasing temperature. As
expected, there is little difference in the yield stress between the 0C
and 1C alloys because of the low volume fraction (∼1%) of carbides
and the relatively large carbide size of 0.64/H9262m in the 1C alloy. How-
ever, adding 10 at.% C does improve signiﬁcantly the yield strength,
which is consistent with the high volume fraction of carbide in the
10C alloy.

three alloys.Fig. 1 shows stress–strain curves obtained from the 0C

appeared after upper yield at 1000
◦C. The reason for this difference
will be discussed later.

3.2. Deformation structure
The deformation mechanisms which were operative in the
alloys were investigated by post-mortem TEM examination of the
deformed microstructures.
3.2.1. Constant strain rate deformation
3.2.1.1. Room temperature. The alloys exhibited serrated yielding
during the initial stages of compression tests. Optical microscopy
was used to show that this was associated with the formation of
a high density of linear features (e.g. Fig. 5) similar to those which
would be expected after twinning in, for example, a b.c.c. metal.
TEM analysis of these features (Fig. 6) was used to show that whilst
they had a crystallographic relationship with the matrix which
is similar to that of a b.c.c. twin, they are actually regions which
have transformed to an orthorhombic crystal structure. Similar fea-
tures have been identiﬁed previously in Nb–Ti–Al alloys [13] and
are thought to be formed by a stress-induced martensitic trans-
formation. In addition to these “pseudo-twins” a high density of

3.2.3. Deformation mechanisms in the carbide
In the 1C and 10C alloy, there are two phases:/H9252phase (matrix)
and carbide. Two types of carbides (primary and secondary) were
observed in the 10C alloy; whereas only small carbides formed
in the 1C alloy. TEM study showed that, in the as-cast alloys, the
primary carbide is ordered fcc-type (Ti,Nb)
2C, whereas the small
(secondary) carbide is disordered fcc (Ti,Nb)C. More details are
given in Ref.[17]. However, forging plus annealing caused the small
carbides to order [18]. It is of interest to determine the deforma-
tion mechanism in the carbide phase. However, it was not possible
to study the deformation microstructure after deformation at room
temperature, because, in essence, the carbide remains undeformed.
In samples deformed at higher temperature, dislocation debris was
observed and observations of these defects are presented here.
Carbides were found either to be free of dislocations or to sus-
tain a relatively low dislocation density. It would be expected that
the local stress state and carbide orientation would inﬂuence the
occurrence of plastic deformation in the carbide. A set of two-beam
bright-ﬁeld images recorded in the carbide taken from a 10C sam-
ple deformed at approximately 6% at 800

4. Discussion
4.1. Serrated yielding
Serrations in stress–strain curves are associated with
microstructural instability. There are four explanations: inter-
action of mobile dislocations with solute atoms [21,22], also
known as the Portevin-Le Chatelier (PLC) effect; interaction
between dislocations and ﬁne particles [23]; interaction of planar
slip systems in ordered alloys[24]; formation of twins[25,26].I ti s

tion process is involved, to assist either solute atoms or precipitates

immediately on yielding, which is not as usually observed in

elevated temperature compression samples, no twin was found,
serrated ﬂow did not occur immediately on yielding and a sharp
upper yield point was observed in the stress–strain curve (Fig. 1b).
The alloys investigated appear to be disordered. The 0C alloy is
single phase (no precipitate). Therefore, serrated yielding during
high temperature compression could be attributed to Portevin-Le
Chatelier effect. Nevertheless, serrated ﬂow during room temper-

and thus probably corresponds to the formation of the linear
twin-like features in the microstructure (see Figs. 5 and 6a ). This
type of behaviour has also been identiﬁed in the Nb–Ti–Al [13]
and Nb–Al–V alloys [25,26]. These authors have shown that the

4.2. Evaluation of deformed microstructure
Examination of the defect microstructures for the three alloys
revealed clearly that the deformation was effected predominantly
by dislocation motion and that there was little or no effect of
carbide on deformation mode at room temperature. The defor-
mation in the three alloys at room temperature occurred mainly
via dislocation glide with some pseudotwinning. The most signiﬁ-
cant difference observed was a variation in dislocation morphology
with temperature: the dislocation conﬁgurations became more
homogeneous and curved and dislocation networks were formed,
indicating a transition from glide to climb mechanisms with
increasing testing temperature. The majority of dislocations have
b = 1/2<1 1 1>, although some short segments withb =< 100 >w e r e
also observed where two dislocations with b = 1/2<1 1 1> interact.

on creep can be twofold, namely: (i) as a direct strengthener, hence
reducing the minimum strain rate and (ii) increasing the activa-

of 1 at.% C does not improve the strength (Table 1), i.e. adding 1 at.%

that there is little difference between the values of the activation
energy and creep rate obtained for the 0C and 1C alloy. This could be
attributed to a relatively large carbide size (0.64/H9262m) and a low vol-

containing 12% volume fraction of carbides, exhibited a lower creep
rate and signiﬁcantly higher activation energy, which is consistent
with the presence of extensive obstacles to dislocation motion in
diffusion-controlled high temperature creep.
The defect microstructures of the crept specimens were not
clearly different from those deformed at constant strain, which is
consistent with the fact that the creep behaviour was predomi-
nantly steady state.
5. Summary
In this study, the mechanical behaviour of Nb–Ti–Al alloy with
different C contents has been evaluated. The samples were tested at
room temperature, 800 and 1000
◦C under constant strain rate and
creep tested at 800–1000◦C under constant load. The deformation
microstructures were then examined using TEM. The main points
that have been established are:
1. The carbide phase present in the 1C and 10C alloys deforms at
high temperature by the glide of dislocations with 1/2<1 1 0> on

{111 } planes. The presence of extensive carbides led to a sub-
stantial increase in yield strength at room and high temperatures
and to improved creep resistance.
2. At room temperature, the deformation was very inhomogeneous
and dominated by the glide of dislocations withb = 1/2<1 1 1> on
{110 } and {112 } planes. A transient serrated yielding regime
was associated with martensitic pseudo-twinning.
3. At 800
◦C and above, both dislocation glide and climb operate and
the dislocations were distributed more homogeneously. Similar
deformation structures were observed for both constant strain
rate compression and creep at high temperatures.
