Hot Deformation Behavior and Processing Map of
High-Strength Mg-2.5Nd-0.2Zn-1.0Zr Alloy
Xingwei Zheng, Peng Luo, and Zhilin Han
Submitted: 23 March 2021 / Revised: 30 June 2021 / Accepted: 12 August 2021 / Published online: 4 October 2021
Hot deformation behavior of high-strength Mg-2.5Nd-0.2Zn-1.0Zr (in wt.%) alloy was investigated by the
aid of tensile tests at the temperatures ranging from 200 to 400 /C176C and strain rates varying from 0.001 to 1
s21. The constitutive relationship of the alloy was modeled by a hyperbolic sine equation based on
experimental result. A processing map of the alloy was designed by dynamic material model, which formed
a domain of instability at inferior degree of deformation temperature (200-300 /C176C) and relatively large
strain rate (1-0.02 s
21). Accordingly, a domain of stability was developed implying the maximum efﬁciency
of power dissipation as high as 46.2%, and relating a couple of optimum parameter values to deformation
process, i.e., deformation temperature of 380 /C176C and strain rate of 0.006 s
21, respectively. Different mor-
phological features of the fracture surface on tensile sample were revealed varying from quasi-cleavage to
ductile fracture by decreasing strain rate and simultaneously increasing deformation temperature.
Keywords constitutive equation, magnesium alloy, processing
map
1. Introduction
As an important light metal, magnesium alloy has gained a
wide spectrum of applications in industries of automobile,
aerospace, defense and electronic manufacturing. However, a
number of technical disadvantages are appreciable to magne-
sium alloys suffering from unsatisfactory mechanical proper-
ties, inferior heat capacity and poor corrosion resistance (Ref 1,
2). The limitation of mechanical properties is referred to as a
primary barrier to the development of magnesium alloys for
engineering use. To date, two types of technologies, i.e., severe
plastic deformation and alloying, have been designed to
improve mechanical properties of magnesium alloys (Ref 3-
8). In general practice, metals (and alloys) are effectively
strengthened by grain reﬁnement after severe plastic deforma-
tion (Ref 6-8). However, complex and/or costly process
designed for severe plastic deformation constrains technical
outreach of itself in industrial applications. In particular, it is
difﬁcult to achieve continuous process of severe plastic
deformation. Instead, the addition of alloying elements, espe-
cially rare earth, has become an important way to improve the
mechanical properties of magnesium alloys.
In view of performance improvement by alloying, it is
promising to enhance mechanical properties, heat proof, and
corrosion resistance by adding heavy rare earth (Gd) to
magnesium alloy, e.g., Mg-Gd-Y -Zr (Ref 3), Mg-Gd-Nd-Zr
(Ref 4), and Mg-Gd-Sm-Zr (Ref 5), in which large content of
Gd (more than 8 wt.%) guarantees prominent effect of
strengthening although this beneﬁcial effect is at the cost of
ﬁnancial budget and weight increase. In contrast, as a light rare
earth, Nd, even though with a small value of content, is efﬁcient
to achieving strong effect of aging strengthening because the
solid solubility of Nd in magnesium varies greatly, i.e., from
3.6 to 0.08 wt.% under the cooling from 200 /C176C to room
temperature. In addition, the deformability of magnesium alloys
was improved due to a weakened texture caused by the addition
of Nd (Ref 1, 2). Furthermore, Nd is much cheaper than Gd. As
a result, high strength is compatible with low cost in
neodymium-containing magnesium alloys, e.g., Mg-2.5Nd-
0.5Zn-0.5Zr (in wt.%) alloy with tensile strength of 260 MPa,
yield strength of 140 MPa, and elongation of 30% (Ref 9), and
Mg-3Nd-0.2Zn-1.0Zr (in wt.%) alloy with 325 MPa, 314 MPa,
and 19.3%, respectively (Ref 10). According to Ma (Ref 11),
Mg-2.5Nd-0.2Zn-1.0Zr (in wt.%) was designed endowing
tensile strength and yield strength as high as 379 MPa and
377 MPa, respectively, and considerable elongation /C24 11.5%.
By these works, excellent deformability was demonstrated in
Mg-Nd-Zn-Zr system (Ref 12-15).
The application of considerable degree of temperature is
essential when wrought magnesium alloys are deformed. To
evaluate high-temperature ﬂow stress (by a constitutive equa-
tion obeying hyperbolic sine law) is the key to understand hot
deformation of novel wrought magnesium alloys (Ref 16-19).
Dynamic materials model (DMM) and the resulting processing
maps are effective to understand ﬂow behavior, manipulate
microstructure, and optimize process parameters (Ref 20-24),
on which deformation mechanisms under various conditions
can be further unveiled. Studies of deformation using process-
ing map have been extensively reported in order to develop
novel wrought alloys, e.g., Mg alloy (Ref 20, 21) and Pb alloy
(Ref 22-24). Motivated by the precedent efforts, in this paper,
hot deformation behavior of newly designed high-strength Mg-
2.5Nd-0.2Zn-1.0Zr alloy (in wt.%, branded with NZ250K) was
systematically studied by analyzing ﬂow stress–strain relation
(i.e., constitutive equation) and establishing processing maps.

2. Experimental and Materials
2.1 Materials Preparation
In the circumstance of protective gases mixed with SF 6 and
CO2, NZ250K alloy was melted with pure Mg, pure Zn, and
master alloys of Mg-20%Nd and Mg-20%Zr in a steel crucible
heated by an electrical resistance furnace. Then, the melt was
cast into a copper mold after a standard process stepped by
degasiﬁcation, slag removing, and reﬁning at 720 /C176C. The cast
ingots subjected to solution treatment (at 540 /C176C for 2 hours)
were extruded at 350 /C176C with a speed of 1 mm s
/C0 1. Finally, T5
treatment (artiﬁcial aging after extrusion) was conducted at
relatively low temperature of 200 /C176C, but for a long period up
to 10 hours, in order to improve the mechanical properties of
the extruded alloy. Chemical composition of as-cast alloy was
analyzed by an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission
spectroscopy (ICP-AES). The actual values of Nd, Zn and Zr
are 2.48, 0.23 and 1.03%, respectively. The balance is
magnesium, which indicates actual concentrations of chemical
elements are similar to the nominal compositions of the alloy.
2.2 Microstructure Characterization and Tensile Tests
Tensile samples having a planar shape like ‘‘dog-bone’’
were machined from the center of the extruded billets (along
the direction of extrusion). The sizes of the samples were 10
mm in width, 2 mm in thickness and 30 mm in gauge length.
Tensile tests were carried out on a Zwick/Roell-20 kN machine
at the temperatures ranging from 200 to 400 /C176C and strain rates
varying from 1 910
/C0 3 to 1 s /C0 1. Homogeneous distribution of
the temperature was guaranteed in tensile samples by holding
them at desired temperature for 10 min. The tensile tests under
the identical condition were repeated for at least three times for
representative average. Rapid cooling was realized by quench-
ing the tensile samples in hot water (50 /C176C) in order to prevent
the occurrence of recrystallization or grain growth, and as a
result, to maintain the microstructure feature formed with the
failure at high temperature. Metallographic samples were
mounted, ground, and polished. An optical microscopy was
used to inspect the polished surface etched with a solution of
phosphoric-picral (0.7 ml phosphoric acid, 4.2 g picric acid,
and 100 ml ethanol). Morphological feature of the fracture
surface of tensile sample was observed by scanning electron
microscopy (SEM, Leo 1455 at 20 kV).

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Microstructure and Mechanical Properties
Figure 1 shows the microstructures of as-cast and as-
extruded NZ250K alloys. The microstructure of the as-cast
alloy was characterized by coarse equiaxed grains with the
diameters ranging from 30 to 50 lm (as shown in Fig. 1a).
Eutectic compounds are discontinuously distributed along grain
boundaries in the as-cast alloy. Similarly, the as-extruded
NZ250K alloy is composed of equiaxed grains but with ﬁner
grain sizes of 10-25 lm. Streamlines of second phase are
clearly exhibited after extrusion thanks to the disruption of the
as-cast state of compound distribution (as shown in Fig. 1b).
The XRD patterns of as-cast and as-extruded NZ250K alloys
are shown in Fig. 1c, which identify phase composition of a-
Mg and Mg
12Nd, the latter of which is also conﬁrmed in other
Mg-Nd alloys (Ref 10, 15, 25). However, the particles
containing zirconium (e.g., Zn 2Zr3 and ZnZr 2) clustered in
the interior of grains were identiﬁed by TEM (Ref 10), but they
were not identiﬁable with the XRD pattern in this work,
probably because the volume fraction of zirconium-containing
particles is too small. Since both types of particles are
unsolvable to melt, constitutional super-cooling was possible
in molten magnesium alloy with the addition of Nd and Zr as
effective grain reﬁners, beneﬁcial to the nucleation and growth
of equiaxed a-Mg grains (Ref 26).
Figure 2 shows stress vs. strain curves of the as-extruded
alloy tensioned at different temperatures and strain rates. It can
be seen that stress–strain curves can be divided into two types.
The ﬁrst type stress–strain curves exhibit a sharp increase at the
initial stage and then slowly increase up to the peak, after which
the ﬂow stress decreases slightly to fracture as the tensile
temperature is lower than 300 /C176C and strain rate is higher than
1910

sharp increase at the initial stage and then remain nearly
constant for a long time as the tensile temperature is higher than
300 /C176C and strain rate is lower than 1 910
/C0 3 s/C0 1, suggesting
that the deformation of NZ250K alloy is very uniform in this
process range. It also reveals that the ﬂow stress behavior of the
NZ250K alloy is signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced by the tensile
temperature and strain rate.
High-temperature plastic ﬂow was a resultant effect of
thermal softening and strain hardening. This effect combining
the softening and hardening was governed by the competition
between dislocation strengthening and dynamic recovery
during the tensile testing of either magnesium alloys (Ref 16-
18) or aluminum alloys (Ref 27, 28). Dislocation density was
dramatically increased at the initial stage of plastic ﬂow. As a
result, strain hardening was superior over thermal softening, as
evidenced by the observation that tensile stress was tremen-
dously increased until the occurrence of necking. The exhibi-
tion of peak stress implied the equilibrium between the
hardening and softening. However, strain hardening was
counteracted by dynamic recovery. Subsequently, thermal
softening took priority leading to the drop of ﬂow stress at
the ultimate stage of the tension. As a primary cause for thermal
softening, dynamic recovery is most likely to occur in
magnesium alloys of low stacking fault energy. The plastic
ﬂow behavior of the extruded NZ250K wrought alloy is typical
for the case in which dynamic recovery took place.

3.4 Deformed Microstructures and Fracture Morphologies
of Mg-2.5Nd-0.2Zn-1.0Zr Alloy
Dynamic recovery (DRV) and dynamic recrystallization
(DRX), highly sensitive to deformation temperature and strain
rate, take remarkable effect on hot deformation of magnesium
alloy. Figure 10 shows the microstructures optically inspected
on the section of tensile samples where ﬂow stress was mostly
localized (i.e., the place where necking and ultimate failure
were occurred) at different temperatures and strain rates of
tension. It is clear that compared with as-extruded microstruc-
ture, streamlines were sparsely presented at strain rate of 1 s
/C0 1
and the temperature of 200 /C176C (see Fig. 10a), and totally
disappeared at 0.01 s /C0 1 and 200 /C176C (see Fig. 10b). Obviously,
DRX was prominent at decreased strain rate. Fig. 10c shows
ﬁne grains formed owing to the occurrence of DRX at 0.01 s /C0 1
and 250 /C176C, most likely on precedent grain boundaries formed
after the extrusion. Moreover, nearly one-third of grains were
elongated following tensile direction, consistent with the
observation of hot extruded NZ30K alloy (Ref 13), considered
to be primary cause responsible for improving the resistance to
creep and thermal fatigue of magnesium alloys (Ref 13). The
fraction of grains indebted to DRX was gradually increased
with tensile temperature. Fully recrystallized grains were
produced at 0.001 s
/C0 1 and 400 /C176C (see Fig. 10d). DRV
contributed mostly the softening of the alloy at tensile
temperature below 200 /C176C, whereas DRX took the place of
DRV in activating the softening at the temperature higher than
250 /C176C. No matter which type of thermal restoration is
beneﬁcial to the workability of hot deformation by lowering
ﬂow stress (Ref 27).
The failure of magnesium alloy is generally considered to be
brittle because of a pronounced characteristic of cleavage or
quasi-cleavage likely to be observed in hexagonal close-packed
structure (Ref 13). Figure 11 shows the morphologies of the
fracture surface of the NZ250K alloy tensioned under the
conditions with tensile temperature of 200 /C176C or 350 /C176C, and
strain rate of 1 s
/C0 1 or 0.001 s /C0 1, respectively. The phenomenon
of necking was negligible on the sample tensioned at the
temperature of 200 /C176C and strain rate of 1 s /C0 1. Although the
morphology of the fracture surface was entirely smooth
(Fig. 10a), tear ridges, cleavages, and more or less dimples
were revealed by SEM inspection. Obviously, brittle failure (a
quasi-cleavage fracture) is appreciable to the alloy tensioned at
200 /C176C and strain rate of 1 s
/C0 1, i.e., the parameters with which
poor workability was indicated on the processing map of
NZ250K alloy.
Figure 11c shows SEM fractograph of the alloy tensioned at
350 /C176C and strain rate of 0.001 s /C0 1. Good workability of
NZ250K alloy was guaranteed by this deformation condition
indicated by the processing map. A phenomenon of necking
was prominently presented. Rough morphology of the fracture
surface was found by SEM inspection. Homogeneous distri-
bution of a lot of dimples was revealed by a closer inspection of
SEM on the fracture surface (Fig. 10d), which implied a
continuous uniform plastic ﬂow prior to ductile fracture
(Fig. 12).
