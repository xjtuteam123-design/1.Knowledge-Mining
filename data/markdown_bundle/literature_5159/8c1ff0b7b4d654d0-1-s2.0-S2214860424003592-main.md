ABSTRACT
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) processed Al – Ni alloys typically exhibit cellular structures, contributing to su-
perior strength at ambient temperature as the cell walls can effectively impede dislocation motion during plastic
deformation. However, it is still unclear whether cellular structures can lead to excellent elevated-temperature
performance. In this study, we conducted a systematic investigation into the elevated-temperature mechanical
properties and thermal stability of an LPBF Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy with cellular structures. The as-built alloy exhibits
a yield strength of 368 MPa at 25 ℃ and 332 MPa at 250 ℃ , with an exceptional strength retention rate of ~90 %
at 250 ℃ . Moreover, the alloy maintains a hardness of ~146 HV following exposure at 250 ℃ for 100 h and shows
no apparent decrease despite increasing the exposure time to 500 h. The excellent elevated-temperature per-
formance can be attributed to the cellular structures. The cell walls effectively confine dislocations at elevated
temperatures, suppressing the dislocation annihilation and leading to exceptional strength retention. On the
other hand, even if the cell walls decompose into nanoparticles after prolonged thermal exposure, the reduction
in strength due to the decomposition can be compensated by the strengthening of the nanoparticles, resulting in
excellent thermal stability. By studying the influence of the cellular structures on elevated-temperature perfor-
mance, this study could provide valuable insights into developing high-performance Al alloys for elevated-
temperature applications by additive manufacturing.
1. Introduction
High-strength age-hardening Al alloys, such as the 2xxx, 6xxx and
7xxx series alloys, are widely used as structural materials in the auto-
motive and aerospace industries, primarily due to their remarkable
specific strength [1 – 3] . However, the application of conventional
age-hardening Al alloys in elevated-temperature environments remains
plagued by their rapid strength degradation [4,5] . This is because the
precipitates in conventional Al alloys are easily coarsened when exposed
to temperatures exceeding 200
◦
C, which results in a decrease in the
precipitation-strengthening efficacy [6] . Moreover, Al alloys are known
to display high stacking-fault energy. Elevated temperatures facilitate
the annihilation of dislocations via the dislocation climbing mechanism,
leading to a reduction in the strengthening effect of dislocations [7] .
These factors typically result in conventional Al alloys exhibiting
unsatisfactory elevated-temperature strength and thermal stability.
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) technology has been identified as a
promising approach to the design and fabrication of high-performance
Al alloys for elevated-temperature applications [8,9] . This can be
attributed to the special microstructures resulting from the unique
thermal history during LPBF. Such structures generally include bimodal
grain structures [10,11] , supersaturated solid solutions [12] , and
cellular structures. Notably, cellular structures have been increasingly
recognised in recent years for their potential to enhance the strength of
alloys [8,13 – 15] . This is because the cell walls of the cellular structures
are considered the stronger obstacles to dislocation motion compared to
other structural features such as element solutions and precipitates
[16 – 18] . For instance, LPBF processed Al – Si alloys with cellular struc-
tures show an impressive improvement of nearly 60 % in ultimate tensile
strength (UTS) compared to their cast state [19,20] .

The Al – Ni system is one of the most promising alloys for elevated-
temperature applications. This is because the Ni element in the Al ma-
trix has a relatively low diffusion rate and solubility compared to other
common alloying elements such as Si, Mg, and Zn [5] . This implies
enhanced resistance to coarsening for Al – Ni phases at elevated tem-
peratures, which is advantageous for elevated-temperature use. In
addition, previous studies have demonstrated that the LPBF processed
Al – Ni eutectic alloys can exhibit cellular structures, comprising the Al
matrix cell interior and divorced-eutectic Al – Ni cell walls [21] . The
Al – Ni cell walls are expected to contribute to an effective strengthening
for LPBF Al – Ni alloys at ambient temperature. However, few studies
have focused on the elevated-temperature mechanical properties and
thermal stability of LPBF Al – Ni alloys. Furthermore, even fewer studies
have explored the intrinsic relationship between cellular structures and
elevated-temperature performance in Al alloys. Thus, it is still unclear
whether the cell walls can effectively impede dislocation motion at
elevated temperatures, and whether the cellular structures can be sus-
tained during prolonged thermal exposure.
In this study, we attempt to develop an Al – Ni alloy with complete
cellular structures to obtain improved elevated-temperature mechanical
properties. The elements Sc and Zr with slower diffusion rates are
introduced into the Al – Ni alloy, to further enhance the thermal stability
of the alloy [22 – 26] . The microstructure, mechanical properties and
thermal stability of an LPBF Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy will be evaluated and
examined. We expect to check whether the cellular structures, which are
effective barriers to dislocation motion and storage at ambient temper-
ature, can be effective at elevated temperatures. By understanding the
formation mechanism of cellular structures and their relationship to
elevated-temperature performance, this study will provide valuable in-
sights for the development of high-performance Al alloys suitable for
elevated-temperature applications.

generate cellular structures, which inevitably results in the formation of
many hypereutectic or eutectic regions in positions with lower under-

below 3 wt%) narrow the undercooling range within which cellular
structures can be formed, potentially resulting in the inability to form
sufficient cellular structures [21] . Therefore, to acquire cellular struc-
tures, the Ni contents within the range of 4 – 6 wt% are considered to be
reasonable. Additionally, near-eutectic compositions exhibit a narrow
solidification interval, indicating a reduced susceptibility to hot
cracking [27] . Consequently, a near-eutectic alloy with a Ni content of
5.6 wt% was selected as the focus of this study. During the initial stage of
solidification in the Al – 5.6Ni alloy, an undercooled state exists at the
solid – liquid (S – L) interface (e.g. point 1 in Fig. 1 ), which leads to the
formation of cellular/dendritic structures. α -Al solidifies with the
composition at point 2, while the residual concentrated liquid is dis-
played and trapped along the cell boundaries. Meanwhile, the trapped
liquid enters the eutectic couple zone (point 3), resulting in the forma-
tion of a eutectic network as cell walls.
2.2. Laser powder bed fusion process
Argon gas atomized powder with diameters ranging from 15 to 53
μ m was subjected to LPBF fabrication. The chemical compositions of the
alloy powder were measured by inductively coupled plasma atomic
emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES, iCAP7600), as presented in Table 1 .
The LPBF experiments were conducted using a commercial ProX DMP
200 machine (3D Systems, USA) equipped with a 300 W fibre laser. The
laser spot size is 75 μ m and the wavelength is 1070 nm. Fig. 2 shows the
schematic diagram of the scanning strategy employed for the as-built
samples. The side view is a surface parallel to the building direction
(BD) and perpendicular to the Y-axis. A parameter study was conducted
with the objective of fabricating defect-free parts. The optimal pro-
cessing parameters were identified, which can be used to achieve the
highest relative density. After the optimisation of process parameters,
both cubic-shaped and dog bone-shaped samples were built with a laser
power of 285 W, scan speed of 1500 mm/s, hatch space of 90 μ m, and
layer thickness of 30 μ m. A separate-area scanning strategy was adopted
with a 90
◦
rotation between layers. The angle between the scanning
direction and the X-axis is 45
◦
for every layer ( Fig. 2 ). The fabrication
process was performed in a high-purity argon atmosphere with an ox-
ygen content below 200 ppm.

3. Results and analysis
3.1. Printability assessment
The inherent characteristics of Al alloys, such as the high laser
reflectivity, low viscosity in the molten state, and high thermal con-
ductivity, contribute to the formation of various micro defects in the Al
alloy components processed by LPBF. These defects include hot cracks,
gas pores, and a lack of fusion [28,29] . The excessive presence of these
defects can deteriorate the mechanical properties of LPBF components.
The Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy with near-eutectic compositions has a narrow
freezing range, and the transformation from liquid to solid is nearly
instantaneous, which effectively reduces the hot cracking sensitivity
[27] . A magnified 3D porosity distribution image ( Fig. 3 a) was recon-
structed using CT to evaluate the printability of the LPBF Al – Ni – Sc – Zr
alloy. No visible cracks are observed in the as-built alloy, and all defects
exhibit a nearly spherical shape. Fig. 3 b illustrates that over 90 % of the
defects have an equivalent diameter below 10 μ m. However, the larger
defects account for the majority of the volume cumulative. This high-
lights the significance of reducing the number of large defects to
enhance the relative density of the LPBF sample. The defects larger than
26 μ m are almost absent in the as-built Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy. In contrast,
other LPBF Al alloys (e.g., AlSi10Mg alloy) typically process larger
defect sizes ( > 200 μ m) [30] . Consequently, the LPBF Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy
demonstrates exceptional printability, as evidenced by its maximum
relative density of 99.99 %. This can be primarily attributed to the
successful elimination of large defects.

3.3. Mechanical properties
Fig. 8 a displays the tensile stress – strain curves of the as-built
Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy at different temperatures. Fig. 8 b illustrates the var-
iations in strength and elongation with increasing temperature. At
ambient temperature, the as-built samples exhibit an average ultimate
tensile strength (UTS) of 460 MPa and a yield strength (YS) of 368 MPa
( Table 2 ). Remarkably, the YS and UTS of LPBF Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy at
200 ℃ are about 367 and 394 MPa, respectively ( Table 2 ). The YS at
200 ℃ is identical to that at ambient temperature, indicating that the YS
can be sustained without any reduction when exposed to the elevated
temperature. At 250 ℃ , the as-built alloy exhibits a YS and UTS of 332
and 337 MPa, respectively. In comparison to the ambient temperature,
the YS only has a slight decline, and the strength retention rate (defined
as the ratio between the YS of the alloy at elevated and ambient tem-
peratures) reaches 90 %.

3.4. Thermal stability at elevated temperatures
Fig. 9 a – e illustrate the microstructure evolution of the LPBF
Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy under thermal exposure. It can be observed that the
majority of the melt pool is comprised of partial and intact-fine cell
regions, with a limited fraction of the intact-coarse cell region ( Fig. 5
and 9). Consequently, this study mainly focuses on the microstructure
evolution of the partial and intact-fine cell regions after thermal expo-
sure. Following thermal exposure for 100 h at 250 ℃ , the intact-coarse,
intact-fine and partial cell regions can still be observed from the bottom
to the top of the melt pool ( Fig. 9 a). Most of the cellular structures can be
retained in comparison to the microstructure at ambient temperature
( Fig. 9 a).
As the exposure time increases to 300 h, most of the cell walls lose
their cellular morphology and are replaced by nanoscale particles. Only
a few residual intact-fine cells maintain the initial shape ( Fig. 9 b). In
addition, a small number of coarse particles with an average diameter
exceeding 100 nm are observed in both the intact-fine and partial cell
regions ( Fig. 9 b). Notably, these coarse particles are arranged in a reg-
ular linear pattern, as indicated by the white arrows in Fig. 9 b. All the
coarse-particle lines possess a nearly fixed direction parallel to the BD or
epitaxial growth direction. The distance between these lines is ~5 μ m,
which is consistent with the width range of the columnar grains ( Fig. 1 ).
In addition, it has been observed in the as-built alloy that Al – Ni phases
can indeed exist at the columnar grain boundaries ( Fig. 7 a). Conse-
quently, it is reasonable to infer that these coarse particles are located at
the boundaries of the columnar grains, as grain boundaries can enhance
atom diffusion rate. This coarsening of the Al – Ni phase at grain
boundaries is further demonstrated by the EBSD maps ( Fig. S2 ).

sample exposed at 250 ℃ for 300 h, most of the nanoscale particles in

4.1. Formation mechanism of multiple cellular structures
The as-built Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy exhibits multiple cellular structures
with a decreasing size gradient along the BD ( Fig. 5 ). Traditional so-
lidification theories suggest that G and V are crucial parameters that
determine the crystal growth interface and length scale of the sub-
structure [47] . A microstructure selection map ( Fig. 11 a) in the G – V
space with fixed C
0
(5.6 wt% Ni) illustrates the microstructure charac-
teristics under various G and V values [21] . Fig. 11 b illustrates the
variations of G and V within the melt pool. G gradually decreases as V
increases with distance from the bottom of the melt pool. The simulation
of G was performed using the commercial software ABAQUS, and V was
obtained through Eq. 1 . The solidification path associated with the
changes in G and V is shown in Fig. 11 a.
The morphology of the crystal growth interface is influenced by the
G / V ratio [47] . During the onset of solidification (i.e. near the MPB), the
G / V value rapidly decreases with the increase of the distance from the
bottom ( Fig. 11 c). This leads to a rapid transition of the S – L interface
morphology from a dendritic to a cellular interface ( Fig. 11 a). Conse-
quently, the microstructure of the as-built Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy does not
exhibit pronounced dendritic branching ( Fig. 5 ) resulting in the for-
mation of an almost complete cellular morphology ( Fig. 5 ).

The formation of the cell walls is typically influenced by the diffusion
of solute elements during cellular/dendritic growth [48] . In the as-built
Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy, the solidification front rejects the Ni element to the
cell boundaries, facilitating the formation of the Al
9
Ni
2
cell wall. In the
lower region of the melt pool (the intact-fine cell region), the relatively
lower V ( Fig. 11 b) suggests that the supersaturated solubility of Ni is
lower, as more Ni tends to be rejected from the Al matrix, ensuring the
formation of intact Al – Ni cell walls. Conversely, the V value in the top
region of the melt pool (the partial cell region) is relatively high
( Fig. 11 b). Therefore, the Al matrix has a higher solute supersaturation
of Ni [5] , and the rejection of Ni from the Al matrix is reduced compared
to the lower region of the melt pool. As a result, precipitation of the
Al
9
Ni
2
phase tends to be suppressed. Furthermore, the partial cells in the
top region of the melt pool have a smaller cell size. This means that the
area fraction of the cell walls increases. Consequently, a greater amount
of Ni is required to form the increased cell walls. Therefore, the partial
cellular structure is formed in the top region of the melt pool due to the
insufficient amount of rejected Ni solute.
For the intact-coarse cells, the cell walls coincide with the boundaries
of the UEGs ( Fig. 7 c and d), as the small size of the UEGs can prevent the
formation of smaller cells within the grains. The solidification front re-
jects Ni elements into the liquid phase, resulting in the formation of
Al – Ni phases at the UEG boundaries.
In summary, with the increase of distance from the bottom of the
melt pool, there is a transition from the intact cells to partial cells along
the BD ( Fig. 5 ). Consequently, the as-built Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy exhibits
unique multiple cellular structures.

4.3. Thermal stability mechanism
The as-built Al – Ni – Sc – Zr alloy demonstrates excellent thermal sta-
bility, and retains its hardness without an obvious decrease ( Fig. 9 f).
This excellent thermal stability is also closely related to its microstruc-
ture. The previous results show that the cells can retain their size and
shape even after the thermal exposure at 250 ℃ for 100 h ( Fig. 9 a). This
indicates that the cellular structures have excellent size stability so that
the hardness does not decrease after exposure at 250 ℃ for 100 h
( Fig. 9 f). The size stability of the cellular structures is, on the one hand,
due to the relatively low diffusion rate and equilibrium solid solubility of
Ni in the Al matrix [58,59] . On the other hand, the APT results reveal the
segregation of Sc at the cell walls ( Fig. 14 ). A segregation layer of Zr is
also identified at the cell wall interface, although its enrichment is
insignificant because of its low concentration ( Fig. 14 c). From a kinetic
perspective, the segregation of slow-diffusion elements between the
Al – Ni cell wall and the Al matrix can effectively impede interface
migration, thereby slowing down the ripening process of the Al – Ni
phases [22,23] . Furthermore, from a thermodynamic perspective, the
segregation of Sc and Zr at the cell wall interface has the potential to
reduce the interfacial energy between the Al

trix, thereby effectively reducing the driving force for the coarsening of
the Al – Ni phases [21] .

5. Conclusion
This study introduces a high-performance LPBF Al–Ni–Sc–Zr alloy
for elevated-temperature applications, showing excellent strength and
thermal stability. Detailed analysis and discussion reveal the potential
mechanisms for excellent elevated-temperature performance. The
following conclusions can be drawn:
(1) A eutectic Al–Ni–Sc–Zr alloy containing ~5.6 wt% Ni is designed
and fabricated by LPBF, exhibiting multiple cellular structures
with Al matrix cell interior and Al
9
Ni
2
phase cell walls. The
multiple cellular structures containing intact-coarse, intact-fine
and partial cell regions, are identified along the building
direction.
(2) The as-built alloy shows excellent elevated-temperature perfor-
mance, maintaining a yield strength of 332 MPa with 90 %
retention at 250℃. The high strength at elevated temperatures is
primarily due to the continuous integral cell walls, which can
effectively confine dislocations within the cell walls, thereby
suppressing dislocation annihilation and strength reduction.
(3) The alloy exhibits remarkable thermal stability, maintaining the
cellular structures after exposure at 250℃ for 100 h, without any
loss of hardness. After exposure for 500 h, the cell walls gradually
decompose into high density of nanoparticles that can compen-
sate for the weakening effect caused by cell decomposition. As a
result, the hardness can be maintained at almost the initial value
even after exposure at 250℃ for 500 h.
