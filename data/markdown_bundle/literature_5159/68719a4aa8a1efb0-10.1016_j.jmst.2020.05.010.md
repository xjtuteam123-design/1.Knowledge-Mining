Recently, high- and medium-entropy alloys (HEAs and MEAs) have been found to exhibit 
excellent cryogenic mechanical properties, but most of them contain high-priced Co 
element. Therefore, developing HEAs or MEAs with high strength and ductility and 
relatively low cost is urgent. In this work, novel Co-free FexMn75-xNi10Cr15 (x=50 and 55 
at.%) MEAs were developed, which exhibit a good combination of low cost, high strength 
and ductility at cryogenic temperature. It was found that the Fe50Mn25Ni10Cr15 MEA 
exhibits a combination of cryogenic tensile strength of ~ 0.98 GPa and ductility of ~ 83%.

The excellent cryogenic mechanical properties were attributed to joint of twinning-

present study sheds light on developing low cost MEAs with high performance for

1. Introduction 
Metals with high strength and ductility at cryogenic temperature s are strongly demanded 
since the ductile -to-brittle transition often occurs with decreasing temperature [1-3]. Recently, 
medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) and high -entropy alloys (HEAs), as advanced structural 
materials with high performance properties, were extensively investigated [4-7]. Especially, face -
centered-cubic (FCC) structured HEAs/MEAs become an interesting class of cryogenic 
structural materials, because their strengt h and ductility are further improved with the decrease 
of temperature [8-14]. The underlying cryogenic strengthening mechanism of these HEAs/MEAs is 
the substantial interaction between lattice dislocations and deformation-induced nanoscale 
twinning [11, 12, 15] . To further improve their mechanical properties, transformation -induced 
plasticity (TRIP) is introduced in HEAs/MEAs [1, 16 -18]. Via a strain-induced FCC -to-hexagonal-
close-packed (HCP) transformation, the Co 50Cr25Fe10Ni10Mo5 HEA exhibits ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS) of 1.1 GPa and ductility of ~61% at room temperature [16], while the 
Fe40Mn40Co10Cr10 HEA has high cryogenic UTS of ~1.0 GPa and ductility of ~65% [9]. Bae et 
al.[1] reported a Fe 60Co15Ni15Cr10 MEA exhibiting cryogenic UTS of ~1.5 GPa and ductility of 
~87% via a str ain-induced FCC -to-body-centered-cubic (BCC) transformation. However, most of 
these TRIP HEAs/MEAs contain high -priced Co element [1, 9, 16, 17, 19 -21], largely limiting their 
commercial applications [7]. Therefore, it is strongly desirable t o design new Co -free 
HEAs/MEAs with excellent mechanical properties at cryogenic temperatures [22-24]. 
Zhao et al. [22] prepared a nanometer -sized precipitation strengthened 
(FeNi)67Cr15Mn10Al3Ti5 HEA, exhibiting UTS of 1.25 GPa with ductility over 20 % at room 
temperature . The FeNiMnCr 18 HEA can be stretched to ~ 51% strain and its UTS can reach to ~ 
0.91 GPa at 77 K due to the twinning induced plasticity (TWIP) [23]. Joint contributions of TRIP 
and TWIP might be a good strategy to improve the cryogenic strength and ductility of Co -free 
HEAs/MEAs, which has not been reported yet. In this work, we have exploited the FCC-to-HCP 
phase transformation in Co -free non-equiatomic Fe xMn75-xNi10Cr15 (x=50 and 55 at .%) MEAs for

2. Experimental 
The ingots of the Fe xMn75-xNi10Cr15 (x=40, 45, 50 and 55 at .%) MEAs, labeled as Fe40, 
Fe45, Fe50 and Fe55 , respectively, were pr epared in vacuum arc melting equipped with an 
electromagnetic stirring under a Ti -getter argon atmosphere. The purity of the elements (Fe, Mn, 
Ni and Cr) was greater than 99.9 wt%. To ensure chemical homogeneity, each ingot was flipped 
and re-melted 5 time s, and then drop casting into Cu molds with a size of 100 mm × 20 mm × 10 
mm. Both MEAs were subsequently rolled at room temperature with a thickness reduction ratio 
of 50%. The rolled plates were annealed at 1173 K for 10 min, and subsequently water -quenched 
to obtain fully recrystallized microstructures. The structures of the plates were checked by X -ray 
diffraction (XRD) with a Cu Kα radiation. Phase characterization and deformation behavior of the 
specimens were investigated by scanning electron microsco pe (SEM) equipped with electron 
back-scatter diffraction (EBSD). During the EBSD measurement, the step size of 0.3 μm was 
used. Details of the preparation method of EBSD specimens are described in Ref. [14]. 
Flat dog-bone shape specimens with gauge dimensi ons of 10 mm × 2 mm × 1.5 mm were 
cut from these plates. The uniaxial tensile tests at 298 K and 77 K were conducted using a SUNS 
5105 testing machine with a constant grips displacement rate of 0.5 mm/min. Prior to testing, all 
faces of their gage sections were polished by 800-grit SiC paper. For the tests at 77 K, the 
specimens and grips are fully immersed in liquid nitrogen for 10 min to stabilize the temperature 
before the testing. Keep adding liquid nitrogen to make sure that the specimens are always 
soaked in liquid nitrogen during the tests. Scale lines were made along the specimen gage 
lengths, the change in the distance between the scale lines was used to calculate the engineering 
strain. The test method of engineering strain in this work is similar to the method in Ref. [ 23]. 
Three samples for each composition were tested to ensure repeatable mechanical properties.

Fig. 1 shows the XRD patterns of the Fe50 and Fe55 MEAs. Apparently, all the MEAs 
clearly revealed a single FC C phase structure within the limit of XRD instrument resolution. Fig. 
2 shows the EBSD inverse pole figure (IPF) and phase maps of Fe50 and Fe55 MEAs, 
respectively. It can be seen from the IPF maps that the equiaxed grain structures were observed 
in both the Fe50 and Fe55 MEA samples before tensile deformation (Fig. 2 (a1) and (b1)). The 
average grain size was estimated to be ~15 μm for Fe50 MEA and ~10 μm for Fe55 MEA. Only 
FCC phase exists in both Fe50 and Fe55 MEAs, which is consistent with the XRD results in Fig. 
1. Abundant twin boundaries exist in both Fe50 and Fe55 MEAs, as shown in red lines (Fig. 
2(a2) and (b2)), indicating that both Fe50 and Fe55 MEAs might have a relative low stacking 
fault energy (SFE) . Low SFE can facilitate phase transformation at cryogenic temperatures. 
Fig. 3(a) shows the representative engineering stress –strain curves of the Fe50 and Fe55 
MEA at 293 K and 77 K, respectively. Only the plastic components of the curves were presented. 
The flow stress of Fe55 MEA is slightly higher than that of the Fe50 MEA at 293 K, whereas the 
ductility (~58%) remain nearly the same for both MEAs. The mechanical pr operties of the Fe50 
and Fe55 MEA s are comparable to the Fe40Mn40Co10Cr10 HEA at room temperature [8, 26, 27] . The 
strength increases sharply with temperature decrease from 293 K to 77 K, i.e., the UTS increases 
from ~490 MPa to ~980 MPa for the Fe50 MEA and from ~560 MPa to ~1040 MPa for the Fe55 
MEA. Surprisingly, the ductility (~59%) of the Fe55 MEA do not decrease at 77 K, and the 
ductility of the Fe50 MEA even increase to ~83% at 77 K. The true stress -true strai n and 
corresponding strain hardening rate (SHR) -true strain curves for both Fe50 and Fe55 MEA at 77 
K are displayed in Fig. 3 (b). Normally, the SHR continues to decrease with increasing strain, but 
Fe50 and Fe55 MEAs exhibit high SHR during deformation eve n at strains up to 45% and 55%, 
respectively, resulting in ultrahigh ductilities for both MEAs at 77 K. 
Representative fracture surfaces for the Fe50 MEA at 293 K and 77 K are shown in Fig. 4. 
Necking eventually occurs at both temperatures (Fig. 4(a) and (c)), but less necking is observed 
at 77 K, implying that the MEA has better uniform deformation ability at 77 K. The ductile 
nature of the Fe50 MEA is verified by the dimple rupture (Fig. 4 (b) and (d)) while the average 
dimple size at 293 K is much larger than that at 77 K. In addition, two kinds of dimples, i.e. 
larger dimple and small dimple, are observed on fracture surface at 77 K (Fig. 4 (d)). This might 
be origined from two possible types of phases ( FCC and strain-induced HCP phases) after 
fracture at 77 K. The EBSD IPF maps combined phase maps of Fe50 MEA after tensile fracture 
at 293 K and 77 K are shown in Fig. 5. The higher fraction of HCP phase and higher degree of 
grain elongation were observed in the fractured sample at 77 K than that at 293 K, indicating 
the strain-induced FCC -to-HCP phase transformation occurs at 77 K. In addition, denser twin

and phase maps of Fe55 MEA after fracture at 77 K in Fig. 6 reveal that more FCC phase

The tensile ductility can strongly link with the TRIP effect, which can be adjusted by 
varying SFE of the materials. In the Fe xMn80-xCo10Cr10 alloy system[17], it was reported that SFE 
can be tuned by changing the Mn content. Thus, to optimize our alloy system, the mechanical 
properties of the Fe40 and Fe45 MEAs were also tested. Fig. 7 shows the engineering stress –
strain curves of FexMn75-xNi10Cr15 (x=40, 45, 50 and 55 at .%) MEAs at 293 K . Obviously, the 
ductility of the MEAs at room temperature gradually decreas es with the decrease of Fe content. 
Fe50 and Fe55 MEAs have better comprehensive mechanical properties in the Fe xMn75-xNi10Cr15 
alloy system. Furthermore, the UTS versus elongation fo r typical HEAs [2, 3, 8, 9, 23] and other high -
performing alloys in Fig. 8 (a) clearly shows that the Fe50 and Fe55 MEAs have an outstanding 
combination of strength and ductility at 77 K. For instance, the mechanical properties of the 
present MEAs alloy are comparable to the typical CoCrFeNi HEA [10] and Fe40Mn40Co10Cr10 
HEA [8, 9], and are even better than the Co -free FeNiMnCr 18 HEA [23]. Meanwhile, the present 
MEAs have the minimum cost of raw materials compared to other typical HEAs (Fig. 8 (b)). 
Interestingly, the newly -developed Fe50 MEA exhibits higher strength and ductility with 
decreasing temperature from room -temperature t o liquid-nitrogen temperature, similar to 
phenomena observed in CoCrFeNi and Fe 40Mn40Co10Cr10 HEAs[8-10]. The Fe50 MEA has a low 
SFE (Fig. 2) and the SFE of HEAs/MEAs decreases with the decrease of temperature [9, 28]. The 
synergistic effect of high flow stress at 77 K and the decreased SFE by temperature effect 
promotes the FCC to HCP phase transformation. The HCP phase is “harder” than the FCC phase 
due to the lack of slip systems [16, 20, 29] , and then the FCC/HCP phase interface is not conducive 
to dislocation glide. Moreover, the formation of HCP plates reduces the mean free path of 
dislocations [16, 30], which is similar to the dynamic Hall –Patch effect. These “TRIP effect” during 
cryogenic-temperature deformation are responsible for the ultra -high SHR of Fe50 MEA (Fig. 
3(b)), which delay s the onset of necking, resulting in excellent combina tion of cryogenic 
strength and ductility. In addition, the abundant mechanical twinning in the Fe50 MEA also leads 
to a dynamic Hall -Petch effect (Fig. 5(b2))[31, 32]. The TWIP effect is also advantageous for the 
Fe50 MEA to have a higher ductility at 77 K [26, 31, 33] . In short, t he joint contribution of TRIP and 
TWIP are responsible for the excellent strength -ductility combination of the newly -developed 
Fe50 MEA at 77 K.

We successfully exploited TRIP effect under cryogenic conditions through the newly -
developed Co-free FexMn75-xNi10Cr15 alloy system. The microstructure evolution and mechanical 
properties of the MEAs were studied. The following conclusions can be made: 
(1) Two beneficial features were acquired by adjusting the element proportion in low cost Fe-
Mn-Ni-Cr alloy system: (i) it offered a novel route to develop cost-effective MEAs, and (ii) it 
played a key role in tuning the phase stability at cryogeni c temperatures. 
(2) The Fe50Mn25Ni10Cr15 MEA exhibits a combination of tensile strength of ~ 0. 98 GPa and 
ductility of ~8 3% at 77 K. 
(3) The primary deformation mechanisms of the FCC Fe50Mn25Ni10Cr15 and Fe55Mn20Ni10Cr15 
MEA were associated with the joint contribution of TRIP and TWIP effects at 77 K.
