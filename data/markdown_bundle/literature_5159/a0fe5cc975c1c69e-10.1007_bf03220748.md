High. Temperature Applications 
M.G. McKimpson and D. O'Donnell

Oxide-dispersion-strengthened (ODS) 
high-temperature alloys represent a unique 
class of powder-metallurgy-based engineer­
ing materials. They offer combinations of 
high-temperature strength, oxidation resis­
tance, and hot corrosion resistance that can­
not be obtained in other alloys. The alloys 
were initially developed for the aircraft gas 
turbine industry; since then, however, appli­
cations have expanded to include industrial 
gas turbines, equipment for handling molten 
glass, high-temperature furnace assemblies, 
and a variety of other industrialcompo­
nents. Internationally, the materials are also 
of interest for nuclear power systems (both 
breeder and fusion reactors) since ferritic 
ODS alloys exhibit both excellent swelling 
resistance and good elevated-temperature 
creep resistance. Many of these applications 
require that the ODS alloys be joined to 
either themselves or to other materials. The 
purpose of this paper is to review some of the 
techniques available for making these joints. 
INTRODUCTION 
Oxide-dispersion-strengthened (ODS) 
high-temperature materials (described 
in the sidebar The Production of ODS 
Material) have been-and are being­
joined successfully in a number of com­
mercial applications.5-7 The key require­
ment is developing a joint that performs 
adequately for the intended application. 
Often, this involves both material and 
design considerations. 
ODS materials can be joined success­
fully for some applications using con­
ventional fusion processes, such as gas 
tungsten arc (GTA) and gas metal arc 
(GMA) welding. Mechanically alloyed 
MA 758 for molten glass service, for 
example, has been joined by GTA weld­
ing using 30% chromium filler metals 
such as Inconel filler metal 52. Melting, 
however, disrupts both the oxide distri­
bution and the elongated grain structure 
in the material. Yttrium oxide disper­
soids have very low solubility in molten 
superalloys; they also resist wetting by 
these melts. With melting, the disper­
soids rapidly agglomerate and rise to 
the top of the molten pool. Even partial 
melting can produce semi-continuous 
oxide networks, which may contribute 
to premature weld failures. In addition, 
melting may release entrapped gases 
from the mechanically alloyed base 
metal, producing fine porosity in either 
the weld pool or along the edge of the 
fusion zone. 
Fusion welding also destroys the in­
terlocked, elongated grain structure in 
the ODS material and replaces it with a 
much weaker cast structure. The as-so­
lidified grains are generally either equi­
axed or oriented parallel to the direction 
of heat flow. Accordingly, fusion weld­
ing often results in grain boundaries that 
lie transverse to the direction of maxi­
mum applied loads. Such boundaries 
seriously degrade the high-temperature 
performance of the joint. Indeed, the 
creep resistance obtained from the weld 
metal in a fusion-welded ODS alloy is 
often similar to that observed in a con­
ventional cast superalloy of similar com­
position. 
Several approaches have been de­
veloped to address these concerns. First, 
assemblies fabricated from ODS materi­
als are frequently designed so that joints 
are located away from areas of maxi­
mum temperature and/or maximum 
load. When possible, joints are used as 
attachments to principal load-carrying 
members rather than as part of the load­
carrying members themselves. In addi-

FRICTION AND EXPLOSION 
WELDING 
Friction and explosion welding are 
attractive for ODS materials because they 
involve little, if any liquid formation. In 
friction welding, one of the two work­
pieces to be joined is held stationary. The 
other is rotated at high speed and then 
pressed against the stationary piece. The 
frictional heating and forging action of 
the process extrudes surface material 
out of the joint region, producing a solid­
state metallurgical bond. Since friction 
welding has little effect on oxide dis­
persoid distribution, friction-welded 
ODS materials frequently exhibit good 
ambient- and elevated-temperature ten­
sile strengths. Unfortunately, the out­
ward flow of metal from the joint during 
friction welding also produces transverse 
grain boundaries in the part. Accord­
ingly, the elevated-temperature creep 
resistance of friction-welded joints is of­
ten similar to that observed in fusion­
welded parts. 7 
Explosion welding is another solid­
state process that produces little degra­
dation of dispersoid quality in ODS 
materials. In parallel gap explosion weld­
ing (the process most widely used for 
joining of ODS materials), a controlled 
explosive charge is used to force a top 
flyer plate against a parallel baseplate 
along a linear detonation front. Under 
appropriate conditions, the resulting 
high-velocity impact produces a wave­
like interface with good solid-state bond­
ing between the two workpieces. The

BRAZING 
Both iron- and nickel-based ODS al­
loys can be brazed readily, generally 
using vacuum furnace brazing tech­
niques. 7 The brazing alloys used fre­
quently require brazing temperatures in 
excess of l,230°C and are often based on 
proprietary compositions. Alloys with a 
high silicon/boron ratio are frequently 
preferred in order to minimize erosion 
of the base metal-and corresponding 
agglomeration of the oxide dispersoids­
during joining. Typical alloys include 
B93 (Ni-14Cr-9 .5Co-4.9Ti-4.5Si-4W-
4Mo-3Al-0.7B), AM 788 (Co-22Cr-21Ni-
14W-2B-0.03La), and TD6 (Ni-l7Mo-
16Cr-5W-5Si-1Al). Joint preparation of­
ten consists of first grinding or machin­
ing the faying surfaces and then rinsing 
these surfaces with a volatile solvent. 
Alloys such as MA 956, PM 2000, and 
MA 6000, which contain substantial 
quantities of aluminum and/ or titanium, 
are frequently plated with a thin layer of 
nickel in order to minimize oxidation 
during bonding . 
TRANSIENT LIQUID PHASE 
BONDING 
Work has also been reported on the 
transient liquid phase diffusion bond­
ing (TLP) of ODS materials. In this proc­
ess, a low-melting interlayer is first 
placed between the two materials being 
joined. This "sandwich" is then heated 
to the bonding temperature and held at 
this temperature for an appropriate time. 
The interlayer melts and interdiffusion 
occurs between the molten material and 
the substrate, eventually producing iso­
thermal solidification of the molten ma­
terial. Tsuji and Mino have reported work 
on the diffusion brazing of MA 6000 bu tt 
joints using three different alloys .12 The 
alloys include MBF80 (Ni-15Cr-4B), N3 
(Ni-12W-1OCo-6Cr-4Ta-3B-2Mo-0.25Y), 
and MBF50 (Ni-19Cr-8Si-1.3B). Joining 
was done by heating the materials at 
l,200°C for 16 to 20 hours under applied 
stresses of 0.2 MPa to 5.0 MPa. Stress 
rupture testing at 950°C showed these 
joints to exhibit strengths on the order of 
one third that of the base metal in the 
transverse orientation. These low 
strengths were attributed to the devel­
opment of a recrystallized, dispersoid­
poor zone near the joint interface .

DIFFUSION BONDING 
Diffusion bonding is attractive for ODS 
materials because it may be a way to 
overcome the weight, contamination, 
and temperature limitations associated 
with current ODS brazing technologies. 
This process, also termed diffusion weld­
ing, involves simultaneously applying 
heat and pressure to a joint to produce 
solid-state bonding with a minimum of 
macroscopic deformation. Lamilloy, a 
diffusion-bonded, high-temperature su­
peralloy composite sheet, has been fab­
ricated from both MA 956 and MA 754.14 
The MA 956 material has been used suc­
cessfully in several military applications, 
and the MA 754 sheet has the potential 
for being the world's strongest high­
temperature wrought sheet product. 
These patented Lamilloy composites 
are specifically designed to provide im­
proved cooling for the hot-section com­
ponents of gas turbine engines. They 
contain a complex network of internal 
holes and passages through which cool­
ing gases must pass before leaving the 
component. The materials are made by 
first photochemically machining an ar­
ray of pedestals and holes onto sheets of 
superalloy material and then diffusion 
bonding these sheets into a complete 
assembly. Lamilloy was used for both 
the external exhaust troughs of the 
Northrop/McDonnell Douglas YF-23A 
and the nozzle of the YF-119 Pratt & 
Whitney engine of the Lockheed/Boe­
ing/General Dynamics YF-22A.1S 
Development of high-quality diffu­
sion bonds in ODS materials requires 
careful control over both surface cleanli­
ness and material recrystallization. The 
recommended surface preparation is 
generally cool, wet grinding to nearly a 
mirror finish, followed by storage in an 
inert liquid until joining.6 To achieve 
optimum high-temperature properties, 
growth of large, recrystallized grains 
across the prior joint interface is also 
desirable. As in transient liquid-phase 
bonding, this involves both avoiding fine, 
equiaxed grains along the joint plane 
and promoting migration of large grains 
across this plane. Work on early ODS 
alloys such as TD-NiCr (Ni-20Cr-2Th0Z> 
showed that one way to accomplish these 
objectives was to minimize the cold de­
formation of material adjacent to the

Mechanically alloyed ODS materials 
have been found to exhibit similar be­
havior. Moore & Glasgow 17 found that 
unrecrystallized MA 6000 could be dif­
fusion bonded to itself at 1,000°C and 
27.6 MPa using a hold time of 1.25 hours. 
After recrystallization, these joints ex­
hibited grain growth across the joint line 
and showed elevated-temperature creep 
rupture strengths of about 30-40% of 
those observed in the parent metal. Joints 
made between recrystallized and unre­
crystallized MA 6000 materials, as well 
as those made between recrystallized 
materials, showed lower strength. Simi­
larly, a patent issued to Rydstad and 
Singer teaches that dispersion-strength­
ened superalloys can be joined success­
fully using a pressure-bonding method 
in which the unrecrystallized materials 
are machined, annealed below the recry­
stallization temperature of the alloy to 
remove residual cold work, pressed to­
gether at a temperature below the re­
crystallization temperature, and given a 
final grain-coarsening recrystallization 
anneal,1B Work by Bucklow has also 
shown that MA 956 can be joined suc­
cessfully by bonding recrystallized and 
unrecrystallized materials at 1,200°C and 
200-300 MPa pressure and then subject­
ing the bonded joint to an appropriate 
grain coarsening anneal. 19 
Recent work suggests that high-pres­
sure diffusion bonding may also be use­
ful for joining recrystallized ODS alloys. 
A patent by Verpoortteaches that coarse­
grained ODS alloys can be joined suc­
cessfully by hot isostatically pressing 
materials with finely machined surfaces, 
if pressure is not applied until after the 
material has been heated above cold­
working temperatures. Pressing pres­
sures are specified to be 100-300 MPa, 
with bonding times ranging from 1-24 
hours.2o Stover et aU1 have investigated 
the hot isostatically pressed diffusion 
bonding of MA 956, MA 754, and MA 
6000 at pressures up to 360 MPa. The 
alloys were presumably joined in the as­
recrystallized, coarse-grained condition, 
since no information to the contrary was 
provided. For materials bonded at 
1,150°C for two hours, the 800°C tensile 
strengths of the bonded joints ranged up 
to 65% of the parent metal's strength at 
the same temperature. The tensile 
strength of the MA 6000 joint increased 
markedly with increasing hot isostatic 
pressure up to the maximum pressure 
evaluated (360 MPa). The MA 754 mate­
rial showed a similar, but much smaller, 
increase in joint strength with increasing 
pressure.
