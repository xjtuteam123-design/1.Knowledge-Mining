ABSTRACT
In the present study, (Ti, Nb)B reinforced Ti-Nb-Zr-Mn alloys were successfully 
fabricated through ball milling and spark plasma sintering (SPS) process with the 
addition of TiB2. The microstructure, mechanical behavior, and corrosion resist-
ance in simulated body fluid of the as-prepared alloys were systematically inves-
tigated. It was found that (Ti, Nb)B phases were in situ formed through reaction 
between the Ti-Nb-Zr-Mn matrix and TiB2 particles, which significantly enhanced 
the mechanical strength of the alloys. As the TiB2 with equivalent B mass fractions 
increased from 0 to 1 wt.%, the ultimate tensile strength of the alloys improved 
significantly from 945 ± 13 MPa to 1259 ± 8 MPa, while the elongation reduced 
sharply from 20.2 ± 2.2% to 1.9 ± 0.5%. Microhardness tests showed that the mate-
rials’ hardness increased as the fraction of the (Ti, Nb)B phase increased. Notably, 
the elastic modulus increased from 77.1 ± 0.5 GPa to 93.7 ± 0.7 GPa. Moreover, the 
appropriate introduction of B was beneficial to the corrosion resistance of the 
alloy in simulated body fluids.

Materials and methods
Alloy preparation
Elemental powders of Ti, Nb, Zr, and Mn purchased 
from Changsha Tianjiu Metal Materials with a weight 
composition of Ti-24Nb-4Zr-2Mn (TNZM) were used 
as the raw materials. The average particle size of metal 
Ti, Zr, and Mn powders is approximately 45 μm, 
whereas that of metal Nb powder is around 15 μm. 
All these powders have a purity higher than 99.9%. 
 TiB2 with a particle size of approximately 50 nm and 
a purity greater than 99.95% was selected as the boron 
source. The total mass of the powders was 100 g, and 
the ball-to-powder ratio was set at 10:1. The powders 
were uniformly mixed at a rotation speed of 200 r/min 
for 1 h and then ball milled at a speed of 300 r/min 
for 12 h. Argon was filled into the container to pre-
vent the oxidation of the powders during ball milling. 
Afterward, the ball-milled alloy powders were loaded 
into a graphite mold. Using LABOX-350 spark plasma 
sintering (SPS) equipment, the powders were vacuum 
sintered at 1250 °C with a heating rate of 100°C/min, 
held at this temperature for 10 min, and then furnace 
cooled. The uniaxial pressure was maintained at 30 
MPa throughout the sintering process. The as-pre-
pared alloys were named TB0, TB01, TB03, TB05, and 
TB1 according to the equivalent B mass added (TiB2 
with equivalent B mass fractions of 0, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, and 
1 wt.%).

Over recent decades, Ti matrix composites (TMCs) 
have captured the attention of materials research-
ers owing to their excellent properties. By incorpo-
rating hard and robust ceramic particles or fibers, 
the strength, hardness, and wear resistance of Ti 
alloys can be significantly enhanced [3 ]. A variety 
of reinforcement components, such as SiC, WC, TiC, 
and TiB, have been utilized in the development of 
Ti-based composites [6 ]. Among them, TiB, charac-
terized by high hardness, low density, and favora-
ble interfacial bonding with the matrix, is often 
employed as a reinforcement phase in Ti alloys. 
Given the Ti-B phase diagram, the solubility of B in 
Ti is extremely low (< 0.02 at%), enabling the in-situ 
generation of TiB whiskers [7 ]. Due to its excellent 
compatibility with the Ti matrix, TiB demonstrates a 
strong interfacial bond with the matrix, resulting in 
significant property enhancements after being com-
pounded with Ti alloys [8 , 9].
In the past, research on TiB whiskers was mostly 
based on commercially pure Ti [10, 11] and Ti-6Al-
4V [8, 12–14]. For instance, Joseph et al. have devel-
oped a low-cost TiB-reinforced α-Ti matrix com-
posite through an in-situ reaction between Ti and B 
nitride nanopowder. The hardness of the as-prepared 
Ti matrix composite exceeded 10.4 GPa, the elastic 
modulus exceeded 165 GPa, and the ratio of hard-
ness to Young’s modulus was 0.062 [15]. However, 
the effect of B on the mechanical properties and cor-
rosion behavior of β alloys has been rarely studied. 
Samuel et al. investigated the microstructure and wear 
resistance of boride–reinforced composites fabricated 
by laser deposition of Ti-Nb-Zr-Ta alloys; their study 
revealed a significant improvement in the wear resist-
ance [4 ]. Majumdar et al. found that, compared to 
B–free alloys, the presence of TiB particles in Ti-35Nb-
5.7Ta-7.2Zr with added B increased its hardness and 
elastic modulus while reducing its ductility [16].
Our previously developed metastable β-type 
Ti-24Nb-4Zr-2Mn (TNZM) alloy exhibits a low elas-
tic modulus and is a promising candidate for medical 
implants [17]. As a potential material for load-bearing 
and wear-resistant implants, it is essential to enhance 
the strength and hardness while maintaining a cer-
tain low elastic modulus. In the present study, TNZM 
alloys were selected and the effect of the B addition on 
the microstructure and mechanical properties of these 
alloys were investigated. Furthermore, the corrosion

Mechanical Properties tests
A universal mechanical testing machine (MTS, 
E43.104) with a video extensometer was used to evalu-
ate the tensile strength and plasticity of the alloy sam-
ples at room temperature. The tensile specimens are in 
dog–bone–shape, with a gauge length of 5mm and a 
width and thickness of 1.5mm and 1mm, respectively. 
The samples were subjected to tensile tests at a strain 
rate of 10–3 s−1. To ensure the validity of the data, at 
least three tensile tests were conducted on each alloy 
sample. An ultrasonic material characterization sys -
tem (UMS-100, RITEC) was employed to measure 
the elastic modulus of the alloys. The hardness of 
the samples was measured using a Vickers indenter 
(MHVD-50AP). The indentation load was set to 200 g. 
At least seven indentations were made on each sample.
Electrochemical corrosion behavior and XPS 
analysis
Corrosion tests were conducted to evaluate the cor-
rosion resistance of the alloy samples. After the sam-
ples were polished to a mirror-like finish and dried, 
they were embedded in acrylic with an exposed area 
of 50 mm2. The electrochemical tests were conducted 
using a PARSTAT V4 electrochemical workstation with 
a three-electrode system, comprising a reference elec-
trode (Ag/AgCl), a counterelectrode (Pt bar), and a 
working electrode (the sample). The electrolyte was 
Ringer’s solution (9 g/L NaCl, 0.42 g/L KCl, 0.48 g/L 
 CaCl2, and 0.2 g/L NaHCO3), which was de-aerated 
by high-purity Ar for 1 h before the electrochemical 
test. The open-circuit potential (OCP) was recorded 
for 1 h. For the electrochemical impedance spectros -
copy (EIS) measurement, the frequency range was 
from 105 to 10–2 Hz, with an amplitude of 10 mV. The 
potential range for measuring the potentiodynamic 
polarization curve was from − 0.25 V to 1.6 V versus 
SCE at a scan rate of 1 mV/min. During the electro-
chemical corrosion testing process, the temperature 
was maintained constantly at 37 °C to simulate the 
bodily fluid environment.
Results and discussion
Phase and microstructure
The XRD patterns of the as-prepared boron-bearing 
TNZM (TNZMB) alloys are depicted in Fig. 1. As 
anticipated, the β phase prevails in these alloys. Aug-
menting the TiB2 content results in a progressive inten-
sification of the diffraction peaks corresponding to the 
TiB phase. This phenomenon can be rationalized by 
the increment in the phase content. Rietveld refine-
ment was performed to calculate the mass fraction 
of TiB in the TNZMB alloys, which are 0.3, 0.7, 2.6, 
and 6.6 wt.%, corresponding to the alloys with TB01, 
TB03, TB05, and TB1), respectively. It was reported 
that in situ reaction between Ti and TiB2 for creating 
the TiB phase can occur when SPS sintering tempera-
tures are above 900 ºC [18]. The TiB reinforcement is 
considered one of the most promising reinforcements 
for the Ti matrix due to similar density, good thermal 
compatibility, and strong interfacial bond [19].
Figure 2 depicts the OM images of as-sintered 
TNZMB alloys. The acid etching effectively reveals 
both the grain boundaries and the precipitated 
phases; no apparent pore defects are detected. This 
result confirms the viability and dependability of the 
chosen preparation method. Following milling, TiB2

which not only modifies the lattice constants but 
also enhances the interfacial bonding with the β-Ti 
matrix. Interestingly, the (Ti, Nb)B reinforcement 
shows much better ductility than the TiB [23].

strength and reducing ductility upon B addition

Ti–Nb–Ta–Zr alloys. Notably, our TNZM alloy with

The typical tensile fracture morphologies of TNZMB 
alloys are shown in Fig. 8. As shown in Fig. 8a, TB01 
exhibits numerous dimples, indicating its ductile frac-
ture. However, partial intergranular fracture is also 
observed simultaneously, which belongs to brittle frac-
ture. From the inset in Fig. 8a, the separation between 
the needle-shaped (Ti, Nb)B and the Ti matrix in TB01 
can be observed. River-like tearing ridges and dimples 
are seen near the (Ti, Nb)B. The fracture surface of 
TB1 in Fig. 8d contains more whisker fracture pits and 
fewer dimples. This is because a further increase in 
the content of the reinforcement phase will reduce the 
number of dimples and tearing ridges, thus decreasing 
the ductility of the alloy [27]. TB01 exhibits the high-
est count of dimples, covering an area exceeding 90% 
and possessing a size range of approximately 2–4 μm. 
As the (Ti, Nb)B phase content increases, the dimples 
become shallower and less distinct, with TB1’s dimple 
area accounting for less than 8% and a size predomi-
nantly smaller than 0.5 μm. The (Ti, Nb)B precipitates 
in the TNZMB alloys show a precipitation hardening 
effect, which is beneficial for increasing the strength of 
the alloy. They also act as stress raisers, making them 
the crack initiation sites for fracture. As a result, the 
ductility of the alloys decreases as the content of (Ti, 
Nb)B increases. It can be observed that in the fracture 
surfaces of all samples, mature (Ti, Nb)B needles have 
been formed, and there are clear pull-out pits on the 
fracture surfaces. The integrity of in situ-formed (Ti, 
Nb)B needles in the fracture proves the excellent inter-
facial bonding strength between the reinforcement 
phase and the matrix. It is worth noting that high B 
content induces local stress concentration, and cracks 
propagate along the TiB cluster area, leading to quasi-
cleavage fracture.

Corrosion resistance
TNZM alloys can spontaneously and rapidly form a 
compact oxide film, which confers upon them out-
standing corrosion resistance when utilized as an 
implant [17]. Nevertheless, upon the incorporation 
of B into the alloy, the TNZM alloys experience dis-
cernible alterations in both their phase composition 
and microstructure. These changes are liable to exert 
an influence on the alloy’s corrosion-resistant prop-
erties. Figure 10a presents the open-circuit potential 
tests for four alloys. Following an hour-long test, the 
open-circuit potential shifted in the positive-potential 
direction and ultimately reached a stable state. Nota-
bly, the open-circuit potential of the TB03 sample was 
higher, signifying that TB03 exhibited a more favora-
ble corrosion resistance inclination. Subsequently, as 
demonstrated by the results derived from the Tafel 
fitting of the potentiodynamic polarization curves in 
Fig. 10b, TB05 had a lower corrosion current density 
 ( Icorr), indicating a slower corrosion rate. Overall, TB05 
was superior. When the B content increased to 1 wt.%, 
its Icorr slightly rebounded. It should be noted that the 
 Icorr of TB05 was comparable to Ti-6Al-4V (0.12 μA/
cm2), superior to the pure Ti in Ringer’s solution.

Ramkumar investigated the corrosion properties 
of Ti-Ti boride composites through electrochemi-

corrosion of the material, thus reducing the corro-

at the TB05 composition. Therefore, the influence of 
TiB on the corrosion resistance of Ti alloys is rather

adsorption and doping, making the oxidation process 
more inclined to generate thermodynamically stable 
and more protective TiO2, thereby improving the 
growth quality and stability of the passivation film 
[36]. Therefore, the TB05 sample exhibited superior 
corrosion resistance in electrochemical testing, which 
is fundamentally due to the optimization of the com-
position and structure of the passivation film by the B 
element, reducing the density of transmission defects 
in the film and enhancing the interfacial barrier effect. 
For TB1 alloy, excessive (Ti, Nb)B phase volume frac-
tion can lead to a severe decrease in the corrosion 
resistance of the alloy due to the fact that these brit-
tle intermetallic compounds are the preferred sites 
for corrosion initiation and damaging the protective 
properties of the passivation film [37]. Moreover, more 
(Ti, Nb)B precipitates can lead to an intensification of 
galvanic corrosion [38].

ing mechanism, solid solution strengthening is gener-
ally described by the Labusch model [39]. Consider-
ing that the solubility of B in the Ti solid solution is 
extremely low, the solid solution strengthening effect 
of B can be neglected.

Residual stresses often arise in composites due to 
the mismatch in the coefficient of thermal expansion

composites. Furthermore, detailed TEM characteriza-

incompatibility, a primary driver for geometrically 
necessary dislocation (GND) generation and associ-

in this system [44]. Therefore, while some level of

Conclusion
In summary, the effects of B on the microstructure, 
mechanical properties, and corrosion resistance of the 
TNZM alloys were systematically investigated. After 
the addition of TiB2, the (Ti, Nb)B reinforcement phase 
was in situ formed. The pinning effect of the (Ti, Nb)
B phases led to grain refinement. The average grain 
size decreased from 27 μm for TB01 to 12 μm for TB1. 
With the increase in the content of the (Ti, Nb)B phase, 
the yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, hardness, 
and elastic modulus of the alloys all increased, while 
the plasticity decreased. Moreover, appropriate pre-
cipitation of (Ti, Nb)B was beneficial to the corrosion 
resistance of the alloy in simulated body fluids. These 
findings indicate that the rational introduction of B 
to Ti-Nb-Zr-Mn alloys can improve the strength and 
hardness while maintaining a certain level of plastic-
ity and low elastic modulus. These properties suggest 
their potential as a candidate for biomedical implants. 
However, further studies on biocompatibility, cyto-
toxicity, and tribological performance are needed to 
confirm their clinical suitability.
