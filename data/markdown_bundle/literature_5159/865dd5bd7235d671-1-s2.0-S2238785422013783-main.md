abstract
The compressive mechanical behavior and fracture characteristics of FeAl intermetallic
alloys containing 0 e9 at.% Nb were investigated over a wide strain rate range of 10
/C0 3 s/C0 1 to
5 /C2 103 s/C0 1. For all of the alloys, the ﬂow stress and strain rate sensitivity increased with
increasing strain rate; however, the fracture strain decreased. The addition of Nb improved
both the compressive strength and the ductility of the alloys. Among the various alloys, the
alloy containing 6 at.% Nb showed the highest yield stress and fracture strain. The optical
microscopy observations and X-ray diffraction results showed that the change in me-
chanical behavior of the various FeAl eNb alloys was due mainly to differences in the
precipitation of (Fe,Al)
2Nb Laves phase. Moreover, a higher level of Nb addition prompted a
change in the fracture morphology from a brittle fracture mode to a ductile fracture mode.

1. Introduction
Intermetallic compounds (IMCs) have many attractive prop-
erties for engineering applications, including high strength at
elevated temperatures, good creep resistance, low wear,
excellent oxidation and corrosion resistance, and so on.
Among the many IMCs which have been developed, FeAl-
based IMCs, such as FeAl and Fe
3Al have attracted particular
attention due to their relatively lower cost, lighter weight, and
better sulfurization and corrosion resistance [ 1e4]. These
properties render FeAl IMCs ideal alternatives to the stainless
steels and superalloys used in many high temperature appli-
cations nowadays, including turbine blades, aircraft structural
parts, automobile parts, furnace tube ﬁxtures, and nuclear
reactor components. However, FeAl IMCs have thus far found
only limited application due to their relatively poor ductility
and toughness, particularly at room temperature.
The literature contains many attempts to improve the
ductility and toughness of FeAl IMCs through the addition of
doping elements, particles and ﬁbers, or the actuation of
microstructural controls [ 5]. Zhang et al. [ 6] investigated the
mechanical properties of sintered Fe

In practice, the structural components used in many
aerospace and automotive applications are subjected not only
to static loads, but also dynamic impacts, such as bird strikes,
accidental collisions, missile defense, and so on. Many studies
have found that the deformation behavior of metals and al-
loys under dynamic loads is signiﬁcantly different from that
under static conditions. For example, Campell and Ferguson
[11] showed that the yield stress of mild steel increases
signiﬁcantly at strain rates higher than 10
3 s/C0 1. Zan et al. [ 12]
examined the tensile mechanical behavior of TiAl alloys
under strain rates of 1 /C2 10/C0 3 to 1.35 /C2 103 s/C0 1 and tempera-
tures ranging from room temperature to 840 /C14 C. The results
showed that the brittle-to-ductile transition temperature in-
creases with an increasing strain rate. Chen and Wu [ 13]
investigated the deformation and fracture characteristics of
NiAl IMCs containing 5 e7 at.% Cu under dynamic loads of
10
/C0 3 s/C0 1 to 5 /C2 103 s/C0 1. It was shown that the ﬂow stress and
ductility of the IMCs increased with an increasing strain rate
and Cu content.
However, the literature lacks a detailed investigation into
the dynamic mechanical behavior and fracture properties of
FeAl IMC with Nb addition. Accordingly, the present study
prepares FeAl alloys with 0 e9% Nb addition and investigates
their compression deformation behavior at strain rates
ranging from 10
/C0 3 to 5 /C2 103 s/C0 1 using a material testing sys-
tem (MTS) and split-Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB). The mi-
crostructures and fracture morphologies of the impacted
specimens are observed by optical microscopy (OM) and
scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Moreover, the phase
compositions of the various alloys are determined by X-ray
diffraction (XRD). The OM and SEM observations are then used
to interpret the mechanical behavior of the FeAl samples
under different loading conditions.
2. Experimental
The FeAl alloys were prepared in a vacuum arc melting
furnace under an Ar atmosphere using appropriate quantities
of high-purity (99.99%) Fe, Al and Nb powders (Golden Opto-
electronic Co. Ltd., New Taipei City, Taiwan). Table 1 presents
the chemical compositions of the four as-cast samples. The
alloys were prepared with Nb additions of 0, 3, 6 and 9 at.%,
respectively. Each ingot was remelted four times in order to
ensure compositional homogeneity, and 5 ± 0.1 mm long test
specimens with a diameter of 5 ± 0.1 mm were machined from
the as-cast ingots and ﬁnished by a center-grinding process.
The mechanical properties of the various specimens were
evaluated under both quasi-static and dynamic loads. For the
quasi-static tests, the specimens were deformed under the
room temperature condition 25
/C14 C in accordance with the
ASTM E9 standard at strain rates of 10 /C0 3,1 0 /C0 2 and 10 /C0 1 s/C0 1,
respectively, using a material testing system (MTS 810 series;
MTS Systems Corporation, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The dy-
namic tests were performed at strain rates of 3 /C2 103,4 /C2 103
and 5 /C2 103 s/C0 1, respectively, using a split-Hopkinson Pressure
Bar (SHPB) system (Long Win Science and Technology Corpo-
ration, Taoyuan, Taiwan). At least, three repeated experi-
ments were performed for each condition in order to ensure
the reliability of the results.
In the dynamic tests, the specimens were placed between
the input bar and output bar of the SHPB system, and the free
end of the input bar was impacted axially by a strike bar. The
stressestrain response of the specimen was then determined
by measuring the incident, reﬂected and transmitted strain
pulses recorded by two strain gauges mounted at the mid-
point positions of the input and output bars, respectively.
The experiments were performed under room temperature
conditions (25
/C14 C) in a lubricated condition. (Note that full
details of the experimental setup, data acquisition process,
and computational procedure are available in [ 14].)
The microstructures of the specimens were characterized
by optical microscopy (Carl Zeiss NEOPHOT 2; Carl Zeiss, Now
York, NY, USA). Prior to the observation process, the speci-
mens were mounted in epoxy resin and then ground pro-
gressively with 180 e1200 mesh abrasive paper to remove any
asperities or contaminants from the sample surface. The
ground specimens were polished to a mirror-like ﬁnish using
a micro-cloth with a slurry of 0.3 mm alumina, and were then
etched in a solution of H
2O:HF (90:10) for 20 e4 0st or e v e a lt h e
underlying microstructure. The volume fractions of second-
ary phases were determined by a systematic manual point
counting procedure outlined in accordance with ASTM
standard E562. The average point fraction for 25 measured
ﬁelds gave an estimate of the volume fraction of secondary
phases. In order to examine the effects of Nb addition on the
fracture mode, the fracture surfaces of the impacted speci-
mens were examined by SEM (JEOL-6330TF; JEOL Ltd.,
Akishima, Japan). Finally, the phase compositions of the
various alloys were examined by X-ray diffraction (Philips
X'Pert-MRD X-ray diffractometer; Spectris plc, Almelo, The
Netherlands).

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Stress estrain response
Fig. 1 (a)e(d) show the stress estrain curves of the FeAl IMCs
with 0, 3, 6 and 9 at.% Nb addition, respectively. Note that the
ﬁgures show both the quasi-static response of the samples
and the dynamic response. In general, the results show that
the mechanical properties of the FeAl alloys are strongly
dependent on the strain rate and Nb content. For all of the
samples, the ﬂow stress increases with an increasing strain
rate due to the corresponding increase in the rates of dislo-
cation generation and tangling [ 15]. The larger modulus at
dynamic loading indicates that the dislocations should have a
higher stress with a higher velocity to overcome the barriers.
Similar results are presented in the publication of Meyers and
Chawla [16] and the study of Rohde and Pitt [17]. Moreover, the
alloys undergo a strengthening effect following Nb addition.
The present ﬁndings are thus consistent with those of previ-
ous studies, which reported that Nb addition induces a variety
of strengthening effects, including precipitation strength-
ening, grain reﬁnement, and solid solution strengthening
[9,18,19]. However, the results presented in Fig. 1 (e) and (f)
suggest that the strengthening effect is limited to Nb additions
of up to 6 at.% Nb. In other words, as the Nb content is further
increased to 9 at.%, virtually no change in the ﬂow stress oc-
curs (see Table 2).

3.3. Microstructural observations and fracture analysis
Fig. 5(a)-(d) present OM micrographs of the as-cast FeAl IMCs
with 0, 3, 6 and 9 at.% Nb, respectively. In general, the mi-
crographs show that the Nb content has a signiﬁcant effect on
the microstructure. The XRD patterns presented in
Fig. 6 (a)e(d) show that the microstructures of the four alloys
consist mainly of FeAl IMC and (Fe,Al)
2Nb Laves phase. The
volume fractions of second white phases are 0, 8 ± 2%, 60 ± 3%
and 100% for 0, 3, 6 and 9 at.% Nb addition. As expected, the
relative intensity of the (Fe,Al)
2Nb peaks increases with an
increasing Nb content and gives rise to a change in both the
microstructure and the mechanical properties of the FeAl
samples, as described in the following.
As shown in Fig. 5(a), the undoped FeAl sample has a large
grain size. However, as the level of Nb addition increases, the
grain structure undergoes a signiﬁcant reﬁning effect (see
Figs. 5 (b)e(d)). It was reported in [ 18,24] that this grain
reﬁnement effect, together with solid solution strengthening,
results in a higher ﬂow stress under impact deformation, as
shown in Fig. 1. Fig. 5(b) shows that the sample with 3 at.% Nb
contains secondary white phase of 1.57 ± 0.2 mm linewidth
along the grain boundaries of the primary FeAl phase. Yildirim
et al. [ 25] reported that this phase has a eutectic structure
consisting of FeAl-based phase and (Fe,Al)
2Nb Laves phase.
The formation of this eutectic structure inhibits the grain
growth of the FeAl matrix [ 26,27] and hence results in a
smaller grain size, as shown in Fig. 5(b).
As the Nb content increases beyond 3 at.%, the FeAl-based
phase and eutectic phase exhibit a dendritic morphology with
a diameter of dendritic arms of 8 ± 2 mm (see Fig. 5 (c)). The
resulting reﬁnement of the grain structure improves both the
strength and the ductility of the sample through a grain-
boundary strengthening effect and formation of sufﬁcient
slip systems for plastic ﬂow, respectively [ 5,28]. However,
when the Nb content is further increased to 9 at.%, the
microstructure transforms to a eutectic structure. The OM
image in Fig. 5 (d) shows that the dendritic morphology has

band [47], and the dislocation mechanism [ 5], respectively. In
this study, the materials through the addition of Nb provide
as-cast IMCs with more potential applications under a wide
range of strain rates.
4. Conclusions
This study has investigated the effects of Nb addition (0, 3, 6
and 9 at.%) on the compressive mechanical behavior of FeAl
alloy under quasi-static and dynamic loading conditions. The
results have shown that the impact response of the FeAlNb
samples is sensitive to both the strain rate and the Nb content.
In particular, the ﬂow stress and strain rate sensitivity in-
crease with increasing strain rate, while the fracture strain
decreases. In general, the addition of Nb enhances the
strength of the FeAl alloys. However, a ductility improvement
is observed only for Nb additions of 6 and 9 at.%. The
maximum strengthening effect and ductility occur in the
sample with 6 at.% Nb addition. The change in mechanical
properties and fracture features of the FeAl samples with
different levels of Nb addition is associated with differences in
the precipitation of (Fe,Al)
2Nb Laves phase. Finally, the addi-
tion of Nb prompts a change in the fracture morphology from
a brittle fracture mode with intergranular and transgranular
fracture features in the FeAl and FeAl eNb
3 samples to a
ductile fracture mode with dimple-like features and a layered
fracture surface in the FeAl eNb6 and FeAleNb9 samples.
