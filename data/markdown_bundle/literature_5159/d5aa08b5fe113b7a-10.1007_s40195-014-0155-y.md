High Temperature Properties of Discontinuously Reinforced
Titanium Matrix Composites: A Review
Lin Geng • Lujun Huang
Received: 27 May 2014 / Revised: 21 August 2014 / Published online: 9 October 2014
/C211The Chinese Society for Metals and Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg 2014
Abstract Further improvement on high temperature durability is one of the most important aims except for high speciﬁc
strength, high speciﬁc stiffness, and excellent wear resistance, to design and fabricate discontinuously reinforced titanium
matrix composites (DRTMCs). Their superior properties render them extensive application potential in aerospace and
military industries due to the urgent demand for the materials with characteristics of lightweight, high strength, high
stiffness and high temperature durability. With development on fabrication methods and room temperature properties,
testing, characterizing, evaluating and further increasing high temperature properties of DRTMCs are becoming more and
more important to promote their applications. This review provides insights and comprehensions on the high temperature
tensile properties, superplastic tensile properties, creep behaviors, and high temperature oxidation behaviors of DRTMCs,
and research proposals to further improve the high temperature properties of DRTMCs are given.
KEY WORDS: Titanium matrix composites; High temperature tensile properties; Creep behaviors;
Oxidation behaviors; Superplasticity
1 Introduction
Discontinuously reinforced titanium matrix composites
(DRTMCs) possess high speciﬁc strength, speciﬁc stiff-
ness, wear resistance and high temperature durability,
which render them extensive application potential in
aerospace, weapon, automotive, and so on [ 1–4]. In fact,
the main aim of developing DRTMCs is to widen the uti-
lization ﬁeld at high temperatures by increasing their high
temperature properties such as oxidation resistance,
strength, creep resistance, service temperature, etc., on the
basis of titanium alloys [ 5–7]. In the past several decades,
most of researches focused on the fabrication methods,
reinforcement, interface, and room temperature properties
of DRTMCs, and powder metallurgy and vacuum casting
technique are viewed as the two suitable fabrication
methods [8–10]. In situ synthesized TiB whiskers (TiBw)
and TiC particles (TiCp) are unanimously considered as the
most effective reinforcements for the Ti matrix [ 1, 11–13]
due to not only their high modulus and hardness, good
chemical compatibility with Ti but also similar density and
thermal expansion coefﬁcient (CTE) with those of tita-
nium. Moreover, the microstructure and interface charac-
teristics between TiBw or TiCp and Ti matrix were clearly
presented [ 14–17]. Based on the above successes, room
temperature properties of DRTMCs including strength and
ductility are signiﬁcantly improved by using titanium
alloys as matrix, tailoring reinforcement distribution, and
performing hot deformation and heat treatment on
DRTMCs in recent years [ 18–22].

However, the most important problem impeding the
practical application of DRTMCs has already transferred to
how to further improve their high temperature properties.
Therefore, after the above remarkable development of
DRTMCs, the subsequent work certainly focuses on how to
characterize, evaluate and improve their high temperature
properties. It is meaningful to review the progress on the
high temperature properties of DRTMCs. Increasing the
volume fractions of ceramic reinforcements and using high
temperature titanium alloy as matrix are the two common
routes to do this [ 1, 13, 18, 23]. The former probably
improves the high temperature strength but would deteri-
orate their ductility and oxidation resistance. The latter
would effectively increase their high temperature strength
and service temperature.
However, signiﬁcant improvements on strength and oxi-
dation resistance can be achieved by tailoring the network
distribution of ceramic reinforcements according to our recent
work [9, 24]. In particular, the ultimate tensile strength of
TiBw/Ti60 composites with a network microstructure can be
increased to almost 1,000 and 800 MPa at 600 and 700 /C176C,
respectively. The smooth oxidation scale without any spalla-
tion and detachment of the network structured TiCp/Ti6Al4V
composites after holding for 100 h at 700 /C176Cc o n s t r u c t sa
strong contrast to the serious spallation scale of Ti6Al4V alloy
after 40 h in our previous work [25], which visually conﬁrmed
the improved oxidation resistance by tailoring network dis-
tribution of TiCp reinforcement.
In this paper, the recent research progress on high tem-
perature tensile behaviors, creep behaviors, and high tem-
perature oxidation behaviors are reviewed. In addition, the
existing problems in the present research are also revealed and
then the further research directions in future are proposed.
2 High Temperature Tensile Behaviors
At high temperatures, strength is more important than
ductility for DRTMCs. Increasing the volume fractions of
reinforcement [ 1, 26] and employing high temperature
titanium alloy as matrix [ 13, 23, 27, 28] are the two main
routes to improve the high temperature strength of
DRTMCs. It is well known that the ceramic reinforcements
possess much higher melting temperature, strength, mod-
ulus and hardness compared with titanium matrices.
Therefore, adding ceramic reinforcements is expected to
enhance the high temperature strength of DRTMCs. For-
tunately, DRTMCs especially reinforced by TiBw or TiCp
exhibited a much higher strength compared with the
monolithic titanium alloy below some critical temperatures
by load-bearing effect of reinforcement [ 9, 29, 30].
Kondoh et al. [ 30] investigated high temperature prop-
erties (200–400 /C176C) of TiC(CNTs)/Ti composites fabri-
cated by spark plasma sintering and hot extrusion. The
results showed that the matrix grains were remarkably
reﬁned by the pinning effect of TiC reinforcement, which
positively contributes to the improvement of high temper-
ature mechanical properties. The prepared composites
exhibited higher strength due to the dispersion strength-
ening mechanism of the second phases and the grain
reﬁnement effect. The reason is that the in situ TiC rein-
forcement can effectively retard grain boundary sliding and
matrix grain recrystallization and inhibit matrix grain
coarsening at high temperatures. Therefore, the high tem-
perature strength of the composites increases with
increasing the reinforcement volume fractions.

In our previous work [ 9], the service temperature can be
remarkably increased by 200 /C176C based on the similar ten-
sile strength, as shown in Fig. 4. In addition to reinforce-
ment, the main reason for the improved high temperature
durability is attributed to that the weakening effect of grain
boundary at high temperatures can be inhibited by intro-
ducing ceramic reinforcement into grain boundary (net-
work microstructure). Furthermore, the highest strength of
the as-sintered composites reported in literatures at 600 and
700 /C176C belongs to TiBw/Ti60 composites with a network
microstructure, which can further conﬁrm the contribution
of the reinforcement distribution and the high temperature
titanium alloy as matrix. In addition, the whisker and
branched morphologies of reinforcements certainly play
positive roles in improving the strengthening effect [ 9].
Additionally, rare earth elements are believed to improve
the tensile properties by adsorbing the oxygen in matrix
and reﬁning the microstructure, which can play dispersion
strengthening effect [ 31].
The subsequent deformation and heat treatment can
effectively enhance the tensile strength of DRTMCs by
aligned reinforcement distribution, matrix work hardening
effect and heat treatment strengthening effect below some
critical temperature. Moreover, the conditions of heat
treatment and deformation also affect the strengthening
effect [22, 34–36]. The tensile strength of TiBw/Ti6Al4V
composites with network microstructure can be signiﬁ-
cantly increased to almost 1,000 and 900 MPa at 400 and
500 /C176C, respectively, just by proper heat treatment [ 35].
Recent experiment results show that for the TiBw/Ti60
composites with network microstructure, the strength can
be further enhanced to almost 1,000 and 800 MPa at 600
and 700 /C176C, respectively, after extrusion deformation. In
general, the superior tensile strength of DRTMCs after hot
deformation such as forging, rolling and extrusion and heat
treatment is related with the strengthening effect of heat
treatment and deformation. However, this improvement
almost disappeared above some critical temperature, such
as 600 /C176C for Ti6Al4V matrix composites [ 35], 750 /C176C for
Ti–6Al–2.5Sn–4Zr–0.7Mo–0.3Si–0.2Y matrix composites
[33], 800 /C176C for Ti60 matrix composites, which is attrib-
uted to the matrix softening effect and disappearance of
work hardening effect. The matrix softening above the
critical temperature is mainly attributed to phase transfor-
mation from a phase to b phase, and also related to the
decrease of dislocation density and interatomic forces at
high temperatures. In addition, recovery, DRX and dislo-
cation climb are responsible for the strength degradation
above the critical high temperature [ 32, 33].
In summary, the strengthening mechanisms of DRTMCs
include the load-bearing strengthening, dispersion
strengthening, dislocation strengthening, and solid solution
strengthening. In addition, microstructure reﬁnement is
beneﬁcial to strengthening the composites at relatively high
temperatures. When the tensile temperature is lower than
the critical temperature, all the above strengthening effects
can play an important role in improving the strength of the
composites.
In addition, although ductility is not a considerable
question for DRTMCs, superplasticity of DRTMCs is still
an important factor which can reveal their deformability
and application ﬁeld. In general, the superplasticity of
DRTMCs is lower than that of the matrix alloy due to the
introduction of hard reinforcement. However, the superior
superplasticity of DRTMCs are frequently reported in
recent years [ 37–41]. Lu et al. [ 42] reported a maximum
elongation of 462% for the 5 vol% (TiBw ? TiCp)/
Ti6Al4V composites at 920 /C176C. Wang et al. [43] reported a

Ti1100 composites at 980 /C176C. Even, a maximum elonga-
tion of 659% was obtained at a very low temperature of
800 /C176C for the 5 vol% (TiC ? TiB)/Ti composites fabri-
cated by casting and forging technology [ 39]. It was also
pointed out that the addition of reinforcements reduced the

3 Creep Behaviors
Numerous literatures showed that DRTMCs exhibited lar-
ger creep stress exponents and higher creep activation
energies compared with the monolithic titanium alloys [ 1,
45, 46], which means the enhancement of creep resistance.
This phenomenon can be mainly attributed to the existence
of hard ceramic reinforcements, which obviously impede
the creep deformation of DRTMCs. Therefore, an extra
threshold stress ( r
0) arises for creep deformation of
DRTMCs. That is to say, the creep deformation of
DRTMCs is determined by not the applied stress (r) but an
effective stress (r
c = r - r0)[ 1]. It is unanimous that the
threshold stress increases with increasing the volume
fractions of ceramic reinforcements. However, Ma et al.
[47, 48] also reported that the threshold stress disappeared
for in situ 15 vol% TiBw/Ti composites and ex situ
15 vol% TiCp/Ti6Al4V composites at 873–923 K. It is
believed that the threshold stress determines the creep
stress exponent, which therefore increases with increasing
the volume fractions of reinforcement. As reported by
Tsang et al. [ 49], the creep stress exponent increases from
4.2 to 6.2 when the TiBw fraction increases from 0 to
15 vol%. Ranganath and Mishra [ 50] also reported high
stress exponents ranging from 6 to 7 at 823 K for
(TiB ? Ti
2C)/Ti and Ti 2C/Ti composites owing to the
transition of creep mechanism from lattice to pipe diffu-
sion-controlled dislocation climb. Even, abnormally high
stress exponents ranging from 8.1 to 14.3 at 823–873 K are
reported for ex situ 15 vol% TiCp/Ti6Al4V composites in
[48]. In addition, the creep rates of DRTMCs signiﬁcantly
decrease compared with the monolithic titanium alloy
under the identical stresses and temperatures. Tsang et al.
[49] reported that the creep rates of in situ TiBw/Ti
composites were between one and three orders of magni-
tude lower than the matrix material. Ma et al. [ 47] also
reported that the creep rate of in situ TiBw/Ti composites
was about one order of magnitude lower than that of the
monolithic titanium material at 923 K. In summary, the
creep properties can be improved by introducing ceramic
reinforcements and increased with increasing the volume
fractions of reinforcements.
Liu et al. [ 51] claimed that the creep resistance of the
10 vol% TiCp/TA15 composites prepared by laser melting
deposition process is signiﬁcantly increased compared with
the monolithic matrix alloy at 600 and 650 /C176C, which is
attributed to the load transfer from the matrix to the rein-
forcements and the reﬁnement of matrix microstructure.
The 10 vol% TiCp/TA15 composites exhibited signiﬁcant
improvement on the creep rupture lives compared with the
8 vol% (TiB ? TiC)/Ti6242 composites reported in [ 52].
The analysis about creep rupture behaviors indicates that
the creep rupture mechanism of the in situ TiCp/TA15
composites is associated with a mixture of particle crack-
ing, interface debonding and interparticle voiding. More-
over, voids nucleated at broken particles, debonded
interfaces and interparticle matrix at the initial stage of
rupture. The ﬁnal creep failure of the composites is still
related to the ductile failure through the growth, coales-
cence and transverse linkage of the voids in the matrix.
In order to further improve the creep properties of
DRTMCs, Xiao et al. [ 53, 54] selected near alpha high
temperature titanium alloy (IMI 834) with the highest
service temperature of 600 /C176C as matrix and the hybrid
(TiBw ? TiCp) and La
2O3 as reinforcements to fabricated
(TiBw ? TiCp ? La2O3)/Ti composites and (TiBw ?
La2O3)/Ti composites by casting and hot working tech-
nologies. It was reported that the composites exhibited a
signiﬁcant enhancement in creep resistance, accompanied
by higher stress exponents compared with the matrix alloy.
As shown in Fig. 5, the threshold stresses of DRTMCs
always exist in both low stress region and high stress
region, while they are much higher in high stress region
compared with those in low stress region. It is worth
pointing out that the threshold stress increases with
decreasing the temperatures.
After considering the threshold stresses, the compensated
activation energies of the composites can be reduced, which
are lower than those of the matrix alloy both in the low stress
region and in the high stress region, as shown in Fig.6.
Except for the reinforcements and matrix types, the heat
treatment and hot deformation can also improve the creep
properties by changing the matrix microstructure. As
reported by Li et al. [ 34], the creep resistance of the
(TiBw ? La
2O3)/Ti composites after the TRIPLEX heat
treatment is slightly lower than that after b heat treatment
due to microstructure reﬁnement.

4 High Temperature Oxidation Behaviors
It can be conﬁrmed that the oxidation behaviors are inﬂu-
enced by extrinsic factors such as oxidation temperature
and oxidation form (continuous in thermal gravity analyzer
(TGA) [55, 56] or discontinuous in air furnace [ 5, 57, 58])
and intrinsic factors such as matrix composition and rein-
forcement type, volume fraction and distribution.
Zhang et al. [55] investigated the oxidation behaviors of
a Ti-6Al-1.2B alloy (i.e., 6.3 vol% TiBw/Ti6Al
composites) fabricated by casting technology in the range
of 600–950 /C176C using a Seiko 320 DTA-TG. The accurate
data showed that the oxidation behavior of the 6.3 vol%
TiBw/Ti6Al composites obeyed the parabolic law; more-
over, the oxidation process with activation energy of
250 kJ/mol was mainly controlled by both the outward
diffusion of Ti and inward diffusion of O through the
oxidation scale. As shown in Fig. 7, pores at the interface
of TiBw–Ti and within the matrix, even cracks were
formed by the interconnecting pores. This phenomenon
indicated that the TiBw reinforcement probably reduces the
oxidation resistance of the composites by evaporation of
B
2O3 during oxidation:

In addition, Zhang et al. [ 55] also pointed out that the
oxygen diffusion along the interface of TiBw–Ti was easier
and faster than that within the matrix alloy, which reduced
the oxidation resistance of the composites. However, they
did not compare the oxidation resistance of the composites
with that of the corresponding Ti–6Al alloy.
In a recent work, Hu et al. [ 59] further conﬁrmed that
the oxidation resistance of the TiBw/Ti60 composites was
indeed deteriorated through comparing the weight–gain
curves of the composites and the matrix alloy due to the
easier and faster diffusion of O element. Moreover, they
pointed out that not only the interface but also the unstable
region including phase boundary and residual stress ﬁeld
formed around TiBw during the fabrication process was
easily oxidized when compared with the matrix alloy far
away from TiBw. Furthermore, the oxidation resistance
decreased with increasing volume fractions of TiBw due to
the increasing interface and grain boundary.
Zhang et al. [ 56] investigated the oxidation behavior of
in situ 10 vol% TiCp/Ti6Al composites in the range of
600–800 /C176C using a TGA. They found that the in situ
composites exhibited a superior oxidation resistance at 600
and 700 /C176C, but their oxidation resistance is tremendously
decreased at 800 /C176C. Certainly, the oxidation behavior of

Liu et al. [ 62] investigated the oxidation behaviors of
Mo
2C/Ti and VC/Ti composites with different volume
fractions in the range of 600–700 /C176C. The results showed
that the addition of Mo 2C was beneﬁcial to the oxidation
resistance which was not changed with increasing the
Mo2C volume fractions. However, the addition of VC is
detrimental to the oxidation resistance due to that the
oxidation product of V

oxidation scale. In addition, they also reported that a small

In our previous work [ 25], TiCp/Ti6Al4V composites
with a tailored network microstructure exhibited a superior
oxidation resistance in the temperature range of
600–800 /C176C. The reasons for the improved oxidation
resistance can be attributed to the following three factors as
shown in Fig. 9. First, the network structures of the com-
posites can divide the oxidation scale into very small units,
which can signiﬁcantly decrease growth and thermal
stresses avoiding spallation of the oxidation scales. Second,
the prior formation of oxidation products near the interface
of TiC-Ti can effectively peg the oxidation scales to avoid
the detachment of the scales. Thirdly, self-assembled TiCp
wall-structure can effectively block oxygen diffusion into
inner matrix.
Velasco and Aswath [61] studied the oxidation behavior
of 5.2 vol% TiBw/Ti8.5Al1Si composites in both ﬂowing
air and static air and found that the oxidation activation
energy of the composites in static air was much higher than
that in ﬂowing air. That is to say, the oxidation resistance
of the composites is decreased in ﬂowing air, which is also
an extrinsic factor.
According to the above results, researchers always claimed
that the oxidation kinetic law of DRTMCs obeys the parabolic
law [5, 55–58]. However, parabolic-linear and linear laws are
also revealed and reported besides the parabolic law in recent
years [59, 62, 63], as shown in Fig. 10. According to the
characteristics of the parabolic law, the oxidation rate gradu-
ally decreases with increasing the oxidation time. Originally,
the whole fresh surface of the specimen is presented in the
high temperature oxidation environment, which results in the
fastest oxidation rate. With the formation of the ﬁrst oxidation
scale, the oxidation rate remarkably decreases. Subsequently,
the oxidation rate gradually decreases with increasing the
thickness of the oxidation scale. These are attributed to the
impeding effect of the growing oxidation scale to oxygen
diffusion. Therefore, the perfect parabolic law corresponds to
the integrated oxidation scale without any detachment and
spallation at relatively low temperatures or continuous oxi-
dation. The intrinsic reason is the relatively low stress due to
the slow growth or absence of thermal stress which is just
generated during cooling process. However, the integrated
oxidation scale can be ﬁrstly formed and then detached at
relatively high temperatures, which corresponds to the para-
bolic-linear law.
Furthermore, with further increasing the oxidation
temperatures, it is probable that the integrated oxidation
scale cannot be formed due to the very large growth and

It should be pointed out that the ceramic reinforcements are
not necessary to enhance the oxidation resistance of DRTMCs
compared with the monolithic titanium alloy. If so, the oxi-

that of the monolithic titanium alloy [59]. First, some ceramic
reinforcements are oxidized prior to the titanium matrix [56].
Second, some ceramic reinforcements such as TiBw would
easily form the evaporating oxidation products which result in
the formation of hole, crack and detachment of oxidation scale
[55]. Finally, with increasing the volume fractions and
decreasing the particle sizes of reinforcements, the interface of
reinforcement and matrix and matrix grain boundary increase,
which certainly decrease the oxidation resistance of DRTMCs
due to the inferior oxidation resistance of the interface and
grain boundary [55, 59]. In contrast, the prior formation of the

by inhibiting the oxidation scale detachment and spallation

to rugged and rough interface in the composites [ 5, 60].
Therefore, before a continuous oxidation scale is formed, the
oxidation kinetic depends on the grain size of matrix and the
type, size, and volume fraction of reinforcements. After the
formation of a continuous oxidation scale, the oxidation
kinetic depends on the stunting effect of oxide scale on O
element inward diffusion and Ti outward diffusion.
High temperature oxidation resistance of DRTMCs
determines their application ﬁeld and lifetime. Although
some conclusions were deduced in the past investigations,
such as oxidation kinetic law, oxidation products, oxidation
scales, etc., research work on oxidation behaviors of
DRTMCs is still insufﬁcient. Moreover, most of the exper-
imental results have not reached a consensus.

5 Summary and Future Outlook
(1) Selecting near a high temperature titanium alloys as
matrix, increasing volume fraction of reinforcement,
tailoring architecture of reinforcement and subsequent
hot deformation and heat treatment can effectively
enhance the tensile strength of DRMTCs at high tem-
peratures. However, the strengthening effects of rein-
forcement, deformation, and heat treatment become pale
due to interface debonding at much high temperatures.
(2) The ceramic reinforcements can effectively enhance
the creep resistance of DRTMCs by impeding the
creep deformation, which is attributed to the threshold
stresses at lower temperatures and stress transfer
effects at higher temperatures. Moreover, the thresh-
old stresses of DRTMCs in high stress region are
much higher than that in low stress region. In
addition, the threshold stress increases with decreas-
ing creep temperatures.
(3) The oxidation resistance of DRTMCs decreases with
increasing volume fractions of ceramic reinforce-
ments such as TiB and VC which can form volatile
oxides, while the oxidation resistance increases with
increasing volume fractions of reinforcements such as
TiC which can form ﬁne and dense oxides. Therefore,
the oxidation resistance of DRTMCs is mainly related
to the quality, constituent of oxidation scale, and the
volume fraction and distribution of reinforcements.
(4) Designing and fabricating multi-scale architecture of
multiple reinforcements and selecting high tempera-
ture titanium alloys as matrix are probably two
effective routes to improve high temperature strength
and creep resistance. Reﬁning microstructure of
matrix and reinforcement and improving distribution
of reinforcement are beneﬁcial to further increase the
superplasticity of DRTMCs.
