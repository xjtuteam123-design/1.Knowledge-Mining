Gradient structure is emerging as an effective strategy to fabricate metals with remarkable mechanical 
performance, but have not been veriﬁed in intermetallic compounds for high-temperature applications. 
Through experiments and atomic simulations, we show that a typical intermetallic TiAl alloy with gra- 
dient structure has a signiﬁcant strengthening effect both at room temperature and high temperatures. 
The room-temperature compressive strength of TiAl alloys with gradient grain obtained by additive man- 
ufacturing is 2.57 GPa, which is ∼2.7 times as strong as that with equiaxed grain. The strengthening 
effect is attributed to more sessile dislocations in gradient structure caused by the intersections of mul- 
tiple slip systems in gradient grain. More importantly, the strengthening effect is still effective at high 
temperatures and the compressive strength is 1.28 GPa at 750 °C. The simulation results show that this 
strengthening effect is due to the increased Hirth dislocation at high temperatures. This study expands 
the applications of TiAl alloys for load-bearing structures and provides a new strategy for improving the 
strength of intermetallic compounds at both room temperature and high temperatures.

1. Introduction 
Gradient structures are common in natural materials, such as 
shells, bones, and many biological tissues [1] . Inspired by the gra- 
dient structure in nature, researchers have been trying to introduce 
gradient structure into engineering materials for more than 40 
years to obtain enhanced strength, ductility, strain hardening, cor- 
rosion, and fatigue resistance through unique deformation mech- 
anisms derived from the structural gradient [2] . The use of struc- 
tural gradient has proven to be an effective strategy for manufac- 
turing metals and metallic alloys with extraordinary mechanical 
properties at room-temperature [3] , but has not yet been proven 
in intermetallic compounds for high-temperature applications. 
Intermetallic compounds have attracted extensive attention 
for high-temperature applications owing to their high speciﬁc 
strength, superior heat resistance, high thermal conductivity, and 
excellent oxidation resistance compared with metallic materials, 
as well as their higher toughness and superior hot workability 
compared with ceramic materials [ 4 , 5 ]. Particularly, intermetallic 
TiAl alloys are the most promising candidate materials to meet 
the requirements of high load-bearing capacity and low density

in aerospace [6–9] , which are increasingly used in the propulsion 
systems in aerospace and aeronautics to effectively im prove fuel 
eﬃciency and reduce harmful pollutant emissions [10] . However, 
the high-temperature strength of polycrystalline TiAl alloys is in- 
suﬃcient, and the strength starts to decline sharply from about 
650 °C, which limits its long-term service temperature not exceed- 
ing 700 °C [ 11 , 12 ]. 
There are four distinct microstructures in polycrystalline TiAl al- 
loy: fully lamellar, nearly lamellar, near gamma and duplex [13] . 
The coarse-grained full lamellar and near lamellar microstructures 
show relatively high fracture toughness and creep resistance, but 
poor strength and ductility, especially at room temperature [ 14 , 15 ]. 
Comparatively ﬁne-grained equiaxed near gamma and duplex mi- 
crostructures exhibit good ductility but poor strength and creep 
resistance [13] . This various performance incompatibility requires 
careful selection of microstructure, making microstructural opti- 
mization crucial for achieving balanced mechanical properties. Mi- 
crostructural reﬁnement is proved to be a valid way to improve the 
strength of TiAl alloys [ 16 , 17 ]. However, recrystallization occurs in 
the nanostructures after heating up to 875 °C, resulting in material 
softening [18] . It is suggested that microstructural reﬁnement can 
enhance the strength of TiAl alloys within a speciﬁc temperature 
range, but this strengthening effect diminishes at high tempera- 
tures.

2. Methodology 
2.1. Experimental and material testing 
In this study, the gas-atomized alloy powder with a nominal 
composition of Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb was adopted to obtain the gradi- 
ent structure with additive manufacturing using the electron-beam 
melting technique. Fig. 1 shows the microstructure and electron 
back-scattered diffraction (EBSD) observational result (inverse pole 
ﬁgure) of the experimental samples. It is shown that the grains 
are in γ phases and the grain sizes increase from hundreds of 
nanometers to tens of microns, exhibiting apparent gradient struc- 
ture. The samples of diameter 15 mm were built with an ARCAM 
A2X machine. The rectangular samples with a length and width 
of 4 mm, a height of 8 mm, and a polished loading surface were 
utilized for the compression experiments. Uniaxial compression ex- 
periments were performed using a servo-hydraulic material testing 
system (Instron, USA) with a strain rate of 1.0 × 10 −3 /s at 27, 300, 
600, and 750 °C. The room-temperature tensile test was performed 
at a strain rate of 1 × 10 −4 /s and the high-temperature tensile test 
was performed at a strain rate of 5 × 10 −4 /s. 
Microstructures of the gradient samples were analyzed with an 
Oxford HKL Symmetry EBSD detector at 20 kV, and the EBSD data 
were analyzed via HKL’s CHANNEL5 software. Meanwhile, defor- 
mation features were examined by a FEI Tecnai 20 transmission 
electron microscope (TEM) and an aberration-corrected FEI Titan 
G2 60-300 TEM.

3. Results and discussions 
3.1. Experiments of strengthening in gradient TiAl alloys 
Fig. 3 (a) presents the room-temperature compressive engineer- 
ing stress-strain curves of the experimental samples. It is shown 
that the compressive strength is 2.57 GPa, and fracture strain is 
31.43% in our TiAl alloys with gradient grain. The compressive 
strength is ∼2.7 times as strong as casting Ti-48Al-2Nb-2Cr al- 
loy (0.95 GPa) with equiaxed grain [30] . As shown in Fig. 3 (b), 
the TiAl alloy with gradient structure manufactured in our work 
has the highest compressive strength compared with those of pub- 
lished TiAl alloys while also shows signiﬁcant ductility. More de- 
tailed data are summarized in Table 1 . Moreover, TiAl alloys are 
mostly employed in low-pressure turbine blades, where the ser- 
vice temperature is often above 600 °C, so it is necessary to ex- 
plore its high-temperature properties [10] . The high-temperature 
compression performance of TiAl was then tested and the engi- 
neering stress-strain curves were presented in Fig. 3 (b). It is shown 
that at 300 °C the compressive strength is 2.06 GPa, and the frac- 
ture strain is 41.0%. At 600 °C, the compressive strength is 1.83 
GPa, and the fracture strain is 41.5%. At 750 °C, the compressive 
strength is 1.28 GPa, and the fracture strain is 54.5%. Remarkably, 
the high-temperature compression properties of TiAl with gradient 
structure are excellent, which is better than that of Ti-48Al-2Nb- 
2Cr alloy without gradient structure (about 1.00 GPa at 600 °C) 
[30] . The compressive strength of TiAl with gradient structure at 
750 °C is ∼1.28 times as strong as that with equiaxed grain at 
600 °C. 
Fig. S1(a) shows the room-temperature tensile engineering 
stress-strain curves of the experimental samples. It is shown that 
the room-temperature tensile yield strength ( σ0.2 ) is 571.8 MPa in 
our TiAl alloys with gradient grain, which is higher than casting 
Ti-48Al-2Nb-2Cr alloy (about 310 MPa) with equiaxed grain [44] . 
Meanwhile, the TiAl alloy with gradient structure manufactured in 
our work has tensile yield strength of 462.7 MPa with gradient 
grain at 750 °C (Fig. S1(b)), which is higher than casting Ti-48Al- 
2Nb-2Cr alloy (about 290 MPa at 700 °C) with equiaxed grain [44] . 
The results show that our alloy has not only excellent compressive 
properties but also excellent tensile properties.

To elucidate the strengthening effect in the TiAl alloys with gra- 
dient structure, the TEM characterizations of the deformed struc- 
tures are shown in Fig. 4 . The experiments show that the si- 
multaneous activation of different slip systems intersects with 
each other, leading to the formation of deformation intersections 
( Fig. 4 (a)). The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern 
shows that the angle between two twin phase spots is ∼70 °, in- 
dicating that twin/stacking fault intersects. Previous studies have 
shown that the shear strain increases with grain size in a deformed 
gradient structure [45] , so more slip systems will be triggered in 
the larger grain, resulting in the deformation intersections. Due 
to the intersections of twin/stacking fault, some sessile disloca- 
tions will be formed, further hindering the movement of dislo- 
cations. Detailed analysis of the dislocation structures shows that 
the twin/stacking fault intersection will block the ordinary dislo- 
cations ( Fig. 4 (b)). The movement of ordinary dislocations in the 
interaction region was thus substantially suppressed and accumu- 
lated, which contributes to additional strength enhancement and 
further proves the strengthening effect of gradient structure. 
3.2. Atomistic mechanism of strengthening in gradient TiAl alloys 
To address the atomistic mechanism of strengthening in gra- 
dient TiAl alloys, molecular dynamics simulations in gradient 
and equiaxed grain TiAl alloys were performed. The stress-strain 
curves obtained from the uniaxial tensile simulation are shown in 
Fig. 5 (a). The gradient structure with grain sizes increasing from 
9 to 46 nm has a higher yield strain, yield stress, and ﬂow stress 
than the structure with equiaxed grain, suggesting that TiAl alloys 
with gradient structure have an extra strengthening effect than 
those with equiaxed grain. The compressive performance of gra- 
dient structure is also better than that of grain sizes of 9 and 46 
nm. Similar effects of gradient grain on the mechanical properties 
of metal have also been reported in our experiments. To investi- 
gate the evolutions of internal defect with strain, the dislocation 
densities of gradient and equiaxed grain were calculated. Fig. 5 (b) 
shows that the dislocation densities vary with strain both in gra- 
dient and equiaxed grain cases. It is interesting that the gradient 
structure has a lower dislocation density than the structure with 
equiaxed grain, which means that the gradient structure has an in- 
hibition effect on dislocation propagation and movement. These re- 
sults suggest that the dislocation mechanisms are totally different 
in gradient and equiaxed grains. 
Typical atomistic structures of gradient and equiaxed grains at 
9% strain are shown in Fig. 6 . It is shown that dislocations, stacking 
faults, and slip bands appear both in gradient and equiaxed grains, 
indicating that multiple plastic mechanisms are triggered during 
loading. In addition to these conventional deformed structures in 
gradient and equiaxed grains, some sessile dislocations will occur 
due to special crystal structure. It is well known that dislocation

3.3. Effect of temperature on strengthening 
As a kind of superalloy, it is necessary to study the effect of 
temperature on the strengthening of TiAl alloys by gradient struc- 
ture. The tension samples with gradient and equiaxed grains were 
simulated at different temperatures to explore the effect of tem- 
perature on strengthening. The stress-strain curves at 27, 30 0, 60 0, 
750, and 900 °C of the compression samples are shown in Fig. 8 (a). 
The stress decreases after the yielding and then enters the ﬂow 
stress stage with increasing strain. As the loading temperature in- 
creases from room to high temperatures, the average ﬂow stress 
decreases with temperature both in gradient and equiaxed grains 
( Fig. 8 (b)), showing the temperature-dependent mechanical prop- 
erties of TiAl alloys. The gradient structure enhances the strength 
by 10.30% at room temperature, 18.66% at 300 °C, 11.61% at 600 °C, 
12.57% at 750 °C and 9.16% at 900 °C than equiaxed grain. This ob- 
servation shows that the strengthening effect by gradient structure 
also acts at high temperatures compared with equiaxed grain.

4. Conclusions 
The study reveals the strengthening effect in gradient TiAl al- 
loys as compared with their equiaxed counterpart. It is found that 
the compressive strength of TiAl alloys with gradient grain is 2.57 
GPa, which is ∼2.7 times as strong as that with equiaxed grain. 
The strengthening effect is attributed to more sessile dislocations 
in gradient structure caused by the activated multiple slip systems 
in gradient grain. On the contrary, a large number of slip systems 
with the same direction aggregate to form slip bands, resulting in 
strain localization in equiaxed grain. Meanwhile, the strengthen- 
ing effect is still valid at high temperatures until 750 °C due to the 
added Hirth dislocation. However, the enhancement is weakened 
at 900 °C due to the degeneration of the Hirth dislocation in the 
gradient grain. Our results on the strengthening in gradient struc- 
ture by sessile dislocations (from structural gradient) promote the 
basic understanding of the deformation mechanisms in gradient 
intermetallic compound TiAl and provide a new way for enhanc- 
ing the strength of intermetallic compound materials both at room 
temperature and high temperatures.
