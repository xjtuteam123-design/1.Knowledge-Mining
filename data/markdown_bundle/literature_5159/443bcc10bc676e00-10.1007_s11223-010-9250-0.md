A β-titanium alloy, obtained by cooling a melt at a rate of ~ 800°C/s, has been studied by the
methods of scanning and transmission electron microscopy, short-duration and long-duration
macroindentation, as well as uniaxial compression in a temperature range of 20–1000 °C. The alloy
in the solid state contains ultrafine dendritic crystals, in the interstices between which are nanosized
particles of minor phases. The alloy may be regarded as heat-resistant alloy: it possesses a high
thermal stability of mechanical properties, higher values of high-temperature strength and creep
resistance.
Keywords: multicomponent β-titanium alloy, high melt cooling rate, thermal stability, increased high-temperature
strength.
Introduction. When fabricating cast β-titanium alloys with structure ensuring a definite combination of
mechanical characteristics, one usually proceeds from two basic principles: alloying of titanium with a large number
of β-stabilizing and other elements and cooling of the melt at high rate [1–4]. As a result, when multicomponent
titanium alloy solidifies, its microstructure acquires the appearance of dendritic crystals, which are a β-titanium based
solid solution and are distributed in a matrix. The matrix contains a β-titanium based solid solution of different
composition and chemical compounds of titanium with alloying elements (in most cases, intermetallics). In this case,
primary dendritic crystals (which are usually large and branched) contribute ductility to alloy, and chemical
compounds of titanium are in the fine-grained state, due to which high values of strength characteristics are achieved.
By changing the composition and concentration of alloying elements and the melt cooling rate, one can change the
ratio of the volume fractions of dendritic crystals and fine particles and their size and hence control the combination
of the strength and ductility characteristics of β-titanium alloy in the solid state.
In recent 5–10 years, a thermodynamic approach to the design of multicomponent alloys has received a large
development effort [5–7]. Its essence is that multicomponent alloy can be obtained in the state of single-phase
substitutional solid solution, which can be highly strong and thermodynamically stable. This is primarily achieved by
selecting the appropriate number of components and their concentration ratio in the alloy. These factors can ensure in
the calculated (charged) composition a higher mixing entropy value (and hence a lower free energy value of the alloy
in accordance with the Gibbs equation) and maintain the alloy in molten state and under special cooling conditions in
solid state as well. In this case, the larger the number of elements, the higher the value of mixing entropy, and its
maximum value for the given number of elements is achieved for alloy of equiatomic composition. Because of the
difference of the atomic radii of the substitutional elements, the crystal lattice (as a rule only bcc lattice or in
combination with fcc lattice) is greatly distorted, which ensures, together with the fine-grain structure formed at the
nanosize level, improved strength characteristics of the alloy. When the alloy itself acquires a lower free energy, it
becomes stable against solid solution decomposition on subsequent heat treatment.

This “entropic” approach to the design of multicomponent alloy allows one to determine the number of
elements and their ratio a priori and to assess the phase and structural states after crystallization and hence its thermal
stability. Unfortunately, it is impossible to solve with the aid of this approach the problem of choosing concrete alloy
elements to obtain the required values of mechanical characteristics. Therefore, having taken the results of the
“entropic” assessment of its behavior as a basis, one must determine the qualitative composition empirically using
the results of investigation to assess the effect of large number of factors (from atomic to technological one) on
mechanical properties.
As to titanium alloys, high mixing entropy, resulting from the large number of alloy elements (5–9) and their
appropriate ratio, can ensure alloy stability in liquid state under cooling and hence the possibility of considerable
melt supercooling below the initial crystallization temperature in comparison with low-entropy composition, which
will result in intensive branching and considerable reduction of dendrites in size. Decreased diffusion mobility of
elements in solid state (again because of lower free energy) may lead to an additional decrease in βα→ phase
transition temperature. It follows that to design β-titanium alloy, it is more expedient to use, as far as possible, a large
number of β-stabilizing elements with low concentration of each of them than several elements with high
concentration. In this case, the scope for achieving different combinations of mechanical properties becomes wider.
In this work, a seven–component β-titanium alloy has been obtained using the above-mentioned ideas of
creating high-strength thermally stable titanium alloys; after rapid cooling from the liquid state to room temperature,
this alloy has a fine-grain dendritic structure in combination with fine particles of minor phases. The mechanical
behavior of this alloy under the conditions of thermal action as well as short- and long-duration loading in a
temperature range of 20–1000 °C is studied.
Experimental Procedure. To fabricate the alloy, the following compact materials in the following ratio
(charged composition) were used (at.%): 34.9 Ti, 15.0 Zr, 30.1 Co, 5.0 Ni, 7.9 Cu, 5.0 Ga, and 2.2 Si.
Known data were used in the formation of the qualitative and quantitative composition of the titanium alloy.
Basing on the definition of the concept of “high-entropy” alloys, in particular that the number of elements in
them must be such that the maximum concentration of any of them does not exceed 35 at.% [6], the titanium content
was set at 34.9 at.%.
The metals Co, Ni, Cu, and Zr are β-stabilizing elements and in combination with Si efficient β-titanium
based solid solution “strengtheners” [1]. In spite of the fact that 5 at.% Ga increases slightly the βα→
transformation temperature, it acts, as silicon, as a modifying element, i.e., branching and reduction of dendrites in
size during crystallization are ensured through the limitation of their unidirectional growth. All chosen elements form
with titanium substitutional solid solutions with different solubility limit [1, 8]. The calculated mixing entropy value
of alloy of the given charged composition Smix =13 3. J/(mol K⋅ ), which is higher than the maximum possible fusion
entropy value of pure metals, which is not over 11 J/( mol K⋅ ) [5].
A charge of mass 100 g was melted under purified argon by the electric are method with a nonconsumable
tungsten electrode on a water-cooled copper hearth in an indentation of hemispherical geometry. The small ingots
obtained were remelted six or seven times to homogenize the composition, after which they were poured into a
water-cooled copper mold 10 mm in diameter and 100 mm in length. Under these conditions, the cooling rate of the
liquid melt in the copper mold is 800–900 °C.
The following methods of investigation and testing were employed: X-ray phase analysis (DRON–UM1
diffractometer in monochromatic Cu Kα -radiation) and differential thermal analysis; scanning (Superprobe-733) and
transmission (JEM-2100F) electron microscopy in combination with INCA X-ray microanalyzer (electron beam
diameter 0.7 nm); low-temperature (T =°20 C ) automatic Berkovich pyramid microindentation on a “Mikron-gamma”
device by the method described in [9–11]; high-temperature ( T = 20–1000°C) short-duration (1 min) and long-
duration (1–60 min) Vickers pyramid indentation; uniaxial compression under vacuum in a temperature range of
20–900°C( 335×× mm specimens, strain rate 81 0 41⋅ −− s ).

Phase Composition and Structural State of the Alloy. X-ray phase analysis of the alloy shows that its base
consists of two β-titanium based solid solutions with bcc lattice, whose parameters a1 0 3081= . nm and a2 =
0.3057 nm, the amount of titanium-based β1 and β2 phases is approximately the same. Moreover, a small amount
of a third phase ( ~5 wt.%), Ti3Si3 intermetallic with hexagonal unit cell and the constants: a = 0 7871. nm and c =
0.5451 nm, is noted (Fig. 1a). The presence of titanium silicide Ti 3Si3 in the alloy is also corroborated by the results
of transmission electron microscope investigations (Fig. 2b). In accordance with known data pertaining to the lattice
parameter value of β-titanium alloys [1] and the results of a microanalysis of the composition of the above alloy
(Table 1, Fig. 2), it can be classified as an alloy based on two β-titanium based solid solutions, which contains
particles of intermetallic phases.
After annealing at 750°C (Fig. 1b), one β-titanium based solid solution with smaller lattice constant
aβ = 0 3031. nm is identified by X-ray radiography. The lattice constants of titanium silicide Ti 3Si3 also decrease to
the values a = 0 7857. nm and c = 0 5434. nm. In addition, small amounts of the following phases are identified
diffractometrically: hexagonal phase GaNiZr ( a = 0 6902. nm and c = 0 3421. nm) and rhombic phase Ga 2Zr
(.a =1 2894 nm, b = 0 3994. nm, and c = 0 4123. nm).
When the annealing temperature is raised to 1000°C, the two last phases containing Ga and Zr disappear.
The lattice constant of the β-titanium alloy increases slightly and is a = 0 3037. nm, for the intermetallic Ti 5Si3 the
values of the constants decrease to a = 0 7846. nm and c = 0 5431. nm.
The structure of the alloy has the appearance of fine dendritic crystals, which are relatively uniformly
distributed in the ultrafine-grained matrix (Fig. 2a), in which several size levels can be discerned. Level 1: large
100–150 μm conglomerates, orientationally arranged dendritic colonies acting as their boundaries. Level 2: dendritic
colonies of globular type within each conglomerate; the size of dendrite colonies is 10–20 μm. Level 3: ~ (1–5) μm
dendrite globules and branches. Level 4 interdendritic space, which is a β-titanium based solid solution containing
fine particles of minor phases.

The microanalysis carried out allows one to establish the characteristic features of alloy element distribution
between the structural constituents (Table 1, Fig. 2). It can be seen that both structural domains of the alloy
(dendrites and interdendritic space) contain all the elements introduced in the charged composition of the alloy, but
the concentration ratio of the elements is changed, in comparison with the charged composition.
Thus, due to the given qualitative and quantitative charged composition of the alloy and to its high cooling
rate in conjunction with the higher mixing entropy value of the melt, an alloy based on two β-titanium based
substitutional solid solutions with bcc lattice has been obtained, which was called TK35 β-titanium alloy. The alloy
has an ultrafine-grain dendritic structure and contains fine particles of minor phases. Its characteristic feature is a
relatively low initial melting temperature ( 1320°C).
Thermal Stability of the Alloy. Effect of Annealing on Mechanical Properties at Room Temperature.
The dependence of the mechanical properties of the alloy at room temperature on high-temperature annealing
conditions was studied by the automatic indentation method using a new methodology, developed earlier [10, 11], of
processing and analysis of results.
The TK35 cast alloy in the hardened state possesses high hardness values ( HIT = 9.5–10.5 GPa), which are
level with or higher than those of known high-strength titanium alloys [4, 12]. Young’s modulus of the alloy E =
110–130 GPa. When a Berkovich indenter is used, the noncontact elastic strain of the alloy (“purely” elastic strain
which occurs in the alloy indentation region that is not in contact with the indenter) [10, 11] is at the level ε es = 25.% ,
and the stress corresponding to it σes = 3 GPa.

Conclusions. An alloy of the charged composition (at.%): 34.9Ti–15.0Zr–30.1Co–5.0Ni–7.9Cu–5.0Ga–
2.2Si (mixing entropy Smix =13 3. J/(mol K⋅ ) has been studied. By cooling a melt at a rate of the order of
800–900°C/s, an alloy based on two β-titanium based substitutional solid solutions with bcc lattice (lattice constants:
a1 0 3081= . nm and a2 0 3057= . nm) has been obtained, which was called TK35 β-titanium alloy. The alloy has a
fine-grain dendritic structure (1–5–10 μm) and contains fine particles of intermetallic phases. The initial melting
temperature is 1320 °C.
At room temperature, the alloy has high hardness values ( HIT = 9.5–10.5 GPa). Young’s modulus
E = 110–130 GPa. In the case of indentation with a Berkovich indenter, the noncontact elastic strain of the alloy
ε es = 25.% , and the stress corresponding to it σes = 3 GPa.
This alloy has a high thermal stability: after annealing at 400–900 °C, i.e., at up to 07 4. Tmelt , and indentation
for 10–120 min, the values of micro- and macrohardness, Young’s modulus E, noncontact elastic strain ε es, and the
stress σes corresponding to it remain constant at 20 °C.
The results of measurement of long-time hardness indicate that at a temperature below 800°C( 06 7. Tmelt ), the
alloy shows practically no creep and may be regarded as heat-resistant.
Uniaxial compression tests show that the alloy possesses at elevated temperatures of 700–900 °C
[(0.61–0.74)Tmelt ] high strength ( σ pr = 1.7–3.5 GPa) and ductility ( ε= 5–50%) values.
The factors that ensure the good strength characteristics of the TK35 cast β-titanium alloy under consideration
in a wide temperature and loading time range are a high fineness, a high degree of branching of solid solution
dendritic crystals, uniformity of their distribution and the presence of fine particles of minor phases in the
interdendritic space. These factors result, in their turn, from the qualitative and quantitative composition of the alloy
in conjunction with rapid cooling from the liquid state.
