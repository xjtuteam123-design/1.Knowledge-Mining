1. Introduction
The performance requirements for metal components are becoming 
increasingly diversified, particularly in fields such as aerospace, marine 
engineering, automobile manufacturing, and construction. In these 
fields, large metal components are often fabricated with various types of 
steels, such as duplex (DP) steel, complex phase (CP) steel, 
transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) steel, and quenching and par -
titioning (QP) steel [1] . They have different chemical compositions, 
microstructures and mechanical properties and can provide the required 
strength, toughness and corrosion resistance of metal components under 
certain conditions. However, the joining of dissimilar steels can raise 
serious safety concerns, for example when different welding processes 
are used to join the steels. On the one hand, Welded joints, whether 
formed by similar or dissimilar metals, exhibit a propensity for defects 
such as pores, trapped slag, and cracks. These defects arise due to mis -
matched thermal expansion coefficients and phase transformations 
during the welding process. These defects diminish load-bearing ca -
pacity, induce stress concentration, and heighten the risk of brittle 
fracture [2,3] . On the other hand, electrochemical performance dis -
parities may emerge among dissimilar metals. For instance, during a 
ship ’ s voyage, titanium alloys exhibit minimal self-corrosion in 
seawater. However, the weld formed between titanium alloy and steel is 
susceptible to galvanic corrosion, which significantly impacts its service 
life and safety [4] . At the same time, steels with diverse alloy compo -
sitions incur higher manufacturing costs, diminished productivity, and

"one steel for multiple uses" [5] may offer a solution to these challenges. 
By designing steels composed of a single alloy and ensuring high

Low-alloy CrNiMo steel is widely used in key fields such as marine 
engineering [5,6]
, 
automobile manufacturing [7] , bearings [8] , and 
nuclear industry [9] owing to its high strength, good low – temperature 
toughness, wear resistance, and anti-hydrogen embrittlement perfor -
mance [10 – 13] . It is one of the best candidates to the low-alloy steel 
owning varying strength levels at the same composition [14] . Adjusting 
the heat-treatment process is currently one of the most direct and 
effective means to achieve strength-toughness matching of low-alloy 
CrNiMo steel. Recent advancements in microstructural engineering 
through thermo-mechanical processing have yielded unparalleled 
strength-toughness combinations in low-alloy steels, as evidenced by 
Zhao et al. [15] . The synergistic optimisation of the strength and 
toughness of such steels relies on the modulation of the microstructure 
by quenching and tempering parameters, including martensitic slat 
refinement, carbide precipitation and residual austenite (RA) stabilisa -
tion. However, for applications requiring both high strength and frac -
ture resistance, the mechanisms linking heat treatment conditions, 
tissue evolution and mechanical properties still need to be revealed.
A substantial body of research has demonstrated the remarkable 
sensitivity of mechanical properties in low-alloy steels to quenching 
parameters [16,17,26] . For instance, Pan et al. [16] found that 
increasing the quenching temperature of Fe-Cr-Ni-Mo steels from 800
◦
C 
to 1200

and the effective grain size (EGS) decreased drastically from 119 J to 
38 J (a decrease of 68 %), suggesting that the refinement of the EGS is a 
key factor in maintaining toughness. In contrast, Zhao et al. [17]
introduced a stable RA phase in low-Ni steel by two – phase zone 
quenching and utilised the phase transition – induced plasticity (TRIP) 
effect to achieve a high impact energy of 130 J while achieving GPa level 
strength. Such studies highlight the potential of two-phase zone treat -
ment in disrupting the strength – toughness inversion relationship. 
Additionally, the quench cooling rate significantly affects the tissue 
evolution: Du et al. [18] demonstrated that when the cooling rate of 
30CrMoNiV511 steel was reduced from 50

bide – rich upper bainite and precipitated ferrite, and the impact tough -
ness decreased abruptly from 66 J to 7 J. This suggests that precise 
control of the cooling rate is essential to inhibit the formation of brittle 
phases.
Generally, the lath martensite formed in low-alloy CrNiMo steel after 
quenching is the highest tensile strength. The dislocation substructure 
and second phase precipitation behavior in the lath martensite matrix 
can be regulated by medium/high temperature tempering. In this way, 
its strength and toughness can be well matched to meet the mechanical 
performance requirements of the workpiece. For example, As Wen et al. 
[19] found, the precipitation of fine M
2

steels, when tempered at 530 – 600 
◦
C, optimises the matching of 
strength and toughness. Conversely, the coarsening of M
2
C and MC type 
carbides at temperatures above 600
◦
C results in a significant attenuation 
of strength. Concurrently, Zhang et al. [20] stabilised RA films in 
bainitic-based 690 MPa grade offshore steel by tempering at 660 
◦
C, 
thereby concurrently enhancing strength and crack extension resistance. 
Nevertheless, improper high – temperature tempering (700 
◦
C tempering 
of SA508 – 3 steel near Ac1) induces grain – boundary embrittlement M-A 
islands, which severely impairs toughness. These studies imply that the 
tempering process must find an equilibrium between the inhibition of 
embrittlement and the retention of strength [21] . Recent studies have 
further expanded the design space of low-alloy steels through synergistic 
strengthening mechanisms. Wang et al. [22] achieved an ultra-high 
tensile strength of 2420 MPa and an elongation of 8.5 % in 20Cr2Ni2 -
MoV gear steel using carburizing and secondary quenching processes. 
These results are attributed to the synergistic effect of nano-precipitated 
phases and grain-boundary structure, while Yin et al. [23] achieved a 
yield strength of 871 MPa and an elongation of 25 % in a low-alloy steel 
through austenite refining and dislocation engineering. These results 
demonstrate that fine slat martensite and nano – twinning can synergis -
tically inhibit crack extension and activate slip system plasticity. Addi -
tionally, Wei et al. [24] developed a Cu-rich precipitation – strengthened 
steel with a yield strength of 930 MPa and a brittle transition temper -
ature below – 90 
◦
C through the synergistic effect of nano-Cu phases and 
fine – slab martensite. However, it is challenging to cover a wide range of 
strengths with a single precipitation strengthening, and it is necessary to 
dynamically modulate the microstructure with the combination of heat 
treatment parameters.
Multi – level strength matching can be achieved under the same 
composition by adjusting the heat treatment process, but most of the 
existing studies focus on the optimization of a single process parameter 
(tempering temperature or cooling rate) to achieve a static balance of 
strength – toughness, whereas the dynamic regulation mechanism to 
achieve a wide range of tensile strengths under the constraints of high 
toughness is still not clear. This limitation severely restricts the adapt -
ability of single alloy composition materials in complex service sce -
narios, and there is an urgent need to establish multiscale performance 
designability through the synergistic regulation of cooling rate -
– tempering parameters.
In this study, the variable tensile strength ranges of low-alloy 
CrNiMo steels under conditions of high toughness are investigated 
through the optimization of quenching and tempering parameters. The 
study elucidates the influence mechanism of matrix type and precipi -
tation phase evolution on the strength and toughness of low-alloy 
CrNiMo steel. This provides theoretical guidance for the heat – treat -
ment process design of low-alloy steel with a single alloy composition for 
different mechanical properties.

4. Discussion
4.1. Influence mechanism of microstructure type on the strength and 
toughness of low-alloy CrNiMo steel
Low – alloy CrNiMo steel had a strong sensitivity to the quenching 
cooling rate. The matrix microstructure obtained in the WC specimens 
was LM. Compared with the G-B formed in the OC and AC specimens, the 
grains had higher dislocation density and lath substructure, with higher 
strength. Dense laths were distributed inside the prior austenite grains, 
which have a grain – refining effect. Both lath bundles and lath blocks 
belonged to high – angle grain boundaries, which can effectively prevent 
the propagation of microcracks. Therefore, the strength and toughness 
of the WC specimens were well matched.
As the cooling rate decreased, the bainite phase transformation 
occurred in the OC and FC specimens. The alloying elements (especially 
carbon) had sufficient time to diffuse and enrich at the lath boundaries 
or prior – austenite grain boundaries (PAGB) to form blocky M-A islands. 
At this time, blocky M-A islands were distributed between prior -
– austenite grains, while elongated M-A strips were distributed within 
the grains ( Fig. 5 ). This was consistent with literature reports [28] . In OC 
specimens at higher cooling rates, the area proportion of M-A islands 
decreased, and the distance between adjacent M-A islands increased. 
Zhao et al. [29] suggested that the toughness of granular bainite was 
related to the distribution of M-A islands. The M-A island was a brittle 
phase prone to becoming a nucleation site for crack sources. The more 
dispersed the M-A island distribution, the farther apart the crack sour -
ces, and the more difficult the crack propagation, resulting in tougher 
granular bainite. In addition, the increased plastic ferrite phase had an 
increased dislocation slip free path during deformation, which signifi -
cantly improved the toughness of granular bainite in OC specimens.
In the bainitic ferrite matrix, the M-A islands acted as a hard brittle 
phase. The critical stress for the cleavage fracture of the M-A islands can 
be calculated using Griffith ’ s theory [30,31] . The increase in the size of 
the M-A island reduced its initial stress for cleavage fracture and 
increased the probability of fracture nucleation inside the M-A island or 
at the interface with the matrix. It was not difficult to observe that both 
AC and FC specimens formed relatively coarse, blocky M-A islands near 
the grain boundaries ( Fig. 5 c, d ), leading to brittle fracture and severe 
deterioration of low – temperature toughness. Owing to the formation of 
a large amount of proeutectoid ferrite in the FC specimens, the dislo -
cation density and substructure within matrix, which, according to the 
mixing rule of multiphase composite materials, led to a decrease in the 
strength of the FC specimens [32] .
