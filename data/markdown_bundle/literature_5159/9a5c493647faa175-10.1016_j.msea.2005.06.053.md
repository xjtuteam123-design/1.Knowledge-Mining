Abstract
In order to develop an ultra-ﬁne grained vanadium alloy dispersed with nano-sized dispersoids having superior ductility and high strength,
powder metallurgical methods utilizing mechanical alloying (MA) and hot isostatic pressing (HIP) processes were applied to vanadium with
1.7 mass% yttrium addition. The developed alloy was annealed at 1273–1573 K for 3.6 ks and subjected to TEM observations and tensile tests
at room temperature.
The alloy has average grain sizes from 330 to 750 nm, and average particle sizes of Y
2O3 and YN from 7 to 23 nm, depending on annealing
temperature. It exhibits superior ductility and high strength even in the as-HIPed state (no plastic working). Eighty percent cold rolling slightly
improves the tensile properties. These results indicate that the microstructure of the alloy is almost free from weak portions associated with
gaseous interstitial impurities that cause signiﬁcant loss of ductility.

1. Introduction
It is well known that vanadium is very chemically reactive
with gaseous interstitial impurities, particularly nitrogen and
oxygen. Dissolution of the interstitials into the matrix leads
to signiﬁcant hardening and ductility loss. In order to mini-
mize such ductility loss, vanadium and its alloys have been
so far processed only by electron-beam melting that is effec-
tive in reduction of the interstitials in the matrix. In melting
processes, however, the capability of microstructural control
is limited, and hence it is difﬁcult to expect the ﬁnding of
newly advanced properties and improvements of mechanical
and physical properties of vanadium and its alloys.

Mechanical alloying (MA) [1] is a very effective means
for microstructural control. It can provide powders with
nano-sized grains of matrix materials supersaturated with
selected constituent elements and a very high density of
dislocations. These features of MA powders lead to a vari-
ety of microstructures, including our aimed microstruc-
ture that consists of ultra-ﬁne grains and very ﬁnely dis-
persed, thermally stable particles in a consolidated state.
Microstructural reﬁnement can contribute to improvement
of strength without sacriﬁcing ductility. In the applica-
tion of the MA process to fabricate vanadium alloys hav-
ing the aimed microstructure, we proposed a process [2]
that the interstitials of oxygen and nitrogen not only con-
tained in the starting powders but also introduced during the
fabrication processes are consumed to form yttrium com-
pounds (Y
2O3 and YN) as ﬁne dispersoids. We demon-
strated that the process was very effective in removing the

Ultra-ﬁne grained microstructures with very ﬁne disper-
soids are expected to be a solution to serious embrittle-
ment caused by high-energy particle irradiations [3–11].
Grain boundaries and ﬁne dispersoids are effective sinks for
irradiation-induced defects. Microstructures introduced by
MA for vanadium showed improved resistance to fast neu-
tron irradiations to 0.25 displacement per atom (dpa) at 563 K
and 0.7 dpa at 873 K[10,11].
However, the microstructures in V–Y alloys reported in
the previous papers[2,10,11] were not satisfactory in the fol-
lowing respects: coarse-grained and dispersoid-free regions
scattered among the ultra-ﬁne grained regions having an
average grain size of approximately 300 nm and ﬁne disper-
soids of Y
2O3 and YN with an average diameter of 20 nm.
Some crack-like pores existed mostly around the interfaces
between coarse grained and ultra-ﬁne grained regions. Such
microstructural inhomogeneity was considered to be mainly
due to insufﬁcient MA processes. Therefore, in this study
efforts to optimize the MA conditions were made. Using the
optimized MA and hot isostatic pressing (HIP) processes, a
consolidated alloy of V–1.7 mass% Y with ultra-ﬁne grains
and nano-sized dispersoids of Y
2O3 and YN was successfully
fabricated. It was demonstrated in this work that the devel-
oped alloy has shown high strength and sufﬁcient ductility
even in the as-HIPed condition.
2. Experimental procedures
2.1. Material and specimen
Powders of pure vanadium (particle size: <150 /H9262m,
0.08 mass% oxygen, 0.07 mass% nitrogen) and pure yttrium
(<750 /H9262m, 1.56 mass% oxygen, 0.05 mass% nitrogen) were
used as the starting materials. They were mixed to pro-
vide a nominal composition of V–1.7 mass% Y in a spe-
cially designed glove box, where outgassing of the inside
wall and all the necessities including MA vessels and balls,
spoons and a balance was carried out around 420 K and
1 × 10
−4 Pa for 36 ks before introducing a puriﬁed Ar gas
(purity 99.99999%). The yttrium content of 1.7 mass% is
excessive with respect to the stoichiometry of Y
2O3 and YN
so that all the oxygen and nitrogen contained in the start-
ing powders and introduced during the fabrication processes
can be consumed to form Y
2O3 and YN. The mixed pow-
der and WC/Co balls (10 mm in diameter) were charged
into two WC/Co milling vessels having an inside volume of
250 cm
3. Each vessel was sealed with an oxygen-free copper
gasket in the glove box prior to MA by a planetary ball mill
(model:Fritch P 5).
Optimization of the MA process was performed so as to
meet the following three requirements: (1) to reach the ﬁnal
stage of MA [12–14], (2) to collect a sufﬁcient amount of
MA powder and (3) to suppress processing contamination.

This value of 1.68 at.% corresponds to 2.93 mass%, which
is larger than the yttrium content in the alloy shown inTable 1.
In general, the atomic radii of solute and solvent atoms in
solid solutions are known to be different from those in the
pure metals of each constituent. In view of this aspect, the
value of 1.68 at.% (2.93 mass%) is a rough estimate, how-
ever, the yttrium concentration in vanadium is far beyond the
equilibrium value, which is extremely small according to the
V–Y phase diagram [17]. The vanadium powder is therefore
highly supersaturated with yttrium in the as-mechanically
alloyed state.
In the as-HIPed specimens, distinct peaks of Y
2O3 and YN
are observed, which are due to the reaction of solute oxygen
and nitrogen with solute yttrium during the HIP treatment.
Since the yttrium content of 1.7 mass% is excessive with
respect to the stoicheometry of Y
2O3 and YN as mentioned
above, it is most likely that all of the oxygen and nitrogen
contained in the specimen are consumed to form Y
2O3 and
YN.
Fig. 3 shows OM microstructures of the specimens
annealed at 1373 and 1573 K. It is obvious that there are no
coarse-grained regions and crack-like pores as observed in the
previous work. Even after 1573 K annealing the grain is too

images showing the grain structures after annealing at vari-

as 750 and 23 nm, respectively. The thermal stability of the
microstructure of the alloy was achieved by uniform distri-
bution of the dispersoids.

of the alloy can be controlled by post HIP annealing since
ductility tends to increase with decreasing strength. There-

It should be emphasized that such good ductility and high
strength is achieved without any plastic working after HIP ,
providing that the alloy is annealed at a suitable tempera-
ture. This means that the present fabrication technique can
be applied to develop vanadium alloys that have not been
successfully fabricated due to difﬁculty in plastic working
of ingots required to break up the cast structure. A study
dealing with this issue has been done for V–Cr alloys con-
taining 28–52 mass% Cr and will be described elsewhere
[18].
In general, a high degree of plastic working is very effec-
tive in improving the ductility of metallic materials because a
large amount of plastic deformation introduced can eliminate
weak portions in microstructures that cause crack initiation
and propagation. In the as-HIPed specimens, such weak por-
tions are likely grain boundaries and pores: the grain bound-
aries are originally powder surfaces mostly contaminated
with gaseous impurities and act as sites for segregation and
precipitation of impurities. The specimens contained 0.5%
porosity. In view of the fact that the detrimental effects of
pores and grain boundary precipitation become less signiﬁ-
cant as the maximum size of such weak portions decreases,
plastic working after HIP may improve the ductility of the
alloy. In order to examine the effect of plastic working on
ductility of the alloy, specimens with 50 and 80% reduction
by cold rolling, followed by annealing at 1373 K for 3.6 ks
were tensile tested at 10 s
−1. High strain-rate tests generally
magnify difference in ductility between materials or mate-
rial conditions compared to low strain-rate tests [19]. The
result is shown in Fig. 12. It is obvious that 80% cold rolling
appreciably improves the tensile properties; but the effect is
not very signiﬁcant even at 10 s

from 590 to 630 MPa, the ultimate tensile stress from 670 to

In the present fabrication processes, it is very likely that all
of the solute oxygen and nitrogen were consumed by forma-
tion of very ﬁne dispersoids of the yttrium compounds. The
vanadium matrix is hence almost free from solute oxygen and
nitrogen, and the weak portions associated with the intersti-
tials can be thus considered to be eliminated in the alloy. The
observed less signiﬁcant effect of cold rolling on the tensile
properties as shown inFig. 12 indicates that the matrix is suf-
ﬁciently ductile as a result of removal of the interstitials and
the existing pores are not very inﬂuential as crack nucleation
sites.
On the other hand, the as-HIPed specimen annealed at
1573 K exhibited the highest reduction in area, whereas it
showed less total elongation of 11% and no uniform elonga-

effects of grain-size strengthening and dispersion strengthen-

4. Conclusions
An alloy of V–1.7 mass% Y having a desirable microstruc-
ture of an ultra-ﬁne grained pure vanadium matrix with very
ﬁne dispersoids of Y
2O3 and YN and superior ductility and
high strength was successfully fabricated by powder met-
allurgical methods utilizing mechanical alloying and HIP
processes. For specimens subjected to post HIP annealing
at 1273–1673 K for 3.6 ks, XRD analysis, TEM observations
and Vickers microhardness measurements were made. 3PB
impact tests were conducted at 77–290 K at 5 ms
−1 and ten-
sile tests at room temperature at 1× 10−3 and 1× 10 s−1. The
effect of cold rolling on tensile properties was also examined.
The main results obtained are as follows:
(1) The alloy has average grain sizes ranging from 330 to
750 nm, Y
2O3 and YN particle sizes from 7 to 23 nm,
depending on annealing temperature. This indicates that
the microstructure of the alloy is stable against high-
temperature heating.
(2) 3PB impact tests showed that the annealed specimens
exhibit almost full bending without break even at 77 K.
(3) Even in the as-HIPed condition (no plastic working), the
specimen annealed at 1373 K exhibits high strength and
superior ductility. The yield strength is 560 MPa, the ulti-
mate tensile strength is 650 MPa, the total elongation is
16%, the uniform elongation is 8% and the reduction of
area is 62% when tested at 1 × 10
−3 s−1.
(4) These results indicate that the microstructure of the alloy
in the as-HIPed condition is almost free from weak por-
tions associated with interstitial impurities that cause
signiﬁcant loss of ductility. This, in turn, conﬁrms that the
application of the present fabrication technique enables
to develop vanadium alloys that have not been success-
fully fabricated due to difﬁculty in plastic working of
ingots required to break up the cast structure.
(5) Eighty percent cold rolling slightly improves the tensile
properties of the 1373 K annealed specimen. The yield
stress is increased from 590 to 630 MPa, the ultimate
tensile stress from 670 to 720 MPa, the total elongation
from 17 to 19%, the uniform elongation from 9.0 to 9.8%
and the reduction of area from 58 to 74% when tested at
290 K and 10 s
−1.
