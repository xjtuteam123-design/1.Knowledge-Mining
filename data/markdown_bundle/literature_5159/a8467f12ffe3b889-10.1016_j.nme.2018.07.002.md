ABSTRACT
Tungsten is considered as the most promising material for plasma facing components (PFCs) in the magnetic
conﬁnement fusion devices, due to its high melting temperature, high thermal conductivity, low swelling, low
tritium retention and low sputtering yield. However, the brittleness, poor machinability and low strength at
high-temperatures of tungsten limits its application. Focusing on this issue, various W alloys with enhanced
mechanical properties have been developed over recent decades. Among them, the W-ZrC alloys exhibit high
strength, high ductility, low ductile-to-brittle transition temperature, good high-temperature stability, excellent
resistance to thermal shock and low H/He plasma etching, making it one of the most promising candidate plasma
facing materials (PFMs) for the future fusion devices. Therefore, the R&D experience of W-ZrC materials for
PFCs, including the design idea, optimization of composition, fabrication technology (from powder metallurgy
processing to hot working, from small specimen to large-sized bulk material), microstructure and performance
(mechanical properties, thermal conductivity, resistance to thermal shocks and to H/He irradiation, H isotope
retention behaviors) were reviewed in this paper.
1. Introduction
The technological challenges in the peaceful use of fusion energy are
closely linked to the development of high-performance materials cap-
able of withstanding the extreme conditions including severe thermal
loads (up to 20 MWm −2), high-energy neutrons (14.1 MeV) bom-
bardments and high ﬂux plasma erosion in magnetic con ﬁnement fu-
sion reactors [1,2]. Meanwhile, the deuterium-tritium fusion reaction
produces high level of transmutant solutes such as H and He in struc-
tural materials that aggravate the radiation-induced degradation of
materials properties [3]. The tolerable peak power load on highly
loaded plasma-facing components (PFCs), like the divertor targets, re-
presents a key constraint on the design of a future fusion reactor such as
demonstration (DEMO) reactor or China fusion engineering test reactor
(CFETR) [4]. A fundamental improvement of the performance of such
components can only be achieved by developing advanced materials
with improved properties.
Tungsten (W) is considered as one of the most promising candidates
for various fusion applications due to its supreme properties such as
high melting temperature, high thermal conductivity, good erosion
resistance, low vapor pressure, low swelling and low tritium retention
[1,5,6]. However, in contrast to these excellent properties, the main
disadvantage of W is its brittleness in several regimes, i.e., low-tem-
perature embrittlement (relatively high ductile-to-brittle transition
temperature (DBTT) of 200– 400 °C), recrystallization embrittlement
(recrystallization temperature ∼1200 °C) and radiation-induced em-
brittlement [7,8]. The low ductility and poor formability at tempera-
tures below DBTT limit the engineering application of W alloys, parti-
cularly, in the nuclear energy ﬁelds the low-temperature embrittlement
and radiation-induced embrittlement are the main concerns [3,9,10].
The thermal shock resistance of material is also intimately linked to the
strength and DBTT, because the cracks would initiate when the thermal
stress is larger than the ultimate strength of material at temperatures
above DBTT or than the yield strength at temperatures below DBTT
[11–14]. Therefore, tungsten materials with excellent mechanical
properties, high-temperature stability and irradiation resistance would
be highly desirable for PFCs in fusion reactors.
For tungsten alloys, improving the low temperature ductility has
been a major goal over recent decades. Several approaches are em-
ployed to increase ductility and fracture toughness and to decrease the
DBTT. The addition of Re as a solute element in tungsten is e ﬀective in
improving the ductility and increasing the recrystallization temperature

costly owing to the expensive Re [16]. It is generally understood that a
major cause of the low ductility of tungsten materials is their weak
grain boundaries (GBs), due to the segregation of interstitial impurities,
such as O and N, at GBs [17,18]. In this sense methods that modify
impurity distribution at GBs have in ﬂuences on the strength and duc-
tility of tungsten. By reducing the interstitial impurities via vacuum and
zone melting to fabricate W with high purity, the DBTT can be de-
creased to about 200 °C. However pure tungsten undergoes re-
crystallization at relatively low temperature ( ∼1200 °C) and the
strength decreases dramatically at temperatures above 1000 °C [19,20].
Trace active elements such as Zr, Ti, Y, in tungsten can react with
oxygen and diminish the in ﬂuence of atomic oxygen on GBs via forming
thermally stable refractory oxide particles instead of low-melting oxide
layers, and thus purify and strengthen the GBs [21–23].
Tungsten materials with high strength and high thermal stability
can be obtained by the dispersion of thermally stable ﬁne second-phase
particles, which are usually oxides (e.g. La
2O3,Y 2O3) or carbides (e. g.
TiC, ZrC, HfC), forming the so called oxide or carbide dispersion-
strengthened (ODS or CDS) materials [24–32]. The ﬁne particles can
reﬁne the grains by hindering the motion of dislocations and GBs, and
thus improve the strength, recrystallization temperature and creep re-
sistance. In addition, the dispersion of nanoscale particles provides a
large amount of phase interfaces that could act as sinks for irradiation-
induced point defects, and thus has potential in improving the irra-
diation resistance [33]. Recently, various ODS-W or CDS-W materials
with enhanced strength and thermal stability were fabricated and in-
vestigated for fusion applications [3,19]. For examples: W-La
2O3 and
W-Y2O3 showed enhanced strength, high recrystallization temperatures
and high thermal shock resistance [3,27,28,34]. However, they still
exhibits poor low-temperature ductility and higher DBTT, which can be
mainly attributed to that the oxide particles tend to segregate and grow
into coarser particles at GBs, and thus introduce stress concentration at
the GBs and lead to weak GBs and poor toughness [19,22,35,36].I n
order to inhibit the segregation and coarsening of oxide particles at
GBs, some wet-chemical synthesis routes o ﬀering molecular-level
mixing were developed, and have shown the potential to fabricate
nano-structured ODS-W [27,36–38]. As compared with the oxides
strengthening phases, the carbides such as TiC, ZrC and HfC, have much
higher melting temperatures and better compatibility with tungsten due
to their similar physical or chemical properties, which may leads to
excellent comprehensive performance. Kurishita et al. reported that
ultraﬁne grained (UFG) W-1.1%TiC fabricated by severe plastic de-
formation exhibits very high fracture strength up to about 4.4 GPa and
appreciable bend ductility at room-temperature (RT) [24] and the UFG
W-0.3TiC shows better irradiation resistance than K-doped W and pure
W [24,39]. These favorable properties of W-TiC alloys imply the po-
tential of other carbide dispersion-strengthened tungsten materials with
ﬁne microstructures.
Among the strengthening phases, ZrC has very high melting tem-
perature ( ∼3540 °C), high hardness, high thermal stability, low neu-
tron-absorption cross section as well as good compatibility with tung-
sten, making it one of the most promising strengthening phases for
tungsten based materials. Recently, the nano-structured W-ZrC alloys
for PFCs application in fusion devices were developed by some Chinese
groups [14,29,30,40,41]. Among them, the W-0.5%ZrC alloy developed
by the Institute of Solid State Physics, Chinese Academy of Sciences
(ISSP, CAS) exhibits high strength, high ductility, high thermal con-
ductivity, low DBTT, excellent resistance to high thermal-loads and to
high ﬂux plasma etching as well as enhanced high-temperature stability
[30,42]. Therefore, this paper reviews the R&D experience of W-ZrC
materials as PFMs and the outline is: (1) design ideas, (2) fabrication
and optimization, (3) performance and microstructure. Note: In this
paper, all chemical compositions are given in weight percent (wt%) if
not explicitly mentioned.
2. Design ideas of W-ZrC alloys
As a candidate PFM, the main drawback of tungsten is its brittleness,
which causes poor machinability and low reliability. How to improve
the strength and ductility of tungsten is always a technical challenge.
It was clearly understood that the segregation of interstitial im-
purities, such as N and O at the GBs leads to poor cohesion strength of
GBs and intergranular fracture at low temperatures [17,22,35].
Therefore, the methods that can reduce the level of impurities segre-
gating at GBs should be e ﬀective in improving the toughness and
ductility of tungsten. Kurishita et al studied the e ﬀects of oxygen and
nitrogen impurity contents on the DBTT of tungsten, and found that the
DBTT increased drastically from 230 K to over 420 K when the oxygen
content increases from 160 wt ppm to 870 wt ppm [17]. Liu and Xie
et al [21,22] studied the e ﬀect of trace Zr on the mechanical properties
of W materials, and found that Zr could improve the low-temperature
ductility and decrease the DBTT of tungsten, because Zr could react
with impurity oxygen and form ZrO
2 (melting temperature 2715 °C)
particles, and thus diminish the embrittlement e ﬀect of oxygen on GBs.
Therefore, if the oxygen impurity can be diminished as much as pos-
sible, the ductility and strength of tungsten would be signi ﬁcantly im-
proved. In this sense, ZrC as an oxygen getter can react with oxygen to
form stable Zr –C–O or ZrO
2 particles at GBs, and thus is bene ﬁcial to
improving the cohesion strength of GBs and enhancing the low-tem-
perature ductility of tungsten.
In addition, ZrC has a high melting temperature of 3540 °C, which is
much higher than TiC (3160 °C), VC (2810 °C), and rare-earth oxides
such as La
2O3 (2215 °C) and Y 2O3 (2425 °C). Meanwhile, ZrC has good
compatibility with tungsten due to their similar physical or chemical
properties, and the lattice match of d
(110)W ≈ d(200)ZrC ≈ 0.221 nm
might introduce coherent interface between W matrix and ZrC phase,
which will signi ﬁcantly increase phase boundary (PB) cohesion. All
these advantages make ZrC one of the most promising strengthening
phase for tungsten especially for fusion applications.
For dispersion-strengthened materials, the size, number density and
distribution of strengthening phases are important factors a ﬀecting the
mechanical and thermal properties. It is well known that coarser par-
ticles tend to induce severe stress/strain concentration and initiate
cracks at the particle-matrix interfaces, and they are also more likely to
break because of their lower fracture strength [35]. Besides, the re-
duced number density in materials containing coarse particles also
leads to inferior strengthening and grain-re ﬁnement eﬀects. In contrast,
when the particles are ﬁne in size (nanoscale) and are homogeneously
dispersed in matrix especially in grains interior, they could improve the
strength by accumulating/ pinning dislocations and GBs without in-
itiating cracks at GBs, and thus lead to both high strength and ductility
[35]. In addition, theoretical modeling and experimental research
works showed that nano-grained or nano-structured materials may
have excellent irradiation resistance because the large amount of GBs or
phase-interfaces could act as sinks for irradiation-induced point defects
[25,33,43]. For example, the UFG W-TiC exhibits better irradiation
resistance to neutron and 3 MeV helium ions as compared with the
commercial pure W/R (Rolling), K-doped W/R (Rolling) and K-doped
W/SR (swaging + rolling) [25].
Basing on the above comparative analysis, nano-structured W-ZrC
materials with homogeneous dispersion of nanoscale ZrC particles were
proposed and expected to possess enhanced strength, ductility, high-
temperature stability, acceptable thermal conductivity and potential
resistance to neutrons and hydrogen/helium ions irradiation.

Here, the optimization of composition was done by testing the me-
chanical properties of the small specimen fabricated by mechanical
ball-milling and spark plasma sintering (SPS) technology. SPS provides
fast heating rate (up to 1000 °C/min) and short sintering time, and
allows the consolidation of powder materials to form dense ﬁne-grained
products at lower sintering temperature [21, 22]. To obtain W-ZrC with
ﬁne microstructures, submicron W powders (purity > 99.9% trace
metals basis) and nanoscale ZrC (20 –50 nm, purity > 99.99%) powders
were used as starting materials. The powders were ﬁrstly ball-milled in
a planetary ball mill in argon atmosphere using tungsten carbide balls
and mortars. The subsequent consolidation of the samples was carried
out using a SPS system (FCT Group, SE-607, Germany), and a multi-step
sintering program was developed to obtain tungsten materials with
both ﬁne grains and high relative density [21,22]. Fig. 1 shows the
schematic diagram of SPS and the typical temperature and pressure
proﬁle of the multi-step SPS process [44]. Fig. 2a shows the small
specimens fabricated by SPS. Dense (relative density >97.4%) W-(0,
0.2, 0.5, 1.0) %ZrC specimens with ﬁne grains (average grain size
ranges from 2.7 µm to 4.2 µm) were fabricated [45].
The e ﬀects of ZrC content on the mechanical properties of W-ZrC
alloys were investigated through tensile tests performed at various
temperatures using small dog-bone-shaped specimens with a cross-
section of 1.5 × 0.75 mm
2 and a working length of 5 mm. Fig. 2b
showed the typical engineering stress-strain curves of SPS W and SPS
W-(0.2, 0.5, 1.0) % ZrC at 700 °C [45]. Signiﬁcant enhancement in both
strength and ductility were achieved by addition of a small amount of
ZrC nano-particles into tungsten. The ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of
SPS pure W and W-(0.2, 0.5, 1.0) % ZrC at 700 °C are 337 MPa,
419 MPa, 535 MPa and 749 MPa, respectively. The tensile strength in-
creases signiﬁcantly with the elevating ZrC content from 0.2% to 1.0%,
noting that the strength of W-0.5%ZrC (W-0.5ZrC) is over 50% higher
than that of pure W. Meanwhile, the ductility could also be e ﬀectively
improved by the addition of a small amount of ZrC. The total elongation
(TE) of SPS W-(0.2, 0.5, 1.0) % ZrC is 30.4%, 24.8% and 10.4%, re-
spectively. Among these SPS W-ZrC specimens, the W-0.2ZrC exhibits
the highest elongation at 700 °C, but its strength is lower than that of
W-0.5ZrC [45]. The SPS W-1.0ZrC has the highest strength, but its
ductility is relatively low (only 10.4%). Therefore, the W-0.5ZrC with
both high strength and high ductility becomes the most promising one
for fabricating high performance PFMs.
Fan et al. [29,40] fabricated W-ZrC materials by high temperature
sintering in hydrogen atmosphere using W-ZrC composite powders
prepared by sol-heterogeneous precipitation-spray drying-thermal re-
duction and investigated the in ﬂuence of ZrC content (from 1% to 4%)
on the mechanical properties and microstructure of W alloys. The re-
lative density of their W-(0, 1, 2, 3, 4)%ZrC samples is in the range of
98.5–99.7%. They also found that the addition of ZrC could re ﬁne
grains and improve the strength of tungsten ( Fig. 3). The grain size of
W-3%ZrC (10∼15 µm) is much smaller than that of pure W ( ∼100 µm),
and the ZrC particles are micron-sized and most of them are distributed
at grain boundaries. With the increasing ZrC content from 1% to 3%,
the grain size decreases while the strength increases.

3.2. Fabrication of bulk W-ZrC alloys
The W-0.5ZrC fabricated by SPS methods showed higher strength,
better ductility and lower DBTT as compared with some other SPS
tungsten materials. However, the relative density (about 98%) and the
size of specimens (normally several centimeters in diameter) is in-
adequate for engineering applications. Therefore, bulk tungsten mate-
rials with large size and enhanced mechanical properties are required
for fusion applications. It is well known that high-temperature plastic
deformation (e.g. swaging, forging, rolling) is a very e ﬀective method
to improve the relative density and low-temperature ductility of tung-
sten materials, and more importantly, is suitable for large scale pro-
duction. Therefore, basing on the composition optimization of W-ZrC
alloys, W-0.5ZrC was selected for scale-up to produce bulk tungsten
rods, sheets or plates, as shown in Fig. 7.
Firstly, W-0.5ZrC rods of about 1 kg were fabricated by powder
metallurgy method including powders ball-milling, cold iso-static
pressing, high-temperature sintering in ﬂowing dry hydrogen and
subsequent rotary swaging, as shown in Fig. 8a. The relative density of
the swaged W-0.5ZrC rods is about 99.4%, which is much higher than
those of SPS W-ZrC (about 98%). The swaged W-0.5ZrC rods were cut
into dog-bone-shaped specimens along the longitudinal direction for
tensile tests. Fig. 8c shows the typical engineering stress-strain curves of
the swaged W-0.5ZrC at di ﬀerent temperatures. As compared with SPS
W-0.5ZrC, the swaged W-0.5ZrC exhibits signi ﬁcantly improvement in
low-temperature ductility and tensile strength. The swaged W-0.5ZrC
rod is brittle at 150 °C but shows plastic deformation at 200 °C. The TE
of swaged W-0.5ZrC at 200 °C and 250 °C is 4.0% and 12.6%, respec-
tively. These results suggest that the DBTT of swaged W-0.5ZrC is about
200 °C, which is much lower than that of SPS W-0.5ZrC (500 –600 °C).
Besides, the DBTT of the swaged W-1.0Y
2O3 (200–300 °C) and swaged
W-0.2Zr-1.0Y2O3 (150–200 °C) are also signi ﬁcantly decreased as
compared with the SPSed ones [18,22,46]. The enhanced low-tem-
perature ductility of tungsten can be attributed to the higher relative
density and the microstructure formed during the hot swaging process,
in which tungsten grains are elongated in longitudinal direction
(Fig. 8b).
Meanwhile, the swaged W-0.5ZrC shows both higher strength and
better ductility than those of the swaged W-1.0Y 2O3 and swaged W-
0.2Zr-1.0Y2O3 (Fig. 9) [18,46]. The UTS of the swaged W-0.5ZrC at
500 °C is about 550 MPa, which is higher than those of the swaged W-
1.0Y2O3 (489 MPa) and swaged W-0.2Zr-1.0Y 2O3 (504 MPa), espe-
cially, the TE of the swaged W-0.5ZrC is signi ﬁcantly higher than those
of the swaged W-1.0Y 2O3 and swaged W-0.2Zr-1.0Y 2O3 [18,46]. At low
temperatures, the swaged W-0.5ZrC also exhibits superior strength and
ductility. These results further verify that ZrC nanoparticles as
strengthening phase are very promising for improving the mechanical
properties of tungsten.
As well know, hot-rolling is also a good kind of thermomechanical
route for producing bulk plate metals, which may be more suitable for
industrial production. A W-0.5ZrC sheet with 1.0 mm thickness was
fabricated through multistep hot and cold rolling from original thick-
ness of about 18 mm [47]. The relative density of the W-0.5ZrC sheet is
99.8%. The tensile tests were performed along rolling direction (RD).
Fig. 10 shows the optical image and the engineering stress-strain curves
of the rolled W-0.5ZrC sheet [47]. The W-0.5ZrC sheet exhibits very
little plastic deformation and a fracture strength of 1100 MPa at 50 °C.
At 80 °C, the UTS is as high as 1413 MPa and the TE reaches 4.2%. The
DBTT of the rolled W-0.5ZrC sheet determined by tensile test is in the
range of 50 °C –80 °C, which is remarkably lower than those of swaged
W-0.5ZrC and SPS W-0.5ZrC. Although the strength decreases with the
elevating temperatures, the tensile strength at 500 °C is still higher than
900 MPa. As compared with the swaged W-0.5ZrC, the rolled W-0.5ZrC
sheet has both higher strength and low-temperature ductility. These
results indicate that hot-rolling is very e ﬀective in enhancing the me-
chanical properties of tungsten materials. Nevertheless, the thickness of
the rolled W-0.5ZrC sheet is inadequate for PFMs and the ductility at
high temperatures is relatively low.

4.2. Microstructure of W-ZrC plates
It is well known that the performance of materials is closely related
to composition and microstructure. High magni ﬁcation BSE-SEM ana-
lysis of the rolled W-0.5ZrC (RD –ND) shows that tungsten grains are
equiaxed and have an average grain size of 1.03 µm ( Fig. 13 a, b). The
reﬁned grain could be attributed to the dynamic recrystallization of W-
ZrC alloy during the hot-rolling process. For the nanoparticles in the
rolled W-ZrC, most of them disperse in tungsten grains interior
(Fig. 13a, e), and a small fraction of nanoparticles segregate at the GBs.
The intragranular particles can generate, pin down and accumulate
dislocations within the grains, as indicated in Fig. 13 e, and thus ef-
fectively enhance the strength and simultaneously improve the ductility
of alloys [35]. In addition, some particles tightly bounding to the GBs
could inhibit GBs sliding, as indicated by the open arrows in Fig. 13a, f.
The size distributions of the particles shown in Fig. 13c–d, indicate that
the second-phase particles (ZrC) locating in W grains have an average
size of 51 nm (with total area fraction of 79%). The small particles at
GBs are dominantly ZrC while the large particles are Zr –C–O particles.
The ZrC particles at GBs have an average particle size of 60 nm ranging
from 40 to 200 nm, while Zr –C–O particles at GBs have a larger average
particle size of 385 nm ranging from 250 to 400 nm.
The interface structure between the W matrix and second phase
particles in W-ZrC alloy were investigated by high-resolution
transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) ( Fig. 14). Fig. 14a –b show
the typical HRTEM lattice image and SAED pattern of an intragranular
particle with diameter of about 50 nm. The SAED result and the cor-
responding EDS results reveal that the particle is ZrC with a face cen-
tered cubic (fcc) structure. In addition, the particle-matrix phase
boundary has a similar di ﬀraction pattern with W-ZrC and ZrC
(Fig. 14 c–d). Fig. 14e exhibits perfect coherent structure interface be-
tween W matrix and ZrC particle. This kind of coherent structure always
exists in all the particle-matrix phase boundaries in W-ZrC combination
including both intragranular ( Fig. 14a) and intergranular ( Fig. 14f) ZrC
particles in W-ZrC alloy. It is well known that the coherent or semi-
coherent internal boundaries play an important role in strengthening
materials [50,64]. The coherent or semi-coherent interfaces between W
matrix and the intragranular or intergranular ZrC dispersoids lead to
good ductility/strength of W-ZrC alloy. The ZrC nanoparticles in the
grain interior could pin and accumulate dislocations, and thus e ﬀec-
tively improve the strength of the alloy without causing stress con-
centration at GBs. Moreover, there is a channel for the gliding of dis-
locations along coherent structure between particle and matrix
interfaces [64], which would help sustain work hardening and uniform
elongation and thus enhance the ability to accommodate plastic de-
formation.
The enhanced mechanical properties of the rolled W-0.5ZrC plate
can be mainly attributed to the following aspects. Firstly, homo-
geneously dispersed nanoscale particles could pin and accumulate dis-
locations
and thus improve the strength. Secondly, ZrC could capture
impurity oxygen and thus purify and strengthen GBs, and the nano-
particles such as Zr –C–O or ZrO
2 particles could pin GBs and re ﬁne
grains leading to improved strength and high-temperature properties.
Thirdly, coherent phase interface and the good compatibility of ZrC
particles with W matrix help sustain work hardening and uniform
elongation. Therefore, the synergistic strengthening e ﬀects of the above
factors lead to the excellent strength and ductility of W-ZrC alloy plate.
The coherent interface structure comes from the good compatibility of
ZrC phase with tungsten matrix and the adjustable lattice constant by
forming a solid solution with W and non-stoichiometric ZrC
x. This
eliminates the stress concentrations on the interfaces (including PBs
and GBs), alleviates the propensity for intergranular fracture, and in-
creases the total elongation before failure. These results emphasize
again that in W alloys the phase boundaries and grain boundaries
should be carefully tailored to reach high strength and ductility.

desorption spectrum (TDS) analysis.
The D plasma irradiation was carried out in the linear plasma device
STEP at Beihang University, China [75]. Rolled pure W and W-0.5ZrC
were exposed to low-energy high ﬂux deuterium plasma under the same
condition (D
+ energy∼ 90 eV, ﬂux ∼5×1 0 21 ions/m2s, ﬂu-
ence∼7.02 × 1025 ions/m2, temperature 400 K), as described in
Section 4.4 . Fig. 22 shows the TDS results of the tungsten samples ir-
radiated by D plasma. It can be seen that the rolled W-0.5ZrC exhibits
much lower hydrogen retention than pure W. This result indicates that
the hydrogen retention in W-ZrC is not increased although they con-
tains Zr element, because the Zr element exists in the form of stable
carbide or oxide particles, whose properties are signi ﬁcantly di ﬀerent
from that of the Zr metal. Both the rolled W-0.5ZrC and pure W showed
a desorption peak at temperature around 560 °C, and the peak of W-
0.5ZrC is much lower than that of pure W. The lower retention in the
rolled W-0.5ZrC may be due to the multi-scale interfacial micro-
structure with homogeneously dispersed nanoscale particles, mother
grain, and ultra- ﬁne sub-grain ( Fig. 23) [14], because GBs could act as
rapid diﬀusion paths for hydrogen [77]. Therefore, the hydrogen could
easily diﬀuse to the surface of sample even at low temperatures. A large
fraction of the D may escape from the materials during the D plasma
irradiation process at 400 K, and lead to low retention of D in material.
The low hydrogen retention behavior of the rolled W-ZrC may also
relate to its superior irradiation resistance to D plasma ( Figs. 18 and
20). In addition, Oyda et al. [78] found that D retention in W-TaC and
W-TiC is higher than that of pure W. This may be resulted from their
diﬀerent microstructures.

5. Summary and outlook
During the recent decades, the improvements in di ﬀerent aspects of
tungsten have been enriching the knowledgebase and help to show the
way to high performance tungsten materials for fusion applications.
Bulk W-ZrC alloys with excellent mechanical properties, high thermal
shock resistance, good high-temperature stability and excellent re-
sistance to low-energy high ﬂux plasma irradiation were successfully
developed through powder metallurgy process including ball-milling,
cold iso-static pressing, sintering, hot-rolling and stress relief annealing.
These outstanding properties of W-ZrC make it one of the most pro-
mising materials for PFCs and the design ideas and fabrication tech-
nology of W-ZrC materials could also be used for developing other types
of high performance tungsten materials. Nevertheless, to satisfy the
requirements of PFCs, the performance of current tungsten materials
needs to be further improved, which may be achieved through several
approaches as follows. Firstly, optimize the microstructure including
the size and shape of tungsten grain and the size, number density and
distribution of the second-phase particles in tungsten materials. The ZrC
particles in W-ZrC alloy have an average particle size of about 50 nm,
while some intergranular particles are as large as 300 ∼400 nm in
diameter. If the second-phase particles could be re ﬁned, e.g., to 10 nm
or even smaller, the mechanical properties and resistance to thermal
loads and irradiation might be further improved due to the increased
particle-matrix interfaces. Secondly, to improve the low-temperature
ductility and decrease the DBTT, the content of interstitial impurities
(i.e, O, N) should be as low as possible, for example, by using ultra high
purity tungsten powders and by processing in inert or reductive at-
mosphere. Thirdly, the hot working process could be further optimized
to obtain tungsten materials with optimal microstructure and proper-
ties.
In addition, most of the properties of W-ZrC alloys were evaluated
under highly simpli ﬁed conditions, which are inadequate to reveal the
actual scenarios in fusion reactors. For the materials used in fusion
reactors, the synergistic e ﬀects of multi-loadings including high thermal
loads, high ﬂux H/He plasma etching and neutron irradiation may lead
to much more severe degradation of properties. For example, He ion
implantation causes nanoscale bubble layers, which also deteriorate
mechanical and thermal properties. Therefore, to evaluate the perfor-
mance of the tungsten materials more accurately, the synergistic e ﬀects
of high heat loads, low-energy high ﬂux plasma etching and neutron
irradiation as well as transmutation elements on the microstructures
and properties of tungsten materials need to be systematically in-
vestigated in the near future and the corresponding database for the
performance of W-ZrC as well as other tungsten materials should also
be established.
