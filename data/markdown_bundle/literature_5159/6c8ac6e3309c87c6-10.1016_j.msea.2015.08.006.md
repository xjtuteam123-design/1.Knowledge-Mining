abstract
Four Nb silicide based ultrahigh temperature alloys with compositions of Nb –22Ti–16Si–3Cr–3Al– 2B– xHf
(x¼0, 2, 4 and 8) (at%) were prepared by vacuum non-consumable arc melting. The effects of Hf content
on the phase selection, microstructure, room-temperature fracture toughness, compressive performance
and isothermal oxidation behavior at 1250 °C of the alloys have been investigated. The results show that
the formation of α(Nb,X)5Si3 is suppressed and instead the formation of γ(Nb,X)5Si3 is promoted by Hf
addition. The Hf addition (especially 8 at%) improves the room-temperature fracture toughness of the
alloy. The hardness of
γ(Nb,X)5Si3 increases with its Hf concentration. The compression strength of the
alloy is the highest at 4 at% Hf, and then obviously reduces at 8 at% Hf due to embrittlement of the alloy
(especially that of γ(Nb,X)5Si3). The oxidation resistance of the alloy has been ﬁrstly ameliorated by the
lower Hf addition (2 at%) and then degraded by the higher Hf addition (especially 8 at%). The details have
been discussed.

1. Introduction
Development of aero-technology promotes higher require-
ments for turbine blade materials of aeroengine, such as good
fracture toughness and ductility at room temperature, exceptional
endurance strength, creep and oxidation resistance at elevated
temperatures [1,2]. The operating temperatures of the most ad-
vanced Ni based single crystal superalloys are essentially close to
their limits (about 1 150 °C, i.e. 85% of their melting temperatures)
[3]. Therefore, new high-temperature structural materials that can
survive above 1200 °C for a long time are desired. Because of their
high melting temperatures (above 1750 °C) and low densities
(6.6–7.2 g/cm
3), Nb silicide based ultrahigh temperature alloys
possessing microstructures consisting of Nb solid solution (Nbss)
and silicides ((Nb,X)
5Si3 or (Nb,X) 3Si, “X” represents the elements
substituting for Nb such as Ti, Hf and Cr etc.), are considered as
potential candidates for turbine blade materials [4,5], exhibiting
good balance of mechanical properties at room and high tem-
peratures. Furthermore, (Nb,X) 5Si3 possesses three crystal struc-
tures, i.e. α(Nb,X)5Si3 (tetragonal D8 l structure, a¼ b¼6.57 Å and
c¼ 1 1.88 Å),β(Nb,X)5Si3 (tetragonal D8m structure, a¼b ¼10.018 Å
and c ¼5.072 Å) and γ(Nb,X)5Si3 (hexagonal D8 8 structure,
a¼b¼7.536 Å and c ¼5.249 Å). However, poor oxidation resistance
is a major barrier to their high-temperature applications in the
ﬁeld of aerospace [1,2]. In order to get an ameliorated balance
between mechanical properties and oxidation resistance, alloying,
especially multi-alloying has been employed in Nb silicide based
ultrahigh temperature alloys.
The additions of Ti, Cr and Al in Nb silicide based ultrahigh
temperature alloys are generally considered to be very effective to
ameliorate the oxidation resistance [6–10]. However, the higher
additions of these elements would be detrimental to their me-
chanical properties [1 1–13]. The element B can ameliorate the
oxidation resistance of the alloys through the formation of a
continuous and dense scale [14–17]. Also, minor additions of B
improve both the room-temperature fracture toughness and high-
temperature strength of the alloys [18,19]. In addition, based on
the results of our previous researches with regard to alloying, di-
rectional solidi ﬁcation and phase diagram calculation etc. of Nb
silicide based ultrahigh temperature alloys [9,15–17, 20–22],a
multi-component Nb –22Ti–16Si–3Cr–3Al–2B (at%) base alloy has
been selected in this study, consequently.
Alloying with Hf has recently received considerable attention.
Hf as a reactive element can enhance the adherence of the scale
[23]. Some researches have pointed out that for Nb silicide based
ultrahigh temperature alloys, the addition of Hf has no signi ﬁcant
inﬂuence on the oxidation resistance at intermediate tempera-
tures while has obvious improvement at elevated temperatures
[24,25]. Also, it has been shown that the addition of Hf obviously
degrades the oxidation resistance due to the embrittlement of the
scale, especially in the absence of Cr [26]. Hf ameliorates the room-

2. Experimental procedures
Four button ingots with 62 mm in diameter for its upper ﬂat
roof of Nb silicide based ultrahigh temperature alloys, each
weighing about 200 g, were prepared using vacuum non-con-
sumable arc melting furnace equipped with an electromagnetic
stirring device, and each ingot was remelted for six times in order
to ensure compositional homogeneity. The single edge notched
three-point-bending specimen for room-temperature fracture
toughness ( KQ) measurement with the dimensions of
30 mm /C2 5m m /C2 2.5 mm was prepared by electro-discharge ma-
chining (EDM), and the notch is with the depth of 2.5 70.1 mm.
The KQ value was measured on an electronic universal mechanical
testing machine (Instron-3382). Three specimens were tested and
the average KQ value was employed for each specimen condition.
The Vickers microhardness (Hv) of the microstructures was
measured using an HXP-1000TM hardness machine with a load of
0.98 N. The microhardness value was the average of at least 5 in-
dentations. The compressive tests at 1250 °C were conducted in an
argon atmosphere at a strain rate of 5 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1 using a Gleeble
3500 testing machine. The temperature rising rate was 3 °C/s and
the specimens were held for 3 min at the testing temperature,
prior to testing, in order to homogenize the temperature
throughout the heating area. The compressive cylinders were
6 mm in diameter and 9 mm in length.
The specimens used for oxidation tests were 8 mm /C2
8m m /C2 8 mm cubes cut from the ingots by EDM. After the six
surfaces being ground, the actual dimensions of each specimen
were measured using a vernier caliper and its surface area was
calculated before oxidation tests. The isothermal oxidation beha-
vior of the specimens was examined in an open-ended tube fur-
nace. After holding them at 1250 °C for 1 or 50 h, the specimens
were furnace-cooled down to room temperature. The mass chan-
ges of the specimens after oxidation tests were obtained using an
electronic balance with an accuracy of 0.0001 g.
X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD, Panalytical X'Pert Pro, Cu K α)
was used to identify the constituent phases in the specimens.
Microstructure of the specimens was observed by scanning elec-
tron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Supra-55), and the chemical compo-
sitions of their constituent phases were analyzed by an electron
probe microanalyzer (EPMA, JXA-8100) or energy dispersive
spectroscopy (EDS, Inca X-sight). The compound BN and high-
purity metals were used as standards for analyzing the
concentrations of elements in the specimens by EPMA. Both the
width and length of the analyzed phase were larger than 5 μm,
and also at least ﬁve analyses were performed for each phase.
Quantitative metallographic analysis of constituent phases was
performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 high resolution imaging
analysis software.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure of the alloys
Fig. 1 shows the XRD patterns of the four Nb silicide based
ultrahigh temperature alloys. It can be seen that the constituent
phases of 0Hf and 2Hf alloys are Nbss (PDF Card number, 01-1 183),
α(Nb,X)5Si3 (PDF Card number, 65-2781) and γ(Nb,X)5Si3 (PDF
Card number, 65-3599), while those of 4Hf and 8Hf alloys are Nbss
and γ(Nb,X)5Si3. There is no β(Nb,X)5Si3 and (Nb,X) 3Si detected in
the XRD patterns. It can also be seen from Fig. 1 that the diffraction
peaks from Nbss and γ(Nb,X)5Si3 gradually shift to the left with
increase in Hf content in the alloy, indicating their increased lattice
parameters caused by the solution of large Hf atoms in these two
phases. However, the diffraction peaks from α(Nb,X)5Si3 hardly
shift, as shown by the XRD patterns of 0Hf and 2Hf alloys.
Fig. 2 shows the back-scattered electron (BSE) images of the
four alloys and Table 1 presents the compositions of their con-
stituent phases. From the XRD, BSE and EPMA results, it can be
determined that the microstructure of 0Hf alloy is comprised of
primary α(Nb,X)5Si3 blocks, Nbss/ α(Nb,X)5Si3 and
Nbss/γ(Nb,X)5Si3 eutectics, as show in Fig. 2 a. The microstructure
of 2Hf alloy consists of primary α(Nb,X)5Si3 and γ(Nb,X)5Si3 blocks
together with Nbss/ α(Nb,X)5Si3 and Nbss/ γ(Nb,X)5Si3 eutectics, as
shown in Fig. 2b. The addition of 2 at% Hf suppresses the formation
of α(Nb,X)5Si3 whilst promotes the formation of γ(Nb,X)5Si3, since
the amounts of both primary α(Nb,X)5Si3 blocks and
Nbss/α(Nb,X)5Si3 eutectics signi ﬁcantly decrease, as shown by the
quantitative metallographic analysis results ( Table 2). It can also be
seen that α(Nb,X)5Si3 phase has lighter contrast than γ(Nb,X)5Si3
phase in 0Hf and 2Hf alloys. Notably, Nbss slices in the outer part
of eutectic colonies have progressed into Nbss dendrites, as shown
in Fig. 2 a and b. Despite becoming not so conspicuous, the similar
solidiﬁcation characteristics also exist in 4Hf and 8Hf alloys ( Fig. 2c
and d).
Both
α(Nb,X)5Si3

below) have higher Ti, Cr, Al and sometimes Hf concentrations
than primary α(Nb,X)5Si3 and γ(Nb,X)5Si3 in 0Hf or 2Hf alloy re-
spectively, and also the similar phenomenon is found in other two
alloys of this study, as shown in Table 1 . Moreover, the con-
centrations of these elements are obviously higher in γ(Nb,X)5Si3
than in α(Nb,X)5Si3 in 0Hf or 2Hf alloy ( Table 1).
With increase in Hf content in the alloy, the formation of
α(Nb,X)5Si3 is completely suppressed, and the microstructures of
4Hf and 8Hf alloys are both comprised of primary γ(Nb,X)5Si3,
Nbss/γ(Nb,X)5Si3 eutectic together with a very limited amount of
black regions (indicated by circles), as shown in Fig. 2 c and d. The

increase in Hf content in the alloy. A few ﬁne bright HfO 2 particles

Because of possessing typical metal –nonmetal eutectic char-

eutectic in 0Hf and 2Hf alloys, indicating that the growing rate of

However, with increase in Hf content in the alloy (4 and 8 at%), the
growth of
γ(Nb,X)5Si3 in the eutectic is signi ﬁcantly blocked, since

feature of γ(Nb,X)5Si3 in this eutectic along a particular direction is
remarkably enhanced with increase in Hf content in the alloy
(especially 8 at%).
During solidi ﬁcation of 4Hf and 8Hf alloys, γ(Nb,X)5Si3 nucle-

theory about eutectic solidi ﬁcation. Assuming that the growth of
some speci ﬁc crystal planes for γ(Nb,X)5Si3 is suppressed by the
higher addition of Hf, the Si-rich liquid regions would form near
these planes, which in turn promotes the nucleation and growth of
γ(Nb,X)5Si3. This illustrates that the 8Hf alloy has the ﬁnest
Nbss/γ(Nb,X)5Si3 eutectic structures, to some extent.

3.2.2. Microhardness
Table 4 presents the microhardness values of the micro-
structures in the four alloys. It can be seen that the primary
γ(Nb,X)5Si3 has a higher hardness than primary α(Nb,X)5Si3, and
the hardness values of both silicides increase with Hf content in
the alloy (the hardness of primary γ(Nb,X)5Si3 in 8Hf alloy even
approaches to 1530 Hv, 25.4% and 19.4% higher than that in 2Hf
and 4Hf alloys, respectively). Moreover, the hardness of eutectic
structures shows a similar variation tendency to that of primary
silicides with Hf content in the alloy ( Table 4 ). From the data in
Table 1 , it can be found that the Hf concentration in primary
γ(Nb,X)5Si3 or γ(Nb,X)5Si3
E obviously increases with Hf content in
the alloy (especially 8 at%), which should be responsible for their
increasing hardness. This can be interpreted as that solution of Hf
in silicides improves hardness by solid solution strengthening
[27,28] and also such strengthening is enhanced by its increased
concentration, which is in accordance with XRD results to some
extent, i.e. the diffraction peaks of γ(Nb,X)5Si3 gradually shift to
the left with increase in Hf content in the alloy ( Fig. 1 ), as dis-
cussed above.
3.2.3. High-temperature compressive behavior
Fig. 6a–d shows the macroscopic morphology of the specimens
of the four alloys upon compression at 1250 °C with a strain rate of
5 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1. It can be seen that the specimen of 0Hf alloy exhibits
relatively regular jar shape, but the specimens of 2Hf and 4Hf al-

the compression direction. This suggests that 0Hf alloy has good
plastic deformability while 2Hf and 4Hf alloys display somewhat
brittleness at 1250 °C. Attentively, such brittleness is signi ﬁcantly
enhanced by further increase in Hf content in the alloys since 8Hf
alloy is completely ruptured, exhibiting approximately a lotus
shape ( Fig. 6 d).

3.3. Oxidation behavior of the alloys
For the four alloys with different Hf contents, the scales formed
upon oxidation at 1250 °C for 1 or 50 h in the static air spalled out,
indicating their poor adherence. The mass gains per unit area of
these alloys after oxidation are relatively larger, as shown in Fig. 8.
It can also be seen that the mass gain per unit area exhibits a trend
of ﬁrstly reducing and then rising with increase in Hf content in
the alloy, regardless of oxidation time being 1 or 50 h. In other
words, a lower Hf addition ameliorates the oxidation resistance of
the alloy (the 2Hf alloy displays the lowest mass gains, i.e. 14.3 and
89.1 mg/cm
2 after oxidation for 1 and 50 h, respectively, having
the best oxidation-resistant performance in this study), while
higher Hf additions degrade it (particularly, the 8Hf alloy possesses
a mass gain per unit area relatively close to that of 0Hf alloy)
(Fig. 8 ).

then characterized by XRD analysis, as shown in Fig. 9 . It can be
found that the constituent phases in these scales do not change
and are mainly TiNb

On one hand, the dynamic-segregation theory proposed by Pint
et al. [30] indicates that the transportation speed of reactive metal
(such as Hf, Zr, Y, Ce and La etc.) is very slow due to their large
atomic radius, thus, in turn restraining the outward diffusion of
other metal atoms (such as Nb, Ti and Cr etc.). This would lead to
the growth of the scale to be chie ﬂy controlled by the inward dif-
fusion of oxygen ions. On the other hand, the Hf addition sup-
presses the formation of
α(Nb,X)5Si3 and promotes the formation of
γ(Nb,X)5Si3 instead. However, the latter phase has worse oxidation
resistance than the former phase, as discussed above. Moreover,
because of possessing relatively lower enthalpy of formation (about
/C0 1 128 kJ/mol) and higher stability, HfO2 does not easily react with
Nb and Ti oxides, even though it could participate to form amor-
phous silicates in the scale according to the studies by Lefez et al.
[29]. Thus, the numerous HfO 2 particles at the phase boundaries
probably give rise to severe embrittlement and cracking during
oxidation, further resulting in strong intergranular diffusion of
oxygen ions. This would be enhanced by a higher addition of Hf due
to more and larger HfO
2 particles (especially in 8Hf alloy). It can also
be understood that the existence of HfO 2 is prohibitive to the for-
mation of a continuous and compact scale.
These two aspects above dominate the oxidation process of the
alloy, depending on their competition with each other. This illustrates
that a lower addition of Hf (2 at%) slightly ameliorates the oxidation
resistance of the alloy by hindering the outward diffusion of some
metal ions of Nb and Ti etc., and a higher addition of Hf (especially
8 at%) does not further improve but degrades it due to the extremely
severe internal oxidation behavior. In this work, the oxidation rate of
the alloy is relatively high due to the absence of an effectively pro-
tective scale at such an elevated temperature of 1250 °C, allowing
oxygen to easily diffuse into the alloy substrate.
4. Conclusions
(1) The microstructures of the four Nb silicide based ultrahigh
temperature alloys with different Hf contents are composed of
primary (Nb,X)
5Si3 blocks together with one or two types of
eutectic structures. Hf addition suppresses the formation of
α(Nb,X)5Si3 but promotes the formation of γ(Nb,X)5Si3. More-
over, the silicide in the alloy with higher Hf addition (4 or 8 at%)
exists only in the form of γ(Nb,X)5Si3. The eutectic structure in
the alloy has been signi ﬁcantly re ﬁned by 8 at% Hf addition.
(2) The alloys rupture in a brittle quasi-cleavage mode and the
room-temperature fracture toughness increases with Hf addi-
tion (especially 8 at%). The hardness of α(Nb,X)5Si3 is lower
than that of γ(Nb,X)5Si3. Moreover, the hardness of γ(Nb,X)5Si3
shows a rising trend with increase in its Hf concentration. A
lower Hf addition (2 and 4 at%) increases while a higher Hf
addition (8 at%) obviously decreases the compression strength
of the alloy at 1250 °C.
