REVIEW
A Review on Manufacturing Pure Refractory Metals
by Selective Laser Melting
Biyao Liu, Peilei Zhang , Hua Yan, Qinghua Lu, Haichuan Shi, Zhenyu Liu, Di Wu, Tianzhu Sun, Ruifeng Li, and Qingzhao Wang
Submitted: 26 September 2023 / Revised: 1 December 2023 / Accepted: 3 April 2024 / Published online: 10 June 2024
Refractory metals have increasingly attracted the attention of researchers due to their excellent high-
temperature strength, thermal conductivity, radiation resistance, and biocompatibility for applications in
extreme environments such as aerospace and nuclear industries. However, beyond traditional manufac-
turing processes, the complex post-treatment process, high cost, and difﬁculty in manufacturing complex
geometry components limit its further application in modern industry. Compared to conventional manu-
facturing processes, selective laser melting (SLM) technology, an emerging technology, can signiﬁcantly
simplify the production process and has the advantage of manufacturing parts with complex geometry.
Therefore, there is increasing research on manufacturing refractory metals using SLM technology. This
article describes the current research progress of pure refractory metals manufactured by SLM regarding
the preparation process, microstructure, metallurgical defects, and mechanical properties of refractory
metals (tungsten, molybdenum, niobium, tantalum, etc.). Generally speaking, there are some technical
difﬁculties in the fabrication of refractory alloys by SLM, summarized in this paper. Finally, the article
presented the prospect of developing SLM refractory metals.
Keywords mechanical properties, metallurgical defects,
microstructures, refractory metals, selective laser
melting
1. Introduction
Refractory metals have a melting point above 1650 /C176 C and
have a body-centered cubic (BCC) or hexagonal close-packed
(HCP) structure. Examples of refractory metals include tung-
sten (W), titanium (Ti), tantalum (Ta), niobium (Nb), molyb-
denum (Mo), hafnium (Hf), zirconium (Zr), vanadium (V),
chromium (Cr), and rhenium (Re). These metals have unique
advantages such as high-temperature strength, biocompatibility,
low coefﬁcient of thermal expansion, superconductivity/heat,
and high density (Ref 1-3). These outstanding characteristics
allow refractory metals to be used in highly radioactive and
extreme environments (Ref 4), including the aerospace and
nuclear industries (Ref 5, 6). Refractory metal alloy with Cu-
Mo-Si (Ref 7), Ni-Ti-Si (Ref 8), Ni-Mo-Fe-Si (Ref 9), Ti-Zr-Nb
(Ref 10), etc. Due to the distinct beneﬁts offered by these
metals and alloys, the production of refractory metals has
captured the attention of numerous researchers. However, the
low-temperature brittleness, high strength, and high oxidation
sensitivity of refractory metals make it difﬁcult for traditional
metal manufacturing processes to meet the processing require-
ments of refractory metals and to manufacture complex
structural parts (Ref 11). These reasons make it difﬁcult for
refractory metals to be promoted and applied in industry.
Selective laser melting (SLM) is an emerging technology
that builds 3D objects by adding deposited thin layers of
material as directed by a digital model. SLM boasts a rapid
cooling rate (10
5-107 K/s), enabling customized production and
excelling in automation (Ref 12, 13). Simultaneously, SLM is a
highly efﬁcient integrated machining and manufacturing tech-
nology that ensures product quality and increases production
efﬁciency. Additionally, compared with traditional casting and
forging processes, additive manufacturing technology can
produce parts with very complex structures and high processing
accuracy (Ref 14, 15). Meanwhile, the laser-based technique
can efﬁciently handle even refractory metals due to the high
energy concentration of the laser beam (Ref 16). Thus, additive
manufacturing using SLM promises to transform the metals
industry, especially when traditional methods are insufﬁcient to
meet the processing conditions. SLM has emerged as a new
paradigm for creating and producing advanced components for
various ﬁelds, including aerospace, medical, energy, and
automotive sectors (Ref 17-21). Therefore, SLM additive
manufacturing techniques to prepare refractory metals have
shown great potential in industrial production. Nevertheless,
due to a range of issues like the high susceptibility to oxidation
in pure refractory metals and the performance limitations of
products manufactured using SLM, a substantial gap persists
between refractory metals prepared via additive manufacturing
technology and those produced on an industrial scale (Ref 22).

2. SLM Process of Refractory Pure Metals
2.1 SLM Manufacturing Process
SLM technology is an advanced manufacturing technology
that integrates the advantages of rapid prototyping manufac-
turing technology and laser cladding technology (Ref 23, 24).
The technology is based on the discrete/stacking principle. By
layering the 3D CAD model of the part and obtaining the 2D
contour information of the cross section of each layer, and then
generating the processing path, the metal powder is used as the
raw material and the high-energy laser is the energy source to
scan the pre-layered metal powder melting with the predeter-
mined processing path, rapidly solidify, and deposit layer by
layer to realize the direct manufacturing of metal parts (Ref 25-
27). Figure 1 displays the working principle of SLM.
SLM is a powder bed melting technology renowned for its
ability to create complex metal parts. By utilizing metal powder
as the forming material, SLM offers numerous advantages,
including its comprehensive functionality, short assembly
times, high material utilization, and cost savings (Ref 28-30).
Notably, SLM breaks free from the constraints of traditional
manufacturing processes, allowing for the production of
intricate structures that were once difﬁcult to achieve (Ref
31-33). This opens up new horizons in manufacturing possi-
bilities. With the precise focus of laser beams, SLM ensures
high precision and excellent mechanical properties in the
printed parts. Furthermore, the ﬂexibility and quick production
cycles of SLM processes enhance its competitiveness, provid-
ing a reliable solution for rapidly meeting market demands (Ref
34-38). However, SLM is not suitable for manufacturing large
monolithic parts due to the limitation of building container size
(Ref 39).
Due to these above-mentioned characteristics, SLM exhibits
signiﬁcant potential for industrial applications when compared
to traditional metal manufacturing technologies (such as
casting, forging, cutting and other methods). Steel, amorphous
alloys, high entropy alloys, and refractory metals have been
successfully prepared by SLM technology (Ref 40-45). How-
ever, commercially available SLM systems currently face
challenges in effectively processing refractory metals. These
metals possess strong mechanical properties, allowing them to
withstand high temperatures and exhibit exceptional corrosion
resistance. As a result, they are widely chosen for the
manufacturing of high-strength medical devices, rocket noz-
zles, and support hardware in the nuclear industry. Furthermore,
their radio-opaque properties make them highly suitable for
medical imaging devices. Thus, the application of SLM
technology to 3D print refractory metals is an area of active
research (Ref 46-50). The utilization of refractory metals is
poised to rise alongside the adoption of SLM processing. This
method effectively addresses processing hurdles, signiﬁcantly
reduces costs, and enhances affordability. Moreover, it presents
novel prospects for leveraging these distinctive alloys through
cellular structures featuring high-temperature refractory metal
properties. This innovation not only reduces weight but also
enables the creation of structures previously unattainable
through sintering or mechanical processing. In addition, since
refractory metals are mainly used for small parts such as
medical, weapons, and rocket nozzles, the disadvantage that
SLM cannot print large quantities of parts is irrelevant.

During the SLM process, the metal powder in the bed
absorbs the laser energy, leading to the generation of localized
heat. This heat triggers solid and liquid mechanisms, facilitating
the sintering of particles and ultimately fusing them (Ref 51).
Complex phenomena occur during the rapid melting and
solidiﬁcation process, such as laser energy absorption and
scattering, heat transfer, phase transformation, and melt ﬂow (as
shown in Fig. 2) (Ref 52, 53). The Marangoni effect is triggered
by the high-temperature gradient within the molten pool and
has a signiﬁcant effect on ﬂuid ﬂow, temperature ﬁeld
distribution, and molten pool growth behavior (Ref 54, 55).
Optimizing process parameters allows manipulation of the
thermodynamic and kinetic properties of the molten pool. This
can be achieved by adjusting the grain size, shape, phase
content, and composition during the solidiﬁcation process (Ref
53). Therefore, developing a suitable set of process parameters
is particularly important.
However, SLM printing of refractory metals in the industrial
and medical ﬁelds has not yet seen signiﬁcant adoption. The
primary reason for this is that the research process of SLM is
still in its nascent stages and requires further advancement. The
inherent characteristics of pure refractory metals, such as high
melting point, high thermal conductivity, high temperature, and
easy oxidation, make parts printed by SLM extremely prone to
defects, such as pores and cracks, resulting in low density.
Therefore, how to solve these problems has become the
research focus (Ref 56).
2.2 Pure Tungsten
Tungsten (W) metal boasts outstanding attributes, including
an exceptionally high melting point (3420 /C176 C), remarkable
density and hardness, minimal thermal expansion, exceptional
resistance to oxidation, and impressive electrical conductivity
(Ref 57, 58). W exhibits a low coefﬁcient of thermal expansion
and high strength at elevated temperatures, making it well
suited for applications in furnaces and high heat ﬂux compo-
nents. Due to the above characteristics, refractory metals W are
widely used in the military, electronics, medicine, aerospace,
nuclear industry, and other ﬁelds (Ref 59).
However, due to W’s extreme hardness and brittleness,
conventional forming methods for creating complex shapes
have proved challenging (Ref 60). As a consequence, the
escalating popularity of SLM printing technology stems from
its high energy density and precise machining capabilities,
contributing to its growing adoption. Table 1 summarizes the
development history of pure W prepared by SLM, mainly
summarizing the printing parameters and the generation of
cracks.
In 2012, Zhang et al. (Ref 61) used SLM to prepare nano-
crystalline W for the ﬁrst time. It was observed that W grains
tended to grow from the direction of entanglement to the
mutually perpendicular direction, and this was achieved with a
signiﬁcant amount of laser energy input. High-temperature
gradients and metal powder supercooling could lead to the
formation of crystalline structures. Optimal laser energy input
could enhance the process of liquid phase sintering. Their
experiments demonstrated the feasibility of using SLM to print
pure W. Nevertheless, their research employed polyhedral
powders known for susceptibility to rupture and spheroidiza-
tion during preparation, leading to the signiﬁcant formation of
large defects. To delve deeper into the impact of powder
morphology on formability, Wang et al. (Ref 62) conducted a
study in 2017. They compared the surface morphology of pure
W components crafted from spherical and polyhedral powders.
Successfully utilizing spherical powder, they achieved pure W
samples with an impressive density of up to 96%, as depicted in
Fig. 3(A). This marked the ﬁrst application of spherical powder.
It can be seen from Fig. 3(A), (a
3) and (a4) that the two powders
presented different formability after SLM preparation. In
Fig. 3(A), (a 3) for the sample printed with polyhedral powder,
many pores and cracks can be seen on the surface of the
sample. In contrast, a continuous trajectory can be formed when
a laser of the same power is applied to a spherical powder
(Fig. 3A, a 4). Additionally, their study also showed that the
exposure time required for spherical powders is less than that
for polyhedral powders, further conﬁrming that spherical
powders possess superior molding properties and laying the
foundation for subsequent research.
After this, many related studies using SLM to print pure W
poured out. Figure 3(B) shows the inﬂuence of laser energy
density, scanning speed, and hatch spacing on pure W print
density in the SLM process (Ref 63). The results show that the
density of W increases with the increase in energy density, but
it is inversely proportional to the hatch spacing and scanning
speed. Although the relationship between printing parameters
and density has been preliminarily explored, defects such as
pores and cracks still exist. In addition to the inﬂuence of
printing parameters, the formability of the material is also
related to the scanning strategy. Figure 3(C) shows the effective
elimination of cracks through remelting and rotary scanning
strategies (Ref 60). Among them, remelting can only change
the crack form, but cannot reduce the number of cracks. In
addition, the longitudinal cracks can be effectively suppressed
by using the rotational scanning strategy. However, the
calculated surface temperature difference of 7300 K signiﬁ-
cantly surpasses the cracking criterion for W, set at 800 K. This
stark contrast implies that in laser additive manufacturing, the
occurrence of W cracking is nearly unavoidable.

2.3 Pure Tantalum
In recent years, Ta has seen widespread adoption in medical
equipment, notably in orthopedics and dentistry, owing to its
remarkable biocompatibility, a response to the escalating
demand for medical applications. However, similar to the
problems in the industry, medical equipment has higher
requirements for product accuracy and structural complexity,
and traditional casting and powder metallurgy processes are
increasingly unable to meet market demand. Therefore, the
SLM printing process has gradually entered people’s vision.
Since the addition of material layer by layer in the SLM process
allows for a wide range of geometric freedom, highly complex
parts can be manufactured. One application involves the
integration of porous Ta bone scaffolds into orthopedic or
dental implants to enhance bone ingrowth (Ref 75).
The exploration of pure Ta mainly falls into two categories:
bulk dense structure and porous structure. The process of
preparing pure Ta from SLM was ﬁrst studied in Thijs’ article
in 2013, and anisotropy of yield strength due to crystal texture
was reported (Ref 75). In addition, the direction of cooling and
solidiﬁcation is determined by the local heat input. The
interaction of epitaxial growth, partial melting of the preceding
layer, various growth mechanisms, and Ta’s unique global heat
ﬂow direction throughout SLM resulted in elongated columnar
grains with a preferred crystal orientation along the < 111 >
axis aligned with the building direction (Fig. 7A). This proves
that SLM printing pure Ta parts is feasible and lays the

As times continue to evolve, the demands on material
performance also evolve. The primary focus of research has
become improving the density and mechanical properties of
pure Ta samples. In 2017, Zhou et al. (Ref 78) investigated the
effects of laser power, scanning speed, and attendant energy
density on pure Ta samples’ density, microstructure, and
mechanical properties. The density of the prepared pure Ta
samples could reach 96.92%, and the microhardness and tensile
strength of pure Ta increased from 120 and 310 to 445 HV and
739 MPa, respectively, compared with the powder metallurgi-
cal samples. It was proved that SLM-printed pure Ta sample
was possible for industrial application.
After 2019, many research reports have been on pure Ta
SLM additive manufacturing. Oh et al. (Ref 79) used SLM to
print almost completely dense pure Ta parts; its relative density
exceeded 99%, and it had a speciﬁc crystal orientation
of < 111 > ﬁber-like texture along the building direction. In
Guo et al. study, the results of SLM technology printing pure
porous Ta as a bone implant were presented, and the porous
structure not only has regular pore morphology and connectiv-
ity, but also has a controllable elastic model and compressive
strength (Ref 80). In 2020, Zhou et al. (Ref 16) annealed pure
Ta parts printed by SLM. They found that the elongation rate of
pure Ta parts after annealing and heat treatment could reach
12%, and the tensile strength could exceed 750 MPa. During
annealing, the columnar grains gradually spheroid and undergo
dislocation slippage, resulting in high tensile elongation. The
accumulation of dislocations and the precipitation of HCP
phases maintained the high-strength properties of the material

3. Performance of SLM Refractory Pure Metals
3.1 Density
In the SLM process, it is crucial to ensure thorough melting
and solidiﬁcation of the material to attain superior overall
properties and prevent issues like balling effects and structural
looseness. This pursuit is essential for achieving densiﬁcation.
Adequate melting enhances the adhesion between particles,
facilitates the fabrication of complex structural parts, and
promotes the discharge of gaseous impurities from the melt
(Ref 94). Therefore, the density of SLM printed parts directly
determines the parts’ most basic forming and performance. To
date, SLM has been well established for Fe-based, Ti-based,
Ni-based, and Co-based alloys such as 316 L steel (Ref 95, 96),
Ti-6Al-4V (Ref 97-99), Inconel 718 (Ref 100-102), Co-Cr
alloy (Ref 103, 104), with fully melted powders and densities
close to full density. However, there are still some problems
with the density of pure refractory metals printed by SLM. Due
to refractory metals’ extremely high melting points, high
energy density is required to melt them. Moreover, Nb readily
forms low-melting-point oxide eutectics in high-temperature
molten pools, resulting in many pores and cracks and ultimately
affecting the density of parts. Thus, improving printed parts’
density by optimizing process parameters is very important.

Another factor affecting the performance of parts is the
scanning strategy. Figure 18 shows the compressive strength
under different scanning strategies (Ref 70). Similar to the
results for hardness values, the scanning strategy that appeared
to have the highest compressive strength and elongation was
the sawtooth scanning strategy. The checkerboard scanning
strategy was relatively low, and the remelt scanning strategy
was the worst. Xiong et al. (Ref 71) believed that increasing the
scanning speed could reﬁne grains and increase the compres-
sive strength of parts. They printed pure W parts with a
compressive strength of 1200 MPa. However, they found that
increasing the scanning speed also led to instability of the
molten pool during processing, ultimately leading to an
increase in defects such as cracks. So, while elevating the
scanning speed could enhance the sample’s compressive
strength to a degree, it also heightened the likelihood of more
cracks. Thus, identifying an optimal scanning speed was
crucial. Kaserer et al. (Ref 85) found that the three-point
bending test results revealed that Mo-0.45 wt.% C processed by
SLM reached a 340% increased mean bending strength of up to
1180 ± 310 MPa compared to pure Mo.
The primary method to improve the compressive strength of
many experimental surfaces is ﬁne-grain reinforcement, which
enhances the compressive strength of parts by using the rapid
cooling speed of the laser to reﬁne grains. However, the
inherent brittleness and high thermal conductivity of refractory
metal materials, combined with the extremely rapid cooling rate
of the molten pool caused by the laser in SLM, render the
printed parts highly susceptible to cracking. Hence, relatively
little research has been done on tensile strength, and the main
focus has been on compressive strength.
3.4 Other Performance
In addition to density, hardness and compressive strength,
SLM refractory metal prints have some other properties, such
as friction and wear properties, thermal conductivity, and
biocompatibility. These characteristics have not been fully
studied, as they are not as valuable as some of the previously
described characteristics. Here, we bring them together.

and wear resistance by printing pure W parts using SLM. They
found that increased energy density decreased the samples’
friction coefﬁcient and wear rate. To determine why densiﬁ-

behavior. When the applied energy density was low, powder
particles could not fuse completely to form dense parts. The
pores formed could increase the roughness of the surface,
which could increase the coefﬁcient of friction (COF) and wear

wear of materials. In general, harder materials had better wear
resistance. If near-complete densiﬁcation was achieved, it could

helped to form a hard friction layer to avoid further wear of the
material. With the increase in energy density, the wear

4. Metallurgical Defects
4.1 Main Defects and Causes
SLM printing refractory metal parts have many advantages
and are one of the research hotspots in additive manufacturing.
Metallurgical defects are critical topics in the preparation of all
metal parts. The refractory metal parts produced by SLM
inevitably have problems, such as cracks that cannot be
eliminated entirely, and the density still needs to be improved.
These drawbacks directly limit the application of refractory
metal parts in the military industry because the existence of
cracks that cannot be eliminated makes the tensile strength of
refractory metal printing pieces of no measurement value.
However, the low density cannot guarantee the safety of
materials. Therefore, how to solve these metallurgical defects
has become the focus of current research.

4.2 Solutions
Faced with so many excellent properties of refractory
metals, it is always challenging to put them into use on a large
scale because of cracks and other problems. Many experi-
menters have made various attempts, including continuous
optimization of process parameters, upgrading of equipment,
use of different scanning strategies, initial powder particle
balling, preheating or post-treatment, etc. Although these
methods can suppress cracking to some extent, they cannot
completely eliminate defects such as cracks, and industrial
applications produce parts that vary greatly. Currently, there are
two effective methods to eliminate cracks. One is the use of
alloying methods combined with O elements to eliminate
cracks. Alternatively, the SEBM process utilizes vacuum
printing of refractory metals. Both methods aim to eliminate
cracks by preventing the combination of O elements with
refractory metals.
The main idea of alloying is to prevent eutectic formation at
low melting points by adding other elements instead of
refractory metals to combine with oxygen elements in the
printing process and ﬁnally to eliminate cracks. Wang et al.
(Ref 66) and Li et al. (Ref 119) attempted to suppress cracks by
adding the element Ta to pure W. Wang found that the W-6
wt.% Ta sample was similar to the pure W sample in that the
nano-pores were enriched in the cell walls rather than the grain
boundaries. The distribution of nano-pores could be combined
with the solidiﬁcation process. W-6 wt.% Ta alloys resembled
W in the liquid state, where the alloy combined with O to
produce WO
x gas. However, the W-6 wt.% Ta alloy ﬁrst
formed a cellular organization during cooling. As a result, pores
were squeezed between cells, trapped in the cell, and captured
by the cell wall. Residual pores were observed in the cell wall
at room temperature. Thus, nano-pores could be trapped in the
cell walls of such cell structures. The results showed that the
oxidation pores at GBs were reduced. Accordingly, the GBs
strength was increased after Ta alloying. Therefore, the
cracking potential of W and Ta alloy was much lower than
that of pure W. However, after alloying W with Ta, the presence
of cracks was still not completely eliminated. It was presumed
that Ta was a refractory metal and might form a low melting
point eutectic with oxygen, so it was still difﬁcult to solve the
interference of oxygen element fundamentally. In addition, the
crack can be eliminated by introducing C element (Ref 85). For
pure Mo, the addition of 0.45 wt.% C causes the coagulation
pattern to change from plane to cell. Under the pretreatment of
preheating to 800 /C176 C, ﬁne microstructure with interlinked
grains could be obtained, resulting in complete inhibition of
cracks and near-zero porosity. In SLM processing, oxygen
content could be reduced by CO degassing, thus avoiding the
binding of oxygen elements to refractory metals. According to
the ﬁnal results, the relative density, hardness and ﬂexural
strength of Mo with 0.45 wt.% carbon added increased by 1.9,
65 and 340%, respectively, compared to pure Mo. Since W and
Mo had similar properties, they were to some extent analogous
to each other. Compared to Ta alloying, the addition of C alloy
could effectively eliminate cracks, resulting in minimal porosity
levels close to zero. However, both can form microstructures
with better mechanical properties. Still, element C can effec-
tively combine with O to form CO gas to leave the molten pool
quickly, thus avoiding the possibility of element O combining
with refractory metals to produce eutectic with a low melting
point.

inclusion of impurities, such as oxygen and nitrogen, and
reducing residual thermal stress and defects in the ﬁnal product
(Ref 148). Table 3 summarizes some of the key features of

5. Summary and Prospect
SLM breaks through the limitation that it is challenging to
manufacture complex and precise parts with traditional metal
processing technology and can print complete complex com-
ponents of refractory metals. SLM technology provides a new
way to manufacture refractory metal. In addition, compared
with refractory metal parts produced by traditional process,
refractory metal parts produced by SLM process have better
density, hardness, ultimate compressive strength, and so on.
W, Mo, Ta, and Nb are the most representative and
researched refractory metals, which can be divided into two
categories. The properties of W and Mo are very similar and are
mainly used for industrial and weapon components requiring
high-temperature resistance. Throughout the part preparation
process, continual exploration and optimization of printing
parameters are essential. The primary methods for optimizing
these parameters involve adjusting laser power, scanning speed,
and scanning strategy. These adjustments aim to ensure
