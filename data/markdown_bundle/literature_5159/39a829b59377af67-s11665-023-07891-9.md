Pure Tungsten Fabricated by Laser Powder Bed Fusion
with Subsequent Hot Isostatic Pressing: Microstructural
Evolution, Mechanical Properties, and Thermal
Conductivity
Qi Shi, Wenhao Du, Feng Qin, Chong Tan, Khashayar Khanlari, Huanwen Xie, Xin Liu, and Anru Wu
Submitted: 25 July 2022 / Revised: 7 January 2023 / Accepted: 13 January 2023 / Published online: 13 February 2023
In this study, pure tungsten parts were fabricated by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF). The effects of
volumetric energy density (VED) during LPBF process and hot isostatic pressing (HIP) treatments on the
densiﬁcation behavior, microstructural evolution, mechanical properties and thermal conductivity of
printed parts were systematically investigated. The maximum relative densities of 95.91 and 98.97% for the
as-built and HIP treated samples were achieved, respectively. The microstructural characterization showed
that the as-built samples had columnar grains with a strong texture along the building direction. The
preferred orientation disappeared and the deformed microstructure transferred into substructured and
recrystallized grains after HIPing at 1600 /C176C. The mechanical testing results indicated that the highest
microhardness of 450 HV and the maximum ultimate compressive strength of /C24 1.22 GPa were achieved in
the specimen fabricated at the VED of 312.50 J/mm3, respectively, under the conditions of as-built and
HIPped at 1600 /C176C. The corresponding thermal conductivity was improved to 158 W/m •K after HIPping,
which implies a promising candidate for the application in the nuclear fusion reactor.
Keywords hot isostatic pressing, laser powder bed fusion,
mechanical properties, microstructure, thermal
conductivity, tungsten
1. Introduction
Due to the high melting point, high thermal and electrical
conductivities, high hardness, low expansion coefﬁcient and
good chemical corrosion resistance, tungsten ( W) has been an
irreplaceable material in various ﬁelds including aerospace,
electronics, mining, national defense and nuclear industry (Ref
1). As typical high-melting-point and hard-to-work materials,
tungsten and its alloys are usually processed through powder
metallurgy techniques, such as hot-pressing sintering (Ref 2),
powder injection molding (PIM) (Ref 3-5), spark plasma
sintering (SPS) (Ref 6), etc. Nonetheless, these techniques have
signiﬁcant limitations in fabricating parts with complicated
structures and shapes.
In recent decades, the rapid development of additive
manufacturing (AM) technologies offers a novel approach to
producing metallic parts with complex geometrical shapes. As
the most promising AM technique, laser powder bed fusion
(LPBF), by using a high-energy laser beam to melt the metallic
powder with a layer-wise paradigm based on the 3D model
enables the fabrication of components with high dimensional
accuracy and good surface quality. However, as a typical hard-
to-process refractory metal, due to the extremely high melting
point ( /C24 3410 /C176 C), high cohesive energy, high viscosity and
low wetting and spreading of the molten metal, the LPBF
process of pure W usually faces more challenges than other
mentioned metals (Ref 7, 8).
Deprez et al. (Ref 9) reported the fabrication of pure W
multi pinhole collimators with a relative density of 89.92% by
the LPBF technique. However, no details about the powder
feedstock and the processing parameters were presented in their
work. After that, most of the open literature has been focused
on the exploration of LPBF processing strategies to reduce
microcracks and porosity existing in the microstructure of W
parts and to improve their densiﬁcation (Ref 10-14). Enneti
et al. (Ref 10) investigated the effects of hatch spacing and
scanning speed on the densiﬁcation of fabricated parts, and the
maximum relative density of 75% was achieved. It is notewor-
thy that the W powder used in their study was non-spherical and
irregular-shaped. These signiﬁcantly reduced the ﬂowability
and the laser absorption of used powder. Using spherical W
powder, Wang et al. (Ref 11) examined how scanning strategies
affect the cracking distribution in the LPBF processed W

research is to ﬁll this gap and understand the effects of HIP
treatments on the microstructural evolution, mechanical prop-
erties and thermal conductivity of the LPBF densiﬁed W parts.
The results obtained from this study can provide insight into
additively manufactured pure W components aimed to be used
in high-performance applications.
2. Experimental Procedures
2.1 Raw W Powder and LPBF Process
The raw W powder used in this work, as shown in Fig. 1,
was prepared by the radio frequency plasma spheroidization
technique. It can be seen that most of the particles had a good
spherical shape and smooth surface (Fig. 1a), although a few
particles had a crumpled surface appearance, as indicated in
Fig. 1(b). This might be because some particles did not gain
sufﬁcient heat during the spheroidization process, and only
partial melting occurred on their surfaces (Ref 19). The
physical properties, including particle size distribution, Hall
ﬂowability, apparent density and tap density, and the chemical
composition of powder impurities are listed in Table 1.
Cubic specimens with a 6 mm height and a 10 9 10 mm
square cross-section were fabricated using an EOS M290 LPBF
machine. In this process, a stainless steel plate, preheated to
180 /C176 C, was used as the substrate and the angle of the scanning
direction between the successive printing layers was 67 /C176 . The
scanning strategy and the schematic of the LPBF processed
samples are illustrated in Fig. 2. In order to minimize the
oxidation, the LPBF process was conducted under a high-purity
argon protective atmosphere. The laser power ( P) and scanning
velocity ( v) were set in the range of 150-350 W and 200-
500 mm/s, respectively. The layer thickness ( d) and hatch
spacing (h) were kept constants as 30 and 80 lm, respectively.

2.3 Characterization and Tests
The densities of the as-built and HIP-treated samples were
measured by Archimedes /C213 method using deionized water. The
theoretical density of pure W (19.35 g/cm 3) was used for the
relative density calculations.
The phases present within these samples were identiﬁed
using an x-ray diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku Ultima VI)
equipped with Cu K a radiation. The metallographic samples
were mounted in conductive Bakelite and were then progres-

colloidal silica. A scanning electron microscope (SEM, JEOL

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Phase Analysis
As shown in Fig. 4(a), the diffraction peaks corresponding
to (110), (200) and (211) crystalline planes of BCC structured
W are clearly detected in the raw powder and the specimens
fabricated at various VEDs. Owing to the low oxygen content
in the initial powder and the protective atmosphere during both
LPBF and HIP processes, no secondary peak of WO
3 or other
oxides were found in the spectra. As magniﬁed in Fig. 4(b),
when the VED increased from 156.25 J/mm 3 (S1) to 312.50 J/
mm3 (S4), the (211) diffraction peaks shifted from right (high
angle) to left (low angle). Nonetheless, when the VED
continued to increase to 364.58 J/mm 3 (S5), the corresponding
peak shifted to a higher angle. This phenomenon is likely
related to the residual stresses existing in the as-built samples.
As reported by Mercelis et al. (Ref 20), the residual stresses in
the LPBF-processed parts are highly dependent on the material
properties, the processing parameters, the laser scanning
strategy and heating conditions. Based on the temperature
gradient mechanism and the cool-down mechanism, large
tensile stresses may occur at the top and bottom of the as-built
parts (Ref 20, 21). According to the Bragg /C213 s law equation,
2dÆsinh = k; the left or right shift of diffraction angle suggests
that the interplanar crystal distance of the corresponding crystal
plane increases or decreases. Therefore, the left shift of
diffraction peaks for samples S1-S4 can be explained by the
fact that the increased VED resulted in a higher temperature in
the molten pool and the top layer. Under such conditions, when
the melt has cooled and solidiﬁed, the thermally stretched
region contracts to a greater extent, and hence an enhanced
residual tensile stress is generated in parts. This consequently
can inﬂuence the lattice parameters of the samples. Nonethe-
less, when the VED continued to increase (sample S5), the
generation of a higher thermal accumulation within the molten
pools resulted in an equivalent heat treatment in the subjacent
layers leading to a reduced residual tensile stress and subse-
quent increase in diffraction angle.
Figure 5 shows the XRD spectra of the S4 sample in the
conditions of as-built and HIP-treated. As shown in Fig. 5(a), it
is obvious that the main peaks of HIP-treated samples were
basically identical to those of the as-built specimen. As
magniﬁed in Fig. 5(b), the (211) peak shifted to higher
diffraction angles after the conduction of HIP treatments, and
the shifting degree slightly increased with the HIPping
temperature. During the HIP process, the isotropic pressure
and the elevated temperature could effectively relieve residual
tensile stresses existing at the surface. This leads to the
observed shift of the diffraction angles.

Insufﬁcient fusion: For the samples fabricated at relatively low

the insufﬁcient fusion. Due to its extremely high melting
temperature, relatively high energy input is required to
completely melt pure W particles. The insufﬁcient energy

molten droplets, and hence the existence of noticeable unmelted
particles and shrinkage holes. (2) Gas entrapment: In the
specimens produced at higher VEDs, the amounts of irregular
pores were reduced and the residual near-spherical pores were
resulted from the gas entrapment. During the LPBF process,
protective gas is likely to roll into the molten pool due to the
Marangoni effects, and the fail-to-escape gas leads to the
formation of keyholes. It should be pointed out that the
nanopores distributed at the grain boundaries could be ascribed
to the formation of tungsten oxides (W
xOy) (Ref 23). In this
study, the oxygen source is expected to be from the initially
used powder and the atmosphere in the LPBF process, although
the corresponding oxygen contents were strictly controlled. For
the pure W, the solubility of interstitial atoms (mainly oxygen)
at the austenitizing temperature is 3 or 4 orders of magnitude
higher than that at room temperature (Ref 24), and the molten
W is highly prone to react with the supersaturated oxygen to
form W
xOy. Because the melting point of pure W is higher than
the boiling point of W xOy, the gaseous oxides would remain in
the molten pool, and ﬁnally segregate at grain boundaries under
the effect of Marangoni convection to form nanopores (Ref 25).
As illustrated in Fig. 7 and 8, it is observed that microcracks
are distributed as a checkerboard pattern and along the
columnar grains perpendicular and parallel to the building
direction, respectively. Multiple reasons lead to the formation
of microcracks. The ﬁrst major reason is the thermal shrinkage
generated in the LPBF process. The dimensional shrinkage
caused by the fusion and densiﬁcation of a loose powder bed
would result in the plastic deformation of the material, since the
yield strength of metals signiﬁcantly decreases at elevated
temperatures (Ref 26). This thermal tensile stress generated by
the shrinkage deformation, if it exceeds the limit that the
material can withstand, is usually released by the formation of
cracks. The relatively high ductile–brittle transition temperature
(DBTT) of pure W exacerbates this situation (Ref 15, 27).
Under the irradiation and movement of the laser beam, the
molten pool of pure W rapidly forms and solidiﬁes. The
microcracks perpendicular to the molten track are likely to be
generated at the solidiﬁcation front of the ripple under the effect
of the shrinkage stress along the scanning direction. Mean-
while, microcracking also might occur along the welding line
under the action of the transverse shrinkage between the
scanning tracks. The above explains the reason for the
generation of net-shaped microcracking in the top view
(Fig. 7). For the cracks formed along with the building
direction in the side view (Fig. 8), because the shrinkage of
the previous layer can be compensated by the subsequent
solidiﬁed layer, fewer microcracks were observed perpendicular
to the building direction inside the columnar grains. Moreover,
the stress-induced microcracks may also initiate at the accu-
mulated nanopores and propagate along the low-strength grain
boundaries (Ref 23, 28).

3.3 EBSD Analysis
The grain structures of S4 samples treated in different
conditions, as shown in Fig. 10, were revealed by EBSD
analysis conducted in the side view. Figure 10(a), (a-1), and (a-
2) show the band contrast images of the as-built, HIP-1400 and
HIP-1600 treated samples, respectively. It can be observed that
cracks were aligned along the building direction. According to
the superimposed band contrast and grain boundary images in
Fig. 10(a-3), it is clear that the remained microcracks and pores
were distributed along grain boundaries, especially the high
angle grain boundaries (HAGB), as indicated by the arrows.
This is because of the high DBTT of tungsten, leading the
cracks to preferentially initiating and propagating along HAGB
(Ref 16, 17).
As shown in the orientation maps (Fig. 10b, b-1, and b-2), it
can be clearly seen that the microstructure of the as-built
specimen was consisting of columnar grains being formed
substantially parallel to the building direction. The grains had a
length larger than several hundred micrometers, and a corre-
sponding width ranging from several micrometers to approx-
imately 100 lm. According to the statistic, the average grain
diameter was 2.09 lm. The formation of columnar grains can
be attributed to the epitaxial growth occurred between the
adjacent layers. It is known that during the LPBF process, the
previously solidiﬁed layer acts as the substrate and will be re-
melted when the new layer is deposited. Thus, the pre-existing
grain grows epitaxially along the direction parallel to the
maximum thermal gradient at the solidiﬁcation front, which is
usually perpendicular to the rear melt pool surface. This
phenomenon can be observed in many metals fabricated by the
LPBF technique, especially for pure metals (Ref 34) and single-
crystal alloys (Ref 35, 36). This is because, under such
conditions, there is a little constitutional supercooling acting as
the nucleation force and the formation of stray grains that
interrupt the epitaxial growth of crystals is decreased (Ref 37).
For the specimens treated by the HIP treatments, the EBSD-
derived orientation maps (Fig. 10b-1and b-2) suggest that the
microstructural features of columnar grains along the building
direction remained substantially unchanged as compared to the
as-built samples, and the average grain diameter increased to
2.96 and 14.8 lm, respectively. The pole ﬁgure of the as-built
and HIP-1400 treated samples (Fig. 10c and c-1) conﬁrmed the
strong {111) texture along the building direction, and the
maximum intensity of 6.10 mud was achieved after conducting
the HIP-1400 treatment, implying the fabrication of a sample
with the strongest anisotropy. In contrast, the part treated at
1600 /C176 C showed no apparent preferential crystallographic
alignment (Fig. 10c-2).

pure W had completed after heat treatment at 1400 /C176 C for 1 h.
This might be because of the rolling deformation, as the
recrystallization temperature decreases with increasing strain.
3.4 Mechanical Properties
Figure 11 shows the microhardness variations of the sam-
ples produced using different VEDs. For the as-built samples,
when the VED ranged from 156.25 to 312.50 J/mm
3 (S1t o S4),
the microhardness increased with the VED, and the maximum
microhardness value of 450 HV was achieved at the sample S4.
This trend can be explained by the densiﬁcation behavior. At a
low VED, the amounts of pores and cracks were relatively
large. These lead to the enlargement or even the collapse of the
indentation when the load is applied to the sample surface. With
the increase in VED, the relative density increases and the pores
and microcracks reduce causing the consequent increase in the
surface deformation resistance. However, when the VED
continued to increase up to 364.58 J/mm
3 (S5), the microhard-
ness reduced as compared to those parts processed under
312.50 J/mm 3 VED (S4), as the higher energy input resulted in
an excessive thermal accumulation and hence a coarsened
microstructure.
The microhardness of the HIP-1400 samples was generally
lower than that of the as-built one, except the S1 sample. This is
because the heat treatment usually leads to grain coarsening,
residual stress relief and dislocation annihilation giving rise to
the reduction of hardness. Nonetheless, for the sample S1,
because of its relatively low density in the as-built condition,
the inﬂuence of densiﬁcation, beneﬁted from the HIP process,
was more signiﬁcant than that of grain growth and residual
stress relief. It is noteworthy that the gap of the microhardness
values observed in the sample S2 was narrow between its
conditions of as-built and HIP-treated. This implies that these
two inﬂuences were mutually compensated in the HIP treated
parts. For the samples HIPed at 1600 /C176 C, the microhardness
reduced with the VED. Considering the results obtained from

3.5 Thermal conductivity
As shown in Fig. 14, the thermal conductivity of 147 W/
mÆK was achieved for the as-built S4 specimen, and it was
improved to 152 W/m ÆK and 158 W/m ÆK after HIP-1400 and
HIP-1600 treatments, respectively. According to the micro-
graphs and EBSD analysis, most of the micro-cracks and pores
had been eliminated and the deformed structure had recovered
and recrystallized after HIP treatments. It is known that the
thermal conductivity of pores, whether vacuum-ﬁlled voids or
gas-ﬁlled bubbles, is signiﬁcantly lower than the pristine solid
(Ref 40). In addition, dislocation annihilation and grain
boundary reduction during HIP treatments would promote the
thermal conductivity, because defects such as dislocations and
grain boundaries would increase the opportunity of phonon
scattering and shorten the mean free path of phonon (Ref 18).
The thermal conductivity of the HIP-1600 treated specimens in
the current study was close to that of ITER grade pure W
(/C24 162 W/m ÆK), and it was much higher than the LPBF
processed ones which were reported in the previous literatures
(Ref 13, 18). This suggests that the pure W component
fabricated by LPBF and post treated by HIP is a promising
candidate for the application in the nuclear fusion reactor.
4. Conclusions
In the present study, pure W parts were fabricated by the
LPBF process and subjected to the HIP treatments. The effects
of volumetric energy density and HIP treatment on the
microstructural evolution, mechanical properties and thermal
conductivity were investigated. The main conclusions can be
drawn as follows:
The volumetric energy density (VED) performed a signiﬁ-
cant effect on the densiﬁcation of the LPBF processed
pure W parts. In the scope of the experiment, the relative
density of the as-built samples increased with the VED,
and the maximum value of 95.91% was achieved at the
VED of 364.58 J/mm
3. The proper VED could effectively
reduce the pores and microcracks, although the existence
of microcracks in the microstructure of as-built parts
seemed to be inevitable in this study.
The HIP treatment could effectively improve densiﬁcation
by eliminating pores and microcracks. The relative density
increased with the HIP processing temperature, and a near-
ly fully dense part with a relative density of 98.97% was
obtained by conducting HIPing at 1600 /C176 Co n S5 samples
processed with a VED of 364.58 J/mm
3.
The as-built pure W parts showed columnar crystal
microstructure in the side view with a strong texture. The
preferred orientation was remained unchanged after doing
