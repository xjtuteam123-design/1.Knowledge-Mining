abstract
Tungsten is considered in fusion technology as structural material in divertor for future application in
DEMO. Helium cooled divertor design includes joints between W and W alloys. Present study analyzed
the brazeability of pure tungsten using a commercial high temperature nickel-based alloy in vacuum fur-
nace. Although nickel application of interlayers in the W–W joints of the structural zone is not desirable,
as it can promote helium accumulation under irradiation and a subsequent embrittlement enhancement
of the brazed joint, this experimental study could help to deﬁne conditions of the braze application inside
the W–W joints, besides obtaining useful information to control the brazing conditions and avoid unde-
sirable brazeability problems (cracking, dilution, W recrystallization, grain growth, etc.). High vacuum
brazing tests have been carried out at temperatures higher than the liquidus one of the braze alloy. Char-
acterization studies, both microstructural and mechanical, of the W–W brazed specimens demonstrated
the development of new brazing ﬁllers which meet requirements such as strength and ﬂexibility for
adaptation to the shape of the joint.

1. Introduction
The use of tungsten as structural material for plasma-interac-
tive components of the fusion DEMO reactors, such as the helium
cooled divertors, could be a promising solution against the high
heat ﬂux (HHF) and sputtering energy present in the fusion power
plants with heat ﬂux operation condition of at least 10 MW/m 2.
Tungsten or tungsten alloys are most suitable as the structural
material [1] due to its excellent thermophysical properties: high
melting point, high thermal conductivity, and low thermal expan-
sion. Furthermore, the thermal conductivity of tungsten does not
decrease under neutron irradiation considerably. Due to the oper-
ation conditions, tungsten is usually considered in the divertor de-
sign as thermal shield and sacriﬁcial layer for plasma-facing
components due to its high resistance against sputtering. The sys-
tem consists of a monoblock constituted by small tungsten tiles
(about 5 mm thick), in order to reduce raised thermal stresses dur-
ing the service operations [2]. This layer is joined to a thimble-like
structure also made of tungsten alloy forming a cooling ﬁnger unit,
being this joined to a low activation steel cartridge.
Manufacture of this complex structure needs of the developing
of suitable welding methods to produce W–W joints which ensure
the correct operation of the divertor. Although different joining
technologies have been evaluated to make this joint, high temper-
ature brazing is an alternative to welding that produces grain
growth and other microstructural changes of the W [3]. Brazing
has a limited effect in base metal properties and it has been ac-
cepted widely as a method of joining structures and devices man-
ufactured with refractory metals [4]. One of the main aims of
brazing pure tungsten to tungsten (W–W) is the selection of alter-
native ﬁller alloys constituted by low activation elements to be
used for high temperature brazing (900–1200 /C176C).
Several authors have investigated the W–W joints by means of
high-temperature brazing [2,5–10]. Norajitra et al. [2] has shown
that a brazed W–W joint with STEMET 1311 (Ni-base, 16.0Co,
5.0Fe, 4.0Si, 4.0B, 0.4Cr) is reliable enough up to a heat ﬂux of
15 MW/m2 for ﬂat joints and 14 MW/m 2 for realistic geometry
joints of a helium-cooled divertor. STEMET 9 (Ni based, 4Mo, 4Fe,
15Cr, 7.5Si, 1.5B) shows acceptable results up to a heat ﬂux of
14 MW/m2. Furthermore, Ni alloys show an increase in the yield
strength and no relevant reduction in the elongation and strain
hardening as a consequence of neutronic irradiation [11]. Addition-
ally, when both neutronic irradiation and high heat ﬂux are work-
ing together on Ni alloys, any loss of strain hardening is obtained
[11].
Therefore, from a mechanical point of view, the use of Ni based
alloys as ﬁller in the W–W joints for divertor application should be
considered. However, Ni shows a radioactivity close to the allowed
values after exposure to a typical fusion neutron irradiation. Thus,

Ni–Mn alloys show no signiﬁcant variations in their structure by
neutronic irradiation [12]; therefore, no change is expected in both
the structure and properties of these brazing alloys during the
divertor operation conditions.
The main objective of this work is to study the reliability of Ni–
Mn alloys as ﬁller metal for brazing pure tungsten to pure tungsten
(W–W) by vacuum furnace tests. The microstructure of the brazed
joints has been studied using microscopy images and the mechan-
ical properties have been obtained by microhardness and shear
tests.
2. Experimental details
2.1. Materials
The base material was purchased from Alfa Aesar consisting of a
commercial tungsten rod with 13 mm of diameter and it was used
for brazing in the vacuum furnace tests.
AWS BNi-8, according to AWS classiﬁcation, was selected as a
ﬁller metal. This brazing ﬁller metal was originally developed for
its low interaction and good brazing properties of nickel. Addition-
ally, it shows excellent ﬂow under brazing conditions. The Ni based
ﬁller was supplied by Sulzer Metco with the commercial designa-
tion of AMDRY 930. The ﬁller consisted of spherical gas atomized
powder imbibed in an organic binder that is discomposed during
the brazing tests. The Ni–Mn alloy has an atomic percent composi-
tion of Ni–23Mn–7Si–5Cu and the size of the powder was ranged
from 45 to 106 lm. This ﬁller was provided in tape form, which
was an advantage because it can adapt its shape to the complex
form required for the joint. The thickness and width of the tape
were 200 lm and 3 mm, respectively.
2.2. Brazing techniques
Brazing tests were made in a high vacuum furnace that allows
working with a residual pressure of 10 /C05 mbar at the brazing tem-
perature. The brazing conditions were chosen at 25 /C176C and 50 /C176C
above the liquidus temperature of 1050 /C176C (1075 and 1100 /C176C,
respectively) during 10 min. The heating and the cooling rates
were 5 /C176C/min. Jiang et al. observed that slow cooling allows to re-
lease the residual stresses at elevated temperature, avoid thermal
cracks and increase the strength of brazed joints using Ni based ﬁl-
ler metal [13].
For the brazing tests, W slices with thickness of about 1.5 mm
were cut from a 13 mm diameter bar. The Ni ﬁller metal supplied
in tape form was cut in strips with lengths of 13 mm and lower,
and were placed at the gap between the joined parts covering
the whole surface area. The brazing gap between the W pieces
was about 200 lm. The only load applied was that of the W slice
weight over the ﬁller, which was about 200 mg.
2.3. Characterization techniques
Brazing temperatures were determined by Differential Thermal
Analysis (DTA) from Setaram Thermic Analyzer Setsys 16/18 in Argon
atmosphere. The experiment consisted of a 5 /C176C/min heat ramp
from room temperature to temperature 100 /C176C above the theoret-
ical liquidus temperature. Then the samples were cooled down
using the same ramp. The heat exchanges during heating and cool-
ing processes were recorded.
The microstructure of different brazing tests was characterized
by Light Microscopy (LM) and Environmental and Scanning
Electron Microscopy ( Philips ESEM-XL30 and Hitachi 3400 , respec-
tively) equipped both with Energy Dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX).
Vickers hardness tests ( Buehler 2101 ) were done from fusion
line to tungsten base material to evaluate the local hardness asso-
ciated to interdiffusional and dilution phenomena and to estimate
the interface resistance of the brazed joints. The hardness tests
were carried out according to the requirements of ASTM: E384-
11e1 Standard Test Method for Knoop and Vickers Hardness of
Materials. The maximum indentation load was ﬁxed to 100 g and
it was applied for 30 s. The distance between indentations was
larger than two times the residual imprint size. Lines of ﬁve

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Characterization of Ni based ﬁller
To characterize the behavior of the commercial Ni ﬁller used
during the brazing process, DTA curves were recorded during heat-
ing and cooling from room temperature to 1100 /C176C( Fig. 2 ). The
melting process results in an endothermic peak, it started at about
1010 /C176C, was centered at 1030 /C176C and all the ﬁller was completely
molten around 1050 /C176C, that was ﬁxed as liquidus temperature.
During the solidiﬁcation, again only one peak was observed cen-
tered at 1000 /C176C and was narrower than the melting one.
3.2. Microstructural characterization of brazed W–W joints
3.2.1. Brazing temperature of 1100 /C176C
In the case of the joints made at 1100 /C176C, the ﬁller after melting
and solidiﬁcation formed a braze with a thickness of less than
50 lm. The reduction of the original gap between W pieces from
200 lm down to 50 lm was due to the elimination of the binder,
the contraction of the melting ﬁller during solidiﬁcation and the
expulsion of the melted metal by capillarity out of the joint clear-
ance. The last phenomenon is favored as the brazing temperature
increases.
The joint was continuous and the porosity was very low along
the joint ( Fig. 3 ). Internal cracks could be observed in the brazing
interface after the solidiﬁcation (arrowed in Fig. 3). It is an indica-
tion of the brittleness of the phases where the cracks nucleated and
propagated. Nevertheless, these solidiﬁcation cracks always
stopped when reaching the interface with other characteristic
phase. Additionally, cracking was also observed in the base mate-
rial, even at zones that were at 50 lm from the interface. According
to other authors, the use of Ni alloy ﬁllers gives rise to cracks which
do not propagate along the interface with the base material. This
indicates a good adhesion between the ﬁller metal and tungsten al-
loy [14].
Other microstructural effect that was observed at the brazing
interface was the incorporation of some spherical W particles into
the ﬁller. Its shape and location indicate that there was a dilution
phenomenon between the molten ﬁller and the W rod, which pref-
erentially progressed along the grain boundaries of the parent
material. Partial dissolution of the W grain boundaries have been
also observed using Ti–Fe ﬁllers for brazing [9]. Ni–W phase dia-
gram shows a decrease of the melting point of W from 3410 /C176C
(100% W) to 1498 /C176C at the eutectic point (45 wt.% in W). Although
we have not data of the ternary Ni–Mn–W phase diagram, studies
carried out in the development of ternary heavy alloys based on
tungsten–nickel–manganese [15] showed that liquidus tempera-
ture could decreases down to 1014 /C176C for composition of
90W–6Mn–4Ni. It corresponds approximately with the melting
point of a 60Mn:40Ni alloy according with the Ni–Mn binary phase
diagram. Thus, liquid formation in the ternary system could occurs
at a temperature slightly above 1000 /C176C, so it is theoretically possi-
ble that the molten ﬁller with a composition of Ni–23 wt.% Mn
could penetrate, by diffusion, following the W grain boundary

the heating stage and therefore the W enrichment of the molten
ﬁller at this zone. The extremely short brazing time used and the
proximity to the liquidus temperature avoided the braze homoge-

sisted of three different phases ( Fig. 4 ). Their main constituents
were identical to those of the ﬁller previously shown, although
zones with different content in Mn and W were observed. The zone
identiﬁed with (1) it is mainly constituted by Ni with a 17 wt.% of
Si and 2 wt.% of Cu. The zone (2) is similar but shows the presence
of 6 wt.% of Mn. In none of these zones, W was detected. The zone
(3) closer to the W substrate showed higher amount of Cu (9 wt.%)
and some W was also detected there (4 wt.%). Finally, the (4) zone
presented more W (7.7 wt.%) and Cu (5 wt.%). Therefore, zones (1)
and (2) that contain Si are brittle as it is shown in Fig. 4. Jiang et al.
observed that one of the negative effects of the silicon, as alloying
element of Ni based ﬁllers, is that some brittle compounds were
generated [16].
3.2.2. Brazing temperature of 1075 /C176C
The Ni based ﬁller brazed at 1075 /C176C formed a joint with a thick-
ness of about 110 lm; this value doubles that of the joint treated at
1100 /C176C, due to the expulsion of the melted metal by capillarity out
of the joint clearance is less favored at lower brazing temperature.
The joint obtained was continuous and free from porosity all
along the joint, although some cracks were again observed across
the brittle phases such as in the previous case ( Fig. 5 a). These
cracks are formed during the solidiﬁcation, and could be associated
to the mismatch in coefﬁcients of thermal expansion of W and ﬁl-
ler alloy. Again, liquefaction phenomenon was also observed in
certain zones of the joint ( Fig. 5 b), but in this case not only incor-
poration of W particles into the ﬁller was observed, and also lique-
faction cracking occurred.

After solidiﬁcation, the ﬁller microstructure consisted of two
well deﬁned phases ( Fig. 5). Their main constituents were identical
to those of the ﬁller previously shown, although the darker zones
were richer in Si (15 wt.% Si, 21 wt.% Mn, 63 wt.% Ni and 1.7 wt.%

3.3. Mechanical properties of the joints
3.3.1. Brazing temperature of 1100 /C176C
Fig. 6a shows the hardness values evolution of the Ni–Mn ﬁller–
W base material system treated at 1075 and 1100 /C176C. Dashed lines
represent the joint-base material interfaces. The left dashed line
corresponds to the bottom interface in Figs. 3 and 5a, respectively.
In the case of joints brazed at 1100 /C176C, indentations made in
upper interface and those made on the W next to the joint are
shown in Fig. 6 b. The hardness of the ﬁller was 435 ± 36 HV at
its center and it increased to values of about 536 ± 26 HV close
to the bottom interface. The hardness values measured at the W
rod ( Fig. 6 a) were not much affected by the dilution cracking ob-
served, showing homogeneous values from the inside to the inter-
face. No cracking was observed in the edges of the
microindentations residual imprints in any indentations ( Fig. 6 b)
showing that there was a good bond between the ﬁller and base
material, which resulted in a strengthened joint with adequate val-
ues of hardness.
Fig. 6 c shows the SEM image of a residual imprint correspond-
ing to a microindentation carried out in the ﬁller of a joint treated
at 1100 /C176C. The residual imprint covered the different phases of the
ﬁller microstructure generated after solidiﬁcation of the ﬁller
material ( Figs. 3 and 4 ). Therefore, the hardness values measured
were the average values of the responses of the phases involved
in the contact area. In addition, different deformation processes
of each involved phase can be observed as a consequence of the
indentation test. The phases observed in dark gray (Ni–Si–Mn) in
Figs. 3 and 4 showed microcracking during indentation. This is
one of the phases identiﬁed as brittle. The phase containing more
W (Ni–Si–Cu) (in lighter gray in Figs. 3 and 4 ) exhibited plastic
deformation by means of pile-up process. This phase was identiﬁed
as the phase where the cracks stopped. In summary, the measured
properties were the combined effect of the different phases, and
the phase that allows plastic deformation seems to be responsible
of the good behavior of the ﬁller during cooling of the sandwich
structure, limiting the fracture of the brittle phase. To determine
the behavior of each phase, more local technique, such as nanoin-
dentation, would be necessary.

3.3.2. Brazing temperature of 1075 /C176C
A hardness proﬁle has been obtained from the center of the
joint brazed at 1075 /C176C up to a distance of 300 lm( Fig. 6a). Unlike
what was observed in the brazing carried out at higher tempera-
tures, the hardness of the ﬁller was homogenous along its section
with a value of 434 ± 20 HV.
On the other hand, the hardness values measured at the W rod
were very stable at different distances of the joint, with an average
value of 380 ± 10 HV. Finally, no cracking was observed at the
interface due to the microindentations.
Several W–W brazed samples at 1075 /C176C were chosen in order
to measure their shear strength. The maximum load resisted in
both cases was about 27 kN, which corresponds with a nominal
shear strength around 213 MPa. This value is in the range previ-
ously reported using pure Ni and Ni alloys as interlayer ﬁller
[17–20].
Fig. 7 shows the shape of a W piece after a shear test. The ﬁller is
well distributed in the inner zones of the W pieces but is no longer
present in the other zone of the joint (arrowed areas), so that the
actual joint surface was smaller than that of the W pieces. After
the test, part of the ﬁller remains in both W substrates, indicating
that cohesive failure has taken place.
Topographical features in the fractured ﬁller were analyzed by
SEM. The surfaces were rough ( Fig. 8 a) and there were no notice-
able composition differences between the phases that formed the
joint (Fig. 8b). At higher magniﬁcation, it is possible to observe ﬂat
zones in which cleavage planes were formed during the transgran-
ular crack growth ( Fig. 8 c) and zones with particles ( Fig. 8 d). The
cleavage planes in fracture surface using Ni alloys interlayers have
been also reported by Kundu et al. [18].
This morphology adequately corresponds with the microstruc-
ture of this ﬁller in which different phases were observed. The
one constituted by Ni–Mn–Si was brittle and showed abrupt
boundaries, and was even cracked immediately after brazing. On
the other hand, there was a phase richer in Cu, i.e. Ni–Mn–Si–Cu
that stopped the propagation of the cracks. This phase would have
had a more ductile behavior and be the main responsible of the
high resistance of this ﬁller.
From these images the actual joined surface was determined
and the shear resistance increases up to 280 ± 8 MPa. This value
is high enough for the manipulation of the tiles and for the
mechanical requirements that these joints must fulﬁll.
4. Conclusions
Obtained results have shown the suitability of Ni–Mn alloys ap-
plied as ﬂexible ﬁller tapes to produce W–W brazed joints with
suitable mechanical properties measured at room temperature.
The selected method has also deﬁned the possibility to braze these
W–W components using a short brazing cycle. However, Ni–Mn al-
loys do not meet the requirements of maximum service tempera-
ture (approximately 1200 /C176C). Then, these results must be
considered as a contribution to the development of new brazing
ﬁllers which, besides meeting the high service temperatures, must
also reach other requirements such as strength and joint adapta-
tion that have been obtained in the case of the tested Ni–Mn ﬁller.
Despite Ni has a radioactivity after neutronic irradiation similar
to that of W, it would be necessary to perform a study on the suit-
ability of Ni–Mn alloys to neutronic irradiation at high
temperature.
