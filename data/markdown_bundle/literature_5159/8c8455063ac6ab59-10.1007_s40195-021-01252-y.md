Abstract
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are composed of multiple principal elements and exhibit not only remarkable mechanical 
properties, but also promising potentials for developing numerous new compositions. To fully realize such potentials, high-
throughput preparation and characterization technologies are especially useful; thereby, the fast evaluations of mechanical 
properties will be urgently required. Revealing the relation between strength and hardness is of significance for quickly 
predicting the strength of materials through simple hardness testing. However, up to now the strength–hardness relation 
for HEAs is still a puzzle. In this work, the relations between tensile or compressive strength and Vickers hardness of vari-
ous HEAs with hundreds of compositions at room temperature are investigated, and finally, the solution for estimating the 
strengths of HEAs from their hardness values is achieved. Data for hundreds of different HEAs were extracted from stud-
ies reported in the period from 2010 to 2020. The results suggested that the well-known three-time relation (i.e., hardness 
equals to three times the magnitude of strength) works for nearly all HEAs, except for a few brittle HEAs which show quite 
high hardness but low strength due to early fracture. However, for HEAs with different phase structures, different strengths 
should be applied in using the 3-time relation, i.e., yield strength for low ductility body-centered cubic (BCC) HEAs and 
ultimate strength for highly plastic and work-hardenable face-centered cubic (FCC) HEAs. As for dual-phase or multi-phase 
HEAs, similar 3-time relations can be also found. The present approach sheds light on the mechanisms of hardness and also 
provides useful guidelines for quick estimation of strength from hardness for various HEAs.

1 Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), alternatively referred to as 
multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) or complex con-
centrated alloys (CCAs), have attracted extensive academic 
interests since their first discovery over a decade ago [ 1, 
2]. In contrast with the conventional alloys that are based 
on one or two major elements, HEAs generally contain 
5–13 principal elements with the concentration of each 
element being between 5 and 35 at.% [1 , 3]. Based on the 
multi-component design strategy, a variety of HEAs have 
been produced [2 , 4]. The high configurational entropy of 
HEAs could benefit the formation of single-phase solid 
solutions by lowering the overall mixing free energy. The 
most common phases in HEAs are simple face-centered 
cubic (FCC) and body-centered cubic (BCC) [5 ], whereas 
the number of HEAs with hexagonal close-packed (HCP) 
phase is small. The phase constitution of HEAs could be 
tailored by alloy elements. Various studies have found that 
adding Al, V or Mo et al. to HEAs with a simple FCC 
or BCC phase can lead to a transition of microstructure 
from simple solid solution to multiphase structure (e.g., 
FCC + IM, BCC + IM, 2BCC, FCC + BCC + IM, 3BCC, 
2FCC + BCC) by forming the intermetallic (IM) phase or 
BCC crystal structure [6 –9].
HEAs with different phase structures exhibit signifi-
cantly distinct mechanical properties. For example, HEAs 
with FCC structure such as CoCrNi and CoCrFeMnNi show 
not only high plasticity [10, 11], but also ultra-high frac-
ture toughness [12], or ultra-high impact toughness (e.g., 
CoCrFeNi and Al0.1CoCrFeNi) [13, 14]. In contrast, BCC 
single-phase HEAs display high hardness and high com-
pressive strength but low ductility [15]. However, some 
refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) with BCC phase 
show relatively good tensile plasticity [16], excellent high-
temperature strength [17], good high-temperature creep 
resistance and good high-temperature oxidation resistance 
[18]. Most recently, the emphasis on developing multiphase 
HEAs has been primarily driven by increased interests in 
improving mechanical properties, since single-phase HEAs 
usually display a disappointing combination of strength 
and ductility. Hence, dual-phase (DP) HEAs have been 
developed. For example, AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 eutectic HEA 
with FCC + BCC DP structure shows remarkable combined 
strength–ductility performance [19]. By forming the precipi-
tation phase (η-Ni3Ti), Yeh’s group [20] improved the wear 
resistance of Co27.3Cr18.2Fe18.2Ni27.3Ti9.1 alloy significantly, 
and they also increased the thermal softening resistance of 
 AlCoCrxFeMo0.5Ni alloy at high temperatures by the forma-
tion of σ phase [21].
Although mechanical properties of HEAs have been 
extensively studied, the research on the relation between 
strength and hardness is very rare [22, 23]. The empiri-
cal relation ( H ≈ 3/u1D70Ey , where σy is yield strength and H is 
hardness) was used to calculate the tensile yield strength of 
 AlCuCrFeMnWx (x = 0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5) HEAs [22], while 
Zhou et al. [23] illustrated that some BCC HEAs may follow 
this relation, but FCC HEAs do not. Despite such studies, 
the clear correlation between strength and hardness has not 
yet been widely developed. On the other hand, to realize the 
promising potentials for developing numerous new composi-
tions and good properties of HEAs, high-throughput prepara-
tion technology is usually required due to the large number of 
compositions and microstructures. In this case, the definite 
relation between strength and hardness has very important 
practical significance, which would allow researchers to eval-
uate the strengths of HEAs through simpler, quicker hardness 
testing using smaller sample volumes than the uniaxial ten-
sion testing.
The aim of this work is to reveal the relation between 
strength and hardness for various HEAs. We have col-
lected from the literature available the values of tensile 
strength, Vickers hardness and elongation for 43 HEAs, 
and the values of compressive strength, Vickers hard-
ness and fracture strain for 112 HEAs. For each HEA, 
the ratio of hardness-to-strength has been calculated and 
compared. According to the phase structures, the HEAs 
have been divided into 3 main categories (single-phase, 
dual-phase and three-phase HEAs) and 6 sub-groups 
(FCC, BCC, FCC + BCC, FCC + IM, BCC + IM and 
FCC + BCC + IM HEAs). The strength–hardness rela-
tions are discussed for each kind of HEA separately and 
the solutions to estimate the strengths of HEAs from the 
hardness values are obtained eventually.

In this work, the well-known 3-time relation between 
strength and hardness will be verified thoroughly for a lot

ined because of the limited data available for Young’s 
modulus and strain-hardening exponents. Two questions 
naturally arise: (1) does the 3-time relation hold for all 
HEAs? (2) If yes, what kind of strength is in the 3-time 
relation, yield strength, ultimate tensile strength or frac-
ture strength?

As mentioned above, the HEAs discussed in this 
paper are divided into three main types and 6 sub-groups 
according to the phases: single-phase HEAs (including 
FCC and BCC HEAs), DP HEAs (including FCC + IM,

4.4 Strength–Hardness Relation in Three‑Phase 
(TP) HEAs
TP HEAs contain not only FCC and BCC phases, but also 
IM phase. The volume fractions of various phases in the 
TP HEAs significantly influence the mechanical properties. 
As shown in Table  2, the Al0.5CoCrFeMoxNi (x = 0.2, 0.3, 
0.4) HEAs with a large volume fraction of FCC phase dis-
play relatively high compressive fracture strain (18–34%) 
but moderate yield strength (556–1091 MPa). For other TP 
HEAs listed in Table 2, the volume fractions of BCC phase 
and IM phase are higher than that of FCC phase; as such, 
they show high yield strength (1105–1544 MPa) but reduced 
compressive plasticity (1.4–10%), as well as low work-hard-
ening ability with relatively small compressive fracture-
to-yield strength ratio, R c. To reveal the relation between 
hardness and strength for TP HEAs, the Vickers hardness 
as functions of compressive strengths (σ yc, σf and σ 10) is 
plotted in Fig. 13. For relatively ductile TP HEAs with high 
fracture strain and high work-hardening ability, σ10 and hard-

Fig. 13c. On the other hand, for those relatively brittle TP 
HEAs, both the yield and fracture strengths can be approxi-
mated by HV/3 (see Fig. 13a and c).

been frequently observed in brittle BCC HEAs [97, 98] 
or HEAs with intermetallics phases [9 , 61]. The brittle 
cracking can be suppressed significantly by compressive 
loading but promoted by tensile stresses. Therefore, brittle 
HEAs, which exhibit tendency of cleavage cracking, usu-

strength under uniaxial tension, while these alloys could 
experience plastic deformation under compressive loading 
such as compression and indentation. This explains why 
the hardness is well related to the compressive strength 
rather than tensile strength. In the extreme case, however, 
if brittle fracture happens under uniaxial compression with 
no compressive plasticity, plastic deformation can also 
occur under indentation due to the more confined stress 
state. In this case, the σ f will be lower than H V/3, as con-
firmed by experiments [9 ].
5 Conclusions
Based on the above analysis of tensile or compressive 
strength and Vickers hardness of HEAs with hundreds of 
compositions, the relations between strength and hardness 
for typical HEAs with different phases are revealed. The 
following conclusions can be drawn.
5.1 Effect of Microstructural Phases
(1) For single-phase HEAs, such as all FCC and some duc-
tile BCC alloys, the ultimate tensile strength (σ UTS) or 
compressive flow stress (σ10) can be well predicted by 
HV/3.
(2) For dual-phase HEAs of FCC + IM and FCC + BCC, 
σ10 is approximately equal to HV/3, while for BCC + IM 
DP HEAs, the compressive fracture strength σf ≈ H V ∕3
.
(3) For three-phase HEAs of FCC + BCC + IM, σ10 ≈ H V ∕3 
for HEAs with large compressive plasticity and 
σf ≈ H V ∕3 for low ductility or brittle alloys.
5.2 Effect of Plastic Deformation Ability
(1) For ductile HEAs ( /u1D6FF≥ 10% or /u1D700f ≥ 20% ), both σUTS and 
σ10 can be well predicted by HV/3.
(2) For low ductility or brittle HEAs ( 𝛿< 10% or 
𝜀f < 20% ), the compressive fracture strength (σ f) 
approximates to H V/3. These relatively brittle HEAs 
usually exhibit weak work hardening ability, thus 
/u1D70Eyc ≈ HV ∕3.
(3) For extremely brittle HEAs with nearly zero plasticity 
( /u1D6FF= 0 or /u1D700f → 0 ), the HV/3 will be much higher than 
fracture strength because of the early fracture under 
uniaxial loading.
The present approaches emphasize the critical roles of 
both the microstructure and plastic deformation ability (rep-
resented by the overall plasticity and work-hardening abil-
ity) of alloys in affecting the strength–hardness relation of 
HEAs. Based on this work, one can easily estimate tensile or 
compressive strengths by performing simple hardness test-
ing, which will be helpful for high-throughput characteriza-
tion of various new HEAs.
