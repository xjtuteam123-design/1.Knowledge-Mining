trade-off of conventional metal alloys. Such extraordinary mechanical properties were attributed to a suitable 
strain hardening capability, stemming from the synergistic effect of hetero-deformation-induced hardening, 
twinning-induced plasticity, and deformation-induced phase transformation during tensile deformation. 
In 2004, Ye et al. proposed a new alloy design concept of using equal 
or near equal atomic proportions of a high number of elements to form 
single phase alloys, named high-entropy alloys (HEAs) [1] . Such design 
concept provides an enormous compositional space for alloys and has 
the promise of maximizing the effect of solid solution strengthening 
[2 – 4] . To date, most of developed HEAs are single-phase face-centered 
cubic (FCC) and body-centered cubic (BCC) alloys. Generally, BCC HEAs 
are stronger but less ductile whereas FCC HEAs are more ductile but less 
strong. Among the FCC HEAs, CrCoFeNi-based alloys appear to be the 
best performers with the highest tensile strength, ductility, and fracture 
toughness both at room temperature and cryogenic temperatures down 
to the liquid helium temperature [ 3 , 5-13 ]. For example, the equiatomic 
CoCrFeNiMn HEA exhibits a tensile yield strength of 750 MPa, a tensile 
elongation of ~70 % and a fracture toughness of ~220 MPa ⋅ m
1/2 
at 77 K 
[5] . 
Despite a significant enhancement in strength, there is still room for 
improvement conforming to the fundamental principles of physical 
metallurgy [ 2 , 3 ]. This can be achieved by applying the common 
strengthening methods, such as dislocation strengthening, grain 
boundary strengthening and precipitate strengthening. However, these 
approaches increase the strength at the expense of ductility and tough -
ness, which is known as the strength-ductility trade-off phenomenon 
[13 – 15] . This obviously compromises the advantages of FCC HEAs, 
which needs urgent solutions for overcoming the above issues. 
Generally, an alloy may lose its ductility for either of two reasons. If 
the alloy has too high a resistance to dislocation movement, it hardens 
rapidly to reach the fracture strength upon loading, causing brittle 
failure at low prior plastic deformation. On the other hand, if the alloy 
has too low resistance to dislocation movement, plastic deformation 
occurs in an uncontrollable manner, causing the alloy to rupture pre -
maturely by necking. Therefore, the key to achieving a good combina -
tion of strength and ductility is to have a suitable strain hardening rate 
that is not too high to allow reasonable dislocation movement without 
rapid hardening to the fracture strength of the alloy, and at the same 
time not too low to prevent strain localization and necking, as per the 
Consider `e criterion [16 – 18] .

high density of twins or deformation-induced phase transformations, so
