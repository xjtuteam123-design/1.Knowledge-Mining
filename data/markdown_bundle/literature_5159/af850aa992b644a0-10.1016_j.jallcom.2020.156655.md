Abstract Ultrafine-grained Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 was synthesized by powder metallurgy from partially-
amorphous powder precursors. A good combination of mechanical properties was obtained by 
optimizing size and density of second phases. The resultant alloy shows high specific strength and 
specific modulus as well as a tensile ductility of ~5 % at room temperature. Particularly, this alloy 
exhibits good mechanical stability at elevated temperature, which is superior to most high-
temperature aluminum alloys. These results provide evidence for the effectiveness of Al-based 
metallic glasses as precursors for the development of high-temperature aluminum alloys. 
 
Keywords: ultrafine-grained aluminum; Al-based metallic glass; high temperature aluminum alloy; 
mechanical properties 
 
1. Introduction 
Aluminum alloys are among the most widely used structural materials owing to their low 
density, good mechanical properties, good workability, superior corrosion resistance as well as 
excellent recyclability [1]. One limitation of most Al alloys lies in the low service temperature, 
which is typically limited to 473 K since the precipitates responsible for strengthening coarsen 
rapidly at elevated temperature [2-4]. Alloying elements that exhibit both limited solid solubility and

low diffusivity in Al have been used to develop high strength and thermally-stable Al alloys [5]. 
These include rapid solidified Al-Fe alloys with ternary and often quaternary additions of elements 
such as Mo, V, Ni, Co, Zr, Ce [5, 6] and systems forming Al 3TM (where TM is a transition metal) 
coherent L1 2 precipitates from a supersaturated solid solution containing at least one of the elements 
Sc, Zr, Ti, Hf, Er and Yb [7-9]. Based on the diffusion-controlled coarsening theory, the second 
phase resulting from the addition of these elements would be resistant to Ostwald ripening. 
Ultrahigh strength and appreciable compressive ductility in Al-rich Al-RE-TM (RE is a rare 
earth metal) metallic glasses or derived nanostructured alloys have been extensively reported [10-12 ]. 
However, limited ductility under tension remains a constant issue for these alloys, significantly 
restricting their applications. The microstructure of devitrified Al-RE-TM alloys usually consist of 
nano-sized α-Al and Al-based binary and/or ternary intermetallic compounds [13 , 14 ]. 
Nanocrystalline Al shows low ductility because of the lack of dislocation multiplication and 
accumulation, as grain-boundary-controlled dislocation activity rapidly gives rise to a dynamic 
equilibrium, thereby strain hardening is absent leading to plastic instability [15 ]. A compromise can 
be achieved at submicron grain size, as ultrafine-grained metals following the Hall-Petch relationship 
present a better balance between strength and ductility [16 ]. Yttrium locates at the same main group 
as Sc in the periodic table and the two elements show similar chemical properties. One can expect 
that an Al alloy containing Y and/or other low diffusivity elements would resist to particle 
coarsening, and yielding higher recrystallization temperatures. Indeed, a former study has 
demonstrated that Al-Y-Ni-Co alloys have potential for high temperature applications [17 ]. 
In this study, bulk ultrafine-grained Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 was synthesized by powder 
metallurgy (PM) from partially-amorphous powder precursors. The alloy shows good mechanical 
properties at both ambient and elevated temperature. In particular, the tensile strength at elevated 
temperature is superior to most high temperature aluminum alloys, while the room temperature 
Young’s modulus, yield strength and elongation reach 90 GPa, 500 MPa and 5%, respectively. The 
mechanisms regarding strength and thermal stability of this alloy are discussed. 
 
2. Experimental 
Gas-atomized Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 (at. %) powder (mass median diameter of 19 µm, Fig. 1(a)) 
was used as the starting material. Thermal stability of the as-atomized powder was studied by 
differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Perkin-Elmer DSC 7) at a heating rate of 40 K/min under a 
continuous flow of purified argon. The powder was consolidated by uniaxial hot pressing followed 
by extrusion. Hot pressing was performed under argon atmosphere at different temperatures (683,

723 and 773 K) using a pressure of 640 MPa and a holding time of 10 min. Hot extrusion of the hot-
pressed billets was carried out at the same temperatures used for hot-pressing using an extrusion ratio 
of 9:1. The phases of the samples were identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Panalytical 
X’pert Pro diffractometer with Co K α radiation source operating at 40 kV and 40 mA. Quantitative 
phase analysis by Rietveld method was conducted using the GSAS program [18 ] utilizing a Pseudo-
voigt profile peak shape function. For this, LaB 6 standard powder was measured to correct the 
diffractometer . The Young´s modulus was measured using a computer-controlled ultrasonic pulser-
receiver (Olympus 5900PR). The microstructure was investigated by scanning electron microscope 
(SEM, Zeiss Gemini 1530) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai G2 F20, 
acceleration voltage 200 kV). Microhardness tests were carried out using a Shimadzu HMV-2000 
microhardness tester with a load of 100 g and holding time of 10 s. All aforementioned 
characterizations were performed on the cross-section perpendicular to the extrusion direction. Room 
temperature tensile tests were conducted on cylindrical dog-bone shape specimens with gauge length 
of 12.5 mm and diameter of 2.5 mm using an Instron 5869 mechanical testing facility. Tensile tests 
at elevated temperature were performed on samples with 2.5 mm diameter and 25 mm gauge length 
using an MTS C45.305 electromechanical testing system. Before the tests, the specimens were 
heated to the specified temperature then hold for at least 10 min to avoid temperature gradient. Both 
room and high temperature tensile tests were conducted with an initial strain rate of 1×10 -4 s-1 and 
the strain was measured directly on the samples using an extensometer.

3. Results and discussion 
The isochronal DSC scan of the gas-atomized Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 powders (Fig. 1(b)) shows 
two exothermic peaks associated to the crystallization of the glass. The onset temperature of the first

and second exothermic crystallization peaks are T x1 = 600 K and T x2 = 640 K, which can be 
interpreted as formation of Al 19 (Ni, Co) 5Y3 and Al 3Y phase respectively (see XRD results in Fig. S1). 
Fig. 2(a) shows the XRD patterns of the gas-atomized Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 powders and alloys 
synthesized at different temperatures. The gas-atomized powders exhibit sharp Bragg peaks 
corresponding to fcc Al superimposed on a broad maximum characteristic of amorphous materials at 
2θ = 40 - 50º, indicating the coexistence of crystalline and glassy phases. Al-based metallic glasses 
are marginal glass formers [19 ] and the formation of α-Al nanocrystals are observed especially for 
alloys with high Al content. The amorphous phase is fully crystallized after hot pressing and 
extrusion, presenting same phases as in the devitrified powders. Additionally, the consolidation 
temperature does not affect the types of intermetallic phase. The inset SEM image in Fig. 2(a) shows 
a representative microstructure of the hot extruded Al alloy, presenting a large number of acicular 
intermetallics (light gray contrast) with nanometer or submicron size.

intermetallic was formed, as Co atoms were dissolved into Al 19 Ni 5Y3 phase (Fig. 4(f)). This aspect is 
thought to be beneficial to the high temperature mechanical properties as will be discussed later.

their solid solubility limitations in the principal component. Microstructures with higher volume 
fraction of second phase can be obtained from devitrification than from the precipitation of Al solid 
solutions, such as in conventional heat-treatable Al alloys [22 ]. The influence of temperature on the 
grain size of α-Al is limited. One can find that in many cases, the boundaries of the Al grains are 
heavily decorated with intermetallic phases, indicating that the motion of Al grain boundary is 
strongly inhibited.

Figs. 6(a) and (b) show the elevated temperature tensile properties of the Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 
alloy synthesized at 773 K. At both 473 K and 623 K testing temperatures, the Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 
alloy shows ultimate tensile strength comparable or superior to Al alloys or composites for high 
temperature applications, suggesting high mechanical and thermal stability. Moreover, most Al 
alloys and composites display strain-softening at high temperatures [25 , 26 , 28 , 29 , 31 ], while the 
current alloy shows rather pronounced work-hardening ability (inset in Fig. 6(a)); this aspect can be 
attributed to the high density of Al 19 (Ni, Co) 5Y3 crystals and their sluggish coarsening rate [23 , 35 ], 
thus the dislocation gliding and climbing can still be inhibited to a large extent. Fig. 6(c) shows the 
fracture surface of Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 alloy after tensile test at 473 K, displaying a large number of 
small dimples mingle with a few larger ones, and intermetallic particles in the bottom of the dimples. 
This suggests a fracture resulting from void nucleation, growth and coalescence [36 ]. At higher 
testing temperature, the size of the dimples becomes more uniform and the depth also increases (Fig. 
6(d)).

It has been suggested that the Al 19 Ni 5Y3 phase is probably metastable [37 , 38 ]. Therefore, the 
Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 alloy synthesized at 773 K was annealed at 623 K for 48 h to investigate the 
phase stability. The XRD patterns in Fig. 7(a) indicate no additional phase formed in the annealed 
alloy compared with the as-extruded one. The stability of the Al 19 Ni 5Y3 phase at high temperatures

has also been demonstrated by recent in-situ X-ray diffraction studies showing that this phase can 
exist even in the α-Al melt [14 ]. Grain size of α-Al after 48 h annealing was analyzed as well (by 
XRD Rietveld method), indicating limited growth to 413 ± 35 nm. Gordillo et al. [35 ] proposed a 
grain growth model in Al-TM-RE systems: it suggests the fcc Al grains formed during 
consolidation/devitrification process are distributed between the intermetallic phases (that is, mainly 
Al 19 (Ni, Co) 5Y3 plates for current alloy), if the Al grain size at this stage is less than the interparticle 
distance, the grains will firstly grow until the Al domains are essentially single grains, while further 
Al growth requires coarsening of intermetallic plates. High temperature tensile strength is for a short-
term load, one may wonder the performance of an ultrafine-grained alloy under long-term load (i.e. 
the creep resistance). Conventional wisdom suggests a degradation of creep resistance with large 
reduction in grain size for pure metals, due to an increase in the volume fraction of grain boundaries, 
which lack long-range crystalline order and lead to processes like diffusional creep, sliding and 
rotation [39 ]. The creep behavior of the current alloy was not investigated and this should be a future 
work. Instead, the Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 alloy synthesized at 773 K was annealed under no external 
load. Fig. 7(b) shows the room-temperature hardness after static annealing at 623 K for different 
periods of time. The microhardness of Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 alloy fluctuates slightly around 150 HV, 
suggesting that the intermetallic phases have negligible coarsening at (or below) this temperature and 
high thermal stability of the alloy. In contrast, a naturally-aged Al2024 alloy loses around half of the 
strength after only a short time of annealing. 
 Golumbfskie et al. [
17 , 34 ] used spray forming to prepare Al-Y-Ni-Co alloys for high 
temperature applications (see Fig. 6(b)). They suggested that an optimum composition would be 
situated between Al 93.1 Y3.4 Ni 2.5 Co 1.0 and Al 86.5 Y6.6 Ni 4.8 Co 2.1 (at.%), corresponding to a content of 
alloying elements ranging from 15 to 30 wt.%. The former produces large elongation at ambient 
temperature while the later provides sufficient high temperature strength. The present 
Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 alloy has a total amount of Y, Ni and Co of 22 wt.%, which is exactly in the 
middle of the range predicted by Golumbfskie et al. The present alloy exhibits both ambient and 
elevated temperature strength higher than the Al 86.5 Y6.6 Ni 4.8 Co 2.1 alloy and maintains moderate room 
temperature ductility. This can be attributed to the significant amount of binary intermetallic phases 
(Al 3Y and Al 9Co 2) formed in Al 86.5 Y6.6 Ni 4.8 Co 2.1 alloy. Binary phases have notably higher coarsening 
rate than the (pseudo-) ternary phase in both Golumbfskie’s and this work (see equiaxed phases in 
Fig. 3(c)), and spray forming may result in an increased porosity of the materials. In other words, the 
binary phases are suggested to be suppressed or kept to a minimum for better thermal stability. A 
recent study proved that phase formation from crystallization of the glassy phase and from liquid

4. Conclusions 
A high-temperature Al-Y-Ni-Co alloy was synthesized by powder metallurgy using glass 
powder precursors. The resultant Al 90.4 Y4.4 Ni 4.3 Co 0.9 alloy can be regarded as an aluminum matrix 
composite reinforced with ~30 vol.% in-situ intermetallic phases. With increasing the processing 
temperature up to 773 K, the size of the intermetallic phases gradually increases along with an 
interparticle distance increment. The alloy synthesized at 773 K exhibits low density, high strength 
and Young´s modulus and ~5 % tensile ductility at room temperature, and the high temperature 
strength is comparable or superior to the best high-temperature aluminum alloys.
