ABSTRACT
An amorphous Zr 58.6Al15.4Ni20Co6 foil was fabricated and applied as the ﬁller metal to vacuum braze TiAl- and
Ni-based alloys. The obtained joint has high strength both at room temperature (RT) and high temperature (HT).
To investigate the eﬀ ect of the brazing temperature and holding time on the joint strength, experiments were
performed at di ﬀerent temperatures and held for di ﬀerent time. Five di ﬀerent regions derived from the inter-
facial atomic di ﬀusion and crystallization of the amorphous ﬁller metal were formed after brazing. The energy
dispersive spectrometer (EDS) results indicated that su ﬃcient interdi ﬀusion occurred between the ﬁller metal
and base alloys after brazing. As a result, ﬁne phases that were di ﬀerent from the base alloys were formed and
dispersively distributed in the joint. Shear tests were performed at 30 ℃ and 600℃ and the results show that the
joint strength ﬁrst increased and then decreased with increases in holding time and heating temperature. The
maximum shear strength of the joint at 30 ℃ and 600 ℃ were 353 MPa and 214 MPa respectively.
1. Introduction
The development of advanced industries such as aerospace and
nuclear power has placed increasing demands on structural materials
[1,2]. One of the most important requirements is the heat resistance of
the material, such as the heat resistance at high temperature (strength
under sustained loading and creep limit) and heat stability (anti-oxi-
dation and corrosion resistance). High-temperature alloys such as TiAl-
and Ni-based alloys have been developed to satisfy this requirement
[3–5]. The Ni-based superalloys have high strength, good plasticity,
excellent oxidation and corrosion resistance, and have been widely used
in the high-temperature and high-e ﬃciency parts [ 6–10]. It is said that
high-temperature superalloys take up approximately 70% in the engine.
The TiAl-based alloys have been considered as potential high-tem-
perature structural materials for their high speci ﬁc strength and ex-
cellent oxidation and creep resistance at high temperature [ 11,12].
More importantly, the density of TiAl-based alloys is very low and only
half that of the Ni-based superalloys. Partially substituting the Ni-based
alloys with TiAl-based alloys is an e ﬀective method to realize the
weight reduction and enhance the ﬂight e ﬃciency of the aircraft.
Among the numerous manufacturing methods, joining has been veri ﬁed
as an important technology for sustainable manufacturing and has been
successfully applied in the combination of the two alloys [ 13–15].
The joining of TiAl- and Ni-based alloys has always been a
challenging work for their large di ﬀerences in physical and chemical
properties [ 16–19]. For conventional fusion methods such as arc
welding, laser beam welding, friction welding, it was di ﬃcult to avoid
the quenching e ﬀect on the heat a ﬀected zone (HAZ), which would
cause the hot cracking in the joint, especially for the brittle materials
such as TiAl [ 20–22]. Di ﬀusion bonding(DB) and brazing are two
common methods to join the two alloys. For DB, to obtain a high-
strength joint, a high applied force is always required during joining,
and the requirement for the contacting surfaces is extremely high to
facilitate the atomic di ﬀusion. As a result, it is not appropriate to be
widely used in industry ﬁelds. Regarding the brazing method, the base
metals are not melted, whereas the ﬁller metals are melted during
brazing. Brazing is the most economical and feasible method and has
been successfully used to join titanium or its alloys [ 23–27]. Selecting
appropriate ﬁller metals played a key role in obtaining a satisfactory
joint. To satisfy the requirement of the aerospace industry for the high-
temperature performance, the joint brazed with the selected ﬁller
me-
tals should have excellent high-temperature strength.
In this work, an amorphous Zr 58.6Al15.4Ni20Co6 foil was fabricated
by melt-spinning and used as the ﬁller metal to join TiAl- and Ni-based
alloys. The high-temperature performance of the amorphous ﬁller metal
should be better because Zr, Ni, and Co have high melting tempera-
tures. In addition, compared to traditional crystalline ﬁller metals, the
non-equilibrium structure of the amorphous can facilitate the atomic

3.2. Microstructure of the brazed joint
Fig. 3 shows the SEM images of the joints brazed at 980 ℃, 1000℃ ,
and 1020 ℃ for 15 min. It can be seen from Fig. 3(a) that when the
brazing temperature is 980 ℃, the bonding is not good; there are un-
welded holes in the interfacial region of the TiAl-based alloy, which
form a short and ﬁne line. The main reason is that during the heating
process, Ti and Ni in the base alloys di ﬀuse into the ﬁller metal, which
increases the melting point. Therefore, the brazing requires a higher
temperature for the atomic di ﬀusion to obtain a sound joint. Fig. 3(b)
shows that when the temperature increases, the holes disappear, which
indicates that a su ﬃcient interdi ﬀusion occurs between the ﬁller metal
and the base alloys. The interface is wider than the initial state of the
amorphous ﬁller metal, and the joint is divided into the following ﬁve
regions: The TiAl-based di ﬀusion layer (DL), the TiAl-based crystal-
lization and reaction layer (CRL), the crystallization layer (CL), the Ni-
based crystallization and reaction layer (CRL) and the Ni-based di ﬀu-
sion layer (DL). When the temperature increases to 1020 ℃, the tem-
perature is too high, and because of the overheating, the grain size of
the newly formed phases is very large, as shown in Fig. 3(c), which is
detrimental to the joint strength. The holding time has a similar e ﬀect
on the microstructure of the joint to the joining temperature. Figs. 3(d)
and (e) show the joints brazed at 1000 ℃ and held for 5 min and 30 min,
respectively. As seen in Fig. 3 (d), when the holding time is 5 min, the
newly formed phases in the joint are small. The reaction layers in both
sides were very ﬁne. When the holding time was 30 min, a uniform
overgrowth was formed in the intermediate layer or the interfacial re-
gion as shown in Fig. 3(e).
Fig. 4 shows the EDS mapping of the joint brazed at 1000 ℃ for
15 min and it can be seen that after brazing, the principal elements Ti,
Al, Zr, Ni and alloying elements Co, Cr in the base metals and ﬁller
metal, are uniformly distributed in the joint area, indicating that the
inter-diﬀusion between the base materials and ﬁller metal was su ﬃ-
cient and no composition segregations.

magniﬁcations of the interfacial regions and intermediate region
marked with zones Ⅰ, Ⅱ,
and Ⅲ in Fig. 5(a) were examined using SEM.

3.3. Mechanical properties
The microhardness distribution across the di ﬀerent locations of the
joint was shown in Fig. 7. It reveals that the hardness in the base ma-
terials was lower than the reaction layers and the interlayer, which
could result from the precipitation of the ﬁne phases in the central
region.
Fig. 8 shows the shear strength of the joint tested at 30 ℃ and at
600℃. As observed from the picture, the two curves exhibited the same
trend. When the processed parameters were 980 ℃/15 min and 1000 ℃/
5 min, both room temperature and high-temperature shear strengths
were very low at only approximately 150 MPa, 180 MPa and 80 MPa,
140 MPa, respectively. The main reason is that the low brazing tem-
perature and short holding time will cause an insu ﬃcient reaction be-
tween the molten ﬁller metal and the base alloys. When the joining
temperature and holding time increased, a maximum shear strength
was approximately 353 MPa for the room temperature and 214 MPa for
600℃, which indicates that increasing the brazing temperature and
holding time can e ﬀectively facilitate the di ﬀusion of atoms and en-
hance the metallurgical reaction. However, further increasing the
brazing temperature and holding time did not increase the shear
strength of the joint as expected. When the joining parameters were
1020℃/15 min and 1000 ℃/30 min, the room-temperature and high-
temperature shear strength decreased to 175 MPa and 140 MPa and to
225 MPa and 180 MPa, respectively. Large phases resulted from over-
heating, and excessive holding time will deteriorate the joint.
The fracture mechanism was discussed in the fractographic analysis.
The fracture surfaces of the joint tested at room temperature are shown
in Fig. 9. Fig. 9(a– (c) show the fracture surfaces of the joints that were
brazed at di ﬀerent temperatures. Increasing the brazing temperature
made the fracture surface move from the interface of the Ni-based alloy
to the intermediate region and subsequently to the TiAl-based reaction
layer. The brazing temperatures obviously played a key role in the
metallurgical reaction and phase formation. When the temperature was
low, the atomic di ﬀusion between the ﬁller metal and the Ni-based
alloy was not su ﬃcient. A large faultage can be found in the fracture
surface in Fig. 9(a). When the temperature increased, the metallurgical
reaction between the ﬁller metal and the base alloys was enhanced. The
fracture occurred in the intermediate region. Several minor bright fa-
cets can be observed on the fracture surface as shown in Fig. 9(b).
Further increasing the brazing temperature coarsened the phases. Big
fracture facets can be observed in Fig. 9(c). All fractures exhibited a
typical brittle fracture pattern. The fracture morphologies of the joints
brazed at 1000℃ for diﬀerent
 time lengths were shown in Figs. 9(d) and
(e). The evolution of the fracture pattern versus brazing time was si-
milar to that of the brazing temperature. When the holding time was
5 min, a fracture appeared in the Ni-based alloys interface because of
insuﬃcient di ﬀusion. Herringbone patterns can be found on the frac-
ture surface, as shown in Fig. 9(d). When the brazing time increased to
30 min, because of the long isothermal solidiﬁ cation, the phases began
to coarsen. The formation of large phases accumulates residual stress in
the joint and induces the fracture.
Fig. 10 shows the fracture morphology of the joint tested at 600 ℃.
During heating, the chemical activities of elements Ti and Zr in the ﬁller
metal and TiAl-based alloy were enhanced, and they would absorb the
gases, which would make the joint brittle. For example, pure titanium
would absorb hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen when it was heated to
300℃, 400℃ and 600℃, respectively. Because of the high temperature,
the impurities di ﬀused to the intergranular boundary, weakened the
intercrystalline force and deteriorated the joint strength. Fig. 10(a) –(e)
show that the fracture exhibited a multisource cracking characteristic.
There were high-density short and curved tearing lines and several
bulky impurities in the fracture surfaces. The fracture also exhibited a
quasi-cleavage characteristic, and the fracture depth increased with the
increases in brazing temperature and holding time.
4. Discussion
The joining and phase formation process can be described as fol-
lows: In the initial stage, the temperature is low and oxide ﬁlms in the
base alloys are broken by the intermediate layer under the e ﬀect of the
applied force. The assembled samples obtain a preliminary contact from
the compression molding and creep of the ﬁller metal. When the tem-
perature increases, the atoms in the base alloys and ﬁller metal are
activated and enter a new equilibrium position through interfacial

melting point of the ﬁller metal, the ﬁller metal begins to melt; under
the eﬀect of the concentration gradient between the ﬁller metal and the
base metals, the elements of Zr, Al, Ni, Co di ﬀuse into the base alloys;
simultaneously, the Ti, Al and Ni elements also dissolve into the molten
ﬁller metal and metallurgically react with it. Finally, the joint is divided
into ﬁve di ﬀerent regions: The TiAl-side reaction layer, the TiAl-side
crystallization layer (RCL), the intermediate crystallization layer, the
Ni-side reaction layer and the Ni-side crystallization layer, as illustrated
in Fig. 11 . Because of the chemical compatibility (similar crystal
structures and atomic radii), Ti and Zr can be considered to be the same
elements. The successive di ﬀusion of Zr into the base metal dilutes the
Al concentration, which causes the transformation of γ-TiAl to α-Ti. In
region C, the increase in Al content facilitates the formation of various
aluminides and nickel compounds, such as AlNiZr, Ni
2Al3,Z r 3Al2, and
Ni3Ti. For region D, because of the slow atomic di ﬀusion in the Ni-base
alloy, the change in elements is very low. The phases in this region were
mainly Zr-Ni intermetallics.
The high strength of the joint at room temperature and high tem-
perature can be attributed to the following two strengthening me-
chanisms: ﬁne crystal reinforcing and dispersal reinforcing. During
solidiﬁcation, since the forces among the elements are di ﬀerent, mul-
tiple active elements in the joint increase the competition among the
phases. Therefore, orientated growth, which often occurs in the fusion
welding, is avoided. The newly formed phases in the joint are ﬁne and
irregular, which is bene ﬁcial to the joint. In addition, when the tem-
perature decreases, the solid solubility of the solid solution phases such
as γ-TiAl, γ-Ni is reduced. Some intermetallics such as Ti 3Al and Zr 2Ni
would precipitate from the solid solution phases and dispersively dis-
tribute in the joint, which induces a dispersal reinforcing e ﬀect at the
joint. Compared with the joint brazed using the Ti-Nb, Ti-Ni-Nb, TiNb-
and NiCr-based alloy, AgCuPd, BNi2, Ag-27.5Cu-12.5In-1.25Ti, 80 μm
Ti, 20 μm Cu as the ﬁller metal, the joint brazed using the amorphous
Zr-Al-Ni-Co ﬁller metal has a good shear strength at both room tem-
perature and high temperatures [ 13,32–35,36,37].
5. Conclusions
(1) TiAl and Ni-based alloys were successfully bonded using the
amorphous Zr
58.6Al15.4Ni20Co6 ﬁller metal. A sound joint with no
cracks and holes was obtained after brazing.
(2) The microstructure of the joint was determined by interfacial
atomic di ﬀusion among the molten ﬁller metal, the substrates and
the crystallization of the amorphous ﬁller metal. Fine phases
Ni2Al3,A l 2Zr3 and Ni 3Ti were formed through the two ways and
dispersed in the joint and it was bene ﬁcial to the joint.
(3) The shear tests showed that the maximum shear strength tested at
30℃ and at 600℃ were 353 MPa and 214 MPa when the heating
temperature and holding time were 1000 ℃ and 15 min, respec-
tively.
(4) The fracture of the joints exhibited a brittle fracture morphology.
When the brazing temperature and holding time increased, the
crack initiation varied from the interface of the Ni-based alloy and
ﬁller meal to the intermediate region.
