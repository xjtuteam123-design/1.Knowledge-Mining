Improved laser metal deposition (LMD) of nickel base superalloys by 
pyrometry process control 
 
Bernabe Carcel*, Jesus Sampedro, Irene Perez, Estrella Fernandez, Jose A. Ramos. 
 
AIDO, Nicolás Copérnico 7, 46980 Paterna (Spain) 
 
 
ABSTRACT 
Laser metal deposition (LMD) with high power lasers consists of manufacturing precise layers of materials by fusing 
metal powder with a laser beam over a substrate. Typical dilu tion of 5% allows metallurgical adhesion of the coating. 
This technique provides a uniq ue combination of high accuracy and low heat affecting zone which is attractive for 
processing high added value components such aeroengines. Nickel (Ni) base superalloys are widely used in aeroengines 
because of their high mechani cal properties when working at high temperat ures (creep). A repairing or manufacturing 
chain of these components by LMD requires a good understanding of many parameters; therefore process control plays 
an important role. This work is focused on the study of the LMD processing of a Ni base superalloy using two colour 
pyrometry for the process monitoring. Presented results sh ow how temperature and cooling rates of the LMD tracks 
affect the shape, microstructure and corrosion of the LMD coatings. 
 
LMD, laser cladding, pyrometry, superalloys, nickel, thin wall. 
 
 
1. INTRODUCTION 
 
Laser Metal Deposition (LMD) with powders is an additiv e repair / manufacturing technique suitable to provide 
corrosion and wear resistance to machine components, to repair high value parts like in aero engines, dies or moulds, and 
to process distortion vulnerable components 1. In this technique the laser beam melts the fed powder and a thin layer of 
the substrate simultaneously. Thus the melted powder and the base material are metallurgically welded with up to 100% 
density. Because the heat input is limited to the local area, which results from the good focusability of the laser beam, 
only very little heat is applied to the base material. As a result, a small heat affected zone (HAZ) and low dilution can be 
achieved. The metallic powder can be fed into the melt pool either laterally or coaxially to the laser beam. Coaxial 
deposition method is a more flexible process giving the possibility to clad multidirectional layers by depositing the 
powder homogeneously in the laser focus. A shielding gas is used (usually He or Ar) as carrier gas (powder feeding) and 
shielding gas (corrosion protection).The use of coaxial nozzles has open new application in direct building of 3D parts. 
As an example, LENS ® developed by The National Sandia Laboratories is based on this technology 2. There are many 
parameters (laser power, powder feeding rate, scanning speed, beam shape, surface roughn ess, shielding gas, etc.) 
affecting the final quality of the product. Therefore, a good understanding of the pr ocess is necessary in order to obtain 
defect free high quality parts. Monitoring the process with the use of temperature signals is useful for comprehending the 
process and to control the deposition in order to obtain improved results
3. Two-colour pyrometry is a noncontact 
technology suitable for the LMD monitoring of the melt pool temperature and thermal history of the deposited layers. 
Unlike 1 colour pyrometry, two-colour temperature measurement is independent of the emissivity, giving more accurate 
temperature values. 
 
During the last years, the aero engine industry has increased its interest in the LMD technique, and new manufacture and 
repair process chains have been developed
5. Nickel base alloys are widely used in aero engine components due to its 
high mechanical and corrosion resistance at high temperature 6. This study is focused on the LMD deposition of thin 
walls made of Inconel 718 using two-colour pyrometry for process monitoring. The effect of the substrate temperature, 
melt pool temperature and cooling rate on the geometry, surface corrosion and microstructure has been profusely studied.

2. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE 
 
The experiments were carried out with the setup shown in Fig. 1. LMD process has been performed by means of a 
Nd:YAG laser (wavelength 1064 nm) TRUMPH model HL1006D, with maximum output power of 1kW at the work 
piece. The beam is conveyed to a lens assembly of focal length 200 mm within a coaxial deposition head via an optical 
fibre with internal diameter 0.6 mm. This focused the beam to a circular spot with diameter 1,95 mm. and nominally 
Gaussian beam profile at the deposition point, which was positioned 12 mm beyond the end of the nozzle. The powder is 
delivered by a Sulzer Metco Twin 10C accurate powder feeder. Nozzl e system consists of a coaxial nozzle with three 
side beams running coaxially to the laser beam on the surface of the base material. Shielding gas control is integrated in 
the nozzle coaxially to the laser beam. Fo r the experiments described in this paper Helium has been used as carrier and 
shielding gas. Relative motion between nozzle and substrate is done by a X-Y-Z motion table controlled by CNC. 
 
For noncontact surface temperature mon itoring, a digital highly accurate tw o-color pyrometer (Impac IGAR 12-LO 
MB22) has been used. The temperature range for this equipment is 550-2500ºC and the spot size is 0.8 mm for an 
aperture of 17 mm,. The pyrometer measures in the 2-color principle (ratio principle) in which two adjacent wavelengths 
(1.28/1.65 µm) are used to calculate the temperature
4.. The temperature acquisition time was 10 ms. As it has been 
mentioned previously, main advantage of 2-color pyrometry is that temperature measurement is independent of the 
emissivity (of the object in wide ranges, it is unaffected by dust and other contaminants in the field of view, it is 
unaffected by dirty viewing windows 7 and the measuring object can be smalle r than the spot size. For an accurate 
measurement it is necessary to know the emissivity slope K (ratio ε1/ε2). From previous experimental calibration 
(temperature 600-900ºC), K=1.1425 was set as the emissivity slope value for In718. A Notch filter ( λ=1064nm) was also 
included in the system optics in order to prevent the damage of the pyrometer and to avoid noise measurement from the 
fundamental wavelength of the laser.

The temperature was measured in a fixed point of each layer, close to the middle of the track (Fig 2). Temperature data 
was converted into graphs with Microsoft Excel software. From these graphs the maximum interaction temperature and 
cooling rate of each layer were calculated. 
 
In order to analyze the structure of the deposited walls, resulting samples were prepared for metallographic 
characterization. Firstly, samples were cut transversely to the laser track by a Jeanwi rth cuto-20 cutting wheel, after 
cutting, the samples were hot mounted (Struers labopress-3) and finally grinded (mesh 120 – 1000) and polished with 
diamond paste to 1 μm. (Jeanwirth TG 200/1). Samples were etched with Marble reagent. Metallographic samples were 
then inspected by light microscopy (Nikon optiphot-M). Digital analysis image (Visilog v.5.3 and ImageJ 1.40G.) was 
performed in order to study the microstructure.

In general, when depositing LMD layers, their geometry is affected by the temperatur e of the substrate. As the 
temperature increases the energy absorption and the dilution of the clad increases as well, this way, LMD layers become 
wider while their height decreases. In these tests, as the inte rval time increases, the temper ature of the previous layers 
reduces. So, the shape of the subsequent LMD layers are affe cted in the way that the wall height increases with the 
interval time between layers. In turn, while the thickness in the middle of the wall (layer 5) remains constant for all the 
tests (value ≈ 1.8 mm), the thickness in top of the wall (layer 10) decreases with the interval time, from 2.1 mm for test 1

4. CONCLUSIONS 
 
LMD is a suitable technique for the additiv e repair of aeroengine components. Reconstruction of thin walls is one of the 
fields in which LMD offers high versatility. Nevertheless, the work carried out has proven the high dependence of the 
geometry, surface corrosion and microstructure with the temperature history of each wall’s layer. Four In718 walls have 
been deposited by LMD with the same processing parameters but different interval between layers (10). 2 colour-
pyrometry has been used to measure the temperature in the mi ddle of each track. From the results of this work we are 
able to conclude: 
 
o The maximum temperature (interaction temperature) increases for subsequent layers. Negligible effect was 
found of the interval time between layers. 
o The cooling rate decreases with the number of layers (height of the walls). For the top layers (9, 10) this 
cooling rate reaches a quasi asymptotic value. 
o The cooling rate is significantly faster as the interval time between layers increases. 
o The wall geometry is dependent on the interval time between layers. Wall height and wall thickness 
homogeneity increases with the interval time between layers. 
o The microstructure obtained is dendritic. In this wo rk, the inter-arm spacing is notably higher (35%) for 
continuous deposited wall (test 1) than for long interv al time (3600 s.) deposition (test 4). The arm spacing 
is directly related with the thermal history (cooling rate) of the wall.
