ABSTRACT
The influence of solution treatment temperature and cooling rate on the mechanical behavior of Ti60 alloy at 
600 ℃ was investigated. Five different solution treatment temperatures and five different cooling methods were 
used for the Ti60 alloy. The study revealed that, at the same solution treatment temperature the ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS) and elongation (Z%) of the water-quenched (WQ) microstructure were 32 % and 61 % higher, 
respectively, compared to the furnace-cooled (FC) microstructure. The WQ microstructure exhibited significantly 
superior strength and ductility. This phenomenon was primarily attributed to the differences in microstructural 
morphology caused by varying cooling methods. The effects of different cooling methods on high-temperature 
tensile behavior were discussed in terms of fracture morphology, high-angle grain boundaries, and void distri -
bution. On the other hand, the UTS of the air-cooled (AC) microstructure initially increased and then decreased 
with decreasing solution treatment temperature, while the UTS of the FC microstructure decreased with 
decreasing solution treatment temperature. This phenomenon was mainly due to the competing effects of 
effective slip length (colony size) and elemental distribution. The correlation between solution treatment tem -
perature and cooling rate parameters and high-temperature mechanical properties was established using the 
response surface methodology (RSM). The optimized solution cooling scheme was determined to be at 1012 ℃ 
with a cooling rate of 4238 ℃ /min.
1. Introduction
Near- α titanium alloy Ti60, known for their excellent strength-to- 
weight ratio and high-temperature performance, was widely used in 
high-temperature structural components of aerospace engines [1,2] . The 
high-temperature tensile properties were crucial for components oper -
ating under extreme working conditions [3,4] . Near- α titanium alloys 
such as IMI834, Ti-1100, BT25y and Ti60 alloy exhibit high tensile 
strength and fatigue life at 600 ℃ [5 – 11] . The tensile properties of Ti60 
alloy was greatly influenced by their microstructure, which can be 
effectively controlled through thermomechanical processing to optimize 
performance [12,13] . Solution treatment temperature and cooling rate 
were two critical parameters in the heat treatment of titanium alloys 
[14,15] . Therefore, studying the effects of solution and cooling methods 
on the high-temperature mechanical behavior of the Ti60 alloy was very 
important.
The solution treatment temperature could cause diffusion among 
certain alloying elements and homogenizing the microstructure, which 
significantly affects the phase distribution, grain size, and tensile 
properties of the alloy. Sun et al. found in their study on the Ti60 alloy 
that the solution treatment temperature significantly affects the phase 
distribution and grain size within the alloy, thereby influencing its 
tensile properties [16] . Similar phenomena were observed in Ti-6Al-4V 
and IMI834 alloys [17,18,22] . On the other hand, the solution treatment 
temperature affects the stability of the α / β phases. Studies on 
Ti-15V-3Cr-3Sn-3Al and Ti-15Mo-2.7Nb-3Al-0.2Si alloys found that: 
Higher solution treatment temperatures promote the stabilization of the 
β phase, leading to the precipitation of fine lamellar α ( α
L
) phase upon 
cooling, thereby enhancing strength and hardness. Conversely, lower 
solution treatment temperatures favor the formation of the α phase, 
improving the alloy ’ s ductility and toughness [19,20] . In summary, the 
solution treatment temperature influences the initial microstructure of 
titanium alloys such as Ti60, thereby affecting tensile properties such as 
strength and plasticity. When the temperature exceeds the phase

4. Discussions
4.1. Explanation of the effect of cooling rate on high temperature 
mechanical behavior
At the same solution treatment temperature, the tensile strength of 
the specimens decreased with a reduction in cooling rate. Among the 
deformation mechanisms — slip, twinning, and crystal plane slip, the slip 
mechanism was the most significant for high-temperature deformation 
of titanium alloys, as it involves the transmission of dislocations [32] . 
The transmission and accumulation of dislocations affect the material ’ s 
strength and plasticity. The Burgers orientation relationship allows slip 
to pass through α or β grains [33] . Fig. 14 illustrated the statistical 
analysis of high-angle grain boundary content for specimens that have 
not undergone deformation and those subjected to high-temperature 
deformation, all at a solution treatment temperature of 1015 ℃ under 
different cooling rates. It was observed that the content of high-angle 
grain boundaries increased with the cooling rate in both undeformed 
and deformed conditions. During deformation, approximately 50 % of 
high-angle grain boundaries transformed into low-angle grain bound -
aries. High-angle grain boundaries, due to their higher boundary energy, 
significantly impede dislocation transmission, causing dislocations to 
accumulate at these boundaries and leading to an increase in geomet -
rically necessary dislocation density (GND density), as indicated by the 
red line in Fig. 14 , which showed higher GND density at higher cooling 
rates. The accumulation of dislocations results in higher material 
strength. On the other hand, with increasing cooling rates, the width of 
the α
L 
phase significantly decreased. Under water quenching conditions, 
the structure exhibited a disordered α
L 
phase morphology without col -
onies, effectively hindering dislocation slip and thus contributing to 
higher tensile strength of the alloy [34] .
Under high-temperature conditions, specimens subjected to high 
cooling rates exhibited better plasticity. This phenomenon can be 
attributed to two factors: 1) High- temperatures reduced the critical 
resolved shear stress (CRSS) of slip systems, which facilitated the acti -
vation of "hard" slip systems that were difficult to initiate at room 
temperature. The increase in the number of slip systems led to improved 
ductility in the alloy [35] . 2) The initiation and distribution of voids 
played a significant role. At elevated temperatures, the fracture mode of 
the alloy primarily involved the coalescence of voids to form cracks, 
ultimately leading to specimen failure [36] . Uniformly distributed voids 
help to evenly distribute stress within the alloy, preventing localized 
stress concentrations and thereby enhancing the alloy ’ s ductility.
For Ti60 alloy, there are four slip systems: Basal < a >< 1 210 > (0001), 
Prismatic < a >< 1 210 > {10 10}, I-Pyramidal < a >< 1 210 > {01 11} and II- 
Pyramidal < c + a >< 2 113 > { 2112} [37,38] . At room temperature, the 
I-Pyramidal and II-Pyramidal slip systems have higher CRSS, making 
them more difficult to activate. Consequently, the prismatic slip system 
predominantly governs deformation at room temperature [39] . For 
specimens deformed at high-temperature(600 ℃ ) with a solution treat -
ment temperature of 1015 ℃ and different cooling rates, the average 
Schmid factor (SF) values for the four slip systems were statistically 
analyzed, as shown in Fig. 15 . It can be observed that under 
high-temperature conditions, each slip system exhibits a high SF value, 
increasing the likelihood of activation and resulting in good plasticity 
under such conditions. The activation probability of the I-Pyramidal and 
II-Pyramidal slip systems was greater than that of the Basal and Pris -
matic slip systems across different cooling rates, indicating their domi -
nant role in high-temperature deformation. However, the overall SF 
values for the I-Pyramidal and II-Pyramidal slip systems did not vary 
significantly with different cooling rates, suggesting that the activation 
of slip systems was not the primary reason for the improved plasticity of 
specimens subjected to high cooling rates under high-temperature

As described in Section 3.3 , under low cooling rates, voids are pri -
marily distributed at the α / β and α / α grain boundaries. This distribution 
can be attributed to the accumulation of significant stress at these 
boundaries during stress transfer, which results in localized stress con -
centration and facilitates void nucleation [36,40,41] . As tensile strain 
increases, larger voids are formed to accommodate the coordinated 
deformation. Additionally, dislocations can climb along the grain 
boundary cavities, creating favorable conditions for void coalescence 
and crack formation. In contrast, for specimens subjected to high cooling 
rates, the high cooling rates suppress the nucleation and growth of the α 
phase at grain boundaries [42] . Consequently, voids are mainly located 
within the α
e

α
L

concentration. This results in smaller void sizes and a random distri -
bution, making it more difficult for voids to coalesce into cracks. Thus, 
the size and distribution of voids are the primary reasons for the 
improved plasticity of specimens subjected to high cooling rates under 
high-temperature conditions.
4.2. Explanation of effect of solution treatment temperature on high 
temperature mechanical behavior
Under slower cooling rates, different solution treatment treatment 
temperatures result in distinct microstructural features such as lamellar 
microstructure (LM), duplex microstructure (DM), and equiaxed 
microstructure (EM). In the context of this study, under FC conditions, it 
was observed that at 1045 ℃ (the β -transus temperature), the micro -
structure exhibited the LM feature. At 1030 ℃ , the volume fraction of the 
α
e 
phase was approximately 28 %, corresponding to a DM feature. At 
985 ℃ , the volume fraction of the α
e 
phase was about 85 %, indicating 
an EM feature. Therefore, this range of solution treatment temperatures 
effectively reflects the evolution of microstructural morphology under 
slow cooling conditions. Under moderate cooling rates, different solu -
tion treatment temperatures led to microstructural features comprising 
both LM and DM structures [15,43] . In the context of this study, under 
AC conditions, at 985 ℃ , the α
e 
phase content was 46 %, resulting in a 
DM feature. This range of solution treatment temperatures effectively 
captures the evolution of microstructural morphology under moderate 
cooling rates.
For the LM, the primary factor affecting its tensile properties is the 
size of the colonies. In the case of the DM, both the size of the α colonies 
and the solute element distribution play significant roles [43,44] . The 
size of the colonies limits the effective slip length within them; shorter 
slip lengths can effectively impede dislocation transmission [39] . On the 
other hand, the colony size also restricts the effective slip length parallel 
to the grain boundaries. The tensile ductility is primarily related to the 
resistance to crack nucleation [45,46] , which is largely dependent on the 
effective slip length parallel to the grain boundaries. A reduction in 
colony size can increase the resistance to crack nucleation, resulting in 
improved ductility.
During the slip transmission process, when adjacent α
L 
phases 
exhibit the same grain orientation, slip can smoothly traverse the 
retained beta layer with almost no change in direction. However, when 
there is a significant difference in the orientation of adjacent grains, slip 
transmission terminates [33] . Thus, for LM, the size of regions with the 
same grain orientation (referred to as colonies) can be approximately 
considered as the effective slip length. During the post-processing of 
EBSD data, Band Contrast maps can effectively identify areas composed 
of grains with identical orientations. Therefore, this study utilized EBSD 
technology to accurately measure the sizes of the colonies. Selected 
three regions of 600 × 400 μ m each on the specimens, spaced 1 mm 
apart, and statistically analyzed the colony sizes within these regions, 
the average value was then calculated. Table 3 summarizes the effects of 
solution treatment temperature on colony size and α

decreased with decreasing solution treatment temperature, which led to 
an increase in both the UTS and TYS of the alloy.

5. Conclusion
This study primarily investigated the effects of solution treatment 
temperature and cooling rate on the high-temperature mechanical 
behavior of Ti60 alloy. The research also investigated the reasons behind 
different mechanical behaviors caused by various combinations of so -
lution treatment temperatures and cooling rates. The main conclusions 
of this study are as follows: 
1. Both the strength and ductility of the Ti60 alloy improved signifi -
cantly with increasing cooling rates under high-temperature 
conditions. The effect of cooling rate on ductility was mainly related 
to the size and distribution of voids. Under FC conditions, voids were 
predominantly located at grain boundaries, which facilitates crack 
propagation and results in reduced ductility.
2. The solution treatment temperature affected the high-temperature 
mechanical strength by altering both the effective slip length and 
the distribution of solute elements, the influence degree that affected 
by the cooling rate. At higher cooling rates, as the solution temper -
ature decreased, the dominant factor shifted from effective slip 
length to the solute element distribution. Meanwhile, at lower 
cooling rates, the solute element distribution consistently played the 
dominant role.
3. The optimized solution treatment temperature was 1012 ℃ and 
cooling rate of 4238 ℃ /min, achieved the best balance of strength 
and ductility for the Ti60 alloy under high-temperature work 
conditions.
