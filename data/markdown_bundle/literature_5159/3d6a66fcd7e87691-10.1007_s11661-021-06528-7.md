Revealing the Precipitation Sequence with Aging
Temperature in a Non-equiatomic AlCoCrFeNi High
Entropy Alloy
VICKEY NANDAL, BUSHRA HARUN, R. SARVESHA, SUDHANSHU S. SINGH,
E.-WEN HUANG, YAO-JEN CHANG, AN-CHOU YEH, JAYANT JAIN,
and SURESH NEELAKANTAN
Sequential transformation in precipitate structure from L1
2 ﬁ B2-NiAl precipitate has been
observed for Al 0.5Co1.5CrFeNi1.5 alloy with the increase in aging temperature. At 650 /C176 C, the
FCC matrix contained L1 2 precipitates ( c¢), which were transformed into ordered B2 structure
precipitate at 750 /C176 C. A coarser and more stable B2-NiAl rich precipitate has been observed for
higher aging temperature (850 /C176 C). This transformation is attributed to the higher thermody-

temperatures.

HIGH entropy alloys (HEAs) are the emerging class
of multicomponent alloying systems mainly based on
the concept of equiatomic or non-equiatomic elemental
compositions.
[1] Among the various types, the face-cen-
tered cubic (FCC)-based CoCrFeNi system has gained a
lot of attention due to its wide-range properties. [2,3]
However, they lack in strength, although they exhibit
good ductility. [4] Precipitation hardening signiﬁcantly
improves its strength and the addition of Al signiﬁcantly
enhances its precipitation hardening behavior.
[2] The
eﬀects of Al addition in the CoCrFeNi system have
mostly been explored in terms of precipitate formation
and the resulting eﬀect on mechanical properties that
arise ( i.e., hardness and tensile strength).
[5,6] The
mechanical properties of the Al xCoCrFeNi system have
been found to be signiﬁcantly improved by precipitate
phases (i.e., L1
2 and B2).[2] It is worth noting that in the
AlxCoCrFeNi system, a low Al content ( x < 11 at. pct)
results in the formation of L1 2 (c¢) + B2 structured
precipitates and also the FCC ( c) matrix.[7] Studies have
reported that in Al xCo1.5CrFeNi1.5Tiy HEA, the trans-
formation of precipitate structure from L1 2 to L2 1 has
been observed and ﬁnally to stable B2 structure with
increase in Al and decrease in Ti concentrations. [8]
Firstly, most of the published research [9–11] in the
literature mainly focused on Al addition in the
equiatomic CoCrFeNi-base system. In contrast, the
current study focuses on Al addition in the non-e-
quiatomic Co
1.5CrFeNi1.5 system and its precipitation
characteristics as a function of aging temperature and
time. The main variation in the current system is that the
FCC phase remains relatively stable due to non-e-
quiatomic proportions, resulting in a distinct precipita-
tion behaviour. Secondly, a systematic understanding of
phase transformation/evolution as a function of aging
temperature and aging time has not been attempted,
even in the few publications that discuss non-equiatomic
compositions.
[12,13]
For instance, Dasari et al .[12] investigated the cold
rolling and annealing behavior of Al 0.5Co1.5CrFeNi1.5
alloy. They revealed that heterogeneous nucleation of
the B2 phase increased yield strength and ductility.
Nonetheless, there has been no systematic investigation
of phase transformation in the as-cast alloy as a function
of aging temperature and time. The inﬂuence of ther-
momechanical processing on phase change of
Al
0.3CoCrFeNi alloy was explored by Gwalani et al .[13]
Their ﬁndings show that at 620 /C176 C: 50 h, L1 2 precipitates
form and that the B2 phase pins grain boundaries.
However, the change of precipitate phases as a
function of aging temperatures, time and their transfor-
mation sequence, as well as related mechanisms, for

As-cast ingot (60 9 10 9 5m m 3) was prepared using
vacuum arc melting in the form of button. The chemical
composition (in at. pct) of the ingot button sample was
Al
9Co27.3Cr18.2Fe18.2Ni27.3 (in atomic ratio
Al0.5Co1.5CrFeNi1.5), commonly known as Al 05 alloy.
The nominal chemical composition of the ingot was
determined using an optical emission spectrometer
(OES) and validated in scanning electron microscopy
(SEM) using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy
(EDS). During melting, the Ti getter was used to absorb
residual O
2 and N 2. The arc melted ingot was ﬂipped
and re-melted four times before being dropped into the
copper casting section to improve the chemical homo-
geneity of the produced alloy. After solutionizing the
alloy at 1150 /C176 C for 6 hours and water quenching, the
samples were subjected to subsequent aging treatment at
650 /C176 C, 750 /C176 C, and 850 /C176 C. It should be mentioned that
the solutionization temperature and aging temperatures
was chosen from References 8 and 12 for the same alloy
composition. The analysis has been primarily carried
out in sample conditions of 50 and 240 hours. The aging
curve has been obtained previously.
[14] The samples for
SEM (JEOL, JSM-7100F) were chemically etched in
aqua regia containing one part of HNO
3 and three parts
of HCl to reveal the microstructural characteristics such
as precipitates and grain boundaries. The electron
backscattered diﬀraction (EBSD) was performed at an
accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a probe current of ~
15 nA. The EBSD scans were performed with a step size
of 7 lm. The TEM images were obtained with an
accelerating voltage of 200 kV using FEI Tecnai T20.
The samples for TEM were prepared in an electrolytic
solution of 10 pct perchloric acid in ethanol at /C0 30 /C176 C
at a voltage of 30 V using the twin-jet electropolishing
equipment (Struers Tenupol-5). The TEM–EDS spectra
of alloy was acquired on a TEM (JEM-1400, JEOL,
Japan) equipped with an EDS. TKD along with EDS
was carried out using EBSD detector (Oxford, Nordlys)
and EDS detector (Oxford, X-max50), respectively. For
TKD scanning, probe current and voltage were main-
tained at ~ 13 nA and 20 kV, respectively. The XRD
(Rigaku, Ultima IV model) measurements were per-
formed with Cu K a radiation operated at 40 kV and 40
mA between 30 deg and 100 deg (2 h) with a scanning
rate of 0.5 deg/min.

It should be noted that the L1 2 and B2-NiAl
precipitates are both rich in Al and Ni. [18] As shown in
Table I, the very negative mixing enthalpy of the Al–Ni
atomic pair oﬀers suﬃcient thermodynamic driving
force when compared to the Al–Co, Al–Cr and Al–Fe
atomic pairs. In addition, since Al has a higher atomic
radius than other elements present in the alloy (see
Table I), it causes lattice strain in the surroundings.
Thus, Al may prefer to segregate itself to relieve the
strain and lower the free energy of the system.
[18] The
enthalpy of formation, elemental diﬀusion in alloys and
interfacial energy required for nucleation can all be used
to explain the formation of L1 2 precipitate and its
transformation to B2-NiAl. Enthalpies of formation for
B2-NiAl (BCC-type) are nearly 20 kJ/mol higher than
Ni
3Al (L1 2).[19] Therefore, the B2-NiAl phase is more
stable thermodynamically than the Ni 3Al phase.
