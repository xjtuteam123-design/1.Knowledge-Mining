1. Introduction
Mar-M247 nickel-based superalloy is extensively used in the
hot sections of aircraft engines, which experience service tempera-
tures exceeding 600 ◦C and high stresses as demonstrated (Liu et al.,
2008). Smid et al. (2016) studied the high temperature creep frac-
ture properties of Mar-M247 and reported its excellent oxidation
resistance and mechanical properties at high temperature. Joining
of this alloy is widely applied to reduce the cost of repair processes
as stated (Steuer and Singer, 2013). Three different industrial meth-
ods of joining (fusion welding, brazing and diffusion bonding) are
used for materials joining as demonstrated (Lee et al., 1998). High
sensitivity to fusion zone and heat affected zone (HAZ) cracking
during welding make fusion welding impractical to join precipita-
tion hardened nickel based alloys as proposed (Henderson et al.,
2004). Brazing leads to degradation of elevated temperature joint
properties, due to the involvement of a low melting point ﬁller
alloy. High pressure and long bonding time for diffusion bonding
processes make them quite expensive as stated (Egbewande et al.,
2010). Transient liquid phase (TLP) bonding, which can produce
near-ideal joints, is regarded as an optimum method for joining
nickel based superalloys as put forward (Wu et al., 2001).

Investigations concerning TLP bonding of nickel based super-
alloys have been documented in the literature. Jalilvand et al.
(2013b) found that continuous centerline eutectic phases resulting
from insufﬁcient isothermal solidiﬁcation consisted of gamma solid
solution, Cr-rich boride and Ni-rich boride. The increase in bond-
ing time led to decrease of athermally solidiﬁed zone width when
TLP bonded IN-738LC with Ni-B-Si-Cr-Fe ﬁller alloy. Pouranvari
et al. (2013) found that the microstructural evolution in joint areas
were controlled by solid state diffusion of B into the IN718 base
alloy when a Ni-Si-B interlayer was used. This study also proposed
that the microstructural evolution of athermally solidiﬁed zones
was governed by B diffusion at low temperature. Dissolution and
diffusion of base metal alloying elements took place at high temper-
ature in a TLP bonded GTD-111 nickel based alloy as demonstrated
(Pouranvari et al., 2009). Liu et al. (2011) reported that Cr-rich
boride CrB, Ni-rich M23B6, Cr-W rich M6C and M3B2 borides formed
in TLP bonded single crystal DD98 and polycrystalline K465 super-
alloys.
Previous investigations have primarily focused on TLP joining
of gamma prime strengthened nickel alloys using commercial ﬁller
alloys based on the Ni-Cr-Si-B systems. The compositions of result-
ing joints were dissimilar to those of the base materials. In the case
of Mar-M247, an appreciable amount of high melting point ele-
ments, including W, Co and Ta, was added to the matrix. Results
showed that the elevated temperature performance of Mar-M247
was highly dependent on these ingredients. An ideal interlayer
would not only be similar to the base metal in composition, but

microstructure and mechanical properties to the base metal.
The bonding parameters were systematically varied to inves-
tigate the inﬂuence of ﬁller composition and process parameters

polycrystalline Mar-M247 superalloy.

4. Discussion
4.1. Theoretical model of TLP bonding process
The TLP bonding process can be simpliﬁed by using binary sys-
tems to describe the most signiﬁcant phenomena. Assuming a
sandwich structure consisting of base metal A and ﬁller metal B,
the TLP bonding process can be divided into four stages: dissolution
of the ﬁller metal, homogenization of the liquid phase, isothermal
solidiﬁcation and homogenization of the bonded area. This sim-
pliﬁed approach has been demonstrated by MacDonald and Eagar
(1992).
In the ﬁrst stage, both base metal and ﬁller metal are in the
solid state and B begins to dissolve. Fick’s law can be applied with
the condition of unidirectional diffusion as proposed by Tuah-Poku
et al. (1988):

minutes and the diffusion process is too complex to be discussed
in this paper.
In the third stage, the width of liquid zone which consisted of
majority A and minority B atoms begins to narrow. The time, t3,
needed to complete the isothermal solidiﬁcation is controlled by
diffusion of solute B from the liquid to the solid phase. Fick’s second
law can also be applied to this stage. Several hours are needed for
complete isothermal solidiﬁcation.
In the last stage, the heat treatment time for homogenization,
t4, is considerably longer than for the bonding process. So the vast
majority of the time for TLP bonding is taken up by the third and
last stage.
As Fick’s law indicates in the ﬁrst and third stage, the solidiﬁ-
cation kinetics of the isothermal solidiﬁcation process satisﬁes the
parabolic relationship as demonstrated (Nakao et al., 1989).
4.2. Isothermal solidiﬁcation for nickel based superalloys
Unlike the simple base metal A and ﬁller metal B theoreti-
cal model described above, Nickel-based superalloys are actually
made up of many alloying elements. When considering the respec-
tive ﬁller alloys, TLP bonding can be a very complex interdiffusion
process. However, numerical approaches instead of mathematical
analytical methods can be applied to this situation as demonstrated
by Zhou and North (1998). Sheng et al. (2014) have proposed a prac-
tical model for Nickel based superalloys. In contrast to the standard
model in which the DAZ is free from precipitates, the DAZ and sub-
strate near the bond area actually contain many precipitates. The
kinetics of liquid/solid interface movement are governed by the
fastest diffusing element, in this case, boron, as proposed (Sinclair
et al., 2000).
In the ﬁrst stage, boron diffuses rapidly into the substrate and
is consumed in the substrate area near the liquid/solid interface.
After a short time, the concentration of boron is saturated since the
diffusion coefﬁcient of boron in nickel is large and the solubility
of boron in M247 superalloy is very low as demonstrated (Portnoi
et al., 1967). So the linear relationship between the width of the ASZ
and the square root of the bonding time from Fick’s law is expected.
Then in the second stage, boron forms ﬁne borides near the ISZ
and substrate interface. During this stage, the ISZ grows between
the liquid phase and DAZ. Gradually, the growth rate of the ISZ
decreases with the increase of boron in the substrate and the
diffusion distance of boron from the liquid phase to the remote
substrate.
Once the proportion of borides becomes larger and the substrate
more saturated, the boron is harder to diffuse across the ISZ and
DAZ, which leads to a slower growth rate of the ISZ.
In the last stage, boron’s diffusion across the ISZ controls the
growth velocity of the ISZ and when the boron reaches the DAZ, it
is consumed to form borides. The ASZ also has a linear relationship
with the square root of the bonding time even though the rate was
much slower than in the ﬁrst stage.
4.3. Microstructure evolution in the ASZ
The SEM ﬁgures show the microstructure of the ASZ to be a
combination of Ni solid solution, gamma prime, CrB, MC and other
borides. Ohsasa et al. (1999) used numerical modelling to calcu-
late the products of isothermal solidiﬁcation during TLP bonding
for a Nickel alloy using a Ni-Cr-B interlayer. They proposed that a
ternary eutectic reaction occurred at 997 ◦C during solidiﬁcation.

the MPD element from the interlayer into the gamma phase of

completion of the isothermal solidiﬁcation process.
In analysis of the ASZ, dendritic formation and solute parti-
tioning govern microstructure development as proposed (Cieslak
et al., 1988). When the solidiﬁcation direction is considered, the
microstructure of the ASZ can be understood. The gamma phase
growing from the solid/liquid interface is formed in the ﬁrst stage.
It grows as dendrites to form the gamma prime phase. In the next
stage, solute partitioning begins due to the differences in partition
coefﬁcients of the elements. The elements whose partition coef-
ﬁcient with Ni are <1, such as B, are rejected and diffuse to the
adjacent melt. Since the solubility of B in Ni is low, when gamma
prime is saturated with B, the formation of Ni-rich and Cr-rich
borides occurs.
4.4. Microstructure evolution in the DAZ
Extensive discrete second phase particles, such as borides and
carboborides have been observed in the DAZ of joints as reported
(Idowu and Chaturvedi, 2006). The morphology of the precipitates
in the DAZ suggests that they are not formed during solidiﬁcation
but during the bonding process. B-rich phases form in the DAZ as
the diffusion of boron from the bond centerline to the base metal
occurs. Since the solubility of B in the base metal is limited and
elements such as Cr, W, Co and Ta have a strong afﬁnity for B, other
Cr-W-rich borides and complicated carboborides form during the
TLP bonding process.
4.5. Mechanical properties
The hardness distribution can reﬂect the inﬂuence of secondary
phase particles on the mechanical properties of the joint area
as demonstrated (Bakhtiari and Ekrami, 2012). Before isothermal
solidiﬁcation is complete, the maximum value of hardness was
obtained in the ASZ since the bond centerline eutectics contain hard
brittle boride phases. The insufﬁcient diffusion of gamma prime
promoting and solid solution elements leads to a minimum value
in the ISZ. The Ni-Cr-Co-W-Ta-B interlayer contains elements like
W, Co and Ta, whose diffusion from the interlayer to the base alloy
leads to the enrichment of solid solution elements in the joint area.
This results in more hard and brittle W and Co-rich borides remain-
ing in the DAZ. The boride precipitates observed in the DAZ result
in its peak hardness compared to the low value of the ISZ. When
isothermal solidiﬁcation is complete, the differences in hardness
are removed and the bond area strength is much more homoge-
neous. The hardness value of the base alloy increases with holding
time, which can be attributed to the diffusion of solid solution ele-
ments (Cr, Co, W and Ta) from the bond centerline to the base
alloy.
The stress-strain curves of joints bonded at 1150 ◦C for 60 min
and 240 min after the tensile test are given in Fig. 7. The tensile
strength increases with longer holding times. The failure location
of both joints was in the bond centerline. From the fracture behav-
ior of the joints, it was found that, when isothermal solidiﬁcation
was not complete, little plastic deformation occurred. Fracture sur-
faces consisted of mixed ductile and brittle modes. However, when
isothermal solidiﬁcation was complete, obvious plastic deforma-
tion was observed. The solid solution strengthening elements like
W, Ta and Hf in the interlayer were resulted in hard, brittle parti-
cles near the bond centerline. The formation of B-rich intermetallics
in the DAZ of the joint played a signiﬁcant role in the process
of fracture initiation and propagation. This was conﬁrmed by the
observation of the Hf-rich boride on the fracture surface by analyz-
ing the XRD (Fig. 5).
To improve the joint homogeneity and tensile strength, opti-
mized bonding parameters as well as the proper post-bonding heat
treatment are needed. The discussion of the effect of the post-
bonding heat treatment will be made in the future.
5. Conclusion
TLP bonding of Mar-M247 nickel based superalloy was per-
formed successfully at 1150 ◦C using a Ni-Cr-Co-W-Ta-B interlayer.
A three-stage isothermal solidiﬁcation process for TLP bonding
Mar-M247 nickel-based superalloy was described. With increase
in bonding time, alloying elements diffused across the joint zone
and formed a solid solution and intermetallic compounds. Dendritic
formation and solute partitioning governed the microstructure
development. A maximum tensile strength of 443 MPa was
obtained for the bond made at 1150 ◦C for 240 min. It took 240 min
to produce complete isothermal solidiﬁcation. After complete
isothermal solidiﬁcation, microhardness of the joints was more
homogeneous. Enrichment of refractory elements in the Ni-Cr-Co-
W-Ta-B interlayer made it more difﬁcult to complete isothermal
solidiﬁcation. The eutectic products in the joint bonding region
formed hard and brittle intermetallic particles which reduced hard-
