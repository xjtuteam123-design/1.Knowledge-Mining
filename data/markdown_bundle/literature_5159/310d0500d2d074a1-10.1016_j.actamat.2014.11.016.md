Synthesis of a new tungsten-free c–c0 cobalt-based superalloy by tuning
alloying additions
S.K. Makineni, B. Nithin and K. Chattopadhyay ⇑
Department of Materials Engineering, Indian Institute of Science, Bangalore 560012, India
Received 21 September 2014; revised 6 November 2014; accepted 7 November 2014
Abstract—The paper presents the synthesis of a new class of c–c0 cobalt-based superalloy that is free of tungsten as an alloying addition. It has much
lower density and higher speciﬁc strength than the existing cobalt-based superalloys. The current superalloys have a base composition of Co–10Al
and are further tuned by the addition of a binary combination of molybdenum and niobium, with the optimum composition of Co–10Al–5Mo–2Nb.
The solvus temperature of the alloy (866 /C176C) can be further enhanced above 950 /C176C by the addition of Ni to give the form Co– xNi–10Al–5Mo–2Nb,
where x can be from 0 to 30 at.%. After heat treatment, these alloys exhibit a duplex microstructure with coherent cuboidal L1
2-ordered precipitates
(c0) throughout the face-centred cubic matrix ( c), yielding a microstructure that is very similar to nickel-based superalloys as well as recently devel-
oped Co–Al–W-based alloys. We show that the stability of the c0 phase improves signiﬁcantly with the nickel addition, which can be attributed to the
increase in solvus temperature. A very high speciﬁc 0.2% proof stress of 94.3 MPa g /C01 cm/C03 at room temperature and 63.8 MPa g /C01 cm/C03 at 870 /C176C
were obtained for alloy Co–30Ni–10Al–5Mo–2Nb. The remarkably high speciﬁc strength of these alloys makes this class of alloy a promising mate-
rial for use at high temperature, including gas turbine applications.

1. Introduction
The superalloys represent a special class of high-temper-
ature-resistant materials that can withstand severe load at
temperatures above 800 /C176C. In this class, the most commer-
cially successful alloys for applications in gas turbines are
nickel-based alloys. These are mainly used for making crit-
ical components, like turbine blades and selected parts of
hot sections of the engine [1–3]. The main reason for the
sustained high-temperature strength of these alloys is the
occurrence of a stable microstructure containing cuboidal
L1
2-ordered c0-Ni3(Al, Ti) coherent precipitates distributed
in a face-centred cubic (fcc) c-Ni matrix. They have a stable
interface with the matrix, having a low lattice mismatch
(<0.5%) that makes them resistant to coarsening [4–6].
For gas turbine applications, in addition to high-tempera-
ture strength, the alloy should have hot corrosion resis-
tance. At high temperatures, hot corrosion and oxidation
are the main mechanisms for faster material degradation
[7,8]. The eﬀect becomes more severe when a low quality
of fuel (that contains more impurities, including sulfur,
sodium and halides etc.) is used, typically in land-based
gas turbines in chemical, power and marine applications
[9–13]. For example, in the case of nickel-based superalloys,
liquid ﬂux – mainly halide sulfate – forms and dissolves the
protective oxide layers, leading to accelerated oxidation of
the alloy. This becomes even more severe when sulfur dif-
fuses into the alloy and forms sulﬁdes (mainly Cr
2S3,
NiS) [11,14,15]. To prevent hot corrosion, nickel-based
superalloys are used with special barrier bond coatings,
which are both expensive and technically challenging [16–
18]. Although, the protective coatings improve the resis-
tance to hot corrosion, they are still inadequate for long
exposure at high temperatures. Thus, challenges remain
to develop a new class of alloy with similar high-tempera-
ture strength but having superior oxidation and hot corro-
sion resistance.
Cobalt-based superalloys in general have the best hot
corrosion resistance in aggressive environments. These
alloys form continuous dense protective oxide scales, such
as CoO, Cr
2O3, SiO 2 and Al 2O3. These oxides are thermo-
dynamically more stable, slower growing and more adher-
ent to the surface compared to oxides formed on nickel-
based superalloys [19–21]. In addition, these alloys exhibit
better creep rupture properties above 1000 /C176C, combined
corrosion–wear resistance, weldability and thermal fatigue
resistance [22,23]. The mechanical strength of these alloys
is, however, lower than that of nickel-based superalloys,
which limits the applicability of these alloys in critical tur-
bine blade applications. These alloys are mainly strength-
ened by solid solution elements and metal carbides [24–
27]. Thus, there is an as yet unfulﬁlled requirement for an

alloy that will have both high-temperature strength and hot

Over the last decade, a number of reports on cuboidal
coherent L1 2-ordered precipitates in c-Co matrix in a Co–
Al–W system have been published following the paper by
Sato et al. [28]. An earlier report exists of superlattice
ordering of the Cu 3Au type in a Co–Al–W alloy [29]. These
have motivated diﬀerent groups to study diﬀerent aspects of
this class of alloy in recent times [30–34]. Unlike Ni 3Al,
Co3Al phase does not exist in the binary Co–Al phase dia-
gram [35], but the addition of tungsten leads to precipita-
tion of an ordered ternary compound with the
stoichiometry Co 3(Al, W) in fcc c-Co matrix after suitable
heat treatment. These alloys have been developed further to
improve their ductility and high-temperature capability by
the addition of minor alloying elements, such as B, Ta,
Nb, Mo, Hf and Ti [36–40]. These additions either
strengthen the grain boundary [41–43] or alter the volume
fraction of c
0 [44] and the misﬁt between the c matrix and
c0 precipitate [45,46]. In some cases, the strength
approaches values close to that of the existing nickel-based
superalloys. Even though these alloys exhibit a c–c0 micro-
structure in an fcc cobalt matrix, the addition of tungsten is
crucial for stabilizing the c0-ordered precipitates, and this
represents a major drawback for these alloys. Due to the
presence of W, these alloys have a much higher density
(>9.5 g cm
/C03), making them unattractive for applications
like turbine blades for aero engines and other applications
where the strength to weight ratio is crucial. Tungsten as an
alloying addition has deleterious eﬀects even for less
demanding applications like blades for land-based gas tur-
bines, especially in the presence of halide sulphates. At high
temperatures, WO
3 forms, which reacts with the liquid
halide sulphates and makes the oxide-ion activity lower
enough such that the protective Al
2O3 coating also starts
to deteriorate [47,9,48]. Further, the homogenization of
cast components made from these alloys presents a techni-
cal challenge since tungsten diﬀusion in Co is slow due to its
high melting point and large size. Therefore, although the
ability to develop c–c
0 microstructures in cobalt matrix is
exciting, the above diﬃculties make this class of alloy less
attractive for high-temperature applications, including tur-
bine blades for both aero- and land-based engines.
Attempts to develop Co-based high-temperature alloys
with a c–c
0 microstructure without the addition of tungsten
have not yet been successful [39,49].
The present paper addresses this problem and unveils a
new class of cobalt-based alloy with a composition that
does not contain tungsten and is strengthened by the pre-
cipitation of a cuboidal L1
2-ordered compound ( c0) distrib-
uted throughout the fcc c-Co matrix. These alloys are based
on cobalt, aluminium, molybdenum and niobium. We also
report further tuning of the properties through the alloying
addition of nickel in the base alloy.
2. Experimental methods
Alloy ingots of nominal compositions (in at.%)
(1 /C0 x)Co–xNi–10Al–5Nb–2Nb (where x = 0, 10, 20, 30),
weighing 30 g, were prepared by melting the constituent ele-
ments (four nine purity) under argon atmosphere using lab-
oratory-scale vacuum arc melting unit with a water-cooled
copper hearth and a tungsten electrode. For benchmarking,
we also prepared a Co–9Al–9.8W alloy. Table 1 shows the
nomenclatures for the respective alloys and their composi-
tions. Also included are the actual compositions deter-
mined using an electron probe microanalyser (EPMA;
JEOL) from the statistically signiﬁcant sampling of areas
in our samples.
The above-mentioned alloys were melted 10–12 times to
ensure homogenization. They were subsequently cast under
an argon atmosphere into 3 mm rods and slabs using a vac-
uum arc casting unit equipped with a water-cooled copper
mold. Solutionizing of the cast rods was carried out at
1300 /C176C for 15 h under a vacuum of 10
/C06 mbar in a tube
furnace, followed by quenching in cold water to obtain a
supersaturated solid solution. Samples were cut from these
solutionized rods and vacuum sealed in a quartz tube. The
sealed samples were peak aged at diﬀerent temperatures
(generally 800 /C176C) in a box furnace and subsequently fur-
nace cooled. For the Co–9Al–9.8W alloy, the aging was
done at 800 /C176C for 24 h, followed by water quenching
according to Ref. [28].
The alloys were characterized for microstructure, ther-
mal and mechanical properties. For microstructural analy-
sis and the determination of localized composition
distribution, a ﬁeld emission-tipped EPMA was used.
Details of the microstructure and composition partitions
across phase interfaces were obtained using a transmission
electron microscope equipped with ﬁeld emission source
(F30 model, FEI). The solvus temperatures of our alloys
were determined by carrying out thermal analysis using a
diﬀerential scanning calorimeter (DSC; STA449F3 NETS-
CH). To evaluate the precipitation hardening, the hardness
of the samples was measured using a Vickers microhardness
tester using a load of 0.5 kg. Tensile and compressive tests
were performed to determine the mechanical properties of
the alloys using a DARTEC hydraulic machine operated
with a strain rate of 10
/C03. The density of the alloy was mea-
sured in accordance with the ASTM standard B311-08 at
room temperature [50], while the volume fractions of the
c
0 precipitates in the aged alloys were measured using the
manual point count method (ASTM standard E562-11
[51]). A square test grid with 100 points was used.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Development of Co–10Al–5Mo–2Nb c–c0 alloy
The optimized alloy contains 5 at.% Mo and 2 at.% Nb
in the base alloy of Co–10% Al. In the following sections,
we ﬁrst describe the microstructure and heat treatment of
this alloy.
3.1.1. Cast structure
Fig. 1(a) is a low-magniﬁcation scanning electron micro-
graph taken in backscattered mode, revealing the cast
structure. The microstructure reveals a ﬁne dendritic struc-
ture, with some solute segregation in the interdendritic
regions (see S1). Compositional analysis using EPMA indi-
cates that in the as-cast condition the composition of the
dendrites can vary between 79 and 83 at.% Co and 13%
and 17% Al, while the interdendritic regions contain
67–70% Co and 8–9% Al. Under the prevailing solidiﬁca-
tion conditions, molybdenum partitions more in the

3.2. Development of alloys with Ni as an alloying addition
The solvus temperature of our new c–c0 alloy (866 /C176C)
is lower than the typical solvus temperature of a nickel-
based c–c0 alloy (typically 1000 /C176C and above). This
potentially restricts the possible application of our alloy
at higher temperatures. Thus, in order to enhance the sol-
vus temperature, we systematically replaced cobalt by the
addition of nickel up to 30% in the base alloy. However,
we shall discuss the results of only three alloys, with
compositions Co– xNi–10Al–5Mo–2Nb, where x = 10, 20
and 30.

3.3. Evaluation of mechanical properties
We now present the mechanical behaviour of these new
superalloys. For benchmarking, we have also included the
data obtained by us for the mechanical properties of Co–
9Al–9.8W alloy (under identical conditions). The 0.2%
compressive proof stresses at room temperature for Co–
10Al–5Mo–2Nb, CoNi10 and CoNi20 are 720, 730 and
760 MPa, respectively ( Fig. 6 a). These are all lower than
that observed for Co–9Al–9.8W alloy, which has a value
of about 780 MPa. However, the CoNi30 alloy shows a
higher proof stress value of 800 MPa. On the other hand,
the speciﬁc proof stress for all the current alloys is higher
than that observed for the W-bearing alloy ( Fig. 6 (b)).

We note that the Co–10Al–5Mo–2Nb alloy shows a lower
strength at high temperatures, most likely due to its lower
solvus temperature of 866 /C176C. The coherent precipitates in
this case will not be stable at 870 /C176C but will start to dis-
solve into the matrix, resulting in a decrease in strength.
The temperature dependence of the proof stress of all
the alloys is also presented in Fig. 6 (a). The proof stress
is higher for the nickel-containing alloys. For nickel-based
superalloys having a c–c0 microstructure, an anomaly in the
ﬂow stress behavior is often reported at high temperatures
for alloys containing large c0 precipitates. In general, this
corresponds to the activation of the cross-slip pinning pro-
cess when a suﬃcient number of dislocation pairs are
trapped inside the ordered precipitates [55–57]. No such
anomaly could be observed in our alloys. In the present
case, the peak-aged Co–10Al–5Mo–2Nb alloy has ordered
cuboidal precipitates with sizes <50 nm. Upon the applica-
tion of load, the dislocation pair is expected to move
through the precipitate and exit without any accumulation
inside the ordered precipitate. In fact, we observed disloca-
tions mostly in the matrix channels. Fig. 7 shows a pair of
bright- and dark-ﬁeld micrographs taken under weak beam
conditions ( g–3g condition, g = 002 near [1 1 0] zone axis)
of a dislocation present in the matrix channel which has
passed through the ordered precipitates without leaving
any dislocation loop around them.
To evaluate ductility, tensile tests were carried out for
the base alloy and CoNi30 at both room temperature and
870 /C176C. Fig. 8 shows the tensile stress–strain curve for these
alloys. The 0.2% tensile proof stress for the CoNi30 was
found to be around 790 MPa, with 17% elongation at room
temperature. This can be compared with that obtained for
base alloy, which has a tensile proof stress of 720 MPa and
elongation of 19%. At 870 /C176C, Co30Ni showed a 0.2%
proof stress of around 535 MPa, with 17% elongation.

4. Conclusion
In summary, we have reported a new class of c–c0
cobalt-based superalloy. The optimum composition of the
quaternary alloy is Co–10Al–5Mo–2Nb. The cobalt can
be further replaced by Ni up to 30%. The alloys do not con-
tain tungsten as an essential alloying addition, and has a
much higher speciﬁc 0.2% proof strength both at room tem-
perature and at elevated temperatures (>800 /C176C) compared
to Co–9Al–9.8W alloy. The results indicate the potential
for such alloys to be developed as credible alternatives to
nickel-based superalloy. One expects further improvements
with additional alloying additions. The solvus temperature
of the alloy with a substitution of Co with 30% Ni reaches
990 /C176C. Nickel addition also increases the volume fraction
of c
0 precipitates from 54% to 76%. These alloys exhibit
high 0.2% speciﬁc proof strength (94.3 MPa g /C01 cm/C03 at
room temperature and 63.8 MPa g /C01 cm/C03 at 870 /C176C for
the alloy containing 30% Ni). These features make them
attractive for high-temperature applications.
