A review of Haynes ® 230 ® and 617 alloys for High Temperature Gas

Michael Katcher 1,a and Dwaine L. Klarstrom 1,b

Keywords: Effect of coldwork on tensile and creep properties, effects of cooling rates on 
creep properties, high temperature corrosion, thermal fatigue, low cycle fatigue 
Abtract. HAYNES ® 230 ® and 617 alloys are competing for use on Generation IV, high temperature 
gas cooled reactor components because of good high temperature creep strength in the temperature 
range between 760°C and 982°C and resistance to oxidation in the gas cooled reactor environment. 
A review of the metallurgy affecting the properties in each alloy will be discussed. Grain size and 
carbide precipitation developed during fabrication effect short term and long term ductility, fatigue, 
and creep. For example, 230 alloy has a finer grained structure which promotes fatigue strength 
with a slight sacrifice in creep strength. The 617 alloy has a coarser grain structure which provides 
slightly higher creep resistance while sacrificing some fatigue strength. Thermal aging also 
introduces gamma prime precipitation to the 617 alloy as well as grain boundary carbides, and this, 
in addition to grain boundary oxidation, reduces the low cycle fatigue strength of 617 alloy 
compared to 230 alloy. Independent studies have shown that 230 alloy possesses higher resistance 
to thermal fatigue than 617 alloy. However, welds of both base metals with similar weld 
composition have about the same thermal fatigue life. Cooling rates from solution annealing 
temperatures during processing effect the ductility and creep strength of these alloys with the 
highest cooling rates preferred for retention of ductility and creep strength. The reason; slow 
cooling rates promote carbide precipitation in the grain boundaries which reduces ductility and 
creep strength. 
Introduction 
GEN IV promises safe, economically competitive, proliferation resistant nuclear power without the 
danger of increasing greenhouse gas emissions [1]. Ten of nations have joined together in the 
Generation IV International Forum (GIF) agreeing on a framework for international cooperation in 
research. The project objectives include clean air, long term availability of electricity, life cycle cost 
advantages, comparable financial risk to other electricity sources, and low diversion risk. With an 
emphasis on long term usage, present reactors in service now for thirty years or more, when they 
come up for renewed licenses, will be analyzed for potential usage for another thirty years and 
hence materials in these reactors will be in service for very long times. Therefore, new reactors that 
come on line may be licensed for 60 years [2]. There is a renewed interest in Gas Cooled Reactors 
(GCR) that seems promising as a 4 th generation nuclear power system. One concept is the Very 
High Temperature Reactor (VHTR), a helium-cooled thermal spectrum reactor in a direct cycle. In 
this case the core structure is made of large amounts of graphite. The coolant helium should stream 
out of the reactor vessel at average temperatures as high as 1000 oC in order to gain high-efficiency 
energy conversion. It is therefore imperative for the materials subjected to a high temperature,

In the selection of materials for construction of the VHTR, 617 alloy has been identified as one of 
the first priority candidates for application in the temperature range of 760 oC to 1000 oC [3]. The 
VHTR design life under the GEN IV program is 60 years. The alloy has been around since the 
1970’s and was incorporated into the ASME Boiler Code to 982 oC for pressure vessel construction, 
Section VIII, Div. 1. It has high creep strength and oxidation resistance. A draft code case for 
nuclear rules was started but never completed because of cancellation of the VHTR in an earlier 
program. One problem recently identified with 617 alloy was the variability of creep properties 
from heat to heat. As a consequence, a study was done to determine the cause of such variability 
and a specification is under development at ORNL with narrower chemistry ranges for iron (1% 
max.), aluminum (1.2%-1.4%), titanium (0..40%-0.60%) and boron (0.002-0.005) so as to remain 
compatible with present ASTM, ASME and AMS specifications. This was done in consultation 
with producers who have expressed some reservations [4]. The chemistry for 617 listed in these 
national specifications is shown in Table 1. 
HAYNES 230 alloy was developed in the 80’s because of good oxidation resistance and high creep 
strength at very high temperatures. It was developed to compete with 617 alloy with 14.0% tungsten 
and 3% more nickel than 617 alloy. In comparison, this replaced 7% of the 9% Mo and all of the 
12.5 % cobalt. The aluminum was also decreased from 1.2% to 0.5% maximum. See Table 1 for the

Cold work 
To illustrate the requirements for recrystallization from Cadden and Tawancy [6], samples from 
production sheets of 617 and 230 alloys were strained by 10% and 20% and then heat treated at 
temperatures below generally recommended annealing temperatures, 1040°C (1900 oF) and 1120 oC 
(2050 oF) to simulate conditions that might be experienced during the manufacture of hardware. See 
Table 2 for test results. Samples were annealed in an air furnace and air cooled. At least 20% cold 
work and 1120 oC (2050 oF) were required to initiate recrystallization as shown in Table 2 by the 
refinement of the grain size. The recommended solution annealing treatments for production sheet 
are 1175 oC for 617 alloy and 1230 oC for 230 alloy, which are higher than used in this test program. 
Coldwork increased the yield and ultimate strengths even when followed by the low temperature 
anneal. The high temperature anneal returned the materials generally back to as-received properties. 
Creep strength was shown to be best for the cold worked plus low temperature anneal. One can 
surmise, therefore, that creep strength is not promoted by recrystallization without grain growth.

Cooling rates 
Cooling rates from solution annealing temperatures, and the magnitude of the temperatures of 
solution annealing during processing effect the ductility and creep strength of 617 and 230 alloys as 
was shown in a study by Klarstrom [7]. The higher temperatures and the faster cooling rates were 
preferred for the retention of ductility and creep strength. The reason; slow cooling rates promote 
carbide precipitation in the grain boundaries which reduces ductility and creep strength. Higher 
solution annealing temperatures promote total carbon dissolution. Cooling rates in the furnace cool 
or air cool range produced grain boundary carbide precipitation in both alloys. When solution 
annealed at 1175 oC, 617 alloy exhibited grain growth while 230 alloy solution annealed at 1230 oC 
did not. Improved ductility was observed as the cooling rates increased. The ductility of 617 alloy 
was least affected by cooling rate most likely because of its large initial grain size, 2-21 /2 , as 
compared to 5-51 /2 for 230 alloy.

Increasing cooling rates by water quenching, in fact, returned or improved the creep strength for 
617 alloy compared to the as-received properties. In addition to the results of air cooling from the 
solution anneal temperatures, the results on creep lives for furnace cooled and water quenched 
samples of 617 alloy are shown in Table 3. The normal solution annealing temperature for 617 alloy 
of 1175 oC was used. A marked improvement in the 870 oC/48 MPa creep properties were observed 
for water quenched materials. Slower cooling rates of air cooling or furnace cooling showed a 
significant degradation. This was metallurgically explained by matrix carbon depletion by carbide 
precipitation during the slower cooling cycles. Carbon supersaturation is also the likely cause for 
the increased creep strength in 617 in the water quenched condition. 
High Temperature Corrosion 
The long-term resistance of structural alloys at high temperature relies on the formation of a 
compact, adherent, slow-growing surface oxide layer. Corrosion in Gas Cooled Reactor helium 
coolant is a critical issue where impurities such as H 2, H 2O, CO, and CH 4 in the microbar range 
interact with metallic materials at high temperature [5]. Certain high aluminum and titanium 
containing nickel-base alloys like UDIMET 720 alloy, compared to INCONEL 718 or 800H alloys, 
exposed to 750 oC showed thicker scales and also exhibited internal oxidation [8]. The tendency 
toward internal oxidation in chromia-former alloys seems to be correlated with aluminum, and 
possibly titanium contents. Cabet et al. recommend the use of low aluminum varieties of 230 alloy 
to improve the oxidation resistance of the alloy toward intergranular precipitation of alumina [5]. 
Certain impure helium test gases in the GCR environment corrode 617 and 230 alloys above 920 oC 
. The aluminum content may have an impact where it is 0.5% for 230 and 1.3% for 617. Tests were 
run for 239, 518 and 813 hrs in impure helium at 950 oC [5]. Results are shown in Table 4. Alloy 
617 alloy suffered a heavier surface oxide than 230 alloy and about the same depth of internal 
oxidation. However, the internal oxidation of Al in 617 was both internal and intergranular. The 
scale was an irregular rather than thick, chromia scale, significantly doped in Ti and Mn. Alloy 230 
showed a dense Cr based oxide scale and some oxidation of Cr and Al limited to the grain 
boundaries. Thermal aging introduces secondary carbide precipitation in both alloys but a carbide 
free zone at the surface due to carbon depletion. The depth of depletion is directly related to the 
scale thickness and was greater for 617 alloy as shown in Table 4.

Further evidence of the susceptibility of 617 to greater internal oxidation than 230 alloy was 
reported in the work of Rothman et al [9] on sheet samples which were exposed to flowing air at 
980 oC for 1008 hours and cycled to room temperature once a week. Metallographic evidence shows 
continuous internal penetration of about 25 µm per surface for 617 alloy versus 10 µm per surface 
for 230 alloy. Average metal loss at the surface was about the same at 8 µm as measured through 
weight loss conversions. Evidence of the effects on mechanical properties due to internal oxidation 
appeared in tensile tests of materials exposed to 980 oC for 1000 hours [9]. The ductility of 617 
alloy, as measured by percent elongation, was reduced in room temperature tests from 60% to 30% 
while for 230 alloy, it dropped from 50% to 40%. 
Static oxidation testing in still air at 982 oC for two years and 1149 oC for one year showed the 
importance of long-term testing as reported by Harper [10]. Some of the results in still air at 982 oC 
are shown in Table 5 for 230 and 617 alloys.. At the end of each test, practically all the oxide scale 
spalled off the samples on return to room temperature and then weight gain or loss was measured 
and converted to thickness loss using density and sample area.. Most of the thickness damage was 
internal penetration rather than metal loss with 617 exhibiting 1.65 times the internal penetration or 
metal loss of 230 alloy. Samples of other alloys such as HR-120, 556, HR-160, 601, RA85H and 
800HT were also tested but these faired even worse. Samples that had the highest content of Al+Si 
showed the greatest amount of internal penetration, but this finding is restricted to alloys with an Al 
content less than 2%. With alloys having greater than 2% Al, the combination of Al + Si reduces 
metal loss. The external scale present on the 230 alloy was rich in Cr and the presence of Al-rich 
oxide precipitates at the grain boundaries was observed. The internal precipitates near the surface of 
the 617 alloy were Al-rich oxide and Ti-rich nitride particles were observed deeper within the alloy. 
Alloy 230 ranked first and 617 alloy second among a list of chromia-forming alloys having the best 
oxidation resistance.

Thermal Fatigue 
Studies of thermal fatigue in air reported by Meyer-Obersleben et al. show 230 alloy to be 
somewhat superior to 617 alloy after thermal cycling [11]. Wedge samples with and without welds 
perpendicular to the wedge edge were inductively heated while cycling between 200 oC and each of 
850 oC, 950 oC, and 1050 oC. The thermal fatigue crack initiation life (TF) was defined as the number 
of thermal cycles to form a crack of 0.3 mm length. For the same maximum cycle temperature, 230 
alloy exhibited higher resistance to crack initiation as shown in Figure 1. Because the crack 
propagation rates da/dn are essentially the same for both alloys, this translates into a longer TF life 
for 230 alloy. Also, the crack initiation life is reduced with increasing maximum temperature. 
Specimens of 617 base metal welded with filler matching the base metal showed only slight 
reductions in TF life as shown in Figure 2. Specimens of 230 base metal welded with matching 
filler had reduced lives to the point where welds of both alloys with similar filler metals 
composition had about the same thermal fatigue life. Dissimilar weld metal reduced TF life 
considerably in 230 samples. A notch placed on the 230 weld also showed considerable life 
reduction, indicating that weld beads need to be carefully smoothed.
