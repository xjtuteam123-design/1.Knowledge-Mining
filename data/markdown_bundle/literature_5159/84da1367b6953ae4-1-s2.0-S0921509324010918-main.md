ABSTRACT
The reduction of strength and ductility due to structural relaxation induced by high-temperature annealing has 
greatly limited the application of tungsten-based alloys in structural functional applications. In this study, W-25 
(wt%)Re (WRE) alloy, which exhibits significant yielding behavior and tensile strength above 1.4 GPa at room 
temperature(RT), was successfully prepared by a combination of wet-chemical and rotary swaging process. After 
annealing at 1000 
◦
C, WRE has an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 1253 MPa and total elongation (TE) of 9.8 % 
at 200 
◦
C. Furthermore, following annealing at 1400 
◦
C, WRE exhibits a UTS of 897 MPa at 500 
◦
C, thereby 
demonstrating excellent resistance to annealing embrittlement and high-temperature strength. Microstructural 
analysis reveals that WRE retains a substantial number of fine subgrains and a considerable proportion of low- 
angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) following high-temperature annealing. The structural stability of WRE enables it 
to exhibit excellent high-temperature mechanical properties.
1. Introduction
Tungsten, with a series of excellent properties such as high melting 
point, high thermal conductivity, and excellent high-temperature me -
chanical properties, is widely used as an ideal high-temperature heat- 
resistant material in the fields of plasma-facing materials (PFMs), stir -
ring head materials for friction stir welding (FSW), and anode targets for 
CT machines [ 1 – 4 ]. However, tungsten materials are prone to brittle 
fracture and strength reduction due to grain coarsening and structural 
relaxation at high temperatures, which greatly limits the processability 
of tungsten-based materials and narrows the temperature range of their 
applications [ 5 – 7 ].
The inherent brittleness and poor ductility of tungsten are mainly 
due to its BCC crystal structure with high Peierls stress, as well as low 
grain boundary bonding strength and coordinated deformation ability 
between grains [ 8 , 9 ]. The feasible way to improve the ductility of 
tungsten is to add alloying elements to achieve the purpose of 
strengthening grain boundaries (GBs) [ 10 ]and reducing dislocation 
resistance [ 11 ], such as rhenium (Re), molybdenum (Mo), osmium (Os), 
titanium (Ti), potassium(K) [ 12 – 16 ]. Among them, Re solid solution can 
cause tungsten dislocations in the nucleation structure of the change and 
reduce the low-temperature 1/2 < 111 > screw dislocation slip resis -
tance, this phenomenon is known as "rhenium effect" [ 17 ]. In addition, 
Re can also enhance the thermal stability of the tungsten alloy, reduce 
the recrystallization and grain growth thermodynamic driving force 
[ 18 ], and thus improve high-temperature strength and creep resistance 
[ 19 ]. Therefore, Re alloying is a key means to achieve tungsten-based 
materials toughness and high temperature stability.
Powder metallurgy can allow near-net-shaping of high melting point 
metals in multiple dimensions and is widely used for the preparation of 
W-Re alloys [ 20 , 21 ]. The conventional powder metallurgy techniques 
encompass high-energy ball milling and solid-liquid admixture methods. 
However, these techniques are beset with significant challenges, 
including poor compositional homogeneity, particle agglomeration, and 
the proclivity to introduce impurities, particularly in the preparation of 
tungsten-based alloys with a high Re content ( > 20 wt%) [ 22 – 24 ]. As a 
new preparation process based on co-precipitation of W-Re precursors 
and in-situ reduction of W-Re alloy powders by H

addition, this study further reveals the mechanism of WRE alloy to 
maintain high strength and good ductility through the analysis of 
microstructure and fracture mechanism, which provides reference for 
the preparation of other high-performance refractory metals and alloys.

4.2. Influence of sub-grains
In order to further investigate the formation process of fine sub- 
grains during rotary swaging process, the microstructure of as-swaged 
WRE is characterized more deeply in this study. Although the micro -
structure has been finalized after rotary swaging, some microstructures 
in the formation process are still preserved. Fig. 8 shows the typical 
bright field TEM image of the microstructure of WRE formed by rotary 
swaging. At the initial stage of rotary swaging, a substantial number of 
dislocations are generated within the grains under the plastic defor -
mation of WRE. With the sustained application of mechanical stress, the 
dislocations become intertwined, as illustrated by the dislocation 
entanglement region indicated by the red arrow in Fig. 8 (a). With the 
continuous increase of dislocation density inside the grain, the disloca -
tion entanglement region and gradually form the dislocation wall, such 
as the region indicated by the yellow arrow in Fig. 8 (b). In response to a 
specific strain, the dislocation generation and recovery rates reach a 
dynamic equilibrium state, resulting in the formation of a dislocation 
wall to stabilize the sub-grains boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. 8 (c) by 
the orange arrows in the converted region of GBs. The final WRE sub- 
grains formed by rotary swaging is shown in Fig. 8 (d), and it can be 
found that a large number of mobile dislocations still exist inside the 
sub-grains. The equiaxial fine sub-grains formed by mechanical defor -
mation in HERF-W has good strain compatibility as well as shielding or 
blunting of crack extension, which results in excellent low-temperature 
ductility of pure W [ 31 ]. In addition, high-density GBs will redistribute 
impurities (e.g., O, P) in W and diffuse them to other regions along GBs, 
increasing the GBs strength of the W [ 45 ]. Consequently, the fine 
sub-grain structure generated within WRE during the rotary swaging 
process can, on the one hand, achieve strain transfer without fracture 
through well-coordinated deformation capability, thereby significantly 
alleviating strain incompatibility. On the other hand, the generation of a 
large number of GBs by the sub-grains can effectively mitigate the 
negative effects of impurities in WRE. At the same time, the entangled 
dislocations not transformed into sub-grains boundaries as well as the 
dislocation walls can be used as dislocation sources to provide more 
dislocation nucleation sites, which can promote the improvement of the 
ductility of WRE alloys. In addition, the presence of a large number of 
mobile dislocations inside the sub-grains can also further enhance the 
WRE ductility.

C, a significant decrease in the 
number of dislocations within the A14-WRE grains can be seen in Fig. 9
(d), which is also the reason for the decrease in the ductility of 
A14-WRE, but its fine sub-grains have not disappeared, and it still 
maintains a higher stability of the organization structure. The retention 
of sub-grains and dislocations in WRE at high temperatures inhibits 
structural relaxation due to high-temperature annealing, resulting in 
good resistance to annealing embrittlement.
5. Conclusion
WRE rod was prepared by wet chemical method and rotary swaging 
process, and the effects of different annealing temperatures on the me -
chanical properties and microstructure of WRE were also investigated. 
The results of this study were summarized as follows:
1) The wet chemical method can achieve the preparation of single- 
phase ultrafine WRE nano-powders ( < 100 nm), and uniform distri -
bution of W and Re elements in as-sintered WRE. As-swaged WRE has 
UTS of 1413 MPa at RT and still maintains high UTS ( > 950 MPa) at 
500 
◦
C. After annealing the ductility has been improved, A10-WRE 
showed UTS of 1253 MPa, TE of 9.8 % at 200 
◦
C, and 48 % higher 
TE than WRE. The results demonstrate that the prepared WRE alloy 
exhibits favorable mechanical properties and microstructure stabil -
ity, and is capable of functioning stably under high temperature 
conditions.
2) After 1400 
◦
C annealed, A14-WRE at 500 
◦
C UTS of 897 MPa, with 
excellent thermal stability, which could be derived from Re solid 
solution strengthening. Rotary swaging resulted in the creation of a 
high percentage of LAGBs (36.6 %), which improved WRE strain 
compatibility. Following annealing at elevated temperatures, the 
proportion of LAGBs remained largely unchanged, suggesting that 
the impact of temperature on the WRE alloy structure was not as 
pronounced as in the case of W alloy. The observed stability of the 
WRE structure can be attributed to the resistance effect of Re.
3) The fine sub-grains boundaries generated during rotary swaging and 
a large number of mobile dislocations can be utilized as the source of 
dislocations to further enhance the ductility of WRE alloys. These 
dislocations did not disappear after annealing at 1400 
◦
C. The wet 
chemical method of adding a high Re content in combination with 
the rotary swaging process can achieve high tensile strength and the 
tackling of structural relaxation in tungsten-based alloys. This 
method is conducive to the development of tungsten-based alloys in 
high-temperature application fields.
