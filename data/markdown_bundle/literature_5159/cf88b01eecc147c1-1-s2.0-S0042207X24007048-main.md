ABSTRACT
The carbide/matrix interface in superalloys is susceptible to cracking under mechanical stress, yet the failure 
mechanisms require further investigation. The cohesive strength and stability of 32 interface models, including 
HfC(001)/Ni(001), HfC(011)/Ni(001), HfC(111)/Ni(001), HfC(001)/Ni
3
Al(011), and HfC(111)/Ni
3

within the DZ125 superalloys, were investigated using first-principles calculations and experimental methods. 
The results indicate that the majority of interfaces demonstrate negative adhesion work (W
ad

instability. However, Bridge4 model in HfC(001)/Ni
3

ad 
and lower interface energy, 
suggesting improved stability. The interfacial cohesion is attributable to strong Ni-C covalent bonds. But inter -
facial fracture toughness results reveal that the majority of models are more susceptible to fracture at the 
interface. Fracture morphology analysis from tensile tests at room temperature and endurance tests at 760 
◦

725 MPa confirms that cracks primarily initiate at the carbide/matrix interface. This study suggests that intro -
ducing Ta atoms could improve interface strength, as Ta-rich carbides reduce interfacial energy while increasing 
elastic energy, resulting in the formation of skeletal structures. The relationship between Hf-rich and Ta-rich 
carbides and their respective morphology was investigated. The findings provide insights into the failure 
mechanisms of carbide/matrix interface and offer theoretical guidance for enhancing interface strength in su -
peralloy applications.
1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys are widely utilized in advanced engine turbine 
blades due to their excellent high-temperature, creep, and corrosion 
resistance properties [ 1 , 2 ]. Their outstanding performance is mainly due 
to their internal strengthening phase, γ ′ phase, which accounts for 60% – 
70% of the volume fraction [ 3 – 5 ]. Metal carbide is also one of the major 
precipitated phases in nickel-based superalloys, and its relative hard -
ness, brittleness, and other properties mean that its influence on the 
strength and toughness of materials is significant [ 6 ]. Currently, 
extensive research has been conducted on the formation mechanism, 
morphology, and mechanical properties of carbides in nickel-based su -
peralloys [ 7 – 10 ].
The influence of carbide on mechanical properties of Ni-based su -
peralloy depends not only on the strength of the carbide itself but also on 
the adhesion strength of the interface between carbide and Ni matrix. 
Generally, the interface between carbide and matrix in superalloys is a 
weak point and is prone to become the source of creep and fatigue cracks 
[ 11 ]. A deep understanding of interface performance is crucial for un -
derstanding both the influence and underlying mechanisms of precipi -
tated carbides on the mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloys. The 
orientation relationship between the precipitated phase and the matrix 
has a significant influence on the interface structure and morphological 
evolution [ 12 , 13 ]. To the best of our knowledge, there is still a lack of 
fundamental understanding of atomic bonding at the interface between 
Ni-based alloys and carbides. Currently, achieving quantitative mea -
surements at the interface through experimental methods remains 
challenging due to technical limitations [ 14 ]. First-principles calcula -
tion has shown strong predictive and explanatory capabilities in 
studying interfacial performance and the atomic and electronic struc -
tures at interfaces [ 15 ]. Analyzing at the atomic scale provides impor -
tant insights into the bonding characteristics at the interface of the

2.2. Simulation methods
All geometry optimization and total energy calculation of the inter -
face models were adopted by the Cambridge Sequential Total Energy 
Package (CASTEP). The exchange-correlation energy was described 
using generalized gradient approximation (GGA) using the Perdew- 
Burke-Enzerhof (PBE) functional. The ultra-soft pseudopotential was 
employed to characterize both ionic and electronic changes. The 
Monkhorst-Pack scheme was applied to sample the first Brillouin region 
at k-points. For the bulk phases, a 9 × 9 × 9 k-points was used, and the 9 
× 9 × 1 grid was utilized for interface calculation. The cutoff energy for 
the plane wave expansion was defined as 420 eV. The cutoff energy and 
k-point grid of Ni and Ni
3
Al were determined according to our previous 
research [ 29 ]. The cutoff energy and K-point grid of HfC were added in 
Fig. S2 . All the calculated results were tested for convergence. The 
Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shannon (BFGS) algorithm was used to 
optimize the atomic structure and reach the ground state. The conver -
gence criteria in the optimization process are as follows: the total energy 
converges to 1 × 10

3. Results and discussions
3.1. Experiment
Fig. 1 shows the SEM images and the corresponding EDS analysis of 
Sample A and Sample B. These gray precipitated phases can be identified 
as MC-type carbides. Simultaneously, it is evident that the morphology 
characteristics and alloy composition of MC-type carbides in Sample A 
and Sample B are significantly different. The carbides in Sample A are 
dispersed in a linear or skeleton shape have higher Ta content compared 
to Hf. The carbides in Sample B are characterized by tiny size, with a 
higher concentration of Hf compared to Ta. Actually, we detected the 
energy spectrum information of more than ten locations in the SEM test, 
and we can confirm that the conclusion that skeletal carbides are rich in 
Ta and massive carbides are rich in Hf is correct, as confirmed by rele -
vant literature [ 9 , 30 ]. In our previous research, we explained the causal 
relationship between the inclusion of HfO
2 
and its impact on the carbide 
in DZ125 superalloy. So we will refrain from reiterating this information 
here. We conclude that the interfacial bonding strength between the two 
carbides and the matrix appears to vary. In this paper, a scheme is 
designed as follows: we will use Sample B as an example to explain and 
analyze the interface bonding strength and fracture mechanism between 
Hf-riched carbide and matrix from the atomic perspective. Then, the 
relationship between the binding stability of the skeleton carbide and 
the matrix is studied on the side. Specifically, Ta atoms are introduced at 
the HfC interface to effectively substitute the Ta-rich skeletal carbide, 
which can help reduce the need for repeated simulation calculations.

tween the carbide and the matrix in Sample B. Fig. 2 (b – d) and Fig. 2 (h) 
and (p) illustrate the element distribution in the carbides, which is

Hf, Ta, and other elements, with Hf accounting for approximately 40 %

3.5. Discussion on the influence of Ta and Hf atoms for MC morphologies 
and interface bonding
The morphology of the precipitated phase is determined by the 
interaction between anisotropic interfacial energies and elastic strain 
energies. Because the interfacial energy rises with the precipitation area 
whereas elastic strain energy increases with the volume. When the 
precipitation size is minimal, anisotropic interfacial energy predomi -
nantly influences the morphology of the precipitated phase. Conversely, 
as the size of the precipitated phase increases, elastic strain energy be -
comes the primary factor affecting its shape [ 42 ]. Research [ 42 ] pre -
dicted that the γ -Al
2
O
3 
precipitates initially nucleate and grow in 
spheroidal or elliptic shapes, and then gradually transform into rod-like 
structures as they continue to expand. The morphological variations of 
different carbides during precipitation in nickel-based superalloys are 
attributed to changes in carbide composition during nucleation and 
subsequent development, which are caused by solubility. Carbides have 
significant thermodynamic stability, and nucleation is expected to begin 
at the core, which is rich in Hf elements. Over time, the Hf elements in 
the surrounding environment become depleted, and solute atoms like Ta 
and Ti replace Hf in the carbides [ 43 ]. The formation of carbides within 
the alloy is influenced by both interfacial energy and elastic strain en -
ergy at the semi-coherent interface between the matrix phase and car -
bides, which are crucial factors affecting the carbide morphology. In this 
work, to clarify the difference in interfacial bonding strength between 
Ta-rich and Hf-rich carbides and the matrix, Hf atoms in the 
HfC-terminated interface were substituted with Ta atoms. The findings 
reveal that introducing Ta atoms at the interface improves the W

contributing to the distinct morphologies and alloy compositions of the 
two carbide types. As the carbide grows, the elastic strain energy 
increasingly influences the morphology of the precipitated phase. The 
introduction of Ta into the carbide raises the W

interfacial energy. When the anisotropic interfacial energy surpasses the 
elastic strain energy, the carbides tend to form block-like shape.
On the one hand, the increase in interfacial energy can be attributed

radius. The atomic radius of Hf is larger than that of Ta. Therefore, in the 
presence of doped elements with larger atomic radius, the low interfa -

bonding strength due to their strong Ta-C covalent bond and Ta-Ni metal
