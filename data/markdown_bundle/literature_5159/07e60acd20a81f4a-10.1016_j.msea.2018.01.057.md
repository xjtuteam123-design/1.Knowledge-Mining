ABSTRACT
The microstructure of Ni alloy produced by hot isostatic pressing (HIP) and selective laser melting (SLM) using
pre-alloyed Ni 41Al41Cr12Co6 spherical powders was studied by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high-
resolution electron microscopy (HRTEM). In-situ studies of the evolution of the ﬁne structure of Ni 41Al41Cr12Co6
alloy during the heating at 20, 350 … 850 °C has been performed. Homogeneous precipitation and growth of the
spherical Guinier-Preston zones rich in Cr (size < 5 nm) on concentration ﬂuctuations of dopants in the β-NiAl
matrix by spinodal decomposition was detected in the range of 20 –450 °C. In addition, the precipitation of the
Guinier-Preston zones by heterogeneous nucleation on the crystalline defects of β-NiAl matrix has been revealed.
Prismatic dislocation loops 10 –25 nm in size with a stacking fault are formed from a vacancy cluster in the β-
NiAl grain at 750 °C. In the 750 –850 °C interval, heterogeneous nucleation and growth of α-Cr nanocrystallites
having a prismatic shape and longitudinal size of 20 –50 nm was observed on the prismatic dislocation loops.
Also, high-temperature strength characteristics of the alloy produced by HIP in the temperature range of
600–1100 °C on a Gleeble System 3800 testing system was studied. The high-temperature strength of the HIP
samples produced using pre-alloyed spherical powders is higher by 80 –100 MPa compared to that of the as-cast
alloy.
1. Introduction
Nickel superalloys have been gradually failing to satisfy the steadily
increasing requirements to refractory alloys that are used to design
critical components of modern turbojet engines [1,2]. Nickel mono-
aluminide β-NiAl is a promising material that can be applied to produce
next generation of refractory alloys, which can replace Ni-based su-
peralloys. NiAl has a unique combination of physico-mechanical
properties such as high melting point, thermal conductivity, and
Young's modulus; it is also characterized by a relatively low density and
excellent oxidation resistance at temperatures above 1000 °C [3–5].
However, the near-zero crack resistance at room temperature, as well as
the insuﬃcient strength and low creep resistance at temperatures above
700 °C, impede the industrial application of NiAl [6,7]. The strength
characteristics of NiAl can potentially be improved by choosing the
optimal doping system that would reinforce the NiAl matrix with a
second phase consisting of refractory metals, such as Cr, Mo, W, etc.
[7–13]. Designing eutectic in situ compositions, such as NiAl –(2…
28)Cr–(0… 6)Mo, ensures good crack resistance and elevated-tempera-
ture strength due to formation of the NiAl-Cr(Mo) eutectic interlayers in
the intergrain space and nucleation of α-Cr precipitations in the grain
body within the β-NiAl matrix [8–16]. However, the use of these
compositions in aircraft engines remains infeasible because of in-
suﬃcient high-temperature strength. Doping with small (0.2 –4 at%)
amounts of Hf signi ﬁcantly increases creep resistance of the alloy
[5,14]. Despite the increase in total strength of the alloy, the NiAl in-
termetallic matrix retains cracking susceptibility. Machining of the
parts manufactured using the conventional technologies inevitably re-
sults in local degradation related to microcrack formation [16–19]. The
use of net-shape powder metallurgy by hot isostatic pressing and the
cutting-edge additive technologies, such as selective laser melting and
electron beam melting (EBM), allows one to exclude the rough ma-
chining stage [20–23].
The afore-listed technologies use pre-alloyed spherical powders as
the initial material. The requirements speci ﬁed to these powders are as
follows: spherical grain shape, high bulk density and ﬂow rate; no sa-
tellites or gas porosity; the homogeneous chemical composition and
oxygen content less than 0.2 wt%; grain surface must be unoxidized. As
opposed to HIP, the SLM and EBM employ precursor powders of strictly
regulated granulometric composition. Powders with the upper grain

Spherical powders are currently produced using the main three
commercial atomization techniques: gas atomization (GA), electrode
induction guide inert gas atomization (EIGA), and plasma rotating
electrode processing (PREP). These technologies ensure steady pro-
duction of powders for additive technologies and the HIP method,
which fully meet the requirements for them. The high crystallization
rate of melt droplets results in formation of non-equilibrium structures
with the content of doping components being higher than their solu-
bility limit in the main component of the alloy [27–29]. EIGA belongs
to the group of gas atomization technologies where powders are fab-
ricated by melt dispersion using an energy carrier (an inert gas). A
distinctive feature and an advantage of the EIGA method [27] com-
pared to the conventional crucible gas atomization technologies is that
the contact between the melt and a crucible is completely eliminated,
which allows one to obtain powders devoid of ceramic inclusions. The
PREP method has the same advantage. The ﬁndings reported in [25–30]
indicate that GA-powders have a number of signi ﬁcant drawbacks
limiting their use in additive technologies: presence of satellites, gas
porosity, broad particle size distribution, and high content of particles
of irregularly shaped particles. Contrariwise, the PREP technology
[25,29,30] allows one to produce narrow-fraction powders character-
ized by high chemical purity, low content of impurities (O, N, C), the
ideal spherical shape, homogeneous composition, and absence of sa-
tellites and gas porosity. Zhong et al. [29] have demonstrated that
application of PREP powders in additive technologies yields items
characterized by homogeneous microstructure and better processing
properties than those fabricated using GA-powders. In this connection,
the PREP method is the most promising technique for producing pre-
alloyed spherical powders to be used in additive technologies.
This study logically continues the publications [31,32], which fo-
cused on optimizing the composition of the β-NiAl-based alloy and
developing the integrated technology for fabrication of cylindrical
preforms (electrodes) intended for manufacturing of spherical powders
using the PREP technology. The structure and mechanical properties of
the β-NiAl-based alloys doped with Cr, Co, and Hf were studied in [31].
The total content of chromium and cobalt in all the experimental alloys
was 18 at%, while their ratio was varied. It was shown that the
Ni
41Al41Cr12Co6 alloy with the 2:1 Cr: Co ratio has the highest strength
characteristics at room temperature and can be recommended for
manufacturing of large electrodes using vacuum induction melting
(VIM). The microstructure transformations in the SHS (self-propagating
high-temperature synthesis) alloy and the alloy subjected to VIM upon
heating to 850 °C were studied by high-resolution transmission electron
microscopy directly in the TEM column [32]. The high-temperature
properties of the alloy were studied at temperatures up to 1100 °C and
the integrated technology for electrode fabrication including synthesis
of SHS preforms followed by vacuum induction melting thereof was
proposed.
The key objective of this study was to elucidate the relationship
between the microstructure and high-temperature strength of the β-
NiAl-based alloy fabricated by HIP and SLM of pre-alloyed spherical
powders of Ni
41Al41Cr12Co6 composition. The main focus was placed on
in situ studies of nucleation and growth of α-Cr precipitations upon
heating to 850 °C directly in the TEM column.
2. Experimental procedure
Pre-alloyed spherical powders based on β-NiAl, which had been
produced by centrifugal plasma sputtering of the electrode, were used
as initial material. The electrodes (D = 60, L = 550 mm) made of
Ni41Al41Cr12Co6 refractory alloy were fabricated using the integrated
technology proposed in [32]. Consolidation of pre-alloyed spherical
powders with grain size ranging from 40 to 160 µm was performed by
hot isostatic pressing and selective laser melting methods. The cylind-
rical samples (D = 10, H = 60 mm) were fabricated by HIP on a HIRP
10/26–200 gasostat (Abra AG, Switzerland) under isothermal exposure
at 1300 °C and 150 MPa. The capsule was manufactured from low-
carbon steel 20; argon was used as a pressure transmitter. Samples 10
× 10 × 10 mm in size were fabricated using an SLM 280 setup (SLM
Solution, Germany) equipped with a 400 W YLT ﬁber laser. Bidirec-
tional laser scanning was used to construct the samples; the scanning
vector was rotated by 90° for each new layer. The laser scanning rate
was 200 mm/s; laser intensity, 325 W; spot size, 80 µm; hatch distance,
0.2 mm; and layer thickness, 160 µm. The SLM samples were subjected
to thermal treatment in a VSl-16 –22-U vacuum furnace (VakETO,
Russia) at 860 °C, a residual pressure of 10
−5 MPa, and isothermal
exposure time of 3 h.
The granulometric composition of the powders was measured by
laser di ﬀraction in a liquid on an ANALYSETTE 22 MicroTec plus laser
particle sizer (FRITSCH, Germany). Bulk density of the powder was
determined using a calibrated funnel (Hall ﬂowmeter funnel) with the
oriﬁce diameter of 2.5 mm and the nominal capacity of 25 cm
3 in ac-
cordance with the ISO 3923– 1:2008 standard procedure. The natural
angle of repose was measured with a Hall ﬂowmeter using the ISO
4490:2014 standard procedure. The quantitative elemental composi-
tion of the initial material was determined by ICP-MS (internal standard
calibration) on an iQAP RQ instrument (Thermo Fisher Scienti ﬁc, USA)
using IV-STOCK-4 and ICP-MS-68A Solution B calibration solutions. An
accurately weighed sample (50 mg) of the powder was decomposed in
aqua regia in autoclaves in the Milestone ETHOS ONE microwave
system (Italy) at 220 °C and isothermal exposure duration of 25 min.
The contents of gaseous impurities (O, N) were determined by reductive
melting on a TC-436 nitrogen oxygen analyzer (LECO, USA). The phase
composition was identi ﬁed by X-ray powder di ﬀraction (XRPD) on a
DRON-3 instrument (Russia) using Co-K
α radiation at 2θ = 100–140°.
The microstructural studies were performed using a S-3400N scanning
electron microscope (SEM) (Hitachi, Japan) equipped with a NORAN X-
ray energy-dispersive spectrometer. In situ investigation of nucleation
and growth of the excess phase in Ni
41Al41Cr12Co6 alloy upon heating
was carried out by HRTEM on a JEM-2100 microscope (Jeol, Japan).
TEM foils were prepared on a PIPS II high-precision system for dual-ion
beam etching (Gatan, USA). Foils were heated at a rate of 100 °C/min in
a Gatan heating in situ holder (model 652). The structure was recorded
upon isothermal exposure at 20, 350, 450, 550, 650, 750, and 850 °C
for 15–20 min. The elemental composition of the nanosized phases that
had been formed during heating of the foil was measured by electron
probe microanalysis (EPMA) in the Gatan cooling in situ holder (model
671). The Young's modulus (E), hardness (H), and elastic recovery (R)
were measured by instrumented indentation using a nanohardness
tester (CSM Instruments, Switzerland) at a load of 20 mN, loading rate
of 40 mN/min, the increment of 10 µm, and the loading time of 5 s. The
indentation curves (load vs. indentation depth) were processed ac-
cording to the Oliver –Pharr method. Strength and ductility character-
istics of the Ni
41Al41Cr12Co6 alloy at room temperature were de-
termined by uniaxial compression of cylindrical samples (Ø 5 ×
7.5 mm) on an LF-100 kN universal testing machine (Walter + Bai AG)
at a loading rate of 3·10 −4 s−1. High-temperature tests were performed
under vacuum of 10 −7 bar using uniaxial compression of the samples
(Ø 5 × 7.5 mm) on a Gleeble 3800 testing system using a Pocket Jaw
module and a DIL 805 A/D quench and deformation dilatometer. The
temperature curves for the Young's modulus and yield point were re-
corded in the temperature range from 700° to 1100°C at strain rate of
0.1, 0.01 and 0.001 s −1 and small (~ 0.5 –0.1%) degree of residual
strain. The steady-state creep rate as a function of temperature was
assessed at loads of 300, 250, 200, and 150 MPa.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Structure, composition and properties of pre-alloyed spherical powders
of β-NiAl-based alloy
Examination of the morphology ( Fig. 1a, b) of pre-alloyed spherical
powders demonstrated that particles containing no satellites or pores
are predominantly spherical in shape, with the equiaxity coe ﬃcient K
eq
= 1.025 ± 0.018. According to the laser di ﬀraction data ( Fig. 1c),
spherical powders with fraction size of 40 … 160 µm have a unimodal
particle size distribution, with the maximum observed for the 120 …
130 µm fraction; d 50 is 114 µm.
Bulk density and natural angle of repose are the key characteristics
of powder materials used in additive technologies and in the conven-
tional HIP method. The natural angle of repose is the main parameter of
powder ﬂow and depends on powder shape and granulometric com-
position. The measurements demonstrated that the powder being used
has a bulk density of 4.2 g/cm
3 and the natural angle of repose of 19.3°.
The quantitative elemental composition of spherical powders of the
Ni41Al41Cr12Co6 alloy is shown in Table 1. The contents of the main
components (Ni and Al) lie in the homogeneity range of β-NiAl, making
the formation of γ-Ni3Al, which reduces elevated-temperature strength
of the alloy, impossible. Chemical analysis demonstrates that con-
centrations of oxygen and nitrogen impurities are less than 0.0043 and
0.00061 wt%, respectively.
The microstructural studies of the surface and the cross-section of
spherical powder ( Fig. 2a, b) demonstrate that all particles have a ty-
pical dendritic microstructure. The dendrites consist of the ﬁrst- and
second-order branches with the cross-section size of 1 … 5 µm. EPMA
showed that the central portion of the dendrites consists of an over-
saturated solid solution of Cr and Co in β-NiAl. Chromium and cobalt
contents in dendrite body are equal (~ 6 at%). The increased chromium
content in the alloy being higher than the solubility limit (~ 7 at%) in
β-NiAl, as well as non-equilibrium crystallization, results in formation
of thin interlayers (400 … 500 nm thick) of the degenerate Cr(Co)
e eu-
tectic in the interdendritic space. That is, the nuclei of the β-NiAl phase,
which is included in the composition of the NiAl-Cr(Co) eutectic,
crystallizes onto the surface of primary β-NiAl dendrites crystals,
whereas in the interdendritic space is crystallized only a Cr(Co)
e solid
solution. Besides, nano-sized particles (size 100 –150 nm) of the haf-
nium-based phase being precipitating in these interlayers.
The bright- ﬁeld (BF) TEM image of the ﬁne structure of spherical
powders observed along the [ −111] zone axis of β-NiAl matrix and
nanosized Cr-rich ﬁne precipitates is shown in Fig. 3a. The size of Cr-
rich ﬁne precipitates ranges from 20 to 45 nm. The EPMA analysis of
nanosized precipitates showed the following composition (% at.): Cr,
82.28; Al, 8.98; Ni, 5.5; and Co, 3.24. The composition of ﬁne pre-
cipitates indicates that α-Cr is formed, which is typical of Cr-rich NiAl
alloys [5,8,10,13,14,16,32–35]. The zero contrast of the line in the
middle of some α-Cr spherical precipitates that they are coherent with
the β-NiAl matrix [14]. The dark contrast along the β-NiAl/α-Cr in-
terface is indicative of the deformed state of the crystal lattice. The
contrast between some regions within the β-NiAl phase is also related to
crystal lattice defects mainly caused by ﬂuctuation in chromium con-
centration, which emerge upon crystallization of a melt droplet during
PREP and remain because of the rapid (~ 10
4 K/s) cooling rate.
The HRTEM image ( Fig. 3b) of the interface along the [ −111] zone
axis veriﬁes that α-Cr precipitates are coherent with the β-NiAl matrix.
The di ﬀraction pattern ( Fig. 3c) for the matrix-precipitate interface
speciﬁed in Fig. 3b contains no additional re ﬂections, which also attests
to the coherence of the β-NiAl matrix and α-Cr ﬁne precipitates and
conﬁrms the cube-on-cube orientation relationship [14,35]. According
to the di ﬀraction data, the lattice parameter is 0.2885 nm, which cor-
responds to the β-NiAl phase. X-ray powder di ﬀraction analysis of
spherical powders revealed no α-Cr phase and showed 100% content of
nickel monoaluminide with the lattice parameter of 0.2887 nm, which
agrees well with the di ﬀraction data. The phase components of the
Ni41Al41Cr12Co6 alloy cannot be quanti ﬁed from the XRD spectrum,
since the α-Cr and β-NiAl phases belong to the same crystal system
(bcc) and have very close lattice parameters.

3.3. Evolution of the microstructure of consolidated β-NiAl-based alloy
upon heating
The in situ TEM and HRTEM studies of nanosized precipitates of the
excessive α-Cr phase in β-NiAl-based alloy after SLM allowed us to
investigate their nucleation mechanism and reveal three characteristic
temperature ranges (20 … 450, 450… 650, and 650… 850 °C) where the
most signi ﬁcant structural phase transformations take place ( Fig. 8).
Homogeneous precipitation and growth of the Guinier-Preston zones
rich in Cr on concentration ﬂuctuations of dopants in the β-NiAl matrix
by spinodal decomposition was revealed in the temperature range of
20–450 °C ( Fig. 8 a, b). The resulting Guinier-Preston zones less than
5 nm in size are enriched in Cr and have a typical spherical shape. In
addition, the precipitation of hardening Guinier-Preston zones occurred
by heterogeneous nucleation on the clusters of interstitial defects and
packaging defect of crystal lattice in the β-NiAl matrix. The spinodal
decomposition [37] is a continuous homogeneous segregation of second
solute-rich phase (Guinier-Preston zones) by spontaneous separation of
the supersaturated solid solution of parent phase, which is due to short-
range di ﬀusion. In the articles [37–43], the precipitation of hardening
Guinier-Preston zones by the spinodal decomposition mechanism, as
well as their in ﬂuence on the mechanical properties of alloys, has been
studied in detail using the example of the Cu-Ag, Al-Mg, Fe-Cr, Cu-Ag,
etc. systems. However, the analysis of articles showed that the pre-
cipitation of Guinier-Preston zones by the spinodal decomposition
mechanism in the NiAl-based alloys has not been previously detected.
The dark contrast of the areas in the NiAl matrix gradually disappears,
which indicates of annihilation of point defect clusters and stress re-
laxation. The size of spherical Guinier-Preston zones increases as tem-
perature rises to 650 °C ( Fig. 8c), while their count per unit surface area
decreases, being indicative of collective recrystallization of relatively
coarse (3 –6 nm) precipitates or the precipitates formed in the NiAl
matrix zones with high energy of packing faults due to di ﬀusion ab-
sorption of the adjacent precipitate less than 3 nm in size. This process
is caused by the fact that the system tends to the minimal Gibbs free
energy by reducing the total interface of the matrix- precipitates and,
correspondingly, the surface energy, by decreasing the number of pre-
cipitates. Further increase in temperature to 850 °C results in

The results of experiments focused on nucleation studies and the
revealed structural features of α-Cr nanocrystallites give grounds for
suggesting the main stages of their formation and growth in the β-NiAl

(at ~850 °C) involves the intense di ﬀusive growth of plate-like solute-
rich zones and formation of α-Cr nanocrystallites with the ordered bcc
lattice.
To sum the discussion of structural studies, we would like to note
that the samples produced by SLM have a three-level hierarchical
structure after annealing at 850 °C, which has been described in [31].
The ﬁrst level is formed by co-oriented β-NiAl-based dendritic grains.
The second level characterizes the structure inside a dendrite. The
central portion of dendrites consists of β-NiAl. The structure on the
dendrite periphery is the degenerate Cr(Co)e eutectic, 150 –500 nm
thick, which was crystallized in the interdendritic space and contains
dispersed inclusions of ssHf < 100 nm in size. The third level contains
α-Cr spherical precipitates 15– 45 nm in diameter and prismatic nano-
crystallites with longitudinal dimension of 20 –50 nm, which are ar-
ranged in the dendritic body within the β-NiAl phase.
