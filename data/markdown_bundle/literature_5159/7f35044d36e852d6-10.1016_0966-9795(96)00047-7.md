TiAl alloys with controlled microstructures

The objective of this study is to improve the mechanical properties by careful 
control of both microstructure and alloy additions in two-phase TiAl alloys 
based on Ti-47Al-2Cr-2Nb (at%). Hot extrusion at temperatures above T, pro- 
duces refined lamellar structures, whose microstructural features can be further 
controlled by subsequent heat-treatment at and above 900°C. The mechanical 
properties of the alloys with lamellar structures depend on three factors: colony 
size, interlamellar spacing, and alloying additions. The tensile elongation at 
room temperature is strongly dependent on lamellar colony size, showing 
increasing ductility with decreased colony size. The strength at room and ele- 
vated temperatures is sensitive to interlamellar spacing, showing increasing 
strength with decreased colony spacing. The fracture toughness at room temper- 
ature can be substantially improved by heat-treatment at 1320 and 1350°C. 
The tungsten addition at a level of 0.2% improves the tensile strength, whereas the 
silicon addition at a level of 0.3% reduces the castability of the TiAl alloys. The 
TiAl materials produced by hot extrusion are much superior to those produced 
by conventional thermomechanical treatments. Copyright 0 1996 Elsevier

INTRODUCTION 
Two-phase TiAl alloys containing 45-49 at% Al 
are attractive for structural use at elevated tem- 
peratures. ’ ’ These alloys have a very low material 
density (-4 g/cm’) and a relatively high melting 
point (> 1450°C). They have promising mechanical 
properties at elevated temperatures; in particular, 
the alloys with lamellar structures possess good 
strength and creep resistance at temperatures to at 
least 800°C. Also, the alloys have adequate oxida- 
tion resistance in air at elevated temperatures. The 
major concern for structural use of TiAl alloys is 
their low ductility and poor fracture resistance at 
ambient temperatures. The mechanical behavior of 
the TiAl alloys is sensitive to their microstructure, 
(e.g. lamellar structure versus duplex structure).‘-~‘2 
The TiAl alloys with coarse lamellar structures 
show adequate fracture toughness but poor tensile 
ductility; .on the other hand, the alloys with fine 
duplex structures exhibit adequate tensile ductility 
but poor fracture toughness. This dilemma has 
not been solved satisfactorily at present. 
429 
Considerable efforts have been devoted recently 
to understanding and improving both tensile 
ductility and fracture toughness of two-phase 
TiAl alloys. 1,2*7.13 The objective of this work is 
to substantially improve these properties by care- 
ful control of both alloy additions and micro- 
structure. In this study, Ti-47Al-2Cr-2Nb (at%) 
developed previously by Huang’.” was selected 
as the base composition, and alloy additions at 
a combined level of less than 1% were added to 
enhance its properties. This study also focused on 
the design of the TiAl alloys with lamellar struc- 
tures, which have much better creep resistance 
than those with duplex structures.‘l’1.‘4 The micro- 
structural features of the lamellar structures, such 
as colony size, lamellar spacing, grain-boundary 
equiaxed phases, etc. were carefully studied and 
controlled by material processing and heat-treat- 
ment. Our study has shown that hot extrusion 
together with proper heat-treatments produced 
refined lamellar structures in the TiAl alloys with 
excellent balanced properties for structural appli- 
cations.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES 
Table 1 lists the alloy compositions and hot extru- 
sion conditions of the TiAl alloys used in this 
study. The base composition Ti47Al-2Cr-2Nb 
(at%) was alloyed with 0.15 B, 0.2 W, and/or 0.3 Si. 
Boron and tungsten additions were added for 
refining the lamellar structure and improving its 
stability.‘5,‘6 Boron has the beneficial effects of 
improving tensile ductility, reducing hydrogen- 
induced embrittlement, and refining grain struc- 
ture.‘,5*‘5 Tungsten and silicon were reported to 
have the beneficial effect of enhancing high-tem- 
perature properties and oxidation resistance.7 The 
five alloys containing 46-48 at% Al were prepared 
by arc melting and drop casting into a copper 
mold (25 mm diameter X 125 mm long), using 
high-purity charge materials. The alloy ingots 
were then canned in molybdenum billets and 
hot extruded at temperatures above T, (the (Y 
transus temperature = 1320°C for Ti47Al-2Cr-2Nb 
alloy).2 
As-cast and hot extruded alloys were heat- 
treated for 2 h at temperatures from 900 to 1350°C 
in vacuum. Buttonhead-type tensile specimens (gage 
section: 9.53 mm length X 3.18 mm diameter) and 
chevron-notched specimen (5 X 5 X 45 mm) were 
prepared from heat-treated materials by electro- 
discharge machining (EDM) followed by grinding. 
The specimens were mechanically polished to 
remove surface scratches (using ‘OO-grade’ SIC 
papers) prior to testing. Tensile tests were performed 
on an Instron testing machine at temperatures to 
1000°C in air at a cross-head speed of 0.25 cm/min 
(strain rate: 4.4 X 10m3/s). The specimens tested 
above room temperature were heated inductively 
inside a Pt susceptor, and the test temperature was 
controlled using a Pt-10% Rh thermocouple 
located at the specimen center. Tensile properties 
were determined from a strip chart recorded at a 
speed of 12.7 cm/min. In order to accurately 
determine small strains at room-temperature and 
300°C tensile strains measured from the strip 
chart were corrected on the basis of strain mea- 
surement using a clip-on Instron strain gage. The 
tensile strain at 800 and 1000°C were determined 
mainly from broken specimens. Fracture tough- 
ness was determined at room-temperature in air 
by three-point bend testing of chevron-notched 
specimens I7 The fracture toughness was calculated 
from the area of the measured load-displacement 
curves. Fracture surfaces on tensile specimens and 
chevron-notched specimens were examined by 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) using a 10 kV 
beam energy. 
Microstructural features in selected specimens 
were studied by optical microscopy and transmis- 
sion electron microscopy (TEM). Metallographic 
samples were polished on a Syntron and etched in 
a solution of 100 ml H20, 8 HN03, and 0.5 HF to 
reveal lamellar structures. TEM disks were pre- 
pared by twin-jet electropolishing in a solution of 
6% perchloric acid, 60% methanol, 33.5% butyl 
cellusolve, and 0.5O/0 glycerin at a temperature of 
-20°C and at 32 V. The thinned foils were then 
examined using Philips CM 12 (120 kV) and 
Philips CM200/FE6 (200 kV) electron micro- 
scopes.

remained. During testing, the load applied by the 
crosshead of the testing machine at the center of 
the specimen was monitored as a function of the 
crosshead displacement. The work IV, required 
for initiating a crack at the tip of the triangle

Our studies show that the mechanical properties 
of the TIA alloys with lamellar structures are 
dependent on three interrelated factors: (1) colony 
size, (2) interlamellar spacings, and (3) alloy addi- 
tion. The room-temperature tensile ductility of the 
alloys appears to increase with decreasing colony 
size and to decrease with the tungsten addition. 
The alloy TIA-23 with the fully lamellar structure 
having a fine colony size of 22 ,um shows a room- 
temperature tensile elongation of 4.7% the highest 
tensile elongation ever reported for two-phase 
TiAl alloys with fully lamellar structures. The 
substantial decrease in elongation (from 4.7 to 
1.9%) is due to the increase in colony size from 22 
to 258 pm. Note that for two-phase TiAl alloys 
with a coarse colony size (-600 pm), the tensile 
ductility is typically less than 1%~‘~~ Based on these 
results, we conclude that hot extrusion and subse- 
quent heat-treatment are effective in controlling 
colony size in two-phase TiAl alloys and that such 
alloys with refined lamellar structures (~40 pm) 
possess good tensile ductility at room-tempera- 
ture.
