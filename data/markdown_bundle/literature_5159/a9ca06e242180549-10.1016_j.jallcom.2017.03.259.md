abstract
The microstructure and mechanical behaviour of a novel magnesium (Mg) doped Co-9Al-4.5W-4.5Mo-
2Ta-0.02B alloy (doped 0.1, 0.3, 0.7 and 1.0 at.% Mg, referred to as 0.1Mg, 0.3Mg, 0.7Mg and 1Mg al-
loys hereafter, respectively) at room- and high- temperatures have been investigated. All alloys aged at
800
/C14 C for 50 h consist of a Co solid-solution matrix ( g-CoSS) and nano-scale cuboidal g0-Co3(Al, W)
precipitates. Mg is observed to segregate primarily at the grain boundaries and the g0-Co3(Al, W) phase.
In the 0.7Mg and 1Mg alloys more Mg segregation at the grain boundaries decreases the fraction of S1~3
type grain boundaries and the capability of excluding impurity O from the grain boundaries. With
decreased S1~3 boundaries and higher O content, the 0.7Mg and 1Mg alloys fracture in a mixed
transgranular-intergranular mode at room temperature with lower strength and ductility under tension.
However, the 0.1 and 0.3Mg alloys fracture in a transgranular mode with higher strength and ductility.
The four alloys exhibit compressive ﬂow stress anomalies at 700
/C14 C and the strength obviously decreases
when the nominal Mg contents increase to 0.7 e1 at.% at 600 e800 /C14 C. As for the 0.1Mg and 0.3Mg alloys,
the strengths from room temperature to 800 /C14 C are higher and not obviously affected by the nominal Mg
contents, primarily owing to the two alloys having almost the same and higher S1~3 grain boundary
fraction and the lower O concentration in the grain boundary.

1. Introduction
The successive development of superalloys has been motivated
by the requirements in promoting the performance and ef ﬁciency
of gas turbines in aerospace and power-generation industries. With
superior corrosion, oxidation and wear resistance, the conventional
Co-based superalloys have a microstructure consisting of a
continuous Co solid-solution matrix ( g-CoSS) and various carbides,
in which the carbides are the main strengthening phases [1,2].
However, the conventional Co-based superalloys exhibit lower
temperature capability in comparison with Ni-based superalloys,
because the Ni-based superalloys are mainly strengthened by the
stable intermetallic g0-Ni3Al precipitates and the coherent interface
of the g/g0 microstructure [3]. Recently, nano-size cuboidal L1 2-
type g0 precipitate, i.e., an intermetallic compound Co 3(Al, W), has
been found in the g matrix of a Co-Al-W ternary [4]. This g0-Co3(Al,
W) phase introduces a strong combination of the strengthening
effect of the precipitate and coherent interface, which enhances the
high temperature strength to a level that is considerably greater
than that of the conventional Co-based alloys by 50 e100% [5e8].
In order to further develop this new class of superalloys as
promising structural materials, macro-alloying the Co-Al-W ter-
naries by Ta, Nb, Mo, Hf and Ti [9e13] has been achieved to
investigate the microstructural evolution including the elemental
partitioning in each phase [9e11 ]and the g0 solvus temperature
[12,13]. These element additions, strengthen the grain boundary by
borides or intermetallic phases [14] or improve the high-
temperature oxidation resistance [15] by Boron, alter the volume
fraction of the g0 phase [16] and the mis ﬁt between the g matrix
and g0 precipitate [17,18]or increase the high-temperature stability
of the g0 phase [5,16] and are bene ﬁcial to the bulk strength of the
Co-Al-W based alloys. Ta has been con ﬁrmed to be the most ef ﬁ-
cient strengthening element for Co-Al-W based alloys [5]. For
example, with the addition of 2 at.% Ta, the solvus temperature of
the g0 phase in the Co-9Al-9W alloy increases from 1000 /C14 Cu pt o* Corresponding author.

For the Co-Al-W based system, the composition window for the
formation of the two-phase g/g0 coherent microstructure is nar-
row; therefore, macro-alloying Co-Al-W compositions, which cau-
ses a slight shift from the composition window, may result in a
third intermetallic phase precipitating along the grain boundaries
[6,8,14], which is harmful to the tensile ductility of g'-strengthened
Co-base superalloys. Micro-alloying, i.e., alloy addition of generally
less than 1 at.%, is probability the most effective approach to
improve the properties mainly via changing the metallurgical
characteristics of the grain boundary [25]. At present, systemic
works regarding the effect of micro-alloying on the microstructure
and mechanical behaviours of Co-Al-W based alloys are still
seldom. B was found to be the most effective alloying element to
improve the ductility of the Co eAleW ternary. A considerable
increment in elongation of 7.6% takes place, i.e. from 4.7% to 12.3%,
in Co e9.2Ale9.5W alloy when doped with 0.02 at.% B [7]. In this
study, magnesium (Mg) is employed as a micro-alloying element in
a novel Co-9Al-4.5W-4.5Mo-2Ta-0.02B composition with a g/g0
coherent microstructure, in which 4.5 at.% W is replaced by 4.5 at.%
Mo to reduce the density of the Co-9Al-9W based composition. It is
expected that Mg will affect the microstructure, especially the grain
boundary character distribution (GBCD) and the element distribu-
tion at the grain boundaries, which will subsequently alter the
deformation and fracture behaviours of the Co-Al-W based alloys at
different temperatures. A detailed investigation on the micro-
structural evolution and mechanical responses of Co-9Al-4.5W-
4.5Mo-2Ta-0.02B-xMg alloys (where x ¼ 0.1, 0.3, 0.7 and 1.0 at.%) is
performed.
2. Experimental procedures
Button ingots of the Co-9Al-4.5W-4.5Mo-2Ta-0.02B-(0.1, 0.3, 0.7
and 1.0) Mg alloys (referred to as 0.1Mg, 0.3Mg, 0.7Mg and 1Mg
alloy, hereafter), each with a mass of 150 grams, were prepared by
arc melting ﬁve or six times using the vacuum arc melting (VAR)
method to homogenize the compositions of the ingots. An arc-
melting furnace (FD-600H, KYKY Technology Development, She-
nyang, China) was used for preparation of ingots of Co-Al-W based
alloys. The purity of all of the raw elements was 99.95% by mass or
higher. In this work, thin magnesium piece was used as raw ma-
terials. Actually, there is a huge difference between the melting and
vaporization temperature of Mg and the temperature of the melt,
and this may result in obvious vaporization and loss of Mg during
melting if no any protection. To reduce the loss of Mg during
melting as less as possible, the following routes were employed: 1)
Enwrapped the thin Mg pieces with the Al foil to form a package,
and then put the package on the bottom of the melting crucible; 2)
Piled up the other raw material plates from the package to the top
according to the order of Al, Co, Mo, Ta and W; 3) At the beginning
of arc melting, the W, Ta and Mo plates on the top were ﬁrstly
melted by arc and then the W, Ta and Mo melt with a temperature
of approximately 3500
/C14 C (larger than the melting point of W,
3410 /C14 C) dropped down to the crucible bottom through gaps of the
raw Al and Co plates. At the same time, the melt heated the raw Al
and Co plates along with the arc, and ﬁnally it fully enwrapped the
package of the Al foil and Mg pieces. In this case, the package was
insulated from furnace chamber and Mg was melted through heat
transmission of the surrounded W, Ta and Mo melt, as well as Co
and Al melt, but was not melted by the directly raying of arc. Thus,
the obvious vaporization and loss of Mg could be effectively sup-
pressed. The ingots were solution-treated at 1350 /C14 C for 8 h in a
vacuum, followed by furnace cooling. Then, they were sealed in an
evacuated quartz capsule back-ﬁlled with argon and aged at 800 /C14 C
for 50 h, followed by quenching. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis
was performed on the bulk samples to identify the phase consti-
tution using Cu K a (l ¼ 0.15405 nm) radiation at 40 kV and 40 mA
(Rigaku D/Max 2500PC). Back-scattered electron (BSE) images were
taken to investigate the microstructures of the as-cast and aged
samples and the crack paths of the tensile and compressive tested
samples using an SEM (JEOL JXA-8100), which was equipped with
energy-dispersion X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Oxford Instruments,
UK) to analyse the phase compositions and bulk compositions of
the as-cast alloys. Secondary electron images (SEI) of the fracture
surfaces were obtained using an FEI Quanta 200F scanning electron
microscope. The phase area fraction was statistically determined by
quantitative metallographic analysis of the microstructure images
taken in BSE mode. The misorientation of the grain boundaries was
measured from a crystal orientation map using electron backscat-
tering diffraction (EBSD) with scanning step sizes of 1 mme3 mm.
The distribution of Mg and O across the grain interior and grain
boundary was analysed by an Auger electron spectrometer (AES)
through line scanning manner. Tensile tests were conducted at
room temperature in air on an Instron 8810 machine at a strain rate
of 3 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1. Dog-bone plate specimens were used for the room-
temperature tensile tests. The dimensions of the parallel parts of
the dog-bone plates were 3 mm /C2 2 mm in cross section and 16 mm
in length. Compressive tests at room temperature, 600 /C14 C, 700 /C14 C
and 800 /C14 C were conducted in atmosphere at a strain rate of
3 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1 using an Instron 8810 machine. The compressive
cylinders were 3 mm in diameter and 6 mm in length. All speci-
mens for the tensile and compressive tests were mechanically
polished using SiC paper (1500-grit) with water before testing. To
obtain the average strength, three samples of each alloy were taken
for measurements of the tensile and compressive stress-strain
curves at each testing temperature. Transmission electron micro-
scopy (TEM) samples were prepared to investigate the dislocation
morphologies and deformation mechanisms of the samples com-
pressed at different temperatures using a JEOL JEM-2100F micro-
scope. The electron diffraction patterns and energy-dispersive X-
ray spectroscopy (EDS) of selected areas were obtained from the
TEM samples to con ﬁrm the phase crystal structure and composi-
tion of the aged samples, respectively. The preparation of the TEM
samples has been reported elsewhere [8].

Table 1 summarizes the bulk compositions of the as-cast alloys
and the phase compositions of the as-cast and aged samples. It is
known that Al and Mg have a burning loss during arc melting. For
all four as-cast alloys, the Mg additions do not cause any consid-

The EDS analysis indicates that in the as-cast condition, Mg atoms
partition to the Co 3Ta-based phase rather than to the g matrix, and
the Mg concentration in the Co 3Ta-based phase exhibits an
increasing trend, from 0.67 at.% to 2.15 at.%, as the nominal Mg
content increases from 0.1 at.% to 1.0 at.%, respectively. Most of Ta
atoms dissolve to the Co3Ta-based phase. Mo atoms partition to the
Co3Ta-based phase rather than to the g phase, and the Mo con-
centration in the Co 3Ta-based phase is almost two times that in the
g phase; however, the opposite occurs with Al. The W concentra-
tion is found to be slightly higher in the Co 3Ta-based phase than in
the g phase.
After the aging treatment, the cuboidal g0-Co3(Al, W) phase
precipitates from the supersaturated g matrix, while the Co 3Ta-
based phase disappears. This phase transformation results in a re-
distribution of the alloying elements in each phase of the g0-CoSS/
g0-Co3(Al, W) coherent microstructure, when compared to that in
the as-cast samples, especially in the distribution of Mg. It is clear
that Al distributes evenly in both phases. For Mo and W, their
concentrations in each of the g0 and g phases are almost at the same
level, but the concentrations in the g0 phase are slightly greater
than in the g phase. The Ta concentrations have a small variation in
each phase, but Ta obviously segregates in the g0 phase. These
elemental distributions are fairly consistent with those of Mg-free

To identify the mechanisms allowing the coherent
g/g0 micro-
structure to have such a signi ﬁcant variation in ductility with the
nominal Mg content at room temperature, the deformation and
failure behaviours were investigated. Fig. 5 shows the deformation
morphologies of the g/g0 microstructure in a position away from
the fracture plane 0.3 e0.5 mm along the tensile axis direction. The
four fractured alloys all show two groups of dense slip bands with
different orientations, between which the angle is approximately in
the range of 90 e120/C14 , indicating severe plastic deformation with
strain of 7.1 e18.4%, which correspond to the different densities of
the slip bands. The enlarged inserts in Fig. 5a and 5d reveal that the
long and straight slip lines go through the aligned g and g0 phases
simultaneously. Thus, one can understand that at room tempera-
ture, both the g-CoSS and the g0-Co3(Al, W) intermetallic phases can
continuously plastically deform with the same slip system in ten-
sion, which conﬁrms the excellent plastic deformation ability of the
intermetallic phase and reveals the reason why the g/g0 micro-
structure has such a higher tensile elongation [28]. Even the bulk
elongation has a larger difference when the nominal Mg content
changes from 0.1 to 1.0 at.%.
The SEM fractographic analysis shown in Fig. 6 displays the
tensile fracture mechanisms of the coherent g/g0 microstructure at
room temperature. For the 0.1Mg and 0.3Mg alloys, transgranular
fracture is observed, as seen in Fig. 6 a and 6b. The enlarged SEM
images inserted in Fig. 6a and 6b demonstrate dimple morphology,
revealing the dominant fracture mode to be transgranular ductile.
As the nominal Mg content increases to 0.7 and 1.0 at.%, a mixed
transgranular-intergranular fracture takes place, as observed in
Fig. 6 c and 6d. In the transgranular failure area, as seen inserts in
Fig. 6 e and 6f, the g/g0 microstructure fractures in a dimple mode,
whereas in the intergranular failure area, relatively smooth surfaces
reveal an intergranular cleavage mode. Partial intergranular
cleavage happening in the 0.7Mg and 1Mg alloys is most likely the
reason why their elongations are lower than those of the 0.1Mg and
0.3Mg alloys with fully transgranular ductile failure.
The compressive stress-strain curves of the four aged alloys at
RT, 600 /C14 C, 700 /C14 C and 800 /C14 C are shown in Fig. 7. All of the alloys
show noticeable plastic deformation and similar compressive
stress-strain characteristics. The alloys exhibit a strong strain-
hardening stage in the wide range of 20 e35% plastic strain after
yielding, and as the stress reaches the peak value, it drops quickly
corresponding to failure of the testing sample. The temperature
dependence of the 0.2% yield strength, s0.2, is plotted to determine
the effect of Mg on the strength, which is shown in Fig. 8. It is clear
that the 0.1Mg and 0.3Mg alloys have the same s0.2P level at the
various temperatures, and with further increase of the nominal Mg
content, s0.2P obviously decreases. The compressive strengths of
the Mg free alloy (702 MPa at 600 /C14 C, 771 MPa at 700 /C14 C and
739 MPa at 800 /C14 C [27]) are lower than those of the 0.1Mg and
0.3Mg alloys, but higher than those of the 0.7Mg and 1Mg alloys.
Under compression, the four alloys more or less show yield
strength anomalies ( Fig. 8 ). That is, s0.2P decreases with tempera-

4. Discussion
The backscattered SEM observations show that the additions of
0.1Mg~1Mg do not result in visible metallurgical changes in the
element distribution in each phase, the phase constitution and
fraction and the microstructure of the aged Co-9Al-4.5W-4.5Mo-
2Ta-0.02B alloy; however, the mechanical responses of the aged
Co-9Al-4.5W-4.5Mo-2Ta-0.02B alloy at room and high tempera-
tures are signi ﬁcantly affected by the nominal Mg contents, or the
Mg concentrations in the grain boundary. The tensile strength and
ductility at room temperature and the compressive strength at
600e800 /C14 C obviously decrease only when 0.7e1 at.% Mg are added
(Figs. 4 and 8 ), whereas tiny additions of 0.1 e0.3 at.% Mg show no
effect on these properties. The fractographic analysis shown in
Fig. 6 suggest that dependence of the mechanical properties of the
coherent
g/g' microstructure on the Mg content at room temper-
atures is relative to the intergranular failure, which may be caused
by a decrease of the grain boundary strength when more Mg seg-
regates on the grain boundary ( Fig. 3 and Table 1) to some extent in
this investigation. Degradation of the grain boundary strength in-
creases the tendency of intergranular failure ( Fig. 6 c and 6d) and
decreases the bulk ductility of the coherent g/g' microstructure
when intergranular cleavage occurs ( Fig. 4 and Table 2). The above
phenomena con ﬁrm that the segregation of more Mg may bring
about some metallurgical changes of the grain boundary and
enhance intergranular cleavage.
In particular, the transgranular or intergranular fracture of
polycrystalline intermetallics is found to usually depend on the
grain boundary precipitation, the grain boundary character distri-
bution (GBCD) [33e36], segregation of the impurity in the grain
boundary, and sliding and migration of the grain boundary. With
the perfect and clear grain boundaries (no precipitates) shown in
Fig. 3 , the grain boundary failure of the coherent g/g' microstruc-
ture caused by the precipitates is excluded. Early works on L1 2-
Ni3Al [37] found that only low-angle S1 and possibly S3 boundaries
are particularly resistant to intergranular fracture through avoiding
the cutting and stress concentration effects at the grain boundaries.
Therefore, the fracture strength or the ductility of a microstructure
with intergranular failure may be increased only by increasing the
fraction of S1 and S3 boundaries [32]. The frequency of the S1-3
counted is approximately 78% for the 0.1Mg and 0.3Mg alloys
(Fig. 10 ), which reinforce the fracture strength, as well as the
ductility of the coherent g/g' microstructure ( Table 2). In contrast,
the fraction of high-angle or high-CSL grain boundaries, which
particularly enforce intergranular fracture, increases with
increasing Mg concentration in the grain boundary. A high high-CSL
grain boundary fraction, especially in the 1Mg alloy, signi ﬁcantly
reduces the grain boundary strength and brings more intergranular
fracture fraction ( Fig. 6 d), which subsequently decrease the
strength and ductility of the coherent
g/g' microstructure at room
and/or high temperatures ( Table 2 and Fig. 8). Secondly, O is such a
well-known element which decreases the binding energy and
strength of the grain boundary [35,38,39]. From the failure modes
and mechanical properties versus the Mg content or the Mg con-
centration in the grain boundary ( Fig. 3 and Table 1 ), the phe-
nomenon that the higher Mg additions (0.7 e1.0 at.%) reduce the
strength and ductility of the Co-9Al-4.5W-4.5Mo-2Ta-0.02B alloy
can be naturally explained also from the concentration of the im-
purity O in the grain boundary. The correlation is that a higher Mg
content maintains a higher O concentration in the grain boundary,

5. Conclusions
A comprehensive study of the phase constitution and
morphology, grain boundary character distribution (GBCD), O
segregation at the grain boundary and mechanical behaviours of
the Co-9Al-4.5W-4.5Mo-2Ta-0.02B-(0.1, 0.3, 0.7, 1)Mg alloys was
conducted. The major conclusions are summarized as follows.
1) The microstructure of the as-cast 0.1 e1.0Mg alloys consisted of a
g-CoSS matrix and Co 3Ta-based chains, exhibiting a dendritic-
like morphology. After aging at 800 /C14 C for 50 h, the Co 3Ta-
based phase disappeared, and the microstructure was
composed of the g-CoSS matrix and nano-scale cuboidal g'-
Co3(Al, W) precipitates, and the two phases cohered with each
other. An increasing Mg content from 0.1 to 1.0 at.% did not
obviously change the cuboidal g'-Co3(Al, W) size (150 e200 nm
in the cube edge length) and fraction (80 e85%).
2) The Mg atoms were found to mainly segregate at the grain
boundaries and g'-Co3(Al, W) phase, and the peak value of the
Mg concentration of approximately 0.9 at.% at the grain
boundaries was reached in the 0.7Mg and 1Mg alloys. The S1~3
boundary fraction and the capability of excluding impurity O
from the grain boundaries obviously decreased when the
nominal Mg content increased up to 0.7 and 1 at.%.
3) The four alloys exhibited compressive ﬂow stress anomalies at
temperatures above 600 /C14 C. With less S1~3 boundary fraction
and higher O concentration at the grain boundary, the 0.7Mg
and 1Mg alloys always showed lower strength than the 0.1Mg
and 0.3Mg alloys at room temperature and 600 e800 /C14 C, while
the strength of the 0.1Mg and 0.3Mg alloys were almost at the
same level.
4) Under tension at room temperature, the 0.1Mg and 0.3Mg alloys
showing higher strength and elongation failed in a trans-
granular dimple mode owing to their higher S1~3 boundary
fraction and lower O concentration at the grain boundary, while
the 0.7Mg and 1Mg alloys with lower strength and elongation
fractured in a mixed transgranular-intergranular mode. Partial
intergranular cleavage was found to be the reason for the lower
tensile strength and ductility in the 0.7Mg and 1Mg alloys.
