In the present investigation, Cr 3C2–NiCr cermet coatings were deposited on two Ni-based superalloys,
namely superni 75, superni 718 and one Fe-based superalloy superfer 800H by detonation-gun thermal spray
process. The cyclic hot-corrosion studies were conducted on uncoated as well as D-gun coated superalloys in
the presence of mixture of 75 wt.% Na 2SO4 + 25 wt.% K 2SO4 ﬁlm at 900 °C for 100 cycles. Thermogravimetric
technique was used to establish the kinetics of hot corrosion of uncoated and coated superalloys. X-ray
diffraction, FE-SEM/EDAX and X-ray mapping techniques were used to analyze the corrosion products for
rendering an insight into the corrosion mechanisms. It was observed that Cr 3C2–NiCr-coated superalloys
showed better hot-corrosion resistance than the uncoated superalloys in the presence of 75 wt.% Na 2SO4 +
25 wt.% K 2SO4 ﬁlm as a result of the formation of continuous and protective oxides of chromium, nickel and
their spinel, as evident from the XRD analysis.

1. Introduction
Cermet coatings such as WC –Co, CrC and TiC deposited by
detonation-gun thermal spraying have been developed basically for
wear resistant properties. It is very essential to investigate corrosion
behaviour of the cermet coatings for extending its applicability in
protecting the turbine engines from the severe corrosive environ-
ments [1]. Due to depletion of high-grade fuels and for economic
reasons, residual fuel oil along with coal is used extensively in the
energy generation systems such as turbines, boilers, and industrial
waste incinerators. The impurities namely, Na, V, K and S present in
the fuel oil, react together to form low melting point compounds on
the surface of materials and induce accelerated oxidation (hot
corrosion). The degradation of material occurs when these molten
compounds dissolve the protective oxide layers that naturally form on
materials during boiler/gas turbine operation, inability to either
totally prevent the hot corrosion or its detection at an early stage
has resulted in several accidents, leading to loss of life and/or
destruction of engines/infrastructures [2]. L. Shi et al. [3] reported
that the co-deposition of Na
2SO4 + 25 wt.% K 2SO4 (m.p. 823 °C),
frequently occurs in gas turbines operating in a coal- ﬁred combined
cycle. To protect the turbine engines from the high temperature
corrosion attack facilitated by these impurities, thermal sprayed
coatings are deposited over the hot section components of turbine
engines and boiler tubes. The thermal sprayed coatings consist of
oxides and voids originating from spraying process that are found at
the splat boundaries, through which the coatings were mainly
attacked [4]. It has been proposed that the distance from the coating
surface to the coating/substrate interface along the splat boundaries
has a great effect on corrosion resistance of thermally sprayed coatings
[5]. D-gun process, offers the highest velocity (800 –1200 m s
− 1) for
the sprayed powders that are unattainable by the plasma and HVOF
processes [6]. The splat thickness of the D-gun sprayed coating is less,
as impact velocity is 2 –3 times higher than that of the other thermal
spray processes [7]. As a result, higher particle velocity during
deposition provides a minimum decomposition of the feed powder,
due to lower heat enthalpy and shorter duration involved in the
coating process [8]. Y.J. Zhang et al. [9] reported that D-gun process
entraps air during spraying and micropores acted as shelters for the
residual air; owing to the occurrence of rapid solidi ﬁcation during the
formation of coatings as well as time is too short for any deleterious
reaction, even if there were oxygen in the pores. Various thermal spray
processes, such as detonation-gun, plasma and high velocity oxy-fuel
(HVOF) spraying methods are mostly used to apply chromium carbide
coating to impart a wear resistance against abrasion and erosion in
corrosive environment at high temperature up to 900 °C [10]. The
superalloys are subjected to high temperature oxidation and hot
corrosion during service conditions in the gas turbines used for
various applications such as air craft, thermal power plants, energy
conversion systems, etc. D-gun-sprayed coatings exhibit several
advantages such as lower porosity, higher bond strength and hardness
with ﬁne grain structure [1 1]. G.Y. Lai [12] and S. Kamal et al. [13] have
studied hot corrosion behaviour of D-gun sprayed Cr
3C2–NiCr in high
temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) helium and Na 2SO4–60% V2O5
environments, respectively. However, the coatings with these micro-
structural characteristics need to be tested for combating hot

2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Development of coatings
2.1.1. Samples and coating powders
Nickel-based and iron-based superalloys superni 75, superni 718
and superfer 800H were used as the substrates, which were procured
from Mishra Dhatu Nigam Limited, Hyderabad (India) in the rolled
sheet form (the chemical compositions of the superalloys are shown
in Table 1 ). These superalloys are used for high temperature
application in boilers (super heater zones) and gas turbines. These
superalloys were chosen to study the effect of substrate elements on
hot corrosion properties of coating at high temperatures, especially
the effect of interdiffusion of coating and substrate elements on hot
corrosion. Rectangular specimens of dimensions (in mm) 20 × 15 × 5
were cut from the alloy sheets, polished using emery papers of 220,
400, and 600 grit sizes and subsequently on 1/0, 2/0, 3/0 and 4/0
grades, samples were degreased with acetone and subsequently grit
blasted with alumina powders (Grit 45) (36 mesh size) before
deposition of the coating by the D-gun process. A commercially
available 75 wt.% Cr
3C2–25 wt.% (Ni20Cr) powder is used in this study
(AMPERIT/584.072-H.C.Starck). It is evident from the micrographs of
the coating powder particle ( Fig. 1 ) that the agglomerated and
sintered Cr
3C2–NiCr powder gives a clear contrast between the
carbide and NiCr matrix phases, the powder particle show irregular
shaped morphology.
2.1.2. Detonation gun coating
Detonation-gun (Model: Awaaz) process was used to apply
coatings on all six surfaces (sides) of the superalloys at SVX Powder
M Surface Engineering Pvt. Ltd, New Delhi (India). The D-gun was
originally developed and patented by Union Carbide (now Praxair) in
1955 and developed independently in 1969 at the Institute of
Materials Science, Kiev, Ukraine [14], ARCI Hyderabad, INDIA has
procured this equipment from Ukraine. Standard spray parameters
were designed at ARCI Hyderabad for depositing the Cr
3C2–NiCr
coatings. All the process parameters, including the spray distance,
were kept constant throughout the coating process. The spraying
parameters were, oxygen/acetylene ﬂow rate (ratio) — 1:1.21, carrier
gas ﬂow rate (N
2) — 1.2 (m3/h), frequency 3 shots/s, spot size diameter
20 mm, spraying distance from nozzle 165 mm and powder ﬂow rate
1.0 to 2.0 g per shot.
2.2. Characterization of the coatings
The coated samples were wheel polished and then subjected to,
XRD, X-ray mapping and ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscope
(FE-SEM, FEI, Quanta 200F Company) with EDAX Genesis software
attachment; made in Czech Republic is used to characterize the
surface and cross-sectional morphology. The microstructural mor-
phology of the Cr3C2–NiCr coatings exhibits high density and very low
porosity, the details regarding characterization of the coating have
been discussed in our earlier publication [15].
2.3. Hot corrosion studies in molten salt environment
A salt mixture of Na
2SO4 + 25 wt.% K 2SO4 (wt.%) was properly
mixed in distilled water. After washing samples with acetone, the
specimens were then heated in an oven to about 250 °C. The
preheating of the specimens was found to be necessary for proper
adhesion of the salt layer. Thereafter, a layer of Na2SO4 + 25 wt.% K2SO4
mixture was applied uniformly on the warm specimens with the help
of a camel hair brush. The amount of the salt coating was kept in the
range of 4.0 –5.0 mg/cm
2. The salt coated specimens and the alumina
boats were then kept in the oven for 4 h at 150 °C. The samples taken
in the alumina boat were again weighed before exposing it to hot

3. Results
3.1. Thermogravimetric analysis
The kinetics of hot corrosion was determined from the weight
change (mg/cm
2) versus time plots for the uncoated and coated three
superalloys subjected to hot corrosion in Na 2SO4 + 25 wt.% K 2SO4
environments at 900 °C up to 100 cycles as shown in Fig. 2. The curve
shows an initial weight gain in all the cases. During the initial period,
oxidation of the free surface of the coated and uncoated superalloy
resulted in a high oxidation rate. The oxide grains have grown with the
extended exposure to corrosive environment. The increased oxide
grain size led to a reduced density for the diffusion pathways, which
resulted in a slow oxidation rate. The Cr3C2–NiCr-coated superalloys in
all cases show a much lower weight gain than the uncoated specimens
in the given molten salt environment. Coated superni 718 showed the
lowest weight gain, whereas uncoated superfer 800H showed the
highest weight gain. The weight gains of uncoated superni 718 and
superni 75 after 100 cycles are nearly three- ﬁfth and four-ﬁ fth,
respectively, as compared to that of superfer 800H. In general, the
oxidation behaviour of Cr
3C2–NiCr-coated superalloys follows nearly a
parabolic rate law, except for coated superfer 800H and coated superni
75, as it slightly deviates from the parabolic rate, the deviation has
been observed after 35 cycles, whereas uncoated superalloys totally
deviate from the parabolic rate law, as can be inferred from the square
of weight change (mg 2/cm4) versus number of cycle plots ( Fig. 2). The
parabolic rate constants (kp in 10 − 10 g2 cm− 4 s− 1) for the Cr 3C2–NiCr-
coated and uncoated superalloys after 100 cycles of hot corrosion run
are shown in Table 2. Cumulative weight gain per unit area for coated
and uncoated superalloys is shown in Fig. 3.

superalloy increases continuously, whereas weight gain of the coated
specimens is relatively high during the ﬁrst few cycles of hot
corrosion, but subsequently increase in weight gain is found to be
gradual for coated superalloys. The initial higher oxidation rate of the
coated specimens might be attributed due to air which is entrapped
during D-gun deposition and sheltered in the pores, since the cooling

to react with the surrounding coating alloys [9]. However, the coating
underwent in-situ reaction during high temperature oxidation
(900 °C) and was partially oxidised, during the subsequent cycles,
the formation of oxides has blocked the pores and acted as diffusion
barriers to the inward diffusion of corrosive species. This leads to a
slow oxide scale growth; consequently the weight gain is low.
Chromium exhibits higher af ﬁnity for oxygen to form Cr
2O3 during
the earlier stages of hot corrosion [16]. This shows that the oxidation
resistance of Cr 3C2–NiCr coating is solely based on the formation of
Cr2O3, which acts as a diffusion barrier for the corrosive species as
reported by Seierstein and Kofstad [17]. Once the oxides are formed at
the places of porosity, the growth of the oxides becomes limited
mainly to the surface of the specimens. This would relatively minimize
the weight gain and result in the steady state oxidation behaviour
with the progress of long term high temperature exposure, which are
in accordance with the similar observation reported for the plasma
sprayed NiCrAlY coatings on Nimonic 80A and SS41 alloy substrates,
respectively by Choi et al. [18] and Niranatlumpong et al. [19].
Poor corrosion resistance of uncoated superalloy superfer 800H is
due to intense spallation and sputtering which can be attributed to the
severe strain developed as a result of Fe
2O3 precipitation from the
liquid phase and the interdiffusion of intermediate layer of iron oxide.
Presence of Si, Al Fe, Mn and K phases in a thin layer might have
imposed severe strain on the scale, which may result in the spallation,
cracking and exfoliation of the oxide scale, the cracks might have
allowed the corrosive liquid to reach the metal substrate [20]. The
presence of Fe
2O3 in the spalled scale has also been reported to be
non-protective by Das et al. [21] during their hot corrosion study
on Fe 3Al-based iron aluminides in Na 2SO4 atmosphere. Sidhu et al.
[22–24] have evaluated the hot corrosion behaviour of HVOF-sprayed
Cr3C2–NiCr-coating on Fe- and Ni-based superalloys in a Na 2SO4–60%
V2O5 at 900 °C under cyclic conditions for 50 cycles. The weight gains,
reported in their work, were 2 to 3 times higher than that of the same
coatings, formulated by D-gun process, exposed to Na
2SO4 + 25 wt.%
K2SO4 environment for 100 cycles at the same temperature in the
present work.
The cumulative weight gain of coated and uncoated superalloys
after 100 cycles of hot corrosion is plotted in Fig. 3. The overall weight
gains for the uncoated superni 718, superni 75, and superfer 800H
alloys are 5.58, 5.80 and 6.21 mg/cm 2 respectively. The uncoated Ni-
based superni 718 and superni 75 superalloys showed a better
resistance to the hot corrosion and the weight gain was less as
compared to the uncoated Fe-based superfer 800H superalloy, higher
weight gain of coater superfer 800H may be due to the presence of
iron oxide which might have diffused into the coating up to the surface
(Fig. 5c), since the presence of Fe
2O3 in the scale has been reported to
be non-protective as discussed above. An overall weight gain of 2.800,
4.072, and 4.9800 mg/cm
2 was observed for the Cr 3C2–NiCr-coated
superni 718, superni 75, and superfer 800H superalloys (Fig. 3 ),
respectively. The Cr 3C2–NiCr coating provides the maximum hot
corrosion resistance to superni 718 and has been found successful in
reducing the weight gain by around 50% of that gained without a
coating. Coatings on superni 75 and superfer 800H superalloys reduce
the weight gain by 30% and 20% of that gained by the uncoated
superalloys, respectively, The superior corrosion resistance shown by
the Cr
3C2–NiCr-coated superni 718 might be ascribed to the formation
of Cr 2O3, NiO and NiCr 2O4 spinel present in the top surface scale,
further it has been observed that the scales formed on the coated
superni 718 superalloys were observed to be dense, compact and
without any spallation/sputtering or peeling of the scale, this also
contributes to the lower weight gain as evident from the lower
parabolic rate constant (0.221 × 10− 10 g2 cm− 4 s− 1).

effectiveness of the coatings due to the scale spallation resistance as
reported by Stott [29].
5. Conclusion
• Cr3C2–NiCr coatings, deposited on selected superalloys superni 75
superni 718 and superfer 800H using the detonation gun process,
showed a higher hot corrosion resistance in the molten salt
environment (Na 2SO4 + 25 wt.% K 2SO4) at 900 °C for 100 cycles as
compared to the uncoated superalloys.
• The surface oxide scale developed on coated superalloys is massive,
crack free, with dense clusters mainly consisting of oxides of Cr and
Ni, in which, irregular disc shaped Cr 2O3 distributed throughout the
top surface of the scale.
• The Cr 3C2–NiCr-coated superalloys in all cases showed a lower
weight gain than that of the uncoated specimens in the molten salt
environment.
• Coated superalloy 718 showed a lowest weight gain and parabolic
rate constant among the other coated superalloys.
• Better performance of coated superni 718 in the given environment
has been attributed for its tendency to form protective dense oxides
of chromium, nickel and their spinel.
• Cr
3C2–NiCr-coated and uncoated superalloys superni 718 and
superfer 800H showed the lowest and highest weight gains,
respectively, in the given environment.
