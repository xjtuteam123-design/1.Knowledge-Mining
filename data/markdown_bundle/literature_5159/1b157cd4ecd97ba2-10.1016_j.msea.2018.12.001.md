ABSTRACT 
Al2O3-dispersion-strengthened tungsten matrix alloys were fabricated by hydrothermal 
synthesis and powder metallurgy. The Al 2O3 content was varied from 0 to 1 wt%, and 
sintering was conducted for 8 h at 2350 °C. The addition of Al2O3 was found to significantly 
affect the grain size and compressive strength of the sintered alloys. The grain size of the 
sintered samples ranged from approximately 10 to 3 μm with increasing Al2O3 content. When 
the Al2O3 content was increased to 0.25 wt%, the microhardness and compressive strength 
reached the maximum value s of 386 HV and 1628 MPa, respectively. Subsequently, the 
deformation behavior of the W–0.25 wt% Al2O3 alloy at high temperature was studied. The 
constitutive equation relating the flow stress and deformation parameters of the W –0.25 wt% 
Al2O3 alloy was established, and the hot deformation activation energy ( Q) of the W–0.25 wt% 
Al2O3 alloy was found to be 321.79 kJ/mol.

1. Introduction 
Plasma-facing materials (PFMs) are the key to successful realization of commercial 
operation of fusion reactor s [1–3]. Therefore, research related to PFMs has long attracted 
attention. Tungsten and tungsten -based alloys are considered the most important candidate 
materials because of their excellent physical and noteworthy high-temperature properties, 
including a high melting point (3410 °C), low thermal expansion coefficient , outstanding 
creep resistance, low vapor pressure, good thermal conductivity [168 W/(m·K)], and excellent 
corrosion resistance [4–7]. However, certain characteristics, including low -temperature 
brittleness, high ductile –brittle transition temperatures ( ≥200–400 °C), radiation brittleness , 
and low recrystallization temperatures, limit the applications of these W-based alloys [8–10]. 
In recent years, researchers have performed in-depth stud ies to reduce the brittleness of 
W-based alloys. Particle dispersion strengthening is an effective way to improve the work 
brittleness, increase the recry stallization temperature, lower the ductile –brittle transition 
temperature, and enhance the irradiation ability [11–14]. In p articular, high -melting-point 
oxides such as ThO2, Y 2O3, La 2O3, CeO 2, ZrO 2, and Al 2O3 are added to the W matrix to 
enhance the high-temperature properties [15 –17]. It has been shown that the addition of 
oxides can refine the W grains effectively and modify the recrystallization behavior [13]. 
Among these oxides, due to its high hardness, excellent wear resistance, low density, 
excellent thermal stability and good chemical stability, Al2O3 has been widely used in copper, 
aluminum and magnesium matrix composites as the strengthening phase [18-21]. 
Furthermore, Al2O3 also exhibits excellent strength at elevated temperatures [21]. A nother 
outstanding advantage is the low cost of Al 2O3, which could significantly improve economic 
benefits. According to Wesemann [ 22] and Faleschini et al. [ 23], the fracture strength, 
recrystallization temperature, and fracture toughness of W–La2O3 composites are much better 
than those of pure W. The addition of Y 2O3 particles reportedly improved the density and 
microhardness of sintered samples [14, 17]. 
Most oxide-dispersion-strengthened ( ODS) composite materials have generally been 
prepared by pow der metallurgy, especially ODS tungsten materials. The densification 
temperature of tungsten powder is usually relatively high, ranging from 2200 to 2800 °C. It is 
difficult to reach the desired density using l ow-temperature sintering, whereas higher-

## Page 5

temperature sintering leads to a coarse -grained microstructure. Therefore, hot working is 
needed to eliminate the residual void s to obtain fully dense tungsten alloys. The constitutive 
equation of hot deformation is a bridge connecting the deformation temperature, strain rate , 
and flow stress [ 24]. It is very important to establish the constitutive equation of tungsten 
alloys for formulation of the hot working process. 
In this paper, Al2O3-dispersion-strengthened tungsten matrix alloys were fabricated by 
hydrothermal synthesis and powder metallurgy. The effects of the Al 2O3 content on the 
microstructure, grain size , and compressive propert ies of the W –Al2O3 composites were 
investigated, and the relative densit y and microhardness of the sintered samples were 
measured. Additionally, the constitutive equation relating the flow stress and deformation 
parameters of the W –0.25 wt% Al 2O3 alloy was established using data obtained from hot 
compression experiments. 
 
2. Materials and methods 
W–Al2O3 composite powders were prepared using a hydrothermal route that included 
hydrothermal process ing, calcination , and hydrogen reduction steps, and the detailed 
preparation process and technological parameters were shown in our previous work [25]. 
Subsequently, the reduced mixed powders of W (with an average particle size of 750 nm and 
a purity of 99.9%) and Al2O3 (with an average particle size of 10 nm and a purity of 99.9%) 
were immediately loaded into a rubber mold with a diameter of 30 mm and then squeezed 
into a cylindrical compact by cold isostatic pressing. The final pressure and hold time were 
200 MPa and 3 min, respectively. The cylindrical green compact was 22 mm in diameter and 
300 mm in length. The compact was consolidated by sintering at 2350 °C for 8 h under 
hydrogen atmosphere. 
Room-temperature compression tests were performed using a SHIMADZU AG -I 250 
KN precision universal testing machine with dimensions of 6 mm in diameter and 10 mm in 
length at a strain rate of 1. 67  10-3 s-1. The samples for hot compression tests were cylinders 
with a diameter of 5 mm and length of 9 mm. Hot compression tests were conducted on a 
Gleeble 1500D thermal simulat ion test machine at a heating rate of 10 °C/s and a holding 
time of 3 min. The experimental temperatures of the hot compression tests were set to 1000,

## Page 6

1100, 1200, 1300, and 1400 °C, and the strain rates were set to 0.01, 0.1, and 1 s -1. The entire 
experiment was conducted in a vacuum environment. 
The phases of the sintered samples were evaluated by a Bruker D8 X-ray diffractometer 
with a Cu Kα X -ray source. The microstructure of the sintered samples was characteriz ed 
using a TESCAN VEGA3 -SBH scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with an 
EDAX Octane Prime energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDXS) instrument. The relative 
densities of the sintered samples were measured via the Archimedes method. The hardnesses 
of the sintered samples were evaluated by Vickers microhardness testing using a 200 g load 
and a hold time of 15 s. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1 Microstructure of the sintered W–Al2O3 alloys 
The typical XRD data obtained from the sintered samples with different Al 2O3 contents 
are presented in Fig. 1. All of the peaks in these data correspond to the body-centered cubic 
polymorph of W, and no peaks corresponding to Al 2O3 were detected in these data. This is 
probably due to the low mass fraction of the Al 2O3 in the W substrate. The optical 
micrographs of the etched sintered samples with different Al2O3 contents are shown in Fig. 2. 
The change of grain size for the sintered samples can be clearly seen from the Fig. 2. As the 
Al2O3 content changed from 0 to 1.0 wt%, the grain size of the alloys changed from 
approximately 10 to 3 μm (see Fig. 3). The grain size of the W –1.0 wt% Al 2O3 alloy is 
approximately 70% smaller than that of the pure W alloy. It is reported that even when a fast 
sintering technology, spark plasma sintering, wa s used and 2 wt% Y 2O3 was added to the 
matrix, the grain size of the W –2% Y 2O3 alloy was 13.1 μm [13]. This indicates that the 
addition of Al2O3 can greatly refine the grain size of W –Al2O3 alloys. The reason is probably 
that Al2O3 doping increases the diffusion distance of tungsten atoms and hinders migration of 
grain boundaries during sintering, leading to grain refinement. Backscattered electron SEM 
images of the unetched sintered samples with different Al 2O3 contents are shown in Fig. 4. 
The images show that many black particles are distributed uniformly in the tungsten matrix. 
The EDXS point spectra in Fig. 5 show that the black particles are Al 2O3 particles, and t he 
black regions in Fig. 4(a) marked by red arrows are holes in the pure tungsten alloy . In 
addition, with the increasing Al 2O3 content, the grain size of Al2O3 in the sintered alloys

shown in Fig. 4. Large particles are distributed at grain boundaries and they are long or

3.2 Microhardness and relative density of the sintered W–Al2O3 alloys

The microhardness of alloy s is known to be closely related to their density and grain 
size. The measured values of the relative density are shown in Fig. 6 and indicate that the 
addition of Al2O3 leads to an increase in the relative density from approximately 94% for 
pure W to >96% for the W –1.0 wt% Al2O3 alloy. Dispersion of Al 2O3 can improve the 
sintering activity of the W alloys. Fig. 7 shows the microhardness of the sintered samples 
with different Al2O3 contents. As can be seen from Fig. 7, the composite with 0.25 wt% Al2O3 
possesses the highest hardness of 386 HV , presumably owing to a combination of dispersion 
and grain refinement strengthening. The alloys with 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 wt% Al 2O3 exhibit a 
lower microhardness (approximately 200 HV) that is 82.5% lower than that o f pure W. This 
is probably owing to the grain size of Al 2O3 particles increases and most of the bigger Al 2O3 
particles are distributed at grain boundaries, which results in cleavage between W grains, 
giving a relatively poor microhardness.

3.3 Compressive tests of the sintered W–Al2O3 alloys 
Fig. 8 shows the compressive stress –strain curves of W alloys with different Al2O3 
contents at a strain rate of 1. 67  10-3 s-1 and room temperature . The results in Fig. 8 are 
clearly consistent with the microha rdness analysis above . The variation of the ultimate 
compressive strength violates a commonly assumed tenet of science ( the Hall–Petch 
relationship), as illustrated in Fig. 9. The W–0.25 wt% Al 2O3 sample possesses the highest 
ultimate compressive strength of 1318 MPa and the largest strain-to-failure of more than 23%. 
The ultimate compressive strength and strain -to-failure increased by 14% and 32% in 
comparison with those of pure tungsten, respectively. This is probably the result of a 
combination of dispersion and grain refinement strengthening. When the Al2O3 content 
increased to 0.5 wt%, the ultimate compressive strength decreased sharply to approximately 
750 MPa.

3.4 Characteristics of high-temperature stress–strain curves of the W–0.25 wt% Al2O3 alloy 
Fig. 10 shows the true strain –stress curves of the W –0.25 wt% Al 2O3 alloy at elevated 
temperature, which indicate the relationship between the true stress and true strain at 
temperatures of 1000, 1100, 1200, 1300, and 1400 °C and strain rates of 1, 0.1, and 0.01 s-1. 
As shown in Fig. 10, when the deformation temperature is constant, the flow stress increases 
with increasing strain rate. The reason is that the time required for dynamic recovery and 
recrystallization decreases with increasing deformation rate. The work hardening caused by

## Page 12

deformation cannot be completely eliminated, so the deformation resistance increases. In 
addition, when the strain rate is constant , the flow stress decreases with increasing 
deformation temperature. The reason is probably that an increase in deformation temperature 
will enhance the atomic thermal activation and increase the atomic kinetic energy. This 
provides a greater driving force for diffusion of vacancy atoms and cross -slip and climb of 
dislocations, which makes it easier for dynamic recovery and recrystallization to occur. 
Except under strain rate s of 0.1 and 0.01 s-1 and a temperature of 1400 °C, the strain –
stress curves do not show obvious peak stress or steady -state stress. This indicates that there 
is on dynamic recrystallization during deformation of the W –0.25 wt% Al 2O3 alloy. In 
summary, dynamic recovery plays a dominant role in the high-temperature deformation of the 
W–0.25 wt% Al2O3 alloy, and the high deformation temperature and low strain rate are more 
conducive to dynamic recovery.

Conclusion 
In summary, Al2O3-dispersion-strengthened tungsten matrix alloys were fabricated by 
hydrothermal synthesis and powder metallurgy. The main findings of this study are as 
follows: 
(1) As the Al2O3 content changed from 0 to 1.0 wt%, the grain size of the alloys changed 
from approximately 10 to 3 μm. The addition of Al 2O3 can greatly refine the grain 
size of the W–Al2O3 alloys, probably because Al2O3 doping hinders the migration of 
grain boundaries, which is considered the main refinement factor during sintering. 
(2) Samples with an Al2O3 content of up to 0.25 wt% sintered at 2350 °C gave uniform 
distributions of Al2O3 particles in the W matrix, which inhibited W grain growth and

## Page 17

enhanced the strength. Owing to dispersion and grain refinement strengthening, the 
W–0.25 wt% Al 2O3 sample possessed the highest Vickers microhardness of 386 HV 
and room temperature compressive strength of 1318 MPa. 
(3) The strain–stress curves do not show obvious peak stress or steady -state stress. This 
indicates that there is on dynamic recrystallization during deformation of the W–0.25 
wt% Al 2O3 alloy. Dynamic recovery plays a dominant role in the high -temperature 
deformation of the W–0.25 wt% Al2O3 alloy, and a high deformation temperature and 
low strain rate are more conducive to dynamic recovery. 
(4) The hot deformation activation energy (Q) of the W–0.25 wt% Al2O3 alloy is 321.79 
kJ/mol. The thermal deformation constitutive equation of the alloy is
