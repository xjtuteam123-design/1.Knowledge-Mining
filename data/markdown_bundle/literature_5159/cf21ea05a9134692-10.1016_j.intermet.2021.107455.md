ABSTRACT 
The influence of V addition on the microstructure and high-temperature tensile performance of 
Ti46Al7Nb0.4W0.6Cr alloy, fabricated by using the cold crucible directional solidification method, is compre -
hensively investigated. The results show that V addition can refine the columnar grain width and promote the 
brittle-to-ductile transition (BDT) of the TiAl alloy. After the addition of 2 at.% V, the ultimate tensile strength 
and elongation increased at 973 – 1173 K. The fracture mode change from interlamellar to translamellar; this is 
attributed to the decrease in the angle between the lamellar alignment and growth direction. The BDT behaviour 
of V-containing TiAl alloy at 1073 K results from the transition from dislocation-dominated to twin-dominated 
deformation as indicated by transmission electron microscopic investigations. 
1. Introduction 
Owing to their low density, high specific strength, and excellent 
creep resistance and fatigue performance, TiAl alloys have broad 
application prospects in the aerospace and automotive fields [ 1 – 3 ]. 
Among them, high-Nb TiAl alloys have been widely studied because of 
their higher service performance at elevated temperatures than that of 
traditional TiAl alloys [ 4 – 6 ]. Room- and high-temperature properties of 
TiAl alloys can be significantly improved by alloying and optimizing the 
microstructure. The addition of Nb, W, Cr, V, and other alloying ele -
ments to TiAl alloys can enhance the strength and plasticity through the 
effect of solid solution strengthening, particularly for high-temperature 
service performance. Zhang et al. [ 7 ] reported that the yield strengths of 
Ti – 45Al – 10Nb alloys were much higher than those of conventional TiAl 
alloys having similar microstructures. Liu et al. [ 8 ] showed that 
Ti – 42Al – 6V – 1Cr alloys exhibit considerably high tensile strength and 
ductility at both room and high temperatures; this is attributed to the 
high lamellar colony fraction, fine grain size, and lamellar spacing. 
Couret et al. [ 9 ] studied the creep properties of TiAl alloys containing W 
at 1123 K and reported that the presence of W leads to the formation of 
pairs of jogs, hindering the propagation of dislocations due to its low 
diffusion. Furthermore, studies have been conducted to achieve micro -
structure optimisation by employing various processing methods. Kan 
et al. [ 10 ] found that the microstructure of as-EBM Ti – 45Al – 8Nb alloys 
contained a very limited B2 phase and very fine lamellar colony grains, 
which helped to improve the tensile properties of this alloy compared to 
cast-TiAl in the heat-treated condition. Ding et al. [ 11 ] introduced a 
gradient twin structure into a high-Nb TiAl alloy to improve the 
high-temperature tensile properties by applying a high-temperature 
torsion pre-deformation process. TNM alloys having a fine lamellar 
size prepared by using the spark plasma sintering method exhibit 
excellent room temperature and creep resistance, as reported by Voisin 
et al. [ 12 ]. It is worth noting that the B2 phase in the high-Nb TiAl alloy 
can inhibit the growth of α grains and prevent the coarsening of the 
lamellar grains [ 13 ]. In addition, the directional solidification (DS) 
method can achieve precise control of the lamellar microstructure of 
TiAl alloys, eliminate the transverse grain boundary, and improve its 
axial service performance at high temperatures. Many researchers have 
used the DS method to prepare TiAl alloys with controllable micro -
structures, such as lamellar orientation and phase distribution, thereby 
achieving excellent mechanical properties [ 14 – 17 ]. 
The brittle-to-ductile transition (BDT) is a common phenomenon in 
which the strength and plasticity of materials, such as nickel [ 18 ], iron 
[ 19 ], tungsten [ 20 ], TiAl [ 21 ], NiAl [ 22 ], AlLi [ 23 ], and metallic glass 
[ 24 ], depend on the temperature change. Imayev et al. [ 25 ] found that 
the BDT in TiAl alloys consists of two steps: the first is an increase in 
ductility controlled by thermally activated relaxation processes at the 
grain boundary, and the second is within the grains. Booth et al. [ 21 ] 
studied the BDT of γ -TiAl single crystals and states that the temperature 
of the BDT was closely related to the strain rate, whereas the activation

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Materials preparation 
Ti46Al7Nb0.4W0.6Cr and Ti46Al7Nb0.4W0.6Cr2V alloys were 
prepared by utilising high-purity Ti, Al, Nb, W, Cr and V. The master 
ingots were prepared by applying the vacuum consumable melting 
technology and then remelted in a vacuum cold crucible induction 
melting furnace under an argon atmosphere to ensure compositional 
homogeneity. The actual chemical compositions of the two alloys, listed 
in Table 1 , were measured by using inductively coupled plasma atomic 
emission spectrometry method. DS experiments were performed by 
cutting φ 20 mm bars from the master ingots. The steps and methods of 
CCDS can be found in Refs. [ 30 , 31 ]. Finally, a directionally solidified 
square ingot, having dimensions of 30 mm × 30 mm × 120 mm, was 
obtained.

2.3. High-temperature tensile test 
To ensure that tensile specimens were sampled from the stable 
growth zone of DS, each specimen was taken from the middle of the 
directionally solidified ingot, as shown in Fig. 1 (a). The total length of 
the ingot was 120 mm, whereas the total length of the high-temperature 
tensile specimen was 45 mm with a thickness of 2 mm ( Fig. 1 (b)). The 
distance between the sample and the top and bottom of the ingot was 
37.5 mm, that with the left and right sides was 7.5 mm, and that with the 
front and back was 14 mm. The CCDS ingots were cut into high- 
temperature tensile test specimens having a cross-section of 5 × 2 mm 
and gauge length of 15 mm. The surface of the specimens, particularly 
the two end faces, were further ground by using 2000-grit SiC to obtain 
an adequate surface finish and eliminate stress concentrations caused by 
surface defects. The axial high-temperature tensile tests were performed 
by using an electronic universal material testing machine (AG-X plus 
250 kN, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) with a strain rate of 5 × 10
 4 
s
 1

 5

control method of the strain rate was implemented according to the GB/ 
T 228.2A standard. At least three tests were performed for each condi -

remained in a resistance furnace, which was equipped with 
temperature-measuring devices at the top, middle, and bottom. Once all 
three-measuring devices reached the preset temperature and stabilised 
for 5 min, the experiment was started. 
3. Results and analysis 
3.1. Macro/microstructure of directionally solidified high-Nb TiAl alloys 
Fig. 2 shows the macrostructures of the longitudinal sections of the 
two directionally solidified TiAl alloys where the DS growth direction is 
from bottom to top. It can be observed that the columnar crystals of the 
two alloys are arranged vertically along the direction of DS growth. 
However, owing to the presence of lateral heat dissipation of the cold 
crucible, there is a small angular deviation from the axial direction. The 
upper part is the meniscus, the position where the DS process stops [ 32 ], 
which is the quenching zone. The fine columnar grains are arranged 
along the direction of heat flow because of the chilling effect. Fig. 2 (a) 
shows a macrostructure image of the Ti46Al7Nb0.4W0.6Cr alloy. It can 
be observed that the columnar crystals are considerably large and grow 
continuously from bottom to top. The average and maximum widths of 
the columnar crystals are 1.83 mm and 5.38 mm, respectively. After the 
addition of V, the columnar crystals produce a significant refinement 
effect ( Fig. 2 (b)), with an average crystal size of 1.37 mm. Compared 
with the macrostructure without V, the grain distribution along the di -
rection of heat flow is more uniform and the degree of dispersion is less. 
Fig. 3 shows the SEM images of two directionally solidified high-Nb 
TiAl alloys. The red arrow indicates the direction of DS growth. It can be 
observed that the microstructures of the high-Nb TiAl alloy consist of 
lamellae and a bright network structure. There are long strips of white 
and dark grey coupled in the lamellar phase, and these are arranged 
along the DS growth direction and connected with each other, forming a 
bright network structure. According to a study conducted by Chen et al. 
[ 33 ], adding a large amount of Nb to TiAl alloys produces a distinct 
microscopic segregation phenomenon. In addition, owing to the

3.2. High-temperature tensile properties 
The high-temperature tensile properties of the two alloys were tested 
at 973, 1023, 1073, 1123, and 1173 K, and the results are shown in 
Fig. 6 . Compared with the Ti46Al7Nb0.4W0.6Cr alloy, the addition of V

4.1. Temperature-dependent fracture behaviours 
Fig. 7 shows the SEM images of the fracture morphology of the 
directionally solidified Ti46Al7Nb0.4W0.6Cr alloy after the high- 
temperature tensile test. The fracture mode of this alloy is mainly an 
interlamellar fracture. Fig. 7 (a), (b), and (c) show the fracture mor -
phologies of the alloy at 973 K, 1073 K, and 1173 K, respectively. Fig. 7 
(a)displays the separation of two large lamellae, indicated by the posi -
tion shown by the yellow dotted line, representing the two front and rear 
lamellae. As the high-temperature tensile samples are cut along the DS 
growth direction, the stress direction of the sample is the same as the DS 
direction. As indicated by the observation of the microstructure 
described in Fig. 3 (b), the angle between the lamellar direction and the 
DS growth direction is greater than 60
◦

applied, the alloy tends to fracture along the lamellae, resulting in the 
peeling between the lamellae and exposing the fracture morphology of a 
large lamellae surface. Fig. 7 (b) represents a magnified view of the 
dotted frame indicated in (a) where it can be seen that the edges and 
corners of the lamellar boundary at 973 K are clearly demarcated, 
confirming brittleness characteristics. The interlamellar fracture 
morphology at 1073 K is observed in Fig. 7 (c) revealing the presence of 
large planes in multiple lamellar directions. The blue arrow points out 
that the lamellar plane is stripped. Magnification of the yellow dotted 
frame in (c) is represented by Fig. 7 (d). Here, the torn edge of the 
lamellar microstructure exhibited spheroidisation and degeneration to 
some extent at 1073 K. The fracture morphology at 1173 K is shown in 
Fig. 7 (e) where cracks are caused by the separation between lamellae, as 
indicated by the position marked with a blue arrow. It is evident that the 
tearing edges exhibit obvious plastic characteristics where the parallel 
lamellar boundaries are seriously degraded and connected to form a 
small dimple structure ( Fig. 7 (f)).

TiAl alloy [ 40 ]. The TiAl alloy deformation at room temperature is 
accompanied by normal and superlattice dislocation movement where 
the slip of the 1/2[110] dislocation is the dominant deformation mode.

However, the [011] and [101] superlattice dislocations are impeded by

mobility. This phenomenon is attributed to the following two reasons: 
first, the stress required for dislocation movement decreases with an 
increase in temperature; second, the mobility of the 1/6[112] part of the 
pinned dislocation increases [ 41 ]. The b = 1/2[110] dislocation is more 
likely to slip because of thermal activation. Moreover, the b = 1/6[112] 
twin dislocation is expected to slip more easily owing to the significant 
increase in twinning at high temperatures. Therefore, the deformation of 
TiAl alloy at low temperature is dominated by the 1/2[110] dislocation 
slip, and the 1/6 [112] partial dislocation will form the faulted dipoles in 
order to impede movement of the [011] and [101] superlattice dislo -
cation. At high temperatures, the deformation mode of TiAl alloy is 
twinning, dominated by 1/6 [112] twin dislocations accompanied by 
1/2[110] dislocation slip. The change in the deformation mechanism 
leads to the BDT of the TiAl alloys as the temperature changes, which is 
consistent with the results presented in Fig. 6 .

5. Conclusions 
In this study, the effects of V addition on the microstructure and BDT 
of DS Ti46Al7Nb0.4W0.6Cr alloy are investigated in detail. The main 
conclusions are summarized as follows. 
(1) Compared with the Ti46Al7Nb0.4W0.6Cr alloy, the addition of V 
can: refine the columnar crystal and banded B2 phase spacing of 
DS TiAl alloy; and reduce the angle between the lamellar orien -
tation and DS growth direction. 
(2) V addition can improve the ductility of high-Nb TiAl alloys at 
high temperatures and reduce the BDT temperature to 
1023 – 1073 K, relating to more grain and twin interfaces. 
(3) The fracture morphology of the TiAl alloy reveals that a change in 
fracture mode from interlamellar to translamellar occurs after the 
addition of V, with the lamellar microstructure degradation 
becoming evident with an increase in temperature. 
(4) The BDT behaviour of the high-Nb TiAl alloy at 1073 K after V 
addition results from the transition from dislocation-dominated 
to twin-dominated deformation.
