Segregation at stacking faults within the c0 phase of two Ni-base
superalloys following intermediate temperature creep

Using state-of-the-art energy dispersive spectroscopy, it has been established for the ﬁrst time that there exists signiﬁcant com-
positional variation (enrichment of Co and Cr and deﬁciency of Ni and Al) associated with superlattice intrinsic stacking faults cre-
ated in the ordered c0 precipitates following intermediate temperature deformation of two commercial superalloys. The results
indicate that long range diﬀusion of these elements is intimately involved in the precipitate shearing process and is therefore closely
linked to the time-dependent deformation of the alloys.

A complete understanding of the shearing of the
strengthening c0 phase in Ni-base superalloys is essential
for developing physics-based deformation models of
these important materials for aerospace and power gen-
eration systems. There is considerable evidence that
under creep conditions shearing in the c
0 phase occurs
predominantly by the formation of superlattice stacking
faults (SISF and/or SESF) at intermediate temperatures
[1–8]. The formation of these stacking faults is a key
recovery process that relieves the build-up of dislocation
content within the c channels. It has been proposed that
the rate-limiting step for c0 shearing is associated with
the waiting time at interfaces required for diﬀerent 1/2
<1 1 0> matrix dislocations to climb and react to form
appropriate 1/3 <1 1 2> dislocations for shearing to
occur [3]. Dislocations of the type 1/3 <1 1 2> have also
been proposed to cause microtwinning in CMSX-4
/C2101
alloys at moderate temperatures [4].
Alternatively, it has been proposed that SESFs and
microtwins form by propagation of two closely sepa-
rated 1/6 <1 1 2> partials on consecutive (1 1 1) planes
[5–9]. However, without any atomic rearrangement, a
two-layer complex stacking fault (CSF) or pseudotwin
would be formed due to the creation of wrong nearest
neighbors. Since the energy of these faults should be
very large, it has been hypothesized that the rate of
shearing by Shockley partial pairs is controlled by
the elimination of the wrong nearest neighbors via
local diﬀusion – a process termed “reordering”. This
diﬀusion-mediated reordering process can convert the
high-energy, two-layer CSFs into a low-energy SESF.
A detailed analysis of the possible atomic movements
associated with such reordering using Vienna Ab-initio
Simulation Package (VASP) calculations has also been
presented by Kovarik et al. [9].
In these previous studies, it has been assumed that the
local composition at the faults remains constant, and
that shearing/reordering involves only the rearrange-
ment of atomic conﬁgurations adjacent to the faults.
However, as pointed out by Kovarik et al. [9], enhanced
intensity of the atomic columns at SISF, SESF and

In order to demonstrate conclusively that segregation
to stacking faults may be an important general phenom-
enon, results from two widely employed commercial
superalloys following creep deformation are presented.
In the ﬁrst, a polycrystalline sample of an advanced
powder metallurgy alloy-ME3 has been examined after
tensile creep at 677 /C176C and 724 MPa and a strain of
2%. The second sample is from a single crystal of
CMSX-4 subjected to creep at a temperature of 750 /C176C
and a stress of 750 MPa to a strain of 8.6% [10]. Trans-
mission electron microscopy (TEM) foils were extracted
by cutting samples parallel to the tensile axis by spark
erosion, followed by subsequent electropolishing with
a 6% perchloric acid in methanol (10% solution using
60% perchloric acid) at 18 V and /C0 10 /C176C.
In the alloy ME3 sample, a number of faults within
both the c and c0 phases, as well as microtwins, were
observed. Note the slightly enhanced HAADF intensity
at the fault ( Fig. 1 a) which is an SISF oriented along
<1 1 0> zone axis with the {1 1 1} fault plane parallel
to the beam direction. The enhanced contrast observed
at this camera length (100 mm and 300 kV) may be
due to both Z-contrast and diﬀraction eﬀects [11], and
EDS analysis is required to conﬁrm variation in the
local composition. Several elemental maps recorded
from the same area are also depicted in Figure 1 a. The
composition mapping was performed over an area of
12.5 /C2 7.2 nm. The EDS spectra were collected at an
interval of 0.03 nm. Segregation of Co and Cr is clearly
observed, whilst Ni and Al are deﬁcient at the stacking
fault. Composition proﬁles for the same dataset are
shown in Figure 1 b. At each position shown in Figure
1b, the intensities have been integrated parallel to the
fault over the entire image to increase the signal-to-noise
ratio of the data. The intensities were converted to atom
fractions using the standard k factors for the EDS anal-
ysis using Bruker Esprit EDS analysis software. Intensi-
ties for K a radiation were utilized for Ni, Al, Co and Cr.
For W and Ta, M peaks were preferred over L peaks
since the latter were masked by the strong Ni-K b peaks.
For Nb and Mo, L peaks were chosen over the K peaks
since the latter had lower intensities. Using this
approach, the composition of the bulk c
0 phase shown
in Figure 1b is in good agreement with atom probe data
previously obtained for this alloy [12]. It can be seen that
the principal results observed in the chemical maps are
reinforced in the quantitative analysis, with Co and Cr
clearly segregating to the fault, while Ni and Al are
reduced locally. Note that the data are plotted on a log-
arithmic scale, so the reduction of Ni is much larger than
for all other elements. A more subtle trend is the slight
enrichment of Mo at the stacking fault; this is not as
clear as with Cr or Co due to the lower concentration
of Mo (<2 at.%) in the alloy itself. While the segregation
trends shown in Figure 1 b are qualitatively correct, the
magnitude of the change in composition at the fault will
depend on details of the incident probe geometry and
delocalization of the electron probe due to phonon scat-
tering, as discussed recently by Forbes et al. [13].

This study has established for the ﬁrst time that there
exists signiﬁcant compositional variation associated
with stacking faults during intermediate temperature
deformation of two commercial superalloys. The obser-
vation that there is little heavy element segregation to
the faults is surprising when considering the enhanced
HAADF intensity at the faults. The question is whether
the enhanced intensity can be understood on the basis of
these new EDS results. Taking the data in Figure 1 and
averaging the composition in a narrow rectangular
region (dimensions 7 nm long by 2 nm wide) parallel
to the fault, it is possible to obtain an average atomic
number for both the matrix and spanning the fault.
The standard k-factors from the EDS software were
used for this analysis, which yields an “average” atomic
number at the stacking fault of 27.4, while that in the
adjacent matrix is 26.6. Although this is not a large dif-
ference, it is consistent with the contrast observed in
HAADF images, where the image intensity scales
approximately as the Z
/C24 1.7-2. Apparently the loss of Ni
and Al from the fault region is suﬃciently oﬀset by the
gain in Co, and to a lesser extent Cr, to result in a net
gain in average atomic number.
There are few previous reports of Suzuki segregation
on extended stacking faults in other alloys [14–17]. For
example, a higher concentration of Si was seen at stack-
ing faults in a Cu–Si alloy [14], leaving behind a Si
denuded region adjacent to the fault. Han et al. [15] have
shown that wider stacking faults with increased solute
levels of mostly Mo, Al and occasionally Ti and Nb in
a Co–Ni–Cr based superalloy. Further, Koizumi et al.
[16] have shown that extended SFs are produced in the
matrix phase of Co–Ni based superalloys following sta-
tic annealing when Nb is added in excess of 2%,
although chemical analysis failed to reveal the presence
of segregation. Recently, Yang et al. [17] have shown
Suzuki type segregation on the SFs in Mg–Zn–Y alloy
based on higher intensity on the atomic columns. How-
ever, none of these studies has provided decisive evi-
dence in terms of experimental measurement of speciﬁc
elements segregating to stacking faults.
The segregation observed in this study is unique in
comparison to previous work since the eﬀect is only
observed in the c
0 ordered phase, not in the face-centered
cubic matrix. As described recently [9,10], shearing of the
c0 phase by forming either SISFs or SESFs is hypothe-
sized to involve a local diﬀusion process involving
reordering. The hypothesis forwarded is that vacancy-
mediated reordering can occur between adjacent anti
phase boundary (APB)/CSF faults, thereby forming an
SISF, while reordering a two-layer CSF will result in
an SESF. The segregation behavior observed presently
suggests that long-range diﬀusion of elements is also
intimately associated with these shearing processes. A
rudimentary explanation is obtained by simply noting
that the local composition of the faults is closer to that
of the c phase as a result of segregation. Therefore, a rea-
sonable hypothesis is that the energies of the faults that
are precursors to the SISFs and SESFs (namely APB/
CSF and two-layer CSFs) are being lowered by the local
compositional changes.
Another important question concerns the diﬀusion
process itself. In the ﬁrst scenario, it can be postulated
that diﬀusion occurs perpendicular to the faults in which
case it might be expected that Ni and Al could “pile up”
near the faults while Co and Cr might be depleted par-
allel to the faults. However, there is presently no evi-
dence for this based on EDS compositional proﬁles
presented above. A second scenario is that diﬀusion
occurs parallel or along the faults, with Co and Cr
diﬀusing from the c channel, while Ni and Al diﬀuse

However, faults have been observed to span across
entire c

sion would not be fast enough to enable formation of
the local compositions exhibited by these extended
faults. Furthermore, additional analysis reveals no
evidence for variation in elemental concentration along
these faults, as might be expected if this is due to diﬀu-
sion from the c channel. A third possibility is that there
is indeed diﬀusion to/from the c channels occurring
along the dislocation lines of the shearing partials.
Faster diﬀusion rates (and distances) along the disloca-
tion cores of partial dislocations may also provide
natural conduits for ﬂow of atomic species necessary
to lower the energy of the faults and enable shearing
to take place.
In actuality, both reordering and long-range diﬀusion
may be occurring simultaneously in the shearing
process. However, since reordering requires atomic
movements over only short distances, of the order of
interplanar spacing, it might be expected that the move-
ment of partials is instead controlled by the rate of dif-
fusion of segregating (and de-partitioning) elements.
The present results demonstrate a clear compositional
signature associated with the c
0 shearing process, and
fundamentally changes the perspective with regard to
the rate-limiting process associated with the formation
of these faults at intermediate temperatures.
In conclusion, observations of segregation to stacking
faults within the c0 phase are presented for the ﬁrst time
in a commercial disc alloy (ME3) and a single crystal
blade alloy (CMSX-4). In both alloys, there is a clear
enrichment of the faults with Co and Cr and a depletion
of Ni and Al; in addition the fault in ME3 is also Mo
enriched and the fault in CMSX4 is enriched in W.
Quantitative analysis of the compositions on the fault
and in the matrix indicates a small increase in the
average atomic number of the elements, consistent with
the enhanced brightness of the faults in HAADF imag-
ing. There is no evidence of a depletion zone adjacent to
the fault, and no consistent variation in composition
along the faults. Calculated diﬀusion distances for
CMSX-4 are too short to account for the width of the
stacking faults that are observed, suggesting that diﬀu-
sion may occur along the partial dislocation “pipes”
during the shearing process.
