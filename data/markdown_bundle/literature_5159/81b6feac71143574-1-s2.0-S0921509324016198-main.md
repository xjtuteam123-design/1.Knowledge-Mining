ABSTRACT
91W-5.5Ni-3.3Fe-0.2Co alloy was prepared by powder metallurgy and rotary swaging post-treatment, and the 
effects of different annealing temperatures on the evolution of microstructure and mechanical properties of the 
alloy were investigated. The results show that annealing at different temperatures can effectively improve the 
mechanical properties of 91W-5.5Ni-3.3Fe-0.2Co. Compared to the swaged alloy, the hardness of the W-phase 
and the bulk of the alloy annealed at 300 
◦
C increases significantly, with strength maximized through strain 
ageing. When the annealing temperature reaches 900 
◦
C, the alloy recovery process is significant, the hardness of 
the W-phase and the bulk decreases, and the strength decreases by about 5 %, while elongation increases by 70 
%. Higher annealing temperatures can refine the W grain size and reduce the W-W continuity. Furthermore, the 
fracture mode of the annealed alloys at 900 
◦
C occurs predominantly through matrix phase tearing. A detailed 
analysis of the relationship between mechanical properties and dislocation motions indicates that annealing at 
900 
◦
C increases the degree of co-deformation of the W-phase and the matrix phase, improves interface bonding, 
and optimizes the mechanical properties.
1. Introduction
Tungsten heavy alloys consist of W particles and a matrix phase 
containing transition metal elements (Ni, Fe, Co, etc.). The matrix phase, 
with a face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal structure, surrounds spherical 
tungsten grains with a body-centered cubic (BCC) crystal structure 
[ 1 – 5 ]. Tungsten heavy alloys exhibit excellent properties, including 
high strength, high density, and high hardness, which give them a wide 
range of applications in aerospace, military, and other essential fields 
[ 6 – 9 ]. Sintered products are typically manufactured by liquid phase 
sintering, followed by vacuum heat treatment to eliminate hydrogen 
embrittlement and related effects [ 10 ]. However, as technology ad -
vances, the performance of sintered products often fails to meet service 
requirements.
Recently, researchers worldwide have investigated how to optimize 
the strength and toughness of tungsten alloys to enhance their service 
capability in extreme environments. Two main methods exist to 
strengthen tungsten alloys: the addition of alloying elements and work 
hardening. The alloying elements currently introduced into W-Ni-Fe 
alloys include Co, Mo, Cr, Re, Cu, etc. [ 11 – 15 ], with Co being the most 
effective. The addition of Co enhances the solubility of W in W-Ni-Fe 
alloys, strengthens the interfacial bonding between W and γ phases, and 
optimizes the comprehensive performance of tungsten alloys [ 16 ]. 
Typically, work hardening is achieved through swaging, rolling, and 
extrusion, which significantly enhances strength by refining the W grain 
size. Çalıs ¸ kan et al. [ 17 ] employed cold rotary swaging on a 
vacuum-sintered 90 W-7Ni-3Fe alloy, achieving 15 % deformation. The 
resulting strength and elongation were 1105 MPa and 7.3 %, respec -
tively. Das et al. [ 18 ] introduced Co and Mo elements into a W-Ni-Fe 
alloy, and after 20 % swaging, the resulting strength and elongation 
were 1290 MPa and 5.3 %, respectively. Subsequent heat treatment at 
1373 K for 1 h resulted in reduced strength (1179 MPa) and increased 
elongation (12.3 %).

plasticity of tungsten alloys are typically adjusted through annealing 
heat treatment[ 19 – 21 ]. Panchal et al. [ 22 ] studied the changes in the

2.1. Materials
The 91W-5.5Ni-3.3Fe-0.2Co alloy was experimentally prepared 
using commercially available tungsten, nickel, iron, and cobalt powders. 
The properties of the raw powders are summarized in Table 1 . The 
powders were placed proportionally in ball milling jars, combined with

2.2. Test methods
Dog-bone-shaped specimens with a standard cross-section size of 8 
× 3.4 × 2.5 mm were made by Electrical Discharge Machining (EDM). 
Tensile samples were sharpened with 180#, 400#, 800#, 1500#, 
2000# sandpaper sequentially. Mechanical polishing was carried out 
until the surface was smooth and free of scratches to eliminate surface 
contaminants and residual stresses. The microhardness was determined 
using a Vickers hardness tester (THV-1MDX) with a dwell time of 15 s. 
The overall load was set at 500 g, with the W-phase and γ -phase loads at 
50 g each. Five points were measured for each sample and averaged to 
yield an average value. Tensile tests were carried out using an Instron 
5982 high strength universal testing machine with a strain rate of 0.5 
mm/min. At least three experiments were conducted for each group, 
with the tensile properties determined according to ASTM E8M-15a 
standards.

4.2. Failure behavior of annealing alloys
During the tensile fracture process, the γ phase, acting as a plastic 
phase, undergoes plastic deformation initially. The γ phase exhibits a 
higher dislocation density due to dislocation generation and slip pro -
cesses. Because dislocations cannot traverse the phase boundary, they 
accumulate at the interface between tungsten grains and the γ phase, 
activating the slip system within the tungsten grains and ultimately 
leading to fracture. Consequently, the movement of dislocations and 
their density are closely linked to the plastic deformation of tungsten 
alloys. In recent years, researchers have employed various methods to 
elucidate the relationship between dislocations and plastic deformation, 
thereby optimizing alloy properties [ 31 – 33 ]. Currently, the character -
ization of the density of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) 
through EBSD technology is increasingly gaining popularity. GNDs are 
derived from measurements of lattice rotation and curvature. Although 
this represents only an estimated lower bound of dislocation densities, it 
remains scientifically reliable for analyzing dislocations [ 34 ].
Fig. 9 illustrates the EBSD results for the 91 W-A300 and 91 W-A900 
alloys. Fig. 9 a – b demonstrate that the W phase is enveloped by the γ 
phase; additionally, numerous small cracks are evident near the primary 
crack region. In the case of 91 W-A300, the cracks primarily result from 
the debonding of the WW and WM interfaces, whereas 91 W-A900 ex -
hibits a greater number of W cleavage cracks than 91 W-A300. Fig. 9 c – d 
presents GND images for the 91 W-A300 and 91 W-A900 samples. The 
color gradient from blue to red corresponds to dislocation density, 
ranging from low to high. The dislocation density in the GND correlates 
with the degree of plastic deformation, and changes in dislocation 
density exhibit similarities to mechanical properties. A comparison of 
the two figures reveals a significant increase in dislocation density at the 
crack, along with a greater degree of plastic deformation within the 
grains. The GND color predominantly concentrates in the γ phase. 
Simultaneously, a substantial number of dislocations are present at the 
interface between the W phase and the γ phase. This occurs because, 
with increasing tensile stress, the γ phase initially undergoes plastic
