Nb-Base FS-85 Alloy as a Candidate Structural Material
for Space Reactor Applications: Effects of Thermal Aging
KEITH J. LEONARD, JEREMY T. BUSBY, DAVID T. HOELZER,
and STEVEN J. ZINKLE
The proposed uses of ﬁssion reactors for manned or deep space missions have typically relied on
the potential use of refractory metal alloys as structural materials. Throughout the history of
these programs, a leading candidate has been Nb-1Zr, due to its good fabrication and welding
characteristics. However, the less-than-optimal creep resistance of this alloy has encouraged
interest in the more complex FS-85 (Nb-28Ta-10W-1Zr) alloy. Despite this interest, only a
relatively small database exists for the properties of FS-85. Database gaps include the potential
microstructural instabilities that can lead to mechanical property degradation. In this work,
changes in the microstructure and mechanical properties of FS-85 were investigated following
1100 hours of thermal aging at 1098, 1248, and 1398 K. The changes in electrical resistivity,
hardness, and tensile properties between the as-annealed and aged materials are compared.
Evaluation of the microstructural changes was performed through optical microscopy (OM),
scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The
development of intragranular and grain-boundary precipitation of Zr-rich compounds as a
function of aging temperature was followed. Brittle tensile behavior was measured in the
material aged at 1248 K, while ductile behavior occurred in samples aged above and below this
temperature. The eﬀect of temperature on the under- and overaging of the grain-boundary
particles is believed to have contributed to the mechanical property behavior of the aged
materials.

I. INTRODUCTION
REFRACTORY metal alloys have traditionally been
candidate materials for high-temperature applications,
oﬀering the potential for large improvements in perfor-
mance or thermodynamic eﬃciency over more conven-
tional alloys. However, due to poor oxidation resistance,
their use has been limited to speciﬁc applications. One
application for which there is great interest in refractory
metal alloys is compact, space-based ﬁssion reactors. In
this application, high-temperature stability and strength,
corrosion resistance to liquid metal coolants, radiation
resistance, and fabricability are key issues in materials
selection. The interest in Nb-base alloys for these space
applications originated in the early programs of the
1960s
[1] and has continued on to the more recent Jupiter
Icy Moons Orbiter (JIMO) and Prometheus programs
of the U.S. National Aeronautics and Space Adminis-
tration (NASA).
[2] Despite a long history, the maturity
of the database on Nb-base refractory metal alloys is
limited. This is due in part to program cancellations and
the lack of current commercial development. As
reported by Wojcik,
[3] niobium alloy additions to steel
and nickel-base superalloys account for approximately
98 pct of total niobium use, with the remainder used in
Nb-base alloys or in pure metal form.
From the start of the space reactor programs, a large
number of refractory metal alloys have been developed
to varying degrees. Of these refractory alloys, Nb-base
alloys oﬀer excellent formability with a reduced weight
penalty, due to a lower density as compared to other
refractory metals. With a premium placed on ductility
and fabrication, selections of relatively weak but form-
able candidates were initially evaluated. Of these
Nb-base alloys, Nb-1Zr was the most promising and,
as a result, oﬀers the largest database of mechanical
properties, in particular, creep properties.
[4] However,
the high-temperature strength of Nb-1Zr is among the
lowest of all the refractory alloys, thus furthering
interest in the development of stronger and more
creep-resistant alloys.

The density-compensated strength of FS-85 is one of
the highest for the refractory metal alloys and exceeds
that of the Ta-base alloys at temperatures to 1300 K. In
addition, FS-85 exhibits considerable creep resistance
over the other Nb-base alloys. As part of the recent
JIMO and Prometheus programs of NASA, the FS-85
alloy was investigated further as a Nb-base alloy that
could be used as an alternative to Nb-1Z. While
demonstrating improved high-temperature properties
over Nb-1Zr,
[15–17] however, there is still a relatively
limited mechanical property database for FS-85.
Phase stability and microstructural changes are key
issues in understanding and predicting the long-term
performance of refractory alloys at high temperatures.
Preliminary work conducted on the eﬀects of aging
FS-85 has shown decreased bend ductility near
1255 K,
[18,19] while tensile data indicate no observed
embrittlement.[20] In this current work, thermal aging
treatments have been conducted at 1098, 1248, and
1398 K for approximately 1100 hours on annealed
FS-85, to examine both the upper operating temperature
regime and the temperature range considered for space
reactor applications, in addition to potential material
embrittlement. The results of metallographic examina-
tion by optical and electron microscopy are reported
along with the changes in material hardness, electrical
resistivity, and tensile properties with aging. This
investigation represents the ﬁrst in-depth analysis of
the structural property relationships observed in this
alloy upon thermal aging and expands upon the
preliminary results reported earlier by Leonard et al.
[21]

grip regions of the SS-3 tensile specimens, well away
from the gage length, so that later tensile tests were not
inﬂuenced.

Precipitation along the grain boundaries was now visible

particles were discontinuous along the grain boundary,
with localized variations in distribution density observed
at the diﬀerent grain boundaries contained in the
samples examined. Examples of typical grain-boundary

observed in the material aged at 1398 K; this will be
discussed next.

ranges. In addition, the ZrC phase also has a cubic Fm3m
structure. However, its lattice constant of 0.4693 nm is
slightly too large for the diﬀracted intensities analyzed
from the particles. Nonetheless, it, too, cannot be ruled
out. It is possible that a mixed Zr-C-O-N cubic compound
is present in the aged specimens. A review of the powder
diﬀraction database showed cubic structures for the
ZrON (a
o = 1.8334 nm) and Zr 2ON2 (ao = 1.0140 nm)
phases, the lattice parameters of which are much too large
compared to that measured. Pending further chemical
analysis to determine the exact stoichiometry, for
simplicity, the particles will be identiﬁed hereafter as
Zr(CON).

occurs, is on the order of 10 n X-m or less, based on work

Grain-boundary precipitation in the material aged at
1248 K appears to have increased the susceptibility of
the material to intergranular failure. The extensive
electron microscopy performed on the aged materials
showed that, while particle size increased with aging,
the particle density along the grain boundaries
decreased. The embrittlement observed following aging
at 1248 K may be the result of an optimized eﬀect of
the particle size and density on the grain boundaries.
The overaging of the grain-boundary particles observed
in the material aged at 1398 K allowed some recovery
in the total tensile elongation, despite the uniform
elongation still being considerably low, compared to
similarly tested annealed material. Conversely, aging at
1098 K reduced the kinetics enough that 1100 hours of
aging was insuﬃcient for creating embrittlement,
although the uniform elongation values were also lower
than those of the as-annealed. Further aging studies at
longer and shorter times are needed to evaluate this
issue.

treatments performed at 1088 K showed no change in
the bending DBTTs for aging times up to 10,000 hours.
In addition, the only increases in bending DBTTs that
were observed at temperatures above 1255 K were
following aging times of 5000 hours or greater. Even
then, the increase in bending DBTTs was only half that
observed at 1255 K.
Similar aging embrittlement eﬀects were also reported

[18] and
Moss.[19] In these studies, embrittlement occurred on

solution. Electron microscopy was not performed to
conﬁrm this hypothesis in these prior studies.

IV. CONCLUSIONS
The elevated-temperature mechanical properties and
the changes in mechanical properties with 1100 hours of
thermal aging were examined in detail for the Nb-base
FS-85 alloy. An in-depth analysis of this refractory
metal alloy using electron microscopy techniques was
used to evaluate the microstructural changes occurring
in this material and their inﬂuence on the mechanical
behavior. It was found that aging the as-annealed FS-85
material resulted in the precipitation of both intragran-
ular and grain-boundary precipitates. These precipitates
were determined through electron diﬀraction analysis to

Increases in the aging temperature resulted in an
increase in the average precipitate size.
Aging the FS-85 at 1248 K resulted in tensile
embrittlement of the material, leading to a fracturing
of the sample along the grain boundaries after only
3 pct total elongation. This is attributed directly to the
development of the grain-boundary precipitates. Aging
above or below 1248 K resulted in only a modest
decrease in material ductility, as compared to similarly
tested as-annealed samples. The lack of embrittlement
at 1098 and 1398 K is attributed to the under- and
overaging of the precipitate particles on the grain
boundaries following 1100 hours of aging. However,
the eﬀect of the ﬁner or coarser grain-boundary
precipitates at these aging conditions on the fracture
toughness, corrosion properties, or notch sensitivity of
the materials is not known.
The tensile data for both the aged and annealed
material compared well with the historical data for the
Nb-base FS-85 alloys from earlier space reactor pro-
grams, although the overall database for this alloy is
very limited. Further analysis of the behavior of the
materials with regard to aging for longer durations is
needed; it is also necessary to learn how to minimize the
degrading behavior of the grain-boundary precipitation
through possible pre-aging thermal treatments.
