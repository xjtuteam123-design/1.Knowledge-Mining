ABSTRACT
Fe-based BCC superalloys, composed of ~two thirds Fe and Al by weight, offer a cost effective and sustainable 
solution for high temperature applications around 700 
◦
C. Introducing Cu into α / α ′ / α ″ superalloys can be used to 
tune lattice misfit and may contribute to enhance toughness and yield strength. However, understanding how Cu 
and its interaction with other elements affects the α / α ′ / α ″ microstructure is crucial, as microstructural config -
uration significantly impacts creep resistance. This study investigates elemental partitioning, segregation, and Cu 
clustering in an Fe – Al – (Ni,Co) – Ti based α / α ′ / α ″ superalloy containing 1.5 at.% Cu — originally added to improve 
toughness, inspired by prior ferritic steels. Atom Probe Tomography (APT) and Transmission Electron Micro -
scopy (TEM) reveal that Ni, Al, Co, Ti, and Cu partition into α ′ / α ″ precipitates. Within these, Cu segregates at 
former Anti Phase Domain Boundaries, where Co and Ti are locally depleted. Due to Cu ’ s limited solubility in Fe 
and competition for Ni and Al sites, ~13.7 % and 33.0 % of Cu atoms cluster in the matrix and α ′ / α ″ precipitates, 
respectively, while ~86.3% and 67% remain statistically distributed. Cu clusters show asymmetric size distri -
butions: in primary α ′ / α ″ precipitates, the most frequent and median sizes are ~14.3 nm and 23.7 nm; while in 
the matrix, where clusters co form with α ′ precipitates, the most frequent and median sizes are ~2.4 nm and 42.0 
nm. Additionally, two interfacial dislocation decorations are observed: one enriched with Co and Ti as Cottrell 
atmospheres and another with a Cu clusters “ necklace ” . These findings offer critical insights for alloy and heat 
treatment design strategies aimed at balancing ductility and creep strength through controlled clustering and 
dislocation interaction.
1. Introduction
In the process of transitioning toward a green and circular economy, 
sustainable and affordable high-temperature (HT) materials, such as Fe- 
based ferritic superalloys, are poised to take a share of current and new 
applications in the range of 700 – 800 
◦
C. These promising materials 
belong to a broader class of BCC Superalloys that are gaining increasing 
attention [ 1 ] and result from strengthening Fe(Al-X) alloys through the 
dispersion of coherent precipitates [ 2 – 7 ], similar to those found in Ni- 
superalloys. These ferritic superalloys consist of a body-centered cubic 
(bcc) derived microstructure, where ordered α ′ (B2) and/or α ″ (L2
1
) 
(Heusler structure) precipitates reinforce a disordered α (A2) matrix 
[ 4 , 6 , 2 ]. Among these materials, advanced alloys based on the Fe-Al-(Ni, 
Co)-Ti system, where α ′ partially transforms into α ″ forming 
α ′ / α ″ -precipitates, stand out due to their extraordinary creep rupture 
strength. Some of them already withstand the load conditions of 
100.000 h for rupture at 35 MPa and 760 
◦
C required for their appli -
cation in ultra-supercritical (USC) steam turbines [ 8 – 10 ] or the 20 MPa 
required for operating below 800 
◦
C in the next generation of concen -
trated solar plants (CSP) [ 11 , 12 ].
These materials exhibit low mass densities (~ 7 g/cm
3
) [ 6 , 13 ] and 
the bcc structure provides important advantages such as good thermal 
conductivity, a low thermal expansion coefficient, and a high diffusion 
permeability. This permeability enables the rapid formation of Al-based 
protective oxide scales that give the alloys exceptional corrosion and 
oxidation resistance [ 14 , 15 ]. In contrast, the inherent peculiarities of

via coherent precipitates, typic of ferritic Fe-based superalloys, is also 
beneficial compared to B2 or L2
1

improves creep resistance but also simplifies the ductility challenge, 
reducing it, to mainly enhance the ductility of the disordered A2 matrix. 
A combination of these measures can achieve reasonable ductility for 
some iron aluminides [ 27 ] and it has been demonstrated that the pres -
ence of a passive oxide scale can preserve the cohesion of cleavage 
planes and toughness [ 19 ]. However, further strategies are still needed 
to improve intrinsic RT plasticity and reduce the BDTT, which, 
depending on composition, can range from roughly 350

1000

ductility would facilitate processing and unlock further HT strength -
ening potential, aiding the faster and broader adoption of these 
materials.
Based on the above considerations, potential approaches are called 
to focus on enhancing slip in the non-close-packed structure, reducing 
the high Peierls stresses, and lowering the activation enthalpy for va -
cancy migration, particularly in the intermetallic phases [ 31 – 33 ]. In this 
context, given that most plastic deformation in Fe-based superalloys is 
expected to occur within α phase [ 5 , 34 , 35 ] a promising strategy to 
explore is the multipurpose addition of Cu. With proper optimization, Cu 
can serve a dual role — strengthening the bcc-Fe matrix at HT, while 
simultaneously enhancing its plasticity at RT. This approach is partic -
ularly attractive in alloys with an α / α ′ / α ″ microstructure, as replacing Ni 
with Cu in Ni
2
AlTi increases the temperature stability of L2
1 
[ 36 ], the 
precipitate phase with the highest intrinsic strength [ 37 ]. Moreover, in 
Ni and Al containing bcc
–
Fe, bcc-Cu particles can co-precipitate with α ′ 
when Cu solubility is exceeded, providing further strengthening [ 38 , 39 ].
To effectively tailor potential Cu additions to this system, it is 
essential to understand the mechanisms and evolution of Cu clustering 
and precipitation. Given that Cu precipitation is the primary source of 
strengthening in many Cu-bearing ferritic steels, numerous studies have 
investigated its behavior in BCC matrices [ 40 – 44 ]. The phase diagram of 
the Fe
–
Cu system presents a broad bcc-Fe + fcc-Cu miscibility gap 
indicating a wide range of spinodal decomposition, but the region of 
greater technological relevance lies at low Cu concentrations. These 
alloys exploit the limited Cu solubility that has a maximum of 1.8 at.% at 
850 
◦
C [ 45 ]. At this Cu concentration, as first confirmed through field 
ion microscopy and APT studies [ 41 , 42 ], metastable Cu-rich clusters 
form through a kinetically driven process, reflecting the mutual low 
solubility of Cu and Fe. These clusters represent an early stage in the 
decomposition process that ultimately leads to Cu precipitation 
[ 40 , 46 , 47 ]. These clusters act as embryos that retain bcc structure, 
where Cu atoms concentrate during aging until they reach a quasi-stable 
precipitate composition. Conflicting reports exist regarding the 
composition of precipitates after aging and whether their composition 
evolves over time, even in binary alloys [ 48 , 49 ]. However, recent 
simulation results and theoretical analyses indicate that the kinetic 
process of precipitation and the Cu concentration of precipitates depend 
on aging temperature and alloy composition [ 46 ]. According to high 
resolution transmission electron microscopy (TEM) [ 43 , 44 , 50 ], during 
further aging, bcc-Cu precipitates undergo structural transformations to 
reach the equilibrium phase, following the sequence bcc-Cu → 9R → 3R 
→ fcc-Cu, with the critical size for the bcc-Cu → 9R transformation being 
temperature dependent [ 51 ]. A similar process occurs in Fe-based alloys 
containing α ′ . Although, in these alloys, Cu precipitation and evolution 
are influenced by the presence of additional alloying elements and their 
concentration ratio respect to Cu [ 39 ]. In such systems, Cu accumulation 
to form Cu-rich precipitates is slower [ 52 , 53 ] and the critical size of bcc- 
Cu → 9R transformation is larger [ 54 ]. In these materials, Cu strength -
ening has been reported to be affected by the interaction with other 
elements, coprecipitation [ 38 , 39 ], interfacial interactions [ 54 , 55 ], co -
herency, and, thus, heat treatment [ 56 , 57 ].
On the other hand, while Cu addition in bcc-Fe is mostly associated 
with precipitate strengthening, it can also contribute to toughening 
when present at low Cu concentrations and with reduced cluster or 
precipitate size [ 58 – 61 ]. The effect of Cu on enhancing plasticity of bcc- 
Fe has been linked to its interaction with a /2 〈 111 〉 screw dislocations, 
which dominate plasticity at low temperatures. This interaction is pri -
marily explained based on the softening theory involving solutes 
[ 62 , 63 ] and nanoclusters [ 58 , 59 , 64 ], and which may also apply to some 
extent to precipitates [ 61 ]. The fundamental concept suggests that Cu 
facilitates the nucleation of double kinks, which are essential for screw 
dislocation motion ultimately leading to improved toughness. For solute 
atoms and clusters, an interaction energy with a dislocation arises when 
they are located within or near its core. While elastic effects such as size 
and modulus misfits contribute to this interaction, recent studies indi -
cate that the primary factor is chemical misfit — understood as the local 
distortion in the energy field caused by the solute difference in bonding 
[ 62 ]. The introduction of chemical misfit alters the generalized stacking 
fault energy ( γ -surface), i.e. the slip energy on the slip plane [ 16 , 62 ], 
which in bcc-Fe is closely linked to the core structure of screw disloca -
tions. As a result, solutes of elements with an attractive interaction en -
ergy can induce a transition from the stable, low energy, non-polarized 
(non-degenerate, easy) core structure of pure Fe to polarized (degen -
erate) core structures [ 16 , 62 , 65 ]. This transition reduces the energy 
required for double-kink nucleation and provide favorable sites for this 
process to occur [ 62 , 66 , 67 ]. Ab initio and atomistic simulations have 
shown that Cu solutes are particularly effective in this regard. They 
significantly enhance the edge component of the dislocation core, 
facilitate kink nucleation, and promote core planarity in a so-called 
“ lubricant effect ” [ 58 , 68 ]. Considering that bcc softening mechanisms 
are highly dependent on solute concentration and have generally been 
studied in binary alloys, it was unclear whether this effect would persist 
in more concentrated solid solutions. These systems are challenging to 
model, and beyond kink nucleation, the motion and interaction of kinks 
may also influence the overall behavior [ 69 , 70 ]. Nevertheless, the area 
of application appears to be broader than initially expected, as evidence 
suggests that specific bonding effects and interactions among combined 
elements often play a more significant role in toughening than the 
overall solute concentration [ 62 , 69 ].
In the case of Cu bcc-Fe alloys, promising results have been reported 
in low-carbon multicomponent Cu-steels like ASTM A710 grade B [ 61 ], 
where Cu reduce the temperature dependence of flow stress and 
enhance toughness. These findings also seem to apply to Cu/NiAl ferritic 
steels, like CF-steels [ 71 ], as well as ultra-high strength steels based 
strengthened by Cu/(Ni, Mn)Al and carbides [ 52 ], which have been 
developed for RT applications such as bridges and naval structures. The 
authors of the study on low-carbon multicomponent Cu steels [ 61 ] hy -
pothesized that Cu-rich semi-coherent clusters/precipitates may serve as 
misfit centers within the strain field, facilitating the formation of double

This context raises two questions. First, whether the inclusion of Cu 
is fully compatible with the α / α ′ / α ″ microstructure of Fe-based super -
alloys [ 38 , 39 ]. Second, whether the effect of Cu on plasticity can be 
leveraged to lower the BDTT and enhance the toughness of these ma -
terials, and if so, under what specific conditions. In this work, we focus 
on the first question by evaluating the compatibility of Cu incorporation 
with the α / α ′ / α ″ microstructure, specifically examining its effects on 
FSA2, a Fe-based ferritic superalloy developed in our earlier work 
through the addition of Ti to alloys based on the Fe-Ni-Al-Co system 
[ 6 , 10 ]. The second question can only be thoroughly addressed in a 
separate study. However, in a previous investigation [ 10 ], we found that 
the addition of Cu to an otherwise unmodified ferritic superalloy of 
mentioned system did not significantly alter the yield strength and 
plastic deformability. Nonetheless, it is important to note, that these 
tests were conducted in compression, the grain size of the arc-melted 
specimens was significantly coarse, and the heat treatment was not 
optimized to maximize the potential effects of Cu. Building on insights 
from both past and present research, the discussion section of the pre -
sent study evaluates results with the aim of developing a potential 
strategy for achieving optimal toughening conditions, based on obser -
vations in FSA2.
In the previous study [ 10 ], we investigated the relationship between 
microstructure yield strength and creep resistance in Fe-based ferritic 
superalloys. Our findings established that, in FSA2, the incorporated Cu 
tends to form what appear to be Cu-rich nanoparticles, likely corre -
sponding to the early stages of Cu precipitation in the evolution process 
previously described. Therefore, in the present study, Atom probe to -
mography (APT) is employed to characterize the compositions of α , α ′ , 
and α ″ , determine the partitioning behavior of elements, assess the na -
ture, size distribution and allocation of Cu nanoparticles, and investigate 
their interactions with the elements of the α / α ′ / α ″ microstructure. 
Additionally, Scanning and Transmission electron microscopy (SEM and 
TEM) are utilized to complement and contextualize the APT analyses.
2. Experimental methods
For the procedures, an ingot of an alloy designated as FSA2 (Ferritic 
Super Alloy 2) was manufactured with an Edmund Bühler Gmbh arc- 
melter under a protective Ar atmosphere. Elemental pellets of 99.95 % 
purity and a NiMo master alloy were used as raw materials. To ensure 
homogeneity, the ingot was melted, flipped, and re-melted three times 
during the process, followed by cooling in a copper mold. To minimize 
elemental segregation, a homogenization and solution treatment (HST) 
was performed at 1200 
◦

◦

dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using an FEI Helios NanoLab 600i 
microscope equipped with an Xmax probe from Oxford Instruments. The 
average measured and nominal compositions are listed in Table 1 .
After homogenization, specimens for microstructure characteriza -
tion were aged in single steps at 900 
◦

◦

◦

However, as α ″ also forms during furnace cooling, this condition exhibits

suitable for APT analysis.
APT experiments were performed on specimens prepared by FIB in- 
situ lift-out using a Zeiss Crossbeam 540 FIB/SEM, following standard 
procedures [ 74 , 75 ] (see Supplemental Materials A2). The APT mea -
surements were conducted using a CAMECA LEAP 4000 × HR (CAMECA 
INC, Madison, Wisconsin). Laser pulsing was selected to enable the 
measurement and analysis of larger datasets. The experiments were 
conducted with a UV-laser of 355 nm wavelength at a base temperature 
of 49 K. A pulse repetition rate of 200 kHz, and a pulse energy of 75 pJ 
was used to trigger field evaporation. 3D visualization of element atomic 
distribution (see Supplemental Materials A3) and chemical analysis was 
done using commercial software IVAS 3.8.6 from CAMECA [ 76 ]. The 
main features were reconstructed by using the algorithm described in 
[ 77 , 78 ]. The reconstructions were used to evaluate phase compositions 
using small volumes within the regions of interest (ROIs). The compo -
sitions of α matrix and α ″ domains were determined with the biggest 
cuboid possible without containing any other feature, such as repreci -
pitated particles or APDBs. In contrast, the compositions of the repre -
cipitated phases were determined using spheres inside the 
corresponding isosurface, keeping a minimum distance to reduce the 
influence of phase boundaries. Multiple particles were considered for 
each tip (see Supplemental Materials A4) and the average compositions 
were used for further calculations. APDBs were identified using a Ti 8.5 
at.% isosurface, and were extracted and modeled using a custom coded 
MATLAB Script [ 79 , 80 ] and Blender [ 81 ].
Inside the APT reconstruction, the distribution of Cu atoms was 
analyzed using a method proposed by Felfer et al. [ 82 ], which tests and 
extracts elemental clustering and ordering. The method evaluates de -
viations from random atomic distributions to determine a concentration 
threshold that delineates between clustered and non-clustered atoms. 
Atoms above this threshold must occupy Voronoi cells significantly 
smaller than those in a random distribution and can be extracted for 
analysis using their individual Voronoi cells. Further details can be 
found in Supplemental materials A7.

4. Discussion
Here, the key findings of this research are discussed as follows. First, 
we evaluate the elemental concentrations within the FSA2 matrix and 
their role looking toward enhancing the Cu toughening effect. Second, 
we discuss the formation and size distribution of Cu-clusters and their 
expected influence on the surrounding microstructural constituents. 
Finally, we address the significance of the observed segregation of Cu, as 
well as Co and Ti. This is particularly noteworthy considering that a 
major breakthrough in Ni-based superalloys was the inclusion of Re in 
second generation single crystals, which nearly doubled the creep life 
due to Re segregation at dislocations.
To assess the potential of Cu-clusters for enhancing the RT toughness 
of FSA2, it is crucial to first consider the composition of the α matrix. 
According to Table 4 , the α
m 
matrix ’ s is predominantly composed of Fe 
(~72 at.%), with two major contributions from Al (~7.5 at.%) and Cr (~ 
5.5 at.%). This composition appears to exceed the limits of a diluted 
solid solution where double kink nucleation is expected to dominate 
plasticity. However, in A2 Fe solid solutions, solutes such as Cr, Mo, and 
Co are identified as having no attractive interactions with screw dislo -
cations, thus not increasing the energy barrier for dislocation glide [ 62 ]. 
In FSA2, Al and Cr concentrations are carefully balanced, and any 
attempt to reduce these levels must be approached cautiously, as it could 
destabilize the alloy toward the γ phase [ 6 ] or comprise environmental 
resistance. Al is crucial for forming protective oxide scales against 
corrosion, oxidation and environmental embrittlement [ 15 , 108 ], how -
ever, high Al levels can lead to brittleness by lowering vacancy forma -
tion enthalpy [ 23 , 24 , 32 ]. Cr mitigates the required Al for protective 
scales [ 15 ] and improves α ″ ductility by reducing superlattice disloca -
tion dissociation energy [ 28 ]. Before considering alterations to Cr and 
Al, adjusting Cu content alone may be sufficient to improve toughness, 
as both, Ni and Al solutes exhibit a moderate beneficial chemical misfit 
in A2 Fe solid solutions, which may also facilitate double-kink nucle -
ation [ 69 , 109 ].
As shown in previous section, due to the low Cu solubility at low 
temperatures [ 92 , 93 ], even microalloying with 1.5 at.% Cu, results in Cu 
being not only incorporated as a substitutional solute, but also stabi -

dragging their movement [ 69 , 112 ]. This phenomenon, also likely active 
for Co and Ti in FSA2, may enhance HT creep resistance by hindering or 
trapping of dislocation movement within regions of chemical fluctua -
tion. A similar phenomenon has been observed in Ni- and Co-based 
superalloys, where dislocation segregation of elements such as Re is 
well documented. Recent studies have clarified how segregation to 
dislocations and planar faults can significantly influence beneficially 
creep rupture strength via a mechanism known as segregation-assisted 
HT plasticity [ 113 – 117 ]. In ferritic Fe-based superalloys, the underly -
ing mechanisms are likely to differ, partly due to slip incompatibility 
that limits precipitate shearing — although this effect may still occur in 
small precipitates. Furthermore, in contrast to fcc superalloys, where 
continuous dislocation segregation is often required for precipitate 
shearing, such behavior may not occur in BCC-based systems like ours. 
Nevertheless, understanding segregation and its influence on local atom 
arrangement, kinetic processes, and plasticity remains critically 
important.
The importance of dislocation segregation for ferritic Fe-based su -
peralloys is multifaceted. At high temperatures, considering that in 
alloys strengthened by the Orowan mechanism — and particularly in BCC 
superalloys with phase distinct slip systems — interfacial dislocations 
networks play a crucial role in modulating dislocation interactions and 
controlling creep rates [ 10 , 106 , 107 ]. Whereas at low temperatures, as 
tuning local atomic arrangements through controlled segregation can 
reduce local chemical order (LCO) stability, thereby promoting the 
formation of anti-phase domains and twins to improve ductility in BCC 
superalloys [ 118 ]. In this context, the study of elemental partitioning 
behavior is highly relevant as it determines the lattice misfit and 
therefore precipitate morphology, interfacial dislocation density and the 
stress field trapping or otherwise influencing matrix dislocations [ 119 ]. 
Continued research is essential to improve our understanding of this 
behavior, explore ways to control it, and evaluate the influence of 
elemental concentrations. For example, if the independent segregation 
of Cu, and of Co and Ti at dislocations as analyzed in the previous sec -
tion, result associated with different types of dislocations, these be -
haviors could potentially be exploited to strengthen Fe-based 
superalloys at HT by reinforcing two distinct slip systems.
In summary, the analysis of composition, phase fractions, and 
reconstruction using APT data evidenced element partitioning behavior, 
Cu-clustering and elemental segregation in the studied alloy, offering 
insights into the roles of Cu and other elements within α / α ′ / α ″ micro -
structure. Additionally, the dissolution of Cu-clusters at high- 
temperatures and segregation of Cu, Co, Ti solutes to dislocations offer 
a promising approach for designing bimodal precipitate strategies, 
potentially balancing adequate ductility with yield strength and 
improved creep resistance of Fe-based superalloys.
5. Conclusions and outlook
This study investigated the elemental partitioning behavior, segre -
gation, and Cu-clustering in an α / α ′ / α ″ Fe-based superalloy based on the 
Fe – Al – Ni – Co – Ti system with an addition of 1.5 at.% Cu. The alloy was 
characterized after heat-treatment using APT and TEM to understand 
the role of Cu toward developing a strategy that optimizes strengthening 
and room temperature toughening. The key findings from this study are 
summarized as follows: 
 The preferential partitioning of Ni, Co, Cu, and Ti into α ′ / α ″ pre -
cipitates and their considerable Fe content, as detected by APT, can 
be explained by the strong tendency of these elements to form (XAl) 
B2 and (XYAl) L2
1 
compounds. Meanwhile, Fe, Cr, and Mo prefer -
entially partition to the α -matrix.
 The local composition at prior APDBs, where α ′ is stable, revealed 
that α ′ is enriched in Cu and depleted in Co and Ti compared to α ″ . 
However, given the relatively low Ti content detected in α ″ and the 
prolonged ordering in parent precipitates, the extended presence of 
Ti site defects or its substitution by other elements is expected. Since 
Cr and Mo primarily partition to the matrix, and Ni has the highest 
affinity for its own site, potential candidates are Al, Co, and Cu.
 Alloying with 1.5 at.% Cu in the studied heat-treatment led to the 
stabilization of a size-composition distribution of Cu-clusters upon 
cooling, representing an early stage of Cu-rich precipitation, as 
identified through Voronoi Volume randomness tests of APT data. 
The tendency of Cu to cluster in both the matrix and precipitates is 
attributed to its low mutual solubility, particularly with elements 
concentrated in the matrix, and its relatively low affinity for α ′ and α ″ 
precipitates. The last one explains why, under site competition 
within parent precipitates, Cu is rejected to the precipitate/matrix 
interface and to α ′ at prior APDBs.
 Due to the partitioning characteristics of Cu, the distribution of Cu- 
clusters differs between the matrix and α ′ / α ″ precipitates. In the 
matrix, Cu-clusters were less abundant, smaller in their most 
frequent size and distributed in three distinct locations; freely 
dispersed, or either at the interface or within α ′ reprecipitated par -
ticles. Based on the anticipated phase transformation order of studied

 Two types of decorations were detected at interfacial dislocations: a

achieve a more uniform distribution and smaller cluster sizes may 
enhance the potential effects of Cu in improving RT toughness.
