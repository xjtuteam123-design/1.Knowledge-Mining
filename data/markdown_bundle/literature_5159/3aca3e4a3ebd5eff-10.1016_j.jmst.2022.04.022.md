a b s t r a c t 
In traditional physical metallurgy, once recrystallization occurs, it will proceed to 100% along with time 
even at relatively low temperatures, resulting in the limited thermal stability of partially recrystallized al- 
loys. Here, we proposed the strategy of achieving the endless recrystallization state at high temperature 
( ∼0.6 T m ) in high entropy alloys for the ﬁrst time. The partially recrystallized microstructures remained 
stable after annealing at 700 °C for 1440 h toward endless recrystallization with kinetics analysis. Ben- 
eﬁting from the ultra-thermostable heterostructures, the alloy exhibited excellent mechanical properties 
of ∼1.6 GPa tensile strength at room temperature and ∼1.1 GPa tensile strength at 600 °C even after 
exposure at 700 °C for 720 h. The kinetics of recovery, recrystallization, grain growth, and precipitate 
coarsening were quantitatively analyzed to uncover the mechanisms of endless recrystallization. The re- 
sults revealed that the stable state of 50% recrystallization at 700 °C can be attributed to the precipitates 
inhibited recrystallization and the continued recovery decreased stored energy in the non-recrystallized 
regions. Furthermore, the grain size was stable in the recrystallized regions due to the strong pinning ef- 
fect of the intergranular precipitates with slow coarsening rates. These ﬁndings created a brand-new state 
of endless recrystallization with the combination of recovery and recrystallization, which can signiﬁcantly 
broaden the service temperature range of heterogeneous materials.

1. Introduction 
As a critical approach to regulating microstructures and me- 
chanical properties of metallic materials, recrystallization is a fun- 
damental scientiﬁc issue in physical metallurgy [ 1 , 2 ]. By control- 
ling the recrystallization kinetics, single crystal [3] , ultraﬁne grains 
[4] , and textured grains [5] can be fabricated for different appli- 
cations. Recently, partially recrystallized heterostructures with bi- 
modal grain size distribution were proposed for enhanced mechan- 
ical properties [6] . As typical heterogeneous materials, during the 
plastic deformation of partially recrystallized alloys, long-range in- 
ternal stress will generate between the soft and hard zones, pro- 
ducing additional hetero-deformation induced hardening and thus 
enhancing the strength and ductility [ 7 , 8 ]. Besides, the partially re- 
crystallized heterostructures can be easily realized in different al- 
loy systems, showing excellent universality [ 9 , 10 ]. 
However, once recrystallization occurs, it will proceed to 100% 
along with time even at limited temperatures, resulting in the poor 
thermal stability and signiﬁcantly degraded performance of par- 
tially recrystallized alloys, which has been a key bottleneck re-

mostable microstructures is to control the recrystallization kinet- 
ics, which is affected by the alloy compositions, original grain 
size, textures, deformation conditions, and annealing conditions. 
Recent studies have shown that the concurrent precipitation pro- 
cess can signiﬁcantly inhibit recrystallization at increased temper- 
atures [ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 ]. However, since the interaction of precipitation 
and recrystallization is very complicated in these dual-phase al- 
loys, the long-term thermal stability of partially recrystallized al- 
loys at high temperatures has never been evaluated, and the ki- 
netics of recrystallization remains unclear. The long-term stability 
of the mechanical properties in these partially recrystallized alloys 
with precipitates is also intriguing and important for further appli- 
cations. 
Grain growth in the recrystallized regions is another problem 
affecting the thermal stability of partially recrystallized alloys [17] . 
There are two ways to improve the thermal stability, i.e., reducing 
the driving force and increasing the resistance of grain boundary 
migration [ 18 , 19 ]. Speciﬁcally, for pure metals, manipulating low- 
energy boundaries is the only way to improve thermal stability. 
Zhou et al. [20] proposed the idea of relaxing grain boundaries into 
low-energy structures through their interactions with partial dislo- 
cations and prepared ultrastable nanograins in Cu and Ni. In binary 
or multi-element systems, alloying can effectively im prove thermal

For the recently developed high-entropy alloys (HEAs) 
[ 23 , 24 , 25 ], partially recrystallized heterostructures have been 
introduced to enhance the strength of face-centered cubic (FCC) 
HEAs [ 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 ]. Jo et al. [27] signiﬁcantly improved the 
cryogenic-temperature yield strength of VCrMnFeCoNi HEA to ∼1 
GPa by retaining the deformed grains partially. Wu et al. [28] pre- 
pared partially recrystallized Al 0.1 CoCrFeNi HEA and achieved an 
excellent strength-ductility combination by activating dislocations 
and twins sequentially in the non-recrystallized and recrystal- 
lized grains. Furthermore, the mechanical properties of partially 
recrystallized alloys can be further improved by combining pre- 
cipitation strengthening [ 30 , 31 , 32 ]. For example, Sathiyamoorthi 
et al. [31] engineered the Al 0.3 CoCrNi HEA to multi-phase partially 
recrystallized microstructures and achieved a high tensile strength 
of ∼1.2 GPa. He et al. [32] developed high-strength precipitated 
heterogeneous HEAs with superior tensile elongation of ∼20% by 
utilizing the unique strain partitioning behavior. These results in- 
dicate that HEAs are promising alloys for heterogeneous materials 
with partially recrystallized microstructures. Moreover, consider- 
ing the abnormal grain growth [33] and precipitate coarsening 
[34] behaviors, thermally stable heterogeneous HEAs would be 
available by uncovering the interactions between precipitation, 
recrystallization, and grain growth. 
In this study, we achieved an endless recrystallization state by 
utilizing the concurrent precipitation and recrystallization behavior 
to inhibit complete recrystallization and grain growth at the same 
time. In an FCC-based HEA, the partially recrystallized microstruc- 
tures were stable when annealing at 700 °C even for 1440 h. High 
tensile strength of ∼1.6 GPa at room temperature and ∼1.1 GPa at 
600 °C were achieved even after exposure at 700 °C for 720 h. The 
stabilization mechanisms at 700 °C were discussed in terms of re- 
covery, recrystallization, grain growth, and precipitate coarsening. 
2. Experiments 
2.1. Alloy preparation 
Ingots with the nominal composition of 27Ni-34Co-12Cr-12Fe- 
12Al-3W (at.%) were prepared by arc melting in a Ti-gettered high- 
purity argon atmosphere. Raw elements of Ni, Co, Cr, Fe, Al, and W 
with 99.95% purity were used. Each ingot was re-melted at least 
four times for chemical homogeneity and then drop-casted into a 
water-cooled copper mold with the dimensions of 60 mm × 12 
mm × 5 mm. Firstly, the as-cast ingots were homogenized at 1200 
°C for 2 h, followed by water quenching. Then, the as-homogenized 
samples were cold-rolled to 1.5 mm thickness along the longitu- 
dinal direction with a total thickness reduction of ∼70%. Subse- 
quently, the as-rolled samples were annealed at 700 °C for the du- 
ration of 6, 12, 24, 48, 120, 288, 480, 720, and 1440 h to check 
the thermal stability. To eliminate the effect of slow cooling on 
the morphologies of precipitates and grain boundaries [35] , the 
rapid cooling method of water quenching was used for all anneal- 
ing treatments. 
2.2. Microstructure characterization 
Phase identiﬁcation was conducted by X-ray diffractometer 
(XRD, Shimadzu, MAXima XRD-70 0 0) using Cu K α radiation with 
a scanning rate of 5 ° min −1 . Microstructures were character- 
ized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, TESCAN MIRA3) 
equipped with an electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) and 
a transmission electron microscope (TEM, Talos F200X) equipped 
with energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS). For SEM and EBSD ob- 
servations, the samples taken from the longitudinal section were 
mechanically ground and ﬁnely polished by colloidal silica suspen- 
sions. The step size of EBSD data collection was 0.05 μm and the 
index rate over 85%-90% was guaranteed to ensure the high resolu- 
tion of the misorientation value. For TEM observation, the sample 
with a thickness of 0.5 mm was cut by electro-discharge machin- 
ing and mechanically ground to ∼60 μm using SiC paper, followed 
by punching into 3 mm diameter discs and further ion milling. 
2.3. Mechanical tests 
Hardness tests were performed on a Vickers hardness tester 
(TIMES, TH701) under a load of 1 kg and a duration of 15 s. At 
least ﬁve points were tested for each sample to ensure data re- 
liability. Tensile tests were performed on a universal testing ma- 
chine (TSMT, EM6.504) with an initial strain rate of 10 −3 s −1 . For 
room-temperature tests, dog-bone-shaped samples with the gauge 
dimensions of 12.5 mm × 3.2 mm × 1.5 mm were cut along the 
rolling direction, and a mechanical extensometer was used to en- 
sure strain accuracy. For high-temperature tests, the gauge dimen- 
sions of 12 mm × 2 mm × 1.5 mm were used. The samples were 
heated to the preset temperature and kept for 10 min before test- 
ing. 
3. Results 
3.1. Initial microstructure 
The alloy composition was extracted from a typical 
Ni 30 Co 30 Cr 10 Fe 10 Al 18 W 2 eutectic HEA with excellent proper- 
ties by measuring the chemical composition of the constituent 
phase FCC [ 36 , 37 ]. With the aid of the calculated phase diagram 
(CALPHAD), the equilibrium phase constitution was conﬁrmed by 
the Thermo-Calc software using the TTNi8 database, as shown in 
Fig. 1 (a). The alloy was predicted to be composed of FCC (97.5%) 
and ordered body-centered cubic (B2) (2.5%) phases at 1200 °C. 
As temperature decreased, the solid solubility of the B2 phase de- 
creased gradually, and the μ phase began to precipitate at ∼1100 
°C. At 700 °C, the mole fraction of B2 and μ phases was predicted 
to be 27.4% and 6.1%, respectively. The plain phase transformation 
makes the concurrent precipitation and recrystallization much eas- 
ier. Fig. 1 (b) shows the microscopy image of the as-homogenized 
alloy with an undissolved black phase, identiﬁed as B2 based on 
the corresponding XRD pattern in the inset. The microscopy image 
of the as-rolled alloy is shown in Fig. 1 (c), the undissolved B2 
phase tended to align parallel to the rolling direction (RD), with a 
volume fraction of ∼7.0%, higher than the predicted value of 2.5%.

cal properties. Fig. 6 (a) shows the variation of hardness with an- 
nealing time. The hardness ﬁrst increased to ∼560 HV at 24 h, 
followed by a slow decrement to ∼420 HV at 1440 h. The incre-

4. Analyses 
In this section, we will quantitatively analyze the kinetics of mi- 
crostructure evolution, i.e., recrystallization, grain growth, and pre- 
cipitate coarsening behavior at 700 °C, to show the thermal stabil- 
ity of the heterogeneous microstructures. 
4.1. Recrystallization kinetics 
Fig. 8 presents the variation of recrystallized fraction with an- 
nealing time. Typically, recrystallization follows the S-curve, i.e., 
occurs rapidly after the incubation period, and becomes slow grad- 
ually after the midpoint until saturation at 100%. In this study, 
however, it is surprising that the saturated recrystallized volume 
fraction was not 100% but ∼50% in Fig. 3 (c), which cannot be de- 
scribed by the classical recrystallization theory. Up to now, there 
is no model to describe the stable partially recrystallized behavior 
with a fraction termination. Theoretically, recrystallization kinet- 
ics can be described using the Johnson-Mehl-Avrami-Kolmogorov 
(JMAK) equation [ 41 , 42 , 43 ]: 
X =1 −exp ( −At n ) (1)

recrystallized volume fraction maintained stable at ∼50% at 700 
°C, here we proposed a modiﬁed JMAK equation by multiplying a 
coeﬃcient of B to represent the terminal fraction:

4.2. Grain growth kinetics 
Fig. 9 shows the grain growth behavior after recrystallization. 
As shown in Fig. 9 (a), the grain size only increased gradually from 
∼0.67 μm at 48 h to ∼0.93 μm at 288 h and then remained stable 
without obvious growth until 1440 h. Fig. 9 (b) shows the double 
logarithmic plots of the average grain size with annealing time. The 
alloy annealed at 700 °C showed an abnormal high grain growth 
exponent of ∼6.25, which can reﬂect the high resistance against 
grain growth [45] . For comparison, the grain growth exponent of 
nanocrystalline and coarse-grained Fe approached 2 when anneal- 
ing at ∼0.6 T m [46] .

5. Discussion 
5.1. Stabilization mechanisms of the 50%-recrystallized microstructure 
The endless recrystallization state at high temperature is abnor- 
mal and needs to be discussed. The competitions among recovery, 
recrystallization, and phase transformation are critical to the evolu- 
tion of heterogeneous microstructures [51] , which determined the 
formation of non-recrystallized and recrystallized regions together 
with their stability. 
At the initial stage of annealing, recrystallization was re- 
tarded, and a large amount of B2 phase precipitated in the non- 
recrystallized regions. As indicated by Fig. 4 (a), the strip-shaped 
B2 phase nucleated along the {111} planes of the FCC matrix in 
the non-recrystallized regions. The {111} deformation bands with a 
wall of high-density dislocations can promote precipitation in the 
following three ways. Firstly, the higher free energy of defects can 
reduce the activation energy barrier of phase transformation, pro- 
78

## Page 9

Q. Wu, Z. Wang, F. He et al. Journal of Materials Science & Technology 128 (2022) 71–81 
viding nucleation sites [52] . Secondly, the remaining dislocations 
can attach to the newly formed semi-coherent phase boundaries 
to compensate for the mismatch, thereby accelerating interface mi- 
gration by reducing the strain energy [53] . Thirdly, the disloca- 
tions can provide pipe-diffusion channels, accelerating the diffu- 
sion for precipitate growth [50] . As a result, the B2 phase precipi- 
tated rapidly in the non-recrystallized regions at the initial stage of 
annealing. The precipitation of B2 will decrease the stored energy 
of the non-recrystallized regions, and thus reduce the driving force 
and increase the critical nucleus size for recrystallization, making 
it more diﬃcult to happen. 
As annealing time prolonged, the observed recrystallized behav- 
ior can be attributed to the heterogeneous nucleation around the 
undissolved B2 phase. As shown in Fig. 3 , all the recrystallized re- 
gions were around the undissolved B2 phase and there were no 
recrystallized regions within the non-recrystallized regions, indi- 
cating that it is extremely diﬃcult for the FCC matrix to recrystal- 
lize due to the precipitation of strip-shaped B2. Generally, there are 
three effects of the second phase on recrystallization. (1) Increase 
the stored energy during deformation; (2) Particle-stimulated nu- 
cleation of recrystallization [ 54 , 55 ]; (3) Pin the grain boundaries 
and delay recrystallization [56] . In this study, the undissolved B2 
phase with high hardness accumulated more dislocation density 
and stored energy around during deformation. Moreover, the ex- 
istence of the undissolved B2 phase aggravated heterogeneous de- 
formation and lattice rotation of the FCC matrix, resulting in large 
misorientations around and providing potential nucleation sites for 
recrystallization. Here the large undissolved B2 phase did not delay 
the recrystallization, and with the increase in annealing time, the 
recrystallized regions expanded very slowly from the enveloped 
undissolved B2 phase. 
On the contrary, the plenty of ﬁne B2 precipitates in the re- 
crystallized regions reduced the grain boundary mobility and re- 
tarded recrystallization, which is one of the reasons for the sta- 
bilization of 50%-recrystallized microstructure. Along with the re- 
crystallization, the stripe-shaped B2 precipitates became equiaxed 
and distributed randomly among the grains. In this case, the ratio 
of volume fraction ( f ) to radius ( r ) of the B2 phase can be used to 
evaluate the hindering effect of the precipitated phase on recrys- 
tallization [57] . The dispersed B2 phase exhibited a high volume 
fraction of ∼22.3% and a small size of less than 2 μm even after 
annealing for 1440 h. Consequently, the large value of f / r reveals an 
effective hindering effect on recrystallization. It is worth mention- 
ing that the striped shape of B2 can further increase the interface 
migration resistance. Nes et al. [57] investigated the effect of pre- 
cipitate shape on boundary migration and found that stripe-shaped 
precipitates can induce a higher pinning effect than spherical pre- 
cipitates. Since the elongated anisotropic shape of the B2 precip- 
itates, it is beneﬁcial to maintaining the strong pinning effect for 
recrystallization. 
Once the precipitates inhibited recrystallization, there will be 
more time for recovery to further reduce the driving force of re- 
crystallization. For the non-recrystallized regions, the long-time re- 
covery can consume strain energy and reduce the driving force for 
recrystallization [ 58 , 59 ]. In some alloys, recovery can even release 
up to 70% of the total stored energy [60] . Since the KAM value can 
be used to estimate the density of geometrically necessary disloca- 
tion (GND) [ 61 , 62 ], we analyzed its variation to reﬂect the recov- 
ery process at 700 °C. In Fig. 12 (a), the KAM value of FCC in the 
48 h-annealed alloy showed a typical bimodal distribution, where 
the high and low KAM values correspond to the non-recrystallized 
and recrystallized regions, respectively. With increasing annealing 
time, the peak of the high KAM value decreased gradually. The in- 
set shows the KAM distribution in the non-recrystallized regions, 
which also shifted to the lower angle gradually. Fig. 12 (b) quan- 
titatively shows the variation of the average KAM value of FCC 
with annealing time. In the recrystallization process, the average 
KAM value decreased for the whole region. While the decrement 
of average KAM value in the non-recrystallized regions can be at- 
tributed to the sustainable recovery process. The non-recrystallized 
regions in the 1440 h-annealed alloy showed an average KAM 
value of ∼0.9 °, much lower than that of the 48 h-annealed alloy 
( ∼1.4 °), indicating a decreased dislocation density after long-term 
annealing. Therefore, a decreased driving force for recrystallization 
caused by the recovery can be conﬁrmed, which also contributed 
to the stabilization of the 50%-recrystallized microstructures. 
The formation and stabilization mechanisms of the endless re- 
crystallization at 700 °C can be summarized in the schematic di- 
agram in Fig. 13 . At the early stage of annealing, strip-shaped B2 
phase precipitated along the {111} deformation bands in the non- 
recrystallized regions, while recrystallization nucleated preferen- 
tially around the undissolved B2 phase. With the increase in an- 
nealing time, the precipitation and continuous recovery reduced 
the driving force for recrystallization, and the strip-shaped precipi- 
tates inhibited recrystallization. As a result, recrystallization cannot 
continue when it reached a certain level (50%), eventually form- 
ing ultra-thermostable partially recrystallized microstructures, i.e., 
endless recrystallization. 
5.2. Stabilization mechanisms of the submicron recrystallized grains 
In addition to the stability of the partially recrystallized struc- 
ture, the grain size stability after recrystallization also promotes 
heterogeneity and ensures high tensile strength. For nanocrys- 
talline alloys, the poor thermal stability has been the bottleneck 
restricting their applications. In this study, after recrystallization 
around the undissolved B2 phase, the nanocrystalline structures 
exhibited ultra-thermostable behavior at 700 °C, which ensured 
the distinct grain size distributions between the recrystallized and 
non-recrystallized regions.

boundary migration and inhibit grain growth [22] . By assuming the 
pinning pressure equal to the driving force for grain growth, the

Based on the above discussions, there are several key points 
to obtaining ultrastable partially recrystallized microstructures. 
Firstly, there should be an undissolved hard phase with a large size 
in the undeformed state to promote heterogeneous deformation 
and subsequent heterogeneous recrystallization. Secondly, concur- 
rent precipitation of massive incoherent phases during annealing is 
needed to suppress recrystallization by consuming the stored en- 
ergy. Thirdly, the coarsening rate of the precipitates should be slow 
enough to provide a sustainable pinning effect on grain growth in 
the recrystallized regions even after long-time annealing. 
6. Conclusion 
To summarize, we achieved a brand-new state of endless re- 
crystallization with ultrastable co-existing recovered and recrystal- 
lized microstructures in HEA at high temperature (0.6 T m ). The het- 
erostructured endless recrystallization ensured superior and sta- 
ble mechanical properties at both ambient and high temperatures. 
The microstructures were characterized using SEM, EBSD, TEM, and 
the stabilization mechanisms were discussed based on the kinetics 
of recovery, recrystallization, grain growth, precipitate coarsening. 
The main ﬁndings are as follows: 
(1) Endless recrystallization and concurrent precipitation were 
achieved by annealing the as-rolled HEA at 700 °C. The recrys- 
tallized volume fraction remained stable at ∼50% even after an- 
nealing for 1440 h, accompanied by the precipitation of high 
content of B2 and μ phases in both the recrystallized and non- 
recrystallized regions. 
(2) The competitions among recovery, recrystallization, and precip- 
itation are responsible for endless recrystallization. In the non- 
recrystallized regions, the precipitation of ﬁne B2 and μ phases 
consumed the stored energy and retarded recrystallization, pro- 
viding suﬃcient time for recovery, which in turn further de- 
creased the driving force for recrystallization. 
(3) In the recrystallized regions, the grain size remained stable at 
the submicron scale even after annealing for 1440 h with an 
abnormally high grain growth exponent of ∼6.25. This can be 
attributed to the strong Zener pinning effect caused by the in- 
tergranular B2 and μ phases with slow coarsening rates. 
(4) The ultra-thermostable heterostructures from endless recrystal- 
lization ensured excellent mechanical properties. After exposure
