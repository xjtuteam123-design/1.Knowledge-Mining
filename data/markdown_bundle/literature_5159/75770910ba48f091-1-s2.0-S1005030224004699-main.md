a b s t r a c t 
Metallic scaffolds with lightweight, low elastic modulus, and high energy-absorbing capacity are widely 
utilized in industrial applications but usually require post-heat treatment to enhance their comprehen- 
sive mechanical properties. However, it is unclear how to utilize the impact of β-Nb on the surrounding 
matrix for NiTiNb ternary alloys to achieve strength-ductility-superelasticity enhancement. Here, we pre- 
pared rhomboidal dodecahedral NiTiNb porous scaffolds with a porosity of 85.9% by additive manufac- 
turing. Subsequently, annealing treatment was employed to drastically reduce the phase transformation 
temperatures and expand the thermal hysteresis. Interestingly, the 850 °C annealed scaffold exhibited 
exceeding double compressive strength of the as-built sample, with a remarkable improvement in ductil- 
ity and superelasticity. From the microstructure perspective, high-temperature annealing caused a further 
eutectic reaction of the unmelted Nb particles with the NiTi matrix and the transformation of mesh- 
like β-Nb into dispersedly distributed spherical β-Nb particles. The microstructure evolution after defor- 
mation indicated that stress-induced martensitic transformation occurred in the matrix away from the 
NiTi-Nb eutectic region whereas almost no martensite formed nearby β-Nb particles. Atom probe tomog- 
raphy characterization revealed an element diffusion zone in several nanometers surrounding the β-Nb 
particle, where the substitution of Nb with Ti led to a higher Ni: Ti atomic ratio, lowering transforma- 
tion temperatures. Molecular dynamics simulations illustrated that β-Nb particles can not only entangle 
dislocations internally, acting as reinforcements but also hinder the twin growth, contributing to strain 
hardening. This work elucidates the inﬂuence of β-Nb particles on the deformation mechanism of the 
NiTi-Nb eutectic region through in-depth atomic-scale investigation, which can provide inspiration for 
the improvement of comprehensive mechanical properties of NiTiNb alloys.

tages of lightweight, low elastic modulus, and large energy absorp- 
tion capacity, which have enormous potential in aerospace, auto- 
motive, and biomedical applications [ 1 , 2 ]. Compared with tradi- 
tional powder metallurgy, brazing, and other technologies, additive

perelasticity [15–17] . In the early development of NiTi scaffolds, 
they were mainly prepared by powder metallurgy and brazing 
methods, including self-propagating high-temperature synthesis, 
injection molding, hot isotactic pressing, eutectic brazing, and so 
on [18–21] . All these techniques can hardly realize precise mold- 
ing of complex topography and have been gradually superseded 
by AM techniques. Preparation of NiTi SMAs with AM technol- 
ogy, also known as 4D printing, has been maturing in recent years 
based on processing parameter optimization and state-of-the-art 
macrostructural design [22–24] . For NiTi-based SMAs, the addition 
of new elements is considered to be the main approach to improve 
their properties. The addition of the Nb element will react eutecti- 
cally with the NiTi matrix, generating a NiTi-Nb eutectic structure 
containing the β-Nb phase [ 25 , 26 ]. In general, the addition of Nb 
element is believed to be an effective means to expand the ther- 
mal hysteresis [27] . Previously, there have been numerous studies 
on NiTiNb alloys, basically prepared by traditional melting method, 
and mainly focusing on their shape memory effect and NiTi-Nb eu- 
tectic structure [ 28 , 29 ]. In contrast, less attention has been given 
to the mechanical properties and deformation mechanism of AMed 
NiTiNb alloys. Therefore, it is of vital signiﬁcance to investigate the 
deformation mechanism of AMed NiTiNb alloys from the stand- 
point of NiTi-Nb eutectic structure for the improvement of their 
comprehensive mechanical properties. 
To achieve the synergistic strength-ductility-superelasticity en- 
hancement of NiTiNb alloys, the key lies in the simultaneous im- 
provement of their stress-induced martensitic transformation (SIM) 
and strain hardening capability [ 30 , 31 ]. During the deformation of 
NiTiNb alloys, the NiTi matrix will undergo a series of SIM pro- 
cesses, including reorientation and de-twinning [ 32 , 33 ]. These gen- 
erated twins will intersect with each other, plugging dislocations 
and providing a strong twinning-strengthening effect, but cracks 
will also sprout at these intersections [34] . As for the β-Nb phase, 
it plays a soft role in relieving the stress ﬁeld and coordinating 
the deformation [35] . For AMed NiTiNb alloys, their rapid cooling 
rate suppresses the β-Nb precipitation, which leads to oversatu- 
ration of the NiTi matrix and high residual stress [36] . Moreover, 
in AMed NiTiNb alloys, since the melting point of the Nb element 
(2469 °C) is much higher than that of NiTi alloys (1300 °C), there 
will always remain some unmelted Nb particles [37] . The β-Nb 
phase is mostly located around the unmelted Nb particles, exhibit- 
ing a mesh-like morphology [38] . It has been shown that modu- 
lating the morphology of the β-Nb phase by heat treatment can 
be an effective approach to improve the strain-hardening capabil- 
ity of NiTiNb alloys. Speciﬁcally, the transition of mesh-like β-Nb 
into dispersed β-Nb particles can lead to favorable SIM of the ma- 
trix in their vicinity, along with enhanced dislocation storage ca- 
pacity on their own [39] . However, the underlying mechanisms of 
this phenomenon, i.e., how β-Nb particles affect the SIM process 
in the surrounding matrix, as well as the dislocation behavior in- 
side β-Nb particles, have to be eagerly revealed by more in-depth 
atomic-scale investigation. 
In this work, we prepared rhomboidal dodecahedral NiTiNb 
scaffolds using laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) technology. To fur- 
ther improve the comprehensive mechanical properties of NiTiNb 
scaffolds, we annealed them at various temperatures and achieved 
a synergistic enhancement of strength-ductility-superelasticity. The 
microstructure evolution of the high-temperature annealed NiTiNb 
alloy was explored through atomic probe tomography and molec- 
ular dynamics (MD) simulation for the explanation of the mecha- 
nism of mechanical property enhancement. This work sheds light 
on the deformation mechanism of β-Nb particles in the NiTi-Nb 
eutectic region and their inﬂuence on the neighboring NiTi matrix, 
which can inspire obtaining AMed NiTiNb alloys with better serv- 
ing performance. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Sample preparation 
The raw materials were pre-alloyed spherical NiTi powder and 
irregular pure Nb powder, both prepared by atomization method 
with the morphology shown in Fig. 1(a) . The pre-alloyed NiTi pow- 
der and irregular Nb powder were mixed in the weight ratio of 4:1 
and ball milled at 400 r/min for 1 h. The nominal composition of 
the mixed powder was Ni44 Ti44 Nb12 . The chemical composition of 
the mixed powder ( Fig. 1 ( b –d )) was analyzed using energy disper- 
sive spectroscopy (EDS), indicating that the Nb powder was homo- 
geneously adhered to the NiTi powder. The powder size distribu- 
tion was measured with a laser particle size analyzer (BT-9300S), 
with a range of 10–60 μm ( Fig. 1(e) ). 
The rhomboidal dodecahedral porous scaffolds with unit cell 
dimensions of 2.56 mm × 2.56 mm × 2.56 mm, strut thickness 
of 0.35 mm, and designed porosity of 85.9 % were prepared us-

2.2. Microstructure characterization 
To analyze the phase composition of the NiTiNb scaffolds, 
micro-X-ray diffraction (micro-XRD) was carried out using a Rigaku 
X-ray diffractometer (Co- K α radiation) with a scanning speed of 
2 °/min, and a scanning range of 20 °–90 ° All the XRD specimens 
were sampled from the central strut with the XRD scanning plane 
parallel to the horizontal plane. Differential scanning calorimetry 
(DSC 8500) was used to measure the shape memory transforma- 
tion temperatures. The whole DSC experimentation was carried out 
under the nitrogen atmosphere with a heating and cooling rate of 
10 °C/min in the range of −150 to 150 °C. The specimens were me- 
chanically ground and polished according to standard metallurgi- 
cal procedures, followed by microstructural characterization using 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi 8030) equipped with 
a backscattered electron (BSE) and energy dispersive spectrome- 
ter. The microstructures were further characterized using a JEOL 
2100F transmission electron microscope (TEM) with an operating 
voltage of 200 kV. To probe the interfacial elemental distribution at 
the atomic scale, APT was performed using a Cameca LEAF 40 0 0X 
Si instrument with an experimental temperature of 50 K, a laser 
pulse energy of 45 pJ, a pulse frequency of 200 kHz, and an ion 
collection rate of 0.7 % per pulse. APT data were reconstructed and 
analyzed using IVAS 3.8.2 software. 
2.3. Mechanical testing 
Both quasi-static uniaxial compression tests and incremen- 
tal loading/unloading tests were performed at room temperature 
(25 °C). All compression specimens were wire cut from the as-built 
scaffolds to the same size (10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm). Uniaxial 
compression tests were loaded with a loading rate of 0.1 mm/min 
until specimen fracture. Subsequent incremental loading/unloading 
tests were performed at a strain rate of 1 × 10−4 s−1 with incre- 
ments of 100 N per cycle. To ensure the accuracy of strain mea- 
surements, we used an extensometer (Epsilon 3541, meeting the 
Class ASTM B-1 and ISO9513 Class 0.5 accuracy requirements) for 
all uniaxial compression and cycling tests conducted here. Fabri- 
cation of the micropillars was realized using a focused ion beam 
(FIB) of the SciosDualBeam, with the beam current being gradually 
reduced from 15 nA to 0.1 nA to ensure dimensional accuracy of 
the micropillars. A ring milling technique was used to remove the 
material around the micropillars to produce micropillars of approx- 
imately 6 μm in height and 2 μm in diameter, ensuring that the 
aspect ratio of the micropillar compression was maintained at ap- 
proximately 3:1. The selected micropillars were selected from NiTi- 
Nb eutectic regions (with a dispersed distribution of β-Nb parti- 
cles) and unmelted Nb particles. The micropillar compression tests 
were performed in displacement-controlled mode using a Zeiss Au- 
riga SEM-FIB equipped with a Hysitron PI88 system. The inden- 
ter used was a standard diamond indenter etched from FIB with 
a 5 μm tipping platform. The compression rate was maintained at 
5 nm/s throughout the whole process. 
2.4. Molecular dynamics simulation 
To better understand the experimental results and the related 
deformation mechanism at the atomic scale, MD simulation was 
carried out in this study. Simulations were performed using the 
parallel molecular dynamics package LAMMPS [40] . The simula- 
tion sample was constructed as a columnar shape to simulate the 
NiTi matrix with the B2 phase. The β-Nb particle with a cubic BCC 
structure was implanted in the NiTi matrix. The equilibrium struc- 
ture of the model was obtained after an initial energy minimiza- 
tion and the following system annealing procedure. The atomic ﬁg- 
ures are illustrated by the visualization tool Ovito [41] . The dislo- 
cation extraction algorithm (DXA) is used to extract the dislocation 
lines in the sample during deformation [42] . The equilibrium struc- 
ture of the model was obtained after an initial energy minimiza- 
tion and the following system annealing procedure. Before loading, 
the system was annealed in an isobaric-isotherm system of 300 K 
for 100 ps to ensure zero pressure in all directions, after which 
uniaxial compression was applied with a constant strain rate of 
5 × 108 s−1 . 
3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure characterization of NiTiNb scaffolds 
Fig. 2 shows the micro-XRD patterns of NiTiNb samples in 
different states, including as-built, annealed, and deformed, re- 
spectively. All NiTiNb scaffolds were composed of NiTi-B2 austen- 
ite, β-Nb, and NiTi-B19′ martensite. During the LPBF process, the 
faster cooling rate resulted in higher residual stress, causing some 
martensite to appear in the as-built sample [43] . We noticed that 
the relative peak intensities of both (−111) B19′ and (012) B19′ in 
annealed samples decreased compared to as-built samples, indicat- 
ing that the content of martensite was reduced, mainly attributed 
to the reduction of residual stresses after annealing. Meanwhile, 
(10 0)β-Nb and (20 0)β-Nb peaks appeared in the annealed samples, 
indicating the β-Nb precipitation during the high-temperature an- 
nealing process. In addition, for the deformed samples, the en- 
hancement of the peak intensities of (110)B2 and (110)β-Nb indi- 
cated the formation of a stronger deformation texture along the 
(110) direction [44] . As the deformation proceeded, the (110)B2 
diffraction peaks gradually increased to higher values, the same as 
previously reported [45] .

The measurements of phase transformation temperatures for 
the as-built sample and annealed sample were performed, as 
shown in Fig. 3 and Table 1 . We noticed that only the as-built 
sample appeared with two peaks upon cooling, indicating the van- 
ishing of the B2→ R→ B19′ two-step transformation after annealing

ture increases due to the presence of residual stress in the as-built 
sample, which is consistent with the previous numerical predic- 
tion [46] . The combination of the weakening of residual stress and 
the enhancement of elemental diffusion also leads to the inconsis- 
tency between the martensitic phase transformation peak and the 
annealing temperature. The phase transformation peaks of NiTiNb 
alloys weaken signiﬁcantly after high-temperature annealing, ac- 
companied by the expansion of the shape memory thermal hys- 
teresis ( As - Ms ). This was mainly attributed to the precipitation of 
massive β-Nb particles, and the substitution of Ti atoms by Nb el- 
ements in the nearby matrix, leading to the increase of Ni content 
and the weakening of the phase transformation peaks. The ther- 
mal hysteresis exhibits a wide range after annealing at different 
temperatures, especially for the high-temperature annealed sam- 
ples, with the thermal hysteresis being larger than 20 °C. As far 
as the martensitic start transformation temperature ( Ms ) was con- 
cerned, high-temperature annealing caused a drastic decrease in 
the Ms temperature (by up to 76.9 °C), implying a steep increase 
in the diﬃculty of the martensitic transformation [47] . The gen- 
erally decreased phase transformation characteristic temperatures 
and increased thermal hysteresis implied that the change in β-Nb 
morphology had a huge impact on the diﬃculty and reversibility 
of the phase transformation.

To further explore the inﬂuence of the β-Nb phase on its sur- 
rounding NiTi matrix, we performed APT characterization in the 
NiTi-Nb eutectic region and investigated its elemental distribu- 
tion at the atomic scale as shown in Fig. 5 ( e , F ). First of all, 
Fig. 5(e) shows the selected region as well as a side view of the 
tip, which contained both the β-Nb particles and the NiTi matrix. 
This was also conﬁrmed by the elemental 3D reconstruction maps, 
where a large amount of Nb elements were distributed in the β-Nb 
particles, while a small portion was also present in the NiTi ma- 
trix, which was mainly attributed to the substitution of Nb with 
Ti elements [54] . Particularly, we perform a one-dimensional line 
concentration analysis for the interface between the two phases, 
as shown in Fig. 5(f) . It can be seen that the NiTi matrix had a rel- 
atively Ni-rich composition, where the decrease in Ti content was 
precisely due to the substitution of Nb. For the β-Nb region, the 
Nb content here was 91.18 % higher than that in the NiTi matrix. 
Subsequently, we focused on the interfacial elemental concentra- 
tion changes, and the Nb element was divided into a slowly rising 
stage (0–1, with a width of about 4 nm) and a rapidly rising stage 
(1–2). One can assume that the 0–1 stage was the Nb diffusion 
zone, while the 1–2 stage was the real interface between the two 
phases. As well known, the martensitic transformation tempera- 
ture is highly sensitive to the change of Ni content in nearly equal 
atomic ratio NiTi alloys. For every 0.2 % increase in the Ni content 
in the matrix, the martensitic transformation temperature will de- 
crease by 20 °C [55] . One thing should be noted here that there 
is a reversal of Ni: Ti ratio in the 0–1 stage, i.e., the Ti content 
became higher than Ni in the latter half of the 0–1 stage, which 
would play a signiﬁcant role in the NiTi phase transformation 
behavior. 
3.2. Mechanical properties 
The mechanical properties of as-built and annealed NiTiNb scaf- 
folds were evaluated using uniaxial compression testing, and the 
results are shown in Fig. 6(a) . Compared to the as-built samples, 
the annealed samples exhibited signiﬁcant strength-ductility syn- 
ergistic enhancement. Impressively, for the sample annealed at 
850 °C, the compressive strength (45 MPa) was more than doubled 
compared to the as-built state (20 MPa). Regarding the compres- 
sive deformation behavior, all the porous samples show the typical 
multi-stage deformation characteristics. In other words, the sam- 
ples did not fracture as a whole when the peak stress reached 
but maintained the stress plateau for a while, and then the inter- 
nal struts fractured sequentially. One should note that due to the 
stress concentration inside the porous structure, the whole scaffold 
exhibited a mixed deformation mechanism, i.e., the actual strains 
varied in different localizations [ 56 , 57 ]. It leads to the blurring of 
the stress plateau in the deformation curves of solid NiTi alloys,

and right nodes with the maximum curvature, and then expanded

cracks met, the scaffolds slightly collapsed, and then the longitu-

which had the maximum curvature, suffered the most severe stress

longitudinal fracture, which was consistent with the behavior of 
multi-stage fracture behavior in the stress-strain curve in Fig. 6(a) . 
In addition, the compressive load applied to the rhombic dodec-

gential force components, corresponding to the compressive and 
bending deformation of the strut, respectively [65] . It is also con- 
ﬁrmed by this ﬁnding that bending deformation plays a dominant 
role in such unit-cell scaffolds [66] .

4. Discussion 
In this work, a synergistic improvement of strength-ductility- 
superelasticity of NiTiNb scaffolds was achieved by high- 
temperature annealing. Such an improvement in comprehensive 
mechanical properties was predominantly attributed to the mor- 
phological alteration of the NiTi-Nb eutectic region after annealing, 
and the underlying mechanism will be discussed in this section. 
4.1. Effects of annealing on shape memory transformation 
temperatures 
The serving performance of NiTiNb eutectic SMAs is mainly de- 
pendent on the phase transformation characteristic temperatures 
and the morphology of the NiTi-Nb eutectic region [73] . Firstly, we 
focus on Ms , Af temperatures and thermal hysteresis. As can be 
seen from Fig. 3 and Table 1 , compared with the as-built sample, 
the phase transformation temperatures of all annealed samples 
decreased dramatically and were accompanied by a signiﬁcant 
expansion of the thermal hysteresis. Especially for the high- 
temperature annealed samples (750 and 850 °C), such a huge 
drop has never been previously reported. In addition, for the high- 
temperature annealed samples, the thermal hysteresis has ex- 
panded to more than 20 °C, indicating that the austenite ↔ 
martensite mutual transformation was pretty diﬃcult [74] . The 
above phenomena need to be explained in terms of the mi- 
crostructure characteristics, especially the morphology and distri- 
bution of β-Nb particles. 
From Fig. 4 , it can be observed that after the high-temperature 
annealing, the unmelted Nb particles underwent further eutec- 
tic reaction with the NiTi matrix, and their integrity was dam- 
aged. Meanwhile, the morphology of β-Nb particles in the NiTi- 
Nb eutectic region realized a transition from mesh to dispersed 
sphere, as reported in our previous work [39] . Brieﬂy, the rapid 
cooling during LPBF caused NiTi-B2 to exist as ﬁne equiaxed 
grains, whereas β-Nb tended to precipitate along the grain bound- 
aries to form a mesh-like morphology. During subsequent high- 
temperature annealing, the β-Nb phase would experience fusion 
and spheroidization processes, and the mesh-like morphology of 
β-Nb would be damaged [35] . Therefore, after high-temperature 
annealing, on the one hand, the content of the β-Nb phase in- 
creased, and on the other hand, the dispersed distribution of β-Nb 
particles had a larger contact area with the NiTi matrix. 
For NiTi-based SMAs, the phase transformation temperatures 
are highly correlated with the Ni: Ti atomic ratio, i.e., the higher 
the Ni: Ti ratio, the lower the phase transformation temperatures 
[ 75 , 76 ]. To investigate the inﬂuence of β-Nb particles on the ma- 
trix in their vicinity, we employed APT to analyze the elemental

By carefully comparing the phase transformation temperatures 
in Fig. 3 and Table 1 , we noticed that the phase transformation 
temperature of NiTiNb alloys ﬁrst decreased and then increased 
with the elevation of the annealing temperature. From the pre- 
vious analysis, the unmelted Nb particles would partially react 
with the NiTi matrix during annealing and precipitate β-Nb par- 
ticles in the vicinity. Given the DSC results, we considered that 
the 650 °C annealing process could only allow a few unmelted 
Nb particles to react with the matrix, while high-temperature an- 
nealing (750 °C and above) was suﬃcient to enable the eutectic 
reaction to be more abundant, and the destruction of unmelted 
Nb particles to be more pronounced. As the annealing tempera- 
ture continued to increase, the phase transformation temperatures 
showed a tendency to rise back up again, which was highly proba- 
ble due to the Ostwald ripening mechanism at high temperatures, 
which caused the merging and growing of β-Nb particles between 
them [79] . From the APT results, β-Nb particles can only affect the 
Ni: Ti atomic ratio within several nanometers in their vicinity, so 
the magnitude of phase transformation temperature reduction by 
coarser and fewer β-Nb particles was rather weakened. Moreover, 
the thermal hysteresis of NiTiNb alloys after high-temperature an- 
nealing also expanded greatly, indicating that the diﬃculty of the 
austenite ↔ martensite mutual transformation increased. Like many 
previously reported NiTiNb alloys prepared by conventional melt- 
ing, the larger thermal hysteresis was mainly attributed to the 
crystallographic hindrance caused by more β-Nb particles during 
the phase transformation in the NiTi matrix [ 74 , 80 ]. Returning to 
Fig. 4 , the precipitation of more dispersed β-Nb particles in the 
matrix inevitably hindered the onset of the mutual phase transfor- 
mation, allowing for greater potential in biomedical applications. 
4.2. The improvement mechanism of mechanical properties in NiTiNb 
alloys 
The strength-ductility of NiTi-based SMAs strongly depends on 
their strain hardening capability, i.e., the segment after the stress 
plateau of the stress-strain curve. It can be seen from Fig. 6(a) that 
as-built and annealed samples almost overlap before 2 % strain. 
However, compared to the as-built sample, the annealed sample 
exhibited a higher strain hardening rate and better ductility (espe- 
cially for the 850 °C annealed sample), which ultimately enabled a 
substantial increase in strength and ductility. Therefore, the key to 
improving the mechanical properties of NiTiNb scaffolds lies in the 
continuous provision of a strong strain hardening capability [81] . 
As can be seen in Fig. 10 , the strength of the NiTi-Nb eutec- 
tic micropillar was much higher than that of the Nb micropillar, 
suggesting that the embedded Nb particles played almost no re- 
inforcing role. Zhang et al. [ 39 ] have already reported the defor- 
mation mechanism of NiTiNb eutectic alloys in detail. Brieﬂy, the 
NiTi matrix mainly undergoes the SIM process, and the resultant 
twins can provide strong twinning strengthening and contribute to 
strain hardening [82] . Meanwhile, the β-Nb phase can store dis- 
locations inside, which can coordinate the deformation and im- 
prove the ductility [83] . In NiTiNb alloys, the morphology of the 
β-Nb phase will affect the room for SIM and the dislocation stor- 
age capacity itself. In general, NiTiNb alloys containing predomi- 
nantly dispersedly distributed spherical β-Nb have been shown to 
have a superior strength-ductility combination. 
Therein, we focus on the inﬂuence of β-Nb on the NiTi marten- 
sitic transformation in its neighborhood. The microstructure evolu- 
tion of the NiTi matrix and the NiTi-Nb eutectic region after defor- 
mation was shown in Fig. 9 , where numerous twins can be found 
only in the NiTi matrix ( Fig. 9 (a, c )). Notably, these twins were 
(11−1) type I twin variants, conﬁrming the occurrence of reorien- 
tation in the SIM process [84] . Whereas, in the NiTi-Nb eutectic 
region, the matrix was mainly composed of austenite with dislo- 
cations pinned by β-Nb particles. The above phenomena indicated 
that the β-Nb particles enabled their nearby matrix to still have 
room for SIM even under large strain, and were able to continu- 
ously release the strain energy through a series of twinning pro- 
cesses [85] . 
Apart from the microstructure evolution results, we also per- 
formed MD simulations for the NiTi-Nb eutectic region ( Figs. 11 
and 12 ). The main deformation mechanism activated in the NiTi 
matrix was SIM, whereas dislocations initiated inside β-Nb, ac- 
companied by the dislocation entanglement. More than that, the 
presence of β-Nb particles hindered the twin growth, i.e., higher 
stress was needed for the twin nucleation in the vicinity of β- 
Nb particles [72] . All these phenomena indicated that β-Nb par- 
ticles in the NiTi-Nb eutectic region can not only store dislocations 
and coordinate the deformation, but also act as reinforcements, en- 
tangling dislocations, and hindering twin growth, thus providing 
strain hardening effect. 
As mentioned in Discussion 4.1, the 850 °C annealing can 
maximize the reaction of unmelted Nb particles with the matrix 
with the precipitated β-Nb particles experiencing Ostwald ripen- 
ing. These larger β-Nb particles can store more dislocations, and 
the internal dislocation entanglement also contributes to stronger 
strain hardening, yielding the best mechanical properties. Gener- 
ally speaking, adding hard phase or nanoparticles is an effective 
way to strengthen the alloy. However, in this study, we proposed a 
novel material design concept of adding a soft phase to inﬂuence 
the phase transformation behavior of the matrix via the element 
diffusion within several nanometers. Meanwhile, the dislocation 
movement inside the soft Nb phase contributed to strain harden- 
ing, which is beneﬁcial to improving the comprehensive mechani- 
cal properties. 
5. Conclusions 
In this work, we prepared rhomboidal dodecahedral NiTiNb 
scaffolds with 85.9 % porosity by the LPBF technique. After high- 
temperature annealing, the compressive strength of the NiTiNb 
scaffolds was more than doubled, the phase transformation tem- 
peratures were drastically decreased, and the thermal hysteresis 
was expanded. The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1) High-temperature annealing caused the unmelted Nb particles 
to further react with the NiTi matrix, while the mesh-like β- 
Nb was damaged and transformed into dispersedly distributed 
spherical β-Nb particles. 
(2) The APT results of the NiTi-Nb eutectic region revealed an ele- 
mental diffusion zone in the range of several nanometers in the 
vicinity of the β-Nb particle. The substitution of Nb with Ti led

(3) The microstructure evolution of the deformed NiTiNb alloy

(4) MD simulations indicated that β-Nb particles can store dislo-
