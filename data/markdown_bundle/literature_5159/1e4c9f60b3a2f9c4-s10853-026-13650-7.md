ABSTRACT
The formation of B2 structures is an important feature of many Al-containing 
refractory complex concentrated alloys (RCCAs). However, the term “B2 
ordering” is commonly used to describe a broad range of ordered states that 
differ in origin, morphology, stability, and mechanical response, often leading 
to inconsistent interpretations of experimental observations. This review 
critically reassesses B2 ordering in RCCAs by analysing its thermodynamic 
origin, formation mechanisms, thermal stability, and influence on mechanical 
behaviour. The available evidence reveals that B2 structures emerge through three 
distinct formation pathways, which differ in their transformation mechanisms 
and microstructural manifestations. The analysis further demonstrates that the 
formation pathway governs the stability of B2 structures and their contribution 
to mechanical performance, explaining the wide range of behaviours reported 
in the literature, from exceptional high-temperature strength to simultaneous 
improvements in strength and ductility. By establishing explicit links between 
formation pathway, degree of B2 ordering, microstructural evolution, 
and mechanical properties, this review provides a unified framework for 
understanding B2 ordering in RCCAs and for guiding the design of next-
generation alloys.

Origin of B2 structure in RCCAs
Driving forces upon Al addition
The strong negative mixing enthalpies between Al and

interactions promote the local ordering of atoms 
rather than a random solid solution, establishing 
the thermodynamic basis for the development of an 
ordered structure.
The specific selection of the B2 structure over 
other possible ordered arrangements is explained 
by the electronic structure of Al–TM systems.

The incorporation of Al simultaneously addresses 
multiple performance deficiencies while introducing 
intriguing microstructural complexity: Al additions 
reduce alloy density through its low atomic mass (2.7

[9–24]. This B2 ordering phenomenon has emerged 
as one of the important features governing the 
mechanical performance of Al-containing RCCAs, 
creating opportunities for extra strengthening and 
unique deformation mechanisms not accessible in 
disordered solid solutions.
Despite the rapidly growing interest in 
Al-containing RCCAs, the nature of B2 ordering 
remains interpreted inconsistently across the 
literature. Depending on alloy composition and 
thermodynamic conditions, B2 structures may 
form through fundamentally different pathways, 
including spinodal-induced phase separation [12, 15, 
25], classical nucleation-and-growth mechanism [17, 
26], or continuous disorder–order transformation 
[11, 13, 27–29]. Importantly, despite their different 
thermodynamic origins, the earliest stages of all three 
pathways may generate comparable experimental 
signatures of B2 ordering. As a result, structurally 
similar features may correspond to fundamentally 
different ordering phenomena, complicating the 
interpretation of experimental observations and 
contributing to inconsistent use of concepts such as 
the degree of B2 ordering [11, 16, 18, 19, 24, 30].
This ambiguity is particularly significant because B2 
ordering has been associated with a remarkably broad 
spectrum of mechanical responses. Alloys containing 
continuous B2 matrices can exhibit exceptional 
high-temperature strength but limited plasticity 
[9, 31, 32], whereas alloys with B2 nanodomains 
may simultaneously achieve high strength and 
substantial room-temperature tensile ductility [18, 
20, 21]. Similarly, the thermal stability of B2 structures 
ranges from dissolution or transformation into other 
intermetallics at elevated temperatures [14, 33–37] 
to Ostwald ripening [26, 38] and persistence during 
prolonged annealing [29, 39]. These contrasting 
behaviours suggest that the effects of B2 ordering

The quantitative ordering tendency in a

competition among several Al–TM pairs and on the 
configurational entropy of the ordered phase. For the 
prototypical AlNbTiV alloy [42] simulations revealed 
a B2 ordering transition below approximately 1700

of Al and Ti atoms occupying opposite sublattices, 
while Nb and V showed much weaker sublattice 
preferences (Fig. 1). This sublattice disorder of Nb 
and V results in a configurational entropy of the

would be expected from an idealised B2(AlNb)

intermetallic” character of these phases.
In Zr-containing alloys, the picture changes: 
similar calculations on the AlNbVTiZr alloy [43] 
exhibited that the Al–Zr bond dominates, so that the 
most energetically stable configuration corresponds 
to Al and V occupying one sublattice while Nb, Zr, 
and Ti occupy the other. These composition-specific 
ordering tendencies are crucial for understanding 
why the critical Al content, the thermal stability, 
and the mechanical properties of B2 phases vary so 
widely across different RCCA families.
Formation criteria and predictability of the B2 
phase
Traditional solid-solution predictors for HEAs—
atomic size difference (δ), mixing enthalpy (ΔH mix), 
the Ω parameter (Ω = TmΔSmix/|ΔHmix|), and valence 
electron concentration (VEC)—offer only limited 
guidance for B2 formation in RCCAs. Recently, more 
targeted criteria for B2 ordering [16] were proposed: 
ΔHmix < −14 kJ/mol and Bo < 0.4Md + 1.9, where Bo 
and Md are the bond order and d-orbital energy 
level, respectively. Adapted from Ti alloy design, 
these electronic parameters underscore the role of 
bonding effects beyond simple size and enthalpy 
considerations.
CALPHAD (CALculation of PHAse Diagrams) 
methods, despite their widespread use in alloy 
design, frequently mispredict B2 phase formation 
in Al-containing RCCAs because the underlying 
thermodynamic databases describe ordering 
inadequately [14, 23, 39, 44]. The B2 phase is 
absent from many binary Al–TM phase diagrams, 
preventing reliable extrapolation into multicomponent 
compositional spaces. Even with the progressive 
expansion of high-entropy databases (e.g. the

Pathways of B2 phase formation
Although the driving forces for B2 phase formation are 
increasingly understood, the transformation pathway 
by which ordering develops can differ substantially 
between alloy systems. In Al-containing RCCAs, B2 
formation proceeds through three thermodynamically 
distinct pathways:
Pathway I—Spinodal decomposition coupled with 
ordering. When the alloy composition lies within a 
miscibility gap of the high-temperature A2 phase, 
the separation into two compositionally distinct 
A2 regions occurs. Ordering to the B2 structure 
takes place either concurrently with this phase 
separation or shortly thereafter, depending on the 
relative positions of the chemical spinodal and 
the disorder–order transition temperature. The 
characteristic microstructural hallmark is a nanoscale, 
elastically aligned mixture of B2 channels and A2 
cuboids [9 , 12] that coarsens during subsequent 
ageing, with the phase composition evolving 
continuously while the volume fraction remains nearly 
constant. Prolonged annealing may lead to a “phase 
inversion” into discrete B2 precipitates embedded in 
the A2 matrix [25]. This pathway usually operates in 
Ta+Zr-containing RCCAs. It was first identified in 
the AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr alloy [9, 47], then definitively 
established for the Al0.5NbTa0.8Ti1.5V0.2Zr alloy [12, 25] 
and for low-Al (Ti-Ta-Zr)100−xAlₓ compositions [15].
Pathway II—Nucleation and growth from a 
supersaturated A2 matrix. In the absence of a miscibility 
gap, the B2 phase forms by a classical nucleation-
and-growth mechanism. Quenching from the single-
phase A2 field may yield a solid solution that already 
contains nanometre-sized B2-ordered clusters with 
different chemical composition. These clusters 
evolve into precipitates during subsequent ageing by 
moving the sharp A2/B2 interface, with no chemical 
modulation of the B2 phase with time. This behaviour, 
accompanied by a broad exothermic DSC (differential 
scanning calorimetry) peak characteristic of a 
diffusion-controlled reaction, has been documented in 
Cr-containing low-Al alloys such as 82(TaMoTi)−8Cr-
10Al [17, 26].
Pathway III—Continuous, second-order A2 → B2 
ordering. When the A2 phase is thermodynamically 
stable at high temperature and there is no miscibility 
gap, ordering can proceed by a continuous, second-
order transition at a critical temperature. Because 
the transformation involves only local atomic 
rearrangements over distances of a few unit cells and 
no long-range diffusion, it cannot be suppressed by 
rapid quenching and invariably produces antiphase 
domain boundaries (APBs). The transition is 
characterised by a λ-shaped DSC peak, reversibility, 
and negligible compositional partitioning between 
the ordered and disordered regions at the transition 
point. Experimentally confirmed (by DSC analysis) 
examples are Ta–Mo–Cr–Ti–Al alloys [13, 27]. Alloys 
such as the Alx(NbTiZr) 100−x and AlxNb40Ti40V20−x, 
extensively studied by Yurchenko et al. [18, 19, 28, 
29, 48], are also assigned provisionally to Pathway III. 
This assignment is based solely on kinetic inference 
and microstructural analogy with (Nb,Ti)-Al-(Mo,V) 
alloys [49–53], but DSC confirmation of a second-order 
transition is still lacking.
Although these three pathways originate 
from fundamentally different thermodynamic 
mechanisms, they may produce similar experimental 
signatures. After phase inversion in Pathway I, 
the final microstructure can become difficult to 
distinguish from that produced by Pathway II, 
while the identification of B2 ordering alone does

phase without detectable compositional partitioning 
and compositionally distinct secondary B2 phases 
separated by discernible interfaces. In discussing 
these nanoscale features, the former are referred 
to as B2 nanodomains (Pathway III), whereas the 
latter are referred to as B2 precipitates (Pathways I 
and II), unless the original terminology used in the 
cited study is essential for the interpretation of the

The transformation pathway determines not only 
how the ordered state forms, but also how it evolves 
during subsequent thermal exposure. In Pathway 
I alloys, the B2 phase is frequently metastable, 
experiencing dissolution, transformation into other 
intermetallics or disordering at T > 700 °C [ 34–37, 
77]. In Pathway II alloys, the dominant degradation 
mechanism is Ostwald ripening of B2 precipitates 
[26], which can be retarded by proper microalloying, 
reducing the lattice misfit [ 38]. In turn, Pathway 
III alloys are characterised by stable B2 phases, 
with constant [29] or variable sizes of B2-ordered 
domains [48, 71, 76], and the independent formation 
of other intermetallics [29, 39]. Thus, thermal history 
must be interpreted in a pathway-specific manner: 
similar increases in an apparent B2 feature size 
can correspond to phase inversion, precipitate 
coarsening, or domain growth, with different 
consequences for strength and plasticity.
Quantitative description of B2 ordering
While the pathways of B2 phase formation have been 
largely clarified, the definition and measurement 
of the “degree of B2 ordering” remain a significant 
source of confusion in the literature. Two broad classes 
of descriptors have been employed: chemical order 
descriptors, which characterise sublattice occupation, 
and dimensional descriptors, which characterise 
the spatial extent and amount of ordered regions. 
These quantities capture different aspects of the 
ordered state, and they frequently yield contradictory 
assessments of the same alloy.
The classical measure of order in crystalline 
materials is the long-range order parameter (LROP), 
defined from the ratio of the integrated intensities 
of a superlattice and a fundamental peak in an XRD 
pattern, corrected for Lorentz polarisation factors 
and the structure factors of the two sublattice sites. 
This method was first applied by Yurchenko et al. to 
the AlNbTiVZrₓ alloys, yielding LROP values that 
ranged from 0.71 (x = 0) to 0.37 (x = 1.5) [11]. The 
trend suggested a decrease in order with increasing 
Zr content. A simplified variant of this approach was 
used by Qi et al. [30]: the simulated at 0 K supercell 
was taken as the reference state with LROP = 1, to 
which the XRD patterns of the experimental alloys 
were compared.
Several well-recognised problems limit this 
approach. First, it requires an assumed site 
occupancy—i.e. which elements occupy which 
sublattice. In the absence of direct experimental 
evidence, the authors [11] assumed, based on the 
literature for binary Ti–Al and Zr–Al systems, that 
Ti, Nb, and Zr occupy one sublattice and Al and V 
the other. However, DFT calculations on different 
alloy systems have yielded diverse site preferences: 
Al–Ti driven in Zr-free AlNbTiV [42], but Al–Zr 
driven in Zr-containing AlNbVTiZr [43]. Without 
direct experimental determination of sublattice 
occupancy for the specific alloy (e.g. via high-
resolution STEM–EDS analysis [24, 70]), the assumed 
assignment remains uncertain. Consequently, LROP 
values derived from XRD alone must be interpreted 
with caution.

factor for the superlattice reflection is proportional to 
the difference in the average atomic scattering factors 
of the two sublattice sites. In a multicomponent 
alloy, it is highly possible for this difference to be

Recognising the limitations of the LROP, several 
groups began to characterise the degree of B2 ordering 
using dimensional descriptors. In their pioneering 
work, Qiao et al. traced the growth of B2 domains in 
the Ti2ZrHf0.5VNb0.5Alx alloys from ~2 nm to >100 nm 
with increasing Al content and interpreted this as a 
continuous progression from “SRO” to “LRO” [16]. 
This work introduced the concept that the degree of 
B2 ordering can be characterised not only by chemical 
order descriptors, but also by dimensional descriptors 
describing the spatial extent of ordered regions.
Yurchenko et al. extended this approach [18]. For 
semi-quantitative characterisation of the degree of B2 
ordering in the Alₓ(NbTiZr)100−x alloys, they combined 
the analysis of superlattice diffraction spot intensities 
(from line profiles across SAEDs) with dimensional 
descriptors, namely B2 domain size and volume 
fraction, determined by threshold segmentation of 
dark-field TEM images.
The use of dimensional descriptors to characterise 
the degree of B2 ordering was rapidly adopted and, 
since 2022, had become the de facto standard in the 
community [19– 22, 24, 55, 56, 58–60, 64], offering 
clear advantages over the classical LROP: it directly 
visualises the ordered regions, can detect very small 
domains even when bulk XRD is silent, and provides 
metrics (size and volume fraction) that are more 
readily connected to mechanical properties (Fig. 3).
However, this approach is not without its own 
limitations. Dimensional descriptors depend on 
imaging conditions and threshold segmentation 
procedures and do not provide direct information 
on the chemical order within individual domains. 
Two alloys with similar B2 domain sizes and volume 
fractions may therefore have different sublattice 
occupancies and, consequently, different mechanical 
property profiles [24].
Thus, the “degree of B2 ordering” in 
multicomponent RCCAs cannot generally be 
represented by a single descriptor. Chemical order and 
dimensional descriptors provide complementary but 
non-equivalent information: the former characterise 
sublattice occupation, whereas the latter describe the 
spatial extent and amount of ordered regions (Table 3). 
These quantities should therefore be distinguished 
explicitly when comparing ordering states and their 
relationships with mechanical properties.
Throughout the remainder of this Review, we 
therefore retain the term “degree of B2 ordering” 
as a general description of the ordered state, while 
specifying whether it is characterised using chemical 
order or dimensional descriptors and, where possible, 
identifying the specific descriptor used in the cited 
study.
Effect of B2 ordering on mechanical 
properties and deformation behaviour
The three transformation pathways produce 
fundamentally different B2 morphologies 
and, consequently, distinct structure–property 
relationships. To preserve these relationships, the 
available mechanical property data are discussed 
separately at room and elevated temperatures and, 
within each temperature range, according to the B2 
formation pathway. The corresponding strengthening, 
deformation, and fracture mechanisms are considered 
together with the reported properties and testing 
modes. Because compression, tension, hardness, and 
creep measurements probe non-equivalent mechanical 
responses, direct comparisons are made only where 
the loading mode and experimental conditions are 
explicitly identified.

boundaries (Fig. 5b). These particles are substantially 
harder than the surrounding B2/A2 regions, exhibit 
negligible dislocation activity under indentation, 
and crack readily [79] (Fig. 5b1). The Al–Zr-rich grain 
boundary precipitates also produce pronounced 
tension–compression asymmetry: regardless of 
whether B2 or A2 serves as the matrix, all variants fail 
almost elastically in tension at <0.15% strain and <200 
MPa through fully intergranular fracture [81].
However, grain boundary intermetallics alone may 
not fully account for the observed brittleness. In situ 
tensile experiments coupled with calculations suggest 
that the B2 phase itself possesses a lower fracture 
resistance than both the A2/B2 interface and the A2 
phase. This behaviour originates from the antibonding 
character of Al–Zr interactions, which promotes 
preferential crack initiation and propagation despite 
the activation of plastic deformation mechanisms such 
as twinning and amorphisation [82].
Pathway II alloys
Room-temperature uniaxial mechanical data are not 
currently available for Pathway II alloys. The reported 
evidence is restricted to hardness measurements for 
the 82(TaMoTi)−8Cr-10Al alloy and therefore cannot 
be directly compared with the tensile or compression 
data reported for Pathways I and III. Moreover, 
because the available mechanical property data for 
Pathway II are essentially limited to this single alloy 
family, its behaviour cannot yet be generalised to the 
pathway as a whole. The peak hardness (≈7.3 GPa) 
in the 82(TaMoTi)−8Cr-10Al alloy is achieved at B2 
precipitate sizes ≤10 nm and volume fractions of 
0.1–0.2 [26] (Fig. 6).
Deformation involves shearing of B2 precipitates 
by paired a/2<111> dislocations, with the antiphase 
boundary (APB) energy of 150 ± 39 mJ/m2, which is 
back-calculated from the peak strengthening using 
the TEM-derived precipitate volume fraction (an 
alternative SEM-derived fraction gives 202 ± 52mJ/m2). 
The transition from weakly to strongly coupled pair 
shearing occurs at a critical radius of ~ 2.5 nm, causing 
a 6–12% hardness drop upon overaging [83]. No 
Orowan looping, twinning, or phase transformation 
is observed.
Pathway III alloys
Pathway III alloys exhibit a strong dependence of 
room-temperature mechanical properties on the 
degree of B2 ordering. Alloys with single-phase 
(e.g. AlNbTiV, Al20Nb40Ti40, 85(TiCrMo)−15Al, 
 A l25Hf25Nb25Ti25) or dominant (e.g. AlNbTiVZr x, 
 AlCrxNbTiV) B2 structure and a high degree of B2 
ordering (in terms of site occupancy), though being 
generally softer than Pathway I counterparts (yield 
strengths < 1700 MPa), often exhibit limited plasticity, 
in some cases even under compression loading [ 11, 
19, 27, 30, 39, 84–86]. The strength in these alloys has 
been attributed to solid-solution [11] and B2 order 
strengthening [87], although quantitative estimation 
of the latter requires new strengthening models 
and deconvolution of the contributions from grain 
boundaries, secondary phases, and interstitial solutes. 
The origin of the limited plasticity in these alloys, 
however, remains unclear. A systematic investigation 
of the underlying deformation mechanisms has been

The improvement of plasticity reported for some B2

change in the character of dislocation motion. While 
dislocation glide by overcoming the Peierls–Nabarro 
barriers remains the rate-controlling mechanism of 
plastic deformation [18, 28], as in the corresponding 
A2 alloys, B2 ordering causes the formation of multiple 
dislocation bands produced by the glide of a/2<111> 
screw dislocations, leading to a local reduction of 
order [18] or its disappearance [20, 70]. Interactions 
with B2 nanodomains may locally pin and curve 
dislocations, producing stress heterogeneity proposed 
to facilitate cross-slip and secondary/intersecting slip 
bands [18, 20, 21, 24]. The resulting redistribution of 
dislocation activity into multiple interacting bands 
improves the macroscopic stability of plastic flow and 
extends the strain-hardening stage, thereby delaying 
strain localisation and necking [18, 20, 21, 24, 28, 
95–97] (Fig. 8).

demonstrate compression–tension asymmetry: while 
being apparently ductile during compression, high 
Al-containing alloys appear brittle in tension [97]. The 
origin of this phenomenon remains unclear, which 
necessitates further exploration.

effects can reduce the plasticity of some Pathway III 
alloys through either precipitation of intermetallics 
or early macroscopic localisation induced by an 
accelerated oxidation [29, 39].
Elevated‑temperature mechanical performance
Pathway I alloys
The elevated-temperature mechanical behaviour 
during compression tests of Pathway I alloys remains 
strongly dependent on the morphology of the B2 phase. 
Alloys with a continuous B2 matrix with embedded 
A2 precipitates demonstrate a classical decrease in 
strength with increasing temperature, while retaining 
remarkable compressive yield strengths (~700 MPa, e.g. 
 AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr) up to 1200 °C [9 , 31, 32], thereby 
outperforming conventional Ni-based superalloys 
(Fig. 9). Coherency strengthening associated with the 
B2/A2 interfaces is believed to contribute significantly 
to this strength retention [78]. However, at T > 1200 °C, 
the B2 phase disappears and the yield strength collapses 
to ~150 MPa [32]. This abrupt loss of strength highlights 
the decisive role of B2 ordering in maintaining elevated-
temperature performance, but does not isolate B2 
ordering as the sole strengthening source.
In a discrete form, the B2 phase can give rise to an 
unusual positive temperature dependence of yield 
strength within the 400–600 °C interval [12, 74]. 
Observed in several alloys (e.g. Al0.5NbTa0.8Ti1.5V0.2Zr, 
 A l10Nb15Ta5Ti30Zr40), this anomalous behaviour is 
attributed to a transition from weakly to strongly 
coupled dislocation pair shearing of B2 precipitates 
as their size increases during thermal exposure [74]. 
The peak strengthening contribution of B2 precipitates 
is indirectly estimated to reach ~635 MPa, assuming 
an APB energy of 176 mJ/m 2, which indicates order 
strengthening as the dominant strengthening 
mechanism [74]. Direct observations at 600 °C further 
showed paired a/2<111> dislocations shearing the B2 
precipitates, accompanied by their dynamic coarsening 
and the development of highly localised wavy 
deformation bands associated with extensive cross-slip 
[101]. At higher temperatures, strength decreases rapidly 
owing to precipitate coarsening beyond the critical 
size, progressive dissolution of secondary phases, and 
the consequent loss of both precipitation and solid-
solution strengthening [74]. As a result, B2 precipitates 
provide a smaller overall strengthening contribution 
than a continuous B2 matrix and lose their effectiveness 
at substantially lower temperatures, making phase-
inverted alloys competitive with Ni-based superalloys 
only below ~700 °C (Fig. 9).
Despite the excellent high-temperature strength 
of some Pathway I alloys, especially those with a 
continuous B2 matrix, the main concern is their virtually 
zero tensile ductility, which persists up to 1200 °C and is 
generally attributed to the presence of grain boundary 
Al–Zr-rich precipitates [81]. Although new strategies 
aimed at suppressing these undesirable phases and 
improving microstructural stability have recently been 
proposed [102], a broader range of mechanical testing 
modes is needed, as reliance on compression data alone 
may lead to an overly optimistic assessment of alloy 
performance.
Pathway II alloys
Elevated-temperature mechanical data for Pathway 
II alloys remain extremely limited and are currently 
restricted to the 82(TaMoTi)−8Cr-10Al alloy [103].

For Pathway III alloys, the elevated-temperature 
mechanical behaviour has received considerably 
less attention than room-temperature performance. 
Nevertheless, the available data suggest that B2

counterparts, irrespective of whether the ordered state

with a dominant B2 phase structure display a yield 
stress anomaly, manifested by a positive temperature 
dependence of yield strength between ~600 and

dissociated a<111> superdislocations [85].

environmental degradation, fatigue resistance, and

B2-ordered RCCAs.
Conclusions and outlook
The present review demonstrates that B2 ordering in 
Al-containing RCCAs cannot be regarded as a single 
phenomenon. Instead, B2 structures emerge through 
three distinct formation pathways, each characterised 
by different thermodynamic origins, microstructural 
manifestations, stability ranges, and mechanical 
consequences.
Pathway I, involving spinodal decomposition 
coupled with ordering, produces microstructures in 
which the B2 phase forms either a continuous matrix or 
discrete precipitates. A continuous B2 matrix provides 
the highest strength at both ambient and elevated 
temperatures, whereas the formation of discrete B2 
precipitates improves plasticity at the expense of 
strength. Nevertheless, these alloys generally suffer 
from pronounced tension–compression asymmetry 
and susceptibility to microstructural instability at 
elevated temperatures.
Pathway II, based on nucleation and growth of 
B2 precipitates from a supersaturated A2 matrix, 
represents the closest analogue to conventional 
precipitation-strengthened superalloys. Although 
precipitation mechanisms, coarsening behaviour, 
and creep performance have been investigated in 
considerable detail, the available mechanical property 
dataset remains restricted to a single alloy system, 
preventing a comprehensive assessment of the 
structural potential of this pathway.
Pathway III, involving continuous A2→ B2 
ordering, produces either single-phase B2 structures 
or B2 nanodomains. Unlike the other pathways, 
this route promotes a B2 ordering state that offers 
the most promising strategy for overcoming the 
traditional strength–ductility trade-off in RCCAs at 
room temperature, while providing a measurable 
strengthening up to elevated temperatures until 
the diffusion-assisted processes become active. 
The mechanical behaviour of these alloys is highly 
sensitive to the degree of B2 ordering, which can 
be characterised using different, non-equivalent 
descriptors. In single-phase B2 alloys, chemical order 
descriptors such as site occupancy are commonly 
used, whereas in alloys containing B2 nanodomains, 
dimensional descriptors such as domain size and 
volume fraction are also relevant.
Future progress in the field will require advanced 
quantitative measurements of the degree of B2 
ordering, direct determination of APB energies, 
systematic evaluation of mechanical behaviour 
under tension, creep, fatigue, and fracture toughness 
loading. Particular attention should also be given 
to crack growth resistance and in situ deformation 
studies, which are needed to establish the mechanisms 
controlling damage initiation and fracture, and 
to atom-probe and complementary atomic-scale

sublattice occupancy. Improved predictive models 
capable of linking composition, ordering pathway, 
microstructure, and properties will also be required. 
Achieving these objectives will transform B2 ordering 
from a largely descriptive concept into a predictive 
design tool for the development of next-generation 
Al-containing RCCAs intended for demanding 
structural applications.
