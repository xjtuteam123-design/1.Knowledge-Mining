Ni-based superalloys are composed of a matrix with an austenitic face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal structure
(γ phase) which is generally strengthened by the presence of a high fraction of ordered FCC precipitates γ′
(Ni3(Ti,Al) type) and/or γ″ (Ni3Nb type). Plasma assisted nitriding of various Ni-based superalloys, either poly-
crystals (Haynes®230, Inconel®718, Udimet®720Li, N18) or single crystals (MC2 and MC-NG), presenting
from 100 vol.% to 30 vol.% of γ phase, has been performed at moderate temperature (400 °C) to shed light on
the speciﬁc responses of the different (δ, γ′, γ″) precipitates under nitriding. Characterizations by glow discharge
optical emission spectroscopy and scanning electron microscopy have evidenced the formation of nitrided layers
with thicknesses varying from ~1 μmt o~ 8 μm depending on alloys and treatment duration. In all alloys, X-ray
diffraction on nitrided samples con ﬁrms that the γ phase is nitrided, as in austenitic stainless steels, to form
the expanded austenitic FCC phase γN, a nitrogen insertion solid solution, with a mean concentration of
~25 at.% N. CrN nitrides were also identi ﬁed. The incorporation of nitrogen within the precipitates appeared to
depend on the alloy microstructure: for polycrystals, regardless of their proportion, the δ, γ′ or γ″ precipitates
seem nitrided in similar proportion than the matrix γ although the exact nature (nitrides or expanded δ, γ′, γ″
phases) of the obtained nitrided phases was not conﬁrmed. On the contrary, in the single crystal MC2, theγ′ pre-
cipitates accommodate a much lower nitrogen amount, in the range 3–5 at.%. Whatever the materials, the surface
swelling induced by the nitrogen uptake is only relative to the total incorporated nitrogen quantity and is roughly
independent on the nature of the nitrided phases.

1. Introduction
Thanks to their good mechanical properties, nickel-based superal-
loys are widely used in pressurized water heat exchangers or in the
hottest sections of aeroengines or industrial gas turbines, where they
are subjected to high temperature and severe mechanical solicitations
[1,2]. Superalloys typically have a matrix with an austenitic face-
centered cubic (FCC) crystal structure called γ phase [3,4].I na d d i t i o n
to the hardening by substitution solid solution of the γ phase, some
Ni-based superalloys gain their impressive mechanical properties
from the presence of a high fraction of ordered L1 2 face-centered
cubic γ′ (Ni3(Ti,Al) type) and/or tetragonal DO 22 γ″ (Ni3Nb type)
precipitates [1–6]. However, increasing their resistance to very
aggressive conditions (wear, fretting, oxidation, corrosion, higher
temperatures and pressures, higher mechanical solicitations…)i ss t i l l
challenging in order to enhance the life time and to ful ﬁll the
expected future conditions of operations of the next generation gas
turbines. As most of the limitations are coming from the surface
behavior of the material, researches in surface treatments are
worthwhile in conjunction with (bulk) material development.
Nitriding (gas nitriding, plasma nitriding, glow discharge, ion im-
plantation …) improves the mechanical, tribological or corrosion resis-
tances of Ni-based superalloys. When performed at“high” temperature
(N450 °C), the “internal nitriding” leads to more or less ﬁne nitride pre-
cipitates responsible for the strengthening of the nitrided layer [7–17].
Chromium nitrides are mainly formed as Cr is usually the major nitride
forming element in the studied alloys. However, when they are present
in signi ﬁcant amounts, Ti, V or Nb can form nitrides too, which can
provide a higher hardness than CrN [18,19]. The nitrided layer formed
by moderate temperature ( b450 °C) nitriding of Ni-based superalloys
is usually composed of the expanded austenitic face-centered cubic
phase, a nitrogen insertion solid solution called γN or S-phase, like in
austenitic stainless steels (ASS) or CoCr alloys [20].T h i sn i t r i d e dp h a s e
usually shows higher hardness and gets improved tribological proper-
ties, without the loss of corrosion resistance, which can be interesting
for applications keeping the superalloys at moderate temperature
[1,13,15,18,21–27]. The expanded phase is metastable and decomposes
into nitrides at high temperature [20]. The nitriding below 450 °C may
however be an interesting way to introduce nitrogen in the materials,
in order to obtain, after annealing process, speciﬁc structures and prop-
erties that cannot be achieved by direct high temperature nitriding.
Most of the previous studies are relative to solid solution strength-
ened Ni-based materials, i.e. withoutγ′ or γ″ strengthening precipitates

In this work, the nitriding at 400 °C of several Ni-based superalloys
with various compositions and amounts of precipitates was performed;
the study aims merely to determine whether the different precipitates
(i.e. γ′, γ″ or δ) get nitrided as the γ phase does. The in ﬂuence of
these precipitates as well as of the composition of the different superal-
loys on the nitriding efﬁciency is beyond the scope of this work.
Polycrystals and single crystals were used and theγ proportion was
varying from 100 vol.% to 30 vol.%. Thus six superalloys were nitrided at
low temperature to determine the behavior of the different types of
precipitates. In conjunction with usual chemical and microstructural
characterizations, the surface swelling induced by the nitrogen uptake
in the FCC matrix was used as an additional indicator of the nitrogen in-
corporation [29]. After a presentation of the main characteristics of the
Ni-based superalloys in their as-received state (composition and vol-
ume fraction of the different phases), the modi ﬁcations induced by
the nitriding treatment are then given and discussed as a function of
the γ′, γ″ or δ precipitate volume percentage.
2. Experimental details
2.1. Materials
The main characteristics of the different alloys are summarized
in Tables 1, 2 and 3 . Haynes® 230 (Ha230), Inconel® 718 (IN718),
Udimet® 720Li (U720) and N18 are polycrystals while MC2 and MC-
NG are single crystals. Ha230, IN718, and U720 are cast and wrought al-
loys while N18 is a powder metallurgy one. It should be noted from
Table 2 that direct aged IN718 samples have been used in the present
study (i.e. without any solution heat treatment). In the different poly-
crystalline superalloys, the γ phase grains are of few tens or hundreds
of μms i z e .Table 3 shows the typical volume proportion and composi-
tion of each phase gathered from literature. Considering the applied
thermal treatments ( Table 2 ), these data can be considered roughly
valid for the studied samples as only the main differences between γ
phases and ( γ′, γ″ or δ) phases will be discussed in this article. More
precise distinction between the various precipitates in the different ma-
terials would have required additional detailed analysis.
Ha230 can be here considered as a “reference” material because it
consists of 100 vol.% of γ phase, without any precipitate. The (M 6C
(Ni3W3C) and M23C6 (Cr21(W,Mo)2C6 carbides in Ha230 will not be con-
sidered due to their low proportion[30]. In the other materials, the vol-
ume fraction of the γ phase then decreases down to 30 vol.% with few
intermediary points at 75, 60 and 45 vol.%. Most of the chromium in
the different alloys is found in the γ phase, with a concentration higher
than 22 at.%. The γ phase in IN718 contains a signi ﬁcant amount of Fe
whereas theγ phase in U720 and N18 is rich in Co. MC-NG is signiﬁcant-
ly different with only 11 at.% of Cr in theγ phase thanks to the high con-
tent in Re and Ru.
U720 and N18 alloys contain a multimodal γ′ distribution with pri-
mary, secondary and tertiary precipitates (γ′I, γ′II, γ′III respectively) of
various sizes[31,32]. In addition to a small amount ofγ′, IN718 contains
γ″ and δ (the thermodynamically stable Ni3Nb phase, with orthorhombic
structure) precipitates and few primary (Nb,Ti)C and secondary (M23C6)
carbides [33]. Single crystalline MC2 and MC-NG alloys have only one
type of precipitates ( γ′I,) whose size is close to 0.35 –0.5 μm [34,35].
The Cr, Fe and Co concentrations in these γ′, γ″ and δ precipitates do
not exceed 5 at.% whereas they contain almost all the Al, Ti and Nb com-
ponents. It should be noticed that the elemental composition of the γ′
precipitates depends on the investigated materials (see references in
Table 3). In the polycrystals the Al and Ti contents of the γ′ precipitates
are in the range 8–13 at.% and 9 –14 at.%, respectively. For the MC2 and
MC-NG single crystals, the γ′ precipitates have a higher Al content
(15–17 at.%) and lower Ti or Ta content ( b3 at.%). This means that
they are closer to the “perfect” intermetallic Ni
3Al compound.

2.2. Sample preparation and characterizations
Samples were previously polished with increasing grade SiC papers
in order to eliminate the hardened layer resulting from machining.
Polishing was completed using solutions of diamond powder (6 to
0.25 μm) and a colloidal suspension of alumina (~0.1 μm). The samples
were thus degreased and cleaned in an ultrasonic bath of acetone, then
ethanol and ﬁnally dried under nitrogen gas.
The nitriding treatments were performed for 1 h or 4 h at 400 °C in
a home-made R.F. plasma assisted thermal nitriding reactor previously
described in Refs. [41,42].Ag a s ﬂow of 100 sccm consisting of 60 sccm
N2 and 40 sccm H 2 was used to obtain the working pressure regulated
at 8 Pa. During the whole treatment, the samples were maintained
at the ﬂoating potential, avoiding any sputtering by energetic ions.
The heating and cooling processes were performed under vacuum
(10−4 Pa).
The nitrogen up-take and pro ﬁles were determined by Glow Dis-
charge Optical Emission Spectroscopy (GDOES) performed on a
Jobin-Yvon GD-Pro ﬁler™. Observations of the cross-sections of the
samples were realized by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) on
Jeol JSM 7001F-TTLS equipped with a ﬁeld emission gun. The in-
duced modi ﬁcations of the different crystallographic phases were
characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) in Bragg –Brentano geometry
with the Cu Kα line. XRD was performed on a Bruker D8 diffractometer
for the polycrystalline superalloys and on an automated home-made
two circle goniometer with a vertical linear focus in combination with
a ﬂat monochromator for the single crystalline MC2. The surface topog-
raphy was observed by White Light Interferometry (WLI) with a
Talysurf CCL from Hobson Taylor. To determine the speci ﬁce l a s t o -
plastic response of each alloy, a small 50 μm silicon piece was used as
a mask hence avoiding the underneath surface to be nitrided during
the plasma treatment. The swelling was then determined by measuring
the step between the nitrided and non-nitrided zones using WLI as
described in [29].
3. Results
3.1. Nitrogen proﬁles
The nitrogen proﬁles obtained by GDOES in the different Ni-based
superalloys nitrided at 400 °C for 1 h or 4 h are presented in Fig. 1 .
The proﬁle obtained in the austenitic stainless steel (ASS) 316L nitrided
within the same conditions is also included for comparison. Except for
MC-NG, nitrogen has been introduced and a nitrided layer a few micro-
meters thick has been produced. When extending the time of nitriding
from 1 to 4 h, the nitrogen penetration depth is multiplied by ~2 for the
superalloys Ha230, IN718 and MC2, in agreement with a diffusion pro-
cess. Whatever the nitriding time, the surface concentration is in the
range 27–30 at.% for all the superalloys.
The polycrystalline Ha230 exhibits a N pro ﬁle typical of a nitrogen
diffusion mechanism by trapping/detrapping like other kinds of alloys
with a homogeneous fcc structure like the ASS 316/304 or CoCr alloys
[20,43]. The pro ﬁles obtained with the other polycrystals are rather
similar to that of Ha230. Indeed, there is a more or less decreasing
plateau over few microns down to a nitrogen concentration around
20–25 at.%, followed by a sharper decrease.
MC2 and MC-NG single crystals have not the same type of nitrogen
proﬁles: there is a peak of concentration at the surface (~27 –30 at.%)
which extends on about 0.2 μm regardless of the nitriding duration.
Then, in the case of MC2, there is a rapid drop followed by a plateau
(at around 10 –12 at.%) and then a sharp decrease. This shape below
the surface nitrogen peak resembles to the one observed in polycrystals,
although the nitrided depth is signiﬁcantly lower (3–5 μmi n s t e a do f5–
7 μm for 4 h treatments). MC2 with b001N surface orientation is nitrid-
ed over a slightly deeper thickness compared to MC2 withb011N surface
orientation. For MC-NG, the N concentration and the N penetration
depth appear considerably lower than in MC2.

3.3. X-ray diffraction
The θ–2θ diffractograms obtained after nitriding for 4 h at 400 °C are
presented in Fig. 3. For all studied polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys
(Fig. 3a), XRD performed on the non-nitrided samples in the interval
35–55° (only IN718 is presented) gives two very narrow peaks of the
fcc γ matrix, at 43.6° (111 reﬂection) and 50.8° (002 reﬂection), corre-
sponding to a lattice parameter of about 0.360 nm. The phases γ′ and
γ″ cannot be distinguished from the γ phase by XRD because they
have a lattice parameter very close to that of γ phase. For Ha230 or
IN718, additional very narrow and weak peaks corresponding to car-
bides and/or δ phase [33,44,45] are observed at 2 θ = 40.0°, 41.7° and
42.5°. In the case of virgin single crystal MC2 (insert of Fig. 3 b), the
peak obtained around 50.8° is composed of two contributions: a main
one at 50.9° is corresponding to the γ′ (002) peak whereas a second
one at 50.8° is related to the γ (002) peak [28].
After 4 h of nitriding, the initial peaks are always detected but with a
signiﬁcantly reduced intensity. Considering that the main part of the
signal is coming from the nitrided layer and the high volume fraction
of γ′ precipitates, like in U720 for instance, this reduction is an indica-
tion of a modiﬁcation of the γ′ precipitates structure, likely induced by
nitrogen incorporation. Otherwise unmodi ﬁed γ′ precipitates would
give rise to a strong contribution at the initial position. In addition to
the initial contributions, more or less asymmetric and broad peaks are
observed around 39° and 43 –46°. By comparison with nitrided ASS
[46], they can be attributed to the (111) and (002) peaks of an“expand-
ed FCC phase”, with a gradient of lattice parameter related to the nitro-
gen concentration gradient. This “expanded phase ” may naturally
correspond to the expansion of the γ phase due to nitrogen incorpora-
tion, i.e. the γ
N phase. However, ifγ′ and γ″ precipitates incorporate ni-
trogen and are subjected to an expansion of the cell similar to the one of
γ phase, there could be an additional contribution of an expandedγ′N or
γ″N phases at the position of the detected peak. For the sake of simplic-
ity, the term “expanded phase” will then be used to gather all the
possibilities.
The diffractograms wereﬁtted with a pseudo-Voigt function in order
to determine the corresponding inter-reticular distances.Table 5 gives
the deduced“effective” lattice parameter. Its dependence on the consid-
ered peak is related to fact that the expanded phase can be considered
as a faulted and deformed fcc phase[29,46]. Compared to the lattice pa-
rameter of the γ phase (0.360 nm), they correspond to a mean lattice
expansion ranging from 9 to 12%. Similar relative changes in lattice
parameter have been reported for nitrided Inconel 690 or IN718
[13,24]. The weak and broad peak around 37.6° likely corresponds to
the (111) contribution of chromium nitride CrN which, in addition
with other nitrides like AlN, TiN and Cr2N, may also contribute to the de-
tected intensity around 43.5°–44°. In the case of nitrided MC2, the same
peaks can be observed with an additional peak at 50.20° which was
attributed to a nitrided γ′ phase with very low N content [28].
It can be deduced from the XRD observations that nitrogen can be in-
corporated either in interstitial in the γ matrix or in the precipitates to
form “expanded” nitrided phases ( γN, γ′N …) or nitrides. Therefore,
this might result in differences in the occupancy density and then in
the swelling of the surface.

4. Discussion
The experimental observations con ﬁrm that the plasma assisted
treatment at 400 °C leads to nitrogen incorporation in Ni-based superal-
loys. Depending on the material, it produces in various volume propor-
tions a nitrided “expanded phase”, some nitride precipitates and the
swelling of the surface. All polycrystalline superalloys exhibit a similar

4.1. Nitrogen incorporation in polycrystalline Ni-based superalloys Ha230,
IN718, U720 and N18
To determine the behavior of the γ′, γ″ and δ precipitates and their
inﬂuence on the nitriding ef ﬁciency, Fig. 4 draws the average nitrogen
concentration CN taken in the nitrided layer that corresponds to the pla-
teau in nitrogen proﬁles versus the volume proportion of theγ phase for
the different alloys.
Fig. 4 shows that C N is almost the same, around 25 at.%, for all in-
vestigated polycrystals, whether they contain precipitates (N18,
IN718, U720) or not (Ha230). Although C N in the N18 sample is
slightly lower (21 at.%), it could be considered that it behaves almost
like IN718 and U720 for its nitriding incorporation There is no obvi-
ous relationship between the nitrogen content and the volume frac-
tion of γ phase for the polycrystals. It suggests that nitrogen might be
also incorporated in the precipitates. The ﬁrst clue about nitrogen in-
corporation in the precipitates is given by the SEM photographs of
the cross-sections in Fig. 2 . Nitrogen incorporation has induced a
modiﬁcation of contrast in the γ phase which gets darker, enabling
to de ﬁne the nitrided zone. A similar modi ﬁcations of contrast can
also be observed in the δ and γ′I precipitates in the nitrided zone
compared to the ones in the bulk. This is particularly visible in the
photographs showing large δ and γ′I precipitates situated at the ni-
trided zone/bulk interface ( Fig. 2 b, c, d). Although the contrast may
be modi ﬁed due to other reasons, this can be considered as an indi-
cation of the presence of nitrogen in these precipitates. However it
does not give any information about the introduced nitrogen con-
centration nor the obtained phases.
The hypothesis of an “expanded phase ” richer in nitrogen than
Ha230 and no nitrogen in the precipitates can be rejected because,
from Table 5, the different alloys have an “expanded phase” with a lat-
tice parameter similar to the one found in Ha230. The colossal expan-
sion (9–12%) of the γ phase parameter needed to form γN is related to
the nitrogen content. The positions of the maximum of the XRD peaks
are mainly determined by the N concentration close to the surface, in
a layer of ~500 nm. Table 5 indicates the average nitrogen content with-
in this depth, which is around 25–30 at.% whatever the polycrystal. The
γ phase response to such nitrogen incorporation is given by Ha230
(100 vol.% of γ), with a(002) = 0.399 nm for 27.3 at.% of nitrogen. In
the other polycrystals, if nitrogen is not incorporated in the γ′, δ nor
γ″ precipitates, the resulting concentration in theγ phase would neces-
sarily be as high as 35 –48 at.%. Such very high values were never
observed before. Moreover the FCCγ cell would have almost all its octa-
hedral sites occupied and this would induce a much more pronounced
expansion of the parameter than the one observed (see for instance
the linear relationship between the nitrogen content and theγN param-
eter proposed by Christiansen et al. [48]).

There is no clear XRD identiﬁcation of the phases produced by the ni-
trogen incorporation in the precipitates of polycrystalline superalloys:
nitrides (TiN, AlN, NbN) or “expanded” nitrided γ′N, δN or γ″N phases?
Some of the nitrides (AlN, TiN) would contribute to XRD peak intensity
around 43°–44° but the low global content in Al, Ti or Nb makes difﬁcult
to distinguish these phases which are moreover likely at the nanoscale.
An “expanded” nitrided phase like γ′N or γ″N would probably lead to
XRD peaks more or less at similar positions than theγN ones. So only lo-
calized study (e.g. by SAED in TEM) of the crystal structure of precipi-
tates in the nitrided zone would help to determine the exact nature of
the phases obtained by the nitrogen incorporation in the precipitates.
If the precipitates accommodate the nitrogen atoms similarly to the
γ matrix, is there response also similar in terms of expansion? Fig. 5
shows that, whatever the material and within the accuracy of the mea-
surements, the step height follows a proportional relationship with the
total amount of the introduced nitrogen calculated from the GDOES pro-
ﬁles. It means that, for a certain amount of nitrogen introduced into the
material, the step height is almost independent: 1) of the nature and
proportions of the initially present phases 2) of the nitrogen distribution
and the phases produced by nitriding. This suggests a similar elasto-
plastic response of the different phases γ, γ′, γ″,a n d δ to the introduc-
tion of nitrogen. Therefore, as the nitrogen level is moreover similar in
all the polycrystals, the swelling is then almost constant, in the range
[20%–25%].

4.3. Efﬁciency of nitriding of γ′ precipitates in polycrystals and in single
crystals
During plasma nitriding at 400 °C, theγ′ structural particles in poly-
crystalline superalloys U720 and N18 are nitrided similarly to the sur-
rounding γ matrix, whereas a very low nitrogen content (2 –6a t . % )i s
incorporated in the γ′ phase of single crystal superalloys. Although the
exact repartition of nitrogen has to be quantiﬁed in the different γ′ pre-
cipitates (γ′I, γ′II, γ′III) of the polycrystals, such a difference with the γ′
phase of the single crystal is not straightforward. No evident relation
with the γ′ precipitate size exists: the γ′II are nitrided in U720 or N18

nitrided γ′ precipitates in the single crystals. Moreover the γ′I precipi-

One explanation may lie in the elemental composition of theγ′ pre-
cipitates. In polycrystals, γ′ precipitates are Ni3(Al,Ti), with equivalent
Al and Ti contents (8 –14 at.%). In single crystals, the γ′ precipitates
have a higher Al content and lower Ti content; moreover they possess
an atomic ordering of higher quality than theγ′ precipitates in polycrys-
tals [50–54]. Consequently, they are much more similar to the“perfect”
intermetallic compound Ni3Al which cannot be nitrided [28,49] within
the used conditions. Without an accurate determination of the compo-
sition of each phase of the investigated materials, it is however difﬁcult
to say unambiguously that it is the main reason.
Several other parameters might play a signi ﬁcant role as the nitro-
gen to be incorporated in theγ′ precipitates has to come from the nitrid-
ed γ phase and to go through the γ/γ′ interface: the shape of the γ′
precipitates (cuboïdal, butterﬂy, …), modifying the quality of the γ/γ′
interface (γ/γ′ lattice mismatch, defect density) or its crystallographic
orientation; the γ proportion, conditioning the γ channel width and
the available nitrogen chemical potential; the inﬂuence of the internal
stress induced by theγ nitriding (possible modiﬁcations of the diffusion
coefﬁcients under an evolving mechanical stress state),… So, additional
localized characterizations are then needed to discriminate the different
hypotheses.
