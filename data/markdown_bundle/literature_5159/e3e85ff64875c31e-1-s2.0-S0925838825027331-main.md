ABSTRACT
Metastable β Ti alloys based on Ti-Nb possess good biocompatibility and a low elastic modulus, which makes 
them candidate materials for implant devices within the biomedical industry. However, these alloys typically 
suffer from inferior mechanical strengths compared to commercial alternatives. One method shown effective at 
improving the strength of Ti-Nb alloys is the formation of precipitate species that pin and refine the grain size. 
However, currently little is known about the phase equilibria surrounding such compositions and the effect of 
this strengthening mechanism on the elastic properties of the alloy. As such, this study considers a series of Ti-Nb- 
Sn alloys to indicate the phase boundaries of the Ti
3
Sn intermetallic phase and considers the effect of this 
intermetallic precipitate on the mechanical response. The strengths of the precipitate forming samples were 
shown to be highly correlated with precipitate fraction, however, some suffered from incomplete recrystallisa -
tion and abnormal grain growth which significantly limited the absolute strengths that could be achieved. As 
such, potential routes forward for the development of these alloys have been discussed.
Metastable β -Ti alloys have attracted large scale industrial attention 
across multiple sectors due to their combination of desirable properties, 
which includes a good biocompatibility and a low elastic modulus [1,2] . 
Of the metastable β Ti alloys, those based on Ti-Nb have received 
particular attention and have been considered as candidate materials for 
producing biomimetic implants devices. However, industrial uptake of 
these alloys is limited by an intrinsically low yield stress (~ 400 MPa) 
compared to ordered systems such as NiTi [3] . As such, engineering 
biocompatible alloys with a higher yield strength is critical for these 
systems to gain widespread commercial viability.
The low modulus of Ti-Nb based alloys is often correlated with a low 
β phase stability [4] . However, this makes these alloys susceptible to 
shear transformations to phases which consequently raise the modulus 
value. For example, if the β phase stability is too low, the alloy is sus -
ceptible to forming the orthorhombic α ″ martensite on cooling [5] . The 
α ″ formed on cooling often forms a self-accommodating twinned 
morphology, to maintain a macroscopically invariant plane consistent 
with the phenomenological theory of martensitic transformations 
(PTMT) [6,7] . However, subsequently loading an alloy in this condition 
above a critical stress can result in detwinning of the martensite variants 
to accommodate the new macroscopic shape, which results in perma -
nent plastic deformation of the material that can only be recovered by 
heating the sample to the β phase [8] .
Therefore, the lowest modulus structures exist for compositions that 
have the lowest β phase stability before the onset of α ″ on cooling [4,9, 
10] . Many of these alloys undergo a hysteretic stress driven trans -
formation from the β phase to α ″ [11] . This transformation is accom -
panied by a lower effective modulus of the alloy, however, is still often 
accompanied by an unacceptable accumulation of residual plastic strain.
An additional transformation pathway in low stability alloys is the 
formation of the ⍵ phase [12,13] . This phase has been shown to 
significantly stiffen and embrittle the alloy and as such is considered 
deleterious to biomedical implant alloys [14] . Because of this, research 
has focussed on suppressing the formation of the ⍵ phase, which is 
commonly achieved by alloying with Sn [15,16] . This has allowed lower 
modulus values to be accessed, however, many of these alloys still suffer 
from unacceptably low strengths on the order of ~400 MPa [1,17,18] .
Recently, work on the Ti-Nb-Sn system identified the formation of 
Ti
3
Sn precipitates during recrystallisation heat treatment, which pinned 
the grain boundaries resulting in a refined grain size on the order of 
microns [19] . This resulted in an ~ 1 GPa yield strength; far higher than 
the other Ti-Nb-Sn alloys studied. Furthermore, the alloy remained cold 
processable as the precipitates were not present in the as-cast material, 
forming only during the recrystallisation heat treatment of 30 min. After

or hot deformation steps, which introduce a higher density of nucleation 
sites. This is thought to enable rapid precipitation at low temperatures, 
with precipitation primarily occurring on the moving sub-grain 
boundaries of the recrystallising grains [24] . Similar observations 
have linked this effect to the pinning of sub-grain boundaries during the 
recrystallisation process [25] . As such, alloys exhibiting precipitation 
concurrent to recovery and recrystallisation often display slow recrys -
tallisation kinetics [26,27] , which in some cases can be suppressed 
entirely [28] .
After the 500 hour heat treatment, 20Nb-8Sn and 24Nb-8Sn con -
tained sub-grain boundaries, however, 12Nb-8Sn no longer contained 
any appreciable fraction. This is despite a comparable volume fraction of 
precipitates to 20Nb-8Sn. Despite this, one notable difference in the 
precipitates of 12Nb-8Sn is the elongated morphology. The size and 
shape of second phase particles has been suggested to affect the pinning 
pressure they exert on grain boundaries. For example, higher aspect 
ratio precipitates have been shown to increase the frictional force by 
encouraging grain boundaries to align with the long axis [29] . This is 
thought to be related to an increase in the total precipitate surface area, 
even when volume fraction remains constant. As such, the coarsening of 
precipitates has been shown to have the opposite effect and result in a 
decrease in the pinning of grain boundaries, as the precipitate surface 
area tends towards a minimum [30] . Therefore, the reduced Nb content 
may be influencing the coarsening rate of the Ti
3
Sn precipitates. 
Coarsening requires Sn diffusion through the matrix from smaller pre -
cipitates to larger ones. However, this will necessarily require some 
movement of the Nb atoms in solid solution, with Nb known to have a 
slower rate of diffusion than Sn in β Ti [31] .
In 12Nb-8Sn and 20Nb-8Sn there is evidence of abnormal grain 
growth in the 30 min condition, and for the latter also after 500 hours. 
Abnormal grain growth has been linked to precipitate dissolution, either 
as a consequence of heating up to the solutioning temperature or as a 
result of precipitate coarsening [32] . This allows some grains to break 
free of their pinning environments and grow at a much faster rate than 
their neighbours. However, in 16Nb-8Sn and 24Nb-8Sn, grain growth

The decrease in mechanical strength across the 4Sn alloy series is 
unexpected given the increase in Nb content. However, the minimal 
effect of Nb on the lattice parameter of the β phase suggests it is not a 
significant solid solution strengthening element [35] . Instead, the 
greater strength for lower Nb content alloys may be related to their 
ability to transform to the α ″ martensite, which has previously been 
shown to increase strength due to the dynamic Hall-Petch effect [10] , 
similar to the increase in strength achieved through mechanical twin -
ning [36] .

The modulus of the alloys increased with both Sn content and Nb 
content. The modulus of metastable β Ti alloys is thought to be strongly 
related to the β phase stability with respect to metastable phases such as 
α ″ martensite or ⍵ [9,10] . With respect to these two phases, both Sn and 
Nb are β stabilisers which would rationalise their effect on modulus. 
Nevertheless, all of the studied alloys had modulus values significantly 
lower than commercial biomedical alloys such as commercially pure Ti 
(E~105 GPa) [37] , Ti-6Al-4V (E~110 GPa) [38] and 316 L 
(E~200 GPa) [39] .
As such, there may be a necessary trade-off between modulus and 
strength. Despite this, if recovery and recrystallisation could be 
increased to reduce the texture and remove the low angle sub-grain 
boundaries, as is evidently possible in 12Nb-8Sn, the strength could be 
increased in a low modulus alloy whilst retaining a refined, stable 
microstructure. To ensure commercially viable heat treatments, this 
would necessitate a modified alloy composition.
The phase diagram overlayed with the microstructural information 
from the present study highlights a large two-phase region at 900 ˚ C. The 
 8Sn alloys lie near the end of the tie line, close to the single phase β 
region, consistent with the low volume fraction of Ti
3
Sn precipitates. 
However, this volume fraction of precipitates seems to already be 
impacting the ductility of the alloys, and as such it is unlikely that any 
higher volume fractions could be tolerated.
Instead, it is likely that the most appropriate routes forward for 
developing low modulus precipitate strengthened alloys is to consider 
other intermetallic species, or to adjust the precipitation temperature of 
the lower Ti
3
Sn volume fraction alloys. This would be expected to in -
crease the volume fraction of precipitates to more comparable levels to 
24Nb-8Sn and concomitantly deplete the matrix in Sn, which is a potent 
β stabilising element. However, any precipitate would also need to 
possess a suitably low modulus and this would need to be achieved 
whilst avoiding α phase precipitation or significantly impacting the alloy 
ductility.
4. Conclusions
A series of Ti-Nb-Sn alloys were investigated to explore the compo -
sitional space for which grain boundary pinning intermetallic pre -
cipitates could be produced. Alloys containing 8Sn were found to 
contain Ti
3
Sn intermetallic precipitates which were effective at refining 
the grain size after 30 min and 500 hour heat treatments, even when 
present in very low volume fractions.
However, the Ti
3
Sn precipitates were also shown to significantly 
hinder recovery and recrystallisation resulting in highly textured sam -
ples with large fractions of sub-grain boundaries. Furthermore, grain 
growth was abnormal in the 8Sn alloys in all but the highest precipi -
tate containing alloy. These combined effects were shown to limit the 
effectiveness of the Hall-Petch strengthening, with these samples 
conferring limited strength advantage over the large-grained 4Sn 
alloys.
Finally, the increase in solute content was shown to be at a 
compromise to the modulus, which rose as the β phase stability was 
increased. As such, in order to produce high strength and low modulus 
alloys, the precipitate volume fraction must be increased for lower so -
lute content alloys, without compromising alloy ductility.
