Keywords: Alloy 104, microstructure, aged, annealed, LCF, HCF. 
 
Abstract “Alloy 104” is a novel high strength, α+β titanium alloy primarily aimed at aero-engine 
fan disc applications. Two microstructural variants of Alloy 104 have been assessed. Room 
temperature tensile strength and elongation have been investigated alongside a more detaile d study 
of low and high cycle fatigue behaviour . The alloy clearly demonstrated an improved fatigue 
resistance in both microstructural conditions, whilst maintaining forg eability and a comparable 
density to Ti-6Al-4V. Furthermore, the alloy has been subjected to a load regime with a hold period 
at peak loads and proven to be insensitive to dwell fatigue. 
Introduction 
Since the mid twentieth century, titanium has seen increasing use as a structural metal for 
aerospace applications [1]. For the gas turbine, α+β and near α titanium alloys have seen extensive 
use within the fan and compressor stages primarily due to their excellent strength to weight 
characteristics, corrosion resistance and fatigue strength at temperatures up to approximately 600°C 
(dependent on composition). In addition, these variants are readily machined, wrought and forged 
[2]. Titanium is the second most common material within the gas turbine behind nickel superalloys, 
and accounts for 1/3 rd of the structural weight of the entire engine [3]. For fan disc applications, 
alloys such as TIMETAL 550, TIMETAL 6246, Ti -17, an d particularly Ti -6Al-4V have seen 
extensive use in many civil and military aircraft [3]. 
A continuous drive exists for improvements in engine emi ssions, with recent targets set by 
bodies such as ACARE [4]. To address such requirements , designers have moved towards larger, 
more fuel efficient engines to power long range and extra wide body aircraft. This raises a demand 
for the development of novel alloys with increased static and fatigue strength, potentially leading to 
a reduction in fan disc cross sectional area and subsequent loss of weight. 
TIMET’s developmental Alloy 104 is a high strength α+β titanium alloy with a c omparable 
density to Ti -6Al-4V and a reduced β transus temperature. Recent research has focussed on 
microstructure evolution as a means to optimising mechanical behaviour. In this respect, previous 
studies on existing α+β alloys such as Ti -6Al-4V have prov en invaluable [5,6]. During early 
investigations on Alloy 104, it was deduced that a solution heat treatment (SHT) followed by ageing 
could enhance the strength of the material, notably without any significant loss in ductility. After 
comprehensive heat treatment trials, both “aged” and “annealed” variants were identified to offer a 
desirable combination of static strength and ductility. The findings of a detailed fatigue assessment 
are reported here, including LCF, HCF and dwell fatigue test ing. Post-test metallography and 
fracture surface analysis are presented to understand the role of key microstructural constituents in 
controlling the strength and failure mechanisms of the alloy.

Experimental Methods 
Material and Specimen Preparation An ingot of Alloy 104 was manufactured at TIMET UK. 
A proprietary sequence of β break down, hot chopping , re -heating and forging resulted in three 
250mm diameter billets. For this study, two 76mm thick slices were cut transversely from one billet, 
sectioned longitudinally in 60° increments from the centre. These 60° “cheeses” were then heated to 
transus -50°C for 2 hours before a 3:1 forging reduction. The subsequent “pancakes”, designed to 
mimic a typical fan disc forging, were then assigned two separate heat treatments, namely “aged” 
and “annealed” described in Table 1.

Annealed Transus -50°C/2hr FAC, + Anneal 700°C/2hr AC

Mechanical Tests and Insp ection Servo hydraulic and servo electrical test machines were 
employed for the tensile, LCF and dwell LCF tests. Dual rate tensile tests were performed at room 
temperature and 300oC to BS EN 2002-1:2005 and BS EN 2002-1:2005 respectively [7,8]. A dual 
averaging extensometer cage was connected directly to ridges machined at the extremities of the 
specimen gauge section. 
Room temperature, constant amplitude load control LCF tests under a load ratio of R=0 were 
conducted to BS3518-1 1993 [10], employing a 15 cycle per minute trapezoidal waveform (one 
second linear rise and fall ramps, one second hold at both peak and minimum load ). Dwell LCF 
tests were essentially performed to the same standard, however, these incorporated a 120 second 
hold at peak load. An Amsler vibrophore was used for HCF testing resonating at R=0 and frequency 
100Hz. Complete specimen rupture was the criterion for fatigue failure. 
Microstructures were characterised by preparing metallographic sections from the threaded ends 
of post tested specimens on a longitudinal plane . Standard polishing and etching techniques were 
applied. Microstructures were imaged using a Hitachi S4800 ultra high resolution SEM and a Jeol 
JSM 6100 SEM . Fatigue fractures were imaged using both a Keyence microscope and again a Jeol 
JSM 6100 SEM.

Fig. 2 clearly shows a marked strength difference between the aged and annealed variant at both 
room and elevated temperature, whilst any scatter in elongation appears relatively consistent across 
temperature and variant . In all cases, the strength of Alloy 104 exceeds that of Ti -6Al-4V under 
equivalent conditions. Referring to comparable Ti-6Al-4V data from room temperature testing [11], 
the 0.2 proof stress and UTS for Alloy 104 are improved by 10.8% and 10.5% respectively, with a 
minor loss in RinA of 2.4% when considering in the aged condition. 
Fatigue tests Fatigue data from the combined LCF, HCF and dwell fatigue tests are presented in 
Fig. 3. At relatively high peak stress levels, the aged Alloy 104 variant appears to offer a marginally 
greater strength over the annealed, however, with an increased dataset any difference may be within 
scatter. Below approximately 950MPa both variants offer a markedly similar response, even 
extending into the HCF regim e. Specimens that exceeded 50 million cycles without fracturing were 
considered as “run outs”, marked accordingly by an arrow in Fig. 3. Comparison against Ti -6Al-4V 
LCF data [12] superimposed on Fig. 3 demonstrates that both Alloy 104 variants show a superior 
response at comparable stresses. In terms of dwell sensitivity, alloys susceptible to this effect 
typically demonstrate clear reductions in fatigue life for applied stress conditions below the cyclic 
yield strength of the alloy, a notable example bein g Ti 834 [12]. Therefore, the current data 
suggests that Alloy 104 is insensitive to dwell, similar to Ti-6Al-4V, where any reduction in fatigue 
lives under dwell compared to cyc lic waveforms only occurs at extremely high levels of applied 
stress, approaching 95% of the static strength and above. 
At an applied peak stress level of 900MPa the trends in the separate SN curves for the LCF and 
HCF data appear to indicate a difference in fatigue strength with test frequency (15cpm compared to 
100Hz). A similar effect was previously noted for the alloy Ti 834 [13] where it was argued that the 
“cyclic” 15cpm waveform actually incorporates a short “dwell” period of 1 second at peak load. In 
that sense, the resonant performance at 100Hz may be considered the true fatigue response. 
Regardless, a HCF test performed at the relatively high peak stress of 950MPa has provided a very 
similar life to the equivalent test performed at 15cpm. Further tests are planned at 15cpm towards 
lower applied peak stress levels to explore the overlap in this stress/life regime.

As well as the observable microstructural differences, it may be possible that finer 
microstructural constituents exist within the aged microstructure. The composition of Alloy 104 was 
incorporated into a PANDAT morphology model [15]. A phase fraction of DO19 α2 precipitates of 
around 6.5% at 500°C was computed, with no α2 precipitates at 700°C. The presence of such 
precipitates within the aged variant would inevitably enhance the strength. Further SEM/TEM 
investigations are required to resolve this proposal. 
Although the HCF data from Fig. 3 indicate identical fatigue strength between the two variants, 
as the applied peak stress for LCF tests is raised a tendency for the annealed variant to offer a 
weaker cyclic response is noted. Although the effect is minor, this is consistent with the differences 
already discussed in static strength. Under HCF loading, it has been reported that fatigue cracks are 
preferentially initiated in primary grains within forged microstructures [16], such evidence has 
been documented in this study and can be seen in Fig. 5. I n this respect it may be pertinent that the 
two variants offer very similar primary grain sizes, shape and distributions.

In the LCF regime, initiation in the current specimens was dominated by surface sites, for 
example see Fig. 6 . Distinct fatigue crack arcs wer e defined prior to failure by tensile overload. 
Within these regions of sub -critical crack growth a transgranular mode of cracking dominated. 
Invariably, the size of the fatigue crack in the aged variant exceeded that measured in the annealed, 
when tested at identical magnitudes of applied peak stress. This infers that the aged variant may also 
offer superior fracture toughness. A detailed study of fatigue crack propagation rates in the two 
variants is planned to provide a damage toleran ce assessment. It is intended that these specimens 
will be closely monitored during the test, by in situ optical observ ations and post test SEM 
inspection, to identify any potential differences in the interaction of the crack tip with the very fine 
scale structural phases of the opposing variants.

Conclusions 
The current research has raised the following high level conclusions: 
 
 Both the monotonic and fatigue strength of Alloy 104 are superior to Ti-6Al-4V, whilst 
maintaining comparable ductility and density. 
 Alloy 104 is not susceptible to “cold dwell” fatigue effects. 
 Fine scale, tertiary α laths exist in the transformed β phase of the aged variant. A tri-modal 
or triplex microstructure may be responsible for improving fatigue and static strength.
