The spheroidization of the Widmanstätten structure through thermo-mechanical processes, leading to 
the formation of ﬁne recrystallized and sub-grain structures, is crucial for achieving a balance between 
strength and plasticity. This study systematically examined the spheroidization mechanism of the Wid- 
manstätten structure in Ti-25Zr-4Al-1.5Mn (wt.%, TiZrAlMn) alloy under varying rolling temperatures and 
its inﬂuence on microstructure and mechanical properties. After rolling at 900 °C, the specimen exhib- 
ited a mixed morphology of Widmanstätten and Basket-weave structures, with a high yield strength of 
approximately 1038 MPa but low plasticity ( ∼5.2%). While the rolling temperature was reduced to 850 
°C, the specimen exhibited reﬁned prior- β grains, discontinuous grain boundaries and a small amount 
of equiaxed α grains, which collectively enhanced plasticity ( ∼12.4%) while preserving yield strength. 
As the rolling temperature further decreased, the dynamic recrystallization mechanism shifted from the 
discontinuous dynamic recrystallization (DDRX) to continuous dynamic recrystallization (CDRX). Speci- 
mens rolled at 800 °C and 750 °C showed excellent strength-plasticity synergy, with yield strengths of 
1070 MPa and 1110 MPa, respectively, and total elongations of 15% and 18%, respectively. The enhanced 
yield strength is attributed to both ﬁne-grain and sub-grain strengthening. Furthermore, the lower de- 
gree of recrystallization in the 750-AC specimen preserved a relatively high dislocation density, offering 
additional strengthening. The favorable plasticity results from a combination of equiaxed α grains, “soft”
barrier sub-grains, and a small number of twins. Additionally, the 750-AC specimen retained 6.4% of the 
ﬁne β grains and the weak basal texture. These characteristics contribute to the enhanced plasticity. 
Therefore, 750 °C is the optimal rolling temperature for achieving the best strength-plasticity synergy 
in the hot-rolled TiZrAlMn alloy. These ﬁndings demonstrate that selecting the appropriate temperature 
during thermomechanical processing to optimize recrystallized grains and sub-grain content ensures ex- 
cellent plasticity at high yield strength. This offers valuable guidance for developing near- α Ti alloys with 
superior mechanical properties.

1. Introduction 
Near- α titanium alloys have garnered signiﬁcant attention in 
the aerospace industry due to their outstanding properties, such 
as high speciﬁc strength, good weldability, a wide service temper- 
ature range, excellent corrosion resistance and fatigue resistance 
[ 1–4 ]. To produce aerospace structural components with superior 
mechanical properties, speciﬁcally strength-elongation synergy and 
optimizing the microstructure and phase distribution is crucial 
[ 5–7 ]. Alloys are typically prone to forming coarse lamellar struc-

transition from liquid to solid state in melt preparation and ho-

rium phase transitions, where lamellar structures with up to 12 
orientations form during the body-centered cubic (bcc) β to close- 
packed hexagonal (hcp) α phase transition [ 11 , 12 ]. The microstruc- 
ture exhibits a moderate yield strength of approximately 850 MPa 
but poor plasticity, with values below 6% [ 13 ]. The signiﬁcant im- 
balance between strength and plasticity severely restricts engineer- 
ing applications. Therefore, developing Ti alloys with high yield 
strength (above 1050 MPa) and improved plasticity (10%–15%) to 
expand aerospace applications has become a top priority for re-

The poor plasticity of the Widmanstätten structure is attributed 
to several factors. First, its coarse original β grain boundaries 
hinder uniform deformation during plastic deformation, leading 
to localized necking [ 14 ]. Second, the lamellar α phase colonies 
strongly impede dislocation motion, limiting dislocation slip during 
tensile loading [ 15 ]. Lastly, the continuous grain boundaries pro- 
mote the enrichment of α-stabilizing elements (e.g., Al) in these 
regions, while the failure of the β phase to form a secondary α
phase results in a soft precipitation-free zone (PFZ) [ 16 ]. The sig- 
niﬁcant stress difference between the soft PFZ and the stronger 
lamellar α phase leads to localized stress concentrations, resulting 
in early crack nucleation in the alloy. Therefore, it is essential to 
effectively reﬁne this microstructure to achieve a balance between 
strength and plasticity. 
Researchers have found that spheroidizing the lamellar struc- 
ture may help optimize both the microstructure and mechani- 
cal properties [ 8 , 15 , 17 ]. The spheroidization process includes two 
forms: grain boundary α phase spheroidization and grain α phase 
spheroidization [ 18 , 19 ]. The spheroidization process introduces nu- 
merous dislocations through deformation, promoting the recrys- 
tallization of the lamellar α phase to obtain a uniform and ﬁne 
equiaxed α phase. The deformation process is typically classi- 
ﬁed into hot deformation and cold deformation [ 20–23 ]. In the 
case of hcp-dominated near- α Ti alloys, the limited slip sys- 
tem makes grain reﬁnement through cold deformation challeng- 
ing, and recrystallization annealing after cold rolling adds com- 
plexity to the process. Therefore, achieving excellent mechanical 
properties may be more feasible through thermomechanical pro- 
cessing (TMP). Zherebtsov et al. [ 24 ] have demonstrated different 
mechanisms of spheroidization of the lamellar structure by differ- 
ent rolling temperatures in warm deformation and annealing of 
Ti-6Al-4V alloys. At 800 °C, the spheroidization mechanism was 
associated with boundary splitting, followed by termination mi- 
gration to give spheroidized α grains. However, at 600 °C, the 
main mechanism was associated with the CDRX, with only lim- 
ited dynamic or static spheroidization. Roy et al. [ 25 ] have re- 
ported the orientation-dependent spheroidization response and the 
formation of the macro-zone of lamellar α phase in hot rolled 
state Ti-6Al-4V specimens at different annealing times. It was ob- 
served that the lamellar α phase exhibits two different behaviors, 
which were the equiaxed α-phase formed during spheroidization 
and the preservation of the original lamellar α phase. The effect 
on static spheroidization was investigated from multiple perspec- 
tives in terms of interfacial formation, interfacial energy and mul- 
tiple slip systems [ 25 ]. Xu et al. [ 8 ] investigated the spheroidiza- 
tion of the Widmanstätten structure in near- α Ti alloys through 
a two-stage thermomechanical process. They found that geomet- 
ric dynamic recrystallization (GDRX) transformed the lamellar α
phase into a ﬁne-grained structure, which signiﬁcantly enhanced 
the alloy’s plasticity. Liu et al. [ 6 ] reported that in Ti-6Al-4V alloys, 
continuous α grain boundaries formed in low-temperature region, 
and discontinuous α grain boundaries formed in high-temperature 
region of the α+ β phase transition. They conﬁrmed that discon- 
tinuous α grain boundaries can effectively reduce anisotropy, lead- 
ing to improved plasticity. Ma et al. [ 26 ] reported that adjust- 
ing the hot rolling temperature and strain rate induced dynamic 
recrystallization in near- α titanium alloys, which reduced dislo- 
cation density and enhanced alloy plasticity. The aforementioned 
studies aimed to achieve excellent plasticity by spheroidizing the 
lamellar α phase into fully recrystallized grains through the inﬂu- 
ence of high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs). However, the role of 
low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) and sub-grains in alloys has 
received less attention. Secondly, dynamic recrystallization during 
spheroidization leads to a large number of dislocations rearrange- 
ment and elimination. The large reduction in dislocation density 
causes dynamic softening, which in turn limits the yield strength 
increase [ 27 ]. To produce Ti alloys with both high yield strength 
and high plasticity, a thermomechanical processing (TMP) method 
with precise temperature control is necessary to regulate the ex- 
tent of dynamic recovery and recrystallization. Further control of 
sub-grain size and recrystallized grain content is crucial for achiev- 
ing a balance between strength and plasticity. 
In this work, the optimal rolling temperatures for achieving an 
excellent strength-plasticity balance during the spheroidization of 
Widmanstätten structures in near- α Ti alloys were explored by se- 
lecting hot rolling temperatures in full solid solution zone (900 °C, 
850 °C, 800 °C, and 750 °C) followed by air cooling. The inﬂu- 
ence of microstructural evolution on mechanical properties during 
spheroidization was systematically studied. Additionally, the mech- 
anisms of dynamic recrystallization and crack initiation were dis- 
cussed. 
2. Materials and methods 
TiZrAlMn alloy ingots were produced by melting in a vacuum 
non-consumable electric arc furnace. Titanium, acting as an oxygen 
absorber, effectively prevented oxidation. The ingots were turned 
and re-melted at least eight times under a pure argon atmo- 
sphere to ensure a uniform composition. Each alloy ingot, weighing 
approximately 65 g, displayed a coarse Widmanstätten structure 
composed of lamellar α phase and residual β phase ( Fig. 1 (a)). 
The phase transition temperatures of TiZrAlMn alloys were using 
differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) by ASTM F2004–05 (2010) 
at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The onset and end temperatures of 
the alloy were determined to be 785 ±4 °C and 869 ±10 °C, respec- 
tively, using the tangent method on the DSC curves ( Fig. 1 (b)). To 
optimize the coarse Widmanstätten structure, hot rolling was per- 
formed at different phase transition temperature regions (900 °C, 
850 °C, 800 °C and 750 °C). Each TiZrAlMn alloy ingot was held 
in the muﬄe furnace for 40 min before rolling to ensure uniform 
heating and reaching target temperature. Button ingots, approxi- 
mately 14 mm thick, were rolled to 2 mm using two-high rolls 
mill, achieving a total deformation of 85.7%. Between each rolling 
pass, the ingots were reheated in the furnace for 6 min, with a 
rolling reduction of 1.5 mm per pass (rolling speed: 0.5 m/s). Air 
cooling was used after rolling. The rolled specimens were labeled 
as 900-AC, 850-AC, 800-AC and 750-AC, according to their respec- 
tive rolling temperatures. 
X-ray diffraction (XRD, SmartLab) was used to analyze the 
phase composition and crystal structure of the specimens, with 
Cu K α radiation over a scanning range of 20 °−90 ° and a scanning 
rate of 10 °/min. The microstructure was analyzed using an opti- 
cal microscope (OM, Zeiss Ax 200 MAT) and a scanning electron 
microscope (SEM, Hitachi S3400) equipped with electron backscat- 
ter diffraction (EBSD) and energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) de- 
tectors. The alloy specimens were ground by 60 0#, 120 0# and 
30 0 0# sandpaper, polished until free of visible scratches, and then 
etched with a mixture of 10% hydroﬂuoric acid, 15% nitric acid and 
75% distilled water for OM and SEM observation. EBSD analysis 
specimens were prepared by electrolytic polishing with a scanning 
step of 90 nm, using an electrolyte solution of 10% perchloric acid 
and 90% methanol at 22 V and −20 °C. Transmission electron mi- 
croscopy (TEM/STEM, Talos F200x) was performed under a vacuum 
with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV to observe the alloy speci- 
men details. For TEM specimen preparation, 35 μm sheets (ground 
by sandpaper) were polished using dual-jet electrochemical polish- 
ing at 14 V and −30 °C. The corrosion solution was the same as the 
electrolyte solution. 
Room temperature uniaxial tensile tests were conducted at a 
strain rate of 5 × 10−4 s−¹ by using an Instron 5982 tensile test- 
ing machine. The tensile specimens were dog bone-shaped with a 
gauge section measuring 3 mm in width, 2 mm in thickness and 20

3. Experimental results 
3.1. Phase composition and microstructure 
To determine the phase composition of the hot-rolled TiZrAlMn 
alloy at different temperatures, XRD analysis was conducted on the 
specimens rolled at the four selected temperature ranges ( Fig. 2 ). 
Observation found that all specimens showed dual-phase diffrac- 
tion peaks, consisting of stable hcp α phase and bcc β phase, with 
no other phase diffraction peaks appearing. Comparing the second 
diffraction of all patterns, the peak intensity of the 750-AC spec- 
imen was signiﬁcantly higher than the other three specimens, in- 
dicating the presence of the (0 0 01) basal plane texture. Previous 
studies have reported a similar phenomenon in α-Ti alloys or near- 
α Ti alloys during thermomechanical processing at lower tempera- 
tures [ 18 , 28 ]. 
The microstructural changes of TiZrAlMn alloy at different 
rolling temperatures were analyzed by OM and SEM micrographs 
in Fig. 3 . The 900-AC specimen exhibited a microstructure sim-

Widmanstätten and Basket-weave structures, mainly composed of 
coarse prior- β grain boundaries with internally parallel and inter- 
secting lamellar α phases ( Fig. 3 (a)). A small amount of β phases 
was present between the lamellae. The size of the prior- β grains 
was measured to be about 280 μm using the line intersection 
method. Observation of the embedded map revealed that the Wid-

3.2. EBSD analysis 
The lamellar α phase regions of the 900-AC and 850-AC spec- 
imens were analyzed using EBSD. Fig. 4 presents the phase distri- 
bution diagrams, inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) maps and kernel average 
misorientation (KAM) maps for both specimens. Fig. 4 (a) shows 
that the volume fractions of the α phases and β phases in the 
900-AC specimen were 99.2% and 0.08%, respectively, indicating 
that rolling at this temperature was dominated by the α phase. 
In comparison, the volume fraction of the β phases in the 850-AC 
specimen (1.02%) slightly increased relative to the 900-AC speci- 
men (( Fig. 4 (d)). The IPF results for the 900-AC and 850-AC spec- 
imens ( Fig. 4 (b, e)) revealed a mixture of coarse and ﬁne lamellar 
α in both specimens. This occurs because the preferentially formed 
lamellar α phases undergo nucleation and growth, creating numer- 
ous phase boundaries that restrict the development of the newly 
formed lamellar α phases [ 29 ]. The KAM value allows the equiv- 
alent observation of geometrically necessary dislocations to deter- 
mine the local lattice strain in the specimen [ 30 ]. The KAM val- 
ues at the lamellar α grain boundaries in the 900-AC and 850-AC 
specimens were signiﬁcantly higher than those within the grains 
as observed in Fig. 4 (c, f), indicating that the dislocation density 
was higher at the grain boundaries. To further analyze the width 
and dislocation density of the lamellar α phase in the 900-AC and 
850-AC specimens, the size distribution and local misorientation 
content of lamellar α were statistically presented in Fig. 5 . The 
width of the lamellar α of the 900-AC specimen ( ∼1.8 μm) was 
smaller than that of the 850-AC specimen ( ∼2.1 μm) as shown in 
Fig. 5 (a, b). As the rolling temperature decreased, the average lo- 
cal misorientation decreased from 0.19 ° to 0.18 ° ( Fig. 5 (c, d)). The 
presence of a small number of equiaxed α grains in the 850-AC 
specimen was evident from the OM and SEM micrographs. Gener- 
ally, recrystallized grains exhibits lower KAM values due to dislo- 
cation reduction [ 31 ]. Therefore, the 900-AC specimen has a higher 
dislocation density.

The extent of dynamic recrystallization and microstructure evo- 
lution in the 800-AC and 750-AC specimens were further analyzed 
by EBSD. Fig. 7 presents LAGBs (green line) and HAGBs (black 
line) distribution maps, grain orientation spread maps and KAM 
maps. A small amount of LAGBs was observed in the 800-AC spec- 
imen distributed near the grain boundaries of the broken α grains 
( Fig. 7 (a)). In contrast, the 750-AC specimen exhibited a signiﬁ- 
cantly higher proportion of LAGBs, distributed both within broken 
sub-grains and along the grain boundaries ( Fig. 7 (d)). Zhou et al. 
[ 34 ] demonstrated that the degree of grain recrystallization during 
rolling deformation can be quantiﬁed using the grain orientation 
spread (GOS) map. The GOS values between 0 ° and 2 ° are deﬁned 
as recrystallized grains (blue region), between 2 ° and 5 ° are de- 
ﬁned as sub-grains (yellow region), and greater than 5 ° are deﬁned 
as deformed grains (red region). Fig. 7 (b) showed that the 800- 
AC specimen cont ained fewer def ormed grains, mainly composed 
of recrystallized grains and sub-grains. While the 750-AC speci- 
men had fewer recrystallized grains and mainly consisted of bro- 
ken sub-grains and deformed grains. This suggests that hot rolling 
at 800 °C provides stored energy, driving dynamic recrystallization 
[ 35 ]. As shown in Fig. 7 (c, f), the 800-AC specimen exhibited lower 
KAM values, with only a few regions of higher KAM values concen- 
trated at grain boundaries. Compared to the 800-AC specimen, the 
areas with high KAM values in the 750-AC specimen signiﬁcantly 
increase and are more extensively distributed within the grains. 
Fig. 8 exhibits the distribution of grain boundary orientation differ- 
ences, the percentage of different grain types and local misorienta- 
tion maps for the 800-AC and 750-AC specimens. Fig. 8 (a, d) found 
that the percentage of low-angle grain boundaries increased from 
45.7% to 68.1% as the rolling temperature decreased. Additionally, 
the recrystallized grains content of the 800-AC specimen (65.3%) 
was approximately double of that of the 750-AC specimen (33.2%) 
( Fig. 8 (b, e)). This ﬁnding is generally consistent with the above re- 
sults on the distribution of grain boundary orientation differences. 
This indicates that the degree of dynamic recrystallization of the 
800-AC specimen is higher than that of the 750-AC specimen. As 
can be seen from Fig. 8 (c, f), the average local misorientation of 
the 800-AC specimen (0.18 °) was weaker than that of the 750-AC 
specimen (0.36 °). This further conﬁrms that the 800-AC specimen 
consumes a signiﬁcant number of dislocations to undergo dynamic 
recrystallization during the hot rolling process.

3.5. Fracture mechanism 
Fig. 12 shows the fracture morphology of tensile-tested 
TiZrAlMn specimens rolled at different tem peratures, and the 
macro-regional morphology of the fracture surface is inserted in 
upper right region. The 900-AC specimen ( Fig. 12 (a)) exhibited 
a different fracture morphology from the other three specimens.

observed in the other three specimens. Among them, the 750-AC

formed during the tensile process, likely due to stress absorption, 
were observed as the origin of cracks [ 49 ]. 
4. Discussion 
4.1. Spheroidal evolution of the lamellar α phase 
The hot rolling temperature signiﬁcantly inﬂuences the 
spheroidization of the lamellar α phase. Fig. 13 presents a 
detailed ﬂow chart illustrating the microstructure evolution 
to provide a clearer understanding of this process. Since this 
experiment focused on the spheroidization of α lamellae, the 
residual β phase content was minimal and had little impact on 
the spheroidization process, as shown in the phase distribution 
diagram in the abovementioned EBSD analysis. Thus, it was not 
included in the ﬂow chart. At a rolling temperature of 900 °C ( β
single-phase region), the high-temperature β phase fully formed 
and grew rapidly during the hot rolling. The rolling process could 
not entirely break down the coarse high-temperature β phase, 
while after a lower cooling rate, the air-cooling led to the pro- 
cess formation of parallel arrangement of the lamellar α phases, 
resulting in the Widmanstätten structure with residual β phases. 
Additionally, the retained β phase contributed to the formation 
of a basket-weave structure of lamellar α phases ( Fig. 13 (a)). 
As the rolling temperature decreased to 850 °C, a large amount 
of high-temperature β phases was formed, but it did not have 
suﬃcient time to grow up and maintained a small grain size 
during the rolling process at this low temperature. Meanwhile, 
a small portion of the original lamellar α phases was retained 
and underwent a dynamic recrystallization, resulting in equiaxed 
α grains at the interior and boundaries of the initially formed 
β grains. During the air-cooling, the β phases transformed back 
to α phases, leading to the precipitation of ﬁne lamellar grains 
( Fig. 13 (b)). Since 800 °C belonged to the low-temperature region 
of α+ β transformed phase region, only a small amount of the 
high-temperature β phases generated short lamellar α phases 
during the cooling process. No distinct prior- β grain boundaries 
were observed, and dynamic recrystallization of the original 
lamellar α phase occurred. EBSD analysis ( Section 3.2 ) determined 
that the 800-AC specimen had a high degree of recrystallization 
so that the deformation grains almost disappeared and a large 
number of equiaxed α grains existed, alongside a small number

4.2. Evolution of dynamic recrystallization mechanism 
Notably, the degree of recrystallization in the 800-AC speci- 
mens was higher than that of the 750-AC specimens, however, 
the difference in sub-grain content between them was minimal 
( Fig. 8 (b, e)). This may be inﬂuenced by the dynamic recrystalliza- 
tion mechanism. Generally, the dynamic recrystallization mecha- 
nisms of metal materials during deformation are categorized into 
DDRX, CDRX and GDRX [ 50–52 ]. These mechanisms are typically 
mediated by dislocations. The GDRX process is challenging to acti- 
vate. At high temperatures and large strains, serrated HAGBs are 
pinched to form small equiaxed grains. However, a multi-pass 
rolling process was employed in this experiment. To prevent crack- 
ing in the 800-AC and 750-AC specimens during the rolling, the 
deformation and strain rate per pass were kept low, insuﬃcient to 
activate the GDRX. However, related studies have conﬁrmed that 
the introduction of nano-twins through very high strain rates and 
strains promotes a dynamic recrystallization process that reﬁnes

metallic grains into the nanograin category, deﬁned as nano-twin- 
assisted dynamic recrystallization (NTDRX) [ 53 ]. The 800-AC and 
750-AC specimens only produced a small amount of twins, which 
were inadequate to assist in driving NTDRX. Therefore, this exper- 
iment mainly involved the CDRX and DDRX. 
Related studies have identiﬁed the dynamic recrystallization 
mechanism through cumulative misorientation [ 18 , 50 , 51 ]. Speciﬁ- 
cally, a cumulative misorientation greater than 15 ° indicates that 
CDRX is dominant while less than 15 ° suggests DDRX. To further 
conﬁrm the dynamic recrystallization mechanism, Fig. 14 shows 
a localized magniﬁcation of the IPF maps and misorientation dis- 
tribution of the 800-AC and 750-AC specimens. As observed in 
Fig. 14 (a), the cumulative misorientation for all three groups of 
800-AC specimens was less than 15 °, with a maximum cumulative 
misorientation of about 9 °. This indicates that the DDRX process 
mainly occurs during hot rolling at 800 °C. Recrystallized grains 
are formed by bowing at grain boundaries, and the sub-grains were 
relatively stable with low rotation angles. However, two groups (C–
D and E–F) of 750-AC specimens exhibited cumulative misorien- 
tation of nearly 15 ° between neighboring sub-grains, while only 
one group (A–B) had a cumulative misorientation of less than 15 °
( Fig. 14 (b)). This suggests that hot rolling at 750 °C enhances the 
sub-grain’s activity, making them more prone to absorbing dislo- 
cations and producing recrystallized grains by merging or rotation. 
As the rolling temperature decreases, the dynamic recrystallization 
mechanism gradually shifts from DDRX to CDRX. Variations in the 
dynamic recrystallization mechanism may be the main reason for 
the small difference in substructure content between the 800-AC 
and 750-AC specimens. 
4.3. Effect of spheroidization process on mechanical properties 
The 900-AC and 850-AC specimens exhibit a morphology dom- 
inated by lamellar α phase. The yield strengths of these two spec- 
imens are similar, primarily due to the strong dislocation hinder- 
ing the ability of the lamellar α phase [ 54 ]. However, the plas- 
ticity of the 850-AC specimen is signiﬁcantly better than that of 
the 900-AC specimen. Thus, this experiment focused on the dif- 
ferences in plasticity between the two specimens. The excellent 
plasticity exhibited by the 850-AC specimen (total elongation of 
12.4%) is inﬂuenced by multiple factors. The OM diagrams re- 
vealed a signiﬁcant difference in the size of prior- β grains be- 
tween the two specimens, and hot rolling at 850 °C limited the 
growth of the prior- β grains ( Fig. 3 (a, b)). The size was about 
100 μm, signiﬁcantly smaller than the 280 μm observed in the 
900-AC specimen. Related studies have shown that ﬁne prior- β
grains can reduce the length of microcracks compared to coarse 
ones [ 14 ]. The 900-AC specimen displayed numerous longer cracks 
across the macro-region of the fracture surface. In contrast, the 
cracks in the 850-AC specimen were not observed due to the 
small size ( Fig. 12 (a, b)). Fine cracks were hindered from expand- 
ing within the lamellar α phases, which improves plastic defor- 
mation. This may contribute partially to the excellent plasticity. 
Compared to the 900-AC specimen, the 850-AC specimen exhibits 
partially lamellar α phases that undergoes dynamic recrystalliza- 
tion to form a small amount of equiaxed α phases. The pres- 
ence of the equiaxed α phases consumes some dislocations, which 
tend to slip within the recrystallized grains [ 55 ]. This facilitates 
the uniform force applied to the alloy in the deformation process 
and reduces the stress concentration phenomenon, thus improv- 
ing the plasticity of the alloy. It can be observed from Figs. 3 and 
9 that the 900-AC specimen produced a large number of parallel 
lamellar α phases ( α colonies) due to the formation of the Wid- 
manstätten structure, which led to the generation of continuous 
grain boundaries. The presence of continuous grain boundaries fa- 
cilitates dislocation stacking and makes penetration diﬃcult. The 
resulting higher back stresses causes cracks to initiate and propa- 
gate at the grain boundaries, leading to premature failure during

discontinuous grain boundaries. These discontinuous grain bound-

stress distribution. Consequently, smaller back stresses could be 
generated compared to continuous grain boundaries [ 6 , 56 , 57 ]. A 
greater amount of deformation may occur before crack initiation 
thereby improving plasticity. Fig. 15 illustrates the fracture orga- 
nization morphology of the 900-AC and 850-AC specimens, high- 
lighting crack initiation and extension. A small number of promi- 
nent large cracks were observed in the fracture macro-region of 
the 900-AC specimen, while numerous small cracks were identiﬁed 
in the 850-AC specimen. A related study found that the formation 
of such small cracks was due to the concentration of dislocations 
at the boundary of lamellar α phases under tensile loading of ti- 
tanium alloys [ 58 , 59 ]. The prevalence number of small cracks con- 
ﬁrms the dispersion of stresses during the tensile process, allowing 
the deformation to effectively absorb energy. Cracks in the 900-AC 
specimen appeared at grain boundaries and within the lamellar α
phases, with a maximum crack width of 55 μm at grain boundaries 
( Fig. 15 (a, b)). In contrast, cracks in the 850-AC specimen were ob- 
served solely in the lamellar α phase, with no signiﬁcant cracks 
noted at the grain boundaries. The crack width ( ∼17 μm) was 
considerably smaller than that of the 900-AC specimen ( Fig. 15 (b, 
c)). This further conﬁrms that the presence of discontinuous 
grain boundaries can effectively relieve stress concentration and 
enhance plasticity. 
The 800-AC and 750-AC specimens exhibited excellent strength- 
plasticity synergies. This superior balance of yield strength and 
plasticity may result from a combination of composite strength- 
ening mechanisms that are challenging to describe quantitatively. 
In terms of yield strength, grain boundary strengthening can be 
classiﬁed into ﬁne-grain strengthening and sub-grain strengthen- 
ing. Both types of strengthening are consistent with the Hall-Petch 
theory. Speciﬁcally, a smaller grain size leads to an increase in the 
number of grain boundaries, enhancing the dislocation pinning and 
interaction abilities, which helps to overcome the increased fric- 
tional resistance and, consequently, raises the yield strength. For 
this reason, the morphology of the grains and the average grain 
size were further observed in Fig. 16 . Interestingly, there was lit- 
tle difference in the average grain size between the 800-AC ( ∼5.27 
μm) and 750-AC specimens ( ∼4.84 μm) ( Fig. 16 (b, d)). This may re- 
sult from the different dynamic recrystallization mechanisms and 
recrystallization growth. As indicated by the cumulative misori- 
entation within the grains discussed in Section 4.1 , the 800-AC 
specimen conﬁrmed that the main mechanism of dynamic re- 
crystallization was DDRX. High-density dislocations cause bowing 
at grain boundaries, leading to formation of strain-free equiaxed 
α phases through nucleation and growth processes. Only a lim- 
ited number of sub-grains undergoes merging and migration, re- 
sulting in the retention of some sub-grains. The ﬁne equiaxed α
phases grow up during air-cooling leading to grain sizes compa- 
rable to those of broken deformed grains. Moreover, as shown in 
Fig. 16 (a, c), the 750-AC specimen contained slightly more and 
ﬁner sub-grains compared to the 800-AC specimen. Sub-grains 
can accumulate additional dislocations during plastic deformation, 
enhancing the strengthening effect [ 60 ]. The combined effect of 
ﬁne-grain strengthening and sub-grain strengthening increases the 
yield strength. A small amount of ﬁne lamellar α phase (about 500 
nm in width) was present in the 800-AC specimen ( Fig. 9 (e, f)). 
The α/ β phase boundaries formed between the lamellar α phase 
and the β matrix can hinder the dislocation motion, producing 
an effect similar to ﬁne-grain strengthening. Additionally, the 750- 
AC specimen exhibit s a lower degree of recrystallization. A large 
number of dislocations are introduced during the hot rolling pro-

In terms of plasticity, the HAGBs formed by the equiaxed α
phase could absorb the dislocation density and undergo uniform 
deformation, thereby reducing stress concentration and enhancing 
plasticity. However, it has been demonstrated that LAGBs domi- 
nated by sub-grains act as “soft” barriers that selectively allow 
dislocations to pass [ 60–62 ]. This occurred because dislocations 
with similar orientations to those in the LAGBs can pass dur- 
ing deformation, while dislocations with larger orientation differ- 
ences are hindered by the grain boundaries [ 60 ]. Dislocations gen- 
erated during deformation interact with LAGBs and dissociate into 
Shockley partial dislocations. The small orientation difference be- 
tween neighboring sub-grains effectively disperses stress during 
plastic deformation, thereby reducing the stress concentration at 
grain boundaries [ 63 ]. The presence of sub-grains signiﬁcantly con- 
tributes to achieving high-yield strength and plasticity. In addi- 
tion, a small number of twins are activated during the hot-rolling 
process, with the 750-AC specimen exhibiting more types and a 
greater quantity of twins than the 800-AC specimen. The forma- 
tion of twins could alter the orientation of the crystals to a more 
favorable slip position, further promoting slip. The 750-AC speci- 
men retains a small amount of β phases (6.4% volume fraction) 
and displays a weak basal plane texture. The β phase of the bcc 
lattice is more susceptible to dislocation slip than the α phase of 
the hcp structure. Furthermore, the small amount of β phase has 
minimal impact on the yield strength reduction but increases the 
total elongation of the alloy. The weak basal plane texture allows 
crystals to be preferentially oriented during deformation, facilitat- 
ing coordinated deformation that optimizes plasticity [ 64 ]. As a re- 
sult, the 750-AC exhibites the highest total elongation. 
5. Conclusions 
This work systematically investigates the processes and mecha- 
nisms of Widmanstätten spheroidization in the TiZrAlMn alloy dur- 
ing hot rolling at different temperatures, as well as the relation- 
ship between the microstructure and mechanical properties. The 
key ﬁndings are summarized as follows. 
(1) After rolling at 900 °C, the specimen exhibits a mixed mor- 
phology of coarse Widmanstätten and Basket-weave struc- 
tures. When the rolling temperature decreases to 850 °C, 
the Widmanstätten structure disappears, replaced by a small 
number of equiaxed α grains at grain boundaries and within 
the prior- β grains. The 800-AC specimen displays a signiﬁ- 
cant amount of equiaxed α grains, along with a few sub- 
grains and short lamellar α grains. At 750 °C ( α single-phase 
region temperature), the microstructure primarily consists of 
broken sub-grains and deformed grains, as well as a few 
equiaxed α grains. 
(2) As the rolling temperature decreases from 900 °C to 850 °C, 
the prior- β grains are reﬁned and the grain boundaries vary 
from the continuous to discontinuous. Obvious dynamic re- 
crystallization occurs in the 800-AC and 750-AC specimens.

As the rolling temperature decreases, the degree of recrystal-

(3) The notably improved plasticity of the 850-AC specimen, 
compared to the 900-AC, is primarily attributed to the com- 
bined effects of discontinuous grain boundaries, equiaxed α
grains and reﬁned prior- β grains. Both 800-AC and 750-AC 
specimens show excellent strength-plasticity synergy. The 
yield strength is as high as 1070 MPa for 800-AC and 1110 
MPa for 750-AC. And their plasticity in terms of total elonga- 
tion is 15% and 18%, respectively. Apart from the same main 
strengthening mechanisms of ﬁne-grains and sub-grains, the 
lower degree of recrystallization and high dislocation density 
of the 750-AC specimen produce extra strengthening. Their 
excellent plasticity originates from the equiaxed α grains, 
“soft” barrier sub-grains and a small number of twins. The 
better plasticity of 750-AC is due to its retained 6.4% of ﬁne 
β grains and weak basal texture.
