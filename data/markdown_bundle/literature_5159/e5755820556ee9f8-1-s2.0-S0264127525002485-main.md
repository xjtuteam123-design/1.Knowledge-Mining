ABSTRACT
TiAl alloys are lightweight, high-strength, and have good mechanical properties at elevated temperatures, 
rendering them appealing for high-temperature applications. However, their difficult processing, and limited 
ductility at ambient temperatures have hindered their widespread application. Here, we report fabrication of a 
Ti-43Al-9 V-0.3Y alloy with a novel lamellar-network two-scale structure comprising an inner α
2
/ γ lamellar 
colony + outer β
0
/ γ phases via semi-solid forging process. The formation of this lamellar-network two-scale 
structure is elucidated from the perspective of the solute diffusion and redistribution occurring, and occurs due to 
liquid segregation and a non-equilibrium transition of L → β ( β
0
) + α at late solidification. Compared to the as-cast 
alloy, the semi-solid forged alloy exhibits significant increases in elongation and tensile strength at room tem -
perature and 800
◦
C. The high density of dislocations and mechanical twins in the β

/ γ phases and special α

lamellae during tensile deformation effectively release the plastic deformation potential of the TiAl alloy at room 
temperature. Moreover, the abundant nano-twins in the β

/ γ phase and γ dynamic recrystallization behavior at 
800 ℃ significantly enhance the high-temperature plasticity. This approach and microstructure offer a promising 
solution to the engineering challenges posed by the low room-temperature ductility and limited hot-working 
ability of TiAl alloys.
1. Introduction
The titanium aluminide (TiAl) alloy is a novel advanced engineering 
material with low density (3.9 – 4.2 g/cm
3
), high specific strength, and 
exceptional high-temperature resistance, and has successfully replaced 
nickel-based superalloys in applications within the temperature range of 
650 – 750 ℃ . An additional benefit is a weight reduction of as much as 
~50 % [ 1 – 8 ].
However, difficult processing with high manufacturing costs and a 
lack of sufficient ductility at ambient temperature are major concerns for 
structural applications [ 9 – 12 ]. Moreover, the mechanical properties of 
industrial cast TiAl alloys are poor and limited yields are obtained. These 
outcomes are often attributed to serious problems such as segregation, 
shrinkage porosity, and microcracks [ 13 – 15 ]. Additionally, TiAl alloys 
exhibit poor deformation ability at high temperature; therefore, shaping 
of these alloys through hot forging or rolling is challenging [ 16 ]. These 
obstacles increase the difficulty and cost of preparing TiAl-alloy 
products.
Semi-solid forming is a promising near-net shaping technology pio -
neered by Flemings [ 17 ], which enables the production of complex- 
shaped parts in a single step with reduced forging force. Semi-solid 
forming has been extensively researched for Al [ 18 – 20 ], magnesium 
[ 21 ], and steel [ 22 ] alloys. For example, Chen and Tsao [ 18 ] examined 
the phenomenological deformation behaviors of non-dendritic macro -
structures in the semi-solid state. They suggested that four major 
mechanisms are responsible for the semi-solid deformation of non- 
dendritic structures and validated their phenomenological model 
through experiments conducted on A356 alloys with non-dendritic 
structures. Further, Liu et al. [ 22 ] studied the solidification micro -
structures of semi-solid forged Cr4Mo4V steel (where Cr, Mo, and V are 
chromium, molybdenum, and vanadium, respectively) and analyzed the 
influence of the semi-solid forging process parameters on the dendrite 
spheroidization and liquid flow behavior. Chang et al. [ 23 ] studied the 
solidification defects, microstructures, and mechanical properties of 
7075 Al alloy under different semi-solid forming pressures. Additionally, 
Jiang et al. [ 24 ] studied the high-temperature deformation behavior and

time, which is performed using an industrial hydraulic press. A Ti-43Al-

pathway for industrial production and engineering application, and 
have significant economic value.
2. Materials and experimental procedures
2.1. Process design and material preparation
To determine the suitable temperature for semi-solid forging of the 
Ti-43Al-9 V-0.3Y alloy and to aid analysis of the phase transition 
mechanism, thermodynamic calculations based on the CALPHAD 
method were conducted. Hence, the constituent phases and related 
transition temperatures were predicted. Fig. 1 (a) and (b) show the bi -
nary equilibrium phase diagram and the calculated phase fractions as 
functions of temperature, respectively, for the investigated Ti-43Al-9 V- 
0.3Y alloy. The results were derived from the commercial TiAl database 
in the Thermo-Calc software package (2024b) [ 39 ]. The thermodynamic 
calculation of the liquid – solid phase region temperature was validated 
by the differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, NETZSCH STA449F3) test 
result shown in Fig. 1 (c). The temperature of 1300 
◦
C fell within the 
middle of the (L + α ) solid – liquid phase region of the Ti-43Al-9 V-0.3Y 
alloy. At this temperature, the alloy was in a semi-solid state with 40 % 
liquid- and 60 % solid-phase volume fractions ( Fig. 1 (b)). Therefore, 
1300 
◦
C was selected as the forging and intermediate heat preservation 
temperature for investigation of both the microstructural evolution 
mechanism during semi-solid forging and the mechanical properties of 
the Ti-43Al-9 V-0.3Y alloy.
A Ti-43Al-9 V-0.3Y alloy ingot measuring Φ 200 mm × 250 mm was 
initially produced using vacuum arc remelting (VAR). The ingot was hot 
isostatically pressed. A cylinder was cut from this ingot and placed in a 
steel can. The cylinder was sealed in the can for subsequent semi-solid 
forging by 4000KN four-column hydraulic press. The forging process 
involved a two-step procedure with an intermediate heat preservation 
stage at 1300 
◦
C. The strain rate was approximately 0.01 s
 1 
and the 
total reduction was approximately 70 %. Prior to forging, the cylinder 
was heated to 1300 
◦
C in a furnace for 90 min. Between the two forging 
steps, a TiAl billet was reheated for 45 mins and then annealed at 900 
◦
C 
for 12h with furnace cooling.
2.2. Microstructural analysis
The samples for microstructural evaluation were cut from the forging 
pancake, parallel to its compression axis. The microstructural observa -
tion and fracture morphology analysis was conducted using a scanning 
electron microscope (SEM, ZEISS, Gemini 300) with backscatter scan -
ning electron mode (BSE). The crystalline orientations of the TiAl alloy 
were examined using electron backscattering diffraction (EBSD, Oxford 
Symmetry), and the corresponding data were processed using Aztec -
Crystal Software 2.1 (Oxford Instruments). The EBSD acceleration 
voltage was 20 kV, the step size was 0.15 μ m, and the orientation dif -
ference angle between the high- and low-angle grain boundaries was 
10
◦
. The standard used for indexation (CIF file, etc.) was obtained from 
the AztecCrystal Software files. The fine microstructural features were 
further analyzed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI 
Talos F200x). To prepare the SEM and EBSD samples, electrolytic pol -
ishing was performed in a solution consisting of 12-ml perchloric acid, 
68-ml butanol, and 120-ml methanol.

3.2. Tensile properties
3.2.1. Room-temperature tensile properties
Fig. 4 (a) illustrates the tensile properties of the as-cast Ti-43Al-9 V- 
0.3Y alloys. An average tensile strength of 429 ± 17 MPa was obtained, 
with an elongation of 0.41 ± 0.007 % at room temperature. The me -
chanical properties were significantly enhanced by the semi-solid forg -
ing process. Hence, an average yield strength of 796 ± 7 MPa, tensile 
strength of 886 ± 19 MPa, and elongation of 1.64 ± 0.24 % at room 
temperature were obtained. Compared to the as-cast alloy, the semi- 
solid forged alloy exhibited a 300 % increase in elongation and a 106 
% increase in tensile strength at room temperature. Fig. 4 (b) shows the 
tensile engineering stress – strain curves of the as-cast and semi-solid 
forged Ti-43Al-9 V-0.3Y alloy at room temperature. Fig. 4 (c) shows 
the room-temperature mechanical properties of typical TiAl alloys 
produced through casting, forging, rolling, and powder metallurgy 
[ 33 , 34 , 41 – 48 ]. The microstructure evolution during the semi-solid 
forging process primarily involves solidification. Consequently, the 
mechanical properties of the semi-solid forged microstructure we 
compared with those of the solidification microstructure (that is casting 
microstructure), revealing that the semi-solid forged microstructure 
exhibits excellent mechanical properties at room temperature. In addi -
tion, the room temperature mechanical properties of the alloy produced 
using this novel processing method are comparable to the alloy 
processed through different methods in the literature. The results of this 
study demonstrate that both enhanced room-temperature strength and 
ductility can be obtained under the action of a lamellar-network two- 
scale structure of an inner α
2
/ γ lamellar colony + outer β
0
/ γ phases, 
obtained through the semi-solid forging. Therefore, application of the 
semi-solid forging process is a promising approach towards tailoring the 
mechanical properties of TiAl alloys. A comparison with our previous 
work on Ti-43Al-9 V-0.3Y (Ref 38) reveals significant differences in 
microstructure and mechanical properties due to the two-step forging 
process with 40 % liquid fraction employed in the present study. The 
liquid fraction promotes the formation of a more homogeneous lamellar- 
network two-scale structure, while a hybrid microstructure is obtained 
by forging without liquid phase in Ref 38. Compared to the hybrid 
microstructure obtained, the strength and elongation of the alloy in 
current results are lower.

4.2. Fracture morphologies of semi-solid forged TiAl alloy
Fig. 8 (a) and (b) show the room-temperature tensile fracture 
morphology of the Ti-43Al-9 V-0.3Y alloy. Classic indications of brittle 
fractures, such as cleavage facets, tear ridges, and river patterns, are 
apparent. Fig. 8 (a) shows that the fracture morphology of the as-cast 
microstructure consisted of a crack, a wide smooth cleavage facet, and 
a large area of tearing edges, indicating its low ductility. Research by 
Apple et al. [ 56 ] has demonstrated that γ -TiAl alloys are prone to 
cleavage fracture along the {111}
γ 
planes. The resistance to crack 
propagation in this cleavage mode is remarkably low. This phenomenon 
can be attributed to the fact that the {111} γ cleavage planes simulta -
neously serve as both dislocation slip planes and twinning habit planes. 
Consequently, impeded slip or twinning deformation can readily facili -
tate crack nucleation. Once nucleated, these cleavage cracks can rapidly 
propagate and reach their critical length with minimal resistance. This 
fracture mechanism directly contributes to the limited room- 
temperature ductility observed in TiAl alloys. Fig. 8 (b) reveals that the 
room-temperature fracture morphology of the semi-solid forged alloy 
was characterized by river pattern facets, fine tearing edges, and 
tortuous cracks, with no significant cleavage facets. Therefore, the semi- 
solid forged microstructure not only strengthened the material but also 
effectively coordinated the deformation and impeded the crack initia -
tion and propagation. Thus, the semi-solid forging process is conducive 
to microstructural regulation for improving the TiAl-alloy 
strength – ductility relationship. These findings are consistent with the 
room-temperature tensile properties of as-cast and semi-solid forged 
alloys.
Fig. 8 (c) and (d) show the high-temperature tensile fracture mor -
phologies of the Ti-43Al-9 V-0.3Y alloy at 800 
◦
C. The fracture 
morphology of the as-cast alloy was composed of shallow dimples at low 
density ( Fig. 8 (c)). This dimple morphology resembles that of an inter -
granular dimple fracture. The relatively low plasticity at room temper -
ature is primarily attributed to the dominant cleavage fracture 
mechanism. However, at elevated temperatures, thermal activation 
enables dislocations to engage in both slip and climb processes. This 
thermally activated deformation facilitates strain accommodation 
within the plastic zone ahead of crack tips, effectively alleviating con -
straints imposed by localized slip geometry. Consequently, the fracture 
mechanism transitions from cleavage-dominated behavior at low tem -
peratures to energy-absorbing plastic deformation modes within con -
stituent phases at high temperatures. This difference in fracture 
mechanisms manifests as distinct morphological characteristics between 
room-temperature and 800 ℃ tensile fracture surfaces in TiAl alloys. 
Fig. 8 (d) shows the semi-solid forged alloy high-temperature tensile 
fracture morphology at 800 ℃ , which featured deep dimples of high 
density. This result indicates the superior ductility and coordinated 
deformation of the semi-solid forged TiAl alloy observed during high- 
temperature tensile testing. The fracture morphology is consistent 
with the tensile elongation results obtained for the as-cast and semi-solid 
forged alloys at 800 ℃ .
