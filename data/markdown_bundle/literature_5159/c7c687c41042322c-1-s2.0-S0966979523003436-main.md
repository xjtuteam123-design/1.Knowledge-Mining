1. Introduction 
Multi-principal element alloys or High Entropy Alloys (HEAs) are a 
relatively new class of alloys based on the near equiatomic elemental 
composition design concept and explores the centre of the phase dia -
gram [ 1 ]. They exhibit promising properties like high strength, good 
ductility, wear resistance, superior fatigue properties, thermal stability, 
excellent corrosion resistance etc., and are portrayed to be potential 
candidates for aerospace, energy and biomedical engineering applica -
tions [ 2 – 5 ]. 
Among several HEAs, a eutectic alloy developed by Lu et al. with 
lamellar L1
2 
and B2 structure (AlCoCrFeNi
2.1
) is one of the extensively 
studied alloys [ 6 ]. Apart from exhibiting good mechanical properties in 
as-synthesized conditions, AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 
has demonstrated the poten -
tial for enhanced properties via various thermo-mechanical processes 
[ 7 – 11 ]. Thermomechanical processing parameters including tempera -
ture, strain rate and strain path of deformation were reported to 
influence the microstructural characteristics like lamellar breakdown, 
twinning, precipitation, recrystallization and back stress strengthening 
[ 12 – 14 ] and improve the mechanical properties of the alloy [ 7 , 8 , 10 , 11 , 
15 , 16 ]. 
High-temperature tensile deformation studies on the alloy between 
900 and 1050 
◦
C displayed precipitation of L1
2 
within the parent B2 
phase and B2 within parent L1
2
/FCC lamellae. This precipitation 
changed the FCC: B2 phase fraction from 6:4 to 3:7 [ 17 ]. Further, the 
alloy displayed a sequential disordering of L1
2 
at low and high tem -
peratures [ 18 ]. Apart from commendable mechanical behaviour, the 
alloy also displayed excellent thermal stability even at temperatures as 
high as 91 % of the melting point [ 12 , 19 ]. Thus, the eutectic alloy has 
demonstrated great versatility for microstructural and property modi -
fication via thermo-mechanical processing. 
In addition to the eutectic composition, researchers have also studied 
the microstructural characteristics and properties of the near eutectic 
compositions of the AlCoCrFeNi
2.1

2. Experimental details 
The near eutectic alloy with a composition lean in Al and rich in Ni 
compared to the eutectic composition was produced from elemental 
metal pieces of 99.9 % purity in a high vacuum system via vacuum arc 
melting. The arc furnace (Arc200, Arcast Inc., USA) with a replaceable 
thoriated tungsten tip electrode was equipped with an electromagnetic 
stirrer for improving melt homogeneity. A Ti getter was melted before 
alloy casting to minimize the oxidation within the chamber. The melt 
was chill casted into rods of 10 mm diameter and 100 mm height. From 
the cast rods, hot deformation samples of 6.5 mm diameter and 10 mm 
height were prepared using Electrical Discharge Machining. Uniaxial 
compression tests were conducted at temperatures of 800, 900, 1000 
and 1100 
◦

by soaking at that temperature for 2 min. The deformation samples were 
quenched instantaneously using water with a quenching attachment. 
Scanning electron microscopy was conducted on the cast and 
deformed samples using Helios G4UX equipped with Energy Dispersive 
X-Ray Spectroscopy (EDS) for both imaging (backscattered electron 
imaging) and Electron Back Scattered Diffraction (EBSD). EBSD was 
performed with a step size of 50 nm at 20 kV accelerating voltage, 3.2 nA 
beam current and a working distance of 10 mm.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Microstructural correlation with stress-strain behaviour 
Within the studied temperature - strain rate domain, the alloy 
exhibited a diverse stress-strain behaviour ( Fig. 2 ). Correlating with the 
microstructural observations, Type 4 stress-strain behaviour character -
ized by relatively high work hardening rate (800 – 900 
◦
C and 10 s

and 
800 
◦
C and 1 s

of the FCC phase, evident from extensive deformation twinning ( Figs. 6g 
and 7 a
2

reported to be low ( < 30 mJ/mm
2
), and the addition of Al is further 
known to decrease the SFE [ 38 ]. Besides low SFE, the presence of the B2 
phase is also known to promote strain hardening within the FCC phase 
[ 39 ]. Thus, low SFE and the presence of the B2 phase in the present alloy 
can be presumed to impede dislocation motion at 800 and 900 
◦
C. 
Further, at these temperatures, the dynamic softening rate is expected to 
be lower ( Fig. S5a

deformation twinning, localized deformation, flow instability and 
cracking ( Fig. S1c

accurately predicted to be unstable via the dynamic materials model 
( Fig. 6 a). 
At 800 
◦

in strain rate. The stress-strain behaviour changed from Type 4 to Type 2 
(0.1 s

work hardenability and strain rate was observed at all temperatures 
( Fig. 2 b – d). This change in stress-strain behaviour at lower strain rates 
can be ascribed to the enhanced contribution of softening mechanisms, 
leading to steady-state (Type 2) or flow softening behaviour (Type 1) 
( Fig. 2 a). This softening prevalence at lower strain rates can be attrib -
uted to the extended time available for dislocation annihilation and/or 
rearrangement. 
The decrease in work hardenability with an increase in temperature 
( Fig. 2 a – d) is due to the increase in thermal activation energy available 
for dislocation annihilation. Interestingly, for a specific strain rate, the 
dominance of softening mechanisms (Type 1) observed at 800 and 
900

◦

This can be attributed to the precipitation of B2 within the FCC phase 
( Fig. 8 ) [ 39 ]. 
4.2. Effect of temperature and strain rate on precipitation 
Though there are several studies correlating precipitation during 
thermal mechanical processing to the mechanical behaviour, they are 
constrianed to the eutectic alloy [ 14 , 17 , 40 , 41 ]. Further, studies delin -
eating the effect of temperature and strain rate on precipitation 
behaviour is still lacking in literature. During high-temperature defor -
mation characterized by precipitation and work softening mechanisms, 
the interrelation is complicated and the precipitation depends on the 
thermal exposure period, temperature and strain. 
A comparative analysis of the microstructure of heat-treated [ 25 ] 
and hot-deformed alloy shows that B2 precipitation occurs in the pro -
eutectic and irregular eutectic regions irrespective of deformation. This 
preferential precipitation of the B2 phase within the proeutectic FCC and 
irregular eutectic region can be attributed to the supersaturation of el -
ements in the FCC phase and/or the reprecipitation of B2 from the 
eutectic region [ 25 ]. The increase in overall B2 fraction 
post-deformation implies that the precipitates have evolved from the 
FCC phase ( Fig. 8 a). A similar trend of increase in B2 fraction was 
observed after heat-treating the pro-eutectic alloy with identical 
composition [ 25 ]. However, heat treatment resulted in a higher overall 
B2 fraction of ~35 % (the fraction observed in eutectic alloy) [ 25 ]. 
The lower precipitate fraction in the deformed sample can be 
attributed to the shorter thermal exposure duration during the high- 
temperature deformation (~6 min) as opposed to 1 h exposure during

4.4. Comparison of high-temperature mechanical behaviour of near- 
eutectic alloy with the eutectic alloy 
Although this study is primailry focussed on understanding the 
microstructure evolution in a near-eutectic HEA under thermo- 
mechanical processing, the flow stress behaviour at elevated tempera -
tures gained in this study is useful in assessing the high-tempertaure 
strength. In comparison to the eutectic counterpart [ 15 , 46 ], the near 
eutectic alloy of this study exhibited a superior high-temperature 
strength across the studied temperature range ( Fig. 11 b). This superior 
mechanical behaviour of the near eutectic alloy can be ascribed to the 
higher fraction of low stacking fault energy (SFE) L1
2 
phase ( Fig. 7 a
2
). 
Low SFE associated with L1
2 
phase has been widely reported to impart 
high strength and ductility in this alloy [ 42 , 50 , 51 ]. Further, the pre -
cipitation of B2 phase within L1
2 
has also been found to enhance the 
mechanical behaviour of the alloy by forming semi-coherent interfaces 
capable of sustaining higher stress levels [ 40 ]. However, at high tem -
peratures and low strain rates where there is sufficient time for the 
activation of softening mechanisms, the strength of near eutectic and 
eutectic alloy was found to be similar ( Fig. 11 b). Thus the present study 
extends the knowledge about the temperature and strain rate on pre -
cipitation behaviour of near eutectic alloy and validate the ability of the 
alloy to retain the superior strength even at high temperatures.

• Post-deformed microstructures at lower temperatures were charac -
