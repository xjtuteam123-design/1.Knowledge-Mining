Abstract: 10 
 11 
The size, morphology and fraction of each of the γ′ precipitate populations in 12 
polycrystalline γ- γ′ nickel-base superalloys are highly sensitive to th e cooling rate from 13 
the solution temperature. As a result, thermal processing parameters are carefully selected 14 
to control precipitate populations in an effort to modify specific microstructural features 15 
that can impact resulting properties. The present i nvestigation reports on how a stepped 16 
cooling rate was able to modify the microstructure of two experimental Ni-base 17 
superalloys. Cooling rates of 0.1˚C/s and 0.01˚C/s were maintained through the γ′ solvus 18 
temperature to a transition temperature ~10˚C to 15 ˚C below the solvus. Following the 19 
transition temperature, a controlled cooling rate o f 1˚C/s was applied to control the size 20 
and distribution of the secondary γ′ precipitates. For both experimental alloys, slow 21 
cooling rates through the γ′ solvus temperature resulted in the formation of co arse grain 22 
boundary precipitates while secondary γ′ precipitates formed upon the subsequent faster 23 
cooling rate of 1˚C/s. Coarse grain boundary γ′ precipitates resulted in higher amplitudes 24 
and wavelengths of serration along the boundary. In addition to cooling rate, the grain 25 
boundary misorientation was also found to influence the precipitation of grain boundary 26 
γ′ with larger precipitate populations found along hi gh angle grain boundaries. Results 27 
from this study appear to support the concept that meso-scale engineering of grain 28 
boundary structures may potentially be used to enha nce and optimize the high 29 
temperature properties of Ni-base superalloys. 30

1. Introduction: 33 
 34 
Nickel base superalloys exhibit high strength at el evated temperature, good 35 
environmental and crack growth resistance thereby m aking them suitable for use in a 36 
variety of structural components used in the hot se ction of gas turbine engines [1-6]. The 37 
primary strengthening mechanisms operative in nicke l-based superalloys are solid 38 
solution strengthening and precipitation strengthen ing via intermetallic γ′ precipitates. 39 
The γ′ precipitates possess an ordered /g18381/g2870 crystal structure dispersed within the 40 
disordered FCC γ matrix. The /g18381/g2870 crystal structure exhibits a high degree of cohere ncy 41 
with γ phase. They have a nominal Ni /g2871Al composition where Co, Cr, Mo and W solute 42 
atoms may occupy the nickel sites and γ′ forming elements such as Al, Ti, Ta, and Nb 43 
occupy the Al sites. Meanwhile, the FCC γ matrix is a disordered phase that can be 44 
strengthened by forming a solid solution where solu te atoms possessing different atomic 45

Most of the advanced polycrystalline nickel base su peralloys intended for use at high 47

Over the years, improvements in the understanding o f underlying physics 50 
associated with Ni-base superalloys has led to the development of numerous models 51 
relating compositional and microstructural paramete rs with the resulting properties. 52 
Microstructural parameters such as volume fraction, morphology, and size distribution of 53 
γ′ precipitates are all known to play an important ro le in strengthening of Ni-base 54 
superalloys [11-16]. Compositional changes that inc rease the overall concentration of γ′ 55 
forming elements will serve to increase the volume fraction of γ′ precipitates and provide 56 
a higher degree of strengthening. However, these ch anges need to be balanced in order to 57 
maintain a suitable level of processing and phase s tability. Polycrystalline Ni-base 58 
superalloys can be thermal-mechanically processed a t sub-solvus temperatures where 59 
discrete grains of γ′ , also referred to as primary γ′ , sit at grain boundaries and restrict 60 
grain growth to maintain average grain sizes of les s than 10microns. For applications 61 
where strength and crack growth resistance are impo rtant, fine grain sizes are desired as 62 
they confer higher yield strengths and improved res istance to crack growth. Super-solvus 63 
processing of polycrystalline Ni-base superalloys o ccurs at temperatures above the γ′ 64 
solvus and yields coarser grain sizes that contribu te to extending the temperature 65 
capability of the material by improving creep resis tance. In most super-solvus processed 66 
Ni-base superalloys, the morphology of secondary γ′ precipitates is controlled by the rate 67 
of the cooling from the super-solvus temperature. T he magnitude of the lattice is also 68 
another factor in controlling the morphology of the secondary γ′ precipitates. Depending 69 
on the magnitude of the misfit, secondary γ′ precipitate morphologies may vary from 70 
being spherical to cuboidal [17-19]. The cooling ra te also affects the resulting size 71 
distribution of secondary γ′ precipitates as the microstructure may consist of either a 72 
mono-modal size distribution, bi-modal size distrib ution, and in some cases multi-modal 73 
size distribution of γ′ . [20-25]. 74 
In recent years, the development of various physics -based constitutive models has 75 
shown that the mechanical properties of Ni-base sup eralloys are highly sensitive to the 76 
underlying microstructure [4,12,26,27]. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms by 77 
which microstructures develop and the development o f processes that can control their 78 
formation is critical for the optimization and tail oring of properties. Provided that 79 
residual stresses can be minimized and quench crack ing can be avoided, rapid cooling of 80 
Ni-base superalloys from super-solvus temperatures is preferred as this tends to increase 81 
the number density of γ′ precipitates due to the high driving force for nucleation and yield 82 
a mono-modal size distribution of secondary γ′ precipitates that confers high strength. 83 
Unfortunately, only physically small Ni-base supera lloy components, or alloys 84 
possessing low equilibrium γ′ precipitate volume fractions can be thermally proc essed in 85 
this manner. For many Ni-base superalloy component s, the rate of heat extraction is 86 
dependent on the section thickness and volume. Thi s leads to comparatively slower 87 
cooling rates that lead to less nucleation and a lo wer number density of secondary γ′ 88 
precipitates. Additionally, the morphology of secon dary γ′ precipitates that form during 89 
the slow cooling rate can be highly irregular as th e morphology gradually evolves from 90 
sphere to cube, octo-cube, octo-dendritic or dendri tic [22,28]. This is largely due to the 91

As Ni-base superalloys are being used at ever incre asing temperatures to extend 97 
the performance and efficiency of advanced gas turb ines, understanding the mechanisms 98 
by which creep and fatigue interact in polycrystall ine structures is becoming increasingly 99 
important. Ideally, a polycrystalline Ni-base super alloy microstructure containing a fine 100 
dispersion of secondary γ′ precipitates combined with a small fraction of gra in boundary 101 
γ′ precipitates that serrate and mechanically interlo ck the grain boundaries would exhibit 102 
optimal resistance to high temperature deformation mechanisms. Although a continuous 103 
slow cooling rate will result in the formation of s errated grain boundaries. This will also 104 
lead to the development of coarse secondary γ′ precipitates in the entire microstructure 105 
which limits the strength as shown in many in many previous studies [21,35,36]. On the 106 
other hand, rapid cooling is able to form the fine dispersion of secondary γ′ precipitates to 107 
confer strength. However, the rapid cooled microstr ucture will lack the coarser grain 108 
boundary precipitates that mechanically serrate the boundaries and inhibit grain boundary 109 
sliding during creep. Thus, conventional thermal pr ocesses are not able to achieve this 110 
ideal microstructure and new innovative processes a re required to engineer desired 111 
features within the microstructure. 112 
The purpose of this investigation was to study the effect on a two-step or 113 
interrupted cooling rate on the formation of γ′ precipitates within the microstructure. The 114 
introduction and control of two different cooling rates, where the transition temperature is 115 
below the solvus temperature, could potentially be used to populate the microstructure 116 
with both coarse grain boundary γ′ precipitates to serrate the grain boundaries and f ine 117 
secondary γ′ precipitates to confer strength. The initial cooli ng rate from above the 118 
solution temperature will result in limited heterog eneous nucleation of γ′ precipitates 119 
along the grain boundaries that will form serrations as growth occurs. This will transition 120 
into a comparatively faster cooling rate to allow p recipitation of another population of γ′ 121 
precipitates. While many studies have investigated the effect of continuous cooling rate 122 
on microstructure development in Ni-base superalloy s [22,23,34,37], few studies exist 123 
where the cooling process is intentionally staggere d in such a manner. Two experimental 124 
high Nb content Ni-base superalloys were used in th is study to investigate the effect of 125 
compositions on the development of the microstructu re during the interrupted cooling 126 
process. 127

4. Discussion: 462 
 463 
Modified thermal processes possess the potential to modify and optimize the 464 
microstructure of advanced Ni-base superalloys and contribute to the enhancement of 465 
mechanical properties. For many polycrystalline Ni- base superalloys, understanding and 466 
controlling the cooling processes from above the so lvus temperature plays an important 467 
role in modifying and altering the microstructure w here they impact and control the 468 
morphology, number density, number of populations ( burst), and the size distribution of 469 
γ′ precipitates. A stepped cooling process with a transition just below the γ′ solvus has the 470 
ability to form a unique microstructure. During the initially slow cooling rate, 471 
heterogeneous nucleation and growth of large γ′ precipitates along the grain boundaries 472 
contribute to the formation of serrated grain bound aries. The eventual transition to a 473 
standard or conventional cooling rate will then gov ern the formation of the secondary γ′ 474 
precipitates. Compared to the conventional continuo us cooling process, the 475 
microstructure of the stepped cooled samples may po tentially extend the temperature 476 
capability of the alloy as the serrated grain bound ary structure is likely to inhibit grain 477 
boundary sliding mechanisms [30,31,38-40]. 478 
The γ′ solvus temperature for RRHT3 and RRHT4 superalloys are provided in Table 479 
2 where RRHT3 was measured to possess a slightly lo wer solvus temperature than 480 
RRHT4 by ~5°. Since Co additions to Ni-base supera lloys are known to affect the γ′ 481 
solvus temperature and destabilize the γ′ phase, it is likely that this difference can be 482 
attributed to the higher Co content in RRHT3 alloy [41-43]. Moreover, Antonov et al., 483 
[44] investigated the partitioning of elements in t he γ-γ′ of RRHT3 and RRHT4 by APT 484 
(Atom Probe tomography) and showed that the composi tional differences exist between 485 
the γ′ phase in RRHT3 and RRHT4 which is responsible for the slight reduction in the γ′ 486 
solvus temperature. 487 
All three cooling treatments investigated led to di fferences in the resulting 488 
microstructures where the γ′ precipitate distributions and morphologies was cle arly 489 
modified and altered. For the baseline continuous c ooling specimen, the intermediate 490 
cooling rate (1˚C/s) produced a microstructure larg ely containing secondary γ′ with 491 
irregular cuboidal morphologies and a small fraction of relatively coarser precipitates that 492 
decorate the grain boundaries, Figure 2. From the s uper-solvus temperature, the driving 493 
force for γ′ nucleation increases with undercooling. Since nucl eation of grain boundary γ′ 494 
precipitates occurs heterogeneously, only a minimal undercooling is required. 495 
Conversely, nucleation of secondary γ′ occurs homogeneously and requires a more 496 
significant undercooling where the rate of cooling is inversely proportional to nucleation 497 
temperature but is proportional to nucleation rate [21]. Once nucleated, the γ′ precipitates 498 
grow during the remainder of the cooling process wh ile temperatures are still sufficiently 499 
high enough to enable diffusion and atomic mobility . This is consistent with the larger 500 
average size of grain boundary γ′ precipitates observed in RRHT3 and RRHT4 which 501 
were measured to be 0.5 µm and 0.65 µm, respectively. Compared to the average size of 502 
the secondary γ′ of 0.23 µm for RRHT3 and 0.24 µm for RRHT4, the grain boundary γ′ 503 
precipitates are over twice the size in the continu ous cooled specimens. In select 504 
instances, grain boundary precipitates are desirabl e as they also result in the formation of 505

For both of the two-step cooling processes, the ini tial cooling rate was intentionally 509

nucleation of a high density of secondary γ′ precipitates. The resulting size distribution of 521 
the secondary γ′ precipitates for the three conditions investigated were similar, but 522 
modest changes were noted. As expected, comparison of the two-step cooling treatments, 523 
revealed that increasing the size and volume fracti on of the grain boundary γ′ precipitates 524

initial rate of 0.01˚C/s possessed the largest grain boundary γ′ precipitates paired with the 527 
smallest secondary precipitates. This is consistent with the slightly higher γ′ solvus 528 
temperature associated with RRHT4 which gave the gr ain boundary γ′ precipitates more 529 
time to grow during the initial cooling. The increa se of the volume fraction of γ′ 530 
precipitates during the initial cooling will partia lly consume some of the γ′ forming 531 
elements in the matrix and affect the ability of th e matrix to form a supersaturated 532 
solution. As a result, a higher undercooling is required to drive nucleation of secondary γ′ 533 
and leads to the formation of a higher number densi ty of smaller secondary γ′ 534 
precipitates. It should be noted that the large maj ority of the grain boundary γ′ 535 
precipitates exhibit an elliptical or elongated blo cky morphology. Their sizes increase by 536 
expanding along the grain boundary and thickening i nto the parent grain where the 537 
interface appears to be coherent. The presence of g rowth ledges were noted to be present 538 
along the coherent parent grain and grain boundary γ′ interface. The opposite interface of 539 
the grain boundary γ′ precipitate was determined to be incoherent with t he grain 540 
boundary and exhibit a smooth, nearly planar interf ace, Figure 4a, 4c, 6a, 6c and more 541 
clearly shown in the deep etched sample Figure 10. It is probable that the enhanced 542 
diffusivity along the grain boundary allows the γ′ precipitate to extend its length along the 543 
grain boundary at a faster rate than its thickness which will be largely dependent on 544 
diffusion across the γ- γ′ interface. 545

Various studies have shown that serrated grain boundaries provide polycrystalline Ni-554

This interlocking mechanism will make it more diffi cult for grain boundary sliding 557 
mechanisms to occur during high temperature creep. Growth of the grain boundary γ′ 558 
precipitates during cooling from super-solvus tempe ratures resulted in formation of 559 
protrusions along segments of the grain boundary th at are in contact with the precipitate. 560 
Slower cooling rates allow the grain boundary γ′ precipitate time to grow more 561 
extensively which increases the magnitude of the pr otrusions along the grain boundary 562 
[31,34]. The modestly sized grain boundary γ′ precipitates in the baseline continuous 563 
cooling process were only able to produce slight pr otrusions along the grain boundaries. 564

wavelengths. It is essential to mention that not al l grain boundaries have serrated 574 
morphology as shown in Figures 8 and 9 which indica te the effect of the grain boundary 575

Understanding the effect of grain boundary misorien tation on the heterogeneous 577 
nucleation behavior of grain boundary γ′ precipitates will provide insight on the 578 
mechanisms that govern the formation of serrated gr ain boundaries. In general, the 579 
misorientation angle of grain boundary has an impac t on the grain boundary energy 580 
where the increase in the misorientation angle incr eases the grain boundary excess free 581 
energy. As a result of the increased excess free e nergy of the grain boundary, a lower 582 
energy for nucleation and increased diffusivity due to the higher vacancy concentration in 583 
those distorted boundaries can be expected [46-48]. Detailed characterization and 584 
statistical assessment of the distribution of grain boundary γ′ precipitates showed that the 585 
average size of grain boundary γ′ precipitates was related to the grain boundary 586 
misorientation. As shown in Figure 12, grain bounda ries with low angles (less than 10°) 587 
did not incur any precipitation of coarse γ′ . This is likely due to low angle grain 588 
boundaries exhibiting comparatively lower interfaci al energies [49,50] and a decreased 589 
driving force for heterogeneous nucleation during c ooling. The energy associated with 590 
low angle grain boundaries increases with increasin g misorientation due to the grain 591 
boundary structure becoming more distorted, thereby leading to an increase in the excess 592 
free volume. This is consistent with studies showin g the effect of misorientation angle on 593 
the grain boundary energy for tilt and twist bounda ries where results consistently show 594 
comparatively higher energies for random high angle grain boundaries with cusps at 595 
special grain boundaries (CSL) and low energy for l ow angle grain boundaries [50]. In 596 
the present investigation, coarse grain boundary γ′ precipitates were found to form 597

To summarize, controlling the initial cooling rate of super-solvus processed 610

size of the grain boundary γ′ precipitates, especially when the transition tempe rature is 612 
slightly below the γ′ solvus temperature. Compared to continuous cooling using a 1˚C/s 613 
rate, stepped cooling where a slow initial rate of 0.1˚C/s or 0.01˚C/s is maintained 614 
through the γ′ solvus temperature enables the formation of large grain boundary γ′ 615 
precipitates that grow and serrate the grain bounda ry morphologies before the nucleation 616 
of the secondary γ′ precipitates. Once the grain boundaries are serrat ed, transitioning to a 617 
faster cooling rate of 1˚C/s can be used to control the size and density of the secondary γ′ 618 
precipitates. Utilizing a stepped cooling rate may potentially enable further 619 
microstructural tailoring to modify properties of t he polycrystalline Ni-base superalloys. 620 
By carefully controlling and optimizing the cooling rates and transition temperatures, 621 
desirable meso-scale grain boundary features can be introduced into the microstructure 622 
that can potentially extend the temperature capabil ity without drastically impacting the 623 
other intrinsic attributes of the alloy. 624
