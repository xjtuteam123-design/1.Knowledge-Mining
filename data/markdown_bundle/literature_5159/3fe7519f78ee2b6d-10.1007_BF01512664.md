WEAR OF TUNGSTENLESS HARD ALLOYS BASED ON 
SHS-TITANIUM CARBIDE 
S. Yu. Sharivker, I. P. Golovinskaya, 
I. N. Vorob'eva, and V. M. Bunin 
UDC 669.295.5:621.762 
The structure, porosity, strength, and wear resistance of tungstenless hard alloys based on titanium carbide 
are investigated. Titanium carbide is produced by self-propagating high-temperature synthesis (SHS). The 
strength of this alloy and normal hard alloy of similar composition is about the same, but SHS alloy exhibits 
higher wear resistance. The use of SHS titanium carbide alloyed with molybdenum results in an increase in 
strength. Addition of molybdenum to the nickel binder results in a twofoM increase in wear resistance. 
The technology of self-propagating high-temperature synthesis (SHS) makes it possible to prepare titanium carbide of 
high quality [1] although the features of forming the properties and structure of hard alloys based on this refractory compound 
have not been studied sufficiently. To a certain extent this holds back extensive industrial application of SHS titanium carbide 
whose production is distinguished by considerable savings and corresponds to modern ecological requirements. 
The authors of the present study have examined the structure, porosity, strength, and wear resistance of this material. 
The studies involved titanium carbide (TU 48-42-6-84) and alloyed molybdenum carbide Tio,97Moo,03C0,05, whose basic 
properties are described in [2]. Both carbides were prepared by the SHS method in the Institute of Structural Microkinetics 
of the Russian Academy of Sciences. After synthesis the carbides were crushed in a jaw crusher to a size of less than 1 mm 
(their composition after this operation is shown in Table 1) and then they were ground with powders of nickel PNI~-I (GOST 
7922-79) and molybdenum (TU 48-19-316-80). 
In order to improve the properties of tungstenless hard alloy based on SHS carbides it is recommended that traditional 
hard alloy technology is substituted by dry grinding of carbide in a ball mill with a weight ratio of balls to charge of 5:1. This 
operation lasts 26 h and is carried out before normal grinding-mixing in ethanol for 96-144 h. As a result of the different 
treatment the size of carbide particles is naturally reduced. However, this is insufficient for direct use of the powder in a hard 
alloy charge since d90 (particle size, whose number in an analyzed sample exceeds 90%) is 8-10/zm (Fig. 1)*. Probably it 
is more important that with dry grinding in large carbide particles defects accumulate and during wet grinding they are broken 
more effectively: d90 is reduced to 1.01 txm (with absence of the additional operation d90 = 1.65/.tm). The strength of hard 
alloy given prior dry grinding increases by 15-20%. 
In other respects the authors maintained the traditional hard alloy technology: after drying a charge it was plastified 
by introducing 3% solution of rubber in benzene, granulated, passed through a screen, pressed at a pressure of 50-70 MPa, 
and then sintered in a vacuum with burning off the rubber at 600°C and subsequent sintering for 1 h at 1050 and 1360-1400°C. 
The properties of the alloy obtained were compared with the similar characteristics of standard tungstenless hard alloy 
TN-20 prepared by the Svetlovodsk Hard Alloy Combine. Strength in bending was measured in unground specimens with a 
size of 36 x 5 x 5 mm and fragments of these specimens were used for wear testing by the procedure given in [3]. The 
friction pair was the end of a small bar of test material and a counterbody 50 mm in diameter and 10 thick with a diamond- 
containing coating AS6 125/100 in a ceramic binder over the outer diameter. The load during testing was 30 N and the sliding

Given in Fig. 2 is the structure of standard hard alloy TN-20 (a) and one of the alloys prepared by the authors (b). As 
can be seen, both materials are structurally similar and fine-grained. In our view in hard alloy based on SHS-carbide the carbide 
particles are more faceted. 
The strength in bending for alloy TN-20 (Fig. 3) corresponds to published data [4], but published information of 
wear in friction in a pair with a diamond roller has not been found by the authors. The strength of prepared alloys CBC-TiC 
+ 16% Ni + 5% Mo, and analog of TN-20, showed that at the level of a prototype the wear resistance is a little higher. 
Probably the wear resistance of hard alloy is a property integrating the mechanical characteristics of the carbide and metal 
binder. Since the binder in comparable alloys is the same, it was concluded that further improvement of the wear resistance 
is possible with optimization of the ratio of component properties. The authors suggested that a step in this direction is 
substitution of SHS-TiC by SHS-(Ti, Mo)C. Since in SHS-(Ti, Mo)C there is about 5% Mo as a binder only 16% Ni was

An increase in molybdenum in the binder on the whole was accompanied by a reduction in overall alloy strength 
although its wear resistance increased. The authors managed to reduce wear by more than a factor of two for this alloy 
compared with TN-20 with a molybdenum content in the hard alloy charge based on (Ti, Mo)C equal to 13%. The low level 
of properties of prepared hard alloys with a 2% molybdenum content in the binder (Fig. 3) may be explained by an increase 
in porosity: this reached 0.8-1.5%, whereas in the rest of the alloys it did not exceed 0.5%. Additional optimization of alloy 
preparation technology is possibly required in this case. 
Thus, use of SHS-titanium carbide makes it possible to obtain tungstenless hard alloys with high wear resistance, and 
alloying carbide during synthesis and the metal binder during alloy preparation makes it possible to improve markedly this 
index.
