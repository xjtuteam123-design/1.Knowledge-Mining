Effects of Chromium Content, Temperature and Strain
Rate on Microstructure and Mechanical Properties
of As-Cast AlCoCr xFeNi2.2 High-Entropy Alloys
Mingyun Yu, Tingjun Song, Jingan Chen, Peipei Sun, Shijing Yan, Jinming Zhu, and Cailiu Yin
Submitted: 24 September 2024 / Revised: 6 May 2025 / Accepted: 6 June 2025
A series of AlCoCr xFeNi2.2 (x = 0, 0.3, 0.7, 1.1, 1.5, 1.9, 2.3) high-entropy alloys were designed and pre-
pared. The effects of the Cr content on the microstructures and mechanical properties of the AlCoCr x-
FeNi2.2 alloys were studied. All the alloys consisted of face-centered cubic (FCC) and body-centered cubic
(BCC) phases. The Cr-free AlCoFeNi 2.2 alloy exhibited a ﬁne alternating lamellar eutectic structure, which
became coarser with increasing Cr content. When x = 2.3, the lamellar eutectic structure disappears. Room
temperature uniaxial tensile tests revealed signiﬁcant changes in the mechanical properties with increasing
Cr content. For 0 £ x £ 1.1, with increasing Cr content, the yield strength and hardness of the alloy
decrease, whereas the ductility increases. Conversely, for 1.1 £ x £ 2.3, increasing the Cr content re-
sulted in a higher yield strength and hardness of the alloy, accompanied by a decrease in ductility. The
AlCoCr0.7FeNi2.2 alloy exhibited superior overall mechanical properties at room temperature. The tensile
yield strength, hardness, and elongation were 472.9 MPa, 294.8 HV , and 26.3%, respectively. Uniaxial
tensile tests on the AlCoCr
0.7FeNi2.2 alloy at various temperatures and strain rates revealed that the
strength increased with increasing temperature and decreasing strain rate. A mathematical description of
the effects of temperature and strain rate on the strength of an alloy can be given by the Arrhenius-type
equation. These results demonstrate that the AlCoCr
0.7FeNi2.2 alloy maintains excellent properties even at
elevated temperatures.
Keywords elevated temperature tensile, high-entropy alloys, hot
processing map, mechanical properties, microstructure
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), also referred to as multi-
principal element alloys (MPEAs), have attracted extensive
interest from researchers since the concept of HEAs was
proposed by Yeh et al. (Ref 1) and Cantor et al. (Ref 2) in 2004.
The high-entropy, lattice distortion, sluggish diffusion, and
cocktail effect due to multi-principal element mixtures are the
core factors affecting the microstructure and properties of high-
entropy alloys (Ref 3). Compared with traditional alloys, HEAs
have a series of superior properties, including high strength
(Ref 4), high hardness (Ref 1), corrosion resistance (Ref 5, 6),
and high resistivity (Ref 7, 8). The type of crystallographic
structure is a primary factor affecting the properties of HEAs.
Body-centered cubic (BCC) HEAs typically exhibit high yield
strength and limited ductility, whereas face-centered cubic
(FCC) HEAs generally have lower yield strength but greater
ductility. The BCC + FCC dual-phase structure offers a balance
between high strength and good ductility (Ref 9). Nonetheless,
merely combining the FCC and BCC phases without meticu-
lous structural design cannot resolve this issue (Ref 10).
Furthermore, certain HEAs with simple structures often
encounter challenges, such as inferior castability and elemental
segregation. These issues invariably degrade their mechanical
properties, thereby hindering their widespread application in
engineering applications (Ref 11, 12).
To achieve a balance between strength and ductility, Lu
et al. (Ref 13) employed the concept of eutectic alloys in
designing HEAs. They proposed the AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 eutectic
HEA, which not only exhibits good castability and ﬂuidity but
also addresses the challenge of balancing strength and ductility.
Researchers subsequently conducted extensive investigations
on eutectic HEAs, leading to the discovery of a growing
number of such compositions, such as CrFeNi
2Al (Ref 14),
Al0.9Cr0.8FeMn0.8Ni2.0 (Ref 15), and Co 30Cr10Fe10Al18Ni30-
Mo2 (Ref 16). The AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 alloy has been conﬁrmed to
have superior mechanical properties across a wide temperature
range from 77 to 1000 K (Ref 17). Research has shown that the
AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 alloy features a complete eutectic structure and
outstanding mechanical properties, whereas the AlCoCrFeNi 2.2
alloy, with a hypereutectic structure containing a certain
amount of primary phase, demonstrates superior mechanical
properties (Ref 18). As a potential alloy, studying the mechan-
ical properties of AlCoCrFeNi 2.2 at elevated temperatures is
necessary for comprehensively understanding its performance.

Typically, variations in the elemental composition of alloys
can lead to microstructural alterations, which in turn affect their
properties. Cr is strongly stable in the BCC phase and has the
potential to form a sigma phase in alloys. Therefore, the Cr
content should be reasonably adjusted to optimize the perfor-
mance of the alloys (Ref 19).
Hamzah et al. (Ref 20) reported that the solid solution
strengthening of Cr in the phase has a signiﬁcant effect on the
tensile properties. Liao et al. (Ref 21) revealed that the addition
of Cr to the Fe
30Ni20Mn35Al15 alloy could inhibit the
environmental embrittlement that occurs in Cr-free alloys at
low strain rates. Jin et al. (Ref 22) reported that all the alloys in
the AlCoCr
xFeNi2 (1.0 £ x £ 2.4) HEAs consisted of FCC
and BCC/B2 phases, and the strength of the alloys varied
nonmonotonically with increasing Cr content. The stability of
the phases and structural transformation can be characterized by
calculating the distribution ratio of Cr in the FCC/(BCC + B2)
regions. Jiang et al. (Ref 23) observed that in AlCoCr xFeNi2.1
(0 £ x £ 1.0) HEAs, a change in Cr content induces solid
solution strengthening of the primary structure and eutectic
composite strengthening of the eutectic structure, which are
crucial for strengthening the alloys. Moreover, it can still
maintain high strength under high-temperature compression at
700 /C176 C. Cr is an important constituent element in many HEAs,
making it essential to research the impact of its content on alloy
properties.
In this study, an AlCoFeNi
2.2 eutectic HEA with a ﬁne
alternating lamellar structure was designed and prepared. The
Cr content was gradually increased to investigate its effect on
the microstructure and mechanical properties of the AlCoCr
x-
FeNi2.2 HEAs. Additionally, the effects of temperature and
strain rate on the tensile properties of the AlCoCr 0.7FeNi2.2
HEA, which has excellent comprehensive performance, were
further researched.

The AlCoCr xFeNi2.2 (x = 0, 0.3, 0.7, 1.1, 1.5, 1.9, and 2.3)

respectively. The purity of the metallic materials involved in the
experiment was not less than 99.95 (wt.%). The cast ingots
were remelted at least six times to achieve chemical homo-
geneity. The size of each ingot was 30 9 10 9 80 mm

sample was held at the target temperature for approximately 10
min. Each test condition was tested 3 /C0 6 times to ensure the

and a load of 1000 g was applied for 15 s. Each sample was
measured at a minimum of 10 different points, and the resulting
experimental data were averaged to obtain the ﬁnal hardness

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Microstructure and Phase Identiﬁcation

AlCoCrxFeNi2.2 alloys, along with local magniﬁcations of the
(111) diffraction peak of the FCC phase and the (110)

does not affect the phase composition nor noticeably affect the
size of the crystal plane spacing. As the Cr content increases,
the intensity of the (111) diffraction peak of the FCC phase ﬁrst
tends to increase and then decreases, whereas that of the (110)
diffraction peak of the BCC phase tends to decrease and then
increases. This observation indicates that Cr acts as a stabilizer
of the FCC phase at lower concentrations, whereas at higher

The lattice parameter of the FCC lattice ﬁrst increases and then
decreases, whereas that of the BCC lattice ﬁrst decreases and
then increases. Moreover, the magnitudes of both the increase
and decrease are relatively small. The primary reason for the
gradual changes in the lattice parameters of the two different
lattice types is the lattice distortion effect inherent in HEAs
(Ref 3). Owing to the characteristics of HEAs, which contain
multiple distinct elements, when the difference between the
radius of the introduced atoms and that of the intrinsic elements
is large, this effect becomes more pronounced. As a result, the
magnitude of the change in the lattice constants becomes more
pronounced, which is reﬂected in the XRD patterns as shifts in
the corresponding diffraction peaks. However, the difference
between the atomic radius of the Cr atom (1.28 A ˚ ) and that of
the other elements in the alloy is small, so its addition does not
signiﬁcantly amplify the lattice distortion effect. Therefore, the
changes in the lattice parameters of the two lattice types in the
AlCoCr
xFeNi2.2 alloy are limited, which is reﬂected in the
small shifts in the positions of the corresponding diffraction
peaks in the XRD patterns.
Figure 2(a)-(g) shows SEM images of the microstructures of
the as-cast AlCoCr
xFeNi2.2 alloys with various Cr content. The
Cr0 alloy exhibited an alternating lamellar eutectic structure,
consisting of regular lamellar structures with a minor presence
of irregularly shaped structures. With increasing Cr content, the
eutectic regions gradually become coarse. The Cr0.3 alloy
consists of a primary phase and eutectic mixtures. When the Cr
content is x ‡ 1.1, Cr starts to act as a promoter of BCC phase
formation, and ﬁne precipitates begin to appear. After that, the
Cr content continues to increase, the volume fraction of the
BCC phase also increases, the precipitation growth and
quantity increase, and the solid solution strengthening effect
is enhanced. When the Cr content x = 1.9, the precipitation
disappears. As shown in Table 2, the ﬁne precipitates were
analyzed by EDS point analysis, revealing that the elemental
composition content was almost identical to that of region B.
When the Cr content reached x = 2.3, the two phases were
redistributed, and the lamellar structure disappeared completely.
The EDS point analysis in Table 2, reveals that region A is
composed primarily of Co, Cr, Fe, and Ni, whereas region B
consists predominantly of Ni and Al. The chemical mixing
enthalpies of atomic pairs between the elements used in the
experiment are listed in Table 3 (Ref 26). Among the elements
involved in this experiment, the mixing enthalpy of Ni and Al is
the most negative, facilitating the formation of stable atom pairs
and the NiAl-rich phase. In agreement with Jin et al. (Ref 22),
the Al content in the FCC phase is lower than that in the BCC
phase, indicating that region A corresponds to the FCC phase
and that region B corresponds to the BCC phase. Figure 2(h)
shows the volume fractions of the FCC and BCC phases, which
were analyzed and statistically derived from the difference in
color contrast between the FCC and BCC phases in the SEM
images using ImageJ software. With increasing Cr content, the
volume fraction of the FCC phase ﬁrst increases but then
decreases, whereas the volume fraction of the BCC phase ﬁrst
decreases but then increases. These results are consistent with
the XRD analyses.
3.2 Mechanical Properties
3.2.1 Mechanical Properties of AlCoCr xFeNi2.2 Alloys
at Room Temperature. Figure 3(a) shows the tensile
engineering stress–strain curves of the as-cast AlCoCr xFeNi2.2
alloy at a strain rate of 0.5 mm/min at room temperature. The
curves of the yield strength, ultimate strength, and elongation
with increasing Cr content are shown in Fig. 3(b). The analysis
revealed that as the Cr content increased, the yield strength of
the alloy decreased from 674.7 MPa in the Cr0 alloy to
451.0 MPa in the Cr1.1 alloy, and then increased to 797.0 MPa
in the Cr2.3 alloy. Conversely, ductility exhibited the opposite
trend, increasing from 15.2% in the Cr0 alloy to 26.5% in the
Cr1.1 alloy and then decreasing to 8.3% in the Cr2.3 alloy. This
demonstrates typical strength-ductility trade-off behavior (Ref
27, 28). The Cr content affects the yield strength of the alloys
mainly by softening the FCC phase and increasing the degree
of atomic bonding. As indicated by the EDS point analysis in

the FCC phase, reducing the segregation of Shockley partial
dislocations. This results the FCC phase being more susceptible
to cross-slip, thereby reducing the length of the dislocation
plugging, leading to a reduction in yield strength (Ref 29). With
a further increase in Cr content, the Cr-induced increase in
atomic bonding begins to play a dominant role, which in turn
leads to increased cohesive strength and solid solution
strengthening at the boundaries of the FCC and BCC phases
(Ref 20, 30).
The SEM images of the tensile fracture surfaces of the as-
cast AlCoCr
xFeNi2.2 alloys at room temperature are presented
in Fig. 4. V arious fracture mechanisms are clearly present in the
as-cast AlCoCr xFeNi2.2 alloys. Speciﬁcally, an increase in the
Cr content leads to a transition from ductile to ductile–brittle
mixed fracture. This observation aligns with ﬁndings reported
for other eutectic alloys, such as AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 (Ref 18, 31)
and AlCrFeNi 3 (Ref 32). Inﬂuenced by the microstructure and
relative ductility of the material itself, the dimples exhibit
different morphologies and sizes. The fracture surface of the
Cr0 alloy is depicted in Fig. 4(a). Owing to the more lamellar
eutectic structure of the Cr0 alloy, the BCC phase remains
almost undeformed, whereas the FCC lamellae between the
BCC phases are stretched, resulting in numerous ductile
interlamellar fractures. Dimples are evident in the irregularly
shaped structure region. Therefore, its mechanism of tensile
fracture is ductile fracture. The fracture surfaces of Cr0.3,
Cr0.7, and Cr1.1 alloys are depicted in Fig. 4(b)-(d), respec-
tively. The number of dimples gradually increases with
increasing FCC phase content, and the dimple surface also
tends to gradually increase. As a whole, the fracture surface
consists of dimples accompanied by partial ductile interlamellar
fractures, indicating a ductile fracture mechanism. Figure 4(e)
shows the fracture surface of the Cr1.5 alloy. Owing to the
coarser eutectic structure, the BCC phase content increases,
leading to a decrease in the number of dimples compared with
that of the Cr1.1 alloy, and wider ductile interlamellar fractures
are observed. The fracture surface consists predominantly of
dimples, tearing ridges, cleavage steps, and a small number of
ductile interlamellar fractures, indicating a ductile–brittle mixed
fracture mechanism. Figure 4(f) shows the fracture surface of
the Cr1.9 alloy, where a river pattern begins to emerge on the
fracture surface, accompanied by tearing ridges and a few
dimples. The tensile fracture mechanism also falls within the
ductile–brittle mixed fracture. Figure 4(g) shows the fracture
surface of the Cr2.3 alloy. The fracture surface is relatively ﬂat
and consists of tearing ridges with few dimples. Similarly, the
tensile fracture mechanism of this alloy is classiﬁed as ductile–
brittle mixed fracture.
Figure 5 presents the variation curve of the hardness with
the Cr content of the as-cast AlCoCr
xFeNi2.2 alloy. Notably, as
the Cr content increases, the alloy hardness initially decreases
from 310.0 HV in the Cr0 alloy to 275.6 HV in the Cr1.1 alloy,
followed by an increase to 395.1 HV in the Cr2.3 alloy. The
hardness variation curves of the FCC and BCC phases indicate
that the hardness of the BCC phase is signiﬁcantly greater than
that of the FCC phase and that the hardness of the FCC phase
exhibits minimal variation with the Cr content. Therefore, it can
be inferred that the hardness of the alloy is attributed primarily
to the BCC phase. As shown in Fig. 3(b), the trend of hardness
variation with Cr content aligns with the trend in yield strength
variation. Additionally, the variation in the hardness of an alloy
is closely associated with its microstructure. Figure 2(h) reveals
that the variation in the volume fraction of the BCC phase also
induces changes in the hardness of the alloy. Figure 6 shows a
comparison of the tensile yield strength and ductility of the
AlCoCr
xFeNi2.2 alloys in this study with those of other
conventional alloys and HEAs (Ref 24, 33–37). AlCoCr xFe-
Ni2.2 alloys exhibit better tensile properties than some conven-
tional alloys do, with slightly lower ductility but higher yield
strength than other HEAs do. Moreover, among the AlCoCr
x-
FeNi2.2 alloys, the Cr0.7 alloy demonstrates the optimum
strength-ductility trade-off.

4. Conclusion
In summary, AlCoCr xFeNi2.2 (x = 0, 0.3, 0.7, 1.1, 1.5, 1.9
and 2.3) alloys were prepared in this study. An investigation of
the microstructure and mechanical properties of the AlCoCr x-
FeNi2.2 alloys at room temperature and the tensile properties of
the AlCoCr0.7FeNi2.2 alloys at different temperatures and strain
rates was carried out. The following conclusions were obtained:
(1) As-cast AlCoCr xFeNi2.2 alloys consist of FCC and BCC
phases. The Cr0 alloy has an alternating lamellar eutec-
tic structure, which becomes coarser, decreases with
increasing Cr content, and disappears completely when
x = 2.3.
(2) The yield strength and hardness of the AlCoCr
xFeNi2.2
alloys at room temperature initially decrease but then in-
crease with increasing Cr content. In contrast, the ductil-
ity shows the opposite trend, initially increasing and
then decreasing. This overall change pattern in strength
and ductility demonstrates typical strength-ductility
trade-off behavior.

of an alloy can be expressed mathematically by an
