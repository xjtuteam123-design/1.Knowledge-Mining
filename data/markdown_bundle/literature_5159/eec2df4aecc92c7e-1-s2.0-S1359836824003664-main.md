1. Introduction 
As the aerospace industry develops, the materials required for next 
generation aero-engines will encounter higher temperatures and harsher 
environments, which will necessitate them to be lighter, stronger, and 
more thermally stable [ 1 , 2 ]. Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), 
mainly composed of multiple principal elements in the 
high-melting-point family Ti – Zr-Hf-V-Nb-Ta-Cr-Mo-W [ 3 ], have shown 
exceptional high-temperature mechanical properties, specifically 
compressive strength retention at elevated temperatures [ 4 – 6 ] 
(although their tensile properties are often inferior [ 7 ]). Since most re -
ported RHEAs offer a good strength retention above 800 
◦
C (some even 
up to 1600 
◦

to replace Ni-base superalloys. However, most reported RHEAs have a 
trade-off between high-temperature strength and light weight – the 
heavy-weight RHEAs containing elements such as W, Mo, and Ta 
invariably exhibit the better high-temperature strength, whereas 
light-weight RHEAs containing more low-density elements, such as Ti, 
V, and Zr, show inferior high-temperature mechanical properties. To 
address this trade-off, a promising method is to enhance the

2. Materials and experimental procedures 
2.1. Materials design strategy 
Ti, Nb, and Mo are relatively light elements for elevated-temperature 
alloys. As Ti has the lowest density of these elements, the Ti-rich RCCAs 
would effectively solve the high-density problem [ 23 ]; accordingly, a 
high content of 40 at.% Ti was firstly considered. However, with such a 
composition, it is hard to exceed a service temperature of 600 
◦
C [ 24 ], 
such that an excessive fraction of Ti in the alloy system would be 
detrimental to the high-temperature mechanical properties. Nb and Mo 
are β -stabilizer elements, which would guarantee phase stability and 
prevent unstable phase decomposition in Zr-rich RHEAs/RCCAs [ 25 ]. 
High Mo-containing RHEAs often present excellent high-temperature 
strength originating from the large electronegativity of Mo and resul -
tant higher electronegativity difference in the alloy system [ 26 ]. Thus, it 
is imperative to carefully regulate the distribution of Ti and Mo to 
achieve an appropriate combination of high-temperature strength and 
light weight. Based on our recent study [ 10 ], the replacement of Ti by Nb 
in NbC is energetically favorable, such that a high content of NbC was 
selected as a raw material. Using the in situ reaction between Ti and NbC, 
more light Ti can be transferred into the strong ceramic phase with an 
improvement of Mo in the matrix, although as noted above, the higher Ti 
leads to inferior high-temperature strength. After this in situ element 
repartition, however, the stability of microstructure can be improved 
which serves to enhance the high-temperature mechanical properties. 
Accordingly, for optimal properties, we designed a composite with the 
composition of Ti

4.1. Phase evolution 
The as-sintered Ti

high-energy ball milling, the metallic powders underwent severe plastic 
deformation, fragmentation, cold welding, and diffusion. The original 
metallic powders were refined, resulting a large amount of enthalpy 
stored in the grain boundaries. The stored energy can serve as driving 
force for the diffusion of solid solutes when sintered, promoting the 
formation of metallic solid-solution phase [ 47 ]. Compared to previous 
studies [ 10 ], the ball-milled powders, with addition of the hard NbC 
particles in present work, were further refined from an average size of 
4.8 μ m – 0.9 μ m ( Fig. S9 ). Therefore, it can be deduced that the hard NbC 
particles also contributed to the particle and grain refinement and the 
energy storage of the metallic powders. 
The generation of (Ti, Nb)C can be explained from the thermody -
namic perspective, during sintering, it is energetically favorable that Nb 
atoms can be substituted by Ti atoms which means a higher Ti content

As the introduction of a high content of reinforcement by a casting 
method often leads to aggregation, coarsening, inferior interfacial 
bonding, and poor stability of the reinforcements [ 9 , 11 , 52 ], powder 
metallurgy in the current study was regarded as an effect way to solve 
these problems. During the high energy ball-milling, the NbC particles 
were dispersed quite uniformly and surrounded by the metallic powders, 
which can serve as the nucleation sites of the (Ti, Nb)C phase. The 
evenly distributed NbC particles can further ensure the uniform distri -
bution of (Ti, Nb)C phase after sintering. 
As for the coarsening issue, the dual-phase, massively dispersed 
carbides (more than 40 vol%) in the interconnected microstructure act 
as the barrier for grain-boundary migration which can serve to hinder 
grain growth and avoid the coarsening. Moreover, the in situ reaction, 
defined in Eq. (2) , is related to the elemental exchange between the 
metallic and carbide powders, causing a sluggish effect in the kinetics 
and resultant grain refinement. In short, high content (Ti, Nb)C was 
introduced into the composite and dispersed homogeneously by powder 
metallurgy, effectively avoiding any aggregation or coarsening. 
4.2. Strengthening mechanisms 
The excellent mechanical properties of the as-sintered Ti
40

30
(NbC)

with its high-content reinforcement phase, ultrafine grains and inter -

highly related to this microstructure. 
After this in situ element repartition, the content of Mo in the bcc 
phase was increased, which is beneficial to high-temperature mechani -
cal performance of the composite [ 26 ], leading to a higher slip resistance 
at elevated temperatures. The strong dislocation interactions in the bcc

this. Added to this is that the reinforcement phase (Ti, Nb)C, can provide 
effective load-transfer strengthening effect. Compared with the hard 
reinforcement phase (Ti, Nb)C, the bcc matrix phase conducts plastic 
deformation firstly under the compressive stress. The increased stress is 
transferred through the phase boundaries to the reinforced phase to
