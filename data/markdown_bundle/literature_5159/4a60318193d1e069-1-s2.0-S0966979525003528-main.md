1. Introduction
Single-crystal superalloys have been widely used in manufacturing 
turbine blades for aero-engines due to their excellent high-temperature 
mechanical properties and creep resistance. With increasing demands 
for inlet temperatures and thrust-to-weight ratios in advanced aero- 
engines, refractory elements are increasingly incorporated into single- 
crystal superalloys to enhance their high-temperature performance. 
Rhenium (Re) is a commonly added element that preferentially parti -
tions to the γ phase, effectively reducing the γ / γ ′ lattice misfit and 
promoting the formation of dense γ / γ ′ interfacial dislocation networks 
(superior interface enhancements) during high-temperature creep, 
thereby significantly improving creep resistance [ 1 – 3 ]. Accordingly, the 
Re content is often used to classify superalloy generations: 
second-generation single-crystal superalloys contain ~3 wt% Re, 
third-generation ~6 wt% Re, and fourth-generation incorporate 5 – 7 wt. 
% Re with additional Ru. However, as a typical TCP (topologically 
close-packed) phase-forming element, excessive Re addition promotes 
TCP precipitation, which severely deteriorates mechanical properties 
[ 4 – 6 ]. To mitigate this, ruthenium (Ru) is introduced to suppress TCP 
formation [ 7 – 9 ]. Thus, in the design of single crystal superalloys, the 
combined addition of Re and Ru has become a common strategy for 
optimizing high-temperature creep properties.

the design, development, and fabrication of single-crystal turbine blades 
for next-generation aero-engines with high thrust-to-weight ratios.
