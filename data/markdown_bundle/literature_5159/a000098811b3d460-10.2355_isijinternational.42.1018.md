1. Introduction
In response to the demand for increasing gas turbine inlet
temperatures, Ni-based single crystal superalloys have been
used for turbine blades and vanes because of their excellent
high temperature properties.
1) It is generally accepted that
the temperature capability increases with decreasing Cr
content in the alloys.
2) In view of the alloy chemistry
among the 1st-, 2nd- and 3rd-generation single crystal (SC)
superalloys, there is a usual trend of lowering Cr content
and increasing Re content in them in order to improve the
high-temperature capability.
3) As a result, the 3rd-genera-
tion SC superalloys contain only 2 or 4 mass% Cr but in-
stead contain about 6 mass% Re, which is a great contrast
to the 1st-generation superalloys containing about 10
mass% Cr but no Re. Re is a unique element which can in-
crease both the high-temperature creep strength and the hot
corrosion resistance of superalloys.
4,5) In this study, an at-
tempt was made to see the prospect of those low Cr super-
alloys which contain a higher amount of Re than the 3rd-
generation superalloys. Even a Cr-free superalloy was ex-
amined in this respect. For comparison, three superalloys
were investigated in which both Cr and Re contents were
varied in a wide range.
2. Alloy Design
T wo groups of superalloys (hereafter referred to as A-
group and B-group) were made with the aid of d-electrons
concept.
6) According to this concept, two alloying parame-
ters were utilized. One was the bond order between an al-
loying element and nickel atom, Bo, and the other was the
d-orbital energy level of alloying element, Md.
7) The com-
positional average of these d-electrons parameters, Bo—– and
Md—– , are known to be related to the phase stability of nickel-
based superalloys.4) Also, the target region for alloy design
can be speciﬁed concretely in the Bo—– –Md—– map.4) For de-
signing the present superalloys, the Bo—– and Md—– values were
intended to keep as closely as possible to the values of the
target region (Md—– : 0.980, Bo—– : 0.665)

There is a clear trend of lowering Cr and increasing Re content in the evolution from the 1st- to the 2nd-
and to the 3rd-generation Ni-based single crystal (SC) superalloys. Following this trend, two groups of su-
peralloys were made with the aid of the d-electrons concept. One is the A-group which is characterized by
the lower Cr and the higher Re contents in the alloys than in the 3rd-generation SC alloys. A homogeneous
microstructure was obtained in these A-group superalloys containing 0, 1 and 2 mass% Cr, while their Re
and Co contents were set at about 8.5 mass% and 13.5 mass% respectively. The other is the B-group
which is characterized by the lower Re content than the A-group. Three alloys in the B-group containing 2.5,
4.2 and 12.1 mass% Cr were studied for the comparison with the A-group. According to a hot corrosion
test, weight gain occurred in a Cr-free alloy instead of observing weight loss in Cr containing alloys. Hot cor-
rosion resistance of 2 mass% Cr alloy was quite good as long as the alloy contains about 8.5 mass% Re.
The oxidation resistance was improved remarkably in 1 mass% Cr alloy than in the Cr-free alloy. It was
found that the addition of at least 2 mass% Cr into superalloys is necessary in order to keep good surface
stability at high temperatures. Also, it was seen that Re is a harmful element for oxidation resistance.

3. Experimental Procedure
3.1. Alloy Preparation
Button ingots of the designed alloys were prepared by
arc-melting under a puriﬁed Ar gas atmosphere. Each but-
ton ingot was cut into pieces and remelted three times in
order to get compositional homogeneity. The conditions for
the solution- and the aging-heat treatments were deter-
mined on the basis of the data obtained from the
Differential Thermal Analysis (DTA) of the alloys. The
heat treatment conditions are given in Table 2. The ingots
ﬁrst underwent the solution heat treatment and then fol-
lowed by the aging heat treatment. The volume fraction of
the 
g/H11032phase in each alloy was measured after the aging
heat treatment from the SEM images following a conven-
tional image analyzing method.
3.2. Hot Corrosion and Oxidation Tests
Both gravimetric and metallographic methods were em-
ployed to evaluate the extent of hot corrosion and oxidation
resistance of the experimental alloys.
3.2.1. Gravimetric Measurement 
Specimens for hot corrosion test were prepared by cut-
ting each alloy ingot and the surface was polished mechani-
cally using emery papers up to 600 grits. A coating
method
4) was employed for the hot corrosion test using the
specimens of 10 /H110035/H110031 mm in size. The specimen surface
was coated with a Na 2SO4–25wt%NaCl salt by 0.2 kg/m 2,
and then the specimen was put into a furnace for the ex-
posure to static air at 1 173 K for 72 ks. Corrosion prod-
ucts formed on the specimen surface were descaled with 
a brass-wire brush after boiling in a 18wt%NaOH–
5wt%KMnO
4 aqueous solution for 1.8 ks, followed by fur-
ther boiling in a 10wt%(NH 4)2HC6H5O7 aqueous solution
for 1.2 ks. The weight change of the specimen before and
after the corrosion test was measured and used as a measure
of hot corrosion resistance of the alloy. 
Also, oxidation test was conducted at 1 373 K for 86.4 ks
using the specimens of the same size as used for the hot
corrosion test but without employing any salt coating. The
weight change of the specimen before and after the oxida-
tion test was measured and used as a measure of oxidation
resistance of the alloy. 
3.2.2. Metallographic Measurement
Average thickness of the total corroded layer (external
layer/H11001internal layer) was measured on the characteristic X-
ray images of Al taken from the cross section of hot corrod-
ed specimens without any descaling treatment, because the
thickness of the inner layer was not clear in SEM images.
Total thickness of the corroded layer is deﬁned as the length
measured inwards from the external surface whose compo-
sition is different from the inherent alloy.
In a similar manner, average thickness of the total oxi-
dized layer was measured on the SEM images taken from
the cross section of oxidized specimens just after the expo-
sure. Total thickness of the oxidized layer is deﬁned as the
length measured inwards from the external surface.
3.3. Observation of Corrosion and Oxidation Products
Specimens just after the exposure (without any descaling
treatment) were taken for the observation of corrosion and
oxidation products. After the test, both sides of the corrod-
ed surface (10 /H110035 mm) were covered by Ni sheets so that
the corroded layer would not tear out during the subsequent
polishing. In order to observe the section perpendicularly,
the plate specimen was set up perpendicularly on the hori-
zontal base plane of a mounting instrument by using a clip
(BUEHLER SAMPL-KLIP NO. 20-4000-100) and then it
was mounted with conductive ﬁlled phenolic mounting
compound. The side of mounted specimen which was in
contact with the horizontal base plane of the mounting in-
strument was polished with abrasive papers, and then with
Al
2O3 powders down to 0.05 mm mesh. A carbon ﬁlm was
coated on the polished surface using a SC-701C type car-
bon coater. Subsequently, the cross-section of the corroded
layer was observed using a SEM operated at 20 kV and then
EDX analysis was performed in order to get characteristic
X-ray images of the corroded layer. 
Oxidation products formed on the oxidized specimen
were also observed in the same manner as used in the ob-
servation of corrosion products.

4.2. Hot Corrosion Resistance
The gravimetric results of the hot corrosion test are
shown in Fig. 2. These data are obtained from the samples
after the descaling treatment. T wo data are shown for each
alloy, and there is agreement between them, indicating that
data are well reproducible in the present hot corrosion test.
The results reﬂected the difference in the alloy chemistry
between the A-group and the B-group, so it was difﬁcult to
deduce the Cr effect on the hot corrosion from these data.
For example, the weight change is much smaller in the
A–2Cr alloy than in the B–2.5Cr alloy, even though these
two alloys contain a similar level of Cr. However, the corro-
sion data in each group exhibited a systematical change
with the Cr content.
Only the Cr content is different among three alloys in the
A-group. The weight gain occurred in the Cr-free A–0Cr
alloy, which was in contrast to the weight loss observed in
other two Cr-containing alloys (A–1Cr and A–2Cr). This
result indicates that a similar corrosion mechanism is sup-
posed to be operating in the two Cr-containing alloys. It is
well known that sulﬁdation occurs prior to oxidation in the
case of hot corrosion.
9,10) Cr is an effective element for im-
proving hot corrosion resistance, because a certain sulﬁde
(e.g., CrS) may be formed due to the strong afﬁnity be-
tween Cr and S. Therefore, when the alloy contains Cr, the
prior sulﬁdation is limited to occur in it. This is the case of
A–2Cr alloy, which shows better hot corrosion resistance
than the other two alloys. The reason why A–0Cr alloy ex-
hibits weight gain will be discussed later in this section.
In the B-group, all the alloys exhibited weight loss as
shown in Fig. 2(b). The amount of the weight loss de-
creased with increasing Cr content. This trend is consistent
with the Cr effect mentioned above. The difference in the
alloy chemistry between the B–4.2Cr and the B–2.5Cr is
only in the Cr content, but the B–12Cr contains lower
amounts of Co, Mo, Ta, Re and Al than other two alloys
and its Cr content is the highest among the B-group alloys.
Therefore, B–12Cr alloy has higher Ti/Al ratio than the
other two alloys. It is reported that a high Ti/Al composi-
tional ratio leads to the better hot corrosion resistance in su-
peralloys.
10) The highest Cr content as well as the higher
Ti/Al ratio of the B-12Cr alloy is responsible for its higher
corrosion resistance than other two alloys in the same
group. 
Among all the experimental alloys, both the Cr content
and the Ti/Al compositional ratio are the highest in the
B–12Cr, but this alloy was inferior in the hot corrosion re-
sistance to the A–2Cr alloy. The B–12Cr alloy had higher
W content than the A–2Cr alloy. It is well known that the
presence of W deteriorates the hot corrosion resistance of
nickel-based superalloys by enhancing an acidic ﬂuxing re-
action.

4.4. Oxidation Resistance
The measured weight changes of the specimens oxidized
at 1 373 K for 86.4 ks as a function of the Cr content are
shown in Fig. 6(a) for the A-group alloys and 6(b) for the
B-group alloys. These weight changes were measured using
samples just after the exposure without any descaling treat-
ment. The weight change was remarkably large for the A-
0Cr alloy. A small amount of weight gain occurred in both
the A–1Cr and the A–2Cr alloys instead of the weight loss
in the A–0Cr alloy. The magnitude of the weight change for
the A–1Cr and the A–2Cr alloys was almost the same.
These results indicate that at least a certain amount of Cr is
necessary to get good oxidation resistance, and 1 or 2
mass% Cr has approximately the same inﬂuence on the oxi-
dation resistance of the alloys. In the B-group, all the alloys
exhibited small weight changes. In particular, both the
B–4.2Cr and the B–12Cr alloys showed an excellent indica-
tion of the oxidation resistance.

tance was the best for the B–4.2Cr alloy. Both the B–4.2Cr
and the B–2.5Cr alloys have higher Al content than the

5. Conclusion
In order to investigate a low limit of Cr content in super-
alloys, some alloys containing high Re and low Cr have
been investigated from the views of hot corrosion resistance
and oxidation resistance. The results are summarized as fol-
lows;
( 1 ) An alloy (A–2Cr) containing 2 mass% Cr, 1.1
mass% Ti and 8.5 mass% Re exhibited good hot corrosion
resistance. 
( 2 ) Besides Al, the addition of Cr is needed in order to
get good oxidation resistance. In fact, compared to the Cr-
free alloy, a 2 mass% Cr containing alloy showed the re-
markable improvement in the oxidation resistance.
