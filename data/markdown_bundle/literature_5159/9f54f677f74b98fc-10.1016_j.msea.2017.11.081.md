Abstract 
Tensile properties of an as-cast CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy were investigated at various 
temperatures ranging from −160 to 1000 C. The tensile strength and ductility did not vary 
significantly with loading direction , despite the alloy’s strongly preferred crystallographic 
orientation. The impact toughness values of the as-cast high-entropy alloy were much higher 
than those of many traditional alloys, particularly at low temperatures. The mechanical 
properties of the as -cast high-entropy alloy were compared with those of wrought high -
entropy alloy and noticeable differences between the two alloys were found. The maximum 
tensile ductility and three different strain hardening stages were observed at 500 C in the as-
cast structure. Transmission electron microscopy observations demonstrated that the initiation 
of deformation twinning was very active even at 500 C. A simple calculation suggest s that 
very large grains of the as-cast structure induced a reduction in twinning stress, retarding the 
onset of strain localization. 
 
 
Keywords: high entropy alloys; casting structure ; mechanical properties ; deformation 
twinning; strain hardening

1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are materials that contain five or more elements in roughly 
equal proportions [1]. This new family of alloys has attracted considerable attention because 
of their unique properties and consequently their scientific importance [2]. For example, the 
face-centered cubic ( fcc) CoCrFeMnNi HEA, which was first reported by Cantor et al. [3], 
exhibits improvements in both strength and ductility [4] due to deformation twinning at low 
temperatures. Hence, HEAs are expected to replace conventional austenite steel for cryogenic 
applications such as cryogenic pipes (wrought steel) or cryogenic valves (cast steel). 
The HEAs are often produced by vacuum arc melting followed by drop -casting into a 
copper mold [5–7]. They are then homogenized at high temperature s (above 1000 C) for 
several hours to prevent dendritic segregation. After homogenization, the alloys are hot-rolled 
to break down their cast structures and obtain a recrystallized microstructure. HEA materials 
have been produced both by hot rolling at temperatures around 800–1000 C [8,9] and by 
cold rolling witho ut any intermediate annealing [6,10,11]. Such an excellent room -
temperature workability is ascribed to deformation twinning, whic h is one of the few 
mechanisms that can simultaneously increase strength and ductility [12–14]. The formation 
of deformation twins is known to be significantly affected by strain, strain rate, temperature, 
stacking fault energy ( SFE), loading direction, and grain size [7]. In particular, deformation 
twinning seems to be very sensitive to the change of temperature, grain size, and loading 
direction. As a result , twinning behavior has been extensively studied by many researchers 
[14,15]. 
Most of the previous reports mentioned above have dealt with wrought CoCrFeMnNi 
HEA alloys, which either ha ve a fine grain size (generally less than several tens of microns )

or are dendrite-free after the cast structure solidifies . However, the first step in preparing 
wrought CoCrFeMnNi is to break down the dendritic cast structure of the alloy ingot. In 
addition, it is necessary to pay attention to mechanical properties of CoCrFeMnNi alloy in as-
cast dendritic state to examine the applicability of as -cast CoCrFeMnNi alloy. Therefore, in 
the present work, we investigated the mechanical properties and deformation behavior of 
CoCrFeMnNi HEA s in as-cast dendritic state at various tem peratures. Tensile and Charpy 
impact tests were conducted along different loading directions to characterize the anisotropy 
of the alloy. 
 
2. Materials and experimental methods 
Ingots (8 kg) with nominal compositions were fabricated by vacuum induction melting. 
The melt was poured into a Y -block graphite mold to minimize the size of the shrinkage 
cavity in the bottom part of the ingot (the dimensions of this part were 110  60  80 mm). 
Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) was used to measure 
the chemical composition s of the top and bottom regions of the ingot . These were 21.5Co-
18.4Cr-20.1Fe-18.4Mn-21.2Ni-0.2Si-0.05C and 21.5Co -18.5Cr-20.1Fe-18.4Mn-21.2Ni-
0.2Si-0.05C (in wt.%), respectively, which demonstrates the excellent chemical uniformity of 
the ingot. To investigate the mechanical properties and deformation behavior of the as -cast 
ingots, uniaxial tensile tests (ASTM E8) were conducted at temp eratures ranging from −160 
to 1000 °C with a strain rate of 1  10−3 s−1. Charpy impact tests (ASTM E23) were also 
carried out at temperatures ranging from −150 to 150 °C using V -notched samples. To 
examine the anisotropy, the tensile and impact tests were performed along three directions of 
the as-cast ingot, WS, WL, and H (Fig. 1a).

The microstructure s were examined on the gage section s of the as-cast tensile test

3. Results 
3.1. Anisotropy behavior from tensile and Charpy notch impact tests 
Figs. 1a and b show the machining directions used to obtain the tensile test samples and 
microstructures of the as-cast HEA , respectively . Prior to tensile testing, t he a verage 
measured grain size was very large at over 1,000 m, with a strong ly preferred crystal 
orientation distribution. Fig. 1c represents the inverse pole figures (IPF s) along the WL 
direction. The grains were oriented close to the <111> or <001> planes with reference to the 
WL analysis direction, demonstrating that the as -cast HEA had a strong crystallographic 
texture. 
Fig. 2 summarizes the dependencies of the tensile yield strength (YS), ultimate tensile 
strength (TS), and elongation to fracture (Ef) on temperature and loading direction , based on 
the engineering stress –engineering strain curves. YS, TS, and Ef all strongly depende d on 
temperature. The maximum YS and TS were obtained at the lowest temperature of −160 C; 
above this, YS and TS gradually decreased with increasing temperature. Despite the strongly

preferred crystallographic orientation, the influence of loading direction on YS and TS was 
almost negligible. In contrast, Ef revealed a lower ductility along the WS direction. Compared

the as-cast HEA were generally inferior. Interestingly, the wrought HEA exhibited a ductility 
minimum at an intermediate temperature of ~400 C. In contrast, the as-cast HEA showed a 
maximum Ef at 500 C. This Ef value at 500 C was higher than that of the wrought HEA at 
400 C. As the temperature increase d to above 500 C, E f of the as-cast HEA decrease d 
rapidly. This result implies that a significant change in the deformation mechanism occurred 
at around 500 °C.

3.2. Tensile hardening rate 
The strain hardening rate s of the as -cast HEAs were calculated from the true stress vs. 
strain data converted from the initial engineering stress and strain curves. During the 
calculation, the raw data was smoothed using a fast Fourier transformation filter to reduce the 
random electronic noise between points. However, some noise is still visible as slight 
oscillations between data points. Fig. 4 shows the strain hardening rate s at −160 C, room 
temperature, 500 C, and 700 C. Considering the results obtained at these temperatures, the 
hardening rate does not appear to be a function of the loading direction. Also, the effect of 
loading direction on the critical strain for twinning initiation appears to be marginal.

## Page 11

10 
 
Therefore, we propose that hardening and twinning initiation behaviors are less sensitive to 
crystallographic orientation. 
 
 
Fig. 4. Strain hardening rates obtained from the true stress–true strain curves collected at (a) 
−160 C, (b) room temperature, (c) 500 C, and (d) 700 C. 
 
It is interesting to note that at all the temperatures except 700 C, the strain hardening 
rate curves exhibit ed three distinct stages . Stages I, II, and III are in turn characterized by a 
decreasing, increasing, and decreasing strain hardening rate with strain. Such an interesting

## Page 12

11 
 
hardening behavior has been previously reported in TWIP steel [20]. Deformation was 
mainly caused by planar dislocations at stage I, and deformation twinning was activated as an 
additional mechanism at stage II, causing a transition in the strain hardening rate. Twi n 
boundaries with a high misorientation angle are known to act as obstacles to the movement of 
mobile dislocations ; this increased the strain hardening rate, i.e. , the dynamic Hall –Petch 
effect. At stage III, twin activation bec ame saturated and dislocatio n slip then dominated the 
overall plastic deformation. Analogously, similar deformation mechanisms seem to operate in 
HEAs. Even though three stages were observed in Fig . 4, the occurrence of three distinct 
stages is not very common in CoCrFeMnNi HEA s. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, 
such behavior has not yet been observed at high temperatures. 
The hardening rate at room temperature was compared with that of wrought HEA 
with a grain size of around 5 m (refer to the Supplementary Figure for the microstructure 
and tensile curve). Interestingly, the hardening rate of the wrought HEA gradually decreased 
without exhibiting distinct stage I, II, and III regions, as shown in Fig. 4b (green line). This 
result implies that the twinning activity in wrought HEA is limited compared with the as-cast 
HEA. 
 
3.3. TEM observations 
As described prev iously, the occurrence of the stage II region strongly indicates 
twinning initiation and subsequent saturation. To verify the occurrence of twinning at 500 C, 
the fracture tip microstructures of the tensile test samples were investigated using TEM , as 
shown in Fig. 5. The n ormal direction of the TEM foil s lay perpendicular to the loading 
direction. Figs. 5a and b show low- and high-magnification images of the room temperature -
deformed sample , respectively. Twins covered most areas of the sample . In some regions,

## Page 13

12 
 
such as th at shown in Fig. 5a, the formation of secondary twin systems was also observed. 
Such a double twin system has often been observed in grains orientated close to <111>//TD 
(where TD denotes the tensile direction) [9]. In contrast, we observed the double twin system 
in grains not exactly aligned along <111>//TD, indicating a very significant twinning activity. 
Figs. 5c and d show TEM images for the 500 C tensile specimen. Similar to the room 
temperature sample, a large area was covered by twin s, although a double twin system was 
not observed in this case. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this active twinning behavior 
at such a high temperature has not yet been observed in HEAs. Between the twin bands, a 
planar slip of 1/2<110> -type dislocations on the {111} planes was observed, which is 
consistent with the report from Otto et al. [21]. The presence of the planar slip and the high 
fraction of deformation twins are believed to be the major causes of the excellent ductility at 
500 C. In contrast, at 700 C, only a few deformation twin boundaries were observed 
throughout the microstructure , as shown in Fig. 5e. Many recrystallized grains with size s of 
several microns were found. Also, a transition from planar slip to wavy slip was noticed, as 
indicated in Fig. 5f, which shows a two -beam bright field image of a recrystallized grain. 
This indicates that a large number of slip planes were activated, dislocation cross -slip was 
enabled, and the activation of twin s was suppressed. A t win structure is known to 
accommodate large plastic strains by absorbing dislocations , which leads to an enhanced 
ductility [22]. The abrupt decrease in ductility at 700 C is therefore most likely related to the 
suppression of twin activity.

4. Discussion 
The stress required to generate a twin structure, usually termed the twinning stress, is

## Page 15

14 
 
a combination of the stresses required for twin nucleation and growth. Here, it is thought that 
the twinning stress of the as-cast HEA might be influenced by its grain size. Gutierrez-Urrutia 
et al. [23] reported that th e deformation twinning stress was lower in coarse -grained than 
fine-grained TWIP steel ( coarse TWIP: 270 MPa, 50 m; fine TWIP: 400 MPa, 3 m). 
Rahman et al. also found that the twinning initiation stress decreased with an increase in grain 
size [24]. El-Danaf et al. [25] reported that the average slip length during straining in large -
grained fcc alloys with a low SFE did not change significantly , whereas the average slip 
length of the fine-grained alloys decreased with strain. Even with a relatively small reduction 
in the average slip length, dislocation generation—which is necessary to nucleate 
deformation twins—is strictly prohibited. Typical as-cast HEAs have very large grain sizes of 
over 1,000 m, which may accelerate the initiation of twinning.

dislocations with a Burgers vector of a/6<112> on successive {111} planes [26]. Since 
twinning is influenc ed by the SFE [27], Narita and Takamura [28] proposed a

5. Summary 
The d eformation behavior of a CoCrFeMnNi HEA with an as -solidified dendritic 
microstructure was studied. In particular, deformation twinning—which is active even at high

## Page 18

17 
 
temperatures—was observed and discuss ed. The main conclusions of the present work are 
given below: 
 
1. The tensile properties of an as-cast CoCrFeMnNi HEA was investigated over a wide 
range of temperatures . Despite the strong ly preferred crystallographic orientation, the 
influence of loading direction on YS and TS was not significant. In contrast, E f depended 
on loading direction and revealed the lower ductility along the WS direction. 
2. Charpy V-Notch impact tests showed that the absorbed energy values of the as-cast high-
entropy alloy were much higher than those of many traditional alloys, particularly at low 
temperatures. The anisotropy seen in the Charpy V-Notch impact tests was not very 
significant, which was also true of the tensile tests. 
3. The s train hardening behavior of the as -cast HEA was quite different from that of 
wrought HEA. Strain hardening rate curves of as-cast HEA exhibited three distinct stages. 
In contrast, the hardening rate of wrought HEA gradually decreased without exhibiting 
distinct stage I, II, and III regions. 
4. As-cast HEAs with very large grain sizes exhibited active twin generation even at a hi gh 
temperature of 500 °C. Consequently, a large tensile ductility was obtained due to the 
beneficial effect of twinning. In contrast, above 700 °C, the tensile ductility was 
significantly decreased primarily due to weak twinning activity. 
5. A simple calculation proposed that extensive deformation twinning at relatively high 
temperatures was influenced by the very large grain size of the as -cast structure, which 
induced a reduction of twinning stress.
