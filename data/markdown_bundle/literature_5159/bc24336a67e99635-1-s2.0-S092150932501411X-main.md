ABSTRACT
Fe-12Mn alloy possesses one of the most interesting microstructures - a blocky α ′ martensitic structure with 
twinning relationships and sub-micrometer grain size. Although these indicate the possibility of a very desirable 
grain refinement effect, this alloy shows low yield stress and is inherently brittle; its ductile-to-brittle transition 
temperature (DBTT) occurs around ambient temperature, causing poor impact toughness at subzero tempera -
tures. Here, we fabricated a composite-like Fe-12Mn alloy that exhibits an ultrahigh yield strength (~1 GPa) and 
a DBTT of - 50 
◦
C. Such an excellent strength-impact toughness combination is achieved by utilizing the syn -
ergistic effect of chemical heterogeneity and metastability engineering concepts, e.g., transformation-induced 
plasticity effect from a unique two-step γ→ ε → α ′ martensitic transformation. The feasibility of this newly 
designed alloy lies in its sustainability, i.e., containing an impurity amount of carbon yet exhibiting exceptional 
properties, and its economic potential as an alternative to other high-cost alloys for extreme environments.
1. Introduction
The Fe-12Mn alloy was one manganese-containing steel developed to 
substitute a lower-cost alloying element for nickel [ 1 – 3 ]. This alloy 
stands out for its distinguished α ′ martensitic microstructure, presenting 
a blocky shape with ultra-fine-grained sizes [ 2 , 4 – 6 ]. The formation of 
this unique microstructure was attributed to a two-step martensitic 
transformation upon cooling, in which the γ -austenite firstly trans -
formed to ε -martensite, immediately followed by the formation of six 
α ′ -martensite variants within a single preformed ε -martensite variant 
plate [ 7 ]. This transformation path is possible when the martensitic start 
temperature for the γ→ ε transformation (M
s

that for the γ→ α ′ transformation (M

martensite laths are the blocks themselves and are mostly surrounded by 
twins and high-angle boundaries; each block can, therefore, act as a 
single grain. Consequently, the Fe-12Mn alloy shows a significantly 
smaller effective grain size (sub-micrometer [ 2 , 4 , 5 , 7 ]) than the typical 
as-quenched α ′ -martensite structure in carbon steels, nickel steels, and 
medium manganese steels (several to dozens of micrometers [ 9 – 11 ]). 
Such an ultra-fine-grained microstructure was expected to help suppress 
cleavage fracture and improve the mechanical properties, especially the 
low-temperature impact toughness [ 12 ].
Nevertheless, the as-quenched Fe-12Mn alloy is well-known for its 
inherent brittleness. This alloy has a ductile-to-brittle transition tem -
perature (DBTT) around room temperature, leading to poor impact 
toughness at subzero temperatures [ 5 ]. Intergranular fracture is the 
primary failure mechanism responsible for the poor low-temperature 
impact toughness, even though there was no evidence for the enrich -
ment of alloying or impurity elements potentially deteriorating grain 
boundary strength [ 5 ]. For this reason, the tendency of fracture along 
the prior austenite grain (PAG) boundaries was concluded to be a nat -
ural feature in the Fe-12Mn system [ 5 ], i.e., its intrinsic microstructural 
properties, even though its unique ultra-fine substructures could effec -
tively prevent transgranular fracture. Conventional heat treatment ap -
proaches, such as solution treatment and tempering, were ineffective in 
improving the low-temperature mechanical properties [ 13 ]. Further -
more, although alloying with expensive elements such as titanium and 
boron was explored, any improvements in the impact toughness for this

2. Experimental procedures
2.1. Materials preparation
Fe-12Mn binary alloy with commercial impurity levels of carbon was 
prepared by induction melting followed by a conventional hot rolling 
process to 15 mm-thick plates. The hot-rolling finishing temperature 
was above 900 
◦
C, and the hot-rolled plates were air-cooled to room 
temperature. By utilizing the micro-segregation behavior of Mn during 
the solidification process and applying this advantage to the proper alloy 
system, i.e., Fe-12Mn, the HR composite-like Fe-12Mn alloy was pre -
pared at this stage. Since straightening is usually used to improve the 
flatness of the thick plates, a small amount of strain is imposed on the 
plates, especially near the plate surface. To simulate this process on a 
laboratory scale, we applied about 3.3 % cold rolling, corresponding to 
~500 μ m reduction in the thickness. Because our alloy ’ s ε and γ phases 
are metastable, deformation-induced martensitic transformation could 
occur partially during this process. The CR composite-like alloy is ob -
tained at this point, containing a higher fraction of α ′ martensite than the 
HR alloy. It has been demonstrated that tempering at low temperatures 
(170 – 300 
◦
C) promotes carbon partitioning to austenite, thereby 
enhancing its stability [ 20 , 26 , 27 ]. Therefore, the CR specimen was 
tempered at 200 
◦
C for 1 h, producing CR-T alloy. The details of the 
thermomechanical processing design can be seen in Fig. 2 a.
For comparison, a reference alloy was produced ( Fig. 2 b), which has

2.3. Microstructure characterization
The specimens for XRD, EPMA, and EBSD analyses were cut into a 
rectangular shape with a size of 15 mm × 10 mm × 2 mm, with the 
longest side being the full thickness (15 mm) of the steel plates. For XRD

3. Results
3.1. Differential alloying content patterns in heterogeneous and 
homogeneous alloys
Fig. 3 reveals the apparent differences in the distributions of Mn 
content between the composite-like and the homogeneous Fe-12Mn 
alloy. In the composite-like structure, Mn is distributed into alter -
nating layers of low- and high-Mn regions ( Fig. 3 a – c). The average Mn 
content of the low- and high-Mn bands are 11.5 wt% and 12.5 wt%, 
respectively. Also, their bandwidths are 32.8 ± 20.0 μ m and 35.5 ±
28.7 μ m, which are larger than those previously reported for medium 
and high Mn steels due to different hot-rolling reductions and final 
thicknesses [ 18 , 34 , 35 ]. It can also be seen that Mn distribution varies 
across the thickness of the steel plate, such that the fluctuation in Mn 
content is smaller in the outer section than in the quarter and center 
sections ( Fig. 3 a – c). Specifically, the range of Mn content between the 
high- and low-Mn bands in the top section is 11.0 – 13.1 wt% ( Fig. 3 a). 
The quarter and center sections are 10.6 – 15.1 wt% and 10.1 – 14.8 wt%, 
respectively ( Fig. 3 b and c). Meanwhile, the reference 12Mn steel shows 
a more uniform Mn distribution across the thickness with a more minor 
fluctuation in Mn content, in ranges of 11.1 – 12.6 wt%, 10.6 – 12.8 wt%, 
and 11.2 – 12.6 wt% in the outer, the quarter, and the center sections, 
respectively ( Fig. 3 d – f).

3.6. Transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) effect and changes in 
phase fractions upon deformation
In this study, large fractions of metastable phases (austenite and 
ε -martensite) were present in the microstructures ( Fig. 4 d). Upon 
deformation to fracture, most of the retained austenite and ε -martensite 
transformed to α ′ martensite, providing a significant TRIP effect 
( Figs. 8 – 10 ). Interrupted tensile tests combined with XRD revealed dif -
ferences in TRIP kinetics between the homogeneous and HR composite- 
like alloys ( Fig. 8 ). At the same applied strains, smaller fractions of 
austenite and ε -martensite remained in the homogeneous alloy 
compared with the composite-like alloy, indicating a faster trans -
formation rate in the former. Quasi-in situ tensile tests integrated with 
EBSD further confirmed this difference. In the composite-like alloy, a 
significant portion of austenite and ε -martensite in high-Mn regions 
persisted up to ~10.3 % strain ( Fig. 9 a), whereas most of these phases in 
low-Mn regions transformed to α ′ martensite at ~3.1 % strain ( Fig. 9 c). 
Such a faster transformation in the low-Mn areas combined with a 
slower transformation in the high-Mn regions resulted in a gradual TRIP 
effect as a bulk response. In contrast, in the homogeneous alloy, the 
majority of retained austenite and ε -martensite had already transformed 
by ~6.2 % strain ( Fig. 10 ). As a result of this rapid transformation, the 
homogeneous alloy exhibited consistently higher work hardening rates 
compared with the HR composite-like alloy across all strain levels 
( Fig. 11 ).

First, since the present alloys contained both hard α ′ martensite and 
metastable phases (austenite and ε -martensite), a high degree of strain 
partitioning among them can be expected. Higher stress concentrated on 
these metastable phases promoted the TRIP effect. In our study, rela -
tively large fractions of the metastable phases were present in the mi -
crostructures ( Fig. 4 d). Noting that although ε -martensite is a brittle 
phase due to lack of deformation modes in the hexagonal-close-packed 
structure, the ε -martensite in our present study is metastable and is 
prone to transform to α ′ martensite during deformation. The role of the 
TRIP effect in improving mechanical properties has also been well- 
documented in previous studies on dual-phase and multi-phase alloys 
[ 19 , 20 , 54 ].
However, the TRIP effect alone would not be sufficient to induce 
beneficial effects on the impact toughness, especially when considering 
comparable fractions of austenite and ε -martensite in the homogeneous 
alloy with that in the HR composite-like alloy ( Fig. 4 d). Here, we show 
that the kinetics of TRIP also play a vital role. In the homogeneous alloy, 
most of the retained austenite and ε -martensite rapidly transformed to α ʹ 
martensite by ~6.2 % strain ( Fig. 10 ). When TRIP occurs too early, the 
majority of retained austenite and ε -martensite quickly transform to 
brittle BCC α ʹ martensite, limiting the capacity of the alloy to accom -
modate impact energy via the TRIP effect. This early transformation also 
produced a high work hardening rate and corresponding high flow stress 
( Fig. 11 ). The rapid rise of flow stress generated large pile-up stresses at 
PAG boundaries, which could readily approach the grain boundary 
decohesion strength [ 26 ]. Under impact loading at low temperatures,

composite-like and homogeneous alloys had similar PAG size, compa -

dislocation density. They also underwent the same γ→ ε → α ′ martensitic 
transformation path, which led to the formation of the ultra-fine blocky

martensite and metastable γ and ε , strain partitioning between these 
phases promoted the TRIP effect. However, the kinetics of TRIP were 
different between the two alloys. The composite-like alloy exhibited a 
gradual TRIP effect as Mn fluctuations across the microstructure created 
regions with different stability ( Fig. 20 b). Low-Mn areas transformed 
quickly, while high-Mn areas retained metastable γ / ε phases to higher 
strains. This gradual transformation distributed the TRIP effect over a 
wide strain range, accommodating impact energy as the first mechanism 
for improved toughness. In contrast, the homogeneous alloy underwent 
a rapid TRIP effect ( Fig. 20 h), causing a high work hardening rate and 
flow stress. Once the TRIP fully occurred and deformation proceeded in 
the resultant α ′ martensite, high pile-up stresses concentrated at PAG 
boundaries, promoting crack initiation and propagation, and ultimately 
leading to intergranular fracture and poor impact toughness ( Fig. 20 i – j). 
The TRIP effect also promoted grain refinement, benefiting the impact 
toughness. Another contributor to improved impact toughness in the 
composite-like alloy was strain partitioning and dynamic deformation of 
α ′ martensite with different strengths ( Fig. 20 c). Mn-rich α ′ martensite 
remained stronger, while low-Mn α ′ martensite accommodated more 
plastic strain (higher dislocation density). This ensured continued 
dislocation activity even at high strain and low temperatures. Eventu -
ally, cracks along PAG boundaries occurred at higher strain levels as α ′ 
martensite experienced severe plastic deformation ( Fig. 20 d – e). Finally, 
the composite-like alloy benefited from delamination toughening, 
where cracks were deflected along interfaces between Mn-rich and Mn- 
low α ′ martensite bands, dissipating energy and slowing crack propa -
gation ( Fig. 20 f). Such delamination cracks were absent in the homo -
geneous alloy, which fractured predominantly by intergranular mode 
( Fig. 20 j). Although Mn segregation weakened cohesion of at PAG 
boundaries in both alloys, the composite-like alloys with multiple 
deformation and toughening mechanisms effectively suppressed and 
delayed intergranular fracture, achieving a remarkable strength and 
impact toughness balance.
5. Conclusions
Chemical heterogeneity-induced microstructure design and meta -
stability engineering concepts were utilized to attain an outstanding 
combination of ultrahigh strength (YS: ~1 GPa) and high low- 
temperature impact toughness (81.5 – 101.0 J at – 40 
◦
C), overcoming 
the longstanding brittleness issue of the binary Fe-12Mn alloy. Tailoring 
a homogeneous structure into a composite-like configuration, the yield 
strength and low-temperature toughness exceed the previous records. 
Microstructure characterization, including quasi-in-situ tensile EBSD, 
EPMA, and TEM analyses, reveals that the dynamic phase trans -
formation and strain partitioning among Mn-lean and Mn-enrich bands 
has a crucial role in the improvements of tensile properties and tough -
ness. Both the simple composition with an impurity level of C content 
and the straightforward industrial processing route involving hot- 
rolling, straightening, and tempering demonstrate the competitiveness 
of this alloy system in view of cost-effectiveness and sustainability, 
promoting its widespread applications in extreme environmental con -
ditions. The suggested microstructural design concept can further be 
extended to other alloy systems, potentially possessing possibly analo -
gous microstructures such as 304 austenitic stainless steel [ 64 ], medium 
[ 18 , 65 ], and high Mn (16 – 17 wt%) [ 66 , 67 ] steels, and an FeMnAlSi steel 
[ 68 ].
