Factors contributing to the strength of a polycrystalline
nickel–cobalt base superalloy
Toshio Osada, ⇑ Nobuo Nagashima, Yuefeng Gu, Yong Yuan, Tadaharu Yokokawa
and Hiroshi Harada
High Temperature Materials Center, National Institute for Materials Science, Sengen 1-2-1, Tsukuba 305-0047, Japan
Received 15 October 2010; revised 13 January 2011; accepted 17 January 2011
Available online 22 January 2011
The strengthening behaviors of a new polycrystalline Ni–Co base superalloy are analyzed by measuring its Vickers hardness over
a range of structural scales, from nano to micro. The results clearly show, for the ﬁrst time, the contributions of boundaries (grain,
annealing twin and primary c
0) and secondary and tertiary c0 precipitates in solid solutions to the total strength of such polycrys-
talline superalloys.

Keywords: Nickel–cobalt base superalloys; Precipitation; Hardness test; Nanoindentation; Atomic force microscopy (AFM)
Polycrystalline Ni base superalloys with a two-
phase structure consisting of c-phase matrix (face-
centered cubic) and intermetallic c0-phase precipitates
(L12) have been widely used as turbine disk materials
in advanced aircraft engines owing to their excellent
properties such as high-temperature strength, creep and
fatigue resistance, hot corrosion resistance, and oxida-
tion resistance [1]. Superalloys develop high-temperature
strength through solid solutions [2], grain boundaries [3]
and precipitate strengthening [4,5]. However, the contri-
bution of each strengthening factor to the total strength
of polycrystalline superalloys is not clearly understood
because of their complicated microstructures, which nor-
mally consist of ﬁne grains (in the 10–100 lm size range)
and c
0 phases of variable sizes and volume fractions
[6–10]. It is well known that trimodal distribution of
the c0 precipitates in a matrix provides a large degree of
strengthening and that the distribution varies depending
on the heat-treatment temperature and cooling rate
[8,10]. The primary-generation c0-phase precipitates that
remain undissolved during solution heat treatment at
high temperature are more than approximately 1 lmi n
size and can be located on both the grain boundaries
and within the grains themselves. The secondary-genera-
tion c
0-phase precipitates are medium sized, ranging
from approximately 100 nm to 1 lm, and they nucleate
during cooling from solution temperature, and grow dur-
ing aging heat treatment at an intermediate temperature.
The tertiary-generation c0-precipitates have the smallest
size, i.e. less than approximately 100 nm. Therefore, it
is diﬃcult to analyze the contributions of such small pre-
cipitates to the superalloy strength.
Several attempts have been made to model the contribu-
tion of each strengthening factor to the total strength of a
typical polycrystalline Ni base superalloy[9–11]. However,
most of these models have been consistently constructed
using quantitative calculation with limited experimental
results [9,10] or using alloys with simple microstructures
[11]. Therefore, these models cannot divide or indicate
clearly the contribution of each generation of precipitate
to the total strength. Therefore, it would be time consum-
ing and costly to explore the optimum combination of c
0
phases in a polycrystalline superalloy.
An indentation test is one of the most common tech-
niques used to extract the local material properties at the
nano-, sub-micron- or micron-scale [12–15]. In particu-
lar, nanoindentation combined with atomic force
microscopy (AFM) is useful for evaluating the hardness
of a speciﬁc nanoscale site in a material with complex
microstructures, because an in situ AFM image enables
the accurate setting of a Berkovich indenter with a trian-
gular pyramidal shape at the desired site [12,13]. The
National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS), Japan,
has developed an empirical approach for converting
indentation force–penetration depth ( F–h) curves into
the corresponding Vickers hardness [13–15], making it
possible to obtain Vickers hardness values at scales
ranging from nano- to micro-levels. Tests conducted
using a micron-sized quadrangular diamond indenter

The purpose of this study was to clarify the contribu-
tion of each generation of c0 precipitates combined with
the hardening of various boundaries and a solid solution
to the strength of a polycrystalline Ni–Co base superal-
loy, TMW-4M3 [7]. The hardness of TMW-4M3 was
measured in the nano- to micro-scale range. On the basis
of the results, the strengthening mechanisms of the alloy
with a complex microstructure are discussed.
The Ni–Co base superalloy, TMW-4M3, used in this
study was cut from pancake-shaped disks forged by Mits-
ubishi Materials Co. Ltd. [7]. The chemical composition
of the alloy, itsc0 solvus temperature, volume fraction cal-
culated using Thermo-Calc. and ultimate tensile strength
at room temperature are listed in Table 1 . The material
fabrication processes have been described in detail else-
where [7], and the processes for TMW-4M3 are outlined
brieﬂy here. Ingots with a diameter of 500 mm were pre-
pared by triple melting. The ingots were forged at
1100 /C176C into 200 mm diameter billets. The billets were
then isothermally forged at 1100 /C176C into a 440 mm diam-
eter pancake-shaped disk. Next, 5 /C2 5 /C2 2 mm specimens
were cut out of the pancake-shaped disk for hardness test-

perature of 1100 /C176C for 4 h, followed by air cooling

[10], the air-cooled sample was primary aged at 650 /C176C/
24 h/AC and then secondary aged at 760 /C176C/16 h/AC.
The heat-treated samples were electrolytically polished
in a solution of 8% perchloric acid, 70% ethanol, 10% but-
oxy-ethanol and 12% H
2O at a voltage of 40 V for 10 s.
The microstructures of the specimens were observed by
ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM)
using a microscope operating at 15 kV. Transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) was performed using a Tec-
nai instrument operating at 200 kV. The specimens for
TEM observation were ground to a thickness of 50 lm,
and then twin-jet electropolished using a chemical solu-
tion of 50 ml perchloric acid, 100 ml ethanol and 300 ml
2-butoxyethanol at 0 /C176C and 40 V. From the obtained
FE-SEM and TEM dark-ﬁeld images, the sizes of the
grain and c
0 precipitations were measured using a linear
intercept method. The volume fraction of the precipita-
tion was measured using a point counting method.

(1–1000 mN) and Vickers (0.196–490 mN) hardness tests

lar diamond indenter with face angles of 136 /C176(Akashi)
was used. For the micro-hardness test, a triangular
pyramidal indenter with an apical angle of 115/C176was used.

In summary, the individual contribution of c0 precip-
itates, boundaries and solid solution to the strength of a
polycrystalline Ni–Co base superalloy disk with a c/c0
two-phase structure was clariﬁed using three types of
hardness tests including an ultra-micro-, a micro- and a
Vickers hardness tests. The results presented in this paper
indicate that the overall hardness, Hv, of the alloy can be
divided into DHvGB + DHvTB + DHvc0 I, DHvc0 II, and
DHvc0 III + DHvsol, in addition to Hvbase, by investigating
the converted Vickers hardness at size scales ranging
from the nano to the micro. In particular, secondary c
0
contributes greatly to the strength increment of the alloy.
This implies that the optimization of the aging condition
is the key to strengthening such superalloys.
