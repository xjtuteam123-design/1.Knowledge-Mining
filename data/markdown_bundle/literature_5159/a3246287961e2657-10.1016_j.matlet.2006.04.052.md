Abstract
The NiAl –2Ta–7.5Cr–0.5Nb alloys (IP 75 alloy) were prepared by high-pressure die cast (HPDC), and tested for compressible strength and
fracture behavior in the temperature range 300 –1373 K. The fine structures with a homogeneous distribution of Laves phase at the boundary
regions created by high-pressure die cast led to improvements in both the compressible yield strength and fracture strain. The high temperature
(1373 K) 0.2% compressible yield strength of the HDC IP 75 alloy (160 MPa) is larger than that of the IP 75 alloys prepared by other processes.
The room-temperature compressible fracture strain of the HDC IP 75 (14%) is also superior to the IP 75 alloy (5%) prepared by an ingot-casting
process. The effects of size refinement and the more homogenous distribution of Laves phase and the formation of a ductile Cr-rich phase due to a
rapid solidification contribute to the increments of the compressible yield strength and the fracture strain of the HPDC alloy.

1. Introduction
The lack of strength at an elevated temperature and the low
fracture toughness at an ambient temperature are the major
deficiencies preventing the use of the B2 aluminide NiAl as a
high temperature structural material [1–3]. A promising NiAl
based alloy, i.e. IP alloy with strengthening Laves phase
(NiAlTa) has been developed [4–10]. The alloy exhibits higher
melting point (about 1460 °C), lower density (6.20–6.35 g/cm
3),
higher thermal conductivity and excellent oxidation resistance
compared to the Ni-based superalloys. Furthermore, this alloy
can be processed by a series of different production routes,
which may be used for property variation within a wide range
[8–10]. However, the alloy prepared by conventional ingot
metallurgy with coarse grains exhibits excellent high tempera-
ture strength but seriously brittle fracture at ambient tempera-
tures. On the other hand, the alloy processed by powder-
metallurgical with finer microstructure shows improved room
temperature brittleness, but lower yield stresses [6,7].I ti s
suggested that the properties of the alloy depend sensitively on
the distribution of the NiAl matrix grain size and the
strengthening Laves phase which is controlled by temperature,
time and cooling rate during processing [7]. Therefore, in this
study, we investigate the deformation behavior and mechanism
of this alloy prepared by high-pressure die cast (HPED). The
results of compressible tests demonstrate that the HPED alloy
exhibits improved compressible fracture strain at room temper-
ature as well as superior compressible yield strength at high
temperatures when compared with the ingot-casting alloy.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure and chemistry
The microstructural morphology and the phase content were
observed and analyzed by Scanning electron microscopy (SEM).
Fig. 1 shows the microstructure of the alloy prepared by ingot-casting
process as a representative example. The coarse β-NiAl dendrites with
a fine precipitate of a Cr-rich phase are almost surrounded by the Laves
phase, which covers the grain boundaries more or less completely, with
residual eutectics in between. The solid-state diffusivities of Cr solutes
are low which result in solute enrichment of the residual liquid whose
composition eventually reaches the eutectic composition (e.g. for the
pseudo-binary NiAl–Cr alloys, the eutectic composition, C
E, is 33 at.%
Cr). As a result, the eutectic solidifies in the last freezing interdendritic
colonies as shown in the photomicrograph. This microstructure is
similar to the observations by Zeumer and Sauthoff [9].
The SEM of the alloy prepared by the high-pressure die cast
(HPED) is shown in Fig. 2 . The HPED alloy exhibits a fine
microstructure with an average grain size of about 10 μm and a
homogeneous distribution of the Laves phase in between NiAl
dendrites. The compositions of the observed phases are determined
quantitatively by EDS for the alloy and are given in Table 1. Obviously
the observed Laves phase compositions in the alloy can be described as
(Ta, Nb)(Cr
1 − x − yNixAly)2 in rough approximation, which corresponds
to the familiar binary Laves phase TaCr 2 in the binary system, i.e. Ni
and Al primarily substitute for Cr. However, no residual eutectics can
be observed in the interdendritic colonies. It is interesting that a lot of
Cr-rich phases (dark phase) can be observed in the NiAl matrix. The
high cooling rate of the high-pressure die cast process can account for
this phenomenon. The rapid solidification undercooling decreases the
segregation of the Cr element and more Cr elements are dissolved in the
NiAl dendrites. Without the eutectics growing conditions under the
rapid solidification, the disordered Cr phase nucleates and grows
independently in the NiAl matrix.

3.2. Compressive behavior
3.2.1. Compressive behavior at room temperature
Fig. 2 compares the true compressive stress –strain curves of the IC
and HPDC alloys at room temperature, and a summary of the
compressive properties is also listed in Table 2 . A remarkable
difference between the IC and HPDC alloys is the compressive frac-
ture strain, i.e. compressive strain. The compressive fracture strain at
the ultimate compressive strength (UCS) is consistently about 5% for
the IC alloy, and 14% for the HPDC alloy. The test results are also quite
reproducible.
The fractured surface observed by SEM for the HPDC alloy is
shown in Fig. 3. The main mode of the fracture is the phase boundary
fracture between the NiAl phase and the Laves phase. Thus, one
possibility for further improving the fracture toughness of the ternary
eutectic would be by locating an interstitial alloy addition that would
improve the phase boundary strength without lowering the stress
needed to initiate cleavage fracture. However, many dimple-like caves
with large sizes can be observed on the fractured surface, which is
propitious for the alloy to attain good compressive ductility. Based on
the SEM observation, these caves are formed by a Cr-rich phase pulling
out from the NiAl phase. Some researches have shown that large
additions of Cr resulted in two-phase NiAl –Cr alloys with the soft
disordered Cr-rich phase as a second phase, which was used for
improving the mechanical behavior of NiAl based alloys [11,12].O n
the other hand, for the IC alloy, more Cr elements have segregated at
the NiAl interdendritic colonies and formed eutectics in the Laves
phase, so the contribution of the addition of Cr to the NiAl matrix
deformation decreased significantly. Therefore, combining with the
observation of the microstructure, the effects of size refinement and the
inhibition of the segregation and the formation of a Cr-rich phase due to
rapid solidification contribute to the increment of the compressive
ductility of the HPDC alloy.
From Table 2, we can see that in addition to the large difference in
the compressive plastic deformation, other compressive properties are
also quite different between the IC and HPDC alloys. The compressive
0.2% offset stress (0.2% CYS) of the HPDC alloy is substantial higher,
with an average value of 1568 MPa, than that of the IC alloy. This value
is also higher than the results reported by Zeumer and Sauthoff [9].
This increment may come from the strengthening effects of the grain
size refinement.
3.2.2. Compressive behavior at high temperatures
Fig. 4 shows the effect of processing on the compressive yield
strength of the studied alloys, which shows that the deformation
behavior of the alloys depends sensitively on prior processing. The
HPDC alloy exhibits superior yield strength at all tested temperatures.
Significantly lower yield stresses at all temperatures are shown by the
PM alloy and similar values are observed for the HE alloy. The lowest
yield strength at all temperatures is found for the isothermally forged
PM alloy with the smallest grain sizes. Literature [7] has shown that for
the NiAl–Ta alloys, the 0.2% proof stress at all temperatures is affected
significantly by the distribution of the Laves phase. There exists a steep
increase with an increasing covering of the NiAl grain boundaries with
isolated Laves particles. Furthermore, a less steep proof stress increase
is also observed when the Laves phase is distributed continuously on
the grain boundaries to form a skeleton. Therefore, for the IC and
HPDC alloys, the superior compressive strength can be explained by

the fact that the Laves phase is located at the NiAl interdendritic
colonies. Especially, for the HPDC alloy, the Laves phase is formed on
the grain boundaries of the NiAl grains with a continuous skeleton, as
shown in Fig. 1(b). Accordingly, the compressive stresses of the HPDC
alloy at all temperatures are higher than that of the IC alloy.
Besides the Laves phase, the strengthening effect of the Cr phase
must be considered for the test alloy. Literature [7] showed that the fine
Cr precipitate particles are effective obstacles for dislocation move-
ment and the hardening effect of the fine particles counterbalances the
effect of the reduction of the volume fraction of the Laves phase. For
the HPDC alloy, the Cr-rich phase with a large size in the NiAl matrix
produces a similar strengthening effect as the fine Cr precipitate
particles. Fig. 5 shows the TEM examination of the HPDC alloy
deformed at 1373 K. It shows that there exist numerous dislocations in
the NiAl matrix, while fewer dislocations were observed in the Cr(Mo)
phases. Additional strengthening should result from the formation of
imperfect dislocations within the interface as mobile dislocations in the
NiAl crystal cross the dislocation network into lamellar Cr(Mo) phases,
as shown in Fig. 5 . As more dislocations gather at the NiAl/Cr(Mo)
interface, each should leave behind an imperfect dislocation. These
dislocations soon make it difficult for a new dislocation to cross and
strengthen the HPDC effectively.
4. Conclusions
(1) The microstructure of the IP 75 alloy prepared by high-
pressure die cast can be characterized by the size refine-
ment and a more homogenous distribution of the Laves
phase and the formation of a ductile Cr-rich phase in the
NiAl matrix.
(2) The obtained alloy shows an improved compressible
yield strength and a compressible fracture strain com-
pared with the alloy prepared by other processes.
