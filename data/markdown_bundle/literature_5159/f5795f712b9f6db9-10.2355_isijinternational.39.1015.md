It is interesting to note that as the concentration of
alloying elements and hence the strength of an alloy
increases there is a general tendency to also increase
the N. numberand hence alloy instability, thus further
emphasising that a balance must be found between
mechanical properties and thermal stability. It can also
be seen that the data is also clustered into manygroups
of three which have similar strengths and slightly differ-
ent N. values, these correspond to the 3Ievels of Co in
the alloys. The different Cr concentrations can also be
distinguished as shownin Fig. 4, again the strength levels
are similar but there is a very large difference in N.
numbershowing that controlling the Cr level is very
important with respect to stability. Of the 129 alloys
which passed the tensile test only 59 alloys had a N.
numberless than 2.32. It was found that in order to
achieve these high tensile properties the Ti and Ta levels
have to be close to the maximumof the compositional
range and that to achieve thermodynamic stability the
Cr content is maintained at a low level.

6. Summaryof Initial Iteration
Figure 4showsthat a large range of predicted strengths
are possible with the compositional range, but the
majority of alloys fail to meet the strength and stability
criteria. If an experimental optimisation had been per-
formed attempting to cover the full range it is clear
that manyof the experimental alloys would fail to make
the tensile and/or the stability limits even before the
fatigue and creep performance was investigated. It is
proposed that by using this method of alloy design
these alloys would not have been madeup saving time
and money.
Considerations of short time tensile properties, sta-
bility and cost criteria would lead the author to propose
a smaller sub-set of alloys for further consideration.
Fromcost considerations a lower concentration of Ta
was selected. Co was selected at the higher level since
Jarrett and Tienl4) and Maurer et al.15) have shown
that although Co has little effect on the short time
tensile properties it has a strong beneficial role on the
creep properties of disc alloys. Both authors found that
as Co contents were increased stress rupture lives
increased and the minimumcreep rate decreased. This
was attributed to two factors firstly reductions in the
stacking fault energy as Cowas addedls) and secondly
from small increased in y' volume fractionl4) in a heat
treated disc alloy. The final Cr level has been reduced
downto close to the minimumlevel andthe final optimum
concentration should be determined during the micro-
structural and heat treatment optimisation since the Cr
content will affect the type of carbide produced and also
the oxidation and corrosion reslstance of the alloy.
Limited oxidation trials of the RRIOOOalloys indicated
that a Cr content at the lower end of the window still
provided sufficient oxidation resistance.16)
Throughthe application of the neural network models
attention has been focused on a muchsmaller number
of potential alloys which are predicted to meet the yield,
tenslle and stability criteria. Thus the use of the neural
network models as alloy design tools has been achieved
and successfully demonstrated.
7. Conclusions
This paper has successfully demonstrated how the
neural network modelscan be used in alloy design. These
modelsdo not aim to replace all of the experimental test
program but to provide a valuable tool to focus the
available resources on the more profitable regions of
compositional space. Hencethe arnount of experimental
work can be reduced resulting in a more cost-effective
and rapid al]oy development process.
Neural network models of yield strength and ultimate
tensi]e strength were used to highllght a subset of
potential compositions from the wide range of alloys
possible within the RRIOOOcompositiona] experience.
This list was further refined by consldering the micro-
structural stability and cost resulting in a muchsmaller
numberof potential newalloys.
At the current time no complete quantitative models
exist whereby the creep and fatigue lives can be accu-
rately predicted from inputs such as composition and
microstructure. At this point in time therefore, final
selection of composition will depend on' a detailed
knowledge of the role of microstructure and chemistry
on these important mechanical properties. However, the
final selection can be madeby looking at a dramatically
reduced set of compositions whlch are knownto meet
the necessary tensile and thermal stability criterion.
