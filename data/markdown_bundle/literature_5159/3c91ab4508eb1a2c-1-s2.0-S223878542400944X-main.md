ABSTRACT 
Super-gravity field was reported to strongly intensify the buoyancy-driven flow of liquid during solidification, 
affecting the solute convection and morphology of eutectic structure in binary alloys. In ternary eutectic alloys, 
certain two-phase regions with a coarse structure can usually be detected concurrently along with three-phase 
eutectics, leading to the formation of heterogeneous structure with limited ductility. Currently, Al – Ce – Ni 
ternary eutectic alloy was chosen as a modal system, which was reported for the first time to be solidified under

1. Introduction 
Heat-resistant Al alloys have been widely utilized as the replacement 
of heavier alloys in weight-critical applications, mainly due to their 
enhanced specific strength and terrific corrosion resistance [ 1 ]. How -
ever, most of the commercial Al alloys (Al – Si, Al – Cu) fail to perform at 
elevated temperature over 250 
◦
C [ 2 , 3 ]. Recently, advances in preser -
vation or formation of the stable strengthening phase at 300 – 400 
◦
C are 
obtained on several Al alloys systems: (i) Al – Cu alloys with interfacial 
segregation of transition metal (TM, such as Sc), resulting in stable (Al, 
Cu, Sc) nano-precipitates (referred to as V phase) in Al – Cu – Mg – Ag-Sc 
alloys [ 4 ]. (ii) Al-TM alloys with L1
2
-ordered precipitates, resulting in a 
core-shell structured precipitation phase in Al-Sc-Zr alloy [ 5 ]. (iii) Al – Si 
alloys with a networked architecture, exemplified by the formation of 
interconnected Ni-containing and Fe-containing intermetallic com -
pounds (IMCs) with enhanced thermal stability [ 6 ]. 
As mentioned above, the designed phases, such as V and IMCs, are 
thermal stable at 300 – 400 
◦
C. However, the critical issue is the coars -
ening or dissolution of existing strengthening phases, such as eutectic Si 
and θ’ / θ ′ ′ -Al
2
Cu, which may lead to the decrease in strength at elevated 
temperature [ 4 ]. Examination of published papers reveals that the 
strength retention of most commercial Al – Si and Al – Cu alloys at 300 
◦
C 
is 27 – 55 % compared with the strength at room temperature and rapidly 
declined to 7 – 30 % at 350 – 400 
◦
C [ 2 , 3 , 7 ]. Therefore, in order to meet 
the critical requirement of modern industry serviced at 300 – 400 
◦
C, it is 
essential to develop a novel Al alloy that can withstand high tempera -
tures while maintaining acceptable strength.

Ternary eutectic alloy is one of multi-component systems, encom -

contribute to the emergence of intricate patterns that are conditioned by 
the interplay among multiple constituents, the nature of solid-solid and 
solid-liquid interfaces, as well as the processing history [ 19 ]. In 
Al – Ce – Ni alloys, it is often observed that there exists a coarse two-phase 
eutectic structure, which may potentially impair mechanical properties 
[ 16 , 18 ]. The similar phenomena were reported in Ag-Cu-Sb [ 19 ], 
Al-Cu-Si [ 20 ], Al-Cu-Mg [ 21 ], and Al – La – Ni [ 22 ] alloys.

Fortunately, a review of the published papers revealed that super- 
gravity field was able to strongly intensify the buoyancy-driven flow 
of liquid during solidification [ 25 , 26 ], affecting the solute convection 
and morphology of eutectic structure, such as Al-Si [ 27 , 28 ], Al-Cu [ 29 ], 
Al-Ni [ 30 ], and Al – Ce [ 13 ] binary alloys. At present, for the complex 
ternary eutectic system, it remains uncertain whether super-gravity field 
can affect the spatial arrangement and structure of three-phase eutectics. 
In the spirit of exploring the above-mentioned question, Al – Ce – Ni 
ternary eutectic alloy [ 18 , 31 ] is selected as a model system to investi -
gate its solidification behavior under the super gravity field. The main 
aim is to investigate how super-gravity field influences the nucleation 
and growth of the three-phase eutectic structure, as well as its 
strengthening and fracture mechanisms at elevated temperatures. 
2. Material and methods 
2.1. Material preparation 
Pure Al (99.99 at. %), pure Ni (99.99 at. %) and Al-7.63 at. % Ce 
master alloy were selected as raw materials to prepare the as-cast Al- 
2.16 at. % Ce-2.56 at. % Ni alloy. Pure Al was placed into the crucible 
and heated up to 900 
◦
C in a crucible furnace (Model SG2-3-12). Once Al 
was completely melted, pure Ni was wrapped in Al foil and added into Al 
melt, followed by the addition of Al-7.63 at. % Ce master alloy. The 
melting process was at 900 
◦
C for 40 min with even stirring. Throughout 
the process, the crucible cover is kept tightly closed to reduce oxidation. 
The Al – Ce – Ni melt was poured into a preheated graphite mold (200 
◦
C). 
The Al – Ce – Ni alloy ingot was placed into a graphite crucible (260 
mm in diameter and 190 mm in length) and then introduced into a high- 
frequency induction resistance furnace (Model ZGJL0, 025-100-2.5P). 
The re-melting process of the pre-cast ingot was at the temperature of 
900 
◦
C for 15 min for homogenization of the composition. Subsequently, 
the graphite crucible containing the molten metal was transferred into 
the modified centrifugal apparatus (Model H2050). The various super- 
gravity fields (1000, 2000, 3000 g) were corresponded to different 
rotation speeds (3273, 4629, 5670 r/min), respectively. It should be 
noted that the Al – Ce – Ni alloy solidified under normal gravity (1 g) and 
various super-gravity fields (1000, 2000, 3000 g) were labeled as S0, S1, 
S2, and S3, respectively. 
2.2. Microstructure characterization 
To proceed with additional analysis regarding the microstructures of 
the alloys, preparations were made for metallographic samples. Metal -
lographic samples were excised from a position 12 mm above the base of 
the ingot, following alignment with the direction of gravity, and were 
subjected to a rigorous and orderly regimen of sanding with sandpaper 
followed by careful polishing. These samples were then exposed to both 
standard and extensive etching by immersing them in a 10 % solution of 
sodium hydroxide (diluted in 90 % distilled water) for durations of 0.5 
and 1.5 min, correspondingly. To characterize the morphology of the 
eutectic structures, cutting-edge imaging techniques were employed, 
utilizing optical microscopy (OM, Axioplan2 imaging), scanning elec -
tron microscope (SEM, FEI Nova 400 Nano SEM) and transmission 
electron microscope (TEM, JEM-2100). Further understanding of the 
eutectic phase composition, detailed phase analysis was undertaken by 
employing X-ray diffraction (XRD, SmartLab-SE apparatus), executing a 
scan at a rate of 2
◦
per minute across a sweeping range from 10
◦
to 100
◦
. 
2.3. Mechanical testing 
The alloy was subjected to mechanical testing using a high- 
temperature universal testing machine (UTM5305-G) at both ambient 
and elevated temperatures (300 
◦
C, 350 
◦
C, and 400 
◦
C). Specimens 
extracted from the alloy were prepared with dimensions aligned parallel 
to the gravitational field, specifically having a gage length of 10 mm and 
a thickness of 3 mm consistent with the standards for tensile testing. 
Each specimen was tested in triplicate to ensure the reproducibility of 
the results. Subsequently, fracture surfaces and cross-sections of the 
tensile-tested specimens were examined under SEM to elucidate the 
microstructural features and to analyze the potential fracture mecha -
nisms of the alloy.

Scholar Rios asserts that at high solidification rates, the presence of 
primary phases around eutectic structures intensifies competitive 
growth, preventing the cooperative development of binary or ternary 
eutectics [ 32 ]. In alloys S0 and S1-3, high initial solidification rates 
foster the formation of a coarse, primary phase-enriched peripheral re -
gion near the crucible wall as the alloy transitions from liquid to as-cast 
state. However, at lower solidification velocities, there is an enhanced 
synergy in eutectic growth and more extensive diffusion paths. In the 
core and the transition region of S0 and S1-3 under low solidification 
velocities, a fine binary or ternary eutectic structure emerges. Further 
microstructural analysis will concentrate on the single fully three-phase 
eutectic regions.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Refinement of interconnected ternary eutectics 
In reference to the preceding statement, it can be ascertained that the 
solidification conditions required for the synergetic growth of three- 
phase eutectics in ternary alloys are relatively rigorous. Certain two- 
phase regions can usually be detected concurrently along with three- 
phase eutectics. Interestingly, the high volume fraction of three-phase 
eutectics with limited two-phase regions can be obtained in Al – Ce – Ni 
alloys by super-gravity field solidification. Noted that the nucleation and 
growth of eutectic structure might be affected by the reduced differences 
in composition of the liquid phase and enhancement of the mass transfer 
during super-gravity solidification.
