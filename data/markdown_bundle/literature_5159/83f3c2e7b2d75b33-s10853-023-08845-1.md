ABSTRACT
Enhancing the high temperature mechanical properties and the thermal stabil-
ity of aluminum alloys are in urgent demand for the lightweight application 
scenarios (such as pistons, fan blades, crankshafts) working at elevated tempera-
tures. The fabrication of aluminum matrix composites is a promising solution. In 
this work, TiB2 nanoparticles were in-situ introduced to a typical heat resistant 
Al–Cu–Mg–Fe–Ni (Al2618) alloy matrix and subjected to hot extrusion. Micro-
scopically, the extruded TiB2/Al composite exhibited refined and bimodal sized 
grain structure, which was stable after long-time thermal exposure associated 
with TiB2 particle banding along the extrusion direction. The TiB2/Al composite 
showed an evident improvement in high temperature strength as well as the 
strength retention after long-time thermal exposure up to 300 °C, compared to the 
unreinforced alloy. The strengthening effect was remarkable especially at 200 °C 
with an increase of ~ 20% (60–70 MPa) in tensile strength and strength retention 
after thermal exposure but without the trade-off of ductility. The effects of TiB2 
particles on the temperature-dependent strengthening mechanisms and micro-
structure evolutions were investigated. This work inspires the design, process -
ing and evaluation of novel high strength and heat resistant particle-reinforced 
aluminum matrix composites for the potential applications under elevated tem-
perature service conditions.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT
Introduction
The target for improving energy efficiency in the aero-
space and automotive industries leads to the increas-
ing demands of light-weighted structural materials, 
such as aluminum alloys. Aluminum alloys typically 
show a high specific strength at ambient temperature, 
while lack robustness in strength and heat resistance 
at elevated temperatures [1 –4]. The investigation on 
high performance heat resistant aluminum alloys has 
attracted much attention, therefore. The mechanical 
properties of aluminum alloys at elevated tempera-
tures can be effectively enhanced by forming ther -
mal stable intermetallic phases in Al matrix with the 
addition of low diffusive elements, such as Fe and 
Ni [5–7]. In this way, heat resistant aluminum alloys 
have been developed, such as the commercial Al2618 
(Al–Cu–Mg–Fe–Ni) alloy, which has been already 
applied in the components working at elevated tem-
peratures, such as pistons, fan blade, crankshaft, in 
replacement of ferrous and titanium alloys at elevated 
temperatures up to 300 °C [1, 8, 9]. However, limited 
by the intrinsic low stiffness, hardness and strength 
of Al matrix, conventional heat resistant aluminum 
alloys are still challenging to provide reliable mechani-
cal performance for potential applications at elevated 
temperatures, including the high temperature strength 
and strength retention after long-time service at ele-
vated temperatures [10].
A promising strategy to enhance the high tem-
perature mechanical properties of aluminum alloys is 
fabricating the particle-reinforced aluminum matrix 
composites (PRAMCs). Compared to the unreinforced 
aluminum alloy matrices, PRAMCs are advantageous 
for higher modulus, hardness and especially superior 
high temperature mechanical performances with the 
introduction of thermal stable ceramic particles, such 
as TiC, TiB2, Al3Ti, Al2O3, TiN, SiC [2 , 11–14]. The 
ceramic particles reinforce the soft Al matrix via the 
strengthening mechanisms as load bearing, enhanced 
dislocation density and Orowan strengthening [15, 
16]. Meanwhile, the microstructure thermal stabil-
ity of Al matrix is also enhanced by the reinforcing 
particles at elevated temperatures. The grain growth 
can be retarded via the Zener pinning effect [17]. In 
addition, the gliding of grain boundaries and the rotat-
ing of grains during high temperature deformation, 
which attribute to the low high temperature strength 
of Al matrix, are alleviated by the particles at grain 
boundaries [18].
Amongst the reinforcements, TiB2 particles can be 
in-situ synthesized in Al melt via the salt–metal reac-
tion. In-situ TiB2 particles are more appreciable for 
its well dispersion, small size (sub-micron- or nano-
sized), clean interface and strong bonding with Al 
matrix, compared to other ex-situ ceramic particles 
[15, 19]. In addition, TiB2 is a well-known inoculant 
for grain refinement in Al [20]. Moreover, in-situ TiB2/

grains while more particles agglomerate along the 
extrusion direction with fine grains among them [21]. 
As a result, a heterogeneous grain structure is formed, 
which is indicated to provide additional strengthening 
effect and maintain the ductility [27].
Therefore, introducing the ceramic particles, such 
 TiB2, is promising for achieving superior tempera-
ture mechanical properties into a heat resistant alu-
minum alloy matrix such as Al2618. Our resent work 
has revealed the excellent microstructure stability in 
a TiB2/Al2618 composite under the high environment 
temperature of over 400 °C during selective electron 
beam melting, attributed to the coexistence of coarsen-
ing resistant intermetallic Fe, Ni-rich phases in Al2618 
matrix and TiB2 particles [28]. Other works have 
attempted the fabrication of PRAMCs in Al2618 matrix 
with Al2O3 and SiC reinforcements, which presented 
enhanced room temperature mechanical properties 
than the Al2618 matrix. However, the investigations 
on their high temperature performance were absent 
[29, 30]. Additionally, the microstructure evolution of 
the particle-reinforced Al2618 matrix composite under 
thermal exposure has also not been systematically 
investigated so far.
In this study, an in-situ TiB2 particle-reinforced 
Al–Cu–Mg–Fe–Ni (Al2618) matrix composite was 
fabricated and subjected to hot extrusion. The high 
temperature mechanical performance was evaluated, 
including high temperature tensile properties and 
the retention of mechanical properties after thermal 
exposure in comparison to the unreinforced alloy. 
The microstructure evolution at elevated tempera-
tures and the temperature-dependent strengthening 
mechanisms associated with the TiB2 particle rein-
forcements were studied systematically. This work 
intends to inspire the design, processing and evalua-
tion of novel high strength and heat resistant PRAMCs 
for the potential applications under high temperature 
service conditions.
Materials and methods
An Al–Cu–Mg–Fe–Ni (Al2618) alloy targeting with 5 
wt.% TiB2 particles was fabricated via the in-situ reac-
tion of mixed salt in Al melt. An Al–TiB2 master alloy 
was initially fabricated by adding high-purity reactive 
salts (≥ 99.0%) of K2TiF6 and KBF4 into the molten pure 
Al (99.99%) in a high-purity graphite crucible using an 
electrical resistance furnace at 900 °C. The melt was 
mechanically stirred at a rotation speed of 600 rpm 
for 15 min. After slag removal, the melt was cast in 
a graphite mould to obtain the Al–TiB 2 master alloy. 
Then the pre-weighted pure Al, Al–Cu, Al–Ni, Al–Fe 
and Al–TiB 2 master alloy ingots were melted and 
homogenized at 850 °C for 15 min. Pure Mg was sub-
sequently added into the melt at 760 °C and held for 
another 5 min before casting into the graphite mould 
to obtain the TiB2/Al2618 composite ingots. The Al2618 
matrix alloy without TiB2 addition was also prepared 
in the same way for comparison. Both the alloy and the 
composite ingots were homogenized at 480 °C for 24 h 
and hot extruded into bars at 450 °C with an extrusion 
ratio of 16:1. Subsequently, T6 heat treatment was con-
ducted as follows: solid solution treated at 530 °C for 
2 h, water quenched and artificial aged at 190 °C for 
20 h for the alloy and 12 h for the composite, respec-
tively. The peak aging time was determined according 
to the hardness at different aging time (Fig. 1a). Some 
T6 treated alloy and composite samples were then sub-
jected to thermal exposure in the furnace at 200 and 
300 °C for up to 120 h.
Both the T6 treated and thermal exposed alloy and 
composite were machined into dog-bone shaped cylin-
drical tensile bars along the extrusion direction. Room 
temperature (25 °C) tensile tests were conducted on 
the tensile bars with a diameter of 5 mm and a gauge 
length of 25 mm for by the Zwick Z100 machine at 
a strain rate of 5 ×  10–4 s−1. Tensile tests at elevated 
temperatures (200 and 300 °C) were carried out on

Mechanical properties
Figure 9a compares the tensile stress–strain curves 
of the Al2618 alloy and TiB2/Al2618 composite with 
the tensile properties listed in Table 3. At RT, the TiB2/
Al2618 composite exhibits an evidently improvement 
in yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength 
(UTS) by ~ 50 MPa compared to the Al2618 alloy 
without the trade-off of ductility. With increased ten-
sile temperature, the YS and UTS of both the alloy 
and composite gradually decrease. The TiB2/Al2618 
composite still maintains an effective strengthening 
effect with a 40–60 MPa improvement in YS and UTS 
compared to the Al2618 alloy at 200 °C and also has a 
comparable ductility. In comparison, the improvement 
of YS and UTS is only 20–30 MPa at 300 °C with an 
evidently decreased elongation. The tensile YS of the 
Al2618 alloys and TiB2/Al2618 composites in this study 
are compared with other Al alloys and TiB2, Al2O3, 
SiC and TiC particle-reinforced Al–Cu composites as 
a function of temperature [12, 29, 35–38], as shown 
in Fig. 9b. The heat resistant Al2618 alloys generally 
have obvious higher YS at elevated temperatures than 
the Al2024 and Al7075 alloys, due to the existence 
of coarsening Fe, Ni-rich intermetallic phases. The 
addition of TiB2 in Al–4.5Cu also presents evidently 
improved YS at 200 °C, compared to the Al2024 alloy 
with similar Cu content. The strategy in this work by 
introducing TiB2 particle reinforcements into heat 
resistant Al2618 alloy to obtain TiB2/Al2618 compos-
ite, has shown a synergic effect in improving the YS 
of aluminum alloy at elevated temperatures. Further-
more, compared other particle-reinforced Al–Cu com-
posites in Fig. 9b to the TiB2/Al2618 composite in this 
work, the AA2618/20 vol.% Al2O3p composite presents 
a slightly higher YS at RT and elevated temperatures 
with a large amount addition of Al2O3 particles, but 
the ductility of the composite is poor [29]. A 4 vol.% 
micro-SiC/Al2014 composite with a similar particle 
volume fraction as the current work shows a lower 
high temperature strength [38]. It is also reported that 
the addition of very small amount of nano-sized parti-
cles can achieve good high temperature strength, such 
as a 0.5 vol.% nano-SiC/Al2014 composite [38] and a 
0.7 wt.% nano-TiC/Al–4.5Cu composite [12]. The YS 
of both composites achieve a comparable or slightly 
higher strength at around 200 °C than that of the TiB2/
Al2618 composite in this work. However, the fabrica-
tion of the nano-sized particle-reinforced composites 
typically requires long-time ball milling of ceramic 
and metal powders, which is complicated and expen-
sive [12, 38]. Comparatively, the in-situ TiB2/Al2618 
composite in this work can be fabricated via the con-
ventional casting method, which is much easier and 
cost effective.

The dimples of the TiB2/Al2618 composites are evi-
dently smaller than those of the Al2618 alloy at the 
same temperature, indicating a refined grain size in 
the composites. With increased tensile temperature, 
the dimple size of both the alloy and the composite 
gradually increases, indicating an increased ductil-

large-sized dimples appear on the fracture surface, as 
observed in Fig. 10c. Such large dimples suggest the 
occurrence of grain boundary sliding [39]. However, 
the large dimples are rarely seen in the T300 TiB2/
Al2618 composite, indicating the role of TiB2 in 
restricting the grain boundary sliding during high 
temperature deformation.

In addition, apart from the improved strength, the 
hot-extruded TiB2/Al2618 composite in this work 
also shows a comparable ductility as the Al2618 
matrix alloy despite the introduction of ~ 5 wt.% of 
particle reinforcements at RT. On one hand, the fine 
size and strong interfacial bonding of in-situ TiB2 
particle and Al matrix can effectively inhibit the par-
ticle cracking and interface debonding, beneficial for 
the ductility of the composite. On the other hand, the 
bimodal grain structure of the composite also pro -
motes the strength-ductility synergy. The bimodal 
grain structure, consisting of the fine grains in the 
 TiB2 particle banding area and the coarse grains 
between them, can be treated as a heterogeneous 
structure (HS) [62]. During tensile deformation of 
HS materials, GNDs will generate and pile up at the 
boundaries of the soft and hard domains to accom-
modate the strain gradient, i.e. the coarse grain 
regions and the fine grain regions of the composite in 
this work [27]. The storage of GNDs results in long-
range internal stress (back stress) and increases the 
strain hardening capacity. A high strain hardening 
capacity consequently leads to the strength-ductility 
synergy. As a result, a good combinations of strength 
and ductility can be achieved in HS materials [63], 
which is also suggested in the bimodal grain struc-
tured TiB 2/Al2618 composite with the increased 
strength and the retained ductility at RT.
At elevated temperatures, from the stress–strain 
curves in Fig. 9a, the T200 composite still shows 
enhanced strain hardening than the T200 alloy and 
presents an increased strength without the loss of 
ductility. However, at T300 state, the composite pre-
sents a more evident work softening and lower elon-
gation than the alloy. The strength-ductility synergy 
no longer remains. It is suggested that at 300 °C (~ 0.6 
 Tm of Al), thermal activated dislocation climbing and 
cross-slipping becomes severe during deformation, 
which can weaken the grain size strengthening effect 
by releasing the dislocations pile-ups at grain bounda-
ries [64]. As a result, the high strain hardening capacity 
and the strength-ductility synergy at RT due to the HS 
effect in the bimodal grained TiB2/Al2618 composite 
are adversely affected at 300 °C. Moreover, the higher 
density of dislocations stored in the bimodal grained 
composite (Fig. 8d) can undergo a more rapid dynamic 
recovery process during high temperature tension, 
also attributing to the more evident work softening 
than the coarse grained Al2618 alloy. In addition, the 
decrease of the ductility in the composites reinforced 
with submicron or micron-sized ceramic particles at 
elevated temperatures is also suggested compared to 
the unreinforced alloy, since the micro-voids initiated 
at the particle/matrix interfaces accelerate the frac-
ture process during high temperature tension [13, 38]. 
Overall, the results of the current work suggest that 
the strength-ductility synergy owing to the HS effect 
is temperature dependent. Systematic and quantitative 
investigations are expected in the future work.
Conclusions
In this study, in-situ TiB2 particles are introduced into 
the Al2618 alloy matrix and subjected for hot extru -
sion. The TiB2/Al2618 composite exhibits a remarkable 
improvement the mechanical performance and heat 
resistance at elevated temperatures on the basis of the 
heat resistant Al2618 matrix alloy. The main conclu-
sions are drawn as follows:

The TiB2/Al2618 composite furtherly enhances the

Meanwhile, the composite presents a comparable

higher strength retention than the matrix alloy after 
long-time thermal exposure, despite the coarsening

ment of high temperature performance is on one hand 
attributed to the strengthening mechanisms associated 
with TiB2 particles. On the other hand, the thermal 
stable bimodal grain structure, as a heterogeneous
