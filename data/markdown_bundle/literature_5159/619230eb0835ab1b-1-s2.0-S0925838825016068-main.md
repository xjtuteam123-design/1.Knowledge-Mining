ABSTRACT
The wide application of Mo and its alloys is limited due to the inadequate ductility at room temperature, 
recrystallization embrittlement and strength degradation at high temperature. In this work, Mo-(0.3, 0.6, 0.9) wt 
% HfC alloys with enhanced strength, good ductility and high thermal stability were prepared by controlling 
grain boundaries with HfC nanoparticles and rotary swaging. The as-swaged Mo-0.9 wt% HfC alloy shows a high 
tensile strength of 917 MPa and a total elongation of 31.4 % at room temperature, while the 1300 
◦
C annealed 
Mo-0.9 wt% HfC has a tensile strength of 887 MPa and a total elongation of 35.1 %. After annealing at 1500 
◦
C, 
the elongation of Mo-HfC alloys increased to above 40 %. The dispersion of highly stable HfC nanoparticles can 
refine grains and form stable interfaces with Mo, and significantly improve the strength and thermal stability. 
HfC nanoparticles can also react with impurity O to form stable particles, which inhibit the brittle intergranular 
fracture caused by O segregation at grain boundaries. In addition, high fraction of low-angle grain boundaries 
introduced during rotary swaging can improve the strength by hindering dislocation motion while maintaining 
good ductility.
1. Introduction
Molybdenum (Mo) is a body-centered cubic (BCC) metal widely used 
in the fields of steel metallurgy, machinery and aerospace due to its high 
melting temperature (2610 
◦
C), excellent high-temperature strength, 
high electrical and thermal conductivity, and low coefficient of thermal 
expansion [1 – 4] . In addition, Mo alloys exhibit good compatibility with 
liquid alkali metals and possess a lower density compared to tungsten 
and tantalum alloys, making them primary candidates for space reactor 
structural materials [5,6] . However, the further applications of Mo al -
loys are greatly limited by the poor fracture toughness at low temper -
atures and high temperature recrystallization induced embrittlement 
and strength degradation [7 – 10] . Fabricating high strength Mo alloys 
with good ductility and high stability remains to be a vital topic in the 
research of Mo alloys.
It is well known that trace interstitial impurity elements such as 
oxygen (O) tend to segregate at the grain boundaries (GBs), resulting in 
weakened GB cohesion and brittle intergranular fracture of Mo alloys 
[11 – 15] . Therefore, reducing the impurity elements at GBs can 
effectively enhance the ductility of Mo alloys. The dispersion of ther -
mally stable oxide (such as La
2
O
3
, Y
2
O
3
, etc) or carbide (such as ZrC, 
TiC
, 
TaC, etc) particles in Mo alloys can refine grains, and significantly 
improve the strength and recrystallization temperature of the alloys 
[16 – 24] . The refined grains by the pinning of second-phase particles 
would increase the GBs area and reduce the average concentration of 
impurities at the GBs, resulting in enhanced ductility of the alloys [17, 
22] . For instance, G. Liu et al. [17] prepared nanostructured Mo-0.6 wt% 
La ₂ O ₃ alloy with high tensile strength (865 MPa) and exceptional 
ductility (37.5 %) by using a liquid-liquid mixing method. K. Jing et al. 
[25] prepared Mo-ZrC alloy with excellent mechanical properties at 
both RT and high temperatures by rotary swaging, and the alloy 
exhibited a tensile strength of 928 MPa and an elongation of 34.4 % at 
RT, and a high tensile strength of 562 MPa at 1000 
◦

fabricated Mo-1 wt% TiC alloy via novel freeze-drying technology fol -
lowed by low-temperature sintering, and found that the addition of TiC 
nanoparticles can effectively refine grain structure and enhance both 
hardness and strength.

expected that the dispersion of fine HfC particles in Mo may achieve 
excellent mechanical properties and thermal stability in Mo alloys.
In this study, HfC nanoparticle strengthened Mo (Mo-HfC) alloys 
rods with high strength, good ductility and excellent thermal stability 
were prepared through powder metallurgy and rotary swaging. The ef -
fects of HfC dispersion and annealing temperatures on the mechanical 
properties, microstructure, and thermal stability of the Mo alloys were 
investigated, and the mechanisms for the high strength, good ductility 
and exceptional thermal stability in Mo-HfC alloys were discussed.
2. Experimental
2.1. Starting materials and fabrication
The Mo-(0.3, 0.6, 0.9) wt% HfC alloys rods (hereafter abbreviated as 
MHC03, MHC06, MHC09, respectively) were prepared using pure Mo 
powders (purity ≥ 99.95 % trace metal basis, average particles~2.0 µ m) 
and HfC powders (purity ≥ 99.5 % metals basis, average 
particles~80 nm) as the starting materials. The powders were ball- 
milled in tungsten carbide (WC) vessels filled with purified argon at a 
rotational speed of 240 rpm for 4 h. WC balls with diameters of 5 mm 
and 8 mm were used as the grinding media, and the ball-to-powder 
weight ratio was 8:1. The ball-milled powders were subsequently com -
pacted into cylindrical rods by cold isostatic pressing at a pressure of 
180 MPa. Then, the green compacts were sintered in flowing dry 
hydrogen at 2050 ℃ for 4 h. The as-sintered Mo-HfC rods of about 
28 mm in diameter were heated in a hydrogen atmosphere and subjected 
to multiple passes of rotary swaging to obtain thinner rods with a 
diameter of 12 mm. The rotary swaging temperature is from 1200 
◦
C to 
1000 
◦
C, and the area reduction per pass is 15 – 20 %. The temperature of 
rods was measured by infrared thermometer. For comparison, pure Mo 
rods were also prepared following the same process. The relative den -
sities of as-swaged pure Mo, MHC03, MHC06 and MHC09 were 99.8 %, 
99.6 %, 99.5 % and 99.5 %, respectively. The as-swaged Mo-HfC sam -
ples were annealed at different temperatures from 1000 to 1600 
◦

under argon atmosphere for 1 h. Due to the relatively low recrystalli -
zation temperature of pure Mo (~1000

temperature annealing treatments were only performed on the Mo- 
HfC alloys.

3. Results
3.1. Mechanical properties
Fig. 1 shows the tensile engineering stress-strain curves of as-swaged 
pure Mo and Mo-(0.3, 0.6, 0.9) wt% HfC alloys at RT. It can be seen that 
the tensile strength of the as-swaged Mo-HfC alloys with different HfC 
contents is significantly higher than that of pure Mo. With the increase of 
HfC content, the tensile strength of the as-swaged Mo-HfC alloys in -
creases gradually, while maintaining good ductility. Specifically, the 
ultimate tensile strengths (UTS) of MHC03, MHC06, and MHC09 are 
787 MPa, 824 MPa, and 917 MPa, respectively, and the total elongation 
(TE) is around 30 %, which is comparable to that of pure Mo. Notably, 
MHC09 exhibits the most superior performance with an UTS of more 
than twice that of pure Mo (437 MPa). These results indicate that the 
addition of HfC nanoparticles can significantly improve the strength of 
Mo alloys while keeping good ductility, effectively overcoming the 
traditional strength-ductility trade-off.

3.4. Microstructure of Mo-HfC alloy
Fig. 10 shows the TEM images and the distribution of second-phase 
particles in the as-swaged MHC09 alloy. It can be seen that the size of 
the particles is in the range of 20 – 500 nm. As indicated by the red ar -
rows in Fig. 10 a-b, the majority of the particles are uniformly dispersed 
in Mo grain interior, with an average particle size ~91 nm. Meanwhile, 
a few larger particles (~300 nm) are located at the GBs (indicated by 
blue hollow arrows in Fig. 10 b), which can act as barriers to hinder the 
migration of GBs. The EDS results demonstrate that the large second 
phase particles at the GBs are Hf-C-O particles ( Fig. 10 d-g), which could 
be attributed to the reaction of HfC nanoparticles with impurity O at the 
GBs.
To further investigate the structure of the second-phase particles and 
interface relationship between intragranular particle and Mo matrix, 
HRTEM was employed for characterization of the as-swaged MHC09 
alloy, as shown in Fig. 11 . It can be seen that the lattice spacing of an 
intragranular small particle is 0.223 nm, corresponding to the (200) 
plane of cubic HfC. While, the lattice spacing of the Mo matrix is 
measured as 0.220 nm, corresponding to (110) plane. The calculated 
lattice mismatch ( δ ) at the phase boundary is 1.3 %, indicating the 
formation of a coherent interface between HfC and the Mo matrix. This 
stable coherent phase interface could inhibit the growth of grains while 
enhancing the cohesion bonding of GBs, thereby contributing to the 
mechanical properties of MHC09 alloy.
4. Discussion
4.1. Mechanisms for high strength and excellent ductility
The mechanical properties of dispersion strengthened alloys are 
closely linked with the microstructure. In the swaged Mo-HfC alloys, 
HfC nanoparticles uniformly distributed within the Mo matrix can 
effectively refine grains by hindering grain boundary migration during 
high-temperature sintering, and improve the strength by impeding 
dislocation movement. HfC nanoparticles can also react with impurity O 
to form Hf-C-O particles ( Fig. 10 d-g). Noting that interstitial impurities 
such as O were considered as the primary factor causing poor GB 
cohesion and brittle intergranular fracture in W and Mo alloys [25,29] . 
The HfC nanoparticles reacting with impurity O would alleviate the 
embrittlement effect of O on GBs and improve the GB cohesion, thereby 
suppressing intergranular brittleness. Besides, stable coherent interface 
between HfC nanoparticles and the Mo matrix serves as dislocation 
obstacles and sources, reducing dislocation pile-up and thus achieving 
both high strength and good ductility [30] . Additionally, the hot rotary 
swaging process introduces high density GBs in the Mo-HfC alloys, as 
shown in Fig. 6 . Both LAGBs and HAGBs can serve as obstacles to 
dislocation motion, thus improving the strength of the alloy. In partic -
ular, high fraction LAGBs can improve the strength while maintaining 
good ductility because LAGBs allow dislocations to transmit across [50, 
51] . In the Mo alloys with high fraction LAGBs, dislocation accumula -
tion induced plastic incompatibility at some HAGBs can be released by 
the coordinated deformation of neighboring GBs, which can help to 
eliminate the GB cracking and thereby delay the premature fracture 
[51] . Therefore, the swaged Mo-HfC alloys exhibit both high strength 
and excellent ductility due to the high fraction of LAGBs and the mul -
tiple effects of HfC nanoparticles, including dispersion strengthening, 
reacting with impurity O to enhance GBs, and stable coherent interface 
with Mo matrix.

4.2. Mechanism for exceptional thermal stability
It is well known that second-phase dispersion strengthening is an 
effective method to enhance the high-temperature stability of Mo alloys. 
For example, in oxide-dispersion-strengthened (ODS) Mo alloys such as 
Mo-La ₂ O ₃ , the La ₂ O ₃ particles, with a relatively low melting point 
(~2217 
◦
C), tend to segregate and coarsen at GBs, and the large lattice 
mismatch with the matrix can lead to structural instability at elevated 
temperatures, thus reducing the strength of Mo alloy [17,21] . In 
contrast, carbide-dispersion-strengthened (CDS) Mo alloys, such as 
Mo-ZrC [22,25] , exhibit superior thermal stability due to the higher 
melting point of ZrC (~3540 
◦
C) and the formation of a coherent 
interface with Mo matrix, which hinders the recrystallization process 
and enhances the high-temperature performance.
Among various carbides, HfC has the highest melting point and 
excellent thermal stability. The dispersion of stable HfC nanoparticles 
within the Mo matrix can effectively improve the strength and thermal 
stability by pinning dislocations and GBs. Additionally, HfC nano -
particles can form a stable coherent interface with the Mo matrix, with a 
lattice mismatch of only 1.3 %, which plays an important role in hin -
dering dislocation motion and enhancing grain boundary cohesion, ul -
timately leading to both high strength and high thermal stability of Mo- 
HfC alloys. Therefore, the Mo-HfC alloys retain the mechanical prop -
erties and microstructure stability after high temperature annealing, 
primarily due to the high thermal stability of HfC particles and their 
stable interface structure with the Mo matrix.
5. Conclusions
High strength Mo-HfC alloys with excellent room-temperature 
ductility and high thermal stability were fabricated by powder metal -
lurgy and hot rotary swaging. The effects of HfC nanoparticles disper -
sion and rotary swaging on the mechanical properties and thermal 
stability were studied. The main results are summarized as follows: 
(1) The swaged Mo-HfC alloys exhibit high strength and good 
ductility at RT. As the HfC content increases from 0.3 % to 0.6 % 
and 0.9 %, the tensile strength of the Mo alloys gradually in -
creases from 787 MPa to 824 MPa and 917 MPa without 
sacrificing ductility, and the TE is around 30 %. In particular, the 
tensile strength of the swaged Mo-0.9 wt% HfC is more than twice 
that of pure Mo.
(2) The swaged Mo-0.9 wt% HfC alloy exhibits excellent thermal 
stability with a high starting recrystallization temperature of 
about 1300 – 1400 
◦
C, which is about 400 
◦
C higher than that of 
pure Mo. After annealing at 1300 
◦
C, the Mo-0.9 wt% HfC alloy 
still maintains fine grains, and has a high tensile strength of 
887 MPa and a total elongation of 35.1 %. After annealing at 
1500 
◦
C, the strength of the Mo-HfC alloys decreases remarkably, 
but their ductility further increases and the TE values are above 
40 %, indicating that the GBs brittleness is effectively inhibited.
(3) In the swaged Mo-0.9 wt% HfC alloy, the majority of second- 
phase particles are distributed in Mo grain with an average size 
of 91 nm, while a few larger particles (~300 nm) are located at 
the GBs. Besides, high-fraction LAGBs were introduced in Mo-HfC 
alloys during the hot rotary-swaging process.
(4) The dispersion of HfC nanoparticles in Mo can effectively refine 
the grains and improve strength and thermal stability by pinning 
dislocations and GBs. The high-fraction LAGBs introduced by 
rotary-swaging can significantly improve the strength while 
maintaining good ductility due to LAGBs allowing dislocation 
transmission across. In addition, HfC nanoparticles can react with 
impurity O to form stable particles, thereby alleviating the 
embrittlement effect of O on GBs. These results indicate that Mo 
alloys with high strength, good ductility and excellent thermal 
stability can be prepared by controlling GBs with highly stable 
HfC nanoparticles and hot rotary swaging.
