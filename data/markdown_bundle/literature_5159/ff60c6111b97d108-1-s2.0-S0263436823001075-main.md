ABSTRACT 
The low strength and plasticity of molybdenum (Mo) fusion welding joints are the key problem hindering the 
application in the engineering structures field. The main reason for the low strength and plasticity of Mo joints is 
the low grain boundary bonding strength, which is also known as grain boundary brittleness. The segregation of 
oxygen (O) at the grain boundaries and the distribution of the lamellar MoO
2 
phases are the intrinsic reasons for 
brittleness. In this study, the Mo fusion welding joints were processed by laser beam welding. The maximum 
tensile strength of the unalloyed joint could only reach 169.8 MPa. Meanwhile, the maximum tensile strength of 
joints with Nb alloyed could reach 426.7 MPa. During the welding, the niobium (Nb) element preferred to reduce 
the O content and realize the second-phase strengthening at the grain boundaries. The results of XPS and TEM 
confirmed that Nb mainly existed in the form of Nb
2
O
5 
and NbC in the fusion zone (FZ) of joints with Nb alloyed. 
The SEM and EBSD showed that a certain amount of NbC is precipitated at the grain boundaries, and the amount 
of MoO
2 
is significantly reduced. The lamellar MoO
2 
could be clearly observed on the grain boundary surface of 
the FZ of the joints without alloying. 
1. Introduction 
Zirconium (Zr) fuel cladding, which is commonly used in nuclear 
power reactors, may meet some security risks in accidents, such as the 
lack of cooling water (LOCA). Zinkle et al. [ 1 ] in the Oak Ridge National 
Laboratory thought Zr may react with high-temperature water vapour to 
produce large amounts of hydrogen, posing a risk of explosion under 
some extreme conditions. Terrani [ 2 ], after systematically analysing the 
zirconium-based cladding, pointed out that to further improve the safety 
of nuclear power operation, the key is to optimize a material to replace 
the Zr alloy cladding with another material such as, ferritic alumina- 
forming alloy cladding, or silicon carbide fibre reinforced silicon car -
bide matrix composite cladding to improve the ability to deal with a 
LOCA event. 
Molybdenum (Mo) and its alloys have good chemical stability, high 
temperature strength and good corrosion resistance. It is an ideal ma -
terial to use for fuel cladding. Zhang et al. [ 3 ] used a laser to weld a Mo 
alloy (0.25 wt% La
2
O
5
) with preheating and heat preservation to reduce 
the uneven stress distribution, and the results showed that the maximum 
tensile strength of the joint can only reach approximately 30% of the 
base metal (BM). Therefore, the fusion weldability of Mo and its alloy 
are very poor. The reduction in the bond strength of the grain boundary 
is mainly caused by the segregation of the impurity elements oxygen (O), 
nitrogen (N), etc., and the effect of the O is most dramatic. 
Olds and Rengstorff [ 4 ] [ 5 ], while systematically analysing the effect 
of O, N and C on the plasticity of cast molybdenum alloy, found that 1 – 2 
ppm of O could raise the ductile – brittle transition temperature (DBTT) 
to room temperature, while N needed 30 ppm, but this amount is still 
very small,. Leitner et al. [ 6 ] used an atom probe and found the segre -
gation of ppm-level O at the grain boundaries of Mo. Zhang et al. [ 7 ] 
found that O mainly existed in the form of MoO
2 
on the grain boundary 
surface of the fracture of the Mo laser beam welding joints using X-ray 
photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The results of the study of Magneli 
and Andersson [ 8 ] showed that MoO
2 
has a Mo-O-Mo lamellar structure 
and that the strength of the Mo
–
O bond is weak. Kumar and Ryre [ 9 ] 
confirmed that when MoO
2 
precipitates at the Mo grain boundary, a 
single atomic layer of O is formed at the Mo grain boundary. The 
Mo
–
Mo metal bond is replaced by a Mo
–
O covalent bond, which 
weakens the bonding strength of the Mo grain boundary. 
Alloying is the main method to improve the Mo grain boundary 
strength. Ti, Zr, C, Re, and Hf are common alloying elements. First, 
Matsuda et al. [ 10 ] found that Nb could reduce the possibility of porosity

relatively small compared to other solid solution elements that can cause 
large lattice distortions in the Mo lattice. 
In view of the above analysis, it is proposed to add a certain amount 
of Nb to Mo laser welding joints and then to analyse the properties and 
microstructures of the alloyed joints. The analyses were carried out 
using XPS, high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to investigate the type, distri -
bution and morphologies of the precipitated phases in the fusion zone 
(FZ), to clarify the influence of the microstructure on the strength of the 
joints and to provide a reference for the selection of alloying elements 
for the laser welding of Mo.

2.1. Materials 
The material used in this study was a 0.25 wt% La
2

2.2. Test methods 
The Nb rings (the size shown in Fig. 1 (b)) were prepositioned at the 
butt position of the socket joints to realize alloying with Mo during laser 
beam welding. The laser power was 1.6 kW, the welding speed was 2.2 
m/min, the amount of defocus was 0 and the number of welding turns 
was 5. The laser machine was a the SPI Qbue LT 2 kW single-mode fibre 
laser, which has a laser wavelength of 1070 nm and a minimum focal 
spot diameter of 0.05 mm. The laser was held still while the workpiece 
was rotated, and the entire welding process was carried out under argon 
protection. The specific welding process is shown in Fig. 1 (c). The 
following Nb alloyed joints are represented by Mo + x Nb - W, where x 
indicates the thickness of the Nb ring and unalloyed joints are repre -
sented by pure Mo. The specific alloying and welding parameters are 
shown in Table 1 .

at a rate of 0.1 mm/min. The tensile specimens were socketed joints, as 
shown in Fig. 1 (a). The micro-Vickers hardness tests of the cross section 
were carried out with a load of 50 gf and a holding time of 15 s, and the 
position of the cross section is shown in Fig. 1 (d). The etching solution

3. Results 
3.1. Composition and microstructure 
Fig. 2 shows a metallographic photograph of the cross-section of the 
Mo + 0.15 Nb - W joint. The cross-section of the joint can be divided into 
the FZ, the heat affected zone (HAZ) and the BM according to the grain 
morphology. The microstructure of the FZ was a coarse columnar crys -
tal, as shown in Fig. 2 (b); the grains in the HAZ severely grew and 
coarsely recrystallized, as shown in Fig. 2 (c); and the original rolled 
structure was still retained in the BM, as shown in Fig. 2 (d). The 
metallographic morphologies of the Mo + 0.15Nb-W joint was not 
significantly different from that of a unalloyed joint (LW) (as shown in 
Fig. 3 ). The line scan results of the EDS showed that the Nb was mainly 
distributed in the FZ ( Fig. 4 (b)). The area scan results of the EDS showed 
that Nb was uniformly distributed in the FZ without macroscopic 
segregation ( Fig. 4 (a)). 
3.2. Mechanical properties of the joints 
The micro-Vickers hardness of the FZ increased as the Nb content 
increased, as shown in Fig. 5 . The hardness values of the HAZ and BM of 
the different joints were similar; however, the hardness values of the 
HAZ of the same joint were lower than those of the BM. This was mainly 
due to the recrystallization transformation of the microstructure in the 
HAZ during the welding heat cycle, which produced a softening phe -
nomenon. The hardness of the HAZ of the joints increased with 
increasing distance from the weld beam centre, and the average hard -
ness values were approximately 200 – 220 HV. The average hardness 
values of the BM were between 240 and 250 HV. The hardness values of 
the FZ increased with the amount of Nb addition. The average hardness 
value of the FZ of the LW joint was 200.5 HV. The average hardness 
values of the FZ of the Mo + 0.025Nb-W, Mo + 0.05 Nb
–
W, Mo + 0.075 
Nb – W, Mo + 0.1Nb – W, and Mo + 0.15Nb – W were 213.4 HV, 218.8.0 
HV, 231.0 HV, 233.0 HV, 239.1 HV, respectively. The diagonal length of 
the hardness indentations was approximately 20 μ m, and the in -
dentations were basically located inside the grain, as shown in Fig. 6 . 
There are two possible reasons for the increased hardness. On the one 
hand, this was caused by the solution strengthening of Nb in Mo. On the 
other hand, it was caused by the precipitation of the Nb-containing 
second phase. 
The tensile strength of the joints was increased by alloying with Nb. 
Fig. 7 shows the tensile stress – displacement curves of the joints obtained 
with different Nb additions. The Mo + 0.075Nb-W joints had the highest 
ultimate tensile strength (UTS) (426.7 MPa), and the unalloyed joint had 
the lowest UTS (169.8 MPa). As the amount of Nb added to the fusion 
zone increased, the UTS values of the joints tended to increase and then 
decrease. The UTS of the Mo + 0.025 Nb
–
W joint was 259.6 MPa. The 
UTS of Mo + 0.05 Nb
–
W joint was 379.7 MPa. 
The fracture locations of the joints obtained for the different Nb 
additions were mostly at the centre of the weld bead (WB) or near the 
fusion line (FL) and there were a few in the HAZ, as shown in Fig. 8 . 
Fig. 8 shows the typical fracture locations of the different joints, which 
do not reflect the fracture locations of all welds. First, the fractures of the 
LW joints were all close to the centre of the WB ( Fig. 8 (a1) - (a4)). The 
Mo + 0.025 Nb
–
W joints fractured partly at the centre of the WB and 
partly near the FL ( Fig. 8 (b1) - (b4)). The Mo + 0.05 Nb
–
W joints 
fractured partly at the centre of the WB, partly near the FL and rarely in 
the HAZ ( Fig. 8 (c1) - (c4)). The Mo + 0.075 Nb
–
W joints fractured 
partly at the centre of the WB and partly near the FL; a significant 
portion of the fractures were in the heat-affected zone ( Fig. 8 (d1) - 
(d4)). Most of the Mo + 0.1Nb-W joints fractured at the centre of the WB 
and near the FL ( Fig. 8 (e1) - (e4)). Mo + 0.15Nb-W joints mostly 
fractured at the centre of the WB and near the fusion line ( Fig. 8 (f1) -
