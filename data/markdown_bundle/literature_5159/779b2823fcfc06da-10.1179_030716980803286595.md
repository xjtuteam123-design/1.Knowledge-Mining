An investigation has been made of the influence of tantalum, hafnium, and carbon
on the strength of Ni3Al (yl). Ternary and quaternary alloys have been studied,
based on the Ni-Al-Ta and Ni-Al-Hfsystems, with aluminium contents in the
range 1"0120-25at.- %,tantalum or hafnium cOntents up to 5 at.- %,and carbon up to
1·4 at.- %.These alloys consisted wholly or predominantly of yl, with compositions
on the (AI +Ta, Hf)-rich side of yl-stoichiometry. Also, a multicomponent alloy
(Ni-Al- Ta-Hf- W-Cr-Co-Zr-B-C) on the nickel-rich side of stoichiometry has'
been studied. Structural observations have been made, together with measurements
of yield stress and of secondary-creep rates at temperatures up to 1"oI1000°C.
Tantalum and hafnium conferred substantial solid-solution strengthening on y';
however, with carbon additions, carbideformation significantly reduced the matrix
strength as a result of solute depletion. The strength and creep resistance of the
multicomponent alloy consisting of y+y' +carbide were improved by heat
treatment. MT/645

In the as-cast Ni-Al- Ta-C alloys (8C and 9C) and
Ni-AI-Hf-C alloys (11C and 12C), the hardness and room-
temperature strength were lower than for the corresponding
ternary alloys (7C and 10C). This is attributable to carbide
formation causing solute depletion of the matrix, particu-
larly in tantalum and hafnium, and hence reducing the
solid-solution strengthening, while the small volume
fraction of coarse carbide particles does not confer a
comparable strengthening contribution. This loss of
strength is more marked with hafnium, because of its
greater effectiveness as a solid-solution strengthener, and
also because the extent of matrix solute depletion appears
to be greater (seeabove, section on 'Structural features'). At
temperatures above ~800°C the strength varies little with
composition in the Ni-AI-Hf-C alloys (Fig.4). However,
in the tantalum-containing series above ~400°C, the alloys
with carbon show a higher strength than the ternary alloys;
this is difficult to interpret and may partly depend on a
smaller loss of matrix solid-solution strength with tantalum
compared with hafnium, but other factors, such as differ-
ences in the nickel content of the y' and in the amounts of
jS-phase, may also affect the temperature dependence of the
strength.
The alloys in the series 16C-19C have a constant nickel
content of 74·6 at.- %.The strongest alloy of this series is
18C (Table 1), which has the greatest 'total content of
tantalum and hafnium; there is a trend to decreasing
strength with decreasing content of these elements as
anticipated from solid-solution strengthening consider-
ations.
In most of the alloys, homogenization did not markedly
affect the strength. However, in alloy 5, homogenization at
1320°C or homogenization at 1320°C followed by aging at
l000°C gave a significant increase in strength. In view of
the scatter of the yield stress (Fig.3) a detailed correlation
between yield stress and the homogenization temperature is
not established; furthermore, hardness measurements did
not clearly establish this correlation. The data suggest that
the strengthening in alloy 5 results mainly from the 1320°C
homogenization rather than from the subsequent aging at
l000°C. Possible contributory factors may be a change in
the proportion of y', including fine y' -precipitation in the
y-regions (seeabove, section on 'Structural features').
