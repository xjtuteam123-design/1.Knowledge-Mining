Advanced alloys with lightweight, gigapascal strength, and suﬃcient ductility are vitally crucial for en- 
gineering applications. However, the intrinsic conﬂicts between strength and ductility or strength and 
density cause a big challenge to improve the comprehensive performance of alloys. In the current study, 
we adopt a two-step method to overcome this trade-off by tailoring the atomic mixing of lightweight 
elements and refractory elements to form a lightweight refractory complex concentrated alloy (LRCCA). 
Then, a lightweight, high-strength, intrinsically ductile complex concentrated matrix (LRCCM) alloy is de- 
veloped, modiﬁed with nano/submicron multicomponent intermetallic compound particles (MICPs). The 
developed MICPs-LRCCM exhibits a high yield strength, suﬃcient ductility and a relative low density 
( ∼5.9 g/cm 3 ), as well as high speciﬁc strength of ∼230 MPa ·cm 3 /g. High solution strengthening together 
with precipitation are simultaneously operated in the MICPs-LRCCM. Our results provide a guidance for 
designing high-performance lightweight alloys for structural applications.

1. Introduction 
Lightweight structural alloys with a gigapascal strength and suf- 
ﬁcient ductility are highly required for environmentally friendly, 
energy-eﬃciently engineering systems. However, developing such 
lightweight, strong, ductile alloys has been a long-time challeng- 
ing task in materials research ﬁeld. The strength and ductility are 
usually mutually exclusive [1] , and the strength generally decreases 
with reducing mass density. Up to date, three kinds of traditional 
lightweight alloys, i.e., Mg-, Al- and Ti-based alloys, show a low 
density and a high speciﬁc strength. However, these traditional 
lightweight alloys face a challenge in further enhancing the com- 
bination of speciﬁc strength and ductility. For example, Mg- and 
Al-based alloys display relatively low strength ( < 800 MPa) [2–4] , 
Ti-based alloys exhibit serious strength-ductility trade-offs [5–8] . 
Moreover, all of these three traditional alloys can hardly work at a 
temperature of above 650 °C [9–11] . In the last twenty years, a new 
kind of alloys, often referred as high-entropy alloys (HEAs) [12] ,

In this study, we adopt a two-step method to develop LWC- 
CAs with a high strength without signiﬁcant ductility loss at am- 
bient temperature and maintaining a high strength to a moder- 
ate temperature of ∼873 K. In the ﬁrst step, based on HEAs/CCAs 
concept, we tailor the atomic mixing of lightweight elements and 
refractory elements to form a complex concentrated matrix (LR- 
CCM) with a single-phase disorder solid solution structure and se- 
vere lattice distortion. The LRCCM can have good comprehensive 
properties, such as low density, intrinsic ductility, high melting 
point and high solid-solution hardening behavior, which is distin- 
guished from a dilute matrix (based on one or two principal el- 
ements) [ 12–14 , 16 , 18 ]. In the second step, the LRCCM is modiﬁed 
by nanostructured/submicron multicomponent intermetallic com- 
pound particles (MICPs) via microalloying then to form a complex 
composite microstructure. Thus, MICPs-LRCCM alloys not only can 
be strengthened by the severe lattice distortion from multicompo- 
nent systems, and the precipitation of MICPs, but also can couple 
the intrinsically ductile LRCCM to produce a high strain-hardening 
capacity, thereby achieving an excellent comprehensive strength- 
ductility-lightweight property. 
2. Results 
2.1. Alloy design concept 
To concurrently break through the narrow composition range 
of traditional dilute light alloys, overcome the intrinsic brittle- 
ness of the LWCCAs, and acquire comprehensive strength-ductility- 
lightweight performance, the principal elements constituting LR- 
CCM must be a low-density, an intrinsically ductile, and with a 
high melting temperature. Searching for such metallic elements 
without radioactivity in the periodic table, these elements of Be, 
Sc, Y, Ti, V, Zr, Cr, and Nb are initially taken into consideration 
(Fig. S1A). However, considering the toxicity of Be, the high cost of 
Sc, the brittle Cr, and Y that can embrittle alloys [ 27 , 36 ], only Ti, 
V, Zr, and Nb are suitable to be the principal elements of LRCCM 
(Fig. S1A and B, and Table S1). Therefore, a Ti-V-Zr-Nb alloy system 
should be a good model alloy to adopt the two-step method to 
design target alloy. Firstly, a Ti/V atomic ratio of 1.5 rather than 
an equimolar ratio is used to achieve the low density and the 
high plasticity, which can meet the conceptual range of CCA. In 
this case, Ti has the lowest density and valence-electron concen- 
tration (VEC) value among Ti, V, Zr and Nb, which can largely 
solute other elements. V can effectively improve the ductility of 
the target alloy although it has a relative high density (Table S1). 
Then, to signiﬁcantly improve the solid-solution strengthening ef- 
fect, and the melting temperature, T m , Zr and Nb are modiﬁed to 
be equal atomic ratios. Consequently, Zr with the largest atomic 
size among these four elements provides a severe-lattice distortion 
by the highest atomic-size mismatch with Ti and V, which gener- 
ates signiﬁcant solution strengthening effect (Table S1). Moreover, 
Nb with a high T m can effectively increase the melting tempera- 
ture of the target alloy, which is critical to maintain high strength 
at a moderate temperature. Then, a Thermo-Calc software is used 
to determine the target composition, (Ti1.5V)(1-2x)ZrxNbx, shown 
in Fig. 1 (a). The atomic proportions of Zr and Nb in the target al- 
loy are represented by the value of x. Increasing the x value re- 
sults in a large improvement in the target alloy’s strength, melting 
temperature, density and, more importantly, a narrowing of single 
bcc solid-solution phase zone. As a result, on balance, the x value 
is assigned to be 0.25, leading to a representative atomic ratio of 
Ti:V:Zr:Nb = 3:2:1:1 in the LRCCA with a density of ∼5.85 g/cm 3 
[ Fig. 1 (a) and Table S2]. This tailored LRCCA of Ti3V2ZrNb has 
higher mixing entropy, electronegativity that is positively associ- 
ated with the lattice distortion [37] , and melting temperature than 
those in the dilute alloys (Ti, and Ti1.5V) [ Fig. 1 (b)]. Compared to 
other alloys [38–41] and the equimolar ratio of NbTiVZr [27] , as 
shown in Fig. 1 (c), Ti3V2ZrNb has the low density and intrinsic 
ductility, which is easy to form a single phase. 
After that, to change the microstructure by microalloying 
and then forming the MICPs-LRCCM, Mo is adopted to pro- 
mote phase separation for increasing mixing enthalpy, /Delta1H mix , 
in the TiVZrNbMo-alloy system [28] . Based on Thermo-Calc 
calculations [ Fig. 1 (d)], there is a dual-phase region (bcc 
and Sigma phases) when the content of Mo is in a range 
from ∼0.03 to ∼0.056 (atomic ratio). Therefore, a minor Mo 
with ∼0.05 (atomic ratio) is added to induce the MICPs to 
strengthen the LRCCM (Fig. S1). As a result, the composi- 
tion of the MICPs-strengthened LRCCM (MICPs-LRCCM) is tar- 
geted as Ti3V2ZrNbMo0.05 [Ti 42.55 V 28.37 Zr 14.19 Nb 14.19 Mo 0.7 (at.%)/ 
Ti 33.07 V 23.45 Zr 21 Nb 21.38 Mo 1.1 (wt.%)] with a density of ∼5.9 g/cm 3 
(Table S2). 
2.2. Microstructures of MICPs-LRCCM 
The MICPs-LRCCM was synthesized by arc melting and then fol- 
lowed by thermomechanical treatments (see Methods) to obtain a 
homogeneously recrystallized microstructure with uniformly dis- 
tributed MICPs. The MICPs-LRCCM is composed of a bcc LRCCM, 
and the MICPs with a sigma-phase structure, as shown in Fig. 2 (a). 
In contrast, it is a single-phase bcc structure in Mo-free LRCCA 
(Fig. S2). The polycrystalline microstructure of the MICPs-LRCCM 
is composed of uniformly equiaxed grains with an average size of 
∼75 μm [ Fig. 2 (b)]. Many near-spherical nanostructured/ submi- 
cron MICPs with a diameter of 50 ∼ 300 nm and a volume frac- 
tion of 10 ∼ 15% are uniformly distributed in the equiaxed grains 
[ Fig. 2 (c)]. A scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) 
coupled with an energy dispersive X-ray technique is used to de- 
tect the elemental distributions of the LRCCM and the MICPs. We 
found that the MICPs are enriched in Zr and Nb, while the LR- 
CCM is enriched in Ti, and V [ Fig. 2 (d)]). An observation by high- 
resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) further ver- 
iﬁes the bcc structure of the LRCCM [ Fig. 2 (e), region 1] and the 
sigma-phase structure of the MICPs [ Fig. 2 (e), region 3]. The lattice 
mismatch ( ∼12.7%) between the MICPs and LRCCM is calculated 
based on the HRTEM image in Fig. 2 (e), which conﬁrms a semico- 
herency. There also exist dislocations at the interface between the 
LRCCM and the MICPs [ Fig. 2 (e)]. 
Three-dimensional atom probe tomography (3D-APT) is used to 
quantitatively compositional analysis of the MICPs and the LRCCM 
(Table S3). Fig. 2 (g) shows a typical reconstructed APT map high- 
lighted by the iso-composition surfaces of 50 at. % Zr-Zr, respec- 
tively. A one-dimensional compositional proﬁle analysis quantita- 
tively provides elemental partitioning of each element [ Fig. 2 (f) 
and Table S2]. Strong segregation of Zr (from 15 ± 2 to 42 ± 2 at. 
%), and the pronounced depletion of V (from 27 ± 1 to 13 ± 1 at. 
%), Ti (from 42 ± 2 to 30 ± 2 at. %), and Nb (from 14 ± 1 to 15 ± 1 
at. %) occurs in the MICPs (Table S3), whereas the reverse elemen- 
tal partitions occur in the LRCCM. On the other hand, Mo is slightly 
depleted (from 1.1 ± 0.2 to 0.6 ± 0.2 at. %) in the MICPs [ Fig. 2 (f)]. 
Besides, the LRCCM is compositionally homogeneous without ele- 
mental clustering [ Fig. 2 (h)].

3. Discussion 
The developed MICPs-LRCCM by two-step method overcomes 
the intrinsic brittleness of LWCCAs and most RHEAs, and the 
strength-ductility-lightweight trade-off of the light alloys. The 
MICPs-LRCCM exhibits an exceptional synergy of lightweight, 
strength, and ductility at both room and moderate temperature 
(873 K). The low density of the MICPs-LRCCM is due to the alloy 
design paradigm of LRCCM by choosing the relatively light princi- 
pal constituent elements, e.g., Ti, V, and Zr [ Fig. 4 (a)]. The solid- 
solution hardening in the LRCCM and the precipitation-hardening 
from the nanostructured MICPs lead to the high strength and also 
appreciable tensile ductility. 
For the MICPs-LRCCM, the strengthening contributions include 
solid-solution hardening ( /Delta1σ SS ), precipitate-hardening ( /Delta1σP ), and 
grain-boundary hardening ( /Delta1σGB ), induced by the obstacles of the 
lattice distortion of the LRCCM, the MCIPs, and grain-boundaries, 
respectively, which impede the dislocation movement. On the 
other hand, the strain-hardening effect resulted from rolling is 
minimized after annealing, which cannot be considered. Therefore, 
the yield strength is contributed from three deformation mecha- 
nisms, which is a simple sum of three strengthening increments,

To reveal the underlying deformation mechanisms behind the 
three-stage strain-hardening behavior of the MICPs-LRCCM, the 
microstructural evolutions at different strains are characterized 
by TEM [ Fig. 5 (c–f)]. At the strain of ∼5%, the plastic deforma-

ping planes is considerably decreased [ Fig. 5 (d)], and numerous 
slip traces intersection are observed on the surface (Fig. S4B). The 
multiple dislocations with various Burgers vectors slip and inter- 
sect, forming ﬁnely-dislocation network structures, namely Taylor 
lattices, in which many high-density dislocation arrays surround 
the core region of low-density dislocations [ Fig. 5 (d)]. At the strain 
of ∼15%, many Taylor lattices generate domain boundaries (DBs) 
[82] and high-density dislocation walls (HDDWs) [ Fig. 5 (e)]. Fur- 
ther increasing the strain to 19% causes twinning to be activated 
and nanotwins to be formed [ Fig. 5 (f) and Fig. S5]. During the plas- 
tic deformation process, the MICPs are obstacles to dislocations. 
Therefore, there are multiple deformation mechanisms, i.e., the 
strain hardenings from dislocation substructures, including Taylor 
lattices and HDDWs, and from twinning as well as from the com-

substructures, and deformation twins. In contrast, in the LRCCA 
without the MICPs, the dislocation gliding is easily operated with- 
out any obstacles, such as Taylor lattices, twins and precipitations 
(Fig. S2). Thus, the strain-hardening capacity of the LRCCA is lower 
than that of the MICPs strengthened LRCCM.

4. Conclusions 
In summary, we use a two-step method to tailor atomic mix- 
ing of light elements and refractory elements, which forms an 
intrinsically-ductile LRCCM with severe lattice distortion, and mod- 
iﬁed by nanostructured/ submicron MICPs, i.e., a MICPs-LRCCM al- 
loy. This MICPs-LRCCM alloy has a good comprehensive strength- 
ductility-lightweight performance. Experiments demonstrate that 
the MICPs-LRCCM alloy is simultaneously lightweight, accompa- 
nied by high strength and suﬃcient tensile ductility at ambient 
temperature. Moreover, the designed MICPs-LRCCM can be poten- 
tially applied at the moderate temperature of 873 K without in- 
termediate temperature brittleness because its speciﬁc strength 
is higher than other traditional lightweight alloys and RHEAs. 
Our alloy-design method for developing high-strength and ductile 
lightweight CCAs may guide to other high-performance lightweight 
alloys. These high-strength and lightweight alloys will be of great 
interest for a broad range of aerospace, automotive, navigation, nu- 
clear power, chemical engineering, and other applications. 
5. Materials and methods 
5.1. Sample preparation 
Thermodynamic calculations based on Thermo-Calc 3.0.1 soft- 
ware [86–88] with a Ti-based database. The designed LRCCA 
(NbTi3V2Zr) and MICPs-LRCCM (NbTi3V2ZrMo0.05) were prepared 
by arc-melting a mixture of pure metals (Ti, V, Zr, Nb, and Mo) 
(purity ˃ 99.95%) in a high-purity argon atmosphere. The master 
alloys were repeatedly melted at least ﬁve times to promote chem- 
ical homogeneity, and then drop-cast into a water-cooled copper 
mold to form plates with a size of 10 mm (width) × 80 mm 
(length) × 5 mm (thickness). The as-cast plates were cold- 
rolled step-by-step with a ﬁnal thickness reduction of ∼80% in a 
laboratory-scale two-high rolling machine. The cold-rolled sheets 
were annealed at 1073 K for 1 h and then followed by air cooling. 
All the heat treatment process was carried out in vacuum environ- 
ment. 
5.2. Microstructure characterization 
Phase structures were analyzed by a Rigaku D \ max-2550 
diffractometer (XRD) with the Cu-K α radiation at 40 kV. The mi- 
crostructures at the strains of 0, 5, 10, 15 and 19% and fracture 
were characterized by an EPMA-8050G equipped in a scanning 
electron microscope (SEM). The SEM sample surface was mechani- 
cally polished, and then electro-polished in a mixture of 6% per- 
chloric acid, 34% n-butanol and 60% methanol (volume percent, 
vol. %) at –4 0 °C. The average sizes of the grains and particles 
were measured from their area fractions by employing an Image- 
Pro Plus software package5. At least 5 images were used for ob- 
taining the average values. The scanning step size was 1 μm. The 
TEM observations were carried out in a JEM-2100F TEM operated 
at 200 kV. Two-beam dark-ﬁeld (DF) imaging was conducted to 
evaluate the deformed substructures. TEM samples were cut from 
the gauges of the stretched specimens by a diamond-saw, and 
then, ground down to ∼30 μm in thickness. A twin-jet polishing 
technique was applied to prepare the TEM foils in a mixture of 
6% perchloric acid, 34% n-butanol and 60% methanol (vol. %) at 
- 40 °C. The 3D-APT characterizations were performed in a LEAP 
40 0 0X HR atom probe. The needled-shaped specimens were pre- 
pared by a FEI 600i dual-beam-focused ion beam/scanning electron 
microscope (FIB/SEM). 
5.3. Tensile test 
The dog-bone-shaped specimens for quasi-static tensile tests 
were cut by electrical discharge machining (EDM) with a gauge 
length of ∼12.7 mm parallel to the rolling direction, a width of 
∼3 mm, and a thickness of ∼1 mm. At least three specimens were 
tested to conﬁrm the data reproducibility. The direction of tensile 
tests was parallel to the rolling direction. Tensile tests were car- 
ried out at room temperature in an MTS Criterion Model 44 ma- 
chine with a strain rate of 3 × 10 −3 s −1 . An extensometer with 
a gauge length of 12 mm was used to monitor the strain. Ten- 
sile tests at 873 K were conducted in an INSTRON-8801 machine 
equipped with a furnace with a strain rate 3 × 10 −3 s −1 as well. 
Before tensile test at 873 K, the tensile specimens with a gauge 
length of ∼6 mm, and a width of ∼2 mm were coated with an 
Al 2 O 3 ﬁlm with a thickness of ∼50 μm to prevent oxidation. The 
specimens were heated to 873 K at a rate of ∼20 K/min., followed 
by a holding time of 5 min.
