abstract
In this study, the heat resistant Al e8Ce-xY (x ¼ 0, 1, 2, and 3) alloys as designed and
fabricated by gravity casting. The tensile strength of the alloys at room temperature
increased with the Y addition from 117.5 to 142.7 MPa, and the tensile strength at 350
/C14 C
increased signiﬁcantly from 40.4 to 57.2 MPa, which is an increase of >40%. Even after heat
exposure at 350 /C14 C for 200 h, the alloys still retain the morphology of the eutectic colony
and hardness stable. The outstanding performance is primarily attributed to excellent heat
stability of eutectic lamellar Al 11(Ce,Y)3 phase at 350 /C14 C and its volume fraction increased
from 10.45 to 19.15% with Y additions from 0 to 3%. The increasing volume fraction of the
eutectic Al
11(Ce,Y)3 phase effectively improves the tensile strength of the alloys at ambient
and high temperatures. Our work provides a new ternary Al eCeeY system which is a
marvellous candidate for castable heat resistant Al alloys.

researchers have shown substantial interest in improving the
performance of aluminium at high temperatures. In recent
years, Al eCe alloys have received considerable attention
owing to the development of Al eCe alloys by the Lawrence
Livermore National Laboratory and Oak Ridge National Labo-
ratory [ 4e6]. Owing to the extremely low thermal diffusion

alloys include the precipitation and second phase
strengthening mechanisms. The addition of Zr/Sc in-
troduces Al
3Sc/Al3(Zr, Sc) nano-precipitates, which induce
precipitation strengthening, greatly improving the yield
strength of Al eCe alloys [ 22,23]. However, owing to the weak
thermal stability of the Al
3Sc/Al3(Zr, Sc) nano-precipitates,
the strength and hardness of alloys containing Zr/Sc grad-
ually decrease after exposure to temperatures above 300
/C14 C
[24,25]. A higher volume fraction of the ﬁne thermally-stable
phase results in a higher strength at room and high tem-
peratures. However, the volume fraction of the eutectic
Al
11Ce3 phase is limited by the eutectic composition. To
achieve a relatively higher volume fraction of the second
phase, adding other elements, such as Mn, Ni, Si, and Cu, to
AleCe alloys introduces new intermetallic compounds
[26,27]. Additions of Mn and Ni lead to the production of
Al
3Ni and Al 6Mn phases in the Al eCe alloys, which effec-
tively increase the volume fraction of the second phase and
greatly improve the mechanical properties [ 20,21].
Numerous studies reported that Y is an effective modiﬁer
and reﬁner for Al [ 28e30]. Li demonstrated that adding
0.3 wt % Y to Al-7.5Si-0.5 Mg increased the ultimate tensile
strength (UTS), yield strength (YS), and elongation (EL) to
353 MPa, 287 MPa, and 12.1%, respectively, due to the
eutectic Si being modiﬁed from a coarse plate-like structure
to a ﬁne branched structure [ 31]. Wan reported that adding
0.3 wt % Y to an Al e7Si-0.3Mg-1.0Fe casting alloy reﬁned the
secondary dendrite arm spacing to 16 mm, and the alloy
achieved a UTS of 383.86 MPa [ 32]. Liu demonstrated that
the Y additions to Al e30%Mg
2Si composite changed the
morphology of the primary Mg 2Si phase from coarse lotus-
type particles to ﬁne polyhedral shaped particles and
clearly reduced the average size of the primary Mg
2Si phase
[33]. Yttrium increases the undercooling of the solid-liquid
interface and promotes Si twinning formation in hypo-
eutectic Al eSi alloys [ 34]. Wang reported that the addition of
0.1 wt % Y reﬁned the Al grain and inhibited the recrystal-
lization of Al-0.2Ce [ 35]. In our previous study, 3 wt % Y was
added to Al e8Ce using a high-pressure die casing, resulting
in a tensile strength of 87 MPa after holding the alloy at
350
/C14 C for 200 h, which is signiﬁcantly superior to that of
Mahle piston Al alloys [ 36].
Our previous research has shown that the high-pressure
die-casting Ale8Cee3Y alloy exhibits the best comprehensive
mechanical strength and thermal stability among the casting
AleCe alloys. However, the inﬂuence of the Y content on AleCe
alloys is unclear. Therefore, the effect of variations in the Y
content on the microstructure and mechanical strength of
gravity casting Ale8Ce-xY (x ¼ 0, 1, 2, and 3) were investigated
in this study using multiscale microstructure characterization.
The volume contents of Ce and Y in the Al
11(Ce,Y)3 phase were
analysed. Finally, the relationship between the Al 11(Ce,Y)3 s
phase volume fraction and strength at ambient and high tem-
peratures are discussed. The ternary Al eCeeY system may be
an excellent candidate for castable heat resistant Al alloys.
2. Experimental methods
2.1. Alloy composition and preparation
The experimental Al e8Ce-xY (x ¼ 0, 1, 2 and 3) alloys were
synthesised using permanent mould casting with a steel cru-
cible using pure commercial Al and Ale20Ce and Ale5Y master
alloys. First, pure bulk Al was melted in a resistance furnace at
750
/C14 C. The Al e20Ce and Al e5Y master alloys were dried at
200 /C14 C for 10 h and were then added to the melt. Subsequently,
the melt was reﬁned and degassed at 750 /C14 C to remove dross/
oxides from the melt surface. Finally, the temperature was
decreased to 720
/C14 C, and the melt was poured into a permanent
metal mould to obtain ﬂat cast ingots. All alloys compositions
are quoted in wt. % unless otherwise stated. The ingot con-
tained <0.2% Fe and <0.1% Si as impurities. The elemental
concentrations of the Ale8Ce-xY alloys (listed in Table 1) were
examined using inductively coupled plasma optical analysis
(ICAP6300, Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc Inc.).

2.3. Thermal exposure and tensile testing
To measure the thermal stability, the Al eCeeY alloy speci-
mens were exposed to 350 /C14 C for 0 e200 h. After thermal
exposure, the samples were quenched with water to ambient
temperature. The specimens were mechanically ground using
400e2000 grit SiC grinding papers to prepare them for Vickers
microhardness tests. At least 20 indents with a 500 g load and
10 s dwell time were preformed across several grains using a
Struers Duramin 5 hardness tester. Dog-bone-shaped speci-
mens, 50 mm long and 2 mm thick, were prepared for tensile
testing. Tensile tests were performed at a strain rate of

4. Discussion
4.1. Effect of Y additions on the Al 11(Ce,Y)3 morphology
and structure
According to the AleY phase diagram [42], the Y atoms should
form an Al3Y phase with the Al atoms at room temperature. As
shown in Figs. 2 and 6, the Al3Y and primary Al11(Ce,Y)3 phases
were not found in the Al e8Ce-xY alloys; however, the cellular
colony eutectic Al 11(Ce,Y)3 phase appeared. With an increase
in the Y content, as shown in Table 2, the Y and Ce contents in
the Al 11(Ce,Y)3 crystal increased from 0 to 6.41 at. % and
reduced from 16.18 to 11.57 at. %, respectively. Czerwinski
reported that a coarse primary Al
11Ce3 phase appears in AleCe
alloys when the Ce content exceeds 10 wt % (about 2.11 at. %)

4.2. Effect of Y additions on strength
The strengthening mechanisms of cast aluminium alloys
include the precipitation, solid-solution, and grain-size
strengthening mechanisms [ 45,46]. According to the Al-Ce
[39] and Al eY[ 42] phase diagrams and Fig. 7 , Ce and Y are
insoluble in the Al matrix, and solution strengthening is
insigniﬁcant for the Al e8Ce-xY alloys. Figs. 1 and 2 show that
a large number of eutectic colonies and a few primary Al
matrix were present in the Al e8Ce-xY alloys. As there is little
signiﬁcance in discussing grain boundary strengthening in
these alloys, the inﬂuence of grain boundary strengthening on
the alloys has been ignored. There were low density disloca-
tions in the Al matrix [ 47] due to gravity casting and no nano-
precipitation phases in the Al e8Ce-xY alloys. Therefore, the
main strengthening mechanism in the Ale8Ce-xY alloys is the
Peierls-Nabarro stress and second phase strengthening which
comes from Al
11(Ce,Y)3 phase. Contributions from different
strengthening mechanisms that acting independently are
considered to be additive [ 48], and the yield strength of
Ale8Ce-xY alloys can be estimated from this:

5. Conclusions
The microstructure and mechanical properties of Al e8Ce-xY
(x ¼ 0, 1, 2, and 3) alloys containing eutectic colonies and Al
matrices were investigated. The following results were
obtained.
C Yttrium additions produced the eutectic lamellar
Al
11(Ce,Y)3 phase whose volume fraction increased
from 10.45 to 19.15% with increasing Y additions from
0 to 3%. The eutectic spacing and width of the eutectic
phase increased slightly. The Y content of the
Al
11(Ce,Y)3 crystal increased from 0 to 6.41 at. % and the
Ce content decreased from 16.18 to 11.57 at. %.
C The yield and tensile strength of the Ale8Ce-xY alloys at
room temperature increased from 65.1 to 72.5 MPa and
from 117.5 to 142.7 MPa, respectively, as the volume
fraction of the Al
11(Ce,Y)3 phase increased from 10.45 to
19.15%. The tensile strength of the alloys at 150, 250, and
350 /C14 C increased from 100.1 to 114.3 MPa, from 73.7 to
91.0 MPa, and from 40.4 to 57.2 MPa, respectively. The
tensile strength increased and the elongation decreased
with an increase in the volume fraction of eutectic
Al
11(Ce,Y)3 phase.
C The hardness and morphology of the eutectic
Al11(Ce,Y)3 phase remained stable under heat exposure
at 350 /C14 C for 200 h, and the eutectic colony exhibited
excellent heat stability at 350 /C14 C.
C The second phase strengthening from eutectic
Al11(Ce,Y)3 phase is the main strengthening mechanism
for Al e8Ce-xY alloys. At 25, 150, 250, and 350 /C14 C, the
tensile strength of the alloys increased by 2.87, 2.48, 1.88,
and 1.73 MPa, respectively, for each 1% increase in the
volume content of Al
11(Ce,Y)3 phase. Increasing the vol-
ume fraction of Al 11(Ce,Y)3 phase effectively improves
the tensile strength of the alloys, especially at 350 /C14 C.
