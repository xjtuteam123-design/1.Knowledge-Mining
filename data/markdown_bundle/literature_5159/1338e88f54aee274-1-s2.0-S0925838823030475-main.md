ABSTRACT 
Nickel-based superalloys are widely used in aircraft engines and land-based gas turbines due to their high yield 
strength and good creep resistance. Co in nickel-based superalloys can not only reduce the solubility of Al and Ti 
in the matrix and increase the content of the γ ′ phase in the alloy but also reduce the stacking fault energy, which 
is of great significance in improving the creep and stress rupture properties. The effects of Co on the micro- 
structure and strengthening mechanism of nickel-based superalloys are briefly summarized in this paper, and 
the effects of Co on the creep and stress rupture properties are reviewed from the perspectives of stable stacking 
faults, mismatch, volume fractions of γ ′ phases, dislocation networks, antiphase boundaries and γ channels. 
1. Introduction 
Superalloys are unique multi-component metal systems with excel -
lent mechanical properties at elevated temperatures. Among the various 
types of superalloys, such as iron-based, cobalt-based and nickel-based 
alloys, nickel-based superalloys have superior properties under static, 
fatigue, and creep loading conditions to achieve outstanding high- 
temperature performance [1] . With the rapid development of the 
aerospace industry, aero-engines require high-temperature alloys with 
higher temperature capability and superior performance [2] . In 
aero-engine components and gas turbine hot end components such as 
turbine blades, guide vanes, and turbine disks [3] , nickel-based super -
alloys are the dominant structural materials used worldwide [4 – 6] . They 
account for more than 40% of the total weight of modern aircraft is 
made up of nickel-based superalloys [7] . Typical nickel-based superal -
loys contain a variety of alloying elements that strengthen the alloys 
through solid solution strengthening and precipitation strengthening 
[8] . The excellent comprehensive properties of nickel-based superalloys 
are mainly due to the two strengthening phases γ’ (Ni
3

γ’’ (Ni

the γ’’ phase at higher service temperatures due to the transformation of 
the γ’’ phase into the δ phase at high temperatures [7,9 – 12] . According 
to numerous experiments and studies, nickel-based superalloys 
strengthened by γ’ precipitates have greater high-temperature 
capability than those strengthened by γ’’ precipitates due to the higher 
thermal stability of γ’ precipitates [13,14] . 
The addition of alloying elements has a significant effect on the 
precipitation-equivalent micro-structure and properties of nickel-based 
high-temperature alloys [15] . Cr is the most important element for the 
prevention of high-temperature oxidation and corrosion in nickel-based 
alloys [16] . The addition of Cr can significantly improve oxidation and 
corrosion resistance and increase the volume fraction of the γ ′ phase and 
the γ - γ ′ phase mismatch in the alloy, thereby improving its creep prop -
erties [17,18] . However, excessive Cr addition can promote the pre -
cipitation of the topologically close-packed (TCP) phase, which can 
degrade the mechanical properties of the alloy [19] . Zhang et al. [20,21] 
demonstrated that the addition of Mo to nickel-based superalloys in -
creases the volume fraction of precipitated TCP phase and retards the 
coarsening of the γ ′ phase, thereby destabilizing the alloy structure. 
Other studies showed that the addition of Ru inhibited the formation of 
the TCP phase [22 – 25] and produced stacking faults that impeded 
dislocation motion, which serves to improve the creep properties of 
high-temperature nickel-based alloys [26,27] . 
The addition of Co affects the size, volume fraction, and morphology 
of the γ ′ phase, which is directly related to the creep and stress rupture 
properties. In addition, the addition of Co can reduce the stacking fault 
energy (SFE) to promote the formation of twins and micro-twins (MTs) 
to improve the mechanical and creep properties. The yield strength of

In addition to the dislocation sub-structure, the reduction in the

can act as barriers to dislocation movement and impede dislocation 
recovery by separating dislocations into smaller regions, which in -

improved deformation uniformity, and twin boundary surfaces that act 
as dislocation slip surfaces to accommodate plastic strain are the main 
factors that enhance plasticity. To date, SISP has been confirmed in other 
alloy systems, including Cu-Zn [45] and stainless steel [63] ; in all of 
these cases, alloying elements are added to reduce he SFE and cause 
deformation twins to dominate plastic deformation. 
It is difficult for DTs to form in multiphase superalloys with complex 
chemical compositions during plastic deformation at room temperature. 
In contrast, a unique mechanism that involves MTs is often reported, as 
shown in Fig. 5 . The significant differences between MTs and DTs are the 
twin thickness and twinning mechanism. DTs are smaller than MTs with 
only 40 – 50 at. layers and more complex, involving atomic diffusion and 
rearrangement during the thermal activation process, as shown in Fig. 6 . 
MTs are formed by dislocations cutting the γ ′ phase, in which defects 
such as anti-phase boundaries (APBs), complex stacking faults (CSFs), 
superlattice intrinsic stacking faults (SISFs), and solute-enriched stack -
ing faults (SESFs) are also formed [64] , which in turn hinder dislocation 
movement and improve material strength. 
The addition of Co decreases the SFE of nickel-based superalloys, 
making it easier to form stacking faults during deformation [34,65,66] . 
When an SISF is formed in the alloy, the mobile perfect dislocations from 
other slip systems are blocked when they encounter the stacking fault, 
decomposition occurs in the adjacent atomic layers, and the formed 
partial dislocations move in a specific direction on a certain crystal 
surface to form a stacking fault ( Fig. 7 ), thus transforming the 
single-layer SISF on the original surface into a two-layer staggered SESF. 
This mechanism may explain the formation of SESFs in the alloy. At the 
same time, on the adjacent second slip surface, the continuous sliding of 
the end partial dislocations becomes a transition form, and as multiple 
dislocations are successively blocked by the above SESF when its 
decomposition occurs to form the LC-locked group. The sliding of the 
twin dislocations formed on the adjacent slip surface can form 
micro-twins with three layers of MTs, and so on. As deformation con -
tinues, MTs of multilayer thickness form. Due to the external force and 
SFE, the twin dislocations move on different slip surfaces to form a step 
dislocation. In addition, stacking faults in the matrix often intersect as 
they form, and the resulting T-shaped configuration can lead to an in -
crease in the number of adjacent stacking faults based on the initial layer 
dislocations, causing the stacking faults to transform into micro-twins.

3. Effect of Co on the creep and stress rupture properties of 
nickel-based superalloys 
During the creep deformation of superalloys, the creep curve can be 
divided into three stages according to the variation in the deformation 
rate with time [74] . In the deceleration creep stage, deformation is 
concentrated in the γ phase, and structural evolution is not obvious due 
to the short duration of this stage. In the steady-state creep stage, the γ 
and γ ′ phases in the alloy form a rafting micro-structure under applied 
stress. In the accelerated creep stage, the intact γ ′ phase rafting 
micro-structure is gradually destroyed, and the material finally un -
dergoes creep fracture [75] . Therefore, the creep curve is not only 
related to external conditions such as temperature and stress but also 
directly controlled by the internal microstructural and structural factors 
of the material [76] . 
Creep damage caused by centrifugal stress is one of the main failure 
mechanisms of nickel-based superalloys, which are usually used for long 
periods of time at high temperatures and high stresses; therefore, su -
peralloys must have high creep resistance. Microstructural parameters 
such as γ ′ phase size, volume fraction, and γ - γ ′ two-phase lattice 
mismatch, which are influenced by Co, have different degrees of influ -
ence on the creep properties of single-crystal superalloys. 
3.1. Mismatch degree 
Generally, the addition of Co to the alloy has a slight influence on the 
lattice constants of the γ and γ ′ phases, as shown in Fig. 10 , and the effect 
of Co addition on the γ - γ ′ two-phase mismatch degree of nickel-based 
single-crystal superalloys is not significant [77,78] . Wang et al. [79] 
showed that the effect of Co on the mismatch degree was not obvious for 
alloys with different Co contents. Nathal et al. [80] determined that the 
γ - γ ′ two-phase mismatch degree was significantly influenced by Co 
during the initial creep stage of the alloy, and the γ - γ ′ two-phase 
mismatch degree gradually increased with increasing Co content. 
The degree of mismatch is one of the important structural charac -
terization parameters of single-crystal superalloys, and the positive or 
negative sign and magnitude of its value have important effects on the 
micro-structure evolution and creep behavior during creep. Nickel- 
based single-crystal superalloys usually have a negative mismatch. 
Murakami et al. [24] showed that the thermal stability of nickel-based 
single-crystal superalloys increased as the mismatch became negative. 
This is because dislocations moving in the γ matrix under creep defor -
mation conditions are subjected to a combination of the applied stress 
and the stress of the mismatch degree. When the loading direction is in 
the < 001 > direction, the total stress in the γ matrix of an alloy with a 
negative mismatch is lower than that of an alloy with a positive 
mismatch [77,81] .

Recent studies have shown that the relationship between mismatch 
and creep properties depends on the temperature and stress conditions. 
Walston et al. [82] found that increasing the Co content in nickel-based 
superalloys was beneficial to the structural stability and creep properties 
of the alloy. The addition of Co was beneficial to the creep properties of 
the alloy at 982 ℃ /248 MPa, and the creep rupture life at 
925 ℃ /207 MPa increased with increasing Co content. However, Nathal 
et al. [80] observed that the lifetime of the alloy undergoing creep was 
reduced by the addition of high cobalt content at 1093 ℃ /152 MPa, and 
an opposite trend was observed at 982 ℃ /248 MPa. This is because the 
γ ′ phase dissolution temperature decreases with increasing Co at higher 
test temperatures, and the decrease in γ ′ phase dissolution temperature 
may lead to faster particle coarsening, which reduces the creep resis -
tance of the alloy. 
Hobbs et al. [83] investigated the relationship between mismatch 
and 1.0% creep life for a series of experimental alloys, which can be 
significantly affected by temperature and pressure. At 750 ℃ /800 MPa, 
the 1.0% creep life increased as the increase in the absolute value of the 
mismatch degree increased. At 950 ℃ /1392 MPa, the 1.0% creep life 
increases with decreasing mismatch. Meanwhile, Carroll et al. [82] 
showed that the creep life of the alloy at 950 ℃ /290 MPa increased with 
increasing degree of mismatch. 
3.2. γ ′ phase volume fraction and raft exclusion 
Usually, during creep, the γ - γ ′ phases undergo directional coarsening 
and raft exclusion due to the combined effect of the applied tensile stress 
and the intrinsic mismatch stress, which is thought to be due to the 
directional diffusion of elements under the combined effects of tem -
perature and stress [75,84] . When a unidirectional tensile stress is 
applied to an alloy with a negative mismatch, dislocations preferentially 
move along the channels perpendicular to the stress axis direction, and 
these dislocations accelerate the diffusion of γ phase forming elements 
Ni, Ti, Al, etc., along the γ phase edges into the matrix channels parallel 
to the stress axis, thus promoting the growth of the γ phase along the 
direction perpendicular to the stress axis [84] . The γ - γ ′ two-phase 
mismatch affects the rafting behavior of the γ -phase during creep, and 
the larger the mismatch is, the more likely the alloy is to form rafting 
micro-structure during creep [85] .

In addition, the most significant change in the micro-structure during 
creep of single crystal superalloys with γ - γ ′ two-phase micro-structure is 
raft rowing under loading due to the combined effect of temperature and 
stress. During creep in the [001] direction, rafting behavior of single-

the mismatch [89] , with two specific rafting rows ( Fig. 11 ): (1) N-type 
rafting, where the resulting rafting structure grows in a direction 
perpendicular to the stress axis, and (2) P-type rafting (p-type plates), 
where the P-type structure grows in a direction parallel to the stress axis. 
Under drawing conditions, negatively mismatched alloys and positively 
mismatched alloys can form N-type rafting structures perpendicular to 
the stress axis under compressive conditions. In contrast, alloys with 
negative mismatches under compressive stress or positive mismatches 
under tensile stress form P-type raft structures in the direction parallel to 
the stress axis. 
Shi et al. [90] subjected nickel-based single-crystal superalloys with 
different Co contents to long-term aging at 1100 ℃ and found that the 
coarsening of bonding and rafting of the γ ′ phase slowed when the Co 
content in the alloy increased. Wang et al. [91] concluded that the in -
crease in Co content inhibited the coarsening and growth of the γ ′ phase. 
Additionally, Burgel et al. [92] found that the addition of too much Co 
increased the rate of coarsening of the γ ′ phase. 
MacKay et al. [93] investigated the initial micro-structure of 
single-crystal superalloys where the volume fraction of the γ ′ phase is the 
structural parameter that has the most significant effect on creep 
properties. Murakumo et al. [94] studied single crystal alloys and 
showed that the creep properties of the alloys increased and then 
decreased as the volume fraction of the γ ′ phase increased, as shown in 
Fig. 12 . When the volume fraction of the γ ′ phase reached 65% 70%, 
the creep properties of the alloy reached a maximum at 
900 ℃ /392 MPa; when the volume fraction was approximately 55%, 
the creep properties of the alloy were best at 1100 ℃ /140 MPa. 
The effect of the formation of this rafting micro-structure on the 
creep properties was reported in the literature: the greater the mismatch 
degree (negative) is, the easier it is for alloys to form rafting structures 
perpendicular to the stress axis under high-temperature loading condi -
tions. At high temperatures, the formation of rafting micro-structures is 
closely related to the alloy γ - γ ′ two-phase mismatch, and the larger 
(negative) the mismatch is, the easier the formation of the rafting micro- 
structure perpendicular to the direction of the stress axis under high- 
temperature loading conditions. Dislocations bypass the γ ′ particles by 
means of climbing, and the formation of a rafting micro-structure in -
creases the dislocation climbing distance, leading to an improvement in 
creep performance. Under high temperature and low stress conditions, 
rafting structures favor creep strengthening because the increased 
climbing distance in the rafting structure delays the thermally activated 
climbing of dislocations around the γ phase. However, it has been re -
ported that the presence of a rafting exclusion micro-structure reduces 
the creep properties of the alloy under low temperature and high stress 
conditions [90] . This is because dislocations are more likely to cut 
through the γ ′ phase under these experimental conditions. The formation 
of the rafting micro-structure formation does not effectively prevent 
dislocations from cutting through the γ phase, and rafting is not bene -
ficial for improving the creep properties of the alloy. In addition, fewer 
research studies have reported on the effect of the rafting 
micro-structure formed during creep of nickel-based superalloys on their 
creep properties [95] .

channels, which inhibits dislocation movement in the initial creep stage 
[96,97] . In the initial stage of the creep of nickel-based superalloys, 
dislocations in the matrix are confined to γ ′ precipitation, and the 
solid-solution strengthening effect of the high γ matrix provides appre -
ciable creep resistance.

scopic dislocation motion, and the creep rate of the alloy can be 
expressed in terms of the dislocation motion velocity. 
In the initial stage of creep, the number of γ phase dislocations in the 
matrix is small, so the dislocations largely slip and proliferate in the 
matrix. Under the influence of the stress field, dislocations of different 1/

mixed dislocations at the γ - γ ′ phase interface [102] . Disloca -
tion networks form at the γ’ - γ phase interface due to lattice mismatch at 
the γ’ - γ phase interface after high-temperature creep, tensile and fatigue 
deformation of nickel-based single crystal superalloys [99,100] , as 
shown in Fig. 13 . These interfacial dislocations react to form a dense 
dislocation network that blocks the interface and prevents further 
movement of dislocations in the matrix. The dense interfacial disloca -
tion networks can effectively prevent matrix dislocations from cutting 
into the γ ′ phase, preventing creep failure and prolonging creep life. 
These dislocation networks are formed by the combined action of 
external loading and γ - γ ′ phase lattice mismatch. 
The dislocation networks are mainly tetragonal and hexagonal, and 
the dislocation networks can regulate the lattice mismatch stress of the 
alloy and improve the stability of the γ - γ ′ phase interface. In particular, 
during the creep process, the dislocations in the matrix move toward the 
γ - γ ′ phase interface, and when they meet, the dislocation network can 
undergo a dislocation reaction. The dislocation component decomposed 
in the reaction changes the original direction of motion, promoting the 
dislocation to rise to the raft γ ′ phase and slowing formation of the stress 
concentration. Thus, the dislocation network coordinates strain hard -
ening caused by dislocation accumulation in the matrix and recovery 
softening caused by climbing, thus maintaining a dynamic balance be -
tween strain hardening and recovery softening that improves the creep 
resistance of the alloy. 
During the accelerated creep stage, mobile dislocations accumulate 
at the γ - γ ′ phase interface, forming dislocation entanglements and 
leading to stress concentrations. When the concentrated stress is greater 
than the strength of the γ phase, the interfacial dislocation network is 
destroyed. Once an interfacial dislocation network is destroyed, the 
dislocation network loses its coordinating role in maintaining dynamic 
equilibrium, and the dislocations in the matrix are cut into the γ ′ phase 
along the collapse site of the dislocation network. After the dislocations 
are cut, resistance to deformation of the γ ′ phase is weakened, and the 
strain rate of the alloy increases to fracture. 
When the dislocations encounter γ ′ phases of different sizes, they 
continue to move by cutting through and bypassing modes. However, 
these two modes of action can hardly hinder dislocations and slightly 
improve the properties of the alloy [69,70,101,102] . As deformation 
proceeds, the density of stacking faults increases, which increases their 
hindering effect on matrix dislocations. However, mutual cutting be -
tween the high-density stacking fault further limits further sliding of 
partial dislocations at both ends of the dislocations, which enhances the 
tensile strength of the alloy and improves the tensile strength of the 
alloy.

the creep temperature increases, the alloy energy increases to a critical

addition of alloying elements reduces the alloy SFE and gAPB; therefore, 
the alloy can still form layer dislocations or KeW locks during high- 
temperature creep, which is considered an important means of γ ′ 
phase strengthening. 
3.5. γ channel 
A prominent channel width dependence on the compositional evo -
lution can be observed where the concentration of Co increases for 
narrow channels [104] . Similarly, the concentrations of the γ ′ formers, i. 
e., Ni and Al, decrease as the channel width decreases. 
This channel width-dependent compositional evolution is likely to 
influence the formation of tertiary γ ′ observed within the γ matrix during 
operation. These tertiary γ ′ precipitates significantly affect creep prop -
erties [105] and were reported in γ channels with a width of 50 – 100 nm 
[106] . Wide channels exhibit a slower transition to equilibrium than 
narrower γ channels due to the longer diffusion distances. Consequently, 
wider channels have a higher concentration of γ ′ formers compared to 
the equilibrium concentration. Additionally, dislocation sliding and 
climbing motion, due to solute drag, is affected by such positional var -
iations. Therefore, instead of a uniform mechanical response, wide and 
narrow channels may respond differently not only because of inherently 
different channel interfacial compositions but also because of adjacent 
γ - γ ′ mismatch. 
The higher lattice mismatch in a nickel-based superalloy with 5 wt% 
Co content (T5 alloy) leads to a higher γ - γ ′ in the surface dislocation 
network, which significantly contributes to superior creep strength. At 
the early stage of creep deformation to γ - γ ′ when rafts form [107] , 
interfacial creases decrease, and saturation is reached. Based on the 
results of neutron diffraction measurements and estimates of dislocation 
spacing, the magnitude of unconfined lattice mismatch at 1% strain 
interruption is larger in the T5 alloy than in the T0 (nickel-based su -
peralloys with 0 wt% Co contents) alloy, and higher magnitudes of 
lattice mismatch in the T5 alloy can produce higher coherent stresses. 
Under the applied tensile stress, the net stress within the γ channel is 
lower, resulting in slower dislocation activity within the γ channel, 
which facilitates creep resistance. In raft structures, the accumulation of 
interfacial dislocations resists dislocation movement in the γ channel 
and prevents the movement of slip dislocations toward the γ - γ ′ phase 
[108] . 
4. Conclusions 
This paper provides a detailed review of the effects of Co addition on 
the creep and fatigue life of nickel-based high-temperature alloys. The 
main focus of this paper is to review the literature on the micro-structure 
and mechanical properties of nickel-based high-temperature alloys. Due 
to their advantageous mechanical properties and good creep resistance, 
nickel-based high-temperature alloys are used in important structural 
component parts in the aerospace industry, especially for aircraft en -
gines. The results of current research show the following: 
(1) Co can increase the number, shape, and stability of γ ′ phases in 
nickel-based superalloys. It also increases the volume fraction of 
secondary and tertiary γ ′ phases, which interact with dislocations 
to produce work hardening. 
(2) The addition of Co reduces the SFE and promotes the formation of 
DTs in nickel-based superalloys, which improves both the 
strength and plasticity of an alloy by stabilizing stacking defects. 
In addition, MTs, APBs, CSFs, SISFs, SESFs and other stacking
