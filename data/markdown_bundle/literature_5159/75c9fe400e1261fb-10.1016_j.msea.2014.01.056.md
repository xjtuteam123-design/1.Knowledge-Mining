abstract
Ni-based alloy Haynes 230 is of interest for high temperature applications (e.g. Intermediate Heat
eXchanger (IHX)) because of its high temperature strength and oxidation resistance. In this work,
the temperature dependence of the tensile behavior of the alloy and the strain rate effect was studied
by tensile tests in the temperature range of 25 –950 1C, at strain rates of 10
/C03 s/C01,1 0 /C04 s/C01, and
10/C05 s/C01. The tensile strength decreased with increasing temperature regardless of the strain rate. The
total elongation at rupture ﬂuctuated more with temperature. The alloy showed an abnormal variation of
the yield stress with increasing temperature i.e. the yield strength decreased slightly with temperature
up to about 600 1C and then increased between 600 and 850 1C followed by a rapid decrease. This Yield
Stress Anomaly (YSA) was however absent at the slowest strain rate of 10 /C05 s/C01. SEM and TEM
characterization was performed on fractured specimens to determine the predominant deformation
mechanisms accounting for the observed Yield Stress Anomaly.
Also, serrations were observed in the stress –strain curves at temperatures above 300 1C, the shape
and amplitude of which depended on temperature and strain rate. The analysis of the stress –strain
curves coupled with the microstructure investigation of the fractured samples showed that two different
deformation mechanisms occur depending on the temperature and strain rate. The saw-tooth type
serrations at intermediate temperatures and higher strain rates were associated with dynamic strain
aging whereas the oscillations with a sinusoidal shape were associated with dynamic recrystallization at
temperatures higher than 800 1C and slower strain rates.

1. Introduction
Haynes 230 is a solid solution nickel-based alloy being considered
for high temperature applications such as heat exchangers due to
its superior creep resistance, and excellent mechanical properties.
In addition, a protective oxide layer is formed by the additions of
chromium and aluminum, offering oxidation and carburization
resistance [1–3]. While the mechanical properties of the alloy have
been assessed, the exact mechanisms of strengthening and deforma-
tion of this alloy have not been elucidated.
This paper examines the tensile deformation behavior of Alloy 230
and aims to relate mechanical properties to observations made in the
microstructure. For that matter, uniaxial tension tests were performed
at various temperatures and strain rates; and samples were subse-
quently examined with scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-
dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDX), and transmission electron
microscopy (TEM). Such microscopy techniques were used to inspect
microstructural evolution in terms of carbide formation/dissolution,
dislocation motion and interaction, recrystallization, and deformation
mechanisms in relation with the mechanical properties.

2.2. Experiments
Tensile specimens were machined with a “dog-bone” geometry
with dimensions of 0.02 in. thickness, 0.25 in. gage width and
a 1.25 in. gage length. The tensile tests were conducted from 25 1C
to 950 1C with focus on the higher temperatures for which strain
rates of 10
–3,1 0 /C04 and 10 /C05 s/C01 were investigated, using an
Instron 5980 machine with integrated furnace. All tests were
carried out in high purity air. The samples remained at testing
temperature for 30 min before being placed in tension. Specimens
were taken to fracture. To determine the yield strength (YS), a 0.2%
offset was used and the Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS) was taken
from the maximum stress on the ﬂow curve.

4. Discussion
This section discusses the shifts observed in YS, UTS, and TE as
well as stress serrations as the temperature and strain rate change
in relation with the microstructure observations.
4.1. Yield stress anomaly
As reported in Section 3 , the yield strength was observed to
decrease monotonically with increasing temperature especially at
a strain rate of 10 /C05 s/C01. However, at the faster strain rates of 10 /C03
and 10 /C04 s/C01, an increase in yield strength from 600 –850 1Cw a s
observed (i.e. yield strength anomaly (YSA)). Several mechanisms
have been suggested to account for it, especially in ordered alloys
[6,7], but a generalized model has yet to be developed. The YSA
has generally been reported to occur in ordered, intermetallic L1 2
alloys at about half the melting point. This seems to be also the
case for Haynes 230 whose melting point is about 1330 1C, so that
YSA occurs between 45% and 60% of the melting point. As far as
the underlying mechanisms, the microstructure observations
reported in the previous section, suggest that YSA could be related
to the dissolution of the primary M
6C carbides, starting at about
600 1C and happening more extensively at 800 1C (as evidenced by
the microstructure characterization). Indeed, the dissolution of
these large carbides releases a signi ﬁcant amount of solute atoms
into the matrix which works to harden the alloy; these solute
atoms also re-precipitate to form secondary carbides as evidenced
in the TEM and SEM micrographs. Both solute atoms and smaller
carbides impede dislocation motion and contribute to the increase
in YS observed from 600 to 800 1C. The dissolution of the initial
large carbides and the precipitation of M
23C6 carbides in this range
of temperatures is in accordance with Dresh ﬁeld's study of
the effect of refractory elements on the stability of complex
carbides in Ni-base superalloys [9], and with other experimental
studies [8].
Also observed were pinned dislocations and the formation of
serrated subgrain boundaries which also contribute to hardening
of the alloy. At 900 1C and higher temperatures, the observed
decrease in YS may be explained by the fact that, while carbide
dissolution and re-precipitation continues to an even greater
degree at these higher temperatures, the softening process of
recrystallization/grain-growth process has now become the domi-
nant deformation mechanism. Also the fact that the YSA is not
observed at the lowest strain rate of 10 /C05 s/C01 may be due to the
fact that the recrystallization is the dominant one at this slow
strain rate even at 800 1C.
4.2. Flow serrations
The deformation behavior of Haynes 230 at elevated tempera-
tures showed an onset of serrated ﬂow at about 300 1C. Saw-tooth
type serrations occurring during the strain hardening process
reached a maximum in amplitude at 600 1C. Other studies con-
ducted on Haynes 230 which reported serrations curve behavior at
some temperatures without providing an exact mechanism to
account for it [10–12]. In this study, the saw-tooth type serrations
were systematically observed accompanying hardening section of

between 300 1C and 700 1C. This type of serrations is usually
associated with Portevin Le-Chatelier effect based on dynamic
strain ageing (DSA) i.e. diffusing solute atoms pinning dislocations
in place [13,14]. There are two prevalent models explaining the
ﬁner mechanisms of how DSA occurs. In the dislocation-inter-
action (DSI) model proposed by Beukel et al., solute atoms interact
with mobile dislocations which are pinned by obstacles causing an
increase in the ﬂow stress [15]. In the dislocation-dislocation
interaction (DDI) model, Kocks et al. suggested that mobile
dislocations interact with forest dislocations resulting in DSA
[16]. In our case, the microstructure observations suggest that
both mechanisms are likely at play: (i) as the dissolution of the
large precipitates put large amounts of solute into the matrix, such
as C atoms which then act to drag the dislocation motion; and
(ii) forest dislocations were indeed observed in TEM at such
temperatures where the saw-tooth serrations were observed
(Fig. 10 ). Similarly, in a study conducted by El-Azim on ferritic
stainless steel Incoloy MA956, the serrated ﬂow of this alloy
observed at 300 1C and 400 1C was associated with DSA based
on the role of carbon and nitrogen solute atoms [17]. Chen and
Chaturdevi also observed serrated stress –strain behavior in
Inconel 718 at 200 –545 1C and suggested that the serrations
involved the segregation and depletion of interstitial elements to
and from dislocation cores [18].
Associated with softening sections of the stress –strain curves at
the higher temperatures (800 1C and above) and lower strain rates
were serrations of sinusoidal appearance and smaller amplitude as
opposed to the saw-tooth type of serrations discussed above.
These sinusoidal serrations are associated with dynamic recrys-
tallization which was also evidenced in the microstructure char-
acterization. This process must compete with dynamic recovery
and is generally observed in materials with a low stacking fault
energy [19]. Dislocations are eliminated by the migration of
recrystallized high angle grain boundaries causing the material
to soften [20]. According to this mechanism, the recrystallization
nuclei form during straining, and continue to grow when the
deformation is interrupted. Li et al. observed this type of serrated
ﬂow in compression tests conducted on Inconel 625 in the
temperature range 900 –1200 1C where full recrystallization
occurred at 1 150 1C at a strain of 0.7 [21]. In our study, the
amplitude of the serrations increased with increasing tempera-
ture; such correlation between temperature and the occurrence
of serration was also observed by Petkovic et al. [22]. As far as
the effect of strain rate, a transition from periodic serrations to
continuous behavior was observed as the strain rate went from
10/C05 s/C01 to 10 /C03 s/C01: the fastest strain rate of 10 /C03 s/C01 showed a
continuous behavior (i.e. no marked oscillations) whereas the
sinusoidal serrations became more and more distinct at 10 /C04
and 10 /C05 s/C01. This is in accordance with the model developed
by Luton et al. showing that a low strain rate produces cyclic
sinusoidal serrations consistent with the periodicity of softening
and hardening [23]. These softening and hardening processes are
due to changes in the microstructure as the nucleation of serrated
sub-grain boundaries ( Fig. 18) results in hardening whereas grain
growth causes softening.
5. Conclusions
The tensile behavior of Ni-based alloy Haynes 230 was inves-
tigated at different temperatures ranging from 25 1C to 950 1C and
different strain rates. The analysis of the stress –strain curves
coupled with microstructure investigation of the fractured sam-
ples (through SEM and TEM) showed that two different deforma-
tion mechanisms occur depending on the temperature and strain
rate. Serrations were observed in the stress –strain curves at
temperatures above 300 1C, the shape and amplitude of which
depended on temperature and strain rate. The stress oscillations
observed between 300 and 800 1C (especially at higher strain
rates), accompanied strain hardening sections of the stress –strain
curves, and were characterized by a saw-tooth shape and reached
an amplitude as large as 25 MPa. They were associated with
dynamic strain aging with an in ﬂux in solute atoms involved in
the pinning and unpinning of dislocations ( “solute drag” by carbon
and nitrogen intersticials). At higher temperatures (above 800 1C)
and at slower rates, oscillations with a sinusoidal shape accom-
panied softening sections of the curves with amplitudes between
2 and 5 MPa. This type of oscillations is associated with dynamic
recrystallization. The critical factors controlling mechanical prop-
erties of these alloys at elevated temperatures such as the yield
stress anomaly observed above 600 1C, are (i) the dissolution
of the initial large M
6C carbides and the re-precipitation and
accumulation of small tungsten and molybdenum rich M 23C6
precipitates (as evidenced by electron microscopy observations),
