ABSTRACT 
Refractory high-entropy alloys have attracted widespread attention as potential structural materials for high 
temperature applications. However, there is limited understanding of their high temperature deformation 
behavior and tribological response. Here, the temperature dependence of strain rate sensitivity and wear 
behavior was evaluated for two refractory high-entropy alloys, namely HfNbTiZr and HfNbTaTiZr. HfNbTaTiZr 
showed higher hardness, strength, and lower strain rate sensitivity compared to HfNbTiZr. The extrinsic strain 
rate sensitivity of both alloys obtained from micro-pillar compression was higher compared to the intrinsic values 
from nanoindentation due to the larger surface to volume ratio in micro-pillars leading to annihilation of dis -
locations and their easier movement. The decrease in strain rate sensitivity with increase in temperature was 
attributed to activation of both edge and screw dislocations as well as phonon drag controlled screw dislocation 
motion at high temperatures. Wear rate for both alloys increased with increase in sliding cycle frequency due to 
frictional heating. Wear rate also increased with increase in temperature from 298 K to 423 K and then decreased 
with further increase in temperature to 573 K. The underlying mechanism changed from abrasive wear at room 
temperature to extensive ploughing with fine wear debris at intermediate temperatures and oxidative wear at the 
higher temperatures. 
1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), with multiple principal elements, 
represent a new alloy design strategy focused on the central region of a 
multi-component phase space. They have attracted widespread atten -
tion [ 1 , 2 ] due to their excellent properties including high strength, 
outstanding fracture toughness, and enhanced resistance to creep, 
corrosion, wear, and irradiation damage [ 3 – 10 ] arising from the core 
high entropy effects [ 1 , 2 ]. Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), with 
constituent refractory elements, are particularly attractive for extreme 
environments [ 11 – 13 ] because they maintain relatively high strength up 
to temperatures of 1600 
◦
C making them appealing for aerospace ap -
plications as potential substitute for nickel-based superalloys [ 12 , 14 ]. 
However, majority of the reports on mechanical properties of RHEAs 
and their strain-rate dependent deformation behavior are limited to 
room temperature [ 15 – 18 ]. 
The deformation mechanisms of high-entropy alloys at room tem -
perature have been widely studied using uniaxial tension/compression, 
nanoindentation and micro-pillar compression [ 18 – 26 ]. Strain rate 
sensitivity ( m ) has been reported to be in the range of 0.01 – 0.065 for 
face centered cubic (FCC) HEAs [ 19 – 22 , 25 , 26 ], 0.01 – 0.06 for body 
centered cubic (BCC) HEAs [ 18 , 22 ], and 0.04 – 0.2 for multi-phase HEAs 
[ 23 , 24 ]. Strain-rate dependence of deformation has been studied at 
elevated temperatures for FCC HEAs showing strong thermally activated 
deformation in the temperature range of 100 – 150 
◦
C and decreasing 
thermally activated contribution at higher temperatures [ 27 ]. However, 
there are few reports and very limited understanding of temperature 
effect on thermally activated deformation in refractory HEAs. 
Studies on tribological response of RHEAs at elevated temperatures 
are critical in evaluating their application merit [ 28 ]. Most studies on 
wear behavior of HEAs are related to the influence of different alloying 
additions. Wear resistance of HEAs was improved by surface treatments 
such as nitriding [ 29 ] and boriding [ 30 ] as well as by addition of 
alloying elements such as Al [ 31 , 32 ], Ti [ 33 ], Cu [ 34 ], Ag [ 35 ], Pb [ 36 ], 
Bi [ 36 ], C [ 37 ], TiC [ 38 ], and Y

Here, we report on the rate-dependence of deformation and tribo -

the temperature range of 298 K – 573 K. Both these RHEAs showed single-

compressive plasticity under quasi-static conditions [ 12 , 13 ]. The effect 
of temperature on intrinsic deformation behavior of the RHEAs was 
investigated by nanoindentation and corresponding extrinsic mecha -
nism was evaluated using micro-pillar compression at different 
strain-rates. The strain rate sensitivity (SRS) obtained using micro-pillar 
compression provides insights into the effect of surface to volume ratio 
and is typically referred to as extrinsic SRS. In contrast, SRS obtained by 
nano-indentation is a measure of the intrinsic response of the alloy and 
the influence of confined volume, where surface effects are smaller 
compared to micro-pillar compression tests [ 18 ]. These findings pave 
the way for further research on thermally activated deformation 
behavior and tribological properties of RHEAs over a wide range of 
temperatures to evaluate their potential in high-temperature structural 
applications.

Deformation in BCC metals is dictated by thermally activated motion of 
screw dislocations, particularly at low temperatures [ 45 – 47 ]. In 
contrast, deformation in FCC metals is dominated by dislocation glide 
over obstacles [ 48 ]. HfNbTiZr showed nearly two times higher m value 
than HfNbTaTiZr, but the range in strain rate sensitivity indicates that 
deformation mechanism is dislocation dominated in both the alloys 
[ 49 ]. The lower m for HfNbTaTiZr may be attributed to solid solution 
strengthening and higher lattice distortion in the five-component alloy 
with the addition of larger-sized Ta atoms leading to greater resistance 
to dislocation motion [ 22 ]. Increased interaction of dislocations with 
local composition fluctuations from the addition of alloying elements 
may restrict dislocation motion and thus lower strain rate sensitivity 
[ 24 ].

3.4. Effect of temperature on deformation behavior 
The average hardness for HfNbTiZr and HfNbTaTiZr, measured using 
nanoindentation at a peak load of 1000 mN, is shown in Fig. 6 (a) as a 
function of temperature. The hardness for HfNbTiZr and HfNbTaTiZr 
were in the range of 2.7 – 3.1 GPa and 3.4 – 3.8 GPa, respectively. The 
hardness decreased by about 15% for HfNbTiZr and by 9% for 
HfNbTaTiZr with increase in temperature from 298 K to 573 K. The 
small change in hardness over the temperature range studied indicates 
good resistance to thermal softening for both the alloys, attributed to 
enhanced solid solution strengthening effect [ 62 , 63 ]. HfNbTaTiZr 
showed ~ 25% higher hardness compared to HfNbTiZr in the temper -
ature range studied, indicating enhanced solid solution strengthening 
and lattice distortion with the addition of Ta. Slight increase in hardness 
at 473 K for HfNbTaTiZr and at 573 K for HfNbTiZr may be due to solute 
drag effect at higher temperatures, which increases the resistance to 
dislocation movement [ 64 , 65 ]. Hardness versus strain-rate at tempera -
tures of 373 K, 473 K, and 573 K for HfNbTiZr and HfNbTaTiZr are 
shown in Fig. 6 (b) – (c), respectively, in double logarithmic scale. Strain 
rate sensitivity was calculated from slope of each curve and shown in 
Fig. 6 (d) as a function of temperature. SRS decreased with the increase 
in temperature. Similar behavior was reported previously for pure Cr 
[ 64 , 66 ] as well as CoCrFeMnNi HEA [ 27 ] and included in Fig. 6 (d) for 
comparison. SRS for single-crystal MoNbTaW HEA micro-pillars was 
found to decrease from ~ 0.025 at room temperature to ~ 0.01 at 873 K 
[ 4 ]. Higher SRS indicates higher resistance to fracture and necking and 
greater degree of strain hardening in the alloy [ 67 ]. In contrast, lower 
SRS favors localized deformation and leads to plastic instability during 
deformation [ 68 ]. BCC metals/alloys typically show kink pair defor -
mation mechanism, where screw dislocation mobility is thermally 
activated [ 69 ]. Below a critical temperature, screw dislocations are less 
mobile and thermal energy is not sufficient for activation over the 
Peierls barrier. This is attributed to the complicated three-dimensional 
core structure of screw dislocations in BCC metals resulting in higher 
SRS at lower temperatures [ 66 ]. Above the critical temperature, Peierls 
stress decreases leading to double-kink nucleation and movement of the 
screw segment [ 47 , 70 ]. Therefore, both screw and edge dislocations 
have similar mobility due to thermal activation over the Peierls barrier 
[ 64 , 71 ]. Decrease in SRS value for both the alloys at higher tempera -
tures may be due to the reduction in Peierls barrier and activation of 
edge and screw dislocations resulting in their increased interaction and 
entanglement. Phonon drag may also control screw dislocation motion 
at high temperatures which leads to lower SRS [ 61 , 62 ]. Negative SRS 
was obtained at the temperatures of 473 K and 573 K for HfNbTiZr and 
573 K for HfNbTaTiZr. Negative strain rate sensitivity has been reported 
for nanocrystalline metals as well as conventional aluminum alloys [ 69 , 
72 , 73 ] and may be attributed to dynamic strain-aging effects and 
instability in plastic flow associated with interaction between moving 
dislocations and solute atoms (Portevin-le Chatelier effect) [ 69 ].

4. Conclusions 
Deformation behavior and tribological response of two refractory 
high-entropy alloys, namely HfNbTiZr and HfNbTaTiZr, were evaluated 
from room temperature to 573 K. HfNbTaTiZr refractory high-entropy 
alloy showed higher hardness and strength as well as lower strain rate 
sensitivity compared to the four-component HfNbTiZr. This may be 
attributed to solid solution strengthening and lattice distortion with the 
addition of Ta to the four-component alloy. The extrinsic strain rate 
sensitivity obtained from micro-pillar compression was higher 
compared to the intrinsic effect measured from nanoindentation due to 
larger surface to volume ratio of micro-pillars, which led to annihilation 
of dislocations through the free surface. Wear rate at room temperature 
increased by ~ 30% for HfNbTiZr and by ~ 400% for HfNbTaTiZr with 
increase in reciprocation frequency from 5 Hz to 50 Hz. Scanning elec -
tron microscopy of the wear tracks at higher sliding cycle frequency 
showed plastic deformation and wear debris without any evidence of 
oxidation due to frictional heating. Strain rate sensitivity decreased with 
increase in temperature due to: (i) activation of both edge and screw 
dislocations at higher temperature leading to their increased
