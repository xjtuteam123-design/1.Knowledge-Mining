ABSTRACT 
The cryogenic mechanical properties and deformation behaviour of additively manufactured (AM) medium/ 
high-entropy alloys (MEA/HEA) and their composites are seldom studied. This work fills this gap by systemat -
ically studying the mechanical properties, deformation behaviour and strengthening mechanism of the additively 
manufactured CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy (HEA) and 2 wt% TiC/CoCrFeMnNi composite (HEC) at room 
(293 K) and cryogenic (93 K) temperatures. The TiC nanoparticles promote the densification behaviour, and they 
are uniformly distributed around and in interior of the cellular substructures of the HEC sample. The tensile tests 
reveal that the strength and ductility can be simultaneously increased in both HEA and HEC samples when the 
temperature decreases from 293 K to 93 K. The TiC nanoparticles significantly promote a remarkable combi -
nation of strength and ductility in the HEC at both 293 K and 93 K, and the cryogenic tensile strength of HEC 
(1506 ± 6.6 MPa) is the highest value reported for AM-fabricated MEA/HEAs. For the HEA sample, the signif -
icant increase in strength and ductility is attributed to an additional hardening contribution induced by multiple 
twin systems at 93 K, which results in a steady and even increased strain hardening rate. When the temperature 
decreases from 293 K to 93 K, a remarkable yield strength increment (344.4 MP) is found in the HEC sample, 
which is significantly higher than that of HEA (229.0 MPa). The difference in the increase in yield strength 
between HEA and HEC sample is ascribed to the difference in dislocation strengthening and Orowan strength -
ening, which are related to the temperature-dependent thermal expansion coefficient and elastic modulus, 
respectively. However, the HEC sample has a smaller ductility increase (10.3%) than the HEA sample (26.4%), 
since no additional hardening mechanism is generated during the cryogenic deformation in the HEC sample. This 
research provides a new route to design materials with a good combination of strength and ductility for cryogenic 
applications. 
1. Introduction 
Medium/high-entropy alloys (MEA/HEAs) are a newly developed 
material class that garnered extensive interest in the field of structural 
materials [1] . In particular, the equiatomic CoCrFeMnNi, which was 
first developed in the HEA family, has superior comprehensive proper -
ties, such as outstanding room and cryogenic mechanical properties [2] , 
hydrogen embrittlement resistance [3] and fracture toughness [4] . 
Despite these advantages, the CoCrFeMnNi HEA still has some problems, 
such as a relatively low yield strength. Therefore, new routes must be 
developed to achieve high-performance face-centered cubic (FCC) HEA 
materials with better mechanical properties, such as fabrication 
methods, composition modifications or a combination of both. 
The current preparation methods for HEA materials mainly includes 
powder metallurgy [5] , electromagnetic-suspension casting or vacuum 
arc-melting and subsequent cold/hot rolling [6,7] . However, refined 
microstructures and geometrically complex structures are difficult to 
achieve using these traditional fabrication methods. Recent

advancements in additive manufacturing (AM) provide a new route to 
fabricate high-performance metallic materials. As a classical laser ad -
ditive manufacturing (AM) technology for metallic materials, laser 
powder bed fusion (LPBF) technology is straightforward to acquire 
intricate three-dimensional (3D) components with material-structure- 
performance integration [8] . In recent years, the research topic of 
AM-fabricated HEAs has drawn much attention in the research field of 
AM [9] . In addition to the advantage in fabricating geometrical-complex 
components, the formation of heterogeneous cellular substructures with 
high-density dislocations plays an important role in strengthening and 
toughening HEA materials by modulating the dislocation motion during 
the deformation of AM-fabricated HEAs [10,11] . To further improve the 
performance of CoCrFeMnNi HEAs, adding reinforcements into the 
matrix is an effective method. Ceramic particles are considered excellent 
candidates to strengthen pure metallic materials because of their low 
density, high hardness and elastic modulus as well as extraordinary wear 
resistance, which have been successfully used in additively manufac -
tured iron matrix composites [12,13] , nickel superalloy matrix com -
posites [14] and Al metal matrix composites [15] . However, few works 
have examined AM-fabricated MEA/HEAs reinforced by ceramic parti -
cles [16,17] to date. 
A cryogenic temperature environment introduces higher re -
quirements on the mechanical properties of HEAs. HEAs with FCC 
structures fabricated by traditional processing usually exhibit superior 
cryogenic strength and ductility. Gludovatz et al. [4,18] firstly found a 
fracture-resistant CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy and an exceptional 
damage-tolerance of a CoCrNi medium-entropy alloy for cryogenic ap -
plications. However, there are only a few studies on the cryogenic me -
chanical properties of AM-fabricated MEA/HEAs and no reports in the 
field of MEA/HEA matrix composites (to the best of the authors ’ 
knowledge). Most reports have found simultaneously enhanced 
strength-ductility in AM-fabricated FCC MEA/HEAs [19] . However, 
Weng et al. [20] found that when the temperature decreased from 298 K 
to 143 K, the strength of the CoCrNi MEA almost linearly increased, 
whereas the elongation decreased. This behaviour was ascribed to the 
interaction of grain boundaries with massive twin boundaries, which 
formed microvoids along the grain boundaries and ultimately caused 
premature failure under higher stress at cryogenic temperature. Similar 
results were found by Kim et al. [21] , where the in-situ nano-oxide 
reinforced CoCrFeMnNi HEA matrix nanocomposite fabricated by ad -
ditive manufacturing had decreased elongation at cryogenic tempera -
ture. Therefore, the cryogenic deformation mechanism of high entropy 
alloys needs to be further studied. Ceramic particles are a good candi -
date to increase the yield strength. Some studies examined the me -
chanical properties of Al matrix composites reinforced by microsized 
ceramic particles at cryogenic temperature [22] , the studies about 
cryogenic properties and strengthening mechanisms of MEA/HEA ma -
trix composites have never reported. 
Motivated by these issues, CoCrFeMnNi HEA was selected as a model 
material, and a small faction (2 wt%) of TiC nanoparticles were added to 
the CoCrFeMnNi matrix in order to achieve a better combination of 
strength and ductility. Here we explored the influence of TiC nano -
particles on the as-printed microstructure and mechanical properties of 
CoCrFeMnNi HEAs at 293 K and 93 K. The pure CoCrFeMnNi HEA, 
manufactured with the same processing parameters, serves hereby as a 
counterpart. Systematical investigations of the deformation substructure 
evolving in the un- and reinforced materials at 293 K and 93 K are used 
to reveal the difference in mechanical properties. The difference in un -
derlying strengthening mechanisms at cryogenic temperature between 
HEA and HEC is investigated by tensile testing and theorical calcula -
tions. The present work sheds light on achieving a good strength- 
ductility combination of FCC HEA matrix composites at room and 
cryogenic temperature. 
2. Experimental method 
2.1. Materials and preparation 
Equiatomic CoCrFeMnNi HEA powders with a size range of 15 – 53 
µ m were fabricated by gas atomization; the average grain size of the 
atomized powders is 7.9 ± 2.1 µ m ( Fig. 1 a and b). The composition (at 
%) of powders measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spec -
trometry (ICP-MS), was 19.3% Co, 20.5% Cr, 20.3% Fe, 20.5% Mn and 
19.4% Ni. This result is highly consistent with the nominal composition 
( Table 1 ). Nano-sized TiC particles (2 wt%) with a mean size of ~50 nm 
were mixed with the HEA powders to obtain the composite powders

milling parameters can be found in our former work [16] . After ball 
milling, the particle size of HEA powders changes a little in Fig. 1 c, and 
no significant damage is observed in the shape of the particles. The 
dispersed TiC nanoparticles were uniformly distributed on the surface of 
the HEA powders according to secondary electron microscopy and en -

3.2. Grain structures of HEA and HEC samples 
Fig. 6 displays the grain orientation, grain size distribution and 
corresponding pole figures (PF) of the HEA and HEC samples. Fig. 6 a and 
e show the grains and grain orientation along the TD-ND planes: the 
grain refinement from 18.3 µ m to 10.9 µ m was obvious after the addi -
tion of TiC nanoparticles, which indicates that the TiC nanoparticles 
have a strong pinning effect at grain boundaries to hinder grain growth 
during the processing. This phenomenon was observed in the micro -
structure along the transverse direction-building direction (TD-BD) 
planes, where the grain size decreases from 27.2 µ m to 15.2 µ m. Fig. 6 i 
and j show the PF that corresponds to the TD-BD plane, which quantifies 
the microtexture intensities along the three crystallographic directions. 
Generally, the overall crystallographic microtexture is described by the 
highest maximum intensity. The maximum texture index is 6.83 for HEA 
and 8.15 for HEC. It is well accepted that LPBF-fabricated materials 
normally present a strong anisotropic microstructure and texture index 
along the building direction due to the epitaxial growth mode [9] . The 
addition of reinforcements often reduces the texture intensity of com -
posites fabricated by traditional manufacturing methods because re -
inforcements often restrict grain rotations during deformation [38,39] . 
However, the texture development also depends on the size of rein -
forcement particles, and micrometer-scale particles have a greater effect 
on the texture development than that of nanoparticles [40] . In the field 
of additive manufacturing, the effect of nanoparticles on texture also 
depends on the volume fraction of the particles [13,15,41] . In this study, 
there is no obvious difference in the texture index between HEC and 
HEA samples, which indicates that the addition of 2 wt% TiC nano -
particles has no significant influence on the grain anisotropy. In addi -
tion, we suspect that the nanoparticles mainly distributed around the 
cellular substructure also have a weaker influence on the grain anisot -
ropy than that of deformed materials, where most of reinforcements are 
distributed at grain boundaries. The XRD pattern has often been used for

4.2.2. Nanoparticle-dependent strain hardening behaviour and ductility 
The HEC shows a significantly improved strength but a slightly 
decreased elongation at 293 K compared to the HEA, which are mainly 
attributed to the fewer defects and higher strain hardening rate. As 
shown in Figs. 8 and 9 , although the deformation mechanisms of both 
HEA and HEC samples at 293 K are dominated by wavy dislocation 
glide, the strain hardening rate of the HEC sample is always higher than 
that of the HEA sample during the deformation because TiC re -
inforcements promote more dislocation accumulation. Dislocation loops 
are created around the TiC nanoparticles associated with the dislocation 
bypassing the nanoparticles. There is a large difference in the Burgers 
vector and elastic modulus between the matrix and TiC nanoparticles, 
which results in higher back-stresses than other deformation micro -
structure on gliding dislocations in matrix, thus producing significantly 
high strain hardening rate and corresponding high strength of the HEC 
sample. However, the incompatibility of plastic deformation across the 
matrix-nanoparticle interface results in stress concentration, which 
often acts as a source of micro-crack initiation. Thus, the addition of TiC 
nanoparticles results in slightly decreased ductility in comparison to that 
of the HEA sample. Besides, the semi-coherent interface between TiC 
nanoparticles and HEA matrix also contributes to a good elongation of 
the HEC (22.3 ± 0.8%) when deformed at 293 K.

of 5%, and then keeps a steady strain hardening rate. This behaviour is 
attributed to the formation and propagation of DTs, which provides 
additional work hardening by acting as additional obstacles to disloca -
tion glide. This phenomenon is known as the so-called dynamic Hall & 
Petch effect. Since it becomes more difficult to create new DT systems in 
the already strain hardened grains, the subsequent stage 4 is charac -
terized by a decline in the work hardening rate of HEA, as reported in 
multiple TRIP steels [65] . Such a strain hardening behaviour yields a 
significant ductility increment. The dominant strengthening mechanism 
is still wary dislocation slip in the interior of cellular substructures and 
dislocation pile-up around the network consisting of nanoparticles and 
cellular substructures in the HEC sample, which leads to a higher but 
more quickly decreased strain hardening rate at stage 2 than that of the 
HEA sample. The high strain hardening rate results in high strength of 
HEC at 93 K. Although the dominant hardening mechanism is un -
changed, due to reduced SFE, there are some additional hardening 
mechanisms in the HEC sample when deformed at 93 K, such as higher 
density DTs and SFs, and they contribute to slowly decreased strain 
hardening behaviour at stage 3. However, high-density dislocation 
concentration around nanoparticles and grain boundaries results in 
earlier crack initiation and relatively low strain hardening capacity of 
the HEC sample at 93 K after the strain of 16%, which is the reason for 
difference in ductility increment between HEA and HEC samples at 93 K. 
5. Conclusion 
The CoCrFeMnNi HEA matrix composite reinforced with 2 wt% TiC 
nanoparticles was successfully fabricated via laser-based additive 
manufacturing. Compared with the performance at room temperature, 
the strength and ductility of both HEA and HEC samples are simulta -
neously improved at cryogenic temperature. The underlying deforma -
tion mechanisms of the HEA and HEC samples at room and cryogenic 
temperature have been analyzed. Following conclusions can be drawn 
from this study: 
(1) TiC nanoparticles are distributed uniformly in the AM-fabricated 
HEC sample, mainly along the cellular substructure. The addition 
of nanoparticles not only prevents Mn loss, so as to have better 
densification, but also yields better strengthening effect in the 
HEC sample in contrast to the pure HEA sample. This effect 
contributes to a significant increase of strength (41%) along with 
only a slightly decreased ductility (10.8%) at 293 K. 
(2) When the temperature decreases from 293 K to 93 K, the strength 
and ductility of the HEA sample simultaneously increase, which is 
attributed to an additional hardening mechanism induced by 
multilevel deformation twins (DTs). A significant strength 
increment is found in the HEC sample from 293 K decreased to 
93 K, significantly higher than that of the HEA. The difference in 
the strength increment is mainly ascribed to the more efficient 
dislocation strengthening and Orowan strengthening mecha -
nisms, due to the temperature-dependent elastic modulus and 
CTE difference between the matrix and TiC nanoparticles. While 
the ductility increment of the HEC sample is smaller than that of 
the HEA sample, which is related to the unchanged dominant 
hardening mechanism in the HEC sample. 
(3) The comprehensive mechanical properties of the HEC sample are 
significantly improved at 293 K and 93 K, due to the incorporated 
TiC nanoparticle. Particularly, the cryogenic tensile strength of 
the fabricated HEC sample shows the highest value in the re -
ported AM-fabricated MEA/HEAs, to date. The research provides 
a new route for the design of high-performance materials with 
also geometrically complex structure for cryogenic applications.
