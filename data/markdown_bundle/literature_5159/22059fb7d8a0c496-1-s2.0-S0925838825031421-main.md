ABSTRACT

1. Introduction
Owing to the prolonged operation of mechanical components in 
harsh service environments such as high temperature, high speed, heavy 
load, irradiation, and low temperature, the pursuit of wear-resistant 
alloy materials remains strong [1 – 4] . Particularly in high-temperature 
applications, alloy systems are commonly used as dynamic sealing 
pairs, linkage adjustment systems, bearings, and other components in 
the power and transmission systems of aerospace equipment. 
High-temperature wear typically leads to a degradation of equipment 
performance and may even trigger safety incidents [5,6] . With the rapid 
development of high-end equipment, the requirements for these alloys 
have become increasingly stringent, not only involving complex service 
environments at higher temperatures ( > 600
◦
C), but also often serving as 
frictional pairs for the high-temperature-resistant ceramic components, 
such as alumina and silicon nitride [7,8] . By enhancing the tribological 
properties of alloys under these conditions, it is possible to reduce en -
ergy loss, improve energy utilization efficiency, and simultaneously 
decrease maintenance costs and enhance economic benefits [6] .
In recent years, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have garnered wide -
spread attention due to their unique combination of four major effects: 
high-entropy effect, lattice distortion effect, sluggish diffusion effect, 
and cocktail effect. These effects endow HEAs with a distinctive and 
superior set of properties [7,9 – 12] . Among them, the CoCrFeNi series of 
alloys, as one of the most classic HEA systems, exhibits excellent 
ductility, oxidation resistance, and high-temperature phase structural 
stability, which endow them with potential applications at elevated 
temperatures [13 – 15] . Unfortunately, their wear resistance under 
high-temperature conditions is quite limited [16,17] . Although the 
in-situ-formed oxide layer at elevated temperatures serves to some 
extent as a lubricant or load-bearing component, the alloy ’ s inferior 
strength/hardness, coupled with its thermal soften behavior as

nucleation and propagation of cracks, causing the alloy material to 
detach from the wear surface and thereby resulting in adhesive or

To address the aforementioned issues, the performance configura -
tions of the CoCrFeNi HEA system can be optimized through strategies 
that involve compositional or structural regulation [19 – 21] . The 
concept of eutectic design in HEAs was first proposed by Lu et al. [22] . 
Eutectic HEAs typically consist of alternating soft and hard dual-phase 
structures. Generally, the soft phase is a solid solution composed of 
multiple elements, while the hard phase is an intermetallic compound 
that lies between ceramics and metals [23] . Studies have shown that the 
excellent wear resistance of eutectic HEAs at room temperature (RT) is 
attributed to the synergistic effect between the soft and hard phases, 
where the hard phase contributes to resisting wear deformation, and the 
soft phase avoids crack initiation induced by potential stress concen -
tration through plastic deformation [24] . In high-temperature environ -
ments, the design of eutectic structures is also expected to effectively 
enhance wear resistance. Research by Yang et al. [25] indicates that the 
performance improvement is not only due to the excellent anti-softening 
ability of the eutectic structure but also related to the additional 
load-bearing/lubricating effects provided by the oxidation products on 
the wear surface. In addition to the aforementioned factors, the tribo -
logical characteristics of eutectic HEAs during high-temperature friction 
processes also involve the evolution of the subsurface microstructure 
and the original microstructure, including the formation of gradient 
grains and the spheroidization behavior of the eutectic structure [26, 
27] . However, the tribological mechanisms related to structur -
e/composition/temperature are not well understood, which signifi -
cantly impede the further development of high-temperature 
wear-resistant eutectic HEAs.
Based on the above information, in this work, two HEAs, namely the 
CoCrFeNi HEA and the eutectic-strengthened CoCrFeNiTa0.4 HEA, were 
prepared using vacuum arc melting. The tribological behaviors of the 
two alloys at RT, 600

focus on studying the tribological mechanisms related to temperature, 
composition, and structure. The results obtained provide insights for the 
development of high-temperature wear-resistant HEAs.
2. Experimental methods
2.1. Material preparation
Selecting pure metallic blocks of Co, Cr, Fe, Ni, and Ta as raw ma -
terials, HEA ingots were prepared through a vacuum arc melting pro -
cess. Two types of HEA ingots were synthesized, namely CoCrFeNi and 
CoCrFeNiTa0.4 (in atomic ratio), which are designated as Ta0 and 
Ta0.4, respectively, based on the content of Ta atoms. The specific 
procedure is as follows: The pure metallic blocks were weighed ac -
cording to the atomic ratios required for the two alloys. After ultrasonic 
cleaning and drying, they were transferred into a water-cooled copper 
crucible within a vacuum arc melting furnace. Prior to melting, the 
furnace chamber was purged with gas, with the purging process being 
repeated more than three times to ensure the absence of residual air 
within the chamber. Subsequently, the melting was conducted under an 
argon atmosphere. To ensure the homogeneity of the composition, both 
alloys were remelted more than five times. The ingots were then allowed 
to cool, resulting in the desired bulk materials.

2.3. Tribological performance testing and characterization
High-temperature reciprocating friction and wear performance tests

Zhongke Kaihua Technology Development Co., Ltd.) equipped with a 
high-temperature chamber, with the temperatures set at RT, 600

roughness of the balls were 6 mm and less than 10 nm, respectively. The 
test conditions at different temperatures were set to a constant load of 
10 N, a reciprocating stroke of 5 mm, a frequency of 5 Hz, and a total 
sliding time of 30 min. The built-in software of the testing machine can 
record the relationship between the coefficient of friction and sliding 
time in real-time. After the friction tests, the dimensions of the wear 
track cross-section perpendicular to the sliding direction were measured 
on a 2D profilometer (Alpha-Step-D300, KLA Instruments), and the wear 
volume was calculated in the built-in software. The wear rate was 
calculated using the following equation: W = V/FD , where W is the wear 
rate, V is the wear volume, F is the normal load, and D is the total sliding 
distance. Note: Before the experiment, the sample surface must be finely 
mechanically polished to avoid the adverse effects of surface defects on 
the test results. Reciprocating sliding friction tests at different temper -
atures were repeated at least three times to ensure accuracy.
After the friction tests, SEM were used to observe the wear track 
surfaces, wear track cross-sections, and elemental distribution of the 
alloys. Transmission electron microscope (TEM, FEITalos F200X) was 
employed to further examine the structural and compositional evolution 
of the wear track cross-sections. For TEM samples, extraction was per -
formed along the normal-direction and sliding direction (ND-SD) using a 
focused ion beam system (FIB, FEI Helios G5 UX) with a gallium ion 
source. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, PHI 5000 VersaProbe 
III) was utilized for the analysis of the chemical composition of the wear 
surfaces. The binding energy of the narrow spectrum was calibrated by 
setting the characteristic peak of hydrocarbon C 1 s to 284.8 eV.

3.7. TEM analysis of wear track cross-section
In order to elucidate the mechanism by which the evolution of the 
wear surface microstructure affects the wear performance of the alloy, 
TEM samples were extracted along the SD-ND direction using FIB 
technology, followed by observation with TEM, with results as shown in 
Fig. 12 and Fig. 13 . After friction testing at 800 
◦
C, it can be clearly 
observed that an oxide layer with a thickness of about 1.1 µ m was 
formed on the wear subsurface of the Ta0.4 alloy ( Fig. 12 b). The 
microscopic morphology of the different regions shows a clear depth- 
dependence ( Fig. 12 a). The topmost layer is an ultrafine oxide nano- 
crystalline layer with a thickness of about 400 nm, containing a large 
number of Ta-rich ultrafine oxide particles ( Fig. 12 c). Beneath it is a 
transition layer composed of unoxidized matrix and oxide nano- 
crystalline, where the Cr-rich oxide layer (with a thickness of 600 nm) 
formed by preferential oxidation of Cr elements and stress-induced 
refinement of Laves phase particles are observed. No significant large- 
sized pores and cracks were found in the ultrafine oxide nano- 
crystalline layer and the transition layer, and these layers are tightly 
bonded to the deformed matrix, indicating their effective protective 
effect on the matrix. Moreover, studies have also shown that this 
friction-induced dense heterogeneous layer not only provides effective 
protection but also facilitate regulating the stress distribution from the 
wear surface to the interior, preventing stress concentration that could 
initiate crack nucleation [27,54,55] .
Fig. 13 presents the TEM analysis of the wear subsurface of the Ta0.4 
alloy after friction tests at 1000 
◦
C. It can be observed that the wear 
morphologies exhibit significant differences compared to that at 800 
◦
C 
( Fig. 13 a). The ultrafine oxide nano-crystalline surface layer undergoes 
noticeable fragmentation, with shear cracks extending along the sliding 
direction, leading to increased wear mass loss and a relatively rough 
wear surface morphology ( Fig. 13 b). The maximum depth of the oxide 
layer increases to 4 µ m , where the rapid diffusion and oxidation of Cr 
elements have led to the formation of a coarse Cr-rich oxide layer (with a 
thickness of 1.1 µ m), a result is attributed to the increased oxygen 
driving force ( Fig. 13 d) [26,56] . It is noticeable that cracks are primarily 
distributed in the vicinity of the coarse Cr-rich oxide layer ( Fig. 13 a, c), 
indicating that the formation of such coarse oxide layers leads to strain 
regulation instability. Moreover, the dissolution-globularization of the 
Laves phase weakens the load-bearing capacity of the matrix, which 
makes the interface between the different layers very rough ( Fig. 13 a). 
This also implies that localized regions have underwent uneven defor -
mation, which is another potential factor contributing to crack forma -
tion. Therefore, at this temperature, the rapid oxidation caused by the 
increased oxygen driving force and the structural evolution of the 
lamellar eutectic structure are detrimental to the tribological 
performance.
