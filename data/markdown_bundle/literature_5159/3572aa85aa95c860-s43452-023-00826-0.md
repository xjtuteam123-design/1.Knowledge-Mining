REVIEW ARTICLE
A review on the effect of various rolling regimes (cryo, cold, warm, hot) 
and post‑annealing on high‑entropy alloys: microstructure evolution, 
deformation mechanisms, and mechanical properties
Moslem Tayyebi1,2  · Mahdi DerakhshaniMolayousefi2
Received: 26 June 2023 / Revised: 31 October 2023 / Accepted: 12 November 2023 / Published online: 8 December 2023 
© Wroclaw University of Science and Technology 2023, corrected publication 2023
Abstract
Despite their complex composition, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have a simple structure and have been extensively researched 
for their ability to achieve unique properties through thermo-mechanical processing (TMP). This review studies the effects 
of different rolling regimes and post-annealing on single and multiphase HEAs, analyzing how TMP leads to microstructural 
changes and improved mechanical properties. The rolling changes the shape and utilizes different mechanisms determined 
by the rolling temperature to strengthen the raw materials, thus affecting the HEAs' properties. The microstructural evolu-
tion of HEAs during annealing is affected by various parameters such as annealing time, annealing temperature, and heating 
rate, which impact the strength–ductility combination of HEAs. According to the literature, cryogenic rolling (cryo-rolling), 
as opposed to cold rolling, provides greater strengthening. This is due to the faster microstructural evolution kinetics in 
cryo-rolling. Thus, cryo-rolling enhances the strengthening by activating deformation twinning at earlier stages through the 
intersection of twins and more shear banding, which is preferred to microbands for HEAs with low stacking fault energy 
(SFE). Rolling at high temperatures is the most suitable approach for HEAs with low workability. Warm and hot rolling 
enable microstructure evolution through deformation mechanisms, including grain growth, recovery, recrystallization, and 
phase transformation based on the process temperature. The ratio of recovery to recrystallization depends on temperature 
and SFE, with recovery dominating in alloys with high SFE and at lower rolling temperatures, while recrystallization is more 
prevalent for alloys with low SFE and at higher temperatures, leading to specific ductility–strength synergy.

1 Introduction
A class of multi-component alloys is high-entropy alloys 
(HEAs) [1] that comprise solid solutions with five or more 
principal elements in similar or dissimilar atomic percent-
ages [2]. Despite the existence of copious components, these 
alloys usually possess relatively simple crystal structures 
instead of complex or intermetallic phases, like face-cen -
tered cubic (denoted as FCC), body-centered cubic (denoted 
as BCC) [3], BCC + FCC [4], and hexagonal close-packed 
(denoted as HCP) [5]. The remarkable attribute of HEAs has 
been linked to the high configurational entropy due to mix-
ing multiple constituents that stabilize simple solid solution 
phases [6]. Meanwhile, the presence of numerous elements 
leads to lattice distortions in HEAs, which stem from the 
atomic sizes of the surrounding atoms. This phenomenon, 
depicted in Fig.  1a, is more pronounced in HEAs than in 
conventional alloys, where the same atoms likely encircle 
the matrix atoms. The lattice distortions strongly impact the 
properties of HEAs and the effect of temperature on their 
properties. Also, lattice distortion induces solid solution 
strengthening, which significantly enhances the strength 
and hardness [7 ]. It is imperative to consider all elements

HEAs. Among single-phase HEAs, the BCC and FCC HEAs 
have received extensive research attention in recent years 
[9]. Although most of these alloys (FCCs) exhibit high duc-

of BCC type acquire hardness at the expense of ductility 
[11]. On the other hand, multiphase HEAs consist of two or 
more phases that differ in composition and structure [12], in 
which the hard phases enhance the strength of the material. 
In contrast, the soft phases ensure their ductility [13].
Recent research has demonstrated that HEAs offer a 
range of attractive [14] and adjustable properties in con-
trast to typical alloys [15]. HEAs are often distinguished 
by their exceptional and unconventional characteristics, 
namely, excellent thermal stability [16], high hardness and 
strength, friction, and soft magnetic properties [17], low/
high-temperature strength, and commendable corrosion [18] 
and oxidation resistance [19]. Also, the peculiar nature of 
these HEAs’ properties can be ascribed to the main influ-
ence of multi-component solid solution formation, which 
includes distorted lattice structures, cocktail effects, slug-
gish diffusion (see Fig. 1d) [20], and substantial deformation 
nano-twins formation likely due to the higher number of 
elements present [21]. Nevertheless, as-cast HEAs are typi-
cally unsuitable for direct exploitation due to the presence of 
inherent defects arising from casting processes, which may 
eventually lead to the formation of cracks [22] and, conse-
quently, premature material failure [23]. Furthermore, the 
coarse microstructure resulting from casting further exac-
erbates the mechanical properties issue [24]. Therefore, to 
address these challenges, researchers often employ a rolling 
process [25] followed by heat treatment [26] to mitigate cast-
ing defects [27] and refine microstructure [28] prior to con-
ducting their investigations. An optimized post-annealing 
heat treatment facilitates achieving a good combination of 
strength–ductility balance [29].
Rolling is a simple, cost-effective, and energy-efficient 
process involving metal compression between rolls, trans-
forming its cross section into a thin sheet [30]. Through this 
process, symmetrically or asymmetrically, [31] the material 
undergoes severe plastic deformation (SPD) [32] at a range 
of different temperatures [33]. Numerous rolling processes 
have been developed recently to meet the growing demand 
for processing HEAs [34]. There are several different types 
of rolling, including cryogenic rolling [35], cold rolling [36], 
warm rolling [37], and hot rolling [38]. Cryo-rolling, an SPD 
technique, has emerged as a process that is used to produce 
an ultrafine grain structure with low strain compared to other 
techniques [39]. This technique involves the deformation 
of materials at very low temperatures, cryogenic tempera-
ture [14], a factor that helps to restrict the dislocation in the 
material by destroying the dynamic recovery [40]. Cold roll-
ing is another form of rolling conducted at room temperature 
[41]. The kinetics in cold rolling is slower than cryogenic 
rolling [14]. It is reported that rolling at room temperature

Rolling increases the strength of the HEAs at the expense 
of their ductility [48]. A subsequent annealing is employed 
to overcome this problem [49]. During the annealing pro-
cess, various factors influence the microstructure and the 
properties of the HEAs, including annealing temperature 
[50], heating rate [ 20], and annealing time [ 51]. The main 
controlling factor is the annealing temperature. Annealing 
of alloys that have undergone deformation generally results 
in softening caused by dislocation annihilation [40] due to 
recovery [52], dislocation cross-slip [53], or climbing dur -
ing recrystallization [54]. However, some alloys that have 
undergone large plastic deformation exhibit annealing hard-
ening [55], attributed to three mechanisms that occur dur -
ing annealing at temperatures below the recrystallization 
temperature [56]. Various mechanisms can be employed to 
hinder a second-phase formation [57], dislocation source 
reduction, annealing-induced twinning or martensitic 
transformation, and solute segregation [58]. Studies have 
identified that some HEAs show second-phase formation 
after annealing under two discrete conditions, namely SPD 
before and after annealing for an extended time [59]. Pre-
cipitates induced by extending the annealing time precipi-
tate normally along the grain boundary, whereas precipitates 
induced by SPD and annealing are also observed [60]. Hard-
ening induced by annealing typically occurs at low tempera-
tures primarily because of the multiphase nanostructures’ 
formation in the HEA matrix [61].
Although numerous reviews have been conducted on 
HEAs, this current review aims to comprehensively ana-
lyze and summarize the most pertinent information on the 
microstructure evolution and mechanical properties of HEAs 
subjected to cryo, cold, warm, and hot rolling processes, and 
a section on the constituent phases and strengthening effect 
of rolling and subsequent annealing summary. Specifically, 
the focus will be on purposefully single and multiphase 
HEAs. This review comprises core sections, each dedicated 
to how the microstructure changes and the corresponding 
mechanical properties of single and multiphase HEAs. The 
sections are arranged logically, beginning with the intro-
duction, followed by the cryo, cold, warm, and hot rolling 
sections. Finally, the review will conclude with a summary 
of findings and prospects for these technologies.
2 The deformation of HEAs’ during rolling 
and post‑annealing
The rolling technique has been successfully utilized in the 
strengthening of as-cast HEAs. Rolling, followed by anneal-
ing treatment, has been effectively utilized to enhance the 
strength and ductility of HEAs [62]. During rolling, specific 
mechanisms are activated based on various factors, includ-
ing rolling temperature, constitutional phases [14], and 
SFE [63]. Thus, the remarkable strengthening of HEA as 
a result of rolling is evident in the literature [64]. In addi-
tion, post-annealing tailors the combination of ductility and 
strength [65]. The microstructural changes are controlled by 
annealing heat treatment temperature [66]. These mecha-
nisms include recrystallization [ 29], precipitation, recov -
ery [67], annealing twins [68], and grain growth inhibition 
[69]. Therefore, optimized heat treatment after the rolling 
process enables the strength–ductility balance [70], result-
ing in improved mechanical properties compared to as-cast 
material [71] both in single-phase [72] and multiphase struc-
tures [52]. The rolling parameters, HEAs’ composition, yield 
strength, ultimate tensile strength, and elongation values 
have been presented in Table  1 for the alloys studied by 
researchers.
2.1 Single‑phase HEAs
Among the single-phase HEAs, FCC and BCC phases are 
the focus of much research. However, the HCP phase in 
HEAs is uncommon because many elements favor FCC or 
BCC phases [73]. Under low strains, single-phase HEAs 
with FCC structure strengthen due to the dislocations result-
ing in cell formation [74], deformation twins [13], micro-
bands [35], and shear bands[39]. However, in addition to 
the mentioned mechanisms, the deformation mechanisms of 
BCC HEAs are also governed by deformation bands [72], 
dislocation density increase [75], and kink bands [76]. At 
constant temperature, FCC HEAs deformation is mainly 
governed by SFE [77]. For HEAs with low SFE, deforma-
tion twinning is dominant [78 ]. In contrast, higher SFE 
facilitates cross-slip, which makes microband formation the 
primary mechanism [79]. In sum, before intermediate strain, 
microstructure changes with SFE [80]. However, the shear 
banding mechanism governs the deformation under heavy 
deformation for both BCC and FCC HEAs [75].

2.2 Multiphase HEAs
In addition to the single-phase deformation mechanisms 
[81], multiphase HEAs also possess additional mechanisms 
for strengthening, such as martensitic transformation [82] 
induced by strain and strengthening via back stress, which 
originates from the dislocations’ accumulation at the bound-
aries of constructing phases [83]. The geometrically nec-
essary dislocations (GNDs) [80] and the martensite (FCC 
toward HCP) transformation [84] have a further strengthen-
ing effect in dual-phase HEAs. In some dual-phase HEAs, 
Soft FCC phases tend to experience a great deal of strain in 
comparison to hard phases [85 ]. As a result, the strength -
ening influence of the dual-phase HEAs is generally more 
significant than that of the single-phase HEAs [86].
3 Cryo‑rolling
3.1 Single‑phase HEAs
3.1.1 Microstructure evolution
During cryo-rolling, microstructure evolution involves the 
elongation of grains, twinning, and the twin lamellae rota-
tion toward the direction of the metal flow. Figure  2a1–a3 
shows the grains’ elongation at low temperatures in the roll-
ing direction [14]. Microstructure development is controlled 
by twinning and the activity of dislocations, which is contin-
gent upon the SFE of HEAs [39]. Notably, the temperature 
exhibits a direct correlation with the SFE of the HEA [77]. 
The deformation twinning activation during cryo-rolling 
occurs earlier due to the lower SFE at the temperature of 
liquid nitrogen [77]. The increase in strain activates vari-
ous twin systems and distorts the nano-twins, causing frag-
mentation of the microstructure. Following the cryo-rolling, 
shear bands were introduced by inducing higher strain [39]. 
The evolution of a single-phase FCC HEA microstructure 
under rolling conditions at cryogenic temperature is dem-
onstrated schematically in Fig.  2b1–b3. During the defor -
mation’s initial stages, the strain is relatively low, and the 
striped grains undergo slicing by thin twins. By increasing 
strain to about 60%, the twinning onset happens, which is 
related to the increased dislocation density. When the strain 
further increases, the twins’ number increases with the pro-
gression of strain, holding a significant portion of the grain 
volume. During the final stages of thickness reduction, dis-
locations are rearranged into dense arrays or cell boundaries 
in twinned and un-twinned regions [31].

structure evolution. Also, cryo-rolling before heat treatment 
affects the annealed structure of alloys [13]. It was reported

3.1.2 Mechanical properties
The cryo-rolling process effectively reduced grain size 
and hindered dislocation movement, leading to improved 
mechanical properties [13]. To show this grain refinement 
efficiency during cryo-rolled and annealing, note the com-
parison made between cryo-rolled and cold-rolled samples 
in Fig. 3a. Due to the increased number of nucleation sites 
present in the cryo-rolled and annealed sample, grain size 
reduction is considerable [39]. The calculation of twin vol-
ume fraction (Fig.  3b) in rolling deformation reveals that 
cold rolling produces higher dislocation density, while cryo-
rolling results in a higher fraction of twins during the initial 
stages of deformation, indicating higher strengthening by 
twin boundaries in cryo-rolling [14].
The higher twinning activity, as evidenced by a smaller 
strain, increased fraction of twinned grains, and decreased 
spacing between twins, describe the onset of twinning dur -
ing rolling at the cryogenic temperature [14]. Therefore, the 
activation of twins during cryo-rolling is a crucial factor in 
achieving excellent mechanical properties at low tempera-
tures, while grain refinement was attributed to the increased 
number of grain boundaries hindering dislocation movement 
[39].

confined space between twin boundaries. Figure  3c shows

3.2.1 Microstructure evolution

HEAs by introducing various mechanisms [10]. Figure  4a

 CoCrFeNi2.1nb0.2 HEA that is composed of a gray matrix

5.1 Single‑phase HEAs
5.1.1 Microstructure evolution
The deformation mechanisms alter with increasing tem-
perature as diffusive processes become more significant, 
leading to changes in slip systems and deformation activa -
tion across grain boundaries [14]. Warm rolling refers to 
the rolling process at an intermediate temperature between 
cold and hot rolling [24]. Recovery is the main mecha-
nism during warm rolling. The as-cast microstructure of 
the Al5Co15Cr30Fe25Ni25 HEA is depicted in Fig.  13a, b, 
which is a single-phase structure comprising inhomoge-
neous, coarse, equiaxed grains [24]. The deformation of 
the mentioned alloy during warm rolling is governed by 
temperature [52]. Most grain boundaries are high angle, 
with certain grains separated by LAGBs. Misorientation 
within the grains of nondeformed HEAs before rolling is 
practically negligible [24].
The highly elongated parent grains and lower disloca-
tion density [6 ] of the Al5Co15Cr30Fe25Ni25 HEA rolled 
at 450 °C with 83% reduction microstructure share simi-
larities with that of the cold-rolled alloy, see Fig.  13c, 
d [24]. Reheating during intermediate rolling steps may 
assist in certain dislocation annihilation through recov -
ery processes. At temperatures below the recrystallization 
of the FCC HEA, recovery and partial recrystallization 
are mainly observed [52]. The microstructure of the alloy 
consists of both fine-grained and coarse-grained regions, 
with various grain sizes in the fine-grained areas, indicat-
ing insufficient strain to prompt new grain nucleation but 
adequate strain to induce significant local changes in ori-
entation, resulting in the development of new grains [ 24].

6 Hot rolling
6.1 Single‑phase HEAs
6.1.1 Microstructure evolution
Various attempts have been undertaken to understand the 
microstructural modifications in HEAs during hot rolling, 
which is a recognized technique for improving their strength. 
The mechanisms of recrystallization, recovery, phase trans-
formation, and grain growth at extreme trolling temperatures 
significantly impact the development of microstructure dur-
ing high hot rolling [26]. The temperature of rolling and 
SFE would determine the mechanisms and the proportional 
dominance of each mechanism [63]. By the way, at relatively 
elevated temperatures, recrystallization is the predominant 
mechanism during hot rolling [ 38]. Figure  16a shows the 
microstructure of the 90% hot rolled single-phase CrMn-
FeCoNi HEA sheet comprising equiaxed and slightly elon-
gated grains, with a substructure and annealing twins. How-
ever, hot-rolled and annealed HEA at 800 °C demonstrates 
fully recrystallized microstructure (see Fig.  16b). Notably, 
the recrystallized grain size of the hot-rolled material is 
smaller than that of the initial material but larger than that 
of the cold-rolled material recrystallized at 700°C because 
of hot rolling deformation that occurred during inter pass 
heating [26]. Annealing the hot rolled sheet at low tempera-
tures like CrMnFeCoNi at 450 °C does change the hot rolled 
microstructure; whereas, above 700 °C, the recrystallization 
temperature changes to a fully recrystallized state [38]. The 
plastic deformation during intermediate rolling passes leads 
to improved kinetics of second-phase formation [87].
6.1.2 Mechanical properties
The customization of mechanical properties of HEAs can 
be achieved through deformation and annealing, resulting 
in similar hardness of thermo-mechanically processed and 
annealed samples in cases where recrystallization does not 
occur at temperatures below 600 °C [26]. However, thermo-
mechanically treated alloys show higher hardness compared 
to annealed alloys upon recrystallization because of the for-
mation of dislocations during processing, which strengthens 
the recrystallized grains. In contrast, annealed alloys lack 
dislocations [ 24]. However, the dynamic recovery process 
can improve ductility, while annealing at low temperatures 
increases yield strength and reduces ductility due to pre-
cipitation strengthening and the static recovery process 
[88]. Annealing at higher temperatures can significantly 
decrease strength and increase ductility, particularly as the 
temperature rises [38]. This phenomenon is mainly caused 
by the recrystallization and grain growth processes [87]. An 
ideal combination of strength and ductility can be achieved 
through a microstructure that has undergone significant 
recovery after hot rolling or through a partially recrystal-
lized state following annealing at approximately 700 °C [89].

7 Conclusion
This review focuses on the impact of distinct rolling 
regimes on the mechanical properties of a diverse range 
of HEAs. The microstructure evolution and associated 
mechanical properties brought about by rolling and anneal-
ing, which can be adjusted to enhance the performance 
of synthesized HEA, have been discussed and analyzed. 
The mechanisms responsible for strengthening HEA, 
which operates during deformation, have been elucidated 
regarding microstructure deformation. These changes 
depend on the phase type, working temperature, and SFE 
of the HEA. Additional strengthening can be applied by 
adjusting the total strain and strain path during rolling. 
Additionally, annealing temperature plays a crucial role in 
microstructure evolution during processing. Optimization 
of annealing time, heating rate, and other related param-
eters can further enhance the strength–ductility synergy. 
After assessing the influence of potential parameters on 
microstructure evolution and tensile properties during 
annealing in HEA, some potential futuristic ideas are pre-
sented below:

1. After cold rolling, crystal defects such as dislocations 
are produced in the material, which stores high defor -
mation energy. Subsequently, annealing releases the 
stored energy, recrystallizing the deformed structure to 
refine the grains and obtain ideal structures via reason-
able heat treatment. At the same time, HEAs exhibit a 
high recrystallization temperature and strong resistance 
to grain growth during annealing due to precipitates near 
grain boundaries that suppress the grain coarsening of 
the FCC matrix.
2. The influence mechanism of rolling at cryogenic and 
room temperatures is almost similar; however, the kinet-
ics of the former are rather faster, which causes the supe-
rior strengthening effect of cryogenic rolling compared 
to cold rolling.
3. Under rolling deformation, the presence of strain gradi-
ents at interface/domain boundaries during deformation 
necessitates the creation of GND due to the mechani-
cal incompatibility of the heterogeneous microstructure 
between different hardness domains. Dislocation pile-up 
generates long-range back stress, making it difficult for 
dislocations to move in softer recrystallized grains until 
the surrounding hard areas yield, resulting in improved 
strength–ductility synergy.
4. Grain boundary strengthening is the main factor impact-
ing yield stress in cold-rolled HEAs. In contrast, hot-
rolled HEAs combine dislocation, precipitation, and 
grain boundary strengthening, with precipitation having 
a minor influence.
5. Cold rolling and warm rolling are inappropriate for 
large-size samples due to increased casting defects and 
large deformation resistance. However, based on the 
research data, high-temperature hot rolling can achieve 
comparable tensile properties with small-size samples, 
suggesting its feasibility for engineering applications.
8 Future remarks
1. The result of Reddy et al. [86] data has proposed and 
implemented a pioneering hybrid processing method, 
which is anticipated to be strategically utilized for refin-
ing and optimizing. Based on their result, investigating 
various combinations of thermo-mechanical processing 
routes can produce unparalleled mechanical properties.
2. Further investigation is required to examine the detailed 
impact of temperature on the SFE on twinning in differ-
ent HEAs, as the available literature data regarding this 
matter remains contentious.
3. Given the vastness of HEAs, their related research data, 
and design parameters window utilizing computational 
methods are desirable due to the growing power of com-
putational and artificial tools. Among them are newly 
emerged machine learning approaches to design, predict 
the properties, and optimize new and existing HEAs. 
Meanwhile, simulating innovative thermo-mechanical 
processing routes, including hybrid rolling and subse -
quent combination of heat treatments, is an interesting 
field to study further.
4. There is a great need to further investigate single BCC 
and HCP phase HEAs in terms of rolling and heat treat-
ment processing to unearth the relationship between the 
microstructural metamorphosis of these alloys.
5. It is reported for HfNbTiZr refractory HEA [65] that 
by annealing at elevated temperatures, the hardness 
increases while the recrystallization and grain growth 
occur, which conflicts with the Hal-Petch relationship. 
Thus, additional examinations, such as in situ micros-
copy or molecular simulations, are necessary to verify 
the phenomenon above.
