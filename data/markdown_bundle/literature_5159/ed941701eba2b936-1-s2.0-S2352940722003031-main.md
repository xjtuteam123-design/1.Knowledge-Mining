ABSTRACT 
Materials are needed that can tolerate increasingly harsh environments, especially ones that retain high strength 
at extreme temperatures. Higher melting temperature alloys, like those consisting primarily of refractory ele -
ments, can greatly increase the efficiency of turbomachinery used in grid electricity production worldwide. 
Existing alloys, including Ni- and Co-based superalloys, used in components like turbine blades, bearings, and 
seals, remain a performance limiting factor due to their propensity, despite extensive optimization efforts, for 
softening and diffusion-driven elongation at temperatures often well above half their melting point. To address 
this critical materials challenge, we present results from integrating additive manufacturing and alloy design to 
guide significant improvements in performance via traditionally difficult-to-manufacture refractory alloys. We 
present an example of a multi-principal element alloy (MPEA), consisting of five refractory elements and 
aluminum, that exhibited high hardness and specific strength surpassing other known alloys, including super
-
alloys. The alloy shows negligible softening up to 800◦C and consists of four compositionally distinct phases, in 
distinction to previous work on MPEAs. Density functional theory calculations reveal a thermodynamic expla -
nation for the observed temperature-independent hardness and favorability for the formation of this multiplicity 
of phases. 
1. Introduction 
An estimated 80% of US [1] and 73% of world [2] electricity pro-
duction is derived from fossil fuels and nuclear power, which rely on gas 
turbines for conversion of heat to electricity. The energy efficiency of 
these gas turbines for grid electricity generation, along with gas turbines 
for aircraft propulsion, is limited by their peak operating temperature. 
The availability of structural materials that exhibit and retain high 
strength at a high temperature remains a limiting factor in the design of 
more efficient turbomachinery; devices that will likely continue to have 
a prominent role in urgent efforts to decarbonize global energy pro -
duction. For example, intermittency challenges associated with solar 
and wind power can be addressed by generating and storing hydrogen 
[3], which could be used to fuel turbines during periods of high demand, 
as well as enabling more efficient placement of wind farms offshore. 
Recently proposed strategies to address climate change underscore the 
importance of developing higher-temperature tolerant structural mate
-
rials and enabling processing methods. 
Currently, the state-of-the-art materials for fabrication of turbine 
blades, which are exposed to extreme combinations of mechanical 
stress/strain and thermal energy, are Ni- and Co-based superalloys [4]. 
These alloys achieve high strength, creep resistance, and fracture 
toughness by combining brittle but high-strength intermetallic precipi
-
tate phases (e.g., the L12 compound γ’-Ni3Al) in a ductile face-centered 
cubic (FCC) solid-solution matrix phase. While superalloys are primarily 
produced via conventional casting and single-crystal growth methods,

exist for the processing of traditional superalloys using AM methods, 
including electron beam melting (EBM), selective-laser melting (SLM), 
and directed-energy deposition (DED), as these techniques rely on rapid

Meeting energy and transportation needs of the future will require 
rapid development and integration of higher-performance materials that 
can survive increasingly demanding operating environments. For 
example, significant effort has focused on developing Mg- and Al-based 
alloys, especially for light-weighting, but they remain unsuitable for 
high-temperature applications due to relatively low-strengths (< 800 
MPa), unfavorable strength-ductility relationships, and low melting 
temperatures [10,11]. Intermetallic alloys, such as those based on the 
Ni-Al, Ni-Si, Co-Ti, and Ti-Al systems, are another promising category 
for achieving higher strength while promoting light-weighting and 
high-temperature resiliency due to stable ordered atomic structures [10, 
11]. Indeed, aluminides have long been of interest to the gas turbine 
community but also have experienced limited adoption for aerospace 
applications due to processing challenges and low material ductility at 
ambient temperatures. As a third example, refractory metals and asso
-
ciated alloys (e.g., Niobium C-103 alloy, refractory sulfides, and Ta-W 
alloys) have potential to enable high-temperature systems, but gener
-
ally are incompatible with light-weighting opportunities due to intrin -
sically high material density. Also, refractory metals are often difficult to 
process into complex bulk geometries due to limited ductility. 
A rethinking of materials design criteria may provide a pathway for 
the development of a new class of superalloys that are tolerant to, and 
arguably enabled by, AM. Multi-principal-element alloys (MPEAs) 
constitute a sharp divergence from the aforementioned traditional alloy 
development philosophy of adding low concentrations of secondary el
-
ements to a primary constituent as a means of improving strength, 
corrosion, and other properties [12,13]. MPEA materials consisting of 
multiple (typically more than three) elements in high concentrations 
with high (configurational) entropy have received growing attention 
from the metallurgy and additive manufacturing communities [14]. 
Since the pioneering publications of Yeh et al. and Cantor et al., research 
on MPEAs has challenged conventions and ushered a paradigm shift in 
the design of high-performance alloys. An expansive set of alloys have 
been developed that exhibit unique properties often exceeding the 
performance of conventional alloys, such as high-strength at 
high-temperatures, high strength-to-weight, high fracture toughness, 
resistance to radiative and corrosive environments, high wear resistance 
and even soft magnetic behavior [13,15–24]. 
Here we explore a subset of MPEAs that thermodynamically favor the 
formation of multiple phases, including partially ordered compounds 
and fully disordered solid solutions, with nearly identical elemental 
concentrations and interatomic spacings despite large differences in 
mechanical properties and deformation behavior. We show that it is 
possible to design and additively manufacture high strength, lightweight 
multi-principal-element superalloys (MPESs) with nearly temperature- 
independent strength up to 800
◦C, exceeding the performance of cur -
rent Ni-based superalloys. Our MPES exemplar is Al- and Ti-rich, with a 
nominal composition Al 0.42Ti0.25Nb0.13Zr0.08Mo0.08Ta0.04, and was 
found to have high hardness (H = 10 15 GPa) and scratch-based 
(mode II) fracture toughness [25,26] (K c = 40 85 MPa m1/2), 
along with low density ( ρ= 5.7 g/cm3), notable room-temperature 
indentation-based ductility [27] (> 10%) and high-temperature resil -
iency (8 GPa hardness at 800 ◦C). The specific strength of the MPES 
based on the ratio of hardness and density is 1.8–2.6 GPa-cm3/g, a value 
that surpasses all known alloys, including (1) intermetallic compounds 
and legacy titanium aluminides, (2) refractory MPEAs, and (3) 
conventional Ni-based superalloys. This specific strength is a 300% 
improvement over Inconel 718 based on measured peak hardness [28] 
of 4.5 GPa and density of 8.2 g/cm 3, which gives a ratio of 0.55 
GPa-cm3/g. Notably, the present work builds upon recently proposed 
properties characteristic of MPEAs, such as those having multiphase 
microstructures that enable retention of high-strength at high temper
-
atures exceeding the performance of conventional Ni-based alloys [29, 
30]. 
However, most MPEAs literature has explored process-structure- 
property relationships developed from conventional processing 
methods, such as casting, powder metallurgy, and thin coatings [17, 
31–37], while emerging methods like AM are comparably less utilized 
[14,38–46]. In this study, metal AM was employed to simultaneously 
construct bulk MPES specimens and enable unusual nanoscale micro
-
structures that were insensitive to exposure at 800 ◦C for 1 h with 
desirable characteristics like high phase boundary coherence and 
phase-dependent strength and ductility. The use of AM here strongly 
aligns with two recent trends that improve the value proposition of AM 
by shifting away from process optimization of conventional dilute alloys 
toward (1) AM-enabled high-throughput materials discovery of MPEAs 
[12], and (2) alloy design for circumventing inherent limitations of AM 
techniques [7]. Achieving desirable structure-properties relationships 
with traditional alloys via AM remains a challenge due to the inherently 
extreme thermal conditions; for example, a century of steel 
manufacturing optimization has led to metallurgical ingot processing 
sequences that are highly misaligned with the rapid layer-wise melting 
and solidification signatures typical of AM methods [47]. Our study 
represents a novel result by demonstrating the utility of an AM-enabled 
high-throughput materials discovery platform for investigating MPEAs 
via laser-based additive methods [48,49].

2.2. Microstructural and compositional characterization 
These mechanical property data revealed several interesting features 
that are distinctly linked to the material microstructure. Specifically, 
gradual and spatially dependent trends of (1) increasing strength to
-
wards the core of the specimen normal to the build direction, (2) slightly 
decreasing strength along the build direction away from the specimen- 
baseplate interface, and (3) significant variability in strength (high 
standard deviation) with small changes in location. These trends suggest 
that the gradient in strength is correlated with build height, though this 
is likely the result of multiple factors that cannot easily be deconvoluted, 
such as variations in thermal history and accumulation of residual 
stresses. Furthermore, given the relatively coarse grain size of the as- 
built specimen, as shown via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) 
channeling contrast micrographs in Fig. 3A, B, it is likely that the vari-
ability in nanoindentation hardness is indicative of strength differences 
between phases. Conversely, variability in micro-scratch hardness, 
which is averaged over comparatively larger volumes with a coarser 
(roughly 500 nm) lateral resolution, is more indicative of an aggregate 
response, and dominated by the weakest phase and the presence of de-
fects on similar length scales. Representative SEM micrographs of the 
MPEA microstructure following high-temperature nanoindentation, 
which included exposure to 800
◦C for 1 h, are also provided in Fig. 3C, D 
and show negligible change in terms of both feature morphology and 
size compared to as-built versions of the MPEA. These results suggest 
that the alloy exhibits thermal stability in environments that approach 
half the alloy melting temperature (homologous) and supports the 
observed persistence of high hardness both during and following high- 
temperature testing. While the present results are encouraging for 
retention of hardness at extraordinary temperatures, based on negligible 
microstructure evolution following high-temperature indentation, a 1 h

have identified the origin for this multi-phase MPES with high strength 
as arising from a metastability in the complex energy-composition space 
that only occurs in a certain composition range. For a parent MPEA in a 
specific composition range, multiple alloy phase compositions with 
common BCC phases compete via segregation to lower the average Eform, 
creating a steady-state multi-phase alloy with a large number of grain 
boundaries – with associated coherency strains. Furthermore, to have 
this thermodynamically favored, we established limits for the parent 
phase (here Al/Ti and refractory elements) to affect BCC stability and its 
relative stability versus FCC and HCP. In particular, the parent alloy 
must have %Al ranging from 20 to 70%, as determined from the binary

those supporting results, see the supplemental materials (Figs. S3 and

quaternary, this thermodynamic local segregation should be possible 
to arrange in other MPEA systems to create other “high-strength” multi- 
phase alloys, e.g., with higher melting temperatures, including by grain-

been cited to be the primary cause for improving mechanical properties

When compared to other alloys, a few notable conclusions can be 
drawn in the context of AM. Alloys explored to date with AM generally 
possess hardness values ranging from 1 to 10 GPa, and corresponding 
estimated yield strength values from 0.2 to 1 GPa, which is notably 
lower than the present alloy for both properties, in some cases by an 
order-of-magnitude. Similarly beneficial results were noted in the 
context of AM processing efficiency. Most alloys, explored with laser- 
based additive methods, require significantly higher VED values to 
achieve high-quality metallurgical specimens, particularly for HEAs/ 
MPEAs and refractory-based alloys, where VED values range from 
approximately 20 to 2000 J/mm
3. Overall, the combination of high 
material hardness/estimate yield strength (10-15/3-5 GPa) and energy- 
efficient processing (VED = 40-50 J/mm 3) for the 
Al0.42Ti0.25Nb0.13Zr0.08Mo0.08Ta0.04 alloy represents a paradigm shift 
compared to the current state-of-art. 
It is useful to draw explicit comparisons with conventional Al- and 
Ti-based alloys often explored in the context of AM for light weighting 
applications. While the MPES developed in this study is enriched in 
these two elements and requires comparable VED to these conventional 
alloys for processing metallurgical specimens, the additional alloying 
constituents enable favorable multiphase microstructures that promote 
significant enhancements in specific strength/hardness. 
Notably, compositionally segregated and high-impurity content 
cryo-ball milled powder was utilized as feedstock, wherein large nega -
tive heats of mixing and very significant in situ exothermic reactions 
assisted with particulate melting and molten alloy homogenization in 
the melt pool during processing (see supplemental). This, in combina -
tion with the reduced alloy melting temperature, led to a low beam 
power (100–110 W) and, consequentially, a very low (volumetric) en

sition AM technique (see Ref. [97] and supplemental materials) to 
sufficiently heat and melt the heterogeneous powder. This case suggests 
a route for careful feedstock design (perhaps with blended master alloy 
powders in a uniform bed or in a powder feeder) to enable 
energy-efficient AM processing of alloys, particularly those with com
-

metallurgy practices (e.g., casting of the full alloy to use in gas atomi

with high concentrations of elements having dissimilar melting tem
-
peratures, such as in this case, with extremes of 933 K (Al) and 3290 K 
(Ta) for the lowest and highest constituent melting temperatures, 
respectively. While there are advantages to the cryo-milled particulate 
feedstock for exothermic assisted melting during AM, the use of con
-
ventional gas atomized master alloy powders is more scalable and can 
capture some of this beneficial effect, e.g., with blended master alloy 
powders, albeit with possible local compositional variation. We fully 
recognize that further work is necessary to optimize AM processing 
conditions with GA feedstock powders for enhanced strength and/or 
improved ductility, and to establish a viable pathway for scaling-up part 
geometries with minimal defects. However, the combination of 
cryo-milled feedstock particulate, AM-enabled high-throughput mate

rials discovery, and defect-tolerant, high-throughput mechanical testing 
can circumvent the need for process optimization during the materials 
discovery phase, enabling rapid establishment of structure-property 
datasets that then justify follow-on costly manufacturing scale-up and 
optimization. This work also diverges from trends in the MPEA literature 
of studying near-equiatomic structures of 4+element combinations [12, 
13,59], often driven by computational challenges in modeling and de

sires for maintaining single-phase solid solutions. We also demonstrate 
that, despite the use of cryo-ball-milled powder, which is known for 
possessing poor flowability properties, our approach of combining AM 
with unconventional characterization tools, e.g., micro-scratch mea
-
surements of strain rate dependent strength [51] and fracture toughness 
[25], is extremely effective for materials discovery. 
3. Summary and conclusions 
The multi-principal-element superalloy (MPES) exemplar evaluated 
here is in a unique region of composition space, being significantly 
enriched in Al and Ti compared to most other studies on other complex 
alloys. These are ideal constituents to enable light-weighting of struc -
tural parts. A reasonably close comparison to this alloy is the Al-based 
HEA first published by Senkov, et al. [98], targeting potential replace
-
ment of traditional Ni-based superalloys, which consisted of the same 
constituents but with notably higher concentrations of the refractory 
elements. Compositionally, the present alloy is expected to be signifi
-
cantly less dense than refractory-rich MPEAs, while also retaining higher 
strength and more refined microstructure achievable with the rapid 
solidification of AM. This highlights the value proposition of combining 
advanced alloys with advanced manufacturing methods to achieve un
-
usual materials properties. This MPES has some noticeable similarities 
with classical Ti-Al intermetallic alloys that have been revered for po -
tential use in structural aerospace applications, due to their high specific 
strength. Unfortunately, poor alloy workability has long challenged 
their widespread adoption [98–100]. Given that this newly discovered 
MPEA is processable via near-net shape additive methods, and that it has 
unusually high strength in an as-built form (exceeding conventional 
alloys), multiple opportunities lie ahead to accelerate the development 
of this or closely related alloys into next-generation lightweight struc
-
tural aerospace alloys by using more conventional gas-atomized (GA) 
feedstock powders. This would enable more direct comparisons of 
strength and toughness at similar part geometry length scales with other 
AM process results that use GA superalloy powders.
