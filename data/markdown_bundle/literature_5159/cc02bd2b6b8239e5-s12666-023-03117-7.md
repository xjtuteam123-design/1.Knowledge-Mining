Abstract The present work deals with microstructure evo-
lution and room temperature mechanical properties of elec-
tron beam melted Nb–10 wt.% W (Nb–W) and Nb–10 wt.% 
W–2.5 wt.% Zr (Nb–W–Zr) alloys. The consumable elec-
trodes were prepared through mechanical mixing, cold iso-
static pressing and vacuum sintering using the raw materi-
als in the form of powders. These electrodes were used for 
the preparation of ingots through electron beam melting. 
The ingots were forged at 1250 °C; subsequently, they were 
subjected to warm rolling at 1000 °C and cold rolling at 25 
°C with an intermediate annealing. The alloys were char -
acterised by optical microscope, scanning electron micros-
copy, electron backscattered diffraction and room tempera-
ture tensile test. The results show that both alloys exhibit a 
single-phase microstructure. The Nb–W–Zr alloy displays 
a higher tensile strength as compared to the Nb–W alloy. 
The Nb–W alloy under warm rolled and annealed condition 
shows a sharp yield point during tensile test, while such a 
sharp yield point is not observed for Nb–W–Zr alloy. The 
fracture surfaces of tensile tested samples reveal that Nb–W 
alloy shows a completely transgranular cleavage fracture. A 
mixed mode of fracture consisting of both dimple and cleav-
age is observed for Nb–W–Zr alloy.
Keywords Niobium alloy · Electron beam melting · Hot 
forging · ZrO2 particle
1 Introduction
Niobium and its alloys are one of the most promising high 
temperature materials for aerospace, defence and nuclear 
applications owing to their high melting point, good strength 
at high temperature and relatively low density along with 
good workability and weldability [1 –3]. The envisaged 
applications of these alloys are beyond the application tem-
perature of commonly used high temperature nickel base 
superalloys (1000 °C). Unalloyed niobium generally exhib-
its a low strength at elevated temperature and is not suit-
able for any high temperature applications. Hence, niobium 
is alloyed with a variety of alloying elements such as Mo, 
W, Ti, Zr and Hf to enhance its high temperature strength 
[4, 5]. The alloying elements such as Mo and W have been 
added to Nb to improve their high temperature strength due 
to solid solution strengthening, while elements such as Hf 
and Zr have been added both for solid solution strengthening 
as well as for dispersion strengthening [5 , 6]. Among the 
niobium-based alloys, C–103 (Nb–10Hf–1Ti) and Cb–752 
(Nb–10W–2.5Zr) are ideally suited for fabrication of an 
actively cooled combustor chamber for hypersonic vehicles 
and thrust chamber of rocket because of their combination 
of high temperature strength and room temperature ductility 
[4]. However, the strength of Cb–752 alloy is better than that 
of C–103 alloy at all temperatures. The strengthening effect 
of Cb–752 alloy comes from both solid solution strengthen-
ing as well as dispersion strengthening.
The conventional melting and casting routes are gener -
ally not suitable for preparation of niobium-based alloys 
because their melting temperatures are above the working 
temperatures of most of the common refractories. Further, 
the mechanical properties of Nb-based alloys largely depend 
on the presences of interstitial impurities such as oxygen, 
nitrogen, hydrogen and carbon. The Nb alloys become

the preparation of Nb-based alloys containing alloying ele-

alloy Cb–752 contains both low vapour pressure element W 
and high vapour pressure element Zr apart from differences 
in melting temperatures and densities as compared to Nb. 
Therefore, it is a real challenge for a metallurgist to prepare 
this alloy. Till date the information available in the litera-
ture pertaining to melting, processing and fabrication tech-
nologies of Nb–10 W–2.5Zr alloy is scanty and inadequate. 
Hence, through the present work, an attempt has been made 
to prepare Nb–10 wt.% W– 2.5 wt.% Zr alloys through EB 
melting technique using the raw materials in the form of 
elemental powders. Further, we process them under different 
processing conditions to make into sheets and compare the 
microstructure and mechanical properties. An attempt has 
also been made to prepare and process Nb–10 wt.% W alloy 
for the purpose of comparison.
2 Experimental
The raw materials used in the present work are Nb (99.5%), 
W (99.9%) and Zr (99.0%) powders. Nb–W and Nb–W–Zr 
alloy electrodes with a nominal composition of Nb–9% W 
and Nb–8% W–12%Zr were prepared through powder metal-
lurgy route using mechanical mixing, cold isostatic pressing 
and vacuum sintering with an aimed average chemical com-
position of Nb–10% W and Nb–10% W–2.5% Zr after EB 
melting. The chemical composition of the alloys is expressed 
in weight per cent throughout the text unless otherwise 
stated. Hereafter, Nb–10% W alloy is referred as Nb–W 
and Nb–10% W–2.5% Zr alloy is referred as Nb–W–Zr. The 
electrodes were drip melted in a water cooled copper cruci-
ble using a 60 kW EBM furnace. A single melting was used 
for preparation of Nb–W alloy ingot, while double melting 
was used for preparation of Nb–W–Zr alloy ingot. A power 
input of 45 kW and a mould of diameter 60 mm were used 
for melting of both the alloys.
The 60 mm diameter ingots were vacuum annealed at 
1400 °C for 24 h for homogenization. Then, they were 
machined to 56 mm diameter and applied coating with 
composition of Si–20% Cr–20% Fe. The green coating was 
diffusion annealed at 1400 °C under 1 × 10–5 m bar vacuum 
for 2 h. The coated ingots were heated to 1250 °C and forged 
to achieve a final thickness of 26 mm using a pneumatic 
forge hammer of one ton capacity. The hot forged plates 
were cut, machined to 18 mm thick and vacuum annealed at 
1400 °C for 12 h. They were then warm rolled at 1000 °C 
with a commercially available deltaglaze coating to get 
9 mm thick plates, which were again annealed at 1400 °C 
for 4 h. They were again rolled at room temperature to get 
sheets with a final thickness of 3 mm and finally annealed at 
1350 °C for 1 h. All metallic impurities present in the alloys 
were analysed by inductively coupled plasma optical emis-
sion spectroscopy (ICPOES). A LECO carbon and sulphur 
analyser was used for analysis of carbon, whereas a LECO 
hydrogen determinator was used for analysis of hydrogen. 
The analysis of oxygen and nitrogen were carried out using 
a LECO oxygen–nitrogen gas analyser.
Standard metallographic techniques were used for prep-
aration of samples for microstructural observations. The 
samples were etched with an etchant consisting of 15 ml 
 H2O, 30 ml Hf, 30 ml HF, 15 ml H2SO4 and 10 ml HNO3. 
The microstructure of the alloys was examined using opti-
cal microscope, scanning electron microscope and electron 
backscattered diffraction. The mechanical properties of the 
alloys at room temperature were evaluated by tensile test. 
Tensile samples were prepared according to ASTM E8M 
standard. Tensile tests were conducted using an Instron Uni-
versal testing machine at an initial strain rate of 1 × 10–3 per 
second. The fracture surfaces of tensile tested samples were 
examined under SEM.
3 Results and Discussion
3.1 Characterisation of as‑Cast Alloys
The as-cast macrostructure of Nb–W and Nb–W–Zr alloys 
is shown in Fig.  1. It can be seen that Nb–W alloy shows 
a three distinct regions of grain structure such as chilled 
zone, columnar zone and equiaxed zone, while Nb–W–Zr 
alloy does not exhibit such distinct regions of grain structure. 
Instead, the Nb–W–Zr alloy displays a relatively fine and 
uniform macrostructure. It is known from solidification of 
alloys that, when a liquid metal contacts the mould surface 
the chilled zone is formed due to the fast cooling of molten 
metal. The grain structure of chilled zones is fine, equiaxed 
and has a random crystallographic orientation. The nuclea-
tion of grains in the chilled zone is attributed to undercool-
ing effect that occurs when the molten liquid first makes 
the contact with a cold mould wall. As the solidification 
continues towards the centre of the casting, the undercooling 
is reduced, and the grains grow in an elongated manner. As

a result, the columnar grains are obtained. As the solidifica-
tion progresses further, the molten pool at the centre of the 
casting results in the formation of uniform grains structure. 
It is known that the centre equiaxed grains nucleate due to a 
significant amount of supercooling exists due to the solute 
segregation. The supercooling of alloy due to solute segrega-
tion is called the constitutional supercooling [12]. It gener -
ally arises from the fact that the solute segregation lowers 
the local liquidus temperature of alloy. The relatively fine 
macrostructure observed for Nb–W–Zr alloy is attributed to 
the presence of Zr in the alloy. It is generally known that Zr 
is a reactive metal, and it reacts with a residual impurities 
particularly oxygen present in the molten pool during solidi-
fication. As a result ZrO2 particles are formed in Nb–W–Zr 
alloy before the commencement of solidification. These 
 ZrO2 particles act as a heterogeneous nucleation sites dur -
ing solidification and aid for refinement of grain structure in 
Nb–W–Zr alloy. Moreover, the melting point of ZrO2 (2780 
°C) is high as compared to melting temperature of Nb–W–Zr 
alloy (2450 °C) [13].
It is generally accepted that ZrO2 is highly stable once 
it is formed because of its large negative formation of free 
energy (ΔG) as compared to the free energy formation of 
niobium and tungsten oxides. Besides, it is known that ZrO2 
generally exist in a three allotropic forms depending upon 
the temperatures [14]. It has a cubic crystal structure form 
at high temperature (> 2380 °C) with a lattice parameters 
of a = b = c = 5.09 Å. It transforms to a tetragonal form of 
structure at 2380 °C and below with a lattice parameters of 
a = b = 3.57 Å, c = 5.18 Å. Later, it transforms to a mono -
clinic crystal structure at 1205 °C and below with a lattice 
parameters of a = 5.15Ȧ, b = 5.21Ȧ and c = 5.31Ȧ. However, 
the phase transformation from a tetragonal form of ZrO2 
into a monoclinic form of ZrO2 is reported to be sluggish 
in nature [15]. It may be inferred that the cubic form of 
 ZrO2 particles is likely to act as a heterogeneous nucleation 
sites for Nb–W–Zr alloy during solidification. Moreover, the 
structural compatibility is expected between Nb and ZrO2 
particles at high temperature because of both exist in BCC 
structure. As a result, the molten liquid easily wets the ZrO2 
particles. Thus, the cubic ZrO2 assists for increasing the 
nucleation rate to obtain a fine grain structure in Nb–W–Zr 
alloy.

is shown in Table  1. It can be noted that Nb–W alloy is 
melted once, while Nb–W–Zr alloy is melted twice in order 
to get the required chemical composition in the ingots as 
the initial oxygen content is not same for both alloys. It is 
clearly seen from the Table  1 that the initial concentration 
of oxygen in Nb–W–Zr alloy is 0.65 wt.%, and in Nb–W 
alloy is 0.25 wt.%. The removal of a large quantity of oxy -
gen from Nb–W–Zr is very difficult as compared to Nb–W 
alloy. Hence, Nb–W–Zr alloy is initially melted at lower 
melt rate in order to remove maximum oxygen content, and 
subsequently, it is melted at a higher melt rate in order to 
reduce the losses of Zr during melting. It is known that the 
lower melt rates allow a longer residence time for the molten 
pool in the liquid state and enables better removal of oxy -
gen during melting of the alloy. It is obvious from Table  1 
that the oxygen content is reduced to very low levels (well 
below the specified upper limits) after double melting of 
alloy. Besides the other interstitial impurities such as C, N

3.2 Characterisation of Thermomechanically Processed 
Alloys
The optical and SEM microstructures of alloys processed 
under warm rolling and annealing conditions are illustrated 
in Figs. 2 and 3, respectively. It can be seen that both alloys 
exhibit a single-phase microstructure with completely 
recrystallized grains. However, it is found that the grain sizes 
are different for the alloys. The grain size of Nb–W–Zr alloy 
is relatively fine as compared to grain size of Nb–W alloy. 
This may be attributed to the presence of ZrO2 particles 
in Nb–W–Zr alloy. These ZrO2 particles inhibit the grain 
growth during annealing of warm worked alloy. The ten -
sile graphs of warm rolled and annealed alloys are shown 
in Fig.  4. It can be observed that the Nb–W alloy exhibits 
a sharp yield point while such a sharp yield point is not 
observed for Nb–W–Zr alloy during the tensile test. It is 
believed that the yield point phenomena occurring in steels 
during the tensile testing at low temperature is associated 
with the presence of interstitial impurities, particularly car -
bon [19]. It has also been reported for commercial grade 
Nb [20] and Ta [21, 22], that the occurrence of yield point 
phenomenon is clearly associated with presence of residual

As pure Nb has low strength, it is alloyed with W to 
increase its strength. The increase of strength of Nb by 
W addition is mainly due to the combined effect of size 
and modulus misfit associated with Nb and W atoms 
 (rNb = 145  pm and rW = 136  pm; GNb = 38 GPa and 
 GW = 160 GPa). However, the addition of W content is 
limited to 11% to maintain a reasonable plasticity in Nb 
alloy [25]. The potency of strengthening of Nb by W addi-
tion is the same for both alloys as W concentration is the 
same in both alloys. The additional strength observed 
in the Nb–W–Zr alloy as compared to the Nb–W alloy 
might have come from the grain boundary strengthening 
and solid solution strengthening of Nb–W by Zr. In addi-
tion, fine scale Zr-rich precipitates may be expected to 
be formed in Nb–W–Zr alloy. According to Nb–Zr phase 
diagram, the solid solubility of Zr in Nb is very low at 
room temperature because of their differences in crystal 
structure and atomic size [rNb = 145 pm and rZr = 160 pm). 
It may be inferred from the Nb–Zr binary phase diagram 
that it is a completely immiscible system. Further it has 
been reported that the enthalpy of mixing of Nb and Zr is 
positive (∆H mix = 17kJmol −1) [26]. Thus, Zr precipitates 
are likely to be present in Nb–W–Zr alloy. The evidences 
of Zr precipitates in Nb matrix have also been reported 
for Nb–Zr binary alloys [27]. In the present study, the 
presence of submicron sized Zr precipitates may be par -
tially responsible for obtaining higher strength values in 
Nb–W–Zr alloy. However, the kinetics of precipitation of 
Zr in Nb-based alloys is still not fully understood. The 
EBSD analysis was carried out on cold rolled and annealed 
Nb–W–Zr alloy in order to qualitatively make out whether 
any ZrO2 particles and Zr precipitates are present or not. 
The EBSD phase mapping and inverse pole figure (IPF) 
orientation image of Nb–W–Zr alloy is illustrated in Fig.  9 
and Fig.  10, respectively. It can be seen that the alloy 
exhibits a three distinct kinds of particles in Nb matrix. 
They are qualitatively identified as hexagonal Zr, tetrago-
nal ZrO2 and monoclinic ZrO2. The corresponding lattice 
parameters of them have also been measured. The lattice 
parameter of hexagonal Zr is a = b = 3.23 Å and c = 5.15 Å, 
the lattice parameter of tetragonal ZrO2 is a = b = 3.64 Å, 
c = 5.27 Å and the lattice parameter of monoclinic ZrO2 
is a = 5.14 Å, b = 5.20 Å, c = 5.31 Å. These lattice param-
eters values are comparable to the lattice parameters 
reported for these phases in the literature [28]. However, 
a further in-depth investigation of these precipitates using 
a transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is essential 
to quantitatively pinpoint whether any Zr precipitates 
are present or not in Nb–W–Zr alloy. Further the TEM 
analysis would also be helpful to predict whether the Zr

annealed alloys. The Nb–W alloy displays a completely

4 Conclusions
Nb–10W and Nb–10W–2.5Zr ingots were produced through 
EBM process using the raw materials in the form of pow -
ders. Subsequently they were subjected to thermomechanical 
processing to produce plates and sheets. The following con-
clusions are drawn based on the microstructure and tensile 
properties.
1. The electron beam melted ingots of both Nb–W and 
Nb–W–Zr alloy exhibit a single-phase microstructure. 
The alloys possess the required chemical composition 
with low levels of interstitial impurities.
2. Nb–W–Zr alloy exhibits relatively fine grains structure 
as compared to Nb–W alloy.
3. Nb–W alloy displays a sharp yield point during the 
tensile test in warm rolled and annealed condition. The 
yield point phenomenon is not observed for Nb–W alloy 
tested under cold rolled and annealed condition. Nb–W–
Zr alloy does not show any yield point phenomenon dur-
ing tensile test.
4. The strength of Nb–W–Zr alloy is higher than that of 
Nb–W alloy in both warm rolled and annealed as well 
as cold rolled and annealed conditions.
5. Nb–W alloy displays a completely transgranular cleav -
age fracture mode, while Nb–W–Zr alloy shows a mixed 
fracture mode.
