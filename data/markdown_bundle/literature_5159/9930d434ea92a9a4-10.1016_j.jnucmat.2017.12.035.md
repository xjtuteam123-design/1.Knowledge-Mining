Abstract 
 
Low-Cr oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) FeCrAl alloys were developed as accident 
tolerant fuel cladding because of their excellent oxidation resistance at very high 
temperature, high strength and improved radiation tolerance. Fe-12 Cr-5Al wt.% gas 
atomized powder was ball milled with Y
2O3+FeO, Y 2O3+ZrO 2 or Y 2O3+TiO 2, and the 
resulting powders were extruded at 950ºC. The resulting fine grain structure, particularly 
for the Ti and Zr containing alloys, led to very high strength but limited ductility. 
Comparison with variants of commercial PM2000 (Fe-20Cr-5Al) highlighted the 
significant impact of the powder consolidation step on the alloy grain size and, therefore, 
on the alloy mechanical properties at T<500ºC. These low-Cr compositions exhibited 
good oxidation resistance at 1400ºC in air and steam for 4h but could not form a 
protective alumina scale at 1450ºC, similar to observations for fine grained PM2000 
alloys. The effect of alloy grain size, Zr and Ti additions, and impurities on the alloy 
mechanical and oxidation behaviors are discussed.

1. Introduction 
After the Fukushima Daiichi accident, research programs were initiated in the United 
State (US) to develop new accident-tolerant fuel (ATF) cladding materials [1,2]. A key 
requirement is excellent oxidation resistance in steam at very high temperature, and 
extensive work at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) has been conducted to 
develop new low-Cr high strength oxidation resistant FeCrAlY alloys [3-8]. Lowering the 
Cr level hinders the formation of the embrittling Cr-rich α´ phase under irradiation at 
intermediate temperature [7,8]. Increasing the alloy strength at typical cladding operating 
temperature by alloy additions [4,6] allows for the use of thinner cladding, thus limiting 
the neutronic penalty from the replacement of Zr-based alloys by Fe-based alloys [2,9]. 
To further increase the FeCrAlY alloy strength and radiation tolerance, one solution is to 
develop low-Cr oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS) FeCrAl alloys. As for FeCrAlY 
class alloys, former commercial ODS Fe-20Cr-5Al-Y2O3 (all compositions in weight 
percent, wt.%) alloys such as Plansee’s PM2000 and Special Metal’s MA956 exhibit 
excellent oxidation resistance at high temperature in air and steam [10-13], but these 
alloys are also prone to the formation of the α ´ brittle phase in service at ~400-500ºC 
without irradiation due to their high Cr levels [14,15]. Recent work on new ODS Fe-(14-
18)Cr-(4-9)Al (all compositions in weight %) alloys confirmed that α ´ formation leads to 
alloy embrittlement after being annealed at 430-475ºC [16]. Several ODS alloys based 
on Fe-(12-15)Cr-(4-5)Al containing minor additions such Zr, Ti or Mo were investigated 
at ORNL for both ATF cladding and fusion energy (for improved Pb-Li compatibility) 
applications [17-20]. An additional alloy fabricated by ball milling Fe-12Cr-5Al powder 
with Y 2O3 + HfO 2 is not considered here because Hf is a high neutron absorber making it 
unsuitable for cladding applications. Due to the α ´-related embrittlement concern, recent 
work was focused on Fe-12Cr-5Al+0.3Y2O3 and versions with ZrO2 or TiO2 additions to 
modify the alloy oxide dispersion. In this article, we compared the tensile behavior from 
room temperature to 800ºC and the oxidation behavior up to 1450ºC in air and steam of 
these three alloys with the behavior of PM2000 variants processed by three different 
routes. The impact of alloy composition and fabrication parameters on these properties is 
discussed and compared to prior work on ODS FeCr and wrought FeCrAl alloys.

2. Experimental Procedure 
2.1. Materials 
The Fe-12Cr-5Al gas atomized powder was purchased from ATI Powder Metals with a 
powder size ranging from 45 to 150 µm. A Zoz model CM08 Simoloyer was used to ball 
mill the FeCrAl powder for 40h in Ar with Y 
2O3 and FeO powder (125YF), Y 2O3 and 
ZrO 2 powder (125YZ) and Y 2O3 + TiO 2 powder (125YT). All the powders were then 
degassed at 300ºC for 24h in an extrusion can, sealed, and finally extruded at 950ºC after 
annealing at this temperature for 1h. A similar procedure was used to produce alloy 
PM2KP using PM2000 powder purchased from Plansee. Finally, a PM2000 rod 64mm in 
diameter was purchased from Plansee in the un-recrystallized state. The alloy was tested 
in the non-recrystallized small grain state (PM2K_SG), and after annealing for 1h at 
1380ºC, which resulted in a grain size of several millimeters (PM2K_LG). 
The composition of the powders and alloys are given in Table 1. The goal of adding FeO 
125YF was to keep the O content in all the Fe-12Cr-5Al alloys at ~0.2wt%. The PM2000 
variants had very similar compositions and lower level of impurities, C, N and S, 
compared with the alloys fabricated at ORNL from the Fe-12Cr-5Al powder, referred to 
as 125Y alloys in this article. The higher C and N concentrations in the 125Y alloys in 
comparison with the Fe-12Cr-5Al gas atomized powder is likely due to the ball milling 
step. Low-carbon steel balls were indeed used as ball milling media, and small leaks will 
lead to slight air ingress even if the powder is processed in Ar. Details on the fabrication 
procedure for the PM2000 powder and rod were not provided by Plansee, but the same 
company was able to produce Fe-14Cr-2W-0.3Ti-0.3Y 2O3 ball milled powder with 
similar low levels of C and N, 43 and 85 wppm, respectively [21]. The extrusion step is 
not expected to affect alloy chemistry, and the slightly lower C and N concentrations in 
the PM2KP alloy in comparison with the PM2000 powder reflects small local variation 
of the powder/alloy chemistry.

2.2. Tensile testing 
Sub-sized tensile specimens (SS-3) with a 7.62 mm long and 0.762 or 1 mm thick gage 
section were machined along the alloy extrusion direction. Tensile testing was carried out 
at 20ºC-800ºC in ambient air with a strain rate of 10 
-3s-1 using an MTS hydraulic machine 
or an Instron electro-mechanical machine. One tensile specimen per composition was 
tested at each temperature. Tensile specimens were held at the test temperature for 10 
minutes prior to initializing each test to ensure a homogeneous sample temperature 
during mechanical testing. 
2.3. Oxidation 
Rectangular ~20x10x1-1.5mm coupons were machined from the extruded bars, polished 
to a 600 grit surface finish and then cleaned in acetone and methanol.

As extruded and oxidized coupons were mounted and polished using standard

extracted by Focused Ion Beam (FIB, Hitachi model NB500) using the in-situ lift-out 
method. TEM microstructure analysis was conducted using an FEI Talos F200X

3.2. Tensile properties 
The ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and total plastic deformation from room temperature 
to 800 ºC for the 125Y alloys, alloy PM2KP, and a high strength wrought Fe-13Cr-4.5Al-
2Mo (C135M) developed at ORNL [6] are compared in Figure 4. The 125YF, 125YZ and 
PM2KP alloys exhibited very similar tensile strength up to 600ºC, from ~1300MPa at 
room temperature to ~540MPa at 600ºC. Alloy PM2KP exhibited lower strength at 
800 ºC in comparison to all of the 125Y series alloys. However, PM2KP alloy had a 
higher ductility at all temperatures compared to 125YF and 125YZ, with a plastic 
deformation approaching 20% at 400ºC. The behavior of 125YT differed drastically at 
temperatures below 400 ºC, with very high tensile strength but very limited ductility, and 
more variability. This variation is to be expected with the low number of specimens 
mechanically tested and the small specimen size. At temperatures above ~600ºC, the 
UTS of 125YT was similar to the UTS measured for 125YF and 125YZ, with a peak in 
the ductility at ~600 ºC, as was observed for the PM2KP alloy. All the 125Y FeCrAl ODS 
alloys exhibited significantly higher mechanical strength but lower ductility than the 
wrought FeCrAl alloy.

4. Discussion 
 
4.1 Tensile properties 
 
Schneibel et al. analyzed the different strengthening mechanisms in alloy PM2000 and 
the Fe-14Cr ODS 14YWT alloy that account for the alloy yield strength at temperatures 
ranging from 25 to 1000ºC [34]. Their results showed that Hall-Petch strengthening for 
these very fine grain alloys was greater than the direct strengthening due to the fine

the Hall-Petch effect and direct strengthening by nano-precipitates, forest dislocation 
hardening was also playing a significant role in strengthening at temperatures ranging 
from 25 to ~600ºC [35]. To assess the effect of grain size on the 125Y and PM2K alloys, 
the yield strength versus d-1/2 (d being the alloy grain size in the transverse direction) was 
plotted in Figure 13. Literature data for PM2000 and 14YWT were also added [28,34,35]. 
The PM2K, 125Y and literature PM2000 data followed similar trends that confirmed the 
significant effect of grain size on the low temperature tensile properties of the FeCrAl 
ODS alloys. The 14YWT data followed a similar progression but with a higher yield 
strength for a given grain size, perhaps due to solid solution strengthening of the 3%W in 
this alloy. A broad range of yield strength values were obtained from the same PM2K 
powder, which indicated that the alloy properties can be controlled by changing the 
consolidation parameters, which in turn control the microstructure, in particular the grain 
size. Similarly, Dou et al. have shown that increasing the extrusion temperature for an 
ODS Fe–15.5Cr–2W–0.1Ti–4Al–0.35Y 2O3 alloy led to an increase of the grain and 
precipitate size [22]. Changing the extrusion temperature is expected to have a similar 
effect on the microstructure and strength of the 125Y alloys. These results suggest that if 
the grain size remains less than ~0.5um, the yield strength of the alloy should be much 
higher than the yield strength of wrought FeCrAl alloys.

significantly affect fabricability. Takaki et al. used mechanical alloying and hot rolling to 
produce ultra-fine grain Fe sheets, and their tensile results showed that refining the grain 
structure below ~0.3 µm greatly reduced the Fe sheet uniform elongation [36]. Therefore, 
increasing the 125Y alloy grain size to ~0.5 µm should improve the alloy ductility. This 
might be the reason why the PM2K_SG alloy exhibited better ductility than the PM2KP 
alloy, Figure 5. Another reason for the greater ductility of the PM2K alloys in

these alloys, particularly the C and N, Table 1. Atom probe characterization of ODS FeCr 
revealed that C and N often segregated at grain boundaries [37,38], with or without the 
formation of carbonitrides, and recent work on ODS FeCr alloys suggested that lowering 
the level of C, N, and O could improve alloy ductility [39]. Much earlier work by 
Quadakkers et al. concluded that the high temperature oxidation behavior of ODS FeCrAl 
alloys could be improved by decreasing the C and N in the alloy [40]. The low C and N 
content in the PM2K alloys used in this study appears to be the result of a long-term 
effort by Plansee to improve the control of alloy chemistry in PM2000, particularly 
compared to competitor alloys MA956 and MA956HT. As a result, PM2000 has a 
substantially higher spallation resistance and time to breakaway in long-term oxidation 
testing at 1100°-1200°C [11,13,41,42] The substantial effort required to reduce the C and 
N levels in a new composition is evident in Table 1. The precursor 125Y gas atomized 
powder contained 31 wppm C, and ball milling with carbon steel media will inevitably 
increase the C content. The chemical analysis of an 14Cr-2W-0.3Ti-0.3Y 2O3 powder 
before and after ball milling by Plansee for 48h did not reveal any increase in C 
concentration, which suggests the use of a different milling media by the company [21]. 
 
The addition of Zr and Ti resulted in finer grains, which led to higher strength but lower 
ductility for 125YT. Unocic et al. clearly demonstrated that Zr was mostly associated 
with C and N in 125YZ, [19] and Ti-rich carbides are known to be present in alloy 
PM2000 [25,40]. In addition, co-precipitation of (Y,Al)-rich precipitates with Zr(C,N) or 
Ti(C,N) has been reported [19,26]. Other studies have observed the formation of fine 
Y
4Zr 3O12 precipitates in a FeCrAlZr alloy [27], but those were not observed in this study. 
Further work is needed to clearly understand how Zr and Ti affect the ODS FeCrAl grain 
size and nano precipitate density, and the effect of adding oxide additions versus 
alloying.

alloy grain size by pinning grain boundaries during the extrusion process [19].

4.2. High Temperature Steam Oxidation 
 
The first performance metric for ATF cladding was established as a >100X reduction in 
the oxidation rate at 1200°C in steam compared to Zr-based cladding [2,4]. Once FeCrAl 
was selected as a primary ATF cladding candidate, a secondary goal was the maximum 
temperature where alumina formation could be maintained during the escalating

## Page 19

MANUSCRIPT 
ACCEPTED
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
 18 
temperature during most severe accident scenarios. These criteria with short-term 
performance objectives are very different than the previous long-term studies of the 
oxidation behavior of ODS FeCrAl alloys at 1000-1300°C [10-13, 53-55]. For long-term 
lifetime modeling of heat exchanger or heat shield applications, a “reservoir” model [53] 
was proposed and several extensive subsequent studies confirmed that alloy lifetime 
depends mainly on the consumption of Al due to oxidation leading to a decreasing Al 
concentration in the alloy to a critical Al value (C Alb for breakaway) at the end of service 
where alumina cannot form anymore and the metal is rapidly consumed by fast growing 
Fe-oxides. The main lifetime parameters were therefore the oxide scale growth and 
spallation rates as well as the initial Al content C Al0 and C Alb . 
For the cladding application, prior work had confirmed that the 125Y ODS alloys, 
like other FeCrAl alloys, met the 1200°C rate constant metric [17,18] indicated by the 
thin oxides shown in Figure 6. Of more interest is defining the maximum use 
temperature for these various ODS alloys which quantifies their benefit in terms of 
increased coping time after a severe accident [56]. Above 1400°C in flowing steam, 
alloy coupons and thin walled 9.5mm diameter tube specimens of Kanthal alloys APM 
and APMT were able to form a protective alumina scale to 1500°C [57,58]. However, the 
lower Cr content FeCrAl alloys have consistently shown a decreased temperature 
capability compared to ~20%Cr alloys like APM, APMT and PM2000 [3,4,17,18,58]. 
The results for the 125Y ODS alloys also show a lower temperature capability (1400°C 
or less for 125YT in steam) compared to PM2K_LG, Table 4. For those alloys able to 
form a protective scale, the scale thickness measurements (Table 3) showed that the 
growth rate for the 125Y and PM2K alloys were quite similar to previous studies of 
Kanthal alloy APM and APMT [58,59], with a scale thickness of ~6-8 µm after 4h in air 
or steam at 1400ºC. These results are also consistent with the 3-5 µm thick oxide scale 
observed by Perez after oxidizing alloy PM2000 for 1h at 1400ºC in air. [60] Considering 
that 1 µm correspond to a mass gain of ~0.18mg.cm -2 for a dense alumina scale, then the 
parabolic rate constant, kp at 1400ºC is ~10 -10 g2.cm-4.s -1, consistent with the 4.5.10 -11 
g2.cm-4.s -1 value measured by Lours et al. for alloy PM2000 annealed in air at 1400ºC for 
up to 300h [61], 4-10x10 -11 g2.cm-4.s -1 measured for ODS MA956 (Fe-20Cr-4.5Al-0.4Ti) 
in air [62], or the 1.7 10 -10 g2.cm-4.s -1 value measured by Wang et al. after oxidation of a

~20wt% for the PM2000 alloys to 12wt% for the 125Y alloys, or the addition of Zr or Ti 
in the ODS 125YZ and 125YT alloys, respectively, did not significantly affect the scale 
growth rate of the ODS FeCrAl alloys at 1400ºC in air or steam. For FeCrAl alloys 
containing both Y and Zr, the Zr addition does significantly change the scale growth rate 
[64,65]. Numerous (Zr,Y),(Ti,Y) or Y-rich precipitates were observed after 4h at 1400ºC 
but mainly at the scale surface. As was reported previously for the oxidation at 1100-
1300ºC of alloy PM2000 or MA956, these precipitates likely nucleated during the 
transient oxidation stage and then grew due to the outward diffusion of Y and Ti or Zr 
along the alumina scale grain boundaries due to the oxygen potential gradient. [30-31,66] 
When a composition was not able to form a protective scale (Table 4), determining the 
failure mechanism was difficult because the specimen typically was fully consumed. For 
the alloys that formed a protective scale at 1400°C but not at 1450°C, the remaining 
1400°C scales typically showed significant degradation including spallation, deformation 
and cracking, Figures 11-12. Line profile measurements by EPMA conducted on the 
125YZ and PM2K_LG specimens after exposure for 4h at 1400ºC showed no Al gradient 
at the specimen surface. However, the very rapid initial rate of alumina formation could 
cause Al depletion at the specimen surface at 1450ºC. The only alloy that formed alumina 
at 1450ºC was the recrystallized PM2K_LG which suggests that microstructural stability 
may play a significant role in the high temperature performance. For the other alloys that 
could not form a protective alumina scale during a 5 minute exposure at 1450°C, there 
are two possibilities. The first is that an external protective alumina scale was never 
really formed and the metal was consumed. Recent work on wrought Fe-13Cr-6Al alloy 
has shown that increasing the heating rate decreased the alloy maximum allowable 
temperature in steam [67]. This might be attributed to the exothermic formation of 
alumina that can lead to a temperature increase at the interface and disturb the formation 
of a continuous protective alumina scale. Similarly, the very thin scale formed when 
1400ºC is reached could be disrupted by the additional heat coming from both the furnace 
and the alumina scale formation when 1450ºC is the set temperature. It is, however, 
worth mentioning that no liquid phases are expected to form for FeCrAl alloys in steam 
at temperature lower than ~1560ºC [68].

A second explanation is a disruption of the scale initially formed at 1450ºC, e.g. due to 
cracking or spalling leading to O ingress and rapid attack of the substrate. The unusual 
thicker oxide scale observed for the 125YF alloy (Figure 11g) indicates that these alloys 
might be at the limit between internal and external formation of alumina, or a location 
where a disruption occurred but the scale was able to reform. Presumably, the higher Cr 
alloys would be better able to reform the scale than the lower Cr alloys. The role of Cr in 
FeCrAl is referred to as the “third element” effect [69] but this effect has not been studied 
at such a high temperature. In addition, the small intergranular cracks observed in most of 
the scales may also provide a fast access of oxygen to the substrate. These intergranular 
cracks are not typically observed at lower temperature, Figure 6. The 125Y alloys clearly 
showed a higher propensity for spallation in comparison with the PM2K alloys, most 
likely due to the formation of large voids at the scale/alloy interface, Figure 8 and 11, and 
the higher C and N impurity levels in the 125Y alloys compared to the PM2K variants 
[70,71]. Similar interfacial voids were noted in TEM cross-sections of the scales formed 
at 1200°C in steam [18]. However, spallation after exposure at 1400ºC appeared to take 
place during cooling rather than providing a disruption at temperature, which is not a 
concern for this ATF cladding application. Besides void formation, deformation of the 
alloy/scale interface may affect the scale integrity due to a switch from a bi-axial 
compression stress in a flat interface to more complex stress state in convoluted scale 
[72]. In addition to the growth stress due to the molar volume change produced by metal 
oxidation [73,74], stresses in the scale and at the interface could also come from strain in 
the substrate induced by the alloy high temperature recrystallization. Increasing the 
temperature from 1400ºC to 1450ºC has an impact on several thermally-activated 
mechanisms that could impact the scale integrity, such as the alumina growth stress, void 
formation and growth at the interface, visco-plastic deformation of the ODS FeCrAl, 
extend and rate of grain recrystallization, etc., leading to oxidation failure of the 125Y 
alloys at 1450ºC. The recrystallized large grain PM2K_LG alloys exhibited the best high 
temperature oxidation behavior in steam but annealing the 125Y alloys to stabilize the 
microstructure does not look like a realistic strategy. Figure 5 shows that the mechanical 
strength of PM2K_LG was significantly lower than the strength of all the other ODS

5. Summary 
Three low Cr ODS FeCrAl alloys were fabricated by ball milling Fe-12Cr-5Al gas 
atomized powders with Y 
2O3+FeO, Y 2O3+ZrO 2 and Y 2O3+TiO 2. Extrusion at 950ºC led 
to the formation of a very fine grain microstructure, particularly for the Zr and Ti-
containing alloys which resulted in alloys with high strength but low ductility. 
Comparison with commercial Fe-20Cr-5Al-0.4Ti+0.5Y 2O3 PM2000 ODS alloys 
fabricated using different processing routes confirmed that the consolidation step has a 
drastic impact on the ODS FeCrAl alloy grain structure and mechanical properties below 
~500ºC. The PM2000 variants also suggest that controlling the alloy grain size and 
impurity content, particularly the C and N levels, may offer an effective way to improve 
the alloy ductility and fabricability. Both the ODS Fe-12Cr-Al and PM2000 alloys 
exhibited good oxidation resistance in air and steam up to 1400ºC, but most failed at 
1450ºC. The recrystallized PM2000 alloy was the only alloy showing excellent oxidation 
behavior at 1450ºC in steam, but the large grain structure of the alloy resulted in lower 
mechanical properties. Overall, due to their excellent oxidation resistance in steam up to 
1400ºC and high mechanical strength at low temperature, the Fe-12Cr-5Al ODS alloys 
are promising candidates for accident tolerant fuel cladding.
