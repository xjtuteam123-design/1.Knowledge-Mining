Alloys with high wear resistance at elevated temperatures are highly desired for modern application un- 
der extreme working conditions. Conventionally, nano-layered pearlitic structure could greatly improve 
the strength and wear resistance for steels, but they are not heat resistant. Therefore, it is promising to 
make a new pearlite structure that could withstand heat. In this study, we revitalize the pearlite structure 
by inheriting it from single principal element iron alloys into a newly developed multi-principal element 
alloy (MPEA) through a classic eutectoid reaction, producing a nanometre sized layered microstructure. 
Taking advantages of the thermal stability of MPEAs, formation of strong pearlite not only signiﬁcantly 
improves the room temperature wear resistance of the MPEA, more importantly, it endues the MPEA with 
excellent elevated temperature strength and wear resistance. Therefore, pearlitic structure is a direction 
worthy of pursuing in microstructural design of high performance MPEAs.

1. Introduction 
Pearlite structure in steels was discovered by Sorby over 100 
years ago [1] . Steel pearlite is featured as nanometre sized lamel- 
lar structure consisting of ferrite and hard cementite, which is able 
to plastically deform and realign the ferrite and cementite lamellae 
towards the strain direction during sliding wear [ 2 , 3 ]. Such reori- 
entation increases the area fraction of hard cementite in the con- 
tact, thus leading to high wear resistance of pearlitic microstruc- 
tures [2] , therefore pearlitic steels usually have the integration of 
high strength and good wear resistance, having a broad applica- 
tion in rails. However, in steels, since C atoms play a signiﬁcant 
role in forming pearlite but they diffuse extremely fast at elevated 
temperatures, the cementite phase will coarsen and spheroidize or 
even become unstable leading to decomposition when temperature 
is increased. Therefore, pearlitic steels cannot ﬁll the requirement 
for wear resistant components working at elevated temperatures. 
To keep such a strong layered structure still stable at elevated tem- 
peratures is worthy of exploring.

Multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) which were proposed in 
1995 [4] have a unique property of sluggish diffusion [5] owing to 
the large lattice potential energy ﬂuctuation leading to strong trap- 
ping effect thus the low diffusion rate. Sluggish diffusion effect can 
endue MPEAs with impressive high-temperature structural stabil- 
ity [6] . In addition to the thermal stability, MPEAs share another 
common feature of severe lattice distortion, which enhances the 
strength. Therefore, MPEAs can be quite strong and softening re- 
sistant at elevated temperatures. If layered structures could be de- 
veloped in MPEAs, they are supposed to be much more thermally 
stable than steel pearlite due to sluggish diffusion effect. Several 
groups have been looking at layered structures in MPEAs formed 
by phase transformations, and have successfully made eutectic lay- 
ered structures through eutectic transformations which show very 
interesting mechanical properties [ 7 , 8 ]. However, the eutectic lay- 
ered structures are formed from the liquid at high temperatures, so 
the layers are quite coarse (normally several hundred nanometre 
thick).Therefore, it would be nice instead to make layered struc- 
tures at lower temperatures to get a much ﬁner scale spacing, and 
pearlite and discontinuous precipitates are two examples. Finer 
layered structures have been seen in the work by Banerjee’s group 
[ 9 , 10 ]. But it is not really clear if it is formed by discontinuous pre- 
cipitation and then decomposition of the face centred cubic (FCC) 
and body centred cubic (BCC) lamellae behind the interface (i.e.

In this study, we demonstrate a pearlite structure that has

perature applications requiring softening and wear resistance, such

ture of pearlite and its evolution during transformation process, ef- 
fect of pearlite on hardness of MPEAs and its thermal stability, the 
wear property of pearlitic MPEAs at both room temperature and 
elevated temperatures up to 600 °C and the corresponding wear 
mechanisms. This study will open up a new structural option in 
designing elevated temperature wear resistant MPEAs. 
2. Experimental methods 
2.1. Material preparation 
The multicomponent alloy Fe 44.5 Co 25.4 Ni 24.4 Ti 5.7 ingot was cast 
by vacuum levitation melting. Homogenization treatment was per- 
formed at 1150 °C for 24 h in an argon atmosphere controlled hor- 
izontal tube furnace followed by water quenching. Chemical analy- 
sis was conducted using ICP-AES. The homogenized ingot was then 
subjected to cold rolling with a cold reduction of 85%. The cold 
rolled specimens were then held at 500, 550, 600 and 650 °C for 
various time for pearlite transformation followed by water quench- 
ing. 
2.2. Mechanical tests 
Vickers hardness was measured by FM-700 microhardness 
tester, 5 kg load was employed, and hardness value was averaged 
from 10 indents for each sample. Specimens for hot tensile tests 
have a gauge length of 50 mm, width of 10 mm and thickness of 
1.5 mm, which were cut parallel to rolling direction using electric 
discharge machining (EDM). Hot tensile tests were performed in 
Instron 5982 with 250 kN load cell, operating with a crosshead 
speed of 0.02 mm/s, stain was measured by extensometer clipped 
onto the gauge section. The dry sliding wear test was carried out 
in a high temperature ball-on-plate wear testing machine (MPX- 
3 G, Hengxu, Jinan) at room temperature, 550 and 600 °C. The 
tested samples were rubbed against a Al2O3 ball with a diame- 
ter of 6.25 mm at a sliding velocity of 0.1 m/s for 1 h at room 
temperature test and 20 min at elevated temperature test. The nor- 
mal load was 10 N. The samples for wear tests were mechanically 
ground and polished ﬁnishing with 1 μm diamond paste. The vol- 
ume loss after wearing was measured using ContourGT-X Bruker 
optical proﬁlometer. 
2.3. Microstructural characterizations 
Phase identities were determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) 
and the data were collected with Cu K α radiation on a Bruker 
D8-discover diffractometer, samples for XRD were ﬁnished with 
colloidal silica suspension polishing. The microstructure was char- 
acterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Sirion FEG) 
equipped with equipped with energy-dispersive spectroscopy 
(EDS), transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI T20) and 
scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM, Talos F200X) 
equipped with EDS. Samples for SEM were ﬁnished with 1 μm 
diamond suspension polishing, and etched by 50% HNO3 + 25% 
HF + 25% distilled water. The TEM specimens were ﬁrst ground to 
about 60 μm in thickness and then twin-jet electropolished using 
5% HClO4 + 95% ethanol electrolyte at −40 ∼50 °C. 
3. Results 
3.1. Microstructure of pearlite and its evolution 
The alloy studied here is Fe 44.5 Co 25.4 Ni 24.4 Ti 5.7 . According to the 
calculated phase diagrams by Thermo-Calc [11] ( Fig. 1 , where red 
dotted line indicates the Ti content in the current alloy), when 
the temperature is above 880 °C, it falls in FCC single phase ﬁeld, 
and as the temperature goes down to below 770 °C, the alloy is 
in FCC + BCC + Ni 3 Ti three-phase ﬁeld, as temperature further de- 
creases to below 630 °C, the alloy goes into the BCC + Ni 3 Ti two- 
phase region, where the phase fraction of each phase is constant 
until 400 °C as shown in Fig. 1 b). In this case, if the alloy with 
FCC structure is held in the two-phase or three-phase ﬁeld, there 
might be an opportunity to form a pearlite structure through the 
eutectoid reaction. 
In order to realize the FCC → BCC + Ni 3 Ti eutectoid reaction, 
the initial microstructure prior to the reaction should be FCC. Us- 
ing X-ray diffraction (XRD), it is found that the homogenized and 
cold rolled sheet shows mostly FCC structure ( Fig. 1 c) due to the 
thermal stability of FCC phase at room temperature. Hence, the as- 
cold rolled sample is directly subjected to isothermal holding at 
temperatures between 500 and 650 °C for a series of time followed 
by water quench, during which BCC and Ni 3 Ti phases are expected 
to nucleate on prior FCC grain boundaries and then cooperatively 
grow towards the inner FCC grains progressively. Fig. 2 presents 
the microstructure evolution of the as-cold rolled sample during 
500 °C isothermal holding from as-cold rolled state ( Fig. 2 a) until 
14 h ( Fig. 2 d), in which the prior FCC phase is ﬁnally almost swal- 
lowed by the bright pearlite regions. Magniﬁed version of Fig. 2 d 
is shown in Fig. 3 a, it is indeed an extremely ﬁne pearlite lamel- 
lar structure, which conﬁrms our expectation from the phase dia- 
grams. 
In order to clearly view the nano-layered structure, transmis- 
sion electron microscopy (TEM) is employed. TEM bright ﬁeld (BF) 
image of the pearlite formed at 500 °C is present in Fig. 3 e, and 
the inserted electron diffraction pattern ( Fig. 3 f) tells the exis- 
tence of Ni 3 Ti and BCC two phases. Further magniﬁed high reso- 
lution (HRTEM) image in Fig. 3 b along with the corresponding fast 
Fourier transformation patterns acquired from Fig. 3 b double con- 
ﬁrms the formation of BCC and Ni 3 Ti lamellae. The EDS mappings 
in Fig. 3 g and h show the enrichment of Ni and Ti in the Ni 3 Ti 
lamellae. 
Apart from 500 °C, pearlite evolves in the same manner dur- 
ing transformation at 550, 600 and 650 °C, but it takes shorter 
time to reach the nearly full occupation of prior FCC grains due 
to the faster diffusion at higher temperatures, these pearlite struc- 
tures are characterized in Fig. 4 . 
3.2. Interlamellar spacing of pearlite 
It is known that transformation temperature controls the in- 
terlamellar spacing of pearlite [12] , and this also works in MPEA 
pearlite. The TEM BF images are used for measuring the interlamel- 
lar spacing of each pearlite formed at 500, 550, 600 and 650 °C, 
and ten TEM images like Figs. 3 e, 4 b, f and j are taken for each 
pearlite to get the average spacing. Variation of interlamellar spac- 
ing with pearlite forming temperature is summarized in Fig. 5 b 
(red), and it increases from 26 ± 4 nm to 59.5 ± 13.3 nm when 
temperature is increased from 500 to 650 °C. Such interlamellar 
spacings of the MPEA pearlite are smaller than those in steels 
transformed within the same temperature range [ 13 , 14 ], for the 
reason of different pearlite reaction rate controlling process be- 
tween MPEAs and steels. Since there is no interstitial element in 
the current MPEA, it can be proposed that the pearlite growth 
must be accompanied by the redistribution of substitutional ele-

3.4. Thermal stability of MPEA pearlite 
Thermal stability of MPEA pearlite is evaluated at 500 and 
550 °C by means of hardness measurement, as shown in Fig. 6 a. 
At 500 °C, it reaches the peak hardness of ∼860HV at 14 h, stay- 
ing at the plateau until 60 h followed by only slight decrease dur- 
ing longer treatment, and the hardness is still as high as ∼820HV 
at 300 h and ∼760HV at 600 h. Fig. 6 b and c compare the mi- 
crostructures after 14 and 300 h holding at 500 °C, revealing that 
the pearlite does not tend to spheroidization which is normally the 
main reason for the thermal softening of steel pearlite. In addi- 
tion, the minor increase of the average interlamellar spacing from 
25.9 ± 3.7 nm at 14 h (peak hardness) to 28.1 ± 4.8 nm at 300 h 
also rationalizes the hardness stability at 500 °C. Therefore, good 
thermal stability could be ascribed to the sustained lamellar struc- 
ture and stable interlamellar spacing. At 550 °C, it takes less time 
(2 h) to achieve the peak hardness ( ∼810HV) owing to the faster 
diffusion rate of pearlite forming elements, after staying at the 
plateau for a few hours, the hardness starts to decrease gradually 
at ∼7 h holding to 780HV, the decreasing rate becomes lower af- 
ter 80 h holding, and the alloy still possesses ∼635HV at 600 h.

3.5.1. At room temperature 
We obtain a typical inverse correlation between the wear rate 
of pearlite and its hardness, consistent with Archard’s law [15] in 
which the wear volume loss is inversely proportional to the hard- 
ness of the material. The wear rate of the as-cold-rolled sample 
with mainly FCC phase is ∼ 1 . 1 ×10 −4 m m −3 / Nm , and the value 
is gradually suppressed down to the order of 10 −5 m m −3 / Nm once 
hard pearlite forms, as the pearlite fraction increases at 500 °C, the 
wear rate continuously decreases to ∼ 2 ×10 −5 m m −3 / Nm when 
the microstructure is almost fully occupied by pearlite ( Fig. 5 a), 
which is more than 80% smaller than that without pearlite. There- 
fore, formation of pearlite dramatically enhances the wear resis- 
tance of MPEA at room temperature. Since the hardness of pearlite 
is directly dependent on the interlamellar spacing, smaller inter- 
lamellar spacing with higher hardness could lead to lower wear 
rate thus better wear resistance of pearlite, which is conﬁrmed by 
the blue curve in Fig. 5 a. 
3.5.2. At elevated temperatures 
The pearlites formed at 500 and 650 °C with distinct inter- 
lamellar spacing are chosen for elevated temperature wear tests, 
and testing temperatures are 500, 550 and 600 °C. Fig. 5 c com- 
pares the wear rates at different sliding temperatures, and Fig. 5 d 
records the friction coeﬃcients. Wear rates of the pearlite trans- 
formed at 500 °C range from ∼ 3 . 5 ×10 −5 m m −3 / Nm to ∼ 4 . 7 ×
10 −5 m m −3 / Nm , which are always larger than those transformed 
at 650 °C with wear rates between ∼ 2 . 5 ×10 −5 m m −3 / Nm and 
∼ 2 . 9 ×10 −5 m m −3 / Nm . A comparison with the elevated temper- 
ature wear rates of other alloys, including high speed steels and 
recently developed wear resistant MPEAs, is shown in Fig. 7 . Al- 
though a few high speed steels possess equal or slightly lower 
wear rates than the current MPEA pearlite at 500 °C, MPEA pearlite 
shows superiority as temperature exceeds 550 °C. Therefore, in- 
troducing pearlite into MPEAs effectively produces a material with 
superior wear resistance than existing alloys, proving that inherit- 
ing pearlite structure from steel into MPEAs is a right direction to 
make full use of this structure and realize the potential of MPEAs 
at the same time.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Wear mechanism at room temperature 
Wear mechanism of MPEA pearlite at room temperature is an- 
alyzed in the way of wear track characterization. Fig. 8 a–d show 
the morphology of worn surfaces from four pearlite formed at 
different tem peratures characterized by SEM. All the wear tracks 
display abundant ploughing grooves along the sliding direction, 
and such grooves are indications of abrasive wear mechanism. Al- 
though these pearlite with different interlamellar spacings hold 
different wear rates, they share the same wear mechanism in room 
temperature sliding tests. 
4.2. Wear mechanism at elevated temperature 
As shown in Fig. 5 c, there seems no regular trend of wear 
rate as a function of wear temperature. For the pearlite formed 
at 650 °C, the wear rates at 500, 550 and 600 °C are even quite 
close within the experimental errors. Hence, it could be speculated 
that the interlamellar spacing and hardness of the pearlite does 
not directly impact the elevated temperature wear rate, instead 
some other mechanisms must be dominant. The pearlite with the 
lowest wear rate at elevated temperature is selected to study the 
wear mechanism, which is the one formed at 650 °C and worn at 
550 °C sliding test. The morphology of the wear track is shown 
in Fig. 8 e and f, revealing the presence of craters and patches and 
fewer grooves compared to those at room temperature, and this 
supports the occurrence of delamination wear. The chemical com- 
position of the worn surface is also analyzed by EDS point scan- 
ning in four different regions including crater and patch ( Table 1 ). 
It is found that the worn surface contains oxygen in high concen- 
trations along with other elements in all four regions, the oxygen 
content is even higher in the patch regions (regions 1, 2 and 4) 
than in the crate (region 3) as the exposure time to the high tem- 
perature air of the patch regions is longer than crate regions, hence 
apart from delamination wear, another key wear mechanism at el- 
evated temperature is oxidation wear. Therefore, the wear mecha-

5. Conclusions 
An eutectoid reaction has been designed in an MPEA with 
the composition of Fe 44.5 Co 25.4 Ni 24.4 Ti 5.7 , transformed from FCC to 
BCC and Ni 3 Ti during isothermal heat treatment between 500 and 
650 °C. Lamellar pearlite structure is produced, which ﬁrstly nu- 
cleates on the prior FCC grain boundaries and then grows towards 
the inner grains until full occupation, compared to the conven- 
tional steel pearlite, the lamellar MPEA pearlite is ﬁner due to 
the slower diffusion of substitutional elements. Similarly to steel 
pearlite, the interlamellar spacing of MPEA pearlite could also be 
tuned by transformation temperature, and higher temperature re- 
sults in larger spacing, it ranges from 26 ± 4 to 59.5 ± 13.3 nm 
during transformation between 500 and 650 °C. Consequently, both 
the fraction of pearlite and interlamellar spacing could affect the 
hardness of the alloy. Because of the sluggish diffusion effect in 
MPEAs, the pearlite exhibits good thermal stability at 500 °C and 
550 °C, although the hardness of the pearlite keeps decreasing in 
long time thermal treatment until 600 h, the decrease rate is not 
signiﬁcant, therefore it still possesses ∼760HV and ∼635HV after 
ageing at 500 °C and 550 °C for 600 °C, respectively. 
High hardness of MPEA pearlite also results in excellent wear 
resistance at both room temperature and elevated temperatures. 
The wear rate is as low as ∼2 ×10 −5 m m −3 / Nm at room tem- 
perature, and below 3 ×10 −5 m m −3 / Nm at elevated temperatures 
(550 and 600 °C). Abrasive wear is the dominant wear mecha- 
nism at room temperature, and the MPEA pearlite shows a typi- 
cal inverse correlation between the wear rate and hardness which 
is consistent with Archard’s law. Oxidative and delamination wear 
are the main mechanisms at elevated temperatures, thanks to the 
protective dense oxide layer formed over the wear track along with 
the strong subsurface including highly strain hardened nano-grains 
with suﬃcient hot strength and thermal stability. Therefore, MPEA 
pearlite is a promising structure that is worth further investigation 
and optimizing to be applied for future high performance alloys.
