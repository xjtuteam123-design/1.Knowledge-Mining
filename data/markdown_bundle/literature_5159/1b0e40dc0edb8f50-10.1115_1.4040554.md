A Microstructure-Based Model
for Creep of Gamma Prime
Strengthened Nickel-Based
Superalloys
This paper outlines a microstructure-based model relating gamma prime microstructure
and grain size of Ni-base alloys to their creep behavior. The ability of the model to
explain creep of multiple superalloys with a single equation and parameter set is demon-
strated. The only parameters that are changed from alloy to alloy are related to the
gamma prime characteristics and grain size. This model also allows prediction of creep
performance as a function of heat treatment and explains some apparently contradictory
data from the literature. [DOI: 10.1115/1.4040554]
Keywords: superalloy, creep, model, gamma prime, particle spacing, continuum damage
mechanics

Introduction
Availability of a physics-based predictive model for creep can
enable the following: (1) rapid alloy design for better creep resist-
ance, (2) design of heat treatments for optimum creep perform-
ance, (3) reliable creep liﬁng of components at the design stage,
and (4) remaining life assessment of creep limited turbine compo-
nents. The model that came closest to a realistic assessment of
creep in superalloys is that of Dyson [ 1]. This model with no ﬁt-
ting parameters predicted the creep behavior of Nimonic 90 at
higher stresses, but underestimated life at low stresses. The applic-
ability of the model to other superalloys was not discussed in the
paper though the general “Dyson approach” had been tried with
limited success on other superalloys [ 2,3]. Subsequently, other
models from Ma et al. for CMSX-4 [ 4], Zhu et al. for single crys-
tal Ni-base superalloys [5], Karthikeyan et al. for disk superalloys
[6], and others were also published though none of them explained
the creep behavior over a wide range of stresses and temperatures
even for a single alloy.
This paper outlines a model that works for a wide range of super-
alloys and is based on some modiﬁcations to the Dyson approach. It
has been used to predict the creep behavior of 14 superalloys.
Model
The model of Dyson has been the basis of most of the models
published in the literature and the present case is no different. The
three basic tenets that underlie the microstructure-based creep
model of Dyson are the following:
(1) c0 particles have two roles to play
(a) Holding dislocations between neighboring c0 particles
and releasing them through thermally activated climb :
Since the spacing of the particles controls the release
rate, creep strength decreases continuously with
increasing c0 spacing with no obvious lower limit.
(b) Composite strengthening due to plastic inhomogeneity
between the creeping matrix and the noncreeping c0
particles [7]: Consequently, the strengthening effect of
c0 particles, quantiﬁed by the back-stress generated in
the matrix, is determined by their volume fraction [8].
(2) The process of load transfer from the creeping matrix to the
hard particles (in situations much below the yield strength)
causes primary creep.
(3) Tertiary creep is primarily due to increase in mobile dislo-
cation density.
(4) Secondary creep is only an inﬂection point between pri-
mary and tertiary creep and typically superalloys do not
show any extended period of steady-state creep.
These tenets form a good theoretical framework but do not lead
to a model that is able to represent the creep behavior of superal-
loys over all stress and temperature ranges. The common approach
of researchers to arrive at better concordance between model pre-
dictions and experimental data has been to add more mechanisms
and equations to the basic approach outlined above [ 3–6]. Our
proposal is that a few small but important changes to the princi-
ples outlined above lead to a much better creep model with very
few additional assumptions. These changes are as follows:
(1) On the role of c
0 particles:
(a) c0 particles have only one role to play—composite
strengthening due to plastic inhomogeneity as men-
tioned earlier.
(b) When c0 particles are spaced close together, the con-
straints placed on matrix deformation by such closely
spaced particles [1,9,10] cause an increase in the appa-
rent volume fraction of c0 and consequently an increase
in back stress.
While Dyson did propose that closely spaced particles
in high c
0 volume fraction alloys would lead to higher
values of back stress, he left open the question of how
to account for it or whether it has a deeper signiﬁcance.
We are now taking this a step further and proposing
that this constraint is the sole and fundamental reason
for the spacing effect and this leads to an apparent
increase in the c
0 volume fraction.
(2) The creep mechanism is thermally activated release of dis-
locations from nodes rather than from neighboring c0 par-
ticles [ 11,12]. This dislocation network is formed in the
attempt to accommodate the plastic inhomogeneity men-
tioned previously.
(3) The apparent c0 volume fraction will decrease as particles
coarsen and reach a lower limit deﬁned by the thermody-
namic volume fraction.
(4) In addition, grain boundary sliding needs to be incorporated
for ﬁne grained superalloys (parts manufactured through the
cast and wrought, powder metallurgy, or additive routes).

Parameters and Calibration
Two microstructural parameters of the model change from alloy
to alloy and these are grain size d and the apparent c0 volume frac-
tion f, both of which can be determined from micrographs. The
parameters for Eq. (7) (apparent c0 volume fraction evolution) can
be determined separately for each alloy of interest depending on
the level of predictive precision required.
The parameters A, Qc, Ks, and Qs in Eq. (3) are calibrated only
once using short-term creep data ( <1000 h) of one alloy (DS-
GTD111). Thereafter, these creep mechanism-related parameters
are maintained constant for all other alloys. That this works rea-
sonably well points to the strong possibility that the fundamental
creep mechanism has remained the same over multiple genera-
tions of superalloys and over a wide range of stresses and temper-
atures (below yield).

broadening measurements on interrupted creep samples of DS-
GTD111. These parameters were found to work very well for all
the alloys and hence were maintained unchanged.
Thus, it can be seen that the parameters that need ﬁtting to
creep data are very few and remain unchanged for different alloys.
The only parameters that change from alloy to alloy are micro-
structure related and measurable. Also, this approach does not
need complex assumptions about bimodal and trimodal c

tions as in the case of Ref. [3].
Predictions
Equations (3) through Eq. (7) can be integrated to obtain creep
strain as a function of time. An example of such a plot is shown in
Fig. 1(a) for DS-GTD111. It is seen that the model is able to
match the experimental data reasonably well within experimental
error. Isostrain plots for different stress–temperature combinations
can be derived from such creep strain versus time plots and one
such plot for DS-GTD111 is shown in Fig. 1(b). Figure 1(b)
includes data, predictions from the current model, and predictions
from Dyson’s modeling approach. It is clear from this ﬁgure that
the problem of underestimation at lower stresses that is character-
istic of the Dyson model is avoided by the current model. As
pointed out earlier, this is primarily enabled by the new constitu-
tive approach embodied in Eq. (3)—that the fundamental creep
mechanism is hold–release of dislocations from networks and not
neighboring c
0 particles.

In the long term, on the other hand, the apparent volume frac-

ther change occurs. Thus, the long term predictions are not
sensitive to initial variations in the apparent volume fraction lead-

no more weakening. The point of convergence of the peak-aged
and overaged material shifts to lower times as the temperature
increases. The overaged material deﬁnes the lower limit of creep
capability of this material. All these aspects are captured accu-
rately by the current model (lines in the plot).
Explanation of Literature Data. The model explains some
apparent contradictions seen in superalloy creep literature. One
such instance is as follows: Stevens and Flewitt’s experiments on
IN738 [ 14] showed that the creep capability of this material
showed a strong dependence on the prior aging treatment.
1 On the
other hand, Tipler and Peck quoted by Dyson and McLean [ 15]
showed that standard aged samples and overaged samples had
almost identical lives (about 8000 h).2 This apparent contradiction
can be explained using the framework proposed in this paper: The
high stresses used in Stevens and Flewitt’s tests ensured that the
test durations were much shorter than the tests of Tipler and Peck.
Therefore, the differences in initial apparent volume fraction
between the standard aged and overaged samples sustained over
the duration of their tests leading to different lives. Tipler and
Peck’s tests, on the other hand, ran much longer and the initial dif-
ferences in apparent volume fraction disappeared early leading
eventually to similar lives.
This result shows that at typical operation temperatures of for-
ward stage turbine blades ( >850
/C14C), the creep resistance of
superalloys rapidly approaches their stable value. Beyond this sta-
bilization point, the creep resistance has no dependence on the c0
distribution.3
Summary
(1) The model outlined in this paper is able to predict the creep
behavior of multiple superalloys with two measurable
microstructural inputs, the grain size and the apparent c0
volume fraction (and its evolution with time). The apparent
c0 volume fraction is a combination of the thermodynamic
volume fraction and that due to constraint.
(2) An important corollary of this model is that a large part of
the improvements in creep capability of superalloys over
the past few decades can be attributed to increases in appa-
rent volume fraction of c
0. This proposition is worth investi-
gating further since it has implications for future alloy
design.
(3) The increase in creep capability arising from ﬁner c0 size
and spacing is explained by the increase in apparent c0 vol-
ume fraction. The approach also predicts that this increase
will completely disappear after a ﬁxed time and tempera-
ture exposure with the lower limit of creep resistance being
deﬁned by the thermodynamic volume fraction of c
0. For
turbine components operating at temperatures in the range
of >850 /C14C, this limit is reached in a few tens of hours to a
few hundred hours.
(4) An important conclusion from this work is that c0size/
spacing alone cannot be used to predict remaining life since
it ceases to have any role on creep resistance beyond a cer-
tain value. Having said this, remaining life assessment is
possible if other microstructural parameters, such as dislo-
cation density and its evolution, are also taken into account.
This will be the subject of a future publication.
