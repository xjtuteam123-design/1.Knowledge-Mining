homogeneity range of 4 at.% 2,12. The D8 8-type structure is
stabilized by small interstitial atoms like carbon, nitrogen,
oxygen, or boron, or other impurities on octahedral sites, as
discussed by Nowotny 13 and Sambasivan and Petuskey 14.
The brittleness is due to the complex crystal structure. Due
to the low symmetry, the size of the Burgers vector is large
in the Ti
5Si3, raising the self energy of the dislocation. The
low symmetry and highly covalent bonding, is responsible
for a large Peierls stress, required for dislocation movement.
As a result, even if dislocations are present inside the Ti
5Si3
grains 9, they are hardly mobile. The Young’s modulus of
Ti5Si3 is 156 GPa 9, which is almost one-third of that
demonstrated by MoSi 2
10. The reason for much lower yield
strength of Ti 5Si3 is its open or non-close packed structure
with low symmetry.
Another major problem affecting the strength and fracture
toughness of Ti 5Si3 is the coefficient of thermal expansion
(CTE) anisotropy between the c and a axes 9,10,11,15,16. The
CTEs along the c and a axes are reported as 22.2 × 10 -6 and
5.1 × 10 -6 K -1, respectively, which leads to an anisotropy of
4.115. Such a large difference in the CTEs along the c and
a axes, generates thermal residual micro-stress inside the
grains during cooling. The residual micro-stress is centered
at the grain boundaries below the brittle to ductile transition
temperature (BDTT), owing to differences in the CTE between
neighboring grains along the same direction. At room
temperature, this internal residual stress is a function of the
elastic modulus, anisotropy in CTE, grain size, and BDTT 9.

ABSTRACT
The demand for materials to be used in the components
operating above 1100 oC in advanced aero-engines drives the
development of the silicide-based intermetallic alloys and
composites, including the titanium silicides. The mechanical
behaviour of Ti
5Si3 and its composites has been reviewed with
emphasis on the microstructure-property relationships. It is
found that the grain size is a critical parameter, and smaller
grain sizes are desirable for reducing the magnitude of internal
residual stress caused by the crystallographic anisotropy in
coefficients of thermal expansion. The reduction in grain size
leads to significant improvement in hardness, room temperature
flexural strength and fracture toughness. On the other hand, the
high temperature strength observed at slow strain rates and
creep resistance are higher in the samples with the coarser
grain sizes. Further improvements in the strength, fracture
toughness and high temperature creep resistance are possible,
either through the development of multiphase alloys, or by the
use of ceramic reinforcements in composites.
1. INTRODUCTION
Intermetallic alloys and composites based on the silicides of
molybdenum, niobium and titanium have gained significant
attention in recent years for applications in the automotive
and aero-engine components operating at elevated
temperatures above 1000 oC, particularly when the
conventional Ni-base superalloys are not usable. The interest
in the refractory metal silicides is due to their high melting
points, moderate density, as well as oxidation resistance and
the ability to undergo plastic deformation at elevated
temperatures
1,2. However, the poor room-temperature ductility
and fracture toughness of the silicides are the major
drawbacks. Furthermore, the yield strength and creep
resistance also decrease sharply at elevated temperatures.
Thus room temperature fracture toughness, as well as the
high temperature strength retention and oxidation resistance
are among the most critical issues needing to be resolved for
wide-spread application of these materials, and hence, these
topics are the major themes of research.
A great deal of research has been devoted to molybdenum
and niobium silicides over last couple of decades
1-4. In
comparison, the knowledge and understanding on titanium
silicides is limited. Ti
5Si3 has received the maximum attention
among the binary Ti-silicides. Meschter and Schwartz 5
identified the MoSi 2 and Ti 5Si3 as the high temperature
silicides with significant oxidation resistance and proposed

2. PROCESSING AND MICROSTRUCTURE
The samples of Ti 5Si3 and its alloys for experimental study
have been processed by both ingot and powder metallurgy
routes. The ingot metallurgy technique, as reported in the
literature
15, involves arc-melting. On the other hand, the
powder metallurgy processing has been carried out either by
hot pressing of the Ti
5Si3 powder, or by reaction sintering
accompanied by hot pressing of the elemental powders in
their stoichiometric ratios. The Ti
5Si3 powder is obtained by
pulverizing and grinding the arc-melted buttons. In reaction
sintering of Ti 5Si3, either the mixtures of elemental Ti and
Si 9, or those of TiH 2 and Si powders 17 have been used. A
mixture of high purity Ti and Si powders in stoichiometric
proportion was first reaction sintered (without application of
pressure) in vacuum between 1400 o and 1500 oC, ground to
fine powder in argon and then hot iso-statically pressed at
1300
oC under 200 MPa for 5 h 9. In another study 17, the
mixture of TiH 2 and Si was first heated to 800 oC and held for
4 h to allow the decomposition of TiH 2, following which
reaction hot pressing was carried out under a pressure of 26
MPa for 1 h at 1500
oC. A temperature exceeding the melting
point of Si ( ≈1410 oC) has been found suitable for the
completion of chemical reaction as well as densification.
Enhanced diffusivity of atoms in the liquid Si increases the
kinetics of reaction at the Ti-Si interfaces. Again, the contact
between the Ti and Si atoms is improved by the increase in
fluidity of Si above its melting point.

microstructure of the Ti 5Si3, is shown in Fig. 1 17. A dispersion
of second phase particles, identified as the TiSi is identified.
Typical bright field TEM images of the Ti
5Si3-TiC composite
are shown in Fig. 2. On examination of the bright field TEM
image in Fig. 2(a), it is clear that the TiC particles contain
dislocations. Dislocations are expected to form inside the TiC

oC. Dislocations are expected as

BDTT (800

deformation during processing. In another study 18, where
the Ti 5Si3-TiC composite has been fabricated by the reaction

1380 oC, about 3-4 vol.% of the needle-shaped Ti 3SiC 2
particles with an high aspect ratio have been observed in the
microstructure. The formation of Ti
3SiC2 took place during
hot pressing, because of the incomplete chemical reaction.
The major disadvantage of using temperatures lower than
that of melting point of Si for the purpose of reaction hot
pressing is that the chemical reaction is incomplete.

3 MPa √m9,11,17-19,21. A few studies have also been carried

and its composites. The fracture toughness of Ti 5Si3 shows
a remarkable improvement with a decrease in grain size in the
microstructure, as shown by Ruess and Vehoff
22. The Ti 5Si3
prepared by hot pressing and with a grain size of 6 μm has
shown fracture toughness of as much as 6 MPa √m, in
contrast to a value of as low as 2 MPa √m, demonstrated by
the hot pressed samples with grain sizes in the range of
10-30 μm.

brittleness at 1100 oC. On the basis of the high temperature

attributed to the sharp reduction in the density of
microcracks, when compared with that in the samples with
relatively coarse grain size. The brittle to ductile transition
temperature is sharply reduced, largely because of strain
accommodation by short circuit diffusion through the grain
boundaries.

toughness. However, the BDTT of the Ti 5Si3 samples has
been found to be reduced on alloying with Al. The
substitution of Si sites by Al atoms in the Ti 5Si3 lattice, alters
the covalent character of bonding, which in turn affects the
brittle-to-ductile transition temperature. Presence of ductile
metallic phase alongside the brittle Ti
5Si3 is one of the ways,

Ti

25 have shown that the 0.2% offset yield stress varies

the 3

6. CREEP BEHA VIOUR
Creep studies have been carried out on the polycrystalline
Ti
5Si3 as well as on the Ti 5Si3-20 vol.% TiB 2 composite, and
the Ti 5Si3-8 wt.%Al alloy 28. Plots showing the steady state
creep rate at 1200 oC against the applied stress for the different
Ti5Si3 based materials, are presented in Fig. 5. On examination
of the creep data (as depicted in Fig. 5), it is clear that for
a specific stress, the Ti 5Si3 samples with the coarsest grain
size have the highest resistance to creep, and is followed by
the Ti
5Si3-20 vol.% TiB 2 composite, Ti 5Si3 samples with
average of 5-10 μm, and Ti5Si3-8 wt.% Al alloy. At 1200oC, the
creep stress exponents have been found to be ≈2.8 and 3.0
for the Ti 5Si3 samples with grain sizes of 6 μm and 25 μm,
respectively. On the other hand, the creep stress exponent of
both the Ti 5Si3-TiC composite and the Ti 5Si3-8wt.% Al alloy
has been found as ≈ 2. A stress exponent ≈ 3 implies that
creep progresses by the process of dislocation glide, while
the stress exponent ≈ 2 signifies the involvement of grain
boundary sliding. At high temperatures, thermally activated
motion of dislocations is possible. The presence of
dislocations in the samples deformed by creep has been

7. CONCLUSIONS AND DIRECTIONS FOR
FUTURE RESEARCH
The mechanical behaviour of Ti 5Si3 has been reviewed with
emphasis on its relationship with microstructure related
parameters, particularly the grain size and presence of second
phase as reinforcement. The mechanical properties examined
include the hardness, flexural strength, and fracture
toughness at room temperature, the brittle-to-ductile
transition temperature, as well as the high temperature
fracture toughness, compressive strength and creep
behaviour. Experimental studies have shown that the residual
stress present inside the grains due to the large anisotropy
in the coefficient of thermal expansion can be significantly
minimized by reduction in grain size or alloying with Nb. As
a result, the reduction in grain size has led to a significant
rise in the hardness, flexural strength and fracture toughness,
compared to those found in the relatively coarse-grained
samples. Reduction in the grain size also leads to a decrease
in the brittle to ductile transition temperatures. On the other
hand, the high temperature compressive strength at slower
strain rates and the creep resistance are higher, if the grain
size is coarser.
Addition of ceramic reinforcements has been found beneficial
for improvement in mechanical properties, both at the ambient
as well as at high temperatures. Addition of TiC as
reinforcement has shown significant grain refinement, as well
as improvements in the flexural strength and fracture
toughness. The Ti
5Si3-20 vol.% TiB 2 composite has shown
lower creep strain rates than the monolithic Ti 5Si3 with a
similar matrix grain size.
The directions for future research on Ti 5Si3 should be aimed
at further reduction in the brittle to ductile transition
temperature, and further improvements in strength and
toughness as well as creep strength, without compromising
the oxidation resistance. One of the approaches would be to
have a network of a relatively ductile, but high strength Ti
alloy phase around the fine Ti 5Si3 grains ( ≈5 μm). Although
fabrication of such a composite is feasible, and toughness is
expected to increase, the disadvantage is the lower melting
point and inferior creep strength of the ductile alloy, which
would bring down the temperature of operation.
Simultaneous improvement in strength and fracture
toughness is also possible by inclusion of the Ti
3SiC2 phase
in the Ti 5Si3-TiC composites. In recent years, research on
Ti3SiC2 has generated significant interest. The Ti 3SiC2 is a
soft and ductile phase, with impressive strength, toughness
and machinability, but it is not suitable for use at or above
1700
o. The other option could be the development of
laminated composites with ductile refractory alloys, such as
Nb. As the density of Ti
5Si3 is less than that of silicides of
Nb, an added benefit will be the reduction in weight of the
component. Again for high temperature applications, further
research is required to improve the oxidation resistance at
1300
oC and above.
