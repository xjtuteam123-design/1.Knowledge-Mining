Attractive Characteristics of High-Chromium
Iron-Based Alloys for Nuclear Reactor Application
A. Hishinuma (a), S. Isozaki (b), S. Takaki (b), and K. A biko (b)

High-chromium iron-based alloys have attractive characteristics compared to those of conventional
ferritic steels which have been considered to be promising candidate alloys for the next generation
machines in place of austenitic stainless steels. Their expected properties of good corrosion resis-
tance, low thermal stress factor, high strength at elevated temperatures, and in some cases low
ferromagnetism are very desirable for advanced nuclear reactor application.
1. Introduction
With increasing world energy consumption, about one third of which is obtained from
nuclear power generation, the role of nuclear energy becomes more and more important.
Therefore, promotion of development and utilization of nuclear energy, that is establish-
ment of technology and systems needed for using nuclear fuel efficiently, is essential for
securing energy resources. From this point of view, development of advanced nuclear
reactors is a necessity. Advanced light water reactors (ALWR) characterized by the
capability to utilize nuclear fuel efficiently, and fast breeder reactors (FBR) that can
produce more nuclear fuel than it consumes while at the same time generating electricity
for example, were strongly favored. Besides these fission-type reactors, fusion-type reac-
tors have excellent advantages such as abundance in fuel resources which are widely
distributed throughout the world.
Typical and common nuclear reactor materials currently used and/or being candidate
are Zircaloy alloys, austenitic stainless steels, and ferritic steels. The Zircaloy alloys such
as Zircaloy-2 and Zircaloy-4 are used as fuel cladding materials of the light water reac-
tors such as the boiling water reactor (BWR) and the pressurized water reactor (PWR),
respectively. Austenitic stainless steels are widely used in nuclear reactors as structural
materials for all reactor systems and fuel cladding materials for FBR.
In place of austenitic stainless steels, ferritic steels are candidate alloys for the ad-
vanced reactors of LWR, FBR, and also thermonuclear fusion reactors (TFR), which
have been developed vigorously during this decade under international cooperation. Fer-
ritic steels have some advantages in irradiation resistance, thermal properties, strength
at elevated temperature and so on, compared with austenitic steels. Among the ferritic
steels, Fe ±(7 to 12)Cr steels are now selected as candidate for reasons of phase stability
and irradiation resistance [1, 2]. However, such steels with relatively low chromium con-
tent have a disadvantage of corrosion resistance for general application. Other disadvan-
tages considered are creep strength for FBR high-temperature application, and shift of

3. Advanced Reactor Systems and Materials Required
3.1 Advanced light water reactors
In ALWR, i.e., high burn-up light water reactors, one of the key issues is the fuel clad-
ding material. Today Zircaloy-type alloys are used consisting of mainly zirconium with
some iron, vanadium and niobium, and others. However, corrosion and irradiation resis-
tances are not high enough for extending the expected lifetime up to 100 GWd/t burn-
up, about two times of the BWR currently coperated. Generally a linear dependence of
oxide layer thickness on fuel burn-up was observed in Zircaloy-4 up to about 40 GWd/t
and grew sharply thereafter as shown schematically in Fig. 5 [11 to 13]. In the figure,
the increase rate of the oxide layer is accelerated sharply by three to seven times after
passing the critical point. The corrosion behavior is one of key factors to give a limit to
the Zircaloy-type alloy for ALWR application.
Austenitic stainless steels are one of the candidates because of their better irradiation
resistance compared with Zircaloys, but corrosion resistance, especially stress corrosion
cracking (SCC) becomes a problem. The SCC is well known to be enhanced very dras-
tically under irradiation, which is called irradiation assisted SCC (IASCC). The IASCC
increases remarkably above the fluence of about 1  10
25 n/m2, roughly corresponding to
0.5 dpa in stainless steel [14], which is much less than the total fluence of 2  1026 n/m2
in ALWR. Usually there occurs no SCC in the conventional ferritic steels, for general

3.2 Fast breeder reactors
Ferritic steels are candidate materials for FBR core materials, fuel cladding and wrapper
tubes in place of austenitic stainless steels currently used, because of their superior swell-
ing resistance. Swelling determines sometimes the lifetime of materials and, therefore,
much efforts have been carried out to improve the swelling resistance for a long time,
but it has been concluded that there is a limitation for their improvement. Ferritic steels
are selected as the next candidate materials following austenitic stainless steels. How-
ever, their strength at elevated temperatures, especially the creep strength, is not suffi-
cient for the requirements of the advanced FBR. Therefore, advanced ferritic steels are
required to be developed to fulfil the criteria or target values, as for example, the creep
strength of the target of fuel cladding, which is more than 118 MPa for 30000 h and
147 MPa for 10000 h at 923 K as shown in Fig. 6 and 7 [1]. In the figures, the character-

target values. As shown in the figures, conventional ferritic steels cannot meet the target

3.3 Thermonuclear fusion reactors
In fusion reactors, materials used under most severe conditions are plasma facing
materials and the first wall materials of the blanket structure, where materials are
exposed both to high heat flux and additionally to high neutron flux as usual nucle-
ar materials. According to the design study of the International Thermonuclear Ex-
perimental Reactor (ITER), the blanket structure is cooled by water [15] and then,
the operation temperature of the structural material may be less than 573 K. It
should be mentioned that temperature will become higher in the next generation
machines like the demonstrated reactors (DEMO). Today the austenitic stainless steel
is a candidate for the blanket structural material, but cannot be applied to DEMO,
for example, a steady state Tokamak reactor (SSTR) [16], because of its low thermal
conductivity; the heat flux increases from 0.3 MW/m
2 in ITER to 0.5 MW/m 2 in
SSTR. The increase in heat flux causes a limit of the capable wall thickness due to
thermal stress. A calculation indicates that the heat flux of 0.5 MW/m
2 requires a
limit of 1 mm in wall thickness for austenitic stainless steel and 5 mm for ferritic
steels corresponding to thermal conductivity of 18.2 and 26.6 W/mK, respectively.
Therefore, ferritic stainless steels are selected as a primary candidate material for the
next generation machines like DEMO. Another important factor for the selection of
materials is a low radioactive activation. In fusion reactor development, structural
materials are strongly required to be made of low activation materials, meaning that
the materials consist of elements which generate no long-life radioactive isotopes by
nuclear reactions between energetic neutrons and the composing elements. Responding
to the request, the fusion community has now developed the low activation ferritic

4. Discussion
The high-purity and high-chromium alloys have very attractive characteristics concern-
ing nuclear reactor application as described above. However, on actual application, neu-
tron irradiation induced embrittlement is concerned as much as conventional ferritic
steels and even also austenitic stainless steels. In the case of structural application, the
DBTT shift due to irradiation is greatly concerned along with irradiation induced em-
brittlement, i.e., loss of tensile ductility. For example, the DBTT of ferritic steels with
(7 to 12)Cr is easily shifted to above ambient temperature after irradiation at around
500 K to a dose of less than 10 dpa, accompanied with significant loss of tensile ductility
[18]. The decrease in toughness is much worried about for nuclear application of these
ferritic steels.
In high-chromium Fe ±Cr alloys, the toughness is usually strongly influenced by s
phase formation and phase decomposition into a and a
0, in addition to effects of gaseous
impurities observed in low-chromium alloys. These phase changes become more serious
with increasing chromium content and then the problems are more serious, for example
for Fe ±50 mass% Cr alloys, although they have very attractive properties of corrosion
resistance, high strength at elevated temperature, and low ferromagnetism. These phase
changes are supposed to be accelerated by irradiation, because during irradiation, accel-
eration of diffusion of s formation elements and/or irradiation induced segregation,
which are caused by the different diffusion characteristics of alloy elements, could en-
hance the phase decomposition through either spinodal decomposition or nucleation and
growth kinetics. These factors should again enhance irradiation induced hardening
which is usually caused only by irradiation induced dislocations. As a result, the embrit-
tlement may further be accelerated. Recent neutron irradiation experiments on
Fe±(9 to 30)Cr alloys have shown that the larger increase in yield stress by irradiation
was observed in higher chromium alloys after irradiation at 673 K to a dose of 0.3 dpa
[19]. The rate of increase was more than twice in 18 and 30 Cr alloys without depending
on the impurity level. Together with these phase changes and hardening, the DBTT
could be shifted to high temperatures more rapidly than that of conventional ferritic
steels.
Anyway, the alloy development itself and irradiation studies for understanding the
damage mechanisms are necessary for nuclear application of these high-chromium alloys.
The fundamental studies using ultra-high-purity alloys, especially for understanding the
effects of impurities on toughness including aging and irradiation effects, might surely
give us very useful knowledge for the development of high-chromium iron-based alloys.
5. Summary
Austenitic stainless steels are now widely used for nuclear materials. However, advanced
materials are strongly required for the next generation or advanced reactors of types
such as advanced light water reactors, fast breeder reactors and thermonuclear fusion
reactors. Ferritic steels are one of the materials that might replace austenitic steels in
future. High-purity and high-chromium alloys have much better characteristics in terms
of corrosion resistance, high strength and creep strength at elevated temperature com-
pared with conventional ferritic steels. Moreover, in fusion applications, the low ferro-
magnetism is also a desirable feature for plasma-controlling aspects.
