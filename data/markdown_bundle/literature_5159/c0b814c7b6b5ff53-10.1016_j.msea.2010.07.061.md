abstract
The uniaxial tensile tests were conducted with the initial strain rates range of (0.0001–0.01) s −1 and
the temperature range of (1123–1373) K for typical high-strength alloy steel. Based on the experimental
results, the modiﬁed Johnson–Cook model, which considers the coupled effects of strain, strain rate
and deformation temperature, was proposed to describe the tensile behaviors of the studied alloy steel.
Results show that the stress–strain values predicted by the proposed model well agree with experimental
ones, which conﬁrmed that the modiﬁed Johnson–Cook model can give an accurate and precise estimate
of the ﬂow stress for the studied typical high-strength alloy steel.

1. Introduction
It is well known that the mechanical behavior of most alloys
or metals is dependent on strain rate and temperature. During
the hot forming process of metals and alloys, the materials are
subjected to complex strain, strain rate, and temperature histo-
ries in industrial forming processes. So, material ﬂow behaviors
during hot formation process are often complex. An understand-
ing of deformation behavior of alloys or metals over a wide range
of strain rates and temperatures is very important in the design
of structures or components. Some researches have been car-
ried out to study the ﬂow behaviors of alloy steels during hot
deformation and both experimental and theoretical approaches
have been employed to predict dynamic recrystallization, recovery
kinetics and their effect on ﬂow behaviors. Also, some constitutive
equations of materials were developed from the experimentally
measured data to describe the sensitivity of the ﬂow stress to the
strain, strain rate and deformation temperatures in commercial hot
working applications[1–10]. The high-temperature ﬂow stress in a
Ti-modiﬁed austenitic stainless steel and modiﬁed 9Cr–1Mo (P91)
ferritic steel were predicted by the constitutive equations, which
considers the effects of strain on material constants [1,2]. Khamei
and Dehghani [3,4] investigated the hot deformation behaviors of
60Nitinol (Ni60 wt%–Ti40 wt%) alloy and developed constitutive
equations to model the hot deformation behaviors of 60Nitinol.
Lin et al. [5] proposed revised models to describe the relation-

constitutive ﬂow behaviors of 42CrMo steel during hot deforma-
tion, and the excellent capability of the developed ANN model was
veriﬁed.

not advanced enough to account for the whole complexity of the
dynamic response of metals. In this study, the effects of strain rate

2. Experiments and results
A typical high-strength alloy steel with chemi-
cal composition (wt%) of: 0.450C–0.280Si–0.960Cr–
0.630Mn–0.190Mo–0.016P–0.012S–0.014Cu–(bal.)Fe, was used
for the experiments. Dog-bone-shaped bulk tensile specimens
were machined from the ingot bar, in accordance with ASTM
standards E21-92 (1998). The specimens had a gage length of
25 mm and a diameter of 10 mm. The uniaxial tensile tests were
conducted on an INSTRON machine (model 3369) with the initial
strain rates range of (0.0001–0.01) s
−1 and the temperature range
of (1123–1373) K. The testing system operates on the principle of
closed-loop servo control. Testing apparatus and fractured sample
are shown in Fig. 1.
Assuming the constant volume of the gage-length section of the
test specimen during the test, the true stress–true strain curves
can be easily obtained before necking. Fig. 2 shows the typi-
cal true stress–strain curves obtained from uniaxial tensile tests
of the studied alloy steel. It is noted that the ﬂow stresses are
strongly dependent on the deformation temperature and strain
rate under all the tested conditions. The deformation resistance
increases with the decrease of deformation temperatures for a
given strain rate and decreases with the decrease of strain rates
for a given deformation temperature. The material experiences
simultaneous work hardening and dynamic recovery when they
are deformed. The work hardening and dynamic recovery will
in turn affect the mechanical properties of material. The for-
mer hardens the material while the latter leads to recovery and
softening. Additionally, the temperature rise during the defor-
mation will result in the softening of the material, and then
reduce the ﬂow stress. So, the deformation mechanism asso-
ciated with the isothermal uniaxial tensile test is a thermally
activated process, and the measured stress–strain curves repre-
sented the combined effects of strain, strain rate and deformation
temperature.

5. Conclusions
The tensile characteristics of a typical high-strength alloy steel
have been investigated by means of the uniaxial tensile test over a
practical range of temperatures and strain rates. Based on experi-
mental stress–strain data, a modiﬁed Johnson–Cook model, which
considers not only the yield and strain hardening phenomenon,
but also the coupled effects of the temperature and strain rate,
is proposed to predict the ﬂow stress. Comparisons between the
experimental and predicted results were carried out and conﬁrmed
that the developed constitutive equations can presented an accu-
rate and precise estimate of the ﬂow stress for the studied typical
high-strength alloy steel.
