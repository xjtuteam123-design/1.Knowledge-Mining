abstract
A Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn (at.%) alloy was prepared by casting, heat treating at 525 ◦ C for 10 h, extruding at 360 ◦ C
and ageing at 180 ◦C for 90 h. Microstructure characterization revealed that this alloy consists of the ﬁne-
grained /H9251-Mg matrix composed nearly of the 14H long period stacking ordered (LPSO) phase and a small
amount of (Mg, Zn) xDy precipitations. Tensile tests showed that the yield and ultimate tensile strengths
of this alloy can reach 245 MPa and 260 MPa at 300 ◦ C, which are only slightly lower than those at room
temperature (287 MPa and 321 MPa), the elongation of this alloy is 36% at 300 ◦ C, which is far higher than
that at room temperature (11.6%). The high volume fraction of the 14H LPSO phase in the ﬁne-grained /H9251-
Mg grains and the high thermal stability of the microstructure during elevated temperature deformation
are responsible for the excellent elevated temperature properties observed for this alloy.

1. Introduction
Magnesium alloys, as high performance structural materials,
have been used in automotive, aerospace and electronic industries
due to their low density, high speciﬁc strength, good damping and
ease of recycling. However, poor strengths of Mg alloys at elevated
temperature (e.g. above 200
◦C) limit their application. Therefore,
many attempts have been made to develop new Mg alloy systems
for various elevated temperature application. Kawamura et al. [1]
developed a new nanocrystalline Mg
97Zn1Y2 alloy by powder met-
allurgy process. This alloy exhibits a yield strength of∼600 MPa and
an elongation of ∼5% at room temperature. Besides ultra-ﬁne grain
structure, the superior mechanical properties of this alloy originate
from the presence of long period stacking ordered (LPSO) phase.
The further investigations demonstrated that the presence of LPSO
phases in Mg alloys can increase the critical resolved shear stress
for the basal plane slip and hence can result in the activation of the
non-basal plane slip [2] and LPSO phases can exhibit high thermal
stability even at temperatures above 400
◦C [3,4]. These investiga-
tions indicated that LPSO phases have a potential role in improving
the mechanical properties of Mg alloys both at room temperature
and elevated temperatures.
LPSO phases have various types, e.g. 10H, 14H, 18R and 24R-
types [5], and can form mainly in Mg–Zn(Ni,Cu)–RE alloys (RE
represents rare earth elements) during casting [6–10] and can

and ageing treatment [14–16]. The mechanical properties of
Mg–Zn–RE alloys containing LPSO phases can be thus improved by
altering the type, morphology and amount of LPSO phases through
appropriate designs of casting and subsequent treatments. Further-
more, hot extrusion is an effective way in evaluating strengths and
improving ductility of Mg alloys due to its structural reﬁnement
role. For Mg–Zn–RE alloys containing LPSO phases, hot extrusion
can lead to a signiﬁcant improvement in the strengths and ductility
through the effective reﬁnements of LPSO phases and /H9251-Mg grains
[13,17,18]. The investigation of Yamasaki et al. [13] showed that
the yield strength and elongation of the extruded Mg
96.5Zn1Gd2.5
(at.%) alloy containing LPSO phases can reach 345 MPa and 6.9% due
to highly dispersed LPSO phases and ﬁne/H9251-Mg grains caused by hot
extrusion. Similar result was also reported by Yoshimoto et al. [17]
for the extruded Mg
96Zn2Y2 alloy containing LPSO phases, which
exhibited yield strength of 390 MPa and an elongation of 5% at room
temperature. The recent experiments revealed that the presence
of LPSO phases can also improve greatly the elevated tempera-
ture strengths of the extruded Mg
97Y2Cu1 alloy [7] and the rolled
Mg90.5Ni3.25Y6.25 alloy [8], the yield and ultimate tensile strengths
of these two alloys remain similar levels in a temperature range
from room temperature (RT) to 200
◦C, but drop rapidly above this
temperature [8].
Recently, a criteria for the formation of LPSO phases in
Mg–Zn–RE alloys was proposed by Kawamura and Yamasaki [18],
i.e. RE elements should have a hcp structure at room temperature,
a large solid solubility limit above 3.75 at.% in binary Mg–RE alloys
and an atomic size larger than that of Mg by 8.4–1.9%. Accord-

2. Experimental
Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn (at.%) alloy was prepared from pure Mg and
Zn and Mg–(20 wt.%)Dy master alloy in a graphite crucible under
an anti-oxidizing ﬂux. The melts were homogenized at 750
◦C
for 0.5 h and then were poured into a water-cooling mould of a
diameter of 85 mm and a length of 350 mm at 720
◦C. The ingots
were homogenized at 525 ◦C for 10 h and were machined into the
round bars with a diameter of 80 mm. The bars were extruded
by an extrusion ratio of 17 at 360
◦C and then were aged at
180 ◦C for 99 h. Microstructure, phase structure and composition
of the extruded Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn alloy were characterized by optical
microscope (OM) (Olym-pusGX71), scanning electron microscope
(SEM) (JSM-5600) with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS)
and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM-2100F). Thin foil
samples for the TEM observation were prepared using the Ion Pol-
ishing System (RES101). Samples for the optical examination were
polished and then etched in a solution of 4 mL nitric and 96 mL
ethanol. Tensile tests were carried out in an Instron-type tensile
testing machine (Instron 1211) at room temperature (RT), 100, 200
and 300
◦C, respectively, with a strain rate of 1 × 10−3 s−1 for all the
specimens tested. The tensile specimens with a gauge dimension of
40 mm× 5m m× 1.8 mm were cut from the treated bars with their
length direction parallel to the extrusion direction.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructures
Fig. 1 (a) shows the optical microstructure of the as-cast
Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn alloy. Lots of secondary phases can be observed
at the grain boundaries and in the grain interior of /H9251-Mg matrix.
The XRD analysis in Fig. 2 reveals that these secondary phases
are mainly the Mg
12ZnDy phase with a 18R LPSO structure. Such
18R LPSO phase has been commonly observed in the as-cast
Mg
97Zn1Dy2 alloy [18] and Mg97Zn1Y2 alloy [6]. In order to obtain
a microstructure containing the high content or high volume frac-
tion of lamellar LPSO phase, we performed the heat treatments at
a given temperature for different time. The result in Fig. 1 (b) and
(c) showed that heat treatment at 525
◦C for 10 h can lead to the
formations of the ﬁne lamellar structure in all the /H9251-Mg grains and
some (white) precipitation particles at the grain boundaries and in
the grain interior. The mean grain size of the /H9251-Mg matrix is about
80 /H9262m after the above treatment. The latter TEM observation and
analysis revealed that this lamellar structure is the 14H LPSO phase
and the precipitation particles are the (Mg, Zn)
xDy phase. Such 14H
LPSO phase was transformed from the original 18R LPSO phase
formed during casting and formed from the supersaturated /H9251-Mg
grains through the diffusions of Dy and Zn during heat treatment.

The transformation from the 18R LPSO phase to the 14H LPSO
phase during heat treatment can be explained by the minimization
of the shear strain energy [19,20]. Both 18R LPSO and 14H LPSO
phases are composed of the structure units that have a hexagonal
structure with different stacking sequences of the closely packed
plane [19]. The structure unit of the 18R LPSO phase contains three
ABCA-type building blocks that arrange in the same shear direc-
tion and result in a shear strain with respect to the /H9251-Mg matrix.
In contrast, the structure unit of the 14H LPSO phase contains two
ACBA-type building blocks that arrange in the opposite direction
and do not create any shear strain. Therefore, the 18R LPSO phase
is not thermodynamically stable and is gradually replaced by the
14H LPSO phase during heat treatment so as to relax the high shear
strain energy. So the 18R LPSO phase was considered to be an ele-
vated temperature phase and exists mainly in as-cast Mg–Zn–RE
alloys [6], while the 14H LPSO phase was often observed in heat
treated Mg–Zn–RE alloys [6,18].
The 14H LPSO phase can form from the /H9251-Mg matrix can be
attributed to a modulated structure-like mechanism [9,10]. The
formation of the 14H LPSO phase involves the introduction of the
stacking ordering and the formation of the chemical ordering [21].
In Mg–Zn–RE alloys, the diffusion of Zn and RE atoms to the stack-
ing fault planes can enhance the stacking ordering by reducing the
stacking fault energy and also can promote the chemical order-
ing [21,22]. The formation of the 14H LPSO phase from the /H9251-Mg
matrix takes place when both stacking order and chemical order
are satisﬁed. Such process has been observed in the Mg–Zn–RE
alloys after heat treatment where higher temperature and/or longer
time allow the full diffusion of Zn and RE atoms [11–13]. The high
volume faction of the 14H LPSO phase observed after heat treat-
ment in our Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn alloy can be explained by the above
two mechanisms.
Fig. 3(a) shows the optical microstructure of the Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn
alloy after hot extrusion at 360
◦C. The microstructure of the alloy
has been reﬁned signiﬁcantly during hot extrusion. The mean size
of the /H9251-Mg grains is measured to be about 3 /H9262m. This struc-
tural reﬁnement arises from the fragmentation of the 14H LPSO
phase through deformation kinking under higher extrusion ratio
of 17 [7,13,17,18], whereas usual dynamic recrystallization pro-
cess could not be expected in the present case since high thermal
stability of the 14H LPSO phase [3,4]. Fig. 3 (b) and (c) shows the
SEM microstructure after the ageing treatment at 180
◦C for 99 h.
As shown, the /H9251-Mg grains are composed nearly of the 14H LPSO
phase and have random orientation. The high volume fraction of
the 14H LPSO phase in our alloy involves the further dispersion
and growth of new 14H LPSO phase during ageing treatment since
the diffusion processes of Dy and Zn atoms can still occur during
prolonged ageing time. Such dispersion and growth processes of
the 14H LPSO phase during ageing treatment were also reported
in other Mg–Zn–RE alloys [14–16,21]. In addition, it is noted from
Fig. 3(a) and (b) that the mean gain size of the alloy was not altered
nearly after ageing treatment and is still about 3 /H9262m.
Fig. 4 (a) and (b) shows the TEM images of the extruded
Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn alloy before tensile test. Consistent with the SEM
microstructure shown in Fig. 3 (b) and (c), the ﬁne lamellar phase
can be observed nearly within all the /H9251-Mg grains (Fig. 4(a)). Some
coarse and bent lamellar phases can be also observed as indicated
by the arrows in Fig. 4 (b), which were transformed directly from
the original 18R LPSO phase. Our TEM observation revealed that
these lamellar phases have the same structure despite their differ-
ent morphologies. The magniﬁed TEM image of a typical lamellar
structure in Fig. 4(c) reveals that these lamellar structures consist
of the white nano-meter thick /H9251-Mg slices and the black bands that
arrange alternately and have the same spacing. The periodic diffrac-
tion spots at the interval of 1/14 of the distance between the direct
spot and ( 0002 )
Mg reﬂection in the selected area electron diffrac-
tion (SAED) pattern (see the insert of Fig. 4(c)) reveal that the black
bands are the 14H LPSO phase. In addition, our TEM observation did
not reveal the presence of the twin structures although they have
similar morphologies to those of the 14H LPSO phase under the
optical and SEM microscopes ( Fig. 3(a), (c) and (d)). Some ﬁne pre-
cipitates can be observed in the regions near the grain boundaries
in Fig. 4 (d). The SAED pattern in Fig. 4 (d) indicates that these ﬁne
precipitates are the (Mg, Zn)
xDy phase, which has a fcc structure
with a lattice constant of a = 0.7432 nm.
Fig. 5 shows the TEM images of the Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn alloy after
tensile test at RT and 300 ◦C. The signiﬁcant signs of plastic defor-
mation, i.e. the bending of the 14H LPSO phase and the detectable
dislocation structure formed in the nano-meter slices of the /H9251-Mg
grains can be observed for the specimens deformed at RT and 300
◦C
as shown in Fig. 5(b) and (d). These dislocations should be the basal
plane dislocations rather than the prismatic dislocations that locate
between the lamella of the 14H LPSO phase [2,23] and can be usu-
ally identiﬁed in high resolution TEM. The mean grain sizes of the
specimens after the tensile deformation were not altered signiﬁ-
cantly as compared with that before the tensile deformation, which
were estimated from the TEM images to be about 3 /H9262m and 4 /H9262m
for the specimens deformed at RT and 300
◦C, respectively. This
conﬁrms the important role of the 14H LPSO phase in stabilizing
the microstructure during elevated temperature deformation.

Besides the strengthening roles due to the grain reﬁnement
caused by hot extrusion and the presence of a small amount of the
(Mg, Zn)
xDy precipitations, the strengthening role caused by the
high volume fraction of the 14H LPSO phase could be a main mecha-
nism responsible for the excellent elevated temperature properties
observed for our Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn alloy. It has been suggested that
this strengthening of the LPSO phase comes from the increased
critical shear stress for the basal plane slip [2] and the limita-
tion to the dislocation activity in the nanometer thick /H9251-Mg slices
[23]. The higher thermal stability of those LPSO phase [3,4] can
also resist effectively the grain growth so that the strengthening
role of the grain reﬁnement can be maintained during elevated
temperature deformation. The recent investigation on the as-cast
Mg
97Zn1Y2 alloy revealed also that the deformation can result in
the fragmentation of LPSO phases through the formation of the
kink bands and boundaries and the rotation of the formed LPSO
blocks, the increased dislocation density in these kinking defor-
mation regions would lead to the additional strengthening [23].

phase, the kink mechanism is difﬁcult to operate due to insufﬁcient

the microstructure or the grain size. It is noted from Fig. 5(a) and (c)
that although deformation has led to the signiﬁcant bending of the
14H LPSO phase and the introduction of the dislocation structures,
the basic morphologies of the 14H LPSO phase and the mean grain
size did not show the signiﬁcant change for the specimens tested
at RT and 300

14H LPSO phase and the grain reﬁnement still remain till 300 ◦C.
The smaller drop in the strengths at 300 ◦C as compared with that
at RT can be explained by the dynamic recovery process of the dis-
location structures in the regions between the nano-meter thick
/H9251-Mg slices where the dislocation density appears to be lower than
that observed in the specimen deformed at RT as shown in Fig. 5(b)
and (d). Since this dynamic recovery process takes place only in
some local regions, it leads only to a small drop in the strength,
but is favorable for improving the ductility of the Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn
alloy. As indicated by the true stress-strain curves in the inset of
Fig. 6 , this dynamic recovery process of the dislocation structures
is also revealed well by the weaker strain hardening capability at
the initial strain stage for the specimen deformed at 300
◦C than
that the specimen deformed at RT. In addition, the ease activation
of the basal plane and non-basal plane slips at higher temperature
is also a possible factor in contributing to the improvement in the
elongation observed for our Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn alloy.
4. Conclusion
We have examined the microstructure and mechanical prop-
erties of the extruded Mg–2Dy–0.5Zn alloy. The alloy consists of
the ﬁne-grained /H9251-Mg matrix composed nearly of the 14H LPSO
phase and a small amount of (Mg, Zn)
xDy precipitations. The yield
and ultimate tensile strengths of the alloy can reach 245 MPa and
260 MPa at 300 ◦C, which are slightly lower than those at room
temperature (287 MPa and 321 MPa); the elongation of this alloy is
36% at 300
◦C, which is far higher than that at room temperature
(11.6%). The excellent elevated temperature strengths of the alloy
are attributed to the high volume fraction of the 14H LPSO phase
within the ﬁne-grained /H9251-Mg grains and the high thermal stability
of such microstructure during elevated temperature deformation.
