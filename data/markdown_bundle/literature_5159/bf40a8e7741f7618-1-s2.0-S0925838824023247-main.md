ABSTRACT
VNbTa refractory complex concentrated alloy (RCCA) possesses broad application prospects in a wide temper-
ature window owing to its remarkable strength-ductility synergy and super rolling formability, whereas poor
oxidation resistance will give rise to excessive wear above 800 ℃ . In this contribution, we aim to fabricate a
protective silicide coating on VNbTa RCCA for the purposes of enhancing high-temperature oxidation as well as
wear resistance. Microstructural characterizations revealed that a uniform and intact coating was obtained under
the optimized processing condition of pack cementation. The coating is mainly composed of a single-phase
(VNbTa)Si
2
, along with a thin M
5
Si
3
-type interdiffusion zone. Compared with bare alloy, the coated speci-
mens exhibit remarkable oxidation resistance at 1000 ℃ due to the preferential formation of protective SiO
2
scale. Meanwhile, the coated specimens maintain desirable wear resistance from 800 ℃ to 1000 ℃ . The results of
this study would offer a valuable reference to expedite the future engineering applications of RCCAs.
1. Introduction
The significant progress in aerospace engineering has placed greater
demands on materials served in aggressive environments. It is generally
acknowledged that, in real-world applications, increasing service tem-
perature of hot-end components provides potential opportunities to
achieve higher thermal efficiency and propulsion ratio for aero-engines
[1] . Of note, moving parts operating at high temperatures will confront
sustained damages of thermal oxidation and wear, which negatively
affects their service life. As a result, advanced materials with satisfactory
high-temperature resistance to oxidation and wear need to be urgently
exploited.
Over the past decade, an innovative class of metallic materials,
known as refractory complex concentrated alloys (RCCAs) or refractory
high entropy alloys (RHEAs) based on the pioneering design philosophy
of multiple principal compositions, has been valued as promiseful can-
didates suitable for elevated-temperature structural applications on ac-
count of ultra-high strength and melting temperatures [2 – 4] . Thus far,
several countermeasures have been mentioned to overcome strength
and ductility trade-off of RCCAs [5 – 7] . It is also worth noting that RCCAs
composed of refractory metals are subjected to catastrophic oxidation at
elevated temperatures, resulting in fatal degradations of components
[8 – 10] . For instance, investigation by Sheikh et al. has indicated that the
ductile Hf

[12 – 14] . Li et al. has pointed out that Si addition could inhibit the

permeability normally contributes to embrittlement of bulks owing to
the formation of hard and brittle phases [17 – 19] . In contrast to alloying
method, fabricating protective coatings is perceived as another solution

alloy exhibits superior wear resistance at around 800 ℃ , regrettably it
will suffer from excessive wear due to the severe thermal oxidation at
higher temperatures [37] . Hence, improving oxidation resistance of
VNbTa alloy has become a pivotal problem to be solved. In this study, a
protective silicide coating was deposited on VNbTa alloy via pack
cementation. The microstructure and phase constitute of the coating
were investigated. We subsequently evaluated the oxidation and wear
behaviors at high temperatures.
2. Experimental details
2.1. Material preparations
The substrate RCCA with a nominal composition of VNbTa (in at%)
was prepared by vacuum arc-melting in a water-cooled copper crucible
under the high-purity argon protection, and more than six melting cycles
were repeated to make sure the chemical homogeneity. Specimens for
the following coating fabrication were excised from the alloy ingots by
electric spark cutting. Thereafter, all sides of the specimens were me-
chanically ground using a series of silicon carbide abrasive papers and
ultrasonically rinsed in absolute ethyl alcohol to remove surface
contaminant.
The deposition of silicide coating was carried out by means of pack
cementation technique, as schematically shown in Fig. 1 . Firstly, a
uniform powder mixture of 30 wt% Si + 65 wt% Al
2
O
3
+ 5 wt% NaF was
obtained using planetary ball mill at 150 r/min for 4 h. Specimens were
then buried in an alumina crucible which was filled with the pack
mixture. Afterward, the crucible was placed into a tube furnace and
heated to 1300 ℃ at a heating ramp rate of 10 ℃ /min, holding for 0.5 h,
1 h, 1.5 h and 2 h and followed by furnace cooling. During the process of
pack cementation, high-purity argon was constantly introduced into the
tube furnace with a flux of 300 mL/min. After deposition, coating
specimens were then carefully polished to remove the adhered pack
mixture.
2.2. High-temperature oxidation and wear tests
Isothermal oxidation tests were performed in a chamber furnace
under static lab air at 1000 ℃ . Prior to the tests, alumina crucibles were
calcined at testing temperature for 4 h to eliminate the mass change of
crucibles. Rectangular specimens with the dimension of 6 mm × 6 mm
× 4 mm were placed in alumina crucibles. The crucibles and specimens
were put into the center of furnace after reaching testing temperatures,
and removed at the designated intervals to weigh the mass gain by an
electrical balance with the accuracy of 10
 4
g. Dry-sliding rotating wear
tests of the coated specimens were conducted at 800 ℃ , 900 ℃ and 1000
℃ using a ball-on-disc tribometer (HT-1000). Commercial Si
3
N
4
spheres
with the diameter of 6 mm were used as counterparts. The detailed
experimental methods and parameters of wear tests have been described
in our previous work [38] .
2.3. Microstructural characterization
X-ray diffractometer (XRD, PANalytical) with Cu-K α radiation was
utilized to identify the phase constituent. Morphologies and composi-
tions were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Nova
nano SEM 450) coupled with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS,
Oxford Instruments). The cross-sectional elemental distribution of the
coating was analyzed using electron probe micro-analyzer (EPMA, Shi-
madzu 8050 G). Detailed microstructural characterization was further
employed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Talos F200X),
and the TEM foil was prepared using focused ion beam (FIB) technique.
In addition, micro-beam XRD (Bruker D8 discover) was performed to
detect the composition of oxides on the worn surfaces.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure and phase analysis
The cross-sectional SEM morphologies of silicide coatings fabricated
at 1300 ℃ for various duration time of 0.5 h, 1 h, 1.5 h and 2 h are
presented in Fig. 2 . One can notice that the thickness of coatings
increased from 37 μ m to 68 μ m as the holding time prolonged from 0.5 h
to 2 h. It is widely believed that the growth kinetics of pack cementation
is dominantly diffusion-controlled [31,39] , thus improving holding time
facilitates the entry of Si atoms into the substrate alloy to form thicker
coatings. However, from Fig. 2 (d), obvious cracks, delamination and
peeling were observed in the most regions of the coating after pack
cementation for 2 h, which might be the result of thermal mismatch
between coating and substrate. In addition, a mass of cracks as well as
breakages appeared at the edges of the coating obtained after 2 h of pack

3.2. Oxidation kinetics and oxide scales
Isothermal oxidation tests at 1000 ℃ were performed to investigate
oxidation behaviors of the uncoated and coated specimens. Fig. 6 pro-
vides the mass gain per unit area versus exposure time during oxidation
tests, and the inset images show macroscopic morphologies after
oxidation tests. As shown in Fig. 6 (a), the mass gain of uncoated alloy
increased pronouncedly during the overall oxidation process. Clearly,
the kinetics curve of substrate alloy obeys a nearly linear oxidation with
a great mass gain of 69.20 mg/cm
2
after exposure for 60 min. Note that
the bare alloy suffered from a certain volume expansion, and there are
severe spalling and cracking on the edges of specimen. By contrast, the
oxidation kinetics of coated specimen can be fitted as a parabolic law
with the final increase in mass gain of 1.68 mg/cm
2
. In addition, it can
be seen from the inset image of Fig. 6 (b) that the coated specimen
notably retained a pristine form after oxidation for 24 h, indicating the
oxidation resistance is well improved. Therefore, the as-deposited
coating is deemed to be an effective barrier for thermal oxidation.

specimen after oxidation tests at 1000 ℃ for different exposure time are

The morphologies and auxiliary composition analysis of worn sur-
faces were investigated to disclose the wear mechanisms of coated
specimens at 800 – 1000 ℃ , as shown in Fig. 10 and Table 1 . Clearly,
ploughing furrows emerge on the worn surfaces at 800 ℃ and 900 ℃
( Fig. 10 (a-b)), which indicates the feature of abrasive wear. As the
testing temperature escalates to 1000 ℃ , in Fig. 10 (c), the worn surface
is covered with extensive oxide scale. From Fig. 10 (d), the magnified
view reveals that, at 800 ℃ , a relatively smooth worn surface, wherein
scattered oxide products are observed. The EDS analysis manifests that
abundant Si exists in the smooth areas, while scattered debris possess
relatively high concentration of O ( Table 1 ). These hard and flaky oxide
debris were exfoliated and scratch the surface during sliding contact,
resulting in the three-body abrasion [45] . It can be seen from Fig. 10 (e),
the content of oxide debris increases significantly, and thus more
distinctive furrows are appeared on the worn surface at 900 ℃ . In
addition, there are a few microcracks on the worn surfaces at 800 ℃ and
900 ℃ due to the stress concentration induced by recurrent loads.
Therefore, the most involved wear mechanism at 800 ℃ and 900 ℃ is
abrasive wear, coupled with mild oxidation wear. At 1000 ℃ , the worn
surface is rather rough with large areas of oxide flakes ( Fig. 10 (f)). These
extensive oxide scales probably contribute to enhance tribological
properties by impede the direct contact between the friction pairs. It is
allowed to draw a conclusion that the governing wear mechanism at
1000 ℃ is determined to be the representative oxidation wear.

4. Conclusions
In this study, a silicide coating with sufficient thickness and good
adherence to the substrate was fabricated via pack cementation tech-
nique at 1300 ℃ . The microstructure, high-temperature oxidation and
wear behaviors were carefully investigated. The main conclusions are
outlined as follows:
(1) The thickness of coatings increased with the holding time pro-
longed, but obvious cracks and peeling appeared after too long
holding time. The optimized processing condition of pack
cementation was determined to be 1.5 h.
(2) It was demonstrated that the silicide coating with a total thick-
ness of approximately 60 μ m is mainly composed of single-phase
(VNbTa)Si
2
, along with a thin M
5
Si
3
-type interdiffusion zone.
(3) At 1000 ℃ , the coated specimens exhibit remarkable oxidation
resistance due to the preferential formation of protective SiO
2
scale which blocks the inward permeation of oxygen and outward
diffusion of metallic atoms.
(4) The average COF and wear rate of the coated specimens show a
certain extent of decrease with the testing temperature
increasing. Most notably, the silicide coating with excellent
antioxidant ability protects bare alloy from excessive wear at
high temperatures.
