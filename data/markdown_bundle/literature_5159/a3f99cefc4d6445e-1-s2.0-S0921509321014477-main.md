ABSTRACT 
C103 is a refractory-based niobium alloy for high temperature applications in aerospace. This paper aims to 
evaluate the compatibility of C103 alloy as a choice of material for laser-based additive manufacturing (AM) 
technologies. Crack free AM specimens with relative densities of greater than 99% were successfully fabricated 
via laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) method with horizontal and vertical build orientations. Static strength levels 
of the stress-relieved specimens exceeded that of their wrought counterparts. Higher yield and ultimate tensile 
strength of AM specimens were attributed to high dislocation density and the formation of dislocation cell 
structures, which was revealed by transmission electron microscopy. Loss of ductility in AM specimens was 
partially due to high density of dislocations (i.e., shorter mean free path for dislocations), while the presence of 
defects may also have compromised the total elongation to failure. Electron backscattered diffraction analysis 
revealed formation of sharp 001 || BD rod texture in AM samples, which resulted in anisotropic mechanical 
behavior. Uniaxial tensile and compressive deformation led to the development of (001) < 110 > parallel to 
tensile direction and < 111 > parallel to build direction, respectively. 
1. Introduction 
Refractory metals and alloys, such as niobium (Nb), tantalum (Ta), 
tungsten (W), and molybdenum (Mo), are known for their high melting 
temperature (i.e. above 2400 
◦
C), high corrosion resistance in liquid 
alkali metals and molten salts, and maintaining structural integrity at 
elevated temperatures up to 1400 – 1800 
◦
C, where other engineering 
alloys cannot be used [ 1 – 6 ]. Full industrial adoption of refractory alloys 
has been restricted primarily due to manufacturing difficulties stemming 
from their demanding fabrication processes, such as vacuum arc melting 
or powder metallurgy methods followed by thermomechanical pro -
cessing [ 7 ]. High bulk density and intrinsic lack of ductility at lower 
temperatures for refractories like W and Mo alloys further limit their 
widespread application. Poor low temperature ductility of these mate -
rials originates from a high ductile to brittle transition temperature 
(DBTT) associated with their body-centered cubic (BCC) lattice struc -
ture, while interstitial contamination (e.g. oxygen) further exacerbates 
the ductility issues [ 8 ]. 
Though Nb is a refractory metal with BCC crystal structure, it is 
ductile at room temperature. In fact, elongation to failure as high as 40% 
at room temperature has been reported for Nb [ 9 , 10 ]. Bulk density of Nb 
is 8.57 g/cc, which is comparable to that of Ni based superalloys and 
stainless steels [ 11 ]. Nb-based alloys can serve at temperatures up to 
1400 
◦
C, at which Ni-based superalloys completely lose their structural 
integrity [ 7 , 12 ]. Thus, Nb can be a viable candidate for high tempera -
ture industrial applications where density is required to be under 9 g/cc. 
Nb-based alloys with high to low strength were developed in the late 
1950s and early 1960s. The alloy was used in nozzle extended segment 
of the apollo manned lunar module spacecraft as well as in command 
module in 1965. However, it did not survive as they were strong-armed 
by economic pressure and unsuccessful/costly development efforts [ 13 ]. 
However, C103 with nominal composition of Nb-10Hf-1Ti wt.% and 
bulk density of 8.85 g/cc has been popular ever since [ 14 ]. C103 has 
been adopted for various ultra-high temperature applications, such as

Modern manufacturing techniques can alleviate some manufacturing 
challenges by virtue of removing the need for pressing, sintering, and 
thermomechanical processing, which will drive down the production 
costs and will make the refractory products more marketable. Additive 
manufacturing (AM) is an appealing manufacturing technique for re -
fractory metals and alloys, owing to the ability to reach very high melt 
pool temperatures [ 18 – 20 ]. Inherent advantages of AM such as complex 
geometry design and fabrication to near-net shape which significantly 
reduces the need for machining and post-processing as compared to 
conventional manufacturing process, which is one of the main drivers 
for manufacturing cost of some refractories. However, issues associated 
with AM of refractories, such as grain boundary cracking is yet to be 
addressed. New refractory alloys are also needed to be developed so as to 
fully deploy the capabilities of AM techniques. 
In this work, we used laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) AM method to 
manufacture C103 specimens as a benchmark to examine the compati -
bility of Nb alloys with laser-based AM technologies, which will serve as 
a starting point for developing new Nb-based alloys for AM. The authors 
aim to use a proper set of process parameters to produce crack-free and 
near fully dense C103 specimens to evaluate their mechanical properties 
and microstructural characteristics. Nano to micro-scale characteriza -
tion of the microstructure helped us understand the key differences 
between AM samples and thermomechanically processed ones, which 
explained the differences in their mechanical behavior.

3.2. Microstructural characterization 
3.2.1. Initial microstructure 
The initial microstructure of a stress relived sample is shown in 
Fig. 5 a & b, which are EBSD scans conducted parallel and perpendicular 
to build direction (BD). This was conducted to analyze the crystallo -
graphic texture and grain structure of the AM C103 specimens. The in -
verse pole figure (IPF) map in Fig. 5 a shows a columnar grain structure, 
where the longitudinal axis of the grains is aligned with BD as a result of 
heat conduction towards the build plate [ 32 , 33 ]. The average grain size 
of this sample was 56 μ m with a standard deviation of 41 μ m. High 
standard deviation in grain size is due to a wider spread of grain size in 
this specimen (grain size distribution is shown in Fig. 5 e). The micro -
structure is composed of single-phase BCC grains, and EBSD scans with 
higher resolutions did not reveal the presence of any non-equilibrium 
phases. The average thickness of lamellar grains in Fig. 5 a was 10 μ m 
with a standard deviation of 8 μ m, which translates to a grain size aspect 
ratio of 5.6. It is worth noting that Sankar et al. [ 16 ] reported grain size 
of 35 – 45 μ m for the wrought C103 with equiaxed grain structure. The 
average grain size in Fig. 5 b was 40 μ m with a standard deviation of 35 
μ m with grain size distribution shown in Fig. 5 f. The pole figures in 
Fig. 5 c & d shows a strong fiber texture where (001) poles are parallel to 
the BD, which is consistent with the observed anisotropy in mechanical 
properties of AM C103 specimens in this work [ 34 , 35 ].

TEM characterization was done on a stress relieved sample to explore 
the dislocation density and configurations within the grains. TEM results 
for a stress relieved sample are shown in Fig. 7 . Fig. 7 a is a bright field 
image for [ 113 ] zone axis and 30 1 diffraction vector (g). Fig. 7 a is at the 
exact zone axis with grains in darker contrast in [ 113 ] orientation. High 
dislocation density is evident from the micrographs with cell structure 
for the stress relieved condition. High density of statically stored dislo -
cations is due to fast cooling rates during AM process, which does not 
provide enough time for the nucleated dislocations to fully rearrange 
and leave a “ cleaner ” array of cell structure walls. As seen from Fig. 7 c, 
the inside of the cells has contrast attributed to the presence of dislo -
cations. Cell structure formation accompanied by high density of sta -
tistically stored dislocations explains the higher strength levels and 
lower ductility of the stress relieved AM C103 as compared to its 
wrought counterparts. Presence of high dislocation density, dislocation 
cell-structures, and abundance of low angle grain boundaries act as 
barriers for dislocation motion [ 37 ]. Cell structure and dislocations can 
be seen in Figs. 6c, 7d and 7 e, which are bright field images with slightly 
different tilts to reveal the “ incompleteness ” of the cell structures evo -
lutions. Similar observations consistent with the results of this work 
have been reported for AM materials. Aydin ¨oz et al. [ 38 ] reported 
higher static strength levels in an as-built AM Inconel 718 (IN718) 
specimen as compared to the solution treated one, which was rational -
ized by formation of dislocation cell structures in as-built condition. 
Solution treatment resulted in dislocation annihilation/recovery, which 
led to growth of cell structure or sub-grain into larger sub-grain/grains 
[ 31 ]. 
The TEM analysis also confirmed that the constituent phase on its 
entirety was BCC, which proved the EBSD results. We also examined the 
TEM samples for the presence of any non-equilibrium or undesired 
phases, for which second phase contrast was not observed. 
3.2.2. Deformation microstructure 
EBSD scans in Fig. 8 were taken from the gauge length and the vi -
cinity of the fracture surface of the horizontally fabricated tensile 
specimen after testing, which was sectioned perpendicular to the BD. 
The IPF and IQ map from the gauge length show grain deformation/ 
refinement during deformation as the grain size was reduced to 28 ± 20 
μ m. The corresponding pole figures in Fig. 8 c showed no discernible 
change in the orientation of the grains as compared to undeformed state 
in Fig. 5 b. However, texture evolution because of tensile deformation 
was observed in the pole figures corresponding to the vicinity of fracture 
surface in Fig. 8 f, which originated from the localized deformation 
bands observed in Fig. 8 e. 
The change in texture at the vicinity of fracture surface appears to 
evolve an evenly distributed < 001 > fiber texture by aligning (110) 
poles with the tensile direction. Tensile deformation in body centered 
cubic (BCC) materials forms < 110 > fiber texture [ 39 ]. However, the 
preexisting < 001 > ||BD fiber texture in the AM C013, as opposed to an 
initially random texture, lead to predominant formation of (001) 
< 110 > ||TD component. Transverse direction (TD) and tensile direction 
is the same for this tensile specimen. The texture intensity was decreased 
from ~14 multiple random distribution (m.r.d) to ~8 m.r.d. due to the 
deformation induced texture evolutions, which was similar to changes in 
texture under compressive deformation.

4. Conclusions 
The mechanical properties and microstructural characteristics of AM 
C103 alloy fabricated via laser powder bed fusion method was evaluated 
in this study. The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1) C103 specimens were successfully fabricated via L-PBF AM 
method with relative densities of greater than 99% though 
further process parameter optimization is required as lack of 
fusion defects were present. 
(2) YS and UTS of the stress-relieved samples at room temperature 
exceeded that of their wrought counterpart at oxygen levels 
around 500 ppm. However, this came with a loss of ductility 
though ductile fracture behavior was still observed at defect free 
sites. Loss of ductility could be partially due to presence of lack of 
fusion defects and low work hardening capacity originating from 
high density of statistically stored dislocations and cell structure 
formation, thereby reducing mean free path of dislocations. 
(3) TEM observations proved the formation of cell structures and 
high dislocation density, which were the main driver for higher 
static strength values. AM C103 alloy in this study had a single- 
phase BCC microstructure with no observable nonequilibrium 
phases. 
(4) The lamellar grain structure came with a sharp 001 || BD fiber 
texture, which led to apparent anisotropy in mechanical 
behavior. The horizontal specimen had lower compressive yield 
strength, while its higher work hardening rate resulted in a higher 
max flow stress under compressive deformation. 
(5) Tensile deformation led to formation of (001) < 110 > ||TD texture 
component, while compressive deformation led to formation of 
< 111 > ||BD fiber component. Texture evolutions resulted in 
texture randomization under both compression and tensile stress 
states. 
Recommendations for future work 
The limitation and lack of resources in this work have indicated 
recommendation for further work. These include but are not limited to 
optimizing the process parameter to build defect free specimens. 
Manufacturing vertical tensile samples and studying anisotropy in me -
chanical properties in depth accompanied by fatigue tests at different 
temperatures will help understand the mechanical performance of AM 
C103 better. Additionally, characterization techniques such as TEM can 
be performed in the deformed samples at different condition to under -
stand the deformation mechanisms in detail.
