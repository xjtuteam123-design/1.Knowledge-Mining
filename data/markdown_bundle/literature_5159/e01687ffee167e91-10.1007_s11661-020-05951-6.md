Effect of Ageing on Microstructure, Mechanical
Properties and Creep Behavior of Alloy 740H
A.H.V. PAVAN, R.L. NARAYAN, KULVIR SINGH, and U. RAMAMURTY
A study on the mechanical property changes and the creep behavior of a Ni-based alloy 740H
on ageing at 800 /C176 C for 10250 hours is conducted. Microstructural characterization reveals that
the alloy initially contains ﬁne c¢ phase and carbide precipitates within grains and at grain
boundaries. Ageing does not aﬀect the grain size but coarsens the c¢ phase and facilitates further
precipitation of carbides at grain boundaries, which gradually form a continuous network. This
signiﬁcantly deteriorates the mechanical properties in ambient conditions with marked
reductions in the impact energy and yield strength. The strength of the aged alloy further
reduces at 710 /C176 C. In contrast, ageing appears to have insigniﬁcant inﬂuence on the ductility,
which is > 30 pct at both testing temperatures. Ageing also reduces the creep strength of the
alloy and accelerates the creep rate. The creep stress exponent was found to increase from 6.7 to
11.2 after ageing, suggesting a transition in the deformation mechanism. The observed
reductions in yield strength, impact toughness and creep strength are attributed to the
coarsening of the c¢ precipitate, formation of network carbides at grain boundaries and
formation of c¢ precipitate-free zone adjacent to grain boundary carbide network. This c¢
precipitate-free zone also weakens the grain boundary and facilitates grain boundary sliding at
higher temperatures. However, under quasi-static conditions, c¢ precipitate-free zone undergoes
plastic deformation and helps in preserving the ductility of the aged alloy. Implications of these
changes are discussed in the context of high-temperature structural applications, such as in
power plants.

I. INTRODUCTION
THE development of advanced ultra supercritical
(AUSC) power plants, which are more eﬃcient and
greener as compared to the sub-critical and supercritical
power plants, mandates the identiﬁcation of new mate-
rials that can retain their structural integrity at temper-
atures and pressures in excess of 700 /C176 C and 31 MPa,
respectively.[1–4] The usage of Ni-based superalloys for
the fabrication of high temperature AUSC components
is inevitable as steels cannot sustain prolonged service
temperatures beyond 700 /C176 C. Various Ni-based super-
alloys such as Alloy 230, HR6W, Alloy 617, Alloy 263,
Alloy 740/740H, Alloy 282, etc. have been developed for
meeting AUSC power plants operation criteria. [5–10]
The Ni based superalloy, Alloy 740, which exhibits
high strength and toughness even at a temperature of
760 /C176 C, appears to be an ideal candidate material for
making high temperature AUSC boiler components. [5,6]
Detailed studies on this alloy [11–14] conﬁrmed its supe-
rior creep strength and also provided insight into
microstructural changes occurring and precipitation of
g, G phases on creep deformation. Elevated temperature
ageing studies in the range of 750 /C176 C to 810 /C176 C for
durations of 100 to 5000 hours reported by References
15–18 indicate that a stable microstructure evolves with
an increase in the size of the principal strengthening
phase, c¢, leading to a marginal lowering of the strength
and precipitation of various carbides along with g and G
phases. Additionally, Alloy 740 also possesses excellent
ﬁreside corrosion resistance as reported by References 5,
19, 20. As a result, it meets the prescribed standards for
AUSC applications.
Despite the above mentioned advantages, making
boiler components using Alloy 740 is a challenge as it
has weldability issues due to the formation of cracks and
ﬁssures in the heat aﬀected zones and degradation of
mechanical properties, which is attributed to the

Such brittle phases, primarily composed of Ti and Si,

Although the mechanical behavior of Alloy 740H has
been thoroughly characterized in the as-received condi-
tion[8,24,25] and to some extent in the aged condi-
tion,[26–30] its susceptibility to possible embrittlement
during long term high temperature service, especially
creep behavior, has not been examined yet. For instance,
it is likely that any component that is used in AUSC
power plants will be exposed to elevated temperatures
for more than 100,000 hours, [1–5,7,16] which is likely to
induce microstructural changes with service time. It is
not yet known if these microstructural changes will
aﬀect the mechanical and creep behavior of Alloy 740H.
To address this particular aspect, we subjected it to a
high temperature ageing treatment and examined the
microstructural changes occurring due to this ageing
treatment. The mechanical properties such as strength,
hardness, impact toughness of the alloy in the as-re-
ceived, and aged conditions are compared. In particular,
creep tests are conducted on aged alloys for durations
exceeding 6000 hours, in order to understand the role of
ageing on the creep behavior of the alloy and identify
the deformation mechanisms in quasi-static and
creep-loading conditions.
II. EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS
Alloy 740H was procured in the form of a hot-rolled,
annealed and aged plate and complies with American
Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Boiler and
Pressure Vessel (BPVC) Code Case (CC) 2702. [25] This
alloy was manufactured by Vacuum Induction Melting
(VIM) of raw materials and remelting of the ingot
obtained from VIM process was performed, so as to
ensure better chemical homogeneity, lower defects and
lower inclusion content. This remelted ingot was
homogenized and hot worked, following which it was
solution annealed at 1140 ( ± 20 /C176 C) for 16 hours
followed by ageing at 800 /C176 C( ± 5 /C176 C) for 4 hours
before being air cooled. This as-received alloy will
hitherto be referred to as ‘‘AR’’ alloy in this work. The
nominal speciﬁed composition of the alloy and actual
chemical composition evaluated using Optical Emission
Spectroscopy (OES) are provided in Table I. Blocks
were sectioned from the AR alloy plates along rolling
direction as shown in supplementary Figure S-1(a) (refer
to electronic supplementary material). These blocks
were subjected to ageing treatment in a three-zone
furnace ﬁtted with thyristor based Proportional-Inte-
gral-Derivative (PID) controllers. Ageing was carried
out at 800 /C176 C for 10250 hours before being air-cooled.
Note that the components made from this alloy typically
operate at service temperatures close to 750 /C176 C for
140,000 hours. However, since the ageing time is
enormously high and may not be accessible for labora-
tory tests or otherwise, we subject the material to a
higher temperature to achieve the aged condition as a
part of a standard accelerated ageing approach. Details
of this approach are as follows. The target service
temperature and time are used to derive Larson Miller
Parameter (LMP) which is deﬁned by the following
equation[31, 32]:
LMP ¼ Tr log10 tr þ CðÞ ½ 1/C138
where Tr is the test temperature (in K), tr is the time to
rupture (in hours), and C is a material-speciﬁc constant
referred to as the Larson Miller constant. The LMP
obtained from the target service conditions is equated
with the LMP of the accelerated ageing service condi-
tions. Therefore, by specifying accelerated ageing tem-
perature, the ageing duration can be obtained from
Eq. [ 1] and vice versa . The main advantage of this
approach is that it signiﬁcantly reduces the ageing time,
10250 hours in this case, which was obtained by the
Larson Miller Parameter extrapolation approach. The
alloy subjected to ageing treatment will be hitherto
referred to as ‘‘aged’’ alloy.
Samples for metallographic investigations were
extracted from blocks of the aged alloy and were
ground using emery papers for up to an American
National Standards Institute (ANSI) grit size of 1200
and then polished using polycrystalline diamond solu-
tion on a microﬁber cloth in steps of 6, 3, 1, and 0.25 lm
solutions. Final polishing was carried out using 0.05 lm
alumina suspension. Etching was carried out using a
freshly prepared solution of H 2O2 and concentrated HCl
mixed in the proportion 1:25. Microstructural investi-
gations were conducted using Leica DMI 5000M
inverted metallurgical microscope in the bright ﬁeld
mode for low magniﬁcation imaging and Carl Zeiss
Supra 55 VP Field Emission scanning electron micro-
scope (FESEM) for high magniﬁcation imaging. The
composition of various phases was determined using
energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) inside the
FESEM at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV.
Shimadzu HSV-30 hardness tester was used for
conducting Vickers diamond indentation on AR and
aged alloys at room temperature. Samples were polished
such that the two opposing surfaces are parallel to the
horizontal for the indenter tip to make normal contact
with surface. A load of 5 kg and dwell time of 15
seconds were maintained in all tests. Each indent is large

and > 0.5 pct, respectively. [5,12,13,16,20,36] Since these
conditions are not met in Alloy 740H, the above-men-
tioned phases do not form on ageing.
B. Mechanical Properties
The hardness of AR and aged alloys is examined to
understand the strengthening eﬀects of the phases and
precipitates that form during ageing. The average
hardness, H, for AR and aged alloys are 328 ± 16 and
299 ± 15 HV, respectively (see Table IV). Similar values
of H suggests that the formation of carbides and
coarsening of the c¢ phase during ageing does not
signiﬁcantly aﬀect the hardness. In contrast, the impact
energies (IEs) of AR and aged alloys, which are also
listed in Table IV, show that the IE of the aged alloy is
only one-fourth of that the AR alloy, indicating severe
embrittlement of the alloy due to ageing. Fractographs
obtained from the impact tested samples are shown in
Figure 3. The fracture surface of the AR alloy is rough
and contains several dimple-like features, which suggests
that it undergoes predominantly ductile fracture (see
Figure 3(a)). The fracture surface of the aged alloy is
faceted and exhibits inter-granular brittle fracture fea-
tures (see Figure 3(b)).
The yield strength, YS, ultimate tensile strength, UTS,
pct elongation, ef, of the AR and aged alloys measured
using tensile tests at RT and 710 /C176 C are presented in
Table IV. At both temperatures, YS as well as UTS of
the aged alloy are signiﬁcantly lower than that in the AR
condition. In contrast, the reduction in ef upon ageing is
only marginal, which suggests that ageing does not
deteriorate the ductility in a signiﬁcant manner. In fact,
since ef of the aged alloy at 710 /C176 C is similar to that
measured at RT, it appears that ageing is beneﬁcial for
retaining high temperature ductility of the Alloy 740H.
Representative fractographs obtained on the tensile
tested specimens are shown in Figures 4 and 5. It can be
observed that the fracture surfaces of the AR alloy that
was tested at RT and ~ 710 /C176 C contain voids and
faceted fracture features. These features indicate that at
RT, tensile failure in the AR alloy occurs via a mixture
of brittle intergranular and ductile fracture mechanisms.
It was also noted that fracture features on tensile
specimens tested at 710 /C176 C have a higher proportion of
intergranular fracture features compared to those tested
at RT (See Figures 4(a) and (b)). This suggests that at
higher temperatures, the grain boundaries in the AR
alloy weaken and lead to brittle intergranular failure,
which in turn reduces ef. In Figures 5(a) and (b), the
fracture surfaces of the tensile tested aged alloy speci-
mens, tested at RT and 710 /C176 C, respectively, are
displayed. It was observed that the fracture surfaces
on both specimens contain faceted and ductile features.
However, the surfaces of the facets have a rough
appearance unlike the smooth facets observed on the
fracture surfaces of the tensile tested AR alloy. Closer
inspection (see inset of Figures 5(a) and (b)) revealed
vein patterns and voids that are associated with ductile
failure. From this an important distinction regarding the
fracture mechanism in AR and aged alloys is made.
While the fracture surfaces of Alloy 740H in the AR and
aged conditions have a mixture of brittle and ductile
fracture features, the contrasting length scales of these
features indicate that the fracture mechanisms in them
are diﬀerent. In the AR alloy, the ductile fracture
features, i.e., voids and vein patterns, are observed at the
same scale as that of brittle intergranular facets. This
implies that the likelihood of the crack growing across
grains (transgranular) or along grain boundaries (inter-
granular) depends on their relative strengths. However,
in aged alloys, crack propagation occurs along the grain
boundaries, which leads to a completely faceted appear-
ance of the fracture surface. Although intergranular
crack propagation typically leads to brittle fracture,

High stress exponents ( n > 8) have also been reported
in the case of c/c¢ strengthened Ni-based superal-
loys,[41,42] oxide dispersion strengthened (ODS)
steels[41,43] and whisker/short ﬁber-based metal matrix
composite systems. [44–46] Such high stress exponents
were rationalized by incorporation of a back stress
term in Eq. [ 2], which now takes the form [41,43]:

where, rb is the back/resisting stress, no is the true stress
exponent, Q is the true creep activation energy and E is
the elastic modulus at the test temperature. Back stress
is deﬁned in case of Ni-based superalloys as the stress
required to either shear a c¢ precipitate or bypass it,
depending on its size. [41] Similarly, it is deﬁned as the

to dislocation motion by precipitates or dispersoids due
to the back stress, high stress exponents could be
explained. While the value of no could not be measured
in this study as tests were performed only at one
temperature, prior studies have shown that it is typically
close to ~ 4, which corresponds to that of dislocation
creep in pure metals or solid solution strengthened
alloys that do not contain any secondary phases. [41,43,45]

aged state, further creep studies at diﬀerent tempera-
tures are necessary.

IV. DISCUSSION
The process of ageing introduces two major
microstructural changes that inﬂuence the mechanical
properties of Alloy 740H. They are: (a) coarsening of the
c¢ precipitate, which is accompanied by the formation of
the c¢ free zone near the grain boundaries, and (b)
coarsening and linking up of carbides at grain bound-
aries. The reduction in strength after ageing is associated
with the coarsening of the c¢ precipitate. Prior studies
have shown that the coherency between c¢ and c, which
is necessary for dislocations to percolate through the
interface and shear the c¢ precipitate, is the main source
of strengthening in Ni-based superalloys. [13,17,26,27]
However, if c¢ coarsens beyond a critical size, it loses
coherency with the c phase.[13,17,26–28] In such a situa-
tion, dislocations have to bow around the precipitate
leaving behind dislocation loops as they move beyond
the precipitates. The creation of this potential pathway
for motion of dislocations reduces the strength of the
alloy.[7,13,26,27,47]

In principle, the formation of carbides during ageing
could have potentially counterbalanced the reduction in
strength arising from coarsening of c¢. This is because
carbides strengthen the alloy by blocking the motion of
dislocations in the matrix. However, in the aged Alloy
740H, the interaction of dislocations with carbides is
limited as they form at the grain boundaries. Neverthe-
less, the c¢ precipitate-free zone adjacent to these
networked carbide structures present at the grain
boundaries inﬂuences the fracture of the aged Alloy
740H at both RT and elevated temperatures. The
venous and rough appearance of the facets in the
tensile-tested specimen fractographs suggests the c¢
precipitate-free zone deforms plastically under quasi-
static loading conditions. In fact, the aged alloy exhibits
these characteristics even at elevated temperatures,
which is the primary reason for the retention of ductility
of aged Alloy 740H specimens tested at Ttensile ~ 710 /C176 C.
In contrast, AR Alloy 740H has poor high temperature
ductility as there is no the c¢ precipitate-free zone
adjacent to grain boundaries to undergo plastic defor-
mation. This particular phenomenon of ductilization
due to the formation of the c¢ precipitate-free zone
adjacent to the network structure of carbides at the
grain boundaries has not been previously reported and
further studies are necessary to understand this mech-
anism. In contrast, these network carbides exhibit brittle
behavior under dynamic loading conditions, which
manifests as low impact resistance of the aged alloy.
In the context of creep resistance, prior studies [12]
have suggested that it and rupture duration are directly
correlated with the following microstructural changes:
(a) grain size, (b) gradual variations in size, shape and
distribution of c¢, (c) carbide precipitation, (d) g phase
precipitation, and (e) the formation of r or Laves or
other Topologically Close Packed (TCP) phases. In the
present study, neither g nor TCP phases were observed.
Hence, these shall not be further discussed. With respect
to grain size, it is well known that materials with larger
grains typically exhibit higher creep resistance. How-
ever, in the present work, while the grain sizes of the AR
and aged alloys are similar, the average size of c¢ in the
latter is larger. Therefore, it is evident the lower creep
resistance and rupture duration of the aged alloy
compared to that of the Alloy 740 [12] is not related to
the grain size. It is to be noted that the average grain size
of Alloy 740 (heat E mentioned in Reference 12) is 113
lm which is close to that of the grain size of AR alloy
(118 lm) and hence, the aforementioned inference
appears to be valid. This, in turn, suggest that the
lowering of creep resistance is indirectly related to
coarsening of c¢ during ageing.
It is well known that the c¢ phase, irrespective of the
size, resists dislocation motion by interacting with them
and hence contributes to the creep-strength of the alloy.
A similar mechanism of dislocation pinning by c¢ was
also reported for Alloy 740. [13,37] Increased precipitation
of carbides at grain boundaries during ageing led to the
formation of c¢ precipitate free zone adjacent to grain
boundaries during ageing treatment. This, in turn, leads
to local reduction in creep-resistance at these locations.
This promotes accelerated creep failure in the alloy due
to grain boundary sliding, which is also consistent with
the elevated value of the creep exponent. Such a grain
boundary sliding mechanism was also reported in Alloy
740.[37] Moreover, it appears that the formation of the
networked carbide structure does not inhibit this grain
boundary sliding. Macroscopically, creep fractured
samples in our study exhibit intergranular fracture,
which suggests that the grain interiors are stronger. A
higher magniﬁcation of the fractured facets shows the
presence of vein patterns over them, which indicates that
the c¢ precipitate free zones adjacent to the grain
boundaries have locally deformed. This validates the
hypothesis by Unocic et al.,[37] who proposed that the c¢
precipitate free zones adjacent to the grain boundaries
would enhance the creep rates. Detailed characterization
studies with Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
are required to further understand this mechanism in
detail.
Before closing, the eﬀects of ageing on some crucial
mechanical properties of alloy 740H are taken into
perspective. While, results indicate that ageing deterio-
rates the yield strength and accelerates creep damage in
the alloy, these eﬀects would have insigniﬁcant out-
comes if the applied r < 200 MPa on the material.

V. CONCLUSION
In summary, microstructural and mechanical behav-
ior of Alloy 740H in the as-received and aged state at
room temperature and elevated temperature (710 /C176 C)
was studied. Ageing leads to coarsening of c¢ and growth
of MC, M 23C6, carbides within the grains and at grain
boundaries. Moreover, in this process certain elements
diﬀuse to the grain boundary and lead the formation of
a c¢ precipitate free zone adjacent to grain boundaries.
While, coarsening of c¢ reduces the yield strength and
hardness of the alloy, the presence of hard intermetallic
carbides at the grain boundaries deteriorate its impact
strength. However, the c¢ precipitate free zone adjacent
to grain boundary carbide network deforms in quasi-
static loading conditions, which preserves the ductility
of the aged alloy at ambient and elevated temperatures.
Coarsening of c¢ also diminishes the creep strength of
the alloy and the formation of a c¢ free zone and a grain
boundary carbide network does not signiﬁcantly
improve the creep resistance of the grain boundaries.
This leads to higher creep strains due to grain boundary
sliding. The utility of this material for static applications
is recommended at stresses lower than 200 MPa at
operating temperatures up to 750 /C176 C for upto 10000
hours. If the component needs to be operated for longer
durations, a compromise must be made on the applied
stress or temperature.
