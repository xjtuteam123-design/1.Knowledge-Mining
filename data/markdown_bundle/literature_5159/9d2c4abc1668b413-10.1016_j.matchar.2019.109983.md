Enhanced mechanical properties of carbon-doped FeNiMnAlCr high entropy
alloy via hot-rolling
Margaret Wu∗, Chao Yang, Michael Kuijer, Ian Baker
Thayer School ofEngineering,DartmouthCollege, Hanover,NH, 03755,USA

ABSTRACT
This work demonstrates an improvement in the mechanical performance of the high entropy alloy
Fe40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 containing 1.1 at. % carbon (CHEA) by hot-rolling followed by annealing at 1323 K.
The microstructure was examined using scanning electron microscopy, electron backscatter diffraction, X-ray
diffraction, and transmission electron microscopy. Room-temperature tensile tests were used to assess the alloy's
mechanical properties. The presence of dense dislocation walls indicate that the alloy has not fully recrystallized
and, moreover, the deformation substructure and the large fraction of Σ3 twin boundaries contribute to the high
room temperature yield strength (~523 MPa). Despite the presence of substantial dislocation density, the an-
nealed, hot-rolled CHEA displays an improvement in elongation to fracture (~33%) compared to the negligible
ductility (< 2%) observed for the alloy after it has been simply annealed. The present work shows that hot-
rolling can produce good strength and ductility in the alloy while establishing the processing conditions ne-
cessary for the large-scale manufacture and utilization of its remarkable properties.
1. Introduction
High entropy alloys (HEA) consisting of five or more elements, with
more than 5 at. % each, have emerged as a promising class of structural
engineering materials. One of their unique features includes their
formation as single-phase solid solutions despite containing several
principal elements. Recently, it was discovered that the single-phase
f.c.c. HEA Fe 40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 containing 1.1 at. % carbon
(CHEA) displays a reasonable yield strength (~355 MPa) alongside an
impressive elongation to fracture (50%) and high work-hardening rate
(1895 MPa at a true strain of 20%); previous work has shown that the
addition of carbon led to a linear increase in the yield strength along-
side an increase in carbon content due to interstitial solute strength-
ening [1] while an observed increase in the work-hardening rate and
the ductility was attributed to carbon-induced microband formation
[2]. When cold-rolled to a thickness reduction of 70% and annealed at
1273 K for 1 h, an increase in room-temperature yield strength
(~557 MPa) and a reduction in ductility (27%) were observed for the
recrystallized CHEA. The increased strength was attributed to a re-
duction in grain size from 118 μm to 5 μm while the loss in ductility and
working hardening rate was due to the suppression of microband
formation by grain boundaries, carbides and B2 particles [3]. Other
methods of strengthening the CHEA have included solely annealing the
alloy at 773 K and 1073 K. An increase in hardness from 266 HV for the
as-cast material to 395 HV and 360 HV for the 773 K and 1073 K an-
nealed specimens, respectively, was observed. However, precipitation
of both grain boundary and matrix carbides of the (Mn,Cr)
23C6-type led
to brittle fracture during tensile testing [4]. Thus, while the re-
crystallized CHEA contains fewer carbides, thereby circumventing the
issue of brittle failure, exposure to elevated service temperatures may
induce additional precipitation of deleterious carbides.
Hot-rolling is a processing technique which involves deforming an
alloy above its recrystallization temperature [5]. Compared to cold-
working, hot-rolling allows higher formability of steels into sheets and
rods and is thus commonly implemented during the large-scale pro-
duction of commercial steels. Additionally, hot-rolling has been widely
used to control the grain boundary carbide morphology and distribu-
tion in commercial steels [6]. Therefore, the motivation for the present
study is to hot-roll the CHEA to achieve a more uniform distribution of
one type of carbide in a fully recrystallized material. Based on the large
and irregularly distributed M 7C3 carbides previously observed in the
cold-rolled and annealed CHEAs as well as the deleteriously high vo-
lume fraction of M 23C6 matrix and grain boundary carbide films ob-
served in the solely annealed CHEAs, the optimization of carbide dis-
tribution in order to improve mechanical performance is needed. The
alloy was hot-rolled to a single-pass thickness reduction of 50% at

introduced during the hot-rolling process and the high density of Σ3
twin boundaries formed during the partial recrystallization process.
2. Experimental
62 g ingots of Fe 40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 (at%) doped with 1.1 at%
carbon were arc-melted in a water-chilled copper crucible under an
argon atmosphere. The starting materials were 99.97% Fe, 99.90% Mn,
99.95% Ni, and 99.70% Al, and 99% Fe 3C powder. Due to its high
vapor pressure and, hence, tendency to evaporate, an additional 5 wt %
Mn was added. To ensure homogeneity, all ingots were flipped and re-
melted twice following the initial melting. The CHEA was hot-rolled to
a thickness reduction of 50% at 1323 K using a FENN rolling mill,
quenched in ice-water, and subsequently annealed at 1323 K for 24 h.
As previously reported, when the CHEA is heat-treated above 1373 K,
the alloy substantially softens due to decarburization, i.e. the hardness
decreases from the as-cast value of 260 HV to 196 HV and 139 HV after
annealing for 24 h at 1373 K and 1423 K, respectively. Therefore,
1323 K was chosen as the optimal hot-rolling and annealing tempera-
ture. For electron microscopy, disks ~3 mm in diameter and 100 μm in
thickness were mechanically milled and then electropolished using 25%
nitric acid in methanol at − 20 °C at a current of ~100 mA and voltage
of ~10 V.
Microstructures were examined in backscattered electron (BSE)
mode using a FEI XL-30 field emission gun (FEG) scanning electron
microscope (SEM) operating at 15 keV and a FEI Tecnai F20 FEG
transmission electron microscope (TEM) operating at 200 kV. The grain
size and orientation were characterized using electron backscattered
diffraction (EBSD) conducted in the aforementioned SEM system. The
EBSD data were analyzed using orientation imaging microscopy soft-
ware (OIM Analysis). Crystal structures were identified using X-ray
diffraction (XRD) measurements were performed using a Rigaku D/
MAX 2000 XRD with Cu-Kα radiation and X-ray wavelength of 1.54 Å.
The operating voltage and current were 40 keV and 300 mA, respec-
tively. A step size of 0.02° was used.
Room-temperature tensile tests were performed on dog-bone tensile
specimens with gauge length ~10 mm, width ~2.65 mm, and thickness
~1.10 mm at an initial strain rate of 5 × 10
−4 s−1 at room tempera-
ture. The dog-bone shaped specimens were polished using fine grades of
silicon carbide papers from 400 to 1200 grit. To determine elongation
to fracture, the specimens’ gauge lengths before and after testing were
measured directly from the specimens using an optical microscope.
3. Results
Fig. 1 is a backscattered electron (BSE) SEM image of the annealed,
hot-rolled CHEA. The direction of rolling is indicated on the micro-
graph. The microstructure is primarily comprised of high angle grain
boundaries (> 15°) outlined in black and twin boundaries outlined in
red as seen in the inverse pole figure (IPF) map in Fig. 2a and the
boundary misorientation map (Fig. 2b) displays a peak around 60°
which corresponds to Σ3 coincident site lattice boundaries. The re-
crystallized fraction is 78% and the average grain diameter measured
from 337 grains is 80 μm. Fig. 3 is a XRD pattern which indicates that
the hot-rolled and annealed CHEA is single-phase f.c.c. The bright field
(BF) TEM images in Fig. 4 show an absence of nanoprecipitates and
instead the presence of numerous dislocations (Fig. 4a) and dense dis-
location walls (Fig. 5b), indicating that the material has not re-
crystallized despite the long anneal (24 h) at 1323 K.
Fig. 5 shows the engineering stress-strain curves for the CHEA in the
as-cast state and after it has been subjected to various processing con-
ditions. The as-cast CHEA displays a yield strength of ~355 MPa,
elongation to fracture of 50%, and UTS of ~754 MPa. High strength, yet
negligible elongation to fracture is observed for the CHEA specimens
after they have been simply annealed at 773 K for either 13 d or 42 d, or
at 1073 K for 24 h. Additionally, a decrease in room-temperature yield
strength from ~954 MPa to ~758 MPa is observed for the CHEA after
the ageing time has increased from 13 d to 42 d at 773 K. Annealing the
alloy at 1073 K for 24 h causes a further decrease in yield strength to
~642 MPa. When the CHEA is simply annealed at 1423 K for 24 h, the
yield strength has substantially decreased to ~146 MPa while the ten-
sile ductility has increased to 33%. Finally, the CHEA after it has been
hot-rolled and annealed at 1323 K for 24 h shows a yield strength of
523 MPa, an elongation to failure of 33%, and a UTS of 765 MPa. A
summary of the mechanical properties for the as-cast and processed
CHEA specimens can be found in Table 1. The fracture surfaces of the
hot-rolled and annealed specimen in Fig. 6 shows a ductile dimple
fracture mode.
Fig. 7a and b are plots of the elongation to fracture versus the yield
strength and UTS, respectively. The values for the solely annealed
CHEAs are from a previous study [2] while those for the as-cast and
thermo-mechanically treated CHEAs have been previously reported [1].
The value for the hot-rolled specimen investigated in the present study
is highlighted in black. The hot-rolled CHEA shows a drastic improve-
ment in elongation to fracture compared to the solely annealed CHEAs,
as well as a good balance between strength and ductility.
4. Discussion
The mechanical properties of the CHEA after it has been either
simply annealed [4] or cold-rolled and annealed [3] have been pre-
viously reported. For the simply annealed specimens, the precipitation
of (Mn,Cr)
23C6 carbides at the grain boundaries and within the matrix
led to rapid age-hardening but brittle fracture during tensile testing.
Similar carbide embrittlement observed for Ni-based superalloys and
commercial steels has been alleviated through secondary processing
steps such as hot-forging and hot-rolling [7]. Factors such as the rolling
temperature and the degree of deformation contribute to the

It can be seen that the processing temperature of 1323 K has pre-
vented carbide precipitation as evidenced by the XRD pattern which
indicates a single-phase f.c.c. structure (Fig. 3). Therefore, the lack of
full recrystallization for the hot-rolled and annealed CHEA cannot be
attributed to the presence of carbides which are known to pin grain
boundaries and prevent recrystallization [14]. In contrast, Wang et al.
showed that full recrystallization is achieved after cold-rolling and
annealing the HEA and CHEA at 1073 K and 1273 K, respectively, de-
spite both alloys containing M7C3 carbides. The latter may be due to the
size of the carbides (600 nm) which are too coarse to exert a pinning
effect on the grain boundaries.
The presence of the dense dislocation walls in the hot-rolled and
annealed CHEA, as seen inFig. 4., indicate that full recrystallization has
not taken place despite primarily containing high angle grain bound-
aries (Fig. 2a, b). There are three possible reasons for this result: (1) the
cold-rolled material experienced a greater thickness reduction (70%)
compared to the hot-rolled material (50%), meaning the former con-
tained a higher dislocation density and, hence, higher driving force for
recrystallization [15], (2) recovery during the hot-rolling reduced the
dislocation density and, hence, lowered the driving force for re-
crystallization, and (3) the higher annealing temperature and duration
of 1323 K for 24 h possibly retained the carbon in solution which con-
sequently inhibited recrystallization. As previously noted, the presence
of interstitial carbon at the grain boundaries in the cold-rolled and
annealed CHEA delayed recrystallization and this phenomenon has
been observed for other systems as well [3,16]. Therefore, since 1323 K
is above the solvus temperatures for several carbides including M
23C6
(~1223 K) [17] and moreover, an absence of carbides in the hot-rolled
and annealed CHEA was observed, it is suggested that the carbon in
solution may have also inhibited recrystallization.
Despite not having fully recrystallized, the hot-rolled and annealed
CHEA exhibited good tensile ductility (~33%) while maintaining a
good yield strength (~523 MPa). Fig. 5 is a plot of the engineering
stress-strain curves for the CHEA after various processing conditions.
One can see that the simply annealed CHEA has negligible elongation to
fracture and that the yield strength gradually decreases with an in-
crease in annealing duration from 13 d to 42 d at 773 K and an increase
in temperature to 1073 K. Previous work has shown that the colonies of
M
23C6 carbides had coarsened after 42 d of annealing and that a co-
pious amount of these discrete carbides are present in the matrix of the
1073 K-annealed specimen. The fracture surface of the specimen ex-
hibits a mixture of dimple-type rupture as seen in Fig. 6a, and the
presence of microvoids is indicated by the yellow arrows in the mag-
nified image of the fracture surface in Fig. 6b. The change in fracture
mode from brittle fracture previously observed for the solely annealed
CHEA specimens [1] to a ductile dimple-type observed for the hot-
rolled CHEA in the present study demonstrates an improvement in
ductility for the hot-rolled material that is further seen in Fig. 7a and b
which are the plots of elongation to fracture versus the yield strength
and the UTS, respectively, for the CHEAs after various processing
conditions. The solely annealed CHEAs display < 2% elongation to
fracture while the hot-rolled and annealed CHEA displays ~33%

5. Conclusions
Based on the analysis of the microstructure and mechanical per-
formance for the annealed, hot-rolled CHEA, the following conclusions
can be established:
• The alloy is single-phase f.c.c. and contains a mixture of equi-axed
and elongated grains with an average grain diameter of 80 μm as
well as dense dislocation walls which indicate the alloy has not fully
recrystallized. The occurrence of recovery during hot-rolling and/or
the retention of carbon in solution due to the high annealing tem-
perature of 1323 K for 24 h may have impeded full recrystallization.
• Despite achieving only partial recrystallization, the experimental
alloy displays an excellent balance of room-temperature strength
(~523 MPa) and elongation to fracture (~33%) which may be at-
tributed to the high dislocation density introduced during hot-
rolling and the large fraction of Σ3 twin boundaries.
• Compared to the brittle fracture mode and negligible room-tem-
perature ductility observed for the solely annealed CHEAs, the
ductile dimple fracture mode and enhanced mechanical properties
observed in the present study demonstrates that hot-rolling is a
suitable processing method for mitigating carbide embrittlement in
the CHEA and moreover reflects the hot-rolling conditions necessary
for the large-scale manufacture of the alloy for future structural
applications.
