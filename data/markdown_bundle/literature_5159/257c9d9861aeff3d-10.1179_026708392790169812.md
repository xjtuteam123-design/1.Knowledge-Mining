Effect of selective oxidation of chromium on
creep strength of Alloy 617
P. J. Ennis, W. J. Quadakkers, and H. Schuster
In high temperature alloys, the selective oxidation of chromium toform a chromia scale leads to subsurface depletion of
chromium in the alloy which in turn leads to the dissolution of chromium carbide in the depleted zone. The effect of this carbide
depleted subsurface zone on the creep properties of IncoJ:lelAlloy 617 (Ni-22Cr-12Co-9Mo-1AI-O'08C; wt-%) has been
determined. The specimens were subjected to heat treatments before creep testing to simulate long term service exposure of a
thin walled heat exchanger tube operating at high temperatures. It wasfound, surprisingly, that in creep tests carried out at
900°C, specimens having extensive chromium depleted and carbidefree subsurface zones exhibit higher creep strength than
specimens thermally agedfor the same durations, but having no chromium depleted zone. As chromium was removedfrom the
matrix owing to selective oxidation, the carbon, released as the carbides in the chromium depleted zone dissolved, migrated to
the centre of the specimen, producing enhanced carbide precipitation. This led to an increase in the creep strength of the
specimen core, which offset the loss in creep strength of the subsurface zone. The expected detrimental effect of chromium
depletion was.therefore not observed. MST/1487

Introduction
The interaction between corrosion processes and mechan-
ical deformation' processes is an important consideration
for components exposed under stress at high temperatures.
The effects of corrosion on mechanical behaviour, as well
as the effects of deformation on the corrosion mechanism,
must be addressed. Several reviews of the interactions
between corrosion and mechanical properties have been
published, e.g. Refs. I, 2, and 3. The possible interactions
generally lead to a material degradation, but there are
some corrosion effects which may result in an improvement
in the mechanical properties. A fewexamples of interactions
between mechanical properties and corrosion effects are:
(i) loss of load bearing cross-section owing to corrosion
(damaging)
(ii) internal corrosion, for example, oxidation and sulph-
idation, leading to crack initiation in surface regions
(damaging) or strengthening through compressive
stresses (beneficial), depending on the morphology
of the phases precipitated
(iii) alloy deple'tion due to selective corrosion causing
structural changes in the matrix (may be damaging
or beneficial)
(iv) adherent corrosion scale formation contributing to
load bearing capability (beneficial)
(v) diffusion of corrosion products, e.g. carbon, into the
alloy matrix, increasing rupture strength (beneficial)
and decreasing ductility (damaging)
(iv) mechanical strain causing fracture of protective
scales leading to enhanced corrosive attack
(damaging).
The principal problem in the investigation and evaluation
of corrosion-creep interactions is that creep is a volume
effect, whereas corrosion is a surface effect. This means
that the magnitude of the measured interaction will depend
to a large extent on the surface area/volume ratio, more
precisely on the thickness of the corrosion affected zone in
relation to the total specimen cross-section. Furthermore,
there is the additional problem of simulating in a mechanical
properties testing machine the service atmosphere under
consideration. A generally applicable investigation of
corrosion-creep interactions is therefore not possible and
it is obvious that, if technologically relevant data are to
be determined and appropriate modelling procedures
developed, the experimental conditions, especially specimen

size and corrosive atmosphere, must be chosen to reflect
the application for which the corrosion-creep interaction
is to be investigated.
Because technical high temperature alloys rely on the
selective oxidation of one of the alloy components to form
a protective scale, there is always a zone immediately
below the scale that is depleted in the selectively oxidised
element. For many commercial, high temperature alloys,
the formation of a protective chromia scale causes loss of
chromium from the matrix, which will in itself reduce the
degree of solid solution strengthening. The depletion of
chromium leads also to a dissolution of chromium carbides
as the chromium activity decreases.4 It has been shown5•6
that grain boundary carbides play a major role in
establishing the high creep rupture strength of the alloy
Ni-22Cr-12Co-9Mo-IAI-0'08C (Inconel Alloy 617) and
that decarburised specimens or specimens taken from low
carbon heats exhibit high creep rates, short rupture
lifetimes, and extensive grain boundary damage during
creep.
The specific aim of the present research was to investigate
whether the chromium depleted (and therefore carbide
depleted) zone resulting from chromia scale formation on
a thin walled, heat exchanger tube (20 mm diameter, 2 mm
wall thickness) of Alloy 617 reduces the creep strength of
the tube wall during long term service at 900°C, as expected
from decarburisation experiments.5,6 Such tubes are typical
for heat exchangers to be used in advanced high temperature
reactor systems, such as nuclear process heat plants.7

CREEP TESTS
The creep curves determined for solution treated specimens,
for thermally aged specimens (no initial carbide free zone),
and for oxidised specimens (with initial carbide free zone)
are shown in Fig. 5. The highest creep strengths were
exhibited by the solution treated specimens. The thermally
aged specimens exhibited low creep strength and in fact
the specimen aged for 6000 h at 1000°C before creep testing
at 900°C ruptured after about 6000 h. It was surprising to
find that those specimens having extensive carbide free
zones exhibited a secondary creep rate similar to that of
the solution treated specimen, although there was a more
pronounced primary creep region. These specimens were
examined metallographically to establish the reason for
the unexpectedly high creep resistance.
METALLOGRAPHY OF CREEP SPECIMENS
During the creep tests further chromium depletion occurred
as the surface oxide was formed. In Fig. 6, the micro-
structures of two specimens without an initial carbide free
zone (one solution treated and one thermally aged) after
creep testing are shown. Because the creep tests were
carried out at a lower temperature than the oxidation
exposure before testing, the carbide free zone produced by
oxidation of chromium during the creep test was rather
narrow, even after 15 000 h. From the figure, the depth of
this zone was estimated to be 0·1 mm; this can be compared
to a carbide free zone of about 0·5 mm produced by prior
oxidation at 1000°C for 6000 h. The additional depletion
during the creep test can therefore, to a first approximation,
be neglected.

The creep strength of the oxidised specimens was
noticeably higher than that of the corresponding thermally
aged specimens. This is because the loss of strength due to
the dissolution of carbides in the chromium depleted zone
is compensated by the increase in creep strength of the
central regions of the specimen as a result of enhanced
carbide precipitation. Testing in decarburising atmospheres
leads to carbide free zones· owing to loss of carbon from
the alloy (selective oxidation of carbon), whereas exposure
in strongly oxidising atmospheres produces carbide free
zones as a result of selective oxidation of chromium. The
selective oxidation of chromium leads to a redistribution
of carbon in the specimen. No carbon is removed from
the specimen; instead, the carbon that is released as the
carbides in the chromium depleted zone dissolve, diffuses
into the central core of the specimen, producing in effect a
carburisation of the core. The higher creep strength of the

core offsets the loss in creep strength of the carbide
depleted zone.
The susceptibility of the carbide free zone to. localised
damage has been discussed previously.ll The formation of
grain boundary cracks and extensive separation of the
grain boundaries in the carbide free zone will be detrimental,
especially with regard to surface sensitive properties such
as fatigue. However, the strains at which cracking begins
are greater than those which could be tolerated in a heat
exchanger, so for technical plant operation this aspect is
not significant.
Conclusion
The results have shown that for the specific application
under consideration (a thin walled heat exchanger tube of
Alloy 617 operating at about 900°C in an oxidising
atmosphere) the selective oxidation of chromium produces
a chromium depleted, and thus carbide free, subsurface
zone, but this does not lead to a deterioration in the creep
strength properties of the tube wall as a whole. The lower
strength of the chromium depleted, carbide free zone is
more than offset by a redistribution of carbon in the matrix
to produce enhanced grain boundary carbide precipitation,
and therefore higher creep strength, in the ~ore of the
specimen.
