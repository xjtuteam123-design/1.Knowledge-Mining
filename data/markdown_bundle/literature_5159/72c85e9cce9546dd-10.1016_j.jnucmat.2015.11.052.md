abstract
Reduced-activation ferritic/martensitic steels are prime candidate materials for structural applications in
nuclear power reactors. However, their creep strength is much lower than that of creep-resistant steel
developed for conventional fossil- ﬁred power plants as alloying elements with a high neutron activation
cannot be used. To improve the creep strength and to maintain a low activation, a high-throughput
computational alloy design model coupling thermodynamics, precipitate-coarsening kinetics and an
optimization genetic algorithm, is developed. Twelve relevant alloying elements with either low or high
activation are considered simultaneously. The activity levels at 0 e10 year after the end of irradiation are
taken as optimization parameter. The creep-strength values (after exposure for 10 years at 650
/C14 C) are
estimated on the basis of the solid-solution strengthening and the precipitation hardening (taking into
account precipitate coarsening). Potential alloy compositions leading to a high austenite fraction or a
high percentage of undesirable second phase particles are rejected automatically in the optimization
cycle. The newly identi ﬁed alloys have a much higher precipitation hardening and solid-solution
strengthening at the same activity level as existing reduced-activation ferritic/martensitic steels.

1. Introduction
Reduced-activation ferritic/martensitic steels belonging to the
so-called 9Cr family are potentially suitable materials for ﬁrst wall
and blanket structures of fusion power reactors [1,2]. For nuclear
applications, the composition of these steels are to be modi ﬁed
from existing creep-resistant steels used in fossil- ﬁred power plant
applications. In addition to the harsh thermal and mechanical
conditions in fossil-ﬁred power plants, materials in critical nuclear-
power-plant applications also suffer from the effects of neutron
ﬂuxes [3], which will activate elements directly or induce radio-
activity by transmutation of alloying elements [4]. This causes a
great problem in the disposal of waste materials at the end of their
working life. To create reduced-activation ferritic/martensitic steels
which are either not activated or if activated decay in a short time,
low-activation elements are selected to replace the high-activation
elements in the conventional ferritic/martensitic steels for non-
nuclear applications. For instance, Mo is replaced by W; Nb is
replaced by Ta, and alloying elements with a very high activation
such as Cu, Ni and Co are avoided [5e9].
The lowering of the concentrations of the alloying elements
contributing to the solid-solution and precipitation hardening
however will have a strong and negative effect on the creep
strength of such steels. The common strengthening precipitates in
existing 9Cr reduced-activation ferritic/martensitic steels are
M
23C6, Laves phase and MX carbonitrides (TaCN and VCN). The
M23C6 and Laves phase precipitates have much higher coarsening
rates than the MX carbonitrides [10e14]. A high coarsening rate
causes a relatively fast and strong decrease in precipitation hard-
ening with time and is detrimental to the creep behaviour. Hence
MX carbonitrides with their low coarsening rate are the main
strengthening precipitate in reduced-activation ferritic creep-
resistant steels. Recent work on the stability of MX carbonitrides
showed that TaC has the highest stability of all MX precipitates
followed by TaN and VN [15,16]. Therefore, TaC can be a promising
candidate precipitate for nuclear materials. In contrast to precipi-
tation hardening, solid-solution strengthening in high-temperature
steels is in principle time-independent once the matrix reaches its
equilibrium composition. W is the most common solid-solution-

previous studies, we have developed a computational alloy design

by considering the activation as a new optimization parameter in
the design of new reduced-activation steel for nuclear applications
with optimal combination of precipitation hardening and solid-
solution strengthening. The optimization is done for a ﬁxed high
use temperature of 650

of new power plants. The differences in composition and properties
of the conventional and the reduced-activation steels are discussed.
2. Model description
As described in the introduction, a high creep strength can be
achieved by a uniform distribution of ﬁne TaC with an extremely
low coarsening rate in a solid-solution-strengthened martensitic
matrix. In addition, it is of great importance to have decent corro-
sion and oxidation resistances, which can be realized by ensuring a
sufﬁcient Cr content in the matrix upon the completion of the
precipitation reactions. These features can all be achieved by using
the computational alloy-design approach coupling a genetic opti-
mization framework.
The alloy-design approach is summarized as follows. First, the
genetic algorithm generates random ‘solutions’, i.e. combinations
of composition and heat treatment parameters selected from the
search space de ﬁned in Table 1 . Per element or heat treatment
temperature the search space is de ﬁned at 32 equidistant levels
between the lowest and the highest value indicated.
Then, the algorithm checks whether for this candidate solution
the desired microstructure can be obtained, using six go/no-go
criteria de ﬁned in the order of the thermal treatments to which
such steels intended for long life-high load applications are to be
subjected. The typical heat treatment for martensitic creep-
resistant steels starts with an austenisation/solution treatment,
followed by quenching and a tempering treatment to form desir-
able precipitates in the martensitic matrix. In the present study the
tempering temperature is set equal to the service temperature
(650 /C14 C) since the installations in which the steels are used have a
very long service time and thus reach the equilibrium at the service
temperature. At the (variable) austenisation temperature, ther-
modynamic calculations are performed and three go/no-go criteria
are deﬁned: (1) the equilibrium volume fraction of austenite should
be no less than 99%; (2) the amount of primary carbides should be
no more than 0.5% in volume; (3) liquid and delta phase should be
absent. To ensure that the austenite transforms completely to
martensite during quenching to room temperature, a fourth go/no-
go criterion is imposed: (4) the Martensite start ( TMs) temperature
should be higher than 250 /C14 C. After quenching, the alloy will not
experience any higher temperatures than the service/ageing tem-
perature. Two additional go/nogo criteria at this use temperature
are enforced: (5) the amount of precipitates other than TaX car-
bonitrides should be less than 1 vol%; (6) The equilibrium Cr con-
centration in the matrix should at least be 9 wt.% to assure decent
corrosion, oxidation resistance and a high toughness at low tem-
perature [21].
Solutions that meet the six go/no-go criteria will be further
compared and ranked using three optimization criteria, which are
activity, Precipitation Hardening (PH), Solid Solution Strengthening
(SSS) respectively. These optimization factors are introduced below.

3. Model application
Applying the alloy-design approach, six separate optimization
runs, each typically considering about 10 5 potential solutions to
ﬁnd optimal solutions (compositions and austenization tempera-
ture) for minimal activity after 0, 1, 5, 10y and maximal PH of TaC
and SSS, respectively. These alloys are labelled ‘alloy 0y’, ‘alloy 1y’,
‘alloy 5y ’, ‘alloy 10y’, ‘alloy TaC’, ‘alloy SSS ’ respectively, and their
composition and austenisation temperature are listed in Table 4 .
Interestingly, all the alloys have quite different compositions. The
similarity between the alloys with optimized activity at various
times is that they all do not contain the alloying elements Ni, W, Co,
Nb and Mn, which have a relatively high activity in comparison to
the other alloying elements chosen in this study. ‘Alloy TaC’ also has
a composition with few alloying elements but has a high level of Ta
to reach a high volume fraction of TaC carbides, as well as a large
amount of Co to reduce the coarsening rate of TaC. In contrast, ‘alloy
SSS’ contains all chosen alloying elements at relatively high levels
to reach a maximal SSS.
Clearly, these six designed alloys have different activities in line
with the differences in their composition. To show the difference,
the activity as a function of time after irradiation for six designed
alloys are shown in Fig. 1. Three trends can be identi ﬁed. ‘Alloy TaC’
a has very high activity because of its high Co content ( Table 4), and
the activity decreases only slowly with time. ‘Alloy 0y ’, ‘Alloy 1y ’,
‘Alloy 5y ’ and ‘Alloy 10y ’ have much lower activity which addi-
tionally decrease signi ﬁcantly with time. ‘Alloy SSS ’ shows an in-
termediate drop in activity. Although ‘Alloy 0y’, ‘Alloy 1y’, ‘Alloy 5y’
and ‘Alloy 10y ’ have quite similar values of activity, small differ-
ences exists. The different values are listed in Table 5 . It can be
noted that each of the ‘Alloy xy ’ (x ¼ 0, 1, 5 or 10) has the lowest
activation at their target optimization time and that ‘Alloy TaC’ and
‘Alloy SSS’ have the highest PH and SSS respectively. These results
demonstrate the robustness of the GA optimization model.

4. Discussion
In the results section, the designed alloys only have one desir-
able property, either low activation, or high PH or SSS, and thus
these alloy cannot be applied in the practical applications. To have a
good combination of PH and activation, the qualiﬁed solutions of all
the optimizations that satisfy all the six go/no-go criteria are
plotted as PH vs Activity after 10 y in Fig. 2 . Three regions can be
easily identiﬁed according to the activity. This separation is caused
by different contents of Ni and Co in the solutions which have very
high activity even after 10 y. The solution with highest precipitation
hardening is optimised to its PH of TaC which is reasonable.
Furthermore, this solution located in the high activity. And the top
precipitation hardening value in each region decreases with
decreasing activity. This phenomenon is probably caused by the
presence of Co. Co can decrease the coarsening rate of TaC and
leading to a high PH value after 10
5 h of exposure (Equation (2)).
Consequently, a high level of Co is needed to obtain a maximized
precipitation hardening. In the meantime, the high Co leads to an
extremely high activity because of its high activity and long half-
life. Existing creep-resistant steels for fossil-fuel power plants
also have a fair amount of Co and thus will locate in the high-
activation region. Reduced-activation steels should be in the re-
gion with the lowest activation. The signi ﬁcantly different PH
values in the low- and high-activation regions clearly indicate why
conventional creep-resistant steels have a much higher creep

In analogy to Fig. 2, Figure 3 shows the results of SSS vs activity

In both Figs. 2 and 3 , the most interesting area is the zone with
the lowest activation since the design target in this study is to reach
a best combination of high strength and low activation, while for
conventional creep-resistant steels the region is the one with high
activation. However, in both two ﬁgures, one can only ﬁnd the
solution with either best combination of PH and Activation or the

of all three properties, the lowest activation zone in Fig. 2 is chosen
and each solution is mapped with SSS factor, as shown in Fig. 4. The
colourful bar indicate the different solid-solution strengthening
values of each solutions. The recalculated values for existing
reduced activation ferritic/martensitic steels are also put as the star
dots in the plot for comparison to the newly designed solutions.
Their composition are listed in Table 6 . The existing alloys are
mapped with SSS factor and generally have a low activation and a
low SSS. Compared to existing alloys, a signi ﬁcant amount of
qualiﬁed solutions can be found with much higher PH but with a
comparable activity. Furthermore, these better solutions also have
greater SSS according to the colour (red colour stands for high SSS
and blue colour stands for low SSS). The best combinations of PH,
SSS and activation are located in the area indicated by the black
arrow in Fig. 4 . Clearly, in that area, a large number of solutions
with much higher PH and SSS than that of existing alloys yet at
similar activity can be found. Besides the present strategy to opti-
mize the combination of three properties, there is also real multi-
objective algorithm which simultaneously optimizes two or more
different properties [24,25] in which a non-dominated sorting
process has to be introduced to construct a Pareto front [26].
Nevertheless, previous study [27] by authors proves that the
approach by running various groups of dominant optimizations
leads to a global simultaneous optimization, although may not at
the best ef ﬁciency.
Five solutions with a good trade-off are chosen and listed in
Table 6. In all ﬁve alloys the contents of C, Cr and Ta are almost the
same, while differences in W, V, Si change the PH and SSS.
Furthermore, the types of alloying element in designed and existing
alloys are quite similar. All the elements with high activation are
automatically rejected by the approach. The main difference be-
tween the new and existing alloys is in the Si and W levels: the Si
level is high in designed alloy to obtain a high SSS, and the content
of W is relatively low as it will be consumed in the precipitation of
Laves Phase (Fe
2W) and thus will not act as a strong SSS element in
our model. In addition, C is relatively low to obtain a low MX
coarsening rate.
Besides reduced-activation ferritic/martensitic steels, the PH
and SSS of a typical martensitic steel P92 for fossil- ﬁred power
plant application can be found in our previous study, which is
1.65E6 and 48 respectively [18]. These values are indeed higher
than the reduced-activation steels, but P92 has a much higher and
unacceptable activity (1.24). The designed alloys listed in Table 6
are ‘on par ’ with P92 and maintain a low activation at the same
time.
5. Conclusions
A high-throughput approach has been successfully developed to
design high-temperature steels to be used at 650 /C14 C having a
suitable composition leading to a low neutron activation and a
decent precipitation and solid-solution hardening. Alloys were
optimized with one single property, either low activation, or high
PH, or high SSS with a genetic-algorithm searching framework.
Through combining all the quali ﬁed solutions of the individual
optimizations, compositions with the ideal trade off of low activa-
tion, high PH and high SSS have been obtained. According to the
prediction, the designed optimal compositions can have a much
higher strength than the existing reduced-activation ferritic/
martensitic steels while maintaining similarly low activities.
