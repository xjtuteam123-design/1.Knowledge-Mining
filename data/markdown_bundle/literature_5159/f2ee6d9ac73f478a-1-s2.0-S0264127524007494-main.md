ABSTRACT
High entropy alloys (HEAs) with single FCC structure exhibits unique structure and properties. However, lack of 
strength hinds the engineering application ability seriously. Therefore, it is urgent to propose effective methods 
and theories to enhance the strength while maintaining favorable plasticity. Introducing reinforced phases is one 
crucial research approach for improving the mechanical properties of HEAs. However, the resulting reduction in 
ductility has been neglected. In this work, we propose a novel multi-scale TiC coupling reinforced alloy which 
preserves the high plasticity while increasing the strength. The results demonstrate that TiC ( μ m) hinders the 
dislocations movement and improves strength through the second phase strengthening mechanism. Apart from 
acting as barriers to dislocation motion, the TiC (nm) also provides more dislocation sources in the distortion 
area at the junction with the matrix, which increasing the number of movable dislocations and enhancing the 
plastic strain capacity. Compared with the Al
0.4 
alloy, the tensile yield strength of the Al
0.4
-TiC ( μ m + nm) alloy 
is increased by 145 %, the ultimate tensile strength is up to 574 MPa, while maintaining a high plastic strain by 
30.1 %. The addition of multi-scale ceramic phase TiC provides a novel approach to obtain high strength and 
high plasticity HEAs.
1. Introduction
Solving the challenge of balancing alloy strength and plasticity has 
been a long-standing research focus, and high entropy alloys (HEAs) 
[1,2] also encountering difficulties. The single face-centered cubic HEA 
exhibits high plasticity but low strength, whereas the body-centered 
cubic HEA demonstrates high strength but poor plasticity. In recent 
years, various approaches have emerged to address the strength- 
plasticity balance in alloys. These include altering the phase structure 
of alloys, designing dual-phase alloys [3] , introducing HCP phases [4] , 
incorporating non-matrix alloy elements to enhance properties [5,6] , 
and modifying alloys through heat treatment or processing methods 
such as rolling or forging [7,8] . These methods for improving the 
strength of the alloy are typically based on solid solution strengthening 
mechanisms [9 – 11] , grain boundary strengthening mechanisms 
[12,13] , second phase dispersion strengthening mechanisms [14] , 
deformation strengthening mechanisms [15] , dislocation strengthening 
mechanisms [16] , and so on. However, increasing strength often leads to 
a decrease in the plasticity of the alloy, so the plastic deformation 
mechanism needs to be considered. Plastic deformation occurs when 
two parts of a crystal slide along a specific crystal plane and crystal 
phase [17] . This sliding is manifested by dislocations, stacking faults, 
dislocation slip bands, etc., accompanied by twins and phase transitions 
[18] . These theories are often inseparable when designing and studying 
the mechanism of strength and plasticity. In order to enhance the 
strength of face-centered cubic HEAs, ceramic particles can be intro -
duced as a second phase based on these aforementioned strengthening 
mechanisms. Leveraging the good plastic strain capacity of face- 
centered cubic alloys, high-strength and high-hardness ceramic parti -
cles are utilized to enhance alloy strength. For instance, Abdukadir Amar 
et al. [19] added ceramic TiC to face-centered cubic CrMnFeCoNi high- 
entropy alloys resulting in an increase in ultimate tensile strength from 
550 MPa to 723 MPa. Therefore, this method is considered feasible and 
effective. In traditional alloy materials, introducing ceramic phases al -
ters the microstructure of face-centered cubic alloys leading to cellular 
structures dominated by dendritic structures which form new subgrains

phase SiC particles also produce a new dislocation slip system around 
the interface between the SiC particles and the matrix due to the stress 
concentration. The increase of the dislocation slip system improves the 
plastic strain capacity of the alloy.
Multi-scale ceramic phase coupling reinforcement design has 
become a feasible method to balance strength and ductility in traditional 
alloys. The Al matrix composites reinforced by nano-b4c particles and 
micron-sized composite particles were prepared by He et al. [30] The 
alloy has excellent yield strength and plasticity. Farther, Fang et al. [31]
prepared three kinds of multi-scale particles (micro-sized TiC particles, 
submicron M23C6 particles and TiCxNy nanoparticles) reinforced super 
duplex stainless steels, which performed better than the ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS) and uniform elongation of the matrix alloy. Among them, 
a large number of dislocations strengthening effect at grain boundaries 
and phase boundaries caused by large-sized reinforced particles. But 
micro-sized TiC particles were easy to induce cracking between den -
drites. The in-situ generation of TiCxNy nanoparticles further increased 
the strength of the alloy while retaining the good plasticity of the alloy. 
The Orowan mechanism is enhanced when the size of reinforcement 
particles is mixed by microns and nanometers found by Cao et al. [32] . 
Al-Cu matrix composites were prepared by Jin et al. [33] with uniformly 
distributed double-sized TiC particles (micron + nano) reinforcement 
phase. The grain boundary segregation was inhibited by supercooling 
nucleation of micron-sized TiC particles, and Cu solute redistribution 
was promoted. The precipitation of dendritic θ -Al2Cu phase was limited, 
resulting in more α + θ phases with semi-coherent structure with TiC. 
Micro-interface stress damage was reduced with dislocation increment. 
The solidification rate of the alloy increased and the grain refined with 
the uniform distribution of nano-sized TiC particles in the grains. 
Compared with the matrix alloy, the strength and elongation of the dual- 
size TiC reinforced alloy increased by 51.0 % and 118 %, respectively. 
Therefore, ceramic particle reinforced alloy and nano-phase enhanced 
alloy plastic deformation ability are combined in this study. The TiC 
nano-phase, possessing the same face-centered cubic structure as the 
matrix alloy, is strategically chosen as a nucleation site for inducing 
dislocations to enhance the plastic strain capacity of the alloy. Large- 
sized micron-sized TiC particles are selected to hinder dislocation 
movement and improve the tensile strength of the alloy.
The AlCoCrFeNi HEA with good casting prospects is selected for the 
alloy matrix. Lu et al. [34] has prepared a bulk AlCoCrFeNi alloy with 
well fluidity and pouring. The alloy has broad prospects for preparing 
casting parts. Therefore, it is of practical significance to explore the alloy 
structure with high plasticity and high strength on the basis of appli -
cation. The AlCoCrFeNi alloy with face-centered cubic structure has 
high plastic strain capacity provides a good matrix choice for the 
Ceramic phase reinforcements [35] . Therefore, it is of theoretical 
background and practical application prospect to design multi-scale 
ceramic phase TiC particles to enhance the strength of the alloy while 
retaining the high plastic strain value of AlCoCrFeNi alloy.

4.4. The second phase strengthening
4.4.1. Mechanism of dislocation shearing for second phase strengthening
The yield strength of the alloy with the reinforced phase TiC is 
influenced by the second phase, in contrast to the matrix alloy. A ma -
jority of research on ceramic phase particle reinforced HEAs focuses on 
investigating the relationship between dislocations and reinforcing 
phases. It is widely accepted that dislocations bypass “ hard phase par -
ticles ” and shear through the “ soft phase ” [52] . The effectiveness of 
strengthening phases depends on their inherent shear modulus and 
coherence with the matrix alloy. Through calculation, it is determined 
that there is a modulus mismatch between the Al
0.4 
matrix alloy and TiC 
particles. According to the Orowan shear strengthening phase mecha -
nism, which includes coherent enhancement (Drcs), modulus enhance -
ment (Drms), and ordered enhancement (Dros) [53,54] , the strength of 
dislocation cut through spherical hard strengthening phases can be 
calculated. While the result of coherent enhancement stress caused by 
modulus mismatch is larger, making it theoretically impossible to shear 
the TiC ( μ m) particles. However, it was observed that the TiC ( μ m) 
particles fracture during actual tensile testing, as depicted in Fig. 7 . 
Therefore, the aforementioned mechanism is not applicable, and it was 
found that the shear effect may be related to the lath-shaped 
morphology and large aspect ratio of the TiC ( μ m) particles.
Research have revealed that the majority of second-phase reinforced 
alloys typically lack spherical particles, and there is a scarcity in 
considering the relationship between ceramic phase size, shape, and 
dislocation mechanism during the assessment of second-phase 
strengthening. For instance, aluminum and magnesium alloy re -
inforcements commonly exhibit rod-like, strip-like or plate-like geom -
etries [55] . Therefore, the influence of the size and shape of the 
reinforcing phase on the dislocation motion mechanism needs to be 
taken into consideration [56] .
Numerous ceramic phase TiC ( μ m) particles are segregated between 
dendrites, with a width ranging from 0.1 to 0.3 μ m and a length of 5 – 8 
μ m. Due to their large aspect ratio, these particles cannot be approxi -
mated as spherical reinforcing phases and can instead be considered 
lath-like reinforcing phases. The strengthening strategy of Orowan anti- 
shear spherical particles is no longer applicable in this context. The 
movement of dislocations towards the interdendritic region is impeded 
by TiC ( μ m) particles, resulting in their accumulation around TiC ( μ m). 
Dislocations exhibit two distinct trajectories: bypassing or cutting 
through TiC ( μ m) particles. However, it proves challenging for larger- 
sized TiC particles to offer sufficient areas for dislocation bypass. With 
the tensile fracture of the alloy, the TEM image in the dark field reveals a 
gradual accumulation of dislocations with increasing deformation. 
When the stress reaches a critical point, dislocations manage to over -
come the obstruction posed by ceramic phase and ultimately sever TiC 
( μ m) particles to facilitate further slip.
