ABSTRACT
The effect of a small amount of Zr addition on the temperature-dependent stability of Laves phase particles and
mechanical properties of Fe-13.5Cr-4.73Al-2.07Mo-(0.34 –0.5)Nb-(0.65–0.98)Ta-(0–0.33)Zr (wt. %) ferritic alloys
was investigated in the present study. The designed alloy ingots were hot-rolled, aged at 1073 K for 24 h, and then
re-treated at 1273 K, 1323 K, 1373 K, and 1473 K for 1 h, respectively. It was found that the Zr addition could not
only stabilize the Fe
2M Laves phase (M ¼ Mo,Nb,Ta,Zr) to a much higher temperature, but also induce the for-
mation of stable Fe 23Zr6 phase. The high-temperature (HT) microstructural stability of the alloys signi ﬁcantly was
improved, as evidenced by the fact that a certain amount (0.66 –1.19%) of precipitates (Fe 2M, Fe 23Zr6, and
core(Fe23Zr6)-shell(Fe2M)-structured particles) with an appropriate size (~1.0 μm) uniformly distributed in the
ferritic matrix even after being re-treated at 1473 K. Particularly, the formation of core-shell-structured particles
at HTs was studied from the viewpoint of both solid solubility and diffusion coef ﬁcient of M in the matrix. All
these aged alloys exhibited prominent mechanical properties at both room and elevated temperatures, showing
high yield strength with
σYS ¼ 490–560 MPa at room-temperature and σYS ¼ 80–85 MPa at 1073 K. The
strengthening effect was further discussed in light of various strengthening mechanisms, and the calculated
strength are in good agreement with the experimental results.
1. Introduction
Fe–Cr–Al-based ferritic stainless steels (FSSs) have been widely used
as engineering structural materials due to their prominent mechanical
properties and resistances to steam oxidation and corrosion, and low
dilatation at high temperatures (HTs) [ 1–3]. The fascinating neutron
irradiation resistance of Fe –Cr–Al-based FSSs renders them with great
potential to be as the accident-tolerant fuel cladding materials of nuclear
reactors for the replacement of existing Zr alloy claddings that would be
out of operation at the temperature above 1000 K [ 4–6]. The HT
microstructural stabilities and mechanical strength of Fe –Cr–Al FSSs are
primarily dependent on the precipitation of Fe2M Laves phase (hexagonal
MgZn2-type) in the body-centered-cubic (BCC) ferritic matrix [ 7–9],
which can not only pin grain boundaries to inhibit grain growth, but also
play a role in precipitation strengthening. These FSSs were based on the
Fe–Cr–Al ternary system, in which the Cr and Al contents are determined
as 13 –15 wt. % (weight percent) and 4 –5 wt. %, respectively [ 1,10].
Minor amounts of Mo and Nb additions into the ternary alloys (Fe –13-
Cr-4.5Al–2Mo–1Nb, wt. %) could signi ﬁcantly enhance the microstruc-
tural stability due to the precipitation of Fe2(Mo,Nb) Laves phase, and the
mechanical properties are also greatly improved with the yield strength
of σYS ¼ 100 MPa at 1014 K. However, when the temperature increases
above 1273 K, at which a fuel reactor coolant loss accident occurs, a large
amount of Fe 2(Mo,Nb) particles would be coarsened and dissolved into
the ferritic matrix, leading to a softening of mechanical properties [ 11].
In order to inhibit the dissolution of the second-phase particles into the
matrix, the Ta-modi ﬁed alloy (Fe-13.5Cr-4.7Al-2.1Mo-0.5Nb-1.0Ta, wt.
%) also shows a much higher microstructure stability than the

It is known that the HT mechanical strength of an alloy is closely
related to its stable microstructure. Ideally, all these alloying elements to
form precipitates should be dissolved into the ferritic matrix after the
solid-solution treatment, and then appear in the form of precipitated
particles during the subsequent aging process. It means that there exist
preferred atomic nearest-neighbors in the solutionized state, as charac-
terized by the chemical short range orders (CSROs) in solid solution al-
loys [ 12], which would induce a second-phase precipitation under an
appropriate condition. Based on CSROs, we proposed a cluster formula
approach to design alloy compositions in multi-component systems,
being expressed with the formula of [cluster](glue atoms) m (one cluster
matching with m glue atoms) [ 13,14]. Generally, the nearest-neighbor
cluster is centered around a solute atom that has strong interaction
with the solvent to represent the strong CSRO, and the atoms on the
second-nearest-neighbor act as glue atoms to ﬁll the space among clusters
for the balance of the atomic-packing density [ 13,14]. Speci ﬁcally, for

both experiments and thermodynamic computations. Finally, the me-
chanical properties of these designed alloys at both room and high
temperatures will be measured, in which the strengthening mechanisms
are further discussed.
2. Experimental
Alloy ingots with about 100 g for each were prepared by vacuum arc-
melting, in which the purities of the raw metals are 99.99 wt. % for Fe, Al,
Zr, 99.5 wt. % for Mo, Nb, Ta, and 99.9 wt. % for Cr, respectively. These
ingots were homogenized (i.e., solid-solutionized) at 1473 K for 2 h, hot-
rolled at 1073 K for several times with a total deformation of about 90%.

3.1. Microstructure characterization with different heat treatments

all these hot-rolled alloy plates exhibited a streamlined microstructure,

3.2. Mechanical properties
The engineering tensile stress-strain curves of heat-treated alloys at
both RT and HT (873 K and 1073 K) are shown in Fig. S4, from which the
yield strength σYS, tensile strength σUTS, and the elongation to fracture δ
were measured and presented in Fig. 8 . All these 1073 K-aged alloys
exhibited high strength with σYS > 490 MPa and σUTS > 630 MPa, as well
as good ductility with δ > 10% at room temperature (RT) ( Fig. 8 (a)).
Among them, the highest σYS (~560 MPa) reached at the aged F1-0.1Zr
and F2-0.2Zr alloys, which might be ascribed to the relatively smaller
particle size of precipitates in these two alloys than that in the F0 –0Zr
alloy. Moreover, the RT mechanical properties of 1473 K-retreated alloys

4. Discussion
4.1. Evolution of precipitated particles at HTs with the Zr addition
From above results of the designed alloys, a small amount of Zr
addition indeed enhances the HT microstructural stability. The thermo-
dynamic calculations show that there existed mainly Fe 2(Mo,Nb,Ta) and
(Cr,Al)2Zr phases in Zr modi ﬁed alloys. The thermal stability of Fe 2Mi s
much better than that of (Cr,Al) 2Zr, since the former could remain up to
1300 K while the latter exists only up to about 1073 K. Thus, only the
Fe2(Mo,Nb,Ta) Laves phase could be retained when aging at 1073 K for
24 h, and the α0-Cr and (Cr,Al) 2Zr would be avoided, which is in a good
consistence with the experimental results, as presented in Fig. 1 . In the
F0–0Zr alloy, there still existed a very small amount of Laves phase
particles distributed on GBs when being re-treated at 1373 K and 1473 K
for 1 h (Fig. 2(0-b, 0-c)), which agrees well with the calculated result that
the upper-limit temperature of Fe 2M is about 1355 K. While for the Zr-
containing F1 ~ F3 alloys, a relatively-high amount ( f ¼ 0.76–1.50%)
of precipitates distributed uniformly into the matrix after re-retreated at
1373 K ( Fig. 2 ), and the volume fraction could be remained at about
0.08–1.19% even after re-treated at 1473 K, which could not be veri ﬁed
by the calculated result that the Laves phase would be dissolved into the
matrix to form a single solid solution at about 1373 K ( Fig. 9). In fact, the
experimental results show that the Laves phase at HTs contains the Zr
element, which is not consistent with the thermodynamic calculation
results that the Laves phase is enriched by Mo, Nb, and Ta. More
importantly, another kind of intermetallic compound, Fe 23Zr6, with a
FCC structure also appeared in these alloys at HTs, which was also not
predicted by the thermodynamic calculation. Therefore, the phase pre-
cipitation during HT-aging process in the present experiments can pro-
vide practical data to complement the disadvantage of calculations.
Experimentally, the Fe 23Zr6 phase exhibited a high thermal stability
since the Fe23Zr6 particles could be retained even after being re-treated at
1473 K in F1-0.1Zr alloy ( Fig. 2 (1-c)). In particular, the addition of 0.2
wt. % Zr could maintain the volume fraction of precipitates (Fe 23Zr6 and
Fe2M) at a relatively-high value of f ~ 1.08% (1373 K-retreated) and f ~

4.2. Mechanisms of microstructural evolution at HTs
From the above-mentioned, the addition of Zr can form a FCC Fe 23Zr6
phase and core (Fe 23Zr6)-shell (Fe 2M)-structured particles, besides the
Laves Fe2Mp h a s e .Fig. 9 (e) generalizes the microstructural evolution of
Zr-containing alloys with different heat treatments. The solid-solution plus
aging treatments could make the Fe 2M Laves phase particles precipitated
from the BCC α-Fe matrix ( Fig. 9 (e/C0 I, e /C0 II)), in which the M in Fe 2M
represents the combination of Mo, Nb, Ta, and Zr elements. When the aged
alloys were re-treated at 1323 K for 1 h, another kind of Fe 23Zr6 phase
would synergistically precipitate with the Fe 2M( Fig. 9 (e/C0 III)), which is
closely related to both the diffusion coefﬁcient of M and the solid solubility
of M in the matrix at HTs [20–25]. At 1323 K, both the diffusion coefﬁcient
(1.03 /C2 10/C0 13 m2/s [20]) and the solubility (9.3 at. %) of Mo in the BCC
α-Fe matrix are large (Fig. 10(a)), which leads to the dissolution of Mo into
the matrix, as a result of the domination of Nb, Ta, and Zr in Fe 2M. In
contrast, the extremely-low diffusion coefﬁcient (3.72 /C2 10/C0 16 m2/s [24])
and the solubility (~0.3 at. %) of Zr in α-Fe would inevitably result in a
segregation of Zr element in Fe 2M phase with the time prolonging, since
the diffusion coef ﬁcient (1.82–4.44 /C2 10/C0 14 m2/s [ 21–23]) and the sol-
ubility (0.6–0.75 at. %) of Nb and Ta are relatively larger, which is also
identiﬁed by the composition variation of Fe 2M particles at different heat
treatments (Fig. 10(b)). Thus, the segregation of Zr would induce a phase
transformation of Fe 2M þ Ζr → Fe23Zr6 to form a new Fe 23Zr6 phase,
which certainly produces the core-shell-structured particles, besides ﬁne
Fe2Ma n dF e23Zr6 particles [26,27]. Further increasing the temperature to
1473 K, the Fe 2M phase is continuously consumed and the Fe 23Zr6 be-
comes dominant during the synergetic precipitation ( Fig. 9 (e/C0 Ⅳ)). Oc-
casionally, the serious segregation of Zr could form another kind of
core-shell-structured particles with a Zr-rich phase in the core and
Fe23Zr6 phase in the shell ( Fig. 3(d)). Moreover, the single Fe 23Zr6 phase
formed by the accumulation of Zr element in a certain period, while the
growth of Fe23Zr6 particles was slow. And the size of Fe23Zr6 particles was
relatively small due to the low diffusion coef ﬁcient of Zr element. The
presence of core-shell-structured particles could delay the re-dissolution of
Fe2M particles to a higher temperature with the aid of the stable Fe 23Zr6,
resulting in the enhancement of the microstructural stability. In fact, this
kind of core-shell-structured particles have also been found in some other
alloy systems, such as the L1 2-Al3(Sc,Er,Zr) core-shell structured nano-
precipitates in Al-Sc-Zr-Er alloys [28,29] and compact γ'/γ'' co-precipitates
with a core (L1 2 γ0-Ni3Al) - shell (tetragonal γ''-Ni3Nb) structure in In718
Ni-base superalloy (Ni –19Cr–19Fe-3.0Mo-4.4Nb-1.1Al-1.0Ti wt.%),
which all signiﬁcantly improve the microstructural stability [30,31].
In addition to the formation of core-shell structure particles, the Fe 2M
Laves phase particles will undergo the sequence of coarsening and
dissolution during the high temperature retreatment. Fig. 10 (b) com-
pares the amount of Mo, Nb, Ta, and Zr in Fe 2M particles of F0 –0Zr and
F2-0.2Zr alloys in different heat-treated states, which is expressed with
the relative ratio of Mo, Nb, Ta, and Zr on the premise of the total amount
of these elements being set as 1.0. For the F0 –0Zr alloy, the Fe 2M Laves
phase particles in the 1073 K-aged state were mainly enriched by Mo, Nb,
and Ta elements with an approximately equimolar ratio (Mo:Nb:Ta ¼
0.34:0.31:0.35), however, the proportion of Mo element decreased
gradually with the temperature increasing, as evidenced by the value of
Mo:Nb:Ta ¼ 0.23:0.35:0.42 in 1323 K-retreated state. This ratio of Mo
would be close to zero ( <0.08) after being re-treated at above 1373 K,
and the Nb and Ta were dominant in Laves phase with a near-equimolar
ratio. For the F2-0.2Zr alloy, the Laves phase was still mainly enriched by
Mo, Nb, and Ta with an extremely-small fraction of Zr in the 1073 K-aged
state, similar to that in aged F0 –0Zr alloy. The fraction of Zr increased
rapidly in the 1323 K-retreated state with a ratio of Mo/Nb/Ta/Zr ¼
0.13/0.25/0.27/0.35, in which the fraction of Mo decreased signi ﬁ-
cantly. Then it would dominate in the Laves phase when the re-treated
temperature was above 1373 K, as evidenced by the ratio of Mo/Nb/
Ta/Zr ¼ 0.07/0.15/0.16/0.62 in the 1473 K-retreated state. Therefore, at
a much higher temperature, Zr, Ta, and Nb would be enriched in the
Laves phase particles, especially the Zr element, while the Mo element
would be dissolved into the ferritic matrix. Actually, this variation ten-
dency is also closely related to the large difference between Mo and Zr in
diffusion coef ﬁcient and solubility, which would inevitably lead to the
dissolution of Mo in the matrix and the enrichment of Zr in the Laves
phase particles.
From the above results, the multi-component Laves phase
Fe2(Mo,Nb,Ta,Zr) (f ~ 0.66% for F2-0.2Zr alloy at 1473 K/1 h) is more
stable than simple Fe 2Mo, Fe 2Nb, and Fe 2Zr Laves phase ( f < 0.05% at
1473 K/1 h), which might bene ﬁt from the high entropy effect caused by
multiple alloying elements M in Laves phase. This effect has been widely
reported in recently-developed high-entropy alloys [ 32–34],
high-entropy intermetallic compounds [ 35,36], and high-entropy ce-
ramics [37–39]. For example, the multi-component intermetallic L12-(Ni,
Co,Fe)3(Ti,Al) nanoparticles were introduced into the Fe –Ni–Co-based
matrix for the enhancement of the tensile strength up to 1500 MPa
without sacriﬁcing the ductility (with a larger elongation of about 50%)
[35]. The high-entropy (Hf 0.2Zr0.2Ta0.2Nb0.2Ti0.2)C carbide ceramics
exhibits signi ﬁcantly lower thermal and electrical conductivities than
any single carbide ceramics of HfC, ZrC, TaC, and TiC [ 39].
