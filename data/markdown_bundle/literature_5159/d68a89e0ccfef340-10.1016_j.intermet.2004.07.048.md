The strengthening effect of (Ni,Fe)Al precipitates on the mechanical
properties at high temperatures of ferritic Fe–Al–Ni–Cr alloys
C. Stallybrass*, A. Schneider, G. Sauthoff
Max-Planck-Institut fu¨r Eisenforschung GmbH., D-40074 Du¨sseldorf, Germany
Received 1 June 2004; received in revised form 1 July 2004; accepted 9 July 2004
Available online 9 April 2005
Abstract
Strengthening through a homogeneous distribution of a second phase is a concept that is widely employed in high-temperature materials.
The most prominent among this group are nickel-based superalloys which owe their high-temperature strength to ﬁnely dispersed Ni 3Al
particles. Similar microstructures can be obtained in the Fe–Al–Ni–Cr system with B2-ordered (Ni,Fe)Al precipitates in a ferritic matrix.
These precipitates lead to an increase of high-temperature strength compared to conventional iron-base high-temperature alloys. However,
secondary precipitates form during air cooling from high temperatures and affect the ductility. The results show that the ductility can be
improved by a two-step aging treatment. Within the stress and temperature range investigated, the dependence of the secondary creep rate on
the applied stress of aged alloys can be described by a power law if a threshold stress is introduced.

1. Introduction
Ferritic high-temperature materials are an interesting
alternative to austenitic steels because of their lower thermal
expansion, higher thermal conductivity and lower material
costs. However, conventional ferritic alloys—in particular
steels—do not possess sufﬁcient creep resistance for
structural applications above 600 8C [1]. Considerable effort
has therefore been made to increase the high-temperature
strength of ferritic materials, in order to achieve higher
operational temperatures. There is a miscibility gap in the
Fe–Ni–Al system, which makes precipitate hardening with
the B2-ordered (Ni,Fe)Al-phase possible [2,3]. Fig. 1 is a
pseudo-binary section through the system along the
compositions Fe
0.975Al0.025–Ni0.4875Al0.5125.
The lattice parameters of a-Fe (0.28665 nm) and NiAl
(0.28864 nm) are very similar [4], which means that NiAl
can precipitate coherently within a-Fe or vice versa. By
appropriate selection of composition and heat treatment,
microstructures can be achieved that are similar to the g–g0
microstructures of nickel-base superalloys. These micro-
structures result in a high coercivity which led to the use of
Fe–Ni–Al alloys as materials for permanent magnets
(Alnico alloys) [5,6]. However, these alloys could also be
interesting as structural materials for high-temperature
applications as an alternative to austenitic steels or wrought
nickel-base superalloys, and several studies were carried out
with the aim to explore the evolution of the microstructure
[7,8] and mechanical properties [9,10]. Strengthening steels
with NiAl precipitates was investigated by Pickering [11]
and has led to the development of alloys such as 17–7 PH or
PH 13-8 Mo, which contain low volume fractions of NiAl
[12,13]. However, their application is limited to moderate
temperatures. It follows from Fig. 1 that the solvus
temperature of the (Ni,Fe)Al-phase decreases with decreas-
ing volume fraction. This sets a limit for the application
temperature of these alloys. Another limiting factor is the
coarsening of precipitates at high temperatures, which
increases the interparticle spacing, thereby reducing the
strengthening effect. The present work aims at combining
the excellent oxidation resistance of ferritic Fe–Cr–Al alloys
with the high-temperature strength of Fe–Ni–Al alloys with
a high volume fraction of the B2-(Ni,Fe)Al phase. Cr was
found to distribute mostly in the ferritic phase [5], where it

2. Experimental
Three Fe–Al–Ni–Cr model alloys with compositions
based on the pseudo-binary section Fe
0.975 Al0.025 –
Ni0.4875Al0.5125 of the Fe–Ni–Al system were produced
with the aim to obtain two-phase materials consisting of a
ferritic matrix with (Ni,Fe)Al precipitates. 10 wt% of Cr
was added and substituted for iron and nickel. The nominal
chemical compositions of the alloys are listed in Table 1.
The alloys were vacuum induction melted and cast into a
copper mould with a diameter of 25 mm. The alloys were
designated according to the volume fraction of precipitates
after heat treatment at 900 8C, i.e. high (37 vol.%), medium
(24 vol.%) and low (9 vol.%) volume fraction [15].
These were subsequently cut into cylinders with a length
of 4 cm and solution treated at 1200 8C for 24 h (S) in order
to ensure a homogeneous distribution of the alloying
elements and avoid segregation effects. The cylinders
were then aged either 48 h at 900 8C( SCA1) or 300 h at
750 8C( SCA2) only, or 48 h at 9008C followed by 300 h at
750 8C( SCA1CA2). The heat treatments were carried out
in air and were followed by air cooling. From these
cylinders, samples for four-point bending tests of 18 !6!
3 mm size, for creep tests of 5 !5!10 mm size and for
transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were cut by
electro-discharge-machining. The four-point bending tests
were performed at a strain-rate in the outer ﬁbre of 10
K4 sK1.
The maximum strain in the outer ﬁbre is limited to about 4%
due to the experimental setup. The samples were optically
checked for cracks after the experiment. The alloys were
considered ductile at a given temperature if the samples
achieved more than 3% plastic strain in the outer ﬁbre and
were free of cracks. The creep tests were performed in
compression with stepwise loading, in order to investigate the
stress dependence of the secondary creep rate. The load was
kept constant during each step until a constant creep rate was
reached. The creep tests were carried out at 600, 700 and
750 8C. The TEM samples were taken perpendicular to the
axis of the ingots. The TEM used was a Philips CM 20 which
was operated at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The images
images were taken in dark ﬁeld using the {100} superlattice
reﬂections from the B2-phase.
3. Results and discussion

Optical inspection of the bent samples revealed cracks in
LVF up to 700 8C and in MVF up to 750 8C. Alloy HVF did
not reach more than 1.5% even at temperatures as high as

In the solution-treated and air-cooled condition, the
microstructure consists of a homogeneous distribution of
(Ni,Fe)Al precipitates with a size of around 50 nm in a
ferritic matrix. No inhomogeneities were observed by light-
optical or scanning electron microscopy. TEM investigation
of LVF (S) revealed additional precipitates below 10 nm
size within the matrix and within the larger precipitates (see
Fig. 3 ). The larger precipitates are believed to form at a
temperature close to the solvus temperature. As cooling
proceeds, the solubility of the ferritic phase for Ni and Al
and the solubility of the (Ni,Fe)Al-phase for Fe decreases
and additional precipitates form.
Samples that were ﬁrst solution treated at 1200 8C and
then heat treated at 900 8C followed by air cooling also
showed these two types of precipitates, apart from large
equilibrium precipitates which were formed by coarsening,
as shown in Fig. 4 . The coarsening of (Ni,Fe)Al-
precipitates was investigated in previous studies [7,8,10,
15]. Very ﬁne secondary or cooling precipitates were found
in the denuded zone around the equilibrium precipitates but
also between the 50 nm secondary precipitates. There were
also secondary precipitates present after the two-step aging
treatment. However, their volume fraction, which is
determined by the composition of the matrix at 750 8C
and not by the overall composition, is considerably lower
compared to the solution treated alloy. Large precipitates
with a mean precipitate size of around 400 nm were found
and the small precipitates were below 10 nm, as shown in
Fig. 5. However, the 50 nm precipitates found in the matrix
after air cooling from 1200 and 900 8C were not observed.
Cooling precipitates are also found in g
0-hardened Ni-
base superalloys, where they have a strong inﬂuence on
the mechnical behaviour at low temperatures [16]. The
yield stress of LVF, MVF and HVF was tested as a
function of heat treatment condition and temperature in a
previous study [15]. At low temperatures, the solution
treated alloys showed higher yield stresses compared to
alloys that were aged at 750 8C. Since the precipitate size
and the interparticle spacing is on such a small scale in the
solution treated condition, dislocation motion through
the matrix is heavily restricted. The reduction in the
brittle-to-ductile transition temperature after aging is
believed to be caused by the increase in interparticle
spacing between the larger precipitates compared to the
solution treated condition. That cooling precipitates with a
small interparticle spacing were again observed after the
750 8C heat treatment explains why the transition tem-
peratures are still relatively high.

3.2. Creep tests
Fig. 6 shows the dependence of the secondary creep rate
at 600 8C on the true stress for alloys that were aged for
300 h at 750 8C. During this heat treatment, the (Ni,Fe)Al
precipitates coarsen to such an extent that they can be
considered stable during the creep test. The coarsening
constant was found to be around 3 !10
K5 mm3/h at 750 8C
[15]. Therefore, the preaged equilibrium precipitates reach a
size of around 200 nm. The additional precipitates formed
during air cooling, however, may coarsen during testing.
The secondary creep rates of the alloys with a low and
medium volume fraction were in the same order of
magnitude within the investigated stress range. At higher
stresses, the material with the medium volume fraction
showed lower secondary creep rates. The situation was
different for the material with a high precipitate volume
fraction. In this case, far higher stresses were necessary in
order to achieve similar secondary creep rates. The stress
necessary to obtain a secondary creep rate of 10
K7 sK1 was
around 159 MPa in the case of LVF, 176 MPa for MVF and
270 MPa for HVF.
The results of the creep tests at 700 8C after the two step
aging treatment showed similar behaviour. As in the case of
the tests at 600 8C, the idea behind the heat treatment was to
maintain a relatively stable precipitate size during the
experiment. The stress necessary to obtain a secondary
creep rate of 10
K7 sK1 was around 67 MPa in the case of
LVF, 71 MPa for MVF and 110 MPa for HVF, i.e. there was
again a gap between LVF and MVF on the one hand and
HVF on the other hand.

4. Conclusions
The mechanical behaviour of (Ni,Fe)Al-strengthened
ferritic Fe-Al-Ni-Cr alloys was investigated with the focus
on ductility between room temperature and 1000 8C and
creep properties between 600 and 750 8C. The results of the
four-point bending tests show that heat treatment has a
strong inﬂuence on the brittle-to-ductile transition tempera-
ture. In the solution treated condition, the interparticle
spacing is smallest and the investigated alloys showed the
highest brittle-to-ductile transition temperatures. After two-
step aging, the transition temperature was reduced signiﬁ-
cantly. This is caused by an increase in interparticle spacing.
The presence of cooling precipitates after two-step aging is
believed to be the reason why the transition temperatures
remain relatively high, i.e. between 400 and 500 8C. A
further reduction in transition temperature can be expected
if the temperature of the second aging step is reduced, either
because the volume fraction of cooling precipitate will be
smaller or because precipitation is suppressed due to slower
diffusion.
The creep tests show that the stress dependence of the
secondary creep rate can be described by a power law with a
threshold stress within the temperature and stress range
investigated. The results of the creep tests at 600 8C show
that the alloys compare favourably with the alloy investi-
gated by Zhu et al. [17] which contains a lower volume
fraction of NiAl precipitates.
