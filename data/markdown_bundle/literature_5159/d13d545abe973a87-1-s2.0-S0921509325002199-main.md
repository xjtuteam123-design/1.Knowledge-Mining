1. Introduction
Refractory high-entropy alloys, comprising refractory elements such 
as Ti, V, Cr, Zr, Nb, Mo, Hf, Ta, and W, are recognized as promising 
materials for high-temperature structural applications due to their 
exceptional thermal stability and mechanical properties [ 1 ]. These 
properties make RHEAs among the most promising candidates for 
extreme environments, including turbines, leading edges of 
ultra-high-speed vehicles, and aero-engine shafts [ 2 ]. RHEAs can be 
classified based on their chemical composition [ 3 ]. Single-phase high- -
entropy alloys generally consist of either face-centered cubic (FCC) or 
body-centered cubic (BCC) phases. Multi-phase high-entropy alloys 
have also been proposed [ 4 ], with matrices primarily based on FCC or 
BCC phases, often coexisting with other secondary phases [ 5 ]. Notably, 
BCC-based HEAs exhibit limited deformability at room temperature.

The stress-strain curve can be divided into three stages. Fig. 12 il -
lustrates the division of the high-temperature compressive stress-strain 
curve of the alloy at 1073 K and the corresponding schematic diagram 
of microstructure transformation. The first stage is the stress growth 
phase [ 33 ]. As deformation increases, the stress rapidly rises to its 
maximum value. The rapid increase in pressure leaves insufficient time 
for dislocations to slip, causing a rapid increase in dislocation density. 
An increase in dislocation density leads to a stronger interaction force 
between dislocations, resulting in phenomena such as dislocation 
crossing, climb, and pile-up [ 41 ]. The movement of dislocations is 
further hindered, and higher stress levels cause additional plastic 
deformation. As a result, the compressive stress of the high-entropy alloy 
increases significantly.
The second stage corresponds to the stress reduction phase, charac -
terized by dynamic softening [ 10 ]. Notably, significant dynamic soft -
ening is observed only in Ti2.0 and Ti2.5, while the other two alloys do 
not exhibit this behavior. This is attributed to the abundant second 
phase in Ti2.0 and Ti2.5, which facilitates dislocation accumulation and 
nucleation at particle interfaces, promoting dynamic recrystallization 
and reducing flow stress [ 54 ]. Additionally, the second phase promotes 
dislocation rearrangement and annihilation through pinning, triggering 
dynamic recovery and reducing internal stress [ 41 ]. In summary, the 
second phase significantly reduces both flow and internal stresses, 
leading to dominant dynamic softening effects in Ti2.0 and Ti2.5 during 
high-temperature compression.
The third stage is the stress growth phase, also known as the work 
hardening stage. The large number of second phases in Ti2.0 provide 
nucleation sites for dynamic recrystallization, increasing dislocation 
density and leading to dislocation accumulation and entanglement. This 
increases internal stress, hinders dislocation movement, and results in 
dynamic hardening after dynamic softening [ 55 ].
