Random body-centered-cubic (BCC) “High Entropy” alloys are a new class
of alloys, some having high strength and good ductility at room temperature
and some having exceptional high-temperature strength. There are no theo-
ries of strengthening of screw dislocations in BCC metals that span naturally
from the dilute limit to the multi-component, non-dilute concentrations typical
of the high-entropy domain. Here, such a theory is developed and validated.
Unlike low-temperature elemental BCC metals and very dilute BCC alloys,
strength is not controlled by the kink-pair nucleation mechanism. Rather,
screw dislocations naturally adopt a kinked structure as the minimum total
energy conﬁguration in the ﬁeld of random alloying atoms. The characteristic
length and energy scales for the low-energy kinked screw dislocation are de-
rived for random alloys, leading to a characteristic spacing of both kinks and
cross-kinks that depends on the kink formation energy and a characteristic
collective solute/screw dislocation interaction energy parameter. Glide motion
of this initially-kinked screw dislocation occurs via Peierls-type motion, lat-
eral kink glide, and failure of cross-kinks. All these features are observed in
molecular dynamics simulations. The resulting strength versus temperature,
strain rate, and composition is analytic. The theory is validated by compar-
ison to experiments on non-dilute Fe-Si, Nb-Mo, Nb-W, and Ti-Nb-Zr-based
high entropy alloys versus composition and temperature. The theory provides
a framework for tailored design of new high-performance BCC alloys.

1 Introduction
High Entropy Alloys (HEAs) are alloys with many elemental components all at non-dilute
concentrations but retaining a single-phase crystal structure [1, 2]. The HEAs are further thought
to be predominantly random alloys, in which the probability of occupation of a given lattice
site by a typen solute is equal to the solute concentrationcn in the alloy. Many HEAs in face
centered cubic (FCC) and body centered cubic (BCC) structures have now been fabricated and
tested. Material engineering has further extended HEA metallurgy to include multi-phase alloys
and alloys that undergo phase transformations under load [3–5]. Of interest here are single-phase
BCC high entropy alloys, such as MoNbTaVW and TiZrHfNbTa, that exhibit high strengths
(∼1.0-1.5 GPa) at room temperature and retain high strength at intermediate temperatures [6–9].
The MoNbTaVW alloy further retains high strength at very high temperatures of 1900K. Even
within one alloy class, e.g. Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb-Ta, the possible composition space is immense and only
a few compositions have been studied to date.
Understanding and predicting the mechanisms of strengthening in these alloys is thus highly
desirable to guide selection of alloys in a given family. The origins of the differences in
temperature dependence among different HEA classes has also not been established [2]. More
generally, there are no mechanistically-based theories of plastic ﬂow in BCC HEAs, and so the
choices of new alloys have been mainly limited to equicomposition alloys or equicomposition
alloys doped with small additions of other elements. The development of a predictive theory for
the motion of screw dislocations through a random BCC alloy of any composition above the very
dilute case (binary alloys with solute concentration less than a few percent) is thus a valuable
goal, and is the subject of this paper.
The low temperature strengthening of BCC metals and dilute alloys is traditionally understood
in terms of the motion of screw dislocations, which glide by a process of kink-pair nucleation
and propagation [10, 11] that is clearly consistent with TEM observations of long straight screw
dislocations [12–15]. In dilute alloys, low temperature plasticity is understood to be controlled
by kink nucleation, which can be enhanced or inhibited by solutes. Intermediate-temperature
plasticity is controlled by kink glide. In addition, a screw dislocation can nucleate double kinks
on two possible glide planes. The intersections of kinks on different glide planes gives rise to
cross-kinks (also called jogs or dipoles) that serve as strong pinning points. The breaking of
cross-kinks requires the creation of high-energy-cost point defects, which thus create high energy
barriers to ﬂow. The temperature-sensitivity of the ﬂow stress thus decreases and the ﬂow appears
increasingly “athermal”, relative to the more-rapid decrease from low to moderate temperatures.
At some high temperature, the strength of many HEAs often decreases quite abruptly.
Suzuki [16] developed a model for BCC strengthening in dilute binary alloys that accounts for
the effects of solutes on kink nucleation, kink glide, and cross-kink formation. The Suzuki model

has been applied successfully to several different dilute alloys ( <10% solute concentration)
using one solute/dislocation interaction energy parameter that is estimated using classical models
[17, 18]. New work by Rao et al. [ 19] treats high entropy alloys within the Suzuki model by
considering each alloying element separately, and then using ad-hoc strength combination rules
to make predictions for the true multicomponent alloy. While not unreasonable, and with good
results, this is not a derived theory. Moreover, there seem to be a number of issues and limitations
of the Suzuki model; we will discuss these later and compare with the new model here. Thus,
while the Suzuki model captures some mechanistic features of BCC alloys, new insights and
models are needed and especially for HEAs.
Here we present a new predictive model for the motion of screw dislocations in random
multicomponent BCC alloys. We outline the overall theory now, to set the stage for detailed
analyses presented in subsequent sections of this paper. As shown schematically in Figure 1,
under zero applied load the statistical ﬂuctuations in the local solute conﬁgurations around a screw
dislocation core cause an initially straight dislocation to lower its total energy by spontaneously
forming a kinked structure. The driving force for the kinking is the fact that certain ﬂuctuations
in the spatial solute conﬁguration around a dislocation are energetically favorable and other
ﬂuctuations are energetically unfavorable. The screw dislocation thus lowers its energy by
residing predominantly in the favorable regions. This comes at the cost of forming kinks, and
so the lowest-energy state is achieved at a characteristic screw segment lengthζc that balances
the competition between lowering the energy by moving to favorable solute ﬂuctuations and
increasing the energy due to kink formation. Plastic ﬂow then occurs by stress-assisted thermal
activation of the dislocation segments out of the low energy state and over the barriers presented
by adjacent regions of unfavorable solute ﬂuctuations. This motion can occur either by lateral
kink glide or by glide of the entire segment over the adjacent barrier. Furthermore, due to the local
solute ﬂuctuations, screw segments can also kink into the available cross-slip plane to lower the
energy even further. Kink glide then creates cross-kinks at a characteristic scale that is a multiple
ofζc. Failure of the cross-kinks requires formation of vacancies and self-interstitials, which
provides an added strengthening with high activation barrier and thus low temperature sensitivity.
All of these processes are controlled by only a few material properties. The most important
quantity is a collective solute/screw dislocation interaction energy that will be derived below.
The other important material quantities are the kink formation energy, the Peierls barrier of an
equivalent homogeneous alloy, and the average vacancy and self-interstitial formation energies
in the alloy. The analysis is applicable to alloys having an arbitrary number of elements and
concentrations, except in the limits of very low interaction energies or very low concentrations
where formation of the initial low-energy kinked structure may be kinetically-limited.

2 Theory of screw dislocation motion in BCC alloys
2.1 Solute/screw dislocation interaction energies
The basic framework for the present analysis has some commonality with recent work on
strengthening by edge dislocations in FCC alloys [ 23] and on BCC alloys [ 24]. Similarities
are (i) the usage of a conceptual homogeneous alloy [ 22], (ii) the existence of a statistical
solute/dislocation energy parameter computed in analogy with [23], and (iii) a resulting kinked
dislocation (wavy in the FCC and BCC edge cases). However, the major features particular to
the motion of BCC screw dislocations are then signiﬁcantly different. In particular, the three
strengthening mechanisms identiﬁed here are speciﬁc to the case of BCC screw dislocations.
Proceeding with the theoretical framework, we consider a random alloy consisting of N
solute types, each at concentration cn with∑N
n=1cn = 1. By random, we mean that there is
no correlation between the types of solutes on different distinct lattice sites. The probability
that any speciﬁc lattice site is occupied by a type n solute is thencn. For a true random alloy,
there is a corresponding conceptual homogeneous alloy that is composed of a single average
atom [22]. The homogeneous alloy has all of the macroscopic properties of the true random
alloy (e.g. lattice constant a0, elastic constantsCij, unstable stacking fault energy Γusf, screw
kink energyEk, and screw kink widthwk). In addition, relevant to our theory here, the average
alloy has vacancy and self-interstitial formation energies, Ev andEsi, respectively. Finally, a
screw dislocation in the average alloy has an associated Peierls potentialVP and Peierls stressτP
that encode the chemical changes inﬂuencing the average motion of a straight screw dislocation.
While the average alloy can be constructed explicitly using EAM-type interatomic potentials, the
important point here is the general conceptual existence of such a homogeneous or average alloy.
The individual elemental atoms are then considered as solutes residing within this average
alloy matrix. Each solute thus has typical solute properties such as a heat of solution, a misﬁt
volume, and vacancy-mediated migration barrier [22]. The solutes can also interact with defects
in the average matrix. Of particular importance here is the interaction energy between a solute

The formation of the kinked structure may be kinetically inhibited if the driving force is too
small. That is, if the solute/screw interaction energy parameter is too small, either because of low
solute concentration or weak solute/screw interactions, the formation of the kinked structure may
require thermal activation over barriers comparable to the double-kink barriers. Also, in the same
limit, if the predicted characteristic lengthζc is much longer than the dislocation junction spacing,
the kinked structure may not be relevant. So, although the kinked structure is the low energy
structure, the model may not be directly applicable in this limit. A speciﬁc example might be
the Nb-Ta system because Nb and Ta are chemically quite similar, so that the interaction energy
parameter may be small; this is consistent with experimental observations [ 30] that the yield
strengths of Nb-Ta alloys do not show any signiﬁcant increase over the strengths of elemental
Nb and Ta.
In summary, a screw dislocation in a random alloy at zero stress will spontaneously form
a low-energy in-plane kinked structure with characteristic length ζc and amplitude a. Kinks
are thus intrinsically present along the screw dislocation. This differs fundamentally from the
situation prevailing in an elemental BCC metal, and differs from all previous assumptions in
the modeling of BCC alloys. The length scale ζc and energy scales ∆ ˜Ep,Ek, and ∆Eb play
critical roles in the subsequent glide motion of the screw dislocation under applied loads and
ﬁnite temperatures, as described in Sections 3 and 4.
2.3 Length and energy scales associated with cross-kink formation
The above analysis only considered kinks in a single{110} glide plane of the screw dislocation.
In reality, at T=0K, the dislocation can kink on any of the three{110} planes in the⟨111⟩-zone
aligned with the Burgers vector. Kinking on multiple planes is possible because the random
distribution of solutes around the dislocation core may enable a lower energy to be obtained by
kinking into that direction where the solutes are most favorable for lowering the energy. Kinking
on different planes then naturally gives rise to cross-kinks (jogs, or “dipoles” [16, 17]).
Failure of cross-kinks leads to debris in the form of closed prismatic loops, vacancies,
and interstitials, as observed in multiple TEM studies on dilute and highly concentrated alloys
[31, 32], as shown by kinetic Monte Carlo simulations [33], and in recent simulations [19]. Figure
4 shows the atomistic process that leads eventually to the formation of self-interstitial/vacancy
prismatic loops. These are high energy defects, and so create high energy barriers that are only
defeated at high temperatures.
Cross-kinks have a very high energy ∆Exk since they consist of two individual kinks on
different planes plus costly elastic interactions [34]. Thus, the spontaneous formation of cross-
kinks is energetically highly unfavorable. A segment is generally unable to lower its potential
energy due to solutes sufﬁciently to pay the larger cost of directly forming a cross-kink. The
12

cross-kinks then arises due to lowering of the total energy by kinking, but with the constraint
that the kinking process on different planes does not form any initial cross-kinks.

To determine the fundamental spacing of cross-kinks in the random alloy we ﬁrst note that,
in a random solute environment, the statistical distribution of possible potential energy changes

driving stress to move the dislocation forward, the “backward” kinks are irrelevant to cross-kink

formation because they just glide forward to the original plane. In addition, to preserve the
average screw dislocation character, and thus avoid very complex 3D dislocation geometries that
are also not observed experimentally, it is necessary that the “forward” kinks on different planes
are isolated from one another by unkinked segments.
The in-plane model for energy minimization gave rise to a characteristic kink spacing of
2.5ζc. The fundamental units for understanding cross-kink formation are then blocks of length
5ζc containing two kinks on the same{110} plane. Each block can exist on any of the{110}
planes with equal probability, since the potential energies due to solutes of screw segments on
each plane are drawn from the same statistical distribution. These individual blocks on different
planes can be pieced together to form a long dislocation line. Two neighboring blocks then have
a probability of 1/3 to have formed on the same{110} plane and a probability of 2/3 to have
formed on two different planes. The latter cases can then form cross-kinks by glide of the kinks
in each block. The average spacing of the cross-kinks is thusζxk = 5ζc/(2/3) = 7.5ζc.
The above discussion applies for applied stresses resolved on any slip plane (including
{110}) except for the case when the shear is resolved near{112} slip. In that case, two{110}
slip planes will be far more favorable than the third. Thus, the probability that the neighboring
blocks lie on cross-slipped planes is 1/2 rather than 2/3. The cross-kink spacing in this case
is thenζxk,112 = 5ζc/(1/2) = 10ζc We will account for the difference in cross-kink spacings
for shear resolved on{112} versus other slip planes as needed when comparing theory with
experiments and simulations. In the highly concentrated, high temperature limit, any stress
bias will be overwhelmed by local solute and temperature ﬂuctuations. Thus the theory with 3
possible{110} cross-slip planes andζxk = 7.5ζc will apply most generally.
Note that the predicted cross-kink spacing scales withζc and is thus independent of tempera-
ture and decreases with increasing solute concentration. Both of these predictions are consistent
with experimental observations in moderate-concentration alloys (e.g. Fe-Si [32]).

3.2 Kink glide mechanism
Under stress, the kinks that are formed spontaneously to minimize the total dislocation energy
can glide laterally across the total straight segments of average length 2.5ζc. Opposing kinks
on either side of the segment only interact and are annihilated at the end of the process when
they approach closely. Kink annihilation processes and energies are thus not relevant. As a
kink glides, it shifts segments of the original low-energy segment to the adjacent higher-energy

state further along the glide plane, with re-creation of any necessary kinks. The applied stress
provides a driving force for the lateral kink motion, lowering the enthalpy barriers and hence
lowering the stress required for kink glide.

3.3 Cross-kink mechanism
Strengthening due to the existence of cross-kinks is caused by the need for the dislocation to
break the cross-kinks so that overall glide of the dislocation line can continue. The cross-kinks
form in alternating pairs of nascent (incomplete) vacancy and self-interstitial prismatic loops.
The failure of the array of cross-kinks thus occurs by thermally-activated failure of the weakest
type of cross-kink (vacancy or interstitial) at the scale 7.5ζc followed by failure of the stronger
type of cross-kink at the scale of 15ζc. There is thus an interplay between the energy barriers for
the weak and strong cross-kink types and the change in spacing.
Failure of a cross-kink is modeled in the same way for both vacancy and self-interstitial
types. The failure process proceeds by the creation of individual vacancy or self-interstitial
defects. Note that even when the cross-kinks extend over multiple planes such that the ﬁnal
formation of a prismatic loop requires the formation of many point defects, the low-energy path
for that failure is via the thermally-activated formation of one point defect at a time until all
necessary point defects have been created. Also, as the cross-kink structure grows, the atomistic
conﬁgurations near the original point of the cross-kink are evolving to slowly create the point
defect as the cross-kink extends (see Fig. 4). Failure of the cross-kink thus does not correspond
to the sudden creation of a number of point defects from an otherwise perfect lattice but rather is
the culmination of deformation within the region of the prismatic loop that then appears as the
point defect debris upon thermal activation.
Our analysis thus follows standard thermally-activated failure for point defect barriers [42].

3.4 Comparison with the Suzuki model and further comments
Here, we brieﬂy discuss the above theory as compared to the Suzuki model and then discuss a
few other aspects of our analyses.
The Suzuki model is also intimately tied to the glide motion of kinks through a random solute
ﬁeld [16]. However, there are crucial and fundamental differences between our analysis and
that of Suzuki. First, as discussed previously, the Suzuki model has a solute/screw interaction
energy parameter that is based on the notion that only the interaction energies at the ﬁrst and
second neighbors around the screw core are non-zero and equal. The present model considers
the entire solute/dislocation interaction energy ﬁeld in a consistent manner, and reduces to the
Suzuki model if the ﬁrst and second neighbor energies are dominant and equal. The Suzuki
model has no natural extension to multiple solutes in the non-dilute limit.
Second, the Suzuki analysis does not consider that, due to the spontaneous kinking, there is
an intrinsic underlying energy to move segments from low-energy (favorable) to high-energy
(unfavorable) positions during the kink glide (Figs. 1 and 6). The Suzuki model does assume
that kink-nucleation is not a relevant barrier, except possibly at very low T, but otherwise ignores
most aspects that follow as a consequence.
Third, the Suzuki model considered the energy change during kink glide due to a particular
solute conﬁguration to change abruptly, as if the kink width waswk =b. Only thereafter does
Suzuki average this result over the kink width. While the energy scale of the ﬂuctuations emerges
to be similar to the present work,≈√wk∆ ˜Ep, we believe this leads to a substantial overestimate
of the magnitude of the energy ﬂuctuations during kink glide. This averaging procedure also
leads, it appears, to the unphysical result for the activation volume at T=0K.
Fourth, the Suzuki model considers the formation of cross-kinks or dipoles as a result of
glide, but does not address why or how many kinks might form on different planes. Suzuki
then considered the operative barrier for kink glide to be governed by the maximum barrier
encountered over some lengthL that is the distance between the cross-kinks, or “dipoles” or, as
in later work [25, 27], the much longer distance of “forest dislocation spacing”. In the Suzuki
model, L is ultimately determined by minimizing the total strength (kink glide model plus
temperature-independent model for cross-kink pinning). This seems to lead to a much larger

estimated barrier controlled by statistical ﬂuctuations over a larger length. Furthermore, the
cross-kink spacing L in the Suzuki formulation then depends on temperature, which is not
consistent with experiments. A further consequence is that the strength versus temperature of
the cross-kinks is controlled by the temperature dependence of the kink glide motion, not by
any barriers associated with the failure of the cross-kinks themselves. Thus, the Suzuki model
tends to predict that the strength, across all temperatures, is dominated (approximately 80%) by
the kink glide. This also seems inconsistent with experiments at moderate temperatures where
cross-kink strengthening appears to dominate.
In spite of these substantial conceptual differences, the Suzuki predictions for total strength
can be comparable to results here at low to moderate temperatures. We attribute this to the much
higher statistical estimate of kink glide strength in the Suzuki model, relative to the present
theory, that partially compensates for the overall kink glide energy over 2.5ζc in the present
model, and to an underestimate of the cross-kink strengthening. The Suzuki model does predict
that the concentration dependence of strengthening is approximately linear inc except at very
low temperatures, which is consistent with our theory but again for very different reasons. A
recent extension of the Suzuki model to HEAs [19, 46], discussed further in Sec. 6, provides
reasonable agreement with a range of experiments on HEAs at room temperature, although
decreasing too rapidly with increasing temperature relative to experiments.
In the present model, we use average values for several inherently statistical processes. The
spontaneous kink formation is predicted to have a characteristic scaleζc but a distribution around
this value does not affect the analysis. The kink glide barrier versus glide distance is the average
value but there are clearly ﬂuctuations (see Figure 6c). These ﬂuctuations around the mean
values obtained here are expected to have little effect on the macroscopic predictions. During
glide of the entire long screw dislocation over distances of many Peierls barriers, the entire
dislocation encounters all the ﬂuctuations, both favorable and unfavorable, around the mean.
However, the dislocation must move as a connected line. The dislocation therefore cannot be
held back by local highly favorable ﬂuctuations over ζc that locally try to pin the dislocation
because the remainder of the advancing dislocation is exerting driving forces on these lagging
segments. Similarly, the dislocation cannot locally move ahead signiﬁcantly into local highly
favorable locations because the remainder of the dislocation lags behind and exerting retarding
forces on these advanced segments. Fluctuations in the local kink widthwk in response to the
local environment are also possible. But the theory is weakly dependent on the kink width
and plasticity requires the motion of vast numbers of kinks gliding, so that the spectrum local
environments are sampled and thus the typical kink is presumably close to the average. All
of these features prevent high local ﬂuctuations from having effects on the net motion of the
dislocation over long length scales. This justiﬁes the use of average quantities that encode the
major statistical ﬂuctuations, without considering the ﬂuctuations of those ﬂuctuations.

4 Strength vs temperature and strain rate
The results of the previous section are now integrated into an overall model for the temperature-
and strain-rate dependent screw dislocation strengthening in BCC alloys.
When multiple processes are involved, but under a macroscopic imposed plastic strain rate
˙ε, the total strain rate for each process must be the same over the scale ¯d of the largest-scale
process. If this were not the case, one process would occur much faster than the others and
move the dislocation forward in some regions, thereby exerting additional stresses acting on
the other processes and increasing their rates of occurrence. In steady-state, all processes must
thus generate the same strain rate. This is ultimately achieved by, effectively, partitioning of the
applied stress among the different processes at a common strain rate. This result also implies that
the inverse activation volumes are additive, and conforms with the general analyses of Kocks,
Argon, and Ashby [42] and other analyses for thermally activated motion through multiple types
of thermally-activated obstacles. Thus, for simplicity, and as widely done in the literature, we

6 Discussion
We have presented a new theory for strengthening of screw dislocations in BCC alloys ranging
from the low-concentration (5%) binary alloys to the complex multicomponent high entropy
alloys. While some features of the theory bear a resemblance to the early Suzuki model in
the fact that kink glide and cross-kink formation/failure are important mechanisms, the entire
formulation of the new model differs signiﬁcantly in nearly every aspect.
As mentioned earlier, a very new adaptation of the Suzuki model to HEAs was made by Rao
et al. [19]. The essence of that model is that the HEA is considered as a set of pseudo-binary
alloys. For an ABCD alloy, and the corresponding average alloy ABCD, the Suzuki model
is applied to the sub-alloys where each individual solute A, B, C, D is considered as a single
solute in the ABCD matrix. The strengths τi of these four pseudo-binary alloys are then used to
compute the total alloy strength as τalloy =

of the various technical issues about the Suzuki model noted earlier. This adapted Suzuki model
may thus be useful for broad design purposes, at least at room temperature. The agreement here

the interaction of the solute at the ﬁrst neighbor site of the screw core, (ii) using the Zhou et al.
interatomic potentials that indeed show interaction energies extending over a range of sites, (iii)
some of the core structures predicted by the homogeneous alloy Zhou potentials are polarized,
and (iv) using the Zhou et al. potentials that, in the limited cases available for comparison
[25], deviate signiﬁcantly from DFT results. Ref. [ 19] did, however, execute DFT studies
on a realistic TiZrNb screw core, and showed qualitatively similar results with the Zhou et al.
potentials for both the complex core structures and the spectrum of estimated solute/dislocation
interaction energies. Thus, the Zhou et al. potentials may have some accuracy for Ti-Zr-Nb. The
predictions of this adapted Suzuki model for TiZrNb at elevated temperature do not agree well
with experiments, however, consistent with our earlier remarks about the T-dependence in the
Suzuki model. Finally, the complex cores that appear in the DFT study are outside the scope of
both the present Suzuki model and the present model; this requires further analysis.

dilute binary alloy. This leads to a kink-glide back stress scaling as c as well as a cross-
kink strengthening scaling as c. These contributions to strengthening grow in importance
with increasing temperature, giving rise to a near-linear dependence of strength with solute

nucleation and thus behaves like pure Nb. This conclusion is fully consistent with experimental
work [30]. In the dilute limit, other phenomena such as solute-softening due to either the
alteration of the Peierls landscape [ 25, 27, 49] or the effect of discrete solutes lowering the
double-kink nucleation barrier [62, 63] may also become relevant. Solute-softening in this limit
deserves separate treatment but may not be typical in HEAs.
The present theory does not account for any very local effects and distortion at the atomic-
scale level, which might contribute to strengthening. Furthermore, we neglect possible core
polarization, which does not appear to exist in elemental and dilute alloys [29] and also not in W-
Ta alloys [64] but which does occur in DFT studies of the Ti33Nb33Zr33. Given well-established
artifacts in classical MD interatomic potentials, all these effects would require extensive DFT
work, which has been been almost entirely limited to few compositions and mostly binary alloys
[25, 27, 65]. DFT work could be pursued also for determining the input parameters of the theory,
especially the energy parameter ∆ ˜Ep. As for the kink formation energy, further work could
also pursue new machine-learning potentials based on DFT data-set, such as the Fe and W GAP
formulations used here [50, 57]. The theory also neglects possible short-range order as well as
speciﬁc solute-solute interaction energy. These latter features may be present in some HEAs, but
these issues can only be addressed starting from a valid theory, such as the present theory, for the
random alloy without solute-solute interactions.
Finally, we have not yet made predictions for most existing refractory HEAs. There are
35

## Page 37

two reasons for this. The ﬁrst reason is that the material inputs to the theory are not easily
determined. For instance, the core structures of the screw dislocations in both MoNbTaW
and MoNbTaVW alloys [6, 7] as predicted by the Zhou et al. potentials are polarized so that
estimates of the solute/screw interaction energies are likely not valid. The second reason is that
the strengths of some of the refractory HEAs may be controlled by edge dislocations rather
than screw dislocations. This idea is pursued in new work by the present authors [ 24], and
parameter-free predictions are in reasonable agreement with the MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW
alloys over a wide temperature range. Thus, some refractory HEAs may be screw controlled and
others edge controlled, and some may show a transition between screw and edge control versus
temperature.
To conclude, we have proposed a general theory of strengthening of screw dislocations in
BCC alloys, from dilute to random (“high entropy”). Starting from the spontaneous kinking
of the screw dislocation over a characteristic length scaleζc determined by solute/dislocation
interactions and kink energy, the theory identiﬁes three strengthening regimes (a Peierls-like
mechanism, lateral kink gliding, and cross-kink unpinning). The theory is consistent with Molec-
ular Dynamics simulations exhibiting these regimes. Theory also captures strengthening from
low to high temperature in several screw-controlled non-dilute and highly concentrated alloys.
The theory thus provides a uniﬁed understanding of strengthening of screw dislocations in a broad
class of BCC random alloys. Along with a complementary theory for edge dislocations [24], the
two theories can be used to guide design of stronger BCC random alloys from among the huge
space of compositions available in multi-component alloys.

A Validation using Molecular Dynamics on a Model BCC
High Entropy Alloys
There are a number of material properties that enter the BCC screw strength model. Many of these
are not directly accessible for real alloys, making direct comparison between the theory and real
alloys challenging. While we made such comparisons in the Section 5, here we use Molecular
Dynamics simulations on a model HEA alloy, the equicomposition NbTaV alloy, where all
material properties needed in the theory can be computed from the interatomic potentials for this
alloy. There remain challenges with molecular simulations that we mention as relevant during
the presentation below. The most important outcome of the simulations is the demonstration of
the operation of the distinct kink glide strengthening and cross-kink strengthening contributions
to the overall strength.
Details of our simulations are as follows. All Molecular Dynamics simulations have been

Speciﬁcally, at a given imposed stress, the system is ﬁrst relaxed for 20 ps to exclude effects of
relaxation under stress and thermal expansion; the dislocation position after this 20 ps interval is
taken to be the initial dislocation position. The simulation is then executed for an additional 980
ps. The dislocation position at the end of this period is measured. Two yield stresses are then
identiﬁed. The “initial yield stress” corresponds to single-slip glide and is deﬁned as the stress
at which all points along the dislocation line have move a distance≥ a/2. This “initial yield
stress” converges to the initial Peierls stressτP,0 asT→ 0 K. The “yield stress” is the stress
at which the dislocation has moved through the entire simulation cell within the speciﬁed time
period. In practice, once the dislocation breaks through some initial cross-kink pinning points, it
then moves without arrest through the remaining sample; this suggests some dynamic effects in
Molecular Dynamics (inertia, wave reﬂections, ﬁnite length dislocation) because the dislocation
should be successively pinned and unpinned by formation and thermally-activated destruction of
cross-kinks during the glide. The formation and destruction of the cross-kinks is observed, as
evidenced by the continual production of vacancy and self-interstitial debris during the motion,
but the dislocation does not arrest. This “yield” stress also converges to the value measured
in molecular statics asT→ 0 K. Figure (2b) shows the measured “initial yield strength” and
“yield strength” versus temperature for 10 different realizations of the random NbTaV alloy up to
T=500K. Both yield stresses drop fairly steeply with an initial increase of temperature followed
by a more gradual decrease. The difference between the “yield stress” and the “initial yield
stress” quickly reaches an average value of≈ 200 MPa over the range T=100K–500K.
