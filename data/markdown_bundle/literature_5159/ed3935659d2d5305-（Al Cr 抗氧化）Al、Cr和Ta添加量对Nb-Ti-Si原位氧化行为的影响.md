Abstract
The oxidation behaviour of as-cast Nb–24Ti–18Si and heat-treated (1500 or 1400 ◦C/100 h) Nb–24Ti–18Si–5Al, Nb–24Ti–18Si–5Cr,
Nb–24Ti–18Si–5Al–5Cr, Nb–24Ti–18Si–6Ta–5Al–5Cr, Nb–24Ti–18Si–8Cr–4Al and Nb–24Ti–18Si–6Ta–8Cr–4Al (at.%) alloys in static air at
800
◦C for 100 h has been studied using TG, XRD and EPMA. All the alloys exhibited linear oxidation kinetics. The as-cast (Nb,Ti)3Si containing
Nb–24Ti–18Si alloy suffered from catastrophic pest oxidation. The other alloys, in which the Nb5Si3 was present, did not pest. Al addition reduced
the oxidation rate signiﬁcantly. Higher oxidation rates and lower adherence of the oxide scale were observed with increase of Cr concentration
and/or Ta addition in the Al-containing alloys. In the oxide scales, Si-containing and non-Si-containing oxides of the stoichiometry MO
2 were
present together with remnants of 5-3 silicide and/or Laves phase. The oxygen solubility in the latter two phases was not high and these phases
did not oxidise easily compared to the (Nb,Ti)
ss. Below the oxide scale of the Nb–24Ti–18Si–5Al alloy a thick (∼100 /H9262m) internal oxidation zone
was formed where the (Nb,Ti) ss contained up to 30 at.% dissolved oxygen. In the Nb–24Ti–18Si–5Cr alloy this zone was thinner and leaner in
oxygen. In the Cr- and Al-containing alloys the internal oxidation zone was almost eliminated and the diffusivity of oxygen through the (Nb,Ti)ss
was reduced owing to the synergy of the Ti, Cr and Al additions, which was not affected by the addition of Ta to the alloy. The presence of Cr-rich
C14 silicide Laves phase in the microstructure of some alloys was not beneﬁcial regarding their oxidation at 800
◦C.

1. Introduction
A major barrier to the development of the Nb silicide-based
in situ composites for high temperature applications is their poor
oxidation behaviour. Nb–Si in situ composites with microstruc-
tures consisting of the Nb
ss (bcc solid solution), Nb5Si3 and/or
Nb3Si phases exhibit excellent creep strength but poor oxidation
resistance and fracture toughness [1–3]. The high temperature
oxidation behaviour of Nb–Nb 5Si3 alloys is characterized by
high metal recession rates (>225 /H9262m/h at 1200 ◦C), spallation
of the oxide scale and general structural disintegration[4]. Oxy-
gen diffuses very fast throughout the oxide scale of these alloys,
dissolves within the Nb
ss and causes substantial hardening and
embitterment of the Nbss.
Current investigations focus on Nb–Ti–(Hf)–Si–Cr–Al in situ
composites that consist of niobium solid solution, (Nb,Ti)ss and

Nb5Si3 silicides and with/without a Cr-rich C14 Laves phase
[4–9]. The presence of the Laves phase improves the oxidation
resistance of the alloys at 1204 and 1316 ◦C, but is detrimental
to their fracture toughness [6]. It has been suggested that bal-
ance between fracture toughness and oxidation resistance can be
achieved when the volume fractions of these phases are: 40–45%
Nb
ss, 45–55% Nb5Si3 and 0–15% C14 Laves [5,6].
Alloying with transition metal elements that include Ti, Cr
and Hf allows for the diffusivity of interstitials in niobium
to be controlled by the alloying elements, the latter either
interacting more strongly with oxygen and nitrogen than Nb
and Al or having smaller atomic radii thus providing traps to
reduce oxygen diffusivity in the Nb
ss [10]. Aluminium plays
an increasingly important role at higher oxidation temperatures
[6].
Research has conﬁrmed that the relationship between alloy
composition and oxidation behaviour varies with temperature.
Effects of Ti, Hf, Cr, Al and Si additions on the oxidation of
niobium silicide-based in situ composites have been studied in
the temperature range 760–1370
◦C.

At T < 850◦C, refractory metals can suffer from pest oxida-
tion [11]. According to Bewlay et al., the Al and Hf additions
can reduce the pesting susceptibility of Nb silicide-based in situ
composites, while Sn addition ( ∼1.5 at.%) can eliminate this
problem in the temperature regime 750–950
◦C [5]. The mecha-
nism by which Sn prevents pesting has not been investigated in
detail.
At 1200 and 1300
◦C, the oxidation resistance of niobium
silicide-based in situ composites is substantially improved by
additions such as Ti, Cr and Al [6]. Chromium and Al improve
the oxidation resistances of both the silicide and the Nb
ss [9] but
are detrimental for the fracture toughness of the Nb ss [12,13].
Addition of Ti improves the oxidation resistances of both the
silicide and the Nb
ss, and also the ductility of the Nb ss [9].
According to the oxidation data of Subramanian et al. for
selected Nb–Ti–Cr–Al–Si and Nb–Ti–Hf–Cr–Al–Si alloys that
was obtained after exposure of the alloys in static air at 1200
◦C,
the best oxidation resistant alloys had high Cr ( ≥8 at.%) and
high Al ( ≥6 at.%) contents [4]. In the presence of Cr and Al,
Hf addition did not have any effect on the oxidation behaviour
of the Nb–Ti–Cr–Al–Si alloys. However, it has been reported
that Hf addition reduces oxygen solubility and diffusivity in the
alloy and slows down its embrittlement at elevated temperatures
[6].
Bewlay et al. have reported that Si is the most beneﬁcial ele-
ment in reducing losses by oxidation in niobium silicide-based
in situ composites, followed by Cr and Ti additions [6]. They
suggested that optimum oxidation resistance can be achieved for
alloy chemistries with Nb/(Ti + Hf) ratios in the range 1.8–2.1
and Si concentration in the range 17–19 at.%; such composi-
tions offer also optimum creep strength [6]. This is supported
by recent studies of Chan, which also revealed that the oxida-
tion resistance of Nb silicide-based in situ composites would be
improved by decreasing the volume fraction of the Nb
ss phase
in their microstructure [14].
The effects of minor additions of elements such as Mo, W,
V , Ta, Zr, B and Ge on the oxidation of Nb silicide-based in situ
composites have also been investigated[6]. It has been suggested
that for Nb silicide-based in situ composites:
• more than 3 at.% B is beneﬁcial to their oxidation perfor-
mance,
• tantalum and Zr at ∼6 at.% cause the least damage to their
oxidation,
• molybdenum, W and V are detrimental for their oxidation
resistance, and
• partial replacement of Si with Ge improves their oxidation
resistance.
In this paper we report results of our study of the effects of
Al, Cr and Ta additions on the oxidation behaviour of Nb–Ti–Si
in situ composites that were subjected to isothermal oxidation
in static air at 800
◦C for∼100 h. The alloys studied in this work
were heat-treated in an Ar atmosphere at 1500 or 1400 ◦C for
100 h. The effects of the alloying additions and heat treatment/s
on solidiﬁcation path and phase transformations in Nb–Ti–Si in
situ composites have been reported elsewhere [15,16].
2. Experimental
The alloys were prepared using clean melting and casting in
a water-cooled copper crucible and specimens taken from the
centres of the ingots were heat-treated in an inert atmosphere
as described in [15,16]. Thermo-gravimetric (TG) studies in
static air at 800
◦C for 100 h were done for the alloys using
a Stanton-Redcroft thermo-balance. The constituent phases of
the heat-treated alloys and area fractions of the niobium solid
solution are summarised in Table 1. For the oxidation exper-
iments, cubic samples of 5 mm × 5m m× 5 mm size were cut
from the centres of the heat-treated specimens and their sides
were ground to 1200 grit. The dimensions of each sample were
measured using a micrometer and their surface area was cal-
culated. Each sample was placed in a small alumina crucible,
which was then positioned on the TG balance. The balance was
then calibrated to the initial total weight of the crucible and then
a tube furnace, which had been preheated at 800
◦C, was low-
ered around the crucible. In this way the sample was brought to
the exposure temperature quickly. The base of the crucible was
in touch with a thermocouple. Thus, both weight change and
temperature were monitored throughout each TG experiment,
during which a continuous curve of weight gain versus time was
recorded. The weight gain was normalised against the initial sur-
face area of the sample. The weight of the sample was also mea-
sured before and after each TG experiment, to verify the weight
change.
The surfaces of the oxide scales formed on the oxidised
samples were studied after each TG experiment ﬁrst macro-
scopically and then using XRD and EPMA (JEOL 8600 super-
probe equipped with an Oxford Instruments Pentafet EDX
detector). Four or ﬁve of the six oxide layers were removed
from each oxidised cubic sample, crushed and powdered, and
the powder was subjected to XRD. A Philips X-ray diffrac-
tometer with a monochromatic Cu K/H9251(λ = 1.5418 ˚A) radiation
was used. The presence of background noise from the sam-
ple holder in these X-ray diffractograms was signiﬁcant owing
to the small amount of oxide powder used. The peaks corre-
sponding to the specimen holder are identiﬁed in the diffrac-
tograms, where the JCPDS numbers for the oxides are also
given.
Some of the oxidised samples were mounted and polished
perpendicular to the remaining oxide layer, for the EPMA stud-
ies. BSE images of the oxide scales were acquired and spot
analysis was performed. For the quantitative analysis, Nb, Ti,
Ta, Cr, Al and SiO
2 standards were used. In all cases the oxygen
concentration was determined by difference.

3.2. Thermo-gravimetric analysis at 800 ◦C
The TG curves of the alloys and the oxidised samples at the
end of each TG experiment and prior to further study by XRD
and EPMA are shown in Figs. 1 and 2 . The worst oxidation
behaviour was exhibited by alloy KZ3 ( Figs. 1a and 2 ). The
TG curves of the Cr-containing alloys exhibited a non linear
oxidation stage at the beginning of oxidation, the duration of
which depended on alloy composition. The TG data for the ﬁrst
(non linear oxidation) stage were analysed using the equation
ln(/Delta1w)=l n K + nln t, where /Delta1w is the weight change per unit
area, t the reaction time, K and n are constants [3]. The kinetic
constant n for all the Cr-containing alloys for the ﬁrst 10 h was
higher than 0.5, which indicates faster than parabolic oxidation
kinetics.
The highest oxidation rate was exhibited by KZ3; after 10 h at
800
◦C the weight gain was 200 mg/cm2 (Fig. 1a). The 1500KZ4
exhibited the worse oxidation behaviour among the other alloys;
after 11 h at 800
◦C it had gained 3.8 mg/cm 2, and after this
time the oxidation rate increased resulting in a weight gain
of 83.7 mg/cm
2 after 85 h. The weight gains of the 1500KZ7,
1500KZ5, 1500KZ6, 1400KZ2 and 1400KZ8 alloys after 85 h at
800

The Al-containing alloy 1500KZ7 exhibited the lowest weight
gain rate than the Cr-containing and the Cr and Al-containing
alloys.

4. Discussion
At 800◦C, the KZ3 alloy exhibited fast oxidation and disinte-
grated into powder (pest oxidation). The other alloys exhibited
linear oxidation kinetics and the Al addition reduced the oxi-
dation rate signiﬁcantly. In the early stages of the isothermal
oxidation of the Cr-containing alloys, the TG curves exhibited a
non linear oxidation period, the duration of which depended on
alloy composition.
Two-stage oxidation has been reported for the high tem-
perature oxidation of Nb [17] and for niobium silicide-based
multi-component alloys that were exposed to temperatures in
the range 600–1300
◦C [3]. The oxidation of pure Nb in static
air (formation of Nb2O5) followed a parabolic law at short times
(n = 0.5) and became linear at longer exposure times. In niobium
silicide-based multi-component alloys, the value of the kinetic
constant n was found to be very close to unity at short times[3].
Likewise, in this work, the kinetic constant n was higher than
0.5 for the ﬁrst 10 h of isothermal oxidation for all the alloys,
indicating faster than parabolic oxidation kinetics.
In the ﬁrst stage of oxidation, rapid oxygen diffusion occurred
through the niobium solid solution and rapid oxidation took
place, while in the second stage breakaway oxidation occurred,
as the stresses generated within the growing oxide caused scale
cracking, in agreement with Menon et al. [3]. Furthermore,
in the second stage of oxidation, as the oxide scale thickened
it developed cracks or lifted away from the alloy partially or
completely, and thus did not provide an effective barrier to
oxygen ingress towards the Cr-containing alloys as oxidation
progressed.
In the Cr-containing alloys investigated in this work, the
higher oxidations rates in the second stage of oxidation and
the resulting lower oxide scale adhesion are attributed to the
increase of the Cr concentration or the Ta addition in the alloy.
Separation between the alloy and the oxide at the edges and the
corners of the samples, and porosity and cracking in the scale
and the scale/diffusion zone interface, with the cracks being par-
allel to the surface of the sample, indicated very low oxide scale
adhesion.
The XRD data indicated that in all alloys a mixture of Nb
2O5,
3Nb2O5·TiO2, 5Nb 2O5·2TiO2 and Nb 2O5·TiO2 had formed.
Aluminium, Cr and Ta additions probably promoted the forma-
tion of the CrNbO
4, AlNb04 and 2Nb2O5·Ta2O5 oxides, respec-
tively. Cristoballite SiO2 has been detected using XRD in the
oxides formed on niobium silicide-based alloys at 600–1300◦C
[3]. SiO2 could not be detected in our study as discussed above.
The following sections focus on the effects of the alloy
composition and microstructure on the oxidation rate and the
diffusion of oxygen in the alloy.

In the Nb–24Ti–18Si alloy (KZ3) linear oxidation occurred,

Regarding pest oxidation, Westbrook and Wood [11] pro-
posed a model that is based on micro-hardness measurements
along grain boundary paths perpendicular to the gas/metal
interface. According to this model, at low temperatures oxy-
gen/nitrogen diffusion in pest susceptible intermetallics is low
and oxidation takes place at the surface of the sample. Over some

intermediate temperatures oxygen diffuses rapidly through the
sample along grain boundary paths. In the early stages of oxida-
tion, the oxygen is conﬁned at the grain boundaries and causes
them to embrittle. Then oxide formation occurs and internal
stresses arise that cause the fragment of the specimen along
the grain boundaries. At high temperatures the local hardening
and embrittlement is relieved and pest oxidation does not occur.
Thus, the lower temperature of pest oxidation is the temperature
at which appreciable inter-granular diffusion occurs, while the
upper temperature limit of pest oxidation is the temperature at
which grain boundary hardening decreases abruptly.
Therefore, the pest oxidation of intermetallic compounds
is related with the formation of oxides at grain boundaries,
pre-existing cracks and pores and the resulting large volume
expansion at these sites at a range of temperatures that are spe-
ciﬁc for each intermetallic compound. V olume change during
oxide formation varies among the elements, and a third element
with higher afﬁnity to oxygen may change the oxide formation
at grain boundaries, pre-existing cracks and pores. When the
volume expansion during oxide formation is small the pesting
phenomenon can be suppressed. For example, during pest oxi-
dation of MoSi
2 selective oxidation of Si to SiO 2 takes place
at the grain boundaries, because the afﬁnity of Mo to oxygen
is much smaller than that of Si [22]. The volume expansion
accompanying selective oxidation of Si is signiﬁcant, +85.6%,
and generates large internal stresses at the grain boundaries that
cause the disintegration of MoSi
2. Addition of Al to MoSi 2,
which has higher afﬁnity for oxygen than Si, leads to a change
in crystal structure with increasing Al content from C11
b to C40
to C54 [23,24,25] and suppresses pesting [22,24]; the volume
change in Mo(Si,Al)2 is 4.9% [22].
It is suggested that the pest oxidation of KZ3 occurred
because of the generation of inter-granular cracks in the brit-
tle (Nb,Ti)
3Si, and the formation of voluminous oxides at its
grain boundaries that increased the oxygen intake and led to
linear catastrophic oxidation.

4.3. Oxidised Nb–24Ti–18Si–5Cr alloy (1500KZ4)
In 1500KZ4 the oxide scale was quite porous and thick, and
consequently the adhesion between the scale and the alloy was

oxidation of the Nb

is substituted by Al in the 5-3 silicide.

remaining Nb

smaller atomic radius, and therefore acts as a trap that reduces
oxygen diffusivity. Therefore, in 1500KZ4 the oxide scale

negligible. Considering that Al has greater afﬁnity for oxygen

CrNbO

contamination resistance [20].

5. Conclusions
The main conclusions of this work are as follows:
• All alloys studied in this work exhibited linear oxidation kinet-
ics.
• The oxidation of Nb–Nb
5Si3 silicide-based in situ composites
is controlled by the diffusion of oxygen in the (Nb,Ti)ss.
• The pest oxidation of the (Nb,Ti) 3Si containing KZ3 alloy
occurred because of the formation of voluminous oxides at
the grain boundaries of (Nb,Ti)
3Si and the generation of inter-
granular cracks that increased the oxygen intake and led to
accelerated oxidation.
• In the Nb
5Si3-containing alloy 1500KZ7, Al addition reduced
the oxidation rate signiﬁcantly compared to the other alloys.
• Higher oxidation rates and lower adherence of the oxide scale
were observed with increase of the Cr concentration and/or
the Ta addition in the Al-containing alloys.
• Si-containing and the non-Si-containing oxides correspond-
ing to the stoichiometry MO
2 were present in the oxide
scale together with remnants of 5-3 silicide and/or Laves
phase.
• The Cr-rich C14 silicide Laves phase is not beneﬁcial regard-
ing oxidation behaviour at 800
◦C.
• The oxygen solubility in the Cr-rich C14 silicide Laves phase
and Nb5Si3, both of which exhibit better oxidation resistance
than the (Nb,Ti)ss, is very low and is not affected by the sub-
stitution of Nb with Cr and Ta in these phases.
• An internal oxidation zone is formed below the oxide scale. In
the Nb–24Ti–18Si–5Cr alloy this zone was thinner and leaner
in oxygen compared to the Nb–24Ti–18Si–5Al alloy. In the
Cr- and Al-containing alloys the internal oxidation zone was
almost eliminated, which would suggest that the diffusivity of
oxygen through (Nb,Ti)
ss was reduced because of the synergy
of the Ti, Cr and Al additions.
