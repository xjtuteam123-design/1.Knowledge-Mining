Dendrite Deformation in the Rejoined Platforms
of Ni-Based Single-Crystal Superalloys
Miao Huo,* Lin Liu, and Wenchao Yang
1. Introduction
Ni-based single-crystal superalloys have excellent high-
temperature comprehensive properties. Hence, they are used
as the main materials for the fabrication of turbine blades and
guide vanes for advanced aeroengine. [1–3] Currently, single-crys-
tal (SX) turbine blades are primarily manufactured using direc-
tional solidi ﬁcation technology. The solidi ﬁcation process is
affected by several factors, including dendritic growth, which
is dynamic and easily disturbed. [4] Thus, it is easy to cause local
orientation deviations and solidi ﬁcation defects, which damage
alloy performance and lead to component invalidation. [5–7]
The process of directional solidi ﬁcation involves dendritic
growth under unstable conditions, which can lead to dendritic
deformation.[8,9] Owing to the complex geometric structure of
turbine blades and solidi ﬁcation parameters, local solidi ﬁcation
conditions tend to ﬂuctuate, resulting in frequent occurrence of
dendritic deformation. Additionally, other
solidiﬁcation defects, such as slivers, low-
angle boundaries (LABs), high-angle grain
boundaries, and stray grains (SGs) are also
observed.[10–12] These defects reduce the
yield of SX turbine blades. As a result,
numerous studies have been carried out
to investigate dendritic deformation and
the underlying mechanism for the forma-
tion of these defects. In 1993, Siredey
et al. [13] discovered that orientation
deviation of dendrites occurred during
the directional solidi ﬁcation of single-
crystal castings. They assumed that, if the
stress of γ´ precipitation were large enough,
it might cause this phenomenon. In 2000,
Napolitano et al. [14] observed the LABs
caused by the orientation deviation of
dendrites during branching. However, no
explanation has been provided for this ori-
entation deviation. In 2005, Newell et al. [15,16] suggested that the
contraction stress caused by an uneven thermal ﬁeld was the
main cause for dendritic orientation deviation and that this devi-
ation could accumulate and increase. In 2014, Bogdanowicz
et al. [17] believed that the lattice distortion stress caused by the
uneven distribution of the solute ﬁeld could be the primary cause
for the deviation in dendrite orientation. Aveson et al. [6] used in
situ X-ray imaging to examine the origin of misorientation
defects during solidi ﬁcation and observed dendrite deformation.
Thus far, it has been widely accepted that orientation deviation is
the result of dendrite deformation. Recently, Sun et al. [18] studied
the effects of secondary dendrite orientation and withdrawal rate
on dendrite growth and misorientation in platforms. Dendrite
deformation and an obvious deformation track were observed
in optical micrographs. It has been demonstrated that such
defects arise from differential thermal contraction. Huang
et al.[19,20] had a similar view, whereas Han et al. [21] indicated that
the thermal contraction stress was a result of the solidi ﬁcation
shrinkage of a solute-rich liquid. Peter et al. [22] simulated the
deformation behavior of dendrites in three dimensions.
However, recent research [23,24] has failed to clarify the cause
of dendrite deformation in different castings. In addition, most
previous studies have focused on solidiﬁcation defects at the edge
plate of the turbine blade. The guide vanes have an obvious
rejoined structure. However, dendrite growth, orientation evolu-
tion, and defect formation in this type of casting remain unclear.
In this work, dendrite growth and dendrite orientation evolu-
tion in rejoined platforms were studied using both experimental
investigations and ﬁnite-element simulations. The investigation
focused on dendrite deformation, dendrite misorientation, and

Dendrite deformation often occurs during the preparation of single-crystal
superalloy blades by directional solidiﬁcation and can cause solidiﬁcation defects,
such as low-angle boundaries (LABs), slivers, and orientation deviations.
Continuous dendritic deformation is uncommon and its mechanisms and
inﬂuencing factors are poorly understood. However, this can lead to signi ﬁcant
orientation deviation and performance degradation. Herein, a model with
rejoined platforms is designed to study the dendrite growth and orientation
evolution in a rejoined platform of Ni-based single-crystal superalloys. It is
indicated in the results that, under certain conditions, the developed secondary
dendrites deform continuously near the mold wall of the rejoined platforms and
the resulting disorientation has a cumulative effect. Finally, LABs and dendritic
fragments appear on the rejoined platforms. Thus, the integrity of the single
crystal is destroyed. This can be the result of the local shrinkage stress on the long
secondary dendrites within a suf ﬁcient growth space.

2. Experimental Section
2.1. Experimental Methods
Ni-based single-crystal superalloys were used in the experiments.
The nominal compositions of the alloys are listed in Table 1 .
To explore the dendrite growth behavior in single-crystal cast-
ings with rejoined structures, a model with rejoined platforms
was designed to prepare single-crystal castings, as shown in
Figure 1a in ref. [25]. For ease of description, the three platforms
were labeled 1, 2, and 3, and divided into three regions: left (L),
middle (M), and right (R).
The single crystals were cast via directional solidi ﬁcation. The
furnace mainly consisted of three units: a vacuum-induction
melting unit, a graphite heater unit, and a cooling zone, as shown
in Figure 1b in ref. [25]. Single-crystal castings were obtained
using the grain selection method. At the beginning of directional
solidiﬁcation, the furnace chamber was vacuumed, and the
ceramic mold was preheated to 1540 °C. The superheated molten
alloy (1580 °C) was then poured into the mold. After holding for
10 min, the mold was withdrawn from the furnace at a constant
rate of 150 μms /C01.
After clearing, the platforms were sectioned longitudinally
(parallel to the XZ plane) using wire-based electrical discharge
machining and prepared for metallographic analysis. To charac-
terize the dendrite growth path, well-polished specimens were
etched using a solution consisting of HNO 3, HF, and C 3H8O3
(volume ratio 1:2:3.5). The microstructure was observed using
an optical microscope (Leica DM-4000 M). To determine the ori-
entation of the dendrites, the well-polished specimens were
etched with a solution consisting of CuSO 4,H 2O, HCl, and
H2SO4 of 20 g, 80, 100, and 5 mL, respectively. Electron backscat-
ter diffraction (EBSD) analysis was performed using a Tescan
Mira 3 XMU scanning electron microscope equipped with the
Channel 5 analysis software. EBSD scanning was conducted at
a step size of 30 mm. All-Euler maps and pole ﬁgures of the
EBSD results were obtained using the Channel 5 analysis
software.

directional solidi ﬁcation process. The solidus and liquidus

sis. The simulation was initiated before the withdrawal process;
the melt and chill-plate temperatures were set to 1550 and 400 °C,
respectively. During directional solidi ﬁcation, the heat transfer
was assumed to be via conduction and radiation, and convection
was ignored. The thermophysical parameters were calculated

3. Results and Discussions
3.1. Dendrite Morphology and Evolution in the Rejoined
Platform
The dendritic morphologies of the three rejoined platforms in
the XZ plane are shown in Figure 1. A similar dendrite pattern
was observed from platforms 1 –3, as shown in Figure 1a –c.
The pattern mainly consists of two regions, namely the original
dendrite (R and L) and lateral growth (M) regions. The primary
dendritic arms grew regularly in regions R and L. These regions
were formed by the branching of the original dendrites from a
spiral grain selector. In region M, lateral growth is evident, as
indicated by the regions within the red-dotted box in Figure 1.
They are mainly composed of long dendritic arms formed
through lateral growth. As the platform height increased from
platform 1 –3, these regions expanded, as shown in Figure 1c.
In platform 1, there were no defects, and only slight lateral
growth was observed, as shown in Figure 1c. On platform 2,
the secondary dendrites branched from the right side and
entered the platform, and one path of the dendrites continued
to grow and occupied approximately 5/6th of the bottom of
the platform. During the growth process, the dendrites along this
path continued to deform until they converged with the remain-
ing dendrites. Finally, a grain boundary was formed at the inter-
face, as shown in Figure 1b. Similarly, there is a grain boundary
in the middle region of platform 3. However, the growth distan-
ces of the dendrites on both sides were similar in the middle
region of the platform, as shown in Figure 1a.
The temperature ﬁeld distribution during solidi ﬁcation was
simulated to understand the dendritic growth morphology in dif-
ferent areas of the platform. The total thermal gradient and its
components in the three directions of the rejoined platform
are shown in Figure 2 . The thermal gradients along the lateral
sides (X and Y directions in Figure 2b,c, respectively) were
the main heat-dissipation driving forces at the rejoined platforms
of such castings, whereas the thermal gradient along the Z direc-
tion had a smaller contribution (Figure 2d). Moreover, with an
increase in the solidi ﬁcation distance, this trend became more
evident. The different dendritic morphologies in the middle
region of the platforms are shown in Figure 1.
3.2. Dendrite Evolution and Deformation in the Rejoined
Platform
A magniﬁed local micrograph of the defect location on platform 2
and the corresponding EBSD polar diagrams are shown in
Figure 3 . Secondary dendrites from the right side grow into
the platform. During growth, an obvious deformation process
with a parabolic shape is observed, as shown by the blue-dotted
line in Figure 3a. The tertiary dendrites derived, which emanate
from the deformed secondary dendrites, form an angle with each
other because they were perpendicular to their mother branches,
as indicated by the red and blue arrows in Figure 3a. Tertiary
dendrites are arranged in a fan shape. It can be inferred that
the secondary dendrites underwent serious deformation.
To conﬁrm the deformation behavior further, the orientations
of the original dendrites on both sides and deformed dendrites
were measured. The EBSD results are shown in Figure 3b,c,
respectively. By comparing the two polar diagrams, it was found
that the orientation of dendrite I at the beginning of deformation
was close to that of the original dendrite, as shown in Figure 3b.
After deformation, the orientation of dendrite II at the end of the
deformation and dendrite I was symmetrically distributed with [1]
as the axis, as shown in Figure 3c. The dendrites in the middle of
the deformation (dendrites indicated by the blue arrow in
Figure 3a) were closer to. [1] At this point, they had the largest

A magni ﬁed local micrograph of the defect location on plat-

The secondary dendrites branching from the right-side deviate

a grain boundary at the convergence interface (indicated by the

entering each platform, and their 3D orientation was different
(Figure 3b and 4b), which determined the competitive advantage
between deformed dendrites and surrounding dendrites. In plat-
form 3, the deformed dendrites were blocked by other dendrites
and deformation was interrupted, resulting in a signi ﬁcant
deviation in the grain-boundary positions of the two platforms
(Figure 3a and 4a).
Dendrite deformation requires disturbances or stresses. Some
studies[7,11,30,31] have shown that the solidi ﬁcation shrinkage
stress can lead to dendrite deformation, and the dendrites near
the mold shell wall are most likely to be affected and deformed.
Shrinkage stress was generated owing to the different shrinkage
coefﬁcients of the mold and alloy. In this experiment, dendrite
deformation occurred near the mold wall, most likely caused by
solidiﬁcation shrinkage stress. To con ﬁrm this possibility, a
ﬁnite-element simulation of the stress distribution during solid-
iﬁcation was performed. The total effective stress evolution with
respect to the advancement of the solidus in the platform during
directional solidi ﬁcation at a withdrawal rate of 150 μms /C01 is
shown in Figure 5 . When the solidus isotherm (shown by the
white-dotted line in Figure 5) reached the platform, a weak stress
began to appear at the bottom of the middle position of the plat-
form, as shown in Figure 5a. This stress initiated the deforma-
tion of the secondary dendrite. As the solidus continued to move
toward the platform, stress was generated near the lower mold
wall and continuously increased, as shown in Figure 5b –d, which
indicated the possibility of continuous deformation of the sec-
ondary dendrites. In addition, the stress distribution was fan
shaped, which is consistent with the dendrite distribution shape
(Figure 3a). It was further con ﬁrmed that stress was the direct
cause of the dendrite deformation.

deformation requires suf ﬁcient growth space and a favorable 3D
orientation to continue uninterrupted.
The developed secondary dendrites were subjected to a stress
ﬁeld during growth, similar to that of a cantilever beam under
stress and would undergo bending deformation. During this pro-
cess, secondary dendrites in the mushy zone are subjected to
shrinkage stress and local orientation changes. When the sur-
rounding eutectic solidi ﬁed, the deformation is retained.
Therefore, the deformation process is gradually completed in
conjunction with the dendrite growth. In this experiment, the
deformation track of the secondary dendrites has a parabolic
shape. Thus, it can be inferred that the stress direction of the
dendrite is downward, which is consistent with shrinkage stress.
It was again proved that the dendrite deformation was the direct
result of the solidi ﬁcation shrinkage stress.

Based on the previous analysis, it was found that the secondary
dendrites developed in the longitudinal section underwent con-
tinuous deformation, which ﬁnally led to the formation of a LAB
at the convergence interface. Owing to severe deformation and
cumulative effects, the angle of the LAB exceeded 10°. Therefore,
such LABs seriously damage the integrity of the single crystal
and must be prevented. Consequently, control measures for such
defects have become a major area of research. Controlling the
solidiﬁcation rate within a certain range and the original orien-
tation of the dendrites could be an effective approach.
4. Conclusions
The mechanism of dendrite deformation and the formation of LAB
defects in the rejoined platforms of Ni-based single-crystal super-
alloys were investigated by microstructural analysis, EBSD testing,
and ﬁnite-element simulation. There were original dendrite
regions and lateral growth regions in the platforms, which were
determined by the thermal conditions. The stress ﬁeld conditions
and orientation relationship of the dendrites in the deformation
regions indicated that the solidi ﬁcation shrinkage stress easily
acted on the long dendrites near the mold wall and caused their
continuous deformation. Finally, LABs were formed on the
platform. However, the stress was necessary but inadequate for
dendritic deformation and LABs formation, because the deformed
dendrites had to compete with the surrounding dendrites for suf-
ﬁcient growth space. Therefore, the continuous deformation and
orientation deviation of secondary dendrites in the platform require
the following conditions: 1) a thermal ﬁeld dominated by lateral
heat dissipation, which could provide the motive force for the sec-
ondary dendrites in the rejoined platforms to develop sufﬁciently;
2) a suf ﬁcient growth space for continuous growth and deforma-
tion; 3) a favorable 3D orientation, because they need to compete
with the surrounding dendrites; and 4) a local stress condition.
Hence, stress is a precondition for continuous deformation.
