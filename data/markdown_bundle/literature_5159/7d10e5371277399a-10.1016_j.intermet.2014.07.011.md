abstract
The microstructural evolution, creep, and tensile deformation behavior of an orthorhombic Ti e22Al
e25Nb (at.%) alloy was investigated by thermo-mechanical processing, including common forging,
isothermal forging, and heat treatment. Three different microstructures were obtained by varying the
isothermal forging temperatures and heat-treatment schedule. Tensile-creep experiments were con-
ducted from 650 to 700
/C14 C and over a stress range of 100 e200 MPa. The alloy tensile strengths at room
temperature and 650 /C14 C were also determined. As the isothermal forging temperature increases from
1040 /C14 C to 1080 /C14 C, three alloy microstructures result, including equiaxial, duplex, and bimodal-size
lamellar orthorhombic microstructures. Of the three, the bimodal-size lamellar orthorhombic micro-
structures have the highest strength but worst ductility, whereas the equiaxial microstructures have the
highest ductility but worst strength. The equiaxial microstructures have the worst creep resistance,
whereas the duplex microstructures and bimodal-size lamellar orthorhombic microstructures have a
similar creep resistance.

1. Introduction
Developments in the aerospace industry have led to a require-
ment for materials with high strength and stiffness and low density,
especially for high-temperature applications [1]. Since the ortho-
rhombic (O) phase in a Ti e25Ale12.5Nb (at.%) alloy was discovered
by Banerjee et al. [2],T i eAleNb alloys that contain a signi ﬁcant
amount of O phases (termed O alloys) have been studied as po-
tential materials for high temperature applications because of their
high speci ﬁc strength, improved oxidation resistance, good creep
resistance, and high temperature strength [3]. Although slightly
weak in terms of ductility, toughness, and workability, Ti 2AlNb-
based alloys with a nominal composition of Ti e22Ale25Nb (at.%)
have excellent potential for application in engineering practice, and
thus have attracted extensive interest from the scienti ﬁc world
[4,5].
Ti2AlNb-based alloys are multiphase alloys, whose phase
structure differs from that of commercial titanium alloys. They
include several different phases: an O phase (Cmcm system based
on Ti 2AlNb), hexagonal close-packed a2 phase (DO 19 structure
based on Ti 3Al), and body-centered cubic phase b (disordered
structure) or B2 phase (ordered structure). The mechanical prop-
erties of the Ti 2AlNb alloys are affected extensively by their mi-
crostructures [6]. However, phase equilibria and microstructural
evolution in orthorhombic alloys is complicated. In these alloys, a
single- or two- or three-phase microstructure can be obtained,
depending on the heat treatment used. The volume fraction, size,
and morphology of these constituent phases are sensitive to their
thermal processing and heat treatment history [7e9]. Thus, the
relationship between the microstructure and mechanical proper-
ties of orthorhombic alloys must be explored further.
The mechanical properties, especially creep properties, of
Ti2AlNb-based alloys can be tailored effectively by microstructural
control. Thus, an optimized microstructure is needed to improve
the strength, creep, and fracture toughness of Ti 2AlNb-based alloys
[10,1 1]. Proper design of chemical composition is one way to obtain
optimized microstructures and mechanical properties, while the
thermo-mechanical process (TMP) as applied to Ti 2AlNb-based al-
loys is another effective way [12]. The latter ful ﬁls more closely the
requirements and expectations for the modern production of
Ti2AlNb-based alloys in real applications. Over the last two decades,
TMP technical parameters, such as forging, solution-, and age-

2. Material and experiments
Tie22Ale25Nb bar (240 mm diameter, 360 mm length) was
provided by Central Iron and Steel Research Institute (CISRI). The
chemical analysis as provided in Table 1 shows that the bar
composition correlates with the nominal composition, and that the
gas impurity content is low (e.g. oxygen /C20 500 ppm, hydrogen
/C20 300 ppm, and nitrogen /C20 800 ppm). The microstructure of the
Tie22Ale25Nb bar is shown in Fig. 1a. The microstructure contains
B2 matrix, equiaxial a2/O particles and lamellar O. The distribution
of the equiaxial a2/O particles is non-homogeneous. The equiaxial
a2/O particle consists of a2 particle and rim-O (in Fig. 1b), where the
rim-O is formed by the peritectoid reaction of the a2 phase and B2
matrix. The pinning effect of the equiaxial particle (in Fig. 1 b) is
beneﬁcial for restraining B2 grain growth. The B2 grain size of the
Tie22Ale25Nb bar is 38 mm on average. The lamellar O (in Fig. 1b)
is precipitated during cooling after forging.
Isothermal forging is used to obtain a homogeneous micro-
structure and enhance the mechanical properties of the alloy. Cy-
lindrical cakes (240 mm diameter, 80 mm length) were machined
from the as-received bars and discharged electrically. Isothermal
forging was conducted on a computer-controlled 500 ton hydraulic
press with nickel-base super-alloy ﬂat dies, and an electric resis-
tance heating furnace that was sealed in an argon- ﬁlled chamber.
The ram position and speed were controlled to produce the
required constant strain rate and reduction in cylindrical cake
height. The cylindrical cakes were heated to the forging tempera-
ture with the tools and were soaked for 2 h. After isothermal
forging, samples were removed from the dies and cooled in air. Test
temperatures were monitored using a chromel-alumel type-K
thermocouple that was located at the furnace centre. All temper-
atures were maintained within ±2 /C14 C of the set temperature. Three
isothermal forging schedules: 980 /C14 C (HT-1: a2 þ B2 þ O phase
region), 1040/C14 C (HT-2: a2 þ B2 phase region) and 1060 /C14 C (HT-3: B2
phase region) were used to improve the alloy properties.
Isothermal forging was carried out at strain rates of 0.05 s /C0 l. The
height reduction of the cylindrical cakes was more than 50% in one
stroke. The isothermal forged alloys were then machined to form
cylinders of 13 mm diameter and 14 mm height for heat treatment.
The specimens were solution-treated at 940 /C14 C for 1 h followed by
water quenching, and then age-treated at 800 /C14 C for 12 h followed
by air cooling to study creep and tensile properties.
For the investigation into the microstructural evolution, mi-
crostructures that were isothermally forged and solution- and age-
treated were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM,
SSX-550 scanning electron microscope, Shimadzu, Japan) in back-
scattered electron mode. SEM specimens were prepared by stan-
dard metallographic techniques. Specimens were polished metal-
lographically and etched chemically using HF:HNO 3:H2O (1:3:5).
Phase structural analysis was performed by X-ray diffraction (XRD,
D/MAX-RB X-ray diffractometer, Rigaku, Japan) using Cu-K a radi-
ation for 2 q ¼ 20/C14 e90/C14 at 2/C14 /min. Sample characterization and ﬁne
microstructure details before and after creep deformation were
determined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM －
200CX TEM, JEOL, Japan). TEM samples were prepared by cutting
samples from specimens after heat treatment and creep
deformation. The samples width was reduced to 30 e50 mmb y
polishing with SiC paper, and the samples were then punched
into 3 mm discs. Electrochemical polishing was the ﬁnal thinning
stage in a solution of 6% perchloric acid, 34% n-butanol, and 60%
carbinol at /C0 30 /C14 C, 70 mA, and 30 V. The volume fraction and
particle size were determined by image analysis on scanning
electron micrographs using Image Pro Plus software (Image-Pro
Plus is a trademark of Media Cybernetics, Inc.) on optical
microscopy and SEM images. Approximately ten images were
selected for each test condition.
Blanks from the heat-treated materials after isothermal forging
were machined to cylinders, which contained threaded grips, and
were used for tensile-creep tests. Creep specimens with a gauge
diameter of 5 mm and a length of 70 mm were machined after heat
treatment. Creep testing was conducted at 650 /C14 C in air with an RD
testing machine. The test temperature was ~650 e700 /C14 C, the
applied stress was ~100 e200 MPa, and the creep time was 100 h.
Specimen temperatures were monitored by the attachment of two
chromel-alumel type-K thermocouples directly to the specimen's
reduced section. The temperature ﬂuctuations were less than ±2
/C14 C.
To minimize the thermal stresses, specimens were soaked at the
creep temperature for at least 30 min prior to applying a load.
Tensile samples were 12 mm in diameter and 70 mm in gauge
length. Tensile tests were carried out at room temperature (RT) in
air using an Instron 1 185 mechanical testing machine. High tem-
perature tension experiments were performed at 650 /C14 C using an
MTS (Materials Testing Systems) mechanical testing machine.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural evolution
3.1.1. Effect of isothermal forging temperature on microstructure
evolution
Fig. 2 shows the microstructure evolution under different
isothermal forging temperatures. According to the phase diagram
of Miracle [15] for a Ti e22AlexNb alloy, alloying was conducted in
the
a2 þ B2 þ O, a2 þ B2, and B2 region for forging temperatures of
980 /C14 C, 1040/C14 C, and 1060 /C14 C, respectively. The as-forged alloys were
forged isothermally at 980 /C14 C( Fig. 2 a), and the microstructure
contained equiaxial O, and a2 phases, a rim O phase, and a coarse-
and ﬁne-lamellar O phase. The coarse-lamellar O phase, equiaxial
O, and a2 were formed during isothermal forging. Precipitation of
the ﬁne-lamellar O-phase occurred during gradual cooling to RT
after forging. When the isothermal forging temperature increased
to 1040 /C14 C, it can be seen from Fig. 2 b that the microstructure
consisted of equiaxial a2 phases, lamellar O phases, and a B2 matrix.
A large amount of equiaxial a2 phases, which restrain the migration
of the grain boundary, are distributed in the B2 grain interior and
boundary. The existence of equiaxial
a2 particles plays an important
role in controlling B2 grain growth, which improves the alloy
ductility [4]. An increase of isothermal forging to 1060 /C14 C results in
the formation of a full lamella microstructure ( Fig. 2c). The B2 grain
size increases rapidly because of grain growth during heating. The
growth rate is much larger than that forged at 1040 /C14 C, and results
from the pinning effect of a2 particles in isothermal forging at
1040 /C14 C.
3.1.2. Evolution of solution- and age-treated microstructure under
different isothermal forging temperatures
Microstructure evolution during heat treatment had been re-
ported on in a previous study [13,14]. Besides the solution and aging
process, the forging temperature also plays an important role in the
ﬁnal microstructure. Thus, when the initial as-forged microstruc-
ture is different, the distinction of heat-treated microstructure
should be considered. Fig. 3a shows the microstructure of a sample
forged isothermally at 980 /C14 C and then solution treated at 940 /C14 C. It
contains equiaxial a2 and O, coarse-lamellar O, a rim O around the
a2 phases, and B2 matrix. In comparison with the as-forged alloy,
more O-phase particles precipitated and ﬁne-lamellar O phase was
transformed into equiaxial particles in prior B2 grains. The primary
coarse-lamellar O phase became wider and shorter and some re-
gions became globular during heat treatment. Some small equiaxial
O phases were connected during the static spheroidizing process.
As the isothermal forging temperature increases to 1040 /C14 C
(Fig. 3b), the microstructure contains equiaxial a2, ﬁne plate-like O-
phases within a2-phases, and lamellar O within the B2 matrix. In
comparison with the as-forged alloy, more ﬁne plate-like O phases
precipitated in the a2 phase, and the primary lamellar O phase
became wider and shorter during heat treatment. This phenome-
non is different from the microstructure of Fig. 3a, such as the O rim
around the a2 phases, which are present in the microstructure of
the sample forged isothermally at 980 /C14 C, but no ﬁne plate-like O
phases exist within the a2 phases. The rim O phase, which forms at
the periphery of the a2 phase, is considered to be partially
responsible for the diffusion kinetics for a2. It appears to act as a
diffusion barrier between the relatively low Nb-containing a2 phase
and the high Nb-containing body-centered cubic phases [16].I n
this microstructure, the ﬁne plate-like O phases within the a2
phases are distributed over the primary a2 grains. As a result of Nb
diffusion, the a2 phase separates into Nb-lean and Nb-rich regions.
Nb-rich regions with a composition closer to that of Ti 2AlNb are
transformed into the O phase [17], while the Nb-lean regions
remain untransformed, i.e., retained a2 phase. As the forging tem-
perature is increased to 1060 /C14 C, the microstructure contains
mainly the lamellar O phase and B2 matrix. Static globularization
occurs for a small amount of the previous lamellas in Fig. 3 c. In
comparison with the as-forged alloy, the amount of equiaxial a2
particles was reduced signi ﬁcantly.

3.2. Effect of three different microstructures on tensile behavior
The RT and 650/C14 C high-temperature tensile properties, including
0.2% yield strength (YS), ultimate tensile strength (UTS), elongation-
to-failure ( εf), and reduction in area (RA), are listed in Table 2 .I n
most cases, the results represent the average of at least three tests.
The bimodal-size lamellar O microstructures (HT-3) have the high-
est YS and UTS at RT, however, their ductility ( εf) and RA are the
lowest; the equiaxial microstructures (HT-1) have the lowest YS and
UTS at RT, with the highest ductility ( εf) and RA; the tensile prop-
erties of the duplex microstructures (HT-2) lie between these limits.
The trends for the 650 /C14 C tensile properties were similar to those at
RT; however, each of the samples exhibited a lower strength and
higher ductility (εf) and RA values than those at RT.
The various parameters that in ﬂuence the alloy yield strength
are (1) the grain size of the B2 phase, (2) the strength of the indi-
vidual phases as in ﬂuenced by changes in their composition with
heat treatment, (3) the volume fractions of the
a2/O and B2 phases,
(4) the lath size of the O phase, and (5) the dislocation substructure.
The basic strengthening mechanisms in orthorhombic alloys are
similar to those in conventional titanium alloys. Boundary
strengthening through microstructural re ﬁnement has the most
important in ﬂuence on strength; and the secondary O lath size
shows another important factor in strengthening, which can be
attributed to a Hall-Petch type. The grain sizes of the B2 phase are
different for the three microstructures from different isothermal
forging temperatures. The HT-3 has the largest grain size of the B2
phase at ~236 mm, while the HT-1 has the lowest value at ~87 mm.
According to the Hall-Petch formula, HT-1 should have the highest
yield strength, however, the facts are to the contrary. Except for the
B2 grain size, the thickness of the lamellar O phase can also in ﬂu-
ence the yield strength signi ﬁcantly. For HT-3, the microstructure
contains lamellar O phase and B2 matrix. Compared with equiaxial
a2/O particles, the lamellar O shows a high strengthening effect, but
poor ductility, as the lamellar O reduces the ductility of the B2
phase [18]. The principal factors in ﬂuencing ductility are the size of
the a2/O particles, the volume fraction of the B2 phase, and its
continuity around the a2/O phase, but for HT-1, the microstructure
contains mainly equiaxial O/ a2 phase, a tiny amount of ﬁne-
lamellar O phase, and B2 matrix. Compared with the lamellar O,
the equiaxial O phase particles contribute more to the alloy
ductility. The HT-1 has the largest elongation and reduction in area.
The fractographs of the room-temperature tensile specimens
with three different TMP schedules are shown in Fig. 5. The fracture
behavior is related to the tensile ductility. The peak ductility con-
dition for HT-1 is associated with a relatively smooth fracture sur-
face (at low magni ﬁcation) which shows no indication of the
underlying prior B2 grain size. The fracture mechanism of the
material is a mixture of cleavage and faceted fracture. The speci-
mens also exhibit a ﬂat fracture characteristic of brittle metals. The
alloy microstructures exhibit a combination of fracture character-
istics: both cleaved and faceted O grains and dimples within the B2
regions. For HT-1, the fracture surface consists ofﬁne-scale cleavage
associated with equiaxial a2/O ductile tearing, and a fraction of
dimpled failure. For HT-3, the tensile fracture occurs by cleavage of
the O laths, and an examination of the longitudinal cross-sections
of failed samples shows that failure to the neck occurred immedi-
ately on initiation of cleavage.

Boehlert and Miracle [20] overviewed the reported creep be-
haviors of a2-and O-based TieAleNb alloys. They divided the creep
behaviors into three regimes and identiﬁed the creep mechanism in
each regime according to the values of n and Q in Eqs (2) and (3) .
When n ¼ 1 to 1.9 and Q ¼ 107e187 kJ/mol, the creep process is
controlled by grain-boundary diffusion. When n ¼ 1.9 to 2.8 and
Q ¼ 256e327 kJ/mol, the creep process is controlled by grain-
boundary sliding. When n ¼ 3.5 to 7.2 and Q ¼ 241e376 kJ/mol,
the creep process is controlled by dislocation climb. It is generally
accepted that high-temperature dislocation climb is associated
with lattice self-diffusion, while grain-boundary sliding is associ-
ated with either lattice self-diffusion or grain-boundary diffusion.
Typically, the activation energy for grain-boundary diffusion is half
that of lattice self-diffusion [21]. The values of Qapp from 230.85 to
329.72 kJ/mol and the values of n from 3.84 to 5.14 for these alloys
correspond to Qapp and n for high-temperature dislocation climb-
controlled creep. Therefore, it is believed that the creep process is
controlled by the high-temperature dislocation climb. The apparent
stress exponent n can vary signi ﬁcantly in alloys with different
microstructures. New ﬁndings exist in this work compared with
