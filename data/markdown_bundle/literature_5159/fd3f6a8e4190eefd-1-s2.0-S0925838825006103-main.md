1. Introduction
High-performance structural materials play a crucial role in energy 
storage and transportation [1 – 4] . However, traditional metallic mate -
rials commonly face challenges such as low-temperature toughnes -
s-embrittlement transitions, which pose significant obstacles in 
developing high-performance cryogenic structural components [5 – 8] . In 
recent years, the emergence of multi-principal element alloys, known as 
high-entropy alloys (HEAs), particularly face-centered cubic (FCC) 
HEAs, has garnered considerable interest among researchers due to their 
favorable room temperature and cryogenic ductility (77 K) [9 – 11] . 
Currently, moderate yield strength remains a primary limitation for FCC 
HEAs, hindering their widespread application [12 – 14] .
Traditional strengthening methods (such as precipitation strength -
ening, work hardening, and grain refinement) have been proposed to 
enhance the yield strength of HEAs, often at the expense of ductility 
[15 – 18,61 – 63] . Currently, metastable engineering and heterogeneous 
structure engineering strategies have been proven effective in syner -
gistically enhancing alloy strength while maintaining ductility 
compatibility. In heterogeneous structure engineering, the principle 
involves strain distribution between soft and hard domains during 
deformation, inducing back stress strengthening in soft domains to 
enhance strain hardening capability and delay necking initiation [19, 
20] . However, the introduction of coarse scales typically limits the yield 
strength of alloys to not exceed 1 GPa [13,21 – 23] . Additionally, 
metastable structure engineering often requires the addition of signifi -
cant amounts of the expensive alloying element Co to reduce the 
stacking fault energy (SFE) [19,24] . However, the high cost of alloying 
elements restricts the industrial application of these HEAs. In recent 
years, Si has been recognized as a low-cost alloying element that effec -
tively reduces the SFE of HEAs, thereby enhancing tensile strength and 
ductility [25,26] . Therefore, the synergistic addition of abundant and 
low-cost Fe elements with Si holds promise for achieving a balance be -
tween high strength and ductility in HEAs, as well as reducing alloy 
costs. However, research on the influence of Si addition on the formation 
of heterogeneous structures and strain-induced martensitic phase 
transformation (SIMT) in Fe-HEAs with precipitation strengthening, and 
the response mechanisms of their mechanical properties at cryogenic 
temperature, remains limited.

neering and metastable phase engineering strategies to achieve excellent

3.2. Tensile properties at cryogenic temperature
Fig. 5 a-c present the engineering stress-strain curves, work hard -
ening rate curves, and comparison of cryogenic tensile properties of 0Si 
and 3Si alloys. As depicted in Fig. 5 a, the 3Si sample exhibits higher 
yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and uniform elongation 
compared to the 0Si sample. The yield strength (YS), ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS), and uniform elongation (UEL) of the 0Si sample are 
1028 MPa, 1121 MPa, and 6 %, respectively. And the YS, UTS, and UEL 
of the 3Si sample are 1324 MPa, 1446 MPa, and 20 %, respectively. The 
superior cryogenic tensile properties of the 3Si sample are attributed to 
its tensile deformation behavior. As shown in Fig. 5 b, significant dif -
ferences are observed in the work hardening rate curves of the 0Si and 
3Si samples. While the work hardening rate curve of the 0Si sample 
shows a continuous decrease, the one of the 3Si sample exhibits char -
acteristics of multi-stage hardening. Multi-stage hardening contributes 
to enhanced strain hardening capability and UEL of the alloy. Moreover, 
the work hardening rate of the 3Si sample is notably higher than that of 
the 0Si sample, thereby facilitating excellent tensile strength of the 3Si 
alloy. To demonstrate the exceptional cryogenic tensile properties of our 
3Si Fe-HEA, we compared the cryogenic mechanical properties of the 
3Si sample with several other FCC high-entropy alloys, as shown in 
Fig. 5 c. It is clear that our 3Si sample exhibits high yield strength and 
good strength-ductility synergy [2,13,29 – 41] .
3.3. Phase and substructure after tensile at cryogenic temperature
To further understand the origins of differences in cryogenic tensile 
properties and deformation behavior of the alloys, we characterized the 
phase volume fractions and substructure features of 0Si and 3Si samples 
after tensile rupture. Fig. 6 a and b show the EBSD phase maps of the 0Si 
and 3Si samples, respectively. From Fig. 6 , it can be observed that the 
FCC phase, BCC phase, and HCP phase fractions of the 0Si sample after 
tensile rupture are 82.6 %, 13 %, and 4.4 %, respectively. And corre -
spondingly 79.2 %, 4.8 %, and 16 % in the 3Si sample. It is evident that 
both 0Si and 3Si samples undergo FCC → BCC / HCP martensitic 
transformation during cryogenic tensile deformation. Moreover, the 
degree of martensitic transformation is slightly higher in the 3Si sample 
compared to the 0Si sample. Interestingly, the 3Si sample exhibits a 
greater tendency towards FCC → HCP transformation compared to the 
0Si sample, possibly influenced by alterations in stacking fault energy 
(SFE) of the alloy matrix due to Si addition [14] . Although the magni -
tude of change in the volume fraction of martensitic phase trans -
formation (FCC → BCC / HCP) before and after tensile test is smaller for 
the 3Si sample than for the 0Si sample (10.5 % for the 3Si sample and 
16.1 % for the 0Si sample, respectively). It is worth noting that the 
stability of the phase is related to the grain size in addition to the SFE of 
the FCC matrix. In general, the finer the grain size, the higher the sta -
bility of the FCC matrix [68] . For the annealed samples, the 3Si sample 
has a finer average grain size due to the more significant recrystalliza -
tion; the finer grain size improves the stability of the FCC phase, leading 
to an increase in the resistance to the phase transformation of FCC → 
BCC / HCP. Although there is a certain amount of Si in the matrix, which 
reduces the SFE, as Si atoms mainly exist in the form of precipitates, the 
content of Si in the matrix is limited. It means that the Si has a limited 
effect on the reduction of the SFE, resulting in the stability of the FCC 
phase being largely influenced by the FCC grain size. In addition to the 
volume fractions of phases, the substructures of the alloys after tensile

strain hardening rate until fracture.
3.6. The origin of Si addition to the yield strength enhancement
Based on the discussion of enhanced contributions above, it is 
evident that the differences in yield strength between the 3Si and 0Si 
alloys are primarily associated with grain boundary strengthening and 
precipitation strengthening. Therefore, it is crucial to clarify how the 
addition of Si influences the microstructure and subsequently affects the 
yield strength. As shown in Fig. 2 , compared to the 0Si alloy, the average 
grain size of the 3Si alloy after cryogenic rolling and annealing is 
significantly reduced due to a higher degree of recrystallization, 
exhibiting a bimodal grain heterostructure characteristic. Additionally, 
as seen in Figs. 3 and 4 , the fraction of nano-precipitates after cryogenic 
rolling and annealing is notably higher in the 3Si alloy compared to the 
0Si alloy. This implies that the addition of Si primarily enhances the 
grain boundary strengthening and precipitation strengthening contri -
butions of the 3Si alloy through its effects on alloy recrystallization and 
the volume fraction of nanoscale precipitates. However, intriguingly, as 
far as we know, the conclusion that Si addition promotes recrystalliza -
tion contradicts common knowledge because typically, Si addition helps 
reduce stacking fault energy (SFE), thereby suppressing dislocation cross 
slip and thus inhibiting recrystallization. Furthermore, according to the 
reference [55] , Si addition also suppresses recrystallization of the 
FeCoCrNiMn HEA. Therefore, in this study, does the beneficial effect of 
Si addition on recrystallization relate to precipitates? It is noteworthy 
that related researches [56,57] have reported that precipitates can act as 
nucleation sites for recrystallization, thereby promoting recrystalliza -
tion. On the other hand, research has indicated that Si addition helps 
increase the volume fraction of precipitates in the FeCrNi MEA and re -
fines the radius of precipitates [58] , which is consistent with our 
research results. Therefore, it can be concluded that the high-density 
nano-precipitates in our 3Si alloy provide nucleation sites for recrys -
tallization and overcome the inhibitory effect of Si addition on SFE, 
effectively refining grain size and enhancing grain boundary strength -
ening. Additionally, these nano-precipitates further impede grain 
growth by pinning grain boundaries, thereby enhancing grain boundary 
strengthening contributions. According to formula (5) , the hindrance of 
precipitates to grain boundary movement can be estimated [59] .
