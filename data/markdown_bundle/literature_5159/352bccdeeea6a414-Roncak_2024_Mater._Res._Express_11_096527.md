Abstract
AlCoCrFeNi2.1 alloy, which belongs to the group of eutectic high-entropy alloys(EHEAs), possesses a
combination of increased strength and ductility. It should retain these properties over a wide
temperature range due to the high entropy effect of the system. At the same time, eutectic alloys are
generally considered to have good castability, which increases the possibility of casting the alloy in
larger volumes. One of the processes, that the alloy does not avoid when applied in industry, are the
various joining techniques including electron beam welding. The weld area is often in a non-
equilibrium state, which increases the risk of failure during operation. The paper therefore discusses
the stability of the microstructure and mechanical properties of AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 alloy when exposed to
short-term elevated temperatures. The material heated at 900°C for 1 h in a vacuum furnace was
observed using light and electron microscopy, analyzed for chemical and phase composition and
ﬁnally subjected to HV0.1 hardness measurement and tensile strength test. The resulting condition
was compared with the welded joint before exposure to elevated temperature. The microstructure of
the weld was formed by aﬁne lamellar eutectic over the entire observed area. EBSD analysis conﬁrmed
the presence of a combination of FCC and BCC phases. The material hardness reached an average
value of 370 HV0.1. Maximum tensile strength of the weld joint was measured at 944 MPa with the
corresponding displacement of the crosshead 6.1 mm. The welded joint demonstrated sufﬁcient
stability and the ability to withstand short-term severe elevated temperature conditions.
1. Introduction
A constantly evolving industry requires the research and development of new materials capable of coping with
challenging conditions such as temperature gradients or mechanical stresses. One group of materials developed
for these purposes are eutectic high-entropy alloys(EHEAs) [1–5] combining structural stability due to the high
entropy of the system and the good castability of eutectic alloys. At the same time, compared to other high-
entropy alloys (HEAs) [6–10], there is no so-called strength ductility trade-off. With the idea of EHEAs, the
AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 alloy was also introduced. Its eutectic structure is formed by a ductile FCC phase(enriched in Co,
Cr, Fe and Ni) and a BCC phase(Al and Ni) providing enhanced strength[11]. The result is a tensile strength of
944 MPa and an elongation of 25.6% at room temperature[1], with the possibility of changing properties by
thermo-mechanical processing [12–15]. Furthermore, a microstructural stability test in the range of 600—
1200 °C for 50 h conﬁrmed that the eutectic alloy can retain its properties even after exposure to elevated
temperatures. On the contrary, dendritic areas, which were subject to phase changes and precipitation, proved
to be problematic[16].
The applicability of new alloys also depends on their bonding properties in complex structures such as joints
in pipework, pressure vessels or turbine blades connected to the shaft. Primarily, these are applications that
require increased mechanical properties and stability at a site exposed to elevated stresses over a wide

the base material. Thus, according to the available data, the AlCoCrFeNi

investigation in theﬁeld of welding and remelting.
Among the properties, that have not yet been extensively veriﬁed, is the thermal stability of welded joints
during heat treatment. Paradoxically, these areas tend to be the critical point due to the non-equilibrium state of
the material. The temperatures, at which changes in phase composition and properties occur, can be determined
either within a certain range or by thermal analysis. Thermal analysis methods include differential scanning
calorimetry (DSC), which uses the heatﬂux of a material to determine the temperatures of exothermic and
endothermic reactions [34, 35]. Subsequent methods involving observation, chemical and phase analysis
accurately identify the type of reaction taking place. DSC has been used for both welded joints of different
materials [36, 37] and cast AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 alloys [38–40]. The alloy in the as-cast condition exhibited an
equilibrium state in most cases, resulting in DSC curves showing the melting and solidiﬁcation temperatures.
On the contrary, the material after welding exhibited the transition to the equilibrium state accompanied by the
precipitation of new or dissolution of the original phases.
Based on the above, the aim of the work is to verify the resulting material properties when subjected to short-
term heat treatment at a temperature exceeding the phase changes in the microstructure. The material in the
heat-treated state is directly compared with the as-welded state in terms of phase composition, chemical
concentration, lamellae thickness, hardness and tensile strength. The heat treatment is carried out in a vacuum
furnace at a temperature exceeding the reactions in the material revealed by the DSC method(900 °C). The
heating time is set to 1 h.
2. Materials, process parameters and methodology
2.1. Materials
The AlCoCrFeNi2.1 alloy ingots were cast in an Indutherm MC15 vacuum induction furnace using pure metals.
The vacuum chamber of the furnace wasﬁlled multiple times with argon and pumped to a vacuum of 0.1 mbar
to limit melt oxidation. The calculated content of chemical elements in the charge is given in at.% in table1. The
reason for the modiﬁcation compared to the typical composition of the eutectic alloy was the loss of chromium
in the cast ingots due to the reaction of the melt with the crucible wall and the consequent disruption of the
eutectic structure formation.
2.2. EB process parameters
After casting, the ingots were machined to a cylindrical shape with a diameter of 14 mm and a height of 20 mm.
In order to make a circumferential weld at the interface of the base materials, a pin and a hole were made on the
specimens for an axis alignment(ﬁgure 1).
The parameters used to make the welded joints are shown in table2. The working distance is taken as the
distance from the centre of the focusing coil to the surface of the welding component.

3. Results
Figure 3 shows the shape of a weld joint typical of electron beam welding. The depth of the weld exceeds its width
several times in the narrowed region, conﬁrming sufﬁcient power density and welding in keyhole mode. The
observed surfaces showed no signs of macroscopic defects in the form of cracks and porosity. At the same time,
the approximate evaluation areas of the welded joints before and after heat treatment are plotted in theﬁgure.
Areas whose data are used to demonstrate the results in the paper are marked in red. For the results of the
averages and standard deviations presented in the tables, data from all analysed areas were used.
Subsequent observation of the microstructure at the interface between the fusion zone(FZ) and the base
material (BM) revealed that the weld joint is formed by a lamellar eutectic prior to thermal exposure(ﬁgure 4).
The difference from the eutectic present in the base material is theﬁne grain size of the lamellae associated with
the rapid solidiﬁcation after electron beam welding in the vacuum chamber.
The DSC results of the sample cut from the material can be seen in the form of curves(ﬁgure 5), which show
the change in heatﬂux as a function of temperature. The 1st heating curve deﬁnes 2 temperature regions where 1
exothermic (527 °C) and 1 endothermic(803 °C) change occurs in the material. These are mostly phase changes
in the form of precipitation(exothermic reaction) or dissolution of the precipitates(endothermic reaction).
When compared to the 2nd heating curve, the material has stabilized at about 850°C and the curves overlap
from that point on. At the same time, no signiﬁcant reactions occurred during the second heating, and the
material showed microstructural stability. Thus, based on the above results, a temperature of 900°C was chosen
to verify the thermal stability and the resulting material properties.
A closer look at the microstructure in the middle part of the fusion zone(ﬁgures 6(a), (d)) showed no
signiﬁcant difference between as-welded and heat-treated fusion zones. The weld joints were formed byﬁne
lamellae and the dendritic structure was fully suppressed on the observed surfaces.

Fractography of the fractured surfaces was performed to determine the reason for the decrease in mechanical
properties in the specimen subjected to heat treatment(ﬁgure 11).
The fracture area can be divided into several sub-areas depending on the type of fracture and the defect

fracture containing miniature dimples occurred. In contrast, brittle fracture occurred in the upper region

material measured by the DSC method.

Observation by light and electron microscopy showed that the entire welded area before and after thermal
exposure consisted ofﬁne-grained lamellae of similar thickness. In terms of precipitating of new phases or
formation of non-lamellar eutectic, the material demonstrated its stability.
However, exothermic and endothermic reaction occurred during DSC analysis. Due to the typically narrow
proﬁle of the weld joint, the DSC sample included a base material that contained coarser grained structure and
microscopic areas of dendritic structure. In[41], the short-term thermal stability of AlCoCrFeNi2.1 alloy was
investigated in the temperature range of 800 to 1000°C from 15 to 60 min. The resulting microstructure
contained, in addition to the eutectic, dendritic regions. At elevated temperature, the formation of a B2 needle
phase occurred in the original FCC dendritic phase. According to thermodynamic calculations, the FCC phase
had a higher than equilibrium content which caused the subsequent precipitation and formation of B2 phase in
the dendritic phase of FCC. The primary dendritic regions, in which new precipitates were formed in the original
phase, were also problematic during the long-term heat treatment in[16]. However, this time BCC phase
precipitates were formed in the FCC phase at temperatures up to 800°C. When increased to 1000°C, the
precipitates were not present, and dissolution occurred. Thus, the formation of a particular type of precipitates is
strongly related to the state of the material in terms of phase composition ratio and solid solution
supersaturation. The supersaturation and non-equilibrium state is also related to rapid solidiﬁcation during
electron beam welding. Therefore, the DSC analysis could also be affected by the precipitation of phases caused
by the supersaturation of the solid solution and their subsequent dissolution. The idea is supported by a second
DSC measurement, in which no reactions occurred and the material showed thermal stability.
Within the welded joints, the formation of the dendritic region was completely suppressed both before and
after heat exposure. Electron beam is due to that suitable not only for the formation of welded joints, but also, for
example, for the modiﬁcation of surface layers.
The resulting thickness of the lamellae in both the post-welded and heat-treated states correlates with recent
research on electron beam remelting of AlCoCrFeNi
2.1 [42]. The alloy exhibits stability in terms of grain size.
An interesting ﬁnding is the change in the proportion of FCC and BCC phases in welded joints. The change
was mostly visible in the centre of the weld, where FCC decreased from a 90% to 67% and BCC increased from

10% to 33%. Additionally, the proportion of BCC phase decreased gradually from the top to the root of the weld
in the as-welded condition. This behavior was not observed in the heat-treated condition. This may be
inﬂuenced by the slightly higher dispersion of Al values observed in the middle region of the weld. Al is primarily
contained in the BCC phase of the eutectic due to its favourable mixing enthalpy with Ni(−22 kJ mol−1) [41].
Hence, the larger dispersion in a complex process such as electron beam welding may affect the resulting phase
ratio.
Despite the increased proportion of the BCC phase in the heat-treated condition, the hardness of the fusion
zone did not change signiﬁcantly in any of the measured regions. The highest values were always near the root of
the weld, which is probably due to the smallest remelting area, the rapid solidiﬁcation of the melt and the
associated ﬁne grain size. In the case of the hardness of the base material, it was increased after heat treatment. At
the same time, the variances of the values do not overlap. This is probably due to the metastable state of the base
material after casting into the copper coke. Subsequent heating adds energy which is used to reach the steady
state and precipitate new grains in the original phase. The precipitation is conﬁrmed by the image taken during
the hardness test(ﬁgure 13).
The strength of the material in the heat-treated state reached the maximum values obtained in other
experiments [1]. The standard deviation of the strength values shows that there was a greater range of values in
the heat-treated condition. The main reason for this is the result for one of the samples whose tensile strength
reached 659 MPa at a crosshead displacement of 3.4 mm. This is due to incomplete penetration of the base
material acting as a stress concentrator. The loss of tensile strength is thus caused by a technological error in the
welding process and not by the material itself. It is therefore likely that the material in the heat-treated condition
could, without the presence of the defect, reach values similar to those in the as-welded condition. Since the
fracture in the as-welded condition occurred in the base material, it was not possible to compare the fracture
mechanisms with each other.
5. Conclusion
The cast AlCoCrFeNi2.1 alloy was electron beam welded and subjected to a short-term heat treatment for 1 h to
verify the thermal stability of the material. The temperature was determined using the DSC method to exceed the
phase changes in the microstructure. When comparing the joints after welding and in the heat-treated state, the
following conclusions were reached:
 The microstructure was formed byﬁne lamellar eutectic throughout the weld proﬁle without the presence of a
dendritic structure.
 The lamellae consisted of a combination of FCC and BCC phases similar to the base material.
 The chemical composition of the elements was uniformly distributed throughout the weld joint with the
greatest variation in values observed for Al.
 The joint hardness averaged 370± 14 HV0.1. Heat treatment reduced the variance of the values compared to
the as-welded condition.
 The maximum tensile strength 944 MPa at a crosshead displacement of 6.1 mm was reached.
