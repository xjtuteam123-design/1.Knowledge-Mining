Abstract 
Corrosion of process equipment during ammonothermal synthesis at high temperature and pressure 
is still one of the big limitations of this process because of reduced crystal quality and safety issues. In 
this paper we present preliminary ammonothermal corrosion experiments with two Ni -base 
superalloys (Inconel®Alloy 718 and Haynes®282®Alloy) and two newly developed Co-base superalloys 
(CoWAlloy1&2) in a supercritical NH 3/NH4Cl-solution (24 h/550 °C/105 MPa). After the experiment, 
the samples were analyzed by their mass change per initial surface area ( specific weight change ). 
Additionally, the morphological and elemental changes of the corrosion layers were evaluated. None 
of the investigated alloys developed a passivation layer and the corrosion layers were very fragile. In 
agreement with previous studies, the Ni-base alloys showed intergranular attack with severe general 
corrosive degeneration. Despite their high potential for high -pressure at high -temperature 
applications and in contrast to a ternary Co -Al-W-alloy, the investigated multinary Co -base alloys 
were strongly attacked, too. However, CoWAlloy2 presented the lowest specific weight change of the 
investigated alloys . Thus, it shows the best potential for application in acidic environment of the 
investigated alloys. Additionally, CoWAlloy2 proved good stability in a supercritical NH 3/Na-solution 
(24 h/550 °C/93 MPa).

1 Introduction 
Ammonothermal synthesis has become a promising solvothermal crystal growth method for nitride-
based semiconductors. The most studied ammonothermal product is gallium nitride (GaN), which is 
now widely known as the semiconductor in blue LEDs, that was the topic of the 2014 Nobel Prize in 
Physics [1]. The basic idea of the ammonothermal process is to grow high quality nitride crystals from 
a supercritical ammonia (sc -NH3) solution at high pressure and temperature. Typical process 
conditions are in the range of 100-300 MPa and 400-800 °C [2,3]. 
Acidic or basic additives, so called mineralizers, are used to increase the solubility of the substance to 
be crystallized. The type of mineralizer not only de termines the solubility behavior of the product of 
interest dependin g on pressure and temperature , but also the corrosive potential of the solution 
against the pressure vessel itself. In general, ammonobasic mineralizers ( MNH2 with M = Li, Na, K …) 1 
are less corros ive against state of the art Ni-base superalloy pressure ve ssels than ammonoacidic 
mineralizers (NH 4X with X = F, Cl, Br, I) [4–6]. Even though ammonothermal synthesis has been 
studied thoroughly in the last two decades, handling of aggressive ammonothermal solutions at high 
temperatures and pressures is still one of the big limitations of this process. Corrosion of non -
protected process equipment results in an increased impurity concentration in grown crystals , both 
for acidic and basic systems [5,7], and especially stres s corrosion cracking in acidic environments 
leads to critical safety issues at the described high pressures and temperatures [8,9]. 
Noble metal liners have been used or show good potential to reduce corrosion in ammonthermal 
environment, e.g. Pt in sc -NH3/NH4Cl [7,10,11] or Ag in sc-NH3/NH4F [12] and sc -NH3/Na-systems1 
[13]. Mo capsules could improve the crystal quality of ammonobasic grown GaN [14]. Pure Mo and a 
Ti-Zr-Mo Mo-base alloy (TZM) have shown good resistance especially in NH 4Cl containing sc -NH3 
[15,16]. Crystal quality and protection of the autoclave improve with the use of liners . However, 
there is still a need to know about the mechanisms of ammonoacidic corrosion to increase corrosion 
resistance of the pressure bearing parts themselves, as most of the liner systems are non -
hermetically closed and thus corrosion can still take place although on a longer time scale. 
State of the art materials for ammonothermal pressure vessels are Ni -base superalloys. Among 
others René 41 [11,17], Alloy 625 (Inconel®Alloy 625; 2.4856) [6,15], Alloy 718 (Inconel®Alloy 718; 
2.4668) [18], and Alloy 282 (Haynes®282®Alloy) [19] are used for different pressure and temperature 
ranges and ammonothermal chemi stries. Those alloys were selected primarily due to their excellent 
strength at elevated temperatures because of precipitation hardening. Additionally, they possess 
sufficient corrosion and oxidation resistance at high temperature, but not necessarily unde r 
ammonothermal conditions. Hertweck et al. published results on the different types of attack on 
autoclaves made of Alloy 718 in ammonoacidic and ammonobasic environment: general corrosion 
and intergranular attack combined with high internal pressures in acidic solution leads to str ess 
corrosion cracking in contrast to a nitrided but not passivated surface in basic environments [8,9].

Pimputkar et al. as well observed severe corrosive attack of pure Ni and Ni -base alloys in acidic 
environment [15]. Autoclaves made of René 41 were used in pure sc -NH3 and sc-NH3/Na-solutions, 
whereas autoclaves made of Alloy 625 provide higher resistance but are still not recommended for 
long-term use in sc-NH3/NH4Cl because of a strong etching of the autoclave walls. Pure Ni and other 
Ni-alloys (Hastelloy C-276 and thermocouple junctions Chromel C/Alumel, Nicrosil/Nisil) were only 
found to be stable in pure sc -NH3 (6 d/575 °C/103 MPa) and sc -NH3/Na-solutions 
(6 d/575 °C/218 MPa): the mass gain was below 0.5 wt%. In sc-NH3/NH4Cl (6 d/575 °C/100 MPa) they 
showed a high mass loss of about 50-60 wt%. These authors also investigated pure Co and a ternary 
Co-Al-W-base superalloy. Similar to Ni, pure Co showed a high mass loss of about 40 wt% in 
sc-NH3/NH4Cl (6 d/575 °C/100 MPa) [15]. In contrast to pure Co, the ternary Co -base alloy 
(Co80W10.6Al9.4) appeared stable in pure sc-NH3 (6 d/575 °C/104 MPa), sc -NH3/NH4Cl 
(6 d/575 °C/100 MPa), and sc-NH3/Na (6 d/575 °C/207 MPa): the mass change was below 0.1 wt% for 
all investigated environments. However, detailed metallographic results on the described co rrosive 
attack are missing. The described class of Co -base superalloys has become a focus of research, 
because the system Co-Al-W provides the opportunity to harden the alloys by the precipitation of the 
ordered phase (’) in the Co solid solution matrix phase (). The Co 3(Al,W) phase exhibits the same 
L12 crystal structure as the ’ phase Ni3Al in Ni-base superalloys [20]. The higher melting point of Co 
resulted in high liquidus and solidus temperatures for these alloys. Additional alloying improved the 
oxidation resistance and the mechanical properties [21–24]. This opens the field of high pressure at 
high-temperature applications. In ammonothermal synthesis, h igher temperature and pressure are 
crucial for new synthesis routes, especially when developing new compounds [19], yet investigations 
on the stability of higher alloyed Co-base superalloys in ammonothermal environments are needed 
to define possible application areas. 
In this paper, we present a detailed corrosion study about four different superalloys suitable for 
autoclave design in ammonothermal synthesis: two Ni-base superalloys (Alloy 718 and Alloy 282) and 
two newly developed Co -base superalloys (CoWAlloy1&2, [23]). The two Ni -base superalloys are 
commercially available and already in use in autoclave design for ammonothermal research in our 
group. Mainly because of their high strength at elevated temperature and good machinability. The 
two Co-base superalloys are promising due to their potential to broaden the temperature/pressure 
range of ammonothermal synthesis in comparison to the presented Ni -base superalloys . All four 
alloys are tested in a sc -NH3/NH4Cl-solution (T = 550 °C/p ≈ 100 MPa). CoWAlloy2 is additionally 
tested in sc-NH3/Na (T = 550 °C/p ≈ 100 MPa). The aim is a direct comparison of the corrosion 
behavior of the different alloys and the definition of possible application areas.

2.2 Experimental setup 
Corrosion experiments were carried out in a similar experimental setup to the one presented by 
Pimputkar et al. [15]; see Figure 5 a). The autoclave (11) is made of Alloy 625 and sealed with a 
gasket of the same material. To reach a homogeneous temperature profile inside the autoclave, a 
second heater (21) was used to heat the top part of the autoclave. A pressure transducer 
(Omegadyne, PX02, 207 ± 2 MPa or Honeywell, HP, 414 ± 4 MPa) (42) was connected to the reaction 
chamber and one thermocouple (Type K, Omega Engineering) (41) per heater monitors the outside 
temperature of the autoclave continuously. To reduce corrosive attack on the autoclave setup itself , 
the mineralizer and the sample (31) were placed inside a non -hermetically sealed pure Mo capsule 
(32), the design is similar to the capsule used by Pimputkar et al. [14]. To allow uniform attack of the 
solution to the sample, it was fixed to a rack with wire, both pure Mo; see Figure 5 b). All Mo parts 
were 99.95% (metals basis) or purer and purchased from Alfa Aesar.

2.3 Experimental Procedure 
The autoclave was assembled inside a nitrogen -filled glove box (MBraun, Labmaster 130, O 2 and 
H2O < 1 ppm): Mineralizer – NH4Cl (purchased from Alfa Aesar, 99.999% purity metals basis) – and a 
sample rack with one sample per run was placed in the capsule, which was then placed inside the 
autoclave body. Subsequently the autoclave was closed and filled with ammonia. The filling station 
and filling procedure is described in detail in [13,15]. The amount of ammonia was chosen to reach a 
maximum pressure of about 100 MPa at the maximum temperature of 550 °C. The resulting 
ammonia density was determined according to equation (1) by mass flow controller and the 
measured free volume of the autoclave. 
The autoclave was heated up with a rate of 2 °C/min, kept at a final temperature of 550 °C for 24 
hours and then let cool down to room temperature. 
As it was soon visible that severe attack in the ammonoacidic solution takes place for all investigated 
alloys, an additional experiment in ammonobasic environment was conducted with the most 
promising of the investigated alloys, CoWAlloy2. In this case , the Mo capsule was removed and 
metallic Na (purchased from Sigma Aldrich, ACS reagent grade) was used as mineralizer. 
The mass of the mineralizer (NH 4Cl or metallic Na) was measured with a high -resolution balance 
(A&D Company Limited, HR -250AZ; ±0.1 mg). The molar concentrations of NH 4Cl and Na for each 
experiment were determined according to equation (2). 
Pictures of the corroded samples after the experiment were taken with a digital microscope 
(Keyence, VHX -5000). To quantify the corrosive degeneration of the samples , the specific weight 
change due to corrosion was calculated by dividing the mass change of the sample by its initial 
surface area, equation (3).

The samples were not cleaned after the experiment, but placed in a vacuum furnace at 70 °C for at 
least 8 hours , to remove volatile compounds from the porous co rrosion layer. After that they were 
mounted directly in hot mounting resin , as preliminary experiments showed that the formed layers 
are very fragile and would be removed significantly during the cleaning process. As the focus of this 
work is on the metallographic characterization of the formed corrosion layers, the authors tried to 
keep all layers intact. Subsequently, the samples were ground and polished to prepare micrographs. 
Structural and elemental changes in the alloy and the morphology o f the corrosion layers were 
analyzed in a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Gemini Ultra 55, Carl Zeiss) using a back-scattered 
electron detector (BSE detector) . The elemental composition of the corrosion layer was determined 
via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, Noran System SIX, ThermoScientific).

3.1 Experiments with NH4Cl as mineralizer 
Quantitative and microstructural analysis of corrosive attack 
All four alloy samples show severe corrosive degeneration after the one -day experiment in 
sc-NH3/NH4Cl ammonothermal solution at about 105 MPa and 550 °C. In contrast to a ternary 
Co-Al-W-alloy studied by Pimputkar et al. [15], which showed good corrosion resistance, our 
multinary Co-base alloys were strongly attacked, too. The formation of black to gr ay colored, porous 
and loose upper corrosion layers and corrosion products can be observed ; see Figure 6 b). The 
engraved numbers, which allowed differentiation of the alloy samples before the experi ment, are 
not visible any more.

Co as main alloying and matrix element of the two investigated CoWAlloys and secondary alloying 
element in Alloy 282, is removed almost completely from the corrosion layers. Fe, which is 
substituting Ni in Alloy 718 is removed completely from the layer as well. Ni is reduced in all 
investigated alloys except in the case of CoWAlloy2, where we see a slight increase in the main parts 
of the corrosion layer. Cr shows a slightly reduced proportion in the case of CoWAlloy1, Alloy 718, 
and Alloy 282. For CoWAlloy2 we observe a significant reduction of Cr in the layer. In contrast , 
elements that are part of the ’-precipitates (e.g. Co3(Al,W) in the case of the CoWAlloys) , like W, Al, 
Ti, and Ta show good resistance and enrichment in the layers . Mo, which provides good creep 
resistance, shows higher amounts in the layers as well. Those findings imply a preferred dissolution 
of the matrix elements (Co, Ni, Fe) as the other elements of the y’-precipitates (W, Al, Ta, Ti) are still 
present in the layer after the corrosive attack. CoWAlloy2 is the alloy with the highest W content, 
shows the least specific weight change and is the only investigated alloy that shows an enrichment of 
Ni in the layer. 
CoWAlloy1 and Alloy 282 showed darker colored sections in the layer that looked like cracks filled 
with a different material. The composition of those areas revealed a high Ni and Co content together 
with traces of Cl . Cl--ions are known to be very aggressive in aqueous high-temperature solutions 
[38]. Therefore, we assume that Cl --ions are playing a key role in the presented ammonothermal 
experiments as well. Other elements were only present to a sma ll extent. Both, Ni and Co were the 
elements that were the most reduced in the other parts of the layer. One explanation for the 
different composition of material inside the cracks and at the outermost section of the formed layers 
could be the precipitatio n of already dissolved corrosion products. During the experiment, parts of 
the alloys dissolve in the sc-NH3/NH4Cl-solution, e.g. Fe, Co, and Ni. The formed corrosion layer has a 
different thermal expansion coefficient than the original alloy and cracks form during cooling. At the 
same time, corrosion products that dissolved in the sc-NH3/NH4Cl-solution at high temperature might 
not be soluble in liquid ammonia at room temperature and thus precipitate inside the cracks or o n 
the sample surface. Although this is an educated guess, s olubility data of potential corrosion 
products like Ni-, Cr -, Co- or Fe -chlorides in sc -NH3 are currently not available. In the case of 
CoWAlloy2, the darker sections of the corrosion layer showed a higher amount of Cl as well ,

Combining the results on the elemental composition of the corrosion layers with the microstructural 
findings does not yet lead to an unequivocal conclusion. For example, it is not clear why the 
intergranular attack is less prominent in Alloy 282 than in Alloy 718. Both alloys possess Cr-rich M23C6 
carbides at their grain boundaries. Alloy 718 additionally possesses Nb carbides (NbC) and the Nb-
rich δ-phase at their grain boundaries . However, Nb shows an enrichment in the corrosion layer of 
Alloy 718 and therefore does not seem to be preferably attacked. To define the origin of the different 
intergranular attack of the four investigated alloys under ammonothermal conditions further studies 
are needed. Not only the presence of aggressive anions (e.g. Cl -) and the solubility of potential 
corrosion products d efine the corrosive behavior. The acidity, mainly defined by the added 
mineralizer in ammonothermal solutions (reduced or elevated NH 4
+-ion concentration), the physical 
state of the solution (liquid/gaseous/supercritical) and electrochemical properties als o need to be 
taken into account. In terms of NH3 density the system lies with about 14.6 mol/l between the liquid 
(ρlq,25°C,1MPa = 35 mol/l) and gaseous state ( ρg,25°C,1MPa = 0.5 mol/l) and near to the critical density of 
13.2 mol/l. This is in a range where we assume that ionic reactions are still dominant. Additionally 
and in analogy to water , the acidity of the investigated solution should be higher compared to pure 
NH3 because of the added mineralizer NH4Cl and the faster autoprotolysis reaction of NH3 at elevated 
temperature, which would support the assumption of dominant ionic reactions . However, detailed 
physical data are not or only partial available for ammonothermal solutions an d have to be 
addressed in the future to help to establish a reliable model of the corrosion mechanism for the four 
investigated alloys under ammonothermal conditions. 
3.2 Experiment with metallic Na as mineralizer 
An additional experiment in ammonobasic environment was carried out with CoWAlloy2, as this alloy 
showed the best potential in the ammonoacidic environment. The alloy sample showed no significant 
modification after the experiment in sc-NH3/Na-solution after 24 h at 550 °C and 93 MPa (compare 
Table 2). The sample gained 0.01 wt%, which results in a specific weight change of about -
1.2 µg/mm². Only a slight reddish/yellowish discoloration could be observed , see Figure 6. Those 
findings are confirmed in the morphological ( Figure 14) and elemental ( Figure 15) analysis of the 
micrographs: no layer formation, elemental modification or significant nitridation, as observed by 
Hertweck et al. [9], could be observed in this short term experiment . Addition al long -term 
experiments with repeated heating and cooling cycles should follow to make a reliable declaration 
about long-term stability in sc-NH3/Na ammonothermal solution.

4 Summary and conclusion 
In summary, it can be noted that all alloys show a severe corrosive attack after experiment s in 
sc-NH3/NH4Cl. A formation of a passivation layer could not be observed, in fact the corrosion layer is 
very unstable and porous. Strong intergranular attack was observed for all alloys except Alloy 282. 
Cracks pervade the layer of CoWAlloy1&2 and Alloy 282. The main alloying elements Ni, Co, and Fe 
that were already found to be unstable in sc-NH3/NH4Cl in their pure state [15], were not stabilized in 
their alloy. Only in the case of CoWAlloy2, which showed the lowest specific weight change and thus 
the best potential of the investigated alloys in ammonoacidic solution , could an enrichment of Ni in 
the layer be observed. CoWAlloy2 is the alloy with the highest W content. In the case of the Ni -base 
alloys a high Fe content is unfavorable. With additional alloying of ternary Co-W-Al superalloys, the 
investigated CoWAlloys improved the oxidation resistance and the mechanical properties. 
Nonetheless, the corrosion resistance has to be further addressed in order to use those alloys in the 
acidic ammonothermal environment. However, CoWAlloy2 proved good stability in a short -term 
experiment in sc-NH3/Na.
