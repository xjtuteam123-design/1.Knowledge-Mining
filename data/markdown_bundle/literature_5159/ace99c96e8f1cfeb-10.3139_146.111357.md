Microstructural evolution and creep
of Fe – Al – Ta alloys
The microstructural evolution in Fe – Al – Ta alloys contain-
ing 23 – 31 at.% Al and 1.5 – 2.2 at.% Ta has been studied in
the temperature range 650 – 750 8C by annealing for 1, 10,
100 and 1 000 h. The experiments confirm that in this tem-
perature range the precipitation of the stable hexagonal
C14 Laves phase is preceded by formation of coherent, me-
tastable L2 1 Heusler phase precipitates within the Fe – Al
matrix. However, precipitates of C14 are observed after
much shorter annealing times than previously assumed.
Creep strength increases substantially with increasing Al
content of the alloys because the solid solubility for Ta in
the Fe – Al matrix increases with increasing Al content and
solid-solution hardening contributes substantially to the ob-
served high creep strength. It may therefore be that the
microstructural changes during creep have no noticeable ef-
fect on creep strength.
Keywords: Intermetallics; Microstructure; Precipitation
kinetics; Mechanical properties; Creep
1. Introduction
Fe – Al based alloys have for a long time been considered as
replacements for high-alloyed steels due to their outstand-
ing corrosion and wear resistance [1 – 3]. However, insuffi-
cient strength at high temperatures inhibited a wider use of
these alloys up to now [4, 5]. Limited ductility at ambient
temperature seems to be a minor issue, because Fe – Al al-
loys are less brittle than other intermetallic alloys, and
therefore, for example, machining using standard equip-
ment is usually possible [6 – 10].
Regarding strength at high temperatures, recently a num-
ber of alloying concepts for Fe – Al alloys have been devel-
oped by which the strength of these alloys can be markedly
increased [11 – 14]. Specifically, alloys with coherent preci-
pitates of the L2 1 ordered Heusler phase Fe 2XAl ( X = Ta,
Nb, Ti, V. . .) show high strength and creep resistance up to
800 8C [15 – 22]. These microstructures are comparable to
those observed in Fe – Al – Ni(–X) alloys with coherent B2
NiAl + bcc or fcc phases [23 – 29] or the familiar c + c’ mi-
crostructures of the Ni-based superalloys, which are essen-
tial for the strength of the respective alloys.
Because of their high strength and good forgeability,
Fe – Al – Ta alloys are currently being investigated for the
production of forged steam turbine blades [30, 31]. In these
alloys with about 25 at.% Al the Fe – Al matrix
1 is strength-
ened by nanometre-sized coherent precipitates of the Heus-
ler phase Fe 2TaAl (L2 1) [31, 32]. However, L2 1 forms me-
tastably in these alloys and is replaced by the stable Laves
phase (Fe,Al) 2Ta (hexagonal C14) during prolonged an-
nealing [16].
The microstructural evolution and creep of Fe – Al – Ta
alloys have been studied before, however on alloys of quite
varying compositions [16, 33 – 35]. Therefore, specifically
changes in the microstructure in dependence of composi-
tion and its influence on creep are not well understood and
the authors stated that further investigation is needed to
clarify the kinetics of L2 1-Heusler phase precipitation [16,
32]. Therefore, the present systematic study of the time–
temperature–transformation (TTT) behaviour and its effect
on creep has been performed.
2. Experimental procedure
Several alloys with Al contents between 23 – 31 at.% and
1.5 – 2.2 at.% Ta were produced by vacuum induction melt-
ing under argon and their actual compositions were estab-
lished by wet chemical analysis. Microstructures of the al-
loys in the as-cast condition and after heat treatment
between 600 and 750 8C for 1, 10, 100 and 1 000 h were ex-
amined under a light optical microscope and scanning elec-
tron microscope (SEM; Jeol JSM–6490, Jeol JSM–6500F;
Japan). The latter SEM was equipped with an energy-dis-
persive spectrometer (EDS; EDAX Gemini; USA). For
transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Phillips CM20;
The Netherlands) at 200 kV thin foils were prepared by
electro polishing at –30 8C in a solution of 30 vol.% HNO 3
and 70 vol.% of methanol. Chemical compositions of indi-
vidual phases were established by wavelength-dispersive
spectrometry (WDS) carried out on an electron probe mi-
croanalyser (EPMA; JEOL JXA–8100; Japan). Measure-
ments were performed at 15 kV, 20 nA and the pure ele-
ments were used as standards. For establishing the
composition of a phase, at least 12 individual measure-
ments were performed. Crystallographic structures of the
phases were established by X-ray diffraction (XRD) of me-

According to the present results, the Ta lean phase bound-
ary of C14 at 750 8C is at about 24 at.% Ta, which is some-
what lower than data reported for 800 8C [16, 39]. The solid
solubility of Ta in Fe – Al is limited. However, when the
structure changes from disordered aFe,Al (A2) to B2 or-
dered FeAl with increasing Al content at about 25 at.% Al
at 750 8C, the solid solubility increases from 0.3 to
1.9 at.% Ta in the investigated alloys. This observation is
in accordance with previous data for 800 – 1 150 8C, which
showed that the solid solubility for Ta increases in Fe – Al
with increasing Al content up to 40 at.% Al [16, 39].
In view of the above phase relations the microstructural
evolution with increasing annealing time (Fig. 1) can be ex-
plained as follows: In the as-cast alloy (and still after 1 h an-
nealing) the Fe – Al matrix is supersaturated with Ta. With-
in larger Fe – Al grains, micro-segregation of Ta is observed
(Fig. 1a). Supersaturation of the Fe – Al matrix with Ta dur-
ing cooling leads to the precipitation of small, oriented par-
ticles of a Ta rich phase below 800 8C. However, instead of
C14, which according to the phase diagram investigations is
the stable phase [16, 39], the metastable phase L2 1 precipi-
tates. The reason for this is that the lattices of the Fe – Al
matrix – A2 or B2, depending on temperature and composi-
tion – are crystallographically very similar to the L2 1 struc-
ture, i. e. the structures have the same cubic lattice with a
similar lattice parameter but differ only in the ordering of
the Fe and Al atoms. Therefore, L2 1 can precipitate coher-
ently (Figs. 1c and d, 3) on the (001) plains of the Fe – Al
matrix, forming small \chains" parallel to the <001> direc-
tion of the matrix [31], which is easier than the incoherent
precipitation of C14.
The precipitation of coherent L2 1 precipitates within a
Fe – Al matrix has been studied using TEM in detail for
Fe – Al – Nb [17, 18, 20] and Fe – Al – Ti [38] alloys. In the
initial stages the L21 precipitates are too small for direct ob-
servation by conventional TEM but their presence is indi-
cated by a fringe contrast, which results from the coherency
strain fields surrounding the particles [36]. After annealing
at 650 – 750 8C for about 10 h the precipitates have coar-
sened so much that they are easily visible in the TEM. The
precipitates then have a size of about 100 nm and are
aligned in chains of several hundred nm parallel to the
<001> direction of the matrix [18]. The formation of the
chains maybe explained by strain field effects on precipita-
tion [17]. The shape of the L2 1 precipitates has been var-
iously described as needles/discs or cuboids [17, 18, 20,
38]. The initial precipitate will start as disc/plate [36] which
then will coarsen to a cuboid shape by Ostwald ripening.
However, a disc cut perpendicularly will have a needle
shape appearance while if cut under a shallow angle may
appear more cuboid. In summary, the above observations
on Fe – Al – Nb and Fe – Al – Ti alloys are in full agreement
with the precipitation of L2 1 in the present Fe – Al – Ta al-
loys.
On prolonged annealing, C14 finally forms. Because the
grain boundaries provide favourable sites for nucleation,
precipitation of C14 starts at the grain boundaries (Fig. 1c).
The Ta is supplied by dissolution of L2 1 (Fig. 1c and d).
Therefore, diffusion of Ta in the Fe – Al matrix is the rate
determining step for the dissolution/precipitation kinetics
[16]. As C14 forms predominantly at the grain boundaries
– there are hardly any precipitates of C14 within the Fe –
Al grains even after annealing for 1 000 h – the process of
dissolution of the L2 1 phase/precipitation of C14 slows
down continuously with increasing width of the depletion
zone parallel to the grain boundaries, in which L2 1 disso-
lutes (Fig. 1c and d). Within the Fe – Al grains, L21 coarsens
on prolonged annealing through Ostwald ripening (Fig. 1d)
[16].
The above kinetics change substantially by adding a
small amount of boron. Figure 6 shows SEM-BSE micro-
graphs of the alloy Fe-25.8Al-1.5Ta-0.05B. In the as-cast
state (Fig. 6a) a few precipitates are visible within the
Fe – Al grains and at the grain boundaries. As they are too
few and too small for XRD and EPMA, respectively, it can
only be assumed that these are borides and possibly a few
C14 precipitates. After annealing at 700 8C for 100 h
(Fig. 6b) large, incoherent precipitates are visible in the
Fe – Al matrix and along grain boundaries. According to
EPMA, these precipitates are C14 (Table 2). Apparently,
the precipitates that were already present in the as-cast state
acted as nucleation sites for the Laves phase and therefore
no metastable formation of L2 1 Heusler phase was ob-
served. This supports the above finding, that the absence
of favourable nucleation sites for C14 leads to metastable
formation of L2 1.

tem is comparable to that in the Fe – Al – Nb system, where
metastable precipitation of L2 1 also precedes the formation
of the stable C14 phase [17, 18, 40]. This behaviour is dif-
ferent from that in the Fe – Al – X systems with X = Ti, V,

Based on the results shown in Table 1 and additional data
from the literature [16], a time–temperature–transition
(TTT) diagram has been established for the two alloys
Fe-25.7Al-1.6Ta and Fe-25.9Al-1.6Ta, i. e. of nearly identi-
cal composition (Fig. 7). According to the data of [16], L2 1
only forms at temperatures below 800 8C. At higher tem-
peratures the stable C14 phase forms. Below 800 8C meta-
stable L2 1 initially forms which is then slowly replaced by
the stable C14 phase. According to Fig. 7, this substitution
proceeds faster with increasing temperature. However,
though the C14 phase precipitates, L2 1 persists for a very
long time. Even after the longest heat treatments of several
1 000 h, precipitates of L2 1 are still present in the alloys
(Fig. 7).
The TTT diagram basically confirms the one by Risanti
and Sauthoff [16]. However, according to the present data,
the precipitation of C14 starts earlier at lower temperatures
than suggested in [16], e. g. at 650 8C at about 100 h instead
at about 2 000 h. One reason for this difference could be that
the TTT diagram in [16] was set up from Fe – Al – Ta alloys
of varying compositions. Comparison of the TTT diagram
in Fig. 7 with all data compiled in Table 1 shows, that for
most of the other alloys with different Al and Ta contents
the diagram does not predict the actually observed phases.
The reason for this are variations in the compositions of
the stable phases which yield different extents of supersa-
turation on cooling, which is a decisive factor for the forma-
tion of metastable phases.
3.2. Effect of the microstructural changes on creep
For the envisaged application of steam turbine blades, the
strength of the alloy under high stresses is a key property.
While it has been established that creep strength is suffi-
cient for alloys tested in the as-cast condition and after for-
ging [31], it is necessary to investigate whether the above
continuous changes in the microstructure, i. e. dissolution
of coherent L2 1 precipitates in the Fe – Al matrix and preci-
pitation of C14 along grain boundaries, influence the creep
behaviour. Therefore, a couple of alloys were heat treated
at 700 8C for 1 000 h and then creep tested in compression.

Therefore, the increase in creep strength should be attrib-
uted to increased solid solution hardening. This assumption
is supported by evaluation of the creep exponent n from the
slopes of the data in Fig. 9 according to Norton’s power law
for creep. For the alloy with 23 at.% Al the stress exponent
n is about 10. Such high values are usually attributed to
creep governed by fine precipitates, where the small free
distances between particles give rise to a threshold stress
for creep [46]. In contrast, the alloy with 31 at.% Al shows
only an n value of about 4, which is typical for solid solu-
tion strengthened Fe – Al alloys, which deform by disloca-
tion creep through glide and climb of dislocations [47 –
49]. Interestingly, the alloys with about 25 at.% Al show
an intermediate behaviour. At very low secondary creep
rates (10 –9 –1 0–8 s–1) their stress exponent is about 5, while
at higher creep rates the stress exponent increases to about
8. This can be interpreted as solid solution hardening is the
main factor determining the creep behaviour at low defor-
mation rates (low creep rates), while at higher deformation
rates the precipitates are the rate determining obstacles.
In view of the above, the creep strength depending on
composition should be reconsidered.
In order to check whether solid-solution hardening contri-
butes substantially to the high observed creep strength, the
Ta content of the Fe – Al matrix of the samples annealed at
700 8C for 1 000 h was established by EPMA (Table 2). With
increasing Al content of the alloys the Ta content in the Fe –
Al matrix increases from 0.3 to 1.5 at.% Ta, i. e. in accor-
dance with data established at 750 8C (Fig. 5). Specifically,
the Ta content in the Fe – Al matrix of the alloys with 23, 26
and 28 – 31 at.% Al is about 0.3, 0.6 and 1.4 at.%, respec-
tively at 700 8C (Table 2). As the creep strength of the alloys
Fe-28.2Al-2.1Ta and Fe-30.9Al-1.5Ta is the same and the Ta
content in the Fe – Al matrix in these alloys is the same at the
creep temperature, the increase in creep strength should
rather relate to an increase in the Ta content in the matrix
than to the increase in the Al content of the alloys.
The above results are corroborated by the comparison of
the creep behaviour of alloys containing 1.5 and about
2.2 at.% Ta (Fig. 10). The two pairs of alloys with about
the same Al content but varying Ta content show the same
creep behaviour, although the alloys with higher Ta content
have a considerably higher volume fraction of precipitates.
This is in line with the above finding that solid-solution
hardening is the main factor determining the creep behav-
iour.
4. Conclusions
Although previously extensively studied, the present sys-
tematic study of Fe – Al – Ta alloys adds to their understand-
ing. Based on a new partial isothermal section at 750 8C, the
microstructural evolution in the Fe – Al – Ta alloys during
progressive annealing is satisfactorily explained. Precipita-
tion of the metastable L2 1 Heusler phase precedes that of
the stable C14 Laves phase if no nucleation sites are avail-
able. This was also verified by annealing a boron-contain-
ing alloy, which already contained precipitates in the as-
cast state and where no formation of metastable L2 1 on an-
nealing was observed.
Compared to other Fe – Al alloys, the investigated alloys
have a very high creep strength which is not noticeably af-
fected by the microstructural changes occurring during
creep. Microstructural analysis and evaluation of the stress
exponent n reveals that solid solution hardening substan-
tially contributes to the high creep strength. It may there-
fore be that the concurrent dissolution of L2 1 and precipita-
tion of C14 have no effect on creep. Although an apparent
increase in creep strength is observed when the overall Al
content of the alloys increases, it is not the increase in Al
but the Ta solid solubility in the Fe – Al matrix which
causes the increase in creep strength.
