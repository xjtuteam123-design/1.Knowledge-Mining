Highlights 
 Effect of Gd on cyclic oxidation behavior of Ni-based superalloy is studied. 
 Gd reduces the oxidation rate and prevent spallation of oxide scale. 
 Gd is distributed uniformly in the depth direction throughout the oxide scale. 
 No segregation of Gd at the oxide/metal interface is found. 
 Addition of 0.04 wt% Gd does not deteriorate creep life of the alloy. 
 
 
ABSTRACT 
 
The oxidation resistance and creep property of alumina -forming Ni-based super alloys with 
varying amounts of Gd are studied at 1100 ℃ in ambient air. Regardless of the Gd content, 
from the outermost surface inwards, a typical oxide scale consists primarily of NiO, spinel, 
spinel with oxide particles containing refractory elements, and Al2O3. However, spallation and 
abnormal growth of the scale are observed in alloys without Gd, and Gd addition significantly 
improves oxidation resistance. The creep rupture life is almost identical for the alloys with 0.04 
wt.% Gd and those without Gd. The addition of Gd improves alloy oxidation resistance without 
compromising creep property.

1. Introduction 
 
Increasing the turb ine inlet temperature (TIT) is critical for achieving high-efficiency and 
low-emission gas turbines. The increase in TIT has been driven by the development of 
materials that can be used at high temperature s. Therefore, the development of materials with 
high-temperature capabilities is the key to higher TITs. Although mechanical properties such 
as high-temperature strength, creep resistance, and fatigue resistance are critical considerations 
in developing materials for gas turbines (especially blade materials), oxidation resistance is an 
equally important property to consider. Oxidation resistance becomes more significant in 
trailing edges where the thickness is thin, and the cooling holes are unprotected by the coating. 
In general, the addition of Cr, Al , and Si enhances oxidation resistance [1 –5]. However, 
increase in the proportion of these elements leads to changes in microstructure such as γ' 
volume fraction and phase stability, such that there can be a significant deterioration in 
mechanical properties [6–11]. Alloying with a reactive element (RE) is a solution to this 
problem. Oxidation resistance is improved by the addition of a RE in the order of tens to 
hundreds of ppm. Because the addition amount required to improve oxidation resistance is very 
small, the effect on the mechanical properties is minimal. 
The beneficial effect of RE s on oxidation resistance reported in the literatu re can be 
classified roughly into three categories. First, the RE facilitates selective oxidation of Cr and 
Al and suppresses the formation of transient oxides [12,13]. The RE oxide particles formed at 
the initial stage of oxidation provide heterogeneous nucleation sites for Cr2O3 and Al2O3 [14,15] 
and lower the oxygen partial pressure at the interface, such that the formation of transient 
oxides is inhibited. 
Second, the RE reduces the oxidation rate by altering the diffusion process across the oxide 
scale. RE atoms segregate at the grain boundaries of oxides and impede diffusion along the 
grain boundary [16,17]. Furthermore, RE atoms also segregate at the oxide/metal interface and 
prohibit the annihilation of cation vacancy at the interface [18,19]. 
Third, the RE keeps the oxide scale from spalling. It has been proposed that RE oxides 
increase the adhesion strength between the metal and its oxide scale by absorbing and removing 
vacancies and impurities such as S and P from the interface [20,21]. The formati on of oxide

reduced oxidation rate caused by the RE effect also lowers the growth stress that causes

La, Y, and Ce are the most common RE s used commercially, and their effects have been 
extensively studied and reported in the literature [12,13,16,25,26]. The beneficial effect of Gd 
on oxidation resistance has also been investigated by other researchers [26–33]. Wei found that 
Gd reduces growth rate and growth stress by lowering Cr diffusion across the scale in a Fe -
27Cr-1.3Gd alloy [26]. Nagai reported that Gd 2O3 improves oxidation resistance and 
suppresses spallation via the doping effect of Gd2O3 on Cr2O3 in a Ni-15Cr alloy with 1 wt.% 
Gd2O3 dispersion [29]. Nagai also proposed that in a Fe-20Cr-0.7Gd alloy, the dissolution of a 
RE stabilises the Cr 2O3 scale, and internal oxide layers induced by the RE act as a diffu sion 
barrier [31]. Guo found that Gd suppresses scale rumpling and modifies the oxide scale 
properties via segregation of Gd at the oxide grain boundaries in NiAl with 0.04 at.% Gd [30]. 
However, most of these studies were conducted in simple binary systems such as Fe-Cr, Ni-Cr, 
and Ni-Al [26-31], with others using stainless steel [32,33]. However, in many of these studies, 
Gd was applied as a coating [27,28,32,33]. Studies on the effect of Gd as an alloying element 
on the oxidation of Ni-based superalloys are lacking in the literature. Moreover, applying a RE 
oxide coating and adding about 1 wt.% of the RE are impractical. 
In this study, the effect of 0.01–0.2 wt.% Gd on the cyclic oxidation behaviour of an alumina-
forming Ni-based superalloy was investigated at 1100 ℃ in ambient air. The optimum amount 
of Gd for enhanced oxidation resistance was also examined, on which there are very few studies 
in the literature. Weight changes were measured during a cyclic oxidation experiment to 
evaluate the oxidation be haviour. The microstructure of the oxide scale after the cyclic 
oxidation experiment was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM). Secondary 
ion mass spectroscopy (SIMS) and X -ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were used to 
observe the distribution of Gd in the depth direction. The effect of Gd on the creep property 
was also investigated. 
 
2. Materials and Methods 
 
To examine the effect of alloying Gd on the cyclic oxidation behavio ur of a Ni -based

superalloy, Ni -based superalloys were prepared with nine different Gd content. The 
compositions of the specimens are presented in Table 1. To minimise compositional differences 
between the specimens, except for the experimentally varied Gd, an alloy ingot with a base 
composition that does not contain any Gd was prepared first, cut out , and then remelted with 
different amounts of Gd. The 5 kg base ingot was prepared via vacuum induction melting with 
elements of 99.95% purity or more. The base ingot was cut into 100 g pieces , which were 
remelted after adding a different amount of Gd to each piece via vacuum arc remelting. The 
Gd-free specimen was also remelted via vacuum arc remelting. All specimens were 
homogenised at 1320 ℃ for 10 h and then air-cooled. 
After the heat treatment, the specimens for the cyclic oxidation experiment were cut via 
wire-cutting into discs 10 mm in diameter and 3 mm in thickness. Both sides of the specimens 
were ground with 1000-grit SiC papers. The specimens were then cleaned with ethanol and 
acetone before commencing the experi ment. Each oxidation cycle comprised 60 min of 
exposure at 1100 ℃ in a tubular furnace and 30 min of cooling in open air. The details of the 
cyclic oxidation experiment and the actual temperature profile during the cyclic oxidation 
experiment were adopted from an earlier study [34]. 
The experiment was interrupted at specific cycles for weighing. Naturally spalled oxide scale 
was omitted from the weight . Three identical specimens were tested, and their weights were 
averaged to reduce experimental error. The average weight changes were divided by the initial 
surface area of the specimen to obtain the weight change per unit area (mgcm-2). 
After cyclic oxidation , t he oxide scale was observed via backscattered electron imaging 
(BEI) under an SEM, using a JEOL SM-54060RBEI detector fitted to a JEOL JSM-7001F, at 
an acceleration voltage of 15 kV . To identify the oxide phases , X-ray diffraction ( XRD) 
patterns for specimens with and without Gd were obtained using a Rigaku D/Max -2500VL 
diffractometer with CuKα radiation source at 40 kV and 20 mA over a 2θ range of 20 –90°. 
Depth profiles of the alloying elements including Gd were analysed via SIMS (CAMECA IMS-
6F), with the specimen containing 0.04 wt.% Gd oxidised for 10 min. For metallic elements, 
an oxygen source was used as the primary ion (impact energy: 7.5 keV, current: 200 nA, raster 
size: 180 x 180 µm 2, analysis area: 30 µm) . For oxygen, a c aesium source was used as the 
primary ion (impact energy: 15 keV, current: 20 nA, raster size: 150 x 150 µm2, analysis area: 
33 µm). XPS (VG scientific ESCALAB 250) was used to confirm the presence of Gd in the 
oxide scale and to measure the Gd content quantitatively. Monochromatic Al X-ray with an 
acceleration voltage of 15 kV and a beam current of 10 mA (beam size: 500 µm) was used for

3. Results and Discussion 
 
3.1. Weight Changes during Cyclic Oxidation 
 
The weight change behaviours of the specimens tested during cyclic oxidation at 1100 ℃ are 
presented in Fig. 2(a). The average and standard deviation of the weight changes for three 
identical specimens are presented as markers and error bars, respectively. The weight changes 
in the literature for TMS-196, the fifth-generation single -crystal superalloy with similar 
amounts of Re and Ru content as KSC-06 [35], are also presented in Fig. 2(a) for comparison. 
Although the weight loss in the Gd-free KSC-06 was somewhat smaller than that of TMS-196, 
KSC-06 experienced continuous weight loss via spallation of its oxide scale a fter the initial 
weight gain. The specimens with 0.01–0.2 wt.% Gd showed no or only a little weight decrease 
for up to 50 cycles, indicating that spallation of the oxide scale was significantly suppressed 
by the addition of Gd. 
Fig. 2(b) presents the weight changes after 50 cycles of the oxidation experiment categorised 
by Gd content. The weight change of the specimens with 0.02–0.08 wt.% Gd were smaller than 
that of the other specimens, and no apparent spallation was observed in these specimens. 
Although some spallation occurred in the specimens with 0.01 w.t% Gd, they recorded a greater 
weight gain than the specimens with 0.02–0.08 wt.% Gd, indicating that the specimens with 
0.01 wt.% Gd had a relatively faster oxidation rate than specimens with 0.02–0.08 wt.% Gd. It

appears that a 0.01 wt.% Gd addition was insufficient to suppress spallation or to reduce the 
oxidation rate sufficiently. Specimens with 0.1 wt.% or more Gd also recorded greater weight 
gain than the specimens with 0.02 –0.08 wt .% Gd. Apparently, the oxidation rate increase s 
when the Gd content is more than 0.1%. 
Fig. 3 presents the weight change, for each Gd content, at the 2nd cycle, which was the first 
weight measurement. Because the effect of spallation on the weight change is relatively small 
at the initial stage, this provides information on the initial oxidation rate. As the Gd content 
increases to 0.1 wt.%, the initial weight gain also tends to increase and becomes saturated after 
0.1 wt.%. This trend in the initial weight gain indicates that the initial oxidation rate increases 
with increasing Gd content to a certain level. Gd accelerates oxide formation during the initial 
stage by providing heterogeneous nucleation sites around Gd oxide particles [14,15]. The 
oxidation of Gd itself also attributes to greater initial weight gain. When the Gd content was 
more than 0.1 wt.%, the oxidation rate in the later stage was also increased, resulting in greater 
weight gain (Fig. 2(b)). 
 
3.2. Microstructure of the Oxide Scale 
 
Fig. 4 shows the microstructure of the oxide scale formed on the Gd-free specimens after 50 
cycles via BEI mode. Fig. 4(a) and (b) show the typical microstructure of the oxide scale. From 
the outermost surface inwards, the oxide scale consist s of a NiO layer, a Ni(Al,Cr) 2O4 spinel 
layer, a Ni(Al,Cr)2O4 layer with oxide particles containing refractory elements (a mixed oxide 
layer), and an Al2O3 layer. The oxide particles containing refractory elements such as Re, Ru, 
W, and Ta appear bright in BEI because these are high-atomic-number elements. The total 
thickness of the oxide scale was approximately 6 μm , and n eedle-like TiN particles were 
formed beneath the oxide scale. A γ’-depleted denuded zone approximately 14 μm thick formed 
via depletion of Al was also observed. Spallation and crack formation were frequently observed 
across the entire oxide scale, with most cracks propagat ing along the oxide/metal interface. 
Vertical cracks through the oxide scale and horizontal cracks along the oxide particles 
containing refractory elements w ere also observed. Apparently, N readily penetrates through 
the cracks and forms AlN, which accelerates the depletion of Al. Similar microstructures were 
observed in a previous study [34]. 
Abnormal growth of the oxide scale was often observed in the Gd-free specimens, as shown 
in Fig. 4(c) and (d). In some areas , as shown in Fig. 4(c), the structure of the oxide scale is

similar to that shown in Fig. 4(a) , except for an approximately 14 μm thickness, which is 2.3 
times thicker. Due to the thermal stress cau sed by the thick oxide scale, delamination of the 
oxide scale from the metal and formation of vertical cracks occurred in these areas. In other 
areas, as shown in Fig. 4(d), an oxide scale with a thickness of 30 μm or more was formed. 
NiO and mixed oxide l ayers constituted the bulk of the oxide scale. This kind of thick oxide

interface drops below the level required to maintain a stable Al2O3 layer. Then, rapid oxidation 
toward the bulk occurs until the Al concentration at the oxide/metal interface reaches a point 
at which a stable Al2O3 layer can be formed. 
Fig. 5 shows the microstructure of the oxide scale formed on the specimens with Gd content 
after 50 cycles via BEI mode. For the specimens with Gd content, the oxide scale was uniformly 
formed across the entire surface. Similar to the Gd-free specimen, from the outermost surface 
inwards, the oxide scale is comprised of a NiO layer, a Ni(Al,Cr) 2O4 spinel layer, a mixed 
oxide layer, and a n Al2O3 layer. However, AlN was not detected in the specimens with Gd 
content. The thickness of the denuded zone was 2 –3 μm, which is much thinner than the 
denuded zone in the Gd-free specimens. Abnormal growth of the oxide scale was not observed 
in the specimens with Gd content. Spallation of the oxide scale and crack formation along the 
oxide/metal interface were observed only in the specimen with 0.01 wt.% Gd (Fig. 5(c)). Gd 
oxide formation was not detected in any specimen. The bright particles observed in the Al 2O3 
layer of the specimen with 0.2 wt.% Gd are oxides containing refractory elements (Fig. 5(d)). 
With increasing Re and Ru content, the oxides appear brighter in BEI.

Fig. 7 shows the XRD patterns for specimens with and without Gd obtained after 50 cycles 
of the oxidation experiment. Diffraction peaks for NiO, spinel, CrTaO 4, and Al 2O3 can be 
observed. The unidentified peaks in Fig. 7 appear to be from the oxide particles of refractory 
elements. For the specimen with Gd, the peak intensity for NiO, spinel, and CrTaO4 was strong, 
while that of Al 2O3 was weak (Fig. 7(a)). Because the oxide scale of the specimens with Gd 
was almost intact, the uniform layers of NiO and spinel were maintained after the oxidation 
experiment (Fig. 5). Due to the limited penetration depth of X -ray signals, the peaks for the 
outer part of the oxide scale were strong and those for the inner part were weak. For the Gd-
free specimen, a relatively intact part of the oxide scale was chosen for obtaining the XRD 
pattern (Fig. 7(b)). Compared to the pattern of the specimen with Gd, the peak intensity for 
NiO was stronger and that for Al2O3 was weaker. This is due to the formation of a thick NiO 
layer in the Gd-free specimen (Fig. 4(d)). The XRD patterns are in good agreement with results 
from the SEM observations. 
From these results, it can be concluded that the addition of 0.02 wt.% Gd or more suppresses 
spallation and reduce s oxidation rate, such that a uniform Al 2O3 layer was maintained even 
after 50 cycles of 1 h exposure at 1100 ℃. Because the protective Al2O3 layer was maintained, 
the AlN formation accelerating Al depletion was also suppressed. The thickness of the denuded 
zone was reduced by the addition of Gd through the decrease in Al consumption via oxidation. 
The reduced thi ckness of the denuded zone slows down degradation of the mechanical 
properties of thin parts such as trailing edges during operation.

thickness of the oxide scale varied with Gd content. The thickness of each oxide layer in Fig. 
5 was analysed using ImageJ software and are presented in Table 3. For the Gd-free specimen, 
the measurement was impossible due to the irregular ity of the oxide scale. The thickness of

thicknesses were greater in the specimens with 0.02 wt .%, 0.01 wt.%, and 0.2 wt.% Gd. The

Based on these results, the optimum amount of Gd for the alloy in this study is 0.02–0.08 wt.%. 
 
3.3. Distribution of Gd in the Depth Direction in Initial Oxidation 
 
Fig. 8 shows the SIMS depth profiles for the specimen with 0.04 wt.% Gd after 10 min of 
oxidation at 1100 ℃. The oxidation time was chosen based on the depth limit of the SIMS 
analysis. Because there were no standard specimens, quantitative analysis was impossible. The 
thickness of the oxide scale is estimated from the oxygen curve to be about 1.5 μm. Due to the 
tailing effect induced by the rough oxide surface and irregular thickness of each oxide layer, it 
was impossible to identify the exact location of each oxide layer. The oxide scale primarily 
comprised an outer Ni(Al,Cr) 2O4 spinel layer and an inner Al 2O3 layer. Gd is distributed 
uniformly in the depth direction throughout the oxide scale. Segregation of Gd at the 
oxide/metal interface was not detected. Gd appears to affect diffusion behavio ur at the grain 
boundaries of the oxide scale and to increase plasticity of the oxide scale . However, no effect 
on the oxide/metal interface was detected. Pint and Alexander reported the segregation of Gd 
at the grain boundary of Al 2O3 [36]. Rumpling of the oxide scale due to growth stress and 
thermal stress was observed in all specimens (Fig. 4 and Fig. 5). The oxide scales formed on 
the Gd-free specimens and specimens with insufficient Gd content were cracked and spalled 
by the stress. However, the oxide scales formed on the specimens with sufficient Gd content 
were not cracked and maintained adhesion to the metal. The addition of Gd appears to increase 
the plasticity of the oxide scale, reduces the stress , and inhibits spallation of the oxide scale 
[13,24]. 
XPS analysis was performed on the same specimen used in the SIMS analysis to confirm 
the presence of Gd in the oxide scale and to measure the amount of Gd quantitatively. Fig. 9 
shows the Gd3d core -level XPS spectra and results of the quantitative analysis of the major 
elements comprising the oxide scale at depths of 30 nm and 200 nm from the surface. The Ni 
content decreased and the Al content increased in the inner part of the oxide scale. For Gd, the 
content was approximately 0.2 wt .% regardless of the depth, which is higher than the bulk

3.4. Effect of Gd Addition on Creep Property 
 
To investigate possible adverse effects of Gd addition on the creep property, the creep rupture 
life of single crystals without Gd and with 0.04 wt .% Gd were evaluated at 1100 ℃ under a 
stress of 137 MPa, 1050 ℃ under a stress of 245 MPa, and 950 ℃ under a stress of 392 MPa. 
The misorientation angle was measured using EBSD and the ang les are presented with the 
creep test results in Fig. 10. The misorientation angles of the specimens were within 6.5° , 
except for one Gd-free specimen that was 11.1°. Fig. 10(a–c) presents the creep-rupture curves. 
The rupture life of both specimens was almost identical for 1377 h without Gd and 1386 h with 
Gd at 1100 ℃ under a stress of 137 MPa (Fig. 10(a)). The rupture life of the fifth-generation 
single-crystal alloy TMS-196 in the literature [35] is also indicated in Fig. 10(a) for comparison. 
The alloy used in this study shows superior creep-rupture properties than TMS-196 at this creep 
condition. The rupture life of both specimens was also almost identical at 1050 ℃ under a stress 
of 245 MPa (Fig. 10(b)). There was a small difference in the rupture life of the two specimens 
at 950 ℃ under a stress of 392 MPa (Fig. 10(c)). However, the difference did not result from 
the addition of Gd but from the relatively large misorientation angle of the Gd-free specimen 
[37]. Fig. 10(d) presents the Larson-Miller plot of specimens with and without Gd, the fifth-
generation single-crystal superalloy TMS -196 [35], and the second-generation single-crystal 
superalloy CMSX -4 [38]. The creep -rupture properties of the alloy used in this study are 
superior to that of CMSX-4, and appear to be slightly superior or similar to that of TMS-196. 
Because the difference in creep rupture life between specimens with and without Gd is 
negligible, the effect of a small amount of Gd addition on the creep property appears to be 
insignificant. 
 
4. Conclusions 
 
To investigate the effect of Gd addition on oxidation resistance and creep property, cyclic 
oxidation experiments and creep -rupture tests were performed using alumina -forming Ni -
based superalloys containing 0–0.2 wt.% Gd. Oxidation resistance was evaluated by measuring 
Journal Pre-proof

## Page 12

11 
weight changes during cyclic o xidation at 1100 ℃ in ambient air. After 50 cycles of 1 h 
exposure, the oxide scale was observed via SEM. The Gd content and distribution in the oxide 
scale was analysed via SIMS depth profile and XPS using the specimen oxidised for 10 min. 
The creep-rupture test was performed at 1100 ℃ under a stress of 137 MPa. 
Regardless of the Gd content, the typical microstructure of the oxide scale , f rom its 
outermost surface inwards, is comprised of NiO, Ni(Al,Cr)2O4 spinel, a mixed oxide layer of 
spinel and oxide pa rticles containing refractory elements, and Al 2O3. However, the Gd-free 
specimen experienced spallation and abnormal growth of the oxide scale. 
The oxidation resistance of the alloy was enhanced most significantly by the addition of 
0.02–0.08 wt .% Gd, with a uniform and thin oxide scale formed across the entire surface. 
Spallation of the oxide scale was not observed , and the thickness of the denuded zone was 
reduced from 14 μm to 2 –3 μm due to improved oxidation resistance. When the Gd content 
was 0.01 wt.%, the beneficial effect of Gd was not sufficient to fully suppress crack formation 
and spallation of the oxide scale. However, when the Gd content was more than 0.1 wt.%, the 
oxide scale became thicker than in the specimens with 0.02–0.1 wt.% Gd. 
The difference in creep rupture life of the specimen with 0.04 wt .% Gd and the specimen 
without Gd was negligible for the temperature range of 950–1100 ℃ under the stress range of 
137–392 MPa. Therefore, it can be concluded that the effect of a small amount of Gd addition 
on creep property is insignificant. 
The addition of Gd is an effective method for improving the oxidation resistance without 
compromising the creep property of the alumina-forming Ni-based superalloy.
