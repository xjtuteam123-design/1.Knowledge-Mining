Effect of carbon content on elevated temperature stability and
tensile properties of Fe–8.5 Al Alloys
R.G. Baligidad *, A. Radhakrishna
Defence Metallurgical Research Laboratory , PO Kanchanbagh , Hyderabad 500 0058 , India
Received 12 August 1999; received in revised form 22 October 1999
Abstract
Fe–8.4Al–0.04C, Fe–8.26Al–0.46C and Fe–8.35Al–1.1C alloys were prepared by a combination of air induction melting and
electroslag remelting. The low (0.04 wt.%) carbon alloy exhibited microcracks therefore it was not studied further. The ESR ingots
of high (0.46 and 1.1 wt.%) carbon alloys exhibited a signiﬁcant amount of Fe
3AlC0.5 precipitation. The cast ESR samples of
Fe–8.26Al–0.46C and Fe–8.35Al–1.1 C were placed in a hearth furnace at 873, 1073 and 1273 K for 24 h and then furnace
cooled. The high carbon alloys do not undergo decarburization and exhibit stable microstructure up to 873 K. Decarburization
appears to be a problem only after exposure at temperatures of 1073 K and above. The ESR Fe–8.26Al–0.46C alloy exhibited
greater elongation and signiﬁcantly better elevated temperature strength up to 873 K than those reported for cast VIM low carbon
multicomponent alloy with similar Al content. This may be due to the presence of a large volume fraction of stable Fe
3AlC0.5
precipitates. All the high carbon alloys exhibit a sharp drop in strength at 873 K regardless of carbon content. These alloys are
therefore targeted for potential structural application at or below 873 K. © 2000 Elsevier Science S.A. All rights reserved.

1. Introduction
Fe–Al alloys containing 8.5–20 wt.% Al are being
developed for elevated temperature structural applica-
tions for temperatures up to 873 K [1–3]. Carbon
additions in the range of 0.074–1.1 wt.% to these alloys
have been shown to result in signiﬁcant increases in
strength as well as creep and stress rupture properties
[1–7]. These improvements in mechanical properties
have been attributed to the presence of a signiﬁcant
amount of Fe
3AlC0.5 perovskite precipitate. The stabil-
ity of these precipitates may, therefore, play an impor-
tant role in retaining improved mechanical properties.
Recently we have reported that Fe–Al alloys contain-
ing 8.35 wt.% Al and 1.1 wt.% C are prone to decarbur-
ization after elevated temperature exposure at 1273 K
[8]. This susceptibility was not observed in alloys with
higher Al content. Recently, alloys in the FeAl–C
system have also been studied by other investigators
[9–11]. In the present work we report the effect of
carbon content and temperature on stability and tensile
properties of Fe 8.5 wt.% Al alloys.
2. Experimental procedures
Melts of Fe–8.4Al–0.04C, Fe–8.26Al–0.46C and
Fe–8.35Al–1.1C (all compositions in wt.% unless oth-
erwise speciﬁed) alloys (40 kg) were prepared in a
medium frequency air induction melting (AIM) fur-
nace. Commercial aluminium and steel scrap (pure iron
for the low carbon alloy) were used as raw materials.
Melting was carried out under a ﬂux cover, using a
proprietary charging schedule. The AIM ingots were
subsequently processed through electroslag remelting
(ESR). The melting practice has been described in detail
elsewhere [12,13]. Longitudinal sections of ESR ingots
were cut using a high speed abrasive silicon carbide
cut-off wheel. For heat treatment, sections of Fe–
8.26Al–0.46C and Fe–8.35Al–1.1C ESR ingots mea-
suring 161660 mm were placed in a hearth furnace

3. Results and discussion
Optical microscopy and SEM showed that the Fe–
8.26Al–0.46C ESR ingot exhibited the presence of sec-
ond phase (Fig. 1a and b). This was identiﬁed to be
Fe
3AlC0.5 perovskite precipitate in previous studies [1].
The Fe–8.35Al–1.1C alloy exhibited higher volume
fraction of these precipitates. In the alloys containing
0.46 and 1.1 wt.% C no signiﬁcant change in mi-
crostructure was observed after heat treatment at 873
K. At 1073 K, in both the alloys, partial dissolution of
the carbide phase was observed and this was accompa-
nied by decarburization in the region adjacent to the
sample surfaces (Fig. 2a and b). At 1273 K, Fe–
8.35Al–1.1C alloy showed enhanced decarburization
(Fig. 2c) while practically no carbide was observed in
Fe–8.35Al–0.46 C alloy (Fig. 2d). After heat treatment
at 1273 K the carbon content of Fe–8.35Al–0.46C
alloy was found to be reduced to 0.019 wt.% because of
the severe decarburization. This was accompanied by a
reduction in hardness from 250 to 215 HV. The Fe–
8.4Al–0.04 C alloy exhibited a bulk hardness of 220
HV. The hardness of the decarburized alloy thus com-
pares well with that of the ESR cast low carbon alloy.
From the present work it appears that the extent of
decarburization increases signiﬁcantly with an increase
in heat treatment temperature. At elevated tempera-
tures (1073 K and above) the decarburization appears
to be aided by partial dissolution of the carbide phase
in the matrix and enhanced removal of carbon from the
surfaces. It has been reported that [15] the removal of
carbon in a Fe–5.5Al–0.55C alloy may take place by
diffusion of carbon atoms to the sample surface fol-
lowed by reaction with oxygen in the atmosphere. The
above mechanism is supported by the recent observa-
tion [8] that the resistance to decarburization depends
on the resistance to oxidation in these alloys. Decarbur-
ization was not observed after heat treatment at 1273 K
for 24 h in alloys containing 16 wt.% (or more) alu-
minium [8]. The alloys with 8.5 wt.% Al, however
exhibited partial decarburization (Fig. 2c and d). This
was attributed to a lower aluminium content and there-
fore a lower resistance to oxidation was likely in the
alloy.

study, the alloys do not undergo decarburization and
have a stable microstructure at 873 K. Decarburization
appears to be a problem only after exposure at temper-
atures of around 1073 K and above which these alloys
are unlikely to be subjected to in practice.
The low carbon alloy exhibited presence of microc-
racks and was therefore not studied further. Similar
microcracks have been observed in low carbon elec-
troslag remelted Fe–Al alloys with higher Al contents
[4] and have been attributed to (a) high levels of
residual hydrogen in the ESR ingots and (b) susceptibil-
ity to hydrogen embrittlement at low carbon contents.
At an Al content of 8.5 wt.%, wrought low carbon
Fe–Al alloys do not show susceptibility to environmen-
tal embrittlement [17] and therefore exhibit good tensile
ductility. It appears however that even at this low Al
content, the residual hydrogen picked up during melt-
ing is sufﬁcient to cause cracking in the ingots. The
high carbon alloys are free from microcracks suggesting
that addition of carbon may be necessary to process
these alloys by air induction melting.
4. Conclusions
1. At low carbon levels, the ingots contained microc-
racks. The high carbon alloys are free from them
suggesting that addition of carbon may be necessary
to process these alloys by air induction melting.
2. The ESR alloys exhibit greater elongation and sig-
niﬁcantly better elevated temperature strength than
those reported for VIM cast low carbon multicom-
ponent alloy with similar aluminium content.
3. The high carbon Fe–Al alloys containing 8.5 wt.%
Al do not undergo decarburization and exhibit a
stable microstructure up to 873 K. Decarburization
appears to be a problem only after exposure at
temperatures of 1073 K and above.
4. All the alloys exhibit a sharp drop in strength at 873
K regardless of the carbon content. These alloys are
therefore targeted for potential structural applica-
tion at or below 873 K.
