ABSTRACT 
Lifetimes of MCrAlY-type coatings can easily surpass 25 kh when the criteria of ß -depletion is used. To reduce 
experimental effort, it ’ s necessary to develop models capable of simulating interdiffusion and oxidation. In the 
present study, NiCoCrAlY coatings were high velocity oxyfuel deposited on PWA 1483, MARM247, and CMSX-4 
substrates, and samples were exposed at 900 
◦
C for 5 – 20 kh in air + 10 vol%H
2
O to study interdiffusion rates. 
Thermo-Calc/DICTRA was used to reproduce the observed differences, and microstructures were compared. 
Based on the agreement between calculated and observed rates, simulations were projected past the experi -
mental exposures, utilizing ß -depletion as lifetime criteria. 
1. Introduction 
In gas-turbines, thermal barrier coatings increase turbine inlet tem -
peratures by providing thermal insulation and imparting greater 
oxidation resistance to hot gas path components [1 – 3] . In general, TBCs 
are multilayered, comprised of a yttria stabilized zirconia (YSZ) topcoat 
that is thermally sprayed or physical vapor deposited onto an alumina 
forming MCrAlY or NiAl bondcoat, that in turn has been deposited onto 
a metallic Ni-based superalloy. Upon high temperature exposure, the 
bond coat forms an alumina thermally grown oxide (TGO), and damage 
accumulates at this interface from coefficient of thermal expansion 
mismatch over the cycling course [4,5] , eventually leading to the me -
chanical failure (spallation) [6] of the coating system. Aero gas turbines 
conditions, where duty cycles are short with high temperatures, are 
typically simulated in laboratory environments with ≥ 1100 
◦
C furnace 
cycle testing (FCT) lifetimes only occasionally surpassing 1000 h life -
times [7] . 
For power generating stationary gas turbines, where duty cycles are 
longer and lower in temperature, conditions are simulated in a labora -
tory environment with longer 900 
◦
 1000 
◦

lower temperatures, the magnitude of coefficient of thermal expansion 
(CTE) mismatch stress and rate of TGO formation are typically not large 
enough to induce coating spallation. The MCrAlY coating microstructure 
is also different at these temperatures [12] , favoring the formation of 
less ductile and non-desirable phases like γ

close packed (TCP) phases [13 – 15] . In the absence of mechanical failure 
a useful lifetime criteria is the depletion of ß -NiAl phase from the 
MCrAlY coating [16,17] . However, lifetimes can easily exceed 25 kh, 
quickly surpassing experimental scope. Therefore, it ’ s of extreme benefit 
to develop modelling techniques capable of replacing such long and 
arduous experimental efforts. 
Nesbitt developed COSIM (Coating Oxidation and Substrate Inter -

earlier formulated COSP (Cyclic Oxidation Spalling Program) [19] was 
used as a boundary condition in COSIM to account for Al depletion. 
Renusch et al. developed a software tool that allowed analysis of 
measured Al profiles for the assessment of diffusion rates and conse -
quently, lifetime predictions [20] . Eriksson et al. developed a technique 
that interfaced with Thermo-Calc/DICTRA to account for Al depletion 
and interdiffusion [21] . More recently, Pillai et al. have developed a

2. Experimental 
For fabricating coating-substrate specimens, substrates (2 mm thick, 
~16 mm diameter with chamfered edges) were grit blasted with 
alumina and then coated using a standard commercial-type, high ve -
locity oxygen fuel (HVOF) process. In all cases, the coated substrates 
were then annealed in a vacuum of 10 – 4 Pa (10 – 6 Torr) for 4 h at 
1080 
◦
C. All coatings were of the same NiCoCrAlY composition, Table 1 . 
In the case of X4 substrates, Table 1 , coatings were nominally 200 µ m 
thick. For 247 and 1483 substrates, also in Table 1 , coatings were 
nominally 180 µ m thick. 
For furnace cycle testing, specimens were placed in alumina boats, 
heated to temperature over ~4 h in an alumina tube with flowing argon, 
and then held for 500 h in flowing air with 10 ± 1%H2O. Carrier gas was 
typically flowed at 500 ml/min with distilled water atomized into the 
gas stream above its condensation temperature. Injected water was 
measured to calibrate its concentration. Samples were taken out, 
weighed, and visually inspected after each 500 h cycle. Exposures were 
carried out up to 20 kh in this manner. Due to the substrates being 
coated on only one face, mass change data was not informative of 
NiCoCrAlY oxidation and was not used to evaluate kinetics. Over the 
course of 20kh there were varying degrees of superalloy spallation that 
occurred on the uncoated faces. 
After desired exposure time was reached, specimens were mounted 
in either epoxy or Cu and then sectioned for polishing. Specimens were 
optically characterized via light microscopy. Electron probe micro -
analysis (EPMA) was performed using a JEOL model 8200. EPMA line 
scan data was only taken from the NiCoCrAlY coated X4 system, and as 
such, much of the results and discussion are focused on the X4 system. 
Modelling was performed using Thermo-Calc/DICTRA software, 
utilizing the TCNI8 and MOBNI4 databases. DICTRA calculations were 
run by approximating the coating-substrate system as a single region 
diffusion couple with a discrete composition change representing the 
interface and using the rule of mixtures as the homogenization function. 
In the case of the X4 and 247 substrate systems, only the Ni-Co-Cr-Al 
elements were considered. For 1483, an additional configuration 
including Ti was used. Only the β , γ , and γ
′
phases where considered, as 
mobility data for other phases was unavailable in MOBNI4.

As can be seen by the compositional profiles, the effects of oxidation 
and coating-substrate interdiffusion are complex. Starting at the 
ambient interface, Al is locally depleted, enriching the vicinity of the 
TGO in Co and Cr, Fig. 1 (b). There is evidence of a very minor amount of 
W having diffused from the X4 substrate and through the coating, Fig. 1 
(c). On the coating side of the coating-substrate interface, Al and Ni 
deplete, Cr and Co enrich, and Ta, W and Ti have diffused into the 
coating. On the substrate side of the interface (when O content is zero at 
~260 µ m), a Ni concentration gradient has developed, as well as minor 
Al, Co, and Cr gradients in the opposite direction. To summarize the net 
diffusion across the interface, Al, Cr, and Co have diffused into the 
substrate, while Ni, Ta, W and Ti have diffused into the coating. Diffu -
sion of Al and Cr into the substrate increase their activities at the 
interface, and combined with the much lower mobility of refractory 
elements like Mo, Re, and W, result in the enrichment of the refractory 
elements at the interface. The combined increase in Cr and Al activity 
and Mo, Re, and W concentrations results in the formation of TCP phases 
[22] . Previous calculations predicted the TCP phases in Fig. 1 to be 
σ -phase and EDS analysis indicated enrichment in Re and W [23] . As 
mobility data for Ni, Co, Cr, and Al in TCP phases was not available in 
MOBNI4, the effect of TCP phase formation on interdiffusion is not 
captured in the following simulations.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Lifetime criteria 
As noted by Naumenko et al., the absence of the ductile γ -phase in 
ternary NiCrAl coatings is undesirable as it leads to reduced lifetimes 
under thermal-mechanical fatigue conditions [12] . The two-phase β / γ 
field can be extended towards lower temperatures via the judicious 
addition of Co, which suppresses formation of γ
′
[13,15] ; as Co is 
depleted from the coating via interdiffusion γ
′
-suppression will decrease. 
However, the reduction in γ
′
- suppression due to outwardly diffusing Co 
is not detrimental to coating performance from a ß -depletion standpoint, 
as comparatively to Al the Co mobility is low and ß -depletion should be 
dominated by Al diffusion. The net diffusion behavior of Co and Cr 
appear is opposite that of Al on the coating side of the interface ( Fig. 8 ). 
In the IDZ, Al content reaches a minimum, while Co and Cr reach a 
maximum. This trend continues as the IDZ increases in width – Co and Cr 
enrich in the IDZ while Al content continually decreases. The primary 
mechanism then of ß -NiAl depletion is the outward diffusion of Al from 
the coating into the substrate. 
An additional consideration at the oxidizing interface is that the ß - 
phase can act as an Al reservoir, maintaining the concentration of Al 
high enough in the TGO vicinity to encourage further alumina formation 
[1] . Examination of the coating microstructure after 5 kh, shown for the 
X4 system in Fig. 3 (f), would at first glance indicate an extensive supply 
of ß -phase that appears suitable to maintain alumina formation up to at 
least 20 kh, Fig. 3 (h). However, the γ -phase region that forms in the TGO 
vicinity increases in Cr and Co content as exposure continues, shown via 
EPMA in Fig. 8 (d)-(f). The result is γ enrichment. In the event of local -
ized oxide/coating spallation in an actual service environment, the 
γ -phase enriched region may be detrimental to alumina formation, with 
the high Cr and Co content suppressing Al diffusion from the two-phase 
ß / γ region. Further work is needed elucidate the impact this region has 
on long term oxidation.

5. Conclusions 
Long term 900
◦
C oxidation exposures were conducted on NiC -
oCrAlY coated P1483 (1483), M247 (247), and CMSX-4 (X4). Significant 
coating recession due to interdiffusion that could not be accounted for 
via volumetric changes was observed in all systems over the course of 
5 – 20 kh of exposure. TGO growth rates and associated ß -depletion were 
not observed to change significantly over 20-kh of exposure, but in the 
case of 20-kh exposed NiCoCrAlY-1483, ß -depletion in the vicinity of the 
oxidizing interface did increase. During interdiffusion, Al was the pri -
mary diffusing species from the coating into the substrates, and lower ß - 
depletion rates correlated with higher Al content substrates. Rates of 
interdiffusion driven ß -depletion were observed to be greatest for 1483, 
followed by 247 and X4. The different interdiffusion rates were repro -
duced with Thermo-Calc/DICTRA, which only considered the 
quaternary Ni-Co-Cr-Al coating-substrate compositions for the 247 and 
X4 systems and only accounted for the ß -, γ -, and γ
′
-phases. For 1483, it 
was necessary to include Ti to obtain the necessary γ
′
superalloy 
microstructure, but Ti inclusion resulted in the most inaccurate and 
slowest interdiffusion rates amongst the systems studied. In most cases, 
simulated interdiffusion rates were faster than experimental rates due to 
the quaternary compositions likely being insufficient for accurate γ : γ
′
superalloy composition. Simulated interdiffusion was projected past the 
experimental exposures to determine coating lifetimes and indicated 
that when approximating the effects of oxidation as a constant amount 
of ß -depletion at the oxidizing interface and using complete coating ß - 
depletion as lifetime criteria, 1483 depletes first, followed by 247, then 
X4. Future modelling efforts will need to incorporate the full elemental 
composition of superalloys and be able to account for any anticipated 
changes in oxidation mechanisms associated with substrate elements 
diffusing to the vicinity of the oxidizing interface.
