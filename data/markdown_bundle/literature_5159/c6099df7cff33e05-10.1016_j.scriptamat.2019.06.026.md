Ultrastrong duplex high-entropy alloy with 2 GPa cryogenic strength
enabled by an accelerated martensitic transformation

The existing deformation-induced martensitic transformation mostly focuses on overcoming the trade-off of
cryogenic strength-ductility; however, an enhancement of cryogenic strength further is still challenging. We
present a concept to yield a cryogenic strength of 2 GPa in a duplex V10Cr10Co30Fe50 alloy. We adopt a thermody-
namic calculation to reduce the stability of metastable face-centered-cubic (FCC) matrix, signiﬁcantly promoting
the martensitic transformation. In conjunction with the chemically driven promotion, the duplex structure in-
cluding athermal body-centered-cubic (BCC) martensite enables mechanical strain partitioning to accelerate
the transformation further. Thisﬁnding could be an appropriate design strategy to develop new ultrastrong alloys
for cryogenic applications.

Development of new energy sources such as lique ﬁed natural gas
and hydrogen gas demands advanced structural materials to transport,
preserve, and utilize them safely [1–3]. Since these gases are generally
carried at cryogenic temperatures such as 111 or 20 K, designing of ma-
terials available under extreme environments is essentially needed
[1–5]. High- and medium-entropy alloys (HEAs and MEAs) of face-
centered-cubic (FCC) structure,e.g., CoCrFeMnNi and CrCoNi alloys, rep-
resentatively, have been attracted attentions due to their remarkable
mechanical performance at cryogenic temperature [ 6–10]. TWinning
Induced Plasticity (TWIP) is nominated as a primary mechanism gener-
ating excellent cryogenic strength, ductility, and fracture toughness. Re-
cently, a deformation-induced martensitic transformation and
consequent TRansformation Induced Plasticity (TRIP), which frequently
occur in metastable FCC-base stainless steels, high-Mn steels, and
Fe\\Co alloys, have been introduced into a ﬁeld of HEAs and MEAs
[2,11–25]. Though this metastability engineering deviates from the con-
cept for initially proposed HEA designs [ 20], the feasibility to adapt
them has drawn great interests for cryogenic applications.
Based on the metastable FCC, a hexagonal close-packed (HCP) mar-
tensitic transformation enables the tensile strength and ductility of
1.3 GPa and 45%, respectively, at 77 K in an Fe
49.5Mn30Co10Cr10C0.5 (at
%) interstitial HEA [ 24]. Recently, Bae et al. [ 23] reported another
fascinating transformation mechanism to show a body-centered-cubic
(BCC) transformation in an Fe x(CoNi)90-xCr10 (x = 55, 57.5, and 60)
MEA system. This system represents the tensile strength and ductility
of 1.5 GPa and 80%, respectively, at 77 K, which readily override the
properties of previous HEAs/MEAs as well as conventional steels. The
FCC to BCC transformation is worthy of notice due to its highly-
sustainable strain hardening, and consequently yields a remarkable bal-
ance of strength and ductility under cryogenic environments. This TRIP
effect can pave the way to solve a dilemma of strength-ductility trade-
off which is an essential challenge in alloy designing for engineering ap-
plications [19,20,22].
The TRIP mechanism plays a signi ﬁcant role in overcoming the
trade-off of cryogenic properties deviating from conventional alloys
such as 9%Ni steel or Invar alloy; however, the mechanism enhancing
the cryogenic strength further is still limited. A rate of martensitic trans-
formation is known as a critical parameter determining a strain harden-
ing capability in metastable FCC alloys. Though the TRIP effect occurs
along with the TWIP in metastable FCC-base stainless steels, high-Mn
steels, HEAs, and MEAs, the consequent cryogenic strength has not
exceeded ~1.6 GPa yet. Here, we present a novel duplex high-entropy
alloy showing 2 GPa cryogenic strength. Based on a VCrCoFeNi HEA sys-
tem, we control thermal and mechanical stabilities of FCC phase by
adjusting Fe and Ni contents. The resulting V
10Cr10Co30Fe50 alloy con-
sists of an FCC matrix and an athermal BCC martensite as a starting mi-
crostructure, and the metastable FCC exhibits an additional
deformation-induced BCC martensite during the deformation. This
transformation results in the tensile strength of 1 GPa at 298 K.

Interestingly, an accelerated transformation rate at 77 K leads to the
cryogenic strength of 2 GPa, overcoming the limitation of conventional
alloys for cryogenic applications. We found that the promoted transfor-
mation results from the chemical driving force and the mechanical
strain partitioning in the duplex microstructure. Our results demon-
strate the critical role of martensitic transformation and adjusting trans-
formation rate as an appropriate design strategy to develop new
ultrastrong alloys for cryogenic applications.
The V
10Cr10Co30Fe50-xNix (x = 0, 5, and 10 at%) HEAs were fabri-
cated by using a vacuum induction melting equipment (model;
MC100V, Indutherm, Walzbachtal-Wossingen, Germany) under an
Ar atmosphere. For convenience, the alloys are referred to as
‘50Fe’, ‘45Fe’,a n d ‘40Fe’, respectively, according to the Fe content.
Commercial pure elements (powders of 99.9% purity at least)
were alloyed in a ZrO 2-coated alumina crucible (weight; 150 g).
The molten alloys were cast into a graphite mold (size; 100 × 35
× 8 mm). The ingots were homogenized at 1474 K for 24 h in evac-
uated quartz ampules. They were cleaned in a 20% HCl solution,
sand-blasted, and then cold-rolled at room temperature with a re-
duction ratio of 75% (thickness: 1.5 mm). The sheets were annealed
at 1173 K for 10 min in evacuated quartz ampules and then water-
quenched.
Phases were characterized by an X-ray diffraction (XRD, Cu Kα radi-
ation, scan rate; 2 deg min−1, scan-step size; 0.02 deg) analysis. Volume
fractions were measured from XRD pro ﬁles based on Moser et al. [ 26]
using integrated intensities of (111) FCC, (200) FCC, (220) FCC, (311) FCC,
and (222) FCC peaks for FCC, and (110) BCC, (200) BCC, (211) BCC,a n d
(220)BCC peaks for BCC. Here, the present FCC and BCC are equivalent
to the austenite and α′-martensite, respectively. The bulk chemical
compositions of all the alloys were conﬁrmed by an inductively coupled
plasma mass spectrometry [27,28]. Detailed compositions were identi-
ﬁed by using an energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) in a ﬁeld-
emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, model; XL 30S FEG,
FEI Company, USA). Phase characterization and deformation behavior
were investigated by using an electron back-scatter diffraction (EBSD).
The EBSD specimens were prepared by mechanical grinding and then
by an electro-polishing in a solution of 92% CH 3COOH + 8% HClO 4.
The data were interpreted by orientation imaging microscopy (OIM)
analysis software. The average misorientation of a given point relative
to its neighbors is calculated by using a kernel average misorientation
(KAM) approach. The KAM was calculated up to the third neighbor
shell with a maximum misorientation angle of 5°.
Tensile tests were conducted on dog-bone specimens (thickness;
1 mm, width; 2.5 mm, gage length; 6.4 mm, longitudinal orientation)
at room (298 K) and cryogenic (77 K) temperature by using a univer-
sal testing machine (model; 8801, Instron, Canton, MA, USA) at a
crosshead speed of 6.4 × 10
−3 mm·s −1. For the cryogenic test, a
chamber (size; 50 × 40 × 38 cm) was attached by enclosing the tes-
ter. Engineering stress-strain curves were calculated from the load-
displacement data ﬁrst, and the total elongation of gauge region
was directly measured on the tensioned specimens. Then, the elon-
gation was calibrated after obtaining the load-displacement data
from the test. Entire tensile tests were conducted three times for
each datum.
Fig. 1a–c represents EBSD phase maps for the as-annealed HEAs. The
40Fe and 45Fe alloys consist of a single FCC structure. In contrast, the
50Fe alloy exhibits a duplex microstructure of FCC and BCC, and the
BCC comprises a volume fraction of 34%. The grain size of FCC is similar
at ~2.6 μm in the three alloys, and that of BCC is 2.0μm in the 50Fe alloy.
The 40Fe alloy maintains a single FCC structure after the tensile test at
298 K, while the 45Fe and 50Fe alloys show a deformation-induced
martensitic transformation to BCC. The 50Fe alloy transforms to the
whole BCC structure. After the tensile test at 77 K, the 40Fe alloy still re-
mains as a stable FCC, but the 50Fe alloy consists of 100% of BCC (Fig. 1g–
i). In the 45Fe alloy, the martensitic transformation is negligible at
298 K, while it reaches the fraction of 95% at 77 K. These as-annealed
microstructures and their associated evolutions by the tensile deforma-
tion reveal that the increased Fe content against Ni content leads to the
enhanced transformation tendency, i.e., low stability of FCC. The XRD
proﬁles for the states of as-annealed, tensioned at 298 K, and tensioned
at 77 K are attached in Supplementary Fig. 1.
Fig. 2a shows the engineering tensile stress-strain curves at 298
and 77 K for the three HEAs. The 40Fe and 45Fe alloys show similar
tensile properties at 298 K. A strain hardening is remarkable as the
Fe content increases: the tensile strength increases from 0.8 to
1.0 GPa while the ductility reduces to 29%. At 77 K, both strength
and ductility improve signi ﬁcantly in all the HEAs, as reported from
conventional HEAs [ 6–9,29–31] .T h e4 0 F ea l l o yd i s p l a y sat y p i c a l
parabolic ﬂow at both 298 and 77 K. The ﬂow behavior of the 45Fe
alloy changes from a parabolic shape at 298 K to a sigmoidal shape
at 77 K. In the 50Fe alloy, interestingly, the sigmoidal ﬂow is shown
at both 298 and 77 K. As this becomes more predominant at 77 K,
the tensile strength is almost doubled to reach 2 GPa at 77 K. Consid-
ering the increase in yield strength at 77 K is relatively small

compared with tensile properties of high- and medium-entropy al-
loys as well as high-strength steels used for cryogenic applications,
as shown in Fig. 2b[ 2,6,8,9,19,32–35]. The substantial strengthening

genic strength over 1.6 GPa ( Table 1 ).
These remarkable mechanical properties are associated with the
aforementioned microstructural evolution, i.e., FCC to BCC martensitic
transformation. The stability of FCC against the transformation in the
metastable FCC alloys is known to signi
ﬁcantly affect the strain harden-
ing [36–38]. The optimum stability, particularly, is requisite to exhibit
the balanced strength and ductility. This stability can be divided into
thermal or mechanical stability which corresponds to the transforma-
tion under the cooling or deformation, respectively, but generally arises
from a chemical driving force between BCC and FCC in common
[38–40]. We calculated a difference in Gibbs free energy between BCC
and FCC ( ΔGFCC➔BCC) at 298 K according to the Fe content in the
V10Cr10Co30Fe50-xNix (x = 0, 5, and 10 at%) system using ThermoCalc
software [ 41] with a thermodynamic database TCFE2000 and its
upgraded version [ 42–44]. The ΔGFCC➔BCC reduces from −4t o
−6 kJ mol −1 as the Fe content increases from 40 to 50 at%, indicating
that the FCC becomes less stable against the BCC. As shown in Fig. 1 ,
thus, the thermal ( Fig. 1a–c) and mechanical ( Fig. 1d–i) stabilities get
lower in the order of the 40Fe, 45Fe, and 50Fe alloys. This low FCC sta-
bility, or active martensitic transformation, can be attributed to the en-
hanced strain hardening effect of the 50 alloy. Meanwhile, it is known
that a stacking fault energy (SFE) is an important parameter to deter-
mine the deformation mechanism of FCC alloys [45]. Although it is chal-
lenging to provide the quantitative SFE values in the study, we
estimated the SFE from the Gibbs free energy difference between FCC
and HCP. The ΔGFCC➔HCP reduces from 1.8 to 1.4 kJ mol−1 as the Fe con-
tent increases from 40 to 50 at%, indicating that the SFE would decrease
with increasing Fe content. This calculation tendency well conﬁrms the
mechanical stability of FCC.
However, note that the 50Fe alloy consists of the duplex structure of
FCC and BCC, contrast to the 40Fe and 45Fe alloys, as seen in Fig. 1a–c.
Therefore, the strain hardening cannot be considered to be the effect
of FCC stability alone. Instead, it is essential to investigate in detail
how the duplex structure affects the mechanical properties and associ-
ated hardening mechanisms. Fig. 3a–c shows EBSD image quality (IQ),
phase, and KAM maps of the as-annealed 50Fe alloy. The FCC is identi-
ﬁed as a representative recrystallized equiaxed structure, based on the
high IQ value and the low geometrically necessary dislocation (GND)
density (low KAM value) [46,47]. In contrast, the BCC shows an irregular
lath morphology, low IQ value, and high GND density. This characteriza-
tion implies that this BCC is not generated from an isothermal transfor-
mation at elevated temperature. Besides, the chemical composition is

The low thermal stability is re ﬂected in the strengthening mech-
anism. Fig. 4 a displays the true stress-logarithmic strain curve and
the associated strain-hardenin g rate curve at 77 K for the 50Fe
alloy. The hardening rate begins to increase from the strain of
0.03, and shows an extremely high r ate of ~12 GPa during the defor-
mation to 0.15. Then, the rate gradually decreases to reach the plas-
tic instability. Fig. 4 b shows the BCC fraction measured by the XRD
in three regimes (logarithmic stra ins of 0.05, 0.14, and 0.22, as indi-
cated by dashed lines in Fig. 4 a) for the 50Fe alloy to investigate the
rate of martensitic transformation. The rate was compared to that of
the 45Fe alloy at the strains of 0.08, 0.15, and 0.34. In 45Fe alloy, the
FCC almost maintains until the strain of 0.15, and the BCC fraction
begins to increase gradually to 40% at the strain of 0.34. The 50Fe
alloy, however, produces a lar ge amount of mechanical BCC mar-
tensite (35%) even under a small deformation (strain; 0.05), in
addition to the thermal BCC martensite (34%, Fig. 1 c). As a result
of the additional tensile deformation, the transformation takes 20
and 10% more, and eventually generates a full BCC martensitic
structure. In other words, the 50Fe alloy has the lower mechanical
stability than the 45Fe alloy. This reduced stability leads to a very
steep linear hardening ( Fig. 2 a) in the 50Fe represents in contrast
to the sigmoidal ﬂow in the 45Fe. The low FCC stability, enhanced
transformation rate, and consequent linear hardening contribute
signiﬁcantly to the cryogenic strength of 2 GPa.
As aforementioned, however, it should be noted that the 50Fe
alloy consists of the duplex structure of FCC having low stability
and thermal BCC martensite. Fig. 4 c shows EBSD phase maps ob-
tained from the 50Fe alloy specimens deformed at 77 K by strains
of 0.05, 0.14, and 0.22 as well as the as-annealed state. The GND den-
sity calculated from the average KAM values [ 50] at each strain for
both phases is visualized in Fig. 4 d. The recrystallized FCC in the as-
annealed state has a GND density of 8.44 × 10 14 m−2,b u tt h a to f
the BCC is higher as 1.75 × 10 15 m−2 because of to the nature of mar-
tensite. Interestingly, while the GND density of FCC increases during
the deformation, that of BCC remains almost unchanged. The density
becomes similar at 0.14 strain each other, and then the density of
BCC increases after all the FCC is exhausted. Although the BCC mar-
tensite itself represents a signi ﬁcant strengthening effect, based on
a rule of mixtures in the duplex structure [ 51], the GND result sug-
gests that most of strain is partitioned into the FCC until all the FCC
transforms to the BCC. Therefore, the remarkable strain hardening
arises from not only the low stability of FCC to form the thermal mar-
tensite but also the accelerated transformation due to the strain
partitioning between the FCC and BCC martensite.
In summary, we revealed that the FCC stability was effectively tuned
by a variation in Fe content in the V 10Cr10Co30Fe50-xNix (x = 0, 5, and
10 at%) HEA system. The single FCC structure in the 40Fe and 45Fe alloys
and its gradual strain-induced transformation exhibited the balanced
strength and ductility equivalent to those of conventional cryogenic al-
loys. In the 50Fe alloy, however, (1) the formation of thermal BCC mar-
tensite due to the reduced FCC stability, (2) the strengthening effect of
the martensite itself, and (3) the accelerated mechanical transformation
by strain partitioning in the duplex structure enabled the designed alloy
to pave the way for the unrevealed cryogenic strength yet. The present
study suggests that this breakthrough of properties is not just valid in
