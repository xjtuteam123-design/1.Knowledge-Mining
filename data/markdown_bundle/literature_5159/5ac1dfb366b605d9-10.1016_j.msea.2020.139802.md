A series of high-entropy alloy CoCrFeNiV 0.5Cx (x = 0.01, 0.02 , 0.03 and 0.04) with low 
C content was fabricated. The alloys were hot rolled, homogenized, cold rolled and annealed 
for treatment processes. The structure and mechanical properties of the alloy were studied. 
The formation process of different interstitial carbides and their role in the alloy were 
discussed. In the original state, these alloys all exhibit single-phase FCC structure, after 
annealing at different temperatures, a large number of M 7C3-type interstitial carbides are 
produced at temperatures of 700 °C and above. The yield strengths (σy) of CoCrFeNiV0.5C0.02 
and CoCrFeNiV0.5C0.03 alloys annealed at 700 °C are 1339.7 MPa and 1264.6 MPa, 
respectively, with excellent performance. In the transmission analysis of CoCrFeNiV0.5C0.02, a 
new type of interstitial carbides, namely non-stoichiometric interstitial carbides M xCy, were 
found. This interstitial carbide promotes the formation of fractured dimples during tensile 
fracture, and increases the alloy's plasticity without losing strength. The addition of interstitial 
carbon atoms in the FCC matrix high-entropy alloy synergistic effects of forming two 
different carbides, and the improved performance of the alloy were investigated . A variety of 
different carbides are introduced into the alloy through post-cold-rolling heat treatment to 
further strengthen the alloy, which provides new ideas for the practicality of high-entropy 
alloys. 
Keywords: High-entropy alloy; Interstitial carbide; Microstructures; Mechanical properties

1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloy is an emerging alloy containing five or more elements mixed in 
equiatomic ratio or non-equiatomic ratio. [1-4] Researchers often changes the content ratio of 
main elements or introduces a small amount of new metal or non-metal elements in high-
entropy alloys.[5-10] Adding interstitial elements (such as carbon) is a powerful method to 
improve its performance in the transition metal high entropy alloy.[11-13] This is due to the 
higher lattice distortion energy caused by the interstitial element compared to the substituted 
element. [14, 15] In addition, the addition of interstitial carbon atoms may also change the 
phase stability and stacking fault energy of high-entropy alloys.[16] According to the research 
of Lu et al.[17], interstitial oxygen was added to the high-entropy alloy TiZrHfNb. A new 
existence state of interstitial atoms was found in the high-entropy alloy, and it was named as 
ordered interstitial atom complex. It is this new ordered atom that allows the alloy to increase 
both strength and plasticity. The study by Li et al. [12, 18] added ~ 0.5 at.% carbon in non-
isoatomic high-entropy alloys, which will lead to the combined activation of twin-induced 
shaping (TWIP) and TRIP effects. But addition of interstitial carbon atoms, the stacking fault 
energy of the non-isoatomic high-entropy alloy is adjusted to the critical point, and the 
combined effect of TWIP and TRIP occurs. 
In the field of metal research, there is a kind of non-metal element, carbon (C) element, 
that plays a strong role. In the research of high-entropy alloys, element C plays an important 
role in two aspects. On the one hand, a small amount of C element is added as an alloying 
element in high entropy alloys to improve alloy properties. For example, Wang et al. 
[11]added C element to non-isoatomic ratio Fe 40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 high entropy alloys. 
There is a linear relationship between the yield strength and the C content, and the increase 
rate of yield strength is 184 MPa/at%. On the other hand, carbide high-entropy ceramics have 
been studied in large areas in recent years. [19-22]However, the current research on the effect 
of these interstitial carbon atoms on isoatomic or non-atomic ratio high-entropy alloys is 
mostly based on the primary processing stage, that is, the as-cast state.[9, 23] Therefore, in-
depth studies on the interaction between interstitial carbon atoms and high-entropy alloys 
such as homogenization and grain refinement are relatively scarce, and these studies are of 
great significance to further improve the mechanical properties of high-entropy alloys. 
Therefore, the FCC structure in the as-cast CoCrFeNiV 0.5Cx high entropy alloy was selected 
when the carbon content was low. The effects of interstitial carbide and processing 
technology on the properties and microstructure of the alloy were studied. 
2. Experimental details 
A series of high-entropy alloys CoCrFeNiV0.5Cx [where the molar ratios ( x) 0.01, 0.02, 
0.03, 0.04, correspond to 1C, 2C, 3C, and 4C, respectively] are prepared by melting different 
kinds of high-purity elements into alloy ingots through a vacuum non-consumable arc melting 
furnace. In order to ensure the uniformity of the alloy ingot, electromagnetic stirring is used 
during the smelting process, and the ingot is remelted at least 5 times. The as-cast alloys were 
obtained by arc smelting. All HEAs were subsequenty hot rolled to a 20% reduction in 
thickness, followed by homogenized at 1200 °C for 3 hours, followed by water cooling 
treatment. After that, the rolling amount of cold rolling was 70%, followed by annealing at 
600 °C for 1 hour, 700 °C for 1 hour, and 800 °C for 1 hour. In order to facilitate the 
description and understanding of the paper, the alloys in various states are named for short, as 
shown in Table 1. The size of the dog-bone tensile sample obtained by wire cutting is 8 mm × 
2 mm × 1 mm. The tensile experiments were carried out at room temperature by using an 
Instron 5982 electronic tester at a strain rate of 1 × 10-3 s-1. 
The crystal structure, microstructure, and phase composition of the present alloys were 
identified by X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscope (SEM), Transmission 
electron microscope (TEM) measurements. The XRD analysis was performed, using a 
D/MAX-2500/PC diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) scanning from 20° to 100°

with a scanning rate of 4°/min. The working voltage and current are 40 kV and 200 mA. 
Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis was performed using Hitachi S-3400 
scanning electron microscope (SEM) and TSL OIM data acquisition software. And the 
chemical compositions were analyzed by the SEM energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS). 
Transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis was performed by using a Talos F200X 
with an accelerating voltage of 200 KV. Samples for TEM observation were ground to nearly 
30 μm, and then twin-jet electro-polished in a solution composed of 10 vol % HClO 4 and 90 
vol % C2H5OH at room temperature.

= 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04) under different processing conditions. In Fig. 1 (a), the XRD 
spectrum of the as-cast alloy is a single FCC structure, but the diffraction peaks are relatively

miscellaneous peaks appear, indicating that the sample has good crystallinity, and the

3.2 Mechanical properties of the alloy after annealing

## Page 7

The alloy subjected to the homogenization treatment and the annealing treatment is 
subjected to a tensile test. The results of the tensile test are shown in Fig. 4. The homogenized 
gap alloy used is well-shaped, with strains exceeding 40% when fracture occurs, and the yield 
strength of the alloy is about 300 MPa, which is slightly higher than the yield strength of the 
as-cast alloy. Fig. 4 (a) shows the tensile curve of 1C alloy at different annealing temperatures. 
There is almost no direct fracture at 600 °C, and the shape is slightly increased at 700 °C. At 
800 °C, the yield strength was significantly reduced to 935.4 MPa, but the fracture strain was 
increased to 10.45 %. In Fig. 4 (b), the 2C alloy is stretched at different annealing 
temperatures. Direct fracture at 600 °C has almost no plastic deformation. At 700 °C, the 
plastic shape increases significantly, the fracture strain is 7.64%, and the yield strength is 
slightly. At 800 °C, the yield strength was significantly reduced to 862 MPa, but the strain at 
break was increased to 10.7%. In Fig. 4 (c), the 3C alloy is stretched at different annealing 
temperatures. At 600 °C, the plasticity is very poor, and after fracture, fracture occurs. At 
700 °C, the plasticity is slightly increased but the strength is reduced significantly. At 800 °C, 
the strength continues to decrease, and the plasticity change is similar to that of the 1C and 2C 
alloy. Figure 4-5 (d) is the tensile curve of 4C alloy at different annealing temperatures. The 
strength is significantly improved at 600 °C, but there is still no plasticity. The strength and 
plasticity at 700 °C are lower than those of 2C and 3C alloys. At 800 °C, the plasticity and 
strength decrease more obviously, but the plasticity is lower than that of 3C alloy. After the 
alloy is annealed at different temperatures for one hour after cold rolling, the mechanical 
properties of the alloy can be significantly improved after cold rolling, which can be said to 
be several times higher than that of the alloy after homogenization. The detailed data of 
tensile yield strength ( σy) and fracture strain ( εf) of interstitial high-entropy alloy 
CoCrFeNiV0.5Cx (x = 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04) treated at different annealing temperatures are 
summarized in Table 3. According to the data in the table, it can be found that the 2C and 3C 
alloys annealed at 700 °C have superior properties. Their yield strengths ( σy) are 1339.7 MPa 
and 1264.6 MPa, respectively, and their fracture strains (εf) are 7.64 % and 6.38 %.

3.3 Microstructure of the alloy after annealing 
As shown in Fig. 5, the XRD diffraction patterns of the interstitial high-entropy alloy 
CoCrFeNiV0.5Cx (x = 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04) were annealed at 600 °C, 700 °C, and 800 °C 
for one hour, respectively. Fig. 5 (a) is an XRD spectrum obtained at 600 °C. At this time, the 
crystal structure of the alloy is an FCC structure, and no other diffraction peaks are seen. Fig. 
5 (b) shows the XRD spectrum obtained at 700 °C. The diffraction peaks of the interstitial 
carbide M7C3 are more obvious in 1C, 2C and 3C alloys. However, it was not found in the 4C 
alloy, which may be more favorable for the production of M 7C3 at lower carbon content. Fig. 
5 (c) is an XRD pattern obtained at 800 °C. The diffraction peaks of the interstitial carbide 
M7C3 were found in the alloys 1C, 2C, 3C, and 4C, but the intensity of the diffraction peaks in 
the 4C alloy is low. The relative ratio of XRD at different temperatures can be concluded that 
lower carbon content is beneficial to the production of M 7C3, and the higher the temperature, 
the more favorable it is to produce M 7C3. M7C3 is a complex hexagonal carbide, but the 
crystal system type of M 7C3 includes pseudo hexagonal crystal system (triangular crystal 
system), orthogonal crystal system and hexagonal crystal system, Cr element will make 
M7C3 carbide more stable.

3.4 Interstitial carbide microstructure 
In order to further observe the microstructure of interstitial carbides after annealing, a 
TEM analysis was performed on the 2C- AN-700 alloy, as shown in Fig. 7. It can be seen 
from the high-angle annular dark-field phase (HAADF) that the position of this picture is in 
the completely recrystallized region. The grain size of the matrix phase is about 2 μm, and the 
size of the carbide is about 0.5 μm. It can be seen from the STEM-EDS diagram that the 
distribution of each element in the matrix is uniform without segregation, but there is obvious 
element segregation in the interstitial carbides that are precipitated. An interesting 
phenomenon was discovered, that is, there are two different carbides in the alloy. According 
to the results of the XRD diffraction pattern (Fig. 5), it can be seen that M 7C3 carbides must 
exist. In order to determine the type of newly discovered carbides, the regional elemental 
analysis of the two carbides was performed, as shown in Table 4. Each element of the FCC is 
evenly distributed in the matrix, and the element content is basically the same as the nominal 
content of the alloy. The content of Co, Fe, and V elements in M7C3 carbides does not change 
significantly, the segregation of Cr elements and the reduction of Ni elements. In unknown 
carbides (non-stoichiometric carbides M xCy), the contents of Co, Fe, and Ni all decreased 
significantly, while the content of Cr increased sharply and the content of V increased 
significantly.

the upper left part of the Fig. 8 (a), and the band axis is [010]. In the area of the blue box in 
Fig. 8 (a), it can be clearly found that the atoms are in an ordered arrangement. The 
diffraction spots obtained by the FFT in Fig. 8 (c) are also verified as an ordered FCC (L1 2) 
structure. It is well known that transition group metal elements can easily generate interstitial 
compounds with carbon elements in alloys. There are many types of interstitial compounds, 
some of which have a certain stoichiometric ratio, and some have no definite stoichiometric 
ratio. When the atoms of the transition group metal element form a face-centered cubic 
structure, the carbon atoms have octahedral interstitial positions. At this time, the carbon 
vacancies of the carbon atoms can reach up to 50%, so they will be stable when they are far 
from the stoichiometric ratio. V element is a strong carbon compound, and it is easy to form 
carbide VC. However, no carbide VC was found in the TEM image. As can be seen from 
Table 4, there are two different carbides in the alloy, one is the enrichment of M 7C3 mainly 
Cr element, and the other is the non-stoichiometric ratio MxCy is mainly the enrichment of Cr 
and V element. In the solid solution alloy, there is no Cottrell atmosphere, so there is no

## Page 12

serration behavior during the yielding. We speculate that this non-stoichiometric carbide 
generation process first generates VC and then due to the existence of a large number of 
carbon vacancies, the aggregation of Cr elements is induced by VC to generate an ordered 
FCC (L12) structure. This non-stoichiometric carbide is formed. The presence of carbides was 
not found in alloys that were homogenized after hot rolling. During the process of grain 
nucleation and growth during homogenization, the carbon elements dissolve alloy due to the 
"cocktail effect" of the high-entropy alloy and the low carbon content in the alloy cause. 
Therefore, no interstitial carbide is formed. 
The generation of such non-stoichiometric carbides is closely related to the processing 
technology of the alloy. A universal mechanism of ordered carbon self-assembly driving 
mechanism exists in transition metal carbides [24-27]. In high-entropy alloys, especially in 
transition-group high-entropy alloys, the atomic distribution is more chaotic and easier to 
combine with other non-metal atoms. After the alloy is cold-rolled, a large amount of energy 
is stored in the alloy. After annealing and heat treatment, it provides power for the formation 
of this carbon vacancy orderly phase. The formation of such ordered carbides was also found 
in previous research by our research group[28], which is similar to the results obtained in this 
study.

3.5 Fracture morphology analysis 
Fig. 9 is a scanned image of the tensile fracture morphology of an interstitial high-
entropy alloy CoCrFeNiV0.5Cx (x = 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04) after an hour at 700 ° C. It can be 
seen from the figure that the fractures of the four different alloys all have uneven areas and 
relatively flat areas. Stepwise or jagged tear marks are mostly formed at the junction of these 
two areas (there are obvious bright marks in the figure), this tear is caused by the presence of 
interstitial carbides between the crystals. Among uneven areas, a fine layered structure with a 
width of about 2 μm also is existed. Dimples formed with interstitial carbides as particles 
were found in the fine layered structure. The width of the fine layered structure is the same as

4. Conclusions 
The microstructure of the alloy under different processing techniques, and the 
mechanical properties of the alloy under different processing techniques were systematically 
analyzed, the types of interstitial carbides, and the relationship between carbides and 
mechanical properties were studied. The main conclusions were as follow: 
(1) The as-cast alloy and the homogenized alloy were observed by the EBSD orientation 
diagram, and the alloys in both states are typical coarse-grained structures with coarse grains. 
According to XRD diffraction patterns, it can be seen that the alloys in both states are FCC 
single-phase structures. In both states, alloys are rapidly cooled after high temperature. At 
high temperature, carbon atoms are dissolved in the solid solution of the alloy, no interstitial 
carbide is formed. After the medium temperature heat treatment of the alloy, there is still no

## Page 14

carbide precipitation at 600 °C, and the existence of interstitial carbide M 7C3 exists at 700 °C 
and 800 °C. 
(2)After cold rolling, the alloy's mechanical properties are mainly manifested by a 
decrease in yield strength and an increase in shape with increasing annealing temperature. At 
700 ℃, the shape of the alloy increases first and then decreases with the increase of the carbon 
content. The shape of the alloy does not change significantly at other temperatures. The 2C 
and 3C alloys annealed at 700 °C have superior properties. The yield strength ( σy) are 1339.7 
MPa and 1264.6 MPa, respectively, and the breaking strains ( εf) are 7.64% and 6.38% , 
respectively. 
(3) Further analysis by TEM revealed a completely new type of carbide, the ordered 
interstitial carbide (M xCy) with non-stoichiometric carbon vacancy configuration. Ordered 
interstitial carbides (MxCy) with non-stoichiometric carbon vacancy configurations are easy to 
form in the completely recrystallized region of the alloy after annealing, which is beneficial to 
the formation of dimples, while maintaining the strength and improving the plasticity of the 
alloy.
