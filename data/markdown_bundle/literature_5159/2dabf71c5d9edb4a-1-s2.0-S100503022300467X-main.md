Reducing the hot working temperature and high-temperature deformation resistance of titanium alloy 
to improve hot rolling and hot extrusion workability of products with thin walls and complex section 
shapes has always been an important topic in the ﬁeld of titanium alloy processing. This paper proposed 
a strategy of adding Mo and Fe elements to simultaneously reduce the hot working temperature and 
high-temperature deformation resistance of Ti-6Al-4V alloy. The effects of Mo and Fe contents on the mi- 
crostructure, β transus temperature ( Tβ), and high-temperature ﬂow stress (HFS) of Ti-6Al-4V- x Mo- x Fe 
( x = 0−5) alloys were investigated. The results showed that adding Mo and Fe can substantially reduce 
the Tβ and HFS of the alloy, and greatly improve its room-temperature strength. Compared with com- 
mercial Ti-6Al-4V samples, the Tβ of Ti-6Al-4V-2Mo-2Fe and Ti-6Al-4V-3Mo-3Fe samples was decreased 
by 68−98 °C, and the HFS at 80 0−90 0 °C was decreased by 37.8%−46.0%. Compared with hot-rolled 
Ti-6Al-4V samples, the room-temperature tensile strength of hot-rolled Ti-6Al-4V-2Mo-2Fe samples was 
increased by about 30%, while the elongation hardly decreased. The increased strength was mainly at- 
tributed to ﬁne grain strengthening and solid solution strengthening. The hot workability and room- 
temperature strength of Ti-6Al-4V alloy can be signiﬁcantly improved by adding 2−3 wt.% Mo and Fe 
simultaneously.

1. Introduction 
Titanium alloys are key materials in the ﬁeld of aerospace, 
ocean engineering, national defense, and medical treatment due 
to low density, high speciﬁc strength, high-temperature resistance, 
corrosion resistance, and good biocompatibility [1–3] . Ti-6Al-4V al- 
loy is a typical dual-phase titanium alloy with α+ β phases. The 
alloy can be strengthened by heat treatment to achieve excellent 
comprehensive mechanical properties. Ti-6Al-4V alloy is the most 
widely used titanium structural material, and its usage accounts 
for about 50% of the total amount of titanium alloy, which accounts 
for more than 80% of the amount of titanium alloy in the aerospace 
ﬁeld [ 4 , 5 ].

Ti-6Al-4V alloy undergoes an allotropic transformation during 
heating. With increasing temperature, the α phase will gradu- 
ally transform into the β phase, resulting in a single β phase 
above Tβ ( β transus temperature). α+ β titanium alloys are usu- 
ally processed at high temperature because of their poor plasticity 
and high yield strength. However, when the heating temperature 
reaches above Tβ, the rapid growth of β grains is prone to cause 
β brittleness, which damages plasticity seriously. Therefore, except 
for ingot breakdown in the temperature range of 30−40 °C above 
Tβ (low deformation resistance), extrusion and rolling for dual- 
phase titanium alloys are generally carried out in the dual-phase 
area of 30−100 °C below Tβ to obtain more uniform and ﬁner 
α+ β dual-phase microstructure with good comprehensive proper- 
ties [6–8] . However, this results in higher resistance to deforma- 
tion. 
The Tβ of Ti-6Al-4V alloy is about 950−10 0 0 °C depending on 
the content of impurity elements, and the hot working tempera- 
ture range is 800−950 °C. However, hot extrusion and rolling re- 
quire billet to have lower deformation resistance, so the processing 
temperature is usually 920−950 °C. At higher processing tempera-

is more prominent during high-speed extrusion, easily forming de- 
fects in the product such as cracks, stress concentration, and over-

lower high-temperature deformation resistance or/and a lower hot 
working temperature is beneﬁcial to reduce the hot working dif- 
ﬁculty and improve the surface quality of titanium alloy products, 
particularly those with thin walls and complex section shapes. This 
is also an important way to expand the application range of tita- 
nium alloy materials. 
The room-temperature tensile strength of Ti-6Al-4V is about 
90 0−10 0 0 MPa, belonging to medium-strength titanium alloy with 
dual phases. The development of high-strength titanium alloys has 
become one of the hot spots as the demand for reducing structural 
weight, improving structural eﬃciency, and extending equipment 
life has increased. High-strength titanium alloys are generally de- 
veloped by adding alloying elements such as Mo, Cr, Nb, Zr, and V 
to make the alloy’s room-temperature tensile strength reach above 
1100 MPa [9–11] . The solid solution strengthening formed by the 
addition of these elements can improve the strength of the alloy, 
but it also increases the high-temperature deformation resistance, 
which is not conducive to the hot work forming of titanium alloys 
[ 12 , 13 ]. Consequently, it is a major challenge facing the develop- 
ment of titanium alloy to reasonably select the type and content of 
alloying elements to greatly improve the strength of titanium alloy 
at room temperature while reducing its deformation resistance at 
high temperature. 
Mishin et al. [14] analyzed the latest progress of experimen- 
tal and theoretical research on the diffusion behavior in various 
phases of titanium alloy and concluded that Fe is a fast diffusion 
element in titanium alloy. The diffusion rate of Fe in α-Ti is 104 
times the self-diffusion rate of α-Ti, and the diffusion rate of Fe in 
β-Ti is 102 times the self-diffusion rate of β-Ti. Therefore, adding 
Fe in titanium alloy can not only lower the β transus tempera- 
ture but also signiﬁcantly promote dislocation movement during 
deformation and reduce the alloy’s deformation resistance. In other 
words, it is conducive to reducing hot working temperature and/or 
high-temperature deformation resistance. However, the addition of 
fast diffusion elements alone tends to reduce the mid-temperature 
mechanical properties of titanium alloys in the temperature range 
of 30 0−50 0 °C, especially damaging the creep properties [ 15 , 16 ]. 
Fortunately, Zhou et al. [17] obtained Gibbs free energy of a series 
of Ti-X alloys using ﬁrst-principles calculation and Debye model, 
and found that Mo element has a strong stabilizing effect on β
phase. Cai et al. [18] reported that Mo element can improve β
phase stability, thermal strength and oxidation resistance of tita- 
nium alloy at temperatures below 500 °C. In addition, Mo element 
can also improve the corrosion resistance [ 19 , 20 ], plasticity and 
strength of titanium alloy [ 21 , 22 ]. Therefore, the addition of Mo 
element in this study is to compensate for the degradation of Fe 
to the intermediate-temperature mechanical properties of titanium 
alloy. 
Based on the above background, this paper proposes a strat- 
egy of simultaneously enhancing the hot workability and room- 
temperature strength of Ti-6Al-4V alloys via adding both Mo and 
Fe, and then focuses on the effects of Mo and Fe contents on the β
transus temperature Tβ and hot-compression ﬂow stress (HFS) of 
Ti-6Al-4V- x Mo- x Fe ( x = 0−5, as a mass fraction) alloys. Moreover, 
the microstructure and phase of the samples are investigated to 
analyze related mechanisms. The results can provide a reference

2. Materials and methods 
The ingots were melted in a vacuum induction suspension 
melting furnace with a water-cooled copper crucible under an ar- 
gon atmosphere with raw materials of pure Ti, Al, V, Mo, and 
Fe (purity ≥ 99.99%), and re-melted ﬁve times to enhance com- 
position homogeneity. The size of the prepared cylindrical in- 
got was /Phi150 mm × 55 mm, with a mass of about 500 g. The 
measured composition of ingots and the room-temperature equi- 
librated phase calculated by the JmatPro software are listed in 
Table 1 . Ti-6Al-4V- x Mo- x Fe ( x = 0−5) samples are represented by 
X0, X1, X2, X3, X4 and X5, respectively. 
The hot compression samples were cut at 1/2 radius on the 
cross-section within the height range of 5−45 mm of the ingot, 
and the sample size was /Phi18 mm × 12 mm. The hot compres- 
sion was carried out on the Gleeble-3500 thermomechanical sim- 
ulator with graphite ﬂakes placed between the indenter and the 
sample end to reduce friction. The sample was heated at a rate 
of 10 °C s−1 and held for 3 min after reaching the target temper- 
ature to ensure homogenized temperature. The deformation tem- 
peratures were 70 0, 80 0, 850 and 900 °C with a strain rate of 0.01 
s−1 and a maximum reduction in height of 50%. The average value 
of the peak ﬂow stress of the three samples was taken as the peak 
ﬂow stress (HFS). The high-temperature deformation microstruc- 
ture was retained by water quenching immediately after the com- 
pression. 
Two pieces of block samples with dimensions of 45 mm × 40 
mm × 10 mm were cut from X0 and X2 alloy ingots, and then 
hot-rolled and hot-forged in the dual-phase region at about 70 °C 
below the β transus temperature of X0 and X2 alloys, respectively. 
The hot rolling reduction is about 80%, and the diameter of the rod 
after hot forging is 15 mm. After hot working, the samples were 
annealed at 700 °C for 1.5 h. Dog-bone-shaped room-temperature 
tensile samples were cut along the rolling direction of the plates, 
with a gauge length of 20 mm and cross-section of 5 mm × 1.5 
mm. The tensile tests were performed with a strain rate of 0.5 
mm min−1 at room temperature. The average value of four sam- 
ples was taken as the tensile test results. Rod-shaped intermediate- 
temperature tensile samples were cut from the rod along the axial 
direction, with a gauge length of 25 mm and cross-section diam- 
eter of 5 mm. The intermediate-temperature tensile temperatures 
were 350, 400, and 450 °C. The schematic diagram of hot compres- 
sion and hot working experiment is shown in Fig. 1 . 
The SmartLab X-ray diffractometer was used for phase analy- 
sis. The scanning speed was 1 ° min−1 , the scanning range was 
10 °−90 °, the working voltage was 40 kV, and the working cur- 
rent was 150 mA. The differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) ex- 
periment was performed using the SDT-Q600 instrument under 
high purity Ar ﬂow to obtain the DSC curve and its ﬁrst deriva-

3. Results 
3.1. β transus temperature, phase and microstructure of as-cast 
samples 
Fig. 2 shows the OM microstructure morphology of X0−X5 as- 
cast samples, the β phase has a darker color. The illustrations at 
the upper right corner of Fig. 2 (a−d) reveal the local SEM sec- 
ondary electron images of the samples, the β phase is bright white 
and the α phase is black gray in the ﬁgure. 
The OM microstructure morphology in Fig. 2 shows that the 
grain size of the samples decreases gradually with the increase of 
x . The grain size of X0 surpasses 10 0 0 μm, and those of X1−X5 are 
about 800, 570, 560, 414 and 385 μm, respectively. 
As can be seen from the SEM images of Fig. 2 , there are a large 
number of interlaced lamellar α phase in X0−X2 samples, and the 
lamella thickness gradually reduces with the increase of x , from 1.5 
μm of X0 to 0.3 μm of X2. When x further increases to 3, the sam- 
ple contains plenty of ﬁne and diffuse acicular martensite phases 
with cross-arrangement ( Fig. 2 (d)). When x increases to 4 and 5, 
the formation of α phase and martensite phase is inhibited, and 
the sample becomes predominantly single β phase. 
The XRD patterns of X0−X5 as-cast samples are shown in 
Fig. 3 (a). When the content of Mo and Fe is 0%−3%, the existence 
of diffraction peaks (101)α/ α’ and (211)β indicates that the sam- 
ples contain both α/ α’ and β phases. As x increases, the intensity 
of α/ α’ phase diffraction peak decreases while that of β phase in- 
creases, suggesting that β phase content increases with increas- 
ing Mo and Fe. Combined with the microstructure morphology in 
Fig. 2 , it can be determined that there is α phase in X0−X2 sam- 
ples, and martensitic α´phase in X3 sample, which is due to the 
high Mo and Fe content in the X3 sample and the fast cooling rate 
during the solidiﬁcation inhibiting the transformation of β phase 
into α phase. As a result of the diffusionless transformation, the 
β phase transforms into the martensitic α´phase, so the X3 sam- 
ple is mainly the α´+ β phases. When the Mo and Fe content ≥
4%, the diffraction peak of α/ α´ phase disappears, and only the β
phase diffraction peak exists in the XRD pattern, so the samples 
are mainly β phase. 
However, as shown in Table 1 , the room-temperature equili- 
brated phases of X3, X4, and X5 samples are all α+ β structures. 
For this reason, the X3, X4, and X5 as-cast samples are annealed 
at 700 °C for 2 h and cooled slowly with the furnace, and then 
XRD tests are carried out. The results of XRD tests are shown in 
Fig. 3 (b). The presence of α and β phase diffraction peaks in all 
three samples indicates their equilibrated microstructures are all 
α+ β structures. According to the results in Fig. 3 (b), during an- 
nealing at 700 °C, the α´phase of the X3 as-cast sample is decom- 
posed into α+ β phases and the α phase of X4 and X5 as-cast sam- 
ples is precipitated from β phase. 
The DDSC curves of X0−X5 as-cast samples are shown in Fig. 4 , 
with the peak temperature corresponding to the samples’ β tran- 
sus temperature Tβ. It can be seen that the Tβ gradually decreases 
with increasing Mo and Fe contents. In the range of x = 0−4 wt.%, 
the Tβ of the samples decreases by about 30−36 °C with the in- 
crease of 1 wt.% Mo and Fe. The Tβ of X2 and X3 alloys drops 
by 68−98 °C in comparison with that of X0. The lower β tran- 
sus temperature means the hot working of alloys can be performed 
at lower temperatures, thus reducing the diﬃculty of hot working 
[23] . 
3.2. Hot-compression deformation behavior of as-cast samples 
Fig. 5 (a−d) shows typical true stress-true strain curves of hot 
compression for X0−X5 as-cast samples. At small strains ( ε < 3%), 
the ﬂow stress increases rapidly with strain. When the strain ex- 
ceeds 7%, the ﬂow stress decreases continuously or tends to be sta- 
ble with strain. Balasubrahmanyam et al. [24] and Semiatin et al. 
[25] reported a similar trend in the hot deformation of titanium 
alloy. When the softening rate caused by dynamic recovery or dy- 
namic recrystallization exceeds the work-hardening rate, continu- 
ous softening is exhibited. When the work-hardening rate balances 
with the softening rate, steady stress is observed. 
Fig. 5 also suggests that the stress-strain curves of X5 samples 
at all compression temperatures show a sharp peak stress at the 
beginning of deformation, followed by a sharp drop, as marked by 
the black circle in the ﬁgure. The stress-strain curves of X4 sam- 
ples at 80 0−90 0 °C are the same. This is because the discontin- 
uous yielding phenomenon occurs during the hot deformation of 
titanium alloys, which can be explained by the dynamic theory 
of rapid dislocation multiplication [ 7 , 26 ], as discussed in detail in 
Section 4.1 . 
The average HFS of the samples is shown in Fig. 5 (e) and 
Table 2 . Table 2 also includes the measured β transus tempera- 
ture from Fig. 4 . /Delta1in Table 2 represents the decreased proportion 
of HFS for X1−X5 samples relative to the X0 sample. According to 
Fig. 5 (e), at the compression temperature of 700 °C, the Mo and Fe 
content has less effect on the HFS when the content is 0−3 wt.%.

3.3. Mechanical properties and microstructure of hot working 
samples 
Among the ﬁve kinds of as-cast samples with added Mo and 
Fe elements, the X2 sample has relatively lower ﬂow stress and 
less alloying element content. Considering the sustainable develop- 
ment of materials and the hot workability improvement of alloys, 
we select X2 alloy as the main research object in this paper and in- 
vestigate the room-temperature and intermediate-temperature me- 
chanical properties after hot working compared with the X0 alloy. 
The room-temperature tensile stress-strain curves and statisti- 
cal results of tensile properties of X2 and X0 hot working samples 
are shown in Fig. 6 (a, b). Compared with those of the X0 sample, 
the yield strength and tensile strength of the X2 sample increase 
from 863 and 944 MPa to 1109 and 1191 MPa by 29% and 26%, re- 
spectively. While the average elongation after fracture of X0 and 
X2 samples remains almost equal, 13.7% and 13.5%, respectively. 
Thus, the comprehensive room-temperature mechanical properties 
of X2 alloy are signiﬁcantly superior to those of X0 alloy. 
The intermediate-temperature tensile stress-strain curves of X2 
and X0 hot working samples at 350−450 °C are shown in Fig. 6 (c, 
d). At 350−450 °C, the tensile strength and plasticity of X2 sample 
are signiﬁcantly improved. Compared with those of the X0 sample, 
the yield strength of X2 sample is increased by 192−233 MP, and 
the value of elongation after fracture is increased by 1%−5%. This 
indicates that the intermediate-temperature mechanical properties 
of X2 alloy are better than those of X0 alloy. 
The microstructure of X0 and X2 hot-rolled samples is shown in 
Fig. 7 . The hot-rolled samples are composed of residual lamellar α, 
striped α, equiaxed α phase , and β phase, in which the dark-gray 
area is α phase and the gray-white area is β phase. As demon- 
strated in Fig. 7 (a) and (c), the initial lamellar microstructure is 
tiny after hot deformation, and the lamellar α phase is bent, bro- 
ken, and spheroidized during rolling. The interface of the lamellar 
α phase is no longer smooth but wavy, and the tendency of frac- 
ture spheroidization is obvious. As can be seen from Fig. 7 (b) and 
(d), compared with the X0 hot-rolled sample, the X2 hot-rolled 
sample contains more β phase, and the thickness of the lamel- 
lar α without spheroidization and the grain size of the striped α
and the equiaxed α phase with spheroidization are smaller. The 
strengthening mechanism of X2 alloy will be discussed in detail in 
Section 4.2 .

4.1. Mechanism analysis of high-temperature ﬂow stress reduction 
EBSD analysis is carried out on the samples after compressed at 
70 0, 80 0, and 90 0 °C, and the results are shown in Fig. 8 . Accord- 
ing to the microstructure characteristics, the EBSD images of X0 
and X2 hot-compressed samples can be divided into three types of 
regions: recrystallized microstructure (intragranular misorientation 
< 2 °), recovery microstructure (2 ° ≤ intragranular misorientation 
≤ 15 °) and deformed microstructure (intragranular misorientation 
> 15 °) [27] , which are marked with blue, yellow and red, respec- 
tively. The Tβ of the X0 and X2 samples is 966 and 898 °C, re- 
spectively. Therefore, except for the X2 sample which is the single 
phase at 900 °C, all the other samples are deformed in the dual- 
phase region. 
As shown in Fig. 8 (a−d), after compressed at 700 and 800 °C, 
the recrystallized microstructure and recovery microstructure of X0 
and X2 samples account for a large proportion. After compressed 
at 700 °C, the proportion of recrystallized microstructure is ap- 
proximately 22% for X0 samples and 37% for X2 samples ( Fig. 8 (a, 
b)). It implies that the recrystallization degree of the X2 sample 
is greater than that of the X0 sample. Since recrystallization can 
substantially reduce the ﬂow stress and enhance the plastic defor- 
mation capacity of the alloy [28] , the HFS of X2 samples is lower 
than that of X0 samples compressed at 700 °C ( Fig. 5 (a)). Similarly, 
after compressed at 800 °C the proportion of recrystallized mi- 
crostructure for X2 samples (about 60%) is much higher than that 
for X0 samples (about 16%) ( Fig. 8 (c, d)), so the HFS of X2 sam- 
ples drops by about 43.7% by comparison with that of X0 samples 
( Fig. 5 (b)). 
The X2 sample compressed at 900 °C is mainly β phase with 
high stacking fault energy [29] . The higher the stacking fault en- 
ergy is, the more likely the dynamic recovery (DRV) will occur 
[30] . Therefore, the X2 sample mainly undergoes DRV during com- 
pression, and the proportion of recrystallized microstructure is 
small, only about 3% ( Fig. 8 (f)). Nevertheless, the β phase of body- 
centered cubic structure has more slip systems and stronger plas- 
tic deformation capacity, which makes the HFS of X2 samples de- 
crease by about 46.0% by comparison with that of X0 samples 
( Fig. 5 (d)).

Furthermore, the sharp decline of peak stress marked by the 
circles in Fig. 5 is caused by the discontinuous yield during the 
hot compression of titanium alloy. According to the dynamic the- 
ory [7] , plenty of movable dislocations are blocked by grain bound- 
aries, thus resulting in dislocation pile-up during the deforma- 
tion of materials, which leads to an increase in ﬂow stress. Sub- 
sequently, due to the thermal activation, a large number of stack- 
ing dislocations enter grain boundaries through climbing and other 
ways, resulting in a sharp decline in ﬂow stress and discontinuous 
yield phenomena. The higher the Fe content is, the ﬁercer the dis- 
location motion related to thermal activation is, which facilitates 
the occurrence of discontinuous yield. Moreover, the addition of 
Mo and Fe reﬁnes the grain size and expands the grain boundary 
area. It is widely assumed that the more grain boundaries there 
are, the more favorable the occurrence of discontinuous yield will 
be [49] . Therefore, for X4 and X5 alloys, discontinuous yield can 
occur at lower temperatures with increasing Mo and Fe contents, 
as shown in Fig. 5 . 
In most related literature on titanium alloy [ 7 , 26 ], the discon- 
tinuous yield phenomenon will be more obvious with the increas- 
ing deformation temperature. Whereas, the discontinuous yield 
of X5 alloy weakens with the increase of deformation tempera- 
ture. There are two possible reasons for this: ﬁrst, when the de- 
formation temperature increases, the α phase content decreases 
and grain coarsening occurs, which leads to a signiﬁcant reduc- 
tion in phase boundary and grain boundary density. The reduction 
of phase boundary and grain boundary density will decrease the 
dislocation source of discontinuous yield, resulting in the reduc- 
tion of yielding drop magnitude, which manifests as the discontin- 
uous yield gradually decreasing with the increasing temperature. 
Second, the higher driving force promotes dislocation movement 
and dynamic softening at higher deformation temperature, greatly 
consuming the accumulated dislocation and resulting in the weak- 
ening of discontinuous yield. A similar discontinuous yield phe- 
nomenon of titanium alloys has also been reported by Li et al. 
[50] and Zhao et al. [51] .

5. Conclusions 
(1) The addition of Mo and Fe elements can promote the de- 
formation dislocation movement and dynamic recrystalliza- 
tion process, and increase the content of the deformable β
phase, which can signiﬁcantly reduce the high-temperature 
ﬂow stress HFS. The HFS of Ti-6Al-4V-2Mo-2Fe and Ti- 
6Al-4V-3Mo-3Fe samples decreases by 37.8%−46.0% at 
80 0−90 0 °C. 
(2) Under compressed at 700 and 800 °C, the main deformation 
mechanism is dislocation movement and twinning for the Ti- 
6Al-4V sample, while that for the Ti-6Al-4V-2Mo-2Fe sample is 
dislocation movement only. The ﬁrst-principles calculation re- 
veals that the addition of Mo and Fe elements reduces the ten- 
dency of producing twins in titanium alloys and makes them 
more susceptible to producing stacking faults. 
(3) The addition of Mo and Fe can signiﬁcantly improve the room- 
temperature strength of the alloy, while the average elonga- 
tion remains almost equal. The increased strength is mainly at- 
tributed to ﬁne grain strengthening and solid solution strength- 
ening. 
(4) Considering the hot compression deformation behavior and 
room-temperature mechanical properties, adding 2 wt.%−3 
wt.% Mo and 2 wt.%−3 wt.% Fe to Ti-6Al-4V alloy can simul- 
taneously improve the hot workability and room-temperature 
strength of the alloy.
