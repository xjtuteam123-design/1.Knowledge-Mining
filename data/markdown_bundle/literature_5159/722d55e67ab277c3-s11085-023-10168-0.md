Abstract
The cyclic oxidation behavior of HK30Nb heat-resistant steel processed by laser 
powder bed fusion (LPBF) was compared to its cast counterpart during exposures 
in air and air + 10% H2O at 800 °C. The specific finer microstructure and lower Mn 
of the LPBF alloy resulted in lower oxidation rates in dry air and faster establish-
ment of a continuous Cr2O3 scale in air + 10% H2O compared to coarse-grained 
cast HK30Nb with higher Mn. Differences in alloy mechanical strength and there-
fore their ability to accommodate high temperature and oxidation-induced stresses 
as well as differences in thermal expansion coefficients between the alloy and the 
formed oxides (Cr2O3 only for the LPBF and Cr2O3 and MnCr2O4 for the cast speci-
mens) during temperature cycling were found to result in a greater extent of spalla-
tion for the LPBF than for the cast alloy in dry air at 800 °C.

vapor
Introduction
Vehicle electrification combined with the production of CO2-free electricity as 
well as the use of low net carbon fuels such hydrogen or biodiesels is the prom-
ising route to de-carbonize the economy. Fossil fuels will, however, remain in use 
for decades, and cutting the CO2 emissions of gas turbines and heavy-duty diesel 
trucks is necessary to reduce their impact on global warming and meet stringent 
fuel efficiency standards. One straightforward way to decrease CO2 emission is to 
increase system efficiency by increasing the operating temperature and mechanical

loads. In addition, additive manufacturing offers the opportunity to develop unique 
designs to optimize the performance of key components such as heat exchangers [1]. 
Unfortunately, cost-effective austenitic steels capable of withstanding temperatures 
above 650  °C for thousands of hours in industrial environments are not currently 
produced via additive manufacturing. For high-temperature applications (550 °C), 
Ni-based alloys such 625 or 718 are typically considered, while at lower tempera-
tures (< 450 °C), stainless steel 316L is the alloy of choice [2–7].
The microstructure and tensile properties of additively manufactured (AM) aus-
tenitic stainless steels using laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) have now been widely 
reported [3–6, 8]. Elongated grains are commonly observed along the < 001 > build-
ing direction for FCC materials together with equiaxed grains perpendicular to the 
building direction with variations in the grains shapes and dimensions as a func-
tion of building parameters and build geometries [3]. Room-temperature tensile 
properties have shown promise in terms of strength and ductility [4, 6] but decrease 
in ductility has been observed after various annealing times and temperatures [5]. 
Recently, heat-resistant steels such as type HK (HK30Nb) of similar composition to 
310-type austenitic stainless steels were fabricated by LPBF [2]. The tensile proper -
ties of HK30Nb were studied between room temperature and 900 °C, and superior 
yield and ultimate tensile strength along the build direction were measured for the 
LPBF HK30Nb in comparison with cast HK30Nb [2].
A limited number of studies have been conducted on the high-temperature oxi-
dation properties of austenitic and heat-resistant steels [9, 10]. Most studies were 
performed in atmospheres with high carbon or sulfur contents and at temperatures 
below 800 °C [11]. Parabolic oxidation behavior was observed for HK40 (Fe-22Ni-
25Cr-1.1Mn) after isothermal exposures for 6–100  h between 875 and 1000  °C 
in high-purity oxygen at 20 kPa [10]. Characterizations of the formed oxide scale 
revealed the presence of a Cr2O3 scale overlaid by an MnCr2O4 scale along with 
Cr and Mn depleted zones and the presence of voids in the underlying alloy [10]. 
Mass losses were measured for cast HK steel (Fe-18Ni-26Cr-0.9Mn-0.2Nb) after 
up to 5000-h exposure in 100-h cycle simulated exhaust gas atmospheres (flowing 
air + 10% H2O) between 650 and 800 °C [9]. Below 800 °C, mass losses were lim-
ited to about 1 mg  cm−2 after 5000 h and were attributed to Cr2O3 volatilization [9]. 
At 800 °C, the formation and spallation of Fe, Cr- Ni, and Si mixed oxides resulted 
in about − 5 mg  cm−2 mass losses after 5000 h [9]. Isothermal oxidation of HH-type 
steel (Fe-24Cr-11.5Ni) with two different Nb contents (0.8 and 0.1%Nb) was studied 
in atmospheric air for 1–960 h at 900 °C [12]. For both specimens, Cr2O3 scale was 
formed during the first 200 h of exposures and was followed by the formation and 
spallation of Fe-oxides (Fe2O3 and Fe3O4) up to 960 h at 900 °C. The formation of 
Fe-oxides was found to result in higher compressive stresses in the oxide and there-
fore correlating with the observed spallation of the alloy [12].
The impact of additive manufacturing on the oxidation behavior of alloys has been 
studied for Ni-based alloys [13–17] Ti-based alloys [18] and 316 type stainless steel 
[19]. It was found that the surface topography of electron beam melted (EBM) and 
LPBF materials increased the surface area available to oxidize and therefore affected 
the determination of the oxidation rate constants if not considered [13, 16, 18]. The 
microstructure and composition of EBM and LPBF alloys were also found to affect

isothermal oxidation, cyclic oxidation and intergranular oxidation behavior [14, 15, 
17, 19–22]. Greater Mn and Si contents in EBM Hastelloy than in LPBF and wrought 
Hastelloy X increased oxidation rates during isothermal and cyclic oxidation in dry 
air at 950 °C [15]. Larger oxidation rates were measured for LPBF than for wrought 
Ni-based 718 during long-term isothermal oxidation in air between 500 and 600  °C 
[14]. The depth of intergranular oxidation was found slightly higher in LPBF Ni-base 
718 compared to its wrought counterpart [14] but was lower for LPBF Ni-based 625 
than the conventionally manufactured one after long-term isothermal oxidation in air 
at 900 and 1050 °C [17]. Additionally, the oxidation-induced Cr depletion was found 
to be affected both by AM composition and microstructure in [15], where lower Cr 
concentration at the alloy-oxide interface and deeper Cr depletion profiles were meas-
ured for AM alloys with large elongated microstructures. In [19, 20], the microstructure 
of AM materials was also mentioned to affect the oxidation-induced Cr depletion in 
wrought and LPBF 316L oxidized in air and flowing air + 10% H2O between 700 and 
1000 °C, where lower Cr concentration at the alloy oxide interface was measured for 
the wrought alloy than the LPBF alloys. Very different spallation behaviors were also 
observed between AM and wrought Hastelloy X alloys during 50-h [15] and 100-h [23] 
cycle oxidation in dry air at 950 °C. Romedenne et al. [15] observed that EBM alloys 
with large and elongated grains spalled the most, while LPBF and wrought alloys with 
equiaxed grains spalled the least. In addition, the nature and microstructure of the oxide 
scales can also play a role in the spallation behavior since the wrought alloy with finer 
and duplex oxide microstructure spalled more than the LPBF alloy with larger Cr2O3 
only oxide grains [15]. Alloy and oxide scale microstructures also played a role on the 
spallation behavior of cast, EBM and LPBF Haynes 282 alloys during 50-h cyclic oxi-
dation at 950 °C [22]. Finally, Sanviemvongsak et al. [21] observed different spallation 
behavior between wrought, LBM and EBM 718 alloys during 1-h cycles at 900  °C. 
The greater extent of spallation for LPBF alloy 718 compared to wrought alloy was 
attributed to its larger Mn and S contents using thermodynamic calculations and by 
determining the extent of S segregation rate for a FCC(Ni)-S binary system at 900 °C 
using the Langmuir–McLean relationship [21]. However, similar calculations were per-
formed for both Hastelloy X and Haynes 282 and no correlation was found between the 
sulfur segregation rate and the extent of spallation of the alloys [15, 22].
The objective of this study is to understand the effect of microstructure and com-
position on the cyclic oxidation behavior of LPBF and cast HK30Nb steel in air 
and flowing air + 10% H2O at 800  °C. The observed differences in oxidation and 
spallation behaviors between the two materials are discussed in terms of measured 
composition in the underlying alloy, oxide scale nature (single or duplex layers) and 
density of alloy grain boundaries.

Characterization
Before and after high-temperature exposures, the specimens were cross-sectioned, Cu 
electroplated and mounted in conductive epoxy. The mounts were polished using SiC 
papers, diamond pastes (1  µm) and colloidal silica. The initial microstructures were 
etched using 10%vol. oxalic acid etchant and characterized using electron backscatter 
diffraction (EBSD) on a TESCAN MIRA3 XMH Schottky FE-SEM equipped with 
an EDAX Velocity™ Plus EBSD Camera and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS). Scanning electron microscopy was used for alloy and oxide imaging and EDS 
for element composition profiles and maps on the same equipment. EBSD data were 
processed using OIM software, and image analyses were performed using ImageJ 
software.
Compositional data were also acquired on an electron microprobe (JEOL 8200) 
equipped with 4 tunable wavelength dispersive spectrometers (WDS). EDS spectra 
were acquired and processed using a Thermo Scientific Pathfinder EDS system. The 
accelerating voltage was set to 15.0  kV, and the beam current was 20 nA. Elements 
were acquired using analyzing crystals LIFH for Mn Kα, PETJ for Nb Lα, Mo Lα, TAP 
for Si Kα and EDS for Cr Kα, Fe Kα, Ni Kα. Pure standards were used to calibrate 
all elements. Unknown and standard intensities were corrected for deadtime. Standard 
intensities were corrected for standard drift measured between the beginning and the 
end of the run. Interference corrections were applied to Mn Kα for interference by Cr 
Kβ. The ZAF quantification algorithm used was Armstrong/Love Scott [24].
X-ray diffraction analysis was conducted using a PANalytical X’pert diffractometer 
with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å). The detector scanned the sample from 20 to 120° in 
Bragg–Bentano configuration to determine the nature of precipitates before and after 
high-temperature exposures.

Results
As‑Fabricated Grain Morphology and Texture
The as-fabricated microstructure and texture of LPBF specimens was previously 
reported [25]. It was also shown that the scale, morphology of grains and texture 
depend on the AM build parameters [3]. In this study, V-shaped and square-shaped 
grains were observed along and perpendicular to the build direction, respectively 
(Fig. 2), and no grain texture was observed [25]. Gaussian distributions of the grain 
size were observed along and perpendicular to the build directions with a peak cen-
tered around 25 ± 5 μm and corresponding average grain size of 7 ± 7 μm.
Cast HK30Nb had a typical dendritic grain structures of about 100 ± 20 μm size 
(Fig. 3) and colonies of interdendritic Cr, Nb, and Mo precipitates (Fig. 4).

Mass Change in Dry and Wet Air
The mass changes of the LPBF and cast HK30Nb alloys in dry air at 800 °C as 
function of time and square root of time are reported in Fig.  6a, b, respectively. 
Parabolic mass change was observed after about 200  h for the cast and LPBF 
specimens, with slightly slower oxidation kinetics for the LPBF specimen than 
for the cast specimen (0.09 ± 0.02 mg  cm−2 and 0.14 ± 0.5 mg  cm−2 measured for 
LPBF and cast specimens, respectively, after 200 h in Fig.  6). More significant 
mass change variation from one specimen to another was observed for the cast 
alloy in comparison with the LPBF alloy. After two cycles, the mass change devi-
ated from parabolic oxidation kinetics likely due to scale spallation, with signifi-
cant mass losses after 200 h for the LPBF specimens and after 2000 h for the cast 
alloy.

Finally, it has been shown that the surface segregation of sulfur can have a det-
rimental effect on the spallation behavior of alumina formed on single crystal [34, 
35]. For chromia formers, the effect of sulfur was found to play a secondary role 
on the chromia scale adhesion [36]. More recently, Sanviemvongsak and co-authors 
performed thermodynamic calculations to evaluate the effect of free-sulfur on the 
observed differences in spallation behavior between LPBF, EBM and wrought 718 
at 900  °C [21]. Considering two sulfides (Alabandite and Pyrrhotite), the calcu-
lated segregation rates were not in agreement with experimental observation, while 
agreement was found considering only alabandite [21]. However, the authors did not 
characterize and discuss the effect of different alloy microstructures and oxidation 
behaviors that could also play a role on the spallation behavior of alloy 718. Fur -
thermore, Romedenne and co-authors performed similar calculations for Hastelloy 
X and Haynes 282 alloys, but no correlations were found between the sulfur seg-
regation rate and the extent of spallation of the alloys [15, 22]. In this study, using 
the same method described in [21], TCFE11 database, relevant phases and (Cr, Fe, 
Mn)S_B1 sulfide at 800 °C, the calculated segregation rate was higher for the LPBF 
alloy (θ = 8.5%) which spalled more than the cast specimens (θ = 1.4%). Therefore, 
the thermodynamic calculations suggest a potential role of the sulfur segregation 
on the spallation behavior in addition to the alloy and oxide microstructures as dis-
cussed previously. A detailed experimental investigation to evaluate independently 
the role and the extent of each mechanism on the spallation behavior of HK30Nb 
would be necessary.
Effect of Water Vapor
The accelerated Cr loss due to the volatilization of the Cr2O3 and Mn-rich oxide 
scales in the presence of water vapor [37–40] was responsible for the observed 
breakdown of the protective oxidation behavior for the cast specimens after 1000 h 
in wet air at 800 °C (formation of Fe- and Cr-rich mixed oxides in in Fig.  14b and 
Fig. 15). In the present study, the larger amount of grain boundaries in the LPBF 
specimen (Fig.  2) could have allowed faster Cr supply to maintain the Cr2O3 for-
mation after 1000 h in wet air compared to cast specimens in agreement with the 
higher Cr concentration measured below the oxide scale for the LPBF alloy than the 
cast specimen (Fig. 11a). This is in agreement with the work by Siri and co-authors 
which observed similar differences between LPBF and wrought 316L [20]. Indeed, 
lower Cr concentration was measured below the oxide scale for the wrought 316L 
than for the LPBF one after 100 h [19] and 1000 h [20] dry air at 900 °C. This effect 
was also observed in air + 10% H2O at 900 °C and was associated with the formation

Summary and Conclusions
Oxidation, spallation and volatilization behaviors of LPBF and cast HK30Nb alloys 
were studied in 100-h cycle dry air and flowing air + 10% H2O (5000 cm3  min−1) at 
800 °C. The higher Mn concentration in the cast alloy, 1.2wt% versus 0.2wt% for the 
LPBF alloy, led to faster oxidation rate and the presence of an Mn-rich spinel atop 
a Cr2O3 scale for the cast alloy, while a single Cr2O3 oxide layer was observed for 
the LPBF alloy. The specific LPBF microstructure and greater mechanical strength 
at high temperature likely resulted in lower stress relaxation in the alloy and thus 
larger extent of oxide spallation for the LPBF alloy in comparison with the cast 
alloy. In addition, thermodynamic calculations suggested that the sulfur segregation 
rate was higher for the LPBF alloy than for the cast material at the alloy/Cr2O3 scale 
interface, which could decrease oxide scale adhesion, but contradictory results were 
reported in the literature. Additional experiments are needed to further assess the 
effect of sulfur segregation on oxide scale spallation.
