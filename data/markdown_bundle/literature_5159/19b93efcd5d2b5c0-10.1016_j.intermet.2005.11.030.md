Abstract
Iridium is one of the most promising base metals for future high-temperature structural materials. Attempts to improve the high-temperature
strength of Ir have involved solid-solution hardening and coherent hardening. Hf and Zr having a larger atomic size misﬁt with Ir were found to
be the most effective solid-solution hardening and coherent hardening elements on Ir. The idea of multi-component alloying Ir by Hf and Zr was
used for the improvement of high-temperature properties of Ir-based alloys in this work. The results show that the monolithic saturated fcc phase
has an outstanding Vickers hardness at room temperature, whereas a dual-phase fcc/L1
2 structure is favorable for high-temperature strength and
creep resistance. With a dual-phase fcc/L1 2 structure composed mostly of fcc phase, the Ir e5Hfe5Zr alloy has a 0.2% yield strength as high as
175 MPa, and a stable creep rate as low as 1.33 /C2 10/C255 7 S/C255 1 at a stress of 40 MPa even at 1950 /C14 C. Finally, a principle for the design of Ir-based
alloy based upon the composition and microstructural morphology was discussed.

1. Introduction
To develop new generation high-temperature structural
materials at temperatures beyond 2000 /C14 C, Ir-based refractory
superalloys have been investigated and developed. Similarly
to Ni-based superalloys, Ir equilibrates with most of the
transition metals, such as Ti, Nb, Hf, Zr, Ta, and V , and forms
a coherent dual-phase fcc/L1
2 structure, where the L1 2 phase
is the Ir 3Me intermetallics. Approaches to improve the
high-temperature strength of Ir-based alloys should involve
solid-solution hardening, coherent and incoherent precipitate
hardenings [1e5].
Research has revealed that three factors affect solid-
solution hardening. They are the structure difference between
Ir and the alloying element, the size misﬁt parameter and the
solubility limitation of the alloying element in Ir [6,7]. The
larger the size misﬁt parameter, the lower the solubility, and,
subsequently, the higher the solid-solution hardening rate.
With a coherent relationship to the fcc phase, Ir
3Me interme-
tallics precipitates may also play an important role in the high-
temperature strength of the Ir-based alloys by coherent and
incoherent precipitate hardenings. The effect of coherent and
incoherent precipitate hardenings in the Ir eMe alloy depends
on the lattice misﬁt between the fcc and the L1
2 phases (Me:
Zr, Hf, Nb, Ti, Ta and V). With increasing lattice misﬁt,
morphology of the L1 2 phase would change from an irregular
shape, to a cube, and ﬁnally to a plate-like structure [2].
The plate-like L1 2 precipitates may increase the strength of
Ir-based alloys by impeding dislocation movement [5].
Comparing Ir and transition metals, such as Zr, Hf, Nb, Ta,
V and Ti, the elements Hf and Zr seem to be potential candi-
dates for simultaneously maximizing solid-solution hardening
and coherent precipitate hardening in Ir, because the Ir/Zr and
Ir/Hf couples have an atomic size misﬁt as high as 16.5%, and
the fcc/L1
2 structure (Ir 3Hf and Ir 3Zr) has a peak level lattice
misﬁt of about 2.4 e2.7%. Researches have showed that with

2. Experimental procedures
Due to the unavailability of an Ir eHfeZr ternary phase di-
agram, four alloys with different microstructures were care-
fully designed according to the Ir eHf and Ir eZr binary
phase diagrams [8] for preliminary investigations of the de-
pendence of mechanical response of Ir eHfeZr alloys on mi-
crostructure; they are Ir e3Hfe1Zr alloy with an un-saturated
fcc phase, Ir e3Hfe3Zr alloy with a saturated monolithic fcc
phase, Ir e5Hfe5Zr alloy with a dual-phase fcc/L1
2 structure
and Ir e15Hfe10Zr alloy with a monolithic L1 2 phase. Ele-
ments with 99.9 mass% or higher purity were used. Button in-
gots were prepared by arc-melting in a vacuum for ﬁve or six
times to homogenize compositions throughout ingots. The in-
gots were ﬁnally annealed at 2000
/C14 C for 24 h. The phases
were identiﬁed at room temperature using the high-resolution
X-ray diffraction technique and the microstructure was ob-
served using scanning electron microscope (SEM). The Vick-
ers hardness of bulk materials and each phase was measured
under applied loads of 4.9 N (500 g) and 0.147 N (15 g), re-
spectively. Seven to 10 points were taken for indentation to ob-
tain an accurate average. Compression and creep tests were
conducted at 1950
/C14 C in a vacuum at an initial strain rate of
3 /C2 10/C255 4 s/C255 1 and at a stress of 40 MPa, using an Instron
8560 testing machine. The test cylinders were 3 mm in diam-
eter and 5 mm in length, and the cylinder surfaces were ﬁnally
polished using 1000 mesh SiC paper prior to testing.
3. Results and discussions
3.1. Phase and microstructure
The X-ray diffraction patterns of the four samples heat-
treated at 2000
/C14 C for 24 h are shown in Fig. 1 . All the peaks
detected on the X-ray diffraction patterns of the Ir e3Hfe
(1e3)Zr alloys correspond exactly to Ir fcc phase and no
evident L1 2 peaks were observed, as seen in Fig. 1 (a) and
(b). This means that the samples with a nominal composition
of Ire3Hfe(1e3)Zr should have a monolithic fcc microstruc-
ture. The lattice parameters of the Ir e3Hfe1Zr and Ir e3Hfe
3Zr are about 0.386 and 0.3863 nm, respectively. The X-ray
diffraction peaks of the fcc and L1
2 phases are clearly ob-
served for the Ir e5Hfe5Zr alloy, as seen in Fig. 1 (c). In the
Ire5Hfe5Zr alloy, the lattice misﬁt between the fcc and L1 2
phases is about 2.48%, very close to that of the eutectic
Ire15Hf alloy, about 2.4% [2]. Since the Ir e15Hfe10Zr alloy
has a nominal composition the same as the stoichiometric ratio
of the L1
2 phase Ir3(Hf,Zr), only X-ray diffraction peaks of the
L12 phase were detected in the Ir e15Hfe10Zr alloy (see
Fig. 1 (d)).
Fig. 2 shows SEM morphologies of the four alloys heat-
treated at 2000 /C14 C for 24 h. The Ir e3Hfe1Zr and Ir e
15Hfe10Zr alloys have a monolithic fcc and L1 2 phases,
respectively, as seen in Fig. 2 (a) and (d). Black holes on the
L12 phase matrix are etching pits. It is thought that the Ir e
3Hfe3Zr alloy is very close to prefect monolithic fcc phase
even though small L1 2 particles of size up to 5 m were found
in the fcc matrix, as shown in Fig. 2 (b). The microstructure
of the Ir e5Hfe5Zr alloy is a dual-phase fcc/L1 2 structure,
the fcc phase is white and the L1 2 phase is grey, as seen in
Fig. 2 (c). The volume fraction of the L1 2 phase is about
15e20%. EPMA analysis reveals that the composition of the
fcc phase in the Ir e5Hfe5Hf alloy is about Ir e3.06Hfe2.86Zr,
similar to that in the Ir e3Hfe3Zr alloy, which proves that
the sum of solubility of Hf þ Zr in Ir is actually very close
to the nominal composition of Ir e3Hfe3Zr. Therefore, the
fcc phase in the Ir e3Hfe3Zr and Ir e5Hfe5Zr is at the satu-
rated state. Comparing Fig. 1 with Fig. 2 suggests that micro-
structural observations for the four alloys are in consistent
with the X-ray diffraction analysis. Thus, four different micro-
structural morphologies are obtained in the Ir eHfeZr ternary
alloys by careful composition design.
3.2. The Vickers hardness (HV)
The Vickers hardness (HV) of the four heat-treated samples
is shown in Table 1, in which the HV of pure Ir and Ir e3Hf
alloy is also reported for comparison. Pure Ir has an HV value
of about 329, which is doubled when 3 mol% Hf is added. A
considerable improvement in the HV is achieved with a further
addition of Zr. The peak HV value of about 834 is reached
when the sum of solutes of Hf þ Zr is close to the solubility
of about 6 mol% in Ir, or, when the microstructure is a
monolithic saturated fcc phase. Further increasing Hf and Zr
contents results in formation of the L1
2 phase in the Ir e
5Hfe5Zr alloy, and the HV decreases. This is because the
L12 phase shows a lower HV value of about 408, as seen for
the Ir e15Hfe10Zr alloy. It is seen that the monolithic satu-
rated fcc is the hardest in the Ir eHfeZr ternary alloys, while
those alloys containing un-saturated fcc phase or the L1 2 phase
have a low HV value. The fcc or the L1 2 phase deforms plas-
tically at room temperature with no cracks showing around the
Vickers hardness indentation within the fcc or the L1
2 grain.

4. Discussion
It is known that a small amount of Hf and Zr can effectively
strengthen pure Ir at room temperature and at 1950
/C14 C.
However, the dependence of strengthening on the microstruc-
ture at 1950
/C14 C is a little different to that at room temperature.
For example, at room temperature, the highest HV is found for
the alloy with a monolithic saturated fcc phase (Ir e3Hfe3Zr,
see Table 1), while the s0.2 and peak strength smax at 1950 /C14 C
are a maximum when the alloy has a dual-phase fcc/L1 2
structure (Ir e5Hfe5Zr, see Fig. 3 ). Nevertheless, multi-
component alloying, the saturated solid solution of the fcc
phase and the coherent relationship between the fcc and L1
2
phases could best lead to hardening at room or high tempera-
tures when these hardening methods were combined; there-
fore, a principle of composition and microstructure design
for the improvement of strength of the Ir-based alloys at
both room and high temperatures in this work has been
demonstrated and a considerable strength increase has been
obtained.
With a monolithic saturated fcc structure, the Ir e3Hfe3Zr
alloy has the highest HV of about 834, and maximum solid-
solution hardening is achieved. When 3 mol% Hf and
3 mol% Zr is added, the lattice parameter increases from
0.3819 nm of pure Ir to 0.3863 nm. Such large lattice param-
eter change results in outstanding solid-solution hardening. In
general, a high solution hardening rate is related to a structure
difference (hcp for Hf and fcc for Ir), large size misﬁt param-
eter and low solubility of solute to solvent [6,7]. The solid-
solution hardening on Ir supplied by Hf is mainly attributed
to the structure difference, the moderate solubility (5 mol%,
solid-solution processing at 2000
/C14 C) and the large size misﬁt
parameter (16.5%), i.e., the lattice parameter. For Zr, the struc-
ture difference (bcc for Zr) may also contribute to the harden-
ing of Ir, besides the large size misﬁt parameter of 17% and
the low solubility of 5 mol% (solid-solution processing at
2000
/C14 C) [8]. The interaction between Hf and Zr improving
the solid-solution hardening of Ir should be evaluated, since
this is the key of the multi-component alloying principle.
The degradation of the bulk HV of a dual-phase fcc/L1
2 struc-
ture is caused by the soft L1 2 phase. Similar results have also
been observed in other Ir-based alloys. In our early work [9],
multi-component alloying and saturated solid-solution harden-
ing had been investigated in Ir eHfeNb ternary alloys. For the
monolithic saturated Ir solid solution containing 3 mol% Hf
and 5 mol% Nb, the HV is 878, while that for a dual-phase
Ir-based alloy with only 5 mol% Hf and 7 mol% Nb is 690.
It is believed that saturated solid-solution hardening is the
main contributor to the room temperature strength of Ir-based
alloys, and dislocation movement obstructed by solute atom is
the dominant mechanism.
Comparing mechanical responses of the Ir e3Hfe3Zr alloy
and the Ir e5Hfe5Zr alloy it is clear that outstanding coherent
precipitate hardening is found at 1950
/C14 C. The 0.2% yield
strength and the peak strength rapidly increase from about
125 and 235 MPa for monolithic saturated fcc phase to about
175 and 465 MPa for a dual-phase fcc/L1 2 structure. In
addition, the stable creep rate of the latter is one-sixth of the
former. The difference of these properties is believed to beneﬁt
from the coherent precipitate hardening. Research has showed
that the lattice misﬁt between the fcc and L1
2 phases in
Ire15Hf and Ir e15Zr binary alloys is as high as 2.4 e2.7%
[2]. For the Ir e5Hfe5Zr alloy in this work, the lattice misﬁt
between the fcc and L1 2 phases is about 2.48%. With such
high lattice misﬁt, the coherent relationship of the fcc and
L1
2 phases greatly promoted the high-temperature strength.
It is believed that the coherent multiphase structure is the
main factor for the excellent behavior of Ir eHfeZr ternary
alloys at elevated temperatures, considering both strength and
creep resistance.
Preliminary investigations reveal that multi-component
alloying by Hf and Zr, which have a large atomic size misﬁt
in Ir, can improve the strength of the Ir-based alloys greatly
at both room and high temperatures through solid-solution
or coherent precipitate hardening. In this work, the strength
of the Ir-based alloys is increased to as high as 175 MPa
even at 1950
/C14 C when the alloys compose of a dual-
phase fcc/ L1 2 structure. Applications of these Ir eHfeZr
ternary alloys at or above 1950 /C14 C under monotonic
compression or creep conditions may be expected after
a further composition and microstructure optimization.
For this purpose, some major issues concerning the
IreHfeZr alloys under development need to be studied.
These issues basically include microstructural optimization
by composition design and analysis of deformation and
failure mechanisms under the monotonic compression and
creep conditions, in order to obtain the best strength at the
elevated temperatures.

5. Conclusions
By composition design, Ir eHfeZr ternary alloys with dif-
ferent microstructures were obtained and the following con-
clusions were drawn:
(1) In the Ir eHfeZr ternary alloys, solubility of Hf þ Zr on
the Ir solid solution is about 6 mol%. A single L1
2 phase
appears at compositions of Hf þ Zr larger than 25 mol%.
A dual-phase fcc/L1 2 structure exists when compositions
of Hf þ Zr are between 6 mol% and 25 mol%.
(2) The monolithic saturated fcc phase, typically Ir e3Hfe3Zr
alloy, has the highest HV of about 834 at room tempera-
ture, while the L1
2 phase has the lowest HV value.
(3) The dual-phase fcc/L1 2 structure shows an excellent high-
temperature mechanical response, better than the
monolithic saturated fcc phase or the L1 2 phase. With a
dual-phase fcc/L1 2 structure, the Ir e5Hfe5Zr alloy has
a 0.2% yield strength as high as 175 MPa at 1950 /C14 C,
and a stable creep rate as low as 1.33 /C2 10/C255 7 s/C255 1 at a stress
of 40 MPa and at 1950 /C14 C.
