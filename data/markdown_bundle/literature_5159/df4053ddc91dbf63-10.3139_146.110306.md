Phase-field simulation
of c(A1) + c’(L12)+ c’’(D022) three-phase
microstructure formation in Ni-base superalloys
The calculation of c(A1) + c’(L12)o r c(A1) + c’’(D022)
two-phase microstructure changes in Ni-base superalloys
has been one of the main subjects in the phase-field method,
because the morphology of these two-phase microstruc-
tures crucially influences the high temperature mechanical
properties of Ni-base super alloys. In this study, more com-
plicated phase decomposition behavior, in which three
phases, c (A1), c’(L12) and c’’(D022) are simultaneously in-
cluded, is modeled on the basis of the conventional phase-
filed method. The results obtained are as follows: The pres-
ent simulation model reasonably reproduces the complex
feature of microstructure changes including c, c’ and c’’
three phases, e. g. the characteristic morphology of the c’
particles covered with c’’phase is simulated realistically.
The morphology of the microstructure is mainly controlled
by the elastic strain energy induced from the lattice mis-
matches among these three phases. In particular, the magni-
tude relation among the lattice parameters of the three
phases is a key factor to control and understand the morpho-
logical changes of the complex microstructure including c,
c’and c’’three phases.
Keywords: Phase-field method; Phase transformation;
Phase decomposition; Diffusion equation; Superalloy
1. Introduction
It is well known that the c’(L12) phase is a primary strength-
ening phase in Ni-base superalloys utilized for turbine
blades, and the mechanical properties at high temperature
are crucially influenced by the morphology of the micro-
structure [1]. Therefore, the calculation of c(A1) + c’(L1
2)
two-phase microstructure changes has been one of the main
subjects in the phase-field simulations [2 – 4]. On the other
hand, as for the turbine disks, the primary strengthening
phase is known to be a c’’(D0
22) phase which has a tetrago-
nal crystal structure [1]. Since the tetragonal phase has three
variants depending on its c-axis orientation when it precipi-
tates in a cubic matrix, the c(A1) + c’’(D0
22) two-phase mi-
crostructure often shows very complicated morphology
such as chessboard microstructure, chessboard-like micro-
structure, and maze-microstructure [5]. These complex mi-
crostructures have also been simulated based on the phase-
field method [6, 7], in which the complex morphology of
the experimentally observed microstructure is calculated
realistically. Therefore, the basic calculation method of the
phase-field simulation relevant to the two-phase micro-
structure, c(A1) + c’(L12)o r c(A1) + c’’(D022), is now
well established. In this study, we move on to the
c(A1) + c’(L1
2)+ c’’(D022) three-phase microstructure for-
mation and try to model the complex microstructure
changes including c(A1), c’(L1
2) and c’’(D022) three phases
on the basis of the conventional phase-field method [2].

(1) or (2), is the major one that controls the microstructure
changes of Fig. 1.
2. Simulation method
The phase-field method is the computer simulation method
for calculating the dynamics of temporal microstructure
changes by solving the continuum non-linear evolution
equations for the conserved and non-conserved order pa-
rameter fields [2]. Since the crystal structure of c and c’
phases is cubic and the c’’phase has a tetragonal structure
with three variants depending on the c-axis orientation, the
following variables are required to describe the morphology
of the ( c + c’+ c’’) three-phase microstructure in phase-
field method. The local volume fraction of i phase, f

microstructure changes are also accounted.

3. Simulation results and discussion
3.1. Phase-field modeling of microstructure changes
Figure 3 shows the two-dimensional simulation of the iso-
thermal phase transformation and microstructure changes
including c, c’and c’’three phases. Black and blue parts
are c matrix and c’ particle, respectively. Red and green
parts are c’’phases with different variants, each c-axis of
which is indicated by an arrow (see upper-right corner of
Fig. 3).
Initial state (a) is a supersaturated solid solution with
small fluctuation of order parameter fields generated by a
random number in the computer. At the early stage of
aging, the c’ phase is formed (see (b) and (c)), then c’’
phase precipitates at the c/c’interface region (see (c) and
(d)). In particular, the red c’’phase which has the c-axis
along the horizontal direction is formed preferentially at
the both side regions of a c’ particle, on the other hand,
the green c’’phase which has the c-axis along the vertical
direction is formed at the top and bottom regions of a c’
particle, as shown in (d). This specific variant selection
takes place due to the effective relaxation of lattice mis-
match among three phases. Therefore, this precipitation
behavior is strongly controlled by the elastic strain en-
ergy. With further aging, the shape of c’’(D0
22) phase
changes to a plate and simultaneously the c’(L12) particles
wet the c’’(D022) plate as shown in (e) and (f). When we
compare the simulated microstructures of Fig. 3 with the
experimental microstructures in Fig. 1, the morphological
feature of the calculated microstructure agrees well with
the experimental results. We note that the present simula-
tion model reproduces the complex feature of microstruc-
ture changes including c, c’and c’’three phases, reason-
ably.
3.2. The effect of the gradient energy coefficient
on the microstructure
Since the L1 2 and D0 22 crystal structures resemble each
other, the interfacial energy at c’/c’’interface will be smal-
ler than that of c/c’(or c/c’’) interface as mentioned in the
introduction. Therefore, the magnitude relation among the
gradient energy coefficient is assumed to be j12 ¼ j13 and
j23 ¼ j12=2 in the simulation of Fig. 3. If we change
this balance of gradient energy coefficients, i. e., j12 ¼
j13 ¼ j23, the phase decomposition behavior of Fig. 3
slightly changes to Fig. 4. Although the global sequence of
microstructure changes is almost the same between Fig. 3
and Fig. 4, i. e., the preferential variant selection of c’’phase
at the c/c’interface is hardly changed, but the frequency of
the heterogeneous formation of c’’phase at the c/c’inter-
face in Fig. 4d is decreased compared with the case of
Fig. 3d. Note that the microstructure of Fig. 4d is different
from the experimental evidence of Fig. 1b, the assumption
of j
23 ¼ j12=2, i. e., the interfacial energy at the c’/c’’inter-
face is smaller than that of the c/c’ (or c/c’’) interface,
j23 < j12ðor j13Þ, will be reasonable at least from a qualita-
tive point of view. It is concluded that the sufficient forma-
tion frequency of c’’phase on c’particle is required in order
to obtain the characteristic morphology of the co-precipi-
tates that is the c’particles are covered with c’’phase [8].

matches and ignored the effect of the elastic inhomogeneity
for simplicity. Generally speaking, the experimental obser-
vations are attributed to either effect or both. But it can be
concluded at least that the lattice mismatches among c, c’
and c’’phases are the important factors for controlling the
microstructure morphology. Furthermore, the wetting is of-
ten discussed based on the interfacial energy only, but these
simulation results demonstrate that the wetting is controlled
not only by the interfacial energy but also by the elastic
strain energy.
4. Conclusions
In this study, the complex microstructure formation ob-
served in the practical Ni-base superalloy (Inconel 718 type
alloy) including c, c’and c’’three phases was simulated by
using the conventional phase-field method. The results ob-
tained are as follows:
(1) When we compare the phase-field simulation results
with the experimental microstructures that have been
investigated by Cozar and Pineau, the morphological
feature of the calculated microstructure is in good
agreement with the experimental results. Therefore,
the present simulation model reproduces the complex
feature of the microstructure changes including c, c’
and c’’three phases, reasonably.
(2) In order to produce the characteristic morphology of
the co-precipitate, i. e. a c’particle is covered with c’’
phase, the sufficient formation frequency of c’’phase
on a c’particle is required, which will be influenced
by the low interfacial energy at the c’/c’’interface.
(3) Morphology of the microstructure is mainly controlled
by the elastic strain energy induced by the lattice mis-
matches among the three phases. In particular, the mag-
nitude relation among lattice parameters of c, c’and c’’
phases is a key factor to control and understand the
morphological changes of the complex microstructure
including c, c’and c’’three phases.
