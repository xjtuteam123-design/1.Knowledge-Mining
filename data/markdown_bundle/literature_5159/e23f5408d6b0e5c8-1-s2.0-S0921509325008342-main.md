ABSTRACT
Nickel-based superalloys prepared by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) typically exhibit poor mechanical prop -
erties at intermediate or high temperatures, particularly in terms of the strength-ductility balance, which limits 
their application in the aerospace field. Therefore, achieving a better match between strength and ductility under 
intermediate-temperature serving conditions is crucial for the development of LPBF nickel-based superalloys. 
This study aims to enhance the strength and ductility at 650 
◦
C by designing the addition of Y in Inconel 718 
alloy (IN718) with higher carbon content. In this paper, three IN718 alloys with different Y contents (0, 0.06, 
0.34 wt%) were designed, and the effects of Y on the formability, microstructure and mechanical properties were 
investigated. Results indicated that the addition of a trace amount of Y (0.06 wt %) did not affect the formability 
of the alloy. However, as the solidification temperature range of the alloy widened by increasing Y content, 
solidification cracks in grain boundaries formed in the alloy with 0.34 wt% Y. Additionally, the strong affinity of 
Y to oxygen led to the formation of multi-scale oxides during the LPBF alloy process, which reduced grain size, 
weakened texture strength, and decreased dislocation density. Tensile tests showed that the ultimate tensile 
strength of the 0.06Y-IN718 alloy reached 1037 MPa at 650 
◦
C. Due to the addition of Y element, the elongation 
of 0.06Y-IN718 alloy is 25 % at 650 
◦
C, which is 233.3 % higher than that of 0Y-IN718 alloy and exceeds that of 
cast and wrought IN718 alloy. In this study, a new composition optimization strategy is proposed, which pro -
vides a theoretical basis for the industrial application of high-performance nickel-based superalloys produced by 
LPBF.
1. Introduction
Nickel-based superalloys play an indispensable role in the aerospace 
field due to the excellent high-temperature mechanical properties, 
corrosion resistance, and oxidation resistance [ 1 , 2 ]. However, common 
manufacturing methods such as casting and forging face numerous 
limitations in the fabrication of complex-shaped components [ 3 ]. As an 
advanced additive manufacturing technique, laser powder bed fusion 
(LPBF) technology offers a highly promising approach for fabricating 
complex structural components, demonstrating unique advantages [ 4 ,
5 ]. However, in practical applications, the LPBF technology still con -
fronts a series of formidable challenges. Among them, the effective 
improvement of the strength-ductility matching of the formed parts to 
meet increasingly stringent engineering demands has emerged as one of 
the most prominent issues [ 6 – 10 ].
At room temperature, the tensile properties of LPBF superalloys are 
superior to those of forged or cast alloys [ 11 ]. This is attributed to the 
repeated compression-tension cycles caused by the non-uniform local 
heating and cooling during the LPBF process, which leads to the for -
mation of cellular structure composed of high-density dislocations 
within the alloys [ 12 , 13 ]. During deformation, the cellular structure can 
impede dislocation movement, resulting in hardening effect. Re -
searchers have attempted to incorporate various elements to enhance 
the mechanical properties of the alloys. For instance, adding trace 
amounts of grain boundary strengthening elements to nickel-based su -
peralloys can enhance their mechanical properties. Boron (B) can

Additionally, hafnium (Hf) can enhance grain boundary strength [ 17 ], 
but it also increases crack sensitivity in the CM247LC alloy [ 18 ]. Carbon 
(C) is another common grain boundary strengthening element, since 
carbides can inhibit grain boundary sliding and migration, enhancing 
tensile strength and creep resistance [ 19 ]. The addition of high C content 
in the traditional processing of alloys tends to form skeletal MC-type 
carbides, which may degrade the ductility and creep resistance of the 
alloy [ 20 ]. This adverse effect constrained carbon content in cast/forged 
nickel-based superalloys to below 0.2 wt% [ 21 ]. However, the unique 
conditions of additive manufacturing, characterized by high cooling 
rates and thermal gradients, facilitate the formation of refined micro -
structures and fine precipitates, which may allow for the incorporation 
of higher C contents. Zhou et al. [ 22 ] found that an increase in C content 
from 0.11 wt% to 0.6 wt% in LPBF IN738 alloy can improve the form -
ability. In another study on IN738, the addition of 0.55 wt% C also 
yielded a crack-free IN738 alloy after LPBF processing [ 23 ]. It is worth 
noting that this positive effect of C on formability does not occur in all 
systems of AM superalloys. It is reported that increasing the C content in 
additive manufactured superalloys raises the difficulty of forming [ 24 ,
25 ], leading to the increased crack density of the alloy reported by Chen 
et al. [ 24 ]. Consequently, the effect of C content on nickel-based su -
peralloys varies under different processing methods and alloy 
compositions.
Notably, LPBF superalloys typically exhibit lower ductility at inter -
mediate temperatures, primarily due to oxygen accumulation at grain 
boundaries and crack tips [ 26 ]. This accumulation causes LPBF super -
alloys to embrittlement at intermediate to high temperatures [ 27 ]. 
Additionally, excessive oxygen can disrupt the dynamics of the melt 
pool, causing spattering and spheroidization, which negatively affect the 
quality of parts [ 28 , 29 ]. Rare earth elements are commonly added to 
mitigate oxygen impurities in superalloys [ 30 ], with yttrium (Y) being 
particularly effective and cost-efficient for oxygen removal, which helps 
to improve creep properties and oxidation resistance [ 31 , 32 ]. Zhang 
et al. [ 33 ] reported that the addition of Sc and Y to Ren ´e 104 signifi -

grain and promoting the formation of high-density stacking faults, 
Lomer-Cottrell locks, and nano-Al
3

et al. [ 34 ] found that by adding 0.07 wt% Y into IN718 alloy, the 
ductility was improved by adjusting the morphology of δ phase at grain 
boundaries, grain boundary coalescence strengthening, and oxygen 
purification. However, excessive Y content can promote the formation of 
the Ni
5
Y brittle phase, which accelerates the nucleation and propagation 
of cracks, leading to a reduction in ductility. Banoth et al. [ 35 ] incor -
porated 0.046 wt% Y into Hastelloy-X, which eliminated cracks. Due to 
the purification effect of Y on O, the elongation of Y-containing alloys 
improved at intermediate and high-temperature tensile tests, and their 
creep performance was enhanced. In another study by the same team 
[ 36 ], the formability of the alloy initially increased and then decreased 
as the Y content reached 0.12 wt%. Conversely, Cheng et al. [ 37 ] found 
that no cracks occurred in LPBF Hastelloy-X when the Y content was 
increased to 0.76 wt%. Therefore, the effect of Y on the formability and 
the mechanical properties of LPBF superalloy has been controversial.
In summary, adding grain boundary strengthening elements or rare 
earth elements alone does not simultaneously improve the strength and 
ductility of LPBF superalloys. To solve this problem, a composition 
design idea was proposed in this study, that is, the content of C was 
increased (about 0.44 wt%) in IN718 superalloy, and Y element was 
added at the same time to achieve synergistic improvement of strength 
and ductility. The effects of Y content on the formability, microstructure 
and tensile properties of the alloys were investigated. Moreover, the 
influence of Y on the hot cracking of IN718 alloy was clarified. The 
modulation mechanism of high strength-ductility at 650 
◦
C in IN718 
alloys prepared by LPBF was explained under the dual effects of C- 
increasing and Y-modulation. This research provides a reference for the 
compositional optimization of nickel-based superalloys with excellent 
performance fabricated by LPBF at intermediate temperatures.

3. Results
3.1. Powders characterization
Fig. 3 (a – c) show the morphologies of the 0Y, 0.06Y, and 0.34Y alloy 
powders obtained via gas atomization, predominantly consisting of 
spherical and near-spherical particles, with sphericities of 84.6 ± 2.6 %, 
85.2 ± 2.9 %, and 82.3 ± 3.5 %, respectively. Some satellite particles 
are adhered to the surface of the powders. The morphologies of the three 
powders are similar, indicating that the addition of Y has no adverse 
effect on the surface quality of the powders. The particle size distribu -
tions of the 0Y, 0.06Y, and 0.34Y alloy powders all follow a normal 
distribution, as depicted in Fig. 3 (d – f). With increasing Y content, the 
median particle size (D
50
) of the alloy powders decreases from 35.64 μ m 
(0Y) to 29.94 μ m (0.34Y).
The elemental distribution of the cross-sections of 0Y, 0.06Y, and 
0.34Y alloy powders is characterized by EPMA, as shown in Fig. 4 . The 
results indicate that the addition of Y has little effects on the element 
segregation of the powders. Within the powders, Ti and Nb elements 
exhibit dendritic segregation, which is consistent with the findings of 
Wei et al. [ 38 ]. Enrichment regions of O element are observed in all 
three alloy powders, indicating the presence of oxides, which is an 
inevitable phenomenon during the preparation and storage of metal 
powders [ 34 ]. Furthermore, Y element is uniformly distributed in the 
0.06Y and 0.34Y alloy powders, as shown in Fig. 4 (b and c).
3.2. Formability and microstructure of LPBF alloy samples
Lack of fusion and gas pores are the two primary porosity types 
during the LPBF process. The lack of fusion pores typically exhibit an 
irregular shape, with sizes ranging from tens of micrometers to several 
millimeters, generally attributed to insufficient energy input [ 39 ]. In 
this study, no lack of fusion pores is detected in the 0Y, 0.06Y, and 0.34Y 
alloy samples, while some regular gas pores with sizes below 20 μ m were 
observed. Furthermore, no cracks are found in the 0Y and 0.06Y alloy 
samples ( Fig. 5 (a – c)), while the 0.34Y alloy exhibited numerous cracks 
along the building direction, with 100~200 μ m in length, as shown in 
Fig. 5 (e). The defect density (pores and cracks) of three samples was 
quantified using Image Pro Plus software, with the results illustrated in 
Fig. 5 (g). The defect densities of 0Y and 0.06Y alloy samples are allless 
than 0.05 %. The melt pool with fish-scale-like morphology can be 
observed on the YOZ plane of the alloy samples, as indicated by the 
white dashed lines in Fig. 5(b – d,f) . Statistical analysis shows that the 
width of the melt pool increases and its depth decreases with the in -
crease of Y content ( Fig. 5 (h)).
Fig. 6 presents the X-ray diffraction (XRD) results for the 0Y, 0.06Y, 
and 0.34Y alloy powders and LPBF-processed alloy block samples. All 
the powders and bulk samples primarily exhibit diffraction peaks cor -

the gas-atomized powders, the diffraction peaks of the bulk samples 
broadened, which indicates that the grains of the LPBF alloy have 
become finer [ 40 ]. Fig. 6 (b) is an enlarged view of (111) and (200) 
diffraction peak from Fig. 6 (a). Except for the (111) diffraction peak of

4. Discussion
4.1. Effects of carbon and yttrium on cracking
Carbides are generally viewed as an inducement to crack formation 
in additive manufactured nickel-based superalloys because carbides 
hinder the shrinkage of the residual liquid phase during the later stages 
of solidification [ 24 , 46 ]. However, in this study, the addition of 0.44 wt

microstructure to further enhance the mechanical properties of the 
alloy.
5. Conclusions
In this study, 0.44C - IN718 alloys with varying Y contents were 
prepared by LPBF technology. The effects of Y content on the form -
ability, microstructure, and tensile properties were elucidated. The main 
conclusions are drawn as follows: 
(1) The increase in Y content has little effect on the sphericity of the 
alloy powders, but it reduces the median particle size. Under the 
same printing parameters, adding 0.06 wt% Y, the formability of 
the alloy remains good, and the defect density is less than 0.5 %. 
However, the addition of 0.34 wt% Y to the alloy resulted in the 
formation of solidification cracks
(2) The addition of 0.44 wt% C in IN718 can promote the formation 
of more dispersed carbides. The addition of Y can form multi- 
scale Y-rich oxides within the alloy during the LPBF process. 
Adding an appropriate amount of Y can enhance the ability to 
remove oxygen, purify the grain boundary.
(3) The heterogeneous nucleation effect of the oxides promotes the 
columnar to equiaxed transition and grain refinement in the 
alloy. Upon the addition of 0.06 wt% Y, the aspect ratio of the 
grains decreases to 3.28, accompanied by an average grain size of 
15.17 μ m. The aspect ratio of the 0.34Y alloy further decreased to 
3.26, and the grain size decreased to 15.04 μ m.
(4) Due to the high volume fraction of carbides, IN718 alloy with 
high carbon content exhibits high strength at both 25
