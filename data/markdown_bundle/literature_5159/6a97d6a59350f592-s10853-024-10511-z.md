High‑entropy alloys for nuclear applications

1 Materials Group, Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, Mumbai 400085, India
ABSTRACT
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) because of their attractive properties, provide a 
unique opportunity to develop materials suitable for structural applications in 
the harsh environment (high-temperature and intense irradiation) prevailing in 
Gen IV nuclear power reactors. HEAs based on refractory elements (RHEAs) can 
display excellent stability at high temperatures and superior irradiation resistance 
due to the BCC-based structures obtained in most alloys. In this regard, the design 
and development of RHEAs based on the ZrNbVTiAl system have been actively 
pursued at BARC. Detailed characterization of microstructure and mechanical 
properties of these alloys having equiatomic and non-equiatomic compositions 
have been carried out. The study revealed the possibility of attaining significantly 
higher strengths up to 1.25 GPa and excellent fabricability as a result of dynamic 
recrystallization at high temperatures. In addition, the dissolution of AlZr-type 
intermetallics was observed after proton irradiation which helped in bringing the 
system towards a single-phase BCC structure.
Introduction
Nuclear energy has been crucial in supplying the 
world’s energy needs, as it provides clean, dependable 
and sustainable power that has a potential to signifi-
cantly meet the ever increasing energy demands for 
present and future generations [1]. Traditional nuclear 
power plants, like Pressurized Heavy Water Reactor 
(PHWR) or Pressurized Light Water Reactors (PWRs) 
have long met the aforementioned objectives, but they 
have a lower efficiency (< 30%) and are bulky in dimen-
sion [1, 2]. With emphasis on reduction in carbon foot 
print, advanced nuclear reactors offering enhanced 
safety, efficiency and reduced environmental impact 
have been proposed [3 ]. These reactors encompass a 
diverse range of designs, including liquid metal cooled 
fast reactors, molten salt reactors (MSRs), high-temper-
ature gas cooled reactors (HTGRs) and small modular 
reactors (SMRs), each with unique demands on materi-
als to ensure their functionality and longevity [4 , 5].
However, full realization of their benefits hinges 
upon the meticulous selection and development of 
materials that can withstand extreme environments 
prevailing in these advanced nuclear reactors [6 ]. 
Materials utilized in such advanced reactors must 
endure high temperatures (500–800 °C), non-aqueous 
corrosive environments and intense radiation expo-
sure (~ 100 dpa) [ 6, 7]. Conventional materials fall

In this context, high-entropy alloys (HEAs), which 
represent a revolutionary class of materials that devi-
ate from conventional alloy compositions by incor-
porating multiple elements in nearly equal propor-
tions, could provide a solution [9 ]. It may be noted 
that irradiation imparts defects in materials which 
induce a disorder into the system, thereby increasing 
its entropy. This is the one of the primary reasons why 
ordered alloys, though having attractive properties, 
have never been considered for reactor applications 
[10]. In this backdrop, HEAs provide a unique oppor-
tunity, as the alloys are already in the state of high 
entropy and thus cannot be subjected to further sub-
stantial increase in their entropy by irradiation [11]. 
Therefore, the entropy-driven stability contributes 
to the unique thermodynamic and phase stability of 
HEAs under extreme environments including irra-
diation [12]. In addition, HEAs characterized by the 
multiple principal element approach can present sev-
eral advantages like significantly high melting points, 
enhanced high-temperature mechanical properties, 
and improved irradiation and corrosion resistance 
which make them a compelling alternative to Zircaloy 
in advanced reactor designs [11–14]. Furthermore, the 
ongoing worldwide research in the field of HEAs have 
uncovered several new compositions and processing 
techniques, expanding their potential applications in 
nuclear reactor technology [11–21].
In this regard, certain HEAs consisting predomi-
nantly of refractory elements such as Nb, V, Zr, Ta, 
Hf, Mo and W, commonly referred as refractory high-
entropy alloy (RHEA) has been recently emphasized 
as promising materials for the next-generation high-
temperature reactors [14]. These RHEAs exhibit better 
high-temperature performance due to the high melt-
ing points of constituent elements and superior radia-
tion resistance due to their BCC-type structures which 
are inherently resistant to irradiation swelling [15]. 
Recently, considering applications in high-energy neu-
tron environment (e.g. in fusion reactor), a very recent 
separate class of low-activation HEAs (LAHEAs) have 
been proposed as an alternative to low-activation ferri-
tic/martensitic steels [16]. Table 1 categorizes common 
alloying elements in structural materials according to 
their radioactive decay time limit when subjected to 
high-energy fusion neutrons [17]. As per the accepted 
criterion by fusion community, the radioactivity of 
materials used in the fusion blanket should reduce 
to an acceptable limit (safe for disposal in geological 
repository) within 100 years [18, 19].
It could be observed from Table 1 that only a few of 
the common alloying elements like W, V, Ta, Ti, Cr, V, 
Mn, Fe and Si satisfy this criterion and thus are known 
as low-activation elements which form the major con-
stituents of LAHEAs. These HEAs not only possess 
high melting temperatures, superior mechanical prop-
erties and good oxidation resistance but also minimize 
the quantity of radioactive wastes. Their potential to 
function beyond the maximum operating temperature 
of current materials (e.g.  ~ 550 ℃ in ferritic/martensitic 
steels restricted by its creep) makes them suitable for 
various components in fusion reactor-like plasma fac-
ing material, diverters, etc. [20].
In this aforementioned backdrop, a comprehensive 
programme has been initiated at Materials Group, 
BARC to develop HEAs based on refractory elements 
(i.e. RHEAs) for advanced nuclear reactor’s applica-
tions, and the key activities and findings from this 
research work have been highlighted in this paper. It 
may be noted that HEAs belonging to the RHEA cat-
egory are associated with specific challenges in their 
synthesis primarily due to the high melting point of 
refractory elements [15, 21]. Therefore, before examin-
ing the suitability of such alloys, challenges associated 
with the synthesis of this class of alloys need to be 
addressed. The present paper addresses some of these 
issues like selecting a right composition, introducing

HEAs developed for nuclear applications
The ZrNbVTiAl‑based system
In order to design a HEA composition suitable for 
nuclear applications, the Zr–Ti–Al–Nb–V system was 
selected primarily due to the following unique advan-
tages it offers:
1. Nb and V have BCC-based structures, while Ti 
and Zr show BCC phase at elevated temperatures. 
Therefore, there was a possibility that alloying of 
these elements together may form a BCC phase-
based structure which is resistant to avoid swell -
ing.
2. It has been observed that the equiatomic NbVTiAl 
alloy exhibits a single BCC phase [22]. Since Zr 
demonstrates 100% solubility with Ti, therefore, 
its introduction should increase the overall stabil-
ity this ZrNbVTiAl alloy system [23].
3. Nb, Zr and Al have low neutron absorption cross 
sections, which is a prerequisite for an alloying ele-
ment to be used in nuclear structural materials. [7].
4. Al being a low density element is expected to 
improve the specific strength of the alloy by 
decreasing the overall density. Furthermore, the 
atomic radius of Al (143 pm) is similar to V, Ti and 
Nb which satisfies one of major criteria of forma-
tion of solid solutions in the HEA.
At the initial stage, specimens of the equiatomic 
ZrTiAlNbV alloy were synthesized by arc melting 
under pure argon atmosphere. In spite of melting sev-
eral times in order to achieve chemical homogeneity, a 
small quantity of un-melted Nb was detected in most 
of the alloy buttons. To tackle this issue, the alloy was 
later synthesized by the innovative route of induction 
levitation melting (ILM) route [24]. In ILM, a metal-
lic charge is heated, melted, levitated and continually 
churned with the help of electromagnetic field. In case 
of HEAs, which have a high concentration of several 
elements, ILM provided significant advantages over 
the conventional arc melting route. On one hand, rapid 
heating within the sample ensures better homogeneity 
by introducing a churning action during melting. On 
the other hand, a shorter duration of operation and 
minimal contact with containment walls in the molten 
state reduces chances of contamination.
Figure 1 shows a schematic of the ILM unit includ-
ing a segmented copper crucible which is housed 
inside a quartz tube for maintaining adequate vacuum 
(~  10–3 mbar). It was noted that a single melt in ILM 
could successfully produce the desired homogeneity 
so that multiple remelting was not required. Therefore, 
most of the alloy synthesis was carried out using the 
levitation melting route where an alloy button of ~ 20 g 
weight was obtained in each batch. After successfully 
melting the equiatomic NbVTiZrAl alloy, a non-equi-
atomic alloy composition with the similar constituent 
elements (35Nb25Ti25V10Al5Zr at %) was also melted 
using the same technique. Later, density of the alloy 
buttons was measured using water displacement method 
based on Archimedes principle. For this purpose, a 
Mettler Toledo make electronic weighing balance with 
an accuracy of 0.1 µg was used.

It should be noted that the formation of multiphase 
HEAs has been frequently reported, even though 
HEAs have historically been described as consist-
ing of a single phase [30, 31]. In certain cases, pres-
ence of an ordered 2nd phase in the matrix has led 
to increased mechanical strength [ 32]. For example, 
the CrMnFeCoNiAl 0.75 HEA consisting of a mixture 
of BCC, FCC, and B2-type phases displayed a higher 
yield strength (~ 1.2 GPa) at room temperature dur-
ing compression tests, as compared to the single FCC 
phase CrMnFeCoNi alloy [32, 33]. However, this may 
not always be the case since many aspects, such as 
size, shape, location, stability, etc., may determine the 
effect of intermetallic phases on the overall properties 
of a material at a particular temperature [34, 35]. With 
reference to nuclear applications, Zr-based intermetal-
lics (Zr2Ni, ZrFe2, etc.) invariably exist in Zircaloys, 
which are commonly used as structural material in 
nuclear thermal reactors [36]. Therefore, presence 
of multiple phases in the present case is not deemed 
harmful as long as the objective of developing a high-
strength material for nuclear applications is fulfilled.

Mechanical properties
The true stress–true strain curve obtained after com-
pression tests on the equiatomic ZrNbVTiAl HEA at 
room temperature, as shown in Fig. 8a, exhibited a 
high yield strength (1.045 GPa) followed by strong 
strain-hardening reaching a higher value of UTS at 
1.25 GPa. In addition, the satisfactory ductility exhib-
ited by the ZrNbVTiAl HEA was ascertained by the 
absence of crack initiation before reaching a signifi-
cantly high strain of ~ 14% corresponding to the UTS 
(Fig. 8a). This suggested that the alloy can exhibit suffi-
cient deformability/ workability at room temperature.

Irradiation resistance
Evaluation of radiation damage in HEAs using ener-
getic ions have established their superior radiation 
resistance which has been attributed to sluggish 
mobility of dislocation loops, reduced radiation-
induced segregation and formation of fewer voids as 
compared to conventional alloys [ 49, 50]. However, 
in certain cases, irradiation of BCC-based low-activa-
tion HEAs resulted in the segregation of constituent 
elements like Cr and V atoms at the grain bounda-
ries [51]. Such segregation can adversely affect the 
mechanical properties of the alloy and compromise 
their intended applications. This indicates that it is 
necessary to understand the irradiation response of a 
specific alloy composition to justify its suitability for 
nuclear applications.

It has been reported that irradiation of ordered 
intermetallics may induce disorder and/or amorphi-
zation in them due to the displacement of atoms from 
their original sites in collision cascades and radiation 
enhanced diffusion [60– 62]. The dissolution of pre-
cipitates/intermetallics takes place when the rate of 
radiation enhanced dissolution is greater than their 
growth by irradiation enhanced and thermal diffusion 
[63]. In the case of high-entropy alloys, diffusivity of 
constituent elements is known to be inherently low 
due to multiple sizes of atoms leading lattice distortion 
and their sluggish diffusion [64]. Thus, the effect of dif-
fusion led growth is expected to be less prominent in 
high-entropy alloys, thereby aggravating the chances 
of instability under irradiation.

Summary
As research and development efforts continue in the 
field of HEAs, it is expected that they may play a cru-
cial role in advancing the safety, efficiency and sus -
tainability of nuclear energy technologies. HEAs based 
on refractory elements have evolved to be most suit-
able for applications in next-generation reactors due 
to their excellent strength at high operating tempera-
ture and resistance to higher neutron energies/flux. In 
this context, ZrNbVTiAl system has been explored at 
BARC and found to have promising attributes suita-
ble for advanced nuclear applications. The system has 
been thoroughly examined using both equiatomic and 
non-equiatomic combinations, evaluating microstruc-
tural characteristics, mechanical properties and resist-
ance to irradiation yielding satisfactory results. The 
alloys have displayed a high yield strength (1.045 GPa) 
at room temperature and dynamic recrystallization 
at during high-temperature deformation indicating 
good workability. On the other hand, irradiation of 
these alloys have resulted in reduction of second phase 
intermetallics which can drive the structure towards a 
single BCC phase HEA, which is inherently resistant to 
void swelling. High-entropy alloys derived from this
