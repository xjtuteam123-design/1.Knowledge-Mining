ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Microstructure and Mechanical Behaviors
of Electron-Beam Melted Ta10W Alloy at High
Temperatures
Bang Xiao, Guangyu Yang, Ming Wei, and Jian Wang
Submitted: 19 June 2025 / Revised: 6 August 2025 / Accepted: 1 September 2025
Ta10W refractory alloy was recently fabricated utilizing powder-bed electron beam melting technology. To
investigate the mechanical behaviors of this alloy under a harsh environment, a tensile test and a split-
Hopkinson pressure bar experiment were employed. It has been suggested that the ultimate tensile strength
(UTS) of Ta10W alloy under air is sharply reduced at 600 and 800 /C176C compared to that at room tem-
perature and keeps dropping at 1000 /C176C under argon protection. The detailed UTS values of Ta10W alloy
under 600, 800, and 1000 /C176C are 467, 232, and 111 MPa, respectively. The peak values of the strength of the
Ta10W refractory alloy at 300, 600, and 1000 /C176C in the split-Hopkinson pressure bar experiment are 694,
633, and 400 MPa, with quite high phase stability, respectively. The obtained results provide signiﬁcant
data for the application of the additively manufactured Ta10W refractory alloy in harsh environments.
Keywords additive manufacturing, harsh environment,
high-temperature strength, microstructure, refractory
alloy
1. Introduction
Refractory elements like W, Mo, Ta, Nb, V , Re, Cr, Ti, and
Hf display quite high stability at elevated temperatures, which
indicates excellent performance of these metals under extre-
mely harsh environments (Ref 1-5). Therefore, refractory alloys
are widely accepted in ﬁelds such as the aerospace industry,
biomedical engineering, and nuclear industry (Ref 6-8).
However, the brittleness, high hardness, and high melting
points lead to difﬁculties in manufacturing these refractory
metals or alloys (Ref 9, 10). The thriving additive manufac-
turing technologies, such as laser/electron beam melting of
materials, allow the fabrication of alloys with complex shapes
under the guidance of computer-aided design (Ref 11, 12).
Alloys like WMoTaNb, W, Mo, and many other refractory
alloys are manufactured utilizing additive manufacturing,
which suggests that the trend to develop printable refractory
alloys is unstoppable (Ref 13-16). Therefore, hunting for more
printable refractory alloys is prospering.
The recent investigation into refractory metals, for instance,
tantalum, is prosperous. Researchers studied the microstructure
and mechanical performances of the additively manufactured
pure Ta and Ta alloys (Ref 17, 18). The high melting point
(2997 /C176C) and excellent ductility at room temperature enable
this alloy /C213s inestimable potential to be applied in extremely
harsh environments. Efforts have been made to strengthen Ta;
thus, the Ta10W alloy has been fabricated previously (Ref 19).
Ta10W refractory alloy has excellent mechanical behaviors at
elevated temperatures, which inspires researchers to engage in
further investigation on it (Ref 20-22). Moreover, the desire to
widen the applications of Ta alloys provides more chances for
the Ta10W alloy to be utilized in the aerospace industry.
Additive manufacturing of Ta and Ta alloy progresses quickly,
which also provides opportunities for the high-efﬁciency
fabrication of Ta10W alloy (Ref 20). Our recent work reported
the additively manufactured Ta10W alloy, which suggests the
good ductility and strength synergy of the as-built Ta10W alloy
(Ref 23). However, we also noticed the rarely seen report on
Ta10W alloy working in extremely harsh environments, which
may be an important research direction. In this study, we
investigated the mechanical behaviors and microstructures of
the selective electron beam melted Ta10W alloy under different
conditions, which can provide further insight into the potential
of the additively manufactured Ta10W alloy in applications.
2. Experimental Procedures

Ta and W powders were physically mixed using a blender
for 8 h. The spherical powder Ta and W have diameters smaller
than 50 lm, as well as purity higher than 99.9 wt.%. The
Ta10W alloy was fabricated utilizing a Y150 additive manu-
facturing facility (Xi /C213an Sailong Metal Materials Co., Ltd.),
under a vacuum atmosphere of 10
/C01 Pa. Preheating of the
substrate was accomplished when reaching a temperature of
1100 /C176C. The scanning current is 20 mA with a scanning speed
of 0.85 m/s, and the running voltage of the process is 60 kV .

The as-built Ta10W alloy features dimensions of 50 mm in

2.2 Materials Characterization
The crystal structure of the as-fabricated Ta10W alloy was
displayed using x-ray diffraction (XRD, Brucker D8 AD-
V ANCE) patterns with a Cuka source, a step size of 0.02 /C176, and
a scanning range of 20 /C176-100/C176. Grinding and polishing of the as-
cut surfaces were completed using abrasive papers (with silicon
carbide or SiC particles) and thin silk polishing cloth. The
obtained microstructure and fracture morphology of Ta10W
alloy were observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM,
HITACHI SU5000) with an energy-dispersive x-ray spec-
troscopy (EDX) operating at an acceleration voltage of 20 kV .
The samples were sliced and taken out from the as-deposited
WTaRe alloy and thinned to /C24100 nm using a focused ion
beam (FIB) (ETHOS NX5000). FE-STEM (HITACHI
HF5000) was applied to uncover the microstructure of the
samples with an acceleration voltage of 200 kV .
2.3 Mechanical Tests
The tensile tests were performed (with their axis normal to
the build direction) on an ETM105D W ANCE (100 kN) testing
machine at a constant strain rate of 10 /C03/s. These samples were
fabricated with a gauge length of 16 mm and a diameter of
3 mm (ASTM E8/E8M). The tensile tests were carried out in an
air atmosphere at 600 and 800 /C176C, with an argon atmosphere at
1000 /C176C. The heating duration is 26-50 min with a holding
time of 5 min at the ﬁnal testing temperatures. The Hopkinson
pressure bar experiment (ZDSHPB-20, China) was performed
in a bar diameter of 16 mm, and the dimensions of the samples
were /4m m 9 2 mm. The strain rate was 4000/s, and the
testing temperatures were 300, 600, and 1000 /C176C.
3. Results and Discussion
After the layer-by-layer deposition process (Fig. 1a), the as-
mixed Ta10W powder was transformed into a bulk Ta10W
alloy. The as-fabricated Ta10W alloy is shown in Fig. 1. The
upper layer is quite ﬂat with only a few noticeable over-melted
bulges, which are usually generated with splatter accumulation
caused by the moving electron beam. Moreover, the residual
stress-induced deformation cannot be seen in the as-fabricated
Ta10W alloy. This suggests that powder-bed electron beam
melting is suitable for manufacturing alloys like Ta10W with an
elevated melting point ( /C243000 /C176C), and the Ta10W alloy also
has good printability under stable and appropriate processing
parameters. This seems different from other BCC crystal
structure refractory alloys like WMoTaNb, W, and Mo alloys
and pure metals (Ref 13, 16, 24, 25). The underlying reason
may be the excellent ductility and printability of tantalum and
its alloys (Ref 26, 27).
The tensile engineering stress-strain curves of the as-built
Ta10W alloy deformed at different conditions are shown in
Fig. 2(a). The ultimate tensile strength and the ductility of the
as-built Ta10W alloy are 467 MPa and 1.7% at 600 /C176C in air.
This is signiﬁcantly different from the additively manufactured
Ta10W alloy deformed at room temperature (the room temper-
ature ultimate tensile strength of the as-built Ta10W alloy is
764 MPa) (Ref 23, 28). Some previously reported research
suggests that oxidation of the refractory alloys is commonly
seen in experiments under oxygen or air (Ref 29-31). And
tungsten is peculiarly prone to oxidation. Moreover, previous
studies show that the ductility of alloys will be improved when
deformed at higher temperatures (Ref 10, 32). However, the
experimental results presented here indicate an opposite
tendency compared to the early research. Therefore, it is
believed that the oxidation of the as-fabricated Ta10W alloy
here substantially inﬂuenced the ductility of this alloy. A
similar tendency also occurs in the as-fabricated Ta10W alloy
deformed at 800 /C176C in air. The ultimate tensile strength of the
as-built Ta10W alloy deformed at 800 /C176C is 232 MPa, and the
ductility is 1.7% (a little smaller than that of Ta10W alloy
deformed at 600 /C176C in air when accurate to two decimal
places). This means increasing deformation temperature can
signiﬁcantly weaken the ductility of the as-fabricated Ta10W
alloy. However, the strength degradation is mostly attributed to
the increasing deformation temperature, which is almost a rule
of thumb in metals and alloys (such as refractory alloys) (Ref
33, 34). The ultimate tensile strength and ductility of the as-
built Ta10W alloy are 111 MPa and 8.6% at 1000 /C176C with
argon atmosphere, respectively. Notably, the argon atmosphere
helps improve the ductility of the as-built Ta10W alloy. The
underlying reason may be the attenuated oxygen by Ar, which
undermined the degree of oxidation in the Ta10W alloy. In
Fig. 2(b), the fracture morphology of the additively manufac-
tured Ta10W alloy shows a large deformation crack with
oxidized edges; however, most of the fracture surface is
cleavage fracture mode with some tearing edges. This means
oxidation somewhat inﬂuenced the fracture process of the
additively manufactured Ta10W alloy at 600 /C176C, which led to a
reduction in the ductility. But whether the oxidation affected the
whole strength of the additively manufactured Ta10W alloy at
600 /C176C in air is not certain. In Fig. 2(c), the fracture
morphology is covered by the oxidized residue. This suggests
that intensive oxidation happened during the exposure of the
fresh microstructure of the additively manufactured Ta10W
alloy bit by bit. The crumbs are left and spread along the whole
fracture surface. The detailed microstructure is shown in
Fig. 2(c1), where there are many small cracks and loosely
distributed oxide. Such results indicate a seriously destructed
microstructure during the tensile test, and it prematurely caused
the fracture of the Ta10W sample at 800 /C176C in air. When the
temperature increases to 1000 /C176C, the residual oxygen can
quickly react with the exposed fresh surface and form loose
metallic oxide (the oxidized microstructure is shown in
Figs. 2(d) and (d1)), which is similar to many oxygen-sensitive
refractory metals (Ref 29, 35).
Therefore, the oxidation generated in the as-built Ta10W
alloy during tensile deformation, though the oxidized layer is
quite thin initially (Fig. 2b). The oxidation that occurred in the
as-fabricated Ta10W alloy at 600 and 800 /C176C in air shows a
signiﬁcant inﬂuence of oxygen on the ductility of this alloy
compared to that at 1000 /C176C with argon protection. Such a
result correlates well with previous research that tantalum
alloys need protection above 500-600 /C176C and tungsten above
750 /C176C, but with negligible effects on strength (Ref 19, 36, 37).
Therefore, it can be deduced that increasing temperature still
plays an important role in the strength degradation of Ta10W
alloy (Ref 38).

4. Conclusion
The microstructure and mechanical behaviors of the addi-
tively manufactured Ta10W alloy under extremely harsh
environments are investigated in this study. The obtained
results suggest that,
1. The as-built Ta10W alloy has quite high phase stability at
a temperature range of 300-1000 /C176C under a deformation
strain rate of 4000 s
/C01.
2. The oxidation process has a signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the
tensile ductility of the as-built Ta10W alloy, and the ther-
mal softening effect affects the tensile strength of this al-
loy.
3. The ultimate tensile strength of Ta10W alloy in air at
600, 800, and 1000 /C176C under argon protection is 467, 232,
and 111 MPa, respectively, which suggests that thermal
softening seriously affects the high-temperature behaviors
of the additively manufactured Ta10W alloy.
4. The peak values of the strength of the Ta10W refractory
alloy at 300, 600, and 1000 /C176C in the split-Hopkinson
