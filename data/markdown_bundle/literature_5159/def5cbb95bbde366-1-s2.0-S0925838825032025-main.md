1. Introduction
Thermal-resistant HEAs, based on the concept of HEA, are designed 
through multi-element optimization, particularly by incorporating re -
fractory metal elements. These alloys aim to enhance strength, stability, 
and oxidation resistance under high-temperature conditions for use in 
areas such as aerospace and energy. Research on thermal-resistant HEA 
is typically classified into two categories: high-entropy superalloys 
[1 – 3] and refractory HEA systems [4,5] . While elements like Ti, Hf, Nb, 
Mo, W, and C enhance strengthening, excessive alloying may cause 
embrittlement due to lattice distortion, brittle precipitates, or grain 
boundary segregation, particularly in BCC refractory HEAs [6,7] . This 
brittleness presents a considerable challenge to the processing and 
application of these alloys.
Recent studies have increasingly reported thermal metastability in 
HEAs, including high-entropy superalloys, under high-temperature 
conditions. Particularly during annealing at elevated temperatures, 
many HEAs undergo phase separation and form metastable phases. 
Stepanov et al. [8] reported that after annealing at 873 – 1273 K, the 
hexagonal close packed (HCP) particles enriched Hf and Ta elements in 
the HfNbTaTiZr HEA precipitated within the body-centered cubic (BCC) 
matrix, leading to enhanced hardness. Laplanche et al. [9] investigated

The (Cr, Mo)-rich phase is also identified as the χ phase with a BCC 
structure, as shown in Fig. 4 (d). However, unlike the (Mo, Nb)-rich 
phase, the "streak" characteristic of diffuse scattering is not observed 
in the SAED pattern of the (Cr, Mo)-rich phase, suggesting fewer 
stacking faults in this phase. Table 1 shows that the Cr and Mo contents

significantly lower compared to the alloy annealed for 12 h. This in -
dicates that the (Ni, Al)-rich phase with higher Cr and Mo contents forms 
initially during the early stages of annealing. As the annealing time is 
extended, Cr and Mo elements gradually migrate towards the phase 
boundaries, where the Mo-rich phase first forms and subsequently grows 
to create the (Cr, Mo)-rich phase. The extensive stacking faults in the 
(Mo, Nb)-rich phase significantly contribute to its transformation into 
the (Cr, Mo)-rich phase, promoting this phase evolution. This process 
emphasizes the dynamic changes in phase composition and structure 
during extended heat treatment, which affect the overall microstructure 
and properties of the alloy. In essence, the initial (Ni,Al)-rich phase loses 
Cr and Mo over time, contributing to the growth of (Cr,Mo)-rich phases, 
a process potentially influenced by stacking faults in the (Mo,Nb)-rich 
phase.
3.3. Lattice distortion during annealing
Based on the characteristics of HEA, the mixed entropy during the 
solidification process may hinder the formation of intermetallic com -
pounds, promoting the formation of solid solution phases with severe 
lattice distortion [20,21] . Severe lattice distortion can destabilize the 
single-phase solid solution and promote the precipitation of secondary 
phases, and is therefore considered a key factor contributing to phase 
separation in high-entropy alloys. To investigate whether the observed 
thermal metastability originates from such distortion, the strain field of 
the matrix phase during annealing is analyzed [22 – 24] . GPA is a widely 
used method for quantifying local lattice distortion by calculating strain 
fields relative to a “ virtual ” average lattice constructed from a selected 
region [25 – 27] . Fig. 5 presents the GPA results for the FCC phase in the 
as-cast alloy, showing the strains in the xx, xy, and yy directions. The

3.4. Deformation mechanism
Fig. 9 presents the TEM images of the region near the tensile fracture 
at 973 K of the as-cast alloy. The images reveal that deformation twins 
primarily form in the same direction across the entire FCC dendritic 
structure, with only a small number of cross-twins observed. This in -
dicates that the main deformation mechanism of the alloy at 973 K is 
twin-mediated. In Fig. 9 (e), large number of second-phase particles are 
dispersed and distributed between the twins, appearing as dark regions. 
Fig. 9 (c) and (f) show that second-phase particles are present at both the 
twin boundaries and the junctions where the twins intersect. These 
particles are identified as having an ordered BCC structure, which is 
inferred as the (Ni, Al)-rich B2 phase from the precipitation sequence of 
the metastable phase of the alloy. These microstructural observations 
suggest that during deformation, several mechanisms contribute to 
stress release, including slip, deformation twinning, the formation of 
cross-twins, element diffusion, and the precipitation of the second 
phase.

At position 1, high density of dislocations is observed in the FCC phase.

dislocation twisting is observed in the BCC phase, which is identified as

BCC phase gradually evolve into subcells, as shown in Fig. 10 (d). 
Meanwhile, the FCC solid solution between the ordered phases forms 
deformation twins. The presence of dispersed ordered phases divides the 
original continuous FCC solid solution into smaller segments, which 
significantly increasing the number of potential sites for deformation 
twin formation. The reduced spacing between ordered phases further 
promotes the formation of finer deformation twins, which can reach 
nanoscale dimensions, as shown in Fig. 10 (e, f). At the same time, the 
dispersed ordered phases (including (Ni, Al)-rich, (Mo, Nb)-rich, and 
(Cr, Mo)-rich phases) effectively hinder dislocation movement, making 
its slip more difficult. This provides additional stress that facilitates the 
formation of deformation twins. These observations are visually sum -
marized in Fig. 11 , which illustrates how the dispersion of ordered 
phases promotes multiple stress-relieving mechanisms, including the 
formation of high-density dislocations and deformation twins. There -
fore, the thermal metastability of the alloy resulting from the formation 
of ordered phases plays a crucial role in enhancing the high-temperature 
plasticity of the material [30–33] . By increasing the number of available 
deformation mechanisms, the alloy exhibits enhanced plasticity at 
elevated temperatures, which is beneficial for its high-temperature 
applications.
