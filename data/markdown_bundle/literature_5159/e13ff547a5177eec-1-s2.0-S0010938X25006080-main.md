ABSTRACT
Ni-based superalloys produced via additive manufacturing (AM) face challenges in achieving optimal oxidation 
resistance due to elemental segregation and microstructural anisotropy inherent to the process. In this work, the 
oxidation resistance of additively manufactured Ni-based superalloys AMS-OR was remarkedly improved by heat 
treatment, reducing mass gain by 35 %, and oxide layer thickness by 38 % compared to the as-printed condition. 
This enhancement arises from microstructural optimization during heat treatment: Dendritic W segregation is 
eliminated through heat treatment, which redistributes aluminum into the γ matrix. This redistribution, com -
bined with a lower oxygen partial pressure resulting from the coarser spinel grains in the oxide layer, enhances Al 
availability and promotes the formation of a continuous, protective Al
2
O
3 
layer. In addition, the localized W 
enrichment in dendritic cores in the as-printed alloy led to the formation of volatile WO ₃ , causing porous oxide 
morphologies and transient mass loss. Heat treatment suppresses WO ₃ evolution by homogenizing W distribu -
tion, while partial dissolution of interdendritic (Nb,Ta)C carbides further stabilizes the oxide structure. By 
prioritizing microstructural homogenization over compositional changes, this work demonstrates that post-build 
heat treatment effectively addresses oxidation challenges in additive manufacturing, offering a scalable strategy 
to enhance high-temperature durability for aerospace and energy applications.
1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys are essential for critical applications like in -
dustrial gas turbines and aircraft engine turbine blades due to their high 
strength, resistance to deformation under stress, and strong ability to 
withstand oxidation [1,2] . As operating temperatures and performance 
demands rise, the need for complex-shaped parts has grown beyond 
what traditional manufacturing methods can achieve [3,4] . Additive 
manufacturing (AM) has become a key technology, enabling efficient 
production of intricate metal parts [5] . However, turbine blades exposed 
to extreme heat and mechanical stress still face serious oxidation-related 
damage, such as cracking caused by oxidation and fatigue from pro -
longed stress [6 – 8] . These challenges highlight that oxidation resistance 
remains vital for ensuring the reliability and durability of nickel-based 
superalloys, even when using advanced manufacturing methods like 
AM [9,10] .
Commercial Ni-based superalloys typically rely on sufficient 
additions of Al and Cr to form protective Al
2
O

and Cr

elevated temperatures [11] . Elements such as W, Nb, Ta, Re are further 
incorporated to enhance high-temperature mechanical properties [12] . 
However, W, Nb, and Ta are prone to severe segregation during pro -
cessing, which detrimentally affects oxidation behavior [13 – 16] . 
Post-processing heat treatments are thus essential to mitigate micro -
structural inhomogeneity and optimize performance [17,18] . In 
AM-processed alloys, anisotropic microstructures and elemental segre -
gation introduced during rapid solidification further complicate oxida -
tion dynamics, necessitating tailored heat treatment protocols to 
eliminate these adverse effects [19 – 21] . Whether for conventionally cast 
or AM-fabricated superalloys, understanding the interplay between heat 
treatment, microstructure, and oxidation behavior is vital for real-world 
applications [22] .

performance for both conventionally and additively manufactured su -
peralloys can hardly reach consensus or even yield conflicting

4. Discussion
The experimental results demonstrate the differences in oxidation 
mass gain behaviors and oxide layer characteristics between the as- 
printed and heat-treated AMS-OR alloy, highlighting the significant 
enhancement in oxidation resistance achieved through heat treatment. 
However, the root causes underlying the different oxidation behaviors 
between the two conditions necessitate further investigation. Specif -
ically, the mechanisms driving differences in oxidation product forma -
tion and oxidation kinetics disparities require systematic elucidation to 
establish a comprehensive understanding of microstructure- 
performance relationships in additively manufactured superalloys.
4.1. The element configuration in as-printed and heat-treated samples
The additive manufacturing process, characterized by non- 
equilibrium solidification, inherently induces elemental segregation 
during solidification. Subsequent heat treatment aims to homogenize 
the microstructure, achieving a more uniform elemental distribution.

A fundamental question arises as to why as-printed samples exhibit 
greater aluminum consumption — evidenced by deeper γ ′ depletion 
zones — yet demonstrate inferior oxidation resistance. This paradox can 
be explained by two interrelated mechanisms. First, the lack of a 
continuous alumina layer under the as-printed condition prevents the 
establishment of a low oxygen partial pressure environment required to 
stabilize Al ₂ O ₃ , resulting in continuous non-protective aluminum con -
sumption. Second, as confirmed by Fig. S4 in Supplementary Materials , 
the as-printed microstructure contains notably finer spinel grains 
compared to the heat-treated material. This refined granularity facili -
tates enhanced oxygen ingress, which accelerates spinel 
growth — consuming aluminum from γ ′ dissolution without forming a 
protective alumina barrier. Together, these mechanisms act 
synergistically to deplete aluminum reserves via non-beneficial oxida -
tion, further promoting γ ′ dissolution while failing to develop a contin -
uous protective alumina layer.
