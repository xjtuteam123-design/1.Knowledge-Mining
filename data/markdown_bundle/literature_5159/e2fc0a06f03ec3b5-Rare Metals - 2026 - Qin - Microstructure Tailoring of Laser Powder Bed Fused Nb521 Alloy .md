ABSTRACT
Niobium‐based refractory alloys are promising candidates for high‐temperature aerospace structures, yet their limited ambient‐ 
temperature ductility and poor processability remain major barriers to broader implementation. Here, Nb521 alloy was fabri -
cated by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), and the effects of processing conditions and subsequent hot isostatic pressing (HIP) on 
microstructural development and mechanical response were systematically examined. The as‐printed alloy exhibited a het -
erogeneous grain structure composed of columnar and equiaxed grains, together with pronounced solute segregation and dense 
dislocation substructures generated by rapid solidification. HIP promoted chemical homogenization, reduced the fraction of 
low‐angle grain boundaries, weakened the crystallographic texture, and induced a partial tetragonal‐to‐monoclinic trans -
formation of dispersed ZrO
2 
particles. These microstructural modifications led to a marked improvement in the strength‐ 
ductility balance. In particular, after HIP treatment, the specimen fabricated with a laser power of 240 W and a scanning 
speed of 500 mm s
− 1 
(LED = 480 J m
− 1
) exhibited an ultimate tensile strength of 581.9 ± 2.98 MPa, a yield strength of 
442.2 ± 8.66 MPa, and a tensile elongation of 22.3% ± 0.62%. Quantitative strengthening analysis indicates that the yield 
strength arises from the combined contributions of lattice friction, grain‐boundary strengthening, dislocation strengthening, and 
precipitation strengthening. The precipitation contribution is governed predominantly by Orowan bypassing, with an additional 
contribution from particle shearing, enabling simultaneous strengthening and ductility retention. These findings clarify the 
microstructural origins of the enhanced mechanical performance of LPBF‐processed Nb521 alloy and provide guidance for 
designing additively manufactured niobium‐based alloys with improved strength‐ductility synergy.

Introduction
Refractory metals, such as niobium, tungsten, and molybdenum, 
have attracted considerable attention due to their excellent high‐ 
temperature properties [ 1–7 ]. Among them, niobium‐based alloys 
have long played a critical role in aerospace applications, 
including rocket engine nozzles, satellite propulsion systems, and 
hot‐section components of hypersonic vehicles, owing to their 
low density, high melting point, and excellent high‐temperature 
mechanical properties [ 8–10 ]. These characteristics make 
niobium‐based alloys ideal candidates for a new generation of 
high‐temperature structural materials, with a maximum service 
temperature reaching up to 1800°C, far exceeding that of con -
ventional nickel‐based superalloys [ 11, 12 ]. Within the niobium 
alloy family, Nb521 alloy (Nb‐5W‐2Mo‐1Zr, wt%) is an important 
dispersion‐strengthened alloy that offers an attractive combina -
tion of high‐temperature strength, creep resistance, and oxidation

resistance. These properties arise from solid‐solution strength -

associated with the reaction between Zr and interstitial oxygen. 
However, the high strength and hardness of niobium alloys make 
the conventional fabrication of complex components particularly 
challenging [ 13, 14 ]. Aerospace components often contain intri -
cate geometries and internal cooling channels, for which tradi -
tional manufacturing routes typically involve long production 
cycles, low material utilization, and limited design flexibility. 
These limitations highlight the need for advanced manufacturing 
strategies capable of producing complex niobium‐alloy compo -
nents with improved structural integrity and performance.
With continued advances in materials processing, metal additive 
manufacturing (AM) has emerged as a powerful route for pro -
ducing high‐performance components with complex geometries 
[ 15–18 ]. Based on a layer‐wise deposition strategy, AM employs 
high‐energy sources, such as lasers, electron beams, or electric 
arcs, to selectively melt and consolidate feedstock materials. 
This mold‐free process offers substantial design flexibility, 
improved manufacturing efficiency, and enhanced material 
utilization. In addition, the localized molten pool and rapid 
solidification inherent to AM can refine microstructures and 
provide opportunities for property optimization. Among various 
AM techniques, laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) is particularly 
attractive for fabricating precision niobium‐alloy components 
because of its high dimensional accuracy, excellent geometric 
freedom, and material utilization exceeding 95% [ 19–24 ].
In recent years, considerable efforts have been devoted to the 
LPBF processing of niobium alloys. Liu et al. [ 19 ] showed that 
the ductility of LPBF‐fabricated pure Nb is highly sensitive to 
oxygen content: increasing oxygen from 0.05 wt% to 0.23 wt% 
reduced the elongation from 26% to below 2%. Atom probe to -
mography further revealed oxygen segregation at grain bound -
aries, identifying oxygen‐induced grain‐boundary embrittlement 
as a critical factor limiting ductility. Similarly, Chen et al. [ 20 ] 
optimized the LPBF parameters for Nb521 alloy and achieved a 
relative density of 99.2% at a volumetric energy density of 
146 J mm
− 3
, while also demonstrating that excessive oxygen 
content, up to 0.25 wt%, severely deteriorates ductility. These 
studies established the processing basis for LPBF of niobium 
alloys and emphasized the necessity of precise oxygen control.
Microstructural regulation has also been widely investigated. 
Awasthi et al. [ 21 ] reported that LPBF‐fabricated C103 alloy 
(Nb‐10Hf‐1Ti) contains dense dislocation networks and cellular 
substructures, together with a pronounced < 001 > texture, 
which enhances yield strength but restricts ductility. Chen et al. 
[ 22 ] introduced semi‐coherent nanoscale ZrO
2 
particles into 
Nb521 alloy through LPBF followed by heat treatment, 
achieving a tensile strength of 628.7 MPa and an elongation of 
17.8%, thereby demonstrating the effectiveness of oxide precip -
itation in improving the strength‐ductility balance. Qin et al. 
[ 23 ] further showed that heat‐treatment‐induced ZrO
2 
precipi -
tation and subgrain formation increased the elongation of 
Nb521 alloy to 23.6%, suggesting that subgrain boundaries can 
alleviate local stress concentration. In addition, Chesetti et al. 
[ 24 ] quantitatively evaluated the strengthening mechanisms in 
LPBF‐processed pure Nb and attributed a yield‐strength 
contribution of 490 MPa to the combined effects of dislocation 
strengthening, oxygen and nitrogen solid‐solution strength -
ening, and grain‐boundary strengthening.
Although substantial progress has been made in defining LPBF 
process windows and characterizing as‐printed microstructures 
of niobium alloys, several critical issues remain unresolved. 
First, the effect of volumetric energy density is often treated as a 
lumped parameter, whereas the individual and interactive roles 
of laser power and scanning speed in controlling solidification 
behavior, grain development, and defect formation have not 
been sufficiently decoupled. Second, most existing studies have 
focused on as‐printed microstructures obtained under a single 
processing condition, leaving the subsequent heat‐treatment 
response of different initial microstructures poorly understood. 
In this work, four representative LPBF parameter sets were 
selected to systematically clarify the independent and coupled 
effects of laser power and scanning speed on the microstructure 
and mechanical properties of Nb521 alloy. Furthermore, hot 
isostatic pressing was employed to reveal the microstructural 
evolution pathways associated with different as‐printed states 
and to elucidate the strengthening and toughening mechanisms 
governed by nanoscale precipitates.

Materials and Processing
In this study, commercially available Nb521 powder prepared by 
the plasma spheroidization method was used as the raw mate -
rial, with a nominal composition of Nb‐5W‐2Mo‐1Zr (wt%). The 
powder exhibits a near‐spherical morphology with a Gaussian 
particle size distribution (Supporting Information S1 : Figure 
S1A), ranging from 15 to 53 μm, an average particle size of 
33.7 μm, and a purity of approximately 99.9%. The morphology 
and elemental distribution of the powder were analyzed using 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) combined with energy‐ 
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), revealing that all alloying ele -
ments are uniformly distributed within the powder particles 
without significant segregation (Supporting Information S1 : 
Figure S1B). Prior to use, the powder was vacuum‐dried at 
120°C for 4 h.
The processing of metal parts by LPBF is a complex process 
governed by multiple parameters, and the underlying principle 
is illustrated in Supporting Information S1 : Figure S1C. The 
Nb521 sample blocks were fabricated using a Beijing Eplus 3D 
EP‐M150 (Yijia 3D Company) laser melting system. To ensure 
process stability and suppress high‐temperature oxidation of Nb, 
the entire process was conducted under a continuously flowing 
high‐purity argon protective atmosphere, with the oxygen con -
tent in the processing chamber monitored in real time and 
strictly maintained below 40 ppm. Prior to printing, the polished 
TC4 substrate was preheated to 150°C in an argon environment 
to reduce the cooling rate and mitigate thermal stress.
To systematically investigate the influence of process parame -
ters on the forming quality and establish the optimal process 
window, the linear energy density (LED) and volumetric energy 
density (VED) were adopted to describe the combined effects of

samples printed within the aforementioned LED range. The 
results indicate that the forming quality is primarily determined 
by four types of defects: cracks, layered voids, spherical pores,

Microstructural Characterization
Figure 2 shows the microstructures of the LPBF Nb521 alloy 
fabricated with different process parameters before and after 
HIP. Prior to HIP, the microstructure is primarily composed of 
columnar and equiaxed grains. At a low LED, lack‐of‐fusion 
defects are present (Figure 2A ), which is attributed to the 
rapid cooling rate during the LPBF process. Meanwhile, typical 
melt pool boundaries can be clearly identified, indicating a 
pronounced layered stacking structure as shown in Figure 2A1 . 
This phenomenon suggests that the solidification characteristics 
of the melt pool under this parameter are well preserved, and 
the heat input is insufficient to trigger significant subsequent 
thermal cycle modification. However, as the laser power in -
creases, this feature disappears under the subsequent higher 
LED. This is attributed to the enhanced thermal cycling effect 
brought about by the higher energy input, that is, the subse -
quent deposited layers exert an in situ annealing effect on the 
already solidified layers. Notably, all as‐printed specimens 
exhibit significant contrast differences in brightness and dark -
ness between grains, which may result from the combined ef -
fects of grain‐scale elemental micro‐segregation induced by 
nonequilibrium solidification and the differential etching rates 
of grains with different crystallographic orientations. Further -
more, except for the as‐printed‐1 sample, fine intragranular 
stripe structures can be observed in the magnified regions of 
samples processed with other parameters, as shown in 
Figure 2A1–D1 . The formation mechanism of these stripes can 
be attributed to periodic micro‐segregation under the extreme 
nonequilibrium solidification conditions of LPBF. Under 
extremely high cooling rates, solute trapping and constitutional 
undercooling occur at the solid‐liquid interface front, causing 
heavy elements, such as W and Mo, to periodically enrich at the 
solidification front [ 28 ]. The stripes are most pronounced in as‐ 
printed‐4, indicating that although reducing the scanning speed 
prolongs the melt pool lifetime, it does not eliminate this peri -
odic segregation; instead, it may enhance solute redistribution 
due to the heat accumulation effect.
Compared with the as‐printed samples, HIP induced significant 
microstructural evolution as shown in Figure 2E–H . The 
contrast differences in brightness and darkness between grains 
were substantially diminished as the alloying elements under -
went volume diffusion and grain boundary diffusion at elevated 
temperatures, leading to full homogenization of the micro‐ 
segregation retained from nonequilibrium solidification. In

The grain boundaries are primarily composed of high‐angle

Furthermore, kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps reveal 
significant local plastic deformation in all as‐printed samples 
(Figure 3A1–D1 ), indicative of high‐density geometrically

may facilitate some stress relief, the dislocation configuration

the extremely high thermal cycling rates and temperature gra -

generated during processing; however, the subsequent thermal 
cycles are insufficient to annihilate dislocations or transform 
dislocation cells into high‐angle grain boundaries, thus leaving a 
substantial number of LAGBs in the final microstructure.
After HIP, the grain morphology remains similar to that of the 
as‐printed samples, with columnar grains still exhibiting 
preferred growth along the heat flow direction, as shown in 
Figure 3E–H . The high‐density dislocations introduced in the 
as‐printed state provide a sufficient driving force for recrystal -
lization, promoting the nucleation and growth of new grains, 
thereby refining the original coarse columnar grain structure. 
Meanwhile, some fine equiaxed grains undergo preferential 
nucleation and growth during recrystallization. Compared with 
as‐printed samples, the microstructure after HIP exhibits lower 
KAM values (Figure 3E1–H1 ), indicating that the residual stress 
has been effectively relieved. After HIP, the low‐angle grain 
boundaries within the grains are significantly reduced and tend 
to coincide with high‐angle grain boundaries (Figure 3E2–H2 ). 
Moreover, the proportion of low‐angle grain boundaries shows a 
decreasing trend, as shown in Supporting Information S1 : 
Figure S3. This phenomenon indicates that recrystallization 
during HIP optimizes the grain boundary distribution by pro -
moting the gradual transformation of residual subgrain 
boundaries into high‐angle grain boundaries or their migration 
toward existing grain boundaries. As a result, the intragranular 
orientation becomes more uniform, which facilitates a homo -
geneous stress distribution during plastic deformation [ 29 ].

Tensile Property
After tensile testing, as‐printed‐3 exhibited an ultimate tensile 
strength of 708.5 MPa and an elongation of only 3.36%, dis -
playing a combination of high strength and extremely low 
ductility. This is primarily attributed to the high‐density dislo -
cations, dislocation cell structures, and abundant low‐angle 
grain boundaries introduced during the rapid solidification of 
LPBF, along with significant stress concentration [ 23 ].
The engineering tensile stress‐strain curves of the Nb521 samples 
after HIP are shown in Figure 5A . The curves of all samples in the 
elastic deformation stage are essentially consistent, indicating 
that HIP effectively eliminated the possible residual stress dif -
ferences present in the as‐printed samples. The corresponding 
statistical data are listed in Figure 5B . The HIPed‐3 sample ex -
hibits the optimal comprehensive mechanical properties, with a 
UTS of 581.9 ± 2.98 MPa and an EL of 22.3% ± 0.62%, and is thus 
determined to be the optimal LED parameter. In contrast, HIPed‐ 
1 shows the lowest UTS and EL, which are 543.8 ± 3.37 MPa and 
16.0% ± 0.82%, respectively. Compared with HIPed‐1, HIPed‐3 
exhibits a 7.0% increase in UTS and a substantial 39.4% increase 
in EL. HIPed‐2 exhibits an EL of 16.3% ± 1.00%, which is similar to 
that of HIPed‐1, but its UTS reaches 573.6 ± 8.62 MPa, repre -
senting an 18.2% increase compared to HIPed‐1. HIPed‐4 exhibits 
a UTS of 577.8 ± 8.02 MPa, which is comparable to that of HIPed‐ 
3, but its EL is 19.7% ± 1.53%, which is 11.7% lower than that of 
HIPed‐3. The decrease in EL for HIPed‐4 may be related to the 
presence of hot cracks in this sample (Figure 2H ). Although the 
yield strength (YS) values of HIPed‐2 and HIPed‐4 are slightly 
higher than that of HIPed‐3, considering the error range, this 
difference can be reasonably neglected. A comparison of the 
properties of HIPed‐3 with those of additively manufactured and 
wrought Nb521 alloy and C103 alloy reported in the literature is 
shown in Figure 5C .

Fracture Surface Analysis
The tensile fracture morphologies were observed and analyzed 
using SEM, as shown in Figure 5D–G . Overall, the fracture 
surfaces of all samples exhibit a mixed morphology character -
ized by the coexistence of dimples and cleavage facets 
(Figure 5D1–G1 ), indicating a composite fracture mechanism 
involving both ductile and brittle failure. Notably, as shown in 
Figure 5D1–E1 , unmelted powder particles were found in 
HIPed‐1 and HIPed‐2, which is an important factor contributing 
to their relatively low elongation (Figure 5B ). Meanwhile, a 
large number of dimples are distributed on the fracture surfaces, 
suggesting that the fracture mode is predominantly ductile, 
providing the main contribution to the limited elongation.

− 1

disappear in HIPed‐3, and large areas of dimples along with 
small areas of cleavage facets can be observed (Figure 5F1 ),

fracture being the dominant mode. With a further increase in 
LED, the fracture morphology of HIPed‐4 consists of extensive 
cleavage platforms with a few dimples distributed between them 
(Figure 5G1 ), indicating that the fracture mode has transitioned 
to being predominantly brittle [ 36 ]. Consequently, compared 
with HIPed‐3, the elongation of HIPed‐4 shows a marked 
decrease. In addition, some large dimples are detected in the 
fractures of all samples; these voids result from microstructural 
defects in the LPBF Nb521 samples during tensile testing, as 
shown in Figure 2 , and they are detrimental to the mechanical 
properties.

3 sample. The fracture surface is flat and exhibits typical river‐

observed, indicating that no significant microvoid coalescence

Precipitation Behavior
TEM was employed to study the as‐printed‐3 and HIPed‐3 
samples as shown in Figure 6 . In Figure 6A , pronounced 
chemical inhomogeneity and associated substructural features 
are observed in as‐printed‐3. Due to the differences in diffusion 
rates of the constituent elements during the rapid solidification 
process of LPBF, refractory heavy elements, such as W and Mo, 
become enriched in certain subgrain regions, resulting in the 
observed compositional fluctuations as shown in Figure 6B . 
These micro‐segregation bands correspond to the intragranular 
bright and dark stripes observed in the earlier SEM images 
(Figure 2C1 ). Concurrently, a high‐density dislocation network 
formed by the extreme nonequilibrium solidification process of 
LPBF is present in the sample. Under rapid solidification con -
ditions (cooling rate > 10
6 
K s
− 1
), dislocations lack sufficient 
kinetic conditions for rearrangement and annihilation, 
becoming “frozen” in the lattice and forming entangled dislo -
cation cellular structures [ 37 ]. These cellular structures consist 
of dense dislocation walls, with relatively low dislocation den -
sities in the cell interiors, whereas the cell walls are populated 
by numerous entangled dislocations as shown in Figure 6C . 
This dislocation configuration is a common characteristic of 
LPBF‐fabricated alloys, and its formation mechanism can be 
attributed to the accumulation of thermal stress during solidi -
fication and subsequent thermal cycling. On the one hand, this 
high‐density dislocation structure acts as an effective strength -
ening phase, enhancing the tensile strength; on the other hand, 
when the applied stress exceeds a critical threshold, the stress 
concentration at dislocation pile‐ups can easily induce prema -
ture fracture, thereby limiting the ductility of the material 
[ 38, 39 ].
After high‐temperature treatment, the chemical homogeneity is 
significantly improved as shown in Figure 6D , and the pro -
nounced elemental segregation observed in the as‐printed 
sample is diminished, which is consistent with the homogeni -
zation of intergranular contrast observed in the SEM images 
(Figure 2G ) and the rightward shift of the XRD peaks (Sup -
porting Information S1 : Figure S2B). Although heat treatment 
promotes sufficient elemental diffusion, the complete chemical 
homogenization of refractory elements, such as W and Mo, is 
difficult to achieve within the limited holding time due to their 
high melting point characteristics. Meanwhile, the characteris -
tics of sub‐grain boundaries are weakened, indicating that the 
high‐temperature creep and diffusion mechanisms during HIP 
effectively promote dislocation climb, glide, and annihilation. 
Notably, a large number of nanoscale precipitate particles are 
dispersed within the grains and at the grain boundaries, and 
dislocation loops can be observed around them (Figure 6E ), 
which are more common than dislocation tangles.

solid solutions with the Nb matrix [ 40 ]. In contrast, the active 
element Zr tends to react with interstitial elements (such as O 
and C) to form nonmetallic compounds [ 41 ]. Supporting

Conclusions
In summary, this study systematically elucidates the intrinsic 
relationships among process parameters, microstructure, and 
mechanical properties in the LPBF of Nb521 alloy. It clarifies 
the independent and coupled effects of laser power and scan -
ning speed on solidification behavior, defect formation, and 
grain evolution, thereby establishing the critical process window 
for achieving high densification. By comparing the microstruc -
tural evolution of as‐printed and HIPed samples, the differential 
response of distinct initial microstructures to HIP is elucidated. 
Regarding mechanical properties, an excellent synergy of 
strength and ductility is achieved through HIP. Quantitative 
strengthening model analysis further reveals the primary con -
tributions of lattice friction stress, precipitation strengthening, 
and dislocation strengthening to the YS.
The densification behavior of LPBF‐fabricated Nb521 alloy is 
highly sensitive to energy input. Insufficient linear energy 
density leads to lack‐of‐fusion defects, whereas excessive input 
induces gas porosity and hot cracking, resulting in a narrow 
process window. High‐density samples are obtained within the 
optimized linear energy density range of 400–500 J m
− 1
.
The as‐printed microstructure exhibits a mixed characteristic of 
pronounced columnar and equiaxed grains, featuring periodic 
micro‐segregation bands induced by solute trapping and 
constitutional undercooling, along with a high‐density disloca -
tion network. HIP promotes sufficient elemental diffusion and 
dislocation annihilation, leading to mitigated micro‐segregation 
and a reduced proportion of low‐angle grain boundaries, 
accompanied by the partial transformation of dispersed t‐ZrO
2 
particles to m‐ZrO
2 
within the grains and at grain boundaries.
The mechanical properties of HIPed samples are closely related 
to the initial processing parameters. The HIPed‐3 sample, 
manufactured using a laser power of 240 W and a scanning 
speed of 500 mm s
− 1
, exhibits optimal comprehensive me -
chanical performance, with a UTS of 581.9 ± 2.98 MPa, a YS of 
442.2 ± 8.66 MPa, and an EL of 22.3% ± 0.62%. The fracture 
surface displays a mixed‐mode morphology comprising dimples 
and cleavage facets, in which nanosized ZrO
2 
particles inhibit 
microvoid coalescence and crack propagation during plastic 
deformation.
Quantitative analysis of the strengthening mechanisms in -
dicates that the high strength of the HIPed‐3 sample primarily 
originates from lattice friction stress, grain boundary strength -
ening, dislocation strengthening, and precipitation strength -
ening. Among these, the Orowan mechanism dominates the 
precipitation strengthening, acting synergistically with the 
shearing mechanism to achieve a desirable combination of high 
strength and good ductility. The theoretically calculated YS is in 
good agreement with the experimental values, validating the 
reliability of the strengthening model.
