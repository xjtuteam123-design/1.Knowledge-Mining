Twin formation through repeated dislocation 
impingement on phase boundary in Ni‑based 
superalloys using atomistic simulations
Sagar Chandra1,a), Mahendra K. Samal2, Vivek M. Chavan1
1 Reactor Technology Division, Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, Mumbai 400 085, India
2 Reactor Safety Division, Bhabha Atomic Research Centre, Mumbai 400 085, India
a) Address all correspondence to this author. e-mail: sagarc@barc.gov.in
Received: 1 June 2025; accepted: 21 August 2025; published online: 9 September 2025
Formation of stacking faults and deformation twins in the matrix as well as precipitate phase of Ni‑based 
superalloys governs their proficiency in high‑temperature service. In view of this phenomena, the 
present work reports a qualitatively different pathway for the formation of twins in γ ′ phase during 
plastic deformation. The involved mechanism relies on the prevalence of multiple matrix dislocations on 
adjacent slip planes in the vicinity of γ − γ ′ interface along with sufficient magnitude of glide stress and 
temperature. An attempt is also made to compare the results with experiments available in the literature. 
It is envisioned that this work shall open up novel avenues for probing other deformation pathways also 
that may lead to formation of twins in γ ′ phase of Ni‑based superalloys. This may ultimately lead to an 
advanced understanding of deformation mechanisms in Ni‑based superalloys at high temperatures.
Introduction
Interfaces are an ubiquitous trademark of microstructures in 
crystalline metals and alloys. In pure polycrystalline metals, they 
prevail in the form of grain boundaries existing between mis-
oriented grains. In alloys like precipitation hardened Ni-based 
superalloys, they may also exist in the form of phase boundaries 
between the γ matrix (Ni) and γ ′ ( Ni3Al) precipitates. Indeed, 
interfaces are an inevitable part of a materials microstructure 
and affect the deformation behavior by acting as sources, sinks, 
and barriers to dislocations [1, 2]. The interaction of dislocations 
with these interfaces is one of the primary mechanisms of plastic 
deformation in crystalline metals and alloys [ 3, 4].
However, dislocation glide and subsequent interactions with 
interfaces, although fundamental, are not the only mode through 
which metals deform plastically. In addition to dislocation slip, 
twinning is another conspicuous feature of plastic deformation. 
Investigations on relative contributions from slip and twinning 
to metal plasticity offer key insights into the dominant mode of 
deformation under a given condition of loading. Recognition 
of this aspect has led to the emergence of a number of seminal 
works focusing on the interplay of dislocation slip and twinning 
and their inherent mechanistic competition in various metals 
and alloys [5–9]. Owing to their excellent mechanical properties, 
particularly at high temperatures, various studies have investi -
gated the deformation behavior in precipitation-strengthened 
Ni-based superalloys also [10– 14]. These unique properties 
are attributed to the presence of γ ′ precipitates (L12 structure) 
embedded coherently in the γ (FCC structure) Ni matrix. The 
presence of these precipitates in the γ matrix induces several 
strengthening mechanisms inhibiting dislocation glide like pre-
cipitation strengthening, solute strengthening, and stacking fault 
strengthening [15]. Interactions of matrix dislocations with the 
γ − γ ′ interface under different loading conditions result in a 
variety of deformation mechanisms involving various planar 
defects like superlattice intrinsic/extrinsic stacking faults (SISF/
SESF), anti-phase boundaries (APB), and twins in the γ ′ phase 
[15, 16]. These faults may either appear isolated in the γ ′ phase 
or may appear as extended bands traversing through both the γ 
matrix and the precipitate [17, 18].
Within the γ matrix, perfect dislocations split into Shockley 
partials bounding the intrinsic stacking fault [ 19]. These stack-
ing faults may either lie completely within the matrix travers-
ing few atomic distances (depending upon the stacking fault 
energy of the matrix) or may extend to large atomic distances 
if the trailing partial is pinned by an interface [ 4, 20]. In the γ ′

phase, glide of a perfect matrix dislocation leads to the forma -

by another perfect trailing dislocation. Given the high cost of 
energetics involved with this process, deformation in γ ′ may 
also occur via formation of complex stacking faults (CSF) and 
superlattice stacking faults [15]. Another competing deforma-
tion mechanism in the γ ′ phase is the formation of twins [21, 
22] at intermediate and high temperatures which are formed 
by the concomitant glide of Shockley partials on adjacent glide 
planes. This is somewhat interesting since twinning in FCC type 
packed crystals is considered to be a thermally activated phe-
nomena observed generally at low temperatures and/or high 
strain rates [23].
The prevalence of aforementioned deformation mechanisms 
in Ni-based superalloys depends on applied stress and tempera-
ture. Extended APBs due to glide of 1/2⟨110 ⟩ dislocations are 
observed at temperatures ≈ 900◦ C [24, 25]. At intermediate 
temperatures of 600◦ C - 800◦ C, APBs coupled with superlat-
tice stacking faults are also observed due to shearing through 
1/6⟨112 ⟩ and 1/3⟨112 ⟩ partial dislocations [26, 27]. At tem-
peratures greater than 900◦ C, precipitate shearing via passage 
of ⟨110 ⟩ and ⟨100 ⟩ dislocations has also been reported in single 
crystal Ni-based superalloys during tensile creep [28, 29]. Shear-
ing of γ ′ precipitates by twins and isolated superlattice stacking 
faults [30–33] has also been reported during tensile and creep 
tests at intermediate temperatures of ≈ 600◦ C. The present work 
is focused on this intermediate temperature range wherein twin-
ning is a competing deformation mechanism to dislocation slip 
in γ ′-strengthened Ni-based superalloys.
While the theory for deformation twinning in regular FCC 
lattices is well developed [23, 34], the mechanisms involved 
in twin formation in Ni-based superalloys are still debatable. 
Unocic et al. [18] and Kovarik et al. [35] provide an extensive 
review of the existing theories postulating twin formation in 
Ni-based superalloys. These theories include the mechanisms 
proposed by Kear et al. [36], Knowles and Chen [37], Kolbe 
et al. [21, 38], and Decamps et al. [39, 40]. According to the 
mechanism proposed by Kear et al. [ 36], the interactions of 
⟨110 ⟩ superdislocations on two different slip systems lead to 
the formation of ⟨112 ⟩ dislocations which shear the γ ′ precipi-
tate via formation of SISFs and SESFs [18, 35]. However, this 
mechanism requires a high symmetry orientation of the loading 
axis so that two slip systems become operative and experience 
high shear stresses [18]. The mechanism proposed by Knowles 
and Chen [37] relies on the formation of SESFs in the γ ′ phase 
by glide of two different 1/3⟨112 ⟩ partials on adjacent {1 1 1} 
planes. While the first partial will form SESF , the second par -
tial will form a twin [35]. These partials are supposed to form 
through the classical pole mechanism [ 34] via interaction of 
two identical 1/2⟨110 ⟩ dislocations in the matrix. This mecha -
nism requires shearing of {1 1 1} glide planes in directions which 
possess small value of Schmid factor and is, therefore, conserva-
tive in nature [18, 35]. The mechanisms proposed by Decamps 
et al. [39, 40] rely on the formation of APB in the precipitate by 
the passage of 1/2⟨110 ⟩ matrix dislocations. Due to high energy 
of this planar fault, the subsequent Shockley partial glides on 
adjacent {1 1 1} plane that transforms this high-energy APB 
to a low-energy SESF via the formation of composite 1/3⟨112 ⟩ 
partial. The mechanism by Kolbe et al. [21, 38] relies on vacancy 
mediated atomic reordering in the wake of 1/6⟨112 ⟩ partials in 
the precipitate phase. Specifically, atomic diffusion is required to 
transform the CSF to SESF through local atomic rearrangements 
in the L 12 lattice. Further diffusion at high temperatures then 
converts the planar fault to a twin. The temperature depend-
ence of this thermally activated phenomena arises from the 
temperature dependence of atomic reordering process [18, 35]. 
Formation of SESFs and eventual twinning via glide of 1/6⟨112 ⟩ 
partials on {1 1 1} planes can also be assisted by elemental seg -
regation ahead of these faults, which minimizes the CSF energy 
and facilitates twin formation [41, 42].
Given such diverse nature of twin formation mechanisms 
in precipitation-strengthened Ni-based superalloys, the afore -
mentioned observations suggest that some other mechanisms of 
twin formation are also possible. It can be seen that all the above 
mechanisms require either the prevalence of multiple active slip 
systems [18, 36], activation of slip systems with lower Schmid 
factor [18, 37], special atomic reordering or rearrangements to 
convert the CSF to SESF [38, 40] or presence of solute segrega-
tion elements [32, 41, 42] that assist twin formation. However, 
experimental evidences do exist that suggest the existence of 
other possible alternative twin formation mechanisms in the γ ′ 
phase of Ni-based superalloys. For instance, transmission elec -
tron microscopy (TEM) investigation into plastic deformation 
behavior of γ ′-strengthened Ni-based superalloy Rene-104 crept 
at 724 MPa and 677◦ C by Unocic et al. [43] revealed the exist -
ence of extended faults in γ matrix preceding the onset of twin-
ning in γ ′ phase. Specifically, Unocic et al. [43] hypothesized that 
under appropriate shear loading, the leading partial of 1/2⟨110 ⟩ 
dissociated matrix dislocation percolates the γ − γ ′ interface 
and lays out large areas of ISFs. With the arrival of second matrix 
dislocation, the pair of identical partials traverse the γ ′ phase 
resulting in the removal of Shockley partial loops around the 
precipitate and formation of SESF in the sheared γ ′ particles, 
a precursor to twin formation. V ery recently, Zhang et al. [44] 
revealed underlying deformation mechanisms in turbine disk 
superalloy GH4720Li deformed under hot compression at 900◦ 
C and 0.01 s−1 strain rate. The authors revealed the aggregation 
of 1/2⟨110 ⟩ matrix dislocations on the γ side of the γ − γ ′ inter-
face preceding the onset of twinning in γ ′ phase.
The existence of stacking faults in the γ phase [43] and the 
aggregation of matrix dislocations on the γ side of the γ − γ ′ 
interface [44] preceding twinning in the precipitate suggests that

captured through a numerical modeling setup employing mul-

[22] also reported a pileup of matrix dislocations at the γ − γ ′ 
interface prior to onset of deformation twinning in the γ ′ phase. 
Moreover, repetitive dislocation-phase boundary interactions 
are inevitable during plastic deformation under external loads 
and are, therefore, expected to play a key role in dictating the 
strain hardening characteristics of the material [45, 46]. Multiple 
dislocations can exert additional significant stresses at the phase 
boundary that can influence the involved plastic deformation 
mechanisms and lead to twinning. In view of this behavior, the 
numerical simulation setup aimed to capture twinning in Ni-
based superalloys should employ multiple lattice dislocations in 
the matrix which impinge continuously on the interface. Given 
the inherent nanoscale nature of this problem, atomistic simu -
lations are best suited to tackle this and provide fundamental 
insights into the involved mechanisms.
Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations offer a distinctive 
opportunity to gage the underlying deformation mechanisms 
under different loading conditions right at the atomic scales of 
inception. This fact has led to a resurgence of interest in simulat-
ing twin formation in Ni-based superalloys using MD [47–49]. 
The review by Kedarnath et al. [50] summarizes various atomis-
tic investigations aimed at understanding the mechanical behav-
ior of crystalline materials. With the development of new suite 
of interatomic potentials that serve as input to MD, atomistic 
simulations have also proven their utility in tackling various 
problems related to plastic deformation in various alloy systems. 
Concerning Ni-based superalloys, a lot of atomistic investiga-
tions have been performed aiming to grasp the micromechanics 
of deformation behavior under fatigue loading [51, 52], uniaxial 
loading [5 , 53–58], fracture [59– 61], creep [62, 63], and spall 
damage [64, 65], to name a few. Building upon these applica -
tions of MD simulation approaches, the present work aims to 
provide a mechanism of formation of twins in γ ′-strengthened 
Ni-based superalloy.
Therefore, this paper reports a mechanism of twin forma-
tion assisted by continuous impingement of 1/2⟨110 ⟩ matrix 
dislocations on the γ − γ ′ interface using MD simulations. 
The matrix dislocations are made to glide on adjacent {1 1 1} 
planes to favor the explicit formation of CSF/SESF and conse-
quent pseudo-twins. As already stated, this simulation setup is 
directly motivated by recent experimental investigations which 
have reported a pileup of matrix dislocations at the γ − γ ′ inter-
face prior to onset of deformation twinning in the precipitate 
[22, 43, 44]. This is necessary for an advanced understanding 
of deformation twinning behavior in precipitate-strengthened 
Ni-based superalloys during high-temperature deformation. The 
present work shall directly contribute to the materials commu-
nity by revealing the formation of deformation twins in γ ′ phase 
via another mechanism which does not involve the requirement 
for additional slip systems (as in Kear mechanism [18, 36]), spe-
cial atomic rearrangements for conversion of CSF to SESF (as 
in Kolbe mechanism [21, 38]), or solute segregation [41, 42] 
during external loading.

 Atomistic simulation results
Formation of SESF
Under the application of externally applied load, a matrix dis-
location can penetrate the γ − γ ′ interface only under suffi-
ciently high stress. Figure 1 depicts the snapshots from atomis-
tic simulations of edge dislocation interactions with the γ − γ ′ 
interface. The applied shear stress in this case is 750 MPa. At 
around 8 ps (picoseconds) at 750 MPa, the leading partial of 
the first extended matrix dislocation impinges the interface 
[see Fig.  1(a)]. This dislocation cannot instantly enter the γ ′ 
precipitate. With continuous application of shear stress, other 
matrix dislocations gliding on adjacent [1 1 1] plane approach 
the interface. At around 34 ps at 750 MPa, the second matrix 
dislocation closes in on the γ − γ ′ interface [Fig.  1(b)]. Fol -
lowing this, nucleation of 1/6[¯12 ¯1] partial is observed from the 
interface into the γ ′ phase [Fig.  1(c) at 39 ps]. The embryonic 
partial dislocation is observed to nucleate on [1 1 1] plane and 
starts sweeping the γ ′ precipitate under the influence of exter -
nal shear loading. The partial sweeps in the precipitate leaving 
behind CSF attached to the interface [Fig.  1(d) at 44 ps at 750 
MPa]. The nucleation and glide of 1/6[¯12 ¯1] partial are observed 
only after the arrival of second matrix dislocation at the inter -
face. This implies that the nucleation of Shockley partial into 
the γ ′ phase is a high energy process which is facilitated by local 
stress concentration at the interface arising due to continuous 
impingement of matrix dislocations.
At around 56 ps at 750 MPa, the third extended matrix 
dislocation gliding on adjacent [1 1 1] plane approaches the 
γ − γ ′ interface and pushes the second matrix dislocation into 
the precipitate [Fig.  1(e)]. Meanwhile, the leading partial of 
the nucleated dislocation in the γ ′ phase sweeps the precipitate 
leaving behind CSF and fourth matrix dislocation on adja-
cent glide plane also starts to close in at the interface. At this 
instant, at around 65 ps at 750 MPa, another 1/6[¯12 ¯1] partial 
dislocation with screw character is nucleated just adjacent to

A comparison can also be drawn between the Kolbe [21, 
38] mechanism of twinning and the observations reported 
here based on MD simulations. The Kolbe mechanism relies 
on thermally activated atomic reshuffling/rearrangements in the 
wake of the CSF to form SESF , a precursor for twin formation 
[18]. This atomic reshuffling is dependent on diffusion, which 
is mediated by movement of vacancies at high temperatures. 
Conversely, the atomistic process of SESF formation reported 
here relies on stress-assisted thermally activated nucleation of 
1/6[¯12 ¯1] screw dislocation on adjacent [1 1 1] plane so that the 
previously nucleated 1/6[¯12 ¯1] partial and the current partial 
travel concomitantly in pair as a 1/3[¯12 ¯1] partial. Therefore, the 
rate-limiting phenomena in this case is the nucleation of second 
1/6[¯12 ¯1] partial from the interface on adjacent [1 1 1] plane that 
removes the CSF in its wake and forms a SESF during its glide 
in the precipitate [Fig. 1(f,g)].

 Discussion
The simulations reveal that twinning is also possible when the 
dislocation configuration in the matrix and driving stress is suit-
ably oriented so as to enable dislocation nucleation from the 
γ − γ ′ interface on adjacent slip planes and subsequent pre-
cipitate shearing. This work yields an explanation for observa-
tions of extended stacking faults in the precipitate preceding 
twin formation in the γ ′ phase in Ni-based superalloys [22, 43]. 
A consistent picture emerges from MD simulations which dem-
onstrate the possibility of twin formation via precipitate shearing 
through apt combination of matrix dislocation impingement at 
the interface and glide shear stress on suitably oriented γ − γ ′ 
microstructure.

As also mentioned in Sect.  1, various experimental studies 
have reported the existence of extended faults in the precipi -
tate, aggregation of matrix dislocations at the γ − γ ′ interface, 
and shearing of precipitate via passage of 1/6⟨112 ⟩ partials on 
adjacent slip planes preceding the formation of twinning in γ ′ 
phase [22, 43, 44, 70]. This work provides a mechanistic ration-
ale for the same. In-fact, the presented atomistic observation 
in this work involving the passage of 1/6⟨112 ⟩ Shockley par -
tial propagating into the γ ′ phase has precedence even with the 
experimental observations of Freund et al. [71] in γ ′ strength-
ened Co-based superalloy deformed at intermediate temperature 
of 760◦ C. The authors reported that these faults extend during 
deformation and evidently develop into twins in the precipitate 
phase [71]. Y uan et al. [72] revealed the presence of CSFs pre-
ceding twin formation in the γ ′ phase through high-resolution 
TEM observations. Through in situ TEM and density functional 
theory calculations, Hong et al. [73] noted that Ni-based super-
alloy single crystals loaded along ⟨110 ⟩ crystallographic orienta-
tion tend to deform via twinning in γ ′ phase through successive 
passage of ⟨112 ⟩ partial dislocations. The effect of temperature 
on the competition between slip and twinning was investigated 
by Qi et al. [74]. The authors observed that at temperatures of 
around 400◦ C, dominant deformation mechanism is disloca-
tion slip and at higher temperature of 750◦ C, twinning becomes 
operative in the γ ′ phase [ 74] which is also in agreement with 
the present work. A pile-up of dislocations around the γ ′ pre-
cipitates was also observed by Hu et al. [75] before the onset of 
twinning in the precipitate at high temperatures.
W e would like to point out at this moment that the pre-
sent work offers an alternative mechanism in addition to those 
already presented by Kolbe et al. [21, 38] or Decamps et al. [40], 
for example, thereby offering insights into the corresponding 
experimental evidences (see above) reporting twin formation 
in the γ ′ phase. This may help to resolve the mechanistic debate 
on twin formation mechanisms especially in cases where exist -
ence of extended faults and dislocations at the interface are 
observed experimentally preceding the formation of twin in the 
precipitate. As already mentioned, in the mechanism proposed 
by Kolbe et al. [21, 38], 1/6⟨112 ⟩ partial dislocations shear the 
precipitate and create a CSF by successive shear on adjacent {1 
1 1} planes. Thermally activated atomic reordering then con-
verts this high-energy CSF to SESF . Therefore, the rate-limiting 
process in the mechanism of Kolbe et al. [21, 38] is atomic 
reshuffling mediated by thermal diffusion. The mechanism of 
Decamps et al. [39, 40] involves the formation of APB via glide 
of 1/2⟨110 ⟩ matrix dislocation [40] in the precipitate before 
special atomic rearrangements transform these planar faults to 
twins. In both the aforementioned mechanisms, the rate-limit-
ing process is atomic reordering. Conversely, the rate-limiting 
process envisaged in this work is the nucleation of 1/6⟨112 ⟩ 
partial on adjacent { 1 1 1} plane in the γ ′ phase that converts 
the CSF to SESF and this process is triggered by the prevalence 
of stress concentration ahead of multiple matrix dislocations 
impinging the boundary. This stress concentration conceivably 
overcomes the high-energy penalty required to convert the CSF 
into SESF via nucleation of Shockley partial in the γ ′ phase with-
out the need for thermally mediated atomic reordering.
It may be noted that the twin observed in Fig.  2 and Fig.  3 
is rather a pseudo-twin and thermally activated re-ordering is 
required to convert it into a true-twin [35, 48]. Therefore, for

Therefore, the atomistic mechanism presented in this work 
represents an alternative pathway for the formation of twins 
in γ ′ phase of γ ′-strengthened Ni-based superalloys. Thus, in 
order to fully comprehend the underlying plastic deformation 
behavior of Ni-based superalloys during loading, it is impera-
tive to consider this mechanism also in addition to other well-
known mechanisms. The modeling factors that have led to the 
recognition of this mechanism are sufficiently high externally 
applied stress, deformation temperature, and multiple matrix 
dislocations in the vicinity of interface. While the latter condi-
tion involving multiple matrix dislocations is expected in plastic 
deformation of Ni-based superalloys during high-temperature 
service, we shall now see and discuss the effect of other indi-
vidual modeling factors, viz. temperature and applied stress on 
the mechanism of twin formation.
A temperature of 900 K and applied shear stress of 750 
MPa/975 MPa is utilized in MD simulations reported above. 
With respect to applied stress, it may be noted that at 750 
MPa, the absorption of fourth and fifth matrix dislocations 
is not observed and the applied stress has to be increased to 
975 MPa to enable the absorption of fourth and fifth matrix 
dislocation into the boundary and consequent growth of twin 
in γ ′ phase (c.f. Figure  2, Fig.  3 and related discussion). With 
respect to deformation temperature, additional simulations 
at 300 K and 600 K are performed using the same simulation 
setup described in Sect.  5 (Fig.  7) to check the temperature 
dependence of the aforementioned deformation mechanism. 
Figure  6 presents the atomistic snapshots at different inter -
vals during the course of deformation in MD simulations at 
300 K temperature for representation, although it should be 
noted that similar observations are made for a temperature 
of 600 K also. The applied shear stress is increased in steps 
of 50 MPa to enable the matrix dislocations to impinge the 
interface. At around 13 ps at 1050 MPa, first matrix disloca-
tion enters the boundary [Fig.  6(a)]. At around 29 ps at 1050 
MPa, the second matrix dislocation closes in on the bound-
ary and a screw 1/6[¯12 ¯1] Shockley partial is nucleated in the 
γ ′ phase on [1 1 1] plane [Fig.  6(b)]. The partial sweeps the 
precipitate and third dislocation arrives at the boundary at 
around 45 ps at 1050 MPa [Fig. 6(c)]. Similar to Fig. 1, another 
Shockley partial of screw character is nucleated on adjacent 
[1 1 1] plane in the γ ′ phase and a SESF is formed [Fig.  6(d)]. 
At 300 K deformation temperature, the applied shear stress 
has to be increased to 1500 MPa to enable the absorption of 
fourth dislocation in the boundary. At this point, instead of 
the nucleation of another partial on adjacent glide plane to the 
previously nucleated partial in the γ ′ phase (for assisting twin 
formation), a dislocation loop is emitted from the interface 
into the matrix on [¯111] plane [Fig.  6(e)]. Subsequent stress 
application only leads to expansion of this partial dislocation 
loop into the matrix with its trailing end backed up at the 
interface. No nucleation of Shockley partial dislocation in the 
γ ′ phase is observed that can transform the SESF into a twin 
even after extended MD runs till 1 ns. This highlights the role 
played by deformation temperature in governing twin forma-
tion during loading.
Thus, it can be noted that the mechanism reported in this 
work requires sufficient stress and temperature. Ni-based super-
alloys are typically used in high-temperature applications (thus 
meeting the temperature requirement) and higher stresses can 
be readily generated by multiple matrix dislocations which can 
lead to a pile-up effect at the interface. In such cases, the effec-
tive stress at the leading dislocation of the pileup due to large 
number of trailing dislocations [76] can be so large that the 
externally applied stress required for this mechanism to occur 
can be much less (in comparison to stress levels applied in this 
work), not to mention the length/time scale effects inherent to 
MD simulations in this work on the magnitudes of stresses. If 
that happens, the twin formation mechanism reported in this 
paper can occur at much lower applied stresses and tempera-
tures because the mechanism relies on the prevalence of suf -
ficient stress concentration to trigger partial dislocation nuclea-
tion in the γ ′ phase. Pandey et al. [77] recently reported the 
occurrence of twins in the γ ′ phase of CoNi-based superalloy. 
The authors also reported the presence of multiple dislocation 
pileups at the γ − γ ′ interface preceding the onset of twinning 
in γ ′ phase. On the other hand, if the local γ − γ ′ microstruc-
ture is not conducive to the conditions where the nucleation of 
partial dislocations as reported here can occur, then other twin 
formation mechanisms as discussed in Sect.  1 or reviewed by 
Unocic et al. [18] and Kovarik et al. [35] may get transpired. 
For instance, frequent cross-slips of matrix dislocations on dif-
ferent {1 1 1} planes in the matrix [ 78] may result in a uniform 
slip distribution and may suppress the formation of pileups at 
the interface. Under such conditions, the mechanism reported 
in this work may get suppressed thereby opening the doors to 
other mechanisms. That being said, this work opens up pos-
sibilities of new investigations on other possible pathways also 
that may favor twin formation in the precipitate phase in Ni-
based superalloys.

 Conclusion
To summarize, this work reports a mechanism of twin forma-
tion in γ ′-strengthened Ni-based superalloys through atom-
istic modeling. The mechanism relies on the prevalence of 
multiple matrix dislocations in the vicinity of the γ − γ ′ inter-
face to facilitate the formation of SESF (a precursor to twin 
formation) without the need for thermally activated reorder -
ing. Under the combination of applied shear stress and tem-
perature, precipitate shearing occurs via passage of Shockley 
partial dislocations on adjacent slip planes that form planar 
faults like CSF , SESF and twin. These shearing processes may 
be relevant to an in-depth understanding of twin formation in 
Ni-based superalloys under the conditions that may not favor 
other conventionally known processes [77]. It is reasonable to 
expect that this work can provide additional stimulus to the 
scientific community to explore other possible deformation 
pathways leading to the formation of twins and comprehend 
the underlying plastic deformation processes at high tempera-
tures in Ni-based superalloys more exhaustively.
 Computational methodology
The MD simulations are performed using LAMMPS (Large-
Scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator) code [79] 
employing the embedded atom method [80] potential of Mishin 
[69]. This potential reproduces the experimental and ab initio 
data and has, therefore, been extensively utilized to simulate 
the plastic deformation behavior of γ ′-strengthened Ni-based 
superalloys using MD [56, 57, 60, 81, 82]. The simulation model 
employed in this work is depicted in Fig. 7. The composite sys-
tem contains both the γ (FCC Ni) and γ ′ (L12 Ni3Al) and con-
tains approximately 10 million atoms. The γ matrix contains ≈ 
12.0 % Al atoms in solid solution of Ni [see Fig. 7(a)]. After per-
forming static relaxation using conjugate-gradient minimization 
scheme, a hybrid MD/Monte Carlo (MC) approach is adopted 
to achieve coherency between the matrix and precipitate phases 
in order for the microstructure to be representative of a real 
aggregate. Specifically, after every 500 MD runs at a temperature 
of 900 K using Nose–Hoover thermostat and barostat [83], MC 
exchanges for 4% Al atoms are performed. The Al atoms are 
chosen randomly in the simulation box and 1000 trial exchanges 
of Al atoms to Ni atoms are performed in each MC cycle. Con-
sequently, at the end of MD/MC run, the interface between γ 
and γ ′ phase becomes less sharp as shown in Fig.  7(b). Several 
studies have reported the microstructure of Ni-based superal-
loys wherein the particles are coherent with the matrix with very 
few defects at high temperatures [84–86].
The hybrid MC/MD relaxation procedure adopted here 
is aimed at providing a thermodynamically realistic configu -
ration of the system before applying shear loading. Initially 
created γ − γ ′ interfaces are sharp, which can introduce unde-
sired stresses and can act as artificial barriers/sources for dis-
locations. Therefore, in order to achieve a coherent interface, 
the present work leverages the strengths of both MC and MD 
to efficiently handle the configurational changes and arrive at 
an adequate thermodynamic equilibrium at the desired tem -
perature. The percentage of exchanges and number of MD 
runs are determined to balance the efficiency of the equili-
bration process and the computational time. Attempting to 
exchange every Al atom during MC simulations would be very 
expensive and time-consuming. Choosing a smaller subset 
ensures sufficient perturbations in the system to explore new 
configurations without unnecessary over-sampling. Similar 
values have been utilized in previous studies to ensure a bal -
ance between comprehensive sampling and computational 
time [ 87–89].
It may also be noted that the exchange process is an exchange 
of atomic lattice sites involving Al and Ni atoms. Therefore, the 
move is a computational exchange but with a physical basis; 
the exchange allows to efficiently sample the configurational 
space of the material to reach the desired state of thermody-
namic equilibrium at the target temperature [ 90]. The physical 
basis implicitly lies in how diffusion occurs; the computational 
exchange mimics the outcome of such a diffusion process with-
out explicitly modeling the process itself because modeling of 
vacancy diffusion at the inherent timescales of MD simulations 
will be computationally difficult. In this regard, the exchanged 
atoms are not necessarily nearest neighbors. Additionally, it is 
highly unlikely that this random exchange inadvertently cre-
ates CSFs in the γ ′ phase or artificially bias the system toward 
fault formation. This is because if a particular atomic exchange 
were to create a CSF in the system/lead to a state of the system 
wherein CSF will be readily formed during deformation, then 
that state of the system (or that particular atomic exchange) is a 
high energy state and is likely to be rejected by the Metropolis 
criteria [91].
In order to put the matrix dislocations on one of the 12 
available glide systems of FCC crystal, the simulation box is 
oriented along [¯110] , [1 1 1], and [11¯2] crystallographic orien -
tations in x-, y-, and z-directions, respectively [Fig.  1(b)]. The 
interface is perpendicular to the slip plane so that the gliding 
matrix dislocations encounter the precipitate directly along their 
path during slip. This simulation setup maximizes the conse-
quent interaction between the defects in γ phase and the γ − γ ′ 
boundary. Additionally, twinning in FCC-based crystals also 
occurs typically on {1 1 1} planes [19]. Therefore, the forma-
tion and propagation of stacking faults (CSF , SESF) and twins 
are readily observed in this setup. Putting the interface perpen-
dicular to {1 1 1} plane represents a clear case where the matrix 
dislocations must encounter and interact with the obstacle in 
its glide path ( γ − γ ′ interface), thereby allowing for a detailed

atomistic study of the underlying deformation process. The cell 
dimensions are approximately 300 nm (250 nm of γ and 50 nm 
of γ ′ ) × 30 nm × 20 nm along x-, y-, and z-directions in order to 
minimize the image forces and achieve convergence of results. 
Such a large size of simulation box along the direction of Burg-
ers vector ensures that the distances between original matrix 
dislocations and their periodic images is large enough to mini-
mizing unintended interactions. Five equidistant straight edge 
dislocations are introduced in the γ matrix such each dislocation 
glides on adjacent [1 1 1] plane under the action of applied shear 
stress along x- direction [see Fig. 1(a)]. The edge dislocations are 
created in the system using the procedure described by Osetsky 
and Bacon [92] and Atomsk tool [93] is used for creation of the 
atomistic system. The simulation box is under the influence of 
periodic boundaries along the dislocation line direction (z) and 
direction of Burgers vector (x). As is customary in the presence 
of periodic boundaries, another interface is present at x = 300 
nm. For simulating dislocation glide under the action of shear 
stress along the direction of Burgers vector, free surfaces are 
employed along y-direction [94].
The relaxation of the system is achieved using conjugate-
gradient method available in LAMMPS code using a tolerance of 
10−7 eV and 20,000 maximum iterations for forces and energies. 
After relaxation, the leading partial of the extended dislocation 
nearest to the γ − γ ′ interface is approximately 12 nm away 
so that no influence of the interface is initially sensed by the 
extended matrix dislocation. Preliminary simulations confirmed 
that for distances (of the leading partial of the extended disloca-
tion nearest to the γ − γ ′ interface) less than around 8 nm, the 
dislocation would begin to glide toward the γ − γ ′ interface 
during the static relaxation itself; this implies that the presence 
of interface at these small distances is sensed by the matrix dis-
location and the system is trying to achieve a minimum energy 
state through glide. Therefore, a 12 nm distance of the leading 
partial of the extended dislocation nearest to the γ − γ ′ interface 
is chosen for reliability and visual inspection at different stages 
of the static relaxation procedure confirmed that dislocations 
did not move during equilibration phase and remain at their 
intended positions during this relaxation period. After inserting 
dislocations and performing hybrid MD/MC steps, to bring the 
system to final thermodynamic equilibrium, dynamic relaxa-
tion at a temperature of 900 K is performed using Nose–Hoover 
thermostat and barostat [83] for 100 picoseconds. T o simulate 
matrix dislocation glide toward the γ − γ ′ interface, four to five 
atomic layers are selected on either side of the simulation box 
along y-direction. Shear stress along the direction of Burgers 
vector (x-direction) is applied on the top surface while keeping 
the bottom surface fixed. A specific value of shear stress τ is 
selected, from which the force acting on top surface is evalu -
ated as F = τ .A , where A is the area of slip plane ( A = lx× lz , 
lx and lz are simulation box lengths along x- and z-directions, 
respectively). This force is then applied to the top surface to 
simulate shear under a particular value of applied stress. The 
temperature of the system is kept at the desired value using the 
NVT ensemble. Applied shear stress is increased in steps of 50 
MPa until all the five edge dislocations impinge the γ − γ ′ inter-
face. Other than this stress controlled loading, another way to 
apply deformation is through shear strain increments via con-
tinuous shifting of upper layer of atoms by a fixed amount every 
time step. However, this approach poses a limit to the maximum 
amount of strain that can be imposed; beyond a certain amount 
of deformation, the applied loading itself may cause undesired 
dislocation nucleation from the interfaces [95]. Additionally, 
inertial effects can also incur during shear strain loading that 
may favor dislocation transmission across the interface [96]. In 
view of these observations, the stress-based approach has been 
used by various researchers in the past [97, 98]. During loading, 
the temperature is kept to the desired value of 900 K. A timestep 
of 1 femtoseconds is utilized and OVITO (Open Visualization 
T ool) is used for visualization of atomistic snapshots [99].
