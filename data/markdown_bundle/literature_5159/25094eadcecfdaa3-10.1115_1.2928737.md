Developments in Ferrous Alloy 
Technology for High-Temperature 
Service 
Developments during the past twenty-five years are outlined for the technology of 
ferrous alloys needed in elevated temperature service. These developments include 
new alloys with improved strength and corrosion resistance for use in nuclear, fossil, 
and petrochemical applications. Specific groups of alloys that are addressed include 
vanadium-modified low alloy steels, 9Cr-lMo- Vsteel, niobium-modified lean stain­
less steels, and high chrome-nickel iron alloys. A brief description of coating and 
claddings for improved corrosion resistance is also provided. 
Introduction 
When the ASME Pressure Vessels and Piping Division was 
established in 1966, the emphasis in materials technology was 
on nuclear power and the development of ASME Boiler and 
Pressure Vessel Code (BPV) Section III. High-temperature 
materials technology was well-established and incorporated 
into the Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Sections I, II, VIII, 
and IX and the Piping Code, B31. These codes were used in 
the fossil fuel-fired boiler and petrochemical industries within 
the United States. High-temperature alloys were characterized 
in a series of data compilations developed through the efforts 
of the ASTM-ASME Joint Committee on Effect of Temper­
ature on the Properties of Metals and The Metal Properties 
Council. There was little incentive to develop new alloys for 
pressure vessels and piping in the fossil fuel-fired boiler and 
petroleum industries during the ensuing decade. Even high-
temperature nuclear programs such as the gas-cooled reactor 
(GCR) and liquid-metal-fast breeder reactor (LMFBR) were 
structured toward a better understanding of existing alloys 
rather than new alloys, and consequently, the extension of 
BPV Section III into the creep range (Code Case N-47) was 
limited to materials such as 2 l/4Cr-lMo steel, types 304 and 
316 stainless steels, and alloy 800. 
During these years, however, significant advances were made 
in materials science, and tools were developed that made it 
possible to characterize alloy microstructures in considerable 
detail. Progress was made in understanding of factors that 
contribute to strength and toughness. In the late 1970s, re­
quirements for improved materials began to develop. These 
were identified early in the synfuels initiative, where heavy wall 
processing vessels were needed [1], and later in the fossil power 
industry where power cycling, plant life extension, and the 
desire to burn more corrosive coals began to reveal materials 
limitations [2]. Several new materials were produced that could 
improve performance of existing power boilers, and these al-

Low Alloy Steels 
Low alloy steels such as 1 l/4CR-0.5Mo steel (Grade 11) 
and 2 l/4Cr-lMo steel (Grade 22, Class 2) have long been used 
to fabricate heavy section vessels and piping for fossil power, 
petroleum, and petrochemnical applications. Typically, vessels 
are designed for construction under Section VIII, Div. 1 or 
Div. 2 rules and normally operate at temperatures where time-
independent properties control the design allowables. Both 
monoblock and layered vessels have been used for many years 
with relatively few problems, although some cracking in nozzle 
regions has been detected in vessels exposed to long time service 
[4]. Piping and tubing, such as SA-335 Pll and P22, and SA-
213 Tl 1 and T22, are used at temperatures well into the creep 
range. Similarly, these low alloy steel systems have operated 
reliably for decades, although recently there have there been 
an increasing number of instances of cracking in reheat lines 
and headers in fossil power plants [5, 6]. During the 1970s, 
however, the oil shortage produced a demand for increased 
hydrotreating and desulfurization capabilities, and, briefly, a 
synfuels program supported by the United States Department 
of Energy examined the possibility of producing solvent refined 
coal (SRC) and other liquids by direct liquefaction. Single-
stage liquefaction required vessels exceeding 4 m (12 ft) in

Lean Austenitic Stainless Steel
Interest in advanced, high-strength, lean austenitic stainless
steels, containing less than 20 percent chromium, peaked in
the 1950s when efforts were undertaken to improve the heat
rate of pulverized coal power plants [20]. Alloys such as 17­
I4CuMo stainless steel, 15-15N, and Esshete 1250 were ex­
amined for service at steam temperatures to 6S0°C (l200°F)
[21]. The most notable example in the United States was the
use of 17-14CuMo stainless steel in the superheater of the
Eddystone Unit 1 plant. Fireside corrosion in the tubing and
thermal fatigue, creep embrittlement of stainless steel hard­
ware, and other concerns over lack of availability, however,
led to a lowering of steam temperatures and pressures. In the
United States today most plants operate at conditions where
300 series stainless steels such as types 304H and 347H are
favored in superheaters, and low alloy steels are widely used
at lower temperatures. Even so, corrosion problems have con­
tinued and may be getting worse, as the need to burn cheaper
but more corrosive coals is recognized. There have been in­
centives to replace superheater tubing with materials having
better performance [22]. Recently, for example, a fine-grained
version of type 347 stainless steel has been produced with
improved fireside and steam side corrosion resistance [23].
From a longer range viewpoint, however, the continuing need
to reduce cost has kindled interest in increasing the reliability
of the advanced steam cycle with its higher efficiency. The
initial emphasis has been on the increase of steam temperatures
to 593°C (llOO°F) as a near-term goal, but some consideration
has been given to achieving the steam conditions of the Ed­
dystone Unit 1, once again [3]. So far, however, such interest
has been confined to academic and research institutions, and
there have been no units ordered or offered by the power
industry and its suppliers in the United States. New consid­
eration is also being given to a gas-cooled reactor in which
main steam lines could run at temperatures near 593°C
(lloo°F).
Aggressive alloy development programs have-undertaken by
Japanese research groups, and several niobium and nitrogen­
modified lean austenitic stainless steels have been developed
[24-26]. This research thrust has been reviewed by Viswana­
than [27] and involves the application of the basic understand-
ing of the principles of allo)' design for strength and stability.
The technology is closely related to parallel research performed
on microalloyed lean stainless steels of interest in nuclear and
fusion energy applications [28]. Alloys of interest include mod­
ifications of 17-14CuMo stainless steel such as TEMPALOY
17-14CuMo@ and ST3Cu@, both of which are copper­
containing alloys, and HT-UPS (High Temperature Ultra fine
Precipitate Strengthened Alloys) examined by Maziasz [28],
which contain titanium, niobium, and vanadium additions.
The new alloys have excellent creep strength, and design stresses
tend to be limited by the time-independent properties at tem­
peratures to 620°C (lISO"F). To take advantage of the creep
strength, it was necessary to increase the yield and ultimate
strengths by one of three methods: nitrogen addition, copper
phase precipitation, or cold/warm work. Nitrogen has been
recognized as a potent strengthener which increases the yield
strength, increases creep strength, and stabilizes the micro­
structure against sigma formation. The Japanese alloys contain
nitrogen at levels in the range 0.1 to 0.2 percent, similar to the
Code approved 316N grade. When in solution, 'copper has
little influence on the yield strength of stainless steels. How­
ever, after suitable heat treatment, it was noticed that a fine
copper-rich precipitate can be produced that significantly in­
creased the yield strength of lean stainless steels. About 3
percent copper was required to produce this effect. Another
role that copper played in the strengthening of the MC forming
stainless steels was related to its increase of the stacking fault
energy. Small amounts of cold or warm work were required
to produce a fine, stable MC precipitate structure in lean stain­
less steels. The copper addition was thought to refine this
microstructure, thus requiring less work to produce the same
hardening effect [29].
The enhanced strength lean austenitic steel have been found
to be suitable for long-time service at temperatures to 704°C
(l300°F) due to the formation of a fine MC precipitate sta­
bilized by the dislocation structure. The creep rupture strength
of several of the lean stainless steels is compared in Fig. 11.
The new alloys are 25 to 100 percent stronger than type 316
stainless steel in the temperature range 650 to 704°C (1200 to
1300°F). Data accumlliated to times beyond 40,000 hr at 700°C
(l292°F) indicate that the enhanced strength produced by these
'methods can be maintained with minimal the loss of ductility.
Indeed, some alloys show little or no creep cavitation and are
somewhat more resistant the intergranular cracking under
creep-crack and creep-fatigue-crack loading conditions than
standard 300 series stainless steels. With suitable claddding,
the lean stainless steels show excellent potential for use at steam
temperatures to 593°C (HOO°F), even in highly corrosive coal
ash. Their high strengths and excellent ductilities should im­
prove the thermal cycling capabilities as well.

Claddings have been used for many years in pressure vessels
and piping systems. Nuclear and petrochemical pressure vessels
are weld overly clad with 300 series stainless steels or high
chromium nickel base alloys. Bimetallic tubing is typically
coextruded. However, the potential for severe corrosion in
various coal combustion and incineration environments has
renewed interest in cladding technology for both Code-ap­
proved and enhanced strength developmental alloys. Typical
cladding alloys include stainless steels, such as types 304, 309,
and 310, and nickel base alloys such as alloys 625, 825, Inconel
690@, InconeI671@, CR35A@, InconeI8XA@, and HR­
160@ [26, 36, 37]. Clad tubing is expensive, but service ex­
perience has been good [37] and its use is common and justified
in several applications. Figure 14 shows some typical extruded
tubes.
The 300 series stainless steels have been modified with var­
ious levels of aluminum, silicon, or rare earths for scaling
resistance. Examples are MA253 and RA85H@ (38). Often,
these alloys have been used for internal structures, rather than
pressure vessels and piping, but with increased interest in com­
ponents for fluidized bed combustors, gasifiers, and inciner­
ation the aluminum and silicon-bearing alloys are gaining
service experience.
A whole new class of sulfidation-resistant ferrous alloys is
represented by the iron aluminides, containing 11 percent alu­
minum, 2 percent chromium, and balance iron. The alloys
have been shown to be uniquely resistant to sulfidation [39]
as indicated in Fig. 15. Although in the development stage,
there are expectations that some aluminides may someday be
approved for pressure-bearing applications.
