ABSTRACT
Laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) enables the fabrication of geometrically complex Ti
2
AlNb alloys. However, the 
high-temperature brittleness of the as-built alloy remains a major limitation. Ti
2
AlNb alloys inherently exhibit 
multiple phase transformation behavior. Rapid solidification during L-PBF suppresses these transformations, 
preventing precipitation of the brittle α
2 
phase but resulting in a heterogeneous microstructure. Although this 
heterogeneity enhances room-temperature properties, its effect on high-temperature deformation remains un -
clear. In this study, a high-density and crack-free Ti
2
AlNb alloy was successfully fabricated using optimized L- 
PBF parameters, exhibiting excellent room-temperature strength (1020 MPa) and ductility (19.7 %). At 650 ℃ , 
the tensile properties exhibited pronounced orientation dependence. Specimens tested along the building di -
rection (BD) achieved a strength of 796 MPa and an elongation of 18.2 %, among the highest values reported for 
L-PBFed Ti
2
AlNb alloys. In contrast, specimens tested along the transverse direction (TD) showed brittle fracture. 
Further analysis indicates that although the heterogeneous microstructure has minimal effect on room- 
temperature anisotropy, it induces localized stress concentrations during tensile deformation. Under high- 
temperature conditions, these stress concentrations promote multiple phase transformations, particularly the 
in-situ precipitation of the brittle α
2 
phase at grain boundaries, identified as the primary cause of premature 
failure. Therefore, enhancing high-temperature performance requires reducing microstructural heterogeneity or 
optimizing the relationship between microstructure and loading direction. This study provides new insights into 
fracture mechanisms associated with heterogeneous microstructure and offers guidance for process control 
strategies to enable the fabrication of high-performance Ti
2
AlNb alloy components via L-PBF.
1. Introduction
The rapid development of the aerospace industry has imposed 
increasing demands on high-temperature structural materials. There is 
an urgent need to develop lightweight high-temperature structural 
materials that combine excellent service performance with low density. 
To address this challenge, Banerjee et al. [1] developed a novel 
titanium-aluminum intermetallic compound, the Ti
2
AlNb alloy 
featuring an orthorhombic phase (O phase). Compared to conventional 
titanium-aluminum intermetallic compounds, Ti
2
AlNb alloy exhibits

three main constituent phases, including B2/ β , α

β /B2 phase (BCC structure), serves as the matrix phase, appearing as the 
ordered B2 structure at high temperatures and transforming into the 
disordered β structure at lower temperatures, where it provides the best 
plasticity. The α
2 
phase (HCP structure) is a brittle phase due to its 
limited slip systems and poor deformability. The O phase (ordered 
orthorhombic structure) acts as the primary strengthening phase at 
high-temperatures. Therefore, controlling the proportion, morphology, 
and distribution of the three phases through tailored manufacturing 
processes is essential for optimizing the overall properties of Ti
2
AlNb 
alloys [5] . Moreover, these phases are susceptible to multiple phase 
transformations under high-temperature or stress conditions, which 
significantly affect the microstructural stability and mechanical perfor -
mance of the alloy.
For Ti
2
AlNb alloy, high-temperature isothermal forging is commonly 
employed to produce fine grains and tailored phase compositions to 
meet service requirements [6] . However, its high cost, processing 
complexity, and elevated forming temperatures have limited its wide -
spread application in Ti
2
AlNb alloy fabrication. In addition, aerospace 
structural components often feature complex geometries, making it 
challenging for conventional fabrication methods to meet processing 
demands. Comparatively, additive manufacturing (AM) offers distinct 
advantages in the near-net-shape fabrication of complex structures, 
owing to its layer-by-layer deposition approach [7] . Among various AM 
methods, laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) has attracted considerable 
attention due to its superior surface quality, higher dimensional accu -
racy, and reduced material waste [8] .
Extensive studies have demonstrated that AM, owing to its charac -
teristic rapid melting and solidification process, can fabricate alloys with 
heterogeneous microstructure [9,10] . Such heterogeneity has been re -
ported to synergistically enhance both strength and ductility. Current 
studies have optimized process parameters, such as laser power, scan -
ning speed and scanning strategy, to successfully fabricate high-density, 
crack-free Ti
2
AlNb alloys that exhibit distinct heterogeneous structural 
characteristics [11,12] . Although these alloys demonstrate relatively 
favorable mechanical properties at room temperature, process optimi -
zation alone remains insufficient to overcome high-temperature brit -
tleness and mechanical anisotropy. Further studies [13] suggest that the 
brittle fracture of L-PBFed Ti
2
AlNb alloys at high temperature may result 
from significant grain boundary decohesion and the weakened pinning 
effect of nano-O phases. Given that Ti
2
AlNb is a high-temperature 
structural alloy typically employed in the range of 600 – 750 ℃ , 
enhancing its high-temperature mechanical performance is critically 
important. To address this challenge, Chen et al. [14] introduced ni -
trogen interstitial solid-solution strengthening through in-situ reactive 
gas during L-PBF. Nitrogen was found to preferentially dissolve in the β 
phase, increasing lattice distortion and dislocation density, thereby 
improving yield strength from 932 MPa to 1080 MPa without significant 
compromising ductility. However, high-temperature testing still reveals 
a brittle fracture mode, mainly due to nitrogen-induced suppression of 
the O phase and deterioration of the thermal stability of the β phase. In 
addition, post-processing heat treatments have been explored as a means 
to alleviate high-temperature brittleness, but improvements in 
high-temperature performance remain limited and are often achieved at 
the expense of room-temperature properties [15,16] . This underscores 
the dominant role of the as-built microstructure in determining the 
overall mechanical behavior. However, most existing studies have 
largely overlooked the critical influence of multiple phase trans -
formations on the high-temperature mechanical properties of Ti
2
AlNb 
alloys. As a complex intermetallic system, Ti
2
AlNb exhibits multiple 
phase transformations [17,18] , among which the O phase preferentially 
precipitates within grains or along grain boundaries, thereby enhancing 
strength while retaining moderate ductility. In contrast, the α
2 
phase 
tends to precipitate at grain boundaries or stress-concentrated regions, 
severely degrading ductility and promoting brittle failure. Notably, the 
heterogeneous microstructure inherent to the AM process may signifi -
cantly affect in-situ phase precipitation behavior, thereby influencing 
the mechanical response at both room and high temperatures ( Fig. 1 ). 
Despite this, a systematic understanding of how such in-situ multiple 
phase transformations impact high-temperature brittleness and me -
chanical anisotropy in L-PBFed Ti
2
AlNb alloys remains lacking. This 
critical knowledge gap continues to hinder the engineering application 
of L-PBFed Ti
2
AlNb alloy.
To address the above issue, this study first integrates thermal- 
mechanical simulations with experimental investigations to systemati -
cally optimize the L-PBF processing parameters (laser power and scan -
ning speed) for Ti
2
AlNb alloy, enabling the fabrication of high-density,

4. Discussion
4.1. Microstructure evolution mechanism of Ti
2
AlNb alloy during L-PBF 
process
Fig. 13 a illustrates the thermal evolution curves along the center of 
the melt pool, which undergoes a similar thermal history along the laser 
scanning direction as well as in the adjacent melt tracks. As the laser 
passed through the central region of the melt pool, the surface temper -
ature of the material (~3250 K) exceeds the liquidus line (2093 K), 
resulting in complete melting of the material. Additionally, it can be 
found that the temperature gradient (G) at the melt pool center is 
significantly high (inset of Fig. 13 a), promoting rapid grain growth 
during solidification and leading to the formation of coarse β grains [17, 
27] . Combined with Fig. 11 a, the large temperature gradient under the 
ultra-rapid solidification conditions of L-PBF accelerates the growth of 
B2/ β grains while simultaneously suppressing O phase precipitation. 
Correspondingly, Fig. 13 b shows the thermal history along the over -
lapping region of the melt pool, the overlapping region undergoes two 
thermal cycles (red region). The temperature gradient in the over -
lapping region is relatively low, resulting in slower grain growth. 
Moreover, this region experiences partial remelting and reheating, 
leading to microstructural reconstruction after solidification [28] . Dur -
ing the L-PBF process, the thermal cycling and high residual stress (inset 
of Fig. 13 b) induced the formation of fine O phase in the overlapping 
regions [4] . However, due to the limited time for reheating or remelting 
of the previous layers during L-PBF process, the recrystallization of the 
overlapping regions has not yet been completed, resulting in the pres -
ence of residual LAGBs in the as-built samples [29] , as shown in Fig. 12 d. 
These factors collectively result in the formation of fine grains in the 
overlapping zones. Thus, during the L-PBF process, the combination of 
high cooling rates and substantial temperature gradients promotes the 
formation of coarse β grains in the central region of the melt pool, while

4.2. Fracture mechanism for Ti
2
AlNb alloy at room-temperature and 
high-temperature
As depicted in Fig. 8 , the as-built samples exhibit subtle anisotropy of 
mechanical properties at room-temperature, but show pronounced 
anisotropy at high temperature. This suggests that during tensile testing 
at room and high temperatures, the variations in heterogeneous 
microstructure along different loading directions contribute to signifi -
cant differences in fracture behavior.
At room-temperature, both the TD and BD samples exhibit numerous 
dimples on their fracture surfaces, indicating ductile intergranular 
ductile fracture ( Fig. 9 a-b). However, the slight anisotropy observed is 
closely related to the microstructural differences between the XY and XZ 
planes, as shown in Fig. 14 a. During room-temperature tensile defor -
mation, the microstructural characteristics of the XZ plane significantly 
affect the fracture behavior of the BD samples. The XZ plane exhibits a 
columnar grain structure, and during the tensile process of the BD 
samples, the columnar grains deform along the long-axis direction. At 
this stage, the grains are relatively large, and the dislocation density is 
lower, which facilitates dislocation accumulation and slip, thereby 
enhancing plasticity. In contrast, for the TD samples, during the tensile 
process, the columnar grains in the XZ plane deform along the short-axis 
direction, and the equiaxed grains alternately distributed in the XY 
plane also undergo deformation. Specifically, due to the smaller grain 
size and higher dislocation density in the XY plane, although these 
characteristics enhance the strength of the alloy, they also render it more 
prone to stress concentration, thereby reducing the ductility of the alloy. 
Although the O phase is present in small quantities and exhibits minor 
distribution differences between the two planes, its influence on room- 
temperature anisotropy can be considered negligible.
At 650 ℃ , BD and TD samples exhibit pronounced anisotropy of 
mechanical properties. The TD samples show pronounced brittle frac -
ture, while the BD samples still exhibit ductile fracture. This is due to the 
fact that the differences in microstructure between the XZ and XY planes 
during the tensile process significantly influenced the anisotropy of 
mechanical properties at high-temperature, as shown in Fig. 14 b. EBSD 
analysis of the post-fracture XZ plane reveals a higher Schmid factor for 
BD samples ( Fig. 15 a), indicating that when loading along the long-axis 
of the columnar grains, the dislocation density is relatively low 
( Fig. 15 c). As a result, the main slip systems, {110} < 111 > and 
{112} < 111 > , are more likely to undergo slip, enabling sustained 
plastic deformation. Furthermore, the BD samples exhibit greater strain 
capacity ( Fig. 8 c) and visible necking, confirming their superior 
ductility. Fractographic analysis indicates that cracks in the BD samples 
primarily initiate and propagate along the β grain boundaries. Combined 
with the observed fracture morphology, it can be concluded that the BD 
samples undergo intergranular ductile fracture. In contrast, TD samples 
show a lower Schmid factor ( Fig. 15 b) and a higher geometrically 
necessary dislocation (GND) density ( Fig. 15 d), indicating more intense 
dislocation interactions and localized strain accumulation when loaded 
along the short-axis of the columnar grains. The elevated GND density 
reflects greater plastic incompatibility and enhanced dislocation stor -
age, which hinder the activation of slip systems and reduce the plastic 
deformability of the material [34] . Prior studies have shown that such 
localized dislocation pile-ups promote crack initiation, particularly at 
grain boundaries or slip band intersections [35,36] . As a result, the TD

5. Conclusions
In summary, based on the successful fabrication of high-quality 
samples, this study elucidates the formation mechanism of heteroge -
neous microstructure in L-PBFed Ti
2
AlNb alloys and reveals its critical 
role in high-temperature brittleness. The main conclusions are as 
follows: 
(1) During L-PBF process, rapid solidification suppresses the precip -
itation of the α
2 
phase, while repeated thermal cycling promotes 
the precipitation of nano-O phase. The complex thermal history 
ultimately induces the formation of heterogeneous β grains, 
revealing the link between processing parameters and phase 
transformations behavior in Ti
2
AlNb alloys. These findings pro -
vide a basis for controlling solidification behavior in other addi -
tively manufactured alloys with complex transformation 
pathways.
(2) The microstructure composed of refined β grains, high-density 
dislocations, and dispersed nano-O phases contributes to 
enhanced strength without sacrificing ductility. As a result, the 
alloy exhibits excellent room-temperature mechanical properties, 
with a tensile strength of 1020 MPa and an elongation of 19.7 %.
(3) At 650 ℃ , microstructural heterogeneity induces local stress 
concentrations along the transverse direction (TD), the in-situ 
precipitation of brittle α ₂ phase at grain boundaries and leading 
to premature fracture. In contrast, the build direction (BD) sam -
ple shows superior strength (796 MPa) and ductility (18.2 %), 
highlighting the importance of microstructure alignment with 
loading direction. These findings provide valuable guidance for 
the microstructural optimization of other additively manufac -
tured or directionally solidified intermetallics, and emphasize the 
potential of advanced strategies such as in-situ remelting, laser 
forging, or thermal management to achieve performance 
enhancement.
(4) Looking forward, this work lays the foundation for 
microstructure-informed design strategies in AM. Future studies 
can extend these insights to other Ti-, or Al-based intermetallics 
to verify the generality of the fracture mechanisms and support 
the development of unified processing-design guidelines for 
advanced structural materials.
