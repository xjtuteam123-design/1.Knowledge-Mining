ABSTRACT 
In this study, we fabricated a Ni-base superalloy with three different carbon contents using laser 
metal deposition (LMD) and systematically investigated its microstructure and mechanical 
properties. The characterization results showed that carbides precipitated along the grain 
boundaries in the additive manufactured alloys, the amount increased with the carbon content, 
and the residual stress reduced with the carbon content. In addition, carbide precipitation was 
mainly MC (M is mainly Ti, Ta). These samples exhibited excellent mechanical properties 
compared to the cast samples. Rupture tests conducted at 760 
◦C/780 MPa indicated that the high 
carbon content in the additively manufactured alloy affected the rupture life, with the medium- 
carbon additive manufactured alloy exhibiting better mechanical properties than the other carbon 
content. 
1. Introduction 
Ni-base superalloys have been widely used in aerospace due to its high strength and cost-effectiveness [1]. These days, Institute of 
Metal Research, Chinese Academy of Science has designed a novel alloy based on second-generation superalloy by adding grain 
boundary strengthening elements C and B. Recently [2], the importance of the addition of trace elements to the mechanical properties 
of nickel-based superalloys has been widely recognized. It has been reported [3] that adding trace elements such as C and Zr can 
improve the stress fracture properties of Ni-based superalloys. Reed [4] found that limiting the concentrations of elements that 
promote precipitation of the γ phase, as well as grain boundary strengthening metalloids such as C and B, can restrict the solidification 
range and improve processability, but may compromise creep properties at high temperatures. Wei [5] investigated the significant 
impact of carbon content on the microstructure and elevated temperature tensile strength of fine-grain CM-681LC superalloy, where 
the total area fraction of carbides significantly increases with carbon content, but the carbide shapes and sizes remain similar between 
the alloys with 0.11 wt% and 0.15 wt% carbon content. Wei [5] investigated that carbon content has a significant impact on the 
microstructure and elevated temperature tensile strength of fine-grain CM-681LC superalloy. Increasing the carbon content from 0.11 
wt% to 0.15 wt% significantly increases the total area fraction of carbides, but the carbide shapes and sizes of the two alloys are similar. 
The carbon content not only affects the morphology, size, and distribution of carbides but also impacts the mechanical properties of 
superalloys. Fine-grained carbides impede grain boundary slip and dislocations, which are believed to be the main factor in the better 
creep properties of alloys. However, coarse or continuous carbides are detrimental, where these carbides are prone to become an 
embryonic zone of crack [6,7]. The addition of trace elements has been cited as a variety of possible strengthening mechanisms,

Additive manufacturing AM, also known as 3D printing. Additive manufacturing (AM), as a novel process forming technology, has 
been paid great attention by domestic and international industrial circles in recent years, which has caused an essential response in the 
world manufacturing industry. 
It is based on the traditional digital 3D model and generates 3D parts through the continuous superposition of forming materials. It 
has the advantages of laser processing technology and rapid prototyping technology. Amongst AM techniques, Laser metal deposition 
(LMD) technology utilizes high laser power and a giant laser beam size to achieve high build efficiency. Laser metal deposition also has 
a high temperature gradient (10
5–107 K/m) and speedy cooling rate (10 3–105 K/s), which is suitable for the preparation of nickel- 
based superalloy components with low element segregation [16]. In recent years, there have been many studies on additive 
manufacturing of superalloys [16–18] at domestic and international. The weldability of superalloys with (Al + Ti) content over 4% is 
difficult, which would produce large cracks during additive manufacturing [19–21]. In this paper, we have fabricated a new alloy with 
(Al + Ti) content of 8%. Under the premise of optimizing process parameters, nickel-based superalloys were manufactured by laser 
metal deposition technology. The effect of C content on the microstructure and mechanical performance (tensile and rupture prop

2. Materials and methods 
The superalloy powder used by LMD additive manufacturing was prepared by the Argon Gas Atomization. Table 1 shows the 
nominal and actual compositions of the powder, with carbon content determined using a CS-444LS carbon-sulfur measurement meter 
and other elements validated using an inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometer (ICP-AES). The composition is shown 
in Table 1. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the powder was mainly spherical, with satellite powder attached to the surface. The particle size of 
the powder ranges from 80 to 150 μm, with an average size of 101 μm. 
The LMD additive manufacturing equipment adopted the Laser deposition CNC machine tool of the Institute of Metal Research, 
Chinese Academy of Sciences Fig. 1(b). The process parameters were as follows: the spot diameter 2 mm, hatch spacing 1.14 mm, layer 
thickness 0.2 mm, overlap rate 43%, the laser power 1700 W, the powder feed rate 12 g/min, the scan velocity 1300 mm/min, and the 
Z axis increment 20 
μm Fig. 1(c). The selected substrate was cast polycrystalline GH3536 alloy Fig. 1(d)(The composition is shown in 
Table 2), which was cut into small pieces of 20 × 20 × 10 mm3 by wire cutting. Then polished it with #50 sandpaper to remove the 
oxide scale, and ultrasonically cleaned with 98% alcohol. 
Fig. 1(e,f,g) shows that there are obvious “layer-to-layer” overlapping boundaries between the various melt pools inside the sample, 
and the melt pool boundaries in the microstructure are distributed in a fish scale pattern. Furthermore, the melt pools are combined 
closely, which ensures the continuity of the microstructure in the growth direction. Additionally, columnar crystals penetrate multiple 
layers of melt pools. 
The shape of the melt pools resembles a quadratic curve due to the Gaussian distribution of the laser beam energy, which causes 
higher temperatures in the center of the melt pool than at the edges. Consequently, the depth of remelting in the center of the cladding 
layer is more substantial than that at the edges, forming new melt pools. The accumulation of heat during the forming process leads to 
the increase in the width of the sample as its thickness increases. 
To produce a polished surface for SEM analysis all samples were planarized with #150, #240, #400, #600, #800, #1000, #1200, 
#2000 grit SiC paper polish respectively, then polished down with M2.5 colloidal silica polishing paste. 
Representative transverse and longitudinal sections of the LMD specimens, perpendicular, and parallel to the build direction (Z), 
respectively, were prepared for microstructural analysis. In addition, in order to be easy to characterize, AM samples were etched by (5 
g CuSO4+25 ml HCl +50 ml H2O). 
The microstructures of the samples were imaged using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, EBSD) in a MIRA4 microscope. The 
SEM image was analyzed by the software ImageJ. The EBSD microscopes were operated at 20 kV with a step size of 2.5 μm. Subsequent 
analysis of the inverse pole figure (IPF) and kernel average misorientation (KAM) obtained from the Channel 5 software was performed 
using. 
The microstructure of the AM sample and the property samples were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM F20).

Fig. 3(a~f) shows the BSE SEM micrograph of carbides with different carbon contents, in which the carbides appear bright white. 
Carbides are abundantly distributed at grain boundaries and less in interdendritic. The statistical results indicate that (as shown in 
Fig. 4) the carbides increase significantly with further increase in carbon content. In addition, the number of carbides in the longi -
tudinal section is more than in the transverse section; as carbides precipitate at grain boundaries, the laser deposition process generates 
columnar crystals, as shown in Fig. 8(a), (b), (c), which have longer grain boundary in the longitudinal section than the transverse 
section, so more carbides are precipitated. 
In Fig. 5(a~f), the morphology of the γ
′ phase in alloys with varying carbon contents is illustrated, with the γ′ phase being uniformly 
distributed throughout the crystal. The size and volume fraction of the γ′ phase were analyzed statistically using the IPP software, and 
the results indicate a decrease in both parameters with increasing carbon content, as depicted in Fig. 5. (g, h, i). The γ′ phase is 
observed to possess irregularly shaped cubes with a uniform size of approximately 0.2 μm, while a single plate-like structure is present 
owing to the narrow matrix channel. The increase in carbon content leads to a reduction in the content of elements such as Ti and Ta 
that are originally present in the matrix, and instead forms carbides such as MC and M6C, thereby reducing the precipitation of the γ′
phase. However, despite these changes, the overall effect of carbon on the size and morphology of γ′ remains negligible. 
Carbides in AM samples are confirmed to be primary MC (M is Ti, Ta) type Fig. 6(a,b) and M6C (M is W) type carbides (TEM carbide 
morphology is shown in Fig. 6(c,d)), respectively, while M23C6 (M is Cr) type carbides hardly exist. However, M23C6 is the most stable, 
the transformation process of MC decomposing to M23C6 demands a long time and high temperature [22]. Meanwhile, MC and M6C 
carbides do not necessarily exist independently; as shown in Figs. 6 (c) and Fig. 7(a)), the edges of MC carbide decompose into uniform 
bright M 6C carbide. The orientation relationship between M 6C and the matrix is: ({001}M 6C//{001} matrix 
<001>M6C//<001>matrix) MC carbide is unstable at high temperature and reacts with the matrix to produce a slight decomposition 
into M6C carbide. In contrast, M6C, as a secondary carbide, can hinder dislocation motion and strengthen the matrix. 
The carbides in the AM samples are rich in W, Ta, and Ti Fig. 7(b ~ g). These elements have different concentration gradients in the 
multilayer structure of MC/M6C. The concentration of carbon in MC carbide is higher than that in the γ matrix [23], so carbon is mainly 
diffused from MC to γ. In contrast, W, Mo, and other elements in the γ matrix can only reach the edge of MC due to the low diffusion 
rate [24,25], resulting in the formation of a small amount of M6C in MC carbide and W element in γ matrix at the edge of MC carbide. 
The IPF maps and kernel average misorientation (KAM) distribution maps analyzed by EBSD are shown in Fig. (8), which shows 
coarse columnar crystals. In the laser metal deposition process, columnar crystals grow preferentially in the deposition direction with a 
higher heat loss rate due to the direction opposite to the heat flow heat dissipation faster. Moving heat source causes the AM sample to 
be heated locally and then rapidly cooled, resulting in the generation of residual stress due to large temperature gradients. The KAM 
map can reflect the residual stress; the stress concentration increases with the color changing from cool color to warm color (Fig. 8(d), 
(e), (f)). The residual stress is mainly concentrated at the grain boundary, from the average KAM results in Fig. 8(g), (h), (i), compared 
with (0.10%C and 0.15%C), the low-carbon KAM value is 4.76 higher, which is due to the carbides precipitating and transforming a 
consuming part of the internal energy and reducing the residual stress.

and mechanical properties was investigated under an optimized preparation process. The results are summarized as follows. 
1. The carbon content does not affect the γ′ phase and the dendritic structure of the additively manufactured superalloy. The carbides 
in samples were confirmed to be primary MC type and M6C type carbides, while M23C6 type carbides were found to be rare. The 
precipitation of carbides consumed internal energy, resulting in a reduction of residual stress with the addition of carbon. 
2. The carbon content had a significant effect on the morphology, size, and fraction of carbides, with the number of carbides 
increasing as the carbon content increased. 
3. The rupture tests conducted at 760 ◦C/780 MPa revealed that the grain boundary strengthening effect is significant when an 
appropriate amount of carbon (0.10 wt%) is added to the alloy, resulting in the strengthening effect of MC and M23C6. In contrast, 
the mechanical properties test results showed that a higher volume fraction of carbide was detrimental to room temperature 
properties. These findings provide valuable insights for the optimization of carbon content in the additive manufacturing of 
superalloys.
