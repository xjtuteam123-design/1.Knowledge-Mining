ABSTRACT
The long-term goal of materials science is to predict material properties directly from their compositions. This 
study established a high-entropy alloy (HEA) composition-hardness database and developed a machine learning- 
based high-throughput screening platform. Beyond hardness prediction, the platform calculates key parameters 
like valence electron-to-total electron ratio ( e/n ), valence electron concentration, mixing enthalpy, configura -
tional entropy, atomic size mismatch, and electronegativity difference across 15 essential elements. Additionally, 
we identified a previously overlooked parameter, e/n , which shows a strong correlation with the phase structure 
and hardness of HEAs. Through extensive high-throughput screening, we designed a novel HEA composition,

1. Introduction
Over the past two decades, extensive research on high-entropy alloys 
(HEAs) has demonstrated that incorporating various major constituents 
can significantly enhance the properties of these alloys [1,2] . Unlike 
traditional alloys, which are typically composed of one or two principal 
elements, HEAs are characterized by the presence of multiple principal 
elements in roughly equal proportions (typically 5 – 35 at%) [3] . This 
results in a complex microstructure, which in turn leads to unprece -
dented properties such as promising radiation resistance [4,5] , 
enhanced mechanical strength [6,7] , significant corrosion [8,9] , and 
wear resistance [10] . The inclusion of multiple principal elements cre -
ates a vast compositional space for HEA development, offering 
numerous opportunities to enhance alloy properties. However, this 
expansive compositional space also introduces challenges in terms of 
optimization, particularly when using traditional "trial-and-error" or 
"intuitive" exploration methods.
Advances in computational simulation technologies have greatly 
promoted the development of materials science. Among them, the rapid 
development of machine learning (ML) has profoundly transformed 
numerous scientific fields. ML has not only fundamentally transformed 
several domains of computer science, such as computer vision and 
natural language processing, but has also revolutionized various scien -
tific disciplines, including particle signal detection [11] , protein struc -
ture prediction [12] , discovery of new alloys [13] and medical image 
analysis [14] . In the field of materials science, ML offers tremendous 
potential for accelerating the discovery and optimization of advanced 
materials: for instance, to address the conflict between strength and 
plasticity, Wei et al. [15] developed a new type of lead-free solder alloy 
with both high strength and high ductility based on ML technology, 
using an active learning "divide and conquer" strategy; Giles et al. [16] , 
on the other hand, combined advanced ML frameworks with optimiza -
tion methods to construct a yield strength prediction model, and 
discovered refractory high-entropy alloys with excellent

In alloy performance evaluation, hardness serves as a crucial metric 
for assessing a material ’ s resistance to local plastic deformation, which

to develop a ML model capable of precisely depicting the intricate 
relationship between the composition and hardness of HEAs. While 
existing ML models have initially established a connection between HEA 
composition and hardness [19 – 21] , these models are generally limited 
to specific alloy systems. Unlike previous ML models that focused on 
composition-hardness correlations of one or two kinds of HEAs, our goal 
is to train an accurate and reliable ML model that encompasses nearly all 
common HEAs, involving 15 elements: Al, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Ni, Mo, Ti, W, 
Nb, Ta, V, Mn, Hf, and Zr. Although the ML model proposed in Ref. [22]
covers 16 elements, it performs poorly in prediction accuracy. They 
predicted the compositions of 7 high-hardness HEAs, but the mean 
relative error was up to 45 %, indicating that the model still needs to be 
improved in terms of prediction accuracy. Moreover, the current ML 
models for alloy properties exhibit a significant degree of homogeneity, 
as they all utilize similar databases and analogous feature engineering 
techniques. This not only constrains the full expansion of dataset scales 
but also fails to facilitate new discoveries in the physical mechanisms of 
materials science.

predicting the relationship between alloy composition and hardness, 
incorporating 15 key elements. During the process of feature engineer -

electrons to total electrons ( e/n ) and hardness. Through density func -
tional theory (DFT) calculations, we delved into the underlying physical

2.1. ML framework
The framework for the ML model designed to construct the 
composition-hardness relationship of common HEAs (involving ele -
ments such as Al, Co, Cr, Cu, Fe, Ni, Mo, Ti, W, Nb, Ta, V, Mn, Hf, and Zr) 
consists of four main components: data collection, feature engineering, 
ML algorithm selection, and the construction of the composition- 
hardness relationship. This process is illustrated in Fig. 1 . 
(1) Data collection and cleaning 
The collected database of HEAs with 15 elements is from 
several ML databases as well as published literature, with a total 
of 1680 pieces of initial data, as shown in Supplementary Table 1
(including composition, hardness, and references). Since 
different heat treatments and preparation processes can signifi -

melting, with their properties measured in the as-cast state.

collected datasets. By identifying and removing duplicate records 
and eliminating unreasonable outliers, we ensured the quality 
and reliability of the datasets, laying a solid foundation for sub -

(2) Feature engineering 
In the field of ML, the selection of feature descriptors is crucial 
for building high-performance models. These feature values, as 
the basic units in a dataset, directly reflect the intrinsic properties 
and underlying patterns in the data. Common features used to 
construct ML models for HEAs include mixing enthalpy, config -
urational entropy, melting point, and valence electron concen -
tration. Among these, mixing enthalpy is particularly significant 
for accurately describing the performance of HEAs. To calculate 
the mixing enthalpies of 15 elements, we employed a previously 
modified Miedema model developed by our research group [24] . 
The results are presented in Table S1 . 
Collecting features for each element is a complex process. To 
facilitate subsequent research building upon this foundation, we 
have compiled and summarized all the characteristic values 
commonly used in this study, including specific heat capacity 
( SHA ), Young ’ s modulus ( YM ), specific latent heat of fusion 
( SLHF ), and other feature values. Detailed descriptions are pro -
vided in Table S2 . Based on the features in Tables S1 and S2 , we 
have obtained 57 descriptors, which are detailed in Table S3 . 
Notably, in the research on the phase structure and mechanical 
properties of HEAs, the concept of electron concentration is 
typically defined by two core parameters: e/a (number of itin -
erant electrons) and VEC (valence electron concentration). At the 
single-atom level, e/a represents the number of itinerant elec -
trons per atom, specifically referring to the number of outermost 
electrons in an atom, which is usually determined by the number 
of electrons in the s orbital; VEC , on the other hand, represents 
the number of valence electrons per atom, generally determined 
by the total number of electrons in the s and d orbitals. At the 
alloy level, both parameters characterize the overall electron 
concentration of the alloy through the weighted average of all 
constituent atoms. Building upon this, we propose a third elec -
tron concentration parameter — e/n : at the single-atom level, it is 
defined as the ratio of the total number of valence electrons to the 
total number of electrons, serving to characterize the proportion 
of valence electrons in a single atom; at the alloy level, it also 
characterizes the overall electron concentration of the alloy via 
the weighted average of all constituent atoms. Although this 
parameter has been overlooked in existing HEA studies, it has 
been confirmed to have a more significant correlation with the 
mechanical properties and phase structure of HEAs, and its pre -
dictive ability is superior to that of VEC and e/a .

(3) Selection of ML algorithms
In the final step of feature selection, we employed seven common ML 
algorithms [27] to select the features, and simultaneously identified the 
best-performing ML algorithms. These algorithms include Random 
Forest (RF) [28] , Logistic Regression (LR) [29] , Multi-Layer Perceptron 
(MLP) [30] , K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) [31] , Neural Network (NN) 
[32] , Support Vector Regression (SVR) [33] , and Extreme Gradient 
Boosting (XGBoost) [34] . Scikit-learn [35] stands out as a premier ML 
library, integrating numerous cutting-edge and widely applied ML al -
gorithms, thus providing a potent toolkit for data scientists and re -
searchers to address medium-scale supervised and unsupervised 
learning challenges. All algorithms we employed were implemented 
within the scikit-learn framework.
During the ML algorithms selection phase, the preprocessed dataset 
was split into an 80 % training set and a 20 % testing set. Throughout 
the model training process, we systematically fine-tuned multiple 
hyperparameters and utilized a 10-fold cross-validation technique to 
precisely evaluate model performance. All hyperparameter settings are 
included in Part II of the Supplementary information . The best ML model 
was determined based on R
2 
and RMSE values across various models.

3.1. Feature engineering and ML algorithms selection
In Part III of the Supplementary information , we describe the 
detailed process used to select 11 significant features from a pool of 57 
candidates. Based on these carefully selected 11 features, we trained an 
RF model. To gain a deeper understanding of the contribution of these 
features in the model, we analyzed their frequency of use in the splitting 
process of the decision trees within the random forest algorithm, 
resulting in a feature ranking depicted in Fig. 2 (a) based on the calcu -
lated feature importance scores. Additionally, to delve deeper into the 
interpretability of ML models, we employed the SHAP values from game 
theory as a quantitative tool to accurately assess the importance of 
various features. This assessment result is visually presented in the 
ranking shown in Fig. 2 (b).
Comparing Fig. 2 (a) with Fig. 2 (b), it is evident that although the 
feature rankings based on RF importance scores and SHAP values differ, 
the top 8 features remain the same in both rankings. We trained two ML 
models based on the RF algorithm, using the top 9 features ranked by RF 
feature importance scores and SHAP values, respectively. The results 
showed that the model trained with SHAP value-ranked features ach -
ieved a lower RMSE than the model trained with RF feature importance 
scores. Therefore, we will next adopt the feature ordering based on 
SHAP values and train the model with seven different ML algorithms to 
select the best features and ML algorithms. Furthermore, as can be seen 
from Fig. 2 , the absolute relative deviations ( ad- ) of many physical pa -
rameters are ranked higher than the weighted averages of many physical 
parameters. This indicates that the degree of deviation of physical pa -
rameters from their average values has a high correlation with hardness. 
For example, ad-YM , which represents the absolute relative deviation of 
Young ’ s modulus, has a higher correlation with hardness than VEC . This 
suggests that the spatial inhomogeneity of Young ’ s modulus in the 
material (i.e., the degree of deviation from the average value) is more 
determinant of the material ’ s hardness than the valence electron 
concentration.

3.4. The influence of physical parameters on the phase structure and 
hardness of HEAs
Hardness, as a crucial index for evaluating a material ’ s resistance to 
local plastic deformation and elastic deformation, is essential for a 
profound understanding of its wear resistance, plasticity, and elastic 
properties. Study in reference [20] points out that VEC can indirectly 
affect the hardness of HEAs by regulating their phase formation. 
Research in reference [44] , on the other hand, found that there is a 
Gaussian distribution relationship between the hardness of HEAs and 
VEC . In addition, a further study in reference [45] shows that for BCC 
structured HEAs, their hardness increases with the increase of VEC . In 
conclusion, all the above studies confirm that there is a significant 
correlation between VEC and the hardness of HEAs. However, as 
observed in Fig. 2 (b), the correlation between e/n and hardness is the 
most significant, prompting us to further investigate the relationship 
between e/n and hardness. Fig. 5 provides a detailed illustration of the 
intrinsic relationship between e/n , VEC , and hardness. It is quite 
intriguing that a distinct dividing line emerges when e/n = 0.25, as 
depicted in Fig. 5 (a). When e/n > 0.25, the material ’ s hardness gradu -
ally increases as the e/n value decreases. Conversely, when e/n < 0.25, 
the material ’ s hardness gradually diminishes with the decrease in the 
e/n value. As can be seen from Table S2 , V, Ti, Mo, Nb, Zr, W, Ta and Hf 
alloys exhibit relatively low e/n values. Upon meticulous analysis, it is 
revealed that when the total atomic percentage of V, Ti, Mo, Nb, Zr, W, 
Ta and Hf components surpasses 40 %, the e/n < 0.25. This category of 
HEAs is typically referred to as refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) 
[46] . To differentiate from RHEAs, alloys with e/n values greater than 
0.25 are subsequently designated as ordinary high-entropy alloys 
(OHEAs). As depicted in Fig. 5 (b), although the relationship between the 
VEC and hardness exhibits a similar trend to that of e/n and hardness, 
the e/n values are more concentrated, whereas the VEC values are 
relatively scattered. Most crucially, VEC fails to distinguish between 
RHEAs and OHEAs conclusively, with some falling into a mixed state. 
This could explain why VEC does not score as highly as e/n in the SHAP 
value, indicating that e/n is more pivotal than VEC in describing the 
hardness of HEAs.
The hardness of a material is closely related to its phase structure, 
and subsequently, we will elaborate on the impact of various physical 
parameters on phase structure. From the extensively studied Al-Co-Cr- 
Cu-Fe-Ni system, Guo et al. [47] found that the VEC is a crucial phys -
ical parameter that governs the stability of FCC or BCC solid solution 
phases. Specifically: when VEC ≥ 8.0, there exists solely the FCC phase; 
at 6.87 ≤ VEC < 8.0, a mixture of FCC and BCC phases will coexist; and 
when VEC < 6.87, there exists solely the BCC phase. Dong et al. [48]
utilized the Δ χ to predict the stability of topologically close-packed 
(TCP) phases in HEAs. They found that TCP phases can be stable at Δ χ 
> 0.133, except in some HEAs containing a large amount of Al. Poletti 
et al. [49] observed that when VEC > 7.5 and 1.6 < e/a < 1.8, an FCC 
structure is formed; when VEC < 7.5 and 1.8 < e/a < 2.3, an FCC 
structure is also formed; whereas, a lower e/a tends to favor the for -
mation of intermetallic. Zhang et al. [50,51] argue that HEA stable solid 
solutions are located where Ω ≥ 1.1 and δ ≤ 6.6 %, and the Δ H cannot be 
too positive or too negative. According to Guo et al. [52] , when δ 
< 0.066 and 11.6 < Δ H < 3.2 kJ/mol, the alloy tends to form solid 
solution phases; whereas when δ > 0.064 and Δ H < -12.2 kJ/mol, the 
formation of amorphous phases is more favorable. From these studies, it 
can be seen that parameters such as VEC , Δ χ , Ω , Δ H , e/a , and δ have 
significant effects on phase structure. However, these studies are not 
systematic enough. To clarify the intrinsic relationship between these 
key parameters and phase structure, we have integrated 178 solid so -
lution (SS) data, 27 intermetallic (IM) data, and 58 amorphous (AM) 
data from references [53] and [54] , and conducted an in-depth analysis 
of the relationship between factors such as e/n , VEC , Δ χ , Ω , Δ H , e/a , and 
δ , and the phase structure of HEAs, as depicted in Fig. 6 . Fig. 6 (b) 
summarizes the specific ranges for distinguishing between SS, IM, and 
AM in OHEAs and RHEAs based on the information presented in Fig. 6
(a). From Fig. 6 (b), the following can be observed: (1) The trends of e/n 
and VEC are similar to those in Fig. 8 , indicating a positive correlation 
with the hardness and δ ; (2) A larger δ is the most significant physical 
parameter for AM formation; (3) In terms of the degree of influence on 
phase structure by electron concentration, e/n is the most important, 
followed by VEC , and then e/a ; (4) For OHEAs, no single parameter can 
distinguish between SS and IM.

SS, the most critical factors are an electrochemical effect related to the 
difference in electronegativity, Δ χ , and a size factor effect related to the 
difference in the atomic radii of the elements involved, δ . The latter

However, when using VEC as a criterion to distinguish between BCC and 
FCC phase structures, a crucial issue arises: systems composed of ele -
ments from the same main group, such as VFeCoNi, NbFeCoNi, and 
TaFeCoNi, have the same VEC but exhibit significant differences in the 
stability of the formed BCC phase. As shown in Fig. 7 (c), as e/n increases, 
the stability of the BCC structure gradually decreases. Therefore, using 
e/n to distinguish phase structures has a more significant advantage. In 
fact, the dependence of crystal structure on the VEC or e/n stems from 
the differences in the characteristics of the density of states (DOS) dis -
tribution of d -orbitals across various lattice symmetries [61,62] . Spe -
cifically, when the VEC is low, the system follows the principle of 
minimum energy, preferentially occupying the low-energy states within 
the bimodal DOS to minimize the total energy. At this point, the BCC 
structure becomes the stable phase due to its characteristic of a sepa -
rated bimodal d -band. Conversely, when the VEC is high, the 
high-energy region of the unimodal d -band exhibits a more gradual DOS 
distribution, significantly reducing the energy increase associated with 
electron filling. Under these conditions, the FCC structure becomes the 
stable phase due to the continuous distribution of high-energy states 
within its unimodal d -band.
In summary, Figs. 2, 5 and 7 all clearly demonstrate that, compared 
to VEC , e/n exhibits a closer correlation with the mechanical properties 
and phase structure of alloys. Therefore, we strongly recommend 
adopting e/n as the criterion for distinguishing the formation of BCC and 
FCC structures.
3.5. A predictive platform for high-throughput HEAs screening with 
hardness and thermodynamic data
To fully exploit the value of data and construct a more accurate 
model for the composition-hardness relationship of HEAs, we employed 
the RF and XGBoost algorithms to train on the entire dataset, ultimately 
obtaining two ML models. It should be noted that, to ensure the reli -
ability of the prediction results, we have set the following criterion: if 
the difference between the hardness prediction values given by the two 
models exceeds 70 HV, the prediction result corresponding to that 
composition will be deemed inaccurate. The above two models have 
been uploaded to the platform ( https://github.com/16611808637/P 
rediction-of-hardness-of-HEAs ), along with simple executable code. 
This link contains our ML models and code for high-throughput 
screening. Materials scientists can directly obtain the corresponding 
hardness prediction results by inputting specific alloy compositions or 
generating high-throughput compositions according to their own needs, 
thereby realizing the performance customization of HEAs based on 
personalized requirements. As mentioned above, e/n , VEC , Δ χ , Ω , Δ H , e/ 
a and δ have an important influence on the phase structure. Therefore, in 
addition to outputting the predicted hardness, this platform also calcu -
lates these important physical parameters at the same time, aiming to 
help material scientists provide high-throughput screening data.
