ABSTRACT
Conventional casting Mg alloys tend to lose strength quickly when exposed to elevated temperatures 
(200 – 300 
◦
C). Here, we report a cast Mg alloy decorated by honeycomb LPSO structure via high pressure die 
casting, with an impressive strength of 191 MPa at 300 
◦
C, exceeding all traditional cast Mg based alloys and 
common Al-Si alloys. Such remarkable strength ascribes to the strong geometric confinement of the LPSO cellular 
shell to grain boundaries and dislocations, coupled with extra strain hardening derived from < a > dislocation 
interactions with stacking faults. In addition, non-basal < a > and < c > dislocations avoid macro-plastic instability 
at high strains.
1. Introduction
Magnesium (Mg) and its alloys, recognized as the lightest structural 
metals, are garnering extensive interest as next generation structural 
materials [1 – 3] . Among them, as-cast Mg alloys remain predominant in 
the automotive and aerospace sectors, favored for their high precision 
and low cost. Nevertheless, the insufficient strength of cast Mg alloys, 
especially at elevated temperatures (200 – 300 
◦
C), hinders their scaled- 
up use in internal combustion engines. For instance, the typical Mg-Al 
alloy variants, including AZ91, AM40 and AE44, demonstrate low 
strength below 130 MPa at 200 
◦
C, and the strength drops sharply to 
around 60 MPa when the temperature shifts to 300 
◦
C [4,5] . While 
certain heat-stabilized Mg-RE alloys are visible at high temperatures, 
they typically involve solid solution and aging treatments, complicating 
the manufactured process and reducing productivity.
Recently, the innovative cellular structure that produces outstanding 
mechanical properties has become popular in Al and Ti alloys, which 
allows the fatigue durability of the AlSi10Mg alloy to be beyond all other 
Al alloys [6] . The thermally stable long period stacking ordered (LPSO) 
with distinctive structure is advantageous for bolstering strength and 
modifying resistance to fracture [7,8] . However, how to break the 
inherent weaknesses that mechanical strength obviously deteriorates in 
conventional cast Mg alloys at elevated temperatures by integrating 
cellular structure and LPSO remains to overcome. In this context, we 
have fabricated a cast Mg (Mg-6Y-3Zn-1Al in wt. %, WZ631) alloy that 
features fine α -Mg grains enveloped by LPSO honeycomb shell through 
the rapid cooling rate and large temperature gradient of high pressure 
die casting (HPDC). Trace Al was added to optimize the die casting 
ability and to form Al
2
Y nucleation sites for grain refinement [9] . The 
received WZA631 alloy shows the best strength in all as-cast Mg alloys at 
300 
◦
C, even outperforming commercial cast Al-Si alloy.

3. Results and discussion
Fig. 1 c gives the engineering stress – strain curve of the WZA631 alloy 
at 300 
◦
C. The studied alloy exhibits a yield strength of 125 ± 4 MPa, an 
ultimate tensile strength of 191 ± 10 MPa and a continuous strain to 
fracture of 26.3 % ± 3.0 %. The strain hardening exponent n is calcu -
lated as 0.18 from the strain-hardening curve (inset of Fig. 1 c), higher 
than some as-cast or extruded Mg-Y-Zn alloys ( n = 0.1 – 0.15) [8,11] . 
This suggests a good work hardening capacity. Fig. 1 d shows the 
comparative analysis of the tensile strength and elongation (EL) for the 
WZA631 alloy and other prevalent cast Mg-based alloys at 300 
◦
C, 
including the gravity casting (GC) LPSO-Mg alloy, high strength GC Mg- 
RE-Zn alloys and HPDC Mg alloys. We find that the current alloy pre -
sents greater strength-ductility synergy with respect to GC Mg alloys and 
the best strength among all HPDC Mg alloys. In particular, the alloy 
outperforms common cast Al-Si alloys in strength.
The microstructural evolution of tensile specimens with strain levels 
of 5 % to 26.3 % is inspected to uncover deformation physics behind 
exceptional thermal resistance in the present WZA631 alloy ( Fig. S1 ). 
There are almost no apparent changes to the grain-level features, 
including grain size and distribution. Even though most grains are 
elongated along the tensile direction at 26.3 % strain, the grain size 
presents a slight increment from 5.1 μ m to 7.1 μ m. Concurrently, the 
LPSO phase maintains the nearly same cellular shell configuration as the 
initial state at 5 % to 26.3 % strains ( Fig. S1 ). These appearances suggest 
that the honeycomb LPSO is stable and effectively resists Mg grain 
deformation at elevated temperatures.
Fig. 2 shows bright-field TEM visuals of α -Mg regions in the WZA631 
alloy tension strained to various levels. At 5 % strain, profuse < a >
dislocations are found in the reflection g = 0 110 while invisible in the 
reflection g = 0002, marked by yellow arrows ( Fig. 2 a and b) [12] . 
However, some < a > dislocations appear curved with partial segments 
extending outside the basal trace or exhibit straight lines perpendicular 
to the basal trace, the typical expression of non-basal (prismatic or py -
ramidal) < a > dislocations. Besides, straight and parallel streaks are 
examined in the dislocation contrast ( Fig. 2 c), where the streaks feature 
a 1 / 6 < 20 23 > Frank partial dislocation from the Burgers circuit of 
Fig. 2 f, indicative of I
1 
intrinsic stacking faults (SFs) [3] . Interestingly, 
< a > dislocations are bound by adjacent SFs ( Fig. 2 a – e), meaning a drag 
effect on their movement. Reaching 26.3 % strain, dislocations having a 
< c > component are visualized under g = 0002 (orange arrows, Fig. 2 d 
and e), which potentially arises from the decomposition of the < c + a >
dislocations. This process is driven by a decrease in total dislocation 
energy, with new < c > dislocations having lower energy relative to slide- 
prone < c + a > dislocations [13] . Note that the area fraction of activated 
{ 10 12 } twins is only 1 % for various strain levels (EBSD maps of Fig. S2 ), 
thus the strain effect of the twins is negligible in the current scenario.
Based on above results, the honeycomb shell crafted by the LPSO 
phase may be presumed to be the formidable obstacle against grain 
coarsening, inducing a minimal variation in the grain size from 5.1 μ m to 
7.1 μ m ( Fig. S1 ). It appears that dislocation lines and dislocation stacks 
are located around LPSO (white and yellow arrows in Fig. 3 a and c), 
creating traffic congestion that fosters dislocation rapid multiplication 
and enables strain hardening. Meanwhile, the restricted space inside 
LPSO cells curtails the generation of failure dislocation patterns, and 
therefore offers a continuous resistance to crack propagation [6] . There 
are no debonding or nano voids present at the interfaces between the 
LPSO and Mg matrix across different positions in Fig. 3 a and c ( Fig. 3 b

On the other hand, the germination of < c > dislocations in the

( < 2.5, Fig. 3 e – h) and enhanced thermal formability. Therefore, the 
stable flow behavior is advanced at moderate hardening rates for the 
WZA631 alloy ( Fig. 1 c).
4. Conclusions
In this study, we have developed a heat-resistant cast Mg alloy 
endowed with the LPSO cellular network based on the large temperature 
gradient of die casting. Leveraging structural benefits of LPSO honey -
comb shell at the micron/nano-scale, the grain breakage and dislocation 
movement are evidently suppressed, which facilitates the continuation 
of strain-hardening post-yield. SFs provide additional strain-hardening 
by interacting with < a > dislocations. Consequently, the alloy achieves 
an extraordinary strength of 191 MPa at 300 
◦
C that surpass conven -
tional cast Mg and Al-Si alloys. These findings not only propel the 
practical use of Mg alloys in components that operate at elevated tem -
peratures, but also shed light on designing stable metallic alloys with 
superior performance.
