The Effects of Grain Size and Carbon Content on the
Ductility of Recrystallized Molybdenum Wire*
By Noriyoshi Aritomi** and Kazuo Tsuya**
To clarify the effects of grain size and carbon content on the ductility of molybdenum, the vvvvvvariations in ductility with
grain size were investigated on the three grades of 1mmφ wire specimens by means of tensile tests at 25゜and -76℃. The
results are as follows: (1) Low carbon specimens (C≦10ppm) were extremely low in ductility due to an intergranular brittleness
when the average grain diameter in the transverse cross section of the specimen (dT) was larger than about 0.04mm, though the
specimen showed a large ductility when dT was smaller than 0.04mm. (2) At -76℃, the ductility of medium carbon speci-
mans (C=80ppm) increased with increasing dT when dT was larger than about 0.08mm. (3) In high carbon specimens (C=210
ppm) the ductility increased nearly linearly with increasing dT-1/2. (4) When dT was larger than about 0.04mm, both the
medium and high carbon specimens were more ductile than the low carbon specimen. The most ductile was the medium carbon
specimen which was free from the intergranular brittleness and also from coarse carbides. (5) When dT was smaller than 0.04
mm, the ductility. of the low carbon specimen was as large as, or slightly larger than, that of the high carbon specimen.
However, it could not be concluded whether carbon increases or lowers the ductility in an equiaxed fine grained molybdenum
because of a uniaxed recrystallized structure of the low carbon specimen.

I. Introduction
It has been believed that the ductility of polycry-
stalline molybdenum is impaired by small amounts of
impurities such as oxygen, nitrogen and carbon(1)(2).
Oxygen is the most detrimental because it promotes
intergranular fracture(1)(3). Lawley et al.(4) have
shown that when oxygen and nitrogen were throughly
eliminated, the specimen containing a limited amount of
carbon shows a large ductility even at very low tem-
peratures. The respective effects of these impurities,
however, are not simply additive. Nitrogen(1)(5) or
carbon(6)(7) tends to offset the harmful effect of
oxygen.
The present authors have shown elsewhere the
following results on electron-beam melted coarse
grained specimens. When the specimen contains a
considerable amount of carbon, the ductility lowers with
increasing oxygen content from less than 10ppm to 20
ppm(8). When the carbon content is small, the speci-
men shows a marked intergranular brittleness even
when the oxygen content is less than 10ppm(9). The
intergranular brittleness can be suppressed by 50～300
ppm carbon existing in the specimen(9), and it has been
considered that the amount and/or the distribution of
carbon in the specimen are the main factors controlling
the ductility, at least when the oxygen content is
small(10).
On the other hand, in fine grained molybdenum, the
effect of carbon is not clear. The specimen containing
a smaller amount of carbon generally contains a larger
amount of oxygen, and vice versa(11)(12). The increase
in ductility of sintered molybdenum by the addition of
carbon has been considered to be due to the decrease in
oxygen content(13)(14) or to the change in grain size or
in density(15). It has also been reported that carbon
reduces the impact ductility(16), though the oxygen
content in the specimen used is unknown. Moreover,
the grain -size dependence of ductility has not been
clarified in the specimen containing almost no carbon,
although there are some studies on the specimen
containing a considerable amount of carbon(17)(18).
Therefore, it can be said that it remains uncertain
whether the ductility of fine grained molybdenum is
improved by carbon as in the case of coarse grained
material.
The present study has been carried out so as to clarify
the effects of grain size and carbon content on the
ductility of molybdenum. On three grades of 1mmφ

The temperature of the specimen was raised to a fixed
temperature in 2-3 min. After annealing the speci-
men was cooled to 600℃ in 10min, followed by rapid
cooling to room temperature with a high purity argon.
The annealed specimens were electropolished once again
and then used for tensile tests.
The analyses were made on the wires which were
annealed by the same way as in the preparation of the
tensile specimens, followed by electropolishing and
washing with water and alcohol and drying. Specimen
B was also analysed on the wire in the wrought state.

annealed wire. Table 1 shows that specimen A is
higher in oxygen content and lower in carbon content
than specimen B. However, in view of the limited
accuracy of analysis, it may be said that the oxygen
content is of the same order and the carbon content is

and nitrogen contents in specimen C are thought to be
of the same order as those of specimen A, though no
analyses were made. Thus, these specimens can essen-

Microphotographs were taken from the longitudinal

III. Experimental Results and
Discussion
1. Microstructure and grain size
Both wrought and recrystallized structures are shown
in Photo. 1 for specimens A, B and D, respectively.
The recrystallized grain size was finer in specimen B
than in specimen A. This difference was remarkable
when the annealing temperature was below 1500℃.
Both the specimens, particularly specimen B, tended to
show an exaggerated grain growth at about 14000～
1500℃, and some of the specimens showed an extremely
mixed grained structure. These extremely mixed
grained specimens, when they had many fine grains,
generally showed a large room-temperature ductility, as
reported earlier(10). Also, at -76℃ these specimens
were larger in fracture stress and in ductility than the
specimens having nearly uniform grains of the same
order in average grain size. A similar result has
already been reported on tungsten wire(19). In the
present experiment, however, the tensile test results ofthe specimens, which were judged to be extremely
mixed grained from observations of the fracture surfaces
and the cross sections after testing, were all rejected.
In specimen D the extremely mixed grained structure
was not observed. The wrought structure of this
specimen was the finest. The recrystallized grain size
was always smaller than those of spocimens A and B
under the same annealing condition. The size of
carbides in matrices of specimen D was 1～2μ in
average diameter (maximum, 2×7μ granular) and
showed no remarkable change with annealing tempera-
ture. The number of carbides in matrices tended to
increase and the carbides at grain boundaries grew in
size with decreasing grain boundaries (Photo. 2 (a)).
Specimen C contained a small amount of carbides as
shown in Photo. 2 (b). In both specimens A and B,
carbides were not observed with an optical microscope
even in a coarse grained state.

At -76℃, the ductile-brittle transition is observed
at dT-1/2≒4.5mm-1/2. The amount of intergranular
facets decreases and the cleavage facets increase with
increasing dT-1/2 The fundamental mechanism of frac-
ture can be considered as the stress concentration by the
pile-up of dislocations regardless of whether the

change of dT, therefore, may be explained qualitatively
by Cottrell's theory(23), which predicts that the fracture
changes from ductile to brittle cleavage with increasing
grain size under a proper testing condition.

specimen is nearly the same as that of the single crystal.

tility and the difference between the yield and fracture

(3) high carbon specimen
The ductility of high carbon specimens increases
nearly linearly with increasing dT1/2 at 25゜and -76
℃. In this specimen, the ductility must be affected
also by the changes in size and distribution of carbides;
i.e., coarse carbides decrease the ductility as shown in

5. The effect of carbon content on the
ductility
In the range of dT-1/2<5mm-1/2 (dT>0.04mm),
carbon suppresses the intergranular brittleness and in-
creases the ductility. Although Photo. 4 does not
negate the fact that carbon suppresses the extreme
intergranular brittleness in the low carbon specimen, it
shows that coarse carbides act as fracture initiation
sites. An excess amount of carbon yields coarse carbides
and promotes cleavage fracture, thus decreasing the
ductility(9)(10). The maximum ductility can be obtained
when the amount of carbon is barely enough to suppress
intergranular brittleness. This condition may be
accomplished in the medium carbon specimen. As
shown in Fig. 5(b), one of specimens C of dT-1/2≒2.5
mm-1/2 showed 90% of the area of intergranular facets.
The ductility and the fracture stress of this specimen,
however, were as large as those of other specimens C.
From this fact it cannot be said that the specimen
showing a large amount of intergranular facets is always
low in ductility. But the amount of intergranular
facets is large in the low carbon specimen of low
ductility, and is small, in general, in the medium and
high carbon specimens of high ductility: this shows
that the low ductility in the low carbon specimen is
attributable to the low cohesion of grain boundaries,
and that carbon increases the grain boundary cohesion.
In fine grained specimens of dT-1/2>5mm-1/2, the
ductility of the low carbon specimen was as large as, or
slightly larger than, that of the high carbon specimen
as shown in Fig. 3 (c) and (d). However, the effect of
carbon, per se, could not be determined because the low
carbon specimen had a uniaxed recrystallized structure.
