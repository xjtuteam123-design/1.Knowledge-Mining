1. Introduction
Since their introduction in 2010, refractory complex concentrated 
alloys (RCCAs), including high-entropy alloys (HEAs), have garnered 
significant interest as candidates for high-temperature structural appli -
cations [1,2] . Composed primarily of refractory elements, these alloys 
frequently form single-phase body-centred cubic (bcc) structures. 
Single-phase bcc RCCAs based on Group V and VI elements exhibit 
outstanding high-temperature strength but limited cold workability 
[2,3] . Conversely, RCCAs dominated by Group IV elements demonstrate 
excellent tensile ductility down to cryogenic temperatures, albeit with 
mediocre creep resistance [4 – 9] .
Precipitation strengthening offers a viable pathway for improving 
the mechanical performance of RCCAs. Inspired by commercial Ni-based 
superalloys, researchers have sought alloying elements that can 
generate a microstructure consisting of a soft disordered bcc matrix 
reinforced by coherent intermetallic precipitates. Among the possible 
candidates, the B2 phase is particularly attractive due to its crystallo -
graphic compatibility with the bcc lattice, which enables a high degree 
of coherency at the matrix-precipitate interface [10,11] . However, 
achieving a stable bcc + B2 microstructure in RCCAs remains 
challenging.
RCCAs that exhibit such microstructures commonly contain Al. 
Although Al additions promote the B2 phase formation, this phase is 
often metastable. Particularly, in the Al-Mo-Nb-Ta-V-Zr alloy system, 
the Al-based B2 phase dissolves or transforms into undesirable inter -
metallic compounds at temperatures above 700 
◦
C [12 – 20] . Improved 
thermal stability has been reported for the Al-Cr-Nb-Mo-Ta alloy system, 
in which the Al-based B2 phase remains stable up to 1060 
◦
C, providing 
distinct strengthening without decomposing into other intermetallics 
[21 – 24] . Nevertheless, this temperature remains lower than the inten -
ded operating range of RCCAs, necessitating the exploration of alter -
native elements capable of forming B2 phases with higher solvus 
temperatures.
One promising candidate is Ru, which forms B2 phases with both Al 
and Group IV elements [25 – 31] . Depending on the secondary elements, 
such Ru-based B2 phases demonstrate solvus temperatures ranging from 
1300 
◦
C to as high as 1900 
◦
C, combined with low lattice misfits. Despite 
the outstanding thermal stability and controllable precipitation behav -
iour of certain Ru-based B2 phases, the high cost of Ru imposes

Fortunately, more cost-effective alternatives exist that can create B2 
phases with competitive thermal stability. These B2 phases typically 
form between the early (Ti, Zr, Hf) and late (Fe, Co, Ni) 3d transition 
metals. Specifically, a FeTi-based B2 phase was recently discovered in 
the W-Fe-Ti alloy system, where this compound nucleated after 
annealing at 1250

interfaces, providing improved strength at 1000

B2 phases consisting of Co and Hf/Zr/Ti in the Nb-Mo-Co-Ti-Hf-Zr alloy 
system, which appeared both in the as-cast state and after heat treat -
ment. In the as-cast state, these B2 phases create a composite-like 
structure with the bcc phase, enhancing both strength and plasticity, 
while providing exceptional strain hardening at ambient-to-high tem -
peratures [34,36,38] . After heat treatments at T = 1000 – 1200 
◦
C, the B2 
phases additionally precipitated within the bcc phase as nano-sized 
semi-coherent particles, affecting the mechanical properties in a com -
plex manner. Specifically, the CoHf-based B2 phase led to strength 
improvement [37] , whereas the CoTi-based B2 phase doubled room- 
temperature ductility without compromising compressive strength [35] .
Nevertheless, the effects of adding late 3d transition metals on phase 
equilibria, especially concerning the B2 phase formation, and mechan -
ical properties of RCCAs remain insufficiently understood. Depending 
on the specific alloy system, these elements can cause either strength -
ening [39,40] or softening [41] . This diversity can be attributed to 
competing tendencies for solid solution formation, intermetallic phase 
precipitation, and elemental partitioning [41] . For instance, in the Ti- 
Nb-Cr-V-Ni-Al alloy system, gradual substitution of Ti and Nb with Al 
and Ni led to the unexpected formation of a (Ti, Ni)-rich σ phase and

microstructural evolution increased room-temperature strength, 
reduced plasticity, and caused softening at 800

exclusion of Al from the same alloy system enabled the formation of the 
desired B2 phase, thereby providing measurable room-temperature 
tensile ductility coupled with high-temperature strength comparable 
to that of Inconel 718 [44] .

calculations predict the appearance of the B2 phase at lower tempera -

driving force indicates that the B2 phase is more stable than the μ phase 
at reduced temperatures and may therefore consume it upon further 
cooling. Given the overall reliability of CALPHAD in predicting phase 
constitution and relative phase stability, these results support the use of 
CALPHAD-guided compositional optimisation to alter the secondary 
phase formation sequence from μ to B2.
Preliminary experimental results already corroborate this strategy. 
Specifically, the removal of V, identified here as an additional μ -phase- 
stabilising element, or deliberate adjustment of the Ti-to-late-3d- 
transition-metal ratio markedly reduces the μ -phase fraction in favour 
of the B2 phase. These observations highlight the strong sensitivity of 
phase stability to elemental partitioning in these RCCAs and demon -
strate the potential of composition-structure tailoring to suppress un -
desirable intermetallic phases. Detailed results of these optimisation 
efforts will be reported in future studies.
4.2. Mechanical properties
The strength of the alloys studied revealed a complex dependence on 
the late 3d transition metal (Co, Fe, Ni), the secondary alloying element 
(Ti or Al), and the testing temperature. To directly evaluate the effect of 
these compositional variations, a data-driven machine learning (ML) 
model was employed to predict yield strength.
The ML reasonably predicted both strength values and strength 
ranking depending on the alloy composition ( Fig. 11 ). At room tem -
perature ( Fig. 11 a), the ML captured a higher strength of the Al- 
containing alloys compared to their Ti-containing counterparts, 
though with differing accuracy. For the Al-containing alloys, the 
maximum discrepancy between the predicted and experimental yield 
strength values was below 7.5%, while it achieved 20 – 25% for the Ti- 
containing counterparts. The improved strength of the Al-containing 
alloys likely stems from the significant influence of Al on electronic 
structure and interatomic bonding, which increases the intrinsic lattice 
friction [62] . The ML also reflected the strength changes governed by 
the late 3d transition metal, with Ni or Co providing higher room- 
temperature yield strength than Fe, which could be attributed to their 
higher μ -phase volume fractions.
A shift in the strength hierarchy occurred at 1000 
◦
C, with the 
experimental ranking changing to Fe > Co > Ni for both Ti- and Al- 
containing alloys ( Fig. 11 b). The ML model successfully predicted this 
altered ranking, while underestimating the strength. The magnitude of 
the discrepancy was comparable to that observed at room temperature 
for the Ti-containing alloys and somewhat higher for the Al-containing 
alloys. The observed transition suggested that the strengthening 
advantage associated with a high μ -phase fraction decreased at elevated 
temperature, and that the dominant contribution to strength arose from 
the bcc matrix itself. In this regime, the strength trend followed the 
solubility of the late 3d transition metal in the bcc matrix. The signifi -
cantly higher solubility of Fe compared to Co and Ni corresponded to its 
superior elevated-temperature strength, confirming the role of Fe in 
enhancing the resistance of Nb-rich RCCAs to thermally activated 
deformation processes [39] .
