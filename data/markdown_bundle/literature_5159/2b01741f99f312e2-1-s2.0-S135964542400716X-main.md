ABSTRACT
Developing high-performance metallic materials with high yield strength and excellent ductility is important for 
various applications, such as automobiles, power plants, and aerospace industries. However, conventional alloys 
typically exhibit a trade-off between strength and ductility, making it difficult to develop materials that are both 
strong and ductile. In this study, we report that a cast Co-Cr-Fe-Ni-Al alloy can achieve a high room temperature 
yield strength of up to 500 MPa, which is twice that of conventional high-entropy alloys (HEAs) with face- 
centered cubic structures, with a tensile strain of 29 %. After thermomechanical treatment, the alloy exhibits 
even better synergy of strength and ductility, with a yield strength of 900 MPa and 30 % elongation. These 
exceptional mechanical properties are conferred by a multiscale hierarchical heterostructure, which was intro -
duced through careful control of the alloy composition using a negative enthalpy alloy design strategy. The 
heterostructure ranges from the micrometer to sub-micrometer and nanometer scales. This multiscale hierar -
chical structure acts as a continuous impediment to dislocation motion, greatly increasing strength, and facili -
tating hetero-deformation induced hardening via strain partitioning, resulting in sustained ultrahigh strain 
hardening. Importantly, multiscale hierarchical heterostructures facilitate coordinated plastic deformation and 
multiple plastic deformation mechanisms, and stress concentration relieving, and play important roles in 
improving ductility. This work reveals the effect of different types (and scales) of heterogeneities on the 
deformation mechanism of HEAs and opens new perspectives for constructing heterostructures, which serve as a 
new design approach for high strength and excellent ductility by using a negative enthalpy alloy design strategy.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are a newly developed class of metallic 
materials composed of five or more principal elements in equiatomic or 
near-equiatomic concentrations, allowing for a wide range of composi -
tional regulation [ 1-4 ]. HEAs are being extensively investigated due to 
their excellent mechanical and physical properties, including high 
strength and hardness [ 5 ], excellent strength and ductility at cryogenic 
temperatures and high strain rates [ 6 , 7 ], superior radiation resistance 
[ 8 ], exceptional oxidation resistance [ 9 ], high fatigue and creep resis -
tance [ 10 ], and attractive superconductivity [ 11-13 ]. HEAs typically 
exhibit face-centered cubic (FCC), body-centered cubic (BCC) and hex -
agonal close-packed (HCP) crystallographic structures due to their high 
configurational entropy stemming from their multicomponent nature 
[ 13 , 14 ]. Most FCC HEAs, in particular CrCoNi-based HEAs with low 
stacking fault energies, exhibit excellent strain hardening rates

Heterogeneous structure strengthening has emerged as one of the 
most effective strengthening mechanisms for HEAs due to the high 
hetero deformation-induced (HDI) hardening, which is much greater 
than that for alloys with a homogeneous microstructure [ 19 ]. As a result, 
HDI hardening markedly increases yield strength [ 20 ]. A prototypical 
heterogeneous microstructure is characterized by a change in grain size 
[ 21 ], which includes gradient grains, laminated grains, and core-shell 
(bimodal) grains in single-phase structure HEAs. Chemical heterogene -
ity is a distinct type of heterogeneity that can be achieved by tailoring 
the composition of HEAs and manipulating the chemical affinity be -
tween atoms. This can induce various types of chemical heterogeneities, 
such as local chemical fluctuations (LCFs) [ 22 , 23 ], local chemical or -
derings (LCOs) [ 24 , 25 ], spinodal decomposition [ 26 ], and multiphase 
structures [ 27 ]. Recently, a negative enthalpy alloy design strategy was 
reported to lead to chemical affinity bias, thereby sparking LCOs, LCFs 
and nanoprecipitates in a solid solution system [ 28 ]. At the nanoscale, 
the presence of LCFs and LCOs hinders dislocation motion, enhances 
dislocation interactions, and enhances the strain hardening capability 
[ 29 , 30 ]. The advantage of achieving these chemical heterogeneities is 
that they can be obtained in both cast and thermomechanical treated 
alloys [ 23 ]. However, due to the weak interaction between dislocations 
and heterogeneities, the strengthening effect seems to be less effective 
than that of long-range ordered precipitates. Moreover, the interactions 
between dislocations and LCFs and LCOs on a short-range scale do not 
fully exploit the effect of HDI hardening.
In the field of materials design, the deliberate engineering of chem -
ical heterogeneities at various scales offer a promising approach to 
modulate dislocation behavior, optimizing the synergy between 
strength and ductility. It has been widely assumed that hierarchical 
heterogeneous structures spanning multiple scales in HEAs can play a 
crucial role in enhancing their mechanical properties [ 23 , 31 , 32 ]. These 
hierarchical structures offer a multitude of benefits, including facili -
tating extensive dislocation interactions and accumulation, which in 
turn promotes high ductility and delivers higher strength by hindering 
dislocation motion throughout the material [ 33 ]. Moreover, this 
distinctive hierarchical heterogeneous structure produces back stress at 
various scales, effectively leveraging the HDI hardening effect [ 32 ]. This 
results in the uniform distribution of local plastic strain and delays the 
onset of necking, ultimately leading to superior strength – ductility per -
formance. However, it is important to note that the formation of such 
hierarchical heterogeneous structures is not readily achieved in HEAs. 
This is because the high entropy effect tends to favor a random homo -
geneous structure, while the grains tend to have similar sizes with 
comparable driving forces for growth during the casting process [ 14 ].

4.2. Mechanisms giving rise to strength – ductility synergy
The strength of a heterostructured alloy is closely tied to the resulting 
HDI hardening, while ductility is related to the coordinated plastic 
deformation between heterostructures [ 20 ]. In particular, plastic 
deformation starts in soft areas, such as large grains with a soft FCC 
phase, which generates back stress in soft areas and forward stress in 
hard areas. This hetero-deformation strengthens the soft areas by 
increasing the stress required to drive dislocation slip. It also exerts 
forward stress on hard areas and reduces the critical stress for disloca -
tion activities in hard areas. However, when hard areas have a signifi -
cant strength mismatch with soft areas, plastic deformation in the hard 
area is limited, leading to a large strain mismatch between the two types 
of areas and reducing ductility through the introduction of microcracks 
around the interface between these two types of areas [ 78 ]. With this in 
mind, we can reasonably expect that exploiting HDI hardening while 
maintaining coordinated plastic deformation between hard and soft 
areas holds promise to achieve alloys with superior combination of 
strength and ductility.
In this alloy, the BCC/B2 phases serve as the hard phases, which 
becomes even harder due to nanoscale heterogeneities. This results in a 
hardness difference of 3.8 GPa between the BCC and FCC phases in the 
as-cast alloy (Fig. S10). Accordingly, a high HDI stress of up to 311 MPa 
was reached in the as-cast alloy at 2 % strain, as estimated by loading ‒ 
unloading ‒ reloading tests (Fig. S11). The HDI stress is even higher than 
the yield strength of the CCFNA
6 
alloy and most as-cast HEAs with 
coarse FCC grains ( Fig. 1 c) [ 17 , 21 , 38 , 40 ]. The superior combination of 
high strength and excellent ductility makes the CCFNAl
11 
alloy ready for 
use in its as-cast state.
Compared to the as-cast alloy, the annealed alloy exhibited a reduced 
size of the soft coarse-FCC grains, along with the formation of B2 
nanoparticles and annealing twins. This slightly increases the hardness 
of the soft area to 5.9 GPa (Fig. S10). Conversely, FCC grains formed in 
the hard BCC grains, together with a reduction in the proportion of B2 
nanoparticles, leading to a reduction in the hardness of the BCC areas 
(Fig. S10). The synergistic development of the microstructure in com -
bination with the intervening fine-FCC grains (medium hard) results in 
reduced strength mismatch and the formation of a gradient hardness. 
During deformation, a gradient strain is introduced, leading to a higher 
HDI stress (approximately two times greater) than that of the as-cast 
alloy, which was the main contributor to the increased strength 
(Fig. S11). Moreover, the gradient hardness can also facilitate cooper -

5. Conclusions
Our work presents a microstructure design approach to achieve 
distinctive high yield strength and excellent ductility by constructing 
multiscale hierarchical heterostructures at various length scales, ranging 
from the micrometer scale to the nanometer scale, utilizing the negative 
enthalpy alloy design strategy. The multiscale hierarchical hetero -
structures not only trigger hetero-deformation induced hardening but 
also act as effective dislocation barriers, leading to dislocation pinning 
and pile-up from nanometers to micrometers, thereby resulting in 
strengthening (The yield strength increases from 500 MPa for as-cast 
samples to 900 MPa for the annealed samples, respectively). On the 
other hand, the multiscale hierarchical heterostructures facilitate 
cooperative plastic deformation between the soft and hard phases, 
leading to high strain hardening and ductility (strength-elongation 
products of 29.0 and 41.4 GPa% for the as-cast and annealed alloys, 
respectively). Importantly, we demonstrate that a multiscale hierarchi -
cal heterogeneous structure can be directly obtained in as-cast alloys 
and further optimized via simple thermomechanical treatments. Hence, 
our work helps widening the microstructure design, and multiscale hi -
erarchical heterostructures can be constructed for other multicompo -
nent alloys, such as medium- and high-entropy alloys, steels and 
superalloys, by tailoring the negative enthalpy of the alloy.
