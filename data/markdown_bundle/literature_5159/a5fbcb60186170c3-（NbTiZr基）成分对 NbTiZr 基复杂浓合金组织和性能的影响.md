abstract
Phase composition, microstructure and mechanical properties of six complex concentrated alloys,
NbTiZr, NbTiZrV, NbTiZrVMo, NbTiZrVTa, NbTiZrVCr, and NbTiZrVAl 0.24, are reported. The alloy density
was in the range from 6.34 g/cm 3 to 8.43 g/cm 3. After homogenization annealing at 1400 /C14 C or 1200 /C14 C,
only the ternary NbTiZr alloy had a single-phase BCC crystal structure. NbTiZrV and NbTiZrVTa produced
predominantly single-phase BCC structure with clusters of ﬁne V-rich precipitates inside NbTiZrV grains
and ﬁne, Zr-rich grain boundary precipitates in NbTiZrVTa. The other three alloys contained BCC and
Laves phases, and NbTiZrVMo also contained a second BCC phase. After compression deformation at
1 000
/C14 C, noticeable changes in the phase composition were found in NbTiZrV, NbTiZrVTa and
NbTiZrVAl0.24, while the phase composition of other alloys retained unchanged. NbTiZr, NbTiZrV and
NbTiZrVTa showed good ductility and noticeable work hardening rate at room temperature, while the
ductility of other alloys rapidly decreased with increasing the amount of Laves phase. The RT yield stress
varied from 975 MPa for NbTiZ to 1510 MPa for NbTiZrVMo. At 1000
/C14 C, all the alloys showed excellent
compression ductility; however only NbTiZrVCr and NbTiZrVMo were stronger than NbTiZr. The results
indicate that increasing the composition complexity of refractory near-equiatomic alloys by increasing
the number of the alloying elements does not necessary result in a simpler phase structure and/or better
mechanical properties. The type of the alloying elements seems to be more important than their number.
Additions of V, Al or Ta decrease while additions of Cr or Mo increase the 1000
/C14 C strength of NbTiZr.

1. Introduction
Refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) have recently attracted
much attention due to their promising elevated temperature me-
chanical properties, reduced density and improved oxidation
resistance relative to conventional refractory alloys [ 1e3]. For
example, densities of many reported RHEAs are between the den-
sities of Ti alloys and Fe- or Ni alloys, while the melting tempera-
tures (and thus potential maximum operation temperatures) are
much higher [ 2]. The reduced densities are mainly achieved by
using lower density refractory elements, such as Cr, V, Nb and Zr
[4,5], and/or additional alloying with Al and Ti [ 6e10]. The analysis
of the current results published in open literature indicates that,
with few exceptions, the refractory alloys with reduced densities
generally show remarkable drop in their strength at temperatures
above ~800
/C14 C. Indeed, the RHEAs, which were used as proof of
concept of this approach, MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW [ 11,12], pro-
duce a rather weak yield strength decrease with an increase in
temperature from 600 /C14 C to 1600 /C14 C resulting in the yield strength
values exceeding 400 MPa at 1600 /C14 C[ 12]. Subsequent composi-
tional modi ﬁcations of these two alloys designed to decease the
alloy density have led to stronger (more negative) temperature
dependence of yield strength above ~800
/C14 C, although at lower
temperatures considerably higher strength values have been ach-
ieved [5,7e10,13]. An additional issue with using Al, Cr, V and Zr as
main alloying elements is formation of brittle intermetallic phases
(B2, C14 and/or C15) that can increase brittle-to-ductile transition
temperature of BCC-based RHEAs above the room temperature. It is
still not clear if increasing the number of alloying elements is
beneﬁcial for mechanical properties of RHEAs.
There has been extensive metallurgical research for RHEAs
within the groups IV, V and VI transition elements. Detailed anal-
ysis of 151 RHEA compositions reported in open literature in the
period from 2010 to the end of January 2018 was conducted in a
recent review of RHEA publications [ 14]. This analysis showed that

2. Experimental procedures
Alloys were prepared from high purity (/C21 99.9%) elements by
vacuum arc melting in a water-cooled Cu hearth. A low pressure
high-purity argon atmosphere additionally puriﬁed with a Ti getter
was used to produce the arc. To ensure complete dissolution and
mixing of the alloying elements in liquid state, each sample was
ﬂipped-over and re-melted at least ﬁve times. The weight of each
sample was 200 g and the sample dimensions were approximately
10 0 mm/C2 30 mm x 12 mm, with rounded surfaces. The prepared
alloys were solidiﬁed inside the water-cooled Cu hearth where they
were melted. The solidi ﬁed alloys, except one containing Cr, were
hot isostatic pressed (HIP'd) by holding at 1400
/C14 C for 2 h under a
pressure of 276 MPa of high-purity argon to close solidi ﬁcation
porosity and then additionally annealed in high-purity argon at
140 0
/C14 C for 6 h. The Cr-containing alloy was HIP'd and annealed at
1200 /C14 C, and the annealing time was 24 h. During heat treatment,
all the samples were wrapped (each individually) in a Ta foil. The
heating and cooling rates were ~20 /C14 C/min. The compositions of the
alloys measured using the energy dispersive spectroscopy (SEM-
EDS) are shown Table 1.
Microstructure was studied using conventional SEM techniques,
such as secondary electron (SE) and backscatter electron (BSE)
imaging, electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) and energy
dispersive spectroscopy (EDS). Grain size and volume fraction of
different phases were measured using orientation image micro-
scopy (OIM) software and/or Fovea Pro (Reindeer Graphics) soft-
ware. X-ray diffraction was conducted using Cu K
a1 radiation in the
2q range of 20e120/C14 . Microhardness was measured using a Vickers
microhardness tester by applying the load of 1000 g for 15 s. The
reported microhardness values are the average values taken from at
least 10 measurements for each sample/condition. Density of the
alloys was measured using a helium pycnometer. Compression
tests were conducted at a constant ram speed of 0.008 mm/s using
a hydraulic testing machine. Rectangular compression specimens
were extracted from the middle sections of the HIP'd and annealed
ingots. The longitudinal direction of the specimens was parallel to
the longitudinal direction of the ingots. The specimens were grin-
ded and polished to the ﬁnal dimensions of 5 mm /C2 5m m x 8m m
using sand paper down to 1200 grits. Room temperature tests were
conducted in air, while high-temperature tests were conducted in
vacuum (~2 10
/C0 3 Pa). Image correlation software was used to
measure strain and a load cell was used to measure stress during
testing.
3. Results
3.1. X-ray diffraction
X-ray diffraction patterns of the annealed alloys are shown in
Fig. 1. A BCC phase was identi ﬁed as a base crystal structure for all
six alloys. Additionally were registered a cubic (C15) Laves phase in
NbTiZrVMo and NbTiZrVCr, a hexagonal (C14) Laves phase in
NbTiZrVAl
0.24 and a secondary BCC2 phase in NbTiZrVMo. The
lattice parameters of the respective phases were calculated from
these diffraction patterns and are given in Table 2.
3.2. Microstructure of annealed alloys
After homogenization annealing, the alloying elements were
homogeneously distributed in NbTiZr providing even contrast
across the grains, both in secondary and backscatter imaging
modes, and individual grains were hardly recognized. To identify
grains and grain boundaries in this alloy EBSD inverse pole ﬁgure
maps were taken, which revealed an equiaxed grain structure, with
the average grain diameter of ~2 mm ( Fig. 2 ).
NbTiZrV also had an equiaxed BCC grain structure, with the
average grain size of ~0.7 mm ( Fig. 3). Bright spherical spots inside
grains were observed on the BSE images, indicating micro-
segregation of high atomic number elements. High magni ﬁcation
images from these bright spots revealed the presence of ﬁne, low Z-
contrast (dark) precipitates spatially arranged in chain-like mor-
phologies ( Fig. 3 b). EDS analysis showed that these precipitates
were rich in V and the surrounding areas (bright spots) were rich in
Zr (Table 1). EBSD phase mapping showed that all the regions had a
BCC crystal structure.
NbTiZrVMo had a dendritic microstructure consisting of three
phases with characteristic morphologies and different chemical
compositions ( Fig. 4 a, b; Table 1). Discontinuous dendrites (grey-
colored in BSE images) were depleted in Zr and slightly enriched
with other elements. Continuous (bright-color) channels (i.e. inter-
dendrite regions) between the dendrites were rich in Ti and Zr and
depleted in Mo and V. Finely, near-globular-shaped bright pre-
cipitates were located mainly inside the channels, but ﬁner pre-
cipitates were also found inside the dendrites. These particles were
rich in Mo, V and Zr. The volume fractions of the dendrites, channels
and particles were 0.59, 0.19 and 0.22, respectively. EBSD analysis
showed that the dendrites and the channels had BCC crystal
structures ( Fig. 4 c) and the same crystallographic orientations
(Fig. 4 d), while the precipitates had a face center cubic crystal
structure and different crystallographic orientations ( Fig. 4 c,d).
Combining this information with the results of X-ray diffraction
(Fig. 1 , Table 2 ) and taking into account that Zr has the largest
atomic radius, one can conclude that the Zr-depleted dendrites
have the BCC1 crystal structure with a ¼ 326.0 pm, the Zr-rich
channels have the BCC2 crystal structure with a ¼ 342.4 pm and
the Mo, V and Zr rich precipitates are the (Mo,V)
2Zr cubic Laves
phase(C15). In the Laves phase, Nb likely occupies Mo and V sites,
while Ti occupies Zr sites.
NbTiZrVTa had an essentially single-phase BCC equiaxed grain
structure ( Fig. 5 a). The average grain size was 48 mm. Higher
magniﬁcation BSE images revealed the presence of ﬁne, dark pre-
cipitates at grain boundaries ( Fig. 5b). These precipitates were rich
in Zr ( Table 1) and their volume fraction was less than 0.1%. These

suggested that the Laves phase has a formula (Al,Nb,V) 2(Ti,Zr). The
volume fraction of the BCC matrix phase and precipitates were

3.3. Density, microhardness and compression properties
The measured density and microhardness values for the studied
alloys after HIP and annealing at 1400
/C14 C are given in Table 3 .
NbTiZr had density r ¼ 6.63 g/cm3 and microhardness
Hv ¼ 340 ± 2. Equiatomic substitution of Nb, Ti and Zr with 25% V
produced NbTiZrV with reduced density (6.52 g/cm 3) and micro-
hardness (335 ± 6 Hv). When a ﬁfth element (Mo, Ta, Cr or Al) was
added, hardness increased considerably and was in the range from
409 Hv for NbTiZrVTa to 494 Hv for NbTiZrVMo. As expected, the
alloy density increased with addition of heavier Cr, Mo or Ta, to
6.57 g/cm3, 7.23 g/cm3 and 8.43 g/cm3, respectively, and decreased
to 6.34 g/cm3 with addition of low-density Al.
The room temperature engineering stress vs. engineering
strain curves of the studied alloys are shown in Fig. 8 and the
obtained compression properties are tabulated in Table 4 . The
ternary NbTiZr alloy had elastic modulus E ¼ 80.4 GPa and yield
stress s0.2 ¼ 975 MPa, and it did not show any evidence of fracture
after compression with the engineering strain ε ¼ 55%. After
yielding, the ﬂow stress of NbTiZr dropped to 935 MPa and stayed
at this level until plastic strain of ~4.5% was reached. After that the
engineering stress of NbTiZr continuously increased with an in-
crease in plastic strain ( Fig. 8 ). The four-component NbTiZrV alloy
had a weaker elastic modulus, E ¼ 72.5 GPa, higher yield stress,
s0.2 ¼ 1 1 10 MPa, and no stress drop after yielding. In this alloy the
engineering stress increased continuously until deformation
stopped at ~55% compression strain. The ﬁve-component alloys had
noticeably higher E and
s0.2 but smaller fracture strain ( εf) than the
three- and four-component alloys. For example, NbTiZrVCr had
E ¼ 10 0 GPa, s0.2 ¼ 1295 MPa and εf ¼ 2.8% and NbTiZrVMo had
E ¼ 1 10.3 GPa,s0.2 ¼ 1510 MPa andεf ¼ 9.3%. NbTiZrVAl0.21 had only
slightly smaller s0.2 (¼ 1240 MPa) but almost twice higher εf (¼
5.4%) than the respective properties in NbTiZrVCr ( Table 4). Among
the ﬁve-component alloys, NbTiZrVTa had the highest fracture
strain, εf ¼ 19.1%, at moderate values of s0.2 ¼ 1395 MPa and
E ¼ 105.3 GPa.
Compression behaviors (engineering stress vs. engineering
strain curves) at 1000 /C14 C of the studied alloys are shown in Fig. 9
and the respective compression properties are given in Table 5 .
All the alloys showed excellent ductility, without any evidence of
fracture. Deformation of the specimens stopped after reaching 55%
compression strain. The behavior of the ternary NbTiZr alloy was
similar to that at room temperature. Namely, after reaching the
peak stress
sp ¼ 150 MPa almost immediately after plastic yielding
the ﬂow stress dropped to 100 MPa and then started increasing
continuously with strain. After 10% and 20% compression strain the
ﬂow stress was s10 ¼ 1 10 MPa and s20 ¼ 123 MPa, respectively
(Table 5). The quaternary NbTiZrV alloy was noticeably softer and
had s0.2 ¼ 75 MPa and sp ¼ 79 MPa, which were almost half of the
respective properties of NbTiZr. The strain hardening rate (after
initial stress drop to 64 MPa) was also noticeably weaker, and after
10% and 20% compression strains NbTiZrV had
s10 ¼ 68 MPa and
s20 ¼ 77 MPa, respectively. Further addition of 5.2% Al
(NbTiZrVAl0.24) slightly increased s0.2 (to 82 MPa) and sp (to
93 MPa) relative to the respective properties of NbTiZrV, but the
properties were still inferior to NbTiZr. Moreover, after overcoming
the peak stress, NbTiZrVAl
0.24 experienced continuous softening
and at strains above 10% it became even softer than NbTiZrV (Fig. 9).

regions inside grains experienced continuous, very considerable
(up to ~45 /C14 ) changes in the crystallographic orientations ( Fig. 10),
probably due to the presence of dislocation pile-ups and/or sub-
grain boundaries.

4. Discussion
4.1. Phase composition of the studied alloys
The results of the present work indicate that single-phase BCC
microstructure is a rarity in the studied refractory high entropy
alloys. Only the ternary NbTiZr alloy shows a single phase micro-
structure in all studied conditions. The four and ﬁve-component
alloys have more than one phase in their microstructure. The
additional phases are Laves and/or BCC phases. The BCC matrix
phase observed in NbTiZrV and NbTiZrVTa after high-temperature
annealing and slow cooling is metastable at lower temperatures
and experiences phase transformation during/after compression
deformation at 1000
/C14 C resulting in three or two BCC phases.
NbTiZrVCr, NbTiZrVMo and NbTiZrVAl 0.24 have BCC and Laves
phases both after high-temperature annealing and after deforma-
tion at 1000 /C14 C. These results indicate that increasing the compo-
sition complexity of refractory near-equiatomic alloys by increasing
the number of the alloying elements does not necessarily result in a
simpler phase structure. The type of the alloying elements and
phase stability in the temperature range of use seems to be more
important.
Phenomenological criteria developed for the selection of HEAs
with solid solution or intermetallic phases do not work well for the
studied alloys. For example, the con ﬁgurational entropy of mixing,
DSm, increases with an increase in the number of components
(Table 7), however this does not stabilize the BCC phase observed in
the base NbTiZr alloy. According to another criterion suggested by
Zhang et al. [ 16], intermetallic phases can be present in HEAs for
which the atomic size difference, dr > 3% and the enthalpy of mixing
DHm < 0 kJ/mol, while solid solution phases can be present if dr
<6.2% and DHm > /C0 12 kJ/mol. Following these conditions and
calculated dr and DHm values (Table 7), none of the studied alloys,
except NbTiZr, should contain solid solution phases, which dis-
agrees with the experiment. On the other hand, the
DHm- dr cri-
terion correctly predicts the presence of an intermetallic (Laves)
phase in NbTiZrVMo, NbTiZrVCr and NbTiZrVAl
0.24. Yang and Zhang

tion with dr to identify HEAs which would contain solid solution
phases only. Here Ts ¼ P ciTsi and Tsi is the melting temperature of
alloying element i. According to this criterion, only solid solution
phases are present in HEAs for which Ώ /C21 1.1 and dr /C20 3.6% or
Ώ /C21 10; both solid solution and intermetallic phases can be present
when dr > 3.6% and 1.1 /C20 Ώ < 10; and mostly intermetallic phases
are present when dr > 6.6% and Ώ < 2. When these conditions are
compared with dr and Ώ values calculated for the studied alloys
(Table 7), it can be found that the dr - Ώ criterion correctly predicts
the presence of both SS and IM phases in NbTiZrVCr and
NbTiZrVAl

contradicts our current observations.

reason for the delayed phase separation.

5. Summary and conclusions
Compositional effect on phase composition, phase stability,
microstructure and mechanical properties of NbTiZr-based com-
plex concentrated alloys was studied using six near-equiatomic
refractory alloys, NbTiZr, NbTiZrV, NbTiZrVMo, NbTiZrVTa,
NbTiZrVCr, and NbTiZrVAl0.24, produced by arc melting followed by
HIP and homogenized annealing.
Only NbTiZr had a stable single-phase BCC crystal structure in
the temperature range of 25 e1000 /C14 C. Additions of V and other
elements led to formation of additional BCC and/or Laves phases.
Deformation at 1000 /C14 C accelerated the phase transformations.
Empirical predictions of phase stability using thermodynamic
criteria [ 18,19] provide better agreement with the current experi-
mental observations than other phenomenological criteria.
Room temperature strength increased with an increase in the
number of alloying elements. In the ﬁve-component alloys the
strength strongly depended on the type of the ﬁfth element and it
was the highest in NbTiZrVMo. All the ﬁve-component alloys were
less ductile than NbTiZ and NbTiZrV. Their ductility decreased
rapidly with an increase in the volume fraction of the Laves phase.
At 1000
/C14 C, only NbTiZrVCr and NbTiZrVMo were stronger than
NbTiZr. Additions of V, V and Ta, or V and Al caused softening. The
microstructure/phase instability was likely the reason of the
reduced high-temperature strength of NbTiZrV, NbTiZrVTa, and
NbTiZrVAl0.24. Among the elements used for alloying, Mo had the
strongest strengthening effect both at 25 /C14 C and 1000 /C14 C.
Formation of additional BCC phases and Laves phases in the
four- and ﬁve-component alloys was associated with the presence
of Zr. Interactions of Zr with Nb and Ta were responsible for the BCC
phase separation and interactions of Zr with Cr, Mo, Al and V were
responsible for the Laves phase formation. To avoid formation of
intermetallic phases, the amount of Zr should be controlled in Al,
Cr, Mo and/or Al-V containing RHEAs.
The reported results indicate that increasing the composition
complexity of refractory near-equiatomic alloys by increasing the
number of the alloying elements does not necessary result in a
simpler phase structure and/or better mechanical properties. As
expected from classical metallurgy, the type of the alloying ele-
ments and phase stability in the temperature range of use are more
important.
