ABSTRACT 
Ultra-fine grain TiAlV
0.5
CrMo refractory complex concentrated alloy (RCCA) was prepared by using mechanical 
alloying (MA) and spark plasma sintering (SPS), and the effects of heat treatment temperature on phase 
composition, microstructural evolution and mechanical properties were investigated. The results indicate that 
the phase composition of as-SPSed alloy comprises disordered BCC1 and BCC2 solid solution phases, along with a 
minor amount of Al
2
O
3 
phase. The system demonstrates exceptional microstructural and phase stability after the 
heat treatment at 1200 
◦
C for 6 h, with only a slight decrease in hardness to a value of 9.86 GPa. With the 
increase in heat treatment temperature, the volume fraction of recrystallized grains gradually increases, leading 
to an initial growth and subsequent refinement in grain size. Under heat treatment at 800 
◦
C and 1000 
◦
C, the 
increase in volume fraction of Al
2
O
3 
was found to enhance the room temperature compressive yield strength of 
the alloy while deteriorating the plasticity. Specifically, the strength-plasticity trade-off was achieved after heat- 
treated at 1200 
◦
C for 2 h, with a compressive yield strength of 2667 MPa and a plastic strain of 10.7% at room 
temperature. The excellent mechanical properties were attributed to the synergistic effects of lattice distortions, 
reduced dislocation density, weakened texture and refined grain structure. Furthermore, this alloy demonstrated 
a compressive yield strength of 153 MPa at a temperature of 1100 
◦
C without fracturing at a strain of 50%. The 
flow stresses exhibited typical dynamic recrystallization (DRX) characteristics at elevated temperatures. 
1. Introduction 
Complex concentrated alloys (CCAs) have become a new hot spot in 
the field of metallic materials due to their potentially attractive me -
chanical properties, including ultra-high strength and acceptable frac -
ture toughness, as well as excellent oxidation and corrosion resistance at 
elevated temperatures [ 1 – 3 ]. Among them, refractory complex 
concentrated alloys (RCCAs) based on high-melting-point elements are 
promising structural materials for apply at temperature significantly 
above advanced Ni-based superalloys [ 4 ]. Thermal stability determined 
the ability of a material to maintain its performance at the desired 
temperature over an extended period of service. To further expand the 
range of RCCAs applications, it is important to understand their phase 
and microstructure stability at high temperatures. 
Numerous research have found that multicomponent CCAs are 
thermodynamically stable at elevated temperatures, but 
thermodynamically unstable at mediate temperatures [ 5 – 7 ]. The high 
temperature stable solid solutions can be maintained at ambient tem -
perature mainly due to the rapid cooling rate limited the diffusion of 
elements during the preparation process. For instance, F. Otto and co- 
workers [ 5 ] reported that the CoCrFeMnNi alloy with a single FCC 
solid solution structure has no phase transformation discovered after 
annealing at 900 
◦
C for 500 days. However, a Fe/Cr-rich sigma phase 
started to precipitate after annealing at 800 
◦
C. Similar results were also 
obtained for the TiZrHfNbTa alloy, which was considered to be a single- 
phase solid solution with high stability under both as-cast and annealing 
conditions (above ~1000 
◦
C) [ 6 , 8 ]. Surprisingly, the thermodynamic 
instability study of TiZrHfNbTa confirmed that a phase decomposition of 
the single solid solution structure into a NbTa-rich BCC phase and ZrHf- 
rich phases after annealing at intermediate temperatures [ 6 , 9 ]. A review 
of the published literatures have found that solid solution phases (single- 
phase or double phase) could also be obtained in e.g., the MgAlSiCrFeNi,

Currently, the thermodynamic stability of ultra-fine/nanocrystalline 
grain CCAs are receiving over whelming attention from metallurgists 
[ 9 , 12 ]. It has been found that the abundance grain boundaries in ultra- 
fine/nanocrystalline grained alloys reduced the diffusion distance, 
leading to an increase in the propensity for phase decomposition and 
grain growth during heat treatment. On the contrary, the same alloy 
usually required extensive annealing times to achieve equivalent 
behavior in its coarse-grained state. The investigation on the thermo -
dynamic stability of ultra-fine/nanocrystalline grained materials mainly 
included two parts: the retention of ultra-fine/nanocrystalline grain size 
and the stability of the phases formed. Benjamin Schuh [ 13 ] discovered 
that after annealing at 1100

BCC phase transformed into a multi-phase structure, and the average 
grain size increased from 40 nm to 2 μ m. Inspection of the published

evolution and grain size, as well as their impact on mechanical prop -

application of this alloy as high-temperature structural components.

grained TiAlV

evolution of TiAlV

behavior of the alloy in the ultra-fine grain size regime. 
2. Experimental procedures 
The ultra-fine grained TiAlV
0.5
CrMo RCCA (1:1:0.5:1:1, at.%) was 
synthesized using powder metallurgy processes. High purity ( > 99.9 wt 
%) elemental powders of Ti, Al, V, Cr, and Mo with particle size of ≤ 15 
μ m were milled in stainless steel tanks for 30 h at 400 rpm. The stainless 
steel ball to powder weight ratio of 30:1. The MA process was performed 
under argon atmosphere to prevent oxidation. It was interrupted every 
30 min, followed by a 15 min halt to avoid excessive heating within the 
vials. The powder exhibited a single BCC structure (IM3m, space group 
No. 229; a = 3.165 Å) after milled for 30 h, as illustrated in Fig. 1 . 
Subsequently, the milled powders were sintered via SPS (SPS-1050) at 
1200 
◦
C for 5 min under vacuum with a uniaxial pressure of 40 MPa. The 
bulk alloys were encapsulated in evacuated quartz tubes, annealed at 
temperatures ranging from 800 to 1100 
◦
C for 2 h, as well as at 1200 
◦
C 
for both 2 and 6 h, followed by air cooling. The chemical composition of 
the as-sintered alloy and the heat-treated alloys was determined by 
employing inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry 
(ICP-OES, iCAP 7400), as shown in Table 1 . The phase composition was 
carried out by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D/max-rb diffractometer) using 
the Cu K α radiation at a scanning speed of 5
◦
/min. The microstructure 
was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss supra 
55) equipped with backscatter electron (BSE), energy dispersive X-ray 
(EDX) spectroscopy and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) de -
tectors. The acquisition of BSE, EDX, and EBSD maps was performed at 
20 kV and 10 nA with a step size ranging from 0.01 to 0.2 μ m. EBSD 
samples were processed using cross section ion polishing machine (IB- 
09020CP). The step size for all EBSD scans was set to 0.01 μ m, ensuring 
high precision and accuracy in the data acquisition process. The speci -
mens for EBSD measurements were obtained by cutting from the cross 
section in the direction of uniaxial pressure, as depicted in Fig. 2 . Then, 
the EBSD data were evaluated by the HKL Channel 5 analysis software. 
Elemental distribution and high resolution transmission electron mi -
croscopy (HRTEM) were analyzed using scanning transmission electron 
microscopy (STEM, Talos F200x) equipped with EDX. Thin specimens 
for TEM investigations were attained by ion polishing using GATAN695. 
The micro-hardness tests were conducted on a Vickers hardness in -
strument (HVS-1000 A) with a load of 4.9 N and a holding time of 15 s. 
The average of the five test results was regarded as the hardness values 
of the alloys. Compressive tests were performed at ambient temperature 
using cylindrical specimens of Φ 3 × 4.5 mm and an Instron-5569 uni -
versal testing machine. Cylindrical specimens of Φ 6 × 9 mm were sub -
jected to compression tests using the dynamic thermal simulation testing 
machine (Gleeble-1500D) at 1100

4.1. Microstructure evolution during heat treatment 
There are two primary factors contributing to the precipitation of the 
Al
2
O
3 
phase during heat treatment at temperatures below 1000 
◦
C. On 
the one hand, the strong ceramic compounds possess larger heats of 
formation, as well as Al exhibits a higher affinity for oxygen compared to 
other alloying elements. On the other hand, the precipitation of the 
Al
2
O
3 
phase (Al: ~1.43 Å, O: ~0.66 Å) can mitigate atomic size misfit 
and reduce elastic energy in the solid solution phase. Conversely, an 
increase in heat treatment temperature leads to a corresponding in -
crease in inter-element solubility, resulting in the re-dissolution of Al 
and O into the matrix. Achieving the synergistic diffusion and rear -
rangement of elements during cooling process is a significant challenge 
due to the sluggish diffusion effect in CCAs. Therefore, the precipitation 
of the Al
2
O
3 
phase is inhibited, as shown in Fig. 3 . The calculation of the 
equilibrium diagram for the alloy enables more accurate prediction of 
the phase composition at different temperatures in the system. Liu and 
colleagues [ 24 ] utilized the Calculation of Phase Diagrams approach to 
determine the equilibrium phase fraction in the Ti-V-Nb-Ta system with 
respect to oxygen partial pressure. The results demonstrated that the 
presence of oxygen facilitates the formation of HCP-Ti (Hexagonal 
Close-Packed) phase in the alloy. Furthermore, an increase in oxygen 
content led to a continuous reduction in the fraction of BCC phase. 
Therefore, the equilibrium phase diagram for the Ti-Al-V-Cr-Mo system, 
including oxygen (6 at.%), was determined using Thermo-Calc software 
in conjunction with the Ti alloy database TCTI4, as shown in Fig. 17 . 
Due to the complexity of the system and limitations inherent in the 
database, it is possible that the accuracy of theoretical predictions may 
not be flawless. Nonetheless, it provides valuable guidance for further 
phase analysis. Within the temperature range of 1000 
◦
C to 1200 
◦
C, 
there are three phases with distinct crystal structures in the system. The 
precipitation or dissolution of second phase at different annealing 
temperatures is expected to exert an influence on the volume fractions of 
BCC1 phases.

5. Conclusion 
Ultra-fine grain TiAlV
0.5
CrMo RCCA was synthesized via MA and SPS 
in this study. Subsequently, the phase composition, microstructural 
evolution, and their effects on mechanical properties of both as-SPSed 
and heat-treated alloys were thoroughly investigated. The following 
key conclusions were derived: 
1. The as-SPSed alloy consisted of two disordered BCC solid solutions 
and a hexagonal Al
2
O
3 
phase. No phase transition was observed after 
heat treatment, except for the alteration in diffraction peak intensity 
and the emergence of a weak peak. Even after heat-treated at 1200 
◦
C 
for 6 h, the alloy exhibited excellent microstructural stability without 
grain coarsening. 
2. The heat treatment temperature had a positive effect on the volume 
fraction of recrystallized grains, while its impact on grain size 
demonstrated a non-monotonic trend. 
3. The volume fraction of Al
2
O
3 
in the alloy increases with increasing 
heat treatment temperature below 1000 
◦
C. The room temperature 
compressive yield strength decreased from 2375 MPa at 800 
◦
C to 
2201 MPa at 1000 
◦
C, and the plastic strain increased from 6.6% to 
7.8%. 
4. The alloy heat-treated at 1200 
◦
C for 2 h presented a significantly 
enhanced room temperature compressive yield strength of 2667 
MPa, accompanied by a reasonable plastic strain as high as 10.7%. 
The superior mechanical properties were primarily attributed to the 
reduced dislocation density, weakened texture and grain refinement. 
5. The plastic strain of the alloys is > 50% at a deformation temperature 
of 1100 
◦
C. Moreover, the flow stresses displayed typical character -
istics of DRX under elevated temperatures.
