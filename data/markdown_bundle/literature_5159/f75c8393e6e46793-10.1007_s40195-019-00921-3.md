A Novel Series of Refractory High‑Entropy Alloys Ti2ZrHf0.5VNbx 
with High Specific Yield Strength and Good Ductility
Dong‑Xu Qiao1 · Hui Jiang2 · Wen‑Na Jiao1 · Yi‑Ping Lu1 · Zhi‑Qiang Cao1 · Ting‑Ju Li1
Received: 28 March 2019 / Revised: 24 April 2019

Abstract
A series of Ti2ZrHf0.5VNbx (x = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0) refractory high-entropy alloys were prepared to investigate the 
alloying effect of Nb on the microstructures and mechanical properties. All the alloys displayed a simple BCC structure. 
The microstructures of the alloys changed from the initial single-phase columnar structure (x = 0) to dendrite microstructure 
(x > 0). At room temperature, all the alloys exhibited high ductility (with the compressive strains of more than 50%). With 
the increase in Nb content, the yield strength slightly decreased from 1160 to 980 MPa and the hardness dropped from 338 
to 310 HV. Moreover, the alloys exhibited low density from 6.47 to 6.84 g/cm
3 and high specific yield strength (SYS) from 
143 to 179 kPa m 3/kg. The comprehensive performance of ductility and SYS was superior to most of the reported high-
entropy alloys. The yield strength of the alloys increased from 405 to 859 MPa and from 85 to 195 MPa with the addition 
of Nb element at 873 K and 1073 K, respectively.
Keywords Specific yield strength · Refractory metals · Structure · Mechanical properties
1 Introduction
Recently, a new alloy concept of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) 
or multicomponent alloys was proposed by Yeh et al. [1] and 
Cantor et al. [2]. HEAs generally contain principal elements 
with the concentration between 5 and 35 at% [ 3–11] and 
exhibit many excellent physical, chemical and mechanical 
properties, such as high strength and ductility [3, 4, 12, 13], 
high fracture toughness [5 , 6], excellent corrosion resist-
ance [7, 8] and good irradiation resistance [9, 10]. Thereinto, 
refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) were designed using 
high-melting-point metals such as V, Nb, Ta, Mo, W, Ti, 
Zr and Hf, which possess high strength and excellent high-
temperature softening resistance [11, 14, 15]. Hence, they 
became potential candidates applying to the field of aero -
space and aviation. For example, Senkov et al. first reported 
the WMoNbTa(V) RHEAs with a single BCC phase that 
could retain the yield strength of more than 500 MPa at 
1600 °C, which were superior to conventional superalloys 
[16]. Since then, more RHEAs were designed, such as TiZ-
rHfNbTa, TiZrHfVMo, TiZrHfMoNb, Ti(V)WMoNbTa, 
TiVNbTa and so on [17– 23], which exhibited excellent 
strength at room temperature (RT) and high temperature. 
However, most of them generally had two disadvantages: 
poor ductility (compressive strain ε < 50%) or high density 
(ρ > 7.5 g/cm
3), which were not conductive to industrial 
applications. Therefore, some light-weight HEAs contain-
ing Al and Cr elements, such as CrNbTiVZr, TiVNb(Ta)Al
x, 
 TiVNbAlZrx and AlMo0.5NbTa0.5TiZr etc. were designed 
to reduce the density [ 13, 24–29]. These alloys possessed 
high strength with low density (~ 6.5 g/cm3) but exhibited 
poor plasticity. Therefore, it was difficult for most reported 
RHEAs to have high strength, good ductility and low density 
at the same time.
To address the above mentioned problems in the real 
application of RHEAs, this study proposed to use light ele-
ments, such as Ti, Zr, Hf, V and Nb, to design low-weight 
RHEAs, aiming at high strength and good plasticity to 
resolve the strength-ductility trade-off. According to the

2 Experimental
The Ti2ZrHf0.5VNbx (when x = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1, 
denoted as Nb0, Nb0.25, Nb0.5, Nb0.75 and Nb1, respectively) 
alloys were prepared by vacuum arc melting in a Ti-gettered 
argon atmosphere using pure elements of Ti, Zr, V, Nb and 
Hf (99.95 wt%). Approximately 35 g of alloy ingots was 
remelted at most seven times for improving the homogene-
ity of alloys. The samples were ground using SiC papers 
up to 2000 grit, and then the crystalline structure of the 
samples was characterized by the X-ray diffractometer 
(XRD, EMPYREAN with CuK
α radiation target) with the 
angle of 2θ scanning from 20° to 100° at a scanning rate of 
0.106103°/s. After polishing and corrosion, the microstruc-
ture and compositions of the samples were analyzed using 
the scanning electron microscope (SEM/Zeiss Supra 55). 
The RT compressive tests were conducted using a Wipper -
mann materials testing machine according to the standard 
of ASTM E9-09. The compressive properties at 873 K and 
1073 K were tested by a Gleeble-3500 thermal simulation 
machine, and they were heated to the test temperature at a 
heating rate of 5 K/s and held for 5 min. The test specimens 
were cylindrical samples of Φ5 × 10 mm and Φ6 × 8 mm 
at room temperature and elevated temperature, respec-
tively. The compression rates were all 10
−3 s−1. Hardness 
measurements of the alloys (test standards used ASTM 
E384-11e1) were conducted using a Vickers hardness tester 
(MH-50) under a load of 1000 g for 15 s. Seven measure-
ments were made on each sample to obtain the averaged 
experimental data. The density of the alloys was measured 
by the Archimedes drainage method (ME204E balance).
3 Results and Discussion
3.1 XRD Analysis and Phase Identification
Figure 1a shows the XRD patterns of the Ti2ZrHf0.5VNbx 
alloys with different Nb contents. A single disordered BCC 
phase was found in the Ti
2ZrHf0.5VNbx alloys. No other 
phase diffraction peaks appeared with the increase in Nb 
content, indicating that the addition of Nb element has no 
obvious influence on the phase formation for this series of 
alloys. Figure  1b shows the detailed scans for the peak of 
(110) of BCC solid solution phase. It can be found that the 
peak of the BCC phase integrally shifted toward the higher 
2θ angle with the addition of Nb element, indicating that the 
lattice constants decrease slightly on the whole, as shown in 
Table 1. It is because of the radius of Nb element is 1.429 Å 
which is a little bit less than the average value (1.437 Å) of 
 Ti
2ZrHf0.5V matrix.

from 4.22 to 4.37 with the increase in Nb content which 
manifests the alloys tend to form BCC phase; Besides the

3.2 Microstructure
The SEM images of Ti2ZrHf0.5VNbx alloys are displayed in 
Fig. 2. It can be seen that the Ti2ZrHf0.5VNb0 alloy shows 
a coarse columnar crystal structure, confirming the Nb0 
alloy has a single-phase structure. Combined with the XRD 
results, a conclusion can be drawn that the single-phase 
structure is the BCC solid solution phase. With the addition 
of Nb element, dendrite structures are observed in Nb
0.25, 
 Nb0.5, Nb0.75 and Nb1 alloys. As the Ti2ZrHf0.5VNbx alloys 
are a single-phase structure, the Nb0.5 alloy is chosen to 
carry out the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) test, 
as shown in Fig. 2f. It can be seen that the microstructure is 
uniform, and only one set of diffraction spots of BCC phase 
was found. In addition, the elemental mapping of Nb
1 alloy 
was investigated as a typical Ti2ZrHf0.5VNbx alloys, and it 
can be seen that the all elements are evenly distributed as 
shown in Fig.  3. The above results prove that the series of 
alloys exhibit single BCC structure with homogeneous com-
position distribution. 
3.3 Mechanical Properties
