Some observations on the high temperature oxidation
behaviour of plasma sprayed Ni3Al coatings
H. Singh a,∗, S. Prakash b, D. Puri b
a Mechanical Engineering Department, BBSB Engineering College, Fatehgarh Sahib 140407, India

Received 15 June 2006; received in revised form 24 August 2006; accepted 24 August 2006
Abstract
High temperature oxidation resistance of the superalloys can be greatly enhanced by plasma sprayed coatings and this is a growing industry of
considerable economic importance. The purpose of these coatings is to form long-lasting oxidation protective scales. In the current investigation,
Ni3Al powder was prepared by mechanical mixing of pure nickel and aluminium powders in a ball mill. Subsequently Ni3Al powder was deposited
on three Ni-base superalloys: Superni 600, Superni 601 and Superni 718 and, one Fe-base superalloy, Superfer 800H by shrouded plasma spray
process. Oxidation studies were conducted on the coated superalloys in air at 900
◦C under cyclic conditions for 50 cycles. Each cycle consisted
of 1 h heating followed by 20 min of cooling in air. The thermogravimetric technique was used to approximate the kinetics of oxidation. All the
coated superalloys nearly followed parabolic rate law of oxidation. X-ray diffraction, SEM/EDAX and EPMA techniques were used to analyse the
oxidation products. The Ni
3Al coating was found to be successful in maintaining its adherence to the superalloy substrates in all the cases. The
oxide scales formed on the oxidised coated superalloys were found to be intact and spallation-free. XRD analysis revealed the presence of phases
like NiO, Al
2O3 and NiAl2O4 in the oxide scales, which are reported as protective oxides against high temperature oxidation. The XRD results
were further supported by SEM/EDAX and EPMA.

1. Introduction
Gas turbine technology for power generation and aero engine
applications places an increasing demand on the use of high tem-
perature Ni- and Fe-based superalloys. Although the Ni-base
superalloys possess adequate strength at the turbine operating
temperatures, they often lack resistance to the combustion envi-
ronments especially used for long periods of time. In such envi-
ronments protective coatings on the surface of superalloys are
frequently considered. An enormous challenge exists to develop
and apply these techniques to other high temperature applica-
tions [1].
Many coating techniques have been suggested such as pack
cementation, plasma spraying and chemical vapour deposition.
However, among the various coating methods, plasma spraying
is reported to be versatile technology by Fauchais et al. [2] that

has been successful as a reliable cost-effective solution for many
industrial problems. Plasma spraying is the most ﬂexible thermal
spray process with respect to the sprayed materials. The high
temperatures of plasma spray processes permit the deposition of
coatings for applications in areas of liquid and high temperature
corrosion and wear protection and also special applications for
thermal, electrical and biomedical purposes [3].
With no promising turbine materials beyond the coated
nickel-base superalloys apparent in the foreseeable future, con-
tinued progress will likely be made by further reﬁnement of
control of thermally grown oxide adherence, and by more cost-
effective manufacturing processes for contemporary types of
coatings [4]. Manufacture of overlay coatings by EBPVD dom-
inated through the 1970s. Low-pressure (LPPS) and then the
Union Carbide (Praxair) shrouded plasma torch were favored
for more complex formulations towards the end of 20th century
[4]. According to Goward[4] a complete understanding of oxide
adherence is still elusive and is one of the most important areas
of research, for both coatings and superalloys, to provide further
advances in engine efﬁciency and service life.

The aim of this paper is to study the oxidation behaviour of
Ni3Al coated superalloys at an elevated temperature of 900 ◦C
in air by accelerated testing with an objective to access the
performance of Ni
3Al coating on the given Ni- and Fe-based
superalloys. Ordered intermetallic aluminides such as Ni 3Al
have received much attention as potential structural materials
because of their low density, high strength at elevated tem-
peratures, oxidation resistance, and excellent creep properties
[5,6]. The superalloys under study were procured from Mishra
Dhatu Nigam Limited, Hyderabad (India), among them three
are Ni-based namely Superni 600, 601 and 718, and the fourth
is Fe-based, Superfer 800H. Superni 600 has been developed
for furnace parts and heat treating jigs; Superni 601 and 718
for jet engines, pump bodies and parts and other high temper-
ature heat resisting applications; and Superfer 800H for steam
boilers, furnace equipment, heat exchangers and piping in chem-
ical industry, reformer, bafﬂe plates/tubes in fertilizer plants.
This investigation regarding the high temperature oxidation
behaviour of Ni
3Al coating may be useful for exploring the pos-
sibility of use of Ni3Al as a high temperature protective coating
in applications such as gas turbines, boiler tubes, etc. The knowl-
edge of the reaction kinetics and the nature of the surface scales
formed during oxidation are important for evaluating the alloys
for their use and degradation characteristics in high temperature
applications [7].
Furthermore, the high temperature oxidation behaviour in air
under cyclic conditions will also provide useful information on
the adhesion of the Ni
3Al coatings to the substrate superalloys
and the spalling tendency of the oxide scale as per the sugges-
tions of Burman and Ericsson[8]. The presented experiment has
been conducted under cyclic conditions since the cyclic condi-
tions constitute a more realistic approach towards solving the
problem of metal corrosion (oxidation) in actual applications,
where conditions are more or less cyclic, rather than isothermal
[9].
2. Experimental procedure
The substrate superalloys were obtained from Mishra Dhatu
Nigham Limited, Hyderabad (India) in the rolled sheet form.
The chemical compositions (wt.%) of the substrate superalloys
have been detailed in Table 1. The specimens with dimensions
of approximately 20 mm × 15 mm× 5 mm were cut from the
sheets. The specimens were polished and grit blasted with alu-
mina powder (grit 60) before being plasma sprayed. The Ni
3Al
powder was prepared in the laboratory. Nickel powder with min-
imum assay 99.5% and size 200 mesh (Art. 4860) and aluminium
ﬁne powder with minimum assay 99.7% (Art. 880) both supplied
by Loba Chemical (India) were mixed in stochiometric ratio 3:1
in the laboratory ball mill for 8 h to form a uniform mixture of
Ni
3Al powder. A 40 kW Miller Thermal Plasma Spray Appara-
tus was used to apply the coatings. Argon was used as powder
carrying and shielding gas. All the process parameters including
the spray distance were constant throughout coating process. The
parameters include arc current of 700 A, arc voltage 35 V , pow-
der ﬂow rate 3.2 rpm, spraying distance 90–110 mm, the plasma
arc gas (argon) and carrier gas (argon) pressures of 59 and 40 psi,
respectively. NiCrAlY (Praxair NI-343) was applied as bond
coat between the substrate superalloy and the top coat of Ni
3Al
in all the cases, in order to enhance the bond strength and reduce
the effect of thermal expansion coefﬁcient mismatch, subse-
quently the ﬁnal coating of Ni
3Al alloy powder was deposited on
the base superalloys. The details of coating powder and poros-
ity (2.25–4%) measurement of as-sprayed coatings are reported
in the earlier publications of the authors [10,11]. The detailed
characterisation of these coatings has already been published
elsewhere [11]. The average thickness of the Ni
3Al top coat
as measured from back scattered electron images (BSEI) was
found to be 247 /H9262m, and that of the bond coat was 166 /H9262m.
Cyclic oxidation studies were performed in air environment for
50 cycles. Each cycle consisted of 1 h heating at 900
◦C in silicon
carbide tube furnace followed by 20 min cooling at room tem-
perature. The methodology for oxidation testing and analysis
can be found elsewhere [10,11]. SEM/EDAX analysis was car-
ried out with scanning electron microscope [JEOL (JSM-5800)]
with EDAX attachment of Oxford (Model-6841) made in Eng-
land. The equipment could directly indicate the phases (oxides)
present along with their compositions (wt.%) based on built-in
EDAX software, which is a patented product of Oxford ISIS300.

4. Discussion
The method of preparation of Ni 3Al powder has also been
reported by La et al. [12] and Liu and Gao [13]. They have also
conﬁrmed the formation of Ni3Al as a main phase by XRD analy-
sis similar to the current investigation. Ni3Al has been reported
as NiO former by McCarron et al. [14], which has also been
observed in the present study for all the oxidised Ni 3Al coated
superalloys. Apart from XRD, EDAX and EPMA analysis, the
presence of green colour scale further indicates the formation of
NiO as reported by Bornstein et al.[15]. The presence of nickel
oxide on surface of nickel aluminide coatings on mild steel has
also been observed by Malik et al.[16] during their studies per-
formed at 800
◦C in air. The formation of NiO as a main phase
along with NiAl2O4 spinel, similar to the current study, has also
been reported by Liu and Gao [13] for Ni3Al alloy after oxida-
tion at 900 ◦C for 50 h. They suggested that during oxidation,
the alloy grain boundaries promoted the formation of Al 2O3,
which further reacts with NiO in the vicinity to form NiAl 2O4
spinel. La et al. [12] also observed the formation of NiAl 2O4
phase during oxidation of Ni 3Al coating prepared by the SHS
casting on carbon steel. These oxides particularly Al 2O3 may
partially inhibit oxidation of the substrate alloys by blocking the
diffusion of reacting species towards the substrate alloys, as has
been suggested by Stroosnijder et al. [1] and Toma et al. [17].
Presence of the spinel NiAl
2O4 in the oxide scales also helps to
develop oxidation resistance as these spinel phases usually have
much smaller diffusion coefﬁcients of the cations and anions
than those in their parent oxides [18].
The high temperature cyclic oxidation of Ni
3Al coated super-
alloys proceeded by a diffusion controlled mechanism as could
be perceived from the parabolic nature of the weight gain versus
number of cycles plots (Fig. 1). The behaviour is in good agree-

formation of oxides at the coating splat boundaries and within
open pores due to the penetration of the oxidizing species along
the splat boundaries/open pores during the early cycles of the
study. However, once the oxides are formed at places of porosity
and splat boundaries, the coating becomes dense and the diffu-
sion of oxidizing species to the internal portions of the coatings
gets slowed down and the growth of the oxides becomes limited
mainly to the surface of the specimens. This, in turn, will make
the weight gain and hence the oxidation rate steady with the
further progress of exposure time as has been observed in the
current study. The oxide formed at the splat boundaries looks
to be mainly of aluminium in the top coat, as can be perceived
from EPMA for the coated Superni 601 ( Fig. 7 ), whereas in
the bond coat region of the scale, the oxides at the boundaries
are most likely of aluminium and yttrium with chromium. Iden-
tical ﬁndings have also been reported by Choi et al. [19] and
Niranatlumpong et al. [20] for plasma sprayed NiCrAlY coat-
ings.

5. Conclusions
(1) The shrouded plasma spray process provides the possibility
of developing coatings of Ni
3Al powder on the given super-
alloys Superni 600, 601, 718 and Superfer 800H. The plasma
sprayed Ni
3Al coating when subjected to cyclic oxidation
in air at 900 ◦C, was found to be successful in maintaining
its adherence with the substrate superalloys under investi-
gation. The oxide scales were also found to be intact and
spallation-free in all the cases.
(2) Rate of oxidation was observed to be high in the early cycles
of the study for the Ni
3Al coated superalloys, which may be
attributed to the interconnected network of pores and splat
boundaries present in coating structure.
(3) In all the Ni
3Al coated cases, the oxidation behaviour was
found to be governed by a parabolic rate law. Moreover,
the internal oxidation of the substrate superalloys was not
observed in general.
(4) The Ni
3Al coating after exposure to air oxidation showed
the presence of mainly oxides in its surface scale. The
phases revealed were oxides of nickel and aluminium, and
spinels containing nickel–aluminium type mixed oxides.
These oxides and spinels are reported to be protective in
nature against high temperature oxidation.
