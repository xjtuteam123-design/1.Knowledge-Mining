ABSTRACT
Advanced creep life at 1100 
◦
C/140 MPa was obtained in a nickel-based single crystal superalloys (Ni-SX alloys) 
by varying the Al content. The lattice distortion phenomenon and γ’ phase shape induced by Al content matters 
with systematic investigation. Novel results demonstrated that the γ’ phase shape transitioned from nearly 
spherical to square, while the creep life increased from 2 h to 269.05 h with Al content increase from 4.5 to 5.7 
wt%. Further increasing the Al content to 6.4 wt% enhanced the γ’ phase content while reducing the creep 
rupture life to 132.08 h. Dense dislocation networks with irregularly arranged raft γ’ structure were observed at 
γ / γ’ phase interface. Diffraction patterns and high-resolution interface images revealed that the modulation of 
lattice constants by Al content further affects the lattice misfit and γ’ phase shape, subsequently influencing the 
dislocation migration and creep performance.
1. Introduction
Ni-SX alloys were characterized as excellent stability and perfor -
mance at service temperature above 1000 
◦
C and critically serviced in 
aerospace engines and gas turbines [ 1 ]. The temperature tolerance for 
Ni-SX alloys is increasing as the requirement of engine performance and 
fuel efficiency improves [ 2 ]. The solid solution in γ phase and precipi -
tation of γ’ phase are two major strengthening mechanisms for Ni-SX 
alloys [ 3 , 4 ]. Therefore, the design of chemical composition and 
manufacturing processes are critical for controlling microstructure to 
obtain an excellent creep life at service temperature. With the contin -
uous advancement of single-crystal fabrication techniques, composition 
optimization has emerged as a pivotal factor in enhancing the perfor -
mance of Ni-SX alloys.
Currently, the widely used Ni-SX alloys from 1st to 3rd commercial 
generation are classified based on the elemental contents of Re (0 wt%, 
3 wt%, 6 wt%), W, Mo, and Ta elements [ 5 – 7 ]. As a key element, Re with 
a particularly low diffusion coefficient and is mainly distributed in the γ 
phase for solution strengthening to the superalloy. Re could also alle -
viate the diffusion kinetics of the alloy. This effect had the advantage of 
enhancing the alloy ’ s creep resistance [ 8 , 9 ]. However, raising the Re 
content will increase the alloy density and lead to harmful TCP phases, 
which cause instability of Ni-SX alloys at service temperature. The 
control of alloying elements such as W, Mo, and Ta has also been 
investigated by numerous researchers to improve the performance and 
microstructure stability of Ni-SX alloys. Ru, as a typical element in the 
platinum group, was found to be able to suppress the TCP precipitation. 
The reason was considered as the driving force reduction when Ru was 
introduced [ 10 ]. Reported by Chen et al. [ 11 ], the creep life of a 2nd 
generation Ni-SX alloys could also be improved to exceed that of Ren ´e 
N5 by adding a small amount of Ti in instead of Re. Such effect of Ti was 
considered as dislocation strengthening and solid solution strengthening 
mechanism. Element W could also accelerate the γ’ phase rafting during 
thermal exposure, it was considered that the increased TCP phase mat -
ters [ 12 ]. Discovered that in the alloy with the increase of W content, the 
rafting rate of the γ’ phase accelerated during the thermal exposure 
process, and the area fraction of the precipitated TCP phase also 
increased. While few research reports the effect of Al on the micro -
structure and creep life of Ni-SX alloys in the background of lightweight, 
high performance, and energy-saving.
As for the precipitation of γ’ phase, Al is the critical element. The

3.3. Creep properties
Fig. 2 . reveals the strain – time curves of the three alloys at 1100 
◦
C/ 
140 MPa. The creep time of the 4.5Al alloy is short, leading it to tran -
sition directly into the accelerated fracture stage without undergoing a 
significant steady-state creep period. This is because the 4.5Al alloy has 
a larger γ’ phase spacing and smaller γ’ phase size, which reduces the 
strength at high temperatures. The high cubicity of the γ’ phase corre -
sponds to a relatively longer fracture life span [ 23 ]. The 5.7Al and 6.4Al 
alloys exhibit a higher degree of cubicity in their γ’ phase, which con -
tributes to a longer fracture life. Both alloys entered the steady-state 
creep phase after decelerated creep and maintained this phase for an 
extended period before accelerating to fracture, indicating three stages 
of creep. Notably, the entire creep test of the 5.7Al alloy lasts approxi -
mately 269 h. The 5.7Al alloy demonstrates lower elongation and 
shrinkage upon fracture during creep tests ( Table 2 ).
In order to further evaluate the creep properties of the experimental 
alloys, the Larson-Miller (L-M) [ 11 ] curve was used to evaluate the creep 
properties of the alloys. The three alloys in this experiment were 
compared with the current commercial 3rd generation Ni-SX alloys 
Ren ´eN6, CMSX-10 and TMS-75 [ 21 , 24 , 25 ], as shown in Fig. 3 (d). It can 
be seen that the creep properties of the 4.5Al alloy are poor and do not 
meet the standard of the 3rd generation Ni-SX alloy. The data points of 
the 5.7Al alloy are above those of the Ren ´eN6 alloy, and the 6.4Al alloy 
data points are basically close to the TMS-75 alloy. This indicates that 
the creep properties of both 5.7Al and 6.4Al alloys reach the level of 3rd 
generation Ni-SX alloys, and that the performance of 5.7Al alloys is 
better, even better than some commercial 3rd generation Ni-SX alloys.
3.4. Microstructure after fracture
The fracture morphologies are shown in Fig. 4 after creep test of the 
three alloys at 1100 
◦
C and 140 MPa. In Fig. 4 (a-f), no distinct dimple 
features of creep fracture are observed in the 4.5Al alloy, and the γ’ 
phase exhibits a well-defined, regular near-circular morphology. The γ’

4.5Al alloy. In contrast, fracture surfaces of the 5.7Al and 6.4Al alloys 
consist of circular dimples. Therefore, in the later stages of creep, the 
dominant mechanism for fracture is ductile type due to micropore 
aggregation, leading to better creep properties for these two alloys.
To further examine the γ’ phase changes during creep test, the lon -
gitudinal section of the fracture was analyzed. Fig. 4 (g-i) shows the 
rafted microstructure of γ’ phase at a position 5 mm away from the 
fracture surface. It could be found that the 4.5Al alloy forms a raft 
structure during the creep test. The direction of rafting is parallel to that 
of stress. It belongs to P-type raft structure. In contrast, during long-term 
creep, the 5.7Al alloy develops a continuous and relatively uniform 
while coarse rafted structure of γ’ phase. And the direction of rafting is 
parallel to that of stress, indicating a raft structure with N-type. As for 
the 6.4Al alloy, the γ’ phase undergoes coarsening, forming a raft 
structure with an indistinct configuration. The angle between the di -
rection of rafting and stress is 45
◦
. Moreover, the TCP phase precipitates 
in the raft structure ( Fig. 4 (i)). In Fig. 4 (j-l), the 4.5Al alloy fractures 
before obvious rafting occurs in the γ’ phase. The fracture even takes 
place directly at the γ’ phase in some areas, because the γ’ phase fails to 
form an effective raft structure to impede the movement of dislocations. 
Stress concentration occurs in the alloy, leading to fracture as a result. 
However, 5.7Al and 6.4Al alloys form coarse raft structures, hindering 
the movement of dislocation effectively, improving the creep life. The

6.4Al alloy by the TCP phase, causing the rafting direction to deviate. 
This diminishes the hindrance to the dislocation movement. In addition, 
the N-type raft structure formed by 5.7Al alloy is more effective in 
hindering dislocation movement and delaying alloy fracture. As a result, 
the creep life of 5.7Al specimen is significantly longer than that of 6.4Al 
specimen.

4. Discussion
4.1. Effect of Al on the morphology of γ ’ phase
As a γ’ phase forming element, the change in Al content directly 
influences the size and precipitation amount of γ’ phase. As the Al 
contents rise, both the volume fraction and size of γ’ phase in nickel- 
based superalloys increase, and its morphology becomes more cubic 
and regular. In this experiment, the Al content varied from 4.5 wt% to 
6.4 wt%, and the volume fraction increased from 58.2 % to 73.3 % after 
heat treatment. However, the degree of cubicalization first increases and 
then decreases. The γ’ phase from the 4.5Al alloy to the 5.7Al alloy 
becomes more cubic as the Al content increases, and its distribution 
becomes more uniform and regular. However, the 6.4Al alloy also 
contains a coarser and more irregular strengthening γ’ phase. This is 
because excess Al at high temperatures increases the γ’ phase precipi -
tation driving force, resulting in the precipitation of more γ’ phase. Over 
time, smaller γ’ phase particles may be absorbed by larger particles, 
forming coarser and more irregular γ’ phases. Based on the study of 
Takao et al. [ 22 ], alloys with high γ’ phase cubicity and volume fractions 
in the range from 60 % to 70 % typically have better creep property. 
Excessive precipitation of the γ’ phase will reduce the area of the γ / γ’ 
phase interface, but weaken the effect of the γ / γ’ phase interface on 
hindering dislocation migration, resulting in a decrease in creep 
strength. When the Al content is increased to 6.4 wt%, a relatively large 
amount of the γ’ phase precipitates. In Fig. 2 (f), some of the coarse γ’ 
phases can be seen to be in a semi-coherent state with the γ matrix. The 
semi-coherent interface hinders the motion of dislocations by interact -
ing with the dislocation network. But during the high temperature creep 
process, the γ’ phase is partially dissolved, resulting in the detachment of 
dislocations at the interface and their subsequent pinning. This phe -
nomenon reduces the creep resistance of the alloy [ 28 ]. And with the 
increase of γ’ phase precipitation, the concentration of local elements for 
forming the TCP phase, such as W, Mo and Re, in the γ matrix increases. 
The brittle TCP phase precipitates under the combined action of tem -
perature and applied stress, thereby reducing the creep properties of the 
alloy [ 29 ]. Therefore, it can be observed in Fig. 4 (l) that there is more 
precipitation of TCP phase in the creep fracture microstructure of 6.4Al 
alloy, which makes the creep life of this alloy shorter than that of 5.7Al 
alloy.

The closer ∣ δ ∣ is to 0, the more spherical the γ’ phase is [ 14 , 32 ]. 
Therefore, the spherical shape of γ’ phase appeared in the 4.5Al alloy. As 
the ∣ δ ∣ increases, the shape is influenced by both interfacial and strain 
energy, causing it to grow along the direction of least resistance. Strain 
energy is closely related to the γ’ phase volume fraction and tends to 
grow along the {001} γ’ plane, resulting in a cubic γ’ phase morphology, 
in the 5.7Al and 6.4Al alloys. When ∣ δ ∣ increases to exceed the elastic 
range, the γ / γ’ phases can no longer maintain a coherent state. This 
allows strain energy to be released, making interfacial energy the 
dominant factor. At this point, the γ’ phase takes on an irregular 
morphology. The larger the ∣ δ ∣ , the higher the cubicity will be as for the 
γ’ phase. However, when ∣ δ ∣ is too large, the morphology of the γ’ phase 
will tend to be irregular [ 1 ]. Nathal et al. [ 13 ] found that nickel-based 
superalloys with good creep life generally have a γ’ phase with a high 
degree of cubicity. The lattice misfit of these γ’ phases usually falls

creep testing. As shown in Fig. 5 , dislocations in these three alloys 
mainly move through cross-slips. When creep starts, few dislocations 
moved in the matrix, allowing dislocations to glide and multiply 
extensively within the matrix. Driven by the applied stress, there is much 
dislocation motion in the a/2 〈 011 〉 slip system on the {111} slip surface,

The creeping mechanisms could be summarized as shown in Fig. 13 . 
The γ’ fails to form adequately in 4.5Al specimen because of the low Al 
content. As a result, an effective rafted structure cannot develop during 
creep to hinder dislocation motion. Additionally, the wider γ channels 
provide ample space for dislocation movement. The low Al content also 
results in a low lattice misfit, leading to the formation of an irregular and 
sparse dislocation network, which is unable to effectively impede 
dislocation motion. In contrast, the 5.7Al alloy has a suitable Al content 
to form a desirable γ’ phase. During creep, a well-ordered rafted struc -
ture forms, and the relatively high lattice misfit degree gives rise to a 
dense and regular dislocation network at the γ / γ’ interface. It impedes 
motion of dislocation, significantly enhancing the creep performance of 
alloys. On the other hand, the 6.4Al alloy, with its higher Al content, 
leads to some course γ’ phases and an uneven distribution of 
strengthening phases. The rafted structures are poorly ordered, and the 
interfacial dislocation networks are less dense and less regular compared 
to the 5.7Al alloy. This diminishes the effectiveness of both the rafted 
structures and dislocation networks in hindering dislocation motion, 
resulting in poorer creep properties for the 6.4Al alloy compared to the 
5.7Al alloy.
5. Conclusion
The effect of Al content (4.5 wt% – 6.4 wt%) on the microstructure 
and creep performances (1100 
◦
C/140 MPa) of 3rd generations of Ni-SX 
alloys was investigated. The differences in microstructure and creep 
performances of the alloys with varying Al contents were characterized 
using SEM and TEM. Additionally, the effect of Al content gradient on 
creep resistance was analyzed by quantitatively calculating the solution 
strengthening index and effective diffusion coefficient. This study rea -
ches the following main conclusions: 
(1) In 3rd generation Ni-SX alloys, appropriately increasing the Al 
content (4.5 wt% – 5.7 wt%) can increase the γ’ phase content, 
reduce its size, and improve its regularity and cubic morphology. 
And it increased the γ / γ’ misfit, improves the creep resistance of 
alloys. However, excessively high Al content (5.7 wt% – 6.4 wt%) 
results in coarser γ’ phase and a reduction in lattice misfit. This 
reduces the creep life of alloys.
(2) During creeping, the 4.5Al alloy fails to form an adequate γ’ 
caused by insufficient Al content. The dislocation network is 
sparse and irregular, allowing dislocation motion to occur 
without hindrance, leading to rapid fracture. The 5.7Al alloy, 
however, exhibits a well-developed, regular rafted structure. The 
interfacial dislocation network is dense and regular, which im -
proves creep resistance, resulting in a creep rupture life of 269.05 
h. The excessively high Al content in the 6.4Al alloy causes some 
of the γ’ phase to grow coarsely. During creeping, the rafted 
structure is disorderly and irregular, unable to effectively 
enhance the alloy ’ s creep resistance. Consequently, its creep life 
is shorter than that of the 5.7Al alloy.
(3) The solid solution strengthening and effective diffusion coeffi -
cient were obtained through a computational model. The results 
indicate appropriate Al content could enhance the solid solution 
strengthening by reducing the elemental diffusion. However, an 
excessive addition of Al content will instead lead to negative 
impacts.
