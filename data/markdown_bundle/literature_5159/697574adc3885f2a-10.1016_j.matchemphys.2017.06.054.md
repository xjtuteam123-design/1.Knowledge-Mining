abstract
The synthesis and characterization of Ti xWTaVCr alloys for high-temperature and fusion plasma-facing
applications are presented in this study. Specimens of Ti xWTaVCr containing 0 to 7 at% Ti were pre-
pared via spark plasma sintering of an elemental powder mixture at 1500 /C14 C. Characterization of sintered
samples via X-ray diffraction and scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy
revealed increased formation of a BCC solid solution due to the addition of up to 7 at% Ti. Mechanical
characterizations of the Ti xWTaVCr alloys indicate that they are harder and stronger than several re-
ported high-entropy alloys. The alloy that contained 7 at% Ti also exhibited improved oxidation resis-
tance in air at 1000
/C14 C. Its improved strength, hardness, and oxidation resistance suggest future
applications for this high-entropy alloy in high-temperature and nuclear applications.

1. Introduction
Fusion power plants will require innovative materials to with-
stand unforeseen thermo-mechanical loads and irradiation. These
transient conditions will expose the critical components of fusion
reactors to unique service environments [1]. For example, the heat
load on the ITER diverter may rise as high as 40 MJ/m 2 [2]. There-
fore, innovative high-temperature and plasma-facing materials are
required.
Tungsten (W) is an effective future fusion application material,
as it exhibits a refractory nature along with low erosion, suppressed

to the use of W include radiation-induced embrittlement and its

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have several characteristics that are
better than those of conventional alloys. These include high me-
chanical strength, hardness, and thermal stability, along with good
corrosion, oxidation, and creep resistances [12]. This suggests that
they may be effective in high-temperature and plasma-facing ap-
plications [13]. The development of W-containing, refractory, high-
entropy alloys with enhanced properties such as MoNbTaW,
MoNbTaVW [14], and other refractory HEAs including CrMo 0.5Nb-
Ta0.5TiZr, Cr xNbTiVyZr [13], CrNbTiVZr, CrNbTiZr [12] and
TaNbHfZrTi [13], produced by methods such as arc melting [15],
conventional sintering [16], physical vapor deposition [17], and
mechanical alloying [18] have been reported. However, current
research lacks information about high-entropy alloys that consist of
refractory metals with the low neutron activation properties
necessary to minimize hazards from the irradiated materials, and
thus enable maintenance and recycling [19]. This paper presents
reduced-activation Ti xWTaVCr alloys developed via elemental
powder mixing followed by spark plasma sintering.
Owing to the segregation that occurs during melting [20],
porosity from conventional sintering [21], thickness limits associ-
ated with physical vapor deposition [17], and contamination and
oxidation [22] from mechanical alloying, elemental powders were
mixed in a Turbula shaker mixer. The mixture was sintered into
high-density bulk samples in a few minutes via spark plasma sin-
tering (SPS), which offers short soaking times and high heating and
cooling rates [23].
The constituent elements were carefully selected to provide
compositions with reduced activation behaviors. The purpose of
this was to minimize activation of the alloys due to exposure to the
neutron ﬂuxes produced in deuterium-tritium fusion reactions
[24]. W, Ta, V, Cr, and Ti were used to develop HEAs that combine
strength with irradiation and oxidation resistances, while ensuring
reduced activation properties [25]. However, enough Ti content
was provided for solid solution formation to avoid signi ﬁcant re-
ductions in mechanical properties [26]. The development of
TixWTaVCr and the effect of Ti addition are presented in this paper.
2. Experimental
To fabricate the Ti xWTaVCr samples, 99.9% pure W (1.21 mm
from Daegu Tek. Co.), V, Cr, Ti ( <75 mm, 63 mm, and 45 mm,
respectively, from Kojundo Korea) and Ta (<45 mm, from Seo Gwang
Metal) powders were mixed using a Turbular shaker-mixer in
plastic vials at 30 RPM for up to 3 h. Steel balls were added in a 1:1
ratio with the powder to ensure rigorous mixing. SPS (SPS, Dr.
Sintering SPS-515S, Japan) was carried out in a carbon felt-covered
graphite mold to prevent heat loss [37]. Prior to sintering at
150 0
/C14 C, the 1300 /C14 C and 1400 /C14 C sintering temperatures were also
tested. A temperature of 1500 /C14 C and axial pressure of 50 MPa were
applied in a vacuum (10 /C0 3 torr) for up to 10 min. The detailed
sintering process and size of sintered samples are shown in Fig. 1.
Gas fusion analysis (ONH-2000 & CS-2000, ELTRA, Germany)
and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry
(ICP-OES, iCAP 6300 Duo, Thermo Scienti ﬁc Co., UK) were per-
formed to determine the actual compositions of the alloy samples.
Archimedes' principle was used to measure density. The crystallo-
graphic and microscopic characteristics of the samples were stud-
ied via X-ray diffraction (XRD, D/MAX-2500, RIGAKU, USA),
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI Magellan 400, USA), and
transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM2100F, JEOL Ltd.,
Japan). The chemical homogeneity and phase content were
analyzed via energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, FEI Magellan
400, USA). Electron probe microanalysis (EPMA 1610, AE1 1, Shi-
madzu, Japan) was performed to determine the compositions of
various phases.
Disk-shaped sintered samples were cut through their cross-
sections for micro-Vickers hardness tests (402MVD, Wolpert Wil-
son Instruments, Germany). Room temperature compression tests
on cylindrical samples with 6 mm in height and 4 mm in diameter
were performed using an Instron 5982 (Instron, USA) machine with
a strain rate of 10 /C0 3/s. A Gleeble 3500 (Dynamic Systems Inc., USA)
system was used with cylindrical samples 6 mm in height and
3 mm in diameter for high-temperature (1200 /C14 C) compression
tests in Ar at the same strain rate (10 /C0 3/s). Thermogravimetric
analyses were performed to determine sample weight gain during
oxidation at 1000 /C14 C in air for up to 3 h. The oxidized samples were
examined via XRD and SEM-EDS for oxide layer characterization.

3.2. Microstructural characterization
Fig. 2(a) and (b) show XRD patterns of the powdered (xTi p) and
sintered samples (xTi s), respectively. Fig. 2 (a) clearly shows sepa-
rate peaks for all the elements in the powdered mixture. Reaction
between the powders due to Ti addition is evident from Fig. 2 (b).
0Tis does not form a solid solution after sintering at 1500 /C14 C, and
remains unalloyed even with the addition of 4 at% Ti. However, it
converts into a BCC solid solution via a liquid phase sintering when
the Ti content is increased to 7 at% (see pattern 7Ti s in Fig. 2 (b))
[28]. The peaks from (1 10), (200), and (21 1) at 40 /C14 ,5 8 /C14 , and 73 /C14 ,
respectively indicate a BCC crystal structure similar to that of W in
the majority of the sintered sample. Such patterns have been pre-
viously observed in refractory alloys [29] and HEAs [30,31].
The microstructures of the as-polished xTi s samples captured
using backscattered electrons exhibit multiple phases (black, dark
gray, and light gray) in a bright matrix. The elemental distribution
determined via EDS reveals W, V, and Cr enrichment in various
regions, as shown in Fig. S1 in Supplementary information. In
addition, Ti-rich phases were observed in samples prepared with
4 at% and 7 at% Ti. The volume fractions of the Ti-rich phases must
have been lower than the XRD detection limit, as no Ti-rich phase
was observed in the XRD patterns [29].
The increase in microstructural homogeneity with the Ti con-
tent suggests that reaction between powders because of the for-
mation of a liquid phase. This can be attributed to formation of
localized Ti-Cr and Ti-Cr-V-based alloys, which melt at
1400e160 0 /C14 C [32,33]. Therefore, localized melting enhances re-
action between powders by accelerating diffusion [22]. Moreover,
the relatively high Ti diffusion coef ﬁcient [28] also accounts for
enhanced diffusion and sintering in Ti-bearing compositions. Re-
action sintering is also evident from the increased density
mentioned in Table 1.

sample. The addition of 4 at% Ti produces a microstructure with

7Tis), respectively. The detailed grain size distribution of xTi s

3.3. Mechanical properties
Hardness was assessed by subjecting the cross-sections of the
sintered samples to the micro-Vickers hardness test. At least ten
readings were taken to determine the representative hardnesses.
As shown in Fig. 5 (a), the hardness of 0Ti s is 714 HV, which is
signiﬁcantly higher than that of pure W. Adding up to 7 at% Ti
converts the sample to a BCC solid solution; however, no signiﬁcant
change in hardness is observed. Adding Ti decreases the mechan-
ical properties of W alloys [26] and high-entropy alloys [46].
However, addition of up to 7 at% Ti causes no signi ﬁcant change in
the hardness of xTi s. The 7Ti s sample is harder than several other
refractory, HEAs ( Table S1 in Supplementary information).
As shown in Fig. 5 (b), the compressive strengths of the xTi s
range from 2327 to 2034 MPa and 979 e586 MPa at room tem-
perature and 1200 /C14 C, respectively. The higher strength of 0Ti s is
attributed to the Laves phases [47] observed via XRD ( Fig. 2 ). The
remarkable strength of 0Tis is also attributed to the relatively high V
[48] and Cr contents [49] of the sintered sample ( Table 1 ), which
promote solid solution strengthening. Ti addition is known to
negatively affect the mechanical strengths of W alloys [26] and
HEAs [46]. Accordingly, adding 4 at% and 7 at% Ti gradually reduces
material strength, as shown in Fig. 5 (b). Although the grain
reﬁnement due to the addition of Ti gives rise to grain size
strengthening [40]. However, solid solution strengthening, which is
known to be the main cause of exceptional mechanical properties
of HEAs, dominates the mechanical properties of xTis [40].
Slight reductions in the amounts of Cr and V upon transitioning
from 0Tis to 7Tis (Table 1) also shed light on strength reduction due
to solid solution strengthening. Moreover, the elimination of Laves
because of sintering promotion via Ti-addition results in relatively
low-strength 7Ti s samples. Even after this strength reduction, the
BCC solid solution of Ti xWTaVCr that contains 7 at% Ti (denoted as
7Tis) remains stronger than several other Ti-containing, refractory,
HEAs ( Table S1 in Supplementary information). This indicates the
potential of this alloy system for future use in challenging
applications.
The fracture strains of 0Ti s, 4Tis, and 7Ti s, in ambient and
elevated temperature compression tests were 1 e2% with insignif-
icant and abrupt variations. Further addition of Ti (even up to 20 at
%) did not cause any signi ﬁcant improvement in strain. This is in
contrast to the results obtained for somewhat similar HEAs,
NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW[14]. The addition of 20 at% and 16.6 at%
Ti to NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW to form TiNbMoTaW and TiVNb-
MoTaW improved the fracture strains of the alloys from 2.6% to
14.1% and 1.7% to 10.6%, respectively [14]. Chromium and its alloys

addition of Cr to from AlCr 0.5NbTiV [51] and Ti 20Zr20Hf20Nb20Cr20,
respectively [52]. The TiNbMoTaW and TiVNbMoTaW HEAs lack in

3.4. Oxidation resistance
Fig. 6 summarizes the oxidation behaviors of xTi s samples with
the same surface areas that were subjected to 1000 /C14 C air for up to
3 h. The weight gain started at a high rate, but decreased steadily
after ~10 min because the oxide layer forms via solid state diffusion
[54]. The positive slope of the weight gain curve indicates the
absence of metal evaporation during oxidation [54]. The 0Ti s
sample gains ~75 mg/cm 2 after 3 h. However, a signi ﬁcant reduc-
tion in weight gain is observed in the 7Ti s sample under the same
experimental conditions, as shown in Fig. 6 .
The effectiveness of Ti in improving the oxidation resistance of
xTis is in line with research conducted by Liu et al. [55], in which the
presence of Ti signi ﬁcantly decreased the oxidation resistance of
NbCrMoTiAl0.5 [55]. In xTi s, the addition of 7 at% Ti not only pro-
vides the sample with another oxide-forming element, but also
produces a near equiatomic single phase with 5 elements (the HEA
condition) that exhibits better oxidation resistance than conven-
tional alloys [56] and higher concentrations of oxide layer-forming
elements [57]. The 7Tis alloy exhibits improved oxidation resistance
as compare to 0Ti s and shows weight gain up to ~30 mg/cm 2
(45 mg/cm 2 less than 0Ti s). The weight gain in 7Ti s, which is rela-
tively higher than that of MoWAlCrTi [54], can be reduced further

This study showed that the addition of 7 at% Ti to a Ti xWTaVCr
alloy can lead to the formation of a BCC solid solution ( >95 vol% by
image analysis) via SPS of an elemental powder mixture. The
resulting materials exhibit lower activation characteristics and
higher strengths and hardnesses (at both room temperature and
high temperatures) than several other refractory HEAs, as well as
better oxidation resistance than Ti-free samples. Thus, Ti xWTaVCr
has the potential for use in high-temperature and plasma-facing
applications.
4. Conclusion
The effect of Ti on the sintering and properties of Ti xWTaVCr
have been explored. Adding 7 at% Ti helped form solid solutions in
the majority of the sample via enhanced diffusion. The room
temperature and high-temperature compressive yield strengths of
the BCC alloy TixWTaVCr (x ¼ 7 at%) were higher than those of pure
W and several other refractory, HEAs owing to solid solution
strengthening and the effects of Cr and V. Improved oxidation
resistance was observed at 1000 /C14 C owing to the formation of
thinner non volatile oxide layers (from ~1.16 mm (0Tis) to ~0.22 mm
(7Tis)). The improved mechanical and oxidation properties of
TixWTaVCr indicate the potential for future use in fusion
applications.
