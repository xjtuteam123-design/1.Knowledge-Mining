ABSTRACT
In this study, the effect of sintering temperature on the microstructure evolution and mechanical properties of 
Al
0.5
CoCrFeNi high-entropy alloy (HEA) was systematically investigated using Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS). By 
combining experimental methods and molecular dynamics simulations, the densification behavior, phase 
composition, grain size, dislocation density, and mechanical properties of the high entropy alloy sintered at 
900 
◦
C, 1000 
◦
C and 1100 
◦
C were studied. The experimental results showed that with increasing sintering 
temperature, the porosity decreased significantly (fully densified at 1100 
◦
C), the content of the BCC phase 
decreased and the content of the FCC phase increased, while the average grain size increased markedly. The 
mechanical properties exhibited significant temperature dependence. The HEA exhibited optimal mechanical 
performance at a sintering temperature of 1000 
◦
C, achieving a yield strength of 717 MPa and a tensile strength 
of 1022 MPa. The yield strength and tensile strength at 1000 
◦
C increased by 54.5 % and 68.0 %, respectively, 
compared with those of the sample sintered at 900 
◦
C. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations revealed that 
increasing sintering temperature accelerates densification by enhancing atomic diffusion, which evolves from 
surface diffusion at lower temperatures to grain boundary diffusion and ultimately to bulk (lattice) diffusion at 
higher temperatures. Theoretical analysis indicates that the sintering temperature optimizes the synergy between 
strength and ductility of HEA by dislocation evolution, recrystallization, and phase transformation, thereby 
providing a theoretical foundation for the process design of Al
0.5
CoCrFeNi HEA.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEA), as a new class of alloy materials, have 
attracted significant attention due to their unique composition and 
structural characteristics. These HEA exhibit exceptional mechanical 
properties, good high-temperature resistance, and stability in extreme 
environments, making them a hot topic in current materials research [ 1 ,
2 ]. Due to the multi-principal-element nature of the alloy system, HEA 
experience four major effects, including high-entropy effect, lattice 
distortion, slow diffusion, and the “ cocktail effect ” . The high-entropy 
effect suppresses the precipitation of intermetallic compounds, the lat -
tice distortion effect enhances solid solution strengthening, the slow 
diffusion effect delays grain coarsening, and the cocktail effect optimizes 
functional properties. These effects contribute to the unique perfor -
mance of HEA, including excellent strength and hardness, good 
structural stability and ductility, as well as outstanding corrosion 
resistance, wear resistance, and oxidation resistance. The perfect com -
bination of all these properties enables HEA to show great potential for 
applications in aerospace industries, coating applications, and the 
electronics industry [ 3 – 12 ].
Traditional casting and arc melting processes tend to cause compo -
sitional segregation and grain coarsening in alloys [ 13 , 14 ]. In contrast, 
Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS) technology, which utilizes pulsed electric 
current and uniaxial pressure, enables rapid densification at lower 
temperatures, suppressing grain growth and resulting in a uniform 
microstructure and excellent mechanical properties [ 15 ]. For instance, 
Xiang et al. successfully prepared the NbTaTiZr HEA using SPS, 
achieving a compressive fracture strength of 2274 ± 91 MPa and a yield 
strength of 2172 ± 47 MPa, respectively, which significantly out -
performed the cast NbTaTiZr high entropy alloy [ 16 ]. However,

influences the diffusion behavior of powder particles, phase trans -

while high-temperature sintering may lead to grain coarsening or

In the study of high entropy alloys, molecular dynamics (MD) sim -
ulations have become an important tool for revealing their micro- 
mechanical behaviors [ 18 ]. MD simulations play an irreplaceable role, 
especially in analyzing the micro-mechanical behavior of HEA and the 
sintering dynamics mechanisms [ 19 ]. For example, Sahni et al. revealed 
the melting and sintering behavior of Fe35Ni nanoparticles through MD 
simulations, finding that the atomic diffusion rate increased by a factor 
of 10 at high temperatures, with surface diffusion and grain boundary 
diffusion dominating the sintering process, thus laying the foundation 
for multi-scale sintering modeling [ 20 ]. Liu et al. used MD simulations to 
uncover the three-stage mechanism of neck growth during the sintering 
of Fe nanoparticles, discovering that the sintering temperature was 
linearly correlated with particle size [ 21 ]. They also clarified the atomic 
migration mechanisms at different stages through atomic displacement 
and structural analysis. These atomic-scale dynamic observations pro -
vide theoretical support for optimizing the sintering process parameters 
of high entropy alloys and controlling multi-component diffusion paths.
Recent progress has also been made in understanding the mechanical 
properties and deformation mechanisms of HEAs. Liu et al. investigated 
the hot deformation behavior of AlCoCrFeNi HEAs and highlighted that 
FCC/BCC interfaces and B2 substructures play a crucial role in strain 
hardening, while developing an evolutionary neural network model for 
accurate prediction of flow stress [ 22 ]. In addition, molecular dynamics 
simulations on nanocrystalline HEAs revealed that plastic deformation 
arises from the combined action of dislocation glide, grain 
boundary-mediated processes, twinning, and stacking faults, with a 
pronounced grain size effect [ 23 ]. These findings provide important 
atomistic insights into the deformation mechanisms of HEAs, particu -
larly at the nanoscale, and further complement the current under -
standing of their microstructure – property relationships.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructural analysis
Fig. 4 shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Al
0.5
CoCrFeNi 
HEA sintered at different temperatures using SPS. It can be observed that 
Al
0.5
CoCrFeNi HEA exhibits the characteristics of a simple solid solution 
in Fig. 4 (a), consisting of both FCC and BCC phases. The FCC phase 
shows three diffraction peaks, namely (111), (200), and (220), while the 
BCC phase only displays a single (110) diffraction peak. These results are 
consistent with those reported in the literature [ 31 ]. Fig. 4 (b) presents a 
magnified view, further analysis reveals that as the sintering tempera -
ture increases, the intensity of the BCC phase diffraction peaks signifi -
cantly decreases, indicating a transformation from BCC to FCC during 
high-temperature sintering. Meanwhile, the diffraction peaks corre -
sponding to the FCC phase shift to a lower 2 θ angle as the sintering 
temperature increases, indicating lattice expansion. This is due to the 
accelerated diffusion of the alloying elements in the high entropy alloy 
at higher sintering temperatures, which intensifies lattice distortion.
Fig. 5 shows BSE images of Al
0.5
CoCrFeNi HEA sintered at different 
temperatures via SPS, illustrating the microstructural evolution of the 
HEA. Evidently, all samples exhibit a uniformly distributed dual-phase 
structure, characterized by bright and gray contrast regions, which is 
a typical feature of the Al-Co-Cr-Fe-Ni HEA system [ 32 ]. Numerous 
irregular pores are clearly observed in the alloy sample sintered at 
900 
◦
C in Fig. 5 (a – d). These pores result from insufficient atomic 
diffusion of the alloy at low temperatures, which hinders neck growth 
between powder particles and limits pore closure [ 33 ]. The number of 
pores is significantly reduced in the alloy sample sintered at 1000 
◦
C in 
Fig. 5 (b – e). The pores are nearly eliminated in the alloy sample sintered 
at 1100 
◦
C in Fig. 5 (c – f), indicating that the alloy has reached a highly 
densified state. This phenomenon indicates that increasing the sintering 
temperature can significantly reduce the porosity and enhance the 
densification.
Fig. 6 shows the EDS surface scan image of the Al
0.5
CoCrFeNi HEA 
prepared at a sintering temperature of 1100 
◦
C. Fig. 6 (a) is the BSE 
image and Fig. 6 (b – f) are the elemental mapping images. Obviously, the 
distribution of elements in the alloy is relatively uniform, exhibiting the 
characteristics of a homogeneous solid solution. Mapping analysis in -
dicates that the gray phase is enriched in Al and Ni and the white phase 
is rich in Fe, Co, and Cr elements. The gray phase content is relatively 
low, while the white phase content is higher in Fig. 6 (a). In combination 
with the XRD results, it can be inferred that the gray phase is primarily 
the BCC phase, while the white phase is the FCC phase. This phenom -
enon is closely related to the atomic characteristics of Al and Ni. Al has a 
larger atomic radius, and the relatively large negative mixing enthalpy 
between Al and Ni induces local lattice distortion, promoting the for -
mation of the BCC phase. In contrast, Fe, Co, and Cr, with their smaller 
atomic radii and higher valence electron concentration (VEC), tend to 
stabilize the FCC matrix. These results are consistent with the “ multi- 
principal element synergistic phase separation ” mechanism proposed by 
Anber et al., where the addition of Al drives the precipitation of the BCC 
phase through size effects and chemical bonding, while the Fe-Co-Cr 
ternary system stabilizes the FCC matrix through valence electron con -
centration [ 34 ].

The Al

CoCrFeNi HEA is primarily composed of an FCC solid- 
solution phase. The FCC structure has 12 close-packed {111} 〈 110 〉 slip 
systems that are readily activated at room temperature, characterized by 
low critical resolved shear stress and facile dislocation mobility. As a 
result, the FCC phase contributes to good plasticity and ductility, albeit 
at the expense of lower strength [ 47 ]. In addition, a minor fraction of the 
BCC solid-solution phase is also present. Although BCC crystals possess a 
larger set of crystallographically permitted slip families (e.g., {110} 〈 
111 〉 , {112} 〈 111 〉 , {123} 〈 111 〉 ), the activity of these systems is
