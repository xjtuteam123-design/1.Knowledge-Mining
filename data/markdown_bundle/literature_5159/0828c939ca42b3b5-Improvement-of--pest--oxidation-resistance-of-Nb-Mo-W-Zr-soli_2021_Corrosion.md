1. Introduction 
Niobium (Nb) base alloys can be used as optimal hot-ended com -
ponents material in the combustion chamber and nozzle of rocket en -
gines due to the advantages of low density, high melting point and 
excellent mechanical properties at higher temperatures [ 1 – 4 ].With the 
service life of the new generation of rocket engines being prolonged to a 
few hour level, the oxidation resistance properties of rocket engines in 
the moderate temperature interval between 600
◦
C and 800
◦
C during 
the cruise stage is required to be higher [ 5 , 6 ]. However, the Nb base 
alloys may suffer catastrophic oxidation “ pest ” in this temperature range 
[ 7 – 9 ]. Therefore, improving “ pest ” oxidation resistance at moderate 
temperatures has become critical issue for the implication of the Nb base 
alloys. 
Generally, there are two main approaches to solve this problem. The 
first one is to add active elements (such as Al, Ti, Hf, ect.) to the Nb base 
alloys. However, this strategy just plays a limit role in improving the 
oxidation resistance [ 10 , 11 ], and sometimes degrades the mechanical 
properties of Nb [ 12 , 13 ]. For instance, Nb-22Ti-16Si-2Hf-2Al-xCr sam -
ples exhibited better oxidation resistance at 1250
◦
C when Cr, the most 
beneficial element to oxidation resistance through formation of Cr
2
Nb 
intermetallic compound, increased from 2 at.% to 17 at.%, whereas the 
room temperature fracture toughness decreased from 14.4 MPa m
1/2 
to 
10.3 MPa m
1/2 
due to increase of the brittle phase fraction, and the 
compressive strength at 1350
◦
C decreased from 300 MPa to 80 MPa 
[ 12 ] because of a decrease in the melting point of the alloy by the 
addition of Cr. Another method is to design the oxidation-resistant 
coatings (such as silicide and aluminide coatings) to protect Nb alloys 
from oxidation at elevated temperatures [ 14 , 15 ]. Aluminide coating can 
form a dense Al
2
O
3 
oxide scale in atmosphere at high temperatures to 
prevent further oxidation of substrate [ 16 ]. Unfortunately, the alumi -
nide coatings have poor mechanical properties at high temperature and 
are easy to spallation at inter-diffusion layer [ 17 ]. This may expose the 
substrate in air and result in quick “ pest ” of the substrate, which limits 
the applications of aluminide coatings. The silicide coatings have been 
extensively studied for their strong bonding with substrate and 
self-healing behaviours [ 9 , 18 ]. It was reported that the Nb-Si based alloy 
with Mo-62Si-5B coating showed a mass gain of 0.41 mg/cm
2 
after 
oxidation at 1250
◦
C for 100 h [ 14 ]. However, the silicide coatings are 
still brittle at room temperature [ 19 ] and also show “ pest ” phenomenon 
at the moderate temperature [ 20 ], which means that the silicides are 
unsuitable for being protective coatings on the Nb base alloys [ 21 ]. 
Therefore, it is necessary to find a new coating to protect Nb from “ pest ” 
failure at the moderate temperatures. 
Among various coating techniques, nitridation is commonly 
employed to improve the surface hardness, wear resistance, as well as 
oxidation resistance of metals [ 22 – 26 ]. It was reported that the nitri -
dation increased the oxidation resistance of 304 steel at 800

C because 
of the rapid formation of protective Cr
2

of oxidation [ 27 ]. Moreover, the nitride layer promoted the oxidation 
resistance of pure Ti, Ti-6Al-4 V (at.%) and Ti-7Nb (at.%) alloys, while 
the improvement degree depended on the alloy composition and the

2. Experimental procedures 
2.1. Nitridation processing 
The rolled commercial Nb521 alloy with a nominal composition (at. 
%) of Nb-5W-2Mo-1Zr was selected as substrate alloy. The fan-shaped 
samples, 10 mm in radius, 3 mm in height and the central angle is 90 
degrees, cut from Nb521 bar, were mechanically ground using SiC paper 
(1500-grit) with water and cleaned ultrasonically in ethanol before 
nitridation. The nitridation processing was conducted in a tube furnace 
filled with flowing atmosphere consisting of nitrogen, argon and 
hydrogen, in which hydrogen and argon were used to consume residual 
oxygen in the furnace and adjust the atmosphere ratio, respectively. To 
control the proportion of atmosphere in the furnace, the two ends of the 
tubular furnace were connected with air passages and the flow rates of 
the atmosphere were controlled by three flow meters, respectively. 
During the entire nitriding process, the flow rate was 1 L/min for ni -
trogen, 0.5 L/min for argon and 0.1 L/min for hydrogen. Before the 
nitridation processing, the samples were placed on an alumina crucible 
with rectangular holes drilled at the bottom to allow the samples to 
stand in the trough so that the same nitriding degree could be obtained 
on both sides of the samples. Then, the combination of crucible and 
samples was placed in the furnace and a vacuum pump was used to 
pump the furnace to negative pressure (8 Pa), followed by the flow gas 
(gas composition N
2
+ 31.25 %Ar + 6.25 %H
2
) was injected. When the gas 
pressure in the furnace exceeded atmospheric pressure, the furnace 
outlet valve was opened, so that the gas in the furnace maintained cir -
culation. The samples were nitrided at 1200
◦
C for 12 h, and finally with 
furnace cooling to room temperature. 
2.2. Oxidation tests 
The bare and nitrided samples were isothermally oxidized at 800
◦
C 
for 100 h using an open-ended tube furnace in air. After cleaned ultra -
sonically in ethanol, the samples were held in an alumina crucible and 
placed in the uniform hot zone of the furnace so that the total weight 
change dependent on the oxidation time could be obtained. The weight 
gains of the oxidation samples were measured at the intervals of 1, 2, 3, 
4, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 h using an analytical balance with an accuracy 
of 10
 5 
g (Model CPA225D, Germany). Three samples were used at each 
time to get the average weight gains value. In order to investigate the 
mechanism of the nitride layer impeding oxidation and the structural 
evolution of the oxide layer, five additional samples of each bare and 
nitrided alloy were isothermally static oxidized at 800
◦
C for 1, 2, 5, 20, 
50 h, respectively. 
2.3. Characterizations 
The bare and nitrided samples, as well as the oxidized samples, were 
mounted in cold-setting epoxy resin followed by ground to 2000-grit SiC 
paper with water and polished with 50 nm aqueous silica colloidal 
suspension. X-ray diffraction (XRD, Model D/M-2500PC Rigaku, Japan) 
using Cu K α radiation was used to identify phase constitutions of the 
samples under different states. Microstructural evolution of the nitride 
layer and oxide products were determined by field emission scanning 
electron microscopy (SEM, Model 200 F, Holland) with Energy Disper -
sive Spectroscopy (EDS). The element distributions in the cross-sections 
of the nitrided samples before and after oxidation were investigated by 
using an Electron Probe Micro Analysis (EPMA, Model JXA-8530 F, 
Japan) with wave dispersive spectroscopy (WDS). 
To further observe the microstructures of the nitride layer and the 
oxide products formed on the substrate during the nitridation and 
oxidation and accurately identify their specific phase structures, the 
regions of interest of the samples were made into Transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM) samples. The TEM samples of two areas of interest 
(see yellow boxes in Figs. 5 and 14 ) were prepared using a focused ion 
beam (FIB, TESCAN LYRA 3, Czech Republic). After the interesting areas 
were processed by ion beam, a miniature mechanical arm moved them 
to a copper grid and fixed it with platinum, and then the samples were 
further milled by ion beam to remove the surface amorphous layer. The 
obtained thinning samples were analyzed using a field emission trans -
mission electron microscopy (FEI TECNAI G
2
F20, America) with Oxford 
INCA EDS detector.

4.3. Mechanism of the nitride layer preventing oxidation 
It can be seen from Figs. 6 and 8 that the nitrided Nb521 sample 
shows better oxidation resistance than the bare sample. Table 3 still 
summarizes the standard Gibbs free energy of the Nb
2
N reacting with O 
for comparison. More energy and greater O content (corresponding to 
the higher O partial pressure, see Table 3 ) are needed to meet the 
requirement of the reaction of the Nb
2
N with O (Eq. (7) ) rather than 
those of the dominant elements Nb and W with O (Eqs. (3) and (4) ). 
Therefore, the nitride layer cannot be oxidized easily, which indicates 
that the nitrides are more stable when at the atmosphere and high 
temperatures, as compared with the Nb
SS 
alloy. This is the first factor 
why the nitridation treatment is an effective technique to prevent the 
Nb521 substrate from attack of O in the atmosphere. 
Secondly, the nitride layer may further impede O diffusing toward 
the substrate to suppress the oxidation. Fig. 17 b and d show the 
elemental concentration profiles at various depths in the cross-sectional 
images of Fig.17 a and c of the bare and nitrided Nb521 samples after 
oxidation for 2 h, respectively. In order to obtain a more accurate 
composition, five positions at the same depth were selected and detected 
by EPMA, and naturally, the O content decreases with an increase of the 
depth. Comparison reveals that for any position with the same distance 
from the front of the oxide layer, the O content in the nitrided sample is 
always less than that in the bare sample. For instance, at the point just 
beneath the oxide scale (position A in Fig. 17 a) the O content is 
approximately 50.68 at.% for the bare sample and that is 23.35 at.% 
(position B in Fig. 17 c) for the nitrided sample. This suggests that the 
formation of the nitride layer can reduce the rate of O diffusion into the 
substrate, thereby improving the oxidation resistance of the Nb521 
alloy. It should be emphasized that this mechanism is also available in 
the grain boundaries (GBs) because of existence of the nitrides in the 
grain boundaries. The nitrides in the grain boundaries may also play a 
significantly role in impeding the diffusion of O or the oxidation, 
because the diffusion of O along the grain boundary is quite faster than 
that along the grain interior. 
Finally, the nitrides in the grain boundaries and the LEC zones may 
act as “ mechanical locking ” to avoid spallation of the oxide products at 
early stage of oxidation and “ pest ” at long time oxidation ( Figs. 8 and 
10 ). As shown in Fig. 18 , the nitrides in the grain boundaries (A, B, C in 
Fig. 18 ) and the LEC zones obviously connect with the oxide layer, 
without “ pest ” and spallation of the oxide layer in the nitrided samples 
( Fig. 8 b) when oxidized for 5 h even though short cracks parallel to the 
sample surface frequently arising within the oxide layer ( Figs. 10 b, e, h 
and 18 ). This phenomenon reveals that the nitrides hinder diffusion of O 
toward the front of the horizontal crack tip (proved by lower O content 
in the nitride layer, see Tables in Fig. 17 ) and postpones further prop -
agation of the crack and spallation of the oxide products enforced by 
oxidation until oxidation for 20 h ( Fig. 8 c). Combining the aforemen -
tioned mechanisms of the nitride layer and the role of “ mechanical 
locking ” of the nitrides in the grain boundaries ( Fig. 18 ) and the LECs 
( Fig. 10 l, m and o), we can conclude that the “ pest ” of the oxide products 
is significantly suppressed by the nitrides ( Fig. 8 d), and the nitridation is 
beneficial to improve the oxidation resistance of the Nb521 alloy. For 
the bare sample, the phenomena that heavy spallation of the oxide 
products ( Fig. 8 f), crack continuous propagation toward the substrate 
( Fig. 9 d), as well as full “ pest ” at oxidation for 10 h ( Fig. 8 g), strongly 
confirm much poor oxidation resistance of the bare sample.
