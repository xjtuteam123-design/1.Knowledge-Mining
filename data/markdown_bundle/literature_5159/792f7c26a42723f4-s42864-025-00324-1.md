Abstract
To meet the increasing demand for continuously enhancing engineering performance and energy efficiency in a variety of 
aerospace and energy applications, structural materials with high strength at ultra-high temperatures are urgently required. 
In the past decade, refractory multi-principal element alloys (RMPEAs), particularly those containing elements with high 
melting temperatures (T m) such as W and Ta (hereafter denoted as WTa-RMPEAs), have garnered extensive interest due 
to their exceptional strengths and thermally stability at high temperatures. Characterized by high T m, sluggish diffusion, 
and severe lattice distortion, WTa-RMPEAs exhibit chemical composition fluctuations at different scales, leading to unique 
mechanical properties and deformation behavior. In this paper, an initial summary is provided of the mechanical proper -
ties of typical WTa-RMPEAs at room and high temperatures and the differences in deformation behavior and underlying 
mechanisms between RMPEAs and conventional alloys are discussed. Additionally, strengthening and toughening strate-
gies and the suggested deformation mechanisms were reviewed. Finally, the challenges in revealing the actual deformation 
mechanism of WTa-RMPEAs were described, and a brief perspective on the future research of the mechanical behavior of 
WTa-RMPEAs was proposed.

1 Introduction
The development of emerging gas turbines, aviation-engines, 
and hypersonic applications urgently requires high-perfor -
mance hot-end materials that exhibit exceptional properties 
at both ambient and elevated temperatures. For example, 
the efficiency and performance of gas-turbine engines are 
strongly dependent on the highest temperature within the 
engine-the inlet temperature of the high-pressure turbine 
(HPT) [1]. The hotter the engine, the higher the efficiency 
(Fig. 1a). Modern gas-turbine engines have required HPTs 
to withstand temperatures exceeding 1600 °C [2]. Similarly, 
when the flight speed is above Mach 7 (i.e., seven times 
the speed of sound), the leading-edge temperature of the 
National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) 
X-43 hypersonic vehicle exceeds 1600 °C (Fig.  1b) [3 ], 
which poses significant challenges for the vehicle’s 
materials.
The selection of alternative hot-end materials is limited 
by various performance criteria, with mechanical proper -
ties being the most crucial factor for structure materials. 
As shown in Fig.  1c, d, there have been several categories 
of high-temperature structural materials. Ni-based super -
alloys have been the preferred metallic materials for high-
temperature applications since their emergence in the 1950s. 
However, due to the limited melting temperature of Ni 
(Tm
Ni = 1450 °C ), it is extremely challenging for Ni-based 
superalloys to work above 1600 °C even with advanced

2 Mechanical behaviors of typical 
WTa‑RMPEAs
2.1 Mechanical behaviors at room temperatures
The WTa-RMPEAs usually exhibit high compressive 
strength but poor plasticity at room temperature. For exam-
ple, the equal-atomic NbMoTaW and NbMoTaWV alloys, as 
the first two developed and widely studied RMPEAs, show 
a high compressive yield strength above 1000 MPa (Fig. 2a) 
[18, 19] but low plasticity (only 0.7% for NbMoTaWV as 
shown in Fig. 2b) [20] with a quasi-cleavage fracture mode 
at room temperature (Fig. 2c) [21]. In tension, the bulk-scale 
polycrystalline NbMoTaW fails in the elastic regime without 
exhibiting any measurable ductility, resulting in a fracture 
strength as low as 70 MPa (Fig.  2a) [18]. Compared with 
the compression test conditions, the average yield strength 
and maximum tensile ductility decreased by 96% and 98%, 
respectively. In the case of NbMoTaWV, the tensile sample 
exhibits a highly dispersed fracture strength ranging from 54 
to 110 MPa with almost no plasticity at room temperature 
[22]. The significant variation in strength and the substan-
tial difference between tension and compression are remi-
niscent of conventionally brittle materials like ceramics. 
The room-temperature fracture toughness of both poly- and 
mono-crystalline NbMoTaW WTa-RMPEAs is limited. Par-
ticularly, the bi-crystal NbMoTaW exhibits a significantly 
lower fracture toughness value of 0.2 MPa·m 1/2 compared 
with structural materials commonly used in safety–critical 
applications (~ 75–200 MPa·m1/2) [23].
The high strength of the WTa-RMPEA comes from its 
high entropy as well as the rugged atomic and energy land-
scape generated by concentrated solid solutions [24, 25]. 
Compared with conventional metals, the WTa-RMPEA 
exhibits intrinsic lattice distortion that varies on a lattice-
by-lattice due to the mismatch in atomic size and elastic

energy landscape hierarchy and its high barrier variation

rugged energy landscape, a novel cross-slip locking mecha-
nism is observed for screw dislocation motion, which can 
provide extra strengthening (Fig. 3) [29].
The low room-temperature plasticity of WTa-RMPEAs 
is attributed to the limited dislocation mobility and deco-
hesion of GBs [12, 20]. The plastic deformability depends 
on the ease of dislocation nucleation and motion. Unlike 
most body-centered cubic (BCC) alloys, both screw and 
edge dislocation segments are equally present in NbMoT -
aWV WTa-RMPEA regardless of the operative slip plane 
(Fig. 4a), indicating that plasticity is influenced by these two 
types of dislocations. The deformation enthalpy and strength 
models suggest that kink migration is the rate-controlling 
step at room temperature. Compared with ductile RMPEAs 
like TiZrHfNbTa, the dislocation in NbMoTaWV has a more 
compact core (Fig.  4b), and the energy of the dislocation 
core is much higher (Fig. 4c). Consequently, it’s difficult to 
nucleate and activate many dislocations in WTa-RMPEAs 
at room temperature. Moreover, the plasticity of WTa-
RMPEAs is also influenced by the weakened GBs [19, 21]. 
In various mechanical tests including compression, tension, 
bending, shearing, and microhardness testing, the fracture 
patterns of the WTa-RMPEAs are dominated by cleavage 
rather than shear [22]. In particular, during compression, 
NbMoTaW bi-crystal micro-pillars fracture along the GBs 
at a much lower strain compared with a single crystalline 
(SC) micro-pillar which can bear large strain upon bending 
(> 75°) without fracture [30]. When a GB is present, the 
fracture toughness is dramatically reduced by an order of 
magnitude.
2.2 Mechanical behaviors at high temperatures
As the testing temperature increases, the strength of WTa-
RMPEAs decreases while the plasticity increases. When 
compressed at 600 °C, the yield strength of the NbMoTaWV 
decreases by 30% – 40% compared with that at room tem-
perature. Notably, the alloy shows significant plastic strain, 
indicating that the DBTT lies between room temperature 
and 600 °C (Fig.  2b). Compared with conventional super 
and refractory alloys, WTa-RMPEAs exhibit a relatively 
lower sensitivity of yield strength change with tempera-
ture. Furthermore, WTa-RMPEAs demonstrate remarkable 
resistance to high-temperature softening, as indicated by

The creep behavior of WTa-RMPEAs is influenced by 
temperature and grain size. Shen et al. studied the compres-
sive creep behaviors of NbMoTaWV WTa-RMPEA with a 
grain size of 138 ± 36 μm within an intermediate tempera-
ture range (700–900 °C) and under high-stress conditions 
(130–520 MPa) [31]. The stress exponent was found to 
remain stable (~ 1) at relatively low temperatures (700–800 
°C), while a stress-dependent transition behavior occurred at 
high temperatures (850–900 °C). The creep activation energy 
increased from 139‒ 156 kJ·mol −1 at low temperatures to 
307–373 kJ·mol−1 at high temperatures. These results sug-
gest that dislocation pipe diffusion governs creep at low 
temperatures, whereas lattice diffusion dominates at high 
temperatures. When the grain size decreased to 67 ± 17 nm, 
the creep resistance slightly decreased, but remained much 
higher than that of previously reported coarse-grained and 
ultrafine-grained high-entropy alloys (HEAs) [32]. The 
dominant mechanism for creep in the nanocrystalline NbMo-
TaWV WTa-RMPEA was attributed to grain boundary dif-
fusion. Specifically, the diffusion of Mo and Nb elements 
controlled the creep deformation due to their relatively lower 
diffusivities among the five constituents.
The atomic-scale complexity of WTa-RMPEAs con-
tributes to their high-temperature strength and tempera-
ture-insensitive properties. The severe lattice distortion 
effectively modifies the structure of dislocation cores, 
thereby influencing the motion of edge and screw disloca-
tions [33]. The significant distortion induces a higher shear 
stress that impedes the gliding of edge dislocations, while 
local chemical fluctuations cause variations in the core struc-
ture along screw dislocations, making thermally activated 
kink nucleation not necessarily the rate-limiting step for 
the motion of screw dislocation. Additionally, the presence 
of thermal kinks and cross-kinks caused by compositional 
fluctuations in equilibrium also contributes to a strengthen-
ing effect [ 34]. Consequently, the dislocation velocities in 
the WTa-RMPEAs are slower than those in pure refractory 
metals. With increasing temperature, the reduction in criti-
cal friction stress for edge motion is much slower compared 
with that for screw motion (Fig.  3b, c). The nature of edge 
dislocation strengthening results in resistance against high-
temperature softening and a weak temperature dependence 
of strength in WTa-RMPEAs [35]. Furthermore, the exist-
ence of thermal super-jogs in edge dislocations acts as strong 
obstacles to dislocation movement. The balance between 
super-jog pining and super-jog diffusion confers supplemen-
tary strength to edge dislocations at intermediate-to-high 
temperatures [36].

3.1 Enhancing mechanical properties via grain 
boundary engineering
Grain boundary decohesion has been identified as a major 
factor causing room-temperature brittleness in WTa-
RMPEAs, which exhibit severe intergranular fracture. A 
similar phenomenon has also been observed in other high-
temperature materials, such as conventional refractory alloys 
and intermetallic compounds. In these materials, interstitial 
or impurity elements (O, N, H, and S) tend to segregate at 
GBs, altering the bond state and hybridize. Consequently, 
disruptions occur in the d-orbital electrons around grain 
boundary sites, leading to decohesion along the GBs [ 88]. 
To mitigate this embrittlement effect, various dopants have 
been introduced to tailor interactions between interstitial 
constituents and substitutional alloying elements [89]. This 
strategy has proven effective in improving the room-temper-
ature plasticity of WTa-RMPEAs.
To investigate the brittleness origin of WTa-RMPEAs, 
Wang et al. conducted a comprehensive analysis of grain 
boundary characteristics using three-dimensional atom 
probe tomography (3D-APT) and transmission electron 
microscopy (TEM) [21]. As shown in Fig. 6a, TEM analysis 
revealed localized disordered regions along the GBs in the 
as-cast alloy, where incidental micro-cracks were observed. 
According to the 1D composition profile, O concentration 
reached ~ 2.3 at.% at the GB, significantly higher than that 
within the grains. The formation of the amorphous local 
regions may arise from the chemical complexity and seg-
regation of O at the GBs [90, 91]. Similar findings were 
reported by Zou et al. [92], where segregation of O, N, and 
C along GBs resulted in intermetallic compound formation. 
The density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed a 
positive strengthening energy of O at the GBs, suggesting a 
decrease in GB strength upon O addition. Furthermore, the 
charge density distribution indicated weak interatomic inter-
actions between O and adjacent metallic elements. Upon 
loading, micro-cracks predominantly propagated along the 
weak GBs, leading to the O-segregation-induced intergranu-
lar fracture.
Although the O element is not intentionally introduced 
into the WTa-RMPEA, its presence, on the order of ~ 0.01 
at.%, is unavoidable in the fabrication process [93, 94]. Wang 
et al. [21] proposed the grain boundary engineering strategy 
to solve the oxygen embrittlement of WTa-RMPEAs. By 
doping ~ 0.5 at.% B, the O segregation at GBs was effectively 
suppressed, and the localized disordered regions disappeared 
(Fig. 6b). Consequently, the room-temperature plasticity sig-
nificantly increased from 2.6% to > 10%. Similar plasticizing 
effects can also be achieved by adding C. The reason is that 
compared with O, B, and C have lower segregation ener -
gies, so they have a stronger tendency for segregation at 
GBs (Fig.  7a). The negative GB strengthening energies of 
B and C suggest that their doping is beneficial for enhancing 
GB strength (Fig.  7b). Besides, the incorporation of B and 
C on the GB interstitial sites results in a higher charge den-
sity, which promotes strong electronic interaction between 
B/C atoms and host metallic atoms, thereby enhancing the 
cohesion of GBs (Fig.  7c). The newly formed electronic 
interactions between B/C-metallic atoms are closer to metal-
lic bonds (Fig.  7d), indicating reduced directionality and 
enhanced deformation of GBs. Consequently, the deform-
ability of B/C-doped NbMoTaW WTa-RMPEA is signifi-
cantly improved, leading to a transition from intergranular 
to transgranular fracture mode.
The addition of metalloids B and C significantly improves 
the yield strength through the solution-strengthening effect. 
WTa-RMPEAs doped with B or C show strength of around 
700 MPa at 1600 °C, which is ~ 200 MPa higher than that 
of undoped WTa-RMPEAs. Similar strengthening effects 
induced by B doping can also be observed in other WTa-
RMPEAs [ 95]. In addition to doping with metalloid ele -
ments, the incorporation of metallic elements such as Ti 
or the construction of ultrafine grains (UFG) can solve the 
susceptibility of GBs to O and improve plasticity [86, 96]. 
Through precise control of O concentrations and increas-
ing GB density, the occurrence of O segregation and oxide 
formation at GBs is effectively mitigated, thereby preserv -
ing the intrinsic cohesion of GBs. Compared with coarse-
grained alloys, the UFG NbMoTaW WTa-RMPEA dem-
onstrates simultaneous enhancement in both strength and 
plasticity [96].
These results indicate that grain boundary engineering 
can effectively improve the room-temperature plasticity of 
WTa-RMPEAs while simultaneously enhancing their high-
temperature strength. On this basis, combined with other 
strategies to promote dislocation mobility within grains, it 
is hopeful to obtain WTa-RMPEAs with excellent compre-
hensive mechanical properties.

3.3 Enhancing mechanical properties 
via refinement of grain size
During the deformation process, the GB serves as an effec-
tive barrier for dislocation movement. In alloys, there is usu-
ally a Hall–Petch relationship between the yield strength and 
grain size, i.e., the yield strength increases with decreasing 
grain size. However, at high homologous temperatures, grain 
refinement softening will take place, impairing the high-
temperature strength. In WTa-RMPEAs, the grain size also 
plays a significant role in flow stress, and due to the unique 
chemical and structural characteristics, WTa-RMPEAs 
exhibit distinctive features about grain refinement strength-
ening effect.
The obvious size effect of mechanical properties is 
observed in NbMoTaW WTa-RMPEAs. At the millimeter 
length scale, the arc-melted polycrystalline NbMoTaW 
WTa-RMPEA exhibits a compressive strength of ~ 1.2 GPa 
and a low plasticity of 2.6%, accompanied by severe crack-
ing and damage [19]. When reducing the size to single-
crystal pillar samples with diameters ranging from 2 μm 
to 200 nm, the alloy exhibits extraordinarily high strength 
(~ 4–4.5 GPa) and improved plasticity (> 12%) [30]. In 
micropillars, the cleavage cracks and GB cracks observed 
in the bulk samples are no longer present, indicating a 
change in the failure behavior. Similar strength-size rela-
tionship is also observed in VNbMoTaW WTa-RMPEA 
[105]. Moreover, the small-sized pillars of nanocrystalline 
NbMoTaW WTa-RMPEAs exhibit a higher yield strength 
of up to ~ 10 GPa, which is one of the highest reported 
strengths in micro-/nano-pillar compression (Fig.  10a, b)

°C. Moreover, the second phase can also be introduced by 
adding elements that have positive mixing enthalpy and 
large atomic radius differences with the component elements 
[118]. For example, by adding large Zr atoms into VTaW 
or NbMoTaW alloys, second phases were induced in the 
inter-dendritic structures, and the microstructure can then 
be controlled by adjusting the W or Zr contents. The exist-
ence of dendritic regions helps to improve strength and alter 
fracture mode [54, 62, 63, 119, 120]. However, it should be 
noted that the addition of Zr may result in the softening of 
NbMoTaWZr WTa-RMPEA at 1000 °C due to its relatively 
low Tm [54].
The metalloid elements, such as Si, C, and N, exhibit 
highly negative enthalpy and significant differences in 
atomic radius with refractory elements, which facilitates 
the introduction of dispersed ceramic phases into WTa-
RMPEAs, leading to enhanced mechanical properties at 
room and high temperatures. C is extensively utilized for 
designing ceramic phase-strengthened WTa-RMPEAs. 
Quasi-binary phase diagrams indicate that eutectic reac-
tions occur during solidification in most binary alloy 
systems composed of C and refractory metallic ele-
ments. Consequently, it is predicted that introducing C 
into WTa-RMPEAs will lead to the formation of eutec-
tic carbides, which has been verified by experimental 
results in various systems such as NbMoTaWCx [87–89], 
 Re0.5 NbMoW(TaC) x [ 59, 67], Hf0.25 NbTaW 0.5 Cx [ 68], 
 Re0.5 NbMoTaW(TiC) x [ 52], Re0.1 Hf0.25 NbTaW 0.4 C0.25 
[74, 75], and NbTaW 0.5C0.25 [ 61]. The introduction of 
the C element can be achieved by arc-melting a mixture 
of refractory metals and their carbides, such as TaC [67], 
NbC [68], TiC [52], Mo2C [61], or graphite.
By adding C, the WTa-RMPEAs exhibit a dual-phase 
microstructure consisting of multi-principal-element 
alloy phases and multi-principal-element carbide phases. 
With increasing C content, the microstructure undergoes 
a transition from hypoeutectic to fully eutectic to hypere-
utectic states (Fig.  11a) [52, 55]. The dual-phase struc-
ture exhibits excellent thermal stability. Especially, alloys 
with eutectic and hypereutectic microstructures show no 
discernable coarsening even under high-temperature 
deformation up to 1200 °C [67]. Furthermore, an approxi-
mate Kurdjumov–Sachs (K–S) orientation relationship 
was observed at the semi-coherent interface between the 
BCC alloy and FCC carbide phase (Fig.  11b), which is 
helpful for stress transfer at the interface [ 67]. Obvious 
element partition can be observed between the carbide 
and alloy phases. Metallic elements that have signifi-
cantly negative chemical mixing enthalpies with C, such 
as Nb and Ta, tend to segregate in the carbide phase, 
while other elements like Mo, W, and Re are enriched 
in the alloy phase (Fig.  11c), thereby providing greater 
control over the interface structure [59, 79, 83]. However, 
previous studies have shown that in C-containing WTa-
RMPEAs with different composition, the carbide phase 
exhibits different crystalline structures (HCP or FCC) and 
the microstructure is influenced by fabrication methods 
[72, 112]. Further research is required to understand the 
formation mechanism of microstructures.
The above characteristics make the introduction of 
the secondary carbide phase an effective way to improve 
the mechanical properties of WTa-RMPEAs. When 
deformed at room temperatures, the layered metallic 
phase can effectively buffer crack propagation in brit-
tle carbide phases, delay fracture, and promote plasticity 
(Fig.  12a) [59]. As a result, a fracture strain of ~ 10% or 
even exceeding 30% can be obtained [68]. Under high-
temperature deformation, the solid-solution strengthening 
of the multi-principal-element matrix, effective disloca-
tion blocking at the interwoven metal-carbide interface, 
and stable interface hindering grain boundary migration 
result in a superb strength (Fig.  12b, c) [75, 121]. For 
example, the Re0.5NbMoW(TaC) 0.9 alloy exhibits a com-
pressive strength exceeding 2 GPa at 1200 °C (Fig.  12d) 
[67]. The compressive yield strengths of Mo20Ta30W20C30 
alloy at 1600 and 2000 °C are 1257 and 222 MPa, respec-
tively [79]. Additionally, these alloys possess remarkable 
hardness and wear resistance [58].
The addition of N, B, and Si elements can also introduce 
multicomponent ceramic phases into the WTa-RMPEAs. 
For example, after adding N, the as-cast NbMoTaWHfN 
alloy consists of BCC, FCC, and HCP phases. Due to the 
high softening temperature, grain boundary strengthening, 
and second phase strengthening, the alloy exhibits a yield 
strength of 288 MPa at 1800 °C [80]. When doped with the 
silicide phase (Nb, Ta) 5Si3, the NbMoTaWSi0.25 exhibits a 
yield stress of 926 MPa with a fracture strain of 13.11% at 
1200 °C [48]. These results indicate that the incorporation of 
a second ceramic phase is an effective method for designing 
high-temperature materials by overcoming the inherent limi-
tation of low T m in conventional eutectic alloys and diffu-
sion-controlled softening in conventional high-temperature 
alloys. Furthermore, the second ceramic phase can also be 
introduced through the mechanical alloying process [ 95, 
122, 123], but it should be noted that the poorly matched 
interfaces may cause local stress concentration, decreasing 
the plasticity.
In short, GB engineering can effectively improve the room-
temperature plasticity and ultra-high temperature strength of 
WTa-RMPEAs. By alloying with different elements, a wide 
range of strength and plasticity can be achieved at room 
temperature. However, there is often a trade-off between

strength. It is very important to select appropriate alloying 
elements based on the targeted mechanical performance. 
Compared with other strengthening methods, WTa-RMPEAs 
strengthened via grain refinement exhibit the highest room-
temperature strength, but GB sliding during ultra-high tem-
perature deformation can lead to a decrease in strength. By 
introducing a second ceramic phase, WTa-RMPEAs showed 
outstanding strength at ultra-high temperatures while main-

4.1 Local lattice distortions and its influences 
on the mechanical properties
The conventional solid solution strengthening of metallic 
solid solutions is based on the elastic interactions between 
dislocations and local stress fields of solute atoms. However, 
this mechanism cannot be directly applied to WTa-RMPEAs 
because their atomic ratios are almost uniform and there is 
no clear distinction between solute and solvent elements. 
Although some theories for solid solution strengthening 
in RMPEAs have been recently developed, their accuracy 
and universality need further verification [124, 126, 127]. 
Numerous studies have shown that the presence of local lat-
tice distortions (LLDs), as one of the most notable features 
of WTa-RMPEAs, can affect the movement of dislocations, 
thereby influencing the mechanical properties [26, 33, 35]. 
However, quantitatively characterizing LLDs and deter -
mining their influence remain a challenge. The atomic size 
mismatch, which quantifies the deviation of atomic radii 
from the mean radius of individual elements, was initially 
derived to assess the LLDs. However, this approach is based 
on an ideal hard sphere model that overlooks critical factors 
such as charge transfer effects. Estimates of LLDs obtained 
from the atomic size mismatch method exhibit significant

In conventional dilute solid solution, as the tempera-
ture increases, the intensified thermal motion of atoms 
usually leads to relaxation of the lattice structure. During 
deformation, dislocations can overcome barriers through 
a combination of thermal activation and external stress, 
resulting in a decrease in flow stress. Numerous studies 
have indicated that the strength of WTa-RMPEAs is con-
trolled by edge dislocations due to the large energy bar -
riers created by LLDs for thermally activated edge glide 
[35, 131]. However, there is limited research on LLDs at 
high temperatures. The variation of LLDs with increasing 
temperatures and its influence on dislocation climb/glide 
at high temperatures are still unclear. Therefore, further 
investigation is necessary to gain a clear theoretical under -
standing regarding the behavior of LLDs in WTa-RMPEAs 
and to identify effective methodologies for studying and 
quantifying this phenomenon.
4.2 Compositional heterogeneity and its influences 
on the mechanical properties
Multi-principal element alloys were initially considered as 
ideal solid solutions where constituent elements are ran-
domly distributed. However, continuous exploration has 
revealed the existence of local chemical fluctuations (LCFs) 
in multi-principal element systems due to the complex chem-
ical interactions between elements [132]. In WTa-RMPEAs, 
the LCFs generate variations of local bonding configurations 
and thus affect dislocation motion and multiplication, lead-
ing to unique mechanical properties [ 125]. Pozuelo et al. 
[24] revealed that the spatial distribution of chemical com-
position in the NbMoTaW WTa-RMPEA exhibited distinct 
oscillatory patterns, forming soft and hard local regions 
that influence the nucleation and movement of dislocations. 
Richie et al. found that LCSs can hinder screw dislocation 
motion by reducing the rate of double-kink while accelerat-
ing edge dislocation motion [29]. However, there are vari-
ous manifestations of nanoscale compositional fluctuations, 
and currently, no definitive criteria are available to distin -
guish them. Besides, it is challenging to characterize LCSs 
at high temperatures and draw a definitive conclusion on 
the extent of their influence on high-temperature mechanical 
properties. Further quantitative investigations are imperative 
for a more comprehensive elucidation.
Gradually increasing the compositional fluctuation can 
eventually lead to precipitation, forming a multiphase struc-
ture. In conventional metallic materials, it is well known 
that secondary phase strengthening is highly effective in 
improving strength but often at the expense of ductility. In 
multi-principal element alloys, the second phase precipitated 
from the matrix usually exhibits a multi-component nature 
[133]. The multi-principal element characteristics of both 
the matrix and second phase provide large compositional 
flexibility in tuning the structure and mismatch degree of the 
phase interface, potentially contributing to unique mechan-
ical properties. Besides, the high-temperature strength of 
multi-phase RMPEAs is significantly influenced by the dis-
solution of secondary phases. It is believed that secondary 
phase strengthening can be noticeable at temperatures as 
high as ~ 0.7 Tm for alloys with Ts ≥ 0.7 Tm (where Ts is the 
solvus temperature of the secondary phase) [13]. Therefore, 
it is important to choose a secondary phase with high T s 
in multi-phase WTa-RMPEAs. The incorporation of a sec-
ondary phase with higher entropy and thermal stability is 
promising for enhancing their high-temperature mechanical 
properties.
4.3 Difficulties in fabrication of WTa‑RMPEAs
The fabrication of WTa-RMPEAs, especially those with 
large dimensions and complex shapes, is extremely challeng-
ing due to their ultra-high Tm and significant differences in 
Tm of constituent elements. Additionally, high DBTT poses 
difficulty in machinability. These factors present challenges 
in preparing samples for mechanical property measure-
ments, especially for tension and creep tests. Currently, arc 
melting is widely used in laboratories to synthesize almost 
all WTa-RMPEAs. However, it is difficult to prepare large-
sized samples through arc-melting. Besides, the composi-
tional complexity, significant difference in density, and wide 
solidification temperature range of WTa-RMPEAs lead to 
the formation of various defects during solidification that 
are difficult to mitigate through the post-treatment process, 
significantly impairing the mechanical properties. The high 
Tm and high strength at elevated temperatures also pose 
strict requirements (high temperature, high pressures, and 
extended sintering durations) on powder metallurgy fabri-
cation and post-processing. Furthermore, efforts have been 
made to fabricate WTa-RMPEAs using additive manufac-
turing, an advanced near-net shape process for efficient and 
effective production of complex parts [65, 134]. However, 
despite measures such as optimizing process parameters 
and composition adjustments that have been conducted, 
defects like warping, cracks, and porosities are still com -
monly observed in additive manufacturing components. The

fabrication of large-sized WTa-RMPEAs samples remains a 
challenge until now. Consequently, compression tests have 
predominantly been employed to study the mechanical prop-
erties of WTa-RMPEAs while only a few reports on tension 
and fracture toughness tests can be found [18, 22]. This limi-
tation hinders further research on the deformation behavior 
and mechanism of WTa-RMPEAs through experiments.
In summary, WTa-RMPEAs have emerged as a prom-
ising high-temperature structural material. Due to their 
unique characteristics of multi-principal elements, signifi-
cant lattice distortion, and chemical fluctuations, the defor -
mation mechanism derived from conventional alloys may 
not fully be applied to describe the deformation behavior 
in WTa-RMPEAs. Besides, the high Tm poses challenges in 
sample preparation for mechanical property testing. There-
fore, further research is needed to develop correspond-
ing deformation theories and fabrication technologies for 
WTa-RMPEAs.
5 Prospects
Despite the aforementioned achievements, there still exist 
challenges in investigating the mechanical properties. The 
deformation mechanism of WTa-RMPEAs, especially at 
high temperatures, needs further research. In this final sec-
tion, several future trends and prospects of WTa-RMPEAs 
are proposed based on the existing literature as follows:
(a) The 3D atomic structure and its evolution under exter-
nal stress require further investigation. The revelation 
of deformation behavior and mechanism, as well as 
the development of a quantitative deformation model, 
are greatly related to the understanding of the atomic 
structure. Due to the chemical complexity and the lack 
of accurate experimental characterization, the quanti-
fied structural-property correlation of WTa-RMPEAs 
is still missing. The key to establishing the structural 
model lies in the revealing of the long-range chemi-
cal disordering and short-range ordering, as well as 
the quantitative characterization of the lattice distor -
tion. Moreover, it is necessary to study the influence of 
temperature on these structural characteristics of WTa-
RMPEAs.
(b) Developing WTa-RMPEAs with exceptional compre-
hensive mechanical properties requires more effort. As 
a supersaturated solid solution, there are ample oppor-
tunities to introduce various strengthening strategies in 
WTa-RMPEAs, such as solid solution strengthening, 
fine-grain strengthening, and second-phase strength-
ening. However, achieving the desired combination of 
high strength at high temperatures and good room-tem-
perature ductility in WTa-RMPEAs remains challeng-
ing. For example, when incorporating eutectic carbide 
phases, WTa-RMPEAs not only exhibit outstanding 
high-temperature strength through the synergistic 
effect of multi-principal-element mixing and dislo-
cation blocking at phase interfaces but also alleviate 
room-temperature brittleness by blunting microcrack 
tips. However, obtaining tension properties in this alloy 
type remains a significant challenge due to the inher -
ent brittleness of the carbide phases. It is necessary to 
optimize compositions or develop innovative strength-
ening strategies to achieve outstanding comprehensive 
mechanical properties.
(c) Innovative alloy design and manufacturing techniques 
are required to obtain excellent mechanical properties. 
Except for high-temperature, WTa-RMPEAs can also 
be widely applied in situations facing more severe chal-
lenges. For example, WTaCrV did not generate disloca-
tions under Kr ion irradiation with 1 MeV at 800 °C 
and exhibited outstanding irradiation resistance proper-
ties [101], indicating its potential application as radi-
ation-resistant material. NbMoTaWAg demonstrated 
superb tribological properties [135]. VNbMoTaW 
thin films can provide protection for substrates and 
maintain high-temperature resistivity at relatively low 
values [136], and WTa-RMPEAs can also be utilized 
at low temperatures if further works are conducted 
to lower the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature 
[137]. However, the increased number of constituent 
elements makes it extremely difficult to quickly screen 
desired alloys with specific mechanical properties. 
More efforts and attempts should be made to develop 
high-throughput experimental and computational meth-
ods. Besides, most data are generated from small but-
tons fabricated by arc melting, which not only limits 
investigations on tensile strength, creep resistance, and 
fracture toughness that require larger samples but also 
restricts the exploration of the influence of microstruc-
ture on mechanical properties. Therefore, it is neces-
sary to develop advanced manufacturing technologies 
to fabricate large-sized WTa-RMPEA samples for both 
fundamental research and practical applications.
(d) More research concerning the applications of WTa-
RMPEAs at high temperatures is desirable. Despite 
the great mechanical properties, there are still scien-
tific and technological challenges to the application 
of WTa-RMPEAs at high temperatures. For exam-
ple, poor high-temperature oxidation resistance of 
WTa-RMPEAs is a tricky issue that requires resolu-
tions through compositional design or developing 
environmental barrier coatings. The high density of 
WTa-RMPEAs also poses a great challenge for their 
application in aircraft engines. Some efforts have been 
made to address this challenge. On the one hand, ele-

also be used in the form of coatings and porous materi-

6 Conclusion
The development of emerging gas turbines, aviation engines, 
and hypersonic applications urgently demands high-per -
formance structural materials that can withstand ultrahigh 
temperatures. WTa-RMPEAs, which exhibit high thermal 
stability and high-temperature strength, are considered a 
new generation of high-temperature materials. In the present 
paper, the mechanical properties are identified as the pri-
mary factor limiting the development of WTa-RMPEAs, and 
are critically reviewed. The mechanical properties of typical 
WTa-RMPEAs at room and high temperatures were sum -
marized, and then the strengthening and toughening mecha-
nisms were discussed. Subsequently, the effects of different 
strengthening strategies, including boundary engineering, 
solid solution strengthening, grain refinement strengthen-
ing, and second phase strengthening, on the mechanical 
properties and deformation mechanism of WTa-RMPEAs 
were analyzed and summarized. The challenges for under -
standing the deformation mechanism of WTa-RMPEAs 
were thus highlighted. Finally, future research directions 
for developing novel high-performance WTa-RMPEAs were 
also proposed.
