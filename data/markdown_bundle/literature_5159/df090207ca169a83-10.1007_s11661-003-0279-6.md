Creep Behavior of TiAl Alloys with Enhanced 
High-Temperature Capability
F. APPEL, J.D.H. PAUL, M. OEHRING, U. FRÖBEL, and U. LORENZ
For high-temperature applications, creep strength is of major concern, in addition to oxidation and cor-
rosion resistance, and determines the application range of titanium aluminide alloys in competition
with other structural materials. Thus, this work was aimed at identifying mechanisms of creep defor-
mation and microstructural degradation and at developing alloying concepts with respect to an enhanced
high-temperature capability. The analysis shows that dislocation climb controls deformation in the range
of the intended operation temperatures. Further, complex processes of phase transformations, recrystal-
lization, and microstructural coarsening were observed, which contribute to microstructural degradation
and limit component life in long-term service. By alloying with high contents of Nb, both room- and
high-temperature strength properties can be improved as Nb increases the activation energy of diffu-
sion and increases the propensity for twinning at ambient temperature. For alloys with enhanced high-
temperature capability, microalloying with carbon is also of particular use, because carbide precipitates
effectively hinder dislocation motion and are thought to increase microstructural stability.
I. INTRODUCTION
OVER the past decade, titanium aluminide alloys have
been considered as viable engineering materials for high-
temperature technologies.
[1] For such applications, good creep
resistance and structural stability are of major concern. Design-
safety margins will certainly depend on the operation condi-
tions of the respective components, which makes predictions
of benchmarks for critical stresses and temperatures diffi-
cult. For long-life engine components, only relatively low
stresses and temperatures can be tolerated, because the total
creep strain accumulated during the useful life of the part
should not exceed 0.1 to 0.2 pct. Under such conditions,
dislocation interactions with point defects and mechanisms
that are associated with structural changes are probably import-
ant. For other parts, such as the roots of turbine blades, higher
stresses and temperatures are relevant, because larger creep
strains up to a few percent can be tolerated; thus, cavitation
and rupture become failure concerns.
[2] A substantial amount
of research effort has been directed toward the understand-
ing of microstructure/property relationships and the micro-
mechanisms of creep.
[3–11] While differences of interpretation
are common, there has been general agreement about the major
features. As with many other materials, the creep character-
istics of TiAl alloys involve primary, secondary (or steady-
state), and ternary regions, the extent of which depend on
stress and temperature. The dependence of the steady state or
minimum creep rate upon the testing conditions is often
described in terms of the Dorn equation:

On a more practical side, the consequences are high
creep rates, which cannot be accepted in view of the pre-

resistance is therefore a major concern in the develop-
ment of TiAl alloys. These problems will be addressed
in the present article with the following areas being
considered:
1. factors governing high-temperature strength and creep
resistance,
2. origin of structural changes upon long-term creep, and
3. alloy design toward improved creep resistance.

II. EXPERIMENTAL INVESTIGATIONS
A. Alloy Systems
The compositions and microstructures of the alloys inves-
tigated are listed in Table I. In the present study, particular
emphasis was placed on optimizing the alloy composition
toward improved high-temperature strength and creep resist-
ance. With this perspective in mind, the strengthening
phenomena due to carbon and niobium additions will be dis-
cussed. In the Ti-Al system, the extension of the individual
phase fields between 
/H92512(Ti3Al) and /H9253 (TiAl) depends on the

[12] Thus,
microalloying alters the path by which microstructures evolve.
Extensive experimental investigations have shown that the
mechanical properties of two-phase alloys are particularly
sensitive to the microstructure.
[13] This may easily mask
strength variations caused by any other hardening mechanism.
Thus, for an assessment of solid solution or precipitation
strengthening, the effects of off-stoichiometric deviations and
structural changes on the mechanical properties have to be
considered.
The strengthening effect of Nb additions was studied on
alloys 1 through 8. These experiments involve Nb containing
alloys and binary alloys with the equivalent Al contents and
are thought to ascertain the effects of off-stoichiometric
deviations on the microstructure and yield strength. The
effects of carbon additions were investigated on alloys
9 through 12, with the carbon concentration ranging between
0.02 at. pct (undoped material) and 0.4 at. pct. Annealing
at 1250 °C and quenching resulted in a carbon solid solution,
whereas Ti
3AlC precipitates of perovskite type were formed
by subsequent aging at 750 °C. Alloys 1 through 14 used
for the basic investigations were 35-g ingots produced
by nonconsumable electrode argon-arc melting followed
by hot isostatic pressing (HIP). Alloys 15 through 21 are
engineering alloys with optimized compositions and
microstructures, which were subjected to hot working by
forging or canned extrusion mostly below the respective
/H9251-transus temperature T /H9251, where the alloys become single-
phase /H9251.
B. Mechanical Tests
The mechanical properties were characterized in the
temperature range 25 °C to 900 °C by conventional com-
pression and tensile tests performed in a servohydraulic
closed loop machine using the elongation as the feedback
parameter. Tensile creep tests were performed in air or an
inert gas atmosphere using constant lever arm machines. The
test temperatures were 700 °C and 800 °C and the initial
stresses were in the range 80 to 300 MPa. The specimen
elongation was measured by an extensometer attached to the
ridges defining a specimen gage length of 27 to 33 mm.

too large for climb. In the present evaluation, V was referred
to the Burgers vector of ordinary dislocations, which mainly

climb. Concerning this complex situation, it is difficult to
make generalizations about the ability of dislocations to
climb in TiAl. Apart from deformation conditions, care-

microstructure, and impurity content. However, in view of
the strong dependencies of 1/V on T and /H9255, it might be specu-

A. Interface Related Creep Mechanisms
The lamellar structure is the result of phase transforma-
tions and ordering reactions occurring during solidification
and cooling. After solidification, the platelets grow from
prior grains of the 
/H9251or /H92512 phase with a crystallographic
alignment according to [29]
The /H9253phase is formed as an ordered domain structure; this
gives rise to six variants of the preceding orientation rela-
tionship and to a variety of different 
/H9253//H9253interfaces in addi-
tion to the /H92512//H9004interfaces. An ideal lamellar morphology
comprised of lamellae possessing flat faces should be intrin-
sically metastable; the principal radii of the flat faces are
infinite and therefore no driving force exists for coarsening.
However, the morphology of real lamellar alloys is much
more complex and rich in detail. Figure 3 demonstrates high-
resolution electron micrographs of the characteristic features,
which might be important for structural changes. There are
domain boundaries and lamellar terminations possessing
small radii of curvature (Figure 3(a)). Accommodation of
lattice misfit between adjacent lamellae leads to dense
arrangements of interfacial dislocations with different Burg-
ers vectors and high residual coherency stresses.

B. Yield Strength and Ductility
The implementation of strengthening mechanisms for
improving the creep resistance is often counter to the alloy
attributes that are desirable for low-temperature ductility
and damage tolerance. The balance of properties than can
be achieved with the Nb-bearing alloys is demonstrated in
Figure 15 by load/elongation traces of tensile tests performed
at 25 °C, 700 °C, and 900 °C. The materials generally exhibit
high yield stresses with room temperature values of 900 to
1000 MPa, which are combined with an appreciable plastic
elongation of 1 to 2 pct. This combination of high strength
and good ductility has not been achieved up to the present
date with conventional TiAl alloys, which suggests that
significant changes of the deformation mechanism occur due
to the Nb additions.
In Nb-containing alloys, an abundant activation of twin-
ning has been recognized and the superdislocations were
found to be widely dissociated.
[26] As planar faults, twins,
and dissociated superdislocations often coexist in the same
grain or lamella, it is speculated that the stacking fault
energies of 
/H9253(TiAl) are lowered by the Nb additions and
that twin nucleation originates from the superposition of
extended stacking faults on alternate {111} planes. In this
context, it is tempting to speculate that the enhanced twin-
ning activity in Nb-bearing alloys is beneficial for the
low-temperature ductility, in that it compensates for the lack
of independent slip systems in 
/H9253(TiAl) that can operate at
comparable stresses.[31]
VII. CONCLUSIONS
The creep resistance of lamellar titanium aluminide
alloys is limited by nonconservative dislocation processes
and degradation of the microstructure due to recrystal-
lization and phase transformation. These processes can
be greatly reduced in alloys that are subjected to pre-
cipitation hardening based on the general composition
Ti-45Al-(5-10)Nb (at. pct). Optimized alloys can be
