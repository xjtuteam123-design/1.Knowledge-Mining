The current research of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) is mainly focused on simple solid-solution alloys or al- 
loys with precipitation strengthening phases. In the present work, we developed a series of high-entropy 
intermetallic compounds (HEICs). In particular, the multi-principal element Fe 0.75 Co 0.75 Ni 0.75 Cu 0.75 TiZrHf 
with B2 ordering shows an ultrahigh compressive yield strength of 2.25 GPa and a fracture strength of 
2.52 GPa. The developed HEIC also demonstrates exceptional high-temperature phase stability with re- 
tained yield strengths up to 905 MPa and 705 MPa at 800 °C and 900 °C, respectively. The underlying 
phase formation rule of the HEICs is discussed within the framework of a proposed electronegativity re- 
lated parameter combined with the atomic size dispersity. Our study broadens the family of high-entropy 
materials and provides a new perspective for the high-temperature alloy design based on multi-principal 
element intermetallics.

High-entropy alloys (HEAs) are a class of emerging materials 
with a novel alloy design concept based on multi-principal ele- 
ments in equal or near-equal atomic ratios [1] . This radical de- 
parture from the conventional alloy design strategy opens up am- 
ple opportunities for designing a number of compositions with 
promising properties, such as high strength, great toughness, and 
strong corrosion resistance [2–5] . HEAs are mostly solid solution- 
based alloys and they have generally demonstrated promise for 
high-temperature applications [6] . Among currently developed 
HEAs, two major groups can be identiﬁed according to their con- 
stituent elements: (i) HEAs based on 3d-transition metals (TM 
HEAs) and (ii) HEAs based on refractory elements, which form so- 
called refractory HEAs (RHEAs) [7] . 
Similar to conventional Ni-based superalloys, the strength of 
TM HEAs drastically decreases with increasing temperatures above 
800 °C, despite their excellent mechanical properties with strong 
strain-hardening capability at room temperature [8] . The high- 
temperature use of the TM HEAs has been therefore limited by 
their relatively low melting temperatures. In contrast, RHEAs often 
show remarkably higher melting temperatures of 160 0~250 0 °C

Due to their high conﬁgurational entropy, more than half of the 
reported RHEAs are single-phase disordered BCC solid solutions 
with retained high strengths at elevated temperatures [10] . For 
example, one of the benchmark RHEAs, HfNbTaTiZr, shows a sta- 
ble body-centered-cubic (BCC) structure with a yield strength of 
295 MPa at 10 0 0 °C [10] . Although these RHEAs have demon- 
strated superb thermal stability, they still rapidly lose the strength 
at elevated temperatures above 0.5 T m ( T m , melting point) [ 2 , 3 ]. 
Such softening behavior is largely due to the loss of the solid- 
solution strengthening mechanism at high temperatures. In com- 
parison with solid-solution alloys, it is well known that intermetal- 
lic compounds (ICs) typically possess unusual high-temperature 
phase stability and strengths [ 11 , 12 ]. For example, FeAl exhibits an 
abnormal strength increase at 600 °C, and its ordered-B2 structure 
can remain stable up to 1200 °C [13] . Likewise, TiAl and NiAl-based 
superalloys are widely used as aerospace materials due to their 
retained elevated-temperature strengths [ 12 , 14 , 15 ]. As such, many 
researchers exploited HEAs with secondary ICs to strengthen the 
solid-solution matrix. This design wisdom has been recently re- 
alized in both TM HEAs and RHEAs, such as (CoCrNi) 94 Al 3 Ti 3 [4] , 
Al 0.5 Cr 0.9 FeNi 2.5 V 0.2 [5] , Cr 0.3 NbTiZr [9] , CoCrFeNiNb 0.45 [16] , and 
AlCrMoNbTi [9] . Nevertheless, the secondary intermetallic com-

cal structural design motif of FCC/L1 2 in Ni-based superalloys. Very 
recently, to further explore the content of the intermetallic phase, 
Zhou et al [18] fabricated single-phase high-entropy intermetallic 
compounds (HEICs), (Fe 1/5 Co 1/5 Ni 1/5 Mn 1/5 Cu 1/5 )Al of a B2 structure 
and (Ti 1/4 Nb 1/4 V 1/4 Zr 1/4 )Al 3 of a D0 22 structure. This concept of 
multi-component HEICs opens a new platform to explore compo- 
sitionally complex intermetallic alloys. 
To harness the combined advantages of the high-entropy sta- 
bilization of HEAs and the exceptional thermal stability of ICs, 
we design a new series of HEIC and explore their thermal sta- 
bility and high-temperature mechanical behavior. Using multi- 
component Fe x Co x Ni x Cu x TiZrHf ( x : atomic ratio) alloys as a pro- 
totype system, we demonstrate that multi-component HEICs can 
form by tuning the contents of Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu so as to tai- 
lor the entropy, enthalpy, electronegativity, average atomic radius, 
and other physical parameters. The constituent elements in this 
alloy system are comprised of both TM elements of Fe, Co, Ni, 
and Cu, and refractory elements of Ti, Zr, and Hf. The TM ele- 
ments have inﬁnite mutual solubility and are frequently used in 
TM HEAs [ 19 , 20 ], while these refractory elements are critical com- 
ponents for RHEAs and have high mutual solubility as well [10] . 
Of particular interest is that between the two groups of elements 
there exist substantial differences in physical and chemical proper- 
ties, such as melting point, atomic radius, electronegativity, and va- 
lence electron concentration, which are often the necessary condi- 
tions for the formation of ICs (e.g., AB, AB 2 and A 3 B type ICs) [21] . 
Within this HEIC series, in particular, Fe 0.75 Co 0.75 Ni 0.75 Cu 0.75 TiZrHf 
(i.e., (Fe 1/4 Co 1/4 Ni 1/4 Cu 1/4 )(Ti 1/3 Zr 1/3 Hf 1/3 )) shows a B2 intermetal- 
lic structure that leads to an ultrahigh compressive strength up 
to 2.52 GPa, combined with excellent high-temperature phase sta- 
bility and retained compressive yield strengths of 905 MPa and 
705 MPa at 800 °C and 900 °C, respectively. Our ﬁnding offers a 
new paradigm in the design of high-temperature materials via the 
HEIC strategy. 
Ingots of Fe x Co x Ni x Cu x TiZrHf ( x = 0.5, 0.6, 0.75, 0.85, and 1, the

spectively) were prepared by vacuum arc-melting in a high-purity 
argon atmosphere. The purity of all the raw materials is above 
99.9wt%. The alloys were re-melted for ﬁve times to ensure chemi-

focus our discussion on T 0.75 with the ordered B2 structure, and 
the phase structures of other alloys are shown in the supplemen-

60 0 °C, 80 0 °C, 10 0 0 °C, and 120 0 °C, respectively, for 2 h. Crystal 
structures of both the as-cast and the annealed alloys were iden-

spectrometry (EDS, AZtec X-Max 50) was used to investigate the 
microstructure and the elemental distribution. The metallurgical 
specimens were polished and etched with nitrohydrochloric acid. 
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations were carried 
out using a Titan G2 60-300 microscope. The specimen for TEM 
observation was prepared by the standard preparation technique, 
which included the cutting of 3-mm disks, grinding, dimpling, and 
subsequent ion-milling at 5 kV at an angle of 5 °. To study the me- 
chanical properties of the developed T 0.75 HEIC, uniaxial compres- 
sion tests were conducted over a wide range of temperatures of 
25 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, 800 °C, and 900 °C. Cylindrical samples of 
3 mm (diameter) × 6 mm (height) were tested on an Instron 5969 
universal testing machine at a strain rate of 10 −4 s −1 . Three tests 
were performed for each condition. The room-temperature Vickers 
hardness measurements of at least ﬁve points were also performed 
for both the as-cast and annealed samples by an HVS-10 0 0CCD 
hardness testing machine. 
The XRD patterns of the as-cast and annealed T 0.75 alloys are 
shown in Fig. 1 . Interestingly, the as-cast T 0.75 alloy that contains 
seven constituent elements shows a mixture of B2/B2 ′ ordered in- 
termetallics. Since B2 and B2 ′ have very close lattice constants and 
their peaks are almost merged and diﬃcult to distinguish in the 
XRD patterns, peak separations were conducted by a PeakFit soft- 
ware. According to the phase identiﬁcation, B2 and B2 ′ are con- 
ﬁrmed to be CoHf-like and CoZr-like ICs, respectively, with the 
same crystal structure of an ordered BCC. Namely, all other con- 
stituent atoms (i.e., Cu, Fe, Ni, and Ti) are essentially dissolved into 
the intermetallic matrix as solute atoms. Meanwhile, as shown in 
Fig. 1 a, the diffraction peaks of T 0.75 exhibit high intensities with a 
small full width at half maxima (FWHM), suggesting a high degree 
of crystallinity. As compared to T 0.75 , other HEICs in the developed 
alloy series were featured by multi-phase intermetallic structures 
with predominant B2/B2 ′ intermetallics and additional intermetal- 
lic phases of FeHf 1.2 Zr 0.8 (T 0.5 , T 0.6 ) or Fe 0.7 Hf 0.3 (T 0.85 , T 1 ) (see Fig. 
S1). Notably, the diffraction peaks of other alloys were signiﬁcantly 
broadened associated with decreased intensities, which suggest a 
decreased crystallinity or even some amorphous components may 
have formed (Fig. S1). This is not surprising if one carefully consid- 
ers the robust glass-forming ability in the Ti-Zr-Hf-Cu-M (M = Fe, 
Co, and Ni) alloy system revealed by Inoue et al . [22] . Fig. 1 b shows 
the XRD patterns of the T 0.75 alloy after annealing at different tem- 
peratures. It is clear that the intermetallic phase of B2/B2 ′ was 
maintained in the annealed T 0.75 alloys after 2-hrs exposure up 
to 10 0 0 °C, indicating the superb high-temperature stability. More- 
over, anti-site defects (having equal anti-site A atoms for the A-rich 
composition and anti-site B atoms for the B-rich composition) are 
commonly present in conventional ICs and the anti-site defect con- 
centration is a proper parameter to measure the phase stability of 
the alloy [18] . Therefore, to further characterize the stability of the 
T 0.75 HEIC, we calculated the anti-site defect concentration of the 
B2 and B2 ′ phases following Pike, Chang, and Liu’s approach [23] .

Fig. 3 illustrates the compressive properties and the thermal 
stability of the T 0.75 HEIC over a wide range of temperatures. At 
room temperature, the as-cast T 0.75 alloy demonstrates exceptional 
mechanical performance –V i c k e r s hardness of 600 HV (Fig. S4), 
yield strength of 2.25 GPa, and compressive fracture strength of 
2.52 GPa ( Fig. 3 a). These properties are substantially higher than 
those of most other HEAs, amorphous alloys (or bulk metallic 
glasses, BMGs), and ICs [27–38] . A direct comparison of the room- 
temperature compressive strength of the T 0.75 HEIC with most 
well-known BMGs and ICs is summarized in Fig. 3 b. The T 0.75 
HEIC exhibits an extraordinary strength that rivals most BMGs 
and ICs. We argue the mechanistic origins of such high strength 
from the following ﬁve aspects on the basis of the microstruc- 
ture ( Fig. 2 ) [39] : (1) The unique fully-intermetallic-matrix based 
HEA contains a large number of covalent and metallic bonds rather 
than a single metallic bond [40] ; (2) The seven principal ele- 
ments with near-equimolar ratios in the HEIC leads to even more 
severe lattice distortion and solution strengthening than conven- 
tional HEAs; (3) The ﬁne micro-sized grain/phase domain induces 
strong grain/interphase boundary strengthening; (4) The ordered 
B2 sub-grain nanoparticles introduce precipitation-like strengthen- 
ing; (5) The pileup of parallel dislocations acts as an effective dis- 
location barrier [27] . Further studies are required to separate and 
understand these strengthening mechanisms and their contribu- 
tions. Additionally, the micro-hardness (Fig. S4) and yield strength 
of the annealed specimens at 600 °C slightly increased by ~16% 
and ~6%, respectively. With no evidence of extra nanoprecipitation, 
such annealing-induced hardening is possibly analogous to the be- 
havior in nanocrystalline metals where dislocation source-limited 
strengthening prevails [41–43] . As the annealing temperature in- 
creased to 10 0 0 °C, the strength was essentially unchanged, sug- 
gesting that the grains and sub-grain nanoprecipitates were com- 
pletely reorganized to a stable state at that temperature. When an- 
nealed at 1200 °C, the micro-hardness and the yield strength in- 
creased again to 763.9 HV (Fig. S4) and 2.52 GPa ( Fig. 3 a), pre- 
sumably due to the formation of the hard Fe 2 Zr Laves phase as 
detected by the XRD ( Fig. 1 b). 
To explore the high-temperature mechanical performance, we 
measured the compressive properties of the T 0.75 HEIC at elevated 
temperatures from 400 °C to 900 °C ( Fig. 3 c). With increasing 
the test temperature, the alloy gradually softened with generally 
increased plasticity. Speciﬁcally, at 400 °C the sample yielded at 
1.77 GPa, followed by a marginal plastic strain of 3% before catas- 
trophic fracture at 2.23 GPa. At 600 °C, the sample still exhibited 
an ultra-high yield strength of 1.5 GPa, followed by a ~8% plas- 
tic strain to failure. At 800 °C and 900 °C, retained yield strengths 
of 905 MPa and 705 MPa were measured, followed by continuous 
strain softening with slight stress serrations in the plastic regime. 
The plasticity was tremendously increased to ~30%. The high- 
temperature strengths of the T 0.75 HEIC out-perform that of the 
benchmark nickel-based superalloys (e.g. Mar-M247 and Inconel 
718) and traditional ICs, such as CoHf and Co 50.5 Zr 49.5 ( Fig. 3 d) 
[ 3 , 12 , 14 , 15 , 29–31 ]. For example, at 800 °C, the yield strength, σy 
≈ 905 MPa, 800 MPa, 580 MPa, 500 MPa, and 200 MPa, for 
T 0.75 , Mar-M247, Inconel 718, CoHf, and Co 50.5 Zr 49.5 , respectively 
[ 3 , 12 , 14 , 15 , 29–31 ]. Altogether, these results suggest the promise of 
the developed T 0.75 HEIC for high-temperature applications.

705 MPa at 800 °C and 900 °C, respectively. The underlying phase 
formation rule of the HEICs is discussed within the framework 
of the atomic-size dispersity, δ, and the electronegativity-related 
parameter, η. We reveal that fully HEICs are prone to form in the 
region of 0.2 ≤ η ≤ 0.4 and δ ≥ 5.5. Our ﬁndings broaden the 
family of high-entropy materials and provide a new perspective 
for a multi-component alloy design based on intermetallics for 
elevated-temperature applications.
