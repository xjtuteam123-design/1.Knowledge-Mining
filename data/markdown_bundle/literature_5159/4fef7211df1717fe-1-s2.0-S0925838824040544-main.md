ABSTRACT
This study attempts to analyse the effect of Nb and Mo addition on sinterability, microstructural features, phase 
evolution, and mechanical properties in a W-Ni-Fe composition-based alloy. The base alloy (90W-7Ni-3Fe) and 
the alloy compositions with Mo and Nb addition of (1.25, 2.5, & 3.75 wt%) independently were sintered in a 
reducing atmosphere at 1500 
◦
C for 60 min. Nb was selected for its lower thermal conductivity and specific heat, 
whereas Mo was selected for its grain refinement capabilities. Microstructural analysis by SEM revealed tungsten 
grain refinement from 28.60 µ m for the base alloy to 18.21 µ m upon 3.75 wt% Mo addition, while Nb addition, 
on the contrary, leads to a slight tungsten grain growth to 32.75 µ m. The grain size increase was directly pro -
portional to contiguity variation and inversely proportional to dihedral angle variation. Microstructure and phase 
analysis revealed Nb addition resulted in the formation of NbO
2 
along with the emergence of Nb rich – Fe lean 
phases (relative to matrix) with channel and stripped morphology. A maximum hardness value of 385.78 ± 25.4 
HV
1 
was found for the alloy with 3.75 wt% Nb addition, while the least hardness value of 296.65 ± 9.15 HV
1 
was 
found for the base alloy. An increment in compressive strength was seen with the addition of Mo and Nb to the 
base alloy, further Nb addition resulted in a higher compressive strength as compared to Mo addition in the alloy. 
The addition of Nb, a novel aspect of this study, could serve as a foundation for its inclusion as a viable alloying 
element in tungsten heavy alloy.
1. Introduction
Tungsten heavy alloys (WHAs) are considered as a metal matrix 
composite, where hard W-spheroids are embedded in the soft binder 
matrix phase [1] . The primary element, tungsten, has a very high 
melting point (3422 
◦
C) and a low vapor pressure (0.009 MPa at 2227 
◦
C) [2] . Thus, processing of tungsten becomes a challenging task using 
melting route. However, through the powder metallurgy route, tungsten 
can be processed and shaped at relatively lower temperatures of 
1800 – 2000 
◦
C [3] . In order to reduce the temperature of sintering to a 
level of 1470 – 1500 
◦
C, a minor amount of transition elements such as 
Fe, Ni, Co, and Cu are generally incorporated [1] . Owing to properties 
like high density, high strength and hardness and lower coefficient of 
thermal expansion, WHAs find applications in various strategic sectors, 
such as kinetic energy penetrators, counterweights, vibration damping 
devices, etc [1,4] .
Ni-based binders such as Ni-Fe, Ni-Co, Ni-Cu, and Ni-Fe-Co are 
mainly used to produce WHAs, due to the higher solubility of W in liquid 
Ni which occurs during liquid phase sintering [5 – 9] Typically, WHAs 
consists of 88 – 98 wt% of W and the rest is a binder. Near full densifi -
cation can be achieved through liquid phase sintering process [10] . The 
binder matrix phase plays a vital role while designing of WHAs as it not 
only binds the tungsten particles together but also provides toughness to 
the alloy by impeding crack propagation at its interface with tungsten 
[11,12] . In order to cater to the various applications mentioned above, 
microstructural parameters such as tungsten grain size, volume fractions 
of W-spheroids and binder phase, dihedral angle and contiguity must be 
modified. Mechanical properties of WHAs depend on the fraction of each 
phase: a strong but brittle tungsten phase and a soft but tough matrix 
phase. Tungsten heavy alloys with higher W content are used for kinetic 
energy penetrator, radiation shields, etc., while WHAs with higher 
matrix content are used for heavy duty electrical contacts [1] . The 
addition of alloying elements can also enhance strength, triggering 
strengthening mechanisms such as grain boundary strengthening and 
solid solution strengthening [5] . Addition of alloying elements such as 
Co, Mo, Mn, Re and Ta and compounds such as NiB, ZrB

WHAs used as kinetic energy penetrators are expected to have per -
formance parameters such as higher penetration depth and lower limit 
velocity [25] . These parameters can only be achieved if the penetrator 
maintains a sharp head during penetration. This ability to 
self-sharpening the head can be achieved by sloughing the inverted 
material, which depends on adiabatic shear banding in the plastically 
deformed head of the penetrator [25,26] . A lower specific heat as well as 
thermal conductivity helps to concentrate the heat generated during 
high strain rate deformation, taking place during penetration, which 
promotes adiabatic shear banding [6,8] . Elements such as Mo, Co and 
Mn have either thermal properties i.e. specific heat or thermal con -
ductivity in the desired range but not both, while Re and Nb possess both 
a lower specific heat and a lower thermal conductivity. Re cannot be 
used for economic reasons even though its high density (21.02 g/cc) 
favours using it. Nb, on the other hand, has a density of 8.57 g/cc, which 
is at the same level as that of Ni, Fe and Co, hence no drop in density is 
expected when using Nb. Niobium, being fully soluble in tungsten [27] , 
is also expected to refine tungsten grains as observed with Mo addition.
Despite Nb ’ s ideal physical properties as mentioned above, it has not 
been explored as an alloying element in WHA for kinetic energy pene -
trator applications. While a few studies have been carried out on me -
chanical alloying of Nb based WHA with oxide reinforcements [28,29] , 
none focuses on the effect of Nb on the microstructure, phase formation 
and mechanical properties of WHA. On the other hand, Mo addition in 
WHAs has been studied earlier and was found to be effective in refining 
tungsten grain size and providing solid solution strengthening to the 
matrix [30 – 35] . However, it has been observed in most reports that 
either molybdenum substituted tungsten or a very high concentration of 
Mo upto 50 wt% was used, which reduced the overall density of the 
WHA to under 17 g/cc. Meanwhile, a high density of 17 g/cc is required 
for WHA to be used as kinetic energy penetrators [36] , approximating to 
89 wt% W concentration-based WHA. Considering the above research 
gap, this study investigates the incorporation of Nb and Mo to a 
90W-7Ni-3Fe composition WHA. For this work three compositions of Mo 
(1.25, 2.5 and 3.75 wt% Mo) and three compositions of Nb (1.25, 2.5 
and 3.75 wt% Nb) in a 90 wt% fixed tungsten content with Ni and Fe in a 
ratio of 7:3 were studied. The alloy additions were done without altering 
the W content which was fixed at 90 wt% while alterations were done in 
the Ni and Fe content to compensate for the varying Mo and Nb addi -
tions. This was done not to allow the density of the alloys to drop below 
17 g/cc as it would impede their use as a kinetic energy penetrator. The 
comparative study helps to understand the role of these alloying ele -
ments individually. All the samples were subjected to a sintering tem -
perature of 1500 
◦
C for 60 min in a reducing atmosphere. The 
microstructure of all the samples was analyzed for various microstruc -
tural features such as tungsten grain size, dihedral angle, contiguity and 
phase fraction. XRD and EDS analysis confirm the phase formed in the 
alloys with multiple concentrations of alloying addition. Compressive 
strength, bulk-hardness of the sintered samples, and microhardness for 
individual phases were also evaluated to study the effect of alloying 
elements on mechanical properties.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Preparation of alloy powder mix and alloy fabrication
Alloys of different compositions with Mo and Nb addition were 
prepared using elemental powders. The base composition is 90W-7Ni- 
3Fe, denoted by WNF. The base composition was modified by adding 
elements Mo and Nb independently in the steps of 1.25 wt%. For all the 
compositions, a constant Ni to Fe ratio of 7:3 was maintained; the 
compositions of the investigated alloys are listed in Table 1 . The alloys 
are broadly classified into WNF, WNFMo, and WNFNb series of alloys. 
The theoretical density of all the compositions was calculated using the 
inverse rule of mixture [37] . Theoretical densities for all the composi -
tions of the WNFNb series of alloys and WNF alloy were found to be 
similar when density values were considered till 2nd decimal places. 
This is because density of Ni, Fe and Nb lies in a narrow range of 
7.87 – 8.90 g/cc due to which no significant change in densities is 
observed for the WNFNb series of alloys. The required quantity of 
powder for each alloy was measured carefully and hand-mixed in a 
mortar and pestle for 20 minutes. The hand-mixed powder was further 
mixed rigorously in a turbo mixer at 80 rpm for 60 minutes without any 
medium.
The mixed powder was compacted using a uniaxial press at 350 MPa 
pressure to obtain cylindrical samples of a diameter of 6 mm and height 
of 3 mm and cylindrical samples for compression testing of 6 mm 
diameter and 8 mm height. Further, these compacts were sintered in a 
tubular horizontal furnace (Tehreek, India) at a sintering temperature of 
1500

Solution reprecipitation causes grain growth during the liquid phase 
sintering of alloys such as WHAs [4] . Solution reprecipitation is a 
continuous step in which tungsten diffuses into the liquid matrix, which, 
upon getting supersaturated, diffuses out of the liquid matrix over 
tungsten grains [42] . Smaller tungsten grains or tungsten atoms from 
areas of high energy, such as sharp edges of powder particles, asperities, 
or concave regions diffuses in the liquid formed. Based on the data from 
tungsten content in the matrix ( Fig. 4 ) and the solution reprecipitation 
concept, some predictions for trends in tungsten grain size can be made 
for the alloys. High tungsten concentration in the matrix should lead to a 
higher tungsten grain size [10] .
Tungsten grain size distribution curves for all the alloy series are 
shown in Fig. 5 . WNF should have the highest tungsten grain size, fol -
lowed by WNFMo and WNFNb series alloys as discussed above. The 
prediction is true for the WNF and WNFMo series of alloys. Relatively 
finer tungsten grains were observed for WNFMo series alloys. As seen 
from Fig. 5 (a – d), the tungsten grain size distribution curve constantly 
shifts towards lower grain size. In contrast, coarser tungsten grains were 
found in WNFNb series alloys. WNF alloys contain tungsten grains with a 
mean size of 28.6 ± 11.17 µ m, which decreases to a lowest of 18.21 ±
8.75 µ m for WNF3.75Mo alloy. The bar representing the maximum grain 
count can be observed to shift to a lower grain size, and the spread of 
tungsten grain distribution narrows with the increase in Mo content. 
With the addition of Mo to the alloy, tungsten ingress in the matrix is 
restricted, reducing the kinetic rate constant for tungsten grain growth. 
As per the binary phase diagram for the Ni-Mo system a low temperature 
eutectic reaction is observed at 1317 º C at 35.8 at% Mo [27] . This liquid 
phase formed will be rich in Ni and Mo. The presence of Mo in the liquid 
phase can hinder the W diffusion in the matrix, resulting in lower 
tungsten grain size in the case of the WNFMo series of alloys [43] . Re -
striction to tungsten diffusion in the matrix leaves behind some of the 
smaller tungsten particles undissolved. These smaller tungsten particles 
then acts as sites for reprecipitation for tungsten atoms. The increase in 
the number of sites and the smaller size of these sites results in finer 
tungsten grains.
On the other hand, the WNFNb alloy series was found to have a 
higher tungsten grain size than other alloys, ranging from 29.42 to 
32.75 µ m, which opposes the general prediction. This is because Nb 
along with Ni and Fe, separately form these low temperature eutectics at 
a very early stage of sintering. As per the binary phase diagrams for Ni- 
Nb and Fe-Nb systems eutectic reactions takes place at 1282 º C and 1175 
º C at 18 at% and 40.5 at% Nb respectively in Ni-Nb system and 1372 º C 
at 12.1 at% Nb in Fe-Nb system [27] . Whereas no low temperature 
eutectic reaction is found for the Ni-W and Fe-Mo binary system. During 
heating the alloy to the sintering temperature Ni and Nb is expected to 
form a liquid phase. Rapid diffusion rates in liquid state will cause an 
increase in additive concentration in the liquid matrix. The formation of 
liquid phase at a very early stage of heating in WNFNb series of alloys 
causes an increased duration of the presence of liquid phase. Hence, the 
increased duration of liquid phase being present in the alloy increases, 
results in higher diffusion of W into the liquid phase, which upon rep -
recipitation, causes increase of grain size. This grain growth phenome -
non can also be confirmed using the classic grain growth Eq. (4)

The formation of oxides and their lower miscibility with the matrix and 
entrapment of pores are the reasons for the lower sintered density for the 
WNFNb series of the alloys. The densification parameter is defined by a 
relation ( Eq. 1 ) between green density, sintered density, and theoretical 
density, used to determine sinterability, as tabulated in Table 4 . The 
higher the densification parameter, the better is the sinterability of an 
alloy. The WNFNb series of alloys exhibit a lower densification param -

process on the basis of different diffusion mechanisms dependent on 
sintering temperature [49] . At lower sintering temperatures ( < 1400 
◦

through the matrix and grain boundary helps in densification. On the 
other hand, at high temperatures ( > 1400 
◦
C) in the liquid phase region, 
densification takes place by pore elimination due to rearrangement of 
tungsten grains, pore filling by the liquid phase and solution reprecipi -
tation of tungsten grains.
Fig. 8 (a) shows variation in contiguity with respect to mean W- grain 
sizes for WNF, WNFMo and WNFNb series of alloys. The contiguity 
values are tabulated in Table 4 . WNF alloy was found to have a low 
contiguity of 0.27. This lower contiguity results from a structure with 
high matrix penetration between the tungsten grains. Meanwhile, the 
WNFMo series of alloys were found to have a highly contiguous 
microstructure with contiguity increasing from 0.33 to 0.52 with Mo 
concentration. The highly contiguous microstructure found for WNFMo 
series of alloys was because of less tungsten being dissolved in the 
matrix, and a high fraction of smaller tungsten grains are available to 
form a skeleton. Smaller tungsten grains in WNFMo series alloys in -
crease the solid surface area to be covered by the liquid phase. Lower 
contiguity for the WNFNb alloy series is due to the larger tungsten grain 
size surrounded by the matrix. A larger tungsten grain size will need 
more liquid phase to fill in the space between the tungsten grains than 
smaller tungsten grains (of the same volume fraction). This lower con -
tiguity can help transfer the load to the matrix or change the crack path, 
if any, thereby increasing the strength of the alloy. The need is to opti -
mise contiguity and grain size. As per the discussion above, a smaller 
grain size microstructure leads to a higher contiguity, defeating the 
purpose of low contiguity. An ideal microstructure will have a tradeoff 
between grain size and contiguity to have good mechanical properties.

phases. It depends on the energy balance between solid-solid and liquid- 
solid surface energies. The lower the liquid-solid surface energies, the 
higher will be the liquid penetration between the solid phase [9] . A 
larger dihedral angle corresponds to a lower degree of liquid phase 
penetration between tungsten grains or high contiguity, as seen in Fig. 8
(b). It cannot be measured directly from the microstructure, as apart 
from the interfacial energies, it is also dependent on crystallographic 
orientations and duration for which liquid phase is present in the system,

alloys.

4. Conclusion
The focus of the study was to gain information about microstructure 
and phase formation in tungsten heavy alloy as a response to Nb and Mo 
addition. The microstructure revealed the formation of a self-similar 
microstructure of the alloy with and without Nb and Mo addition, 
manifesting rounded tungsten grains bound together by a matrix. Mo 
addition resulted in tungsten grain size refinement from 28.60 ±
