Until recently, the main way to increase the high-temperature strength of dispersion-hardening alloys con- 
sisted in changing the content of the alloying elements in order to determine their optimum concentrations that 
would ensure specified sets of properties. As a result, modern industrial high-temperature nickel alloys contain 
10 to 15 alloying and microalloying elements and the solubility of components in the reinforcing phases is 
maximum. However, this has not solved completely the problem of creep resistance and fracture of high-tem- 
perature materials, because the typical fracture in polycr3,stalline castings occurs along grain boundaries 
perpendicular to the axis of load application. The present work is concerned with the structure and properties 
of single-crystal high-temperature nickel alloys under cyclic loading. 
The use of directed crystallization has made it possible to 
prevent transverse (relative to the acting stresses) intergrain 
boundaries and create a columnar grain structure in castings. 
This has increased the high-temperature strength, plasticity, 
and heat resistance of alloys simultaneously [ 1 ]. The develop- 
ment of this technology has resulted in a single-crystal struc- 
ture of castings (with completely eliminated intergrain 
boundaries) and the creation of specially alloyed high-tem- 
perature nickel alloys. For example, the well-known single- 
crystal alloys PWA1480 and CMSX-2 [2] have been created 
by excluding carbon, boron, and zirconium from the compo- 
sition of nickel alloys (Table 1). This increased markedly the 
endurance of single-crystal alloys with the <001> axial 
growth orientation compared to the endurance of 
nickel alloys with columnar structure and made it 
possible to realize a high level of mechanical 
properties in castings with a lower number of al- 
loying elements. The high operating properties of 
single-crystal high-temperature nickel alloys are 
due not only to the elimination of intergrain 
boundaries in castings and removal of carbides 
and borides but also to the uniform distribution of 
particles of the ),'-phase in the matrix of the homo- 
geneous ),-solid solution. This is ensured, in par- 
ticular, by the higher melting temperature of sin- 
gle-crystal alloys compared to conventionally alloyed alloys 
(Table 2). This makes high-temperature diffusion annealing 
of them possible without the risk of fusion.

The second approach to the development of single-crystal 
high-temperature nickel alloys is based on the system Ni - A1 
- Cr - W - Ta - Mo (alloys of the SC-83 type [6, 7]). In this 
alloying system the ratio W/Ta is close to 1, and molybde- 
num is a sort of rhenium analog and is distributed preferen- 
tially in the y-solid solution (KMo =- 0.3). The distribution of 
W and Ta is similar to the first case. 
Heat treatment of single-crystal nickel alloys includes 
diffusion annealing in the temperature range (T~u t- T~m), 
quenching, and two-stage aging. In the process of this treat- 
ment there is a decrease in the liquation chemical inhomo- 
geneity accompanied by complete dissolution of the y'-phase 
(including the y'-phase of eutectic origin) and nonequilibrium 
segregations of the excess ~w-phase based on the tungsten 
solid solution. The microstructure formed consists of parti- 
cles of the y'-phase that are homogeneous with respect to 
composition, size, and morphology and are ordered in the 
matrix of the y-solid solution. 
The use of single-crystal alloys for the production of tur- 
bine blades of aircraft engines, which are the most intensely 
loaded of all existing gas turbine installations with respect to 
both the temperature and the spectrum of mechanical loads, 
requires a thorough investigation of their resistance to low- 
cycle and high-cycle fatigue (LCF and HCF). It should be 
noted that the relative proportion of alloy damage under LCF 
is constantly growing despite the high level of strength calcu- 
lations and the wide use of methods for the investigation of 
vibration stresses [8]. 
In the present work we made fractographic and micros- 
tructural analyses of smooth single-crystal specimens of two 
carbonless alloys after testing them for LCF resistance.

were made on a JSM-840 scanning electron microscope. The

Analysis of fractures and the microstructure of specimens 
of the investigated single-crystal alloys allowed us to estab- 
lish that the anomalous decrease in the endurance of some 
specimens in LCF tests (outliers) was caused by such struc- 
tural defects as accumulations of nonmetallic inclusions, 
"parasitic grains," isolated segregations of eutectic ~,'-phase 
undissolved in heat treatment, and surface recrystallization. 
These defects were caused by violations of the casting re- 
gime and the regimes of mechanical and heat treatment, and 
could be easily eliminated. 
Fractures of specimens contain not only surface fatigue 
sources of fracture but also internal ones, which are com- 
monly caused by casting micropores (Fig. 3a). Sources of fa- 
tigue were also observed on subboundaries. 
At 700 and 900°C cracks develop mainly over the y-phase 
or along the interface of the y- and ),'-phases in the plane 
(001) (Figs. 3b and 4), which is typical for LCF cracks in sin- 
gle-crystal nickel alloys [9, 10].

near micropores the thickness of the horizontal y-layers be- 
tween particles of the ,/'-phase becomes much greater than 
that of the vertical layers. Particles of the ,/'-phase are posi-

The relief of the fatigue zone in fractures of specimens is 
composed of microscopic grooves (Fig. 5a). The formation 
of microscopic grooves is caused by the interaction of sliding 
bands at the tips of LCF cracks, which results in dislocation 
pores ~Fig. 5b). 
In specimens with low endurance microscopic grooves 
were observed directly at fracture sources, and in high-endur- 
ance specimens they were located at a distance from them. 
In specimens of ZhS36 alloy tested for LSF at 700°C the 
interaction of sliding bands causes extrusion and entrusion on

It should be noted that in the region of high endurance at 
900°C the micropattem of LCF sources appearing in the proc-

The dislocation structure was investigated on foils cut 
from the working part of intact specimens after 104 loading 
cycles at 700 and 900°C and stresses close to the correspond- 
ing ultimate rupture strengths. 
At 900°C dislocation nets form in ZhS40 alloy in the 
solid solution and on the interface of the 33 and ,/'-phases 
(Fig. 6a). The density of dislocations is 10 I° cm -2. Some par- 
ticles exhibit dipoles (Fig. 6b). This dislocation structure is 
similar to the structure of specimens tested for creep at the 
end of the steady state. Its nature shows that plastic deforma- 
tion in ZhS40 alloy at 900°C occurs by slip in planes { 111 } 
in the "/-phase and dislocation climb in planes {001 } over the 
interface of the 33- and 33'-phases. 
At 700°C plastic deformation in the investigated alloys 
occurs preferentially by dislocation slip in the 33-phase and in 
particles of the ,/'-phase. 
Packing defects appear in ZhS40 alloy when particles of 
the 33'-phase are cut, which strengthen the alloy (Fig. 6c). 
As distinct from ZhS40 alloy, packing defects in ZhS36 
alloy are accompanied by multiple sliding bands (Fig. 6e), 
which initiate the formation of an LCF crack, as described 
above. 
In some regions of specimens of ZhS40 alloy we ob- 
served dislocation nets on the interface of the 33- and ,/'-phases 
(Fig. 6d), and specimens of ZhS36 alloy exhibited dislocation 
rows (Fig. 6f). 
It should be noted that in contrast to ZhS36 alloy, ZhS40 
alloy had no sliding bands even at high stresses. This indi- 
cates that the deformation resistance of 33'-particles in ZhS40 
at 700°C is higher than that for ZhS36. This is also verified 
by the higher LCF resistance of ZhS40 alloy.
