Introduction 
It seems plausible to develop high-strength AI- base alloys useful up to 698K in view of the behavior of nickel base 
superalloys which resist degradation of mechanical properties to 75 pet of their absolute melting temperature. For high 
temperature AI alloys, the dispersed hardening phase must not undergo phase trausformation to an undesirable phase during 
long time exposure at the temperature of interest. An additional factor to be considered is the stability of the hardening 
phase with respect to Ostwald ripening. This coarsening resistance is necessary so that the required strength level can be 
maintained after the long-time service at high temperatures. 
In a review paper by Adam (1), several AI-base systems containing transition metal elements for potential elevated 
temperature use were suggested based on low values of diffusivities and solubilities. The equilibrium crystal structures 
of AI3Ti , AI3V and AI3Zr are tetragonal I~2 , D022 and D023, respectively. At the temperatures of interest, around698K, 
vanadium and titanium are mutually substitutable in the form of AI3(Ti, V) (2). Much of titanium and vanadium can be 
substituted for zirconium in the D0.23-type AI3Zr compound, creating A13(Ti , Zr) and AI3(V, Zr), respectively (3). In 
particular, it has been reported that foe L12 -structured AI3M dispersoids form in the rapidly solidified A1- V- Zr and 
AI- Ti- Zr systems and both L12 and 13023- structured AI3M phases showed slow coarsening kinetics (4- 7). 
The L12 precipitates may form as spherical or cellular fan shaped morphology depending on alloy compositions and 
aging temperatures (4,7). Spherical ones are coherent and coplanar with the aluminum matrix. Even the D0~ phase in 
this system matches the AI lattice rather well, and the D0~ phase is believed to match the matrix better than the 
DO= phase does (3,8). Therefore, it is of interest how to design the A1 - Ti - V- Zr system and optimize the compositions 
of. added transition elements to maodmize the strengthening effects at high temperatures. 
Experimental Procedures 
A knowledge of multicomponent phase equilibria, and the effects of the various components on those equilibria, can 
provide the alloy developer with specific data on which to base decisions regarding compositional modifications" to existing 
alloys. Over the past decade, cousiderable effort has gone into development of computer software for performing 
thermochemieal calculations (9-10). What is required currently is the reliable thermody~amle data which allows realistic 
performance of calculations of phase equilibria of systems containing a wide range of elements. 
In the present study, our attention focuses on the precipitation and stability of the coherent strengthening phase, 
L12 AI3M. However, the effect of the coherency on the equilibrium state is not well systematized and evaluated even though 
Grujieic (11) calculated the coherent phase equilibrium between the ferrite and the coherent M2C carbide in the specific 
AF1410 steel composition with the knowledge of lattice misfit with the matrix. Therefore, only the D023-type AI3M 
phase was considered in this study with the expectation that L12 phase would behave similarly to the D023 phase. The effect 
of the presumably stable D022 and AI10V phases on the phase equilibria will be discussed in the next section.

Results and Discussion 
The efficient hardening of the alloy being designed will rely on its microstructural stability. In the present study, 
it is important to recognize that a significant consideration in the formation of A13M phase is the reduction of the Gibbs 
free energy, driving force, for precipitation from the aluminium matrix. Figure 1 shows the effect of mixed addition of 
Ti, V and Zr on the driving force for precipitation of AI3M. Numbers inside the triangle indicate the constant driving 
force contours, expressed in kJ/mol. While the Ti-rich side gives a higher driving force, the choice of alloy addition 
is not critical because of the shallow slope of the surface plotted in the figure. The effect of the alloy addition on 
the mole fraction of AI3M precipitated at 698K is shown in Figure 2. As the added amount of vanadium is increased, 
the mole fraction decreases generally. It is noteworthy that there exists a broad and flat region of 8 mole percent starting 
from the V-Zr side and extending toward the Ti-rich one. Any composition inside and around this region can be a 
candidate for the design explorations to maximize the amount of AI3M phase. The difference between Figure 1 and 2 
is as follows. The composition of AI3M phase which will precipitate early in aging and thus has the maximum driving 
force for the #oven alloy composition (as in Figure 1) is different from that of A13M phase in equilibrium with the matrix 
(as in Figure 2). 
Another point to be made is that D022- type AIaM and AI10V phases may play a role in equilibrium at the composition 
range of interest. Ternary or quarternary phase diagrams containing these elements are quite limited (12,15); however 
an isothermal phase diagram for this system at 748K is constructed in the form of Figure 3 from the solubility limits of 
D022 and D023 phases (3). It is expected that D023 phase is stable over 13022 in a combined composition range where 
the fraction of added ~conium is greater than 0.25. Though the above analysis is based on the X-ray results made 
at 748K, the situation will not change drastically at 698K, 50 degrees below. Therefore, the region in which the mole 
fraction of AI3M phase is maximized seems free from the interference of D022 phase. Chen et al. (7,17) observed the 
precipitation of stable AI10V and Al3Zr particles on the grain boundaries on aging when the ratio of V/Zr is 3. Lee 
et al. (15) calculated the isothermal Al-V-Zr system at 750K and showed that the stable AI10V phase starts to appear 
from the two phase equilibrium of aluminum matrix + D023 AI3M when the fraction of added vanadium is greater than 
0.74. However, this composition region is far from that of interest in the present work. 
For stable mechanical behavior, the dispersed phase should resist growth by Ostwald ripening. According to the LSW 
coarsening theory, originally developed for binary alloys (18-19), the cubed average particle size increases linearly with

of multicomponent M2C carbides in the secondary hardening reaction (20-21). When this approach is applied with minor 
modifications to the case of spherical multicomponent AI3M phase considered here, the rate constant, when expressed in

The researchers at Northwestern Univeraty (3- 8,17) have made extensive studies on these systems. Their emphasis 
is on how to reduce the misfit between the aluminum matrix and AI3M phase, thereby reducing the value of interfadal 
energy and retarding the coarsening kinetics of aluminides. However, the measured lattice constants of the AI3M phase 
are currently confined to those of Al3(Ti, Zr), AI3(V , Zr) and limited Al3 (Ti, V, Zr) systems with 0.75 vanadium fraction. 
If they cover the full range of quarternary AI3(Ti , V, 7I) compounds, and the proper relationship between interracial 
energy and lattice misfit parameter is formulated, then these effects can be incorporated into the calculation of coarsening 
rate constants and more reliable results could be obtained. At present, in the absence of such data, the values of molar 
volume of the particle phase and interracial energy were assumed constant. 
Calculations of fcc aluminum matrix/Al3M equilibria for mixed compositions involving Ti, V and Zr have been helpful 
for predicting the coarsening resistance of Al3M. The calculation of phase equifibria is not without limitations, of course. 
Results cannot be expected to be accurate if they are based on poor or limited data. In the present study, for instance, 
the data on the Al3M aluminides optimized were based on quite limited sources, the original data were measured at 748K, 
and our calculations were performed at 698K. In addition to such limitations, these approaches ignore strain energy and 
capillarity effects on phase equilibria, which for the very small particle sizes of interest in this study, are bound to be significant 
but difficult to incorporate into the model. 
This approach is suggested as a guide for alloy design and may not be quantitatively accurate for all pu~. 
Experimental work in the progress based on this study will follow.
