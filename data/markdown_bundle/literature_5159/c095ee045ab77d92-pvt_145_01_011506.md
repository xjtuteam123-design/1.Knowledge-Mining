Dissimilar Welding of Inconel
Alloys With Austenitic
Stainless-Steel: A Review
In this review paper, dissimilar welding between Inconel and austenitic stainless steel
along with its application has been outlined for high-temperature applications. The
mechanical and microstructural behavior of this dissimilar joint has been summarized
thoroughly in this article. Dissimilar welding of Inconel alloys and stainless steel (SS)
has massive demand in high temperature and high corrosive applications industries. Aus-
tenitic stainless steel contains 16–26 % of Cr and 6–12 % of Ni elements showing FCC
structures have good weldability and high corrosion resistance. Austenitic stainless steel
such as 304, 316 L, 304H, etc., containing austenite microstructure used in high-
temperature applications like power plants, heat exchangers, heating elements, aircraft,
and others. In addition, Ni-based Inconel alloys show high-temperature strength and cor-
rosion resistance and are frequently used in high-temperature applications. Ni-based
Inconel 718 alloy possesses excellent strength, corrosion resistance and creep resistance
at high temperatures are frequently used in combustion chambers, power plants, and tur-
bine blades applications. Inconel alloyed by elements Ti, Al and Nb attain strength by
forming phases such as Ç
/-Ni3(-Ti, Al), Ç//-Ni3Nb, and carbides such as MC and M 23C6,
nitrides, laves phase. The GTA dissimilar welding between expensive Inconel and
cheaper stainless steel is successfully used in nuclear power plants. The dissimilarity in
melting point, chemical composition, thermal, mechanical, and other properties between
these materials make welding challengeable. This review paper focused on problems
related to dissimilar welding like forming unmixed zone, elemental segregation, forma-
tion of laves phase, sensitization, microﬁssuring, and solidiﬁcation cracking.

1 Introduction
The welding technique has been used for many years for joining
similar and dissimilar materials. Dissimilar welding is subject of
interest among researchers because of welding advantages in
terms of joint strength. The dissimilarity is counted from many
factors such as dissimilarity in thickness for similar materials, dis-
similarity in materials like metals and ceramics, incompatible dis-
similar materials (magnesium and steel), etc. Dissimilar welding
is employed for cost reduction, improved energy efﬁciency, and
enhanced ﬂexibility in design. Research in dissimilar welding
proves that the method is successfully utilized in many industries,
including automobile, aerospace, nuclear reactors, automotive,
etc. The difference in melting temperature, thermal conductivity
differences, corrosion resistance, and coefﬁcient of thermal
expansion of two metals leads to high thermal stresses at the weld
zone, making dissimilar welding methods difﬁcult. Also, the dif-
ference in creep property of the materials for high-temperature
applications (power plant boiler tubing) results in failure of the
component [1–3].
For high-temperature applications taking reference to power
plants, the service temperature of power plants is mentioned in
Table 1 [4]. It is clear that to increase the working efﬁciency of
the power plants, required to increase the working pressure and
temperature by reducing the coal consumption and emission of
toxic CO
2 gases [ 5]. Improving the service temperature of power
plants by 10 /C14 C improves the efﬁciency by 0.5% by reducing the
coal consumption and CO 2 emission. Hence, increasing the serv-
ice temperature of the power plants is always advantageous.
Increasing the service pressure and temperature of the power
plants required materials with high thermal conductivity, high
creep strength, and high corrosion resistance. The most advanced
and common materials used in power plants currently for high
temperatures with high creep strength are Ni-based superalloys,
martensitic/ferritic steels, and austenitic stainless steels [ 6–9].
Therefore, this review paper is dedicated to Ni-based superalloy
and austenitic stainless steel for their high-temperature resistive
properties.
Ni alloys and stainless steels have a wide range of applications
extensively in nuclear power industries, aircraft engines, and
high-temperature components. Presently combination of Inconel
(Ni-based superalloys) with SS is the subject of interest among
researchers for high temperature and corrosive environment appli-
cations. Nickel alloys are termed “Superalloys” because these
alloys possess high strength and corrosion resistance at high tem-
peratures. Inconel 718, 625, 601, 316, and 740 are some of the
well-known Ni-based alloys. IN 601 contains aluminum and chro-
mium, which helps in the formation of a shielding oxide layer on
the surface [ 10]. Among existing Ni-alloys, IN 718 is well known
for its excellent strength, corrosion, and oxidation resistance, and
the high temperature creeps suitable for applications like the com-
bustion chamber of aero engines and turbine blades [ 11]. To
increase life and reliability of any component, it is necessary to
improve its corrosion resistance. SS is one of the best materials
used in a corrosive environment. Chromium in SS reacts with iron
to overpower the corrosion forming a passivating ﬁlm [ 12]. Aus-
tenitic SS 304 L has the property of superior corrosion resistance.

Major concerns during the welding of Ni-based super alloys and
SS are the selection of the suitable process and appropriate ﬁller
materials. Failing so causes failure of the welded joint and poor
mechanical and microstructural properties [ 16]. Many researches
have been recorded for the selection of suitable ﬁller material during
dissimilar welding of Ni-based alloys and stainless steel. A strong
conclusion was made that ﬁller materials that are Ni-based show the
best properties due to better thermal stability and good mechanical
properties [17]. Because of extreme differences subject to the melt-
ing point, chemical composition, and thermal expansion coefﬁcient,
dissimilar welding is found to be very difﬁcult and challengeable
[18]. These differences in Inconel and SS generate hot cracking, sol-
idiﬁcation cracking, residual stresses, and element segregation
affecting mechanical properties and fatigue strength of the welded
joints [19,20]. One of the major and primary problems during weld-
ing of Inconel is Nb rich segregation at weld zone and heat-affected
zone. The evolution of the Nb rich phase is called laves phase,
which highly affects mechanical properties like strength, hardness,
ductility, and stress rupture properties [21,22]. The presence of ele-
ments like Nb, Ti, and Mo promotes the formation of lave phase
through microsegregation [ 23,24]. These elements have a large
atomic radius, which enables poor solubility during welding. The
concentration of Niobium must range from 10% to 30% for the
laves phase formation. There are many possible ways suggested to
bring down or control the formation of laves phases High cooling
rate, less heat inputs, heat extraction techniques, pulsing techniques,
steep thermal gradient, low Nb ﬁllers, etc. [21].
Although there are many researches published related to dis-
similar welding of nickel alloys and stainless steel plates of thick-
ness between 3 mm to 200 mm, most of the grades were not
covered in the published reviews. Therefore, the ongoing review
mainly focuses on dissimilar welded joints of Inconel (mainly IN
718) and austenitic stainless steels (mainly SS 304L) by the differ-
ent GTAW processes. Also, thoroughly explains about ongoing
research trend on Inconel and austenitic stainless steels, which
provides answers to many queries about microstructural evolution,
parametric studies such as heat input, different ﬁllers, and their
effects, change in mechanical properties, creep resistance, residual
stress effects on welded joints and, metallurgical problems their
remedies related to different grades. This paper aims to provide a
clear vision of a selection of ﬁller materials and to enhance the
metallurgical and mechanical concerns over dissimilar welding of
Inconel and austenitic stainless steel. This review paper aims to
target the increasing demand for dissimilar welding of Inconel
and austenitic stainless steel over a wide range of industries and
suggests some valuable future considerations.

Inconel Alloys
2.1 Metallurgy of Austenitic Stainless Steel. SS holds an
important place in modern industries because of its extensive
applications, as discussed above. SS has been considered a long-
lasting material in power plants due to its microstructure stability
and mechanical properties [ 25]. SS generally contains at least
10.5% of chromium elements, which forms a surface oxide layer
that prevents oxidation and corrosion at high-temperature. Some
stainless steels (carrying chromium up to 25–30 wt. %) are used in
the application of temperatures as high as 1000
/C14 C. The majority
of stainless steel is weldable, and many need speciﬁc welding
processes. For all stainless steel, known changes occur in the weld
and heat-affected zones. Occurred changes can be in the form of
phase change, intermetallic formation, grain growth, elemental
segregation, or any reactions. These changes degrade the mechan-
ical properties of the welded joints, which should be considered
during design and manufacturing. Classiﬁcation of SS has been
done based on metallurgical phases such as martensitic, ferritic,
austenitic, and duplex steels. Austenitic stainless steels possess
good corrosion resistance in most environments. The service tem-
peratures for austenitic stainless steels are extremely high, up to
750
/C14 C or sometimes more than this temperature. These alloys are
generally listed as weldable stainless steels with proper care. Gen-
erally, 8% of Ni is added to promote austenite formation along
with C, N, and Cu. Carbon is added to achieve strength and is con-
sidered a good austenite promoter along with N. Austenitic stain-
less steels have excellent ductility and toughness and reﬂect good
elongation during tensile loading. The 300 series of austenitic
stainless steels are most common for wide applications based on
the 18Cr-8Ni system. SS 304 and 304 L hold the most important
place in this alloy series. The letter “L” represents the low carbon
level of 0.03 wt. %. 304 L and 316 L are commonly low carbon
grades having superior intergranular resistance in a corrosive
environment. These alloys are frequently used in applications

attack are major concerns. “H” grades (304H), which have a car-

2.2 Metallurgy of Inconel Alloys. Generally, Ni- alloys are
classiﬁed on a composition basis as pure nickel alloys, solid solu-
tion strengthened alloys (e.g., IN 625), Precipitation strengthened
alloys and some special Ni-alloys. Solid-solution strengthened
alloys are alloyed with elements Cr, Fe, Cu, W, and Mo. Addition-
ally, Ta, Co, and Re were also added to achieve solid solution
strengthening. Nb element is mainly added to form carbide or
obtain strength by forming Ni
3Nb. The carbide phases present in
these alloys are MC-type, M 23C6,M 7C3, and M 6C. The second
category of Ni-based alloys is precipitation-strengthened Ni
alloys. The unique combination of corrosion resistance and supe-
rior strength of precipitation-hardened alloys make it different.
The different alloying elements are added for various properties
such as the addition of Cr, Co, Fe, Mo, W, and Ta for solid solu-
tion strengthening, the addition of Nb, Ti, and Al promotes precip-
itate forming, for oxidation resistance Cr, Al, and Ta, for hot
corrosion resistance Ti, cr, La, and Th, for stress rupture and creep
B and Zr imparts required properties. The welding metallurgy of
precipitation-hardened alloys is complex in nature. The formation
of secondary phases was likely to occur in the weld zone, and ele-
mental segregation occurs in HAZ, affecting the mechanical prop-
erties of the alloys. Postweld heat treatment is needed to retain the
properties of the weldments. Precipitation-strengthen alloys are
called superalloys due to their exceptional ability to retain high
strength and corrosion resistance at higher temperatures [ 44].
Inconel alloys belong to Ni-base superalloys well known for
their excellent high-temperature strength and corrosion resistance.
Inconel alloys are primarily alloyed by adding Ti, Al, and Nb to
achieve strength through precipitation strengthening by creating
phases Ç
/-Ni3(-Ti, Al) and Ç//-Ni3Nb. In addition, Ni-alloys also
contain Cr, Fe, Mo, and Co to strengthen by solid solution
strengthening process. Ni-based alloys achieve ultimate tensile
strength over 1380 MPa 0.2% offset yield strength of 1035 MPa
through heat treatment. These properties make Ni-alloys excellent
for applications in a gas turbines, aerospace equipment, heat-
treating equipment, and nuclear reactors [ 45].
The addition of the different elements to the nickel-based alloys
adapts the microstructure enhancement, modifying the mechanical
and metallurgical properties by forming precipitates, carbides,
nitrides, borides, etc. The addition of the Nb element provides
solid solution strengthening. Initially, Nb is added to form car-
bides. Carbide precipitates in Ni-based alloys play an important
role in providing strength at the grain boundaries and also affect
the corrosion behavior of the alloy. Cr is an important carbide for-
mer, having a high afﬁnity with carbon. During the welding pro-
cess, the high heat generation allows Cr to interact with C,
forming M
23C6 and M 7C3 carbide precipitates on the grain boun-
daries. The chromium carbide formation tends to decrease the
amount of chromium in HAZ, lowering the corrosion resistance.

Ti to MC-type carbides [ 46]. The phases commonly present in Ni-

During the welding process, the formation of the laves phases
in the IN 718 fusion zone lowers the mechanical property (tensile,
creep, fatigue, and rupture toughness) of the welded joint. The
brittle and harder intermetallic laves phase compounds show poor
plastic ﬂow during tensile loading [ 48]. Laves phases provide a
low-energy path for crack initiation and rapid propagation [ 51].
The formation of laves phases can be related to the heat input and
cooling rate and can be controlled through welding techniques
and parameters by varying the heat input and cooling rate. It has
been reported that in the GTAW process, high heat input and slow
cooling rate promoted the Nb segregation developing laves phase
around the dendrite cores. In the EBW process, the lower heat
input and fast cooling rate successfully control the laves phase
formation. Through pulsing current in GTAW and magnetic arc
oscillation, laves phase formation was successfully controlled by

3 Need for Dissimilar Joining of Inconel Alloys and
Austenitic Stainless Steels
Currently, dissimilar welding is very popular in various indus-
tries like chemical industries, petrochemical, and nuclear indus-
tries because dissimilar joints provide beneﬁts of two unique and
expensive materials by reducing the cost of materials in different
service conditions such as corrosion and heat resistance. Dissimi-
lar welding joints are challenging in terms of their nonuniformity
in joint microstructure and mechanical and thermal joint proper-
ties. Non- uniformity of dissimilar welded joints is due to differ-
ences in compositions and different properties of individual
materials. Mostly dissimilar welding is used in oil reﬁneries and
nuclear power plants, where the joining of two materials like
stainless steels and Nickel based alloys or manganese steels and
austenitic steels are required to be joined as one unit [ 52,53]. Dis-
similar welding between Inconel and austenitic stainless steel is
very popular in industries operating at high temperatures. This
bimetallic combination preserves superior strength and excellent
corrosion resistance at elevated temperatures, which attracts
industries like nuclear power plants and aerospace industries.
Superalloys such as IN 625, 617, and 718, etc., are frequently
used in high-temperature applications such as submarines, gas tur-
bines, and nuclear reactors. These alloys retain their properties
associated with strength at elevated temperatures, corrosion resist-
ance, creep rupture strength, toughness, and thermal fatigue
between 150
/C14 C and 1500 /C14 C, making them appropriate candidates
for these applications [ 54]. Cost-effective dissimilar joints without
compromising the service life of the component are one more rea-
son for this bimetallic combination. Inconel material is a rela-
tively expensive alloy. To produce economical dissimilar joint
austenitic stainless is suitable material utilized in high temperature
(1150
/C14 C) applications and hence accepted as an excellent alterna-
tive [ 42]. Dissimilar welding of austenitic stainless steels and IN
625 were broadly used in the nuclear and petrochemical indus-
tries. Inconel is considered a costly material, and SS is a compara-
tively cheaper material used in high-temperature applications.
Subscale boiler used for the stirling conversion system is a com-
mon application for this bimetallic combination. For condenser
AISI 316 and boiler IN 625 and SS 304l were speciﬁcally used
[55].
Many welding techniques have been executed to weld Inconel
and austenitic stainless steel to achieve proper mechanical and
microstructural properties in past years. In a study carried out on
IN 617 and SS 310 of 12 mm thick plates, microstructural tests
were performed on Gas tungsten arc welded (GTAW) joints, and
it was noticed that the weld is highly sensitive to solidiﬁcation
cracking, which depends on the right selection of ﬁller materials.
Weld samples with ﬁller SS 310 show the lowest melting point
and contain a Cu-rich secondary phase, which is prone to solidiﬁ-
cation cracking. The researcher also concludes that cracks origi-
nated at the partially mixed SS 310 base metal side due to the
presence of secondary phases. Use of the IN 617 and SS 310 ﬁller
displays the prime and worst weldability, respectively [ 42]. Also,
in similar work, IN 82, IN A, and IN 617 was used as ﬁller to
weld IN 657 and SS 310, and the best mechanical properties were
recorded by IN A ﬁller [ 56]. Likewise, welding of IN 718 and 310
using IN 82 ﬁller material by GTAW gives the best mechanical
and corrosion result [ 41].
Pulsed current gas tungsten arc welding (PCGTAW) is one of
the most advantageous compared with conventional GTAW.
Many of the studies prove promising results by applying the puls-
ing current technique. Pulsing current welding can help in (a)
reducing distortion and residual stresses (b) control upon heat
input (c) reﬁned grains (d) minimizing porosity and amount of
segregation (e) reducing the width of HAZ. These advantages of
Pulsed current gas arc welding solve most of the metallurgical dif-

PCGTA weld with ERNiCu-7 ﬁller, which shows improved
mechanical properties. Also, tensile fracture occurred at the base
metal SS 316l [ 57]. The dissimilar welding of IN 625 and 304
using CrMo-3 ﬁller with CCGTA and PCGTA techniques shows
that less segregation was there when PCGTA was employed, and
also less chromium carbides occurred at HAZ [ 58]. Welding of
aluminum alloys also experienced the best mechanical properties
using the PCGTAW technique. Many researchers have explained
the advantage of using the PCGTAW technique. During PCGTA,
reﬁnement of the weld zone grains takes place along with the
reduced width of HAZ. Also, less amount segregation reduces the
amount of residual stress because of the control over the heat
input, which improves the mechanical properties of the weld zone
[59]. Dissimilar welding through TIG welding of IN 690 and SS
304 L using ﬁllers IN 52 and IN 82 shows the titanium and the
chromium carbide precipitates around grain boundaries and inter-

properties [60].

ﬂow, producing deeper and narrower welds shown in Fig. 6 [61].

strip near the pool boundary [ 72]. The formation of the unmixed

3.1.3 Sensitization. Corrosion is one of the major problems
related to stainless steel weldments. It is mainly related to the sen-
sitization effect of HAZ, which leads to intergranular corrosion.
Sensitization mainly occurs due to the precipitation of chromium
carbides and several other intermetallic phases at grain boundaries
[77]. Chromium is considered the primary alloying element that
provides stainless steel corrosion resistance. The occurrence of
chromium carbide (mainly Cr
23C6) makes chromium a depleted
zone which permits intergranular stress corrosion cracking
(IGSCC). Sensitization occurs mainly when stainless steel is
exposed to elevated temperatures for a long period, such as during
the welding process. Figure 9(a) shows a nonsensitized sample of
SS 304, and Fig. 9(b) shows sensitized SS 304 containing chro-
mium carbide as thick dark lines at the grain boundary. The fac-
tors which promote sensitization are the amount of carbon in the
material, time and temperature of heat treatment, grain size, and
grain orientation [78]. Corrosion can also be affected by heat input
and cooling rate of the weld zone because these reasons may lead
to the segregation effect and generate chromium depleted zones
[79]. A similar conclusion was made when dissimilar welding of
alloy 690 and SS 304 L was carried out by the GTA welding tech-
nique. Chromium carbide precipitates were formed near the grain
boundary. Decreasing the content of chromium leads to a decrease
in the corrosion resistance to intergranular attack [ 60].
3.1.4 Microfissuring and Strain Age Cracking. During fusion
welding of Inconel material, a liquid ﬁlm is likely to form near the
grain boundary of HAZ, which creates plastic strain during cool-
ing, and if a liquid ﬁlm fails to sustain the stress-induced ther-
mally, then microcracks are likely to occur. These processes are
termed intergranular Microﬁssuring [ 80]. The cause of intergranu-
lar microﬁssuring is primarily due to liquation of grain bounda-
ries, which appears after the segregation of the elements at
boundaries (Figs. 10(a)–10(d)). In IN 718, the presence of boron
forms molten carbides to moist the grain boundaries, leads to
microﬁssuring effects. Coarser grains of the base material are
highly prone to microﬁssuring effects. The annealing process can
help in achieving ﬁner grain of base material in the temperature
range of 955
/C14 C–982 /C14 C[ 82]. Postweld heat treatment results in
the formation of strain age cracking at HAZ and further propa-
gates in base materials [ 45].

3.1.6 Elemental Diffusion. Carbon diffusion is another prob-
lem associated with dissimilar welding processes because of dis-
similarity in the coefﬁcient of thermal expansion at weld line and
induction of thermal stresses [ 83]. Carbon diffusion leads to the
form a soft zone, which is carbon deﬁcient, and a hard zone which
is carbon-rich [ 84]. The difference in concentration in chromium
content is one reason for carbon diffusion, diffuses from low to
high chromium side, rather having an equal concentration of car-
bon in both materials. The difference in the carbon content of soft
and hard zone tends to develop a difference in strength, hardness,
and other mechanical properties. Also, the diffusion of carbon is
subject to changes in microstructural properties. Carbon diffusion
formed chromium and molybdenum carbides in the carbon-rich
zone and hardened that zone; also carbon deﬁcient zone forms a
soft decarburized zone having less hardness. The amount of carbon
diffusion, temperature, and types of alloys decides the type of
microstructure evolution during dissimilar welding [ 83–86]. This
problem of the dissimilar joint can be prevented by using nickel-
based ﬁller material and nickel-based alloys having a buttering
layer. The use of nickel-based ﬁller reduces the carbon diffusion as
they have less coefﬁcient of thermal expansion, which induces less
stress around weld interfaces. Whereas a buttering of nickel alloys
during weld preparation limits the carbon diffusion [ 83,84,87].

3.1.8 Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC). Stress corrosion
cracking (SCC) starts with crack initiation and propagation in met-
als having high corrosion resistance, such as SS and Ni alloys.
SCC occurs when these alloys are subjected to tensile loading in a
corrosive medium, promoting the fracture of structural metals
[93]. SCC in SS occurred due to induction of tensile stress, micro-
structural susceptibility, and corrosive environment. The SCC can
be highly promoted by the presence of chloride ions, which may
highly reduce the strength of stainless steel. The induction of resid-
ual stress in HAZ can lead to induce intergranular stress corrosion
cracking, promoting cracks and corrosion along grain boundaries.
In chlorine containing environment, SS shows transgranular stress
corrosion in the form of cracks. Ni-alloys show great resistance
toward SCC because of their less ductility induced by thermal
aging or cold working. Compared to stainless steel, Ni-alloys show
more resistance due to immunity in the hot chloride solution [ 94].
3.1.9 Additional Metallurgical Problems. SS alloys experi-
ence some metallurgical problems during welding includes dilu-
tion of the weld metal, carbon migration, and hot and cold
cracking. Carbon migration mainly affects the life of weld and
ends with premature failure. Carbon migration mainly occurs
when the postweld heat treatment process is carried [ 95]. Dilution
occurs due to the mixing of the base materials and molten ﬁller
material before subsequent fusion [ 96]. Kinetics of the phase
transformation is highly affected by dilution and retains new
phases of composition between ﬁller and base material because of
the ﬁnal weld metal chemical composition difference [ 94]. Ele-
mental composition differences between ﬁller and base materials
promote the melt of the base material and resolidiﬁcation without
ﬁller material dilution. The dilution effect is one of the reasons for
the formation of unmixed zones. The formation of unmixed zones
is higher in Ni-based alloys are used because of the sluggish
nature of molten nickel metal and resist the complete mixing.

4 Creep Behavior of the Dissimilar Welded Joints
In power plants, for high-temperature applications, Ni-based
alloys are used, which offer outstanding creep strength at 700 /C14 C.
To reduce material cost, high Cr martensitic heat resistant steels
are used below 650 /C14 C[ 108,109]. Generally, the combination of
Ni alloys and martensitic steels is used for advanced ultrasuper-
critical (A-USC) thermal power plants. Ni alloys have excellent
creep strength, which makes them a good candidate over ferritic
and austenitic steels in gas turbine applications and high-
temperature components for boilers [ 109].
Premature interfacial creep failure is a major issue related to
dissimilar welding between Ni-base alloys and stainless steels.
The failure mainly occurs on the SS material side due to lower
creep strength than Ni- alloys. Problems quoted for interfacial
failures include carbide and oxide notch and formation of interfa-
cial stresses. Creep failure can also be promoted by the heteroge-
neity of the interface region [ 110,111]. The presence of carbides
(especially M
23C6) at ﬁne grain HAZ (FGHAZ) tends to coarser
at a faster rate than carbides present in base material or weld zone
at the time of PWHT. Coarsen carbide tends to attract the creep
voids in HAZ because FGHAZ is constrained by weld metal and
base metal of high creep strength [ 112]. The M
23C6 carbides are
less stable at high temperatures and coarsen at a faster rate, result-
ing in unable to resist the creep [ 113]. In a study on 9-12Cr steels,
the creep failure follows the type IV failure mechanism, which is
located in the FGHAZ. The microstructure that appeared in
FGHAZ is considered to have the lowest creep strength. FGHAZ
experiences the austenitic phase temperature for a very short
period of time, which does not allow carbide precipitates to dis-
solve properly. On cooling, these precipitates remain compara-
tively ﬁne and modify into martensite [ 114].
Some studies suggested that microstructural problems (carbide
and oxide at the interface) are only facilitating factors, not domi-
nating factors for interfacial failures. The main reason for interfa-
cial failures is the mismatch of coefﬁcient thermal coefﬁcient and
the formation of creep strength gradient across the interface
[110,115–117]. Residual stress in the welded joint is one more
reason for creep crack. The rate of creep growth is two times
more in FGHAZ than in the base material [ 118].

because of the presence of a higher quantity of delta ferrite in the
Multipass welded zone. The austenite and ferrite interface at the
weld zone promotes the constructive path for creep deformation.
Cavitation is the prime reason that leads to creep failure of the aus-
tenitic steel 316 L(N) in both cases, as presented in Fig. 19 [120].
The dissimilar shielded metal arc welding of ferritic steels
(2.25Cr-1Mo, 9Cr-1Mo, and 9Cr-1MoVNb) and alloys 800 were
investigated for creep failure at 832 K of joints. The creep strength
of each joint shows lower creep strength than the ferritic base
material. The fracture location and fracture mechanism are found
to be different with varying load. Initially, at higher load creep
fractures were observed in the ferritic base metal. Lowering the
creep load shifts the fracture location to the intercritical HAZ of
ferritic steels following the type IV failure mechanism. Type IV
failure moves in HAZ and ends with creep cavitation in HAZ. Fur-
ther decreasing the load shifted the failure location to the weld
zone of dissimilar welded ferritic and Inconel joints. Creep cavities
were found at the weld interface subject to the presence of coarser
gains at the weld interface. Cavitation mechanism is a because of
the higher stress concentration across the ferritic and austenitic
weld interface. Cavitation initiated with the nucleation of the small
creep cavities links together and ends with creep failure [ 117].
There is very limited work recorded on the study of creep fail-
ure of the dissimilar joint of Inconel alloys and austenitic stainless
steels. The authors aim to perform an experiment on the dissimilar
joint of IN 718 and austenitic SS 304l by GTAW process using
different ﬁllers and to analyze the creep failure of these joints.

such as nuclear power plants, aerospace industries, chemical
industries, and many more high-temperature applications.

In Arc welding, induced residual stresses are dangerous for
structural service life. The nature of the induced RS can be either
tensile or compressive, which majorly affects the complete
strength of the weldments. The tensile stresses formed at the weld
zone promote brittle fracture and fatigue failure, and compressive
RS tends to enhance the yield property of the weldments. Tensile
stress is likely to form in the weld zone in the welding direction,
whereas compressive stress likely to form aside from the weld
centerline balancing the tensile stresses [ 132]. In arc welding, the
induced value of residual stress depends mainly on types of joints,
heat input, number of passes, welding speed, base plate clamping
method, root gap, and misalignment of parts to be welded [ 133].
The clamped base materials experience more longitudinal RS than
unclamped base materials. Also, unclamped base materials experi-
ence less angular distortion [ 134]. Less residual stress induction
has been reported with V groove conﬁguration than with U groove
geometry [135].
Related to dissimilar welding, the residual stress generation
mechanism is very complex and not easy to predict due to differ-
ences in mechanical, metallurgical, and physical properties of the
materials and ﬁller material involved. The dissimilar joint is
widely used for high-temperature applications such as fast
Breeder test reactors, steam generators, power plants, etc. It is
very prone to frequent failures. Residual stress in the dissimilar
welded joint is one of the major problems subject to the failures,
degrading the service life much before the expected time. Dissimi-
lar welding of ferritic/austenitic steels experiences failures in
HAZ region of ferritic steel. These bimetallic combinations are
frequently owned in steam generators. At Indira Gandhi Center
for research, failure of 2.25Cr-1Mo ferritic and SS 316 L austen-
itic stainless steel dissimilar weld occurs. Later investigation con-
cludes that nucleation of a crack in the root region propagates
through the fusion line up to HAZ of ferritic steel extended to the
fusion line. Residual stress at the welded joint is one major reason
stated for this failure. Considering it a serious concern, an experi-
ment was performed by buttering ferritic steel side by IN 82 (as
shown in Fig. 23) and stress relief heat treatment was performed
before welding. Residual stress was evaluated by the nondestruc-
tive XRD method. Comparing the results, it was found that the
residual stress at the weld is almost the same with and without
buttering dissimilar welds. Comparing HAZ, reduction in residual
stress was recorded in the case of buttering dissimilar weld attrib-
uted to the stress relief heat treatment of ferritic steel before weld-
ing and also related with the coefﬁcient of thermal expansion and
thermal conductivity of IN 82 having the intermediate value of
base materials [ 136]. Generally, the formation of residual stress
results in premature failure and cracking of welded joints. Resid-
ual stress is most likely to occur in HAZ along weld length and
weld metal, limiting the yield stress [ 94].
Brittle fracture, SCC, and fatigue failures are effective causes
of welded joints by residual stresses. There are types of mechani-
cal methods to calculate the intensity of RS in welds along with
the ﬁnite element method. The stress concentration sites at the
welded joint promote premature failure and cracking in the
welded structures. During the cooling of the weld, the residual
stress is distributed across the weld length in HAZ and weld
metal, limiting the yield stress [ 25].

face residual stress measurement due to less penetration. It has
frequently been used in many studies related to dissimilar weld-

input results in lower residual stress at weld joint [ 138].
The hole-drilling method is the most used semidestructive
method for residual stress measurement. The hole-drilling method
is capable of measuring residual stresses along with the thickness
in magnitude, direction, and sense. It gives good accuracy and reli-
ability, standardized test procedures, and easy practical application.
The hole produced or level of damage is a very small drilled hole,
which is later repairable or tolerable. The drilled hole relieved the
locked-in stresses and related strains on the surface to be measured
with the help of strain gauges ﬁxed around the drilled hole. With
the help of measured strain, stress around the hole is calculated.
The ring-core method generally provides larger surface strain over
the hole drilling method, but the ring core method is hardly used
because of the high degree of damage to the work [131].
In a study, IN 718 and SS 316 of 5 mm thick plates were fabri-

BM and HAZ of both metals. The result in the IN 718 HAZ shows
the acceptable result free of localized permanent deformation.

Postweld heat treatment (PWHT) is the most widely used
method to reduce residual stress effectively. There are countable
disadvantages of the PWHT process limiting the use to reduce
residual stress of dissimilar welded joints. First, because of the
difference in the thermal expansion coefﬁcient of the two materi-
als, PWHT process is not as successful as for similar welded
joints. Second, RS distribution in dissimilar welding is much
more complex that, after applying PWHT, becomes more complex
and difﬁcult to evaluate. Third, in the case of large component
applications such as large pressure vessels and pipes, the PWHT
process becomes costly and requires a huge heating setup. This
limitation of PWHT forces researchers to opt for modern techni-
ques, including ultrasonic peening, localized cooling, and local-
ized high-pressure rolling and vibratory stress relief methods. In
addition, recently an experiment including dissimilar welding
proves that distortion can be eliminated by adding a CO
2 cooling

and buckling results in reducing residual stress in the dissimilar
welding. This process was named dynamically controlled low-
stress no distortion (DC-LSND) by its introduced Guan. The rapid
cooling process still has many unknown parameters, which limits
this process up to the research level only. Improper application of
this process can lead to adverse effects on residual stresses [ 144].

7 Conclusions
From the above literature review, the following conclusion has
been obtained:
/C15 Dissimilar welding of Inconel and SS includes many metal-
lurgical and mechanical problems such as unmixed zone, for-
mation of laves phases, sensitization, microﬁssuring and
strain age cracking, Boron segregation, elemental diffusion,
hot cracking, stress corrosion cracking, and many more.
These problems promote the degradation of the weld
mechanical property.
/C15 The laves phase formation is a very common problem related
to welding of the Inconel material. There are many ways
mentioned to restrict the formation of lave phases and lower-
ing the effect on weld properties.
/C15 A suitable selection of ﬁller material can restrict the forma-
tion of laves phases and many metallurgical problems. From
the above review, Nb ﬁllers are considered well suited to
lower the amount of laves phases and improve the mechani-
cal and metallurgical properties.
/C15 The difference in composition and melting point of ﬁller and
base material promotes the formation of the unmixed zone
near HAZ.
/C15 Heat input and cooling rate is an important factor that affects
the weld characteristics. High heat input and slow cooling
rate lead to grain growth at the fusion zone, the reason for
many metallurgical like elemental segregation and laves
phase formation.
/C15 Employing the pulsing current GTAW (PCGTAW) process
controls the heat input and provides grain reﬁnement at the
fusion zone, enhancing the metallurgical and mechanical
properties.
/C15 Creep failure in the dissimilar joint of Inconel and SS is
mainly due to the formation of carbides, oxides, and interfa-
cial stresses.
