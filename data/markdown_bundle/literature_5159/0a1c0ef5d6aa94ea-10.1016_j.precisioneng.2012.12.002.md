Metal cutting is a process that uses tools to create new surfaces by imparting intense shear stresses and
high
 strain rates on the work material. Consequently, the mechanical properties of the work material
directly
 inﬂuence its machinability, and high strength materials such as titanium are notoriously difﬁ-

machining
 difﬁcult-to-cut materials. The method involves using a laser beam to locally heat and reduce
the
 ﬂow stress of the material ahead of an advancing cutting tool, making the metal shearing process
easier.

ability

 cutting temperature and chip formation is discussed. The effectiveness of the LAM process in
reducing
 the cutting pressure of Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al alloy is also compared against other alloys including
commercial-purity
 titanium and Ti–6Al–4V.

1. Introduction
Titanium alloys are in high demand due to their superior combi-
nation of physical and mechanical properties such as high speciﬁc
strength and corrosion resistance. Most of the current and potential
applications involving titanium alloys require signiﬁcant machin-
ing. Of all of the titanium alloys, metastable /H9252-titanium alloys are
becoming particularly attractive for a number of high-value added
applications due to their unique range of properties. These alloys
retain the /H9252-phase upon quenching from above the /H9252-transus,
however, a variety of other phases including /H9275 and /H9251-variants
can occur during heat treatment below the /H9252-transus. Metastable
/H9252-alloys can exhibit diverse properties depending on the com-
position and phase constituents. Some metastable /H9252-alloys are
pseudoelastic owing to the reversible /H9252 → /H9251′transformation. These
alloys can offer super elastic ductility, low stiffness and shape mem-
ory behaviour. Other alloys are /H9251-precipitation hardened and offer

They offer potential advantages such as ﬂexibility in heat treating

A metastable /H9252-titanium alloy was ﬁrst applied on the Lock-

Since then the alloy has been extensively applied in landing gear
and other applications for its high strength forging capabilities and
corrosion resistance, resulting in signiﬁcant weight and cost reduc-
tions [4]. Recent applications of this alloy are the mast and main
motor system of the helicopter, arrestor hook structures for mili-

low thermal conductivity. The high material strength requires
high cutting forces to instigate plastic shear, and consequently,
high cutting temperatures result. The problem is exacerbated for

temperature strength. Not surprisingly, Arrazola et al. [5] have
reported that metastable /H9252-titanium alloys are the most difﬁcult
of all titanium alloys to machine.
A recent technological development to improve the machinabil-
ity of various materials is known as laser assisted machining (LAM).
In this technique, the laser beam acts as an external heat source to
locally heat and soften the workpiece material ahead of the advanc-
ing cutting tool. This creates a rise in temperature in the shear zone,
reducing the hardness, ﬂow stress and work hardening character-
istics of the workpiece material, thereby facilitating ductile plastic
deformation during machining and enhancing the productivity [6].
The laser assisted machining method has been widely investi-
gated and Sun et al. [7,8] and Dandekar et al. [9] have reported
to improve the machinability of various /H9251//H9252 or /H9251-alloys such
as Ti–6Al–4V and commercial purity (CP) titanium. However, it
remains unknown whether the process is suitable for metastable /H9252-
alloys because such materials are speciﬁcally designed to maintain
strength at high temperatures, hence, the machinability of these
materials may not improve using the LAM process. The purpose of
this paper is to investigate whether the cutting pressure of the high
strength Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al /H9252-titanium alloy can be reduced using
the laser assisted machining method. The cutting forces and tem-
peratures over a wide range of cutting parameters including cutting
speed and feed rate is investigated and compared to conventional
machining. The cutting pressures during laser assisted machining
the /H9252-titanium alloy at a range of strain rates are compared to CP
Ti /H9251-alloy and Ti–6Al–4V /H9251//H9252-alloy.
2.
 Experimental procedure
2.1. Workpiece material
The Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al titanium alloy was cast using vacuum arc
melting process (remelted 3 times). The /Phi1620 mm cast ingot was
then forged in the /H9252-ﬁeld above 830 ◦C, deformed 60%, and then
deformed 70% in the dual phase zone in order to reduce the grain
size of the coarse cast grain structure. The material was ﬁnally
forged to /Phi1110 mm in the dual phase zone and subsequently
reduced to /Phi150 mm. The chemical composition of this titanium
alloy is shown in Table 1.
The forged bar was then solution heat treated at 750 ◦C for 3 h
followed by air cooling, then aged at 510 ◦C for 8 h followed by
air cooling (STA). Fig. 1 shows the optical microstructures of the
Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al alloy after heat treatment. The solution treatment
is a standard sub-/H9252 transus treatment that produces equiaxed-
/H9251 of approximately 1–2 /H9262m and the ageing produces very ﬁne
acicular-/H9251 within retained /H9252-grains. The hardness of the material
is 375 ± 10 HV.

3.2. Cutting temperature
The cutting temperature varied signiﬁcantly and depended on
the cutting speed and whether or not the laser was used during
machining (shown in Fig. 7). During conventional machining there
is a sharp increase in temperature from about 300 ◦C at 5 m/min
to 800 ◦C at 50 m/min, after which there is a more gradual increase
in temperature to about 1000 ◦C at 150 m/min, beyond which there
was no ﬂuctuation in the temperature measured. An opposite trend
was observed when laser assisted machining was performed on the
alloys. At lower cutting speeds the cutting temperatures were high.
As the cutting speed increased the cutting temperatures sharply
decreased from about 1700 ◦C at a cutting speed of 5 m/min to
1150◦C at 50 m/min, after which it gradually decreased to about
1100◦C at 150 m/min and then continued to gradually decrease to
about 1050 ◦C at 270 m/min.
The cutting temperature was also found to be a function of
the feed rate for both the LAM and CM tests. Fig. 8 shows that
the temperature is fairly constant over all tested feed rates dur-
ing conventional machining with gentle linear increase with feed
rate. There appears to be a certain degree of scatter in this mea-
surement. This may occur because the feed rate directly inﬂuences
the chip thickness, which in turn could affect the temperature mea-
surement (since thicker chips have a higher heat capacity or could
shield the cutting zone more effectively than thin chips). In the LAM
tests, however, the cutting temperature was observed to decrease
with increasing feed rate, similar to that observed with increasing
cutting speed. A reduction in temperature of approximately 270 ◦C
occurred as the feed rate is increased from 0.05 mmpr to 0.28 mmpr
during LAM.

4. Discussion
The fundamental principal of laser assisted machining is the use
of a laser beam to impart localised heating ahead of the cutting tool
which softens the material thereby reducing the cutting forces and
improving the machinability of the material. The extent of heating
depends strongly on the material properties, the cutting param-
eters (speed, feed rate) and the laser parameters (source, power,
tool-beam distance). Clearly, the data presented in Figs. 5–8 con-
ﬁrms that there exists a very complex relationship between the
cutting variables when machining the Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al /H9252-titanium
alloy.
A close examination of the cutting forces with respect to the
cutting speeds revealed a number of general trends. During con-
ventional machining, from the lowest speeds the forces tended
to increase and peak around the cutting speed of 50 m/min, after
which the forces tended to slightly decrease with further increases
to cutting speed. The reduction in cutting forces with increasing
speed is a well observed phenomenon and is believed to occur
because at high speeds more heat is generated during cutting and
this heat reduces the ﬂow stress of the material [11–13]. Indeed,
the temperature data presented in Fig. 7 shows a clear increase in
cutting temperature with speed for the conventionally machined
material. What this well known observation does not account for
is the initial increase in cutting forces with cutting speed (at low
speed), despite the fact that the temperature is also increasing from
about 400 ◦C to 800 ◦C over this range.
A possible explanation for the increase and subsequent decrease
in cutting forces with increasing speed may be related to the chip
formation mechanism. Fig. 11 shows a clear transition from con-
tinuous chips at low speed to segmented saw-tooth chips at high
speed. At cutting speeds greater than 48 m/min it appears that seg-
mented chips are formed whereas continuous chips form below
this speed. The transition from continuous to segmented chips with
increasing cutting speed is commonly understood to occur when
the rate of thermal softening exceeds the rate of strain hardening
during plastic deformation, resulting in localised deformation along
the adiabatic shear plane. Continuous chips, on the other hand, form
by constant deformation at the shear plane because the tempera-
ture increase is not sufﬁciently large to trigger a thermal instability
(decrease in ﬂow stress), so instead, the entire length of the chip is
deformed as material ﬂows through the shear plane.
Although the continuous to segmented chip transition during
conventional machining appears to occur at the same speed corre-
sponding to a peak in cutting force (about 50 m/min), it is unlikely
that the transformed chip morphology can account for a reduc-
tion in force since the same transition occurs in the LAM chips yet
there is no corresponding peak in cutting forces. Others have also
reported that the continuous-to-segmented chip transition occurs
with increasing cutting speed [14–17], however, the transition is
not reported to inﬂuence cutting force. Furthermore, Sun et al.
[16] demonstrated that there is no clear or abrupt transition from
continuous to segmented chip and that saw tooth segments are
often observed in “continuous chips”. Although not strictly clas-
siﬁed as saw-tooth segmented chips, close examination of some
lower speed chips (such as the 32 m/min chip in Fig. 11) display
what looks like localised adiabatic shear bands. This supports Sun
and co-workers observations and conﬁrms that the chip transition
is not abrupt in Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al alloy, but rather, it is a gradual
process. In any case, it is clear that the transition in chip morphol-
ogy cannot account for the increasing cutting force with speed up
to 50 m/min.
Fundamentally, the dominant mechanism by which plastic
strain occurs in b.c.c. crystals such as /H9252-titanium is dislocation
motion. In fact, the majority of plastic deformation at high strain

low temperatures and high strain rates a large stress is required to
initiate dislocation motion.
In effect the strain rate and temperature have competing inﬂu-
ences on the ﬂow stress during conventional machining because
both factors increase with cutting speed. Thus the overall ﬂow
stress for a given crystal is a function of two components, an ather-
mal component which is mostly dependant on strain rate and long
range dislocation barriers that cannot be overcome with thermal
energy (such as grain boundaries), and a thermally activated com-
ponent which is largely dependent on short range barriers that can
be overcome with thermal energy (Peierls barriers) [19].
In many materials (including CP-Ti [18] and Ti–6Al–4V [20]),
the ﬂow stress is experimentally observed to increase rapidly with
strain rates up to 106 s−1. Considering that the typical strain rates
during machining are between 103 s−1 and 106 s−1 at conven-
tional speeds and up to 107 s−1 at very high speed machining
[21,22], the increase in cutting forces observed at speeds between
5 m/min and 50 m/min is very likely a result of increased mate-
rial ﬂow stress associated with increased strain rate hardening.
This is quantiﬁed by performing a microhardness test on the
plastically deformed chips (Fig. 14). The fact that the hardness
of the deformed chips rapidly increases with strain rate (cutting
speed) lends support that strain rate hardening accounts for the
increased cutting forces at speeds up to 50 m/min. What this ﬁnding
also suggests is that the corresponding temperature increase over
this range (approximately 400–800◦C) is insufﬁcient to dominate
strain rate hardening. However, it is clear that thermal soften-
ing begins to dominate strain rate hardening at cutting speeds
above 50 m/min because both the hardness of the deformed mate-
rial and the cutting forces begin to decrease beyond this critical
speed. This indicates that the critical temperature for this transi-
tion must be approximately 800 ◦C for the Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al alloy.

to strain rates at even greater temperatures, approaching 1100 ◦C
(between 10−3 s−1 and 10 s−1) [22].
The cutting forces during laser assisted machining appear to
depend strongly on the cutting temperature. At low cutting speeds
and feeds the forces are low because the temperature is high,
and similarly, at high cutting speeds and feeds the forces are high
because the temperature is relatively low. The dependency of the
ﬂow stress on temperature for LAM tests is expected consider-
ing that all cutting temperatures are above the critical 800 ◦C, and
therefore, thermal softening is expected to dominate over strain
hardening at all speeds. The relationship between the cutting forces
and the cutting temperature for all machining trials is shown in
Fig. 15. The cutting forces are all inversely proportional to cutting
temperature for all machining trials (whether LAM or CM) where
the cutting temperature exceeds 800 ◦C. As mentioned earlier, at
temperatures below 800 ◦C the cutting forces are less dependent
on temperature and are more dependent on the strain rate.
The heat generated during a machining operation is a result of
both the plastic deformation due to chip formation at the cutting
tool tip and the friction between the cutting tool rake face and the
surface of the newly formed chip [24]. This heat generation is sig-
niﬁcant, and for the Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al alloy, temperatures exceeding
1000 ◦C are reached when cutting speeds approach 110 m/min with

Localised melting presents a number of practical concerns
when machining titanium. Firstly, titanium is extremely reactive to
the atmosphere and oxygen/nitrogen contamination is inevitable.
Severe oxygen and nitrogen contamination is expected to have
adverse affects on the cutting force as interstitial hardening and
strengthening of the material will occur. But of greater prac-
tical concern is that liquid titanium is likely to react strongly
with the tool which may lead to premature tool failure. More
research is needed in this area. In turning operations localised
melting is also likely to result in liquid metal being splashed
outwards under centrifugal forces which is a safety hazard and
could damage the equipment. Evidence of molten droplets of
titanium leaving the rotating workpiece was captured using the
IR camera and is shown in Fig. 18. For these reasons it is rea-
sonable to expect that localised melting is undesirable during
LAM.

5. Conclusions
For the ﬁrst time, the improvement offered by laser assisted
machining Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al
 metastable /H9252-alloy was investigated
over
 a wide range of feed rates and cutting speeds. The key ﬁndings
of the work are:
• Cutting Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al
 without laser assistance results in
increased cutting pressures as the cutting speed is increased
to 50 m/min. In this region, strain rate hardening predominates
thermal softening and the material’s hardness in the shear zone
is increased.
• Beyond speeds of 50 m/min, thermal softening predominates
over strain rate hardening and further increases in speed result in
further reductions in ﬂow stress and cutting force. This transition
generally corresponds with a change in chip formation from con-
tinuous unsegmented chips (whereby continuous strain occurs as
material ﬂows through the shear plane) to periodic segmented
chips where plastic deformation is localised to adiabatic shear
planes.
• The transition from strain rate hardening to thermal softening
appears to occur when the cutting temperature is approximately
800 ◦C for this particular /H9252-Ti alloy.
• At all tested speeds during laser assisted machining, thermal
softening dominates over strain hardening because the cutting
temperature was found to be always higher than 800 ◦C. How-
ever, the efﬁciency of the laser in heating the material decreased
with increasing cutting speed (due to less laser dwell time) which
caused the temperature to decrease and the cutting pressure to
increase as the cutting speed was increased. An inverse linear
type relationship is observed between cutting force and cutting
temperature during shearing of Ti–10Fe–2V–3Al if the tempera-
ture is >800 ◦C.
• The machinability during LAM of Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al /H9252-alloy was
compared to commercial purity titanium and Ti–6Al–4V alloys
using near-identical experimental parameters. It was found that
the machinability improvement of the /H9252-alloy was about 1/3
of that achievable in the conventional /H9251- and /H9251//H9252-alloys using
LAM. This suggests that high strength-high temperature titanium
alloys may be less responsive to laser assisted machining than
traditional titanium alloys.
• Greater
 machinability improvements for Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al
rivalling that encountered during LAM of /H9251 and /H9251//H9252 titanium
alloys is possible if the temperature of the heated material at the
laser spot is increased beyond 1000 ◦C. The cutting pressure of
Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al was reduced by almost 50% when the cutting
temperature was heated to near the melting temperature of the
alloy. However, localised melting is undesirable and should be
avoided
 as it poses safety concerns, alloy contamination and is
likely to damage equipment (including the tooling).
