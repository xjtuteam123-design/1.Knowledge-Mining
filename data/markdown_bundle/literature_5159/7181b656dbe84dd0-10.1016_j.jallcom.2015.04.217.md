abstract
The Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloys, which exhibit high strength and high elongation, have great potential for appli-
cations in many ﬁelds, such as automotive, aircraft, and aerospace industries. However, plastic deforma-
tion behavior under hot compression is still not fully understood. Hot compression tests of extruded Mg–
Zn–Y–Zr alloy were performed at temperatures from 250 /C176C to 450 /C176C and strain rates from 0.001 s
/C0 1 to
1s /C0 1. A constitutive equation and processing map were developed to describe the ﬂow characteristics. In
addition, isothermal forging tests were conducted to verify the optimal deformation parameters. The
results show that the strain rate and deformation temperature exert remarkable inﬂuences on the ﬂow

1. Introduction
Magnesium (Mg) alloys are widely used in the aircraft, automo-
tive, and aerospace industries due to their low density, high speci-
ﬁc strength, good casting ability and ﬁne welding ability [1–3].
However, due to their hexagonal close packed (HCP) structure
and low stacking fault energy, it is difﬁcult to deform magnesium
alloys into components at low temperatures. The workability of
magnesium alloys substantially increases due to additional slip
systems available at elevated temperatures [4,5]. The ZK60 magne-
sium alloy is an engineering material with widespread applica-
tions. To further improve the strength of this alloy, the structure
phases in the alloys have been optimized, especially by alloying.
The addition of Y to the ZK60 alloy further improves its strength
at ambient and elevated temperatures, which, in turn, led to the
extensive study of Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloys. The phase structure of
Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloys with different Y/Zn mole ratio has been deter-
mined in the previous study [6–8], and the mechanical properties
and microstructures of Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloys fabricated via different
processes, such as equal channel angular extrusion, were reported
[9,10].
The ﬁnal mechanical properties of the wrought magnesium
components processed by plastic deformation are strongly inﬂu-
enced by the initial state of the material [11]. To increase the
mechanical properties of the magnesium components, the
extruded rods usually use as perform billets to form the compo-
nents. However, most previous reports have addressed the issues
regarding the hot deformation behavior of the primarily cast or
homogenized Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloys [12,13], with few exceptions
relating to extruded Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloys, and thus their applica-
tions are limited. Therefore, the primary objectives of this study
were to obtain the optimal parameters for hot deformation and
to model the hot deformation ﬂow stress of extruded Mg–Zn–Y–
Zr alloys. A constitutive equation and processing map were
adopted to optimize the processing parameters and to control
the microstructure of the extruded Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloy component
under various processing conditions. In addition, isothermal forg-
ing tests were conducted to verify the optimal deformation
parameters.

Zn, 0.73 wt% Y and 0.36 wt% Zr. The billet with a diameter of 160 mm was prepared
using semi-continuous casting, and then the cast billet was extruded at 400 /C176C with
an extrusion ratio of 4.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure of the extruded Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloy
The initial microstructure of the extruded alloy is shown in
Fig. 2 . It can be seen that the grains are elongated along extrusion
direction. The dynamic recrystallization grain in the alloy cannot
be observed, and the second phase is also elongated parallel to
the extrusion direction. Fig. 3 shows the XRD pattern of the
extruded Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloy. The extruded Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloy con-
sists of a-Mg solid solution, Mg 3Zn3Y2 (W-phase) and MgZn 2.A s
reported in our previous work, the cast Mg–Zn–Y–Zr alloy consists
of a-Mg matrix and W-phase [13]. However, a new MgZn 2 phase is
observed in the alloy after extrusion, which always exists in Mg–Zn
alloys after aging treatment [14]. This phase generally precipitates
due to the low cooling rate after extrusion.
3.2. Flow stress behavior
The true stress–true strain curves for the experimental alloy
under various deformation conditions are presented in Fig. 4 . The
general characteristics of the true stress–true strain curves are
similar at all deformation conditions, i.e., at the initial stage of
deformation, the stress rises sharply as strain increase, afterwards,
increases slightly with further strain increase, and then the ﬂow
stress reaches a maximum value. Finally, the ﬂow stress decreases
with additional strain increase, and a steady state is observed.
Compared the true stress–strain curves at all deformation condi-
tions, it can be observed that the deformation temperature and
strain rate exert remarkable inﬂuences on the ﬂow behavior. The
variation in peak ﬂow stress with temperature under different
strain rates is shown in Fig. 5 . As shown, the ﬂow stress decreases
as strain rate decrease at a given deformation temperature,
whereas it increases as deformation temperature decrease at a
given strain rate. Compared to the ZK60 magnesium alloy reported
by Li et al. [15], the addition of the element Y to the ZK60 magne-
sium alloy increases the ﬂow stress, and therefore, increases the
deformation load under all deformation conditions. The shape of
the ﬂow curves contains some information related to the mecha-
nisms of hot deformation. At the initial stage of deformation, work
hardening caused by dislocation generation and multiplication
dominates the deformation and results in a rapid increase in ﬂow
stress. The peak stress is obtained with increasing strain due to
the dislocation density and the potential driving force of dynamic
recovery and dynamic recrystallization increases, and then the
