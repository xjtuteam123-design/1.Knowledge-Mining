ABSTRACT 
Several columbium base alloys in coated sheet form were 
evaluated for use as gas turbine engine vane materials. 
These alloys included FS-85, D-43, Cb-752, and Cb-132. 
This article describes the mechanical properties of these 
alloys and the various tests performed during the study. 
Based on strength, ductility, and voidability, D-43 was 
found to be satisfactory for use as a turbine vane material. 
Turbine vanes were subsequently fabricated from D-43, us­
ing a welded sheet metal airfoil joined to forged platforms. 
The product was finish machined to hardware within draw­
ing requirements, coated, and engine tested. 
COLUMBIUM ALLOYS have long been of interest to aircraft 
turbine engine designers as a possible means of allowing sig­
nificant increase in turbine inlet temperatures. The prom­
ise of columbium alloys for advanced turbine engine struc­
tures lies in outstanding strength characteristics at elevated 
temperature, good ductility, high melting point, low vapor 
pressure, attractive density, and adequate availability. De­
spite this, use of columbium alloys for turbine applications 
is restricted by the lack of adequate oxidation resistance. 
Two methods by which the oxidation resistance of co­
lumbium can be increased are alloying and coating. Al­
though alloying has resulted in some improvement in oxi­
dation resistance, the gains are far less than required, and 
it does not appear likely that a structural alloy having suf­
ficient oxidation resistance to be used without a coating will 
be developed. Considerable progress has been made in coat­
ing columbium and columbium alloys, but the lack of dur­
able and reliable coatings has to date prevented the use of 
columbium in long-life aircraft gas turbine engines. 
The purpose of this paper is to report the results of a test 
program designed to culminate in experimental engine test­
ing of coated columbium alloy hardware. In so doing, the 
problems inherent in the use of columbium alloy hardware 
for aircraft turbine applications are discussed. Results show 
promise that the eventual use of columbium alloys in long-
life aircraft turbine engines may become a reality. 
The use of columbium alloys for aircraft turbine appli­
cations has been actively pursued by Pratt & Whitney Air­
craft, since current superalloys are strength limited above 
1900 F without cooling. Columbium alloys have undergone 
progressive improvements, resulting in higher strength and 
more ductile alloys. Concurrently, coating development 
has resulted in protective coatings for columbium useful at 
temperatures in excess of 2400 F. Alloy coating composites 
have received considerable study in the past several years, 
with reliability as the main goal. 
The first aircraft engine component to see the effect of 
increased temperature is the turbine nozzle guide vane. 
The turbine vane, as a stationary part, sees a local tem­
perature that may be as much as ±150 F different from the 
average temperature at that stage. However, the load on 
the turbine vane is imposed primarily by the high velocity 
gas stream, which is relatively lightly stressed as contrasted 
to the high loads imposed on the rotating turbine blades. 
Hence turbine vanes may employ a lower creep strength ma­
terial than that for turbine blades. 
A hollow turbine vane was designed which required a 
0.050 in. refractory sheet metal airfoil attached to refrac­
tory metal platforms. This design imposes certain charac­
teristics on the alloy: 
1. The alloy must be fabricable into sheet material. 
2. The sheet material must be ductile, preferably at 
room temperature, to permit forming to an airfoil config­
uration. 
3. Weldability is desired because the airfoil trailing edge 
must be joined, and the airfoil must be attached by weld­
ing to the platforms. Fig. 1, a mock-up of the vane, shows 
several of the construction features. 
Engine operation imposes other requirements 
1. Since vanes are subject to foreign object damage, 
the alloy must possess impact ductility, and since operating

STRENGTH - Tensile Properties - Ambient and elevated 
temperature and smooth and notched tensile tests were con­
ducted on the four sheet materials. With the exception of 
Cb-132, all materials exhibited good tensile ductility in 
the recrystallized condition at room temperature. Cb-132 
showed the highest room temperature strength; D-43 showed 
the lowest. 
However, as temperature approached 2700 F, FS-85, 
Cb-752, and D-43 became nearly equivalent in strength 
while Cb-132 remained superior in ultimate and yield 
strengths. Tensile data are shown in Figs. 4-6. Notched 
tensile tests were conducted on the recrystallized sheet al­
loys at room temperature and 2400 F (Table 2), with a notch 
severity of Κ = 7.5. No notch sensitivity was observed.

It can be seen that a break in the stress rupture curve occurs

COATING 
A review of available information (4-6) led to the selec­
tion of coatings produced by two coating vendors.* Each 
coating incorporates one of the two primary constituents, 
silicon or aluminum, present in nearly all columbium alloy 
coatings. However, each system differs greatly in method 
of application as well as in room temperature ductility 
(Table 5). 
STATIC OXIDATION - Static oxidation test specimens 
were prepared from the sheet material of the four alloys. 
Samples were approximately 1 × 0.5 × 0.030 in., and edges 
and corners were prepared by abrasive belt grinding. Tabs 
were then sent to the vendors for application of their coat­
ings. 
Tests were conducted in an open end, resistance heated 
tube furnace at 1300 F, 2000 F, 2200 F, 2400 F, 2600 F, 
and 2700 F. At 25 hr intervals, specimens were visually 
examined and weighed at room temperature. Tests at each 
temperature were continued to 200 hr or until coating fail­
ure, as evidenced by the appearance of columbium oxide. 
Visual failure correlated well with weight change data in 
that a sudden and rapid change in the weight change per 
unit area versus time curve immediately preceded visual 
failure. 
Condition of the specimens after testing is shown in Figs. 
9-12. In each case, coating 3 performed significantly bet­
ter than coatings 1 and 2. No evidence of "pesting" was 
observed in either coating system. 
During static oxidation testing, D-43 specimens, which 
had received the same coating 3 treatment as the other al­
loy samples, failed considerably sooner than did the other 
samples. The vendor modified the coating treatment of new 
samples and a significant improvement in coating life was 
realized. These data, shown in Table 6, indicate that coat­
ings can be modified for each alloy to provide optimum oxi­
dation protection for that system.

THERMAL SHOCK - To simulate the rapid acceleration 
and deceleration encountered in an aircraft turbine engine, 
a thermal shock rig was employed in which a vane shape 
was subjected to 30 minutes steady state in the combustion 
products of JP-5 fuel and air. This treatment was followed 
by 100 cycles of thermal shock, each cycle consisting of 1 
minute hot and 30 sec cold. The test promoted thermal fa­
tigue cracking of base material and spalling of a coating. 
Two simulated sheet metal vanes were fabricated from 
each of the four alloys and coated with coating 3. One vane 
of each alloy was cooled to 1000 F (above the ductile-brittle 
transition temperature of the coating); the other, to 200 F. 
The results of these tests are shown in Table 6. 
The D-43 vane was cycled between 2200 F and 1000 F 
and is shown in Fig. 17, where progression of the coating 
failure is seen. Fig. 18 shows the severe cracking that oc­
curred when the D-43 vane was cycled between 2200 F and 
200 F. Examination revealed that "craze" cracking of the 
coating occurred in all vanes, resulting in spalling of the 
coating in small flakes. Oxidation progressed down these 
craze cracks and into the base metal; the embrittled base 
metal then cracked during thermal fatigue (Fig. 19). 
A review of these data with the vendor resulted in a sec­
ond modification of the coating to attain a balance of oxi­
dation protection and thermal fatigue resistance. Data on 
the results of this modification are not yet available. 
BALLISTIC IMPACT - To assess the ability of coated 
hardware to resist foreign object damage, coated sheet sam­
ples were impacted with a 1/16 in. diameter steel ball sol­
dered to a lead pellet (total weight 1.0 gr) fired from a Cross-
man air rifle. The specimens after test are shown in Fig. 
20. All material was in the recrystallized condition prior 
to coating. The extent of damage can be seen visually; only 
D-43 alloy did not exhibit cracking on impact. 
Subsequent static oxidation testing of the impacted D-43 
sample resulted in 18 hr of protection at 2200 F. Although 
the outer coating layer had spalled on impact, the inner lay­
ers provided limited protection. This information indicates 
that some useful life of engine hardware can be expected 
after foreign object damage.

JOINING 
It has been shown that tungsten inert gas (TIG) and elec­
tron beam welding of several columbium alloys is feasible 
(7). However, joint configurations studied by others were 
not representative of those required for turbine vane fabri­
cation. Since vane design required a hollow sheet metal 
airfoil joined to platforms, two types of joints were required: 
joining of the trailing edge of the airfoil, and attachment 
of airfoil to platforms. Each joint presented its own prob­
lems and will be discussed separately. 
AIRFOIL TRAILING EDGE - It was decided that forming

the airfoil and joining at the trailing edge would present 
fewer difficulties than other means of construction. Several 
types of joints were available, as shown in Fig. 21, and type 
"a" was chosen because it provided the least difficulty in 
fabrication. Clamping the airfoil prior to welding avoided 
problems due to springback of the formed airfoil. 
Although electron beam welding and resistance seam 
welding trials were conducted, normal TIG welding was se­
lected as the method for trailing edge joining, for the fol­
lowing reasons 
1. An internal radius is required at the joint to ensure 
complete coating coverage. TIG welding, with the result­
ant flow of molten metal, provided the necessary radius, 
whereas the radius was difficult to attain by EB welding and 
was impractical by resistance seam welding. 
2. Sufficient material must be present on the trailing 
edge to allow machining to drawing requirements. TIG 
welding with filler readily provided the needed material. 
AIRFOIL TO PLATFORM - TIG welding was again selected 
as the joining method because fixturing for electron beam 
welding was extremely difficult. The type of joint to be 
welded is shown in Fig. 22. It was necessary to ensure com­
plete fusion of the joint so as to eliminate crevices that the 
coating could not cover. TIG welding resulted in satisfac­
tory welds in all but Cb-132 alloy, which showed severe lon­
gitudinal weld shrinkage cracks that propagated well into 
the base metal (Fig. 23). Cracking was not overcome by 
back-stepping, preheating, or free state welding. 
Bend tests were conducted at room temperature on butt-
welded Cb-752, D-43, and FS-85, in the welded and welded 
plus heat treated conditions. The heat treatment consisted 
of a simulated vendor coating cycle of 1900 F (6 hr) plus 
2300 F (10 hr) plus 2100 F (8 hr), all in vacuum. The re­
sults are summarized in Table 7. It is noted that the heat 
treatment resulted in increased bend ducility in nearly every 
case.

a result of the brittleness problem; however, all four alloys 
were carried through the initial joining studies. The high

Consideration of the data obtained from coating studies 
and mechanical property evaluations resulted in the follow­
ing: 
1. The four materials are equally coatable for oxidation 
protection. 
2. The alloys possess good thermal fatigue properties 
when not embrittled by interstitials such as oxygen. 
3. Coated D-43 exhibits superior impact properties when 
compared to the other three materials. 
4. Coating 3 is superior to coatings 1 and 2 for the in­
tended use. 
5. All four alloys have adequate strength properties for 
use as a turbine vane material. 
6. Cb-132 is excessively brittle for turbine vane use. 
FABRICATION 
For airfoil fabrication, 0.050 in. thick D-43 alloy sheet 
was procured from E.I. du Pont de Nemours, Inc., in the 
form of 3-1/2 × 8 in. sheet blanks. The material was in 
the stress relieved condition. Preforming of the airfoil was 
accomplished in the dies shown in Fig. 24. Coining and 
springback forming were performed as shown in Fig. 25, 
where an undersized mandrel was utilized to attain the proper 
airfoil contour. The airfoil trailing edge was then trimmed 
flush. 
Trailing edge fusion was conducted in a dry box by man­
ual TIG welding, using D-43 filler wire. A helium atmos­
phere of 40 F dewpoint or better was required before weld­
ing could proceed. 
Vane platforms were precision forged from centerless 
ground D-43 bar stock as produced by du Pont. Forging se­
quences were developed to produce these components, util­
izing a three step forging operation at a working tempera­
ture range of 2000-2100 F. A 1 hr heat treatment at 2100 F 
was used to reduce and equalize the stresses induced by forg­
ing.

Dimensional inspection of the forged parts was conducted 
by the vendor to ensure adequate stock for machining to 
finished part configurations by Pratt & Whitney Aircraft. 
The forged platforms prior to machining are shown in Fig. 
26. 
The ends of the welded airfoils were machined square 
and the platforms were rough machined, with the area of 
the weld joint finish machined. Platforms and airfoil were 
then joined by manual TIG welding with D-43 filler in a 
chamber, at helium atmosphere and 40 F dewpoint. After 
welding, finish machining to final dimensions was conducted. 
The finished product is illustrated in Fig. 27. 
It should be noted that no stress relief heat treatments 
were given the material at any stage of final fabrication. 
Only the starting materials, that is forged platforms and 
sheet blanks, were in the stress relieved condition. No warp-
age or cracking of vanes was observed at any stage of the 
process, and stress relief heat treatment of trial hardware 
resulted in dimensions within drawing tolerances. Final ma­
chined vanes, therefore, were forwarded in the as-welded 
condition to the vendor for coating. It was felt that the 
coating heat cycles would adequately relieve residual form­
ing, welding, and machining stresses imparted to the ma­
terial. 
Finished D-43 turbine vanes coated with coating 3 were 
successfully mounted and tested in an experimental engine, 
confirming the results anticipated by this test program.
