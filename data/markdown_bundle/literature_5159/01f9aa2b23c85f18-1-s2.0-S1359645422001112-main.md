The interface control is critical in the development of high-performance molybdenum (Mo) alloys for 
high-temperature applications like space reactors. In this work, nanostructured Mo-ZrC alloy with excel- 
lent mechanical properties at both room temperature and high temperatures was fabricated by nanoscale 
ZrC dispersion and interface control. The average grain size of Mo-ZrC alloy is only 0.67 μm owing 
to the homogeneous dispersion of nanoscale ZrC particles. At room temperature, the ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS) and total elongation (TE) of the Mo-ZrC alloy are 928 MPa and 34.4%, respectively. At 
10 0 0 °C, the UTS is still as high as 562 MPa, which is signiﬁcantly higher than those reported in oxide 
dispersion-strengthened Mo alloys, while the TE remains a high value of 23.5%. Additionally, the recrys- 
tallization start temperature of Mo-ZrC alloy is about 1400 °C, indicating remarkable thermal stability. 
First-principles calculations revealed that the interstitial oxygen reduces grain boundary cohesion, while 
C and ZrC could increase the fracture strength of the boundaries owing to the strong Mo-C bonds. The ex- 
cellent mechanical properties and thermal stability of the Mo-ZrC alloy can be attributed to a synergistic 
effect of nanoscale ZrC particles dispersion strengthening, ﬁne-grain strengthening, and grain boundary 
puriﬁcation. The strategy could be applied to design other refractory alloys with both high strength and 
ductility for high-temperature applications.

1. Introduction 
Molybdenum (Mo) and its alloys have been widely used 
in machinery, military and aerospace industries because of the 
high melting temperature, high thermal conductivity, low ther- 
mal expansion and excellent compatibility with liquid alkali metal 
coolants [1–3] . For instance, as fuel cladding materials in space ﬁs- 
sion reactors where a high thermal conversion eﬃciency and high 
speciﬁc energies are required, Mo alloys should remain stable for 
an operating life of 15 to 20 years in the extremely harsh environ- 
ments such as high temperatures ( > 10 0 0 K), neutron irradiation 
and liquid metal corrosion [ 4 , 5 ]. In such an application the pesting 
phenomenon, which is one of the most detrimental disadvantages 
of Mo alloys in other common applications [6] , can be neglected 
owing to the oxygen-free environment. However, the disadvantages

The interfaces including grain boundaries (GBs) and phase 
boundaries (PBs) play a crucial role in the mechanical properties of 
polycrystalline materials [ 9 , 10 ]. The segregation of some interstitial 
impurities such as oxygen (O) and nitrogen (N) at GBs is believed 
to be a predominant reason for GB embrittlement and low ductil- 
ity of Mo alloys [11–15] . In this sense, any methods to reduce the 
content of such impurities at GBs would be effective in improving 
the strength and ductility. For example, Miller et al. [ 16 , 17 ] found 
that after the addition of Zr, C and B to inhibit the O segregation 
at GBs, the tensile elongation of Mo weldments increases from 3 to 
20%. In addition, the average concentration of O at GBs can be de- 
creased by reﬁning the grains owing to the increased GB areas, as 
in the case of ultraﬁne- or nano- grained materials [18] . Besides, 
GBs could act as sinks for irradiation defects, and thus the large

Compared with GBs, the PBs between the second-phase par- 
ticles and metal matrix generally have higher thermal stability 
[25] . So, one of the recent effort s to improve the properties 
of Mo alloys especially at high temperatures is the dispersion 
strengthening with nanoscale oxides or carbides [26–35] . For ex- 
ample, the ﬁne-grained Mo-0.6wt% La 2 O 3 (NS-Mo) alloy strength- 
ened with nanoscale La 2 O 3 particles exhibit high yield strength 
(YS ∼813 MPa) and excellent total elongation (TE ∼37.5%) at RT 
and increased recrystallization temperature [29] . Nevertheless, the 
NS-Mo alloy is not strong enough at high temperatures close to 
the typical operating temperature of space reactor [5] . The ul- 
timate tensile strength (UTS) at 10 0 0 °C of the NS-Mo alloy is 
only 368 MPa, which is much lower than that of TZM alloys 
(524 MPa), despite the TE of 23.7% is signiﬁcantly higher than that 
of TZM ( ∼3.6%) [36] . This strength-ductility trade-off dilemma at 
high temperatures for Mo alloys limits their application in space 
reactors. 
The low strength of NS-Mo at high temperatures may originate 
from the low melting temperature of La 2 O 3 (2217 °C) and the large 
lattice mismatch between La 2 O 3 and Mo matrix. A low melting 
point of the second-phase may lead to microstructural instability 
of the particles against coarsening at high temperatures. Further- 
more, La 2 O 3 phase has a hexagonal crystal structure with inter- 
planar spacings of 0.308 nm (101), 0.351 nm (100) and 0.321 nm 
(002) [28] , while Mo matrix is body-centered cubic (bcc) with in- 
terplanar spacings of 0.222 nm (110), 0.157 nm (200) and 0.128 nm 
(211). Therefore, the La 2 O 3 /Mo interface should be incoherent ow- 
ing to the large lattice mismatch, leading to a weak interface bond- 
ing. Because the coherent interface exhibits signiﬁcant strengthen- 
ing effects without sacriﬁcing ductility [ 37 , 38 ], it would be an eﬃ- 
cient way to ﬁnd a suitable kind of particles with a very low lattice 
mismatch with the matrix to strengthen Mo alloys at high temper- 
atures. 
Compared with oxides, carbides such as TiC, ZrC, HfC have 
been more and more exploited to strengthen metals especially 
for high-temperature applications due to their high melting point, 
high-temperature stability and high hardness [ 27 , 39 , 40 ]. It was re- 
ported that the carbides tend to form coherent or semi-coherent 
interfaces with Mo and W [ 28 , 41 ]. Especially, nanoscale ZrC par- 
ticles, with high melting temperature ( ∼3540 °C) and low neu- 
tron absorption cross-section, exhibit three advantages in acting as 
a strengthening phase. Firstly, nanoscale ZrC particles can reﬁne 
grain size and improve the strength and stability by pinning dislo- 
cations and GBs. Secondly, ZrC can form a strong coherent or semi- 
coherent interface with Mo/W matrix to improve the strength. And 
ﬁnally, ZrC as an O getter, can react with O at high tempera- 
tures and thus alleviate GB embrittlement. A successful example is 
the ZrC dispersion strengthened W alloys with enhanced strength, 
thermal stability and irradiation resistance [ 42 , 43 ], which is one of 
the most promising candidate materials for divertors in fusion re- 
actors. 
In this work, we proposed a strategy to synergistically improve 
the mechanical properties of Mo alloy at both RT and high tem- 
peratures through nanoscale ZrC dispersion and interface control. 
To further reduce the content of O in Mo alloy, ﬂowing dry hydro- 
gen atmosphere was adopted during the sintering process. Mean- 
while, hot plastic deformation was used to further reﬁne grains 
and enhance the cohesive strength of GBs. Ultimately, nanoscale 
ZrC dispersion-strengthened Mo alloy with excellent mechanical 
properties at both RT and high temperatures was fabricated, and 
the interface strengthening mechanisms were investigated by ﬁrst- 
principles calculations.

2. Methods 
2.1. Starting materials and fabrication 
Pure Mo powders (purity ≥ 99.95% trace metals basis, average 
particle size ∼2.0 μm, oxygen content ∼ 0.09%) and nanoscale ZrC 
powders (purity ≥ 99%, average particle size ∼50 nm) were used as 
starting materials. Rods of the ZrC dispersion-strengthened Mo al- 
loy were prepared successively through powders ball milling, cold 
isostatic pressing, high-temperature sintering, rotary swaging and 
annealing processes. The mixed Mo and ZrC powders in a weight 
ratio of 99.4/0.6 were ball-milled in a horizontal planetary ball mill 
for 4 h under an argon atmosphere using WC balls of 5 mm and 
8 mm in diameters and WC pots of 500 ml in volume. The ro- 
tating speed is 240 rpm and the ball to powder weight ratio is 
8:1. The ball-milled powders were subsequently compacted into a 
cylindrical rod with a diameter of 24.4 mm by cold isostatic press- 
ing at a pressure of 180 MPa using a rubber mold and then sin- 
tered at 1900 °C for 4 h in ﬂowing dry hydrogen. The as-sintered 
Mo-0.6wt%ZrC rod of about 21 mm in diameter was heated in a 
hydrogen atmosphere and subjected to multiple passes of rotary 
swaging to obtain thinner rods with a diameter of 8.8 mm. The ro- 
tary swaging temperature is from 1200 to 700 °C, and the area re- 
duction per pass is 20–25%. The as-swaged Mo-ZrC samples were 
annealed at different temperatures from 10 0 0 to 180 0 °C in vac- 
uum for 1 h. Among them, the sample annealed at 1200 °C shows 
both high strength and good ductility. So in this work, if not ex- 
plicitly mentioned, the results of Mo-ZrC alloy were obtained from 
the samples annealed at 1200 °C. For comparison, pure Mo rods 
were also prepared following the same process. The oxygen con- 
tent in the Mo samples measured by the nitrogen and oxygen 
analyzer (LECO, TC600C) was 18 ppm. The consolidation and hot 
rotary swaging were carried out by collaborating with ATTL Ad- 
vanced Materials Co., Ltd. The density of the pure Mo and Mo-ZrC 
specimens was measured by the Archimedes method, and the rela- 
tive density was calculated based on the theoretical density of Mo 
(10.28 g/cm 3 ) and ZrC (6.73 g/cm 3 ). As listed in Table 1 , the rela- 
tive densities of pure Mo and Mo-ZrC alloy are 99.88% and 99.48%, 
respectively. 
2.2. Mechanical property tests 
The Mo-ZrC rods were cut by electric discharge machining 
(EDM) and then mechanically polished for Vickers micro-hardness 
tests and tensile tests. The hardness measurements were carried 
out at RT using a Vickers micro-hardness tester with a load of 
500 g and a dwell time of 15 s. For RT and low-temperature ten- 
sile tests, dog-bone-shaped samples were prepared with a cross- 
section of 1.5 × 0.75 mm 2 and a gauge length of 5 mm along the 
longitudinal direction [44] . To ensure the accuracy of data, at least 
three samples were tested at each temperature using an Instron- 
5967 machine with a constant strain rate of 2 × 10 −4 s −1 . In the 
low-temperature tensile tests, the temperature was controlled by 
using an environmental chamber cooled with liquid nitrogen. The 
high-temperature tensile tests were carried out on a Gleeble-3500 
thermo-mechanical simulator at 10 0 0 °C, 1100 °C and 1200 °C at a

2.3. Microstructure characterization 
The fracture surfaces of tensile-tested samples were charac- 
terized using a ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscope (SEM

G2 F20) was employed to analyze the size and distribution of

mental analysis. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy 
(HRTEM) was employed to characterize the interfaces between 
second-phase particles and Mo matrix. Electron backscatter diffrac- 
tion (EBSD) with an acceleration voltage of 15 kV was used to char-

ZrC samples, using a CRYSTAL detector (Oxford Instruments, Ox-

2.4. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations 
Theoretical calculations involved in this work were performed 
using Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with the pro- 
jected augmented wave method [45–47] . Generalized gradient ap- 
proximation in a form proposed by Perdew et al. was used to de- 
scribe the exchange and correlation effect [48] . The plane-wave 
cutoff energy was 500 eV for all the calculations. All the super- 
cells were optimized by structural relaxations, where the energy 
and force convergences were 10 −6 eV and 10 −2 eV/ ˚A, respectively. 
3. Results 
3.1. Mechanical properties 
Fig. 1 shows the tensile engineering stress-strain curves and the 
corresponding true stress-strain curves of pure Mo and Mo-ZrC al- 
loy at RT. The Mo-ZrC alloy exhibits a high yield strength (YS) of 
920 MPa and a TE of 34.4%, while the pure Mo has a similar TE 
of 33.8%, but a YS of only 424 MPa. The UTS of Mo-ZrC and pure 
Mo is 928 MPa and 493 MPa, respectively. It is noteworthy that 
both the UTS and YS of Mo-ZrC alloy are almost twice those of 
pure Mo. These results indicate that the addition of a small amount 
of ZrC nanoparticles can signiﬁcantly improve the strength of Mo 
alloy while keeping good ductility, evading the strength-ductility 
trade-off dilemma. The true stress-strain curve in Fig. 1 b was con- 
verted from the engineering stress-strain curve through standard 
equations. It can be seen that the true UTS of Mo-ZrC and pure 
Mo is 1125 MPa and 609 MPa, respectively. For both samples, the 
true stress continues to increase even at a high strain of 20%. As 
indicated in the insets in Fig. 1 a, the fracture micrograph of pure 
Mo mainly consists of transgranular fracture, showing a cleavage 
morphology, while the fracture micrograph of Mo-ZrC reveals a lot 
of tiny dimples that are characteristics of ductile fracture. 
Fig. 2 a shows the YS and TE of the present Mo-ZrC alloy and 
pure Mo at RT, in comparison with the results of Mo materials re- 
ported in literature, including LCAC-Mo, CP-Mo, ODS-Mo, Mo- Re , 
ODS-Mo- Re , Mo-La 2 O 3 -ZrC, TZM, La-TZM, La 2 O 3 -TZM and NS-Mo 
[ 29 , 33 , 36 , 49–51 ]. These data were also listed explicitly in Table 2 . 
It can be seen that the YS of Mo-ZrC at RT (920 MPa) is about 
26% higher than that of TZM alloy (730 MPa), while the elonga- 
tion of Mo-ZrC (34.4%) is more than twice that of TZM alloy [36] . 
The rolled La-TZM alloy and La 2 O 3 -TZM sheets with a thickness of 
0.5 mm exhibit high tensile strength of 1405 MPa and 1263 MPa, 
respectively, but their TE is lower than 8% [51] . Similarly, the Mo- 
La 2 O 3 -ZrC alloy exhibited a high UTS of 988 MPa, while the TE is 
only 10% [50] . As noted in Fig. 2 a, most bulk Mo alloys have either 
limited strength or low ductility, as marked in the green shaded 
region, and only the Mo-ZrC and NS-Mo alloys exhibit both high 
strength and extraordinary ductility at RT.

3.2. Microstructure of Mo-ZrC alloy 
Ultraﬁne grains are one of the most important microstructural 
features contributing to the high performance of materials. The av- 
erage grain size of the Mo-ZrC alloy is about 0.67 μm in width 
and 6.21 μm in the longitudinal direction, corresponding to an as- 
pect ratio of about 9:1, as shown in Table 1 . In contrast, the av- 
erage grain size of pure Mo is 23.5 μm in width and 78.2 μm 
in the longitudinal direction. This indicates that the dispersion 
of nanoscale ZrC particles in Mo alloy can effectively inhibit the 
grain growth during high-temperature sintering process. The av- 
erage grain size of the present Mo-ZrC alloy is similar to that of 
NS-Mo alloy (0.5 ± 0.3 μm) prepared through liquid-liquid dop- 
ing and swaging [29] , but is much smaller than those of the most 
ODS-Mo alloys, such as Mo-ZrO 2 alloys prepared by hydrothermal 
synthesis (10–60 μm) and Mo-La 2 O 3 /Y 2 O 3 alloys prepared by sol- 
gel method (3–5 μm) [ 30 , 31 , 35 ]. 
Fig. 3 shows the TEM images and the size distribution of intra- 
granular and intergranular particles in the 1200 °C annealed and 
as-swaged ( Fig. 3 d) Mo-ZrC alloy. It can be seen that most of the 
particles are homogeneously distributed in the Mo grains, and the 
particle size is in the range of 20–150 nm with an average size 
of 54 nm, as indicated by solid arrows in Fig. 3 a,b and d. A small 
fraction of particles ranging from tens to hundreds of nanometers 
are located at GBs, with an average particle size of 169 nm, as in- 
dicated by the hollow arrows in Fig. 3 a,b. Fig. 3 d shows the inter- 
action between intragranular particles and dislocations in the as- 
swaged samples. These ﬁne particles can act as barriers for dislo- 
cations, and thereby improve the strength of the material.

3.3. Thermal stability 
To evaluate the thermal stability of Mo-ZrC alloy, the as-swaged 
samples were annealed at different temperatures from 10 0 0 to 
1800 °C in vacuum for 1 h. As shown in Fig. 5 , the hardness of 
the as-swaged pure Mo and Mo-ZrC alloy is 221 HV and 315 HV, 
respectively. When the annealing temperature (T a ) increases, the 
hardness of both pure Mo and Mo-ZrC alloy shows a decreas- 
ing trend. As the T a increases to 1200 °C, as listed in Table 1 , 
the hardness of pure Mo decreases to a stable low value of 187 
HV, while the Mo-ZrC still has a high hardness of about 303 HV, 
which is just 5% lower than that of the as-swaged one. After an- 
nealing at 1600 °C, the hardness of Mo-ZrC decreases to 213 HV 
and tends to be stable at higher annealing temperatures. It is 
well known that the hardness is closely related to the microstruc- 
ture and can be used to estimate the recrystallization temperature 
based on its evolution with annealing temperature. Therefore, the 
recrystallization start temperature is about 10 0 0 °C for the swaged 
pure Mo, while it is about 1400 °C for the Mo-ZrC alloy. Gener- 
ally, recrystallization start temperature of commercially pure met-

4. Discussion 
4.1. Mechanisms for high strength 
It is well known that solid-solution strengthening, ﬁne-grain 
strengthening, dislocation strengthening, and second-phase dis- 
persion strengthening are the main strengthening mechanisms in 
polycrystalline materials [55–57] . From the microstructure of pure 
Mo and Mo-ZrC samples shown in the insets in Fig. 1 b, the in- 
crease in yield strength and UTS of Mo-ZrC could be mainly at- 
tributed to two kinds of mechanisms, i.e. ﬁne-grain strengthening 
and second-phase dispersion strengthening. However, the strength- 
ening effect by the residual dislocations is not considered in the 
Mo-ZrC alloy owing to the relatively low dislocation density (see 
Fig. 3 a,b). In principle the fundamental origin of strength enhance- 
ment is the addition of nanoscale ZrC particles. On one hand, the 
uniform dispersion of ZrC particles in Mo alloy impedes the move- 
ment of GBs during the high-temperature sintering and annealing 
processes and reﬁnes the grains. On the other hand, the nanoscale 
ZrC particles within the grains inhibit the movement of disloca- 
tions.

cate that the presence of O reduces the interface cohesion strength, 
whether it exists alone or co-segregate with other elements. In this 
work, sintering in ﬂowing dry hydrogen greatly reduces the oxy- 
gen content in the matrix, and the O content in pure Mo samples 
was reduced to a level of 18 ppm, much lower than the value of 
34 ppm in previous NS-Mo materials [29] . This will avoid the seg- 
regation of lots of O at GBs. In addition, the particle ZrC as an oxy- 
gen getter could react with oxygen at high temperature and form 
zirconium dioxides (ZrO 2 ) or Zr-C-O particles to further reduce the 
oxygen content at the GBs, and thus increase the GB cohesion. It 
is known that the fracture toughness along the GB is proportional 
to the square root of separation work [62] . Therefore, the above 
analysis clearly interpreted why the present pure Mo has a better 
ductility ( ∼34%) at RT compared to previous results ( ∼13%) [36] .

4.3. High thermal stability 
The high operation temperature during service can alter the mi- 
crostructure of Mo alloys and lead to recrystallization and grain 
growth, resulting in decreased mechanical properties. So Mo alloys 
should be operated below their recrystallization temperature as 
structural materials in space reactor [5] . According to the changes 
in microstructure and hardness after annealing at different tem- 
peratures, the recrystallization start temperature of Mo-ZrC alloy is 
about 1400 °C, which is about 400 °C higher than that of pure Mo 
[ 29 , 36 ]. The main reason of stability improvement of Mo-ZrC alloy 
is that the dispersed nanoscale ZrC particles could hinder the GB 
migration, and thus inhibit the grain coarsening. The highly stable 
microstructure of Mo-ZrC alloy helps to maintain good mechanical 
properties at high temperatures. For instance, even after annealing 
at 1500 °C, the Mo-ZrC alloy still has a UTS of 723 MPa and a TE of 
about 40%. The excellent high temperature mechanical properties 
and stability of Mo-ZrC alloy make it a very promising candidate 
for structural material in space reactors. 
5. Conclusions 
In the present study, nanoscale ZrC dispersion strengthened Mo 
alloy rods with enhanced strength and ductility were fabricated 
through powder metallurgy and rotary swaging. The main results 
can be summarized as follows: 
(1) The Mo-ZrC alloy exhibits high strength and excellent ductility 
at both RT and high temperatures. The ductile-to-brittle tran- 
sition temperature is below -80 °C. The yield strength (YS) of 
the Mo-ZrC alloy is 920 MPa and the total elongation (TE) is 
34.4% at RT. At 10 0 0 °C, the UTS and TE are 562 MPa and 23.5%, 
respectively. The UTS of Mo-ZrC alloy is obviously higher than 
that of previously reported ODS-Mo, La-TZM, La 2 O 3 -TZM, TZM, 
and NS-Mo at 10 0 0 °C. At a higher temperature of 1200 °C, the 
UTS of Mo-ZrC alloy remains as high as 483 MPa and the TE is 
20.9%. 
(2) A remarkable thermal stability of the Mo-ZrC alloy is observed. 
The recrystallization start temperature of the Mo-ZrC alloy is 
about 1400 °C, which is about 400 °C higher than that of pure 
Mo. 
(3) The microstructure analyses show that most of the particles are 
homogeneously distributed in the ultraﬁne Mo grains interior, 
with an average particle size of 54 nm. Only a small fraction of 
the particles are located at GBs and the average size of these 
intergranular particles is 169 nm. Nanoscale ZrC particles can 
form a coherent interface structure with Mo matrix. 
(4) The excellent mechanical properties and thermal stability can 
be mainly attributed to the nanoscale ZrC particles dispersion, 
ultra-ﬁne grain structure and GB puriﬁcation. First-principles 
calculations reveal that the interstitial oxygen segregated at in- 
terfaces has a negative effect on strength and ductility of Mo 
materials, while C atom and ZrC particles could increase the 
fracture strength of the interface. The detailed electronic struc- 
ture analyses show that the stronger Mo-C bonds and weaker 
Mo-O bonds are responsible for the interface strengthening and 
embrittlement. 
(5) The present study demonstrates the possibility of a design to- 
wards Mo alloys with a combination of high strength, good 
ductility and thermal stability, through the nanoscale ZrC dis- 
persion and interface control. The prepared Mo-ZrC alloy has 
good application prospects in space reactor and high tempera- 
ture industries.
