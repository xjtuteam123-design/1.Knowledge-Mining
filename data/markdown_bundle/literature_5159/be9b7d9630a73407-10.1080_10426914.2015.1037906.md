Ni-based superalloys with different contents of Nb addition were fabricated by vacuum melting and casting. High temperature oxidation
behaviors of the designed alloys were investigated by thermogravimetry. Microstructure and phase constituent of the oxidation scales were
studied using scanning electron microscope and X-ray diffraction (XRD), respectively. Results showed that irrespective of the composition,
all specimens obeyed the parabolic law during different stages. The scales on specimens B and C with 2.0 wt. % and 2.5 wt.% Nb addition,
respectively, were mainly comprised of Cr 2O3 and NiCr2O4. However, specimen A, with no Nb addition showed only NiO in the outer scale.
The oxidation scales all exhibited a layered structure. With 2.0 wt. %Nb addition, the superalloy’s high temperature oxidation resistance was
enhanced by about 25 %.
Keywords Kinetics; Niobium; Oxidation; Superalloy.
INTRODUCTION
Ni-based superalloys have been widely used in aero-
space, energy, metallurgy, and chemical industries owing
to their superior high temperature properties, the current
operating temperature of which is about 650–1000
/C14 C
[1–3]. Different elements have been introduced into
Ni-based superalloys to obtain better high temperature
strength and oxidation resistance. For Example, Cr, Mo,
and W can dissolve in Ni, forming c-Ni solid solution
and causing solution strengthening. The added elements
would also affect the diffusion process in Ni-based super-
alloys, which is essential for the processes of solidiﬁcation,
homogenization, c
0 precipitation, and so on [ 4]. With the
addition of appropriate amount of Cr, a protective
Cr
2O3 layer can be obtained on the surface of Ni-based
superalloys, impeding further oxidation [ 5]. In addition,
third-element effect (TEE) will occur by introducing Cr
and Al simultaneously. In this case, the internal oxidation
of Al will be prevented but the external Al
2O3 will be
generated [ 6]. The Ni-based superalloys containing Cr,
W, and Mo have also been studied and the effect of the
elements has been extensively discussed [ 7].
According to relevant studies,c0-Ni3(Al, Ti), g-Ni3Ti, d-
Ni3Nb, g-Ni6AlNb, and similar intermetallic strengthening
phases can form in Ni-based superalloys with the addition
o fA l ,T i ,a n dN b[8]. These intermetallic phases tend to
precipitate along the grain boundaries in Ni-based superal-
loys, which is favorable to their mechanical properties [9].
With Al addition, a compact Al
2O3 layer will form on the
surface of metals or alloys and protect the materials from
oxygen or other aggressive media, restraining further
oxidation or corrosion [10].
As previously mentioned, high temperature oxidation
resistance is an important property for Ni-based super-
alloys and numerous studies have been carried out to
investigate this subject. Apart from Cr and Al, the
impacts of other additives such as C [ 11], Si [ 12], Co
[13], Cb [ 14], La [ 15], Y [ 16], Zr [ 17], and Y
2O3[18]o n
the high temperature oxidation behaviors of Ni-based
superalloys have also been investigated.
In addition, Nb was also introduced in Ni-based alloys or
other alloys to improve the high temperature oxidation
resistance. For example, the high temperature oxidation
resistance of Ti–Ni–Al–Nb alloys was improved notably
due to the effect of Nb. Nb can decrease the oxygen vacancy
in TiO
2 and suppress the diffusion of oxygen inward [19].
In this study, a new series of Ni-based superalloys was
manufactured with the addition of certain elements, such
as Cr, W, Mo, Ti, Al, and so on. High temperature oxi-
dation properties of the designed alloys were studied and
the action mechanism of Nb was investigated. This study

MATERIALS AND METHODS
The Ni-based superalloys were manufactured by vac-
uum melting. A vacuum arc melting furnace equipped with
an induction stir was used in this study. The nominal com-
position of the Ni-based superalloys is listed in Table 1.
After normalizing (1200
/C14 C, 4 h) and aging (800 /C14 C, 10 h)
in pure argon, specimens (10 mm /C2 10 mm/C2 3m m ) w e r e
cut from the ingots. All the specimens were mechanically
abraded to remove the oxides on their surface. In order
to guarantee the surface uniformity of all the specimens,
the same grade abrasive paper (800 #) was used. Subse-
quently each specimen was cleaned with alcohol and acet-
one in sequence and dried before oxidation testing.
The high temperature oxidation test was carried out
according to the Chinese National Aviation Standard
(HB 5258-2000, test method for determination of
oxidation resistance of steel and superalloy). Thermogra-
vimetry was used to study the oxidation kinetics. A
traditional chamber electric furnace was employed to
carry out the oxidation test at 800
/C14 C for 100 h. An air
hole with a diameter of 15 mm was set on the furnace
door to guarantee the oxidizing atmosphere in the fur-
nace. An electronic balance with an accuracy of 0.1 mg
was used to record the mass changes. The mass gain rate
was calculated to evaluate the oxidation rate. X-ray Dif-
fractionmeter (XRD, D =max-cB, Rigaku Corporation,
Tokyo, Japan) was used to analyze the phase constituent.
Scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-6380LA,
JEOL Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) was employed to examine
the microstructure of the oxide scales. The linear elemen-
tal distribution was analyzed with the attached energy
dispersive spectrometer (EDS).

Figure 1 shows the high temperature oxidation kinetics
curves of the three specimens, as normal plots (Fig. 1(a))
and parabolic plots (Fig. 1(b)). The oxidation kinetics
curves of the three specimens are divided into three
stages. It is worthy to note that a high oxidation rate
appears in the ﬁrst stage lasting for about 10 h. Then oxi-
dation process undergoes a transitional stage (10–20 h).
The oxidation process tends to stabilize after 20 h.
During the ﬁrst stage, the specimens were exposed to
air and oxidation proceeded quickly. Along with the
oxidation process, the oxides grew on the surfaces of
the specimens. The early formed oxides acted as a shield-
ing layer from outer oxygen. After 20 h of oxidation, all
surfaces of the specimens were covered with an oxide
scale and further oxidation was restrained, corresponding
to the ﬁnal stage. The oxidation rates for different stages
were calculated, as shown in Table 2.

different oxidation stages follow parabolic rate law

Figure 2 is the XRD results of the specimens after
oxidation test. The oxide scales on the three specimens
consist of a mixture of oxides such as Cr 2O3, TiO 2,
Al2O3, and a composite oxide (NiCr 2O4). The formed
oxide scales are so thin (less than 15 mm) that the c-Ni
diffraction peak appears in the spectrums. However,
NiO is just picked out in specimen A.
The surface morphologies of the oxide scales on three
kinds of alloys after oxidization for 100 h at 800
/C14 C are
shown in Figs. 3 and 4. There are lots of assembled white
particle-like phases on specimen A (Fig. 3(a)), which may
be TiO2. High magniﬁcation image (Figs. 3(d) and 4(a))
shows rutile TiO 2 dispersed in irregular shapes.
Apart from the white particles, the oxide scale is mainly
comprised of Cr 2O3 and NiCr 2O4, which are seen as
dense gray phases. The oxide scales on Ni-based superal-
loys with 2.0 wt. % and 2.5 wt. % Nb show similar surface
morphology as specimen A, as Fig. 3(b) and (c) show. No
cracks or obvious agminated particles are detected on the
surface at low magniﬁcation. Layered scales can be dis-
tinguished in high magniﬁcation, as shown in Fig. 3(e)
and (f). The layered scales will be further discussed by
referring to the following cross-section analysis.
As Fig. 4(b) and (c) show, the oxides on specimen B
grow densely in cone-shape. The dense oxides will pre-
vent the oxygen from diffusing inward. However, no
similar morphology is observed on specimen C. The oxi-
des on specimen C distribute in chain-like aggregates, as
Fig. 3(f) and Fig. 4(d) show. The chain-like aggregates
destroy the integrity of the dense Cr
2O3, resulting in a
relatively poor high temperature oxidation resistance,
compared to specimen B.
The cross-section microstructure and corresponding
linear element distribution of the oxide scales are shown
in Fig. 5. The oxide scale thickness is about 13.3 mmo n
specimen A (Ni–Cr–M alloy). The scale can be divided
into three layers, i.e., a loose outer layer (TiO
2, NiO,
and NiCr 2O4), a middle layer with dense Cr 2O3, and an
internal layer (TiO 2 and Al 2O3), as Fig. 5(a) shows.
Appropriate internal oxidation has been proved favor-
able for the adhesion between the oxide scales and the
substrate [ 20]. Nevertheless, the internal layer was too
thin to be detected in the cross-sectional image. The
linear element distribution corresponds with the above
analysis. However, the concentration of Ti and Al does
not change obviously except a slight rise near the substra-
te=scale interface, which implies a low content of TiO
2
and Al 2O3 in the whole scale. Moreover, a Ni depleted
zone appears in Cr 2O3, due to its outward diffusion
forming NiO and NiCr 2O4.
The oxide scale formed on specimen B (Ni–Cr–
2.0%Nb–M alloy) is about 7.7 mm and is dense and uni-
form in the cross section as observed in Fig. 5(b). The
white bright band between the substrate and the oxide
scales is rich in Mo, W, and Nb. It is noteworthy that
the white bright band is beneﬁcial to the oxidation resist-
ance of the superalloys, since it can impede the diffusion
of oxygen inward. Similar to that of specimen A, the lin-
ear elemental distribution of Ti and Al does not change
notably throughout the whole scale. The concentrated
O and Cr throughout the whole scale implies the exist-
ence of Cr
2O3, corresponding to the XRD analysis.
Besides, a Cr depleted zone emerges near the surface of
the specimen B, which is probably attributed to the out-
ward diffusion of Cr. A low Ni content is spotted both in
specimens B and C. It is deduced that the added Nb pre-
vents Ni diffusing outward to form NiO, corresponding
to the absence of the diffraction peak of NiO in the
XRD results of specimens B and C (Fig. 2).

The high temperature oxidation resistance is better
with proper amount of Nb (i.e., 2.0 wt.%) addition.
One reasonable explanation is that the dense Cr 2O3 acts
as a shielding layer preventing the Ni 2þ diffusing
outward and thus preventing the formation of NiO and
NiCr2O4. Moreover, the layer rich in Nb, Mo, and W
would suppress the diffusion process of Ni 2þ signiﬁ-

in the oxidation zone as the liner element distribution

In addition, the Ni-based superalloy with 2.5 wt.%Nb
addition exhibits more oxidation mass gain compared
with that containing 2.0 wt.%. Though Nb is propitious
to enhance the high temperature resistance of Ni-based
superalloys, the amount should be appropriate. Too
much Nb (i.e., 2.5 wt.%) leads to a discontinuous inner
layer, losing its protective function and accelerating
the oxidation process.

ONCLUSIONS
Ni-based superalloys with different contents of Nb
addition were fabricated by vacuum melting and casting
in the current work. The high temperature resistance of
the newly designed alloys was studied and the action
mechanism of Nb was discussed. Conclusions could be
summarized as follows:
1. After high temperature oxidation test at 800
/C14 C for
100 h in static air, the oxidation resistance of Ni–
Cr–2.0 % Nb–M (specimen B) Ni-based superalloy
is enhanced about 25%, evaluated by the average
oxidation rate coefﬁcient ( k
p).
2. The layered oxide scale mainly consists of Cr 2O3 and
small quantities of NiCr 2O4,A l 2O3, and TiO 2. NiO
is spotted out in the oxidation scale of specimen A
(Ni–Cr–M alloy). Proper amount of Nb (i.e.,
2.0 wt.%) in Ni-based superalloy will help enhance
the high temperature oxidation resistance notably,
due to the formation of a Nb-rich layer which pre-
vents the outward diffusion of Ni.
3. Though Nb is propitious to enhance the high tem-
perature resistance of Ni-based superalloy, the
amount should be appropriate. Too much Nb (i.e.,
2.5 wt.%) leads to a discontinuous inner white layer
which allows for inward oxygen diffusion, thus affect-
ing the high temperature oxidation resistance.
