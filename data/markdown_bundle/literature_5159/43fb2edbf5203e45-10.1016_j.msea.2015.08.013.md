1. Introduction
Due to the high strength-to-weight ratio, high strength and
ductility at room temperature and excellent castability, Al–Cu alloys
are wildly applied in automobile, aircraft construction and space
technology [1–9]. However, the application of the Al –Cu alloy is
limited at elevated temperatures (above 423 K), because the coar-
sening of the
θ′ precipitates at elevated temperatures can swiftly
decreases the mechanical properties of the Al –Cu alloy [8].H e n c e ,
further improve the mechanical properties of the Al –Cu alloy at
elevated temperatures has become an urgent problem to solve.
Modiﬁcation/inoculation is a common method to improve the
mechanical properties of Al alloys. Zhang et al. [10] suggested that
adding cerium into the Al –Cu alloy can induce the formation of the
Al8Cu4Ce phase, which will strengthen the grain boundaries and
pin the grain boundaries sliding at elevated temperatures. Yao
et al. [8,11 ]reported that the Al –Cu alloy modi ﬁed by La can in-
crease the peak age hardness and improve the creep resistance at
elevated temperatures. Xiao et al. [12] found that the Al –Cu–Mg–
Ag modi ﬁed by Ce can induce much ﬁner and denser precipita-
tions of
Ω and θ′ phases, and the tensile strength of the alloy both
at room temperature and high temperatures was greatly im-
proved. Recently, the Al –Cu Alloys inoculated by Zr-based metallic
glass showed an obviously smaller grain size and much ﬁner and
denser
θ′ precipitations phases, and the high strength of 569 MPa
and ductility of 1 1.1% of the inoculated Al –Cu alloys at room
temperature was reported by us [13]. Hence, further investigate
the deformation behaviors of the inoculated Al –Cu Alloys at ele-
vated temperatures are bene ﬁted for the future alloy design and
the industry applications.
In the present work, the tensile tests of the uninoculated and
inoculated Al –Cu alloys were carried out at various temperatures
from 433 K to 493 K and strain rates from 10 /C0 4 s/C0 1 to 10 /C0 1 s/C0 1.
The mechanism of the enhanced tensile properties at elevated
temperatures was discussed.
2. Experimental
The Al –Cu alloy with chemical composition (in wt%) of 5.5Cu,
0.45Mn, 0.30Ti, 0.15V, 0.15Zr, 0.004B and balance Al was used in
this work. The alloys were melted at 1003 K in the graphite cru-
cible using the electric resistance furnace. After addition of 0.2 wt%
Zr55Cu30Al10Ni5 metallic glass for 45 s, the Al –Cu alloy melt was
poured into steel die of 200 /C2 90 /C2 12 mm3. Subsequent, the dog-
bone shaped tensile samples with a gauge cross section of
5.0 /C2 2.5 mm
2 and a gauge length of 30.0 mm were manufactured
after the T6 heat treatment (solution at 81 1 K for 10 h and aging at
438 K for 10 h).
The tensile tests were performed by a servo-hydraulic materials
testing system (INSTRON, 5869, England) at 433 and 493 K
with the strain rates 10/C0 4,1 0/C0 3,1 0/C0 2 and 10/C0 1 s/C0 1. Each specimen
was heated to the setting temperature by thermo-coupled

When the tensile temperature was 433 K, the strength and ductility
of the inoculated alloys were 362 MPa and 1 1.6% at 10 /C0 4 s/C0 1,
83 MPa and 5.3% higher than the uninoculated alloys, respectively;
at 10

ductility of the inoculated alloys were 340 MPa and 16.5% at
10
/C0 1 s/C0 1, with the increment of 36 MPa and 9.8% than the unin-

strain rate, the strength decreased with the increasing tensile

3.2. Mechanism of the enhanced mechanical properties at elevated
temperatures
Obviously, the enhanced tensile properties are due to the mi-
crostructure change by addition of Zr
55Cu30Al10Ni5 metallic glass,
and the two aspects of re ﬁned grain strengthening and pre-
cipitation strengthening are considered. Fig. 2 shows the micro-
structures of the uninoculated and inoculated Al –Cu alloys after T6
heat treatment. As indicated, the grain size of the inoculated alloy
(about 35
μm) was much smaller than the uninoculated alloy
(about 180 μm). Hence, the re ﬁned grain strengthening is the one
of the strengthening mechanism, according to the classical Hall-
Petch relation [16]. However, the grain boundary will soften at
elevated temperature, which can weaken the re ﬁned grain
strengthening effect. The higher the temperature is, the more
obvious softening is. It is clearly found that at 493 K with 10
/C0 4 s/C0 1
the strength and ductility of the inoculated alloys were only
18 MPa and 1.2% higher than the uninoculated alloys, indicating
that the re ﬁned grain strengthening effect decreased obviously.
On another hand, precipitation strengthening plays a sig-
niﬁcant role in improving tensile properties for aged Al –Cu alloys,
and the precipitates' size and number are of great signi ﬁcance
[1 7,18]. Moreover, the smaller grain size can obtained the smaller
θ
′ precipitates [19,20]. Fig. 3 shows the micrographs of precipitates
in the uninoculated and inoculated Al –Cu alloys under various
condition after tensile test. At the strain rate of 10 /C0 1 s/C0 1, the θ′
precipitates in the uninoculated Al –Cu alloy ( Fig. 3 a and c) had a
long mean length (about 140 nm); while the larger number and
small average length (about 80 nm) of the
θ′ precipitates can be
found in the inoculated alloys ( Fig. 3 b and d); while at the same
temperature of 493 K, with decreasing strain rate, some pre-
cipitates coarsening were obvious, however
θ′ precipitates in the
inoculated Al–Cu alloy (about 90 nm, as shown in Fig. 3f), also had
smaller size than the uninoculated alloys (about 160 nm, as shown
in Fig. 3e). Hence, the θ′ precipitates in the inoculated Al –Cu alloys
always have smaller size, more number and more homogeneous
distribution than the uninoculated alloys at the same tensile
condition, which is more helpful for impeding the dislocation
movement.
