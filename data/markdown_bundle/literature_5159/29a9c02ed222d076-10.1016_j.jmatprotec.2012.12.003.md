1. Introduction
To date, the practical utilization of alloys based on the
intermetallic compound /H9253-TiAl (hereinafter, TiAl alloys, its
microstructure, phase transformation behavior and fracture behav-
ior, etc. were summarized by Sha and Malinov (2009)) has centered
on the cast material used in the form of small-scale precision cast-
ings, such as automotive turbocharger turbine wheels, as reported
by Noda (1998) and Tetsui (2001), and low-pressure turbine blades
in jet engines, as reported by Fujimura et al. (2008–2009). Very
little consideration has been given to the practical application of
forged material. The reason for this is that the high-temperature
deformability of TiAl alloys is quite poor; meaning that hot-forging
(required in ordinary industrial processes for the manufacturing
of a large component) could not be employed. Moreover, even if
forged material were available, because of the remarkably poor
machinability of conventional TiAl alloys (from the results of Aust
and Niemann (1999)), it would still be difﬁcult to make a prod-
uct from a conventional TiAl alloy within the industrially allowable
cost.
In recent years, however, a new type of TiAl alloy contain-
ing /H9252 phase that can be hot forged in general-use facilities has

use for military application (the exact nature of the applications
has not been disclosed). Since this alloy is characterized by the
presence of the /H9252 phase having excellent deformability at forging
temperature, it can be forged without cracking even when subject
to conventional hot-forging conditions of extremely high-speed
deformation and rapid cooling. Moreover, the machinability also
improved because of the presence of the /H9252 phase. Therefore, manu-
facturing a meter class component from TiAl alloy, which is difﬁcult
to make with current precision casting and isothermal forging tech-
nology of TiAl alloy have become feasible. A number of other alloys
based on the same principle have also been proposed as reported
by Clemens et al. (2008) and Niu et al. (2011).
Other uses anticipated for the Ti–42Al–5Mn (at%) forged alloy
include turbine blades in the intermediate temperature range for
various types of engines, and structural components for aerospace
applications. The fabrication method for such components would
consist of ﬁrst manufacturing a large ingot, followed by repeated
hot-forging for rough shaping, and, after microstructural control
by means of heat treatment, substantial machining (usually, more
than half of the entire volume is removed) to obtain the desired
shape. The minimal required properties for such applications with
respect to the manufacturing process would be hot-workability
and machinability, as well as high-temperature strength from the
perspective of the material properties, and reasonable cost in an

industrial context (in particular, an ingot cost that is not excessive).
Also, depending on the speciﬁc application, other characteristics
that would of course be required would include oxidation resis-
tance, fatigue properties, impact resistance, and weldability, etc.
The alloy examined here is composed of a lamellar structure
with /H9252 and /H9253 phases. It should be noted that the use of ingots
melted in ceramic crucibles, which was considered in recent years
for the purpose of cost reduction, necessarily leads to increased
oxygen concentration due to contamination from the crucible, as
reported by Gomes et al. (2008) and Tetsui et al. (2012a,b,c). This
combination of structure and oxygen concentration is thought to
have a major inﬂuence on the aforementioned minimal required
properties. For example, an increased /H9252 phase associated with
superior high-temperature deformability would boost forgeability,
but would reduce high-temperature strength. Accordingly, in seek-
ing to realize a wider industrial applicability for the Ti–42Al–5Mn
(at%) forged alloys, the inﬂuence of various factors on their respec-
tive properties must be quantitatively ascertained to achieve an
optimal balance of these properties.
The research reported here was carried out with the inten-
tion of clarifying the optimal speciﬁcations for Ti–42Al–5Mn (at%)
forged alloys, considering the inﬂuence of the /H9252phase and lamel-
lar structure fraction as well as the oxygen concentration on the
above-noted minimal required properties for industrial utilization,
speciﬁcally, hot-forgeability, machinability, and high-temperature
strength.
2. Experimental procedure
2.1. Materials, and evaluation method for hot-forgeability
Four types of Ti–42Al–5Mn (at%) ingots (or Ti–28.7Al–7.0Mn
in terms of wt%) with their chemical compositions presented in
Table 1, were produced using triple melt VAR and induction melt-
ing in yttria crucibles. Each of the ingots had a diameter of approx.
260 mm, weighing approx. 120 kg. In the case of induction melting,
two types of yttria crucibles measuring 320 mm in diameter and
480 mm in height were used. The porous-type yttria crucible was
used for the melting of Y2O3-1 and Y2O3-2 ingot and the dense-
type yttria crucible was used for the melting of Y2O3-3 ingot. Both
crucibles were specially manufactured for this research. Porosity
measured in accordance with JIS R 1634 of the test piece (approx.
∅ 75 mm × t 15 mm) that manufactured from the same conditions
as crucible (mixing ratio of yttria powder of various particle size and
sintering temperature, etc.) was 28.9% in the porous-type, and 1.0%
in the dense-type. The raw materials used consisted of 3N purity
sponge titanium, 4N purity aluminum pellets, and 2N purity Mn
ﬂakes. The melting atmosphere was Ar 5.3 × 10−4 Pa, and the hold-
ing time after melting of all raw materials was varied in the range
of 5–10 min so as to change the contamination-induced oxygen
concentration. The melt temperature measured by a Pt-30Rh/Pt-
6Rh thermocouple protected by a quartz tube was approx. 1540 ◦C.
Subsequently, the melt was poured into a cast iron mold measuring
265 mm in diameter, thus forming column-shaped ingots.
For the composition under consideration, the elevated
temperature is accompanied by the phase transformation
/H9251 + /H9252 + /H9253 → /H9251 + /H9252 → /H9252, and the hot forging is operated in the
/H9251 + /H9252 phase ﬁeld. However, this phase transformation behavior is
affected by the oxygen concentration. Small test pieces from each
ingot were subjected to water-quenching tests from various tem-
peratures for the purpose of conﬁrming the phase transformation
temperature as well as obtaining the /H9252-phase fraction at the forging
temperature.
Next, each ingot was subjected to hot-forging testing under
industrially required conditions at 1250 ◦C. After upset forging to
approx.
 1/2 the height along the vertical axis of the ingot (reheating
after each of several forging operations), the direction of forging on
the ingot was changed by 90◦ and extend forging was undertaken.
Here the forging time was approx. 2 min after removal from the
furnace, with continuous forging performed while the material was
rotated. Repeating this extend forging operation, the initial cylin-
drical shape was changed to a square bar, and then the cross section
was gradually thinned to a ﬁnal size of approx. 70 mm × 70 mm.
The presence of any cracking was evaluated with respect to this
industrial hot-forging test.
2.2. Evaluation methods for properties
With regard to the four types of material after hot-forging,
heat treatment was undertaken in accordance with the conditions
selected on the basis of the above-noted water-quenching test
results. The three heat-treatment temperatures selected for each
material corresponded to the upper side of the /H9251+ /H9252+ /H9253 region,
the lower side of the /H9251 + /H9252 region, and the middle of the /H9251 + /H9252
region. Because the transformation temperature of /H9251+ /H9252+ /H9253 → /H9251 + /H9252
changes according to the oxygen content, we have executed the
water-quenching test with a very detailed temperature span, and
conﬁrmed the transformation temperature for the four alloys. In
the heat treatment of the lower side of the /H9251+ /H9252 region, the tem-
perature was set from this transformation temperature to approx.
10 ◦C for each material. This means that from the viewpoint of dis-
tance from the transformation temperature, the inﬂuence of the
heat-treatment temperature is similar for each material. Holding
times were in the range of 3–20 h, and the lower the tempera-
ture, the longer the time. The cooling rate after heat treatment
was 1000 ◦C/h in all cases. Microstructural observation of each
heat-treated material was performed using optical microscopy and
scanning electron microscopy, and image processing was under-
taken to determine the area fraction of lamellar structure and
/H9252-phase. Image processing was applied to about 10 image captures
for each sample, and average values were employed.
For each of the heat-treated materials, machinability and tensile
properties were evaluated. In this study, all heating for the hot-
forging and heat treatment were executed in the usual atmosphere,
therefore, a lot of oxide scale was ﬂaked off, and microstructural
changes occurred in the surface area. Then in the following evalu-
ations, to eliminate the inﬂuence of microstructural changes, the
surface of each of the heat-treated materials was shaved off by
3 mm or more before examination. Machinability was assessed in
terms of inﬂuence on tool wear and the soundness of the machined
portion. Lathe machining was employed using a cemented carbide
cutting tool made of K10 material and having an equilateral tri-
angle shape, with a 0◦ relief angle. The inﬂuence on tool wear
was evaluated according to the procedure described below. Cut-
ting conditions were maintained to be constant, with a depth of cut
of approx. 0.25 mm, feed rate of approx. 0.3 mm/rev, and material
removed volume of approx. 8 cm3; only the rpm was varied. When
the rpm is increased while maintaining a constant depth of cut and
feed rate, the amount of material removed per unit time increases
substantially, and tool wear occurs more easily. Therefore, in the
present research, the nominal material removal rate (cm3/min) was
deﬁned as a cutting condition that inﬂuences tool wear. Note that
the reason the actual material removal rate was not used was that
this rate decreases with the progress of tool wear, meaning that the
removal rate appears to decrease. Next, ﬂank wear measured by the
digital stereoscopic optical microscope was used as an indicator of
tool wear. The targeted 8 cm3 of material removed was not achieved
for every set of conditions and machining testing was stopped in
cases where tool wear was substantial, even before the removed
amount reached 8 cm3. Therefore, ﬂank wear per removed volume
(/H9262m/cm3) was used.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Hot-forgeability
Given that the /H9252-phase area fraction at 1250 ◦C measured for
each water-quenched ingot sample was VAR: 48%, Y2O3-1: 33%,
Y2O3-2: 29%, and Y2O3-3: 11%, it was conﬁrmed that the /H9252-phase
fraction tended to decrease with higher oxygen concentration. This
is thought to be due to /H9251-phase stabilization caused by oxygen.
Fig. 1 shows the external appearance of each material after indus-
trial hot-forging testing. Cracking occurs only in the case of the
Y2O3-3 ingot, having an oxygen concentration of 0.26 wt% and
a /H9252-phase fraction of 11% at the forging temperature. The other
ingots are free of noticeable defects and were successfully forged
into
 the desired bar shape. Thus, repeated hot-forging (as indus-
trially required) is possible if a /H9252-phase fraction of around 30%
can be achieved even when the oxygen concentration is as high
as 0.16 wt%.

corresponds to the upper side of the /H9251 + /H9252 + /H9253 region, medium tem-

the high-temperature tensile strength, an elevated oxygen concen-
tration, reduced /H9252-phase fraction, and increased lamellar structure

optimize both manufacturability and high-temperature strength
simultaneously. However, for an ingot melted in a yttria crucible
and having a comparatively high oxygen concentration of 0.16%,
it was observed that manufacturability and high-temperature
strength could coexist at relatively high levels.
From our previous study (Tetsui et al., 2012a,b,c), it was found
that the amount of inclusion and oxygen content increases by
ceramic crucible melting. However, it was also found that the dete-
rioration in properties by this increase is small. Moreover, this
results in rather positive effects depending on the property (for
instance, strength). This is also conﬁrmed in the present study. For
example, Y2O3-1 material of oxygen content 0.13 wt% has almost
the same room temperature ductility and higher strength as the
VAR material shown in Table 2. Moreover, in the case of Y2O3-2
material of oxygen content 0.16 wt%, room temperature ductility is
slightly lower, but the strength is much higher than that of the VAR
material.
Accordingly, in the case of a TiAl alloy, we are convinced
that more attention needs to be paid to ceramic crucible melted
material, both from the standpoint of cost reduction and desired
properties.
4.
 Conclusion
Consideration was given to the inﬂuence of oxygen concentra-
tion and phase composition on manufacturability (hot-forgeability
and machinability) and high-temperature strength with respect
to Ti–42Al–5Mn (at%) forged alloys. The following results were
obtained.
-
 Reduced oxygen concentration and increased /H9252-phase fraction
are required for improved hot forgeability and machinability.
- Increased oxygen concentration, reduced /H9252-phase fraction, and
increased lamellar structure fraction are required for improved
high-temperature strength. Thus, it is difﬁcult to optimize the
characteristics in question simultaneously.
- However, at a high oxygen concentration of 0.16 wt% for an ingot
melted in a ceramic crucible, it was found that these properties
could coexist at comparatively high levels. They suggest that for
this alloy ceramic crucible melting can be better suited than water
cooled copper crucible melting, but more experiments and a more
detailed study are needed to strongly validate that behavior.
