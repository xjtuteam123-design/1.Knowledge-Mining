The study on low temperature sintering of a W–Ni–Cu–Sn heavy alloy 
Wentan Zhu, Wensheng Liu, Qingshan Cai *, Yunzhu Ma* , Xinkuan Pang, Chaoping Liang 
State Key Laboratory of Powder Metallurgy, Central South University, Changsha 410083, PR China 
ABSTRACT 
In this work, we explore the possibility of reducing the sintering temperature by 
designing a new tungsten heavy alloy. A new W–6.3Ni–2.7Cu–1Sn heavy alloy was 
fabricated by high –energy ball milling –assisted vacuum sintering. According to DSC 
analysis, the liquidus temperature of the alloy appears at a very low temperature, 
1145 °C, and results in the densification during sintering of milled W–6.3Ni–2.7Cu–1Sn 
powders. 98.5% relative density was obtained with an average W grain size of 7 μm for 
the powder compact sintered at 1175 ° C. The XRD reveal that, the phase composition of 
the sintered alloy surfaces are mainly composed of W phase and γ–(Ni, Cu, W, Sn) 
phase. In addition, t he effects of sintering temperature on the microstructural 
characteristics and m echanical properties of the alloys were studied. The hardness and 
tensile strength of the alloys first increase to a maximum and then decrease as a function 
of sintering temperature. The highest hardness (479. 5 HV) and tensile stre ngth (752.7 
MPa) were obtained at 1125 °C and 1175 °C, respectively. The mechanical properties of 
W–6.3Ni–2.7Cu–1Sn alloys are comparable to the conventional W –Ni–Cu alloys, 
despite the fact that the sintering temperature is 250–300 °C lower. 
Keywords: Tungsten heavy alloy ; Low t emperature sintering; Microstructure; 
Mechanical properties; Fracture mode.

1. Introduction 
Tungsten heavy alloys (WHAs) are two–phase composites consisting of nearly 
spherical BCC W particles embedded in a FCC matrix phase, such as Ni–Fe or Ni–Cu. 
Due to its high density, high strength and good ductility, WHAs are widely used in 
aerospace and national defense industries. The conventional WHAs is fabricated by a 
powder metallurgical process employing liquid phase sintering (LPS) of the mixed 
powders of W, Ni, Fe，Cu or Co in a range of 1450–1550 °C [1-3]. However, high 
sintering temperature leads to a coarse-grain microstructure (about 40 –60 μm ), which 
may limit the further improvement of WHAs mechanical properties. Generally, 
ultrafine–grained materials provide better performance, such as high tensile strength, 
toughness and hardness according to Hall –Petch relationship. Studies [4, 5] have found 
that low sintering temperature WHAs shows a fine grain size and attractive mechanical 
properties. Therefore, the research es on low sintering temperature WHAs attract 
interests. 
For WHAs, when sintering temperature reaches melting point of the matrix phase, 
there is a rapid initial densification due to capillary and concurrent with the 
rearrangement of W particles. As densification slows, the solution –
reprecipitation 
become dominant, in the meantime, grain growth and pores gradually eliminate [6]. 
Therefore liquidus temperature of WHAs is determined by melting point of the matrix 
phase. German et al. [7] reported that the lower sintering temperature W –Ni–Cu–Mn 
system (about 1200 °C) is now critical to research on microgravity process 
(densification, distortion and coarsening) since it matches with furnace capabilities on 
international Space Station. Nizhenko et al. [8, 9] demonstrated that such as W –Ni–Sn 
and W –Co–Sn systems, a liquid phase as a result of forming eutectic there is rapid 
specimen densification at 1200 ° C. However, up to now, the relative literatures on lower

3.3 Microstructure 
Fig. 5 shows the back scattered electron (BSE) micrographs of W–6.3Ni–2.7Cu–1Sn 
alloys sintered at 1100–1200 °C. With increasing of sintering temperature , the alloy 
presented a large different in microstructure, which can be attributed to the change from 
solid to liquid phase sintering. For the low sintering temperature of 1100 °C (Fig. 5a), 
the tiny W particles appear in bright areas having an agglomerate structure whereas t he 
matrix phase displays as darker regions with an irregular shape. The microstructure is 
typical of those expected from solid –state sintering. At 1125 °C (Fig. 5b), W grains 
grow up slightly and the distribution of matrix phase is more uniformly observed in the 
microstructure. The interconnectivity is decreased slightly due to W grains surrounded 
by the matrix phase. As sintering temperature rises to 1150 ° C (see Fig. 5c), the liquid 
phase forms (according to DSC analysis shown in Fig. 3) and the W grains size increase 
and become spherical. Newly formed liquid phase penetrated into W–W particles, and 
connectivity between W grains is reduced. However, there are still some tiny and 
irregular W particl es embedded in matrix phase which indict ing that LPS proceeded 
insufficiently. Along with sintering temperature further raising (see Fig. 5d and 5e), the 
W grains become rounder and larger which exhibit ed the typical W grain growth 
phenomenon. This is due to small W particles dissolved into the matrix phase and 
precipitate on the larger ones, which promoting the growth and spheroidization of W 
grains.

3.4 Mechanical properties and fracture morphology 
Fig.8 shows the variation of hardness and tensile strength with the sintering 
temperature. As the sintering temperature rises, both the hardness and tensile strength of 
the alloys first increased then decreased. The highest hardness (479. 5 HV) and tensile 
strength (752.7 MPa) were obtained at 1125 °C and 1175 °C respectively. The variation 
in mechanical properties of the alloys is affected by the relative density and 
microstructural characteristics. At 1100 ° C, the sintered alloy has a relatively low 
density (Fig. 4) and high porosity (Fig. 9a), which leading a poor mechanical properties. 
At 1125 °C, an increased in hardness and tensile strength of the alloy is observed. This 
is due to the decrease of pores and i ncrease of densification. As the sintering 
temperature increases to 1175 °C, a visibly increases and a more uniform distribution of 
the dark matrix phase can be observed (Fig. 5d), thus, hardness decreases slightly and 
tensile strength reaches a maximum value. When the sintering temperature is up to 
1200 °C, some micro –cracks (show ed in Fi g. 9e) appear on the fracture surface of 
alloys. In addition, the relative density of the alloy slightly decreases . Therefore the 
comprehensive mechanical properties of the alloys are decreased. 
The effects of different sintering temper atures on the fracture morphology are shown 
in Fig. 9. It is generally known that there are four kinds of fracture modes in WHAs: W 
cleavage (W); intergranular rupture of the tungsten grain (W –W); W–matrix interfacial 
failure (W–M) and matrix rupture (M) [17]. For the alloy sintered at 1100 °C (Fig. 9a), 
it is clearly seen that many micro –pores were observed on the fracture surface. As a 
result of little liquid phase formation, few W grains were surrounded by the matrix

4. Conclusions 
In the present study, fine–grained W–6.3Ni–2.7Cu–1Sn heavy alloys were successfully 
fabricated by low temperature sintering. Effect of sintering temperature on the 
microstructure and mechanical properties of W–6.3Ni–2.7Cu–1Sn alloy has been 
studied in detail. Some important conclusions are drawn as follows: 
(1) For W–6.3Ni–2.7Cu–1Sn alloy, the liquidus temperature is about 1145 ° C. Relative 
densities of 98.5% can be obtained for W–6.3Ni–2.7Cu–1Sn alloy at 11 75 °C. 
Average grain size can be controlled bellowed 10 μm due to low sintering 
temperature. 
(2) For W–6.3Ni–2.7Cu–1Sn alloy, the ultimate tensile strength and hardness are 75 2.7 
MPa and 479.5 HV , respectively. The obtained tensile strength is comparable to the 
conventional sintered W–Ni–Cu alloys but with 250 –300° C lower sintering 
temperature. 
 (3) The fracture mode of samples was most of W –W intergranular fracture sintered at 
the temperature various from 1100 to 1200 ° C, and the ductile tearing of matrix 
phase increases gradually with increasing sintering temperature.
