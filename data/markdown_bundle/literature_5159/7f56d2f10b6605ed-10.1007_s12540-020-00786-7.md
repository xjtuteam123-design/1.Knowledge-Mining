Effect of Microstructural Features on the High-Cycle Fatigue Behavior 
of CoCrFeMnNi High-Entropy Alloys Deformed at Room and Cryogenic 
Temperatures
Gyung Tae Lee1,2 · Jong Woo Won1 · Ka Ram Lim1 · Minju Kang3 · Heoun Jun Kwon1 · Young Sang Na1  · 
Yoon Suk Choi2

Abstract 
In this study, we examined the effect of deformation twins and dislocation cell structures on the fatigue properties of the 
CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy using rotational bending fatigue tests. The dislocation cell structures and deformation 
twins were generated by prestraining the CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy at room temperature (27 °C) and a cryogenic 
temperature (− 196 °C), respectively. To eliminate the effect of different material strengths on fatigue behavior, the tensile 
strengths of the specimens evaluated in the fatigue tests were kept similar by controlling the prestraining under room and 
cryogenic temperatures. The results of the rotational bending fatigue tests revealed that the CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy 
alloy prestrained at room temperature exhibited higher fatigue resistance and fatigue limit than the specimen prestrained at 
a cryogenic temperature. A small quantity of large micro-voids was formed at the triple junction of the grain boundaries in 
the specimen prestrained at room temperature, whereas a large quantity of small micro-voids was formed in the region where 
the deformation twins intersected the grain boundaries in the specimen prestrained at a cryogenic temperature. Therefore, 
it is concluded that the different aspects of micro-void formation affected the crack initiation and, consequently, the fatigue 
properties of the room and cryogenic temperature-prestrained alloys.
Keywords High-entropy alloy · CoCrFeMnNi · Deformation twin · Dislocation cell structure · High cycle fatigue
1 Introduction
For conventional alloys [1 , 2], the addition of alloying 
elements is typically limited to relatively small quanti-
ties because increases in the number of alloying elements 
enhance the formation intermetallic compounds that can 
degrade mechanical properties. However, high-entropy 
alloys are expected to suppress the formation of intermetallic 
compounds due to their high configurational entropy, even 
though they are composed of numerous main elements in 
similar fractions [1 –4]. High-entropy alloys exhibit high 
strength, ductility, and fracture toughness due to the severe 
lattice distortion caused by their structural and composi-
tional characteristics, and exhibit greater resistance to cor -
rosion and wear than commercial stainless steel [5 , 6].
High-entropy alloys can be classified as face-centered 
cubic (FCC) or body-centered cubic (BCC) alloys. Studies 
have been actively conducted on the replacement of the main 
high-entropy alloy elements, adjustment of their atomic 
ratios, and development of new alloys composed of three 
or four elements [7 –19]. The CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy 
alloy is a representative FCC alloy and has high strength 
and toughness at cryogenic temperatures due to the genera-
tion of deformation twins at low stacking fault energy (SFE). 
Owing to such characteristics, CoCrFeMnNi has been inves-
tigated for use as a substitute for conventional alloys applied 
in extreme environments, such as cryogenic temperatures 
[6, 7, 19, 20].

In general, for a metal to be used as a structural material, 
a high yield strength that can inhibit permanent deformation 
and excellent fatigue properties that can resist repeated stress 
and deformation environments are required. The research 
team of this study previously reported the efficient improve-
ment of the strength of the CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy 
by activating deformation twins through the rolling of the 
alloy at cryogenic temperatures [21]. However, few studies 
have been conducted on the fatigue properties of the CoCr-
FeMnNi high-entropy alloy, and no studies have been con-
ducted on the effects of different deformation mechanisms 
associated with temperature and the resulting microstruc -
tures on fatigue behavior. Therefore, systematic research 
on the fatigue properties of the CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy 
alloy is essential before high-entropy alloys can be utilized 
as structural materials.
The CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy used in this study 
exhibits different SFEs depending on its temperature. Thus, 
it also manifests different plastic deformation mechanisms 
according to temperature, forming dislocation cell structures 
during deformation at room temperature, or deformation 
twins during deformation at cryogenic temperatures. It also 
exhibits different microstructures and mechanical proper -
ties depending on the deformation temperature [20, 22]. 
In particular, the formation of deformation twins has been 
observed to have the same effect as grain boundaries and 
promote grain refinement, thereby contributing to excellent 
mechanical properties with relatively small deformation [21, 
23, 24].
Accordingly, in this study, the effects of initial micro -
structural differences in a CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy 
obtained through different deformation mechanisms on its 
high-cycle fatigue properties were examined. In addition, 
the fatigue failure and crack formation mechanisms of the 
deformation-induced microstructures were investigated to 
observe the relationship between the deformation mecha -
nism and mechanical behavior.
2 Experimental Method
The parent elements of the CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy 
used in this study had a purity of at least 99.5%. A 24-kg 
ingot of the subject alloy was prepared through vacuum 
induction melting to ensure internal homogeneity and pre-
vent the oxidation of the casting alloy melt. The internal 
homogeneity of the prepared ingot was also increased by 
homogenizing heat treatment at 1200 °C for 24 h, followed 
by furnace cooling to 900 °C, and then air cooling to room 
temperature. After the homogenizing heat treatment, rolling 
for a rolling reduction of approximately 70% was performed 
at 700 °C. Next, specimens were annealed at 1000 °C for an 
hour and denoted as as-received (AR). The AR specimen 
prestrained (30%) at room temperature was denoted as the 
RT specimen, and the AR specimen prestrained (18%) at 
a cryogenic temperature (− 196 °C) was defined as the CT 
specimen.
Tensile tests were then conducted at a strain rate of 
10−3s−1 using a 100-kN Instron 5982 tensile testing machine 
to investigate the mechanical properties of each specimen. 
To analyze the microstructures of the specimens, electron 
backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analyses were conducted on 
their transverse-direction surfaces using an SU-6600 field 
emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM). For this, 
electrolytic polishing was performed by mixing 95% glacial 
acetic acid with 5% perchloric acid. Fatigue specimens were 
prepared in accordance with the ISO 1143 [25] standards: 
the length of each specimen was 60 mm and the diameters 
of the test and grip parts were 6 and 8 mm, respectively. The 
high-cycle fatigue tests were conducted at a rate of 50 Hz 
using a KDMT-240 rotating bending fatigue testing machine 
with a stress ratio (R) of − 1 at 2 kgf·m. The resulting fatigue 
fracture surfaces were observed using a JSM-6610LV scan-
ning electron microscope (SEM) focused on the fatigue 
crack initiation site, which is an important location affecting 
fatigue failure under high-cycle fatigue. After performing 
electrolytic polishing on the vertical surface of the fatigue 
fracture, microstructural analysis of the fatigue crack initia-
tion site was also conducted using an SU-6600 FE-SEM and 
EBSD analysis.
3 Experiment Results
3.1 Mechanical Properties and Microstructural 
Analysis
The fatigue strength of a metal is affected by its tensile 
strength [26]. Therefore, it was necessary to ensure that both 
the RT and CT specimens had the same tensile strength to 
reliably compare their fatigue behaviors and the effects of 
their microstructures. To achieve this, the RT specimen was 
subjected to 30% prestraining at room temperature, and its 
tensile strength was determined. The tensile strength of the 
CT specimen was then designed to be as close as possible to 
that of the RT specimen by adjusting the prestraining condi-
tions based on the cryogenic temperature.
The stress–strain curves and variations in the yield and ten-
sile stresses of the CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy according 
to prestraining at cryogenic temperature and prestrain applied 
to the CT specimen are shown in Fig. 1a and b, respectively. 
As the degree of prestraining at the cryogenic temperature 
increased, the strengths of the specimens increased and their 
elongation decreased. The tensile strength of the CT specimen 
could be closely matched to that of the RT specimen as possi-
ble by applying 18% prestraining at the cryogenic temperature,

dislocation cell structures of the RT specimen and deforma-

3.3 Fatigue Fracture Surface Analysis
The SEM images of the fatigue fracture surfaces of the RT 
and CT specimens are shown in Figs. 5 and 6, respectively, 
in which the fatigue crack initiation and crack propagation 
sites can be clearly identified. In general, fatigue cracks in 
metals are caused by irreversible dislocations of the grains 
near the material’s surface that initiate at grain boundaries, 
slip bands, and inclusions [30].
The fatigue crack initiation site of the RT specimen is 
shown in Fig. 5a, b, whereas Fig. 5c, e, and f show the crack 
propagation sites, and Fig.  5d shows the final fracture site. 
Close observation of the fatigue crack initiation site (Fig. 5b) 
reveals that the crack initiated near the specimen’s surface. 
When observing the crack propagation site, fatigue striation, 
which is evidence of fatigue failure, can also be identified.

During the high-cycle fatigue test, the fatigue crack initiation 
stage represents most of the overall fatigue life. As a result, 
the difference in the fatigue crack initiation site characteris-

life of the material. For the crack propagation sites shown 
in Figs. 6c, e, and f, fatigue striations can be observed in the 
RT specimen. These sites are clearer at lower magnifica-

The differences in the fatigue crack initiation sites and 
crack propagation sites of the RT and CT specimens appear 
to have been caused by the microstructural differences 
between the two specimens. Among these differences, the 
fatigue striation spacing at the crack propagation site was 
examined in more detail. The fatigue striations represent the 
pattern of crack propagation and closure under cyclic stress. 
The fatigue striation spacing formed in this instance thus 
reflects the growth rate of the fatigue crack: the larger the 
fatigue striation spacing, the higher the fatigue crack growth 
rate [31]. Therefore, the fatigue crack growth rate of a metal 
can be determined by measuring the fatigue striation spacing 
under specific stress conditions.
The average fatigue striation spacings at the crack propa-
gation sites of the RT and CT specimens subjected to cyclic 
fatigue loading at the same stress amplitude (

are shown in Fig.  7. These spacings were measured at the 
same distance from the fatigue crack initiation site. The 
spacings indicate that the fatigue crack growth rate of the 
CT specimen was faster than that of the RT specimen. Thus, 
it is estimated that the CT specimen exhibited lower fatigue 
resistance under cyclic stress than the RT specimen. These 
differences in fatigue striation spacing appear to account 
for the different S–N curves of each specimen and the lower 
fatigue limit of the CT specimen.
4 Discussion
The process of fatigue failure can be divided into three 
stages: fatigue crack initiation, crack propagation, and final 
fracture [30]. In the high-cycle fatigue tests, the fatigue crack 
initiation represents most of the overall fatigue life, and the 
crack nucleation formed in this instance is the result of the 
fatigue damage caused by local stress concentration. Inter -
actions between and among dislocations, inclusions, grain 
boundaries, and deformation twins lead to the accumulation 
of dislocations, resulting in local stress concentrations and 
fatigue damage [32–34]. Therefore, the interactions between 
different microstructures in the RT and CT specimens and 
the dislocation movements due to cyclic stress were ana -
lyzed to explain the observed differences in fatigue crack 
behavior.
4.1 Slip Reversibility and Deformation 
Homogeneity
Fatigue damage is related to irreversibility, when disloca-
tions move and cannot return to their original positions dur-
ing fatigue [32]. As can be seen in Fig.  2b, dislocation cell 
structures were formed in the RT specimen during prestrain-
ing, as well as a considerable number of LABs inside the 
grains. Dislocations can easily move under cyclic stress at 
these LABs, and there is no dislocation accumulation [35, 
36]. Additionally, as LABs exhibit a very small difference in 
orientation compared to their adjacent grains, dislocations 
move in very similar directions [35, 37]. Therefore, disloca-
tions can easily move using the LABs as boundaries. As no 
local stress concentrations were observed in the LABs under 
cyclic stress, they do not act as crack-nucleation regions. 
Hence, in the case of the RT specimen, it is predicted that 
the grain boundaries or inclusions, instead of the LABs, will 
act as crack nucleation regions. As shown in Fig.  8a and b,

The inside of the CT specimen was observed to be com-

to the fatigue crack behavior, deformation twins can delay 
dislocation accumulation at grain boundaries by accepting 
shifting dislocations under cyclic stress [38]. However, in 
most fatigue-related studies, deformation twins have been 
observed to accelerate crack nucleation by acting as dislo-
cation accumulation locations, similar to grain boundaries 
[39–42]. That is, dislocation accumulations at deformation 
twins can be expected to accelerate local stress concentra -
tion and crack nucleation. Actually, as shown in Fig. 8c and 
d, in the case of the CT specimen, a large quantity of small 
micro-voids was generated along the grain boundaries or in 
regions where the twin and grain boundaries intersect.
Improving the deformation homogeneity can, therefore, 
minimize the local stress concentrations by inducing uni-
form stress inside the material under cyclic stress, and thus 
has a positive effect on the fatigue properties [38, 43]. Ker-
nel average misorientation (KAM) analysis was conducted 
at the fatigue crack initiation site to identify the degree of 
stress concentration on the microstructures in each specimen 
after fatigue failure under the same stress condition (

606 MPa). The results of this analysis are shown in Fig. 9 b 
and d. It can be observed that micro-voids formed in the 
stress concentration distribution region of both the RT and 
CT specimens. However, these specimens exhibited different 
stress distributions. The RT specimen in Fig.  9b shows that 
stress was distributed along the grain boundaries as well as 
inside the grains. This stress distribution reduces the local 
stress concentrations at the grain boundaries and improves 
the fatigue properties of the specimen. Additionally, micro-
voids were observed at the grain boundary triple junctions, 
indicating that these points acted as crack nucleation regions 
in the RT specimen. For the CT specimen in Fig.  9d, how-
ever, intensive stress distributions were observed along the 
grain and twin boundaries. In particular, micro-voids were 
found to be concentrated in regions where the grain and twin 
boundaries intersect, indicating that these boundaries acted 
as crack nucleation regions in the CT specimen.
4.2 Fatigue Crack Nucleation
The findings of this study indicate that the RT and CT 
specimens exhibited different crack nucleation patterns 
as the stress concentration regions differed depending on 
the microstructure of the material. According to Thomp-
son [44], twin boundaries with low surface energy intersect 
with dislocations under cyclic stress to form non-coherent 
ledges, steps, or static extrusions. In this instance, the twin 
boundaries act as fatigue crack nucleation regions due to

the occurrence of dislocation accumulation and stress con-
centration. Additionally, according to Lewis [45], grain 
boundary triple junctions exhibit the highest stress under 
an external load, followed by regions where grain and twin 
boundaries intersect. Under external loads, stress concen-
trations mainly occur at these boundaries, thereby lower -
ing the critical energy required for crack nucleation and 
allowing micro-voids to form more easily [42]. Therefore, 
as shown in Fig.  9, micro-voids were mainly formed at the 
grain boundary triple junctions for the RT specimen and at 
regions where grain and twin boundaries intersect for the 
CT specimen.
Under cyclic stress, the crack at the fatigue crack initia-
tion site repeatedly propagates and closes, thereby forming 
fatigue striations. During this process, if there are voids 
inside the material, they will act as notches facilitating crack 
nucleation and allowing adjacent voids to be readily con -
nected and grow into cracks. In the CT specimen, relatively 
smaller micro-voids were generated in large quantities, and 
thus the distances between the micro-voids were smaller 
compared to those in the RT specimen. This is confirmed 
by the results shown in Fig.  8. Similarly, the micro-voids 
inside the CT specimen can easily grow into cracks under 
cyclic stress, which can be indirectly confirmed by compar-
ing the spacing between the micro-voids in Fig.  9c and e, 
with the fatigue striation spacing shown in Fig. 7. As shown 
in Fig. 9e, the distance between the micro-voids in the CT 
specimen was similar to the fatigue striation spacing. This 
indicates that the micro-voids also affected the growth of 
the cracks. Moreover, the closely spaced micro-voids in the 
CT specimen may have adversely affected crack nuclea-
tion, as they promoted the growth of cracks. As all closely 
spaced micro-voids were involved in the crack nucleation 
and growth processes, fatigue failure is likely to more easily 
occur in the CT specimen than in the RT specimen.
The fatigue strengths and fatigue ratios of Ti–6Al–4V 
[46], CP-Ti [47], TWIP steels [38, 48, 49], stainless steels 
[48, 50], TRIP steels [51, 52], and the CoCrFeMnNi high-
entropy alloy evaluated in this study are compared at the 
same cyclic stress ratio (R = − 1) in Fig. 10. In this study, the 
CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy exhibited a lower fatigue 
strength when prestrained at the cryogenic temperature than 
when prestrained at room temperature. However, Fig.  10 
shows that both the RT and CT CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy 
alloy specimens exhibit a higher fatigue strength than stain-
less steels, TWIP steels, TRIP steels, and commercially 
available pure titanium (CP-Ti). Additionally, both the RT 
and CT CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy specimens exhib-
ited fatigue ratios similar to that of Ti–6Al–4V alloys.
The CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy achieved observed 
high tensile strength because the deformation twins are eas-
ily activated at cryogenic temperatures, causing grain refine-
ment. That is, despite the deterioration in fatigue resistance 
due to the formation of deformation twins, the CoCrF -

5 Conclusions
In this study, experiments were performed to examine the 
effects of different microstructures formed under different 
prestraining temperatures on the high-cycle fatigue behav -
ior of the CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy. Tensile and 
high-cycle rotational bending fatigue tests were conducted, 
and the microstructures at the fatigue fracture surfaces and 
fatigue crack initiation sites were observed. The following 
conclusions were drawn:
(1) Dislocation cell structures composed of LABs were 
mainly formed in the specimen prestrained at room 
temperature, while deformation twins were generated 
in large quantities in the specimen prestrained at a cryo-
genic temperature of − 196 ℃ . These characteristics 
increased the yield and tensile strengths of the CoCrF-
eMnNi high-entropy alloy.
(2) While LABs do not interfere with the movement of dis-
locations under cyclic stress, grain and twin boundaries 
can act as obstacles to limit such movement. Therefore, 
twin boundaries act as crack nucleation regions due to 
the local stress concentrations.
(3) In the specimen prestrained at − 196 ℃, a large quantity 
of deformation twins generated numerous small micro-
voids. These micro-voids adversely affected the fatigue 
properties of the specimen as they promoted the growth 
of cracks.
(4) The CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy exhibited higher 
fatigue strength than stainless steels, TWIP steels, 
TRIP steels, and pure titanium. Therefore, it is a poten-
tial substitute for these alloys.
