deposited and heat-treated states, was investigated. Microscopic characterization results revealed that the mi -

1. Introduction
Nuclear power materials are subjected to long-term exposure to high- 
temperature and high-pressure environments, while simultaneously 
facing multiple threats from corrosion and radiation. Nickel-based alloys 
and their welding consumables are distinguished by their excellent 
corrosion resistance and mechanical properties at elevated tempera -
tures, making them widely used in pressurized water reactors [1] . 
NiCr-3 and NiCrFe-3 series nickel-based alloy welding consumables are 
primarily utilized for overlay welding of isolation layers on the safety 
ends of nozzle connections in nuclear power plant main equipment, as 
well as for butt welds, J-groove welds, and extensive overlay welding on 
tube sheets [2,3] . Although the NiCrFe-7 alloy exhibits superior corro -
sion resistance due to its higher chromium content [4,5] , its elevated 
ductility-dip cracking (DDC) susceptibility presents significant chal -
lenges for application in thick-walled nuclear components and highly 
constrained welds [6 – 8] . In contrast, ERNiCr-3 welding consumables 
demonstrate enhanced metallurgical quality and reduced crack suscep -
tibility [9] . However, their strength properties remain underexplored, 
with limited reports available on their application in component repair, 
overlay welding, or additive manufacturing components.
Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW) has found extensive application 
in the fabrication of critical nuclear power equipment due to its ad -
vantages, including low energy input, minimal spatter, and excellent 
formability. Recent studies have indicated that nickel-based alloys 
possess characteristics such as high liquid metal viscosity and poor 
weldability. Consequently, when applying GTAW to nuclear nickel- 
based alloys, the inherent high cooling rates and thermal gradients 
during the welding process often induce metallurgical defects such as 
porosity, incomplete fusion, and micro-cracks [10] . Furthermore, the 
rapid precipitation of the γ’ phase during welding can readily lead to 
strain-ageing cracking [11,12] . To suppress the precipitation of the γ’ 
phase, the aluminium content in nickel-based alloy welding consum -
ables should be maintained at a low level; however, this often results in a 
reduction of the mechanical properties of the alloy post-welding. To 
address this issue, Nb emerges as a potential substitute for γ’ phase 
formation. By promoting the formation of carbides or facilitating other 
precipitation phases, Nb can contribute to the strengthening of the alloy. 
When combined with heat treatment processes, this approach shows 
promise for synergistically optimizing the mechanical properties of 
nickel-based alloys without compromising weldability. This has signif -
icant engineering implications for meeting the increasingly stringent

Recent studies indicate that the microstructure of welded nickel- 
based alloys consists of stable γ austenite along with various pre -
cipitates [13] . At the end of the crystallization stage, niobium facilitates 
the formation of NbC. This type of incipient carbide exerts a significant 
pinning effect on grain boundaries [14] . Furthermore, the strong affinity 
between niobium and carbon can mitigate the formation of 
chromium-poor zones, thereby enhancing high-temperature strength 
[15] . However, when the Nb content is excessively high, a substantial 
amount of brittle and hard MC phases, as well as detrimental Topolog -
ically Close-Packed Phases (such as P-phase, μ -phase, Laves-phase, etc.), 
will form, significantly diminishing the mechanical properties [16,17] . 
Jeng et al. [18] reported that as the Nb content in Inconel 690 weld 
metal increased, the volume fraction of precipitated MC/MN during 
solidification also increased. This enhancement improved the curvature 
of high-angle grain boundaries and strengthened the ’ bite force ’ of these 
boundaries. Qi et al. [19] demonstrated that an increase in the Nb 
content of nickel-based alloys resulted in a higher area fraction of 
Nb-rich phases. This increase negatively affected the toughness of the 
weld metal while simultaneously enhancing the tensile strength. It is 
evident that the type, quantity, and size of precipitated phases signifi -
cantly influence the mechanical properties of Ni-Cr alloys, with the Nb 
content being a critical factor determining the type and quantity of 
precipitates formed.
Nickel-based alloys in the welded state have limited precipitation 
strengthening brought about by the Nb element alone, which often 
makes it difficult to meet the high standards required for practical nu -
clear power applications. At this point, post heat treatment, as an 
effective means, is introduced into the alloy preparation process. Lee 
et al. [20] conducted an aging heat treatment of Inconel 690 alloy at

precipitation of chromium-rich carbides, provided that the aging time 
did not exceed 16 h. Jiao et al. [21] developed a kinetic model that links 
carbide precipitation with chromium atom diffusion, investigating the 
relationship between carbide morphology and chromium content during 
the heat treatment of Inconel 690 alloy. The results indicate that at 
715

duces a micro-locking effect, which hinders grain boundary sliding and 
effectively enhances the mechanical properties of the material. In 
summary, while considerable research has been conducted on 
nickel-based alloys by scholars both domestically and internationally, 
systematic investigations into the precipitation kinetics of niobium 
under the non-equilibrium conditions of GTAW rapid solidification, as 
well as the strengthening and toughening mechanisms during subse -
quent heat treatment processes, remain insufficient. This deficiency is 
particularly pronounced in studies concerning NiCr-3 series welding 
consumables specifically designed for nuclear power applications.
In this study, two newly developed ERNiCr-3 welding wires with 
independently adjusted Nb contents were designed. Deposited metals 
with varying Nb levels were produced using Gas Tungsten Arc Welding 
(GTAW), followed by a systematic post heat treatment. The influence of 
Nb content on the precipitation behavior and mechanical properties was 
systematically examined in both the as-deposited and heat-treated 
conditions. The goal of this study is to investigate how changes in Nb 
content affect the microstructure and properties of ERNiCr-3 weld 
metals, in order to provide practical guidance for developing more 
reliable nickel-based welding materials for critical nuclear applications 
under harsh service conditions.

3. Results
3.1. Microstructure characterization of the deposited alloys
Fig. 2 presents the typical SEM morphology of the as-deposited al -
loys. It is evident that both as-deposited alloys display coarse γ -austenite 
dendrites that are arranged nearly parallel to the deposition direction, 
with a noticeable distribution of white precipitates at the γ boundaries. 
In Nb-L alloy, the grain boundaries are straight. As the Nb content in -
creases from 2.13 wt% to 3.00 wt%, there is no significant change in 
grain morphology. However, the curvature of the grain boundaries in -
creases, accompanied by a notable rise in the quantity of white 
precipitates.
EPMA line scan analysis of the dendritic regions of austenite at high 
magnification, as illustrated in Fig. 2 (b) and (d), reveals significant non- 
equilibrium segregation of alloying elements at the dendritic scale. This 
phenomenon is characterized by a markedly higher concentration of Nb, 
Ti, and C in the interdendritic regions compared to the interior of the 
dendrites, demonstrating a clear co-enrichment feature. Additionally, 
the white precipitates in the interdendritic regions are enriched with Nb, 
Ti, and C. Conversely, the concentrations of Cr and Fe in the 
interdendritic regions are notably reduced.
To investigate the dendrite growth characteristics of the as-deposited 
alloys, a low-magnification EBSD analysis was conducted, as illustrated 
in Fig. 3 . The results indicate that due to the maximum temperature 
gradient along the deposition direction, the austenite dendrites in the 
deposited metal grow outward along the [ 001 ] direction. Coarse 
columnar austenite grains can penetrate through the entire deposition 
layer. The austenite grains exhibit a high degree of orientation, with five 
{ 100 } -type principal polar density points clearly visible in the 
( 100 ) -pole diagram of Nb-L. Similar microstructural and morphological 
features are also observed in the Nb-H as-deposited alloys; however, the 
{ 100 } -type principal polar density points appear more discrete, indi -
cating a slight reduction in the texture strength of this alloy. Concur -
rently, as the Nb content increases, the microstructure of the alloy shows 
a slightly higher percentage of low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs, 
2 – 15

counted under EBSD, and the average grain sizes were 121.8 μ m and 
89.5 μ m, respectively. The increase of the Nb content (2.13 – 3.00 wt%) 
resulted in a gradual decrease of the average grain size, and thus it can 
be speculated that the slight difference in the growth orientation of the 
as-deposited alloys has a close relationship with the grain refinement 
brought about by the change of the Nb content.

Plus software to determine the average size and area fraction of the 
precipitated phases, with the results presented in Fig. 5 . Figures (a) and

tated phases. Fig. 5 (c) further quantifies the size distribution charac -

size of the precipitated phases for the Nb-L alloy is 0.98 μ m, whereas it

of the precipitated phases increases from 0.68 % for the Nb-L alloy to 
0.79 % for the Nb-H alloy. This suggests that a higher Nb content not 
only promotes an increase in the nucleation density of the precipitated 
phases but also inhibits the coarsening process of these phases.
3.2. Microstructure characterization of the Post heat-treated alloys
As mentioned in the introduction, Ni-Cr alloys often struggle to meet 
the stringent standards required for nuclear power applications due to 
the limited strengthening provided by the Nb element alone. At this 
stage, heat treatment methods are introduced into the alloy preparation 
process to adjust the quantity and size of the precipitated phases, 
enhance the strengthening effect of these phases, and improve the me -
chanical properties of the alloys.

by distinct peaks at (200), (220), and (311). No diffraction peaks cor -

the yield strength increased by 18 MPa. Following heat treatment, the 
alloy ’ s strength improved significantly, with the Nb-H alloy containing 
3.00 wt% Nb achieving the highest strength, reaching 675 MPa in ten -
sile strength and 442 MPa in yield strength.
It is evident that the impact energy value of the as-deposited alloys 
with two different Nb contents do not differ significantly; however, both 
the yield strength and tensile strength increase with higher Nb content. 
By comparing the data of the as-deposited alloys with those of the post 
heat-treated alloys, it can be observed that the heat-treated alloys 
increased the alloy strength but decreased the impact energy value, and 
that sufficient Nb content is the key factor for the increase of the alloy 
strength after heat-treatment. It is worth mentioning that the strength 
and ductility of the studied Nb-H alloys significantly exceeded that of 
NiCrFe-7A alloys and NiCrFe-7 alloys in the same states [28,29] .

Under TEM observation, the primary precipitates in the two depo -

from the microscopic segregation of elements. In the late stages of so -
lidification, the elements in the alloys, such as Nb, Ti, and C, become 
progressively enriched in the residual liquid phase due to liquid-phase 
segregation, with Nb and Ti exhibiting a strong affinity for C [31,32] . 
Due to the high TiC precipitation temperature, Ti will preferentially 
combine with C to form TiC cores during the solidification process of the 
alloy, and these primary TiC phases provide a favourable substrate for 
the subsequent heterogeneous nucleation of NbC by lowering the 
interfacial energy, in addition, due to the low Ti content in the alloy, 
NbC will also precipitate in the interdendritic crystals individually to act 
as a nucleation substrate for the subsequent nucleation [23 – 34] . As the 
solidification process progresses, C atoms contribute to the growth of the 
MC phase by diffusing and stacking in the Nb-Ti enriched regions. Once 
the Nb and Ti atoms are depleted by the C atoms, the remaining C atoms 
bond with the substrate until they are fully consumed [35] . Conse -
quently, a higher Nb content enhances the nucleation opportunities for 
the precipitated phase, leading to increased interactions with other 
remaining elements. This phenomenon is ultimately reflected in the 
Ni-Cr as-deposited alloys, where the area fraction of the precipitated 
phase increases with higher Nb content. The elevated Nb/C ratio in the 
residual liquid phase between the dendrites promotes the precipitation 
of additional independent NbC phases. These nascent phases compete 
with the (Ti, Nb)C phases for solute atoms [36] , further restricting the 
growth of individual MC(M = Nb, Ti). This competition results in 
smaller carbide particle sizes in Nb-H alloys.

4.2. Effect of Nb content on mechanical properties
Fig. 12 illustrates the impact on the Vickers hardness of both the as- 
deposited and post heat-treated alloys. In the multilayer multi-pass 
deposition process of nickel-based alloys, the repeated heating and 
cooling cycles induce a significant plastic hardening tendency. Conse -
quently, this results in the weld metal exhibiting elevated hardness 
values. In the vicinity of the heat-affected zone, a higher concentration 
of Fe elements from the base metal enters the molten metal, thereby 
diluting the Ni content and the elements responsible for carbide pre -
cipitation in the alloy. This phenomenon leads to a gradient distribution 
of Vickers hardness at the molten metal position, showing a gradual 
decrease from the center to the heat-affected zone, which is consistent 
with previous studies [47,48] . The addition of Nb content to the alloy, 
along with heat treatment, resulted in an increase in the average hard -
ness at the center of the weld. This improvement is primarily attributed 
to the increased quantity of precipitates in the alloy and the formation of 
the hard Laves phase, which contributes significantly to the precipita -
tion hardening effect. The heat treatment also facilitated the develop -
ment of grain boundary MC(M = Nb, Ti), which serves as a barrier to 
dislocation motion, thereby positively influencing the hardness of the 
alloy. Despite the grain coarsening observed after heat treatment, the 
average hardness values of the Nb-L and Nb-H alloys in the heat-treated 
state were higher compared to those of the as-deposited alloys. This 
indicates that precipitation strengthening is the dominant mechanism 
enhancing the hardness properties.
Two Ni-Cr alloys with different Nb content have similar impact en -
ergy in the same state, and the impact energy value decreases signifi -
cantly after heat-treated. The fracture morphology of the impact 
specimens in Nb-H deposition state and heat-treated state was observed 
under SEM, and the results are shown in Fig. 13 . Under low magnifi -
cation SEM observation, the fracture surfaces of the two alloys exhibited 
distinct characteristics of transcrystalline fracture. The dimples on the 
fracture surfaces gradually became shallower and smaller from the as- 
deposited alloys to the heat-treated state, while the degree of undula -
tion of the fractures decreased. This trend indicates a progressive 
reduction in the materials ’ ability to resist impact fracture. Both fracture 
morphologies exhibit distinct strip characteristics. Combined with the 
analysis of the orientation results from Sections 3.1 and 3.2 , it is evident 
that the fracture direction and the arrangement of the dendrites share 
certain similarities. This observation aligns with the anisotropic prop -
erties of the columnar crystals, suggesting that the alloy primarily relies 
on the separation of the dendrites to absorb energy during the impact 
process.
High magnification SEM observation shows the presence of pre -
cipitates at the bottom of the dimples, which are mainly niobium-rich 
phases as confirmed by EDS analysis. Combined with the previous 
microstructural analysis, it can be seen that the main precipitates in the 
ligamentous fossa are mainly MC (M = Nb, Ti) precipitates and a small 
amount of Laves phases, which are fragmented at the bottom of the 
dimples. Combining the area fraction and size statistics of the precipi -
tated phases from the previous section, it can be inferred that the vari -
ation in the number of precipitated phases is the primary reason for the 
differences observed in the impact absorbed energy of the alloys. Under 
impact loading, the precipitates in Ni-Cr alloys exhibit brittle and friable 
characteristics, leading to their fragmentation, which serves as a source 
of cracks. The broken precipitate particles may partially detach, forming 
small voids at the fracture sites, while some fragments remain lodged at 
the bottom of the dimples [49,50] . Subsequently, the cracks propagate, 
causing the dendritic crystals to separate and resulting in fracture. As a 
result, the heat treatment process significantly enhances the precipita -
tion of precipitates, leading to an increase in the number of crack sources 
and a reduction in the impact energy value of the alloy.
The microstructural features of the plastic deformation zone at the 
parallel ends of the specimen are illustrated in Fig. 14 . Fig. 14 (a) clearly 
demonstrates that the plastic deformation mechanism is primarily 
governed by dislocation slip within the deformed matrix. It is evident 
that as deformation increases, progressive interactions occur between 
primary and secondary dislocations within the FCC grains, resulting in 
the formation of dislocation arrays with spacings exceeding 100 nm. 
These dislocation arrays serve as effective barrier structures within the 
grains, significantly hindering the free movement of subsequent dislo -
cations while concurrently suppressing the dynamic recovery process of 
dislocations [51] . Consequently, this enhances the material ’ s 
work-hardening capacity to a certain extent. Fig. 14 (b) further illus -
trates the strong interaction between the precipitate phase and dislo -
cations. Under tensile loading, dislocations continue to slip along 
specific slip planes. When the dislocation front encounters a hard pre -
cipitate particle, its motion is significantly impeded, causing the dislo -
cation line to bend, as depicted in region I of Fig. 14 (b). As the applied 
stress increases, the dislocation cannot directly cut through the particle 
and must instead continue sliding via a bypassing mechanism. This re -
sults in the formation of closed dislocation loops around the particle in 
certain segments, as shown in region II of Fig. 14 (b), which represents 
the typical Orowan mechanism [52,53] . This process substantially ele -
vates the energy barrier for dislocation motion, necessitating that the 
dislocation expends additional energy to overcome the obstacle. 
Consequently, the yield strength of the alloy is effectively enhanced.

5. Conclusion
In this study, two types of Ni-Cr alloys with varying Nb contents were 
prepared using ERNiCr-3 wire as the raw material and the GTAW 
welding method. Combined with a heat treatment process involving 
aging at 615
◦
C for 30 h, the mechanisms by which Nb content in -
fluences the precipitation behavior and mechanical properties of both 
as-deposited and heat-treated alloys were systematically investigated. 
The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1) The microstructure of Ni-Cr alloys is characterized by γ dendrites 
that grow along the deposition direction. Elements such as Nb, Ti, 
and C are enriched in the interdendritic regions, leading to the 
formation of MC (M = Nb, Ti) precipitates. A higher Nb content 
increases the likelihood of precipitate nucleation, thereby 
enhancing the precipitate area fraction. The heat treatment pro -
cess facilitates the desolvation of alloying elements, which re -
duces the lattice distortion of the matrix and promotes the 
formation of fine MC particles.
(2) The precipitates in Ni-Cr alloys are hard and brittle, which is the 
primary factor contributing to the decrease in impact energy 
value following heat treatment; however, this characteristic also 
enables the alloy to demonstrate improved hardness values. In as- 
deposited alloys, the increase in Nb content, fine grain strength -
ening, and second phase strengthening together contribute to 
88.9 % of the yield strength increment.
(3) The strength of the alloy increased significantly after heat treat -
ment, primarily due to the strengthening effect of second phase 
precipitation, which counteracted the negative impact of grain 
coarsening. Notably, the tensile strength of the Nb-H heat-treated 
alloy with a Nb content of 3.00 % reached 675 MPa. Therefore, 
appropriately increasing the Nb content enhances the alloy ’ s 
mechanical properties post heat treatment.
