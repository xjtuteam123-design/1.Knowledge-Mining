1. Introduction
Recently, Ni-based superalloys have been
used in various applications, including
gas turbines in thermal power plants and
jet engines in aircrafts. However, machin-
ing of Ni-based superalloys is dif ﬁcult;
therefore, research and development of
high-precision electrical discharge machin-
ing have been active. [1– 3] These alloys are
manufactured by precision casting using
a lost-wax process. [4] The temperature of
an alloy decreases during metal ﬂow inside
a mold. This may cause metal solidi ﬁcation
and incomplete mold ﬁlling.[5] Therefore,
the ﬁlling behavior of molten alloy in molds
must be investigated via casting simula-
tions to reduce the number of casting
defects resulting from insuf ﬁcient ﬂow.[6]
Highly accurate casting simulations
require accurate thermophysical property
values of Ni-based superalloys, particularly
the viscosity, which is the most important
thermophysical property in thermal ﬂow
analyses. However, measuring the viscosities
Accurate data on the high-temperature thermophysical properties, which are
density, surface tension, and viscosity, are indispensable for performing high-
precision casting simulations of Ni-based superalloys. Viscosity is the most
important thermophysical property for thermo ﬂuidic analysis. However, mea-
suring the viscosity of an alloy, which is lower than that of molten glass, is
difﬁcult, and experimental viscosity data are limited. Herein, the density of Ni-
based superalloys is measured using the maximum bubble pressure (MBP)
method to determine viscosity. The viscosity is evaluated using the oscillating
crucible method. The surface tension is simultaneously measured using the MBP
method. In these results, the average density values [kg m /C0 3] of Alloy 65, Alloy
718, Alloy WA, and Alloy 720 are 7.52 /C2 103, 7.43 /C2 103, 7.82 /C2 103, and
7.52 /C2 103, respectively. The average surface tension values [N m /C0 1] of Alloy 65,
Alloy 718, Alloy WA, and Alloy 720 are 1.55, 1.54, 1.47, and 1.51, respectively.

their values are lower than those of molten glass. Recently,
Novakovic et al. summarized experimental and theoretical ther-
mophysical properties of Ni-based superalloys. [7] These data
compiled from experimental data obtained with different meth-

ing simulations with respect to parameter predictions. However,
further accumulation of error-evaluated measured data is essen-

The viscosity of molten metals can be measured using the
capillary,[8] rotating cylinder,[9] vibrating ﬁnger,[10,11] and oscillat-
ing crucible (OC) methods. [12– 16] The capillary method exhibits
optimal measurement accuracy. However, the capillaries cannot
withstand alloys at high liquidus temperatures. The rotating
cylinder method is a simple viscosity measurement method.
However, it cannot be used to measure low viscosities such as
those of molten metals as the diameter of the cylinder is similar
to that of a spindle. Therefore, the OC method, which overcomes
the aforementioned disadvantages, is used to determine the vis-
cosity of the molten metals in this study.
To accurately evaluate the viscosity of molten metals, a highly
precise value of the density must be used as an input parameter.
The density of molten alloys can be measured using various
methods, such as sessile drop, [17,18] Archimedes’,[19] and
maximum bubble pressure (MBP) methods. [20– 28] Archimedes’
method provides the most accurate measurements. However,
owing to its complex setup and expensive parts, systematic
measuring of density of molten metals, using this method, is dif-
ﬁcult. In contrast, the sessile drop method is relatively simple;
however, evaluation of the volume from photographs of molten
metals incurs considerable errors. Therefore, the MBP method
was used to measure the densities of the molten metals.
To accurately determine the viscosities of the Ni-based super-
alloys, ﬁrst, their densities were measured using the MBP
method. The viscosities of the superalloys were determined
using the OC method, [16] referring to the previously measured
density data. Surface tension was simultaneously measured
using the MBP method.
2. Experimental Section
2.1. Ni-Based Superalloy Samples
The nominal compositions of the Ni-based superalloys are listed
in Table 1 . The samples were supplied by Daido Steel Co., Ltd.
Due to open heating, only the surface of each sample was scraped
with a grinder before measuring the density using the MBP
method. Since the oxygen content of each sample, analyzed by
Daido Steel Co., Ltd., was <0.002 mass%, it was considered neg-
ligible in these measurements. The Ni-based superalloys were
primarily composed of Ni, Cr, and Co and strengthened with
Mo and W. Furthermore, the addition of Al, Ti, and Nb strength-
ened the alloy through the precipitation of the γ 0 phase. Alloy 718
contains Fe instead of Co. Although the other three alloys had
nearly identical compositions, Alloy WA had relatively high Ni
and Cr contents, Alloy 720 had a relatively high Co content,
and Alloy 65 had a high W content.
2.2. Density and Surface Tension Measurements via the MBP
Method

tensions of the superalloy samples. When the bubbles were
continuously injected from the capillary into the liquid metal,
the pressure in the capillary changed periodically. The pressure
reached its maximum value when the radius of curvature of the
bubble was equal to that of the capillary tip. Subsequently,
the bubble expanded rapidly, and the pressure decreased. This
method was comprehensively described in a previous report. [20]

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Density and Surface Tension of Molten Ni-Based
Superalloys
The results of the density and surface tension measurements are
shown in Figure 1. The uncertainties in the density and surface
tension, which were evaluated from the deviations of ﬁtting, were
<4.0% and <10.0%, respectively. The literature values for the
density and surface tension of the main components of Ni-based
superalloys are shown in Figure 1. [30– 32] The density of each
molten Ni-based superalloy was found to be reasonable.
This is because the density of each molten Ni-based superalloy
was larger than that of molten Fe and smaller than those of mol-
ten Ni and Co. However, the surface tension of each molten Ni-
based superalloy was lower than those of molten Fe and Co. We
could not ﬁnd any literature on the surface tension of molten Ni.
However, the addition of surfactant components, such as Cr, to
an alloy is known to decrease the surface tension of the molten
metal.[33] Thus, we presumed that the surface tension value of the
molten Ni-based superalloy was smaller than that of the molten
Fe and Co. Both the density and surface tension exhibit a nega-
tive temperature dependence over the investigated temperature
range. In this study, the average values of both the density and
surface tension at each temperature were used to determine vis-
cosity, because the slope obtained using the least-squares method
(See Figure 1) showed a large uncertainty. The average densities
and surface tensions are listed in Table 2 .

The relationship between the liquidus temperature and B
value for all Ni-based superalloys is shown in Figure 4 .
Hirai[36] proposed a linear relationship between the liquidus tem-
perature and B value of molten binary alloys. The B value
increased with the liquidus temperature due to the enhanced dif-
fusion rate of the atoms in the melt at high temperatures.
Furthermore, the relationship between the liquidus temperature
and B value suggested by Hirai is valid for Ni-based superalloys.
The viscosity decreased in the order: Alloy 65 > Alloy
718 > Alloy WA > Alloy 720 (Figure 2), and the surface tension
decreased in the order: Alloy 65 > Alloy WA > Alloy 720 > Alloy
718 (Figure 1b). As the uncertainty of the surface tension is large,
establishing the relationship between the viscosity and surface
tension is dif ﬁcult. The uncertainty of the surface tension needs
to be improved. However, currently, determining the relation-
ship between viscosity and surface tension is dif ﬁcult. This is
because these alloys are complex, and the effect of each element
in the system on bubble formation and, consequently, surface
tension is yet to be clari ﬁed.
4. Conclusion

sity values [kg m /C0 3] of Alloy 65, Alloy 718, Alloy WA, and Alloy
720 are 7.52 /C2 103, 7.43 /C2 103, 7.82 /C2 103, and 7.52 /C2 103,
respectively. The average surface tension values [N m /C0 1]o f
Alloy 65, Alloy 718, Alloy WA, and Alloy 720 are 1.55,
1.54, 1.47, and 1.51, respectively. 2) The uncertainties in density,
surface tension, and viscosity were <4.0%, <10.0%, and 3.0%,
respectively. 3) All molten Ni-based superalloys exhibit good lin-
