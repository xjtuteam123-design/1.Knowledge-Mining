Effect of processing parameters on the density, microstructure and strength
of pure tungsten fabricated by selective electron beam melting

ABSTRACT
Pure tungsten samples were prepared by the selective electron beam melting (SEBM) process. The effect of the
SEBM process parameters on the density, microstructure and compression strength of pure tungsten was studied.
In addition, the influence of substrate preheating temperature during SEBM was studied. A processing window
for additive manufacturing of pure tungsten by SEBM was preliminarily determined. Pure tungsten samples with
relative density of 99.5% and without obvious pores and microcracks were successfully fabricated. The as-built
pure tungsten samples showed strong columnar grain structures. Compression strength along the columnar
grains in the build direction was measured to be 1560 MPa. Fracture occurred predominantly along the co-
lumnar grain boundaries by decohesion, in addition to brittle transgranular fracture. Refinement and
strengthening of the columnar grain boundaries are expected to improve the compression strength of the SEBM-
fabricated pure tungsten.
1. Introduction
As an important refractory metal material, tungsten has many at-
tractive properties such as high melting point, high hardness, low ex-
pansion coefficient and good thermal conductivity [1].These properties
have drawn great interest for many critical applications. For example,
tungsten is believed to be one of the most capable materials that may
withstand the extraordinary conditions in a nuclear fusion reactor di-
vertor [2–4] . However, due to its very high melting point, the main
route of manufacturing tungsten and tungsten alloys is by conventional
powder metallurgy, followed by hot working (forged, rolled, drawn,
etc.), which is challenging and costly [5,6].
Additive manufacturing (AM) offers many advantages over tradi-
tional manufacturing, such as little or no waste, customization and free
form fabrication. In addition, the tensile mechanical properties of many
additively manufactured alloys can reach the level of forgings due to
their much finer microstructures as a result of the tiny molten pool and
near-rapid solidification conditions. At present, metal AM has been
widely used to manufacture stainless steels, aluminium alloys, super-
alloys, titanium alloys etc. [7–15]. With regard to refractory metals,
selective laser melting (SLM) has been used to fabricate tungsten alloys,
e.g., W-Ni-Fe, W-Ni-Cu, W-Cu alloys [16–20]. The densification process
is similar to liquid phase sintering, where SLM melts only the lower-
melting-point additives (i.e., Ni, Fe, Cu), which then act as a ‘sintering
liquid’ to combine the unmelted or slightly melted tungsten particles. It
differs from the normal fusion-based SLM process. Attempts have been
made to additively manufacture pure tungsten by SLM but there was
severe cracking and high porosity [21–25].
Similar to SLM, selective electron beam melting (SEBM) is a fusion-
based powder-bed AM technology. Its high energy (power up to 3 KW
vs. < 1 KW by SLM) is advantageous in the preparation of refractory
metals by AM, which needs much higher energy input. Moreover, since
the electron beam deflection is controlled by a magnetic field during the
SEBM process, the maximum deflection or scan speed can reach
8000 m/s, which is much faster than the scan speed of SLM. Hence,
each new layer of powder can be preheated efficiently, and each soli-
dified layer can also be reheated. Moreover, the high vacuum of the
SEBM process can allow the use of a very high substrate preheating
temperature, e.g., up to 1100 °C. Together they can enable effective
relaxation of the thermal stress developed during SEBM to avoid
cracking. SEBM has proved to be more suited to the fabrication of low-
ductility materials such as TiAl intermetallic than SLM [26–28]. It of-
fers a potential approach to the fabrication of pure tungsten, which has
the melting point of 3400 °C.
To date, no literature has focused on the fabrication of pure tung-
sten by SEBM. This paper aims to fill this knowledge gap through a
detailed experimental study of the effect of the SEBM processing
parameters on densification and microstructure of pure tungsten.

2. Material and methods
Non-spherical commercially pure tungsten powders manufactured
by sintering-crushing method with the particle size of 30–50 μm were
used for SEBM. Their morphology is shown in Fig. 1(a) and their
composition is listed in Table 1. The tap density of the powder is 11.7g/
cm3.
A SEBM system (Sailong S2, Xi'an Sailong Metal Materials Co.,Ltd.,
China) was used to fabricate tungsten samples with dimensions of
10 mm × 10 mm × 4 mm. A tungsten plate with the size of 100× 100
× 10 mm was used as the substrate during the SEBM process. The
vacuum pressure was controlled to be < 10
−2 Pa. The layer thickness
was 50 μm and the hatch distance were 50 mm. Scanning speed (v) and
beam current (I) were the main beam parameters during SEBM process.
The beam power can be calculated by P=I × U, U is the voltage which
is fixed to 60KV. Electron beam power (P), scanning speed (v), layer
thickness (t), hatch distance (h) were combined to the electron beam
energy density (E), E=(I * U)/(v * h * t). Table 2 details SEBM proces-
sing parameters used in this research, with a view to identifying their
influence on density and microstructure of pure tungsten. The scan
strategy is shown in Fig. 1b, where the scan direction is rotated by 90°
between layers. Three different substrate temperatures were used as
summarized in Table 3.
The as-solidified tungsten surfaces and the microstructure of the
SEBM-fabricated tungsten samples were investigated using optical mi-
croscopy (OM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM).
Metallographic samples were ground using silicon carbide papers up to
1000 mesh and then polished using standard metallographic techni-
ques. Selected samples were etched with a reagent (HF:HNO
3 = 3:1 by
volume). Compression testing was performed at room temperature on
an Instron machine 5982 with samples of Φ3.6 mm × 5.7 mm at a
strain rate of 0.0001/s.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Spreading and solidifying of pure tungsten molten pool
For SLM of pure tungsten metal, balling was found to be the main
factor affecting the AM process [29]. Fig. 2 shows the surface mor-
phology of tungsten samples fabricated by SEBM under a variety of scan
speed and bean current conditions. At the fixed beam current of 16 mA,
balling of melt pool was absent at the scan speed of 200 mm/s but
became noticeable with increasing scan speed to 500 mm/s (Fig. 2b)
and substantial to 1500 mm/s (Fig. 2c). Balling of liquid metal led to
unfilled gaps on the surface. In contrast, at the fixed scan speed of
200 mm/s and low scanning current of 10 mA (Fig.2d), the melt pool
failed to completely spread out. Increasing electron beam current to
16 mA resulted in better spreading of the melt pool (Fig. 2e). However,
a further increase in beam current to 30 mA reversed the course; the
continuity of the scanning line became worse (Fig. 2f). This was mainly
due to the excessive current and the poor focusing ability of the electron
beam. The spot size is about 0.2mm when the current is below 20mA.
At higher current, the spot size will increase with the increase of cur-
rent. It is about 0.3-0.4mm when the current is 30mA.

of the competition between spreading and solidification of the small
melt pool. If spreading occurs much faster than solidification, then good

The higher the temperature of the melt pool, the longer the solidi-
fication time of the molten metal. On the other hand, spreading of the
molten metal is controlled by its surface tension and viscosity. The
higher the melt temperature, the lower the surface tension and visc-

spreading of the molten metal. For this purpose, it is necessary to in-
crease the energy density (E) of the electron beam (E=(I * U)/
(v * h * t)), i.e., by increasing scanning current (I) and decreasing
scanning speed (v).

3.3. Control of cracking in pure tungsten during SEBM
In addition to balling and shrinkage holes, cracking caused by
thermal stress is typical of additively manufactured pure tungsten.
Owing to the rapid solidification and thermal gradient during AM,
thermal stress can quickly develop in the process leading to severe
cracking in less ductile materials such as tungsten. For example, SLM-
fabricated tungsten exhibited pronounced micro- and macro-cracks
[23–25]. This was attributed to the high ductile-to-brittle transition
temperature (DBTT) of tungsten, in combination with the high thermal
stress generated during SLM. An effective way to mitigate cracking is
thus to employ substrate preheating by rapid scanning with the electron
beam. Table 3 lists the preheating used in this study for control of
cracking during SEBM.

3.4. Microstructure of pure tungsten fabricated by SEBM
Fig. 7 shows the microstructures of the as-built pure tungsten
sample surfaces. There were obvious overlapping scanning lines and the
overlapping rate between the scanning lines is about 50%. The melt
pool was stirred under the impact and movement of the electron beam
during the scanning process. The agitated melt pool solidified rapidly,
resulting in different shapes of the scan lines. As can be seen fromFig. 7,
with increasing scanning speed and current, the scanning lines become
obscure, because the stirring effect of the electron beam on molten pool
became more significant and the stability of the melt pool became
worse.
Fig. 8 shows the columnar grain structures formed along the build
direction. The columnar grains are about 50–100 μm wide. This was
expected due to heat conduction occurring mainly through the sub-
strate and the subsequently solidified tungsten. The epitaxy growth of
columnar grain can be affected by stirring the melt pool arising from
the electron beam. The strong continuity of the columnar crystals
suggests that reasonable stability of the melt pool was maintained
during SEBM.
3.5. Compressive mechanical properties
Sample with the relative density of 99.5% was tested along the build
direction to measure its compressive mechanical property. The com-
pression strength was determined to be 1560 MPa from the stress-strain
curve shown in Fig. 9. Which is higher than that of the pure tungsten
fabricated by the selective laser melting (1000-1523MPa) [32,33].
SEBMed tungsten had a higher density, but the microstructure was
coarse columnar crystal. The improvement of mechanical properties is
not very significant compared with selective laser melting tungsten.
Fig. 10 shows the fracture surface morphology. Two types of failure
modes were observed. One is decohesion along the parallel coarse co-
lumnar grain boundaries (Fig. 10b) and the other is brittle transgra-
nular fracture (Fig. 10c). Hence, refinement of columnar grains and
improvement of columnar grain boundary strengthening are expected
to improve the compression strength of the SEBM-fabricated pure
tungsten material.
4. Conclusions
1. A processing window for SEBM of pure tungsten has been pre-
liminarily determined based on the study of the balling of the melt
pool. Nearly full dense pure tungsten samples (99.5%) were fabri-
cated.
2. The high energy efficiency of electron beam and the high thermal
conductivity of tungsten ensured that the substrate was kept at a
high temperature (800–950 °C) even with a low preheating tem-
perature (200 °C). This enabled effective relief of the thermal stress
developed during SEBM and therefore effective control of the
cracking issue. However, microcracks still appeared along the grain
boundaries when the sample was etched, suggesting that there was
still residual stress in local areas in the as-built microstructure.
3. SEBM-fabricated pure tungsten exhibited strong columnar grain
structures along the build direction due to epitaxial growth.
4. The compression strength of the SEBM-fabricated samples can reach
1560 MPa along the columnar direction. Fracture under compres-
sion occurred along the columnar grain boundaries by decohesion,
in addition to brittle transgranular fracture. Refinement of columnar
grains and improvement of columnar grain boundary strengthening
are expected to improve the compression strength of the SEBM-
fabricated pure tungsten material.
