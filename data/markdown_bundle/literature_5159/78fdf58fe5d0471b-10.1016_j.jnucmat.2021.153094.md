Directed energy deposition (DED) additive manufacturing process has been attractive to the fabrications 
of FeCrAl alloys due to the advantage of near net shaping of complex shaped components. In order to im- 
prove the strength, TiC particles strengthened FeCrAl alloys have been fabricated by DED using blended 
FeCrAl powder and TiC nanoparticles. The results show that the TiC particles in the DED alloys have 
submicron sizes, and polygonal or dendritic shapes. Most TiC particles are distributed along the den- 
dritic/grain boundaries. The additions of TiC particles effectively reﬁne the grain sizes, and at the same 
time prompt a columnar to equiaxed transition of grains. The grain sizes are reduced from 1377 μm to 47 
μm, 21 μm and 22 μm when the TiC particle contents in the DED alloys increase from 0 wt% to 0.7 wt%, 
2.4 wt% and 3.5 wt%, respectively. The room temperature strength of the FeCrAl alloys increase with the 
increase of TiC particle content. With 3.5 wt% TiC particle content, the FeCrAl alloy has good mechanical 
properties with yield strength of 437 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of 688 MPa, and an elongation of 19 
%. The 650 ◦C high temperature strength of the FeCrAl alloys ﬁrst decrease and then increase with the 
increase of TiC particle content. With 3.5 wt% TiC particle content, the FeCrAl alloy has excellent mechan- 
ical properties with yield strength of 208 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of 223 MPa, and an elongation of 
53 %. The present study provides an important basis for fabricating high strength particle-strengthened 
FeCrAl alloys using the DED process.

1. Introduction 
Iron-chromium-aluminum (FeCrAl) based alloys are promis- 
ing materials with excellent high temperature steam corro- 
sion/oxidation resistance and good formability, thus have poten- 
tial applications in many high temperature industrial ﬁelds, such as 
light-water reactors, chemical reactor, high temperature furnace, as 
well as gas turbines [1–9] . Recently, in order to increase the shape 
factors and reduce the overall cost, laser additive manufacturing 
(LAM) techniques, including the powder bed fusion (PBF) tech- 
nique, and the directed energy deposition (DED) technique, have 
been applied to the developments of custom-build FeCrAl based al- 
loy parts with complex 3D geometries and enhanced performance 
[10–18] . 
Facing high-temperature and complex-stress working environ- 
ments, the high-temperature strength and durability of the ma- 
terials are highly required, which, however, is not applicable for

Besides oxides, carbides, such as ZrC [7] and TiC [20] , have also

and hot-forging. The addition of ZrC nanoparticles signiﬁcantly re-

CrAl alloy shows superior thermal stability after annealing at 10 0 0

2. Experimental 
2.1. Materials 
TiC particle strengthened FeCrAl samples were fabricated from 
atomized FeCrAl powder (average particle size of 107 μm, purity 
> 99%, purchased from Jiangsu Vilory New Material Technology Co., 
LTD), and TiC nanoparticles (average particle size of 39 nm, purity 
> 99.8%, purchased from Shanghai Chaowei-nano Technology Co., 
LTD). Fig. 1 (a) and (b) show the morphologies and particle size dis- 
tributions of the FeCrAl powder and the TiC nanoparticles, respec- 
tively. The FeCrAl powder particles have spherical shapes. The TiC 
nanoparticles have polygonal shapes. The chemical composition of 
the FeCrAl powder is listed in Table 1 . 
Before blending, different contents of TiC nanoparticles (0 wt%, 
2 wt%, 4 wt% and 6 wt%) were added into the FeCrAl powder. 
The powders were then blended individually using a three dimen- 
sional mixer for 4 hours. Fig. 1 (c) shows the morphologies of the 
blended powder with 4 wt% TiC nanoparticle addition. Compared 
to the smooth surfaces and the round shapes of the raw FeCrAl 
powder particles (see Fig. 1 (a) and (d)), the FeCrAl powder parti- 
cles in the blended powder have rough surfaces and less rounded 
shapes (see Fig. 1 (c) and (e)). A coating of TiC nanoparticles on the 
surface of FeCrAl powder particle can be observed in the high mag- 
niﬁcation SEM image in Fig. 1 (e). It is noted that the less rounded 
shapes of the blended FeCrAl powder particles are not desirable 
because it reduces ﬂuidity of the particles. Optimizations of the 
blending method, such as usage of low energy ball milling, would 
help to improve the ﬂuidity [26] . The blended powders were dried 
at 120 °C for 2 hours and then cooled down to room temperature 
in a vacuum chamber before the DED process, to eliminate any 
moisture absorbed from the atmosphere. 
2.2. DED facility and printing procedure 
Ytterbium-doped ﬁber laser unit with a wavelength of 1070 
nm and a spot diameter of approximately 2 mm was used as the 
power source. 316L steel plates with 10 mm thickness were used as 
substrates. The blended powders were delivered through a coaxial 
nozzle to the laser molten pool under the protection of argon, with 
a feeding rate of 7–9 g/min. To protect from oxidation, the depo- 
sition was performed in a chamber ﬁlled with argon. The oxygen 
content and moisture content in the working chamber was main- 
tained below 10 ppm and 20 ppm, respectively. 
Four thin-walled samples were prepared with the FeCrAl pow- 
der and the blended powders consisting of different TiC contents 
by a multi-layer laser scanning method (see Fig. 2 ). After each layer 
scan, the scanning direction was reversed, and the laser head was 
raised by a certain height increment. The TiC nanoparticle addi- 
tions before the blending of powders and the process parameters 
for all samples are listed in Table 2 . Each fabricated sample has 
length of 55 mm, width of approximately 4 mm, and height of ap- 
proximately 45 mm. It is noted that the applied height increments

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Porosity and density 
Fig. 3 shows the OM images on the mechanically polished sur- 
faces of all samples. Micro-sized pores can be visualized in all sam- 
ples. Most pores have rounded shapes, indicating a gas evolved for- 
mation mechanism. The porosities of the samples were measured 
and are listed in Table 3 . All samples have high relative densi- 
ties ( ≥99.6%), indicating near fully dense. Sample FeCrAl-1 has the 
highest porosity. After TiC addition, the porosity decreases sharply. 
As the TiC addition increases, the porosity ﬁrst increases and then 
decreases. The elimination of pores after TiC addition might be 
caused by the decrease of melting point after TiC alloying, which 
results in a lower viscosity of the melt under the same laser in- 
put energy, thus favors the ﬂoating upwards of gas bubbles. Mean- 
while, as the TiC content increases, the shapes of powder particles 
become rougher and less rounded (see Fig. 1 c), which will lead to 
lower apparent densities of the blended powders. Thus more gas

3.2. Microstructure 
3.2.1. Grain sizes 
Fig. 4 (a 1 –d 1 ) show low magniﬁcation OM images taken on 
the cross sections perpendicular to the SDs of samples FeCrAl-1, 
FeCrAl-2, FeCrAl-3, and FeCrAl-4, respectively. Sample FeCrAl-1 has 
large columnar grains with epitaxial growth directions approxi- 
mately parallel to the DD. As the height increases, the widths of 
the columnar grains increase. At the middle height of the sam- 
ple, nearly only one big columnar grain can be viewed, indicat- 
ing a width up to 4 mm. The growth of the grains can be related 
to the reduced cooling rate with the increase of height, which is 
caused by the heat accumulations during the multiple-layer scan- 
ning. Sample FeCrAl-2 has much ﬁner columnar grains with epi- 
taxial growth directions also approximately parallel to the DD. The 
widths of the columnar grains vary with the sample height, which 
might be due to an undermixing of the blended powder particles. 
Samples FeCrAl-3 and FeCrAl-4 have uniform equiaxed grain struc- 
tures along the DD. The grains have ﬁne sizes, which cannot be 
distinguished at the low magniﬁcations. 
High magniﬁcation OM observations were applied to the eroded 
cross sections perpendicular to the TDs of all samples at the mid- 
dle heights, and the results are shown in Fig. 4 (a 2 –d 2 ). Based on 
at least 5 OM images, the average width of the columnar grains of 
sample FeCrAl-1 was measured as 1377 μm. For sample FeCrAl-2, 
the widths of the columnar grains are signiﬁcantly reﬁned, show- 
ing an average width of 47 μm. Further TiC addition prompts a 
columnar to equiaxed transition of the grains, showing average 
grain sizes of 21 μm and 22 μm for samples FeCrAl-3 and FeCrAl- 
4, respectively. The TiC addition reﬁnes the grain sizes of the Fe- 
CrAl alloys by nearly 66 times, which suggests that TiC nanoparti- 
cles can work as effective heterogeneous nuclei for the FeCrAl al- 
loys during the DED process. After adding 4 wt% TiC nanoparticles, 
the increase of TiC addition will not reﬁne the grain sizes further.

the columnar grains along the [100] crystal direction during solid-

equiaxed grain structures.

Compared to the nanoscale sizes and the polygonal shapes of 
the raw TiC nanoparticles (see Fig. 1 b), the submicron sizes and 
the dendritic shapes of the TiC particles in the DED samples sug- 
gest a dissolution-precipitation mechanism of the TiC formation. 
It is proposed that during the heating process, the TiC nanopar- 
ticles will ﬁrst dissolve into the Fe-based melt. When the laser 
moves away, the melt starts to cool. Some TiC particles will pre- 
cipitate from the melt and work as heterogeneous nuclei. There 
are still large amounts of solutes C and Ti preserved in the melt. 
When the Fe-based melt starts to solidify, the solutes C and Ti will 
be rejected into the solid/liquid interfaces, and precipitate as TiC 
particles near the dendritic/grain boundary regions after solidiﬁca- 
tion. During the following cooling and reheating cycles, the precip- 
itated TiC particles can grow by means of Ostwald ripening, which 
is induced by the decrease of the particle/matrix interfacial energy 
[ 27 , 30–32 ]. Finally, large TiC particles grow and small TiC particles 
dissolve, resulting in larger particle sizes of the TiC particles com- 
pared to the raw TiC powder particles.

3.3. Mechanical properties 
3.3.1. Room temperature 
Fig. 11 shows the tensile engineering stress-strain curves of all 
samples tested at room temperature. The values of yield strength, 
ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and elongations of all samples are 
listed in Table 5 . It can be concluded that the FeCrAl alloys are sig- 
niﬁcantly strengthened with the additions of TiC particles. As TiC 
particle content increases, the yield strength and the UTS both in- 
crease. With 3.5 wt% TiC particle content, the yield strength is in- 
creased by 25%, and the UTS is increased by 72%. Although there is 
a decrease of ductility after TiC addition, the ductility increases as 
the TiC particle content increases from 0.7 wt% to 3.5 wt%. Finally, 
sample FeCrAl-4 has a high elongation of 19%, which is comparable 
to that of the DED FeCrAl alloy without TiC addition. 
The strength improvements after TiC additions can be ascribed 
to the grain reﬁnement strengthening and the particle strength- 
ening. However, there is usually a trade-off between the strength 
and the ductility of materials. Nevertheless, exceptions have been 
reported for both the grain reﬁnement strengthening [22] and the 
particle strengthening [33–36] . The grain reﬁnement has the pos- 
sibility to not only improve the strength but also the ductility 
by providing more grains with slip planes and slip directions in 
the direction of maximum shear stress during deformation[22]. 
For particle-reinforced metal-matrix alloys, a mixture of different 
shaped particles, and a non-homogeneous distribution of particles 
with reinforcement rich and reinforcement poor regions can both 
lead to the increase of ductility. In this study, the combined ef- 
fects of the ﬁne grain sizes, the mixture of polygonal shapes and

3.3.2. High temperature 
Fig. 12 shows the tensile engineering stress-strain curves of 
all samples tested at high temperature of 650 ◦C. The values of 
yield strength, UTS and elongations of all samples are also listed in 
Table 5 . The yield strength and UTS of FeCrAl alloys ﬁrst decrease 
and then increase as the TiC particle content increases. With 2.4 
wt% TiC particle content, the yield strength and tensile strength 
are higher than those of the DED FeCrAl alloy without TiC addition. 
The elongations of samples are all improved after TiC additions. 
With 3.5 wt% TiC particle content, the yield strength is increased 
by 20%, the UTS is increased by 17%, and the elongation is in- 
creased by 47%. The shapes of the tensile curves also changes after 
TiC additions. A sharp stress drop after reaching the UTS is shown 
for sample FeCrAl-1. Meanwhile, the stresses of samples with TiC 
additions decrease gradually after reaching UTS. 
It is known that the strength of the grains and the strength of 
grain boundaries are affected by the temperature. Below a certain 
temperature (i.e. equicohesive temperature), the strength of the 
grain interiors is lower than that of grain boundaries. While above 
the equicohesive temperature, the strength of the grain interiors 
is higher than that of grain boundaries [37] . For sample FeCrAl-1, 
the grain size is rather coarse. For the miniature tensile specimens 
adapted in this study, limited softer grain boundaries will partic- 
ipate in the high temperature deformation process. The deforma- 
tion is thus dominated in the harder grain interiors, resulting in 
the high strength. TiC particles along grain boundaries can hinder

ors can also be strengthened by the TiC particles. However, since 
the grain sizes are signiﬁcantly reﬁned, which means more grain 
boundaries will participate in the deformation. For sample FeCrAl- 
2, the strength improvements due to the 0.7 wt% TiC particles are 
not enough to compensate the strength decrease of grain bound- 
aries at the high temperature, thus resulting in the lower strength 
than that of sample FeCrAl-1. As the TiC particle contents increase 
to 2.4 wt% and 3.5 wt%, the grain boundaries and grain interi- 
ors are further strengthened, which ﬁnally lead to the increases of 
yield strength and UTS of samples FeCrAl-3 and FeCrAl-4. 
The room temperature and high temperature yield strength and 
UTS of the DED FeCrAl alloys with and without TiC additions are 
compared with other FeCrAl alloys prepared by various methods, 
such as rolling [9] , extrusion [9] , welding [20] , and hot forging af- 
ter spark plasma sintering [7] , as shown in Fig. 13 . It can be seen 
that the TiC strengthened DED FeCrAl alloys can have comparable 
strength with the rolled and extruded FeCrAl alloys at both room 
temperature and high temperature. With the advantage of near net 
shaping of complex shaped components, the preparation of high 
strength FeCrAl alloys by DED in this study provides an important 
basis for the applications of DED FeCrAl alloys in the extreme en- 
vironments. 
4. Conclusion 
TiC particles strengthened FeCrAl alloys have been fabricated by 
DED using blended FeCrAl powder and TiC nanoparticles. Effects 
of TiC content on the microstructure and mechanical properties of 
FeCrAl alloys have been investigated and the following conclusions 
can be drawn: 
(1) Near fully dense ( ≥99.6%) FeCrAl alloys with different TiC par- 
ticle contents have been successfully prepared. Due to the loss 
of TiC nanoparticles during the blending and delivering pro- 
cesses, the TiC particle contents in the DED FeCrAl alloys are 
lower than what were added to the raw FeCrAl powders before 
blending. 
(2) TiC particles in the DED FeCrAl alloys have submicron sizes, 
and polygonal or dendritic shapes, which suggest a dissolution- 
precipitation mechanism of TiC formation. Most TiC particles 
are distributed along the dendritic/grain boundaries. 
(3) The TiC additions reﬁne the grain sizes of FeCrAl alloys signiﬁ- 
cantly, showing a decrease of average grain size from 1377 mm 
to 47 mm, 21 μm and 22 μm when the TiC particle contents 
in the DED samples increase from 0 wt% to 0.7 wt%, 2.4 wt% 
and 3.5 wt%, respectively. For the sample containing 2.4 wt% 
TiC particles, the grain structures start to transit from columnar 
grains to equiaxed grains. 
(4) The room temperature strength of the FeCrAl alloys increase 
with the increase of TiC particle content. With 3.5 wt% TiC par- 
ticle content, the FeCrAl alloy has good mechanical properties 
with yield strength of 437 MPa, ultimate tensile strength of 688 
MPa, and an elongation of 19 %. 
(5) The 650 ◦C high temperature strength of the FeCrAl alloys ﬁrst 
decrease and then increase with the increase of TiC particle 
content. With 3.5 wt% TiC particle content, the FeCrAl alloy has 
excellent mechanical properties with yield strength of 208 MPa, 
ultimate tensile strength of 223 MPa, and an elongation of 53 
%.
