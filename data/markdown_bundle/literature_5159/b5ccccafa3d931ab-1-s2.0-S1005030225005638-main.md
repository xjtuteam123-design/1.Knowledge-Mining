Many cases investigated so far have offered solutions to overcome high-temperature wear issue of alloys, 
but achieving both satisfactory mechanical and high-temperature tribological properties remains a chal- 
lenge. In the present work, we fabricate a simply model Ni20Cr-based alloys with well-balanced mechani- 
cal and high-temperature tribological properties by using mechanical alloying and spark plasma sintering. 
We ﬁnd that the alloy with 30 wt.% Co and 3 wt.% Al (NA30C) shows a room temperature compressive 
strength of up to 2532 MPa. The wear rate of NA30C at 600 °C and 800 °C is two orders of magnitude 
and seven times lower, respectively, than that of the alloy lacking Co (NA). Such exceptional performance 
is mainly attributed to the synergy of heterostructure, characterized by multi-level grain size, and tribo- 
oxidation of Co and Al with optimum content. The addition of 30 wt.% Co appropriately upgrades grain 
size differentiation and promotes precipitates dispersion, which saliently improves strength and plasticity 
of the alloy. During sliding, the formation of a protective double-layered tribo-oxide layer, consisting of a 
Co-rich nanocrystalline surface layer and an α-Al2 O3 inner one, supplies suﬃcient lubricity, high oxida- 
tion resistance, and ideal interfacial bonding. By elucidating the mechanisms above, this study provides 
a new thinking for designing high-temperature self-lubricating alloys through synchronous regulation of 
microstructure and tribo-oxidation behavior.

1. Introduction 
High-temperature friction and wear have always been a crucial 
issue for core mechanical components (aeronautical bearings, tur- 
bine blades, critical parts of locomotive, etc.) due to extreme oper- 
ating environments, severely affecting the durability and security 
of techniques [ 1 ]. As self-lubricating coatings always suffer from 
the drawbacks of weak adhesion and CTE (thermal expansion co- 
eﬃcient) mismatch [ 2 , 3 ], as well as the liquid lubricants (oil and 
grease) decompose rapidly and degrade when exposed to elevated 
temperatures [ 4 ], many investigations were conducted to design 
and produce novel materials that can achieve and persist optimal 
properties including reliable anti-wear capacity and good mechan- 
ical performance. However, problems and attempts coexist. By in- 
corporating traditional solid lubricants such as graphite, hexago- 
nal boron nitride, transition metal disulphides, ﬂuorides and soft 
metals into alloys, the composites have been empirically proven

to lubricate effectively over a limited temperature range, but the 
strength and toughness are signiﬁcantly reduced [ 5 , 6 ]. It is recog- 
nized that producing an alloy with satisfactory balance between 
mechanical and tribological properties is a crucial but still unre- 
solved issue. 
At elevated temperatures, metal substrates are prone to oxida- 
tion. Previous studies have found out that some metal oxides pos- 
sess lubricity [ 2 , 7 ]. In this regard, controlling oxidation of the alloy 
and generating the desired oxides, rather than adding lubricants, 
may prove an effective approach to achieving high-temperature 
self-lubrication while avoiding a considerable reduction in strength 
and plasticity. The oxide debris can be compacted during friction 
to form a dense oxide layer that effectively reduces the friction co- 
eﬃcient and wear rate [ 8–10 ]. Among the main structural alloy- 
ing elements, Co has been veriﬁed to be advantageous for form- 
ing such a tribo-oxide layer and reducing wear in many investiga- 
tions. Tang et al. employed ultrasonic shot peening to fabricate a 
Co coating on the 24CrNiMo steel whose friction coeﬃcients and 
wear rates were lowered at 600 °C, attributing to the rise in dis- 
location density and the generation of a dense Co-enriched tribo-

of a Co-based alloy (HS25) when subjected to fretting against alu- 
mina at temperatures above 400 °C, which led to a stabilized fric- 
tion coeﬃcient of 0.3 and a quasi no-wear regime [ 13 ]. To con- 
clude, controlling oxidation of metals, especially of Co, to accom- 
plish high-temperature self-lubrication is a rational and feasible 
option. 
Even so, maintain the high plasticity by avoiding the use of 
lubricants is still insuﬃcient to satisfactorily adapt to the high- 
load working condition. Heterogeneous structure is expected to 
achieve the well-balanced strength and plasticity without notably 
changing the chemical composition and the tribo-oxidation prod- 
ucts. In an ODS Fe-10Cr-6.1Al-0.3Zr-0.1Y alloy, a bimodal grain 
size distribution was attained via internal oxidation and sinter- 
ing of oxide-particles-decorated powder particles, which delivered 
a desirable mechanical performance with yield strength (YS) of 
598 MPa, ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 734 MPa, and frac- 
ture elongation of 25.1 %, respectively [ 14 ]. He et al. developed 
the Ni2 CoCrFeTi0.24 Al0.2 high entropy alloy (HEA) with a grain- 
scale heterogeneous structure through single-step rolling and an- 
nealing, which showcased excellent strength-ductility combination. 
High YS of ∼1.3 GPa and tensile elongation of ∼20 % were realized 
[ 15 ]. Similar remarkable outcomes were also obtained from reports 
of hierarchically structured powder metallurgy stainless steels, 
heterogeneous lamella (HL) Fe35 Ni35 Cr25 Mo5 alloy, and hetero- 
structured Co-Cr-Ni medium-entropy alloy, etc. [ 16–18 ]. Recently, 
the investigation of orientation dependent-frictional anisotropy in 
Mg by Li et al. provides an important reference for controlling 
tribological properties via microstructure design [ 19 ]. Neverthe- 
less, there is a notable absence of studies investigating the in- 
ﬂuence of heterogeneous structure on high-temperature oxidation 
and tribo-oxidation. Referring to the study of Xu et al., adjust- 
ing the content of Co and Cr to reﬁne grains and regulate mi- 
crostructures in FeNiCoCr0.5 HEA contributed to the considerable 
improvement in room-temperature mechanical strength and the 
formation of a lubricating layer at 400 °C [ 20 ]. More informa- 
tively, in the Co30 Cr28 Ni27 Al7 Cu6 /Ti6 B2 HEAs, Geng et al . were able 
to realize graded grains together with compositional ﬂuctuation, 
which allowed for incredible mechanical and anti-wear perfor- 
mance throughout a wide temperature range up to 800 °C [ 21 ]. 
Hence, it is reasonable to assume that the combination of compo- 
sition control and microstructure design can achieve optimization 
of mechanical-tribological synergy. 
Building heterostructure typically relies on complex rolling and 
annealing process [ 15 , 18 ]. As a feasible supplement, spark plasma 
sintering (SPS) offers clear advantages in terms of simplicity and 
speed. In the ﬁrst place, severe plastic deformation is applied to 
micron-sized powders with coarse grains by mechanical alloying 
(MA), in order to create an ultraﬁne-grained outer layer particle 
structure; then SPS is adapted to rapidly consolidate powders at 
a relatively low temperature while being heated through a pulsed 
direct current, which eﬃcaciously suppresses grain growth during 
sintering and maintains the heterogeneous structure of the pow- 
der. Herein, simple model Ni20Cr-based alloys with varying Co 
content and 3 wt.% of Al were prepared by using mechanical alloy- 
ing and SPS. The objective of Co addition was to adjust microstruc- 
ture, enhance mechanical strength, meanwhile facilitate the forma- 
tion of a tribo-oxide layer during high-temperature friction, so as 
to optimize the tribological properties. Al was added to improve 
oxidation and tribo-oxidation resistance, thereby ensuring the sta- 
bility of tribo-oxide layer. 
2. Experiment 
2.1. Materials 
The Ni20Cr3Al- x Co powder was produced by mechanical al- 
loying. Ni20Cr powder (30–50 μm, Tijo Co., Ltd, China), Al pow- 
der (2 μm, Tijo Co., Ltd, China), and Co powder (2 μm, Tijo Co., 
Ltd, China) were mixed in a planetary ball mill (XQM-4 L, Chang- 
sha Ten can Powder, China). 316 stainless steel vials and balls 
were utilized as the container and grinding media respectively. The 
ball-to-powder mass ratio was 10:1. Before the mechanical alloy- 
ing process, the vials with mixed powder inside are evacuated to 
−0.1 MPa and then ﬁlled with argon gas using a glove box. The 
total ball milling duration was 27 h, with an intermittent opera- 
tion cycle of 30 min followed by a 15 min pause, and the rota- 
tional speed was 300 rpm. To reduce cold welding, 0.5 ml of n- 
heptane per 100 g of powder was added. The mixtures and the 
corresponding alloys with different contents of Co (0, 10, 30 and 
50 wt.%) were referred to as NA, NA10C, NA30C and NA50C, re- 
spectively. Thereafter, the mixtures were consolidated by cold com- 
paction and then sintered by SPS (HPD250-C, FCT System GmbH, 
Germany) in vacuum (0.1 Pa) at 1100 °C under pressure of 40 MPa 
for 15 min. The sintered alloys were then cut into blocks of dif- 
ferent sizes, ground and mechanically polished, cleaned by ethanol 
and blown dry with a blower for testing and characterization. 
2.2. Characterization 
Phase composition was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, 
Model SmartLab, Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan) with Cu K α radiation source 
operating at 30 kV, 40 mA. Microstructure and composition were 
examined by ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM, 
JSM-7001F, JEOL, Japan) equipped with energy dispersive X-ray 
spectroscopy (EDS, X-Max, Oxford, U.K.) and high-resolution trans- 
mission electron microscopy (HRTEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL, Japan) in 
conjunction with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. A speciﬁc 
focused ion beam (FIB; FEI Helios NanoLab 600i, USA) was em- 
ployed to prepare the TEM samples. Grain sizes were evaluated us- 
ing SEM (JSM-7001 F, JEOL, Japan) with an EBSD acquisition camera 
and Channel 5 software. 
Hardness was measured by Vickers indenter (Buehler Micromet 
5114, Mitutoyo Co., Japan) with a load of 200 g and a dwell time 
of 10 s. Each sample underwent the test ﬁve times, and the av- 
erage hardness was selected. A universal testing machine (WDW- 
100, Changchun Test Equipment Co. Ltd., China) and an electro- 
hydraulic-servo fatigue testing machine (HDT105B, WANCE, China) 
were used to conduct compression test at room-temperature 
and elevated temperature, respectively. The loading speed was 
0.1 mm/min and the scale of samples was /Phi16 mm × 9 mm. For 
each group, the test was performed at least three times and the 
average compressive strength and strain were taken. 
High-temperature wear and friction were tested for the sintered 
blocks (32 mm × 5 mm × 4 mm) by using a MFT-50 0 0 tribometer 
(Rtec Corporation, America) in ball-on-disc mode. A Si3 N4 ceramic 
ball with a diameter of 9.525 mm was selected as the counter- 
part. The applied load was 10 N, the sliding speed was 24 mm/s, 
and the sliding time was 30 min. In addition to the block sam- 
ples, NA and NA30C were processed into bearing bushes (inside 
diameter 15 mm, outside diameter 18 mm, width 3 mm). The high- 
temperature wear and friction of bushes were also tested by using 
a SDZ-300/1 K tribometer (NEU, China), of which the schematic di- 
agram is shown in Fig. 1 . The shaft was made of stainless steel. The 
applied load was 200 N, the reciprocating rotation frequency of the 
shaft was 20 times per minute and the rotation angle was ± 30 °, 
the testing time was 30 min. To guarantee reproducibility, friction 
tests were conducted at least three times. After tests, the wear vol-

2.3. Simulation 
The 2D ﬁnite element (FE) model was implemented by using 
ABAQUS commercial software to simulate stress distribution during 
compressive loading. Elastic modulus of NiCr, Cr7 C3 and Al2 O3 are 
185, 302.3 and 375 GPa, and their Poisson’s ratios are 0.30, 0.336 
and 0.22, respectively [ 22 , 23 ]. The model was constructed on the 
basis of the SEM microstructure of NA30C, with a quadrangle ele- 
ment employed to mesh the model. The bottom of the model was 
ﬁxed and a constant displacement was uniformly applied to the 
upper surface. 
3. Results 
3.1. Phase constituents and microstructures 
Fig. 2 (a–d) shows microstructures of NA, NA10C, NA30C and 
NA50C. The four alloys were sintered densely without visible pores 
and featured with abundant ﬁne precipitates. Combining with TEM 
and XRD results ( Fig. 2 (e–g)), the gray irregular precipitates were 
identiﬁed as Cr7 C3 , and the submicron to nanoscale black parti- 
cles were identiﬁed as κ-Al2 O3 . These phases formed as a result 
of n-hexane and O attaching to the surface of metal particles and 
reacting with Cr and Al respectively during ball milling. Most of 
them were distributed along the particle boundaries and became 
ﬁner as the high-Co alloy grains were reﬁned, taking on a discon- 
tinuous network shape, whereas in NA50C a chain-like distribution 
was found. The XRD patterns in Fig. 2 (e) display that all the four 
alloys had strong γ-Ni (matrix) diffraction peaks, as well as peaks 
of Cr7 C3 and κ-Al2 O3 . No peaks of Co or Co-containing second 
phase were detected, indicating that Co completely dissolved into 
the matrix. A more micrographic image of NA30C was acquired by 
TEM, as shown in Fig. 2 (f). κ-Al2 O3 precipitated both along grain 
boundaries and within grains. HR-TEM images in Fig. 2 (g) reveal 
the coherent interface formed between the κ-Al2 O3 particle and 
the alloy matrix. The orientation relationships of the interface were 
determined as: [ ¯1 11 ] κ-Al2 O3 // [ ¯1 ¯1 0 ] γ-Ni, ( ¯2 ¯2 0 ) κ-Al2 O3 // ( ¯1 ¯1 1 ) 
γ-Ni, and ( 03¯3 ) κ-Al2 O3 // (111) γ-Ni. 
To have a clearer view of microstructure, EBSD results of the 
four alloys are presented in Fig. 3 . According to the reverse pole 
diagrams ( Fig. 3 (a–d)), the addition of Co dramatically promoted 
grain reﬁnement as evidenced by decreasing the average diame- 
ter of Ni-matrix from 5.82 μm for NA to 383 nm for NA30C and 
304 nm for NA50C ( Fig. 3 (i–l)). Both the NA30C and the NA50C 
exhibited a heterogeneous structure, characterized by the coexis- 
tence of coarse and ﬁne grains. Nonetheless, the area fractions of 
coarse and ﬁne grains appeared a distinct polarization. For NA30C, 
the area fraction of grains with diameter < 2 μm was 65 %, while 
that with diameter greater than 6 μm was only 1.4 %. This phe- 
nomenon manifested more prominently in NA50C. Therefore, the 
grain size distribution of the heterostructure in this work showed 
a predominantly unimodal peak, with ﬁne grains accounting for 
the majority of the microstructure, rather than bimodal peak, even 
though the EBSD maps demonstrated obvious coexistence of coarse 
and ﬁne grains. Fig. 3 (o) is a locally enlarged image of the het- 
erogeneous structure in NA30C. The coarse grains were the center 
part of Ni20Cr particles that were not fully reﬁned during MA. Ow- 
ing to the intrinsically low stacking fault energy of the FCC struc- 
ture, deformation and/or annealing twins were preserved after MA 
and SPS processes. Phase maps in Fig. 3 (e–h) illustrate the distri- 
bution of κ-Al2 O3 and Cr7 C3 . They mainly precipitated along grain 
boundaries, and also appeared within small grains. Both phases de- 
creased in size and increased in amount with Co content increase 
and alloy grain reﬁnement, as shown in Fig. 3 (m, n). 
3.2. Mechanical properties 
Fig. 4 (a, b) shows mechanical properties of the four alloys 
at room temperature. After adding 30 wt.% Co, the hardness, 
strength and compressive strain were substantially increased, with 
a 25.58 % rise in hardness and a 51.78 % rise in YS over NA. 
Its compressive strength reached 2532 MPa, and the strain-to- 
fracture exceeded 30 %. When Co content reached 50 wt.%, the 
hardness and yield strength further increased, but the compres- 
sive strength and strain decreased. Overall, the alloy NA30C ex- 
hibited ideal strength and plasticity synergy. Although cracking oc- 
curred during the compression test, the NA30C did not fail pre- 
maturely until it was ﬂattened ( Fig. 4 (c)). NA30C surpassed many 
other reported Ni-based alloys in regard to strength-plasticity bal-

4. Discussion 
4.1. Microstructures 
Both mechanical alloying and spark plasma sintering play vi- 
tal roles in the formation of heterogeneous microstructure. Dur- 
ing mechanical alloying, ﬁne Co powder and Al powder with low 
apparent density and hardness wrap around Ni20Cr particles. Re- 
search has demonstrated that milling with harder metal powder 
contributes to an increase in the apparent rate constant of mi- 
crostructural reﬁnement and a corresponding reduction in the ﬁ- 
nal average grain size for soft metal, while the hard metal in 
the mixture exhibits the opposite behavior [ 42 ]. Accordingly, the 
Co particles surrounding the Ni20Cr particles are more likely to 
be reﬁned. Besides, the stacking faults energy (SFE) is reduced in

During work hardening stage, back-stress strengthening is in- 
tensiﬁed by inhomogeneous deformation of the heterostructure. 
Coarse grains undergo plastic deformation but ﬁne grains still 
maintain elasticity. Incompatible deformation gives rise to strain 
gradients, the synergistic effect of back stress in coarse grains 
and forward stress in ﬁne grains through strain gradients across 
heterointerfaces consequently elevating work-hardening capacity 
and enhancing compressive strength. The incorporation of Co also 
serves as a key factor in the improvement of work hardening rate, 
as the SFE of the alloy is reduced. 
Generally, the high-temperature deformation mechanism of al- 
loys is governed by the coordinated control of grain boundary slid- 
ing and dislocation climb. NA30C that features a reﬁned grain mi- 
crostructure, has a propensity to soften at elevated temperatures 
due to the synergistic effects of grain boundary sliding, accelerated 
diffusion creep, and thermally activated optimization of dislocation 
dynamics. Nevertheless, the abundant Cr7 C3 and κ-Al2 O3 particles 
distributing along grain boundaries, which act as microstructural 
stabilizers, can effectively enhance grain boundary strength by re- 
stricting grain boundary migration while concurrently improving 
interfacial cohesion through localized stress redistribution. As a 
consequence, NA30C demonstrates signiﬁcantly enhanced plastic- 
ity while retaining enough strength during high-temperature com- 
pression.

Additionally, tribological performance of the four alloys mostly 
depends on the formation of a dense and intact tribo-oxide layer 
during high-temperature friction, which is in turn controlled by 
the cation diffusion kinetics and tribo-sintering of wear debris 
[ 9 , 55 ]. At 600 °C, the diffusion and oxidation rate of alloying el- 
ements are relatively low, a small amount of oxides that composed 
of NiO, Cr2 O3 and spinel are produced on the worn surface. The 
formation of tribo-oxide layer is precluded because of the high 
melting point of these oxides, in conjunction with their low gener- 
ation rate relative to their removal rate [ 36 ]. The discontinuity of 
the tribo-oxide layer renders it susceptible to peeling and then be- 
ing crushed and forming wear debris under adhesion and abrasive 
ploughing, causing a severe abrasive wear to the substrate and an 
exacerbation of material loss [ 56 ]. By contrast, at an elevated tem- 
perature of 800 °C, the diffusion and oxidation rate of alloying ele- 
ments are accelerated and the generation rate of tribo-oxides sur- 
passes the removal rate on the sliding surface. Thereafter, oxides 
that have been compacted by the counterpart can then be sintered 
by friction heat to form a relatively smooth and dense tribo-oxide 
layer on the worn surface [ 57 ]. The tribo-oxide layer can reduce 
the shear stress, enhance the strength of worn surface, and pre- 
vent direct contact between the substrate and counterpart, which 
compensates for the adverse impact of thermal softening and re- 
sults in a reduction in wear rate and COF [ 58 , 59 ]. Of all the pri- 
mary elements of alloys in this work, Co has the highest auto- 
diffusion coeﬃcient, which aids in the welding of grains and ini- 
tiating the sintering process of oxide debris [ 60 ]. In terms of NA, 
the oxides produced by friction are composed of NiO, Cr2 O3 and 
NiCr2 O4 with poor sintering property ( Fig. 8 (a, b)). Cr2 O3 is dis- 
tinguished by its low ionic potential ( ϕ= 4.8), high phase transi- 
tion stress and high shear modulus [ 8 ], so it can be deduced that 
Cr2 O3 debris is the main cause of abrasive wear. Cobalt oxides are 
detected on the wear scars of NA10C ( Fig. 8 (a, b)), but the im- 
provement in the sinterability of oxide debris is limited, as the 
primary composition remains Cr2 O3 and spinel with high melting 
point, which are similar to those for NA. The undesirable tribo- 
oxide layer with defects and bad adhesion can be easily removed 
during friction. The peeled oxide ﬂakes are thereafter crushed into 
small debris, which exacerbates the abrasive wear. For NA30C, the 
increased grain boundaries resulting from heterogeneous structure 
serve as "short-circuit" diffusion channels that contribute greatly 
to the adequate supply of Co and Co oxides. The Raman spectra in 
Fig. 8 and TEM results in Fig. 9 prove the existence of Co3 O4 in the 
tribo-oxide layer. The low melting point of Co3 O4 (895 °C) signiﬁes 
good sinterability, and the good lubricity of Co3 O4 has been widely 
reported [ 61 , 62 ]. Therefore, the highest density and coverage of 
the Co3 O4 -rich tribo-oxide layer largely determines the lowest fric- 
tion coeﬃcient and wear rate of NA30C. Besides, the presence of a 
continuous alumina inner layer has been demonstrated to provide 
remarkable resistance to softening and the capacity to minimize 
delamination during sliding wear at temperatures above 600 °C 
[ 63 , 64 ], which is also conducive to the anti-wear performance of 
NA30C. NA50C has the highest Co content and the most signiﬁcant 
grain reﬁnement. Oxides can quickly generate, accumulate and be 
sintered as they do on NA30C. However, cracking, fracturing and 
ﬂaky peeling occur with the rapid thickening of tribo-oxide layer. 
This repeated generation, thickening, peeling and crushing of ox- 
ides represents that the alloy surface is constantly exposed, oxi- 
dized, and removed, causing severe oxidative wear and huge wear 
rate of NA50C. 
4.4. Oxidation-wear competition mechanism 
It has been observed that, a protective double-layered tribo- 
oxide layer forms on the worn surface of NA30C, the underly- 
ing causes of which can be elucidated in Fig. 11 . Firstly, the ad- 
dition of an appropriate amount of Co enables NA30C to gener- 
ate plentiful Co3 O4 on the worn surface during friction. The ox- 
ides are rapidly sintered to form a dense Co-rich tribo-oxide layer. 
NA50C could have formed a dense tribo-oxide layer as well. Yet, 
the excessive Co content coupled with its high diffusivity promotes 
accelerated oxidation and tribo-oxidation kinetics, which intensi- 
ﬁes growth stress within the tribo-oxide layer beyond its criti- 
cal fracture threshold, eventually initiating cracking. Secondly, fol- 
lowing the formation of a dense Co-rich tribo-oxide layer on the 
worn surface of NA30C, there is a salient reduction in the oxy- 
gen partial pressure beneath the tribo-oxide layer and the oxy- 
gen solubility ( NO ) in the alloy ( Fig. 11 ). Concurrently, the dif- 
fusivity of aluminum ( DAl ) is increased because of grain reﬁne- 
ment. In which case, the critical Al content ( NAl

5. Conclusions 
In this study, simply model Ni20Cr-based alloys with varying Co 
content and 3 wt.% Al were prepared. Their microstructure, me- 
chanical properties and tribo-oxidation behavior were investigated. 
Here are the primary conclusions: 
(1) After mechanical alloying and fast sintering by SPS, the 
Ni20Cr-based alloy containing 30 wt.% Co and 3 wt.% Al 
(NA30C) with a heterogeneous microstructure was prepared. 
The heterogenous microstructure was characterized by a 
multi-level grain size distribution, which resulted in high 
compressive strength of 2532 MPa and strain-to-fracture of 
over 30 % at room temperature. The high-temperature com- 
pressive performance of NA30C exhibited a superior combi- 
nation of strength and plasticity in comparison to the alloy 
without Co addition (NA). 
(2) NA30C exhibited low friction coeﬃcient and wear rate at 
both 600 and 800 °C, owing to oxidation regulation and 
the formation of a stable tribo-oxide layer. At 600 °C, the 
NA30C alloy demonstrated superior tribological performance 
with a COF of 0.32 and a wear rate of 7.9 × 10−6 mm3 
N−1 m−1 , which represented a two-order-of-magnitude re- 
duction compared to NA. This superiority became more pro- 
nounced at 800 °C, where NA30C maintained a COF of 0.30 
and a wear rate of 2.3 × 10−6 mm3 N−1 m−1 which was 
seven times lower than that of NA. As for bushes, NA30C 
also showed a much better tribological performance than 
NA. 
(3) With optimum Co and Al content, a dense tribo-oxide layer 
consisting of a Co-rich nanocrystalline surface layer and an 
α-Al2 O3 inner one formed on worn surface of NA30C. The 
Co-rich tribo-oxide surface layer provided suﬃcient lubric- 
ity. The α-Al2 O3 inner one hindered outward diffusion of 
Co, Ni and other alloying elements, which ensured the ther- 
mal stability of the tribo-oxide layer without generating ex- 
cessive oxidative growth stress. Furthermore, it served as 
an intermediate layer that increased bonding strength be- 
tween the surface tribo-oxide layer and the alloy matrix of 
NA30C. The double-layered structure resulted in prolonged 
high-temperature mechanical stability of tribo-oxide layer, 
and ultimately, low friction coeﬃcient, reduced wear rate of 
NA30C.
