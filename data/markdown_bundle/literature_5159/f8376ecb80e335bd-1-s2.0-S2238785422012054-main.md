abstract
Cerium (Ce) plays an important role in enhancing the properties of Ni and Co-based alloys
at high temperatures. However, its inﬂuence on the crystallization, microstructure, and
properties of Co superalloys is not fully known. This work investigated the effect of Ce-
doping on the as-cast microstructure of g
0-formed Co eAleW alloys. Co e9Ale9W-xCe al-
loys (x ¼ 0, 0.1, and 0.5) were synthesized and their microstructures were characterized by
scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), electron
backscatter diffraction (EBSD), and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Al occupied interdendritic
spaces and formed Th
2Zn17-type structures. Introduction of Ce to Co eAl based alloys
formed Ce-rich interdendritic precipitates, which do not dissolve upon heat treatment.

1. Introduction
The g0-strengthened Co-based superalloys have become
promising heat-resistant metallic materials [ 1,2]. One impor-
tant way in which g/g0 cobalt alloys have evolved involves
improved oxidation resistance, currently an alloy weak point.
In contrast to highly Cr-alloyed cobalt superalloys [ 3], novel
alloys based on cobalt do not form outer protective shells.
Upon high-temperature oxidation, the alloys form multilay-
ered oxide shells. The outermost porous layer formed on these
alloys comprises cobalt oxides (CoO, Co
3O4), whereas an inner
oxide layer is composed of ﬁnely dispersed oxide phases rich
in Co, Al, and W. After oxidation at 900
/C14 C, the inner oxide
zone is a conglomerate of CoO, CoAl 2O4, CoWO4, and Al2O3 [4].
Although the layer does not fully protect a substrate against
oxidation, oxidation protection of this layer depends on the
amount of W in the alloy [ 5]. Co eAleW-based alloys form an
inner alumina layer upon oxidation [ 6]. Nevertheless, the
Al
2O3 layer is discontinuous in the base alloy and does not
satisfactorily hamper oxidation. Different alloying elements
were used to ensure improved oxidation resistance. Adding
certain elements may enhance the formation of an inner
alumina layer and hamper diffusion at the grain boundaries
due to precipitation there.
Previous reports suggested chromium decreased the
oxidation rates of Co eAleW alloys [ 6e8]. However, due to g
0-
stability, Cr levels were limited and may not be introduced to
the alloys at levels comparable to popular chromia-forming
conventional cobalt superalloys, e.g., Co e29Cre6Mo (wt. %)
or MAR-M-509. A beneﬁcial effect on alloy oxidation behaviors
based on Co eAleW systems may stem from B-doping [ 6,7,9].
Silicon is another element that may improve alloy oxidation
properties [ 10]. However, Si-containing precipitates at the
grain boundary often decrease the mechanical properties of
the alloy.
The addition of oxygen-reactive elements such as rare earth
elements (REE) may increase the oxidation behavior of chromia
and alumina-forming alloys. Several authors described those
mechanisms [ 11e13]. Even a low percentage of large RE ions
considerably altered cation diffusion at the grain boundaries.
Both REE and reactive elements (RE) have the greatest inﬂuence
on high-temperature cyclic oxidation by the formation of oxide
penetration into the substrate, which is called oxide“keying” or
“pegging” [14]. Voids at the alloyeoxide interface may curb the
adhesion of a substrate shell, which promotes scale buckling
and spallation by crack propagation through the shell. The REE
atoms, intermetallic particles, or oxide particles act as vacancy
sinks to prevent void formation. A positive inﬂuence of RE was
reported for both chromia and alumina-forming [ 15,16] Ni-
based superalloys.
Because of the mechanical properties, doping Co eAleMo-
eTaeB with Ce may enhance creep properties [9], furthermore,
isothermal oxidation of these alloys may improve with the
introduction of Ce [17]. It also had a beneﬁcial inﬂuence on the
oxidation behavior of Ni-superalloys [18e20] and was effective
as a co-dopant with B [ 17]. Optimal Ce levels were ~0.1 wt.%.
Multicomponent Ni-based superalloys doped with Ce form
Ni
5Ce phases in the interdendritic regions during crystalliza-
tion [21].
Although Ce can improve the oxidation properties of Ni-
based alloys, the data regarding the effects of Ce on the prop-
erties and microstructures of Co-based superalloys is limited.
Except for Zhong et al. [17], other researchers considered Ce as
an efﬁcient dopant for g
0-strengthened Co-based superalloys.
Those authors examined doping of Co e9Ale10W and
Coe9Ale10We4Cr (at.%) alloys with 0.02% Ce in view of
oxidation resistance enhancement [22,23]. In both studies, Ce-
containing alloys exhibited improved resistance to isothermal
oxidation. No traces of Ce were found in the microstructure or
the oxide shell. In the other study, increased Ce was detected at
grain boundaries [ 17]. However, phases with and without Ce
were recognized within the oxide scale.
The limited data on the REE effects on microstructures and
properties of Co-based superalloys was the motivation for this
study. There are no investigations that show the inﬂuence of
REE on the microstructure and thermal behavior of geg
0 Co-
based alloys. Although some information concerning the
beneﬁts of Ce-doped Co-based superalloys is available, there
was no data on microstructure and phase formation. A
detailed microscopic analysis sought to broaden the effects of
reactive elements effect on Co-based superalloys. In this
work, we investigated as-cast and as-aged microstructures of
CoeAleWexCe alloys (x ¼ 0.1, 0.5 at.%). The microstructural
analysis focused especially on recognition of Ce-rich inter-
metallic phases, not previously described regarding Co-based
superalloys. The behavior of Ce-doped alloys depended on the
Ce form present in the alloy, therefore, Ce distribution in the
alloy, the structure, composition, and morphology of the Ce-
containing phase merit investigation.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Preparation of alloys
Alloys with nominal compositions of Co e9Ale9WexCe (x ¼ 0,
0.1, and 0.5 at.%) were obtained via vacuum induction melting
(VIM). The pure metals Co (99.99%), Al (99.99%), W (99.99%), and
Ce (99.9%) were used as feedstock materials. Co, Al, and W
metals were supplied by Stanchem Sp. z o.o. (Poland), whereas
Ce was supplied by Onyxmet (Poland). The metals were melted
from 1600 to 1700
/C14 C in a vacuum (0.13 Pa). The Ce pieces were
dosed from feeder to Co eAleW liquid. Cylindrical ingots Ø18
mm were fabricated by casting them into graphite molds.
Analogously, the supplementary binary Co eCe alloy with
composition Coe5Ce (at. %) was prepared. The alloy was pre-
pared to compare the thermal behavior of the Ce-modiﬁed
CoeAleW alloy with the binary Co eCe alloy. Afterward,
3 mm slices for microstructural analysis were cut using elec-
trical discharge machining (EDM). Additionally, the alloys were
subjected to heat treatments. The heat treatment of Co eAl-
eWexCe alloys consisted of solutionizing with water
quenching (1200
/C14 C for 24 h, Ar) and aging (900 /C14 C for 100 h, Ar).

4. Discussion
XRD showed that except for hexagonal Co, the microstructure
of binary Co e5Ce contained an a-Ce2Co17 phase. EBSD anal-
ysis and chemical composition in Ce-rich areas implied the
presence of CeCo5. Based on the crystallography, these phases
were similar; however, the lattice parameters of a-Ce2Co17
were nearly double those of the CeCo 5 phase. On the other
hand, they have high-temperature homogeneity ranges; the
homogeneity region of CeCo 5 extends to the Co-rich side and
Ce2Co17 has almost no Co solubility [ 30]. EBSD analysis of
quaternary alloys showed the presence of the CeCo 4Al phase
in the interdendritic spaces of the as-cast Co e9Ale9We0.5Ce
and Coe 9Ale9We0.1Ce alloys. The aged alloys were charac-
terized by the presence of Ce 2Co15Al2, and XRD and SAED
conﬁrmed their presence for quaternary alloys after aging.
The crystallographic differences between 1/5 and 2/17-type
phases were even higher for CeCo
5 and Ce 2Co17 phases in
which Al substituted Co. Ce2Co16Al crystallized in a hexagonal
Th2Ni17-type structure (P63/mmc, space group no. 194), whereas
the CeCoAl4 crystal structure was a hexagonal CaCu5-type (P6/
mmm, space group no. 191). Introduction of more Al (x ¼ 2, 3)
to Ce 2Co17-xAlx formed phases characterized by Th 2Zn17 (R-

and columnar to equiaxed transitions of Fe and Ni-based alloys
[42,43]. One advantage of REE involves alloy desulfurization
and deoxidization due to their considerably large negative free
energies of formation [ 44,45]. Solid particles of rare earth
compounds may act as heterogeneous nucleation sites and
suppress columnar growth. The distribution of Ce-rich phases
within the ingot is uniform due to induction melting, which
provides enough homogenization of alloying elements. The
uniform distribution of Ce is important in view of increase of
alloys properties as well as for puriﬁcation of the casting.

5. Conclusions
CoeAleWexCe superalloys exhibited a two-phase micro-
structure in the as-cast state. The dendritic areas were
composed of a g-Co phase and Ce eCo phases with a sub-
stantial amount of Al located in interdendritic spaces.
Al dissolves in g-Co and stabilizes the face-centered cubic
structure; however, it partially segregates into interdendritic
spaces and forms interdendritic phase constituents. The Ce-
rich phases with formula Ce
2Co17exAlx remain after heating
and were characterized by a Th 2Zn17-type structure. This
phase was an extension of binary Ce2Co17, which formed upon
substitution of Co by Al at the 6c site.
The intermetallic Al-rich Ce eCo phases melt at ~1160 /C14 C,
30 /C14 C lower than binary Ce 5Ce. Therefore, doping with Ce
limits the maximum operating temperature of the alloys.
Vacuum induction melting and gravity casting provided an
even distribution of cerium within the Co-based alloy ingot.
