The structural evolution of misfit dislocation networks at /C13 //C13 0 phase
interfaces in Ni-based single crystal superalloys under tensile loading and
temperatures is simulated by molecular dynamics. From the simulation, we
find that, with increasing load or temperature, the patterns of dislocation
networks on the (100), (110) and (111) phase interfaces change from regular
to irregular or disappear. Under the same load and temperature, the
dislocation networks of the different phase interfaces show different
degrees and patterns of damage. The density and stability of the dislocation
networks decrease with increasing temperature. When the interfacial
dislocation networks become more regular, the /C13 //C13
0 interfaces become
more stable. The simulated results are supported by related experimental
findings. Moreover, based on MD simulations, the averaged stress–strain
responses for different phase interfaces under loading are presented. The
results indicate that the combined influences of temperature and load play
an important role for the structure evolution of misfit dislocation networks
at /C13 //C13
0 phase interfaces of Ni-based superalloys.
Keywords: molecular dynamics simulation; interface; dislocation network;
Ni-based superalloys
1. Introduction
Owing to their excellent reliability and mechanical properties, Ni-based superalloys
have been used as materials for blades and vanes in gas turbines. It is well known
that Ni-based superalloys are strengthened by fine and ordered /C13
0 precipitates
embedded in an fcc /C13 matrix. These alloys are far stronger than pure /C13 or /C13 0 single
phase materials due to the presence of /C13 //C13 0 interfaces which prevents dislocation
motion at high temperatures [1,2]. Moreover, due to the lattice mismatch of the /C13
and /C13 0 phases, an eigenstress field is present. For this reason, after a long aging time
at elevated temperature, dislocations networks are formed at the /C13 //C13 0 interface to
relieve the coherency stress and enhance the creep resistance of the superalloys [3,4].

The morphologies and roles of interfacial dislocation networks in Ni-based
superalloys have been widely studied. For example, Lasalmonie and Strudel [5]
observed and identified the three-dimensional dislocation network covering /C13 //C13 0
interface by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Tian et al. [6] investigated the
formation and role of dislocation networks during high temperature creep in a single
crystal nickel-based superalloy. Luo et al. [7,8] observed the dislocation microstruc-
ture at different temperatures, after tensile deformation, by TEM and detailed the
components of these dislocation networks. Furthermore, the morphologies of
interfacial dislocation networks in various types of alloys have been carefully
observed by TEM and their correlation with creep behavior discussed in reviews by
Zhang et al. [1,9–11]. These studies on the formation of dislocation networks and
their role in creep resistance are based on experimental observations.
Due to the scale of the interfaces, which is less than sub-micrometer, i.e. on the
nanoscale, atomistic simulations play an important role in the study of dislocation
behavior at /C13 //C13
0 phase interfaces. Yashiro et al. [12,13] conducted several molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations to determine the fundamental aspect of dislocations at
the /C13 //C13 0 interface and investigate the interaction between the misfit dislocations and
actual dislocations gliding in mono-lattice [14]. Subsequently, they proposed a back
force model for simulating dislocation cutting of /C13
0 precipitates in Ni-based
superalloys by discrete dislocation dynamics (DDD) [15,16]. Their studies revealed a
new mechanism of dislocation nucleation and dislocation cutting into /C13 0 precipitate.
Zhu and Wang 0s [17] work by atomistic simulations discovered that there are three
different types of edge dislocation networks on the (100), (110), and (111) interfaces.
This simulation shows that the formation of dislocation networks is an important
way to reduce strain energy in the vicinity of the interfaces. Xie et al. [18] investigated
the motion of misfit dislocation in an Ni/Ni
3Al interface by MD simulations. The
results suggest that the stability of the Ni/Ni 3Al interface is strongly related to the
interaction of the misfit dislocations. From the discussion above, it can be seen that
dislocations at phase interfaces are present in single crystal superalloys and that they
have a strong influence on the mechanical properties of superalloys, especially creep
properties. Therefore, it is of interest to investigate the /C13
0 rafts and mechanical creep
properties associated with the formation and evolution of dislocation networks at the
interface.
In this paper, we focus on the structure and evolution of dislocation networks at
/C13 //C13 0 phase interfaces under the influence of mechanical loading and temperature.
Three different interfaces with orientations (100), (110), and (111) are investigated.
We aim to explore the deformation and damage of dislocation networks at the
interfaces and its influence on related mechanical properties in Ni-based superalloys.

4.2. Effect of the temperature on the dislocation networks
It is well known that temperature plays an important role in creep properties of
Ni-based superalloys [31–35]. To examine the effect of temperature on the evolution
of dislocation networks, atomistic simulation at elevated temperature is performed.
4.2.1. Effect of temperature on the structure of interface without loading
Figure 6 shows the structure of dislocation networks at the (100), (110) and (111)
phase interface at 0 and 300 K without a mechanical loading. It can be seen that,
although the square network on the (100) interface, the rectangle network on the
(110) interface and the equilateral triangle network on the (111) interface still exist,
the structures of dislocation networks become irregular at higher temperature. The
two dimensional structure of the dislocation networks at different temperatures are
shown in Figure 7. Some torsion occurs around the nodes of the equilateral triangle
network on the (111) phase interface at 300 K (Figure 7f). On the (110) interface,
damage of the dislocation line parallel to [ /C22110] direction is observed under the
influence of temperature (Figure 7d). However, the bending deformation of
the dislocation line can not be found at higher temperatures, which differs from
the results under mechanical loading at 0 K outlined in Section 4.1.2. In Figure 7b,
on the (100) phase interface, it can be seen that the dislocation lines become thinner
at 300 K compared to the result at 0 K. The reason is that the atomistic energies are
higher and become dispersed at higher temperatures. Thus, the dislocation density
decreases with increasing temperature.

5. Discussion and conclusions
It is well known that irregular coarsened /C13 0 precipitates are enveloped by the
networks of misfit dislocations over the /C13 //C13 0 interfaces during creep. The presence of
an abundance of the misfit dislocation networks at the /C13 //C13 0 interfaces actually
decrease the rate of directional coarsening [6] and increase the cutting resistance of
dislocations [15,16]. In our simulation, the damage of dislocation networks occurs
under the influence of tensile loading and temperature. When a load is applied, four
nodes of the regular square are found to move along the loading direction for the
(100) phase interface, and the square dislocation network is damaged initially at one
node. For the (110) phase interface, two dislocation lines vertical to ½
110/C138 direction
undergo a bending deformation along the loading direction and the other dislocation
line, which is parallel to ½110/C138 direction, are damaged first and finally disappear.
However, for the (111) phase interface, the dislocation network is fully and
synchronously damaged at all nodes and finally disappear. This indicates that the
dislocation networks of different phase interfaces show different degrees and
patterns of damage. The square dislocation network at the (100) interface is found to
be locally damaged at one node when the load is applied along the [100] direction.
The damaged networks lose the co-coordinating role of maintaining dynamic
equilibrium, so that a large number of dislocations are piled up at regions where the
networks have been damaged. Thus, in Ni-based superalloys, slip dislocations in the
matrix first cut into the /C13
0 phase from the interfaces due to non-equilibrium damage
of the dislocation networks, which finally induces rafting.
Moreover, we have investigated the influences of load and temperature on the
structural evolution of dislocation networks at the /C13 //C13 0 phase interfaces. The damage
of dislocation networks occurs at 0 K under mechanical loading. However, at 300 K
without mechanical loading, no node movement or bending deformation is observed.
These non-equilibrium deformations are only observed when a mechanical load is
applied. Section 4.2.2 highlighted how the density and strength of dislocation
networks decrease with increasing temperature. Dislocations are more mobile via
thermal activation; thus, the stability of the dislocation networks at the phase interface
is reduced. When a load is applied, deformation and damage of the dislocation

applied load and misfit at elevated temperatures [34,35]. It can be concluded from our
simulation that the combined influences of temperature and load play an important

The mechanical load is the main driving force for dislocation motion, playing a key
role in the creep properties of Ni-based single crystal superalloys.
In Section 4.3, we investigated the structural evolution of dislocation networks
at /C13 //C13 0 phase interfaces and their relationship to volume averaged stress–strain
responses. It was emphasized that only the stress–strain responses for phase
interfaces were obtained. Stress–strain curves of the whole alloy cannot be obtained
because the simulated systems are too small compared to real alloys. The simulated
results indicate that, before damage of the dislocation networks, the interfaces are in
an elastic state. Once the dislocation networks are damaged at the /C13 //C13
0 interfaces, the
interface will enter a plastic or yielding state. Thus, the structural evolution of
dislocation networks is closely related to the mechanical properties of Ni-based
superalloys. Experimental observations show that dislocation networks are formed
during primary creep and develop at all /C13 //C13
0 interfaces when creep enters the
secondary stage. In the secondary creep stage, a fine dislocation network is the key
factor to small minimum creep rates [6,9,36]. This is because fine dislocation
networks can effectively prevent the slip dislocations in the /C13 matrix channel from
moving through the /C13 //C13
0 interface and shearing the /C13 0 rafts. Moreover, fine
dislocation networks play a co-coordinating role in strain hardening and recovery
softening during secondary creep, and effectively enhance creep resistance. When
creep enters the tertiary stage, a considerable part of the dislocation networks still
exists on the /C13 //C13
0 interfaces. Once the dislocation networks are damaged, the
dislocation networks lose their co-coordinating role of maintaining dynamic
equilibrium. Slip dislocations in the /C13 matrix then cut into the /C13
0 phase from the
interfaces where dislocation networks are damaged. This indicates that the structural
evolution of dislocation networks have a close relationship with /C13 0 rafts and related
mechanical properties. The existence of fine and regular dislocation networks at the
/C13 //C13
0 interfaces enhance creep resistance, which is the key factor in the superior
mechanical properties of Ni-based superalloys.
