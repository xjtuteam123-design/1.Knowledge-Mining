a b s t r a c t 
Owing to their attractive structure and mechanical properties, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) and medium- 
entropy alloys (MEAs) have attracted considerable research interest. The strength of HEAs/MEAs with a 
single face-centered cubic (FCC) phase, on the other hand, requires improvement. Therefore, in this study, 
we demonstrate a strategy for increasing the room-temperature strength of FCC-phase HEAs/MEAs by 
tuning cryo-pre-straining-induced crystal defects via the temperature-dependent stacking fault energy- 
regulated plasticity mechanism. Through neutron diffraction line proﬁle analysis and electron micro- 
scope observation, the effect of the tuned defects on the tensile strength was clariﬁed. Due to the cryo- 
rolling-induced high dislocation density, mechanical twins, and stacking faults, the room-temperature 
yield strength of an equiatomic CoCrFeNi HEA was increased by ∼290%, from 243 MPa (as-recrystallized) 
to 941.6 MPa (30% cryo-rolled), while maintaining a tensile elongation of 18%. After partial recovery via 
heat treatment, the yield strength and ultimate tensile strength decreased slightly to 869 and 936 MPa, 
respectively. Conversely, the elongation increased to 25.6%. The dislocation density and distribution of the 
dislocations were found to contribute to the strengthening caused by forest dislocations, which warrants 
further investigation. This study discussed the possibility of developing single-phase high-performance 
HEAs by tuning pre-straining-induced crystal defects.

1. Introduction 
Owing to their intriguing structures and properties, high- 
entropy alloys (HEAs) and medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) con- 
taining equiatomic or near-equiatomic metallic elements have 
garnered considerable attention over the last decade [1–3] . 
Equiatomic CoCrFeMnNi, CoCrFeNi, and CoCrNi alloys with a single 
face-centered cubic (FCC) phase are the most extensively investi- 
gated HEAs/MEAs owing to their exceptional combination of tensile 
strength and ductility over a wide temperature range [ 1 , 4–6 ]. Their 
mechanical properties have been further enhanced by lowering the 
stacking fault energy (SFE), incorporating interstitial atoms, tuning 
the local concentration undulation, increasing the lattice distortion, 
and controlling the chemical short-range order [7–12] . The SFE is

a critical factor in determining the plastic deformation mechanism 
and mechanical properties of the FCC phase metals and alloys. A 
reduced SFE promotes mechanical twinning and the FCC → hexag- 
onal close-packed (HCP) martensitic transformation [13–16] , result- 
ing in increased strength and ductility, which are referred to as 
twinning-induced and transformation-induced plasticity effects, re- 
spectively [13–18] . Nevertheless, precipitate-free HEAs/MEAs fre- 
quently present insuﬃcient yield strengths. 
Numerous precipitation-strengthened HEAs/MEAs have been 
developed to further enhance their strength [19–25] . To achieve a 
high mechanical performance, however, careful control of the pre- 
cipitation process is required, as the formation of heterogeneous 
intermetallics and/or other brittle incoherent precipitates signiﬁ- 
cantly reduces the strength and ductility [21–25] . Controlling pre- 
cipitation formation in HEAs/MEAs is more diﬃcult than in di- 
lute alloys, owing to the ease with which brittle precipitates form 
in multi-principal elemental solid solutions [25] . It is well estab- 
lished that as the strain increases during plastic deformation, the

ﬂow stress increases due to the strengthening/hardening effects of 
the deformation-induced crystal defects. Thus, pre-straining can be 
used to increase the instantaneous yield strengths of metals and 
alloys. Additionally, a signiﬁcant temperature dependence of the 
SFE has been observed in HEAs/MEAs, where the SFE decreased 
signiﬁcantly as the temperature decreased [ 26 , 27 ]. A decreased de- 
formation temperature lowers the SFE and aids in the accumula- 
tion of defects by suppressing thermally activated dislocation re- 
covery [28–30] . When the SFE decreases, it becomes easier to con- 
ﬁne dislocations to a single slip plane, and thus the cross-slip of 
dislocations is hampered. As a result, the dislocation recovery is 
suppressed, because the cross-slip is required for the recovery. It is 
reasonable to believe that cryogenic pre-straining-induced defects 
could more effectively improve the room-temperature strength. 
The purpose of this article is to demonstrate a strat- 
egy for signiﬁcantly increasing the room-temperature strengths 
of precipitation-free FCC-phase HEAs/MEAs via cryogenic pre- 
straining-induced crystal defects. The equiatomic CoCrFeNi HEA 
was selected as the model alloy for demonstrating the strat- 
egy. Cryo-rolling (CR) was performed to introduce a high den- 
sity of defects, and then heat-treatment (HT) at medium temper- 
atures was performed to ﬁne-tune the microstructures and room- 
temperature tensile properties of the HEA. Subsequently, using the 
well-established neutron diffraction line proﬁle analysis method 
and electron microscopy characterization, we established correla- 
tions among the crystal defects, microstructure, and mechanical 
behaviors. 
2. Experimental method 
Arc melting of high-purity elements (purity > 99.9% in weight 
percentage) in an Ar gas atmosphere yielded an ingot of the 
equiatomic CoCrFeNi alloy. The ingot was re-melted ﬁve times 
to ensure chemical homogeneity. The ingot was homogenized at 
1200 °C for 12 h in an Ar gas atmosphere and then quenched 
with water to room temperature. After hot-rolling the sample to 
a thickness of 3 mm (50% reduction) at 1200 °C, then it was rolled 
at ambient temperature to a thickness of 1.8 mm (40% reduction). 
The sample was then aged for 20 min at 900 °C to obtain recrys- 
tallized grain structures. Following that, CR of the thin plate was 
conducted at -196 °C to a thickness reduction of 10, 20, and 30%. 
Hereinafter, they were denoted by 10% CR, 20% CR, and 30% CR 
samples, respectively. Then, HT of the samples was conducted at 
50 0, 60 0, and 70 0 °C for 1 h. Hereinafter, they were denoted by 
500 °C HT, 600 °C HT, and 700 °C HT samples, respectively. Ten- 
sile samples in the shape of a dogbone were cut from the plates 
with a gauge size of 2.5 mm (width) × 6.4 mm (length). During 
the preparation of the tensile samples, the longitudinal direction 
of the tensile samples, i.e., the loading axis for the uniaxial tensile 
tests, was set parallel to the rolling direction (RD) of the plates. 
Note that the tensile property is orientation-dependent due to the 
strong texture imparted by rolling. To maintain consistency in the 
microstructure and property, we conducted tensile tests and neu- 
tron diffraction line proﬁle analysis along the same direction, i.e., 
the RD of the plates. At room temperature, uniaxial tensile tests 
were conducted using a SHIMADZU AG50VF test frame. Tensile 
tests were conducted using a 50 kN load cell at a strain rate of 
1.0 × 10 −3 s −1 . The tensile strain was measured using a video ex- 
tensometer. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD, JSM-7100F) was 
used to observe the grain morphology, and the EBSD data were an- 
alyzed using OIM analysis software (version 7.0). All samples were 
observed at the same magniﬁcation with a step size of 0.5 μm. The 
samples were polished with abrasive paper and then polished with 
colloidal silica to a mirror ﬁnish (OP-U). Transmission electron mi- 
croscopy (TEM, JEM-20 0 0EXII) was used to observe the microstruc- 
ture, and the TEM specimens were prepared using a precision ion 
polishing system (PIPS, Gatan Model 691) and Ar ion cleaning. 
The samples were then subjected to ex situ neutron diffrac- 
tion measurements using a time-of-ﬂight neutron diffractometer 
at BL19 “TAKUMI” in the Materials and Life Science Experimen- 
tal Facility of the Japan Proton Accelerator Research Complex. The 
conﬁguration is depicted in Fig. 1 (a). During the measurement, the 
RD of the samples was oriented horizontally at an angle of 45 °
to the incident neutron beam. The incident beam was set to a 
width of 3 mm and height of 5 mm. Two detector banks posi- 
tioned at -90 ° and + 90 ° relative to the incident beam were used 
to collect diffraction patterns corresponding to the RD and nor- 
mal directions of the samples, respectively. The d range covered 
was 0.05–0.30 nm, with an instrumental peak resolution ( /2206d / d ) of 
0.3%. The instrument details are provided in another study [31] .

3. Results and discussion 
The EBSD inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) map in Fig. 1 (b) illustrates 
the grain morphology of the CoCrFeNi HEA before CR, where the 
grain structure was recrystallized. Annealing twins are frequently 
observed, as indicated by the arrowheads. The average grain diam- 
eter is 22.4 μm. Additionally, no discernible texture is observed. 
The neutron diffraction proﬁles of the samples before CR, after CR, 
and after subsequent HT at 50 0, 60 0, and 70 0 °C for 1 h are shown 
in Fig. 1 (c). The lattice parameter of the HEA was determined to be 
0.355 nm using neutron diffraction. Each sample contained a sin- 
gle FCC phase, indicating that no phase transformation occurred 
during the CR and/or HT. Prior to CR, the peaks of the neutron 
diffraction proﬁles of the sample were relatively narrow, indicating 
a low density of defects. However, after CR, the peaks widened due 
to the accumulation of crystal defects. After HT, the peaks became 
narrower again, indicating that the CR-induced defects were par- 
tially recovered. We analyzed and compared the defects and tex- 
tures. 
Fig. 2 shows the EBSD IPF maps ( Fig. 2 (a–c)) and boundary 
maps ( Fig. 2 (d–f)) of the CR samples. The average grain size was 
approximately 20 μm, which was comparable to the size before 
CR. However, as seen in the inserted maps, a notable texture of 
grains was formed after CR, with all three samples possessing 
{001} and {111} textures along the RD. The grains were plastically 
deformed and distorted by CR, resulting in a high density of local 
distortion and subgrain boundaries with a misorientation angle of 
2 °–5 °. Additionally, as the CR strain increased, the fraction of sub- 
grain boundaries increased ( Fig. 2 (f)). 
The grain characteristics of the HT samples are depicted in 
Fig. 3 by EBSD IPF maps and in Fig. 4 by boundary maps. After HT 
at 600 °C for 1 h, there was almost no change in the average grain 
size, which remained approximately 20 μm, consistent with the 
size before CR. Additionally, the grain texture remained unchanged, 
and a dense network of local distortions and subgrain boundaries 
was retained ( Figs. 3 (a–c) and 4 (a–c)). After HT at 700 °C, the 10% 
CR and 20% CR samples exhibited grain characteristics similar to 
those of the samples that underwent HT at 600 °C. There was no 
discernible change in the grain size, strong texture, and high den- 
sity of subgrain boundaries within grains. The sample after 30% CR 
and 700 °C HT, on the other hand, exhibited a characteristic that 
was distinct from the other samples. Its grain size was reduced to 
9.2 μm, and the length fraction of the subgrain boundaries was 
decreased signiﬁcantly. Additionally, the texture was altered. This 
indicates that static recrystallization occurred in the sample. 
Neutron diffraction proﬁle analyzes were performed to quanti- 
tatively compare the microstructural characteristics. The stacking 
fault probability (SFP) of the samples is shown in Fig. 5 (a). The SFP 
increased rapidly as the CR strain increased, but decreased signiﬁ- 
cantly after HT, particularly at temperatures of 600 and 700 °C. The 
SFPs of the CR samples were 1.2 × 10 −3 (10%), 2.8 × 10 −3 (20%), 
and 7.7 × 10 −3 (30%), but the corresponding values decreased to 
0.9 × 10 −3 , 2 × 10 −3 , and 5.4 × 10 −3 after HT at 500 °C, de- 
creased to 0.75 × 10 −3 , 1.1 × 10 −3 , and 2.2 × 10 −3 after HT at 
600 °C, then further decreased to 0.4 × 10 −3 , 0.38 × 10 −3 , and 
0.17 × 10 −3 after HT at 700 °C. The SFP of the CoCrFeNi and CoCr- 
FeMnNi HEAs is less than 3 × 10 −3 during in situ tensile defor- 
mations up to a true strain of 45% at room temperature [ 49 , 50 ],

sion of dynamic dislocation recovery at cryogenic temperatures. 
In the present study, the dislocation density reached a relatively 
high value of 45.1 × 10 14 m −2 after 30% CR. Thus, cryogenic pre- 
straining is an effective technique for introducing a relatively high 
density of dislocations. The CR-induced dislocations were partially 
recovered during the subsequent HT, and increasing the temper- 
ature signiﬁcantly increased the rate of thermally activated dis- 
location recovery. Recovery occurs when dislocations are annihi- 
lated or rearranged into lower-energy conﬁgurations [53] . Both 
processes are facilitated by the thermally activated glide, climb, 
and cross-slip of dislocations, the activation energies of which de- 
crease signiﬁcantly as the temperature increases [53] . The disloca- 
tion densities decreased to 8.5 × 10 14 m −2 (10%), 11.9 × 10 14 m −2 
(20%), and 24.8 × 10 14 m −2 (30%) after HT at 600 °C, and then to 
7.6 × 10 14 m −2 (10%), 10.4 × 10 14 m −2 (20%), and 1.8 × 10 14 m −2 
(30%) after HT at 700 °C. The textures of the sample before CR, af- 
ter 20% CR, and the 20% CR samples after HT at 600 or 700 °C for 
1 h, were analyzed using neutron diffraction proﬁles as shown in 
Fig. 5 (c). Prior to CR, no discernible texture was detected, which 
was consistent with the EBSD maps. After 20% CR, a strong {111} 
ﬁber texture was formed, while a relatively weak {001} ﬁber tex- 
ture remained. Although the SFP and dislocation density values sig- 
niﬁcantly decreased following HT at 600 and 700 °C, the texture 
did not fade. 
The plastic deformation mechanism of the CoCrFeNi HEA 
was highly temperature-dependent. At room temperature, the 
deformation-induced dislocations have a wavy morphology at 
small strains, as illustrated in Fig. 6 (a); at large strains, dislocation 
tangles and mechanical twinning occur [54] . The SFE of the HEA 
decreased rapidly as the temperature decreased. When the HEA is 
plastically deformed at a lower temperature, dislocation recovery 
is inhibited and mechanical twinning occurs more readily [ 55 , 56 ]. 
As shown in Fig. 6 (b), nanotwins are formed in the 10% CR sample, 
whereas no twins are formed in the 10% room-temperature-rolled 
sample ( Fig. 6 (a)). Due to the dynamic Hall-Petch effect, the eas- 
ier activation of mechanical twinning increases the accumulation 
of dislocations [57–59] . Apart from nanotwins, dislocation tangles 
are frequently observed in Fig. 6 (b), indicating that the dislocation 
density is greater than that in Fig. 6 (a). Fig. 6 (c) and (d) shows the 
corresponding microstructures after HT at 600 and 700 °C, respec-

To determine the relationship between the microstructures and 
mechanical response of the HEA, room-temperature tensile tests 
were performed, the results of which are shown in Fig. 7 . As il- 
lustrated in Fig. 7 (a), CR effectively increased the yield strength 
and ultimate tensile strength (UTS), with the yield strength in- 
creasing from 243 MPa (pre-CR) to 584.9 MPa (10% CR), 688.7 MPa 
(20% CR), and 946.1 MPa (30% CR). The corresponding UTS in- 
creased from 624.7 MPa (pre-CR) to 761.5 MPa (10% CR), 819.2 MPa 
(20% CR), and 971.9 MPa (30% CR). The tensile properties of the 
samples rolled to an equivalent strain at room temperature were 
shown in Fig. S1 in Supplementary Information, in which both the 
yield strength and UTS are smaller than that of the CR samples in 
Fig. 7 (a), particularly after rolling to a relatively large strain. This 
reveals that pre-straining at a cryogenic temperature is more ef- 
fective than at room temperature in improving the strength of the 
HEA. After HT of the CR samples at 600 °C, the yield strength de- 
creased slightly to 572.2, 652.7, and 869.6 MPa, respectively, and 
then decreased to 525.6, 579.9, and 399.4 MPa after HT at 700 °C. 
Notably, static recrystallization of the sample after 30% CR and 
700 °C HT led to a signiﬁcant decrease in the dislocation den- 
sity ( Fig. 2 (c)), and thus, the yield strength ( Fig. 7 (c)) and UTS 
( Fig. 7 (d)). In the remaining samples that did not undergo recrys- 
tallization, the UTS decreased slightly after HT. The tensile elon- 
gation, on the other hand, increased slightly after HT. The results 
demonstrate that by modulating CR-induced defects, the tensile 
properties of the HEA can be tuned.

4. Conclusion 
We demonstrated in this study a strategy for signiﬁcantly im- 
proving the mechanical performance of an equiatomic CoCrFeNi 
HEA at room temperature by tuning CR-induced crystal defects 
such as dislocations, SFs, and nanotwins. Further tuning of the me- 
chanical properties was accomplished by adjusting the number and

distribution of defects. The contribution of crystal defects to the 
strength of the HEA was quantiﬁed using neutron diffraction line 
proﬁle analysis and electron microscope observations. The results 
indicate that CR-induced defects contribute to strengthening while 
maintaining an acceptable tensile ductility, which provides in- 
sights into the development of high-performance single FCC-phase 
HEAs. For example, the room-temperature tensile yield strength 
of the CoCrFeNi HEA was increased by ∼290% from 243 MPa (as- 
recrystallized) to 941.6 MPa (30% cryo-rolled), while maintaining a 
tensile elongation of 18%. This was ascribed to the dense distribu-

further tuned to 869 and 936 MPa, respectively, and the tensile 
elongation to 25.6%. The present study demonstrates the feasibility 
of developing single-phase high-performance HEAs by tuning the 
crystal defects caused by pre-straining at temperatures other than 
their service temperature. 
Impact statement 
We demonstrated a strategy for signiﬁcantly increasing the 
strength of a precipitation-free single FCC-phase CoCrFeNi high- 
entropy alloy while maintaining a satisfactory ductility by regulat- 
ing cryo-straining-induced crystal defects.
