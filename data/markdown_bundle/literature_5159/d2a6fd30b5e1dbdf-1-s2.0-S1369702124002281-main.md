One of the most popular medium- and high-entropy alloys is CrCoNi alloy, renowned for its
outstanding mechanical properties, particularly at cryogenic temperatures. However, further enhanc-
ing the yield strength of CrCoNi at room temperature while maintaining its high ductility remains
challenging. In this study, we explore the potential of using a pulsed-wave laser in the powder bed
fusion, a dominant metal additive manufacturing (AM) technique, to achieve exceptional room-
temperature strength –ductility synergy in CrCoNi alloy. The pulsed-wave laser induces extra thermal
cycles, generating additional pre-existing dislocations that are uniformly distributed within the
interiors of solidi ﬁcation cells, a phenomenon distinct from conventional AM. These pre-existing
dislocations not only enhance the room-temperature yield strength exceeding 800 MPa but also trigger
the onset of deformation twinning prior to 2% strain. This early activation of deformation twinning
contributes to steady work hardening throughout the entire plastic deformation, resulting in a large
uniform elongation of nearly 40%. Our work offers valuable insights for designing novel AM processes
with pulsed-wave lasers to advance the fabrication of high-value and high-performance alloys.

However, these properties deteriorate as the temperature increases

alloy. Therefore, the medium-entropy alloy (MEA), CrCoNi, a
derivative of the Cantor alloy, was developed to enhance room-

Although these processing techniques achieve an exceptional

Additive manufacturing (AM), also known as three-
dimensional (3D) printing, stands out as a revolutionary technol-
ogy for manufacturing industry due to its capability to directly
produce parts with previously unattainable geometric complex-

tional techniques, the rapid heating and cooling during the build
process promote the formation of high dislocation densities and
ultraﬁne solidi ﬁcation structures, such as nanoscale cellular and
lamellar structures, which contribute to superior strength in
alloys [13,14]. Importantly, such strength enhancement does
not compromise ductility due to the effect of hierarchical
microstructural features in AM-produced alloys [13,14]. Hence,
metal AM techniques, including directed energy deposition
(DED) [15] and laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) [16], offer trans-
formative approaches for producing high-performance MEA
parts without the need of any post-processing treatments (e.g.,
thermo-mechanical processing and machining). Currently, most
of these AM processes employ continuous-wave lasers as the heat
source, because the print quality obtained using a pulsed-wave
laser remains inconsistent. However, some previous AM studies
have shown that discontinuous laser exposure can increase the
cooling rate and reduce the heat accumulation during laser scan-
ning of the powder bed [17,18]. Thus, using a pulsed-wave laser
may lead to a ﬁner as-built microstructure with higher disloca-
tion density, further enhancing the room-temperature strength
of AM-produced MEAs.
In this study, we utilize a pulsed-wave laser in LPBF to produce
CrCoNi alloy with enhanced strength and high ductility at room
temperature. We analyze the as-built microstructural features at
various scales and interpret the deformation mechanism of the
CrCoNi alloy enabled by pulsed-LPBF, which has not been fully
revealed in previous studies. Our ﬁndings prove that the higher
dislocation density inside the solidi ﬁcation cells derives from
the additional heating and cooling cycles induced by the
pulsed-wave laser, rather than from a faster cooling rate. This
work reveals the microstructural features that govern the
strength and ductility of LPBF-produced MEAs and may inspire
novel AM process designs for enhancing other single-phase
MEAs or HEAs.

Results and discussion
As-built microstructure produced by pulsed-LPBF process
A hallmark of additively manufactured alloys is the hierarchical
microstructure exhibiting features at multiple length scales,
which generally explains the excellent strength –ductility syn-
ergy achieved in AM [13,26]. Fig. 1 c shows the as-built
microstructural features, including melt pools, crystallographic
grains, and solidi ﬁcation cells, along the cross-section of an as-
built CrCoNi sample produced by pulsed-LPBF using a scanning
speed of 500 mm/s and a hatch spacing of 80 mm. For compar-
ison, the as-built microstructure produced by continuous-LPBF
using the same printing parameters is shown in Fig. S2 a.
Additively manufactured alloys are built by overlapping melt
pools layer by layer. Due to the strong thermal gradient during
the LPBF process, the columnar grains in the melt pools grow epi-
taxially across multiple layers along the build direction ( Fig. 1 c).
The growth of these grains is mostly aligned with the maximum
heat ﬂow direction at the melt pool boundaries, explaining the
prevalent <1 0 0> and <1 0 1> crystallographic orientations along
the build direction, as shown in the IPF map of Fig. 1c. This tex-
ture is similar to that of the CrCoNi alloy produced by
continuous-LPBF process ( Fig. S2 a) and is comparable to those
of other FCC alloys fabricated by AM [27,28]. The EBSD grain
boundary map in Fig. 1 c shows a high fraction of LAGBs (over
70%), indicating the non-equilibrium state of the as-built
microstructure owing to the rapid cooling rate during the pro-
cess. In the continuous-LPBF CrCoNi alloy, the fraction of LAGBs
among all GBs is also over 70% ( Fig. S2a); however, the densities
of LAGBs and HAGBs, 203 mm
/C0 1 and 76 mm /C0 1 respectively, are
much lower than those in the pulsed-LPBF CrCoNi alloy,
294 mm
/C0 1 and 107 mm /C0 1 respectively. This indicates that the
discontinuous laser exposure may disrupt the lateral epitaxial
growth of solidi ﬁed grains during the build process.
Due to the constitutional undercooling during rapid solidi ﬁ-
cation [29], numerous intrinsic solidi ﬁcation cellular structures
are formed and distributed throughout the columnar grains dur-
ing the AM process, oriented along various directions ( Fig. 1 c).
The elemental maps obtained from STEM-EDS measurements
(Fig. 1 d) reveal weak solute segregation across the cellular struc-
tures of the as-built CrCoNi alloy produced by pulsed-LPBF.
The distribution of Ni is relatively uniform, while the cell bound-
aries are rich in Cr and depleted in Co. The dislocation network
overlapping with these solidi ﬁcation cells will be discussed in
detail in the section below.

Origin of high yield strength in pulsed-LPBF CrCoNi
Because of the fast cooling rate and the resulting non-equilibrium
microstructure, it is common for AM-produced CrCoNi alloy to
show higher strength than as-cast and thermo-mechanically pro-
cessed ones with recrystallized microstructure [4,6]. The remark-
able phenomenon in this work is the superior yield strength
exceeding 800 MPa, signi ﬁcantly stronger than other reported
AM-produced CrCoNi alloy. For polycrystalline CrCoNi alloy

The ﬁner cell size, associated with the faster cooling rate [39],
is the reason why CrCoNi alloy produced by conventional LPBF
processes commonly exhibits higher strength than that pro-
duced by DED processes ( Fig. 2 b). The average size of the solidi-

measured as 540 ± 90 nm (from ten SEM images similar to
Fig. 3 a). This size is signi ﬁcantly smaller than that of DED-
produced cells [15] but not ﬁner than that of previously reported
LPBF-produced cells [16,31,32]. To decouple the in ﬂuence of
interference factors, such as process parameters, we measured
the cell size of the CrCoNi alloy produced by continuous-LPBF
process using the same parameters (200 W laser power,
500 mm/s scanning speed, and 80 mm hatch spacing) as the
pulsed-LPBF process ( Fig. 3 a). The measured cell size of
continuous-LPBF CrCoNi is 520 ± 80 nm, almost the same as that
of pulsed-LPBF one. This observation is counterintuitive given
that the pulsed-wave laser should lead to a faster cooling rate dur-
ing the build process. The comparable cooling rate might be
caused by the high pulse frequency of 1000 kHz employed in
the current pulsed-LPBF process. Given that the pulsed-LPBF
CrCoNi alloy exhibits a higher yield strength, it is concluded that
the cell size and cooling rate are not the main factors explaining
its superior yield strength compared to other AM-produced
CrCoNi alloy.

gb, the difference in

continuous-LPBF CrCoNi alloy shares the same values of the Tay-

density ‘q’ to be the determining factor for the difference in yield
strength between continuous-LPBF and pulsed-LPBF CrCoNi
alloy. In AM-produced alloys, the high-density dislocations in
the as-built microstructure originate from the thermal distortion
induced by rapid heating and cooling cycles during print process
[40]. These dislocations are generally tangled along solidi ﬁcation
cell boundaries due to the existence of solute segregation [13,39].
However, as shown in Fig. 3 b, the high-density dislocations in
the pulsed-LPBF CrCoNi alloy not only accumulate along the cell
boundaries but also spread over the cell interiors. This is distin-
guished from the dislocation-lean cell interiors in the
continuous-LPBF CrCoNi alloy and other previously reported
phenomena [13,41,42]. Based on the measured yield strength

pulsed-LPBF CrCoNi alloy, the higher dislocation density q deriv-

In LPBF processes, the thermal cycles that materials experi-
ence result from the track-by-track and layer-by-layer laser scan-
ning (Fig. 3c). During a single scan track with a continuous-wave
laser, the solidi ﬁed material underneath the molten pool under-
goes only one heating and cooling cycle. Whereas for a single
scan track with a pulsed-wave laser, the solidi ﬁed material sur-
rounding the molten pool endures multiple rapid heating and
cooling cycles (schematically demonstrated in Fig. 3d), and thus,
leading to larger thermal distortion. In other words, the pulsed-
wave laser works on the solidi ﬁed material like a ‘micro-
hammer’ at a high frequency, introducing additional strain
energy into the as-built microstructure. These additional pulse-
by-pulse thermal cycles, on top of the track-by-track and layer-
by-layer ones, contribute to the higher density of dislocations
formed in the cell interiors of the CrCoNi alloy during the
pulsed-LPBF process. The similar cell sizes between the
continuous-LPBF and pulsed-LPBF CrCoNi exclude the cooling
rate as a main factor in the difference in dislocation densities.
Therefore, the dense pre-existing dislocations within the solidi ﬁ-
cation cell interiors are attributed to the unique thermal history
generated by the pulsed-wave laser and are deduced to be the pre-
vailing factor promoting the enhancement of yield strength in
the pulsed-LPBF CrCoNi alloy.

Sustainable work hardening behaviour
Aside from the enhanced yield strength, another attractive fea-
ture in this work is that the as-built CrCoNi alloy produced by
the pulsed-LPBF process preserves high ductility as well
(Fig. 2 b). Because of the low stacking fault energy, the main
deformation mechanisms of CrCoNi alloy include dislocation
slip governing at low strain and deformation twinning triggered
at large strain [44]. When comparing the as-built CrCoNi with
different cell sizes manufactured by DED and LPBF techniques,
it is deduced that the ﬁner cellular structures do not necessarily
lower the ductility. Although the pre-existing cellular structures
formed during these AM processes hinder the dislocation move-
ment, these obstacles still allow the transmission of dislocations
[45]. Similarly, the hierarchical boundaries within the present

Therefore, the synergistic effect of deformation twinning and
grain rotation sustains the dominant plastic deformation.

The difference between the yield strength and ultimate tensile
strength of the pulsed-LPBF CrCoNi alloy is approximately

The very early activation of deformation twinning explains
the relatively stable strain hardening throughout the entire plas-
tic deformation in the pulsed-LPBF CrCoNi alloy ( Fig. 2 d). It is
well-known that the entanglement of dislocations and the dislo-
cation accumulation at grain boundaries yield strain hardening
during plastic deformation. The KAM map in Fig. 5b reveals that
the deformation twins contribute to the pile-up of dislocations
and strain hardening as well, as indicated by the white arrows.
Notably, with the propagation of deformation twinning, the
intersection of primary and secondary twins ( Fig. 5 c) is more
effective than a single twin system in impeding dislocation
movement. The primary twins can prevent inclined migration
of the secondary twins [50], enhancing the strengthening ef ﬁ-
ciency [51]. Moreover, the formation of networks between defor-
mation twins and multi-orientational stacking faults ( Fig. 5 d)
also sustains strain hardening and dominates large plastic strain
[52].
With the multiplication of deformation twins and the
increased strain, the role of deformation twins transits from serv-
ing as barriers against dislocation movement to providing poten-
tial pathways for dislocation transmission (as schematically
shown in Fig. S3c). As the applied stress exceeds the interface bar-
rier strength for slip transmission, dislocations can transmit
across the twin boundary planes, leading to a more uniform
strain distribution and hence sustained plastic deformation
[50]. When interacting with a twin boundary plane, the incom-
ing pair of a/6 <1 1 2>-type partial dislocations can transmit
across the plane, leaving a sessile Frank partial dislocation on
the plane [53]. In addition, the intersections between twins
may construct an ‘overpass’, enabling rapid dislocation motion
along the twin boundary planes and facilitating cross-slip from
one twin boundary to another [50]. Therefore, the early activa-
tion of deformation twinning not only contributes to the high
work hardening but also ensures steady plastic deformation.
General implications of pulsed-LPBF process
Apart from the increased thermal cycles introduced by a pulsed-
wave laser, the periodic laser emission and the short pulse dura-
tion endow the pulsed-LPBF process with other interesting capa-
bilities. For instance, the peak energy density achieved by a
pulsed-wave laser is much higher than that of a continuous-
wave laser ( Fig. 1 b). The feedstock powder of AM enables design
freedom for in-situ alloying [54] or the introduction of hard par-
ticles [55] through blending different powders prior to printing.
This mixture has the potential to increase strength via solid solu-
tion and precipitation strengthening. Those blended refractory
element powders with high melting points, such as tungsten
(melting point around 3420 /C176C), might remain unmelted during
the continuous-LPBF process but are more likely to melt under
the much higher peak temperature achieved in the pulsed-LPBF
process. An additional print trial was conducted to print CrCoNi
powder blended with various amounts of W powder by pulsed-
LPBF using different process parameters. The microstructure
and tensile properties are shown in Fig. S4 and Table S2 . The
SEM-EDS analysis of the alloy matrix con ﬁrms the dissolution
of W ( Fig. S4a) into the alloy matrix. Partially melted W particles
are embedded in the matrix, retaining their initial random orien-
tations (Fig. S4b). The extent of the W dissolution into the matrix
can be improved by reducing the scanning speed or adding a
remelting step, as re ﬂected in the improved strength and hard-
ness ( Figs. S4 c and d). These results demonstrate that the design
of the pulsed-LPBF process can bring forth further strength
enhancement, such as the yield strength of 926 MPa achieved
in the (CrCoNi)
97.7W2.3 alloy.
Another potential application frequently paired with a
pulsed-wave laser is the laser shock peening (LSP) process, known
for enhancing fatigue strength, wear resistance, and corrosion
resistance by introducing compressive residual stresses [56]. Dur-
ing LSP, the interaction with laser vaporizes the ablative layer,
forming high-pressure plasma and a powerful shock wave that
plastically deforms the material. Recognizing the bene ﬁts of
LSP treatment, researchers have explored integrating LSP with
LPBF in a single manufacturing system equipped with both a
continuous-wave laser for LPBF and a pulsed-wave laser for LSP
[57,58]. In our study, the pulse energy and the resulting power
density are 0.2 mJ and 2.5 W cm
/C0 2, respectively, which are far
below the commonly applied values in the J and GW cm /C0 2 range
for LSP. Therefore, the strengthening effect of pulsed-wave laser
through shock peening is negligible in this context. Nonetheless,
we have demonstrated that the excellent print quality and excep-
tional properties are achieved via the pulsed-wave laser. In the
future, with the proper modi ﬁcation of pulsed-wave laser systems
(e.g., a broad range of pulse frequency and duration), it is fore-
seen that the combination of LPBF process and LSP treatment
into an AM machine equipped with a single laser system is
highly feasible, potentially enhancing ef ﬁciency and reducing
costs in hybrid AM processes.
Conclusions
The advancement of manufacturing technologies is driving the
development of high-performance materials. In this work, we
showcase the remarkable potential of a pulsed-wave laser in the
LPBF process leading to novel microstructural phenomena and
the enhancement of mechanical properties in CrCoNi alloy.
The CrCoNi alloy produced by pulsed-LPBF process exhibits an
exceptional yield strength of around 800 MPa while maintaining
a large uniform elongation of 37%. The microstructure and prop-
erties can be slightly tailored by varying the process parameters
within the optimized process window. The higher yield strength
