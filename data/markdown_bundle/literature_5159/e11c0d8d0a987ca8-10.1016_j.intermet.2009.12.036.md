abstract
Fe–Al–Ti alloys with the ordered L2 1-structure (Heusler phase) belong to the few Fe–Al-based alloys
which show comparably high-strength at high temperatures, e.g. at 800 /C14 C. However, like many other
high-temperature materials based on intermetallics they show limited ductility even at high tempera-
tures. In order to further explore the possibilities in increasing their strength and ductility, alloys with
four different microstructures, i.e. single-phase L2
1,L 2 1 with incoherent precipitates of TiB 2 or Laves
phase, and coherent L2 1 þ A2, were produced. Also, the inﬂuence of alloying with Cr and B has been
investigated. The Young’s modulus of Fe–25Al–20Ti–4Cr (at.%) in dependence of temperature up to
900
/C14 C has been determined and results of the compressive ﬂow stress, creep strength and brittle-to-
ductile transition temperatures (BDTT) are summarised and compared to those of binary Fe 3Al (D03), Fe–
Al–Ti-based alloys, and some commercial alloys.

1. Introduction
Fe–Al-based materials are well known for their outstanding
corrosion resistance, good wear behaviour, low speciﬁc weight and
relatively low material cost. Limited ductility at ambient tempera-
ture and low creep strength above 600 /C14 C have prevented a more
widespread use of these alloys up to now. Consequently, recent
efforts focus on improving the strength at high temperatures, see
e.g. [1–3]. Strengthening concepts include solid solution hardening,
precipitation of incoherent phases such as carbides, borides and
other intermetallic phases, increasing the order, and generation of
two-phase coherent microstructures [4]. The Fe–Al–Ti system
offers the possibility to apply the different concepts because Fe–Al
has a considerable solid solubility for Ti, Ti considerably increases
the L2
1(D03)/B2]1 transition temperature, various intermetallic
phases including a Laves phase are in equilibrium with L2 1 in this
system, and a A2 þ L21 miscibility gap exists where coherent
microstructures can be generated [4–6]. Moreover, in the Fe–Al–Ti
system it is possible to combine different mechanisms because they
are available within a limited range of compositions. The present
paper summarises results of the high-temperature strength and
ductility of Fe–Al–Ti alloys with Ti and Al contents which range
from 8.5 to 20 and 21–25 at.% (all compositions in at.%), respec-
tively, and which owe their strength to increased order, boride or
Laves phase precipitates [7–9], or coherent L2
1 þ A2 microstruc-
tures [9,10]or combinations thereof. Some of the alloys also contain
Cr or B in solid solution or TiB 2 precipitates to possibly enhance
ductility.
2. Experimental
Alloys were produced by vacuum induction melting (VIM; for
chemical compositions see Table 1 ). The dimensions of the casts
were 25 mm in diameter and 180 mm in length. The as-cast alloys
were heat treated at 1000 C for 500 h to obtain stable micro-
structures. Phase identiﬁcation has been carried out by means of X-
ray powder diffraction (XRD) and energy-dispersive spectrometry
(EDS) on a scanning electron microscope (SEM). Furthermore,
phase transformations have been determined by differential
thermal analysis (DTA).
Samples for metallographic investigations and mechanical tests
were cut by electro-discharge machining (EDM). The Young’s
modulus in dependence on temperature has been measured

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructures
After annealing at 1000 /C14 C for 500 h the alloys Fe–25Al–15Ti–
xCr [8] and Fe–25Al–15Ti–0.005B ( Fig. 1a ) are single-phase L2 1
(Heusler phase) with a grain size of about 150 mm. Alloys Fe–25Al–
25Ti–xCr or –xB exhibit a two-phase L2 1 þ C14 Laves-phase
microstructure with about 5 vol.% Laves phase ( Fig. 1b ) and the
grain size of the L2 1 matrix is again about 150 mm [8]. The alloys
with 1 and 2 at.% B show a L2 1-ordered matrix with TiB 2 precipi-
tates along grain boundaries and within the grains ( Fig. 1c ) and
a reduced grain size of about 50 mm [8]. Alloys Fe–23Al–8.5Ti and
Fe–21Al–10Ti exhibit coherent microstructures of the ordered L2 1-
phase and minor amounts of the disordered A2 phase ( Fig. 1d ).
Alloy Fe–21Al–10Ti contains additional C14 Laves phase which
precipitated during the heat treatment predominately along grain
boundaries [10]. It is noted that the volume fractions of L2 1 and A2
in the coherent microstructures are strongly dependent on
temperature [10].

3.3. Flow stress
The compressive ﬂow stress in dependence on temperature is
shown in Fig. 4. In this ﬁgure the ﬂow stresses of the single-phase
L21 (Fe–25Al–15Ti–xCr) and two-phase L2 1 þ Laves phase and
L21 þ TiB2 (Fe–25Al–16Ti–2B and Fe–25Al–20Ti–xCr) are combined
in a single curve since they show similar ﬂow stress behaviour [8].A
condensed single ﬂow stress curve is also drawn for the two-phase
alloys (Fe–21Al–10Ti, Fe–23Al–8.5Ti) with coherent L2 1/A2 inter-
faces [10]. In addition, the ﬂow stress of binary single-phase D0 3
(Fe–26Al) [14] and binary coherent A2 þ D03 [15] are shown. Of the
L21-based Fe–Al–Ti alloys, those with coherent L2 1 þ A2 micro-
structures show the highest ﬂow stresses up to 700 /C14 C. The same is
true for the binary alloys, where the alloy with the coherent
A2 þ D03 microstructure exhibits higher ﬂow stresses up to 500 /C14 C
compared to the binary D0 3 single-phase alloy. Furthermore, the
results demonstrate that above 500 /C14 C the ﬂow stress can be
substantially increased by alloying with Ti through forming the L21-
ordered phase. Apparently this is not only due to increased order
but also due to the appearance of a second yield stress maximum
between 700 and 800
/C14 C( Fig. 4) [16].

3.4. Creep
Creep data of several Fe–Al(–Ti) alloys at 650 /C14 C are shown in
Fig. 5 . Beside Fe–23Al–8.5Ti [10], which was the only one of the
present alloys that has been tested at 650 /C14 C, some creep data of
binary and Ti modiﬁed alloys are shown [7,19–21]. The data show
that addition of Ti to Fe–Al alloys strongly enhances the creep
resistance. Fig. 6 reveals that the present alloys do have a remark-
able creep resistance even at 800 /C14 C. Of the present alloys those
with additional Laves phase precipitates show the highest creep
resistance followed by the single-phase L2
1 alloys, alloys with
additional TiB2 precipitates, and the alloys with coherent L2 1 þ A2
microstructures [8,10]. Only the boron doped alloy has a somewhat
lower creep resistance. That additional precipitates of the Laves
phase increase the creep strength of Fe–Al–Ti alloys at 800
/C14 Ci si n
accordance with previous results for ternary Fe–Al–Ti alloys
[18,22]. Compared to those ternary alloys the present alloys with
additional Cr show somewhat higher creep strength at 800
/C14 C [18].
Whether this difference is related to a difference in constitutional
disorder, which has a marked effect on high-temperature creep of
Fe–Al–Ti alloys [18], is not clear, because the degree of order of the
L21-phase has not been established in the present alloys. The creep
strength of the L21-based alloys surpasses that of a B2-ordered alloy
with higher Al content and additional carbide precipitates [23] and
even matches that of the commercial alloy Incoloy 800HT [24]
(Fig. 6 ), an austenitic Fe–Ni–Cr alloy developed for applications at
800 /C14 C. This is remarkable, as the density of the Fe–Al–Ti alloys is
only 75% of that of Incoloy 800HT. It is noted, that the present alloys
also compare favourably to some Ni-based alloys, e.g. Inconel 617
[9,25].

3.6. Oxidation
All investigated alloys show parabolic oxide growth at 800 /C14 C. By
means of XRD and EDS the adherent oxide scale has been identiﬁed
as a-Al 2O3 [8,10]. The parabolic rate constants kp range between
1*10/C0 14 and 5*10/C0 15. All scales are adherent and do not spall during
testing or after cooling to RT at the end of the tests. The parabolic
rate constants match those established earlier for ternary Fe–Al–Ti
alloys [18]. They reveal that neither by alloying with high contents
of Ti nor by additional Cr or B the excellent oxidation resistance of
Fe–Al is affected.
4. Summary
L2
1-ordered Fe–Al–Ti alloys can be easily produced by VIM and
subsequent casting. In view that these are Fe–Al-based alloys they
show outstanding high-strength and creep resistance at least up to
800 /C14 C. If density related strength, i.e. speciﬁc strength, is consid-
ered then it surpasses that of commercial Incoloy 800HT. However,
high BDTTs which range between 725 and 975
/C14 C may at least in the
moment preclude their application at 800 /C14 C as the alloys will fail in
a brittle manner in case of any damage. Modifying the alloys by Cr
and B in solid solution did not reduce the strength and did not
deteriorate the excellent corrosion resistance but also had no
positive effect on BDTT. However, grain reﬁnement by TiB 2
precipitates resulted in the lowest BDTT indicating that there is
some potential in lowering BDTT by reducing the grain size of these
coarse-grained alloys.
Changes in the microstructure do have a more pronounced
effect on strength. Single-phase L2
1 or two-phase L2 1 þ C14/TiB2
alloys show the highest ﬂow stresses above 700 /C14 C while at lower
temperatures alloys with a coherent L2 1 þ A2 microstructure show
even higher ﬂow stresses. While the addition of precipitates,
especially Laves phase, slightly improves the creep resistance at
high temperatures the ﬂow stress is found to be the highest for
single-phase L2
1 alloys.
