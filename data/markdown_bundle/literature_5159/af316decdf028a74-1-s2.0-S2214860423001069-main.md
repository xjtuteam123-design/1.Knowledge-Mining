ABSTRACT 
Refractory tungsten-tantalum (WTa) alloy material was prepared by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) in-situ 
alloying, and the direct fabrication of WTa complex parts with good appearance and structural integrity was 
realized. The interior defects, phase composition, microstructure and mechanical properties of LPBF-deposited 
WTa alloy were investigated. The preferential combination of Ta and oxygen (O) produces an oxygen- 
trapping effect, which can reduce the segregation of O at the grain boundary, thereby improving the grain 
boundary strength and effectively suppressing the generation of intergranular cracks. The W-Ta solid solution 
phase was formed in LPBF-deposited WTa alloy. The solidified grains of the horizontal (XOY) and vertical (XOZ) 
sections are mainly striped grains with an average size of 5.18 µ m and upwardly grown columnar grains with an 
average size of 6.43 µ m, respectively. The grains of LPBF-deposited WTa alloy specimens are finer than those of 
LPBF-deposited W specimens. Inside the solidified grains, cellular structures with a size of around 1 µ m were 
formed in XOY section. In the XOZ section, the transition from columnar/elongated structures at the bottom of 
the molten pool to fine cellular structures at the top of the molten pool was formed as a result of the varies in 
temperature gradients (G) and solidification rates (R). The maximum microhardness of the LPBF-deposited WTa 
alloy was 454.28HV
0.5
. The compression test showed that the ultimate compressive strength (UCS) was 1393 
MPa, exhibiting a transgranular fracture mode. This study demonstrates the feasibility of in-situ fabrication of 
complex parts of refractory tungsten-tantalum alloy materials by LPBF. 
1. Introduction 
As a disruptive metal additive manufacturing (AM) technique, laser 
powder bed fusion (LPBF), also generally known as selective laser 
melting (SLM), has aroused extensive interest from academic research 
and industrial engineering [1,2] . Complex metallic components with 
specific geometry and size can be fabricated directly in a track-by-track 
and layer-by-layer manner by LPBF based on the local instantaneous 
melting of pre-deposited metal powders and rapid solidification of 
molten metal. To date, LPBF has been extensively employed to develop 
various common metallic materials, including Fe-based alloys, Ti-based 
alloys, Ni-based alloys, and Al-based alloys, and the as-deposited spec -
imens exhibit excellent mechanical properties [3,4] . In recent years, 
LPBF additive manufacturing of refractory metallic materials, such as 
tungsten (W), tantalum (Ta), and Molybdenum (Mo), has become one of 
the research spotlights [5,6] . 
As a metal with the highest melting point (3695 K), W also exhibits 
high density and microhardness, good thermal conductivity, low ther -
mal expansion coefficient, excellent strength at high temperature and 
high wear resistance, which is generally applied in a lot of industrial 
fields, such as military, aerospace, electronics. In addition, W is also 
regarded as the most promising candidate material for plasma-facing 
components in thermal nuclear fusion devices, owing to their low 
sputtering yield, and high plasma sputtering corrosion resistance [7] . 
Traditionally, the processing methods of tungsten are limited due to its 
high melting point. Currently, the mainstream processing route for 
tungsten is powder metallurgy (PM), including spark plasma sintering 
(SPS), ultrahigh-pressure (UHP), and powder injection molding (PIM) 
[8,9] . Nevertheless, a set of processing procedures would be required for 
the sake of obtaining dense W parts in the mainstream processing route. 
Meanwhile, directly manufacturing tungsten parts with complex ge -
ometries and micro-scale structures is a challenge for the mainstream 
processing route. In contrast, a series of current studies show that the 
direct preparation of highly dense W specimens can be achieved via the

deeply investigated and discussed, and an insight into the role of Ta in 
the LPBF-deposited W materials was conducted. Therewith, the WTa 
alloy structures with complex geometry and favorable appearance were 
directly manufactured by LPBF.

3.3. Phase composition and Microstructure 
Fig. 8 plots the XRD patterns of the premixed W-Ta powder and the 
as-deposited WTa alloy specimen. It can be distinctly seen that W and Ta 
diffraction peaks exist in the W-Ta premixed powder. In contrast, the 
diffraction peaks of the as-deposited WTa alloy are only W diffraction 
peaks, and the diffraction peaks of Ta have disappeared. Indicating that 
the W and Ta have been completely dissolved under the irradiation of 
the laser beam, and thus the resultant WTa single solid solution phase 
alloy was formed. 
This could be attributed to the fact that infinite miscibility between 
W and Ta, thus a single solid solution phase alloy can be formed when 
the premixed W-Ta powder was completely melted under the laser 
irradiation. Additionally, as can be clearly seen from the inset in Fig. 8 
that compared with the position of the W diffraction peak, the diffrac -
tion peaks of the as-deposited WTa alloy shift toward a lower diffraction 
angle. This is because the atomic radius of Ta (0.146 nm) is larger than 
that of W (0.137 nm). Therefore, the lattice constant of the as-deposited 
WTa alloy is larger than that of pure W. According to the interplanar 
spacing and Bragg ’ s equation, the lattice constant increases, the inter -
planar spacing increases, and the diffraction angle decreases accord -
ingly. Consequently, the diffraction peaks of the as-deposited WTa alloy 
shift to a lower diffraction angle.

4. Discussions 
4.1. Densification and microcracks suppression 
The relative density of the as-deposited specimens is the first issue to 
be solved in LPBF. A high relative density of the LPBF as-deposited 
specimens plays a critical role in the final mechanical properties. The 
existing research results indicate that the applied processing parameters 
have a direct effect on the interior defects and the resultant relative 
density. Earlier reported results showed that tungsten melt has a large 
balling tendency due to its intrinsic properties, such as high surface 
tension, high viscosity, and high thermal conductivity [33] . Therefore, 
compared with other conventional materials, irregular metallurgical 
pores are more likely to form in tungsten materials prepared by LPBF. As 
described above, irregular metallurgical pores can be observed in Fig. 6 
(a) when an inappropriate LED (0.75 J ⋅ mm
 1
) was applied. In this case, 
the W-Ta powder is difficult to melt completely due to the low heat 
input. Part of the WTa melt has an apparent balling tendency under the 
drag of surface tension, forming spherical bumps and then leading to the 
formation of metallurgical pores during the layer-by-layer process. 
Meanwhile, the high viscosity hinders the full spreading of the WTa 
melt, so it will also lead to the occurrence of metallurgical pores between 
adjacent tracks. With the enhancement of applied LED, the heat input 
increased accordingly, and the corresponding temperature of WTa melt 
raised. Commonly, the surface tension and viscosity are inversely pro -
portional to temperature. Naturally, with the continuous increase of 
WTa melt, the surface tension and viscosity are greatly reduced, and 
thus the full spreading of WTa melt was achieved. In this light, the 
number of metallurgical pores can be remarkably reduced, as shown in 
Fig. 6 (b) and (c). 
In addition to the metallurgical pores mentioned above caused by 
improper processing parameters, microcracks can also be found in 
Fig. 5 . Nevertheless, the microcracks in the WTa specimens in work were 
significantly reduced compared to the W prepared by LPBF. Cracking 
occurs when large residual thermal stress and high temperature gradient 
are formed in LPBF-deposited specimens, as a consequence of transient 
heating and rapid cooling of the melt pool with the rapid movement of 
the laser beam. Generally, the temperature gradient and cooling rate of 
the molten pool in LPBF can reach the order of 10
6
. As a typical room 
temperature brittle material, W has a relatively high ductile-to-brittle 
transition temperature. As mentioned above, a large number of micro -
cracks tend to be generated in LPBF-deposited W specimens [16] . 
Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, the microcracks formed in 
LPBF-deposited W are usually intergranular cracks, which are formed 
when thermal stress exceeds the strength of the grain boundary. Existing 
studies show that the segregation of impurity elements (oxygen, carbon,

classical metal solidification, the ratio of temperature gradient (G) and 
solidification rate (R) has a direct influence on the solidified grain 
morphology. Different G/R values determine the transition from equi -

to be found at a relatively low G/R. Fig. 17 presents an illustration of the

molten pool of LPBF, there is a large temperature gradient and a rela -
tively low solidification rate, and the direction of the maximum tem -
perature gradient is perpendicular to the molten pool boundary, 
facilitating the formation of columnar structures. Conversely, at the top 
of the molten pool, the relatively low temperature and solidification rate 
result in a relatively low G/R, inducing the formation of an equiaxed 
cellular structure. At the same time, it can also be found from Fig. 10 (b) 
that the growth direction of the columnar structures in the molten pool 
is different. This can be attributed to the change of laser scanning di -
rection between adjacent layers. When the laser scanning strategy of 
inter-layer rotation was adopted, the direction of heat flow in the molten 
pool will change, and then the growth direction of the columnar struc -
ture will change. 
Relevant studies on the formation mechanism of intragranular 
structure have been reported. Significant Mo and Cr elements segregated 
at the cellular structure boundary can be observed in LPBF deposited 
316 L [40] . A similar intragranular structure also can be found in 
LPBF-deposited AlSi10Mg, which has a Si-rich boundary [41] . Fig. 18

no distinguishable element difference in the interior and wall of the 
cellular structure, and the distribution of W and Ta is relatively uniform, 
However, further TEM characterization can reveal cellular structure 
characteristics, as shown in Fig. 19 . As can be observed from Fig. 19 , the 
cellular structure wall is composed of a series of nanoparticles rich in Ta 
and O. The W-Ta premixed powder rapidly melts and forms a molten 
upon the irradiation of the laser beam. During the rapid solidification of 
high-temperature melt, the high oxygen affinity makes it easier for the 
Ta atoms in the molten pool to trap O atoms, thus forming Ta and O rich 
nanoparticles. At the top of the molten pool, because the thermal 
gradient around is approximately the same, the liquid-solid front pushes 
around at the same speed, resulting in the formation of cellular walls of 
nanoparticles. 
5. Conclusions 
In the present paper, the in-situ preparation of highly dense and 
refractory W-5Ta alloy specimens with a single solid solution phase was 
realized by LPBF additive manufacturing, and the W-Ta alloys complex 
parts with good structural integrity and surface morphology were 
manufactured at the same time. Subsequently, the interior defects, 
microstructure, and mechanical properties of LPBF-deposited WTa alloy 
were characterized and investigated. The main conclusions can be 
summarized as follows: 
(1) The interior defects of WTa alloy specimens include irregular 
metallurgical pores and microcracks. The interior defects can be 
significantly controlled by applying appropriate LED. Meanwhile, Ta 
acts as a preferential bonding agent with O atoms, which can avoid the 
segregation of O at the W grain boundaries, thus improving the grain 
boundaries ’ strength and effectively inhibiting the generation of inter -
granular microcracks in W materials prepared by LPBF. 
(2) The morphology of solidified microstructure in the XOY and XOZ 
section is obviously different. Grains with striped and equiaxed were
