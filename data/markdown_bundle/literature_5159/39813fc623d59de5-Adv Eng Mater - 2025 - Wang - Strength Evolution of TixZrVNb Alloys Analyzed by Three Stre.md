1. Introduction
Multiprincipal element high-entropy alloys have the potential to
achieve superior mechanical properties due to their high entropy,
sluggish diffusion, lattice distortion, and cocktail effect. At the
same time, these alloys have good corrosion resistance, mak-
ing them highly applicable in ﬁelds such as navigation and
military.[1– 6] In recent years, high-entropy alloys have received
widespread attention from materials scholars, and their proper-
ties have been extensively studied. However, unlike traditional
alloys, the solid solution strengthening of high-entropy alloys
is dif ﬁcult to quantify under the combined action of multiple
elements. Therefore, it is necessary to explore a model that is
suitable for evaluating the solid solution strengthening effect
of high-entropy alloys. Huang [7] has studied TiZrVNb,
Ti1.5ZrVNb, and Ti 2ZrVNb alloys, mainly focusing on their as
cast state and properties at different temperatures. His research
shows that this type of high-entropy alloy
has high tensile strength with good
ductility at room temperature. Therefore,
to investigate the changes in tensile yield
strength of this type of alloy, this work
mainly focuses on the calculation of
the strength of TiZrVNb, Ti 2ZrVNb,
Ti3ZrVNb, and Ti 4ZrVNb alloys using
three different solid solution strengthening
models.
For traditional binary and ternary dilute
solute solid solution alloys, Labusch [8]
established a solid solution strengthening
model, and a large number of computational
results indicate that this strengthening
model is not applicable to multicomponent
alloys. Fleischer[9,10] established the relation-
ship between the solid solution strengthen-
ing value and the composition; The
Fleischer model assumes the existence of a
single non interacting atom, which considers
several important factors affecting the solid
solution strengthening, making it appli-
cable to traditional alloys with a single prin-
cipal component. For multicomponent solid solution alloys,
Sadeghpour et al.[11] proposed an empirical formula that is linearly
related to yield strength and alloy composition. However, this for-
mula is based on statistics and has no physical meaning, making it
difﬁcult to ensure calculation accuracy with this empirical theory.
For multielement alloys, due to the relatively small differences in
the content of each element, various alloying elements are consid-
ered as the main elements. Therefore, not only should these param-

This study focuses on four types of Ti xZrVNb (x = 1, 2, 3, 4) high-entropy alloys.
After solution treatment, the alloys are subjected to room-temperature tensile
testing. Characterization methods such as optical microscopy, scanning electron
microscopy, electron backscattering diffraction, and transmission electron
microscopy are used to analyze the microstructure of solution treatment. The
results indicate that the microstructures of all four alloys are composed of grains
with a single BCC structure. The room-temperature tensile test results indicate
that as x increases, the yield strength of the alloy gradually decreases, while the
plasticity gradually increases. On the basis of this experiment and to ﬁnd an
acceptable strengthening model, three solid solution strengthening models are
introduced in this article, which are used to calculate the yield strength of solid
solution alloys. The tensile yield strength of alloys is compared with the calculated
values, and the differences between the three models and the error between the
calculated results and experimental results are discussed. The results show that
all three models can predict the yield strength of alloys well with an error of less
than 8%; compared to other models, these models have smaller computational
errors, among which the Varvenne model has a relatively small error.

The ﬁrst few models are based on statistical analysis. Curtin[18]
developed a screw dislocation model for BCC solid solution
alloys, which is suitable for dilute solute to high-entropy alloys,
based on the Suzuki model. [19] Curtin et al. believe that unlike
low-temperature element BCC metals and dilute BCC alloys,
the strength of multicomponent BCC high-entropy alloys is
not controlled by the nucleation mechanism of kinking. On
the contrary, spiral dislocations naturally adopt a kinked struc-
ture as the minimum total energy con ﬁguration in the ﬁeld of
random alloy atoms. On this basis, Curtin et al. derived the char-
acteristic length and energy scale of low-energy twisted screw dis-
locations in random alloys, and obtained the characteristic
spacing of kink and cross kink, which depends on the kink for-
mation energy and the characteristic collective solute/screw dis-
location interaction energy parameters. The relationship between
the strength calculated by this model and temperature, strain
rate, and composition is analytical. By calculating the yield
strength of Fe-Si, Nb-Mo, Nb-W, and Ti-Nb-Zr based high-
entropy alloys with nondilute solutes with small errors compared
to experimental results, satisfactory results were obtained for pre-
dicting the strength of the aforementioned BCC-structured high-
entropy alloys. Therefore, it is reasonable to use this model to
predict the strength of quaternary Ti xZrVNb alloy in this article.
In order to explore a strengthening model that can accurately
describe the strength evolution of high-entropy alloys containing
Ti, this article focuses on Ti xZrVNb alloys with different compo-
sitions. For the convenience of subsequent description, we
respectively refer to these four alloys as Ti 1,T i 2,T i 3, and Ti 4
alloys. We comprehensively use the three solid solution strength-
ening models to calculate the strength of the alloys, analyze the
changes in model parameters with the change of alloy composi-
tion, and then discuss the reasons for the strength changes of the
alloys from different perspectives and the sources of error in cal-
culating the strength of the alloys using different models. Our
work hopes to provide reference for the strength evolution of
high-entropy alloys containing Ti and the selection of strength-
ening models.
2. Experimental Section
The raw materials used for Ti xZrVNb alloy were sponge tita-
nium, sponge zirconium, vanadium particles, and niobium
ﬂakes with a purity of over 99.9%. Using a high-vacuum noncon-
sumable arc melting furnace, the alloy was melted repeatedly ﬁve
times in order to achieve uniform composition. The alloy ingot
was homogenized at 1200 °C 12 h /C0 1 using a high-temperature
resistance furnace. In order to avoid oxidation of the alloy ingot,
it was vacuum sealed, and the homogenized ingot was cut into
tensile specimens using an electric discharge wire cutting
machine. The tensile samples with the cross section sizes of
2.0 /C2 1.5 mm and gauge length of 8.0 mm were cut out of the
recrystallized plates using electrodischarge machining. The sur-
face of the tensile samples was ground to a 2000-grit with SiC
article. The tensile tests were carried out on a CMT-5305GL uni-
versal electronic tensile testing machine with a strain rate of
1 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1 at room temperature. Five samples of each alloy
were used to determine the tensile properties, and the averaged
results were adopted.
The microstructures were characterized using the OLYMPUS-
PMG3 optical microscope (OM) and the Zeiss-Sigma scanning
electron microscope (SEM) coupled with electron backscattering
diffraction (EBSD), energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS), and
electron probe X-ray microanalyzer (EPMA). X-ray diffraction
was applied to identify phases in the alloys by using a
SmartLab (9) diffraction instrument with an incident beam of
Cu-Kα radiation. Scanning was performed from 20° to 100° at
a scanning rate of 5° min /C0 1. The compositions were measured
using the EPMA. The test results are shown in Table 1. In addi-
tion, the JEM-2100 F transmission electron microscope (TEM)
was used for characterizing sub-microstructures. The metallo-
graphic specimens were mechanically ground, polished, and
then etched with the 10%HF þ 30%HNO3 þ 60%H2O solution.
The SEM specimens were initially polished by 3000-grit SiC arti-
cle and then electrochemically polished for the ﬁnal surface clar-
iﬁcation using the solution composed of methanol, n-butanol,
ethylene glycol butyl ether, and perchloric acid (6.5:2:1:0.5) with
a direct voltage of 25 V at temperature of about 253 K. TEM speci-
mens were primarily punched to Φ3 mm plate sheets and then
mechanically ground to about 55 μm in thickness, followed by
twin-jet electropolishing using the solution of HClO 4:C4H10O:
CH4O = 6:35:59 at temperature of about 233 K.

4.1. Calculation of Lugovy Solid Solution Strengthening Model
The solid solution strengthening effect is manifested as the elas-
tic interaction between dislocation lines and solutes in single-
phase solid solutions, which is mainly determined by atomic size
mismatch and elastic modulus. Lugovy et al. [11] believe that the
main problem in calculating the solid solution strengthening of
multicomponent high-entropy alloys is the mismatch between
atomic size and shear modulus. There are many methods to cal-
culate the shear modulus and atomic size mismatch. [13,26– 29]
Based on the results summarized by Lugovy et al. [11] this study
selects two calculation methods that are most suitable for high-
entropy alloys in BCC structures. This method achieves satisfac-
tory results in calculating the yield strength of TiZrVNbCr series
alloys.[30]

4.1.3. Discussion on the Errors in Calculating the Yield Strength of
Alloys Using Three Strengthening Models
Figure 8 shows the variation of errors in calculating the yield
strength of alloys using three solid solution strengthening mod-
els. It can be seen from the ﬁgure that the errors in calculating
the yield strength of alloys using the three models are all within
8%. The error in calculating the yield strength of the alloy using
the Curtin model is relatively small, while the error in calculating
using the Lugovy and Curtin models is relatively large. As ana-
lyzed above, in the Lugovy model, the key parameters determin-
ing the yield strength of alloys are shear modulus mismatch η
and atomic size mismatch δ. The atomic size mismatch δ is simi-
lar to the unit cell volume mismatch ΔV in the Varvenne model,
but the shear modulus mismatch η is different from the param-
eters in the Varvenne model and Curtin model. The mechanism
by which shear modulus mismatch impedes dislocations is not
yet clear, so the calculation error in the Lugovy model is likely to
come from shear modulus mismatch. For the Varvenne and
Curtin models, they are both based on the calculation of energy
barriers to dislocations. The difference is that the Varvenne
model uses cell volume mismatch, shear modulus, Poisson ’s
ratio, and lattice constant to represent them, while the Curtin

5. Conclusion
1) After melting and homogenizing four types of Ti xZrVNb
alloys, solid solutions with a single BCC structure were obtained.
2) For Ti xZrVNb alloy, as /C2 increases, the strength of the alloy
gradually decreases, and the plasticity of the alloy gradually
increases. However, the magnitude of the strength decrease
varies from slow to rapid; Ti 2ZrVNb alloy has a yield strength
of 1050 MPa and good plasticity greater than 9%, making it
the alloy with the best strength plasticity matching. 3) The
strength of four alloys was calculated using the Lugovy,
Varvenne, and Curtin solid solution strengthening models.
The results showed that for Ti xZrVNb alloys, all three models
can predict the yield strength of alloys well with an error of less
than 8%; compared to other models, these models have smaller
computational errors, among which the Varvenne model has a
relatively small error.
