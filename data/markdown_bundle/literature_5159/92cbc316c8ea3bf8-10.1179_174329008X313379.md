Technology update
Molybdenum alloys for high temperature
applications in air
P. Je´hanno*1,M .B o¨ning2, H. Kestler2, M. Heilmaier3, H. Saage3 and M. Kru¨ger3
Molybdenum base silicide alloys
exhibit promising oxidation resistance
in addition to the inherent high
temperature strength of refractory
metals. However, alloys with sufficient
oxidation resistance are effectively
brittle up to temperatures above 816 uC
(1500uF). Recent progress in alloy and
process development, utilising a PM
manufacturing route with mechanical
alloying as a crucial step, has allowed
significant improvement of both
oxidation resistance and mechanical
properties via micro-alloying additions
including nano-dispersed second phase
oxide particles.
The temperature capability of metallic
materials in the hottest sections of
turbines is a factor limiting the
efﬁciency improvement in aero engines
as well as stationary turbines. Current
nickel base superalloys operate at
homologous temperature of around
0.9T
m, which is typically around
1400uC. Thus, further improvements
with this class of materials will be
incremental. Such strategies as active
cooling and thermal barrier coatings
have been successfully implemented
to lower the metal temperature and
hence to improve the thermodynamic
efﬁciency. The main drawback of active
cooling lies in the decrease of the air
available for motion and therefore in the
air bypass ratio.
1
A revolutionary increase of turbine
efﬁciency requires a substantial
improvement of base metal
temperature capability. It is, thus,
conceivable that one could take
advantage of the outstanding intrinsic
properties of refractory metals and
alloys (melting point .2000uC, good
creep strength), assuming sufﬁcient
oxidation resistance can be provided.
While it is accepted that
molybdenum base alloys suffer from
severe oxidation problems in air (known
as ‘pesting’
2), Nowotny et al. 3 and
Berczik4,5 were the ﬁrst to point out
that three-phase alloys based on a Mo
solid solution (for room temperature
toughness and strength) and the
intermetallic compounds Mo
5SiB2 (T2
phase) and Mo 3Si (for oxidation
resistance at high temperatures) might
fulﬁl the requirements set by Dimiduk
and Perepezko.
1
Objectives
The work described here is part of the
European ULTMAT project, funded
within the 6th European Framework
Programme (www.ultmat.onera.fr).
The focus is to provide a sound
technology base for the introduction of
innovative Mo and Nb base silicide
multiphase alloys, with improved high
temperature capabilities relative to
current Ni base single crystal
superalloys. The ﬁrst step has been to
deﬁne a suitable alloy composition with
balanced low and high temperature
mechanical properties and oxidation
resistance. The strategy used to select
the Mo base silicide alloy composition
and the results of the basic
characterisation are presented below.
A literature study showed that (1)
decreasing the ductile to brittle
transition temperature (DBTT) and (2)
improving the oxidation resistance
while (3) keeping adequate high
temperature strength is of paramount
importance. The strategy was to use a
PM processing route to obtain a ﬁne
grained material and low solubility of
silicon in molybdenum, to improve
ductility, while oxide particle additions
should improve the high temperature
mechanical properties and oxidation
resistance (by oxygen trapping at the
grain boundaries). Lanthanum and
yttrium oxides (commercially known as
ML and MY
6) were selected due to
their thermodynamic stability at high
temperatures and their well known
effect on the ductility, workability and
machinability of pure molybdenum.
Experimental
Borosilicide molybdenum alloys with
the baseline composition Mo–8 .9Si–
7.7B have been produced by the
following routes:
N Route 1: gas atomisation, a standard
PM route for molybdenum alloys at
Plansee,
6 followed by a rapid
solidification (RS) processing step,
as suggested by Berczik. 4 Elemental
powders were blended and
subsequently cold pressed at
2000 bar. Sintering was carried out
in dry hydrogen at 1800 uC for 5 h
before inert gas atomisation via the
electrode induction gas atomisation
(EIGA) process. Subsequently, the
atomised powders were canned and
consolidated by hot isostatic
pressing (hipping) at 1500 uC for 4 h.
N Route 2: here, the RS step was
substituted by mechanical alloying
(MA) of elemental powders (plus a
0.1% addition of Y
2O3 or La 2O3
powder) to produce homogeneous
powders with a supersaturated Mo
solid solution (Mo
ss). Details of the
MA are given elsewhere. 7 Further
processing was essentially as
described above.
Billets with a total weight of
approximately 15 kg were
electrodischarge machined (EDM)
before turning, milling and grinding to
prepare samples. The machining was
performed with standard machines and
tools. Ultrasonic evaluation indicated the
samples were crack free before testing.
The microstructures of the samples
were characterised by X-ray diffraction
(XRD, Siemens D5000) with Cu K
a
radiation and SEM-EDX (JEOL 6400
SEM and FEI XL30 FEG equipped with
EDX and EBSD). Chemical analysis of
the main alloy constituents was carried
out using inductively coupled plasma
optical emission spectrometry (ICP-
OES). Analysis of contaminant (mainly
gaseous) species was realised by hot

Results
Microstructure
Gas atomised MoSiB
The microstructure of the material in
the consolidated state was typically
characterised by a dark phase
surrounding globular light particles and
dendrites (Fig. 1, bottom). It is
noteworthy that micrographs of single
powder particles (Fig. 1, top) show
essentially the same microstructure,
phase and size relations as material
after hipping at 1500 uC, indicating the
extraordinary thermal stability of the
rapidly solidiﬁed microstructure.
However, this temperature seems to
come close to the upper limit of heat
treatment without unacceptable
microstructural change, since annealing
at 1600 or 1700 uC has been reported to
lead to noticeable microstructural
coarsening.

Silicon alloying of pure molybdenum has
been reported

Hence, a substantial effect is expected
on lowering the DBTT (see below).
Mechanical properties
All the alloys were characterised by
extremely high compression yield
strengths (Fig. 3), resulting from the
superimposed effects of silicon solid
solution strengthening and load transfer
to intermetallic phases. It should be
noted, though not shown here, that a
signiﬁcant discrepancy between
compression and tensile yield stresses
is observed. This is explained by the
difference in coefﬁcient of thermal
expansion between molybdenum and
the intermetallic phases (l
Mo,lIM),12,13
leading to internal stresses on cooling
from the processing (usually hipping)
temperature that are superimposed on
the applied stress and lead to premature
failure during tensile loading. Therefore,
strengths measured in compression are
usually higher than those in tension.
The atomised material exhibits a
higher yield stress at temperatures
,1000uC, possibly due to the larger solid
solution strengthening effect of the
increased silicon content in the
molybdenum phase. However, the gap
between MA and atomised materials
strengths decreases with increasing
temperature, suggesting that load
transfer from the molybdenum to the
intermetallic phases may be the
controlling parameter for strength above
the DBTT of the Mo
ss phase (measured
for a silicon content of 1 .5% between
700 and 1000 uCb yS t u r met al.14).
The DBTT was determined from
compression tests. All tests of ductile
samples were stopped after 10%
deformation, which was believed to be
sufﬁcient to discern whether the
material deforms plastically. The gas
atomised alloy could be deformed
plastically only at >1200uC (Fig. 3). No
plastic deformation was observed at
(900uC. The MA MoSiB alloys (with
either Y or La oxide additions) exhibited
,3% plastic deformation at 650 uC and
.10% at higher temperatures. It was
concluded that the DBTT had been
decreased by ,200 K through use of
the speciﬁc PM processing route 2 to
obtain an optimised microstructure.

Conclusion
The feasibility of a PM process to
manufacture molybdenum base silicide
alloys has been demonstrated on an
industrial scale. Characterisation of the
mechanical properties showed that the
chosen PM route (MA of Mo, Si and B
elemental powders with 0.1% Y or La
oxide additions, followed by consolidation
and hipping) results in improved oxidation
resistance and a reduction of the DBTT,
while retaining the compressive (high
temperature) strengths comparable to
the state of the art alloy.
Hypotheses for the mechanisms
leading to better oxidation resistance
were formulated by analogy with the
effect of rare earth oxide additions in
steel: lowering oxygen diffusion at the
grain boundaries and crack trapping
within the oxide scale. Further work will
characterise the oxidation mechanisms in
more detail and explore the high
temperature creep and fatigue behaviour.
Further work towards industrial
implementation within ULTMAT is
planned to deﬁne joining and machining
parameters for the manufacture of real-
scale demonstrators of the selected alloy.
