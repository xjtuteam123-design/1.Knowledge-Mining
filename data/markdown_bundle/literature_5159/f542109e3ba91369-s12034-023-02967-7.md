Study on the relationship between TCP phase and AlN phase
in a fourth-generation single-crystal superalloy
ZHEZHU LAO 1,* , QINGQING DING 1, HONGBIN BEI 1, ZE ZHANG 1, HUA WEI 2
and JIXUE LI 1
1 School of Materials Science and Engineering, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou 310027, China
2 Center of Hypergravity Experimental and Interdisciplinary Research, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou 310027, China
*Author for correspondence (zzlao@zju.edu.cn)
MS received 4 July 2022; accepted 7 March 2023
Abstract. The enhanced mechanical properties of Ni-based single-crystal superalloys induced by refractory elements
such as Re, Wo, Mo, etc. are essential for the manufacture of advanced aero engines’ turbine blades. Whereas these
elements could promote the formation of the topologically close-packed (TCP) phases, and their growth would have a
deleterious effect on the creep rupture life of single-crystal superalloys. Concomitantly, the formation of the brittle phase
of the internal nitrides, especially the aluminium nitride (AlN), would be detrimental to superalloys as well. Nonetheless,
the formation relationship between AlN and TCP phases has maintained opaque. In this study, the relationship between
the TCP phase and AlN phase was analysed by microscopic methods, such as BSE, TEM and EDS. An AlN phase located
near the TCP phase in the deep surface of the superalloy was found, and its formation was suggested to be related to
nitrogen penetration during the thermal exposure. This accompanying growth of the AlN phase and TCP phase in the deep
surface could provide a new perspective on the formation mechanism of these brittle phases in the Ni-based single-crystal
superalloys.
Keywords. Ni-based single-crystal superalloy; TCP phase; AlN; microstructure; SEM; TEM.
1. Introduction
Refractory elements at high concentration are frequently
added into Ni-based single-crystal superalloys in order to
obtain higher temperature capability, and such superalloys
have been widely used as irreplaceable materials for turbine
blades in aerospace jet engines due to their comprehensive
properties, including excellent mechanical properties, oxi-
dation and corrosion resistance at elevated temperatures
[1–4]. However, the disadvantage of these refractory ele-
ments is that they may facilitate the formation of brittle
topologically close-packed (TCP) phases, which have a
deleterious effect on the creep rupture life of single-crystal
superalloys [5–10].
Since nitrogen and oxygen are the major components of
the air, it is worthwhile to note that the oxidation and
nitridation of superalloys are unavoidable when exposed at
elevated temperatures [ 11–17]. Chromia scales have been
observed to transport nitrogen, allowing the formation of Cr
nitrides (mainly Cr
2N) [18–21]. In addition, as service time
increases or if the alloy does not belong to the ‘alumina
former’ group, cracks spalling would present in the oxide
and the oxide scale would lose the protective property
[11,22,23]. Thus, nitrogen can penetrate the alloy, causing
nitridation of Ni-based single-crystal superalloys [ 11,22],
the mechanisms and kinetics of which were proposed in
detail by Krupp and Christ [ 22–26]. Among the stable ni-
trides, AlN is the most common nitride, since Al is the base
alloying element and its nitrides are detrimental to super-
alloys for several critical reasons. First, nitrides formed
during internal nitridation are brittle phases and may induce
stress concentration; and the mismatch of thermal and
elastic properties between nitride particles and the matrix
can cause internal stresses of the alloy in the near-surface
zone [ 22,27]. Second, the formation of Al and Ti nitrides
corresponds to high consumption of c
0 phase former ele-
ments, resulting in a depletion of c0 phase and a formation
of c0-free zone near surface [ 26]. Since the c0 phases con-
tribute to the precipitation strengthening of Ni-based single-
crystal superalloys, the formation of nitrides can cause a
decrease in the strength of the alloy substrate.
Previous studies have suggested that the formation
mechanism of AlN could be brieﬂy described as N pene-
trating into the alloy and reacting with Al [ 22,26].
Nonetheless, in this study, the relationship between AlN and
TCP phases in a fourth-generation Ni-based single-crystal
superalloy was elucidated, which should shed a new light on
the formation of AlN.

2. Experimental
The average composition (in wt.%) of the experimental
Ni-based single-crystal superalloy is 8Co, 7W, 6.6Ta, 6.2Al,
4.7Re, 3.4Cr, 2.9Ru, 2.6Mo and bal. Ni, which was measured
by energy disperse spectroscopy (EDS). The as-cast single-
crystal alloy bars were directionally solidiﬁed along [110]
direction with a conventional spiral crystal selector. Solution
heat treatment (1345/C176C/10 h) was performed after casting to
improve the uniformity of alloy composition and a two-step
aging treatment (1120 /C176C/4 h ? 870/C176C/32 h) was subse-
quently performed to obtain typicalc/c
0 dual-phase structure.
Slices with a thickness approximately 0.5 mm were cut from
the single-crystal alloy bar by electric discharge machine
(EDM). These slices are perpendicular to [110] and [111]
directions, which is suitable for TCP phase observation.
Samples were thermally exposed at 1100 /C176Cf o r1 0 ,3 0a n d
140 h, followed by water quenching. Heat treatments were all
performed in a tube furnace.
Samples for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were
ground to 2000-grit SiC paper and mechanically polished in
a vibration polishing machine to mirror the surface. Sam-
ples for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were
mechanically thinned to around 40 lm and two-jet electron-
polished in a solution containing 5% perchloric and 95%
alcoholic at –30/C176C( ± 5/C176C). A dual beam focused ion beam
(FIB) system was used to prepare TEM samples as well and
observe the cross-section sample. Electron backscattered
diffraction (EBSD) technique was used to determine the
crystallography orientations. SEM imaging was performed
on a FEI Quanta 650 FEG equipped with a secondary
electron detector, a backscattered electron (BSE) detector,
an EBSD detector and an EDS detector. Scanning trans-
mission electron microscopy (STEM) and EDS analyses
were undertaken on a spherical aberration corrected
(C
s-corrected) FEI Titan G2 80-200 ChemiSTEM, equipped
with a super-X EDS detector, a high-angle annular dark
ﬁeld detector and an ADF detector.

4. Conclusion
In summary, the relationship between the TCP phase and
AlN phase in a fourth-generation Ni-based single-crystal
superalloy has been studied by means of BSE, TEM and
EDS analyses. The TCP phase precipitated very fast at
1100/C176C thermal exposure, which leads to a depletion of
refractory elements and an enrichment of Al element.
Simultaneously, nitrogen penetration during the thermal
exposure process made the AlN phase form near the TCP
phase in the deep surface. Subsequently, the formation of
the AlN phase can result in an Al-depletion region forming
nearby, with sufﬁcient refractory elements remaining.
Either way, the AlN phase and TCP phase are accompa-
nying each other if formed in the deep surface of the
experimental alloy. These results provide a new perspective
on the formation of AlN and TCP phases, which could shed
new light on the optimization of single-crystal superalloy
design.
