Abstract 
An extraordinary high strength Mg-8Al-0.5Zn alloy has been developed through 
extrusion at a low temperature of 200 °C and a slow speed of 0.07 mm/s. The as-extruded 
alloy exhibited a tensile yield strength of 403 MPa, an ultimate tensile strength of 437 MPa, 
an elongation of 10.7%, and a low tension-compression yield asymmetry of 0.97. These 
superior mechanical properties are attribute d to the combined effect of ultra-fine 
recrystallized grains, numerous nano-scale Mg
17Al12 precipitates, and strong basal texture. 
Keywords: Magnesium alloys; Extrusion; Dynamic recrystallization; Precipitation; Tensile 
properties

1. Introduction 
With the increasing demand for light-weight transport vehicles, there have been 
numerous studies into the development of high strength magnesium (Mg) alloys via various 
methods, including the addition of rare-earth elements (RE) and severe plastic deformation 
(SPD). It has been recently reported that Mg-Gd-Y based wrought alloys [1-3] exhibit a very 
high strength, with a tensile yield strength (TYS) of 454-575 MPa, an ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS) of 469-600 MPa, and an elongation (EL) of 1.3-8.0%. However, the necessary 
addition of more than 10 wt.% of Gd and Y inevitably leads to a high cost. Other studies [4-7] 
have succeeded in producing high-strength Mg alloys (347-480 MPa TYS, 405-525 MPa 
UTS, and 2-12% EL) by refining the grain size of commercial Mg-Al-Zn alloys through SPD 
processes such as equal channel angular extrusion (ECAE)
/g3[4], high ratio differential speed 
rolling (HRDSR) /g3[5], accumulative roll bonding (ARB) /g3[6], or multi-directional forging 
(MDF)/g3[7]. However, these SPD processes have also proven difficult to commercialize due to 
the difficulties in making them a continuous process, as well as the inherent constraints on the 
size of the material. Consequently, there is a great need for a manufacturing method for 
high-strength Mg alloy that utilizes RE-free commercial alloys and conventional extrusion or 
rolling processes. 
The microstructural and mechanical properties of extruded materials are greatly 
affected by not only the alloy composition and initial billet condition, but also extrusion 
conditions such as the temperature, speed, and ratio. The size of the dynamically 
recrystallized (DRXed) grains of an extruded material is strongly dependent upon the 
deformation temperature. This means that if only the extrusion is reduced, then the

/g3
deformation-induced temperature increase is restricted to the deformation zone by the 
reduction in heat associated with friction and deformation during extrusion. This ultimately 
leads to a finer grain size [8]; and for this reason, a TYS of over 300 MPa has been achieved 
in RE-free extruded Mg alloys
/g3through slow extrusion at a rate of 0.1 mm/s [9, 10]. In 
addition, T.T. Sasaki [11] developed an Mg-9.8Sn-1.2Zn-1.0Al (wt.%) alloy with a TYS of 
308 MPa, a UTS of 354 MPa, and an EL of 12% via slow extrusion (0.1 mm/s) at a low 
temperature of 250 °C. This combination of low temperature and slow speed generates a 
greater number of dynamic precipitates that help restrain the growth of DRXed grains, 
thereby increasing the strength of the alloy. 
This study attempts to achieve a very high strength in the as-extruded state of a 
commercial Mg alloy by controlling the extrusion conditions; wherein a low temperature of 
200 °C and a slow speed of 0.07 mm/s were adopted to refine the microstructure. The 
underlying mechanisms behind the resulting strength characteristics are discussed in terms of 
the microstructural characteristics of the extruded alloy, with a particular focus on the DRXed 
grain size, precipitates, and texture. 
2. Experimental procedure 
A commercial AZ80 (Mg-8Al-0.5Zn-0.2 Mn in wt.%) alloy, which represents a 
common RE-free Mg alloy, was used in this study. Details of the billet casting procedure have 
been described elsewhere [12]; the as-cast billets being homogenized at 400 °C for 15 h, and 
then water-quenched. After pre-heating to 200 °C for 1h, a billet (Ø50 mm × 150 mm) was 
indirectly extruded at a temperature of 200 °C using a ram speed of 0.07 mm/s and an

3. Results and discussion 
Fig. 1a and b show the optical micrographs of the as-cast and homogenized AZ80 
specimens, respectively. We can see fro m this that the cast alloy consists predominantly of 
/g302-Mg grains, with a macro-segregation of the solute element and intermetallic compounds 
occurring mostly at the grain boundaries. After solid solution treatment, this 
macro-segregation and the intermetallic compounds almost disappears, with only a small 
quantity of particles remaining inside the equiaxed grains. Fig. 1c and d show an SEM image 
of homogenized AZ80, and the corresponding EDS analysis of the undissolved second phase 
(evident as bright regions), respectively. The relative amount (in atomic percent) of major 
elements in the precipitate is 9.32% Mg, 56.90% Al, and 33.78% Mn. This corresponds to an 
Al/Mn ratio of 1.68, which is very close to the stoichiometric ratio (1.6) of the Al
8Mn5
intermetallic phases commonly observed in Mg alloys. Slight deviations from the ideal are 
usually related to the /g302-Mg matrix and/or the particle size. In addition, both the intergranular

/g3
and intragranular Al8Mn5 particles share a similar chemical composition. 
The optical and SEM micrographs of longitudinal sections of as-extruded alloy are 
shown in Fig. 2a and b, respectively. This shows that the grain size of the homogenized alloy 
is significantly refined by extrusion, indicating that DRX does indeed occur during this 
process. Furthermore, the as-extruded alloy exhibits a bimodal microstructure consisting of 
equiaxed fine DRXed grains (the dark area in Fig. 2a) and elongated coarse unDRXed grains 
(the white area in Fig. 2a). This implies that LTSS extrusion leads to a partially DRXed 
microstructure (the volume fraction of DRXed grains is ~ 67%), because the DRX fraction 
decreases as the extrusion temperature and speed are reduced [13]. Moreover, undissolved 
Al
8Mn5 particles are found fractured and aligned along the extrusion direction (ED) during 
extrusion (Fig. 2b), but their area fraction is very small (~0.35%). Fig. 2c and d show SEM 
micrographs of the unDRXed and DRXed regions in Fig. 2a, respectively, in which few 
precipitates can be observed in the larger unDRXed grains (Fig. 2c). Meanwhile, a large 
number of fine precipitates are uniformly dispersed throughout the fine-grained DRXed 
region (Fig. 2d). This suggests that the fine precipitates suppress the growth of DRXed grains 
during extrusion, and that this pinning effect causes the refinement of DRXed grains. 
Fig. 3a and b respectively show bright and dark field TEM images taken from the 
DRXed region. It is evident that this region has an ultra-fine structure, with a grain size of 
~0.4 µm. Furthermore, the size of these DRXed grains is dependent on the outcome of 
competition between DRX and grain growth during hot extrusion [14]. That is, as the rate of 
grain growth after DRX increases at higher temperatures, the size of DRXed grains increases 
with increasing deformation temperature. Ben-Haroush et al.
/g3[14] showed that the grain size

that is developed during the hot extrusion of AZ80 at 2 mm/s increases from 4.62 to 7.11 µm 
as the extrusion temperature is increased from 250 to 350 °C. Compared to this previous 
study, the AZ80 extruded under LTSS conditions exhibits a much finer grain size of ~0.4 µm. 
The formation of these ultra-fine grains is thought to be attributed to not only a 
decrease in grain growth rate, but also to the aforementioned grain boundary pinning by 
dispersed precipitates. Given that precipitation in this instance relies on thermal activation, 
the volume fraction of precipitate is strongly dependent on the deformation temperature. It 
can therefore be expected that the maximum volume fraction of Mg
17Al12 precipitate will 
increase with decreasing temperature, especially when considering the Mg-Al phase diagram. 
Xu et al./g3[15] reported that the amount of Mg 17Al12 precipitate in an AZ91 alloy significantly 
increases with decreasing deformation temperature, thus resulting in an increased pinning 
effect. Ding et al. [16] have also confirmed that the volume fraction of Mg
17Al12 precipitate in 
AZ91 extruded at 300 °C is greater than that extruded at 360 °C, and that this increased 
fraction of precipitates promotes refinement of DRXed grains. As shown in Fig. 2b-d, 
numerous fine particles (50-150 nm in size) were precipitated in the DRXed region; these 
being mostly distributed around newly-formed DRXed grain boundaries, especially at triple 
junctions (Fig. 3). The selected area diffraction pattern in Fig. 3a confirms that these particles 
are of the expected Mg
17Al12 phase. The large number of nano-sized Mg 17Al12 particles that 
are dynamically precipitated during LTSS extrusion are considered likely to play a significant 
role in restricting the growth of the DRXed grains by pinning the grain boundaries, thereby 
leading to a remarkably refined grain structure. Additionally, dislocation pile-ups around 
these fine precipitates can act as nuclei for DRX [16, 17], and the activation of twinning at

interfaces [18], which also have a positive effect on grain refinement. 
The inverse pole figure (IPF) of the LTSS-extruded AZ80 is presented in Fig. 4a, 
while that of AZ80 extruded at a temperature of 250 °C using a ram speed of 1.3 mm/s is 
provided in Fig. 4b for comparison. Both alloys exhibit a basal fiber texture that is typical of 
extruded Mg alloys, in which the {0001} plane and <10-10> direction are preferentially 
oriented parallel to the ED. However, it is clearly seen that the maximum intensity (I
max) of 
the LTSS-extruded alloy (5.1) is much higher than that of the alloy extruded at a higher 
temperature and speed (1.7). Furthermore, this maximum intensity is also higher than that 
observed in extruded Mg-8Sn-1Al-1Zn (wt.%) (I max = 4.1) alloy [19], extruded AZ31 
(Imax=2.8) [19], and ECAEed AZ80 (I max = 4.1) [4], and it is similar to that of a high-strength 
extruded Mg-12Gd-3Y-0.6Zr (wt.%) alloy containing a large amount of RE elements (I max = 
5.0) [20]. This strong basal texture of the LTSS-extruded alloy is considered to originate from 
the substantial amount of unDRXed region (V f = ~33%), as unDRXed grains have a much 
stronger texture than DRXed grains in extruded Mg alloys [11, 21-23]. Since unDRXed 
grains with a strong basal texture are less favorably orientated for basal slip than DRXed 
grains under tension along the ED [10], they have a relatively low Schmid factor that also 
makes a substantial contribution to the tensile strengthening of the extruded alloy along the 
ED.
Fig. 5 shows the engineering stress-strain curves of the extruded AZ80 under both 
tension and compression; from which the TYS, UTS, and EL are found as 403 MPa, 437 MPa, 
and 10.7%, respectively. It is remarkable that a TYS of over 400 MPa has obtained in a

8/g3
/g3
commercial AZ80 alloy fabricated by conventional extrusion, and not SPD, and this value is 
substantially higher than the <300 MPa that has been previously reported for extruded AZ80 /g3
[18, 24, 25]. What is more, it is even superior to the severely deformed AZ80 alloys produced 
by DSR [26], MDF [27] or ECAE + forging [4] pr ocesses (Table 1). For instance, the AZ80 
produced by six passes of ECAE and subsequent forging exhibits excellent tensile properties 
of 374 MPa TYS, 434 MPa UTS, and 6% EL [4]; and yet the LTSS-extruded AZ80 shows a 
yield strength that is 29 MPa higher and an elongation that is 4.7% longer. In addition, the 
size of the DRXed grains in LTSS-extruded AZ80 (~0.4 µm) is much smaller than in wrought 
AZ80 (1-7 µm) (Table 1). Thus, the very high-strength of the LTSS-extruded alloy can be 
attributed to the combined effect of its ultra-fine DRXed grains, abundant nano-sized 
Mg
17Al12 precipitates, and strong ba sal texture. These results also demonstrate that the 
optimization of extrusion parameters such as temperature and speed can result in 
extraordinary high-strength Mg alloys, without the need for RE addition or SPD processing. 
Extruded Mg alloys typically exhibit a high tensile-compressive yield asymmetry, 
which can be evaluated by its yield point asymmetry ratio YR (CYS/TYS). Although 
as-extruded AZ80 generally exhibits weak asymmetric yield behavior compared to 
as-extruded AZ31 (YR = ~0.5) [28] and AZ61 (YR = ~0.6) [28], an obvious yield asymmetry 
still exits (YR = ~0.85) [28]. On the other hand, LTSS-extruded AZ80 has a very high YR 
value of 0.97, and is therefore nearly isotropic in terms of yielding. In wrought Mg alloys, 
yield asymmetry results from a difference in the predominant deformation mechanism; i.e., 
basal slip during tension and {10-12} twinning under compression. This asymmetry is known 
to be mainly affected by texture [29], grain size [30], and precipitates [31]. For example,

## Page 10

9/g3
/g3
strong texture causes a strong yield asymmetry by promoting the formation of {10-12} twins 
under compression. Conversely, a small grain size leads to a decrease in yield asymmetry by 
suppressing {10-12} twins under compression; the twinning stress increases more rapidly 
with decreasing grain size than the stress required for slip activation [30]. Numerous 
precipitates are also able to suppress the occurrence of twinning by preventing the motion of 
dislocation, and thus contribute to the decrease of yield asymmetry [32]. For this reason, the 
LTSS-extruded alloy, with its ultra-fine DRXed grains and plentiful Mg
17Al12 precipitates, 
shows a low yield asymmetry in spite of its relatively strong basal texture. 
4. Conclusions 
An extraordinarily high strength has been achieved in AZ80 alloy by the use of 
extrusion under conditions of a low temperature of 200 °C and a slow speed of 0.7 mm/s. 
This so-called LTSS extrusion leads to the formation of finer grains, more precipitates, and a 
stronger basal texture than conventional extrusion, which in turn results in an enhanced 
strength in the extruded alloy. Outstanding tensile properties of TYS = 403 MPa, UTS = 437 
MPa, and EL = 10.7% were achieved, along with an excellent compression/tension yield ratio 
of 0.97, all of which are significantly superior to those obtained by conventional extrusion. 
This high strength is attributed to the ultra-fine size (~0.4 µm) of the DRXed grains, the 
density of nano-sized (50-150 nm) Mg
17Al12 precipitates, and a strong basal texture of 5.1.
