ABSTRACT
To advance the understanding of metallic materials for structural components at cryogenic
temperatures, a review of previous studies on the mechanical properties of austenitic stainless
steels, titanium alloys, aluminum alloys, and copper alloys is presented herein. In particular,
the evaluation of strength and toughness balance intends to clarify key differences between
these alloys to facilitate better alloy design and potentially bolster further alloy development.
Several key points on cryogenic mechanical tests are also introduced. Temperature control,
testing tools, and test conditions should be properly calibrated to avoid experimental errors.
Furthermore, substantial progress for subsurface crack initiation in high-cycle fatigue at cryo-
genic temperatures is presented, in which the mechanism of subsurface crack generation is
highlighted as a key design consideration in microstructure of high-strength alloys.
Keywords
austenitic stainless steel, titanium alloy, aluminum alloy, copper alloy, strength, toughness,
high-cycle fatigue, cryogenic temperature
Introduction
Structural materials in various cryogenic systems must meet requirements in terms of their
mechanical and physical properties.1,2 In particular, high-strength and high-toughness alloys
that can withstand cryogenic temperatures are in demand for applications such as hydrogen
turbo pumps in rocket engines and superconducting magnet systems in linear motor trains
and fusion reactors. At cryogenic temperature, the alloys exhibit higher strength, higher
strain-rate dependence, enhanced heterogeneous deformation, and local yielding. As the
test temperature decreases below room temperature, the strength of the alloy increases.

Cryogenic Mechanical Tests
Here, several key points on cryogenic mechanical tests are noted, because the tests are inherently challenging.
Temperature control, testing tools, and test conditions should be properly calibrated to avoid experimental errors.
TEMPERATURE CONTROL
Tensile, Fracture Toughness, Fatigue, and Crack Propagation Tests
The boiling points of the selected gases and structural materials are summarized in figure 1.1 For the cryogenic
tensile, fracture toughness, fatigue, and crack propagation tests, the specimens are normally immersed in a coolant
such as liquid helium (4.2 K), liquid nitrogen (77 K), or cooled ethanol (200 – 273 K). Because heat transfer in
a liquid is a hundred times higher than that in gaseous helium, the specimen temperature can be held stable at the
boiling temperature while avoiding specimen temperature rise by serration in the tension and high-frequency
cyclic loading. 3 On the other hand, gaseous helium has the advantage of allowing control over the specimen
temperature at any point between 20 K and 100 K using a thermo sensor.

Charpy Impact Test
Liquid helium flow through a transfer tube can directly cool down and keep the Charpy impact test specimen at
4.2 K. Packing the specimen in a capsule made of Styrofoam (styrene foam) provides an easy way to handle test
samples and decreases the consumption of liquid helium. 4 Using this setup, the cooling time is approximately
1 min, and the consumption of liquid helium is less than 1 L.
Cryocooler and Helium Recondensation
Long-term continuous operation at liquid helium temperature is needed for the high-cycle fatigue test. A first-
generation refrigerating system equipped with a recondenser in the test machine cryostat was developed. 5 Then,
a second-generation system equipped with a cryocooler and helium recondenser was installed, which can be used
for fatigue tests in liquid helium, liquid nitrogen, and gaseous helium.
TESTING TOOLS AND METHODS
Jig Frame for Loading
In a test system with axial loading, such as tension and compression systems, a long pull rod and jig frame should be
designed to insulate the sample against heat transfer from outside.
1 This results in a testing system with low stiffness.
Therefore, to evaluate the stress – strain relationship in the yielding and low-strain regime, the strain of specimen
must be directly measured, in addition to displacement measurement. Furthermore, the thermal expansion coef-
ficients of jig materials and test specimen are different so that either tension or compression is applied to the test
specimen during cooling. The jig frame design should be fitted to the compliance correction in the test system.
Strain Measurement
Although there are a number of ways to measure strain mechanically and electrically, the majority of strain
measurements at cryogenic temperatures are carried out using strain gauges owing to their superior measurement
characteristics. Either strain gauge or extensometer is chosen to evaluate the strain of test specimen in tensile,
fracture toughness, fatigue, and crack propagation tests.
Strain Gauge
Strain gauges are used to ascertain the amount of deformation caused by strain.

Extensometer
An axial extensometer is a sensor attached to a specimen that measures a dimensional change (gauge length or

mercially available, for example, the ring-shaped (Shepic) clip gauge,

Strain Rate and Test Frequency
High-strength alloys show higher strain-rate dependence on tensile deformation at low temperatures. As described
by the stress – strain curves, the strain-rate dependence and stress relaxation are remarkable at cryogenic temper-
atures due to larger load-drop as well as higher flow stress, especially for titanium alloys.
8 Tsuchida et al. adopted the
Kocks– Mecking model to clarify the effects of temperature and strain rate on flow stress. 9 The crosshead-arresting
test was found to provide a flow curve at a strain rate of 10 −9s−1 that was apparently dependent on the test temper-
ature. This strain rate is within the engineering limit of strain detection. The athermal stress was much lower than
the flow curve at 10−9s−1 and was difficult to estimate from the stress relaxation behavior below room temperature.
Furthermore, serrated deformation in the flow curves appears in austenitic steels 10,11 and aluminum alloys12
at cryogenic temperature, and it causes a discontinuous specimen temperature rise. Strain rate and gauge length of
test specimen affect the serration.
Significant temperature rise occurs in specimen during cyclic loading at 4.2 K. The magnitude of temperature
rise increases with the increase of the test frequency and the strain range. 13,14 In order to suppress the specimen
temperature as low as possible, the test frequencies at 4.2 K are chosen as follows: In load-controlled fatigue tests,
for tension-tension mode, less than 5 Hz at a stress level of lower than yield strength and less than 1 Hz at a stress
level of higher than yield strength; for tension-compression mode, less than 1 Hz at a stress level of lower than
yield strength and less than 0.1 Hz at a stress level of higher than yield strength; and in strain-controlled fatigue
tests, less than 4 × 10
−3 s−1 at a higher strain level.
Simple Mechanical Test in High-Pressure Hydrogen Embrittlement
Hydrogen embrittlement is a key environmental effect that can influence mechanical properties at low temperatures.
For the mechanical tests using liquid hydrogen and compressed gaseous hydrogen, facilities built to explosion-proof
specifications should be installed. A simple mechanical testing method to evaluate hydrogen embrittlement in a
specimen has been developed. 15,16 The influence of high-pressure hydrogen gas up to 100 MPa on mechanical
properties can be evaluated at the temperature between 20 K and up to 800 K. Instead of using high-pressure
gas vessels, pressurized gases can be filled into a small hole inside the specimen to produce any desired high-pressure
hydrogen environments. The inner diameter of the hole is less than 2 mm (1 mm is sufficient). This method can be
applied to tensile tests, fatigue tests, and other uniaxial tests at cryogenic temperatures.
Strength and Toughness at Cryogenic Temperature
Reference guides for the cryogenic properties of materials are listed in the references. 17– 24 In particular, the
strength and toughness of austenitic stainless steels, titanium alloys, aluminum alloys, and copper alloys are men-

these alloys to facilitate better alloy design and potentially bolster further alloy development.

Austenitic steels are the structural materials of choice for high-field superconducting magnets that operate at 4.2 K
and are used in many structures that operate at liquid nitrogen and liquified natural gas temperatures as well. The
common austenitic stainless steels, such as type 304L (18Cr– 9Ni, mass %) and 316L (16Cr-10Ni-2Mo), are widely
used in cryogenic applications as listed in Table 1. However, their strength is relatively low, and they transform to
martensite when strained at low temperature. For structural applications at 4.2 K, therefore, nitrogen-strengthened
austenitic stainless 316LN (17Cr– 11Ni– 2Mo-0.2N) is commonly used for its high strength and thermodynamic sta-
bility. Their mechanical properties at 4.2 K have been widely studied, and its survey completed by the National

SUBSURFACE CRACK INITIATION
The mechanism of subsurface crack generation is highlighted as a key microstructure design consideration for
improving the high-cycle fatigue strength. A new explanation of microcrack growth, coalescence, or both can
explain the formation process of Stage I crack with a critical size in which the process of microcrack growth
involves a large number of cycles and is not characterized by instantaneous spread. Although the morphology
and size of subsurface crack initiation sites vary by stress level and subsurface crack origins, such as transgranular
cracks, intergranular cracks, nonmetallic particles, and segregation, the microcrack growth concept normally
provides a good solution.
Cyclic softening itself becomes a specific incompatibility at the grain boundaries due to planar slip in
weak grains.
60 Such heterogeneous microplasticity due to planar slip and a restricted system is considered
to play an important role in generating subsurface crack. The accumulated tensile stress on a specific
crystal plane may be responsible for the initial microcracking on the plane and the crack opening. The
microcrack or void in a “soft grain” also assists microcrack initiation in neighboring “hard grain” due to a multi-
axial stress state. Namely, a combination of the shear stress and tensile stress normal to the slip plane at the
boundary may trigger microcracking. To increase the high-cycle fatigue strength as shown in
figure 9,61 much
attention should be paid to using microstructural control to prevent microcrack growth and coalescence. The
microtexture assists the spontaneous microcrack growth, forming aligned facets and resulting in a longer
microcrack length. Therefore, the combination of microstructure refinement and the introduction of movable
dislocations on various types of slip systems results in a higher fatigue resistance to the subsurface mode
of failure.

Summary
Cryogenic mechanical tests and the mechanical properties of high-strength alloys at cryogenic temperatures were
reviewed. The references cited here may serve as a useful tool during initial mechanical design decision-making as
well as for further alloy development. Subsurface crack fatigue initiation in the high-cycle regime is very common
for high-strength alloys at cryogenic temperatures. To increase the high-cycle fatigue strength, much attention
should be paid to microstructural control to prevent microcrack growth and coalescence.
