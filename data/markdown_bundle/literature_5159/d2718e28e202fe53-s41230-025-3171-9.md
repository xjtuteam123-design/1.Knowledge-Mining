1 Introduction
TiAl-based alloys, as novel lightweight materials, 
exhibit high strength, low density, and outstanding 
corrosion resistance, rendering them an ideal choice 
for aerospace applications 
[1, 2]
. Specifically, TiAl alloy 
tubes are primarily utilized in the fabrication of aircraft 
structural components, including fuselage, engine parts, 
and landing gear. In the traditional casting process, the 
lamellar structure and grain size of TiAl alloy castings 
are coarse and difficult to control, thus showing poor 
mechanical properties 
[3-5]
. This study introduced a 
method for preparing high-performance TiAl alloy 
tubes using vacuum centrifugal casting technology.
Among precision casting techniques, centrifugal 
casting emerges as a well-established and prominent 
method 
[6, 7]
. This technique disrupts dendritic structures 
through the application of external forces and redistributes

Abstract: The grain size of TiAl alloy castings prepared by traditional casting process is coarse, thus showing 
poor mechanical properties. In this study, a new type of high performance Ti-46Al alloy tube prepared by vacuum 
centrifugal casting technology was introduced. This research comprehensively examined the influence of 
pouring time on the microstructure and mechanical performance of the castings, employing both experimental

As a specific composition of β-type γ-TiAl alloy, Ti-46Al 
alloy has significant advantages in light weight compared 
with other Ti-Al alloys. As a crucial parameter in centrifugal 
casting, the pouring time has a direct impact on the grain 
size and mechanical properties of the castings. However, 
there currently lacks systematic research on the influence of 
pouring time on the centrifugal casting process of Ti-46Al 
alloy. In this study, numerical simulations of Ti-46Al alloy 
castings under different pouring times were conducted using 
ProCAST software to analyze the solidification behavior 
of the melt under high gravity conditions. Multiple sets of 
castings with varying pouring times were prepared using a 
vacuum centrifugal casting apparatus. The microstructures, 
tensile properties and Vickers hardness of the castings were 
examined and tested at room temperature. The study results 
provide theoretical support and technical insights for vacuum 
centrifugal casting of Ti-46Al alloy.

3 Results and analysis
3.1 Simulation results 
The solidification structure of the casting was simulated by 
CAFE module in ProCAST software and the average grain 
size was counted. Figures 4(a), (b), and (c) describe the 
microstructure of the longitudinal section of the simulated 
alloys P1, P2 and P3, respectively. The extension of pouring 
time results in noticeable variations in grain growth behavior. 
As depicted in Fig. 4(a), the microstructure of the P1 alloy 
primarily consists of coarse columnar crystals growing in the 
radial direction. The microstructure of the P2 alloy is depicted 
in Fig. 4(b), revealing an outer layer composed of columnar 
crystals and an inner layer characterized by an equiaxed 
crystal region. Figure 4(c) portrays the microstructure of 
the P3 alloy, which entirely comprises fine equiaxed crystal 
regions. The pouring time reflects the change of pouring speed. 
When the time is reduced from 9 s to 3 s, the pouring speed is 
significantly improved, and the pouring volume per unit time 
increases significantly. This slows down the heat dissipation 
speed during the casting filling process, which is beneficial to 
the growth of columnar crystals.
Figure 5 shows the change curve of the midpoint temperature 
of the casting with the solidification time at different pouring 
times. In the early pouring process, the metal liquid is 
constantly flowing and filling, and the temperature in the cavity 
is unstable. After the mold filling is completed, the metal liquid 
fills the cavity, the heat in the melt is transferred to the mold, 
and the casting begins to solidify. The latent heat release during 
the solidification process causes the internal temperature 
of the metal change less, resulting in a more slowly initial 
temperature change. Thus, the temperature platform is 
generated, and the longer pouring time causes a longer 
temperature platform at the early pouring process. As time 
progresses, the internal temperature of the casting gradually 
decreases, leading to an increased the heat conduction rate and 
a higher rate of heat loss. This results in an accelerated cooling 
rate of the casting. As the heat is continuously distributed 
vertically from the mold wall, the metal crystallizes from 
the outside to the inside 
[24]
. Simultaneously, the centrifugal 
force enhances convective flow within the molten metal. In 
the case of the P1 casting, with a pouring time of only 3 s, the 
brief duration leads to diminished heat dissipation within the 
mold (as illustrated in Fig. 5), resulting in a slower growth 
rate of the Ti-46Al alloy microstructure. This phenomenon 
promotes the directional growth of columnar crystals in the 
Ti-46Al alloy. With the increase in pouring time, the alloy melt 
is exposed to the colder mold surface for a prolonged period, 
which accelerates the cooling rate, leading to grain refinement. 
Previous research 
[25]
 demonstrates that under higher growth 
rates, the transformation from columnar to equiaxed grains 
is more likely to occur. Therefore, when the pouring time 
extends to 6 s, the microstructure of the ingot displays an outer 
region of columnar grains and an inner region of equiaxed 
grains. Further increasing the pouring time to 9 s reduces the 
solidification duration of the Ti-46Al alloy, leading to an even 
faster cooling rate during solidification.

9 s, the liquid metal remains in the runner for a longer duration,

the mold. This, in turn, accelerates the solidification process of 
the casting. The metal liquid undergoes rapid cooling and 
crystallization during the solidification process, contributing to 
the formation of a finer grain structure.
As illustrated in Fig. 6(b), when the pouring time extends 
to 9 s, the simulated porosity in the P3 casting increases 
significantly to 13%, notably exceeding the porosity levels 
observed in the P1 and P2 castings. This issue arises due to an 
excessively low pouring rate, which results in an insufficient 
volume of molten metal filling the mold cavity. This inadequate 
metal filling disrupts proper mold filling, leading to premature 
solidification during the filling process. Subsequently, this 
can give rise to casting defects, including shrinkage, uneven 
thickness, rough inner surfaces, and incomplete mold filling.
By simulating the solidification behavior and calculating 
porosity using ProCAST software for Ti-46Al alloy obtained 
at different pouring times, a certain understanding of the 
microstructure of Ti-46Al alloy under various pouring times 
was gained. The following analysis would delve into the 
solidification behavior of Ti-46Al alloy under different pouring 
times through experiments.
3.2 Experiment results 
3.2.1 Effects of pouring time on microstructure 
Figure 7 illustrates the microstructures of the vacuum 
centrifugally cast Ti-46Al tubular specimens with different 
pouring times. It is observed that with an increase in pouring 
time, the microstructures evolve from coarse columnar crystals 
growing along the radial direction to gradually transforming 
into fine and uniform predominantly equiaxed grains, aligning 
well with the microstructures obtained through simulation 
(Fig. 4). Additionally, as evident from Fig. 7(c), there are 
numerous shrinkage cavities present in the microstructure of 
the P3 alloy.
During the solidification process of centrifugal casting, 
impurities are typically present, leading to heterogeneous 
nucleation and growth processes. Furthermore, longer pouring 
times improve the ability to break dendrites, further increasing 
the number of heterogeneous nucleation sites 
[12]
. As the 
nucleation rate increases, grain size decreases, leading to grain 
refinement.
Figure 8 displays the microstructure of P1, P2, and P3 alloys 
with different pouring times, revealing a full lamellar structure 
within the alloys. The microstructure of the alloy evolves as the 
pouring time increases. The P1 alloy exhibits columnar grains, 
whereas the P2 alloy poured within 6 s demonstrates a mixture 
of columnar and equiaxed grains. When the pouring duration is 
extended to 9 s in P3, the microstructure transitions to entirely 
equiaxed grains, which is consistent with the results described 
in the simulation. Additionally, it is observed that white particle 
inclusions are uniformly distributed within the alloy.

the centrifugally cast Ti-46Al microstructure in the previous

grain size, has a significantly high porosity rate. The excellent 
tensile strength displays by P1 and P2 alloys can mainly be 
attributed to their lower porosity rates, which reduces the 
generation of defect-induced cracks during the tensile process.

Figure 13 illustrates the Ti-46Al alloy fracture surfaces of 
room temperature tensile specimens at different pouring times. 
Owing to the lamellar microstructure, the fracture morphology 
exhibits alternating features of translamellar and interlamellar 
fractures. When cracks propagate parallel to the lamellar 
direction, they result in cleavage steps and river patterns on 
the fracture surface. Due to the oriented nature of lamellar 
colonies, different-oriented lamellar colonies exhibit distinct 
fracture patterns, primarily categorized as transgranular
and intergranular fractures 
[21]
. Figure 13(b) displays the 
characteristics of translational and interlamellar fractures. 
Cracks primarily initiate at the grain boundaries due to the 
change in lamellar orientation within the columnar crystal, 
extending in a translational manner. When cracks encounter a 
lamellar interface during propagation, deflection and bridging 
lead to the formation of a tear edge on the fracture surface. As 
depicted in Fig. 13(d), with an extension of the casting time to 
6 s, translamellar fractures become the dominant mode, and the 
number of tear ridges increases significantly, impeding crack 
extension. At the same time, specimen hinders the further 
motion of other dislocations and induces the passivation of 
crack tip, thus improving fracture toughness 
[35]
. Consequently, 
the strength and ductility of the alloy witness a significant 
improvement. Figure 13(f) shows a mixed fracture pattern 
including fracture along grain boundaries and fracture along 
lamellar. It can be observed that there are a large number of 
shrinkage cavities and porosity in the diagram, which become 
the crack sources, resulting in a significant reduction in the 
strength and ductility of P3 alloy.
As shown in Fig. 14, the high temperature tensile fracture of 
P2 alloy consists of a continuous plane of disintegration and 
lamellar tearing edges. Figure 14 shows a crack with a width of 
about 2 μm, which propagates parallel to the lamellar interface 
and ends inside the lamellar interface. The crack passes through 
the harder layer during propagation, and its energy gradually 
decays and eventually runs out. Therefore, the as-cast alloy 
with more lamellar structures can still achieve higher ultimate 
tensile strength. In summary, the fracture mechanism of the 
as-cast alloy after high temperature tensile is also a mixed 
mode of cleavage fracture and lamellar fracture.
4 Conclusion
A high-performance Ti-46Al alloy tube was produced using 
the vacuum induction melting centrifugal casting method. The 
microstructure and mechanical properties of centrifugally cast 
alloys under different pouring times were investigated, and the 
summarized results are as follows: 
During the centrifugal casting process, the microstructure 
of Ti-46Al alloy changes significantly with the change of 
pouring time. The increase of pouring time leads to the 
increase of cooling rate of the alloy, which in turn affects the 
ratio of columnar crystals in the structure. When the pouring 
time reaches 9 s, the whole alloy structure is transformed into 
equiaxed crystal. However, due to the increase of shrinkage
