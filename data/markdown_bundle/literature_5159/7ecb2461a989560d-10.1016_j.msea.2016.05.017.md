abstract
In the present study, the effects of Be content on the microstructure and mechanical properties of hot
rolled ZrBe binary alloys were investigated. Microstructures, i.e. the phases present in the alloys, were
characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD), optical microscopy (OM), scanning electron microscopy
(SEM), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). At the same processing temperature, the volume
fraction of
α' martensite and Be 2Zr compound gradually increases but the volume fraction of α phase
decreases with increasing Be content. This can be attributed to the low solubility of Be in Zr alloys and
the decrease of α to β phase transition temperature. Simultaneously, the average Be 2Zr particles size
shows an increasing trend with adding Be content. With increasing Be content, the tensile strength
increases but ductility decreases. The alloy with 1.0 wt% Be possessed highest ultimate tensile strength
(892 MPa) with an elongation of 7.06%. The strengthening and toughening mechanisms of hot-rolled
ZrBe alloys were also discussed.

elements play an important role controlling phase compositions and
volume fraction[7,8]. For instance, phase composition changes as

alloys. Furthermore, Mohamed Abdel-Hady et al.[10] reported that Zr
and/or Nb addition to Ti alloys can obviously enhance the stability ofβ
phase. On the other hand, the mechanical properties can also be im-
proved by adding alloying elements through solid solution strength-
ening, dispersion strengthening, and changing alloy phases. Luan et al.
[1 1]investigated the effects of boron on the fracture behavior and
ductility of cast Ti6Al4V alloys. It was mentioned that a small amount
of boron additions is suf ﬁcient in improving the ductility. Grain re-
ﬁnement and the enhancement of grain-boundary cohesion by adding
boron element are considered to be the mechanism. Hsu et al. [12]
reported that Zr –Ti binary alloys showed a strong solid solution
strengthening response.
Hot-working processing, such as hot rolling and hot forging, can
eliminate shrinkage cavity, solute segregation and other defects[13].
In addition, hot-working processingcan cause tailored microstructure,
such as equiaxed or duplex structure, through tuning phase compo-
sition and precipitation. David [14] strongly recommended equiaxed
microstructure for biomedical applications in Ti alloys. Lindemann
et al. [15] have also reported that duplex microstructures can be
produced in Ti–6Al–7Nb using a variety of thermo-mechanical treat-
ments. Notably, equiaxed and duplex structures are responsible for
excellent mechanical properties of Ti/Zr alloys. Therefore, it is very
useful to develop a series of high performance Zr alloys by using
thermal processing techniques.
Previous study[16] has shown that the addition of Be in as-cast Zr
alloys is effective in the reﬁnement of microstructure and promoting
mechanical properties. However, composition segregation phenom-
enon is very serious in the as-cast Zr alloys. Simultaneously, the me-
chanical properties are restricted due to the existence of the casting
defects. Thus, microstructure and mechanical properties of Zr alloys
with respect to Be addition need to be studied where deformation is
included in processing. The present investigation will focus on the
effects of Be content on the phase compositions, microstructure, and

3. Results and discussion
Tensile stress-strain curves and a summary of the tensile properties of hot
rolled ZrBe alloys are shown in Fig. 1 and Table 1, respectively. Hot rolled Zr0.25Be
sample exhibits lowest yield strength ( s0.2) of 571 MPa, ultimate strength ( sb)o f
672 MPa and highest elongation ( ε) of 13.87%. With increasing Be content from
0.25 wt% to 1.0 wt%, s0.2 and sb increase but ε decreases. After adding 1.0 wt% Be
content, the strength dramatically increases to s0.2 /C24 732 MPa and sb /C24 892 MPa
accompanied with considerably reduced ductility of ε/C24 7.06%. Fig. 2 shows the
fracture surfaces of the hot rolled ZrBe alloys after tensile tests. Small dimples with
different sizes, which are responsible for a typical plastic fracture, can be seen in
Zr0.25Be alloy sample ( Fig. 2 (a)). The feature is consistent with good tensile duc-
tility. However, river-shaped pattern and defects appeared in the fracture surface
when Be content is more than 0.5 wt% ( Fig. 2 (c–d)). These characteristics are re-
sponsible for the brittleness of the specimen, which can con ﬁrm the decrease in
ductility.
In order to reveal the factors governing the tensile properties of the hot rolled
ZrBe alloys with different Be contents, phase compositions and microstructure
were investigated by XRD analyses, OM, SEM, and TEM observations. Fig. 3 shows
the XRD patterns of the ZrBe alloys with various Be contents. As shown in Fig. 3, the
studied ZrBe alloys consist mainly of
α/α' phase. Generally, α phase contains coarse
lath and/or equiaxed grains. However α' phase, quenched martensite exhibits ﬁne
lath morphology. Jing et al. [17] showed that the thickness of the lamellar α' phase
is less than α phase. However, both α phase and α' martensite exhibited the same
hexagonal close-packed hexagonal (HCP) structure and diffraction peaks in XRD
patterns. Except
α/α' phase, several small peaks near 2 θE26.9°,2 7 . 5°, and 38.9 °
were clearly observed in XRD patterns. Obviously, the intensity of the peaks in-
creased with increased Be content. The small peaks were identi ﬁed to be the ( ̅ )1010
plane, (0001) plane, and ( ̅ )1011 plane of Be 2Zr compound phase. Fig. 4 shows the
OM microstructures of the hot rolled ZrBe alloys with different Be contents. After
hot rolling treatment, the dendritic equiaxed prior-
β grain morphology became
slightly elongated along the rolling direction (RD). From Fig. 4 , one can see that a
large number of Be 2Zr particles exist at the prior- β grain boundaries. This can be
explained by a large amount of Be 2Zr compounds precipitated and enriched at the
prior-β grain boundaries during the solidi ﬁcation process [18]. In addition, one can
see that the number of Be 2Zr compound increased with increasing Be content.
Fig. 5 represents the hot rolled SEM micrographs of the studied alloys with various
Be contents. A large number of coarse lath and equiaxed grains of α phase can be
observed in hot rolled Zr0.25Be alloys ( Fig. 5 (a)). However, Fig. 6 [16] has already
showed clearly that regular lath-shaped α phase exhibits in as-cast ZrBe alloys. In
this work, rolling temperature (850 °C) of ZrBe alloys was in αþβ phase region
based on the Zr-Be binary diagram [19]. Both lath-like α phase and β phase exist in
the ZrBe alloys during heat preservation before rolling. In the subsequent rolling
process, elongated
α phase with lath-like structure along the RD is obtained due to
extrusion pressure. Furthermore, a portion of α phase was broken under the action
of rolling extrusion ( Fig. 5). Meanwhile, a large number of distortion energy can be
stored within the deformed specimen [20]. In the process of heat preservation in
the middle of the two rolling, the broken α grain turned into equiaxed grains as
stored energy is released. Numerous experimental and theoretical works indicated
that the stored energy in a deformed specimen is an important metallurgical
factor during spheroidization process [21,22]. A similar result was observed in the

Although all alloys are treated at the same hot rolling temperature, α' martensite in
the alloy with smaller Be content and higher transition temperature undergoes
transformation and growth more easily. By applying the Hall-Petch formula (i.e.,
s¼s

and d is the lath width of α' martensite), the s0.2 of the ZrBe alloy increases with

inferred that α' martensite can improve the tensile strength but reduce the duc-
tility. According to the previous studies [25,26], α' martensite is a hard phase
compared with other common phases in Zr/Ti alloys, such as α, β, and ω phases.
Hence, the formation of α' martensite increases the yield strength and tensile
strength but decreases the elongation. Hsu et al. [12] have also proved that α' phase
in Zr –10Ti alloy possesses highest hardness and bending strength. In this in-
vestigation, the fα' of the alloys gradually increases with adding Be content ( Fig. 7).
Thus, Zr1.0Be alloy comprising mainly of α' martensite should show maximum
yield strength and tensile strength. Liang et al. [9] have also reported similar results
for TiZrAlV alloy.
In the studied alloys, the obvious differences in mechanical properties are not
only due to the presence of α phase and α' martensite but also due to the presence
of Be 2Zr compounds. The size, content, and distribution of the compounds greatly
affect the mechanical properties of the alloys. As shown in Fig. 7 , the content of
Be2Zr compound increases with increasing Be content. During tensile test, the
movement of dislocations can be hindered by the existence of the hard compound
particles According to Orowan mechanism [27], the force needed to overcome the
barrier by a dislocation signi ﬁcantly increases with the increased resistance. Due to
the interaction between the compound particles and dislocations, the strength of
the alloys can be improved signi ﬁcantly. In addition, it is believed that the size of
the second particles signi ﬁcantly affect the tensile properties. Fig. 9 shows the size
