abstract
Nickel-based superalloys have been widely used in applications that require mechanical and corrosion
resistance at high temperatures. Generally, the main problem in some applications such as pressurized
water reactors is the hydrogen embrittlement (HE). The HE phenomenon has been extensively studied
in several superalloys and other materials. Efforts have focused on improving the performance of Ni
based alloys against the main mechanisms associated with HE, developing new strategies such as inter-
granular precipitation to counteract HE. In the present work the aim was to study the hydrogen diffusion
(HD) in Ni-based experimental alloys manufactured by melting and hot rolling processes. Two Inconel
commercial and three experimental Ni-based alloys were studied, considering the Cr content (%wt) as
the main variable element. The alloys were characterized by optical microscopy (OM), scanning electron
microscopy (SEM), Vickers microhardness test and X-ray diffraction (XRD). On the other hand, the effec-
tive diffusion coefﬁcient (D
eff) and steady state ﬂux (J ss) were determined under the ASTM G-148 stan-
dard conditions. A slight hydrogen mitigation effect was found due to the Cr content and
microstructure. The aforementioned results agree with the literature and a recent proposal for mitigating
HD as a function of the irreversible traps density.

1. Introduction
The superalloys have several applications that demand excel-
lent properties at high temperatures such as turbine components,
chemical and petrochemical processing, pressurized water reac-
tors, oil and gas industries, among others [1–4]. In working condi-
tions, these materials are exposed to several aggressive
environments, for instance the hydrogen. The hydrogen is the
smallest element and has been considered as a potential energy
carrier, providing an alternative to the use of fossil fuels [4]. How-
ever, in materials it can dissolve through the matrix causing seri-
ous problems, in particular hydrogen embrittlement (HE) [1–4].
The HE phenomenon has been widely studied using microstruc-
tural characterizations, simulations, and mathematical modeling
from one of the ﬁrst detections made by Johnson in 1875 to the
recent proposal for mitigation of HE by grain boundary precipita-
tion reported by Turk et al. (2018) [4–6]. Several mechanisms have
been proposed for the understanding of the HE phenomenon such
as: Hydrogen Enhanced Local Plasticity (HELP), which hypothesizes
HE is generated due to an increase in the mobility of dislocations
caused by H dissolved into the alloy, despite of appearing brittle
fracture it is actually a consequence of localized plasticity [4,6–
8]. The mechanism of Hydrogen Enhanced Decohesion Energy
(HEDE) postulates an expansion in the matrix generated by excess
H, causing a decrease in fracture and cohesion energy. Hydrogen-
Induced Phase Transformation (HIPT) mechanism suggests the for-
mation of two types of phase transformations, being 1) hydride for-
mation and 2) hydrogen-induced martensitic formation [4,6–8].
Some other mechanisms are important, to name a few:
Adsorption-Induced Dislocation Emission (AIDE), Hydrogen-
Enhanced Deformation-Induced Vacancy Formation, and the for-
mation of hydrides and nanohydrides, which are considered brittle
phases. However, the hydrides and nanohydrides are not expected
in Ni-based alloys [6,8]. The aforementioned mechanisms can
occur simultaneously; therefore, it is necessary to study the vari-
ables that affect to the HE phenomenon. Some research has studied
the effect of different phases formed in Ni-based alloys and their
relationship with heat treatments; Turnbull et al. (1992) [9] report
the effect on effective diffusivity caused by the c0 and c00 phases
and carbides generated by aging in an X-750 alloy, as well as a

et al. (1982) with the reaction kinetics model. Sofronis and
McMeeking (1988) developed a ﬁnite element modeling consider-
ing the effect of hydrostatic stress on the trapping of H in plasti-
cally deformed steels, consequently, Krom et al. (1999) presented
the appropriate formulation for the H balance into the material,
applying strain rate to the advection diffusion equation [4,7,14–
18]. Recently, Turk et al. (2018) carried out a work comparing
the hydrogen absorption of Ni-HR6W-based alloy microstructures
with carbides precipitation and free carbides on the grain bound-
aries; besides, they propose a mathematical model derived from
the hydrogen permeation obtained [19]. In this work, the effective
diffusion coefﬁcient as well as the steady-state ﬂux on melting-
rolling manufactured Ni-based alloys and commercial Inconel
690 and 600 are obtained. The aim was to study the effect on the
H permeation as a function of the Cr content and deformation pro-
cess. The research is part of a specialized study process of the effect
of Cr content and heat treatments on the D
eff and Jss for the possible
mitigation effect of HE phenomenon, as well as its predictive mod-
eling using artiﬁcial neural networks.
2. Material and methods
2.1. Material
Three Ni-based alloys were manufactured at different Cr con-
tents (28Cr, 15Cr and 10Cr), in order to cover the range of chemical
composition of the commercial Inconel 690 and 600 alloys. The
chemical composition of the analyzed alloys is shown in Table 1 .
Regarding the manufactured 28Cr, 15Cr and 10Cr alloys, the
fusions were carried out in vacuum conditions and cast in a metal
mold, subsequently samples were hot rolled at 950 /C176C to obtain
/C241 mm sheets (melting-rolling, MR), these samples were used as
membranes in hydrogen permeability tests. On the other hand,
the commercial alloys Inconel 690 and 600 were obtained in the
25.4 mm thick plate form. The Inconel sheets for the permeability
tests were manufactured by electrical discharge machining (EDM)
obtaining /C241 mm (as received, AR). The 5 resulting samples were
metallographically prepared by conventional polishing in both
faces previously to permeability testing. Microstructural analysis
was performed by optical microscopy (OM) and scanning electron
microscopy (SEM). The alloy electroetching was carried out using
10% oxalic acid aqueous solution as electrolyte. Besides, in order
to observe the change of the interplanar distances as a function
of the Cr content of the MR samples, the X-ray diffraction (XRD)
analysis was used.
2.2. Hydrogen permeation electrochemical testing
The steady state hydrogen ﬂux (J ss) and the effective diffusion
coefﬁcient (D eff) of hydrogen were measured electrochemically
using the technique proposed by Devanathan and Stachursky,
under the ASTM G-148 standard conditions [20,21]. The test con-
sists of hydrogen charging on metallic sheet surface and subse-
quently measure the hydrogen that permeates through the
metallic sample. The experimental set up consists of a double cell:
hydrogen charging and oxidation cells. In the charging cell (ca-
thodic side), atomic hydrogen is produced using an acid solution
composed of 0.1 mol/l H 2SO4 and 5x10 -5 mol/l As 2O3, the late is
used to delay the recombination of atomic hydrogen [3,9–
12,20,21]. On the other hand, the hydrogen diffusion was mea-
sured by obtaining the anodic current, for this, a solution of
0.1 mol/l NaOH was used on the anodic side. The polarization con-
trol of the detection cell was performed potentiostatically at
+300 mV while the cathodic charging was carried out at 0.1 mA/
cm2 current density. The sheets areas exposed to the tests were
1.8 cm2; the Fig. 1 shows the scheme of the experimental arrange-
ment implemented.

3.2. SEM analysis
In Fig. 5 a the SEM-EDS analysis of the Inconel 690 alloy is pre-
sented. It can be observed that all the apparent precipitates charac-
terized by EDS contain titanium, this suggests the presence of
typical c0 phase in these alloys composed of Ni 3 (Al, Ti) [9,11,19].
In general, precipitates are observed on the grain boundaries, con-
ﬁrming the elemental analysis reported by the manufacturer. On
the other hand, the SEM-Elemental mapping analysis is shown in
Fig. 5 b, it is possible to appreciate the main distribution of the
Ni-Cr-Fe system. In addition to Ti precipitates, an apparent
aluminum-carbon compound is observed, it is possible to form it
and it is stable at 1400 /C176C, however, could have been generated
by polishing contamination (see Fig. 5 b). On the other hand,
Fig. 6 a and 6b show the analysis performed on Inconel 600 alloy,
contrasting the analyzed precipitates by EDS on 690 alloy, the
Inconel 600 does not contain mostly Ti compounds in agreement
with the elemental analysis reported by the manufacturer, which
did not report titanium content that could be due to the used ana-
lytical technique. In Fig. 6 b a magniﬁed section is presented, an
apparent precipitates colony is observed; however, these are not
detected by elemental mapping which suggests the presence of
chromium carbides that were removed due to the electrolytic etch-
ing, leaving the cavities. In general, Inconel 690 and 600 alloys pre-
sent the characteristics measured by OM and chemical elemental
report.
3.3. Vickers microhardness test
The Vickers microhardness test was carried out on the MR
(28Cr, 15Cr, 10Cr) and on the commercial Inconel 600 and 690
alloys. The test results are shown in Fig. 7 , the increase in micro-
hardness for hot rolled alloys can be observed. This is due to the
strain hardening they undergo when subjected to the post-
casting process. It is also possible to appreciate a microhardness
increase as a function of the chromium content caused to the pre-
cipitation hardening that is generated by hot rolling. In the case of
the Inconel alloys, the microhardness value is similar.

In the second transient, the D eff for alloys 28Cr, 15Cr and 10Cr

saturated irreversible traps obstructing the HD. On the other hand,
the 690 and 600 alloys present an opposite effect; they exhibit an
increase in D eff in the second transient probably caused for the
existence of rapid diffusion paths generating more reversible than
irreversible saturated traps. This phenomenon is the main cause of
HE since although they contain microstructures with lower defects
such as dislocations, vacancies, among others, this encourages HD
towards the matrix which can increase the number of HE mecha-
nisms. Regarding to the current proposal, a possible mitigation
mechanism can be the high binding energy defects formation
between hydrogen and irreversible traps such as carbides, hinder-
ing the HD and avoiding HE. However, in the case of carbides for-
mation, it is important to be careful in purposefully generating
massive distributions. This because although the carbides could
protect the material from high hydrogen concentrations, the mas-
sive concentration of precipitates at grain boundaries can be a
potential problem of intergranular corrosion which culminates in
the weakening of the Cr passive layer, leading to pitting corrosion.
4. Conclusions
Hydrogen permeation tests were carried out on melting-rolling
manufactured Ni-Cr-Fe and commercial Inconel 600 and 690
alloys. The manufactured alloys were hot rolled, obtaining a
semi-equiaxed microstructure. The steady state ﬂux and the effec-
tive diffusion coefﬁcient were calculated using the time lag
method under the ASTM G-148 standard. The manufactured alloys
(MR, 28Cr, 15Cr and 10Cr) presented a small mitigation effect in
the effective diffusion coefﬁcient, probably caused by eutectic
and secondary precipitates generated from the casting and hot roll-
ing processes respectively. Regarding to the Inconel alloys (AR), the
600 alloy maintains a relatively constant J
ss value while its D eff
increases, suggesting the presence of rapid diffusion paths. Simi-
larly, the Inconel 690 alloy maintains a small J
ss value whereas
its D eff increase, this can be due to the presence of saturated irre-
versible hydrogen traps into the matrix such as vacancies, intersti-
tial sites, carbides and presence of c0 phase.
