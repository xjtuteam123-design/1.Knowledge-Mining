abstract
Refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) are considered promising materials for high-temperature appli-
cations due to their thermal stability and high-temperature mechanical properties. However, most
RHEAs have high density (>10 g/cm 3) and exhibit limited ductility at low temperatures and softening
at high temperatures. In this study, we show that oxygen-doping can be used as a new alloy design strat-
egy for tailoring the mechanical behavior of the TiZrHfNbTa alloy: a novel low-density (7.98 g/cm 3) duc-
tile RHEA. Even though the material is a single-phase BCC with some oxides at room temperature,
secondary BCC and HCP nano-lamellar structures start to form above 600 /C176C in addition to the nano-
twins which are shown to be stable up to 1000 /C176C. This alloy shows superior strength and compressive
ductility due to the nanoengineered microstructure. The present study sheds light on tailoring the
strength-ductility balance in RHEAs by controlling the microstructure of novel RHEAs at the nanoscale
via oxygen-doping.

mal stability at high temperatures [1,2]. The concept of traditional
metallic alloys is based on one or two principal elements with the
addition of small amounts of minor elements. However, a new
alloy design approach is introduced with high entropy alloys
(HEAs), which consist of at least ﬁve principal elements with a con-
centration between 5 and 35 at%, forming a single solid solution
phase [3,4]. A high number of elements in the system increases
the conﬁgurational entropy so that the formation of a single-
phase solid solution is promoted. The alloy design scope is
expanded inﬁnitely for HEAs. Compared to conventional dilute
alloys, HEAs show extraordinary characteristics such as sluggish
kinetics, severe lattice distortion, cocktail effect, and high-
entropy effect, which constitute the ‘‘Four Core Effects” that make
this class of new materials attractive for a wide variety of applica-
tions [5].
Refractory high entropy alloys (RHEAs) are projected as promis-
ing candidates for refractory applications due to their heavily dis-
torted lattice and sluggish diffusion [6,7]. Also, due to the high-
entropy effect, the Gibbs potential is expected to decrease at higher
temperatures, resulting in high phase stability [8]. The ﬁrst studies
on RHEAs were reported on NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW composi-
tions in 2010. They exhibited promising properties, such as
extreme hardness values at room temperature and high strength
up to 1600 /C176C; however, their density was more than 12 g/cm 3
and ductility was limited [9,10]. Since then, various RHEAs have
been developed generally with a combination of 9 elements from
groups IV, V, and VI (Ti, V, Cr, Zr, Nb, Mo, Hf, Ta and W). Additional
elements can be included for tuning the properties of the RHEAs,
such as density, ductility, strength, and oxidation resistance [11–
13]. For instance, Stepanov et al. designed AlTiVNb light-weight
RHEA, which shows 1020 MPa yield strength while having a den-
sity of 5.59 g/cm 3 [11]. Moreover, to improve the high temperature
oxidation properties, Cr and Al were added to the RHEAs to obtain
CrNbTiVZr, AlMoNbTaTiZr, AlHfNbTaTiZr and AlTiVNb alloys
[11,14–16]. In order to obtain high strength, Waseem et al. devel-
oped TixWTaVCr alloys with compressive strength ranging from
2327 to 2034 MPa [17]. However, brittle Cr-rich Laves phases were
reported to form in Cr-containing alloys as a result of much smaller
atomic radius of Cr compared to the other elements, which reduces
the ductility of the alloys [18,19]. Besides, high strength NbMoTaW
RHEAs modiﬁed by the Ti addition exhibited high strength with
improved but limited ductility [20]. In brief, most of the high
strength RHEA compositions so far explored can incur brittleness
at room temperature, which limits their application.
There are several studies focused on improving the ductility of
RHEAs intrinsically [21,22] and extrinsically [23,24]. It was shown
that the ductility of the BCC RHEAs is closely related to their
valence electron concentration (VEC) [22]. On the other hand, a
few RHEAs with VEC values lower than 4.4 show ductility with
an elongation between 6 and 18.8% [22,25]. For instance, equimo-
lar TiZrHfNb alloy, proposed in 2014, shows a fracture strain of
14.9% under tensile loading [25]. Recently, the ductility of this sys-
tem has been reported to be improved by oxygen doping while
enhancing the strength simultaneously [24]. Lei et al. [24] has
reported that the introduction of a small amount of oxygen into
the TiZrNbHf alloy increased the strength by /C24 50% (from
750 MPa to 1110 MPa) and ductility by /C24 95% (from 14.2% elonga-
tion to 27.7%) simultaneously due to the formation of ordered oxy-
gen clusters (OOCs). Besides interstitial clusters, secondary phases
can improve the strength of the ductile TiZrNbHf system. Recently,
Bhardwaj et al. have shown that Al addition in TiZrNbHf alloys pro-
motes the formation of secondary BCC which results in strength
increase without compensation from ductility [26]. Similarly, Tian
et al. have reported that up to 0.1 at.% nitrogen levels, TiZrHfNb-
TaNx alloy shows extensive strength increase while maintaining
high compressive ductility due to the formation of nano-sized
nitride particles [27].
RHEAs are proposed to be used at elevated temperatures; thus,
their thermal stability and high-temperature mechanical proper-
ties possess ultimate importance. High entropy alloys are reported
to be metastable, especially at intermediate temperatures where
the entropy is insufﬁcient to minimize the Gibbs Free energy of
the systems [28–31]. Consequently, thermodynamically stable
phases form when subjected to these temperatures for a long time
or during slow cooling from high temperatures [32–34]. For
instance, NbTaTiZr decomposes into two BCC phases while HfTa-
TiZr alloy forms two BCC phases as well as an HCP phase [28,35].
Rather than single phase RHEAs, multi-phase RHEAs have been
shown to be superior in mechanical properties at both room tem-
perature and elevated temperatures [1,36–41]. Thus, formation of
secondary phases can improve the strength of the RHEAs, espe-
cially at elevated temperatures.
In this study, a novel multi-phase RHEA with improved duc-
tility and strength has been designed, and high-temperature
microstructure and mechanical properties have been investi-
gated. For improved ductility and strength, two strategies have
been applied. First of all, valence electron concentration is kept
below 4.4 for intrinsic ductility, and second of all, oxygen inter-
stitials and oxide particles are introduced to the alloy for simul-
taneous enhancement of strength and ductility. Besides, melting
temperature has been maximized and density has been mini-
mized by controlling the stoichiometry of the alloy. As a result,
a multi-phase novel light-weight alloy, (Ti 25Zr35Nb20Hf5Ta15)95O5,
having extensive room temperature ductility and strength has
been designed. Thermal stability studies on this alloy have indi-
cated the formation of a secondary BCC and HCP nano-lamellar
structures in addition to the nano-twins providing improved
high-temperature strength. Although there are studies on
single-phase oxygen-doped RHEAs, this is the ﬁrst study on
designing a multi-phase oxygen-doped TiZrNbHfTa alloy and
investigating their thermal stabilities at elevated temperatures
to the best of authors’ knowledge. The present study shows that
lightweight RHEAs (comparable to the steel densities) can be
designed with improved strength and ductility under a broad
temperature spectrum for extreme applications.

To determine the phases and their fractions under equilibrium
and non-equilibrium conditions, the CALculation of PHAse Dia-
gram (CALPHAD) method was used by ThermoCalc software. CAL-
PHAD method takes elemental composition and condition of the
system (pressure, temperature) as input and calculates G-x plots
for all possible phases in uniary, binary, ternary, quaternary, and
quinary systems. Then, according to the principle of Gibbs energy
minimization, it plots the phase or property diagrams, providing
information about present phases, their constituents, and volume
fractions at any temperature. Therefore, critical temperatures for
phase transformations can be determined for any HEA system
[14,43]. For the CALPHAD calculations, the TCHEA2 v2.1 database
of ThermoCalc, a specialized database that includes 20 elements
and over 400 possible phase information, was used. This database
consists of thermodynamic functions of Gibbs free energy as a
function of alloy composition, temperature, and pressure [44].
Also, Scheil calculations were used for estimating the non-
equilibrium cooling behavior of the given alloy. These calculations
simulate the structural changes, like segregation or phase transfor-
mations, during cooling at the given composition and condition.
2.2. Materials processing and characterization
After the alloy design stage, determined compositions were pro-
duced using vacuum arc melting (VAM). Ti, Zr, Nb, Hf, and Ta ele-
ments with purities over 99.5 wt% were used in the form of pellets
and granules. 2 at.% oxygen was introduced in the form of TiO
2.
Designed compositions were produced under a Ti-gettered high-
purity argon atmosphere. Ingots were ﬂipped over and re-melted
eight times for chemical homogeneity. The density of the samples
was obtained by Archimedes’ method using water as the immer-
sion medium.
Phase information and structural analysis were performed
using Bruker D8 X-ray Diffractometer with Cu K a radiation and a
scanning rate of 1 /C176/min at 20 /C176-100/C1762h range. It should be noted
that the peak shapes were approximated by Gaussian ﬁtting. X-
ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were conducted both in-situ and
ex-situ. In-situ XRD tests were conducted in a furnace integrated
to the XRD chamber while heating on a platinum holder under a
protective argon atmosphere. The sample was precisely cut into
dimensions of 1 cm /C2 1c m /C2 1 mm. Measurements were per-
formed at room temperature, 200 /C176C, 400 /C176C, 600 /C176C, 800 /C176C,
1000 /C176C, and 1100 /C176C with a heating rate of 10 /C176C/min. Holding time
was 2 h at each temperature. Finally, the sample was quenched to
room temperature, at which another measurement was taken. Ex-
situ XRD analyses were performed at RT on the as-cast alloy and
after the heat treatments at 800 and 1100 /C176C. The heat treatments
were conducted in a vacuum furnace at 800 and 1100 /C176Cu pt o8h
with a 10 /C176C/min heating rate followed by furnace cooling in order
to investigate the effect of heat treatment temperature, time and
route to the formation of HCP phase.
Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) was conducted in con-
tinuous heating under a protective nitrogen atmosphere with 10,
20, and 40 /C176C/min heating rates. For the microstructural investiga-
tion, specimens were cut by a precision cutter. Typical grinding,
polishing, and etching (with Keller etchant) were performed. Pre-
pared samples were investigated under FEI-430 NanoSEM Scan-
ning Electron Microscope (SEM) equipped with EDAX SSDD
Apollo10 Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) camera.
Oxygen concentration of the alloys were characterized by both
LECO ONH 836 analyzer and JEOL JXA-8230 Electron probe micro-
analyzer (EPMA). In-situ TEM heating was performed in an image-
corrected FEI Titan 30–800 Transmission Electron Microscope
(TEM) operating with a ﬁeld-emission gun ﬁlament at 300 keV. A
double-tilt heating holder (Gatan 562) was used for the heating
experiments up to 900 /C176C. During in-situ TEM heating experiments,
sample was annealed at 300, 600 and 900 /C176C, each for 1 h. Post-
annealing characterization within the TEM was performed using
EDX in the scanning TEM (STEM) mode using an EDAX Octane Elite
T Super detector. Temperature range and ramps were chosen to
match with the in-situ XRD experiments.
The arc-melted alloys were suction-casted into the 4 mm diam-
eter copper mold to obtain cylindrical compression test specimens.
The height was adjusted by a precision cutter to 6 mm. Compres-
sion tests were conducted at RT on as-cast and heat-treated sam-
ples with 10 -4 strain rate using an Instron uniaxial
tension/compression system. The heat treatments were performed
at the same conditions as the in-situ TEM heating experiments
(300, 600 and 900 /C176C for 1 h at each temperature) followed by
quenching to preserve the high temperature microstructure.
Besides, microhardness tests were conducted using Shimadzu
HMV-2 E microhardness tester with 1 kg load and 10 s holding
time. At least 10 measurements were taken from different loca-
tions on the surface.
3. Results
3.1. Alloy design: Thermophysical parameters
Nine elements (Ti, V, Cr, Zr, Nb, Mo, Hf, Ta, and W) have been
considered for the RHEA design. A database of 126 combinations
has been generated for the calculation of thermodynamic and
physical properties (
DHmix, d, X,T m, q, and VEC). This database is
composed of elemental (atomic size, density, melting temperature,
and VEC) and binary (enthalpy of mixing) properties of selected 9
elements, given in supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Besides, mixing
enthalpies have been calculated by the Miedema model [45].
Based on the above-stated parameters, together with the con-
sideration of valence electron concentration for intrinsic ductility
[22], the Ti-Zr-Nb-Hf-Ta system has been selected. The stoichiom-
etry is further optimized as Ti 25Zr35Nb20Hf5Ta15 to maximize the
melting temperature and minimize the density. The properties of
the base alloy, such as lattice parameter, density, and melting tem-
perature are calculated based on the rule of mixtures as 3.406 Å,
8.28 g/cm 3, and 2117 /C176C, respectively ( Table 1 ).

3.3. Thermal stability of oxygen-doped Ti 25Zr35Nb20Hf5Ta15 alloys
Since RHEAs are designed to operate at high temperatures, their
high-temperature behavior is critical. DSC analyses in Fig. 3 con-
ducted at different heating rates (10, 20, 40 /C176C/min) for
resolution-sensitivity balance display two exothermic peaks
around 1000 /C176C and 1100 /C176C in all different heating rates. The crit-
ical temperature of the second peak does not change with the heat-
ing rate, which infers the existence of a second-order
transformation such as crystallographic transformations [47]. Gen-
erally, exothermic reactions during heating indicate the transfor-
mation of a metastable state into a more stable state which is
obstructed during the cooling from the liquid [48].
To understand the phase transformation inferred by the DSC
analysis, in-situ XRD and TEM heating experiments were per-
formed. In-situ XRD results of the oxygen-doped alloy are shown
in Fig. 4. Note that, for the homogenous distribution of heat, a thin
specimen was used; therefore, XRD spectra contain the peaks of
the Platinum holder (ICSD ID: 52250). When the strongest peak
of the BCC phase between the diffraction angles of 34 /C176-39/C176is con-
sidered, there is no phase transformation up to 600 /C176C. At temper-
atures between 800 /C176C and 1000 /C176C, the BCC#2 peak existing
between 37/C176-38/C176starts decomposing into two BCC phases, forming
the room temperature BCC (labeled as BCC#1). The peaks of BCC#1
are located at lower diffraction angles, indicating a slightly higher
lattice parameter than BCC#2. At 1000 /C176C, BCC#2 disappears, and
only BCC#1 is stable at higher temperatures (up to 1100 /C176C). It is
expected to have re-transformation to high temperature BCC
phase, BCC#2, at higher temperatures, which is the thermodynam-
ically stable phase, seen in Fig. 1 a. Furthermore, between 600 /C176C
and 1000 /C176C, some of the HCP peaks appear and disappear, as
shown in the XRD spectrum section between 51 /C176–53/C176. HCP phase
starts to form in the BCC#2 phase at /C24 600 /C176C and dissolves above
1000 /C176C. The diffraction angle decreases with increasing tempera-
ture indicating an increased lattice parameter due to the thermal
expansion at elevated temperatures. Also, as the intensity is inver-
sely related to the temperature, atomic vibrations decrease the sig-
nal/noise ratio. Thus, especially at elevated temperatures, there is a
considerable decrease in the intensity of all peaks [49]. It has been
reported that lattice parameter calculations cannot be reliable
above 1000 /C176C [50]. It should also be noted that both HCP and
BCC transformations can be correlated with the ﬁrst and second
peaks of the DSC test (see Fig. 3 ), respectively, which indicates
higher temperature phase transformations as a result of continu-
ous heating causing delayed transformation.

extensive plasticity at room temperature.
4. Discussion
The extraordinary inherent properties of RHEAs in combination
with nano-engineering of the microstructure via oxygen-doping
provide unique opportunities for designing novel functional alloys
for high-temperature applications. The pursuit of improved
mechanical properties while maintaining high ductility, reduced
alloy densities, and good thermodynamic stability remains a chal-
lenge for RHEAs. In this study, we developed a novel oxygen-doped
TiZrHfNbTa alloy having low density ( /C24 7.98 g/cm 3), and high
strength and ductility. Addition of oxygen interstitials in RHEAs
has been reported to result in modulation of the electron valence
conﬁguration, a physical fact that has been correlated with
improvements in the elasto-plastic properties of this novel alloy
system [24]. Exceptional high-temperature mechanical properties
are attributed to the formation of nano-structures at elevated
temperatures.
It has been found that CALPHAD simulations which have been
performed to predict the phase constituents of TiZrHfNbTa alloy
match well with the XRD and TEM ﬁndings. According to CALPHAD
calculations on Ti 25Zr35Nb20Hf5Ta15 alloy, the BCC and HCP phases
are stable at lower temperatures. However, XRD results show no
HCP or second BCC peaks up to 600 /C176C, which indicates that phase
transformation is obstructed by sluggish kinetics of HEA at low
temperatures. It has been reported that obstructed equilibrium
phase of HCP can precipitate if a long time heat treatment is
applied, which is sometimes the case after plastic deformation to
create nucleation sites [57–59]. Pacheco et al. have shown that
the HCP phase (stable between 0 and 730 /C176C) precipitates after
48-hour annealing at 600 /C176C followed by water quenching or 1-
hour annealing at 1000 /C176C followed by slow cooling while it is
obstructed when the treatment is performed for 1 h at 600 /C176C
and 1000 /C176C followed by water quenching [49]. Similarly, in this
study, it has been shown that when the time at the temperatures
below 1000 /C176C is shortened, HCP precipitation is prevented, as seen
in Fig. 9 . In other words, the HCP phase precipitates at the as-cast

The total strength of the HEAs is dominated by solid solution
strengthening since the modulus and lattice mismatches are higher
compared to the conventional dilute alloys. On the other hand,
work hardening and grain boundary strengthening have a rela-
tively weak effect [57,63]. High hardness values of the HEAs can
be explained by severe lattice distortion, one of the HEAs’ core
effects. Similarly, the considerable difference between elemental
and base alloy values can be attributed to the cocktail effect,
another core effect of HEAs. Due to large atomic size mismatch, lat-
tices are always heavily distorted, which improves solid solution
strengthening and causes much higher hardness values than the
rule of mixture calculations.
It has been reported that TiZrHfNbTa HEAs show compressive
yield strength between 840 and 929 MPa in equiatomic and non-
equiatomic compositions in their as-cast state [51,55,64,65]. More-
over, Lei et al. have shown that the strength increases by 40–45%
with the addition of 2 at% oxygen into TiZrNbHf and TaTiZrNbHf
RHEAs [24,66]. Even though in this study, there are some coarser
oxides forming in the oxygen doped alloy, the hardness value of
base alloy increased from 312 HV to 440 HV with /C24 40% hardening
after oxygen doping, similar to the above stated studies. Clearly,
the combination of oxygen doping and cocktail effect results in
high strength compared to elemental values. Furthermore, this
proves that existence of low-density oxides in the as-cast alloy
does not have a considerable effect on the mechanical properties.
The most common problem with RHEAs for industrial applica-
tions is low ductility at room temperature, which originates from
the inherent brittle BCC phase of RHEAs. In this study, two different
strategies have been used for the improvement of ductility. The
ﬁrst strategy utilizes the electron theory, which provides intrinsic
ductility when applied to RHEAs [22]. Lowering VEC under 4.4 is
reported to change the failure mode from screw dislocation glide
to shear deformation [67]. Thus, alloy composition was determined
by considering this criterion. Another strategy is oxygen-doping. It
has been recently shown that 2 at% oxygen addition results in a
considerable improvement in strength and ductility simultane-
ously by creating Ti and Zr rich complex oxygen clusters [24].
These clusters improve strength by pinning the dislocations and
multiplying them while distributing the passing dislocations
homogeneously. Moreover, wavy slip occurs due to the cross-slip
of the screw dislocations. While dislocation density increases, dis-
location movement velocity is enhanced [24,68]. This way, necking
is delayed, and ductility is improved.
It has been shown that 2 at.% doping in TaTiZrHfNb system
results in ordered oxygen clusters rich in Ti and Zr [24,66]. 5 at.%
doping in this study, on the other hand, results in the formation
of coarser Ti and Zr rich oxide particles as well as having OOCs (in-
ferred from the mechanical properties) in the as-cast condition.

5. Conclusion
In this study, low density (Ti 25Zr35Nb20Hf5Ta15)95O5 RHEA with
superior properties has been produced and shown to exhibit high
strength and compressive ductility at RT. Alloy design was accom-
plished by thermophysical parameter calculations and CALPHAD
method. Thermal stability and mechanical properties of the
designed alloy have been investigated using in-situ XRD coupled
with in-situ TEM as well as compression tests. In-situ XRD heating
and DSC studies indicate that secondary BCC phase (BCC#1) forms
at temperatures between 800 /C176C and 1000 /C176C and disappears above
1000 /C176C while an HCP phase appears between 600 /C176C and 1000 /C176C.
Furthermore, in-situ TEM studies conﬁrm the formation of BCC#1
and HCP phases as well as nano-twins in BCC#2 above 600 /C176C.
Room temperature ductility and strength have been improved by
oxygen doping and stoichiometry adjustments to obtain VEC val-
ues lower than 4.4. Moreover, high-temperature strength and duc-
tility result from the discovery of a characteristic nano-lamellar
structure of BCC + HCP phases as well as nano-twin formation. It
has been found that CALPHAD calculations match well with the
experiments, meaning that CALPHAD is a reliable method to gues-
timate the phase diagrams and microstructure of the RHEAs. RHEA
design principles applied in this study to obtain high strength and
ductility can be extended to other RHEA systems having multiple
low-temperature phases in equilibrium conditions to achieve an
exceptional high-temperature strength.
