ABSTRACT
Hot cracking has remained a significant challenge that hinders the broader application of laser powder bed fusion 
(LPBF)-fabricated Ni-based superalloys. In this study, the chemical composition of TMSL-1-a proprietary alloy 
prone to hot crack, was systematically optimized to develop a novel hot-crack-free alloy, TMSL-2. The alloy was 
designed to account for both the solidification behavior and phase-transformation stress during the LPBF cyclic 
process. Two criteria, the solidification cracking index (SCI) and the strain-age cracking (SAC) criterion, were 
used to guide the optimization. As a result, TMSL-2 demonstrated hot-cracking-free fabrication across a wide 
range of LPBF parameters. In addition to its excellent printability, the as-printed alloy exhibited superior me -
chanical performance, achieving an ultimate tensile strength of 1195 MPa and an elongation of 35.1 % at room 
temperature. Furthermore, the combined use of the SCI and the SAC offers an effective evaluation index for 
assessing hot-cracking susceptibility in LPBF-processed Ni-based superalloys. This work offers a practical 
composition optimization route for designing hot-cracking-resistant Ni-based superalloys for additive 
manufacturing.
1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys have broad application prospects in the 
manufacturing of hot-end components in the aerospace industry [1 – 3]
due to their excellent high-temperature mechanical properties, oxida -
tion resistance, and corrosion resistance [4] . Traditionally, Ni-based 
superalloys have been predominantly manufactured through casting, 
forging, or powder metallurgy [5] . However, with the increasing de -
mand for complex and lightweight structures in aerospace hot-end 
components, traditional manufacturing techniques are gradually 
becoming insufficient to meet these requirements. In contrast, Laser 
Powder Bed Fusion (LPBF) additive manufacturing (AM) technology 
demonstrates unique advantages by efficiently fabricating complex 
structural components [6 – 8] . Nevertheless, metallurgical defects, 
particularly hot cracking that forms during AM of Ni-based superalloys 
significantly hinder their large-scale industrial applications [9,10] .
The hot cracking in AM of Ni-based superalloys can be categorized 
into solidification cracks and liquation cracks [11,12] . Solidification 
cracks primarily arise from the obstruction of liquid feeding during the 
solidification process due to high-melting-point MC carbides and 
dendrite arm bridging [13 – 15] . Liquation cracks originate from the 
partial re-melting of low-melting-point phases at grain boundaries, 
leading to the formation of intergranular liquid films [16 – 18] . Inter -
granular liquid films serve as a prerequisite for crack initiation; how -
ever, residual stress and phase-transformation stress are also required to 
provide the driving force for crack propagation [19,20] . Therefore, the 
formation of hot cracking necessitates both intergranular liquid films 
and sufficient stress [21] .
In response to these challenges, several alloy design strategies have 
been reported. Tang et al. [22] proposed an Alloy-by-Design (ABD) 
approach. This approach steers the focus of crack-free Ni-based super -
alloys for AM toward alloy composition design. By optimizing alloy 
compositions, cracking issues caused by elemental segregation and 
phase-transformation stress can be effectively mitigated. For instance, 
Tang et al. [22] designed the ABD-900 based on crack sensitivity, which 
not only exhibits excellent printability but also achieves creep

In this study, three alloys — DZ125, TMSL-1, and TMSL-2 — were 
fabricated using LPBF. The as-printed DZ125 and TMSL-1 alloys 
exhibited hot cracking under various processing parameters, and such 
defects could not be eliminated by parameter optimization alone. 
Therefore, similar to DZ125, TMSL-1 was classified as a hot-crack-prone 
alloy. To fundamentally address this issue, we systematically considered 
solidification behavior and phase-transformation stress during the LPBF 
cyclic process. Guided by the SCI and the strain-age cracking (SAC) 
criterion, the composition of TMSL-1 was modified to produce TMSL-2. 
The optimized alloy demonstrated crack-free fabrication over a broad 
LPBF processing window, and its superior resistance to hot cracking was 
confirmed through microstructural characterization and mechanical 
testing.
2. Method and materials
2.1. Design methodology
The objective of the composition optimization was to eliminate hot 
cracking by targeting two metallurgical conditions known to promote its 
formation in AM: (1) enrichment of local liquid films, primarily asso -
ciated with carbides, borides, and low-melting-point phases such as 
eutectics; and (2) phase-transformation stress during the cyclic process 
that facilitates crack propagation.
Previous studies have proposed several criteria to quantify hot- 
cracking susceptibility in Ni-based superalloys for AM. Among them, 
the freezing range (FR), defined as the temperature interval between the 
liquidus and solidus, has been widely employed as a straightforward 
indicator of solidification cracking tendency, thereby providing an 
intuitive reflection of how compositional design affects hot-cracking 
susceptibility [26,27] . However, since hot cracks are most likely to 
form during the terminal stage of solidification, Kou proposed the so -
lidification cracking index (SCI), which is defined based on the slope of 
the solid fraction – temperature curve in this terminal stage, and thus 
more directly reflects the extent to which feeding is hindered [28] . In 
this study, the SCI was adopted to evaluate the first metallurgical

3.2. Crack analysis
The cross section perpendicular to the BD of TMSL-1 exhibits 
tortuous cracks, as shown in Fig. 4 (a), with bright white particles 
adhering to the crack edges as shown in Fig. 4 (b). Elemental analysis 
confirms that these particles are Ta/W-rich carbides. Elemental mapping 
of the cracked regions ( Fig. 4 (c)) reveals significant enrichment of C 
around the cracks, indicating that crack formation is strongly associated 
with the local distribution of C.
Fig. 5 presents the microstructure on the cross section parallel to the 
BD of TMSL-1 and TMSL-2. The melt pool contains multiple grains, and 
many sub-grain boundaries with a cellular structure exist within each 
individual grain. Upon magnifying the grain boundary region ( Fig. 5 (c), 
(d)), white particles were observed around the grain boundaries of 
TMSL-1, but none were found in TMSL-2. Elemental analysis of the grain 
boundaries in both alloys reveals that the white particles at TMSL-1 
grain boundaries are enriched with W, and these particles are identi -
fied as MC-type carbides [35,36] . In contrast, no enrichment of re -
fractory elements is observed at the grain boundaries of TMSL-2.
During solidification, the formation of carbides hinders the back -
filling of residual liquid, leading to the initiation of cracks [25] . Such hot 
cracking was indeed observed in TMSL-1. By reducing the C content in 
TMSL-2, the formation of carbides was suppressed, thereby decreasing 
the likelihood of hot cracking caused by this mechanism.

3.5. Microhardness analysis
Vickers microhardness testing was performed on samples in the as- 
printed, sub-solvus heat-treated (sub-solvus HT), and super-solvus 
heat-treated (super-solvus HT) conditions. The hardness results for 
both alloys are shown in Fig. 8 , with error bars representing the standard 
deviation. The as-printed TMSL-1 exhibited higher hardness than TMSL- 
2. After sub-solvus heat treatment and super-solvus heat treatment, the 
hardness of both alloys increased, with the hardness of TMSL-1 
increasing by approximately 72 HV, whereas TMSL-2 showed a more 
substantial increase of about 134 HV.
The precipitation of the γ ′ phase not only significantly enhances the 
microhardness of the alloys but also directly influences the phase- 
transformation stress during the printing process [22,48] . In TMSL-1, 
the relatively smaller hardness increment after heat treatment in -
dicates that a considerable fraction of γ ′ phase had already precipitated 
during printing. This earlier precipitation would generate higher 
transformation-induced stress during solidification, thereby increasing 
the susceptibility to hot cracking. In contrast, by adjusting the compo -
sition of γ ′ -forming elements in TMSL-2, the precipitation of the γ ′ phase 
was delayed, which reduced the phase-transformation stress and 
consequently lowered the risk of hot crack formation.
3.6. Tensile properties
Fig. 9 presents the room-temperature tensile stress – strain curves of 
DZ125, TMSL-1 and TMSL-2. As summarized in Table 3 , TMSL-1 exhibits 
a higher ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 1462 MPa compared with 
1195 MPa for TMSL-2; however, TMSL-2 demonstrates a significantly 
higher elongation of 35.1 %, indicating superior ductility. Relative to 
the DZ125 fabricated under the same printing conditions (UTS: 
917 MPa), TMSL-2 still maintains a considerably higher strength while 
achieving excellent ductility. These results indicate that, despite the 
reduction in C and B contents and the adjustment of γ ′ -forming elements 
in TMSL-2, the alloy retains a well-balanced combination of strength and 
ductility, thereby validating that the compositional optimization effec -
tively reduces hot cracking susceptibility without significantly 
compromising overall mechanical performance.

4. Discussion
4.1. Regulation of the SCI by C and B optimization
During the AM process, the final stage of solidification is a critical 
period for hot cracking formation. Therefore, the SCI value within the 
mole fraction of solids range of 0.9 – 0.99 can better reflect hot cracking 
susceptibility [22] . Fig. 10 shows the SCI values of TMSL-1 and TMSL-2. 
When the mole fraction of solids is between 0.98 and 0.99, the SCI value 
of TMSL-1 is significantly higher than that of TMSL-2.
The solidification processes of TMSL-1 and TMSL-2 were also simu -
lated to investigate the formation sequence of precipitates. Under the 
assumptions of complete mixing in the liquid phase and no back diffu -
sion in the solid phase, the solidification paths of the two alloys were 
calculated [30] ( Fig. 11 ). The results indicate that the formation tem -
peratures of carbides are 1345
◦
C for TMSL-1 and 1316
◦
C for TMSL-2, 
suggesting that carbides form earlier in TMSL-1, which can leading to 
a more significant obstruction to liquid feeding [25] . The carbide pre -
cipitation temperatures obtained from the DSC testing are consistent 
with the calculated results.
During the solidification process, high-melting-point carbides act as 
physical barriers that hinder liquid feeding and shrinkage compensa -
tion, thereby promoting the formation of discontinuous liquid films and 
increasing the risk of hot cracking. Both simulation and DSC results 
indicate that in TMSL-1, carbides form earlier and persist longer as 
liquid-phase barriers, further influencing the solidification behavior. In 
addition, SEM and EDS analyses revealed large carbides and significant 
carbon enrichment near crack regions in TMSL-1, whereas no such 
features were observed in TMSL-2. Therefore, by reducing the carbon 
content, the formation of detrimental liquid films was effectively sup -
pressed in TMSL-2, leading to a lower SCI value in the final solidification 
stage and improved resistance to hot cracking compared to TMSL-1.
It is worth noting that during the simulated solidification process, the 
formation temperatures of borides differ between the two alloys. How -
ever, no evident formation of eutectic phases was observed during the 
characterization. Therefore, the influence of B content was not further 
discussed.

AM is a typical cyclic process, during which the alloy undergoes 
repeated heating and cooling through multiple thermal cycles.

Therefore, in order to reduce the susceptibility to hot cracking, it is 
necessary not only to consider solidification-related parameters such as 
the SCI, but also to comprehensively evaluate the impact of phase- 
transformation stress accumulated during the cyclic process. The pre -
cipitation of the γ ′ phase can induce significant transformation stress 
after solidification, thereby exacerbating crack propagation.
To mitigate the precipitation-induced stress and enhance resistance 
to hot cracking, the γ ′ -forming elements were optimized in this study, 
and Nb was appropriately introduced to regulate the precipitation ki -
netics of the γ ′ phase. The SAC was used as a quantitative indicator of 
phase-transformation stress during the AM process. The calculated SAC 
values for TMSL-1 and TMSL-2 were 0.001105 and 0.000503, respec -
tively, indicating that the phase-transformation stress experienced by 
TMSL-2 during printing was significantly lower than that of TMSL-1.
The Vickers microhardness results showed that both alloys exhibited 
increased hardness after sub-solvus and super-solvus heat treatments, 
confirming the critical role of γ ′ precipitation in strengthening the alloys. 
After heat treatment, TMSL-2 demonstrated a larger hardness increment 
compared to TMSL-1, indicating that the precipitation kinetics of γ ′ 
during fabrication were slower in TMSL-2 than in TMSL-1. This trend is 
consistent with the DSC results, which revealed that the precipitation 
temperature during heating and the γ ′ precipitation response during 
cooling were both higher in TMSL-1 than in TMSL-2. Further micro -
structural analysis ( Fig. 12 ) indicated that under the as-printed condi -
tion, γ ′ precipitates were difficult to directly observe in both alloys. 
However, the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns of 
TMSL-1 displayed faint superlattice reflections, suggesting the presence 
of extremely fine γ ′ precipitates and a γ + γ ′ structure. In contrast, no 
such reflections were clearly detected in as-printed TMSL-2, which is 
consistent with its lower phase-transformation stress during fabrication. 
The absence of obvious γ ′ features in the TEM images is attributed to the 
extremely fine scale of the precipitates and the inherent limitations of 
the observation area, rather than their complete absence.
In summary, by optimizing the γ ′ -forming elements, the phase- 
transformation stress in TMSL-2 was effectively reduced, as evidenced 
by its significantly lower SAC value compared to TMSL-1, thereby 
enhancing its resistance to hot cracking.
4.3. Assessment of hot-cracking susceptibility using SCI – SAC
In conventional hot-cracking susceptibility analyses, the SCI is 
typically used to evaluate an alloy ’ s tendency toward solidification 
cracking, while the SAC value reflects its sensitivity to strain-age 
cracking, corresponding to different cracking mechanisms that occur 
at distinct stages of the AM process. However, AM is characterized by 
complex thermal gradients and cyclic thermal stresses, and the forma -
tion of hot cracks often involves the synergistic effects of multiple stages. 
Therefore, SCI and SAC were jointly employed in this study to 
comprehensively assess the overall hot-cracking susceptibility of the 
alloys. Considering that hot cracking mainly occurs during the late so -
lidification stage (solid fraction of 0.9 – 0.99), the average SCI value 
within this range was adopted for characterization.

5. Conclusions
1. The hot-cracking-prone alloy TMSL-1 was compositionally opti -
mized to develop TMSL-2. TMSL-2 achieved hot-cracking-free across 
a wide range of LPBF processing parameters.
2. As-printed TMSL-2 exhibited excellent mechanical properties, with 
an ultimate tensile strength of 1195 MPa and an elongation of 
35.1 % at room temperature.
3. Considering the solidification process and phase-transformation 
stress during printing cycles, we propose SCI and SAC as quantita -
tive indices for evaluating hot-cracking susceptibility. Comparison of 
the compositional optimization and calculated results for TMSL-2 
confirms that this approach effectively reflects the cracking ten -
dency of the alloys and provides a sound theoretical basis for alloy 
design.
