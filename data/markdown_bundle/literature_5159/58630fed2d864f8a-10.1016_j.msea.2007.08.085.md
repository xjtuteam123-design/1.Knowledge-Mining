Abstract
The effect of temperature on mechanical behavior of a nanocrystalline Ni–20% Fe alloy with a grain size of 22 nm was studied in the temperature
range of 4.2–300 K. The results show that this nanocrystalline alloy possesses both high strength and large plastic strain at 300 and 77 K. Moreover,
the mechanical performance at 77 K is better than that at 300 K. However, the plastic strain decreases notably and the stress–strain curves become
serrated at 4.2 K. In addition, it is found that the strain rate sensitivity decreases with decreasing the temperature, and the strength softening happens
at high strain rates at 77 K. Finally, the results demonstrate that the strain hardening ability decreases at 77 K and vanishes at 4.2 K for small grain
sizes.

1. Introduction
As a result of small grain size, nanostructured materials are
usually several times stronger than conventional materials, and
have an important potential as structural materials. Therefore, a
number of studies focus on the scientiﬁc issues related to these
materials [1–5]. Although early results reported that this class of
materials shows very limited ductility prior to failure[1], recent
studies have shown that nanostructured materials are not brittle
[2,3,6]. Based on the tradeoff between the strength and ductil-
ity, a plastic strain of about 590 is acceptable at a stress level of
∼2G P a[7,8]. In addition to conventional applications, the novel
properties of nanostructured materials could also be applied in
the ﬁelds of nuclear engineering, hydrogen storage, and outer
space. These usages typically require that the materials have
a satisfactory mechanical performance at cryogenic tempera-
tures. In the literature, there are some results about materials
having grain sizes larger than 100 nm [9–11]. But, the docu-
mented information regarding nanostructured (<100 nm) metals
is quite limited [12]. In the present study, mechanical testing

2. Experimental procedure
The materials used in the current study are nanostructured
and large-grained (LG) Ni–20% Fe alloys. The nanostructured
metal from Integran was made using an electrochemical method.
The experimental details about fabricating nanostructured Ni–Fe
alloys via electrodeposition have been reported in the litera-
ture [13]. The grain size was examined using the transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) dark-ﬁeld technique. The large-
grained Ni–Fe alloy was purchased from GoodFellow, and the
as-received plates were annealed for 1 h at 700
◦C. The cubic
specimens used for compression tests have a ratio of about 2:1
in the height (3 mm):side length (1.5 mm). The 77 and 4.2 K were
realized using the liquid nitrogen and liquid helium, respectively.

In general, the reported average grain size is usually based on
the number frequency. In this principle, the mean grain size of
this alloy is about 22 nm. Assuming that grains are spherical, the
average grain size computed from the volume fraction is approxi-
mately 34 nm. The increase in the grain size in term of the volume
distribution is due to the fact that large grains occupy a consid-
erable volume fraction of the material. It is worth noting that the
electrodeposited metals can develop a columnar grain structure
[14], and the measured grain sizes may be underestimated.
Fig. 2 presents the stress–plastic strain curves of the nano-
grained (NG) and large-grained Ni–20% Fe alloys at different
temperatures. It is clear that at 300 K and 77 K, nanostruc-
tured Ni–20% Fe alloy exhibited not only a high strength but
also an impressive ductility. In addition, with decreasing the
temperature from 300 to 77 K, the strength increased signif-
icantly without a decrease in the ductility. However, for the
large-grained Ni–20% Fe alloy, the increase in the strength is
quite limited when the temperature was decreased from 300 to
77 K. The strong temperature dependence of the strength in the
nanostructured materials is believed to be associated with the

at higher temperatures. In addition, it is also found that even
at 4.2 K, the deformation behavior in the nanostructured metals
may be different from that in the large-grained metals. For exam-
ple, the ultraﬁne-grained and single crystal copper still exhibited
a strong strain hardening at 4.2 K [9,16], while the strain hard-
ening disappeared for the current nanostructured Ni–Fe alloy at
4.2 K. It has been well accepted that at room temperature, the
deformation mechanism undergoes a transition from dislocation
mediated to grain boundary controlled ones when the grain size
is below one certain value [17–19]. This critical grain size is
about 12 nm for Ni–Fe alloys [20], so that the plastic deforma-
tion of the current alloy (22 nm) is dominated by dislocations.
It is worthy of pointing out that the grain boundary activities
still contribute to the plastic deformation, and such contribution
increases with a reduction in the grain size. At 77 K, the plastic
deformation is still attributed to dislocations and grain bound-
aries [12]. However, at 4.2 K, the dislocation and grain boundary
activities may be decreased due to the low atom mobility, and
the deformation maybe occurs via shear localization, resulting
in the discontinuity and the absence of strain hardening in the
stress–strain curves, similar to what has been observed in amor-
phous alloys [21]. On the other hand, the results about copper
suggest that the serration of the stress–strain curve at 4.2 K is
due to twinning [16]. In the current study, the possibility of the
twinning induced serrations at 4.2 K requires further microscopy
works.
Generally, within the plastic range, the stress–strain curve
of a crystal can be written as σ = Kε
n, where K is the strength
coefﬁcient and n the strain hardening exponent, representing the
capability of the strain hardening. Fig. 4 gives the log–log plots
of stresses and strains of the large-grained and nano-grained
Ni–20% Fe alloys at 300 and 77 K. The slope of the linear ﬁt
is the strain hardening exponent. It is evident that at both tem-
peratures of 300 and 77 K, the strain hardening ability of the
large-grained alloy is much larger than that for the nanostruc-

tured counterpart. Furthermore, a close examination reveals that
for the large-grained specimens, the strain hardening capabil-
ity did not decrease when the temperature was brought down
to 77 K. However, for the nanostructured specimens, decreasing
the temperature leads to a decrease in the strain hardening expo-
nent. These behaviors are probably related to the difference in
the plastic deformation mechanism. At conventional grain sizes,
the dislocations can interact tremendously and form networks
inside grains. Nevertheless, at nano-scaled grain sizes, disloca-
tions are usually generated from grain boundaries and, then, sink
at the opposite grain boundaries, leaving no dislocation networks
within the grain interior [17,18,22].
4. Summary
In this paper, an electrodeposited nanostructured Ni–20%
Fe alloy ( ∼22 nm) was used to characterize the inﬂuence of
the testing temperature on mechanical behavior at nano-scaled
grain sizes. The compression tests were conducted at tempera-
tures of 300, 77, and 4.2 K at two strain rates of 2.5× 10
−4 and
1.0 × 10−1 s−1. The current study ﬁnds that nanostructured met-
als intrinsically behave in a ductile manner at room temperature.
With the remained ductility, the strength increased remarkably
at 77 K. Interestingly, at 4.2 K, the strength increased with a sig-
niﬁcant decrease in the ductility. Furthermore, the stress–strain
curves are obviously serrated, which is probably caused by the
localized character of slip, and also by possible contribution of
twinning during the plastic deformation at 4.2 K. In addition, the
results also reveal that, different from the large-grained Ni–Fe
alloy, the nanostructured Ni–Fe alloy’s strain hardening capa-
bility decreased at 77 K. As far as the effect of strain rate is
concerned, it is found that the strain rate sensitivity decreases
with decreasing the temperature and changes a sign.
