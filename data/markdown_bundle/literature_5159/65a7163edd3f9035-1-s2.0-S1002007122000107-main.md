ABSTRACT
The tensile creep behavior of Ni-based single crystal superalloys was investigated by molecular dynamics simu-
lations. The effects of rhenium (Re) on the creep behavior and microstructural evolution mechanism during
nanoscale creep tests were discussed. The results indicate that the addition of Re can effectively enhance the creep
resistance of the alloys. The reason is that Re plays an important role in the evolution of microstructure at each
stage of the creep. Firstly, Re has a pinning effect to delay the failure of the dislocation network at the primary
creep stage. And then, the addition of Re decreases dislocation density and increases steady creep time at the
steady-state creep. Finally, Re segregates at the dislocations in the γ
0 precipitates phase and prevents the dislo-
cation from cutting into the γ0 phase by dragging dislocations at the tertiary creep stage, which enhances effec-
tively the creep resistance of superalloys. These mechanisms help to further understand the creep behavior and
derive an atomistic description of the Re effects on creep behavior and creep mechanisms in Ni-based single
crystal superalloys.
1. Introduction
Ni-based single crystal superalloys exhibit excellent creep, fatigue and
corrosion resistance, which are widely used to manufacture blade ma-
terials for advanced gas turbines in aeroengines [ 1–3]. In the service
process, the blades mainly bear the high temperature creep failure caused
by centrifugal load. Creep behavior as a time-dependent plastic defor-
mation, which is a critical issue of practical engineering signi ﬁcance for
the application of Ni-based single crystal superalloys [ 3–6]. Recently,
many studies have found that the addition of strategic rare elements can
effectively increase the creep lifetime and improve the temperature
bearing capacity [7–16]. For example, the addition of 3 wt% Re results in
a dramatic improvement in the rupture life and the temperature bearing
capability in the second-generation in comparison with the
ﬁrst-generation superalloy [17]. To analyze this reason, it is necessary to
understand the role of Re in the microstructure evolution during the
creep deformation process of superalloys.
Experimental observations and numerical simulations indicated that
Re can drag dislocations during creep, resulting in the reduce the creep
rate [ 18]. The formation of stacking faults has been observed in the γ
matrix phase and they can reduce the mobility of γ matrix dislocations to
improve the creep behavior of the single crystal superalloys with Re
addition during creep [ 18–24]. The addition of Re plays an important
role in the dislocation movement and creep properties of Ni-based single
crystal superalloys. Warren et al. [ 25] and Blavette et al. [ 26] used an
atom probe investigation of the distribution of Re and its role in
improving creep resistance of Ni-base superalloys, respectively. Yu et al.
[27] found that the addition of Re can reduce creep rate by reducing
dislocation climb velocity and then improve creep resistance of super-
alloys. Ding et al. [ 28] found that Re segregation at dislocation networks
along γ/γ
0 interfaces can effectively hinder crack propagation in Ni-based
superalloys. Huang et al. [ 29] also found that the coupling between Re
segregation and interfacial dislocations can strengthen γ/γ0 interfaces
and then improve creep resistance. He et al. [30] found the segregated Re
may enhance the creep resistance by pinning the low angle grain
boundaries and slowing down dislocation reactions. Wu et al. [ 18,31]
revealed that Re imposed a drag effect on dislocation movement, thus
reducing the creep strain rate and thereby improving creep properties of
Ni-based superalloys. These results suggest the possibility of alloy design
through control of solute partitioning to defects. However, the details of
the evolution of microstructure during creep deformation and “Re-effect”
remain largely speculative, and how Re contribute to the creep defor-
mation and micro-mechanisms in superalloys are not yet fully under-
stood. Therefore, it is important and necessary to understand the role of

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation provides detailed information
on the microstructure evolution of Ni-based single crystal superalloys
[10,32–37]. These previous studies mainly focus on the evolution char-
acteristics of interfacial microstructure and mechanical properties of
superalloys under tensile loading by MD simulations. But creep behavior
of superalloys is rarely simulated, especially the effect of Re on the creep
behavior and microstructural evolution mechanism are not given a
detailed analysis and discussion at the atomic scale based on MD simu-
lations. Although creep is a time-dependent phenomenon that typically
occurs on time scales beyond the reach of MD, the creep behavior and
structure evolution can be nevertheless observed at high strain rates in
MD simulations [38]. Therefore, in the present work, MD simulations are
performed to investigate the creep behavior and Re effect. The work aims
to advance the understanding of Re effect by comparing with creep
curves and microstructure evolution characteristics during creep defor-
mation of Ni-based single crystal superalloys with and without Re addi-
tion. Meanwhile, the effect of Re on the microstructure evolution during
creep deformation are discussed, the present work also provides infor-
mation for understanding the creep behavior and Re effect as well as
designing new generation superalloys from an atomistic perspective.

behavior of Ni-based single crystal superalloys by using the open source

processes of microstructure evolution, and to calculate accurately creep
strain, a three-dimensional cubic structure unit model was adopted. For
the initial model without Re addition, the idealized Ni-based superalloy
is composed of Ni

[36], n /C25 35 can be obtained. It means that a γ0 structural unit of 35 /C2 35

initial cubic structure of the (001) orientation atomic model (the sample
of Ni-based superalloy without Re addition), as shown in Fig. 1 (a). To
observe the effect of Re on microstructure evolution and creep resistance,
according to the same modeling method, a random distribution of 3% Re
(3% is the volume fraction) is added to the γ (Ni) matrix phase, and then
forms a cubic structure model of superalloy containing Re, as shown in
Fig. 1(b). These two models both use a three-dimensional cubic unit and

without Re addition used in the present MD simulations. In order to
eliminate the in ﬂuence of boundary on creep microstructure deforma-

3. Simulation results and discussion
3.1. Creep curves of samples with/without Re addition
Creep is the main deformation mechanism and failure behavior of
superalloys, at constant testing temperature and stress conditions, creep
will cause time-dependent plastic strain, which usually uses creep strain-
time curve to describe the time-dependent creep plastic deformation.
Generally, the creep curve of materials consists of three stages, i.e., a
primary creep stage where the creep rate decreases to a local minimum, a
secondary stage where a constant creep rate reaches, and a tertiary stage
where the creep rate increases until rupture [ 46,47]. Fig. 2 shows creep
strain-time curves of Ni-based single crystal superalloys with and without
Re addition. On the one hand, at the constant temperature of 1000 K and
the applied stress range from 0.5 to 2.0 GPa, regardless of superalloys
with or without Re addition, all the creep curves only show the primary
and secondary stages of creep strain evolution: the creep rate quickly
decreases during the primary stage and then reaches a steady state, i.e.,
the secondary stage. None of the curves enters the tertiary stage of creep,
which indicates that the superalloys have excellent creep resistance. On
the other hand, at the same temperature and applied stress condition, the
creep strain of the alloys with Re addition is lower than that of the alloys
without Re addition, which indicates that the alloys with Re addition has
better creep resistance, see Fig. 2 (a). Especially, at the applied stress of
2.7 GPa, the sample with Re addition does not enter the tertiary stage of
creep in the whole creep time range (0 –20 ns), while the sample without
Re addition enters the tertiary stage and ﬁnal rupture at the creep time of
about 8 ns, as shown in Fig. 2(b). This further indicates that the addition
of Re can effectively increase the creep resistance of the superalloys.
3.2. Effect of Re on creep mechanisms
In order to ﬁnd out why the addition of Re can improve the creep
resistance of the superalloys, the role of Re in each stage of creep was
discussed by comparing with the microstructure evolution of samples
with Re addition and without Re addition during creep.
3.2.1. Effect of Re on the primary creep stage
At the beginning of creep, the γ/γ0 interfacial dislocation networks are
formed by MD relaxation. As we known, the interfacial dislocation net-
works can act as a barrier between γ matrix and the γ0 precipitates to slow
down the minimum creep rate [48,49]. Fig. 3 show the effect of Re on the
damage evolution of mis ﬁt dislocation networks at the initial stage of
creep. The interfacial mis ﬁt dislocation networks basically keep the
regular structure at the beginning of creep (t ¼ 0.01 ns). As creep
continues, the initial regular interfacial dislocation networks begin to
deform under the high temperature and applied stress conditions. the
dislocation network of the sample without Re addition occurs damage at
t ¼ 0.1 ns, while the sample with Re addition basically keep regular
dislocation network at t ¼ 0.1ns. Combined with the evolution of dislo-
cation network in Fig. 3(b) and the distribution of Re atoms in Fig. 3(c),
at t ¼ 0.1ns, it can be found that some Re atoms are pinned at the nodes of
the dislocation network, it indicates that the addition of Re has a certain
pinning effect on the nodes of dislocation network, which protects the
damage and deformation of dislocation network. At t ¼ 0.15 ns, the
dislocation network of sample without Re addition is destroyed, whereas
the damage of dislocation network is effectively delayed until the creep
time of t ¼ 0.3 ns for the sample with Re addition. Therefore, at the
primary stage of creep, although the interfacial dislocation network will
eventually be destroyed, the addition of Re can effectively delay the
destruction of the dislocation network and pin the nodes of the disloca-
tion network, thus it can effectively strengthen and protect the disloca-
tion network. It indicates that the addition of Re can provide more
remarkable interfacial strengthening effect to delay the damage of
dislocation network. The addition of Re contributes to the protect the γ/γ0
interfacial dislocation networks which strongly impeded dislocations
shearing into the γ0 phase.
3.2.2. Effect of Re on the steady-state creep stage
In order to quantitatively calculate the effect of Re on the creep
mechanisms, the change of dislocation density during creep was calcu-
lated. Fig. 4 shows the variation of dislocation density during creep at
different applied stress for two samples with/without Re addition. By
comparing the values of dislocation density of the two samples with/
without Re addition during the whole creep process, under the same
testing temperature and stress conditions, the dislocation density of the
sample with Re addition is always lower than that of the sample without
Re addition during creep. The dislocation density re ﬂects the creep
plastic deformation of the alloys, the lower the dislocation density is, the
smaller the creep plastic deformation is. It indicates that the addition of
Re can reduce the creep plastic deformation and improve the creep
resistance of the alloys. In addition, in the stress range of 0.5–2.0 GPa, the
dislocation density always ﬂuctuates near a certain equilibrium value for
both samples with and without Re addition (see Fig. 4 (a)–(c), which
indicates that the steady-state creep stage is a process of dislocation
proliferation and annihilation, it also demonstrates the hardening and
recovery softening of the alloys at the steady-state creep stage, this is
similar to the experimentally observed phenomenon [ 50]. When the
applied stress reaches 2.7 GPa, the dislocation density of the sample
without Re addition increases rapidly and the sample quickly enters the
tertiary creep stage. Whereas the sample with Re addition is still in the
steady-state creep stage, and the dislocation density is still ﬂuctuating at
a certain equilibrium value (0.14 mm
/C0 2), as shown in Fig. 4 (d). This
further shows that the addition of Re can reduce the creep plastic
deformation and improve the creep resistance of the alloys.

Fig. 6 also summarizes the main dislocations and stacking faults
evolution during the creep process, especially from creep tertiary stage to
the ruptured stage. At the initial stage of creep, the dislocations initiated
in the γ matrix and then interact with the γ/γ0 interfacial dislocations
(damaged dislocation networks), leading to the generations of partial
dislocations and stacking faults inside of the γ
0 precipitates phase. This
initial stage of increasing creep strain may be associated with the
continuous generation and movement of dislocations in the γ matrix. In
the later stage of initial creep, at t ¼ 0.5 ns, Re atoms gradually began to
aggregate to the γ/γ0 interface, as shown in Fig. 7 (a), leading to work
hardening and a reduced creep rate. Meanwhile, some dislocations
sheared the γ0 precipitates to form the stacking faults inside the γ0 pre-
cipitates. It is obvious that these stacking faults inside the γʹ precipitates
are dominated by the shearing of individual γʹ precipitates. In the steady-
state creep stage, the evolution of the dislocation is the dynamics process
of dislocation proliferation and annihilation, leading to the dislocation
density be a ﬂuctuating process in this second stage. Moreover, the
intensive dislocation activity in γ channels leads to the distorted γ/γ0
interface, which changes plastic deformation (dislocation distribution) at
the γ/γ0 interface [ 51]. This change of the plastic deformation further
helps to the enrichment of Re atoms at the γ/γ0 interface (see Fig. 7(b)),
which can act as an effective barrier for dislocation climb, and hence
helps resisting γ0 shearing. Therefore, the addition of Re increases the
creep resistance of the alloy in the steady-state creep stage.
At the tertiary stage of creep, for the sample without Re addition, the
creep strain increases rapidly, the creep rate is proportional to the creep
strain, and the mobile dislocation density also increase rapidly at T ¼
1000 K, σ ¼ 2.7 GPa (see Fig. 3 (d)). The increase in creep strain rate is
related to the generation of new mobile dislocations or the acceleration
of the movement of existing dislocations at the tertiary stage of creep. For
the sample with Re addition, at t ¼ 8ns, a small number of dislocations
and stacking faults cut into the γ0 precipitates phase, the segregation of Re
occurs at dislocations in the γ0 precipitates phase (see Fig. 7 (c)). Re
segregating to dislocations would result in a reduced creep rate, which is
due to the dislocation drag to impede the movement of mobile disloca-
tions [18,31]. In conclusion, the present work reveals the important role
of Re on the creep resistance of Ni-based single crystal superalloys, and
provides important help for further understanding of creep behavior and
the effect of Re on dislocation motion of Ni-based superalloys. And the
present study also offers a guideline for designing next generation
superalloys.
4. Conclusions
MD simulations have been performed to characterize the tensile creep
behavior and study Re effect on the creep resistance of Ni-based single
crystal superalloys at high temperature and different applied stresses.
The major ﬁndings are as follows:
(1) Based on atomistic simulation, it is con ﬁrmed that the superalloys
have good creep mechanical properties, and the addition of Re
further improves the creep resistance of the superalloys.
(2) The creep mechanisms observed in the experiment are con ﬁrmed
by the current atomistic simulations. At the initial stage of creep,
the dislocations initiate in the γ matrix phase and form dislocation
network at the γ/γ
0 interface. The second stage of creep is mainly
characterized by the propagation and annihilation of the dislo-
cations. In the third stage, a large number of dislocations and
stacking faults cut into the γ0 precipitates phase, resulting in the
rapid increase of creep rate.
(3) Re plays an important role in the three stages of creep. At the
primary creep stage, Re has a pinning effect on the nodes of the
interfacial misﬁt dislocation network, which protects or delays the
failure of the dislocation network. At the steady-state creep, the
addition of Re decreases dislocation density and increases steady
creep time. At the third stage of creep, Re segregates at the dis-
locations in the γ0 precipitates phase and prevents the dislocation
from cutting into the γ0 phase by dragging dislocations, which
enhances the creep resistance of superalloys effectively.
