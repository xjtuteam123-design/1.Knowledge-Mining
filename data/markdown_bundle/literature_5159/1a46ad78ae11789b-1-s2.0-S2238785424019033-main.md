ABSTRACT
Mg – 3Y/Al composites were produced through accumulated rolling bonding. During annealing, Al – Y precipitates 
were synthesized in-situ within the Mg – 3Y/Al composites through solid diffusion reactions. In these composites, 
the volume fraction of the interface layer containing abundant Al – Y precipitates can reach approximately 74% 
after three passes of accumulated rolling bonding and diffusion annealing. At room temperature, the Mg – 3Y/Al 
composite exhibits increased strength (~273.9 MPa) but reduced ductility (~3.5%) compared to the base Mg – 3Y 
alloy (~191.0 MPa, ~30%), due to the presence of numerous Al – Y precipitates and residual dislocations. These 
Al – Y precipitates display remarkable thermal stability, maintaining their size without significant coarsening even 
after annealing at 400 
◦
C for 1 – 4 h, thereby contributing to the exceptional thermal stability observed in Mg – 3Y/ 
Al samples. After annealing at 400 
◦
C for 4 h, the tensile strength of the Mg – 3Y/Al samples remained unchanged, 
while the ductility increased to 8.5%. At 300 
◦
C, the Mg – 3Y/Al composite demonstrates exceptional high- 
temperature strength (179.9 MPa), significantly surpassing that of the original Mg – 3Y alloy (110.6 MPa). 
Moreover, compared to the original Mg – 3Y alloy and magnesium alloys reported in the literature, the Mg – 3Y/Al 
composite exhibits a higher RE-equivalent strength at 300 
◦
C. This enhanced strength is primarily attributed to 
precipitation strengthening induced by fine Al – Y precipitates. These findings underscore that introducing fine 
Al – Y precipitates through a solid diffusion reaction is an effective approach for achieving outstanding high- 
temperature strength in magnesium alloys.
1. Introduction
Due to its low density and high specific strength, magnesium alloy is 
highly advantageous for structural applications [ 1 – 3 ]. Its extensive 
range of applications in the aerospace and automotive sectors represents 
a significant opportunity for advancing lightweight technologies [ 4 , 5 ]. 
However, magnesium alloys have certain limitations. One notable 
drawback is their poor performance at high temperatures, which greatly 
restricts their potential applications [ 6 , 7 ]. Therefore, enhancing the 
high-temperature mechanical properties of magnesium alloys is crucial 
to facilitate their use in the aerospace and automotive industries [ 8 , 9 ].
The inferior high-temperature properties of magnesium alloys stem 
primarily from the activation of non-basal slip mechanisms at elevated 
temperatures, along with the weakening of grain boundaries [ 10 ]. 
Therefore, to improve the mechanical properties of magnesium alloys at 
high temperatures, it is crucial to inhibit dislocation slip and stabilize 
grain boundaries. The most widely used commercial magnesium alloys, 
such as the Mg – Al alloy series, feature the Mg
17
Al
12 
phase as the primary 
strengthening phase. However, this phase degrades quickly at elevated 
temperatures and fails to effectively impede dislocation slip and grain 
boundary migration [ 11 , 12 ]. Consequently, the incorporation of 
high-temperature stable second-phase particles represents the primary 
method for improving the high-temperature mechanical properties of 
magnesium alloys [ 12 , 13 ].
As early as the 1930s, scholars reported that adding rare earth ele -
ments (RE) to cast magnesium alloys effectively improves their high- 
temperature mechanical properties [ 14 ]. In the following decades, 
some heat-resistance magnesium alloys were developed [ 11 , 12 ]. From 
the phase diagram of magnesium alloys, it can be observed that the 
majority of the Mg-RE eutectic phases have high melting points, which 
are stable at high temperatures [ 11 , 12 ]. This helps to improve the 
high-temperature properties of magnesium alloys [ 15 ]. For example,

The introduction of strong thermal stable second phases in magne -
sium alloys is currently achieved primarily in the casting stage [ 21 – 23 ]. 
However, the high temperatures associated with the melting process 
often result in the coarsening of the secondary phases [ 23 – 25 ]. Inspired 
by diffusion bonding [ 26 ], whereby the elements in the two closely 
bonded dissimilar materials can diffuse into each other driven by the 
chemical potential, in-situ synthesis of second phase particles with high 
melting temperature by solid diffusion reaction was proposed in our 
previous work [ 27 ]. The addition, the content and size of the precipi -
tated phase can be effectively regulated by modifying the heat treatment 
process. In our previous works [ 17 , 28 ], AZ31/Mg – 3Y composites were 
prepared by accumulative rolling bonding (ARB), and high density of 
Al – Y precipitates were synthesized in situ via diffusion annealing. 
Tensile tests show that the AZ31/Mg – 3Y composites present excellent 
mechanical properties at room temperature and elevated temperatures. 
The Al – Y precipitates can effectively impede dislocation slip even at 
high temperatures.
However, a challenge encountered in previous studies was that the 
precipitates were mainly distributed in the Mg – 3Y layer, with almost no 
reinforcing phase in the AZ31 layer due to the difficulty of Y diffusion 
into AZ31. The absence of highly heat-stabilized particles in the AZ31 
layer represents a weakness in the entire material. Furthermore, the 
thickness of the interfacial reaction layer is relatively small, which limits 
its contribution to high-temperature strength. In this study, to increase 
the proportion of the interface reaction layer and eliminate regions 
without reinforced precipitates (such as the AZ31 layer in the AZ31/ 
Mg – 3Y composite), thin Al foils were employed in fabricating the 
Mg – 3Y/Al composite. The significant difference in chemical composi -
tion between the Al foils and Mg – 3Y facilitates the easy diffusion of Al 
into Mg – 3Y. Moreover, the volume fraction of the Al layer (weak layer) 
after deformation and chemical reaction is negligible. The microstruc -
tures and mechanical properties of Mg – 3Y/Al composites were studied 
at both room temperature and elevated temperatures.

3. Results
3.1. Microstructure evolution of Mg – 3Y/Al composite
Fig. 2 depicts the microstructure evolution of Mg – 3Y/Al composites 
subjected to successive accumulative rolling bonding and diffusion 
annealing processes. As shown, the Al foils were fractured and dispersed 
discontinuously at the interface between two Mg – 3Y layers following 
hot rolling. Due to significant rolling reductions, the fractured Al tightly 
adheres to the Mg-3Y sheets, exhibiting no observable defects at the 
interface. Similar to our previous work [ 17 , 28 ], an interfacial reaction 
layer formed in the Mg – 3Y layer resulting from elements diffusion re -
action. The difference is that the chemical potential gradient between Al 
and Mg – 3Y in current work is greater than that between AZ31 alloy and 
Mg – 3Y alloy in our previous work. In addition, the diffusion annealing 
temperature (400 
◦
C) is relatively higher than that used in our previous 
work (300 
◦
C). Therefore, more intense diffusion of Al has occurred, 
leading to a thicker of interface layer (with a thickness of 40 – 90 μ m) 
compared with the thickness of interface layer in previous work (~20 
μ m).
As the number of ARB passes and diffusion annealing time increased, 
the thickness of individual Mg – 3Y layers decreased, leading to a 
reduction in both the size and quantity of fractured Al particles. The 
thickness of the interface layer remained between 40 and 90 μ m, indi -
cating that the reduction in layer thickness due to rolling was compen -
sated by an increase caused by elemental diffusion reactions. In the AR3 
sample, the interface layer was thicker than the region within the Mg – 3Y 
layer lacking precipitates, with the volume fraction of the interface layer 
reaching approximately 74%. This fraction is significantly higher than 
that reported for AZ31/Mg – 3Y composites in our previous work [ 28 ].

this work. Furthermore, only a small number of broken Al can be

EBSD analysis was utilized to investigate the microstructural changes

that a significant amount of deformation energy accumulates within the 
Mg – 3Y/Al composite during the rolling deformation process, leading to 
dynamic recrystallization. The Al
2

thickness. Furthermore, the IPF color maps revealed that grains within 
the interface layer appeared red, while the pole figure (PF) indicated a 
pronounced texture alignment, with the c-axis of most grains

rolling passes and annealing time increased. In AR1 and AR2 samples, 
the grains of PFR layers are colorful indicating a weak texture. The pole 
figure shows that the PFR layers present a bimodal texture. This is 
similar to the texture of Mg-RE alloys reported in most of the literature

basal texture, similar to that of rolled pure Mg and AZ31 alloy [ 2 , 30 ]. It 
is speculated that the diffusion reaction consumes a significant quantity 
of Y, resulting in a notable reduction of Y that is solubilized in the Mg 
matrix. Therefore, a rolled texture that is close to that of pure Mg is 
formed in the PFR layer. This can also explain why the interface layers 
have a basal texture.

Following the tensile test, the fractured samples were sectioned for 
detailed examination. The morphologies of the fractured composites are 
depicted in Fig. 8 . In the AR1 sample after tension ( Fig. 8 (a)), clusters of 
Al are dispersed within the interface layer between the two Mg – 3Y 
layers, with numerous cracks oriented perpendicular to the tension di -
rection visible on nearly every Al cluster (indicated by white arrows). 
The fracture of the interface layer consistently occurred within these Al 
clusters (indicated by blue arrows), while no defects were observed in 
the Mg – 3Y layers. This suggests that cracks initially formed in the re -
sidual Al during the tensile process and subsequently propagated into 
the Mg – 3Y layers under increasing load. The formation of the cracks in 
the Al can be attributed to that Mg diffused into the Al layer and reacted 
with Al to form brittle Mg – Al intermetallic compounds, and cracks tend 
to form easily on these brittle intermetallic compounds during the ten -
sion process. Similarly, some Al clusters with cracks are also evident in 
the AR2 sample. In the AR3 sample, only a few small residual Al clusters 
remain in the interface layer due to significant deformation and 
extended diffusion annealing. Consequently, no discernible cracks are 
observed in the ND-RD plane of the AR3 sample. Based on these mor -

AR1 and AR2 samples at room temperature primarily resulted from re -
sidual Al clusters in the interface layer, whereas the diminished ductility 
in the AR3 samples likely stemmed from a high density of precipitates 
and residual dislocations.

4.2. Strengthening mechanism for the Mg – 3Y/Al composites at elevated 
temperature
In this study, the Mg – 3Y/Al composites exhibit higher strength 
compared with the original individual Mg – 3Y alloy both at room tem -
perature and high temperature. The high strength of the Mg – 3Y/Al 
composites may be attributed to fine grain size, stronger basal texture, 
Al
2
Y precipitates, and residual dislocations. At elevated temperatures, 
the influence of fine grain size and texture on strength should be very 
weak due to the softening of grain boundaries and the activation of non- 
basal slips. Therefore, the excellent high-temperature strength of the 
Mg – 3Y/Al composites can be mainly attributed to precipitation 
strengthening and dislocation strengthening.
4.2.1. Precipitation strengthening
In this study, the main precipitates are Al
2
Y precipitates in the 
interface layer with a size range from 30 nm to 300 nm. During the high- 
temperature tension, the interaction of Al
2
Y precipitates with disloca -
tions must enhance the strength of Mg – 3Y/Al composites. It is widely 
accepted that the interaction between precipitates and dislocation in -
volves two distinct mechanisms, namely the Orowan mechanism and the 
Kelly-fine mechanism [ 43 ]. The specific strengthening mechanism de -
pends on the size and strength of the precipitate. For high-strength 
precipitates, when the precipitate size is smaller than a critical size, 
the dislocation slip will cut through the precipitates following the 
Kelly-fine mechanism. When the precipitate size is larger than the crit -
ical size, dislocation cannot cut through the precipitates. Instead, it can 
only move further bypassing the precipitates based on the Orowan 
mechanism [ 44 ]. In our previous work [ 28 ], we calculated that the 
critical size of the Al
2
Y precipitates in Mg-matrix is 45.8 nm. In AR3 
samples, the frequency of the precipitates with a size smaller than 45.8 
nm is no more than 1.0%. Therefore, the Orowan mechanism is the main 
precipitation-strengthening mechanism in this study. The contribution 
of the Orowan mechanism can be calculated by the following equation
