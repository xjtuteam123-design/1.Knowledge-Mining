Abstract
Grain growth behaviors of a beta titanium alloy (Ti-2Al-9.2Mo-2Fe) have been 
systematically investigated for various temperatures. Quantitative evaluations of the 
grain growth kinetics over a wide range of temperatures from 820°C to 1000°C have 
been performed. The results showed that the isothermal grain growth kinetics 
followed the normal growth behavior. The solution-treated alloy at lower temperatures 
(820°C~900°C) exhibited a good uniformity of the grain size. It showed a poor 
uniformity of the grain size due to the abnormal grain growth at higher temperatures 
(950°C~1000°C). The grain growth exponent values ( n) were at the range of 0.1~0.2, 
about 0.1 at lower temperatures and about 0.2 at higher temperatures. They were 
lower than other beta titanium alloys. The lower exponent values ( n) and the higher 
activation energy (Q) can be attributed to the solute drag effect and the high Mo 
content with low diffusivity in Ti matrix. 
Keywords: 
Beta Ti alloy ; Grain growth kinetics; Activation energy; Metastable; Uniformity

1. Introduction
Metastable β titanium alloy provides a number of advantages compared to 
conventional α and α+β alloys [1,2]. Some attractive properties of the alloys are 
excellent hot or cold deformability, high hardenability and good corrosion resistance. 
More to the point, good response to aging develops high strength and fracture 
toughness [3,4] . Whereas one of the disadvantages of β alloys is r apid grain 
coarsening at elevated temperatures due to accelerated diffusion in the β phase region 
with open body centered cubic structure [5]. Therefore, the microstructural stability of 
β alloys would depend on their ability to maintain refined grains or microstructures at 
high temperatures. Many researches have expected to improve the ability to hinder 
grain boundary migration. It is proved that solute atoms have a strong influence on 
retarding the migration of grain boundaries during recrystallization [6]. And another 
attempt is, as well known as, Zener pinning by fine particles [7]. These insoluble and 
small particles act to prevent the motion of such boundaries by exerting a pinning 
pressure which counteracts the driving force pushing the boundaries, such as the TiB 
and TiC compound by boron or carbon addition during alloying [8-10]. 
Ti-2Al-9.2Mo-2Fe alloy is a new type of metastable beta alloy which is expected 
to be of high strength but low elastic modulus. As well known, the elastic modulus is 
not sensitive to the grain size of the material for a given composition but the strength 
depends on the grain size with respect to the Hall-Petch theory. Therefore, the strength 
can be improved without increasing the elastic modulus by controlling the grain 
growth and refinement. The mechanical properties such as strength and toughness of 
the fine-grained alloy at room temperature are generally superior to those of the 
coarse-grained alloy [11]. 
The present work is undertaken to provide the insight into the grain growth 
kinetics and the uniformity of β grains for the new designed Ti -2Al-9.2Mo-2Fe alloy. 
An understanding of such behaviors and kinetics is important, for example, with 
regard to the development of selective heat treatment process to produce several 
graded microstructures for specific service applications, especially for a new 
developed alloy.

2.1 Material
The material used in the present study was a new designed alloy with the 
nominal composition of Ti-2Al-9.2Mo-2Fe (wt.%). The alloy was melted by double 
V AR (Vacuum Are Remelting) processing. The ingot after homogenization at 1100°C
for 1 hour was broken down and forged to the square billets with 380 mm in each 
dimension. The square billets were then hot rolled at 800°C into the round bars with 
12 mm in diameter. The chemical compositions of the alloy were listed in Table 1. 
The β transus temperature ( Tβ) was metallographically measured to be approximately 
815°C. Molybdenum and aluminum equivalents ([Mo] eq and [Al]eq) of the alloy were 
calculated to be about 14.3 and 3.1, using the equation given by [12]:
[Mo]eq = [Mo] + 0.2 [Ta] + 0.28 [Nb] + 0.4 [W] + 0.67 [V] + 1.25 [Cr]+ 1.25 
[Ni] + 1.7 [Mn] + 1.7 [Co] + 2.5 [Fe] (1)
[Al]eq = [Al] + 0.17 [Zr] + 0.33 [Sn] + 10 [O] + 10 [C] + 20 [N] (2)
By using the [Mo] eq and [Al]eq and the following empirical equation [13], the β

metallographic method.

2.2 Experimental
For grain growth study, the samples from the as-rolled alloy were isothermally 
heat-treated in a pre-heated air furnace at five different temperatures (820°C, 850°C, 
900°C, 950°C, 1000°C) and kept for the time ranging from 0.5 h to 12 h. The detailed 
experimental plans were shown in Table 2. All the samples prior to heat treatment 
were coated with Acheson’s Deltaglaze 151 protective coating to minimize the 
formation of α case. 
The specimens for optical microscopy (OM) were mechanically polished with 
the standard metallographic procedure and etched in the Kroll’s reagent (5% HF+15% 
HNO3+80% H 2O) to reveal the grain boundaries. Microstructure characterization 
(OM) was performed on the Olympus GX51 optical microscopy. At least ten figures 
were captured in each sample to consider the error minimization of grain size 
measurement (Fig. 1-a). The grain boundaries in these figures were clearly traced by 
hand with an App called Schetchbook in Apple ipad ® (Fig. 1-b). The objective is to

3. Results and discussion
3.1 Microstructures before and after heat treatment
The microstructure of the as-rolled Ti-2Al-9.2Mo-2Fe alloy was shown in Fig. 2. 
It consisted of mainly elongated and small β grains (below 10 μm), as well as primary 
α phases (neck-lace shape along the prior β grain boundaries and lamellar shape in the 
β matrix). The primary α phases were formed due to the hot rolling in the α/β phase 
region followed by air cooling. The microstructures of the samples solution-treated at 
820°C, 900°C and 1000°C for different holding times were shown in Fig. 3, 4 and 5, 
respectively. The alloy solution-treated at 820°C exhibited the slow grain growth with 
the increasing time. The microstructures consisted of the equiaxed β grains with the 
size of 40 ~ 50 μm due to recrystallization as shown in Fig. 3. The alloy solution-
treated at 900°C showed the coarser β grains with the size of about 70 μm in the initial 
stage, as well as the higher grain growth rate. In addition, after recrystallization the 
normal grain growth occurred from 0.5 to 4 hours but the abnormal grain growth was 
observed since holding for 8 hours as shown in Fig. 4. It seems that not all the grains 
can enlarge, but the large ones grow at the expense of the small ones that shrink. Thus, 
the average grain size increases with the holding times, and at any particular instant 
there will exist a range of grain sizes. It leads to the bimodal grain size of β phase and 
also the inhomogeneity. The more serious abnormal grain growth was also found in 
the alloy when was solution-treated at 1000°C since 2 hours and the abnormal 
phenomenon was getting serious with increasing the holding time as shown in Fig. 5.

increasing temperature. The grain growth exponent values ( n) of the alloy were at the 
range of 0.094 ~ 0.216 depending on temperatures. The n value was about 0.1 at 
lower temperatures (820°C ~ 900°C) and about 0.2 at higher temperatures (950°C ~ 
1000°C). The trend towards higher n values at higher temperatures is also indeed in 
good agreement with another study that has been reported [21], which is due to the 
evidence of changes in the grain boundary structure and mobility at very high 
temperatures. The grain growth exponents (n) for α and β phase in pure titanium were 
reported to be around 0.35 and 0.5 respectively [16]. The n values close to 0.5 were 
usually reported for ultra-pure metals and the lower values of n were generally 
associated with solute drag and free surface effects or texture, etc [22]. The n values 
of the present alloy were low (0.1 ~ 0.2) when compared to those of the other beta 
titanium alloys. It’s often attributed to the effects of solute atoms which retard the 
movement of grain boundaries, as called the solute drag effect. It was also reported 
that as the alloying element contents increase, the dislocation density increases by 
high solute content during solution treatment. That causes to the lower grain growth 
exponent and the higher activation energy for grain growth [16,23,24]. On the other 
hand, the high Mo content of 9.2 % should also have the contribution to this 
phenomenon as a result of its low diffusivity in Ti matrix.

Thus, the activation energy for grain growth in Ti-2Al-9.2Mo-2Fe alloy was 
calculated by plotting ln D against inverse temperature (104/T) as shown in Fig. 9. The 
slope of the lines was used to calculate the activation energy according to Equation 8 
and the results were shown in Table 4. The apparent activation energy for grain 
growth was at the range of 442.8 ~ 644.3 kJ/mol and increased with the holding time. 
In the early stage of the holding time, the migration velocity of grain boundary is 
faster than the diffusion of solute atoms and the solute atoms are not accumulated at 
the grain boundaries. As a result, the grain boundaries are not affected by the solute 
drag effect and have lower activation energy in the initial time. As the holding time 
increases, the migration velocity of grain boundary decreases, and the driving force of 
grain growth decreases. So the amount of solute atoms in the boundaries increases and 
promotes the solute drag effect with decreasing the migration velocity of grain 
boundaries [26, 27]. It means that the activation energy for grain growth by grain 
boundary migration increases, as holding time and solute content increases. As shown 
in Table 4, the longer holding time at high solution temperature, the higher activation 
energy for grain growth was needed; at 0.5 hr, activation energy was about 442.8 
kJ/mol, but at 12 hrs, the energy was about 644.3 kJ/mol. With respect to the previous 
work by other researchers, the activation energy for self diffusion in beta titanium 
(900°C ~ 1540°C) was reported to be between 145 ~ 315 kJ/mol [28]. Semiatin et al. 
reported that the activation energy of Ti-6Al- 4V in the β phase was about 312 kJ/mol 
[29]. B.B. Rath reported that the measured activation energy of Beta III was 347 
kJ/mol [30]. According to the previous explanation, the high value of activation 
energy obtained in the developed alloy may be a result of the solute drag effects in the 
grain boundaries and the high content of Mo element (9.2%) with low diffusivity in Ti 
matrix, which causes to retard the mobility of grain boundaries more strongly and 
induces the strong drag effects.
4. Conclusions
The grain growth kinetics in the new designed metastable Ti-2Al-9.2Mo-2Fe 
alloy were studied by the above-transus solution treatment at different temperatures 
(820°C ~ 1000°C) and for the various holding times (0.5 ~ 12 h). The representative 
results are briefly presented in the following three sections:
1. The grain growth exhibited no remarkable rise as time increased (~ 50 μm)

and showed the high uniformity ( η) of grain size (about 0.6) at low temperatures 
below 900°C. At temperatures above 900°C, the grain growth showed a sharp increase 
and was sensitive to the holding time (100 ~ 250 μm). It showed the poor uniformity 
of grain size (about 0.5) and the sharp decline of the η values indicated the serious 
abnormal grain growth of β phase.
2. The grain growth exponent ( n) of Ti-2Al-9.2Mo-2Fe alloy increased with the 
holding temperature. It was at the range of 0.1 ~ 0.2, 0.1 at lower temperatures (820°C
~ 900°C) and 0.2 at higher temperatures (950°C ~ 1000°C). The lower n values than 
other beta alloys can be attributed to the solute drag effect in grain boundaries and the 
high content of Mo with low diffusivity.
3. The activation energy for the grain growth of the alloy increased with the 
holding time. It was at the range of 442.8 ~ 644.3 kJ/mol in the Ti-2Al-9.2Mo-2Fe 
alloy. It shows higher value than other titanium alloys, which may be a result of the 
solute drag effect and the high Mo content with low diffusivity in Ti matrix.
