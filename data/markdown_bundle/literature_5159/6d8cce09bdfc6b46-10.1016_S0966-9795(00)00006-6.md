Abstract
Mechanical behavior of multi-phase alloys, consisting of intermetallic compounds B2 type CoAl and E2 1 type Co 3AlC with pri-
mary solid solution (Co), was investigated by compression tests over a wide temperature range from 77 to 1273 K. It has been
revealed that E2 1 Co3AlC is a good candidate of coexisting phase with B2 CoAl to improve the alloy’s ambient temperature duc-
tility and its elevated temperature strength. Most of the multi-phase Co–Al–C ternary alloys exhibit fairly good compressive duc-
tility at whole test temperatures, even at 77 K. All the alloys show the anomalous positive temperature dependence of strength at
intermediate temperatures. The deformation mechanism of E2
1 Co3AlC is expected to be the same as that of L1 2 intermetallic
compounds because of the close resemblance of their crystal structures. The three-phase B2/E2 1/(Co) alloy, 70Co20Al10C(at%),
exhibits excellent ductility of virtually unlimited ambient temperature compressive plastic strain and the highest strength around
340 MPa at 1273 K. # 2000 Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

1. Introduction
Improvement of ambient temperature toughness has
always been a problem which must be overcome to
develop alloys based on intermetallic compounds for
high temperature structural application [1]. Superior
properties at elevated temperatures, such as high melting
point, adequate strength, good oxidation resistance and
relatively low density, are general attractive advantages
of intermetallic compounds, especially of aluminides
and silicides. E/C128orts to achieve much greater ductility
and toughness at room temperature usually come into
conﬂict with the strategy to enhance, or at least main-
tain, materials’ high temperature strength. In a very
traditional way of metallurgy, the reﬁnement of grain
size would be e/C128ective for simultaneously enhancing
both ductility and strength [2]. However, many inter-
metallic compounds are glass brittle, especially at grain
boundaries [1,3–5]. Moreover, it is a well-known fact
that small grain size causes exacerbation of high tem-
perature strength and creep resistance. Providing that
the extreme brittleness is originated from the intrinsic
nature of intermetallic compounds, the ductility
improvement attained by controlling physical and che-
mical properties on the monolithic intermetallic phase
must be limited. Additionally, the susceptibilities of
intermetallic compounds to impurities and environ-
mental factors raise the diculty of controlling the
toughness of single-phase intermetallic alloys.
The idea of making two-phase or three-phase micro-
structures by introducing ordered and/or disordered
phases which impart plastic deformability ought to be
the best strategy for improving ductility and toughness
for those intermetallic based alloys. With this belief, we
have carried out a systematic investigation for the
design of alloys based on the B2 type CoAl [6–10]. The

2. Experimental procedures
Chemical compositions of alloys prepared for the
present work are listed in Table 1 and positioned on the
isothermal section at 1373 K for Co-corner of the Co–
Al–C ternary system shown in Fig. 2, which was eval-
uated by the present authors [15]. All the alloys were
prepared using raw materials with the highest purity
elements readily available. For the preliminary proces-
sing of alloys, a master ingot of alloy A was cast from
the vacuum induction furnace, and those of alloys F, G
and M, from the Tanmann furnace in air. These cast
ingots were re-melted repeatedly at least several times
by arc-melting under an argon gas atmosphere for their
compositional homogeneity. Portions of these ingots
were also used as raw materials for the preparation of
the rest of the alloys by using arc-melting, with compo-
sitional adjustments made by additions of Co and Al.
Annealing heat treatment conditions conducted after
arc-melting were: at 1273 K for 610.8 ks (7 days) in an
evacuated quartz tube for alloys F, G, M, and at 1373 K
for 108 ks (30 h) under ﬂowing argon gas for the rest of
the alloys. Microstructural observations were performed
by means of optical microscopy using specimens chemi-
cally etched in aqua regia after mechanical polishing.
Wet chemical analysis was carried out for selected alloys
as noted in Table 2. The coexisting phases were deter-
mined by X-ray di/C128ractometry.

3.2.1. Compressive mechanical behavior of the Co–Al–C
ternary alloys
To investigate the overall mechanical properties of the
Co–Al–C ternary multi-phase alloys, compression tests
were carried out over a wide temperature range from 77
to 1273 K for six alloys: alloys B, C, G, H and M are the
B2/E2
1/(Co) three-phase, and alloy F is the E2 1/(Co)
two-phase.
Stress–strain curves obtained from compression tests
conducted at various test temperatures are shown for
alloys B, C, G, M, H and F in Fig. 4(a)–(f). In this ﬁg-
ure, note that each compression test was interrupted
when the plastic strain exceeded about 10%. In some
cases in Fig. 4 (b) and (f), the down-arrow indicates the
fracture of the specimen. Excellent compressive ductility
is attained in alloys B, G, H and M at room tempera-
ture, and moreover, at the even lower temperature 77 K
in alloys B, G and M. All the alloys except alloy C
exhibit fairly good compressive ductility above room
temperature up to 1273 K. All the six alloys maintain
relatively high strength levels in the whole test tempera-
ture range. Strain rate dip tests were conducted under
two conditions: ten times higher and one tenth lower
than the initial strain rate. The responses of rises and
drops in ﬂow stress caused by strain rate changes
become larger as the test temperature increases, as seen
in Fig. 4. This phenomenon is explained by the tem-
perature e/C128ect on the plastic deformability owing to the
thermally activated dislocation motion. Small responses
of ﬂow stress at low temperatures might imply that
plastic deformation is borne by the fcc-based E2
1 phase
rather than by the bcc-based B2 phase.
The temperature dependence of the 0.2% compressive
ﬂow stress of alloys B, C, G and M is shown in Fig.
5(a), and of alloys H and F in Fig. 5(b). At elevated
temperatures, attention must be focused on the highest
strength of 340 MPa at 1273 K achieved in alloy B.
Alloys G and F also show high elevated temperature
strength around 300 MPa. It is also noted that a high
strength level surpassing 200 MPa at 1273 K is attained
in all the alloys except for alloy M.
There is a temperature region between 700 and 1000
K where suppression of softening, or even hardening,
due to increase in test temperature appears in all the
alloys. Furthermore, in the case of alloys B and C, there
exists another temperature region between room tem-
perature and about 500 K, in which the same mechanical
anomaly clearly appears. Similar tendencies were found
in other multi-phase alloys containing B2 CoAl in the
binary Co–Al system, B2 and L1
2 phases, in the Co–Ni–
Al(-Ti) systems at higher temperature region, from 700
to 1000 K, as reported previously by the present authors
[6–9]. As discussed in these papers, this phenomenon
must be attributed to the mixed contribution arising
from the nature of the constituent phases. In the present
alloy system, the E2
1 phase could exhibit a mechanical
anomaly similar to what the L1 2 phase shows because of
the marked resemblance in their ordered crystal struc-
tures, as mentioned in the introduction. The peak tem-
perature, giving the maximum strength within each
temperature region, varies from alloy to alloy. It is
rather sensible to assume that this positive temperature
dependence originates from the E2
1 phase but not from
the B2 phase because of the dependence of strength on
the volume fractions of constituent phases. The more
the alloy contains E2
1 phase, the more prominent this
anomalous tendency of strength becomes. Since the
volume fractions of E2 1 phase are quite a bit larger than
those of B2 in alloys both B and C, the cause of the
mechanical anomaly can be attributed to the nature of

The E21 type ordered crystal structure is also called the
Perovskite type structure. Many Perovskite type oxides,
such as BaTiO 3, SrTiO3 and the prototype of this crystal
structure, CaTiO 3, are well-known to exhibit super-
conductivity. Perovskite compounds could be divided
into two categories from their chemical bonding di/C128er-
ence: (i) ‘‘ionic-type,’’ in which face-center sites of the
lattice are occupied by oxygen atoms as in CaTiO
3, and
(ii) ‘‘metallic-type,’’ by transition metal element cobalt
instead of oxygen as in Co
3AlC. Deformation mechan-
isms of the E21 crystal structure have not been fully clariﬁed.
Operative slip systems are reported only for the ‘‘ionic-type’’
Perovskite compounds as {100}<100>, {110}<00> and
{100}<10> [31,32]. Polycrystallineforms of this material
do not exhibit appreciable plasticity, because they are not
supposed to have enough active slip systems at ambient
temperatures. However, it has been revealed in the pre-
sent work that multi-phase alloys containing a sub-
stantial amount, say more than about 50% volume
fraction, of ‘‘metallic-type’’ Perov-skite compound E2
1
Co3AlC show excellent room temperature compressive
ductility. This fact strongly suggests that slip systems
other than those reported for ‘‘ionic-type’’ Perovskite
compounds should operate at ambient temperature.

close-packed planes and directions, i.e. {111}<110>,
which is the same active slip system of the L1

resemblance of the crystal structures between E2

4. Conclusions
Compression tests were carried out over a wide tem-
perature range on the Co–Al–C ternary multi-phase
alloys to observe the general mechanical behavior of
these alloys. Results of compression tests reveal that
some of the alloys have a certain potential for high
temperature structural application. As we expected, the
Co–Al–C ternary is found to be a very attractive alloy
system for exploring good combinations of phases to
coexist with B2 CoAl for the multi-phase alloy which
shows both excellent ambient temperature ductility and
elevated temperature strength. The following conclu-

of this alloy is dominated by E2
1 dendrites and the E2 1/

B2 phase coexists as relatively small particles.
3. The anomalous positive temperature dependence of
strength appears in all the alloys. The propensity of this
mechanical anomaly is remarkably clear in alloys having
