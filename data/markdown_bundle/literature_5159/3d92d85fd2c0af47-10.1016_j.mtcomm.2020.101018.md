1. Introduction 
Nickel-based superalloys have been extensively used as the materials for hot section 
components in modern gas turbine engines, such as disk and turbine blade [1]. Complex 
alloying effects [2,3], as well as advanced manufacturing process have endowed 
superalloys with excellent mechanical properties at elevated temperatures. Addition of 
Al, Ti, resulting in precipitation of γ′ phase, enhances high temperature strength of 
superalloys [4]. Co, W, and Mo are γ partition elements, which play important roles in 
solid solution strengthening [5,6]. Moreover, Co can effectively reduce the stacking 
fault energy of matrix, and has a significant effect on high -temperature creep 
deformation behavior [6]. Cr is added to promote corrosion resistance of superalloys 
[5], and Ta ca n improve high temperature strength and corrosion resistance [7]. The 
creep deformation behavior of nickel -based single crystal superalloys dominates the 
service life of turbine blades [8], which is significantly prolonged via the alloying 
effects. As studied by Zhu et al [9], the shear strain rate in superalloys is closely related 
to effective diffusion coefficients, dislocation density and microstructural parameters 
like volume fraction of γ′ phase and the matrix channel width. Addition of alloying 
elements with low diffusion coefficients can enhance creep resistance of superalloys.

In the past decades, a number of models for creep resistance have been developed 
in literatures [10–12]. The previously proposed creep models mainly predict creep 
properties s uch as minimum creep rate, creep life and creep strain. Based on 
microstructure, process creep condition, and material properties, Kim et al [13] 
developed a mathematical model by modifying and combining previous models to 
predict the creep properties of nickel-based superalloys. Nevertheless, Kim’s model did 
not consider the microstructural changes during the creep process, which only can be 
applied to an intermediate temperature range (650 °C ~ 850 °C) where microstructural 
changes are less considerable. Considering the diffusion -controlled high temperature 
creep deformation behavior, Reed et al [14] have proposed a creep merit index (MCreep) 
to evaluate the creep properties of single crystal superalloys during high temperature 
creep deformation. Among these researches, it is indicated that good creep resistance is 
strongly relevant to the low diffusion coefficient of components in the superalloy matrix. 
For the purpose of improving creep properties of superalloys, investigation on diffusion 
behaviors of su peralloys, especially the determination of accurate interdiffusion 
coefficients in multicomponent superalloys, is indispensable. 
Interdiffusion coefficient, as an important index of the diffusion rate, has been 
extensively investigated among the sub -binary or sub -ternary systems of the very 
complex superalloy [15–18]. Currently, by means of CALPHAD modeling, database 
based on interaction parameters for unary, sub -binary or sub-ternary systems are used 
to predict the thermodynamic and kinetics behavior of mu lticomponent superalloys. 
Unfortunately, the diffusion mobility database based on the experimental data of low 
component system dissatisfy the quantitative research on the actual preparation and 
service behavior of nickel-based superalloys. Zhao et al [19] found that thermodynamic 
calculations have a less satisfactory for the topologically closed -packed (TCP) phases 
through comparing the thermodynamic predictions with experimental results. Campbell 
et al [20] initially established a nickel -based superalloy diffusion mobility database 
using the CALPHAD method, then evaluated the reliability of the diffusion mobility 
database. Owing to the very complicated interaction parameters of the lower order 
systems, the calculated composition profiles of Al and Ti have large deviation from

experimental data for the Ni/Ren é-88 diffusion couple at 1150 °C for 100 h. 
Consequently, it is very necessary to directly obtain accurate interdiffusion coefficients 
in multicomponent nickel-based superalloys by experimental measurements. 
Incorporating the single -phase diffusion couple technique, traditional 
phenomenological analysis methods are widely used to determine the interdiffusivities 
in binary or ternary alloy systems [21]. However, it is almost impossible to apply these 
traditional methods to determine interdiffusion coefficients in multicomponent alloy 
systems. On the one hand, it requires accurate design of a large number of single-phase 
diffusion couples, and low efficiency. On the other hand, it is difficult to design multiple 
diffusion couples with intersection points on the diffusion path. Single-phase diffusion 
couple technique needs to prepare many individual diffusion couples to specific 
diffusion studies. To improve the efficiency of preparation, Zhao et al [22,23] have 
proposed the diffusion multiple technique for high-throughput preparation of diffusion 
couples in a sample . Recently, a software named as HitDIC (High -Throughout 
Determination of Interdiffusion Coefficients) was developed by Zhong et al [24] in the 
framework of pragmatic numerical inverse method [25,26]. It has been successfully 
applied to high -throughput determination of the composition -dependent 
interdiffusivities in different binary and ternary alloys [27,28], as well as high entropy 
alloys (HEAs) [29–31]. While its feasibility in multicomponent nickel -based 
superalloys need further identification. 
In this work, the diffusion multiple technique was used to yield many diffusion 
couples of nickel -based superalloys in a sample, which significantly impr oves the 
efficiency of diffusion studies. Additionally, the HitDIC software was used to determine 
the composition -dependent interdiffusion coefficients in nickel -based superalloys. 
Furthermore, the modified creep merit index model was established and the maximum 
value of MCreep was identified by using genetic algorithm across the selected 
composition space. The established multicomponent nickel-based superalloys diffusion 
mobility database will accelerate the design and optimization of superalloys with 
optimal creep resistance.

2. Materials and methods 
2.1. Diffusion multiple experiment 
On the basis of one superalloy developed in our research group, the ingots of three 
superalloys were prepared by casting a mixture of constituent elements using vacuum 
induction melting. As listed in Table 1, the nominal compositions of the Alloy2 and 
Alloy3 have more contents of Mo and Ti than the base alloy named Alloy1, respectively. 
The ingots were hot isostatic pressing (HIPed) at 1200 °C /100 MPa for 4 h to eliminate 
the solidification pores and chemical segregation. Then, the ingots were cut into blocks 
of 4mm×4mm except for Alloy1 that was cut into blocks of 8mm×4mm, polished and 
placed in a Ni can as shown in Fig. 1(a). The specific geometry of a diffusion multiple 
depends on the eutectic temperature (to avoid melting during diffusion annealing 
process) and the diffusion coefficients such that the dimensions of various alloy pieces 
should be designed to have pure elements remaining at the end of diffusion annealing. 
Thereafter, the diffusion multiple was electron beam welded and then HIPed at 1180 °C 
for 8 h to achieve good interfacial contact among the alloy pieces. Finally, the diffusion 
multiple underwent two ageing treatment at 1280 °C for 100 h, 1080 °C/4 h+870 °C/20 
h in diffusion annealed furnace, followed by water quenching to retain the 
microstructures and homogeneities at high temperature. The compositions of diffusion 
couples were determined using Micro-XRF (X-ray fluorescence) along the red lines, as 
highlighted in Fig. 1(a). The spot size of Micro-XRF was 15 μm and the step size was 
20 μm. More details about the diffusion-multiple approach were provided in Ref. [32].

4. Conclusion 
In this work, the composition -dependent interdiffusion coefficient matrices of fcc 
Ni-Cr-Al-Co-W-Mo-Ti-Ta superalloys at 1553 K were determined by combining the 
diffusion multiple technique and HitDIC software in the framework of the parametric 
numerical inverse method. Moreover, the correlations between the components and the 
main interdiffusivities were carefully analyzed by modifying the creep merit index 
model. Basically, the following conclusions can be drawn: 
1. The model -predicted composition profiles based on the presently evaluated 
temperature dependent atomic mobility parameters can reproduce the measured 
data reasonably. Moreover, the calculated interdiffusion coefficients of sub-ternary 
agree well with the experimental results from the literatures. These facts indicate 
that presently evaluated atomic mobility parameters in fcc Ni-Cr-Al-Co-W-Mo-Ti-
Ta superalloys are reliable. 
2. On the basis of Alloy1, the sequence for the component with the fastest diffusion 
rate (the highest interdiffusion coefficient) to that with the slowest is: Al > Cr > Ta > 
Ti > Co > Mo > W. Addition of Al+Ti can accelerate the diffusion pr ocess, while

20 
 
refractory elements (W+Mo+Ta) can decelerate the diffusion process in superalloys. 
The correlations between the components and diffusion rates were further analyzed, 
which indicates that there are complex interactions among elements. 
3. The modified creep merit index model can effectively evaluate the creep resistance 
of nickel -based single crystal superalloys during high temperature creep 
deformation. The applicability of the modified creep model would be more accurate 
if interdiffusivities of Re and Ru in multicomponent nickel -based superalloys are 
combined. The presently assessed diffusion mobility database provides guidance 
role for the design and development of a novel Re -free single crystal superalloy 
with optimal creep resistance.
