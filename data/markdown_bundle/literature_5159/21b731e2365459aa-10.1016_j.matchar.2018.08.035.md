ABSTRACT
Metastable β Ti alloys based on Nb exhibit features that make them suitable for load-bearing biomedical ap-
plications, so it is crucial to evaluate their phase transformation and properties. This paper discusses the eﬀ ects
of aging heat treatments on the microstructure, phase transformation and mechanical behavior of Ti e35Nb
alloy. Samples were solution heat treated in the β ﬁeld, quenched and aged under di ﬀerent conditions of heating
rates, isothermal aging temperature and isothermal aging time. To enhance the alloy's mechanical behavior,
thermal analysis and high temperature X-ray di ﬀraction experiments coupled with Vickers hardness tests were
employed to determine the temperature range of isothermal ω phase precipitation, as well as α phase pre-
cipitation temperature, thereby providing information to optimize aging heat treatment conditions. Results
indicate that Ti e35Nb alloy subjected to a heating rate of 30 °C/min and an isothermal aging temperature of
500 °C applied for 4 h would ensure high mechanical strength and low elastic modulus. The application of these
aging conditions led to a microstructure consisting of very re ﬁned α phase precipitates homogeneously dispersed
in the β phase matrix, presenting an ultimate tensile strength of 972 MPa, elongation of 7.5% and elastic
modulus of 78 GPa, making this aged alloy a promising candidate for biomedical applications. These results shed
light on the correlations between phase transformations, microstructure and mechanical behavior in high solute
content Ti eNb alloys for biomedical applications.
1. Introduction
The manufacture of biomedical implants for hard tissue replace-
ment, such as the stem in total hip arthroplasty (THA), requires mate-
rials that are not only highly biocompatible but that also possess sui-
table properties, such as low density, high corrosion resistance, high
tensile strength, high ductility, high fatigue resistance, low modulus
and a ﬀordable cost [ 1,2]. Among the metallic materials employed for
biomedical purposes, titanium and its alloys are an outstanding choice,
particularly Ti-6Al-4 V alloy, which was originally developed for the
aerospace industry [ 2].
Although Ti-6Al-4 V alloy is highly resistant to degradation in the
human body and exhibits interesting mechanical behavior, reports in
the literature suggest that the release of its ions may have toxic e ﬀects
on human cells [ 3]. An additional aspect of Ti-6Al-4 V alloy is its re-
latively high elastic modulus (110 GPa) when compared to that of
cortical bones (10 –25 GPa). In THA, sti ﬀ femoral stem implants alter
bone load distribution, reducing the mechanical stress applied to the
bone due to the bone stress shielding phenomenon, which leads to bone
remodeling, and eventually, to prosthesis failure [ 3,4]. For this reason,
much eﬀort has focused on the development of Ti alloys with improved
biocompatibility and low elastic modulus, while still maintaining high
mechanical strength. This e ﬀort has led to the development of me-
tastable β Ti alloys.
Metastable β Ti alloys based on non-toxic and non-allergic alloying
elements, particularly Nb, possess features that make them suitable for
load-bearing biomedical applications. Among the examples of suc-
cessful application of β Ti alloys in THA implants are Ti-35Nb-7Zr-5Ta
(TNZT) and Ti-29Nb-13Ta-4.6Zr (TNTZ) alloys. When compared to
α+β Ti-6Al-4 V alloy, TNZT and TNTZ alloys show signi ﬁcantly higher
biocompatibility and better mechanical behavior. A common aspect of
the two aforementioned alloys is their high content of Nb, which is a
non-toxic alloy element and an interesting β stabilizing element.

when cooled slowly from high temperatures in the β ﬁeld to room
temperature, the microstructure of Ti e35Nb alloy comprises a β phase
matrix and inter- and intragranular α precipitates, as well as isothermal
ω phase. It is known that ω phase and grain boundary α phase pre-

[6]. On the other hand, when subjected to water quenching, the
Tie35Nb alloy microstructure consists of a mixture of β phase and
orthorhombic martensite, which is soft and ductile, thus leading to
lower mechanical strength and higher ductility [ 6]. If aged at inter-
mediary temperatures, the quenched Ti e35Nb alloy undergoes a
complex process involving martensite decomposition, ω phase pre-
cipitation, and ﬁnally, α phase precipitation [ 7]. Under favorable
conditions, aging heat treatments applied to β Ti alloys can trigger ﬁne
α phase precipitation in the β matrix, signi ﬁcantly increasing their
mechanical strength [ 8–10]. The mechanical behavior of these alloys
strongly depends on the characteristics of α phase precipitates, such as
morphology, size, volume fraction and distribution in the β matrix,
which are substantially a ﬀected by aging conditions such as tempera-
ture, holding time and heating and cooling rates. High heating rates
result in a relatively coarse α precipitates distributed unevenly in the
matrix. On the other hand, lower heating rates favor the uniform dis-
tribution of precipitates in the β matrix [ 11–13].
Considering that the development of novel Ti alloys for load-bearing
biomedical applications is based essentially on the Ti eNb system, and
that correlations between the aging heat treatment conditions and mi-
crostructure of Ti eNb alloys are not readily available, this study fo-
cused on evaluating the response of heating rates, temperatures and
holding times on the microstructure and mechanical behavior of a high
Nb content Ti alloy (Ti e35Nb wt%).
2.
Experimental Procedure
The experimental alloy with a Ti e35Nb (wt%) composition was
prepared from high purity metals by arc melting, under Ar atmosphere
and using a non-consumable tungsten electrode. To achieve high che-
mical homogeneity, the ingots were ﬂipped and remelted at least ﬁve
times, sealed in quartz tubes under Ar atmosphere, heat-treated at
1000 °C for 24 h and furnace cooled to room temperature. The ingots
were then hot swaged at 900 °C to produce 8 mm diameter rods. These
specimens were solution heat-treated at 1000 °C for 1 h, water-quen-
ched (ST) and aging heat-treated (STA) at 400 and 500 °C for 0 h,
10 min, 1, 4, 8 and 32 h, followed by water quenching. The heating
rates applied to the samples to reach the aging temperature were set at
1 and 30 °C/min.
The samples' chemical composition was determined by X-ray
ﬂuorescence spectroscopy (XRF) in a Shimadzu 7000 EDX-7000 spec-
trometer, while O and N interstitial contents were determined using a
Leco TC400 analyzer. Samples used in the microstructural analyses
were subjected to conventional metallographic preparation, including
sanding with 400 and 1500 grit sandpaper and polishing with a 6 μm
diamond paste, and lastly, with a 0.03 μm abrasive solution (260 ml
colloidal silica suspension, 40 ml H
2O2, 1 ml HNO 3 and 0.5 ml HF).
Microstructural features were analyzed by scanning electron micro-
scopy (SEM) (FEI Inspect F50) and transmission electron microscopy
(TEM) (MSC Jeol 2100) operated at 200 kV. Specimens for TEM ana-
lysis were prepared by the conventional route, i.e., punching 3 mm
disks followed by dimpling and polishing in a precision ion polishing
system.
The crystalline phases were identi ﬁed by X-ray di ﬀraction (XRD)
analyses using a PANalytical X'Pert Pro di ﬀractometer operating at
40 kV and 30 mA with a Cu-K α radiation source and a PiXcel X-ray
detector in the angular (2 θ) range of 30 –90°. High-temperature XRD
(HTXRD) experiments were carried out in the aforementioned dif-
fractometer equipped with an Anton Paar high temperature chamber,

3. Results and Discussion
The chemical composition of the experimental alloy was determined
by XRF and the results are described in Table 1. The measured Nb
content matches the nominal one and the amount of interstitial ele-
ments (oxygen and nitrogen) is acceptable, according to the ASTM-
F67–13 standard.
The microstructural analysis of ST Ti e35Nb alloy reveals a micro-
structure consisting of β phase and acicular orthorhombic martensite,
with a β grain size of approximately 400 μm( Fig. 1). The SEM image
was taken from backscattered electrons signal of the unetched sample.
Initially, in order to determine suitable aging heat treatment con-
ditions, ST samples were subjected to thermal analysis by DSC.
According to Fig. 2, the ﬁrst heating cycle elicited an endothermic re-
action (1), which corresponds to martensitic decomposition ( α″ → β
and α″ → α + β) between 200 and 230 °C (determined by di ﬀer-
entiating the DSC curve). The next segment of this curve refers to iso-
thermal ω phase precipitation ( β → ω
iso). Bonisch et al. [ 14], who in-

TieNb alloys with Nb content higher than 33 (wt%). However, the
results of our study clearly show a marked exothermic peak (2) at
around 300 °C pertaining to ω phase precipitation.

to ω phase dissolution, followed by α phase precipitation (4), which
begins at 500 °C. Considering the Ti eNb phase diagram [ 14], the DSC
results suggest that the β-transus temperature is close to 640 °C. An

Fig. 3 shows the HTXRD curves of the ST Ti e35Nb alloy. The
sample was solution heat treated in the β ﬁeld, quenched to room
temperature, and then heated at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. XRD scans
were recorded at 100 °C intervals up to a maximum temperature of
600 °C. This temperature limit was selected to avoid excessive sample

The martensitic α″ phase peaks were detected from room tem-
perature up to 200 °C, at which point its total decomposition was de-
tected. This ﬁnding is consistent with the endothermic peak (1) in the
DSC curve ( Fig. 2). The precipitation of α phase was detected at 500 °C,
while peaks related to ω phase were not identi ﬁed in the HTXRD ex-
periment, probably due to its small volume fraction. Di ﬃculties in
detecting ω phase peaks in this temperature interval using X-ray dif-
fraction were also reported by other authors, although the progressive
precipitation and growth of this phase may be followed by an increase
in the hardness pro ﬁle [ 16–18].
Samples of the ST Ti e35Nb alloy were then aged at 400 and 500 °C,
using heating rates of 1 and 30 °C/min, for time intervals of up to 32 h.
An aspect worth noting is the di ﬀerence in the total time of heat
treatments. At a heating rate of 30 °C/min, the total time of the heating
step ranged from 13 to 17 min, while a heating rate of 1 °C/min
required a total time of > 8 h. Kobayashi et al. [ 19], who studied phase
transformations in Ti e32Nb alloy, suggested a diagram that correlates
ω and α phase precipitation with time and temperature. According to
their ﬁndings, the ω solvus of Ti e32Nb alloy is close to 420 °C [ 19].
Aging heat treatment at temperatures below the ω solvus is essential for
isothermal ω precipitation and growth, which is crucial to ensure
greater mechanical strength and good ductility [ 5,7,20].
However, ac-
cording to Mo ﬀat et al. [ 20], α phase precipitation may occur at tem-
peratures below the ω solvus if the aging heat treatment lasts too long.
Fig. 4 presents the XRD patterns of samples aged at 400 °C, applying
heating rates of 1 °C/min ( Fig. 4 a) and 30 °C/min ( Fig. 4b). The results
of the XRD and SEM analyses do not show all the holding times applied
in this study, but only the most signi ﬁcant ones. All these steps are
shown in Fig. 7, which presents the Vickers hardness pro ﬁle. Martensite
decomposition during aging at the lower heating rate occurred before
10 min of holding time, probably during the temperature ramp-up.
Isothermal ω phase peaks, which were detected after 1 h at 400 °C,
remained in the microstructure for 32 h at this temperature, after which
the ﬁrst evidence of α phase precipitation was detected. However, no
indication of α phase was visible in the micrographs, probably because
of the small size of the precipitates. In this case, ω phase precipitates act
as nucleation sites for α phase precipitation. The ω assisted α phase
nucleation has been investigated by several authors [ 9,21–24] and is
the most traditional heterogeneous nucleation sites in these alloys.
Increasing the heating rate to 30 °C/min resulted in partial mar-
tensite decomposition during the heating ramp-up and ω phase pre-
cipitation took place after the isothermal aging time of 1 h.

aging temperature reduced atomic di ﬀusion in the β phase, thus hin-

According to the DSC results, 500 °C is the proper temperature for α
phase precipitation in Ti e35Nb alloy. To evaluate the in ﬂuence of
heating rates and the extension of isothermal aging time on α phase
nucleation, heat treatments were applied at heating rates of 1 and
30 °C/min to the temperature of 500 °C. Fig. 5 depicts XRD patterns of
Tie35Nb samples aged for 0, 1, 4, and 32 h. Evidence of orthorhombic
martensite formation was found after 0 h of isothermal aging, i.e., when
the sample had just reached 500 °C and was immediately water quen-
ched.
Banumathy et al. [ 25], who investigated Ti e27Nb alloy aged at 450
and 550 °C and then water quenched, found that martensite can be
formed at the onset of α phase nucleation. Solute rejection during phase
transformation leads to regions with lower Nb content, which favors
martensite formation during rapid cooling. Also, the XRD patterns
showed no indication of isothermal ω phase precipitation.
The XRD patterns suggest α phase precipitation occurred after 1 h of
isothermal aging, without evidence of ω phase precipitation by XRD,
regardless of the heating rate applied. Therefore, the intensity of α
phase di ﬀraction peaks increased with isothermal aging time.
Aging heat treatment parameters e ﬀectively control microstructural
features, including size, volume fraction and distribution of pre-
cipitates, and hence, the mechanical behavior of Ti alloys [ 26]. Among
those parameters, the heating rate applied to reach the isothermal aging
temperature greatly a ﬀects precipitation of ω phase, which is important
in α phase precipitation. Figs. 6 and 7 show SEM micrographs of
samples aged at 500 °C for 0, 1, 4 and 32 h, applying heating rates of 1
and 30 °C/min, respectively. These images were taken from SEM
backscattered signal of the unetched samples.
According to the literature, high heating rates lead to micro-
structures containing coarse α phase laths, with precipitate free zones
close to the grain boundaries. On the other hand, lower heating rates
produce homogeneously dispersed microstructures consisting of ﬁne α
phase laths in the β matrix. In such a re ﬁned microstructure, isothermal
ω phase formed during the heating step acts as a nucleation site for α
phase precipitation [ 11,12,27].
While lower heating rates lead to ω
phase precipitation through a two-stage phase transformation pathway,
β → β + ω → β + α, higher heating rates lead to direct phase trans-
formation, β → α. However, the results obtained here suggest that there
was no signi ﬁcant di ﬀerence between the two heating rates (1 and
30 °C/min) applied to reach 500 °C.

Considering the microstructures and corresponding Vickers hard-
ness numbers, the following optimized aging heat treatment parameters
were selected: a heating rate of 30 °C/min, isothermal aging tempera-
ture of 500 °C, and lastly, isothermal aging time of 4 h. In a short period
of time, these conditions successfully resulted in samples with very
reﬁned α phase microstructures dispersed in the β matrix, with an
appreciable gain in hardness.
The TEM images in Fig. 9 show a STA Ti e35Nb alloy (aged at
500 °C, applying a heating rate of 30 °C/min) with α phase laths and the
corresponding SADP in the < 113 > β zone axis, showing spots of α
and β phases and the absence of spots of ω phase. A microstructure
consisting of re ﬁned α phase precipitates in the β matrix, without ω
phase precipitation, corresponds to the best combination to achieve
optimized mechanical behavior for load-bearing biomedical applica-
tions. These conditions were then applied to produce samples for tensile
tests.
Table 2 compares data obtained from tensile tests of Ti e35Nb
samples processed under two conditions: solution heat treated and
water quenched (ST) and solution heat treated, water quenched and
aged (STA). According to a previous report [ 6], the ST Ti e35Nb alloy
presents an ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of 555 MPa and a yield
strength (YS) of 343 MPa. On the other hand, aging heat treatments
lead to considerably increased mechanical strength. Despite a decrease
in elongation and a slight increase in elastic modulus, the STA Ti e35Nb
sample shows UTS of 972 MPa and YS of 889 MPa.
These values are similar to other Ti alloys used in the biomedical
ﬁeld.
For instance, the Ti-13Nb-13Zr alloy (ASTM F1713) which is
standardized for this purpose, presents a 8% elongation, 860 MPa ten-
sile strength and 725 MPa yield strength in aged condition [ 28]. Also,
the Ti-6Al-4V ELI (ASTM F13608) alloy presents a 8% elongation,
825 MPa tensile strength and 760 MPa yield strength [ 29]. Compara-
tively, the Ti e35Nb alloy presents improved mechanical strength while
maintaining the ductility.
Fig. 10 shows the stress-strain curve ( Fig. 10(a)) and fracture sur-
face images ( Fig. 10(b –d)) of the STA Ti e35Nb samples (500 °C/4 h/
30 °C/min). Notwithstanding the relatively low initial hardness of these
samples, their mechanical strength was substantially improved by the
aging heat treatment. Stress-induced martensitic transformation
(double yield point) was not detected in the tensile curve, probably
because the α-lath precipitates prevented the transformation of β into
α″. In this case, α phase precipitates act as barriers to dislocation, in-
creasing mechanical strength [ 30,31].
The SEM fractographs of tensile test samples present a mixed mode
ductile and brittle fracture. The cleavage fracture surface and presence
of dimples in some areas may be attributed to a microstructure of α
phase precipitates in the β matrix. According to the literature, homo-
geneous and ﬁne α phase precipitation increases mechanical strength
while maintaining ductility to an acceptable level [ 32–35].
4. Conclusions
During Ti e35Nb aging treatments, the heating rate, isothermal
aging temperature and aging time strongly a ﬀect the phase precipita-
tion, microstructure, and hence, the mechanical behavior of this alloy.
Thermal analysis combined with high temperature XRD experiments
enabled us to determine phase transformation temperatures, thus op-
timizing the conditions of aging heat treatment. It was found that iso-
thermal ω phase precipitation occurs above 300 °C, its dissolution takes
place between 430 and 500 °C, and lastly, α phase precipitation in this
alloy occurs at close to 500 °C.

The heating rate employed to reach the isothermal aging tempera-
ture is extremely important, since it a ﬀects isothermal ω phase pre-
cipitation, which is fundamental to ω assisted α phase precipitation.
Lower heating rates intensify isothermal ω phase precipitation.
However, the application of 1 and 30 °C/min heating rates did not re-
sult in a substantial di ﬀerence in the morphology, size and volume
fraction of α precipitates in the β matrix. The heating rate of 30 °C/min
was probably insu ﬃcient to hinder isothermal ω phase precipitation
and therefore did not a ﬀect ω assisted α phase precipitation. Our results
indicate that the Ti e35Nb alloy subjected to a heating rate of 30 °C/min
to reach an isothermal aging temperature of 500 °C applied for 4 h
would ensure high mechanical strength, low elastic modulus and ac-
ceptable ductility. Aging in such conditions resulted in a microstructure
with re ﬁned α phase precipitates well dispersed in the β phase matrix,
with an ultimate tensile strength of 972 MPa, elongation of 7.5% and
elastic modulus of 78 GPa, making this aged alloy a promising candi-
date for biomedical applications.
