In many high temperature applications tungsten is superior to molybdenum alloys. For structural
components very often joining technology is the limiting factor. If brazing or welding is used ductility at
room temperature has to be considered. Particularly when handling or transporting they run the risk of
brittle fracture.
Relevant properties are suf ﬁcient fracture strength and ductility at ambient temperatures after high
temperature annealing as well as high recrystallization temperature. We studied several tungsten alloys
performing 3 point bending tests, annealing experiments and metallographic examinations. In respect to
environmental considerations possible alloys should not contain thoria.
Alloying with rare earth oxides enhance the fracture strength at ambient temperatures after high
temperature annealing and increases the recrystallization start temperature. The results indicate that
suitable thermomechanical treatment increases the recrystallization temperature by more than 500 °C and
improve the fracture strength by a factor of 1.5. Favourable results with Th-free tungsten alloys are possible.

1. Introduction
In many applications (e.g. waste incineration or pyrolysis, quartz
glass manufacturing, and fusion reactor ITER) increasing operating
temperatures are needed. Above 1600 °C in most instances chemical,
physical or mechanical properties of tungsten are superior to
molybdenum. For complex shaped components joining of two or
more parts is necessary. If mechanical joining is not potential, welding
or brazing has been taken into account. Typical brazing temperatures
of Pt-, Zr-, Ru-, Rh-based brazes are in the range of 1600 –2000 °C.
Diffusion welding is often performed at 2000 °C. After this heat
treatment tungsten and its alloys are more or less recrystallized.
Whereas a recrystallized microstructure is not a problem above
brittle-to-ductile-temperature the brittleness at ambient tempera-
tures is a critical item. If brazing is ﬁnished successfully the parts have
to be packed and shipped to a customer or are mounted to other
components. In spite of precautions parts sometimes crack in the
brazed/welded zone due to shock loading, vibrations or bending
stresses. Besides the unusable (and often not repairable) part a lot of
costs and trouble are generated. Material with high resistance to
fracture and a suf ﬁcient “ductility” in terms of bending after high
temperature annealing is needed.
We studied two aspects of commercial tungsten production: the
inﬂuence of alloying element and alloy composition as well as process
parameters and annealing temperature on both the bending behav-
iour at room temperature and the recrystallization start temperature.
Over the years thoriated tungsten alloys turned out best
performing of all commercially available tungsten alloys for such
requirements. They combine high temperature strength and high
recrystallization temperature. Recently due to health care and
environmental considerations (Thoria is an alpha radiator) big efforts
have been made to substitute thoriated tungsten products (e.g.
tungsten welding electrodes with additions of 2 wt.% Ce 2O3 or 1 wt.%
La2O3). So a new material should not contain thorium oxides.
Following the “green” trend some lanthanum oxides (La 2O3) doped
tungsten alloys are compared to WVMWT as reference. The total
amount of La 2O3 was varied to ﬁnd the optimum volume content of
secondary phase particles. Further it was assumed that fracture
behaviour at room temperature corresponds with the degree of
recrystallization. Thus, we examined the recrystallization start
temperature in dependence of the deformation temperature and the
degree of deformation. Present paper sets value on the preparation of
experimental alloys on an existing industrial processing route for
which the parameters mentioned have been varied carefully.

As mentioned above, parts often break due to shock loading.
Consequently we performed charpy impact tests at room tempera-
ture. No signi ﬁcant results were found because all alloys failed at the
same (low) impact energy. To get on with this kind of test impact load
has to be reduced tremendous leaving the frame of reproducible
standard test. So we decided to proceed with a three-point bending
test (3PB). Specimens with the dimensions of 4 × 4 × 40 mm were
machined from rolled rods. Tests were done at room temperature
using a servo-hydraulic tensile test machine with a crosshead speed of
2 mm/min. The span of the bearing was 25 mm. Highest stress
sustained (= fracture strength) and maximum bending angle before
cracking of the sample were recorded. All samples (at least 10 per
alloy) were tested in two annealing conditions: ﬁrstly at 1000 °C
which is a typical stress relieve annealing for tungsten materials.
Secondly annealed at 1860 °C to simulate brazing procedure e.g. Zr-
braze which is of common use for joining tungsten parts. Both
annealings were performed in hydrogen atmosphere for 2 h.
Recrystallization behaviour was studied by Vickers hardness
measurement and optical micrographs on heat treated samples.
Given data are mean values of 9 indentations over the cross section of
the rolled rod.
3. Results and discussion
Fig. 1 shows the result of 3PB-tests. In the stress relieved condition
fracture strength and bending angle of all alloys are within a small
range indicating that the bending behaviour is independent of the
alloy composition. The standard deviation for both parameters is 3 –
7%. Additional tests on pure tungsten reveal a fracture strength of
2320 MPa and a bending angle of 4°. This proofs the idea that the
fracture strength of hot worked tungsten alloys is 2300 –2600 MPa
and the bending angle is 4 –5° respectively. As there is no signi ﬁcant
difference over a broad range of different tungsten alloys the bending
behaviour seems to be driven by the properties of hot worked
tungsten matrix and not by the composition of the alloy.
The bending behaviour in the annealed state is different. Here clear
differences for the tested alloys are visible. The best alloy (WL-S)
shows a 50% higher performance compared to the worst alloy (WVM).
The loss in fracture strength/bending angle from the stress relieved to
the annealed state is caused by the annealing, obviously. The
annealing behaviour is documented in Fig. 2 . Vickers hardness of
WVM shows a sharp decrease from 480HV30 to 390HV30 at 1600 °C.
Values of ≤ 390HV30 are typical for recrystallized tungsten. By
contrast WVMWT and WL-S reveal a steady decrease of hardness up
to 2100 °C without reaching the fully recrystallized state.
These results are conﬁrmed by optical micrographs of the tungsten
alloys after an annealing at 1860 °C ( Fig. 3 ). Whereas WVM is fully
recrystallized, WVMWT and WL-S show a heavily deformed micro-
structure with no signs of recrystallization. Nevertheless a decrease in
hardness after annealing is observed for WVMWT and WL-S. So other
microstructural mechanisms like dislocation annihilation or re-
arrangement cause this reduction in hardness. All other alloys
perform something in between these two extrema. The microstruc-
ture of WL10 and doped WL10 is very similar with coarse deformed
grains besides some recrystallized grains. WLZ has a similar
microstructure in all investigated annealing conditions with a
deformed microstructure and ﬁrst signs of recrystallization, e.g.
bowed grain boundaries. From these results we conclude that fracture
behaviour at room temperature is strongly linked to microstructure of
tungsten matrix. A deformed and not recrystallized microstructure is
favourable for high fracture strength/bending angle.

It is well known that cold working increases the strength at least at
room temperature. To bene ﬁt from this effect ﬁnal deformation steps
in the rolling process were performed at a reduced preheating
temperature (1400 °C). Fig. 4 shows the results of the bending tests
after 1860 °C annealing. For WVMWT no data is presented because
cold rolling has induced cracks in the rod and no sample has been
available for bending tests.
Stress relieved samples have not been tested. The annealed
samples from the hot and cold rolled rods have no signi ﬁcant
difference in bending behaviour. The scatter is in the range of the
standard derivation. These results reveal that the energy brought to
the rod by cold working is completely resolved by the annealing at
high temperatures.
Fig. 5 illustrates the fracture strength depending on the degree of
deformation. WL10 receives an increase of 50% by enhancing the
degree of deformation from 1.7 to 4.6. Similar results are gained for
WVMWT. After sintering the micron sized lanthanum oxides are
globular shaped (Fig. 6). During deformation the oxides get elongated
to needle- or plate-like structures ( Fig. 6) [1,2]. They act as obstacles
for dislocation and grain boundary movement rising the recrystalli-
zation start temperature ( Fig. 7 ): the higher the degree of deforma-
tion the higher the recrystallization temperature. Over a wide range of

change their shape. But at very high annealing temperatures the
elongated oxides collapse to small pearl like rows ( Fig. 6) according to
Rayleigh's law which is well described for potassium bubbles in
tungsten or La 2O3 in molybdenum [3–5]. In the area where the
collapsing starts the grain boundaries are free to move until hitting
another insuperable obstacle leaving a recrystallized microstructure
behind. The recrystallization begins. This behaviour results in a steady
decrease of the hardness and increase of the amount of the
recrystallized area respectively. Thus recrystallization progress of
highly deformed WL is quite different from pure tungsten which is
characterized by decreasing sta rt temperature with increasing
deformation and an S-curve behaviour in the hardness-annealing
temperature diagram.
Please note that the recrystallization behaviour of the very high
deformed materials (log φ N 4.6 in Fig. 7) has been determined on thin
wires.
Findings above indicate that a high recrystallization start tempera-
ture is bene ﬁcial for high fracture strength. Increasing the degree of
deformation (at constant La
2O3 content) is one possibility to increase
the recrystallization temperature. Increasing the total amount of oxides
might be another. In Fig. 8 the fracture strength/bending angle for low
La2O3 content containing WL10 and high La2O3 content containing WLZ
at low and medium degree of deformation is plotted. At low degree of
deformation (log φ = 1.7) the fracture strength of WL10 is 15% higher
than that of WLZ. After annealing at 1860 °C both microstructures were
fully recrystallized and consequently the fracture strength was low (on a
comparable level). Increasing the degree of deformation to log φ =2 . 5
improves the bending behaviour signi ﬁcantly: now the higher doped
WLZ is 15% superior to the lower doped WL10 ( Fig. 3 comprises the
corresponding microstructures). A higher total amount of oxide
particles also can be bene ﬁcial, but the main part of improvement
results from the degree of deformation. Whereas WL10 experiences a
20% increase in fracture strength compared to the lower degree of
deformation, the fracture strength of WLZ is improved even more than
50%. Thus, the improvement mainly derives from the higher degree of
deformation and is more advantageous for higher doped alloys. It must
be remarked that the improvement by enhancing the La
2O3 content is
noticeable (at least at a higher degree of deformation) but not as high as
possibly assumed. So, according to the requirements of the application
alloy composition and production process has to be coordinated
properly. With that increasing the degree of deformation is much
more effective than increasing the total amount of alloy content.
4. Conclusions
The bending behaviour of stress relieved annealed tungsten ODS
(oxide dispersion strengthened) alloys is independent of the
composition or volume content of alloying elements. Also production
parameters such as rolling temperature or degree of deformation are
negligible. By contrast after high temperature heat treatment the
fracture strength and bending ductility is mainly dominated by the
microstructure, e.g. the degree of recrystallization of the tungsten
matrix. The higher the degree of recrystallization the lower the
fracture strength/bending angle. The degree of deformation is very
effective to improve recrystallization behaviour in ODS tungsten
alloys while the oxide content has only very little impact. The results
can be summarized as follows:
▪ The higher the degree of deformation the higher the recrystalli-
zation start temperature.
▪ The higher the recrystallization start temperature the higher the
fracture strength.
▪ Deformed microstructure provides high fracture strength whereas
recrystallized decreases it.
▪ The thoria free W alloy WL-S exceeds the fracture strength
properties of thoriated materials.
For lanthanated tungsten a minimum of degree of deformation is
needed to provide high recrystallization temperature. This limits the
dimensions of large structural parts. Using nanosized particles in
combination with adequate deformation could be bene ﬁcial.
