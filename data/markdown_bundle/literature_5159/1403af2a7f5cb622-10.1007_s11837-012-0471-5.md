Titanium aluminide (TiAl) alloys exhibit high speciﬁc strength, low density,
good oxidation, corrosion, and creep resistance at elevated temperatures,
making them good candidate materials for aerospace and automotive appli-
cations. TiAl alloys also show excellent radiation resistance and low neutron
activation, and they can be developed to have various microstructures,
allowing different combinations of properties for various extreme environ-
ments. Hence, TiAl alloys may be used in advanced nuclear systems as high-
temperature structural materials. Moreover, TiAl alloys are good materials to
be used for fundamental studies on microstructural effects on irradiation
behavior of advanced nuclear structural materials. This article reviews the
microstructure, creep, radiation, and oxidation properties of TiAl alloys in
comparison with other nuclear structural materials to assess the potential of
TiAl alloys as candidate structural materials for future nuclear applications.
INTRODUCTION
Titanium aluminide (TiAl) alloys were initially
developed for moderate temperature (600–850 /C176C)
applications in the aerospace and automotive
industries because they have high speciﬁc strength,
low density, good oxidation, and good corrosion and
creep resistance at elevated temperatures.
1,2 The
very ﬁrst production of TiAl parts was for turbo-
charger wheels with a low volume application in
Japan.
3 However, poor room-temperature ductility
and workability have limited their wide commercial
applications.
4 Recently, General Electric (Fairﬁeld,
CT) has used c-TiAl to manufacture low-pressure
turbine blades on its GEnx engine, which powers
the Boeing 787 and Boeing 747-8 aircrafts.
5 This is
the ﬁrst large-scale use of this material in a com-
mercial jet engine. The laboratory impact test to
simulate potential blade damage resulting from the
engine ingesting foreign objects showed that despite
limited ductility, TiAl alloys can withstand a rela-
tively large impact without failing catastrophically.
5
TiAl alloys have also received much attention as
nuclear structural materials because they exhibit not
only good high-temperature creep resistance but also
excellent radiation resistance and low neutron acti-
vation.
6–10 The structural materials for Gen-IV
reactors will need to endure higher temperatures and
more severe radiation damage than those experi-
enced in current light water reactors (LWR).
11–14 For
example, the outlet temperature will need to increase
from about 300/C176C in current LWRs to up to 650/C176Ci n
supercritical water-cooled reactor (SCWR), and
1000/C176C in very high temperature gas-cooled reactor
(VHTR) or molten salt reactor (MSR). 14,15 The
essential considerations in the selection of structural
metallic materials for advanced nuclear applications
are creep properties and radiation tolerances, which
are strongly dependent on alloy composition and
microstructure. In Japan, TiAl alloys have been de-
signed as structural materials for the blanket of the
steady-state tokamak reactor.
10

MICROSTRUCTURE
Most recently developed TiAl alloys have the Al
content of 45–48%, which have two lamellar phases
in the ﬁnal microstructure, a
2-Ti3Al and c-TiAl. The
c phase has face-centered tetragonal structure,
while the a2 phase has a hexagonal close-packed
structure. When the alloys are cooled from the sin-
gle a phase ﬁeld, the c phase precipitates from the a
or a
2 phase, and the Shoji-Nishiyama orientation
relationship holds between the a2 and the c phases,
i.e., the (0001) plane of thea2 phase is parallel to the
{111} plane of the c phase, forming a lamellar
microstructure.16 By adding b stabilizing elements
or C or Si in TiAl alloys and controlling the heat
treatment processes, b phase17 and other hard pre-
cipitates like Ti3AlC1 and Ti5Si3
18 can be introduced
to the microstructure.

siﬁed as the following four types, in terms of the
volume fraction of lamellar colonies and equiaxed c

duplex, and near-gamma structures. 1,19 Figure 1

CREEP RESISTANCE
During creep deformation, the most important
deformation mechanisms are dislocation and/or
diffusion-controlled mechanisms.
23–25 The disloca-
tion mechanisms by dislocation glide or climb are
important deformation modes for metallic materials
and can be the dominant deformation modes for
nuclear structural materials over a wide tempera-
ture range without any signiﬁcant inﬂuence of dif-
fusion creep.
25,26 The creep deformation behavior,
deformation mechanisms, and microstructural
effects on creep of TiAl alloys have been intensively
investigated.
1,17,20,27,28 It has been found that dis-
location creep is the dominant mechanism in creep
deformation for most TiAl alloys at high stress as
well.
23 The dislocation mechanisms involve the
movement of dislocations through the crystal lat-
tice. If the moving dislocations encounter obstacles
along their path, then they must overcome the
obstacles, which decreases their average moving
velocity and improves the creep resistance.
23–25 The
obstacles in TiAl alloys include various microstruc-
tural features such as precipitates, phase interfaces
and grain boundaries, etc. Hence, any microstruc-
tural features that can inﬂuence the movement of
dislocations can inﬂuence the creep resistance of
TiAl alloys.
The initial microstructure has a great effect on
microstructural instability and creep resistance of
TiAl alloys.
20 Equiaxed grains at grain boundaries
degrade creep resistance, and an FL microstructure
provides superior creep resistance compared to a
duplex structure or an equiaxed c structure.
20,27,29
Finer lamellar spacing has been found to improve
the creep resistance of the FL microstructure. Pri-
marily, this is because the lamellar interfaces hin-
der dislocation motion. 20 Figure 2 shows the
deformation microstructure of a 47XD TiAl alloy
after creep at 760 /C176C and 207 MPa. The bowing and
tangling of the dislocations between lamellar inter-
faces are the results of dislocation movement and
interaction during creep deformation. However,
greater fractions of a
2 phase have been found to
increase the creep strain rate because the a2 phase
is softer than the c phase at elevated tempera-
tures.17,30 This suggests a ﬁne lamellar spacing with
the thinnest a2 lamellae at a2/c boundaries is of
beneﬁt to creep properties. 17 Smooth grain bound-
aries, ﬁne grain size, and ﬁne lamellae accelerate
microstructural degradation during creep and pro-
mote the onset of grain boundary sliding, decreasing
creep resistance.
20,28 TiAl alloys exhibit better creep
resistance in the hard orientation, with the lamellar
plates parallel or perpendicular to the compression
axis than in a soft orientation, with the lamellar
plates oriented at an arbitrary angle to compression
axis.
31
Figure 3 depicts a Larson–Miller plot comparing
the creep strength of TiAl alloys with other nuclear
structural materials. Oxide dispersion-strength-
ened (ODS) steels are the most promising candidate
steels for high-temperature nuclear applications. As
shown in Fig. 3, the ODS steels including 14YWT,
12YWT, PM2000, and EUROFER 97 ODS exhibit
better creep resistance than the non-ODS materials
like grade 91, EUROFER 97, and IN617. TiAl alloys
exhibit even more superior creep resistance to most
ODS steels and are comparable to the recently
developed 14YWT. Hence, TiAl alloys exhibit com-
parable or better creep resistance than most
metallic materials used for advanced nuclear sys-
tems.
RADIATION RESISTANCE
TiAl alloys have been irradiated using various
incident species including ion beams, electrons,
protons, and neutrons.
6,39–45 The corresponding
defect formation and structural changes have
therefore been investigated. It was found that pla-
nar defect clusters and dislocation loops were ﬁrst
formed during irradiation.
34,41,44 At higher dose
irradiations, phase transformation was found to
occur. For example, when irradiated with Xe ions at
doses larger than 2.2 9 10
18 ions m /C0 2 or with Ar
ions at doses of 1.1 9 1020 ions m /C0 2 phase trans-
formation was induced. 34,45 Cavities were also
observed after irradiation to a further larger dose or
irradiation at high temperatures. 34,39,44,46,47

OXIDATION RESISTANCE
When TiAl alloys are held in an oxidizing envi-
ronment at elevated temperatures, they react and
form a scale of either titanium oxides or alumina
depending on test conditions and composition.
For good oxidation resistance, the formation of a
continuous layer of alumina is required.
54 The
oxidation resistance of TiAl alloys depends on many
factors including temperature, alloy composition,
microstructure, and environment. 54 The tempera-
ture has a great inﬂuence on oxidation resistance of
TiAl alloys. It is found that the weight gain at
1000/C176C is twice that at 800 /C176C for a Ti48Al2Nb2Cr
alloy for oxidation in 0.1 MPa N
2-20% O2 environ-
ment and that this alloy exhibits good oxidation
resistance up to 1000 /C176C.
55 Increased oxidation
resistance with higher aluminum content has been
observed for oxidation at 900 /C176C in an argon/oxygen
mixture.54 Also, the alloying additions have signiﬁ-
cant inﬂuence on oxidation behavior of TiAl alloys.
Niobium is the most beneﬁcial alloying addition
because it can facilitate the formation of a protective
alumina scale and can improve the oxidation resis-
tance.
54,56 The other elements that have a beneﬁcial
effect on the oxidation resistance of TiAl alloys in-
clude tungsten, silicon, silver, chlorine, and large
amounts of chromium.
56
The oxidation behavior of TiAl alloys also depends
on the alloy microstructure. 55,56 Figure 6 presents
weight gain as a function of exposure time obtained
for TiAl alloys (Ti47Al2Cr and Ti46Al1Cr + Si) with
different microstructures.
56,57 The as-cast alloys
have a FL microstructure, while the heat treatment
or extrusion resulted in duplex microstructure with
coarse a
2 grains and coarse a2 lamellae.55,56 It is
evident that the FL microstructure exhibits better
oxidation resistance than the duplex microstructure
for both alloys. It is found that during high-tem-
perature oxidation, the alumina scale can be stable
in Al-rich c phase, while the titanium oxide is stable
on the a
2 phase, and the c phase composition in
equilibrium with the a2 phase. Hence, a thick, non-
protective titanium oxide scale could form in the
microstructure with coarse a2 phase, instead of
forming an alumina scale, greatly decreasing the

Although experiments in irradiation behavior and
oxidation behavior of TiAl alloys in various extreme
conditions are very limited to date, all the results still
suggest that TiAl alloys are excellent potential candi-
date materials for future nuclear structural applica-
tions. However, one of the factors limiting applications
of TiAl alloys in advanced nuclear systems is still poor
room-temperature ductility and workability, which is
being continually investigated by materials scientists.
Also, advanced manufacturing technologies such as
superplastic forming and diffusion bonding are being
studied for TiAl alloys to manufacture complex com-
ponents.
54,63 On the other hand, TiAl alloys may be
designed to make high-temperature structural com-
ponents without a high requirement on ductility. More
work is needed in studying creep, irradiation, irradia-
tion creep, and oxidation behavior of TiAl alloys in the
nuclear environments.
SUMMARY
TiAl alloys exhibit comparable or better creep,
radiation and oxidation resistance than most
nuclear structural materials, suggesting that TiAl
alloys are good potential materials for advanced
nuclear applications. Also, these materials can be
developed to exhibit various microstructures
enabling optimization to suit various applications
and enabling fundamental studies of microstruc-
tural effects on creep and radiation properties to
develop new radiation tolerant nuclear structural
materials. Much further scientiﬁc research and
development is required in nuclear design, materi-
als development, and manufacturing technologies to
