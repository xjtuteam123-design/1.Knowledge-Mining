1. Introduction 
High temperature shape memory alloys possessing martensitic 
transformation above 100 
◦
C have recently attracted considerable 
attention as functional materials in robotic, aerospace and energy 
exploration industries [ 1 – 3 ]. To date, high temperature shape memory 
alloys (HTSMAs) mainly include Ti
–
Ni-X(X = Zr, Hf, Pd, Pt), Ni-Mn- 
based, Cu-based, Ti-based alloys and so on [ 4 – 8 ]. By comparison, Ti- 
based alloys exhibit excellent cold working ability. They could be pro -
cessed easily into fine wires or thin plates according to the requirements 
of practical application. Consequently, Ti-based alloys including Ti
–
V 
based alloys, Ti
–
Nb based alloys and Ti
–
Ta based alloys have been 
studied extensively [ 9 – 11 ]. 
Systematic researches involved martensitic transformation, micro -
structure and functional properties of the Ti
–
Nb based alloys have been 
carried out [ 12 , 13 ]. Ti
–
Nb alloys undergo a reversible martensitic 
transformation between β parent phase (bcc) and α
′′
martensite phase 
(orthorhombic) [ 14 ]. Kim et al. reported that the increasing of Nb 
content would result in the liner decrease in transformation strain and 
transformation temperature [ 15 ]. The theoretical phase transformation 
strain decreases from 6% to 3%, while the Nb content increases from 16 
at.% to 26 at.%, according to the phenomenological theory of martensite 
crystallography (PTMC) theory. Unfortunately, only 2% recoverable 
strain can be obtained in the solution-treated Ti
–
16Nb (at.%) alloy, 
which is significantly lower than the theoretical value of transformation 
strain [ 16 ]. The terrible recoverable strain is related to the relatively 
high critical stress for martensite reorientation and the relatively low 
critical stress for dislocation slip. Consequently, the martensite reor -
ientation process and plastic deformation simultaneously occurs during 
deformation, which leads to the poor shape memory effect (SME). 
Furthermore, the hexagonal ω phase, formed by quenching from the β 
phase, would result in the brittleness, unstable shape memory effect and 
terrible thermal stability [ 17 , 18 ]. 
Massive efforts have been devoted to solve the above problems to 
improve the shape memory effect of the Ti
–
Nb based alloys. It is well- 
known that the addition of ternary alloying element can enhance the

2. Experimental Procedure 
Ti
82-x
Nb
16
Al
2
Er
x 
(x = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0) (at.%) ingots were 
processed from high-purity stating elements of Ti (99.99 wt%), Nb 
(99.90 wt%), Al (99.99 wt%) and Er (99.90 wt%) by arc-melting furnace 
under an argon atmosphere. To ensure the homogeneous chemical 
composition, every ingot was melted for six times. The ingots were ho -
mogenized at 1173 K for 4 h under vacuum and then water quenched. 
After that, the ingots were hot rolled to a plate with a thickness of about 
2 mm. The samples for the subsequent measures were cut from the plate 
by an electro discharge machine. All samples sealed into quartz tubes 
under a high vacuum were annealed at 1173 K for 2 h, and water 
quenched by breaking the quartz tube. 
The phase compositions were carried out using Panalytical Empy -
rean X-ray diffraction diffractometer with Cu K α radiation at room 
temperature. Microstructural analysis was performed by the field- 
emission scanning electron microscope (FEI, Quanta 200F). The trans -
mission electron microscope (Talos F200X) investigations were also 
applied to the microstructure observation. The TEM specimens were 
mechanically ground to about 80 μ m firstly and then prepared by twin- 
jet electropolishing. The solution of 6 vol% perchloric acid, 60 vol% 
methyl alcohol and 34 vol% n-butyl alcohol was chosen for the elec -
trolyte. Differential scanning calorimetry (Perkin-Elmer DSC 8000) was 
utilized to determine the martensitic transformation temperatures. The 
mechanical properties were carried out by Instron 5569 universal 
testing system at a deformation speed of 0.4 mm/min. To determine the 
shape memory property, samples were deformed in the 4% pre-strain at 
room temperature, followed by heating to A
f 
+ 50 K for strain recovery.

and fracture strain are presented in Fig. 8 (b). It is observed that the yield 
strength and fracture strength are significantly enhanced with the 
increasing of the Er element, which increased from 347 MPa to 780 MPa 
and 601 MPa to 902 MPa, respectively. The fracture stain exhibits a 
slightly decreasing trend. The fracture strain decreased from 13.1% to 
9.9% with the increasing of Er addition from 0.1 at.% to 1.0 at.%. 
However, when the Er addition is 2.0 at.%, the yield strength and 
fracture strength exhibit a significant decrease, while the fracture strain 
is continuously improved by 16.7%. The effect of Er addition on the 
mechanical properties including strength and elongation are discussed. 
The strength increases firstly and then decreases with the increasing of 
the Er addition. Firstly, the strength is enhanced due to the combination 
of grain refinement and precipitation strengthening. On the one hand, 
the grain size decreases with the addition of Er element. According to the 
Hall-Petch theory, the relationship between the strength and the grain 
size can be summarized as σ = σ

movement of the martensite plates is severely hampered, when the 
amounts of Er-rich phase are too high. Consequently, the proper Er 
element addition is beneficial for the improvement of shape memory 
property.
