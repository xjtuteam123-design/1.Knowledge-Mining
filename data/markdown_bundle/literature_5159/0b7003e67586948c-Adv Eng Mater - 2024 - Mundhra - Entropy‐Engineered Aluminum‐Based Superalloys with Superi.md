1. Introduction
Despite having low density, low cost, a creep-resistant FCC
structure, and superior oxidation resistance, achieving high-
temperature (HT) strength has been the Achilles heel in HT
Al alloy research and for over four decades, the pursuit of this
objective has endured, pushing researchers to pursue further
breakthroughs.[1,2] Skinner et al. [1] created nanostructured
Al– Fe– V– Si alloys by utilizing rapid solidi ﬁcation processing
(RSP) in conjunction with vacuum hot pressing and extrusion
for full densi ﬁcation. These alloys have
proven highly successful, with a relatively
stable quaternary cubic Al 12(Fe,V)3Si
phase.[1] Nonetheless, their yield strength
(YS) drops to half at 343 °C, indicating
the need for further improvement in their
HT capability. According to Knipling
et al. [2] producing precipitation-strength-
ened HT Al alloys with exceptional HT sta-
bility and strength requires trialuminide
compounds that possess a L1 2 structure,
low diffusivity, and low solid solubility in
the Al matrix. Al 3Sc has gained signi ﬁcant
attention among the thermodynamically
stable L12 trialuminides discovered in tran-
sition metals (TMs), but Sc is a very expen-
sive element.[2] Although elements like Cu,
Ni, Zn, etc. are known for stabilizing Al3TM
by partitioning into the Al-sublattice, the
volume fraction of the second phase formed
is quite low which limits the mechanical
properties of the resultant alloy. [2] In con-
trast, metastable L1 2-structured trialumi-
nides have been observed in Al alloys
containing Ti, Zr, Hf, V, and Nb during rapid cooling or aging
with precipitation in the Al-TM binary or ternary systems. [2– 9]
The high-entropy concept has led to a paradigm shift in the
material design approaches in the last two decades. [10] More
recently, high-entropy intermetallic compounds with ultra-high
strength and exceptional thermal stability have emerged. [11]
To overcome the previously mentioned ingrained bottleneck,
we have used our in-house CALPHAD-guided compositional
design[12– 14] and entropy maximization [10] to engineer the
TM sublattice in D0 22-type Al 3(TM) to form a disordered

In the quest for lightweight high-performance metallic materials in transporta-
tion, the limitations of high-strength Al-based alloys in high-temperature (HT)
applications have posed a signi ﬁcant challenge. To overcome this long-standing
bottleneck, an innovative design strategy, combining entropy-driven composi-
tional design and rapid solidi ﬁcation processing, to develop “Al-based superal-
loys” with a γ/γ 0 duplex microstructure reminiscent of Ni-based superalloys is
presented. By incorporating multirefractory elements (Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb, and Ta) into
the alloy design, a high-volume fraction of thermally stable coherent γ 0 phase for
strengthening the Al-rich face-centered cubic matrix from room temperature (RT)
to temperatures close to the melting point is achieved. The developed Al-based
superalloys exhibit exceptional speci ﬁc yield strength and speci ﬁc Young ’s
modulus, surpassing aerospace aluminum, cobalt, nickel, and titanium alloys
from RT to HT. Moreover, our ultra-strong alloys demonstrate deformability and
exhibit ﬁne-dimpled fracture behavior. The versatility of our fundamental
research on Al-based superalloys demonstrates their potential and can inspire
further research in this ﬁeld. This work opens new possibilities for the devel-
opment of next-generation lightweight alloys with novel microstructures and
outstanding mechanical properties.

Al’s lower elastic modulus (70 GPa) allows it to maintain
coherency with Al 3TM-type L1 2 IP by accommodating relatively
larger mis ﬁt strains with minimal interfacial dislocation
generation, contrasting Ni-based superalloys [23] (elastic modulus
≈200 GPa) that lose coherency at higher mis ﬁt levels ( >0.1%).
Our Al-based superalloys feature small, uniformly distributed
Al3TM IP particles (20 – 50 nm) which minimize strain energy
and preserves their coherency, unlike larger γ 0 precipitates
(100– 200 nm) in Ni-based superalloys. [23] Rapid solidi ﬁcation
facilitates in situ formation of these nanoscale precipitates, with
fast cooling promoting their coherent structure by restricting
atomic diffusion coupled with sluggish diffusion kinetics. [10]
Increased con ﬁgurational entropy from elements like Ti, Zr,
Hf, Nb, and Ta stabilizes the L1 2 phase under rapid cooling,
maintaining coherency even at relatively higher mis ﬁt
strains than Ni-based superalloys. [23] Strong directional bonding
between TM d-orbitals and Al p-orbitals enhances bonding
strength. γ/γ 0 interface characteristics were investigated using
HRTEM, IFFT, and geometric phase analysis (GPA) [24]
(Figure 6 and 7), revealing higher lattice distortion in the FCC
phase due to its lower modulus compared to the L1 2 phase.

The superior mechanical properties demonstrated by our
Al-based superalloys at elevated temperatures are related to
the high thermal stability of L1 2 IP (Figure 8) and can be
explained as follows. The low lattice mismatch renders the nano-
scale L1 2 IP to remain coherent and bene ﬁt from nanoscale
stabilization even at elevated temperatures. [29,30] In addition,
the TM-enriched chemistry of the nanoscale L1 2 IP confers them
with higher thermodynamic and kinetic stability, since heteroge-
neous coarsening (which is triggered at elevated temperatures)
requires the cooperative diffusion of high MP TMs, which is hin-
dered under such a scenario due to complex entropy-stabilized
phase chemistry. [10] Therefore, these factors facilitate the reten-
tion of solid solution and precipitation strengthening in our
Al-based superalloys even under HT exposure. It should be noted
that the L12 IP in AS6 is a high-entropy Al 3(Hf,Nb,Ta,Ti,Zr)-type
IP, whereas AS3 is a ternary Al 3(Ti,Zr)-type IP. These results
demonstrate that the incorporation of a higher number of refrac-
tory elements in AS6 accounts for its superior solid solution
strengthening, precipitation strengthening, and thermal stability.
3. Conclusion
Our efforts to combine the merits of multielement entropy-
driven phase stabilization with targeted microstructure-design
have led to the discovery of a new class of advanced multicom-
ponent Al-based superalloys. Our alloys possess the characteristic
γ/γ 0 duplex microstructure with high-density coherent γ 0 IP,
reminiscent of typical Ni-, Co-, or high-entropy superalloys.
We have experimentally con ﬁrmed the crystallography and
chemistry of γ/γ 0 nanostructures in our alloys through XRD,
S/TEM, HREM, and 3D-APT. The tailored microstructure char-
acterized by thermally stable nanoscale γ 0 IP (with high-TM con-
centration) coherent with the solid solution strengthened Al-rich
FCC matrix (with enhanced TM solubility) is reported for the
ﬁrst time in Al-based alloys systems. Due to these traits, our alloys
demonstrate outstanding softening resistance even at high
homologous temperatures, resulting in superior temperature-
dependent mechanical properties and respectable RT
deformability. Our Al-based superalloys remarkedly outper-
formed the state-of-the-art aerospace and HT-Al alloys [34,35,41– 45]
in SYS and SYM and can be potential materials of choice for
weight reduction. The present discovery opens new design path-
ways for developing the next generation of materials with both HT
strength and deformability. We envision our alloys to be the next
frontier for lightweight design, in developing materials that are
not only stiff and of low-density, but also strong and ductile
and at the same time cost-effective. Most importantly, our funda-
mental alloy design of Al-based superalloys demonstrates their
potential and can inspire further research efforts in this
ﬁeld for the development of advanced lightweight alloys.
We anticipate our alloys to ﬁnd utility in a wide gamut of
advanced structural applications, ranging from automobiles, air-
craft, rockets, advanced propulsion, and aeronautic systems.
This can contribute to reducing the carbon footprint of aviation
while simultaneously achieving high energy ef ﬁciency in
transportation.
4. Experimental Section
Alloy Fabrication : The chemical compositions of the alloys studied in
this work were Al 87.5Ti6.25Zr6.25
[12] and Al 87.5Hf2.5Nb2.5Ti2.5Ta2.5Zr2.5 (in
at%). The fabrication of as-cast alloys was done in a vacuum arc-melting
furnace under an inert Ar atmosphere (purity higher than 99.99%) (Alfa
Aeser, Thermo Scienti ﬁc). Each ingot was ﬂipped and remelted at least
5 times during the arc-melting process to attain proper compositional
homogeneity and complete alloying. Subsequently, we performed rapid
solidiﬁcation on arc-cast ingots to obtain MS ribbons. Vacuum melt
spinning was performed under an inert Ar atmosphere on a rotating
chilled copper roller with a linear wheel surface speed of around
25 ms/C0 1(1500 RPM). Obtained MS ribbons were 3 – 4 mm wide and
≈40 μm in thickness, depending on the alloy composition. We used
Archimedes’ method to measure the density of the designed alloys.
Structural, Microstructural, Atomic Scale, and Fractographic
Characterization: We performed XRD measurements to resolve the phase
constitution of our alloys ’ using a Bruker D8 discover X-ray diffractometer
(Bruker AXS GmbH, Germany). Cu-K α radiation ( λ = 0.15406 nm, 40 kV,
and 40 mA) was used to perform XRD measurements. The slow-scan range
was from 2 θ of 20 to 90° with a step size of 0.02° with step time of 2 s.
Site-speciﬁc electron transparent cross-sectional TEM lamellae were
extracted from WS region of MS ribbon samples using Ga þ focussed
ion beam (FIB) milling on Helios G4 dual-beam FIB-scanning electron
microscope (FIB-SEM) (ThermoScienti ﬁc, Waltham, Massachusetts,
USA). We performed TEM and STEM investigations on a Talos F200X
G2 high brightness X-FEG TEM (ThermoScienti ﬁc, Waltham,
Massachusetts, USA) at 200 kV accelerating voltage. Using a Super-X
EDS detector system comprising four in-column silicon drift detectors
(SDDs) (Bruker Nano GmbH, Germany), we performed STEM-EDS meas-
urements. The HAADF detector collection angle range was between 76.7
and 200 mrad. We performed scalebar calibration for TEM, HRTEM, and
HAADF-STEM micrographs by using lattice fringes of Si {111} reference
standard with a d-spacing of 0.314 nm. We performed STEM-NBD by
reducing the beam probe size to about 2 –5 nm. TEM and HRTEM micro-
graphs were acquired using Gatan Microscopy Suite-3 software. STEM-
HAADF micrographs were acquired using ThermoScienti ﬁc TIA software.
EDS data were acquired and analyzed on Esprit Quantax software Version
1.9 (Bruker Nano GmbH, Germany). Fourier- ﬁltered (FFT) images were
generated from HRTEM and HRSTEM-HAADF micrographs using
Gatan Microscopy Suite-3 software.

Instruments Inc., Madison, WI). The APT tips were produced using FIB on
FEI Helios G4UX dual-beam FIB-SEM (ThermoScienti ﬁc, Waltham,

tenth of the MS ribbon thickness to avoid glue and substrate effect on the
NI results. We used a video camera for bending tests to digitize the bent
samples and extract the information on bending radius for bending strain
calculation. We tested 5 different samples for each MS alloy composition
by type-1 and type-2 bending test to obtain repeatable values.
