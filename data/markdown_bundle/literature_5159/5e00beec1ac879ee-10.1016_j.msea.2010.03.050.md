abstract
In this investigation, the affect of applied tensile stresses on the isothermal and cyclic oxidation behav-
ior of nickel-based superalloys was studied. The inﬂuence of applied stresses was investigated through
measurements of speciﬁc mass change, oxide morphology and thickness, degree and depth of internal
oxidation, and compositional changes in and around the oxide scales. It was determined that an applied
tensile stress generally led to larger Cr/Ni ratios in the oxide scales, greater overall chromium depletion
values directly under the oxide scales, lower speciﬁc mass change values, and thinner oxide scales. These
affects are attributed to a greater concentration of dislocations, defects, etc. in the materials caused by
the applied stress. These defects act as fast diffusion paths for the Cr atoms to diffuse to the surface, and
thus, cause a reduction in the duration of the less protective transient oxidation period, and promote a
faster formation of the protective Cr
2 O3 layer.

1. Introduction
Due to their favorable strength and excellent resistance to
oxidation (and many other aggressive environments) at elevated
temperatures, nickel (Ni)-based alloys are used in nuclear reactors,
electrical-resistance heaters, gas turbines, and the petrochemical,
aerospace, and heat-treating industries [1–5]. It is the ability of
Ni-based alloys to form protective surface oxide scales at high
temperatures that endows them with resistance to further high
temperature corrosion [1,6–8].
Under the aforementioned applications, Ni-based alloys
encounter elevated temperatures, thermal cycling, and/or applied
stresses. Studies on isothermal and/or cyclic high-temperature oxi-
dation behavior of Ni-based alloys are plentiful in the literature
[1,2,4–7,9–18]. Literature on the affects that applied stresses have
on the oxidation behavior of Ni-based alloys is much sparser, and
thus, is the subject of this research.
In the few available resources concerned with applied stress
affects on oxidation, two possible scenarios were discovered. In
the ﬁrst case, the applied stress caused or was theorized to cause
a greater amount of oxidation due to cracking and spalling of the
oxide layer and/or an increase in specimen surface area [2,19–22].
In case two, the applied stress caused or was theorized to cause a
reduction in the amount of oxidation due to a modiﬁcation of dif-

4. Discussion
The application of a load under oxidizing conditions generally
resulted in lower speciﬁc mass change values and thinner oxide
scales, thus indicating slower oxidation rates under the applied
stress. A possible explanation for the slower oxidation rates of
stressed specimens is rooted in the formation of oxide layers on
a typical Ni–20Cr alloy.
In the early stages of oxidation, a continuous NiO layer forms
on the outer grain surfaces, while Cr
2O3 islands form in the grain
boundaries [18]. As the outer NiO layer grows into the metal, it
encounters islands of Cr 2O3, and the result is the formation of
NiCr2O4 spinel islands [26]. Ni-containing oxides are less protec-
tive than Cr2O3, and thus, considerable amounts of NiO and NiCr2O4
form before the more protective Cr 2O3 layer forms [26]. This affect
is known as transient oxidation [26]. Because they are less protec-
tive, oxide scales containing higher amounts of Ni are thicker over
time than those containing greater amounts of Cr. It has been sug-
gested that tensile deformation could modify transient oxidation
rates, and promote the faster formation of Cr
2O3 layers in contrast
to mixed oxide spinels [19]. Since the stress generally caused the
scales to be thinner, faster diffusion of Cr to the alloy surface to form
a more protective layer is implied. The application of load under
oxidizing conditions generally resulted in larger Cr/Ni ratios in the
oxide scales and greater overall Cr depletion values under the oxide
scales. These trends point to faster Cr diffusion to the alloy surfaces
and shorter transient oxidation periods. A faster diffusion rate of Cr
to the surface of an alloy of similar composition when stress was
applied is supported by the results of another study [23].
Applied stresses may inﬂuence diffusivities in a material by
increasing dislocation densities [19,20]. It is believed that the
applied stress caused more defects to form in the grain boundaries
of the alloys. It is also possible that elongated defect structures were
created within the grains themselves. These grain and grain bound-
ary defects acted as fast diffusion paths for Cr atoms, increased the
ﬂux of Cr (J
Cr) in the stressed specimens, and resulted in a faster for-
mation of Cr 2O3. The faster formation of protective Cr 2O3 reduced
the transient oxidation period, improved oxidation resistance in the
stressed specimens over the intermediate time period, and resulted
in the thinner oxides and lower speciﬁc mass change values. A
rough sketch of this process appears in Fig. 15 . The round struc-
tures symbolize the grain and grain boundary defects that act as
fast diffusion paths for the Cr atoms. The portrayal of these defects
as round structures is not meant to be taken as physically accurate,
but as a means of simplifying the overall process. It is important
to point out that while the faster formation of the chromia layer
caused by the application of stress results in more favorable oxida-
tion properties over the course of the 1000 h exposures, the alloy’s
Cr reservoir is likely being depleted more quickly, which may result
in less favorable oxidation properties over longer time periods,
potentially reducing the alloy’s overall service lifetime.
The formation of a more Cr-rich oxide may account for the
reduction in faceting of the stressed specimens as compared with
unstressed specimens [Figs. 4(b)–(d) and 5(b) and (c)]. The faceting
reduction may also be explained by deformation of the crystals
under stress. The exact cause of the faceting reduction could not
be determined.
The ability of the oxides to remain intact on the surfaces of the
stressed specimens may be called into question. Some oxide scale
deformation under stress is possible without total failure of the
scale [19,20,28,29]. This deformation is attributed to simultaneous
cracking and rapid healing of the oxide layer known as pseudo-
plasticity [19,20,28,29]. At higher temperatures, pseudoplasticity

The amount of spallation, if any, of the oxide layers under the
applied stresses did not seem signiﬁcant. However, spallation could
not be ruled out since there were no means of collecting possible
spallation from stressed specimens. Also, oxidation rate constants
are a function of specimen area, an area that is changing under
stress. Future studies should include methods for collecting poten-
tial spallation and accounting for area changes under stress.
The 230 alloy specimens demonstrated better creep resistance
than the 75 alloy specimens, likely due to compositional differences
and larger average grain size. The oxide scales of the 230 alloy speci-
mens were generally thinner and formed at slower rates than those
of the 75 alloy specimens. The 230 alloy specimens also demon-
strated less internal oxidation damage than those of the 75 alloy
under all conditions. These trends indicated that the 230 alloy had
greater oxidation resistance as compared with the 75 alloy, likely
due to its greater Cr content and minor alloying additions.
5. Conclusions
The following conclusions were drawn from the results of this
study:
1. The application of a load under oxidizing conditions generally
resulted in lower speciﬁc mass change values and thinner oxide
scales, thus indicating a slower oxidation rate under the applied
stress.
2. The application of a load under oxidizing conditions generally
lead to larger Cr/Ni ratios in the oxide scales, greater overall
chromium depletion values directly under the oxide scales, and
thinner oxide scales. All of these trends point to faster chromium
diffusion to the alloy surfaces and shorter transient oxidation
periods.
3. The application of stress resulted in a less-faceted oxide mor-
phology possibly due to the deformation of large faceted oxide
crystals, or the formation of different surface oxides.
4. In comparison with the 75 alloy specimens, the 230 alloy spec-
imens demonstrated better creep resistance likely due to their
larger grain sizes, and better oxidation resistance likely due to
their greater chromium contents and minor alloying additions.
