ABSTRACT 
In the present study, the electron concentration, bond order and metallic average d-electron orbital energy level 
of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys were controlled by adjusting the Hf content, which further determined the 
phase constituents and phase stability. The Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with various Hf contents were 
composed of the α ´´ martensite phase and β phase, regardless of Hf contents. With increasing Hf content, the 
martensitic transformation temperature ( Ap ) slightly increased. Hf addition could enhance the strength and 
optimize the strain recovery characteristics. A higher strength and larger recoverable strain can be obtained in Ti- 
Nb shape memory alloy modified by 2 at.% Hf due to grain refinement, precipitation strengthening and solution 
strengthening. Training can achieve perfect superelasticity with a completely recoverable strain of approximately 
2%. In conclusion, the perfect combination of damping capacity and strain recovery characteristics can be 
attained by tailoring the Hf content in Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Binary Ti-Nb shape memory alloys have been considered the most 
promising candidates for Ni-free biomedical superelastic alloys owing to 
their excellent mechanical properties with good biocompatibility, 
corrosion resistance, shape memory effect and superelasticity [1 – 3] . 
However, the smaller transformation strain is the inherent nature of 
binary Ti-Nb shape memory alloys, which is dramatically less than that 
of commercial Ti-Ni superelastic alloys [ 1 , 4 , 5 ]. 
The transformation strain of Ti-Nb shape memory alloys is primarily 
related to their crystal structure. Thus, the addition of alloying elements 
is most crucial to improve the transformation strain for TiNb-based 
shape memory alloys, because the crystal structure can be affected by 
the alloying elements [ 1 , 6 ] . Moreover, the higher critical stress for 
dislocation slip is also an important factor for obtaining outstanding 
strain recovery properties. To date, the main methods to increase the 
critical stress for plastic deformation consist of solid solution strength -
ening, precipitation strengthening, work hardening and grain size 
refinement, as well as the excellent combination of the abovementioned 
methods [ 1 , 7 – 11 ]. Moreover, several alloying elements (Sn, Al, Ta, Zr, 
Mo, Pd, Pt, Ag, O, N, etc.) have been adopted to improve the super -
elasticity with a larger recoverable strain in Ti-Nb shape memory alloys 
[12 – 24] . Among the various alloying elements, Zr seems to be the most 
effective in enhancing the transformation strain of Ti-Nb shape memory 
alloys [ 6 , 25 , 26 ]. Meanwhile, Zr addition has no obvious influence on 
the martensitic transformation. In addition, thermo-mechanical treat -
ment is also an effective measure to enhance the strain recovery prop -
erties as a result of precipitation strengthening, work hardening and 
grain refinement [1] . A maximum total recoverable strain higher than 
4.3% can be achieved in thermo-mechanical treated Ti-Nb-Zr shape 
memory alloys by optimizing Zr content [ 12 , 26 ]. 
It is well known that both Zr and Hf element belong to the same 
group, which has a similar effect in adjusting the mechanical and 
functional properties of shape memory alloys [ 27 , 28 ]. At the same time, 
it has been revealed that the microstructural features and mechanical 
properties of TiNb-based shape memory alloys can be tailored to develop 
promising materials for various applications by adding Hf in the previ -
ous literature [29] . For instance, the good biocompatibility of Ti-Nb-Hf

2. Experimental procedures 
A series of alloy ingots with the nominal composition Ti-22Nb-xHf 
(x = 1 at.%, 2 at.% and 3 at.%) shape memory alloys were prepared by 
arc melting at an Ar atmosphere from higher purity Ti (99.9 5wt.%), Nb 
(99.95 wt.%) and Hf (99.92 wt.%). To achieve compositional homoge -
neity, the Ti-Nb-Hf alloy ingots were repeatedly melted at least six times. 
The present Ti-Nb-Hf alloy ingots were homogenized at 850
◦

and then hot-rolled into sheets with a thickness of 3 mm. Subsequently, 
the hot-rolled Ti-Nb-Hf sheets were heat treated at 850
◦

lowed by cold rolling with a reduction of 70%. The samples for the 
following characterizations were prepared by wire electrode cutting. 
Then, the specimens with the moderate standards were encapsulated in 
sealed quartz tubes under the protection of Ar and solution treated at 
900
◦
C for 3 h. 
The microstructures of the samples were featured with optical mi -

mechanically polished on silicon carbide abrasive papers and then

energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX). The phase constitutions of Ti-Nb-Hf al -

α

mechanical properties were evaluated by using a universal mechanical 
testing machine with a strain rate of 0.2 mm/min. The size of the tensile 
samples is 40 mm (length) × 2 mm (width) × 0.7 mm (thickness). The 
gauge length of the tensile specimens is 20 mm. The shape memory ef -
fect was characterized by loading – unloading tensile tests with pre- 
strains of 2%, 4%, 6% and 8%. In addition, the effect of cycling on the 
strain recovery characteristics of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys was 
carried out by cyclic loading – unloading tensile tests with a constant 
strain of 3%. The damping behaviors were performed on a dynamic 
mechanical analyzer (DMA; Q800 from TA Instruments) with a single 
cantilever clamp. The damping capacity (tan δ ) was measured during 
the step heating procedure with a frequency of 0.5 Hz and an oscillation 
amplitude of 0.05%. During the step heating procedure, the samples 
were initially heated to the target testing temperature and then kept 
isothermally for 5 min to reach temperature equilibrium. The mea -
surements of the DMA samples were as follows: 30 mm × 4 mm × 1 mm. 
3. Results and discussion 
Fig. 1 represents the optical images of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys 
with different Hf contents. The microstructure of Ti-Nb-Hf shape 
memory alloys with different Hf contents is characterized by equiaxed 
grains, which can be indexed as the lathy α ´´ martensite phase and 
featureless β parent phase. In addition, the grain size of Ti-Nb-Hf shape 
memory alloys is refined slightly as a result of Hf addition. As the Hf 
content is increased from 1 at.% to 3 at.%, the grain size is reduced from 
approximately 85 μ m to 45 μ m.

the Hf contents in Ti-Nb-Hf alloys are listed in Table 2 . The increase in 
lattice parameters can be attributed to the larger atomic radius of Hf 
compared with Ti. The coexistence of the α ´´ martensite phase and β 
parent phase is detected in Ti-Nb-Hf alloys, as the Hf content changes. 
The difference is that the intensity of the diffraction peak in Ti-Nb-Hf 
alloys is closely related to the Hf content. With increasing Hf content, 
the intensity of the (110) peak of the β parent phase gradually increases, 
whereas the intensity of the diffraction peaks corresponding to the α ´´
martensite phase becomes increasingly weaker. This means that Ti 
substitution with Hf in Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys results in a 
reduction in the volume fraction of the α ´´ martensite phase and an in -
crease in the percentage of the β parent phase. Similar results have been 
revealed in Ti-Ta-Hf shape memory alloys [29] . The phase constitutions 
of β -Ti-based SMAs are largely dependent on the electron concentration 
(e/a) [30] . In addition, a single β phase exists in β -Ti alloys, when the 
electron concentration is greater than 4.20 [ 21 , 31 ]. However, the 
electron concentrations of the present Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys 
with different Hf contents (1 at.%, 2 at.% and 3 at.%) are calculated to 
be 4.1915, 4.1878 and 4.1850, respectively. This means that the α ´´
martensite phase and β parent phase coexist in the Ti-Nb-Hf shape 
memory alloy, when the electron concentration ranges between 4.1915 
and 4.1850. Meanwhile, the decrease of electron concentration results in 
a reduction in the volume fraction of the β phase.

Both the yield stress and maximum fracture stress first increase and then 
decrease. As the Hf content is increased from 1 at.% to 3 at.%, the yield 
stress of Ti-Nb-Hf alloys rises from 374 MPa to 440 MPa and then de -
creases to 395 MPa, while the maximum fracture stress elevates from 
515 MPa to 552 MPa and then suddenly drops to 490 MPa. However, the 
addition of Hf causes a reduction in the maximum fracture strain, 
decreasing from 45.5% to 37.3%. The evolution of the mechanical 
properties of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys is largely dependent on the 
microstructure features. First, the Hf addition has a strong solution 
strengthening effect in improving the mechanical properties of Ti-Nb-Hf 
shape memory alloys. In addition, the addition of Hf results in the 
refinement of grain size, which further enhances the mechanical prop -
erties. Therefore, the mechanical performance, including yield strength 
and maximum fracture strength, should be improved owing to Hf 
addition in theory. However, the Ti-Nb-Hf alloy with a higher content of 
3 at.% Hf exhibits a lower strength. It has been revealed that the me -
chanical properties of shape memory alloys are closely related to phase 
constituents and phase stability [29] . First, the amount of the β parent 
phase in Ti-Nb-Hf alloys gradually increases with increasing Hf content. 
Second, the bond order ( Bo ) and metallic average d-electron orbital 
energy level ( Md ) of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with various Hf 
contents are calculated, and the results are shown in Fig. 6 . The rela -
tionship between Md and Mo in Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with 
different Hf contents reveals that both Mo and Md of Ti-Nb-Hf shape 
memory alloys gradually increase with increasing Hf content. However, 
the Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys first approach the boundary of β + ω / β 
and then remain away from the boundary of β + ω / β , as shown in Fig. 6 
[ 32 , 33 ]. This implies that the amount of ω phase in the Ti-Nb-Hf shape 
memory alloy gradually increases and then decreases with increasing Hf 
content. Similarly, the precipitation of the ω phase plays an important 
role in the superior mechanical strength [34] . Hence, it can be specu -
lated that the larger volume fraction of the β phase and the lower volume 
fraction of ω precipitates in Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with 3 at.% 
Hf are the main factors in determining the lower mechanical strength. In 
addition, the elastic modulus of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with a 
higher Hf content is larger than that of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys 
with a lower Hf content. The elastic modulus ( E ) of Ti-Nb-Hf shape 
memory alloys rises from 20.5 GPa to 75.3 GPa, when the content of Hf 
is increased from 1 at.% to 3 at.%. For Ti alloys, the various phases have 
different elastic moduli. The relationship of the elastic modulus in

The cycling loading – unloading tensile results are shown in Fig. 10 . In 
the first cycle, tensile stress was applied until the strain reached 3% and 
then was unloaded. After undergoing the first cycle, a residual strain can 
be observed in Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with different Hf contents. 
With increasing Hf content, the residual strain is reduced from 1.01% to 
0.97%. The difference in residual strain can be attributed to the 
distinction in the volume fraction of the β parent phase in Ti-Nb-Hf 
shape memory alloys containing different Hf contents. In addition, the 
residual strain of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys gradually decreases 
with further cycling number increasing, irrespective of the Hf content. 
Meanwhile, the stress – strain curves show higher work hardening, 
further leading to the formation of a smaller hysteresis loop after several 
cycles. The similar phenomenon is frequently observed in β -Ti-based 
shape memory alloys [ 26 , 32 , 5 ]. The dislocation multiplication formed 
during loading and the residual martensite tangled by the dislocations 
during unloading should be responsible for the decreasing of recover -
able strain during cyclic loading-unloading [36] . In the present Ti-Nb-Hf 
shape memory alloys, the stress-induced martensite during stress – strain 
cycling can possess excellent reversibility and high stability, as the 
moderate strain is applied. As a result, all Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys 
with various Hf contents can fully recover, as five loading – unloading 
cycles are applied. 
Fig. 11 shows the effect of amplitude and frequency on the damping 
capacity ( tan δ ) of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with different Hf 
contents. In contrast, the damping capacity is more sensitive to the 
amplitude. As shown in Fig. 10 (a), the damping capacity of Ti-Nb-Hf 
shape memory alloys first increases and then decreases with 
increasing amplitude, regardless of the Hf content. The maximum value 
of the damping capacity of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with different 
Hf contents can be obtained by optimizing the amplitude of approxi -
mately 200 μ m. Moreover, the damping capacity of Ti-Nb-Hf shape 
memory alloys is also closely related to the Hf content. Hf addition re -
sults in the first increase and subsequent reduction of damping capacity 
for Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys. By adjusting the Hf content, the 
maximum damping capacity is 0.03174 for Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory 
alloys doped with 2.0 at.% Hf, which almost approaches the damping 
capacity (0.035) of Ti-Nb-Ta-Zr-O alloys [37] . However, the damping 
capacity of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys ranges from 0.0008 to 0.0035 
as the frequency changes. It can be inferred from the dependence of 
damping capacity on frequency that internal friction of Ti-Nb-Hf shape 
memory alloys belongs to relaxation type internal friction. Moreover, 
the relaxation type internal friction is closely related to the movement of 
grain boundaries, phase boundaries, twin interfaces and dislocations 
[38] . In the present Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloy with a lower content 
of 1.0 at.% Hf, the damping capacity shows a trend of gradually 
increasing with increasing frequency, whereas, the damping capacity of 
the Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with 2.0 at.% Hf and 3.0 at.% Hf 
firstly decreases and then increases with increasing frequency. 
4. Conclusions 
• The α ´´ martensite phase and β parent phase coexisted in Hf-modified 
TiNb-based shape memory alloys, irrespective of the Hf content. 
Moreover, the volume fraction of β parent phase gradually increased 
with increasing Hf content. 
• The higher strength in Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys can be ach -
ieved by tailoring 2 at.% Hf content, which was attributed to grain 
refinement, solution strengthening and precipitation strengthening 
of the ω phase. The larger number of β parent phases contributed to a 
sudden drop in strength for Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys with 
higher Hf contents. In proportion, perfect strain recovery charac -
teristics can be obtained in Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys modified 
with 2 at.% Hf due to the higher strength for dislocation slip. 
• The damping capacity of Ti-Nb-Hf shape memory alloys first 
increased and then decreased. The maximum value of damping ca -
pacity can be optimized by changing the Hf content, which was 
comparable to the reported Ti-Nb-Ta-Zr-O shape memory alloys.
