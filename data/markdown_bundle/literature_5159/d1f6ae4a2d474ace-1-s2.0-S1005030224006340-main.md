To effectively regulate the grain boundary inﬁltration in CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy (Cantor alloys, 
HEA) caused by the violent atomic interdiffusion, the higher conﬁguration entropy on Cantor alloys sur- 
face was designed and realized via eutectic high-entropy (EHEA) transformation. Meanwhile, to effec- 
tively alleviate the residual stress caused by the notable difference in the thermal expansion coeﬃcient 
(CTE) between Cantor alloys and Zr-3 alloys, a cladding layer was applied to the HEA surface using laser 
cladding technology of Nb, followed by brazing to Zr-3 alloys with Zr63.2Cu ﬁller. The cladding layer’s 
microstructure comprised Nbss and FCC + (Co,Ni)2 Nb eutectic structure, resulting from an in-situ reaction 
between Cantor alloys and Nb. The Nbss and FCC demonstrated good plasticity, and the (Co,Ni)2 Nb Laves 
phase provided increased strength, endowing both good plastic deformation ability and strength of the 
cladding layer. Notably, the existence of EHEA in the laser cladding layer made the Cantor alloy entropy 
from 1.61 R to 1.77 R, greatly enhancing its thermal stability and suppressing the grave grain boundary 
inﬁltration. Joints produced via laser cladding with Nb-assisted brazing exhibited a complex microstruc- 
ture (HEA/Nbss + FCC + (Co,Ni)2 Nb/(Zr,Nb)(Cr,Mn)2 + (Zr,Nb)ss/(Zr,Nb)2 (Cu,Ni,Co,Fe) + (Zr,Nb)(Cr,Mn)2 
+ (Zr,Nb)ss/Zr-3 and a signiﬁcantly improved shear strength of 242.8 MPa at 1010 °C for 10 min, 42.4 % 
higher than that of directly brazed joints. This improvement was attributed to reduced grain boundary in- 
ﬁltration, alleviated residual stress due to CTE disparity, and eliminated micro-cracks in the brazing seam. 
This study presents an effective solution for reducing residual stresses and achieving reliable bonding be- 
tween Cantor alloys and Zr-3 alloys, with potential applications in brazing CoCrFeNi-based HEA and Zr-3 
due to the beneﬁcial eutectic reaction between CoCrFeNi and Nb.

1. introduction 
Zirconium (Zr) alloys, recognized as high-performance materi- 
als, are extensively used in the nuclear industry for fabricating nu- 
clear fuel cladding materials due to their superior ductility, excel- 
lent oxidation resistance, and robust resistance to neutron irradia- 
tion [ 1 , 2 ]. In nuclear reactors, nuclear fuel cladding often requires

alloy (HEA) with a face-centered cubic (FCC) structure, namely 
Cantor alloys, has demonstrated outstanding elevated-temperature 
properties, exceptional irradiation resistance, and wear resistance 
compared to commonly used stainless steel end plug materials [ 4- 
6 ], making it a promising substitute for end plug materials in the 
nuclear industry. 
Brazing, known for its high joining precision and low temper- 
ature [ 7-9 ], is widely used for joining dissimilar metals. However, 
the signiﬁcant CTE difference between Cantor alloys (15 × 10–6 /K) 
and Zr alloys (5.3 × 10–6 /K) can lead to high residual thermal

Surface modiﬁcation technology is an effective method to reduce 
residual stress in brazed joints [ 13 , 14 ]. For example, Zhang et al. 
[ 15 ] prepared a double-layer material composed of WC particles 
and Cu-Ni-WC particles on TC4 alloy using laser cladding tech- 
nology, reducing the thermal residual stress and increasing the 
shear strength by 89 % compared to directly brazed joints. Simi- 
larly, Huang et al. [ 16 ] used a Si-40Al transition layer, similar in 
CTE to SiC ceramics, on SiC ceramics before brazing with Si-60Al 
ﬁller alloys, enhancing the ﬂexural strength of the brazed joints. 
The aim of our study is to prepare a high-strength HEA/Zr- 
3 joint by designing a novel transition layer using laser cladding 
to decrease residual stress in HEA/Zr-3 brazed joints. Considering 
the objectives and background, niobium (Nb) was selected as the 
cladding material due to its lower CTE (8.5 × 10–6 /K) [ 17 ] and 
low thermal neutron absorption cross-section [ 17 ]. Above 1251 °C, 
Nb and CoCrFeMnNi undergo eutectic reactions, forming a eutec- 
tic structure of FCC and (Co,Ni)2 Nb Laves phase [ 18 , 19 ], indicating 
potential for strong metallurgical bonding with the Cantor alloys 
HEA substrate. In this work, Nb was fused onto the HEA surface by 
laser cladding, then brazed to Zr-3 using Zr63.2Cu36.8 (wt%) ﬁller. 
The study comprehensively analyzes the morphology and structure 
of the cladding layer under various laser processing parameters, as 
well as the interfacial microstructure, shear properties, and frac- 
ture behavior of HEA/Zr-3 joints with and without the cladding 
layer.

loys and HEA depicted in Fig. 1 , the Zr-3 alloys showed a hexag- 
onal close-packed (HCP, PDF# 05-0 6 65) crystal structure. whereas 
the Cantor alloys exhibited an FCC crystal structure (PDF#09-0205),

Nb powders, used in the study, were procured from Beijing Yi-

2.2. Laser cladding 
Before the laser cladding process, the HEA was machined into 
blocks measuring 15 mm × 15 mm × 5 mm. These blocks were 
then ultrasonically cleaned in alcohol for 20–30 min. The pole ﬁg- 
ure of HEA, as shown in Fig. 1(c) , indicated that the grains of HEA 
were distributed randomly, suggesting that the direction of laser 
cladding on the HEA would not signiﬁcantly impact the process. 
The surfaces of the HEA blocks were initially ground with SiC 
abrasive paper to a #20 0 0 grit ﬁnish. Subsequently, they were pol- 
ished using 2.5 μm diamond slurry. Following the polishing, the 
blocks underwent a second round of ultrasonic cleaning in alco- 
hol, this time for 10–15 min. This preparation was critical to en-

For the laser cladding itself, a ﬁber laser system (model IPG

carefully chosen to optimize the cladding process, taking into ac-

properties in the ﬁnal cladded material. 
2.3. Brazing 
Prior to the brazing process, the surface of the HEA with the 
cladding layer was meticulously ground to a #20 0 0 grit ﬁnish us- 
ing SiC abrasive paper and then polished with 2.5 μm diamond 
slurry to achieve a ﬂat and smooth cladding layer. Subsequently, 
the HEA with and without the cladding layer and the Zr-3 al- 
loys were cut into dimensions of 5 mm × 5 mm × 5 mm and 
15 mm × 8 mm × 5 mm, respectively. The Zr63.2Cu36.8 (wt%) al- 
loy ingot was prepared by vacuum melting of high-purity Cu and 
Zr metal particles ( ≥ 99.5 wt%). Notably, the melting process was 
repeated ﬁve times to ensure homogenization of the ingot. After- 
wards, the Zr63.2Cu36.8 alloy ingot was machined into the piece 
with dimensions of 5 mm × 5 mm × 400 μm, and ﬁnally ground 
into 5 mm × 5 mm × 200 μm pieces to remove oxides and con- 
taminants. 
According to our previous research [ 22 ], the microstructure of 
Zr63.2Cu36.8 (wt%) alloys consisted of ZrCu, Cu10Zr7 , and Zr2 Cu 
phases. The melting temperature of these alloys was approximately 
952.3 °C. Notably, in our previous study [ 22 ], the HEA/Zr63.2Cu/Zr- 
3 joints exhibited the maximum shear strength when the brazing 
parameter was 1010 °C/10 min. Therefore, the brazing parameter 
was determined to be 1010 °C/10 min in this experiment. The Zr- 
3, ﬁller foils, and HEA components were all ultrasonically cleaned 
in alcohol for 10–20 min before being assembled into a sandwich 
structure for the brazing experiment, as presented in Fig. 3 . 
Following the assembly, the sandwiched samples were placed 
into a vacuum brazing furnace (model VF1600-222) under a vac- 
uum pressure of 5 × 10–3 Pa. It was noticeable that the heat- 
ing rate and cooling rate were 10 and 5 °C/min, respectively. 
Once the temperature in the vacuum brazing furnace reached 
100 °C, the brazing samples were removed from the furnace. 
This step is crucial for ensuring the cleanliness and proper align- 
ment of the components before initiating the actual brazing 
process. 
2.4. Characterization and testing 
After the laser cladding process, in order to optimize the laser 
processing parameters, the morphology of the cladding layers un- 
der various laser processing conditions was examined using an op- 
tical microscope (OM, OLYMPUS). For a detailed analysis of the mi- 
crostructure of the cladding layer, ﬁeld emission scanning electron 
microscopy (FE-SEM, MERLIN Compact) equipped with an energy 
dispersive spectrometer (EDS) was employed. Additionally, micro- 
area X-ray diffractometry (Rigaku Smart Lab SE) and X-ray pho- 
toelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientiﬁc ESCALAB 250Xi) 
were utilized to ascertain the chemical composition and phase 
structure of the cladding layer. The crystallographic orientation of 
phases in the brazed joints was also scrutinized using electron 
backscatter diffraction (EBSD, Nordlys max3). To further investi- 
gate the crystal structure and orientation relationship of phases in 
the brazed joints, the HEA/ﬁller interface was selected for analy- 
sis using a focused ion beam (FIB, Helios 600i), followed by ob- 
servation through transmission electron microscopy (TEM, ZEISS 
Crossbeam 540). Post-brazing, the cross-sectional microstructure 
and chemical composition of the Zr-3/HEA joints, both with and 
without the cladding layer, were examined using FE-SEM, EDS, and 
XRD. FIB and TEM were also conducted to further characterize the 
composition and crystal structure of phases within the Zr-3/HEA 
joints. 
In Fig. 3 , a shear test was performed to evaluate the mechanical 
properties of the brazed joints using a universal testing machine 
(Instron 5967), with a loading speed set at 0.5 mm/min. To ensure 
the accuracy and reliability of the data, the shear strength values of 
four brazed joints under the same conditions were measured and 
averaged. This approach was critical for verifying the consistency 
and repeatability of the results.

3. Results 
3.1. The effect of laser cladding parameters on the morphology and 
structure 
Fig. 4 illustrates the morphology and structure of the cladding 
layer formed under various laser parameters, including laser power 
and scanning speed. Notably, when the laser power was set below 
700 W, it was challenging to form a cladding layer on the HEA 
surface due to the insuﬃcient energy input from the low power. 
As depicted in Fig. 4(b) , a successful cladding layer, characterized 
by a morphology of peaks and valleys in grooves, was achieved us- 
ing a laser power of 700 W. When the laser power increased to 
800 W, the depth variation in the cladding layer reduced signiﬁ- 
cantly from 527.9 to 358.9 μm. This reduction indicates the forma- 
tion of a more uniform and ﬂat cladding layer on the HEA surface, 
as shown in Fig. 4(a) . The depth difference in the cladding layer be- 
came more pronounced with increasing laser power, attributed to 
the greater laser energy providing adequate heat to melt the HEA 
substrate and the incoming powder stream. However, at a laser 
power of 10 0 0 W, deep and wide ravines were observed on the 
cladding layer surface, as evidenced in Fig. 4(d) . The trends in mor- 
phology and structure changes in the cladding layer at different 
laser scanning speeds were similar to those observed with varying 
laser powers. Nonetheless, the depth variation in the cladding layer 
under different laser scanning speeds did not exhibit a signiﬁcant 
change, suggesting that laser power plays a more crucial role in af- 
fecting the cladding layer compared to laser scanning speed. This 
observation aligns with Emamian et al.’s study [ 23 ], which found 
laser power to be the predominant factor in laser cladding param- 
eters. Based on this analysis, optimal laser parameters were identi- 
ﬁed as 800 W-1000 mm/min-0.3 r/min. Under these conditions, a 
relatively uniform and ﬂat cladding layer on the HEA surface was 
produced, facilitating the polishing process prior to the subsequent 
brazing process. 
3.2. Characterization of the laser cladding layer 
The cross-sectional microstructure of the laser cladding layer 
on the HEA surface and the corresponding XRD analysis results 
are presented in Fig. 5 . In Fig. 5(a) , the average thickness of the 
cladding layer was approximately 375 μm, achieved under the laser 
parameters of 800 W power, 1000 mm/min scanning speed, and 
0.3 r/min powder feeding speed. As depicted in Fig. 5 ( b , c ), the 
cladding layer predominantly consisted of two distinct areas: the 
globular area and the lamellar eutectic structure area. Five phases 
labeled A–E, were identiﬁed, with their chemical compositions 
listed in Table 1 . Phases A, B, and C in the globular area, contain- 
ing over 90 at% Nb and small amounts of Co, Cr, Fe, Mn, and Ni, 
were inferred to be an Nb-based solid solution (Nbss). The lamel- 
lar D phase in the eutectic structure, primarily composed of Nb, 
Co, Ni, Cr, Fe, and Mn, was deduced to be the (Co,Ni)2 Nb Laves 
phase (PDF#19-0354), corroborated by the XRD pattern shown in 
Fig. 5(e) . The mixing enthalpies of Nb with Co, Cr, Fe, Mn, and Ni 
atomic pairs are –25, –7, –16, –4, and –30 kJ/mol, respectively, sug- 
gesting a strong aﬃnity of Nb with Ni and Co atoms [ 18 ], which 
promotes the formation of the (Co,Ni)2 Nb Laves phase. This phase, 
one of the hexagonal C14 (MgZn2 -type) Laves phases, exhibits an 
HCP crystal structure [ 24 ].

4. Discussion 
4.1. Formation mechanism of the HEA/Zr-3 brazed joint with and 
without cladding layer 
Fig. 14 illustrates the formation mechanism in HEA/Zr-3 joints 
prepared by laser cladding Nb-assisted brazing, providing insights 
into the interfacial joining mechanism. The entire process can be 
divided into four stages: 
(1) Laser Cladding Stage: When Nb powders entered the laser 
beam, they were rapidly heated. The melting temperatures of 
CoCrFeMnNi HEA and Nb are 1406 and 2468 °C, respectively 
[ 39 ]. As per the studies by Huang et al. [ 18 ] and Jiang et al. [ 19 ], 
the eutectic reaction (L→ FCC + Laves) occurs at around 1251 °C. 
Thus, the partially melted HEA substrate ﬁrst reacts with Nb to 
form the eutectic structure (FCC + Laves) under the laser beam, 
as shown in Fig. 14(a) . It is noteworthy that the exterior area 
of the spherical Nb powders is consumed preferentially, leaving 
residual Nb in the cladding layer to impart good plasticity. The 
cladding layer is then ﬂattened for subsequent brazing. 
(2) Melting Stage of Zr63.2Cu Filler: As depicted in Fig. 14(b) , the 
Zr63.2Cu ﬁller starts to melt at 952.3 °C, wetting the HEA and 
Zr-3 surfaces. A part of Zr-3 and HEA dissolves into the liquid 
ﬁller, evidenced by the irregular shapes of Zr-3 and HEA near 
the brazing seam ( Fig. 11 ). 
(3) Interfacial Reaction Stage: On the Zr-3 side, Zr-3 dissolves and 
diffuses into the liquids, causing the eutectic point to shift to- 
wards the Zr-rich side. On the cladding layer side, atoms of Co, 
Fe, Ni, Nb, Cr, and Mn diffuse into the liquid ﬁller, while Zr 
and Cu in the ﬁller diffuse towards the cladding layer. Accord- 
ing to Tsai et al. [ 40 ], Mn and Cr elements in CoCrFeMnNi HEA, 
having the lowest activation energies, exhibit strong diffusion 
at high temperatures. Therefore, Zr atoms from the ﬁller react 
with Cr and Mn atoms from the cladding layer to form layered 
(Zr,Nb)(Cr,Mn)2 and bulk (Zr,Nb)(Cr,Mn)2 phase in the brazing 
seam due to the complete miscibility of Zr and Nb, as shown in 
Fig. 14(c) . The Gibbs free energies of ZrCr2 and ZrMn2 at 1010 
°C support this reaction [ 22 ].

4.2. Strengthening mechanism of mechanical properties of joints 
The shear strength of joints is signiﬁcantly inﬂuenced by resid- 
ual thermal stress, resulting from the mismatch in the coeﬃcient 
of thermal expansion (CTE) and the level of metallurgical bonding 
[ 41 ]. In this context, a considerable residual thermal stress arises 
due to the difference in CTE between CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy al- 
loy (HEA) and Zr alloys. The residual stress ( σ) in A/B brazed joints 
can be estimated using the following formula [ 42 ]: 
σ = EA EB

where EA and EB represent Young’s modulus of A and B materials, 
ɑA and ɑB denote the thermal expansion coeﬃcient of materials A 
and B, respectively, and /Delta1T refers to the temperature difference 
(1010 °C). In HEA/Zr-3 joints produced by direct brazing, the entire 
joint comprises HEA, HEAP, Zr(Cr,Mn)2 , Zr2 (Cu,Ni,Co,Fe), and Zr al- 
loys. For joints obtained through laser cladding with Nb-assisted 
brazing, the joint is simpliﬁed to HEA, EHEA layer, (Zr,Nb)(Cr,Mn)2 , 
(Zr,Nb)2 (Cu,Ni,Co,Fe), and Zr alloys. The thermal expansion coeﬃ- 
cients and Young’s modulus of these phases can be calculated us- 
ing JMatPro software, as outlined in Fig. 15 (a, b ). Consequently, the 
calculated residual stress values in joints produced by direct braz- 
ing and laser cladding with Nb-assisted brazing were expressed in 
Fig. 15(c) . By comparison, the residual stress in joints obtained by 
direct brazing is signiﬁcantly higher than that in joints created us- 
ing laser cladding with Nb-assisted brazing. This suggests that the 
presence of a cladding layer plays a crucial role in reducing resid- 
ual stresses in the joints and enhancing their shear strength. 
Moreover, the results of the Electron Backscatter Diffraction 
(EBSD) investigation of the HEA/ﬁller interface in the joints are 
presented in Fig. 16 . In Fig. 16(a) , inﬁltration phases were de- 
tected within the HEA adjacent to the HEA/ﬁller interface, originat- 
ing from the intense inter-diffusion between HEA and ﬁller metal 
during brazing. The HEAP phases consisted of slender needle-like 
grains, while the Zr(Cr,Mn)2 phases were characterized by cubic 
grains. The pole ﬁgures of the HEA, HEAP, and Zr(Cr,Mn)2 phases 
indicated no induced texture with a speciﬁc orientation during 
brazing, as shown in Fig. 16 ( e , f , g ). As seen in Fig. 16(b) , the reso- 
lution rate of the Zr(Cr,Mn)2 area was lower, and achieving resolu- 
tion in the Zr2 (Cu,Ni,Co,Fe) area was challenging due to their high 
internal stress and signiﬁcant lattice distortion. Additionally, nu- 
merous high-angle grain boundaries were observed in areas where 
the inﬁltration and HEAP phases were located. The vicinity of the 
solid-liquid interface experienced thermal stress at high brazing 
temperatures, leading to dislocation entanglement and accumula- 
tion, which in turn resulted in the formation of high-angle grain 
boundaries [ 39 ].

5. Conclusions 
A eutectic high-entropy alloy (EHEA) layer was successfully cre- 
ated on a CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy (HEA) surface by laser 
cladding Nb powders. Subsequently, the HEA with and without the 
cladding layer was brazed to Zr-3 alloys using Zr63.2Cu eutectic 
ﬁller. A comprehensive examination of the microstructure evolu- 
tion, shear strength, and residual stress in joints achieved by di- 
rect brazing and laser cladding with Nb-assisted brazing led to the 
following conclusions: 
(1) A uniform cladding layer was achieved with laser parameters 
set at 800 W for power, 1000 mm/min for scanning speed, and 
0.3 r/min for powder feeding speed. The microstructure of the 
cladding layer on the HEA matrix consisted of Nb-based solid 
solution (Nbss) and FCC + (Co,Ni)2 Nb eutectic structure, result- 
ing from an in-situ eutectic reaction between CoCrFeMnNi and 
Nb. 
(2) The microstructure of joints created by laser cladding with 
Nb-assisted brazing was characterized as HEA/Nbss + FCC + 
(Co,Ni)2 Nb (Zr,Nb)(Cr,Mn)2 + (Zr,Nb)ss/(Zr,Nb)2 (Cu,Ni,Co,Fe) + 
(Zr,Nb)(Cr,Mn)2 + (Zr,Nb)ss/Zr-3. Severe grain boundary inﬁltra- 
tion gave rise to the stress concentration in joints produced by

direct brazing, which was completely mitigated in joints cre-

(3) At 1010 °C for 10 min, the joints prepared by laser cladding 
with Nb-assisted brazing exhibited a higher shear strength of 
242.8 MPa, a 42.4 % increase compared to joints obtained by di- 
rect brazing. This signiﬁcant improvement was attributed to the 
reduction of stress concentration caused by severe grain bound- 
ary inﬁltration, the alleviation of residual stress due to CTE dif- 
ferences between the HEA and Zr-3 alloys, and the elimination 
of microcracks in the brazing seam.
