abstract
Ti–40Al–5Si and Ti–39Al–5Si–2Nb (in at.%) alloys were studied as prospective high-temperature struc-
tural composites consisting of c-(Ti,Nb)Al + a2-(Ti,Nb)3Al matrix and Ti 5Si3 reinforcement. The alloys
were prepared by arc melting under helium. Oxidation resistance was studied at 900 /C176C in air. Thermal
stability of alloys was investigated by measuring room temperature hardness and compressive strength
after long-term annealing at 900 /C176C. To prepare oriented composites, directional crystallization at rates of
5–115 mm/h was carried out by the ﬂoating zone technique. It was observed that the addition of 2% Nb to
the Ti–40Al–5Si alloy does not modify eutectic structure. Niobium is almost uniformly distributed in all
present phases. Both alloys show excellent oxidation resistance at 900 /C176C in air. The Nb-addition causes
signiﬁcant improvement of oxidation resistance due to the doping effect and increase of Al activity in the
scales. Room temperature hardness and compressive strength of both as-cast alloys are similar – about
500 HV and 1600 MPa, respectively. Room temperature mechanical properties do not reduce signiﬁcantly
after 300 h annealing at 900 /C176C, due to a high morphological stability of eutectic silicides. Directionally
solidiﬁed alloys consist of columnar Ti–Al grains elongated in crystallization direction and silicides. Nio-
bium reﬁnes both Ti–Al grains and Ti
5Si3 silicides. As a consequence, orientation and elongation of sili-
cides in the Nb-containing alloy are reduced. In the Ti–Al–Si alloy directionally crystallized at 5–
115 mm/h, the silicide interparticle spacing k (in mm) is related to the crystallization rate R (in mm/h)
by a following expression: k1:33 /C1 R ¼ 0:32. In the Nb-containing alloy, silicide interparticle spacing does
not depend on the crystallization rate.

1. Introduction
TiAl-based intermetallics show a large number of attractive
properties, such as light weight, high-temperature oxidation
resistance and creep resistance, making them of interest for pro-
duction of components in aerospace and automotive industry.
Binary Ti–Al intermetallics show rather poor strength, oxidation
and creep resistance. For this reason, commercial materials also
contain other additives, such as Nb, Cr, V, Si, and Ta improving
these characteristics. In this work we focus our attention on
additions of niobium and silicon. A lot of work has been re-
ported on the high-temperature oxidation of Nb-containing Ti–
Al intermetallics [1–6]. These investigations revealed that nio-
bium strongly improves oxidation resistance which is generally
explained in terms of the internal sub-structure of scales formed
during oxidation process. In contrast to niobium, silicon shows
much lower solubility in the Ti–Al phases, leading to the forma-
tion of Ti
5Si3 silicide in microstructure. The Ti 5Si3 silicide is ex-
tremely hard (above 1500 HV [7]), thermally stable (melting
point of more than 2000 /C176C) and oxidation resistant. Therefore,
its presence strongly modiﬁes high-temperature properties of
Ti–Al intermetallics.
In this work, our attention is devoted to eutectic alloys based on
the Ti–Al–Si system. An alloy having a proper composition can be
regarded as a eutectic composite consisting of
c-TiAl + a2-Ti3Al
matrix and Ti 5Si3 reinforcement. Both matrix and reinforcement
are formed together by eutectic crystallization which facilitates for-
mation of a strong adhesion between them. An advantage of eutectic
composites is their good castability which is generally deﬁned as a
set of properties that enable to produce complex shape products
by simple casting. Under proper conditions of unidirectional crystal-
lization, a composite having aligned ﬁber-like morphology of rein-
forcement can be prepared [8]. Fiber-reinforced composites often
show high modulus and strength in ﬁber direction, as well as good
fatigue and thermal shock resistance [9]. Additionally, composites
containing the Ti
5Si3 phase are also resistant to high-temperature
oxidation [8]. As stated before, niobium is a common additive in
the Ti–Al intermetallics strongly affecting their high-temperature
properties. For this reason, inﬂuence of a low Nb-addition on the

2. Experiment
Two alloys with nominal compositions (all concentrations are in
at.%, unless otherwise speciﬁed) Ti–40Al–5Si and Ti–39Al–5Si–2Nb
were investigated. The composition of the Ti–Al–Si alloy was se-
lected according to the study on eutectic reactions in the Ti–Al–
Si system [10]. Eutectic line in this system, representing Ti + Ti
5Si3,
Ti3Al + Ti5Si3 and (Ti 3Al,TiAl) + Ti5Si3 eutectics is shown in Fig. 1 a.
The sequence of eutectics with increasing concentration of alumin-
ium is in accordance with the Ti–Al phase diagram presented in
Fig. 1 b. Niobium was added in a relatively small amount of 2% to
prevent any modiﬁcation of phase composition.
The investigated alloys in amounts of about 200 g were pre-
pared from pure elements (Ti 99.9%, Si 99.99%, Al 99.99% and Nb
99.8%) in an arc furnace in a copper crystallizer under helium
atmosphere. The alloys were remelted seven times to ensure
homogeneous composition. Mass reduction after melting due to
evaporation of more volatile elements was less than 0.05%. Struc-
tures of the as-cast alloys are shown in Fig. 2 . It is observed that
both alloys are dominated by eutectic colonies grown in various
directions depending on local heat ﬂows. The morphology of Ti
5Si3

tre of each eutectic colony, the structure is reﬁned and the eutectic
phase has predominantly ﬁber-like morphology. As the eutectic
colony grows, the recalescence leads to progressive coarsening of
the ﬁbers and to their transition into lamellae. Matrix consists of
a typical lamellar mixture of
c-TiAl and a2-Ti3Al phases which
was also proved by X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD). X-ray elemen-
tal map in Fig. 3 conﬁrms our assumption that niobium is almost
uniformly distributed in all present phases. The as-cast ingots were

mechanical properties and for oxidation tests. Samples of
10 /C2 10 /C2 100 mm were cut from the cast ingots for directional

3. Results
3.1. Oxidation resistance
The 290 h quasi-isothermal oxidation experiment is presented
in Fig. 4. Both alloys show good oxidation resistance, because their
weight changes are relatively low, about 150 and 20 g/m 2 for the
Ti–Al–Si and Ti–Al–Si–Nb alloy, respectively. As suggested in
[11], oxidation of pure Ti at 900 /C176C/300 h would produce a weight
change signiﬁcantly above 1000 g/m 2. According to Fig. 4, there are
signiﬁcant differences between oxidation behaviour of both alloys.
Though their weight increases during the ﬁrst 20 h, further weigh-
ing indicates a fast mass reduction for the ternary alloy. Appar-
ently, after the oxidation of this alloy has produced scales of a
certain critical thickness, the situation becomes favourable for
the scale spallation during cooling of the sample, leading to the de-
tected mass reduction. In contrast, the mass of the Nb-containing
alloy shows a continuous increase with the absence of any abrupt
change. Therefore, the scale spallation plays an insigniﬁcant role in
its oxidation. By taking a simpliﬁed assumption that the mass
reduction due to the scale spallation represents the total weight

3.2. Thermal stability
To determine thermal stability, both alloys were subjected to
150 and 300 h annealing under protective atmosphere. Afterwards,
their microstructures and mechanical properties were compared to
the as-cast state. As shown in Fig. 2 , structures of alloys consist of
Ti–Al matrix and Ti
5Si3 phase. It is assumed that thermal stability is
mainly affected by the high-temperature Ti 5Si3 silicide. For this
reason, structural investigation was focused on this phase. To de-
scribe any structural evolution quantitatively, image analysis was
used.
Fig. 8 presents the histograms of maximum dimensions of sili-
cide particles. These dimensions were measured in planes perpen-
dicular to the growth directions. Therefore, they characterize
thickness of ﬁbers or width of lamellae, not their length. Histo-
grams are presented for the as-cast and annealed states of alloys.
It is seen that the structure of eutectic in the as-cast alloy (blue his-
tograms

lamellae whose dimensions lie between approximately 0.5 and

with a thickness of 1–2 lm. As a eutectic colony grows, crystalliza-

lamellae. Widths of lamella near the edge of colonies do not exceed
approximately 10 lm. It is also observed in Fig. 8 that niobium
plays insigniﬁcant role in dimensions of silicide phases in the as-

Plots of the room temperature hardness and compressive

Hardness and strength values of both alloys are very similar,
around 500 HV and 1600 MPa, respectively. These values reﬂect
the signiﬁcant fraction of hard and ﬁne silicides in the structure,
see Fig. 2 . It can be assumed that hardness and compressive
strength are related to the average silicide size. The smaller the sil-
icides, the higher are the hardness and strength. This relation could
be quantitatively described similarly to the Hall–Petch equation.
Therefore, any coarsening would lead to a reduction of hardness
and compressive strength. However, it was shown in Fig. 8 that
coarsening does not occur even after 300 h annealing at 900 /C176C.
This is the main reason why mechanical properties plotted in
Fig. 10 remain stable and do not decrease with annealing time. It
suggests a high thermal stability of the investigated eutectic alloys.
A small strength reduction observed for the Nb-free alloy (Fig. 10 b)
should be taken with caution, because of a large scatter of the ob-
tained values.
3.3. Directional crystallization
Fig. 11 presents the structures of directionally crystallized al-
loys. In Fig. 11 a and b there are low magniﬁcation optical
micrographs of alloys crystallized at 5 mm/h. Both structures are
composed of large columnar Ti–Al grains elongated in crystalliza-
tion direction (horizontal direction from the right to the left).
Within the grains there are almost randomly oriented silicide par-
ticles. Average dimensions of grains and silicides are inﬂuenced
both by crystallization rate and by niobium, as will be shown later.
More detailed SEM views of alloys crystallized at 5 mm/h are
shown in Fig. 11 c and d. It is seen that the Ti–Al grains have an
internal structure consisting of alternating lamellae of
c-TiAl and
a2-Ti3Al phases. Apparently, neither lamellae nor silicides show
any relation to the crystallization direction (horizontal direction
from the right to the left). By comparing both alloys it is observed
that niobium acts as a reﬁning agent. In Fig. 11 c there are silicide
plates whose length exceeds 1 mm. In contrast, such large particles
are not observed in the Nb-containing alloy. Optical micrographs of
alloys crystallized at the highest rate of 115 mm/h are presented in
Fig. 11 e and f. Here the crystallization direction is oriented verti-
cally upward. There is a signiﬁcant difference between both alloys
in orientation of silicide particles. In the Nb-free alloy, the majority
of silicides is oriented almost parallel to the crystallization direc-
tion, whereas a random orientation of silicides is observed in the
Nb-containing alloy. Moreover, silicides in the Nb-free alloy are
more elongated than those in the Nb-containing counterpart.
Based on the above observation, it is evident that niobium, de-
spite its low concentration, modiﬁes crystallization process of the
(
c-TiAl, a2-Ti3Al) + Ti5Si3 eutectic. Inﬂuence of Nb and crystalliza-
tion rate on structural parameters is quantitatively expressed in
Fig. 12 . Average grain lengths and widths as function of Nb and
crystallization rate are presented in Fig. 12 a and b. In the Nb-free
alloy, the grain size reduces with increasing crystallization rate.

On the other hand, Nb-containing alloy does not show this correla-
tion. Instead, niobium reﬁnes the Ti–Al grains, particularly, it re-
duces the average grain length. This behaviour is the most
pronounced for the lowest crystallization rate, at which the reduc-
tion of grain length is by a factor of two. Measured values of aver-
age silicide dimensions show a similar trend, i.e. they decrease
with both crystallization rate and niobium, see Fig. 12 c and d. It
can be assumed that niobium acts as a nucleation agent during
directional crystallization. Small particles of Nb-containing phases
in the melt become sites where nucleation of Ti–Al grains and sil-
icide particles occurs preferentially. In the Nb–Si phase diagram,
there is a Nb
5Si3 phase whose melting temperature signiﬁcantly
exceeds that of titanium silicide [13]. Perhaps, ﬁne particles of this
phase in the melt support nucleation of silicides and grains.
The nucleation effect of niobium has direct impact on orienta-
tion and shape of silicides in the directionally crystallized alloys.
This is illustrated in Fig. 12e–h. Fig. 12e demonstrate that silicides
are oriented only in the Nb-free alloy crystallized at 115 mm/h. In
this alloy, angles between silicides and crystallization direction are
either low or close to 180/C176. Lower rates do not produce oriented
structure. This can be explained in terms of factors inﬂuencing
the growth of phases: generally, morphology of phases, i.e. their
orientation and elongation, in directionally crystallized alloys is
determined by two simultaneous processes: (1) heat ﬂow and (2)
preferential crystallographic growth. When the effect of the heat
ﬂow predominates over preferential crystallographic growth, a
phase becomes oriented and elongated parallel to the heat ﬂow
direction. This is the case of the high crystallization rate in our
experiment. On the other hand, when the heat ﬂow is low, such
as at low crystallization rates, a phase grows in preferential crystal-
lographic directions which are independent on the macroscopic
crystallization direction. Therefore, a resulting structure would
not show either orientation or elongation. Fig. 12 f reveals that
the orientation of silicides in the Nb-containing alloy is negligible
at all crystallization rates, see also Fig. 11 e and f. Probably, this
can be in part attributed to the nucleation effect of Nb which
supports the formation of new silicide particles rather than growth
of existing ones to elongated shapes. Fig. 12g and h illustrate elon-
gation of silicides, i.e. ratio of their length to their width, as func-
tions of the crystallization rate and niobium content. These
ﬁgures are in a good agreement with the previous ones. In the
Nb-free alloy, elongation increases with increasing growth rate.

4. Summary
The results described in this work can be summarized as
follows:
1. Addition of 2% Nb to theTi–40Al–5Si alloy does not modify
eutectic structure. Niobium is almost uniformly distributed
in all present phases.
2. Niobium signiﬁcantly reduces oxidation rate at 900 /C176C in air.
This is caused by the ‘‘doping effect” and by an increase of Al
activity in the scales.
3. Room temperature hardness and compressive strength of
Nb-free and Nb-containing alloy are similar. Room tempera-
ture mechanical properties do not reduce after 300 h anneal-
ing at 900 /C176C which is a result of high morphological stability
of eutectic silicides.
