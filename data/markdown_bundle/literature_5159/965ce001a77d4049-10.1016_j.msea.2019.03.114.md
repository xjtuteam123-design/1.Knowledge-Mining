Abstract 
 
A nearly fully dense β stabilized γ-TiAl alloy was additively manufactured by electron beam 
melting. The as-fabricated specimen exhibited a fin e structure consisting of α
2/γ colonies (<5 
µm), equiaxed γ (1 µm) and βo (<1 µm) grains. The unique microstructure rendered high strength 
(580 MPa) and high ductility (>50%) in the sample a t 800 °C. Annealing and subsequent 
stabilization treatments resulted in α2/γ colonies (<50 µm) with an ultrafine (20-40 nm) lamellar 
structure, which gave rise to high strength (770 MP a) and good ductility (6%) levels superior to 
those of conventional counterparts and other γ-TiAl alloys at 800 °C. 
 
Keywords : β stabilized γ-TiAl; high-temperature; electron beam melting; ult rafine lamella; high 
strength and ductility

1. Introduction 
Gamma-TiAl alloys have been widely used in various high-temperature applications such 
as aerospace engine blades and high-performance com bustion engines due to their low density 
levels, high specific strength, excellent creep pro perties, and good oxidation resistance [1-3]. 
However, insufficient workability at room temperatu re has limited their structural applications, 
especially in relation to structural components wit h complicated geometries [4]. The emerging 
technique known as additive manufacturing (AM) enables the production of metallic components 
with complex geometries with relatively short lead times, thus contributing to better cost-
effectiveness [5, 6]. With regard to the AM of TiAl -based alloys, much work has been carried 
out on Ti-48Al-2Cr-2Nb [6-8], Ti-45Al-8Nb [9], Ti-4 5Al-7Nb-0.3W [10] and Ti-45Al-2Cr-5Nb 
[11]. It has been found that the mechanical propert ies of AM parts are comparable to those of 
their wrought counterparts [12, 13]. 
TNM-B1 (Ti-43.5Al-4Nb-1Mo-0.1B at.%) alloy is one of the most promising β stabilized 
γ-TiAl alloys designed for structural components suc h as low-pressure turbine blades and 
turbocharger wheels, which typically operate at ele vated temperatures close to 800 °C [14-16]. 
The conventionally processed TNM-B1 alloy exhibits a maximum strength of ~510 MP a (at 800 
°C), which can be enhanced significantly to ~750 MP a by appropriate heat treatments [14]. AM 
of TNM-B1 alloy has been performed using the laser metal deposition (LMD) [17] and selective 
laser melting (SLM) processes [18] in which the stu dies mainly focused on optimizing the 
process parameters and studying the influence of he at treatments on microstructural changes. To 
the best of the present authors’ knowledge, however , no study has concentrated on the 
microstructural and high-temperature mechanical properties of TNM-B1 alloy processed by AM.

It has been reported that compared to LMD and SLM, the electron beam melting (EBM) 
process presents additional advantages such as redu ced residual stress due to the use of 
preheating temperatures up to 1100 oC [19-21]. In the present study, we manufactured a nearly 
fully dense TNM-B1 alloy using EBM. The unique micr ostructure obtained by EBM and the 
microstructural evolution during various annealing and stabilization treatments are studied, and 
the high-temperature mechanical behaviors of as-fab ricated and heat-treated TNM-B1 alloy 
samples were evaluated at 800 °C. The correlation b etween the microstructural features and 
high-temperature tensile properties is discussed. T he performance of the EBM-manufactured 
TNM-B1 alloy is compared to those of conventional counterparts and other γ-TiAl alloys. 
 
2. Materials and experimental procedures 
Pre-alloyed TNM-B1 powders were prepared by the ele ctrode induction-melting gas 
atomization process. The powder size ranged from 45 to 130 µm. All specimens were fabricated 
using an Arcam Q20 plus system with the following p rocess parameters: a scan speed of 4000 
mm/s, a layer thickness of 70 µm, line energy of 225 J/m and a preheating temperat ure of 1000 
°C. Cylindrical specimens (ϕ 13 mm × 82 mm) were fabricated with the length direction parallel 
to the building direction. The chemical composition of the as-fabricated specimen was 41.32Al-
4.15Nb-1.03Mo-0.09B-0.10O-0.03C and Ti balance (at. %). The γ solvus (T 
γ) and β transus (T β) 
temperatures were measured and found to be 1210 °C and 1380 °C, respectively, by a 
metallurgical method. 
Post-heat treatments involving an annealing and a stabilization treatment were conducted 
on EBM TNM-B1 alloy. The aim of the annealing is to produce supersaturated α2 phases, 
whereas the subsequent stabilization treatment help s to form α2/γ lamellar colonies reported in

[14, 22]. Based on the finding of previous research pertaining to the TNM-B1 alloy counterpart 
[14], the following three annealing treatments were chosen for different phase regions: α/β 
(1240 °C-1 h), β (1400 °C-15 min), and β+γ (1400 °C-15 min + 1210 °C-1 h), with a subsequent 
air-cooling step. After this process, the annealed specimens were subjected to stabilization 
treatment at 850 °C for 6 h followed by furnace cooling. 
The specimens for the microstructural observation w ere mechanically ground and then 
electro-polished in a solution of 60% methyl alcoho l, 35% n-butanol and 5% perchloric acid at -
30 °C and at 20 V. In order to characterize microst ructural details, (the morphology, size and 
volume fraction of the involved phases) backscatter ing electron (BSE) imaging and electron 
backscattering diffraction (EBSD) were used. Micros tructural observations were performed 
using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi SU-6600) equipped with an 
EDAX TSL EBSD system operated at 20 kV. EBSD maps w ere taken with a working distance 
of 15 mm and with a step size of 0.4 µm. 
Specimens for high-temperature tensile tests were p repared by electrical discharge 
machining with the building direction parallel to the tensile direction. The tensile specimens had 
a gauge width of 5 mm, a gauge thickness of 1 mm an d a gauge length of 5 mm. The tensile 
tests were performed at 800 °C with an initial stra in rate of 2×10 -4 s-1 using the INSTRON 5982 
(100 kN) high-temperature tensile testing machine. Two specimens of each condition were 
tested to determine the average values of the yield strength (YS) ultimate strength (UTS) and 
total elongation to fracture (El). To measure the E l values, changes in the gauge length were 
measured before and after the tensile tests by putting the fractured specimens back together.

3.1. As-fabricated microstructure 
Figure 1a shows the microstructures of three differ ent planes of the as-fabricated 
specimen. Columnar grains along the build direction are observed on both the XZ and YZ 
planes, but they are absent on the XY plane. The wi dths of the columnar grains range from 65 to 
75 µm. The high-magnification BSE image shown in Figure 1b reveals a very fine microstructure 
consisting of γ (dark), α
2 (gray) and ordered β (βo) phases (white). In terms of the morphology, 
the fine structure contains α2/γ lamellar colonies (4.5 µm long and 2 µm wide), equiaxed γ grains 
(~1 µm), with the remained area the βo phase. Individual lamellae of α2 or γ in the colonies have 
a submicron thickness in the approximate range of 0 .1-0.5 µm. The EBSD phase map shown in 
Figure 1c reveals that the volume fractions of γ, α2, and βo are 55%, 33% and 12%, respectively. 
The coexistence of the three phases could be associ ated with the preheating temperature of 1000 
°C. The as-fabricated structure with the α2/γ lamellar colony is much finer than that processed by 
conventional processing [14, 16] or SLM [18] as shown in Figure 1d and e. 
 
3.2. Annealed microstructure 
Figure 2 shows BSE images of the microstructure of the TNM-B1 alloy after α/β, β, and 
β+γ annealing. In comparison with the as-fabricated mi crostructure, the annealing process 
results in coarser microstructures that mainly cons ist of supersaturated α
2 (dark grey) and βo 
(white) [14, 22]. The previous γ phases are completely dissolved because the anneal ing 
temperature is higher than T γ (1210 °C). Only a very small amount of γ phase can be observed 
due to air cooling from the annealing temperature [ 23, 24]. With regard to α/β annealing (Figure 
2a), the α2 phases exhibit two morphologies with significantly different sizes. Specifically, we 
noted the presence of equiaxed grains ~10-15 µm in size and short rod-like grains of ~1-2 µm.

Irregularly shaped βo phases remained between these α2 grains. The equiaxed α2 grains may 
have been inherited from the previous α2/γ colonies, while the short rod-like α2 grains stemmed 
from the growth of the previous lamella α2. Both are a result of the dissolution of pre-exist ing γ 
phases. However, when the material is subjected to annealing above T β (β annealing in Figure 
2b and β+γ annealing in Figure 2c), the microstructures mainl y consist of α2 with a relatively 
long shape and a small amount of equiaxed α2, as well as the remaining βo. The formation of the 
long shape of α2 is likely due to the phase transformation of β to α2 during the slow cooling 
process (air cooling). Compared to the single β annealing, the additional step (performed at T γ) 
of β+γ annealing does not show the significant microstructural difference. 
 
3.3. Annealed + stabilized microstructure 
Figure 3 shows BSE images of the microstructure of the annealed TNM-B1 alloy after 
stabilizing at 850 °C for 6 h. After stabilization treatment, there is no significant change in the 
overall morphology of the α
2/γ colony (Figures 3a-3c) as compared with the anneal ed 
microstructures. However, as indicated in the high- magnification images in Figures 3d-3f, 
subsequent stabilization treatment leads to signifi cant changes in two main microstructural 
locations: First, the previously supersaturated α2 grains transform to α2/γ lamellar colonies due 
to the formation of γ lath precipitates ( α2→α2/γ), as typically observed in γ-TiAl alloys during 
stabilization [14, 23, 25]. More specific, Figures 3d-3f reveal that the colony structure consists 
of ultrafine lamellae of alternative α2/γ (~20-40 nm), which is very promising for high-
temperature performance [18]. On the other hand, af ter stabilization, the β annealed specimen 
results in finer colonies than those of α/β annealed and β+γ annealed specimens. Second, the 
remaining βo phases are decomposed into nano-scale lens-shaped γ grains and highly stabilized

the gauge area of the as-fabricated specimen is extended much longer as compared with the other

the tensile test. Whereas in the case of β annealed + stabilized specimen, there are no notic eable 
deformed microstructural features except a slight b end of lamellae towards the tensile loading

prior to the tensile test (Figure 3b and e).

4. Conclusions 
The TNM-B1 alloy was successfully fabricated by the EBM process. Various post-heat 
treatment processes were performed to observe the m icrostructural features and evaluate the 
high-temperature (at 800 °C) mechanical properties. From the results, the following conclusions 
can be drawn: 
1. The as-fabricated TNM-B1 alloy showed a columnar gr ain structure along the building 
direction. The columnar structure consisted of γ, α2 and βo phases as well as very fine α2/γ 
lamellar colonies with submicron thickness. 
2. Post-heat treatments at various phase regions signi ficantly altered the microstructure of as-
fabricated alloy. Especially, the alloy after stabi lization treatment showed the formation of 
ultrafine α2/γ lamellae colonies and the decomposition of βo phase to nano-scale lens-shaped 
γ grains.

the as-fabricated TNM-B1 is superior to the cast+HIP TNM-B1 counterpart. 
4. The annealing resulted in the formation of supersat urated α2 phase and showed the strength 
level of 540-580 MPa. Subsequent stabilization trea tment resulted in the formation of
