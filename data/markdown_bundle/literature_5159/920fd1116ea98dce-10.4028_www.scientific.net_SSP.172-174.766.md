Modelling the in-service microstructural evolution of Ni-based 
superalloys for power generation 
Tianxiang Liu 1, a , Simon P.A. Gill 1,b and Helen V. Atkinson 1,c 
1 Department of Engineering, University of Leicester, University Road, Leicester, UK, LE1 7RH 
atl67@le.ac.uk, bspg3@le.ac.uk, chva2@le.ac.uk 
Key words: Particle distribution; Creep; Microstructural evolution; Ni-based superalloys; 
Precipitation 
Abstract. High temperature operation of power plant increases their efficiency and reduces 
emissions. Low gamma-prime fraction nickel-based superalloys, such as IN617, are becoming 
increasingly important in substituting for conventional steel components due to their better 
performance at higher temperatures. The aim of this work is to model the microstructural evolution 
in this alloy under typical operating conditions and relate this to its creep response. Although 
classified as solid solution strengthened alloys, they contain a range of different precipitates which 
contribute to the creep resistance, the most influential of these being gamma prime. The model is 
developed in three stages. Firstly, a precipitate-level variational model is derived to describe the 
nucleation and growth of individual particles and their interactions. The results are then passed up 
to a grain-level simulation where the response of a statistically significant particle ensemble is 
simulated via the evolution of particle distributions. The resulting model incorporates the effects of 
heat treatment and stress to give the simultaneous size and number evolution of the different phases 
in the material. This information is then used to construct plastic strain-temperature-time diagrams 
in order to estimate the creep rupture life of the material. 
Introduction 
Power plant operate more efficiently and generate less emissions at high operating temperatures. 
Low gamma-prime fraction Ni-based superalloys are increasingly popular candidates for steam 
power plant applications to substitute for conventional steels because of their better creep strength 
and corrosion resistance at higher temperatures. The low gamma-prime Ni-based superalloy Inconel 
617 (IN617) has the potential for use in boiler components and casings. This is traditionally 
considered to be a solid-solution strengethened alloy, with additional strengthening from grain 
boundary carbides and a fine dispersion of gamma prime particles [1]. The microstructural 
evolution of precipitate particles in such engineering materials is very important for determining 
their long-term performance such as the creep rupture life. Precipitates act as obstacles to 
dislocation and grain boundary motion to increase creep resistance, but they can also remove solid-
solution strengthening elements from the matrix to reduce the resistance to creep. The growth of 
embrittling phases, particularly on the grain boundary, can also reduce the ductility of the material. 
A microstructure-dependent deformation model for IN617 is presented in this paper. The 
principle microstructural feature is assumed to be the number-size distribution of the gamma prime 
particles. The growth and coarsening rates of particles within this distribution are predicted from a 
non-equilibrium diffusion-controlled phase transformation model [2]. Particle size and particle 
number time-temperature diagrams are produced to provide a summarized illustration of the effect 
of temperature on the microstructural evolution. Finally, a simple creep model is employed to 
determine the strain rate as a function of the particle distribution. A plastic strain-time-temperature 
diagram is then generated from the microstructural diagrams for estimating the rupture life under a 
particular load.

Model description 
This section describes the development of the microstructure-based creep deformation model. 
The instantaneous state of a material microstructure is assumed to be characterized by the mean 
grain size, the mean dislocation density and the number, size, location and composition of the 
different precipitate phases. In this paper we restrict our consideration to the evolution of the 
precipitate phases, as little change in the grain size in IN617 is observed (due to the generous 
coating of carbides along the grain boundaries). The evaluation of the dislocation density is an 
inherent part of the creep model and is assumed to have little effect on the precipitation kinetics. 
The state of precipitation in the microstructure at a particular time is characterized by the number 
distribution of the precipitate particles in the material as a function of particle size. This important 
information is then used to predict the creep response of the alloy under specific conditions. This 
particle distribution has also been determined from micrographs of test specimens at different 
instances in time in an associated study [3]. This is very useful in calibrating and verifying the 
predictions of the model, although it is a costly exercise, which is necessarily limited to cover 
shorter time spans than the life of the material under typical service conditions (expected to be 25-
30 years). Therefore, a representative microstructure-based creep deformation model is a valuable 
tool in estimating the long time creep-rupture life of an alloy based on known thermodynamic and 
kinetic properties. This is particularly useful if precipitation of some phases occurs over a very long 
time frame (after many years) beyond realistic experimental limits and if this results in a significant 
deterioration of the materials creep response within the expected operational period.

Given the evolution of the particle size, the plastic strain rate (7) can be determined for a specific 
stress and integrated over time to generate a plastic strain-temperature-time diagram, such as that 
shown in Figure 3. As expected, the plastic strain accumulates faster at higher temperatures. The 
failure plastic strain at the given stress has been calculated using the commercial software JMatPro 
[8] and is shown as a blue line in Figure 3. It predicts that the material will also fail at low plastic 
strains at higher temperatures, accentuating further the effects of temperature on the creep rupture 
response. Values for the predicted creep life at different temperatures are shown in Table 2. It is 
clear that the creep model is sensitive to temperature, with a drastic reduction in creep life from 
50,000 hours to 6,000 hours predicted for a modest increase of 50 oC in temperature. Experimental 
data shows that these predictions are reasonable at this (undisclosed) stress level [9]. Results for 
this model (which incorporates information about the evolving microstructure) are also shown 
alongside predictions from a model which assumes a fixed particle size (shown in brackets) in 
Table 2. This shows that the creep rate is also highly sensitive to particle size and that a wide range 
of predictions can be generated without a sufficiently detailed model of the microstructural 
evolution during the creep process.

Conclusions 
A microstructure-based creep deformation model has been developed for the low-gamma prime 
nickel-based superalloy IN617. A precipiate growth model has been developed to predict the rate of 
diffusion-controlled phase transformations in multi-component systems. This has been employed in 
a population balance model to simulate the evolution of a statistically significant number of 
precipitates in the material. Mean size and number temperature-time diagrams have been generated 
to summarize the state of the microstructure at different times and temperatures. This information 
has been incorporated into a simple creep model to generate plastic strain-time-temperature 
diagrams for estimating the creep rupture life at different temperatures and stresses.
