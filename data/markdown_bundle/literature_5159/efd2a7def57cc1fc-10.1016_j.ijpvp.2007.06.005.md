Abstract
Long-term creep and rupture data of NIMS Creep Data Sheets show that the time to rupture at a minimum creep rate of 10 /C05%/h is
longer than 10 5 h for most ferritic steels, while it is shorter than 10 5 h for most austenitic steels, Ni base and Co base superalloys. This is
correlated with the larger total elongation in ferritic steels than in austenitic steels and in Ni base and Co base superalloys at a minimum
creep rate of 10
/C05%/h. However, 67% of the average stress to cause rupture at the end of 10 5 h is lower than the stress to produce a
minimum creep rate of 10 /C05%/h for all the materials examined. This indicates that the ASME allowable stress in a high-temperature
creep region is determined by creep rupture data but not by creep strain data. The creep and creep rate curves are compared between
2.25Cr–1Mo steel and 18Cr–8Ni austenitic steel at approximately the same minimum creep rate. 18Cr–8Ni austenitic steel reaches a
minimum creep rate in shorter time and spends shorter time in the acceleration creep before creep rupture than 2.25Cr–1Mo steel at low
stress and long time conditions. This is correlated with different microstructure evolution during creep.

1. Introduction
In a high-temperature creep region, the allowable stress
in ASME Section 2 is determined by several factors, such
as 100% of the average stress to produce a creep rate of
0.01%/1000 h ( ¼ 10
/C05%/h), 67% of the average stress
(below 815 1C) and 80% of the minimum stress to cause
rupture at the end of 10 5 h [1]. The concept of ASME high-
temperature strength standards has been accepted in
Japan. Recently, long-term creep and rupture data have
been accumulated for a number of heat-resistant steels and
alloys in Japan, especially in NIMS Creep Data Sheets [2].
In a previous paper [3], we evaluated 67% of the average
stress and 80% of the minimum stress to cause rupture at
the end of 10
5 h for a number of heat-resistant steels, using
long-term creep rupture data, mainly the data in NIMS
Creep Data Sheets. We have revealed whether the
allowable stress determined by 80% of the minimum stress
or 67% of the average stress to cause rupture at the end of
10
5 h depends on the width of the scatter band of creep
rupture data and on the order of regression equations. For
2.25Cr–1Mo steel speciﬁed as JIS STBA24, which shows a
narrow scatter band of creep rupture data at long times,
67% of the average stress is lower than 80% of the
minimum stress in the temperature range between 450 and
600 1C, with one exception. For 18Cr–8Ni steel speciﬁed as
JIS SUS 304HTB, which shows a wide scatter band of
creep rupture data at long times, 80% of the minimum
stress is smaller than 67% of the average stress at
temperatures higher than 600 1C. The 3rd-order regression
of creep rupture data using the Larson-Miller parameter
causes much more steep decrease in stress for JIS SUS
304HTB steel at high temperature and long-time condi-
tions compared with the 2nd-order regression. This
indicates that the 3rd-order regression results in a wide
scatter band of creep rupture data at high temperature and

In the present research, the stress to produce a minimum
creep rate of 10 /C05%/h and the stress to cause rupture at the
end of 10 5 h have been evaluated for a number of ferritic
and austenitic steels and Ni base and Co base superalloys,

2. Evaluation of stress to produce a minimum creep rate of
10
/C05%/h and stress to cause rupture at the end of 10 5 h
In NIMS Creep Data Sheets [2], the fact data of long-
term creep and rupture tests have been published in each
material. The ﬁrst, second and third editions contain the
creep rupture data up to about 10,000, 30,000–50,000 and
100,000 h, respectively, in the materials except for super-
alloys. The third edition designated B-edition, for example
NIMS Creep Data Sheet No. 1B, contains a full set of data:
creep rupture data, minimum creep rates, short-time tensile
data, evaluation of short-time tensile strength and long-
term creep rupture strength by regression analysis and
optical micrographs.
In the present work, the stress to produce a minimum
creep rate of 10
/C05%/h was evaluated from the stress
dependence of minimum creep rate. In the third edition of
NIMS Creep Data Sheets, a ﬁgure showing the stress
dependence of minimum creep rate and a regression
equation of minimum creep rate in terms of stress are
provided in each material. The stress to cause rupture at
the end of 10
5 h, corresponding to 10 5 h creep rupture
strength, is also provided as a function of temperature for
individual heats in a table format in the third edition of
NIMS Creep Data Sheets, together with appropriate
regression equations. It should be noted that the creep
strain data such as minimum creep rate are provided for
only a few heats in NIMS Creep Data Sheets, although the
creep rupture data such as time to rupture and rupture
elongation are provided for about 10 heats in each
material. Because heat-to-heat variation in creep and
rupture properties is rather large as shown by more than
one order of magnitude difference in time to rupture
between the weakest and strongest heats in some materials
[3], the stress to produce a minimum creep rate of 10
/C05%/h
is compared with the 10 5 h creep rupture strength on the
same heat.

stress to produce a minimum creep rate of 10
/C05%/h. The
chemical requirements of Ni base 19Cr–18Co–4Mo–3Ti–

following, the Ni base 19Cr–18Co–4Mo–3Ti–3Al–B alloy
is designated U500. In ferritic 0.3% carbon steel, 2.25Cr–
1Mo steel and 12Cr–1Mo–1W–0.3V steel, the stress to
produce a minimum creep rate of 10
/C05%/h is smaller
than or approximately the same as the 10 5 h creep
rupture strength for a wide range of temperature. However,
0.67 of the 10
5 h creep rupture strength is smaller than
the stress to produce a minimum creep rate of 10 /C05%/h.
In 18Cr–8Ni austenitic steel and Ni base superalloy U500,
on the other hand, the stress to produce a minimum creep
rate of 10
/C05%/h is larger than the 10 5 h creep rupture
strength, indicating that 0.67 of the 10 5 h creep rupture
strength is inevitably smaller than the stress to produce a
minimum creep rate of 10
/C05%/h. Thus, the long-term
NIMS data indicate that 0.67 of the 10 5 h creep rupture
strength is smaller than the stress to produce a mini-
mum creep rate of 10
/C05%/h for all the materials examined.
This indicates that the ASME allowable stress is not
determined by the stress to produce a minimum creep rate
of 10 /C05%/h.
In the previous paper [3], the stress to produce a
minimum creep rate of 10 /C05%/h was compared with the
stress to produce a strain of 1% at 10 5 h for 1Cr–0.5Mo
(JIS STBA22), 2.25Cr–1Mo (JIS STBA24), 18Cr–8Ni (JIS
SUS 304HTB) and 18Cr–10Ni–Ti (JIS SUS 321HTB)
steels. ‘‘100% of the average stress to produce 1%/
100,000 h’’ is one of the factors that determine the German
allowable stress. The stress to produce a creep strain of 1%
at 10
5 h was equal to or a little bit smaller than the stress to
produce a minimum creep rate of 10 /C05%/h. This results
from the strain accumulation in the transient or primary
creep due to easy deformation. It should be noted that 0.67
of the 10
5 h creep rupture strength is again smaller than
the stress to produce a creep strain of 1% at 10 5 h.
Gianfrancesco and his co-workers [4] have been carrying
out long-term creep and rupture tests for Grade 91 pipes
and tubes in the temperature range of 500–700 1C, where
some of them have reached more than 7 /C2 10
4 h (Tenaris-
Dalmine database). The Larson-Miller method was used to
assess the data. The assessed stress was 93 MPa for the
10
5 h creep rupture strength and 83 MPa for the stress to
produce a creep strain of 1% at 10 5 h at 600 1C. 0.67 of the
105 h creep rupture strength, which is evaluated to be
62.3 MPa, is again smaller than the stress to produce a
creep strain of 1% at 10
5 h, indicating that the allowable
stress is determined by creep rupture data but not by creep
strain data.

3.5. Importance of creep strain data
The creep strain data have been used for stress analysis
of high-temperature components and for remaining life
estimation. Practical design problems usually involve a
more complex stress state than constant-stress uniaxial
tension [14]. The procedure of analysis is basically that of
ﬁtting an empirical expression to the experimental con-
stant-stress tension data and with certain assumptions
applying it to problems of varying and combined stresses.
In the European Creep Collaborative Committee, the
current emphasis for working groups is the analysis and
application of creep deformation and ductility properties to
the assessment of high-temperature components and multi-
axial features [15]. Signiﬁcant effort is being devoted to the
formulation of recommendations concerning the assess-
ment of creep strain. In Japan, Shimakawa and co-workers
[16] are proposing the stress redistribution locus (SRL)
method for creep-fatigue life estimation for high-tempera-
ture components operated under cyclic thermal transient.
In this method, SRL gives elastic follow-up behavior of
structure due to material non-linearity such as plastic or
creep. y-Projection [17,18] and O [10] methods, which are
based on the analysis of creep strain data, would provide
more reliable remaining creep life rather than time–
temperature parameter methods such as Larson-Miller
and Manson-Haferd, which are based on the analysis of
creep rupture data.
The analysis of creep strain data has also been
contributing to better understanding of creep behavior
and strengthening mechanisms of heat-resistant steels and
alloys. Many researchers have shown for engineering heat-
resistant steels that there is an ever-evolving microstructure
and the change in creep rate reﬂects coupled elementary
processes, such as subgrain growth, change in dislocation
density, change in precipitate volume fraction and size.
These results suggest that in engineering heat-resistant
steels there is no dynamic microstructural equilibrium
during creep, which characterizes steady-state creep of
simple metals and alloys.
Thus, it is recommended that creep strain data should be
accumulated especially at low stress and long-time condi-
tions, even if the ASME allowable stress is not determined
by creep strain data.
4. Conclusions
(1) In ferritic 0.3% carbon steel, 2.25Cr–1Mo steel and
12Cr–1Mo–1W–0.3V steel, the stress to produce a
minimum creep rate of 10
/C05%/h is smaller than or
approximately the same as the 10 5 h creep rupture
strength for a wide range of temperature. However,
0.67 of the 10 5 h creep rupture strength is smaller than
the stress to produce a minimum creep rate of 10 /C05%/
h. In 18Cr–8Ni austenitic steel and Ni base superalloy
U500, on the other hand, the stress to produce a
minimum creep rate of 10
/C05%/h is larger than the 10 5 h
creep rupture strength, indicating that 0.67 of the 10 5 h
creep rupture strength is inevitably smaller than the
stress to produce a minimum creep rate of 10 /C05%/h.
The present results suggest that the ASME allowable
stress is determined by the creep rupture data but not
by the creep strain data.
(2) For most ferritic steels, the time to rupture at a
minimum creep rate of 10
/C05%/h is larger than 10 5 h,
while it is smaller than 10 5 h for most austenitic steels,
Ni base and Co base superalloys. This is correlated with
larger total elongation in ferritic steels than in
austenitic steels and in Ni base and Co base superalloys
at a minimum creep rate of 10
/C05%/h.
