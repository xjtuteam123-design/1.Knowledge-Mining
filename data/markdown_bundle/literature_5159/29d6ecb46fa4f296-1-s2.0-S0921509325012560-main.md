ABSTRACT
In this work, we elucidate the plasticity mechanisms and local plastic evolution of Ni-based single crystal su -
peralloys during indentation loading from the perspective of atomic simulations. The results indicate that 1/ 
6 < 112 > Shockley dislocations are the origin of the plastic response and constitute the core deformation 
mechanism of superalloys. At room temperature, dislocations predominantly accumulate within the γ channels, 
forming high-density stacking faults (SFs). As the temperature increases to 600 – 900 K, dislocations shear into the 
γ ′ precipitates, resulting in the formation of SFs, SF rings and immobile dislocation locks, which lead to dislo -
cation pinning. At high temperatures (1200 – 1500 K), atomic thermal activation promotes the disappearance of 
SFs and the formation of irregular dislocation network structures, with dislocations climbing and bypass 
mechanisms dominating the plastic deformation. Local plastic deformation is primarily characterized by the 
nucleation and expansion of dislocations, with the extent of the plastic zone positively correlated with tem -
perature. The simulation results provide a clear explanation of the deformation mechanisms of Ni-based single 
crystal superalloys at different temperatures.
1. Introduction
Ni-based single crystal superalloys typically maintain excellent me -
chanical properties and oxidation resistance under extreme conditions, 
making them widely used in turbine engines and gas turbines, and they 
are irreplaceable candidate materials for turbine blades in aerospace 
engines[ 1 – 3 ]. The superior performance in service is attributed to their 
microstructure, where ordered L1
2
-structured γ ′ precipitates phase in the 
form of cuboids are uniformly embedded within the disordered FCC 
structure of the γ phase, with sizes ranging from approximately 300 to 
500 nm. Compared to single-phase alloys, the typical two-phase 
microstructural distribution and fully coherent {100} interfaces pro -
vide superior mechanical properties. Due to the highly ordered struc -
ture, the γ ′ precipitates exhibit high strength, effectively impeding 
dislocation movement and ensuring the superalloy ’ s strength at elevated 
temperatures[ 4 – 6 ]. However, under more severe working conditions, 
dislocations can still shear into the γ ′ precipitate phase from the 
two-phase interface, leading to the formation of complex defects that 
compromise the superalloy ’ s structure and reduce its mechanical per -
formance[ 4 , 7 – 9 ]. Therefore, studying the degradation/failure of the 
microstructure under mechanical loading at various temperatures is 
crucial for optimizing superalloy fabrication processes and guiding 
compositional design.
Nanoindentation has long been the most common method for eval -
uating the local mechanical properties of materials. In addition to 
measuring mechanical parameters, it can also be used to study the for -
mation and expansion of dislocation defects under load conditions [ 10 ]. 
Furthermore, atomic-scale nanoindentation can effectively reflect 
atomic configurations, especially dislocation evolution, and has been 
widely applied. Over the past decade, atomic-scale simulations have 
been extensively used to describe the internal mechanisms of material 
microstructures during plastic deformation, with particular emphasis on 
dislocation interactions, which play a crucial role in understanding alloy 
plasticity responses and mechanical properties[ 11 – 14 ]. For example, 
atomic simulation studies of nanoindentation in single crystals [ 15 , 16 ], 
polycrystals [ 17 ], high-entropy alloys [ 18 ], and the effects of dislocation

In the present study, a simplified atomic model consistent with the 
typical microstructure of Ni-based single crystals was established. Mo -
lecular dynamics simulations of nanoindentation were conducted within 
a temperature range of 300 – 1500 K, with a focus on investigating the 
temperature-dependent plastic response mechanisms and local plastic 
behavior. During the indentation process, particular attention was paid 
to the dynamic evolution of planar defects and deformation near the 
indenter. Dislocation analyses were performed to discuss the plastic 
deformation mechanisms at different temperatures, with visualizations 
of the corresponding dislocation evolution, thereby facilitating a deeper 
understanding of the underlying deformation mechanisms of Ni-based 
single crystal superalloys. Furthermore, the variation in dislocation 
density for different dislocation types during plastic deformation was 
summarized, and the contributions of each dislocation type to the 
overall plasticity were analyzed. Finally, the influence of temperature on 
local plastic behavior was discussed. The findings of this work provide a 
theoretical basis for understanding the microstructure evolution of Ni- 
based single crystal superalloys under localized loading, offering new 
insights into deformation mechanisms across a broad temperature range 
at the atomic scale.

3. Results
3.1. Temperature dependence of indention response
The loading and unloading processes of nanoindentation on Ni-based 
single crystal superalloys at different temperatures were recorded, with 
the P-h curves shown in Fig. 2 a. In the early contact stage, samples 
across the temperature range followed Hertzian theory, exhibiting an 
initial rising trend, followed by slight decreases or fluctuations corre -
sponding to dislocation multiplication and annihilation. When the 
maximum indentation depth reached 3 nm, the indenter began to 
retract, and the load decreased accordingly. At a depth of 2 nm, the load 
dropped to 0 GPa. Combined with Fig. 2 b, the hardness-depth curve 
increased sharply in the initial stage to reach a peak, then exhibited 
sawtooth oscillations before gradually stabilizing. Notably, the hardness 
decreased with increasing temperature, indicating a reduction in surface 
hardness and weakened surface hardening effects; this is primarily due 
to the temperature-induced decrease in load force ( Fig. 2 c). The influ -
ence of temperature on the elastic modulus is shown in Fig. 2 d, revealing 
that the modulus decreases with increasing temperature. Observing the 
dynamically measured elastic modulus at different temperatures (from a 
forth generation superalloy) and the static elastic modulus [ 36 ] (from 
the CMSX-4 superalloy), the simulation results showed good agreement 
with experimental data, indicating that the model effectively captures 
the deformation evolution of Ni-based single crystal superalloys.
Fig. 3 illustrates the distribution of shear strain within the micro -
structure indicators of the superalloy at different temperatures as a 
function of depth. The atoms in the matrix phase and reinforcement 
phase are color-coded according to the magnitude of the shear strain.

scale and the resulting surface micro-morphology are highly sensitive to 
temperature, with temperature-dependent effects driven by microscopic 
deformation mechanisms and macroscopic mechanical properties.
3.2. Microstructure and dislocation evolution
To further elucidate the nature of the observed mechanical property 
variations, the microstructure evolution during the indentation process 
was systematically analyzed, with particular emphasis on the localized 
plastic deformation mechanisms and dislocation evolution near the 
indenter. Fig. 6 presents the dislocation density variation with inden -
tation depth at different temperatures, revealing an overall increasing 
trend. Throughout the indentation process, the test at 900 K consistently

1500 K, the highest temperature tested, the dislocation density remains

dislocations. When the indentation depth is less than 0.75 nm, the 
plastic deformation is primarily localized within the softer γ phase,

dislocation locks or pinning points, which cause increasing in disloca -

observed in Ni-based single crystal superalloys during tensile tests.

cross-slip systems, appearing as stair-step structures on two slip planes,

4. Discussion
4.1. Plastic deformation mechanism from RT to 900K
The excellent mechanical properties of Ni-based single crystal su -
peralloys are primarily determined by their two-phase microstructure 
and the associated deformation mechanisms. The γ phase is an FCC solid 
solution, while the γ ′ phase is an intermetallic compound with an L1
2 
structure. Elevating the temperature induces compositional changes in 
both phases. The mechanical behavior of the superalloy depends on the 
deformation mechanisms operative at different temperatures. Fig. 10
illustrates the evolution of total dislocations and dislocations within the 
precipitates phase under indentation load over the temperature range of 
300 – 900 K, along with statistical data on the atomic fraction of various 
structural types. As demonstrated in the results section, dislocation 
configurations differ across temperatures. With increasing plastic 
deformation, dislocation tangling emerges as a common feature 
observed at all temperatures ( Fig. 8 ). However, variations in dislocation 
density are evident, especially for some low mobile dislocations such as 
Stair-rod dislocations (1/6 < 110 > ) and Hirth dislocations (1/3 < 100 > ). 
These dislocations contribute differently to the superalloy ’ s plastic 
deformation at various temperatures, leading to the accumulation of 
distinct planar defects within the γ ′ precipitates.
During plastic deformation at room temperature, the dislocation 
density in the γ matrix significantly exceeds that within the γ ′ pre -
cipitates ( Fig. 10 a), indicating initial dislocation activity primarily 
within the γ channels where they interact. The presence of a substantial 
number of tangled dislocations in the γ matrix and some dislocations 
within the γ ′ precipitates represent typical room-temperature deforma -
tion microstructures [ 42 ]. In the early deformation stages, due to the 
low stacking fault energy (SFE) of Ni in the matrix phase, accumulated 
a/2 < 110 > dislocations continuously glide and dissociate within the 
channels, producing high densities of SFs [ 43 , 44 ]. Atomic configuration 
analyses show that, as indentation depth increases, the proportion of 
HCP structure atoms continues to rise, reflecting the accumulation of 
intrinsic stacking fault (ISF) ( Fig. 10 d). As deformation progresses, 
numerous a/2 < 110 > dislocations with different Burgers vectors pro -
liferate and glide within the matrix, potentially hindered at γ / γ ′ in -
terfaces. Once the dislocation density at these interfaces reaches a 
certain threshold, the increased flow stress can cause dislocation 
dissociation and partial glide into the γ ′ phase, forming SFs ( Fig. 8 a), the 
formation mechanisms are illustrated in Fig. 10 g. Compared to dislo -
cation formation within γ channels, the mobility of partial dislocations 
shearing into the γ ′ phase is relatively low and prone to pinning. Addi -
tionally, dislocations shearing into the γ ′ phase generate planar defects, 
including super-lattice intrinsic stacking faults (SISF), complex stacking 
faults (CSF), and anti-phase boundaries (APB). The energy barriers for 
the gliding of the three types of partial dislocations into the γ ′ phase

4.2. Plastic deformation mechanism from 1200K to 1500K
As the temperature increases further, the dislocation density de -
creases, and when the testing temperature exceeds 1200 K, only a few 
SFs are observed. In addition, the dislocation density drops sharply, even 
falling below the levels measured at room temperature ( Fig. 11 a and b). 
The lower lattice mismatch between the two phases and the decreased 
matrix strength at high temperatures ( Fig. 2 ) facilitate rapid dislocation 
motion under thermal activation, leading to the formation of extensive 
irregular interface dislocation networks ( Fig. 8 d). This observation is 
consistent with experimental results [ 49 ]. Elevated temperatures in -
crease the interface strain energy, however, the interface dislocation 
network effectively dissipates this excess energy [ 50 ]. As the tempera -
ture rises, the energy associated with anti-phase boundaries (APBs) 
decreases [ 51 ], causing a rapid decline in ISF defect density ( Fig. 11 c 
and d). Furthermore, the interface dislocation network exerts minimal 
hindrance to dislocation motion; when the APB energy (APBE) is less 
than the SFE, dislocations shearing into the γ ′ precipitates dissociate 
more readily to produce APBs. At 1500 K, dislocation mobility accel -
erates, and dislocations rapidly accumulate at the γ / γ ′ interface, forming 
even more irregular interface dislocation networks ( Fig. 8 e), which also 
facilitate the release of misfit stresses. However, the high temperature 
reduces the dislocation activation energy, making dislocations more 
readily move and shear into the γ ′ precipitates. Consequently, the su -
peralloy softens, resulting in the lowest hardness and elastic modulus at 
1500 K ( Fig. 2 ). This indicates that the strength contribution from the γ ′ 
precipitates is significantly greater than that from the interface dislo -
cation networks. At elevated temperatures (1200 K and 1500 K), the 
deformation mechanisms of Ni-based single crystal superalloys transi -

4.3. Temperature dependence analysis of local plasticity
As previously mentioned, during the indentation process, atoms 
located near the indenter are subjected to compressive forces, resulting 
in a pile-up on the material surface ( Fig. 5 ). Neglecting the effect of these 
pile-up atoms can lead to deviations in the assessment of the material ’ s 
plastic behavior [ 52 , 53 ], therefore, a comprehensive understanding of 
this localized plasticity behavior during nanoindentation across 
different temperatures is importance [ 47 ]. Fig. 12 illustrates the 
microstructure evolution within the localized plastic zones near the 
indenter at an indentation depth of 3 nm over a temperature range of 
300 K – 1500 K. Various degrees of dislocation pile-up are observed in the 
vicinity of the indenter, where the extent and height of pile-up reflect the 
plastic deformation behavior of Ni-based single crystal superalloys. The 
formation of pile-up is controlled by the crystal orientation and slip 
systems active within the indented phase [ 54 ]. It is evident from the 
images that the atomic pile-up primarily results from dislocation 
nucleation and expansion. As the temperature increases, dislocation 
pile-up becomes more frequent and spans a larger area. Using image 
processing software to identify and measure the dimensions of these 
localized plastic zones, as shown in Fig. 12 , the area percentage of 
pile-up regions relative to the total indented surface area was calculated. 
The results show that the area percentages increase with temperature, 
measuring 6.62 %, 9.21 %, 9.85 %, 11.28 %, and 17.14 % at the 
respective temperatures, indicating a temperature-dependent trend of 
deformation activity. This suggests that under the same load conditions, 
the plastic response of Ni-based single crystal superalloys accelerates as 
temperature rises. The alloy exhibits minimal plastic response at room 
temperature, indicating strong resistance to plastic deformation, 
whereas higher temperatures promote further plastic flow. At room 
temperature, pronounced atomic pile-up is observed on the {111} slip 
planes, which is typically observed in face-centered cubic metals due to 
the {111} < 10 1 > slip system [ 55 , 56 ]. However, this deformation 
feature gradually diminishes with increasing temperature, as higher 
thermal activation activates multiple slip systems on the {111} planes, 
leading to pile-up phenomena observed across multiple directions and 
over larger areas. Additionally, it is noteworthy that nanoindentation 
simulations may possess the potential for characterizing planar defects.
5. Conclusion
This study constructed an atomic model of the typical microstructure 
of Ni-based single crystal superalloys and employed molecular dynamics 
simulations to investigate their mechanical responses under nano -
indentation. By combining dislocation and SF analyses, the local plastic 
deformation behavior and mechanisms at different temperatures were 
discussed in detail. The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1) Dislocation analysis reveals that different dislocation types have 
distinct impacts on the superalloy ’ s plastic response. During 
plastic deformation, Shockley partial dislocations are the most 
prevalent and constitute the primary microstructure mechanism, 
whereas Stair-rod and Hirth dislocations tend to form immobile 
dislocation locks that lead to dislocation pinning.
(2) At room temperature, the deformation primarily involves cross- 
slip and interactions of dislocations within the matrix channels. 
High dislocation densities and SFs are generated within γ chan -
nels, along with a small number of partial dislocations shearing 
into γ ′ precipitates to form SFs. As temperature increases into the 
intermediate temperatures (600 K and 900 K), thermal activation 
drives a significant number of dislocations to shear into the γ ′ 
precipitates, and deformation mechanisms are dominated by 
immobile dislocations, SFs, or SF loops within the γ ′ phases.
