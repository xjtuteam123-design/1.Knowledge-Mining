ABSTRACT
High Nb containing TiAl alloys are considered as potential high-temperature materials for low-pressure turbine 
blades. To push these materials towards application, diffusion welding between high Nb-TiAl and superalloys is a 
possible way to connect TiAl alloys to superalloy turbine disks. This study systematically compares the process 
parameters for the diffusion bonding of high Nb-TiAl alloy and GH4169 alloy. The results reveal that the optimal 
parameters are a bonding temperature of 950

1. Introduction
High Nb containing TiAl (high Nb-TiAl) alloys, as high-temperature 
lightweight structural materials used in the aerospace industry, exhibit 
several remarkable advantages, including low density, high specific 
strength, good creep resistance, and excellent high-temperature oxida -
tion resistance [1 – 4] . Especially at temperatures exceeding 750 
◦
C, they 
are poised to emerge as the next-generation high-temperature light -
weight structural materials [5 – 7] . The joining of TiAl alloys with widely 
used structural materials in the aerospace industry, such as nickel-based 
superalloys, is highly significant for improving the thrust-to-weight ratio 
of aircraft [8] . Traditional fusion welding methods often result in poor 
weldability of joints, with a high tendency for post-weld cracking, which 
significantly affects welding performance [9] . In contrast, diffusion 
bonding is a simpler process, yields joints with lower residual stress, and 
ensures high precision [10] , making it suitable for the dissimilar welding 
of brittle-sensitive high Nb-TiAl alloys.
The diffusion bonding of TiAl alloys and Ti-based alloys has been 
widely investigated, with particular emphasis on the bonding between 
TiAl alloys and Ti-6Al-4V (TC4) alloy. Glatz et al. [11] conducted 
diffusion bonding of Ti-47Al-2Cr-0.2Si (at%) alloy and TC4 alloy at 
1000 
◦
C, 20 MPa, and 3 h. Under these conditions, the interface 
exhibited strong interdiffusion, resulting in a serrated morphology. 
Additionally, the TC4 alloy showed coarse microstructures and signifi -
cant plastic deformation after bonding. Although fractures occurred in 
the base material, high-quality joints were achieved. Gao et al. [12]
further reduced the temperature, pressure, and time, obtaining 
defect-free, high-quality joints. The interfacial phase composition was 
identified as β - γ high Nb-TiAl/ β /B2/ α

of the base materials was less than 2 %, meeting the requirements for 
precision joining.
Additionally, the welding of dissimilar materials between TiAl alloys 
and superalloys has also been extensively studied. Luo et al. [13]
investigated the effects of laser surface modification on the diffusion 
bonding of γ -TiAl and K418 alloy. When the coating material was 
Ni-7Cr-15Ti-22Al (wt%), the joint exhibited the best mechanical prop -

employed thermoplastic bonding (TPB) to braze TiAl and Ni-based alloy 
(GH4049) below the melting point of Zr-Al-Ni-Cu filler metal, achieving

2. Material and experimental procedures
The nominal compositions of the high Nb-TiAl alloy and GH4169 
alloy used in the experiment are listed in Table 1 . The high Nb-TiAl alloy 
base material was processed into blocks measuring 10 mm × 35 mm 
× 5 mm, while the GH4169 alloy base material was processed into 
blocks measuring 15 mm × 35 mm × 5 mm. The surfaces to be welded 
were sequentially ground using sandpaper until no visible scratches 
remained, and then ultrasonically cleaned in alcohol for 20 min.
Diffusion welding was performed using a ZKG-13 vacuum high- 
temperature hot-pressing sintering furnace, with temperature control 
accuracy of ± 1 
◦
C and an ultimate vacuum level of 1 × 10
 3 
Pa. To fully 
exploit the superplastic characteristics of TiAl alloys, such as low 
deformation resistance, high deformation rate, and strong atomic 
diffusion capability within a certain temperature range [2] , three 
diffusion bonding temperatures 850 
◦
C, 900 
◦
C, and 950 
◦
C were 
selected, with four bonding pressures 20 MPa, 40 MPa, 50 MPa, and 
60 MPa at each bonding temperature. To avoid insufficient bonding 
time, which could result in poorly connected joints, or excessive bonding 
time, which could lead to the formation of thick brittle intermetallic 
layers [19] , the bonding times selected for this study were 30 min, 
40 min, and 60 min. The specific diffusion bonding process parameters 
are listed in Table 2 .
After bonding, wire electrical discharge machining (EDM) was 
employed to prepare metallographic specimens by sectioning the joint 
specimens along the welding direction. Mechanical polishing was per -
formed using a polishing machine with 1.5 μ m alumina polishing sus -
pension. The joint interface was then etched using Kroll ’ s reagent (HF: 
HNO ₃ :H ₂ O = 1:4:25). The microstructures of the joint interface and 
shear fracture surface were characterized using backscattered electron 
(BSE) imaging on a ZEISS Sigma 300 field-emission scanning electron 
microscope (SEM). The original microstructures are shown in Fig. 1 . The 
high Nb-TiAl alloy exhibits a near-lamellar structure, composed of α
2
/ γ 
lamellar colonies and B2 phase, with the α
2
/ γ lamellar colonies 
measuring approximately 100 μ m in size ( Fig. 1 (a)). The GH4169 alloy 
has an equiaxed structure, consisting of γ -Ni matrix phase, Ni
3
Nb phase, 
and carbides in dark contrast, with the average grain size of the γ -Ni 
phase being about 20 μ m ( Fig. 1 (b)).
The elemental composition and distribution at the joint interfaces 
were analyzed using the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) 
detector attached to the SEM. The grain size and morphology of the joint 
were characterized using the electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) 
probe on a TESCAN AMBER dual-beam microscope, with the joint 
specimen prepared by electrolytic polishing.
Microhardness testing of the joint was conducted using a Wilson 
VH3300 microhardness tester from Buehler, USA, with a load of 1000 g 
and a dwell time of 15 s. Fifteen points were tested for each measure -
ment, with a spacing of 0.2 mm between points, and each characteristic 
region was tested three times, with the average value representing the 
hardness. Room-temperature shear testing of the diffusion-bonded joint 
specimens was performed using an Instron 3382 universal testing ma -
chine, with each set of process parameters tested at least three times.

3.4. Formation process of the diffusion-bonded joint
Based on the observations of microstructure and properties, it can be 
concluded that the formation process of the diffusion-bonded joint can 
be divided into three stages:
(1) Physical contact stage: under the influence of bonding pressure 
and temperature, the GH4169 alloy, which has a lower high- 
temperature yield strength, softens first and undergoes significant 
plastic deformation. This allows it to come into contact with the as -
perities on the surface of the high Nb-TiAl alloy, leading to the defor -
mation of micro-protrusions and creating diffusion pathways for 
subsequent atomic diffusion. However, due to insufficient contact, a 
series of unclosed voids remain at the original contact interface, as 
shown in Fig. 12 (a).

high Nb-TiAl alloy and GH4169 alloy at a bonding temperature of 950
