This paper concerns our recent work on developing a new kind of advanced disk alloys based on the mix-
tures of two kinds of two-phase superalloys, Co-base Co-16.9 wt.%Ti and Ni-base U720Li superalloys, for
the latest turbine design. The tensile creep behavior of these new Ni–Co-base superalloys at temperatures
in the range of 650–760
◦ C and at initial applied stresses of 480–830 MPa were investigated. Comparing
a new Ni–Co-base alloy (TMW-4) to U720Li by a Larson–Miller plot shows that a temperature gain in
stress-rupture strength of 30 can be expected in a TMW alloy with ﬁne-grained microstructure (10 (m).

1. Introduction
Many of the current alloys intended for use as disks either
in aircraft engines or land-based gas turbine engines are Ni-base
superalloys because of their excellent properties, which include
high-temperature strength, resistance to creep and fatigue, hot
corrosion and oxidation resistance [1,2]. Most Ni-base disk alloys
contain 10–20 wt.% Cr, up to 8 wt.% Al and Ti, 5–23 wt.% Co, and small
amounts of B, Zr and C[3]. Other common additions are some refrac-
tory elements like Mo, W, Ta, Hf and Nb, which can provide strength
beneﬁts. The latest design of advanced engines, like new supersonic
engine, has higher requirements for the operating speed and tem-
perature of turbine disks at both the rim and the bore. By increasing
the volume fraction of /H9253
′phase and adding heavily solid-solution-
hardening elements, new modern Ni-base disk superalloys have
been developed to meet the demands [4,5]. Such an increase in
the volume fraction, combined with increased levels of refractory
elements, have led to difﬁculties in the control of grain structure
during the forging process and in the production of an optimum
microstructure after forging because of segregation problems and
the complicated interactions between the forging parameters and
the large number of microstructure constituent elements during
processing via the normal cast-and-wrought (C&W) route. There-
fore, many modern turbine disk alloys such as Rene 95, RR1000 and
ME3 are now produced via the powder-metallurgical (P/M) route
[6,7]. However, the need of making clean powder and using expen-

lead up to some promising disk alloys that can be processed by
the cheap C&W route and have superior creep resistance compared
with C&W U720Li superalloy.

3.2. Tensile creep behavior
Tensile creep tests were conducted with all tested alloys at tem-
peratures in the range of 650–760 ◦C and at initial applied stresses
of 480–830 MPa.
At 650◦C and 628 MPa, the tested alloys exhibited primary creep
and stable minimum strain rates over a long creep time (1000 h).
Time to 0.2% creep strain was about 810 h for TMW 1 alloy (U720Li)
and was over 1000 h for the other tested TMW alloys. It is noted
that 1000-h creep strain was only about 0.02% for the TMW 2 alloy.
At 725
◦C and 630 MPa ( Fig. 3 ), time to 0.2% creep strain was
about 10 h for TMW 1 alloy (U720Li), 62 h for TMW 2 alloy and over
120 h for TMW 4 alloy. So there is 12 times improvement in the life
of creep to 0.2% strain for TMW 4 alloys compared with TMW 1
alloys (U720Li) at such high temperature and stress.

4. Discussion
Upon analyzing all of above results, it appears that our new
design concept may lead to some promising candidate alloys, such
as TMW 2 and 4 alloys, which can be processed by the cheap
“cast-and-wrought” (C&W) route and have superior creep resis-
tance compared with C&W 720Li superalloy.
Keeping in mind that interpretations given below are prelim-
inary and subject to further investigations, we will proceed to
describe what we believe is happening. A striking result of this
investigation is the remarkably higher creep resistance of TMW
4 alloy compared with TMW 1 even though both of these alloys
have the same /H9253
′ volume fraction and grain size ( Table 1). As we
known, the creep strength of this kind of two-phase superalloy
depends on a number of interrelated microstructural parame-
ters, including the volume fraction, particle size, distribution, and
chemical characteristics of the /H9253
′ precipitates present in a disor-
dered solid-solution face centered cubic /H9253matrix, along with the
grain size [12–14]. Under creep conditions, depending on the alloy,
temperature and strain rate, the deformation mode can change sig-
niﬁcantly. According to this research, for steady-state creep, the
apparent activation energy for the tested TMW1, TMW 2 and TMW
4 was 586, 568 and 563 kJ/mol, respectively. These experimen-
tal activation energy values are considerable higher than those
reported for lattice diffusion (280 kJ/mol) in Ni-base superalloys
[15]. There have been several attempts to explain this high acti-
vation energy [16–19]. Leverant and Kear [16] suggested that the
rate-controlling process might involve self-diffusion in the ordered
/H9253
′ precipitates, which almost certainly has an activation energy
considerable greater than that of self-diffusion in Ni or in the
disordered /H9253matrix. Webster and Piearcey [18] suggested that
the activation energy for steady-state creep should be twice the
value of the activation energy for self-diffusion, which makes the

[20–22]. Therefore, the SFs become more energetically favorable as
the ratio of SF energy to APB energy decreases. So it is not surprising
that an increase in cobalt content would result in a higher density
of SFs in TMW 2 and TMW 4 alloys, as was observed. Meanwhile,

5. Summary
The tensile creep behaviors of newly designed Ni–Co-base disk
superalloys were investigated at temperatures in the range of
650–760
◦C and at initial applied stresses of 480–830 MPa. The
results are summarized as follows:
1. TMW-4 alloy has the highest creep resistance in the tested TMW
alloys. TMW-2 alloy have better creep resistance than TMW-1
(U720LI). The tensile creep minimum rate of TMW-2 and -4 are
about one order lower compared with TMW-1 (U720LI) at all
tested temperatures and stresses.
2. Time for TMW-4 to reach 0.2% creep strain at 725
◦C/630 MPa is
