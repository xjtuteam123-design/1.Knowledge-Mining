Statistical treatment of fracture toughness data of Ti-A1-Si-Nb alloys 
B. Wittkowsky, M. Pfuff, B. Dogan, K.-H. Schwalbe and R. Wagner 
GKSS Research Center, D-21502 Geesthacht (Germany) 
(Received August 5, 1993; in revised form December 6, 1993) 
Abstract 
A statistical analysis was applied to the fracture toughness data of brittle hypoeutectic and eutectic Ti-A1-Si-Nb alloys 
consisting of the two intermetallic phases (Ti,Nb)a(AI,Si) and (Ti,Nb)s(Si,AI)3 with differing volume fractions of the 
silicide phase. Model calculations were performed using a simple damage mechanical approach that relates the statistical 
properties of toughness data to a statistical distribution of local strengths which accounts for microstructural features of 
the alloys. The good correlation observed between the experimental findings and the results of the numerical simulations 
do not support the "weakest link" model as the underlying failure mechanism of brittle Ti-AI-Si-Nb alloys. 
1. Introduction 
Recently it has been shown that alloying of more 
than approximately 3 at.% Si to the intermetallic c~ 2- 
TiA1 phase leads to a two-phase microstructure com- 
prising the two intermetallic phases a2-Tia(Al, Si) and 
~-Tis(Si, AI)3 [1, 2]. Comprehensive microstructural 
analyses of several alloys with different compositions 
revealed the existence of a eutectic line separating 
hypoeutectic alloys from hypereutectic alloys [3]. In 
hypoeutectic and hypereutectic alloys primary solidi- 
fied a2-Ti3(Al, Si) dendrites and ~-Tis(Si, AI)3 needles 
respectively are dispersed in the eutectic (a2+~) 
matrix. The addition of about 10 at.% Nb to either 
alloy did not essentially change the basic solidification 
reaction of the corresponding ternary Ti-Si-AI alloys 
(termed TSA alloys) [1, 2]. Hence, the solidification 
microstructure was not modified by the addition of Nb. 
In contrast, the addition of Nb improved drastically the 
strength, rupture strength and oxidation resistance, 
rendering the Nb- modified dual-phase TSA alloys 
(termed TSA(Nb) alloys) as potential candidate struc- 
tural materials for applications at service temperatures 
considerably higher than for the Nb-modified a2-Ti3A1 
alloys [3, 4]. 
As Nb serves as a fl-stabilizing element of the TiaA1 
phase [5], the rate and degree of the fl-* a 2 transforma- 
tion on cooling depends strongly on the given heat 
treatment and the chosen cooling rate. Hence, in an 
analogous manner to the Nb-modified Ti3A1 [6], the 
microstructure of the (Ti, Nb)3(AI , Si) phase of the 
eutectic composite in the TSA(Nb) alloys can also be 
designed to cover a large number of possible struc- 
tures. However, unlike for the Nb-modified a-Ti3A1 
alloys, no heat treatment could be established which 
led to tensile elongations significantly beyond 0.2%. 
Apart from the observed brittleness, the fracture 
toughness data showed a scatter which depended on 
the silicon concentration of the alloy. Both these effects 
ought to be attributed to the presence of the brittle ~- 
(Ti, Nb)5(Si, AI) 3 silicide phase, the interface of which 
with the a2- fl (B2)-(Ti , Nb)3(A1, Si) phase is highly 
incoherent. Hence, the resulting weak bonding across 
the ¢-(Ti, Nb)3(AI , Si) interphase boundaries might be 
regarded as "microcracks" or as "weak links", the 
dimensions of which depend on the size distribution of 
the ~-silicide particles. The latter as well as their 
volume fractions can be controlled via the silicon con- 
centration of the particular TSA(Nb) alloy. It was the 
objective of the present study to apply statistical 
methods to an analysis of experimental fracture tough- 
ness data and their scatter in order to obtain a better 
understanding of the failure mechanisms of brittle 
hypoeutectic and eutectic TSA(Nb) alloys with differ- 
ent volume fractions of the ~-silicide phase. 
Model calculations were performed using the con- 
cept of a "classical bundle" [7] in order to explain the 
experimental findings. The results confirm that the 
fracture of TSA(Nb) alloys proceeds via an accumula- 
tion of damage, and is not as commonly assumed 
induced by the failure of a "weakest link".

described by a "weakest link" concept. In fact, the 
shape of the load-displacement curves (Fig. 2), which

maximum load, contradicts the assumption of a 
"weakest link" inducing the failure of these alloys. At 
ambient temperature the fracture mode of the alloys is 
brittle. This implies that it is not plastic deformation 
which leads to the observed load-displacement curves. 
Figure 6 shows a REM picture of the area in front of 
the notch after the specimen has been exposed to a 
load which has not yet lead to macroscopic fracture. A 
stable crack has developed with a large zone of local 
damage along the silicides and the silicide interfaces 
with the matrix in front of the crack tip. This behaviour 
suggests a concept of cumulative damage rather than a 
"weakest link" concept as the underlying mechanism 
which eventually leads to macroscopic failure. A crack 
which starts from a weak link does not necessarily 
propagate if the weak volume element is surrounded by 
elements of much higher local strength. The stress 
carried by the weak element can, after failure of this 
element, be carried by the surrounding elements. 
A simple model of the fracture process which is 
based on a mechanism of cumulative damage is given 
by the "classical bundle" which is used to replace the

of a large number of parallel elements of identical 
length and cross-section, with a statistical distribution 
of local element strength. Each element remains com- 
pletely elastic until it ruptures once the tensile force in 
the particular element has reached its rupture strength. 
An increment of the internal stress causes rupture of a 
certain fraction of elements and redistribution of forces 
among the elements which have not yet ruptured at the 
given stress. The relation between stress a and strain e 
of the model specimen is then given by [11]

where E is the elastic modulus and a~ characterizes the 
actual damage of the specimen, defined as the ratio (n/

original number of elements. The assumption of con-

fulfilled in front of a notch or a crack. Despite this sim-

simulates the observed accumulation of damage which

on the distribution of rupture strength of the individual 
elements. In order to apply the bundle model to the 
tested TSA(Nb) alloys, the volume elements containing 
silicides or their interfaces with the matrix have to be

amount of damage leading to macroscopic fracture and 
the skewness of the distribution of the macroscopic 
fracture toughness data, an ensemble of 100 bundle 
model specimens was considered. Each specimen con- 
sisted of 100 elements, the strength of which was ran- 
domly chosen according to the distribution function 
given in eqn. (6). For this ensemble the distribution of 
critical stresses corresponding to a given degree q of 
damage, i.e. percentage of broken elements, was calcu- 
lated. In Table 4 the skewness of the simulated stress 
distributions is compared with the skewness of data 
which would be obtained if the critical stress cor- 
responded to the strength of the weakest element, ie. if 
the Weibull assumption were valid. 
The model calculations clearly demonstrate that the 
absolute value of skewness decreases with increasing 
damage at the critical stress. Obviously, the calculated 
skewness agrees quite well with the experimental 
values (eft. Table 2, Fig. 3), if one assumes a certain 
degree of damage prior to macroscopic failure. The 
result gives further support to a "damage mechanical" 
view of the fracture mechanism of the alloys tested, and 
rules out the "weakest link" concept which is the basis 
of the Weibull distribution.

4. Summary 
The results of the present study do not support 
the "weakest link" model as the underlying failure 
mechanism of brittle hypoeutectic and eutectic 
TSA(Nb) alloys consisting of a2-(Ti,Nb)3(A1,Si) and 
¢-(Ti,Nb)5(Si,A1)3. Rather, a damage mechanical 
approach has been successfully applied to understand 
the fracture behaviour of these alloys at ambient tem- 
perature. The results of model calculations on the basis 
of the "classical bundle" model appear to correlate 
reasonably well with the results of the statistical 
analysis of experimental toughness data. Using a statis- 
tical distribution of local strengths which explicitly 
accounts for the two phases the alloys consist of, the 
observed dependence of the toughness data and their 
scatter on the silicon concentration is well reproduced 
by the numerical simulations.
