ABSTRACT
Signiﬁcant improvement of high temperature strength of 2219 alloy was achieved by addition of equal atom
percentages (at%) of Sc and Zr and using a novel three-stage heat treatment route. Processing of the alloy
involved casting in a water-cooled copper mould with a cooling rate in the range of 10 2 to 10 3 K/s. This was
followed by direct ageing of the cast alloy at 375 ℃ to form thermally stable L1 2 type coherent Al 3(Sc, Zr)
precipitates in the matrix. Subsequently, the alloy was solutionized at 535 ℃ and further aged at 200 ℃ which
caused the formation of highly dense θ′′ (Al3Cu) and θ′ (Al2Cu) precipitates. From microstructural character-
ization it was found that the high number density of these precipitates is due to heterogeneous nucleation on pre-
existing Al3(Sc, Zr) precipitates. Presence of Al 3(Sc, Zr) precipitates together with high number density of θ′′ and
θ′ precipitates results in remarkably high 0.2% proof stress of 456 MPa at room temperature, 295 MPa at 200 °C
and 227 MPa at 250 °C along with 5% ductility. It was found that Al 3(Sc, Zr) precipitates also suppress the
coarsening of θ′′ and θ′ precipitates thus making them thermally stable at high temperature.
1. Introduction
2xxx series Al alloys are widely used in aviation industry for
structural applications. Among them, 2219 Al alloy is one of the most
important alloy for high temperature usage up to 150 ℃. It has rela-
tively high tensile strength at room temperature, good creep strength at
elevated temperature, high toughness at cryogenic temperature and
good weldability. However, this alloy loses its strength rapidly above
150 °C due to rapid coarsening of the strengthening precipitates [1].
From the basic understanding of the theory of precipitation hard-
ening and coarsening, it is evident that the alloying elements, which
have low di ﬀusivity and limited solid solubility in Al, are required to
develop a high strength, thermally stable Al alloy. Thermally stable
dispersoid or precipitate formation during thermo-mechanical proces-
sing is usually achieved by the addition of small quantities of transition
metals to Al. Knipling et. al. have reported that most of the transition
metals form stable or metastable trialuminide, which have low di ﬀu-
sivity and limited solubility [2]. Thus, the transition metals are one of
the most promising elements for obtaining high strength and low
coarsening at high temperature.
Addition of Sc in Al and Al alloys has been extensively employed in
recent years to improve their high temperature properties due to its low
diﬀusivity and limited solid solubility. Presence of Sc in Al alloys results
in the formation of stable, coherent, L1
2 ordered Al 3Sc precipitates in
the matrix, which remain coherent even at larger sizes due to the low
lattice mismatch between Al and Al
3Sc [3–5]. The precipitation of Al 3Sc
occurs during ageing in the temperature range from 250 °C to 450 °C
[5]. The maximum strength improvement is achieved after 24 h of
ageing at 250 °C. The precipitates exhibit slow coarsening rate up to
350 °C due to low lattice mismatch between Al and Al
3Sc [6]. Moreover,
Al3Sc precipitates prevent recrystallization through Zener drag me-
chanism [7].
The presence of Zr enhances the e ﬀect of addition of Sc by in-
creasing the volume fraction of Al 3Sc precipitates and decreasing the
precipitate radii. This results in strength improvement of the alloy [8].
It has been observed that the presence of Zr in Al-Sc alloys helps in the
formation of Al 3(Sc1−x,Zrx) precipitates, which are more coarsening
resistant and have higher recrystallization resistance than Al 3Sc
[5,8–10]. The precipitates may have a core-shell structure where the
core is enriched in Sc and the shell is enriched with Zr [11]. Fuller et al.
have reported that Al(Sc,Zr) alloys do not reach a global equilibrium
within reasonable time periods, when aged between 300 °C and 375 °C
[8].
It has been observed that the presence of Sc and Zr in 2618 Al (Al-
Cu-Mg) alloy helps in the formation of ﬁner S′ precipitates, which leads
to a signi ﬁcant increase in the strength of the alloy. Presence of
Al3(Sc,Zr) precipitates produces a coherent strain ﬁeld in the matrix
promoting the nucleation of S′ precipitates resulting in a more

of Sc and Zr designated as 2219ScZr. The precipitate evolution during
heat treatment occurs ﬁrst with the formation of L1

is performed to correlate the grain size, morphology and the pre-
cipitation behaviour with corresponding tensile properties at room and
elevated temperatures. Theoretical estimate of strength due to the
presence of these precipitates has been carried out and compared with
experimental results in order to get an insight into the governing me-
chanisms for the improvement of strength.
2. Experimental procedure
The 2219ScZr alloy was produced by the addition of equal atom
percent (0.15 at%) of Sc and Zr to the commercial 2219 alloy (Al-6.5Cu-
0.32Mn-0.13Zr-0.06V-0.03Ti-0.05Si-0.13Fe (in wt%)) during the
melting process. The resultant composition is given in Table 1. Melting
was carried out in a vacuum arc melting furnace under argon atmo-
sphere. The process of melting and solidi ﬁcation was repeated several
times to ensure proper mixing and achieving compositional homo-
geneity. Subsequently, the alloy was suction cast in a water-cooled
copper mould in the form of plates of dimension of
3 mm*10 mm*70 mm. A higher cooling rate between 10
2 to 10 3 K/s
could be achieved during the suction casting [20].
A novel heat treatment process was applied to the cast alloy. The
cast strips were subjected to direct ageing at 375 °C to allow the pre-
cipitation of Al
3Sc, Al 3Zr or Al 3(Sc,Zr) phase. Following this, the sam-
ples were solution treated at 535 °C for 30 min in order to increase the
Cu concentration in the solid solution without signi ﬁcantly aﬀecting the
already formed Al
3Sc, Al 3Zr or Al 3(Sc,Zr) precipitates. The solution-
treated samples were ﬁnally subjected to ageing at 200 °C to obtain θ′ or
θ″ precipitates in the matrix [19].
At each processing step, the hardness of the alloy was measured
using a Zwick Roell ZHV1-A Vickers micro-hardness tester. For ob-
taining each hardness value, ten measurements were made and the
average value along with standard deviation is reported. The tensile
properties of the alloy after the ﬁnal stage of ageing treatment were
determined at room temperature as well as at 200 °C and 250 °C using
an Instron 5967 machine.
Microstructural examination was carried out using a FEI ESEM
Quanta 200 scanning electron microscope (SEM), JEOL JXA-8530F
electron probe micro analyser (EPMA), JEOL 2000FX II, FEI-F30 and
FEI-TITAN transmission electron microscopes (TEM) ﬁtted with energy
dispersive X-ray spectroscope (EDS). Grain size was calculated from
EBSD images by using TSL OIM Data collection-6 software on.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Solidi ﬁcation
Fig. 1(a and b) show an optical micrograph and an EBSD image of
the suction-cast 2219ScZr alloy. The cast alloy exhibits a Vicker's
hardness of 953 ± 44 MPa. The optical micrograph exhibits presence
of ﬁne equiaxed grains. The average grain size is measured from EBSD
micrograph and is found to be 13 ± 8 µm. Fig. 1(c) shows a re-
presentative SEM micrograph while a HRTEM image of the cast alloy is
shown in Fig. 1(d). The SEM micrograph reveals a discontinuous net-
work of the grain boundary phases. It is reported in the literature that
primary precipitation of Al
3Sc can occur in dilute Al-Sc systems above a
critical solute concentration [2,21]. For Al–Sc–Zr alloys, 0.2 at% of total
solute content of Sc and Zr exceeds this critical concentration under
conventional casting conditions [22]. Though the solute concentration
in the present investigation is more than 0.2 at%, only a very few pri-
mary Al3Sc precipitates in the micrometre size range could be observed
in the SEM micrographs after casting. Additionally, a few nanometre-
sized, faceted L1
2 Al3Sc precipitates were observed during TEM ex-
amination. The HRTEM image taken along the [001] Al zone axis
(Fig. 1 (d)) shows one such precipitate. The corresponding FFT in the
inset shows super-lattice spots that correspond to a L1 2 type precipitate.
The ordered L1 2 structure indicates that this precipitate might be Al 3Sc
or Al 3(Sc,Zr). From the SEM and TEM examination it can be surmised
that the presence of Al 3Sc or Al 3(Sc,Zr) precipitates inside the grains is
rare. Thus, the role of Al 3Sc or Al 3(Sc,Zr) precipitates in re ﬁning the
grain size is minor, the major role being played by the high cooling rate
provided by suction casting in water cooled copper moulds.
The nature of distribution of Sc, Zr and Cu in the suction-cast
2219ScZr alloy is revealed by EPMA shown in Fig. 2. The Cu con-
centration in the matrix was found to be 1.4 wt% while the composition
of the grain boundary phase corresponded to intermetallic Al
2Cu ( θ)
phase. Our results indicate occasional segregation of Sc at some of the
places in the grain boundary. In general, Zr is depleted in the grain
boundary phase and is enriched in the matrix with a homogeneous
distribution. We note that the absence of the detrimental brittle ‘W′
phase (ScCu
7–4Al5–8) in the cast alloy due to the relatively higher
cooling rate encountered in the suction casting process [23].
3.2. Ageing at 375 °C
Owing to higher cooling rates encountered in suction casting, the
concentration of Sc and Zr in the matrix is higher than that obtained in
conventional casting and hence precipitation occurs during the ﬁrst
stage of ageing. Fuller et. al. [8] have reported that ageing of Al-Sc-Zr
alloys at 375 °C yields faster kinetics of precipitation. Hence, we have
carried out ﬁrst stage of ageing at 375 °C. The variation of Vickers
hardness with ageing time at this temperature is shown in Fig. 3. The
hardness increases continuously up to 40 h with a peak hardness of
1270 ± 26 MPa following which it slightly decreases.

3.3. Solution treatment at 535 °C
Following the ageing of the as cast alloy, Solution treatment is
carried out at 535 °C for 30 min in order to dissolve the copper that was
segregated during casting to form copper rich solid solution. However,
the heat treatment is tuned to ensure that the Al
3(Sc,Zr) precipitates
formed after ﬁrst stage of ageing do not get coarsened signi ﬁcantly. The
observed increase in the Cu concentration in the matrix was found to be
from 2.5 to 4.5 wt% that is well below the solid solubility limit of
copper in aluminium (5.7 wt%). The complete dissolution of the copper
containing θ phase at grain boundaries could not be achieved due to the
presence of Sc in some of this phase. As di ﬀusivity of Sc is signi ﬁcantly
lower in Al (D 0 is 5.31 × 10 −4 m2/s) [28], the presence of Sc acts as a
retardant to Cu di ﬀusion in the matrix and hence, some amount of θ
phase remains undissolved. The average size of Al 3(Sc,Zr) precipitates
after solution treatment increases to 2.4 ± 1.6 nm (radius) and pre-
cipitate number density per unit volume decreases to 3.53 × 10

precipitate θ″ or θ″ [1]. Since, in the present study, thermal stability of
the alloy has been investigated at 200 ℃. Therefore, the second stage

second stage ageing of the 2219ScZr alloy, the variation of Vickers
hardness with ageing time is shown in Fig. 6. The alloy reaches a peak
hardness value of 1425 ± 29 MPa at 10 h after which it decreases to a
value of 1367 ± 18 MPa after 20 h. The change in hardness on further
ageing is very sluggish.

3.5. Tensile properties of the alloy
Tensile properties of the peak aged 2219ScZr alloy at room tem-
perature, 200 °C and 250 °C are presented and compared with standard
tensile test data for 2219-T851 wrought alloy in Table 3. It is observed
that the strength of the 2219ScZr alloy is signi ﬁcantly higher than
2219T851 alloy at all the temperatures. In addition, the alloy exhibits

Thus, 2219ScZr alloy exhibits excellent strength at 200 °C and 250 °C as

3.6. Strength calculation
The theoretical yield strength for 2219ScZr alloy is calculated based
on its microstructure and compared with the experimentally obtained
value. The contribution to the room temperature strength of the peak
aged 2219ScZr alloy comes from grain boundary and the precipitates
Al3(Sc,Zr), θ′′ and θ′.
The grain boundary strengthening can be calculated from the well-
known Hall-Petch relationship [37]. It has been experimentally ob-
served that Al3(Sc,Zr) precipitates with radius equal to or less than 2 nm
are sheared and greater than 2 nm are bypassed by Orowan mechanism
[8]. Since average radius of Al 3(Sc,Zr) precipitates in the peak aged
condition is 2.4 nm in the present investigation, we consider them to be
bypassed.

length of the plates in the images is considered the diameter of the
plates assuming that all the plates are completely present in the images.

average length of all three variants. It is established in the literature

4. Conclusions
The possibility of developing a suction cast high strength 2219 alloy
with minor amount of addition of Sc and Zr through a novel two stage
ageing with intermediate solutionising treatment is reported.
Nanometre-sized, spherical, coherent, Al

structure at 375 °C. Following this, copper segregates in the micro-

precipitates. This is achieved by holding at the sample at 530 °C for
30 min. The ordered precipitates from ﬁrst stage ageing have a core-
shell structure with a Zr rich shell and Sc rich core. Subsequently,
during the second stage of ageing at 200 °C, θ′′ and θ′ precipitates with
