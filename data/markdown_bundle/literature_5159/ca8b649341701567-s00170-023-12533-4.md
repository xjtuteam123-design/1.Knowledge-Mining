Abstract
The exceptional mechanical properties of titanium alloy and nickel-based high-temperature alloy make them extensively 
utilized in aero-engine blades. The machinability of these materials is poor due to their high material strength and low ther-
mal conductivity which lead to technical difficulties such as cutting heat accumulation, tool wear, and poor surface quality 
during the machining process. This study commences with an investigation into the material properties and processing 
mechanisms of titanium alloy and nickel-based high-temperature alloy, with a particular focus on recent advancements in 
cutting dynamics, chip formation mechanisms, tool wear, and surface integrity. The important effect of reasonable setting 
of cutting parameters and machining conditions on the machining integrity is discussed. The future trend for high surface 
integrity machining of titanium alloy and nickel-based high-temperature alloy blade is finally predicted.
Keywords Machinability · Titanium alloy · Nickel-based high-temperature alloy · Cutting mechanism · Surface integrity
1 Introduction
With the continuous advancement and implementation of 
advanced technology in the aviation industry, the demand 
for high-performance aviation engines is steadily increas-
ing. As the primary component of aircraft, aviation engines 
frequently operate in extremely harsh environments char -
acterized by high temperatures and pressures, necessitating 
strict material performance requirements for their compo-
nents. Due to their exceptional mechanical properties, tita-
nium alloy and high-temperature alloy have found extensive 
application in the aviation power industry. As depicted in 
Fig. 1, turbofan engine blades are typically manufactured by 
materials such as titanium alloy and high-temperature alloy.
Titanium alloy and high-temperature alloy have excellent 
mechanical properties. However, their high chemical activ-
ity, low elastic modulus, poor thermal conductivity, and high 
temperature strength often result in tool wear and poor sur -
face quality. Therefore, they are typical difficult-to-machine 
materials. Due to the high requirements for machining 
quality and efficiency in blade production, achieving high 
machined surface quality has become a major research focus 
[1]. To address this issue, it is imperative to investigate the 
cutting and surface integrity of these materials, as well as 
thoroughly investigate the tool wear, cutting dynamics, chip 
formation, and surface integrity mechanism of blade during 
the blade machining process.
This paper provides a comprehensive review of recent 
research on the machining process of titanium alloy and 
high-temperature alloy, with a particular focus on the influ-
ence of material properties, cutting parameters, and process-
ing conditions on blade surface integrity.

engine blades due to its exceptional strength-to-weight ratio, 
high temperature resistance, and superior corrosion resistance.

3 Machinability
The machinability of a material refers to the level of 
difficulty in removing material under specific machin-
ing conditions. Currently, the assessment of material 
machinability primarily relies on measurements of tool 
life, monitoring of cutting forces, and observation of chip 
morphology and analysis of surface roughness [ 4]. Tita -
nium alloy and nickel-based high-temperature alloy are 
difficult-to-machine materials due to their poor machina-
bility, which causes high cutting forces, heat build-up, tool 
wear, and poor surface quality.
3.1 Machining cutter
Optimizing tool materials and coatings can improve the 
performance of tools in machine titanium alloy. Tool 
wear uses to evaluate tool performance, as tool wear can 
decrease the machining surface quality and efficiency by 
increasing cutting forces and temperatures.
Commonly used tool materials for machining titanium 
alloy include cemented carbide, cubic boron nitride 
(CBN), and polycrystalline diamond (PCD). Among 
these, cemented carbide is the most widely utilized. To 
further reduce tool wear and improve tool life, coatings 
such as TiN, TiCN, TiAlN, and Al2O3 are also com-
monly used [9 ]. In fact, the proclivity to undergo chemi-
cal reactions with most tool materials is a significant 
contributing factor to the suboptimal machinability of 
titanium alloy.
The high temperature and pressure generated during 
the cutting of titanium alloy with carbide tools promote 
a chemical reaction between the two materials, resulting 
in tool wear mechanisms such as bonded tool wear, diffu-
sion wear, and chemical wear [10]. Tool wear is a result 
of multifactorial coupling, and changes in tool geometry, 
cutting parameters, and machining conditions can signifi -
cantly affect tool wear [11, 12].
The tribological performance of the tool and mate-
rial interface is primarily influenced by the cutting tem-
perature and stress distribution resulting from various 
machining parameters, which are key factors contribut -
ing to carbide tool failure. Liang et al. [ 13] observed 
that the high temperature friction behavior of Ti-6Al-4 V 
workpieces and WC-6Co tools changed with increasing 
temperature, resulting in a transition from mechanical 
wear to coupled mechanical-chemical wear at 920 °C. To 
minimize carbide tool wear when cutting titanium alloy, 
it is recommended to use a low cutting speed during CNC 
machining process [14].
Compared to the carbide, CBN exhibits superior 
strength, hardness, thermal shock resistance, and thermal 
conductivity. As a result, CBN tools can withstand the heat 
and pressure of cutting processes while maintaining sharp 
edges for extended periods without compromising surface 
quality. The chemical reaction between CBN tools and 
titanium alloy is stronger than that of carbide tools [15],

Commonly used tool materials for machining nickel-
based high-temperature alloy include carbides, cubic 
boron nitride, ceramics, and other advanced materials. 
Common coating materials utilized in this process include 
TiN, TiC, TiCN, TiAlN, and Al2O3. Due to their high 
mechanical strength and hardness at high temperatures, 
nickel-based alloys experience significant tool wear during 
cutting process. The wear mechanism, its various types, 
and corresponding results are depicted in Fig.  3.
It was discovered that tool wear in cutting nickel-based 
high-temperature alloy is caused by a combination of vari-
ous wear mechanisms, including bonded, abrasive, and 
diffusion wear [22]. Frequent bonding between the blade 
material and tool results in bonded wear, while the pres-
ence of hard compounds in the blade material leads to 
abrasive wear. Additionally, diffusion wear occurs due to 
element diffusion at high cutting temperatures between the 
tool and blade materials.
Different machining conditions result in various wear 
mechanisms, but bonded wear remains the primary cause 
of tool failure. Bonded wear is frequently the primary 
cause of tool wear, which subsequently results in other 
forms of tool wear. At moderate cutting speeds, bonded 
wear plays a major role. At high cutting speeds, bonded 
wear is the main wear mechanism causing diffuse and 
chemical wear in nickel-based alloy machining. There-
fore, limiting bonded wear is crucial for improving tool 
performance and life span.
Cemented carbide is a popular choice for cutting nickel-
based high-temperature alloy due to its high strength and 
hardness. Common wear patterns of cemented carbide tools 
include rear face, tip, and crescent pits wear, primarily 
caused by bonded and abrasive wear mechanisms. The cut-
ting speed is the main factor affecting tool wear as it gener-
ates a large amount of heat concentrated in the tip of the 
tool due to poor thermal conductivity of carbide tools and 
nickel-based alloy workpieces. To improve the performance 
of carbide tools, coatings are applied to the tool surface.
According to a study [23], uncoated carbide tools dem-

better results in the range of 40–90 m/min. Different coatings

enhance the machinability of nickel-based alloys [33, 34].

whiskers and found that the fracture toughness and hardness 
of the tool material were significantly improved as the SiC 
whisker content increased to 30%.
3.2 Cutting force
Cutting force is a crucial factor in the machining process, 
affecting tool wear and machining surface quality. The cut-
ting force is essential for efficient and precise machining of 
titanium alloy and nickel-based high-temperature alloy. Dur-
ing CNC machining process, titanium alloy and nickel-based 
high-temperature alloy typically exhibit high cutting forces. 
The variation in peak cutting forces is caused by the cutting 
parameters and tool parameters [36– 38]. The variation in 
peak cutting forces is due to both mechanical and thermal 
loads. Different machining parameters cause thermal effects, 
which are a significant factor in the variation of peak cutting 
forces during a single cut period. Meanwhile, tool wear is 
the reason for the gradual increase in peak average cutting 
force during continuous cutting [39]. Cutting speed signifi-
cantly affects cutting forces in a single cut period. In Ti-
6Al-4 V turning, the relationship between cutting speed and 
cutting forces is nonlinear, and the increasing of the cutting 
speed will increase the cutting force [40].
Machining nickel-based high-temperature alloy shows a 
different pattern. High cutting speed leads to high cutting 
temperature, which softens the nickel-based high-tempera-
ture alloy and reduces surface hardness of the workpiece. As 
a result, cutting force decreases with the increase of cutting 
speed (Fig. 4(a)) [41]. During continuous cutting, the wear 
of the back tool face results in increased friction at the tool-
workpiece interface, thereby leading to an increase in cutting 
force [42, 43], as depicted in Fig. 4(b).

3.3 Chip formation
When cutting titanium alloy and nickel-based high-tem-
perature alloy, the elevated temperature in the cutting zone 
induces shear instability of the workpiece material, resulting 
in formation of sawtooth chips. The typical sawtooth chips 
for titanium alloy are illustrated in Fig. 5.
The chips of nickel-based high-temperature alloy are prone 
to accumulation near the tool and workpiece, leading to the 
reduce of tool life and the decrease of machined surface qual-
ity. Therefore, investigating the chip formation mechanism of 
titanium alloy and nickel-based high-temperature alloy can 
enhance machining efficiency and improve surface quality.
The mechanism of sawtooth chip formation is usually 
explained by both periodic fracture theory and adiabatic 
shear theory [49, 50]. The periodic fracture theory indi-
cates that the thermoplastic instability is caused by the 
cyclic expansion of surface cracks without chips. Mean -
while, according to the adiabatic shear theory, the thermal 
softening effect results from the fact that adiabatic tem-
perature rise outweighs plastic strain hardening-induced 
shear slip. Based on this theory, experiments and numeri-
cal simulations have been conducted to investigate the chip 
formation mechanism during cutting, exploring the effects 
of cutting parameters, material properties, cooling, and 
lubrication conditions on the sawtooth chip formation.
Wanger et al. [51] investigated the relationship between 
cutting speed and chip formation in dry cutting Ti-5553; 
the result has shown that the chips were always serrated 
chips in the cutting speed range of 35–65 m/min, and the 
increase in cutting speed resulted in a slight decrease in 
chip thickness in terms of chip geometry. Aramcharoen [52] 
investigated the impact of low-temperature cooling methods 
on chip formation during Ti-6Al-4 V turning, resulting in 
a significantly distinct chip morphology compared to that 
achieved with oil-based cooling. Spiral chips are generated 
through low-temperature machining, whereas oil-based cool-
ants result in entangled chips during the machining process.
The application of low-temperature cooling improves the 
efficiency of cooling and mitigates the friction between the 
tool-chip interface, which has an impact on chip formation. 
Furthermore, the spiral-shaped chips are more conducive to 
material removal and augment process stability.
Numerical simulation is a significant approach to investi-
gate the chip formation mechanism. However, there are limited 
models that possess sufficient capability to predict the process 
of chip formation accurately. Therefore, optimizing failure and 
material intrinsic models is crucial for studying the chip forma-
tion process and morphology prediction in cutting simulations.
Chen et al. [53] developed a Johnson-Cook model for 
Ti-6Al-4V high-speed machining, which incorporates a 
ductile damage criterion based on strain energy. The pre-
dicted chip morphology and cutting forces were consistent 
with experimental results, demonstrating the feasibility of 
this approach. Calamaz et al. [54] utilized a novel material 
instantiation model within a two-dimensional finite element 
analysis to investigate the chip formation process during tita-
nium alloy machining.
The study assumes that serrated chips are caused by the 
adiabatic shear zone only. The model considers strain rate 
and temperature effects on flow stress, introduces strain 
softening effect, and predicts chip morphology and cutting 
force, which is consistent with the experimental result.

4 Surface integrity
4.1 Surface micromorphology and surface 
roughness
Surface integrity is a critical factor in evaluating the quality 
of workpiece machining, encompassing surface morphology, 
microstructure, and residual stresses. Among these factors, 
surface roughness serves as the primary quantitative indi-
cator for assessing surface integrity and can significantly 
affect the friction coefficient, corrosion resistance, and 
fatigue strength of the workpiece [55]. The aim of current 
research is to minimize workpiece surface roughness by 
optimizing machining processes. During the milling process, 
surface roughness is influenced by various factors such as 
tool parameters, cutting parameters, cooling and lubrication 
conditions, and machining system stability. These factors 
affect the mechanical and thermal cycles as well as material 
microstructure transformation.
Ramana et al. [56] optimized cutting parameters for 
dry turning Ti-6Al-4 V to maximize surface roughness. 
An increase in feed rate leads to a higher surface rough-
ness value, whereas higher cutting speed and depth of cut 
result in lower surface roughness values [57]. The high 
feed rate elevates the frictional force at the tool-workpiece 
interface, leading to an increase in cutting temperature and 
promoting chip adhesion, ultimately resulting in a degra-
dation of surface quality. Thakur et al. [41] conducted dry 
turning experiments on Inconel 718 with varying cutting 
speeds and feed rates, while maintaining a constant depth 
of cut at 0.5 mm. The results indicated that the optimal 
surface roughness was achieved at moderate feed rates 
ranging from 45 to 55 m/min, whereas surface quality 
significantly deteriorated when cutting speeds exceeded 
55 m/min.
The increase in cutting speed leads to a significant eleva-
tion of temperature at the tool’s cutting edge, subsequently 
resulting in reduced tool strength and plastic deformation. 
Therefore, the degree of tool wear and cutting-edge defor -
mation increases proportionally with cutting speed. Hence, 
enhancing productivity by increasing feed and cutting speed 
is not conducive to achieving superior surface quality.
In addition to optimizing cutting parameters and tool 
geometry, various cooling and lubrication systems are used 
to minimize surface roughness during machining process. 
These include microlubrication, solid lubrication, low-
temperature cooling, and high-pressure cooling [27, 58, 
59]. An effective cooling and lubrication system can sig-
nificantly reduce the temperature at both the tool-workpiece 
and tool-chip interface, thereby minimizing tool wear and 
improving surface quality of workpieces, productivity, and 
sustainability. Busch et al. [59] investigated the impact of 
various cooling methods on high-temperature alloy cut-
ting and concluded that high-pressure cooling can achieve 
excellent chip control, stable machining process, and longer 
tool life, thereby resulting in superior surface quality. Mia 
et al. [60] found that the application of high-pressure cool-
ing reduced material adhesion, chip formation, and friction, 
ultimately leading to a smoother surface finish during Ti-
6Al-4 V machining process.
4.2 Surface hardening and residual stresses
Surface hardening and residual stresses serve as the impor -
tant quantitative indicator for assessing surface integrity.
Zhang Dinghua et al. [61– 63] conducted machining 
experiment studies on GH4169 high-temperature alloy and 
titanium alloy, respectively (see Fig.  6), and investigated 
the formation laws as well as methods for enhancing the 
surface integrity of aerospace complex structural parts. Li 
Xun et al. [64] conducted an experimental study on the sur-
face integrity of milled titanium alloy TC17, and the results 
showed that the thickness of the plastic deformation layer 
on the milled surface related to the process parameters such 
as spindle speed, feed rate, and depth of cut. Xinmin Feng 
et al. [65] investigated the surface integrity of nickel-based 
high- temperature alloy during machining process, including 
surface roughness, work hardening, white layer, and residual 
stresses. Liu Zhanqiang et al. [66] conducted surface integ-
rity studies on high-temperature alloy and found that Inconel 
718 had a higher work hardening rate and depth work hard-
ening layer than FGH95 under the same machining condi-
tion, indicating different work hardening phenomena among 
different high-temperature alloy.
4.3 Surface modification after machining
Post-cutting surface treatments, such as shot blasting and 
laser impact treatment, have been shown to effectively 
enhance the surface properties of materials. D. A. Axinte 
et al. [67, 68] investigated the formation and influence laws 
of surface integrity during abrasive belt grinding and pol-
ishing of titanium alloy workpiece. The results indicate 
that there were no significant microstructural alterations 
observed on the surface and subsurface following abrasive 
belt polishing. J. Rech et al. [69] developed a residual stress 
distribution model to analyze the influence of abrasive belt 
polishing on surface integrity.
Tam H. et al. [70] investigated the effect of polishing pro-
cess parameters on material removal rate and surface integ-
rity of single crystal materials and revealed the mechanism 
of surface integrity formation under different tool trajecto-
ries, and the experimental results showed that the tool trajec-
tory has a significant effect on surface roughness.

rolling strengthening process (URSP) to improve the surface 
micromorphology and residual stress of the blade [95], and the 
mechanism of titanium alloy blade surface grain refinement is 
revealed based on the SEM, TEM, and EBSD experiment [96].

These process methods serve as a technical guide for 
enhancing blade surfaces and improving surface integrity.
5 Conclusion and future prospect
Titanium alloy and nickel-based high-temperature alloy are 
widely used for blades. However, achieving high-quality 
machining is still a challenging research area due to cutting 
heat, tool wear, and poor surface quality. This paper provides a 
comprehensive review of the impact of material properties, cut-
ting parameters, and machining conditions on the surface integ-
rity of titanium alloy and nickel-based high-temperature alloy 
blade. The specific conclusions are summarized as follows:
(1) The utilization of new tool materials and coating tech-
nologies has significantly enhanced the machining effi-

and ceramic tools exhibit superior performance com-

(2) The impact of cutting parameters on machinability 
characteristics, such as tool wear, cutting forces, and 
surface integrity, has been extensively investigated. 
Variations in cutting parameters have an impact on the 
distribution of cutting temperature and magnitude of 
cutting force, which subsequently influence surface 
quality. The optimal cutting parameters for different 
machining conditions are obtained by experimental 
methods and numerical simulation methods.
(3) Nickel-based high-temperature alloy is machined with 
auxiliary machining processes. The auxiliary machin-
ing processes can either augment the energy input or 
alter the material removal mechanism, thereby exhibit-
ing potential for enhancing the machinability of tita-
nium and nickel-based alloys.
(4) The thermal coupling effect during the machining pro-
cess is the primary factor influencing workpiece surface 
integrity. The subsequent processes such as polishing, 
grinding, shot peening, and laser peening are employed 
to enhance surface quality after milling.
Although there have been many achievements in research-
ing the machining mechanism of typical aerospace diffi-
cult-to-machine materials, there are still some challenges 
in achieving high surface integrity during machining, and 
future research should focus on the following areas:
(1) The current research on machining mechanisms for tita-
nium alloy and high-temperature alloy mainly focuses 
on single tool wear and material deformation mecha-
nisms, neglecting the impact of complex geometric fea-
tures on the blade surface integrity. Machining mecha-
nism research based on the process characteristics and 
geometry of blades is imperative for future studies.
(2) The current research on machining mechanisms focuses 
on the material’s macroscopic characteristics and the 
relationship between cutting parameters, machining 
conditions, cutting forces, chip shapes, and surface 
roughness. The machining process causes significant 
microstructure changes and dramatic plastic deforma-
tion of the material, which fundamentally impacts its 
macroscopic properties. Understand the mechanism 
behind microstructure evolution by integrating macro-
