The evolution of microstructure and tensile properties of a newly designed cobalt-free high entropy alloy,

simultaneously. A highly cost-effective high-entropy alloy, doped with a high concentration of aluminum, has 
been developed. 
1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEA) have attracted significant attention due to 
their extensive application potential [ 1 , 2 ]. The discovery of HEAs in -
creases the diversity of alloy compositions and broadens the ways of 
improving mechanical properties [ 3 ]. Among them, the face-centered 
cubic (FCC) HEAs composed of transition elements have been exten -
sively investigated at both room temperature (293 K) and cryogenic 
temperature (77 K) [ 4 , 5 ]. In addition, various strengthening methods 
have been used to improve the yield strength, including solid solution 
hardening, grain size refinement, precipitation hardening, dislocation 
strengthening, and hetero-deformation induced (HDI) strengthening 
[ 6 – 8 ]. 
HDI, combined with precipitation strengthening, can effectively 
enhance the strength of HEAs without compromising their ductility. 
HDI, or back stress hardening, originates from various hierarchical 
structures, including bimodal structures, gradient structures, and het -
erogeneous lamella structures, etc. [ 9 – 11 ]. For example, the bimodal

4.3. Fracture mechanisms 
Despite its heterogeneous structure and high-density secondary 
phases, this material can still improve tensile ductility at cryogenic 
temperatures. The A10 alloy displays more plane slips at 77 K, and 
HDDWs have been found to effectively delay stress concentration [ 60 ]. 
Although the formation of MBs and deformation twins is prevented due 
to the heavy subdivision of the matrix by precipitated particles. 
Furthermore, the A10 HEA exhibits a high strain hardening rate, which 
can delays crack propagation [ 13 , 46 ]. 
The tensile fracture morphologies of the A10 HEA present mixed 
fracture modes, including cleavage planes and dimples, at both 293 K 
and 77 K, as shown in Fig. 9 (a and b). At cryogenic temperatures, sec -
ondary cracks are found mainly in the bulk B2 phase, while at room 
temperature, cracks are located primarily at the phase interface [ 17 ]. 
Bleak B2 phases at 293 K and crazed B2 phases at 77 K indicate the 
analogous phenomenon in the longitudinal section morphologies shown 
in Fig. 9 (a

(1) The primary B2 – NiAl phase can maintain a relatively stable size 
(~4 μ m) and volume fraction with increasing aging time. Sec -
ondary precipitated phases including B2 – NiAl and BCC-Cr exhibit 
an increasing trend in both size and volume fraction before the 
peak aging stage. There is a nano BCC-Cr phase precipitated in 
bulk B2 – NiAl (~20 nm). The particles on the grain boundary 
(~400 nm) are larger than those within the grain (~130 nm) at 
aged 10 h, and the BCC-Cr obviously grows after aging for 24 h. 
(2) The alloy yield strength increases significantly with the aging 
time, from 250 MPa to 860 MPa. The A10 HEA exhibits a yield 
strength of 1000 MPa, a tensile strength of 1455 MPa, and an 
elongation of 9% at cryogenic temperature. The primary 
strengthening mechanisms are precipitation hardening and back 
stress hardening. The increase in yield strength at cryogenic 
temperature is mainly ascribed to the temperature dependence of 
the intrinsic stress. However, the temperature dependence of 
grain size strengthening, precipitation hardening, and hetero- 
deformation induced strengthening cannot be entirely ignored. 
(3) At cryogenic temperatures, the strain-hardening mechanism of 
the A00 sample is dominated by microband-induced plasticity. 
The A10 alloy, which contains high-density precipitates, can no 
longer activate the microband deformation mechanism. Howev -
er, the high strain hardening capability of the material allows it to 
withstand higher stress concentrations at 77 K, and the final 
fracture mechanism differs from that at 293 K. This makes it an 
effective reinforcement strategy in this work.
