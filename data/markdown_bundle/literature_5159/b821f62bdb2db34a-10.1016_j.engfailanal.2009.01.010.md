Low cycle fatigue, high cycle fatigue, fatigue crack propagation and thermo-mechanical
fatigue in Ni-base superalloys are reviewed in terms of fundamental deformation mecha-
nisms, environmental effects, and interactions between environment and deformation
mode. These factors are related to the chemical composition and underlying microstruc-
ture for all currently-used product forms (i.e. powder metallurgy, wrought, conventionally
cast and single crystal). The basic principles that are developed are used to show how both
intrinsic and extrinsic variables can be manipulated to control fatigue behaviour and as a
guide for formulation of engineering life prediction models.

1. Introduction
It is a basic consequence of thermodynamics that the efﬁciency of heat engines, regardless of the type, increases
with increasing temperature. In the power generation industry (nuclear, coal-ﬁred, oil-ﬁred), increases in operating
temperatures translate into reduced fuel consumption, reduced pollution and lower operating costs. In the jet engine
industry, increased operating temperatures lead to improved performance such as combinations of heavier payloads,
greater speed and greater range. As is the case for the power generation industry, lower fuel consumption, reduced
pollution and lower costs are important consequences. However, as operating temperatures are increased, additional
problems to those encountered at lower temperatures arise. Regardless of type, all engines have moving parts which
experience cyclic loading during operation. At higher temperatures such as those found in the power generation and
jet engine industries, not only is there repeated loading, but depending on strain rates and hold times, time-dependent
damage processes such as creep and environmental attack become important. Two major advances in understanding
high temperature fatigue from an engineering perspective were made in the 1950s and 1960s at NASA Lewis by Man-
son and co-workers [1] and at General Electric by Cofﬁn and co-workers [2]. The ﬁrst was in terms of conceptualizing
the fatigue process in a more physically acceptable, albeit very general manner. The second major advance was asso-
ciated with advances in control technology. The rapid advances in ‘‘controls” paved the way for the development of
closed loop test machines, much more reliable data, and vastly improved life prediction capabilities. Using the then
recently-developed closed loop test equipment L. Cofﬁn and S. Manson were able to do experiments in which the
strain range was controlled. For relatively high strains, they found that when the plastic strain range was plotted

2. Phases and microstructures of superalloys
As mentioned previously, superalloys (both Ni-base and Ni–Fe base) are used in the hot sections of jet engines and,
although less frequently, also in the power generation industry. Microstructurally, the Ni-base alloys consist primarily of
small coherent precipitates embedded in an FCC or
c matrix. The precipitates, denoted c0, have an L1 2 crystal structure based
on the Ni 3Al ordered compound as seen in Fig. 1 a.

intentionally added to form carbides which reduce creep by inhibiting grain boundary sliding. Other elements may be added
to provide solid solution strengthening, (e.g. Ta) to control the volume fraction of
c0, to inhibit grain boundary sliding by
migrating to boundaries (e.g. odd size atomic species such as Zr), to improve castability (Hf) and to control the shapes of
carbides (Hf). The volume fraction of

3. Deformation mechanisms
3.1. Overview
In this section a brief review of basic deformation mechanisms is provided since an important aspect of fatigue damage at
any temperature is related to the way in which deformation occurs. Important features are: (i) Deformation at low temper-
atures is generally by precipitate shearing since the precipitates are closely spaced and coherent. (ii) At higher temperatures,
deformation-induced coarsening may occur leading to dislocation looping and lower strength. (iii) For shearing, strength in-
creases with precipitate size while for looping the strength decreases. This is observed experimentally and predicted theo-
retically. (iv) The maximum strength occurs at the shearing (Fig. 2 a) to looping (Fig. 2 b) transition. (v) At elevated
temperatures and low strain rates, the rate-limiting step for monotonic deformation is a creep-like climb process. Grain
boundary sliding is an additional mode of deformation in polycrystalline alloys. (vi) The
c0 precipitates become stronger
as the temperature is increased via a cross-slip mechanism which greatly increases the dislocation drag. This behaviour lar-
gely explains the high temperature strength of superalloys. (vii) Because of the basic
c0 deformation mode, there is an ob-
served asymmetry in the tension and compression yield stress in single crystals, which may have profound effects on
crack formation and propagation and hence on the life of such materials.

3.2.2. Cumulative glide and cyclic stress strain behaviour
Numerous superalloys harden upon cyclic straining both at room temperature [9–17] and at elevated temperatures
[11,12,17–21]. This hardening is a result of an increase in the dislocation density, extensive cross-slip at higher tempera-
tures, and the formation of complicated networks, all of which restrict the motion of mobile dislocations and constitute
deformation debris. Theories to explain hardening then involve the accumulation of forest dislocations or the formation
of Cottrell-Lomer locks. However, at lower temperatures the dislocations are restricted to the primary {1 1 1} slip planes,
as cross-slip is limited. Hardening at lower temperatures where planar slip predominates has been explained in terms of
a competition between precipitate shearing and increased slip band density [12]. Basically this theory explains the relatively
low cyclic hardening at low strains in terms of precipitate shearing on single slip bands since relatively few active bands are
required to carry the imposed strain. At higher strains, the hardening becomes much more pronounced since there are multi-
ple slip bands and dislocation debris at slip intersections. During cycling, more slip bands must nucleate because of the re-
stricted slip distances and eventually an equilibrium density of slip bands is achieved. In essence, the distance between slip
band intersections acts as a kind of barrier akin to grains in polycrystalline single phase materials.

by Merrick [11]. Resolutioning thus does not explain the softening behaviour in these alloys.

4. High temperature low cycle fatigue
Low cycle fatigue (LCF) limits life in turbine blades where temperatures may range from about 760 /C176C at the platform to
more than 1200 /C176C near the blade tips. It is also an important process for disks as well, especially near the rim in the region of
the attachment points. While most modern turbine blades are made of [0 0 1]-oriented single crystals, many engines are still
in service which use polycrystalline cast alloys such as René 80. Such legacy alloys are characterized by large grains, grain
boundary carbides to restrict grain boundary sliding, a c matrix and a high volume fraction of c0 precipitates with a mismatch
parameter of about 0.3%. High temperature LCF will be considered below for both polycrystalline and single crystalline
superalloys.
4.1. High temperature LCF of cast polycrystalline superalloys
René 80 is a legacy Ni-base alloy that has been (and is) widely used for turbine blades and provides a prototype of high
temperature LCF behaviour. Extensive studies have been carried out at different temperatures, strain rates and hold times
[28]. LCF test results are shown in Fig. 5. In this study continuous softening was observed. Important results were: (i) when
presented on the basis of plastic strain, the LCF life at 871 /C176C was not reduced by tensile hold time and in fact is even in-
creased and (ii) there was continuous softening throughout the duration of the test.
These observations differ from those usually made which show that the life is reduced by creep damage. Likewise, they
also differ from the numerous cases where initial rapid cyclic hardening followed by stress saturation for the majority of life
is observed such as was seen in the austenitic stainless steels. Clearly these results indicate that life prediction based on the
strain range partitioning (SRP) [40,41] or frequency modiﬁed fatigue life (FMFL) [42] approaches require some modiﬁcation.

4.2. Low and high cycle fatigue of single crystalline materials
Single crystal turbine blades have replaced those fabricated from cast polycrystalline materials, especially in the higher
performance military and civilian aircraft. The single crystal turbine blades have greatly extended the life by eliminating
grain boundary sliding as a creep damage mode and by reducing thermally induced stresses since they are oriented with
the long axis of the blade in the low modulus [0 0 1] direction. They are generally produced by a grain selection method
[47,48]. While this ensures the [0 0 1] axis is along the blade vertical axis, the secondary orientation is generally not con-
trolled. This means that the envelope of the blade is essentially randomly oriented with respect to the crystal axes and it
is known that the secondary axis has a large effect on fatigue properties, as shown in the section on fatigue crack propaga-
tion. Additional challenges include anisotropy which is a major issue in analysis and life prediction. In addition, the precip-
itate morphology may change in service from cubes to plates [38,49] (normal or parallel to load axis) and tension/
compression asymmetry may occur [37]. These latter two effects depend upon the relative orientation of the load axis
and the crystalline axes. Such factors may have a profound effect on the creep and fatigue properties [49]. In the following
two sections, the high and low cycle fatigue is reviewed.

4.3. Low cycle fatigue of PM alloys
Powder metallurgy (PM) alloys are used to make disks in jet engines because of savings in materials, processing and
machining time and also because powder products are essentially isotropic. René 95 is an alloy that has been used in pow-
der form in the hip + forged condition for disks in jet engines as has N18, a high strength alloy, which is discussed below.
Studies were carried out on René 95 [52] to assess the effects of hold time on LCF life since disks are subjected to long hold
times during ﬂight. Testing was carried out in fully reversed strain in strain control (R e = /C0 1), at 649 /C176C and a cyclic fre-
quency of 0.33 Hz. In some experiments 900s hold times were applied at the maximum load. This study showed that:
(i) a lengthy hold time extended the life when represented in terms of inelastic strain; (ii) in some cases cracking initiated
at pre-existing pores due to incomplete consolidation, even in the forged material; (iii) for initiation at equivalent pores,
the life was an order of magnitude greater when the initiation was subsurface; (iv) regardless of the initiation mechanism
(pores, slip bands, inclusions) there was always a small region of transgranular propagation followed by intergranular
propagation.
These results demonstrated clearly the huge effect of the environment on fatigue life, especially in the early stages of
crack propagation. The transition from transgranular to intergranular further strengthened this conclusion. Finally, the ab-
sence of a hold time effect when representing the data in terms of inelastic strain demonstrates that creep damage, at least
for these test conditions, is of secondary importance. Indeed, given the fact that the life actually increased with hold time, it
may reasonably be conjectured that some relaxation around the crack tips was occurring giving rise to blunting, slower crack
growth rates and longer lives. The effects of environment will be discussed in a more rigorous manner in a subsequent
section.
As stated previously, LCF at elevated temperatures (550–650 /C176C) is a dominant damage mechanism for turbine disks. In
designing these components, engineers frequently use test results for crack initiation ( 640,000 cycles) obtained from labo-
ratory experiments carried out on smooth, small specimens (typically 5–10 mm dia). This procedure is quite satisfactory if
the density of initiation sites observed in laboratory specimens represents the situation for larger components. This condi-
tion is met for high strain LCF of conventional wrought alloys with a very low defect (e.g. inclusions) concentration. An exam-
ple is In 718 where stage I cracks initiate along intense deformation bands on the surface of laboratory specimens. The
number of these bands is sufﬁciently large to be representative of large components. Under these conditions, no size effect
is expected, and the methodology used for life prediction is essentially deterministic, even though, in practice, the scatter in
the test results is taken into account.
This methodology does not necessarily apply to advanced PM Ni-base superalloys for two main reasons: (i) the high yield
strength of these materials tends to shift the fatigue life calculations to the fully elastic regime where the density of intense
slip bands is reduced and (ii) the density of processing-related inclusions (e.g. Al
2O3 or SiO2) is extremely low in commercial
advanced materials (typically 10 /C0 6) but such inclusions can be extremely detrimental to the fatigue life (see e.g. [53–55]).
Tests results obtained using small laboratory specimens of PM alloys exhibit two trends: (i) surface inclusion initiation
(which is much more detrimental than internal inclusion initiation) and (ii) considerable scatter in the test results (see
e.g. [56]). Such scatter is related to the probability of initiating and propagating a crack from an inclusion. This probability
depends on the inclusion size, shape and specimen size. The size effect arises since the probability of ﬁnding a large inclusion
is higher in a component than in a small laboratory specimen. Several models have been proposed to account for this size
effect (see e.g. [57–59]), and several attempts have been made to include this statistical aspect in life prediction methods (see
e.g. [60]).
4.4. Other metallurgical instabilities at high temperature
It was previously shown that dynamic changes in the material structure have major effects on the fatigue life and cannot
be ignored. In addition to the changes in precipitates, other dynamic changes also occur. In a study to show such effects using
Waspaloy as a model material, it was shown that carbides precipitate on slip bands, which also impinge upon oxidized
boundaries to nucleate microcracks as shown in Fig. 9 [12] . Basically carbides lock the dislocations in the slip bands thereby
blocking relaxation of the high stresses at the tip of the slip band. These stresses in turn crack the oxidized grain boundaries
causing crack initiation.
These experiments, and much of the preceding discussion, demonstrate that instabilities or more precisely, dynamic
changes, occur at surfaces (embrittlement in the surface region, deformation-induced changes in the surface roughness),
at the level of the microstructure (precipitate coarsening, dynamic precipitation on slip bands), and at the level of the dis-
location substructure (slip band dispersion, annealing of dislocations). The inescapable conclusion is that generalized ap-
proaches to life prediction are unlikely to be successful. Instead, the critical damage mechanisms must be incorporated
into material/test condition speciﬁc models. In essence, a table is desired in which test conditions and material type are
the rows and columns and the squares are ﬁlled in with speciﬁc proved models that take the mechanisms into account.

5. Thermo-mechanical fatigue in Ni-base superalloys
In many situations both temperature and imposed mechanical strain vary simultaneously in a repetitive manner. This is
termed thermo-mechanical fatigue (TMF). For example, the start-up and shut-down phases of a jet engine constitute a sim-
ple TMF cycle. Power variations during altitude changes or other manoeuvres of a jet aircraft introduce TMF cycles because of
the changes in power which cause changes in temperature as well as changes in loading. An over-view of TMF is given else-
where [61] and physically based modelling of TMF has been discussed in detail in another article [62]. In this section TMF
results obtained on smooth specimens are emphasized.
In a TMF test temperature and strain change simultaneously and independently. The changes in strain arise from a com-
bination of thermal and mechanical effects. Since the thermal strains are not associated with loading, it is customary to sub-
tract the thermal strain is subtracted to give the mechanical strain. One may deﬁne various paths in terms of temperature
and strain. The most revealing kinds of tests are either in-phase (IP) or out-of- phase (OP) in terms of temperature and strain.
In the OP test (generally the most damaging), the minimum strain is at the maximum temperature. A schematic of the stress/

expected to be quite damaging. On the other hand, if the test is of the IP type then the stresses at the lower temperature

6. Fatigue crack propagation (FCP) of superalloys
6.1. Introductory comments
One of the major applications of superalloys is in the disks of jet engines (both Ni-base and Fe–Ni base) where they are
used in the high pressure compressor and turbine sections of the engine. In these applications, the temperatures vary from
the bore of the disk to the rim, depending on the function and location in the engine. Typical temperatures may be as low as
350 /C176C at the bore to as high as 700/C176C at the ﬁr-tree where turbine blades are attached. Important design parameters are LCF
and fatigue crack propagation (FCP). An appreciation for the importance of FCP in jet engine disks can be obtained by exam-
ination of an accident which occurred in Los Angeles, on June 2nd, 2006. In this incident, ground personnel were performing
a high powered ground run of the engines on a Boeing 767-223 (ER) airplane when the number one (left) engine exploded.
The failure was eventually shown to have originated in the high pressure turbine stage 1 disk as a result of an intergranular
fatigue crack. In addition, some areas of this disk exhibited classical fatigue striations. The on-site investigation revealed that
‘‘one piece of the disk, which initially bounced off of the ground before penetrating the airplane, completely severed the air-
plane’s left-hand keel beam and partially severed the right-hand keel beam before exiting the airplane and becoming lodged
in the No. 2 engine’s exhaust duct as shown in Fig. 12 [64]”. Had this occurred at altitude, the result would undoubtedly have
been complete loss of the aircraft and extensive loss of life.
In this section, factors affecting fatigue crack propagation will be considered for model systems, Waspaloy, In 718, and
powder metallurgy alloys. Waspaloy and In 718 are legacy alloys still widely used for disks in jet engines. It will be demon-
strated that the primary factors controlling FCP are the slip mode and environmental interactions. Simple cycles where the
load is (i) continuously cycled under isothermal conditions and (ii) cycled with a hold time at maximum load are ﬁrst con-

6.2.3. Fatigue crack propagation in practical Ni-base and Fe/Ni-base alloys
The previous section dealt with model alloys in which ﬁrst principles could be tested by controlling composition and heat
treatment to obtain desired physical properties and microstructures. Practical Ni-base and Fe/Ni-base alloys are the result of
enlightened empiricism combining intuitive understanding and vast experience. It is important to see how the preceding
principles might be applied through simple heat treatment since changes in composition require vast development programs
and certiﬁcation exercises. Without changing the composition, basically the precipitate morphology and grain size can be
controlled. At the extremes, there are basically four possible combinations of precipitate and grain size that can affect the
slip mode component of the over-all crack propagation rates. These variables are discussed below for representative Ni-base
alloys.
6.2.3.1. FCP in René 95 and Waspaloy. Some very early studies were carried out in which René 95, a powder metallurgy (PM)

that improvements in FCP behaviour could be brought about by changing the slip mode through varying the precipitate and
grain sizes in a systematic manner, giving perhaps the ﬁrst solid indication that the principles discussed in the preceding
sections could be applied to existing commercial systems.

6.2.3.4. Environmental, microstructural and compositional effects on the FCP rates of In 718. Oxygen can have a very strong ef-
fect on the FCP rate of In 718, as shown in Fig. 16 [84] . The environmental effect may manifest itself in several ways. In one
particularly revealing study, the effects of oxygen partial pressure were examined [85]. The operating hypothesis is that oxy-
gen can have a short range effect (formation of grain boundary oxides) and a long range effect (diffusion of oxygen along
boundaries with the formation of other phases which can release known embrittling agents to the grain boundaries. The ba-
sic hypothesis was that spinels of NiO and FeO can form at the grain boundary under conditions of atmospheric pressure of
oxygen. However, these are fairly porous oxides and oxygen can diffuse to the base metal to continue the growth of this
oxide, albeit at decreasing partial pressure because of the tortuous path that oxygen must follow. After sufﬁcient growth
the oxygen partial pressure is no longer sufﬁcient for the formation of these oxides to be thermodynamically stable at
the interface. This can be understood by straightforward appeal to a Richardson [86] diagram for oxide formation. Simply
put the partial pressure of oxygen drops below what is required for the spinel-like reactions to continue. However, at the
reduced pressure Cr
2O3 is thermodynamically stable and forms beneath the mixed oxide layer thus passivating the surface.
It was found that there was an incubation time for such passivation to occur which increased with increasing oxygen partial
pressure as seen in Fig. 17 .
These ideas were tested by carrying out experiments for R = 0.1 at 650 /C176C for three different conditions. Series (a) contin-
uous cycling, series (b) same as continuous cycling except with a 600 s hold at minimum load and series (c) same as (b) ex-
cept a small 1 Hz cycle was imposed at minimum load. The motivating idea was that 600 s was the transition time for
passivation and the spinels should have stopped forming at this point. In (c) applying a small load (below threshold so as
to avoid crack advance due to ‘‘mechanical” effects) would break up the spinels at the crack tip and in effect would speed
up crack growth by preventing passivation and allowing the rapid spinel formation to continue at what would be effectively
higher partial pressures. This is exactly what happened and these results tend to conﬁrm the two-step crack tip oxidation
model for environmental interaction.

6.2.3.7. Inﬂuence of chemical composition. While it may be attractive to carry out heat treatments to alleviate environmental
degradation, some caution is in order. During service, there is a type of continuous heat treatment being carried out which
may outweigh any inﬂuence of the initial heat treatment and microstructure. For that reason, it is of interest to see if minor
chemical changes can be made which have a very large effect on the FCGR behaviour at elevated temperature.
Some studies have been carried out in which direct compositional modiﬁcations were made. Since the environmental ef-
fect in In 718 is often related to grain boundary oxidation (see above), chemical modiﬁcations that would suppress or elim-
inate grain boundary attract without degrading other properties would be of interest. Some studies were carried out to
examine the effects of boron on FCP behaviour. Boron, being an odd-sized atom, would tend to preferentially segregate at
the grain boundaries. Results for testing at an R-ratio of 0.05 and a test frequency of 30 Hz [100] showed that boron increased
the cohesive strength of the grain boundaries thereby shifting the ‘‘weakest link” from oxygen-embrittled grain boundaries
to fracture by planar, crystallographic slip, which as has been discussed, leads to a lower crack growth rate. SEM examination
of the fracture surfaces showed that the fracture mode became increasingly transgranular with increasing boron. Boron
could both slow down grain boundary diffusion and the formation of complex oxides.
Other studies have shown that trace amounts of phosphorus may have a beneﬁcial effect on strengthening the grain
boundaries of In 718 [101]. It was found that in the range of 0.0008–0.013%, phosphorus had a beneﬁcial effect on stress
rupture and also changed the morphology of the d phase from disks to ﬁlm-like with numerous faults. The beneﬁcial effect
of phosphorus was attributed to impeding grain boundary oxidation. However, as the phosphorus concentration was in-
creased to the range of 0.013–0.049%, the creep life was lowered, probably because of some intrinsic effect of phosphorus
in reducing the grain boundary cohesive strength. It would be logical to assume that, like boron, phosphorus could be a use-
ful addition to In 718 at trace levels in improving the creep–fatigue life.
It has also been shown that other modiﬁcations in the grain boundary chemistry can be made to improve the strength. In
a summary report of the effects of various alloying elements on chemically modifying alloy 718 [102], it was shown that the
addition of trace amounts of magnesium (4–100 ppm) also are useful in increasing cyclic stress rupture life probably by
increasing the grain boundary strength. Again, such increases in the grain boundary strength are very likely to be reﬂected
in improved hold time FCP properties. The grain boundary strength is probably increased by blocking the ingress of oxygen
along boundaries or possibly in tying up sulfur as is familiar in steels. It is also mentioned that magnesium, which is known
to segregate to grain boundaries, has the effect of changing the d grain boundary morphology from disk-like to globular.
Since phosphorus additions change it to a ﬁlm-like morphology, it is not at all clear that the effects of these trace elements
on changing the d morphology have any real direct effect on the mechanical behaviour.
6.2.4. Fatigue crack propagation in Ni-base single crystals
6.2.4.1. Introductory comments. Just as the concept of retirement for cause was introduced into the life management of jet
engine disks, thereby making FCP behaviour of primary importance, crack propagation has taken an added importance in
the management and life prediction of jet engine turbine blades which generally operate at higher temperatures than disks
and which, as mentioned previously, are frequently fabricated from Ni-base superalloy single crystals. While such blades
have greatly extended the life, they are very expensive and there is a great interest in removing them for a reason (i.e. retire-
ment for cause) than simply removing them based on a so-called safe-life calculation. Thus there is a three-fold challenge: (i)
extend FCP concepts to higher temperatures; (ii) apply fracture mechanics concepts to elastically anisotropic materials; (iii)
understand the basic mechanisms of crack propagation in these anisotropic materials. These issues are addressed below.
In the following discussion a nominal fracture mechanics representation is used. However, a word of caution is in order
about using LEFM to represent FCP behaviour in Ni-base superalloy single crystals. Although a defect may be initially normal
to the loading axis, as soon as a true crack is formed, the mechanism of propagation may produce an oblique surface which
calls the use of simple expressions for the stress intensity parameter into question. That is to say, the mechanism of cracking
has an effect on the forcing function in such a way as to render its elementary application inappropriate. Clearly work needs
to be done to represent crack growth in terms of a more local approach.

7. Summary and conclusions
The fatigue behaviour of Ni-base superalloys has been reviewed and the following main conclusions may be drawn:
a. The LCF life of superalloys is determined by an interaction between the slip mode and oxidation. At lower tempera-
tures the microstructure is stable, oxidation is minimal and the life is controlled primarily by deformation behaviour.
At intermediate temperatures, the microstructure is stable but the environment becomes increasingly damaging. The
high stresses associated with the stable structure cause cracking of oxidized boundaries or oxide spikes and the life is
reduced. At high temperatures, even though oxidation is more severe, deformation-assisted precipitate coarsening
leads to reduced stresses, a more damage resistant structure (increased ductility) and an increase in life. The LCF life
is seen to be the result of complex, time-dependent physical processes.
b. For high purity PM alloys, there is a size effect since small specimens are unrepresentative of large components. The
probability of a defect such as an inclusion being at a critical location (e.g. surface) increases with increasing specimen
size and there is a corresponding reduction in life.
c. In TMF, the strain/temperature history is such that a large number of potential damage mechanisms occur and inter-
actions between damage mechanisms occur in TMF which either do not occur during isothemal LCF or occur to a lim-
ited degree. It was shown that TMF is path dependent and that the most damaging interactions occur in the so-called
OP path for which the TMF life is generally much lower than for the IP path.
d. For In 718, the environmental effect is very pronounced and, in hold time experiments, is related to complex oxidation
mechanisms involving porous spinels and a more protective Cr 2O3 oxide that forms later. The thermodynamic com-
petition between formation of these oxides (with their different mechanical properties) gives rise to a threshold oxy-
gen partial pressure for an environmental effect. It was demonstrated that the environmental effect could be reduced
by additions of Cr which favour formation of the protective Cr
2O3.
e. It was shown that the severity of an environmental effect is related to an interaction between the microstructure and
deformation mode. Those microstructures which promote rapid stress relaxation were shown to be less sensitive to
oxidation effects for N18 and In 718, an effect which is probably ubiquitous for Ni-base alloys. It was also shown that
small increases in temperature for short times also promote a relaxation mechanism ahead of cracks and reduce sub-
sequent environmentally-assisted hold time FCP.
f. Air and vacuum testing for different model alloys showed that during continuous cycling FCP the environmental and
microstructural effects often act in opposite directions such that the net FCP rate is the result of a decrease due to a
favourable slip mode and an increase due to the environment. In some cases these effects are such that there is no
apparent environmental effect when in fact it may be very large.
