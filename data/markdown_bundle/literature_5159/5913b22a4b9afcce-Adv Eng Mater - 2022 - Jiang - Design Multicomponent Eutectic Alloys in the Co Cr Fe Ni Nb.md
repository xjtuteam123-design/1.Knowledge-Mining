1. Introduction
Eutectic alloys are known to be one of the most widely used alloys
because of low melting temperature, high castability, controllable
microstructures, wide engineering applications, and so on. [1– 3]
The eutectic alloys are able to yield different kinds of
microstructures during different solidi ﬁca-
tion conditions, [4,5] especially the regular
lamellar or rod-like eutectic organization,
forming an in situ composite material.
The in situ composite material generally
shows excellent mechanical properties,
which is suitable for engineering applica-
tions, such as the aircraft parts, engine
blade, high temperature turbine blade.
Unfortunately, the number of binary
eutectic alloys is quite limited due to the
limitation of alloy system. In order to
further expand the composition and
enhance the comprehensive mechanical
properties of in situ composites, it is
necessary to study the multicomponent
eutectic alloys.
Recently, multicomponent eutectic high
entropy alloys (EHEAs) [6] combining the
concepts of high entropy alloy (HEA) and
eutectic alloy were proposed. Thereafter,
many research works [7– 18] have been car-
ried out to focus on the development of
new eutectic compositions. These EHEAs
usually show simultaneous improvement
in the strength and plasticity. For instance,
Lu et al. [6,19,20] designed the AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 EHEA consisting of
soft order/disorder face-centered cubic (L12/FCC) and hard order
body-centered cubic (B2) phases, which presents attractive
mechanical properties over a wide temperature range. Shi
et al. [7] reported the precipitate-free Al 19.25Co18.86Fe18.36Ni43.53
EHEA shows excellent tensile ductility of ∼ 10 % and high ulti-
mate strength up to /C25 956 MPa. Wang et al. [13] studied
Ni30Co30Cr10Fe10Al18W2 EHEA with FCC and B2 phases. It
exhibits excellent mechanical properties with higher tensile frac-
ture strength ( /C25 1266.5 MPa) and elongation ( /C25 20.3%). Dong
et al. [16] reported the AlCrFeNiMo 0.2 EHEA consisting of body-
centered cubic (BCC) and AlNi-type intermetallic phases. The
alloy shows the good combination of compressive fracture
strength of 3222 MPa and plastic strain of 28.7%. Wei et al. [17]
designed a carbide-reinforced Re 0.5MoNbW(TaC)0.8 EHEA with
a high yield strength of 1340 MPa, ultimate compressive strength
of 2347 MPa and plasticity of 8.90%. Thus, EHEAs with high
strength and plasticity possessed potential of great economic
value and will powerfully promote the industrial application of
multicomponent metallic materials.

microstructure comprising of alternate hard intermetallic and soft

NiFeNb 0.35 ,C o F e N i N b0.5,C o C r F e N i N b0.45 eutectic alloys are prepared. The

eutectic alloys are studied. All of these designed eutectic alloys are composed
of Nb-rich intermetallic compound ph ase and Nb-poor face-centered cubic
(Fcc) phase. However, these eutectic alloys show different eutectic mor-
phology. The FeNiNb 0.35 alloy exhibits a compact seaweed eutectic-dendritic

yield strength of 1316 MPa, fracture strength of 2339 MPa, compressive strain

HV. The solid solution strengthening of the FCC phase and the hard inter-
metallic phase strengthening are the major strength mechanism in this
eutectic alloys.

2. Composition Design
Figure 1 schematically shows the ideal composition diagrams of
eutectic alloys for ternary to quinary alloy systems. The alloy com-
position composed of A1,A 2,A 3,A 4, and B can be regarded as the
A– B binary alloy. For example, the ternary alloy composed of A 1,
A2, and B elements can be regarded as (A 1,A 2)-B pseudo-binary
alloy. The quaternary alloy composed of A 1,A 2,A 3, and B four
elements can be regarded as (A 1,A 2,A 3)-B pseudo-binary alloy.
The quinary alloy composed of ﬁve elements A1,A 2,A 3,A 4, and B
can be regarded as (A 1,A 2,A 3,A 4)-B pseudo-binary alloy. These
elements should show the following features: 1) At the liquid
state, the binding ability of A 1– A2,A 1– A3,A 1– A4 pairs is similar
to that of A 1– A1,A 2– A2,A 3– A3,A 4– A4 pairs. Generally, the mix-
ing enthalpy of the atomic pairs of A 1– A2,A 1– A3,A 1– A4,A 2– A3,
A2– A4, A3– A4 is close to zero. 2) The binding ability of A 1– B,
A2– B, A 3– B, A 4– B is much greater than that of A 1– A1,A 2– A2,
A3– A3,A 4– A4, and B – B pairs. Generally, the mixing enthalpy
of the atomic pairs of A 1– B, A2– B, A3– B, and A4– B is largely neg-
ative. Moreover, each A-type element (e.g., A 1,A 2,A 3,A 4) can
form binary eutectics with element B. In this article, A-type ele-
ments are mixed in equal molar ratio and the ﬁnal calculated
eutectic alloy composition should be located in the center region

3. Microstructures and Mechanical Properties
3.1. Microstructures
In order to investigate the effects of the number of alloy compo-
nents on the microstructures and phase composition of alloys,
the NiNb 0.18, NiFeNb 0.35, CoFeNiNb 0.5, and CoCrFeNiNb 0.45
alloys were prepared by vacuum arc melting. The microstruc-
tures of the eutectic alloys were given in Figure 3 . According
to the Ni – Nb binary phase diagram, eutectic reaction occurs
in the NiNb 0.18 alloy and α-Ni phase with FCC solid solution
structure and β-Ni3Nb phase with orthorhombic Cu 3Ti structure
are formed. From Figure 3a, it can be found that the NiNb 0.18
binary alloy exhibited an irregular eutectic morphology with
the larger volume fraction A phase and a lamellar B phase.
Figure 3b displays the microstructure of FeNiNb 0.35 ternary
eutectic alloy. It can be found that the eutectic-dendritic pattern
is surrounded by the coarse granular eutectic, which may be
formed by remelting and ripening of primary lamellar eutec-
tics.[5] This indicated that the FeNiNb 0.35 ternary eutectic alloy
belongs to a compact seaweed eutectic-dendritic pattern. And
the dendritic pattern shows a strong orientational order while
the seaweed pattern without any apparent orientational order.
This morphology is similar to that the Co – 24% Sn eutectic alloy
at a critical undercooling of 175 K. [32] According to the interface
growth theory, the interface energy anisotropy and undercooling
are the two important factors that determine the morphology of
growth patterns of alloys. The crystal morphologies of alloys can
be divided into four types including fractal seaweed, dense sea-
weed, fractal dendritic, and dense dendritic. The dendritic struc-
ture is favorable for small undercooling and relatively large
anisotropies, while the seaweed structure is favored for larger
undercooling and weak anisotropy. The formation of a compact
seaweed eutectic-dendritic morphology may be result from the

Figure 3c shows the SEM images of the CoFeNiNb 0.5 alloy.
It can be found that the eutectic morphology of CoFeNiNb 0.5
alloy obviously has been changed as the addition of Co element.
The CoFeNiNb0.5 alloy shows dendritic eutectic morphology con-

by regular lamellar structure. The morphology also exists in
some traditional eutectic alloys, such as NiAl – Cr (Mo), [33] Fe–

alloy exhibited eutectic cell morphology consisting with regular
lamellar eutectic structure with lamellae width of 100 – 200 nm,
which is much lower than that of the CoFeNiNb 0.5 quaternary
eutectic alloy. The decreased eutectic lamellae width may be
due to the obvious slow diffusion effect because of the increased
number of elements in the CoCrFeNiNb 0.45 quinary alloy.
In addition, the different partition coef ﬁcients of Cr element
in eutectic phase resulted in the composition undercooling
enlarge in front of eutectic growth interface, which is favorable
for the formation of cell-like eutectic morphology.
The chemical compositions of different regions of these eutec-
tic alloys are measured by EDS and listed in Table 1. It could be
seen that in the NiNb 0.18 alloy, the A phase contained Ni of
75.4 at% and Nb of 24.6 at%, while B phase contained Ni of
84.0 at% and Nb of 16.0 at%. In the FeNiNb 0.35 alloy, the A phase
is enriched with Fe, Ni elements, while the B phase is enriched
with Nb element. The enrichment or depletion of elements at the
solid– liquid interface leads to the instability of the solid – liquid
interface. During the FeNiNb 0.35 eutectic growth process, the
transverse diffusion of Fe, Ni elements will lead to the formation
of eutectic morphology, while the longitudinal diffusion of Nb
element at the solid – liquid interface make the eutectic interface
unstable to form the dendritic or seaweed pattern. The weak
interfacial anisotropy between FCC and Nb-rich Laves phase
leads to the formation of a branch-seaweed eutectic morphology
in the FeNiNb 0.35 alloy.
The EDS elemental mapping of the CoFeNiNb 0.5 alloy are
shown in Figure 4 . From Table 1 and Figure 4, it can be found
that the A phase is enriched with Fe, Ni elements, while B phase
is enriched with Nb, Co elements. For the CoCrFeNiNb 0.45 alloy,
the A phase is enriched with Fe, Ni, Cr elements, and B phase is
enriched with Nb, Co elements. Comparing the element distri-
butions in CoCrFeNiNb 0.45 and CoFeNiNb 0.5 alloys, it can be
found that in the Nb-rich phase, the enrichment degree of Nb
element in CoCrFeNiNb 0.45 quinary alloy is greater than that
in CoFeNiNb 0.5 quaternary alloy. This indicated that the
addition of Cr element enlarges the solidi ﬁcation range of
CoCrFeNiNb0.45 alloy, and it is easy to produce component
undercooling leading eutectic growth from the ﬂat interface to
cell eutectic. In a word, all of these eutectic alloys are composed
of Nb-rich B phase and Nb-poor A phase.
X-ray diffraction patterns of the binary to quinary eutectic
alloys are given in Figure 5. From Figure 5a, it can be found that
the NiNb 0.18 binary eutectic alloy is composed of FCC solid
solution structure and Ni 3Nb phase. For the FeNiNb 0.35,
CoFeNiNb0.5, and CoCrFeNiNb0.45 alloys, the FCC solid solution
and Laves structures can be identiﬁed as shown in Figure 5b. The
Laves phases for FeNiNb 0.35, CoFeNiNb0.5, and CoCrFeNiNb 0.45
are Fe 2Nb-type, Co 1.92Nb1.08-type, and Co 1.92Nb1.08-type,
respectively.
Consisted with the EDS and XRD results, it can be found that
white B phase in the NiNb 0.18 alloy is Ni3Nb phase while A phase
is α-Ni phase. The FeNiNb 0.35 ternary eutectic alloy is composed
of a FCC solid solution structure (A phase) and a Fe 2Nb-type
Laves structure (B phase). In both of the CoFeNiNb 0.5 and
CoCrFeNiNb0.45 alloys, the A phase shows a FCC structure
and B phase shows a Co 1.92Nb1.08-type Laves phase structure.

3.2. Vickers Hardness and Compressive Properties
To examine the effect of microstructure on mechanical proper-
ties, the Vickers hardness and compressive tests were performed.
The Vickers hardness values of binary to quinary alloys are
depicted in Figure 6 . It can be found that the Vickers hardness
values of NiFeNb 0.35, CoFeNiNb 0.5, CoCrFeNiNb 0.45 alloys are
higher than 500 HV. The values are obvious higher than that
of NiNb 0.18 binary alloy with a Vickers hardness of 379 HV.
The higher Vickers hardness values in the NiFeNb 0.35,
CoFeNiNb0.5, CoCrFeNiNb 0.45 alloys result from their ﬁner
eutectic microstructure and larger volume fraction of the hard
Laves phase.
Figure 7 shows the compressive stress-strain curves of all the
alloys, from which the compressive yielding strength ( σ0.2) and
fracture strength ( σbc) are measured and listed in Table 2. It can
be found that with the increase of elements number, there is an
increased tendency for the yield strength. Wherein, the NiNb 0.18
binary alloy exhibited the lowest yield strength of 635 MPa. The
FeNiNb0.35 alloy exhibited a lowest fracture strength and fracture
strain. The reason may be that during the compression process,
the seaweed pattern in the FeNiNb 0.35 alloy hinders dislocation
movement and leads to the failure occurs before the fracture. The
CoFeNiNb0.5 and CoCrFeNiNb 0.45 eutectic alloys with regular
lamellar microstructure exhibited better mechanical properties.
Wherein, the CoCrFeNiNb 0.45 quinary alloy shows the highest
yield strength of 1475 MPa, which is twice as high as that of
the NiNb 0.18 binary alloy.

4. Conclusion
To conclude, a series of binary to quinary eutectic alloys
(NiNb0.18, NiFeNb 0.35, CoFeNiNb 0.5, CoCrFeNiNb 0.45) were
designed/located and prepared. The microstructure and mechan-
ical properties of these alloys were also evaluated. The following
conclusions were drawn: 1) All of ternary to quinary alloys in the
Co– Cr– Fe– Ni– Nb alloy system show eutectic microstructure,
indicating the effectiveness of the simple mixture method.
2) All of the binary to quinary eutectic alloys are composed of
Nb-rich intermetallic compound and Nb-poor FCC phases.
This indicated that as the increased number of components,
the number of phase of these alloys remained the same.
3) Among binary to quinary eutectic alloys, the
CoCrFeNiNb0.45 quinary eutectic alloy shows the best compre-
hensive mechanical properties with yield strength of
1316 MPa, fracture strength of 2339 MPa, fracture strain of
24.2%, fracture toughness of 18.4 MPa m 1/2, and Vickers hard-
ness of 511 HV. The excellent properties resulted from the
smaller eutectic lamellar spacing and larger volume fraction of
intermetallic phase.
