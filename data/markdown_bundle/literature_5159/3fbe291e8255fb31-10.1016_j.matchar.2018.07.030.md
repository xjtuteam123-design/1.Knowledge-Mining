ABSTRACT
Multi-principle component alloys (MPCAs) di ﬀer from traditional alloys in that they consist of four or more elements or components each with concentrations of
5–35 at. %. Since the ﬁrst eutectic multi-principle component alloy (MPCA) was produced in 2008, there has been a growing number of papers on developing eutectic
MPCAs as potential structural materials. Eutectic MPCAs can show high ambient temperature yield strengths that increase with decreasing interlamellar spacing, λ,
according to either λ−1/2 or λ−1, similar to that observed in pearlitic steels, with a tradeo ﬀ between this increased strength and reduced tensile ductility. Ambient
temperature tensile ductility has been observed in eutectic MPCAs only when one phase is f.c.c. and when the harder second phase is itself deformable. The yield
strength in eutectic MPCAs has been shown to decrease with increasing temperature, and, limited data suggest that, this is related to the softening of the harder
phase. Annealing of as-cast eutectic MPCAs, which are not typically at equilibrium, can produce precipitation of ﬁne particles that further increase the strength, and
which often reduce the ductility. Both thermo-mechanical processing and nitriding can increase the strengths of eutectic MPCAs by transforming the lamellar eutectic
into equi-axed grains and producing ﬁne AlN particles (in aluminum-containing MPCAs), respectively. The properties of eutectic MPCAs can largely be explained by
models used for traditional alloys. While a number of di ﬀerent elements have been used to produce eutectic MPCAs, the design of eutectic MPCAs for structural
applications should avoid the use of expensive elements like cobalt and niobium, which have often been used.
1. Introduction
The pioneering work of Cantor et al. [ 1] and Yeh et al. [ 2] published
in 2004 was focused on producing single-phase so-called high entropy
alloys (HEAs) from substantial concentrations (5 –35 at. %) of ﬁve of
more elements or components. The underlying thesis was that in order
to produce a single-phase material the con ﬁgurational entropy resulting
from utilizing many components would more than o ﬀset the enthalpy
associated with the formation of potential intermetallic compounds
[1,2]. This outcome it turns out is rarely the case, and even one of the
original HEAs CoCrFeMnNi, the so-called Cantor alloy, turns out to be
multi-phase when annealed or mechanically tested at elevated tem-
peratures [ 3,4]. That most HEAs are not single phase [ 5–8] is not an
unfavorable feature for developing structural alloys since a second
phase can produce substantial strengthening.
Here, we denote multi-phase HEAs as multi-principal component
alloys (MPCAs) to clearly distinguish them from their single-phase re-
latives since for MPCAs, the idea of the large con ﬁgurational entropy
oﬀsetting the enthalpy of compound formation is no longer relevant. In
fact, multi-phase MPCAs have been studied for much longer than single-
phase HEAs. For example, the original AlNiCo permanent magnets in-
vented in the 1940s were iron-based alloys containing aluminum,
nickel and cobalt that consisted of nanostructured b.c.c. and B2 (or-
dered b.c.c.) phases formed by spinodal decomposition [ 9]. However,
by the late 1950s, AlNiCo 8 and 9 had been developed, which were no
longer iron-based alloys but MPCAs with compositions of 13.8% Al,
13.6% Ni, 31.5% Co, 3.3% Cu, 5.5% Ti and 32.3% Fe (compositions in
at. % throughout). Coincident with the publication of the ﬁrst work on
single-phase HEAs in 2004, research started on a series of multi-phase
(b.c.c./B2 or B2/L2
1, a further ordering of the B2 structure) FeNiMnAl
MPCAs that also appear to form by spinodal decomposition [ 10–15].
These materials, which were studied for possible structural applica-
tions, show phenomenal compressive yield strengths (up to 2350 MPa),
but unfortunately show no tensile ductility. A solution to the poor
ductility problem is still wanting. Wang et al. [ 16] also observed B2/
b.c.c. structures in Al xCoCrFeNi alloys for x = 0.5 –1.0 along with a
f.c.c. phase for low Al contents. The maximum hardness (HV 527) was
observed for x = 0.9, which was a fully spinodal structure with the
ﬁnest phase spacing. The composition Al 0.7CoCrFeNi has been studied
in some detail by two groups [ 17,18] and again was found to consist of
a f.c.c. phase and spinodal B2 + b.c.c. regions Very high compressive
yield strengths were obtained both for the overall alloy (1022 MPa)
[18] and, in particular, for the spinodal regions (up to 2.3 GPa) [ 17].
A little later in 2008, the ﬁrst eutectic MPCA was produced with a
composition of Fe 30Ni20Mn35Al15 [19]. While not as strong as the spi-
nodal FeNiMnAl alloys [ 10–15], this alloy could show signi ﬁcant ten-
sile ductility. Since then eutectic MPCAs have been of growing interest
as possible structural materials. In this paper, we review research on

2. As-cast MPCAs
The central idea behind strengthening in eutectic MPCAs is similar
to that underlying the strengthening of mild steels. The latter form by a
eutectoid transformation and consist of a soft b.c.c. iron phase (ferrite)
and a much harder, undeformable Fe
3C phase (cementite). In mild
steels, slip takes place only in the b.c.c. phase and the cementite's role is
to delineate the size of the ferritic lamellae. Thus, the strength of a mild
steel depends on the b.c.c. interlamellar spacing, λ, which has been
described by either a λ
−1/2 or a λ−1 relationship in di ﬀerent publica-
tions [ 2,20–22]. Whichever relationship is most appropriate, a smaller
value of λ produces a higher yield strength. Thus, the goal when pro-
ducing a eutectic or eutectoid MPCAs is to have softer, more easily
deformable phase to provide ductility and a harder to deform phase,
whose essential role is to deﬁ ne the spacing of the softer phase.

[19] with the composition Fe
30Ni20Mn35Al15. The MPCA has a density

composition of Fe 7Mn18Ni47Al28 and a lattice parameter of 0.291 nm,

Fracture occurred by a mixture of ductile rupture in the f.c.c. phase and
transgranular cleavage in the B2 phase.
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was used to show that
plastic deformation largely took place in the softer (nanohardness –
2.72 GPa) f.c.c. phase with only a few a 〈111〉 dislocations observed in

B2 phase initially acted largely as a hard undeformable strengthening

at least when the B2 lamellae were large [ 28]. The elastic modulus of
the two phases is fairly similar at 130 GPa and 123 GPa for the B2 and
f.c.c. phases, respectively [ 28].

treatments, which consisted of largely equi-axed grains, are shown in
Fig. 11. The yield strength after cold-rolling was very high at 1625 MPa
and the MPCA still exhibited 6% elongation. Deformation took place in
both phases and at heavier rolling reductions ( ≥50%) the L1 2 phase
became disordered. Disordering due to cold-rolling has previously been
noted in L1
2 compounds, particularly Ni 3Al [ 69–74]. Wani et al. [ 43]
suggested, but did not show, that microbands and shear bands could be
involved in the disordering process. It is also possible that anti-phase
boundary tube formation [ 75] contributes to the disordering process.
Annealing produced a microstructure consisting of discrete equi-axed
f.c.c. and B2 grains similar to that noted in Fe
36Ni18Mn33Al13 MPCA
noted above [65]. Note that the authors showed that annealing at 973 K
transforms the f.c.c. phase to L1 2, i.e. the recrystallization anneals were
all above the disordering temperature for the L1 2 phase. Annealing at
1073 K produced ~0.6 μm grains with 55% f.c.c. phase and 45% B2
phase. Annealing at higher temperatures led to larger grain sizes (2 μm
at 1473 K) but the grain growth kinetics were sluggish even though the
highest annealing temperature 1473 K was at 91% of the homologous
eutectic temperature [ 43]. After the anneals, the b.c.c. precipitates
were no longer present in the B2 phase.
Unlike the thermo-mechanical treatments on Fe 36Ni18Mn33Al13 [65]
and Fe 28.2Ni18.8Mn32.9Al14.1Cr6 [68] which transformed the lamellar
structure into an equi-axed grain structure but had minor e ﬀects on the
mechanical properties, AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 annealed at 1073 K showed a
substantial increase in yield strength from 620 MPa in the as-cast state
to 1108 MPa in the recrystallized state with a slight reduction in
elongation in failure from 17% to 12%. Annealing at 1473 K reduced
the yield strength to a similar value to the as-cast MPCA of 648 MPa but
with an improvement in elongation to failure to 27%.
In one of the few studies of texture analysis of a MPCA, Wani et al.
[45], showed that in recrystallized AlCoCrFeNi 2.1, the texture of the
f.c.c. phase had a strong α-ﬁber component while the B2 phase had a
strong {111}〈 011〉 component.
6. Nitriding
Meng and Baker [ 76] nitrided both Fe 30Ni20Mn35Al15 and
Fe28.2Ni18.8Mn32.9Al14.1Cr6 f.c.c. + B2 lamellar MPCAs at either 1073 K
or 1273 K in pure nitrogen. In all cases, the nitriding produced sub-
micron-sized AlN particles near the surface and depleted the nearby
matrix so completely of aluminum that it became single phase f.c.c.
Nevertheless, the nitriding produced a substantial increase in hardness.
For example, nitriding Fe
28.2Ni18.8Mn32.9Al14.1Cr6 at 1073 K for 100 h
produced an increase in hardness from the as-cast state value of 261 HV
to 364 HV.
7. Conclusions
A eutectic MPCA was ﬁrst produced in 2008 [ 19]. Since then there
has been a growing number of papers on developing these materials for
structural applications. A review of the literature has revealed that:
1. Eutectic MPCAs can show high ambient temperature yield strengths
that increase with decreasing interlamellar spacing according to
either λ
−1/2 or λ−1, similar to the behavior observed in pearlitic
steels [ 2,20–22].
2. Good ambient temperature tensile ductility has been observed in
eutectic MPCAs only when the more easily deformed phase is f.c.c.

3. As for traditional alloys, there is a trade-o ﬀ between increased
ambient temperature yield strength and reduced tensile ductility.
4. The limited data on the temperature dependence of the yield
strength show that it decreases with increasing temperature, and is
related to the softening of the harder phase.
5. As-cast eutectic MPCAs are typically not at equilibrium and further
annealing can produce precipitation of ﬁner particles, which further
increase the strength, often at the expense of a reduction in ductility.
6. Thermo-mechanical processing can transform lamellar eutectics into
equi-axed grain structures, with in some cases signi ﬁcantly im-
proved mechanical properties.
7. Nitriding has been shown to increase the hardness of aluminum-
containing MPCA through the formation of ﬁne AlN particles.
8. Several MPCAs have been produced that contain Co, Nb or both that
while scientiﬁcally interesting are unlikely to be useful as structural
materials due to the high cost of these elements (10 and 4.5 times
the price of nickel, respectively).
