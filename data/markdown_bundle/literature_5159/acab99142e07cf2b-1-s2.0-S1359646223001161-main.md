ABSTRACT 
The current study reveals the Hall-Petch relationship, or mean grain size control, and flow properties of 
equiatomic FeCoNi alloy having different grain sizes at room temperature (298 K) and cryogenic temperature 
(77 K). For the first time, various mean grain sizes ranging from the ultra-fine regime (0.7 μ m) to the coarse grain 
regime (145.3 μ m) were achieved in a FeCoNi alloy by high-pressure torsion and subsequent annealing. The 
tensile yield strength depends strongly on temperature and grain size. Hall-Petch plots demonstrate that the grain 
boundary strength coefficient is insensitive to the temperature, whereas friction stress increases as the temper -
ature decreases. For all the grain sizes both strength and ductility were increased with decreasing the temper -
ature down to 77 K. Dislocation slip is responsible for the room temperature mechanical properties. At cryogenic 
temperature, on the other hand, nano twinning appears as an additional deformation mechanism in addition to 
the dislocation slip. 
Often a conflict exists between strength and ductility/toughness in 
metallic materials [1] . However, the newly emerged equiatomic (or) 
high/medium entropy (or) complex concentrated alloys are found to 
show both high strength and large ductility accompanied by high 
toughness in a variety of environments [2 – 5] . For instance, 
face-centered cubic (FCC) equiatomic alloys such as CrMnFeCoNi and 
CoCrNi exhibit superior mechanical properties at both room and cryo -
genic temperatures because of the changing of deformation mechanism 
from dislocation-mediated plasticity to mechanical twinning [4 – 8] . The 
occurrence of mechanical twinning in these alloys is owing to their 
lower stacking fault energy (SFE), which is reported (at room temper -
ature) as 30 mJ/m
2 
for the CrMnFeCoNi alloy [6] and 22 mJ/m
2 
for the 
CoCrNi alloy [7] . On the other hand, all alloys in the CrMnFeCoNi family 
do not necessarily show mechanical twinning because some of the alloys 
have higher SFE, for example, FeMnNi, and FeCoNi [9 – 11] . The SFE of 
FeCoNi alloy is reported as ~70 mJ/m
2
; the dislocation slip is the pri -
mary plastic deformation mechanism in such a high SFE material [ 10 , 
12 ]. In spite of high SFE, FeCoNi alloy showed good strain hardening 
capacity similar to low SFE materials like CrMnFeCoNi alloy and CoCrNi 
alloy at cryogenic temperatures [9] . It should be noted that a reduction 
in deformation temperature would also decrease the SFE, and further 
which can affect the deformation mechanism of the alloy [13] . However, 
to what extent the SFE of the FeCoNi alloy will be reduced in cryogenic 
temperatures and the actual deformation mechanisms behind such 
interesting mechanical properties are yet to be investigated. 
Despite their good ductility, FeCoNi alloy have relatively low yield 
and ultimate tensile strengths, which limit their engineering applica -
tions [ 9 , 14 ]. The grain refinement approach based on the Hall-Petch 
relationship [ 15 , 16 ] is expected to be an effective strategy to attain 
good mechanical properties to the FeCoNi alloy, especially when the 
mean grain size reaches to ultra-fine grain (UFG) regimes. Polycrystals 
with a grain size in the range of 0.1 to 1 μ m are widely known as UFG 
[17] , while a grain size in the range of 1 to 10 μ m are commonly referred 
as fine grain size [ 18 , 19 ], and greater than 10 μ m is considered as coarse

Therefore, this study aims to fabricate the FeCoNi equiatomic alloy 
with various grain sizes ranging from coarse to ultra-fine and then 
investigate the yielding and flow properties at ambient (298 K) and 
cryogenic (77 K) temperatures. Further, precise Hall-Petch relationship 
will be determined at both temperatures to deepen our understanding 
regarding the mechanical properties and deformation mechanisms of 
FeCoNi equiatomic alloy, as well as the grain size and temperature 
effects.

Fig. 2 shows engineering stress-strain curves of the FeCoNi alloy 
having various mean grain sizes tested at 298 K (Fig. 2(a)) and 77 K 
(Fig. 2(b)). As seen in the tensile plots, with decreasing temperature 
from 298 K to 77 K, both the strength (yield and ultimate tensile 
strength) and ductility (uniform and total elongation) were found to 
increase, and their maximum values were observed at 77 K (Fig. S3). 
Further, yield strength (0.2% proof stress) monotonically increased with 
a decrease in mean grain size at both 298 K and 77 K. Yield drop was 
observed in the specimens with the mean grain size smaller than 2.0 μ m 
at both the tested temperatures. In contrast, typical continuous yielding 
was observed in the specimens with the mean grain size larger than 2.0 
μ m. In several cases, including equiatomic alloys, UFG materials (~1 
μ m) with a single-phase structure exhibit limited uniform elongation 
due to early plastic instability [ 14 , 25 , 26 ]. Such behavior was not 
observed in the present FeCoNi alloy ( Fig. 2 ). Despite its UFG size, the 
0.7 μ m specimen showed a good strain hardening ability. As a result, a 
large uniform elongation (32% at 298 K and 58% at 77 K) was observed 
in the 0.7 μ m specimen, which suggests that strain-hardening mecha -
nisms might differ with a decrease in grain size in the current alloy [27] .

occurrence of solid solution strengthening. To validate this assumption, 
the FCC equiatomic alloys-based solid strengthening model derived by

temperature on the deformation behavior of the FeCoNi alloy, 
strain-hardening rate curves of a specific mean grain size d~2.7 μ m, 
obtained at 298 K and 77 K are plotted in Fig. 5 (b), where true 
stress-strain curves are also shown. The strain hardening rate (d σ /d ε ) of 
the specimen tested at 298 K was gradually decreased as a function of 
true strain. In contrast, the specimen tested at 77 K maintained and 
recovered higher strain hardening values with respect to true strain than 
the specimen tested at 298 K. To reveal the reason for higher strain 
hardening capacity at 77 K, deformation microstructures were investi -
gated at both temperatures. Fig. 5 (c) and (d) show TEM-BF images of the 
d~2.7 μ m specimen deformed at 298 K and 77 K, respectively. The 
microstructures were observed near the tensile fractured area. Inter -
estingly, the deformed microstructure of the d~2.7 μ m specimen tested 
at 77 K showed both dislocations and nano-twins with approximately 20 
nm in thickness. At the same time, the room temperature deformed 
microstructure only revealed dislocation structures primarily having a 
wavy pattern. It indicates that nano-twins formed at 77 K could act as 
obstacles for dislocation motion leading to higher strain hardening ca -
pacity at cryogenic temperature in FeCoNi alloy. To understand the 
reason for the activation of mechanical twinning, specifically at the 
cryogenic temperature, we estimated the critical twinning stress for 
FeCoNi alloy based on the mean-field model [39 – 41] . The critical 
twinning stress was found that 866 MPa for d~2.7 μ m. This stress value 
is much higher than tensile flow stress obtained at 298 K ( Fig. 5 (b)). On 
the other hand, the tensile flow stress reaches the required critical 
twinning stress at 77 K ( Fig. 5 (b)), thus leading to deformation twinning 
in this alloy. The results in Fig. 5 (b)-(d) confirmed that the lower strain 
hardening of the FeCoNi alloy (d~2.7 μ m) at room temperature is 
caused by its dislocation slip only deformation mechanism, while the 
excellent strain hardening rate at cryogenic temperature is mainly 
attributed from the activation of nano twins in addition to dislocation 
slip. Moreover, the current study ( Fig. 5 (b)-(d)) shows one of the first 
experimental evidence of deformation twinning in FeCoNi alloy at 
cryogenic temperature (77 K). 
In summary, we successfully fabricated fully recrystallized FeCoNi 
equiatomic alloy specimens with mean grain sizes ranging from 0.7 to 
145 μ m. Subsequently, tensile properties were systematically investi -
gated at ambient (298 K) and cryogenic (77 K) temperatures. The tensile 
properties like strength and ductility of different mean grain-sized 
specimens were found superior at 77 K compared to 298 K. The pre -
cise Hall-Petch relationship was established at both temperatures and 
found friction stress ( σ
0

ture, and the grain boundary strengthening coefficient ( k

due to solid solution strengthening dictated by the local atomic distor -
tions, which is confirmed by theoretical validations using a model pro -
posed by Varvenne et al. [33] . The excellent strain hardening capacity of 
FeCoNi alloy at 77 K was attributed to the successful activation of 
deformation nano twins along with dislocation slip during deformation.
