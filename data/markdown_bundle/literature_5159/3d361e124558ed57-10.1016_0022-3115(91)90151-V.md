Empirical relations for tensile properties of austenitic stainless 
steels irradiated in mixed-spectrum reactors * 
M.L. Grossbeck 
Metals and Ceramics Dwision, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, P.O. Box 2008, Oak Ridge, TN 37831-6376, USA 
An assessment has been made of available tensile property data relevant to the design of fusion reactors, especially near 
term devices expected to operate at lower temperatures than power reactors. Empirical relations have been developed for the 
tensile properties as a function of irradiation temperature for neutron exposures of 10-15, 20, 30, and 50 dpa. It was found 
that yield strength depends little on the particular austenitic alloy and little on the helium concentration. Strength depends 
upon initial condition of the alloy only for exposures of less than 30 dpa. Uniform elongation was found to be more sensitive 
to alloy and condition. It was also more sensitive than strength to helium level. However, below 500°C, helium only appeared 
to have an effect at lo-15 dpa. At higher temperatures, helium embrittlement was apparent, and its threshold temperature 
decreased with increasing neutron exposure level. 
1. Introduction 
In order to provide design data for the ITER project, 
data from irradiations in mixed-spectrum reactors, 
where helium is produced simultaneously with displace- 
ment damage in Ni-containing alloys, were considered. 
Data from the US Fusion Program ORR (Oak Ridge 
Research Reactor) Spectral Tailoring Experiment was 
considered especially relevant since a He: dpa level 
typical of a fusion reactor was maintained. Fast reactor 
data from type 316 stainless steel (316 SS) irradiated in 
the EBR-II have been included in an effort to determine 
the importance of high levels of helium on tensile prop- 
erties. 
In most cases, the curve was fit by eye rather than by 
a regression program. Since the data were often very 
limited, from different sources, and had to be treated 
subjectively in relative importance, a statistical fit was 
not considered meaningful. In some cases, however, a 
regression analysis program was used for ease of de- 
termining parameters. 
Although very elegant models for predicting the 
tensile behavior of irradiated stainless steels are availa- 
ble, [1,2], they were found either not to extend to low 
temperatures or not to fit the recent data. In dismissing 
these models, multiple equations are then necessary to 
express tensile properties as functions of both damage 
level and temperature. As a result, separate expressions 
for each property as a function of temperature were 
developed for 10, 20, 30 and 50 dpa.

3. Correlation of data 
Difficulty arises in correlating data from different 
types and sizes of specimens. In evaluating strength, the 
size of the specimen should have little effect as long as 
the specimens are not so thin that surface effects be- 
come significant. Ductility is more difficult to evaluate. 
Fracture is complicated by the stress conditions, which 
can be plane stress or plane strain for the size of 
specimens used, depending upon the fracture toughness 
and strength of the material. However, the fracture 
mode and mechanism of fracture need not be com- 
pletely understood to obtain valid strength and ductil- 
ity. The issue of correlation of results between speci- 
mens of different geometry is important in determining 
ductility. Until necking occurs, there is good correlation 
between specimen types. The uniform deformation over 
the entire gage length results in a valid measure of 
tensile elongation. Therefore. no corrections were ap- 
plied to the uniform elongations of any specimens. 
However, when necking occurs and deformation be- 
comes restricted to a limited region of the specimen 
gage length, the measured elongation becomes a strong 
function of specimen geometry. Experimental evidence 
suggests that geometrically similar specimens give simi- 
lar results for ductility. More generally, results are simi- 
lar if the value of A0.5/1 is constant, where A is the gage 
area and I is the gage length [13].

significantly, but all are considered miniature. The 
specimens irradiated in the HFIR were either sheet

4. Results and discussion 
Mathematical expressions for tensile properties are 
presented in table 2. A representative plot is shown in 
fig. 1 where yield strength for lo-15 dpa is given. In the 
case of many properties, especially yield strength, the 
behavior of all alloys was so similar that only one or 
two curves were drawn. Although ultimate tensile 
strength has not been plotted, for exposures above 10 
dpa, uniform elongation is usually sufficiently low that 
ultimate tensile and yield strengths are nearly equal. 
Both 316 SS and PCA in annealed and cold-worked 
conditions are plotted on the same graphs. The dif- 
ference between annealed and cold-worked material is 
very apparent at 10 dpa and discernible at 20 dpa but 
insignificant at 30 and 50 dpa. This trend is known 
from fast reactor irradiation experiments [2]. Annealed 
material hardens and cold-worked material softens as 
irradiation proceeds. This latter trend is also apparent 
from the present data, which show that the yield strength 
of 20% cold-worked material decreases approximately 
30% from 10 to 50 dpa. There appears to be little 
difference in strength between all austenitic alloys and 
all heats of the alloys, perhaps with the exception of the 
316 SS irradiated in EBR-II. However the lower strength 
observed in the EBR-II-irradiated alloy could have re- 
sulted from the much lower concentration of helium

Uniform elongation is of the order of 0.2-0.38 from 
50 to 400” C at 10 dpa (fig. 2) and 20 dpa. A slight 
recovery is observed at 30 dpa where values in the same 
temperature range are nearly 1%. for both annealed and 
cold-worked alloys, fig. 3. Recovery appears to have 
progressed further by 50 dpa where uniform elongation 
is several percent at 55°C. Only cold-worked JPCA is 
available for 55°C at 50 dpa, however, it is the irradia- 
tion-induced dislocation structure that is recovering so 
that no significant differences are expected between 
cold-worked and annealed alloys. 
At all damage levels but 50 dpa, uniform elongation 
begins to rise at about 400 o C. Of particular interest in 
the 10 dpa graph are data from the ORR spectral 
tailoring experiment. These specimens have a He : dpa 
value characteristic of a fusion reactor. The 316 SS 
exhibits a significantly higher ductility than PCA. Al- 
though this may be a valid trend, it cannot be verified 
from these data since the PCA has about 30% more 
helium than the 316 SS, resulting from a higher nickel 
concentration. Since it is at high temperatures where the 
difference between the two alloys becomes evident, the 
variation in helium concentration is a possible explana- 
tion for the observed difference. At 20 dpa the curves 
pass through a clear maximum and decrease above 
500 o C. This is believed to be a true helium effect which 
persists at 30 dpa. At 50 dpa the effect of helium is 
present to such an extent that the region of helium 
embrittlement extends below 400°C. This must be 
studied further with lower temperature tests at 50 dpa. 
Some of the specimens contain over 3000 appm helium 
at this exposure level. This is about five times the level 
expected in fusion reactor irradiation, but the curve is 
suggested for use as a very conservative estimate of 
ductility. It is also evident that the EBR-II-irradiated 
316 SS has high ductility at the highest temperatures 
investigated. Since the helium concentration is low, this 
is further evidence that helium is the embrittling agent 
in this regime. 
Low temperature plastic instability, evidenced by 
low uniform elongation, is not indicated by total elon- 
gation. The material loses its ability to work harden but 
not its ability to plastically deform. Total elongation at 
55°C is 8-17% throughout the range of lo-50 dpa. At 
600°C and above, total elongation falls below 1% at 20 
dpa and above due to helium embrittlement. There is a 
discernible difference in the total elongations of an- 
nealed and cold-worked material, more so than with the 
other tensile properties. Since the cold-worked structure 
is not expected to recover significantly between 10 and 
30 dpa, the 30 dpa value for total elongation has been 
used for the cold-worked material at 10 dpa. The 30 dpa 
values have also been used as guidance in developing 
the curves for 20 dpa. Further research remains to 
confirm these assumptions, but they are expected to be 
conservative.

Equations for post-irradiation tensile properties of 
the austenitic stainless steels AISI type 316 and 
PCA have been developed for use by designers of 
near-term fusion devices. 
Strength is not significantly affected by the He : dpa 
value. 
The particular type of austenitic alloy is not a major 
factor in tensile behavior. 
The initial condition is not important for displace- 
ment levels of 30 dpa or higher. 
Helium appears to reduce uniform elongation at 
lo-15 dpa above 400°C but at higher exposures, 
little difference is seen because even fast reactors 
introduce sufficient helium to reduce elongation. 
High temperature helium embrittlement is evi- 
denced by a shift of peak uniform elongation to 
lower temperatures as dpa increases. The peak ap- 
pears to vanish at about 50 dpa and 3000 appm He.
