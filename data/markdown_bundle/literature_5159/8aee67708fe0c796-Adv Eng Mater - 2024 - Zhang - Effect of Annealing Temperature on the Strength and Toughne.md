1. Introduction
The mechanical properties of titanium alloys are signi ﬁcantly
dependent on the microstructural characteristics, such as the con-
tent, morphology, and distribution ofα phase and β grain size.[1–3]
The titanium alloy with high strength and toughness has a good
balance of strength and plasticity, which is mostly attributed to
the precipitation strengthening induced by the decomposition
of metastable β phase.[4] The microstructures of the alloys can
be signi ﬁcantly altered after the heat treatment. A speci ﬁc
heat-treatment process can obtain speci ﬁc
microstructure and mechanical proper-
ties.[5,6] In terms of the metastable β alloys,
the traditional heat treatments mainly
includes two steps: solution treatment and
aging.[7] As the microstructure of titanium
alloys are increasingly sensitive to the heat
treatments, many researchers have investi-
gated the effects of the varied heat treat-
ments on the microstructure, aiming to
acquire an optimized mechanical proper-
ties. Compared with the traditional solid
solution þ aging heat treatment, in recent
years, material researchers have paid more
attention to the effects of multiple heat-
treatment processes on the microstructure
and properties, such as generally duplex
solid solution þ aging, solid solution þ
duplex aging (DAG) and solid solution þ
annealing þ aging.[1,8–14]
From the perspective of multiple
heat-treatment processes, many researchers have investigated
the microstructure properties of titanium alloys. For example,
Ivasishin et al. [15] conducted DAG on Ti-15-3 and β-21S alloys,
respectively. It was found that compared with single aging
(SAG), the yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength
(UTS) of Ti-15-3 alloy increased by about 60 MPa, and the elon-
gation (EL) and section shrinkage increased by 2.1% and 7%,
respectively. The strength of β-21S alloy is increased by about
135 MPa, and the plasticity is also improved. Hao et al. [16] carried
out a two-step aging heat treatment (650 °C/10 min þ 550 °C/3 h)
on Ti – 10V– 2Fe– 3Al alloy and obtained a multi-scale hetero-
geneous structure with coarse and ﬁne α precipitates.
Consequently, the ductility and strength increased by 50% and
10%, respectively, which could be largely associated with the
{1012} 〈1011〉 deformation twins and the high-density entangled
dislocations after deforming. Ye et al. [8] studied the solution þ
annealing þ aging (ST – AN– AG) heat treatment of TC21 tita-
nium alloy. Compared with the solution þ annealing (ST – AN)
treatment, there are a large number of ﬁne-dispersed α precip-
itates. The results indicated that strength is increased by about
75 MPa, while the plasticity is slightly reduced. Similarly, Du
et al. [9] studied the DAG treatment of Ti – 3.5Al– 5Mo– 6V– 3Cr–
2Sn– 0.5Fe alloy at 550 and 400 °C. The results suggested that
the tensile strength of the alloy is about 205 MPa higher than that
after SAG, while the EL decreases from 9.49% to 6.06%. Wang

Herein, it is aimed to reveal the effect of annealing temperature on α
microstructure parameters and mechanical properties of Ti-55531 alloy under the
multiple heat-treatment process of “solution (855 °C) þ annealing (700, 730,
760 °C) þ aging (600 °C). ” The microstructure is characterized by using optical
microscopy, scanning electron microscopy, and transmission electron micro-
scope. In the results, it is indicated that the annealing temperature does not
change the volume fraction of the αs phase of Ti-55531 alloy. The as-annealed
precipitation-free zone αs phase renucleates after aging treatment, resulting in a
bimodal distribution of αs phase in the matrix. The higher the annealing tem-
perature, the larger the average size of αs phase and the greater the heterogeneity
in the alloy. Additionally, the heterogeneity of αs phase affects further the
plasticity and the crack propagation path of the alloy. With the increase of
annealing temperature, the strength of Ti-55531 alloy decreases slowly, but the
elongation and the impact toughness of the alloy increases rapidly resulting from
the heterogeneity of αs phase under multiple heat-treatment conditions.

effective method to obtain excellent mechanical properties by
optimizing the microstructure parameters. Nevertheless, many
investigations of Ti-55531 titanium alloy are related to simple
solid solution plus aging treatment, for example, by altering solid
solution temperature, aging temperature, time, and cooling
method.[24] There are few reports of the effect of multiple heat
treatments on the microstructure evolution and mechanical
properties of Ti-55531 titanium alloy. Additionally, the relation-
ship between microstructure and macroscopic properties in
multiple heat treatments is not clear.
In this work, Ti-55531 titanium alloy was employed to inves-
tigate the multiple heat-treatment methods of “solid solution þ
annealing þ aging.” Here, the morphology and size of the α
phase were controlled by varying the annealing temperature.
Conventional sheet tensile, in situ tensile, and impact tests were
performed respectively. The results indicated that the heteroge-
neity of αs was dependent on the annealing temperatures, which
affected further the plasticity and the crack propagation path of
the alloy.

2.1. Materials and Heat Treatment

3. Results
3.1. Microstructural Evolution
The microstructures of Ti-55531 alloy after solution plus anneal-
ing treatments are shown in Figure 4 . As the temperature
increased from 700 to 760 °C, the thickness of αs phase of the
alloy increased rapidly, while a large range of precipitation-free
zones (PFZs) appeared. The volume fractions of αs phase in the
alloy were 33.32% (700 °C), 28.8% (730 °C), and 11.8% (760 °C)
based on counting by Image-Pro-Plus 6.0 analysis software,
respectively.
Figure 5 shows the microstructure of Ti-55531 alloy aged at
600 °C for 6 h after annealing treatment at different tempera-
tures. The statistical results of the β grain size, the width and
length of αs, and the content of α phase are shown in
Table 2 . Comparing with the annealed alloy, it is found that
the lath and volume fraction of αs phase of the alloy increases
after aging treatment. The ﬁne tertiary α phase was reprecipitated
in the PFZ after annealing treatment at 760 °C plus aging.
Finally, the microstructure with ﬁne αs mixed in coarse αs phase
formed. As the annealed (700 °C) and aged (600 °C) temperature
difference is small, there is little difference in the microstructural
characteristics (Figure 4a and 5a). Because the size of secondary
α formed at 700 °C annealing is similar to that of tertiary α
formed at 600 °C aging, although the volume fraction of α phase
increases after aging, it is dif ﬁcult to distinguish whether it is
secondary α or tertiary α in size. Based on Table 2, with the
increase of annealing temperature, the average width of αs phase
(Wαs) increases from 0.10 to 0.17 μm, and the average length of
αs phase (Lαs) increases from 0.42 to 0.72 μm. The volume frac-
tion difference of total αs phase after aging is very small.
Gaussian ﬁtting is performed on the statistical results of length
and width with ≈150 αs laths, and the curve is shown in Figure 6.
Combined with Table 2, it can be seen that the peak values of
length and width of αs increase with the increase of annealing

3.3. Impact Toughness
Figure 13 a– c shows the displacement – load curves of the alloys
under multiple heat treatments at different annealing tempera-
tures. The black curves are the real-time test data, and the red
curves are the ﬁtting data, and the error is the ratio of the actual
total work consumed during the impact test to the theoretical
total work. During the impact test, there was no obvious yield
stage when the load increased to the maximum value; the load
then decreased sharply, indicating brittle fracture mode. [31] The
maximum load Fm is de ﬁned as the critical point to distinguish
the crack initiation and the crack propagation stage. The decreas-
ing of the load from Fm indicates that the crack is formed and
begins to expand; [32] the Fm is 29.65, 34.53, and 36.20 KN for the
three kinds of samples, respectively. The impact resistance
increases while the strength decreases with the increase
of annealing temperature. This typical strength – toughness
trade-off relationship can be frequently observed in alloys. [33]
The statistical results of the crack initiation energy ( Wi) and crack
propagation energy ( Wp) are shown in Figure 13d. The crack
initiation energy at different annealing temperatures accounts
for 87.97%, 83.48%, and 88.33% of the total impact energy,
respectively. Clearly, the crack initiation work dominates the total
consumed energy. In addition, the improvement in impact
energy was attributed to the increase in crack initiation energy.

4. Discussion
In this study, the effect of annealing temperature on the
microstructure, strength, and toughness of Ti-55531 alloy after
multiple heat treatments was investigated. According to the
experimental results, the microstructure annealed at different
temperatures is signi ﬁcantly different. The strength decreases
gently, while the plasticity increases signi ﬁcantly after annealing
at 760 °C. The higher the annealing temperature, the higher
impact toughness of the sample. The change trend of impact
toughness is the same as the plastic. In addition, the crack prop-
agation path in in situ tension is also different due to the forma-
tion of heterogeneous microstructure. Therefore, it is necessary
to discuss the formation of heterogeneous microstructure and its
relationship with mechanical properties.
4.1. Microstructure Analysis
The formation of coarse and ﬁne αs distribution and content is
mainly controlled by the phase transition, which is largely
affected by nucleation rate and element diffusion rate. [14,36]
In general, low annealing temperature is related to the high
driving force of phase transition, which could increase the
nucleation rate of α phase but reduce the element diffusion rate.
Finally, the α phases can be precipitated adequately resulting in
the high volume fraction and small αs. Otherwise, the higher
annealing temperature is associated with large size and low vol-
ume fraction of αs.
The heterogeneity of the microstructure increases with the
increase of annealing temperature. However, the annealing tem-
perature has little effect on the ﬁnal volume fraction of αs. The
annealed microstructures (Figure 4) and aged microstructures
(Figure 5) revealed that annealing treatments played an impor-
tant role in the precipitation of α phase. The morphology of α
phase is mainly affected by the growth of αs. For example, the
precipitation of α phase is severely inhibited for samples anneal-
ing at 760 °C, resulting in the formation of the PFZ. In addition,
the rapid diffusion rate leads to the coarsening of αs.[37,38] During
the aging process, the precipitation of the α phase not only
depends on the growth of αs but also is related to new αs precip-
itates (Figure 7b). Therefore, the heterogeneity increases and its
width and length distribution also show bimodal characteristics.
4.2. Effect of Heterogeneous Structure on Strength and
Plasticity
The strength of titanium alloys usually increases with the increase
of the volume fraction of the α phase, and it decreases with the
increase of the large-scaleα phase. However, the plasticity presents
opposite trend. Therefore, the increase of strength is usually accom-
panied by a decrease of plasticity. [8,10,14] As seen in Figure 4 and
Table 2, the volume fraction of the α phase has no clear difference
at different annealing temperatures. Thus, the change of strength
and plasticity of the alloy is mainly affected by the size of the α
phase. The large αs formed after annealing at 760 °C can provide
a longer slip distance for dislocations during plastic deformation.[19]
Furthermore, the deformation twins and interface coherence in the
coarseα phase in the heterogeneous structure may reduce the stress
concentration at theα/β interface,[17] resulting in compatible defor-
mation between the coarse α phase and the β phase, thereby
improving the plasticity of the alloy.
With the increase of annealing temperature, the strength
decreases slowly and the plasticity increases gradually. The pre-
vious results indicated that the average size of αs is an effective
factor to control the strength. With the increase of annealing tem-
perature, the average size of αs increases, which results in the
decreased α/β interface, the hindrance to dislocation decreases,
and then the dislocation density in αs phase increases. This effect
belongs to the ﬁne-grain strengthening effect and can be
explained by the Hall – Petch relationship in Figure 9. [36]
However, the plasticity is mainly determined by the size of αs.
With the increase of annealing temperature, the heterogeneity
of αs lath increases, which is bene ﬁcial to improve plasticity.

4.4. Effect of Heterogeneous Microstructure on Impact
Toughness
The impact toughness of the sample increases with the increase
of annealing temperature. The formation of large αs and the
increase of heterogeneity of αs will be bene ﬁcial to the improve-
ment of toughness. Large-size αs deformation can form longer
shear bands, thereby accommodating microstructure deforma-
tion and relaxing stress (Figure 15a – c). The degree of deforma-
tion of the microstructure is related to the size of the plastic zone
at the crack tip.[41] Studies have shown that[42] the size of the plas-
tic zone at the crack tip is proportional to the reciprocal of the
square root of the YS. Therefore, the decrease in strength will
lead to an increase in the plastic zone at the crack tip, the energy
required for plastic deformation increases during crack propaga-
tion, which is the same as the aforementioned changes in
strength and toughness. In addition, the deformation mecha-
nism in the process of high-speed impact deformation must also
be considered. Since the dislocations in the severe plastic defor-
mation zone do not have enough time to slip, and deformation
twins are another deformation mode activated at the crack tip in
addition to dislocations, which can not only effectively release
stress strengthening , [43] but also increase the strain hardening
effect and inhibit the plastic instability at the crack tip. [44] In pre-
vious studies, it has been observed that large α phase can trigger
deformation twins to coordinate deformation and delay crack ini-
tiation.[17,21] From the previous analysis results, the reasonable
explanation of the change law of impact toughness lies in two
aspects. First, with the increase of annealing temperature, the
plastic deformation zone at the crack tip increases. Second,
the large αs in the heterogeneous microstructure can inhibit
the crack instability and propagation, resulting in the increase
of crack initiation work and the increase of impact toughness.
5. Conclusion
1) Under multiple heat treatments, the annealing temperature
does not change the volume fraction of the αs phase of the alloy,
which mainly affects the average size and heterogeneity of αs.
The higher the annealing temperature, the larger the average size
of αs and the greater the heterogeneity. 2) With the increase of
annealing temperature, the Lαs increases from 0.42 to 0.72 μm,
the strength of Ti-55531 alloy decreases slowly (the UTS
decreased by 2.67% and 3.61%, respectively), the EL increases
rapidly (the EL increased by 15% and 82.5%, respectively),
and the increase of αs lath heterogeneity is bene ﬁcial to improve
the plasticity. The fracture mode is mainly intergranular fracture
and cleavage fracture and gradually transformed into micropo-
rous aggregation fracture. The Hall – Petch formula ﬁtting shows
that the average size of αs is an effective unit to control the
strength under the condition of changing the annealing temper-
ature of multiple heat treatments. 3) Microcracks mainly initiated
at the boundary of large-size αs and β phase. The orientation of
large-size αs is an important factor affecting the direction of crack
propagation. The crack passes through many large αs with differ-
ent orientations, which eventually leads to the tortuous crack
path of the heterogeneous structure. 4) The impact toughness
of Ti-55531 alloy increases with the increase of annealing tem-
perature. The brittle fracture occurs in the samples under impact
load, and most of the impact energy is consumed in the crack
initiation stage, and large-size αs is bene ﬁcial to coordinate
microstructure deformation and improve impact toughness of
the alloy.
