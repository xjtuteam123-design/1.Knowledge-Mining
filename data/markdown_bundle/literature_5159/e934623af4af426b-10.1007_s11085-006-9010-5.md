Strength and Corrosion Behavior of Fe–25Cr Alloys
under Various Strain Rates in Ar and N 2–0.1%SO2
at 973 K
Il Ryoung Sohn *,/C160,/C224and Toshio Narita *
Received December 9, 2005; revised June 30, 2006
The mechanical strength and corrosion behavior of Fe–25Cr alloys were
studied in Ar and in N 2–0.1SO2 at 973 K under strain rates of 2.7 /C2 10)4–
10)6/s. A 0.1 lm thick adhering Cr 2O3 layer is formed on the alloy by pre-
treatment in Ar. Scales formed in N 2–0.1SO2 are thicker with poorer adher-
ence to the metal substrate. The tensile strength is higher, about 9–19 MPa, in
Ar than in N 2–0.1SO2. However, the strain-rate sensitivities show similar
values in both environments. Deformation is concentrated near the grain
boundaries of the alloy, and it is more conspicuous at lower strain rates. Scale
growth is rapidly increased by the deformation in N
2–0.1SO2, and the scale
growth rate increases with the increase in strain rates. The scale growth rate is
higher at higher strain rates and the scale increases in sulﬁdes.
KEY WORDS: Fe–25Cr alloy; Ar; SO 2; high temperature; strength

Mechanical Behavior in Ar and in N 2–0.1SO2
Tensile tests were carried out in Ar and in N 2–0.1SO2 at 973 K,
following the pre-treatment. The strain rates were in the range of 2.7 /C2
10)4–10)6/s, and Fig. 4 shows stress–strain curves in both environments.
Tensile strength increased with the increase in strain rates in both envi-
ronments, and at the same strain rate, the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) is
higher (about 9–19 MPa (25–40%)) in Ar than in N
2–0.1SO2.
As will be shown in Fig. 9, the scale thickness is within 20 lm for the

load-bearing area of the substrate is less than 0.7%. This value is far too
small to explain the decrease in tensile strength in N 2–0.1SO2.

Many experimental studies 2–7 have shown that the formation of
thin oxide ﬁlms on alloys generally increases the strength of the alloys.
Mehdizadeh et al.
5 showed that a 0.3 lm-thick anodic oxide layer on an Al
single crystal directly inhibited dislocation slipping at the metal surface and
the strength of the material increased. It was suggested that the residual
stress of the coating would inhibit the motion of dislocations toward the
surface.
5
In some mathematical models, 11–13 it has been shown that a surface ﬁlm
with a shear modulus larger than that of the substrate exerts a long-range
attraction and a short-range repulsion force on near-surface screw disloca-
tions
11,12 and edge dislocations. 13 The dislocations are located at an equi-
librium position near the oxide/metal interface. These analyses indicate that
the formation of a thin oxide coating on a metallic substrate would create an
eﬀective barrier to dislocation egress from metallic substrates.
It is considered that the oxide layer formed in Ar may act as an eﬀective
barrier to restrain the migration of dislocations in the substrate toward the
surface. However, the oxide layer formed in N
2–0.1SO2 would not act as
such an eﬀective barrier because of numerous voids contained in the metal/
oxide interface causing the oxide layer to easily delaminate from the sub-
strate.
The tensile strength of the Fe–25Cr alloys at 973 K reaches its ultimate
value within 0.3% strain, and then stress is maintained, nearly unaﬀected by
the increase in the strain as shown in Fig. 4. This indicates that the test
temperature is high enough to activate internal diﬀusion in the ferritic steels,
so the strain-hardening eﬀect is speedily balanced with recovery in the
substrate.
The strength of the specimens depends on strain rates as shown in
Fig. 4, and grain-boundary sliding is more conspicuous at lower strain rates
as will be shown in Fig. 6. It is considered that grain boundaries are ther-
mally activated at the test temperature and that the process of grain-
boundary creep is prevalent at lower strain rates. It is considered that
the decrease in strength of this alloy at lower strain rate is due to
grain-boundary creep.
The dependence of material strength on strain rate has been represented
as a strain-rate sensitivity,

Multiple cracks form in the Cr
2O3 layer of the Fe–25Cr alloys due to
the tensile deformation in Ar. Figure 6 shows schematic sketches of the
surface of the specimens and SEM images of the cracked oxide layers, after

trated at the grain boundaries of the specimen. At higher strain rates
specimens deform uniformly throughout the specimen and the ﬁne cracks in
the oxide scale are more evenly formed. The crack patterns of the oxide
scales show that uniformity of deformation of the specimen depends on the
strain rates at higher strain rates.

CONCLUSIONS
The strength and corrosion behavior of Fe–25Cr alloys were investi-
gated in Ar and in N 2–0.1%SO2 at 973 K under strain rates of 2.7 /C2 10)4–
10)6/s. The results obtained may be summarized as follows:
(1) About a 0.1 lm-thick, dense Cr 2O3 layer formed on the Fe–25Cr
alloy in Ar after pre-treatment. The oxide maintains good contact
with the metallic substrate. In N
2–0.1SO2, about a 1 lm thick
Cr2O3 rich scale formed on the alloy after the pre-treatment. The

scale is easily delaminated from the substrate due to its poor
adherence.
(2) The ultimate tensile strength (UTS) of the specimens in Ar is about
9–19 MPa (25–40%) higher than those tested in N 2–0.1SO2 at
973 K under strain rates of 2.7 /C2 10)4–10)6/s. The UTS of the
specimens increase with the increase in the strain rate. The strain-
rate sensitivities are similar in both environments.
(3) Creep at grain boundaries is higher than in the grain interior of the
specimens here. Deformation concentrations at grain boundaries
are more conspicuous with decrease in the strain rate of the spec-
imens.
(4) The scale growth on the Fe–25Cr alloy in N

rapidly and formed a multi-layered structure at the grain bound-
aries rather than inside the grains because of the higher strain at
grain boundaries. Scales grew more uniformly throughout the
specimen with increase in strain rates.
