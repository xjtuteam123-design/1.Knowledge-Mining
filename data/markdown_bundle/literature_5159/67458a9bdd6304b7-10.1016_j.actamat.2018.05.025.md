Abstract: 
The distribution of Rhenium (Re) in a Ni-based sing le-crystal superalloy is studied by 
sub-angstrom resolution transmission electron micro scopy (TEM) and energy 
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). It is found that Re atoms segregate at the tensile 
stress regions near the interfacial dislocation cor es, forming the “Cottrell atmosphere”, 
and the segregation process is facilitated by dislo cation pipe diffusion. In situ TEM 
and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) straining st udies reveal that the

Re-decorated dislocation networks along the phase b oundaries act as mechanical 
walls that effectively block dislocation motion and crack propagation. Furthermore, 
the degree of Re segregation can be regulated by th ermal treatment. Theoretical 
analysis demonstrates that this remarkable alloying effect originated mainly from the 
interactions between the local composition strain o f Re and the dislocation strains, 
leading to significantly stabilized interfacial dis location networks. These results

properties in Ni-based superalloys and will be bene ficial to both improving creep

1. Introduction 
It is well-known that rhenium (Re) can significantly improve the creep properties 
of Ni-based superalloys, especially technologically important single-crystal 
superalloys [1-3]. For instance, creep rupture life was improved an order of magnitude 
from 250 h (first-generation alloy, no Re) to 2500 h (third-generation alloy, 6 wt.% Re) 
at 1023 K/500 MPa [4]. Since Giamei [1] first disco vered the “Re effect”, extensive 
efforts have been made to determine the spatial dis tribution of Re in superalloys and 
the mechanism of how Re affects the mechanical prop erties of superalloys. However,

the results often contradict each other, and still no conclusive understanding has been 
reached. For example, as Re atoms are prone to part ition in the γ matrix, the Re effect 
is speculated to originate from the solid strengthe ning effect on the γ phase. However, 
Re is not known as a strong solid strengthener in N i [5]. It has thus been conjectured 
that the spatial distribution of Re atoms is not ra ndom, but occurs in the form of 
nano-clusters, that might act as barriers to disloc ation motion [6-8]. However, some 
researchers have claimed that they were not able to find Re clusters in superalloys by 
using three-dimensional (3D) atom probe tomography (APT) [9] or extended X-ray 
absorption fine-structure spectroscopy [10]. Recent ly, Re segregation, accompanied 
by Co and Cr segregation, was found at the interfac ial dislocation cores in crept 
single-crystalline superalloys [11,12]. The Re segr egation may pin interfacial 
dislocations and hinder their motion, thereby enhan cing the creep resistance of 
superalloys [11,12]. Other researchers [13,14] have claimed that Re segregation at 
interfacial dislocations was not detected by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy 
(EDS) and APT. Therefore, both the spatial distribu tion of Re atoms and the 
mechanism for the “Re effect” in Ni-base superalloys are still unclear. 
According to the distribution analysis of Re in superalloys, several hypotheses on 
the Re effect have been proposed. These include: a) solution strengthening effect due 
to the large lattice distortion [9,10]; b) increasi ng lattice misfit between the γ and γ´ 
phases, resulting in a denser interfacial dislocati on network [15,16]; c) hindering the 
coarsening of the γ´ precipitate phase and dislocation climb due to th e slow diffusion 
coefficient of Re [17-20]; and d) decreasing the st acking fault energy of the γ phase

of those hypotheses have been experimentally valida ted. Specifically, while Re is 
found to be the most effective in the tertiary cree p stage, during which dislocations

impact remains unclear. To summarize, Re distributi on in superalloys and its impact 
on their mechanical properties are not well understood. 
 In this work, using a combination of sub-angstr om resolution analysis on the 
atomic structure, chemical distribution, and 3D def ect structures and in situ straining 
tests using transmission electron microscope (TEM) and scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM), we found that the location of Re atoms in a Ni-based superalloy 
has a significant influence on the dislocation moti on and thus the mechanical 
properties. Specifically, we demonstrated that Re a toms can be engineered into the 
cores of a network of interfacial dislocations alon g the phase boundaries between γ 
and γ´. Consequently, these boundaries acted as strong obstacles to dislocation motion 
as a result of the elastic interactions between the compositional strains of Re and 
elastic strains of the interfacial dislocation networks along the γ/γ´ phase boundaries.

3. Results 
3.1 Microstructures before and after aging process 
To mimic the real structure in practice, we enginee red interfacial dislocation 
networks into an alloy by an aging process. Fig. 1a and 1b show the typical 
microstructures of an alloy before and after aging. Before aging, cubic ordered γ´ 
precipitates dispersed in the disordered γ phase; the phase boundaries were smooth 
and highly coherent. As shown in the elemental dist ribution maps in Fig. 1c, elements 
were distributed heterogeneously in the two phases; Cr, Co, Re, and Mo were 
primarily partitioned into the γ phase while others into the γ´ phase. After aging, the 
shape of γ´ phase tended to be spherical, and hexagonal dislo cation networks formed 
along the γ/γ´ phase boundary. Tomography was carried out to investigate the location 
and structure of the dislocation network in three d imensions. It was found that the

3.2.1 EDS mapping 
EDS analysis on the interfacial dislocation network was carried out using 
Super-X, and the results are shown in Fig. 2a. It w as found that Cr, Co, and Re were

the interfacial dislocations with the beam directio n parallel to the interface to avoid 
possible artifacts.

3.2.2 Statistics of Re concentration at interfacial dislocation cores 
Although the structures of the interfacial dislocat ion networks were quite similar 
in the three samples, A, B, and C, due to the same ageing process, the concentration of 
Re at the dislocation cores is substantially differ ent. Statistical data of Re content 
from approximately 50 interfacial dislocation cores were quantitatively analyzed, and 
the results shown in Fig. 4a-c. Re content at the i nterfacial dislocation core of sample 
A, B, and C were 11-28 wt.% (average content: 17 wt .%), 22-34 wt.% (average 
content: 27 wt.%) and 26-44 wt.% (average content: 33 wt.%), respectively. Since the 
thermal treatments for all three samples were all t he same, the results imply that the 
cooling rate has a remarkable effect on Re concentr ation at the interfacial dislocation 
cores. The slower the cooling rate, the higher the Re content at the dislocation cores.

As the cooling rate was lowered, γ´ precipitates experienced more coarsening during 
which γ´-forming elements (Ni and Al) in the matrix diffus ed from γ toward γ´, while 
γ-forming elements including Re were “pushed-out” from the γ´ phase to the γ phase. 
Furthermore, the low diffusivity of Re [17,27] resu lted in much higher Re content at 
interfacial dislocation cores in samples with slowe r cooling rates. Re segregation at 
the interfacial dislocation cores of sample A, whos e cooling rate is approximately 
1000 °C/s, indicates that Re segregation is developed at high temperature. Presumably, 
the segregation was initiated by heating during whi ch the larger atoms, including Re, 
would tend to diffuse to the tensile region of a di slocation core, where the substitution 
volume is larger. Thus, if aging time is long enoug h, Re content at the core might 
reach “equilibrium concentration”. 
As superalloys are typically used for high temperat ure applications, it is critical 
to determine the possible variation of Re segregati on during reheating. A quantitative 
analysis of the reheated sample C (Fig. 4e and 4f) revealed that Re still segregates at 
the dislocation cores but with a lower concentratio n. The average Re content at the 
interfacial dislocation cores in sample E was 21 wt .%, which is even 24% higher than 
that in sample A. Interestingly, when longer aging time for bulk diffusion was 
provided, the average Re content at the interfacial dislocation cores in sample D (Fig. 
4d) was 18 wt.%, which is only 6% higher than that in sample A. It indicates that the 
diffusivity of Re at dislocation core is much highe r than the bulk diffusivity of Re. 
Consistent with the conclusion above, Re content at dislocation cores increase slowly 
during secondary aging process. For example, as the secondary aging time was

dimensions during aging.

Re segregation has a positive effect on the creep p roperties of superalloys [11,12]. 
However, this inference has never been confirmed by experiments. Thus, we studied

In sample A (Movie 2), dislocations slipped along t he [ 110] direction, directly

negligible. The glide of dislocations across the in terface not only broke the hexagonal

The directions of these steps were along the Burgers vector of the mobile dislocations, 
creating new segments of the γ/γ´ interface (marked by yellow arrows in Fig. 5a) th at 
provided an easy path for dislocations to shear bet ween the γ and γ´ phases. 
Ultimately, a straight crack formed, going directly through the two phases as shown in 
Fig. 5c. In contrast, as Re content at the cores in creased in sample C, the dislocation 
motion was fully constrained in the γ channels, as shown in Supplementary Movie 3.

4. Analysis and discussion 
 To analyze the influence of Re atoms on the pro perties of the γ/γ´ interface, we 
used a combined molecular dynamics and Monte Carlo method. The interatomic 
interactions in Ni-Al-Re were described by a recent ly developed

embedded-atom-method potential [30]. This potential produced a good fit to the basic 
lattice properties of pure Ni, Al, Re, and Ni 3Al. It also provided a good prediction that 
Re does not form clusters in Ni, which is consisten t with experimental observation [9] 
and ab initio calculations [31]. In our simulation, we first cre ated an a/2<110>-type 
edge dislocation at the γ/γ´ interface at 0 K, as shown in Fig. 7a. The simula tion box 
had an orientation of x-[110], y- , and z-[001] in dimensions of approximately 
24.2 nm × 24.0 nm × 2.9 nm. A periodic boundary con dition was applied along the 
dislocation line direction and free boundary condit ions were applied in the other two 
directions. In experiments, the mean distribution o f Re content at the dislocation core 
was about 30 wt.% in alloy C (see Fig. 4c). The maj ority of Re was segregated in the 
core region with a radius of 1 nm (see Fig. 3a), wh ich corresponds to a density of 40 
Re atoms per nm along the dislocation line. After creating the edge dislocation, the Re 
substitutions were then introduced randomly to the γ phase in the amount of NRe /L = 
40 nm -1, where NRe is the number of Re atoms and L is the dislocation line length. To 
optimize the Re distribution in the γ phase, we used Monte Carlo technique to search 
for the energy-favored configurations at 0 K. After minimization, we found that the 
Re atoms preferred to distribute around the disloca tion core (see Fig. 7b and 7c), in 
agreement with experimental observations. Based on these optimized configurations, 
we studied the influence of Re on the core structur e and the stress states of the 
dislocation. All the calculations were carried out using the LAMMPS code [32] and 
the atomic configurations were visualized via AtomEye [33].

5. Conclusions 
By using sub-angstrom resolution STEM and EDS techn iques, Re segregation at 
interfacial dislocation network has been quantitati vely studied in aged samples of a 
single-crystal superalloy. In situ TEM and SEM straining tests were conducted to 
unveil the influence of interactions between Re atoms and interfacial dislocation cores 
on mobile dislocations in the 
γ phase. A combination of molecular dynamics and 
Monte Carlo method was used to probe the influence of Re atoms on the properties of 
the γ/γ´ interface. 
The main conclusions can be summarized as follows: 
(1) Re atoms segregate to the tensile regions of interf acial dislocation cores at 
high temperature. At dislocation cores, the diffusi vity of Re was dramatically 
increased due to the pipe diffusion effect, which m ight facilitate the 
segregation of Re along the dislocation lines. 
(2) Reducing cooling rate is a rapid and effective way to increase the Re 
concentration at interfacial dislocation cores. Re segregation can move with 
the dislocations during the migration of γ/γ´ interface. 
(3) Re segregation substantially strengthens the γ/γ´ interface. Interfaces 
decorated with higher concentration of Re on the di slocation network can be 
more efficient in hindering the shear of mobile dislocations. 
(4) Dislocation energy drops 152.7 eV per nm on average after adding Re. 
However, Re segregation at the core has no signific ant effect on the core

of phase boundaries is the stabilization of the int erfacial dislocation network, 
which may be the origin of the Re effect in superalloys. 
 Our results not only help explain the important “Re effect” in Ni-based 
superalloys, but also provide a new perspective on improving creep properties of 
Ni-based superalloys.
