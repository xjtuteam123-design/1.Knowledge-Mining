ABSTRACT 
In this study, Nb521 (Nb – 5W – 2Mo – 1Zr) parts were successfully fabricated by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) 
using quasi-spherical hydride-dehydride (HDH) powders modified by jet milling. The relative density of the LPBF 
made (LPBFed) Nb521 parts is 98.8 ± 0.2%, which were subsequently subjected to hot isostatic pressing (HIP) to 
achieve almost full densification. Nanoscale ZrO
2 
precipitates were found in the LPBFed parts and those after HIP 
(HIPed), showing semi-coherent with the matrix. The precipitation behavior is strongly related to the melt pool 
solidification process during LPBF in terms of thermal distribution and solidification rate. The LPBFed Nb521 
parts after HIP presented sound tensile properties with the fracture strength of 678.7 ± 1.1 MPa and elongation 
of 5.91 ± 0.32%. The precipitated nanoscale ZrO
2 
underwent plastic deformation after tension, exhibiting the 
stress-induced tetragonal-to-monoclinic phase transformation and shearing by dislocations. This work presents 
the LPBFed Nb521 alloy with promising performance by employing low-cost jet-milled HDH powders. 
1. Introduction 
Due to excellent high-temperature strength, low density in refractory 
alloys, superior heat and corrosion resistance, niobium (Nb) alloys are 
promising in usage for high-temperature service mainly in aerospace 
applications [ 1 – 3 ]. Nb521 (Nb – 5W – 2Mo – 1Zr), a typical 
dispersion-strengthened Nb alloy, has been widely used in 
high-temperature sustained propulsion systems [ 4 , 5 ]. Although Nb521 
is machinable through conventional forging and machining, it still re -
mains intractable to fabricate sophisticated-geometric and affordable 
parts to meet the growing demand of lightweight design. Additive 
manufacturing (AM), however, provides an integrative solution to 
directly produce Nb521 parts with advantages of complexed geometry, 
cost saving and high efficiency [ 6 – 8 ], as compared with traditional 
manufacturing methods. 
To date, there have been a few trials on Nb alloys via AM such as 
Nb521 [ 9 ] and C103 (Nb-10Hf-1Ti) [ 6 , 10 , 11 ] in terms of processing, 
microstructures, and mechanical properties. For instance, Yang et al. [ 9 ] 
fabricated the Nb521 alloy by electron beam melting (EBM) with 
focusing on the investigation of (Nb, Zr)C and Nb
2
C precipitation 
behavior. Additionally, Philips et al. [ 10 , 11 ] utilized atomized powders 
to produce the C103 alloy by both laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) and 
EBM. The additive manufactured (AMed) C103 alloy exhibited compa -
rable performance to equivalent wrought material [ 6 , 10 , 11 ]. In addi -
tion, Refs . [ 7 , 12 , 13 ] suggest that the parts made by LPBF show fine 
geometric features and sound surface finish. Thus, above works 
demonstrate that LPBF of Nb-based refractory alloys is feasible and 
effective from the engineering viewpoint in the aerospace field that re -
quires sophisticated geometry and smooth surface finish. One crucial 
issue remained in the current reports [ 14 – 16 ], however, is that 
employing spherical powders of Nb-based alloys as the feedstock for AM 
e.g. , LPBF pushes up the budget for final counterparts. To some extent, 
the high-cost powder feedstock constrains the extensive deployment and 
future scale-up of Nb-based alloys by AM.

Recently, we developed a novel and facile method i.e. , jet milling to 
modify low-cost hydride-dehydride (HDH) CP-Ti powders that were 
successfully employed in LPBF [ 17 ]. Herein, jet milling is conducted in 
an inert gas environment in a few minutes, where intensive shape 
modification takes place due to collision and friction among powders 
[ 18 ]. The resultant particle flowability fulfills the acquirement of AM yet 
with acceptable impurity control owing to the usage of inert gas. Like -
wise, the present study used jet milling to produce HDH Nb521 powders 
that were subsequently employed as the raw feedstock in LPBF. The 
objective of this work is to investigate microstructures and tensile 
properties of the LPBF made (LPBFed) Nb521 alloys with a particular 
focus on the precipitation effect. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Powder feedstock 
The raw Nb521 alloy powders were house made by the hydrogena -
tion and dehydrogenation process using the pre-alloyed plates pur -
chased from Shaanxi Xingshuo New Material Technology Co., Ltd. P.R. 
China. Then, jet milling was used to modify powders to improve the 
sphericity and flowability. Detailed processing information of jet milling 
can be found in Ref . [ 17 ]. Chemical compositions of the jet-milled HDH 
Nb521 powders are shown in Table 1 with the oxygen level of 0.18 wt%. 
The mean particle size (D
50

determined using a laser diffraction analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, UK). 
Particle flowability was determined as per the ASTM B213-13 standard 
using a Hall flowmeter. 
2.2. Laser powder bed fusion 
The jet-milled Nb521 powders were employed as raw feedstock for 
LPBF processing (SLM Solutions 125HL, Germany) equipped with a 400 
W fiber laser in the continuous-wave mode. The focus diameter of laser 
is approximately 75 μ m, while high-purity argon gas flow was applied 
during the entire LPBF process. Prior to processing, the Nb521 substrate 
was preheated to 473 K to reduce residual stress. LPBF processing pa -
rameters were chosen with a powder layer thickness of 30 μ m, a laser 
power of 180 W, a scanning speed of 400 mm/s, and a hatch distance of 
0.12 mm, respectively. The scanning strategy was chosen as the zigzag 
pattern, while the scanning direction between each layer was increased 
by 33
◦

◦

parts were subjected to HIP at 1773 K with a pressure of 150 MPa for 2 h 
to achieve full densification and alleviate the internal stress caused by 
rapid cooling during LPBF. Here, the nomenclature of HIPed is abbre -
viated for the LPBFed parts after the HIP treatment.

tion. Density and porosity of the fabricated parts were determined using 
the Archimedes method as specified in the ASTM B962 standard. Mi -
crostructures of the fabricated parts were observed by scanning electron

equipped with an electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) detector

Images of high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) 
and elemental distribution data were determined using a transmission 
electron microscope (TEM, FEI Tecnai G2 F20, USA) equipped with an

Germany). TEM samples perpendicular to the building direction (BD) 
were prepared by mechanically grinding to a thickness below 80 μ m and 
then thinning by ion milling (Gatan 691, USA). 
2.4. Tension test 
The HIPed Nb521 sheets perpendicular to the BD were cut into 
tensile bars with a gauge length of 10.0 mm via a wire cutter. Tensile 
properties of the Nb521 bars were determined on an universal testing 
machine (WDW-10E, P.R. China) using an extensometer with a cross- 
head speed of 0.4 mm/min at room temperature. Three samples were 
tested to ensure the value repeatability for each experiment, and average 
tensile properties were given.

3.2. Microstructural characterization 
In this work, the optimal relative density of HIPed part reaches 99.2 
± 0.1%. Fig. 2 presents the EBSD analysis of the LPBFed ( Fig. 2 (a) and 
(b)) and HIPed ( Fig. 2 (c) and (d)) samples in terms of crystal orientation 
map and pole figure. The microstructure for the LPBFed and HIPed 
samples seems similar with each other. It can be observed from Fig. 2 (a) 
and (c) that there are a few equiaxed grains at the melt pool boundaries 
for both samples. By contrast, more columnar grains with a larger size 
are distributed inside the melt pool. The bi-modal microstructure may be 
related to the thermal profile associated with the cooling rate difference 
at varying regions in the melt pool during LPBF [ 22 , 23 ]. From the 
crystal orientation maps parallel to the BD, the columnar crystals grew 
either along the BD or opposite to the heat flux direction. The pole fig -
ures of the LPBFed and HIPed samples perpendicular to the BD in Fig. 2 
(b) and (d) do not exhibit preferred texture orientation. However, in the 
case of parallel to the BD, there is a slight (100) < 111 > texture for the 
LPBFed sample ( Fig. 2 (b)), while it is weakened after HIP as proved in 
Fig. 2 (d). 
Fig. 3 shows the representative SEM images of the LPBFed and HIPed 
samples. The grain size along the BD for both samples is roughly in the 
range of 20 – 80 μ m as indicated in Fig. 3 (a) and (c), while the melt pool 
width ranges from 130 – 160 μ m ( Fig. 3 (b) and (d)). Further careful 
observation of Fig. 3 (a1) and 3(c1) shows there exist clusters of pre -
cipitates dispersed in the Nb solid solution (Nbss) matrix. These quasi- 
spherical precipitates are with the grain size of ca. 50 nm. It is noted 
that the precipitate population is different at each side nearby fusion 
boundaries (FBs) i.e. , more precipitates with a larger size formed at the 
inner bottom of melt pool as compared to its center area as seen in Fig. 3 
(b1) and 3(d1). In other words, the precipitation behavior is different in 
the two particular regions for both samples. Plus, it can be concluded 
from Fig. 3 that the microstructure is similar for the LPBFed and HIPed 
samples. To investigate the composition of the nanoscale precipitates, 
EDS elemental mapping was conducted for the HIPed part as presented 
in Fig. 3 (e), which indicates the existence of oxygen segregation within 
or nearby the precipitates. 
To further confirm the constituent and crystal structure of the 
nanoscale quasi-spherical precipitates, TEM was carried out to investi -
gate for the HIPed sample. It can be seen from Fig. 4 (a) that there are a 
few quasi-spherical precipitates with the size of ca. 50 nm dispersed in 
the Nbss matrix, consistent with the findings in Fig. 3 (a1) and 3(c1). The 
corresponding EDS maps indicate the precipitate is consisted of Zr and O 
elements, as shown in Fig. 4 (b). Fig. 4 (d) is the interface between the 
precipitate and Nbss matrix in Fig. 4 (c), which exhibits a well-bonded 
relationship. The fast fourier transform (FFT) analysis in Fig. 4 (e)

4. Discussion 
4.1. Precipitation behavior 
In the case of Nb521 alloy, alloying elements of W and Mo are infinite 
solid solution in the Nb matrix [ 25 , 26 ]. It has been reported, however, 
the active elemental Zr may prefer to potentially react with interstitial 
elements e.g. , O and C to form non-metallic compounds under certain 
circumstances [ 27 , 28 ]. It has been reported [ 29 ] that the Zr could 
saliently reduce the oxygen solubility via forming ZrO
2
, e.g. , an addition 
of 1 wt% Zr lowering the solubility of oxygen in Nb at 1273 K from 3800 
ppm to below 100 ppm. According to the Zr – O binary phase diagram in 
Fig. 6 (a), the solubility limit of oxygen atoms in the α -Zr phase is ca. 32 
at.% when the temperature is ~2276 K. Herein, assuming that oxygen 
atoms were all solid dissolved in the Zr matrix lattice, the calculated

to saturate with the elemental Zr to form the oxide phase ( e.g. , ZrO

5. Conclusions 
In summary, we successfully fabricated the Nb521 alloy by LPBF 
using jet-milled HDH powders. Microstructures of the LPBFed and HIPed 
samples, precipitation behavior of nanoscale ZrO
2 
particles and its effect 
on the mechanical properties were investigated. The main findings can 
be summarized as follows: 
(1) The precipitation behavior of nanoscale ZrO
2 
particles is inves -
tigated by thermodynamics and kinetics associated with the so -
lidification process in terms of temperature gradient and 
solidification rate within the melt pool during LPBF.
