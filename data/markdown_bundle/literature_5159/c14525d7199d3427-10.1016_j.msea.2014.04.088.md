abstract
Bulk (Ti70.5Fe29.5)100/C0 xNbx (x¼ 0, 3, 5 and 7 at%) alloys were prepared by cold crucible levitation melting,
and their mechanical properties were tested in compression at room temperature. A (Ti 70.5Fe29.5)97Nb3
alloy specimen in compression exhibited an ultimate compressive strength of 2.53 GPa and a
compressive plastic strain of 15%. Electron microscope observations indicated that lamellar structures
present in the eutectic Ti
70.5Fe29.5 alloy could be modi ﬁed by the addition of Nb to obtain a bimodal
structure. The improvement of the mechanical properties is attributed to two factors: (1) the bimodal
phase size distribution with micrometer-sized primary β-Ti dendrites embedded inside a matrix of
reﬁned ultra ﬁne eutectics ( β-TiþTiFe), and (2) the larger lattice mismatches between the β-Ti and TiFe
phases in Nb-modi ﬁed eutectic Ti – Fe alloys that introduce coherency strain at the interface. The
orientation relationship of A2 β-Ti with B2 TiFe in binary and Nb-modi ﬁed Ti– Fe alloys is TiFe (1 10)[001]
|| β-Ti (1 10)[001].

1. Introduction
Nanocrystalline (nc) and ultra ﬁne-grained (ufg) materials are
typically much stronger than conventional materials; however,
their limited plasticity makes them vulnerable to catastrophic
failure, so they are not widely used in engineering applications
[1]. For this reason, improving the room temperature plasticity of
nc and ufg materials has become a key issue in the development of
advanced structural materials. For instance, by creating a mixture
of two or multiple phases with microscale grains precipitated
within nanostructured grains, a Ti 60Cu14Ni12Sn2Nb10 alloy showed
high strength (about 2.4 GPa) and signi ﬁcant compressive plastic
strain (15%) [1– 3]. In addition, materials with nano-twins, disper-
sions of nanoparticles and nanoprecipitation, and phase-
transformation and twinning-induced plasticity can also display
high strength and ductility [3,4]. Recently, ultra ﬁne Ti – Fe alloys
have been developed with strength of /C24 2 GPa and reasonable
plasticity of 4 – 7% in compression [5,6]. To further improve the
mechanical properties of Ti – Fe alloys, alloying elements such as
Sn, Nb, Ta, and Co are added to re ﬁne the eutectic lamellar spacing
and stabilize the β-Ti phase [7– 12]. Das et al. reported that the
addition of 3.85 at% Sn to eutectic Ti 70.5Fe29.5 alloy produced
pronounced eutectic colony boundaries and re ﬁned interlamellar
spacing that increased both strength and plastic strain [7,8].
Similar strategies have been followed to prepare hypoeutectic
Ti– Fe– (Ta) alloys for high yield strength (2 GPa) and plasticity of
5– 8% [1 1]. In another study, Nb was added to hypereutectic
Ti65Fe35 allowing the alloy to form a pseudo-binary eutectic
structure due to the large solubility of Nb in Ti; the alloy has still
higher yield strength (2.2 GPa) and enhanced plastic strain (13%)
[12]. The presence of micrometer-sized dendrites/proeutectic
phase is essential to avoid catastrophic failure by their interaction
with shear bands during deformation [8,13,14]. This scenario
suggests that the strength and the plasticity of ufg and nc
materials could be controlled by introducing different length-
scale heterogeneities. The formation of a bimodal eutectic struc-
ture in (Ti 70.5Fe29.5)Sn9 alloy raised the macroscopic plasticity to
16% while preserving an ultimate strength of 2.26 GPa [15,16].N b
is a β-Ti stabilizer, and the Ti – Nb binary phase diagram [17] shows
that Ti and Nb can form continuous solid solutions. Therefore,
there are reasons to believe that Nb addition might enhance
the strength and improve the plasticity of the eutectic Ti – Fe alloy
by bimodal structure formation. In the present study, we report
on a systematic investigation of the microstructural evolution,

2. Experimental procedure
Ultraﬁne (Ti70.5Fe29.5)100/C0 xNbx (x¼0, 3, 5 and 7 at%) alloys were
fabricated from Ti, Fe, and Nb of 99.99 wt% purity by cold crucible
levitation melting under an Ar atmosphere on a water-cooled Cu
crucible. The ingots were turned over and re-melted four times to
ensure homogeneous compositions. D/MAX-3C X-ray diffraction
(XRD) using Cu Kα radiation was utilized for structural investiga-
tions of the as-cast samples. The compression samples were cut
from the middle of the as-cast specimens by electrical-discharge
machining (EDM) to produce cylinders 3 mm in diameter and
6 mm long. The room temperature compression tests were con-
ducted with a CMT5305 electronic universal testing machine at a
strain rate of 1 /C2 10/C0 3 s/C0 1 [18]. In general, a compressive test is
not as susceptible as tensile testing to minor processing ﬂaws that
lead to premature failure. As such, compression test results can
reveal the intrinsic behavior of the materials [19]. The character-
izations of the microstructure and fracture surfaces were per-
formed using JSM-7001 scanning electron microscopes (SEM).
Discs about 0.2 mm in thickness were cut by EDM for transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) specimens. These discs were mechani-
cally ground to a thickness of /C24 80 μm. Electron-transparent foils
were prepared by jet electropolishing using an electrolyte con-
sisting of 65 vol% methanol, 30 vol% butanol, and 5 vol% perchloric
acid at a voltage of 30 V and temperatures between /C0 20 and
/C0 30 1C. The thin foils were then examined using a Philips CM 200
ST transmission electron microscope equipped with a ﬁeld emis-
sion gun operated at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV.
3. Results and discussion
The XRD diffractograms of the as-cast alloys are shown in Fig. 1.
These alloys consist of a cubic
β-Ti ( Im3m, A2 structure) solid

diffraction angles. The lattice parameters of the β-Ti solid solution

(Ti70.5Fe29.5)97Nb3 (referred to as alloy 3), (Ti 70.5Fe29.5)95Nb5 (alloy
5), and (Ti 70.5Fe29.5)93Nb7 (alloy 7) alloys are 0.3197 nm,
0.3214 nm, 0.3221 nm, and 0.3233 nm, respectively. These lattice

Fe and Nb are both well known β-Ti stabilizers [5,12], the
minimum concentration of the alloying element necessary for the
complete stabilization of the β-Ti in binary Ti – Fe and Ti – Nb alloys
is 5 at% and 23 at%, respectively [21]. Molybdenum equivalency
(Moeq) [22], could be used as a criterion to assess the β phase
stability. The β phase is stable against martensitic transformation
when Mo eq 410 wt%. In the present research, the Mo eq of the
(Ti70.5Fe29.5)10 0/C0 xNbx (x¼0, 3, 5 and 7 at%) alloys is much higher
than 10 wt% and accordingly no martensitic transformation occurs.
This is in good agreement with the experimental results, since
only the β-Ti phase was observed by XRD, SEM and TEM as shown
in Figs. 1 and 2 . The lattice parameters of the β-Ti in the Ti – Fe
eutectic (0.3214 nm), alloy 3 (0.3214 nm), alloy 5 (0.3221 nm), and
alloy 7 (0.3223 nm) are smaller than that of pure β-Ti (0.3306 nm)

On the basis of electron microscope observations, it is clear that
the lamellar structures ( Fig. 2(a) and (e)) usually present in the Ti –
Fe eutectics could be modi ﬁed by the addition of Nb to obtain a
bimodal structure with micrometer-sized primary β-Ti dendrites
embedded in the ultra ﬁne matrix as can be seen in Fig. 2 (b)– (d).
The Ti – Nb and Ti – Fe binary phase diagrams [1 7] show that the
liquidus temperature of β-Ti (Nb) is much higher than that of β-Ti
(Fe). Consequently, the primary β-Ti (Fe, Nb) dendrites precipitate
from the liquid during cooling in alloys 3, 5, and 7.
Yielding of a bulk alloy sample depends on the yield strength of
each phase in the alloy and the interaction between different
phases [25]. The decrease in the yield strength for alloys 3, 5, and 7
(o1.6 GPa) is mainly due to the precipitation of the primary β-Ti
dendrites with low yield strength compared with the Ti – Fe
eutectics (1.7 GPa). Alloy 3 exhibited the highest ultimate com-
pressive strength of 2.53 GPa at 15% plastic strain. The high
strength and enhanced plasticity of the Nb-modi ﬁed Ti– Fe eutectic
alloys are attributed to the bimodal structure, re ﬁned ultra ﬁne
lamellar spacing, and the compatibility between the β-Ti (A2) and
FeTi (B2) structures. The larger lattice mismatches between the A2
and B2 phases in alloys 3, 5, and 7 introduce coherency strain at
the interface, and relieve the dislocation pile-ups by slip transfer at
the A2/B2 interface [7,26], thereby improving the ductility of the
Nb-modiﬁed Ti 70.5Fe29.5 eutectic alloys.
4. Conclusions
In summary, Nb-modi ﬁed ultra ﬁne Ti – Fe eutectic alloys and a
Ti– Fe eutectic alloy were prepared by cold crucible levitation
melting, and the mechanical properties of these alloys were tested
in compression at ambient temperature. SEM observations indi-
cated that lamellar structures present in the eutectic Ti 70.5Fe29.5
alloy could be modi ﬁed by the addition of Nb to obtain a bimodal
structure with micrometer-sized primary β-Ti dendrites
embedded in the re ﬁned ultra ﬁne matrix. The combined effects
of the bimodal structure, reduced lamellar spacing, and the good
compatibility between the β-Ti (A2) and FeTi (B2) structures
contributed to a high ultimate compressive strength of 2.53 GPa
and a large plasticity of 15% for the ternary (Ti 70.5Fe29.5)97Nb3 alloy.
The orientation relationship of A2 β-Ti with B2 TiFe in
(Ti70.5Fe29.5)100/C0 xNbx (x¼ 0, 3, 5 and 7 at%) alloys is TiFe (1 10)
[001] || β-Ti (1 10)[001].
