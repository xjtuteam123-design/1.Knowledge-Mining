Concepts derived from phase diagram studies for the strengthening
of Fe–Al-based alloys
M. Palm*
Max-Planck Institut fu¨r Eisenforschung GmbH, Max-Planck-Str. 1, 40237 Du¨sseldorf, Germany

Abstract
Fe–Al-based alloys, i.e. alloys which contain either disordered A2 a-(Fe,Al), B2-ordered FeAl or D0 3-ordered Fe3Al as majority phase,
have a considerable potential for developing materials for structural applications, but insufﬁcient strength and creep resistance have been
identiﬁed as obstacles for the use of Fe–Al-based alloys at high temperatures. At the ‘Discussion Meeting on the Development of Innovative
Iron Aluminium Alloys’ held at the Max-Planck-Institut fu¨r Eisenforschung GmbH, Du¨sseldorf on March, 9th 2004 a couple of presentations
were made with emphasis on improving these properties at high temperatures. In the current article those strengthening mechanisms which
are provided by the phase diagram—solid-solution hardening, strengthening by precipitates, or ordering—are reviewed. Besides results
obtained for the binary Fe–Al system special emphasis is put forward to those ternary systems for which results have been presented at the
‘Discussion Meeting’.

1. Introduction
Fe–Al-based alloys do have a considerable potential for
structural applications at high temperatures as they possess
an outstanding high-temperature corrosion resistance [1–6]
and they do have a lower density compared to other iron-
base materials such as cast iron and stainless steels. Fe–Al-
based alloys also offer comparably low material costs and
means for their production and processing are widely
available [7–10]. In view of their melting temperatures,
which may roughly range between 1200 and 1400 8C,
Fe–Al-based alloys may be used for structural applications
up to service temperatures of 10008C. As strength and creep
resistance are still insufﬁcient at these high temperatures
further alloy development is needed to improve these
properties.
The basic mechanisms for strengthening Fe–Al-based
alloys at elevated and high temperatures have already been
discussed in some detail before [1,9,11–15]. But to what
extent these mechanisms are available in speciﬁc Fe–Al–X
systems is not yet clear as in many cases systematic studies
about phase equilibria and resulting properties are still
missing. The knowledge about phase equilibria is essential
as application of Fe–Al-based alloys at high temperatures
means that they will rapidly attain equilibrium and therefore
the phases that are present at the service temperature, their
compositions and volume fractions are determined by the
equilibrium phase diagram. For some Fe–Al–X systems
where phase equilibria have been established at high
temperatures, i.e. between 800 and 1000 8C, the results
concerning the solid solubility of the third component in the
binary Fe–Al-phases as well as the adjoining two-phase
equilibria in these systems are reviewed. Based on this
review the relevance of these phase equilibria for the
strengthening of respective alloys will be discussed. The
discussion is restricted to strengthening mechanisms
deduced from the phase diagrams themselves, i.e. solid-
solution hardening, strengthening by incoherent or coherent
particles and ordering, though it is noted that also other
strengthening mechanisms such as strengthening by dis-
persoids, e.g. oxides or borides, or by grain reﬁnement are
also available for Fe–Al-based alloys. After a ﬁrst section

2. Binary Fe–Al alloys
The phase diagram of the binary Fe–Al system according
to Kubaschewski [16] is shown in Fig. 1. In the composition
range from 0 to about 54 at.% Al four solid phases exist.
The solid solubility of Al in f.c.c.g-Fe is limited to 1.3 at.%.
In contrast, in disordered b.c.c. a-Fe (A2) up to 45 at.% Al
can be dissolved depending on temperature. Between 0 and
54 at.% Al two ordered compounds exist. D0
3-ordered
Fe3Al is stable at compositions around 27 at.% Al below
552 8C and B2-ordered FeAl ( a2) occurs between 23 and
54 at.% Al again depending on temperature. In addition
lines are shown for the Curie temperature ( T

different modiﬁcations of FeAl ( a2) and the area in which
the so-called ‘K-state’ is observed. The latter two
phenomena are relevant for changes in mechanical proper-
ties but both are not fully understood yet.
Numerous investigations on the dependence of the
strength of binary Fe–Al alloys on temperature and
composition have been performed and the basic ﬁndings
of these studies concerning the 0.2%-yield stress and the
creep resistance are summarised in the following. Fig. 2
shows data compiled from the literature for the dependence
of the yield stress on Al content at elevated temperatures
[17–21]. At 100 8C there is a continuous increase of the
yield stress up to an Al content of about 24 at.% Al followed
by a sharp drop. Comparably low values for the yield stress
are observed between 27 and 31 at.% Al but at higher Al
contents the yield stress increases again though data scatter
considerably at Al contents exceeding 31 at.%. An analo-
gous dependence of the yield stress from the Al content is
observed up to temperatures of about 500 8C. At 500 8C the
drop in yield stress at about 24 at.% Al is less pronounced
than at 100 8C because the yield stress at higher Al contents
is as high or even higher—between 27 and 31 at.% Al—
compared to that at 100 8C. At 700 8C( Fig. 2 ) the yield
stress is much lower than at 100 and 500 8C and in contrast
to its complex dependence on the Al content at lower
temperatures, a more or less continuous increase of the yield
stress with increasing Al content is observed at 700 8C
though the scatter of the data is appreciable.
The linear increase of the yield stress with increasing Al
content up to about 20 at.% Al in the temperature range
between room temperature and 500 8C has been attributed to
solid-solution hardening of the disordered a-(Fe,Al) (A2)
according to Suzuki’s theory for solid-solution hardening of
b.c.c. alloys [7,17,18,22,23]. Though, it is noted that
according to the phase diagram shown in Fig. 1 a two-
phase ﬁeld a-(Fe,Al)CFe

extends to even lower Al contents than shown in Fig. 1 .
At 300 8C Ikeda et al. [24] found the A2/A2 CD03 phase
boundary at about 17 at.% and according to the slope of
their data this phase boundary could extend down to about
10 at.% Al at room temperature. Why no difference in the
increase of the yield stress is observed between single-phase
a-(Fe,Al) and two-phase a-(Fe,Al)CFe
3Al is not clear yet.
It could simply be the case that the alloys have not been in
equilibrium at this comparably low temperature. But it
could also be that the state of ordering has no effect on the
yield stress in this composition range. Respective
investigations are missing for the yield stress but

For FeAl the yield stress anomaly has not been noticed
initially because quenched-in vacancies, which can reach
rather high concentrations for FeAl, have a considerable
hardening effect and the yield stress anomaly only becomes
apparent after a ‘long-term vacancy-minimizing anneal’ at
about 400 8C [34]. Because the yield stress of FeAl-based
alloys is considerably inﬂuenced by quenched-in vacancies,
representations where the yield stress of FeAl is shown in
dependence of the Al content data vary widely at room
temperature [7] as well as at elevated temperatures [17] as
measured data are dependent on previous processing of the
alloys. According to recent results for stoichiometric FeAl
by Schneibel and Munroe [35] the dependence of properties
on previous heat treatment history is relevant for all
observations below about 600 8C. At 700 8C( Fig. 2 ),
where the vacancies are more mobile and act much less as
obstacles for the glide of dislocations, the scatter of data is
smaller. The strengthening effect which is gained by the
addition of Al at this temperature is considerably smaller as
this temperature is above that of the inﬂuence of the yield
strength anomaly for binary alloys and therefore strengthen-
ing solely depends on solid-solution hardening. It is also
noted that according to Fig. 2 no pronounced increase of the
yield stress at the transition from disordered A2 to ordered
B2 is seen at this temperature.
It can be concluded that binary Fe–Al-base alloys show a
considerable strength up to temperatures of about 500 8C.
But this strength results only to a small part from solid-
solution hardening but mainly from the occurrence of an
anomalous increase of the yield stress with temperature. At
700 8C, where there is hardly no effect of the yield stress
anomaly any longer, the effect of strengthening by solid-
solution hardening through the addition of Al is small.

Only few investigations on the creep behaviour of binary
Fe–Al alloys have been performed [31,39]. Already Lawley
et al. [40] found that the state of order has a fundamental

the effect of grain size and composition on creep in FeAl

concluded that effects on creep strength may be different
than those for yield strength as apparently the effect of
ordering may have a much larger effect on creep strength.

sections are grouped according to whether solid-solution
hardening, precipitation hardening–either by incoherent or
coherent precipitates–or ordering is possible. The effective-
ness of the individual mechanisms in the Fe–Al–X alloys is
shortly reviewed while for the more detailed studies on
these systems the reader is referred to the more detailed

is observed. At 7008C the yield stress increases continuously
with the amount of alloying addition for Ti, V and Mo while
for Cr only an addition of 2 at.% increases the yield stress. At
800 8C again a continuous increase of the yield stress with the
amount of alloying addition is observed for Ti, V and Mo
within the investigated composition range while the addition
of Cr has no marked effect on the yield stress at this
temperature (Fig. 4).
The above behaviour of the yield stress in dependence of
the amount of alloying addition and temperature cannot be
satisfactorily explained right now. From the phase diagram
studies it is clear that within the temperature and
composition range under consideration all alloys are within
the range of solid solubility, i.e. they do not contain any
precipitates. For all reported alloys the L2

No effect of the change of order on yield stress is observed,
though it is noted that an increase of T

accompanied by a shift of the yield stress anomaly to
higher temperatures (see Section 6). It can be assumed that
the effect of the yield stress anomaly is responsible for the
dependence of the yield stress on the amount of alloying
addition at 600 and 700 8C. At 800 8C, which is well above
the temperature for the yield stress anomaly for the
investigated alloying additions, a linear relationship
between atomic size misﬁt and increase in yield stress
may be expected, which has been reported at room
temperature [46]. This is not, however, supported by the
data presented in Fig. 4 . At 800 8C addition of 4 at.% X
gives the strongest increase of the yield stress for V
followed by Mo and Ti, but the atomic size misﬁt of the
three elements is just the other way round, i.e. V has
the smallest atomic size misﬁt compared to Fe while Ti has
the largest one. Although the inﬂuence of solid-solution

4. Strengthening by incoherent precipitates
4.1. Precipitates of intermetallic phases
In many Fe–Al–X systems the solid solubility for the
third element within the Fe–Al phases is limited and the
possibility of strengthening Fe–Al-based alloys by precipi-
tation of another intermetallic compound exists. In several
systems this intermetallic phase is a Laves phase, e.g. in the
Fe–Al–X systems with X ZTi, Zr, Nb and Ta. In order to
study the effect of precipitates on strengthening, the Fe–Al–
Zr system may be considered as a prototype system as only
minute solid solubility for Zr in the Fe–Al phases has been
found, which is independent from temperature, at least
between 800 and 1150 8C [53]. Fig. 5 presents the partial
isothermal section of the Fe-corner at 1000 8C, which is
shown by means of example, for the phase equilibria in the
temperature range 800–1150 8C. The isothermal section
reveals that for a-(Fe,Al)-based alloys strengthening by a
Laves phase is possible in this temperature range while
FeAl-based alloys may be strengthened by precipitates of
the tetragonal phase (Fe,Al)
12Zr (t1). As the solubility for
Zr in a-(Fe,Al)/FeAl does not increase with temperature no
possibility exists for generating ﬁne and evenly distributed
precipitates from a solid solution that is supersaturated with
Zr at high temperatures. A detailed study about mechanical
properties and the oxidation behaviour of Fe–Al–Zr alloys is
presented in a paper by Stein et al. [54] within this issue
where the effect of the volume fraction of precipitates on
yield strength is reported.
In contrast to the Fe–Al–Zr system it is more usual that
some limited solid solubility of the third element is observed
within the Fe–Al–X systems, as is shown for example in the
Fe–Al–Ta system. Earlier preliminary results on phase
equilibria in the Fe corner of this system indicated a solid
solubility of about 1 at.% Ta in a-(Fe,Al) and FeAl and that
both phases are in equilibrium with a Laves phase [55].
According to recent investigations the solid solubility for Ta
is more dependent from the Al content in that it increases
with increasing Al content and ﬁrst results about phase
equilibria and mechanical properties of Fe–Al-based
Fe–Al–Ta alloys strengthened by a Laves phase are reported
by Risanti and Sauthoff [56] within this issue. Also for Fe–
Al–Nb alloys containing 25 at.% Al the effect of strength-
ening by precipitates of Laves phase has been studied
recently and has been compared with other precipitation
strengthened Fe–Al alloys and with solution hardening[57].
Besides Laves phases, other intermetallic compounds
may act as strengthening phases as is exempliﬁed by the
Fe–Al–Mo system. Fig. 6 shows partial isothermal sections
of the Fe corner of this system for 800, 1000 and 1150 8C
[21,58]. At all temperatures a marked solid solubility for
Mo in a-(Fe,Al) and FeAl exists which increases with
increasing temperature. This also means that in alloys
which are single-phase a-(Fe,Al)/FeAl at high tempera-
tures small precipitates of the second phase may form
within the Fe–Al phases during cooling [21,59]. The
second phases which may act as strengthening phases are
the hexagonal m phase Fe
7Mo6, which forms two-phase
equilibria with a-(Fe,Al), and the cubic A15-type phase
Mo3Al, which is in equilibrium with both, a-(Fe,Al) and
FeAl (Fig. 6). At Al contents above about 48 at.% FeAl is
in equilibrium with the monoclinic phase Al 8Mo3 at
800 8C, tetragonal D0 22-type Al 8FeMo3 (t1) at 1000 8C
and t2 (cubic A2) or 3 (hexagonal, Al8Cr5-type) at 1150 8C
[58]. As apparently phase equilibria change frequently, this
composition range may not be considered as suitable for
developing alloys for structural applications.

Fe–Al–Mo alloys has been investigated recently. For two-

4.2. Precipitates of carbides
Besides hardening by precipitation of intermetallic
phases, carbides could also act as strengthening phases.
Fig. 7 shows two partial isothermal sections at 800 and
1000 8C of the Fe corner of the Fe–Al–C system [62].A t
both temperatures a-(Fe,Al) and FeAl are in equilibrium
with the cubic K-phase Fe
3AlC. The solid solubility for
carbon in a-(Fe,Al)/FeAl changes only slightly between 800
and 1200 8C but is considerably lower at lower tempera-
tures, e.g. drops from about 1 at.% C at 1000 8C to about
50 ppm at 320 8C [18,63]. This leads to the precipitation of
ﬁne needle shaped precipitates of the K phase at the grain
boundaries during cooling. As the carbon diffusivity is even
high at ambient temperatures, these precipitates at the grain
boundaries are found at room temperature in all alloys of
appropriate compositions even after quenching and they do
strongly affect mechanical properties at low temperatures
[18,63].
The deformation behaviour of single-phase Fe
3AlC [64]
and two-phase Fe3AlC-based alloys[65–67] at high tempera-
tures has been studied in detail before. The effect of K phase
precipitates on the mechanical behaviour of Fe–Al-based
alloys with Al contents between 25 and 30 at.% is demon-
strated in a paper by Schneider et al.[68] within this issue. In a
second contribution by the same authors the strengthening in
Fe–Al–M–C (M ZTi, V, Nb, Ta) alloys by carbides and
additional Laves phase has been studied [69]. Preliminary
results of both studies have also been reported in[70].
In general a gradual increase of the yield stress with
increasing amount of precipitate is observed. But it has to be
noted that this is paralleled by a decrease in ductility at low
and ambient temperatures, as it is often observed for
precipitate strengthened Fe–Al–X alloys. Moreover, the
gain in strength will not alone be determined by the volume
fraction of the precipitate but also by the size and actual
distribution of the precipitate. This is also one of the reasons
which make it difﬁcult to compare the effects of strengthen-
ing by precipitates and solid-solution hardening with each
other. From results by Stein et al.[54] presented in this issue
it can be seen that for Fe–Al–Zr alloys, where no additional
effect of solid-solution hardening exists, the yield stress at
800 8C for alloys with 10 vol% of precipitate equals that of
adding about 4 at.% of Ti, V and Mo.

5. Strengthening by coherent precipitates
In some Fe–Al–X systems a miscibility gap between A2
and B2 or A2 and L2 1, the ternary equivalent of the D0 3
structure, exists and the formation of coherent particles may
be possible.
In the Fe–Al–Ni system a miscibility gap between
disordered a-(Fe,Al) (A2) and ordered NiAl (B2) exists at
temperatures below about 1200 8C [71]. The lattice
mismatch of both phases is sufﬁciently small that it is
possible to produce very ﬁne-scale coherent two-phase

Except for the Fe–Al–Ni system the mechanical proper-
ties of the coherent two-phase microstructures have not been
studied in detail. The coherent precipitates have a strong
strengthening effect and microstructures can be varied such
that the hard (Ni,Fe)Al phase is either the matrix or the
precipitate and in both cases a strengthening effect has been
achieved. The deformation behaviour of ternary Fe–Al–Ni
alloys at high temperatures has been studied before [72,73].
These studies have now been extended to quaternary
Fe–Al–Ni–Cr alloys and ﬁrst results, especially on the
creep behaviour of these alloys, are reported by Stallybrass
et al. [74] within this issue.
6. Strengthening by order
An additional possibility for strengthening of Fe–Al-
based alloys is to stabilise the D0
3 structure with respect to
the B2 structure to higher temperatures. It is known that
certain elements increase the transition temperature Tc for
the D0 3/B2 transformation and that the effectiveness for
increasing Tc increases with the mismatch of the metallic
atomic radius of the atom which substitutes for iron [75].
However, for many of the effective atoms the solid
solubility is quite limited and therefore only a limited
increase of T
c can be achieved.
One of the most effective atoms in raisingTc is Ti, which
in addition has a considerable solid solubility within the
Fe–Al phases according to the partial isothermal sections
shown in Fig. 9 [76] . At 1000 8C up to 25 at.% Ti can be
substituted for Fe and by this substitution the D0
3 (L21)
ordered structure is stabilised at this temperature. Ohnuma
et al. [77] determined the L2
1/B2 transformation tempera-
tures along the sections Fe 3Al–Fe2TiAl and Fe 2TiAl–FeAl
and found a maximum for Tc at the stoichiometric
composition Fe2TiAl of about 1215 8C.
Though there is no direct relation between the maximum
of the yield stress anomaly and Tc [20,32,78] usually atoms
which increase Tc also shift the yield stress anomaly to
higher temperatures. This is also the case for Fe–Al–Ti
alloys where the maximum of the yield stress anomaly
may be found as high as 800 8C [79,80] and respective
alloys show a comparably high yield stress at high
temperatures [78].

The mechanical behaviour of L2 1-ordered Fe–Al–Ti
alloys has been studied recently [78]. Compared with other
Fe–Al-based alloys they show a high yield stress and creep
resistance at high temperatures up to 1100 8C. The high
yield stress is mainly due to an increased peak temperature
of the yield stress anomaly which is at about 750 8C for the
studied alloys. In addition to the effect of shifting the yield
stress anomaly to higher temperatures there is also evidence
for an inﬂuence of ordering on mechanical properties. In the
temperature range 800–1100 8C, i.e. above the temperature
of the yield stress anomaly, yield stress and creep resistance
increase towards the stoichiometric composition of Fe
2AlTi,
i.e. with decreasing amount of constitutional point defects.

possibility to combine different strengthening mechanisms
with each other. Fig. 9 reveals that in addition to ordering

7. Conclusions
The above short overview shows that strengthening of
Fe–Al-based alloys at high temperatures can be achieved by
solid-solution hardening, precipitation of incoherent or
coherent particles and ordering. By presentations of the
respective phase diagrams it is outlined to what extent these
mechanisms are available within certain Fe–Al–X systems.
Even solid-solution hardening can have a marked effect on
increasing the yield stress at high temperatures. But as this
increase is related to the amount of the solute, it is limited to
the solid solubility of the third component within the Fe–Al
phases. Precipitates, which are available in equilibrium in
many of the Fe–Al–X systems, have a strong strengthening
effect at high temperatures but usually lead to a considerable
loss in ductility at low or even ambient temperatures.
Formation of ﬁne-scaled coherent A2 CB2 microstructures
can improve the creep resistance. In systems where the
stability of the D0
3 structure can be stabilised to higher
temperatures by the addition of third elements further
strengthening by ordering is possible.
To what extent these mechanisms contribute to the
strengthening of Fe–Al-based alloys is not yet clear as only
few data exist, especially for creep. At the ‘Discussion
Meeting on the Development of Innovative Iron Aluminium
Alloys’ a couple of systematic studies have been presented
where the aspect of strengthening Fe–Al-based alloys at
high temperatures has been an important issue. The extent to
which the properties can be varied within certain
systems can be seen in the individual contributions collected
in this issue.
