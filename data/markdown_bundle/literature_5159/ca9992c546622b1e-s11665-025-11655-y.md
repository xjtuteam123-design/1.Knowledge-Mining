Laser Additive Manufactured V-Shaped and L-Shaped
Ti65 Titanium Alloy Interfaces and High-Temperature
Performance
Liping Liu, Xiaodan Li, Jun Yin, Changyu Wang, Jingjing Zhang, and Pengfei Li
Submitted: 20 February 2025 / Revised: 13 April 2025 / Accepted: 25 April 2025
This study examines the microstructural characteristics, mechanical properties, and high-temperature
performance of Ti65 titanium alloy fabricated via laser additive manufacturing (LAM). Microstructural
analysis reveals the presence of columnar grains and a + b lamellar structures, with notable variations
across different regions, including the additively manufactured zones and interface areas. Room-temper-
ature and high-temperature (650 /C176C) mechanical testing indicates pronounced anisotropy in yield strength,
ultimate tensile strength, elongation, and reduction in area, particularly between V-type and L-type joint
conﬁgurations. At elevated temperatures, although the strength decreases, the alloy exhibits enhanced
ductility, which is attributed to the activation of improved plastic deformation mechanisms. The effects of
interfacial features and microstructural anisotropy are discussed in relation to dislocation behavior and
deformation processes. These ﬁndings advance the understanding of how LAM process parameters
inﬂuence the microstructure and mechanical performance of Ti65, providing valuable insights for opti-
mizing its additive manufacturing for applications demanding high mechanical strength and thermal sta-
bility.
Keywords additive manufacturing, anisotropy, high-temperature
performance, microstructure, Ti65 titanium alloy
1. Introduction
Titanium alloys are extensively utilized in the aerospace
industry owing to their exceptional performance characteristics.
Their high strength-to-weight ratio, outstanding corrosion
resistance, and excellent high-temperature capabilities make
them ideal candidates for the fabrication of critical components
in aircraft, spacecraft, and engines (Ref 1). These alloys
contribute to structural weight reduction and improved fuel
efﬁciency, while also maintaining stable mechanical perfor-
mance under extreme service conditions (Ref 2, 3). Typical
applications include the production of essential components
such as fuselage structures, wings, landing gear assemblies, and
turbine engine blades, all of which beneﬁt from the superior
mechanical and thermal properties of titanium alloys. The use
of titanium alloys signiﬁcantly enhances the overall perfor-
mance and reliability of aerospace systems. Aerospace vehicles
are composed of complex structural assemblies that must be
integrated into a uniﬁed system through advanced welding and
joining technologies to ensure structural integrity and load-
bearing capacity (Fig. 1). These technologies play a critical role
in delivering robust, durable joints capable of withstanding the
various mechanical stresses and dynamic loads encountered
during ﬂight operations (Ref 4, 5).
Welding is one of the primary methods for joining large
structural titanium alloy components and has been extensively
investigated through various techniques, including pulsed laser
welding, continuous laser welding, and hybrid welding pro-
cesses (Ref 6, 7). Rauti et al. (Ref 8) explored the effects of
laser welding on the microstructure and mechanical properties
of Ti-6Al-4V alloy fabricated via selective laser melting (SLM),
highlighting signiﬁcant changes in mechanical behavior. Li
et al. (Ref 9) employed vacuum electron beam welding
combined with localized heat treatment on thick TC4 plates,
which fragmented portions of the a phase and transformed it
into a b + a structure, thereby signiﬁcantly enhancing both
strength and ductility. Zhan et al. (Ref 10) used both simulation
and experimental approaches to examine the inﬂuence of
varying welding speeds and laser power on the weld joint and
heat-affected zone (HAZ) microstructure of TA15 titanium
alloy. Their ﬁndings revealed that the HAZ consists of a
mixture of a and a¢ phases. Wang et al. (Ref 11) analyzed the
effects of electron beam welding and post-weld heat treatment
on the microstructure and mechanical properties of Ti-6246
alloy, reporting that both weld strength and hardness were
higher than those of the base material. Through an investigation
of the low-cycle fatigue performance of 140-mm-thick TC4
plates welded using double-sided electron beam welding, Long
et al. (Ref 12) found that the overlap region of the weld
exhibited higher microhardness compared to other zones. They
also identiﬁed this heterogeneous region as a major factor

Therefore, while continuous laser welding has been widely

Despite extensive research on the microstructure and
mechanical properties of laser-welded joints across a wide
range of metals, several critical areas remain insufﬁciently
explored. First, studies focusing on the newly developed Ti65
titanium alloy are limited. Ti65 is speciﬁcally designed for
high-temperature service conditions up to 650 /C176 C and offers
signiﬁcant advantages in extending the operational temperature
range of aerospace components. Second, there is a notable lack
of research on laser joining techniques for large-scale structural
components. Given the size constraints of laser additive
manufacturing systems, alternative joining methods such as
welding and riveting are necessary to assemble large structures.
In light of these challenges, the present study focuses on Ti65
titanium alloy fabricated by LAM and investigates the effects of
two distinct laser joining conﬁgurations—V-shaped and L-
shaped—on the resulting microstructure and mechanical per-
formance. Particular attention is given to the interfacial bonding
strength and the tensile properties of the joints at both room
temperature and elevated temperature (650 /C176 C), providing new
insights for the design and manufacturing of high-performance
aerospace components.
2. Experimental Procedure and Methods
2.1 Experimental Procedure
The laser additive joining experiments were conducted
using an LDM-800 system, as illustrated in Fig. 2(a). The
equipment features a working envelope of 800 9 600 9 400
mm and operates under an argon protective atmosphere, with
the oxygen concentration in the build chamber maintained
at £ 30 ppm. Powder was delivered via a negative-pressure
carrier gas system, and the scanning speed was
adjustable within the range of 0–20 mm/s. The laser heat
source was an IPG ﬁber laser, offering a power range of 100–
6000 W and a spot diameter between 3 and 5 mm.
Considering the geometry of large structural components,
two laser joining strategies were adopted: L-shaped and V-
shaped conﬁgurations. The L-shaped conﬁguration involved
lateral laser joining. After the ﬁrst stage of additive manufac-
turing, the component was rotated 90 /C176 relative to the initial
build direction to initiate the second-stage deposition, as
depicted in Fig. 2(b). The speciﬁc parameters for the L-shaped
joining process were as follows: laser power of 2000 W,
scanning speed of 10 mm/s, layer height of 0.5 mm, hatch
spacing of 2 mm, powder feed rate of 0.8 r/min, and a short-
edge scanning strategy was employed for continuous deposi-
tion. Following the laser additive joining process, all samples
underwent post-process heat treatment at 700 /C176 C for 4 hours,
followed by air coolin (AC).
The V-shaped connection was implemented using prefabri-
cated V-groove joints. Ti65 titanium alloy blocks fabricated via
laser additive manufacturing were machined with 45 /C176 V-
grooves. The two components were aligned and securely
clamped using custom ﬁxtures, followed by laser additive
joining, as illustrated in Fig. 2(c). The V-shaped laser joining
process employed the following parameters: an initial layer
laser power of 2000 W, a ﬁll layer power of 2400 W, scanning
speed of 10 mm/s, layer height of 0.5 mm, hatch spacing of
2 mm, and a powder feed rat 0.8 r/min. Starting from the
second layer, a short-edge scanning strategy was adopted, with
a 1-minute dwell time between layers to mitigate localized
overheating. After laser additive joining, all V-shaped speci-
mens were subjected to post-processing heat treatment at
700 /C176 C for 4 hours, followed by air cooling.
2.2 Sample Dimensions and Testing
Figure 3 illustrates the sampling locations for the V-shaped
and L-shaped specimens. Sample 1 was designed to evaluate
the overall bonding strength of the V-shaped joint, including
tensile strength at both room temperature and elevated temper-
ature. The geometry of Sample 1 is detailed in Fig. 3(d).
Samples 2 and 3 were prepared to assess the interface bonding
strength of the V-shaped joint at room temperature and 650 /C176 C,
respectively. These samples enable a comparative analysis of
the mechanical strength in both transverse and longitudinal
directions, thereby facilitating the evaluation of anisotropic
behavior. Sample 4 was used to measure the interface bonding
strength of the L-shaped side joint. The dimensions for Samples
2–4 are provided in Fig. 3(c).
During the investigation of the laser additive manufacturing
(LAM) process for Ti65 titanium alloy, a series of tests were
conducted, including metallographic sample preparation, room-
temperature tensile testing, and high-temperature performance
evaluation. For metallographic preparation, grinding was
performed sequentially using sandpapers with grit sizes of
240#, 600#, 1000#, 1500#, and 2000#, followed by polishing
with an alumina suspension. Etching was performed using
Kroll/C213s reagent. Microstructural and phase composition obser-
vations and analyses were conducted using an Olympus GX51
optical microscope, scanning electron microscope (SEM), and
transmission electron microscope (TEM). Nondestructive test-
ing of ultrasonic microdefects in the samples was carried out
using a SAM300B ultrasonic microscope. Tensile tests were
performed in accordance with GB/T 228.1-2021, ‘‘Metallic
Materials—Tensile Testing—Part 1: Method of Test at Room
Temperature,’’ and GB/T 4338-2006, ‘‘Metallic Materi-
als—Tensile Testing at Elevated Temperature.’’

Based on cross-sectional sampling of the specimens fol-
lowed by XRD and EBSD analyses, the results indicate that the
Ti65 alloy formed by laser additive manufacturing exhibits
signiﬁcant anisotropy in both its microstructure and crystal
orientation distribution. This behavior is consistent with most
dual-phase titanium alloys and near- a titanium alloys produced
through laser deposition processes. XRD diffraction was
performed on the XZ and XY sections, as shown in Fig. 13.
The diffraction peaks were identiﬁed as corresponding to the a
phase, but signiﬁcant variations in peak intensities were
observed among different section samples. These variations
are closely associated with the preferred orientation of the
parent phase. Due to the inﬂuence of the heat dissipation
direction, the parent b phase grows in a columnar manner along
the laser incidence direction, forming columnar crystals that
maintain a certain crystal orientation. In the EBSD analysis
pole Figures of samples from different cross-sectional posi-
tions, shown in Fig. 14, the parent b phase (110) exhibits a clear
orientation distribution. This leads to signiﬁcant anisotropy in
the a phase after precipitation, which strictly corresponds to the
crystal lattice orientation of the parent phase.
The presence of columnar crystals leads to an uneven
distribution of grain boundaries in the vertical and horizontal
directions. The anisotropic distribution of crystal orientations
in the parent phase contributes to the development of texture
in the lattice of the a phase. The effectiveness of grain
boundaries and lattice texture in hindering dislocation move-
ment is largely dependent on their distribution characteristics.
Therefore, the variation in grain boundary distribution and the
presence of lattice texture suggest that the laser additive
manufactured Ti65 titanium alloy exhibits mechanical aniso-
tropy in its properties.

4. Discussion
4.1 Anisotropy
Pronounced anisotropy was observed in both V-shaped and
L-shaped laser-joined regions, as evidenced by signiﬁcant
variations in mechanical parameters including yield strength,
tensile strength, elongation, and reduction of area (Fig. 10–14).
These discrepancies fundamentally originate from heteroge-
neous thermal histories and microstructure evolution during
additive manufacturing. The inherent microstructural anisotro-
py in AM-processed specimens not only governs mechanical
responses but also modulates wear and corrosion behaviors. For
instance, arc-directed energy deposited 316L stainless steel
exhibits surface-dependent microstructural characteristics: On
side surfaces perpendicular to the laser scanning direction, ﬁner
columnar austenitic grains and acicular ferritic phases grow
vertically along melt pool boundaries, a phenomenon directly
correlated with directional heat dissipation along the build
direction (Z-axis) (Ref 29). The formation mechanism of such
columnar grains is attributed to preferential growth along the
direction opposite to the maximum heat ﬂux, driven by steep
thermal gradients under rapid solidiﬁcation conditions (cooling
rates of 10
3–106 K/s) (Ref 29).
Contrasting microstructural features were identiﬁed on side
surfaces parallel to the build direction and top surfaces. Cyclic
reheating effects induced by layer-wise deposition triggered
competitive grain growth across interlayer regions. Speciﬁcally,
remelting of previously solidiﬁed layers during deposition
establishes sufﬁcient thermal gradients within melt pools to

5. Conclusions
(1) This study examined the microstructure and mechanical
properties of Ti65 titanium alloy joints fabricated using
V-type and L-type laser additive manufacturing (LAM)
techniques. Microstructural analysis revealed signiﬁcant
anisotropy in both types of joints, with distinct columnar
and equiaxed grain zones in the V-type joints and vary-
ing grain sizes and orientations in the L-type joints.
(2) At room temperature, the V-type joints exhibited high
yield strength and ultimate tensile strength (UTS), with
average values of 1087 MPa and 1130 MPa, respec-
tively. The horizontal interface regions of the V-type
joints displayed slightly higher strength compared to the
vertical regions. The L-type joints exhibited strengths
similar to the V-type vertical interface region, with an
average yield strength of 979 MPa and UTS of 1063
MPa.
(3) At 650 /C176 C, tensile strengths decreased across all joints,
with the V-type joints showing an average yield strength
of 477 MPa and UTS of 636 MPa. The V-type horizon-
tal interface regions retained relatively higher strength at
507 MPa yield strength and 658 MPa UTS. L-type
joints exhibited lower high-temperature strength, with
470 MPa yield strength and 608 MPa UTS.
(4) High-temperature testing demonstrated improved plastic-
ity, with the V-type horizontal interface region showing
the highest elongation (21.2%) and reduction in area
(67.2%). Anisotropy analysis revealed that, at both room
temperature and 650 /C176 C, the horizontal direction exhib-
ited higher tensile strength than the vertical direction.
(5) Elemental distribution analysis using EDS indicated that
Si, Ta, and W were predominantly located at the a/b
phase boundaries, enhancing both room-temperature
strength and high-temperature stability. Overall, the
study highlights the signiﬁcant role of microstructural
anisotropy in inﬂuencing the mechanical performance of
Ti65 titanium alloy joints fabricated by laser additive
manufacturing, providing valuable insights for optimiz-
ing welding techniques in high-performance applica-
tions.
