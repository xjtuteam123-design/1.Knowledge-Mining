Abstract
In this work, we investigated whether rhenium doping has a beneficial effect on the mechanical properties of the IN 713C alloy. The alloy powder was 
doped with rhenium using a chemical method and consolidated in the 3D printing. The tensile strength tests conducted at 750°C demonstrated the 
highest tensile strength for the IN 713C alloy doped with rhenium. The results indicate an 8% increase compared to alloys without a rhenium dopant. 
The studies confirmed the enhancement of the operational properties of the IN 713C alloy at elevated temperature by rhenium doping.
Introduction
Rhenium (Re) possesses excellent functional properties due 
to being one of the highest strain hardening exponents among 
elements and having a low friction coefficient. About other 
refractory elements, it also exhibits high tensile strength and 
creep strength up to approximately 2000°C.[1] The abovemen-
tioned properties make Re an attractive candidate for applica-
tions in high-temperature conditions. Re is an alloy additive 
that improves its corrosion resistance, wear resistance, or creep 
strength. It demonstrates good solubility in both BCC (Body-
Centered Cubic) and FCC ( Face-Centered Cubic ) structures, 
which contributes to the solid solution strengthening of the 
matrix and the alloy. [2] Its strengthening effect has led to its 
use in alloys for aircraft engine components. Examples of these 
materials include second-generation superalloys (CMSX—4, 
Rene N5) and third-generation superalloys (CMSX—10, RENE 
M6), which contain Re at levels of 3% and 6% by mass, respec-
tively.[3] The addition of Re to single-crystal nickel alloys 
enhances their creep resistance. This change increases operat-
ing temperature by 30°C and 60°C for second and third-gener-
ation alloys.[4]] For example, under typical operating conditions 
at a temperature of 900°C and a pressure of 400 MPa, the dem-
onstrated creep resistance for single-crystal alloys can increase 
tenfold, from 100 to 1000 h, thanks to the rhenium effect.[5] Re 
is one of the rarest elements in the Earth’s crust, which results 
in its high price. However, its strengthening effect makes it 
widely used in superalloys for components operating in harsh 
conditions. More than 80% of Re consumed worldwide is used 
to produce superalloys.[6]
In additive manufacturing processes, alloys are fabricated 
from the powder, allowing for their modification by introducing 
suitable dopants. As a result, there is an opportunity to enhance 
the functional properties of a material. This manufacturing 
technology enables the reuse of powder material, which is cru-
cial given the cost of Re. Furthermore, it significantly reduces 
the time required to manufacture alloy samples, allowing 
for faster testing of their functional properties for potential 
enhancements.
Kurzynowski et al.[7] investigated the effect of Re addition 
on the tensile strength at 650°C of the nickel alloy Inconel 718 
produced by the additive method. They confirmed the positive 
influence of Re on the strength properties of this alloy at high 
temperatures. An increase in yield strength and tensile strength 
was observed, along with an increase in the Re content in the 
fabricated samples of the Inconel 718. Another example of an 
alloy doped with Re and manufactured by additive technology 
is Inconel 625. [8] In this case, the introduction of the dopant 
at the level of 3 wt% also increased the tensile strength of the 
alloy, both at room temperature and at 650°C. The powders of 
the two alloys were doped with Re using a mechanical method. 
This method utilizes vibrations and centrifugal forces to crush 
and blend Re powder particles with Ni-based alloy powder par-
ticles into a single powder material. [7] The process is carried 
out in high-energy ball mills, generally maintaining a ball-to-
powder weight ratio of 10:1 or higher.[9] Optimizing parameters 
such as rotation speed, milling duration, container capacity, and 
the quantity of powder mixture[7,9] enables achieving a proper 
distribution of the Re dopant within the Ni-based alloy powder.
In our work, we proposed a novel approach to doping alloy 
powders with Re through chemical synthesis. [10] The key role 
during synthesis was played by appropriately selected catalysts, 
including propylene carbonate and triethylene glycol dimethyl 
ether. The former accelerates the esterification reaction between 
citric acid hydrate and ethylene glycol. Triethylene glycol dime-
thyl ether was employed to facilitate gelation at low temperatures 
and to separate the alloy powder particles.[10] The doping process

occurred within a colloidal system based on the polymerization 
process. The new method we used aimed to achieve better dis-
persion of the Re dopant compared to the mechanical approach. 
Achieving a uniform distribution of Re throughout the entire vol-
ume of the nickel alloy powder and subsequently in the micro-
structure of the manufactured alloy is crucial for the operational 
properties of the alloy. Additionally, the conditions of the doping 
process help to prevent the agglomeration of the alloy powder 
particles, which is also essential for the 3D printing process.
In the study, we utilized the precipitation-strengthened 
Inconel 713C alloy (IN 713C). This alloy exhibits outstanding 
strength properties at high temperatures. It is known for its 
good castability, resistance to oxidation and thermal fatigue, 
as well as exceptional structural stability. [11,12] However, the 
disadvantage of the IN 713C alloy is its low weldability, which 
results from the large volume fraction of the strengthening γ’ 
(over 50%) in its microstructure. [12] This defect remains the 
most significant problem associated with the use of the alloy 
as a material for structural components. According to literature 
reports, the addition of Re can improve the functional proper -
ties of alloys at elevated temperatures and their weldability. 
In our previous work, we verified whether the addition of Re 
would enhance the properties of the IN 713C alloy. We dem-
onstrated that Re improves the oxidation resistance of the IN 
713C alloy at elevated temperatures. [13]
The current work focused on the effect of the Re dopant 
on the tensile strength of the tested alloys at a temperature of 
750°C. The applied chemical doping method allowed obtaining 
the IN 713C alloy powder with Re (IN 713C:Re). The exam-
ined alloys were manufactured by the 3D printing process using 
selective laser melting (SLM) technique. The behavior of the 
3D-printed IN 713C and 713C:Re alloys was compared with 
the IN 713C alloy cast.
Materials and methods
The study used commercially available powder of the IN 713C 
alloy from Sandvik Osprey Powders with a particle size range 
of 10–45 µm [Fig 1(a)]. A chemical method, based on a sol–gel 
reaction rather than the commonly known mechanical method 
was employed to introduce Re dopant into the alloy powder. 
Rhenium (VII) oxide, with a purity of 99.995% from Sigma 
Aldrich [Fig 1 (b)], was used as the precursor for Re ions. [10] 
During synthesis at a temperature of 70℃ ± 2℃, a polymer net-
work was formed using citric acid hydrate (p.a., Aktyn) and 
ethylene glycol (p.a., Aktyn), which enclosed the dopant. For 
the synthesis, we utilized nitric acid (65% p.a., Stanlab), ethyl 
alcohol (96% p.a., Chempur) as solvents, along with propyl-
ene carbonate (99.5%, Acros) and triethylene glycol dimethyl 
ether (≥ 98%, Sigma Aldrich) as catalysts. The reactions that 
occurred during the doping process enabled the creation of 
the complexed Re within the polymer network. To this mix-
ture, the IN 713C alloy powder was then added. In the final

The morphology of the powders used in the 3D printing pro-
cess is shown in Fig 1. The applied doping method enabled us 
to achieve good dispersion of Re in the IN 713C alloy powder 
[Fig 1(c)], compared to mechanical milling. Furthermore, the 
chemical method with appropriate parameters allows for the 
production of uniform Re particles, even at nanometric sizes.[10] 
The morphology of the chemical-doped alloy powder differs 
significantly from that obtained by mechanical doping. In the 
literature, examples of mechanically doped Ni-based alloy 
powders with Re can be found, such as Inconel 718[7,14] and 
Inconel 625.[8] Re particles, in these cases, exhibit an irregular 
shape, often with a flake-like structure, and are characterized 
by a non-uniform size.
The IN 713C and IN 713C:Re alloy powders were consoli-
dated by 3D printing process with a Concept Laser GMbH 3D 
printer (model Mlab cusingR) using appropriate parameters.[13] 
During the 3D printing process, the samples of IN 713C and 
713C:Re alloys with dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 6 mm 
were manufactured [Fig 1 (d)]. The chemical composition 
of the IN 713C:Re alloy was analyzed using XRF method, 
revealing the following composition: 6.06Al-12.66Cr-0.79Ti-
1.96Nb-4.26Mo-0.27Si-0.75Fe-0.008-Zr-0.50Re-72.6Ni. [13] 
The density of the samples was determined based on Archi-
medes’ principle using a hydrostatic balance. [15] The average 
value for both 3D-printed alloys was estimated at 99%. Sam-
ples for microstructure observation were etched in Marble’s 
reagent at room temperature. High-temperature static tensile 
tests were conducted on the IN 713C alloy and the 3D-printed 
IN 713C and 713C:Re alloys. The mini-samples with the 5 mm 
gauge section length were cut in the xy plane as schematically 
shown in Fig 1 (e) using electrical discharge machining. The 
tests were conducted with a Zwick Roell Z005 testing machine 
and the MTS 653 resistance furnace at 750℃ ± 5℃. Each alloy 
was tested using three samples, and the results were averaged. 
After tensile strength tests, the fractures were observed using a 
Zeiss EVO MA10 scanning electron microscope.
Results and discussion
From the point of view of mechanical tests for alloys fabri-
cated through the 3D printing process, density is a significant 
issue. The densification of the tested samples was 99%, indi-
cating potential defects from the manufacturing process. The 
microstructural observations conducted aimed to identify these 
defects. For all 3D-printed alloy samples based on IN 713C 
alloy powder, the etching process in the xy plane revealed per-
pendicular melting paths and defects, mainly microcracks and 
some pores [Fig 2(a, b)]. Analysis of additional SEM images 
revealed that the pores were unevenly distributed throughout 
the entire volume of the samples. At the same time, a higher 
number of microcracks was observed at the individual edges 
of the samples. The pore sizes of the IN 713C and IN 713C:Re 
alloys ranged from 3 to 5 µm. The microcrack measurements of 
the IN 713C and 713C:Re alloys indicated sizes ranging from 
11 to 85 µm, with an average of 34 ± 15 µm and 33 ± 17 µm, 
respectively. The microcracks occurred due to residual stresses 
from the 3D printing process. These stresses resulted from sig-
nificant temperature gradients in the solidified material caused 
by rapid melting and solidification during the laser melting 
process. The sample microstructure, regardless of Re pres-
ence, revealed grains with dendrites of a cellular structure [see 
enlarged view in Fig 2 (c, d)]. Re, known for enhancing the 
solid solution of nickel-based alloys, tends to segregate into 
the γ phase. Therefore, its presence should be especially noted 
in the dendritic regions where this phase is found. The EDS 
(Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy) analysis showed the 
segregation of Re into the γ phase, although Re was also found 
in the interdendritic region [Fig 2(e)].[13]
The tensile curves obtained during the examinations, along 
with bar charts that display the average tensile strength and 
elongation at break for the tested samples, are shown in Fig 3 . 
The 3D-printed alloy IN 713C:Re exhibited the highest tensile 
strength, with an average value of 926.6 ± 39.5 MPa. In com-
parison, the IN 713C alloy as-cast and the 3D-printed IN 713C 
alloy reached 848.9 ± 10.9 MPa and 845.8 ± 64.3 MPa, respec-
tively. On the other hand, it was observed that the 3D-printed 
undoped and Re-doped samples exhibited low plasticity. The 
cast samples exhibited the highest plasticity, achieving a maxi-
mum elongation-to-break value of 6.2 ± 0.5%. In contrast, the 
3D-printed samples had elongation-to-break values of less 
than 1%. Both the cast and 3D-printed IN 713C alloy sam-
ples displayed similar tensile strength values. However, in the 
3D-printed IN 713C alloy, rupture occurred immediately after 
reaching the yield point. The introduction of 0.5 wt% of Re as 
a dopant increased the yield stress and resulted in slight per -
manent deformation [Fig 3(a)].
A positive effect of Re doping on the mechanical properties 
of the IN 713C alloy was observed. The IN 713C:Re alloy 
manufactured by 3D printing exhibited the highest strength at 
the tested temperature. Variations in the tensile test behaviors 
of the samples should primarily be attributed to the introduced 
Re dopant and the different fabrication technologies.
Re addition influences changes in the microstructure of 
solution strengthening and can enhance the performance of 
superalloys at high temperatures when used as an alloying 
element. Its presence slows down diffusion-controlled pro-
cesses in the microstructure, increasing stability under high-
temperature exposition. [16] Due to the large size of the Re 
atom, it diffuses the slowest in the nickel matrix. As a result, 
there is a significant tendency for segregation to occur in the 
γ phase, as observed in the samples manufactured through the 
3D printing process. Additionally, the presence of this ele -
ment in the dendritic regions related to the γ phase has been

high temperatures primarily arise from the strengthening effect 
of γ′ phases within the γ-matrix.[4] Research has shown that the 
size, volume fraction, morphology, and distribution of γ′ phases 
significantly affect the tensile properties of nickel alloys.[17,18] In 
alloys with a small part of the volume of the γ′ phase, the particles

are spheroidal, while in alloys with increased Al and Ti content, 
the particles are cubic. The change in morphology is caused by 
the lattice mismatch between the γ′ phase and the surrounding γ 
matrix. It has been found that γ′ particles are spheroidal if the mis-
match is not higher than 0.2%; they become cubic for a mismatch 
of 0.5–1% and lamellar for a mismatch of about 1.25%.[19] The 
presence of Re in the γ-matrix causes a high lattice misfit due to 
the large atomic radius, thus causing a possible change in the shape 
of the strengthening γ′ phase.[20] Additionally, a higher absolute 
value of the lattice mismatch leads to increased lattice mismatch 
strain energy, leading to the cubic structure of the γ′ phase. As a 
consequence of the larger atomic size of Re, its higher concentra-
tion results in a more negative mismatch, thereby enhancing the 
cubic degree of the γ′ phase as the Re content increases.[21] The 
cubic shape of the γ′ phase is generally considered optimal for 
strengthening nickel alloys. For example, in the case of the MGA 
1400 nickel alloy, the addition of Re at the level of 4 wt% resulted 
in the formation of a γ′ phase with a cubic morphology due to 
a higher magnitude with negative lattice misfit (− 0.3857%).[22]
Furthermore, research indicates that strength increases as the 
size of the γ′ phase decreases. Re is added to the alloy to slow 
the growth of the γ′ phase. [23] During exposure to high tem-
peratures, the coarsening of the γ′ phase in nickel alloys is con-
trolled by diffusion. However, as the content of Re increases, 
the diffusion coefficient decreases. This indicates that Re can 
effectively suppress the growth of the γ′ phase, leading to a 
smaller size of the γ′ phase as the Re content increases. Smaller 
sizes of the γ′ phase can create more effective obstacles to the 
dislocation movement, leading to increased high-temperature 
strength of the alloy.[21]
On the other hand, the method of manufacturing samples 
affected the plasticity of the tested alloys. The elongation-
to-break of the 3D-printed IN 713C:Re alloy decreased to 
approximately 0.6%, compared to 6% for the IN 713C alloy 
cast. The differences observed were primarily attributed to 
defects, like microcracks, and porosity present in the alloys. 
During the 3D-printing process, high-temperature gradients 
occurred, causing stresses in the alloy samples manufactured, 
which resulted in the generation of microcracks. An important 
aspect of forming such defects is the classification of the tested 
IN 713C alloy to the group of difficult-to-weld and low plastic-
ity alloys (≈ 6%). The low plasticity of the alloy increases the 
tendency to crack in the heat-affected zone. The connection of 
individual layers during the 3D-printing process can be con-
sidered as the formation of microwelds and which may lead to 
microcracks due to the alloy’s low plasticity. The defects are 
places of stress concentration that promote the initiation of 
cracks. Microcracks in the 3D-printed alloys developed as the 
cracking progressed at high temperatures, causing faster break-
age of the samples manufactured by the 3D printing process. 
These defects were observed on the fracture surface of the 
3D-printed IN 713C and IN 713C:Re alloys (Fig 4).

Conclusions
Adding Re to the IN 713C alloy improved its tensile strength at 
750°C to about 927 MPa compared with a cast, which reached 
about 849 MPa. At the same time, significant reductions in elon-
gation at the break were observed in contrast to the IN 713C alloy 
cast. The alloys, both with and without Re addition, fabricated 
using the 3D printing process, exhibited negligible plasticity.
The reduction in elongation-to-break observed in non-doped 
and Re-doped alloys was a result of the material’s low plas-
ticity and the manufacturing technology used. The selective 
laser melting process generates high-temperature gradients, 
which induce stress in the alloy, leading to the formation of 
microcracks. These defects propagated during the tensile test, 
resulting in the alloy samples breaking faster. At the same time, 
the IN 713C alloy is characterized by low plasticity, which can 
also affect the formation of microcracks during the 3D print-
ing process. This negatively affects the material’s mechanical 
properties, causing alloy failure at high temperatures.
Improving the density of IN 713C alloy samples produced 
through 3D printing by eliminating microcracks or pores may 
positively impact the mechanical properties, including the 
plasticity, of the alloy. In the future, our work will concen -
trate on removing the defects to enhance the density of the 
3D-printed alloys. Eliminating the aforementioned defects will 
require further optimization of the printing process by selecting 
appropriate parameters such as laser power, scanning speed, 
or layer thickness. During subsequent tests, the amount of Re 
introduced into the alloy or the use of HIP (Hot Isostatic Press-
ing) post-process treatment should also be considered. Perhaps 
a dopant level of 0.5 wt% was inadequate for enhancing the 
weldability of the alloy and should be increased. Improving the 
weldability of the alloy would positively affect its behavior dur-
ing the 3D printing process and perhaps eliminate microcracks 
that formed during the process.
