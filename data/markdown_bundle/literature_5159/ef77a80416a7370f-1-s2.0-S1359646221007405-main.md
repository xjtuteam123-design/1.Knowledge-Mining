a b s t r a c t 
This paper investigated an important order-disorder transition in lightweight AlCrTiV high entropy al- 
loy system using CALculation of Phase Diagram (CALPHAD) modeling, differential scanning calorimetry 
(DSC) measurement, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization and mechanical testing. It 
is found that increasing Ti content in AlCrTiV alloys can lower the order-disorder transition temperature, 
reduce ordering and introduces ductility to the alloys. The new Al 10 Cr 10 Ti 70 V 10 alloy shows a measurable 
tensile elongation (1.1%) and excellent strengths (877 MPa yield strength and 889 MPa ultimate tensile 
strength), promising lightweight applications.

In recent years, there has been extensive research in develop- 
ing lightweight high entropy alloys (HEAs) [1–4] . In order to keep 
the density low, almost all reported lightweight HEAs contain high 
concentrations of Al and Ti. The combinations of high Al and Ti 
contents in these alloys often result in an ordered body center cu- 
bic crystal structure, BCC_B2, at room temperature. However, these 
lightweight HEAs with highly ordered BCC_B2 phase generally suf- 
fer from poor ductility with almost zero elongation in tensile test- 
ing, despite very high strength and hardness [ 3 , 4 ]. Previous stud- 
ies [ 4 , 5 ] suggest that equiatomic AlCrTiV HEA might go through 
an order-disorder transformation when cooling from high tempera- 
tures. When a material transforms from a disordered state to an or- 
dered state, the distribution of atoms changes from a random pat- 
tern to a periodic arrangement across a long distance in the crys- 
tal, which is also called long range ordering [6] . Assuming there is 
a A-B binary alloy of BCC structure ( Fig. 1 a), there are two sites in 
a BCC unit cell, one at the body center of the cubic and the other 
at the vertices. In the disordered state, A and B have the same pos- 
sibility of occupying either one of the two sites, but in the ordered 
state, A preferentially occupies the one site and B preferentially oc- 
cupies the other site [7] . 
This order-disorder transition is not unique in AlCrTiV system, 
which can be traced back to Ti-Al binary system. Fig. 1 b shows 
the Ti-Al binary phase diagram generated by CALculation of Phase 
Diagram (CALPHAD). In Ti-Al binary system, there is also an

the same. After the alloy goes through order-disorder transition, 
Ti preferentially occupies corner sites and Al preferentially takes 
center sites. As the temperature decreases, the differences in 
the fractions of the same element (Al and Ti) between the two 
sites grow bigger, which means the degree of ordering increases. 
Similar order-disorder phase transformations are widely reported 
in many Al-containing titanium alloys, such as Ti-Al [ 8 , 9 ], Ti-V-Al 
[ 10 , 11 ], Ti-Nb-Al [12] , Ti-Ta-Al [13] and Ti-Mo-Al alloys [14] . 
The order-disorder transition is considered as a second order 
phase transformation, because there is no change in chemical com- 
position [9–11] . On the contrary, a ﬁrst order phase transforma- 
tion requires the formation of new phases with chemical compo- 
sition changes from the matrix [6] . The driving force of this order- 
disorder transition is believed to be the negative enthalpy of mix- 
ing between Al and Ti [15] . Therefore, Al is not energetically fa- 
vored to be near other Al atoms. Instead, it is energetically fa- 
vored to be surrounded by Ti atoms. According to Atom Location 
by Channeling Enhanced Microanalysis (ALCHEMI) result, Al pref- 
erentially takes the body center position and Ti mostly take the 
corner position [16] .

The paper investigated the order-disorder transition and its ef- 
fects on the mechanical properties of lightweight AlCrTiV high en- 
tropy alloys. Speciﬁcally, it is found that Ti content has a signiﬁcant 
effect on the ordering and mechanical properties of AlCrTiV alloys. 
Bulk alloy ingots of various AlCrTiV alloys were prepared us- 
ing a vacuum induction furnace. The raw materials were melted 
and cooled in a water-cooled copper crucible. Each ingot was re- 
melted twice to minimize alloy chemistry inhomogeneity. Differ- 
ential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC) analysis was carried out using 
a TA Instruments SDT 650 simultaneous DSC/TGA (Thermogravi- 
metric Analysis). The DSC analysis was performed under a ﬂow of 
high purity argon gas, and an alumina crucible was used as the 
sample holder. The alloy samples were quickly heated to 600 °C 
at a heating rate of 20 °C/min, followed by a 20 min dwell. Then 
temperature was slowly increased to 1200 °C at a heating rate of 
5 °C/min. Dog-bone shaped tensile specimens were cut from in- 
gots using Electric Discharge Machining (EDM). The gage length 
is 25 mm and the cross section is 6 mm × 3 mm. The uniaxial 
tensile tests were performed on a MTS Criterion Electromechani- 
cal Universal Test System at room temperature. Tensile specimens 
were pull at constant crosshead speed, 0.003 mm/s. CALPHAD cal- 
culation was performed using Thermo-Calc coupled with TCHEA3 
database. Samples for TEM and scanning transmission electron mi- 
croscopy (STEM) characterization were prepared using a FEI Helios 
Nanolab TM dual beam system. TEM/STEM imaging and electron 
diffraction study were carried out in a FEI Tecnai TF20 TEM/STEM 
microscope operating at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV.

Fig. 4 a displays the Vickers hardness values vs. each alloying 
element content in AlCrTiV alloys. Due to the ordered nature of 
the BCC phase matrix, most of the AlCrTiV high entropy alloys 
demonstrate very high hardness values, which is consistent with 
the observation from other ordered beta titanium alloys [17] . It 
shows that the hardness value, maintaining above 500 HV, does 
not change signiﬁcantly with Al or Cr content when deviating from 
the equiatomic composition. On the contrary, the hardness drops 
considerably when Al/Cr or Al/Cr/V contents are simultaneously re- 
duced. 
It is also evident that the hardness drops the most when Al, Cr 
and V contents are lowered at the same time. For example, the 
hardness of Al 10 Cr 10 Ti 70 V 10 drops by 45% to 308 HV, compared 
to equiatomic AlCrTiV alloy’s hardness of 555 HV. The decrease in 
hardness could be caused by two reasons: ﬁrst, the reduced degree 
of ordering; and second, the reduced solid solution strengthening. 
The ﬁrst reason likely plays a major role in strengthening. It was 
reported in another study [18] that a reduced degree of ordering 
signiﬁcantly led to lower yield strength and lower elastic modu-

Based on above discussion on the effect of Ti content 
on ordering and hardening, tensile specimens of four com- 
positions, Al 25 Cr 25 Ti 25 V 25 , Al 20 Cr 20 Ti 40 V 20 , Al 15 Cr 15 Ti 55 V 15 , and 
Al 10 Cr 10 Ti 70 V 10 , were prepared using vacuum induction melting 
and casting. The tensile test results are listed in Table 2 . The ﬁrst 
three alloys with low Ti contents, 25, 40 and 50 at.%, have zero 
elongation, with samples fractured shortly after tensile loading. 
The Al 10 Cr 10 Ti 70 V 10 alloy is the only one with measurable ductil- 
ity, 1.1 ± 0.1% tensile elongation (based on three tests). A typical 
stress vs. strain curve of Al 10 Cr 10 Ti 70 V 10 is shown in Fig. 4 c. The 
yield strength and ultimate tensile strength are 877 ±5.2 MPa and 
889 ±8.9 MPa, respectively. The tensile test result is consistent with 
the hardness test, showing that with increasing Ti content, the Al- 
CrTiV high entropy alloys become softer and gain ductility, which 
is due to reduced degree of ordering. 
In summary, there is an order-disorder (A2-B2) transition at 
about 900 – 990 °C in the BCC microstructure of lightweight 
AlCrTiV high entropy alloy system. The transition temperatures 
calculated from CALPHAD modeling are in generally good agree- 
ment with experimental measurements by differential scanning 
calorimetry. Increasing Ti content from 40 to 70 at.% can lower the 
A2 to B2 transition temperature from about 966 to 900 °C, which 
reduces ordering and introduces ductility. The new Al 10 Cr 10 Ti 70 V 10 
alloy shows a measurable tensile elongation (1.1%) and excellent 
strengths (877 MPa yield strength and 889 MPa ultimate tensile 
strength), promising engineering applications where ductility 
is not a primary requirement such as automotive pistons. Our 
ongoing research is to introduce additional alloying elements to 
further ordering and improve the ductility of this new alloy.
