INTRODUCTION
Next-generation high-performance structural materials with
gigapascal strength, sufficient ductility, as well as low-density
design, have been pursued in environmentally friendly, energy-
saving engineering systems [1,2]. However, developing such
lightweight, strong, and ductile alloys has always been a chal-
lenging topic within the metallurgical community due to the
mutually exclusive nature of strength and ductility, and the
strength generally declines with decreasing mass density [3]. In
recent years, in pursuing optimal mechanical properties within a
seemingly infinite compositional space of alloys, an emerging
class of alloys containing multiple components in high con-
centrations, commonly known as high-entropy alloys (HEAs) or
complex concentrated alloys (CCAs), offer a vast compositional
space for their application-specific design, and demonstrate
distinct advantages over their dilute counterpart [4–6].
As a sub-group of CCAs, refractory CCAs (RCCAs) that
constitute mainly group IVB and VB refractory elements have
been noted for their high phase stability, unprecedented
mechanical strength, and superior heat-softening resistance [7–
11]. Nevertheless, these alloys commonly with body-centered
cubic (BCC) structures usually suffer from poor ductility and
deformability, with a compressive fracture strain of <10% at
ambient temperature. Besides, a great majority of them display
high densities of more than ~10 g cm
−3, which also hinders their
widespread applications [12,13]. For example, NbMoTaW and
VNbMoTaW can exhibit yield strength up to ~400 MPa at
1600°C, which is much higher than traditional Ni-based alloys
and refractory alloys, but they only show fracture strains of ~2%
under ambient compression tests [14]. To date, considerable
fruitful research has been devoted to developing RCCAs with
low density and outstanding strength-ductility synergy at
ambient temperature. Many light elements, e.g., Al, Ti, and V,
have been adopted to design lightweight CCAs. The lightweight
CCAs (LCCAs) such as Al-Nb-Ti-V-Zr, Cr-Nb-Ti-V-Zr [15,16],
and Mo-Nb-Ti-V-Zr [17,18] systems demonstrate relatively high
compressive strengths but little tensile ductility since their
structures contained intrinsically brittle BCC matrix or Al, Cr or
Zr-rich B2 and Laves phase. Such intermetallics generally pos-
sess large mismatch interfaces with the matrix or exhibit
inherent brittleness, resulting in severe brittleness of the RCCAs
[19–21]. Although there are occasional examples like
TiZrNb
0.5V0.5 [22], Ti 52Zr34Nb8Al6 [23], and Ti 1.5ZrNbAl0.3 [24]
alloys, which have a certain tensile strain, their strength is still
not satisfactory. Thus, developing the LCCAs simultaneously
with high strength and enough tensile ductility is still critical
and challenging work.

One effective and fundamental strategy for further optimiza-
tion of CCA design for balanced strength and ductility is to
regulate the inherent lattice distortions [25,26]. The severe lattice
distortions that arise from atomic radius and shear modulus
mismatch can exert high friction resistance against dislocation
migration, resulting in an increase in the achievable yield
strength of the CCAs without noticeable variation in micro-
structure [27]. Based on the Ti
3Zr1.5NbVAl0.25 RCCA [10,11], a
series of strong and ductile Ti 3Zr1.5Nb(1−x)MoxVAl0.25 (x = 0.1,
0.3, 0.5) RCCAs with a single BCC solid-solution-phase were
successfully developed by modulating Mo element. The high
shear modulus of the Mo element brings a pronounced shear
modulus mismatch and lattice distortion. The newly designed
non-equiatomic RCCAs possess a gigapascal yield strength and
large tensile fracture elongation over 15% in the as-cast state,
achieving an optimized tensile property compared with its Mo-
free counterpart. The current work demonstrates the simplicity
and efficacy of basic alloy-design principles in developing single-
phase RCCAs with outstanding strength-ductility synergy.
MATERIALSANDMETHODS
The Ti 3Zr1.5Nb(1−x)MoxVAl0.25 (x = 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, denoted as
Mo0.1, Mo0.3, Mo0.5, respectively) RCCA ingots were fabri-
cated by vacuum arc-melting technology by using the high-
purity metals (≥99.9 wt%) of Ti, Zr, Nb, V, Mo, Al. A non-
consumable vacuum arc melting furnace with high-purity argon
gas protection and a tungsten electrode was used to melt and
remelt the metals six times to ensure chemical homogeneity.
Each alloy ingot with a mass of about 80 g was then drop-cast
into a copper mold to form plates with dimensions of 12 mm
(width) × 60 mm (length) × 5.7 mm (thickness).
The phase constituents and crystal structures of the bulk
Ti
3Zr1.5Nb(1−x)MoxVAl0.25 alloys were identified via X-ray dif-
fraction (XRD, Rigaku D/max-2500 PC) analysis with Cu-Kα
radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) at a scanning rate of 4° min −1 from 20°
to 100°. The densities of these alloys were measured by the
Archimedes method. The microstructures, chemical composi-
tions, and fracture surfaces were characterized by electron-
backscattered diffraction (EBSD) and scanning electron micro-
scope (SEM, Tescan MIRA3) equipped with energy dispersive
spectroscopy (EDS). Samples for EBSD and SEM characteriza-
tion were first mechanically polished with a 2000-grit SiC
abrasive paper followed by polishing in a suspension of alumi-
num oxide with ~2 μm, then electropolished for 30 s in a mix-
ture of 6% perchloric acid, 35% n-butanol, and 59% methanol
(volume percent, vol%) at −35°C. The observations of the
deformed morphologies and the structure defect morphology
were carried out on a transmission electron microscope (TEM,
Talos F200X) operated at 200 kV. The TEM sample was firstly
ground down to ~50 μm in thickness and then polished to about
100 nm by twin-jet polishing in a solution of 6% perchloric acid,
35% n-butanol, and 59% methanol (vol%) at −35°C.
Uniaxial tensile tests were performed by using a universal
testing machine (Instron 5582) with a constant strain rate of 5 ×
10
−4 s−1 at room temperature. An extensometer with a gauge
length of 12.5 mm was used to monitor the strain. The dog-
bone-shaped tensile specimens had a gauge length of 15 mm, a
width of 2 mm, and a thickness of 1 mm. At least three tensile
tests were carried out to ensure the reproducibility of the results.

While most RCCAs with a BCC structure are known for their
high strength and insufficient ductility, both Mo0.3 and Mo0.5
alloys developed in this study display exceptional strength-
plasticity synergy. To unveil the origin of the unusual mechan-
ical behavior in high-strength Mo0.3 and Mo0.5 alloys, we first
examined the deformation microstructures at the grain scale.
Fig. 6a, c show the tri-color EBSD orientation maps of the two
alloys after tension to fracture. The crystal structures still
remained in the BCC lattice, and there was no sign of trans-
formation-induced plasticity during tensile deformation. Note
that some lenticular structures can be observed in both alloys
after deformation. As shown in Fig. 6e, the crystal misorienta-
tion of the lenticular structures was stochastic but in the range of
20°–40°, which rules out the possibility of mechanical twins,
which hold a misorientation of 60° angle for 112β <111> β twins
and 50.5° angle for 332β <113> β twins in BCC metals and alloys
[46,47]. This above crystallographic feature of the lenticular
structures exactly coincides with the kink bands, which are a
result of the slip-induced lattice rotation and orientate randomly
around the matrix. In this case, as a secondary deformation
process, the formation of kink bands can effectively accom-
modate dislocation slip and relieve stress concentration, thus
improving deformability and enhanced plasticity. Kink band
deformation was generally observed in hexagonal close-packed
(HCP) crystals with high plastic anisotropy, such as HCP Zn
alloys [48]. Recently, they have also been found in oxygen-
containing β-Ti alloys and some RCCAs with BCC lattices, such
as TiZrHfNb and TiZrTaHfNb under uniaxial tensile and
compression tests at various temperatures and strain rates [49–
51]. Another important characteristic in Fig. 6a, c is the color
gradients within the deformed grains, and the Kernel Average
Misorientation (KAM) maps in Fig. 6b, d also reveal that the

We further examined the dislocation configuration at different
tensile strain levels through TEM characterization. Fig. 7a–c
present the dislocation patterns of the Mo0.3 alloy. With a slight
interrupted strain of 5%, the dislocations have been sufficiently
nucleated and slipped. Typical planar slip dominates the plastic
deformation, and many planar-slip traces formed by dislocations
pile up along various directions, which implies that multiple
dislocation systems have been activated, as shown in Fig. 7a.
Subsequently, as the strain increases to fracture, a highly con-
centrated dislocation-wall structure formed because of a high-
stress level and dense planar dislocation arrays, as shown in
Fig. 7b. The accumulation of dislocations in these dislocation
walls inevitably generates dislocation entanglements, accom-
panied by the formation of many jogs and loops (Fig. 7c), which
can effectively suppress the movement of dislocations and
improve strain hardening. Fig. 7d presents the dislocation
morphology of the 5% pre-strained Mo0.5 alloy, where coplanar
dislocation slip dominates in this early plastic deformation, as
observed in the Mo0.3 alloy. As the deformation increases, the
dislocation band structure is preserved, but the spacing between
the bands decreases significantly, and the deformation micro-
structure finally tends to be more homogeneous, as shown in
Fig. 7e. A large number of dislocations with different Burgers
vectors continuously slip and interleave to form fine dislocation
network structures, generally referred to as Taylor lattices, in
which many high-density dislocation tangles surround the core
region of low-density dislocations, as shown in Fig. 7f.
As elaborated above, all core constituents of the observed high
strength and ductility appear to arise from the exceptionally
extensive dislocation interaction in the current RCCAs. Neither
alloy underwent deformation twinning or phase transformation
during tensile deformation. Actually, the diversity of dislocation
pathways in CCAs is considered one of the dominant mechan-
isms of high ductility, which is also greatly related to the che-
mical heterogeneity in the CCAs. Several studies have
demonstrated that the atomic arrangement in CCAs may not be
ideally disordered due to the complex interactions between the
constituent elements and the diversity of atomic sizes, and
verified the existence of chemical short-range ordering (CSROs)
both theoretically and experimentally. Recently, the (Zr, Al)
enriched CSROs were visualized in a similar Ti
50Zr18Nb15V12Al5
alloy, which promotes elastic strains and second-generation
planar-slip bands to cause dynamic hardening and large plasti-
city [45]. Other than its robust solid solution strengthening
effect, Mo appears to have a tendency towards promoting phase
separation in such alloy systems [17]. In particular, Mo displays
an inverse segregation inclination with respect to Zr and Al (as
shown in Fig. 2), and these aforementioned points were used to
afford appreciable degrees of CSROs in these alloys. The for-
mation of CSROs promotes planar slip due to the glide plane
softening effect [52,53]. In the early stage of plastic deformation,
planar slip dominates along various directions (Fig. 7a, d). Upon
reaching higher stress levels, dislocations sustained shear the
CSROs domains. As a result, a continuous coplanar slip of
dislocations appears. Coplanar slip encourages dislocation
nucleation, accelerates dislocation multiplication, and leads to
homogeneity of dislocation substructure [53–56]. These features
enhance work hardening capacity, cause avoidance of premature
necking, and ultimately contribute to high ductility at such high
strength levels.

CONCLUSIONS
In summary, we proposed a series of lightweight Ti 3Zr1.5Nb(1−x)-
MoxVAl0.25 RCCAs with outstanding as-cast tensile properties
and single-phase BCC solid-solution structure. The addition of
the Mo element with a relatively high shear modulus efficiently
promotes distorted lattices, leading to a substantially greater
yield strength with only little sacrifice of ductility in the alloys, as
compared to its counterpart without the Mo element. The cur-
rent Mo0.3 and Mo0.5 alloy exhibit tensile yield strengths
exceeding 1100 MPa with a fracture elongation of over 15%,
better than most reported RCCAs. The solution strengthening
model indicated that the high yield strength of Ti 3Zr1.5Nb(1−x)-
MoxVAl0.25 RCCAs was mainly derived from the atomic radius
misfit and shear modulus misfit. Mo element imparted sig-
nificant solid-solution strengthening to the alloys. The tensile
deformation is dominantly through the typical nucleation, pla-
nar migration, and entanglement of dislocations, while no twins
or second phases were detected. Various deformation sub-
structures (kink bands, Taylor lattices, and DDWs) effectively
prevent dislocation motion, thus improving the work-hardening
capacity and plasticity. Our results provide design guidelines for
novel strong and ductile RCCAs.
