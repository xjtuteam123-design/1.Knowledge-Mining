ABSTRACT
Ni-based single crystal superalloys, as crucial materials in the aviation and aerospace industry, frequently 
encounter fatigue failure induced by cyclic loading, which is one of the primary failure modes. In this study, 
molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are utilized to explore the cyclic strengthening mechanisms of Ni-based 
single crystal superalloys, with a focus on dislocation evolution under cyclic loading. Two typical feature 
atomistic models of the alloys are constructed and dislocations are introduced under cyclic loading, investigating 
the interactions between dislocations and the γ / γ ′ interface. The results highlight the excellent capacity of the 
interfacial dislocation network for dislocation deposition, particularly for those attempting to penetrate the γ ′ 
phase, and capture a transition in emission dislocations slip plane from the { 111 } plane to the { 100 } plane. The 
dislocation absorption is driven by two primary mechanisms: the formation of stable link points at the γ / γ ′ 
interface and the obstructive effect of the γ ′ phase. Additionally, a stress stratification phenomenon at the γ / γ ′ 
interface is observed, hindering dislocation movement during loading and leading to dislocation trapping 
through cross-slip during unloading. Furthermore, the simulations reveal two distinct forms of dislocation bar -
riers pile-up within the γ phase: one arising from the decomposition of the interfacial dislocation network, which 
leads to the emergence of stacking faults (SFs) bands and Lomer-Cottrell lock; the other stemming from the 
formation of SFs bands due to the decomposition of the emission dislocations within the γ phase channel. These 
findings provide meaningful insights into the cyclic hardening behavior of Ni-based superalloys.
1. Introduction
Ni-based single crystal superalloys have garnered significant atten -
tion for their outstanding mechanical properties, including high strength 
and toughness at both room and elevated temperatures, excellent plas -
ticity and fracture toughness, superior creep and fatigue resistance, 
excellent oxidation and corrosion resistance ( Chen et al., 2023a ; Khoei 
et al., 2022 ; Long et al., 2018 ; Shi et al., 2024 ). These characteristics 
make Ni-based single crystal superalloys one of the most extensively 
used high-temperature materials in the aviation and aerospace industry, 
particularly in critical components such as turbine blades in aircraft 
engines ( Chaudhary et al., 2022 ; Lu et al., 2022 ; Zhang et al., 2023a ). 
During service, turbine blades are continuously subjected to 
high-temperature, high-pressure gas flow impacts, as well as cyclic 
stresses caused by the engine start-up, shutdown, speed variations and 
aerodynamically vibratory excitations ( Cervellon et al., 2018 , 2020 ; 
Chen et al., 2020 ; Sun et al., 2020 ). Fatigue failure induced by these 
cyclic stresses is a key factor determining the lifespan and reliability of 
turbine blades ( Jiang et al., 2021 ; Li et al., 2024a ). Therefore, an 
in-depth investigation into the fatigue behavior of Ni-based single 
crystal superalloys under cyclic loading is of great significance for 
improving their performance and extending their service life.
Due to the unique γ / γ ′ dual-phase structure in Ni-based single crystal 
superalloys, the evolution of microstructure in the alloys predominantly 
governs the cyclic response characteristics. Numerous studies have 
demonstrated that the γ ′ phase and the γ / γ ′ semi-coherent interfacial 
dislocation network are the primary reasons for the high fatigue resis -
tance of the alloys ( Fan et al., 2019 , 2020 ; Wang et al., 2023 ). Low-cycle

fatigue (LCF) tests on Ni-based single crystal superalloys reveal that the 
cyclic response exhibits significant γ ′ phase orientation dependence, 
with the fatigue resistance of alloys solidified in the [ 001 ] direction being 
notably superior to those in the [ 011 ] and [ 111 ] directions ( Dou et al., 
2023 ; Zhang et al., 2019 ). The γ / γ ′ interface can deposit dislocations and 
form an interfacial dislocation network that hinders dislocation motion, 
enhancing the strength of the alloy ( Tan et al., 2020 ; Wang et al., 2023 ). 
The cyclic hardening and softening behaviors of Ni-based single crystal 
superalloys, which arise from these microstructural characteristics, have 
become key research focal points. By reason of the high-strength γ ′ 
phase, dislocations primarily accumulate in the γ phase channels, 
forming stacking faults (SFs), Orowan loops, cross-slip structures, and 
continuous Lomer-Cottrell lock structures, which hinder dislocation 
motion and reduce interactions between dislocations on different slip 
systems, leading to cyclic hardening effects ( Chen et al., 2023b ; Liang 
et al., 2023 ). On the other side, cyclic softening is primarily driven by 
the dissolution of the γ ′ phase, the transformation of interface disloca -
tion networks, and the annihilation of dislocations ( Liu et al., 2017 ; 
Wang et al., 2021 ; Zhang et al., 2019 ). Additionally, prolonged cyclic 
loading can lead to directional coarsening in Ni-based single crystal 
superalloys, which in turn results in a deterioration of the alloy ’ s me -
chanical properties ( Brien and D ´ecamps, 2001 ; Fan et al., 2020 ). 
Le ´on-C ´azares et al. describe cyclic hardening by the formation of slip 
bands and the evolution of local dislocation density, while the accu -
mulation of slip irreversibility is employed to describe cyclic softening, 
thereby constructing a microstructural model to describe the low-cycle 
fatigue behavior of Ni-based superalloys ( Le ´on-C ´azares et al., 2020 ). 
Currently, the mechanism of cyclic strengthening in Ni-based single 
crystal superalloys, attributed to the dislocations pile-up in γ phase 
channels, has been widely recognized by researchers. However, the 
formation and functional mechanisms of this dislocation pile-up remain 
underexplored and warrant further in-depth investigation.
Moreover, the conclusions derived from experimental methods can 
only describe the observable subset of phenomena, and in-situ micro -
structural characterization experiments face significant challenges and 
limitations. Consequently, experimental results often fall short in 
providing a thorough explanation of the microstructure evolution of the 
alloys. To fully uncover the processes and underlying mechanisms of 
microstructure evolution in Ni-based singe crystal superalloys, molec -
ular dynamics (MD) simulations have emerged as an essential research 
tool, providing atomic-scale details that complement experimental re -
sults ( Khoei et al., 2021 ; Motamedi et al., 2024 ; Shen et al., 2024 ; Wu 
et al., 2021 ; Xiong et al., 2018 ; Yang et al., 2024 ; Yin et al., 2021 , 2022 ; 
Zhang et al., 2022 ). Wu et al. conduct extensive atomic-scale studies on 
the cyclic loading of Ni-based single crystal superalloys by MD simula -
tions, which indicate that at low temperatures, the deformation mech -
anism under cyclic loads is primarily driven by dislocation and SFs 
shearing of the γ ′ phase, while at higher temperatures, it shifts to the 
formation of Orowan loops and dislocation climb mechanisms ( Chen 
et al., 2020 ; Wu et al., 2022 , 2023 ). Zhang et al. investigate the fatigue 
deformation behavior of Ni-based single crystal superalloys at different 
temperatures through MD method and demonstrate that the fatigue 
process can be divided into a hardening stage, characterized by the 
continuous breakdown of the interfacial dislocation network, and a 
saturation stage, marked by the propagation of emission dislocations 
( Zhang et al., 2023b ). Yin et al. employ MD simulation to analyze the 
evolution of defects under creep-fatigue loading across various orien -
tation models, further substantiating the fracture mechanisms they have 
identified through experimental studies ( Yin et al., 2023 ). These studies 
enrich the understanding of the dislocation evolution mechanisms under 
cyclic loading in Ni-based single crystal superalloys, providing insights 
that are difficult to obtain through traditional experimental approaches. 
Accordingly, MD method can be leveraged to delve deeper into the cy -
clic strengthening mechanisms of the microstructure in Ni-based single 
crystal superalloys, offering a systematic and comprehensive analysis of 
the dislocation pile-up processes that may not be observable in experi -
mental studies.
In this study, MD method is employed to investigate the interaction 
between dislocations and the γ / γ ′ interface in the Ni-based single crystal 
superalloy atomistic models. Two dislocations are continuously intro -
duced under cyclic loading conditions to provide a comprehensive 
analysis of the cyclic strengthening mechanisms. The structure of this 
paper is as follows: Section 2 describes the atomistic model and the 
specific settings of the MD simulations; Section 3 presents the disloca -
tion evolution results from the MD simulations and analyzes the cyclic 
strengthening mechanisms of the Ni-based single crystal superalloy; 
finally, Section 4 provides a summary of the study.

free boundary conditions causes the simulation box to continuously

primary reason for choosing free boundary conditions is that our model

they inevitably lead to additional local stress concentrations in the

that the impact of free boundary conditions on the simulation results is

observation of dislocation movement and reactions under cyclic loading. 
Dislocation climb, a thermally activated mechanism, becomes signifi -
cant at elevated temperatures in macroscopic systems ( Hull and Bacon, 
2001 ). However, due to the inherent limitations of classical MD simu -
lations — such as small time and spatial scales and high strain rate -
s — atomic short-range diffusion can occur even at moderate 
temperatures like 300 K, potentially resulting in dislocation climb. For 
our model, the microscale dimensions, relative to macroscopic mate -
rials, exacerbate the effects of such short-range climb, which could lead 
to dislocations leaving the simulation box and affecting the results. It 
should be noted that while this simulated environment neglects the 
more complex microstructural evolution induced by temperature ef -
fects, it does not diminish the applicability of our findings to Ni-based 
single crystal superalloys. After energy minimization by the conjugate 
gradient (CG) method, the simulations are run for 100,000 timesteps 
(100 ps, ps) without any applied load to ensure sufficient relaxation.

3.2. The dislocation pile-up in model B
Fig. 4 illustrates the structure evolution of Model B during the first 
dislocation emission towards the γ ′ phase. Fig. 4 (a~c) shows that in the 
initial stages of loading, the dislocation glides on the { 111 } plane and 
exhibits a curved shape due to the influence of the stress field from the 
interfacial dislocation network ( Li et al., 2024b ). The interfacial dislo -
cation network on the left side comes into contact with and is truncated 
by the emission dislocation first. As the load continues to increase, the 
interfacial dislocation network on the right side also comes into contact 
with the emission dislocation ( Fig. 4 (d)). The part of the emission 
dislocation in contact with the γ / γ ′ interface does not move further, and 
only the dislocation in the channel continues to bow out as shown in 
Fig. 4 (e~f), similar with previous study ( Xiong et al., 2017 ).
During the unloading status, significant changes occur in dislocation 
evolution. In the early stages of unloading, the bowing part of the 
dislocation in the channel gradually retracts until it is nearly parallel to 
the adjacent γ ′ phases, as shown in Fig. 4 (g). However, as unloading 
continues, the dislocation does not continue to move in the direction 
opposite to the loading status but instead climbs along the [ 011 ] edge 
dislocations of the left interfacial dislocation network onto the { 011 }
plane ( Fig. 4 (h~i)). Since the { 011 } plane is not a slip plane for FCC 
crystal structures, the dislocation movement in this direction primarily 
involves the decomposition of the [ 011 ] edge dislocations.
Fig. 5 displays the structural evolution of the second dislocation 
emission for Model B. After the emission of the first dislocation, the 
{ 111 } plane on which the dislocation moves is almost devoid of dislo -
cations, as shown in Fig. 5 (a). However, even in the absence of an 
interfacial dislocation network, the γ ′ phase still impedes the dislocation 
motion due to the high potential energy ( Xie et al., 2022 ). Only a small 
portion of the dislocation enters the γ ′ phase, with a pronounced dislo -
cation bowing observed in the central channel. Additionally, due to the 
presence of a few dislocation segments at the right boundary of the γ ′ 
phase, the emission dislocation interacts and becomes entangled with 
these segments. During the unloading process ( Fig. 5 (d~f)), the dislo -
cation continues to move on the { 111 } plane, exhibiting a significant 
tendency to move backward. However, due to the interaction and 
entanglement of some dislocation segments, it remains unable to leave 
the interface and piles up in the channel.

3.3. The effect of dislocation position
The simulations for the second dislocation at the upper and lower 
positions are conducted based on the results from Section 3.1 and Sec -
tion 3.2 , where it was observed that, under loading status, the disloca -
tions in the lower half tend to slip while those in the upper half remain 
stationary. Therefore, the second dislocation in the upper half corre -

network, while the second dislocation in the lower half corresponds to 
the interaction without an interfacial dislocation network. On the other 
hand, due to the non-periodic boundary conditions applied, the position 
of the dislocations in the X direction is not adjusted to avoid boundary 
effects, which only affects the time of contact between the dislocation

with the results discussed in former sections, in this part, the analysis 
primarily focuses on the evolution of the second dislocation.

partials neutralizing with the positive shear stress at the γ / γ ′ interface as 
it attempts to traverse into the γ ′ phase, creating a distinct low stress 
zone that hinders the movement of the trailing Shockley partial as 
shown in Fig. 6(c2) . From the simulation results across various sce -
narios, it is evident that the trailing dislocation experiences significant 
difficulty when crossing the γ / γ ′ interface into the γ ′ phase. In contrast, 
the leading dislocation, while somewhat easier to penetrate into the γ ′ 
phase, is constrained due to its connection to the trailing dislocation via 
stacking faults (SFs). The spacing between the two dislocations is related 
to the energy required to form the stacking faults, causing the movement 
of leading dislocations to be obstructed due to the trailing dislocation.
Similarly, the cross-slip phenomenon observed during unloading at 
the γ / γ ′ interface is also attributed to the stress stratification at the γ / γ ′ 
interface. Dislocations entering the γ ′ phase experience interference 
from the stratified shear stress upon re-entering the γ phase through the 
γ / γ ′ interface, leading to the occurrence of cross-slip. Therefore, the 
stress stratification at the γ / γ ′ interface and the neutralization of shear 
stress on the trailing dislocation as it crosses the γ / γ ′ interface are key 
mechanisms behind the dislocation blocking effect of γ ′ phase in the 
absence of an interfacial dislocation network.

It should be noted that our simulations primarily focus on investi -
gating the cyclic strengthening mechanisms arising from the in -
teractions between dislocations and the dual-phase structure of Ni-based 
alloys by constructing dislocations in γ phase. This approach is based on 
the assumption that dislocations predominantly originate from the γ 
matrix phase, an assumption supported by the majority of experimental 
and simulation results, where dislocations are primarily distributed 
within the γ phase matrix ( Chen et al., 2020 ; Liang et al., 2023 ; Liu et al., 
2017 ; Wang, et al., 2018 , 2023 ; Wu et al., 2023 ; Zhang, et al., 2019 , 
2020 , 2023a ). However, due to this assumption, our study cannot 
evaluate the impact of superdislocations formed within the γ ′ phase on 
the cyclic plasticity of the alloy. Additionally, the use of a simplified 
model neglects the effects of multiple solid solute elements present in 
the alloy and suppresses dislocation climb behavior through 
low-temperature conditions. As a result, beyond the two mechanisms 
described in our study, real-world cyclic strengthening mechanisms in 
Ni-based single crystal superalloys are expected to involve other, more 
complex processes. Nonetheless, in conjunction with experimental 
conclusions, the two mechanisms described in our work undoubtedly 
play a critical role in the cyclic strengthening mechanisms of Ni-based 
single crystal superalloys.
4. Conclusions
In this study, two typical feature models of Ni-based single crystal 
superalloy are constructed using MD method to investigate the evolution 
of dislocations under cyclic loading. The following key conclusions are 
drawn. 
(1) The interfacial dislocation network in Ni-based single crystal 
superalloy exhibits an excellent capacity for dislocation deposi -
tion, particularly for dislocations attempting to penetrate the γ ′ 
phase. Additionally, a phenomenon of slip plane transition from 
the { 111 } plane to the { 100 } plane is captured. The dislocation 
absorption primarily operates through two mechanisms: the 
formation of stable link points at the γ / γ ′ interface due to the 
reactions between the emission dislocations and the interfacial 
dislocation network, and the obstructive effect of the γ ′ phase on 
dislocation movement.
(2) During loading status, a stress stratification phenomenon is pre -
sent at the γ / γ ′ interface, causing the trailing Shockley partial 
dislocation, decomposed from the emission perfect dislocation, to 
be stress-neutralized and unable to enter the γ ′ phase. During 
unloading status, the leading dislocation experiences cross-slip as 
it exits, causing the dislocation to become trapped at the γ / γ ′ 
interface. This is a key mechanism by which the γ ′ phase impedes 
dislocation motion in the absence of an interfacial dislocation 
network.
(3) For the dislocation pile-up behavior in the γ phase of Ni-based 
single crystal superalloy, our MD simulations reveal two forms 
of dislocation barrier formation. One is the reaction between the 
emission dislocations and the interfacial dislocation network, 
leading to the decomposition of the interfacial dislocation 
network and the formation of SFs bands and Lomer-Cottrell lock 
structures. The other is the formation of SFs bands from the 
decomposition of the emission dislocations moving within the γ 
phase channel.
Finally, we must emphasize the limitations of our study. First, our 
results are based on atomic-scale simulations of the simplified Ni/Ni
3
Al 
system, which neglect the significant roles played by the diverse solid 
solute elements present in actual Ni-based single crystal superalloys. 
Additionally, our simulations suppress dislocation climb behavior by 
operating at low temperatures, whereas the cyclic strengthening 
mechanisms in real Ni-based single crystal superalloys are likely to be 
more complex than those described in this study. However, our study 
innovatively constructs localized models of Ni-based single crystal su -
peralloys to investigate their dislocation responses under cyclic loading. 
This approach reveals the mechanisms of dislocation deposition at in -
terfaces and within channels, as well as the associated cyclic strength -
ening mechanisms, providing an atomistic theoretical reference for 
further elucidating the cyclic behavior of Ni-based single crystal 
superalloys.
