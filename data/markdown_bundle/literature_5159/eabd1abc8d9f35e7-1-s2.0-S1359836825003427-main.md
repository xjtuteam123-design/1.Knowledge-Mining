ABSTRACT
To address the persistent challenge of balancing room and elevated temperature mechanical properties in con -
ventional near α titanium alloys, this study proposes an innovative approach through compositional design and 
thermomechanical treatment process optimization. A high Zr/Si containing near- α titanium alloy is developed by 
decreasing-temperature multidirectional forging (DMDF) and subsequent heat treatment, yielding a trimodal 
microstructure consisting of lamellar primary α -phase ( α
l
), equiaxed primary α -phase ( α
e
) and transformed 
β -phase ( β
t
). This microstructure features a hierarchical dispersion of dual-scale silicides, where submicron-scale 
silicides are preferentially distributed along grain/phase boundaries, while nanoscale silicides are uniformly 
dispersed within grains. After DMDF followed by solution treatment at 950 
◦
C/40min (HT2), the alloy achieves 
superior room-temperature mechanical properties (UTS = 1306.6 MPa, EL = 7.5 %), primarily attributed to 
synergistic strengthening effects involving multiscale precipitates ( α
S 
and dual-scale silicides), dislocation net -
works and activated slip systems. The alloy maintains excellent strength at elevated temperature up to 650 
◦
C, 
demonstrating a UTS of 799.8 MPa paired with an elongation of 16.2 %. This sustained strength originates from 
strain gradient formation at α / β interfaces combined with dual-scale silicides pinning mechanisms. Additionally, 
the enhanced ductility at 650 
◦
C arises from the slip bands within the α -phase and additional < c + a > dislocation 
activation. This design strategy of trimodal microstructure with hierarchical dispersion of dual-scale silicides 
provides a new perspective for tailoring high-temperature titanium alloys with balanced mechanical properties 
at both room and elevated temperatures.
1. Introduction
The advancement of structural materials with multifunctional per -
formance remains a critical enabler for next-generation high-perfor -
mance technologies. Titanium alloys, especially high-temperature alloys 
known as “ space metals ” , have become indispensable structural mate -
rials because of their low density, excellent high-temperature strength, 
and strong thermal stability [ 1 – 3 ]. In recent years, their rapidly 
expanding applications in complex components operating over a wide 
range of temperature conditions require materials that can better 
harmonize room temperature and elevated temperature properties. 
While significant progress has been achieved in optimizing 
room-temperature strength-ductility synergies through strategies such 
as using interstitial solutes to tune the microstructural variables of the 
α -precipitation phase at the grain boundaries [ 4 ], obtaining a nano 
precipitation phase and ultrafine grains [ 5 ], constructing multiscale 
networks [ 6 ], and designing special heterostructures [ 7 ], the concurrent 
attainment of balanced room temperature and elevated temperature 
performance remains an unresolved challenge in materials engineering.

The conventional microstructures of titanium alloys demonstrate 
inherent limitations in reconciling room- and elevated-temperature 
mechanical properties. Wang et al. [ 14 ] achieved fabrication of an 
ultrafine-grained equiaxed microstructure through quenching and 
thermomechanical processing of engineered Ti6Al4V5Cu alloy. While

combining equiaxed α grains with β -transformed matrix, was initially 
postulated to enable synergistic property enhancement [ 16 , 17 ]. Recent 
investigations [ 18 , 19 ] involving thermomechanical processing (forging, 
rolling) and heat treatment of near- α titanium alloys demonstrated that 
bimodal microstructure underperformed relative to idealized pre -

room-temperature ductility but lacks thermal stability, while trans -

constraining low-temperature ductility. These intrinsic property trade -
offs highlight the fundamental limitations of conventional single- or 
dual-phase microstructures in achieving balanced multitemperature 
performance, necessitating innovative microstructural design strategies 
to resolve these conflicting mechanical requirements.
The resolution to this longstanding dilemma draws inspiration from 
recent breakthroughs in heterogeneous microstructure engineering 
across advanced alloy systems. Xiao et al. [ 20 ] demonstrated that pre -
cisely designed hierarchical grain architectures in intermetallic com -
pounds, achieved through controlled thermomechanical processing 
(TMP), enable remarkable synergy between high strength and superior 
electrical conductivity at both room and intermediate temperatures. 
Complementary work by Lu et al. [ 21 ] on cast eutectic high-entropy 
alloys (EHEAs) revealed exceptional retention of strength-conductivity 
balance across extended temperature ranges. Their analysis identified 
the alternating nanoscale L1

erative hardening-softening effects between phase domains as the 
fundamental mechanisms underlying these enhanced properties. These 
advances collectively underscore the critical importance of multiscale 
phase architecture optimization and interfacial engineering in over -
coming conventional property trade-offs. In titanium alloys, according 
to the phase transition theory, high-temperature titanium alloys un -
dergoing severe plastic deformation (SPD) at different temperatures 
above or below the β -transus temperature can exhibit a single 
basket-weave or equiaxed microstructure [ 15 , 22 , 23 ], and the formation 
of different microstructures is related to the SPD temperature. Building 
on these fundamental insights and integrating design principles from 
heterogeneous alloy systems, we propose a novel approach for 
high-temperature titanium alloys involving cross- β -transus 
decreasing-temperature multidirectional forging (DMDF). This strategy 
enables the development of trimodal microstructure while relying on the 
sensitivity of silicide precipitation to temperature (different deformation 
temperatures cause differences in silicide size) to achieve a gradient 
distribution of silicides.
In this work, we have developed a novel high Zr/Si near- α titanium 
alloy through compositional design and thermomechanical treatment 
process optimization. The compositional design of high Zr/Si is condu -
cive to maximizing the value of developing multiple alloying and pro -
moting silicide precipitation strengthening. Combined with DMDF and 
subsequent heat treatment, we successfully designed a trimodal micro -
structure including lamellar primary α -phase ( α

HT2 alloy exhibits excellent room and elevated temperature properties. 
The evolution of the trimodal microstructure, the precipitation and 
dissolution mechanism of submicron-nanosilicides, and the origin of the 
strengthening-toughening synergism of HT2 alloy at room and elevated 
temperatures are further analyzed.

3.3. Room and elevated temperature mechanical properties
Fig. 6 a shows the room-temperature tensile stress-strain curves of the 
a s- DMDFed alloy and the HT series alloys with different heat-treatment 
temperatures. The as-DMDFed alloy exhibits an ultimate tensile strength 
(UTS) of 1148.7 MPa, a yield strength (YS) of 1065.8 MPa, and an 
elongation (EL) of 8 %. Compared to the a s- DMDFed alloy, the UTS and 
YS of the HT series alloys showed different degrees after heat treatment. 
The UTS increased by 5.5 % – 19.0 % (HT3 to HT1 alloy), while the YS 
showed a more pronounced enhancement from 10.1 % to 24.0 %. 
Compared to the HT1 alloy, the ductility recovery of the HT2 alloy 
comes from the dissolution of the large-sized S

hinders the formation of microcracks. For HT3 alloy, although the size of

the above analysis, the HT1 alloy has the highest tensile strength, with a 
UTS of 1366.4 MPa and a YS of 1321.7 MPa. The HT2 alloy exhibited the 
best room-temperature strength-ductility match, combining a UTS of 
1306.6 MPa with an elongation (EL) of 7.5 %.
Fig. 6 b shows the strain-hardening rate curves of the alloys in the a s- 
DMDFed and heat-treated states. The strain hardening rate curve of the 
a s- DMDFed alloy exhibites three typical stages. The strain hardening 
rate decreases sharply in the first stage, which is due to the lack of active 
dislocations in the predeformation stage, and more dislocation slip 
needs to be turned on in the predeformation stage to accommodate the 
increase in the constant strain rate. During the second stage, the plateau 
period is reached as the dislocations become entangled with each other 
and the number of movable dislocations decreases. In the third stage, 
with the continuous increase in strain, the strain hardening rate de -
creases rapidly, and strain softening dominates. In contrast, the HT se -
ries alloys exhibit a strain hardening rate decline rate in the first stage 
identical to that of the as-DMDFed alloy. However, their second-stage 
plateau period is markedly shorter, accompanied by a uniform reduc -
tion in strain hardening rate, reflecting more pronounced ductility 
instability.
Fig. 6 c compares the room-temperature tensile properties of the 
studied titanium alloys with those of other materials. Although titanium 
matrix composites (TMCs) have high strength due to reinforcement, 
their low elongation limits their application. Conventional types of ti -
tanium alloys exhibit good ductility but low strength. New high- 
temperature titanium alloys demonstrate moderate strength-ductility 
improvements. Examples include the HT1 and HT2 alloys in this 
study, as well as DsTi700 reported in prior works, all exhibiting 
enhanced performance.
Fig. 7 presents representative fracture morphologies of HT-series 
alloys under room-temperature tensile testing, revealing ductile 
fracture characteristics dominated by dimple structures. The HT1 alloy 
fracture surface contains tear ridges and microcracks ( Fig. 7 a), while 
HT2 alloy displays homogeneous dimple distribution with enhanced 
micropore density ( Fig. 7 b). This micropore nucleation within stress 
concentration zones during deformation effectively mitigates premature 
fracture risks and improves ductility. Conversely, HT3 alloy exhibits 
increased microcrack density, enlarged tear ridge dimensions, and 
consequently reduced elongation ( Fig. 7 c).
Detailed analysis reveals that the HT1 alloy exhibits smaller de -
pressions and deeper dimples ( Fig. 7a1 ), suggesting exposure to higher 
stresses and consequently enhanced strength. The HT2 alloy has a high

4.1.1. Precipitation and dissolution of submicron-nanosilicides
The as-DMDFed alloy exhibits dual-scale silicides distribution 
( Fig. 3 c), attributed to the combined influence of deformation temper -

connection with the processing history, it can be speculated that dual- 
scale silicides are precipitated and grown during the DMDF process in 
a sequential order. The preferred precipitation location is mainly at the 
grain boundaries, phase boundaries, and subgrain boundaries, followed 
by intracrystalline dislocations. After heat treatment, the size and con -
tent of silicides decreased with increasing temperature ( Fig. 3 d – i, 
Fig. 4 b, c), indicating that the dissolution of silicides occurred.

mation, silicides preferentially precipitate from grain and phase 
boundaries under thermal activation. From a thermodynamic point of 
view, the high energy stored in dislocations at grain and phase bound -
aries (as facet defects) rather than in line defects elevates interfacial free 
energy, thereby reducing the nucleation energy barrier for silicides

4.2. Strengthening and toughening mechanism at room temperature
The TEM analysis of the HT2 alloy near the fracture surface reveals 
critical insights into its superior strength-ductility synergy, as illustrated 
in Fig. 14 . Fig. 14 a and b demonstrate substantial accumulation of 
geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) and slip traces along α / β 
phase interfaces, suggesting that during deformation, multiple disloca -
tions migrated to these interfaces where they became mutually entan -
gled, creating localized stress concentrations. This interfacial stress 
accumulation ultimately led to slip band formation when the critical 
shear stress threshold was exceeded at these interfaces. The restricted 
dislocation slip distance within this microstructure facilitates effective 
dislocation pinning by the S
2 
phase, resulting in a large number of dis -
locations aligned along their respective slip surfaces, forming charac -
teristic dislocation networks ( Fig. 14 c).
To further investigate dislocation configurations within the α -phase, 
TEM characterization under two-beam conditions employing the g ⋅ b =
0 invisibility criterion [ 62 ] revealed distinct dislocation activation pat -
terns. Both < a > -type and < c > -type dislocations were observed 
( Fig. 14 d and e), and < c + a > -type dislocations were not observed. This 
preferential activation can be attributed to the lower critical resolved 
shear stress (CRSS) required for basal and prismatic slip systems in the 
α -phase under applied loads, promoting the dominance of these slip 
modes and subsequent entanglement of similar dislocation types. 
Notably, the absence of pyramidal slip observations indicates that room 
temperature deformation in HT2 alloy primarily occurs through 
< a > -type dislocation motion.
The exceptional ductility of the HT2 alloy is due mainly to the large 
number of slip bands within the α -phase during deformation, and the 
dislocations become larger in the mean free path, which effectively re -
leases the stress concentration and prevents premature failure at the 
interface [ 63 ].

4.3. The origin of excellent high-temperature strength and ductility
It is widely believed that enhancing the dislocation storage capacity 
of grains and inhibiting grain boundary sliding are the keys to improving 
high-temperature mechanical properties [ 75 ]. In the HT2 alloy, both α
e 
and α
l 
are immobilized by surrounding β
t 
( Fig. 5 a – b), effectively 
restricting grain rotation during high-temperature tensile deformation. 
This configuration simultaneously inhibits interphase boundary slip and 
enhances phase interface stability. Furthermore, the strategically 
distributed S
2 
phase provides additional strengthening by impeding 
dislocation motion.
To elucidate these mechanisms, we conducted post-failure analyses 
of HT2 alloy specimens through dual perspectives of matrix phase in -
terfaces and precipitation phase ( Figs. 16 – 17 ). During high-temperature 
fracture, microcracks first sprout along the α / β
t 
interface due to the 
different microstructures between the two phases, which creates a soft- 
hard mismatch that induces strain incompatibility ( Fig. 9d2 ). Observa -
tion of the two-phase interface reveals that dislocations close to the 
interface are entangled to form geometrically necessary dislocation 
(GNDs) inside the β
t
-surrounded α phase ( Fig. 16 a). Fig. 16 b shows the 
high density of dislocations present in the β -phase. The strain gradient 
formed by interfacial GNDs and β -phase dislocations compensates for 
the structural load-bearing asymmetry between phases through 
dislocation-mediated stress redistribution. This mechanism significantly 
strengthens the softer β -phase, prevents strain localization, and activates 
α -phase deformation, collectively enhancing overall strength [ 76 ]. In 
addition, the slip bands within the α -phase also opened with deforma -
tion ( Fig. 16 c), which improved the dislocation slip capacity and sup -
pressed the dislocation interaction entanglement to a certain extent.
Further identification of the α -phase internal dislocation configura -
tion, many GNDs are distributed within the α phase, with a cross- 
distribution of < a > -type and < c > -type dislocations ( Fig. 16 d and e). 
This is attributed to the fact that the dislocation slip regime of the 
α -phase makes it easier for basal and columnar slips to be activated due 
to the low CRSS, which in turn results in the entanglement of disloca -
tions of the same type with each other. In addition to the two types of 
dislocations, < c + a > -type dislocations are also activated. The CRSS of 
conical < c + a > -type dislocations is two orders of magnitude greater 
than that of basal slip-type dislocations and is extremely difficult to 
activate. One possible explanation is that the strain accumulated during 
high-temperature deformation and that the strain gradient near the two- 
phase interface provides the driving force needed to activate additional 
< c + a > -type dislocations. These < c + a > -type dislocations help to 
accommodate strain along the c-axis and improve ductility. As defor -
mation proceeded, the activation of both slip bands and < c + a > -type 
dislocations effectively increased the high-temperature ductility.
Fig. 16 f clearly shows the HRTEM morphology of the α and β in -
terfaces, and the corresponding Fourier (FFT) patterns reveal that the 
orientation relationship between α and β is [1 10 1]
α

inverse Fourier variation (IFFT) of the FFT images indicates periodic 
interfacial mismatch dislocations at the α / β phase interface to harmo -
nize the interfacial relationship between the two sides. Geometric phase 
analysis (GPA) was used to observe the strain state at the interface 
( Fig. 16g1- h). Results demonstrate that the α -phase has higher xx- 
directional strain values and lower yy-directional strain values than 
the β -phase, thereby establishing the development of a pronounced 
strain gradient across the α / β interface.

on the mechanical properties at room and elevated temperatures has 
been demonstrated. Experimental observations confirm that the α

dual mechanisms: slip band activation within α

grains and the nucle -

e 
under high-temperature conditions. 
Meanwhile, the α

resulting in deformation resistance. Notably, the α

during the thermo-mechanical loading process, it establishes a GNDs 
density gradient. These two aspects have been identified as key factors 
for significantly improving the high-temperature strength retention of 
the material.
Thermodynamic analysis reveals restricted atomic thermal motion 
and limited dislocation mobility in the alloy. Both submicron- and nano- 
silicides achieve second-phase strengthening by pinning dislocations at 
grain boundaries, phase interfaces, and within grains ( Fig. 5d and e2-

Despite the dislocation activation at elevated temperature, the disloca -

5. Conclusions
This study develops a near- α high-temperature titanium alloy with 
trimodal microstructure and dual-scale silicides. Through DMDF and 
heat treatment, HT2 alloy offer an excellent match of room and elevated 
temperature properties. The investigation systematically focused on 
three critical aspects: evolution of the trimodal microstructure, dynamic 
precipitation-dissolution behavior of submicron-nanosilicides, and 
strengthening-toughening mechanisms of the HT2 alloy. The main 
conclusions are as follows: 
(1) According to phase transition theory, sustained application of 
identical strain across temperature domains both above and 
below the β -transus temperature promotes α -phase precipitation 
with distinct morphological features, ultimately leading to the 
development of a trimodal microstructure. With increasing heat 
treatment temperature, the volume fraction of β
t 
increases, the 
proportion of α e to the total primary α -phase increases, and α
S 
increases and coarsens. The recrystallization mechanism of α
l 
during the heat deformation stage is DDRX, and that of α
l 
changes 
to SRX after heat treatment.
(2) The DMDF process generated S
2
-type silicides with dual-scale 
distributions: submicron S
2 
phase preferentially nucleated at 
grain and phase boundaries, while nano S
2 
phase formed intra -
granularly. During the subsequent heat treatment, the submicron 
and nano S
2 
phase undergo some degree of dissolution due to Si 
and Zr self-diffusion and interdiffusion. Furthermore, the inter -
crystalline precipitated S
2 
phase promoted the SRX of α
l 
during 
heat treatment.
(3) The HT2 alloy has the best match of room- and high-temperature 
properties. Its UTS is 1306.6 MPa at room temperature, and it 
maintains excellent average uniform elongation (7.5 %). After 
theoretical calculations, the high strength of the HT2 alloy is 
attributed mainly to the multiscale precipitates ( α
l 
and dual-scale 
silicides) and the dislocation strengthening provided by the α / β 
interface. In addition, the slip bands within the α phase increase
