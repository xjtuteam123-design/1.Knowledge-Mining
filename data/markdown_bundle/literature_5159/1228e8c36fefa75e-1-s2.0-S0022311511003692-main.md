properties of Nb–1Zr
Keith J. Leonard ⇑, Jeremy T. Busby, Steven J. Zinkle
Materials Science and Technology Division, Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak Ridge, TN 37831, USA

Accepted 14 April 2011

abstract
The microstructural changes and corresponding effects on mechanical properties, electrical resistivity
and density of Nb–1Zr were examined following neutron irradiation up to 1.8 dpa at temperatures of
1073, 1223 and 1373 K and compared with material thermally aged for similar exposure times of
/C24 1100 h. Thermally driven changes in the development of intragranular and grain boundary precipitate
phases showed a greater inﬂuence on mechanical and physical properties compared to irradiation-
induced defects for the examined conditions. Initial formation of the zirconium oxide precipitates was
identiﬁed as cubic structured plates following a Baker–Nutting orientation relationship to the b-Nb
matrix, with particles developing a monoclinic structure on further growth. Tensile properties of the
Nb–1Zr samples showed increased strength and reduced elongation following aging and irradiation
below 1373 K, with the largest tensile and hardness increases following aging at 1098 K. Tensile proper-
ties at 1373 K for the aged and irradiated samples were similar to that of the as-annealed material. Total
elongation was lower in the aged material due to a strain hardening response, rather than a weak strain
softening observed in the irradiated materials due in part to an irregular distribution of the precipitates in
the irradiated materials. Though intergranular fracture surfaces were observed on the 1248 K aged tensile
specimens, the aged and irradiated material showed uniform elongations >3% and total elongation >12%
for all conditions tested. Cavity formation was observed in material irradiated to 0.9 dpa at 1073 and
1223 K. However, since void densities were estimated to be below 3 /C2 10
17 m/C0 3 these voids contributed
little to either mechanical strengthening of the material or measured density changes.

1. Introduction
Much of the development of Nb-base alloys has followed the
history of reactor designs for compact space reactors [1–5] as well
as advanced ﬁssion [6,7] and fusion reactor concepts [8,9]. In these
advanced systems, Nb alloys are candidate structural alloys for fuel
cladding, reactor structure, and coolant piping applications. The
refractory nature of Nb alloys (2740 K melting temperature), good
ductility and formability, and compatibility with liquid metal cool-
ants such as Li [10–12] are the key favorable attributes for Nb al-
loys. The mechanical properties of Nb–1Zr have been studied
extensively, with the alloy typically considered as the only nio-
bium-base alloy with a sufﬁciently mature database (mechanical
and creep properties, chemical compatibility, welding and fabrica-
tion) as well as commercial availability for consideration as a via-
ble candidate material for radiation environments [13]. While Nb–
1Zr is not as mechanically strong as other Nb-base alloys below
1200 K such as PWC-11 (Nb–1Zr–0.1C), FS-85 (Nb–10W–28Ta–
1Zr) and the only other current commercially available Nb-alloy,
C-103 (Nb–10Hf–1Ti), the long-term thermal stability in mechani-
cal properties of these other Nb-base alloys remains an issue
[8,9,13–15]. The unirradiated mechanical properties of Nb–1Zr
can show signiﬁcant scatter due to variations in impurity composi-
tions and thermomechanical history [16], furthermore, the peri-
odic programmatic interest in the alloy over the years has also
left signiﬁcant gaps in the data available on the irradiated proper-
ties with no single systematic study.
Most of the radiation effects data are limited to examinations of
swelling and tensile properties at temperatures below 800 K as
summarized in previous reviews [2,6,17]. These studies have found
low void swelling but high radiation hardening and embrittlement
for neutron irradiation at temperatures below /C24 800 K [2,6,11,17].
There are only three prior published studies [6,18,19] that have
examined the tensile properties and microstructure of Nb–1Zr at
temperatures above 1000 K, which is the operating temperature
regime of highest interest for Nb alloys in advanced ﬁssion and
fusion energy systems [11,20–22]. Unexpectedly low uniform
elongations have been reported in the few irradiation studies

2. Experimental
The Nb–1Zr material of this study was produced by Cabot
Supermetals Inc., heat number 236884, in the form of as-rolled
stock. Miniature sheet tensile specimens and metallurgical
coupons were fabricated from the as-received material by Mo-wire
electrical discharge machining using water coolant. Type SS-3
tensile specimens were used in this study having a 0.76 /C2
1.52 /C2 7.62 mm gage geometry and 25.4 mm overall length. Ten-
sile specimens were prepared with gage lengths parallel to the roll-
ing direction of the sheet stock. Following cutting, samples were
polished to a 32 ﬁnish or better, marked at the grip location for
identiﬁcation purposes and electrochemically cleaned in a hydro-
ﬂuoric acid, nitric acid and water mixture following earlier estab-
lished procedures [23].
Nb–1Zr, as with all Group V metals and alloys, is highly suscep-
tible to pickup of impurities such as O, C and N during heat treat-
ment due to the very high solubility and diffusivity of these
interstitial solutes in Nb–1Zr. These impurities can cause substan-
tial matrix hardening, embrittlement and other deleterious effects.
Therefore, special experimental procedures (e.g., emplacement in-
side a sacriﬁcial Nb box or use of ultrahigh vacuum conditions)
need to be followed to minimize impurity pickup for any heat
treatment or irradiation at elevated temperature. In order to min-
imize impurity pickup during annealing, the Nb–1Zr samples were
placed in a Nb–1Zr box and vacuum (<1.3 /C2 10/C0 4 Pa) annealed at
1835 K for 1 h. Samples were heated at a rate of /C24 20 K/min and
furnace cooled at /C24 100 K/min for the ﬁrst few minutes after which
the cooling rate decreased asymptotically with time.
Irradiation of the samples was performed at the High Flux Iso-
tope Reactor (HFIR) at ORNL using the peripheral target locations.
Samples were loaded and sealed in Nb irradiation capsules de-
signed to balance the reactor gamma heating and heat ﬂow across
a controlled thermal gap. Helium was used as the ﬁll gas for the
1073 and 1223 K irradiations, while neon was used for irradiations
at 1373 K. Each irradiation capsule contained several SiC thermom-
etry specimens for the lower temperature capsules, while ZrC was
selected and compatibility tested for the 1373 K irradiations to
avoid reaction with the Nb capsule and specimens [24,25]. Follow-
ing irradiation the specimen temperatures were determined by
measuring the SiC and ZrC electrical resistivity change following
isochronal anneals, as detailed elsewhere [26]. The measured irra-
diation temperature from thermometry was within the ±20 K of
the target temperatures.
The samples were irradiated for one cycle (550 h) in the reactor,
with the ﬂuences for the irradiated capsules calculated based on
the individual capsule axial position, peripheral target location, cy-
cle duration, axial ﬂux distribution and power level provided in Ta-
ble 1 . Numerous previous dosimetry results have determined the
position-speciﬁc ﬂuxes are constant to within /C24 1% for a wide range
of HFIR target conﬁgurations. The ﬂuence to damage conversion
factor was calculated [24,27] for Nb–1Zr with displacement energy
values for Nb and Zr taken to be 40 eV. The fast ﬂuence for the
1373 K irradiation was less than that of the targeted 0.9 dpa value.
A second set of samples was irradiated for two cycles (1100 h) in
HFIR at 1223 K achieving 1.77 dpa. Transmutations occurring in
Nb–1Zr are not expected to affect the phase development differ-
ently than that of the aged material since most of the transmuta-
tions simply produce different Nb and Zr isotopes.
Thermal aging of Nb–1Zr control samples was conducted in
association with the irradiation experiments. Aging temperatures
of 1098, 1248 and 1398 K were purposefully selected as being
20 K higher than the irradiation target temperatures due to the
uncertainty associated with the irradiation capsule thermometry.
The Nb–1Zr specimens were loaded into Nb holders similar to
those used in the irradiation experiment and sealed inside of alloy
600 canisters with alumina inner sleeves. Metallographic samples
were also included and were wrapped in Ta foil and placed with
the Nb holders inside the canisters. The Nb holders and Ta foil
served as getters for any impurities. The thermal aging capsules
were welded shut following repeated evacuation (<1.3 /C2 10
/C0 4 Pa)
and purge cycles under the same inert gas supply used in the irra-
diation study. A leak check was performed on each sealed canister
prior to aging. Aging of the canisters containing the samples was
performed in an open-air furnace for 1100 h, which is equivalent
to two cycles in HFIR.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure analysis
The as-annealed material was evaluated to determine the met-
allurgical condition of the material following annealing at 1835 K.
Examined under both optical and electron microscopy, the as-an-
nealed material displayed an equiaxed, defect-free grain structure
with 70 lm mean grain size, with no evidence of ZrO 2 precipita-
tion. The mean grain size remained unchanged following subse-
quent aging treatments. The annealed microstructure is shown in
Fig. 1, with the TEM micrograph revealing a low density of network
dislocations likely associated with handling of the TEM foil. It
should be noted that a vacuum furnace anneal of the as-received
material at 1643 K produced a microstructure of equiaxed grains
and sub-grains, which under electron microscopy revealed disloca-
tion networks and the presence of 0.2 lm sized ZrO 2 particles dis-
persed throughout the material. The subsequent aging studies
discussed in this paper are for material annealed at 1835 K.
The 1100 h aging of the Nb–1Zr samples at 1098, 1248 and
1398 K produced increasing amounts of visible precipitation in
the OM and SEM images along grain boundaries and in the grain
interiors. A comparison of the optical and SEM images for the three
aging temperatures is shown in Fig. 2. Surface pitting created dur-
ing polishing is believed to be responsible for some of the artifacts
observed in the optical images, though the 1398 K aged sample re-
acted quickly with the etchant and revealed signiﬁcant precipita-
tion in the material. The 1398 K aged sample viewed under
backscatter electron imaging showed a plate-like precipitate mor-
phology appearing in the grain interiors with a deﬁnitive orienta-
tion relationship with the b-Nb matrix phase. This morphology
was different than that of the grain boundary precipitates. The
amount of precipitation was difﬁcult to quantify by SEM or OM
in the 1248 K aged sample due to its small size, while precipitation
in the 1098 K material was too ﬁne to be visible under OM and
back-scatter SEM imaging, though observable during TEM exami-
nation of the material.
Examination of the aged control samples by TEM provided addi-
tional information on the extent of precipitation occurring in the
material for each thermal aging condition. An increase in size
and a gradual development of intragranular and grain boundary
precipitation was observed with increasing temperatures. An
example of the precipitation in the 1098 K aged material is shown
in Fig. 3 . Grain boundary precipitation was observed throughout
the sample, with very few boundaries showing no evidence of par-
ticle formation. These particles were Zr-rich, though due in part to
the size of the particles and the relative thickness of the TEM foil,
crystallographic information was not obtainable. Intragranular
precipitates were observed with a plate-like morphology exempli-
ﬁed in Fig. 3 b, which shows the plates from both an edge-on and
planar normal orientations. The normal direction of the plates

Microstructural examination of the irradiated materials was
performed on the tensile tested samples, with specimens taken
from the gage regions. This was intended to capture both the irra-
diation and thermal induced microstructural changes in the mate-
rial, and also their effect on the deformation behavior of the
material. Due to the limited quantity of material and the difﬁculty
experienced in electropolishing the unirradiated controls, samples
taken from the gage region were ion milled. While this introduced
artifacts into the samples such as small (<3 nm) loops and black
spot-type defects in the microstructure, they are on a scale much
smaller than those features generated during irradiation. As ion
milling was conducted at temperatures typically near 148 K, the
integrity of the as irradiated or deformation generated defects in
the samples are intact and representative of material. The ion
milled samples provided a larger overall area that could be exam-
ined than that typically produced in the electropolished material.
The irradiated materials showed a developmental progression

3.4. Density
The densities of the annealed, aged and irradiated samples were
measured using the immersion density method, with the calcu-
lated values provided in Table 3 . A decrease in density over the
as-annealed condition was recorded for both the irradiated and
aged materials. Plotted in Fig. 16 is the change in density as a func-
tion of irradiation or aging temperature. The change in density over
the annealed material is largest for the lowest aging and irradiation
temperatures and reduces with increasing temperatures. Under
the conditions examined in this work, the density change in the
material is related to the precipitation of the zirconium oxide
phases rather than that of cavity development as little difference
is observed between density values of the aged and irradiated
material for a given temperature.
The density change measured in the 1073 K irradiated material
(/C24 0.56%) is signiﬁcant for the relatively low displacement damage
of 0.9 dpa as compared to early literature values for void swelling
of 1.5–2.5% for 11–22 dpa irradiation at 1073 K [36,37], and later
reviewed by Powell et al. [38]. Void swelling was reported to de-
crease to /C24 0.4% at 1173 K and to zero by 1323 K for irradiations
to 22 dpa [37]. More recently, a 0.9% reduction in density for Nb–
1Zr was reported between 842 and 914 K for 38.2–50.3 dpa Fast
Flux Test Facility reactor irradiations [39,40]. No voids were im-
aged under TEM examination for these conditions [39], but were
present at higher and lower temperature ranges where smaller
changes in density were reported. Phase transformations were
speculated to be responsible for the swelling at the intermediate
temperatures, but microstructural veriﬁcation was not conclusive.
In the Nb–1Zr of this study, the precipitates have a signiﬁcant
effect on mechanical properties as well as density changes. The
cavities observed in the 0.9 dpa irradiations at 1073 and 1223 K
have a negligible effect on swelling, with density more dependent
on precipitate size and density.
3.5. Tensile properties
The tensile properties of the Nb–1Zr samples in the annealed,
aged and irradiated conditions were examined and results tabu-
lated in Table 4 . Tensile tests were conducted at the 1073, 1223
and 1373 K irradiation temperatures, with one test conducted at
room temperature of the material in the annealed condition. These
results are further presented in Figs. 17–19 and compared with lit-
erature values for material in the annealed condition and to the
small amount data available on irradiated Nb–1Zr in this temper-
ature range [19,43]. Direct comparison between the literature data
and that of this study is difﬁcult, due to differences in thermome-
chanical history of the material and impurity levels of the material.
Therefore, considerable scatter is found in the literature for tensile
properties of annealed material and can especially be seen in the
room temperature variations in reported properties. However,
the tensile data presented in Figs. 17–19 provides a good compar-
ison of the property changes between the annealed, aged and irra-
diated samples of this study. The annealed material of this study
does show a reasonable correlation to the trend line of the an-
nealed tensile property data in the literature. Overall, the general
trend for yield and ultimate strength appeared to exhibit a mono-
tonic decrease with increasing anneal and irradiation temperature
above 1050 K.
A reduction in total elongation occurs with aging at all temper-
atures as compared to both the annealed and irradiated materials,
though the differences become less severe for the 1398/1373 K
aging and test conditions. The material shows a marked increase
in strength following aging at 1098 K over the as-annealed and
0.9 dpa/1073 K irradiated material, a direct effect of the precipita-
tion of a higher density of intragranular precipitates. This result
correlates to the micro-hardness data and shows that thermal
aging effects have a strong response on the material. The time at
temperature for the aged samples was double that of the 0.9 dpa
irradiated material, and the difference in strength will likely dimin-
ish at higher displacement damage. Dynamic strain aging (visible

plots of the annealed material tested at 1073 K, but was not pres-

The comparison of the current test results to that of the irradi-
ated literature data should be met with some caution. Irradiation
of Nb–1Zr in these two cases took place at temperature for approx-
imately 2400 h [19,43], but the changes in properties associated
with thermal exposure alone are not known as the properties rep-
resent the combined and interrelated thermal and irradiation in-
duced microstructural changes. The previously reported literature
data are also for exposures to considerably higher displacement
damages. These two prior studies were also conducted in lithium
(which is a strong oxygen gettering environment), rather than inert
cover gas. Therefore the oxygen content in the alloys for this study
may be higher, yielding a greater effect of precipitate formation. In
addition, uncertainties associated with irradiation temperature for
the literature studies may be as high as 120 K from their target
temperatures.
4. Conclusions
The effects of 1100 h aging and neutron irradiation on the phys-
ical and mechanical properties of Nb–1Zr were examined in the
1073–1398 K temperature range. The effect of thermal aging was
found to have a stronger inﬂuence over the mechanical properties
than neutron irradiation to 0.9 dpa. Neutron exposure to 1.8 dpa at
1223 K for approximately the same exposure time at temperature
as thermally aged material resulted in nearly identical mechanical
properties to that of the aged material.
Microstructural characterization of the aged samples revealed
the presence of both intragranular and grain boundary precipita-
tion. Cubic structured zirconium oxide particles were primarily ob-
served at lower aging temperatures with monoclinic structured
precipitates at higher aging temperatures. Indications, based on
directionality of growth, suggest that the monoclinic structured
precipitates initially develop as cubic structured particles. The
intragranular precipitates developed as plate-like structures obey-
ing a consistent orientation with the b-Nb matrix. The presence of
these precipitates in the 1098 K aged material with a density of
9 /C2 10
20 m/C0 3, caused signiﬁcant hardening of the material over
the as-annealed and 0.9 dpa irradiated conditions. Aging at higher
temperatures produced a gradual increase in precipitate size and
decrease in density. The formation of the zirconium oxide precipi-
tates resulted in a measurable decrease in density in the material,

dependent changes as the aged material. Cavities were observed
in material irradiated to 0.9 dpa at 1073 and 1223 K with densities
estimated at 3 /C2 1017 m/C0 2 and <1 /C2 1017 m/C0 3, respectively (corre-
sponding void swelling of /C24 10/C0 4%). Cavity formation was not ob-
served in the 1.8 dpa/1223 K irradiated material possibly due to
their very low density. The distribution of the intragranular precip-
itates was irregular compared to that of the aged material. The lack
of uniformity in the irradiated material created obstacle free chan-
nels for deformation dislocations to move through. Partial recovery
occurred during tensile testing of the 0.7 dpa/1373 K irradiated
material, resulting in the formation of subgrain structures in the
material.
Increase in tensile strength over the as-annealed condition was
measured for the materials aged and irradiated at 1073 and
1223 K, while tensile strengths were similar between the annealed,
aged and irradiated material at 1373 K. The total elongation of the
aged material was lower due to the strain hardening response of
the material, rather than the slight strain softening observed in
the irradiated material. While intergranular fracture was observed
on the fracture surfaces of the 1248 K aged tensile specimens, the
aged or irradiated material showed uniform elongation >3% and to-
tal elongation >12% for all tensile conditions tested. The Nb–1Zr
material examined showed only moderate degradation in physical
and mechanical properties and should be suitable for nuclear
applications in the dose and temperature range examined.
Further work is needed to explore higher dose irradiations at
similar temperature conditions as well as at temperatures below
1000 K, where radiation hardening of the solid-solution phase be-
comes more pronounced. In addition, evaluation of thermome-
chanical treatments in combination with pre-irradiation thermal
aging on microstructure development of ﬁner grain size material
as well as controlling the dispersion strengthening effects of the
Zr-oxide phase may aid in mitigating some low temperature
brittleness.
