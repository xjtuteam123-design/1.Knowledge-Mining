ABSTRACT
Refractory high-entropy alloys with exceptional high-temperature mechanical properties have attracted 
increasing interest in aerospace applications. However, the presence of multiple principal alloying elements 
presents a challenge in finding an appropriate and cost-effective alloy for specific applications. A multi-property 
composition design method combining machine learning and thermodynamic calculations was proposed to 
screen alloys from a vast candidate space. Three TiZrHfNbVMoTa alloys were selected from several hundred 
thousand candidates. The alloys all meet the design objectives, demonstrating the effectiveness of this strategy. 
Additionally, a compressive strength-ductility synergy is improved with a fracture strain exceeding 60 % and a 
yield strength of 1051 ± 23 MPa at room temperature.

2.2. Machine learning model and prediction
The yield strength and fracture strain, as the key properties of RHEAs 
used as structural materials, were predicted using machine learning 
models.
2.2.1. Establishing the datasets and feature pool
The compositional space considered in this study was limited to the 
Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb-V-Mo-Ta system. The data were collected from experiments 
and conducted using vacuum arc melting under the as-cast condition to 
minimize the significant effect of the production method. A dataset 
containing nominal compositions and mechanical properties including 
compression yield strength and fracture strain at room temperature was 
assembled. The dataset was divided into two subsets: yield strength and 
fracture strain sets, containing 102 and 68 instances, respectively.

accuracy of model predictions, features were constructed representing 
aspects of size, bonding, and electrons (see Section 1 in the Supple -
mentary for details). The features are primarily calculated using mixing 
rules for HEAs and do not include information related to phase in -
teractions [10,31] . A feature set consisting of 66 material descriptors has 
been established, as shown in the supplemental materials .

The difference in element distribution between DR and ID regions 
originates from the different melting temperatures of the elements. Nb, 
Mo, and Ta have higher melting temperature ( > 2700 K), while Ti, Zr, 
Hf, and V have lower melting temperature [32,33] . 
High-melting-temperature elements solidify first to form DR, while 
low-melting-temperature elements solidify in the ID regions. According 
to XRD, SEM, and TEM, it can be determined that, the structure is BCC. 
Ti, Zr, and Hf have a BCC structure at high temperatures , they transform 
into HCP structure at low temperatures. Nb, Mo, and Ta always maintain 
a BCC structure. Therefore, the DR regions are enriched in Nb, Mo, and 
Ta, forming the BCC1 phase. The second phase in the ID regions, 
enriched in Ti, Zr, and Hf, solidifies as the BCC2 phase. As the temper -
ature continues to decrease, the saturation of the BCC solid solution 
phase decreases, and the HCP or Laves phase begins to precipitate. The 
precipitation sequence depends on the content of Zr and V in the sample. 
For S1, which is rich in Zr and V, the Laves phase precipitates prefer -
entially, while the opposite occurs in S3. In addition, this is related to the 
precipitation temperature of BCC2, as Zr and V in the Laves phase are 
mainly supplied by BCC2. When the precipitation temperature of BCC2 
is very low, it cannot meet the thermodynamic conditions and thus 
cannot precipitate. Therefore, the Laves phase is not shown in Fig. 12 (b). 
According to the research, severe lattice distortion promotes chemical 
short-range order [34] . In S3, the radius difference between Zr and V is 
the largest, and their content is low, resulting in minimal lattice 
distortion, which leads to a more uniform element distribution ( Fig. 8
(c)). It will be further observed using TEM.
The phase composition of each alloy was further examined through 
TEM images and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns, as 
shown in Fig. 9 . In Fig. 9 (a-c), the TEM images of the S1 alloy, composed 
of BCC, HCP, and Laves phases, clearly reveal a long, rod-like HCP 
phase. For the S2 alloy, the TEM images in Fig. 9 (d) display a relatively 
simple microstructure, composed of the BCC phase. In the case of the S3 
alloy, the TEM images in Fig. 9 (e-h) show three phases: BCC, HCP, and 
Laves. The sizes of the HCP and Laves phases in S3 are much smaller 
than those in S1.

4. Discussion
4.1. Machine learning models analysis
In this work, machine learning models are used to predict the me -
chanical properties of the candidate alloys. The quality of the data and 
features determines the prediction accuracy of the models. Given the 
limited amount of data, we combine prior knowledge with feature se -
lection to construct the models. After feature selection, the feature 
subsets include information on atomic bonding, lattice distortion, and 
electronic behavior. Atomic bonding influences the mechanical prop -
erties of materials, and the mixing enthalpy directly represents the 
bonding strength between atoms [35] . Lattice distortion arises from 
differences in atomic radii and mismatched physical properties, such as 
elastic moduli among elements, including η , Δ K, Δ G, δ R
i
, and Δ V
m 
[36 – 38] . Electronic behavior information includes the valence electron 
concentration, itinerant electron number, and their differences, which 
can represent atomic charge transfer [28] . For HEAs, strength primarily 
comes from solid-solution strengthening [39,40] . The mismatch in 
modulus and size are important factors affecting solid-solution 
strengthening. Based on the feature selection results, η is crucial, as it 
also includes other mismatch-related features. Recent studies have 
shown that the chemical short-range order (CSRO) present in HEAs can 
simultaneously enhance the strength and ductility of the alloys [41] . The 
formation of CSRO is linked to lattice distortion, bonding state, and 
electronic interactions [42,43] . Lattice distortion causes fluctuations in 
local strain energy in HEAs, leading to different mixing enthalpy and 
electronic interactions, which contribute to the formation of CSRO [34] . 
This is consistent with the features included in the selected subset, such 
as Δ H
mix 
and electronic behavior information. The potential influence of 
these selected features on yield strength is discussed to better under -
stand the prediction of mechanical properties. Machine learning models 
are capable of extracting the laws embedded in the data that are 
consistent with theoretical knowledge and can effectively predict the 
mechanical properties of alloys.
SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) is commonly used to inter -
pret machine learning models, leveraging Shapley value theory from 
game theory to provide both global and local, visual and quantitative 
explanations for model predictions. SHAP was applied to the prediction 
model using Tree Explainer as the explainer. SHAP values can quantify 
the positive or negative impact of individual sample features on the 
properties of HEAs. Fig. 10 shows the SHAP value distribution of six 
selected features from the yield strength prediction model. Each data 
point represents a sample, with the horizontal axis indicating the 
magnitude of the SHAP value. A wider spread of feature scatter impacts 
yield strength more. High E, low Δ H
mix
, and low Δ e/a contribute posi -
tively to yield strength, while low Δ K, low VEC, and high η may lead to a 
decrease in yield strength. The SHAP values reveal a strong correlation 
between these six features and yield strength. E (Young ’ s modulus) is 
directly related to the mechanical properties of materials. A negative 
Δ H
mix 
indicates strong interatomic bonding, which favors the formation 
of CSRO and enhances yield strength [35] . Δ K (bulk modulus difference) 
represents lattice distortion, and a decrease in Δ K hinders yield strength 
improvement, consistent with solid solution strengthening theory. 
Huang et al. [44] reported that high plasticity in HEAs is associated with 
low VEC. Further research is needed to clarify the specific effects of VEC, 
Δ e/a, and η . The calculated values of the six features for alloys S1-S3 are 
presented in Table S5 , and the trends in features and yield strength are 
consistent with the above analysis. SHAP values for the fracture strain

4.2. Effect of composition on properties
Research has shown that ductility and strength in RHEAs can be 
controlled by the ratio of HCP to BCC elements [49] . Plastic deform -
ability is influenced by both the facilitation of dislocation nucleation and 
the ease of dislocation motion. Studies have indicated that HCP elements 
reduce dislocation core energy, making it easier to introduce disloca -
tions. Moreover, the electronic structure of HCP elements can enhance 
dislocation mobility, which in turn improves ductility. Therefore, a 
higher content of HCP elements contributes to increased ductility in 
RHEAs [49] . The BCC2 and HCP phases are rich in Ti, Zr, and Hf, which 
enhance compressive ductility. The proportion of these phases in RHEAs 
results in varying strength and fracture strain characteristics. Addi -
tionally, Laves phases can enhance the strength of the alloy, but a higher 
content of Laves phases may lead to brittleness [50] .
Fig. 12 shows the distribution of phase content. At 600 ℃ , the Laves 
phase content in S1 was 26 %, and the HCP phase content was 25 %. In 
S2, the BCC2 phase content was 1.4 %, while in S3, the HCP phase 
content was 35.5 %, and the Laves phase precipitates at a lower tem -
perature. The Laves phase content correlates with the yield strength of 
the alloy in the following order: S1 > S3 > S2.
S2 does not contain Laves phases, and the BCC2 phase content is low. 
However, it exhibits good lattice matching with the BCC1 phase, 
resulting in low strength but good compressive ductility. The S1 alloy 
contains a significant amount of Laves phase, with the HCP phase con -
tent being comparable to that of the Laves phase. Experimental results 
showed that the dendrites in S1 are small leading to high strength but 
brittleness. In the S3 alloy, the HCP phase content is higher than that of 
the Laves phase, providing good toughness while maintaining a certain 
level of strength. It is likely that an alloy with a higher content and ratio 
of HCP (and/or BCC2) phase to Laves phase, along with an appropriate 
content and size of Laves phase, would exhibit improved compressive 
strength-ductility synergy [51] . The thermodynamic calculations 
provide equilibrium phase diagrams. However, the actual solidification 
process occurs in a non-equilibrium state. Nevertheless, the phase 
composition and content can be inferred from thermodynamic calcula -
tions, and their variation follows the same general trends observed in the 
actual samples.

the complex interactions between atoms, the effects of the aforemen -

emphasizes the importance of a multi-property constraint composition 
selection method that combines machine learning, thermodynamic 
calculations, and theory.
5. Conclusion
In this work, a method for multi-property constraints composition 
screening is proposed to customize the mechanical properties of a 
TiZrHfNbVMoTa RHEA based on specific requirements. The following 
conclusions are drawn from the current work:
(1) The proposed multi-property constraints composition design 
method, which combines theory, thermodynamic calculations, and 
machine learning, can effectively screen for mechanical properties, 
melting temperature, phase stability, and more. The S1-S3 alloys ob -
tained meet all design objectives. Notably, the machine learning model 
can screen out key features that align with theory and outperforms 
traditional solid solution strengthening models in extracting informa -
tion related to mechanical properties.
(2) The yield strength of S1, S2, and S3 are 1568 ± 26 MPa, 845 
± 14 MPa, and 1051 ± 23 MPa, respectively. The fracture strain of S1 is 
only 12 %, while both S2 and S3 exceed 60 %.
(3) The addition of the Laves phase enhances the alloy ’ s strength but 
introduces brittleness. To address this issue, improving alloy compres -
sive ductility can be achieved by controlling both the content and size of 
the phase, as well as the ratio of HCP phase to Laves phase.
(4) The multi-property constraint composition screening method can 
be adapted for design under different conditions to meet diverse appli -
cation requirements. Target-oriented material design is an inevitable 
trend that will accelerate material development and enable rapid 
component design.
