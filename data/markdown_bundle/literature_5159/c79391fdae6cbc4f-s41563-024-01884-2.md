strengthened aluminium alloys
Xiangren Bai   1, Haonan Xie   1, Xiang Zhang   1 , Dongdong Zhao1,

Oxided-dispersion-strengthened (ODS) alloys are promising high-strength 
materials used in extreme environments such as high-temperature and 
radiation tolerance applications. Until now, ODS alloys have been developed 
for reducible metals by chemical processing methods, but there are 
no commercially available ODS alloys for unreducible metals, namely, 
Al, Mg, Ti, Zr and so on, owing to the challenge of uniformly dispersing 
oxide particles in these alloys by traditional techniques. Here we present 
a strategy to achieve ODS Al alloys containing highly dispersive 5 nm 
MgO nanoparticles by powder metallurgy, using nanoparticles that have 
in situ-grown graphene-like coatings and hence largely reduced surface 
energy. Notably, the densely dispersed MgO nanoparticles, which have a 
fully coherent relationship with an Al matrix, show effective suppression 
of interfacial vacancy diffusion, thus leading to unprecedented strength 
(~200 MPa) and creep resistance at temperatures as high as 500 °C. Our 
processing approach should enable the dispersion of ultrafine nanoparticles 
in a wide range of alloys for high-temperature-related applications.
Oxided-dispersion-strengthened (ODS) alloys, with outstanding 
resistance to both extreme heat and irradiation, have emerged as the 
frontrunners for the core structural materials used in severe opera-
tional environments1–3. From the aspect of classic mechanics, achiev-
ing high-level mechanical properties in ODS systems relies on good 
control of the oxide particle size at the sub-10 nm level. Moreover, 
limited misfit between oxide particles and the matrix is also be neces-
sary to improve the stability of the interface and microstructure when 
used in harsh high-temperature environments. In general, oxygen has 
a relatively low solubility but a high diffusivity in metals, which makes it 
hard to control the stable precipitated oxide clusters or particles at the 
size of sub-10 nm through the classic solution–precipitation method. 
Another great challenge for achieving good coherency between oxides 
and metals is posed by the huge difference in their intrinsic physical 
and chemical properties. At present, oxides are introduced in alloys 
either by in situ reactions or through external mixing approaches. In 
the light of the in situ reaction concept, numerous ODS alloys for reduc-
ible metals such as Fe (ref. 4), Mo (ref. 5), Ni (ref. 3) and W (ref. 6) have 
been successfully developed through chemical processing methods, 
including liquid mixing and matrix reduction, which takes advantage of 
the good mobility and low surface energy of the nano-oxide precursor 
in the liquid system. However, owing to the high chemical reactivity 
with oxygen and the unreducible nature, the in situ reaction strategy 
is not suitable for fabricating ODS lightweight metals such as Al, Mg, 
Ti and so on.
T o achieve uniform particle dispersion in unreducible metals, 
external mixing strategies based on either liquid-state or solid-state 
methods are employed. These strategies involve inputting high-level 
mechanical energy to overcome the adhesion between particles and 
obtaining stabilized microstructures during the elaborate formation

Herein we propose an ‘interface replacement’ strategy to achieve 
dense uniform dispersion of metal oxide NPs, thus obtaining ODS Al 
with outstanding mechanical properties. This strategy involves replac-

connected van der Waals force between the few-layer graphene-like 
(GL) coatings that were in situ grown on the surface of the MgO NPs. 
During the solid-state PM process, the surface modification of the rein-
forcement particles caused a substantial change in the dispersion state 
from uncontrolled clusters into the thoroughly dispersive individual 
process7. This ensures a consistent and effective distribution of the 
nano-oxides, supporting the overall quality and performance of the 
materials. In most cases, the nanoparticles (NPs) in contact with 
each other form loose polycrystal assemblies due to strong chemical

the regulations of impact breakage, the agglomerations made of tiny 
particles with a firmly bonded interface could even make the ‘high 
input energy’ strategy lose its action, resulting in agglomerations 
with smaller size rather than individual dispersed NPs7. For instance,

exacerbates the coarsening potential of nano-oxides. Turning to the 
solid-state strategies such as powder metallurgy (PM), the mainstream 
high-energy ball-milling, considered a severe deformation process, 
requires relatively high milling speed and duration to fragment the 
agglomerations and ‘dissolve’ the nano-oxides into the matrix.

Densely distributed MgO NPs in the Al matrix
Achieving the dense and uniform dispersion of NPs in a solid matrix 
has always been challenging due to the trade-off relation between par-
ticle size and specific surface energy. T o overcome this challenge, the 
interface-replacement strategy was proposed; the dispersion concept 
is schematically illustrated in Fig. 1a. In our processing, the first critical 
step is to synthesize MgO@GL as the reinforcement using in situ pyroly-
sis of commercial magnesium citrate at 800 °C (Methods). During the 
heating process, magnesium citrate was first decomposed into MgO 
NPs isolated by interconnected networks of GL nanosheets (Fig. 1c and 
Supplementary Fig. 1; the preparation method is discussed in detail in 
Supplementary Note 1). Such a small GL coating content of ~3.0 wt% 
in the pomegranate-like MgO@GL effectively separates the adjacent 
MgO NPs from direct bonding. Transmission electron microscopy 
(TEM) characterization results indicate that the MgO in MgO@GL are 
cubic single-crystal NPs with an average size (diameter) of ~5 nm, as 
evidenced by the lattice data of the dominant {001} crystal planes from 
X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Supplementary Fig. 2).
During the subsequent solid-state PM process, the nanoscale 
GL coating between the MgO NPs dispelled the stereotype of a harsh 
particle dispersion state. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images 
of the bulk surface after ion milling showed a uniform distribution of 
individual MgO NPs (~2–8 nm) embedded in the Al matrix (Fig. 1d). 
Specifically, the majority of MgO NPs were found to be embedded in 
the grain interiors of bulk Al (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 3). The 
uniformity of the densely distributed NPs can also be validated by the 
similar microhardness values measured at various positions in two 
perpendicular directions (Supplementary Fig. 3). On the contrary, the 
materials reinforced by bare MgO NPs without GL coatings (hereafter 
denoted as MgO/Al) formed numerous submicrometre-sized MgO 
clusters under the same processing steps (Supplementary Fig. 4). In 
terms of the diameter statistics of over 2,000 particles from multiple 
samples, we estimated the breakage ratio (defined as the ratio of bro-
ken contacts to primary contacts in the agglomerate) to be ~99.2% for 
MgO@GL/Al and ~1.84% for MgO/Al (Supplementary Fig. 5). In addition, 
the volume ratio of primary particles to agglomerates was determined 
to be ~93.2%, and the final dispersoids in MgO@GL/Al consisted of no 
more than 2 primary particles at the 99.0% confidence interval. By 
contrast, the volume ratio of primary particles to agglomerates was 
measured to be ~0.00354% and the final dispersoids in MgO/Al were 
discerned as agglomerates containing more than 100 primary particles.
In view of the universal ball-milling parameters applied during 
the solid-state dispersion, the current largely improved dispersibility 
should derive from the modification of the surface energy of the fine 
particles. T o realize dense and uniform distribution in the ODS sys -
tems containing ductile–brittle components, three typical steps are 
involved during ball-milling: (1) ductile metal powder particles get 
deformed and brittle oxide particles get fragmented during the ball–
powder–ball collisions; (2) oxide NPs become occluded and trapped 
in the ductile metal particles; and (3) hybrid powder particles get work 
hardened and further refined. During step 1, it is easy for the weakly 
bonded GL coating on the surface of tiny MgO to be exfoliated, which 
results in the release of the discrete MgO NPs into Al, as evidenced by 
the dispersion states in the early stages (Supplementary Fig. 6). The 
density functional theory (DFT) calculation depicts that the binding 
energy for the ‘replaced interface’ of (002)GL/(002)MgO has a low value 
of ~0.07 J m−2 (Fig. 1f ), in strong contrast to the high level of binding 
energy for separating MgO low-index lattice planes (~2.18–10.38 J m−2). 
The latter is far beyond the impact of input energy on the single-particle 
surface in common ball-milling conditions 10. According to the DFT 
results, the Weber numbers for the two dispersion systems were calcu-
lated to be approximately 12.15 for MgO@GL/Al and 0.093 for MgO/Al. 
The substantial differences in dispersion states, as illustrated in Fig. 1d 
and Supplementary Fig. 4d, could be explained by their corresponding 
Weber numbers. More broadly, as shown in Fig. 1g, which shows a com-
parison of the ‘kinetic energy’ effect between MgO/Al and MgO@GL/Al, 
the input energy shows a large difference to achieve the same level of 
Weber number in the two dispersing systems, especially for sub-10 nm 
particles. Furthermore, in contrast to the reactive ball-milling in the 
ODS Fe and Ni systems3,4, the current MgO NPs took shape under the 
physical confinement of the GL coatings rather than being controlled 
by the thermodynamics and kinetics of the diffusion-related reactions. 
Such stabilized individual nano-oxides further promoted the disper-
sion efficiency in steps 2 and 3 and suppressed coarsening during the 
heat treatment (as confirmed by the particle contour in Fig. 1c,e).
T o further validate the effectiveness of the interface-replacement 
dispersion strategy, additional various oxides coated by GL layers, 
including La2O3, Y2O3, Ce2O3 and TiO2, were also successfully prepared 
and dispersed within the Al matrix (Fig. 2a).
Outstanding heat resistance
Herein we highlight MgO@GL/Al, which shows extraordinary mechanical 
properties, particularly its unprecedented high-temperature perfor-
mance. At room temperature, the UTS of 8 vol% MgO@GL/Al was meas-
ured to be approximately 690 MPa, which is 6 times higher than that of PM 
Al (Fig. 2c). More inspiringly, the UTS of MgO@GL/Al is ~420 MPa at 300 °C, 
~310 MPa at 400 °C and ~200 MPa at 500 °C, 5 times higher than that of 
PM Al at the same temperature (Fig. 2c). The superb high-temperature 
properties were also validated by the compressive strength measured 
at the same temperature range (Fig. 2d). Our MgO@GL/Al shows a UTS 
higher than that of all commercial Al alloys across all temperature ranges, 
with its advantage expanding as the temperature increases11 (Fig. 2b). This 
is reinforced by the observation that the downwards trend of microhard-
ness values in MgO@GL/Al is much slower compared with PM Al and other 
reported nanostructured Al alloys (Fig. 2e). More intuitively, by comparing 
the normalized yield strength (YS) values at high temperatures versus that 
measured at room temperature, the MgO@GL/Al loses only ~40% of its YS 
at 300 °C compared with that measured at 25 °C (690 MPa), delivering one 
of the highest mechanical performances among the currently reported Al 
alloys12–18 (Fig. 2f). In addition, the mechanical performance of other ODS 
Al systems with a nano-oxide content of 5.0 vol% was extensively tested, 
including MgO@GL/Al–Zn–Mg–Cu, La2O3@GL/Al, Y2O3@GL/Al, Ce2O3@
GL/Al, TiO2@GL/Al and so on (as shown in Fig. 2a and Supplementary 
Fig. 7). It was shown that the dense and uniform nano-oxides dispersed 
in the Al matrix of all systems above facilitate the near-doubling tensile 
strength in the ODS Al alloys.
The outstanding properties of the MgO@GL/Al could be further 
evidenced by a compressive creep experiment. Overall, an unprec -
edented creep resistance of MgO@GL/Al, with creep rates of ~10–7 s−1 
under 160 MPa at 400 °C (~72% Tm) and 80 MPa at 500 °C (~82% Tm) was 
shown (where Tm is the melting temperature) (Fig. 3a). The observed 
strain rates of MgO@GL/Al are significantly lower than those typically 
controlled by traditional methods, such as grain boundary (GB) diffu-
sion (Coble creep) or lattice diffusion (dislocation climb and Nabarro–
Herring creep), as illustrated in the Weertman–Ashby deformation 
map19 (Fig. 3). On the basis of Fig. 3c, the present MgO@GL/Al sustains 
an exceptional creep resistance, featuring steady-state creep rates that 
are several orders of magnitude slower than those previously reported 
for creep-resistant Al alloys20–23. In addition, the hardness evolution 
with time at an ageing temperature of up to 600 °C also manifests the 
high thermal resistance of the MgO@GL/Al, where no apparent hard-
ness decreases were detected (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Fully coherent MgO/Al interface
T o determine the underlying mechanism for the extraordinary 
high-temperature properties, we first performed atomic-level micro-
structure analysis of MgO@GL/Al. The statistical analysis of the TEM 
images (Fig. 1e) revealed a number density of ultrafine (~5 nm) MgO NPs 
in the matrix, calculated as ~9 × 1023 m−3. More crucially, such a packed 
arrangement of MgO NPs results in an exceptionally high interface 
density (~1.4 × 108 m−1), far exceeding that of common metallic material 
systems24–29 (Supplementary Table 1; the calculation of number density 
and interface density are shown in Supplementary Note 3). Hence, the 
high density of interfaces is believed to notably influence material 
properties by impacting microscale atomic diffusion and macroscale 
deformation processes20,30,31.

The TEM results show that an unusual fully coherent interface 
relationship exists between the MgO NPs and Al matrix (Fig. 4c–g). 
From the illustration shown in Fig. 4b , the similar intrinsic 
face-centred-cubic structures of both MgO and Al at room temperature 
(MgO a = 0.4198 nm, Al a = 0.4050 nm; a is the lattice constant) repre-
sent a minimal mismatch between the two phases. In particular, as the 
temperature increases, the difference in the lattice parameter (a value) 
between the above two phases decreases even further (for example, 
only ~2.0% at 500 °C as indicated in Supplementary Fig. 11). Further -
more, the moiré fringes with identical orientation caused by different 
particles corresponding to the coherency strain in the grain interiors 
were clearly discerned (Fig. 4c). This perfectly matched lattice structure 
indicates a ‘cube-on-cube’ orientation relationship of the MgO/Al 
interface, as proved by the atomic-resolution high-angle annular dark 
field (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) 
images taken with the incident electron beam aligned along the 
[010]MgO, [110]MgO and [ ̄112]MgO axes (Fig. 4d,e,g). Such an orientation 
relationship can be clarified more directly by the fast Fourier transform 
patterns of these images (insets of Fig. 4d,e,g), which revealed the same 
arrangements of spots corresponding to MgO NPs and Al matrix, with 
only a small difference in spot spacing. The perfect matching relation-
ship in all lattice orientations, with a low mismatch of ~3.5%, markedly 
minimizes interfacial energy and lattice distortion, as confirmed by 
the geometrical phase analysis (Supplementary Fig. 12). These char -
acteristics are beneficial for boosting the dispersion stability and 
inhibiting the growth of oxides32,33. Furthermore, this perfect matching 
effectively hinders the climb and glide of dislocations, resulting in high 
tensile and creep strength. This is because it makes dislocation nuclea-
tion challenging due to the high-energy barrier needed to disrupt the 
interface coherency
34. In contrast to other potential ODS Al systems, 
such as Al2O3, Y2O3, La2O3, TiO2 and Ce2O3, where the oxide lattice typi-
cally fails to reach complete coherency with Al (mismatch exceeding 
5%, Supplementary Table 2), the present study demonstrates a proto-
type for achieving full coherence.
In addition, in other reported systems such as ODS ferritic steels, 
oxides such as Y 2Ti2O7 featuring a coherent interface with a steel 
matrix have been challenged by oxide structure transformation due 
to active element diffusion, resulting in the loss of coherency at high 
temperatures35. In this study, the observations confirm that MgO NPs, 
upon embedding into the Al grains, remain unchanged in particle 
size and shape at high temperatures (as revealed in Fig. 1e), implying 
exceptional stability within the lattice of the Al matrix.
Diffusion inhibition by the coherent MgO NPs
The fully coherent MgO NPs could benefit enhanced diffusion inhibi-
tion at interfaces. For substitutional diffusion processes dominated 
by vacancy mechanisms, lattice diffusion coefficients of metal atoms 
are proportional to the jumping probability and vacancy concentra-
tion36. Microscopically, the coherent interface can constrain Al atoms 
to vibrate about their rest positions to maintain the stable minimal 
interfaces and increases the energy barrier of vacancy formation. 
Our DFT calculation results revealed that the binding energy (BE) and 
vacancy formation energy (VFE) of the coherent MgO/Al interface on 
the {111} planes surpassed those of pure Al (111) dramatically (BE MgO/Al, 
7.96 J m−2; BE Al, 1.67 J m−2; VFE MgO/Al, 2.28 eV; VFE Al, 0.97 eV), due to 
the strong Mg–O–Al bond (Supplementary Fig. 13). In contrast, both the 
BE and VFE at the incoherent Al/MgO interface ((111)Al/(100)MgO) were 
sharply reduced (BE (111)Al/(100)MgO, 0.22 J m−2; VFE, (111)Al/(100)MgO, 
0.76 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 13). This suggests that the better the 
coherency for the interface, the stronger the binding and the higher the 
VFE at the interface. As a result, a low vacancy concentration near the 
interface is expected, as the vacancy concentration shows an inverse 
exponential relationship with the VFE36, which leads to limited atomic 
diffusion along the interfaces. Meanwhile, the inherent stability of 
MgO precludes the conditions necessary for trans-interface diffusion. 
Furthermore, the coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) tests in Sup-
plementary Fig. 14 reveal that adding 8 vol% MgO NPs to the Al matrix 
gives rise to a reduction of approximately 20% in CTE, which markedly 
surpasses the expected impact of an 8 vol% composite. This result also 
indirectly verified that the coherent MgO/Al interface is robust and 
highly stable, and can facilitate the interfacial constraint effect. Unlike 
coherent precipitation phases, such as Al3Zr(Sc) (L12 structure) and 
Al2Cu (θ phase) in traditional Al alloys, which would lose their stabili-
zation above 400 °C, these stable coherent MgO NPs serve as a more 
effective barrier for suppressing material softening20,37.

Methods
Materials
In situ synthesis of MgO@GL. Magnesium citrate (Mg3(C6H5O7)2·9H2O) 
powders were hot vacuum decomposed at 800 °C for 30 min under a 
vacuum degree of 10−2 Pa. Specifically, 20 g Mg3(C6H5O7)2·9H2O pow-
der was put in a corundum boat and transferred to a chemical vapour 
deposition quartz tube. The quartz tube was then put into the heating 
zone, in which the temperature was raised to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C 
min−1 with an oil pump to maintain the vacuum degree of ~10 −2 Pa. 
A more detailed discussion of the synthesis of MgO@GL is given in Sup-
plementary Information (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary 
Fig. 17). After the decomposition process at 800 °C for 30 min, the 
resulting thermal decomposition products were placed in a corundum 
boat. These products were then subjected to calcination at 500 °C for 
30 min under an air atmosphere (400 ml min −1). This air calcination 
step was implemented to decrease the carbon content in the MgO@
GL powders. The carbon content of the final MgO@GL was determined 
to be approximately 3.0 wt% according to the testing of a carbon sulfur 
analyser by the full combustion method with powder weights of 5 g.
Fabrication of the MgO@GL/Al composites. Al powder (average 
diameter ~20.0 μm; Changsha Tianjiu Metal Materials) and 8.0 vol% 
MgO@GL powder were loaded into a 304 stainless steel jar. The jar 
was filled with the milling media (304 stainless steel balls) and the 
processing control agent (~1.5 wt% steric acids) in an argon atmos -
phere inside a glove box. The ball-to-powder ratio was 20:1 by weight. 
The powders were then milled for 10 h at a rotation rate of 500 rpm to 
obtain composite powders. The milling process was conducted using 
a planetary ball-milling at a cryogenic temperature of about −100 °C. 
T o maintain the cold environment, liquid nitrogen was injected into the 
freezer. Two resistance temperature detector sensors, positioned at 
the top and bottom of the freezer, were utilized to ensure a cryogenic 
temperature of about −100 ± 20 °C.
The composite powders were cold compacted in a hardened steel 
mould with a diameter of 30 mm under a pressure of 400 MPa fol -
lowed by sintering at 600 °C at a vacuum degree of 10−3 Pa. T o prevent 
oxidation of the samples, all powder transfer procedures were per -
formed inside a glove box. Hot extrusion was further utilized to obtain 
densified MgO@GL/Al. During the processing of hot extrusion, the 
as-sintered samples were preheated at 600 °C for 1 h and then imme-
diately extruded into rods with a diameter of 10 mm. For comparison, 
the MgO/Al samples and PM Al samples were prepared by the same 
PM process as above.
Microstructure observations. Field emission SEM (Hitachi S-4800) 
and TEM ( JEOL F200) were performed to investigate the microstruc-
tures of the MgO@GL and MgO@GL/Al composites. Bright-field STEM 
and energy dispersive spectroscopy mapping images of the MgO@
GL/Al were collected using an FEI Talos F200X and an FEI Titan Cubed 
Themis G2 300 equipped with a probe corrector. Atomic-resolution 
HAADF-STEM observations were conducted using a spherical aberra-
tion probe corrected FEI Themis Z with a Schottky field emitter oper-
ated at 300 KV. Cross-sections of the bulk samples and TEM samples of 
the bulk composites were prepared by grinding to a thickness of ~60 μm 
and thinned by the ion-milling method (Gatan PIPS 695).
Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) was utilized to character-
ize the grain size of bulk MgO@GL/Al samples. Specimens were placed 
in the SEM (ZEISS sigma 300) chamber, tilted 70° from horizontal 
towards the EBSD diffraction camera.
Lattice constants of Al and MgO were obtained by in situ high- 
temperature XRD (Bruker- AXS, D8 Advance) with a Cu Kα (λ  =  
0.1542 nm) radiation. The samples were heated to the expected tem-
peratures (25 °C, 200 °C, 300 °C, 400 °C and 500 °C) under vacuum 
with a heating rate of 10 °C min −1, and then kept at this temperature 
for 20 min before in situ testing.
APT. APT was performed to investigate the discrete MgO NPs 
within the Al grain interior. The APT specimens were derived from 
0.5 × 0.5 × 15 mm3 blocks cut from the as-extruded MgO@GL/Al, and 
then prepared via a 2-stage electropolishing process. Initially, the 
specimens underwent electropolishing in an electrolyte comprising 
30.0 vol% perchloric acid in acetic acid at 15.0 V and room temperature, 
followed by a second electropolishing in a 5.0 vol% perchloric acid 
solution in 2-butoxyethanol at 20.0 V. The APT analysis was conducted 
on a LEAP4000X Si device, with parameters set to an ultraviolet laser 
pulse frequency of 200 kHz, laser energy of 60 pJ, aiming for a 0.5% 
evaporation rate, at a temperature of approximately 40 K, and in a 
vacuum of ~4.0 × 10−11 torr. T omographic reconstruction and analysis 
were carried out using the IVAS 3.6.8 software package from CAMECA.
Mechanical properties measurements. The MgO@GL/Al and PM 
Al samples after hot extrusion were used for tensile and compression 
tests at elevated temperatures in an air environment. T ensile tests 
were conducted on samples with a gauge length of 15 mm and a gauge 
diameter of 3 mm, based on the ISO 6892-1:2016 standard. Compres-
sion samples had a gauge length of 6 mm and gauge diameter of 3 mm. 
T ensile and compressive tests were carried out using a universal testing 
machine (Lloyd (AMETEK) EZ 20) equipped with a resistance heating 
furnace at a strain rate of 2 × 10
−4 s−1. Before testing, the samples were 
heated to the desired temperatures, ranging from 100 °C to 500 °C with 
intervals of 100 °C, and kept at the specified temperature for 30 min. 
Three resistance temperature detector sensors (top and bottom of 
the furnace and ~10 mm from the sample) were used to ensure that the 
desired temperature is achieved and maintained within 0.5 °C.
For in situ Vickers hardness measurements, a microhardness 
tester (ZD-HVZHT-10) with a diamond Vickers indenter was used. The 
measurements were performed on 5-mm-thick disk samples under 
an argon atmosphere. Before testing, the bulk samples were heated 
to the designated temperatures, ranging from 100 °C to 450 °C with 
intervals of 50 °C, and subsequently held at the temperature for 10 min. 
The reported hardness values represent the average of five individual 
measurements, with an error bar.
Creep test. Creep tests were carried out using a Lloyd (AMETEK) EZ 20 
universal testing machine equipped with a resistance heating furnace. 
During the constant load creep test, compression samples with a gauge 
length of 6 mm and a gauge diameter of 3 mm were used. T o ensure 
accurate temperature measurement and control, a thermocouple was 
consistently positioned around the creep specimens. The specimens 
were heated to the expected temperatures (300 °C, 400 °C and 500 °C) 
and held for 0.5 h. Different creep stresses (5 MPa, 10 MPa and 30 MPa 
for PM Al, and 80 MPa, 160 MPa and 240 MPa for MgO@GL/Al) were 
then applied to measure the compressive creep curve and determine 
the steady-state creep rate as a function of applied creep conditions.
CTE. The thermal expansion behaviour of the samples was evaluated 
using thermo-mechanical analysis (NETZSCH DIL402C). Cylindrical 
specimens 20 mm in length and 6 mm in diameter were machined from 
the extruded rods. The testing direction was along the longitudinal 
direction. Under an argon atmosphere, the CTE of MgO@GL/Al and 
PM Al were measured in the temperature range from 25 °C to 500 °C 
with a heating rate of 3 °C min−1.
DFT calculations. Our work was implemented using the Vienna ab initio 
simulation package based on DFT. The projected-augmented-wave poten-
tials48 were utilized to describe the interaction between valence electrons 
and ionic nuclei of all elements. The exchange-correlation energy was 
treated by Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof with a generalized gradient approxi-
mation function49,50 with generalized gradient approximation. The plane- 
wave basis used a kinetic energy cut-off of 400 eV, which was sufficient to 
ensure energy convergence. The first Brillouin area was sampled using a
