Adding Al is an important strategy to obtain ultrahigh speciﬁc strength in BCC refractory high-entropy 
alloys (RHEAs). However, the main structure typically transitions from disordered BCC to ordered B2 with 
increasing Al concentration, leading to poor ductility. In the present study, a phase inversion in a high- 
Al-content B2-RHEA (Zr 40 T i 28 Nb 12 Al 20 ) was systematically studied through thermo-mechanical treatment. 
The grains of the single B2 phase transformed inversely to the BCC + B2 microstructure with a disper- 
sion of spherical B2 precipitates in the BCC grains. The evolution of the microstructure began with the 
decomposition of the B2 phase into Al-rich and Al-poor regions. The subsequent coarsening of the Al-rich 
B2 precipitates continuously consumes Al and Zr atoms from the solution. The depletion of Al and Zr in 
the matrix drives it to gradually form the disordered BCC structure and eventually transform to a single 
BCC phase matrix. This phase inversion enhanced tensile ductility of the RHEA while still maintaining 
its high speciﬁc strength. The current study provides a novel idea for inhibiting Al-induced brittleness of 
RHEAs at high Al content.

1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) break the design concept of a sin- 
gle element as the main component in traditional alloys [ 1 , 2 ]. This 
multicomponent design concept attracts studies of alloys from the 
edge of the phase diagram to the center of the phase diagram, 
which greatly expands the scope of metallic materials [3–5] . Due 
to the unique atomic structure of HEAs, they possess many distin- 
guished physical, chemical and thermodynamic properties, espe- 
cially promising mechanical properties, such as high strength [6–
8] and hardness [ 9 , 10 ], excellent radiation tolerance [11] , oxida- 
tion resistance [12–15] and wear resistance [10] . HEAs have pro- 
found academic value and engineering application prospects and 
are expected to break through the property bottleneck of tradi- 
tional metallic materials. 
Among many HEAs, BCC HEAs composed of several refrac- 
tory metals possess a relatively high intrinsic yield strength [16] . 
For example, the room temperature compressive yield strength of

NbMoTaW HEA is more than 1060 MPa [17] . However, the den- 
sity of these refractory HEAs (RHEAs) is usually very high. There- 
fore, further development of RHEAs is aimed at reducing alloy den- 
sity while maintaining an attractive combination of properties. The 
substitution of low-density Al, Ti, Zr and V for high-density W and 
Ta is one of the important means to achieve light weight [18–20] . 
Notably, the addition of Al not only reduces the density, but can 
also signiﬁcantly improve the strength due to solid solution hard- 
ening as there is a large elastic and atomic size misﬁts between Al 
and most transition metals (TMs) [20] . This strengthening mecha- 
nism makes Al-containing RHEAs maintain high strength [21] . 
Al has a strong interaction with most TM elements as a 
result of the negative mixing enthalpy between them [22] . 
Therefore, the addition of Al will lead to chemical short- 
range order, which promotes the precipitate of B2, L2 1 and 
other ordered phases [23–25] . When 7 at.% Al was added to 
Ti 46 Zr 24 V 15 Nb 15 HEA, the crystal structure changed from the 
single BCC phase to a nanoscale mixture of co-continuous BCC 
and B2 phases [20] . The similar co-continuous microstructure 
was observed in the Al 10 Nb 20 Ta 16 Ti 30 V 4 Zr 20 [26] . In addition, 
Al 11.11 Mo 11.11 Nb 22.22 Ta 11.11 Ti 22.22 Zr 22.22 consists of a continu- 
ous B2 matrix around BCC spherical nanoprecipitates [27] .

Further increase in the Al content can produce single-phase B2 
HEAs such as in Al 15 Nb 12.75 Ti 29.75 Zr 42.5 [28] , Al 20 Nb 12 Ti 28 Zr 40 
[28] , Al 25 Nb 25 Ti 25 V 25 [29] , Al 25 Nb 25 Ta 12.5 Ti 25 Zr 12.5 [27] and 
Al 25 Nb 25 Ti 25 Zr 25 [30] RHEAs. However, these Al-containing RHEAs 
have very limited room temperature ductility, which limits their 
engineering application. The poor ductility is attributed to the 
presence of brittle B2 as the continuous matrix [ 26 , 27 ]. 
Recently, Soni et al. [ 26 , 31 ] reported a new phenomenon of 
“phase-inversion” in an Al-containing RHEA Al 0.5 NbTa 0.8 Ti 1.5 V 0.2 Zr 
(10 at.% Al). They found that the nanoscale mixture of the co- 
continuous BCC and B2 phases was transformed into a BCC matrix 
with discrete B2 precipitates by spinodal decomposition through 
hot isostatically pressed and long-term aging at 600 °C for 120 h. 
After phase inversion, an HEA with a combination of high yield 
stress and good ductility was obtained. Similar to the structure 
of Ni-based superalloys, the continuous soft (BCC) matrix pro- 
vides good ductility, while the hard (B2) precipitates enable very 
high strength by precipitation hardening. According to the previ- 
ous studies of Al-containing RHEAs, a single B2 phase tends to 
form when the Al content is high (e.g. > 15 at.%) [28] . So far, it 
is not clear whether this phase inversion can also occur in single 
B2 phase RHEAs and/or in high-Al-content B2 RHEAs. Furthermore, 
how this phase inversion beneﬁts tensile ductility remains to be 
studied. 
Here, we systematically investigated the microstructural 
and mechanical behaviors of a high-Al-content (20 at.%) 
Zr 40 Ti 28 Nb 12 Al 20 RHEA. We found that the as-cast HEA exhib- 
ited a single ordered B2 structure. Through thermomechanical 
treatment, a phase inversion occurred, where Al-rich B2 precipi- 
tated from the B2 matrix and the matrix transformed to BCC due 
to the depletion of Al and Zr. The effect of these changes in mi- 
crostructure on the mechanical behavior was furthermore studied. 
The new mechanism of attaining soft matrix in RHEAs enables 
a combination of high ultimate strength and good ductility. Our 
ﬁndings show a new way to develop lightweight RHEAs. 
2. Material and methods 
An alloy with a nominal composition of Zr 40 Ti 28 Nb 12 Al 20 was 
produced by induction melting and mixing high-purity metals ( ≥
99.9% weight percent, wt.%) in a high-purity argon atmosphere. 
Each ingot was remelted at least ten times and solidiﬁed in a 
water-cooled crucible with a diameter of 150 mm and height of 
300 mm. Before rolling, the sample was cut into plates with di- 
mensions of 10 mm (thickness) × 20 mm (width) × 100 mm 
(length) by discharge machining. Subsequently, the as-cast sample 
was homogenized at 1473 K for 2 h followed by water quenching 
and then warm-rolled at 923 K to reach a thickness reduction of 
50%. The rolled samples were then isothermally annealed at 923 K 
for 6, 12, 18, 30, 36, 42, 48, 54 and 60 min, and isochronally an- 
nealed at 923, 973, 1023 and 1073 K for 30 min followed by air- 
cooling. 
The tensile testing of the samples was performed by using 
a universal testing machine (INSTRON ElectroPuls E30 0 0) at a 
constant strain rate of 1 × 10 −4 s −1 at room temperature. The 
dog-bone-shaped tensile samples had a gage cross section of 
1.4 mm × 0.8 mm and a length of 5 mm. 
The phase analysis was conducted by X-ray diffraction (XRD, 
Bruker D2 phaser) with Cu- K α radiation ( λ = 0.154 nm) at a scan- 
ning rate of 1 °/min from 20 ° to 70 ° The microstructure was exam- 
ined using Olympus optical microscopy (OM). Morphologies were 
characterized by ﬁeld emission scanning electron microscopy (FE- 
SEM, Zeiss Supra55) equipped with an energy-dispersive spectrom- 
eter (EDS). Samples for OM and SEM characterization were ﬁrst 
mechanically polished to a mirror ﬁnish, and then etched in a 
mixed solution of HF:HNO 3 :H 2 O = 1:1:2 (volume ratio) for 15–
20 s. The sample for SEM-EDS was only polished without etching. 
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) investigations were per- 
formed on an FEI Titan G2 F20 transmission electron microscope 
(TEM) equipped with a Super-X energy dispersive X-ray spectrom- 
etry (EDS) system operated at 200 kV. The samples for TEM analy- 
sis were thinned by ion milling (Gatan model 695). Statistical anal- 
ysis of the size of grain was performed with at least 5 OM im- 
ages. The particle size of precipitates was determined with at least 
5 SEM or TEM images. 
3. Results 
3.1. Microstructural characterisation 
3.1.1. Effect of rolling on microstructure 
Fig. 1 (a) and (b) show the 3D stereographic microstructure re- 
constructed with OMs perpendicular to the rolling direction (RD), 
transverse direction (TD), and normal direction (ND). The grain size 
of the as-cast sample was mainly in the range of 50–800 μm, and 
the average grain diameter ( D ) was approximately 550 ± 98 μm. 
The grain topography of samples after rolling at 923 K displayed 
a stretched lamellar-shaped structure with dimensions of approxi- 
mately 250–1800 μm along the RD, approximately 100–1000 μm 
in the TD, and approximately 20–250 μm in the ND. 
The XRD patterns of the as-cast and as-rolled samples are pre- 
sented in Fig. 1(c). A single family of ordered BCC (B2) peaks is 
clearly observed for the as-cast sample. After rolling at 923 K, the 
sample still retained a single B2 structure without any phase trans- 
formation. The detailed scans (Fig. 1(c)) show that the Bragg an- 
gles of the B2 phase obtained from the (110) B2 peak were 37.571 °
and 37.564 ° for the as-cast and as-rolled samples, respectively. 
The tiny difference between the two Bragg angles indicated that 
the effect of warm rolling on the lattice parameter was negligi- 
ble, which indicated that rolling had little inﬂuence on the crys- 
tal structure of the sample. However, the XRD peak broadened 
signiﬁcantly after rolling. Because the crystallite size of the as- 
rolled samples remained at the micrometer level, the obvious peak 
broadening in the rolled sample was probably attributable to the 
micro-strain caused by the high density of dislocations, as shown 
in Fig. 1 (e). The SEM microstructural observations (back-scattered 
images) suggest a single B2 phase in the rolled sample. The high- 
resolution TEM (HRTEM) image with fast Fourier transform (FFT) 
pattern along the [011] zone axis is shown in Fig. 1(f). The extra 
characteristic (100) superlattice conﬁrmed that the as-rolled sam- 
ple remained a single B2 phase.

samples with isochronal annealing at various temperatures of 923–
1023 K for 30 min are presented in Fig. 2 . In addition to the 
B2 phase, there was a BCC phase in the rolled sample after an- 
nealing at various temperatures, as evidenced by the splitting in 
diffraction peaks of the B2. The BCC peak had a higher Bragg an- 
gle than the B2 peak in the XRD pattern, which implies that the 
lattice parameter of the BCC phase is slightly smaller than that of 
the B2 phase. The Bragg angle of the BCC phase decreased with

4. Discussion 
4.1. Microstructure evolution 
The XRD results ( Fig. 2 ) of the phase transition clearly show 
that the B2 phase was not stable after heat treatment. It decom- 
posed into new B2 and BCC phases within 6 min at 923 K (Fig. 
2(c)). The TEM results of the rolled sample after heat treatment for 
30 min ( Fig. 5 ) show that the spherical precipitate is B2, while the 
matrix is BCC, suggesting that an inversion type of phase transfor- 
mation occurred. Therefore, the transformation path can be sum- 
marized as follows: 
B2 → B2 + B2 / BCC → B2 + BCC (1) 
This kind of phase inversion was ﬁrst reported by Soni et al. 
[ 26 , 31 ] recently in an Al 10 Nb 20 Ta 16 Ti 30 V 4 Zr 20 RHEA. They sug- 
gested that the phase transformation started with spinodal decom- 
position where the single BCC or B2 phases separated to produce 
a co-continuous mixture of BCC and B2 phases. Then, this mixture 
evolved into a microstructure with BCC nano-cuboidal precipitates 
within thin continuous B2 channels around them. Only after long- 
term annealing for 120 h at 600 °C did the phase inversion occur, 
and the BCC phase became continuous with discrete B2 precipi- 
tates. 
In our case, the beginning of the transformation also occurred 
through spinodal decomposition as shown in Fig. 7(b). However, 
the subsequent coarsening of B2 took a different route compared 
with that reported by Soni [ 26 , 31 ]. Here, after only 30 min of 
treatment at 923 K, the matrix already fully transformed into the 
BCC phase as shown in Fig. 7(c). These observations indicated that 
phase inversion from B2 to BCC was completed very quickly within 
a short period of time and the subsequent evolution of microstruc- 
ture involves only the coarsening of B2 in the matrix. The facts 
that the B2 precipitates had a high number density at low temper- 
ature (Fig. 5(c)) and large size at high temperature (Fig. 5(d)) sup- 
ported the above conclusion. Furthermore, Fig. 3(a) shows that af- 
ter the initial jump, the lattice parameters of the B2 precipitate re- 
mained practically ﬁxed, indicating that the composition of the B2 
precipitate kept unchanged mostly. Thus, when B2 coarsened, its 
size increased, but its composition did not change. Meanwhile, the 
lattice parameters of the matrix varied continuously, which sug- 
gested a continuous change in the composition of the matrix. The 
matrix reversed continuously from the B2 phase to the BCC phase 
as the matrix gradually depletes Al and Zr during the precipitation

process ( Fig. 8 ). The main difference in the coarsening processes

The microstructure evolution can also be explained by a sim- 
pliﬁed free energy ( G ) diagram as a function of composition (Al), 
as shown in Fig. 12 . The ﬁrst step is the decomposition of a sin- 
gle B2 region to two regions, Al-poor and Al-rich B2 (dotted line 
in Fig. 12 ). Then, coarsening occurred, resulting in the reduction of 
Al and Zr in the matrix, which in turn promotes the disordering 
transformation of the matrix from B2 structure to BCC structure 
gradually (blue arrow in Fig. 12 ). During the ﬁrst step, it is diﬃcult 
to observe the state where two B2 phases co-exist, which is proba- 
bly attributable to the slight difference in composition between the 
two B2 phases. When the distinction is suﬃcient to be identiﬁed, 
Al-poor B2 is rapidly becoming disordered. Soni et al. [26,31] sug- 
gested that their alloy establishes a single phase at high temper- 
atures. Due to the absence of thermodynamic barriers in spinodal 
decomposition, the instances of single phase are not captured in 
their alloy. This is highly speculative. Fortunately, the experimen- 
tal results in this study appropriately veriﬁed their speculation. 
More interestingly, this study proved that the structure of BCC ma- 
trix + B2 precipitates formed much faster than that in the alloy 
reported by Soni et al. [ 26 , 31 ].

4.2.2. Phase inversion enhances plasticity 
The results of tensile testing clearly showed that the ultimate 
strength of the as-cast samples is only 512 MPa without any ob- 
vious plastic deformation. Compared with a previous report [34] , 
the yield strength of Zr 50 Ti 35 Nb 15 HEA without any Al addition was 
657 MPa. In theory, due to the solid-solution effect caused by the 
Al addition [ 8 , 20 , 35 ], the yield strength of (Zr 0.5 Ti 0.35 Nb 0.15 ) 80 Al 20 
HEAs should be signiﬁcantly greater than that of Zr 50 Ti 35 Nb 15 . 
Therefore, the low ultimate strengths also implied that the as-cast 
sample fractured before reaching its yield strength. The reasons 
for the early fracture may be related to the intrinsic brittleness 
of B2 and casting defects. Although rolling can eliminate defects, 
the sample inherits the brittle nature of B2. As has been generally 
reported [36–38] , short-term strain-hardening ability is one of the 
important factors that inhibit the tensile plasticity of B2 HEAs. The 
plastic deformation of B2 HEAs at room temperature is regulated 
by kink pair nucleation and propagation of screw dislocations. This 
special deformation mode makes B2 HEAs unstable after yielding. 
After phase inversion, the sample has the maximum ductility. The 
obvious change in the inverted microstructure in the sample was 
that the matrix became BCC structure. Considering the structure 
of Ni-based superalloys, the continuous softer (BCC) matrix could 
provide good ductility. The XRD results showed that the intensity 
of the BCC peak increased with increasing annealing temperature, 
which indicated that the relative content of the BCC matrix was in- 
creased. Unfortunately, ductility did not increase after higher tem- 
perature annealing due to the precipitation of coarsened Al 3 Zr 5 . A 
method to suppress the formation of grain boundary precipitates 
during phase inversion at higher temperatures for this RHEA re- 
quires further investigation.

actions between dislocations and particles, precipitation strength- 
ening mechanisms can be classiﬁed as Orowan bypass or parti-

5. Conclusions 
The ultimate aim of the current study was to apply thermo- 
mechanical treatment to obtain balanced mechanical properties for 
initially brittle RHEAs with high Al content (20 at.%). This goal was 
successfully achieved by microstructure modiﬁcation. Based upon 
the above experimental observations and theoretical analysis, the 
role of the resultant microstructure in altering the tensile proper- 
ties was revealed, and the following conclusions can be made: 
(1) A phenomenon of phase inversion from B2 to BCC in a 
Zr 40 Ti 28 Nb 12 Al 20 with high Al content of 20 at.% was observed. 
After annealing, this inversion occurred from a single B2 phase 
to a mixture of BCC matrix with dispersed with B2 precipitates. 
The matrix transformed from B2 structure to BCC structure due 
to the loss of Al and Zr during the precipitation of Al-rich B2. 
The phase inversion based on the disordered transition mech- 
anism is different from previous reports. In this mechanism, 
rolling can accelerate this phase transition due to the introduc- 
tion of deformation energy storage. 
(2) The distribution and morphology (size and number density) of 
coherent B2 precipitates were determined primarily by anneal- 
ing. The precipitated phase of B2 easily nucleated at low tem- 
perature and tended to grow at high temperature. The num- 
ber density of B2 precipitates decreased from ∼225/ μm 2 for 
sample annealed at 923 K to ∼20/ μm 2 for sample annealed at 
1073 K, with sizes ranging from 62 to 180 nm. 
(3) The mechanical behaviors of the current samples were charac- 
terized by tension testing at room temperature. The as-rolled 
sample showed the highest yield strength of ∼1779 MPa, ulti- 
mate strength of ∼1892 MPa and speciﬁc strength of ∼328 MPa 
cm 3 g −1 . The rolled sample after annealing at 923 K for 
30 min can improve the plastic deformation ability (Elonga- 
tion increased from 1.5% to 3.4%) while high speciﬁc strength 
( ∼290 MPa cm 3 g −1 ) was maintained. Grain boundary particles 
(Al 3 Zr 5 ) were the chief reason for brittleness in the rolled sam- 
ple after annealing at temperature above 923 K. 
(4) The sample maintains ultrahigh speciﬁc strength, which was 
probably attributed to the lightweight effect caused by the Al 
additions and coherent precipitation strengthening of the B2 
phase. The transformation of the matrix to the BCC structure 
enhanced the ability to undergo plastic deformation. The above 
reasons led to an extraordinary balance of mechanical proper- 
ties.
