The body centered cubic (BCC) high entropy alloys MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW
show exceptional strength retention up to 1900K. The mechanistic origin of the
retained strength is unknown yet is crucial for ﬁnding the best alloys across the
immense space of BCC HEA compositions. Experiments on Nb-Mo, Fe-Si and
Ti-Zr-Nb alloys report decreased mobility of edge dislocations, motivating a
theory of strengthening of edge dislocations in BCC alloys. The strength of
BCC HEAs can be controlled by edge dislocations, especially at high temper-
atures, because the random ﬁeld of solutes in these high-concentration alloys
creates large energy barriers for thermally-activated edge glide. A parameter-
free theory for this mechanism of edge motion in BCC alloys qualitatively and
quantitatively captures the strength versus temperature for both MoNbTaW
and MoNbTaVW alloys and other BCC HEAs. A reduced analytic version
of the theory, embodying the edge-strengthening mechanism, then enables
screening over >600,000 compositions in the Mo-Nb-Ta-V-W family to iden-
tify promising new compositions with high retained strength and/or reduced
mass density. Overall, the theory reveals an unexpected mechanism - edge
dislocation motion in the random alloy - as responsible for high temperature
strength in BCC alloys and paves the way for theory-guided design of new
high-performance HEAs.

1 Introduction
Among the emerging class of “high entropy alloys” [1–7], are the body-centered-cubic (BCC)
HEAs composed of elements in the family Cr-Mo-Nb-V-Ta-W-Hf-Ti-Zr at near equal concen-
trations with the different atom types occupying the crystalline BCC lattice sites at random [8].
The refractory BCC HEAs of nominal compositions MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW have recently
been shown to possess exceptional strengths at temperatures up to 1900K [1, 2], far above the
limits of∼1100K for existing superalloys (Figure 1).

The underlying physical origins of this impressive behavior in these high-complexity alloys
are unknown. Across the entire class of refractory BCC HEAs, it has been recently highlighted
[9] that “the relationship between composition, microstructure, and high-temperature mechanical
properties needs to be established, which can be quite different from the relationships acquired
at RT”. Here, we establish an important component of this relationship, the connection between
composition and strength at both RT and high temperature. This quantitative connection enables
the possible discovery of new compositions with even better high-temperature performance.
Speciﬁcally, we postulate, develop the theory for, and validate, that the strengthening,

especially at high temperatures, can be controlled by the glide of edge dislocations. This makes
some BCC HEAs distinctly different than elemental BCC metals and dilute BCC alloys, where
screw dislocation motion is well-established to control the yield strength. Since edge dislocations
in BCC alloys are nearly universally neglected, our postulate here is novel and counter to standard
thinking. To motivate the role of edge dislocations, we thus ﬁrst examine the role of screw
dislocations and then highlight often-overlooked experimental evidence that edge dislocations
are increasingly relevant in high-concentration BCC alloys. With this historical background, the
notion that edge dislocations can control the strength in some complex BCC HEAs becomes
more palatable and motivates our detailed study.
The yield strength of BCC pure metals [ 10] is well-understood in terms of the motion of
screw dislocations via thermally-activated double-kink nucleation. While the strength is very
high (1-2 GPa) at T=0K due to the nucleation barrier, it decreases quickly [ 11] to 100-200
MPa at T≃300K. In low concentration binary alloys, the strength increases signiﬁcantly at low
T because, although double-kink nucleation is easier, kink glide becomes strongly inhibited
[12, 13]. Glide on different available glide planes also leads to strengthening via jog/dipole
formation. The dominance of screw dislocations is clear in TEM studies, which show long
straight screw dislocations punctuated by jog/dipoles. These general features are embodied in
the classical screw model of Suzuki [12] for binary alloys, and also in a new theory for screw
motion in BCC alloys of arbitrary complexity [14]. For example, Figure 2a shows the predicted
critical resolved shear strength (CRSS) for screw motion in Nb1−xMox versus temperature up to
x = 25% along with the data of Statham et al. [15] as predicted by our new screw theory [14];
the agreement is very good. Similar agreement could likely be obtained by ﬁtting the Suzuki
model; the point here is that both theory and experiments show that the strengths of many BCC
alloys are controlled by screw dislocations.
However, TEM observations in both Nb1−xMox [15] and the classic Fe1−xSix,x≤ 9% alloy
[16] reveal that edge dislocations have a decreasing mobility (higher strength) with increasing
solute content. The ﬁnal sentence in the paper by Statham et al. [ 15] states “at high solute
contents, the effects [above] lead to an appreciable reduction in the edge:screw mobility ratio”.
Quantitatively, Caillard [16] estimated a CRSS of 125 MPa for the edge dislocation in Fe-9%Si
as compared to 200 MPa for the screw dislocation. Our new theory, presented in detail below, is
consistent with these ﬁndings. Figure 2b shows the predicted CRSS for both screw and edge
dislocations using our new screw and edge theories for Nb 1−xMox up to x = 50% . These
predictions show that indeed the edge strength is increasingly competitive with the screw strength
with increasing temperature and concentration, fully consistent with the conclusions of Statham

Recent studies on HEAs in the BCC Ti-Zr-Hf-Nb-Ta family also provide evidence of
the increasing importance of edge dislocations. X-ray line analysis at low plastic strains in
TiZrHfNbTa [17] indicates dominance of edge dislocations at the start of plastic ﬂow. TEM
studies [18] show screw dislocation dominance at larger plastic strains, but newer observations
at elevated temperatures (∼773K) [19] show dislocations with considerable curvature and a
“viscous” motion, indicating a loss of strong screw dominance (although the jogs on the screws
remain prominent, whether or not the strength is controlled by the screw components). In new
work on Ti 50Zr25Nb25 at room temperature [ 20], Mompiou et al. show that edges are also
sluggish, becoming comparable in strength to screw dislocations. Speciﬁcally, they measure a
velocity of 28.5 nm/s for edge dislocations and 4.5 nm/s for screw dislocations, a difference that
corresponds to only a small difference in stress levels needed to drive the dislocations.
Other evidence for the role of the edge dislocation in the strengthing of BCC HEAs also exists.
Yield strengths have been correlated with solute misﬁt volumes [21, 22]; this is a hallmark of edge-
dominated strengthening. Furthermore, ﬁrst-principles and interatomic potential computations
of solute/screw interaction energy in the refractory metals show no correlation with solute misﬁt

volumes. To explain other experiments in BCC alloys, simpliﬁed versions of the Suzuki model
[13, 23] invoke an athermal stress operative at elevated temperatures and attributed to a vague
“solute pinning”; this is precisely what the transition to edge dislocation dominance achieves.
The above introductory discussion shows that, while never previously considered as relevant
in BCC alloys, there is unambiguous experimental support in both old and new literature for
the emergence of edge dislocation motion as important in high concentration/complex BCC
alloys especially at higher temperatures. This evidence clearly motivates the present detailed
investigation of edge dislocation motion through random BCC alloys.
The remainder of this paper is as follows. In Section 2 we present the theory of edge
dislocation strengthening in BCC random alloys (hereafter referred to as “the theory”). In
Section 3, the theory is validated against Molecular Statics simulations at T=0K. In Section 4,
we compare predictions of the theory to experiments on Nb-Mo-Ta-W-V high entropy alloys
and make predictions for some other existing alloys. In Section 5, we use the theory to search
over>600,000 compositions in the Mo-Nb-Ta-V-W family and we identify new alloys predicted
to have even higher strength or strength/weight ratios than those of NbMoTaVW. Many other
compositions with comparable strengths are predicted, and can satisfy additional performance
requirements. Overall, these insights and the theory open a new direction for theory-guided
design of advanced high-temperature materials based on the high-entropy concept.
2 Theory of edge dislocation motion in BCC alloys
The theory of strengthening for edge dislocations in BCC high entropy alloys follows concep-
tually the analyses previously presented for dilute and HEA FCC alloys [ 24, 25]. However,
the detailed analysis is new and incorporates more details than exist in the previous theories,
and so is discussed fully here. The random distribution of solutes in the lattice leads to local
ﬂuctuations in the solute concentrations. The dislocation is attracted to ﬂuctuations that lower
the system energy and is repelled by ﬂuctuations that increase the system energy. A long BCC
edge dislocation line, which unlike the BCC screw dislocation is very ﬂexible, therefore adopts
a wavy conﬁguration as it ﬁnds energetically-favorable regions of solutes (see Figure 3a). The
waviness is constrained by the energy cost of increasing the dislocation line length and curvature
(i.e. constrained by line tension). A characteristic waviness thus emerges that minimizes the total
dislocation energy, and this waviness is characterized by an amplitudewc and a lateral length
ζc, with the wavelength some multiple of ζc. In this minimum energy wavy state, dislocation
segments of length∼ ζc reside in local minimum energy positions (locally favorable solute
ﬂuctuations); i.e. they are pinned in these locations. Along the glide plane, these segments

3 Theory validation using Molecular Statics simulations
We now validate the edge theory against atomistic simulations of edge motion at T=0K in model
alloys in the Mo-Nb-Ta-V-W family. We use EAM-type interatomic potentials [26–28] for these
elements and their alloys. The accuracy of these potentials relative to real alloys is not important
for this validation: the theory uses material inputs derived from the alloy potentials and predicts
the alloy strength, which is then compared to simulations on exactly the same alloy. We also
note clearly that there are no spurious features that arise in studying BCC edge dislocations
using EAM potentials, as compared to the well-known problems (polarized cores, split cores,
double-hump Peierls potentials) in using EAM potentials to model BCC screw cores.
EAM potentials for an alloy family can be used to create a single “average atom” potential
for the effective matrix of a given alloy composition [30]. The accuracy of the average-atom
EAM potential versus true random EAM alloys is very good. Table 1 shows the misﬁt volumes
of all solutes and the alloy elastic constants as computed in the true random alloys and in the
average-atom alloy, and differences in these key quantities are small. The edge dislocation core
structures in all of the pure elements and in all the alloys studied here show no dissociation
and spreading of the core over a width of a few Burgers vectors, as expected based on Peierls-

The simpliﬁed elasticity-based edge theory can now be used to search for other high-strength
and high-strength/weight compositions in the Mo-Nb-Ta-V-W family.
Inputs to the simpliﬁed theory are the misﬁt volumes obtained from the accurate Vegard’s

elemental concentration varied from 0 to 1 in increments of 1/60). The predicted strengths and
strength/weight ratios atT = 1300K are shown in Figures 9a,b normalized by the top-performing

7 Discussion and summary
Unlike superalloys (Figure 1), the MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW BCC HEAs do not show a
precipitous drop in strength at high T (roughly half the melting temperatureTm). The present
edge strengthening mechanism rationalizes this result. The strength is intrinsic to the nature of
the atomic-scale complexity of the HEA alloy and does not rely on mechanisms that can be easily
defeated by high-temperature diffusional/dislocation-climb processes. Thus, the dislocations
must move through the random alloy itself - there are no easy paths of dislocation motion that
can circumvent the large barriers, which appear throughout the alloy on the scales ofζc andwc,
responsible for the strengthening. It is possible that climb can assist in moving out of some of
the low-energy environments and facilitate glide, but with some compensating strengthening
due to jog formation and dragging. Such issues remain to be investigated. In contrast, the
high-temperature strength of the screw dislocation is dominated by pinning due to jogs/dipoles.
Jog/dipole strengthening is defeated at high temperatures by thermal vacancies that eliminate
the high energy barrier for self-interstitial creation that controls the jog strength. Thus, the
differences in high temperature performance among different BCC HEAs - the pressing issue
identiﬁed by [9] - may be attributable to screw versus edge control of strengthening. The edge
theory alone also provides a lower-bound estimate of the high-T strengthening. That is, if the
edge theory predicts a high strength, then a high strength can be expected. If the edge theory
predicts a low strength, then the alloy may be screw controlled, and still have good high-T
properties up to roughly 0.6Tm [50].
The present theory is not perfect, with some deviations versus experiments and simulations.

The theory also does not include a mechanism to predict the typical strength plateau occurring
between 900K and 1200K. There may also be additional strengthening due to ﬂuctuations at
scale below those that can be represented by a line tension model. The theory does not include
direct solute-solute interactions nor short-range-order that can exist in some alloys. Thus, further
theoretical work remains to be done to address these issues. However, we believe that an old
adage applies well here: “all theories are wrong but some are useful”.
The prediction of a wide range of compositions that match or exceed the strengths of existing
alloys (Figure 9) opens avenues for optimization across a much broader range of properties such
as oxidation resistance, diffusional creep, and ductility, while maintaining high-temperature
strength. With suitable models for those properties, optimization can be performed to discover
alloys having the desired mix of properties. Mechanical property optimization can also be
combined with thermodynamic models to avoid compositions where undesirable intermetallic
phase formation is predicted [44, 51–53], thus allowing for simultaneous computationally-guided
design of performance and processing of new alloys. Together with an associated theory for
strengthening of screw dislocations [14], the theories present a framework for analyzing BCC
HEAs across all families and all temperatures.
In summary, a mechanistic parameter-free theory quantitatively captures the exceptional
high ﬂow stresses from T=0K-1900K of Mo-Nb-Ta-V-W High Entropy Alloys. The trapping of
dislocations in statistically-favorable random solute environments creates intrinsic large energy
barriers for the edge dislocation motion. The nature of this trapping of the dislocations makes
these alloys robust against typical high temperature softening mechanisms of screw dislocation
strengthening. A reduced analytic version of the theory enables rapid screening across the entire
composition space, leading to the identiﬁcation of new promising alloys. This opens avenues for
computationally-guided multi-property optimization and discovery of new high-performance
materials.

A Dependence of theory predictions on dislocation line ten-
sion
The T=0K yield strength and energy barrier depend on the line tensionΓ, scaling asτy0∝ Γ−1/3
and ∆Eb∝ Γ1/3, respectively. Changes in Γ thus change the strength and barrier in opposite
directions, leading to some cancellation of effects at moderate and high temperatures. Thus,
predictions are not strongly sensitive to Γ except at very low temperature.
The line tension is generally expressed as Γ = α ¯µb 2, which captures the proper scalings
with (effective isotropic) alloy shear modulus ¯µ andb. Variations in Γ are thus manifest through
the non-dimensional parameterα. Work in FCC alloys has previously usedα = 1/8 ([24, 54])
based on atomistic simulations of dislocation bow-out [55]. Varvenne et al. [ 24] have shown
that the low-temperature strength may be better-predicted using the value α = 1/16. For
long dislocation lines, the line tension is dominated by elasticity and so is independent of any
underlying crystal structure. Thus, aside from the contributions due to dislocation core energy
that are not negligible at very small dislocation lengths, values forα for BCC metals are expected
to be in the same range as those for FCC metals.
With the above background, Figure 10 shows the predictions for strengths of the BCC HEAs
for the valuesα = 1/16, 1/12, 1/8 that span the expected range. Results in the main text use
α = 1/12. The results in Figure 10a show that the room temperature (RT) predictions are rather
sensitive to the speciﬁc line tension. However, as anticipated, the results in Figure 10b show that
predictions at moderate to high temperatures (296–1900K) are quite insensitive to the speciﬁc
choice of the line tension Γ, and within the uncertainties associated with other details (grain size
effects, as-cast microstructure effects, solute/dislocation interaction energies, model uncertainty).

C Possible origin of the high-T strength plateau
The experiments show a plateau in yield strength in the range 800K–1100K. The origin of
this plateau in BCC alloys is not well-established. We postulate that dynamic strain aging
(DSA) via “cross-core diffusion” [56] may generate additional strengthening in these alloys at
intermediate temperatures. Cross-core diffusion occurs when solutes diffuse locally only across
the core from higher-energy sites to lower-energy sites (see Figure 3b). This leads to a time- and
temperature-dependent strengthening that saturates once all cross-core motion has occurred, after
which the normal decrease in strength with increasing temperature resumes. This is consistent
with the experimental results in Figure 3b. The origins of the plateau require deeper study but do
not detract from the broad success of the theory.
