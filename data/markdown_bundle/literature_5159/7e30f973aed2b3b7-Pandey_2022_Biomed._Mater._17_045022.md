Abstract
Titanium-based biomedical alloys are susceptible as they are used as a substitute for human bone.
In this study, titanium alloy, Ti–5Cu–x%Nb (x = 0, 5, 10, 15) (%wt) was developed by powder
metallurgy route. The effect of alloying niobium with Ti–5Cu alloy and its effect on the
microstructure, mechanical strength, corrosion resistance, and antibacterial properties have been
evaluated. The results show that the sintered alloy has both α-Ti and Ti2Cu phases. With increasing
niobium content in the alloy, β-Ti was also detected. Additionally, it was found that the
micro-hardness and compressive strength of the studied alloy was better than commercially pure
titanium (cpTi), while the Y oung’s modulus was lower than cpTi. These properties are highly
favorable for using this alloy to replicate the human cortical bone. The alloy was also tested for
anticorrosive property in Ringer’s solution. The antibacterial activity was also examined for
Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli bacteria. The alloy showed promising anticorrosive and
antibacterial ability.
1.Introduction
Commercially pure titanium (cpTi) has long been
the primary biomaterial for dental, hip, and knee
implant manufacturing and other biomedical pur-
poses because of its good corrosion resistance, high
ratio of strength to weight, outstanding biocompatib-
ility, and superior mechanical strengths [1, 2]. On the
other hand, titanium has inherent casting challenges
due to its high melting point (1668 ◦C) and substan-
tial reactivity with gases at high temperatures. One of
the main problems with titanium and other metallic
implant is incongruous strength between bone and
implant. In adverse cases, the titanium implant can
also create problems inside the body tissue. The high
elastic modulus of metallic implants, greater than
110 GPa, which is more than three to four times the
elastic modulus of cortical bone (1–30 GPa), is the
main reason behind the stress-shielding and early fail-
ure of the implant inside the body [ 3]. Many bioma-
terials like Nb, Zr, Mo, T a, etc, are used with titanium
to lower its elastic properties, increase hardness, and
enhance corrosion resistance; among them, niobium
plays an excellent role as lower down the elastic mod-
ulus of alloy [ 4–6]. Niobium with 35% mass frac-
tion, when alloyed with Ti–5Zr and Ti–10Zr, both its
hardness and elastic modulus, diminishes to a greater
extent [7]. The presence of the β-phase of titanium
in Ti–Nb alloys results in lower elastic modulus, a
higher strength, and superior biocompatibility com-
pared to other Ti-based alloys [ 8, 9]. The addition of
niobium stabilizes the β-phase of titanium, resulting
in a lower elastic modulus [10, 11]. The hardness and
elastic modulus of Ti-based alloys are affected by the
addition of niobium. It has been reported in the lit-
erature that the hardness of Ti-based alloys increases
with the addition of niobium up to 15%, and it starts
decreasing after that. It has also been observed that
both the elastic modulus and hardness decrease with
niobium content up to 35% [ 12, 13].
One more reason for the early failure of titanium
and the metallic implant is the lack of antibac-
terial activity along with dental plaque shortly after
insertion [ 14, 15]. Metal ions such as Cu 2+, Ag +,

Previous research has focussed on alloying
titanium and titanium alloys with copper or niobium
alone, but the effect of alloying both has not been
investigated yet. The present study is aimed to evalu-
ate the impact of alloying both niobium and copper
with titanium for biomedical applications especially
dental implants. It was reported earlier that titanium
with 5% copper gives excellent hardness properties,
antibacterial activity, lower elastic modulus, and good
cytocompatibility [ 19]. Thus, the Ti–5Cu alloy has
been prepared with the addition of varying concen-
trations of niobium by powder metallurgy route. The
mechanical properties, microstructure, corrosion
behavior, and antibacterial property were assessed
and compared the behavior of the implant with cpTi.
2.Experimentalmethodology
2.1.Alloypreparation
In this present investigation, four Ti–5Cu– x%Nb
(x = 0, 5, 10, 15) alloys designated as, Ti–5Cu (S1),
Ti–5Nb–5Cu (S2), Ti–5Cu–10Nb (S3) and Ti–5Cu–
15Nb (S4) were prepared using conventional sinter-
ing process. Titanium powder (99.5% purity, 44 µm,
Alfa Aesar), copper powder (99.98% purity, 44 µm,
Alfa Aesar), and niobium powder (99.99% purity,
44 µm, Alfa Aesar) were used for the study. The metal
powder of the desired weight ratio was ball milled in
RETSCH PM 400 planetary ball mill using tungsten
carbide vial and 10 mm diameter tungsten carbide
ball (ball to powder ratio 10:1) in an inert atmo-
sphere (argon ⩾ 99.9% purity) to avoid oxidation.
T o prevent the sticking of metal powder in the peri-
phery of the vial and ball, milling was done for 5 h
at the speed of 200 rpm in the presence of toluene.
After it, the alloy powder was uniaxially compressed
by the manual press in a die of 15 mm diameter at
650 MPa for 30 s. All samples were then kept in a
high vacuum (10 −6 torr) quartz tube sealed on both
sides and sintered at 900 ◦C in an inert atmosphere
for 1 h with a heating rate of 5 ◦C min−1. Before any
test, all samples were grinded with SiC paper from 400
to 2500 grit size and polished with 0.9 µm diamond
paste on an ultra-pad (Buehler, USA). cpTi (ASTM
B265-20a UNS R50700, Nextgen Steel, and Alloys,
Mumbai, India) plate of similar dimension was used
as the control sample. The elemental compositions of
the cpTi are listed in table 1 as provided by the com-
pany. Also, the nomenclature of the sintered alloys is
listed in table 2.

2.5.Antibacterialproperties
2.5.1. Preparation
The antibacterial test of all the sintered alloy was per-
formed in the microbiology laboratory of the Insti-
tute of Medical Science, Banaras Hindu University.
About 10.0 g peptone, 5.0 g beef extract, 5.0 g NaCl,
and 15.0 g agar were dissolved in 1 l distilled water
to prepare nutrient broth (NB), and the pH level was
kept between 7.3 and 7.4. The pH of the phosphate
buffer saline (PBS) solution was adjusted to 7.2–7.4
after dissolving 2.83 g Na 2HPO4 and 1.36 g KH 2PO4
in 1 l distilled water. The NB and PBS solutions were
autoclaved for 20 min at 121 ◦C to sterilize them.
The strains employed in this investigation were
S. aureus ATCC 6538 and E. coli ATCC 25 922.
The bacteria were grown to a concentration of
106 CFU ml −1 in nutritional broth at 37 ◦C, then
diluted ten times in PBS solution to 10 4 CFU ml −1
(bacterial suspension). Before starting the experi-
ment, UV irradiation was used to sterilize all glass-
ware and samples.
All the samples with size ϕ15 mm × 4 mm size
were ground polished up to 2500 grit size SiC paper
and finally polished with 0.9 µm fine diamond paste
and spray on cloth. The 3D surface profilometer
(T aylor and Hobson, England) was used to check the
roughness of polished sample. The round surface of
sample was measured in two direction X and Y, and
three reading was taken for accuracy. The average
value of Ra (0.02 µm) was maintained for all testing
on the samples.
2.5.2. Plate count method
The plate count method was carried out accord-
ing to the National Standard of China GB/T 2591
(JIS Z 2801-2000, ASTM G21-96, NEQ). Both the
samples Ti–5Cu–(0%–15%)–Nb and the control
sample (cpTi) were rested in separate Petri dishes.
Nutrient agar was put onto a Petri dish as a neg-
ative blank sample. After this, 0.4 ml of the bac-
terial suspension was poured on all these samples,
including the control sample, test sample, and neg-
ative sample. Then, the dishes were covered with a
relatively large Petri dish and put for incubation for
24 h under a humidity of 90% at the temperature
of 37 ◦C. After incubation, the contaminated strain
was washed in 3.6 ml of sterile physiological saline
solution and transferred to a sterilized Petri dish. The
samples were thoroughly cleaned to ensure that no
bacteria remained on them. About 0.02 ml of the
above-mentioned washing solution was inoculated
into nutrient agar plates and incubated for 4 h with
a humidity of 90% at the temperature of 37 ◦C. After
this, the number of colonies of the bacteria was coun-
ted. Three samples of each alloy were inspected for
repeatability. The antibacterial rate ( R) was obtained
using the following formula:

3.1.Phaseidentificationandmicrostructure

Figures 1 and 2 shows the XRD pattern of the high-
energy ball-milled alloy powder and sintered alloy
of S1, S2, S3, and S4, respectively. For comparison
with cpTi, the XRD pattern of purchased titanium
grade 4 is also depicted in figure 2. The mixed ball-
milled powder XRD pattern clearly shows the distinct
peak of titanium (JCPDS reference no 00-044-1294),
copper (01-089-2838), and niobium (00-002-1108).
This shows that the alloy powder was mixed in a
non-reactive manner, with no additional compound
formed between the metal powders. Besides these,
the XRD patterns of sintered alloys (S1–S4) show
peaks of α-titanium, β-titanium, and compound
of copper and titanium. With increasing niobium
content, the β-phase of titanium increases, and a
major peak is observed. The binary phase diagram
of titanium–copper and titanium–niobium suggests
that at 900 ◦C, only solid-state fusion takes place
between the powder. Titanium in both phases (α and
β) can be observed in the XRD pattern of S2, S3,
and S4. The peak of all sintered alloys shifted slightly
left in comparison to peaks of purchased cpTi; this is
because the inclusion of other doping ions (Cu, Nb)
causes a change in the lattice parameter.
In figure 2, alloy S1 has only titanium and cop-
per peaks, whereas samples S2, S3, and S4 have peaks
for all three elements. In the XRD pattern of the
sintered alloy at 38.5 (2 θ◦), it is observed that with
an increasing amount of niobium, the peak also
increased gradually, and major peaks were observed
for sample S4. This is because of the formation of
the β-phase with increasing niobium percentage in
the alloy. All the major and minor peaks of sintered
alloy are analyzed by X’pert HighScore Plus soft-
ware and match with reference JCPDS No (00-044-
1288, 00-044-1294, 01-072-0441). The average crys-

the XRD pattern for the milled powder and sintered

the average crystalline size and average lattice strain

average crystalline size of S4 is nearly the same as the
average crystalline size of cpTi. The volume fraction
of the β-phases in the sintered alloys also increases
from S2 to S4, and the highest 28.2% was observed

stabilizes the β-phases in the alloys [ 26]. The major
and minor peaks of titanium and compound Ti 2Cu
match [20], and the XRD diffraction peak of titanium
and niobium alloys match exactly with previous
research [12].

is highest compared to other samples. The addition
of niobium reduces the sintered density, and similar
behavior is reported when niobium is mixed with Ag
in titanium [ 28]. The inclusion of copper amplifies
the micro-hardness of titanium [ 19]. Therefore, all
samples have more hardness than cpTi. Here, adding
5% niobium increases the hardness to a maximum
level and again decreases to a lower value for 15%
niobium (S4) because of lower density. So, samples
S3 and S4 have lower hardness values than other
samples. Similar behavior is reported in the literat-
ure when niobium with 10% and 15% alloyed with
Ti–15Mo [4].
Figure 10 shows the compressive stress–strain plot
for samples S1, S2, S3, and S4 obtained by com-
pression test. The addition of niobium increases the
yielding strain of the alloy. The biomaterial should
have a high yield strength and low elastic modulus
to avoid the stress shielding effect. The yield strain
value increases with the addition of niobium and is
maximum for sample S4. Figure 11 shows the rela-
tionship between compressive strength and elastic
modulus with increasing niobium percentage. The
slopes of compressive strength follow the same trend
as elastic modulus slope. The highest value of com-
pressive strength is for sample S2 (1090 MPa) and the
lowest for sample S4 (644 MPa), indicating that 5%
niobium has better compressive strength than that of
10% and 15%. The 0.2% offset strain method determ-
ined the alloy’s elastic modulus. The elastic modulus
value of all four alloys is much nearer to the elastic
modulus of human cortical bone (13–20 GPa). The
S2 has a higher elastic modulus among all the alloys
because of lower-yielding strain. Commercially used
cpTi and stainless steel have a higher young mod-
ulus ( ⩾100 GPa) which is the main reason for the
failure of the implant. Niobium acts as a β-stabilizer
element when alloyed with titanium. The β-phase of
titanium has a body-centred cubic (BCC) structure
and is the reason for the suitable mechanical prop-
erty. The different processing routes and new alloy-
ing elements are the main reason for the sintered
alloy’s lower elastic modulus and higher compress-
ive strength. Therefore, we can conclude that adding
niobium to the Ti–5Cu alloy gives a better mechanical
property.

4.Conclusions
From the above results and discussions, the following
conclusions may be inferred:
(a) The Ti–5Cu– x%Nb ( x = 0, 5, 10, 15) was
developed by conventional sintering by powder
metallurgy route.
(b) With alloying niobium into Ti–5Cu, the XRD
pattern and microstructure of the alloy consti-
tute all α-titanium, Ti2Cu, and α + β titanium.
For 15% niobium, the alloy shows the BCC
structure of titanium and successfully stabilizes
the β-phase.
(c) The sintered alloys had higher compressive
strength and lower elastic modulus than cpTi.
