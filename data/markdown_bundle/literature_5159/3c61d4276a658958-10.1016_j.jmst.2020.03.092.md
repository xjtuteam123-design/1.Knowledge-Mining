Highlights 
 The addition of Nb can change phase composition and microstructure of Ti-Zr alloy. 
 The work hardening of TZ5N alloy is caused by stress-induced martensite and twinning. 
 TZ10N and TZN alloys are promising materials for biomedical applications. 
 
ABSTRACT 
In order to expand the application horizon of Ti-Zr based alloys, the influence of Nb content on 
phase composition, microstructural evolution and biomechanical properties is systematically studied. 
The phase and microstructural characterization of the as-cast alloys is carried out by X-ray 
diffraction, optical microscopy and transmission electron microscopy. The results reveal that

Nb-containing Ti-Zr alloys transformed from single α phase → α + α" + β phase → single β 
phase → double β phases with increasing Nb content. In the case of β-type alloys, the addition of 
Nb improves the bonding energy between atoms, reduces the grain size, increases the elastic 
modulus, improves the yield strength and renders superior work-hardening behavior. Moreover, the 
current study provides mechanistic insights into microstructural evolution and strengthening of 
Nb-containing Ti-Zr alloys with increasing Nb content. Herein, the addition of 5 at.% Nb resulted in 
an abnormal work hardening during compression deformation under the synergistic influence of 
stress-induced martensite transformation of β phase and stress-induced twinning of α phase. 
Moreover, the biomechanical properties are evaluated to demonstrate the potential of Nb-containing 
Ti-Zr alloys in biological applications. 
 
Keywords: Ti-Zr based alloy; Biomaterials; Microstructural evolution; Mechanical properties 
1. Introduction 
Titanium–zirconium (Ti-Zr) binary alloys are promising candidates for biomedical implants 
due to their excellent biomechanical properties, favorable biocompatibility and corrosion resistance 
in body-environment [1, 2]. Compared with other traditional metallic biomaterials, such as Co–Cr 
alloys and stainless steels, Ti-Zr alloys render higher strength-to-weight ratio, low elastic modulus 
and superior corrosion resistance [3]. Moreover, the bone-like apatite layer is formed on the surface 
of Zr-containing alloys, which improves the bioactivity of implants [4]. Despite several similarities 
between the chemical properties of Ti and Zr, the corrosion resistance of both metals in chlorine 
(Cl)-containing environments, such as body fluids, is significantly different. For instance, the 
passive film on Zr-based alloys exhibits lower stability in Cl-containing environment than the 
Ti-based alloys [5-7]. It has been reported that the stability of the passive films on binary Zr-Ti

alloys improves with higher Ti content [6, 8]. Moreover, in-vivo tests in the rabbit tibia indicate that 
Ti and Zr behave similarly to bone [9]. Therefore, Zr alloys, with higher Ti content, are considered 
excellent implant materials. 
However, the large-scale utilization of as-cast Ti-Zr binary alloys is limited due to high prior β 
grain size and poor room-temperature mechanical properties [10]. The demand for biological 
implants has surged due to the higher proportion of senior citizens in different countries [11]. It is 
worth emphasizing that the number of revisions inevitably increase if biologically and mechanically 
compatible materials are not used for implantation [12]. Therefore, it is necessary to improve the 
biomechanical properties of as-cast Ti-Zr alloys. 
The Ti-50Zr (at.%) alloy render optimal mechanical properties in binary Ti-Zr alloys, which are 
2.5 times higher than pure Ti and Zr metals [13]. Also Ti-50Zr (at.%) alloy contains a high 
concentration of Ti element, which is desirable for pitting corrosion resistance in Cl-containing 
environment. Therefore, Ti-50Zr (at.%) alloy was selected as the studied matrix in this work. 
Addition of different elements, such as Y [10], O [14] and Nb [15], is considered as an effective 
strategy to improve the mechanical properties of alloys . The biocompatibility of pure metals can be 
ranked as: Nb > Ta > Ti > Zr > Al > Mo [16]. It has been reported that the addition of Nb can 
significantly improve the microstructure and mechanical properties of alloys [15, 17]. Furthermore, 
the addition of Nb is beneficial to osteogenesis in animal implantation experiments [18], and cell 
adhesion, proliferation and differentiation in-vitro studies [19]. However, the effect of Nb on the 
microstructural evolution and mechanical properties of the Ti-50Zr (at.%) alloy has not been 
studied. 
Herein, we aimed to investigate the influence of Nb addition on phase composition, 
microstructural evolution and mechanical properties of Ti-50Zr (at.%) alloys. Moreover, the

studied. The current work is beneficial in expanding the application range and service time of Ti-Zr

2. Experimental procedure 
Pure Ti (≥99.99 wt. %), Zr (≥99.7 wt. %) and Nb (≥99.99 wt. %) wires were used to prepare 
(100-x)/2Ti-(100-x)/2Zr-xNb (x = 0, 5, 10, 20, and 33.33 at. %) ingots. The components, names and 
calculated values of Bo̅̅̅̅ and Md̅̅̅̅ of the studied alloys are presented in Table 1. Briefly, the ingots 
were re-melted eight times to reduce the compositional heterogeneity via a non-consumable 
electrode arc furnace on a water-cooled copper crucible in a protective argon atmosphere. X-ray 
diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku D/max-2500PC), equipped with Cu Kα radiations, was employed to 
identify the crystal structure of the studied alloys, which were polished and cleaned ultrasonically 
prior to XRD measurements. The specimens for optical microscopy observation (OM, Zeiss 
Axiovert 200 MAT) were mechanically polished by a standard metallographic procedure and 
chemically etched in Kroll's reagent with distilled water, nitric acid and hydrofluoric acid (85:10:5 
by volume). The detailed microstructure observations were carried out by using a transmission 
electron microscope (TEM, FEI Talos F200X). TEM samples were prepared via twin-jet 
electrochemical polishing in an etching solution, consisting of perchloric acid and methanol (10:90 
by volume), at 243 K and 13 V . It should be noted that the hard body tissues are mainly affected by 
compression stress in the body, therefore, the compressive mechanical properties are mainly studied 
in the current work [4]. The cylindrical specimens, with a diameter of 5 mm and a height of 10 mm,

temperature at the strain rate of 1 × 10−4 s−1. At least three independent specimens were tested to

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Phases and microstructure 
Fig. 1(a) presents XRD patterns of the studied alloys with different contents of Nb, indicating 
that the addition of Nb altered the phase composition of Ti-Zr alloy. The TZ0N alloy consists of 
only α phase with close-packed hexagonal (HCP) structure, whereas TZ5N alloy consists of α phase, 
α" phase, with orthorhombic structure, and β phase with body-centered cubic (BCC) structure, 
which can be ascribed to the beta stability of Nb. The α' martensitic transformation of Ti-based 
alloys is a BCC–HCP type transformation, which can be divided into two processes: (1) shuffling of 
parallel adjacent {110}β planes in the <110>β direction and (2) shearing on {112}β planes in <111>β 
direction. The crystal structure of α" martensite is intermediate between the BCC and HCP structure 
[20]. The α" orthorhombic structure, induced by rapid cooling, was attained from a distorted 
hexagonal unit cell, where the c-axis of orthorhombic unit cell is related to the c-axis of hexagonal 
unit cell, and both a and b correspond to the orthogonal axis of the hexagonal unit cell [21]. 
Moreover, α and α" phases have not been detected in TZ10N, TZ20N and TZN alloys, with higher 
Nb content, and only β phase was observed. Furthermore, the diffraction peaks of single β-phase 
alloy gradually shifted to higher angles with increasing Nb content, which can be ascribed to the 
decrease in lattice parameters due to the smaller atomic radius of Nb (0.146 nm) than Zr (0.160 nm). 
Moreover, as shown in Fig. 1(b), the (211) peak of TZ10N alloy is different from TZ20N and TZN 
alloys. The (211) peak of TZ20N and TZN alloys consists of two diffraction peaks with different 
angles, indicating the presence of two different BCC structures in TZ20N and TZN alloys, i.e., β'

3.2 Mechanical characterization 
Fig. 5(a) presents room-temperature engineering compression stress-strain curves of 
Nb-containing Ti-Zr alloys. The elastic deformation stage and compressive yield strength are shown 
in Fig. 5(b). One should note that the phase composition and microstructure significantly influenced 
the mechanical properties of Ti-Zr alloys. For instance, the elastic deformation stage of TZ5N alloy 
is different from other alloys, where the stress-strain curve exhibits a slowly increasing stress area 
(called stress plateau [31]). The stress (156 MPa) required to induce such a plateau is referred as 
“triggering stress” and defined as the intersection of the elastic modulus tangent and tangential

elongation of the slope of the slowly increasing stress region [32]. Similar stress plateau on 
stress-strain curves have been reported in Ti–10V–2Fe–3Al alloy [32] and Ti-24Nb-4Zr-8Sn alloy 
[33], which is due to formation of stress-induced α" martensite during the elastic deformation stage. 
Moreover, TZ0N and TZ5N alloys fractured after a large plastic deformation stage, whereas TZ10N, 
TZ20N and TZN alloys did not fracture until the limit of equipment testing. Hence, the plasticity of 
the alloy is significantly improved due to the complete retention of β phase. Moreover, the 
compressive yield strength of β-alloys increases with the increase of Nb content. One factor for the 
strengthening is the size change of the β grains and subgrains. The influence of grain refinement on 
alloy strength (σ) can be given by the Hall-Petch relation [34, 35]:

overcome the frictional resistance, which is proportional to the volume swept by the interface [35]. 
Hence, a decrease in grain size increases the internal frictional resistance and yield strength. 
Moreover, the grain refinement leads to high grain boundary density which increases the number of 
dislocation accumulations. One should note that solid-solution strengthening is caused by the elastic

under the strain between 3 and 7.5%. One should note that a similar phenomenon has not been 
observed in other alloys. To further investigate the mechanism of a drastic increase in work 
hardening, TZ5N alloy was compressively deformed by 6% and TEM was utilized to observe the 
deformed morphology (Fig. 7). Based on the previous reports, the elastic moduli of different phases 
have shown the given order: Eα" < Eβ < Eα < Eω [37, 38]. Therefore, α" phase is preferentially 
deformed during the deformation process and, then, the stress-induced α" martensite transformation 
occurred in the β phase after the stress accumulated to a threshold limit. Finally, the α phase 
deformed with a further increase in the stress. The morphology of stress-induced α" phase is 
distinctly different from the quenched α" phase. Moreover, the orientation relationship between 
stress-induced α" phase and β phase is found to be [111]β // [001]α" (SAED result). Fig. 7 shows that 
the content of α" phase has significantly increased in the β matrix after 6% compressive 
deformation. Thus, the β phase transformed into α" phase under the effect of stress, which is one 
factor that resulted in an abnormal work hardening rate of TZ5N alloy. Moreover, a large number of

and indicates that α phase is deformed in the form of stress-induced twinning. Therefore, the 
abnormal work hardening rate of TZ5N alloy is caused by the synergistic influence of 
stress-induced β → α" martensite transformation and α phase twinning. Moreover, the strain 
hardening behavior became more obvious with increasing Nb content. As mentioned earlier, the 
stability of β phase increase with the increase of Nb content. However, the β-type alloys are known 
to be deformed by either slip or twin mechanisms, depending on β-phase stability [39]. The plastic 
deformation mode changes from the twin to slip mechanism as the stability of β-phase increases 
[39]. Therefore, different deformation methods lead to inconsistent work-hardening behavior. 
Overall, the more stable β phase renders the more pronounced work hardening behavior in β-type 
alloys. 
3.3 Biomechanical performance evaluation 
The compressive elastic modulus and yield strength were measured to assess the biomechanical 
properties of Nb-containing Ti-Zr alloys, as shown in Fig. 8. The yield strength and plasticity of the 
studied alloys have been analyzed in the previous section. Herein, we have observed that the elastic 
moduli initially decreased with increasing Nb content, followed by a gradual increase. The lower 
elastic modulus of TZ5N alloy can be ascribed to the presence of α" and β phase, which possess low 
elastic moduli. Moreover, the elastic modulus of TZ10N alloy is further decreased due to the 
disappearance of α phase and complete retention of only β phase. In general, the higher stability of 
β phase leads to higher elastic modulus in β-type alloys. Moreover, in the case of BCC structure, the 
variation trend of the bonding force similar to Young’s modulus [26]. As mentioned earlier, the 
higher Nb content leads to higher bonding energy between atoms. Hence, the higher Nb content 
enhanced the elastic moduli of β-type alloys. However, a stress shield can be formed by implanting

4. Conclusions 
The influence of Nb addition on microstructural evolution and mechanical properties of Ti-Zr 
alloys has been systematically investigated. The mechanistic insights into phase and microstructural 
changes provided to understand the mechanical behavior of Nb-containing Ti-Zr alloys. The 
following conclusions can be drawn from the current results: 
(1) The single α phase → α + α" + β phase → single β phase → double β phases 
transformation occurred in Ti-50Zr (at.%) alloy facilitated with the addition of Nb. 
(2) In the case of β-types alloys, Nb addition can improve the bonding energy between atoms, 
reduce grain size, enhance elastic modulus, increase yield strength and improve work hardening 
behavior of the alloys. 
(3) It has been demonstrated that the studied β-types alloys were strengthened due to grain 
refinement, solid-solution and precipitation strengthening under the influence of Nb addition. 
(4) The abnormal work hardening rate of TZ5N alloy was caused under the synergistic influence of 
stress-induced martensitic transformation of β phase and stress-induced α phase twinning." 
(5) Herein, TZ10N has the lowest compressive elastic modulus, and TZN alloy possess the highest 
comprehensive biomechanical properties. Hence, TZ10N and TZN alloys are promising 
materials for biomedical applications.
