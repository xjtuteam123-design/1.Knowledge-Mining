Abstract
A phase-ﬁeld model has been developed to simulate the evolution of both ( c + c0) microstructure and inelastic strain between c0
phases (i.e. c channel) during high-temperature creep in nickel-based superalloys. Inelastic strain is deﬁned as the sum of time-indepen-
dent and time-dependent components. Previously reported mechanical properties of single-phase c alloys are considered in the calcula-
tion of inelastic strain evolution. A two-dimensional phase-ﬁeld simulation is performed, and the results of microstructure evolution and
the creep rate vs. time curve are ﬁtted to the experimental data of the high-temperature creep of CMSX-4. The slope of the creep rate vs.
time curve during the initial stage of transient creep, the plasticity preference in diﬀerent types of c channels, and the rafting phenomenon
are reproduced well by the simulation. Furthermore, it is demonstrated that the creep rate increases locally at c/c
0 interfaces when the
rafted structure is formed.

1. Introduction
Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys exhibit superior
creep strength at high temperatures and are used as gas tur-
bine materials. The excellent mechanical properties of these
alloys are attributed to the c
0 strengthening phase (L1 2
structure) that precipitates coherently in the c phase with
a face-centered cubic lattice. During heat treatment for pre-
cipitation without external stress, the cuboidal c0 phase is
arranged along the h100 i crystallographic directions in
the c matrix. On the other hand, directional coarsening
of the c0 phase occurs in high-temperature creep under
[0 0 1] tensile stress; the c0 phase coarsens in a direction
normal to the external stress axis, resulting in the forma-
tion of the so-called rafted structure [1], in which the c
0
phase becomes lamellar. The rafted structure collapses
under long-term creep at high temperatures. It has been
reported that either the lamellar thickness of the rafted
structure increases [2], or the lamellar structure develops
a wavelike shape [3–5]. The creep rupture life of superalloys
strongly depends on the morphological stability of the
(c + c
0) two-phase microstructure. Miura et al. conﬁrmed
that the creep rate in the accelerating creep stage of
CMSX-4 is correlated with the increase in c-channel width
[2]. Hence, quantitative prediction of microstructure evolu-
tion during creep is an important subject in the develop-
ment of superalloy-based materials.

The initial stage of creep deformation, arising from plas-
tic deformation of the c channels, strongly aﬀects the raf-
ting phenomenon. It has been demonstrated by ﬁnite
element analysis that the rafting phenomenon needs to be
treated in the elastoplastic regime and that creep ﬂow in
the c matrix should be considered [6]. Simulation studies
using the phase-ﬁeld method also indicate that the c-chan-
nel plasticity results in a large driving force for rafting [7–
10], and aﬀects the morphological stability of the rafted
structure [11,12]. These phase-ﬁeld simulations simulta-
neously model both ( c + c
0) coherent microstructure evolu-
tion and plastic deformation in the c phase. In addition,
some phase-ﬁeld simulations have revealed the role of elas-
tic inhomogeneity (the elastic modulus mismatch between
the precipitate and matrix phases) in microstructure evolu-
tion [8,9,12–14]. This inhomogeneity aﬀects the particle-
size distribution, the coarsening rate of the c
0 phase during
precipitation aging [12,14], and the rafting kinetics during
creep [8,9]. Although these parametric studies, using the
phase-ﬁeld method, have led to fundamental understand-
ing of the eﬀect of material parameters on morphological
evolution, a quantitative simulation of microstructure evo-
lution correlated with creep strength is still desired.
For the quantitative simulation, high-temperature creep
properties of Ni–20 mass% Cr single crystals, a model alloy
for the c matrix of nickel-based superalloys, are available
[15–17]. This alloy shows (i) negligible decrease in creep
rate during the transient stage and (ii) a ﬁve-power-law
relationship at a strain of 0.01 under low stresses [16,17].
On the other hand, in an elastoplastic phase-ﬁeld model
[18–20], the evolution of plastic strain ﬁelds is described
by an equation similar to that of classical ﬂow theory. This
model can incorporate experimental mechanical properties
in the phase-ﬁeld method, and can serve as a basis for the
quantitative simulation of rafting phenomenon during
high-temperature creep.
The aim of this study is to develop a model that simulta-
neously treats the evolution of both ( c + c
0) microstructure
and inelastic strain in the c phase. Inelastic strain in the c
matrix is deﬁned as the sum of time-independent and time-
dependent components. The evolution of each strain is
described on the basis of the classical ﬂow and creep theories
that incorporate the previously reported mechanical proper-
ties of c-phase alloys. The results of this simulation are ﬁtted
to the experimental data of high-temperature creep of
CMSX-4 as a practical alloy. The validity of the developed
model is also discussed. In particular, we focus on the repro-
ducibility of a creep rate vs. time curve and the correspond-
ing microstructure evolution of the practical alloy.

stress state in the c phase would be changed, and the
increase in the creep rate could be reproduced. The creep
rate in the accelerating creep stage is closely related to
the increase in the c/c

In the phase-ﬁeld model of dislocations, the sheared
region enclosed by a dislocation loop is described as a plate
with thickness equal to the interplanar spacing [38]. The
eigenstrain of the plate is given by e

2d, where d is the interplanar spacing, n is the unit vector
of the slip plane, and b is Burgers vector. In a simulation
study on Ni–Al single crystal [39], this description has been
extended by introducing a new set of local dislocation den-
sity ﬁelds for characterizing the c-channel plastic deforma-
tion on the micrometer scale. It is expected that this model
will lead to the quantitative reproduction of creep deforma-
tion by rigorous treatment of the mobility of plastic strain
ﬁelds [39]. The macroscopic strain evolution model is
adopted to describe the c-channel plasticity in this study.
The advantage of our model is that the macroscopic exper-
imental data on c-phase mechanical properties can be
incorporated in strain evolution equations. In this study,
by referring to the tensile and creep properties previously
reported for single-crystal c alloys and ﬁtting the simula-
tion results to the experimental data, the creep rate vs. time
curve is successfully reproduced in the initial stage of the
transition creep in CMSX-4.
A limitation of this model is caused by Eq. (20), which is
valid only for strain levels up to 0.01. Furthermore, for
improved quantitative simulations, a rigorous model that
incorporates the anisotropic mechanical properties of sin-
gle-crystal c alloys [15–17] may be necessary. Our model
lacks anisotropic calculations of the inelastic strain evolu-
tion, as shown in Eqs. (16) and (20) . In future studies, 3-
D analysis may also be necessary. For the same volume
fraction of the c
0 phase, the c-channel widths in 2-D and
3-D analyses diﬀer from each other. This diﬀerence seems
to cause the discrepancy in the diﬀusion mobility M,
namely rafting kinetics. It is expected that a large-scale sim-
ulation using our model and having the physical parame-
ters of multicomponent practical alloys as its input would
reproduce the creep rate vs. time curve of nickel-based
superalloys at high temperatures under low-stress
conditions.
5. Conclusions
A phase-ﬁeld model is developed by considering the evo-
lution of both ( c + c
0) microstructure and inelastic strain in
the c phase during creep in nickel-based superalloys.
Inelastic strain is deﬁned as the sum of two components:
time-independent inelastic strain and time-dependent
inelastic strain. To describe the inelastic strain evolution,
the classical ﬂow and creep theories are adopted in which
experimentally reported mechanical properties of c-phase
alloys are incorporated.
The 2-D phase-ﬁeld simulation reproduces the disloca-
tion channel preference immediately after loading the
external tensile stress; the inelastic strain is concentrated
in the c channels normal to the loading direction. By ﬁtting
the creep response from the simulation to the experimental
data, the creep rate vs. time curve of CMSX-4 is success-
fully reproduced; the slopes of the experimental and simu-
lated curves during the initial stage of transition creep are
highly consistent with each other. The rafting phenomenon
during creep is also reproduced. At rafting completion
time, an increase in the creep rate is observed in the
simulation, whereas it is obscured in CMSX-4. It is
