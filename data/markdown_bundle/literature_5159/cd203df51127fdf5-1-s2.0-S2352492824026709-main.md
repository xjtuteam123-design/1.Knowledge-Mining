ABSTRACT
The insufficient high-temperature properties of Al-Cu alloys represent a significant obstacle to their further 
development. The use of master alloy refiners can enhance the mechanical properties while refining the grains. 
This paper describes the preparation and characterization of Al-Ti-C (CNTs) master alloys containing submicron 
TiC particles through the aluminum melt reaction method, utilizing graphite powders (GPs) and (CNTs) as 
carbon sources,respectively.The effects of these two types of intermediate alloys on the microstructures and high- 
temperature mechanical properties of the ZL205A alloy were investigated at varying additive amounts. The 
results indicate that the Al-Ti-C (CNTs) master alloys contain TiC particles measuring up to 183 nm and 480 nm, 
respectively, with the Al-Ti-CNTs demonstrating a superior effect on the refinement of the ZL205A alloy. The 
optimal mechanical properties were achieved upon the addition of 0.3 wt% of the master alloy to the ZL205A 
alloy. The mechanical properties improved from 341.53 MPa (yield strength), 371.68 MPa (tensile strength), and 
3.3 % elongation without the addition of master alloys to 413.20 MPa, 471.82 MPa, and 11.2 % with the addition 
of Al-Ti-CNTs and to 440.85 MPa, 492.61 MPa, and 10.8 % with the addition of Al-Ti-C, respectively.At a high

1. introduction
Casting aluminum alloy offers high-performance advantages and 
economic benefits and is widely used in industrial production [1 – 3] . 
Among these, the Al-Cu alloy represented by the ZL205A alloy, as a 
heat-resistant aluminum alloy, is widely used in engine blocks and cyl -
inder heads, etc [4 – 8] . However, the room-temperature and 
high-temperature performance of the ZL205A alloy is insufficient to 
meet the future demands of the automobile and aviation industries [9] .
In recent years, the enhancement of the strength and plasticity of 
aluminum alloys by adding master alloys has received increasing 
attention [10] . Al-Ti-C master alloys rich in TiC particles have shown to 
be effective grain refiners for pure aluminum [11,12] and have a sig -
nificant refining effect on Al-Cu alloys [10] . TiC particles are 
cubic-oriented crystals that have the advantages of high hardness and 
high thermal stability [13] . By generating the particles in situ and 
introducing them into Al-Cu alloys through intergrowth, the TiC parti -
cles obtain a close and clean interface with the matrix, which can greatly 
enhance the properties of the master alloy [14,15] . The refining effect of 
Al-Ti-C master alloys has been extensively studied.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure of master alloy under different carbon sources
As shown in Fig. 4 , the master alloy phases prepared by the same 
process are similar in terms of TiC and Al, but the morphology and size 
of the TiC particles vary significantly. In Fig. 4 (a), the large TiC particles 
are polyhedral and rounded, while the smaller particles are spherical 
and surround the larger ones. In Fig. 4 (b), the TiC particles prepared 
using CNTs as the carbon source are predominantly spherical, with a 
small number of polyhedral particles of smaller sizes diffusely 
distributed.
Fig. 4 (c) illustrates the distribution of TiC particle sizes in Al-Ti-C/ 
CNTs. 56.3 % of the TiC particles in the master alloy prepared with 
graphite as the carbon source are in the range of 0.4 – 0.6 μ m, whereas 
80 % of the TiC particles in the master alloy prepared with carbon 
nanotubes as the carbon source are ≤ 0.3 μ m. The average grain sizes of 
TiC particles in the master alloys prepared with graphite and carbon 
nanotubes as carbon sources are 183 nm and 480 nm, respectively.
CNTs and GPs are two different carbon sources in terms of size and 
morphology. Compared to graphite particles (GPs) as a carbon source, 
carbon nanotubes (CNTs) provide a larger contact surface with the 
aluminum melt due to their unique elongated structure. This increase in 
contact surface area enhances the reaction between the carbon source 
and the aluminum melt. The larger contact area results in higher reac -
tion efficiency and better carbon utilization. In addition, CNTs have a 
more curved surface with a large number of defect vacancies [21] . This 
will significantly improve the insufficient wettability of the aluminum 
melt with the carbon source [22] . It will naturally accelerate the process 
of melt reaction. During the reaction process where the graphite parti -
cles are the carbon source, the TiC particles and free C generated in the 
melt will agglomerate and accumulate on the GPs. This can hinder the 
generation of TiC particles from the internal GPs particles and also 
reduce the utilization of the carbon source. In contrast, the elongated 
CNTs provide sufficient sites for the TiC atoms to ensure the reaction. 
This undoubtedly increases the number of TiC particles [16] .
3.2. Effect of Al-Ti-C/CNTs on the microstructure of ZL205A alloy
The microstructure of the ZL205A alloy with various master alloy 
additions is illustrated in Fig. 5 . In the 0# alloy, as analyzed by EDS, the 
continuous phase observed is the θ phase (Al ₂ Cu), which is distributed in 
chains of flakes. With the addition of the master alloy, the θ phase be -
comes discontinuous and finer, exhibiting a distribution of small clumps 
or broken bars. At additions up to 0.3 %, the θ phase fragments into 
spheroidal and polygonal shapes, showing a substantial reduction in 
size. As additions are further increased, the θ phases begin to connect 
with each other and coarsen. In contrast, master alloys prepared using 
CNTs as the carbon source are more effective at θ phase fragmentation 
[23] .
The grain sizes of the ZL205A alloy with various master alloy addi -
tions are shown in Fig. 6 . The ZL205A alloy without added master alloy 
consists of equiaxed crystals with an average diameter of 152.34 μ m.

It has been demonstrated that TiC particles can serve as heteroge -
neous nucleation substrates [24 – 26] . However, the morphological 
structure and number distribution are critical factors affecting their 
refinement capability. According to non-homogeneous nucleation the -
ory [27 – 29] , the crystal embryo depends on the surface nucleation of 
solid-phase plasmas within the melt; varying surface states will result in 
different wetting angles between the crystal nucleus and the surface of 
the nucleated plasma. Theoretically, three curvature models of TiC 
particles should exist: nearly polyhedral, nearly spherical, and spherical. 
If an α -Al crystal embryo with the same radius of curvature and contact 
angle is formed on TiC particles with different curvature surfaces, as 
shown in Fig. 7 , the volume size of the α -Al crystal embryo follows: V 
near-spherical < V polygonal < V spherical. Therefore, for individual 
TiC particles with varying morphologies, the formation of α -Al crystal 
embryos on the concave surfaces of spherical TiC particles results in the 
smallest volume. This facilitates reaching the critical nucleation radius, 
which is most favorable for nucleation. Although it is challenging to 
precisely distinguish between the morphologies of near-spherical and 
polygonal TiC particles in practice, theoretical analysis suggests that 
spherical shapes should exhibit the poorest nucleation effect.
However, in the actual nucleation process, particles are prone to 
aggregation, and TiC often exists as particle clusters that act as 
nucleation substrates for α -Al. TiC particle agglomeration occurs 
because clusters of TiC particles have a lower specific surface energy 
compared to individual particles, which enhances their tendency to 
agglomerate. Additionally, the surface of TiC clusters is more irregular 
than that of individual TiC particles. This irregularity is more conducive 
to nucleation. TiC agglomerates with such shapes are more effective as 
heterogeneous nucleation cores. Consequently, the master alloy TiC 
particles, prepared with CNTs as the carbon source, come into contact 
with each other, forming numerous intercalated intervals. This facili -
tates aggregation and growth and enhances refinement properties.
Additionally, it has been postulated that TiC particles may serve as 
direct nucleation sites, attracting and adsorbing Al atoms that subse -
quently grow on their surfaces [11,14,19,24,27] . Accordingly, the 
smaller the TiC particles, the more readily the critical nucleation radius 
condition can be satisfied. An increase in nucleation substrates will 
result in grain refinement.
The presence of large TiC particles during solidification does not 
facilitate nucleation site formation for the ZL205A alloy. Instead, these 
particles undergo morphological refinement. During solidification, the 
initial stage typically begins at the wall and progresses towards the 
center of the melt, ultimately reaching completion. The incorporation of 
substantial TiC particles impedes grain growth towards the melt center. 
Nuclei detached from the wall are deprived of growth space due to the 
obstruction by large particles, ultimately leading to grain refinement.
Based on previous studies [30,31] , the refining ability of TiC parti -
cles is limited, and excessive additions may not yield better refining 
results. This is due to the increasing tendency of TiC particles to 
agglomerate with the addition of intermediate alloys. This results in a 
significant reduction in nucleation sites and the formation of grain 
embryos, leading to poor macroscopic refinement.

4. Conclusion
(1) In this study, master alloys enriched with micrometer- and 
nanometer-sized TiC particles were prepared using an aluminum melt 
reaction method. The average sizes of the TiC particles were 183 nm and 
480 nm, respectively, with GPs and CNTs used as the carbon sources.
(2) Compared to Al-Ti-C, Al-Ti-CNTs exhibited a more effective 
refining impact on the ZL205A alloy. With the addition of Al-Ti-CNTs, 
the grain size initially decreased and then increased, reaching 
97.08 µ m and 82.23 µ m, respectively, with an addition amount of 0.3 wt 
%.
(3) The room temperature mechanical properties of the ZL205A alloy 
were improved through the incorporation of Al-Ti-C and Al-Ti-CNTs 
master alloys, with Al-Ti-CNTs demonstrating superior enhancement 
of these properties. The notable improvement in the tensile properties of 
the ZL205A alloy can be attributed to several factors, including fine 
grain strengthening, oxide strengthening, load transfer, pinning, and 
CET strengthening, all of which were observed with the addition of in -
termediate alloys.
(4) The high-temperature performance of the ZL205A alloy 
improved with the introduction of the master alloy, with tensile strength 
increasing from 144.35 MPa to 174.87 MPa and 195.15 MPa at 300
◦
C. 
Fine-grain reinforcement was ineffective at high temperatures, and the 
coarsening of the θ ′ phase weakened the Orowan reinforcement mech -
anism. Instead, load transfer by TiC particles, the pinning effect, and 
CET reinforcement became the primary strengthening mechanisms.
