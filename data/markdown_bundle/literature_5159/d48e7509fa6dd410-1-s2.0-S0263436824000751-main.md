ABSTRACT 
In this study, a tungsten heavy alloy with a composition of 90 W-4.9Ni-2.1Fe-3.0Cu was processed using powder 
metallurgy. Pure elements were ball-milled to develop a mechanically alloyed nano-grained solid solution to 
reduce the required sintering temperature. The alloy was then liquid-phase sintered in a hydrogenated argon 
atmosphere at 1250 
◦
C. Microstructural characterization revealed that the high-energy ball milling process 
successfully developed a tungsten solid solution with a reduced tungsten grain size to a level of 15 nm, facili -
tating effective low-temperature (i.e., 1250 
◦
C) liquid-phase sintering with 88% relative density. The tungsten 
grains grew mostly in a polygonal shape, coalesced to a large size, and had strong interfacial integrity with the 
nickel ‑ iron ‑ copper matrix. Mechanical properties characterization revealed a significantly high microhardness 
level of 380 HV, compressive yield strength of 910 MPa, and ultimate compressive strength of 1326 MPa. The 
applied compressive stress induced intergranular crack propagation leading to the fracture of the sample with a 
failure strain of 25.6%. 
1. Introduction 
The nuclear industry, with its complex and sensitive operations, re -
lies on a multitude of advanced materials to ensure safety, efficiency, 
and precision. Among these materials, tungsten heavy alloys have 
emerged as crucial components in various aspects of nuclear technology. 
Tungsten ‑ nickel ‑ iron is the one of the two groups of classical heavy alloy 
most widely experimented [ 1 ] for nuclear industry application. Tung -
sten heavy alloy are two phase metal matrix composites, where quasi- 
spherical body centered cubic (BCC) crystal structured brittle tungsten 
particles are embedded in a nickel based tougher face centered cubic 
(FCC) crystal structured matrix [ 2 – 5 ]. The presence of the face-centered 
matrix enhances the fracture toughness of tungsten by enhancing its 
ductility using ‘ductile phase toughening ’ mechanism, which involves 
deliberate incorporation of a ductile phase into a hard and brittle ma -
terial. With their exceptional properties-including high density 
(17 – 18.5 g.cm
 3
), superior radiation shielding (i.e., the denser the 
material, the higher the degree of radiation shielding), and excellent 
mechanical strength (780 – 1060 MPa of yield strength) and excellent 
corrosion resistance-the tungsten heavy alloy has higher potential for 
applications ranging from radiation protection and containment to 
instrumentation and research in the nuclear sector [ 6 – 8 ]. Besides the 
nuclear application, the excellent characteristics made application of 
tungsten heavy alloy in aerospace and military industry as rocket igni -
tion tube, counterbalance weights, kinetic energy penetrator. The 
tungsten heavy metal has relatively higher melting temperature and 
processed by using powder metallurgy employing liquid phase sintering 
(LPS) [ 2 , 3 ]. In liquid phase sintering process the solid-state diffusion 
activated during the heating process, however, nickel rich low melting 
point liquid forms at the tungsten inter-particle boundaries and start to 
dissolve them in at the interface. Very high solubility of tungsten in 
nickel and iron (e.g., 45 wt% and 30 wt% at 1480

2. Experimental methodology 
2.1. Raw materials 
In this study, commercially pure tungsten (W) powder was mixed 
with nanopowders of nickel (Ni), copper (Cu) and iron (Fe). The purity 
and average particles size of tungsten, nickel, copper and iron were 99.9 
+ % and 8 – 10 μ m (from AEM Metal, China), 40 nm and 99.5%, 40 nm 
and 99.9% 40 nm and 99.9% (from Nanoamorphous USA), respectively. 
The weight percentage of the mixture was 90 W-4.9Ni-2.1Fe-3Cu. 
2.2. Powder processing of tungsten heavy alloy 
To create a nano-crystallite tungsten ‑ nickel ‑ iron ‑ copper heavy alloy 
powder, a total of 80 g of elemental powders with a weight percentage 
ratio of 90% W, 4.9% Ni, 2.1% Fe, and 3% Cu were ball-milled for 10 h 
in a tungsten carbide canister using an 8000D Dual Mixer/Mill machine. 
The powders sealed in the canister with two hard tungsten carbide balls, 
each with 11.2 mm diameter, without any process control agent (PCA). 
The ball-to-powder ratio was 1:4. The high-energy ball mill operated at a 
speed of 200 rpm in a cycle of 1-h milling followed by a 1-h pause. The 
resulting mechanically alloyed powders were cold-compacted into three 
cylindrical billets with a diameter of 10 mm and a height of 10 mm using 
a uniaxial hydraulic press under a pressure of 450 MPa for 2 min. The 
tungsten heavy alloy cold compacts were sintered at 1250 
◦
C for 120 
min in a hydrogen (5% volume percentage) mixed argon gas environ -
ment using an electrical resistance heating tube furnace (Carbolite Gero, 
UK). The sintered samples were furnace-cooled to room temperature.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Microstructural evolution during mechanical alloying 
The processing of the tungsten heavy alloy was successfully accom -
plished using relatively low temperature liquid phase sintering process. 
The tungsten powders physically mixed with the alloying elements 
powders followed by high-energy ball milling mechanical alloying 
process. The elemental tungsten particles supposed to experience (a) 
reduction in the particle size, (b) grain refinement and (c) formation of 
solid solution simultaneously [ 22 , 23 , 31 , 32 ]. 
The mechanism of tungsten particles size reduction during high- 
energy ball milling was a complex interplay of various processes. 
Within the milling canister, the tungsten powder in presence of alloying 
elements powders were subjected to high-energy impacts force and 
shear friction during the high-energy ball milling process [ 31 ]. The high 
energy impact force was generated when the grinding balls collide with 
the particles and resulted into cold welding, severe plastic deformation 
leading to fracturing and ultimately the reduction of larger particles into 
smaller ones (see Fig. 1 a). The tungsten powder particles simultaneously 
underwent through shear friction when the ball and/or canister wall and

the powders particles move against each other, contributing to the wear 
and breakdown of the particles. The continuous movement of the milling 
balls also subjects the particles to attrition, wherein repeated deforma -
tion and fracturing lead to a further decrease in size. Cold welding is 
another phenomenon where particles adhere together due to localized 
heating and pressure during the impacts. However, subsequent impacts 
can cause these welded regions to fracture, creating new surfaces and 
smaller particles. 
The refinement of tungsten grain size during high-energy ball milling 
[ 32 ] was regulated by different distinct phases (see Fig. 1 b). Initially, 
under the impact-induced high stress, severe plastic deformation 
generated significantly high dislocation density that lead to the forma -
tion of directionally oriented elongated grains in tungsten particles. The 
increasing high dislocation density in the deformed tungsten particles 
mutually interact among themselves and the distorted grain boundaries 
during further plastic deformation and resulted into alignment and 
formation of subgrains in the particles. The effect of severe plastic 
deformation becomes more evident as elongated tungsten grains sub -
divide to form relatively smaller tungsten grains. Further subdivision of 
the subgrains lead to the formation of equiaxed shaped low angle finer 
grains in the tungsten particles. Increase in the dislocation density per -
sisted with the incessant plastic deformation, the low-angle grain 
boundaries undergo a transition to large-angle boundaries resulting into 
the randomly oriented extremely fine single crystalline grains. 
Additionally, the mechanical alloying process of the tungsten parti -
cles with elemental copper, iron, and nickel metal particles resulted into 
formation of solid solutions where atoms from alloying metals diffused 
into the tungsten crystal lattices. The mechanism of solid solution for -
mation during mechanical alloying of pure elemental powders involves 
several interconnected processes [ 33 , 34 ]. Initially, the high-energy ball 
milling ensures the intimate mixing of the elemental powders at the 
nanoscale. The repeated impacts and collisions between the milling balls 
and powder particles lead to deformation, fracture, and subsequent cold 
welding, resulting in the formation of larger agglomerates. Localized 
heating at collision points enhances atomic diffusion, promoting the 
formation of solid solution phases. Concurrently, microstructural 
changes, including the generation of defects like dislocations and 
stacking faults, play a crucial role in facilitating atomic diffusion to form 
the solid solution. Solid-state diffusion becomes more pronounced as the 
milling process progresses, allowing atoms from the different alloying 
elements to migrate across grain boundaries and fractured fresh in -
terfaces [ 33 , 35 ]. The continuous refinement of crystallites contributes to 
increased reactivity and the homogenization of the alloying elements, 
ultimately establishing a dynamic equilibrium that leads to the gradual 
formation of a solid solution with a uniform distribution of alloying 
elements. 
The x-ray diffraction pattern (see Fig. 2 ) of the as-received elemental 
tungsten powder and mechanically alloyed tungsten heavy alloy showed 
that the tungsten maintained the body centered crystal structure with 
the (a) peak broadening, (b) peak shift toward higher angles, and (c) 
reduction in the peak intensity. 
The broadening of the diffraction peaks (see Fig. 2 b) typically asso -
ciated with reduction in grain size and as well lattice strain due to point 
defects like substitutional atoms [ 4 , 23 , 36 ]. The grain size measurement 
using the Scherrer equation [ 28 , 29 ] revealed that the ball-milling 
reduced the average grain size of tungsten particles from 33 nm of as- 
received condition to 15 nm. Disappearance of diffraction peaks for 
alloying elements was indicative to solid solution formation during the 
high-energy ball milling, which also reported by early researchers for 
tungsten heavy alloy [ 4 , 23 ]. Dissolution of relatively smaller substitu -
tional solute atoms (i.e., atomic radius for solute copper, iron and nickel 
are 0.128 nm, 0.124 nm and 0.125 nm respectively, while it is 0.137 nm 
for tungsten [ 27 ]) reduced the interplanar spacing (i.e., d-spacing) and 
introduces residual tensile stress in the tungsten particles and apparently 
led to the diffraction peak shift toward the higher angles. The relatively 
lower diffraction peak intensity also supportive to the reduction in 
tungsten grain size in addition to the possibility of increasing disorder -
ness in its crystallinity. 
3.2. Microstructural evolution during liquid phase sintering 
The mechanically alloyed fine flake of the tungsten particles grown 
to almost polygonal shape (see Fig. 3 ) due to the liquid phase sintering at 
1250 
◦
C in the hydrogenated argon environment. The sintering process 
in tungsten based heavy alloys progress in a sequence of different deli -
cate steps [ 10 , 11 , 37 ]. Process start with solid-state diffusion at elevated 
temperature and that is extremely slow due to slower atomic mobility in 
the solid. As the temperature rises further, an alloying elements rich 
liquid phase forms typically at the particle and/or grain boundaries 
triggering grain rearrangement facilitated by the presence of the liquid. 
This allows relatively efficient packing of particles and reduces voids in 
the green compact. In the presence of sufficient liquid phase, the smaller 
solid tungsten particles dissolve in the liquid, diffuse through and pre -
cipitate in the larger tungsten particles. In the final step, the tungsten 
grains, having undergone growth and rearrangement, bond together to 
form the solid skeletal structure of the sintered material. This step 
completes the densification process, resulting in a strong and compact 
structure. Nevertheless, homogeneous distribution of alloying elements 
was maintained in the liquid phase sintered tungsten heavy alloy (see 
Fig. 4 ). 
In the liquid phase sintering of tungsten ‑ nickel ‑ iron ‑ copper (W-Ni- 
Fe-Cu) alloys, at the end on initial solid-solid diffusion bonding among 
the tungsten particles (see ‘zone I' of Fig. 5 ) the sintering mechanism 
involved in induction of a thin-layered multi-element (i.e., nickel, iron, 
and copper) liquid phase along the tungsten-interparticle boundaries.

3.3. Mechanical properties 
Investigation of the mechanical properties of the low temperature 
liquid phase sintered tungsten has revealed that the microhardness, the 
indentation impression is shown in Fig. 7 , was at a level of 380 ± 8 HV 
(equivalent to 3.73 ± 0.08 GPa) and that is significantly higher than the 
average value (i.e., 298 HV) reported as a summary of 184 studies [ 10 ]. 
The significantly higher hardness could be attributed to the com -
pounding effect of relatively higher density and higher tungsten induced 
strengthening of the nickel ‑ iron ‑ copper matrix [ 20 ]. It has to be noted 
that the dissolution enthalpy for tungsten in nickel and iron is signifi -
cantly smaller than that of copper. The appreciably small error function 
in the measured macrohardness in indicative to the suitability of the 
used processing parameters in development of the apparent uniform 
microstructure. The hardness value obtained in this study is exception -
ally higher when compared to that of the alloy with the same compo -
sition sintered at the same temperature, and even marginally higher 
when the alloy was sintered at a higher temperature (i.e., 1350

The compressive stress-strain graph of the low temperature liquid 
phase sintered tungsten heavy alloy is shown in Fig. 8 . Result of the 
compressive test revealed that the alloy started yielding at 910 MPa 
followed by strong strain hardening effect that led to the ultimate 
compressive stress level of 1326 MPa at 18% plastic deformation. 
Dislocation storage capacity of metal typically influences the strength 
and the minimum stress requires to initiate dislocation slip determines 
the yield strength [ 27 ]. Yield in the tungsten heavy alloy initiated with 
slip in the relatively softer face centered cubic structured nick -
el ‑ iron ‑ copper matrix [ 42 ] compared to the relatively much stronger 
body centered cubic tungsten grains. The tungsten atom alloyed stronger 
nickel ‑ iron ‑ copper matrix, large number of significantly fine tungsten 
grains and associated large amount of tungsten-matrix interface gener -
ated higher impedance to the dislocation slip under the applied stress 
[ 6 , 43 ] resulting in to the high experimental yield strength. In general, 
the yield strength of a tungsten heavy alloy is dependent on the tungsten 
grain size, tungsten ‑ tungsten contacts and volume of alloy matrix 
[ 6 , 42 , 44 ]. The impeded slip in the alloy microstructure facilitate the 
dislocation built-up mostly at the tungsten-matrix interface and tung -
sten ‑ tungsten boundary. Dislocation from the alloy matrix at tungsten- 
matrix boundary effectively continued through the tungsten grains 
due to the presence of strong tungsten-matrix interfacial integrity. The 
tungsten-matrix interfacial integrity assessed by the absence of 
debonding and/or tungsten pullout. This slip impedance necessitates 
successively higher level of stress to continue the plastic deformation, 
which is strain hardening. Compressive strain hardening of the pro -
cessed alloy persists until 18% of plastic and that led the alloy to 
withstand a maximum stress level 1326 MPa followed by rapid decline 
to fracture with 25.6% failure strain. It has to be noted that the ultimate 
compressive stress achieved in this study was similar level when 
compared with that of alloys reported earlier [ 44 ]. Fracture surface of 
the alloy specimen (see Fig. 5 and Fig. 9 ) revealed that the failure mode 
was almost entirely intergranular in nature. The cracks propagation 
were through either tungsten ‑ tungsten (see Fig. 5 b and Fig. 9 b) or 
tungsten-matrix (see Fig. 5 c). Slip steps at tungsten ‑ tungsten boundaries 
(see Fig. 9 b, d) and parallel line of fracture in some sporadic matrix 
patches (see Fig. 9 c, e) indicative to strong dislocation buildups at the 
boundaries. The compressive stress-strain also revealed that the 
compressive toughness, the energy absorbed till fracture and measured 
by the total area under stress-strain graph [ 27 ], achieved by the pro -
cessed tungsten alloy was 25.5 KJ-cm
 3
.
