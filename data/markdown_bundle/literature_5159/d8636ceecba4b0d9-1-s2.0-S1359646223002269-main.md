Searching for materials with outstanding combinations of strength 
and ductility over a wide temperature range has been a permanent 
pursuit. In particular, the rapid development of the aerospace industry 
raises urgent needs for low-density alloys load-bearing at both room and 
elevated temperatures. Recently, as a category of high entropy alloy 
(HEA), the emergence of high entropy superalloys (HESA) has been 
attracting extensive attention for their great potential in high- 
temperature structural applications due to their excellent high- 
temperature strength, thermal stability and so on [1 – 3] . For example, 
the BCC-structured refractory HEAs NbMoTaW and VNbMoTaW were 
designed by Senkov et al. [ 4 , 5 ], exhibiting extremely high yield 
strengths of over 400 MPa at 1600 
◦

cubic (FCC)-based HESAs were also designed by referring to the com -
mercial Ni-based superalloys [6] , and a high strength at elevated tem -
peratures can be achieved due to the significant precipitation 
strengthening effect. 
Besides the above single-phase HEAs, dual-phase HEAs can also be 
good candidates as high-temperature materials. In dual-phase HEAs, the 
ultrastrong phase boundaries can provide an additional strengthening 
effect and benefits the comprehensive mechanical properties at both 
room and high temperatures [7] . For example, the Ni

Figure 3 (d) shows the deformed substructure of the B2 matrix, 
exhibiting a high density of dislocations. Although the B2 phase is hard 
and brittle at room temperature for the limited independent slip sys -
tems, its yield stress decreases rapidly with increasing temperature [39] 
and exhibits a brittle to ductile transition temperature at 550 – 700 K, 
owing to the thermally activated initiation of new slip systems or the 
onset of dislocation climb and cross-slip [ 40 , 41 ]. The abundant pre -
cipitates in the B2 matrix can provide an extra strengthening effect. On 
the one hand, as shown in Fig. 3 (d), dislocation forests were detected in 
both the interface and interior of the FCC precipitates, as marked by the 
yellow dashed lines, illustrating their strengthening capacity and 
deformability. The K-S interfaces between the B2 matrix and FCC pre -
cipitates can produce the practical strengthening effect caused by the 
mismatch of the shear modulus and lattice parameters [7] . Interestingly, 
there were also many deformation nanotwins inside the FCC precipitates 
( Fig. 3 (e)), as confirmed by the SAED pattern in Fig. 3 (f), which can 
further strengthen the FCC precipitates and B2 matrix. The formation of 
these deformation nanotwins can be attributed to the low SFE of the FCC 
precipitates with nanoscale size, as frequently observed in nanograins 
under serious stress concentration [42] . On the other hand, the disper -
sive σ phase shown in Fig. 1 (e) also acts as an effective barrier to 
dislocation motion in the B2 matrix. The beneficial effect of complex 
intermetallics on improving high-temperature strength has been widely 
reported in HEAs [43] . For example, the BCC-based AlCr
x
NbTiV alloy 
can exhibit excellent yield strength up to 800 
◦
C due to the existence of 
the Laves phase in the matrix [44] . 
The interfacial morphology was also identified, as shown in Fig. 3 (g- 
i). From Fig. 3 (g) and (h), high-density dislocations piled up at both sides 
of the phase boundary, indicating its strengthening effect. Meanwhile, it 
can be found that the phase boundaries become rugged and serrated, as 
marked by the white arrows and dashed line. As shown in Fig. 3 (i), the 
grain boundary also plays an important role in hindering the motion of 
dislocations. However, the grain boundary appeared straight and 
smooth ( Fig. 3 (i)), which is prone to cracking at elevated temperatures. 
According to the analyses of the microstructure and deformation at 
different scales, the high strength of HESA benefited from its hierar -
chical structure. The deformation of the HESA was mainly achieved by 
activating high-density dislocations in both the FCC and B2 matrix. 
Owing to the different crystalline structures, there is strong strain par -
titioning between the FCC and B2 matrix. To accommodate the strain 
gradients, geometrically necessary dislocations will block and pile up at 
the phase boundary [45] , generating long-range back stress and making 
dislocations hard to move in the FCC matrix. Therefore, as the FCC 
matrix was surrounded by the B2 phase, the global yield strength of the 
HESA was elevated at both room and high temperatures. In addition, the 
FCC matrix was strengthened by the existence of the high-density L1

Detailed analyses of the lateral fracture surface were conducted to 
uncover the underlying crack and failure mechanisms at 800 
◦
C. Figure 4 
(a) shows a BSE image of the fractured HESA. There were hardly any 
continuous large cracks observed, implying the considerable ductility of 
the alloy. By zooming in on the fracture end ( Fig. 4 (b)), it can be found 
that the microcracks originate from both the phase boundary and grain 
boundary of the FCC matrix, as marked by the blue and red arrows, 
respectively. The inserted graph in Fig. 4 (b) displays that the 
morphology of the deformed phase boundaries was rough and serrated, 
in accordance with Fig. 3 (g, h). Notably, the microcrack density at the 
grain boundary was only approximately one-third that at the phase 
boundary ( Fig. 4 (c)). 
The formation of more phase boundary cracks and fewer grain 
boundary cracks can be attributed to the existence of the B2 phase. First, 
as revealed by the EBSD kernel average misorientation (KAM) map 
( Fig. 4 (e)) at regions marked by a white dotted line in Fig. 4 (d), severe 
strain concentrations localized at the phase boundary but not the grain 
boundary, promoting the origin of cracks. Such high localized stress can 
be derived from the incompatible deformation between the FCC and B2 
matrix [46] and the high friction along the serrated phase boundary 
(SPB) [47] . Second, the B2 phase occupied most triple junctions of the 
FCC grains ( Fig. 1 (c)), which are usually crack imitation positions, 
thereby avoiding the grain boundary cracking. Third, the SPB can in -
crease the intergranular friction thus reducing the cracks caused by 
grain boundary sliding. Similar serrated grain boundaries were widely 
introduced in Ni-based superalloys to minimize grain boundary cracking 
at high temperatures [48] . 
Even though more cracks formed along the phase boundary, it can be 
observed that their bridging and propagation were blocked ( Fig. 4 (d)) 
because the zigzag array of the cavity formed at the SPB makes it more 
difficult to interlink and compose an intergranular path for crack 
propagation. Therefore, the heterogeneous structure with serrated phase 
boundaries plays a crucial role in enhancing the dislocation activities 
and retards the propagation of microcracks during deformation at high 
temperature, ensuring the excellent tensile ductility. 
In conclusion, we developed a hypoeutectic HESA with an 
outstanding strength-ductility combination at both room and high 
temperatures. The excellent high strength can be attributed to the 
reinforcing effect of the interface and the multiple precipitates in the 
dual-phase matrix. i.e., L1
2 
precipitates in the FCC matrix as well as the 
FCC and σ precipitates in the B2 matrix. The existence of the B2 phase 
with a serrated phase boundary suppresses the nucleation and propa -
gation of cracks, leading to high ductility at elevated temperatures. 
These findings not only unravel the strengthening and deformation 
mechanisms of dual-phase HESA at elevated temperatures, but also 
provide an effective alloy design pathway for developing advanced 
structural materials serving a wide range of temperatures.
