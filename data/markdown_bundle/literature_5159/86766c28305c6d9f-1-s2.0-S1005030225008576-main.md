a b s t r a c t 
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) or refractory complex concentrated alloys (RCCAs) represent a 
promising class of materials due to their high strength and unique heat-resistance properties. However, 
RHEAs or RCCAs often face the challenge that the key factors, including light-weight, ductile, and heat- 
resistant, are mutually exclusive in a single alloy. The present study aims to achieve an excellent combina- 
tion of lightweight, strength, and ductility by controlling the Mo element. Ti40 Nb30 V25−x Zr5 Mox ( x = 0, 3, 
and 5, and referred to as Mo0, Mo3, and Mo5) with low densities around 6.2–6.4 g cm−3 were designed. 
After the addition of Mo, the strength and ductility of Mo0 were simultaneously enhanced, where the 
optimized Mo5 alloy possessed a substantial strain-hardening rate of approximately 2 gigapascals and 
a ﬁnal fracture elongation exceeding 25 % at room temperature. Moreover, the tensile strength of Mo5 
can still exceed 500 MPa at 1073 K, showcasing the potential for broad-temperature-range applications. 
According to the experimental analyses and DFT calculations, multi-effects of Mo in the alloy system 
were revealed: ( ⅰ ) supplying suﬃcient solid solution strengthening by introducing large shear modulus 
mismatch and atomic strain ﬁeld; ( ⅱ ) enhancing strain-hardening capabilities by promoting dislocation 
substructures and facilitating cross-slip mechanism; ( ⅲ ) enhancing high-temperature strengths by rein- 
forcing atomic interactions and increasing covalent bonding composition. These results fully unleash the 
potential of the cocktail effect in HEAs rather than relying on overly complex material processing meth- 
ods, offering new insights into developing novel high-performance single-phase RHEAs or RCCAs.

1. Introduction 
For metallic materials, how to be simultaneously strong, ductile, 
and lightweight has always been the key issue, whether in practi- 
cal application or scientiﬁc interests [ 1 , 2 ]. When it comes to high- 
temperature environments, refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) 
or refractory complex concentrated alloys (RCCAs) have become 
one of the most prevalent research hotspots due to their unique 
heat-resistance properties and higher operating temperatures [ 3–
6 ]. However, the limited tensile ductility and overhigh densities of 
RHEAs are their ‘Achilles heel’. Although NbMoTaW can exhibit a 
yield strength of over 400 MPa at 1873 K, its density of 13.6 g

To overcome the brittleness and overweight problems of RHEAs, 
the main effort s include regulating ﬁne-grain microstructure [ 8 ] 
and designing novel alloy systems. Up to date, the low-density 
Ti-rich RCCAs (the atomic ratio of Ti is around 40 % or even

CAs, the enhanced ductility was mainly achieved by regulating 
the precipitation behavior of B2 phase [ 12 ], local chemical order 
(LCO) [ 13 ], and multicomponent intermetallic compound particles 
(MICPs) [ 14 ], etc. The precipitation can introduce inhomogeneity 
of slip resistance to promote various slip modes and more dislo-

and non-transformation-induced plasticity (non-TRIP) dislocation 
substructures are eﬃcient in improving the strain-hardening rate 
in alloy systems with high stacking-fault energies (SFEs), which can 
improve the inherently low strain-hardening rate of body-centered 
cubic (BCC) RCCAs. However, a signiﬁcant bottleneck of utilizing 
precipitation behavior is that it would undoubtedly complicate the 
fabrication processes. For example, to precisely tailor the distribu- 
tion and content of LCOs, the Ti50 Zr18 Nb15 V12 Al5 ingots were aged 
at 573 K for 168 h [ 13 ]. Also, the inﬂuence of those precipitations 
on high-temperature strength remains to be studied. It is natu- 
rally desirable to develop novel single-phase RCCAs with excellent 
strength-ductility synergy. 
Another bottleneck of current research about ductile RC- 
CAs is the insuﬃcient tensile high-temperature strength (al- 
though their high-temperature compressive strengths may be su- 
perior) [ 17 , 18 ]. Generally, the strength decreases with the de- 
crease of density, which is also called the trade-off between high- 
temperature strength and lightweight [ 19 ]. Unfortunately, this is 
also the case in RCCAs. Ti37 V15 Nb22 Hf23 W3 ( ∼8.4 g cm−3 ) [ 20 ] 
and Hf15 Nb40 Ta25 Ti15 Zr5 ( ∼10.6 g cm−3 ) [ 21 ] can exhibit a ten- 
sile strength over 500 MPa at 1073 K while Ti43.3 V28 Zr14 Nb14 Mo0.7 
( ∼5.8 g cm−3 ) [ 22 ] suffer from a severe softening higher than 
973 K. Considering that the service temperature of titanium al- 
loys is hard to exceed 873 K, high content of Ti in Ti-rich RC- 
CAs would lead to a severe high-temperature softening [ 23 ]. It 
is reported by Hu et al. [ 24 ] that high-temperature strength is 
related to the electronegativity difference ( /Delta1χ) of RCCAs. Com- 
pared with Ti ( χ = 1.54), Mo ( χ = 2.16), and W ( χ = 2.36) have 
higher χ values and thus can enhance the /Delta1χ, which can explain 
the superior high-temperature strength of Ti37 V15 Nb22 Hf23 W3 
and other Mo-rich RCCAs. According to the comparison between 
Ti37 V15 Nb22 Hf23 W3 and Ti43.3 V28 Zr14 Nb14 Mo0.7 , only when the 
content of Mo or W in Ti-rich RCCAs is high enough can the high- 
temperature strength be improved effectively. However, the exces- 
sive content of Mo or W would lead to a sharp increase in density 
and a decrease in ductility; thus, the content of those elements 
should be designed precisely in advance. 
Given that the main approach of the aforementioned success- 
ful cases was accomplished by dispersing the plastic strain, like 
introducing cross-slip behavior while accumulating dislocations at 
the same time, such that the alloy can maintain a stable strain- 
hardening rate and superior tensile elongation [ 12 , 13 ]. Two fun- 
damental questions were thereby raised in single-phase RCCA: (1) 
Can the strength-ductility synergy be similarly achieved by simply 
changing the composition and leading to a different slip mode? 
(2) Can the high-temperature strength be guaranteed while still 
exhibiting the strength-ductility synergy at RT? In view of these 
two main problems, our basic strategy for designing novel strong 
and ductile RCCAs is to regulate the inherent atomic strain, dis- 
location conﬁguration, and electronic structure by simply con- 
trolling the content of Mo in the Ti-Nb-V-Zr alloy system. Ac- 
cordingly, the newly designed Ti40 Nb30 V20 Zr5 Mo5 shows a stable 
strain-hardening rate of ∼2 gigapascals and a large tensile frac- 
ture elongation of over ∼25 % at RT. Moreover, the tensile strength 
of Ti40 Nb30 V20 Zr5 Mo5 , with a low density of ∼6.4 g cm−3 , can 
still exceed 500 MPa at 1073 K. The excellent combination of 
lightweight, strength, and ductility is expected to break through 
the limitation shackles and application bottlenecks of RCCAs. 
2. Materials and experimental procedures 
2.1. Materials fabrication 
The Ti40 Nb30 V25−x Zr5 Mox ( x = 0, 3, and 5 in atomic ratio) alloys 
were prepared by vacuum arc melting under a Ti-gettered argon- 
protective atmosphere. High-purity particles ( ≥ 99.95 wt. %) of Ti, 
Nb, V, Zr, and Mo were used. Each ingot ( ∼70 g) was remelted and 
ﬂipped 12 times in a water-cooled copper hearth. As-cast ingots 
were cold-rolled to a 70 % reduction in thickness, followed by a 
recrystallization annealing at 1223 K for 30 min and water quench- 
ing. To study the potential precipitation behavior, the recrystallized 
Ti40 Nb30 V25−x Zr5 Mox RCCAs were isothermally annealed at 1273 K 
for 1 h under an argon-protective atmosphere, followed by furnace 
cooling at a low cooling rate. 
2.2. Mechanical tests 
For quasi-static uniaxial tensile tests at RT, the dog-bone- 
shaped specimens with a gauge length of 15 mm and a cross- 
sectional size of 4 mm × 1.5 mm were cut by electro-discharge 
machining (EDM). Before tensile tests, all specimen surfaces were 
ground with SiC sandpapers up to 20 0 0#. The tensile tests were 
conducted by a universal testing machine (Instron 5582) at a nom- 
inal strain rate of 5.6 × 10−4 s−1 . An electronic extensometer with 
a gauge length of 10 mm was used to monitor the strain. As for 
high-temperature tensile tests, the specimens with a gauge size 
of 15 mm × 2 mm × 1.8 mm were prepared by EDM followed 
by the same grinding procedure. Thermocouples were used to 
measure the testing temperatures in the resistance furnace. High- 
temperature tensile tests were carried out on an AG-Xplus testing 
machine at a nominal strain rate of 1 × 10−3 s−1 . Three speci- 
mens were tested in each instance. Nanoindentation testing was 
conducted using a Nano Indenter (Agilent G200). For each sam- 
ple, the indentation array was selected to be 5 × 5 with a spac- 
ing of 5 μm between adjacent indents. The digital image correla- 
tion (DIC) technique was also used to track the strain distribution, 
where optical images were recorded every 1 s with a resolution 
of 3072 pixel × 2078 pixel. Black and white paints were uniformly 
speckled on the specimen surfaces. Corresponding images were an- 
alyzed by GOM software. 
2.3. Microstructural characterizations 
Metallographic samples were prepared by mechanical grinding 
and electropolishing using a solution of 60 vol. % carbinol, 35 vol. % 
N-butanol, and 5 vol. % perchloric acid at 45 V for 2 min. The mi- 
crostructure was observed by a Zeiss sigma-300 scanning electron 
microscope (SEM) equipped with a Super-X energy dispersive X- 
ray spectrometer (EDS). The phase constitution was identiﬁed us- 
ing an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Panalytical X’PERT) with Cu-K α
radiation, at a scanning speed of 4 ° min−1 in the range of 20 °–90 °
(2 θ). The phase distribution and grain sizes were investigated by 
electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) using a Hitachi SU50 0 0 SEM 
equipped with the EBSD detector operating at 20 kV. The scanning 
step was selected to be 1 μm, and all EBSD data were analyzed 
by the AZtec software. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI 
Talos F200 ×) operating at 200 kV was utilized. The TEM speci- 
mens were cut by EDM with a thickness of ∼300 μm and then 
mechanically ground to a thickness of ∼50 μm. Finally, the thin 
discs out of the ground sheet were ion-milled with a GATAN PIPS 
II MODEL 695.

using ThermoCalc© software to calculate the phase diagram of 
Ti40 Nb30 V25−x Zr5 Mox alloy system [ 25 ]. Moreover, the phase con- 
tents of selected RCCAs at equilibrium (equilibrium solidiﬁcation 
phase diagrams) were calculated.

3. Results 
3.1. Alloy design 
In the high-melting-point family Ti-Zr-Hf-V-Nb-Ta-Cr-Mo-W, Ti, 
Nb, V, and Zr are the most suitable elements to act as the prin- 
cipal elements for their low density and intrinsic ductility. How- 
ever, unstable phase decomposition could happen in Zr-rich REHAs 
so that the content of Zr was constrained to be a small value of 
5 % for the sake of the alloy’s single-phase stability [ 28 , 29 ]. Conse- 
quently, the target alloy was ﬁrst determined to be Ti40 Nb30 V25 Zr5 
with a density of ∼6.22 g cm−3 . As mentioned above, the room 
and high-temperature strength can be enhanced through the ad- 
dition of Mo with high χ [ 24 ]. To further study the effect of Mo 
alloying, the contents of other elements should be controlled to re- 
main as consistent as possible. Consequently, we chose to replace 
one of the compositional elements by Mo, rather than studying the 
pseudo-binary alloy system (Ti40 Nb30 V25 Zr5 )1 −x -Mox . Given that 
the Zr element is originally minimal in Ti40 Nb30 V25 Zr5 , and Ti and 
Nb are crucial for ensuring the low density and high-temperature 
strength, respectively, replacing these elements was undesirable 
[ 30 ]. While V is reported to reﬁne the grain size and can enhance 
the mechanical performance of RHEAs, the grain-reﬁnement effect 
is not the research focus in this study [ 31 ]. Therefore, V was se- 
lected as the element replaced by Mo. 
The phase diagram of Ti40 Nb30 V25−x Zr5 Mox alloy system 
( Fig. 1 (a)) was calculated to assign the suitable atomic proportion 
of Mo. As shown in Fig. 1 (a), a high value of x (higher than 5.8 
at. %) would weaken the phase stability, causing the decomposition 
of the BCC phase into other BCC, hexagonal close-packed (HCP), 
and Laves-type phases.

3.2. Microstructure characterization of Ti40 Nb30 V25−x Zr5 Mox RCCAs 
After the recrystallization treatment, only one set of BCC phase 
diffraction peaks was detected from the XRD patterns (Fig. S3) 
with no precipitation phase, which is attributable to the fast cool- 
ing rate during the fabrication process. Fig. 2 (a–c) depicts the mi- 
crostructures of three alloys, which consist of ﬁne and equiaxed 
grains. Moreover, each compositional element of Mo5 exhibits a 
totally homogeneous distribution according to the EDS mappings 
( Fig. 2 (c1–c5)). According to the EBSD inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF) 
maps ( Fig. 2 (d–f)), those equiaxed grains show almost random ori- 
entations. The phase maps ( Fig. 2 (g–i)) preliminarily prove that all 
alloys consist of a single BCC phase constitution, which agrees well 
with the XRD patterns. The mean grain sizes of Mo0, Mo3, and 
Mo5 were calculated to be 29.9, 22.5, and 16.6 μm, respectively. 
Thus, the Mo alloying has a slight effect on grain reﬁnement due 
to the enhanced solute-drag effect during boundary migration. 
Given that the resolution limit of EBSD is at the mesoscale, 
the nanoscale microstructures of Mo0 and Mo5 alloys were fur- 
ther examined by TEM ( Fig. 3 ). Generally, the secondary phase pre-

images and corresponding selected area electron (SEAD) patterns 
were examined near grain boundaries. As displayed in Fig. 3 (a, b), 
there is not any nano-precipitate structure being detected in the 
BF images. Besides, there would be diffuse streaks [ 33 ] or even two 
sets of diffraction patterns [ 34 ] in SAED results if the spinodal de- 
composition microstructure exists, while no such phenomenon is 
observed in Fig. 3 (a1, b1). The possibility of the B2 ordering phase 
is also excluded since no superlattice diffraction spots appear (see

( Fig. 5 (e)) of Mo5. As shown in Fig. 5 (e), the local strain value at 
the necking point is quite close to the average strain before the 
necking point. The local strain was almost uniformly distributed 
throughout the whole gage length ( Fig. 5 (f)) before the necking 
stage, indicating that no plastic instability exists before the necking 
stage. Subsequently, strain localization happened after the necking 
stage as the strain increment was limited only to a relative posi- 
tion of 10–15 mm. Moreover, necking occurs at an average strain 
of 16.11 % in Mo5, which is retarded in comparison with the value 
of 9.14 % in Mo0. Thus, it can be summarized that the Mo addi- 
tion can offer substantial strain-hardening ability to resist strain 
localization and increase plastic stability, leading to increased 
ductility. 
The different strain-hardening behaviors of Mo0 and Mo5 alloys 
are attributed to their different dislocation conﬁgurations. Thus, 
the deformed substructures of tensile-strained Mo0 and Mo5 al- 
loys were investigated using TEM characterization ( Fig. 6 ). In Mo0, 
the single slip behavior prevails in the plastic deformation, as evi- 
denced by numerous straight and parallel planar-slip traces on the 
{110} slip planes in a two-beam bright ﬁeld (BF) image ( Fig. 6 (a)). 
Insuﬃcient dislocation interactions in those restricted slip systems 
cannot disperse dislocations and subsequently localize strain distri- 
bution spatially, which accords well with the poor strain-hardening 
abilities and localized strain distribution in Mo0, leading to prema- 
ture necking and deformation instability observed in Fig. 5 (a). 
On the contrary, the well-developed dense dislocation walls 
(DDWs) are activated and terminated at the grain boundary in Mo5 
( Fig. 6 (b)). According to Taylor’s theory [ 37 ], the strain-hardening 
rate is dependent on the dislocation density, and the DDWs in Mo5 
represent its more advanced dislocation accumulation and prolifer- 
ation ability. At the edge of DDWs shown in Fig. 6 (b1), some dislo- 
cations are pinned by nodes. For HEAs, local chemical ﬂuctuation is 
one of the most important intrinsic features, which leads to inho- 
mogeneous slip resistance during the dislocation slip [ 38 ]. In this 
case, the prolonged atomic strain ﬁelds ( Fig. 3 ) introduced by Mo 
are likely to jam up dislocations and act as local obstacles, which 
can act as local pinning. As the external strains keep increasing, 
dislocation cross-slip would be activated to sustain dislocation slip. 
The wavy-dislocation morphology and loop-shaped dislocation de- 
bris ( Fig. 6 (b1)) between adjacent DDWs, referred to as Orowan 
loops, are typical signs of dislocation cross-slip [ 33 , 39 ]. Cross-slip 
can act as a supplementary slip mode in Mo5 rather than single 
slip alone in Mo0. The effects of cross-slip in Mo5 can be sum- 
marized in two aspects: ( ⅰ ) propagating dislocation and prevent- 
ing severe stress concentration and resultant premature failure; 
(ii) combining with local pinning effect, the dislocation junctions 
between different slip systems can form new Frank-Read sources, 
generating more mobile dislocation and giving rise to the strain- 
hardening ability [ 39 , 40 ]. The evolution of dislocation substructure 
can reﬂect and explain the difference in strain-hardening curves of 
Mo0 and Mo5. The strain-hardening curve of Mo5 (see Fig. 4 (c)) 
shows a distinct initial increase around 0.03, contrasting with the 
monotonic decline of Mo0. The initial increase in strain-hardening 
rate is attributed to the cooperative effects of the pinning effect 
and cross-slip, while it is lacking or insuﬃcient in Mo0 and Mo3 
[ 39 , 41 ]. 
Although Mo5 exhibits an initial increase in stain-hardening 
rate, it is challenging for traditional RCCAs to maintain a high- 
level stain-hardening ability. The deformation microstructures of 
Mo5 at different strains were also investigated. As shown in 
Fig. 6 (c), the spatial distribution of dislocations is heterogeneous, 
where the majority of dislocations are narrowly spaced and show 
dislocation tangles. Meanwhile, the dislocation debris is shown 
between adjacent dislocation-rich areas, indicating the extensive 
cross-slip behavior and dislocation interactions. As the deforma- 
tion further increases to 10 % ( Fig. 6 (d, f)), the dislocation net- 
work in the {112} slip system forms, including long dislocation ar- 
rays and dense wavy dislocation conﬁguration inside. It is a mix- 
ture of planar-slip and cross-slip behaviors [ 39 , 42 ] rather than a 
sole dislocation cell microstructure representing cross-slip domi- 
nant [ 43 ] or Taylor lattice microstructure representing planar-slip 
dominant [ 44 ]. The planar-slip on speciﬁc planes can allow sub- 
stantial plastic deformation in conjunction with more dislocation 
interactions caused by cross-slip and resultant complex disloca- 
tion arrangements, which synergistically contribute to the strain- 
hardening ability [ 42 ]. Although the mixture of planar-slip and 
cross-slip behaviors has been reported in other studies already, the 
cross-slip behavior is less distinct, where the dislocation density 
between dislocation bands or arrays is much smaller than in this 
case, showing the excellent cross-slip ability of Mo5 [ 45 ]. The nu- 
merous wavy dislocations help achieve a less heterogeneous dis- 
tribution of dislocation density and a more uniform deformation, 
consequently. 
At ∼15 % engineering strain (near the necking point), the dis- 
location conﬁguration remains largely similar to that observed at 
∼10 % strain. The cross-slip behavior between dislocation arrays 
is still apparent (see Fig. 6 (e, g)). The inter-spacing of dislocation 
arrays is narrower with a higher density of dislocation junctions. 
Those high-density dislocation junctions from the intersected dis- 
location arrays can further impede the dislocation movement and 
improve the strain-hardening ability [ 46 ]. Finally, the fractured dis- 
location conﬁguration of Mo5 is shown in Fig. 6 (h). The dislocation 
density with dislocation arrays further increases and transforms to 
DDWs within numerous dislocation tangles. 
Note that neither deformation twinning nor phase transforma- 
tion was observed in Mo5, which means the deformation pro- 
cess is still dominated by dislocation slip. In other words, both 
the tensile strength and elongation of Mo0 are successfully op- 
timized by the change of dislocation conﬁguration after the Mo 
alloying.

3.6. DFT calculations 
The macroscopic mechanical properties of alloys are highly 
connected with their electronic structure and atomic interaction 
[ 51 ]. To understand the effect of Mo in Ti40 Nb30 V25−x Zr5 Mox 
RCCAs, DFT calculations were carried out. The total den- 
sity of states (TDOS) and partial density of states (PDOS) of 
Ti40 Nb30 V25−x Zr5 Mox were calculated and plotted in Fig. 8 . As seen 
in Fig. 8 (a–c), there is no signiﬁcant difference in TDOS between 
Mo0, Mo3, and Mo5 alloys, indicating that the Mo addition doesn’t 
deteriorate the stable phase structure. However, one difference is 
that Mo decreases the TDOS in the Fermi energy level, represent- 
ing that the metallic properties of RCCAs are weakened, which may 
lead to an increase in covalent bonding. A parameter, the pseudo- 
energy gap, which is deﬁned as the energy difference between two 
strong peaks near the Fermi energy level, can help verify this hy- 
pothesis. It is widely accepted that the wider the pseudo-energy 
gap is, the stronger the covalence of the alloy is [ 52 ]. The pseudo- 
energy gaps of Mo0, Mo3, and Mo5 are calculated to be 2.37, 2.92, 
and 3.00 eV, respectively. Therefore, it can be concluded that the 
covalence of alloys is strengthened by Mo alloying. 
Besides, the PDOS in Fig. 8 can determine the contribution of 
electrons of each element. For each alloy, the PDOS curves of differ- 
ent atoms show heavy overlapping, which means severe electron 
hybridization and covalent property. The covalence mainly comes 
from d-orbital electrons. It is similar to other RHEAs composed of 
transition metal elements [ 53 ]. Moreover, the pseudo-energy gap 
of Mo is larger than the gap value of TDOS, while the V element 
exhibited an opposite tendency, which is the reason for the in- 
creased pseudo-energy gaps.

V, representing the metal bonding, while there is a strong overlap 
between Nb, Mo, and their adjacent atoms, which is the symbol of 
covalent bonding [ 54 ]. Moreover, the electronic density difference

4.3. Multi-effects of Mo in the alloy system 
The strength-ductility synergy of Mo5 alloy is dedicated to Mo 
alloying, which is a great reﬂection of the cocktail effect in HEAs. 
The synergistic mixture of base alloy and Mo possesses better per- 
formance than the sum of the parts. The Mo element plays a cru- 
cial role in the optimization of mechanical properties, and the ef- 
fects of Mo can be explained from different angles. 
(i) Increasing atomic strain ﬁeld. 
The strengthening effect of Mo mainly originates from so- 
lution strengthening, which impedes the dislocation slip by 
a stronger atomic strain ﬁeld. The inhomogeneity of the 
atomic strain ﬁeld is also an important characteristic of 
HEAs, bringing non-uniform dislocation slip resistance. The 
solution strengthening is mainly affected by two factors, 
atomic size misﬁt and modulus misﬁt. The increase of ei- 
ther can enhance the solution-strengthening effect. However, 
the ideal slide stress τis is more affected by the shear mod- 
ulus. It can be anticipated that adding elements with high 
shear modulus to an alloy system with low shear modulus 
can increase the solution strengthening and ideal slip stress 
simultaneously, which is the case in this study. Therefore, 
strengthening elements with a higher modulus (e.g., Mo and 
W) are more eﬃcient in single-phase HEAs. 
(ii) Changing dislocation conﬁguration. 
Low strain-hardening capacity and inferior tensile ductil- 
ity are severe problems for single-phase RHEAs. Regulat- 
ing precipitation behavior is an effective method to increase 
the strain-hardening ability, while it is lacking in single- 
phase RHEAs. Instead, how to trigger more dislocation sub- 
structures (e.g., microbands, DDWs, Taylor lattices, disloca- 
tion cells) is more important in single-phase RHEAs [ 43 ]. 
Cross-slip is a key factor inﬂuencing the dislocation behav- 
ior, while cross-slip alone is not suﬃcient to increase the 
strain-hardening ability, and local pinning effects and dislo- 
cation junctions are needed to form new Frank-Read sources 
and continuously supply dislocations. In this study, Mo can 
meet both needs at the same time. On the one hand, an 
uneven atomic strain ﬁeld creates local slip resistance; on 
the other hand, more cross-slip behaviors were induced by 
larger γusf when dislocations encounter local slip obstacles. 
Ultimately, the excellent work-hardening ability and tensile 
ductility were successfully enhanced by the Mo alloying. 
(iii) Manipulating electronic structure and atomic interaction. 
The addition of Mo also enhances the high-temperature 
strengths. The increased melting point is a key factor that 
should not be neglected. Moreover, the covalent bond- 
ing composition can also explain the enhanced high- 
temperature performance from another perspective. The 
atomic interaction in the alloy system was signiﬁcantly re- 
inforced after the Mo alloying. More covalent bonding com- 
position is the reason for better high-temperature strengths. 
Despite that the DFT calculation is based on 0 K, the devi- 
ations from real high-temperature conditions are acceptable 
and can guide the designs of novel high-temperature struc- 
tural materials.

(1) Mo0, Mo3, and Mo5 showed the same single-phase BCC 
phase constitution. However, the addition of Mo increased 
the atomic strain and made its distribution more uneven. 
Those inhomogeneous atomic strains also mean higher, un- 
evenly distributed resistance for dislocation slip. 
(2) The yield strength of Mo5 at RT was increased by 20 % 
compared to that of Mo0. The strength improvement mainly 
comes from the high shear modulus of Mo, which not only 
increases the ideal slip resistance but also introduces more 
solution strengthening contributions. More cross-slip was 
triggered in Mo5 due to its higher γusf and more dislocation 
slip obstacles, greatly improving the work-hardening ability. 
Through DIC veriﬁcation, the uniform deformation ability of 
Mo5 has been signiﬁcantly improved, therefore, avoiding the 
premature failure in Mo0, and achieving an outstanding frac- 
ture strain of ∼25 %. 
(3) At high temperatures, the Mo5 alloy exhibited excellent 
comprehensive mechanical properties. The tensile strength 
of Mo5 can still exceed 500 MPa at 1073 K. It was found that 
Mo increased the atomic interaction and covalent bonding 
composition by DFT calculations, which explains the excel- 
lent strength retention of Mo5 at high temperatures.
