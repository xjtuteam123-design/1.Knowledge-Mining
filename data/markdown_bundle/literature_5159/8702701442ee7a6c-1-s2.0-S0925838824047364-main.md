ABSTRACT
In this study, a new high-temperature titanium alloy was designed and developed for use in the framework of 
hypersonic vehicle fuel tanks. The effects of heat treatment parameters on the microstructure and high- 
temperature tensile properties of the alloy were thoroughly investigated. At 700 ℃ , the ultimate tensile 
strength (UTS) and yield strength (YS) of the alloy first increase with the increase of solution temperature, and 
then slightly decrease. Optimal strength and ductility are achieved after solution treatment at 1000 ℃ for 2 h, 
resulting in a UTS of 545 MPa, YS of 454 MPa, and elongation (EL) of 15.5 % at 700 ℃ . The fracture behavior 
under the condition is characterized by a mixed brittle-ductile mode, predominantly featuring transgranular 
cleavage fracture. Aging treatments at different temperatures have minimal impact on UTS, YS, and EL, with 
microstructural analysis indicating only slight dimensional changes due to aging. The high-temperature defor -
mation and failure mechanisms of the alloy were then discussed. It was found that nanoscale silicides enhance 
strength through precipitation strengthening, while microscale silicides reduce strength and accelerate fracture. 
The findings of this study offer valuable insights for improving the high-temperature tensile properties of tita -
nium alloys.
1. Introduction
In recent years, with the rapid development of the aerospace in -
dustry, more requirements have been imposed on the high-temperature 
structural materials [1,2] . Particularly, in some cases such as hypersonic 
aircraft, the service temperature can reach 700 ℃ , demanding materials 
with excellent instantaneous high-temperature strength [3] . It is evident 
that nickel-based superalloys exhibit the highest service temperature, 
but their high density compromises the further improvement of 
thrust-to-weight ratio of engine [4] . TiAl intermetallic alloys can also be 
of choice, due to the low density and high-temperature strength [5] . Yet 
their room temperature brittleness has consistently constrained the 
practical applications [2] .
High-temperature titanium alloys with exceptional high- 
temperature strength, oxidation resistance, and high creep resistance, 
have found applications in aerospace fields [6,7] . Currently, traditional 
Ti-Al-Sn-Zr-Mo-Si alloy systems, such as Ti1100 [8] , IMI834 [9] , Ti60 
[10] , and Ti600 [11] , have been used under 600 ℃ and exhibited good 
microstructure and property stability. However, beyond this tempera -
ture, the mechanical properties sharply decline, failing to meet service 
requirements [12] .
Therefore, an imminent task is to develop titanium alloys capable of 
meeting the demands of the rapidly developing aerospace, defense, and 
other fields in the range of 650 – 700 ℃ . In the aerospace and defense 
sectors, now some scholars has made certain progress in the research of 
new high-temperature titanium alloys with a focus on temperatures 
above 600 ℃ . The basic approach involves incorporating higher con -
tents of β -stabilizing elements such as Mo, W, Nb, Ta, as seen in alloys 
like Ti65 [13] , Ti62421S [14] , and BTi-6431S [15,16] . Although these 
new high-temperature titanium alloys can elevate their working tem -
perature to 650 – 700 ℃ by adding alloying elements, they are currently 
in the research and development stage. The high-temperature tensile 
performance within this temperature range still requires further 
enhancement.
Furthermore, solely relying on adding alloying elements to improve 
the high-temperature strength of high-temperature titanium alloys is far 
from sufficient, as excessive alloying elements significantly reduce the 
thermal stability of the microstructure [17] . Additionally, the content of 
alloying elements must be controlled from the perspectives of cost and 
weight. Therefore, for new high-temperature titanium alloys, besides

be controlled through high-temperature deformation methods such as 
forging, rolling, and subsequent heat treatment [20,21] . However, there 
is currently no in-depth exploration of microstructure selection and

At present, the most widely used titanium alloys are the wrought 
types. However, titanium alloy parts are characterized by their chal -

mechanical processing costs. To reduce the manufacturing costs, re -
searchers have conducted extensive studies on various net shape or near 
net shape manufacturing technologies, such as precision casting, preci -

Compared to wrought titanium alloys, cast titanium alloys offer ad -
vantages such as lower cost, shorter production time, and near net shape 
capabilities. Therefore, when processing some large components, cast -

In summary, this study focuses on the short-term application of the 
Ti-Al-Sn-Zr-Mo-Nb-Si-Ta near- α high-temperature titanium alloy at 700 
℃ . The microstructure of the as-cast alloy was thoroughly characterized, 
revealing Widmanst ¨atten structure characteristics, with the distribution 
of silicides confirmed using EDS and HAADF analyses. The research 
examines how variations in heat treatment processes affect the micro -
structure and high-temperature tensile properties of the alloy. Finally, 
the mechanisms of high-temperature deformation and fracture were 
comprehensively analyzed and discussed.
2. Experiment procedure
Utilizing the Vacuum Arc Remelting (VAR) method, a near- ɑ tita -
nium alloy, Ti-5.86Al-3.69Sn-3.56Zr-0.99Mo-0.36Nb-0.38Si-0.18Ta- 
0.13O-0.011 C was melted. The raw materials, including Ti, Al, Sn, Zr, 
Mo, Nb, Si, Ta, each have a purity of 99.95 wt%. After being mixed in 
proportion, the alloy ingots are prepared using the Secondary vacuum 
arc remelting process. To eliminate casting defects, achieve a dense 
microstructure, and reduce the adverse effects of casting defects on 
mechanical properties, the alloy ingot underwent hot isostatic pressing 
(1020 ℃ /150 MPa/180 min). The ingots were removed from the 
furnace after the pressure was released and the temperature dropped 
below 250 ℃ . The chemical composition is shown in Table 1 .
The DSC curve indicates that the β phase transition temperature of 
the alloy is 1050 ℃ , as shown in Fig. 1 . Therefore, solution temperatures 
of 900 ℃ , 950 ℃ , 1000 ℃ , 1050 ℃ , and 1100 ℃ are selected, and the 
solution treatment time is established at 2 h. Aging was conducted at 
temperatures of 670 ℃ , 700 ℃ , 730 ℃ , 760 ℃ , and 790 ℃ . After so -
lution treatment, all the samples are air cooled.
The microstructure, local composition, and fracture morphology of 
the alloy were characterized using a Tescan Clara GMH field emission 
scanning electron microscope. The local composition of the alloy was 
analyzed using an OXFORD energy-dispersive spectrometer (EDS) 
probe. And the transmission samples were characterized using a high- 
resolution Tecnai F30 transmission electron microscope. The charac -
terization included: (1) observation of the microstructure in bright-field 
imaging; (2) observation and compositional analysis of specific areas 
using high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) imaging.
The tensile samples were prepared. Cut a bar of dimensions Ф 12 mm 
× 73 mm from the ingot, and after heat treatment, turn it into a tensile 
specimen as shown in Fig. 2 . The loading speed is set at 2.1 mm/min, 
and three sets of data are obtained under the same test conditions. The 
average of the measured data is taken as the performance data, and a set 
of typical curves is selected to represent the tensile curve.
3. Result and discussion
3.1. Microstructure and tensile property of the as-cast alloy
Fig. 3 (a)-(f) show the microstructure of the as-cast alloy, which ex -
hibits a Widmanst ¨atten microstructure, consisting of transformed β 
grains and grain boundary α phase. As the α phase precipitates from the 
β grains, it follows various Burgers orientation relationships [24] . 
Consequently, within each β grain, different orientations of α / β lamellas 
can be seen, the width of the α / β colonies ranges from 30 to 250 μ m, and 
the width of the α laths is 1 – 10 μ m. Moreover, white, spherical pre -
cipitates can be observed at the α / β phase interfaces.

can precipitate at the α / β phase interfaces in titanium alloys with a 
certain amount of Si and Zr elements [25] . Therefore, elemental 
composition analysis was conducted, as shown in Fig. 3(g)-(l). It can be 
seen that the Al and Mo elements are respectively enriched in the α and β 
phases. This can be attributed to the characteristics of these elements in 
titanium alloys. Al, being a typical α -phase stabilizing element, tends to 
concentrate in the α phase, while Mo, recognized as a β -phase stabilizing

3.3. The effect of heat treatment on tensile property
Fig. 8 illustrates the trend of mechanical properties at 700 ℃ as a 
function of the solution temperature.
Fig. 8 shows that with the solution temperature increases, both the 
UTS and YS of the alloy initially rise and then decrease. In Fig. 7 (a-b), 
when the solution treatment temperature is 1050 ℃ and 1100 ℃ , the α
s 
lamellae and silicides disappear, so the strength decreases slightly. 
While a better balance of strength and ductility is achieved after solution 
treatment at 1000 ℃ for 2 h. The mechanical properties under different 
solution treatment temperatures are listed in Table 3 . It indicates that 
the UTS and EL significantly increased by 108 MPa and 12.9 % after 
solution treatment at 1000 ℃ for 2 h compared to the as-cast 
microstructure.
Solution treatment at 1000 ℃ , which offers a good balance of 
strength and ductility, was selected. The aging temperatures were cho -
sen as 670 ℃ , 700 ℃ , 730 ℃ , 760 ℃ , and 790 ℃ . The performance of 
the alloy after solution and aging treatment was tested and shown in 
Fig. 9 .
Fig. 9 shows that there is no significant change in the UTS with 
varying aging temperatures, with values remaining around 545 MPa. 
However, the YS exhibits a trend of increasing first and then decreasing, 
reaching a peak at 760 ℃ . The ductility does not show a significant 
change with varying aging temperatures. Microstructural analysis re -
veals that after aging treatments between 670 ℃ and 760 ℃ , the 
microstructural dimensions vary by no more than 1 μ m, while the size of 
silicides ranges from 50 nm to 105 nm. Consequently, the performance 
fluctuations are minimal. This suggests that the tensile properties of the 
alloy are not significantly affected by aging temperature under short 
aging durations. The average values of the properties after different 
solution and aging treatments are shown in Table 4 . Changing the aging 
temperature has a minor impact on the mechanical properties of this 
alloy.

fracture mechanism of the alloy after solution and aging treatment is 
predominantly a mixed brittle and ductile fracture dominated by 
transgranular cleavage fracture.

3.5. The role of silicides
After solution treatment at 900 ℃ and 950 ℃ , the alloy precipitated 
dual-scale silicides, and silicides of different sizes play different roles in 
the high-temperature performance and fracture failure behavior of the 
alloy. According to previous research results, microscale silicides do not 
contribute to the strength of the alloy during high-temperature tensile 
testing. Instead, they lead to the enrichment of elements such as Si, Zr, 
Nb, and Ta, which weakens the solid solution strengthening effect of the 
matrix α phase, resulting in a decrease in high-temperature strength. At 
the same time, microscale silicides may undergo fracture themselves 
during the high-temperature deformation process, or decohere from the 
matrix, leading to the nucleation of cracks, which also has a detrimental 
effect on ductility.
TEM characterization of the microstructure near the tensile fracture 
surface at 700 ℃ was conducted, with the results shown in Fig. 13 . It can 
be observed that after 2 h of solution treatment at 1000 ℃ , no silicides 
were detected. Dislocation pile-ups were found both at the interfaces 
and within the α
s 
lamellae. After aging treatment, the presence of 
nanoscale silicides was detected, with some dislocations piling up near 
these silicides. This indicates that nanoscale silicides can pin disloca -
tions during the high-temperature tensile process, thereby hindering 
dislocation motion and providing precipitation hardening. Conse -
quently, this enhances the high-temperature strength of the alloy.
In general, nanoscale silicides can enhance the strength of the alloy 
through precipitation hardening. However, the precipitation of micro -
scale silicides not only leads to elemental enrichment and a reduction in 
strength but also promotes the nucleation of cracks, accelerating the 
fracture failure of the alloy. Therefore, excessive precipitation of 
microscale silicides should be avoided.
