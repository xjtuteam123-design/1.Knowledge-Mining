An overview of rhenium effect in single-crystal superalloys
Ming Huang, Jing Zhu*
Received: 26 May 2015 / Revised: 24 July 2015 / Accepted: 13 August 2015
/C211The Nonferrous Metals Society of China and Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg 2015
Abstract Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys are the
key materials for the manufacturing and development of
advanced aeroengines. Rhenium is a crucial alloying ele-
ment in the advanced nickel-based single-crystal superal-
loys for its special strengthening effects. The addition of
Re could effectively enhance the creep properties of the
single-crystal superalloys; thus, the content of Re is con-
sidered as one of the characteristics in different-generation
single-crystal superalloys. Owing to the fundamental
importance of rhenium to nickel-based single-crystal
superalloys, much progress has been made on under-
standing of the effect of rhenium in the single-crystal
superalloys. While the effect of Re doping on the nickel-
based superalloys is well documented, the origins of the so-
called rhenium effect are still under debate. In this paper,
the effect of Re doping on the single-crystal superalloys
and progress in understanding the rhenium effect are
reviewed. The characteristics of the d-states occupancy in
the electronic structure of Re make it the slowest diffusion
elements in the single-crystal superalloys, which is
undoubtedly responsible for the rhenium effect, while the
postulates of Re cluster and the enrichment of Re at the c/c0
interface are still under debate, and the synergistic action of
Re with other alloying elements should be further studied.
Additionally, the interaction of Re with interfacial dislo-
cations seems to be a promising explanation for the rhe-
nium effect. Finally, the addition of Ru could help suppress
topologically close-packed (TCP) phase formation and
strengthen the Re doping single-crystal superalloys.
Understanding the mechanism of rhenium effect will be
beneﬁcial for the effective utilization of Re and the design
of low-cost single-crystal superalloys.
Keywords Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys;
Rhenium effect; Creep; Strengthening mechanism; Re
cluster; Interfacial dislocations
1 Introduction
Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys are important tur-
bine blade materials for aeroengines and combustion gas
turbines due to their excellent high-temperature compre-
hensive properties [ 1, 2]. To obtain better high-temperature
performances, directional solidiﬁcation method was used to
remove grain boundaries from the single-crystal superalloy
turbine blades, which act as sites of weakness during high-
temperature operation [ 3, 4]. Additionally, a lot of high-
melting-point alloying elements such as Re, W, Ta and Mo
were added to strengthen the single-crystal superalloys.
Since the invention of the ﬁrst single-crystal superalloy
PWA1480 in the early 1980s, different series of single-
crystal superalloys have been developed. Up to now, the
single-crystal superalloys have been upgraded to the ﬁfth
generation in order to support the continuous progress of
advanced aeroengines.

Among all the alloying elements, Re plays a special role in
the enhancement of high-temperature creep properties and
almost all single-crystal superalloys contain a certain
amount of Re since the second generation. The content of Re
is considered as one of the characteristics in different-gen-
eration single-crystal superalloys, and in the newly devel-
oped fourth- and ﬁfth-generation single-crystal superalloys,
Ru has been used in addition to Re doping [ 1, 5].
Re is one of the rarest elements in the earth’s crust;
however, the particular strengthening effect of rhenium
makes it widely use in superalloys for gas turbines. Thus,
Re becomes an element with critical strategic military
importance, and the price of rhenium is very high. In order
to lower the consumption of rhenium and reduce the pro-
duct cost, the research on effect and mechanism of Re
doping is needed.
The development of the single-crystal superalloys and
many previous studies undoubtedly show that the addition
of Re could effectively improve the creep and fatigue
properties [ 6–13]. Both experiments and simulations have
been taken to elucidate the origin of the so-called rhenium
effect [ 14–28]; however, until now, no accepted mecha-
nism has been conferred for the strengthening effect of Re.
In this review, the effect of Re doping in the single-
crystal superalloys and the development of related
strengthening mechanism are overviewed. The summa-
rization will be beneﬁcial for the understanding and uti-
lization of rhenium in the single-crystal superalloys.
2 Effects of rhenium doping in single-crystal
superalloys
As known, almost all single-crystal superalloys since the
second generation contain a certain quantity of Re. Table 1
[1, 29] shows the typical nominal composition of different
generations of single-crystal superalloys. The ﬁrst-genera-
tion single-crystal superalloys do not contain any Re ele-
ment, while the second- and the third-generation single-
crystal superalloys are characterized by 3 wt% and 6 wt%
concentration of Re [ 2]. The newly developed fourth- and
ﬁfth-generation single-crystal superalloys also contain a
high content of Re, but with the addition of some Ru.
During high-temperature operation of single-crystal blades,
creep deformation due to the centrifugal force along the
[001] blade axis is one of the most main origins of failure.
The most important effect of Re in the single-crystal
superalloys is the strengthening of creep properties, and this
is reﬂected in the facts that the service temperatures of the
second-generation and third-generation single-crystal
superalloys are increased by 30 and 60 /C176C, respectively [1].
In addition, many experimental data conﬁrmed the creep-
strengthening effect of Re. Taking the CMSX series single-
crystal superalloys [ 7, 9] developed by the Cannon-Muske-
gon Corporation for example, the third-generation single-
crystal superalloy CMSX-10 (6 wt% Re) shows much
superior high-temperature properties than the ﬁrst- and
second-generation single-crystal superalloy CMSX-2 (no
Re) and CMSX-4 (3 wt% Re). The creeping time of CMSX-
10 to 1 % strain was about 20.0 and 4.6 times that for CMSX-
2 and CMSX-4, respectively, at 982 /C176C/248 MPa. And the
engine test for the CMSX-10 and CMSX-4 superalloys at the
same conditions showed that the mean elongation of CMSX-
10 superalloy blades was 0.1 mm, only 1/8 of that for
CMSX-4 superalloy blades. Some studies on the ﬁrst-gen-
eration single-crystal superalloys PWA1480 and CMSX-2
showed that adjusting the superalloys by reducing the con-
tent of W and adding Re instead could improve the creep
properties of the superalloys [ 30].
Apart from strengthening creep properties, moderate
amount of Re addition can effectively improve the fatigue
strength [ 9] and increase the oxidization and corrosion
resistance [ 31] of the single-crystal superalloys. However,
Re is an effective topologically close-packed (TCP) phase
formation element; thus, excess Re addition may lead to the
formation of TCP phases such as r, l, R and P phases [ 32–
39] which are detrimental to the properties of the superal-
loys. In the newly developed fourth- and ﬁfth-generation
single-crystal superalloys, Ru addition was used to suppress
the formation of TCP phases and stabilize the microstruc-
ture. Thus, understanding the strengthening mechanism of
Re will be beneﬁcial for the effective utilization of Re and
the design of low-cost single-crystal superalloys.
3 Strengthening mechanism of rhenium in single-
crystal superalloys
Since the outstanding and unique strengthening effect of
rhenium in single-crystal superalloys, numerous studies
have been conducted to elucidate the mechanism of the
rhenium effect. In the early days of the study, researchers
mainly focused on the relationship between Re addition
and microstructure evolution during high-temperature
creep.
In 1985, Giamei and Anton [ 15] studied the effect of Re
addition on the microstructure of single-crystal superalloys
for the ﬁrst time in a modiﬁed MAR-M200* alloy based on
former researches of eutectic nickel-based superalloys. It
was shown that Re partitions strongly to the c matrix and
no detectable Re is found in c
0 precipitates. In addition, the
study found that Re doping could effectively lower the
coarsening rate of c0 particles which is considered to be
beneﬁcial for increasing the rupture lives of the single-
crystal superalloys at elevated temperatures. Many fol-
lowing researches studied the effect of Re on rafting

4 Re: the slowest diffusing element in nickel
A signiﬁcant characteristic that distinguishes Re from other
alloying elements is that Re has the slowest diffusion rate
in nickel. Many strengthening phenomena in the single-
crystal superalloys are ascribed to the slow diffusion rate of
rhenium, though with not much direct evidence. In the
early work of Giamei and Anton [ 15], no Re was found in
c
0 precipitates and it is suggested that Re needs to diffuse
away from the c/c0 interface during the coarsening of c0
particles. The diffusion coefﬁcient was expected to be
small since Re is a massive atom compared to Ni; thus, the
coarsening of c
0 particles was retarded by the addition of
Re. Similar analysis has also been applied to many fol-
lowing studies [ 10, 33], and other suggestions for the
rhenium effect such as ‘ ‘rhenium cluster’ ’ and ‘ ‘enrichment
at c/c0 interface’ ’ also take account of the low diffusion
coefﬁcient of Re.
Though the slow diffusion rate of Re is widely accepted,
only a few data on the diffusion coefﬁcient of Re are
reported [ 53–56]. Reed et al. took a series study on the
interdiffusion of alloying elements in Ni, and both exper-
imental and theoretic studies showed that Re has a lower
interdiffusion coefﬁcient compared to other alloying ele-
ments in nickel-based single-crystal superalloys [ 54–56]. A
number of diffusion couple (Ni-Ta, Ni-W, Ni–Re) were
designed to study the interdiffusion coefﬁcient of Ta, W,
and Re, and these three elements are important refractory
alloying elements in single-crystal superalloys. The diffu-
sion couples were heat-treated at 900–1300 /C176C, and the
elemental concentration proﬁle was determined by electron
microprobe. By using a modiﬁed form of Boltzmann–
Motano analysis, the interdiffusion coefﬁcient of Ta, W
and Re at different solute concentrations and temperatures
was determined. The interdiffusion coefﬁcient changes
very little with alloy composition, and Fig. 1 [56] shows

also has great effect on the microstructure of the as-cast
single-crystal superalloys and the following heat treatment
process. Understanding the slow diffusion rate of Re could
help us to comprehend the strengthening mechanism of Re
and improve the single-crystal superalloys.
4.1 Re cluster
The slow diffusion rate of Re is of course an important
origin of its strengthening effect; however, it is not enough
to fully illustrate the rhenium effect. The distribution and
the existence form of Re still are also crucial to the
strengthening effect of Re and need to be studied. Since
dislocations mainly move in the c matrix during high-
temperature creep, and Re segregates strongly to c phase,
so a reasonable assumption is that Re doping could
increase the thermally activation energy of dislocation
motion in the c matrix. It was suggested in the 1980s that
Re may form small clusters, which act as efﬁcient obstacles
for dislocation motion [ 30]. The Re clustering postulate is
widely invoked as a possible explanation for the rhenium
effect, but its reliability is still under debate.
A signiﬁcant amount of works from both experiment
and simulation were conducted in order to provide a
deﬁnitive answer to the question that whether Re forms
clusters in c phase. Atom probe tomography (APT) is an
efﬁcient technique to analyze the chemical composition of
materials at atomic level, so it is considered to be a possible
technique to verify the small Re clusters in c matrix of
single-crystal superalloys. Re clustering postulate was ﬁrst
suggested by Blavette et al. [ 30, 57, 58], based on their
results of one-dimensional atom probe (1DAP). They
modiﬁed the CMSX-2 and PWA1480 single-crystal
superalloys with a small amount of Re additions, which
greatly increased the creep properties. Furthermore, the
elemental distribution in c phase was studied by 1DAP.

tive proﬁles) of Re and Cr in modiﬁed PWA1480 single-
crystal superalloy. The ladder diagrams show the count of
studied elements over the total number of analyzed atoms;
thus, the slope of each curve gives the local concentration
of each element. The change in slope along Re proﬁle
indicates a small Re-enriched region extending over
1.3 nm, and it was suggested that the inhomogeneity
originates from small Re cluster in c phase. Wanderka et al.
[59] further used APT to study the chemical composition in
the second-generation single-crystal superalloy CMSX-4

Following these results, Re clusters are unlikely to be
formed in the superalloys and they cannot be the origin of
the rhenium effect. It is proposed that single Re atoms are
responsible for generating the strengthening effect, which
of course still needs further quantitative analysis.
From a physical point of view, Re cluster originates
from Re–Re interaction. Single-crystal superalloys usually
contain more than ten alloying elements, so the interaction
of Re with other alloying elements should also be consid-
ered in the exploration of the rhenium effect. Though not
much information is available with the synergistic action of
Re with other alloying elements, some studies do give
some indications of the synergy. In the early work of Smith
[14] on a series of simple model alloys, it was shown that
Cr affected strongly the partitioning of Re which con-
tributes to the negative misﬁt of the superalloys. And Re
could not strengthen the superalloys without the addition of
Cr. The following work of Chen et al. [ 47] in the alloys
more closely approximate commercial superalloys showed
similar behavior of Cr on the effect of Re and misﬁt.
Recently, high-angle annular dark-ﬁeld scanning trans-
mission electron microscope (HAADF–STEM) was used to
study the distribution of Re in DD6—a second-generation
single-crystal superalloy [ 65] and some bright contrast
regions in c phase near c/c
0 interface are found. Combining
with the energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping
result that Re, W, Cr and Co were enriched in c phase close
to c/c0 interface, it is suggested that heavy atoms such as
Re and W may form clusters with sizes of about 1–2 nm in
crept superalloys, while no such clusters could be found
near c/c
0 interface before creep test. The current electron
microscopy study can of course not provide strong evi-
dence for the existence of heavy atom clusters, but it gives
us an impression that the formation of these clusters may
relate to the creep deformation, and the c/c0 interface and
the co-segregation of Re with other alloying elements may
be responsible for the strengthening effect of Re. With the
development of aberration-corrected scanning transmission
electron microscope (STEM) and EDS detector, atomic-
resolution elemental maps could be performed [ 66–68],
which provides further opportunity to study the local
enrichment of Re and other alloying elements in single-
crystal superalloys. Therefore, additional work should be
carried out to investigate the co-segregation of Re with
other alloying elements. And of course, theory calculations
are also needed for understanding the interaction of Re
with other alloying elements from the electronic structure.
In summary, the formation of Re cluster is still a con-
troversial postulate for rhenium effect. With the develop-
ment of new techniques and simulation methods, more and
more works tend to against the formation of Re cluster, and
further work should be carried out on the co-segregation
and synergistic action of Re with other alloying elements.
4.2 Enrichment of Re at c/c
0 interface
Nickel-based single-crystal superalloys contain a high
volume fraction of the cuboidal L1 2-ordered c0-hardening
phase, precipitated in disordered fcc c matrix. During high-
temperature creep when diffusion of alloying elements is
expected to be signiﬁcant, c and c0 phases will have a
lamellar rafting structure [ 69, 70]. It was shown that the
addition of Re could slow down the coarsening of c0 phase.
So it is natural to consider that the enrichment of Re near c/
c0 interface may be one of the origins of the so-called
rhenium effect since Re could slow down the interfacial
diffusion at the c/c0 interface.
The elemental distribution across c/c0 interface is the
subject of many studies. In the early work of Wanderka
et al. [ 59] on CMSX-4 single-crystal superalloy after heat
treatment, a sharp interface structure was observed and
only the enrichment of Ti was detected by APT. Soon
afterward, Warren et al. [ 71] investigated the distribution
of Re in RR3000 superalloy after creep test and fatigue test
by 3DAP and a clear evidence was found for the enrich-
ment of Re at c/c
0 interface (Fig. 7 [71] ). It was concluded
that the enrichment of Re is due to the growth of c0 phase
which expels Re into c matrix during cooling process, since
pileup of Re close to c/c0 interface was observed adjacent
to both the primary c0 raft and the secondary c0 particles.
The authors suggested that the pileup of Re may still be
generated after initial heat treatment of the alloy, and
enrichment of Re may affect the local lattice misﬁt and
climb of dislocations during high-temperature deformation
so as to strengthen the superalloy. Further studies [ 72]o n
the as-heat-treated Rene´ N6 single-crystal superalloy also
found enrichment of Re at c/c
0 interface, which conﬁrmed
the work by Warren et al. [ 71]. However, the work by

Recently, Mottura et al. [ 63] studied the concentration
proﬁles across c/c

Re in c phase adjacent to c/c0 interface was observed.
Comparing the concentration proﬁles obtained from APT
with those from phase-ﬁeld modeling, the authors sug-

cannot strengthen the superalloys during high-temperature
operation. By STEM and EDS techniques, Ge et al. [ 73]
found the enrichment of Re and W near c/c0 interface in
crept DD6 single-crystal superalloy. And detailed analysis
of the elemental distribution across c/c0 interface showed
the congregation of Ni and Ta at c0 side ahead of the
enrichments of Re and W at c/c0 interface (Fig. 8 [73]),
which indicates the retarding effect of Re on elemental
interdiffusion.
The enrichment of Re at c/c0 interface seems related to
the alloying elements, heat treatment and deformation
conditions, but whether the enrichment of Re could help to
strengthen single-crystal superalloys during high-tempera-
ture creep is still under debate and further work needs to be
done for more direct evidences.
4.3 Interaction of Re with interfacial dislocations
The c/c
0 interface is believed to be the key factor in
strengthening the single-crystal superalloys, since c0 pre-
cipitate-strengthened superalloys are much stronger com-
pared to single c and c0 phase alloys [74–79]. The evolution
of interfacial microstructure in the single-crystal superalloys
was widely studied [ 80–83], and the formations of rafting
structure and interfacial dislocation networks are known to
play an important role in determining the creep properties of
superalloys. Both the dislocation reaction and the elemental
interdiffusion take place at c/c
0 interface. In order to eluci-
date the strengthening mechanism of Re, the interaction of
Re with interfacial dislocations and interfacial structure
should be understood. Previous studies on Re cluster and the
enrichment of Re at c/c
0 interface ignored the detailed
structure of dislocations and interfacial structure.

stability limitations and the so-called fourth- and ﬁfth-

superalloys could help to suppress the formation of TCP
phases, thus to increase the microstructural stability and
improve the high-temperature creep resistance. In order to
further improve the properties of the Re doping single-
crystal superalloys, a detailed understanding of the effects
associated with Ru on microstructural stability and the
interaction between Re and Ru is required.
In all the commercial nickel-based single-crystal
superalloys, Re strongly partitions to c phase. Therefore, it
is natural to consider whether Ru additions can alter the
distribution of Re. Many studies attributed the effect of Ru
to the so-called reverse partition of Re, and the reduced
concentration in c matrix due to an increased concentration
in c
0 phase [89–93]. However, the mechanism still remains
controversial, since no reverse partition of Re was observed
in many studies [ 37, 50, 94, 95]. In our point of view, the
partition of alloying elements is closely related to the alloy
composition. That is why Ru shows different effects on the
partition behavior of Re, and it can be inferred that other
reasons must be responsible for the microstructural stabi-
lization effect of Ru. A novel investigation was carried out
by Hobbs et al. [ 35, 36], detailed transmission electron
microscope (TEM) studies found a lower density of
structural growth ledges for atomic attachment at the
TCP/matrix interface in alloys with Ru addition, and it was
suggested that the addition of Ru could also reduce the
level of Re supersaturation in c matrix for the destabi-
lization of c
0 phase. While recent work by Pyczak et al.
[96] and Heckl et al. [ 34] attributed the TCP inhibition
effect of Ru to the increasing Re solubility in c matrix,
other ﬁrst-principle calculations [ 17, 22, 23, 28] were
carried out to study the strengthening effect of Ru on c/c0
interface, and the c0-Ni3Al, the direct bonding and
hybridization between Ru and the host atoms were con-
sidered to be the main origin of the strengthening effect.
Since the partition of the alloying elements is closely
related to the alloy composition, the reverse partition of Re is
not enough to illustrate the TCP suppression effect of Ru,
further work on the atomic structure and element distribution
at the TCP/matrix interface maybe a promising direction.
Additionally, studying the effects of alloy elements from
electronic structure will always be of great importance.
5 Summary
The addition of Re in the single-crystal superalloys could
greatly increase the creep resistance of the superalloys,
which leads to the crucial role of Re in the superalloys.
However, the origin of the rhenium effect is still under
debate. Several suggestions were proposed for the origin of
the rhenium effect, among which the slow diffusion rate of
Re caused by its d-states occupancy in the electronic
structure is the basis to support the rhenium-strengthening
mechanisms. Though the theory of Re cluster and the
theory of enrichment of Re at the c/c
0 interface are still
under debate, both these two theories are supported by the
low diffusion coefﬁcient of Re. Considering the
microstructure evolution during high-temperature creep,
the effect of Re should be studied accompanied with
interfacial dislocation networks and interfacial
microstructures. Thus, the interaction between Re with
interfacial dislocations seems to be a promising explana-
tion for the rhenium effect. Additionally, the interaction of
Re with other alloying elements especially Ru should also
be considered. In the future, quantitative studies either by
experiments or by theory simulation are needed for deeply
understanding the rhenium effect and for guiding alloy
design.

electron wave microdiffraction

1980s, Prof. Zhu and her col-

used as the material of the centrifugal machine for uranium in China. In
the last decade, her research work focused on aberration-corrected ana-
lytical electron microscopy, material science, surface/interface, alloy/
oxide, nano-mater./structure/property, etc. Since 2007, she became the
leader of a project ‘ ‘Research and development of axle of high speed
trains.’ ’ She published the ﬁrst monograph on analytical electron
