1. Introduction 
Lightweight aluminum (Al) alloys are widely used in temperature 
ranges below 200 
◦
C in the automotive industry [ 1 – 3 ]. In general, the 
Al-Cu alloy series is commonly used as a heat-resistant Al alloy. 
Numerous studies have attempted to improve the high-temperature 
mechanical performance of the Al-Cu alloys, the strength of which de -
creases at temperatures higher than 200 
◦
C [ 1 ]. For industrial applica -
tions (e.g., centrifugal impellers in automotive turbochargers [ 4 ]), 
wrought Al alloys that allow service up to 200 
◦
C must be developed. 
The thermal stability of fine precipitation morphologies is a key issue in 
achieving Al alloys with superior high-temperature strength and creep 
rupture life [ 5 ]. Conventional Al-Cu alloys are strengthened by fine 
precipitates of metastable phases associated with the Al
2
Cu phase, 
whereby the precipitation morphologies often coarsen through phase 
transformation (from the metastable phases to the stable phase) during 
thermal exposure, thereby resulting in reduced high-temperature me -
chanical performance. The addition of microalloying elements (e.g., Mn, 
Zr, Mn, Fe, V, Sc, and Ag) retards the coarsening of fine precipitates 
[ 6 – 9 ], whereas the low thermal stability of the metastable phases can 
limit the service temperature of Al alloy components subjected to 
long-term thermal exposure. 
One important requirement for achieving superior high-temperature 
mechanical performance is strengthening by thermodynamically stable 
phases. Al alloys strengthened by nanosized precipitates of the stable 
Al
3

2 
structure (in equilibrium with the α -Al phase) 
exhibit superior high strength and creep properties at elevated tem -
peratures exceeding 200 
◦

(such as Zr and Er) improves the thermal stability of the stable L1
2 
phase 
precipitated in the α -Al matrix [ 12 – 14 ]. Recently, considerable effort 
has been focused on developing Al-Ce-based alloys containing inter -
metallic phases with high volume fractions for high-temperature

One promising route for enhancing the high-temperature creep 
rupture strength is the introduction of solute elements with low diffu -
sivity in a metal matrix [ 4 , 26 ]. In Ni-based superalloys, the concentra -
tions of refractory alloy elements (such as Re, W, Ta, and Ru) are 
significant for creep strengthening [ 26 ]. Elements with low diffusivities 
are partitioned into the γ (fcc) matrix, thus enhancing creep resistance 
with the suppression of diffusion during the dislocation climb process. 
Consequently, introducing solute elements with low diffusivity into the 
α -Al matrix may be effective for the creep strengthening of Al alloys. 
However, few elements with high solubility in the α -Al phase are 
available, according to Al – X (X: alloy element) binary systems [ 4 ]. 
Controlling the concentrations of solute elements by the heat-treatment 
(solution treatment) process could be difficult, and creep strengthening 
by solute elements has scarcely been applied in Al alloys. By contrast, 
various transition metals with low diffusion coefficients, such as Ti, V, 
Mo, Nb, and Ce [ 4 , 27 ], have been used in producing Al alloys via 
powder metallurgy (PM) using rapidly solidified alloy powders [ 28 , 29 ]. 
PM-processed Al alloys containing large amounts of transition elements 
exhibit excellent high-temperature strength [ 28 , 29 ], thus suggesting a 
high potential for strengthening by solute elements with low diffusivity. 
Such transition metals (M) often exhibit peritectic phase diagrams on the 
Al-rich side of Al – M binary systems [ 4 , 17 , 30 ]. The partition coefficient 
of M between the solid and liquid phases is higher than one ( k
S/L
M 
> 1) in 
the Al-rich composition, thus indicating that M is partitioned into the 
dendritic α -Al phase during solidification. This insight provides the 
opportunity for introducing solute M (with low diffusivity) into the α -Al 
matrix via the solidification path. The α -Al phase exhibited the largest 
solubility limit for Ti (approximately 0.6 at.% [ 27 ]) among the M ele -
ments forming the peritectic phase diagram of the Al – M binary systems 
[ 30 ]. Therefore, solute Ti, which has an extremely low diffusivity [ 4 , 
27 ], is expected to play a role in creep strengthening at elevated 
temperatures. 
It is well-known that the Ti addition enhances grain refinement of 
cast Al alloys owing to the formation of numerous fine Al
3
Ti-phase 
particles during solidification. The Al
3
Ti-phase particles enhance the 
nucleation in solidification, resulting in the refined solidification 
microstructure of cast Al alloys with a superior tensile strength [ 31 , 32 ]. 
Many previous studies have focused on the grain refinement by Ti 
addition, whereas there are few repots on the effect of solute Ti on the 
precipitation morphology [ 13 ] and the associated creep properties [ 12 , 
33 ] at elevated temperatures. 
This study was conducted to investigate the effect of the addition of 
trace Ti on the microstructure and high-temperature mechanical per -
formance of a heat-resistant Al – Mg – Zn – Cu – Ni quinary alloy subjected to 
heat treatment. The precipitation morphologies of the T phase in the 
α -Al matrix were systematically characterized in terms of the distribu -
tion of the solute Ti. The high-temperature strength and creep properties 
of Ti-added and Ti-free alloy specimens were examined. These experi -
mental results were used to investigate the effect of solute Ti on the 
mechanical properties of the alloy at elevated temperatures. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Materials and heat treatments 
The nominal composition (at.%) of the experimental alloy specimen 
was Al – 5Mg – 3.5Zn – 2Cu – 2Ni (base alloy), whereas the modified alloy 
was doped with 0.1 at.%) Ti. These alloys are designated as Ti-free and 
Ti-added alloys, respectively. Various pure metals with the overall 
compositions of Al – 5Mg – 3.5Zn – 2Cu – 2Ni and Al – 5Mg – 3.5Zn – 2Cu – 
2Ni – 0.1Ti (at.%) were completely melted via high-frequency heating in 
an Ar atmosphere and maintained at approximately 750 
◦
C for 1800 s. 
Subsequently, the alloy melt was poured into an iron mold to prepare a 
rod-shaped ingot with a diameter of approximately 10 mm (designated 
as a small-sized ingot). The small ingot was used for microstructural 
characterization of the alloy samples aged at different temperatures. 
Large-sized alloy ingots were prepared by vacuum induction melting 
and cast into rods with a diameter of approximately 66 mm. The so -
lidification microstructure would vary depending on the cooling rate in 
the casting process using the different-sized molds. In the study, a slight 
difference in microstructural features between small-sized and large- 
sized ingots was confirmed by preliminary experiments (as shown in 
Fig. S1 ). The chemical compositions of the small and large ingots were 
determined using inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spec -
trometry. The results are summarized in Table 1 . The details of Ti con -
tent in the large-sized ingots are presented in Fig. S1 . The alloy ingots 
were solution-treated at 480 
◦
C for 24 h (determined according to the 
calculated phase diagram [ 24 ]), followed by water quenching. The 
solution-treated samples were artificially aged at a temperature of 200 
or 300 
◦
C for different periods ranging from 0.1 to 1000 h.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Refinement of precipitation morphology by solute Ti during 
isothermal aging 
The first part of this study was conducted to investigate the effect of 
the addition of a trace amount of Ti (with much lower diffusivity) on the 
precipitation morphologies of the T-Al

Al – 5Mg – 3.5Zn – 2Cu – 2Ni quinary alloy at elevated temperatures and 
thus understand its high-temperature mechanical properties. One 
important finding of this study is that the added Ti is distributed in the 
dendritic α -Al phases formed during solidification ( Fig. 1 ). The results 
clearly indicate that the added Ti is partitioned into the α -Al phase 
(rather than in the liquid phase) through a peritectic reaction during 
solidification, thus resulting in the enrichment of Ti within the dendritic 
α -Al phases. Such inhomogeneous precipitation morphologies have been 
observed in other Ti-added Al alloys [ 12 , 35 ]. In this study, no Ti-rich 
intermetallic phase was observed in the solution-treated and subse -
quently aged samples ( Fig. 5 ), thus indicating that most of the added Ti 
was present as a solute element in the α -Al matrix. Notably, the dendritic 
distribution of solute Ti was observed in the sample exposed to a high 
temperature of 480

solute Ti is hardly diffused in the α -Al matrix at 480

sponds well to the low diffusion coefficient of Ti atoms in the α -Al phase, 
as previously reported [ 4 , 27 ]. 
Another important finding is that the precipitation morphology 
varied depending on the solute Ti content in the α -Al matrix of the Ti- 
added alloy ( Fig. 3 ). During aging at 200

added Ti. Therefore, the added Ti could have a slight effect on the 
driving force for the nucleation [ 36 ] of the T-phase precipitates in the 
α -Al matrix. Accordingly, the solute Ti presumably suppresses the

4.2. Improved creep rupture life at elevated temperature 
This study demonstrated the enhanced creep rupture life of the 
Al – 5Mg – 3.5Zn – 2Cu – 2Ni quinary alloy at a high temperature of 200 
◦
C 
by adding a trace amount of Ti ( Fig. 9 ). One key result for understanding 
the enhanced creep properties is the lower minimum creep rate of the Ti- 
added alloy ( Fig. 9 (b)). A similar slope of the creep rate – strain curves 
was observed in the creep acceleration stage of the Ti-added and Ti-free 
alloy specimens ( Fig. 9 (b)), which is consistent with the microstructural 
observations of the creep-ruptured specimens ( Fig. 10 (a) and (d) ). These 
findings indicate that Ti addition has a slight effect on creep degradation 
or damage associated with the formation of cavities or voids [ 40 – 44 ]. 
Therefore, these results indicate that the extended creep rupture time 
could be responsible for the reduction in the creep rate due to the 
addition of Ti. Nanoindentation tests ( Fig. 7 ) confirmed that the refined 
precipitation morphologies caused by Ti addition contributed to the 
strengthening of the materials (indicating that numerous fine pre -
cipitates can increase internal stress during deformation). Therefore, the 
stable fine T-phase precipitates dominantly contribute to the enhanced 
creep resistance in the creep transition stage ( Fig. 9 (b)), thus resulting in 
the extended creep rupture life of the Ti-added alloy. Notably, solute Ti 
atoms with a much lower diffusivity are assumed to interact with dis -
locations, thereby resulting in suppressed deformation during creep 
(creep strengthening [ 26 ]). However, the creep strengthening mecha -
nism of solute Ti remains unclear. To fully understand the mechanism of 
creep resistance enhancement by solute Ti in the Al – Mg – Zn – Cu – Ni 
quinary alloy, the temperature and stress dependence of the creep 
curves of the Ti-added and Ti-free alloy specimens should be 
investigated.
