1. Introduction 
The concept of high-entropy alloy (HEA), initially proposed in 2014, 
has opened up a new avenue for the exploration of the advanced alloys 
with promising properties, such as high strength [ 1 ], good ductility [ 2 , 
3 ] and superior corrosion resistance [ 4 , 5 ]. Among the reported HEAs to 
date, which typically possess one of the face-centered cubic (FCC), 
body-centered cubic (BCC) and the hexagonal close-packed (HCP) 
structures [ 6 – 9 ], their outstanding mechanical properties in the as-cast 
state have been demonstrated. For example, the single BCC-structured

3.3. Strengthening mechanisms 
In theory, there are two approaches for boosting the strength of a 
metal. The first one involves eliminating internal dislocations and other 
defects in order to approach the theoretical strength. The second one 
entails intentionally introducing a large number of defects into the metal 
so as to impede the movement of dislocations. However, the high 
strength achieved by the former is inherently unstable and extremely 
susceptible to manipulation effects and surface conditions, whilst once 
dislocations have been created the strength is significantly diminished. 
Therefore, the introduction of defects is the usual convectional approach 
of strengthening metals, namely, alloy strengthening, fine grain 
strengthening, martensitic strengthening, precipitation strengthening, 
etc, which are primarily determined by the interaction of various types

It is demonstrated that heterogeneous structures containing both 
hard and soft regions can offer enhanced hetero-deformation induced 
(HDI) strengthening [ 35 – 37 ]. In this regard, the hard brittle Laves 
phases can be considered as hard regions compared to the FCC phase of 
the matrix. HDI strengthening can generally be described in terms of 
back stress, which refers to the interaction force arising from the 
inconsistent deformation of the soft-hard units within the material 
during deformation. In general, the soft and the hard regions contribute 
to the deformation and the strength, respectively. The presence of back 
stress induces an increase in strength within the softer regions, resulting 
in a synergistic strengthening and an overall enhancement of yield 
strength.
