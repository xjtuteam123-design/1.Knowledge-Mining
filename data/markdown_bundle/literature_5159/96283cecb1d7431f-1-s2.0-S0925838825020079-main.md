ABSTRACT
The high-temperature dual-phase anisotropic crystal plasticity model has been established to investigate the 
anisotropic behavior and microstructural evolution mechanisms of Ti-6Al-4V alloy under elevated temperature 
conditions. The model was rigorously validated using uniaxial hot tensile testing and comprehensive micro -
structural analysis, confirming its predictive accuracy. The findings reveal that the yield strength is predomi -
nantly influenced by the dislocation density within the prismatic slip systems of the α phase. Moreover, a strong 
positive correlation between dislocation density and slip shear strain was observed, highlighting their mutual 
dependence in governing deformation mechanisms. Under diagonal loading conditions, regions of high dislo -
cation density exhibited distinct textural features, further elucidating the material ’ s anisotropic response. These 
results provide a deeper understanding of the interrelations among dislocation density, yield strength, and slip 
mechanics, while emphasizing the pivotal role of texture in shaping the anisotropic properties of Ti-6Al-4V alloy 
under high-temperature conditions.
1. Introduction
The application of lightweight alloys in the aerospace industry 
significantly reduces component weight and improves service perfor -
mance [1] . Compared with aluminum and magnesium alloys, titanium 
alloys exhibit superior toughness, strength, corrosion resistance, and 
wear resistance, accounting for over 60 % of lightweight alloys used in 
aerospace engineering [2 – 4] . Particularly, the Ti-6Al-4V alloy, which 
contains both α and β phases, is widely utilized in manufacturing engine 
fans, compressor discs, blades, and critical load-bearing components 
such as beams, joints, and spacers in aircraft structures [5,6] . However, 
during processing and service, Ti-6Al-4V alloys are subjected to multi -
directional loading at elevated temperatures, leading to complex 
micromechanical behaviors and pronounced plastic anisotropy.
In practical applications, polycrystalline Ti-6Al-4V alloys exhibit 
mechanical anisotropy in terms of elastic modulus, plastic flow, yield 
strength, springback, tensile formability, and damage evolution 
depending on the loading direction [7 – 10] . Yield strength, a critical 
property influencing the service performance of load-bearing 
components, serves as an essential parameter for assessing mechanical 
anisotropy in alloy sheets [11] . Further investigations indicate that the 
yield strength is strongly governed by dislocation density, exhibiting a 
direct positive correlation — higher dislocation density increases yield 
strength, while lower dislocation density reduces it [12,13] . In addition, 
dislocation motion induces slip shear strain, and interactions between 
dislocations affect crystal reorientation, thereby leading to texture 
evolution [14,15] . However, establishing a quantitative relationship 
between dislocation density, microstructural evolution, and mechanical 
properties presents certain challenges, which hinder its widespread 
application. These difficulties primarily arise from the complex multi -
phase microstructure of Ti-6Al-4V alloys, which makes it challenging to 
investigate various aspects through experiments and simplified simula -
tions. Therefore, further in-depth research and exploration are therefore 
required.
To enhance the formability and service performance of titanium al -
loys, a detailed investigation into their anisotropic behavior is essential. 
Titanium alloys are widely recognized for exhibiting heterogeneous 
deformation, primarily due to the pronounced anisotropy in the plastic

heterogeneous deformation leads to non-uniform microstructural 
development, increasing the difficulty of hot working. Consequently, 
many researchers have conducted investigations into the anisotropic 
behavior of titanium alloys. Baral et al. [17] assessed the plastic 
behavior of CP-Ti through uniaxial tension, compression, and 
plane-strain tension tests. They found that material strength increases 
from rolling direction (RD) to transverse direction (TD) to normal di -
rection, while hardening rates remain consistent across these directions. 
Zhang et al. [18] investigated the plastic anisotropy of Ti-6Al-4V alloys 
processed via electron beam direct energy deposition, revealing greater 
work-hardening rates and strain-hardening indices in the horizontal 
direction. Yazar et al. [19] conducted a comprehensive investigation 
into the deformation behavior of CP-Ti under monotonic tension and 
various transient mechanical loading conditions. They observed a large 
planar anisotropy in the dwell fatigue response of commercially pure 
titanium with split-TD type texture. Wu et al. [20] studied the 
high-temperature anisotropy and microstructure evolution mechanisms 
of the rolled TA32 titanium alloy sheet. They found that the TD sample 
exhibited the largest peak strength, while the diagonal direction sample 
showed the highest elongation. Li et al. [21] conducted a detailed 
investigation into the effect of α texture on the anisotropy of yield 
strength in laser-directed energy-deposited (LDEDed) 
Ti-6Al-2Zr-1Mo-1V (TA15) alloy, considering the solid phase trans -
formation. They found that the crystallographic orientation of the 
textured α phase is the key factor influencing the anisotropy of yield 
strength in LDEDed TA15 alloy. Zhang et al. [22] conducted a series of 
in-situ tensile tests on specimens with different sizes and orientations to 
investigate the size effect on the anisotropic deformation behavior of 
α -Ti sheet. They found that the tensile behavior of RD and TD is aniso -
tropic, and the plastic anisotropy is enhanced as the specimen size de -
creases, in terms of fracture strain, r-value, and strain heterogeneity. 
Although existing research has explored the properties of anisotropy, 
none have addressed the influence of dislocation density on it.
In addition to experimental studies, constitutive models have been 
widely used to analyze and characterize anisotropic behavior in tita -
nium alloys. The existing constitutive models are divided into consti -
tutive models based on macroscopic phenomena and physics-based 
constitutive models, both of which have been extensively studied. For 
instance, De Sotto et al. [23] proposed a temperature and 
rate-dependent constitutive model incorporating strength asymmetry 
and nonlinearity. Yang et al. [24] comprehensively characterized and 
modeled the evolving asymmetric and anisotropic hardening behavior 
of CP-Ti sheets under monotonic and reverse loading, towards precision 
forming of high-performance and lightweight CP-Ti components. Agius 
et al. [25] proposed a constitutive plasticity model using an anisotropic 
yield surface and a nonlinear kinematic isotropic hardening model for 
additively manufactured Ti-6Al-4V materials manufactured by the se -
lective laser melting process. The model can accurately simulate the 
anisotropic behavior of materials under monotonic tension and cyclic 
elastic-plastic loading. Kim et al. [26] investigated the anisotropic ma -
terial behavior of CP-Ti sheet for the evolutionary plastic anisotropy and 
differential hardening in this study. The force-displacement curves of RD 
and TD for the uniaxial tensile test were well predicted by both 
Hill48-NAFR and Yld2k-AFR models. Although the constitutive model 
effectively captures the anisotropic behavior of the material, it is unable 
to describe the microstructural evolution and the underlying in -
teractions within the material.
The crystal plasticity model, as a robust and well-established tool, 
enables precise evaluation of the material ’ s mechanical response across 
multiple scales while capturing its anisotropic behavior [27] . For 
instance, Somlo et al. [28] studied the anisotropic behavior of laser 
powder bed fusion Ti-6Al-4V under uniaxial tension through crystal 
plasticity simulation. The slip activity of the basal slip system and the 
prismatic slip system was investigated. Riyad et al. [29] proposed a 
simulation framework based on multilevel crystal plasticity, i.e., the 
correlated structural visco-plastic self-consistent formulation to simu -
late the mechanical response and microstructural evolution of Ti-6Al-4V 
with α -lath/lamellar microstructure. The constructed CS-VPSC model 
can predict the plastic anisotropy/asymmetry of Ti-6Al-4V and the 
consequent texture evolution through a set of intrinsic hardening pa -
rameters. Fan et al. [30] established a crystal plasticity finite element 
model (CPFEM) to accurately predict the anisotropic deformation 
behavior of TA32 titanium alloy rolled sheet during hot tension. It was 
found that the proposed model accurately predicts the microstructural 
evolution of stress-strain response, r-value and dynamic recrystallization 
of TA32 alloy during hot tension along the rolling direction and trans -
verse direction. Liu et al. [31] developed a reduced-order crystal plas -
ticity finite element model to study the effects of microstructural 
morphology and crystal texture on the mechanical anisotropy of selec -
tive laser melted Ti-6Al-4V. It was found that hierarchical and equiaxed 
CPFE models with the same crystal texture exhibit the same mechanical 
anisotropy.
In this research, a high-temperature dual-phase anisotropic crystal 
plasticity model was developed to systematically explore the anisotropic 
mechanical behavior and microstructural evolution of Ti-6Al-4V alloy. 
Guided by experimental results from multi-directional uniaxial tensile 
tests and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis, the model 
revealed that dislocation density is a critical determinant of yield 
strength, particularly within the prismatic slip systems of the α phase. A 
robust positive correlation between dislocation density and slip shear 
strain was established, emphasizing their interdependent role in gov -
erning deformation mechanisms. Additionally, the evolution of distinct 
textures was observed in regions with high dislocation density, under -
scoring the influence of dislocation activity on microstructural anisot -
ropy. These insights not only advance our understanding of Ti-6Al-4V 
behavior but also provide a practical framework for optimizing its per -
formance in aerospace applications.

3. Crystal plasticity simulation
The inverse pole figure (IPF) of Ti-6Al-4V alloy prior to loading is 
shown in Fig. 2 . The volume fraction of α phase is 97.86 %, while the β 
phase accounts for 2.14 %. The white arrows indicate the β phase re -
gions. The average grain size of the α phase is measured as 8.25 μ m, and 
for the β phase, it is 3.02 μ m.
The three-dimensional representative volume element (RVE) was 
constructed based on the EBSD test results for crystal plasticity simu -
lation. The grain morphology, grain size, and orientation relationships 
of the α phase were incorporated into the initial RVE model. The position 
relation and orientation relation are clearly defined in it. The orientation 
and spatial distribution of the β phase were further introduced into the 
model based on EBSD data. The orientation relationship between the 
two phases should be clarified in the dual phase model so that the 
anisotropy and other properties can be explored in the future. To ach -
ieve the above objectives, match the orientation distribution function 
(ODF) obtained from EBSD experiments to the two phases. Replace the 
orientation relationship within the model with the ODF obtained from 
the experiment. Set the minimum threshold for error and iterate within 
this range until the texture function matches within the accuracy range. 
The above 3D representative volume element was constructed using the 
open source software DREAM.3D [34] . Fig. 3 shows the RVE model, 
which contains 64 elements (Fourier grids) in each direction and a total 
of 1111 grains. Fig. 3 (a) shows the grain boundary distribution. Fig. 3 (b) 
shows the phase distribution.
Fig. 4 compares the pole figures obtained from the RVE model and 
EBSD experiments. The distribution state of the model and the experi -

Fig. 5 illustrates the phase distribution, where the simulated α phase

4. Results and discussion
4.1. The mechanical anisotropy
The stress-strain curves under different loading conditions are shown 
in Fig. 7 (a)-(c). At all temperatures, the stress values along the diagonal 
direction (DD) are the lowest at a strain value of 0.1. In contrast, the 
stress values are highest when loading occurs along the rolling direction 
(RD). For instance, at 973 K, the stress decreases as the loading direction 
transitions from RD to DD and increases when transitioning from DD to 
the transverse direction (TD). The similarity in the slopes of stress-strain 
curves during early plastic deformation at different temperatures 
( Fig. 7 ) stems from the limited strain range and the dominance of 
dislocation interactions within α -phase prismatic slip systems.
The yield strength values under varying loading conditions are pre -
sented in Fig. 7 (d). The yield strength exhibits a clear directional 
dependence at all tested temperatures. Specifically, the yield strength is 
highest along the RD and lowest along the DD. Additionally, yield 
strength decreases as the temperature increases. This confirms that both 
directional dependence and temperature significantly influence the 
mechanical response of Ti-6Al-4V alloy.
The stress distribution contour maps at the yield state are shown in 
Fig. 8 . For the α phase, the stress distribution appears irregular, as 
illustrated in Fig. 8 (d), where non-uniform stress regions (denoted as M) 
are prominent. In contrast, the β phase exhibits significantly lower stress 
levels. However, stress concentrations are evident at the phase bound -
aries, particularly in regions labeled P. At the same temperature, the 
stress distribution along DD is the most uniform, with reduced stress 
concentrations compared to other directions. As the temperature in -
creases, the inhomogeneous stress distribution and stress concentration 
effects diminish.
In summary, the stress state and yield strength exhibit pronounced 
anisotropy at all temperatures. The lowest yield strength and the most 
uniform stress distribution are observed along DD, while RD demon -
strates the highest yield strength. Given that the response at 973 K is 
representative, subsequent analyses focus on this temperature to un -
cover the mechanisms underlying anisotropy induced by different 
loading directions.

rections at 973 K. The red dotted line indicates the initial dislocation

In the α phase, the dislocation densities of the { 10 11 } < 11 20 > , 
{ 10 11 } < 11 23 > , and { 11 22 } < 11 23 > slip systems exhibit minimal 
changes compared to their initial values, suggesting negligible contri -
butions to yield strength. In contrast, the dislocation densities of the 
{ 0001 } < 11 20 > and { 10 10 } < 11 20 > slip systems increase signifi -
cantly. When loading along the RD, the dislocation density of { 0001 } <
11 20 > is slightly higher than that of { 10 10 } < 11 20 > . However, as the 
loading direction shifts towards TD, the { 0001 } < 11 20 > dislocation 
density decreases sharply, while { 10 10 } < 11 20 > remains consistently 
high. The { 10 10 } < 11 20 > exhibits the highest dislocation density 
along RD and the lowest along DD, a trend consistent with the observed 
yield strength variations.
While the α phase dominates the mechanical response due to its high 
volume fraction (97.86 %) and active prismatic slip systems, the β phase 
(2.14 % volume fraction) plays a secondary but non-negligible role. Our 
results indicate that: The β phase exhibits increased dislocation density 
under loading. However, the dislocation density in the β phase is an 
order of magnitude lower than that in the α phase ’ s prismatic systems. 
Stress concentrations are observed at α / β phase boundaries ( Fig. 8 ), 
suggesting that the β phase indirectly influences local deformation by 
altering stress partitioning and dislocation motion in the α phase. These 
interfacial effects may contribute to macroscale anisotropy by promot -
ing heterogeneous strain distribution.
In the β phase, the dislocation density of { 123 } < 111 > , 
{ 112 } < 111 > , and { 110 } < 111 > increases significantly when loaded 
in any direction. When loading is applied along the RD, the dislocation 
density reaches its highest value. However, when the loading direction 
changes, the variation in dislocation density differs across the various 
slip systems. When the loading direction changes from RD to TD, the 
dislocation densities of the { 123 } < 111 > and { 112 } < 111 > slip 
systems initially decrease and then increase. The dislocation density is 
lowest when the loading direction is along the DD. The variation trend is 
similar to that of yield strength. However, the dislocation density of {

5. Conclusions
A comprehensive investigation into the plastic anisotropy and me -
chanical response of Ti-6Al-4V alloy under high-temperature conditions 
was conducted through a series of uniaxial hot tensile tests and crystal 
plasticity simulations. The main findings are refined as follows: 
1. The high-temperature dual-phase anisotropic crystal plasticity 
model was developed based on uniaxial hot tensile tests and micro -
structural characterization data. The model effectively addresses 
thermo-mechanical coupling and captures the anisotropic behavior 
of Ti-6Al-4V alloy, with its reliability and accuracy validated through 
comparison with experimental results.

strength reaches 542 MPa along the RD — the highest among all 
directions — while dropping to 424 MPa along the DD, the lowest
