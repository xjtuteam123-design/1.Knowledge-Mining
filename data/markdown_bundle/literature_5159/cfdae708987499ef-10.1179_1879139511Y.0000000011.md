Development of NIMS burner rig and cyclic
testing of nickel base superalloys
K. Matsumoto *, Y. Koizumi, K. Kawagishi and H. Harada
A new burner rig for cyclic and hot corrosion testing of Ni base superalloys and high temperature
coatings has been developed at the National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS) in Japan. The
concepts of the rig design include: use of paraffin as fuel, easy operation for materials research
and multispecimen testing capability per test run. The burner rig consists of combustion block,
corrosion solution injection block, furnace block, specimen block, controller block and outdoor
facility block. During verification testing, it was confirmed that the rig had accurate specimen
temperature control within ¡1uC from 600 to 1100 uC and automated easy operation. Cyclic
testing of several Ni base superalloys was conducted. The results from 50 cycle testing indicated
that new NIMS alloy for 1700 uC class gas turbine had higher oxidation resistance than CMSX-4.
This new burner rig testing capability at NIMS can be very useful for future oxidation, hot corrosion
and coating durability studies.

Introduction
The National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS) has
developed the ﬁfth generation single crystal superalloy
with world’s highest temperature capability of 1100 uC,
new coating materials and next generation wrought
superalloys for turbine disks.1–5 NIMS has been working
with private companies such as gas turbine manufacturers
to promote research into practical use of these high
temperature materials and coatings. The Rolls-Royce
Centre of Excellence for Aerospace Materials, established
in NIMS in 2006, is also promoting joint research to
exploit the materials technology for practical engine
applications. In order to evaluate environmental resis-
tance characteristics of advanced Ni base superalloys and
coating materials, NIMS has developed a burner rig
facility capable of high te mperature oxidation and

NIMS burner rig development
Design concept
There are various types of burner rigs to meet different
research requirements or testing conditions. For heavy
industry manufacturers, actual combustor was used to
evaluate environmental behaviour of coating/superalloy
systems.
6–9 As NIMS burner rig will be used primarily
for materials research, the following features were
considered for the rig design:
(i) use of paraffin as fuel to simulate gas turbine
engine combustion environment
(ii) use of fuel burner with high speed combustion
gas to achieve easy operation under atmo-
spheric pressure
(iii) a carousel accommodating 12 specimens of
9 mm in diameter and 65 mm in length for
multispecimen testing
(iv) cyclic oxidation testing capable of achieving
maximum specimen temperature of 1100 uC
followed by forced air cooling
(v) hot corrosion testing by adding a corrosion solution
of aqueous NaCl or Na2SO4 at specimen tempera-
tures of 700 and 900uC for up to 500 h test duration.
Basic rig structure
In order to achieve the above design requirements, the
basic structure of the burner rig consists of six blocks, as
illustrated in Fig. 1, i.e. combustion block, corrosion
solution injection block, furnace block, specimen block,
controller block and outdoor facility block.
The combustion block includes a fuel burner, a preheater
for combustion air, blowers and a burner control unit. The
high speed fuel burner can reach 150 m s 21 jet ﬂow at the
burner outlet. In addition, the burner has superior stirring
and uniform temperature distribution characteristics. The
fuel burner is controlled by the proportional pressure
control method, in which an air control damper is
controlled by a digital programme controller for achieving
the desired specimen temperature and then the fuel pressure
is proportionally controlled by a fuel ratio regulator.
The corrosion solution injection block has chemical
tanks, pumps and injection tubes. The injection quantity

ladder diagram for automated cyclic testing.
The outdoor facility block includes a fuel tank and
exhaust pipes.

Cyclic testing of superalloys
Experimental procedure
Cyclic tests were carried out at 1100 uC on MarM247
(CC), PWA1480, CMSX-4, TMS-82 z and TMS-XXX
using the burner rig. Nominal compositions of these
alloys are listed in Table 4. The TMS-82 z SC super-
alloy was developed by NIMS and Toshiba. The TMS-
XXX alloy is a turbine blade and vane material for
1700uC ultraefﬁcient gas turbine being designed for 56–
60% (HHV) thermal efﬁciency combined cycle power
plants. The fuel used in the tests was commercial
parafﬁn with 5 ppm S content. The test conditions
included 1100uC specimen set temperature, 1 h exposure
in furnace under atmospheric pressure and 30 min
cooling out of furnace by fan cooling to around
100uC. The tests were operated by programmable logic
controller with an automatic sequence. The typical cyclic
temperature proﬁle is shown in Fig. 6. It can be noted
that the specimen temperature could be ramped from
100uC to 1100 uC in 7 min. The specimen temperature
measurement accuracy was ¡1uC at 1100 uC. After
predetermined thermal cycles, the specimen mass change
(mg cm
22) was determined by the difference between
specimen weight before and after testing using an
analytical balance.
Results
Figure 7 shows the mass change per unit surface area of
the alloys tested using the 1 h cycle proﬁle. The mass
increase of the specimens corresponded to the oxidation
scale formation and the mass loss to the scale spallation.
In the early stage, a large weight gain during the ﬁrst ﬁve
cycles followed by severe weight loss was observed in
conventional cast alloy MarM247 due to fast oxidation
and spallation. A clear difference in mass loss can be
identiﬁed between the superalloys after 30 cycles. In this
ﬁgure, test data from a TMS-XXX superalloy developed
by NIMS were added for comparison. It can be seen that
the TMS-XXX alloy has a higher oxidation resistance
tendency than CMSX-4 in the 50 cycles. It seems that
the clarifying TMS-XXX composition effect for the
oxidation resistance is the future investigation.
The mass change proﬁles are very similar to those of
cyclic oxidation testing at 1100 uC using electrical
furnace in air.
1,10 This correlation indicates that the
NIMS burner rig has excellent temperature control of
test specimens. As a result, cyclic test data generated by
NIMS burner rig can provide a good reference for future
hot corrosion investigations of superalloys and coatings.
Conclusions
A new burner rig has been developed at the NIMS in
order to conduct high temperature cyclic testing and hot
corrosion testing for Ni base superalloys and coated
materials in a combustion gas environment. Concepts of
the burner rig design include use of parafﬁn as fuel, easy
operation for materials research and multispecimen
testing per test run. In order to implement these
concepts, the rig has a high speed fuel burner, a
corrosion solution injection device, a furnace, a rotating
carousel accommodating 12 specimens and a controller
providing computer programmed automated operation.
From veriﬁcation test results, it was conﬁrmed that the
burner rig had accurate specimen temperature control
within ¡1uC from 600 to 1100 uC under injection of
distilled water with maximum 50 mL min
21 at carousel
rotating speeds of 10 and 20 rev min 21. Cyclic tests of
several Ni base superalloys with automated operations
were conducted. The results indicated that there was a
clear difference in mass loss between the superalloys
after 50 cycles, with TMS-XXX NIMS alloy having a
higher oxidation resistance tendency than CMSX-4. It is
expected that cyclic test data generated with NIMS
burner rig can provide a good reference for future hot
corrosion studies of superalloys and coatings.
