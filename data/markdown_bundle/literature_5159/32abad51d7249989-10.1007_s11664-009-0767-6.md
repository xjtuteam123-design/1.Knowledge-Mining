This study characterized the temperature-dependent constitutive parameters
(yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, elastic modulus, strain hardening
exponent) from the mechanical behavior of ﬁve high-temperature solders,
95Sn-5Sb, 95Pb-5In, 90Pb-10Sn, 92.5Pb-5Sn-2.5Ag, and 93Pb-3Sn-2In-2Ag,
chosen such that T
m > 518 K. To model appropriately their mechanical
responses under high-temperature thermal cycling, where the temperatures
exceed 473 K, the material /C213s parameters must be determined as a function of
temperature. Uni-axial tensile tests were, therefore, carried out between
298 K and 473 K to determine the constitutive behavior of each solder.
95Sn-5Sb exhibited the highest strength over the temperature range tested
except near 473 K. Pb-based alloys with a higher degree of solid solution
(>5%) showed greater strengthening than those primarily strengthened by
coarse precipitates. Additionally, microstructure changes in 90Pb-10Sn and
95Sn-5Sb were shown to be responsible for unexpected mechanical behavior at
elevated temperatures.
Key words: High-temperature solders, constitutive relations, mechanical
behavior, microstructures, solid solution, elastic modulus,
ultimate tensile strength, yield strength, strain hardening,
Sn-Sb, Pb-Sn, Pb-In, Pb-Sn-Ag, Pb-Sn-Ag-In
INTRODUCTION
For decades, manufacturers have used eutectic
lead–tin in electronic assemblies because of its
ideal melting temperature (456 K) and superior
mechanical properties.
1–6 However, as electronics
and sensors become integrated into higher tem-
perature environments, such as those associated
with deep-well oil drilling, higher melting temper-
ature alternatives are needed. In oil well drilling
and exploration, studies have shown that signiﬁ-
cant reserves exist below 10,000 ft, where package
temperatures can exceed 473 K, putting signiﬁcant
stress on the electronics and electronics packaging.
Solder joints subjected to high-temperature ther-
mal cycling (HTTC) experience strains resulting
from the thermal expansion and contraction mis-
matches between the component, solder, and sub-
strate. Owing to the ductile nature of solder alloys,
thermal stresses often lead to signiﬁcant strains
which can limit the lifetime of the solder joint.
7,8
Additionally, the mechanical properties of the
solder joint change as a function of temperature
because of their high homologous temperature in
the HTTC range. 9–11 Activation of additional slip
systems and a continuously changing microstruc-
ture at elevated temperatures have a signiﬁcant
impact on the solder /C213s mechanical response to
thermal stresses.
Selection of the appropriate solder alloy must be
made such that its melting temperature is signiﬁ-
cantly higher than the HTTC range but low enough

Starting with a Pb-based solder containing greater
than 90% Pb for a high melting temperature, ele-
ments are added to the base solder to alter its
microstructure and thereby improve its mechanical
properties. The solubility and reactivity of these
elements change the nucleation behavior of the
alloy, thus changing its microstructure. Micro-
structural features such as grain size, particle
coherency, and the presence of intermediate phases
and intermetallic compounds all determine the ease
with which a dislocation will glide through the lat-
tice. Additional strains on the lattice structure from
misﬁt solute atoms create stress ﬁelds, requiring
higher stresses to continue dislocation motion for
slip deformation. Variations of these structural
impediments will determine the magnitude and rate
at which plastic deformation occurs.
With government regulations mandating indus-
try toward Pb-free solders, it is imperative to
consider Pb-free alternatives, even for high-
temperature applications.
12 Despite lower melting
temperatures than high Pb-solders, high Sn-solders
have good solderability and higher strength, due to
their tetragonal crystal structure as compared to
the more ductile face-centered-cubic (FCC) struc-
ture of Pb-based alloys. However, Sn-solders have
shown the formation of thick brittle intermetallic
compounds at the solder joint interface, which can
limit joint reliability.
The aim of this work was to establish the con-
stitutive parameters (elastic and plastic proper-
ties) of several high-temperature solders through
uni-axial tensile testing as a function of tempera-
ture from 298 K to 473 K. This work only char-
acterized the properties of bulk solders and did
not consider factors such as reactions with joining
materials, which may affect the mechanical per-
formance of actual solder joints. Instead, the work
focused on speciﬁc microstructural features that
can explain solder performance at elevated tem-
peratures. Data garnered from this work will be
used in future studies to estimate the fatigue life
of solder joints under HTTC through ﬁnite ele-
ment simulations.
EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE
All experiments were performed on 3.175 mm-
diameter solder wire purchased from the Indium
Corporation (1676 Lincoln Ave., Utica, NY, USA).
Table I shows the solders tested in this study, along
with their chemical compositions. We examined the
microstructure of the wire by embedding the solder
in an epoxy resin and polishing the cross-section.
Both mechanical polishing and chemical etching
were used to expose grain boundaries and examine
the different phases of the solders. A digital circular-
intercept method was implemented to measure grain
size, with a minimum of 400 intercepts per sample.
Mechanical tensile tests were performed on an
858 Tabletop MTS servohydraulic machine, with a
5 kN load cell and self-tightening wedge grips. The
overall sample length was 50 mm. Unless otherwise
noted, a strain rate of 0.05 s
/C0 1 was used for all
testing. Owing to the highly plastic nature of solder
alloys at room temperature, two methods were used
to measure strain. In accordance with American
Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) Standard
E8-04, a 12.7 mm gage length, high-temperature,
extensometer was used to determine accurately the
elastic modulus and yield strength (YS) in the
0–10% strain range. For larger displacements up to
100 mm, a linear variable displacement transducer
was used. Data points in mechanical testing were
averaged from at least three measurements, except
some data points for the indium-containing solders,
for which one measurement was taken. An MTS 651
environmental chamber was used to test the sam-
ples at 0.7 T
m and 473 K. Samples were held at
elevated temperatures for a minimum of 1 h prior to
being tested.

Sn increases until 453 K, where 5% Sb can be com-
pletely soluble in Sn and 95Sn-5Sb becomes a single-
phase solid solution, as shown in Fig. 2j. With the

not observed for temperatures up to 433 K, despite
the considerable driving force of increasing temper-

this temperature range. Figure 2j shows the struc-

20 K over the solubility limit of SnSb, the structure
now shows signiﬁcant grain coarsening, where the
average grain size was 117 lm. Grain growth in this
alloy coincided with dissolution of SnSb. Figure 2j
also shows that the grain boundaries have become
more faceted at 473 K than the grain boundaries in
the as-received sample.

Microstructure–Mechanical Property Relationship
The change in the mechanical properties of alloys
is a consequence of evolution of the microstructure
at elevated temperatures. Most solder alloys expe-
rience a rapidly changing microstructure during
component thermal cycling because of their high
homologous temperatures. The stability of precipi-
tate phases, concentrations of solute atoms in the
matrix, and grain sizes change with temperature,
impacting upon strain hardening and the evolution
of strength with temperature.
Figure 4a shows the elastic modulus, tensile
strength, and YS of 92.5Pb-5Sn-2.5Ag as a function
of temperature. As noted earlier, the microstructure
of 92.5Pb-5Sn-2.5Ag remained stable up to 473 K,
because Ag
3Sn prevented grain boundary migra-
tion. This provided a good baseline material for
isolation of the effect of temperature on the
mechanical response of a high-Pb solder. The result
shows both the UTS and YS to decrease linearly
with temperature, assuming a stable microstruc-
ture. Similarly, 93Pb-3Sn-2Ag-2In displayed a sta-
ble microstructure up to 473 K, due to the presence
of Ag
3Sn and Ag segregation. Figure 4b shows the
UTS and YS to have a linear dependence on tem-
perature for 93Pb-3Sn-2Ag-2In. Similarly, the UTS
and YS of 95Pb-5In had a linear dependence from
298 K to 473 K, as seen in Fig. 4c. However, YS was
lower than expected at 473 K, possibly due to the
large grain size.
Figure 4d shows the elastic modulus, tensile
strength, and YS of 95Sn-5Sb as a function of tem-
perature. It can be seen that both the UTS and YS
at 473 K are signiﬁcantly higher than the linear
projection of the ﬁrst two data points. The greater
than expected strength can be traced back to the
changing microstructure, discussed earlier in this
paper. It appears that the lattice strains induced
from an increased Sb concentration are a more
efﬁcient strengthening mechanism than coarse
SnSb precipitates.
A similar mechanical response was observed in
90Pb-10Sn. Below 408 K, 90Pb-10Sn is primarily
composite strengthened by the large b-Sn phase.
Above 408 K, 90Pb-10Sn changes into a single-
phase solid solution, because of the increasing
solubility of Sn with temperature. Therefore, the
primary strengthening mechanism changes from
composite to solute atom strengthening. Again, it
can be seen in Fig. 4e that the UTS and YS were
higher than the linear projection of the ﬁrst two
data points. As before, the lattice strains from solute
atoms seem to be a more efﬁcient strengthening
mechanism than coarse precipitates.

Strain Hardening
Figure 5a shows the strain hardening behavior of
the Pb alloys as a function of temperature. As
expected for FCC metals, the strain hardening expo-
nent decreases as a function of temperature.
18 At
298 K, 95Pb-5In shows the greatest strain hardening,
followed by 93Pb-3Sn-2Ag-2In, 92.5Pb-5Sn-2.5Ag,
and 90Pb-10Sn. The two alloys with the highest
concentrations of solute atoms in solution with the
matrix (95Pb-5In and 93Pb-3Sn-2Ag-2In) showed
the largest degree of strain hardening. Additionally,
a trend was observed where, at each applicable
temperature, single-phase alloys showed greater
strain hardening than did multi-phase alloys. Sol-
ute atoms in higher concentrations appeared to
have a more effective strain hardening mechanism
than a mixture of coarse precipitates and dilute
solute concentrations.

the limited solubility of Sb in the Sn matrix. Beyond

linearly with temperature, due to the dissolution of

strain hardening exponent is, in fact, expected to
decrease, since there is accelerated grain growth
and a decrease in dislocation density.
CONCLUSION
This study focused on the measurement of
mechanical properties as part of the development of
accurate, ﬁnite-element analysis (FEA), fatigue life
models to predict the lifetime of high-temperature
solder joints. The temperature-dependent elastic
and plastic deformation parameters of several high-
temperature solders were measured and compared
