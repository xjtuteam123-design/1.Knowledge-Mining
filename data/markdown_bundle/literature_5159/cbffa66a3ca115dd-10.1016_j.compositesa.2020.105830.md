S.T. Mileiko,* A.A. Kolchin, S.N. Galyshev, O.F. Shakhlevich, V.M. Prokopenko
Institute of Solid State Physics of Russian Academy of Sciences, 142432 Chernogolovka Moscow

Abstract
Ultra-high temperature metal matrix composites are to be constructed using a sufficiently strong 
refractory matrix. Oxide fibres are primary candidates to reinforce molybdenum matrix by the 
internal crystallisation method. However, strong molybdenum alloys have not been used as the 
matrix until recently because the matrix preparation requires the alloys in form of foil and wire, 
which are not available. Hence, a way to strengthen pure molybdenum is now found through 
introducing reinforcing phases into it on the stage of matrix preparation prior to its reinforcement by 
oxide fibres. This communication presents the results of first experiments which show that such 
modification of the matrix yields composites of high strength at temperatures up to 1400oC. The 
composites are characterized by sufficiently high fracture toughness at room temperature.
Keywords: A. Metal Matrix Composites; B. Fracture toughness; B. High-temperature properties; D. 
Mechanical testing. 
1. Introduction
Development of high temperature materials with the use temperature much higher than that 
characteristic of presently used nickel superalloys is obviously imperative. At present, there are 
three mainstreams in this field: 
1. Molybdenum alloys.

2. Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs). 
3. Metal matrix composites (MMCs).
Molybdenum alloys have been under development for a few recent decades. A large number of 
publications present the results obtained in this research direction, to cite just a few of them 
[1,2,3,4,5]. Certainly, the main problem of this type of heat resistant alloys is a conflict between the 
strength (creep resistance) and fracture toughness, typical of metal alloys [6,7]. 
Ceramic matrix composites [8,9] are excellent materials for structural elements of gas turbines such 
as vanes and shrouds in high-pressure turbine lines in the combustor. However, they can hardly be 
regarded as materials for such heavily loaded elements as turbine blades because of their relatively 
low fracture toughness. 
Metal matrix composites with brittle fibres can be designed in such a manner as to obtain a material 
with fracture toughness higher than that of the metal matrix [6,10]. This is also true of oxide-
fibre/molybdenum-matrix composites [10,11,12]. Such composites are obtained by the internal 
crystallisation method (ICM), which is actually the crystallisation of oxide melts in the continuous 
cylindrical channels in the molybdenum matrix prepared by diffusion bonding of foil and wires of 
technically pure molybdenum [6,13]. 
Composites produced by ICM can be much more oxidation resistant than molybdenum, provided the 
oxide reinforcement contains specific chemical elements capable of forming on the composite 
surface complex oxides with sufficiently low vapor pressure. Yttrium and lanthanides can be such 
element [14,15]. This makes ICM suitable for producing heat-resistant composites. However, the 
drawback of the method is that foil and wire necessary to prepare the molybdenum matrix, made of 
a molybdenum alloy wanted, are not available.

To resolve the problem, it was decided to properly strengthen molybdenum on the stage of matrix 
preparation. The idea came from the experiment, in which the pure molybdenum matrix with 
continuous channels in it was infiltrated with liquid molybdenum disilicide MoSi2 [16,17]. It 
occurred that the channels were filled with the fibres composed of Mo3Si and the matrix was 
strengthened by Mo3Si particles.
This paper presents the first results of experiments, in which the matrix was filled with Mo5SiB2 and 
Mo3Si inclusions and then reinforced with oxide fibres. It is shown that fracture toughness of such 
composites is sufficiently high and high temperature strength of the composites are improved as 
compared to composites with the pure molybdenum matrix.
2. Preparation of the composites
To modify the molybdenum matrix used in the internal crystallisation method presented 
schematically in Fig. 1, the first stage of making the molybdenum carcass was changed. This stage 
now consists of three steps: (1) Making a foil/wire/slurry assemblage by winding a molybdenum 
wire over a flat surface of molybdenum foil, filling the gaps between neighbouring wires with a 
slurry containing silicon and boron, repeating the procedure with the next layer of the wire/foil 
couple. (2) Diffusion bonding of the assemblage in vacuum at a temperature of 1250oC. The organic 
binder of the slurry is burned out during a slow heating of the assemblage. (3) Heat treatment of the 
carcass at a temperature of about 2000oC.
A priori it was not clear what a slurry composition to choose. Therefore, a number of the 
compositions were tested, some of them are presented in Table 1. 
First, slurry # 1 without boron was tested just to be sure that the slurry route in the matrix 
modification process was working. It was shown that the rout does work: Mo3Si phase is present in 
the matrix, Fig. 2. Introducing boron via adding boron nitride to the slurry led to the formation of T2

3. Properties of the composites
A critical property of molybdenum alloys under development now is fracture toughness. Therefore, 
specimens for measuring the critical stress intensity factor were made first. The specimen sizes were 
slightly different from those recommended by ASTM399, ~ 50135 mm3, the notch length was 
about ½ of the specimen height. ASTM399 is a document for testing metal alloys, in which case the 
value of the critical stress intensity factor (CSIF) is a key parameter in linear fracture mechanics 
(LFM). For structural elements made of metal matrix composites LFM can hardly be applied and 
CSIF is the parameter of rather qualitative nature [10]. Hence, non-standard specimens can be used 
for testing MMCs. Typical load/displacement curves obtained in testing specimens are presented in 
Fig. 4. The straight parts of the curves after the crack started propagating do not reflect the real 
behaviour of the specimens since a Mitutoyo Co electronic displacement transducer does not have 
sufficiently high-speed characteristics. A view of some specimens after testing is presented in Fig. 5. 
It can be seen that the cracks do not propagate along a straight continuation of the notch thus giving 
just an indirect evidence of the non-brittle behaviour of the composites.
The values of the critical stress intensity factor K* were calculated according to the approximation 
recommended for metal alloys by ASTM399. Some results of measuring K* for the composites with 
three kinds of the fibres and matrix modified with three kinds of the slurry are shown in Table 2. It 
is seen that the K*- value for the best specimens reaches 15 – 16 MPa∙m1/2 independent of the fibre 
material and slurry composition. At the same time, there are specimens with K* of 11 – 12 
MPa∙m1/2, which is higher than that for sufficiently strong molybdenum alloys [2] but lower than the 
K*-values for oxide/molybdenum composites studied earlier [10,11]. This difference can be

explained by a non-stable fabrication technology of the studied composites. The results reported 
here are of preliminary nature;, the further development of the fabrication technology will hopefully 
lead to more stable mechanical properties.
High temperature strength of the composites with YAlO3 and the modified matrix is essentially 
higher than that of the composites with the pure molybdenum matrix: the values of bending strength 
at 1400oC are 393±41 and 258±28 MPa, respectively. All tests were performed in vacuum. Again, a 
larger strength scatter of the new composites as compared with that of the previously obtained ones 
is, perhaps, a result of a non-mature fabrication technology of them. Hence, the strength – fracture 
toughness balance can be regarded acceptable for the composites as structural materials.
4. Conclusions
The foundations of a new fabrication technology of oxide/molybdenum composites with the matrix, 
which phase composition is close to that of molybdenum alloys, have been laid and is now under 
further development.
The first experiments show that the strength – fracture toughness balance looks acceptable to 
consider the composites as structural high temperature materials.
