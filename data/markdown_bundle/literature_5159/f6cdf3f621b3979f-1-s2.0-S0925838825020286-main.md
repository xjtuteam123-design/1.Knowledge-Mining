ABSTRACT
Metastable titanium alloys usually exhibit low yield strength and rapid decline of strain hardening after reaching 
the peak. In this work, the addition of 3.5 wt% Cu into Ti-10Mo titanium alloy is investigated. The results 
indicate that Cu addition enhances both strength and ductility. Notably, the Ti-10Mo-3.5Cu alloy maintains a 
high strain-hardening ability after the peak, even at a large strain of 27 %. The microstructural evolution at 
interrupted strains shows that the addition of Cu promotes multiple twinning and phase transformation mech -
anisms, evidenced by the formation of {332} twins, stress-induced ω phase, stress-induced α ″ phase, and {112} 
twins. For the purpose of utilizing isothermal ω phase ( ω
iso
) to strengthen the alloy, low-temperature aging 
(150
◦
C to 350
◦
C) is performed, which significantly enhances the strength, but results in sensitivity of ductility 
reduction to the aging temperature. The experiment-based statistics reveal that the addition of Cu promotes the 
rapid precipitation of ω
iso 
phase, facilitating the increase of strength. However, high-resolution TEM shows that 
the addition of Cu accelerates the collapse of ω
iso 
phases, resulting in dramatic decrease of ductility at high aging 
temperatures. This work provides valuable insights into the design of metastable titanium alloys by regulating 
the deformation mechanisms and optimizing the precipitation of ω
iso 
phase to achieve high performance.
1. Introduction
Titanium alloys are highly valued in aerospace and biomedical ap -
plications owing to their high specific strength, excellent corrosion 
resistance, and superior biocompatibility [1 – 3] . Metastable β titanium 
alloys mainly including Ti-Mo, Ti-Nb, Ti-Cr, and Ti-V based alloys, have 
attracted significant attention for their good ductility. Due to the 
harmful effects of V and Cr on human body, Ti-V and Ti-Cr alloys have 
been gradually replaced by other biocompatible elements. Ti-Nb based 
alloys become the second generation of biomedical titanium alloy. More 
Nb is required to retain the single β phase at room temperature after 
water quenching. The high cost of Nb and the low yield strength of these 
alloys, even as low as 100 MPa [4] , strictly limited their application. 
Comparatively, Ti-Mo based alloys are promising in application due to 
their excellent corrosion resistance, superior age-hardening properties 
and hydrogen storage ability [5] .
Twinning-induced ductility (TRIP) and/or transformation-induced 
ductility (TWIP) mechanisms [6] are introduced to achieve a dynamic 
Hall-Petch effect and simultaneously enhance the strength and ductility 
during deformation in several types of alloys, including steel [7] , 
medium/high-entropy alloys [8] , titanium [9 – 11] and so on. Element 
addition has served as a vital strategy for tailoring microstructures and 
deformation mechanisms to achieve enhanced properties [12,13] , and 
has been widely employed in material design. In titanium alloys, with 
increasing Fe content [14] , the primary deformation mode of Ti-10Mo 
alloy transitions from stress-induced α ’’ martensitic transformation 
and {332} twinning to dislocation slips, as Fe addition stabilizes the β 
phase [15,16] . {332} twinning mechanism usually prevails in Ti-15Mo 
[17] alloys. When stress-induced α ″ martensite transformation and 
{332} twinning mechanism primarily accommodate plastic deforma -
tion, the alloys tend to exhibit relatively low yield strength (YS) and 
ultimate tensile strength (UTS) [18] . In contrast, β Ti alloys typically

3.4. Multiple deformation mechanisms of the 3.5Cu-ST alloy
The room-temperature deformation of the Cu-added Ti-Mo alloy in 
this work exhibits complex TWIP and TRIP mechanisms. The sequence 
of the main deformation mechanisms is depicted in Fig. 8 . Initially, the 
Ti-10Mo-3.5Cu alloy consists of equiaxed β grains with uniformly 
distributed nanoscale ω
ath 
phases [16] . At a tensile strain of 1.5 %, the 
activation of {332} twins and stress-induced interfacial ω bands prevails 
within the matrix ( Fig. 4 ). A network of nanoscale {112} twins is acti -
vated among the tips of primary {332} twin bands. Besides, martensite 
and ω variants combine to form a twin-twin-like structure. The rapid 
formation of composite structures at the initial stages effectively initi -
ates strong boundary-dislocation interactions. As the deformation pro -
gresses, phase transformation becomes increasingly evident, further 
improving the deformation accommodation capacity. Upon reaching 
5 % strain, the α ″ phase and a few {112} twins emerge within the 
primary {332} twins, accompanied by the activation of independent 
stress-induced ω bands from the matrix in Fig. 6 . The secondary struc -
ture provides a localized mechanism for accommodating stress/strain 
between the primary twins and the matrix, which is crucial for delaying 
the onset of cracks. When the tensile strain reaches as large as 20 %, 
stress-induced twins and phases further develop to form large nets in the 
β grain, accompanied by the thickening of {332} twins. As demonstrated 
in Fig. 7 , the newly activated small α " and ω phase bands in the β matrix 
between the {332} twins substantially increase interfacial density. 
Continuous twin/phase boundary generation provides abundant sites 
for efficient dislocation storage, enabling sustained hardening. These 
hetero-interfaces reduce the effective dislocation mean free path [49] . 
Additionally, they strengthen the dynamic Hall-Petch effect and pro -
mote stress distribution between adjacent domains, which contributes to 
maintaining high strain-hardening ability.
To be summarized, the Cu-added alloys show a better combination of 
strength and ductility, as detailed in Table 2 . The deformation mecha -
nism of the Ti-15Mo alloy is characterized by {332} twins [50,51] , while 
Ti-10Mo alloy exhibits a stress-induced β to α ″ martensite transformation 
in conjunction with {332} twinning [14,45] . Cu addition evidently 
promotes diverse deformation mechanisms. The complex mechanisms 
maintain a high strain-hardening rate even at large strains mainly via 
dislocation interactions, stress accommodation, and the dynamic 
Hall-Petch effect. This study utilized ex-situ characterization at selected 
stages of tensile deformation, rather than employing in-situ or 
quasi-in-situ techniques. Although this method restricts the ability to 
continuously monitor microstructural evolution and limits the capture 
of deformation structures, the results still highlight the activation of

The low-temperature and short-term aging of Ti-10Mo-3.5Cu can
