ABSTRACT
High-strength materials generally suffer low ductility due to poor strain-hardening, especially at low tempera -
tures. Laminated composites that integrate high-strength and high-toughness materials provide a promising 
solution to this strength-ductility trade-off. Here we fabricate a multilayer composite comprising 42CrMo steel 
and CoCrNi medium-entropy alloy via hot-rolling. The resulting composite achieves an ultra-high yield strength 
of 1.61 GPa, tensile strength of 1.99 GPa, and ductility of 4.9 % at 93 K. The CoCrNi layers significantly enhance 
ductility by activating extensive twinning and stacking faults, which alleviate stress concentrations around 
42CrMo layers while preserving their high strength. Moreover, optimizing the layer thickness and grain size 
further improves strength and ductility.
1. Introduction
High-strength steels with body-centered cubic (bcc) structures often 
suffer from brittleness at low temperatures due to reduced dislocation 
mobility [1 – 4] . Consequently, their inherent brittleness poses a serious 
challenge in cryogenic environments, where brittle fracture can lead to 
catastrophic failure. Addressing this issue has become a critical area of 
research, spurring efforts to enhance ductility through alloying, micro -
structural engineering, and development of composite materials [5 – 7] .
Laminated composites offer a promising approach to improving low- 
temperature ductility/toughness of high-strength steels. By combining 
high-strength and high-toughness materials, layered composites can 
achieve excellent strength, toughness, and ductility [8 – 11] . However, 
the primary challenge lies in maintaining strength of high-strength 
materials while alleviating their brittleness at low temperatures [12] . 
Medium-entropy alloys (MEAs) have gained attention as potential 
toughening layers for composites due to their remarkable mechanical 
properties, particularly at low temperatures [13 – 15] . Among these, 
CoCrNi alloy stands out for its outstanding low-temperature strain- 
hardening capability and ductility, which stem from dislocation slips, 
deformation-inducing nano-twinning, and even martensitic trans -
formation [16 – 18] . These characteristics make CoCrNi an ideal candi -
date for enhancing ductility of laminated composites in cryogenic 
applications. In this study, a 42CrMo/CoCrNi laminated composite was 
produced through hot-rolling, aiming to synergize the strengths of both 
layers: the CoCrNi layer delivers exceptional ductility, while the 42CrMo 
layer ensures high strength.

Fig. 1 a,b presents SEM images of L
4/1 
and L
2/1 
samples. In the L
4/1 
sample, thicknesses of 42CrMo and CoCrNi layers are 119 μ m and 28 
μ m, respectively, while in L

sample, these thicknesses decrease to 5.5 
μ m and 3.4 μ m. Both composites exhibit excellent metallurgical bonding 
without visible pores or microcracks at layer interfaces. The L
4/1 
sample 
shows relatively flat interfaces with slight undulations (marked by red 
arrows in Fig. 1 a). In contrast, the L
2/1 
sample features wavy interfaces 
due to local shear deformation mismatch between layers under multiple 
high-rolling processes.
Fig. 1 c highlights the CoCrNi layer in L
4/1 
sample, showing numerous 
bright second-phase particles (marked by yellow arrows) identified as 
Cr-rich carbides. Corresponding EMPA mappings ( Fig. 1 d i) reveal the 
average composition of these carbides as Cr = 61.5 ± 2.5 at.%, Ni = 3.2 
± 1.9 at.%, Co = 6.2 ± 2.5 at.%, Fe = 0.6 ± 0.1 at.%, and C = 28.3 ± 2.2 
at.%. These carbides form due to carbon diffusion from the 42CrMo 
layer into CoCrNi layer, consistent with previous reports on similar 
composite steels [19] . Interlayer atomic interdiffusion supports the 
strong metallurgical bonding at interfaces ( Fig. 1 h). Slight oxygen 
enrichment near interfaces was detected, but no oxides formed, indi -
cating that oxygen presence does not compromise interfacial integrity.
Fig. 2 shows the phase, grain size, and plastic deformation charac -

4/1 
and L
2/1 
samples. Phase maps in Fig. 2 a,d reveal an 
austenitic structure in the CoCrNi layer and a ferritic structure in the 
42CrMo layer for both samples. Inverse pole figure (IPF) images reveal 
fine grains averaging 4.7 μ m in the 42CrMo layer of L
4/1 
sample 
( Fig. 2 b), while the L
2/1 
sample exhibits a bamboo-like grain 
morphology with a single grain spanning the layer thickness and an 
average size of 9.9 μ m ( Fig. 2 e). This unique morphology likely results 
from dynamic recrystallization during hot-rolling. The grain size of the 
CoCrNi layer in L
4/1 
and L
2/1 
samples is approximately 1.2 μ m and 2.2 
μ m, respectively. Layer interfaces in L
2/1 
sample have a greater influence 
on grain growth due to its much smaller layer thickness. Therefore, the 
grain size of CoCrNi and 42CrMo layers in L
2/1 
sample is much larger 
than those in L
4/1 
sample due to interface-assisted grain growth during 
hot-rolling processes. Kernel average misorientation (KAM) values 
indicate higher geometrically necessary dislocation (GND) accumula -
tion in the CoCrNi layer than in 42CrMo layer for both samples ( Fig. 2 c, 
f). This multilayer composite design not only enhances strength through 
GND accumulation in the lower-strength CoCrNi layer but also effec -
tively suppresses early cracking in brittle 42CrMo layer, demonstrating 
improved mechanical synergy between two phases.
Tensile tests of 42CrMo/CoCrNi composites were conducted at 298 K 
and 93 K, alongside stress – strain curves of 42CrMo steel for comparison 
( Fig. 3 a). In contrast, 42CrMo steel exhibits brittle fracture with minimal 
plastic deformation, achieving a strength exceeding 2.3 GPa at 93 K.
Laminated L

4/1

its high low-temperature strength. This balance relies on selecting tough 
component materials to enable coordinated deformation. Compared 
with other steel composites fabricated by hot-rolling, such as maraging 
steel/CoCrNi [20] and SUS304/Cr17 [21] multilayer composites, cur -
rent 42CrMo/CoCrNi multilayer composites exhibit comparable tensile 
strength but much higher ductility at room temperature. Further opti -
mization can be achieved by adjusting layer thickness, thickness ratio, 
and grain size [22] .
Fig. 3 b e shows the typical fracture morphology of L
4/1 
and L
2/1 
samples after tensile failure at 93 K. The 42CrMo layer exhibits brittle 
cleavage fractures with flat surfaces and no dimples ( Fig. 3 b,c), while 
the CoCrNi layer displays ductile fractures with many fine dimples 
( Fig. 3 d,e). Additionally, cracks originating within the 42CrMo layer are 
effectively arrested by the CoCrNi layer. Cracks initiating in the 42CrMo 
layer are arrested by the ductile CoCrNi layer, contributing to the 
improved ductility of the composite at 93 K.
To clarify the ductilizing mechanism in the 42CrMo/CoCrNi multi -
layer composite, TEM analysis was conducted on the post-deformation 
microstructure. The 42CrMo layer primarily deforms through disloca -
tion activities ( Fig. 4 a), while the CoCrNi MEA accommodates 
deformation through high-density stacking faults (SFs) and extensive 
deformation twinning ( Fig. 4 b – d). Fine grains in the CoCrNi layer 
exhibit abundant SFs and nano-twins ( Fig. 4 b,c), and high-resolution 
transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) image ( Fig. 4 d) reveals 
characteristic deformation twins along with localized martensitic 
transformation from face-centered cubic (fcc) to hexagonal close-packed 
(hcp) structures.
These mechanisms in the CoCrNi layer enhance strain-hardening and 
reduce localized stress concentrations, delaying crack propagation in the 
42CrMo layer and preventing premature failure. This multilayer design 
allows the brittle 42CrMo matrix to achieve ultrahigh strength with 
improved ductility, effectively addressing the brittleness of high- 
strength bcc steels.
4. Conclusion
We successfully fabricated a 42CrMo/CoCrNi laminated composite 
with outstanding strength and ductility at cryogenic temperatures using 
hot-rolling. By optimizing the layer thickness ratio and grain size of the 
two materials, the composite achieved a yield strength of 1.61 GPa, a 
tensile strength of 1.99 GPa, and an elongation of 4.9 % at 93 K. The
