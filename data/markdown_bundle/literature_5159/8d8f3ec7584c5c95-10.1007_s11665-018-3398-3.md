Current-Assisted Diffusion Bonding of Extruded
Ti-22Al-25Nb Alloy by Spark Plasma Sintering: Interfacial
Microstructure and Mechanical Properties
Jianlei Yang, Guofeng Wang, Xueyan Jiao, Yibin Gu, Qing Liu, and You Li
(Submitted October 13, 2017; in revised form March 26, 2018; published online May 14, 2018)
Spark plasma sintering (SPS) technology was used to current-assisted bond extruded Ti-22Al-25Nb alloy.
The effects of bonding temperature (920-980 /C176C) and bonding time (10-30 min) on the microstructure
evolution and shear strength of this alloy were investigated systematically. The temperature distribution in
the specimen during the current-assisted bonding process was also analyzed by numerical simulation. It is
noted that the highest temperature was obtained at the bonding interface. As the bonding temperature and
bonding time increased, the voids in the interface shrank increasingly until they vanished. A complete
metallurgical bonding interface could be produced at 960 /C176C/20 min/10 MPa, exhibiting the highest shear
strength of 269.3 MPa. In addition, the shear strength of the bonded specimen depended on its interfacial
microstructure. With increased bonding temperature, the fracture mode transformed from the inter-
granular fracture at the bonding interface to the cleavage fracture in the substrate.
Keywords diffusion bonding, mechanical property, microstruc-
ture, spark plasma sintering, Ti 2AlNb-based alloys
1. Introduction
Since the orthorhombic phase (O phase) was discovered by
Banerjee et al. in 1980 s, Ti 2AlNb-based alloys have received
increasing attention due to their high strength-to-weight ratio,
great fracture toughness, and excellent workability (Ref 1-4).
As a high-temperature structural material used at 600-800 /C176 C, it
is regarded as a potential successor to Ni-based alloys in many
aerospace parts (Ref 5-7). The joining of individual compo-
nents is necessary for fabricating large and complex assemblies
applied in aerospace. As a consequence, the joining techniques,
which can fabricate high-quality joined structures, are indis-
pensable to expand the application of Ti 2AlNb alloy.
To date, several joining techniques, such as fusion welding
(Ref 8-11), friction welding (Ref 12), and diffusion bonding (Ref
13, 14), have been investigated to develop a suitable process for
bonding Ti 2AlNb-based alloys. For example, Chen et al. (Ref
15) studied the laser beam welding of Ti-22Al-25Nb sheets and
investigated the relationship between the tensile properties and
the microstructure at elevated temperatures. However, solidiﬁ-
cation cracks and pores were difﬁcult to avoid during cooling,
which caused deterioration of the mechanical properties of the
joints. Xue et al. (Ref 12, 16) also reported the linear friction
welding and post-welding heat treatment of as-forged Ti 2AlNb
alloy. In our previous studies (Ref 13), the diffusion bonding
process of Ti 2AlNb alloy at different experimental conditions
(temperature, time, pressure) was investigated, and a ﬂawless
interface was obtained at 960 /C176 C/20 MPa/2.0 h. Based on the
superplastic forming/diffusion bonding (SPF/DB) process, the
multilayer structure was fabricated by Du et al. (Ref 17).
Although diffusion bonding has the advantages of low bonding
temperature and low-defect interface, several disadvantages
cannot be ignored. First, the bonding interface is the weakest
area of the bonded parts and needs further heat treatment to
improve the connectivity (Ref 18). Second, a high bonding
pressure and a long bonding time must be used for enhancing the
bonding strength (Ref 17, 19). Third, microstructure deteriora-
tion induced by grain growth would occur during the traditional
diffusion bonding process (Ref 20).
Recently, the pulsed electric current sintering (PECS)
process, especially for spark plasma sintering (SPS), has
become an effective way to bond high-temperature structural
materials (Ref 21, 22). Compared with traditional diffusion
bonding, the SPS process can heat the specimen rapidly
through pulsed current. For example, He et al. (Ref 23) studied
the diffusion bonding of the Ti-6Al-4 V alloy using SPS
technology. Defect-free bonds and a higher bonding strength
can be obtained by SPS for 5 min. Zhao et al. (Ref 24) also
bonded Ti-45Al-7Nb-0.3 W alloy by SPS and found that
metallurgical bonding can be achieved at a lower pressure and
shorter time than that of the traditional diffusion bonding
process. In addition, SPS of some dissimilar materials, such as
Ti/Fe (Ref 25), W/Fe (Ref 26), Si3N4/Fe (Ref 27), and Al-Si/Ti/
Al-Si (Ref 28), have also been studied. Nevertheless, few
works related to the bonding of Ti 2AlNb-based alloys using
SPS have been published.
We have studied the bonding properties of the Ti-22Al-
25Nb alloy, which is a second generation of Ti 2AlNb-based
alloys. The effects of bonding temperature and bonding time on
microstructure evolution and mechanical properties of the joints
were investigated. The temperature distribution and fracture
mechanisms were also studied.

2. Methods
The experimental Ti-22Al-25Nb alloy was fabricated by
elemental powder metallurgy and subsequent hot extrusion (Ref
29). First, the Ti (99.99%, < 325 mesh), Al (99.99%, < 200
mesh) and Nb (99.95%, < 325 mesh) powders were low-
energy milled using a planetary ball-milling machine for 190 r/
min for a period of 4.0 h under argon protection. The mixed
powders were step sintered at 630 /C176 C/1.0 h/25 MPa and
1250 /C176 C/2.0 h/35 MPa in the hot-pressing sintering furnace
under a vacuum of 10
/C0 2 Pa. Subsequently, the sintered billets
with a diameter of 40 mm were put into a low-carbon steel can
and sealed by welding. Finally, the canned billets were extruded
at 1200 /C176 C with an extrusion ratio of 10.56:1 followed by
annealing treatment at 850 /C176 C/12 h. Figure 1 depicts the x-ray
diffraction (XRD) result and microstructure of the extruded Ti-
22Al-25Nb alloy, which was primarily comprised of B2 phase
and lamellar O (L-O) phase. Small amounts of a
2 phases were
also present, according to the XRD data shown in Fig. 1(a).
The specimens for diffusion bonding tests were cut from the
extruded bars with dimensions of 10 9 5 9 3 mm. Figure 2(a)
shows a schematic illustration of the current-assisted diffusion
bonding process. First, the surfaces of specimens were ground
by SiC abrasive paper up to 2000 grit and cleaned ultrason-
ically with acetone. Next, two specimens were placed in the
vacuum furnace and a pressure of 10 MPa was applied to keep
all the parts in proper contact. Finally, the current-assisted
diffusion bonding process was performed by SPS under a
vacuum atmosphere of 10
/C0 2 Pa, followed by furnace cooling
(FC). The bonding temperature was in the range of 920-980 /C176 C,
and the holding time was 10-30 min (Fig. 2b). In addition, the
three-dimensional (3D) surface morphology of the specimen
was obtained via atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Fig. 2c). Ra
(arithmetic mean deviation), Rq (root-mean-square deviation),
and Rmax (maximum distance from the peak to the valley of
the proﬁle), which are used to characterize the surfaces, were
obtained as 76.8, 60.7, 723 nm, respectively.
After the current-assisted diffusion bonding process, the
interfacial microstructure and fracture morphology were inves-
tigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Helios
Nanolab 600i). The shear strength was tested by an tensile
machine (Instron-5569) at room temperature. The strain rate of
0.5 mm/min was applied in the shear tests.

understood by the schematic illustration of the current-assisted
bonding process (Fig. 7). In the beginning, due to the voids in
the bonding interface, there is less contact area. The current

substrate. In previous studies (Ref 31, 32), both the current ﬁeld
and higher temperature accelerated the atomic motion. There-
fore, the atoms near the bonding interface migrate more
actively than that of the substrate. Compared with the
traditional diffusion bonding, it can promote the movement of
the atoms across the mating surfaces and form the high-quality

3.3 Mechanical Properties
Figure 8(a) shows the micro-hardness of the extruded Ti-
22Al-25Nb alloy bonded at different temperatures for 20 min.
The hardness distribution at the transverse section across the bond
interface was measured in each specimen. It was found that the
micro-hardness of the bonding interface ﬁrst increased and then
decreased with the increment of bonding temperature. This could
be attributed to the percentage of metallurgical bonding and the
microstructure evolution. At lower bonding temperatures of 920-
940 /C176 C, the voids, as shown in Fig. 5(a) and (b), have played the
leading role of weakening and result in a low micro-hardness.
After bonding at 960 /C176 C, both the void vanishing and the O
phase precipitation contributed to the signiﬁcantly improved
micro-hardness. With further increasing of the bonding temper-
ature, the phase transformation of O ! B2 becomes obviously,
resulting in the reduction of micro-hardness (Ref33). In addition,
because the temperature distribution was not uniform in the
substrate shown in Fig. 3, different locations have different
micro-hardness values. When bonding at a2 + O + B2 phase
region (920-940 /C176 C), the micro-hardness gradually increased
with increasing distance from the interface, which results from
the microstructure evolution. But the micro-hardness of the
bonding interface was higher than that of the substrate when it
was bonded at a
2 + B2 phase region (960-980 /C176 C), which is
attributed to the O phase precipitated in the interface.
In order to better assess the bonding quality, a micro-
hardness ratio of the bonding interface to the substrate is
calculated and depicted in Fig. 8(b). Due to the defects bonding
at lower temperature, the micro-hardness ratio is lower than 1.
When the bonding interface is completely metallurgically
combined, the ratio is greater than 1, due to the O phase at the
bonding interface. Therefore, the micro-hardness ratio can also
reﬂect the quality of bonding.

3.4 Fracture Surface
During the shear tests, the failure occurred at the bonding
interface when the bonding temperature was lower than
960 /C176 C, indicating that the surface quality was inadequate for
realizing sound bonds. But the specimens that bonded at higher
temperatures failed in the substrate, indicating reasonably
strong bonds. To further investigate the fracture characteristic,
the fracture morphology of the specimens bonding at 920 and
960 /C176 C is displayed in Fig. 10. A large number of uncontacted
zones with a ﬂat layer can be observed on the fracture surface
(Fig. 10a), which correspond to the large-size voids shown in
Fig. 5(a). Figure 10(c) and (d) shows that the crack was
propagated along the grain boundaries. In addition, the fracture
mode changed from intergranular fracture to cleavage fracture
with the increase in the bonding temperature.
4. Conclusions
We have investigated the current-assisted diffusion bonding
process of the extruded Ti-22Al-25Nb alloy by SPS technology.
The effects of bonding temperature and bonding time on
microstructure and mechanical properties were studied in detail.
The main conclusions are the following:
1. It is feasible and efﬁcient to bond Ti-22Al-25Nb alloy
using SPS technology.
2. In the current-assisted diffusion bonding process, the
temperature is unevenly distributed in the bonding speci-
men, with the highest value at the bonding interface. Due
