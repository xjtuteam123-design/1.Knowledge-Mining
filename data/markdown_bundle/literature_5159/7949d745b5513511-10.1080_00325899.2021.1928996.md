ABSTRACT
NiAl–(Cr, Mo, Zr) alloys were successfully prepared by metal injection moulding (MIM)
technique as well as avoiding common defects produced by other processing techniques.
Debinding and sintering process had an important in ﬂuence on the quality of samples. The
eﬀect of sintering temperature and time on microstructure evolution and room-temperature
mechanical performance of NiAl –(Cr, Mo, Zr) alloys were investigated. Due to the existence
of high strength Cr(Mo), the yield strength of NiAl was signi ﬁcantly enhanced. With the
increasing sintering temperature and time, the Cr(Mo) phase aggregated and grown. The
sample sintered at 1390°C for 3 h exhibited optimal mechanical properties. The yield
strength (YS), ultimate compressive strength (UCS) and elongation of the sample at room
temperature were 1133 MPa, 3223 MPa and 25.5%, respectively. MIM had some advantages
in the preparation of di ﬃcult-to-deform superalloy due to the near-net forming process.

1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys are now commonly used for key
components in the aerospace industry because of
their good high-temperature performance, excellent
corrosion and oxidation resistance [ 1,2]. For
example, Ni-based superalloys account for 40 – 60%
of the total weight of engine materials. Although
the service temperature of superalloys can meet the
requirements, the high density ( ∼7.5– 9.1 g cm
−3)
hinders the future development of lightweight aero-
space engines [ 3,4]. Therefore, it is urgent to carry
out research on new high-temperature materials
and advanced manufacturing technology, in order
to replace Ni-based superalloys and reduce the
weight of key components for new-generated super-
sonic aircraft engines [ 5,6].
Intermetallic compounds are a kind of potential
high-temperature resistant materials in recent years.
NiAl-based intermetallic compound (NiAl-based
alloy) is a typical representative [7, 8]. The density of
NiAl-based alloy is ∼5.9– 6.3 g cm
−3, in particular,
the service temperature can reach 1000 – 1100°C.
Additionally, NiAl-based alloys can possess
suﬃciently high strength and sti ﬀness at high temp-
eratures, and have good creep resistance, excellent cor-
rosion and oxidation resistance [9– 11]. Thus, NiAl-
based alloy is undoubtedly a promising lightweight
and high-temperature resistant material in the new-
generation aerospace industry [12,13]. In the tempera-
ture range of 900 – 1100°C, it has the potential to
replace high-temperature alloys as the structural
component of aviation power systems, achieving a sig-
niﬁcant increase in thrust weight ratio and fuel
eﬃciency [14 ,15].
However, the relatively low fracture toughness
and ductility of pure NiAl alloy at room tempera-
ture and inadequate streng th at high temperature
restrict its commercial application [ 16,17]. In
order to improve the mechanical properties of
NiAl alloy, various approaches have been adopted
[18,19]. Current studies show that an e ﬀective
strategy is the addition of high content alloying
elements such as Cr, Mo and Zr into NiAl,
which results in the formation of NiAl – Cr(Mo),
and NiAl – Cr(Mo)Zr eutectic alloys [ 20– 22].
Sheng et al [23 ] obtained a NiAl – Cr(Mo) – Hf
eutectic alloy by suction- casting technique and
subsequently hot isostatic pressing (HIP) treat-
ment. Compared with the conventionally cast
alloy, the room-temperature compressive ductility
and strength of the suction-cast alloy were signi ﬁ-
cantly improved. In addition, the room-tempera-
ture ductility and elevated temperature strength
of suction-cast alloy were markedly enhanced by
HIP treatment.
Although alloying can signi ﬁcantly improve the
strength of NiAl at high temperatures, it has little
eﬀect on the plasticity at room temperature. On the
basis of NiAl alloying, the researches are adopted to
further improve the material properties by changing
the preparation process. Ren et al. [24] prepared direc-
tionally solidi ﬁed NiAl– 31Cr3Mo– 0.2Si (at.-%) near-

As mentioned above, despite the performance of

Based on the above discussions, one can see that it
is urgent to develop a new preparation process to over-

NiAl-based alloy components. It is well documented
that MIM is an e ﬃcient, mass-produced, and near-

ponents. Thus, the purpose of this paper is to obtain
NiAl-based alloy samples via MIM route and to
study the densi ﬁcation process, microstructure and
mechanical properties of this NiAl – (Cr, Mo, Zr)
alloy prepared by powder metallurgy.
2. Experimental
2.1. Materials and methods
In this study, spherical pre-alloyed NiAl – (Cr, Mo, Zr)
powders with diameters smaller than 30 μm were
obtained using the gas atomisation method ( Figure
1). The nominal chemical composition of the powders
is Ni – 33Al– 28Cr– 5.5Mo– 0.5Zr (at.-%).
The feedstock contained 62 vol.-% NiAl-based
alloy powders and 38 vol.-% para ﬃn-based binder
system (69 wt.% high-density polyethylene, 29
wt.% para ﬃn wax, and 2 wt.% stearic acid). The
feedstock was prepared in a mixer (ZHENFEI 300,
China) at 145°C, stirring for 1 h with a critical pow-
der loading of 62 vol.-%. Mix as much powder as
possible while ensuring that the feedstock had excel-
lent ﬂowability.
All samples were moulded using a TIANJIAN
PL860/290 machine at 100 MPa and the injection noz-
zle temperature was 160°C. Samples with shapes of
cube (44 mm × 6 mm × 4 mm) and column ( ϕ
20 mm × 6 mm) were injected simultaneously into a
mould.
The injected samples required a two-step debinding
process. First, solvent debinding in trichloroethylene
at 40°C for 10 h was applied (GUO HUA HH-8,
China). Then, thermal debinding and pre-sintering
process were conducted at ∼ 600°C and 900°C for
2 h under ﬂowing H
2 gas in the tube furnace (GSL-
1400x, China).
The ﬁnal sintering process was performed in a vac-
uum sintering furnace (ZRC85-25T, China) under a
vacuum of 10
−3 mbar between 1360°C and 1400°C
with four sintering time of 1, 2, 3 and 4 h, followed
by furnace cooling.
2.2. Microstructure analysis and performance
testing
Microstructure investigati ons of the sintered samples
were studied by X-ray di ﬀraction (XRD), scanning
electron microscopy (SEM), electron probe microa-
nalysis (EPMA). The phase identi ﬁcation of samples
was carried out by Empyrean intelligent XRD using
Cu K α radiation. The microst ructural characteris-
ations were performed using a Quanta 200FEG
SEM. Oxygen content analysis was tested by JXA-
8230 EPMA.
The transition temperature point of the solid –
liquid line of NiAl-based powders was obtained
through a di ﬀerential scanning calorimeter (DSC).
For the compression test, the samples with a diam-
eter of 3 mm and a height of 4.5 mm were processed
by a wire cut electric discharge machine. The ambient
temperature compression test was carried out on the
AGX-plus SHIMADZU electronic universal testing
machine (an ultimate load of 50 kN) at a strain rate
of 1 × 10
−3 s−1. At least three samples were tested for
each compression test and the ultimate compression
stress (UCS), yield stress (YS) and elongation at
break of the samples were obtained by relative stan-
dard deviation.

was measured by the Archimedes method. The relative

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Inﬂ uence of processing parameters on
physical properties and oxygen content
After thermal debinding process, the samples need to
be transferred from the tube furnace to the vacuum
sintering furnace for the subsequent ﬁnal sintering
process. Therefore, the pre-sintered samples should
have a low oxygen content while having su ﬃcient
strength. Figure 2 shows the relationship between
pre-sintering temperature and oxygen content, and
diﬀerent pre-sintering temperatures (800°C, 900°C,
950°C and 1000°C) were carried out in tube furnace
in this study. When the pre-sintering temperature
was 800°C, the strength of the pre-sintered sample
was very low and the sample broke into many pieces
during the transfer to the vacuum furnace. Therefore,
it was necessary to further increase the pre-sintering
temperature to ensure the strength requirements of
the pre-sintered samples. As the pre-sintering temp-
erature increased to 900°C, the pre-sintered samples
had enough strength to be transferred to the vacuum
furnace for subsequent ﬁnal sintering process and
the oxygen content of the samples did not change sig-
niﬁcantly compared to 800°C. As the pre-sintering
temperature increased to 950°C, the oxygen content
of the pre-sintered sample increased slightly. In
particular, as the pre-sintering temperature further
increased to 1000°C, the oxygen content of pre-sin-
tered sample increased signi ﬁcantly. According to
these results, 900°C was considered as an optimal
pre-sintering temperature.
A dense NiAl – (Cr, Mo, Zr) alloy injection sample
will be obtained by a ﬁnal high-temperature pressure-
less sintering process. In order to determine the upper
limit of ﬁnal sintering temperature, the thermal analy-
sis of the original alloy powders was carried out using
DSC. Figure 3 shows the thermal analysis curve of
NiAl– (Cr, Mo, Zr) original powders. It can be found
that a heat absorption peak appears at 1430°C, indicat-
ing that the melting point of the NiAl – (Cr, Mo, Zr)
powders is ∼1430°C. The ﬁnal sintering temperature
was generally selected near the melting point because
it was a pressureless sintering process. For the ﬁnal
sintering process, several temperatures, i.e. 1360°C,
1370°C, 1390°C and 1400°C, were selected.
Inset of Figure 3 shows the photographs of green
parts. In this study, the cuboid sample was used for
shrinkage measurement and the cylindrical sample
was used for compression performance test at room
temperature and microstructure analysis.

3.2. Microstructure evolution and mechanical
properties
3.2.1. In ﬂuence of sintering temperature on
microstructure and mechanical properties
Figure 4 shows the XRD patterns of NiAl– (Cr, Mo, Zr)
alloys sintered at 1360– 1400°C for 3 h. As can be seen,
the XRD patterns of the ﬁnally sintered samples are
composed of the NiAl phase and the Cr(Mo) phase.
Furthermore, the intensities of di ﬀraction peaks do
not show obvious di ﬀerences with the sintering temp-
erature rising from 1360°C to 1400°C. It is also noted
that the di ﬀraction peaks located at around 65° and
80° are relatively broader when the sintering tempera-
ture is 1360°C. This result indicates that the crystalli-
nity of the sample sintered at 1360°C is poor and the
sample may not very dense.
Figure 5 shows the SEM micrographs of the
samples ﬁnally sintered at di ﬀerent temperatures for
3 h. It is obvious from the micrographs that NiAl –
(Cr, Mo, Zr) alloys mainly have NiAl matrix phase
with relatively darker contrast and brighter Cr(Mo)
phase, which agrees with the results of XRD patterns
(Figure 4). The speci ﬁc composition of the 1390H3
sample is recorded by EDS, as shown in Table 2 and
in Figure 5(c). It can be seen from the EDS results
that the oxygen content of the sample is few, indicat-
ing that the oxidation of the pre-sintered sample
mainly occurs on the sample surface. A large number
of pores exist in the sample at a sintering temperature
of 1360°C and the boundaries of the spherical powders
are clearly visible, as shown in Figure 5(a). With the
sintering temperature increases to 1370 – 1390°C
(Figure 5(b,c)), it can be found that the number of
pores in the samples is signi ﬁcantly reduced. Particu-
larly, very few pores with sizes smaller than
micrometer are observed in the sample at the sintering
temperature of 1390°C ( Figure 5(c)), suggesting that
nearly full densi ﬁcation has been achieved. With the
sintering temperature further increasing to 1400°C
(Figure 5(d)) or above (not shown here), it is noted
that bending deformation takes place in the samples.
Moreover, as shown in Figure 5(d), there exists
numerous pores in the sample and the size of the
pores is even larger when compared with that in the
sample sintered at 1390°C. It is also important to
note
that the Cr(Mo) phase accumulates together
and the grain size grows gradually with increasing
the sintering temperature. One can also see that the
results of sintering microstructures are consistent
with the measured density values in Table 1, namely
the sample sintered at 1390°C for 3 h in this work
has a compact microstructure and the maximum
density.
As can be seen, Figure 6 shows the room-tempera-
ture compression curves and performances of the
samples sintered at di ﬀerent temperatures for 3 h. As
shown, the sample sintered at 1360°C has minimum
yield/compressive strength (740, 979.6 MPa) and
elongation at fracture (7.3%), resulting from the low
density of the sample and the existence of a great
deal of large pores ( Figure 5(a)). When the sintering
temperature increases to 1370°C and 1390°C, the
yield strength increases to 943.3 and 1133 MPa, and
the ultimate compressive strength also increases to
1837 and 3225.2 MPa, respectively. Meanwhile, the
corresponding elongation even reaches 13.8% and
25.5%. With increasing the sintering temperature to
1400°C, the yield/compressive strength and the
elongation decrease slightly, but the sample is severely
deformed as mentioned above (see insert of Figure 6
(a)). Since the ﬁnal sintering process is free of mould
constraints, the high sintering temperature of 1400°
C will lead to much greater di ﬀerences in variations
of sizes for the sample during sintering/cooling pro-
cess and thus causes the bending deformation. Based
on the above results, one can see that the sample

4. Conclusions
In this study, the NiAl – (Cr, Mo, Zr) alloy has been
successfully fabricated using the MIM process. The
following conclusions could be drawn.
(1) The feedstock with an optimal alloy powder load-
ing of 62 vol.-% was prepared by a para ﬃn-based
binder system. Any amount of metal powder will
cause the feedstock ﬂuidity to decrease. Before the
ﬁnal sintering process, the sample should be
strong enough to support transfer to vacuum sin-
tering equipment, and a low pre-sintering temp-
erature (900°C) should be selected to ensure low
oxidation content.
(2) When the sintering temperature is in the range of
1360– 1390°C, the increase of sintering tempera-
ture is conducive to the di ﬀusion of metal pow-
der, but the further increase of temperature will
lead to sample distortion. The obtained sample
has a maximum relative density of 95.83% and
14.93% shrinkage in the direction along the
length when ﬁnal sintering process at 1390°C

(3) With the increase of sintering temperature and
