Formation of Accumulated
Misorientation During Directional
Solidiﬁcation of Ni-Based Single-
Crystal Superalloys
SONGSONG HU, LIN LIU, WENCHAO YANG,
DEJIAN SUN, MIAO HUO, TAIWEN HUANG,
JUN ZHANG, HAIJUN SU, and HENGZHI FU
The formation of accumulated misorientation during
directional solidiﬁcation of Ni-based single-crystal
superalloys with h001i direction deviating from cylinder
axis by 20 deg was investigated at diﬀerent withdrawal
rates. The accumulated misorientation increased from
the converging side of the dendrite with respect to the
mold wall to the diverging side in the radial direction.
With increased solidiﬁcation distance, the accumulated
misorientation in the solidiﬁcation direction reached
7.6 deg, and was not signiﬁcantly aﬀected by withdrawal
rates.

Ni-based single-crystal superalloys have become the
best option for modern turbine blades because of the
elimination of grain boundary and imposition of the
elastically soft h001i orientation. In fact, the formation
of misorientation during directional solidiﬁcation makes
it diﬃcult to obtain perfect single crystal with exactly the
same orientation everywhere. [1–8] Bellet et al . observed
the mosaic spread of superalloys. [9] Newell et al . exam-
ined the eﬀects of dendrite branching and growth
kinetics on the formation of misorientation, and pro-
posed that it originated from plastic deformation of the
dendrite stems within the mushy zone. [10] Using in situ
synchrotron X-ray radiography, dendrite deformation
in Ni-based superalloys was observed and suggested that
the dendrite deformation was signiﬁcant at converging
side with respect to the mold wall. [11,12] However, the
dendrite deformation process was unclear and little
attention was given to the impact of withdrawal rate on
the formation of misorientation. [13]
Therefore, Ni-based single-crystal superalloys in the
h001i direction that deviated from cylinder axis by
20 deg were prepared using a seeding method at
diﬀerent withdrawal rates in order to characterize the
accumulated misorientation and analyze the inﬂuence of
withdrawal rates. Furthermore, the mechanism of
misorientation formation was discussed.
A third-generation Ni-based single-crystal superalloy
was used, with nominal composition (wt pct) of
Ni-2.5Cr-9Co-1.5Mo-7W-6Al-8Ta-0.1Hf-4Re-0.2Ti and
< 0.02 C. A modiﬁed Bridgman directional casting
furnace with liquid metal cooling (LMC) was used in
this work, and the thermal gradient was approximately
170 /C176 C/cm. The cylinder-shaped seeds ( F4m m 9 20
mm) in the h001i direction that deviated from cylinder
axis by 20 deg were determined by an X-ray diﬀrac-
tometer, and then the projection of the plane consisting
of the cylinder axis and the h001i direction on the end
face was marked. A pure alumina tube was mounted on
a steel chilling plate, which was used as a mold for
preparing single-crystal samples. The alumina tube has
diameter of 4/6 mm (inside/outside diameter) and a
length of 150 mm. The seed and master alloy (machined
into a cylinder with diameter of 4 mm and length of
120 mm) were placed inside the alumina tube and the
position of the alumina tube was adjusted to ensure
complete melting of the master alloy and the top portion
of the seed during the heating stage. The tube was
heated to 1650 /C176 C and held for 30 minutes, and then
withdrawn at a predetermined rate. The direction of
solidiﬁcation was parallel to the cylinder axis of the
samples.
Following directional solidiﬁcation, the casting was
macro-etched by a solution of 50 pct HCl and 50 pct
H2O2 to check whether it formed stray grains. The
samples were subsequently machined into ﬁve half-cylin-
ders using wire electro discharge machining to make
sure that the ﬂanks of half-cylinders were all parallel to
the plane consisting of the seed axis in the h001i
direction and the end faces were all perpendicular to the
cylinder axis, as shown in Figure 1. These half-cylinder
samples were inlaid together with thermosetting resin to
ensure that their ﬂanks and end faces are parallel,
respectively. A scanning electron microscope with elec-
tron back-scattered diﬀraction (EBSD) was used to
obtain the orientation evolution along the solidiﬁcation
distance. Then, EBSD was applied to the longitudinal
section at solidiﬁcation distance of 53 mm. For optical
microscopy observations using Leica DM-4000M, the
samples were etched in a solution of 10 mL HNO 3,
20 mL HF, and 30 mL C 3H8O3. The HKL Channel 5
analysis software and TexTools were used to measure
the EBSD data.

are shown in Figure 2. When the withdrawal rate was
15 lm/s, the dendrite was coarse, and a small amount of
(c + c¢) eutectic was found in the interdendritic region.
As the withdrawal rate increased to 500 lm/s, the
dendrite was reﬁned with signiﬁcant decrease in dendrite
spacing, and a small amount of tertiary arms was
apparent. The primary dendrites were regularly arrayed
in the transversal section, similar to the case of 15 lm/s.
When the withdrawal rate was further increased to
1000 lm/s, the tertiary dendrites became very well
developed and the arrangement of primary dendrites
in the transversal section was in disarray. This is because
at high withdrawal rate, a curved solidiﬁcation front
induced by the enhanced heat extraction leads to the
blocking of primary dendrites by well-developed tertiary
dendrites.

dendrite deformation and solidiﬁcation occurred
simultaneously. As the deformed dendrite transferred
the stress to adjacent undeformed dendrite, the defor-
mation resistance of the undeformed dendrite increased
because of the lower temperature of the undeformed
dendrite, resulting in small deformation and stress for
the next undeformed dendrite. Because of the change in
deformation temperature and stress, the change in
accumulated misorientation was accelerated in the
radial direction (perpendicular to the solidiﬁcation
direction), as shown in Figure 3(c).

et al.[10] Furthermore, there was no signiﬁcant change in
accumulated misorientation at withdrawal rates from 15
to 1000 lm/s. This indicates that withdrawal rate has

Recent reports have shown that the boundary misori-
entation angle monotonically increased with the increase
in the solidiﬁcation path of dendrite,
[8] indicating that
the accumulated misorientation of dendrite is positively
correlated with the solidiﬁcation path. This ﬁnding is
consistent with our results.
The gradient of accumulated misorientation (misori-
entation change in per unit length) in the radial direction
(~ 1 deg/mm, based on the data in Figure 3(c)) was
signiﬁcantly higher than in the solidiﬁcation direction
(~ 0.07 deg/mm, based on the data in Figure 4). This is
strong evidence that no signiﬁcant deformation of
primary dendrite occurred at the diverging side caused
by the diﬀerential thermal contraction between the
diverging mold wall and metal. If primary dendrite
deformation occurs at the diverging side, the deformed
dendrite will transfer the misorientation to newly
formed primary dendrites leading to signiﬁcant accu-
mulated misorientation in the solidiﬁcation direction.
This also indicates that dendrite deformation originated
from the converging side between the dendrite and mold
wall rather than the diverging side. When the secondary
dendrite arm impinges on the diverging mold wall, the
strain location could be conﬁned to the juncture between
the primary and secondary arm resulting in an elevated
stress region.
[12] This phenomenon was demonstrated by
Takaki and Kashima through phase ﬁeld method and
ﬁnite element analysis. [15] In conclusion, dendrite defor-
mation originated from the converging side caused by
diﬀerential thermal contraction. The deformation
behavior transferred to adjacent dendrites and froze
during directional solidiﬁcation, leading to the accumu-
lated misorientation.
The evolution of accumulated misorientation during
directional solidiﬁcation of Ni-based single-crystal
superalloys in the h001i direction, which deviated from
the cylinder axis by 20 deg, was investigated at with-
drawal rates from 15 to 1000 lm/s. The following
conclusions are drawn:
1. At solidiﬁcation distance of 110 mm, the accumu-
lated misorientation can reach 7.6 deg, and was not
signiﬁcantly aﬀected by withdrawal rate.
2. The gradient of accumulated misorientation in the
radial direction was signiﬁcantly higher than that in
the solidiﬁcation direction.
3. The accumulated misorientation originated from
the converging side caused by diﬀerential thermal
contraction.
