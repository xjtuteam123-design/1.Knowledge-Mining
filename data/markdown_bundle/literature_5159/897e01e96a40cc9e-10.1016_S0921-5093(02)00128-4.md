Effects of carbon addition on the microstructure and mechanical
properties of Ir 85Nb15 two-phase refractory superalloy
Y.F. Gu *, Y. Yamabe-Mitarai, H. Harada
National Institute for Materials Science, 1-2-1 Sengen, Tsukuba, Ibaraki 305-0047, Japan
Received 30 November 2001; recei ved in re vised form 20 February 2002
Abstract
To pro vide information rele vant to Ir-base two-phase alloys for future ultra-high-temperature applications, effects of carbon
addition on the structure development and mechanical properties of a two-phase Ir 85Nb15 alloy were investigated. The results show
that carbide formation is not necessary for stabilising the grains in the two-phase Ir 85Nb15 alloy at temperatures up to 1800 8C. At
temperatures up to 1200 8C, the yield strength of the carbon-doped Ir 85Nb15 alloys was higher than that of the carbon-free alloy.
The carbon addition to the Ir 85Nb15 alloy changed the fracture mode from intergranular to transgranular clea vage.

1. Introduction
We reported structures and properties of a new class
of alloys based on platinum group metals (PGMs).
These alloys ha ve a coherent fcc/L1 2 (g/g?) two-phase
structure and are called refractory superalloys [1 /C1/7].
Some Ir-base refractory superalloys, such as Ir 85Nb15,
Ir85Hf15 and Ir 85Zr15, are superior in high-temperature
strength and oxidation resistance compared with Ir
single-phase alloys, and ha ve the potential for use as
structural materials in applications at temperatures up
to 1800 8C [4,5].
For Ni-base superalloys, carbide formation accom-
plished by formation of carbide networks at the grain
boundaries is required to stabilise the structure against
high-temperature deformation [8]. These networks in-
hibit the grain boundary motion, and thus impro ve the
creep and stress-rupture properties. Since the two-phase
refractory superalloys are intended to be used as
structural materials in ultra-high-temperature applica-
tions, the stability and de velopment of the microstruc-
ture at ultra-high-temperatures are taken into
consideration. In this study, we examined the structure
development and mechanical properties of a two-phase
Ir
85Nb15 alloy doped with various carbon contents.
2. Experimental procedure
Starting materials were 99.99 wt% purity Ir, 99.98
wt% purity Nb and 99 wt% purity NbC. First, an
Ir85Nb15 alloy was prepared as 30 g button ingots by arc
melting under argon in a vacuum furnace. Second, the
Ir85Nb15 alloy was doped with 100, 500, 2000 wppm
carbon (ppm in weight, corresponding to about 0.14,
0.75, and 2.96 at%, respecti vely) by arc melting the
Ir
85Nb15 with NbC. Homogeneity of the ingots was
ensured by re-melting the ingots at least ten times.
From the ingots, samples (6 mm in height and 3 mm
in diameter) were prepared by using an electron-
discharge machine (EDM). These samples were an-
nealed at 1800 8C for 100 h accompanied with furnace
cooling under vacuum of about 3 /C29
/10/C284 Pa. For
investigating the microstructure, these heat-treated
samples were cut into disk samples by the EDM and
then prepared by using con ventional metallographic
method. The polished disks were electrolytically etched
in a 5% HCl ethyl-alcohol solution. The microstructures
were examined by using a scanning electron microscope
(SEM). The compositions of the constituent phases in

3. Results
We investigated the grain size developing in the tested
alloys during heat treatment at various temperatures
and times. Up to 1800 8C, no ob vious coarsening in
grain size was in vestigated for both the carbon-free and
the carbon-doped Ir 85Nb15 alloys. Fig. 1 shows the
typical SEM micrographs of the tested alloys after aging
at 1800 8C for 100 h.
The grain size for Ir
85Nb15 was about 300 mm and
decreased greatly with adding carbon. Grain sizes of
about 90 and 30 mm were observed for the 100 and 2000
wppm carbon-doped Ir 85Nb15 alloys, respecti vely. The
microstructure of the Ir 85Nb15 alloy was characterised
by a dentritic structure, where the dentritic corn was
formed by the primary fcc phase (the grey area in Fig.
1a). The fcc/L1
2 (Ir/Ir3Nb) two-phase structure, whose
L12 phase size was about 400 nm, can be seen in the
inter-dendritic corn areas ( Fig. 1 b, which are enlarged
views corresponding to the arrow A denoted in Fig. 1a).
For the carbon-doped Ir 85Nb15 alloys, the microstruc-
tures exhibited similar structure (Fig. 1c/C1/e for the alloys
with 100, 500 and 2000 wppm carbon, respecti vely). The
L12 phase sizes for these carbon-doped alloys were
nearly similar to that of the Ir 85Nb15 alloy (Fig. 1fi sa n
enlarged view corresponding to the arrow B denoted in
Fig. 1 e). Howe ver, some large black phases, which
contain higher carbon concentration than the matrix
(primary fcc phase) according to the EDX analysis, were
found along the grain boundaries for the alloy doped
with 2000 wppm carbon, as denoted by the arrows in
Fig. 1 e. The black phases were in irregular shape and
varied in their sizes from about 1 to 10 mm.
The samples of the carbon-free and carbon-doped
Ir
85Nb15 alloys were compressed from room to high
temperatures to a stress at which cracks began to
propagate. Fig. 2 shows the compression properties of
the tested alloys as functions of the testing temperature
and carbon content. For temperatures up to 1200 8C,
the yield strengths of the carbon-doped alloys were
higher than that of the carbon-free alloy ( Fig. 2a). The
500 wppm carbon-doped alloy had a slightly higher
value than the other carbon-doped alloys, which were
1747 MPa at room temperature and 1226 MPa at
1200 8C( Fig. 2 b). Howe ver, doping carbon had little
effect on the compression ductility for the two-phase
Ir
85Nb15 alloy. The compression ductility for the
carbon-free and carbon-doped alloys had almost the
same value of about 7%.
SEM examination of the fracture surface re vealed
that carbon addition changed the fracture mode of the
two-phase Ir
85Nb15 alloy deformed at room tempera-
ture. The carbon-free Ir85Nb15 alloy fractured mainly by
grain boundary separation ( Fig. 3 a), and the carbon-
doped alloys showed mainly transgranular fracture (TF)
(Fig. 3 b /C1/d). The TF was characterized by crystal-
lographic steps and ri ver patterns similar to a clea-
vage-type fracture. In the alloy with 2000 wppm carbon,
some white phases were obser ved along the grain
boundaries on the fracture surfaces, as indicated by
the arrows in Fig. 3d. The EDX spectra from the white
phase and the primary fcc phase are shown in Fig. 4 a
and b, respecti vely. The chemical composition of the
white phase thus was calculated to be 48 at% of carbon,
46 at% Ir and 6 at% Nb. So the carbide formed in the
2000 wppm carbon-doped Ir
85Nb15 alloy was IrC.

4. Discussion
In most non-ferrous alloys, and e ven in some ferrous
systems, carbon and carbide formation are deleterious
because they usually ha vea na d verse effect on mechan-
ical properties. Carbon is also reported to be a main
impurity that causes polycrystalline Ir and its alloys
intergranular brittleness [9,10]. Handley [9] found that
carbon-free polycrystalline Ir (with concentration of
carbon less than 10 wppm) could be forged in the
same way as platinum, whereas Ir work-piece that
contains more than 10 wppm carbon cracked under
rolling. Howe ver, for Ir-base two-phase refractory
superalloys, no report until now has been gi ven about
the effects of carbon on the mechanical properties and
fracture beha vior. According to the present results, no
harmful effect of carbon addition was obser ved on the
strength and fracture beha vior of the two-phase Ir /C1
/Nb
refractory superalloy, even if carbon addition was up to
2000 wppm. Our results suggest that carbide formation
is not necessary for the stabilisation of the grain size
during high-temperature deformation of the two-phase
Ir /C1
/Nb refractory superalloy.
The strength impro vement by carbon addition may
mainly come from the following points. First, solid
solution hardening caused by the carbon addition is
suggested [11 /C1
/13]. It is reported that solid solution
hardening is primarily connected with a combined
parameter of carbon concentration and difference in
the atomic size and modulus between solute and solvent.
Normally, strength will increase with increasing solute
concentration up to a solid solution limit. According to
binary alloy phase diagram, the solid solution limit of
carbon in pure Ir is 3.12 at% at 2296 8C [14]. Our
investigation show that there were carbides, IrC, formed
along the ground boundaries in the 2000 wppm (2.96
at%) carbon-doped Ir
85Nb15 alloy. This result implies
that carbon segregates to the grain boundaries in the
carbon-doped alloys or that the solubility limit of
carbon in binary Ir
85Nb15 alloy is below 2000 wppm
at the annealing temperature, 1800 8C. At the same
time, our results indicate that the yield strengths of the
carbon-doped alloys showed a moderate increase while
carbon content increases from 100 wppm (0.14 at%) to
500 wppm (0.71 at%). These results suggest that the
strength impro vement in the carbon-doped Ir
85Nb15
alloy is in part attributed to the solid solution hard-
ening. Second, carbon addition resulted in a great
decrease in the grain size for the Ir
85Nb15 alloy. This

The carbon addition to the Ir85Nb15 alloy changed the
fracture mode from intergranular to transgranular. It is
reported that intergranular brittleness in polycrystalline
Ir and its alloys is an intrinsic one [17]. Therefore, the
grain boundary in polycrystalline Ir and its alloys may
always be a weak point. Liu et al. found that the grain
boundary fracture in an Ir /C1
/0.3%W alloy can be
suppressed by doping 200 ppm Th. This suppression
was attributed to the segregation of Th to the grain
boundary [18]. Wolff and Sauthoff showed that adding
a small amount of B to an Ir /C1
/16%Nb alloy can improve
its ductility and strength [19]. They also related this
improvement to the segregation of B to the grain
boundary. Since the carbides are occasionally formed
at the grain boundaries in 2000 wppm carbon-doped
alloy, it is easily assured that carbon is segregated to the
grain boundaries, and thereby enhances the grain
boundary cohesive strength in the carbon-doped alloys.
As a result, the fracture mode changed in the carbon-
doped Ir
85Nb15 alloy. E ven by carbon addition, the
carbon-doped alloys still fractured in the transgranular
cleavage and had little compression ductility. This
brittleness may associate with very strong and directed
atomic binding forces in the lattices.
5. Conclusions
The effects of carbon addition on the microstructures
and compression properties of the two-phase Ir 85Nb15
alloys were in vestigated at various temperatures. Up to
1800 8C, carbide formation is not necessary in the two-
phase Ir 85Nb15 alloy for stabilising the grains at high
temperatures. At temperatures up to 1200 8C, the yield
strength of the carbon-doped Ir85Nb15 alloys was higher
than that of the carbon-free alloy. The 500 wppm
carbon-doped alloy had a slightly higher yield strength
value than the other carbon-doped alloys, which was
1747 MPa at room temperature and 1226 MPa at
1200 8C. The fracture mode changed from intergranu-
lar fracture in the Ir
85Nb15 alloy to transgranular
cleavage in the carbon-doped alloys. Doping carbon to
the two-phase Ir 85Nb15 alloy had little effect on
improving its compression ductility.
