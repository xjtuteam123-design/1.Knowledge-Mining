1. Introduction
A high mechanical biocompatibility is the main requirement that
must be satis ﬁed by metallic biomaterials used for hard tissue re-
placement [1–3]. They are expected to possess high strength, especially
high fatigue strength, good ductility and elastic modulus as low as
possible. The elastic modulus is the most decisive property for the
biomechanical compatibility of an orthopedic implant [1–7]. Namely,
to prevent bone resorption around the implant and eventual implant
failure due to inadequate load sharing between the bone and the im-
plant, the elastic modulus of implant materials should be equivalent to
that of human bone, varying in the range from 4 to 30 GPa [1–5].
Among the current biometallic materials suitable for orthopedic
applications, the titanium alloys are distinguished by their excellent
combination of high strength and low elastic modulus closer to the
bone. Their tensile strength is between 600 and 1100 MPa, while the
elongation lies between 6% and 28% [1,8,9]. On the other hand, the
elastic modulus is superior to that of the other metallic biomaterials
such as stainless steels or Co-Cr-Mo alloys [1,8] and ranges from 136 to
55 GPa [1,8,9]. The much lower elastic modulus of recently developed
β-type titanium alloys (85 –55 GPa) should favor these alloys more than
the conventional α + β-type alloys. However, the β-type alloys possess
lower strength than the α + β-type alloys. Thus, the Ti-35Nb-7Zr-5Ta
alloy with a lowest elastic modulus of 55 GPa has a tensile strength of
596 MPa [1,8]. Furthermore, the α + β-type alloys have a higher re-
sistance to fatigue cracking than the β-type alloys for similar levels of
yield strength [2,10]. In spite of the fact that the elastic modulus of the
β-type alloys is much lower than those of the α + β-type alloys, the
latter is still most widely employed as an implant material. Widespread
application of the new β-type titanium alloys is hindered by high pro-
cessing costs. Therefore, there is a continuous interest to improve the
performance of the α + β-type alloys.
The standard Ti-6Al-4V alloy is the main two-phase titanium alloy
for biomedical applications. Although this ﬁrst generation biomedical

ercised to enhance the mechanical properties of the Ti-6Al-4V alloy.
One way to achieve better mechanical properties is to alter the content
of interstitial elements (O, C, N, H) [1,2,10]. It has been noted that a
decrease in the oxygen content to 0.13 mass% decreases the elastic
modulus [8]. Decreasing the oxygen level in Ti-6Al-4V ELI (extra-low
interstitial) alloy also results in an increase in ductility with some de-
crease in strength [8,11]. Further decrease in the elastic modulus of Ti-
6Al-4V ELI alloy can be achieved through microstructure modi ﬁcation
by thermomechanical and heat treatment procedures. It has been es-
tablished that this alloy with fully lamellar microstructure has the lower
elastic modulus compared to one with equiaxed microstructure [9].
However, the tensile strength and ductility of the same alloy with
equiaxed microstructure are fairly higher than those of alloy with fully
lamellar microstructure.
For achieving additional reduction in the elastic modulus without
compromising the strength, a proper thermomechanical processing to
obtain a favorable microstructure is required. It is well recognized that
the ﬁner microstructure leads to a signi ﬁcant increase in strength and
ductility. In recent years, the re ﬁned microstructure with submicron or
even nanosized grains can be successfully produced by application of
severe plastic deformation (SPD) [12–23]. Using various SPD methods
such as equal-channel angular pressing (ECAP), high-pressure torsion
(HPT), accumulative roll bonding (ARB) and multistep isothermal for-
ging an ultra ﬁne-grained (UFG) material exhibiting improved me-
chanical properties can be obtained. Attempts to produce UFG Ti-6Al-
4V alloy using SPD processing have shown that the microstructure with
reﬁned and fragmented grains strongly contributes to the alloy
strengthening preserving su ﬃcient ductility [13,17,18,24]. The alloy
strength at room temperature after ECAP is higher for 20% compared to
the initial state [13]. Additional rolling or extrusion carried out after
SPD process may cause further strengthening of the UFG alloy [13,17].
Namely, it was found that the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and
hardness of Ti-6Al-4V ELI alloy processed by ECAP followed by a sub-
sequent multicycle extrusion increased by about 50% and 11%, re-
spectively, whereas the decrease in elongation-to-failure was relatively
low compared to the conventionally processed alloy [17]. A conven-
tional low-temperature deformation applied after ECAP process leads to
additional grain re ﬁnement and higher dislocation density in the alloy
[13]. Consequently, the UTS value amounted to ≈ 1500 MPa, whereas
the alloy ductility has a value of 7%. It was also shown that combined
treatment by ECAP and conventional deformation methods resulted in a
pronounced enhancement in the fatigue life in the S-N (Wöhler) dia-
gram of Ti-6Al-4V ELI alloy [17].
Processing by HPT is more e ﬀective because it can lead to stronger
grain re ﬁnement [14].
Due to the signi ﬁcantly higher hydrostatic
pressure inhibiting crack formations in the alloy during deformation
process, this method can provide a large plastic strain [25]. Thus, ap-
plication of HPT to process pure metals contributes to grain sizes less
than 100 nm [16]. However, this SPD technique is used mostly to
produce UFG pure metals and simple alloys [14]. There is little in-
formation concerning its application in the processing of the two-phase
alloys like two-phase Ti-6Al-4V alloy. Apart from the superplastic be-
havior and in ﬂuence of superplastic deformation on the mechanical
properties of HPT-processed Ti-6Al-4V alloy studied by Sergueeva et al.
[12], the bene ﬁts of UFG Ti-6Al-4V alloy produced by HPT has been
rarely investigated. Recently, Hu et al. [25] demonstrated that HPT
method can e ﬀectively re ﬁne the grains of Ti-6Al-4V alloy produced
through a spark plasma sintering process to the nanoscale, inducing
signiﬁcant changes in alloy hardness and tensile properties. To the
author's knowledge, however, no study on the elastic modulus of this
HPT-processed alloy has been reported. Until now, only a study [26] on
the Young ′s modulus of a β-type Ti-24Nb-4Zr-8Sn alloy processed by
HPT with various processing parameters was devoted. It was shown
that HPT deformed alloy has a slightly higher Young ′s modulus in
comparison to the initial unprocessed material so that its value remains
less than 65 GPa. One of the major causes of the limited implementation
of HPT method for obtaining the optimum mechanical response of UFG
Ti-6Al-4V alloy is its low deformability, which makes the HPT process
more complicated. Since the numbers of turns in torsion, the applied
pressure, the frictional e ﬀects and temperature are the factors in ﬂu-
encing the UFG structure [27], it is of particular interest to ﬁnd ap-
propriate HPT regime for generating a required homogeneous ultra ﬁne,
equiaxed-grain microstructure. Moreover, attention should be paid to
the initial microstructure, which could a ﬀect the kinetics of grain re-
ﬁnement as well as the UFG structure homogeneity [16,18,24]. Fu et al.
[27] and Wang and Langdon [28] examined the in ﬂuence of phase
volume fractions on the microstructural evolution and microhardness of
an HPT processed Ti-6Al-4V alloy when the equiaxed α-phase or the
lamellar ( α + β) is the major phase constituent in the initial bimodal
microstructure. The obtained results showed that greater grain re ﬁne-
ment and microhardness are achieved when the initial microstructure
contains higher volume fraction of lamellar ( α + β) structure. Although
their work suggested that the microstructural features of alloy before
HPT processing alter the extent of grain re ﬁnement
 remarkably, the
relationship between various initial microstructures and mechanical
properties of HPT deformed Ti-6Al-4V alloy has not been revealed.
Therefore, the purpose of the present work is to determine the ef-
fects of initial microstructure on the tensile properties and fracture
mode of the Ti-6Al-4V ELI alloy subjected to HPT processing at two
diﬀerent temperatures. In particular, we focus on the elastic modulus
and development of a homogeneous microstructure during HPT.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure evolution
The application of the HPT processing at two temperatures led to a
considerably modi ﬁed microstructure of the heat treated Ti-6Al-4V ELI
alloy, irrespective of its initial microstructural state. Although it is
diﬃcult to estimate the true grain sizes because most of the grain
boundaries are poorly deﬁ ned, the SEM observations of the HPT-pro-
cessed alloy show that the microstructural re ﬁnement takes place in all
cases. The size of the α and β phase separate fragments is within
100–165 nm. Another observed microstructural feature is the presence
of numerous nanometer-sized particles (smaller than 100 nm) inside the
grains, suggesting that the phase transformation occurred during HPT-
deformation. The simultaneous grain re ﬁnement, decomposition of
supersaturated solid solution and formation of stable or metastable
phases are already observed in di ﬀerent SPD-processed alloys with UFG
microstructure [19,20,23,24,32,33]. The strong external forces cause
the atom movements, accelerating di ﬀusion and phase transformations
during the SPD processing [23]. The XRD patterns shown in Fig. 4
conﬁrm this observation.
The broadened XRD peaks for the HPT-deformed alloy indicate the
much smaller grain sizes compared to that for the alloy prior to the
HPT-processing. It is known that both the small grain size and internal
microstrains contribute to the X-ray line broadening [19]. A signi ﬁcant
decrease in average crystallite size after HPT demonstrates that the
severe deformation during the HPT processing results in the grain re-
ﬁnement of the Ti-6Al-4V ELI alloy. The average crystallite size, cal-
culated by analyzing XRD peak broadening, is smaller than 35 nm.
Further, the microstructural re ﬁnement
strongly depends on the alloy
initial microstructure and HPT deformation temperature. Thus, the
achieved crystallites are smaller in the alloy subjected to HPT proces-
sing at room temperature than in the alloy HPT processed at 500 °C.
3.1.1. HPT processing at room temperature
The HPT processing at room temperature results in a two-phase
microstructure with average crystallite size in the 10 –26 nm range. The
smallest crystallites 10– 14 nm in size are identi ﬁed in the alloy with the
initial acicular microstructures. The HPT-processed alloy with the in-
itial fully lamellar microstructure shows the microstructure fragmen-
tation/reﬁnement, which is not completely uniform across the disk
section, Fig. 5. In highly deformed regions, the kinked lamellae and ﬁne
globular grains/subgrains of α phase are observed. Some regions,
however, retain unchanged lamellar microstructure. The average crys-
tallite size of ~ 10 nm, achieved after HPT processing, is 3.5 times
smaller than the crystallite size determined for the starting undeformed
alloy. The presence of lamellae, which are able to survive intact, sup-
ports the results of the several di ﬀerent analytical methods that illus-
trate the in ﬂuence of the initial lamellae orientation on the grain re-
ﬁning and globularization e ﬃciency. It has been reported that breaking
up the lamellar microstructure is best accomplished when both basal
and prism slip systems operate simultaneously in all grains [18,34,35].
Due to the accumulation and redistribution of dislocations, in these
favorably oriented lamellae with the c-axis close to 15 –75° from the
principal stress directions the low-angle boundaries are formed within
α-phase [18,35]. The misorientation of intralamellar low-angle
boundaries increases with the subsequent straining. This leads to the
formation of intraphase boundaries with high-angle misorientation
[18]. At the same time, the α/β interphase boundaries become non-
coherent, contributing to the grooves formation and segmentation of
the α phase lamellae. The less favorably oriented lamellae (with an only
prism, or only pyramidal slip) are deformed through the shear strain.
The formation of shear bands leads to kinked lamellae with the in-
traphase low- or high-angle boundaries. As the deformation tempera-
ture decreases, the shear deformation e ﬀect increases. Hence, the HPT
deformation at room temperature should accelerate lamellae bending
and rotating of lamellae parts so that their resistance to slip decreases.
Namely, the sliding resistance of a microstructure in two-phase Ti-6Al-
4V alloy depends on the area fraction of α/α, β/β and α/β interphase
boundaries [36]. The sliding resistance decreases with decreasing α/α
or β/β and with increasing α/β interphase.
 Since the basal slip facilitates
globularizations, the hard oriented lamellae with the c-axis tilted less
than 15° from the principal stress directions have the slowest rates of
globularization process. Furthermore, continuous β phase decreases α/β
interphase boundary which has the lowest sliding resistance [36].
Therefore, there is little change in such regions of the lamellar micro-
structure. However, the morphology of β phase displayed as the lighter
color component in SEM-BSE images is also signi ﬁcantly changed with
an increase in the deformation strain, Fig. 6 . The elongated β phase is
fragmented to diﬀerent extents. Along with the formed globular α phase
grains, the HPT process led to the formation of small β phase particles
precipitating throughout the α phase grains. From the SEM-BSE images,
it is clear that dispersed globular precipitates inside the α grains are the
β phase. A number of these grains are found to contain a high β pre-
cipitate density, whereas others appeared to have rather low densities.
A distinct di ﬀerence between the morphology of β phase particles al-
lows us to suppose that two opposite processes occur during HPT pro-
cessing. The dissolution of β phase occurs during the initial stages of
deformation, but the reverse phase transformation occurs during the
same HPT process when the grain sizes are reduced signi ﬁcantly. The
second phase dissolution and formation of oversaturated solid solution
have been observed recently in di ﬀerent alloys subjected to SPD pro-
cessing [12,23,32,37]. Sergueeva et al. [12] reported that β phase was
disappeared after HPT processing of Ti-6Al-4V alloy with initial mi-
crostructure consisted of near-equiaxed primary α grains having ~
10 µm in diameter in a continuous β matrix. The observed di ﬀraction
patterns con ﬁrmed that the α phase was the only existing phase in the
as-processed state. In our case the fragmented β interlayers banding
associated with the torsional ﬂow is visible after HPT processing, de-
monstrating that only some β phase fractions are dissolved. The

 In this

3.2.2. HPT-deformed alloy
The HPT-deformed alloy shows improved mechanical properties.
The obtained improvement depends on the initial microstructure of
investigated alloy. In most cases, the enhancement in hardness and
tensile properties is observed. Due to the large uniform pressures and
the drastic shear deformation, the hardness and strength of the alloy are
generally increased after it is deformed by an HPT process. As shown in
Table 2, the values of hardness, yield and ultimate tensile strength are
mainly higher than that of the undeformed alloy. The increase in the
HV0.3, Rp0,2 and Rm of HPT-deformed alloy is found to be less when HPT
process is carried out at a lower temperature. Thus, after HPT-proces-
sing at room temperature the alloy with initial acicular microstructures
shows some minor increase in hardness. However, the hardness of alloy
with initial equiaxed and globular microstructures increases con-
siderably. It is 42 MPa and 63 MPa, respectively, higher than that of the
alloy before HPT-processing at room temperature. After HPT-processing
at 500 °C, the hardness increases to a much higher level, particularly in
the case of the initial globular microstructure. Although the martensitic
alloy shows the highest HV
0.3 value of 455 MPa, the alloy with initial
globular microstructure exhibits the hardness which is 117 MPa higher
than those of the alloy before HPT-processing.
The improved hardness and strength of coarse-grained materials can
be explained by the Hall-Petch (H-P) relationship [25]. Namely, their
hardness and strength increase with decreasing grain size, i.e. with
increasing dislocation density. Some authors [10,20,25], however, re-
ported that H-P mechanism may not be applicable to the most nano-
grained materials. Due to the di ﬀerent e ﬀect of dislocations on the
hardening in such materials, the softening can occur with decreasing
grain size. Our results are consistent with this observation, showing that
HPT-deformation hardens the Ti-6Al-4V ELI alloy when crystallites are
reﬁned to 14 nm. As crystallite size further decreases, the hardness of
investigated alloy remains more or less the same.
A signiﬁcant diﬀerence between the undeformed and HPT-deformed
alloy can also be observed in the stress-strain behavior. The stress-strain
curves of HPT-deformed alloy in Fig. 12 clearly show that for both HPT-
processing temperatures they reach a peak ﬂow stress and then a
sudden stress drop appears implying that plastic instability commences
at the very early stage of plastic deformation. The decrease in the ﬂow
stress with increased strain is probably due to the shear banding process
[43], although Wei et al. [21] suggested that the similar plastic in-
stability observed in the microcompression of HPT nanocrystalline
tantalum may also be due to the plastic buckling. The HPT-deformed
alloy shows higher peak stress and larger plasticity than the un-
deformed alloy except in the case of the alloy with initial globular
microstructure after HPT-processing at room temperature, Fig. 12d.
Moreover, its stress-strain curve exhibits discontinuous yielding related
to the rapid generation of mobile dislocations from grain boundary
sources. Such behavior during tensile deformation can most likely to
explained by taking into account signi ﬁcant grain re ﬁnement during
the HPT-processing and appearance of regions with agglomeration of
ﬁne well-de ﬁned β grains. Also, the e ﬀect of solute atoms could not be
excluded. Namely, the β phase in metastable globular microstructure is
V enriched and Ti and Al depleted when compared to α phase [1]. This
eﬀect is less pronounced after the slow cooling from ST temperature.

interstitial elements such as carbon and nitrogen. The substitutional

3.3. Fractographic analysis
The appearance of the fracture surfaces of the undeformed and HPT-
deformed alloy after tensile tests is shown in Fig. 14.
The SEM observations reveal that the fracture surface morphology
corresponds to the variation in the alloy ductility. The signi ﬁcant dif-
ferences in the fracture surface roughness are already visible between
the four initial microstructures of undeformed alloy, Fig. 14a, d, g and j.
The fracture surface of the alloy with the initial fully lamellar micro-
structure is very rough, with the large quasi-cleavage facets and ridges
at which the crack propagation direction changes, Fig. 14a. The sizes of
these ﬂat regions, comparable to the lamellar colony size, suggest that
the transgranular quasi-cleavage fracture occurs through the colonies.
The ﬁne transgranular dimples in small relatively smooth zones and
numerous dimples on the walls of the ridges indicate that the fracture
also proceeds by a ductile micromechanism. The secondary cracks
created by the linkage of the microvoids at the α/β lamellar interfaces
or in the β phase reveal the crack propagation direction around α la-
mellas.
The fracture surface of alloy with the initial martensitic micro-
structure looks almost similar, indicating that the fracture process is
complex. Two fracture modes predominate: transgranular brittle frac-
ture and ductile intergranular dimple mode, Fig. 14d. The brittle frac-
ture regions, caused by the cleavage of the martensitic plates, are sur-
rounded by the ridges with small and shallow dimples on the walls. The
transgranular cleavage facets are mainly present on the fracture sur-
face, which is clear evidence of the decreasing ductility of the alloy with
this acicular microstructure. The microvoids located at the α′/β inter-
faces suggest that the crack propagates partially intergranularly. The β
phase is a ductile phase and rupture in microvoid coalescence mode of
ductile fracture.
The equiaxed and globular microstructures are bene ﬁcial to the
improvement of the fracture resistance of the alloy. Based on the
fracture surface roughness, it appears that the extent of the main
fracture mode is changed. The relatively ﬂat fracture surfaces indicate
that the transgranular brittle fracture mode is less pronounced, Fig. 14g
and j. The size of the quasi-cleavage facets is on the order of the average
primary α grain size. This means that the majority of the primary α
phase grains are fractured in cleavage mode. These cleavage facets have
larger sizes in the case of the equiaxed microstructure, which is in ac-
cordance with the ﬁner globular microstructure. The greater amount of
the β phase than in the case of both acicular microstructures enhances
the ductile fracture mode, corresponding to the higher elongation to
failure. Dimples of various sizes are present in greater fraction than on
the fracture surface of the alloy with the fully lamellar microstructure.
The large dimples are found to be initiated at α/β interfaces, while the
transgranular dimples are smaller. This indicates that the voids pre-
ferentially nucleate at the phase boundaries. According to Chao et al.
[44],
the voids initiate at the peak stress and gradually evolve in
dimples through further plastic deformation. Numerous microvoids,
clearly observed along the α/β interfaces, suggest that void growth in
the ﬁrst stage of plastic ﬂow produces large dimples. With an increase
in the β phase fraction, more transgranular dimples appear on the
fracture surface. These substantially equiaxed dimples are ﬁner and
shallower on the fracture surface of the alloy with the globular mi-
crostructure than those on the fracture surface of the alloy with the

microvoids, which are associated with the ﬁne β phase particles, sug-

4. Conclusions
The investigation of the microstructure, mechanical behavior and
fracture micromechanisms for the HPT-processed Ti-6Al-4V ELI alloy
with di ﬀerent initial microstructures led to the following conclusions:
(1) The applied HPT process successfully produces ultra ﬁne grained
two-phase ( α + β) microstructure with crystallites 10 –35 nm in
size, which in turn signi ﬁcantly a ﬀects the hardness, tensile and
fracture properties. The microstructural re ﬁnement occurs through
the simultaneous α and β grains fragmentation and phase trans-
formation during the HPT-processing.
(2) The extent of grain re ﬁnement strongly depends on the initial mi-
crostructure and HPT-processing temperature. Among the initial
fully lamellar, martensitic, equiaxed and globular microstructures,
the martensitic one contributes to the highest grain re ﬁnement level
and microstructural homogeneity. A higher processing e ﬃciency is
observed when the HPT-deformation temperature increases from
room temperature to 500 °C. The hot HPT deformation results in
homogeneous fully globularized microstructures, independent on
the alloy initial microstructural state. Developed microstructures
are slightly coarser, but the grains are in the submicron range.
(3) The re ﬁnement of microstructure provides increases in the alloy
hardness, yield and ultimate tensile strength without a loss in
ductility. The exceptional hardness of 455 MPa is achieved for the
alloy with the initial martensitic microstructure after the HPT
processing at 500 °C, although the increase is almost 2.5 times less
than in the alloy with the initial globular microstructure. The extent
of the yield and ultimate tensile strength improvement is also
greater for the alloy HPT-processed at 500 °C. The improvement in
the tensile strength is accompanied by an increase in ductility. The
HPT-processed alloy, with the initial equiaxed and globular mi-
crostructures, shows moderately higher yield and ultimate tensile
strength without much a ﬀecting the ductility (16% elongation to
failure) than before HPT processing. The alloy with the initial aci-
cular microstructures exhibits the signi ﬁcantly higher yield
strength,
ultimate tensile strength and elongation to failure after
the HPT processing at both temperatures. The best combination of
ultimate tensile strength (1546 MPa) and ductility (18.8% elonga-
tion to failure) is obtained for the alloy with the initial martensitic
microstructure subjected to the HPT processing at 500 °C.
(4) The HPT processing at both temperatures causes a remarkable de-
crease in the elastic modulus. Its values are 1.6 –1.9 times lower
than in the case of the alloy initial state. After the HPT processing at
room temperature and 500 °C, they are in a range of 68 –74 GPa and
76–83 GPa, respectively. The lowest elastic modulus is achieved for
the alloy with the initial fully lamellar microstructure. However,
the HPT-processed alloy with the initial martensitic microstructure
is superior in both strength-ductility balance and elastic admissible
strain (strength-to-elastic modulus ratio). The elastic admissible
strain (19.8 × 10
−3) achieved after the HPT processing at 500 °C is

high enough for the orthopedic applications.
(5) The fracture surface characteristics correspond to the variation in
ductility. Fracture process is complex and involves transgranular
quasi-cleavage and ductile dimple fracture mode. The dominant
fracture mode changes with the α and β grains fragmentation and
microstructural homogeneity. The increased fraction of globular
grains, obtained by increasing the HPT processing temperature,
leads to the decreased contribution of the cleavage brittle fracture
mode to overall fracture. When the alloy is HPT-processed at 500 °C
ductile dimple fracture mode is much pronounced.
