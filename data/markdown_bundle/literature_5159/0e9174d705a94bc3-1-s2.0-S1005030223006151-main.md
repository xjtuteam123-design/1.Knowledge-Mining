The precipitation of topologically close-packed (TCP) phases is the result of microstructure instabilities 
of Ni-based superalloys. This review seeks to comprehensively collate all the available information on 
TCP phases in SX superalloys based on the latest ﬁndings. First, the thermodynamics and kinetics of the 
TCP phase precipitation are introduced. Meanwhile, the morphology, composition and orientation of TCP 
phases and their sequential transformation are summarized in detail. Further, the factors affecting the 
precipitation of these phases are sorted out. Besides, the proposed damage mechanisms of TCP phases 
are listed. Finally, several control and prediction methods of the TCP phase precipitation are reviewed, so 
the alloy designer can better balance the relationship between microstructure stabilities and properties 
of the superalloy.

1. Introduction 
Ni-based single crystal (SX) superalloys are usually served as 
high temperature components in aeronautical and industrial gas 
turbine applications such as blade, exposing to severe thermal and 
mechanical loadings in hot combustion gasses. These alloys have 
outstanding high temperature creep, fatigue and oxidation resis- 
tance due to their unique microstructure, which consist of inter- 
metallic γ′ phase with a L1 2 crystal structure embedded in the 
disordered FCC solid solution γ matrix [1–4] . The design of these 
alloys in terms of refractory elements additions (Mo, Ta, W and 
Re etc.) improved high-temperature mechanical properties [5–7] . 
However, excessive quantities of these elements result in serious 
instability of the microstructure, that is, the alloys prone to topo- 
logically close-packed (TCP) phases [8–14] . The structure of TCP 
phases is relatively complex and consist of pseudo-hexagonal lay- 
ers of atoms stacked to produce lattice sites with co-ordination 
numbers as high as 16, which is signiﬁcantly higher than that 
of common FCC and HCP phase, i.e., 12, which is the cause they 
are named [15–17] . As the atoms in TCP phase are arranged very 
closely and have very low symmetry, there are few slip systems. 
Therefore, TCP phase exhibits extremely low plasticity and ex-

The TCP phase precipitates in γ matrix and grows up by deplet- 
ing the solid solution strengthening elements, such as Cr, W and 
Re , thus reduce the strength of γ matrix [19] . Based on the above 
characteristics, TCP phases are detrimental to the alloy by reducing 
the fatigue and creep strength and seriously threatening the ser- 
vice life of the alloy under high temperature service [20–23] . Sev- 
eral types of TCP phases have been widely found in SX superalloys, 
such as μ, σ, P and R phase [15] , and their crystallographic struc- 
tures are shown in Table 1 . More complicated, however, the inter- 
growth is frequently observed, and they can also transform each 
other. 
Since the advent of the third generation SX superalloys at the 
end of the last century, these phases have become an obstacle that 
must be overcome in the development of new these alloys [24–
27] . Platinum group metals (PGM) such as Ir, Pt and Ru are posi- 
tive to inhibit the precipitation of TCP phases, and Ru has the best 
eﬃciency supported by many favorable data [28–30] . The addition 
of Ru promotes the development of the fourth to sixth generation 
SX superalloys [31–35] . However, there have been widely differing 
versions in the published articles on the mechanisms of Ru as a 
TCP suppressant. Many factors affect the precipitation of the TCP 
phase, such as alloying element, exposure condition and applied 
stress. This paper attempt to collate and critically review above in- 
formation on the TCP phase in especially Ni-based SX superalloys.

2. Precipitation behavior of the TCP phase 
2.1. Nucleation and growth mechanism 
For the cast material, TCP precipitates form ﬁrst at the den- 
drite core as a result of the micro-segregation of Re , W, Mo and Cr 
( Fig. 1 (a)) [ 19 , 36–41 ], and eventually spread into interdendritic re- 
gions with the increase of exposure time. However, the threshold 
value for TCP phase formation is only exceeded within dendritic 
cores but not within interdendritic regions, thus TCP phase forma- 
tion is restricted to dendrite cores for most alloys in limited time 
[ 29 , 37 ], as shown in Fig. 1 (b).

2.2. Transformation kinetics 
Kinetics of the TCP phase precipitation follow the “C” curve re- 
lationship with precipitation being most rapid at some intermedi- 
ate temperature, which depends on the diffusion rate and amount 
of the refractory elements required to form the precipitating phase 
[ 36 , 52 , 53 ]. At the nose of the “C” curve, where phase precipitates 
most rapidly, the temperature is high enough for diffusion to sup- 
ply a suﬃcient amount of the refractory elements so that the pre- 
cipitating phase can grow, but low enough to provide suﬃcient 
driving force for the precipitation to occur [52] . At lower temper- 
atures, diffusion of the refractory elements is too slow to sustain 
precipitation of TCP phase [52] . At temperatures above the nose of 
the “C” curve the driving force for precipitation is reduced delay- 
ing precipitation and as the temperature is increased further the 
TCP phase becomes unstable and will go back into solution. How- 
ever, it worth noticed that the temperatures required for dissolu- 
tion of the TCP phases are suﬃciently high that exposure of the 
γ/ γ′ microstructures to these temperatures will result in deleteri- 
ous effects on alloy mechanical properties [52] .

2.3. Composition of the TCP phase 
Solid solution strengtheners like Mo, Cr, W and especially Re , 
segregate to the TCP phase ( Fig. 4 ) [ 29 , 54–56 ]. However, the spe- 
ciﬁc content of these elements varies with the amounts of refrac- 
tory metals in the parent alloy as well as the manufacturing pro- 
cess, even in the same sample [ 24 , 50 , 57 ]. Also, the temperature or 
exposing time also slightly change the composition of TCP phases 
[58–61] . Further, these phases can transform each other into more 
complex mixed structures due to the similarity in the composition. 
Hence, the type of TCP phase can hardly be identiﬁed by chemical 
composition and morphology. Even so, these phases can be dis- 
tinguished by their distinct crystallographic structures ( Table 1 ). A 
reminder is that the lattice parameters are not a deﬁnite value and 
they ﬂuctuate with the different chemical compositions of the TCP 
phases [59] .

When expressing the lattice correspondences between a coher- 
ent precipitate and the matrix, it is customary to express the crys- 
talline orientation relationship with one pair of parallel planes and 
one pair of parallel directions. But generally, such orientation re- 
lationships do not specify explicitly if these are mere parallel cor- 
relations or lattice coherency conditions [60] . Table 2 presents a 
summary of the different orientation relationships between the 
TCP phases and γ matrix. It shows that multiple orientation re- 
lationships have been observed even for the same precipitate. The 
TCP phases nucleate preferentially on low energy planes, i.e., the 
close packed planes {111}, forming a coherent or semi-coherent in- 
terface, and exhibit distinctive orientation relationships with the 
matrix [ 15 , 58 , 77–79 ]. The relationship appears to allow the best 
atomic ﬁt between TCP phase and matrix, which gives rise to 
a very marked anisotropy of interfacial structure and this has a 
major inﬂuence on the shape and growth kinetics of TCP phases 
[ 46 , 80 ]. Besides, Long et al. [ 59 , 60 ] reported that TCP phases with 
different morphologies can share the same lattice correspondence 
with the matrix. They conjectured that the different morphologies 
imply different lattice parameters which are most likely to be re- 
lated to differences in chemical composition. Moreover, the lat- 
tice parameter in turn determines the interface lattice misﬁt be- 
tween TCP phase and matrix and thus shape and orientation of 
the TCP phase. So, compared with the lath-like precipitates, the 
plate-like precipitates achieved a better coherency across the in- 
terface with the matrix, thereby reducing the interface energy. 
This also explains why the plate-like TCP phase usually preferen- 
tially precipitates as metastable phase when it undergoes sequen- 
tial phase transformation. Such transformation will be discussed in 
detail next section.

Tan et al. [65] observed the intergrowth of P phase with μ
phase after long-term exposure, and the stacking fault free area 
and large stacking fault area in TCP phase correspond to μ phase 
and P phase respectively. Accordingly, the authors speculated that 
the P phase may nucleate at stacking faults and subsequently grow 
at the expense of μ phase. Rae and Reed [15] studied the pre- 
cipitation of the TCP phases in Re -containing superalloys compre- 
hensively, and they believed that the formation of the TCP phases 
undergoes a precipitation sequence. More speciﬁcally, in the ﬁrst 
stage the nuclei of the metastable precipitates are formed and 
grow. With prolongation of an exposure time, or with increase of a 
temperature, the metastable phase becomes the nucleation core of 
new phases, and then the ﬁrst phase dissolves and is replaced by 
the secondary precipitates. Actually, this statement was ﬁrst put 
forward by Darolia et al. [77] . In the early stages when the se- 
quence transition was proposed, σ phase was often regarded as 
the one that ﬁrst precipitated and other phases of μ, P or R phase 
grow from it [ 29 , 54 , 71 , 83 , 98 , 99 ]. However, Gao et al. [45] found 
that μ phase can nucleate directly in γ matrix with lath-like mor- 
phology at 950 °C, see Fig. 2 . They also found P phase can trans- 
form to μ phase at 1100 °C [73] ( Fig. 8 ) and μ phase can trans- 
form to C phase (C phase also a TCP phase studied by [100] ) when 
temperature is above 1150 °C . The C phase starts from μ phase 
and grows at the expense of μ phase and increasingly become 
the dominant precipitate. Most notably, no planar defects were ob- 
served in the interior of the C phase [101] . Recently, the study 
[102] unveiled the role of the defects in the μ phase plays in the 
intergrowth of the P phase. They discovered that the P phase orig- 
inates from intrinsic (110) faulted twin boundaries, where the en- 
ergy is very low. 
To summarize, these ﬁndings provide: (i) Some of TCP precipi- 
tates are in fact metastable, and the sequence transformation will 
happen in the precipitation process with the change of tempera- 
ture or time. (ii) The planar defects and intergrowth structure are 
unstable, where tend to promote the phase transform through the 
diffusion and redistribution of elements. (iii) The ﬁrst precipitated 
phase and the sequential transformation are related to the alloy 
system.

3.1.3. Effect of Re and Cr 
Re: Re is the most important TCP-forming element ( Fig. 4 ). Re 
strongly segregated in the dendrite region with the smallest diffu- 
sion coeﬃcient which cannot be fully eliminated even in long-time 
and high-temperature solution treatment [ 38 , 72 , 128–133 ]. This is 
also the reason why TCP precipitates preferentially in dendrite 
cores. It is suggested that high levels of Re expand the precipi- 
tation temperature range and increase the nucleation rate of TCP 
phases. That is to say, the addition of Re shifts the C curve to the 
left [ 52 , 53 ], as shown in Fig. 11 . Under the same exposure time and 
temperature, different morphologies of TCP phase appear in the SX 
superalloys with different Re content ( Fig. 12 ). Consequently, the 
incubation period shortens with increasing the supersaturation of 
γ phase by high levels of Re [53] . Besides, it was also reported 
that Re can increase the volume fraction of γ′ phase which con- 
centrates the refractory elements in the γ phase and hence exac- 
erbating TCP formation [ 15 , 24 , 134 ]. 
Cr: Cr, as well as Re , partition most strongly to γ matrix 
[ 113 , 114 , 135 ], is an important constituent of TCP phases. Cr is a key 
element of the oxidation and corrosion in SX superalloys; however, 
it contributes to σ phase formation [ 15 , 71 , 136 , 137 ]. One explana- 
tion is that high levels of Cr addition signiﬁcantly increases the 
partitioning behavior of Re at high temperatures [ 138 , 139 ]. There- 
fore, the Cr concentration has gradually been decreased and higher 
levels of Re was added in the third and fourth-generations of these 
alloys. Considering the low temperature oxidation and hot corro- 
sion resistance, thus the Cr content has been increased modestly 
to about 4.2 wt.% in the newer generations (containing Ru) of al- 
loys [140] . 
3.1.4. Effect of Ru 
Ru can effectively suppress the formation of TCP phases 
[ 22 , 29 , 44 , 46 , 115 , 141–144 ], as shown in Fig. 13 . Ru partitions to γ
matrix [ 22 , 29 , 83 , 135 , 141 , 142 , 145–148 ] and generally distributes the 
same in the TCP phase and γ matrix [ 28 , 46 , 86 ] ( Fig. 4 ). It was 
suggested that there is no qualitative change of the phase trans- 
formation and no strongly change of the chemical composition of

[ 76 , 86 , 99 , 113 , 115 , 135 , 144 , 149–155 ] ( Fig. 14 ). In other words, the 
partitioning ratio k i ( Eq. (4) ) shall be reduced by the addition of

shown that Ru and other PGM do not signiﬁcantly affect the parti-

between Ru and other elements. As for the volume fraction of γ′

Nevertheless, Hobbs et al. [83] found that the addition of Ru may 
destabilize the γ′ phase at elevated temperatures, i.e., the rate of 
γ′ volume fraction reduction is markedly more rapid in the Ru- 
containing alloy, but this view has not been widely accepted [66] . 
Consequently, the “reverse partitioning” effect is alloy dependent 
and this probably also holds for the γ′ phase fraction. 
Mechanism 2: It is known that the nucleation rate is controlled 
not only by the driving force but also by the activation barrier for 
nucleation. The activation energy barrier for nucleation is a func- 
tion of the volume free energy increase attributed to the creation 
of a TCP/ γ interface. There are two contributions: ﬁrstly, the TCP/ γ
interfacial energy; and secondly, an elastic strain energy due to the 
transformed volume not perfectly ﬁtting the space originally occu- 
pied by the matrix. As the former case, the combination of experi-

interfacial energy of μ/ γ ( Fig. 15 ). Ru segregates to TCP phases, so 
the difference of interfacial atomic coordination type between Ru- 
containing and Ru-free alloy inﬂuences the interfacial energy [163] .

lattice parameter of the matrix is increased. On the other hand, Ru 
was reported to partition to TCP phases at the expense of Re , but 
this should not affect the lattice of TCP phases due to similarities 
in the atomic radii of both elements [66] . Tan et al. [ 86 ] calculated 
the misﬁt of μ/ γ and found that the misﬁt was increased with the 
addition of Ru. Therefore, Ru increases the elastic strain energy for 
the nucleation of the TCP phase by increasing the lattice misﬁt be- 
tween TCP and γ phase.

factors which in return inﬂuences the precipitation of TCP phases 
[50] . Therefore, to ﬁgure out the role of these factors, further ex-

3.1.5. Effect of Ir and Pt 
Apart from Ru, other PGM such as Ir and Pt also draw many at- 
tentions on the microstructural stability of SX superalloys. Pt has 
a preference to partition to the γ′ phase [ 145–147 , 157 ], while the 
partition of Ir is controversial. Murakami et al. [146] found that Ir 
tends to partition to the γ phase, but Sluytman et al. [145] and 
Yokokawa et al. [157] found that Ir partitions equally between on 
the γ and γ′ phase. Meanwhile, Yokokawa et al. [157] presented 
that the addition of 1 at.% PGM does not affect the partitioning be- 
havior. Recently, the in situ observation results proved that they all 
play a positive role in inhibiting the formation of TCP phase [28] , 
but Ru plays the most signiﬁcant effect and Pt has the weak effect 
( Fig. 19 ). The results implied the Ru-containing alloy has the high- 
est nucleation inhibition rate of TCP phases, while the Ir-containing 
alloy has the smallest average length of TCP phase. However, the 
inhibition effect of Ir, Pt and Ru on the TCP phase formation weak- 
ened with the increase of the temperature [28] . Sluytman et al. 
[145] presented that Ir can lower the volume fraction γ′ phase and 
reduce the partitioning of W, Re , Ru, Al and Pt. Therefore, we can 
speculate that the positive effect on the precipitation of TCP phases 
of Ir based on this. 
3.1.6. Effect of minor additions (C, B and Hf) 
The addition of minor elements is usually added to some SX 
superalloys to reduce the solidiﬁcation defects [ 67 , 172 , 173 ]. How- 
ever, these additions were reported to indirectly affect the precipi- 
tation of TCP phases. Firstly, the preferential regions of TCP phases 
changes from dendrite cores to interdendritic regions, where these 
phases prefer to nucleate and grow on MC carbides due to lo- 
cal solute enrichment of TCP-forming elements ( Fig. 20 ). But the 
growth of TCP phases is hindered due to very localized microsegre- 
gation. Secondly, these additions were suggested to affect the nu- 
cleation of the TCP phase. It was revealed that the prior forma- 
tion of carbides absorbs Cr, Mo and Co, resulting in the decrease of 
the concentration of TCP-forming elements, consequently retarding 
the TCP phase transformation [ 67 , 174 ]. However, in some alloys the 
minor additions promote the formation of TCP phases, and it was 
suggested to be related to the content of Cr [175] . 
3.2. Effect of standard heat treatment 
In the as-cast condition, sometimes the TCP phase forms in the 
interdendritic region after cooling from casting due to the strong 
partition of Ta [ 36 , 55 ], and these phases form much more rapidly 
than in the standard heat-treated alloy during thermal exposure 
( Fig. 21 ) [36] . After standard heat treatment, the dendrite core is 
more prone to TCP phase formation, because of the faster diffusion 
of Ta compared to Re [ 36 , 176 ]. The behavior with regard to TCP 
phase formation depends strongly upon the degree of microseg- 
regation remaining [36] . Generally, a proper heat treatment regime 
including solution and aging treatment is very necessary for reduc- 
ing the dendritic segregation and suppressing the formation of TCP 
phases [5] . The better the homogenization effect is, the smaller the 
precipitation tendency of TCP phases is, and the degree of homog- 
enization is positively related to the time and temperature of solu- 
tion treatment. However, a recent study reported that elevated so- 
lution temperature resulted in an abnormal increase of TCP phases 
[177] . Speciﬁcally, the number of TCP phases in the standard heat- 
treated sample was more than that in the as-cast sample after 
long-term thermal exposure ( Fig. 22 ). They suggested that the so- 
lution and aging treatment improve higher volume fraction of γ′ 
phase in the dendrite core and therefore increase the supersatura- 
tion of Re and W in γ matrix [177] .

4.2. Depletion of refractory elements in γ phase 
Depleting of the important strengthening elements is gen- 
erally considered the main reason for TCP phases detrimental 
[ 52 , 133 , 182 , 197 , 198 ], and it is inevitable as long as the TCP phase 
precipitates. The degree of “softening effect” depends on the to- 
tal amount of TCP phases, but is independent of the type. Pessah 
et al. [126] and Simonetti et al. [81] did works on the effect of μ
phase and suggested that the small volume fraction of μ phase ( < 
1 vol.%) did not affect the fracture mechanism during creep, tensile, 
impact and fatigue properties until a large number of TCP phases 
exist. They explained that the deleterious effect may be counter- 
balanced by the increase of ductility resulting from the γ′ coars- 
ening. Actually, this mechanism is diﬃcult to evaluate, as damage 
mechanisms of creep are complex, including loss of the interfa- 
cial coherency, coarsening of the γ/ γ′ microstructure, precipita- 
tion of TCP phases and growth of porosity [198] . The usual proce- 
dure for investigating the effect of the TCP phase is inducing these 
phases by stress free thermal exposure, followed by creep or fa- 
tigue testing. Accordingly, some effects on creep or other mechani- 
cal behavior could be also due to the other reasons [ 64 , 126 , 198 ]. 
Recently, the hardness of γ phase and the concentration of re- 
fractory elements near and far from TCP phases have been mea- 
sured and small differences were found because equilibrium was 
reached[ 19 , 24 , 54 ], as shown in Fig. 29 . However, these results do 
not negate the softening effect of the TCP phase. It is known that 
the dendritic core possess higher refractory elements content and 
smaller γ′ precipitates, so the hardness value in the dendrite core 
are higher than that in the interdendritic region at room tempera- 
ture [199] . However, the results showed that the γ phase has simi- 
lar hardness values in the dendritic and interdendritic regions after 
creep deformation [19] . Therefore, it suggested that the precipita- 
tion of TCP phase take Re out of the matrix leading to a softening 
effect. 
4.3. Disruption to rafting structure of γ′ phase 
The TCP phase was reported to disrupt the rafting structure 
of the γ′ phase and reduce the resistance of the alloy, while 
the complete rafted γ′ phase may effectively hinder the move- 
ment of dislocations during creep [ 22 , 123 , 200–204 ]. Graverend 
et.al [205] stated that the presence of TCP-needles generating 
stress ﬁeld modiﬁcations will cause a local change in terms of 
length and thickness in the morphology of γ′ phase. Such stress 
ﬁeld increases the dislocation density at the γ/ γ′ interface, which 
improve the kinetics of diffusion [206–208] . However, this change 
was proved would not spread over a long range beyond the TCP- 
needles [205] . Therefore, it is reasonable to speculate that the ap- 
pearance of a small amount of TCP phase with small size will not 
cause a great disruption of γ′ rafting. 
4.4. Pores associated with the formation of TCP phases 
This mechanism was put forward by Reed et al. [ 203 , 209 ], and 
they believed that the creep damage mechanisms of CMSX-4 alloy 
must be associated with the TCP-related pores, as shown in Fig. 30 . 
As mentioned before, there is a local stress ﬁeld around the TCP 
phase, which results in a higher density of dislocations, and such 
dislocations can emit vacancies [ 210 , 211 ], so the deformation pores 
prefer to nucleate around TCP phases [ 162 , 202 ]. Meanwhile, solidi- 
ﬁcation pores and homogenization pores also expanded due to the 
presence of TCP phases [202] . 
In conclusion, the size and volume fraction is decisive for the 
harm of TCP phases. There may be a critical amount of TCP phases 
which can be tolerated before the creep life decreases [81] , how- 
ever, it’s diﬃcult to determine the critical amount because it is 
likely to depend on the alloy system.

rated into well-deﬁned but overlapping regions corresponding to 
different structure types. Additionally, this structure map is a good 
way to test the commercially utilized methods, such as CALPHAD 
and new PHACOMP, in predicting the susceptibility of Ni-based su- 
peralloys to TCP-phase precipitation. The validity of this structure 
map was conﬁrmed by ab-initio calculations for numerous inter- 
metallic systems [ 20 , 220 , 221 ]. However, Galilea et al. [55] used this

of TCP phases in ERBO/1 (a second generation of SX superalloy). 
The results show that the TTNi7 thermodynamic database failed 
to predict the presence of all observed TCP phases under as-cast 
and isothermal treatments, but help to interpret the experimen- 
tal results. Therefore, this method will require enhanced thermo- 
dynamic databases to overcome the lack of experimental data to 
become future reliable predictions of TCP stability in Ni-based su- 
peralloys. In summary, although the predictions reported already 
have been proven to exhibit some credibility, they should not be 
are totally relied upon and must be experimentally veriﬁed. 
6. Conclusions 
TCP phases appearance represents the instability of the alloy 
microstructure. TCP phases tend to precipitate in the dendritic re- 
gion after standard heat treatment, where the main TCP-forming 
elements Re , and W segregate. TCP particles nucleate in the matrix 
and grow by absorbing the solid solution strengthening elements. 
The μ, σ, P and R phase are commonly observed in SX superalloys. 
The type, morphology, composition, orientation and lattice param- 
eter of TCP phases vary with the change of alloy system, exposure 
time and temperature. Also, there is no speciﬁc orientation even 
for the precipitate of the same type. These phases usually precip- 
itate on low index planes and show signiﬁcant anisotropy. The in- 
terface with the best atomic ﬁt between the TCP phase and matrix

mation of TCP phase undergoes a precipitation sequence, meaning 
that the new phase can nucleate and grow on the formed phase, 
and then the ﬁrst phase dissolves and is replaced by the secondary 
precipitates with prolongation of an exposure time or temperature. 
The alloying elements, process of standard heat treatment, expo- 
sure time and temperature, and applied stress all affect the for- 
mation of TCP phase. Remarkably, Re exacerbates TCP phase for- 
mation, while Ru suppresses it when low Cr content. TCP phase 
will inﬂuence the mechanical properties, but a small volume frac- 
tion of TCP particles will not cause great damage to the proper- 
ties of the alloy. Some related predictive methods have been de- 
veloped to predict the precipitation tendency of TCP phases. Some 
of them have been successfully applied to the screening of new 
alloys, but a more reliable prediction requires enhanced thermo- 
dynamic databases to overcome the lack of experimental data. Re- 
ducing the content of refractory elements, prolonging the solution 
time, increasing the cooling rate of melt, adding Ru and reducing 
the content of Cr are the effective ways to inhibit the TCP phase 
precipitation.
