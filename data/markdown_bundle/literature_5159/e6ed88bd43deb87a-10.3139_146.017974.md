Microstructures and mechanical properties
of V – V3Si eutectic composites
Dedicated to Professor Dr. Peter Neumann on the occasion of his 65th birthday
V–V 3Si eutectic alloys were directionally solidified in a
high temperature optical floating zone furnace. Depending
on the solidification conditions, several microstructures
were observed, such as well-aligned broken lamellar, fi-
brous, or cellular microstructures. The interphase spacings
increased with decreasing solidification rates in agreement
with the Jackson-Hunt theory. The mechanical properties
of the individual phases were investigated by nanoindenta-
tion. It was found that the modulus and nanoindentation
hardness of V 3Si are 214 and 13.8 GPa, respectively, and
those of the V solid-solution are 165 and 3.4 GPa, respec-
tively. The high-temperature strength was examined by ten-
sile testing at elevated temperatures. Preliminary results
show that the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature is
about 800 °C for this composite, and its strength is signifi-
cantly higher than conventional V solid-solution alloys.
Keywords: Transition metal silicide; Directional solidifi-
cation; Eutectics; Composites; Vanadium alloys; Interme-
tallics
1. Introduction
Vanadium alloys are of interest in first-wall applications in
fusion reactors because of their good mechanical properties
at intermediate temperatures (up to 700 °C), high thermal
shock resistance, and low activation characteristics [1 – 3].
A leading candidate material is the V – 4Cr – 4Ti (wt.%)
ternary alloy having a yield strength of about 230 MPa at
temperatures below 800 °C [1], which is achieved by solid
solution hardening. Vanadium alloys strengthened with in-
termetallic second phases may provide better mechanical
properties because they combine the properties of the vana-
dium solid-solution with those of a hard intermetallic
phase. Among the candidate intermetallics, V
3Si is consid-
ered to be a good strengthening constituent because of its
high melting point, high strength, good stability to
1965 °C, and relatively low density. As can be seen in the
V – Si phase diagram (Fig. 1 [4]), a V – V3Si eutectic exists
at compositions around 13 at.% Si. Eutectic alloys with
rod-like or cellular microstructures, consisting of a V-rich
solid solution as the continuous matrix and the V 3Si inter-
metallic as the discontinuous phase, have been produced in
previous studies using the Czochralski, cold-crucible, crys-
tal growth system [5, 6]. In those studies [5, 6], the V –
V3Si system was categorized as a regular eutectic in which
both phases grow in a nonfaceted manner.
Eutectic structures tend to exhibit a regular morphology
(rod-like or lamellar) if both phases possess a low entropy
of fusion, typically, DS/Rg < 2, where DS is the entropy of
fusion and Rg is the gas constant [7]. In addition, when the
volume fractions of the phases are approximately the same
(0.3 < Vf < 0.5), which is encouraged by a symmetrical
phase diagram, there is a preference for the formation of la-
mellar structures (e. g., in the Cr– Cr 3Si eutectic [8 – 11]),
but when the volume fraction of one phase is small
(Vf < 0.3), rod-like structures are preferred (e. g., in the
NiAl-Mo system [12 – 14]) [15]. In the V – V 3Si eutectic,
the V solid solution phase has a low entropy of fusion
(10.4 J/K mole) allowing it to grow in a nonfaceted manner.
But the entropy of fusion for V 3Si is 21.2 J/K mole [16],
which makes the ratio DS/Rg = 2.5, right around the critical
value at which the transition from faceted to nonfaceted
growth occurs. The volume fraction of V3Si predicted from
the phase diagram is ∼ 0.5. Therefore, if both the V solid so-
lution and V3Si phases grow in a nonfaceted manner, a reg-
ular lamellar structure is expected, which is in conflict with
previously observed structures (rod-like [5, 6]). On the

2. Experimental procedures
2.1. Alloy preparation
Several V – V3Si alloys with nominal compositions from
12.5 to 13.0 at.% Si were arc melted and drop cast into a cy-
lindrical copper mold measuring 10 mm in diameter and
100 mm in length. The vanadium ( > 99.8 % pure) and sili-
con ( > 99.995 % pure) starting materials were carefully
weighed and then mixed by arc melting, with the buttons
flipped and re-melted five times to ensure good mixing be-
fore drop casting. Total weight losses after melting and
casting were less than 0.05 %, which led to negligible
changes in alloy composition after melting. Therefore, all
compositions discussed in this paper are nominal composi-
tions (atomic%) unless otherwise stated.
2.2. Directional solidification of V – V 3Si eutectic
The drop cast ingots were directionally solidified using an
optical floating zone furnace as described elsewhere [8, 9].
Briefly, infrared radiation emitted from a xenon arc lamp
was focussed on specimens enclosed in a quartz tube which
was first evacuated and back filled with flowing argon gas.
Drop-cast polycrystalline rods were used as the feed materi-
al, and pieces cut from several different directionally solidi-
fied rods were used for the seed. The seed rod and feed rod
were rotated in opposite directions during the zone melting
to form a homogeneous molten zone. The effects of growth
conditions on microstructures were systematically investi-
gated in an alloy having the eutectic composition. Growth
rates were varied from 20 to 150 mm/h and the specimen
rotation rate was fixed at 60 rpm.
2.3. Metallography
The directionally solidified rods were cut transversely and
longitudinally using electro-discharge machining, and re-
presentative samples were mounted in epoxy. After grind-
ing the samples flat through 600 grit SiC paper, they were
polished with diamond paste (particle size 0.5 lm) using
an automatic vibratory polishing machine. To reveal the mi-
crostructure of the eutectics, the samples were etched in a
solution of 50 % glycerol, 25 % hydrofluoric acid and 25 %
nitric acid. The etched specimens were examined by both
optical and scanning electron microscopy (SEM).
2.4. Orientation relationships between the two phases
Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) patterns were ac-
quired in a FEI XL30 FEG scanning electron microscope
using an accelerating voltage of 20 kV and a 20 mm work-
ing distance. Patterns from individual lamellae of V
3Si and
V solid-solution were recorded and indexed using orienta-
tion imaging microscopy (OIM). The Kikuchi-line patterns
were automatically indexed by theOIM ANALYSIS package
(release version 3.08, TSLTM http://www.tsl-oim.com/ ).

3. Results and discussions
3.1. Microstructures of drop-cast alloys
Optical micrographs of the arc melted and drop cast rods
(Fig. 2) showed that a eutectic structure was present in all
the samples. In the off-eutectic alloys, an excess of one or
the other of the pro-eutectic phases (V solid-solution or
V3Si) was always present. The pro-eutectic phases were
identified both by their relative hardnesses (the Vickers
hardness of the V solid-solution phase is about HV 185
and that of V3Si about HV 980) and by the presence or ab-
sence of cracks in the V3Si phases and V solid-solution, re-
spectively, during microhardness testing.

3.2. Microstructures of directionally solidified alloys
The typical microstructure of directionally solidified V –
V3Si eutectics is the so-called broken lamellar structure
(Fig. 3). For a wide range of solidification conditions
(growth rates from 20 to 100 mm/h at a fixed rotation rate
of 60 rpm), a well-aligned broken lamellar structure was
observed throughout the rods except for a narrow rim
(about 1-mm thick) near the outer surface of the rods where
the lamellae were misaligned. This surface effect is most
likely caused by quenching from the continuous argon flow
during directional solidification. Typical microstructures of
the transverse and longitudinal sections are shown in Figs.
3a and b, respectively. The reasons for the formation of a
broken rather than a regular lamellar microstructure are dis-
cussed in detail by Hunt and Hurle [17]. They argued that
broken lamellar or rod-like microstructures are formed in
eutectic alloys in which one phase grows in a faceted man-
ner and the other in a nonfaceted manner with the faceted
phase having the larger volume fraction. In contrast, if the
faceted phase has the smaller volume fraction, as in the
Al– Si eutectic [19], a complex irregular microstructure is
expected.
When a pure eutectic alloy is constrained to grow unidi-
rectionally, the lamellae in all the eutectic colonies grow in
an edgewise manner in a direction approximately parallel
to the heat flow direction, and the solid-liquid interface re-
mains planar throughout the solidification process. How-
ever, the rotation of the lamellae around the growth axis is
not restricted. Therefore, many colonies, in which the la-
mellar orientation changes from colony to colony, are
clearly distinguishable on the transverse sections. Since
the lamellae are nearly perpendicular to the transverse sec-
tion, the interphase spacings measured on the transverse
sections are fairly constant across the various colonies, but
sections parallel to the growth direction show different in-
terphase spacings in different colonies due to the rotation
of the lamellae about the growth direction relative to the
section. For this reason, all interphase spacings discussed
in this study were measured on transverse sections.

3.3. Effects of growth rate on microstructure
The eutectic interphase spacing, k, defined as the distance
on a transverse section from the center of the V solid-solu-
tion phase to the center of the adjacent V 3Si phase, was
measured over a range of growth rates, R, from 20 to
100 mm/h at a fixed rotation rate of 60 rpm. Figures 4a
and b show the broken lamellar structures obtained at
growth rates of R = 20 mm/h and 100 mm/h, respectively.
As can be seen in these figures, the lamellar spacing is lar-
ger at the slower growth rate and smaller at the faster
growth rate.
Eutectic growth depends on interplay between the dif-
fusion required for phase separation and the energy re-
quired for formation of interphase boundaries. The inter-
phase spacing is the result of a balance between two
competing tendencies, on the one hand to minimize the
interphase spacing in order to shorten the diffusion path
in the liquid near the interface, and on the other hand to
increase the spacing to minimize the interfacial area and
hence the total interfacial energy. Jackson and Hunt [15]
obtained the following relationship between growth rate

3.4. Crystallographic orientation relationships
The crystallographic orientation of the two phases of the
eutectic, relative to the growth direction and the interface
boundary between the phases, was determined by orienta-
tion imaging microscopy. Specimens directionally solidi-
fied at 40 mm/h and 60 rpm were examined (microstructure
shown in Fig. 3). Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)
patterns were recorded from individual lamellae in different
colonies in the sample and also from different positions
within the same colonies. Figure 7 shows the EBSD pat-
terns from individual V
3Si and V solid-solution lamellae.
The EBSD patterns were converted to {100} pole figures
to conveniently determine the orientation relationships be-
tween the two phases, and the following results were ob-
tained. The ð011Þ planes in V 3Si were found to be always
parallel to the ð112Þ planes in the V solid solution, with
these planes also parallel to the lamellar boundaries. In ad-
dition, the growth direction was found to be 100½/C138in V3Si
and ½111/C138in the V solid solution.
The growth directions observed in this study are the same
as those reported by Bewlay et al. [11], but the lamellar
boundaries are not. A possible reason for this difference is
that the microstructure in their study was more rod-like than
in this study. The microstructure and relative orientations of
the eutectic phases in this system may therefore depend on
the specific growth conditions.

3.6. Tensile properties of drop cast V – V 3Si eutectics
The strength of the arc-melted and drop cast V – V 3Si eu-
tectic [microstructure shown in Fig. 2b] was determined
by tensile testing in vacuum. Below 800 °C fracture oc-
curred in a brittle manner without prior yielding. Therefore
it was decided to measure tensile properties at tempera-
tures above 800 °C. The yield strength, ultimate tensile
strength (UTS), and ductility (represented by percent elon-
gation to fracture) as a function of temperature are plotted
in Fig. 10. As can be seen in this figure, the tensile
strength decreases continuously from 617 MPa to
147 MPa as temperature is increased from 800 to
1200 °C; meanwhile, the elongation to fracture increases
from ∼ 0 % to 203 %. Therefore, the ductile-to-brittle tran-
sition temperature for this material may be defined as ly-
ing somewhere between 800 and 900 °C.
The strength of our V – V3Si eutectic alloy may be com-
pared with that of a V solid-solution alloy (V – 4Cr – 4Ti)
[1] that is a leading candidate for fusion applications. Fig-
ure 11 shows the yield strengths of these alloys as a func-
tion of temperature. It is clear that the V – V 3Si eutectic
composite is significantly stronger than conventional V sol-
id-solution alloys. However, a disadvantage is its brittleness
below 800 °C. Additional work is needed to improve the
ductility and fracture toughness at temperatures below
800 °C. One possibility is to investigate off-eutectic alloys
in which the ductile V solid solution phase has a much high-
er volume fraction than the brittle V3Si phase.
Fracture surfaces after tensile testing were examined by
scanning electron microscopy. Figures 12a, b and c show
the fracture surfaces of V – V3Si eutectic alloys after tensile
testing at room temperature, 800 °C and 1000 °C, respec-
tively. The V solid-solution phase is ductile over the entire
temperature range and exhibits significant necking. In con-
trast, the fracture surface of the V 3Si phase, is flat and
smooth at test temperatures below 800 °C, suggesting that
this phase is very brittle at these temperatures (Fig. 12a
and b). At temperatures higher than 800 °C, however, the
V3Si phase is quite rough and shows considerable evidence
of plastic deformation (Fig. 12c).
4. Conclusions
Well-aligned broken lamellar microstructures of V – V 3Si
eutectic alloys (12.6 at% Si) can be produced by directional
solidification in an optical floating zone furnace. The mi-
crostructure consists of a V solid-solution as the continuous
matrix and the intermetallic V 3Si as the discontinuous
phase.
By controlling the growth rate, the interphase spacing
can be varied from ∼ 1–4 lm. The relationship between
the interphase spacing ( k) and growth rate ( R) is in agree-
ment with the Jackson-Hunt theory ( k / R/C01=2). However,
it is not possible to decrease the interphase spacing without
limit by simply increasing the growth rate. At very fast
growth rates, the lamellar structure breaks down to form
cellular structures. The growth direction of V
3Si was found

The modulus and nanoindentation hardness of V 3Si are
214 and 13.8 GPa, respectively, and those of the V solid-so-
lution 165 and 3.4 GPa, respectively. The Vicker’s hardness
of directionally solidified V – V3Si eutectic alloys is inde-
pendent of interphase spacing in this investigation. The val-
ue of the composite hardness predicted by a simple rule of
mixtures (HV 570), agrees well with the experimentally
measured hardness of the composite (HV 552 /C620).
The high-temperature strength was obtained by tensile
testing at elevated temperatures. Preliminary results show
that the ductile to brittle transition temperature is between
800 and 900 °C. The tensile strength decreases continu-
ously from 617 MPa to 147 MPa as temperature is in-
creased from 800 to 1200 °C; meanwhile the elongation to
fracture increases from ~ 0 % to 203 %. The V – V3Si eutec-
tic composites are significantly stronger, but also more brit-
tle, than V solid-solution alloys that are being considered
for fusion applications.
