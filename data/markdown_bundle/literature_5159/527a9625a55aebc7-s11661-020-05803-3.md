METALLURGICAL AND MATERIALS TRANSACTIONS 50TH ANNIVERSARY COLLECTION
New Opportunities in Refractory Alloys
N.R. PHILIPS, M. CARL, and N.J. CUNNINGHAM
For decades, the promise of refractory alloys (robust structural materials for use above 1500 K
(1200 /C176 C)) has been hampered by concurrent requirements for fabricability via ingot metallurgy
and oxidation resistance. These constraints have thus far proven impossible to meet in a single
material. The work herein demonstrates that the advent of economical powder feedstocks and
consolidation methods, such as additive manufacturing (AM), eﬀectively removes the
constraints preventing widespread utilization of high-strength refractory alloys into aerospace,
defense, and space access applications. High-purity, spherical refractory-alloy powders ( e.g. the
Nb-10Hf-1Ti alloy ATI C103 /C228 ) were made via both atomization and plasma spheroidization
and consolidated using a variety of methods. Consolidated material shows comparable and
sometimes superior high temperature performance to equivalent wrought material. The
availability of cost-eﬀective powder synthesis techniques enables the elimination of critical
barriers in fabrication: namely the requirement for hot and cold working. The availability of
AM and hot isostatic pressing expands the design space for high-strength refractory alloys and
makes the adoption of new paradigms in alloy design, such as refractory complex concentrated
alloys, plausible. Going forward, the most diﬃcult challenge, oxidation, can be tackled with the
new approaches that are now available.

I. INTRODUCTION
THE golden age of refractory alloy development
began in the 1950s, driven by the increasing demands of
turbine engines. Early in this period the foundational
refractory alloys ( e.g. Moly TZM) and key techniques
for production ( e.g. vacuum arc melting) were devel-
oped. The discovery of pyrochlore deposits in Canada
and Brazil allowed the relatively lightweight niobium to
transition from a rare metal to an engineering material
and complete the structural refractory alloy family: Nb,
Ta, Mo, W. The timing, concurrent with the ‘‘space
race,’’ allowed substantial resources to be directed to the
development of high-strength alloys for high-tempera-
ture service, primarily in connection with the orbital and
reentry applications that were the major development
drivers for refractory metals.
Since the beginning of spaceﬂight it has been clear
that very high power levels can only practically be
achieved with nuclear power; thus, the peculiar need for
very high temperature materials has been, in large part,
driven by the space-based nuclear reactor programs. [1]
The other major demand signal for refractories has
historically been linked to the aerothermal heating
associated with high velocity ﬂight and re-entry of
advanced vehicles. Though the missile and rocket
propulsion for defense and space access have been the
mainstay of refractory alloy application in the aerospace
world, the limited size of the market and the lack of
success in developing a suﬃciently oxidation-resistant
replacement for Ni superalloys ( e.g. Nb-aluminides and
silicides[2]) has resulted in the major refractory develop-
ment eﬀorts being conﬁned to the space-based nuclear
and hypersonics programs.
In early 1973, a series of decisions—selection of
radioisotope thermoelectric generators for space-based
power, cancelation of NERVA (Nuclear Engine for
Rocket Vehicle Application) and the dissolution of the
Space Nuclear Propulsion Oﬃce [3]—eliminated space-
based nuclear propulsion as a key driver for refractory
alloy development. At the same time, the other critical
demand, the space shuttle program, down-selected to
a ceramic tile system and ended the development of
coated refractory alloy thermal protection systems.
These decisions eﬀectively brought research and devel-
opment of refractory alloys in the US to an abrupt
halt.[4]
Other than continued work in Russia, [5] there was
little interest in implementation of refractories until the
1980s when the SP-100 and NASP programs initiated.

SP-100 quickly selected Nb-1Zr (and a variant,
PWC-11) for its structural components before it was
shut down in 1994 [6] and the NASP was focused on
lightweight carbon-carbon, titanium aluminide, and
titanium metal matrix composites but was canceled in
1995.[7] The intensity of development in refractories has
never reached the levels of the early 70s and little
progress has been made since then in ﬁelding commer-
cially mature systems. The dominant refractory alloys
for high temperature service today—C103, Ta-10W,
TZM, Th/La/Ce oxide dispersion strengthened W/Mo,
W-Re alloys, and K-doped W/Mo—all were developed
prior to the abrupt cessation of research in 1973. [8–10]
Since the termination of NASP and SP-100, the
materials development work in refractory alloys for
ﬂight has taken place largely outside of the public
domain and progress has been limited to platinum
group coatings for propulsion structures. For 60 years
there has been signiﬁcant promise in refractories but a
failure to achieve cost-eﬀective manufacturing of an
alloy with suﬃcient strength and oxidation
resistance.[2,11–18]
With that history in mind, we must ask: where do we
ﬁnd opportunity after decades of failure? To answer that
question we review the historical constraints that limited
the viable refractory alloy solution space. Above
/C25900 K ‘‘molybdenum oxidizes rapidly at elevated
temperatures to the point of complete disintegra-
tion,’’[19] which has limited Mo to industrial applications
where an inert or reducing atmosphere can be ensured
and its tendency for embrittlement is acceptable. Tung-
sten suﬀers from similar limitations and is precluded for
most aerospace applications by density; tantalum is
suﬃciently similar to niobium in achievable properties
that, when corrected for density, it oﬀers little advantage
for many applications and is signiﬁcantly more expen-
sive. As has been long recognized, the inherent attrac-
tion of density (8.58 g/cm 3)[20] and melting point
(2745 K) [21] makes niobium the refractory element of
choice for structural applications at very high
temperature.
The primary limitation for niobium alloys has always
been their fabricability. After the intense demand in the
50s and 60s the market for refractory alloys contracted,
eliminating all the medium- and high-strength alloys and
leaving only a single low strength alloy, C103,* as the
dominant refractory alloy in the marketplace, due to its
excellent fabricability. The importance of fabricability,
i.e. the diﬃculty to convert an ingot to a sheet or other
semi-ﬁnished mill product, was recognized by Jahnke [22]
in a striking ﬁgure from 1963 (updated by Frank [18] in
1968 and again herein) where it was used as an analog
for cost (Figure 1). This ﬁgure reveals the initial rapid
progress in improving oxidation resistance, strength,
and fabricability though independently. The illustrated
upper bounds denote the relative improvement in
performance (normalized against 1955) for the best
available alloy for that metric. Improvements in fabri-
cability for the best available alloy did not necessarily
translate to the highest strength alloy. Viewed through
this lens it becomes clear that the cost of higher strength
alloys, due to their processing requirements, outweighed
the improved properties and only the most easily
manufactured materials were used.
While the higher strength systems proved very diﬃcult
to manufacture, the alloys with improved oxidation
resistance were nearly impossible to make. Niobium’s
inherently poor oxidation resistance requires signiﬁcant
alloy content and it is, unfortunately, incompatible with
the traditional elements for protective stable oxide ﬁlms
(Al, Cr) due to the high service temperatures. Although
alloys with improved oxidation resistance were devel-
oped (such as the Nb-Ti-Al family [2]), most could not be
fabricated, and they suﬀered from signiﬁcantly reduced
high temperature strength through melting point depres-
sion. This fundamental barrier was recognized as early
as 1968:

Several researchers proposed rapid solidiﬁcation or
powder metallurgy (P/M) as a route to improved
oxidation resistance by avoiding the highly brittle
as-cast microstructures that have proven diﬃcult to
fabricate.[12,23] In fact, powder metallurgy methods (and
related concepts such as functional grading) were seen as
promising even in the early days of refractory alloy
development.[18] Especially promising was atomization
and subsequent hot isostatic pressing (HIP) to form near
net shape parts with properties equaling that of the
wrought material. [24,25] Historically, powder metallurgy
has been stymied by the lack of a cost-eﬀective method
for production of a high quality powder.
Though less problematic than the pursuit of oxidation
resistance, high strength at the temperatures typically
demanded of refractories, 1500 K to 1700 K (1200 /C176 Ct o
1400 /C176 C), has proved incompatible with a commercially
viable hot working fabrication route. The practical
limitations imposed by heat transfer and oxidation limit
hot working to temperatures at or below the target
service temperatures. This leads to a fundamental
paradox requiring low strength and extensive ductility
for fabrication, while maintaining very high strength for
service at the same temperature.
History has consistently shown that increasing high
temperature strength, unsurprisingly, reduces room
temperature ductility and, due to thermal softening,
develops exceedingly high strength at low temperatures.
Again, engineering for fabrication is diametrically
opposed to the performance target and brings minimal
value to the application. This reality leads to the
acceptance that typical metallic ductility at room tem-
perature (elongations of 10 pct or even 20 pct) is not
achievable and thus the recent pursuit of silicide
intermetallics.[26,27] One potential way around this
problem (other than PM methods) is precipitation
strengthening that would allow age hardening to convert
a soft formable alloy to a high-strength material.
Unfortunately, due to the high temperatures and the
reactive nature of most refractories, there is limited
opportunity for stable particles and the available solutes
(e.g. C, N, O) tend to reduce room temperature ductility.
Although there are a few viable candidates [28,29] that
would potentially allow for a heat-treatable alloy that
would be more amenable to deformation processing,
solution temperatures required to achieve stable disper-
sions at 1500 K (1200 /C176 C) in suﬃcient volume fraction
requires temperatures that are diﬃcult to realize in
practice. In Molybdenum, TZM successfully achieves
excellent strength via powder metallurgy and dispersion
strengthening but there are few other refractory exam-
ples. As a consequence, in most cases the major strength
contribution comes from solid-solution hardening,
which has limited eﬀectiveness at very high tempera-
tures. It is for these reasons that conventional ingot
metallurgy has had limited success.
To summarize, for the last 60 years, industry and
academia have unsuccessfully pursued a high-strength,
lightweight and oxidation-resistant refractory alloy that
is suﬃciently aﬀordable to enable use in all but the most
limited applications. Success has been thwarted at every
turn by the lack of a viable fabrication path for the
alloys with the desirable properties. Further progress is
primarily dependent on a cost eﬀective process for
manufacture of a ﬁnished article.
II. CURRENT LANDSCAPE
The development of new powder production tech-
niques[30–32] and additive manufacturing (AM) [33,34]
oﬀers a possibility that the intractable fabrication issues
related to ingot metallurgy ( e.g. melting, primary
breakdown) may be circumvented by direct consolida-
tion of powder via AM or P/M. Thus, some of the
promising historical alloys too costly or too diﬃcult to
produce in useable form may now be practical in the
context of these new techniques.
Historically, the most cost-eﬀective method to pro-
duce reactive metal powders such as Ti, Zr, Hf, Nb, and
Ta is by the hydride-dehydride (HDH) process [35];
however, this results in angular powders that are
diﬃcult to implement in AM methods. Plasma
spheroidization can overcome this challenge by passing
the angular powders through an inductively coupled
thermal plasma, thus melting the angular particles into
spherical particles. [36] While there are some limitations
(W and Mo cannot be hydrided) and disadvantages ( e.g.
interstitial pickup during the HDH conversion) to this
method, it can produce pre-alloyed spherical powder at
much lower cost than the historical rotating electrode
methods.[25,37] Additionally, for alloys that deoxidize at
high temperatures, reduction of interstitial contamina-
tion is possible. [38,39] Further, it is now possible to
manufacture high-purity, pre-alloyed spherical powders
directly from ingot via electrode induction gas atomiza-
tion (EIGA). [40,41] During the EIGA process, the melt
stock (typically 25 to 50 mm diameter bar) is inductively
melted to produce a nominally continuous molten
stream that falls through a gas ring, resulting in minimal
impurity pick-up without contact with the melting
apparatus. EIGA powder yields in the two common
appropriate size distributions for AM ( i.e. for laser and
electron beam methods) are both /C2520 to 40 pct. [30,32]
Despite this relatively low yield, EIGA is ideal for
refractory and reactive metals as it avoids reactions with
ceramic crucibles, tundishes, and nozzles [42] as well as
cost associated with fabrication to wire necessary for
other atomization techniques. [43] In addition, the system
cleanliness enables ﬁnes and oversize particles to be
recycled or reprocessed, thereby lowering the overall
cost to produce powder.
There is limited published work on beam-based AM
technologies of refractory metals and alloys, [34,44] but
much of that work is promising. Both electron and laser
methods have been used to produce W, [45–47] Ta,[48,49]
Mo,[50,51] and Nb[52] parts; however, the vast majority of
the public literature is associated with printing W. Early
work by Ebert et al .[53] investigated the feasibility of
micro-sintering structures of pure W using a femtosec-
ond pulse laser and showed some potential advantages
to create micro/nano-structured devices in thin layers.

Later, Deprez et al .[54] demonstrated the ability to
achieve reasonably high densities (89.9 pct) using selec-
tive laser melting by printing a complex collimator with
oblique pinholes from pure W. Though feasibility had
been shown early, numerous defects, [55] non-ideal
microstructures and microcracking [56–58] were observed.
Various methods reduce microcracking such as pre-
heated substrates,[46,47,59] scan strategies,[60] and alloying
additions.[61,62] However, microcracks are still observed
due to the high DBTT, [63] large thermal gradients, [60]
and interstitial contamination. [46] Though less work has
been published, printing of Mo appears to suﬀer many
of the same problems and suggested solutions. [50,51,59,64]
Nb and Ta do not appear to have the same issues: fully
dense parts without microcracking have been produced
from the pure metals by process optimization, [49,65–68]
though it is sometimes desirable to produce porous
parts.[69,70] Likely this is due to the much lower DBTT
and higher oxygen tolerance associated with Ta and Nb.
Despite this progress, texture control and resulting
anisotropy remains an issue with Nb [52] and Ta [49,67,71]
and must be understood for future applications. To the
authors’ knowledge, there has been no work publicly
published on the additive manufacturing of current Nb
or Ta refractory alloys, though there is some potentially
applicable work in the refractory complex concentrated
alloy (RCCA) community [72–76] further demonstrating
feasibility of AM methods for refractory alloys. For
other alloy systems AM has demonstrated substantial
cost savings over conventional processes, a trend which
promises to have an outsize impact on high-value
refractory alloys. [77]
Recently, there has been an explosion of research
interest in refractory high-entropy (RHEA), multi-prin-
cipal element (RMPE), and complex concentrated
(RCCA) alloys. [78,79] This resurgence in refractory met-
allurgy is aided by new tools such as phase diagram
simulations,[80–83] computational methods, [84–90] analyt-
ical models, [91–94] and selection rules [95–97] that were not
available during initial refractory development pro-
grams in the 1950 to 1970s. All of this, along with
high-throughput experiments, [98–103] has led to a collec-
tion of many previously unidentiﬁed potential high-tem-
perature, high-strength alloys and hints of exciting new
phenomena.[104]
Taken together, the emerging technologies in powder
synthesis and additive manufacturing, along with the
deeper understanding of fundamental behavior through
study of RCCAs, provide a roadmap to avoid the
historical roadblocks to fabricability and realization of
high-strength refractory alloy components. This work
will show that these new techniques do in fact provide
the long sought capability to realize the promise of
refractory alloys.
III. MATERIALS AND METHODS
Two diﬀerent alloys were produced by ATI using two
diﬀerent methods to evaluate the viability of various
powder manufacturing techniques. ATI C103 /C228 alloy
powder, made via the EIGA process, is the primary
focus of this study due to its broad application base. A
model tantalum-tungsten alloy was made via plasma
spheroidization of HDH powder to demonstrate a
complementary pathway and the viability of making
very high melting point alloys. Several consolidation
techniques were then employed with the C103 powder to
demonstrate the properties of powder-based
manufacturing.
Wrought C103 bars were converted to powder
through the EIGA process yielding a full range of
particle sizes /C255 to 300 lm in diameter. The EIGA
powder was subsequently mechanically classiﬁed
through screens to produce the desired particle size
distribution.
A tantalum-tungsten alloy HDH powder was pro-
duced by typical industrial practice for comparison to
the atomized powders and as feedstock for spheroidiza-
tion. The alloy serves as a model alloy for refractory
systems and comprises substantial tungsten content ( /C257
wt pct) as well as typical impurities (Fe, O) that would
be of interest in a production setting. Angular Ta alloy
feedstock with 37 to 53 lm particle size distribution
(PSD) was fed through a He-Ar plasma to produce a
spheroidal powder.
The chosen consolidation technology and desired
component geometric ﬁdelity dictates the required
powder particle size. Numerous AM technologies are
available and have been explored for processing C103
including wire electron beam additive manufacturing
(EBAM), powder bed electron beam melting (EBM),
direct energy deposition (DED), laser powder bed fusion
(LPBF), and cold spray. Typically, AM technologies use
either a mid-fraction PSD, nominally 50 to 150 lm, or a
ﬁne PSD, nominally 15 to 50 lm. This work focuses on
the EBM and LPBF technologies. EBM work was
performed on an Arcam A2 machine at the Center for
Additive Manufacturing and Logistics (CAMAL) at
North Carolina State University (NCSU). For EBM the
50 to 150 lm mid-fraction PSD is preferred to reduce
particle motion due to electric charge accumulation on
the powder bed. The larger particle size results in a
rougher surface ﬁnish and larger minimum feature size.
LPBF typically uses the ﬁne particle size distribution for
improved surface ﬁnish and resolution to print the ﬁnest
possible features. LPBF was performed by ATI using a
SLM 125HL and by Castheon** using a Concept Laser
Cusing M2. Castheon prints used a < 50 micron PSD
while ATI prints used a nominal 15-50 micron PSD.
Both systems are equipped with a 400 W laser.
For HIP consolidation, powder was compacted under
vibration inside a cylindrical ‘‘can,’’ evacuated of air,
sealed, and then consolidated using high temperature
and pressure. The resulting microstructure is typically
isotropic and can achieve full density (elimination of all
porosity). It is possible to HIP a wide range of particle
sizes, and in this work HIP trials in a pure Nb can were
made for each of two PSD’s: below 45 lm, and above

C. Electron Beam Melting
The grain structure of the EBM C103 builds is again
elongated in the z direction and equiaxed in the x-y
direction (Figures 7(a) and (b)) but there is no large
scale porosity evident with the components near 100 pct
density.
Due to the larger powder particle size (relative to the
LPBF feedstock) the oxygen content of the EBM feed-
stock is lower and consistent with a typical wrought
material.[105] In addition, since EBM occurs under vac-
uum, oxygen pickup can be minimized during the build
but will be dependent on the run conditions and quality of
the vacuum. The larger particle size results in a rougher
surface ﬁnish compared to LPBF but still attains excellent
part ﬁdelity (Figure 8). Table VI shows the chemistry of
the initial feedstock powder along with the resulting build

This evaporative loss can be signiﬁcant if the build
temperature is not rigorously controlled.

V. DISCUSSION
The refractory powders formed via EIGA and plasma
spheroidization demonstrate excellent shape and are
available in a wide range of particle sizes (unlike HDH
and P/REP powders). More importantly, the synthesis
processes are scalable and eﬃcient such that production
of near net shape components at lower cost than
wrought is feasible. The EIGA process in particular
has been shown to produce powder with minimal
chemical contamination and composition that mirrors
the feedstock and is suitable for a variety of P/M and
AM processes. As with past work, HIP of C103
produces dense parts without inclusions and mechanical
properties consistent with wrought. Due to the poly-
crystalline nature of the atomized powder (Figure 2
inset), the process is capable of using a wide range of
particle sizes while still achieving typical wrought
speciﬁcations for grain size [108] even without subsequent
conversion.
The availability of high quality powder opens up the
long sought-after possibility of developing refractory
alloys via powder metallurgy routes to allow higher
alloy content and the incorporation of segregation
prone solutes. Nickel alloys have beneﬁted tremen-
dously from the rapid solidiﬁcation of powder to solve
problems of ingot cracking, severe segregation, and poor
hot workability. [109] The rapid cooling during powder
synthesis followed by HIP consolidation creates a
variety of opportunities for precipitation strengthening
by supersaturation of nitrogen or carbon, or incorpo-
ration of dispersoids, [28,29,110] as well as potential for
oxidation resistance by incorporating alumina
formers.[111,112]

The AM consolidation experiments indicate that

anisotropic reduction of hot ductility in the LPBF
material was recovered after annealing and still exceeds
typical wrought material. Oxygen pickup during the
various AM processes is typically limited to about
100 ppm, but controlling oxygen in the printed parts
remains a challenge. Further process developments are
possible to reduce the oxygen in the printed parts:
eliminate powder exposure to atmosphere during han-
dling and screening, remove the ﬁnest powder particles
below 20 microns, limit oxygen levels in the printer
during the build, and reduce oxygen content in the
EIGA bar feedstock. The electron beam method suﬀers
from evaporative loss of relatively high vapor pressure
alloying elements, an issue we observed with Ti loss
during EBM of C103. The loss of Ti, a low-temperature
solid solution strengthener, is consistent with some of
the property observations, which will require additional
process development to mitigate. In all cases, however,
defect-free material is readily produced at the proof of
concept scale. Due to the limited understanding of
thermal boundary conditions during the AM process,
management of gradients and solidiﬁcation rates is not
yet suﬃcient to control the columnar to equiaxed
transition.[114] As a result, microstructure is signiﬁcantly
elongated in the build direction leading to anisotropic
tensile properties /C160though signiﬁcant progress has been
made in other systems. Despite limited process maturity,
especially compared to alloys like Ti-6-4 and Ni718, the
geometric and microstructural ﬁdelity are suﬃcient to
drive active pursuit of AM for refractories in commer-
cialization eﬀorts.
There has been an upswing of interest in refractory
alloy development associated with the suggestion of new
strengthening behavior in RCCAs. [104] The implication
of novel dislocation mechanisms opens the door for the
elusive ductile and high-strength refractory alloy. Yet,
surprisingly, there has been limited application of the
astonishing body of work in the prior literature to the
development of RCCAs. Recently, Senkov et al . have
suggested that in order to achieve desirable strength
above 1500 K (1200 /C176 C), the melting temperature must
be suﬃciently high that an alloy can comprise only the
most refractory elements (with the consequence that
densities would likely have to exceed 10 g/cm 3).[81] This
constraint was recognized early and alloys of this type
were found to be largely impractical due to their lack of
fabricability. Whether the current fundamental studies
of RCCAs to unlock new regimes of ductility and
strength will yield a path to alloys capable of service at
1500 K to 1700 K (1200 /C176 C to 1400 /C176 C) remains to be
seen, but it is ﬁnally possible with powder routes that
these compositions can be practically explored and
implemented.

VI. CONCLUSIONS
The historical landscape of refractory alloys has been
dominated by fabrication challenges which are ulti-
mately manifest as material cost. Due to the apparently
fundamental incompatibility between fabricability and
both very high temperature strength and oxidation
resistance, powder methods have long been sought as a
solution to allow further advancement.
Herein two key technological advances have been
demonstrated:
– The production via industrial scale processes of
high-quality refractory alloy powders suitable for
AM and P/M with excellent sphericity and
polycrystallinity.
– Powder bed additive manufacturing as a feasible
pathway for simpler and faster fabrication of
refractory structures that can achieve similar prop-
erties to wrought material.
These powders and the complementary additive and
powder metallurgical consolidation processes promise to
be revolutionary, especially in the context of RCCAs, in
that they enable the development of a new generation of
refractory alloys.
