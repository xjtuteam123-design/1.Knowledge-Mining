Alloy Composition Screening for Ni-Base Turbine
Disc Superalloys Using the Creep Property of Single
Crystal
YUHI MORI, KYOKO KAWAGISHI, TOSHIO OSADA, HIROSHI HARADA,
MICHINARI YUYAMA, YUJI TAKATA, MAKOTO OSAWA, and AYAKO IKEDA
This paper proposes a method for alloy composition screening using the creep property of a
single crystal (SC) for designing new Ni-base powder metallurgy (P/M) turbine disc superalloys.
A commercial Ni-base disc alloy, U720Li /C210 , a Ni-Co-base disc alloy, TMW-4M3, and a Ni-base
conventionally cast alloy, TM-47, were selected for investigation. Their SC and P/M samples
were prepared such that their c/c¢ structures were similar. Moreover, tensile creep tests were
performed on SC samples, and compressive creep tests were performed on SC and P/M samples.
The tensile creep test results of SC samples at 1073 K (800 /C176 C) and 735 MPa indicate that 0.2
and 1 pct creep times, as well as rupture life, are long in the order of TM-47 SC, TMW-4M3 SC,
and U720Li SC. These results simulate the intragranular creep resistances of the corresponding
P/M alloys. Furthermore, there is no signiﬁcant diﬀerence in 0.2 and 1 pct compressive creep
times between the SC and P/M samples of each alloy. Additionally, the 0.2 and 1 pct creep times
of tensile and compressive creep tests of every alloy had an identical order. Therefore, alloy
composition screening using SC creep property enables the design of new disc alloys with
excellent creep resistance.

I. INTRODUCTION
NI-BASE superalloys are widely used for the
high-temperature components of jet engines such as
turbine blades, vanes, and discs, due to their excellent
heat resistance. The thermal eﬃciency of jet engines has
been improved by increasing the turbine inlet temper-
ature. As the turbine inlet temperature increases, the
creep temperature capability of superalloys for
high-pressure turbine discs needs to be improved. A
Ni-base turbine disc superalloy, ME501, [1] has the
highest creep temperature capability of 1013 K
(740 /C176 C) for a creep rupture life of 1000 hours at
650 MPa in reported powder metallurgy (P/M) alloys.
A Ni-Co-base turbine disc alloy, TMW-4M3, [2,3] devel-
oped by the National Institute for Materials Science
(NIMS), Japan, has the highest creep temperature
capability of 983 K (710 /C176 C) among the reported cast
and wrought alloys.
In order to improve the creep temperature capability
above those of the reported alloys described above, we
will assume that Ni-base conventionally cast (CC)
superalloys for turbine blades were made into P/M
superalloys for high-pressure turbine discs because CC
alloys have similar polycrystalline structure as turbine
disc alloys and are expected to have higher creep
deformation resistance than the existing turbine disc
alloys. However, it is diﬃcult to accurately compare the
creep deformation resistances of CC and the turbine disc
alloys due to signiﬁcant diﬀerences between CC alloys
and turbine disc alloys. The main diﬀerence is in the
grain size. The grain size of CC alloys is much larger
than that of the turbine disc alloys. For example, a CC
turbine blade has an average grain size of 1.5 mm [4];
however, turbine discs have grain sizes of 3 to
130 lm.[5–7] Meanwhile, grain sizes usually inﬂuence
the creep property of polycrystalline alloys. Moreover,
disc alloys have diﬀerent grain boundary strengths from
CC alloys because the compositions and volumes of
grain-boundary-strengthening precipitates such as car-
bides and borides vary with the alloy compositions.
Thus, we will consider an original method of alloy
composition screening using the creep property of a
single crystal (SC), as it is not inﬂuenced by grain size
and grain boundary strength. Furthermore, the alloy
design program[8] developed by NIMS can be used in the

II. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE
A. Alloy Design
A c/c¢ Ni-base CC superalloy, TM-47,[9,10] was selected
as the investigated alloy because it satisﬁes the following
three requirements for high-pressure turbine discs: (i)
both creep strength and high-temperature corrosion
resistance are excellent, [9,10] (ii) Cr content is more than
10 wt. pct to ensure that the carbide precipitates are
thermodynamically stable, (iii) density, calculated using
the alloy design program, [8] is 8.5 g/cm 3 or less to reduce
weight and centrifugal force during operation. A com-
mercial Ni-base superalloy, U720Li /C210 ,[5–7,11] and the
Ni-Co-base cast and wrought superalloy developed by
NIMS, TMW-4M3, were also selected as the investigated
alloys.
Three SC alloys were designed by removing
grain-boundary-strengthening elements, C, B, and Zr,
from TM-47, U720Li, and TMW-4M3. Moreover, three
P/M alloys were designed by adding C, B, and Zr to
each SC alloy again. The nominal compositions of the
investigated Ni-base superalloys are shown in Table I.
B. Preparation of Single Crystal Samples
Each SC alloy was directionally solidiﬁed into SC
round bar samples (diameter 10 mm, length 130 mm) in
a vacuum by withdrawing method in a directional
solidiﬁcation furnace. Each SC sample was then sub-
jected to heat treatments in a vacuum based on the
conditions shown in Table II.
The misorientations of each SC round bar were
analyzed using back reﬂection Laue method. The round
bars, whose angle between the longitudinal and h001i
directions were 6 deg or less, were selected to avoid
crystallographic orientation dependence on creep
resistance.[12,13]
C. Preparation of Powder Metallurgy Samples
Alloy powders, prepared by gas atomization method
(Sanyo Special Steel Co., Japan), were used in this
study. The atomized powders were classiﬁed using a
sieve with 53- lm mesh. From the powders passed
through the sieve, cylindrical P/M samples (diameter
54 mm, height 100 mm) were manufactured by hot
isostatic pressing (HIP) under a condition of 1393 K
(1120 /C176 C), 4 hours, and 98 MPa (Metal Technology
Co., Japan). The same heat treatments for each SC
sample were applied to each corresponding P/M sample
as shown in Table II.
D. Density Measurement
The densities of the as-HIP samples were measured
using the following procedure. Some pieces were cut out
from each P/M sample. The volumes of the alloy pieces
were then measured using a dry density meter (Mi-
cromeritics Instruments Co., AccuPyc II 1340 Gas
Pycnometer). Finally, the weight of each piece was
divided by each volume and the densities of the P/M
samples were obtained.
E. Tensile and Compressive Creep Tests
The specimens for tensile creep tests were machined
only from the aged SC round bar samples. Figure 1
shows a dimension of the tensile creep test specimens
(Japanese industrial standard, JIS Z 2271). Tensile creep
rupture tests were performed at 1073 K (800 /C176 C) and
735 MPa in air.

F. Analysis and Observation
The size distributions of the alloy powders that passed
through the sieve using 53- lm mesh were measured
using a laser diﬀraction particle size analyzer (Shi-
madzu, SALD-2300, ISO 13320), assuming that the
imaginary refractive index of each alloy is the same as
that of pure Ni, 2.11 + 3.96 i (i: imaginary unit).
Moreover, the appearances of the powders were
observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM,
JEOL, JSM-6060).
The initial microstructures of the SC and P/M
samples before and after the heat treatments were
observed using a SEM (Carl Zeiss, Auriga Laser). The
grain size distributions of each heat-treated P/M sample
were measured by electron back-scattered diﬀraction
(EBSD) analysis. However, the edge grains of the EBSD
maps were excluded from the grain size distribution
analysis. The average grain diameters were then calcu-
lated from the measured grain size distributions.
After the compressive creep tests, the cross-sections of
the creep specimens on TM-47 SC and P/M were
observed using the SEM (Carl Zeiss, Auriga Laser).

C. Initial Microstructures
Figure 4 shows the back-scattered electron images of
the P/M samples before and after the heat treatment.
Figure 4 indicates that the as-HIP samples were
completely sintered. However, they had some prior
particle boundaries which inherit the spherical shape of
the alloy powders. The heat-treated samples have larger
grains than do the as-HIP samples. Figure 5 shows the
analyzed grain size distributions of each heat-treated P/
M sample.
The average grain diameters of U720Li P/M,
TMW-4M3 P/M, and TM-47 P/M were 31, 45, and
49 lm, respectively. Figure 5 shows that U720Li P/M
and TMW-4M3 P/M had ﬁner grains than TM-47 P/M
because the solution temperature of U720Li P/M and
TMW-4M3 P/M were lower than that of TM-47 P/M by
60 K.
Figure 6 shows the secondary electron images of c/c¢
structures in the heat-treated SC and P/M samples. The
samples were completely solutionized and eutectic c/c¢
was not observed. Moreover, tertiary c¢ was not
observed due to the high cooling rate of the heat
treatment. The c/c¢ structures of all alloys were thermo-
dynamically stable and topologically close-packed
phases were not observed.
D. Tensile and Compressive Creep Properties
The analyzed misorientation angle of U720Li SC,
TMW-4M3 SC, and TM-47 SC samples were 3 deg,
6 deg, and 4 deg from the h001i direction, respectively.
The misorientation angles were suﬃciently small for the
orientation dependence of creep resistance to be ignored
at 1073 K (800 /C176 C) and 735 MPa. [12,13]
Figure 7 shows the results of the tensile creep rupture
tests at 1073 K (800 /C176 C) and 735 MPa on the SC
samples. The creep rupture life, as well as 0.2 and 1 pct
creep times were long in the order of TM-47 SC,
TMW-4M3 SC, and U720Li SC, as shown in
Figures 7(a) and (b).

The 0.2 and 1 pct creep times of the SC and P/M
samples are compared in Figures 9(a) and (b), respec-

creep times between the SC and P/M samples at 1073 K
(800 /C176 C) and 735 MPa. Moreover, the orders of the 0.2
and 1 pct creep times of both SC and P/M samples were
similar. Therefore, the validity of screening the super-
alloy composition using the creep property of SC has
been conﬁrmed. Using this screening method, promising
alloy compositions can be explored for the design of new
Ni-base P/M turbine disc superalloys having excellent
creep resistance excluding the inﬂuences of grain size
and grain boundary precipitates.
E. Microstructures of Compressive-Creep-Deformed
Specimens
Figure 10 shows the secondary electron images on the
center area of the 5 pct-compressive-creep-deformed
specimens of TM-47 SC and P/M.
Slip bands cutting c¢ precipitates were observed in SC
and P/M specimens after the compressive creep tests, as
shown in Figure 10. These results indicate that disloca-
tion slip motion occurred in both specimens. In partic-
ular, the slip band did not pass through the grain
boundary and was not observed in the next crystal grain
in Figure 10(b). This indicates that the dislocation slip
motion was blocked by the grain boundary.

The slip band in the SC specimen, shown in

direction by 55 deg. The inclination was similar to an
angle (54.7 deg) formed by h001i orientations and {111}
slip planes. In the creep test of TM-47 SC, the

According to other transmission electron microscopy
studies,[12,14–17] the deformation mechanism of the initial
creep part is the slip motion on the {111} planes with
cutting c¢ particles not only in the TM-47 P/M in this
study but also in other Ni-base superalloys, U720Li, [14]
Mar-M200,[15] CMSX-4,[12,16] and ME501 [17] in the low
and intermediate temperature, 998 K to 1123 K (725 /C176 C
to 850 /C176 C), and high stress, 552 to 800 MPa.

and the corresponding P/M alloy are in identical order.
However, if grain boundary sliding is a rate-limiting
factor of creep deformation, the creep time becomes
longer with the larger grain size because coarse grains
suppress grain boundary sliding. Therefore, grain
boundary sliding is not a rate-limiting factor of creep
deformation based on the conditions of this study.
The´baud et al.[18–20] reported that the creep resistance
of the single crystalline Ni-base superalloy, AD730 /C210 ,i s
not better than that of the polycrystalline alloy under all
temperature and stress conditions. The ﬁner polycrys-
talline sample has better creep resistance than coarser
polycrystalline and SC samples at 973 K (700 /C176 C) and
800 MPa. [20] This result is consistent with the results in
this study shown in Figures 8 and 9.
The results of this paper and other studies [12,14–20]
suggest three possible necessary conditions for SC and
P/M samples to have the similar creep times: (1) both
samples have similar c/c¢ structures without the other
phases such as g, (2) the grain boundary sliding is not a
rate-limiting factor of creep deformation, and (3) the
applied stress of creep test is suﬃciently high for
dislocations to move on the {111} planes and cut
through the c¢ precipitates.
IV. CONCLUSIONS
We have conﬁrmed the validity of alloy composition
screening using the creep property of SC to facilitate the
design of new Ni-base P/M turbine disc superalloys with
better creep resistance. The investigated SC and P/M
alloys were designed from Ni-base CC alloy, TM-47,
commercial disc alloy, U720Li, and Ni-Co-base cast and
wrought alloy, TMW-4M3. The main conclusions of
this study are as follows:
(1) Promising alloy compositions for turbine disc
alloys can be explored excluding the influences of
grain size and grain boundary precipitates using
the creep tests of SC samples. The alloy compo-
sition screening can be used when both creep
times of SC alloy and the corresponding P/M
alloy are in identical order.
(2) The creep resistance is excellent in the order of
TM-47 SC, TMW-4M3 SC, and U720Li SC in the
tensile creep tests of SC samples at 1073 K
