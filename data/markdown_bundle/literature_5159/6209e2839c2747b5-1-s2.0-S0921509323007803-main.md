ABSTRACT 
The microstructure evolution, tensile properties, and strengthening mechanisms of a near- α high-temperature 
titanium alloy with high Zr and Si content during isothermal multi-dimensional forging (IMDF) at the β -phase 
region were systematically investigated. The results show that the alloys exhibit a basket-weave microstructure 
after IMDF, with the silicides diffusely distributed in the matrix. As the forging temperature decreased, the 
intragranular primary α laths became more refined and the volume fraction of silicides increased, while the 
average size of silicides gradually decreased. The dynamic spheroidization of α laths occurs preferentially at the 
prior β grain boundary and extends into the grains as the deformation temperature decreases. The IMDF-1100 
alloy possesses excellent high-temperature tensile properties and moderate room-temperature tensile proper -
ties, with ultimate tensile strengths (UTS) of 738.4 MPa and 1124.8 MPa at 650 
◦
C and room temperature, 
respectively, which makes the alloy have tremendous potential for aerospace applications. The strengthening of 
the alloy at room temperature is primarily attributed to microstructure refinement and the thermal mismatch 
between silicide and matrix. However, owing to the weakening of grain boundaries at 650 
◦
C, grain refinement 
leads to a decrease in tensile strengths. When tensile at 650 
◦
C, the fragmentation and decohesion of large-size 
silicides tend to cause premature fracture of the alloy, and the microstructure with small-size silicide distribution 
presents better ductility. 
1. Introduction 
High-temperature titanium alloys have already become one of the 
most critical structural materials in aerospace and military fields for 
their high specific strength and outstanding resistance to high temper -
atures and corrosion [ 1 ]. The service temperature of conventional 
high-temperature titanium alloys cannot exceed 600 
◦
C; otherwise, a 
dramatic decline in oxidation resistance and mechanical properties will 
happen [ 2 ]. Recently, several new high-temperature titanium alloys 
containing massive high melting point β -stable elements (Nb, Ta, W, 
etc.) have been reported sequentially, and their service temperature is 
expected to reach 650 
◦
C or even higher [ 3-5 ]. Apart from alloying, 
thermomechanical processing is necessary to optimize the microstruc -
ture and improve the overall mechanical properties. However, limited 
by the high stacking fault energy (SFE) of titanium alloys, conventional 
high-temperature deformation (e.g., hot extrusion and hot rolling) has 
difficulty in introducing sufficient strain in the matrix to promote 
microstructure refinement and second-phase precipitation [ 6 ]. As a se -
vere plastic deformation (SPD) technique, IMDF can accumulate suffi -
cient strain and dislocation in the material by loading in various 
directions. Moreover, IMDF has the merits of low-cost equipment and a 
relatively straightforward process, making it one of the most competi -
tive methods for preparing high-performance alloy billets [ 7 , 8 ]. 
The microstructure of high-temperature titanium alloys depends on 
the parameters of the thermal deformation process, in terms of tem -
perature, strain rate, deformation passes, etc. In past years, plenty of 
studies have concentrated on the evolution of microstructure and 
improvement of mechanical properties of high-temperature titanium 
alloys and composites during IMDF in the α + β two phase region [ 9-13 ]. 
Sun et al. found that the microstructure of the alloy was significantly 
refined due to dynamic recrystallization (DRX) after IMDF and that the 
DRX mechanisms were dominated by the deformation temperature and

β -phase region, which are critical for optimizing the SPD process pa -

not clear. 
For improving strength and creep resistance, an appropriate content 
of Si has usually been added to high-temperature titanium alloys [ 16 , 
17 ]. Si in high-temperature titanium alloys usually exists as both solid 
solutions in the matrix and silicide precipitation, and under certain 
circumstances, they can be converted into one another. The precipita -
tion strengthening effect of Si in the form of silicides is generally 
considered to be higher than that of solid solution strengthening [ 2 ], and 
the element Zr can significantly reduce the solubility of Si in the alloy 
matrix [ 18 ]. Although the strengthening of titanium alloys or compos -
ites via tailoring the precipitation behavior of silicides has been exten -
sively reported in the literature [ 10 , 14 , 19 ], the contribution of silicides 
to the yield strengths (YS) calculated for most of them is insignificant. 
The reason for this is that silicides precipitate in small amounts and are 
unevenly distributed, making it difficult to achieve effective 
enhancement. 
In order to give full play to the strengthening effect of silicides on the 
matrix, a novel near- α titanium alloy with high Zr and Si content was 
designed in this study, and the precipitation behavior of silicides was 
regulated by the strategy of “ solution quenching → SPD ” . The solution 
treatment prior to the SPD aims to back-dissolve the silicides precipi -
tated by the pre-deformation (blooming forging) into the matrix. This 
prevents the silicides generated by pre-deformation from continuing to 
coarsen during subsequent thermal deformation, thus seriously endan -
gering alloy plasticity. After solution, a high level of strain is imposed on 
the alloy by IMDF in the β -phase region to induce uniform, dense, and 
small-scale re-precipitation of silicides and to obtain a fine basket-weave 
microstructure. This strategy provides a new approach to optimizing the 
distribution and size of the second-phase precipitation and can be 
applied to other alloy systems. Additionally, the evolution of micro -
structure and the precipitation characteristics of silicides during IMDF in 
the β -phase region were quantitatively analyzed, and the strengthening 
mechanisms and failure behavior were discussed in detail.

By using the vacuum arc melting method, the near- α titanium alloy

2Mo-2Nb-1W-0.5Si (wt.%). The actual chemical composition of the 
alloy is listed in Table 1 . The α + β / β transus temperature of the alloy

◦

After solutioning, three 35 mm × 35 mm × 70 mm blocks were cut 
from the alloy, painted with coatings, and ready for the following IMDF 
procedure. The detailed IMDF process is shown in Fig. 1 a. In the con -
stant temperature environment, the specimens were successively 
pressed down along A, B, and C directions, each time with 50% of the 
pressed-down amount, and the shape and size were unchanged before 
and after deformation. Prior to each deformation step, the samples were 
insulated at the corresponding temperature for 20 min. For easy iden -
tification, these three samples were labeled IMDF-1100, IMDF-1060, 
and IMDF-1020, respectively, according to their deformation tempera -
tures. The IMDF process of alloy billet was numerically simulated using 
Deform-3D software, and its distributions of effective strain at different 
work steps are shown in Fig. 1 b. It could be seen that the large strains 
were concentrated in the “ X ” region consisting of the center and diag -
onal of the sample. To ensure accuracy, the samples used for observing 
the microstructure and testing tensile properties were cut from the 
centre ( Fig. 1 c). The planes composed of compression direction (CD) and 
extension direction (ED) were observed in the microstructure samples. 
The microstructures of the alloy were characterized by optical mi -
croscope (OM), scanning electron microscope (SEM, Gemini SEM 300), 
and transmission electron microscope (TEM, Talos F200X). The samples 
used for metallographic observation were sequentially ground, me -
chanically polished, and eroded with Kroll ’ s solution. Quantitative sta -
tistics of microstructures were performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (IPP) 
software. In addition, the crystalline orientations were observed using 
electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) conducted on the JEOL-JSM- 
IT500. Samples for EBSD analysis were mechanically grinded and 
electrolytically polished, and the electrolytic polishing solution con -
sisted of 60 vol% methanol, 30 vol% n-butyl, and 10 vol% perchloric 
acid. The HKL-Channel 5 software was used to analyze the acquired data 
following an automatic noise reduction step. The room-temperature and 
high-temperature tensile tests were carried out on the Instron-5969 
testing machine at crosshead speeds of 0.5 mm/min and 1 mm/min, 
respectively. Fig. 2 a and b shows the specimen dimensions used for 
room-temperature and high-temperature tensile testing, respectively. 
The samples in each condition were subjected to at least three parallel 
tensile tests.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Effect of forging temperature on microstructure 
During the IMDF process, the holding and deformation temperatures 
have a notable influence on the microstructure evolution of the alloys. 
When deformed at 1100 
◦
C, the alloy shows coarse columnar grains in 
the inverse pole figure (IPF) diagram ( Fig. 10 a). Dislocations and 
second-phase particles are reported to be nucleation spots for α phase 
precipitation during phase transitions [ 25 , 31 ]. The low dislocation 
density and low amount of silicide precipitation in the IMDF-1100 alloy 
mean that there are fewer α phase nucleation sites in the β grains. These 
few preferentially nucleated α grains grow along different orientations 
and eventually intertwine to form coarse primary α lamellas with re -
sidual β matrix distributed between them. As the temperature decreases, 
fine α
s 
will precipitate from the residual β matrix and β
t 
will be formed. 
In contrast, the IMDF-1020 alloy has a high dislocation density and 
plenty of silicide precipitation, so there are sufficient nucleation sites for 
the α phase. The massive α grains nucleate simultaneously, and their 
growth processes are constrained to each other and maintain a small 
size, resulting in the refinement of the intracrystalline α laths ( Fig. 7 a). 
Furthermore, there are numerous DRX grains found in the IMDF-1020 
alloy that expand from the prior β grain boundary into the β grains

4.3. Failure behavior 
Fig. 12 shows the room-temperature tensile fracture morphologies of 
the alloys, from which their fracture modes can be determined. The 
fracture morphology of the as-forged alloy consists of mostly smooth 
cleavage steps and a few dimples ( Fig. 12 a), indicating that the as-forged 
alloy is mainly transgranular brittle fracture at room temperature, so its 
elongation is only 3.0%. As shown in Fig. 12 b, the fracture morphology 
of the IMDF-1100 alloy still has some cleavage steps, but the dimples 
occupy the main part, indicating an improvement in its plasticity. In 
comparison, the fracture morphology of the IMDF-1020 alloy consists 
almost entirely of dimples, and the number of microcracks and micro -
pores is obviously increased ( Fig. 12 c and f), which suggests that it is a 
ductile fracture with micropores nucleated at the stress concentration 
areas and joined together to form cracks, and thus the elongation is 
further elevated. The fracture morphology of the alloys corresponds well 
to their room temperature elongation ( Fig. 9 a). In addition, zoomed 
fracture morphology from the as-forged and IMDF alloys revealed 
considerable amounts of ellipsoidal particles spread at the bottom of the 
dimples ( Fig. 12 d-f), which may be central to the formation of 
micropores [ 26 ]. EDS line scanning results of IMDF-1100 alloy frac -
tography show depletion of Ti elements and enrichment of Zr and Si 
elements at the particles, indicating that these particles are silicides 
( Fig. 13 ). Despite the higher quantity density of silicides in the frac -
tography of IMDF-1020 alloy ( Fig. 12 f), the load can be apportioned 
during tensile deformation and the stress concentration degree is lower, 
owing to their smaller size. The above results suggest that the plasticity 
of high-temperature titanium alloys depends more on the size of the 
silicides than on the volume fraction.

In current work, a near- α high-temperature titanium alloy with high 
Zr and Si content was fabricated via the vacuum arc melting method. 
The microstructure evolution, tensile properties, strengthening mecha -
nisms, and failure behavior during IMDF in the β -phase region were 
analyzed in detail. The main conclusions could be summarized as 
follows:

decreasing deformation temperature, the primary α laths become 
more refined and there is an increased tendency towards equi -

average size of the silicides decreases as the deformation tem -
perature decreases, but their volume fraction gradually increases. 
(2) The alloys deformed within the upper β -phase region showed 
excellent high-temperature tensile properties, with the UTS of the 
IMDF-1100 and IMDF-1060 alloys reaching 738.4 MPa and

◦

istics of the silicides affect the plasticity of the alloys, and inho -
mogeneous precipitation of large-size silicides will significantly 
deteriorate the ductility. 
(3) The strengthening mechanisms of the alloys at room temperature 
are mainly fine-grained strengthening and thermal mismatch 
strengthening by silicides. However, the grain boundaries 
weaken at high temperatures, and the fine α laths are more prone 
to coordinated deformation, so grain refinement becomes an 
unfavorable factor for high-temperature strength.

◦

decohesion and thus initiate micropores, while the failure 
mechanism of microstructures with small-size silicide distribu -
