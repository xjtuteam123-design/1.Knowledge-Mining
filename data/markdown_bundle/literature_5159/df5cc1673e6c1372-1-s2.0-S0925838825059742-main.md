1. Introduction
Traditionally designed alloys, typically based on one or two principal 
elements and modified by the addition of minor alloying elements, have 
been developed over several centuries [1,2] . However, with the 
increasing demands on material performance, a new class of 
multi-principal element alloys was discovered in the early 21st century 
by Brian Cantor and others. In 2004, Professor Yeh formally coined the 
term “ high-entropy alloys ” (HEAs). The original definition of HEAs 
referred to alloys consisting of no fewer than five main elements, each 
present in concentrations between 5 and 35 at. percent [3 – 6] . These 
alloys exhibit outstanding mechanical strength, thermal stability, and 
corrosion resistance [7 – 11] . However, despite their excellent overall 
performance, conventional HEAs often face the inherent trade-off be -
tween strength and ductility [12] .
To better address the strength-ductility trade-off in conventional 
HEAs, researchers proposed the concept of eutectic HEAs (EHEAs) [5, 
13 – 19] . These alloys combine the benefits of both eutectic alloys and 
high-entropy alloys, offering a promising pathway to achieve a balance 
between strength and plasticity [19 – 21] . Based on their phase consti -
tution, EHEAs can be broadly categorized into several types [22] , 
including FCC/BCC, FCC/intermetallic compounds (IMCs), and BCC/B2 
structures. In FCC/IMCs-type EHEAs, brittle intermetallic phases such as 
Laves phases and σ phases often form, which tend to cause interfacial 
fracture due to the weak bonding and stress concentration at phase 
boundaries. Examples of such alloys include FeCoNiNb
0.5

hand, BCC/B2-type EHEAs [28] , such as AlCrFeNi [29] which are

strength, but usually suffer from poor plasticity. To overcome this lim -

4. Discussion
4.1. Effect of heat treatment on microstructure of NiCoCrFeAlW EHEA
As noted earlier, with increasing temperature, a significant amount 
of precipitated phases form within the B2 matrix. This phenomenon is 
primarily attributed to the supersaturation of Co, Cr, and Fe elements in 
the B2 phase during solidification. Under non-equilibrium solidification 
conditions, these elements are retained in solid solution beyond their 
equilibrium solubility limits. During subsequent high-temperature heat 
treatment, the diffusion of Co, Cr, and Fe increases substantially with 
temperature, providing the necessary atomic mobility for the nucleation 
and growth of precipitated FCC phases.
Thermodynamically, the driving force for precipitation stems from 
the reduction in Gibbs free energy ( Δ G) as the system approaches 
equilibrium. The total free energy change associated with precipitation 
can be described as:

coarsen. This coarsening behavior follows Ostwald ripening, which is

phase dissolves completely at 1000 ℃ and 1050 ℃ , respectively, due to 
its reduced thermodynamic stability at high temperatures. These find -

inferred that the precipitated phase does not alter the fracture mode of 
the primary phase.
5. Conclusion
This study systematically explored the influence of heat treatment at 
various temperatures and durations on the microstructure and me -
chanical properties of the NiCoCrFeAlW EHEA. Microstructural char -
acterization under different conditions was performed, while tensile 
testing was also conducted. The main findings are outlined below: 
1. Fine FCC precipitates (CoCrFeW phase) are formed within the coarse 
dendritic B2 matrix (NiAl phase), indicating the relatively low 
thermal stability of the bulky dendritic B2 phase.
2. The volume fraction of precipitates increases with prolonged holding 
time as a result of the supersaturation of Co, W, Fe, and Cr elements 
in the B2 phase. Nevertheless, at 900℃, the precipitate fraction de -
creases owing to the thermal instability of the FCC phase.
