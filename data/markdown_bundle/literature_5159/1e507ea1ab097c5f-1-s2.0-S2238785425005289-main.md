ABSTRACT

grains was designed targeted for additive manufacturing on the basis of the composition formula of Ti65 alloy.

1. Introduction
High-temperature titanium alloys have drawn widely attention in
automobile manufacturing and aerospace fields, due to their excellent
specific strength, good corrosion resistance, and high creep performance
[ 1 , 2 ]. Additive manufacturing (AM), instead of investment casting al-
lows processing to occur radically differently, under direct input from a
computer-aided design (CAD) system, confers an as-of-yet untapped
freedom of design with reduced manufacturing steps and minimum
processing waste [ 3 – 5 ]. Laser additive manufacturing is a high accuracy
and flexibility near-net-shape technique for fabricating large and com-
plex Ti parts [ 1 ]. However, most high-temperature Ti alloys contain
low-melting-point Sn element, representative Ti1100 (Ti-6.0Al-2.7
Sn – 4Zr-0.4Mo-0.4Si) [ 6 ], IMI834 (Ti-6.0Al-2.7Sn – 4Zr-0.4Mo-0.4Si) [ 7 ],
Ti60 (Ti-5.8Al-4.8Sn – 2Zr – 1Mo-1Nd-0.4Si) [ 8 ], and Ti65 (Ti-5.9Al
– 4Sn-3.5Zr-0.3Mo-0.3Nb-2.0Ta-0.4Si-1.0W-0.05C) [ 9 ], being unsuit-
able for additive manufacturing. Sn element trends to burnout and
vaporization during additive manufacturing [ 10 , 11 ]. Some bubble
formed by Sn vaporization were not overfow from the melt in time and
might nucleate and grow to form porosity [ 12 ], resulting in loss of
mechanical properties. Zirconium (Zr) is an essential alloying element in
high-temperature Ti alloys. Both Zr and Ti belong to the same main
group (IVB) in the periodic table [ 9 ]. The Ti – Zr system forms a
completely solid solution for both β -phase and α -phase. Jing et al. [ 13 ]
reported that the tensile strength increases with increased Zr content,
due to solid solution strengthening. On the investigation, Yue et al. [ 9 ]
reported that Zr exhibits a β -stabilizing effect in near α titanium alloys.
Zr is beneficial to additive manufacturability due to favorable melt flow
[ 10 ]. And therefore, Zr is an optimal element to replace
low-melting-point Sn for the additive-manufacturing-oriented Ti alloy.
On the other hand, coarse β -columnar grains with a strong < 100 >
texture is readily occurred in additive-manufactured Ti alloys [ 14 , 15 ].
Such coarse β -columnar grains are severely aggravating the mechanical
properties of the alloy [ 16 ]. Recently, the published articles [ 17 , 18 ]
reported that coarse β -columnar grains of Ti alloys are adjusted by the
addition of W, Fe, Cu, and Ta elements, which are conducive to cause
compositional supercooling, contributing to the formation of
fine-equiaxed β grains. However, rarely fine-equiaxed β grains occur in

2. Composition design using the cluster formula approach
2.1. Cluster-plus-glue-atom model
The accurate modeling of chemical short-range ordering in solid
solution is the essential for the alloy composition design. In 2007, Dong
et al. [ 19 ] proposed a cluster-plus-glue-atom model, abbreviated as CPGA
model, to describe the short-range-order structures in solid solutions.
This model identifies a structural unit as the composition gene of the
material, formulated as [cluster](glue atoms), covering the basic
nearest-neighbor coordinate polyhedron “ cluster ” plus a few
next-neighbor glue atoms. The CPGA model was theoretically proven
using Friedel oscillations theorem [ 19 ], which describes the charge
screening behavior. The effective pair potential of Friedel oscillations is
expressed as φ
(r)

F

and mean-density radial distance is obtained, including only a
nearest-neighbor cluster plus a few next-neighbor glue atoms. Such units
represent the smallest particles that are representative of the whole
structures, just like molecules do for chemical substances. On the basis of
CPGA model, various high performences of high-entropy alloys [ 22 , 23 ]
and metallic glasses [ 24 ] have been explored.

3. Experimental and methods
Powders of Ti (size ranges of 50 – 200 μ m), Al, Mo, Nb, Ta, and W (size
ranges of 30 – 150 μ m), Zr and Si (size ranges of 25 – 120 μ m), supplied by
Avimetal Powder Metallurgy Technology Co. Ltd., China, were used in
this work. Ti, Al, Mo and Nb have a spherical morphology, and others Zr,
Ta, W and Si have an irregular morphology, as seen in Fig. 1 (a). Pure
elemental powders were mixed in a blending machine for 4 h at ambient
temperature and then dried in a vacuum drying furnace at 120
◦
C for 4 h,
and then performed on an additive manufacturing system (YLS-6000,
supplied by IPG Photonics Corporation, Germany), as shown in the
schematic diagram of Fig. 1 (b). The working parameters are recom-
mended by Ref. [ 16 ]: laser power of 2000 W, scanning velocity of 10
mm/s, powder feed rate of 1.2 r/min, hatching overlap of 30%, Z-axis
lifted height of 0.6 mm, and beam diameter of 3.0 mm. The substrate is a
Ti – 6Al – 4V plate with a size of 140 mm × 100 mm × 20 mm and is
washed using ethyl alcohol and acetone. A zigzag scanning strategy for
bulk samples is used and rotated by 90
◦
between each layer. Bulk ingots
with a size of 60 mm × 30 mm × 20 mm are layer-by-layer deposited
using the above parameters, as shown in Fig. 1 . The samples are
deposited in an argon protective atmosphere with the oxygen level
below 100 ppm.

4.1. Microstructure
Fig. 2 (a – c) shows OM images of the as-deposited Sn-free Ti alloys

the microstructures and mechanical properties of AZT6115 with com-

Fig. 3 presents XRD spectrum of the as-deposited Sn-free Ti alloys. As

larger than the Ti atoms (0.146 nm), which results in an increase in the
crystal spacing of the α matrix [ 26 ].
