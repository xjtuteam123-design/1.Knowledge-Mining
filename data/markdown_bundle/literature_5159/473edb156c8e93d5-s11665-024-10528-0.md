ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Study of the Microstructures and Fatigue–Creep
Properties of a Laser-Directed Energy Deposition
Epitaxial Ni-Based Superalloy
Wuzhu Yan, Yuchang Guo, Zhen Li, Xifeng Chen, Shujing Liang, Jun Liu, Tang Gu, Kim S. Siow, and Xu Long
Submitted: 24 June 2024 / Revised: 10 November 2024 / Accepted: 15 November 2024 / Published online: 9 December 2024
In recent decades, laser-directed energy deposition (LDED) has become one of the most promising tech-
niques for additive manufacturing. In this work, LDED technology was employed to prepare thin-walled
nickel (Ni)-based superalloys. The microstructures of epitaxial Ni-based superalloys were investigated via a
series of characterization techniques. The results revealed that the constantly increasing number of
microdefects clustered together during the LDED process to transform low-angle grain boundaries into
high-angle grain boundaries, resulting in the formation of stray grains. Moreover, ﬁnite element simulations
of the LDED process were carried out to analyze the transient temperature ﬁeld and stress ﬁeld during and
after deposition, respectively. The maximal residual tensile stress occurred at the epitaxy/stray grain
interface. The experimental results revealed that the fatigue–creep life of the repaired sample was shorter
than that of the as-prepared sample, especially under higher loads. In addition, the as-prepared sample
fractured in the direction of 45 /C176due to crystallographic slipping, whereas the failure of the repaired sample
occurred at the epitaxy/stray grain interface due to a higher defect density and greater residual tensile
stress.
Keywords failure mechanism, fatigue–creep life, FE method,
laser-directed energy deposition, Ni-based superalloys
1. Introduction
Owing to their excellent mechanical properties at high
temperatures, nickel (Ni)-based superalloys have been exten-
sively used in gas turbines and aeroengines (Ref 1-6). As a
second-generation Ni-based superalloy, DD6 was indepen-
dently developed and applied in the turbine blades of advanced
aeroengines (Ref 4-6). DD6 has the advantages of lower costs
and higher performance at high temperatures than other second-
generation Ni-based superalloys (Ref 4-6).
As a type of laser additive manufacturing (LAM) technol-
ogy, laser-directed energy deposition (LDED) can not only
fabricate complex aircraft structural parts (Ref 7-9) but also
repair damaged component parts. In the aviation industry,
replacing damaged turbine blades directly with new blades is
not economical because of their high manufacturing cost.
Moreover, the mechanical properties of turbine blades manu-
factured completely by LDED are inferior to those resulting
from traditional casting technology. Therefore, repairing dam-
aged blades can extend their service life and reduce mainte-
nance costs effectively. The traditional method of repairing
turbine blades involves coordination measurements, manual
welding and polishing. This method has several disadvantages,
such as requiring multiple clamps, relying on experience,
having low efﬁciency and being time-consuming. The manu-
facturing difﬁculty of additive manufacturing technology is
independent of geometrical complexity, so it has broad
prospects in rapid prototyping of thin-walled components.
More importantly, LDED technology, which is clean and
pollution-free, can directly deposit melted powder layer by
layer in the repair area of damaged parts and can also be applied
to various metal materials compared with other additive
manufacturing technologies. It can also signiﬁcantly improve
the mechanical properties and corrosion resistance of materials.
This endows LDED technology with great advantages in
manufacturing or repairing thin-walled structures with spatial
complexity (Ref 8).
To date, many researchers have focused on the microstruc-
tures and mechanical properties of LDED-formed parts. Hanon
et al. (Ref 10) investigated the factors impacting both the
friction coefﬁcient and wear as well as on other mechanical
properties of 3D-printed composite materials. Sreeramagiri
et al. (Ref 11) noted that controlling the cooling direction could
effectively promote the formation quality of single crystals
during the laser deposition process. Yeoh et al. (Ref 12) and
Imbrogno et al. (Ref 13) studied the effects of the deposition
rates and laser frequency on the microstructure morphology and

In this work, we use LDED technology to epitaxially grow
single crystals on a DD6 Ni-based superalloy substrate to form
thin-walled repaired samples. This process simulates the repair
process of turbine blades via LDED technology. Microstructure
characterization technologies, such as x-ray diffraction (XRD)
(Bruker, Germany), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD)
and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) technologies, were
employed to analyze the phase composition and microstructure
distribution of the epitaxial Ni-based superalloy fabricated by
LDED. The FE method was subsequently used to simulate the
LDED process to obtain the temperature ﬁeld and stress ﬁeld of
the deposition layers. Finally, a series of fatigue–creep tests
were carried out on the as-prepared samples and the repaired
samples at 980 /C176 C. The fatigue–creep properties of the two
groups of samples were investigated in terms of their fatigue–
creep lives and fracture morphology. The present work provides
important theoretical guidance for the development and appli-
cation of LDED technology in the fabrication and repair of
single-crystal turbine blades.
2. Experiment
The material of the substrate used in the present work is a
Ni-based single-crystal superalloy DD6 with dimensions of
15 mm 9 15 mm 9 5 mm. The same DD6 metal powder was
used in the LDED process to form Ni-based superalloy thin-
walled samples. The chemical composition of the Ni-based
superalloy DD6 is listed in Table 1.
During the LDED process, the laser beam scanned along a
preset scanning track and moved at a speed of 5 mm/s. At the
same time, the DD6 powders were coaxially injected into the
molten pool at a rate of 10 g/min and deposited along the
building direction layer by layer on the single-crystal substrate.
The building direction was parallel to the crystallographic
orientation of the [001] plane of the substrate. The height of
each deposition layer was 0.35-0.4 mm. As shown in Fig. 1,
two single-track multilayer LDED samples were produced. For
convenience, the Cartesian coordinate system O-XYZ was
established, in which the X-axis and Y -axis are aligned with the
laser scanning direction and the building direction, respectively.
As shown in Fig. 1(a), the injected metal powders were melted
by the laser beam and then quickly solidiﬁed to form deposition
layers on the substrate. After deposition, the applied heat
treatment routine included 1) a solution step at 980 /C176 C for
75 min and 2) a standard double aging step at 720 /C176 C+5 0 /C176 C/
h in a furnace cooled to 620 /C176 C + 620 /C176 C for 8 h.
The thin-walled sample formed by the LDED process on the
Ni-based superalloy DD6 substrate is shown in Fig. 1(b). The
LDED equipment is shown in Fig. 1(c), and an illustration of
the LDED process is shown in Fig. 1(d). After cutting, grinding
and corrosion, the samples were observed via an x-ray
diffractometer (Bruker D8 DISCOVER A25, Germany) and a
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) system. X-ray diffraction
(XRD) analysis was performed to determine the phases of the
Ni-based superalloy thin-walled repaired samples. A scanning
electron microscope equipped with electron backscatter diffrac-
tion (EBSD) was used to observe the microstructure of the Ni-
based superalloys after repair. The EBSD data, such as the
inverse pole ﬁgure (IPF), were analyzed by A TEX software to
obtain a density map of the geometrically necessary disloca-
tions (GNDs). GNDs are dislocations stored in the Burger
circuit, which is related to the curvature of the crystal lattice of
a material. Research results indicate that GNDs play a major
role in the additive manufacturing of Ni-based single-crystal
thin-walled components (Ref 18).
To investigate its fatigue–creep properties, the geometry and
size of the fatigue–creep samples were designed and are shown
in Fig. 2(a). The fabricated thin-walled sample was cut by an
electric spark. Figure 2(b) shows a schematic of the fatigue–
creep test sample, which includes the substrate and the
deposition layers. The substrate/cladding layer interface is in
the center of the fatigue–creep sample. After that, the same
fatigue–creep samples were cut from the as-prepared counter-
parts (substrates). The two groups of small samples, the as-
prepared samples and the thin-walled repaired samples of the
DD6 superalloy, are referred to as the as-prepared samples and
the repaired samples, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3(a), two
groups of fatigue–creep tests with respect to the two groups of
samples were carried out with an electrohydraulic servo
universal test machine (PLW-50).

3.3 Model Development
The size of the Ni-based single-crystal superalloy matrix
was 15 mm 9 15 mm 9 5 mm, and the size of the upper laser
deposition layers on the substrate was 3 mm 9 10 mm 9 6
mm. A raster laser scanning path was adopted. According to the
process parameters of LDED, the lifting amount of each
deposition layer is 0.40 mm. There were 15 deposition layers in
the model. The cell-by-cell activation of elements was per-
formed via ‘‘Model Change’’ in a series of established
analytical steps. As shown in Fig. 6, the mesh of the laser
additive manufacturing region of the model was reﬁned. The
type of mesh was selected for heat transfer, which can be used
for thermal analysis.
During the simulation, it is necessary to ensure that the mesh
and nodes of the thermal analysis are the same as those of the
mechanical analysis. In the thermal analysis model, the initial
temperature ﬁeld was predeﬁned as room temperature, and then
the laser heat source was loaded into the model. Heat source
loading and scanning were realized by performing a specially
written user subroutine of the double ellipsoid heat source
model. In the mechanical analysis, the transient temperature
ﬁeld resulting from each step was introduced into the mechan-
ical analysis as an initial condition.
Through XRD, the residual stress of the laser additive-

4.2 Microstructure Distribution
When the DD6 powder was deposited in the molten pool
formed by laser heating, the grains nucleated on the single-
crystal substrate, epitaxially growing along the preferred
crystallographic orientation into the columnar dendrites. The
epitaxial single crystal had the same crystallographic orienta-
tion as the substrate. In addition, the deposition process also
included the columnar-to-equiaxed transition (CET). By that
time, the nature of a single crystal was lost, resulting in random
orientations of grain growth. The epitaxial single crystals then
became stray polycrystals.
The inverse pole ﬁgure along the Y-axis (IPF Y0) in Fig. 8
shows the microstructure morphology and crystal orientation
on the XOY plane of the Ni-based superalloy repaired sample.
In Fig. 8, the red, green and blue colors represent the [001],
[101] and [111] crystallographic orientations, respectively. The
growth of epitaxial single crystals and the formation of
disordered stray grains can be clearly observed. The black
dashed line represents the substrate/cladding layer interface,
whereas the white dashed line represents the interface between
the stray grains region and the epitaxial single-crystal region
ranging from 500 to 800 lm.
In addition, the red and black lines among the different
grains represent the low-angle ( < 15/C176 ) grain boundaries
(LAGBs) and the high-angle ( > 15/C176 ) grain boundaries
(HAGBs), respectively, which are detrimental to the mechan-
ical properties of the Ni-based superalloy. Figure 8 shows the
HAGBs and LAGBs in the stray grain region due to the
different crystal orientations. Moreover, a few LAGBs can also
be seen in the epitaxial single-crystal region, indicating that

4.3 Transient Temperature Field
During the laser deposition process, the DD6 powders began
to melt when the temperature reached 1370 /C176 C, forming a
molten pool. Figure 10 shows the shape of the molten pool of
the ﬁrst deposition layer. The gray part represents the molten
pool beyond the melting point of the material. The highest
temperature of the molten pool reached up to 1732 /C176 C.
For the thermal analysis, ﬁve points ( A, B, C, D and E) in the
different layers were selected to obtain the temperature–time
curve for each point during the whole laser deposition process,
as shown in Fig. 11. The temperature of the deposition layers
changes periodically. During the deposition of each layer, the
temperature of the selected nodes experienced a thermal cycle
involving a peak point. However, the number of thermal cycles
for the ﬁve points is different because of their different
positions in the deposition layers. At 10-20 s, the temperature
of point C is always higher than that of point B at the same time
and much higher than that of point A (T
C >T B >T A). In
addition, the temperature differences among different points
decrease with increasing time, and the curves of different points
eventually converge at one point. This occurred because heat
was transferred to the lower layers during the laser deposition
process, resulting in a reduction in the temperature gradient in
the stacking direction. The formation of microstructures is
strongly affected by the temperature gradient. The transient
temperature gradient and cooling rate can affect the formation
and growth of grains, thereby altering their size and morphol-
ogy (Ref 19, 20), which results in the formation of stray grains.
The change in the temperature gradient shown in Fig. 11
effectively explains the microstructure gradient in the stacking
direction, as shown in Fig. 8. These ﬁndings indicate that the
temperature simulation results are consistent with the experi-
mental results.
With increasing number of layers, the maximum tempera-
ture at each point increased. This is because the heat in the
layers below it has not been fully conducted or dissipated,
resulting in a reduction in the temperature gradient in the
stacking direction, which further reduces the efﬁciency of heat
transfer from above to below. For each temperature–time curve,
for example, point B, the peak temperature of the ﬁrst thermal
cycle is highest. The temperatures of the other peak points
decrease in sequence. This occurred because the heat trans-
ferred to point B from the upper layers decreased with
increasing number of layers.
4.4 Residual Stress Distribution
To clearly observe the residual stress distribution after laser
deposition, the model was cut in the XOY plane (normal to the Z
direction). Figure 12(a) and (b) shows the residual stress
distributions in the X and Y directions. In Fig. 12(c), the density
of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) was obtained
by analyzing the data of the IPF map in Fig. 8 via A TEX
software. The presence of dislocations can cause local stress
ﬁelds, and an increase in dislocation density can lead to the
superposition of local stress ﬁelds, thereby increasing residual
stresses in the material (Ref 7). Figure 12 shows that
remarkable residual tensile stress occurred near the epitaxy/
stray grain interface, which agreed well with the distribution of
dislocation (mainly GND) density. These ﬁndings indicate that
the residual stress simulation results are consistent with the
experimental results. During laser deposition, the various slip
systems activated near the epitaxy/stray grain interface may
result in a higher density of inhomogeneous dislocations
(mainly GNDs) (Ref 7). Moreover, the activation of the slip
system is dependent on the greater shear stress induced in the
material. Hence, the residual stress induced by laser deposition
can be associated with the dislocation density. The higher

4.5 Fatigue–Creep Life of a Ni-Based Superalloy
As shown in Fig. 13, two groups of samples were used to
carry out a series of fatigue–creep tests at 980 /C176 C. The loading
range applied to the as-prepared samples was 180-350 N,
whereas that applied to the repaired samples was 60-270 N.
The fatigue–creep lives of the as-prepared samples are much
longer than those of the repaired samples.
Figure 13 shows the typical failure mode of the two groups
of Ni-based superalloy samples. The sampling positions of the
local morphology of the repaired sample are marked with a red
block diagram in Fig. 13(a) and (b). Both the as-prepared
sample and the repaired sample fractured in the central region
of the samples. Figure 13(a) and (b) shows that the as-prepared
sample fractures in the direction 45 /C176 with respect to the loading
direction, which is induced by crystallographic slipping driven
by the resolved shear stress. A clear necking phenomenon was
observed in the fracture surface, indicating that the Ni-based
superalloys underwent plastic deformation under fatigue load-
ing. Figure 13(c) and (d) shows that the repaired sample
fractures in the direction parallel to the substrate/cladding layer
interface. The fracture of the repaired sample can be attributed
to defects such as microvoids and microcracks induced by the
LDED process at the epitaxy/stray grain interface. In addition,
the fracture surface experiences no obvious necking phe-
nomenon, suggesting that the ductility of the Ni-based super-
alloy sample decreased after repair via LDED technology.
Through a series of fatigue–creep tests, the fatigue–creep
lives of the as-prepared samples and the repaired samples under
different loading conditions were obtained, as shown in Fig. 14.
The appearance of these microscopic defects during LDED
processing, such as micropores and microcracks, greatly
reduces the strength and life of the repaired specimen (Ref
21). In Fig. 14, the experimental stress life ( S–N) curve of the
repaired sample is obtained by ﬁtting the experimental data. A
comparison of the two S–N curves reveals that the fatigue–
creep life of the repaired samples is shorter than that of the as-
prepared samples, especially under higher loads. This can be
attributed to the growth and coalescence of microdefects at the
epitaxy/stray grain interface (see Fig. 12) induced by the higher
load.
4.6 Failure Mechanism of the Fatigue–Creep Tests
The fracture surfaces of the as-prepared samples and the
repaired samples were observed via SEM. The as-prepared
samples were placed on the specimen table at an angle of 45 /C176 ,
whereas the repaired samples were placed on the specimen
table during the scanning process. Figure 15(a) and (c) shows
the typical overall morphology of their fracture surfaces,
whereas Fig. 15(b) and (d) shows their locally enlarged views.
Figure 15(a) and (c) shows that the fracture surface of the as-

5. Conclusions
This paper presents the manufacturing of Ni-based super-
alloy repaired samples via laser-directed energy deposition
(LDED) technology. Phase ﬁeld analysis and microstructure
examination were conducted via x-ray diffraction (XRD) and
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with electron backscatter
diffraction (EBSD). Fatigue–creep tests were performed on
both as-prepared and repaired samples to study the failure
mechanism. The main conclusions can be drawn as follows:
As deposition progresses, a very small number of microde-
fects occur in the single-crystal region. The constantly
increasing number of microdefects clustered together at the
interface and transferred from LAGBs to HAGBs, result-
ing in the formation of stray grains.
During layer-by-layer deposition, heat is transferred from
above to below, and the temperature history in the deposi-
tion layers changes periodically. With increasing number
of layers, the heat transfer efﬁciency and temperature gra-
dient in the stacking direction decreased, generating a
microstructure gradient in the stacking direction.
After the LDED process, the largest harmful residual tensile
stress occurred near the epitaxy/stray grain interface,
which agrees well with the distribution of the dislocation
density. This indicates that the danger point of the repaired
sample is located at the epitaxy/stray grain interface.
The fatigue–creep life of the repaired sample is shorter than
that of the as-prepared sample, which can be attributed to
the clustering of microdefects at the epitaxy/stray grain
interface.
The as-prepared sample fractured in the direction 45 /C176 with
respect to the loading direction, and its failure was in-
duced mainly by fatigue damage. The repaired sample
fractured at the epitaxy/stray grain interface, which was in-
duced by the increased residual tensile stress and increased
dislocation density.
