Abstract 
Bulk ultrafine structured metallic materials with the bi-modal grain size distribution exhibit both 
high strength and good ductility. Here we show a new family of bi-modal Ti-Nb-Ni-Co-Al alloys. 
Their microstructure consists of an ultrafine structured eutectic matrix and relatively coarse β-
Ti dendrites. Chemical modification of the parent Ti-Nb-Ni-Cu-Al system significantly affected 
type and volume fraction of matrix phases. In its turn, this influenced the deformation 
behavior. The unexpected “double yielding” behavior as well as tensile/compressive fracture 
strength asymmetry of the designed alloys are discussed in detail. For that, the microstructure 
alterations under compressive and tensile loading was in-situ and ex-situ analyzed by scanning 
electron microscopy. 
Keywords: titanium alloys, bi-modal, electron microscopy, mechanical characterization, fracture.

1. Introduction 
The high strength comparable with that of bulk metallic glass composites (BMGCs) [1–3] 
advantageously combined with high plastic deformability attracts the development of new Ti-
based composites consisting of dendrites and nano-/ultrafine structures (DNUS) [4–6]. In 
particular, this is also because they are not prone to a significant strain softening and do not 
contain harmful Be such as some Ti-based BMGCs [3]. The combination of nano-/ultrafine and 
coarse phases in the Ti-based DNUSs allows obtaining the properties beyond the predictable 
ones (deviating from the rule of mixture) as in bi-modal Cu [7]. Furthermore, the Ti-based 
DNUSs are potentially applicable already in the as-cast state, e.g. as biomedical or spring 
materials [6,8]. 
Since He et al. reported about the novel Ti-based DNUSs [4], a number of alloys from different 
Ti-based systems were developed [6,9–13]. The mechanical properties of these composites 
basically depend on volume fraction and size of the β-Ti phase as well as on size and type of 
intermetallic compounds [5,6,9]. Typically, the ductile and tough β-Ti phase provides a 
pronounced plastic deformability, while the strong intermetallic compounds are responsible for 
a high strength. 
The basic principle for designing ductile (in tensile loading) Ti-based DNUSs is an optimization of 
volume fraction/distribution and type of interdendritic phases [6]. It was shown that the 
disconnected distribution of typically brittle intermetallics is more favorable for tensile 
plasticity than the interconnected one [14]. This distribution can be controlled by tuning the 
amount of alloying elements, which normally form intermetallic phases (Cu, Ni, Co) [6], or by

their substitution [14], which exploits a difference in the solubility of these elements in β-Ti. 
The substitution of these elements can also result in the change of the type of the intermetallic 
phases like in Ti-Nb-Ni(-Co)-Al alloys [14], where the complete substitution of Ni by Co results in 
the formation of TiCo (Pm-3m) instead of Ti2Ni (Fm-3m). In its turn, this considerably affects the 
mechanical behavior of the composites. 
In this study we report about phase formation and mechanical behavior of new bi-modal Ti-Nb-
Ni-Co-Al alloys. They are particularly interesting because of an untypical deformation behavior 
(e.g. “double yielding”). A better understanding of compressive/tensile fracture strength 
asymmetry of the Ti-based DNUSs is presented. All conclusions concerning deformation 
behavior are supported by ex-situ and in-situ analyses of the microstructure of mechanically 
tested samples in the scanning electron microscope. 
2. Experimental details 
Two alloys, Ti 66Nb13Ni8Co6.8Al6.2 (alloy A) and Ti 71.8Nb14.1Ni4Co7.4Al6.7 (alloy B), were selected for 
the study, because they show a very good combination of strength and ductility. 
Samples with cylindrical shape of 100 mm in length and 12 mm in diameter were produced 
from pure elements (99.9 wt. %) by inductive melting and casting into a water-cooled Cu-
crucible under pure argon atmosphere (99.999 %). The details are described elsewhere [6]. 
X-ray diffractometry (XRD, STOE STADI P with Mo-K α1 radiation) and scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Leo Gemini 1530) combined with energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX, 
Bruker Xflash 4010) were used for the microstructure characterization. Rietveld refinement 
(X’Pert HighScore Plus) was applied to evaluate the crystal structure. Quantitative analysis of

the microstructure was performed by the ImageJ image analysis software. Flat tensile test 
samples with 8 mm gauge length and cylindrical compressive test samples (6 mm long) were 
prepared by electro-erosive machining according to the DIN 50125 [15] and ASTM: E9 – 09 
standards, respectively. The deformation behavior and the mechanical properties were

temperature. The strain was measured by a laser extensometer (Fiedler Optoelektronik). The 
microstructure change during compressive testing was investigated with a mechanical testing 
device (Kammrath and Weiss) in the SEM. 
3. Results and Discussions 
3.1. Microstructure 
According to the XRD patterns (Fig. 1) the alloys, Ti 66Nb13Ni8Co6.8Al6.2 (A) and 
Ti71.8Nb14.1Ni4Co7.4Al6.7 (B) (at.%), consist of two phases, i.e. β-Ti (Im-3m) and Ti 2Ni (Fd-3m). The 
stabilization of the high temperature β-Ti phase at room temperature is due to alloying by beta-
stabilizers (Nb, Co, and Ni) [16] and relatively high cooling rates (about 10 2 K/s) [17]. The lattice 
parameter of β-Ti is a = 0.324 ± 0.001 nm for both alloys. The determined value is similar to that 
of β-Ti in the parent Ti-Nb-Cu-Ni-Al alloys [6]. Both studied composites contain a big cube phase 
Ti2Ni (Fd-3m). The same Ti 2Ni phase was observed in the previously reported 
Ti71.8Nb14.1Ni7.4Al6.7 [14]. The lattice parameter of Ti 2Ni is a = 1.130 ± 0.001 nm and a = 1.131 ± 
0.001 nm for alloy A and alloy B, respectively. The details of the crystal structure of all phases 
are summarized in Table 1. Thus, one can conclude that the modification of the parent alloys 
(Ti-Nb-Ni-Cu-Al) [6] results in a change of the matrix phases, e.g. the type of the intermetallic 
phases.

The homogeneity of phase distribution was analyzed by microstructure studies of top and 
bottom cross sections of each rod sample in the SEM. The longitudinal homogeneity was 
investigated by means of microstructure analysis of polished tensile samples. The cross section 
of the rods is characterized by three microstructural regions, i.e. near-surface, dendritic (Fig. 2) 
and dendritic with interdendritic porosity, as it was already observed for Ti-Nb-Cu-Ni-Al alloys 
[6]. The tensile and compressive specimens were taken from the dendritic region only. A 
detailed microstructural analysis was also performed for this zone. 
The microstructure of alloy A is composed of micrometer sized dendrites embedded in an 
ultrafine structured matrix (Figs. 2 a, c). According to XRD and EDX analysis (Table 1), the 
dendrites are β-Ti. The two metallographic constituents are distinguishable in the eutectic 
matrix region. Based on XRD data, these are assumed to be Ti 2Ni and β-Ti. The eutectic colonies 
in alloy A have a lamellar spacing of about 300 nm (Fig. 2 a, inset). The thickness of the eutectic 
matrix varies from 1 µm to 25 µm. The size, SDA and the volume fraction of the dendrites in 
alloy A are about 6 ± 1 µm, 60 ± 7 µm, and 60 ± 5 vol. %, respectively. 
The microstructure of the dendritic zone in alloy B is composed of micrometer sized dendrites 
surrounded by an ultrafine structured eutectic network (Figs. 2 b, d). The eutectic colonies in 
alloy B have a lamellar spacing of about 300 nm (Fig. 2 b, inset). The thickness of the eutectic 
network reaches up to 5 µm. The size, SDA and the volume fraction of the dendrites of alloy B 
are about 8 ± 2 µm, 76 ± 17 µm, and 91 ± 2 vol. %, respectively. 
Thus, the reduction of the Co-Ni content in alloy B with respect to alloy A results in a coarsening 
of dendrites and an increasing of their volume fraction. It also affects the distribution of the

interdendritic phases. The comparison of the actual alloys with the parent ones [5,6] reveals 
that the substitution of Cu by Co yields in an increase of the volume fraction of the 
interdendritic phases and coarsening of β-Ti dendrites. 
According to EDX point analysis (Table 1), and elemental distribution mapping (Fig. 3), β-Ti is a 
solid solution mainly consisting of Ti, Nb, and Al whereas Ni and Co are segregated to the 
interdendritic regions. The dendritic morphology and the particular composition of β-Ti indicate 
that it is the primary crystallized phase. Due to limited solubility of Co and Ni in β-Ti, they are 
driven out during solidification from the solidifying core of the β-Ti dendrites into the 
interdendritic melt. The melt solidifies with a fine eutectic structure. 
3.2. Compressive deformation and microhardness 
3.2.1. Compressive properties 
The composite structure of the alloys studied yields in significantly higher strength (Fig. 4) 
compared to that of the as-cast commercial Ti-based alloys [16,18]. For instance, yield strength 
of widely used Ti-6Al-4V alloy is only about 765 MPa in the as-cast state [16]. The maximum 
compressive strengths of alloy A reaches 1620 ± 50 MPa. Unexpectedly, the stress-strain curve 
of alloy A “bends” twice, indicating “double yielding” at 1300 ± 50 MPa and 1560 ± 50 MPa (Fig. 
4). This untypical for DNUS Ti-based alloys behavior is discussed below. The fracture strain of 
alloy A is relatively high and achieves a value of 19 ± 2 % (Table 2). The compressive yield stress 
of alloy B (1040 ± 50 MPa) is slightly lower compared with alloy A. But due to a significant 
strain-hardening the maximum compressive stress of this alloy reaches 1480 ± 50 MPa. Alloy B 
exhibits a good compressive deformability at room temperature and fractures at 45 ± 5 %.

3.2.2. Microhardness 
To analyze the effect of microstructure on mechanical properties, microhardness mapping (Figs. 
5) was performed. Due to the fact that the indentation print size is close to the size of the 
microstructural features, the measured values of hardness are always an average between two 
metallographic constituents (dendrites and matrix). According to the microhardness mapping 
there is a pronounced difference between the microhardness of the matrix and the dendritic 
regions for both alloys. In alloy A, the microhardness of the ultrafine eutectic regions (580 
HV0.01) is almost two times higher than that of the dendritic regions (365 HV0.01). Thus, the 
strength of the matrix in alloy A (using the relation σ ≈ HV/3, where σ = the yield strength and 
HV = Vickers hardness) is about ~60% higher than the strength of the dendrites. So it can be 
assumed that the “double yielding” in alloy A is due to the large difference in strength between 
the matrix and the dendrites. In other words, the first “bending” of the stress-strain curve may 
correspond to the onset of plastic deformation of the soft β-Ti, and the second “bending” to 
that of the matrix. To check this assumption, the compressive tests of alloy A were carried out 
in the SEM (see below). 
For alloy B the difference of microhardness values corresponding to the matrix regions (420 
HV0.01) and the dendritic regions (340 HV0.01) is not as pronounced as for alloy A. This is due 
to the decreased volume fraction of the interdendritic phases and, therefore, the increased 
contribution of the dendritic microhardness to an average value of microhardness. In contrast 
to alloy A, the compressive yield stress of alloy B is mainly determined by the dominant 
dendritic phase due to a relative low volume fraction of the eutectic network. Therefore, the

compressive stress-strain curve of alloy B exhibits only one “bending” corresponding to the 
onset of plastic deformation of β-Ti. 
3.2.3. In-situ compressive deformation of alloy A in SEM 
The microstructure studies of alloy A in the SEM during compressive deformation reveal the 
following: at the onset of yielding the dendritic β-Ti phase starts to deform by slip whereas the 
matrix remains unfractured (Figs. 6 a, b). On progress of deformation only a few cracks are 
observed within the matrix before the second deviation of the stress-strain curve (Figs. 6 a, b). 
The intensive strain hardening between the first and the second “bendings” of the stress-strain 
curve is due to hardening of the dendritic β-Ti phase. The second deviation of the stress-strain 
curve originates from the onset of the inelastic deformation of the matrix, which is harder than 
the dendritic phase. The matrix fractures in a brittle manner accompanied by numerous crack 
formations (Figs. 6 c, d). However, the cracks are blunted and arrested by the tough dendritic 
phase (Figs. 6 c, d). The observed compressive deformation behavior is consistent with that 
assumed above. 
3.3. Tensile deformation 
Although the alloys A and B are ductile under compressive loading, they exhibit a lack of tensile 
plasticity (Table 3). Moreover, samples of alloy A fail before yielding (Fig. 7). The tensile fracture 
strength of the alloys studied is significantly lower than their compressive fracture stress. As 
shown previously for Ti-Nb-Cu-Ni-Al [6] and Ti-Nb-Al-Ni(-Co) [14] alloys, the brittle matrix may 
be responsible for the tensile brittleness. Nevertheless, to prove this, a detailed analysis of the 
microstructure of fractured samples is required.

The SEM analysis of samples of alloy A tested in tension reveals numerous cracks at the 
polished side surface (Figs. 8 a, b). The cracks mostly originate from defects resulting from 
sample preparation and propagate through the matrix (Fig. 8 a). They are effectively blunted 
and stopped by the β-Ti phase already at the interphase between matrix and dendrites (Fig. 8 
b). This indicates a good toughness of β-Ti. Unfortunately, stress concentrations at a blunted 
crack tip promote the formation of a new crack (Fig. 8 b). The new crack deflects from the 
dendritic/matrix interface and further propagates though the matrix. Therefore, the early 
fracture of alloy A is due to crack nucleation and propagation instabilities in the ultrafine matrix 
typical for ultrafine structured metals [19,20]. The two key factors promote these instabilities: 
(i) the low resistivity to crack growth in the matrix (Figs. 8 a, b) and (ii) the continuous 
distribution of the matrix where cracks freely propagate (Fig. 2 a). 
The fracture surface of alloy A is perpendicular to the loading axes. This indicates the 
dominance of cleavage stress under shear stress in tensile loading. The fracture surface has 
very fine features, i.e. fine cleavage facets and some cavities/protrusions (Fig. 8 c, d). The fine 
cleavage features are related to the brittle fracture of the matrix. The round shaped 
cavities/protrusions on the fracture surface were formed by the crack circumventing the 
dendrites (Figs. 8 b, d). 
In contrast to alloy A, alloy B exhibit a minor plastic deformation of about 0.5 % in tensile 
loading. The SEM analysis of tensile tested samples of alloy B reveals numerous cracks at the 
eutectic network (Fig. 9 b). The crack growth and propagation instabilities in these regions are 
responsible for the catastrophic failure of alloy B. However, there is over 1 % difference in 
fracture strain between the alloys. It is assumed that the reason for that is the particular

10 
 
distribution and volume fraction of the interdendritic phases influencing crack propagation. This 
is proven by the profile of fracture surface in the alloys (Figs. 9 a, 10). There is the higher 
amplitude of the profile in alloy B in comparison to alloy A. This is due to a weaker 
connectedness of the matrix network in alloy B (which the crack strives to follow). Therefore, 
the crack in alloy B deviates much stronger from the cleavage stress direction and has a longer 
path compared to that in alloy A. Furthermore, some eutectic regions of alloy B are 
disconnected. Therefore, the path of the crack goes through the tough β-Ti dendrites (Figs. 9 c, 
d). This also promotes the plasticity of alloy B. 
4. Conclusions 
Two as-cast bi-modal alloys, Ti 66Nb13Ni8Co6.8Al6.2 (A) and Ti 71.8Nb14.1Ni4Co7.4Al6.7 (B), were 
developed based on the Ti-Nb-Ni-Cu-Al system. The alloys consist of two phases, i.e. β-Ti and 
Ti2Ni. The β-Ti phase has a dendritic morphology and its volume fraction is 60 ± 5 vol.% for alloy 
A and 91 ± 2 vol.% for alloy B. The remaining volume fraction is adapted for the interdendritic 
matrix consisting of Ti2Ni and β-Ti. 
The bi-modal microstructure has a high strength (e.g. 1620 ± 50 MPa for alloy A) compared with 
the as-cast commercial Ti-based alloys. Unexpectedly, the compressive stress-strain curve of 
alloy A “bends” twice (“double yieldings”). This is due to a large difference in hardness between 
the matrix (580 HV0.01) and the dendritic phases (365 HV0.01). The first “bending” of the 
stress-strain curve (1300 ± 50 MPa) corresponds to the onset of plastic deformation of the soft 
β-Ti phase, while the second “bending” (1560 ± 50 MPa) matches the beginning of the matrix 
fracture. Between the two “bendings” of the stress-strain curve, the β-Ti phase undergoes a 
significant strain hardening.

11 
 
In contrast to pronounced compressive deformability (45 ± 5 % of strain for alloy B), the alloys 
are brittle in tensile loading. The tensile brittleness is associated with the crack nucleation and 
propagation instabilities in the ultrafine matrix. The two key factors promoting these: (i) the low 
resistivity to crack growth in the matrix and (ii) the continuous distribution of the matrix where 
cracks freely propagate. It was found that weaker connectedness of the matrix network 
promotes higher deviation of the crack from the cleavage stress direction and, therefore, a 
better plasticity. Thus, tensile ductility of the bi-modal Ti-based alloys can be improved by 
adjustment of the matrix network, in particular, by tuning of volume fraction of the ultrafine 
structured matrix phases and size of the coarse dendrites.
