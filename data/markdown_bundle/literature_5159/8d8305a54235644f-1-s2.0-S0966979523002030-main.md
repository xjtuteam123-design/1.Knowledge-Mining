1. Introduction 
High Entropy Alloys (HEAs) were discovered independently by Yeh 
et al. [ 1 ] and Cantor et al. [ 2 ] in 2004, and were described as “ multiple 
principal elements ” and “ equiatomic multicomponent alloys ” , respec -
tively. The basic principle behind HEAs is to have high mixing entropy 
( Δ S
mix
), to promote the stabilization of solid solution (SS) phases, and 
inhibit the formation of intermetallic compounds (ICs) [ 3 ]. A common 
accepted definition is that HEAs are a class of new materials composed 
of five or more metallic elements in equimolar or near-equimolar ratios. 
In general, HEAs are mainly composed of middle and late transition 
metals, with Fe, Ni, Co and Cr being the most common [ 4 , 5 ]. The field 
has evolved rapidly into less restricted definitions, bringing new alloys 
with promising properties [ 6 ]. Among them, the most relevant alloys are 
complex concentrated alloys (CCAs) [ 7 ], medium entropy alloys (MEAs) 
[ 8 ], high entropy steels [ 9 ], high entropy aluminum alloys (HEAls) [ 10 ], 
etc. One of the most promising alloys derived from HEAs are the 
so-called HEAls. The definition was recently coined by Asadikiya et al. to 
develop a novel Al alloy category [ 11 ]. It was proposed that although 
the entropy is not as high as in HEAs, HEAls can still take advantage of it 
to achieve enhanced properties [ 12 ]. 
The formation of ICs in HEAls is inevitable due to the high negative 
enthalpy of mixing ( Δ H
mix
) between Al and most of the commonly 
employed alloying elements [ 4 ]. Therefore, HEAls generally exhibit 
multiphase microstructures composed of α -Al SS phase reinforced with 
different types of ICs. Mundhra et al. proposed this novel approach as 
robust design direction to create high-performance Al alloys strength -
ened with multicomponent ICs [ 13 ]. The phase prediction of HEAls is 
mainly performed by two approaches, computational CALculation of 
PHAse Diagram (CALPHAD) and empirical thermodynamic parameters. 
CALPHAD has been extensively used as an efficient tool to accelerate the 
design and development of multicomponent concentrated alloy systems 
[ 14 ]. The other approach to design HEAls is based on the Hume-Rothery 
rules to predict the formation of a SS. Several authors have developed 
empirical thermodynamic parameters as guidelines for phase formation 
in HEAs [ 15 ]. Yang et al. proposed more restricted values for 
multi-principal component alloys containing light-weight elements 
[ 16 ].

2. Experimental 
The software Thermo-Calc (v. 2023a, Thermo-Calc Software AB, 
Stockholm, Sweden) [ 24 ] in conjunction with the TCAL8 thermody -
namic database was used for calculations of the equilibrium phases as a 
function of temperature. The Scheil-Gulliver model was employed to 
calculate the non-equilibrium solidification conditions. 
The experimental alloys were melted in an electric resistance furnace 
model Heerbo 72 (Morgan Molten Metal Systems, Berkatal, Germany) in 
a SiC crucible. Ingots of commercial AlSi10MnMg (Rheinfelden Alloys, 
Rheinfelden, Germany) and recycled AlSi9Cu3(Fe) (Tecnalia, Irun, 
Spain) alloys were employed as base alloy to guarantee a melting bath 
where the other elements were dissolved. To achieve the target 
compositions, tablets of Al – Cu (Bostlan, Mungia, Spain) containing 75 
wt % of Cu, and commercially pure Si (Ilarduya, Amorebieta, Spain) 
were used. Finally, Mg and Zn addition was made using recycling scrap 
returns of AM60B magnesium alloy (Tecnalia, Irun, Spain) [ 25 ] and 
Zamak 5 (Tecnalia, Irun, Spain), respectively. The elemental composi -
tions of the alloys were analyzed using optical emission spectrometry 
(OES) model SPECTROMAXx (Spectro, Kleve, Alemania). Table 1 shows 
the overall bulk composition of the input alloys measured by OES. 
Approximately 80 kg were obtained for each alloy. After degassing 
by injecting pure Ar, the melt was cast using a cold-chamber high 
pressure die casting (HPDC) unit model PT 1000 (Pretransa, Tarragona, 
Spain). The pouring and die temperatures were controlled at 740

Cylindrical samples for compression testing were machined from the

at RT and 200

microhardness FM-700 model (FUTURE-TECH, Kawasaki, Japan) was 
employed on the polished sample surface using a 0.1 kg load, applied for 
10 s. At least 10 random individual measurements were made. The wear 
tests were carried out in accordance with the ASTM G 99-95a standard 
[ 26 ], using a tribometer model MT Series (Microtest, Madrid, Spain). 
The counter face material was chosen as tungsten carbide (WC) balls 
(Goodfellow, Coraopolis, USA). All tribological tests were carried out on 
the polished samples at RT, without lubricant and under normal atmo -
spheric conditions. The testing parameters were a load of 10 N, radius of 
3 mm, sliding velocity of 100 rpm and total distance of 120 m for each 
test. The wear tests were repeated at least three times for each alloy with 
a new WC ball. The coefficient of friction (COF) was continuously 
recorded throughout the duration of the test. The representative curve of 
the COF versus sliding distance was plotted, and the average value of the 
COF was calculated for each alloy. The topography and the wear profiles 
of the samples were measured using a 3D optical surface metrology 
system model DCM 3D (Leica, L ’ Hospitalet de Llobregat, Spain) in 
conjunction with the Leica Map DCM 3D software. Finally, the mor -
phologies of the worn surfaces were studied by SEM.
