ABSTRACT
This study investigates the deformation behavior of extruded WE43 magnesium alloy under a strain rate of 
1400 s
 1 
at both room temperature and 400
◦
C. At room temperature, the alloy exhibits a high yield strength 
(358 MPa) but is accompanied by significant brittleness, with limited crack propagation and insufficient local 
plastic deformation. Under these conditions, deformation is primarily achieved through {10 12} tensile twinning 
and basal slip, while the rare earth phases induce stress concentration by pinning dislocations, thereby further 
enhancing the material ’ s strength. In contrast, at 400
◦
C the peak stress of the alloy decreases to 182 MPa. 
Although the strength is reduced, the overall plasticity is significantly improved due to grain refinement and the 
occurrence of dynamic recrystallization (DRX). At the elevated temperature, the deformation mechanism shifts 
from basal slip to non-basal slip, dynamic recovery, and DRX, processes that promote the formation of new grains 
and grain boundary migration. Moreover, the dynamic precipitation of rare earth phases at high temperature 
enhances grain boundary stability, contributing to improved plasticity and shear resistance. This study em -
phasizes the important role of rare earth phases in facilitating DRX and maintaining grain boundary stability, 
while also revealing the key contributions of twinning and grain boundary migration in enhancing high- 
temperature deformation performance. These findings not only deepen the understanding of the micro -
structure – property relationships in WE43 magnesium alloy but also provide a theoretical basis for its application 
in high-temperature, high-strain-rate environments.
1. Introduction
Due to the low density, high specific strength, and excellent damping 
properties, magnesium(Mg) alloys, have become an ideal material for 
lightweight design in aerospace, automotive, and defense industries 
[1 – 5] . However, the poor plasticity of Mg alloys, especially under 
ambient temperature conditions, limits their widespread application in 
structural components [6 – 9] . To improve the mechanical properties of 
Mg alloys, rare earth (RE) elements are often added to the alloys. RE 
elements not only refine grains and weaken texture but also enhance the 
high-temperature strength and plasticity of Mg alloys through the for -
mation of stable strengthening phases [10 – 17] . Li et al. [14] demon -
strated that alloying with RE elements is an effective approach to 
enhancing the mechanical properties of Mg alloys. The segregation of 
yttrium (Y) atoms at grain boundaries significantly improves both the 
yield strength and grain boundary stability of Mg alloys.Chen et al. [15]
investigated the mechanisms of grain refinement and strengthening ef -
fects of Y in Mg alloys. They derived the precipitation sequence of 
critical rare-earth strengthening phases and observed that the newly 
formed strengthening phases improved the mechanical properties of the 
alloys at both room and elevated temperatures. Mirzadeh [16] found 
that the addition of lanthanide rare earth elements, such as gadolinium 
(Gd) and Y, has a significant effect on the thermal strength of magne -
sium alloys, thereby enhancing the material ’ s creep resistance. Pour -
bahari et al. [17] found that the coexistence of Al and Gd in magnesium 
alloys helps to increase the solidus temperature, thereby further 
enhancing the thermal stability of the magnesium alloys.
Under dynamic loading conditions, the deformation mechanisms of 
Mg alloys are more complex compared to static conditions, involving 
multiple mechanisms such as twinning, dislocation slip, dynamic re -
covery, and dynamic recrystallization (DRX) [18 – 21] . Wei et al. [22]
investigated the deformation mechanism of fine crystal Mg-5Y alloy at

provide new insights into the role of RE phase in enhancing DRX and 
grain boundary stability at high temperatures. The stratified activation

effective theoretical framework for understanding the high temperature

3. Results and discussion
3.1. High strain rate mechanical performance
To investigate the effect of temperature on the high speed impact 
properties of extruded WE43 Mg alloy, Fig. 2 shows the true stress-strain 
curves of the alloy at RT and 400
◦
C at high strain rate of 1400 s
 1
. At 
RT, the peak true stress of the alloy is approximately 358 MPa, indi -
cating high yield strength and resistance to deformation. The true stress- 
strain curve can be divided into two distinct stages: the first stage where 
stress rapidly increases with strain, and the second stage where stress 
decreases after reaching its peak. The curve rises sharply during the 
early stages of plastic deformation, suggesting that the alloy has a higher 
elastic modulus, with deformation primarily occurring through dislo -
cation slip. However, after reaching the peak true stress, the curve drops 
sharply, reflecting the alloy ’ s limited plastic deformation capability at 
RT and exhibiting distinct brittle characteristics. This is mainly due to 
the difficulty in activating non-basal slip systems at RT [34] , which 
limits the deformation mechanisms, with basal slip and twinning 
becoming the dominant mechanisms. At RT, the twinning mechanism 
takes on part of the plastic deformation task, limiting the alloy ’ s 
ductility, which also restricts its use in applications requiring high 
impact resistance.
When the deformation temperature increases to 400
◦
C, the peak true 
stress of the alloy decreases significantly to 182 MPa, indicating that 
high temperatures reduce the yield strength of the material. 400 ℃ The 
stress-strain curve can be divided into three stages: the first stage where 
stress increases rapidly with strain; the second stage where the curve 
enters a steady-state phase after reaching peak stress, characterized by a 
relatively flat region; and the third stage where stress declines. The 
curve at 400
◦
C exhibits a "C" shape, showing a more gradual decline, 
which reflects the alloy ’ s higher plastic deformation capability at 
elevated temperatures. High temperatures promote the activation of 
non-basal slip systems, enhancing the alloy ’ s shear resistance and 
effectively alleviating stress concentrations. The dynamic softening 
mechanisms induced by high-speed impact dominate the deformation 
process, and a dynamic balance between work hardening and softening 
is formed through DRX [34] . At 400
◦
C, the alloy ’ s ductility improves 
significantly, and although the strength decreases, its plastic deforma -
tion capacity is significantly enhanced.
The evolution of mechanical behavior at high temperatures reveals 
the thermally activated nature of the deformation mechanisms in Mg 
alloys: at low temperatures, the twinning-dominated deformation 
mechanism leads to high strength and low ductility, making the alloy 
suitable for high-strength and high-stiffness applications; at high tem -
peratures, the activation of multiple slip systems and the DRX process 
rebalances strength and ductility, giving the alloy better potential for 
hot forming applications.
3.2. Microstructure observation after high speed impact
To further understand the changes in the alloy ’ s high-speed impact 
performance, the microstructures of extruded WE43 Mg alloy at RT and 
400
◦
C under a strain rate of 1400 s
 1 
were analyzed ( Fig. 3 ). At RT, the 
alloy undergoes complete fracture, with the crack width remaining 
consistent overall. At lower deformation temperatures, the material ’ s 
thermal activation energy is insufficient to effectively overcome the 
energy barriers of non-basal slip systems, causing the deformation pro -
cess to primarily rely on basal slip, which is a limited energy dissipation 
path. Additionally, due to insufficient energy accumulation, the for -
mation of twins is restricted, and both slip and twinning, the main modes 
of plastic deformation, are suppressed. As a result, the material ’ s plastic 
deformation capacity is weakened, leading to rapid fracture and failure 
of the sample.
In contrast to the RT condition, at 400
◦
C, the alloy does not undergo 
complete fracture, and the crack width is narrower, indicating that high 
temperatures enhance the material ’ s plastic deformation capability. No 
clear adiabatic shear band (ASB) formation is observed at 400
◦
C and the 
sample shows a tendency toward complete fracture. Furthermore, the 
second phases with poor thermal stability in the alloy may precipitate, 
which, along with the other factors, contributes to the reduced plasticity 
of the material. Overall, while the material ’ s plasticity improves at 
higher temperatures, the combination of grain refinement and precipi -
tate formation leads to a decrease in plasticity at excessively high 
temperatures.

In summary, under shear loading at RT and a strain rate of 1400 s ⁻ ¹ , 
the microstructure of the WE43 alloy is dominated by severely deformed 
grains and a high-density dislocation substructure. The primary defor -
mation mechanism is dislocation slip, accompanied by a small amount 
of {10 12} tensile twinning, with minimal DRX. The observed strain 
hardening behavior in the stress – strain response is closely related to the 
high dislocation density, limited twin activity, and insufficient recrys -
tallization in this region.

4. Conclusions
In this study, high-speed impact experiments were conducted on 
extruded WE43 magnesium alloy using a SHPB device at a strain rate of 
1400 s
 1 
under different temperature conditions. The microstructure of 
the deformed specimens was characterized, and the mechanical prop -
erties, adiabatic shear sensitivity, and deformation mechanisms at 
different temperatures were analyzed. The results indicate the 
following:
(1) As the temperature increases, the true stress-strain curve gradu -
ally transitions from an "S" shape to a "C" shape, and the peak 
stress decreases with increasing temperature. In the temperature 
range of room temperature to 100
◦
C, the alloy exhibits high 
strength and low plasticity, whereas from 200
◦
C to 400
◦
C, the 
plasticity of the alloy gradually improves while the strength 
decreases.
(2) At room temperature, the deformation mechanisms of the 
extruded alloy are {10 12} tensile twinning and basal slip. Dis -
locations accumulate and are hindered at twin boundaries and 
secondary phases, where pinning effects enhance strength but 
reduce ductility. The interaction between dislocations and SFs 
induces local stress concentrations, promoting the formation of 
new twins.
(3) At 400
◦
C, non-basal slip becomes the dominant deformation 
mechanism in the extruded alloy, working in coordination with 
twinning to accommodate deformation. The high temperature 
facilitates the activation of non-basal slip, promoting dynamic 
twin expansion and the dynamic precipitation of rare-earth 
phases. Both CDRX and DDRX contribute to the formation of 
recrystallized grains, enhancing the deformation capability of the 
alloy.
