Abstract
High cycle fatigue (HCF) behavior of Ni superalloy 617M is investigated at
973 K and R-ratio /C0 1 on a resonance-based fatigue testing system at 85 Hz fre-
quency. The alloy experiences an abrupt drop in fatigue life within a narrow
domain of 2.5 –5 MPa near its fatigue strength at 320 MPa. Electron micros-
copy and diffraction techniques were employed to thoroughly analyze the
nominal fatigue damage. The characterization revealed the significance of pre-
cipitation of secondary phases M
23C6, Ti (C, N), and γ0 phase in dictating the
HCF strength of the alloy. Cyclic loading at high temperature causes γ-matrix
hardening and secondary phase precipitation synergistically strengthening the
material beyond its yield strength. Conjunctively, dynamic strain aging was
also seen to play a major role in the evolution of fatigue damage. The work
highlights the collective contribution of γ0-phase precipitation, carbides, and
dynamic strain aging and their influence on the HCF behavior of alloy 617M.
KEYWORDS
high cycle fatigue, microstructural characteristics, superalloy 617M, γ0-phase precipitation
Highlights
• Response of alloy 617M under high cycle fatigue load is studied at high
temperature.
• Near fatigue limit, sharp drop in fatigue life causes nominal microstructural
damage.
• Precipitation of phases γ0 and carbides in matrix, DSA, and cyclic hardening
strengthens alloy.
• Precipitates at the grain and twin boundaries weakened the fatigue resis-
tance of alloy.

2,3 However, the

plants require careful consideration owing to their pro-
longed exposure to harsh service conditions like corrosive
gases, high fluctuating loads, and extreme temperature
and pressure. Many projects and missions have been
undertaken worldwide for the selection of such materials,
and nickel-based superalloys are now popularly accepted
as strong contenders for such demanding applications.
3
Inconel 617 (UNS N06617) is a nickel –chromium–
cobalt–molybdenum-based superalloy which was particu-
larly developed for gas turbines and petrochemical plant
components. Eventually, it was also approved to be used
in European USC boiler and pressure vessel applica-
tions.
3 Superalloy 617 is considered a principal choice for
the tubular sections of intermediate heat exchangers
(IHX) in very high-temperature gas-cooled reactors
(VHTR) which are subjected to creep and fatigue loads
during their design life.
4,5 Alloy 617M, investigated in the
present study, is a variant of the Inconel 617 with reduced
variation in the compositional ranges of the alloying ele-
ments.
6 This alloy primarily achieves its strength by solid
solution strengthening with elements like Cr, Co, Mo,
and Al. Apart from being oxidation and hot-corrosion
resistant, the alloy also exhibits exceptional high cycle
fatigue (HCF) strength at high temperatures owing to the
precipitation of ordered γ
0 phase of Ni 3(Al, Ti) intermetal-
lic in the γ matrix and Cr- and Mo-rich carbides of type
M23C6/M6C.3 Besides, the matrix strengthening associ-
ated with the occurrence of dynamic strain aging (DSA)
in the alloy also contributes to the strengthening of the
alloy under cyclic loading at high temperatures causing
an increase in the fatigue strength.
7,8
In a previous study, 9 the authors reported that the
alloy can sustain a fatigue life of 10 7 cycles at 320 MPa at
973 K. However, a marginal increase of the alternating
stress (σa) to 322.5 MPa causes the material to fail around
105 cycles. Thus, a mere 1% increase in the stress ampli-
tude causes a drastic reduction in the cyclic life. The
observation of such a sharp drop in fatigue life suggests
the existence of a threshold stress limit of 320 MPa for
the strength provided by DSA to the material under the
cyclic loading at 973 K. Load exerted beyond this limit
seems to somewhat neutralize the strengthening
imparted by various factors like DSA, annealing twins,
precipitation hardening, and dislocation rearrangement.
This aspect is further substantiated in this study by per-
forming tests running beyond 10
7 cycles. It is shown that
the fatigue life extends to 10 8 cycles at this value of cyclic
stress and the S –N plot approaches an asymptotic form.
However, this does not imply the existence of a “fatigue
limit” for the material. It has been observed in many
studies10,11 conducted in the very high cycle fatigue
(VHCF) regime (cycles > 10 7) that loading any material
for enough cycles shall ultimately cause its failure while
the shape of the fatigue curve depends upon the material.
In the previous studies 12,13 on superalloy 617, empha-
sis has been given to the precipitation of γ0 phase of
Ni3(Al, Ti) and M 23C6 (M = Cr, Mo) carbides since their
presence is vastly responsible for the improved perfor-
mance and stability of the material at high temperature.
Tytko et al.
14 determined, both experimentally and com-
putationally, that the major phases present in the alloy
after a 100-h annealing period would be M 23C6, Ti (C, N),
γ, and γ0. These observations were also corroborated by
other researchers.15,16
Numerous studies have tried to explore the associa-
tion of low cycle fatigue loading 17–24 with the microstruc-
tural attributes of this alloy in past. However, only a few
investigators have attempted to study the behavior under
pure HCF loading.
7–9,25,26 Karnati et al. 7 studied the per-
formance of alloy 617M at different R-ratios at 973 K
under HCF and reported a significant increment in the
fatigue strength for the life contours of 10 5,1 0 6, and
107 cycles. The authors attributed this superior perfor-
mance under HCF to the DSA occurring in this regime.
Sarkar et al. 8 elucidated the behavior of the alloy 617M
under pure HCF loading within the temperature range of
300 to 1023 K. The study revealed that the extensive
hardening of the matrix at elevated temperatures is
because of the precipitation of M
23C6 carbides as well as
the dislocation rearrangement associated with the DSA.
Recently, Liang et al. 27 employed the EBSD technique to
explore the localized granular deformation near the crack
tip while studying the fatigue crack growth and propaga-
tion behavior in Inconel 617 under the action of corrosive
media.
Considering all the aforementioned viewpoints, the
objective of this paper is to comprehensively examine
the superalloy microstructure to understand its character-
istics near the HCF threshold and to analyze the extent
of deformation under the DSA dominant regime. For this
purpose, uniaxial HCF tests were performed on superal-
loy 617M at constant amplitude near its fatigue strength
at 973 K. Characterization techniques like SEM/EDS,
EBSD, and XRD were employed to analyze various fea-
tures of the microstructure before and after the loading.
Hence, this investigation attempts to provide an insight
into the operative deformation and damage extent in the

2.3 | Characterization
The metallographic examination of representative speci-
mens was carried out under a scanning electron micro-
scope (SEM) (Jeol JSM-6380A) equipped with an electron
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector (Bruker
XFlash 6-30) setup. The samples for the metallographic
study were cut from the cross-section of the specimen
with the minimum gauge radius. These samples were
then mechanically ground successively on the SiC papers
with a grit size of 600, 800, 1000, 1200, 1500, and 2000,
followed by final cloth polishing with 5- μm alumina col-
loidal solution. The samples were subsequently etched
using Kalling's reagent (5-g CuCl
2, 100-mL HCl, and
100-mL CH 3OH). For the electron backscattered diffrac-
tion (EBSD) investigation, unetched samples were elec-
tropolished in a solution of methanol and perchloric acid
(7:1) for 18 s and operated at 16 V and 258 K. The EBSD
scans were performed on SEM (FEI Quanta 3D) fitted
with an EBSD detector (EDAX velocity) on an area of
500 /C2 500 μm and a step size of 0.8 μm. TSL-OIM soft-
ware was used to analyze the EBSD scans. X-ray dif-
fraction (XRD) was also performed using Cu-K α
radiation, with λ = 1.54060 Å, on samples of uniform
shape and size (Rigaku SmartLab-SE). The scanning
range was 20 –100
/C14 , the scanning speed was 2 /C14 /min, and
the step size was 0.02 /C14 . The diffractometer had Bragg –
Brentano optical geometry (B-B). Subsequent analysis of
XRD data was conducted on Highscore Plus, Seasolve
PeakFit, and OriginPro softwares. Vickers microhardness
was determined on an automatic hardness testing system
(HM-200, Mitutoyo) at 0.5 kgf load. All the metallo-
graphic studies conducted on tested specimens were also
performed on the alloy sample in an as-received state
(AR) which refers to its condition after the solution
annealing and prior to the cyclic loading.

3.2 | Fractured surface morphology
The fractured surfaces of the failed specimens were
ultrasonically cleansed in methanol for 2 min prior to
their examination under SEM. The SEM images of the
fractured surface of specimen S3 which sustained
93,525 cycles at σ
a = 325 MPa are illustrated in
Figure 3A–F. Figure 3A depicts four regions on the frac-
ture surface demarcated by the black dotted lines. These
regions represent four stages of fatigue crack propagation
contrary to the three stages usually observed in a fatigue
fracture. Red arrows mark the direction of crack propaga-
tion. Macroscopically, these four stages were identifiable
by virtue of their distinct color shades. Stage I (marked
“1”), shown in Figure
3B, is the crack initiation zone
which is devoid of striation marks and is characterized
by a smooth facet-like surface signifying a slower rate of
crack initiation such that striations are not formed. The
interface of stages I and II of crack propagation is also
shown in the figure. The region marked as 2 in the figure
represents stage II which is the transition stage between
stages I and III characterized by features like cleavage
steps, river patterns (Figure
3B), a few secondary cracks,
and fatigue striations (Figure 3C). The formation of this
intermediate stage is the consequence of the DSA taking
place in the material which delays the crack growth.
Cleavage steps signify the sudden “burst” of strain accu-
mulated because of dislocation locking –unlocking phe-
nomenon of DSA. Similar features were also observed in
an earlier study on the material.
7 Figure 3D shows stage
III of stable propagation of fatigue crack. This stage is
dominantly characterized by striations typical of a fatigue
fracture. The gap between successive striations was found
to be increasing in the direction of crack propagation
indicating an increased rate of crack growth. In
Figure
3E, stage IV, that is, the final stage, is represented
where catastrophic fracture takes place and the zone is
mostly filled with equiaxed dimples formed due to micro-
void coalescence.
Some pockets filled with clusters of carbides were
found in stage II, III, and IV regions (shown in
Figure 3C–E). These carbides had globular morphology
and were identified to be chromium- and molybdenum-
rich carbides. This can be confirmed through the EDS
elemental concentration analysis of one such isolated car-
bide located in stage III of crack propagation, as shown
in Figure
3F. The number of carbides and their sizes was
found to be greater in stage IV compared with stages II
and III. The appearance of such carbides seems to be sim-
ilar to those observed by Sander et al.
30 and Shiozawa
et al.31 The occurrence of these carbide clusters may thus
be attributed to the dissociation of the carbides, which
precipitated within the matrix.
Similar observations were made in the fracture sur-
face of specimen S1, cycled at σ
a of 330 MPa and frac-
tured after 39,422 cycles, as shown in Figure 4A–D.
However, because of a higher value of alternating stress,
the fracture surface was found to be irregular in compari-
son with that of S3. Figure
4A gives a complete view of
the fracture surface with stages I –IV being separated by
black dotted lines. Red arrows point radially outward
indicating the direction of fatigue crack propagation.
Multiple crack initiation sites were noticed in stage I as
indicated in Figure
4B. Figure 4C shows a secondary
crack arrest at an annealing twin boundary in transition
stage II. In Figure
4D, the stage III region of crack propa-
gation comprises typical fatigue striations along with an
intergranular secondary crack. Globular carbide clusters
were also found to be present in small pockets in stages
II, III, and IV of the crack growth in S1. Stage IV of speci-
men S1 covers a larger area as compared with stage IV of
specimen S3 which indicates that the applied cyclic stress
was lower in the latter case.

4 | DISCUSSION
4.1 | Microscale variations in the alloy
post-fatigue loading
It is known that a large fraction of cyclic loading is spent
in the crack initiation rather than the crack propagation
stage under HCF. 40 All the tests conducted in this work
were carried out in the regime of DSA dominance. It has
been shown in an earlier study that the diffusion of Cr
atoms in the lattice toward the mobile dislocation might
be the probable cause for the occurrence of DSA in this
alloy at the tested temperature range.
41 In the presence of
DSA, the stable propagation of the crack is further
delayed. The initiation of the crack results in the genera-
tion of new dislocations and an increase in dislocation
density, hindering the dislocation motion in the immedi-
ate vicinity of the crack tip. The dislocation mobility is
further obstructed by the diffusing solute particles pin-
ning down the dislocations. This leads to the formation
of a transient zone between stages I and II of fracture.
The Cr atoms have been shown to possess sufficient dif-
fusivity in the Ni matrix in the temperature range of 873 –
1223 K.
12 This might also be the possible explanation for
the secondary precipitation of Cr-rich carbides in the
tested samples.
The coherent boundaries of the annealing twins act
as a hindrance to the dislocation motion and the propa-
gation of secondary cracks as shown in an SEM fracto-
graph of the fractured specimen S1 (Figure
4C). As
stated, no significant coarsening of the carbides was
observed which is in line with the observation made by
Xiang et al.
32 who reported no appreciable carbide coars-
ening at temperatures lower than 973 K below 50 h.
However, the presence of very fine carbides in the granu-
lar region, as well as grain boundaries, suggests possible
secondary precipitation of carbides during cycling. A sim-
ilar observation was made in a previous study as well,
12
where the authors reported that the dissolution of pri-
mary carbides and reprecipitation of fresh carbides take
place above 873 K. The increment in stress led to the
cracking of carbides in the failed specimen S1, which is
visible in the SEM images (Figure
6E,F). The precipita-
tion of carbides is known to strengthen the grain bound-
aries against grain boundary sliding thereby imparting
creep resistance to Ni-based superalloys. However, the

5 | CONCLUSIONS
The microstructural evolution of alloy 617M under the
action of HCF loading at R = /C0 1 and 973 K near its
fatigue strength was studied in this article. From the
above discussion, it can be inferred that the analysis of
deformation in such loading conditions would be a com-
plex matter comprising many competing factors and the
alloy won't incur significant damage despite exhibiting a
major variation in the fatigue life. However, from the
characterization performed in this work, the following
general conclusions can be drawn:
1. A marginal increase of the stress amplitude by 5 MPa
beyond the fatigue strength of 320 MPa caused a dras-
tic reduction in the cyclic life of the alloy. The fracture
surfaces of the failed specimens revealed four distinct
regions signifying the impedance in the crack growth
aided by cyclic work hardening, precipitation harden-
ing, and dynamic strain aging. Beyond 320 MPa, the
cyclic load overcomes this strengthening, causing
failure.
2. The carbide precipitates in the microstructure didn't
coarsen under the action of cyclic loading and thermal
exposure at 973 K. However, the presence of cracked
carbides and grain boundary de-bonding in the failed
specimen would provide an easy path for crack propa-
gation. Secondary cracks were found in the failed
specimen, while annealing twins were seen to be
arresting the microcracks. Secondary precipitation of
Cr–Mo-enriched carbides at grain boundaries
occurred in the specimens.
3. The hardness of the cyclically loaded specimens
increases with the number of cycles sustained. This
was attributed to the combined influence of γ
0-phase
precipitation and cyclic work hardening. The results
were substantiated by the γ0-phase XRD peak separa-
tion. The XRD results also hint toward distortion of
the microstructure of the failed specimen and the
accumulation of plastic strain in the material with
higher cycling. Also, a minor coherency strain was
found to be present in the alloy matrix.
4. EBSD results indicate the absence of any substructure
formation. However, the results showed the signature
of damage that occurred in the failed specimen. Anni-
hilation of LAGB and hardening of the matrix were
observed which would add to the strength of the alloy
against HCF loading.
