High strength-ductility synergy in refractory multi-principal
element alloys via special deformation mechanisms
and dislocation behaviors
Zhi-Wen Li, Bao-Xian Su, Liang Wang*, Chen Liu*, Zhe Li, Qing-Da Zhang,
Bin-Bin Wang, Xiang Xue, Rui-Run Chen, Yan-Qing Su
Received: 10 February 2024 / Revised: 1 April 2024 / Accepted: 14 April 2024 / Published online: 24 July 2024
/C211Youke Publishing Co., Ltd. 2024
Abstract Ti-Zr–Nb refractory multi-principal element
alloys (RMPEAs) have attracted increased attention due to
their excellent mechanical properties. In this study,
(TiZr)
80-xNb20Mox (x = 0, 5 and 10) alloys were designed,
and the intrinsic conﬂicts between strength and ductility
were overcome via composition optimization and recrys-
tallization. The causes of the superior strength-ductility
synergy were investigated in terms of their deformation
mechanism and dislocation behavior. The results show that
the strength improvement can be attributed to the defor-
mation mechanism transition caused by local chemical
ﬂuctuations and lattice distortion. Speciﬁcally, the slip
band widths decrease after Mo addition, and the measured
slip traces in the fracture samples are associated with high-
order {112} and {123} slip planes. Furthermore, the grain
reﬁnement achieved via recrystallization promotes multi-
slip system activation and shortens the slip-band spacing,
which reduces the stress concentration and inhibits crack
source formation, thereby allowing the alloy to ensure
sufﬁcient ductility. Consequently, the Ti
35Zr35Nb20Mo10
alloy annealed at 900 /C176C exhibits high yield strength and
elongation. These ﬁndings provide a new strategy for
designing high-strength RMPEAs and addressing room-
temperature brittleness.
Keywords Refractory multi-principal element alloy;
Mechanical property; Strengthening mechanism;
Deformation substructure; Dislocation behavior
1 Introduction
Refractory multi-principal element alloys (RMPEAs), also
known as refractory high- and medium-entropy alloys
(RHEAs/RMEAs), have attracted increased attention over
the past decade because of their unique alloy design
strategies and superior mechanical properties [ 1–4]. Some
RMPEAs exhibit high-temperature properties that are
similar, and even superior, to those of current Ni-based
superalloys [3, 4]. Therefore, RMPEAs are attractive can-
didates for use at extremely high temperatures, such as in
aerospace applications [ 5]. However, most RMPEAs
exhibit brittle fractures or limited ductility under tensile
stress at room temperature, which restricts their practical
application [6–8].
Extensive research has been devoted to investigating the
intrinsic brittleness of RMPEAs, and several strategies
have been proposed to overcoming it, including the valence
electron concentration (VEC) ductility criterion [ 9, 10],
metastability engineering [ 11–13] and heterogeneous
structural strengthening [ 14, 15]. Recently, a new strategy
for achieving a superior strength-ductility synergy was

spinodal structures limits their high-temperature applica-

strength and ductility of MPEAs using the VEC ductility

In general, the most ductile RMPEAs consist of six
elements in Groups IV (Ti, Zr and Hf) and V (V, Nb and
Ta). To date, there are few reports on the effects of Group
VI (Cr, Mo and W) element addition on the mechanical
properties of RMPEAs, as the addition of these elements
with a high VEC value makes it difﬁcult for the alloys to
satisfy the ductility criterion of VEC B 4.5, and in theory,
signiﬁcantly reduces their ductility. However, adding a
certain Mo concentration can signiﬁcantly enhance the
tensile strength of an alloy with good ductility [20–22]. For
instance, Huang et al. [ 22] investigated the mechanical
properties of Al
5Mo5Nb36Hf13Ti41 and Al 5Mo7Nb36Hf13
Ti39 alloys and found that the latter exhibited higher room-
temperature strength and superior high-temperature soft-
ening resistance. Therefore, the ﬁrst objective of our study
was to systematically investigate the effect of Mo on alloy
properties and deformation mechanisms. In addition, sev-
eral studies have shown that ductility can be signiﬁcantly
improved by rolling and annealing [ 23–27]. In this regard,
Xu et al. [23] reported that a simple hot-rolling process can
effectively reﬁne the microstructure of TiVNbTaSi
0.1 alloy
and induce a high nano-silicide density, resulting in
unprecedented yield strength ( * 1250 MPa) and tensile
elongation ( * 8%) properties. In addition, Jia et al. [ 27]
found that multi-stage strain-hardening behavior can be
introduced in (Ti 44V28Zr14Nb14)98.5Mo1.5 alloy via recrys-
tallization, which promotes dislocation interactions,
including cross-slip and multiplication, and ultimately
increases the tensile elongation compared to as-cast alloys.
However, although the effect of recrystallization on the
mechanical properties has been demonstrated in these
previous studies, little attention has been paid to the duc-
tility enhancement mechanisms that contribute to reﬁne-
ment after recrystallization.
In this study, novel (TiZr)
80-xNb20Mox RMPEAs were
designed, and the effects of Mo addition and recrystal-
lization on their mechanical properties and deformation
mechanism were investigated. On this basis, the effects of
dislocation behavior and deformation on the strengthening
mechanisms were explored. It was found that local chem-
ical ﬂuctuations and grain reﬁnement altered the disloca-
tion morphology and facilitated the activation of multi-slip
systems, which play an important role in determining
plastic deformation. The ﬁndings of this study have sig-
niﬁcant implications for the design of RMPEAs with high
strength and ductility.
2 Experimental
2.1 Materials and preparation
The (TiZr)
80-xNb20Mox (x = 0, 5 and 10, hereinafter referred
to as Mo0, Mo5 and Mo10, respectively) ingots were pre-
pared via non-consumable arc melting under the protection of
high-purity argon, using high purity (C 99.9 wt%) metals. To
promote chemical homogeneity, Mo and Nb were ﬁrst melted
to form an intermediate metal; then, Ti and Zr were added,
and each ingot was remelted at least ﬁve times. These ingots
were ﬁrst cut into cubes with a size of 40 mm 9 30 mm 9
11 mm via electrical discharge machining (EDM) and then
cold-rolled to a thickness reduced to 65% of the original via
multi-pass rolling (each pass reduction was 0.2 mm). The
ﬁnal plate samples were initially sealed in vacuumed
(10
-2 Pa quartz tubes), annealed at 800, 900 and 1000/C176Cf o r
1 h, and ﬁnally quenched in water. The cold-rolled and
annealed samples are denoted as Mo10-CR, Mo10-800,
Mo10-900 and Mo10-1000, respectively. A schematic illus-
tration of the heat treatment process and a visualization of the
specimen extraction position are shown in Fig. S1.
2.2 T ensile test
Uniaxial tensile tests were carried out in an Instron 5569
materials testing machine at a strain rate of 1 9 10
-3 s-1.
Dog-bone-shaped tensile specimens with gauge dimensions of
15 mm 9 2m m 9 1.5 mm were cut from the plates along
the rolling direction (RD) via EDM, as illustrated in Fig. S1b.
The 0.2% offset yield method was used to determine the yield
strength of the specimens. At least ﬁve tests were conducted for
each alloy group to ensure data reproducibility.

3.3 Cold-rolled and annealed Mo10 alloy
microstructure
Figure 4 shows XRD patterns and EBSD images of the
Mo10 alloy after cold rolling and heat treatment. XRD
patterns reveal no signiﬁcant phase transformation of the
Mo10 alloy during cold deformation and subsequent
annealing. Note that changes in the diffraction peak
intensity, such as the (110) peak, imply grain reorientation;
that is, recrystallization occurred in the alloy. Figure 4b
shows EBSD band contrast and crystallite orientation maps
of the cold-rolled alloy. The pole ﬁgure (PF) maps shown
in Fig. S3 demonstrate that the main crystallographic ori-
entation of the cold-rolled Mo10 alloy is a typical
{111} \1/C2210[ texture. Figure 4c–e shows the microstruc-
tural evolution of the annealed alloys. The degree of
recrystallization increases with increasing annealing tem-
perature until complete recrystallization at 900 /C176C. As
shown in Fig. 4c, the Mo10-800 alloy undergoes partial
recrystallization at the grain boundaries, which is attributed
to high-dislocation-density accumulation; that is, it has a
higher drive for recrystallization. Figure 4d2 and e2 shows
the grain size distribution of the Mo10-900 and Mo10-1000
alloys. The average grain sizes of the Mo10-900 and
Mo10-1000 alloys ﬁtted by Gauss (Normal) distributions
are (22.08 ± 0.80) and (45.00 ± 1.77) lm, respectively,
i.e., the recrystallized grain grows signiﬁcantly with
increasing annealing temperature.
Figure 5 shows TEM images of the Mo10-900 and
Mo10-1000 alloys. BFTEM images and corresponding
SAED patterns in Fig. 5a-d demonstrate that the alloys
retain a single-phase BCC structure after recrystallization.
Figure 5e shows HRTEM image of the Mo10-900 alloy.
From IFFT image, the (1 /C2210) plane spacing is determined to
be 0.236 nm. The elemental distribution maps at the grain
boundaries show that all elements are uniformly dis-
tributed, which indicates that the annealing process elimi-
nates segregation at the as-cast alloy grain boundaries.

Figure 8 shows the detailed dislocation conﬁguration
within the tensile fracture specimens of the Mo0, Mo10 and
Mo10-900 alloys. In all alloys, the plastic deformation
mechanism is dominated by dislocation slip, and no phase
transformation or deformation twins are observed. In the
Mo0 alloy, as shown in Fig. 8a and b, the Taylor lattices
are formed by the coplanar slip of different slip bands,
where the slip lines in different directions intersect at *
80/C176along the [001] axis. Figure 8b shows the high-density
dislocation walls (HDDWs) and planar slip bands. The slip
band thickness is 200–300 nm, and the spacing between
each slip band is 0.8–1.2 lm. In addition, it is difﬁcult for
dislocations to pass through the slip band owing to the
interaction between dislocations, resulting in a typical
dislocation plug at the boundary (red arrow in Fig. 8b).
Similar slip bands, dislocation plugs, and HDDWs are
observed in the Mo10 alloy. Corresponding HRTEM image
(Fig. 8d) shows planar slip bands with widths of
100–150 nm and slip-band spacings of 300–500 nm. It is
worth noting that the interactions between multiple slip
systems are more intense in the Mo10 alloy than in the
Mo0 alloy.
For the Mo10-900 alloy, we analyzed the dislocation
behavior in both the homogeneous deformation and frac-
ture regions. In the homogeneous deformation stage, the
slip systems in favorable orientations, that is, those with
large Schmidt factor, are preferentially activated, resulting
in a planar-slip band array, as shown in Fig. 8e and f. The
distance between slip bands is * 200 nm. Figure 8g
shows a Taylor lattice consisting of a dense planar dislo-
cation band array. The angle between the intersecting slip
bands is 80 /C176along the [111] axis, which is consistent with
that shown in Fig. 8b. Further magniﬁcation of Fig. 8h
shows a variety of dislocation structures, including dislo-
cation loops, tangles and dipoles, caused by the cross-slip
of screw dislocations. In the Mo10-900 alloy, the slip width
and spacing are approximately 100 and 160 nm, respec-
tively. The slip band width is signiﬁcantly reduced after
Mo addition. Furthermore, compared with the as-cast
Mo10 alloy, the slip band spacing in the Mo10-900 alloy is
signiﬁcantly reduced, indicating that grain reﬁnement
promotes the dislocation interaction and improves the

solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening, grain-
reﬁnement strengthening, and dislocation strengthening,
can achieve a high YS. Based on the microstructural

Generally, the dislocations observed in BCC crystals
deformed at room temperature are predominantly long
straight-screw dislocations with a {110} slip plane. How-
ever, substantial short and curved non-screw dislocations
were unexpected in our study. The dislocation lengths
decreased from 270 nm in Mo0 to 120 nm in Mo10
(Fig. 8). Thus, it is reasonable to assume that the disloca-
tion could have a varying core structure along its line due
to local chemical ﬂuctuations in the BCC RHEAs. Put
simply, long straight dislocations are at an energetic dis-
advantage owing to lattice distortion effects. Consequently,
the dislocation motion is subject to strong nanoscale seg-
ment pinning and forms a more intense interaction with
other slip systems. These dislocation behaviors reveal the
compositional sensitivities of RHEAs and the origin of the
strong solid-solution strengthening.
The slip plane also plays a vital role in the deformation.
In conventional BCC crystals, both {110} and {112} are
typical slip planes, with {112} generally being activated
during low-temperature deformation. In addition, the
{123} crystal surface acts as a slip plane at high temper-
ature and low deformation rates. The effect of temperature
on crystal spacing and stacking fault energy is mainly
responsible for this difference. However, in this study, the
measured slip traces in the room-temperature-deformed
samples are linked to high-order {112} and {123} slip
planes. Clearly, the selection of an extensive slip system
over the crystal indicates that the Peierls dislocation
stresses on various planes, such as {110}, {112} and
{123}, may be at the same level. This result contributes to
understanding the weak temperature dependence of

4.3 Effect of grain reﬁnement on ductility
In terms of ductility, grain reﬁnement increases the
microstructural homogeneity and shortens the dislocation
plug-up group length, which signiﬁcantly improves hetero-
geneous deformation. Conversely, the decrease in the strain-
hardening rate resulting from grain-reﬁnement strengthening
is considered detrimental to homogeneous deformation.
Overall, the total elongation of the alloy increases slightly or
changes slightly with grain reﬁnement. However, our study
shows that the fracture elongation of the fully recrystallized
Mo10-900 alloy is * 128% higher than that of the as-cast
Mo10 alloy (Figs. 3, 6), which is connected to strain char-
acteristics and deformation mechanisms.
Based on the engineering stress–strain curves, the alloys
have no prominent yield platform, and necking occurs
immediately after plastic deformation. This mechanical
behavior is similar to that of gum metals, a speciﬁc class of
beta-titanium alloys with high strength and ductility [ 56].
During tension, favorably oriented dislocations within the
grain are ﬁrst activated, and the leading dislocations slip
through to form slip channels that are more conducive to
the passage of subsequent dislocations, resulting in stress
reduction. However, intact crystals require a higher
resolved shear stress to form new slip channels, and dis-
location movements are conﬁned to narrow slip bands
(Fig. 8)[ 57]. As the plastic deformation progresses, dis-
location pluggs the channel and causes stress concentra-
tion. If the shear stress fails to activate new slip channels,
further deformation leads to microcrack formation and
even fracture.
In this study, grain reﬁnement in the recrystallized
alloys promoted large elongation due to elastic and plastic
strain mismatches originating from the grain boundaries. In
addition, owing to the elastic strain mismatch effect, multi-
slip systems are easily activated at the grain boundaries,
just as the Mo10-900 alloy (Fig. 8d) activated more slip
systems than the Mo10 alloy (Fig. 8b). The activated sec-
ondary slip system interacts with the primary slip system to
promote dislocation cross-slip, multiplication and storage,
inducing a strain-hardening mechanism and contributing to
homogeneous deformation. Furthermore, grain reﬁnement
and multi-slip system activation reduce the slip-band
spacing and shorten the dislocation plug-up length, low-
ering the stress concentration within the grains and
obstructing the formation of crack sources. Moreover,
owing to the plastic strain mismatch effect, dislocation
sources are easily activated at the grain boundaries and
emit dislocations into neighboring grains, improving the
homogeneous deformation capacity and increasing the
elongation. A schematic diagram of the plastic deformation
mechanism is shown in Fig. 10.
