ABSTRACT
FeCoCrNiMn high-entropy alloys (HEAs) exhibit excellent ductility, particularly at cryogenic temperatures. 
However, their yield strength is relatively low. In this study, an inexpensive element, phosphorus (P), was added 
to HEAs at a high concentration (0.4 %). The results indicate that adding P not only enhances both strength and 
ductility, but also avoids embrittlement. The P-added HEAs were single-phase random solid solutions, with P 
distributed inside the grains and partially segregated at the grain boundaries. Within the grains, regions rich in P 
and poor in P were observed, along with significant tensile and compressive strain fields in the P-rich regions. 
These strain fields may lead to large local internal stresses. The presence of P prevents brittleness due to its 
specific atomic distribution, whether in coarse or fine grains. The yield strength increased from 185.27 MPa in P- 
free homogenized HEAs to 296.1 MPa in P-added HEAs, representing an increase of approximately 60 % at 298 
K. At 77 K, the strengths further increased, irrespective of grain size. Further, P added reduced the stacking fault 
energy. At room temperature (298 K), P-free HEAs formed dislocation cells and high-density dislocation wall 
structures, while P-added HEAs formed additional microbands, deformation twins, and stacking faults due to 
increased lattice frictional stress. P-added HEAs demonstrated excellent mechanical properties at cryogenic 
temperatures, with good strength-ductility synergy and strain-hardening capacity. These enhancements can be 
attributed to the synergistic effects of nano deformation twins, hierarchical nano-spaced stacking fault networks, 
and Lomer-Cottrell locks, as well as their extensive interactions.
1. Introduction
In modern engineering applications, especially under extreme ser -
vice conditions in the aerospace, shipbuilding, and energy industries, 
the design of advanced metal structural materials requires not only the 
co-satisfaction of strength-ductility synergy, manufacturability, and 
cost-effectiveness, but also excellent mechanical properties at both 
ambient and cryogenic temperatures. Conventional alloy designs typi -
cally address these challenges individually. For instance, the high cost of 
high-strength titanium alloys [ 1 ], poor manufacturability of 
ultrahigh-strength iron-based alloys [ 2 – 4 ], and limited applicability of 
traditional steels at cryogenic temperatures [ 5 ] have not yet been 
addressed.
In recent years, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) have attracted consid -
erable attention [ 6 – 9 ]. In HEA systems, compositional combinations can 
be selected according to the required properties (strength-plasticity 
combinations, wear resistance, corrosion resistance, and 
high-temperature resistance), significantly expanding the range of alloy 
designs. A notable example is the FeCoCrNiMn HEA with a single-phase 
face-centered cubic (fcc) structure [ 8 ], which has been extensively 
studied because of its unique mechanical properties at cryogenic tem -
peratures [ 10 – 15 ]. However, the yield strength of the as-cast

FeCoCrNiMn HEA is less than 300 MPa [ 16 ], seriously hindering its 
practical application range. Therefore, the strengthening of FeCoCr -
NiMn HEAs and the realization of strength-ductility synergy remains a 
primary research focus. Strengthening methods have been widely re -
ported [ 17 – 23 ]. These strengthening methods can be grouped into four 
categories: solid solution strengthening, precipitation strengthening 
[ 24 ], grain boundary strengthening, and heterogeneous strengthening 
[ 25 ]. Small-radius atoms, such as B, C, and N, are usually added to HEAs 
as interstitial solid solutions [ 17 – 19 ]. However, these elements often 
have a negative mixing enthalpy with the alloying elements, forming a 
brittle second phase, resulting in ductility loss. At the carbon content of 
0.8 %, the yield strength of ~1030 MPa and ultimate tensile strength of 
~1170 MPa can be achieved, however, the uniform elongation only is 
~11 % [ 26 ]. In addition, the addition of refractory metals (such as Nb, 
Mo, and Ta) can significantly enhance the yield strength [ 20 – 22 ]. 
However, poor plasticity and difficult processing are often obtained 
owing to the precipitation of brittle Laves phases. However, the strong 
lattice distortion caused by the addition of V results in a significant in -
crease in the yield strength and only a small decrease in the plasticity 
[ 27 ]. In recent years, heterogeneous structures have been proposed to 
overcome the strength – ductility trade-off and have achieved remarkable 
results [ 28 ]. For example, bimodal grain-sized structures, gradient 
structures, and multimodal structures have achieved strength-ductility 
synergy by virtue of hetero-deformation-induced (HDl) strengthening 
and work hardening [ 29 ].
Element P is inexpensive element, and has a beneficial effect on 
improving the strength, corrosion resistance [ 30 ]. It is an inevitable 
element in some special steels [ 31 , 32 ], such as free-cutting steel, at -
mosphere corrosion resistant steel. However, the addition of P in steels 
cause significant embrittlement, especially at cryogenic temperatures 
[ 33 ]. The embrittlement mechanism of elemental P in steels was 
investigated using first principles, and the embrittlement/strengthening 
effects of substituted P and interstitial P were found to originate from 
mechanical and chemical contributions, respectively [ 34 ]. Although a 
small amount of P can achieve strengthening, the strong lattice distor -
tion of P tends to dominate the embrittlement-inducing mechanical 
contribution, which gradually increases with the P content. Conse -
quently, the P content in conventional steels is typically maintained 
within 0.045 wt%. It has been found that the substitution of B and C for 
some of the interstitial P atoms can mitigate P-induced embrittlement, 
which mainly stems from the fact that interstitial B and C mitigate strong 
lattice distortions as well as their strong chemical interactions with Fe at 
grain boundaries [ 34 – 36 ]. Therefore, P has traditionally been recog -
nized as a harmful impurity in steels and excess P can degrade the me -
chanical properties of the alloy [ 37 ]. However, P is not always 
detrimental to the traditional alloys. In Ni-based superalloys, P has been 
reported to have a positive effect on the stress fracture life and no sig -
nificant deleterious effect on other mechanical properties, and the 
average segregation concentration of P at grain boundaries can be as 
high as 1.1 % [ 38 ]. It can be inferred that the favorable effect of P on 
Ni-based superalloys is mainly related to grain boundary segregation. A 
possible reason for this is that Ni-based superalloys are complex systems 
containing several elements, and the interactions (chemical contribu -
tions) between these elements may alter their grain boundary segrega -
tion, thereby alleviating embrittlement. It has also been found that P has 
strong chemical interactions with Mo or Cr at the grain boundaries [ 39 ]. 
Impurity-defect complexes and chemical interactions between atoms 
may lead to different electronic configurations, and thus, abnormal 
bonding states at the grain boundaries [ 40 ]. Therefore, the anomalous 
effects of P at the GBs were most likely due to the specific interactions of 
the P-GB-M (metal element) complexes. However, there is no convincing 
explanation of the nature and mechanism of this phenomenon. In 
addition, P improves the strength and plasticity of Ni-based superalloys 
at high temperatures [ 40 ], which may be determined by the grain 
boundary segregation entropy [ 41 ]. Segregation entropy determines the 
transition from substitutional segregation to interstitial segregation as 
the temperature increases [ 42 ], and the interstitial segregation of P 
improves strength and ductility. Presently, these phenomena have not 
been clearly elucidated.
As a non-metal atom, P has a larger atomic radius than B, C, and N, 
causing greater lattice distortion, and P addition HEAs have not yet been 
studied. Therefore, the effect of adding P to HEAs with complex com -
positions must be investigated. In this study, we selected CoCrFeMnNi 
HEA as the research alloy and added P at a concentration of 0.4 at.%, 
which is significantly higher than the amount of P in conventional steel, 
to study its tensile and impact properties at room and cryogenic tem -
peratures. Remarkably, even at high levels, P segregated at the grain 
boundaries does not cause embrittlement, and instead achieves a better 
strength-ductility combination. The distribution of P at the grain 
boundaries and within the grain interiors was characterized using 
aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy. Our 
study provides a reference for the addition of P to HEAs.

fields were observed in the rich-P region, potentially leading to large 
local internal stresses, and consequently resistance to dislocation 
sliding.

3.3. Fracture morphology and microstructure after tensile deformation
Representative fracture surfaces of the two HEAs with different grain 
sizes at 298 and 77 K are shown in Fig. 8 . A large number of micro-sized 
dimples were observed on the fracture surfaces of all the HEAs, which 
further confirmed their excellent ductility. At 77 K, no traces of inter -

the coarse or fine grains.

accumulation of dislocations in planar slip bands, which correspond to 
alloys with low stacking fault energy (SFE). In addition, it has been 
proposed that the formation of microbands is responsible for the 
excellent ductility of conventional steels [ 57 ] and HEAs [ 58 ]. The 
appearance of these deformed structures further demonstrated the low

4. Discussion
4.1. Effect of phosphorus on yield strength
Generally, the yield strength of an alloy depends on the complexity of 
the dislocation slip activated at the initial deformation stage, which is 
mainly attributed to factors hindering dislocation slip, such as solid 
solution elements, twins, dislocations, stacking faults, and precipitation, 
as well as their density, distribution, and size [ 19 , 68 , 69 ]. In this study, 
we mainly focused on homogenized HEAs. As shown in Fig. 6 , the 
addition of P led to a significant increase in yield strength. For homog -
enized HEAs, owing to their large grain size (~300 μ m), the absence of 
dislocations and precipitation phases, grain boundary strengthening, 
dislocation strengthening, and precipitation strengthening were 
neglected. Therefore, the strengthening of homogenized HEAs mainly 
originates from lattice friction stress and solid solution strengthening. To 
better understand the strengthening mechanism of HEAs, 
microstructure-related models can be employed to evaluate their yield 
strength. The homogenized P0 and P2 HEAs, which can be explained

4.3. Effect of P addition on deformation mechanism

deformation mechanisms. The TEM micrographs of the P0 HEAs fracture

wave-slip dominates. In P2 HEAs, the presence of dislocation cells, 
microbands, deformation twins, and laminar dislocations indicated that 
dislocation slip dominated in the early stages of deformation, whereas 
twins and stacking faults dominated at high strains. Usually, large fric -

in the SFE and an increase in the lattice frictional stress were observed in 
the P-containing HEAs. Thus, the planar slip in the P-added HEAs can be 
attributed to their lower SFE and increased lattice frictional stress. The 
lattice frictional stress of the HEAs increased dramatically after the

to accumulate dislocations. This not only effectively hinders the slip of 
dislocations, but also serves as a Frank-Read source for emitting dislo -

L-C locks connect a high density of immobile SFs, which facilitates the 
construction of the SFs network and its own stabilization, making them 
highly structurally stable and resistant to dissociation [ 61 , 74 ]. Conse -

the accumulation of dislocations, which significantly enhanced the 
strain-hardening capacity. In summary, the presence and interaction of 
these deformation features are the main reasons for the increased 
strength of the alloy while maintaining its excellent ductility.

5. Conclusions
In this study, the room and cryogenic-temperature mechanical 
properties of FeCoCrNiMn HEAs with and without 0.4 wt% P-addition 
were investigated. TEM images of the dislocation substructures after 
tensile fracture revealed the effect of P addition on the deformation 
mechanisms. The main conclusions are as follows. 
(1) The Phase structures of homogenized P0 alloy and P2 alloy were 
single fcc phase with no detectable second phase. Additionally, 
the XRD patterns of the homogenized HEAs indicated that the 
addition of P significantly increased the lattice constant of the 
alloy. The amount of lattice distortion increased from 3.5 × 10
3 
for the P0 alloy to 22.8 × 10
3 
for the P2 alloy.
(2) P was uniformly distributed inside the grains and partially 
segregated at the grain boundaries. Within the grains, P-rich and 
P-poor regions were observed, with significant tensile and 
compressive strain fields in the P-rich regions, potentially leading 
to large local internal stresses and resistance to dislocation 
sliding. P atoms formed a mixed solid solution of interstitial and 
substitutional groups at the grain boundaries and in nearby 
atomic layers. P-doped FeCoCrNiMn HEAs did not exhibit brit -
tleness, regardless of whether the grains were coarse or fine.
(3) The P-added HEAs exhibited excellent comprehensive mechani -
cal properties without evident brittleness at 298 and 77 K. The 
yield strength increased from 185.27 MPa in P0 alloys to 296.1 
MPa in P2 alloys, representing a nearly 60 % increase ratio at 298 
K. At 77 K, the yield and tensile strengths of the P2 alloys further 
increased, regardless of grain size. The impact toughness of P2 
alloys was slightly higher than that of P0 alloys at both 298 and 
77 K, and they did not exhibit significant brittleness at 77 K 
compared to conventional steels.
(4) The addition of P atoms decreased the stacking fault energy. At 
high strains (298 K), the P-free HEAs formed dislocation cells and 
high-density dislocation wall structures, while the P-added HEAs 
formed additional MBs, DTs, and SFs due to increased lattice 
frictional stress.
(5) P-added HEAs demonstrated excellent cryogenic mechanical 
properties with good strength-ductility synergy and strain- 
hardening capacity. These enhancements can be attributed to 
the synergistic effects of nano deformation twins, hierarchical 
nano-spaced SF networks, and L-C locks, as well as their extensive 
interactions.
