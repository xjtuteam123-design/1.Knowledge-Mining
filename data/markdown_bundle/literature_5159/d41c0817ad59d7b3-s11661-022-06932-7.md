ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Effect of Cr and Ni/Fe Ratio on the Microstructure
and Mechanical Properties of c¢-Strengthened
Ni–Fe-Based Alloys
ZHUO LIU and SAMMY TIN
To continue to meet the future materials’ requirements for advanced power generation systems,
enhancing the mechanical properties and long-term phase stability of Ni–Fe-based alloys is
needed. In this study, alloying modiﬁcations were made to improve the phase stability and
0.2 pct yield strength of modiﬁed cast austenitic stainless steels used for high-temperature heat
exchanger applications. The focus was to investigate the inﬂuence of Cr concentration and the
ratio of Ni/Fe in the alloy system on the elevated temperature yield strength, ductility, and its
matrix phase stability. Over the range of experimental alloy compositions investigated, the best
balance of mechanical properties in tension at 750 /C176 C was achieved through a modest reduction
in Cr as this alloy possessed a yield strength of 630 MPa and 28 pct elongation. The r phase
volume fraction was also limited compared with other alloys. The modiﬁcations in Cr
concentration and Ni/Fe ratio to the alloy system are independent with the c¢ precipitate solvus
temperature and volume fraction formed during aging. However, decreasing Cr concentration
from 21 at to 18 at.pct was observed to increase the yield strength and decrease the fraction of r
phase precipitation. Reductions in the Ni/Fe ratio led to a reduction of the tensile ductility as
this tended to promote the formation of discontinuous precipitation along the grain boundary.

I. INTRODUCTION
EMISSIONS requirements for advanced gas turbines
used for power generation require operation at increas-
ingly high temperatures and require structural alloys to
operate in corrosive environments where temperatures
can exceed 700 /C176 C. Conventional stainless steel alloys do
not possess the temperature capability and c¢-strength-
ened Ni-based superalloys are often considered to be too
costly for these applications.
[1–3] As a result, austenitic
Ni–Fe alloys are widely utilized as structural materials
for many of these application as it possesses an excellent
combination of properties such as high strength, creep
resistance, and corrosion resistance along with a rea-
sonable cost.
[1–4] This class of alloys generally contains
Ni, Cr, and Fe as primary alloying additions that form
an austenitic matrix and is usually strengthened with
minor alloying additions of C and N that contribute to
the formation of carbides, nitrides, or other
precipitates.
[4–6] Compared with conventional Ni-based
superalloys, the composition of Ni–Fe alloys contains
higher concentrations of Fe and lower amounts of Ni to
reduce the overall cost of the alloy. In Ni-based
superalloys, a large fraction of the overall strength is
provided by precipitate strengthening.
[7,8] The inter-
metallic c¢ (L12,N i 3(Al,Ti)) phase is the most common
precipitate utilized for strengthening of Ni-based super-
alloys due to its ability to form coherent and thermo-
dynamically stable precipitates at elevated temperature
that are resistant to dislocation shearing.
[7,9–12] In recent
years, precipitate strengthened Ni–Fe-based superalloys
containing over 10 wt pct Fe to reduce cost and
maintain suﬃcient mechanical properties at elevated
temperature have been studied,
[13–15] and new alloys
with even higher Fe concentrations are currently being
developed.[16–19] The development of new Ni–Fe alloys
strengthened by c¢ precipitates may lead to signiﬁcant
improvements in the performance of advanced power
generation systems while simultaneously reducing mate-
rial costs.

These alloys possess good high-temperature stability

[21–23] As these

to engineer the composition such that c¢ precipitates
form within the microstructure. However, there are
challenges associated with high-temperature phase sta-

Therefore, the impact of the alloying additions on the
long-term phase stability also needs to be understood.
The development of c¢ precipitation-strengthened
Ni–Fe alloys requires an improved fundamental under-
standing and assessment of the long-term phase stabil-
ity. Elemental Fe and Cr both possess BCC crystal
structures and have limited solubility in the c¢ phase.
During precipitation of the c¢ phase, these elements
partition preferentially to the c matrix. Additions of Ni
stabilize the austenitic FCC matrix, but the concentra-
tion of Ni in the matrix decreases when the Ni
3Al, c¢
precipitates form within the microstructure. This grad-
ual depletion of Ni from the matrix when forming c¢
precipitates may result in the matrix instability if the
alloy composition is not suﬃciently balanced. In addi-
tion, additional metallic and intermetallic phases can
also form within this class of alloys. From the Ni–Fe–Cr
phase diagram, for example at 750 /C176 C,
[24] the c matrix
has a solubility of 92 pct of Fe but only 36 pct of Cr,
indicating that the Cr-rich precipitates might form along
with the c¢ precipitates in the alloy system and corre-
spondingly inﬂuence the mechanical properties of the
alloy. r phase (tP30, CrFe),
[25,26] a-Cr (cI2, Cr), [27,28] Cr
carbides[4,6] can all form during extended exposures to
elevated temperatures in these alloys to reduce the
supersaturation of Cr in the matrix. The impact of these
precipitates on the mechanical properties will vary
depending on their crystal structure, morphology in
the microstructure, and volume fraction.
[9] Dispersed Cr
carbides with small sizes are beneﬁcial, [29,30] while r
phase,[31–33] and a-Cr[28] exert a detrimental eﬀect on
mechanical properties. In addition, discontinuous pre-
cipitation (DP) has also been reported to occur in this
class of Ni-based superalloys and inﬂuence thermome-
chanical properties. The DP or cellular precipitate
reaction usually results in the formation of a two-phase
lamellar structure that migrates into the supersaturated
matrix.
[34,35] The driving force for the phase
transformation is the overall reduction in the localized
chemical concentration gradient. [36] DP on the grain
boundary can grow advancing into the matrix or
migrate along the grain boundary, depending on the
diﬀusion rate on its path. In general, DP transforma-
tions are undesirable
[37–39] because of the detriment in
the mechanical properties as small precipitates from the
matrix are dissolved and coarse precipitates are formed
in the cell.
[34] However, some studies [40] indicate that the
DP increases the strength or hardness. In Ni–Fe-based
alloys and stainless steel, the c¢,
[40,41] M23C6,[38,41]
a-Cr,[37] or topologically close-packed (TCP) [42,43] phase
can all form discontinuous precipitates along with the c
matrix during heat treatment
The main focus of this paper was to investigate
whether alloy compositions close to the Centralloy class
of alloys could be modiﬁed to incorporate c¢ precipita-
tion strengthening that would provide enhanced
strength at intermediate temperatures while maintaining
the other desirable intrinsic properties. To avoid the
formation of non-equilibrium phases due to solidiﬁca-
tion segregation, a homogenized and wrought version of
the alloy studied here to get a fundamental understand-
ing of the ability of these alloys to precipitate the c¢
phase. In addition, as c¢ precipitates form, the level of Ni
in the supersaturated matrix decreases and this would
likely induce the driving force for the precipitation of
Cr-rich precipitates. To reduce the Cr-rich precipitates,
two methods were considered: reduction in the overall
Cr concentration in the alloy and increasing the Ni
concentration. In this research, the purpose of analyzing
the Ni/Fe ratio follows the general concept of decreasing
the overall raw material cost of the alloy. There are also
some published studies that reported good mechanical
properties of their alloys with a Ni/Fe ratio about 1.3.
[44]
Increasing the concentration of Fe would also con-
tribute to destabilizing the ability of the alloy to
precipitate c¢. Therefore, the focus of this study was to
see how the Cr concentrations inﬂuence the microstruc-
ture and properties in c¢ precipitates Ni–Fe alloy with
lower Ni/Fe ratio.
II. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE
A. Alloy Preparation and Heat Treatments
Four experimental Ni–Fe-based alloys were fabri-
cated with diﬀerent Ni/Fe and Cr concentrations by
alloying the original 25Cr35Ni alloy with high-purity
Ni, Fe, Al, and Ti-alloying additions in an MRF
ABJ-900 (MRF Inc., Suncook, NH) arc melter under
a gettered Ar atmosphere. The addition of elemental
solutes to the 25Cr35Ni master alloy ingots maintains
nominally similar levels interstitials and other alloying
elements in the alloy. The compositions of these
experimental alloys are listed in Table I. To ensure
chemical homogeneity and complete dissolution of the
elemental additions, the ingots were melted, ﬂipped, and
remelted a minimum of ﬁve times. Then the ingots were
then encapsulated in quartz tubes backﬁlled with Ar gas.
Samples designated A1, A2, A3, and A4 were

homogenized at 1200 /C176 C for 40 hours, following with air
cooling to ambient temperature. Then the specimens
were encapsulated within a stainless steel can prior to
hot pack rolling. After soaking at 1050 /C176 C for 1 hour,
the specimens were subjected to a multi-pass hot-rolling

B. Characterization and Analysis Method
A SETARAM SETSYS diﬀerential scanning
calorimeter (DSC) was utilized to measure the solvus
temperature of various phases in these alloys, providing
suﬃcient information for heat treatment windows.
Samples with an approximate mass on the order of
~ 150 mg were sectioned from the as-cast and as-aged
specimens. The DSC experiment was conducted under a
ﬂowing Ar atmosphere using a sapphire reference. The
samples were heated to 500 /C176 Ca t2 5 /C176 C/min and held at
the temperature for 10 mins prior to heating to 1400 /C176 C
at 10 /C176 C/min. The samples were then soaked at 1450 /C176 C
for 10 min and cooled to room temperature at 20 /C176 C/
min. The solvus temperatures of the c¢ precipitates were
determined using the slope intercept method from the
endset point in the heating curve.
Metallographic samples for as-cast, as-homogenized,
and as-aged condition were prepared for observation
using JEOL JSM 5900-LV scanning electron microscope
(SEM) and JEOL JSM 6701-F (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo,
Japan) ﬁeld-emission scanning electron microscope
(FE-SEM). Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) using
an Oxford Instruments X-Max system was utilized to
measure the composition at an accelerating voltage of
15 kV. Etching was carried out to expose the morphol-
ogy for observation to clearly exhibit the precipitates.
To quantify the c¢ planar area fraction, an etchant
consisting of 33 pct Acetic acid+33 pct Nitric
acid+33 pctH
2O was used to selectively dissolve the c¢
precipitates. To selectively reveal the c¢ and r phase
precipitates, the specimens were ﬁrst electrolytic etched

neutralizing the specimen, electrolytic etching in 10 pct
phosphoric acid+90 pct H
2O at 3.0 V for 5 seconds
was used for selective removal of the surrounding c
matrix. Digital image analysis using ImageJ and the
‘‘Analyze particle’’ function was used to statistically
quantify the microstructural features.
The grain structure and local misorientation were
determined using an Oxford Instrument Nordlys-HKL
electron backscatter diﬀractometer (EBSD) attached to
the JEOL JSM 5900-LV SEM. A region of 300 /C2 280
lm
2 was mapped with a step size of 1 lm. Oxford
Instruments Channel software was used to analyze the
data and generate the misorientation map.
X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) scanning was done in
ThermoARL X-ray diﬀractometer with a Cu Ka (wave-
length 0.15406 nm) tube at an energy of 35 kV and
35 mA. The scanning parameters were 2theta from 30 to
100 deg with a step size of 0.02 deg and the dwell time
was 4 seconds. The scanning was applied on bulky
long-term-aged specimen.
Thermodynamic calculations were also performed
using Thermo-Calc with the TCNi5.0 database. Exper-
imental results were compared to the model predictions

The electrolytic phase extraction was utilized to
determine grain boundary precipitates and phases after
200 hours aging using 30mA/mm

mixture of 10 pct HCl and 1 pct tartaric acid in

powder was cleaned with ethanol to remove the solution

IV. DISCUSSION
Overall, the modiﬁed alloys A1, A2, A3, and A4 all
exhibited an FCC matrix + c¢ precipitates in their
grain interior and c–c¢ discontinuous precipitate zone +
G phase + r phase on their grain boundaries. The c¢
solvus temperature was relatively unaﬀected by the
alloying additions and was approximately ~ 940 /C176 C.
The addition of Al and Ti successfully promoted the
formation of c¢ precipitates and, hence, increases the
mechanical properties at elevated temperature. In spec-
imen A1, the r phase appears on the grain boundary
after 20 hours aging at 750 /C176 C and in the grain interior
after prolonging the aging time to 200 hours. Alloys A2,
A3, and A4 exhibited good matrix phase stability after
200 hours aging, as the Cr concentration in the alloy
system decreased. The modiﬁcation of Cr and the ratio
of Ni/Fe also inﬂuenced the yield strength and grain
boundary phase stability.

In summary, the composition of 25Cr35Ni austenitic

ratio inﬂuenced the tensile ductility by modifying the
tendency to form grain boundary discontinuous precip-

best combination of yield strength, 630 MPa, and tensile
ductility, 28 pct.
V. CONCLUSION
In the present study, the 0.2 pct yield strength
25Cr35Ni austenitic stainless steels at elevated temper-
ature was improved through alloying to induce precip-
itate strengthening. The phase stability of the modiﬁed
alloy systems was also investigated. The focus of the
investigation was to understand how the Cr concentra-
tion and fraction of Ni + Fe, expressed as the ratio of
Ni/Fe, can inﬂuence the yield strength and ductility at
elevated temperature and its matrix phase stability.
Based on the experimental results and model predic-
tions, the following conclusions can be reported:
(1) Over the range of experimental alloy composi-
tions investigated, the best balance of mechanical
properties in tension at 750 /C176 C was achieved in
alloy A2 as this alloy possessed a yield strength of
630 MPa and 28 pct elongation. The r phase
volume fraction was also limited compared with
other alloys.
(2) The modifications in Cr concentration and Ni/Fe
ratio to the alloy system are independent with the
c¢ precipitate solvus temperature and volume
fraction formed during aging. Based on the
results, formation of the G phase
(Ni
16(Ti,Nb)6Si7)a n d r phase reduces the degree
of strengthening in alloy A1 as it serves to deplete
Ti from the matrix and reduces the degree of c¢
formation. The presence of grain boundary c–c¢
discontinuous precipitates was found to signifi-
cantly decrease the ductility of alloy at elevated
temperature.
(3) In the modified alloy systems, decreasing Cr
concentration from 21 at to 18 at pct increases
the yield strength and decreases the fraction r
phase precipitation.
(4) The Ni/Fe ratio influences the tensile ductility by
modifying the formation of grain boundary dis-
continuous precipitates.
