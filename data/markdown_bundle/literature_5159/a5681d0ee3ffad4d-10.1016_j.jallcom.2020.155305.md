Abstract: The deformation behavior of dual-phase (FCC+BCC) Al 0.6 CoCrFeNi 
high-entropy alloy (HEA) was investigated in a temp erature range from 900 °C to 
1200 °C and a strain rate range from 10 -3 to 1 s -1. The BCC phase completely 
recrystallized when the deformation temperature exc eeded 1100°C, while the FCC 
phase did not even at 1200 . Continuing annealing at high temperature with long 
time (1200  for 20h) can achieve an optimized microstructure, exhibiting that the 
island-shaped BCC phase uniformly distributed in the FCC phase with a fine equiaxed 
grain. Compared with the as-cast alloys, the annealed alloy shows an obvious increase 
in fracture strain with a relatively low decrease in yield stress, demonstrating that such 
a thermo-mechanical process is feasible for improvi ng the mechanical property of 
dual-phase HEAs. 
Keywords: High-entropy alloy; Dual-phase; High temperature de formation; 
Recrystallization behavior; Mechanical property. 
 
1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) were initially proposed by Yeh [1] based on a novel

## Page 3

alloy design strategy of containing at least five p rincipal elements with concentrations 
between 5 and 35 at. %. Since being proposed, HEAs have caused wide attention 
focused on their diversified composition design cri terions [2-6], unique 
microstructures [7-10], and considerable mechanical properties [11-16]. To promote 
the wide applications of HEAs as structural materia ls, great efforts were made to 
obtain excellent mechanical properties, including f ine-grain strengthening [17, 18], 
precipitation-hardening [19, 20], transformation-in duced plasticity [16, 21], and 
hierarchical microstructure [22, 23], etc. Except for those strengthening mechanisms, 
a dual-phase structure by integrating the excellent ductility of FCC phase and high 
strength of BCC phase is also an effective way to i mprove mechanical properties [14, 
24-27]. Currently, the majority of dual-phase HEAs were prepared by casting, while 
the as-cast alloys usually involve some inferior ch aracteristics, such as coarse 
dendritic microstructure, chemical heterogeneity, a nd shrinkage porosity [14, 24, 25]. 
Therefore, the thermo-mechanical processing, as a w orthy approach to optimize 
microstructure, is generally needed for the dual-phase HEAs. 
Up to date, the thermo-mechanical processing was ma inly performed on HEAs 
with a single FCC phase [28-32], limited data of th e dual-phase HEAs were reported 
[26, 33, 34]. The concentration fluctuation, severe lattice distortion, sluggish diffusion 
effect, and low stacking fault energy of HEAs would affect dislocation slip and climb. 
For example, massive dislocation cross slip occurs in the early deformation stage of 
FCC HEAs [35], while dense dislocation bands form i n BCC HEAs [36]; and the 
sluggish diffusion effect could make dislocations c limb difficult in HEAs [28]. All of 
these may affect the recovery and recrystallization behavior of HEAs during high 
temperature deformation. Different from the HEAs wi th a single phase, the 
thermo-deformation behavior of dual-phase HEAs depe nds on not only the multiple

## Page 4

elements characteristic of alloys, but also the res pective feature of constituent phases. 
The differences in coordination deformation ability results in that the deformation 
storage energy of constituent phases is different, thereby leading to an entirely 
different crystallization behavior [37, 38]. Therefore, the research on high temperature 
deformation of dual-phase HEAs is necessary, which could optimize the synergistic 
interaction and avoid high local stress concentrati on between the constituent phases, 
improving the mechanical properties of dual-phase HEAs. 
Based on the reasons above, the present work focuse s on the high temperature 
deformation behavior of a dual-phase Al 0.6 CoCrFeNi HEA. The microstructure 
evolution characteristics of constituent phases were discussed in detail. Our study will 
provide a better understanding of high temperature deformation mechanism of 
dual-phase HEAs and promote the industrial applications of this kind of HEAs. 
2. Materials and experimental methods 
The alloy with a nominal composition of Al 0.6 CoCrFeNi (elements in atomic 
ratio) was prepared by arc-melting a mixture of hig h-purity metals (purity better than 
99.9 mass %) in a Ti-gettered high-purity argon atmosphere. Each ingot was melted at 
least four times to promote the chemical homogeneity. Then the ingots were re-melted 
by induction heating and flip-cast into a copper mold with a dimension of 20×18×80 
mm 3. The as-cast alloy was homogenized at 1200°C for 2 4 h followed by a water 
quenching. After that, cylindrical specimens with d imensions of Φ10×15 mm 3 and 
Φ16×24 mm 3 were cut from the homogenized alloy for high temperature deformation. 
Tensile specimens having gauge dimensions of 8 ×3×1mm 3 were cut from the 
specimen after the thermal deformation (as shown in Fig. 1). 
The high temperature compressive tests were perform ed on Gleeble-3500 
thermal simulation testing machine. The specimens were heated with a rate of 5 °C s -1

## Page 5

and held for 3 mins to obtain a homogenized tempera ture state. The graphite lubricant 
and tantalum sheet were used between the surfaces o f anvils and specimens. In this 
case, the anvils can also be largely protected. The tests were conducted in the 
temperature range of 900-1200 °C (0.62 Tm~0.82 Tm) with an interval of 100 °C. The 
strain rates were 10 -3, 10 -2, 10 -1, and 1 s -1, respectively. All the specimens were 
compressed to ~50% height reduction. 
The phase structure of alloys was examined by Bruke r AXS D8 Advance X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) with a Cu K α radiation. The microstructure of alloys was 
investigated by Axios observer A1m metallographic microscope (OM), Hitachi S4800 
scanning electron microscope (SEM), and Electron ba ckscatter diffraction (EBSD) 
measurements which equipped on the same Hitachi S48 00 SEM. The EBSD data 
were analyzed with the OIM Analysis software. The room temperature tensile 
mechanical property was measured by INSTRON5966 tes ting machine with a strain 
rate of 10 -3s-1.

3.3 Microstructure of the deformed alloys 
Fig. 8 shows the OM micrographs of alloys deformed at different temperatures 
with a strain rate of 10 -3 s-1. As shown in Fig. 8, compared with the as-cast all oys, the 
deformed alloys exhibit a severely deformed microst ructure. No obviously localized 
deformation features such as adiabatic shear bandin g, localized plastic flow, and 
inter-granular cracks were observed. Fig. 8(a) show s that the alloy deformed at 
900 °C still exhibits an obvious dendrite and inter -dendrite morphology. Both the 
FCC and BCC phases were stretched along the transve rse direction (perpendicular to 
the loading direction). For the alloy deformed at 1 000 °C, the dendrite and 
inter-dendrite morphology was not obvious anymore ( Fig. 8(b)). As shown in Fig. 
8(c-d), compared with the alloys deformed at 900-10 00 °C, the alloy deformed at 
1100-1200 °C exhibits a relative uniform microstruc ture. The dendrite morphology of 
FCC phase completely disappeared, and the BCC phase uniformly distributed in the

## Page 9

FCC phase. Due to the stretching during deformation, the size of BCC phase along the 
transverse direction is obviously higher than that along the loading direction. 
The microstructure of deformed alloys was further i nvestigated using EBSD 
technique. Fig. 9 shows the inverse pole figure (IP F) and phase distribution maps of 
alloys deformed at 900  and 1200 . For the alloy deformed at 900 , color 
gradient representing the existence of orientation gradient obviously appeared inside 
FCC phase, as shown in Fig. 9(a), indicating that n o dynamic recrystallization (DRX) 
occurred. Simultaneously, many low-angle boundaries (LABs, < 10 °, shown by white 
lines) can be observed in the FCC phase near the ph ase boundary, as shown in Fig. 
9(b). The concentrated distribution of LABs is due to the stress concentration near the 
phase boundaries which led to the formation of disl ocation. The accumulation of 
dislocations led to the formation of subgrains. In contrast, the BCC phase deformed at 
900°C exhibits starting of DRX as equiaxed high-ang le boundaries (HABs, ＞10°, 
shown by black lines) (Fig 9(b)). But the content o f HABs is very low, indicating that 
the DRX just took place in a few areas. As the temp erature increased to1200 ℃, DRX 
occurred in the FCC phase; the low-angle subgrain b oundary transformed into 
high-angle boundary (Fig 9(c, d)). Equiaxed recryst allized grains bounded by HABs 
were achieved in the FCC phase, while many LABs sti ll remained, revealing an 
incomplete recrystallization. The BCC phase turned into a completely recrystallized 
equiaxed grains microstructure without any LABs existence (Fig 9(d)). 
Fig. 10 shows the SEM images of the deformed alloy at 1200 °C with different 
strain rates. Fig. 10(a) and Fig. 10(b) compare the grain size of the BCC phase at 
strain rates of 10 -3 s-1 and 1 s -1, respectively. The grain boundary was marked by th e 
black arrows. Apparently, the latter exhibits a fin er grain size than the former, 
demonstrating that the grain size decreased with an increase in the strain rate at

4. Discussion 
4.1 Microstructure evolution during high temperature deformation 
Figs. 8-10 show that although deformed at relativel y high temperature and wide 
strain rate ranges, the FCC phase was not fully rec rystallized, while the BCC phase 
started DRX at 900 °C and achieved a completely rec rystallized microstructure when 
the temperature was above 1100 °C. In the dual-phas e alloys, recovery or 
recrystallization in one phase could take place independently of the other phase during 
high temperature deformation [38, 42]. The microstr ucture evolution incompatibility 
of the dual-phase alloys is partially ascribed to t he respective features of the 
constituent phases, such as stack fault energy, chemical composition, volume fractions, 
and morphology [34, 37, 38] In addition, the strain and stress partitions of each phase 
also acts as a crucial role on the microstructure e volution of dual-phase alloys during 
high temperature deformation [33]. However, the per formance of each phase is 
strongly affected by the other phase. In the presen t dual-phase alloy with an 
FCC+BCC structure, the BCC phase acts as a “hard” a nd “brittle” role, whereas the 
FCC phase takes on a “ductile” and “toughness” part . The mismatch of strength and 
plasticity between the BCC and FCC phase results in a highly uneven stress and strain 
partition during deformation. Because of having inf erior plastic deformation ability, 
the BCC phase is regarded to undertake more stress partition than the FCC phase for 
coordinating deformation. The high stress partition is considered to have a strong 
contribution for the dynamic recrystallization of t he BCC phase. By contrast, the 
recrystallization driving force of FCC phase is con sidered to be degraded by releasing 
the stored deformation energy during recovery proce ss. That may be a main reason

## Page 11

that why the BCC phase is more liable to recrystallize than the FCC phase. 
In addition, Figs. 8-10 show that both the DRX of F CC and BCC phase took 
place at a relatively high temperature (900-1100 ℃， about 0.62 Tm~0.82 Tm), while the 
recrystallization temperature of traditional alloys is generally below 0.5 Tm. The 
similar tendency was also found in other HEAs [26, 31-33]. The multi-component 
feature and unique sluggish diffusion effect of HEA s were considered to be related to 
the above results. Generally, the recrystallization temperature increases as adding 
solute atoms into alloys [43]. Thus, as a kind of m ulti-component alloys, the 
recrystallization temperature of HEAs is higher tha n the alloys having one main 
component. In addition, the sluggish diffusion effe ct was considered to hinder the 
atomic diffusion and migration of grain boundary wh ich control the growth rate of 
recrystallized grains [41]. With an increase in the deformation temperature, the sluggish 
diffusion effect would obviously weaken. Thus, the DRX of BCC phase just took 
place in a few areas when deforming at 900 ℃, and occur universally when the 
deformation temperature reached to 1100 ℃.

4.2 Microstructure evolution during high temperature annealing 
As shown in Figs. 8-10, the FCC phase did not compl etely recrystallize 
although deformed at a relatively high temperature and a wide strain rate range, while 
the BCC phase exhibited a fully recrystallized micr ostructure when the temperature 
reached to 1100°C. Such tendency is related to the uneven stress and strain 
participation between the two phases and the unique multi-component sluggish 
diffusion effect of HEAs. As previous state, the re crystallization of HEAs inclines to 
occur at a high deformation temperature or annealin g for a long time [26, 31-33]. 
Therefore, it is speculated that the FCC phase woul d recrystallize when annealing at a 
high temperature. To confirm the speculation, the a lloy deformed at 900 °C with a 
strain rate of 10 -3 s -1 was further annealed at 1200 °C for different dura tion. Figs. 
12(a-c) shows the EBSD image-quality (IQ) maps of t he alloys, the BCC phase is 
highlighted by white. It is evident that the initia l deformed microstructure was 
completely replaced by the recrystallized equiaxed grains microstructure both in the 
FCC and BCC phases after annealing for 2h. And abundant annealing twins 
(highlighted in blue lines) formed in the FCC phase , indicating a low stacking-fault 
energy of FCC phase [44]. The specific grain sizes are shown in Fig. 12(d). The 
average grain size of FCC phase is ~17 μm after annealing for 2 h, and the size 
increased to 23 μm when prolonging the annealing time to 20h, demon strating that 
the grain sizes increased very slowly with increasi ng annealing time. It could be 
supposed that the BCC phase sufficiently limited th e migration of subgrain 
boundaries due to the Zener pinning effect [45]. Th erefore, the present dual-phase 
HEA can easily maintain a fine-grained structure ev en undergoing high temperature 
(0.82 Tm) and long-term annealing, while for many single-ph ase HEAs, the 
recrystallized grains can grow to hundreds of microns after annealing at a temperature

## Page 13

less than 0.7 Tm for several hours [28, 29]. Simultaneously, the vo lume fraction of 
BCC phase remains almost constant during annealing. But the dendrite morphology as 
an unfavorable microstructure to the mechanical pro perty degraded slowly with an 
extension in annealing time. Though some BCC phases still kept a banded feature 
when annealing for 10h (Fig. 12(b)), most of them t urned into island structures and 
uniformly distributed at the grain boundaries of th e FCC phase after annealed for 20h 
(Fig. 12(c)). Such a microstructure is expected to improve the ductility of the present 
dual-phase HEAs. 
Fig. 13 shows the tensile engineering stress-strain curves of as-cast, deformed, 
and annealed alloys. The yield stress, ultimate ten sile stress, and fracture strain of 
alloy (deformed at 900 °C with a strain rate of 10 -3 s-1) annealed for 20 h are 443 MPa, 
800 MPa, and 32 %, respectively. Compared with the as-cast alloy, the annealed alloy 
showed a 68.4% increase in fracture strain, and 11% decrease in ultimate tensile stress, 
and a 23.9% decrease in yield stress.
