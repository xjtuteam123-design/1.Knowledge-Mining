An analysis of strengthening mechanisms in a mechanically

intermetallic

Abstract
An iron aluminide alloy of base composition Fe-40Al has been prepared by mechanical alloying and processed using
a variety of powder consolidation methods and heat treatments to produce a range of grain sizes and oxide dispersoid
sizes. The strengths of these materials have been determined at room temperature and related to the various aspects
of microstructure. Fine dispersoid particles may pin grain boundaries and help determine the ﬁne grain size and contrib-
ute very signiﬁcantly to the material strength. Grain size strengthening is shown to be a rather small component of the
material strength, with the matrix strength being rather high for this intermetallic. The inﬂuence of other factors such
as texture and the direction of application of stress (tension or compression) are also brieﬂy discussed.  2002 Acta
Materialia Inc. Published by Elsevier Science Ltd. All rights reserved.

tural aspects that lead to good strength and duc-

analyse strengthening in these materials [10 –13],
as well as to analyse ductility [14]. In these analy-
ses [10 –12], strengthening due to grain size
reﬁnement was interpreted on the basis of the very
limited literature data, showing a strong Hall–Petch
effect [2,7,15 –17], a similar strengthening contri-
bution due to the ﬁne oxide particles, and an equal
contribution of matrix strengthening, justi ﬁed on
the basis of the Fe-40Al matrix containing alloying
additions, notably B. Texture was also deduced to
play some role in modifying the matrix strength
[11,12], with retained vacancies or other solute
also playing some role [11,12,18].
The present study re-examines strengthening in
these mechanically alloyed intermetallics making
use of the many more materials and microstructural
variants now available to allow a better analysis.
A careful analysis of strengthening mechanisms is
also justiﬁed on the grounds that recent studies of
similar materials have shown that very ﬁne grain
sized materials may not be subject to vacancy
hardening because many grain boundaries and par-
ticle–matrix interfaces may act as sinks allowing
ready elimination of excess vacancies [18,19].
Such vacancy hardening is well known to contrib-
ute signi ﬁcantly to the total hardness/strength of
FeAl alloys in general, unless these are very care-
fully annealed to remove all the vacancies [20 –22]
and may be responsible for the anomalous
strengthening observed at intermediate testing tem-
peratures [23]. This detailed analysis of strengthen-
ing mechanisms is also a timely comparison with
recent studies of an oxide dispersion strengthened
iron alloy, where the relative roles of matrix
strength, dispersion strengthening, and grain size
strengthening have been established, and notably
very strong grain size strengthening has been
deduced [24,25].
2. Experimental details
Materials for study were supplied either by
CEREM, CEA (Centre de Recherche en Mater-
iaux, Atomic Research Centre) of Grenoble,
France, initially as part of a European Research
Network (CEASI), or by PLANSEE AG of Ger-
many, as part of a European Research Project
(FIAC). These samples have been designated as
CH samples and F samples for the initial ones, and
as D samples for the later ones. Materials were pre-
pared by milling pre-alloyed powders of nominal
composition Fe-40Al-0.05Zr-50 ppm B with 1
vol% of Y
2O3 powders, and subsequently consoli-
dated to bar or plate by extrusion, forging or rol-
ling, all at 1100 °C. In all the cases preparation
conditions ensured suf ﬁcient straining that excel-
lent powder particle adhesion was obtained, with
full density, and there was no indication of any
inter-powder-particle decohesion during mechan-
ical testing. Most materials, especially the later
ones, were prepared in very large dimensions, for
example as bars/plates of diameter/thickness of the
order of 30 mm and a metre or so in length, such
that the ﬁnal natural cooling stage after processing
was very slow. Some of these materials had their
microstructures modi ﬁed by thermal or thermo-
mechanical treatments, and many of the others
were deliberately aged at low temperatures to
ensure that any retained excess vacancies would be
removed. The D sample materials were not given
any additional treatments after processing, both
because natural cooling after fabrication was
deemed to be very slow, and also because it has
recently been shown that such excess vacancies are
readily lost from such ﬁne grained materials [19].

3.2. Mechanical testing results
Three types of mechanical testing were carried
out, namely hardness, compression, and tensile
testing. Compression testing was carried out to a
strain of about 8 –10%. Tension testing was gener-
ally carried out to failure. Tensile ductility varied
from about 2 to 10% according to the material state
and the surface preparation conditions: details of
fracture behaviour have been analysed elsewhere
[14].
Hardness values, yield stress and ﬂow stress at
8% plastic strain are given in Table 2. The yield
point was generally marked by a discrete disconti-
nuity, which was sometimes a sudden change of
slope of the load –extension curve and other times
was a signi ﬁcant yield drop from a sharp upper
yield point, followed by a yield plateau before
entering the usual stage of linear work hardening.
Such yield discontinuities have been reported else-
where [10,13].

As a ﬁnal point it is of interest to compare the
present value for matrix strength (460 MPa) with
data in the literature. As described previously,
literature data often suggests a matrix strength of
about 400 MPa [2,7,9,10,15], with binary alloys of
similar Al content showing a matrix strength of
about 200 MPa [16,17]. The composition of the
present alloy suggests that B and/or Zr retained in
solution may be responsible for the present high
matrix strength. B is known to produce strong sol-
ution hardening [4,34 –37] as well as lead to
changes of Hall –Petch slope [38]. These publi-
cations typically show B strengthening of the order
of 100 MPa for alloys containing 100 –1200 ppm
B, with strengthening tending to saturate already
at B levels of about 100 ppm [37], suggesting that
this may be near the solid solubility level for typi-
cal processing conditions. In addition to these stud-
ies, Maziasz et al. [39] have examined the mechan-
ical properties of a range of alloys of about 36%
Al containing additions of B with or without Zr
and other alloying elements. Since the grain size
remained rather large for their cast materials and
there were no dispersoid particles present, the
changes observed may be taken to correspond to
changes of matrix strength. As aforementioned,
these authors observed a strengthening by B
additions of about 100 MPa, with again the
strengthening tending to saturate above about 100
ppm B. In this case, the subsequent addition of Zr
tended to reduce the strength, presumably as B was
removed from solid solution as stable boride par-
ticles formed. The importance of precise pro-
cessing conditions on strengthening is demon-
strated by the different behaviour observed by
Gaydosh et al. [2,15], who found that additions of

5. Conclusions
The room temperature mechanical strength of
mechanically alloyed Fe-40Al intermetallic has
been examined and the mechanisms contributing
to strength analysed.

and does not respect the commonly observed

grain boundary strengthening. Finely distributed
particles are major obstacles to dislocations and
may also shield grain boundaries from having a
