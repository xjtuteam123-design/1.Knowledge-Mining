Generally, μ phase preferentially precipitates at the place where the refractory elements segregate. How- 
ever, in this study, the μ phase preferentially formed in the interdendritic regions of Co-Ni-Al-W-based 
superalloys during solution treatment, while the refractory elements W and Cr segregate to the dendritic 
cores. The microstructure evolution of the alloy during solution treatment was investigated. It showed 
that three different mechanisms caused this abnormal precipitation in the interdendritic regions, includ- 
ing primary carbides degeneration, further degeneration of the secondary carbides formed by the Laves 
phase decomposition, and direct precipitation from the interdendritic regions induced by the Laves phase 
decomposition.

Superalloys have a wide application in aircraft engines and in- 
dustrial gas turbines because of their excellent high-temperature 
properties, good high-temperature microstructural stability, and 
excellent oxidation and corrosion resistance [1] . Increasing the 
content of refractory elements, such as W and Re, is an effec- 
tive way to improve the high-temperature properties of superalloys 
[1 , 2] . However, an excessive amount of refractory elements may 
render the alloy prone to topologically close-packed (TCP) phases 
during solution treatment or thermal exposure. The presence of 
TCP phases can severely deteriorate the high-temperature mechan- 
ical properties of superalloys by depletion of the strengthening el- 
ements and formation of cracks induced by local stress concentra- 
tions [3–6] . Among the various types of TCP phases in Ni-based 
superalloys, close attention has been paid to the μ phase in previ- 
ous research due to its various morphologies and different effects 
on the properties of the superalloys [7–1 1] . 
Recently, multicomponent alloys that were developed based 
on γ ’-strengthened Co-Al-W-based superalloys can approach the 
high-temperature properties of second-generation Ni-based single- 
crystal superalloys through coherent strengthening and precipita- 
tion strengthening [12–17] . This means that the Co-Al-W-based su- 
peralloys have great application potential. Due to the high content

Fig. 1 shows the as-cast microstructures of the CoNiAlW alloy 
perpendicular to the growth direction. The as-cast microstructure 
exhibits a typical dendritic structure. The major interdendritic sec- 
ondary phases in the alloy are the Laves phase, ( γ + γ′ ) e eutectic 
and primary MC carbide, and the area fractions of the three phases 
are 0.31%, 4.19% and 0.18%, respectively. According to our previous 
research [20] , the Laves phase is enriched in Ti, Cr, W and Ta. The 
( γ + γ′ ) e eutectic is enriched in Ti, and the primary MC carbide 
is enriched in Ta, W and Ti. Moreover, Cr, W and Co segregated to 
the dendritic cores, and Ti, Al, Ta and Ni segregated to the inter- 
dendritic regions.

Fig. 3 shows the microstructure of the CoNiAlW alloy after heat- 
ing at 115 0 °C for 1 h. It can be seen that needle-like, rod-like 
and irregular block-like white precipitates are formed in the region 
near the Laves phase with an extension of the solution treatment 
time. The white precipitates with three morphologies have a simi- 
lar composition to that of the μ phase. The growth of the μ phase 
around the primary MC carbide is not obvious, but the μ phase 
grows from the edge of the primary MC carbide towards the inside 
of the matrix. The secondary MC carbide formed at the edge of 
the Laves phase experiences signiﬁcant growth and grows from the 
edge to the interior of the Laves phase. This means that the Laves 
phase is gradually transformed into the secondary MC carbide. Ac- 
cording to the TEM analysis, all three different precipitates have a 
rhombohedral structure and are identiﬁed as the μ phase. How- 
ever, the μ phase with different morphologies has different OR 
with the matrix. The OR between the needle-like μ phase and ma- 
trix is (001) μ//(1 1–1) γ ′ and [1–10] μ//[01 1] γ ′ . The long axis of the 
needle-like μ phase is parallel to the [1 10] crystal direction of the 
matrix. In addition, an OR of (001) μ//(1 10) γ ′ and [1–10] μ//[01 1] γ ′ 
exists when the μ phase is rod-like. However, the (001) μ plane 
is not completely parallel to the (1 10) γ ′ plane. There is a 2.2 ° an- 
gle between the [001] μ axis and the [1 10] γ ′ axis. For the irregular 
block-like μ phase, there is no OR between it and the matrix. 
Fig. 4 shows the microstructure of the CoNiAlW alloy after heat- 
ing at 115 0 °C for different time. When the solution treatment time 
was extended to 2 h, the bulk Laves phase in the interdendritic re- 
gion completely disappears, and the μ phase at the edge of den- 
drite signiﬁcantly increases. There are only white μ phases and 
grey MC carbides in the interdendritic region in the CoNiAlW alloy. 
Moreover, two types of MC carbide are observed: the very large 
primary MC carbide that forms during solidiﬁcation and the small 
secondary MC carbide that forms during the Laves phase decom- 
position. The block-like μ phase forms at the edge of both types 
of MC carbides. Upon extending the solution treatment time, the 
μ phase begins to precipitate from the dendritic trunks and ﬁnally 
uniformly distributes in the matrix. The growth of the block-like 
μ phase that forms at the edge of the MC carbide is not obvious. 
In addition, it is worth noting that the μ phase that forms in the 
dendritic trunks is mostly needle-like, and only a small part is rod- 
like. 
Based on the above results, there are three different precipita- 
tion mechanisms for the μ phase in the CoNiAlW alloy: primary 
MC carbide degeneration, further degeneration of the secondary 
MC carbide formed by the Laves phase decomposition, and direct 
precipitation from the matrix.

composition. The primary MC carbides are enriched in Ta, W and

With an extension of the solution treatment time, the primary MC 
carbide gradually becomes stable and no longer decomposes. The 
μ phase grows by absorbing the W and Cr in the matrix, and the 
γ ’-forming elements, such as Al, Ti and Ta, are rejected. Therefore, 
the γ ’-ﬁlm is formed around the μ phase, and this ﬁlm becomes 
increases in width as the μ phase grows. 
In nickel-based superalloys, the Laves phase typically decom- 
poses during the solution treatment and the size and amount of 
the phase decreases continuously, and no new precipitates are pro- 
duced [25 , 26] . However, the results in this paper show that dur- 
ing the solution treatment, the secondary MC carbide forms at the 
edge of the Laves phase and gradually consume the Laves phase 
over time. This indicates that the Laves phase decomposes to form 
the secondary MC carbide during the solution treatment. At the 
same time, the secondary MC carbide has a cube-cube OR with 
the matrix but has no unique OR with the Laves phase. The ma- 
trix can provide favourable conditions for the nucleation of the 
secondary MC carbide. This is similar to the decomposition of 
the M 7 C 3 carbide in Co-based superalloys, as reported in previous 
studies [27] . Moreover, these secondary MC carbides also transi- 
tion to a more stable MC carbide as the solution time increases. 
Therefore, a block-like μ phase is also formed at the edge of the 
secondary MC carbides.

cipitates from the matrix. The morphology of the μ phase depends 
on the OR between the μ phase and matrix. For the needle-like μ
phase, the OR between the μ phase and matrix is (001) μ//(1 1–1) γ ′ 
and [1–10] μ//[01 1] γ ′ . When the μ phase is precipitated via this 
OR, its (001) μ basal plane forms a coherent relationship with the 
(1 10) γ plane of the matrix [9 , 28] . The growth of the basal plane is 
limited. The lateral plane has a high migration rate because of the 
non-coherent relationship with the matrix. At the same time, to 
reduce the interface energy, the lateral plane of the μ phase grows 
along the close-packed direction of the matrix. Therefore, the μ
phase nucleates based on the ( 111 ) γ ′ matrix plane and grows along 
the ⟨ 110 ⟩ γ ′ close-packed direction of the matrix. For the rod-like 
μ phase, the OR between the rod-like μ phase and the matrix is 
(001) μ//(1 10) γ ′ and [1–10] μ//[01 1] γ ′ . When the μ phase is precip- 
itated via this OR, its (001) μ basal plane forms a non-coherent re- 
lationship with the (1 10) γ plane of the matrix. And the (100) μ or 
(1 10) μ lateral plane forms a semi-coherent relationship with the 
(1 10) γ ′ , (001) γ ′ , ( 111 ) γ ′ and (1 12) γ ′ low index planes of the ma- 
trix [8 , 29] . At this time, the growth of the lateral plane is limited, 
while the migration rate of the basal plane is high. Therefore, the 
rod-like μ phase nucleates based on the low index planes of the 
matrix and grows along the ⟨ 110 ⟩ γ ′ close-packed direction of the 
matrix. However, compared with that of the needle-like μ phase, 
the anisotropy of the growth of the rod-like μ phase is decreased 
due to coherent relationship of the lateral interface is poorer. There 
is no OR between the irregular block-like μ phase and matrix. This 
indicates that the irregular blocky μ phase is directly precipitated 
from the matrix, and each interface between it and the matrix is 
non-coherent. 
The formation of μ phase is related to the concentration of the 
refractory elements in the matrix. When the refractory elements 
concentration in the matrix is low, the precipitation of the μ phase 
with a certain OR can reduce the energy required to form the μ
phase. When the refractory elements concentration in the matrix 
is high, the obstruction of the μ phase nucleation is low. The μ
phase can directly nucleate without depending on these special 
crystal planes and therefore has no OR with the matrix. Generally, 
the refractory element concentration required to form μ phase is 
needle-like < rod-like < irregular blocky. 
In the CoNiAlW alloy, the irregular block-like μ phase only 
forms in the interdendritic regions. In the dendritic cores, only the 
needle-like or rod-like μ phase is formed, and the majority of the 
precipitates are needle-like. According to the morphology and dis- 
tribution of μ phase, it can be found that the refractory elements 
concentration in the interdendritic regions is higher than that in 
the dendritic cores. This is attributed to the decomposition of 
the Laves phase which releases a large amount of W and Cr to the 
nearby matrix. Therefore, the μ phase preferentially formed in the 
interdendritic regions. Moreover, it is worth noting that the high- 
est refractory elements concentration in this paper is located in 
the interdendritic regions. This indicates that the decomposition of 
the Laves phase causes the alloy to deviate from the equilibrium 
composition and precipitate the μ phase. This will increases the 
precipitation tendency of μ phase for some alloy. Therefore, the 
formation of the Laves phase during solidiﬁcation should be sup- 
pressed by the optimization of the composition and solidiﬁcation 
process. 
In summary, the μ phase in the CoNiAlW alloy has three differ- 
ent precipitation mechanisms during solution treatment: (1) The μ
phase nucleates and grows at the edge of the primary MC carbide, 
due to the degeneration of the primary MC carbide. The residual 
MC carbide gradually transforms to more stable TaC and TiC. (2) 
The Laves phase in the interdendritic regions decomposed, and the 
secondary MC carbide nucleates and grows at the Laves/matrix in- 
terface. Subsequently, the secondary MC carbide, similar to the de- 
generation of primary MC carbide, is also degenerated to form μ
phase around it. (3) The μ phase is directly precipitated with a 
needle-like, rod-like or irregular block-like morphology from the 
matrix. The μ phase with different morphologies has different 
orientation relationships with the matrix. Moreover, the μ phase 
preferentially formed in the interdendritic regions is induced by 
the Laves phase decomposition.
