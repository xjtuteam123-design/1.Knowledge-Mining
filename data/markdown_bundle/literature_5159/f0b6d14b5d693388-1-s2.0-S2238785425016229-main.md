ABSTRACT
Polycrystalline γ -TiAl alloy is an excellent high-temperature material. To investigate the effect of initial tem -
perature on the impact resistance of γ -TiAl alloys, this work employs molecular dynamics simulations to analyze 
the material responses under varying initial temperatures and impact velocities. The study focuses on three 
aspects: internal stress, microstructural evolution and dislocation movement. The results show that plastic 
deformation in γ -TiAl alloys is induced by both slip-induced plasticity and twinning-induced plasticity. When the 
initial temperature exceeds 780K, the alloy ’ s plasticity and ductility decrease, and the σ
HEL 
also decreases with 
increasing temperature. The sudden reduction in the density of stair-rod and Hirth dislocations at temperatures 
above 780K is identified as a key factor contributing to the alloy ’ s inadequate strength at high temperatures. 
Spalling occurs at grain boundaries, and the strength for spalling generally decreases with increasing initial 
temperature, although the change is relatively small below 780K. This work clarifies the micro-mechanisms of 
the mechanical responses of γ -TiAl alloys under impact loading, providing theoretical guidance for the impact- 
resistant design of high-performance polycrystalline γ -TiAl alloys.
1. Introduction
With the increasing demand for lightweight and high-temperature 
materials in the aerospace sector, titanium aluminide (TiAl) alloys 
have garnered significant attention due to their unique combination of 
properties [ 1 ]. Compared to traditional nickel-based superalloys and 
titanium-based alloys, TiAl alloys exhibit remarkable low density 
(3.9 – 4.2 g/cm
3
, depending on composition and structure) and excellent 
high-temperature specific strength (0 – 800 
◦
C) [ 2 – 4 ]. Furthermore, TiAl 
alloys possess high melting points, excellent thermal stability, high 
specific modulus, low diffusivity, good structural stability, excellent 
oxidation and corrosion resistance, and high flame retardanc [ 5 – 8 ]. 
These properties make TiAl alloys an ideal materials for certain com -
ponents in high-temperature aircraft engines, such as turbine blades [ 9 ,
10 ].
However, the application of TiAl alloys still faces significant chal -
lenges. Low ductility (1 % – 3 %) at room to intermediate temperatures 
(0 – 800 
◦
C), susceptibility to brittle fracture, and difficulties in process -
ing limit their widespread use [ 11 , 12 ]. Moreover, while TiAl alloys 
perform excellently below 750 
◦
C, their creep resistance and strength 
drop significantly above this temperature, limiting their practical usage 
beyond this threshold [ 13 ]. Severe defects induced by impact or thermal 
stress during use lead to a dramatic reduction in the alloy ’ s fatigue and 
fracture resistance, negatively affecting its mechanical properties. 
Optimizing their composition, microstructure, or processing techniques 
to improve overall performance has become a core research direction in 
this field. Polycrystalline γ -TiAl alloys with an tetragonal L1
0 
structure 
are considered the preferred materials for manufacturing certain aero -
space structural components, as they can significantly enhance the 
thrust-to-weight ratio and fuel efficiency [ 9 , 14 ]. Their microstructure 
contains numerous interfaces with complex orientations, which signifi -
cantly influence mechanical properties [ 15 ].
Interface characteristics play a crucial role in mechanical responses, 
and therefore, the study of interface properties has gained increasing 
attention in the design of high-performance material [ 16 – 18 ]. Li et al. 
[ 19 ] employed molecular dynamics (MD) methods to investigate the 
crack propagation mechanism in nanocrystalline titanium, finding that 
the presence of grain boundaries increases brittleness at low

2. Methods
In this work, a polycrystalline γ -TiAl alloy model is established using 
LAMMPS in conjunction with the polycrystal modeling software Atomsk 
[ 26 ], where the Poisson – Voronoi algorithm [ 27 ] is employed to 
generate the polycrystalline structure. The simulation box dimensions 
are set to 260 Å × 390 Å × 260 Å, with 8 grains. The sample of the 
polycrystalline γ -TiAl alloy is shown in Fig. 1 , and the sample consists of 
approximately 1.57 million atoms.
The simulations utilize the embedded atom method (EAM) potential 
function to describe the interactions between Ti and Al atoms [ 25 ], 
which can accurately simulate the mechanical properties of TiAl alloys 
[ 28 , 29 ]. Before the impact loading, all simulation models undergo en -
ergy minimization using the conjugate gradient method. Periodic 
boundary conditions (p p p) are applied, and the system is relaxed under 
constant temperature and pressure (NPT) conditions at 300K for 18ps to 
reach thermodynamic equilibrium. Subsequently, the boundary condi -
tion in the y-direction (the direction of impact loading) is replaced with 
a free surface boundary condition. Throughout the impact simulation, 
the microcanonical ensemble (NVE) is used. The shock wave is gener -
ated by a rigid piston moving at a constant velocity (Up) for 3ps. The 
time step is set to 1fs, and the Up velocity ranges from 0.7 km/s to 1.3 
km/s. The initial temperatures are set to room temperature (300K), an 
intermediate-high temperature limit (780K) [ 13 ], and high temperature 
(1180K).
Post-processing analysis is performed using the software Ovito [ 30 ], 
which visualizes the results from LAMMPS simulations in image format. 
In this work, the common neighbor analysis (CNA) method and dislo -
cation extraction algorithm (DXA) are used to investigate the micro -
structure. CNA is a method that identifies crystal structure types by 
analyzing the local atomic environment and can distinguish between 
crystal structures such as face-centered cubic (FCC), body-centered 
cubic (BCC), hexagonal close-packed (HCP), or disordered (amor -
phous) structures. DXA is a method for detecting dislocations, which are 
linear defects in crystal structures that typically form during plastic 
deformation, phase transitions, or impact loading processes.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Impact loading stage
During the impact loading stage, the simulated sample is in a com -
pressed state. Fig. 2 illustrates the impact pressure, shear stress, tem -
perature curves, and impact stress contour plots under different initial 
temperature conditions at an impact velocity of 1 km/s. Previous studies 
have shown that when the impact velocity is relatively low, the mate -
rial ’ s elastic response is similar, and the shear stress remains nearly 
constant [ 31 ]. However, high-speed impact generates a 
three-dimensional stress state that results in sufficiently high shear stress 
[ 32 ].
As seen in Fig. 2 , in the regions unaffected by the impact, the shear 
stress of all samples is zero, while in the elastic compression zone, the 
shear stress increases abruptly. In the transition zone from pure elas -
ticity to pure plasticity (shock front), the shear stress significantly de -
creases, which is attributed to stress relaxation caused by dislocation 
nucleation. From the impact pressure curve, it is evident that along the 
x-direction, the impact pressure curve initially decreases nonlinearly, 
then reaches an inflection point, and finally decreases linearly. Here, 
σ
HEL 
is defined as the inflection point that connects the linear (elastic) 
and nonlinear (plastic) decline regions [ 33 ]. As the initial temperature 
increases, the σ
HEL 
of the samples decreases to 20.31 GPa, 18.5 GPa, and 
17.1 GPa, respectively. The pressure contours in Fig. 2 also show that 
from (a) to (c), as the temperature increases, the distribution of red re -
gions gradually decreases, indicating a general reduction in stress. It is 
evident that the mechanical properties of γ -TiAl are strongly 
temperature-dependent, with the reduced temperature lowering the 
energy required for dislocation nucleation [ 34 ].
As shown in the temperature curves Fig. 2 , once the shock wave 
reaches the left interface, the temperature rises sharply due to the 
intense compression of the model. Subsequently, as the shock wave 
propagates through the model, the temperature decreases until it returns 
to the initial value. It can be observed that the temperature increases at 
the shock front and forms a plateau (as indicated by the red arrow in the 
figure). This phenomenon is due to stress concentration at the shock 
front. As shown from (a) to (c), the width of the plateau decreases with 
increasing initial temperature, indicating that the stress concentration 
effect weakens as the temperature rises.
To further analyze the dislocation evolution process, the percentage 
variations of BCC, FCC, HCP, and Other phases in the sample micro -
structure at three different times during the impact compression phase 
are shown in Fig. 3 . At t = 3ps, the BCC phase contents corresponding to 
different initial temperatures are 2.4 %, 2.8 %, and 2.7 %, with the 
highest BCC content observed at 780K and the lowest at 300K. It can be 
seen that below 780 K, the increase in temperature promotes the 
transformation from the FCC phase to BCC phase, which yields greater 
plastic deformation [ 35 ]. Therefore, below 780 K, higher impact tem -
peratures are more favorable for plastic deformation. However, above 
780K, the trend reverses. This result is consistent with the conclusions 
drawn by Appel et al. [ 36 ].
As shown in Fig. 3 (e), the FCC content continuously decreases during 
the impact process. This is due to phase transformation under pressure. 
As illustrated in the defect evolution ( Fig. 3 (i)), two FCC unit cells are 
pre-constructed into a body-centered tetragonal (BCT) structure ( Fig. 3
(i) – I). Under compression along the < 110 > direction, the BCT can 
transform into a BCC lattice, which is a process known as the Bain path 
[ 37 ]. With further deformation, the BCC lattice undergoes compression 
along the y-axis and sliding along the z-axis within the middle planes, 
resulting in a transformation from BCC to HCP.
To describe this phenomenon, we captured the defect evolution 
within the G6 grain. In Fig. 3 (h), the G6 grain is FCC before the impact. 
At 1.5ps, BCC atoms begin to appear at the grain boundary, followed by 
the evolution into HCP atoms. In Fig. 3 (e), the reduction in FCC atoms 
under different temperatures is attributed to their transformation into 
BCC and HCP structures; however, the FCC transformation rate does not 
exhibit a significant change. Fig. 3 (f) shows that as the impact pro -
gresses, the number of HCP atoms increases, and the rate of increase 
accelerates, resulting in a trend where the BCC phase content first in -
creases and then decreases. This indicates that with increasing initial 
temperature, the transformation from BCC to HCP is suppressed, leading 
to fewer stacking faults and thus a greater resistance to dislocation 
motion [ 25 ]. "Other" atoms are almost exclusively located at the grain 
boundaries. As shown in Fig. 3 (g), with the progression of impact, the 
destruction of grain boundaries leads to an increasing amount of 
disordered amorphous atoms. When the initial temperature reaches 
1180 K, more amorphous point defects appear in the model, with the 
highest content observed.

3.2. Impact unloading stage
After the impact unloading, due to the absence of continuous shock 
wave loading, the changes in internal defects and dislocations are 
markedly different from those during the loading phase. Fig. 4 (a) – (c) 
show the phase content variation curves at an impact velocity of 1 km/s 
under different initial temperatures. It can be observed that during the 
entire impact process, the FCC phase content first decreases, then 
slightly increases, and then decreases again until it stabilizes. The initial 
decrease occurs because, during the impact compression phase (shown 
by the pink region), a large number of dislocation nucleation and 
propagation events take place within the sample, and both HCP and BCC 
phases appear. From the figure, it can also be observed that the 
maximum BCC phase content increases from 2.8 % to 3.3 % as the initial 
temperature increases, and then decreases back to 2.8 %. The higher the 
BCC phase content, the more intense the model ’ s evolution under 
impact, reflecting more significant plastic deformation of the model. 
This further confirms that 780K is the critical temperature for γ -TiAl 
alloys, as their plasticity decreases when the temperature exceeds 780K.

lead to the formation of defects. When the rarefaction wave reflected

3.3. Spalling behavior
Spalling is a key phenomenon during impact processes, holding 
significant importance in both physics and mechanics. This behavior 
typically occurs when the unloading wave interacts with the reflected 
wave from the free surface. The intensity of spalling is influenced by 
various factors, including the internal microstructure, atomic configu -
ration, grain boundaries, impact stress, and initial temperature. In this 
study, we focus on the spalling process and its microscopic mechanisms 
in polycrystalline γ -TiAl alloys at different initial temperatures.
Fig. 7 shows snapshots at four critical moments from 7ps to 10ps, 
illustrating the evolution of the spallation process. The spallation evo -
lution shows no significant difference under the three temperatures

Fig. 7 (a) , internal defects are identified using the "construct surface 
mesh" method. Fig. 7 (b) and (c) show the pressure and temperature 
distribution maps at different times, respectively.

a pressure peak with a dip at the center, as indicated by the green arrow

= 8ps, certain regions of the sample are already in tension, leading to the 
formation of small visible voids (depicted as purple holes in Fig. 7 (a) ). 
As time progresses, these voids expand and form cracks, which then 
evolve into more distinct bands. The tensile stress in this region

The above analysis primarily focuses on the spallation and thermo -
dynamic behavior. In polycrystalline γ -TiAl alloys, the presence of grain 
boundaries leads to spall evolution that differs from that in single 
crystals. To investigate the evolution of grain boundaries and grains 
during the spallation process, the microstructures of grains G2, G3, G7, 
and G8 at an initial temperature of 300 K are analyzed, as illustrated in 
Fig. 8 . The microstructural evolution at the grain boundaries is most 
clearly at 300 K, so we choose this case for analysis.
As shown in Fig. 8 (a), at the coinciding grain boundaries of grains G7 
and G3 (highlighted by the black ellipse), the boundary expands at 5ps 
compared to its state at 0 ps. Specifically, atomic motion is hindered by 
the grain boundary, causing atoms to slide along it. Thus, the observed 
grain boundary distortion can be attributed to boundary sliding during 
plastic deformation [ 45 ]. From the region of overlapping boundaries in 
Fig. 8 (a), it can be seen that the boundary between grains G7 and G3 
gradually expands inward, and HCP defects appear at the boundary 
between G7 and G8. As shown in Fig. 8 (b), a large number of initial 
defects are present in grain G8. As the impact progresses, the grain 
boundaries further spread. A comparison between 0ps and 6ps shows 
that the marked grain boundary first undergoes distortion, becomes flat, 
and then distorts again. During the impact process, atoms near the grain 
boundary diffuse from one grain into another, leading to the continued 
growth or shrinkage of grains. This phenomenon can be attributed to 
grain boundary sliding and atomic diffusion [ 24 ]. From Fig. 8 (d), it is 
observed that at 6 ps, dislocations are generated at the grain boundary of 
G7 and propagate inward in the form of dislocation loops.
With the propagation of impact waves, the tensile stress at the 
negative pressure region gradually increases. At 9ps, the tensile stress 
reaches its peak ( Fig. 8 (c)). Subsequently, at 10ps, due to the nucleation 
of voids at the grain boundaries, the tensile stress drops sharply, indi -
cating that the local tensile stress has exceeded the spall strength of the 
material. The dislocation density near the boundaries continues to in -
crease, and microcracks initiate at these locations and gradually extend 
along the boundaries. There are three microcrack sites (Void1, Void2, 
Void3) in the figure, all located at grain boundaries and in regions with 
relatively low dislocation density. These regions require higher energy 
for dislocation nucleation, leading to stress concentration and the for -
mation of microcracks along the boundaries. From the dislocation map 
in Fig. 8 (d), it is also evident that the dominant dislocations in γ -TiAl 
alloy are perfect dislocations, distributed inside grains and along grain 
boundaries parallel to the impact direction. Dislocations nucleate at the 
grain boundaries and propagate into the grains, with dislocation motion 
being a key mechanism for accommodating grain boundary mechanical 
processes.
4. Conclusions
In this work, impact simulations of polycrystalline γ -TiAl alloys were 
conducted at different initial temperatures, and the research focused on 
three stages: loading, unloading, and spalling. The following conclusions 
were drawn. 
1) The application temperature of γ -TiAl alloys should not exceed 780K. 
When the initial temperature exceeds 780K, both the plasticity and 
ductility of the alloy decrease significantly. Under impact loading, 
the σ
HEL 
decreases as the initial temperature increases, and a phase 
transformation from FCC → BCC → HCP occurs sequentially.
2) In γ -TiAl alloys, both SLIP and TWIP mechanisms influence the 
material ’ s plastic deformation. The sharp decrease in stair-rod 
dislocation and Hirth dislocation density above 780K is a signifi -
cant reason for the material ’ s insufficient strength at high 
temperatures.
3) Spalling occurs primarily at grain boundaries. The spalling strength 
generally decreases with increasing initial temperature, but this 
change is not significant below 780K. Above 780K, the energy 
required for dislocation nucleation inside the material increases, 
resulting in a decrease in dislocation density.
