ABSTRACT
The Mo-15W alloys with di ﬀerent HfC addition were prepared by using powder metallurgy technology. The
eﬀect of HfC particles on the mechanical properties and ablation resistance of Mo-15W-HfC alloys were also
investigated. The results indicated that the eﬀ ect of HfC particles on the Mo-W-HfC sintered alloys is very small,
but it can obviously improve the micro-hardness and the ultimate tensile strength of rolled alloys. The alloy
containing 1.0%wt. HfC in rolled condition delivers the highest ultimate tensile strength both at room
temperature (~ 1252 MPa) and at 1250 °C (~ 500 MPa), and shows the best overall performance. The ablation
tests show that the HfC particles will form an HfO
2 ﬁlm to prevent the Mo-15W matrix from further ablative
attack at the ablation temperature.
1. Introduction
In recent years, with the rapid development of the aerospace
technology, advanced energy and new materials, the rapidly increasing
demand for high performances in materials has placed no single
material in the position to ful ﬁll the rising requirements.
Molybdenum (Mo) have attracted wide interests owing to the high
electrical conductivity and strength at high temperature [1–2]. How-
ever, the drawbacks, i.e. low temperature brittleness, poor oxidation
resistance at high temperature has restricted their further extensive use
in industry. In order to enhance its mechanical properties at low
temperature and at high temperature, an important solution is adding
some additives as solid solution strengthening [3] and dispersion
strengthening [4–5].
Tungsten (W), as another refractory metal material, can form the
solid solution with molybdenum element in any proportion. Tungsten
and molybdenum belong to one family, so there are many similar
characteristics, such as high melting point, body-centered cubic (bcc)
structure and approximately equal atomic radius and lattice constant.
When the tungsten element is added to the molybdenum alloy, the
newly formed Mo-W solid solution could improve the hardness and
high temperature mechanical properties of alloy materials [6–7].
Another way to improve the properties of the alloys is the
realization of second phase particles in the matrix. Previous studies
have indicated that Ti, Ta, Zr carbide and rare earth oxides can
signiﬁcantly improve the mechanical properties of the alloys at low
temperature and at high temperature [8–14]. HfC has been added to the
alloys to enhance the comprehensive properties of the alloys owing to
the highest melting point (3900 °C) and a low thermal conductivity
(20 W/m·K) [15–19]. For example, Ra ﬀo [15] reported outstanding
mechanical properties of Mo-Hf-C alloys with various compositions.
Also, Wadsworth [16] found the creep strength and high temperature
tensile strength of Mo-based alloys improved with an increase in the
amount of HfC precipitation. Tsao et al. [19] investigated the micro-
structural evolution of Mo-10W-0.5HfC after vacuum annealing. How-
ever, in the open literature, there are little works about the mechanical
properties of Mo-W alloys adding HfC. There is also no study about the
use of Mo-W-HfC under high temperature conditions. Therefore, it is
signiﬁcant to investigate the e ﬀect of HfC addition on the mechanical
properties and ablation resistance of Mo-W alloys at elevated tempera-
ture.
Molybdenum-based refractory alloys are mainly prepared either by
powder metallurgy [7,20–21] or vacuum arc melting techniques
[22–24]. But, melting operation is a challenging job in view of large
variation in the melting temperatures of various components during the
vacuum arc melting process [11]. Therefore, in the present work, the
Mo-15W-HfC alloys were prepared by powder metallurgy route. The
aim of this work was to investigate e ﬀect of HfC particles on the
properties of materials at room temperature and high temperature. In
addition, the ablation resistance of Mo-15W-HfC alloys at elevated
temperature was also discussed.

2. Experimental details
2.1. Preparation of Mo-15W-HfC alloys
The Mo-15W-HfC alloys were prepared by using powder metallurgy
technology. The original elements were commercially available Mo, W
and HfC powders with 99% or higher purity. Fig. 1 shows the SEM
images of the Mo, W and HfC powders, respectively. It is obvious that
the size of the Mo and W powders varies in the range of 1 –5 μm, while
the size of HfC powders is less than 1 μm. In this study, a typical
experiment is carried out as follows: Firstly, the Mo and W powders
were mixed according to the mass ratio of 85:15, and then HfC powder
(0 wt.%, 0.5 wt.%, 1.0 wt.%, 1.5 wt.%) was added to the Mo-W mixed
powders to acquire the Mo-15W-HfC alloy powders. The actual contents
of the four kinds of Mo-15W-HfC alloy powders are shown in Table 1.
To make the mixture evenly mixed, the Mo-15W-HfC alloy powders
were mixed in a V-type mixing machine for 6 h. Secondly, the mixed
powders were compacted in a hydrostatic machine at the pressure of
200 MPa to obtain compacts. Soon afterwards, the compacts were
sintered at 2300 °C for 5 h in hydrogen condition by using medium-
frequency induction sintering furnace. After that, sintered alloys were
successfully obtained. Lastly, the hot-rolled alloys were prepared by hot
rolling deformation at about 1500 °C. The sintered alloys were named
as MW-S, MW-0.5HC-S, MW-1.0HC-S, MW-1.5HC-S, and the hot rolled
alloys were named as MW-R, MW-0.5HC-R, MW-1.0HC-R, MW-1.5HC-
R, respectively.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Microstructure of the Mo-15W-HfC alloy powders
XRD patterns of the four kinds of Mo-15W-HfC alloy powders with
diﬀerent HfC content are shown in Fig. 2. It is obvious that all alloy
powders show high di ﬀraction peak intensity and integrity peak shape.
The peaks with the highest intensity (2-theta = 40.5°, 58.6°, 73.7°)
represent to the matrix of the body centered cubic molybdenum. While
the lower intensity peaks can be identi ﬁed as body centered cubic W
and face centered cubic HfC. It is clear that, the peaks correspond to the
HfC become more and more obvious with the increase of HfC content.
In addition, no extra re ﬂection peaks corresponding to any impurity
phases are observed in the patterns, which means the oxides of hafnium
or molybdenum carbide that form as a result of interfacial reaction or
reaction with impurities did not form in the mixing powders process
and the alloy powders are high purity. In order to prove that W and HfC
are uniformly distributed in the Mo-based alloy powders, the element
mapping was carried out. The EDS elemental mapping images, as
shown in Fig. 3(b) –(d), clearly indicate that the W powder and HfC
powder are both homogeneously distributed in the Mo-based alloy
powders after mixing powders process.

Representative stress-strain curves for Mo-15W-HfC alloys are
shown in Fig. 7. Comparison of tensile properties for four sintered
alloys shows that four kinds of alloy samples belong to brittle fracture
and the ultimate tensile strength slight improve after the addition of
HfC. This is because the ultimate tensile strength for sintered alloys is
mainly related to the strength of the matrix. As shown in Fig. 7(b) and
Table 3, for rolled samples, when the HfC content rises, the ultimate
tensile strength ﬁrst increases to the maximum value of 1252 MPa at
1.0 wt%, and then reduces to 965 MPa due to the dispersion distribu-
tion of second phase after rolling, and the e ﬀect of dispersion
strengthening is obvious. In addition, as the HfC content increases,
the elongation rate of rolled alloys reduces, which suggested the
addition of HfC can cause the tensile ductility of Mo-15W alloy
decreased. When the adding amount is 1.5 wt.%, the alloy is brittle
fracture. It is attributed to be a fact that when the HfC content is more
than 1.0 wt.%, the cracks and defects caused by too many hard points
during the tensile process can result in brittle fracture of the alloy
materials. At the ideal condition, the compressive strength of the
material is only related to the applied load and the contact area.
However, high amount of porosity in this study was formed because of
the addition of HfC particles. So, the presence of porosity HfC also
aﬀects the compressive strength [29]. For the rolled alloys, the pores
are compressed and deformed ﬁrstly. Then, when the applied load
increase further, the crack will formed at the hole wall. Lastly, as the
force increases increased to some extent, the crack propagates along the
void rapidly throughout the sample. Hence, the failure fracture was
occurred at around porosity. As a result, the low tensile strength was
observed. All the rolled alloys show the low work-hardening rate,
which likely results in low resistance to plastic instability or necking
[8].

4. Conclusions
This study investigated the e ﬀects of HfC particles on the properties
of Mo-15W alloys at room temperature and elevated temperature. In
addition, the ablation resistance of Mo-15W-HfC alloys at elevated
temperature was also discussed. The main results obtained are as
follows:
1) For sintered alloys, the ultimate tensile strength and the micro-
hardness slight rise with the increase of HfC content.
2) For the rolled alloys, the addition of HfC can obviously improve the
micro-hardness and the ultimate tensile strength at both room
temperature and elevated temperature.
3) The fracture mechanism of Mo-15W-HfC rolled alloys are gradually
changed from ductile failure mode to transgranular quasi-cleavage
in brittle fracture mode with the increase of HfC content.
4) MW-1.0HC-R alloy balances the density, hardness, strength and
total elongation rate, delivers the best ultimate tensile strength at
both room temperature (1252 MPa) and elevated temperature
(about 500 MPa), and has the best comprehensive performance.
5) The microstructure of Mo-W alloys was changed by adding into HfC
particles. And the HfC particles oxidized at the ablation temperature
and formed HfO
2 pining particles. This ﬁlm can e ﬀectively prevent
the Mo-15W matrix from further ablative attack, and improve the
ablation resistance of the alloys.
