1. Introduction
With the rapid development of the aerospace industry, new energy 
vehicles, and other fields, a lightweight design has become a critical 
focus, demanding metal components with low weight and high strength. 
Among traditional lightweight alloys, aluminum alloys face limitations 
in achieving high mechanical properties because of their inherent 
characteristics. In addition, their density is relatively high compared 
with magnesium alloys. On the contrary, magnesium alloys exhibit poor 
plasticity because of their hexagonal close-packed (HCP) structure, high 
chemical activity, and inferior corrosion resistance [ 1 – 3 ]. These limi -
tations hinder the application of traditional lightweight alloys in certain 
specialized fields. High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have received consider -
able attention because of their exceptional performance and unique 
structural characteristics. The discovery of HEAs has expanded the 
possibilities for material development. At present, research on HEAs is 
primarily divided into two categories: transition group HEAs, which 
consist of transition elements (e.g., Al

for new lightweight materials, researchers have shifted their focus to 
lightweight high-entropy alloys (LHEAs), which utilize lightweight el -
ements such as Al, Ti, V, Li, Mg, and Si as the primary constituents.
To achieve lightweight while maintaining excellent mechanical 
properties, researchers have conducted extensive systematic studies. 
Initially, the study of HEAs faced challenges because of the vast variety 
of alloying elements and the complexity of their structures. Conse -
quently, early research predominantly focused on single-phase solid 
solutions (body-centered cubic [BCC], face-centered cubic [FCC], or 
HCP). Similarly, in the study of LHEAs, many scholars have concen -
trated on alloy systems with single-phase solid solutions. In general, the 
phase structure of LHEAs is dominated by the BCC phase [ 8 – 14 ]. 
However, a single BCC phase structure with uniform properties often 
fails to meet the requirements of certain specialized applications. Thus, 
researchers have modified the microstructure of alloys by incorporating 
additional elements, heat treatment, and thermomechanical processing. 
These methods promote the formation of a second phase, such as 
intermetallic compounds, which enhances the strengthening effects and 
improves the specific properties of LHEAs.

4.3. Wear mechanism
The primary wear behaviors of HEAs include adhesive wear, abrasive 
wear, oxidative wear, and fatigue wear [ 35 , 36 ]. The wear behavior of 
the Al
15
Ti
35
V
20
Nb
15
Zr
15 
LHEA, which was observed on its surface, is 
primarily characterized by adhesive and abrasive wear. In the as-cast 
state, the good plasticity of the alloy facilitates plastic deformation 
during wear, forming dense furrows and generating abundant abrasive 
debris. As the heat treatment temperature rises, the alloy ’ s strength 
improves, but its plasticity decreases, thereby reducing the extent of 
plastic deformation. Consequently, the furrows become less pro -
nounced, and the amount of abrasive debris decreases. In addition, the 
hardness difference between the BCC and HCP phases causes varying 
degrees of deformation, particularly as the volume fraction of the HCP 
phase increases. This hardness difference results in larger abrasive 
particles and non-uniform furrow depths [ 37 ]. The increased HCP phase 
content further increases the heterogeneity of the wear surface, leading 
to uneven deformation across different regions of the alloy. EDS analysis 
( Fig. 9 ) reveals the presence of oxygen on the worn surface, indicating 
the formation of a continuous oxide layer. Therefore, oxidative wear 
plays an important role in the wear process.

initial stage and decreasing in the later stage. In the early wear phase, 
the intense interactions between the wear surface and the counterpart 
ball induce remarkable plastic deformation, generating abrasive debris 
and shallow furrows. As wear progresses, the abrasive debris partici -
pates in the wear process, promoting the formation of a delamination 
layer. During this stage, the alloy experiences combined abrasive and 
adhesive wear. Prolonged friction generates localized heat, inducing 
oxidation of the wear surface and forming a protective oxide layer [ 38 ]. 
This oxide layer reduces further material removal, causing the friction 
coefficient to stabilize and the curve to reach a plateau.
