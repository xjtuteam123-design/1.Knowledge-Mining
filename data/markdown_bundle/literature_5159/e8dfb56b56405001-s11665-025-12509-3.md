ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Effect of Grain-Size Gradient on Strength and Plasticity
of Nano-crystalline CoCrFeMnNi High-Entropy Alloys
Mingjiang Chen, Zhaoyang Hou, Chao An, Kehao Nan, Danni Li, Lei Gao, and Xiaogang Zhao
Submitted: 30 May 2025 / Revised: 31 July 2025 / Accepted: 9 October 2025
The CoCrFeMnNi alloy is the one of the most extensively researched high-entropy alloys (HEAs) due to its
excellent plasticity even at cryogenic temperature, but it usually displays poor tensile strength which has
limited its practical application. In this work, the inﬂuence of grain-size gradient on the strength and
plasticity of nano-crystalline CoCrFeMnNi HEA is investigated by means of crystal plasticity ﬁnite element
method. The crystal plasticity model proposed by us based on the Hall–Petch relation and experimental
data could accurately predict the mechanical response of CoCrFeMnNi alloy. The gradient nano-grained
(GNG) CoCrFeMnNi HEA presents a strength–plasticity synergistic effect comparing with its homogeneous
nano-grained counterpart, and that with gradient rate n = 1 presents an optimal strength–plasticity syn-
ergy. The negative GNG structure exhibits the same mechanical response as that of the positive GNG
structure in the elastic and hardening stages, but the former has better performance in the resistance to
fracture in the post-fracture stage. The ﬁne grains in the GNG structures experience lower strain but higher
stress than those of central coarse grains. The linear spatial distribution of grain size in the GNG structures
displays the maximized strain and stress gradients, which results in the optimal strength–plasticity synergy.
Keywords CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy, crystal plasticity
ﬁnite element, gradient structure, strength–plasticity
synergy
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), pioneered by Cantor (Ref 1)
and Y eh (Ref 2) in the year of 2004, are an innovative class of
metallic materials containing ﬁve or more principal elements.
HEAs usually exhibit a strong tendency to form thermody-
namically stabilized single-phase solid solutions as their
remarkably high conﬁgurational entropy (Ref 3). Furthermore,
the unique atomic microstructures in HEAs bring about
superior mechanical properties, such as enhanced strength,
exceptional thermal softening resistance, excellent wear resis-
tance, and outstanding radiation damage tolerance (Ref 4-7);
thus, much attention focuses on them over the past two decades
(Ref 8).
During the past two decades, many HEA systems have been
reported, and the most extensively researched alloy is
CoCrFeMnNi due to its excellent plasticity even at cryogenic
temperature, outstanding fracture resistance, and low-tempera-
ture damage capacity (Ref 9-11). However, the CoCrFeMnNi
HEAs with coarse-grained structure (grain size d > 1 lm)
usually display poor tensile strength (200-600 MPa (Ref 12-
14)), which has limited its practical application. Grain reﬁne-
ment has been commonly employed to improve the yield
strength of materials, but it simultaneously accompanies the
reduction in the ductility because that the activity and slippage
of dislocations are constrained by grain boundaries (Ref 15). To
address this strength–ductility trade-off, the metallic materials
with gradient nano-grained (GNG) structure have attracted
much attention as their exceptional mechanical performance,
particularly their excellent strength–ductility synergy, superior
fatigue and fracture resistances (Ref 16-20). In recent years,
many GNG structures were fabricated in various metals and
alloys (Ref 21-24). Fang et al. (Ref 21) fabricated GNG copper
with a gradient variation in grain size (20-300 nm) by using a
surface mechanical attrition technology (SMA T). The tensile
experiments displayed that its yield strength enhances by
104.7% while maintaining its ductility. Wu et al. (Ref 22)
fabricated a gradient-structured IF steel sheet by using a surface
mechanical grinding technology (SMGT), achieving a gradient
variation in grain size (96 nm-5 lm). The mechanical exper-
iments demonstrated that an approximately 2.6 times in yield
strength compared to coarse-grained counterpart, without
compromising its uniform elongation. Meanwhile, the GNG
CoCrFeMnNi HEAs have been extensively fabricated by
physical post-processing, 3D printing or chemical electrodepo-
sition (Ref 25-31). Chen et al. (Ref 25) manufactured GNG
CoCrFeMnNi HEAs using laser surface heat treatment tech-
nology (LSHT). The mechanical testing demonstrated that the
tensile strength reaches 838 MPa accompanied by a uniform
elongation of 15.2%, producing an outstanding strength–
ductility synergy. Jiang et al. (Ref 26) fabricated GNG
CoCrFeMnNi HEAs with gradient variation in grain size
(140 nm-5 lm). The mechanical testing revealed showed a
yield strength of near 1 GPa with a uniform elongation of
9.5%. Karthik et al. (Ref 27) produced GNG CoCrFeMnNi
HEAs via laser powder bed fusion. The microstructural

Computational simulations have been extensively employed
to study the mechanical responses of GNG metals at different
spatial scales. Molecular dynamics (MD) method offers an
atomic-level understanding of the deformation mechanisms of
GNG metals (Ref 32-36), but the grain sizes involving in these
gradient structures reside within an inverse Hall–Petch regime
due to the constraints of computational power. Crystal plasticity
ﬁnite element method (CPFEM) has been extensively utilized
in investigating the mechanical response of GNG metals
covering nano- and mesoscales. Some simulations by CPFEM
(Ref 37-40) have demonstrated that within GNG structure, the
coarse-grained regions undergo large strain, whereas the ﬁne-
grained regions undergo large stress. However, the type of
grain-size gradient involving in these simulations is very few,
and our understanding on the grain-size gradient associated
with an optimal strength–ductility synergy remains restricted.
This study employs CPFEM to simulate the equiaxed tensile
deformation of various homogeneous nano-grained (HNG) and
GNG structures that are simulated in terms of CPFEM. The
gradient rate n is used to quantitatively describe the gradient
degree in spatial distribution of grain sizes. The dependences of
strength and plasticity with the gradient rate n are systemat-
ically discussed, and the gradient structure with an optimal
strength–plasticity synergy is revealed. Our simulation results
would valuable insights for experimentally tailoring the
microstructure of GNG alloys.

result comes from the small scale of grain size in these MD
simulations, in which the strength falls in inverse Hall–Petch
relation and presents ‘‘smaller is softer.’’
In contrast to the positive GNG structure where the grain
size tapers from the core toward the surface, the negative GNG
structure where the central ﬁne grains are completely wrapped
by the surface coarse grains has gained much interest recently
(Ref 30, 56-59), and the negative GNG CoCrFeMnNi HEAs
have been fabricated by laser surface heat treatment (Ref 30,
56). These studies have reported that the strength–plasticity
synergistic effect in negative GNG structures is on par with that
of positive GNG structures. Figure 7 shows structural models
of GNG CoCrFeMnNi HEAs with positive and negative
gradients (gradient rate n = 1). Except for the opposite grain-
size distribution, the structure model and simulation condition
of the two models are the same to each other. As shown in
Fig. 7(c), just as the positive gradient structure, the negative
gradient structure also shows gradient distinct in stress. The
surface ﬁne grains sustain stress than the central coarse grains.
Figure 8 presents the stress–strain responses of GNG
CoCrFeMnNi HEAs with both positive and negative gradients
under uniaxial tension. It can be found that the mechanical
response of the negative GNG sample is the same as that of the
positive GNG sample in the elastic and hardening stages, but
the stress in the positive GNG sample decreases remarkably in
the post-fracture stage ( > 16%) than that of the negative GNG
sample. The negative GNG sample exhibits comparable
fracture stress but greater fracture strain than the positive
GNG sample. This means that the negative GNG structure has
better strength–ductility synergy than the positive counterpart,
which has been observed in the experiment of GNG Fe (Ref 57)
but was not discussed in the paper.

4. Conclusion
In order to reveal the effect of grain-size gradient on the
strength and plasticity of GNG CoCrFeMnNi HEAs, the
equiaxed tensile deformation of various HNG and GNG
structures is simulated in terms of CPFEM. The gradient rate
n is used to quantitatively describe the gradient degree in spatial
distribution of grain sizes. The dependences of strength and
plasticity with the gradient rate n are systematically discussed,
and the gradient structure with an optimal synergy of the
strength and ductility is revealed. The main results are
summarized as follows.
(1) The crystal plasticity model proposed by us based on
the Hall–Petch relation and experimental data could
accurately predict the mechanical response of CoCr-
FeMnNi alloy in nano- and microscales.
(2) The GNG structure of CoCrFeMnNi HEA presents a
strength–plasticity synergistic effect comparing with its
HNG counterpart. The negative GNG structure exhibits
the same mechanical response as that of the positive
GNG structure in the elastic and hardening stages, but
achieves superior strength–ductility synergy..
(3) As the gradient rate n increases, the yield strength of
GNG CoCrFeMnNi HEA decreases while the uniform
elongation increases. But the reduction in the yield
strength becomes slow when the gradient rate reaches to
n = 1, and at the same time, the increase in the uniform
elongation becomes slow. The gradient structure presents
an optimal strength–plasticity synergy, as the grain sizes
meet a linear spatial distribution corresponding to the
gradient rate n =1 .
(4) The structure gradient in the GNG CoCrFeMnNi HEA
brings about the deformation gradient. The surface ﬁne-
grained regions in the GNG structure experience lower
strain but higher stress compared to the coarser grains in
the central zones. Among the various GNG structures,
that with gradient rate n = 1 exhibits maximized stress
and strain gradients along the direction of grain-size gra-
dient, which results in the optimal strength–plasticity
synergy.
