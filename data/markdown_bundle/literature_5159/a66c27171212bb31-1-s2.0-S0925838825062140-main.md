 A B S T R A C T
Al𝑥CoCrFeNi is an interesting high entropy alloy system with crystal structures varying from a fully face-
centered cubic (FCC) structure to a fully body-centered cubic (BCC) structure with increasing aluminum 
content. The mechanical behaviors of single-phase FCC Al0.3CoCrFeNi, major FCC phase + minor BCC phase 
Al0.5CoCrFeNi, and dual-phase near-eutectic Al0.7CoCrFeNi alloys subjected to different heat treatments, are 
systematically investigated under quasi-static and dynamic loading at 173−673 K. At a fixed temperature 
or strain rate, the yield strength increases with rising strain rate or decreasing temperature. An increase in 
the aluminum content correlates with enhanced strength in Al𝑥CoCrFeNi alloys, attributed to the formation 
of more FCC–BCC phase boundaries and precipitate strengthening by the intermetallic B2 phase. Deformation 
localization becomes more pronounced with increased aluminum content or at higher strain rates. Under quasi-
static loading, dislocations and deformation primarily accumulate at the FCC–BCC phase boundary within the 
FCC phase. As temperature and strain rate increase, more deformation is accommodated by the BCC phase. 
Compared to the solution-treated Al0.7CoCrFeNi alloy, the furnace-cooled alloy counterpart demonstrates a 
superior yield strength due to the presence of ordered L12 and θ precipitates. Microstructural analyses reveal 
multiple deformation mechanisms in the Al0.7CoCrFeNi alloys, including dislocations, stacking faults, immobile 
Lomer–Cottrell locks, and deformation twins.
1. Introduction
High entropy alloys (HEAs), with multi-element, equiatomic or 
near-equiatomic compositions, have been extensively investigated to 
explore their remarkable composition–structure–property space [1–4]. 
Among the various HEA systems, the Al𝑥CoCrFeNi alloys (𝑥 denotes the 
molar ratio) represent one of the most comprehensively investigated 
systems due to their relative abundance and cost-effectiveness of the 
constituent elements, as well as their superior mechanical perfor-
mance [5].
In the Al𝑥CoCrFeNi system, the crystal structures and mechanical 
properties of the HEAs exhibit marked variations with varying 𝑥, the 
Al content. The addition of Al to the CoCrFeNi system transforms 
the crystal structure from a single FCC phase to a duplex-phase (FCC 
+ BCC/B2) structure, and finally to a single BCC/B2 phase with

Although phases and microstructures for the Al𝑥CoCrFeNi system 
with certain compositions have been investigated [19–21], dynamic 
mechanical properties of this system remain underexplored [22–24]. Li 
et al. [25] reported a single phase FCC Al0.3CoCrFeNi HEA with excep-
tional strain hardening at high strain rate (1800 s−1) and remarkable 
resistance to shear localization. Wan et al. [26] reported that yield 
strength of a dendritic dual phase Al0.5CoCrFeNi alloy increases with 
increasing strain rate, reaching 671 MPa at approximately 2000 s−1. 
Gangireddy et al. [15] developed a hypo-eutectic Al0.7CoCrFeNi HEA 
with a lamellar structure. Due to the athermal nature of the numerous 
phase boundaries, the Al0.7CoCrFeNi alloy demonstrates excellent dy-
namic flow stresses and reduced strain rate sensitivity. Existing studies 
predominantly focus on the mechanical behavior of individual materi-
als, and a systematic investigation of the effect of aluminum content on 
dynamic mechanical properties is absent.
In the present study, we examine the influence of phase and mi-
crostructure on the dynamic mechanical properties of Al𝑥CoCrFeNi
alloys across wide ranges of strain rate and temperature. The ef-
fects of aluminum addition and subsequent heat treatment on the 
microstructure evolution and mechanical behavior are comprehen-
sively investigated, along with an in-depth analysis of the underly-
ing deformation mechanisms. Extensive microstructural characteriza-
tions of solutionized and furnace-cooled Al0.7 alloys elucidate synergis-
tic hetero-deformation activities, including dislocation glide, stacking 
faults, Lomer–Cottrell locks, and nanotwins. A modified Johnson–
Cook–Cowper–Symonds (JC–CS) constitutive model is developed and 
can accurately capture plastic response of solution-treated and furnace-
cooled Al0.7CoCrFeNi HEAs.
2. Materials and experiments
2.1. Materials and characterization
The as-cast alloy plates with a nominal composition of Al𝑥CoCrFeNi
are provided by Beijing Yanbang New Materials Co. Ltd. Here, 𝑥
denotes molar ratio, and is equal to 0.3, 0.5 and 0.7; the corresponding 
Al𝑥CoCrFeNi alloys are referred to as Al0.3, Al0.5 and Al0.7, respectively. 
Three as-cast ingots with dimensions of 20 mm × 20 mm × 50 mm 
are then solution-treated at 1523 K for 12 h (ST), followed by rapid 
quenching in water. For comparison, an additional Al0.7CoCrFeNi plate 
is annealed at 1573 K for 12 h and then furnace-cooled (FC). All heat 
treatments are carried out in an Ar atmosphere.
Initial and postmortem Al𝑥CoCrFeNi alloys are analyzed using var-
ious materials characterization techniques, including X-ray diffrac-
tion (XRD), electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), transmission elec-
tron microscopy (TEM), and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS).

Empyrean X-ray diffractometer in the scattering angle (2𝜃) range of 25◦
−100◦ with Cu-K𝛼 radiation (0.15405-nm wavelength). EBSD analysis 
is performed with an FEI Quanta 250 FEG-SEM equipped with EDS 
detectors for elemental mapping. The EBSD data are analyzed using 
the HKL Channel 5 orientation imaging microscopy software. A critical 
misorientation angle of 2◦ is used to identify grain boundaries in the 
orientation maps. Low angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) and high angle 
grain boundaries (HAGBs) are classified by misorientations of 2◦ −15◦
and >15◦, respectively. TEM characterization is done with an FEI

wire electrical discharge machining (WEDM); the region of interest 
is located in the center of the cross-section. Prior to EBSD and TEM 
analysis, all specimens undergo grinding, mechanical polishing, and

2.2. Compression tests
Cylindrical specimens measuring 5 mm in diameter and 5 mm in 
length are employed for quasi-static and dynamic compression tests. 
Quasi-static compression tests are carried out utilizing a material test-
ing system (MTS) at a strain rate of 0.001 s−1. For dynamic uniaxial 
compression testing, a split Hopkinson pressure bar (SHPB) system is 
employed, with strain rates approximately at 1000, 2000, and 3000 s−1, 
and temperatures set at 173, 298, and 693 K. The SHPB system com-
prises a gas storage cylinder, a striker (1), an incident bar (2), a 
transmission bar (3), and strain gauges (4), as illustrated in Fig. 1. All 
the bars and the striker are made of high-strength steel with a diameter 
of 12 mm. For the heating or cryogenic experiments, the cylindrical 
SHPB specimens (6) are positioned between the incident and trans-
mission bars in a heating/cooling chamber (5) and kept at the desired 
temperature for at least 5 min before the compression loading. Proper 
alignment and surface lubrication are ensured to minimize friction 
effects and maintain measurement integrity. Specific experiments are 
performed using a stop ring to constrain the strain to a predetermined 
value (15% strain) for subsequent microstructure characterization.
3. Results
3.1. Microstructure of the as-processed alloys
The XRD curves of the as-processed Al𝑥CoCrFeNi after different 
heat treatments are presented in Fig. 2. For the solution-treated (ST) 
Al0.3 alloy, only the FCC phase is observed. In the ST Al0.5 alloy, a 
faint {110}BCC peak is observed in proximity to the {111}FCC peak, 
suggesting the presence of a minor BCC/B2 phase [26]. At a higher 
Al content, the ST and FC Al0.7 alloys exhibit an increasing relative 
intensity of the BCC/B2 peaks, suggesting an increase in the volume 
fraction of the BCC/B2 phase. The XRD curves also show a {110}B2
superlattice reflection for Al0.7. The Al0.7 alloys has a double-BCC 
structure, comprising a disordered BCC phase (the A2 structure) and 
an ordered BCC phase (the B2 structure) [9].
Fig. 3 presents the EBSD characterization of the as-processed
Al𝑥CoCrFeNi alloys. The ST Al0.3 consists of a single FCC phase with 
coarse grains of ∼139.8 μ m. The ST Al0.5 displays a characteristic den-
dritic structure, comprising dendritic FCC and interdendritic BCC/B2 
regions; their grain sizes are about 88.0 μm and 10.9 μm , respectively. 
For the ST and FC Al0.7 alloys, the inverse pole figure (IPF) maps (Fig. 
3g and j) reveal columnar dendritic grains with increased interdendritic 
area fraction; the BCC/B2 phase fraction and grain size of the FC Al0.7
are considerably larger than those of the ST Al0.7 alloy. Annealing twins 
are observed in both the FCC and BCC/B2 phases of the ST Al0.7 alloy 
(Fig. 3i), whereas they are present only in the FCC phase in the FC 
Al0.7 (Fig. 3l). The phase fractions and grain sizes of Al𝑥CoCrFeNi after 
different heat treatments are summarized in Table 1.

3.4. Constitutive modeling
An accurate constitutive model is essential for simulating deforma-
tion behavior with high fidelity and elucidating the underlying mech-
anisms of materials. Existing models have been employed to charac-
terize the deformation of Al𝑥CoCrFeNi in our prior research, including 
Al0.3 [28] and Al0.5 [26]. In this study, we adopt a modified Johnson–
Cook (JC) constitutive model to predict the plastic deformation of the 
ST and FC Al0.7 alloys.
The JC constitutive model is widely used for describing the defor-
mation of metals under large strains, high strain rates, and varying 
temperatures. Accordingly, the equivalent flow stress 𝜎f is expressed 
as [35]:

where 𝐴, 𝐵, 𝐶, 𝑛 and 𝑚 are material constants for describing strain 
hardening, and strain rate and temperature sensitivities. 𝜀p is equiva-

and ̇ 𝜀0 are current and reference strain rate. 𝑇 ∗ = ( 𝑇 − 𝑇0)∕(𝑇m − 𝑇0)

4. Discussion
4.1. Effect of Al addition
Al is known to be a stabilizer of the BCC phase in transition 
metal-based HEAs [5,40]. The addition of Al to the CoCrFeNi sys-
tem results in the transformation from a single-phase FCC structure 
to an FCC+BCC dual-phase structure [13,41]. In this study, the ST 
Al𝑥CoCrFeNi alloys exhibit a sequential transformation in its crystal 
structure as the aluminum content (x) increases, specifically, from a 
single FCC phase structure for Al0.3, to a combination of major FCC 
+ minor BCC (∼6%) dual-phase structure for Al0.5, and finally to an 
FCC+BCC dual-phase structure, with considerably increased BCC phase 
fraction (approximately 40%) for Al0.7 (Fig. 3). The empirical valence 
electron concentration (VEC) rule effectively predicts phase stability in 
HEAs [42–45]. A high-throughput CALPHAD study on the Al𝑥CoCrFeNi
system shows that the primary phase is FCC for 𝑥 < 0.45, while a 
mixture of FCC and BCC forms when 0.45 < 𝑥 < 1 [46], consistent with 
our experimental observations. Additionally, the elemental segregation 
behaviors between the FCC and BCC/B2 phases in Al0.5 [26] and Al0.7
(Fig. A.1) alloys can be explained by the Hume–Rothery rules [5,47]: 
elements with similar atomic sizes and compatible crystal structures 
exhibit high mutual solubility. In the Al𝑥CoCrFeNi HEA system, the 
Al–Ni pair has the smallest atomic radius difference compared to the 
Ni–Cr/Fe and Al–Co/Cr/Fe pairs. Thermodynamically, the Al–Ni pair 
in the BCC structure has a lower mixing enthalpy than in the FCC 
structure, which promotes BCC phase stability [40,48,49]. 
Both in quasi-static and dynamic strain rate regimes, the flow stress 
of Al𝑥CoCrFeNi alloys increases with increasing Al content (Fig. 6). 
Al0.7 with the dual phase FCC+BCC microstructure shows much higher 
strength than single-FCC-phase Al0.3 (Table 2). Generally, the BCC/B2 
phase in Al𝑥CoCrFeNi is much harder than the FCC phase [6,7,30]. 
A nano-mechanical testing on individual phase nanopillars of Al0.7
demonstrated that the compressive strength of the BCC phase is approx-
imately 1.8 times that of the FCC phase [50]. Plastic deformation tends 
to initiate earlier in the FCC phase than in the BCC/B2 phases, resulting 
in a mismatch in deformation behavior across the phase boundaries. 
This strain incompatibility promotes the formation of heterogeneous 
deformation fields and facilitates the accumulation of internal stresses 
at phase boundaries [33]. These stresses are partially accommodated by 
the generation of higher-density geometrically necessary dislocations 
(GNDs) at the interfaces [51]. Additionally, the phase boundaries act 
as effective obstacles to dislocation glide, where dislocations are im-
peded by a strong pinning effect. This phenomenon is corroborated 
by the KAM maps shown in Figs. 8 and 9. Regions with high KAM 
values are located around the phase boundaries, indicating significant 
dislocation pile-ups. Moreover, the observed rise in average KAM values 
with increasing Al content (Fig. A.3) suggests a corresponding increase 
of the FCC and BCC/B2 phase interfaces. The near-eutectic lamellar 
microstructure, characterized by a high density of phase boundaries 
(Fig. 3g), significantly contributes to the enhanced strength of the 
Al0.7 alloy. In addition, the FCC and B2 phases exhibit semi-coherent

4.2. Deformation mechanisms of solution-treated and furnace-cooled Al0.7
Different heat treatment processes induce hierarchical structural 
variations in the Al0.7 alloys. Compared to the ST Al0.7, the anneal-
ing and furnace cooling treatment promotes grain coarsening, phase 
transformation, and the formation of multiple intermediate phases in 
the FC Al0.7 alloys. EBSD characterization (Fig. 3h and k) exhibits 
interweaving needle-like or coarse, wall-shaped, dual phase FCC+BCC 
structure for the ST and FC Al0.7, respectively. In contrast, annealing 
twins are observed in the FCC and BCC phases of the ST Al0.7 (Fig. 3i), 
but only in the BCC phase of the FC Al0.7 (Fig. 3l).
The multiscale variations in microstructure significantly affect the 
deformation mechanisms and mechanical properties. In the Al0.7 al-
loys, the hetero-lamellar architecture facilitates considerable plastic 
deformation. Intense stress interactions near the FCC/BCC interfaces 
lead to the accumulation of back stress within the FCC phase, which 
subsequently generates substantial forward stress in the adjacent BCC 
phase [52], thereby activating its plastic deformation (Fig. 10b, e, 
h). In contrast, the brief duration of impact loading limits dislocation 
propagation in the coarsened FCC matrix. Consequently, the high-KAM 
regions are localized near the phase boundaries at various temperatures 
(Fig. 11b, e, h). The FCC matrix interior contains abundant low-KAM 
regions, resulting in reduced 𝜁 FCC
av values (Fig. 11d–f).
As for deformation mechanisms, abundant SFs, LC locks, and even 
nanotwins are observed in ST and FC the Al0.7 alloys (Fig. 12). Given 
the relatively high stacking fault energy (SFE; ∼191 mJ/m2) [50], the 
presence of such high-density two-dimensional (2D) defects, particu-
larly deformation twins, is unexpected [23,53], as further illustrated 
in Appendix A.6. The abnormal twinning observed in Al0.7 alloys can 
be attributed to three factors. First, the hetero-lamellar architecture 
leads to a significant accumulation of dislocations within the FCC 
lamellae near the phase boundaries (Fig. 10b, e, h), resulting in pro-
nounced strain gradients. The high hetero-deformation-induced (HDI) 
stress generated during deformation creates intense stress concentra-
tions around the abundant hetero-lamellar zones [20]. Additionally, 
dynamic compression can induce new deformation mechanisms com-
pared to quasi-static loading conditions [23,53]. Gangireddy et al. 
reported that a dual-phase Al0.7 shows no twinning after quasi-static 
deformation but exhibits nano-scale twins following dynamic defor-
mation [15]. The HDI stress in our Al0.7 alloy is further enhanced 
by the dense accumulation of GNDs around phase boundaries under 
high strain rate compression [15]. Moreover, B2 precipitates in the 
FCC–BCC–B2 triplex microstructure of Al0.7 can act as Eshelby-type 
inclusions [54], locally elevating stress levels during deformation to 
trigger twinning [55]. For these reasons, the local stress levels become 
sufficiently high to reach the ultrahigh activation stress required for 
twinning in the FCC phase of the Al0.7 alloy.
Furthermore, the increased density of phase boundaries, along with 
the precipitation of the ordered L12 (Fig. 5f and g) and θ (Fig. 5h and 
i) phases in the FCC matrix, contribute to the enhanced yield strength 
of the FC Al0.7 alloy.

Quasi-static and dynamic compression experiments are conducted 
on Al𝑥CoCrFeNi HEAs with varying aluminum content (𝑥 = 0.3, 0.5, 
and 0.7). The addition of Al, along with subsequent heat treatments, 
significantly affects the microstructure, dynamic mechanical behavior, 
and deformation mechanisms, as summarized below:
