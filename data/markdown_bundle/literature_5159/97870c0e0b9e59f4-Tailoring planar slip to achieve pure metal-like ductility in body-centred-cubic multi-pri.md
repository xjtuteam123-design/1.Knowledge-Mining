Uniform tensile ductility (UTD) is crucial for the forming/machining 
capabilities of structural materials. Normally, planar-slip induced 
narrow deformation bands localize the plastic strains and hence hamper 
UTD, particularly in body-centred-cubic (bcc) multi-principal element 
high-entropy alloys (HEAs), which generally exhibit early necking 
(UTD < 5%). Here we demonstrate a strategy to tailor the planar-slip bands 
in a Ti-Zr-V-Nb-Al bcc HEA, achieving a 25% UTD together with nearly 50% 
elongation-to-failure (approaching a ductile elemental metal), while 
offering gigapascal yield strength. The HEA composition is designed not 
only to enhance the B2-like local chemical order (LCO), seeding sites to 
disperse planar slip, but also to generate excess lattice distortion upon 
deformation-induced LCO destruction, which promotes elastic strains 
and dislocation debris to cause dynamic hardening. This encourages 
second-generation planar-slip bands to branch out from first-generation 
bands, effectively spreading the plastic flow to permeate the sample volume. 
Moreover, the profuse bands frequently intersect to sustain adequate 
work-hardening rate (WHR) to large strains. Our strategy showcases 
the tuning of plastic flow dynamics that turns an otherwise-undesirable 
deformation mode to our advantage, enabling an unusual synergy of yield 
strength and UTD for bcc HEAs.
Multi-principal element high/medium-entropy alloys (H/MEAs) have 
substantially expanded the alloy design repertoire1,2 and the realm of 
possibilities for mechanical3,4 or functional performance5. The recent 
focus in this community has shifted towards body-centred-cubic (bcc) 
HEAs, as these are often based on refractory metals and may offer supe-
rior properties for high-temperature applications4. While the bcc HEAs 
are characterized by a gigapascal yield strength, they generally have 
a low uniform tensile ductility (UTD) due to early onset of necking, 
limiting their processing and shaping potentials6.
Among the microstructural features that can be tuned to change 
the ductility of H/MEAs, local chemical order (LCO, its definition and 
differences from intermetallic precipitates are described in Supple -
mentary Note 1) is one that has not received much attention. For H/
MEAs, what has been debated more often is whether LCOs can elevate 
the strength7–12, as the rugged landscape caused by LCOs may retard 
dislocation motion9,10,13 or even cause dislocation trapping 14,15. With 
regard to ductility effects of LCOs16, the results have been inconclusive, 
as H/MEAs with LCOs can show a ductility either better or worse than

by Zr would offset each other, and the atomic packing would be more 
‘comfortable’ , indicating that in the presence of increased LCOs, lattice 
distortion would be reduced.
Having established the LCO increase via ageing, we show tensile 
test results of the WQ- and aged-T50s at room temperature to illustrate 
the remarkable effect of LCOs on mechanical properties. Figure 2a 
compares the engineering stress–strain curves of these two HEAs. The 
yield strength increases from 870 MPa for the WQ-T50 to 980 MPa for 
the aged-T50. This strength elevation is expected, considering that 
the enhanced LCOs would add difficulties against dislocation move-
ment7,8,11. Moreover, the ageing or LCO also brings forth a dramatic 
ductility improvement: the UTD reaches 24.7%, doubling that of 12.7% 
for the WQ-T50. The elongation-to-failure also rises to 46.7%, much bet-
ter than that of roughly 30% for the WQ-T50. The strain measurements 
were further confirmed using in situ digital image-correlation tensile 
tests (Supplementary Fig. 12). This outstanding ductility increase is 
rooted in the work-hardening ability of the alloys. T o see this, Fig. 2b 
presents the WHR of the HEAs as a function of true strain. Different from 
the monotonic decrease of WHR in the WQ-T50 after yielding, the WHR 
of the aged-T50 continuously increases across a rather wide true strain 
range from 2 to 17.5%. The WHR remains sufficiently high to ward off 
the plastic instability to delay necking (Fig. 2b). As such, the aged-T50 
achieves a remarkable strength–ductility synergy: the yield strength 
is about 1 GPa, competitive with high-strength steels; yet the UTD and 
elongation-to-failure are very impressive as well, as high as 24.7 and 
46.7%, respectively, on the par with that of ductile elemental metals (see 
Supplementary Fig. 13 for a comparison with individual constituents). 
As compared in Fig. 2c,d, the unusual strength–ductility combination 
sets the new HEAs apart from the previous bcc HEAs 6,20,21,24,27,32–42 and 
conventional bcc-based alloys, including maraging, dual-phase and 
low-carbon steels; β-Ti alloys; V alloys and Nb alloys.
Next, we explain the mechanism underlying the remarkable duc-
tility achieved in this HEA despite the gigapascal yield strength. The 
dynamic increase of WHR to large strains (Fig. 2b) for the aged-T50 is 
apparently the key to effectively delay necking. Previously observed 
dynamic increase of WHR requires deformation-induced twinning 43 
or martensitic transformation44, neither of which was detected in our 
EBSD or SXRD examinations (Supplementary Fig. 14). Instead, disloca-
tion activities dominate the plastic deformation of T50s. Therefore, 
a detailed analysis of the dislocation slip mode and interaction and 
organization is in order.

knowledge would not suggest LCOs as a way to go for improving ductil-
ity, as the LCOs would be gradually destroyed when dislocations repeat-
edly glide across them, reducing the glide resistance for the subsequent 
dislocations. This ‘glide plane softening’ promotes the planar-slip 
mode17,18. Dislocations seldom cross-slip out of the planar-slip bands 
(used interchangeably with ‘dislocation channels’ hereafter), result-
ing in fast saturation in dislocation accumulation inside the bands. 
This leads to a plunging work-hardening rate (WHR) and high stress 
concentrations, and the UTD can be reduced as a result8,19. As the plas-
tic deformation is localized in narrow high-density dislocation walls 
(HDDWs), the UTD and elongation-to-failure of bcc HEAs are usually 
limited to no more than 5 and 20% (refs. 20,21), respectively, drastically 
lower than the roughly 30 and 50% known for their constituent elemen-
tal metals22,23. It is uncertain under what circumstances the adverse 
role of the LCO-instigated planar-slip bands could be turned around, 
to the point that they help mediate rather than threaten ductility.
Here, we present a LCO tailoring strategy to demonstrate that the 
above is indeed plausible. In terms of the possible effects of solute con-
centration and chemistry, we take a cue from the recent observations 
that purposely increased degree of compositional fluctuation
9,14, and 
solute (O)–solvent (Ti, Zr) complexes24, could promote local double 
cross-slip of dislocations to facilitate plastic flow, as well as a new 
report in which the development of planar-slip bands is influenced 
by the interaction between solutes (Al and O together) added into the 
host metal (Ti)19. We propose that LCOs, featuring weak and variable 
chemical order (Supplementary Note 1), can also be exploited to exert 
large and positive effects on ductility. Our approach is to design the bcc 
HEAs to contain elemental species that have large disparity not only 
in chemical affinity to encourage LCO regions all over the place25, but 
also large atomic-size mismatch to increase lattice distortion when 
LCOs dissociate towards random solution (a schematic is shown in 
Supplementary Fig. 1, to be discussed later). We use the evolution 
and interplay of the LCO/distortion during plastic deformation to 
dynamically harden the first-generation planar-slip bands, from which 
second-generation bands readily branch out in undeformed regions 
containing widespread LCOs. This proliferation involves more slip 
systems and interband volume into plastic flow. Taken together, these 
bring interesting outcomes when it comes to the stable strain harden-
ing and stress–strain behaviour in tensile deformation.
In this Article, we chose Ti50Zr18Nb15V12Al5 (T50) HEAs as the model 
alloys26,27, as two of the components, Zr and Al, show both a large 
atomic-size mismatch and a large negative mixing enthalpy (Sup -
plementary Table 1). These features were used to afford appreciable 
degrees of LCO and lattice distortion. The T50 HEAs were recrystallized 
at 700 °C followed by water quenching (WQ-T50). As ageing treatment 
is expected to enhance the LCOs7, parts of WQ-T50s were aged at 300 °C 
for 7 days followed by furnace cooling (aged-T50). Synchrotron X-ray 
diffraction (SXRD) spectra (Fig. 1a) and electron back-scattered dif -
fraction (EBSD) images (Supplementary Fig. 2) prove that both the 
WQ- and aged-T50s are single-phase bcc solid-solutions with equiaxed 
grains with a 16.5 μm mean size. Pair-distribution-function results (Sup-
plementary Fig. 3) revealed the coordination environment differences 
between the WQ- and aged-T50s, possibly due to the LCO increase. As 
expected from the strengthening effects
7,8,11,28 of LCOs, nanoindenta-
tion tests showed that the aged-T50 is 9% harder than the WQ-T50 
(Supplementary Fig. 4).

diffraction (SAED) patterns and lattice images were acquired under 
[110] zone axis in aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron 
microscope (STEM) experiments. As shown in Fig. 1b, diffuse extra 
discs have been observed at {001} and {111} positions in the SAED of 
aged-T50s, indicating the additional or increased chemical order.

of straining (Fig. 3a), planar-slip bands are also observed. Dislocations 
line up in well-defined channels (Supplementary Fig. 17). At 5% strain 
(Fig. 3b), frequent cross-slips appeared in the aged-T50. Different from 
the WQ-T50, before dislocation tangling becomes prevalent inside/
around the channels, dislocations glide far away from the ‘primary’ 
(first-generation) channels to open up a series of second-generation 
channels (the dislocations in the second-generation channels share the 
same Burgers vectors with the corresponding primary channels; Sup-
plementary Fig. 18). This easy proliferation of second-generation chan-
nels (Fig. 3b,c) is partly because the widespread LCOs in the regions 
between the primary channels provide ample opportunities for the 
occurrence of glide plane softening all over the volume. Due to these 
easier dislocation slip paths, the WHR (red curve) starts out lower than 
the blue curve in Fig. 2b. Nevertheless, the bands can be self-hardened, 
when the lattice distortion induced by LCO destruction becomes suffi-
ciently increased to impose undulating strains that hamper dislocation 
passage (as will be explained in Fig. 4). This hardening inside the bands 
is partly the reason dislocations cross-slip away from the bands rather 
than keep accumulating/tangling inside. Different from the WQ-T50 
(Supplementary Fig. 15), these widespread second-generation channels 
formed by ‘overflown’ dislocations make better use of the undeformed 
volume, effectively promoting the deformation delocalization into the 
regions between the already-formed channels.
Note that the above scenario introduces a new mechanism to 
dynamically elevate WHR. This is demonstrated in Fig. 3d,e at 9% strain: 
the proliferation of second-generation channels provides opportuni-
ties for many of them to run into one another. In and around their 
intersection regions, many dislocation tangles (Fig. 3d) and pile-ups 
(Fig. 3e) form, which are rare in the WQ-T50 or other bcc alloys. It is 
these profuse channel intersections that sow dislocation tangles all 
over the regions well beyond the primary channels, causing mutual 
band hindrance and consequently a continuously rising WHR at strains 
more than 9% (Fig. 2b). At strains more than 15% (Fig. 3f–h), while each 
HDDW may look not so different from those in WQ-T50 (Supplementary 
Fig. 15d,e), the regions between the HDDWs are far more permeated 
with dislocation tangles thanks to the profuse bands that proliferate 
dislocation tangles all over the place.
Figure 3i summarizes the area fraction (F ) of the severe defor-
mation zones of the two HEAs at various strains. For the WQ-T50s, the 
F, mainly contributed by HDDWs, rapidly saturates to roughly 30% 
at 10% strain. When the strain further increases, the further increase 
of F is small and localized plastic flow becomes excessive such that 
necking sets in (Fig. 2a). When the sample failures, the F is only roughly 
40%. For the aged-T50s, however, the widespread bands promote 
the F, gradually increasing to over 70% at 24.7% UTD. When the 
sample fails, the F can be over 80%, twice as that in the failed WQ-T50s. 
In a nutshell, spread-out slip-band events make deformation much 
more homogenous, significantly prolonging UTD.

A molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was used to shed light on 
the interplay between lattice distortion and LCOs, and evaluate its effect 
on dislocation propagation. A TiZr0.65Nb0.28 bcc alloy, for which intera-
tomic potentials are available46, was prepared as a model to mimic our 
T50. The MD model started out as a random solution, and was subse-
quently aged at different temperatures (600, 1,000 and 1,400 K). We 
observe in Fig. 4e that the lower the ageing temperatures, the higher 
the LCO degree and the lower the lattice distortion degree, reminiscent 
of the observations in Fig. 1. Again, this trend arises because chemical 
ordering prefers the intimate mixing of large (Zr) and small (Ti) atoms 
(analogous to the Zr–Al LCO in our T50s). Note that, as the Zr–Al affinity 
is higher than Zr–Ti (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 1), 
LCO formation is expected to be much easier in T50s than in the 
TiZr0.65Nb0.28 model. Moreover, as the Al atoms are smaller than the Ti 
atoms (Supplementary Fig. 11), the negative correlation between LCO 
and lattice distortion (Fig. 4e) would be more significant in the T50 HEAs.
A screw dislocation was placed in the centre of each sample and 
the dislocation velocity (v) at 300 K is calculated under various shear 
stresses (τ ) to evaluate the influence of LCO and lattice distortion 
on the glide resistance. The atomic configuration and relation of v-τ 
are documented in Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22, respectively. The 
drag coefficient of dislocation motion (Fig. 4f), determined from the 
inverse of the slope in the linear regime of v-τ in Supplementary Fig. 22, 
shows a non-monotonic behaviour with a valley point (minimum 
around MC1400K) in the TiZr0.65Nb0.28 model. When the LCO degree 
goes higher than the valley point (for example, MC1400K to MC600K), 
the LCO strengthening overpowers the softening due to the lattice 
distortion reduction, showing an apparent LCO strengthening effect. 
When the LCOs were reduced to near zero (for example, MC1400K to 
random), the softening due to the LCO destruction is compensated by 
the strengthening due to the lattice distortion increase, giving rise to an 
apparent distortion strengthening effect. The trend appears consistent 
with what has been observed in our experimental alloys.
By comparing the mechanical behaviours of the WQ-T50s, 
aged-T50s and two additional T50 alloys (Supplementary Fig. 23) 
whose LCO degrees are between the WQ and aged-T50s, we found 
that the valley point of T50 system is around the WQ-T50s. With the 
LCO addition, the LCO strengthening effect becomes dominant in the 
aged-T50s, leading to the increase of yield strength (Fig. 2a). During 
the channel generation in the aged-T50s, the LCO strengthening effect 
gradually diminishes, causing the low WHR at strains of 2 to 9% (Fig. 2b).

that dislocation motion in bcc metals and alloys creates dislocation

order. Each ordering species (Zr and Al) has a size significantly deviat-
ing from the alloy average. This way, when LCO forms, the preference of 
these species as nearest neighbours offsets the strain induced by each 
species. When these ordering agents are randomized on LCO destruc-
tion, each of them adds extra lattice distortion (schematic in Supple-
mentary Fig. 1 with atomic radius data in Supplementary Fig. 11). As 
such, lattice distortion would become more and more significant in the 
channels when dislocations repeatedly glide across these LCOs. This, 
together with accumulating dislocation debris (also enhanced by the 
larger lattice distortion47), rehardens the primary channels by increas-
ing the dislocation glide resistance. Note that this strain hardening is 
not cancelled out by the LCO destruction induced softening, as the 
former and the latter do not synchronously arise in a linear fashion at 
the same rate; the details as to why so remain to be explored in future. 
This stage of net dynamic hardening entails cross-slips to involve new 
glide planes, developing into second-generation channels between 
the primary channels. The proliferation of channels effectively spread 
strains into not-yet-deformed regions, promoting the deformation 
delocalization. Moreover, the prolific channels meet frequently and 
breed close-knit dislocation tangles around the intersections to hinder 
dislocation propagation. All of these, gradually permeating the 
entire sample space, sustains a rising WHR to large strains (red curve 
in Fig. 2b ). Such a tendency towards strain delocalization is much 
less favourable in traditional alloys 19. In particular, for conventional 
precipitates with fully developed (in terms of both the degree and 
extent) chemical order, the interplay between chemical order and 
lattice distortion would not be able to exert such a desirable impact at 
an adequate level. The reasons are discussed in Supplementary Note 5 
and Supplementary Fig. 28.
HEAs dominated by an fcc phase48,49 are relatively easier to achieve 
20% UTD with gigapascal yield strength, as abundant easy slip and 
twinning systems are activated to induce dislocation intersections 
and reactions, readily accumulating dislocations during deforma -
tion to offer high WHR and ductility. By contrast, these mechanisms 
are far less potent in single-phase bcc-structured HEAs, making the 
synergy of 24.7% UTD together with gigapascal yield strength in the 
aged-T50 a milestone achievement. An extra advantage of the T50 is 
the relatively low density (roughly 5.5 g·cm−3) due to the high content 
of lightweight components (Ti, Zr, V, Al). The specific yield strength 
(roughly 180 MPa·cm3·g−1) is as high as commercial Ti alloys, while the 
UTD is two to four times better.
As a final note, our strategy is in a sense akin to the shear band 
proliferation pursued in the metallic glass community to improve 
plasticity50. There also, mild and small shear bands in large numbers 
are preferred to provide controlled plastic flow, whereas major or 
severe shear bands run away to ruin ductility. In fact, the band prolif-
eration strategy is far more suited for HEA crystals because (1) HEAs 
have well-defined slip planes and systems and observable and tune-
able LCOs, affording ample sites for band proliferation; and (2) our 
planar bands are not that severe and inherently have a microstructural 
work-hardening capability, such that they do not rapidly run out of 
stability to trigger a premature catastrophe. These two factors are 
absent for the mature shear bands in metallic glasses. As such, our 
strategy is particularly effective in deformation delocalization, such 
that even though bcc HEAs yield at gigapascal stresses, their tensile 
elongation can still be as high as that normally expected for a pure 
metal (see comparison with the constituent metals in the T50 HEA, 
Supplementary Fig. 13).

Methods
Material preparation
Ti50Zr18Nb15V12Al5 (T50) ingots with the size of Φ60 × 60 mm3 were pre-
pared by vacuum induction melting a mixture of pure metals (purity 
greater than 99.9 wt%). The induction melting chamber was evacu -
ated to a pressure lower than 2.5 × 10−3 Pa. The ingots were remelted 
at least four times to ensure chemical homogeneity. All cast ingots 
were homogenized at 1,000 °C for 2 hours followed by WQ. After that, 
ingots were cut to plates with dimensions 10 × 15 × 60 mm3, followed by 
cold rolling with an 80% thickness reduction. These cold-rolled plates 
were then recrystallization-annealed at 700 °C for 1 hour followed by 
WQ (WQ-T50 HEAs). Part of the WQ-T50 HEAs were aged at 300 °C for 
7 days to increase LCOs, followed by furnace cooling (aged-T50 HEAs).
Mechanical property measurements
Room-temperature tensile tests (Fig. 2) were performed using an MTS 
Landmark testing machine operating at a strain rate of 5 × 10 −4 s−1. 
Dog-bone-shaped tensile samples with a cross section of 1 × 3 mm 2 
and a gauge length of 10 mm were cut using electrical discharging. 
The Instron 2663-821 video-extensometers were used to measure 
the strain in the tensile testes. At least five samples of each condition 
were tested to verify the reproducibility. Room-temperature in situ 
digital image-correlation tensile tests (Supplementary Fig. 12) were 
performed using an Instron 5582 testing machine with a XTDIC-VG120 
high-precision video-extensometer at a strain rate of 5 × 10 −4 s−1. 
Dog-bone-shaped tensile samples with a cross section of 1.5 × 4 mm 2 
and a gauge length of 12 mm were cut using electrical discharging. Two 
samples of each condition were tested to verify the reproducibility. 
Nanoindentation tests were conducted on a Keysight Nano Indenter 
G200 instrument with a 50 nm Berkovich tip. The samples were elec-
trochemically polished on one side in a solution of 5% perchloric acid 
and 95% methanol at −10 °C. Thirty nanoindentations were conducted 
on each sample and the distance between two adjacent indents was 
roughly 10 μm.
SXRD and PDF measurements
SXRD and pair distribution function (PDF) experiments of T50 HEAs 
were carried out at the 11-ID-C beam line, Advanced Photon Source 
at Argonne National Laboratory, USA. The wavelength of the X-ray 
was 0.1173 Å and the beam size was 500 × 500 μm2. Two-dimensional 
diffraction (for XRDs) and total-scattering (for PDFs) patterns were 
collected using a Perkin-Elmer detector. The distances from samples 
to detectors are roughly 1,700 mm for XRD and roughly 300 mm for 
PDF , respectively. Fit2D (ref. 51) and GSAS-II (ref. 52) software packages 
were used to get the XRD and PDF data, respectively.
Microstructural analysis
Microstructures of the deformed T50s were characterized by using 
JEOL JSM-7001F scanning electron microscopy with an EBSD probe. 
Preparation details of the samples are the same as the nanoindenta -
tion samples.
The SAED patterns and atomic-resolution HAADF scanning 
STEM experiments were performed on a Thermo Fisher Themis Z 
TEM equipped with probe-forming spherical aberration correctors, 
operated at 200 kV with the beam current of 34 pA, convergence 
semi-angle of 23.9 mrad and collection angle range of 99–200 mrad. 
The experiments used the following aberration coefficients: 
A1 ≅ 1.06 nm, A2 ≅ 38.9 nm, B2 ≅ 37.6 nm, C3 ≅ 931 nm, A3 ≅ 401 nm, 
S3 ≅ 307 nm, A4 ≅ 2.86 μm, B4 ≅ 3.34 μm, D4 ≅ 1.86 μm, C5 ≅ 303 μm 
and A5 ≅ 53.7 μm, ensuring less than or equal to 0.06 nm resolution 
under normal conditions. Dislocations in the deformed T50s were 
characterized by using a JEM-2100 TEM and a Talos F200X TEM.
All the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) or STEM speci -
mens were thinned to roughly 50 μm using 400–2,000 grit SiC 
papers, and then twin-jet electro-polishing at −15 °C in an electrolyte 
consisting of 6 vol% perchloric acid, 35 vol% butanol and 59 vol% water. 
Atomic-strain maps were obtained by using the GPA method29. For each 
sample, at least five high resolution TEM images were collected to verify 
the reproducibility of the GPA results.
Atom probe tomography (APT) technology was performed on 
the aged-T50 to search for the LCOs. APT specimens were prepared 
using a typical focused ion beam lift-out method. <001> directions 
are a kind of typical order direction of the LCOs in our work. Hence, 
the target region was carefully selected using EBSD to ensure that the 
axial direction of the APT tips (the direction with the highest spatial 
resolution) is along one of the <001> directions. As the APT specimens 
of aged-T50s suffered from premature failure under voltage pulsing, 
the laser pulsing (energy of 40 pJ and repetition rate of 200 kHz) was 
used to obtain the APT dataset.
DFT-based Monte Carlo simulation
A density-function-theory (DFT)-based lattice Monte Carlo approach 
was used to reveal the evolution of LCO during ageing for T50 HEA. The 
250-atom configuration, containing 125 Ti, 45 Zr, 31 V, 37 Nb and 12 Al, 
was generated as a special quasirandom structure53. The Monte Carlo 
simulations were run for a total of 3,000 steps, corresponding to about 12 
swap trials per atom, and the Monte Carlo temperature was set at 600 K. 
Energy calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation 
package54,55. The self-consistent convergence criterion was taken to be 
4.0 × 10−5 eV per atom. A plane wave cutoff energy was chosen at 350 eV 
and Brillouin zone integrations were performed using Monkhorst–Pack 
meshes56 with a single k-point (Γ). It adopted projector augmented wave 
potentials57 with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof generalized-gradient 
approximation58 for the exchange-correlation functional. The method 
of Mathfessel–Paxton with the width of the smearing of 0.1 eV, was used 
to yield the accurate description of the total energy.
MD simulations
Hybrid Monte Carlo and MD simulations of TiZr 0.65Nb0.28 alloy were 
performed using the software package LAMMPS 59 with the empiri -
cally embedded-atom method potential 46. The simulation cells are 
illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 21, with dimensions of approximately 
150 Å in x, 300 Å in y, 400 Å in z and contain 944,622 atoms. The random 
solid-solution (RSS) configuration (periodic boundary conditions 
for all three directions) was used as the starting point of the hybrid 
Monte Carlo and MD simulation, which was performed under the 
variance-constrained semigrand-canonical ensemble60 to obtain the 
equilibrium configurations at different ageing temperatures (includ-
ing 600, 1,000 and 1,400 K). A dislocation line was then inserted in the 
simulation cell for both RSS and the aged sample. Periodic boundary 
conditions were imposed along x and y directions and shrink-wrapped 
non-periodic boundary conditions in the z direction. The loading setup 
and the measurement of dislocation velocity followed those in ref. 10. 
The atomic configurations were displayed by OVITO61.
