ABSTRACT
The eﬀ ects of alloying with Zr on the microstructure, mechanical and oxidation properties of Nb-Ti-Si based
ultrahigh temperature alloys have been investigated in this study. The microstructures of the all alloys were
comprised of primary γ(Nb,X)5Si3 blocks, Nbss and eutectic colonies, and the additions of Zr do not a ﬀect the
microstructure and phase constituents of Nb-Ti-Si based alloys. Zr improves both the room-temperature
toughness and the high-temperature strength. The alloy with addition of 8 at.% Zr shows the highest fracture
toughness of 15.01 Mpa·m 1/2. The compressive strengths of the alloys are improved to 278.89 –293.08 MPa for
the Zr-containing alloys when compare with the Zr-free alloy (194.23 MPa). The oxidation resistance of the
alloys was also obviously ameliorated with Zr addition, showing a reduced weight gain with the increase of Zr
content.
1. Introduction
Nb silicide in situ composites can o ﬀer increased temperature
capability and reduced density that have the potential to replace
nickel-based superalloys for aeronautic and aerospace applications
[1–3]. In these composites, the hard-brittle intermetallic of Nb 5Si3
(and/or Nb 3Si) provides high temperature strength and the ductile
phase of Nb solid solution (Nbss) o ﬀers room temperature toughness. In
recent years, most studies have focused on developing these alloys
based on the ternary Nb-Ti-Si system [2–6], which are considered to
have good combination properties. However, they still are easy to su ﬀer
from poor fracture toughness at room temperature and poor oxidation
resistance at high temperatures. In order to balance the integrated
properties of the alloys, various methods have been employed. One of
the most commonly used method is alloying. In previous works,
alloying Nb-Ti-Si based system with elements such as Cr, Al, Hf, B,
Mo, Ta, Ge and Sn, etc. has been taken to investigate the alloying e ﬀect
on the microstructure, phase formation, mechanical properties and
oxidation behavior of the alloys [7–30]. Notably, the additions of these
elements with appropriate content ranges, have been reported to be
beneﬁcial to the mechanical properties and/or oxidation resistance of
the Nb-Ti-Si based alloys.
The e ﬀects of Cr on the microstructure and properties of Nb –Ti–Si
based alloys have been reported in several previous publications
[7–15]. Cr can destabilize (Nb,Ti)
3Si phase and facilitate the formation
of Laves phase (Cr 2Nb). Generally, high-temperature oxidation resis-
tance, hardness and strength of the alloys can be ameliorated by
alloying of Cr, but fracture toughness is tended to be sacriﬁ ced
[9–15]. The aim of alloying Al is to improve the oxidation resistance,
but also has a negative e ﬀect on mechanical properties of the alloys
[9,10,12]. Alloying with Hf can stabilize the hexagonal γ-Nb5Si3 (D88,
Mn5Si3-type) silicide, but there is no agreed conclusion about whether
the addition of Hf in the alloys can improve the oxidation resistance or
not [13,16–19]. In the case of alloying with B, the integrated properties
of the alloys are usually improved, such as their oxidation resistance,
room-temperature fracture toughness and strength at elevated tem-
peratures [16,20–24]. Mo is used as alloying addition in the alloys to
straighten the materials by solid solution hardening, whereas it has a
detrimental e ﬀect on the oxidation resistance due to the formation of
porous scale and the evaporation of MoO
3 [17,25–27]. The alloying
additions of Ge and Sn in the alloys are also considered to be conducive
to improvement of oxidation resistance [18–20,28,29].
In Nb-Si binary system, the addition of Zr has a signi ﬁcant impact to
the formation of Nb/Nb 5Si3 lamellar microstructure by eutectoid
decomposition reaction of Nb 3Si [31–34]. Furthermore, with a proper
content of Zr addition, the mechanical properties of the alloys can be
ameliorated [34,35]. For instance, Tian et al. [35] studied the e ﬀect of
Zr addition on the microstructures and mechanical properties of cast
Nb-22Ti-16Si-xZr ( x =0 –4) alloys, and found that the Zr addition
increases the hardness of Nbss and (Nb) 3Si phases as well as the yield

2. Experimental procedures
Five button ingots of Nb-22Ti-15Si-5Cr-3Hf-3Al- xZr ( x =0 ,1 ,2 ,4
and 8, which are denoted as 0Zr, 1Zr, 2Zr, 4Zr and 8Zr respectively) (at.
%) alloys were melted by vacuum non-consumable arc melting with
electromagnetic stirring under an ultra high-purity argon atmosphere.
Each button with a weight of 240 g was remelted six times to ensure
compositional homogeneity. For microstructural analysis, cubic speci-
mens with dimensions of 8 × 8 × 8 mm
3 were cut form the ingots by
electrodischarge machining (EDM).
Fracture toughness test at room temperature was measured using a
three-point bending test (Instron3382). The three-point bending speci-
mens with dimensions of 2.5 × 5 × 30 mm
3 were prepared by EDM
form the as-cast and heat treated ingots. The depth of the notch was
2.4–2.5 mm. The tests were conducted at a cross-head speed of 0.2 mm/
min. The average value of three specimens was employed for the
fracture toughness K
Q of each specimen. The details of the fracture
toughness test of the alloys can refer to our previous works [13,24,36].
Vickers microhardness was measured using an HXP-1000TM hardness
machine under an applied load of 0.98 N. High temperature compres-
sion tests were conducted at 1250 °C using a Gleeble 1500 testing
machine in vacuum at a strain rate of 5 × 10
−3 s−1. The dimensions of
the compression test specimens were 6 mm in diameter and 9 mm in
length. The isothermal oxidation behavior of the alloys was conducted
at 1250 °C for 20 h in an open-ended tube furnace. Before testing, all
the surfaces of the three-point bending, high temperature compression
and oxidation specimens were mechanically polished using SiC paper
with water.
X-ray diﬀraction (XRD, Panalytical X'Pert PRO, Cu K α) analysis and
scanning electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy
(SEM/EDS, TESCAN MIRA 3 and Inca X-sight) were used to identify
the phase constituents, compositional distribution and microstructure
of the as-cast alloys, as well as the specimens after mechanical and
oxidation tests.

3.2. Microstructure of the alloys
Fig. 2 shows typical backscattered electron (BSE) images of the as-
cast alloys, and the chemical compositions of the constituent phases of
these alloys are given in Table 1. In the as-cast state, the alloys show
basically similar microstructure, which are mainly composed of white
Nbss, dark primary γ(Nb,X)5Si3 blocks, Nbss/ γ(Nb,X)5Si3 eutectics
according to the XRD, BSE and EDS results as shown in Figs. 1 and 2,
and Table 1. The primary γ(Nb,X)5Si3 blocks exhibit irregularly
hexagonal, lath-like or “H” morphology. The morphology of the
majority of Nbss/ γ(Nb,X)5Si3 eutectics is irregular, and these eutectics
seem to have a tendency to distribute around the primary γ(Nb,X)5Si3
with the additions of Zr ( Fig. 2). Notably, some dark regions with
higher Cr, Ti, Hf and Si concentrations (such as a composition of this
region in Fig. 2a was about 20.0Nb-22.4Ti-10.7Si-40.5Cr-0.8Al-5.6Hf
(at.%) for the 0Zr alloy) can also be found in the as-cast alloys. These
dark regions which are inferred to be a three-phase eutectic consisting
of (Ti,Nb)ss, (Nb, X)
5Si3 and Cr 2(Nb,X), have been con ﬁrmed by the
researches of Guo et al. [13,16,24,39], Yang et al. [37] and Shao et al.
[38,39]. It should be noted that the di ﬀraction peaks of Cr 2(Nb,X) were
not found in the XRD patterns ( Fig. 1a) due to its very low amounts.
Moreover, in Nbss, many Ti and Cr rich areas (a composition of 40.8Nb-
41.2Ti-3.1Si-10.0Cr-1.9Al-3.0Hf (at.%) for the 0Zr alloy shown in
Fig. 2a and a composition of 50.3Nb-30.4Ti-1.5Si-8.7Cr-3.4Al-2.0Hf-
3.7Zr (at.%) for the 8Zr alloy shown in Fig. 2e) were also present in the
microstructure and exhibited a dark contrast, as shown in Fig. 2. Thus,
the main phases present in microstructure of the as-cast alloys were: (i)
the Nbss that exhibited some Ti rich areas (Ti-rich Nbss), (ii) the
γ(Nb,X)5Si3 phase, (iii) the (Ti,Nb)ss and (iv) a Cr-rich C14 Laves phase.
From the data as shown in Table 1, the concentration of Zr in
γ(Nb,X)5Si3 is signiﬁcantly higher than that in Nbss in the Zr-containing
alloys, indicating that Zr mainly partitions into γ(Nb,X)5Si3 which was
similar to the distribution behavior of Hf element. Several studies have
reported that the element of Hf mainly partitioned into silicides and can
promote the formation of hexagonal γ(Nb,X)
5Si3 [11,13,15–19]. The
elements Cr and Al were primarily partitioned into Nbss, which are
similar to those of previous work on the Nb-Si based alloys [7–15].

3.3.3. Compressive properties

4. Discussion
The eﬀects of alloying Zr on microstructure and mechanical proper-
ties of the Nb based alloys have been investigated in several works
[31–35,44]. Similar to the role of Ti, Zr accelerates the decomposition
of Nb 3Si into Nb and Nb 5Si3 phases in Nb-Si binary system [31–33].
Also, the addition of Zr brings visible formation of Nb/Nb 5Si3 lamellar
microstructure in this simple binary system [31–34]. Here, the Nb-Ti-Si
based alloys are multi-alloyed which containing six di ﬀerent elements,
thus their solidi ﬁcation processes.
are very di ﬀerent from the binary or ternary systems. Their
solidiﬁcation is more complex due to the interactions among various
elements in this Nb-Ti-Si-Cr-Hf-Al system. The as-cast microstructures
of these alloys mainly consist of Nbss (including some Ti-rich Nbss),
primary γ(Nb,X)
5Si3 blocks, Nbss/ γ(Nb,X)5Si3 eutectics and a small
amount of three-phase eutectic (Ti,Nb)ss/(Nb, X)5Si3/Cr2(Nb,X)( Fig. 2).

No other silicides and only γ(Nb,X)5Si3 were observed in these alloys
should be attributed to the alloying e ﬀects of Ti, Hf and Zr additions
[16–19,24,35,45–50]. 5-3 silicides (Ti 5Si3,H f 5Si3 and Zr 5Si3) formed
from those three elements have same crystal structure of D8 8 (hP16,
P63/mcm, prototype Mn 5Si3) [45–50], which will contribute to the
formation of γ(Nb,X)5Si3. It is well know that the melting points of Ti,
Cr and Zr are lower than that of Nb, thus they will segregate in the
remaining liquid during this fast solidi ﬁcation. It is because of this, Ti-
rich Nbss and (Ti,Nb)ss/(Nb, X)5Si3/Cr2(Nb,X) three-phase eutectic
solidiﬁed from the remaining liquid. Thus, based on the experimental
results, a schematic illustration of solidi ﬁcation path of Nb-Ti-Si based
alloys can be described in Fig. 9. During the initial period of solidiﬁ ca-
tion, the primary γ(Nb,X)5Si3 blocks are solidi ﬁed from the melt by the
reaction of L →γ +L 1 (t1 in Fig. 9). After then, eutectic reaction L 1→
Nbss + γ +L 2 may be occurred when the remaining melt reaches the
eutectic composition (t 2 in Fig. 9). Due to the microsegregation of low
freezing temperature elements in the front of the solid/liquid interface,
the Nbss formed during the ﬁnal solidiﬁcation stage were rich in Ti and
Cr elements, leading to the formation of Ti-rich Nbss (t 2 in Fig. 9 ).
Lastly, L 2→ (Nb,X)5Si3 + (Ti,Nb)ss + Cr 2(Nb,X) ternary eutectic
reaction occurred in the ﬁnal remaining melt (t 3 in Fig. 9).
Based on these results, the addition of Zr in the Nb-Ti-Si based alloys
has no signiﬁcant inﬂuence on the phase constituent and microstructure
of the alloys ( Figs. 1 and 2). Composition analysis found that Zr is
mainly distributed in (Nb, X)5Si3, while certain amounts of Zr are also
existed in Nbss as shown in Table 1. Owing to the nature di ﬀerence
(such as atomic radius) between Zr and Nb atoms, the features of Nbss
and (Nb,X)
5Si3 are sure to be changed. Thus, the mechanical properties
of the alloys can be a ﬀected by the presence of those Zr atoms. From
mechanical properties testing results, it is a clear indication that the
alloying Zr indeed can improve the room-temperature fracture tough-
ness and high temperature strength of the Nb-Ti-Si based alloys. For Nb-
based in situ composites, the fracture resistance of these composites is
controlled mostly by the fracture toughness, volume fraction, and the
plastic constraint of the ductile phase Nbss in the microstructure
[51–55]. It has been demonstrated that the alloying additions such as
Ti addition can reduce the Peierls-Nabarro (P-N) barrier energy and
increase the dislocation mobility, thus in turn, improve the fracture
resistance of these composites [51,53,55]. Here, the P-N barrier energy
is sensitive to alloy additions which is considered as a function of alloy
composition [24,51–56]. Based on the above results, the improvements
in fracture toughness by Zr addition to Nb-Ti-Si based alloys are also
probably the result of reduction in the P-N barrier energy as similar to
the role of Ti addition [51–56]. For in situ Nbss/(Nb,X)
5Si3 based
composites, the high temperature strength of the alloys is mainly
supplied by the silicides [41,42]. Thus, at high temperature, the

We all know that Nb based alloys are poor in oxidation resistance at
high temperatures due to the formation of non-protective oxides such as
Nb
2O5. Here, multi-alloyed Nb-Ti-Si based alloys can display relatively
better oxidation resistance because of the additions of Si, Cr, Hf, Al, Sn,
Y and B elements, which are bene ﬁcial for improving the oxidation
resistance of those alloys [12,13,19,21,40,56]. Notice that those
improvements are still limited. Being an e ﬀective alloying element to
improve the oxidation resistance, Zr has been introduced into several
alloy systems such NiAl [57], Fe-Al [58,59], Ti-Al-Nb [60,61], Nb-Ti-Al-
V [62], Ti-Al [63] and nickel-based super [64] alloys, as well as into
some coatings such as refractory metal alloy coatings [65], Cr-Al-N
coatings [66], β-NiAl coatings [67] and silicide coatings [68,69].I n
these cases, the oxidation testing indicated that their oxidation
resistance have di ﬀerent degrees of improvements by introducing the
Zr element [57–69]. Although the mechanism explanation for those
improvements is still in debate, some of the proposed opinions can be
summarised as follows: (1) the formation of Zr oxides or Zr ions at grain
boundaries can inhibit the outward di ﬀusion of matrix elements and the
inward di ﬀusion of oxygen, thus reducing the oxidation rate; (2) the
