ABSTRACT 
The high-temperature application of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) fabricated via selective laser melting (SLM) 
relies on an in-depth understanding of the mechanical properties and deformation mechanisms involved. This 
study conducted tensile testing of FeCoCrNi HEA, at various temperatures and strain rates, where the micro -
structure was systematically characterized before and after deformation. The FeCoCrNi HEAs fabricated via SLM 
exhibited a greatly enhanced tensile strength at room temperature compared to those produced by traditional 
processing, but the strength at high temperature was significantly compromised. Experimental data were used to 
calculate the parameters of a constitutive model based on three classical mathematical models to predict the flow 
behavior at elevated temperatures. The softening mechanism was attributed to the evolution of the dislocation 
network, and a structure – mechanism – property relationship at elevated temperatures was established. Further, 
cracks initiated at the grain boundaries at elevated temperatures owing to nano-clustering. These results are 
expected to contribute to the development and improvement of SLM-HEAs for use at high temperatures. 
1. Introduction 
The concept of high-entropy alloys (HEAs) was first proposed in 
2004, after which nearly two decades of research allowed for a gradual 
improvement of their theoretical understanding [ 1 – 3 ]. HEAs offer 
excellent mechanical properties and are a promising alternative for 
traditional alloys. Selective laser melting (SLM) is an important branch 
of additive manufacturing (AM), commonly known as three-dimensional 
(3D) printing. This technique has become one of the most commonly 
studied technologies for HEA production owing to the short processing 
time, low material waste, design freedom, and high geometrical accu -
racy [ 4 – 7 ]. Consequently, SLM-HEAs can make full use of the advan -
tages of SLM and HEAs and have a broad application potential in 
industry. 
FeCoCrNi HEA is one of the most widely studied HEAs [ 8 – 10 ] and 
serves as the basis for most HEA systems, such as FeCoCrNiMn and 
FeCoCrNiAl [ 11 – 14 ]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the me -
chanical properties and underlying mechanisms of FeCoCrNi HEAs 
fabricated via SLM will support further advances in HEA-SLM technol -
ogy. FeCoCrNi HEAs have already been successfully printed via SLM, 
demonstrating the excellent formability of the material in AM [ 15 ]. One 
of our previous studies investigated the effects of process optimization 
on the structure and mechanical properties of FeCoCrNi HEA [ 16 ]; 
another of our works discussed the feasibility of the in situ synthesis of 
FeCoCrNi HEAs and the influence of impurities on the solid-solution 
strengthening effect [ 17 ]. In addition, the effects of the annealing tem -
perature on the structure and mechanical properties were studied [ 18 ]. 
Specifically, the unique dislocation network structures in printed 
FeCoCrNi HEAs were found to have a strong pinning effect on disloca -
tion movement during deformation at room temperature, thereby

3. Experimental results 
3.1. SLM-FeCoCrNi HEA 
SEM images of SLM-FeCoCrNi HEA revealed a typical layered 
structure ( Fig. 3 a), with small elongated columnar substructures (width 
= ~1 μ m) observed inside the grains ( Fig. 3 b). TEM revealed that these 
substructures were separated by many interconnected dislocations at the 
boundary of the substructures ( Fig. 3 c and d). Similar research refers to 
these interconnected dislocations as dislocation networks [ 20 ], but they 
are actually substructure boundaries. The orientation difference of 
adjacent substructures is almost the same, and the lattice mismatch at 
the interface is small because the local temperature gradient is almost 
the same. Therefore, the substructure boundaries are displayed as 
dislocation networks (low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) are often 
equivalent to dislocation walls). The high dislocation density can be 
attributed to the rapid solidification process. The vacancy energy in the 
liquid state is relatively low, whereas it is much higher in the solid state 
[ 11 ]. However, vacancies cannot be effectively eliminated during rapid 
solidification. Here, the high density of vacancies remained in the 
as-printed sample. The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern 
showed that the sample consisted a single face-centered cubic (FCC) 
structure.

4. Discussion 
4.1. Flow behavior at different strain rates and temperatures 
At all temperatures considered in this study, the strain rate affected 
the yield strength and elongation of samples ( Figs. 4 and 6 ). The char -
acterization of materials before and after tensile deformation proved 
that the lattice structure did not change during testing, nor did precip -
itation occur. The SLM-FeCoCrNi HEA has a stable single FCC structure, 
which implies that neither interactions between dislocations and phases 
nor phase transformations affected the deformation mechanism. The 
strengthening effect of the strain rate can thus be explained simply by 
the critical shear stress of dislocation slide, i.e., the Peierls – Nabarro 
stress [ 24 ]. 
The essence of deformation is dislocation movement, and the speed 
of dislocation movement increases with the strain rate. The faster the 
dislocation moves, more is the energy needed to overcome resistance, 
and the dislocation becomes narrower. Thus, the Peierls – Nabarro stress 
increases, and the critical shear stress of slip increases, which causes 
additional strengthening. In addition, more dislocation sources produce 
movable dislocations with increasing local stress, and more slip systems 
participate in the deformation. With increasing strain rate, the strain 
damage decreases, which delays crack initiation and increases the 
ductility. Therefore, the yield strength and elongation are generally 
higher at higher strain rates. As an exception, elongation at 800 
◦
C 
decreased with the increasing strain rate in this study because 
recrystallization occurred at this temperature ( Figs. 4 and 6 ). The local 
stress concentration was eliminated by this dynamic recrystallization, 
which should have increased the ductility at this temperature. Although 
increases in movable dislocations and slip systems promote the 
improvement in plasticity with increasing strain rate, recrystallization 
dominates the increase in plasticity at high temperatures. However, 
grain boundary migration during recrystallization takes time, and at a 
sufficiently high strain rate, recrystallization does not have sufficient 
time to complete. Thus, at 800 
◦
C, the ductility decreased with the 
increasing strain rate.

4.2. Softening mechanism at elevated temperature 
A previous study conducted at room temperature demonstrated that 
the strengthening of SLM-FeCoCrNi HEAs was mainly attributed to the 
strengthening effect of dislocation networks formed due to rapid solid -
ification [ 16 ]. The interaction between the dislocation networks and 
dislocations in that printed FeCoCrNi alloy prevented the dislocation 
movement, thereby increasing the strength at room temperature. In this 
study, similar structures containing nano-substructures in the 
SLM-FeCoCrNi HEAs, namely dislocation networks, were observed using 
TEM ( Fig. 3 ). These HEAs exhibited a high ultimate strength of 700 MPa 
at room temperature. However, increasing temperature led to a dra -
matic decrease in strength ( Fig. 4 ), which indicated the dislocation 
network contributed less to enhanced strength at elevated temperatures. 
Typically, materials for high-temperature usage (e.g., super alloy and 
heat resistant steel) have secondary phases, which can pin the disloca -
tions, thereby increasing the critical strength at elevated temperatures 
[ 30 , 31 ]. Unlike materials strengthened by secondary phases, FeCoCrNi 
HEA has no hard phase. Therefore, its mechanical behavior at elevated 
temperatures is different from traditional alloys [ 32 ]. The most unique 
characteristic of AM is the formation of dislocation networks and a high 
dislocation density [ 33 ]. The dislocation networks are still visible after 
deformation at 400 
◦
C ( Fig. 12 a), where the deformed sample contained 
large number of deformed dislocations within its dislocation networks 
( Fig. 12 b) compared with the as-printed sample ( Fig. 3 d). Furthermore, 
dislocation entanglement was observed within the dislocation networks, 
which indicated that the dislocation network prevented the movement 
of dislocations at 400 
◦
C. TEM images revealed that the number of 
dislocation networks did not decrease, despite the lower yield strength 
compared to deformation at room temperature. The climbing of dislo -
cations was enhanced at elevated temperatures due to thermal activa -
tion, thereby overcoming the hindering effect of the dislocation 
networks. Dislocation networks are comprised of dislocations; thus, the 
interaction between dislocations and dislocation networks represents a 
dislocation intersection. However, the enhanced dislocation climbing at 
elevated temperatures decreased the occurrence of dislocation in -
tersections, and in turn, the resistance of the dislocation network to 
dislocation movements [ 34 ].

cavities and cracks ( Fig. 7 ), where the density of defects increased with 
increasing deformation temperatures. The cavities and cracks were 
observed at the grain boundaries in the sample tested at 600 
◦
C ( Fig. 8 b), 
especially along the long boundaries of columnar grains. The cracks 
formed during deformation at 800 
◦
C were generally perpendicular to 
the deformation direction and stopped by triple points ( Fig. 8 c). 
Although the accumulation of dislocations can be observed at grain 
boundaries ( Fig. 12 ), this is a general tendency in metals and cannot 
explain the ductility loss at elevated temperatures. Recently, Ming et al. 
[ 42 ] reported a novel theory that nanoclustering at grain boundaries is 
the main reason for the ductility loss of HEAs with the FCC structure at 
elevated temperatures, which in turn generates micro-holes at grain 
boundaries. Subsequently, numerous micro-holes connect into curves 
and become the source of cracking during deformation. Consistent with 
this theory, cracks appeared at grain boundaries in this study ( Fig. 11 ). 
To confirm the fracture mechanism, EDS mapping was conducted under 
TEM after high-temperature tensile testing at 600 
◦
C ( Fig. 15 ). Elemental 
segregation obviously occurred at the grain boundaries, which was 
caused by nanoclustering at high temperature. In contrast, however, the 
ductility was higher at 800 
◦
C than that at 600 
◦
C because more dynamic 
recrystallization occurred at 800 
◦
C ( Table 2 ). Previous studies on the 
recrystallization of FeCoCrNi HEAs found that the recrystallization 
temperature was approximately 900 
◦
C in the as-printed state [ 18 ] and 
700 
◦
C in a 50% deformed sample [ 19 ]. The driving force of recrystal -
lization, namely, deformation energy storage, accumulates more easily 
in SLM samples. As a result, the local stress concentration is released by 
dynamic recrystallization. The decrease in strain damage increases the 
plasticity at and above 800 
◦
C.

5. Conclusion 
In this research, the mechanical properties of SLM-FeCoCrNi HEA 
were tested at elevated temperatures. In addition, the SLM-FeCoCrNi 
HEA samples were extensively characterized by SEM, EBSD, and TEM 
before and after testing. The results are summarized below: 
(1) The considerable strengthening effect of the unique dislocation 
networked structure at room temperature was compromised at 
elevated temperatures, where the networks failed to provide 
enough resistance to dislocation movement. Some of the dislo -
cations obtained sufficient activation energy to overcome the 
energy barrier binding them to the networks.
