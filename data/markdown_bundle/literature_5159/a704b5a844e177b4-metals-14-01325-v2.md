Abstract: Advanced manufacturing technologies have imposed higher demands on the strength,
hardness, and high-temperature stability of materials, such as cutting tools, molds, and wear-resistant
parts. Metal matrix composites with excellent comprehensive properties are expected to meet
these demands. High-entropy alloys (HEAs), composed of unique multi-principle elements, offer
high strength, hardness, and excellent high-temperature stability, superior to traditional cemented
carbides in some cases. Here, the AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 HEA reinforced by TiB 2 was fabricated by an
innovative alliance of mechanical alloying (MA) and spark plasma sintering (SPS). It was found that
tuning the milling time and content of the reinforced phase could effectively realize the uniform
distribution of the TiB 2 reinforcement phase in the matrix. The AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 with 5 vol.%TiB2
after MA for 2 h resulted in the particle refinement of TiB2 and the uniform distribution of TiB2 in
the matrix. And the bulk sintered at 1150 ◦C exhibited an excellent combination of a compressive
yield strength of 1510 MPa, a compressive strength of 2500 MPa, and a high hardness of 780 HV .
The analysis of different strengthening mechanisms suggests that the fine grain strengthening and
precipitation strengthening make the HEA composite possess excellent compressive yield strength
and fracture strength.
Keywords: high-entropy alloy; mechanical alloying; spark plasma sintering; reinforcement particles
1. Introduction
As an innovative class of materials, high-entropy alloys (HEAs) introduce a novel
design concept that deviates from the traditional single-element-dominated alloy design,
offering a fresh perspective on alloy development [1,2]. HEAs have been studied widely due
to their remarkable properties, such as high strength and hardness [3,4], good compression
resistance [5–7], excellent thermo-stability under high temperature [8], and remarkable wear
resistances [9,10]. The phase structure and mechanical performances of HEAs are severely
influenced by the interaction among principal elements in the alloy system. Therefore, it is
of great significance to study the relationship between principal elements for understanding
the phase formation mechanism of HEAs and designing the new HEA system. Typically,
the HEAs with a face-centered cubic (FCC) structure exhibit good ductility and toughness
but low strength, while the HEAs with a body-centered cubic (BCC) structure exhibit a
high strength with poor ductility and toughness [ 11,12]. HEAs with a dual-phase FCC
and BCC structure offer the potential to achieve a balanced combination of strength and
plasticity [7,13,14].

Additionally, it has been proven that the appropriate addition of reinforcement
particles is an effective means to improve the comprehensive mechanical properties of
HEAs [15–18]. Numerous studies have reported that adding a reinforcement phase makes
the HEA composites exhibit higher strength and hardness, wear resistance, and high-
temperature oxidation resistance. Their unique microstructure provides more possibilities
for the development and application of HEA composites [19–22]. For example, Cai et al. [23]
introduced the Zr element and Y2O3 nanoparticles into Fe-14Cr-3W-0.4Ti alloy. It exhibited
an outstanding compressive yield strength of 2641 MPa and a good thermo-stability, due
to the synergistic effects of multicomponent co-segregation and nanoparticle Zener pin-
ning. In addition, FeCrNiCu matrix alloy exhibited a notable tensile yield strength of 653.5
MPa and ultimate strength of 1006.5 MPa via introducing the coupled effect of alloying
element V and in situ TiC particles [3]. A non-equiatomic (Fe35Mn10Cr20Ni35)98.8C1.2 HEA
synthesized via mechanical alloying (MA) and spark plasma sintering (SPS) showed higher
strength compared to the same alloy prepared by arc melting, along with good plastic-
ity [24]. Thus, despite the challenge of balancing strength and ductility in HEAs, both their
strength and plasticity can be effectively enhanced through the incorporation of reinforcing
phases, such as ceramic particles and intermetallic compounds. Wang et al. [ 17] added
ceramic particle ZrB 2 into AlCoCrFeNi HEA to incorporate a Lave phase, obtaining a
compressive strength of ~2071 MPa and hardness of ~1222 HV . Pei et al. [25] found that the
addition of h-BN into NbMoWTa HEA led to the in situ generation of (Nb,Ta)N/(Nb,Ta)2N
and (Nb,Ta)B2 ceramic phases, significantly enhancing the hardness and wear resistance of
the composites. These reinforcing phases serve to facilitate plastic deformation by impeding
dislocation movement, thereby contributing to the improvement in material plasticity. The
superior performance of HEA composites is attributed to the synergistic effects of multiple
strengthening mechanisms, which significantly enhance the mechanical properties of HEA
composites [24]. Furthermore, HEA composites also exhibit excellent wear resistance and
corrosion resistance, thus possessing broad application prospects as a structural material,
such as precision instruments and wear-resistant components [7,14].
Currently, HEA composites are usually fabricated by adding the reinforcement phase
to the HEA matrix via powder metallurgy. The combination of MA and SPS is commonly
used to fabricate bulk ultrafine-grained alloys, which helps retain plasticity while enhancing
strength. This process also introduces dispersed nano-sized reinforcement particles [19].
For instance, Li et al. [26] incorporated the oxyphilic element Hf and Y2O3 nanoparticles
into CoCrFeNi HEAs by MA and SPS, where the formation of ultrafine oxide particles
contributed to improved mechanical performance. Moreover, a novel nanocrystalline
Co25Ni25Fe25Al7.5Cu17.5 HEA fabricated by MA and SPS exhibited a compression yield
strength of 1795 MPa and a hardness of 454 HV [27].
In the above review, it was found that incorporating ceramic additives into HEAs
has a great influence on their various properties. In this study, our research aimed to
develop a synthesis technique for TiB2-reinforced AlCoCrFeNi2.1 HEA composites using
the combination of MA and SPS techniques. The AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 HEA with excellent
mechanical properties was selected as the matrix [27]. The effects of the milling parameter
and varying TiB2 content (0–10 vol.%) on the corresponding mechanical properties of the
resulting composites were investigated. The corresponding strengthening mechanisms are
also discussed. The research for enhancing the AlCoCrFeNi2.1 HEA is of great significance
for the development of superhard molds and high-speed grinding.
2. Experimental Procedure
Elemental powders of Al, Co, Cr, Fe, Ni, and TiB2 with high purity (>99.5 wt%) and
average particle sizes of 45 µm (supplied from Alfa Aesar, Haverhill, MA, USA) were used
as starting materials for preparing AlCoCrFeNi2.1 HEA composites. Stainless steel milling
media and the blended powders, in a ball-to-powder weight ratio of 20:1, were placed in
stainless steel vials filled with argon gas. The MA process was conducted in a planetary ball
mill (QM-3SP4) at a rotation speed of 350 rpm of dry milling. Ethanol, as the process control

agent (PCA), was added into ball milling vials to prevent agglomeration and cold welding.
Subsequently, the milled powders were dried in a vacuum drying chamber at 80 ◦C for
2~3 days to volatilize ethanol and later monoaxially compressed into a cylindrical die
with a hydraulic press of 30 MPa. Subsequently, they were sintered via the SPS technique
(LABOX-650F, NJS Co., Ltd. SPS Center, Tokyo, Japan) at 1150 ◦C with a heating rate of
50 ◦C/min and a duration of 10 min. During SPS, an identical pressure of 45 MPa was
set and the vacuum pressure was kept below 20 Pa. Both the powders and the sintered
AlCoCrFeNi2.1 HEA presented a dual phase structure of FCC and BCC, as shown in Figure
S1 of the Supplementary Material [28]. The AlCoCrFeNi2.1 and 5 vol.% TiB2 powders were
first prepared by the MA process for 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 6 h, and then sintered at 1150 ◦C for
10 min (hereafter referred to as MS1, MS2, MS4, and MS6, respectively). To compare the
effect of the ball milling process on the microstructures, the AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 and 5 vol.%
TiB2 composite powders were also synthesized at a low speed of 250 rpm for 2 h and
sintered at the same conditions (hereafter referred to as BS2). For indicating the experiment
process clearly, a flow chart of the experiment is shown in Figure S2 of the Supplementary
Material. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was applied to study the microstructures
and chemical composition. The bulk specimens for microstructural analysis were prepared
using electrical discharge machining (UNIPOL-1200M, MTI Corporation, Richmond, CA,
USA), followed by grinding and polishing with diamond paste (supplied from SANTU,
Wenling, China) to achieve a mirror-like surface finish. The polished surfaces were etched
using a mixture of hydrochloric acid and concentrated nitric acid in a standard molar ratio of
3:1, and then analyzed with an SEM (Zeiss Sigma 300, ZEISS Group, Oberkochen, Germany)
equipped with an energy-dispersive spectroscope (EDS). Vickers hardness measurements
were conducted using an HVS-1000 hardness tester, applying a load of 9.8 N for 10 s.
Each specimen was measured at least ten times to obtain an average hardness value.
Compression tests at room temperature were carried out using an Instron-5982 (INN,
Agia Paraskevi, Greece) testing machine with a strain rate of 1 × 10−3 s−1. Cylindrical
compressive specimens with a 2:3 aspect ratio (ϕ 4 × 6 mm) were prepared in accordance
with ASTM standards. To ensure reliability, each HEA composite was tested three times to
minimize experimental error.
3. Results
Figure 1a–d show the SEM morphologies of the AlCoCrFeNi2.1 and 5 vol.% TiB2 com-
posite bulks. As shown in Figure 1a, the interface between the matrix and the reinforcement
particles of MS1 is relatively clean, with the refined TiB2 particles well dispersed between
the two phases. This clean interface facilitates efficient load transfer, allowing the rein-
forcement to strengthen the matrix under external forces, which ultimately improves the
matrix strength. As the milling time reaches 2 h (Figure 1b), the matrix and reinforcement
particles are further fragmented. As seen in the EDS mapping in Figure 2, TiB 2 not only
appears in the gap of AlCoCrFeNi 2.1 HEA, but is also embedded in the FCC and BCC
phases. During MA, the TiB2 powders and the steel balls impact the alloy powder, causing
some TiB2 to embed in the HEA powders while other TiB2 particles attach to the surface.
Additionally, the size of the HEA powders decreases under this impact, which is beneficial
for the mechanical performance of the HEA composites.
Compared with MS1, the particle fragmentation of MS2 is larger, and TiB2 particles are
more evenly dispersed in the collective. Thus, with the further increase in milling times, the
likelihood and extent of TiB2 embedding continue to rise. Figure 1c,d show that the amount
of embedded TiB2 reaches its limit after MA for 4 and 6 h. At this point, any remaining
nano-TiB2 particles are distributed on the surface of the HEA powders, leading to a decline
in mechanical properties, as illustrated in Figure 3. Figure 1e shows the SEM image of
the AlCoCrFeNi2.1 and 5 vol.% TiB2 composite subjected to 2 h of low-speed mixing and
subsequently consolidated by SPS (BS2). It is observed that TiB2 particles decompose in
the matrix after sintering, with an intense interfacial reaction between TiB2 and the matrix.

Room-temperature compression and hardness tests were conducted to investigate
the effect of TiB2 particle contents on the mechanical behavior of HEA composites. The
typical compression curve and Vickers hardness of these composites are illustrated in
Figure 6. As the volume fraction of TiB 2 particles increases, both strength and hardness
improve. The presence of micro- and nano-sized TiB 2 particles within the composites
hinders dislocation movement under external loads, contributing to the observed increases
in strength and hardness. Specifically, the HEA composite with 10 vol.% TiB 2 exhibits
a compressive yield strength of 2100 MPa and fracture strength of 2710 MPa, but with
a limited plasticity of 1.32%. In contrast, the composite with 5 vol.% TiB 2 demonstrates
excellent mechanical properties, featuring a yield strength of 1510 MPa, fracture strength of
2500 MPa, and plasticity of 2.53%, along with a hardness of 780 HV . Furthermore, the 5 vol.%
TiB2 composite outperforms the one with 3 vol.% TiB2 in terms of compression strain. The
increased number of TiB2 particles enhances the interface area between the particles and the
matrix, effectively transferring loads to the reinforcement phase and limiting deformation
in localized regions during external loading. This observation aligns with the fracture
morphology shown in Figure 7d, which reveals numerous fracture surfaces alongside
shear zones. Stress concentration occurs in TiB 2-reinforced composites due to interface
mismatches between the reinforcement particles and the matrix, as well as differences in
hardness. While TiB2 particles experience significant stress, the uniform distribution of this
stress leads to a localized stress concentration around the particles, ultimately resulting in
brittle fracture of the composite. However, the presence of shear bands indicates that some
plastic deformation accompanies the fracture process, primarily occurring in the matrix
or near the interface with TiB2 particles. This indicates that the composite undergoes both
brittle and plastic fracture mechanisms during failure. Therefore, the fracture mode of the
HEA composite with 5 vol.% TiB2 can be classified as hybrid fracture.

As shown in Figure 7c, during crack propagation, the formation and growth of shear zones

particles plays a crucial role in pinning dislocations, hindering their movement and increas-
ing resistance, which further enhances the compressive strength of the HEA composites.
Consequently, both fine grain strengthening and precipitation strengthening are essential
mechanisms in the reinforcement of HEA composites. However, it is important to note that
the excessive addition of TiB2 can lead to grain agglomeration, reducing grain boundary
strength and compromising the plasticity of the HEA matrix composite. Thus, comprehen-
sive analysis indicates that TiB2 particles have a significant impact on the microstructure
and strengthening mechanisms of HEA materials.
Above the review, by maintaining a constant milling speed, the appropriate milling
duration can help to evenly disperse the TiB2 reinforcing phase in the matrix, which can
not only improve the load transfer ability of TiB2 in the matrix, but also promote the grain
refinement and effectively improve the compressive strength of the composite. Adding an
appropriate amount of TiB2 reinforcement to the HEA matrix can meet diverse application
needs. This approach highlights the pivotal role of materials science in designing high-
performance alloys and offers valuable guidelines for the development and application
of HEA composites. Finally, further adjustment of the composite material design, such
as changing the composition of the HEA matrix, changing the type of reinforcement
phase or introducing the composite reinforcement phase, and comprehensively comparing
the excellent mechanical properties, provides a theoretical basis for the wide application
of HEAs.
5. Conclusions
The AlCoCrFeNi2.1 HEA composite reinforced with TiB2 particles was designed and
fabricated using a combination of mechanical alloying (MA) and spark plasma sintering
(SPS). The effects of TiB2 particles on the microstructure and mechanical properties of the
composites were systematically investigated as follows:
1. A uniform distribution of the TiB2 reinforcement phase within the HEA matrix was
effectively achieved by adjusting the milling time. This uniform distribution not only
refines the particle size of the TiB2 reinforcement but also significantly enhances its
multiple strengthening effects on the matrix, thereby improving the overall mechanical
properties of the composite.
2. As the volume fraction of the TiB2 reinforcement phase increased, the compressive
strength of the HEA composite also gradually improved. However, when an excessive
amount of TiB 2 particles was added, the embedded TiB 2 in the matrix reached a
saturation point, leading to excess TiB2 particles accumulating on the surface of the
HEA matrix. This accumulation resulted in stress concentration and brittle fracture.
3. A reasonable milling time and TiB 2 content were obtained to homogenize the dis-
tribution of TiB 2 reinforcement in the matrix and refine the particle size of TiB 2
reinforcement, further enhancing the good load transfer strengthening, dispersion
strengthening, and fine crystal strengthening of TiB 2 reinforcement acting on the
matrix. The HEA composite with 5 vol.% TiB2 particles exhibits outstanding overall
mechanical performance, achieving a Vickers hardness of 780 HV , a yield strength of
1510 MPa, a fracture strength of 2548 MPa, and a ductility of 2.53%.
