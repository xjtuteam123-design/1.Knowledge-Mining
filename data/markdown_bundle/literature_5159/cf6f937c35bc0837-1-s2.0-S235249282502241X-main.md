1. Introduction
Ni-based superalloys are the material of choice for high-temperature 
applications in aviation, energy, and space exploration due to excellent 
mechanical performance and oxidation resistance [1 – 3] . However, their 
high density ( > 8 g/cm
3
) and cost limitations drive the need for alter -
native materials that can provide similar or superior properties at a 
lower weight.
In this context, lightweight complex concentrated alloys (CCAs) have 
emerged as promising candidates [4,5] . Characterised by 
multi-elemental compositions, with usually high concentrations of Al 
and Ti, these CCAs offer excellent mechanical properties while main -
taining densities comparable to commercial Ti-based alloys (~5 g/cm
3
) 
[4 – 7] . For instance, CCAs belonging to the Al-Cr-Nb-Ti-V-Zr alloy sys -
tem show high specific strength up to 1000
◦
C, surpassing existing 
Ni-based superalloys and TiAl-based alloys [8 – 17] . The advanced 
strength of the Al-Cr-Nb-Ti-V-Zr CCAs originates from the multiphase 
structure, consisting of intermetallics, the most frequent of which are B2 
and Laves phases [10,18 – 20] . However, the dominance of such com -
pounds in the structure results in a very limited, often zero, 
room-temperature plasticity, even under compression loading [21,22] , 
and only reduction of the volume fraction of these intermetallics or their 
full elimination can provide cold workability and tensile ductility [23, 
24] . Moreover, despite the formation of complex, relatively protective 
oxides, the Al-Cr-Nb-Ti-V-Zr CCAs still demonstrate inferior oxidation 
resistance compared to conventional high-temperature materials 
[25 – 30] . All these issues pose challenges to widespread applications of 
such CCAs.
