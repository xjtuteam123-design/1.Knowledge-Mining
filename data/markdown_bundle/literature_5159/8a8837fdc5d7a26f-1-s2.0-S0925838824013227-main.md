ABSTRACT 
This study investigates the reactions occurring at the metal-mold interface during directional solidification of Ni- 
based superalloys, exerting a significant influence on both the surface quality and the desired composition of the 
alloy. The origin and nature of these reactions are systematically investigated by integrating thermodynamics 
and microstructural evolution of the alloy. It was observed that Hf from the melt reacts with the Al
2
O
3
-based 
ceramic mold, leading to the formation of HfO
2 
at the interface. This compound adheres to the surface of the 
ingot and also infiltrates into the melt near the interface. In order to validate these findings, an exploration of 
four alternative Ni-based superalloys, both with and without Hf content, was conducted, revealing an absence of 
comparable reactions at the interface in Hf-free alloys. Additionally, it was explored that the interface formed 
due to the metal-mold reaction may serve as a preferential nucleation site for MC-type carbides in C-containing 
Ni-based superalloys. This study concludes that the Hf content is the primary determinant influencing reactions 
at the metal-mold interface. 
1. Introduction 
The properties and performance of Ni-based superalloys, crucial to 
gas-turbine engines in aircrafts and power plants, are thoroughly refined 
through compositional and microstructural optimization. However, 
their ultimate attributes are also significantly affected by the intricacies 
of the manufacturing processes [1] . For instance, ensuring control over 
internal defects and surface conditions in turbine blades arising from 
metal-mold interactions is essential during the manufacturing process 
[2] . Directional solidification stands as a cornerstone technique in the 
fabrication process, extensively employed in single crystal casting, 
wherein the [001] orientation is carefully regulated [3 – 6] . This method 
also finds widespread application in the directional casting of crucial gas 
turbine engine components, notably blades. A substantial work has been 
done on understanding the evolution mechanism of dendrites and the 
solidification via simulations and experiments [7,8] . Nonetheless, un -
derstanding the interfacial reaction of the melt with the mold requires 
further work to enhance the quality and efficiency of directional 
solidification. 
In the process of directional solidification (DS), molten Ni-based 
superalloys containing reactive elements such as Ti, Hf, Al, or C can 
initiate interfacial reactions with the oxide-based mold material. This 
interaction with oxygen from the mold materials forms interfacial 
compounds, leading to surface defects [9] . Consequently, the desired 
design and composition of the alloy can be compromised due to the 
segregation of one or more constituents at the interface. Thus, a 
comprehensive understanding of the metal-mold interaction during 
casting or directional solidification is inevitable for improving produc -
tion processes. 
Wettability is critical in casting and it requires an optimal balance; if 
it is too high, it can lead to surface defects originating from excessive 
flow into the mold ’ s pores, and if it is too low, it can result in incomplete 
casting [10] . Its correlation with metal-mold reactions has been exten -
sively documented previously [9] . For example, the wetting behavior of 
Ni- and Co-based superalloys on silica-aluminate-based molds is re -
ported to be highly affected by interfacial reactions [11] . Likewise, the 
impact of C and Hf on interface reactions and wettability of intermetallic 
(Ni
3
Al) superalloys along ceramic molds has been investigated, estab -
lishing a critical threshold for reactive elements like C and Hf that are 
required to initiate the interface reaction [12] . Hf content, when present 
in the matrix at levels exceeding 1.17 wt%, leads to the formation of 
HfO
2 
and has been shown to improve the wettability of alloy, as

2. Materials and method 
Five different types of superalloys, IN738, GTD-111, CM247LC, Ren ´e 
N5, and CMSX-4, were directionally solidified at a withdrawal rate of 
4 mm/min utilizing a six-ingot assembly. Each ingot possessed a 13 mm 
diameter, as depicted in Fig. 1 (a). The primary focus of our investigation 
is Hf-containing CM247LC Ni-based superalloy [18] , whose composition 
is given in Table 1 along with the other Ni-based superalloys. Metal -
lography of the samples in the lateral and transverse directions was 
performed along the length of the sample. For etching, a solution 
comprising 33 ml of nitric acid, 33 ml of distilled water, 33 ml of acetic 
acid, and 1 ml of hydrofluoric acid was employed. Detailed micro -
structural analysis was carried out using field emission scanning elec -
tron microscopy (FESEM, JSM-7001F, JEOL), electron backscattered 
diffraction (EBSD, SU-6600, Hitachi), transmission electron microscopy 
(TEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL), and electron probe micro-analyzer (EPMA, 
JXA-8530F, JEOL). Additionally, the simulation of elemental distribu -
tion in various phases of superalloys under equilibrium conditions was 
calculated using Thermocalc with the TTNi-8 database.

3.2. Interface reaction layer and related MC-type carbides 
The sections of the mold from bottom to top in the directional so -
lidification process can be correlated with the stages of the interfacial 
reaction due to the varying timelines available for each section. To 
further substantiate this claim, measurements of the oxide layer thick -
ness have been conducted as presented in Fig. 3 . The results support the 
hypothesis that the average thickness of the oxide layer increases from 
the bottom to the upper region of the solidified part. In the top part, the 
oxide layer reaches its greatest thickness, approaching approximately 
4.5 μ m, while in the bottom region, it measures between 2 and 3 μ m. 
This variation in oxide thickness reinforces the idea that the interfacial 
reaction is between the melt and the mold and it is a function of time 
available for the reaction. The SEM images attached provide visual ev -
idence of this oxide thickness gradient, which clearly illustrates the 
increasing layer thickness as one moves from the bottom to the top 
region. 
Furthermore, a slightly over-etched interface reveals an interaction

4. Discussion 
4.1. Heterogenous MC-type carbide growth along the interface 
The carbide-forming elements of metal are concentrated in the vi -
cinity of the interface layer and inter-dendritic regions, providing 
favorable conditions for the nucleation of MC-type carbides [30,31] . 
When considering the enrichment of carbide-forming elements and 
carbide-melt interface energy, heterogeneous nucleation emerges as the 
preferred mode, potentially occurring at temperatures above the liq -
uidus temperature [21,32] . Extensive reports document heterogeneous 
nucleation of MC-type carbides above γ dendrites, with oxides, nitrides, 
or carbonitrides serving as prominent nucleation sites [21,33,34] . Given 
the notably high melting point of carbides, such as HfC at 3930
◦
C, a 
substantial degree of undercooling is attainable, especially considering 
that the liquidus temperature is below 1500
◦
C. The critical nucleation 
radius for any MC-type carbide nuclei to grow in the liquid is inversely 
proportional to the aforementioned undercooling temperature. In the 
case of the parent superalloy of CM247LC, specifically the MAR-M247 
LC variant, nuclei as small as 8 nm were estimated to be sufficient for 
nucleation of MC-type carbides. Henceforth, the HfO
2 
layer present at 
the periphery of the melt in the current case, forming the melt-mold 
interface, emerges as a strategically favorable position for the nucle -
ation of MC-type carbides. 
There exists a subtle variation in the Hf content within the MC-type 
carbides regions, with higher concentrations observed in smaller MC- 
type carbides and lower concentrations in larger MC-type carbides, as 
evident from the delineated zone in the FE-EPMA Map ( Fig. 4 (a)). This 
discrepancy arises because the composition of MC-type carbides changes 
during nucleation and subsequent solubility-driven growth. Among the 
various carbides such as TaC, TiC, and HfC stands out as the most 
thermodynamically stable MC-type carbides, making it the preferential 
choice for initial formation [21] . The nucleation of MC-type carbides is 
anticipated to commence with a Hf-rich core, aligning with observations 
previously reported for Ni-based superalloys [2,35] . Over time, solute 
atoms such as Ta, W, or Ti are expected to replace Hf as the local 
environment becomes depleted in Hf [2,36] . 
The morphology of MC-type carbides is subject to variation, depen -
dent upon the composition, cooling rate, and their coherence with the 
matrix. Given that MC-type carbides exhibit coherent planes with the 
matrix ( γ ), they endeavor to adopt a shape that minimizes lattice misfit 
energy [22,36,37] . Consequently, a distinctive and crystallographic 
growth pattern of MC-type carbides is observable from the interface 
towards the matrix ( γ ). The presence of a higher Hf content in MC-type 
carbides tends to impart a blocky morphology. This shape adaptation 
serves to reduce the surface area of the carbides, thereby lowering their 
overall interface energy [35,38] .

The surface roughness of the mold should also be considered an 
important factor as it can affect both inclusion formation and melt 
wettability [10,42] . Both higher temperature and interfacial reactions 
causing localized compositional differences can affect the surface ten -
sion or wettability of the melt. This can trigger the Marangoni effect 
[43] — mass transfer due to differences in surface tension among two 
regions — leading the melt to ultimately end up in the mold ’ s pores or 
cracks. Additionally, the formation of oxide can lead to a significant 
volume change that can further induce stresses [44] . If the oxide is 
formed at the flat surface of the mold, the volume expansion can be 
mitigated by the excess melt, not assisting any cracking inside the mold. 
Nevertheless, when the melt is penetrated in the narrow pores or cracks, 
the formation of HfO

5. Conclusion 
In summary, this study comprehensively investigates the interface 
reaction between Ni-based superalloys and Al
2
O
3
-based ceramic molds. 
Our results highlight the pivotal role of Hf content in these alloys, pri -
marily influencing the behavior at the metal-mold interface. 
1. The interaction of Hf with the Al
2
O
3
-based mold results in the for -
mation of HfO
2
. The thickness of the HfO
2 
layer is also influenced by 
time, with a thicker layer forming in the upper section of the mold, 
where the melt solidifies last. 
2. HfO
2 
may act as a nucleation site for the precipitation of energeti -
cally stable Ta/Hf/Ti-rich MC-type carbides in alloys containing 
carbon as an alloying element during solidification. 
3. The increased wettability resulting from the interface reactions, 
combined with higher metallostatic pressure in lower regions of the 
ingot, leads to the penetration of the melt into the mold. 
4. A precise control over the Hf content and the surface roughness of the 
mold is required to effectively manage the aforementioned interface 
reactions.
