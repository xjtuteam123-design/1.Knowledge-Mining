ABSTRACT
Nickel alloys fabricated by laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) show great potential in aerospace application due to 
excellent high-temperature performance. However, non-weldable nickel alloys with high Ti and Al content 
usually lead to crack propagation during LPBF, causing poor formability and mechanical properties. Herein, we 
propose a novel method to enhance crack resistance by introducing trace carbon into IN939 nickel alloy, which 
exhibits a narrower solidification temperature range and lower internal stress level. The new IN939C nickel alloy 
yields crack-free LPBF samples across a wide range of processing parameters, and exhibits 56 % and 15 % higher 
tensile strength than IN939 at 25 
◦
C and 1000 
◦
C, respectively. Furthermore, thermodynamic and first-principles 
calculations show that carbon addition promotes carbide precipitation, modifies the solidification pathway and 
enhances carbide/matrix interfacial bonding strength. These findings provide a new alloy design route for the 
LPBF of crack-free nickel alloys with excellent mechanical properties.
1. Introduction
In the aerospace field, equipment must maintain efficient operation 
under extreme working conditions characterized by high temperatures 
and high stress, with many components operating in oxygen-rich envi -
ronments [ 1 ]. These harsh service environments impose stringent de -
mands on material performance, particularly for critical components 
such as turbine blades and combustion chambers [ 2 ]. Nickel alloys with 
high Al and Ti content were widely used in these applications due to 
their superior high-temperature properties [ 3 ]. However, conventional 
manufacturing of complex-shaped components usually incurs high 
production costs and substantial material waste. Additive 
manufacturing has become a key technology for achieving structural 
light weighting and enhancing component performance, which provides 
a reliable pathway for producing complex nickel alloy components [ 4 ].
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), a prevalent additive manufacturing 
technique, enables effective fabrication of complex aerospace compo -
nents [ 5 ]. However, it presents significant challenges when processing 
nickel alloys with high Al and Ti contents. Conventional nickel alloys, 
such as CM247LC [ 6 – 8 ], IN738LC [ 9 ] and IN939 [ 10 ], are highly sus -
ceptible to cracking during the LPBF process. The high cooling rate of 
LPBF generates significant temperature gradients that influence 
dendrite growth processes which introduces substantial residual 
stresses, increasing crack susceptibility [ 5 ]. Furthermore, the high pro -
portion of Al/Ti in nickel alloys lead to accelerated γ ′ phase precipitation 
rate, resulting in greater stress concentration and consequently 
increased propensity for crack formation [ 11 ].

suffers from severe hot cracking issues, significantly restricting their 
engineering applications [ 13 ]. Solidification and solid-state cracking 
was observed on LPBF IN939 [ 14 ]. Solidification cracks initiate when 
inter-dendritic feeding becomes insufficient to compensate for thermal 
shrinkage strains. This occurs as residual liquid, enriched in 
low-melting-point elements, is isolated within the dendritic network and 
cannot back-fill the areas under tension during the final stages of so -
lidification. Moreover, high residual tensile stresses introduced by the 
LPBF process and substantial precipitation of the γ ′ strengthening phase 
in the solid-state further embrittles the matrix. Consequently, solid-state 
cracks may initiate during cooling, as weak grain boundaries containing 
brittle eutectics fail to withstand these high residual stresses [ 15 – 17 ]. Li 
et al. [ 18 ] showed that enrichment of Ti, Ta, Zr, Nb, and C elements at 
crack tips of IN939 alloy promotes the formation of hard carbides, which 
obstruct dislocation motion and increase local stress. Moreover, a high 
Zr concentration increases crack susceptibility by broadening the so -
lidification temperature range. It prolongs the persistence of 
inter-dendritic liquid films during solidification and subsequent thermal 
cycling. Under severe stress concentration, these liquid films are prone 
to rupture. Therefore, tailoring solidification behavior and reducing 
internal stresses are key strategies to reduce the risk of both solidifica -
tion and solid-state cracking [ 15 ].
Researchers commonly attempt to reduce cracking by optimizing 
process parameters, fine-tuning alloy compositions, applying substrate 
preheating, or using hot isostatic pressing (HIP) [ 3 , 19 – 22 ]. However, 
current crack suppression methods have limitations, and inhibiting 
cracking in non-weldable nickel alloys remains a significant challenge in 
research. To address crack susceptibility, optimizing nickel alloy ’ s 
elemental composition and incorporating heterogeneous nucleating 
agents suppress crack formation effectively [ 23 – 27 ]. Zhang et al. [ 25 ] 
reported that Si addition to IN939 alloy processed by LPBF significantly 
reduced solidification cracking by promoting Si-rich precipitate forma -
tion at grain boundaries effectively, resulting in higher strength but 
lower ductility. Moreover, Bae et al. [ 28 ] demonstrated that the 
decomposition of TiB
2 
during melting led to the in-situ formation of 
Cr-based boride precipitates uniformly distributed in the IN939 matrix, 
which refined the microstructure and effectively inhibited crack for -
mation. These compositional changes impact other alloy properties and 
require systematic investigation.
Carbon is a critical alloying element that strongly affects both the 
solidification behavior and the mechanical performance of superalloys 
[ 29 – 31 ]. Previous studies have indicated that moderate carbon addition 
promotes stable carbide precipitation in nickel-based alloys, improving 
grain boundary strength and suppressing cracking [ 29 ]. They propose 
that a continuous carbide network undermines the stability of 
low-melting-point phases and disperses stress concentrations, thereby 
preventing hot cracking, while the resulting grain refinement further 
enhances mechanical performance. Furthermore, since carbide forma -
tion precedes the precipitation of low-melting eutectic constituents 
during solidification, carbon additions narrow the solidification interval 
of the alloy, which in turn lowers its susceptibility to cracking [ 32 ]. The 
carbide morphology, volume fraction, and size all have a direct impact 
on an alloy ’ s performance [ 31 ]. An insufficient carbon content promotes 
the formation of thin carbides along grain boundaries, which in turn 
degrades mechanical properties. By contrast, an overabundance of car -
bon drives the precipitation of script-like and elongated carbides, 
resulting in reduced mechanical performance [ 33 ]. This prompted us to 
investigate the influence of carbides on the microstructure and me -
chanical properties of IN939 alloy.
In this study, we deliberately introduce a low carbon level into IN939 
via high-energy ball-milled powder blending rather than pre-alloyed 
powder. This route promotes in-situ formation of fine MC carbides 
during LPBF and enables defect-free fabrication across an unusually 
wide processing window. It also refines the microstructure and delivers 
concurrent improvements in mechanical properties at both room and 
elevated temperatures. Theoretical calculations were carried out to 
examine the interfacial strength between Ni and MC carbides, contrib -
uting to a comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms 
governing carbide behavior within the matrix.

and reduced local strain which confirmed by a decrease in KAM. The

3.5. Fracture behavior
The SEM fractography of IN939 and IN939C alloy are presented in 
Fig. 9 (a – c). Fig. 9 (b) and (e) shows the room-temperature fracture 
morphologies of IN939 and IN939C alloy. The cracks of IN939 are 
apparent in Fig. 9 (b) highlighted by yellow arrows. The fracture surfaces 
of the IN939 alloy exhibit evident brittle fracture features, characterized 
by large tearing ridges and cleavage facets. However, dimples are still 
observed in IN939, as shown in Fig. 9 (c). The fracture surfaces with 
cleavage steps and dimples are proved to ductile-brittle mixed fracture. 
In contrast, the IN939C alloy shows abundant dimple features indicative 
of ductile fracture behavior. Only a few tearing ridges and cleavage 
facets are observed in IN939C, as shown in Fig. 9 (e and f). The grain 
boundary network of MC carbides induced by trace carbon in IN939C

4.2. First-principles calculation
The precipitation of carbides is an exothermic reaction that can 
balance the temperature gradient during the cooling process and miti -
gate the thermal stresses induced by rapid cooling. These effects explain 
the observed reduction in stress levels with the addition of carbon. The 
formation energies of carbides (TiC, TaC, WC, NbC, SiC, CrC) calculated 
using density functional theory (DFT), which are 1.659, 1.167, 
 0.275, 1.111, 0.425 and 0.328eV, respectively. TiC carbides are the 
most energetically favorable to form, which is consistent with our 
experimental results.
The atomic bonding characteristics at the interface significantly in -
fluence the mechanical properties, we calculated total and differential 
electron density distributions to investigate the interfacial bonding 
properties of γ -Ni/TiC. The differential charge density distributions and 
the binding energy calculation formulas are provided in the supporting 
materials. In our study, Ni (101) 1 × 2 × 1 supercell and TiC (110) were 
employed to model the γ -Ni (101)/TiC (110) interfaces, the lattice 
mismatch was less than 5 % [ 62 ]. The original surface structure can be 
found in Supplementary Material Fig. S7 . Ab initio calculations were 
carried out using non-spin polarized DFT with VASP [ 63 ]. The 
Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) [ 64 ] was used to treat 
exchange-correlation effects. Equilibrium lattice constants were deter -
mined by minimizing the total energy of k-point sampled supercells. The 
Monkhorst-Pack method [ 65 ] with the k-point grid of 6 × 6 × 1 with a 
smearing width of 0.05 eV were employed for surface structure. The 
kinetic cutoff energy was converged to 450 eV. The energy of conver -
gence tolerance was less than 1.0 × 10

In this study, a universal binding energy relation method was utilized 
to obtain the adhesion work (0.345 eV), which could be found in sup -

high adhesion work suggests that interfacial alloying tends to occur 
readily. Fig. 11 (a) presents the differential charge density for the [010] 
γ -Ni(101)/TiC(110) interface model, where yellow denotes regions of 
charge accumulation and cyan denotes regions of charge depletion. 
These electronic interactions have a significant impact on material 
properties [ 37 ]. A pronounced accumulation of charge at the interface 
indicates strong bonding [ 66 ], with interface Ti and Ni atoms acting as 
electron donors and C atoms as electron acceptors, which is ionic 
character of Ti-Ni interactions. Fig. 11 (b) and (c) show the electron 
localization function (ELF) for two atomic layers across the interface, the

compared to those around Ni atoms. Close to the interface, the ELF 
around C atoms is reduced, indicating more delocalized electrons and 
confirming charge transfer from Ni and Ti to C, Fig. 11 (d) shows the 
partial density of states of this system, and the overlapping states 
contributed to the hybridization of C-2p and Ti-3d orbital, so as to form 
Ni-C and Nb-C covalent bonds, which are in excellent agreement with 
the differential charge density distribution. Strong covalent bonds at the 
interface markedly enhance its resistance to thermal stress, thereby 
reducing the risk of cracking.

5. Conclusion
The effects of carbon addition on mechanical properties, micro -
structure evolution, and cracking resistance of LPBF IN939 nickel-based 
alloy were systematically investigated. An appropriate number of car -
bides is beneficial for enhancing grain boundary co-hessian and 
reducing cracking susceptibility. Based on experimental characteriza -
tion and theoretical calculations, the following major conclusions are 
drawn: 
1. Solidification cracking is the dominant cracking mode in LPBF IN939 
alloy parts. Nano-sized carbides were generated in IN939C by means 
of adding graphite, leading to crack suppression, while achieving 
excellent mechanical properties at 25 
◦
C (1566 MPa, 17.7 %) and 
1000 
◦
C (266 MPa, 7.9 %).
2. Microstructural analysis indicates that the addition of graphite pro -
motes the precipitation of fine carbides, predominantly at inter- 
dendritic regions. Carbides have a semi coherent relationship with 
the matrix.
3. The enhanced strength of the IN939C alloy is attributable to the 
Orowan strengthening mechanism. Carbides, acting as obstacles to 
dislocation movement, suppress grain growth and dislocation slip, 
thereby mitigating the accumulation of residual stresses.
4. Thermodynamic calculations and DSC results reveal that carbides 
elevate the liquidus temperature, and shortens the solidification in -
terval. IN939C alloy has a lower HCS due to smaller CTR and ICS 
index.
5. The more localized charge distribution within the carbides underpins 
their macroscopic stability in the matrix, and the differential charge 
density maps reveal pronounced interfacial charge transfer-arising 
from Ni-C and Nb-C covalent bonds as well as Ti-Ni ionic 
interactions-which gives rise to strong carbide/matrix interfacial 
bonding at the atomic scale.
