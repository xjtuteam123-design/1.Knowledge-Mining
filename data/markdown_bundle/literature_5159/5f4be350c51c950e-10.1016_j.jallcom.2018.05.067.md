ABSTRACT: High entropy alloys (HEAs) have five or more princ ipal elements with four core 
effects: high entropy, sluggish diffusion, severe l attice distortion, and cocktail effects. These 
effects lead to some distinct properties of HEAs. S ome HEAs are promising for high temperature 
applications and have the potential to replace Ni-b ase superalloys as the next generation 
high-temperature materials, such as high entropy su peralloys (HESAs) and refractory HEAs. The 
microstructures of HESAs consisting of γ and γ' phases are similar to that of Ni-base superalloys . 
Refractory HEAs contain refractory elements with hi gh melting temperatures. Thus, a number of 
aspects of HESAs and refractory HEAs are reviewed and discussed in the present paper, including 
microstructure, density, room-temperature mechanical properties, high-temperature strength, creep 
behavior, and oxidation resistance. Furthermore, a number of future research topics are suggested, 
emphasizing on developing high-performance high-temperature materials.

For traditional alloys, one or two principal compon ents are selected based on a specific

High-entropy alloys (HEAs), representing a new conc ept of alloy design, were proposed in 2004

multi-principal element alloys, baseless alloys, co ncentrated solid solution alloys, and

2. Core effects of HEAs 
2.1 High-entropy effect 
The entropy for HEAs refers mainly to configuration al entropy [42-44]. For an equimolar 
quinary random solid solution and stoichiometric in termetallic compounds, their configurational 
entropy is 1.61 R (R= 8.31 J/K mol) and can be considered to be zero, respectively. According to G 
= H − TS (where G is the Gibbs free energy, H is enthalpy, T is temperature, and S is entropy), the 
phases with higher entropy in alloys have lower Gibbs free energy. Thus, the high mixing entropy

in HEAs facilitates the formation of random solid-solution phases, rather than intermetallic phases. 
However, the high entropy effect cannot guarantee t he formation of a simple solid-solution phase 
in multi-components alloys. Other important factors, such as mixing enthalpy (∆Hmix ), atomic size 
difference (δ), and VEC [45-52], should be considered. Thus, som e criteria to predict the stability 
of solid solutions in HEAs have been proposed, which was reviewed in the literatures [53, 54]. For 
HESAs with excellent high-temperature mechanical pr operties, it was reported that the formation 
of solid-solution structures is in the range of 0 kJ/mol > ∆Hmix > 7 kJ/mol and 2% < δ < 6%, while 
precipitation-strengthened HESAs are in the range of 3 kJ/mol > ∆Hmix > 18 kJ/mol and 3% < δ < 
8%. All HESAs are in the range of VEC > 7.8 [55]. 
According to G = H – TS , the contribution of high entropy effect to the st ability of random 
solid-solution phases decreases with the decrease o f temperature. This means that the random 
solid-solution phases may be transformed to interme tallic phases when HEAs are annealed at 
relative low temperature [56-61]. For example, the refractory HEA HfNbTaTiZr has the single 
BCC phase after homogenization treatment. However, the HCP phase enriched with Hf and Ta 
(~25 at.% each) was observed in the BCC matrix afte r annealing at 600 or 800 °C [61]. It should 
be noted that the intermetallic phase may sometimes improve the properties of HEAs. The 
excellent high-temperature properties of HESAs is attributed to the formation of γ'(L1 2) phase (see 
Section 3). Thus, the exploration of HEAs with exce llent high-temperature properties should not 
be limited to simple solid-solution phases.

2.4 Cocktail effect 
The cocktail effect for metallic alloys was first p roposed by Ranganathan [107]. During 
designing elements of HEAs with excellent high-temp erature properties, the cocktail effect 
reminds us that their properties are certainly rela ted to the composing elements. For example, the 
addition of light elements, such as Al, can decreas e the density of HEAs. The addition of 
refractory elements, such as Nb [108], can increase the high-temperature properties. In addition to 
the properties of individual elements, the interact ion among composing elements, which may 
result in some exceptional properties, should also be considered. In Al–Co–Cr–Cu–Fe–Ni alloy 
system, the precipitation of Cu favors the formation of L1 2 ((Ni, Cu) 3Al) phase [109]. Furthermore, 
their hardness increases significantly with the inc reases in the Al content [110] due to the 
formation of a hard BCC phase, strong cohesive bond ing between Al and other elements, and 
solid-solution strengthening in Al–Co–Cr–Fe–Ni alloy system. 
3. High entropy superalloys 
3.1 Microstructure 
The microstructure of Ni-base and Co-base superallo ys with the excellent high-temperature 
strength consists mainly of γ and γ' phases [111-120], where the disordered γ phase exhibits the 
FCC structure and in nearly all cases it forms a co ntinuous matrix phase, and γ' forms as an 
ordered precipitate phase with L1 2 structure. The HEAs containing γ and γ' phases have also been 
reported recently [40,121-136] (see Table 1) and th e typical TEM images are shown in Fig. 1. 
Since their microstructure is similar to that of tr aditional superalloys, it is reasonable to define 
these HEAs as HESAs. 
In the Al–Co–Cr–Cu–Fe–Ni alloy system, Al and Ni ha ve the largest negative mixing

enthalpy among all the element pairs [137], which f avors the formation of ordered γ'(L1 2) phase. 
Cu has positive mixing enthalpy with all other elem ents except Al, and Cu and Ni have the 
smallest positive mixing enthalpy among all the element pairs. This will lead to that Cu is the first 
to be repelled from solid solutions, when the tempe rature decreases after the homogenization at 
high temperature. During the Cu-repelling process, Al and Ni are most favorable to form Cu–Ni–
Al–rich phase in order to keep a lower energy state . When the temperature continues to decrease, 
the phase separation will take place again in the C u–Ni–Al–rich phases, forming the Cu–rich and 
(Ni, Cu) 3Al phases. Consequently, the ordered γ' precipitates are adjacent to the Cu-rich 
precipitates [109, 130] (see Fig. 2). 
Usually, Ni-base superalloys are not sable at high temperature. High Fe content leads to the 
formation of Fe–Cr–rich TCP phase at 900 °C for less than 100 hours in some commercial Ni-base 
superalloys, such as Udimet 700, Hastelloy X, and R ene’ N6 [135, 138, 139]. For RR2071 
superalloy with no Fe and lower Cr, β phase can also form rapidly at 900 °C [140]. HESAs have 
higher stability at high temperature compared with traditional Ni-base superalloys, which is due to 
high entropy and sluggish diffusion effects. After isothermal aging at 900 °C for 300 hours, TCP 
phases cannot be observed in the HESA Ni 40.7 Al 7.8 Co 20.6 Cr 12.2 Fe 11.5 Ti 7.2 [135]. After isothermal 
aging at 1050 °C for 500 hours, the microstructures of Al 7.8 Co 20.6 Cr 12.2 Fe 11.5 Ni 40.7 Ti 7.2 still remain 
stable γ-γ' [40]. In addition to high stability, the high con centration of Al, Fe, Cr and Ti elements 
can decrease the cost and density of HESAs. The den sities for most of reported HESAs are below 
8.0 g/cm 3, as shown in Table 1. In contrast, it is 8.5~9.0 g /cm 3 for conventional Ni-base 
superalloys [141].

4.1. High-temperature strength of refractory HEAs 
The traditional alloys based on refractory metal el ements with melting point higher than

Unfortunately, these refractory alloys show poor du ctility, high densities, and poor oxidation 
resistance at elevated temperature [167-169]. The r efractory HEAs consisting of refractory 
elements were proposed by Senkov et al. [29, 170], where two refractory HEAs NbMoTaW and 
VNbMoTaW with single-phase disordered BCC structure (see Fig. 6) have extremely high yield 
strength of over 400 MPa at 1600 °C. In contrast, the Ni-base superalloy Inconel 718 significantly

[33, 108, 171-189 ]. 
Besides high melting point, refractory HEAs have al so the following high-temperature 
strengthening mechanisms. First, the grain boundary sliding, which softens alloys at high 
temperature, is closely relative to the diffusivity of elements. The diffusion of elements in 
refractory HEAs is sluggish even at high temperatur e. For example, the alloys NbMoTaW and 
VNbMoTaW still have dendritic structure after defor mation at 1400 °C [170]. Thus, the grain 
boundary sliding is difficult. Second, the driving force for recrystallization to eliminate 
dislocations and stacking faults depends on the dis location energy and grain boundary energy, 
which can be regarded as the energy difference between the energy state of dislocation and that of 
matrix, and between the energy state of grain boundary and that of matrix, respectively [190]. The 
severe distortion effect of HEAs increases the ener gy state of matrix and then decreases the 
driving force for recrystallization. Consequently, the recrystallization temperature (above 0.6 Tm 
[191-193], and Tm is the melting temperature) of HEAs is much higher than that (0.33 – 0.5 Tm) of 
traditional alloys.

5.2. Oxidation behavior of refractory HEAs 
Conventional refractory alloys have extremely poor oxidation resistance. For example, V is 
easily oxidizable. Furthermore, V 2O5 has a low melting point (690°C) and boiling point (1750°C) 
and then easily fuses or volatilizes at high temper ature [240]. Mo forms gaseous oxides above 
800 °C that evaporate extremely fast [241]. W forms gaseous WO 3, and the severe evaporation of 
tungsten trioxide appears above 1150 °C [242]. Usually, the evaporation of oxides will lead to that 
the oxide scales become easily porous, which prompt s oxygen ingress towards metal-oxide 
interface. Thus, the oxidation rate increases signi ficantly. In addition, the oxide of Nb alloys does 
not easily fuse or volatilize at high temperature, but the solubility and diffusivity of oxygen are

high [243-245]. Without protective coating, the oxi dation of conventional Nb alloys occurs very 
rapidly with considerable weight gain, which generally exceeds 150 mg/cm 2 per hour. 
Compared with conventional refractory alloys, the o xidation rate of refractory HEAs can 
decrease significantly. It was reported that the magnitude of specific mass change in the refractory 
HEAs MoCrTiAlW and MoCrTiAlNb [229, 246, 247] is ~0 .25 mg/cm 2 per hour, which is 
considerably lower than that (>20-60 mg/cm 2 per hour [248-250]) of pure refractory metals. Thi s 
difference can be ascribed to the following reasons. First, for conventional alloys, the formation of 
preferable metal oxide scale causes a decrease in the concentration of alloying elements in matrix, 
an increase in the oxygen solubility and eventually internal oxidation [251, 252]. In contrast, the 
complex oxides form in refractory HEAs, such as TiN b 2O7 and Nb 2Zr 6O17 for the alloy NbTiZrV 
[253], Nb 3Cr 2O10 and Nb 2Zr 8O21 for NbCrMo 0.5 Ta 0.5 TiZr [234], and CrTaO 4 for TaMoCrTiAl [229, 
246, 247]. Thus, the solubility of oxygen decreases in refractory HEAs during oxidation process 
compared with conventional refractory alloys. Secon d, due to highly alloying, the diffusion of 
oxygen in refractory HEAs is slow. It was reported that the effective coefficient for oxygen 
diffusion through oxide/alloy at 1000 °C is about 3 .0×10 -9 cm 2/s in the alloy NbCrMo 0.5 Ta 0.5 TiZr 
[254], which is two orders of magnitude lower than the oxygen diffusion in pure Nb [255, 256]. 
Third, there is a high content of Cr and Al due to high entropy effect in refractory HEAs. This is in 
favor of the formation of protective oxides Cr 2O3 and Al 2O3 on the surface of refractory HEAs. 
Thus, their oxidation rate will decrease. In additi on, the formation of protective oxides Cr 2O3 and 
Al 2O3 can also suppress the evaporation of oxides of ref ractory elements [122, 240]. It was 
reported that the evaporation of Mo oxides in the r efractory HEA NbMoCrTiAl is presumably 
restricted to the initial transient oxidation stage and Mo is detected in the metallic substrate [229].

6. Concluding remarks and suggestions for future research 
Development of heat engines demands improving furth er the high-temperature properties of 
engine component material. This article reviews the advancement of HESAs and refractory HEAs 
with excellent high-temperature properties. The mic rostructure of HESAs consisting of γ and γ' 
phases is similar to that of Ni-base superalloys and the flow stress anomalies can also be observed 
at high temperature. Compared with Ni-base superall oys, HESAs have higher stability at high 
temperature, lower cost, lower density, and positiv e lattice misfit. Furthermore, the 
high-temperature strength of HESAs can further incr ease due to the severe lattice distortion and 
enhancement of APB energy caused by highly alloying. 
The refractory HEAs consisting of refractory elemen ts have more excellent high-temperature 
properties than those of traditional refractory all oys. Compared with traditional refractory alloys,

the high-temperature strength of refractory HEAs co nsisting of refractory elements can be further 
improved due to restrain of grain boundary sliding caused by sluggish diffusion effect and 
decrease in the driving force of recrystallization resulting from high severe lattice distortion of 
HEAs. Furthermore, the plasticity of refractory HEAs can be promoted significantly by decreasing 
VEC, twining-induced plasticity, transformation-induced plasticity, or inhibiting the formation of 
intermetallic compound and the density of refractor y HEAs can be decreased by the addition of 
light elements or replacement of some heavy elements with relatively light elements. 
The formation of protective Cr 2O3 and Al 2O3 scale in the HESAs containing Al and Cr can 
improve their oxidation resistance at high temperat ure. However, the large CTEs difference 
between the alumina and HESAs substrate facilitates the scale to spall during cooling from 
oxidation temperature. For refractory HEAs, the for mation of protective oxides Cr 2O3 and Al 2O3 
can suppress the evaporation of oxides of refractor y elements at high temperature, though the 
addition of some refractory elements, such as Nb, T i and W, hampers the formation of a 
continuous and protective Al 2O3 layer. Furthermore, the lower solubility and slower diffusion of 
oxygen in refractory HEAs during oxidation process can also lead to the decrease in oxidation rate 
of refractory HEAs, compared with conventional refractory alloys. 
Though the HESAs and refractory HEAs have excellent high-temperature properties, the field 
is still in its incipient stage and there are still some problems involved in the further research: 
1) The volume fraction and size of ordered γ' particles in superalloys affect significantly their 
high-temperature mechanical properties. The optimum volume fraction and size of ordered γ' 
particles in the Ni-base superalloys are close to 70% and 500 nm, respectively. However, for most 
of reported HESAs, the volume fraction and size of ordered γ' particles are less than the optimum

elements or heat treatment. 
2) In addition to high-temperature strength, the en gine component material should also have 
high ductility at room temperature, low density and high oxidation resistance. Thus, designing 
refractory HEAs with excellent comprehensive perfor mance by optimizing microstructure and 
tailoring constituents will be still performed in the further work. 
3) Single-crystal alloys exhibit incipient melting temperature superior to polycrystalline [258]. 
Furthermore, the absence of grain boundaries, which often act as crack initiation sites in these 
alloys at high temperature, provides significant en hancements in thermal-fatigue resistance. 
However, the single crystal of HESAs and refractory HEAs has not been prepared successfully up 
to now. Thus, their directional solidification beha vior and single-crystal preparation should be 
carried out in the further work. 
4) In addition to high-temperature strength, the cr eep behavior and fatigue properties at high

applied to heat engines. Up to now, the studies on creep behavior of HESAs and refractory HEAs 
are very little and their fatigue properties at high temperature have not been reported.
