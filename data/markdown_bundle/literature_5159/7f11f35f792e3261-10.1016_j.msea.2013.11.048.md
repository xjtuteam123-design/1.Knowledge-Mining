abstract
Microstructural features, mechanical and steam oxidation properties of ferritic 18 –23 wt% chromium
model steels, strengthened by the precipitation of intermetallic phases, were investigated. The behavior
of the commercially available ferritic steel Crofer
s 22 H [1–4], originally developed for the application in
automotive solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) powered auxiliary power units (APUs) [5–7], was compared with
that of optimized model alloys with systematic variations in Cr, Nb, W and Ti content. The main aim of
the studies was the estimation of the potential suitability of these steels as construction materials in
power plants with live steam temperatures around 600 1C.
Some of the optimized trial alloys demonstrated remarkable characteristics at 600 1C. Results of
constant load creep experiments and steam oxidation tests indicate the potential of these alloys as
candidate materials for application in highly ef ﬁcient steam power plants with steam temperatures
above 620 1C. At high stress levels some of the presented alloys, especially those containing enhanced
levels of W and Nb, even exhibit higher creep strength than standard ferritic –martensitic steel P92. The
amount of strengthening (Fe,Si,Cr)
2(W,Nb) – Laves phase particles is found to correlate well with the
level of W- and Nb-alloying and in contrast to ferritic – martensitic steels, the formed Laves phase
particles are highly stable at elevated temperature. Owing to the high contents of chromium, the model
alloys have excellent steam oxidation resistance. A way to preserve favorable steam oxidation properties
and diminish undesirable (Fe,Cr)- s-phase formation is outlined.

1. Introduction
Resource protection and decrease of CO 2 emissions by ef ﬁ-
ciency increase of energy conversion systems are the main driving
forces for the development of highly heat resisting construction
materials. Most state of the art steam power plants employ
ferritic–martensitic 9 –12 wt% Cr steels, in some cases – particu-
larly in superheaters and reheaters – also austenitic steels. For a
further improvement in ef ﬁciency to about 50%, a further increase
of steam parameters to approximately 650 1C/300 bar [8,9] would
be necessary. Owing to its con ﬁned creep strength, but even more
because of its steam oxidation resistance being limited by the
relatively low chromium content, the concept of ferritic–martensitic
9–12 wt% chromium steels seems to hit technological limitations
[10]. Creep strengthening of these materials is mainly obtained by
microstructural stabilization due to distribution of ﬁne carbide and
nitride precipitates. The limited thermodynamic stability of the
strengthening precipitates, as well as poor steam oxidation resis-
tance, restrains the use of the well known ferritic –martensitic steel
P92 to temperatures of about 620 1C [9]. Adequate steam oxidation
resistance, up to temperatures of about 650 1C, would require
signiﬁcantly increased chromium contents [1 1,12]. Newly devel-
oped steels with higher chromium contents from 1 1 to 12 wt%, such
as VM12 [13],N F 1 2 [14] and T122 [14], exhibited an accelerated
decrease in creep strength upon long-term operation. Because of
the high chromium content, which is bene ﬁcial for oxidation
resistance, these materials are prone to increased precipitation of

In the year 2001 the Japanese National Institute of Materials
Science (NIMS) published its concept of a ferritic 15 wt% Cr steel
[1 7], strengthened by the precipitation of intermetallic phases.
During the last decade several variants with promising 650 1Cc r e e p
properties [18,19] were developed, some of them equaling, others
even outbalancing current creep strength enhanced ferritic –mar-
tensitic (CSEF) materials like P92 [19–21]. Because of the precipita-
tion of a large number of strengthening intermetallic particles,
which consume chromium from the steel matrix, the remaining
chromium reservoir is likely to drop to values below 13 wt% [20]
and thus may appear to be relatively low to provide suf ﬁcient long-
term resistance against steam oxidation at 650 1C [1 1].
At the same time, the Institute for Energy and Climate
Research, Microstructure and Properties of Materials (IEK-2) of
Forschungszentrum Jülich and ThyssenKrupp VDM (now Out-
okumpu VDM) developed high chromium (22 wt% Cr), ferritic
stainless steels for the application in high-temperature solid oxide
fuel cell (SOFC) stacks [22–26]. This collaboration resulted in the
now commercially available interconnector steels named Crofer s
22 APU [27] and Crofers 22 H [1]. Fuel cell application at operating
temperatures in the range of 700 1C–800 1C requires the formation
of an electrically conductive oxide scale at the surface. Standard
deoxidants like Al or Si therefore have to be avoided in the melting
of Crofers 22 APU, because these elements would form electrically
insulating sub-surface oxides beneath the external chromia base
scale during fuel cell operation. Consequently Crofer s 22 APU is
produced by comparatively expensive vacuum induction melting
processes. The alloy variant Crofer s 22 H is a logical further
development of the original material Crofer s 22 APU. Develop-
ment goals were the reduction of production costs, while main-
taining or improving all fuel cell relevant properties like low
thermal expansion, oxidation and creep resistance as well as
electrical conductivity of the surface oxide scale in the tempera-
ture range from 700 1Ct o8 0 0 1C. All these goals were achieved by
combined alloying of relatively small amounts of tungsten (2 wt%),
niobium (0.5 wt%) and silicon (0.25 wt%). Compared to Crofer s 22
APU a reduction of the minimum creep rates of at least an order of
magnitude was achieved [3] by combined solid solution strength-
ening and the precipitation of intermetallic (Fe,Cr) 2(Nb,W) – Laves
phase particles, ﬁnely dispersed in the fully ferritic matrix. It was
proposed [25] that the addition of Nb decreases the activity of Si in
the ferrite matrix, which is partly related to the fact that the Laves
phase exhibits a substantial solubility for silicon [3,28]. In this way
electrically insulating subscale silica formation beneath the exter-
nal chromia scale during actual service at, e.g. at 800 1Ci s
prevented. Consequently, minor amounts of silicon may be added
as de-oxidants during the alloy melting process, thus allowing the
use of conventional, cost effective manufacturing processes for
Crofers 22 H. Some of the advanced model alloys, which were
further optimized starting from the technological base of the
commercial Crofer s 22 H grade, show very promising creep
properties in the temperature range relevant for supercritical
steam power plants (600 1C–650 1C). Moreover, they exhibit excel-
lent steam oxidation resistance, owing to their suf ﬁciently high
chromium contents of 18 –23 wt%.
In the present study the creep and steam oxidation properties
and corresponding microstructures of optimized model alloys are
outlined and compared to state of the art CSEF steels.
2. Experimental
2.1. Materials and preparation
All presented model alloys were manufactured by Outokumpu
VDM (formerly ThyssenKrupp VDM) by vacuum melting of
approximately 10 kg ingots (initial thickness: 80 mm) with sub-
sequent annealing at 1080 1C for 2 h, followed by hot rolling at
980 1Cd o w nt oa ﬁnal plate thickness of 16 mm and subsequent
air cooling. The chemical compositions are given in Table 1 and
were analyzed by Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission
Spectroscopy (ICP-OES).
The ﬁrst group, consisting of two alloy batches, is characterized
by a composition according to the commercial “Crofers 22 H ”–
speciﬁcation with nominally 22% Cr, 2% W, 0.5% Nb, 0.25% Si (all
values are given in wt%, cf. Table 1 ) and distinct variations in
tungsten and/or niobium contents. The second group ( “zero Ti ”)
differs from the ﬁrst by minimized titanium content only. Com-
pared to this, the “low chromium ” (“low Cr ”) variant features a
reduced chromium content of nominally 18 wt%, in order to
minimize the formation of the intermetallic FeCr-( s)-phase at
temperatures from 600 1C to 650 1C (cf. Fig. 1a).

Supplementary calculations, performed at the actual composi-
tions (cf. Table 1 ) of the 22 wt% Cr batches “1.75W0.57Nb”,
“2.5W0.57Nb0Ti” and the “18Cr” batch, show ( Fig. 1 b) distinct
dependencies of the volume fractions of two different Laves
phases (denoted as “HTLaves”, for high temperature and “LTLaves”
for low temperature Laves phase) from the actual W and Nb
contents, because these elements are the main constituents of the
(Fe,Cr,Si)2(Nb,W) phase. According to the calculations the phases
do not differ in crystal structure (both C14), but in chemical
composition. In the temperature range from 600 1C to 650 1C the
LT-/HT-phase is composed of approximately 20 –25/7% Cr, 40/35%
Fe, 35/15% Nb, 15/45% W and 2/0.5% Si (all units in wt%). The
overall amount (sum) of both the Laves phases gains by approxi-
mately 20% with rising content of tungsten from the
“1.75W0.57Nb” batch (1.96/1.78 vol% at 600/650 1C) to the
“2.5W0.57Nb0Ti” batch (2.48/2.28 vol% at 600/650 1C). On the
other hand, the LT-Laves phase stability range diminishes with
rising W content, what might have distinct impact on coarsening
rates and thus microstructural stability. It is reported, that MX
precipitates form easily in ferrite with Ti-concentrations higher
than 0.002 wt% [33]. The effect of Ti, C and N on the Laves phase
proportion is a complex one: Nb is a strong carbonitride former,
which results in a reduction of the amount of Nb available for the
formation of intermetallic precipitates [34–36]. Comparing the
calculation results for the actual “18Cr” batch, the nominal “low
Cr” speciﬁcation ( Fig. 1b) and the higher alloyed “2.5W0.57Nb0Ti”
batch it is apparent, that comparably small amounts of Ti, C and N
may adversely in ﬂuence at least a part of the bene ﬁcial impact of
increased W- and Nb-contents on the amount of Laves phase.
Compared to the increase in Laves phase content from the
“1.75W0.57Nb” to the “18Cr” composition, the further gain
reached by even higher W- and Nb-contents seems to be com-
paratively small ( “2.5W0.57Nb0Ti” batch, Fig. 1 b), which is attri-
butable to enhanced formation of Nb consuming MX-particles due
to the low Ti content of the “zero Ti ” batches. To increase
complexity, TiN particles are furthermore known to act as pre-
ferred nucleation sites of niobium consuming Nb(C,N) [37], which
may further complicate the impact of MX particle formation on
Laves phase precipitation.
Silicon incorporation into the (Fe) 2(Mo/W) Laves phase was
shown to have a strong effect on Laves phase nucleation and
growth [28,38]. The impact of silicon incorporation into the
strengthening (Fe,Cr,Si) 2(Nb,W) Laves phase [3,4,25] in the pre-
sented model alloys is subject of ongoing research.

Specimens for the investigation of the initial microstructures

characterization the specimens were mounted in epoxy resin,

3.4.1. Grain structure
Fig. 7 displays light optical micrographs, taken from (a) the
necked and (b) the uniform deformation (appr. 10 mm away from
the rupture site) area of the “1.75W0.57Nb” specimen, crept at
600 1C and 145 MPa ( tr: 9,661 h). Obviously creep did not change
the grain morphology signi ﬁcantly: In the area of uniform defor-

state. The grains appear slightly elongated in the tensile direction.
Formation of subgrain structures was neither detected in the
uniform deformation area, nor in the necked proportion of the
specimen. Creep cavities in the necked area formed preferentially
at grain boundaries ( Fig. 7 a). The dark structures alongside grain
boundaries in the area of uniform deformation ( Fig. 7 b) do not
represent creep cavities, but pits caused by sample preparation
for light optical microscopy. (Fe,Cr)- s-phase precipitates, located
at the grain boundaries (cf. Fig. 1 1a), were released by etching.
A detail analysis of the necked area did not yield a correlation
between s-phase formation and the generation of cavities.

3.4.3. Incorporation of silicon into the Laves phase
In addition to the SEM studies a number of TEM analyses were
performed on the 160 MPa/600 1C creep specimen of the
“1.75W0.57Nb” batch (tR ¼ 7,545 h). A TEM micrograph, taken from
the head section is shown in Fig. 10 a, revealing a substantial
number of ﬁne particles in the grain interior, often located along
dislocations. The micrograph in Fig. 10b was taken from the gauge
section of the same specimen and illustrates a nice example of
dislocation pinning by ﬁne particles. The matrix composition of
76.5% Fe, 21% Cr, 0.2% Si, 0.2% Nb and 1.5% W (wt%), evaluated by
energy dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX), indicates a depletion of the
precipitate forming elements (Cr, Nb, W, Si) in the ferritic matrix.
EDX analysis in combination with wave length dispersive X-ray
analysis (WDX) con ﬁrmed the precipitates to be mixed interme-
tallic phases of the AB 2 Laves type with a chemical composition of
43.5% Fe, 8% Cr, 1.5% Si, 25% Nb and 21.5% W (wt%). The measured
composition neither corresponds well to the composition of the
calculated LT, nor the HT Laves phase. For this reason the validity
of commercially available thermodynamical databases for the
outlined alloys is considered to be doubtful. Generation of valid
data thus constitutes a high priority task of future research and
development.

3.4.4. Repartitioning of the Laves phase and s-phase formation
Further microstructural analyses of the selected creep speci-
mens were carried out to get more insight into the Laves phase
redistribution and formation of the undesired (Fe,Cr)- s-phase
during long-term high temperature exposure.
In both the 22 wt% Cr ( Fig. 1 1a: “2.02W0.48Nb”,2 8 , 2 2 4 h )a n d
18 wt% Cr (Fig. 1 1b: “18Cr”, 29,395 h) materials narrow precipitate free
zones occur along grain boundaries, which themselves are decorated
by relatively “coarse” (compared to the intragranular) Laves phase
precipitates after long-term service. Detailed examination of deforma-
tion and damage mechanisms is another future task to clarify a
possible impact of this phenomenon on creep lifetime. As expected
for high Cr alloyed ferritic steels, the undesirables-phase forms during
high temperature exposure at 600 1C. The “2.02W0.48Nb” batch
(22 wt% Cr) occasionally exhibited agglomerates of the s-phase at
grain boundaries, especially at triple junctions (Fig. 1 1a). The Thermo-
calc results ( Fig. 1 a) of the Cr-reduced “18Cr” batch seem to be
qualitatively correct in that reducing the amount ofs-phase formation
(Fig. 1 1b) is possible by lowering the chromium content.
4. Summary and conclusion
The microstructural, mechanical and steam oxidation proper-
ties of three groups of fully ferritic model alloys, strengthened by a
combination of solid solution strengthening and ﬁne, homoge-
nously distributed intermetallic precipitates, was investigated.
Alloys ranging at the upper limit of the commercial Crofer s 22
H speci ﬁcation, regarding the W and Nb-contents, show remark-
ably enhanced creep resistance at 600 1C compared to batches in
the lower range, because of increased amounts of (Fe,Cr,Si) 2(Nb,W)
– Laves precipitates and decreased particle size. The precipitates
remain remarkably small even after exposure durations of more
than 30,000 h at 600 1C. The presented materials show excellent
steam oxidation resistance and can even combat conventional
CSEF steels in terms of 600 1C creep strength over the durations
tested so far (approximately 40,000 h). A reduction in chromium
content down to 18 wt% yielded a signi ﬁcant reduction in s-phase
precipitation without a loss in creep performance and resistance to
steam oxidation.
It remains a high priority task of future alloy development to
generate optimized material properties at 650 1C by means of an
advantageous combination of the bene ﬁcial effects of reduced
Cr- and increased W- and Nb-contents.
