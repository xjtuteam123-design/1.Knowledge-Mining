abstract
The structure and chemistry of Ni eCoeAleTi quaternary superalloys are investigated at the micro-
structural and atomic scale. Atom probe tomograghy (APT) is used to quantify phase compositions and
elemental partitioning behavior. Using aberration corrected electron microscopy, the site occupancy of
the atoms within the A
3 B structure (L1 2etype) g0 phase is determined via atomic resolution energy
dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). Ni is observed to preferentially occupy the A sub-lattice positions
for all alloys, while Al and Ti occupy the B sub-lattice. In contrast, Co's site preference changes from a
random distribution to the A sub-lattice as the alloy composition changes, in agreement with theoretical
predictions from literature. Finally, the lattice strain across the
g0/g interfaces is measured as a function of
alloy composition.

1. Introduction
Niebased superalloys offer superior performance for high-
temperature applications such as aircraft engines and power
generator turbines due to their high creep and corrosion resistance.
These enhanced mechanical properties are attributed to coherent
L12 g0eNi3 Al precipitates dispersed in a continuous g face-centered
cubic (fcc) matrix [1,2]. Through chemical alloying with a wide
range of other elements, the chemical and mechanical properties
can be further improved. Among several alloying elements, Co and
Ti are of particular interest because the addition of Ti to Ni eAl in-
creases the volume fraction and solvus temperature of the L1 2
phase, while Co increases the creep resistance by reducing the
stacking fault energy in the
g matrix [1,3]. To this end, under-
standing of phase separation, site occupancy of constituent ele-
ments, chemical behavior at local features such as interfaces, etc
and their connection with lattice mis ﬁt is of essential importance
for modern alloy design.
The site preference of solute atoms in the L1 2 structure has
signiﬁcant implications on the high etemperature mechanical
properties, microstructural stability and resistance to chemical
degradation [4]. In the g0 phase, the L12eordered structure consists
of two sub-lattices, the Ni sub-lattice or A-site corresponding to the
face-centered position and the Al sub-lattice or B-site corre-
sponding to the corner position of the cubic unit cell. A common
approach to predicting the site preference behavior of elements in
Ni
3 Al was derived from the direction of the solubility lobe of the
L12eNi3 Al phase in the ternary phase diagram [5]. Further, ﬁrst-
eprinciples calculations have been extensively utilized to study the
site preference behavior of ternary alloying elements in L1 2eNi3 Al
at 0 K and elevated temperatures by calculating the defect forma-
tion energies [6e10]. From ﬁrsteprinciples calculations, it was
observed that most transition metals exhibited a strong Al-site
preference, with only a few exhibiting a weak Ni esite preference
[9,1 1]. However, the site preference for some transition metals in
L12eNi3 Al, for example, Co was found to be composition and
temperature dependent [6,9,10]. Experimentally, M €ossbauer effect
study [12], atom probe ﬁeldeion microscopy (APFIM) [13,14] and
atom location by channeling enhanced microanalysis (ALCHEMI)
[15e17] have been utilized to examine the substitution behavior of
solute atoms in Ni3 Al alloys. Nevertheless, site preference study on
atom column epereatom column basis requires methods with
enhanced spatial sensitivity.
Lattice mis ﬁt and coherency strain between the phases also
plays a key role in the precipitate morphology and stability under
stress at high temperature [18]. Conventionally, diffraction tech-
niques, such as X-ray diffraction (XRD) [19,20], neutron diffraction
(ND) [3] and convergent beam electron diffraction (CBED) [21e23],* Corresponding author.

2. Materials and method
Three NieCoeTieAl alloys with nominal compositions listed in
Table 1 were fabricated and compared in this study. Throughout the
manuscript, these are referenced with regard to the Co at%, i.e.
15Co, 30Co, and 55Co alloys. The alloys were directionally solidiﬁed
via the Bridgman process, as adapted from Ref. [3]. Solution heat
treatments were performed in a quartz tube at 1330 /C14 C (15Co &
30Co alloys) and 1245 /C14 C (55Co alloy). Subsequently, all the alloys
were aged at 750 /C14 C for 7 days to ensure homogeneity.
Atom probe specimens were prepared by electrodischarge
machining (EDM) and electropolishing, providing a tip radius of
approximately 50 nm. The APT experiments were carried out with a
Local Electrode Atom Probe (LEAP 3000X Si) using pulsed-voltage
mode. The data collection was performed at a pulse fraction,
evaporation rate, specimen temperature, pulse frequency, and
gauge pressure of 0.2, 0.002 ions pulse /C0 1, 55 K, 200 kHz, and
<5.0 /C2 10/C0 8 Pa respectively. Specimens for TEM were prepared via
focused ion beam (FIB) lift-out using a FEI Quanta 3D FEG to
minimize magnetic interaction by limiting the sample volume. Bulk
sample removal was achieved using a 30 kV Ga þ beam, subse-
quently followed by 5 kV Ga þ a n d2k VG a þ milling to reduce
surface damage. To minimize surface oxide formation, the speci-
mens were kept in vacuum before being transferred to the micro-
scope within 48 h after preparation.
Scanning transmission electron microscopy and EDX were per-
formed using a probe-corrected FEI Titan G2 60 e300 kV S/TEM
equipped with a Schottky ﬁeld emission gun (X-FEG) source and
operated at 200 kV. The probe convergence semi eangle and inner
collection semi-angle for HAADF imaging were 13.5 mrad and
77 mrad, respectively. Revolving STEM (RevSTEM) image series
were acquired and contained a total of 40,1024 /C2 1024 frames with
a dwell time of 2 ms/pixel [24].A9 0 /C14 rotation was introduced to the
scan coordinate system between each frame. Atom column posi-
tions in the STEM images were determined by ﬁtting each atom
column to a two-dimensional Gaussian function and then projected
into a matrix representation using the Atom Column Indexing
approach [27]. Sample thickness was measured by comparing po-
sition averaged convergent beam diffraction (PACBED) captured at
each analysis area to simulations [28]. For the lattice mis ﬁt calcu-
lation, four image series were analyzed in the g0 and g regions,
respectively for each alloy. EDX maps were generated using the X-
ray K-line for each element. These maps were then averaged to a
single unit cell using the lattice-averaging method from Ref. [29].
Atomic resolution EDX simulations were performed using the
mSTEM software package [30] which utilizes a quantum excitation
of phonons (QEP) model [31]. The images were simulated using
experimental conditions. Simulated images were convolved with a
Gaussian function of 1.3 Å full-width half-max to approximately
account for the ﬁnite effective source size. Blurred maps were
resampled to match the pixel size of the experimental maps.
Furthermore, noise was applied to the simulated maps using a
Poisson distribution by taking into account the acquisition time,
beam current and detector solid angle from experiment.

3.2. Site preference investigation in the L1 2eg0 phase
While HAADF STEM images are generally considered to be
directly interpretable in terms of type and number of atoms pre-
sent, the site preference for the atoms in the g0 is not readily
determined here [32,33]. This is due to the number of different
atom types present as well as their relatively close atomic numbers.
Instead, atomic resolution STEM EDX analysis captures site prefer-
ence behavior of the g0 precipitates as shown in Fig. 3. To avoid the
compositional gradient at the g0/g interfaces, the EDX maps are
acquired at or near the center of g0 precipitates.
For all alloys, it is evident that Al and Ti preferentially occupy the
B sub-lattice, and Ni preferentially occupy the A sub-lattice. The site
preference of Co, however, is less clear, particularly for 15Co. It is
worth noting that despite the relatively low concentration of Co in
the g0 phase of this alloy (~ 5 at. %), the total counts of Co eKa signal
detected is roughly the same as that for Ti eK in the same region. In
other words, the Co counts are suf ﬁcient to produce a signal-
etoenoise ratio (SNR) comparable to Ti. To aid in the visualization
of the site preference, lattice eaveraged maps are presented in the
inset for each map. In the case of 30Co and 55Co alloys, Co is
observed to prefer the Ni esites, while 15Co shows no clear
preference.
To clarify the behavior observed in the atomic resolution EDX
maps, simulations are conducting using three-dimensional super-
cells of the L1 2eNi3 Al structure with 10 /C2 10 /C2 140 unit cells
(56000 atoms). The supercell compositions are designed to closely
approximate those of the L12 phase from APT. For the ﬁrst supercell,
the composition is 71.27Ni e4.875Coe18.5036Ale5.3482Ti at.%,
where Ti is located on the B sub-lattice and Co is situated on both A
and B. In the second supercell, the composition is
70.01Nie4.9875Coe19.157Ale5.8429Ti at.%, where Ti atoms are
located on the B sub-lattice while Co occupy only A positions. The
lattice parameter of the unit cell is 3.57 Å, as measured from the
RevSTEM image of the
g0 phase of 15Co [24,27].
Using these supercells, simulated atomic resolution EDX maps
demonstrate the effect of atomic site preference on the signal. As
shown in Fig. 4(a), when Co exists on both A and B sub-lattices, the
Co signal is smeared across the map with a low SNR. In contrast, the
simulated EDX maps with Co located exclusively on the A sub-
lattice, Fig. 4 (b), show an improved SNR with periodicity corre-
sponding to the A sub-lattice.
To compare EDX experiments and simulations, the lattice-
average maps are replicated and integrated along 〈010〉 to create
the concentration pro ﬁles in Fig. 4 (c) and (d). The distinct pro ﬁle
peaks labeled A and A/B correspond to those integrated locations
that include A sub-lattice atom columns and those that contain
both A and B sub-lattices, respectively. As a result, the Ni signals
peaks at A, while Ti and Al peak at A/B for all alloys.
In all cases, Ni, Al, and Ti signals are in excellent agreement for
both experiment and simulation. For the case of Co distributed on
both sub-lattices, Fig. 4 (c), the signal is spread evenly across
simulation pro ﬁle. This is in good agreement with the 15Co EDX
maps, supporting the conclusion that Co lacks a site preference in
these comparatively low Co concentration alloys. In contrast, when
Co is exclusively substituted for Ni, as presented in Fig. 4 (d), clear
peaks are observed that coincide with the A sub-lattice. This is in
excellent agreement with the EDX results from experiment for
30Co and 55Co, con ﬁrming that the Co preferentially occupies A
sub-lattice positions in these alloys.
These results from simulations and experiment indicate that Co
undergoes a change in site preference from random across A and B
to predominately A with increasing Co concentration. This is in
agreement with ﬁrsteprinciples calculations by Zhao et al. who
reported that the strong Al site preference is a result of the
coealloying atoms [34]. Based on these prior results, there are a
number of contributing factors including the large difference in
atomic radii for Ni, Co, Al, and Ti as well as their local chemical
bonding environments as a function of composition.

analyzed for a set of quaternary alloys containing Ni, Co, Al and Ti.
Using aberration corrected STEM imaging and spectroscopy, it was
shown that all alloys exhibit a two phase microstructure consisting
of cuboidal g0eL12 precipitates within a gematrix (fcc). Atom probe
tomography showed that the Ni, Al and Ti preferentially partitioned
to the g0 phase, while Co partitioned to the g matrix. At low con-
centrations of Co and Ti, atomic resolution EDX maps revealed that
Co occupies both A and B sub-lattices. Co transitions to an A sub-
lattice site preference at high concentrations of Co and Ti. Simu-
lated EDX maps for two contrasting site preference behavior of Co
atoms provide supporting evidence of our observations. Signi ﬁcant
lattice distortion is present in each of the alloys, peaking in the g
matrix, with compressive strain in the g0/matrix interface. Lattice
misﬁt measured here is generally higher than that obtained from
neutron diffraction due to the small sample region examined and
stacking faults are observed to compensate for the high coherency
strain built up in the 55Co sample. As a next step in the investi-
gation of this alloy system, it is suggested that the thermodynamic
driving force for the composition dependent site preference be
studied in detailed. In particular, it will also be important to
correlate the sample thermal history with site preference and
mechanical properties.
