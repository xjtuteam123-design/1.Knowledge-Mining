ABSTRACT
Additively manufactured CrCoNi medium-entropy alloy (MEA) has demonstrated moderate room-temperature 
tensile strength, which limits its applicability, particularly in harsh environments. In this study, we present an 
ultra-strong and highly ductile CrCoNi MEA additively manufactured via in-situ alloying with nanoscale TiC 
particles. The bulk tensile strength and ductility reach approximately 1.33 GPa and 18%, respectively, out -
performing MEAs and high-entropy alloys (HEAs) reinforced with other types of particles. Microscale strength 
reaches an impressive 2.7 GPa. The mechanisms responsible for the extraordinary mechanical properties are 
investigated through a combination of in-situ electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) and transmission elec -
tron microscopy (TEM) experimental analyses, as well as crystal plasticity finite element (CPFE) modeling. This 
work makes a significant contribution to the field of H/MEAs by introducing nanoparticles to achieve an 
exceptional strength-ductility synergy and establishing the structure-property relationship through multiscale 
experimental and theoretical approaches.
1. Introduction
The extreme operating environments envisaged for industry sectors, 
such as corrosion, high temperature, radiation, high speed, etc., create 
significant challenges for structural metal alloys, which requires initia -
tive design of new alloys [ 1 , 2 ]. HEAs, with multiple principal compo -
nents, inherently possess unique microstructures and many impressive 
properties [ 3 – 5 ], such as high strength and hardness, excellent corrosion 
resistance, thermal stability, fatigue, fracture, and irradiation resistance. 
The exceptional in-use properties are benefitable for improving perfor -
mance, reducing ownership costs, extending the system life and 
reducing environmental impact [ 6 ]. For instance, the exceptional fa -
tigue resistance could reduce the significant fatigue-failure cost [ 7 ]. 
These exceptional properties and the associated benefits make HEAs fast 
grow as future materials for cutting-edge industrial application of en -
ergy plants, defence, aerospace, transportation, etc. As an important 
derivative of HEAs, CrCoNi MEA presents unprecedented combination 
of strength and ductility [ 8 ], great corrosion [ 9 ] and fatigue resistance 
[ 10 ]. Such excellent properties make CrCoNi MEA possessing high po -
tential for cutting-edge applications. Up to date, CrCoNi MEA is 
preferred to be fabricated via conventional synthesis approach (i.e., 
processes of casting, homogenization, rolling, and recrystallization) 
[ 10 – 19 ]. Despite that excellent mechanical properties can be achieved 
using this approach, its main disadvantage is time consuming [ 8 , 20 ] and 
unable to produce complexed components without additional 
machining assistance [ 21 – 23 ], which seriously hinders the application 
of this new alloy. There is an urgent need to manufacture this new alloy 
with advanced technology to reduce the time and costs associated with 
the traditional approach, in order to boost the translation of these new 
materials into critical industrial sectors.
Laser powder-bed-fusion (LPBF) technology, a key additive 
manufacturing (AM) process, enables the tailoring of layer-by-layer 
structures through adjustments to manufacturing parameters [ 24 – 26 ]. 
LPBF can fabricate metal alloys in a single procedure, significantly 
reducing production time. Consequently, AM is considered a promising 
fabrication method for CrCoNi MEA. The mechanical strength and 
ductility of CrCoNi MEA fabricated via LPBF are comparable to those 
produced using conventional methods [ 27 , 28 ]. However, its 
room-temperature tensile strength remains moderate, averaging 800 
MPa [ 29 – 37 ], which is insufficient for increasingly demanding indus -
trial applications. Thus, improving the mechanical properties of CrCoNi 
MEA through AM is imperative.

One effective strategy to enhance the mechanical properties of metal 
alloys is in-situ alloying with ceramic particles during the LPBF process 
[ 38 – 42 ]. By incorporating certain number of ceramic particles into the 
alloy matrix, microstructures such as grain size and grain boundaries can 
be refined in situ, leading to improved mechanical performance This 
approach, in-situ alloying ceramic particle, has been successfully 
employed to strengthen laser additively manufactured HEAs. According 
to current literature [ 43 – 52 ], various ceramic particles, including TiC, 
TiN, WC, and TiB2, have been utilized to strengthen HEAs. These studies 
demonstrated that adding ceramic particles can enhance mechanical 
strength through contributions from finer grains, secondary particle 
dispersion, and solid solution strengthening.
Our understanding of how ceramic particles influence the micro -
structure and mechanical properties of LPBF-manufactured CrCoNi re -
mains limited. In this study, we systematically investigated the effect of 
TiC on the microstructure, mechanical strength, and deformation 
mechanisms using multiscale experimental and modeling approaches. 
Nano-sized TiC was incorporated into the CrCoNi powder through high- 
speed blending and ball milling. Dog-bone-shaped samples were fabri -
cated using LPBF technology. The as-built multiscale structures were 
characterized through EBSD and TEM. Bulk and microscale strengths 
were evaluated via tensile testing and micropillar compression testing, 
while deformation mechanisms were elucidated using in-situ EBSD and 
TEM analysis. Additionally, we developed a crystal plasticity model to 
accurately predict bulk mechanical responses, microstructural evolu -
tion, and grain-scale variables. This work aims to advance current 
research on H/MEAs by introducing nanoparticles to achieve an 
exceptional strength-ductility synergy and establishing the structure- 
property relationship through multiscale experiments and theoretical 
modeling.
2. Methods for experiments and simulations
2.1. Powder preparation and additive manufacturing
We incorporated nano-sized TiC particles (mean size ~50 nm) with a 
4 wt% fraction into CrCoNi MEA powder (particle size ranging from 15 
to 50 μ m) and blended the mixture for 4 h. The powder was then pre -
pared using the ball milling method. As shown in Fig. 1 (a), the blended 
powder was milled for 120 min using a ball-milling machine operating 
at 300 rpm. After ball milling, TiC particles were well attached on the 
surface of the CrCoNi MEA powder, as confirmed by EDS mapping 
( Fig. 1 (b-g)).

Microstructures in both the as-printed and deformed states were

methods: one employed the PIPS II System with the procedure described 
elsewhere [ 61 ], and the other utilized the FIB method outlined in 
Ref. [ 54 ]. TEM testing was conducted using a JEOL-2000 TEM operated 
at 200 kV.

3. Results
3.1. Optimization of the process parameters
Fig. 2 (a) presents the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) under different 
VED. At low VED (85.7 – 95.2 J/mm
3
), the UTS was small. Such low heat 
input cannot guarantee strong metallurgical bonding between the 
adjacent layers, leading to lack-of-fusion defects. Thereby, the UTS was 
not good. When increasing VED to 104.8 J/mm
3
, the UTS reached the 
highest value of 1.33 GPa, which can be attributed to the full melting of 
adjacent layers under sufficient heat input and thus the interlayer de -
fects were eliminated. However, the UTS decreased when further 
increasing the VED. The excessive heat input can cause the unstable 
keyhole which collapses to form micropores. Moreover, the increase of 
heat input can cause pronounced spatters [ 65 ], which may lead to the 
formation of large defects such as balling. These two types of defects, 
pores and balling, can act as stress concentration sites under the external 
loading, leading to early fracture. Therefore, the UTS was worsened 
when the VED was too high. The elongation under different VED in Fig. 2
(b) shows a similar trend, i.e., first increasing then decreasing with 
increasing VED.

ture CrCoNi alloy with excellent strength-ductility synergy. Thus, we

3.2. Microstructure of the as-built sample
As shown in the IPF in Fig. 3 (a), the grains were non-equiaxed, with 
significantly finer grain sizes near the fusion line (indicated by dashed 
dark lines). The average grain size of the CrCoNi + 4 %TiC alloy was 
measured to be 3.9 μ m ( Fig. 3 (b)). For comparison, the grain size of the 
additively manufactured CrCoNi alloy is displayed in Fig. 3 (c) – (d) [ 80 ], 
which confirmed that the grain size of the CrCoNi + 4 %TiC alloy is 
noticeably smaller than that of the CrCoNi alloy. This grain refinement 
was attributed to the enhanced nucleation and faster cooling rates 
introduced by the addition of nano-sized TiC particles [ 81 ]. The distri -
bution of different grain boundaries (GBs) is presented in Fig. 3 (e). Only 
two types of GBs were identified: low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) 
and high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs). No twin boundaries were 
observed in the EBSD results. The fraction of GBs with varying mis -
orientations is depicted in Fig. 3 (f), showing that LAGBs constituted up 
to 52.4 % of the total GBs. As corroborated by TEM observations and 
previous studies [ 24 ], these LAGBs were associated with dislocations. 
The kernel average misorientation (KAM) map ( Fig. 3 (g)) show that the 
value of KAM is higher in the boundary than that of the interior of each 
hatch. This is due to the relative higher solidification rate in the 
boundary, leading to greater dislocation density. The statistics of KAM is 
shown in Fig. 3 (h), which provides an averaged KAM value of 0.45.
The TEM results are displayed in Fig. 4 . A large amount of cell 
structures was observed, as seen in Fig. 4 (a). A zoomed view ( Fig. 4 (b)) 
showed that the cell structures were consisted of dislocations. A STEM 
figure in Fig. 4 (c) also confirmed that these cells were full of dislocations 
(white lines as indicated by dark arrows). Together with the EBSD re -
sults, it can be concluded that there are high-density dislocations in the 
NiCoCr + 4 %TiC. The nano-sized particles can be seen in the STEM 
figure ( Fig. 4 (c)). To confirm the composition of these particles, we used 
EDS mapping to show the distribution of the element of Ni, Co, Cr, Ti 
and C. As shown in Fig. 4 (d) – (h), Cr, Ti and C were enriched in the cell 
boundaries. To further confirm the exact composition of the nano -
particle, we performed line EDS scanning across one particle. The results 
in Fig. 4 (i) showed that this particle was consisted of Ti and C, confirmed 
that the nanoparticles were TiC instead of chromium carbides. The high- 
resolution (HR) TEM image in Fig. 4 (j) shows a sharp interface between 
the TiC and CrCoNi matrix. According to the calculation in Ref. [ 82 ], the 
planar crystallographic disregistry between TiC and nickel was 20.9 %. 
This value was much smaller than the disregistry berween Ni and other 
ceramic particles such as WC, Zr, ZrC, and SiC.
The enrichment of nanoparticle TiC along the cell boundaries has 
also been reported in AM of TiC/B
4
C-CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy com -
posites [ 83 ], which was attributed to the decomposition of TiC. How -
ever, in our case, we did not observe the decomposition of TiC based on 
our TEM analysis ( Fig. 4 (i)). The particle accumulation structure (PAS) 
mechanism can explain the micro-segregation and distinct elemental 
distribution phenomenon [ 84 ]. In this mechanism, fine particles at a 
dilute concentration flow in a time-dependent (oscillatory) ring vortex. 
Under this oscillatory thermo-capillary flow, the particles, which were 
evenly distributed previously, form loose agglomerations circulating in 
the vortex, thus forming PASs.
4. Discussions

4.2. Deformation mechanisms at micro/nanoscale
To further explore the micro/nanoscale deformation mechanisms, 
we conducted micropillar compression tests. As shown in Fig. 8 (a), the 
top of the pillar fabricated via FIB displayed cell structures. Referring to 
the TEM results in Fig. 4 (a) – (c), these cells were composed of disloca -
tions. Therefore, the original pillars contained high-density dislocations 
and nanoparticles. These unique structural features resulted in smooth 
stress-strain curves for the 3 μ m-diameter pillar, as shown in Fig. 8 (b), 
with no strain bursts typically observed in micropillar compression tests 
of metallic single crystals [ 86 ]. In contrast, the 1 μ m-diameter pillar 
exhibited strain bursts, but only in the early plastic region ( Fig. 8 (b)). 
Furthermore, the 1 μ m-diameter pillar demonstrated higher strength 
than the 3 μ m-diameter pillar, with the peak strength reaching 2.7 
GPa — double the bulk strength (1.33 GPa). These results confirmed the 
size-dependent strength of the TiC-strengthened CrCoNi alloy.
To quantitatively assess the size effect on strength, we extracted 
strength values at strains of 2 %, 8 % and 12 %, and plotted them in 
Fig. 8 (c). A clear size effect, following the "smaller is stronger" trend, was 
observed. The size effect was described by a power law, σ = aD

(b = 0.5 – 1) [ 86 – 88 ] and their welded joints [ 54 ]. However, as the strain 
increased, the size effect diminished, as shown in Fig. 8 (c). 
Post-deformation morphology ( Fig. 8 (d)) revealed slip bands in two 
directions, indicative of dislocation activity during deformation.
To further verify the nanoscale deformation mechanisms, we 
examined the structure of a 3 μ m-diameter pillar using TEM. As shown in 
Fig. 9 (a), the pillar was polycrystalline. Within the pillar ’ s center, 
numerous dislocations entangled and interacted with TiC particles 
(indicated by the red arrow in Fig. 9 (b) and (c)), resulting in a high 
dislocation density. HR-TEM and IFFT image in in Fig. 9 (d) and (e) 
further confirm that dislocations distributed around the TiC. At the pillar 
edges ( Fig. 9 (f)), planar defects (indicated by yellow arrows) were 
observed. The selected area diffraction pattern (SADP) indicates that 
these planar defects were likely to be stacking faults (SFs), as seen Fig. 9
(g). To further verify this argument, we performed HR-TEM analysis. As 
demonstrated in Fig. 9 (h), these planar defects are SFs (indicated by 
yellow arrows). STEM image in Fig. 9 (i) shows the interaction between 
the dislocations and SFs, which impeded dislocation motion and 
contributed to the high dislocation density. The TEM results confirmed 
that nanoscale deformation mechanisms included dislocation in -
teractions with both particles and defects, leading to the observed high 
dislocation density.
Based on these TEM findings, we propose an explanation for the 
reduced size effect in our alloy. The smaller size exponent can be 
attributed to the internal structures containing high-density dislocations 
and nanoparticles ( Fig. 4 ). During the early stages of plastic deforma -
tion, dislocations near the surface can escape the pillar due to image 
stress. However, in our case, the nanoparticle pinning effect and dislo -
cation entanglement made dislocation annihilation much more difficult 
than in other cases, thereby reducing the size effect. As strain increased, 
dislocation annihilation was overtaken by dislocation multiplication, 
further diminishing the size effect.

5. Conclusion
In this study, we successfully utilized an in-situ alloying strategy to 
additively manufacture a strong and ductile nanoparticle-strengthened 
CrCoNi MEA. The as-built samples exhibited exceptional mechanical 
properties and heterogenous microstructures. The deformation mecha -
nisms were elucidated through in-situ EBSD and TEM analyses. Addi -
tionally, a dislocation-based CPFE model was developed to predict the 
mechanical response and microstructural evolution, providing insights 
into the structure-property relationship of this novel metal alloy. The 
key conclusions are summarized as follows: 
(1) Through the optimization of LPBF process parameters, the as- 
built nanoparticle-strengthened CrCoNi sample demonstrated 
an exceptional combination of tensile strength and ductility, 
achieving a tensile strength of 1.33 GPa and a fracture strain of 
18 %. This exceptional combination of strength and ductility 
surpasses that of M/HEAs reinforced with other strengthening 
particles. Compared to the UTS of CrCoNi (880 MPa), the tensile 
strength of the nanoparticle-strengthened CrCoNi increased by 
approximately 51 %. The significant enhancement in tensile 
strength highlights the effectiveness of in-situ alloying with 
nanoparticles in tailoring the mechanical properties of additively 
manufactured metal alloys. At the microscale, the strength of 
nanoparticle-strengthened CrCoNi reached 2.7 GPa, which is 
twice the bulk strength. A size-dependent strength was observed, 
though notably weaker than that of FCC metallic alloys and their 
welded joints.
(2) The nanoparticle-strengthened CrCoNi exhibited heterogeneous 
microstructures, including diverse grain morphologies, fine 
grains with an average size of 3.9 μ m, dislocation cells, and a high 
density of nano-sized TiC particles.
(3) In-situ EBSD analysis revealed a significant increase in LAGBs and 
KAM within grains, confirming dislocation slip as the dominant 
deformation mechanism. TEM analysis identified dislocation in -
teractions, the pinning effect of TiC particles, and deformation- 
induced stacking faults, all of which contributed to the elevated 
dislocation density. Interestingly, no deformation twins were 
observed, a departure from the typical deformation mechanisms 
in CrCoNi alloys.
(4) Based on experimental findings, a dislocation-based CPFE model 
was developed. This model accurately predicted the bulk me -
chanical response, microstructural evolution, and grain-scale 
dislocation density distribution in the nanoparticle- 
strengthened CrCoNi alloy. It serves as a valuable tool for 
investigating the effects of microstructural features (e.g., grain 
size, particle size and volume, and dislocation density) on the 
mechanical properties of AM-produced metal alloys, providing 
guidance for designing strong and ductile metal alloys via AM.
(5) The findings presented in this study not only offer a straightfor -
ward and efficient approach for additively manufacturing CrCoNi 
MEA with high strength and ductility, extending their potential 
applications in harsh environments, but also pave the way for 
similar advancements in a wide range of alloy systems.
