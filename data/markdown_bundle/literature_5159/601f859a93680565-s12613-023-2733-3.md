Abstract: Co–Ni-based superalloys are known for their capability to function at elevated temperatures and superior hot corrosion and
thermal fatigue resistance. Therefore, these alloys show potential as crucial high-temperature structural materials for aeroengine and gas
turbine hot-end components. Our previous work elucidated the influence of Ti and Ta on the high-temperature mechanical properties of
alloys. However, the intricate interaction among elements considerably affects the oxidation resistance of alloys. In this paper,
Co–35Ni–10Al–2W–
5Cr–2Mo–1Nb–xTi–(5−x)Ta alloys (x = 1, 2, 3, 4) with varying Ti and Ta contents were designed and compounded,
and their oxidation resistance was investigated at the temperature range from 800 to 1000°C. After oxidation at three test conditions,
namely, 800°C for 200 h, 900°C for 200 h, and 1000°C for 50 h, the main structure of the oxide layer of the alloy consisted of spinel,
Cr2O3, and Al2O3
 from outside to inside. Oxides consisting of Ta, W, and Mo formed below the Cr2O3 layer. The interaction of Ti and Ta
imparted the highest oxidation resistance to 3Ti2Ta alloy. Conversely, an excessive amount of Ti or Ta resulted in an adverse effect on the
oxidation resistance of the alloys. This study reports the volatilization of W and Mo oxides during the oxidation process of Co–Ni-based
cast superalloys with a high Al content for the first time and explains the formation mechanism of holes in the oxide layer. The results
provide a basis for gaining insights into the effects of the interaction of alloying elements on the oxidation resistance of the alloys
they form.
Keywords: Co–Ni-based superalloys; high-temperature oxidation; Ti and Ta elements; formation mechanism of holes
 
 
1. Introduction
Co–Ni-based superalloys possess a higher solvus temper-
ature of the γ' phase and superior microstructure stability than
Co–Al–W-based alloys [1–2]. In addition, these alloys ex-
hibit excellent hot corrosion resistance, thermal fatigue res-
istance, and desirable creep properties [3–6]. As a result,
scholars recognize the potential of these alloys as crucial
high-temperature structural materials for hot-end compon-
ents of aircraft engines and gas turbines, which are research
hotspots in the field of international superalloys. On this
basis, the design of alloys for engineering applications should
consider the high-temperature oxidation resistance of alloys
as an important indicator. This is because oxidation at high
temperatures exacerbates surface damage to alloys, acceler-
ates crack generation and extension, and reduces the fatigue
life and tensile strength of alloys [7–8].
Al and Cr are widely recognized in Co–Ni-based superal-
loy systems because they promote the formation of continu-
ous α-Al2O3 and Cr2O3 layers, which contribute considerably
to the oxidation resistance of alloys [9–11]. In alloy design,
the contents of Al and Cr are usually maintained above
10wt% to ensure sufficient resistance to oxidation [12–13].
The addition of Co and Ni is advantageous as these elements
promote the formation of protective spinel-type oxides,
which inhibit the oxidation of alloys [14]. However, W and
Mo exert unfavorable effects on the oxidation resistance of
alloys. Such phenomenon is due to their formation of volatile
oxides (e.g., WO3 and MoO3) at high temperatures, which
leads to hole formation on the surface of the oxide layer after
volatilization and substantially reduced oxidation resistance
of alloys [15–17]. Weiser and Virtanen [18] demonstrated
that ternary Co–Al–W alloys with a high W content exhib-
ited the smallest mass gain after oxidation; however, W also
increased the formation tendency of the harmful Co3W phase
at the interface between the oxide and the matrix.
Regardless, further research has shown that some ele-
ments have varying effects on alloys. Ti may have the oppos-
ite effect on Co–Ni-based alloy systems. According to Zhu
et al. [19], the increase in Ti content from 1.4wt% to 2.1wt%
(corresponding to an atomic percentage increase from
1.86at% to 2.72at%) in Co–Ni-based alloys can promote the

that among Co–Ni-based alloys with Ti contents of 0at%,

however, Zhang et al. [16] demonstrated that the granular
TaTiO4 formed by Ta and Ti may increase stress and pro-

were due to the complex interaction between elements, which
changed their mechanism of action as the degree of alloying
increased, posing a challenge to the exploration of interac-
tions between alloying elements.
Four Co–Ni-based superalloys with different Ti and Ta
contents were designed and compounded in this paper. The
oxidation resistance of these alloys at 800, 900, and 1000°C
was compared and studied. We analyzed the mechanism con-
trolling the oxidation process and described it by establishing
an oxidation model of the alloys. The results provide an in-
depth understanding of the effects of Ti and Ta on the oxida-
tion resistance of Co–Ni-based superalloys and a theoretical
basis for their engineering applications. 
2. Experimental
In this study, nine-component alloys (Co–Ni–Al–W–Cr–
Mo–Nb–Ta–Ti) with varying Ti and Ta contents, denoted as
1Ti4Ta, 2Ti3Ta, 3Ti2Ta, and 4Ti1Ta, were designed and
prepared. Table 1 presents the nominal composition of each
alloy. Raw materials with a purity level of 99.99% or higher
were employed. To compensate for the mass loss of Al, we
added an additional 5% of the total weight of elemental Al
during raw material preparation. We repeatedly melted the
alloys eight times under an argon atmosphere to ensure their
homogeneous chemical composition. As a control test, three
button ingots (weight 50 g) were prepared for each alloy,
with 12 button ingots set for the four alloys. We weighed the
alloys after melting and observed a negligible mass loss of
less than 0.15%. The button ingots were remelted using a
suction casting system attached to the vacuum-arc melting
equipment to produce bars with a diameter of ϕ10 mm. Con-
sistent with our previous research [23], the alloys were solu-
tion treated at 1250°C for 12 h, air-cooled, and aged at differ-
ent temperature for 30 min. Using 20°C below the γ' solvus
as the standard, the experimental 1Ti4Ta, 2Ti3Ta, 3Ti2Ta,
and 4Ti1Ta alloys were aged at 1208, 1197, 1185, and
1173°C, respectively.
After heat treatment, the alloy bars were cut into discs
with a diameter of ϕ10 mm and thickness of 3.5 mm and then
polished the surface using 3000-grit sandpaper to ensure flat-
ness. Thorough cleaning of the discs was conducted using al-
cohol in an ultrasonic cleaner, and the sample size was meas-
ured using a micrometer. A static weight gain oxidation test
was performed to evaluate the oxidation resistance of the al-
loys. The cleaned alumina crucibles and aumina lids were
burned at 1100°C until constant weight. After cooling to
room temperature, the samples were transferred to the cru-
cible for oxidation experiments. Isothermal oxidation tests
were conducted in a KSL1200 box resistance furnace at 800,
900, and 1000°C. The weight gain of the alloys was meas-
ured at five time points, namely, 10, 30, 50, 100, and 200 h, at
each temperature. Three parallel samples were tested at each
time point and used in the control experiments. After arriv-
ing at each time point, the samples were retrieved and air
cooled to room temperature. We used an AUX-AX124ZH
precision balance with an accuracy of 0.0001 g for sample
weighing. Each sample in the three control experiments was
weighed thrice, and the alumina crucible was included in the
weighing to eliminate the effect of oxide peel-off. After
sample weighing and data recording, the surfaces of the al-
loys were sprayed with gold, and nickel was plated onto their
surfaces via a chemical method. Observations of the cross-
sections of the oxide layer were then performed.
We utilized a high-power (18 kW) D/max 2550 X-ray dif-
fractometer (XRD) equipped with a Cu Kα1 monochromator
and a rotating sample stage to measure the phase composi-
tion of the oxide layer on the surface of oxidized samples.
The obtained data were analyzed using JADE software in
conjunction with the Inorganic Crystal Structure Database.
Observation of the cross-section of the oxide layer of the al-
loys was achieved through the backscattered electron (BSE)
mode of a Zeiss Gemini SEM 500 field emission scanning
electron microscope (SEM). Element information of the al-
loys was obtained by scanning the surface using an X-Max
extreme energy dispersive X-ray spectrometer (EDS). A fo-
cused ion beam of Ga ions was used to cut the transmission
electron microscopy (TEM) samples from the oxide layer.
The cross-section of the oxide layer was analyzed using a Ti-
tan TEM Themis, an environmental-atmosphere spherical
aberration-correction electron microscope equipped with an
image Cs corrector and a high-angle annular dark field
(HAADF) probe (Ceta24K × 4K CMOS Camera).

4.2. Process of hole formation and oxidation model
As shown in Fig. 10, the position where the holes ap-
peared became a “channel” that facilitated the exchange of
oxygen atoms. The Al2O3 layer was thick and convex to the
matrix, which is not conducive to the overall stability of the
oxide layer. During the oxide layer formation, the alloying
elements increased their thickness via metal ion exchange.
Therefore, the Cr2O3 layer between the CoO and Al2O3 lay-
ers became continuous after the establishment of thermody-
namic equilibrium in the initial stage of oxidation. The holes
in Fig. 9(a) indicate a “loss” of oxide. Possibly, a certain ox-
ide underwent gas-phase volatilization, followed by the
formation of a new thermodynamic equilibrium between the
remaining oxide layers.
To further confirm the “loss” material in the oxide layer,
we observed the adsorption of volatiles from the internal wall
of the oxidized crucible. Fig. 12 shows the morphology and
composition of volatiles in the oxides. The volatile oxides
from the holes were W and Mo oxides. MoO3 has a solvus of

holes in the oxide layer after gaseous volatilization. A new

4.3. Influence of Ti and Ta on oxidation resistance
The cross section of the oxide layer after oxidation of the
alloys at different temperatures was analyzed, and the results
reveal that Ti and Ta mainly affected the formation of CoO
and TaO2, respectively.
The TCNi9 database of Thermo-calc software was used to
calculate the relationship between the activity of Co and the
alloy composition at 1000°C, and the results are shown in
Fig. 14. The increase in Ti content in the alloy increased the
activity of Co, which further induced CoO production
(Figs. 3(d), 5(d), and 7(d)) and improved the oxidized weight
gain of the alloys. The increase in Ta content in the alloy pro-
moted the shape of TaO2 (Figs. 3(a), 5(a), and 7(a)). The es-
timated stress generated by the growth of TaO2 in the Al2O3
layer is ~2.2 GPa [30], which is close to the theoretical com-
pressive strength of the α-Al2O3 layer (~2.6 GPa) [31]. The
superposition of thermal stresses can lead to the rupture of the
Al2O3 layer, which decreases oxidation resistance and in-
creases the oxidized weight gain of the alloys. 
5. Conclusions
This study investigated the oxidation resistance of
Co–35Ni–10Al–2W–5Cr–2Mo–1Nb–xTi–(5−x)Ta alloys
(x = 1, 2, 3, 4) containing varying amounts of Ti and Ta at the
temperature range from 800 to 1000°C. In addition, this work
clarified the influence of the interaction between Ti and Ta on
the oxidation resistance of the alloys. The following conclu-
sions were drawn.
(1) At 800, 900, and 1000°C, the interaction of Ti and Ta
resulted in the best oxidation resistance of 3Ti2Ta alloy, fol-
lowed by those of 2Ti3Ta and 1Ti4Ta alloys. Meanwhile, the
4Ti1Ta alloy presented the poorest oxidation resistance.
(2) After oxidation at 800°C for 200 h, 900°C for 200 h,
and 1000°C for 50 h, the oxide scale structure of the alloys
comprised spinel, Cr2O3, and Al2O3 from outside to inside.
(3) Ti and Ta affected the oxidation resistance of the al-
loys by influencing the formation of CoO and TaO2, respect-
ively.
(4) TaO2 and (W, Mo)O3 formed under the Cr2O3 layer.
Subsequently, (W, Mo)O3 volatilized to form holes, which
easily exchanged oxygen atoms. As a result, the alloy was
further oxidized, which caused the Al2O3 layer below the
holes to protrude into the matrix.
