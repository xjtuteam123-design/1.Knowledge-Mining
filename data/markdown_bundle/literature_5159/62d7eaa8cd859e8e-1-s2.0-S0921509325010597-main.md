ABSTRACT
The promising application potential of refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) in high-temperature extreme 
environments is restricted by the difficulty in their preparation, especially for the components with complex 
shapes. Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF), as an advanced additive manufacturing technique, has been employed 
for fabricating RHEAs; however, their mechanical properties were still limited. In this study, different contents of 
B
4
C nano-ceramic particles were introduced to reinforce WMoTaTi refractory high-entropy alloys via LPBF, and 
the effects of B
4
C on the microstructure and mechanical properties of the alloys were systematically investigated. 
Room-temperature compression tests demonstrated that the yield strength of the alloy was markedly increased 
from 1062 MPa to 1558 MPa with the addition of 1.0 wt% B
4
C, while a high strength of 536 MPa was achieved at 
testing temperature of 1200 
◦
C, indicating exceptional high-temperature properties. Through a comprehensive 
analysis, the multiple strengthening mechanisms were discussed to understand the strengthening effect of B
4
C 
particles. This study not only provides new experimental evidence for the strengthening of RHEAs by nano- 
ceramic particles but also expands the potential of LPBF technology for the fabrication of materials for 
extreme environments.
1. Introduction
With the advancement of aerospace science and technology, more 
stringent requirements have been imposed on the service temperature 
and reusability of high-temperature-resistant structural materials for 
hypersonic aircraft and scramjet engines [ 1 ]. Refractory high-entropy 
alloys (RHEAs) are noted for their high strength at elevated tempera -
tures [ 2 ] (often exceeding that of Ni-based superalloys [ 3 ]). The 
NbMoTaW RHEA initially reported by Senkov et al. [ 4 – 6 ] exhibits 
exceptional high-temperature strength and resistance to 
high-temperature softening, retaining a yield strength of ~400 MPa at 
temperatures up to 1600 
◦
C. However, NbMoTaW RHEA shows poor 
oxidation resistance and limited ductility (typically ≤ 2.1%) due to high 
ductile-brittle transition temperatures and embrittled grain boundaries, 
while the high density and poor processability further restrict their 
practical applicability.
Additive manufacturing (AM), particularly laser powder bed fusion 
(LPBF), utilizes high-energy-density laser beams to sequentially melt 
and consolidate metallic powder layers, enabling the near-net-shape 
fabrication of complex components [ 7 , 8 ]. Its design flexibility and 
capability for constructing intricate architectures overcome the poor 
machinability and intrinsic brittleness of RHEAs. Moreover, the 
ultra-fast cooling rate (10
5
– 10
8 
K/s) inherent to LPBF can refine grains 
and suppress element segregation (compared to that of in casting, for 
example), improving materials properties [ 9 ]. Unfortunately, it is 
difficult for the BCC structured alloy to control different types of defects, 
such as cracks and pores [ 10 – 12 ]. Hence, the laser forming of RHEAs is 
mainly dependent on the formability of the target material.
Titanium (Ti) is commonly incorporated into RHEAs due to its 
relatively low density (4.506 g/cm
3
) and melting point (1668

satisfactory, meeting the prerequisites for printing applications. LPBF 
experiments were conducted using a BLT-S210 system (S210 model, 
Bright Laser Technologies Co., Ltd., China), equipped with a maximum 
laser power of 500 W.
Phases and crystal structure were analyzed by X-ray diffraction 
(XRD) with Cu-target radiation ( λ = 0.15406 nm) on a D8 Advance 
system (Bruker, Germany). Meanwhile, the data obtained were 
normalized. The microstructure and elemental distribution of the ma -
terials were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, 
Gemini SEM 300, Carl Zeiss, Germany) coupled with energy dispersive 
spectroscopy (EDS). To further investigate the distribution of pre -
cipitates and matrix elements, transmission electron microscopy (TEM, 
Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher, USA) was employed, with TEM samples 
prepared using focused ion beam (FIB) milling. The grain size was 
determined using the electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) 
technique.
Room temperature compression tests of RHEAs were conducted by 
an electronic universal material testing machine (Instron-3382, Instorn, 
USA) with a squeeze head speed of 0.3 mm/min. The cylindrical samples 
were 3.6 mm in diameter and 5.4 mm in height with an axis parallel to
