Ru–Al–Ni alloys

Abstract
Ru–Al–Ni ternary alloys, created by adding Ru to NiAl, are promising bond-coat materials. However, limited characterization 
as coatings for Ni-based superalloys necessitates better understanding of their properties to enhance high-temperature perfor-
mance. In this study, we collect data on the basic properties, thermal properties, mechanical properties at high-temperatures, 
and oxidation resistance of Ru–Al–Ni ternary alloys with different compositions. The linear expansion coefficient can be 
controlled through composition, and high thermal conductivity can be achieved by combining the binary β -phase with this 
alloy in a layered configuration. Furthermore, it offers excellent oxidation resistance, with Ni preventing weight loss trend 
in the coating by acting as a diffusion barrier for Ru. This alloy also provides good compatibility with Ni-based superalloy 
substrates. In conclusion, optimized compositions enhance substrate compatibility, control thermal expansion, and prevent 
weight loss trend with Ni-enriched diffusion barriers, making them suitable for aerospace applications.
Introduction
Materials used in gas turbines and jet engines require 
numerous properties, such as thermal and chemical stabil-
ity, oxidation and corrosion resistance, and creep strength. 
Turbine blades are typically coated with a bond-coat layer 
and ceramic topcoat on a superalloy substrate. To prevent 
delamination caused by a mismatch in the coefficient of 
thermal expansion between the substrate and coating, as 
well as the formation of oxides at the interface, Pt and 
other Pt group metals (PGMs) are added to the bond-coat 
materials, typically represented by NiAl. The NiAl layer 
containing Pt improves the adhesion between the top 
ceramic coating and substrate. However, Pt sublimates 
rapidly when exposed to a high-temperature oxidizing 
atmosphere owing to the volatility of Pt oxide [ 1]. Another 
issue is the manufacturing cost owing to the high price of 
PGMs, especially Pt, Rh, and Ir.
Ru is not only relatively inexpensive among PGMs 
but also has a high melting point and lower oxide vapor 
compared with other noble metal oxides, which prevents 
volatilization. RuAl has attracted interest as a bond-coat 
material for Ni-based superalloys because of its strength 
and plasticity at room temperature, as well as its ability 
to maintain these properties even at high temperatures. 
Previous studies have proposed a method to improve high-
temperature oxidation resistance by aluminizing Ru or Ru 
alloys by adhering them to a superalloy [2]. The Ru–Al–Ni 
ternary alloy, incorporating Ru into the well-known bond-
coat material NiAl, an ordered B2 intermetallic com -
pound, is regarded as a potential bond-coat material [3 –5]. 
Moreover, the high-temperature strength of the Ru–Al–M 
system, where M represents Co, Cr, Fe, Ti, Sc, or B, has 
been reported intermittently [6 ]. However, the potential of 
Ru–Al–Ni alloys in the aerospace industry has not been 
fully explored because systematic characterization of these 
alloys has not yet been conducted.
In this study, Ru–Al–Ni alloys with varying compo-
sitions were prepared as ordered B2 intermetallic com -
pounds using the arc melting method. Microstructural 
observations, X-ray diffraction (XRD), melting point and 
specific-gravity analyses, thermal expansion and thermal 
conductivity measurements, homogenization-behavior 
analysis using the CALPHAD method, as well as mate -
rial-strength tests, including room- and high-temperature 
Vickers hardness tests, and high-temperature oxidation/
wear tests, were conducted.

Materials and methods
Sample preparation and thermodynamic 
calculations
Ru (PMR, powder 99.9%), Ni (Kojyundo Kagaku, grain 
99.9%), and Al (Kojyundo Kagaku, wire 99.9%) powders 
were weighed (Table 1). The Ru powder was pelletized using 
a manual hydraulic press (FRITSCH TP-1). An arc melting 
furnace (Dia Vacuum ACM-S14-S2) was used to prepare the 
alloys according to a previously described method [7 ]. The 
alloys were cut into specimens sized appropriately for each 
analysis, and homogenization heat treatment was performed 
using a vacuum-pressurized sintering furnace (Shimadzu 
Mectem VESTA) with an Ar flow rate of 2 L/min and pres-
sure adjusted to 3 kPa. The temperature was raised from 30 
to 1600 °C in 1 h and held for 5 d.
A Ru–Al–Ni ternary phase diagram was constructed 
in Thermo-Calc using the TCS noble metal alloy data -
base (TCNOBL1) [8 ]. The concentration profiles of the 
Ru–Al–Ni ternary system after homogenization heat treat-
ment were simulated using DICTRA. The calculation condi-
tions are listed in Table S1.
Analysis
The alloy cross-sections were observed using field-emission 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi High-Tech 
Fielding: SU-70) at an acceleration voltage of 15 kV for sec-
ondary electron images and energy dispersive X-ray spectros-
copy (EDX, Oxford: X-MAX). Identification of crystalline 
phases by XRD (Rigaku: SmartLab) was performed using 
Cu Kβ at an acceleration voltage of 40 kV and current of 
30 mA in the scan range of 20°–90°. Calorimetric changes 
were measured by differential thermal analysis (Netzsch: DSC 
404 F3) at a temperature increase rate of 10 °C/min from 30 
to 1900 °C in an Ar atmosphere. The average coefficient of 
thermal expansion was measured by thermomechanical analy-
sis (TMA, Netzsch: TMA4200SA) with a load of 20 g. The 
temperature was increased at a rate of 10 °C/min from 30 to 
1100 °C in an Ar atmosphere, and the linear expansion coef-
ficient was calculated at any temperature range. Thermal con-
ductivity was evaluated using a thermal diffusivity measuring 
device (Netzsch: LFA467) by increasing the temperature in 
steps from 30 to 600, 800, and 1000 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min 
in an Ar atmosphere, and the thermal diffusivity and specific 
heat were measured at each temperature. Specific-gravity 
measurements were conducted in ethanol using the Archi-
medes method with an automatic specific-gravity meter (SP-
GR1; MESTEC). The room-temperature Vickers hardness 
was measured at five spots with a load of 500 gf using a Vick-
ers hardness tester (HM-103, Mitutoyo). Vickers hardness at 
high temperatures was measured using a high-temperature 
microscopic hardness tester (Nikon: QM-2) under a vacuum 
of approximately 10–3 Pa. After the temperature was raised 
to 450 °C at 20 °C/min, a three-point measurement was con-
ducted with a loading of 500 gf for 10 s. After measurement at 
450 °C, the sample was heated to 900 °C and measured using 
the same procedure. High-temperature oxidation properties 
were evaluated by measuring the high-temperature weight loss 
in air. The temperature was increased from 30 to 1100 °C 
in an electric furnace under an airflow rate of 100 ml/min. 
After 24, 48, 72, and 120 h, the sample was taken out and the 
weight change was calculated. Then, the weight change per 
unit area was calculated by dividing the weight change by the 
pre-measured surface area of the sample.
Results and discussion
Thermo‑calc calculations for the ternary phase 
diagram
An isothermal cross-section of the Ru–Al–Ni ternary system 
at 1700 °C is shown in Fig. S1, and the compositions evalu-

except for Ru20Al40Ni40. The phase diagram of Ni and Ru 
at% vs temperature, with a fixed Al composition, is shown 
in Fig. S2. It indicates that as the Al concentration decreased 
from 50 to 40 at%, the solidus temperature decreased, facili-
tating the formation of Ru-enriched and NiAl intermetallic 
compound phases, such as Ni3Al (γ′-phase) and Ni5Al3.

Thermal properties and specific‑gravity analysis
The differential thermal analysis results are shown in Fig. 
S5. In sample #2 (Ru25Al50Ni25), it was prepared with 
a high fraction of NiAl phase (1668 °C melting point), 
unsubstituted for Ru, therefore showed the melting par -
tially at around 1600 °C, and the gradual baseline shift. 
Except for Ru40Al50Ni10 (Fig. S5c), the endothermic peak 
was observed, which was approximately consistent with the 
solidus temperature predicted by the phase diagram, and the 
samples were confirmed to have melted after analysis.
Figure S6a shows the linear expansion coefficient results 
at a 30–1000 °C temperature range. As the amount of Ni 
substituted for Ru in NiAl was increased, the linear expan-
sion coefficient decreased linearly with the amount of sub-
stitution. Therefore, in ternary alloys with an Al concentra-
tion of 50 at% and a single B2 phase, the linear expansion 
coefficient could be arbitrarily controlled by substituting 
Ru for NiAl. In Ru20Al40Ni20 (sample #5) and Ru25Al45Ni30 
(sample #6), it decreased rapidly above 800 °C due to the 
precipitation of γ′-phase (Fig. S6c). The change in the crystal 
structure from BCC to FCC caused the volume to shrink.
Figure 1 shows the thermal conductivity results. Results 
for RuAl and NiAl reported by Anderson et al. [9 ] and 
Terada et al. [10] are also presented. For all ternary alloys, 
the thermal conductivity tended to increase with increasing 
temperature. The addition of a tertiary element to the inter -
metallic compound of a binary alloy decreased the thermal 
conductivity [11–13]. Lattice imperfections caused by lattice 
defects and impurities were thought to increase imperfec-
tions in the lattice, thereby inhibiting heat transport by elec-
trons and lattice vibrations, resulting in a decrease in thermal 
conductivity. The reduction in thermal conductivity was 
particularly significant in Ru20Al40Ni20 and Ru25Al45Ni30, 
because of the presence of phases other than the β-phase.
The results of the specific-gravity measurements and esti-
mation using the compound law based on the lattice con-
stants are shown in Table S3.

type solid solutions [14]. Since RuAl also has a B2 structure,

Homogenization behavior
The concentration profiles of each element in as-melted sam-
ple #1 (Ru10Al50Ni40) after heat treatment at 1200 °C were 
estimated using DICTRA (Fig. S7a). After heat treatment, 
the concentration difference of Al almost disappeared, but 
also Ni and Ru did not diffuse at all, and the concentra-
tion difference remained large. On the other hand, after heat 
treatment at 1600 °C more than 4 d, there was nearly no 
difference in the concentration of any of the elements (Fig. 
S7b). It was calculated that the alloy homogenized after 
more than 4 d. Cross-sectional SEM images and EDX analy-
ses of the samples after heat treatment are shown in Fig. S8 
and Table S4. In samples #1–3, forming a single β -phase, 
the macro-segregation disappeared, and the EDX analysis 
results indicated that the alloy had been homogenized.
In sample #4 (Ru45Al45Ni10), the Ru-enriched phase 
was more precipitated owing to the diffusion of Ru atoms 
in the lattice or grain boundaries because of the high Ru/
Ni ratio. Sample #5 (Ru20Al40Ni40) showed a two-phase 
microstructure, and EDX analysis indicated the formation 
of a γ′-phase. Sample #6 (Ru25Al45Ni30) was homogenized, 
in contrast to sample #5. According to the phase diagram, 
sample #6 shows that the γ ′-phase begins to precipitate at 
a lower temperature than sample #5. Therefore, a single

Material‑strength tests
At room temperature, Ru–Al–Ni ternary alloys were harder 
than Ni–Al and Ru–Al because of the effect of solid-solution 
strengthening caused by the substitution of Ru or Ni atoms 
(Fig. 2a). The results of the high-temperature Vickers hard-
ness measurements and those reported by Sabariz et al. [15] 
are shown in Fig. 2b. In both cases, the hardness decreased 
with increasing temperature, similar to the results reported 
by Sabariz et al. In Ru25Al45Ni30 after holding at 900 °C, 
the EDX results after hardness measurement near the grain 
boundary are shown in Fig. S9. In the near-grain boundary 
area with low hardness, EDX analysis indicated the presence 
of γ′-phase (Table S5 point 1 and 2). In addition, the XRD 
patterns showed the γ ′-phase in both samples Ru20Al40Ni40 
and Ru25Al45Ni30 (Fig. S10). In both cases, the Ni–Al inter-
metallic compound phase precipitated, indicating a signifi-
cant decrease in hardness.
High‑temperature oxidation/wear tests
Figure 3 shows the results of the isothermal oxidation tests. 
The weight of sample #4 (Ru45Al45Ni10) and #7 (RuAl) 
tended to decrease with increasing temperature as time 
progressed, indicating that compositions with Ru concen -
trations of 45 at% or higher were susceptible to oxidative 
wear. The weight of samples #1–3, #5, #6, and #8 tended 
to increase over time, indicating that these compositions 
were less prone to oxidative wear. The SEM images and 
EDX analysis results for samples #4–7 after 24 and 120 h of 
isothermal oxidation tests are shown in Figs S11 and S12, 
respectively. In samples #4, #6 (Ru25Al45Ni30), and #7, in 
which the Ru concentration was greater than 25 at%, a Ru-
enriched layer was observed beneath the Al2O3 layer after 
24 h. In only sample #6, a Ni-enriched layer was observed 
below the Ru-enriched layer, which prevented the growth of 
the Ru-enriched layer. The Ru-enriched layer was similar to 
the results reported by Vjunitsky et al. [16]. Among the Ru-
enriched layers, only sample #6 did not change in thickness 
after 120 h, therefore the weight did not show a decreasing 
trend like samples #4 and #7.
