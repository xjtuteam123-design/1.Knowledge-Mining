ABSTRACT 
W-Ni
3
Al alloys have potential application in kinetic energy penetrator, but there are some problems such as high 
sintering temperature, large tungsten grain size, and poor mechanical properties. In this study, fine-grained W- 
Ni
3
Al-B alloys with excellent mechanical properties were successfully fabricated at a low temperature of 1150 
◦
C 
by adding trace boron powder (B) and using spark plasma sintering (SPS). For the addition of B in the W-Ni
3
Al 
alloys, the low melting point B
2
O
3 
phase was formed and its thermite reaction released extra heat, promoting the 
sintering of the alloys. However, with the increasing of B content (0.1 – 0.7 wt%), the more hard brittle phases 
were formed, damaging the strength of sintered alloys; at the same time, the more B
2
O
3 
phase was evaporated 
due to the more released heat, causing occurrence of pores. Among all B-containing W-Ni
3
Al alloys, the 0.1B 
alloy exhibited the optimal mechanical properties with the bending strength of 1375.88 ± 19.20 MPa (higher 
99.32% than that of the 0B alloy) and a high hardness value of 71.6 ± 0.29 HRA. By analyzing 0.1B alloy ’ s 
microstructure, its good mechanical properties were related to the small tungsten grain size, heterostructural 
binder phase, minimal formation of hard brittle phases, dislocation movements being obstructed by the Al
2
O
3 
phase, and the tungsten transgranular cleavage fracture mode. 
1. Introduction 
Tungsten alloys are commonly used in military and civil industrial 
fields due to their high strength, high hardness and density, good radi -
ation shielding properties, and corrosion resistance [ 1 – 4 ], especially 
they are considered to be an ideal armor piercing material as for the 
absence of radiation pollution in the process of destroying a target [ 5 ]. 
However, the penetration ability of tungsten alloy (W-Ni-Fe) is weaker 
than that of depleted uranium (DU) penetrators due to its formation of 
the ‘mushroom head ’ in the penetration process [ 6 ]. The penetrability of 
a kinetic energy penetrator is closely related to the grain sizes of tung -
sten phase, thermal conductivity of binder phase, and mechanical 
properties of alloy [ 7 ]. Consequently, developing tungsten alloys with 
high strength, high hardness, fine tungsten grain size, and low thermal 
conductivity has been a tendency. 
For W-Ni-Mn alloy, it was promising candidate for kinetic energy 
penetrators owing to the low thermal conductivity of Mn and low sol -
ubility of tungsten grains in the γ -(Ni, Mn) binder phase [ 8 ]. Unfortu -
nately, Mn is easily oxidized in the sintering process and formed oxide 
impurities, leading to its common mechanical properties, hindering the 
application of armor piercing materials for this alloy [ 8 ]. For Ni
3
Al 
intermetallic compound, it exhibited the anomalous thermal behavior 
and the similar thermal conductivity value with DU (28 W/Km), which 
was profitably to induce adiabatic shear in Ni
3
Al bonded tungsten alloy 
[ 9 ]. However, the reported W-Ni
3
Al alloy featured high sintering tem -
perature, large tungsten grain size, and poor mechanical properties 
[ 9 , 10 ], which did not meet the requirements for fine tungsten grain size, 
high strength and hardness of armor piercing material. 
B element was simultaneously beneficial for enhancing the strength, 
toughness, hardness of Ni
3
Al [ 11 ] and tungsten alloys. For example, 
Jujur et al. [ 12 ] fabricated W fiber/B-doped Ni-rich Ni
3
Al composites 
with improved mechanical properties, originating from good bonding 
between W fiber and Ni
3
Al matrix. Debata et al. [ 13 ] found that the 
hardness of tungsten alloys containing NiB and FeB was similar to or 
even higher than the W-7Ni-3Fe alloy in some cases. Furthermore, 
lowering sintering temperature was an effective way to obtain fine 
tungsten grain size, among which the generation of a phase with low 
melting point at a lower temperature was the foremost. B was easily 
oxidized to B
2
O
3 
with low melting point [ 13 ], and it would react with 
the Al (thermite reaction) [ 14 ], facilitating the sintering of alloys.

3.3. Element distribution and fine structure of the 0.1B alloy 
Fig. 4 shows the TEM and EDS mapping images of a typical 0.1B 
alloy. Unlike some polygonal tungsten powders after mixing in Fig. 1 d, 
the tungsten grains in the 0.1B alloy all had spherical or nearly spherical 
morphology, featuring liquid phase sintering [ 8 , 35 , 36 ]. Further, in the 
bright field (BF) image, the distribution of tungsten grains in 0.1B alloy 
had the following characteristics. First, the adjacent tungsten grains 
were closely contact with each other, such as No.1 and No.2, No.2 and 
No.3. Seriously, some tungsten grains, such as No.6 and No.7, even 
tended to merge into a larger tungsten grain. The coalescence between 
tungsten grain was related to the minor liquid phase in 0.1B alloy and its 
uneven distribution. Some adjacent tungsten grains were not separated 
by the viscous flow of a small amount of liquid phase but were closer to 
each other and even coalesced into larger tungsten grains under the 
pressure applied by the SPS system. Second, a smaller tungsten grain 
was stuck between two larger tungsten grains, just like No.a. Thirdly, 
two tungsten grains (No.3 and No.2, No.1 and No.8, No.1 and No.9) 
were connected by a ‘bridge ’ constructed by a binder phase (No.b, c, and 
d), which maybe the rich-W channel in the 0.1B alloy mentioned in
