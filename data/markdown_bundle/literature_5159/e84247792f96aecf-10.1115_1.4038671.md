act: The titanium alloy (grade 5) is a two-phase material, finds significant applications 
in aerospace, medical, marine fields, owing to its superior characteristics like high strength-
to-weight ratio, excellent corrosion resistance and good formability. Hence, the dynamic 
characteristics of the Ti-6Al-4V alloy are an important area to study. A compressive Split 
Hopkinson Pressure Bar (SHPB) was used to evaluate the dynamic properties of Ti-6Al-4V 
all
oy under various strain rates between 997 to 1898 s −1, and at temperatures between -10 °C
and 320°C. It was evident that the material strength is sensitive to both strain rate and 
temperature; however the latter is more predominant than the former. The microstructure of 
the deformed samples was examined using electron back-scatter ed diffraction (EBSD). The 
microscopic observations show that the dynamic impact characteristics of the alloy are higher 
at higher strain-rates than at quasi-static strain rates. The SHPB tests show that the force on 
the transmitter bar is lower than the force on the incident bar. This indicates that the dynamic 
equilibrium cannot be achieved during high rate of damage evolution. Various constants in 
Johnson-Cook model were evaluated to validate the results. An uncertainty analysis for the 
experimental results has also been presented. 
Keywords: Titanium, Ti-6Al-4V, strain rate, temperature, microstructural analysis, JC model 
prediction, uncertainty analysis.

at high strain rate and high temperature was studied by few researchers but the correlation

Also, to check the repeatability of the set-up, uncertainty analysis is important. Further, there 
is a good scope to study the effect of sub-zero temperatures on the properties on Ti-6Al -4V at

The objective of this paper therefore is to evaluate properties of Ti-6Al- 4V subjected 
to high strain rates under subzero, room and high temperatures. The microstructural changes 
taking place in the specimen with high strain rate have been investigated to support the rise in 
the yield strength of Ti-6Al-4V alloy with high strain-rate. The uncertainty analysis of the 
obtained result has also been carried out. 
2. Experimental technique 
A standard SHPB set up used by Lindholm et al. [4 ] was used for this study (Fig. 1) . 
The experimental set up consists of three bars, an incident, a transmitted and a striker with a 
propelling mechanism. Three bars were center-less ground from a forged bar of Maraging 
steel 300; see Table 3 for its mechanical properties. All the bars were of Φ12 mm and their 
lengths were: incident and transmitted bars 1400 mm, and striker bar 350 mm. 
The strain gauges (2 nos. each) were placed at the center of each bar, which makes a 
half Wheatstone bridge circuit. Such arrangement is helpful in avoiding superimposition of 
propagating waves. The strain gauge signal was given to the amplifier and then was 
transferred to the specimen, which was flanked by the incident and the transmitted bars on the 
either side. Grease was used as a lubricant at the end faces of the specimen. Upon the striker 
bar striking the incident bar, a compressive stress pulse is generated in the incident bar. The 
stress pulse passes through the bar and gets partially reflected back as a tensile wave from the 
interface of the incident b ar and the specimen. The remaining pulse gets transmitted through 
the specimen. A strain gauge mounted on the incident bar helps measure the incident and 
reflected pulses. The transmitted pulse, on the other hand, is measured by the corresponding 
strain gauge placed on it. It is now possible to evaluate a variety of mechanical properties or 
characteristics by knowing the signals from the strain gauges and 1D wave propagation

4. Calibration of SHPB set up 
The properties of the material were evaluated using the SHPB set up may have errors due to 
many reasons like friction between bar and bearing, misalignment of the bars, bend in the 
bars, bar ends not being flat. Hence to ensure accuracy in the SHPB result, calibration was 
carried out. The calibration of the set up described in detail elsewhere in Naik et al. [23] 
confirms that the state of stress within the incident as well as the transmitter bars are 
identical. It implies that the SHPB has been well aligned and is free from friction so that it 
can be used for further testing.

5. Experimental study 
 This work on high strain-rate behavior of Ti-6Al-4V alloy was carried out at the strain 
rates varying between 997-1813 s-1. For this test SHPB set up was used. It may be noted that 
the projectile impact velocity controls strain rate intensity in the work specimens. The 
temperature effect on the high strain rate behavior of Ti-6Al -4V alloy was studied for the 
temperature range of -10 to 320°C at 1450 s -1. During high temperature testing, it is realized 
that there is a drop in temperature as specimen is brought to SHPB set up from the specimen 
heating setup. Also, there is another a drop in temperature as specimen comes in contact with 
the pressure bar. Based on experience, appropriate correction is introduced during heating of 
specimens. The results and their detailed discussion are presented in the next section. 
The brass and aluminum pulse shapers of Φ12 mm and 2 mm thick were used. The 
pulse shaper helps increase the rise time or smoothen or modify the pulse shape. 
6. Microstructural study 
The tested specimens after completing all the tests were subjected to metallographic 
observations. The specimens were cut along the ir axes using a diamond cutter to perform 
electron microscopy. The specimens were then subjected to polishing using grit papers of 
400, 800 and 1200 grit size followed by diamond cloth polishing. Finally, the specimens were 
subjected to electro-polishing with 85% methanol and 15% perfluoric acid solution. The 
operating conditions during electro-polishing were set at: 12V, -12 to -15°C for 18 sy. The 
electro-polished specimens were observed under an Electron Back Scattered Diffraction 
Microscope.

7.2 Stress-strain behavior of Ti-6Al-4V alloy at high and low temperature 
Typical stress-strain plots for Ti-6Al-4V specimens tested under temperatures (-10°C, 
100°C, 200°C, 320°C) and at a constant strain rate of 1450 s -1 are depicted in Fig. 9 . In these 
figures, the plots obtained at room temperature are included to compare the room and high 
temperature properties. It is observed that with an increase in the temperature, the flow stress 
of material decreases. It indicates that thermal softening occurs in high temperature regions of 
the specimen, and that the dislocations in the specimen tend to annihilate more rapidly than 
they generate. 
The yield strength, Young’s modulus and the maximum strain in a specimen tested at 
various temperatures for a constant strain rate of 1450 s -1 are listed in Table 6 . With an 
increase in the temperature, the upper as well as lower bounds on Young’s modulus decrease, 
while the maximum strain in the specimen increases. 
The yield strength of material decreases by 0.72 MPa for each degree rise in 
temperature (Slope of line in Fig. 10 ). This shows that the temperature affects the flow stress 
is more significantly than the strain rate in the case of Ti-6Al-4V. 
7.3 Johnson-Cook model 
As the flow stress of the Ti-6Al -4V strongly depends on the strain, strain-rate and 
temperature, to design or to simulate a mechanical part or a process, a mathematical model of 
such behavior is required. Currently, there are many material models like power law, 
Zeherilli Armstrong equation, Johnson-Cook ( JC) model. Here, the JC model, which 
incorporates both, the effect of strain rate and temperature on the flow strength has been 
chosen to describe the behavior of Ti-6Al-4V material.

During a higher rate of loading, there is insufficient time for the dislocations 
movement, hence more energy is needed to form and move the dislocations so that plastic 
deformation takes place. This movement is resisted by the grain boundaries of neighboring 
grains. Empirically, the dislocation density varies with inverse proportion to the grain 
diameter (d) square root. Therefore, as the strain rate increas es, the density of dislocations 
increases, the grain size reduces, the number of grain boundary increases, and the resistance 
to the dislocation movement increases as consequence the material yield strength increases 
[27].

7.5 Uncertainty analysis 
The effect of flow stress on strain rate was explained earlier but for ascertaining the 
accuracy of the results of the SHPB test, uncertainty analysis has been carried out [28, 29]. In 
this uncertainty analysis, four specimens each were tested at strain rate and temperature. See 
Fig. 8 and 10 for results. A variation in the yield stress and the strain rate at a particular 
impact velocity is observed. This variation appears due to human error in valve opening of 
propelling mechanism, variation in the specimen dimensions, random orientation of grains 
and friction. 
To define uncertainty in the experiments, mean and standard deviation were 
calculated for each case and presented in Table 8-9 . The combined standard uncertainty for 
the room temperature testing of Ti-6Al -4V alloy is 7.67 MPa and the expanded uncertainty 
with confidence level of 95% is 15.26 MPa. On the other hand, in the high strain rate yield 
strength of 1309 MPa, the combined standard uncertainty is 0.6% and expanded uncertainty 
is 1.16%. The combined standard uncertainty for the high temperature testing of Ti-6Al -4V 
alloy is 10.31 MPa and the expanded uncertainty with confidence level of 95% is 20.63 MPa. 
At the same time, in the high strain rate yield strength of 1246 MPa, the combined standard 
uncertainty is 0.8% and the expanded uncertainty is 1.66%.

8. Conclusions 
This work reports characterization of Ti-6Al-4V alloy under high strain rates and 
varying temperatures using SHPB set up. Resistive GC plate and liquid nitrogen were used to 
heat and cool the sample, respectively. The changes in the flow stress of the Ti alloy under 
varying strain rates and temperatures were validated by observing microstructural changes in 
the sample. Conclusions from the investigations are summarized as follows: 
1. The flow stress of Ti-6Al-4V is sensitive to strain rate and temperature. An increase 
in strain rate causes the flow stress of Ti alloy to increase due to high rate of 
dislocation formation and more resistance to their propagation. 
2. During the high temperature testing, dislocation annihilation takes place, hence the 
resistance to its propagation reduces, which effectively reduces the flow stress. 
Whereas, at low temperature testing, the resistance to the dislocation propagation 
increases, hence the flow stress of the material increases. 
3. The strain-rate-sensitivity of Ti-6Al-4V alloy is 0.117, while the temperature 
sensitivity is 0.55, which shows that the Ti-6Al-4V alloy shows higher sensitivity to 
the changes in the temperature than the changes in the strain rate. 
4. The upper bound of Young’s modulus increases with the strain rate, and decreases 
with an increase in temperature, while the variation in the lower bound of Young’s 
modulus is uncertain.
