1. INTRODUCTION 
Directional coarsening or “rafting” in two-phased 
Ni-based superalloys under stress at high temperature 
(typically 1050-C) is a well-known phenomenon and 
has been reported in the literature since 1967 [l]. This 
morphological evolution has been extensively studied 
experimentally [2-81 and several models have been 
put forward to explain, at least qualitatively, the 
rafting phenomenon (for a recent review, see Ref. [9]). 
However, the kinetics and driving force of the process 
are still a matter of controversy. Rafting is also a 
problem of practical importance since in single- 
crystal superalloys with a high volume fraction of 
y’ precipitates used for turbine blade applications, 
under the typical conditions of use (120 MPa and 
T = 1373 K) it occurs after only 10 h [lo] (whereas 
turbine blades are designed to work in these 
conditions). 
The existing work on the topic has identified several 
pertinent parameters influencing both the rafting 
morphology and its kinetics: the sign and intensity 
of the applied stress; the misfit between precipitates 
and matrix [defined as 6 = 2(ay’ - al;)/(ar’ + a)~) 
where uy and LQ~’ are, respectively, the lattice 
parameters of the matrix and the precipitates], and 
the difference between elastic constants of the two 
phases [1 11. For a given misfit, the coarsening 
morphology differs when the alloy is loaded in 
tension or in compression. This difference is readily 
observed on a bending sample for which the external 
fiber is under a tensile stress, and the internal fiber 
under a compressive stress [12]. Figure 1 illustrates 
this effect for a superalloy with negative misfit: in the 
tensile region the rafts are perpendicular to the fiber 
whereas in the compressive region, the coarsening is 
parallel to the fibers. The convention currently 
adopted is to label the rafting type N when it is 
normal to the stress axis, and type P when it is 
parallel. 
Most of the previous theoretical studies were mainly 
concerned with energetic considerations, relying on 
the calculation of elastic incompatibilities due either 
to the misfit or to the difference in elastic constants 
of the two phases. In these theories, the driving force 
for rafting originated from the coupling between these 
incompatibility stresses and the applied stress [5, 111. 
Moreover, most of these models consider a single y’ 
precipitate in an infinite matrix. The more realistic 
case of a regular array of 7’ precipitates has been 
considered, with the same set of assumptions (elastic 
interaction energy as the driving force) using finite 
element method (FEM) [13]. 
However, several studies have noticed that plasticity 
occurred and should not be neglected over the range 
of temperature and applied stresses involved during 
the rafting process. As early as 1977, Carry [14] 
showed that the orientation of rafts is systematically

The present contribution aims at developing a 
model for directional coarsening which takes into 
account plasticity and misfit stresses, and which is 
able to describe both the morphologies and kinetics 
of the process. This model is a three-dimensional 
computer simulation. at the mesoscopic level (the 
elementary unit for the model is the matrix channel 
between two precipitates, approximately 0.1 pm width, 
with a spacing of 0.5 pm). In the simulation, the 
diffusion fluxes and plastic flow are simultaneously 
computed, leading to a continuous evolution of the 
matrix channels structure. The input of the model is 
an initial distribution of channel widths, the output 
is a distribution of channel widths in a coarsened 
structure. The results of the model will be compared 
first with experiments on the superalloy AM 1 (which 
has a negative misfit). More general results will be 
presented in the form of morphology maps describing 
the expected rafting geometry for given misfit and 
applied stresses. 
In Section 2, we describe the principle of our model, 
and the computation of the local driving forces and 
diffusion fluxes. In Section 3, we present the results 
of the simulation when the program is run with the 
parameters corresponding to the alloy AMl. In 
Section 4 the morphology maps are presented. 
2. MICROSTRUCTURAL MECHANISMS 
OF RAFTING 
The microstructure of a two-phased superalloy 
(with a 7’ volume fraction of 70%) can be described 
by considering the size and aspect ratio distribution 
of precipitates. It can be equivalently described by the 
characteristic distribution of the matrix channels. 
Since directional coarsening basically amounts to a 
simultaneous disappearance of some channels and 
widening of others, we have chosen to model the 
evolution of channel width in order to describe 
rafting. 
More precisely, in order to calculate the width 
evolution of each channel, the flux of matter between 
the different channels, due either to plastic flow, or to 
diffusional flow resulting from a chemical potential 
gradient, is calculated. The different fluxes added 
together lead to a redistribution of matter between 
the different channels (with a constant total volume 
fraction of precipitates) and to a directional 
coarsening. In order to perform this calculation, we 
need to evaluate the local stresses inside each channel. 
This local stress is the superposition of a macroscopic 
applied stress which is distributed between the 
channels and the precipitates according to a composite 
effect, and of misfit stresses which may be relaxed on 
some interfaces due to the presence of dislocations. 
This local stress in each channel leads to plastic flow 
and thus to mass transport from one channel to its 
neighbors. The stresses in a given channel also change 
the local chemical potential of the elements there and 
therefore a difference in the local stresses in 
neighboring channels results in a cross-diffusion flux,

For the sake of clarity we will consider separately 
those two contributions, and qualitatively evaluate 
their effect. In each case, loading in tension and 
compression will always be along a (100) axis. 
First, let us consider for the case of zero misfit, the 
influence of plastic and diffusional flow. When loaded 
in tension, plastic flow will reduce the width of the 
channels parallel to the tensile axis and the net result 
would be coarsening of type N. When loaded in 
compression, the effect would be exactly opposite 
(type P coarsening). 
The contribution of diffusional flow brings material 
from the most loaded regions to the less loaded ones: 
for both the case of tensile and compressive loading, 
the channels perpendicular to the stress axis will then 
disappear (type P coarsening). 
When the misfit stresses are superimposed on this 
picture, the same results will hold but the misfit stress 
has to be added to the local stress. If the stresses 
are large enough, the contribution of plastic flow will 
remain qualitatively the same. When relaxation of 
misfit stresses by plasticity is allowed, the most loaded 
channels will first undergo plasticity: dislocations lie 
first on the interfaces of the channels perpendicular 
to the stress axis. However, when the sample is loaded 
in tension, the dislocations relax the misfit in these 
channels, whereas when loaded in compression, the 
misfit in these channels is increased. The gradients 
in chemical potential generated by this anisotropic 
relaxation promotes, therefore, type N coarsening in 
tension and type P coarsening in compression. 
The model we have developed allows us to take into 
account simultaneously and locally all those different 
contributions. In order to do so, we need to compute 
the local stresses in each channel, to then deduce the 
plastic flow resulting from these local stresses, and 
the chemical potential gradients. We finally calculate 
the diffusional flux resulting from these gradients, 
and the consequences on the channel width 
evolution.

4. MORPHOLOGY MAPS 
The model we have developed has been shown 
to describe correctly the behavior of AMl, both for 
the morphological evolution and its kinetics. Other 
superalloys with similar initial microstructure, but 
different values for misfit 6 have been developed. A 
logical next step was to employ our model to predict 
the coarsening behavior under applied stresses for 
these alloys. For the sake of simplicity, we will now 
only consider the effect of changing 6 on the 
morphology maps. We kept all the input parameters 
constant. Consequently, the results of the simulation 
are only qualitative indications of the morphological 
evolution. The results have been displayed as 
“morphology maps” in the (oa, 6) plane which predict 
the final morphology resulting from aging under a 
stress ga of an alloy similar to the AMI but with a 
misfit 6. 
Qualitatively the effect of changing 6 from a 
negative to a positive value can be described as 
follows: the interfaces which will be relaxed in tensile 
loading by dislocations are those perpendicular to the 
load axis for 6 < 0. When 6 > 0, the interfaces 
parallel to the load axes will be relaxed. For any given 
loading, the diffusion contribution to directional 
coarsening would lead to different morphologies (N or 
P) whether the misfit is positive or negative. However, 
even if the sign of the misfit is changed, the plasticity 
contribution will not alter the direction of the 
morphology. The differences between the diffusional 
and the plastic contributions leads to a rich behavior 
when both of them contribute to directional 
coarsening. 
For 6 < 0, both diffusional flow and plasticity drive 
the system toward the same coarsened morphologies. 
Alternatively, when S > 0, the two contributions 
work in opposite directions. At low applied stresses, 
the behavior will be dominated by the diffusion flow 
contribution, whereas at large stresses, plastic flow 
behavior will dominate the process. This explains the 
general feature of Fig. 9 and the difference between 
positive and negative misfit behavior. 
As shown in Fig. 9, the general features predicted 
are in agreement with the observed morphologies. 
As observed experimentally, when 6 is negative, we 
predict mainly a type N coarsening when loaded in 
tension and type P coarsening when loaded in 
compression [22]. For 6 > 0 and stresses smaller than 
150 MPa, we have predicted the observed micro-

5. CONCLUSION 
The kinetics and morphology of the directional 
coarsening process are ruled by a competition between 
purely plastic mass transport and diffusional mass 
transport induced by gradients in stored elastic 
energies. We have presented a tri-dimensional 
computer simulation able to integrate those different 
contributions to predict the morphological evolution 
and its kinetics. The morphology map we have pro- 
duced is in agreement with experimentally observed 
behavior for different applied stresses and misfit. 
We predict an inversion in the behavior for the case 
6 > 0, when stresses are large enough. 
A natural extension of this work is to consider 
the mechanical properties of the coarsened structure: 
since our model provides a 3-D structure of the two- 
phases superalloy, it is straightforward (Fig. 10) to 
generate (111) sections of this structure which shows 
the distribution of channels in which dislocations 
have to propagate: the problem of plasticity threshold 
becomes equivalent then to a problem of invasion 
percolation.
