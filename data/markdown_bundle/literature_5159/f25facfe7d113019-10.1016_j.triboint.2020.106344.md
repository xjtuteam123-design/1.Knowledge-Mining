Abstract: Mo-Si-B alloy is a promising candidate for high tem perature environment, 
while its high temperature dry sliding wear behavio r is not yet well investigated. In 
the present work, the Mo-12Si-8.5B alloy and its co mposite reinforced with 10 wt.% 
MoAlB ceramic were prepared by spark plasma sinteri ng. Effects of MoAlB ceramic 
addition on the microstructure, mechanical properti es and dry wear properties at RT–
1000 °C were investigated. The result indicates that the composite shows an enhanced 
strength and hardness, and a remarkably improved we ar resistance. The excellent 
wear resistance is mainly attributed to the improve d hardness and thermal softening 
resistance, as well as the formation of Al
2(MoO 4)3 layer at 800–1000 °C that results 
from the dispersive distribution of MoAlB particles. 
Keywords: Mo-Si-B alloy; MoAlB ceramic; High temperature; Wear resistance 
 
1. Introduction 
Mo-Si-B alloys have attracted much attention as pot ential ultrahigh-temperature 
structural materials because of their high melting point and high strength at elevated 
temperatures, which is also regarded as a promising substitute for Ni-based 
superalloys to improve the energy efficiency of gas turbine engines that operating at 
higher temperatures [1-3]. In particular, the Mo-12 Si-8.5B (at.%) alloys that

## Page 4

2 
 
composed of α-Mo matrix and reinforcing phase of Mo 3Si and Mo 5SiB 2 intermetallic 
have been widely investigated for their good balanc e of strength, fracture toughness 
and oxidation resistance [4-6]. However, the critic al problem of poor oxidation 
resistance at intermediate temperature remains rest ricts their further development 
[7-9]. This is due to the rapid volatilization of M oO 3 at 700–800 °C, while the 
protective layer of borosilicate glass cannot forms , and thereby the Mo-12Si-8.5B 
alloys show poor oxidation resistance at this temperature range. At the same time, it is 
reported that the friction coefficient of MoO 3 reaches 0.27–0.2 at 870–1073K, and 
that for B 2O3 is 0.27–0.2 at 823–1000K [10]. Therefore, the oxid ation of the 
Mo-12Si-8.5B alloys is beneficial for the self-lubr icity when sliding at elevated 
temperature in air, while the key issues is how to utilize this merits and meantime 
avoid the vaporization of MoO 3. 
During the past decades, the mechanical properties and oxidation resistance of 
the Mo-Si-B alloys have been extensively studied. M ore recently, some studies have 
also been conducted on their dry sliding tribological behavior at elevated temperatures. 
Li et al. [11] studied the tribological properties of the Mo-Si-B alloys with different Si 
and B contents at 25–900 °C. They found that the al loys showed a lower friction 
coefficient at 700–900 °C because of the formation of MoO 3 films, and the wear rate 
was significantly reduced at this temperature range . Murakami et al. [12] studied the 
tribological properties of Mo, MoB, Mo 5SiB 2, Mo 5Si 3 and MoSi 2-based alloy coupled 
with Si 3N4 ceramic from 25 to 1000 °C. It indicated that the f riction coefficient is 
approximately 0.1 at 800 °C due to the combined eff ect of hexagonal BN and MoO 3 
films. Besides, Hu et al. [13] also investigated th e tribological properties of 
MoSi 2-Mo 5Si 3-Mo 5SiB 2 composite from 25 to 1000 °C. The results showed t hat its 
friction coefficient was 0.30–0.34 at 1000 °C when tested at 5–10 N because of the 
formation of tribo-oxides layer. However, these stu dies are not concerned with the 
poor oxidation resistance at intermediate temperature (～800 °C). 
To improve the intermediate temperature oxidation r esistance, numerous 
methods such as grain refinement [14], alloying ele ments like Al [15] and adding rare

earth [16] have been applied. Recently, the ternary transition metal borides like 
MoAlB have been widely reported for the superior me chanical properties and 
excellent oxidation resistance, which render it pot entially for the applications such as 
wear-resistant coatings and high-temperature struct ural materials [17-18]. Compared 
with the pure Mo alloy, it has lower density, highe r hardness and better oxidation 
resistance from RT to 1350 °C. The excellent oxidat ion resistance can be ascribed to 
the formation of a protective and adherent alumina layer. Hence, there is an impetus to 
improve the mechanical properties, oxidation behavi or and triboligical properties of 
the Mo-12Si-8.5B alloy by adding MoAlB ceramic. 
Therefore, the aim of this work is to study the effects of MoAlB ceramic addition 
on the mechanical and dry wear properties of Mo-12S i-8.5B alloy at a temperature 
range of RT–1000 °C. 
2. Experimental procedures 
The Mo, Si and B elemental powders with the atomic percent of Mo-12Si-8.5B 
were weighted and mechanical alloyed by a Fritsch P ulverisette5 type high-energy 
planetary ball mill. In addition, 1 wt% CeO 
2 was added and ball milled with the 
powder mixture. The mechanical alloying was conduct ed with a weight ratio of 
ball-to-powder 3:1 for 16 h under an argon protecti ng atmosphere. The MoAlB 
powder was fabricated by three steps: mechanical al loying of the Mo, Al and B 
powder, then hot pressed sintering of the mechanica l alloyed powder mixture and 
finally by ball milling to get the ground powder, t he details of fabrication process was 
presented in our previous study [19]. Then the Mo-1 2Si-8.5B/MoAlB composite 
powder with 10 wt% MoAlB ceramic was weighted and b all milled. Finally, both the 
Mo-12Si-8.5B alloy and the Mo-12Si-8.5B/MoAlB compo site (referred to as MoSiB 
and MoSiB/MoAlB) were sintered by a LABOS-3010KF ty pe spark plasma sintering 
furnace at 1450–1500 °C for 5 min. Then the phases and the microstructures of the 
prepared samples were analyzed by a Panalytical X’p ert PRO diffractometer (XRD) 
and a JSM-6700F scanning electron microscope (SEM). The density of the prepared 
samples was measured by Archimedes principle. The f lexural strength and fracture

phases in Fig. 3(b) are MoAlB particles with a uniform dispersion in the matrix.

3.2 Mechanical properties 
To evaluate the mechanical properties of the MoSiB alloy and MoSiB/MoAlB 
composite, their density, microhardness, flexural strength and fracture toughness were 
tested and the results are presented in Table 1. Th e density of the alloy is 8.49 g/cm 3, 
which reaches about 90% of its theoretical density. Since the MoAlB ceramic has a 
much lower density (6.06 g/cm 3) than that of the alloy, the sintered composite shows a 
lower density of 8.35 g/cm 3, suggesting that the addition of MoAlB contributes to the 
decrement in density. Compared with the alloy, the composite exhibits a higher 
Vickers hardness and flexural strength, which indic ates that the hardness and strength 
of the MoSiB alloy is enhanced by adding MoAlB ceramic. However, the existence of 
MoAlB causes a reduction in the fracture toughness.

The worn surface morphologies of the MoSiB alloy and the corresponding Al 2O3 
counterparts after sliding tests at RT–1000 °C are shown in Fig. 10 and Fig. 11. When 
tested at RT, the worn surface of the alloy is rela tive smooth with the presence of 
some fine debris (Fig. 10a), and only a small part of α-Mo matrix is transferred to the 
Al 2O3 counterpart (Fig. 11a), thus the dominant wear mechanism is adhesive wear. At 
200 °C, it can be seen from Fig.10b that the wear s car width and depth of the alloy 
increases sharply. This result is corresponding to the wear scar profile of the Al 2O3

## Page 13

11 
 
counterpart, as shown in Fig.11b. The large scale o f the wear scar of the Al 2O3 ball 
leads to the increased wear area and wear rate of t he MoSiB alloy. Further observing 
the SEM images at high magnification, it is clear that some discontinuous grooves and 
a large amount of wear debris are presented on the worn surface of the alloy, 
indicating third-body abrasive wear occurs during t he sliding. Additionally, a large 
part of patches are attached to the Al 2O3 ceramic ball, which is related to adhesive 
wear. When the temperature rises to 400–600 °C, the worn surfaces of the alloy are 
covered with compact tribo-layer and there is no ob vious wear debris can be detected 
(Fig.10c and Fig.10d). As the EDS result given in T able 2, there is a large amount of 
oxygen on the worn surface, indicating tribo-oxidat ion process is occurred and 
tribo-oxide layer is formed, which is beneficial to reducing the real contact between 
the counterparts and accounts for reduced friction coefficient and wear rate [24, 25]. It 
can be also found in Fig.11c and Fig.11d that the w ear scars of the Al 2O3 balls are 
much smoother and the patches adhered on the worn s urface are quite smaller as 
compared that tested at 200 °C, indicating that adh esive wear is inhibited by the 
tribo-oxide layer. Nevertheless, the major wear mechanism remains adhesive wear. At 
800 °C, the worn surface of the alloy is flat and smooth, and some MoO 3 particles can 
be found on the worn surfaces of the alloy and the Al 2O3 counterpart, as shown in 
Fig.10e and Fig.11e. Owing to the formation of MoO 3 and B2O3, which is also proved 
by the XRD result shown in Fig. 12, the alloy posse ss a self-lubricating effect and its 
friction coefficient reaches the lowest value of 0. 19. However, MoO 3 can be easily 
volatilized at this temperature. The SEM micrograph and XRD pattern of the 
collected MoO 3 whiskers are shown in Fig. 13. In addition, it is evident that a bulk of 
material is attached to the Al 2O3 ball after sliding test (Fig.11e), which indicates the 
predominant wear mechanism transforms to serious ad hesive wear at 800  °C. 
Therefore, the aggravated evaporation of MoO 3 and serious adhesive wear results in 
the high wear rate at 800 °C. At 1000 °C, it is clear that the alloy is extruded along the 
sliding direction and the wear scar is characterize d by the presence of deep grooves 
(Fig.10f), indicating that the alloy experiences a severe plastic deformation because of

the thermal softening. Moreover, a large amount of material is adhered to the Al 2O3 
ball (Fig.11f) due to the severe adhesive wear. Accordingly, the main wear mechanism

Fig. 14 and Fig. 15 show the worn surface morpholog ies of the MoSiB/MoAlB 
composite and the corresponding Al 2O3 counterparts after sliding tests from RT to 
1000 °C. It can be found in Fig.14a–c and Fig.15a–c that the scale of the wear tracks 
of the composite and the wear scars of the corresponding Al 2O3 counterparts increases 
gradually with test temperature up to 400 °C, resul ting in an increasing wear rate. At 
RT, the composite undergoes a much slighter wear th an that of the MoSiB alloy 
(Fig.14a), which results from the enhanced wear res istance by introducing MoAlB 
ceramic. By observing the Al 2O3 counterpart ball, it can be found that the worn 
surface is covered with few transferred material (F ig.15a). In this case, the main wear 
mechanism can be considered as adhesive wear. As th e temperature rises to 200 °C, 
the worn surface of the composite is covered with c ompact tribo-layer and some 
adhesive craters filled with fine debris (Fig.14b), which is consistent with the worn 
surface characteristic of the Al 2O3 counterpart shown in Fig.15b. The presence of 
adhesive craters and the increased scar of Al 2O3 ball indicate that the composite

## Page 17

15 
 
experiences more severe adhesive wear when sliding at 200 °C, thereby increasing the 
friction coefficient and wear rate. At 400 °C, as s hown in Fig.14c and Fig.15c, the 
worn surface of the composite is characterized by the presence of the compacted layer 
with a rippled morphology, and the Al 2O3 ball also shows a rippled surface 
morphology. The formation of the ripples is caused by the stick-slip mechanism, 
which correlates to an increase in the friction coefficient and wear rate [28]. When the 
temperature reaches 600 °C, the worn surfaces of th e composite and the Al 2O3 
counterpart become smoother and the compacted tribo -oxide layers are formed 
(Fig.14d and Fig.15d), which can effectively reduce severe wear between the 
counterparts [29]. For this reason, the composite s hows a decreased friction 
coefficient and reaches the lowest wear rate at thi s temperature. Based on the above 
results, it can be confirmed from that the dominant wear mechanism remains adhesive 
wear at 200–600  °C. After dry sliding at 800  °C, as the XRD result shown in Fig. 16, 
the worn surface is mainly composed of Al 2(MoO 4)3 [30, 31], with part of MoO 3, 
B2O3 and SiO 2. The composite also shows a self-lubricating effec t and the friction 
coefficient reaches 0.16. In addition, the wear rat e of the composite is 4.46×10 -5 
mm 3N-1m-1, which is much lower than that of the alloy (1.10× 10 -4 mm 3N-1m-1). This 
can be explained from the following aspects: the di spersive distribution of MoAlB 
particles descends the mechanical performance deter ioration, which results in a better 
wear resistance for the composite; on the other han d, the formation of Al 2(MoO 4)3

a significant effect on the high temperature tribol ogical properties and wear

the composite at RT–800 °C keeps consistent with th e alloy, while the composite can

RT to 600 °C, owing to the increased temperature co ntributes to the tribo-oxidation

comparison to the MoSiB alloy, the MoSiB/MoAlB comp osite shows a much lower 
wear rate at all the test temperatures. The improve ment in the wear resistance is 
mainly attributed to the improved hardness and ther mal softening resistance that 
results from the dispersed MoAlB reinforcements. Additionally, it is worth noting that 
the addition of MoAlB ceramic contributes to the fo rmation of Al 2(MoO 4)3 layer at

evaporation of MoO 3, while it remains the self-lubricating effect. On the other hand, 
the Al2(MoO 4)3 layer can prevent the severe wear between the counterparts, leading to 
a decrease in the friction coefficient and wear rat e. Therefore, the introduction of 
MoAlB is favorable for the enhancement of the wear resistance of the MoSiB alloy, 
which also provides a new way for improving the oxi dation resistance of the MoSiB 
alloy at 800–1000 °C.

4. Conclusions 
(1) The MoSiB alloy is mainly composed of α-Mo, Mo 3Si and Mo 5SiB 2, and the 
MoSiB/MoAlB composite consists of α-Mo, Mo 3Si, Mo 5SiB 2 and MoAlB. And the 
strength and hardness of the composite is enhanced by the dispersed MoAlB 
reinforcements. 
(2) The friction coefficient of the alloy and the c omposite fluctuates with 
increasing temperature, while both the alloy and the composite exhibit self-lubricating 
effect at 800 °C and their friction coefficient are as low as 0.19 and 0.16, respectively. 
This can be ascribed to the formation of MoO 3 and B 2O3 oxides for the alloy and 
Al2(MoO 4)3 phase for the composite. 
(3) The wear rate of the alloy and the composite is dependent on the test 
temperature, which fluctuates at RT–800 °C and incr eases sharply at 1000 °C. Owing 
to the formed compacted layers, the alloy and the c omposite reaches the lowest wear 
rate at 600 °C, which is 8.76×10 -6 mm 3N-1m-1 and 8.63×10 -6 mm 3N-1m-1, respectively. 
(4) The wear resistance of composite is remarkably improved with the addition 
of MoAlB ceramic at all the test temperatures, whic h is primary due to the improved 
hardness and thermal softening resistance, and the formation of Al 2(MoO 4)3 layer at 
800–1000 °C.
