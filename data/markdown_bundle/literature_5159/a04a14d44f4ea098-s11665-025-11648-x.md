ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Study on Microstructure Evolution and Mechanical
Properties of New Lightweight (NbTiV) 100-xBx/Cx
High-Entropy Alloys
Ruiyuan Wang, Ning Zhao, Daohe Zhang, Shiwen Hu, Xiaoqiang Li, and Dexue Liu
Submitted: 5 December 2024 / Revised: 18 April 2025 / Accepted: 24 April 2025
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) are promising candidates as potential high-temperature materials.
However, the high density and poor room-temperature ductility limit their industrial applications. In this
study, a series of lightweight RHEAs with the compositions of (NbTiV) 100-xBx and (NbTiV)100-xCx (x = 0.5,
1, 5, 10 and 15) were designed and prepared by microalloying with boron (B) and carbon (C). The
microstructure evolution and mechanical properties of these alloys were systematically investigated. The
results revealed that with increasing alloying content, the matrix phase in (NbTiV)
1002xBx alloys transi-
tioned from dual body-centered cubic (bcc 1 and bcc 2) solid solutions to a singular bcc 2 phase, whereas the
(NbTiV)1002xCx alloys exhibited a structural evolution from a bcc conﬁguration to a face-centered cubic
(fcc) arrangement. The (NbTiV) 90B10 and (NbTiV) 90C10 alloys exhibited excellent room-temperature
mechanical properties, with compressive strengths of 1494 MPa and 1466 MPa and fracture strains of 25%
and 29%, respectively. At elevated temperatures (800-1000 /C176C), the strengths of (NbTiV) 90B10 and
(NbTiV)90C10 alloys decreased from 943 MPa and 1042 MPa to 326 MPa and 358 MPa, respectively.
Notably, although both alloys achieve a balance between low density and outstanding mechanical prop-
erties, the (NbTiV)90C10 alloy demonstrates superior strength to the (NbTiV) 90B10 alloy at all temperatures.
This study provides a new approach for developing lightweight RHEAs with enhanced performance for
medium-temperature applications.
Keywords low density, mechanical property, microstructure
evolution, refractory high-entropy alloys
1. Introduction
The rapid advancement of aircraft and aerospace technolo-
gies has driven increasingly demand for high-temperature
structural materials. Traditional nickel (Ni)-based, iron (Fe)-
based, and cobalt (Co)-based superalloys, which can only be
used below 1200, 800, and 1100 /C176 C, respectively, struggle to
meet the stringent high-temperature resistance requirements of
aviation and aerospace applications. Consequently, researchers
have been actively developing a new generation of superalloys.
In 2010, Senkov et al. (Ref 1) introduced a novel alloy design
strategy known as RHEAs, composed of refractory elements
such as W, Mo, and Ta. Among the initial RHEAs, MoNbTaW
and VMoNbTaW alloys exhibit excellent high-temperature
mechanical properties and have attracted wide attention (Ref 2).
Since then, RHEAs have been regarded as a promising
candidate for future superalloys (Ref 3-5).
The high-temperature softening of RHEAs intrinsically
arises from two aspects: the activation of the diffusion-
controlled deformation mechanism and the dissolution of
secondary phases (Ref 6). The former typically occurs at the
temperature above 0.6 T
m (Tm is the absolute melting temper-
ature of the alloy), while the latter depends largely on the
stability of secondary phases. To address these issues, common
design strategies include incorporating high-melting elements,
reducing the content of low-melting elements, or introducing
stable secondary phases (Ref 6-9). For instance, replacing Nb in
the NbMoTaW alloy with Re resulted in the ReMoTaW alloy,
which exhibited a remarkable strength of 244 MPa at 1600 /C176 C
(Ref 10, 11). Similarly, annealed CoCrMoNbTi
0.4 alloy with a
small amount of Laves phase demonstrated signiﬁcant poten-
tial, achieving a compressive strength of 680.42 MPa at
1200 /C176 C (Ref 12). Another example is the Nb
30Mo30Hf20Co20
alloy, which displayed superior strain hardening capacity
between 22 and 600 /C176 C due to its unique ‘‘inverted microstruc-
ture’’ (Ref 13). However, these alloys tend to have a high
density, which poses a challenge for energy conservation and
emission reduction, limiting their practical application. There-
fore, balancing density and mechanical properties is crucial for
the development of RHEAs.
Recent studies have highlighted the potential of intermetallic
compounds in enhancing the mechanical properties of RHEAs

For examples, the silicide M 5Si3 phase, which is often observed
in RHEAs systems (Ref 17-21), has been shown to signiﬁcantly

of silicon (Si) to the NbMoTiVSi 0.4 alloy reduced the average

35% increase in hardness (Ref 23). Furthermore, Wei et al. (Ref
14) developed Re 0.5MoNbW(TaC)0.5 alloy, which exhibited
exceptional yield strength of 901 MPa at 1200 /C176 C and
maintained microstructure stability even after prolonged
annealing at 1300 /C176 C for 168 h. It is noteworthy that the
microstructure of RHEAs transitions from hypoeutectic to
eutectic and eventually to hypereutectic as the concentration of
nonmetallic elements increases (Ref 24). Therefore, precise
control of the volume fraction of reinforcing phases is essential
to avoid excessive brittleness (Ref 25).
Recent studies have reported that the lightweight NbTiV
medium-entropy alloy exhibits promising room-temperature
deformation characteristics; however, its mechanical properties
at high-temperature necessitate further enhancement through
compositional and microstructural optimization (Ref 26).
In this study, intermetallic compounds were incorporated
into NbTiV alloy to achieve a synergistic combination of low
density, good ductility, and high strength at both room
temperature and high temperature. Speciﬁcally, a series of
(NbTiV)
100-xCx and (NbTiV)100-xBx RHEAs (where x = 0.5, 1,
5, 10, and 15) were designed and prepared. The microstructure
evolution and corresponding mechanical properties of these
alloys were systematically investigated. Additionally, the
mechanical properties of (NbTiV)
90C10 and (NbTiV) 90B10
RHEAs at different high temperatures were analyzed in detail.
This work aims to provide insight into the design of lightweight
RHEAs with enhanced high-temperature performance, address-
ing the growing demands of advanced aerospace applications.
2. Experimental Materials and Methods
The raw materials were Ni (purity ‡ 99.999%), Ti (pu-
rity ‡ 99.99%), V (purity ‡ 99.99%), C powder (pu-
rity ‡ 99.98%), and B powder (purity ‡ 99.98%). The
(NbTiV)100-xBx and (NbTiV) 100-xCx (x = 0.5, 1, 5, 10, and
15, respectively) alloys (denoted as B 0.5,B 1,B 5,B 10,B 15,C 0.5,
C1,C 5,C 10, and C15, respectively) were arc-melted in an argon
atmosphere and ﬂipped at least six times to ensure chemical
homogeneity. The theoretical density of the designed alloys is
shown in Table 1. The phase identiﬁcation was conducted by x-
ray diffraction (XRD) with Cu-K a radiation. The 2-theta ranges
from 20 to 80 /C176 at a scanning speed of 10 /C176 /min. The scanning
electron microscopic (SEM) equipped with an energy-disper-
sive spectroscopy (EDS) were used to characterize phase
morphology and element distribution. Mechanical properties of
these alloys were evaluated by an Instron 5565 universal testing
machine (INSTRON, USA) and a Gleeble /C0 3500 thermal
simulator. Cylindrical specimens for compression testing were
5.0 mm in diameter and 10.0 mm in height at room temper-
ature, and were 6.0 mm in diameter and 9.0 mm in height at
elevated temperatures. During the test, the compression test was
terminated when the specimen fractured or the height reduction
reached 50% of the original dimension.
3. Results and Discussion
Figure 1 shows XRD patterns of as-cast (NbTiV) 100-xBx and
(NbTiV)100-xCx alloys. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the B 0.5 alloy
exhibits a single-phase bcc solid solution structure with
diffraction peaks of 39.6 /C176 , 57.2 /C176 , and 71.8 /C176 . Due to sluggish-
diffusion effect inherent in high-entropy alloys and the varying
melting points of the constituent elements, achieving a
homogeneous elemental distribution is challenging (Ref 27).
Consequently, as-cast high-entropy alloys typically display a
dendritic microstructure. When the B content increases to
5at.%, the compositional disparity between the dendritic and
interdendritic regions becomes more pronounced, leading to the
detection of a second bcc phase in the interdendritic region.
These regions are designated as bcc
1 (dendritic) and bcc 2
(interdendritic) for clarify. With the increase of B content, the
diffraction peak intensity of the bcc 2 phase increases. In the B 10
alloy, the B content exceeds the solid solubility limit, resulting
in the precipitation of B. In the B 15 alloy, the bcc 1 phase
disappears, and an excess of Ti was observed alongside the
formation of TiB and VB phases. This is mainly due to solute
oversaturation, and excessive B addition induces lattice insta-
bility. To mitigate this instability, B-rich components (TiB and
VB) precipitate from the lattice, stabilizing the lattice and
driving the formation of a single bcc
2 phase. Besides, the
thermodynamic interplay between alloying elements also plays
a critical role. The positive mixing enthalpy between Ti and Nb
(DHmix
Ti;Nb ¼ 2kJ =mol) (Ref 28) promotes Ti rejection from the
matrix, leading to the formation of Ti-rich phase. Notably, Ti
exhibits a strong preference for forming TiB 2 over other borides
(Ref 29). However, the coexistence of multiple elements (e.g.,
V and Nb) in this system likely suppresses TiB 2 formation,
favoring TiB and VB instead. Further investigation is required
to elucidate the underlying mechanisms. As shown in Fig. 1(b),
the patterns of the (NbTiV) 100-xCx alloys reveal distinct phase
transformations with increasing C content. When the content of
C is less than 10at.%, the alloy structure consists of a bcc
matrix and TiC phase, where the TiC diffraction peak intensity
grows with higher C content, reﬂecting progressive carbide
formation. In contrast, the C
15 alloy undergoes a signiﬁcant
phase transition, exhibiting an fcc matrix, TiC, (TiV)C 2 and
pure C phases. This structural transition is governed by
thermodynamic and kinetic factors. The mixing enthalpies of
C with Ti ( /C0 109 kJ/mol), Nb ( /C0 102 kJ/mol) and V
(/C0 82 kJ/mol) drive competitive carbide formation (Ref 28).

Ti element is a strong afﬁnity from carbon and usually leads to
the formation of TiC and TiC 2 phases during solidiﬁcation.
However, the low thermodynamic activity of Ti requires a
higher local concentration of Ti atoms to nucleate phase (Ref
29). During practical solidiﬁcation, the rapid cooling condition
induces the metastable precipitation of TiC and (TiV)C 2. The
concomitant depletion of Ti and V in the matrix destabilizes the
bcc structure, promoting a transition to an fcc solid solution. At
the supra-saturation C levels, excess unbound C precipitates as
a distinct carbon phase. This complex phase evolution is
ampliﬁed by inherent high-entropy alloy characteristics, includ-
ing high-entropy effect, lattice distortion effect and slow
diffusion effect, which kinetically hinder equilibrium phase
formation and stabilize multicomponent precipitates (Ref 30).

mapping analysis.

Figure 6 shows the compressive properties of the
(NbTiV)
100-xBx and (NbTiV)100-xCx alloys at room temperature.
For the (NbTiV) 100-xBx alloys, the strengthening effect is
negligible when the B content is below 5at.%. However, as the
B content increases to 10at.% and 15at.%, the yield strength
increases signiﬁcantly to 922 MPa and 1804 MPa, respectively,
while the compressive strain decreases to 25.2% and 11.85%,
respectively. The primary reason for this is that element B tends
to form hard borides (such as TiB and VB), which enhance the
alloy strength through precipitation strengthening. However,
excessive B content leads to severe embrittlement, manifested
as a sharp peak in the compressive stress-strain curve, sudden
fracture, and a drastic decline in ductility. In the (NbTiV)
100-xCx
alloys, the compressive strength increases gradually with C
addition, accompanied by a corresponding reduction in com-
pressive strain. Notably, the C
15 alloy achieves the highest
compressive strength of 2778 MPa, but exhibits severe brittle-
ness. The observed mechanical behavior is closely tied to the
microstructure evolution. The formation of carbides and
borides plays a critical role in strengthening these alloys.
While the bcc matrix phase provides ductility, the carbides and
borides enhance strength by impeding grain boundary slid and
dislocation migration. However, these intermetallic compounds
are inherently brittle and do not participate in plastic deforma-
tion, leading to localized stress concentration. When the stress
concentration exceeds the material /C213s tolerance, cracks initiate,
and propagate, resulting in reduced compressive strain (Ref 9).
Furthermore, coarse and irregular intermetallic phases exacer-
bate crack initiation, signiﬁcantly deteriorating the alloys /C213
plasticity. Interestingly, the B
15 alloy exhibits higher ductility
than the C 15 alloy, which can be attributed to two factors. On
the one hand, borides are inherently tougher than carbides due
to boron /C213s versatile bonding ability and ample atomic conﬁg-
urations (Ref 32); on the other hand, the C 15 alloy contains a
higher volume fraction of intermetallic phases, which further
increases its brittleness. Additionally, studies on Ti-6Al-4 V- xB
alloys have shown that large-sized borides can induce brittle-
ness, highlighting the importance of controlling not only the
content but also the size of intermetallic phases to achieve
balanced strength and plasticity (Ref 33).
Above all, the B
10 and C 10 alloys exhibit the attractive
compressive properties at room temperature, prompting further
investigation of their high-temperature mechanical properties.
As shown in Fig .7, the compressive yield strengths of the B 10
and C 10 alloys are as high as 943 and 1042 MPa, respectively,
at 600 /C176 C. However, it decreases to 664 and 701 MPa,
respectively, at 800 /C176 C, and drops signiﬁcantly to 326 and
358 MPa, respectively, at 1000 /C176 C. Conversely, the compres-
sive strain increases with the increase of temperature, a
phenomenon likely attributed to the dissolution of secondary
phases at elevated temperatures (Ref 6). It is noticed that the
C10 alloy demonstrates higher compressive yield strength than
the B10 alloy across all tested temperatures. This superiority can
be attributed to the strong interface bonding and higher stability
of carbides in the C
10 alloy. On one hand, the interface between
fcc structure carbides and bcc structure shows good bonding
due to low lattice mismatch. The study reports that the interface
barrier strength from BCC to MC ranges from 7.9 to 8.6 GPa,
signiﬁcantly larger than that of bcc-fcc HEAs (3.8-4 GPa) (Ref
34). On the other hand, the Nb element promotes and stabilizes
carbides formation. Both experimental and simulated data
conﬁrm that carbide exhibit a broader temperature stability
compared to borides (Ref 28). Therefore, these factors collec-
tively contribute to the competitive mechanical properties of
C
10 alloy during the thermal deformation. Additionally, as
illustrated in Fig. 7(d)-(f), both alloys exhibit a pronounced
strain hardening effect during the initial stages of high-
temperature compression. As the temperature increased from
600 to 1000 /C176 C, the peak strain hardening rate decreases from
14000 to 8000 MPa. With further strain, a mild strain softening

Low density and favorable comprehensive mechanical
properties are critical design criteria for high-entropy alloys.
For instance, AlNbTiV (5.49 g/cm

low densities and good ductility at room temperature, as shown

4. Conclusion
(1) With the addition of B or C element, the matrix phase
in the (NbTiV) 100-xBx alloys evolves from mixed struc-
ture of bcc 1 and bcc 2 solid solutions to a single bcc 2
phase. In contrast, the (NbTiV) 100-xCx alloys transition
from a bcc phase to an fcc phase. Additionally, borides
and carbides form progressively as the B or C content
increases.
(2) As the B or C content increases, the strength of the
NbTiV100-x(B/C)x alloys improved to varying degrees,
but at the expense of ductility. However, the excessive
additions of 15at.% B or C result in brittleness in the
B
15 and C 15 alloys.
(3) Compared to other reported RHEAs, the (NbTiV) 90B10
and (NbTiV) 90C10 alloys exhibit an optimal balance of
low density (6.24 and 6.22 g/cm 3, respectively) and
