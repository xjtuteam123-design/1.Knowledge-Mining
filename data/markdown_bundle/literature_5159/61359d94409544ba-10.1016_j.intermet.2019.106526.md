ABSTRACT
The microstructure and high-temperature strength in Cr –Si alloys with di ﬀerent Si contents were investigated by
SEM and a compression test. Cr –10Si and Cr –13Si alloys have a two-phase microstructure composed of Cr SS and
Cr3Si, and the morphology depends on the Si content. The 0.2% proof stress evaluated at 1000 °C increases with
increasing Si content to 15 at%, and the incremental amount becomes drastically larger in the composition range
of 10–13 at% Si. The optimal Si content in the balance of strength and ductility is about 13 at%. The 0.2% proof
stress at 1000 °C in a Cr –13Si alloy with a low density (6.68 g ･cm−3) is higher than that of a Ni-based superalloy,
Mar-M247, and the speci ﬁc strength is roughly twice as high as that in the Mar-M247.
1. Introduction
Chromium has potential as high temperature materials because,
compared with nickel, it has a higher melting point (Cr: 1857 °C, Ni:
1455 °C) and lower density (Cr: 7.14 g/cm 3, Ni: 8.9 g/cm 3). However,
there are two general problems with using Cr alloys as a heat resistant
alloy; low ductility at low temperatures due to a high ductile-brittle
transition temperature (DBTT) and embrittlement by oxidation and/or
nitridation at temperatures above 1100 °C [ 1–3]. Eﬀ orts have been
made to develop Cr alloys since the late 1940s and they have been
studied from the view of impurity elimination, grain re ﬁnement,
strengthening by solid solution or dispersion particle, alloying for de-
creasing DBTT and improving ductility and oxidation/nitridation re-
sistance [ 4–11]. However, only limited successes have been reported.
More recently, Cr-based alloys strengthened by precipitates of
Laves-Cr
2X (e.g., X = Ta, Nb, Hf, Zr) phases or an A15-Cr 3Si phase have
been researched. For example, Cr –Nb alloys with a soft Cr solid solution
(CrSS) phase and hard Cr 2Nb Laves phase show a high 0.2% proof stress
at 1000 °C [ 12], and its yield strength and fracture toughness at room
temperature were considerably higher than those of monolithic Cr 2Nb
because of the existence of the soft Cr SS phase [13]. In Cr–Ta alloys with
the Cr/Cr 2Ta two-phase structure, adding Si has improved the me-
chanical properties and its fracture toughness is 18.4 MPa m 1/2 at room
temperature [ 14]. In addition, the yield stress of a Cr –Ta–Si alloy at
1000 °C is comparable to other refractory alloys, such as Nb –Si and
Mo–Si–B alloys [ 15]. Cr– Nb and Cr– Ta alloys, however, have a sig-
niﬁcant problem in their oxidation and nitridation resistance. For ex-
ample, Cr– Nb alloys su ﬀer from rapid oxidation and extensive scale
spallation at 1100 °C, and Cr –Ta alloys have better oxidation and
nitridation resistance but there is plenty of room for improvement [ 16].
Therefore, the addition of Si to Cr –Nb and Cr–Ta alloys has been studied
from the viewpoint of improving oxidation and nitridation resistance
[17,18].
The Cr
SS/Cr3Si two-phase structure composed of the soft Cr SS phase
and a hard Cr 3Si phase can be obtained in the Cr– Si system as shown in
Fig. 1(a) [ 19]. However, the fracture toughness, which was reported for
a Cr-10 wt% Si alloy produced by powder metallurgy, is only about
7 MPa m1/2 at room temperature [ 20]. The e ﬀects of alloying elements,
Pt, Ge and Mo, on the microstructure have also been studied and it was
reported that adding Mo in the Cr –Si alloy results in a homogeneous
precipitation of the ﬁne Cr
3Si phase [ 21]. Furthermore, the Cr –Si alloy
that includes the Cr 3Si phase shows a good oxidation and nitridation
resistance at 1200 °C by formation of a Cr 3Si layer on the subsurface
[22]. This Cr 3Si layer is stable at high temperature and acts as a barrier
to the inward di ﬀusion of nitrogen, which protects the internal of the
alloy from nitridation. Therefore, Cr –Si alloys with the Cr SS/Cr3Si two-
phase structure are advantageous for high temperature materials,
especially from a viewpoint of oxidation and nitridation resistance.
However, systematic studies on the mechanical properties at high
temperatures have not been reported in the Cr –Si binary system.
In this paper, the microstructure and mechanical properties of Cr –Si
alloys with various Si content from 0 to 16 at.%, which include the
Cr
SS/Cr3Si two-phase alloys, were investigated.

3. Results
3.1. Microstructure
Fig. 1 (b), (c), and (d) shows a BSE image of a 8Si, 10Si, and 13Si
alloy annealed at 1400 °C for 24 h, respectively. The microstructural
change of a 13Si alloy annealed at 1400 °C for 1 h is shown in Fig. 1 (e) .
These specimens have a two-phase microstructure, and the chemical
compositions of the light and dark gray phases, in a Cr –13Si alloy an-
nealed for 168 h were determined to be 6.4 and 21.5 at% Si, respec-
tively. From the composition analysis and the phase diagram, this
specimen is considered to be composed of a Cr
SS phase with the A2
structure (light gray) and the Cr 3Si phase with the A15 structure (dark
gray), although both the Si compositions obtained for the Cr SS and Cr3Si
phases are slightly lower than those in the reported phase diagram. This
means that the Cr– Si binary phase diagram should be reassessed after
more precise experimental work is conducted. Fig. 1(d) and (e) shows
that the Cr
SS and Cr 3Si two-phase microstructure coarsens with an-
nealing time. The 13Si alloy has the primary Cr SS and the ﬁne
CrSS +C r 3Si microstructure inherited by eutectic solidi ﬁcation, while
the 10Si alloy features the Cr SS surrounded by Cr 3Si, which resulted
from suppression of eutectic solidiﬁ cation because of a lower Si con-
tent.
3.2. Concentration dependence of high temperature strength
Fig. 2(a) shows the stress-strain curves obtained at 1000 °C for Cr –Si
alloys with di ﬀerent Si contents annealed at 1400 °C for 24 h. The 0Si –
10Si alloys show high ductility with a fracture strain over 35% and the
ﬂow stress increases with increasing Si content. However, the ductility
in the 13Si – 16Si alloys dramatically decreases with increasing Si
content, and the 16Si alloy is considerably brittle and broken before
plastic deformation, because of the high fraction of hard and brittle
Cr3Si. The 13%Si seems to be a critical content on ductility, exhibiting
both high strength and high ductility with a fracture strain of about
20%. The 0.2% proof stress of Cr –Si alloys at 1000 °C is summarized as
a function of Si content in Fig. 2(b). The mole fraction calculated by the
lever rule using the experimental result of composition in the Cr
SS and
Cr3Si phases at 1400 °C is also shown in Fig. 2(b). The proof stress in-
creases with increasing Si content, and the increase is almost propor-
tional to the Si content in the Cr SS alloys with Si content up to 6.4 at%,
which is the solubility limit of Si. This means the solid solution
strengthening is linear to the Si content. However, in the Cr
SS +C r 3Si
two-phase region over 6.4 at% Si, the proof stress non-linearly increases
with increasing Si content and a drastic increase appears in 10 –13 at%
Si, and then the increase becomes moderate again in 13 –14 at% Si. This
behavior cannot simply be explained by the increase in the fraction of
Cr
3Si phase.
This implies that the proof stress depends on the microstructure.
One can ﬁnd that the eutectic microstructure starts to appear at about
10Si by comparing it with 8Si as shown in Fig. 1(b) and (c), and that the
fraction of the eutectic microstructure increases with increasing Si
content as shown in Fig. 1 (c) and (d). This microstructural change
suggests that the ﬁne eutectic microstructure could increase the high-
temperature strength. To reveal this, a compression test was performed
in the 13Si alloy with di ﬀerent microstructures obtained by di ﬀerent
annealing times (1 h, 24 h and 168 h) at 1400 °C. The 0.2% proof stress
is plotted in Fig. 2 (b). Despite coarsening of the Cr
SS and Cr 3Si two-
phase microstructure by a long annealing time (1 h: Fig. 1(e), 24 h:
Fig. 1(d), 168 h: not shown), the proof stress is almost the same for
these specimens. Proof stress increases with decreasing lamellar thick-
ness in Ti –Al alloys, obeying the Hall-Petch relation [ 23]. However, it is
concluded that the proof stress at 1000 °C in Cr –Si alloys is not strongly
increased by a ﬁne eutectic microstructure simply. On the other hand,
the morphology of the primary Cr SS changes when the Si content in-
creases from 8Si to 10Si or 13 Si. The primary Cr SS is more ﬁnely iso-
lated by the Cr 3Si in 10Si and 13Si. Therefore, not only eutectic mi-
crostructure, but also the morphology of the primary Cr SS might be
related to the drastic increase in proof stress in Cr –Si alloys. Further
research is necessary to clarify this issue.

3.3. Temperature dependence of proof stress
The 13Si alloy was subjected to compression testing at various
temperatures ranging from room temperature to 1200 °C, and the 0.2%
proof stress is shown in Fig. 3, together with the result of tensile testing
of Ni-based cast superalloys [ 24]. The 0.2% proof stress at room tem-
perature and 400 °C was not evaluated because of fracture before plastic
deformation. The ductility is improved with increasing test temperature
and the fracture strain of 13Si by compression test at 1000 °C is about
25%. This value is not lower than that of Mar-M247 (10% tensile
elongation), Alloy 713C (20%) and IN-939 (25%) at 980 °C [ 24], al-
though the test method is di ﬀerent from the literature data referred.
The proof stresses at temperatures over 600 °C are higher than those of
Ni-based cast superalloys throughout. Although the ductility at low
temperatures is not su ﬃcient at present, the two-phase Cr –Si alloys
have an enough potential as high-temperature materials to overcome
the temperature limit of conventional Ni-based alloys.
4. Conclusion
The microstructure and mechanical properties in Cr –Si alloys with
Si contents ranging from 0 to 16 at% were investigated. The 10Si and
13Si alloys have a two-phase microstructure consisting of Cr
SS and
Cr3Si. The 10Si alloy shows the Cr SS dendrites surrounded by the Cr 3Si
phase, and the ﬁne Cr SS +C r 3Si microstructure inherited by eutectic
solidiﬁcation, is partially observed in addition to the primary Cr SS phase
in 13Si. The ductility at 1000 °C in a compression test is relatively high
in 0Si –10Si alloys, but in 15Si and 16Si, fracture occurs before plastic
deformation. The 0.2% proof stress at 1000 °C increases with increasing
Si content to 15 at%, and a remarkable increase is obtained in the
composition range of 10– 13 at%. The 13Si alloy exhibits excellent high-
temperature strength and relatively high ductility at 1000 °C, while
ductility needs to be improved at temperatures lower than 400 °C.
Because the 0.2% proof stress at high temperatures is higher and the
density (6.68 g ･cm
−3) is lower than those in the Ni-based superalloy
MarM-247, the speci ﬁc strength at 1000 °C (78 N m g −1) in the 13Si
alloy is twice as high as that in the MarM-247.
