ABSTRACT
This study challenges the generally accepted principle that some degree of as-cast microstructure heritability 
(CMH), marked by < 001 > -oriented dendrite-associated inhomogeneity, is tolerable in conventional [001]- 
oriented Ni-based single crystal (SX) superalloys. Our findings reveal that this principle does not hold for 
newly developed [111]-oriented SX superalloys, where < 001 > -directed dendrites experience significant 
resolved shear stress under [111] applied loads. This work examines the stress rupture behaviors of a [111]- 
oriented low-Re Ni-based SX superalloy under various CMH conditions at 1100 
◦
C/160 MPa and 760 
◦
C/800 
MPa. In the absence of CMH, the alloy achieves rupture properties comparable to fourth-generation SX super -
alloys. However, the presence of CMH drastically shortens rupture life and alters multi-scale deformation be -
haviors. High-temperature damage involves submicroscopic dislocation shearing, microscopic crack initiation, 
mesoscopic inter-dendritic crack connections, and macroscopic fractures. Intermediate-temperature damage is 
marked by submicroscopic stacking fault shearing, microscopic shear zone deformation, mesoscopic crack 
propagation, and macroscopic lattice rotation. Moreover, this research investigates the degradation mechanism 
of stress rupture property when the CMH is combined with slow cooling and reveals unique deformation be -
haviors, such as high-temperature subgrain formation and intermediate-temperature isolated micro-twins. This 
work provides new insights into the influence mechanism of the CMH.
1. Introduction
Ni-based single crystal (SX) superalloys have become the primary 
material solution for aero-engine turbine blades because of their excel -
lent combination of high-temperature creep resistance, fatigue resis -
tance, and mechanical properties [1] . The creep properties of Ni-based 
SX superalloys are closely related to microstructure and composition 
heterogeneity between the dendritic (D) and inter-dendritic (ID) regions 
[2 – 7] . During solidification, the differing melting points of alloying el -
ements result in significant dendritic segregation, forming large γ’ phase 
and γ / γ’ eutectic pools in the ID regions [8,9] . These as-cast micro -
structures (CMs) and dendritic segregation remain in SX superalloys 
after incomplete solution treatment, known as as-cast microstructure 
heritability (CMH). With the advancement of the SX superalloys, the 
content of refractory elements such as Re and W with low diffusion rates 
has progressively increased. Homogenizing these elements and elimi -
nating CMs requires higher solution temperatures and extended holding 
times [10,11] . However, the low incipient melting temperature in the ID 
regions narrows the solution window, hindering the increase of the so -
lution temperature [10 – 12] . These constrain the heat treatment design,

The multi-scale effects of the CMH on the heterogeneity of creep 
behaviors at the dendrite scale have been systematically investigated in 
Ni-based SX superalloys with [001] orientation [6,7,13 – 16] . Xia et al. 
[6,7] explored the impact of compositional inhomogeneity between the 
D and ID regions on the high-temperature and low-stress creep behaviors 
of Ni-based SX superalloys. Results showed that faster creep damage 
occurred in the ID regions, and the high-density topologically close- 
packed (TCP) phase formed in the D regions due to the segregation of 
the refractory elements, which accelerated crack initiation. Milhet et al. 
[13] revealed that the γ’ rafting and topological inversion in the D/ID 
regions were asynchronous at high temperatures for a fourth-generation 
SX superalloy with chemical segregations. Br ¨uckner et al. [14,15] found 
that the lattice misfits were related to the dendritic position, and the 
segregation of refractory elements made the misfits more negative in the 
D regions. Hau ß mann et al. [16] explored the intermediate-temperature 
creep properties of the D/ID regions for the ERBO1 alloy by indentation 
creep tests and found that the creep resistance of the D regions is higher 
than that of the ID regions.
It is widely recognized the arrangement of as-cast defects, such as 
solidification pores (S-pores), could affect the fracture mode and the 
creep properties of Ni-based SX superalloys [17 – 19] . G. M
¨
ALZER et al. 
[17] argued that the S-pores aligned along the [001] direction are 
difficult to interconnect in the [001]-oriented alloy, so that the creep 
property was minimally affected by the alignment of the pores, 
compared to the [110]- and [111]-oriented alloy. Liu et al. [18] and Li 
et al. [19] both found that S-pores distributed along the [001] direction 
contributed to dendrite fracture in [111]-oriented alloys at elevated 
temperature, while [001]-oriented alloys did not have this character -
istic. The arrangement of the CMH is consistent with that of S-pores in SX 
superalloys. Therefore, the effect of the CMH potentially depends on the 
spatial relationship between the external stress directions and the den -
dritic directions, which needs to be revealed.
Recently, [111]-oriented Ni-based SX superalloys have been gradu -
ally developed for their remarkable high-temperature creep resistance 
as well as strength-ductility balance [20,21] . However, the influence 
mechanism of the CMH in [001]-oriented SX alloys is not fully appli -
cable to [111]-oriented SX alloys. The multi-scale behaviors reflected 
under the influence of the CMH remain unclear for [111]-oriented al -
loys, which constrains the application of these alloys. It is crucial to 
explore the impact of the CMH on intermediate/high temperature creep 
behaviors of [111]-oriented alloys to advance new SX superalloys as 
well as supplement the understanding of the CMH.
In this work, a [111]-oriented Ni-based SX superalloy is the research 
object and four different heat treatment systems are used to control the 
CMH states as well as the microstructure morphology. Stress rupture 
tests are conducted at 1100 
◦

◦
C/800 MPa to explore 
how the CMH impacts the multi-scale behaviors at intermediate and 
high temperatures, such as submicroscopic deformation, microstructure 
evolution, and fracture mode. This paper will help to better understand 
the influences of the CMH and provide a theoretical basis for the 
development and design of new SX superalloys.

3. Results
3.1. The microstructures after heat treatment
Fig. 2 shows the microstructures of the alloys with different heat 
treatments. Viewed as a whole, the degree of the CMH gradually dete -
riorated as the maximum solution temperatures dropped. In Fig. 2 (a, e, 
i) and (b, f, j), there was significant microstructural heterogeneity in the 
D/ID regions of the alloys treated with HT-1 and HT-2, with the large γ’ 
phase as well as coarse γ’ phase (the white arrows in Fig. 2 (a, i)) in the 
ID regions. It is noticeable that the high Al content in Alloy-A led to a 
greater tendency to precipitate the large γ’ phase in the ID regions 
instead of γ / γ’ eutectic pools [21,22] . No dendritic structure was dis -
cerned macroscopically for the alloy after the standard heat treatment 
(HT-3) ( Fig. 2 (c)). However, the ID regions can be identified based on 
the S-pores which are formed by insufficient shrinkage of the liquid in 
the ID regions during solidification. In combination with Fig. 2 (g, k), no 
microstructural inhomogeneity was found in the two regions of the alloy 
after HT-3 treatment. In Fig. 2 (d, h, i), it is noticed that the size of the 
two phases in the D/ID regions of the HT-4 specimen shows significant 
coarsening, in addition to the microstructural heterogeneity.
Fig. 2 (m, n) and Table 3 summarize the size and volume fraction of 
the γ and γ’ phases in the D/ID regions. The characteristics related to the 
large γ’ phase in the heat-treated alloys are depicted in Table 4 . With the 
same cooling conditions, as the solution temperatures increased (from 
HT-1 to HT-3), the γ’ size and volume fraction of the D regions increased, 
while the γ channel width decreased, as in Fig. 2 (m, n) and Table 3 . 
Meanwhile, the size and the number density of the large γ’ particles 
reduced (see Table 4 ). These changes primarily occurred because, as the 
temperature rose, the coarse and large γ’ phases in the ID regions 
gradually dissolved. The enriched γ’ -forming elements (Al, Ta) diffused 
towards the D regions and tended to become more homogeneous. 
Finally, more γ’ particles precipitated in the D regions during the cooling 
process, with the corresponding reduction in γ width, while the alter -
ations in microstructural features of the ID regions are opposite. For the 
HT-4 treated specimen, the obvious microstructure ripening was 
observed in the D/ID regions, as presented in Fig. 2 (m, n) and Table 3 : 
the γ’ sizes and the γ widths reached around 3000 – 5000 nm and 
400 – 600 nm, respectively; the γ’ volume fractions were also up to 
72 – 77 %. One should also notice that the size and area fraction of the 
large γ’ phase were higher than HT-1, HT-2, and HT-3, as in Table 4 . 
These indicate that the slow cooling mode promotes the precipitation 
and ripening of the γ’ phase as well as the growth of the large γ’ phase.

reduced. When the CMH was eliminated (HT-3), the fracture surface of 
the specimen was perpendicular to the applied load, which was typical 
of an opening-type fracture (ModeI), as shown in Fig. 5 (c). The fracture 
morphology of HT-3 had traces of micropores aggregation and lacked 
dendritic characteristics ( Fig. 5 (g)).
3.3.2. Fracture microstructures
To better understand the impact of the CMH on rupture behaviors, 
the facture microstructures of the ( 1 10 ) longitudinal sections in four 
heat-treated samples are characterized, as shown in Fig. 6 . According to 
Fig. 6 (a), there were some white bright needle-like or blocky Mo-rich 
TCP precipitates (red arrows) in the ID regions. The high Mo content 
in the Alloy-A (7 – 8 % w.t.%) and the enrichment of Mo in the ID areas of 
HT-1 contributed to the precipitation of the Mo-rich TCP phase during 
creep [22] . However, crack initiation in HT-1 primarily occurred at the 
large γ’ phase (highlighted by blue arrows) and the S-pores (indicated by 
white arrows), as depicted in Fig. 6 (a-b). During the stress tests, these 
defects acted as the stress concentration locations prompting the 
microcrack initiation in the HT-1-treated specimens. Combined with 
Fig. 5 (a, e), it could be inferred that these defects which were densely 
arranged parallel to the [001] direction caused microcracks intercon -
nection and propagation along the dendritic direction. Then, numerous 
fatal cracks were generated in the ID regions of HT-1, which ultimately 
contributed to forming the single (110) macroscopic fracture surface. In 
comparison, the microcrack spacings in the ID regions of the HT-2 
sample were larger ( Fig. 6 (c)), attributing to a lower degree of the 
CMH. The crack propagation along the dendritic direction was more 
pronounced in the HT-4 specimen ( Fig. 6 (f)), suggesting that the coarse 
microstructures facilitated the occurrence of this fracture mode. Fig. 6
(d, e) shows the microscopic morphology of the HT-3 specimen after 
rupture. Numerous deformation pores (D-pores) in the HT-3 specimen 
are shown by the white ellipse and the arrow in Fig. 6 (e). Moreover, 
there was a significant increase in the number of TCP precipitates ( Fig. 6
(d)). The phenomenon indicated it underwent long-term creep damage.

3.3.3. Dislocation configurations after fracture
Fig. 8 shows the dislocation configurations observed along the [001] 
zone axis of four heat-treated specimens at 6 mm from the fracture 
surface. The dislocation features in HT-1 and HT-2 are depicted in Fig. 8
(a, b) and (c, d), respectively. The γ’ precipitates were interconnected in 
the D regions of HT-1 and HT-2, and there were a large number of dis -
locations in the γ’ phase ( Fig. 8 (a) and (c)). However, in comparison 
with the D regions of the HT-1 and HT-2, the dislocation densities in the 
ID large γ’ phase of the two specimens were considerably higher ( Fig. 8
(b, d)), which revealed that there was severe plastic damage in the ID 
regions. The phenomenon directly confirms the intrinsic reason for the 
microcrack initiation and propagation at the large precipitates ( Fig. 6 (a, 
c)). As observed in Fig. 8 (e), the dislocation configuration of the HT-3

consists of submicroscopic dislocation shearing, microscopic crack

occurrence of severe plastic damage accelerated the microcrack initia -

alloys [17,49,50] . This phenomenon was also found, as shown in Fig. 6

the main regions of crack initiation.

transverse ends of a single crack is the largest, leading to the propagation 
perpendicular to the direction of the tension axis [17,50,51] . For the 
[111]-oriented SX alloy, its dendritic direction is at an angle (54.7
◦

the resolved shear stress in the [001] direction which the CMs and S-

vicinity of them. This promoted microcracks propagation along the 
[001] direction. Furthermore, at high-temperature and low-stress con -
ditions, the growth of pores and microcracks was closely related to the 
dislocation slip-climb and vacancy diffusion. During high-temperature 
plastic deformation, the vacancy concentration and dislocation density 
increased, and vacancies diffused synergistically with dislocations to the 
closer internal surfaces (pores and microcracks), which resulted in the 
development of pores and microcracks [49,52,53] . These reveal that the 
internal connection of pores and microcracks is a progressive develop -
ment at high temperatures. All of the mentioned conditions facilitated 
the interconnection of pores and microcracks along the [001] direction 
of intensive distribution of stress concentration.
At the macroscopic scale, for the HT-1 specimen with the higher 
volume fraction and the larger average diameter of the large γ’ phase, at 
the center of its gauge length (large strain regions), the macrocracks in 
multiple ID regions connected creating a macroscopic fracture surface 
parallel to the (110) plane ( Fig. 14 (d)). As the level of the CMH 
decreased (HT-2), the areas of stress concentration reduced ( Fig. 6 (c)). 
Therefore, the dendritic fracture appeared exclusively in the regions 
with a dense distribution of CMs, and eventually, partial dendritic fail -
ure was characterized. However, the HT-3 sample only had S-pores in 
the ID regions, which mitigated the risk of rapid fracture along the [001] 
direction. After a long period of high-temperature service, a large 
number of randomly distributed D-pores and TCP precipitates were 
generated ( Fig. 6 (e) and Fig. 14 (f)). The S-pores and the randomly 
distributed areas of stress concentration caused the alloy to undergo 
Mode I fracture, as shown in Fig. 14 (g).

creep rate and strain of the primary creep [25,35,57,58] . Furthermore, 
for HT-1 and HT-2, the high-density and single-oriented continuous SFs 
contained in them have no work-hardening effect. These deformation 
behaviors not only accelerated the creep rate but also led to lattice 
rotation. The features are similar to the [0 1 1]-oriented alloy crystal 
rotations, except that the mechanism leading to their single-slip is not 
identical [24,31,32] . MacKay et al. [67] concluded that a greater degree 
of primary creep strains and crystal rotation would decrease the area of 
the cross-section and increase the actual stress on the cross-section and 
thus accelerate the secondary creep rate and fracture. Moreover, Qu 
et al. [31,32] thought that the lattice rotation was asynchronous, 
contributing to local stress concentration in the specimen and macro -
scopic deformation inhomogeneity, expediting the failure of the single 
alloys. Therefore, for HT-1 and HT-2, the lattice rotation as a conse -
quence of mono-oriented slip due to the CMH further aggravated creep 
fracture.
After the removal of the CMH (HT-3), the narrow γ channels 
increased the Orowan stress, reducing the number of dislocations in the 
γ channels during the initial stages of creep. As a result, a minor amount 
of single-oriented a < 112 > ribbons were formed, as shown in Fig. 16 (e). 
The homogeneous microstructure ensured the deformation uniformity, 
causing more difficult nucleation of a < 112 > ribbons, compared to HT-1 
and HT-2. As the tensile strain increased, multiple < 110 > {111} slip 
systems in the γ channels were activated, producing different oriented 
SFs within the γ’ phase. These SFs interacted forming immovable SF- 
locks [40,68,69] ( Fig. 12 (e, f) and Fig. 16 (e)), which play a work- 
hardening role and reduce the creep strain rate [40,41,57] . Further -
more, the elimination of the ID large γ’ phase decreased the crack 
initiation. Because the alloy contained a small number of continuous 
SFs, it underwent a slight crystal rotation ( Fig. 16 (g)), diminishing the 
macroscopic localized stress concentration caused by lattice rotation.
4.3. Effect of the coupling of the CMH and slow cooling on stress rupture 
properties and deformation behaviors
Compared to other SX superalloys [70,71] , the high Al content of 
Alloy-A facilitated the γ’ ripening behavior during the slow cooling 
process. These coarse microstructures expedited the slips and shearing 
of dislocations and overshadowed the difference in micro-scale defor -
mation behaviors between the D and ID regions. However, the slow 
cooling mode promoted the further growth of the ID large γ’ phase 
( Table 4 ). This feature combined with the anomalous two-phase mi -
crostructures rapidly degraded the properties of the alloy. At high

inhomogeneous deformation, which ultimately leads to significant 
crystal rotation ( Fig. 9 ). However, the initiation of multiple slip systems 
was promoted by the coarse two-phase microstructure in the HT-4 
specimen, which facilitated homogeneous deformation, resulting in a 
lack of shear bands characteristic of the microscale morphology. 
Furthermore, the homogeneous deformation also leads to a homoge -
neous reduction of the macroscopic cross-section, presented as a circular 
cross-section profile instead of an elliptical one.The rapid degradation of 
stress rupture properties due to the coarse microstructure in HT-4 is not 
unexpected. Intriguingly, these detrimental microstructures caused two 
uncommon deformation features in the alloys. One is the γ’ -subgrains 
generated at the high-temperature condition. It is generally accepted 
that recrystallization of SX superalloys occurs in the γ matrix due to 
sufficient deformation storage energy and the dissolution of the γ’ phase 
[72 – 74] . However, it is rarely found that the subgrains exist within the 
γ’ phase [30] . For HT-4, the wider matrix channels weakened the hin -
drance of the γ’ precipitates for dislocations, introducing significant 
plastic deformation within a short period. As mentioned in Section 3.3 , 
the γ’ -subgrain boundaries evolved from multiple dislocation arrays as 
well as dislocation networks in the γ’ phase ( Fig. 10 (f, j, h)). Compared 
to HT-1/2/3, it was easier for multiple sets of parallel-arranged dislo -
cations to form in the wider γ channels of HT-4. Meanwhile, the coarse γ’ 
phase lowered the CRSS of APB shearing, allowing numerous dislocation 
arrays to break through the interfacial networks and cut into the γ’ 
phase. Several slip systems interacted to form dislocation networks 
dividing the γ’ phase into multiple small regions. These reveal that 
recrystallization also occurs in the γ’ phase when the deformation 
storage energy of the γ’ phase exceeds certain thresholds. Typically, in 
Ni-based SX superalloys, the phenomenon is not observed during the 
creep process, due to small damage in the γ’ phase. The deleterious 
microstructures in HT-4 amplify the extent of damage in the γ’ phase, 
causing this phenomenon to be more conspicuous.
The other is the isolated micro-twins formed in the matrix at the 
intermediate temperature condition. After the failure of the rapidly 
cooling SX alloys, the SFs with small extended widths are formed in the 
narrow γ channels, but there are no isolated micro-twins [24,42,75] . For 
HT-4, the slow cooling process caused the solid solution elements (Co, 
Cr, Re, Mo) to diffuse from the γ’ phase into the γ matrix, while Al and Ta 
diffused into the γ’ phase, gradually approaching the equilibrium com -
positions [76] . It is widely known that Co, Cr, and Re reduce the stacking 
fault energy (SFE) of the γ phase [77,78] . Additionally, the wide spacing 
between the γ’ precipitates reduced the obstruction effect on the 
extension of SFs. The two factors contributed to the formation of a large 
number of micro-twins and SFs in the γ channels. These planar faults in 
the γ phase are formed by the decomposition of a/2 < 110 > into the a/6 
< 112 > dislocations. If the leading a/6 < 112 > dislocations cut into the 
γ’ phase, then high energy complex stacking faults (CSFs) are generated 
[54] . This mechanism is difficult to achieve for the [111]-oriented alloys 
due to higher twin nucleation stress [43] . Therefore, the isolated micro- 
twins and SFs formed in the γ phase are hard to enter the γ’ phase. The 
coarse γ’ phase was more susceptible to shearing by APB-coupled 
dislocation pairs due to the reduced CRSS. The microstructures of HT- 
4 resulted in the absence of the continuous SFs in the γ’ precipitates.

activated. These large deformation zones interact with cast defects, 
leading to significant stress concentration around the defects. To release 
the stress concentration, microcracks propagate along the direction of 
the deformation zone. It is worth noting that the direction of the ID crack 
propagation is less affected by the applied stress compared to the 
interference effect between the numerous shear bands and the cast de -
fects. In other words, the deformation inhomogeneity reflected in the 
shear zones is more significantly detrimental to the intermediate tem -
perature creep properties compared to the change of the local stress field 
around the cast defects by the applied load. Therefore, there is no 
macrocracks propagation and coalescence along the dendrite direction.
The impact of CMH is not only confined to [111]-oriented SX su -
peralloys, as its essence is determined by the spatial relationship be -
tween the dendrite and segregation arrangement directions and the 
external stress direction. Therefore, in commercially utilized [001]- 
oriented SX superalloys, the presence of structures like cooling holes 
and spoiler ribs under certain stress concentrations also exhibits the 
high-risk CMH effects described in this study. Accordingly, this study 
should provide useful guidance for future investigations in this field.
5. Conclusion
In this study, we have systematically investigated the impacts of the 
CMH on the high/ intermediate temperature stress rupture properties of 
[111]-oriented Ni-based SX superalloy. The main conclusions are sum -
marized as follows:

(1) The CMH is detrimental to the properties of [111]-oriented Ni- 
based SX superalloy. The high/intermediate temperature stress 
rupture properties of the second-generation [111]-oriented alloy 
with standard heat treatment can reach the level of the fourth- 
generation [001]-oriented superalloy. However, under the in -
fluence of the different CMH in this experiment, its high- 
temperature stress rupture life is reduced almost to half, and its 
intermediate-temperature stress rupture life decreases even in 
magnitude order.
(2) The multi-scale damage mechanisms of the CMH for the [111]- 
oriented superalloy are fully explored. At high temperature, the 
submicroscopic shearing of the γ’ phase and the accumulation of 
plastic damage in the D/ID regions is facilitated, due to the low γ’ 
volume fraction in the D regions and the wide γ channel of the ID 
regions. Moreover, the residual large γ’ precipitates aligned along 
the [001] direction cause rapid microscopic crack initiation and 
mesoscopic crack connection, ultimately leading to macroscopic 
dendritic fracture.
(3) At intermediate temperature, the CMH makes significant differ -
ences between the D/ID submicroscopic deformation mecha -
nisms. There is the nucleation and propagation of numerous 
continuous SFs composed of a < 112 > ribbons in the D regions, 
while the shearing of the γ’ phase by APB is prone to occur in the 
ID regions. Furthermore, the more deteriorative the CMH, the 
higher the density of continuous SFs. These factors ultimately 
contribute to high elongation as well as macroscopic crystal 
rotation.
(4) The slow cooling process weakens the differences in micro-plastic 
deformation between the D/ID regions and enhances the stress 
concentration effect of large γ’ phases. Moreover, the coupling 
effect between the CMH and anomalous microstructures severely 
accelerates the rupture of the alloy and generates two unusual 
deformation behaviors: the emergence of high-temperature sub -
grains within the γ’ phase and the development of intermediate- 
temperature isolated micro-twins within the γ phase.
(5) The spatial relationship between the CMH and applied stresses 
increases the risk of rapid fracture of [111]-oriented alloys at 
high temperatures.
