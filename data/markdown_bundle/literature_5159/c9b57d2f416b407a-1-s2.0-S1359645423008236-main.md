ABSTRACT 
Castable alumina forming austenitic (AFA) alloys have demonstrated superior creep life and oxidation resistance 
at temperatures exceeding 800 ⁰ C. Despite the success in the applicability of these alloys in extreme environ -
ments, there is a limited understanding of the deformation modes and the influence of each alloying element 
guiding the alloy design strategies that could further enhance the creep strength of these AFA alloys, particularly 
at temperatures at and above 900 ⁰ C. In this study, we reveal the mechanism underpinning the superior creep 
performance of castable AFA alloys that involves suppressing primary carbide formation through minor 
compositional modification. This approach results in a three-fold increase in creep strength at 900 ⁰ C and 50 MPa. 
By employing integrated characterization techniques, we analyzed the microstructures of two AFA alloys, both 
before and after the creep process. We discovered that the suppression of primary carbides permits the in-situ 
clustering of now-available interstitial elements such as C, Si, and O during high-temperature creep. This 
improved solid solution strengthening and reduced stacking fault energy of the alloy. Moreover, it also enabled 
controlled secondary carbide formation during testing, further improving the creep resistance. These findings 
underline the important interplay between alloy composition, microstructure, and creep properties, and offer a 
promising design strategy for developing economical high-temperature Fe-based alloys suitable for advanced 
applications.

During steady-state high-temperature creep, there is a balance be -
tween strain hardening and dynamic recovery via dislocation climb and 
vacancy flow [15 – 19] . Typically, alloy design strategies aim to increase 
creep strength by increasing the resistance to dislocation creep and 
diffusion creep [ 15 , 20 , 21 ]. Dislocation creep can be delayed by Orowan 
strengthening. Reducing secondary particle size and inter-particle dis -
tance increases the threshold stress necessary for dislocation climb [ 20 , 
22-25 ]. Diffusion creep can be also reduced by solute atoms, typically by 
adding Cr, Mo, Co, W, and B [ 20 , 21 , 26-28 ]. Solute atoms in solid so -
lutions reduce self-diffusion rates and leads to viscous dislocation mo -
tion within the matrix [21] . In AFA alloy design, the addition of Ti, V, 
and Nb has been introduced to assure precipitation of nano-scale 
MX-type (TiC, VC, NbC, and FeTiP) carbides, which pin dislocation 
motion, although Ti and V can also degrade oxidation resistance [ 8 , 9 , 13 , 
14 , 29 , 30 ]. As for solution strengthening, AFA alloys incorporate W and 
B to assist in the reduction of dislocation recombination during recovery 
[ 9 , 13 , 14 , 30 ].

4.2. High creep performance of AFA alloys 
AFA5 exhibits a three-times improvement in creep rupture life, 
increasing from 400 h (AFA2) to 1200 h when both were subjected to a 
constant load of 50 MPa at 900 
◦
C. To understand the differences in 
creep performance, two key concepts should be discussed: dislocation 
creep (precipitate/dislocation interaction) and diffusion creep (solid 
solution strengthening). These concepts are separated as follows: 
4.2.1. Precipitate/dislocation interaction 
In creep-resistant steels, boundary pinning by secondary precipitates 
is essential to delay recovery and enhance creep strength [ 24 , 49 , 50 ]. 
Therefore, controlling the stability of precipitates (coarsening resis -
tance) and reducing interparticle spacing are topics of interest in alloy 
design. Our microstructural analysis of AFA2 and AFA5 after creep 
shows that the highest dislocation pinning effect is observed within the 
Fe- γ matrix ( Fig. 6 ). The retention of a higher dislocation density in AFA5 
is caused by nano-sized C and Si clustering, decorating dislocation ar -
rangements ( Fig. 8 ). Additionally, B2 and M
23
C
6 
can pin dislocations but 
are less effective than elemental clustering. Finally, Laves appear to be 
the least effective secondary phase due to rapid coarsening. 
Secondary precipitation of M
23
C
6 
and MC has been used as one of the 
primary mechanisms to enhance creep strength in ferritic, bainitic, 
martensitic, austenitic, and even duplex martensitic-austenitic creep- 
resistant steels [ 27 , 47 , 49 , 51-53 ], and even AFA alloys [14] . The pres -
ence of fine M
23
C
6 
precipitates is a known effective methodology to pin 
subgrain motion [ 26 , 49 , 52 , 54 , 55 ]. Whereas finely dispersed nano-sized 
MX is more effective in pinning dislocations [ 30 , 52 , 56-58 ]. Neverthe -
less, the beneficial effect of these two types of precipitates is quickly 
impaired by coarsening. 
Coarsening of grain boundary M
23
C
6 
accelerates creep rupture by 
Ostwald ripening (resulting in reduced boundary pinning efficiency) 
[ 48 , 55 , 59 ], and by localized nucleation and coalescence of pores at the 
carbide vicinities [20] . The coarsening process has been shown to occur 
via Cr and Mo diffusion from the matrix, partially substituting Fe within 
the carbide, until thermodynamic equilibrium is reached [ 28 , 54 , 60 ]. 
However, the high temperature stability of M
23
C
6 
can be increased by 
preferential diffusion of Mo, B, and W [ 28 , 46 , 61 ]. Taneike et al. showed 
that a high density of nano-sized coarsening-resistant MC carbides can 
radically increase creep strength via dislocation pinning [30] . However, 
the co-precipitation of M
23
C
6 
and the coarsening of MC have been shown 
to contribute to the degradation of creep rupture [ 30 , 51 ]. Avoiding MX 
coarsening is fundamental to reducing dislocation climb [ 20 , 22-25 ], and 
thus to delaying creep rupture. 
The secondary precipitation of finely dispersed and intra-granular B2 
has the potential to pin dislocation glide in Fe- γ matrix by resistance to 
slicing [ 13 , 14 , 29 , 31 ]. Our results show dislocation pinning by B2, 
especially at the B2/Fe- γ interface of the fine precipitates in AFA5 (see 
supplementary Figure S9). Laves, on the other hand, has not been 
related as effective creep strengthener in creep-resistant alloys. In AFA 
alloys, the isolated effect of Laves precipitation does not lead to 
enhanced creep strength, and a better creep performance is obtained 
when Laves co-precipitates with NbC [13] . Some dislocation pinning has 
been observed when Laves is at least 300 nm in AFA alloys [43] . Also, 
Laves coarsens faster [62] and exerts a lower boundary pinning effect 
[26] relative to M
23
C
6
. Thermodynamic simulations from Prat et al., 
suggested that the precipitation of Laves does not affect the coarsening 
of M
23
C
6 
[52] . However, during coarsening, Laves are enriched in W and 
Mo, thus leading to matrix depletion of solid solution strengthening el -
ements [ 25 , 47 , 62 , 63 ]. 
4.2.2. Solution strengthening mechanism 
The addition of Mo, Co, W, and B to creep-resistant steels [ 6 , 20 , 21 , 
26-28 ] reduces self-diffusion rates through solute dispersion in solid 
solution. The presence of B in solid solution is fundamental for grain 
boundary strengthening [64] and coarsening resistance of M
23
C
6 
during 
creep [ 46 , 56 , 61 , 64 ]. The addition of 1 – 3 wt.% W reduces the creep rate 
by decreasing self-diffusion and microstructural evolution rates [65] . 
Whereas addition of Mo and Nb have been shown to reduce the kinetics 
of high-temperature dislocation recombination via solid solution 
strengthening, increased lattice distortion, and reduced vacancy for -
mation energy [66] . Diffusion creep is accelerated once these elements 
are extracted from the solid solution through coarsening of secondary 
phases [46] . Therefore, the type and size of secondary precipitation 
during creep can strongly affect the time to rupture. 
Our APT compositional analysis in Table 2 shows that B has been 
extracted from the solid solution in AFA2 and AFA5. This depletion is 
caused by B partitioning into M
23
C
6 
( Fig. 7 ), consistent with [46] . 
Depletion of W from solid solution is also observed, especially in AFA2, 
due to widespread precipitation and coarsening of Laves. Notice that 
Laves is a Fe
2
M phase, where M can be associated with Mo, Si, Nb, and W 
[ 9 , 13 , 46 , 67 ]. In AFA5, the higher nominal C and B contents, as well as 
the reduced nominal Nb and Si contents, synergically limit the kinetics 
of Laves precipitation [ 9 , 13 , 14 , 29 ]. By preventing the widespread pre -
cipitation and growth of Laves in AFA5, there is more availability of W 
and Nb in solid solution, benefiting solid solution strengthening. Also, 
Nb is not observed within the Fe- γ matrix in AFA2 or AFA5 ( Table 2 and 
Fig. 5 g), indicating that this element is completely exhausted by 
co-precipitation of intra-granular nano-sized and grain boundary thick 
NbC. 
4.2.3. Interaction between a Cottrell atmosphere and dislocations 
Our coupled TEM-APT experiments showed that the dislocation 
structures in AFA5 are decorated with C, Si, and O-rich solute particles. 
The collection of solute atoms around the dislocation core also known as 
the Cottrell atmosphere increases the resistance to dislocation motion by 
stabilizing the dislocation structures [68] . The presence of excess C in 
the AFA5 can promote this effect in this alloy and thus can be an 
effective mechanism for higher creep life. 
The clustering of C and Si on defects was observed in the AFA5 alloy, 
where a high density of dislocations is present. However, it ’ s important 
to note that we did not conduct similar investigations into the 
dislocation-prone regions in AFA2. Correlative experiments of this na -
ture require meticulous sample preparation and analysis. Therefore, it 
cannot be ruled out that such clustering could also occur in AFA2. 
Nevertheless, our hypothesis is that AFA2 may have a lower avail -
ability of carbon during creep testing, limiting its capacity to accom -
modate a high dislocation density and sustain continued deformation 
accumulation without fracture. This hypothesis could explain the longer 
creep life observed in AFA5.

4.4. Matrix composition and creep life 
The stacking fault energy (SFE) of AFA2 and AFA5 may also change 
due to the different nominal contents of Ni, Cr, and Mo. However, 
accurately predicting the change in SFE for these AFA alloys could be 
complex in these multi-element alloys. Dong, et al., used ab initio alloy 
theory to determine the effect of Ni, Mo, and Cr, as well as low and high 
temperatures, on the SFE of austenitic stainless steel [70] . At 1200 K 
(~926 
◦
C), increasing Ni, Cr and Mo lead to a reduction in SFE in Fe- γ 
[70] . Tian et al. demonstrated that the creep properties of low SFE alloys 
were enhanced by promoting dislocation dissociation in Ni-based su -
peralloys [71] . Hence, the favorable change in the SFE in AFA5 can also 
explain the lack of stress localization and delay in creep failure in this 
alloy. Hedworth et al. showed that the dislocation density in the crept 
specimen of α -brass reduced as the SFE increased also promoting 
dislocation cell structures [72] . Current work demonstrates similar 
trends indicating the influence of SFE of the matrix on the creep life of 
the alloy. 
5. Summary and conclusions 
The exceptional tensile creep strength at 900 
◦
C and 50 MPa in dry 
air of two cast alumina-forming austenitic stainless steels are explained 
in this work through advanced morphological, crystallographic, and 
compositional characterization. Alloys AFA2 and AFA5 show 400 h and 
1200 h of creep rupture life, respectively, and the differences are 
explained in terms of composition and the resulting effects upon dislo -
cation creep and diffusion creep mechanisms. The most important 
conclusions are summarized as follows: 
• AFA5 retains a high density of tangled dislocations with curved and 
twisted shapes relative to AFA2. In AFA5, dislocation recombination 
within the Fe- γ matrix is impeded by the influence of fine scale 
carbon-rich regions ( Fig. 6 ) and by the decoration of dislocations by 
carbon and silicon clustering ( Fig. 8 ). There is no clear evidence of 
nano-sized precipitation of NbC, and therefore dislocation pinning 
seems to be caused by a Cottrell atmosphere effect. 
• The depletion of W and B from both Fe- γ matrixes occurs during 
creep reducing the solution strengthening effect. However, the 
depletion in W is stronger in AFA2 due to preferential widespread 
precipitation and coarsening of Laves. In AFA5, the higher nominal C 
and B contents and the reduced nominal Nb and Si contents both 
limit the kinetics of Laves precipitation, even after 1200 h of creep 
deformation. This in turn increases the stability of the solution 
strengthening mechanism in AFA5 by a higher W availability. 
• Secondary hardening also occurs during creep deformation. EBSD 
analysis evidence fine intra-granular B2 and M
23
C
6 
precipitates 
effectively pinning dislocations, especially in AFA5. TEM observa -
tions show that B2 can store internal dislocations, but its main 
contribution is to cause dislocation pile-up at the B2/Fe- γ interface. 
Also, M
23
C
6 
carbides undergo fragmentation and storing of internal 
dislocations. Laves are the least effective secondary precipitate 
acting as a secondary hardening particle due to their rapid coars -
ening in AFA2. 
Additionally, the authors acknowledge that due to limited high 
magnification characterization of multiple intermetallic phases such as 
Laves phase and G-phase in both the alloys, their composition and dis -
tribution density is not fully established in this work.
