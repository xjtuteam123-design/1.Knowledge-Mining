High-Temperature Tensile Behavior of Cu-3Ag-0.5Zr Alloy
in the Solution-Treated Condition
S. Chenna Krishna, N.K. Karthick, Ravi Ranjan Kumar, Abhay K. Jha, Bhanu Pant, and Roy M. Cherian
(Submitted July 4, 2018; in revised form August 24, 2019)
In the present investigation, the high-temperature tensile behavior of Cu-3Ag-0.5Zr alloy in the solution-
treated condition was evaluated in the temperature range of 27-700 /C176C using tensile testing, optical,
scanning electron and transmission electron microscopy. The ultimate tensile strength of the alloy decreased
with an increase in the test temperature. In the temperature range of 27-500 /C176C, the decrease in the yield
strength was marginal, whereas at 600 and 700 /C176C, it reduced by 50% compared to that at room tem-
perature. The total elongation increased with the temperature up to 600 /C176C and decreased thereafter. On
the other hand, uniform elongation decreased with the increase in the temperature. Drastic drop in the
uniform elongation at 500 and 600 /C176C is attributed to formation of cavities. The retention of yield strength
at 400-500 /C176C was due to in situ aging by formation of silver precipitates. The mode of failure at all the
tested temperatures was ductile with dimple rupture.
Keywords Cu-3Ag-0.5Zr alloy, SEM, TEM, tensile behavior
1. Introduction
Copper alloys based on Cu-Ag and Cu-Ag-Zr alloys are
widely used for high heat ﬂux applications such as thrust
chamber of cryogenic engine (Ref 1), conductor in pulsed high-
density magnets (Ref 2), contact wire for high-speed trains (Ref
3), continuous casting molds (Ref 4) due to combination of
high conductivity and strength. Cu-Ag is a precipitation-
hardenable alloy strengthened with silver precipitates. Never-
theless, discontinuous precipitation was reported in these alloys
and zirconium was added to aid continuous precipitation,
impart dispersion strengthening and to avoid grain growth at
high temperature (Ref 5). The phases in solution-treated Cu-
3Ag-0.5Zr alloy were reported as zirconium rich and Cu-Ag-Zr
intermetallic phase. After aging, ﬁne nano-sized silver precip-
itates uniformly form in the copper matrix (Ref 1). The effect of
cold and cryo-rolling and aging on the mechanical properties
and microstructure was studied in our earlier work (Ref 6, 7).
The envisaged temperature during the service for the present
alloy is about 500 /C176C which makes it essential to study the
high-temperature tensile behavior. However, there are no
systematic works on the high-temperature tensile behavior of
the alloy in the solution-treated condition. In the present study,
the tensile properties were evaluated in the temperature range of
27-700 /C176C with an emphasis to understand the tensile behavior.
2. Material and Methods
The Cu-3Ag-0.5Zr alloy used in the present study was
melted in a vacuum induction melting furnace using pure
elements as raw materials in a graphite crucible. The molten
metal was poured into mild steel mold coated with graphite
powder. The cast ingot was homogenized at 950 /C176C for 5 h
followed by air cooling. The homogenized ingot was forged
and hot rolled in the temperature range of 850-950 /C176C. The hot
rolled plate was solution treated at 935 /C176C for 0.5 h and water
quenched, followed by tensile testing in the temperature range
of 27-700 /C176C.
The tensile testing was performed under constant cross-head
speed in electro-mechanically operated Zwick Roell Z250
universal testing machine. The machine is equipped with
swivel-type three-zone furnace for high-temperature testing.
Ambient temperature tests were conducted as per ASTM E8
standard at nominal strain rate of 0.001 s /C01. High-temperature
tests were conducted as per ASTM E21 in air at a nominal
strain rate of 0.001 s /C01. Three specimens were tested at each
test temperature. The sketch of the high-temperature tensile
specimen is shown in Fig. 1. Load-extension curves were
obtained with the aid of a 25 mm gauge length extensometer,
from which the engineering stress–strain curve was plotted. The
temperature was controlled with a closed-loop control system
based on thermocouple output, and temperature was maintained
within ± 2 /C176C of the set-point temperature. Specimen was
soaked at speciﬁc test temperature for 20 min prior to start of
the test to achieve uniformity.
The microstructure at the fracture tip and base metal was
investigated using optical microscopy. Samples were prepared
through conventional mechanical polishing using emery papers,
alumina and diamond paste. Polished samples were etched with
100 ml H 2O+8m lH 2SO4 + 2gm K 2Cr2O7 + 4 drops of HCl
and examined under optical metallurgical microscope. Samples
for electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) were prepared by
electro-polishing with a solution of 80 ml H 3PO4 +2 0 H 2O
using a current of 0.4-1 A and voltage of 12 V at /C05 /C176C. The
fracture surfaces of the tensile tested specimens were examined

3. Results
3.1 Microstructure
The microstructure of the solution-treated Cu-3Ag-0.5Zr
alloy comprises of equiaxed grains with aligned dark particles
as shown in Fig. 2. These dark particles were reported as Cu-
Ag-Zr intermetallics with chemical composition of Cu 4AgZr
(Ref 1, 8). The average grain size including the annealing twins
was measured to be 123 ± 8 lm.
3.2 Tensile Properties
The typical engineering stress–strain curves of the alloy in
the temperature range of 27-700 /C176C are shown in Fig. 3. The
ultimate tensile strength has progressively decreased with the
increase in the temperature. The strain to failure has increased
with the test temperature up to 600 /C176C and decreased at 700 /C176C.
However, the portion of uniform elongation decreased with the
increase in temperature and it was the lowest at 600 and
700 /C176C. The average tensile properties are shown in Fig. 4. The
ultimate tensile strength decreased with an increase in temper-
ature from 290 MPa at 27 /C176C to 50 MPa at 700 /C176C. The total
elongation showed a minor decrease at 300 /C176C and increased
thereafter with a maximum of 70% at 600 /C176C followed by a
drop at 700 /C176C. The uniform elongation decreased with the
temperature, with a minimum of 1.5% at 700 /C176C. A minor
decrease in yield strength was observed at 300 /C176C compared to
27 /C176C followed by an increase at 400 and 500 /C176C. Signiﬁcant
reductions in the yield strength was observed at 600 and 700 /C176C
with a value of 72 and 49 MPa, respectively.

3.4 Fracture Surface
The fracture surfaces of the tensile tested specimens are
shown in Fig. 6. The mode of fracture was dimple rupture
caused by coalescence of the micro-voids. Nevertheless, the
morphology of the dimples and voids varied with the test
temperature. Room temperature tested sample showed dimples
with coarse voids as shown in Fig. 6(a). At 300 /C176C, the fracture
surface consists of dimples with very ﬁne voids. The size of the
dimples was relatively uniform, and particles were present in
few dimples, indicating the nucleation of voids at the second-
phase particles (Fig. 6b). With an increase in the temperature,
the size of the dimples and voids increased as shown in
Fig. 6(c) and (d) due to the decrease in nucleation rate and
which makes micro-void coalescence difﬁcult (Ref 9). How-
ever, the voids that nucleate grow at faster rate. This may be the
reason for the decrease in number of dimples and increase in
the size of the dimples. At 600 and 700 /C176C, the surface got
oxidized yet the gross features like voids are visible as shown
in Fig. 6(e) and (f).
4. Discussion
The tensile behavior of the metals and alloys depends on the
temperature and strain rate. As a general trend, strength
decreases and ductility increases with an increase in the test
temperature (Ref 10). Microstructure, second-phase particles
and chemical composition dictate the tensile behavior for a
given temperature and strain rate. Addition of solid solution
strengthening elements to pure metal leads to the increase in the
tensile strength and decrease in ductility both at ambient and
high temperature. The presence of second-phase particles like
oxides and nitrides is reported to improve the high-temperature
strength at the cost of ductility (Ref 11, 12). Nevertheless, the
magnitude of the change in the properties depends on the size,
distribution and shape of second-phase particles.
In precipitation-hardenable alloys, precipitates inﬂuence the
elevated temperature properties as reported in the literature.
Nagarjuna et al. (Ref 13) reported an increase in ultimate tensile
strength and yield strength in the temperature range of 250-
450 /C176C for Cu-1.5Ti alloy which was mainly attributed to
in-situ aging by formation of ﬁne Cu 4Ti precipitates. A similar
increase in tensile strength was reported in Cu-0.6Cr-0.04Ti-
0.03Zr (wt.%) at 500 and 600 /C176C for different combinations of
strain rate and temperature due to formation of ﬁne Cr
precipitates (Ref 14). In addition to these, certain copper alloys
(Cu-Cr and Cu-Ni-Si) were known to suffer dynamic embrit-
tlement at intermediate temperature due to segregation of
elemental sulfur at the grain boundaries (Ref 15-17).
The trend of strength and ductility as a function of
temperature for solution-treated Cu-3Ag-0.5Zr alloy (Fig. 4)
was similar to that reported in Cu-1.5Ti, Cu-Cr-Zr-Ti and Cu-
Cr-Zr alloy (Ref 13, 14, 18). Minor drop in yield strength at
temperature of 300 /C176C compared to that of 27 /C176C is mainly

Figure 7 shows the TEM micrographs of the samples tested

The drop in YS at 700 /C176C is mainly attributed to coarsening of
the precipitates (Fig. 7b). Similar trend was reported in Cu-
1.5Ti alloy as a function of temperature, and the increase in YS
up to 450 /C176C was attributed to shearing of Cu 4Ti precipitates by
dislocations. Beyond 450 /C176C, the drop in strength was
attributed to bowing of dislocations around the precipitates
(Ref 13).
Strengthening due to particle dispersions at elevated tem-
perature will depend on the stress necessary for operation of
Orowan mechanism (dislocation bending) or particle cutting
mechanism (dislocation shearing precipitate). At elevated
temperature (above 0.5 Tm), the dislocations can bypass the
precipitate by a climb motion, and this can occur at small
opposing forces from the precipitate compared to that required
for shearing or the Orowan mechanism (Ref 19). The variation
in the YS at 400-500 and 600-700 /C176C is mainly due to change
in the interaction of dislocations with the precipitates. At 400-
500 /C176C, the dislocations tend to shear the precipitates, whereas
in the case of 600-700 /C176C, due to the coarsening of the
precipitates, the dislocations tend to bow to over the precip-
itates (Ref 13). The stress required for dislocations to shear the
precipitates is higher than that required for bending, and same
trend is evident from Fig. 4.

Microstructure at the fracture tip of specimens tested in the
temperature of 300-500 /C176C exhibits more deformation within the
grains which eventually resulted in elongated grains. There were
no cavities or cracks as shown in Fig. 5(b), (c) and (d). At higher
temperature 600-700/C176C, dynamically recrystallized grains formed
across the grain boundaries, and size of the recrystallized grains
was higher at 700 /C176C. Failure at 600 and 700 /C176C occurred by
cavitation as shown in Fig. 5(e) and (f). The cavities formed
preferentially near the fracture tip, and no cavities were observed
away from the fracture tip. The mechanism for formation of
cavities in metals and alloys is diffusion of vacancies and grain
boundary sliding. One of the common failure modes in low strain
rate tensile tests elevated temperature is grain boundary sliding
which produces cavitation and wedge-type cracks (Ref 20).
Particles and inclusions at the grain boundaries aid in cavitation
and crack linkage which lead to fracture. Fracture occurred due to
coalescence of the micro-voids and their growth. The mode of
failure is ductile with characteristic dimple rupture type of failure
(Fig. 6). Some of the dimples showed second-phase particles
which indicate that nucleation occurred at these particles.
5. Conclusions
Based on the limited high-temperature tensile tests con-
ducted on Cu-3Ag-0.5Zr alloy, the following conclusions are
drawn:
The ultimate tensile strength of the alloy showed decreasing
trend with temperature, and the UTS has decreased from
290 MPa at 27 /C176C to 50 MPa at 700 /C176C. The yield strength was
nearly constant up to 500 /C176C with a value of 100 MPa and
decreased thereafter to 70 and 50 MPa at 600 and 700 /C176C,
respectively. The retention of nearly constant yield strength up to
500 /C176C is due to in situ aging by formation of silver precipitates.
The drop in yield strength beyond 500 /C176C is due to coarsening
and dissolution of the silver precipitates. The total elongation
decreased up to 400 /C176C and was lower than at 27 /C176C. Higher
total elongations at 600 and 700 /C176C is mainly attributed to
initiation of dynamic recrystallization. The uniform elongation
decreased with the temperature, with a minimum of 1.5% at
700 /C176C. The drastic drop in uniform elongation at 600 and
700 /C176C is attributed to the formation of cavities. The mode of
failure is ductile mode of fracture due to coalescence of micro-
voids which nucleated at the second-phase particles.
