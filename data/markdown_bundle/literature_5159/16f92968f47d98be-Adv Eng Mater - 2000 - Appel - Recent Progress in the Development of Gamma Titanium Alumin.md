1. Introduction
In recent years considerable efforts have been made in the
development of c-TiAl-base alloys for use in high tempera-
ture structural application, where low density and good high
temperature strength and Youngs modulus retention are of
major concern.
[1–3] Owing chiefly to these properties titanium
aluminides provide a significant potential for innovative
applications in advanced energy conversion systems, in that
they may be used to replace the heavier nickel-base superal-
loys at moderately high temperatures of about 700 C. The
most important pay-off areas in the automotive and aero
industry have been assessed in a recent conference proceed-
ings.
[2] Fundamental to such applications, almost by defini-
tion, is a good balance of mechanical properties over a wide
temperature range. This includes damage tolerance, ductility,
fracture toughness, fatigue strength, and creep resistance. In
most of these properties the titanium aluminides are inferior
to the nickel-base superalloys, even if the comparison is made
on a strength to weight basis. This impedes their practical use
and is the driving force for the current research and develop-
ment aimed towards improving the mechanical properties of
the titanium aluminides. As the desired properties often lead
to conflicting alloy design concepts the balance of these prop-
erties has to be carefully chosen. This needs a detailed under-

Intermetallic titanium aluminides offer an attractive combination of low
density and good oxidation and ignition resistance with unique mechani-
cal properties. These involve high strength and elastic stiffness with excellent high temperature reten-
tion. Thus, they are one of the few classes of emerging materials that have the potential to be used in
demanding high-temperature structural applications whenever specific strength and stiffness are of
major concern. However, in order to effectively replace the heavier nickel-base superalloys currently in
use, titanium aluminides must combine a wide range of mechanical property capabilities. Advanced al-
loy designs are tailored for strength, toughness, creep resistance, and environmental stability. These
concerns are addressed in the present paper through global commentary on the physical metallurgy
and associated processing technologies of c-TiAl-base alloys. Particular emphasis is paid on recent de-
velopments of TiAl alloys with enhanced high-temperature capability.

2. Experimental Methods
In recent years much emphasis has been placed on the me-
chanical characterization of titanium aluminide alloys. The
experiments described in this paper were designed in order
to identify the microprocesses of strength, plasticity and frac-
ture. The alloys investigated were characterized in terms of
tensile strength, ductility, creep strength, and fracture tough-
ness as has been described elsewhere.
[4–7] The factors control-
ling the dislocation mobility were investigated over a wide
temperature range by determining temperature and strain
rate sensitivities.
[5] Since many aspects of deformation and
fracture are intimately linked to defect configurations at the
atomic level, standard techniques of metallography are often
inadequate to provide the necessary information. Thus, con-
ventional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging
techniques have been coupled with high resolution electron
microscopy (HREM) to characterize the relevant processes.[8]

4. Deformation Behaviour
Titanium aluminides are relatively brittle materials, exhib-
iting little plasticity at ambient temperatures. Typical for such
deformation behavior is that the gliding dislocations are
either too low in density or too immobile to allow the speci-
men to match the superimposed strain rate. At elevated tem-
peratures titanium aluminides suffer from insufficient creep
resistance and structural changes. Such behavior is often as-
sociated with dislocation climb and the operation of diffusion
assisted dislocation sources.
[3] Thus, the factors governing the
multiplication and mobility of the dislocations might be im-
portant in several different ways and will now be considered.

5. Mechanical Properties
For engineering applications titanium aluminides must
possess a good balance of various mechanical properties. In
order to limit the scope of this review, consideration will only
be given to strength, ductility, creep resistance and toughness.
5.1. Microstructures, Yield Strength, and Ductility
The deformation and fracture characteristics of titanium
aluminide alloys are sensitively dependent on the composi-
tion, the partitioning of ternary and quaternary elements and
the microstructure.
[1,2,15,36] While much work has been done
in this field during the past decade our knowledge has not
been progressed far beyond the following rules. In general, a
reduction of the Al content increases the strength level, but
tends to reduce ductility and oxidation resistance. The addi-
tion of ternary elements such as Cr, Mn, or V up to amounts
of about 2 at.-% for each is known to increase the ductility of
two-phase alloys,
[37] for reasons that are not altogether clear.
In two-phase alloys, boron additions are often used for refin-
ing and stabilizing the microstructure.[38,39]
Undoubtedly, the nature and scale of the microstructure
plays an important role in the strengthening of two-phase al-
loys. As with many other materials the flow stress decreases
with increasing grain size and, thus, has been described in
terms of a Hall–Petch relation. Apparently, the effectiveness
of grain boundary strengthening in two-phase TiAl alloys is
much enhanced, which is reflected by relatively large Hall–
Petch constants of K
y = 1.0 to 4 MPa Hm[3,36] and may in part
be attributed to the difficulty of propagating slip across the
various types of internal boundaries under conditions of re-
stricted slip and cross slip.
[3] Significant progress has been
achieved in describing the strength of fully-lamellar micro-
structures in terms of dislocation pile-up models, which indi-
cate that these materials are mainly strengthened by the la-
mellar interfaces.
[40] However, there appears to be no single
theoretical model which satisfactorily accounts for the large
body of experimental observations on more complex micro-
structures. Particular problems arise for duplex structures
which are often rich in detail encompassing different length
parameters being relevant for the dislocation mean free path.
More details on this subject is provided in the literature.
[3,40–42]
Alloys with a fully-lamellar microstructure are beneficial
for fracture toughness and high temperature strength but suf-
fer from poor ductility at low and ambient temperatures. Du-
plex structures are conducive to tensile ductility but exhibit
poor high temperature strength and creep resistance. Because
of these potential trade-offs between the mechanical proper-
ties, microstructural conditions that are advantageous for
toughness and ductility might not be beneficial for other
mechanical properties. In view of the recent conference pro-
ceedings
[2] it might be anticipated that a fine-grained fully-
transformed lamellar structure has the best balance of
strength, ductility, toughness and creep resistance that meets
many demands for engineering applications. There are excel-
lent reviews
[1,2,36] on the correlation between microstructure
and mechanical properties and the reader is referred to these
articles for further details and references.
Titanium aluminides are being considered to replace the
more dense nickel-base or iron-base superalloys over certain
ranges of temperature and stress. Thus, their mechanical
properties have to be assessed against the high standard set
by the superalloys. At an intended service temperature of
700 C strength and creep resistance of the titanium alumi-
nides are inferior to those shown by the superalloys, even if
density compensated data are compared. This is probably a
direct consequence of thermally activated dislocation climb
processes described in the previous section, which make the
mechanical behavior of the titanium aluminides strongly rate
dependent. Thus, the strength properties degrade at low
strain rates such as those which occur under creep conditions.
Thus, now that the basic problems of the titanium aluminides
have been solved, perhaps the greatest interest is now fo-
cused on how to improve their high temperature capability.

5.2. Solid Solution Effects due to Niobium Additions
In an attempt to enhance the strength of titanium alumi-
nides significant efforts have been made in order to establish
solid solution hardening. However, the effects of ternary and
quaternary elements are not always clear as microalloying
may lead to changes of phase equilibria and microstructures.
In this respect, the most reliable information was obtained
from investigations of polysynthetically twinned crystals
(PST) as these materials always have a fully-lamellar micro-

these elements to binary PST crystals.

cently, it has been shown that a significant strengthening effect
can be achieved on polycrystalline alloys when Nb is added
with an amount of 5–10 at.-% to polycrystalline two-phase al-
loys.
[29,37,44] The strengthening effect was investigated by ana-

5.3. Precipitation Hardening due to Carbon Additions
Appreciable improvements in high temperature strength
and creep resistance have also been achieved by carbon addi-
tions.
[48,49] A systematic series of carbon doped materials with
the base line composition Ti–48.5 Al–(0.02–0.4) C were sub-

and quenching resulted in a carbon solid solution, whereas
Ti
3AlC precipitates of perovskite type were formed by subse-
quent ageing at 1050 K. The precipitates are elongated along
the c-axis of the c matrix and exhibit significant coherency
stresses due to their mismatch with the c matrix.
[48] These
structural features lead to a strong glide resistance for all
types and characters of dislocations. Figure 13 shows the de-
pendence of the flow stress on the carbon concentration c for
these two series of materials. For the materials containing per-

On contrary, the flow stress is practically independent of the
carbon concentration when carbon is in solution. This shows
that a carbon solid solution is a much less effective strength-
ening agent than a dispersion of perovskite precipitates. The
different deformation behavior is also manifested in the acti-
vation volume. At room temperature the homogenized and
quenched materials exhibit relatively large values of 1/ V,
which slightly increase with c. It is therefore concluded that
soluted carbon atoms act as weak glide obstacles, which ap-
parently can easily be overcome with the aid of thermal acti-
vation. In contrast, the reciprocal activation volume de-
creased with c, when the material was aged and the carbon
was present as Ti
3AlC precipitates. Figure 13a also includes
the values of carbon doped materials which were tested after
hot isostatic pressing (HIP) followed by slow cooling. After
this treatment carbon is present as a coarse dispersion of the
H-phase Ti
2AlC.[48] These particles form athermal glide
obstacles, which is clearly indicated by the strong decrease of
1/V with c. However, the coarse dispersion of this phase
seems to be less effective in strengthening the material.

5.4. Creep Properties
Design concepts for advanced energy conversion systems
and aeroengines require low creep deformation of the compo-
nents in order to ensure that the clearance between the rotat-
ing parts and their casings can be kept small. Thus, the mate-
rials used should have a good creep resistance, which must
be present in the earlier stages of deformation. Creep strength
and rupture life are therefore critical issues regarding the
high temperature applications of c-base titanium aluminides.
This problem became evident during the past five years and
has attracted many research activities. The creep properties
were found to depend on both alloy composition and micro-
structure
[50–59] and considerable improvements have been

Beddoes et al. [60] As with many other materials the creep
properties are strongly dependent on test parameters, which
is manifested by the large variation of the steady-state creep
stress exponent with stress and temperature. Much emphasis
has been placed on describing the experimental data in terms
of the Dorn equation. A brief review of the recent advances
has been given by Parthasarathy et al.
[61] Most creep studies

high strain rates and almost short-term tests. Thus, not
surprisingly, the observed mechanisms are not so different

5.5. Fracture Toughness
Like other intermetallics titanium aluminides suffer from
brittleness at low and ambient temperatures, which makes
processing difficult and hinders practical use. The fracture
behavior is known to be very sensitive to microstruc-
ture.
[1,67,68] Intergranular fracture and cleavage are the domi-
nant fracture mechanisms in the duplex microstructures,
while interfacial delamination, translamellar fracture and de-
cohesion of lamellar colonies are the most important pro-
cesses in lamellar alloys. This intrinsic brittleness persists up
to temperatures of about 700 C and has been attributed to a
combined effect of low cohesion strength, insufficient defor-
mation modes which can simultaneously operate at a given
stress, and low dislocation mobility. For toughening the mate-
rial a lamellar microstructure is beneficial, because shear liga-
ment bridging, crack deflection, and microcrack shielding oc-
cur.
[67] Fully-lamellar microstructures with randomized
colony orientations exhibit relatively large toughnesses of KQ
= 25–30 MPa Hm. For a more comprehensive discussion of
the correlation between fracture toughness and microstruc-
ture the reader is referred to a recent review.
[67]

from toughening mechanisms related to the lamellar mor-
phology. However, the temperature dependence of the frac-
ture toughness of the near gamma materials suggests that
crack tip plasticity can also lead to appreciable toughening, a
topic, which has received far less attention. In this context
TEM observations of crack tips have been performed on two-
phase titanium aluminides with different microstructures.
[18]
Figure 21 demonstrates a high-resolution image of a crack,
which propagated in a thin foil of a lamellar alloy. The crack
followed {111}c planes which indicates that c-TiAl is prone to
cleavage fracture on these planes. This finding is consistent
with the earlier predictions of Yoo and Fu,
[69] who calculated
the ideal cleavage energies for different crystallographic
planes of c-TiAl. Crack propagation across the lamellae is
characterized by various interactions of the crack tip with la-
mellar boundaries, involving crack deflection, and crack tip
immobilization so that a much more tortuous crack path is
traversed.
[18] Ahead of the crack tip a plastic zone is formed,
which is often extended over several lamellae and consists of
deformation twins and dislocations. Thus, the shape of the
plastic zone is strongly determined by the crystallography of
these slip elements. In the high resolution image shown in
Figure 21 the crack tip plasticity apparently becomes evident
by the two dislocations which are arranged in a dipole config-
uration. Stable crack growth requires the plastic zone to keep
with the cleavage crack, which is difficult if the mobility and
multiplication rate of the dislocations are low. The mecha-
nisms governing the dislocation mobility in the plastic zone
at room temperature were found to be quite similar to those
in bulk material, namely lattice friction, localized pinning,
and jog dragging. Due to this high glide resistance the dislo-
cations may easily be outrun by the crack. In this respect de-
formation twinning might be very effective as twins can rap-
idly propagate. Crack tip shielding by twinning has been
recognized
[25] by HREM of crack tips.
From the discussion in Section 5.2 it can be suspected that
the high glide resistance of the dislocations will be drastically
reduced at elevated temperatures due to thermal activation
and the operation of diffusion assisted climb processes. Un-
like the situation at room temperature, the glide processes
can easily spread within the plastic zone so that the con-
straints due to the local crystallography and slip geometry
are less restrictive. It is therefore expected that the problems
associated with the lack of independent slip systems (which
can simultaneously operate at a given stress) can in part be
compensated so that the crack tips are effectively shielded.
Thus, the fracture mechanism changes from cleavage at low
temperatures to energy absorbing ductile forms at elevated
temperatures.
[67]
In view of the observed cleavage fracture it is suspected
that unfavorably oriented c grains or lamellae may provide
easy crack paths. Once nucleated on {111} c planes, the cracks
are subjected to high tensile stresses and can rapidly grow to
a critical length. It is speculated that large colonies with la-

6. Processing
As already discussed in the previous section, microstruc-
tural optimization and control of c-TiAl alloys is of particular
concern with respect to balanced mechanical properties.
Thus, wrought processing is beneficial because it allows a
broad range of tailored microstructures to be developed and,
in particular, offers the possibility of obtaining lamellar mi-
crostructures with both refined colony sizes and lamellar
spacings. As mentioned above this microstructural form has
the best potential to achieve an attractive and balanced prop-
erty profile.
[1,2,36,70,71] For the refinement of colony sizes there
exist different possibilities. Lamellar colonies are formed
from a grains when the material is cooled from the a field
which for engineering alloys lies above ~1300 C. The rapid
grain growth in this temperature range thus leads to coarse
colonies. Control of the a grain size can be achieved by hot
working directly in the a field. However, for this type of pro-
cessing route further hot working steps, e.g., near-net shaping
of parts by closed-die forging are not possible because the re-
fined lamellar microstructure would be again destroyed by
dynamical recrystallization. If hot working is carried out in
the (a + c)o r( a
2 + c) field multiple hot working steps can be
utilized. In this case, the lamellar microstructure is formed by
a final heat treatment. The a grain size can then be controlled
by the presence of second phases, e.g., borides.
For the work presented here ingots were used, which had
been produced by double or triple VAR (vacuum arc remelt-
ing) or by induction skull melting. The ingot production was
found to be a critical processing step, especially if ingots of
large diameters are needed. One problem are macroscopic
pores which could not be completely closed by hot working
and can even lead to cracking of the material on forging. With
respect to hot working, further the strong texture of ingot ma-
terial has to be regarded, which varies over the cross-section
of the ingot and can give rise to anisotropic flow of the mate-
rial on hot working. The most severe problem, however, con-
nected with ingot production are macro- and microsegrega-
tions of Al because the mechanical properties sensitively
depend on the Al content. Owing to the Ti–Al phase diagram
engineering alloys might pass through two peritectic reac-
tions on solidification and therefore the segregation of Al can
hardly be avoided in the ingot material. In Figure 23 the den-
dritic morphology of a 150 kg ingot of composition Ti–45 Al–
10 Nb ingot is demonstrated.
[72] Elemental energy dispersive
X-ray (EDX) mapping shows that the interdendritic regions
are rich in Al and depleted in Nb as it is expected from the in-
vestigations of the ternary Ti–Al–Nb phase diagram by
Zdziobek et al.
[72] for this composition. By quantitative EDX
analysis taken from this material (one line scan is presented
in Fig. 23) concentrations of about 44 at.-% Al and 10 at.-%
Nb were determined in the dendrites whereas in interdendri-
tic regions the Al and Nb concentrations amounted to 49 at.-
% Al and 7.5 at.-% Nb, respectively. The concentration varies
on a scale of about 1 mm and thus extended coarse-grained
single-phase c regions occur,
[73] which are certainly detrimen-
tal for the mechanical properties.
For ingot breakdown isothermal forging,[74–77] canned non-
isothermal forging, [78,79] and canned hot extrusion [80,81] have
been applied and investigated in quite a number of studies.
In general, c alloys exhibit limited hot ductility and a strong
dependence of the flow stress on strain rate and temperature.
As has been discussed in a recent review by Semiatin et al.
[82]
a critical tensile stress in the workpiece should not be ex-
ceeded for successful crack-free forging. For cast materials
this critical stress varies between 200 and 500 MPa depending
on alloy composition and microstructure. Thus, forging
should be performed at moderate strain rates <10
–1 s–1 and
under isothermal or near-isothermal conditions which can be
realized by canning the billets. Higher strain rates are possi-
ble for canned hot extrusion which usually is carried out at
temperatures above 1250 C.

8. Conclusions
Extensive progress and improvements in the physical me-
tallurgy of titanium alumindes have been made in the last
five years. Alloy chemistry and processing technologies of
a
2(Ti3Al) + c-(TiAl) alloys have been developed to such an
extent that semifinished products and structural parts can be
fabricated with a promising balance of mechanical properties.
In view of the anticipated structural and mechanical require-
ments advanced developments of two-phase alloys base on
the implementation of solid solution and precipitation hard-
ening mechanisms. From the engineering view-point, the
challenge is to establish these mechanisms without compro-
mising other desirable properties. This needs a detailed un-
derstanding of the micromechanisms of deformation and
fracture which limit the desired mechanical properties.
