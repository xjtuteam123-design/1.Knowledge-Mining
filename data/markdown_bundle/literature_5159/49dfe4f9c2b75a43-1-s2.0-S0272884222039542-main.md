ABSTRACT 
TiAl alloys are ideal high-temperature structural materials for aerospace applications. However, their ductility, 
bending strength and machinability are poor at room temperature, thus severely limiting their practical appli -
cation. In this study, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) were used as additives, and Ti – 48Al – 2Cr – 8Nb was used as the 
original powder to prepare CNTs/TiAl alloys by employing spark plasma sintering. The effects of different CNT 
contents on the microstructure, hardness and bending strength of CNTs/TiAl alloys were investigated. The results 
indicated that CNTs reacted with the matrix to generate granular TiC and Ti
2
AlC reinforced phases stacked into 
bands at the grain boundaries, limiting grain growth. The Ti
2
AlC phase content increased with increasing CNT 
content. When the contents of the TiC and Ti
2
AlC phases were high, agglomeration occurred, leading to defects 
such as pits and cracks on the surface, which explains the weakened mechanical properties of the CNTs/TiAl 
alloys at room temperature. When the CNT content was 0.4 wt%, the average grain size was approximately 32 
μ m under the effects of fine grain strengthening and second phase strengthening. Moreover, the mechanical 
properties of the composite were the best at room temperature. The Vickers hardness and bending strength at 
room temperature were 377 HV and 879 MPa, respectively, and the compressive strength and strain were 2554 
MPa and 31.86%, respectively. The fracture mode was mainly transgranular. 
1. Introduction 
TiAl alloys are regarded as a new generation of aerospace structural 
materials which can replace high-temperature Ni-based superalloys 
owing to their low density, high strength, and good thermal conductivity 
[ 1 – 3 ]. TiAl alloys have been successfully applied in medicine, commu -
nication, aerospace, and shipping [ 4 , 5 ]. However, their preparation and 
processing are severely restricted because of their unsatisfactory 
bending strength and additivity at room temperature. The mechanical 
properties of TiAl alloys depend primarily on their composition and 
microstructure [ 6 ]. Typically, four room-temperature TiAl alloy struc -
tures can be obtained after heat treatment: the near gamma structure 
(NG) which is composed of equiaxed γ crystals and a few α
2 
crystals 
distributed at γ crystal boundaries, exhibiting low fracture toughness 
and poor high-temperature performance [ 7 ]; a duplex structure with 
equiaxed γ crystals and lamellar structure (DP) which has high strength 
and plasticity, but low fracture toughness [ 8 ]; a fully lamellar structure 
(FL) which has good fracture toughness and high-temperature proper -
ties, but low strength and plasticity owing to the coarse grain size [ 9 ]; 
and a near lamellar structure (NL) which has comprehensive equilib -
rium mechanical properties, but poor creep properties affected by 
equiaxed γ crystals [ 10 ]. 
The microstructure of TiAl alloys can be adjusted and optimised by 
heat treatment which is a low-cost and simple method to improve its 
mechanical performance [ 11 , 12 ]. The mechanical alloying (MA) 
method of ball-milling TiAl alloy powders can refine the powder size and 
improve the final densification [ 13 , 14 ]. Moreover, the addition of het -
erogeneous elements can improve the mechanical performance of TiAl 
alloys by reducing the lamellar spacing, refining the grain size, and 
densifying the microstructure, thereby playing a role in solution 
strengthening. The added elements can be divided into six categories 
according to their effects on the properties of the TiAl alloy, as listed in 
Table 1 . 
Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are tiny hollow tubular materials 
comprised of several to dozens of hexagonal carbon grids with fixed 
interlamellar spacing. The density of CNTs is only 1/6 – 1/7 that of steel, 
while its tensile strength is 100 times higher than that of steel. There -
fore, CNTs are considered ideal reinforcers for improving the

2. Experimental details 
2.1. Powder preparation 
Fig. 1 shows the preparation process for the CNTs/TiAl powder 
mixture. The pre-alloyed Ti – 48Al – 2Cr – 8Nb powders with a particle size 
of 100 – 150 μ m were prepared using plasma rotating electrode tech -
nology, and the multi-walled CNTs (Shenzhen Nanogang Co. LTD) were 
selected with a length of 3 – 5 μ m and a diameter of 50 nm. Detailed 
parameters are listed in Table 2 . The pre-alloyed powders were placed in 
a planetary ball mill (QM-3SP2, China) with different CNT contents after 
ultrasonication for 30 min. The initial powder mixtures were divided 
into four groups based on the CNT content with weight percentages of 0, 
0.4, 0.7, and 1.0 wt%. Subsequently, WC grinding balls were placed in 
the tank at a mass ratio of 5:1 to the powder mixtures. The ball-milling 
process lasted for 12 h at a rotation speed of 250 rpm. After drying in a 
vacuum drying oven (DZF-1, China) at 120 
◦
C and sifting through a 100 
mesh sieve, the powder mixtures of CNTs/TiAl alloys was prepared. 
2.2. Sintering process 
The mixed powders were sintered in a graphite mould using SPS. The 
graphite mould comprised an inner lining (internal diameter, 20.4 mm; 
external diameter, 35 mm), and an outer lining (internal diameter, 35 
mm; external diameter, 65 mm) with a wrapped high-temperature 
insulating felt ( Fig. 2 ). A temperature-measuring hole with a diameter 
of 2 mm and a depth of 12 mm was located in the middle of the outer 
lining. The powder mixtures were pressurised at 30 MPa for 3 min. An 
infrared laser tester was employed to monitor the sintering temperature. 
The powders were heated to 1000 
◦
C at a rate of 100 
◦
C/min, maintained 
for 10 min, and then cooled to room temperature. Detailed parameters 
are listed in Table 3 .

3. Results and discussion 
Fig. 3 shows the SEM images of the CNTs before and after ultra -
sonication. The agglomerated CNTs with a diameter of approximately 
50 μ m were evident, exhibiting irregular and spherical shapes before 
ultrasonication. In the magnified image of Fig. 3 (a), the tubular powder 
was intertwined and agglomerated, resulting in an increase in the 
powder particle size to 3 – 5 μ m. After ultrasonication, the aggregates of 
CNTs in Fig. 3 (b) were untangled, whereas the agglomeration phe -
nomenon was significantly weakened.

Fig. 6 shows the surface morphologies of the CNTs/TiAl alloys with 
different CNT contents. When CNTs were not added, no significant de -
fects were observed on the surface, indicating excellent surface 
compactness, as shown in Fig. 6 (a). However, with the addition of CNTs, 
the surface quality of the CNTs/TiAl alloys deteriorated. Pits appeared 
on the surface, and extending cracks were also observed ( Fig. 6 (b) and 
(c) ). When CNTs were added at 1.0 wt%, the pit defects continued to 
increase. Also, particles were observed in the pits, as shown in the 
magnified image in Fig. 6 (d). 
To determine the composition of the pits in Fig. 6 , EDS spot scanning 
was performed, and the results are shown in Fig. 7 . The carbon con -
centration at point P1 was as high as 96.44%, indicating that the ma -
terial in the pits contained unreacted CNTs. P2 was mainly composed of 
C and Ti with mass fractions of 17.38 and 69.32%, respectively, indi -
cating that the white granular material in the pit was TiC generated by 
the reaction in Eq. (1) . P3 was mainly comprised of C, Ti, and Al, with 
mass fractions of 7.58, 64.31, and 18.94%, respectively. The results 
indicated that the granular material at the grain boundary on the 
corrosion surface of the CNTs/TiAl alloys was Ti
2
AlC generated by Eqs. 
(1) and (2) , which were stacked and distributed in the lamellar structure 
at the grain boundary interface.

generated by the reaction increased, leading to agglomeration. More -

Fig. 10 shows the Vickers hardness curves of the CNTs/TiAl alloys. As 
can be observed, the Vickers hardness of CNTs/TiAl alloys initially 
increased and then decreased with increasing CNT content. In the 
absence of CNTs, the Vickers hardness of the material was 364 HV. At 
0.4 wt% CNT content, a certain amount of Ti
2
AlC phase and a small 
amount of TiC phase were generated, which strengthened the alloy and 
increased the Vickers hardness value to 377 HV. When the CNT content 
was 0.7 wt%, the contents of TiC and Ti
2
AlC phases further increased, 
and the Vickers hardness value reached a maximum of 384 HV. When 
the content of CNTs was 1.0 wt%, the content of the remaining CNTs, 
TiC, and Ti
2
AlC phases generated by the reaction were large and un -
evenly dispersed, resulting in pits, cracks, and other defects in several 
areas of the surface, leading to a sudden drop in Vickers hardness. 
Fig. 11 shows the bending strength of the CNTs/TiAl alloys at room 
temperature. The bending strength initially increased and then 
decreased with increasing CNT content. When the CNT content was 0.4 
wt%, the bending strength was 879 MPa, which is 7.1% higher than that 
without CNTs. As shown in Fig. 8 (b), the microstructure of the CNT/TiAl 
alloys shows blocky morphology, and the striped Ti
2
AlC phase appeared 
at the grain interface. The striped Ti
2
AlC phase limited grain growth and 
played a role in fine-grain strengthening. Furthermore, TiC and Ti
2
AlC 
phases generated by the reactions can also play a role in the second- 
phase strengthening as ceramic strengthening phases, which improved 
the bending strength of the CNTs/TiAl alloys. When the TiC and Ti
2
AlC 
phases were generated, the stress concentration and residual stress were 
also generated around the grains, which were used as the barrier to stop 
the crack expansion and make it bend and deflect. Thus, the crack en -
ergy was consumed, and the bending strength of the CNTs/TiAl alloys 
was improved. When the CNT content was 0.7 wt%, the TiC and Ti
2
AlC 
phase agglomerated, resulting in pits and cracks on the surface and a 
decreased bending strength. 
Fig. 12 shows the fracture morphologies of the CNTs/TiAl alloys. It is 
evident that the microstructure of the section has different grain sizes 
owing to the different particle sizes of the original powder after ball 
milling. In addition, during the sintering process, the temperature at 
different positions was different. These two phenomena led to an uneven 
microstructure. When the composites did not contain CNTs, the cross- 
section structure was mainly grain phase, and the cross sectional 
morphology at P4 was flat, where grain fracture and pull-out of the TiAl
x 
grains were observed, which indicates that the material was brittle and 
had poor plasticity at room temperature. There was an apparent inter -
granular fracture in region P5. Fig. 12 (b) shows that when the composite 
CNT content was 0.4 wt%, the composite material was given priority to 
transgranular fracture with sectional river lines (P6). Moreover, pull-out 
particles were observed ( Fig. 12 (b)), which elongated the material 
structure and increased the new surface of the sectional structure, 
contributing to the increase of fracture energy required by the material 
during the fracture process and improving the strength and toughness. It 
can be observed from Fig. 12 (c) that when the CNT content was 0.7 wt%, 
the proportion of lamellar structure increased obviously, which resulted 
in the loose microstructure and the acceleration of crack propagation. 
Thereby in Fig. 12 (d), it depicts that the CNTs/TiAl alloy contained 
several large Ti
2
AlC phases with strips, resulting in several cracks in the 
section, which decreased the bending strength. 
4. Conclusion 
In this study, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) were used as additives and 
Ti – 48Al – 2Cr – 8Nb was used as the original powder to prepare CNTs/TiAl 
alloys by spark plasma sintering. The effects of different CNT contents on 
the microstructure and mechanical properties of CNTs/TiAl alloys were 
investigated. The conclusions are as follows: 
(1) Ultrasonication can improve the agglomeration of CNT powders 
and disperse it to a certain extent. With an increase in the CNT 
content, the degree of damage to the CNTs in the CNTs/TiAl al -
loys decreased; however, there was no significant difference in 
the degree of peeling of the CNTs. 
(2) When the CNT content was low, TiC and Ti
2
AlC inhibited grain 
growth, refined the grain size, and enhanced the mechanical 
properties at room temperature. Simultaneously, the ceramic 
reinforcing phases, TiC and Ti
2
AlC, also played a strengthening 
role. When the CNT content was high, the TiC and Ti
2
AlC phases 
agglomerated, resulting in the formation of pits and cracks on the 
surface, which weakened the mechanical properties. 
(3) When the CNT content was 0.4 wt%, the density and Vickers 
hardness of the composites were 4.294 g/cm
3 
and 377 HV, 
respectively, and the flexural strength reached 879 MPa, which 
was 7.1% higher than that of the composites without CNTs. The 
fracture mode was mainly transgranular.
