Synchrotron Microbeam Diffraction Study of the
Microstructure and the Chemical Composition in a
Monocrystalline Ni-Base Turbine Blade After a
Thermomechanical Mission Test
T. Ung´ar,1 H. Biermann,2 and B. von Grossmann3
Received December 5, 2001; revised January 20, 2002; accepted March 1, 2002
High-performance turbine blades composed of monocrystalline Ni-base °=°0 superalloys are often
protected by coatings to resist the high-temperature corrosive atmosphere environment applications.
During applications severe thermal and mechanical loads cause drastic and complicated changes
in the microstructure and the chemical composition, also combined with strong lateral gradients of
both temperature and stress. Microbeam X-ray diffraction experiments with a spatial resolution of
about 5 „m enabled the determination of both the microstructural and the chemical composition
gradients within material volumes of the sizes of the order of about 1 mm
3 or smaller. The high
angular and spatial resolution was achieved by a special focusing technique using a Bragg–Fresnel
focusing monochromator at the BM5 beamline of the ESRF synchrotron at Grenoble. The X-ray
diffraction experiments were complemented and supported by electron microscopy investigations of
the microstructure and the chemical composition.
KEY WORDS: Microdiffraction; synchrotron radiation; Bragg–Fresnel lens; Ni-base superalloy; long-range
internal stresses;°=°0 phases; misﬁt; mission test.

Real turbine blades are often coated on the outside
surface by heat- and corrosion-resistant layers, e.g., of
NiAl [6], and are provided by air-cooling channels. This
makes the turbine blade a complex, functionally graded
piece of monocrystalline material. The heat, stress, and
corrosive load during operation causes the transforma-
tion of the microstructure, called above rafting, and, due
to the heterogeneous compositional structure, especially
near the surface, diffusion of different elements toward
the bulk and out through the free surfaces causes com-
positional changes. Depending on the size of the turbine
blade, the thickness between the coated outer surface and
the inner surface of the cooling channel can be as small as
a millimeter or less [7–9]. Thus, the complex change of
chemistry and mechanical stresses during the operational

hundred microns up to a few millimeters. In the present

and/or chemical composition in the different phases of
the turbine blade with a spatial resolution of about 10„m.
Thus, the effect of local internal stresses induced by defor-
mation and rafting are determined with the same spatial
resolution.

CONDITIONS
The heterogeneities of the microstructure of the in-
vestigated specimen are strongly anisotropic [7–9]. The
gradients of both the microstructure and the chemical com-
position are changing almost only in one direction, which
is perpendicular to the outer surface of the turbine blade.
Within the planes parallel to the outer surface of the tur-
bine blade, properties of the material are, to a large extent,
homogeneous and isotropic, at least within distances up
to several hundred microns. The microbeam experiment
has been designed in accordance with these geometrical
conditions. The schematic setup of the X-ray beam, the

THE SPECIMENS
Monocrystalline turbine blades manufactured from
the nickel-base superalloy CMSX-6 were exposed to an
accelerated mission test in a test turbine. The speed of ro-
tation and the temperature were selected to a speciﬁc test
program for several hours in order to simulate the life-
time in a shorter laboratory test. The simultaneous heat
and stress load were applied by the centrifugal force and
the applied temperature. The outer surface of the turbine
blade was coated by a corrosion- and heat-resistant pro-
tective NiAl coating. A virgin-coated turbine blade was
also investigated by the same methods as the loaded one
in order to correlate the effects of the accelerated mission
test on the microstructure.

Long-Range Internal Stresses
High-resolution diffraction experiments carried out
on plastically deformed single crystals [11], polycrystals
[12], or Ni-base °=°
0 superalloys [13] have revealed a
characteristic asymmetry of the diffraction proﬁles. In par-
ticular, after unloading an [001] oriented single crystal
from an uniaxial tensile test, the (002)/(004) (axial case)
or (020)/(040) (side case) proﬁles are strongly asymmetric
in the opposite sense: (1) in the case of the (002)/(004)
proﬁles the intensities on the small angle sides drop more
slowly than on the high-angle sides, whereas (2) in the
case of the (020)/(040) proﬁles (or the crystallographi-
cally equivalent (200)/(400) proﬁles) the intensities on the
high angle sides drop more slowly than on the small-angle
sides. Especially in the case of°=°
0 superalloys, this char-
acteristic asymmetry can become so strong that the proﬁles
split up into separated, although overlapping peaks, each
corresponding either to the ° or°
0 phases, respectively
[8,13]. The reversal of the asymmetry of the axial and
side case proﬁles can be explained straightforwardly by
the two component composite model of Mughrabi [11,14].
According to this, the hard °
0 and soft° components in
the Ni-base superalloy (in analogy to the hard cell wall
and soft cell interior material in the dislocation cell struc-
ture of a deformed single-phase pure metal) remain under
the effect of forward and backward residual long-range
internal stress, respectively [15]. These long-range inter-
nal stresses alternate in space, together with the hard and
soft°
0 and° components in the superalloy (or with the
regions of high- and low-dislocation densities in the dis-
location cell structure). These internal stresses impose an
alternating tetragonality, which splits the (002)/(004) and
(020)/(040) reﬂections. The tetragonal splitting is obvi-
ously in the opposite direction for the hard or soft phases,
since these internal stresses are acting in the opposite
directions in the two different phases. As a result, the
(002)/(004) partial peaks corresponding either to the hard
or the soft regions will be shifted in opposite directions.
The same occurs in the opposite sense to the (020)/(040)
partial peaks corresponding again either to the hard or the
soft regions, respectively. If these shifts are small, only
an asymmetry will be observed. However, as mentioned
before, the tetragonality can be so large, especially in the
case of °=°
0 superalloys, that the asymmetry turns into
shoulders or even separated, although overlapping, pro-
ﬁles [11,15]. The microscopic model developed for the
long-range internal stresses by Mughrabi assumes that
geometrically necessary dislocations are lined up along
the interfaces between the soft and hard regions of the
material [11,14,15]. These geometrically necessary inter-
face dislocations put the hard or soft components, in the
present case the°
0 or the° phases, under elastic forward
or backward stresses, respectively.

CONCLUSIONS
Microdiffraction with a spatial line resolution of
about 2 „m enables the determination of the changes in
the microstructure of a complex high-temperature struc-
tural alloy in terms of chemical composition and residual
internal stresses.
The microdiffraction experiments were carried out
at the BM5 optical beamline of the ESRF European Syn-
chroton in Grenoble by the application of a Si-base linear
Bragg–Fresnel focusing monochromator.
The X-ray diffraction experiments were supported
by chemical analysis carried out in a scanning electron
microscope.
It has been found that in the tail region of a tur-
bine blade composed of the nickel-base °=°
0 superalloy
CMSX-6 during an accelerated mission test: (1) Al dif-
fuses into the bulk increasing the lattice constants and
the volume fraction of the °
0 phase. At the same time,
Cr diffuses toward the surface and evaporates off; (2) the
centrifugal forces leave the material under the action of
residual internal stresses, which can be observed by the
tetragonal distortion of the originally cubic crystal lat-
tices of the° and°
0 phases; (3) the initially close to zero
lattice misﬁt between the ° and °0 phases becomes
strongly negative in the surface near regions.
