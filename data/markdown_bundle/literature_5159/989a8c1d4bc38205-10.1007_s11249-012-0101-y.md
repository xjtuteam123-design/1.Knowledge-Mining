A Nickel-Alloy-Based High-Temperature Self-Lubricating
Composite with Simultaneously Superior Lubricity and High
Strength
Fei Li • Jun Cheng • Zhuhui Qiao • Jiqiang Ma •
Shengyu Zhu • Licai Fu • Jun Yang • Weimin Liu
Received: 23 October 2012 / Accepted: 31 December 2012 / Published online: 9 January 2013
/C211Springer Science+Business Media New York 2013
Abstract It is a challenge to design self-lubricating mate-
rials that exhibit and maintain reduced friction coefﬁcient as
well as high strength over a wide range of temperatures.
A high-temperature self-lubricating nickel-alloy-based com-
posite was created using the hot pressing technique. The
composite exhibited high relative density, and simulta-
neously superior lubricating properties, average friction
coefﬁcient below 0.25 from room temperature to 800/C176C, and
high strength, 470 MPa of tensile strength and 1500 MPa of
compressive strength. The composite was very promising in
high-temperature tribology.

1 Introduction
There is an increasing need for high-temperature solid
lubricant materials that will operate over wide range of
temperatures from low temperature to 700 /C176C, even to
1000 /C176C[ 1–4]. Examples of applications requiring such
materials under the extreme environment include bearings
and bushings for space satellites, vehicles, hypersonic air-
craft and missiles, high speed foil air bearings, rotating face
valves, stirling engine cylinders, and so on [ 2, 5, 6].
To overcome the shortfall that single solid lubricant has
lubricity only in narrow temperature scope, the strategy of
combining the functions of individual solid lubricant con-
stituents was proposed by Sliney [ 7]. Numerous advances
have been made over the last 40 years. The most important
two series of materials, plasma-sprayed coatings of PS100,
PS200, PS300, and PS400, and adaptive coatings for high
temperatures, were pioneered by NASA and the Air Force
Research Laboratory (AFRL, USA), respectively [2–5, 7–10],
and the materials exhibited remarkable lubricity over a
wide temperature range. Besides the high-temperature self-
lubricating composites in bulk form were made, such as
PM304 [11, 12], ZrO
2-matrix composites [13, 14], Nickel-
alloy-based composites [15, 16], Ni–Al intermetallic matrix
composites [ 17–19] and MAX phases-based composites
[20–22]. Commonly, addition of solid lubricant to bulk
composites will importantly decrease composite’s strength,
so the reported materials that presented either low strength
or poor lubricity are difﬁcult to satisfy practical applica-
tions as part. However, self-lubricating coating materials
usually suffer from the shortcomings of weak binding and
poor thermal expansion match between matrix and coating,
or coating layer being too thin, causing low endurance,
short service life, and poor wear resistance or not meeting
requirement for high speed and high load. Therefore, it is a
major challenge to produce the simultaneously superior
tribological properties and high strength of self-lubricating
composites in bulk form useful over a wide temperature
range for making load-bearing parts required lubrication.
This work is aimed at developing a high-temperature self-
lubricating nickel-alloy-based composite that possesses a
combination of high strength and superior tribological
properties over a broad range of temperatures. To the
author’s knowledge, the composites that exhibit and
maintain low friction coefﬁcient as well as high strength

2 Experimental Procedures
A high-temperature self-lubricating nickel-alloy-based
composite employed a Ni–Cr–Mo–Al alloy as matrix along
with by weight percent, 12 % low-to-moderate temperature
lubricant of Ag and 10 % moderate-to-high-temperature
lubricant of BaF 2/CaF2 eutectic. The composite was fab-
ricated by hot-pressed sintering technique using a graphite
mold under a pressure of 20 MPa and temperature of
1,100 /C176C holding 30 min from a mixed powder of nickel
alloy, Ag, and BaF
2/CaF2 eutectic according to designing
composition. The nickel alloy powder used herein was
obtained by mechanical milling a mixture of individual
element Ni, Cr, Mo, and Al powders by a weight ratio of
72:15:5:8 using a planetary high-energy ball-mill (Fritsch
Pulverisettes5) under high-purity argon atmosphere and
then heat treating the mixed powder.
X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern was obtained using a
PANalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer with a Cu K a
radiation source. A JSM-5600LV scanning electron micro-
scope (SEM) equipped with energy dispersive spectros-
copy (EDS) was employed for microstructure observation
and composition distribution analysis.
The density of the sample was measured by the buoy-
ancy method applying the Archimedes principle. The
Vickers microhardness of the as-produced composite was
measured using an MH-5 tester with a load of 300 g and a
dwell time of 10 s. The quasi-static uniaxial compression
and tensile tests for the composite were done at room
temperature with a crosshead speed of 0.1 mm s
-1.
The tribological tests were conducted using a home-built
ball-on-disk type high-temperature tribometer by sliding
the composite disks against superalloy pins. Hemispherical
tipped pins with 5 mm in diameter were made from the
common heat-treated nickel-based superalloy Inconel 718
with the Vickers hardness of 4.4 GPa. The composite disk,
with a size of 18.5 9 18.5 9 5m m
3, was polished to
mirror surfaces before testing. The selected testing tem-
peratures were 25, 200, 400, 600, and 800 /C176C. The sliding
speed of disk sample was 1.0 m/s, and the pin generated a
10-mm diameter wear track on the disk surface. The
applied load was 10 N, and the running time was 20 min.
The friction coefﬁcient was recorded automatically, and
the average values are reported herein. The cross-section
proﬁle of the worn surface was measured using a Micro-
XAM-3D surface proﬁler, and the wear volume was cal-
culated automatically by the equipment using the integral
method. The wear rate was calculated as the wear volume
divided by sliding distance and applied load. All the
tribological tests and measurements were carried out three
times under identical conditions, and the mean values were
given. The relative error was less than 10 %. The surface
morphologies of the wear scars of the composite were
investigated by SEM. A Renishaw’s inVia Micro-Raman
was employed to examine the phase compositions of the
wear scars using a laser of 633 nm wave length.
3 Results and Discussion
A typical XRD pattern (Fig. 1a) indicates that as desired,
the composite is composed of Ni–Cr alloy, Ni
3Al inter-
metallics, Ag, CaF 2, and BaF 2 phases. Figure 1b shows a
typical SEM secondary electron image of the composite, in
which the distributional Ag and BaF
2/CaF2 are marked by
arrows, which are identiﬁed by EDS maps. The SEM
observations combined with EDS map analyses indicate
that the composite consists of alloy matrix of main roughly
equiaxial grain of about 5–10 lm and a small quantity of
c/c
0 netlike eutectic structure, along with 5–10 lm particle
of Ag and near network of BaF 2/CaF2 eutectic. Also, the
sample is found to be dense from the SEM observations.

The density of the composite is measured to be 7.20 g cm-3,
which is about 97 % theoretical value of the composite
calculated to be 7.45 g cm-3. The Vickers microhardness of
the composite is 3.1 GPa comparable to that of NASA
PS304, 3.7 GPa, and this moderate hardness will contribute
to mild abrasion to counterfaces. As shown in Fig. 2, the
composite exhibits very high room temperature strength,
470 MPa of tensile strength, and 1500 MPa of compressive
strength along with a large strain over 0.35, which are dra-
matically higher than that of notable NASA PM212 below
200 MPa of room temperature compressive strength [1] and
that of PM304 about 50 MPa of tensile strength reported by
Ding [ 11]. Also, the composite displays high bending
strength of 1,140 MPa. The high strength should be attrib-
uted to its high relative density and good bonding of grain
boundary. Such high strength can meet the requirement as
load-bearing tribological parts to some extent.
Table 1 lists the average friction coefﬁcient and the
wear rate of the composite along with those of NASA
PS304 and PS400 reported in literature [ 4, 5] for compar-
ative purposes. This composite exhibits impressively low
average friction coefﬁcient of 0.22–0.25 at a wide range of
temperatures from 25 to 800 /C176C. This result is better than
that of NASA PS304 coating reported at approximate
testing conditions [ 4]. Also, the typical friction curves of
the composite at different temperatures are shown in
Fig. 3, indicating that the friction coefﬁcient is small
ﬂuctuant. With increasing temperature, the friction coefﬁ-
cient slightly drops from 25 to 600 /C176C and then rises a
little. The wear rate of the composite is in the order of
10
-5 mm3 N-1 m-1, belonging to moderate wear accord-
ing to the criterion reported [ 1]. The wear rate of the
composite rises with increasing temperature, which is an
opposite result compared to that of NASA PS304 coating,
but it is signiﬁcantly lower than that of NASA PS304
[4, 5]. NASA PS400 demonstrates lower friction coefﬁcient
and wear rate compared to the produced composite at high
temperatures, but at room temperature PS400 shows very
high friction coefﬁcient of 0.80 and very high wear rate,
which should be a crucial shortfall for practical applica-
tions. Consequently, the composite exhibits very good
lubricity and wear resistance from ambient temperature to
high temperatures, and more signiﬁcantly, it performs
coexisting high strength, indicating that the composite
possesses largely practical application potentials.
Figure 4 shows the SEM images from the worn surfaces
of the composite at different testing temperatures. The
grooves, small ﬂaking pits, and microcracks can be found
on the worn surfaces of the composite at 25, 200, and
400 /C176C, and the ﬂaking off is severer at higher tempera-
tures. The numerous microcracks and less ﬂaking pits are
present on the worn surface of the composite at 600/C176C. The
worn surface of the composite at 800 /C176C shows important
delamination and groove. Therefore, the main wear mech-
anism of the composite is ploughing, fatigue microcracking,
and fatigue delamination. Also, a lot of very small white dot
adhered on the wear scars at 400 and 600 /C176C, which might
be accumulating lubricants, can be found. As a result, the
friction coefﬁcient at 400 and 600 /C176C is the lowest. Com-
monly, the strength or hardness of Ni-based alloys drop
with increasing temperature, in which case the resistance to
wear also decreases, in good agreement with the results of
the variation tendency of the wear rate (Table 1) and the
morphology characteristics of the worn surface (Fig. 4).
The pioneering concept of combining functions of
individual solid lubrications to obtain good lubricity over a
wide temperature range was developed by Sliney [ 7]. The
combining lubricating constituents of Ag and BaF
2/CaF2
eutectic, for low-to-moderate temperature and moderate-
to-high-temperature lubricants, respectively, was applied in
several composite material systems [ 1, 4, 5]. A notable
PS304 coating, composed of 60 % NiCr binder, 20 %
Cr
2O3, 10 % Ag, and 10 % BaF 2/CaF2 eutectic by weight,
was deposited by NASA. To date, NASA PS304 is
believed to have best lubricating property over a wide
range of temperatures. The constituent of Cr
2O3 is favor-
able of enhancing the wear resistance. Formulation of the
composite herein differs from that of PS304, without

4 Conclusions
Overall, by the composition and processing tailoring, a
dense Ni-alloy-based high-temperature self-lubricating com-
posite has been obtained. Particularly, the composite rep-
resents remarkable self-lubricity ( l * 0.25) over a wide
temperature range (25–800 /C176C) and coexisting high strength.
The material with the simultaneously superior lubricity
and high strength might open new applications in high-
temperature tribology.
