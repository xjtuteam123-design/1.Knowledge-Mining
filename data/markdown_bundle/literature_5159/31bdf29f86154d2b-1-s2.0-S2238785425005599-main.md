ABSTRACT
In this work, a Zr-enriched Ti – Zr – Al – Nb alloy was proposed and prepared. The development of microstructure 
during heat treatment was investigated, and the tensile properties were compared. The results showed that the 
martensite phase that formed in quenching, with very a little β phase remained, decomposed incompletely during 
aging. The partially decomposed HCP-structured phases exhibited a promising solid-solution strengthening ef -
fect. The yield strength slightly decreased with the increment of aging temperature, mainly due to the fact that 
the volume fraction of the resultant α
2
′ phase increased with relatively low solid-solution strengthening effect. 
Via quenching and then aging, the alloy exhibited a superb strength-ductility combination. The tensile yield 
strength and ultimate strength of the alloy could reach, respectively, above 1350 MPa and 1430 MPa, while the 
maximum elongation reached 10.3%.
1. Introduction
Lightweight alloys are extensively used in aerospace industries. Due 
to the increased demand for high performances, the conventional 
lightweight alloys need to be developed for the upgrading of major 
equipment. In the past decades, various design strategies have been 
explored for developing titanium alloys [ 1 – 5 ]. However, 
strength-ductility “ trade-off ” is usually one of the bottleneck problems 
limiting the super comprehensive mechanical property. In recent years, 
low-density high entropy alloys (HEAs) have attracted much attention 
[ 6 – 9 ], in which the high entropy effect is valuable to be utilized for 
improving titanium alloys. Alloying with high Zr-content is an effective 
method. As reported in previous researches [ 10 – 18 ], Ti – Zr binary alloys 
exhibited attractive mechanical performances which can be further 
improved through alloying other elements.
Titanium and zirconium have the same structure of BCC and HCP, 
respectively, at high and room temperatures. A Ti – Zr binary alloy would 
form BCC-structured β or HCP-structured α solid-solution phase corre -
spondingly. Due to the fact that the radius of the Zr atom (0.162 nm) is 
larger than that of the Ti atom (0.147 nm), the atomic radius mismatch 
usually results in the lattice distortion that enhances solid-solution 
hardening. With the increase of Zr content, the strength increases 
significantly [ 19 ]. In addition to high Zr content, other elements such as 
V, Nb, Mo and Al, etc., have been usually added to this kind of alloys 
[ 20 – 25 ]. Similar to conventional titanium alloys, the Zr-enriched Ti – Zr 
alloys can form HCP-structured α , BCC-structured β or α + β type 
microstructure, depending on the characteristics and concentration of 
alloying elements.
Including shape memory alloy [ 26 – 29 ], most Zr-enriched Ti – Zr al -
loys have been usually designed with 10~50 wt% Zr combining with 
other elements to form the β phase. The single β phase alloys have good 
ductility, biocompatibility and corrosion resistance, so they are favored 
as a biomedical material [ 30 – 33 ]. Due to the low elastic modulus and 
strength, the single β phase alloys, as a structural material, are generally 
difficult to meet the increased need for engineering applications at 
present.
With similar compositions, HCP-structured solid-solution possesses 
higher shear modulus as compared with BCC-structured solid-solution 
[ 34 – 36 ]. High shear modulus is favorable for high strength in terms of 
solid-solution strengthening mechanisms [ 37 – 39 ], and is desirable for 
developing high strength alloys. As an alphagenic element, Al is usually 
added to promote the formation of the α phase. Doped with betagenic 
elements further, the thermodynamic equilibrium microstructure can be 
adjusted according to the requirement for the relative composition of α

Fig. 11 presents the continuous cooling transformation (CCT) curves

transformation from the β phase to the α phase. In addition, the 
martensite transition temperature of the present alloy is much lower 
than that of the Ti – 6Al – 4V alloy. This result suggests that the phase 
transformation in the present alloy could be greatly delayed.

5. Conclusions
1) In contrast to conventional titanium alloys, phase transformation 
was delayed due to the lattice distortion and diffusion retardation in 
the high-content Zr-enriched Ti – Zr alloy. This intensively influenced 
the martensite transforming and decomposing. The martensite phase 
incompletely decomposed after aging, and there was a very little β 
phase retained.
2) The present alloy exhibited an effective strengthening effect via heat 
treatment. After quenched at 825 
◦
C and then aged at 585 
◦
C for 4 h, 
the alloy possessed a commendable strength-ductility combination 
with the yield strength of 1442 MPa and the elongation of 6.7%.
3) The yield strength of the quenched and then aged alloy was mainly 
contributed by solid solution-strengthening. The yield strength 
slightly decreased with the increment of aging temperature, mainly 
due to the fact that the volume fraction of α
2
′ increased with relative 
low solid solution-strengthening effect. The retained large initial α
p 
phase was conducive to the stress relaxation and strain accommo -
dation for gaining a good strength-ductility combination.
4) The formation of martensite variants in quenching was favorable for 
strengthening the alloy through grain refinement. Stacking faults can 
be found in the alloy, which could also act in strengthening. The 
strengthening contribution by grain boundaries (interfaces) and 
stacking faults (dislocations) was estimated averagely as ~ 179 MPa 
for the quenched and then aged alloy via the different paths.
