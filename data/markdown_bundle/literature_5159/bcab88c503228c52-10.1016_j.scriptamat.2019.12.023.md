Creep behavior of coated superalloys is important since critical metallic parts operating at high tem- 
perature are functionalized to resist oxidation, corrosion, etc. Unfortunately, there is a lack of information 
about creep data of coated superalloys. Polycrystalline and single crystal Ni-based superalloys coated with 
Ta-containing NiCoCrAlY by high-velocity oxy-fuel spraying were creep tested at 980 °C under 150 MPa 
constant load in air to address the inﬂuence of substrate thickness on their creep strength behavior. The 
contribution of the coating to both superalloys creep resistance was found to be negligible. However, a 
superalloy thickness debit effect was observed and is discussed.

Creep testing at constant stress and temperature of alloys used 
for high-temperature applications with extreme operating condi- 
tions, e.g. Ni-based superalloys have been extensively studied in 
the last decades [1–7] . Extreme working conditions on gas turbine 
components require structural materials with good oxidation and 
corrosion resistance as well as high creep resistance. Ni-based su- 
peralloys are widely used in turbine engines and prompt to work 
in hot section under high temperature loadings (T > 900 °C) [8] . 
Moreover, the continuous increase in the operating temperature 
has been driven by an air ﬁlm cooling and the use of thermal bar- 
rier coatings to enhance higher working time of these components 
[9] . 
NiCoCrAlY-type coatings are applied to superalloy components 
for protection mainly against high temperature oxidation and cor- 
rosion as well as bond coat for thermal barrier coatings systems. 
However, the creep deformation is a factor that restricts the op- 
erating time of these components. Reported creep-rupture prop- 
erties of superalloys are affected by characteristics of the ﬁnal 
parts, e.g. microstructure obtained by speciﬁc solidiﬁcation pro- 
cesses (equiaxial or single crystal casting), the section-thickness 
size (thickness debit effect), among others [2 , 6 , 10 , 11] .

Few data on the creep properties of either coated superalloys or 
pure NiCoCrAlY coatings are available in literature. This work was 
undertaken to evaluate creep properties of two Ni-based superal- 
loys coated with HVOF Ta-containing NiCoCrAlY and to assess the 
effect of coating / superalloy thickness ratio ( t c /t s ) on their creep 
behavior. The inﬂuence of oxidation conditions on the failure be- 
havior of the specimens is also presented and discussed. 
Commercial Ta-containing NiCoCrAlY powder (Amdry 997, Oer- 
likon Metco) was utilized to deposit thermally-sprayed coatings 
(BC) on two different Ni-based superalloys (SA). Rectangular ﬂat 
plates of 75 × 18 × 2 mm were used as substrates consisting ei- 
ther of a commercial polycrystalline Inconel 100, hereafter IN100, 
and single crystal M247LC, hereafter M247LC-SX superalloys. Coat- 
ings were manufactured by high velocity oxy-fuel (HVOF) thermal 
spray process using a Diamond Jet 2700 (Oerlikon Metco) gun to 
reach ~200 μm in thickness. Annealing heat treatment was car- 
ried out on the coated superalloys in vacuum ( p O 2 < 10 −5 mbar) at 
1080 °C for 4 h before preparing creep specimens. Creep specimens 
were cut from the heat-treated plates by wire electrical discharge 
machining (EDM, CUT 200 SP, AgieCharmilles). The gauge length 
(GL) was deﬁned with four small ridges at the center of the creep 
specimens (see Fig. 1 a). Subsequently, specimens were ground on 
the uncoated side to reach different superalloy thicknesses either 
1750, 950, or 550 μm leading to coating/superalloy ratios ( t c /t s ) to 
be between = 0.12 and 0.36. These samples were compared with

Fig. 1 shows microstructures (b and c) of annealed samples 
consisting of HVOF Ta-containing NiCoCrAlY coatings deposited on 
IN100 and M247LC-SX, respectively. The studied specimens exhib- 
ited a microporosity (around 7 %) and a microstructure consisting 
of β/ γ/ γ′ phases along the coating which contains (Ti,Ta)-rich car- 
bides at the coating/substrate interface. The typical superalloy mi- 
crostructure of the IN100 and M247LC-SX substrates consisted of 
γ/ γ′ phases and either (Ti,Mo)- and (Hf,Ta)-rich carbides, respec- 
tively. Further details of the microstructural characteristics of these 
systems are given elsewhere [14] . Fig. 1 a shows the schematic rep- 
resentation of creep test specimens machined by wire EDM. Again, 
the coated specimens are classiﬁed according to the thickness ratio 
between the coating and superalloy ( t c /t s ) as 0.12, 0.21, and 0.36. 
The non-coated samples are labeled as 0.00 ratio. 
Fig. 2 shows the obtained creep results of the different spec- 
imens until their rupture as a function of t c /t s . The creep curves 
for specimens with IN100 and M247LC-SX are shown in Fig. 2 a 
and 2 b, respectively. The curve of M247LC-SX with 0.12 ratio is 
not presented due to system-controlling problems that occurred 
during the tests. The rupture strain of the IN100 superalloy speci- 
mens was around 5 % meanwhile, specimens with M247LC-SX sub- 
strate were highly dependent on substrate thickness. In this case, 
differences observed between both studied superalloys lie in the 
fact that superalloys creep properties are closely related to their 
microstructure. It is well known that the grain size (GS) to spec- 
imen diameter (SD) ratio considerably affects alloy creep proper- 
ties, i.e. fewer grains across the specimen thickness decreases the 
creep resistance [ 8 , 15 ]. From a physical point of view, creep dam- 
age in metals is essentially the nucleation, growth, and coalescence 
of intergranular microcracks up to specimen rupture. Thus, con- 
sidering that polycrystalline superalloys have a greater GS/SD ra- 
tio than single crystal alloys for the same specimen thick-size, it 
is expected that polycrystalline alloys show less thickness debit ef- 
fect with section size reductions than the single crystal superalloys 
[16] . 
Fig. 2 c and d show the strain rate ( ˙ ε ) versus strain ( ɛ ) plots of 
creep tested specimens with IN100 and M247LC-SX superalloys, re- 
spectively. In all cases, the curves display the minimum creep rate 
reached during creep tests followed by an increase in the strain 
rate. In the case of IN100 superalloy samples with t c /t s ≤ 0.21, 
the increase of creep rate showed after the minimum strain rate is 
similar up to their rupture. Meanwhile, the strain rate of the sam- 
ple with t c /t s = 0.36 shows a faster strain rate after 3 % of defor- 
mation until its rupture ( Fig. 2 c). Similar behavior is observed in 
samples with M247LC-SX substrate ( Fig. 2 d). Although specimens 
with 0.00 and 0.21 coating/superalloy thickness ratio presented a 
minimum strain rate of approx. 10 −7 s −1 , the followed strain rate 
is lower for the uncoated specimen compared to that of the speci- 
men with t c /t s = 0.12.

mum creep rates as a function of the t c /t s ratio of both sets of 
samples. This ﬁgure shows that the uncoated IN100 and M247LC-

effect of section size on the time to rupture is observed irrespec-

to failure decreases with decreasing specimen thickness. However, 
specimens with M247LC-SX superalloy show higher time to fail-

either for poly- or single crystalline Ni-based superalloys. For 
instance, Strößner et al. [10] investigated the effects of section

The idea of creep testing done in one-side coated superalloys 
is to address the effect of the reduction of superalloy thickness to 
indirectly obtain the creep resistance of Ta-containing NiCoCrAlY 
coatings. A linear regression of the data obtained from both sets 
of specimens suggests that when t s = 0 the creep time to fail- 
ure of Ta-containing NiCoCrAlY coating tends to zero at 980 °C 
and 150 MPa. This result is due to microstructural characteristics 
of the HOVF coating such as splats, inter-splats, unmelted particles, 
porosity, internal oxidation, etc. 
The minimum creep rate, as shown in Fig. 3 b is also inﬂuenced 
by the coating/superalloy ratio and therefore by specimen thick- 
ness. The minimum creep rates of both tested superalloys display 
very similar values. The data point of M247LC-SX with t c /t s = 0.00 
is plotted as an average value of the specimen tested in this work 
and an earlier work done by Bensch et al. [16] . The reduction 
of the sample thickness and thereby the increase of the coat- 
ing/superalloy ratio from 0.00 to 0.36 results in an increase of the 
minimum creep rate by one order of magnitude for both superal- 
loys. 
Another simple explanation is assuming separate creep of both 
coating and superalloy at 980 °C. In this case, the stress required 
to cause the coated superalloy to deform with a steady state creep 
rate is found by summing the stresses of coating and superal- 
loy ( σ c and σ s , respectively) using the rule of mixtures. Applying 
the constraint that the coating and superalloy deform equally in 
a power law with the same exponent ( ˙ ε = A σ n , where A and n 
are constant values at constant temperature), then the true stresses 
in the coated superalloys are greater than the nominal stress (150 
MPa) by normalizing the total applied load to the superalloy cross 
section, considering no contribution of the coating to the creep be- 
havior (see the lower scale of Fig. 3 ). Thus, it becomes evident 
that both the time to failure and the minimum creep rate varies 
with the superalloy true stress. These data show a linear ﬁt with 
the creep power law giving a Norton exponent ( n ) of 9.5 and 7.3 
for IN100 and M247LC-SX superalloys, respectively ( Fig. 3 b). It is 
worth to mention that this ﬁt can only be done when most of the 
stress is carried only by the superalloy substrate. These data are 
compared in the same ﬁgure with literature reports for the same 
superalloys evidencing that similar values have been previously re- 
ported [ 6 , 7 , 10 , 16 , 17 ]. The tendency to reduce the minimum creep 
rate as the stress increases is consistent in all cases, and the Nor- 
ton exponent varies approximately in the range from 8 to 9. 
After creep tests, cross-sectional microstructures of each sam- 
ple were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy. Fig. 4 shows 
cross sections of creep-tested samples focusing on the fracture, 
which occurred within the gauge length. As expected, the frac- 
ture of samples with IN100 superalloy ( Fig. 4 a) mainly occurred 
along their grain boundaries. Meanwhile, the M247LC-SX fracture 
occurred primarily by void growth and linkage ( Fig. 4 b). 
Since creep tests were carried out in air, in these two super- 
alloys the oxidation affected the degradation of coated and un- 
coated superalloys. Insets from Fig. 4 show oxide characteristics af- 
ter creep tests. The coated IN100 superalloys showed a thin oxide 
scale (~2 μm), which mainly consisted of alumina ( Fig. 4 a-c). At 
this level, the formed oxide seems to be uninﬂuenced by the sub- 
strate due to the homogeneous coating thickness (~200 μm). In 
contrast, uncoated IN100 superalloy ( Fig. 4 d) presented a thicker 
oxide layer of ~35 μm of Ni-rich spinels. Moreover, ~14 μm alu- 
minum oxide followed by a γ′ -free layer was observed in the al- 
loy. The coated M247LC-SX superalloy shows an increase in the 
formed oxide layer in the range of ~2 - 4.6 μm as the creep time

Summarizing, creep tests at 980 °C under normal stress of 150 
MPa were conducted on coated and uncoated IN100 and M247LC- 
SX superalloys. The superalloy thickness was varied respective to 
the coating. In both coated superalloys, a thickness debit effect was 
observed. The smaller the specimen thickness, the lower the creep 
resistance and the higher the minimum creep rate. Ta-containing 
NiCoCrAlY coatings do not contribute to the load bearing section 
and therefore shows no signiﬁcant creep resistance. Under these 
conditions, considering that the stress is carried only by the super- 
alloy substrate, the true stresses in the coated superalloys vary be- 
tween 150 and 205 MPa. The power law ﬁtting of minimum creep 
rate versus superalloy true stress leads to n exponents between 8 
and 9 for both superalloys, which is consistent with literature val- 
ues. The M247LC-SX superalloy in either coated or uncoated condi- 
tions showed better creep behavior compared to IN100. Minimum 
creep rates of coated IN100 and M247LC-SX were very similar at 
coating/superalloy ratios of 0.21 and 0.36. The primary mechanism 
responsible for stress-rupture life in IN100 superalloy was related 
to the behavior of grain boundaries in thin sections. The set of 
coated and uncoated IN100 superalloys showed lower creep time 
to failure compared to those with M247LC-SX superalloy. Since 
creep tests were carried out in air, the oxygen diffusion causes 
grain boundary embrittlement in IN100 and void growth and link- 
age in M247LC-SX. Ni depletion in uncoated IN100 occurs faster 
than in M247LC-SX, and in both cases, the oxygen diffuses into the 
alloy forming a region of internal oxidation of aluminum.
