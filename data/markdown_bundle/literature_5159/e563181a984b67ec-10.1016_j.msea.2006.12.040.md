Effect of Mo and Hf on the mechanical properties and
microstructure of Nb–Ti–C alloys
Rengen Ding, Ian P. Jones∗, Huisheng Jiao 1
Department of Metallurgy and Materials, University of Birmingham, Birmingham B15 2TT, UK
Received 2 October 2006; received in revised form 8 December 2006; accepted 11 December 2006
Abstract
The microstructures and mechanical properties of Nb–20Ti–12.5C (at.%) containing different Mo or Hf contents have been investi-
gated. The microstructures were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM).
The alloy without Mo and Hf contains Nb solid solution (Nbss), (Ti,Nb)
2C/Nbss eutectic, primary (Ti,Nb) 2C and secondary (Ti,Nb)C.
The addition of Mo or Hf restrains the precipitation of (Ti,Nb)C. No Mo-rich precipitates were observed. Part of the Hf appeared as
(Hf,Ti,Nb)
2C-type carbide. Compression testing has been carried out at room temperature and 1473 K. The alloys showed good compression
ductility at room temperature and Nb20Ti12.5C20Mo produced a 0.2% offset yield stress of 300 MPa at 1473 K. The room tempera-
ture and high temperature strengths increase with increasing Mo, but the room temperature ductility decreases. Hf additions however did
not much change the strength because half the Hf dissolved in the carbides and there was not much modiﬁcation of their volume frac-
tion.

1. Introduction
Niobium-based alloys are one of the most promising refrac-
tory alloys for high temperature structural application in place
of Ni-based superalloys, which have a maximum operating
temperature of about 1400 K. Notwithstanding the sensitiv-
ity of niobium alloys to oxygen, niobium has a very high
melting point of 2741 K, a relatively low density (8.57 g/cm
3)
and good ductility at room temperature. However, the strength
of niobium decreases substantially at temperatures above
1273 K.
Various approaches have hence been used to improve the
strength of Nb at elevated temperature, including solid solution
strengthening with Mo, W, Ta and Hf [1–3], and disper-
sion or composite strengthening with intermetallic compounds
such as Nb
3Al and Nb 5Si [4,5], as well as with a carbide
[6–9]. For example, Tan et al. [8] found that the 0.2% ﬂow
stress of Nb–ZrC alloys increases with increasing ZrC con-

2. Experimental procedure
The nominal compositions of the alloys investigated
in this study are Nb20Ti12.5C, Nb20Ti12.5C10Mo,
Nb20Ti12.5C20Mo, Nb20Ti12.5C4Hf and Nb20Ti12.5C8Hf
(compositions in at.%). High purity Nb, Ti, Mo, Hf and TiC
powder were used as the starting materials. The alloys were
prepared by arc-melting and casting into buttons.

4. Discussion
The results obtained from the above study clearly illustrate
that the microstructure of the alloys is inﬂuenced by Mo and
Hf. Microstructural observation indicated that, in addition to
primary carbide, secondary carbide (Ti,Nb)C is formed in the
alloy without Mo and Hf. This is because the solubility limit of
carbon in Nb is about 0.55 at.% at 1873–2453 K with almost
no solubility at room temperature [10]. Therefore, needle-like
secondary carbide is formed in Nbss during cooling after solid-
iﬁcation. No secondary carbide however was observed in the
alloys with Mo or Hf, which means that the addition of Mo

or Hf modiﬁes the solubility limit of carbon in Nb or/and the
kinetics of precipitation of carbide.
Diffraction revealed that the primary carbide is an ordered
Ti2C-type carbide. Loria [11] also observed superlattice reﬂec-
tions in the carbide of a Nb–Ti based alloy. Such ordered
Ti
2C-type carbide was found usually in Ti based alloys. Goretzki
used neutron diffraction to study the Ti–C system and found that
superstructure lines were observed in the concentration range
between 32 and 36 at.% C[12]. Chen et al.[13] and Li et al.[14]
also determined that ordered Ti
2C precipitated in the Ti–V–Cr
alloy system. Wanjara et al. [15] found that the ordered Ti 2C
formed at the interface between TiC and the matrix at high
temperature in Ti–Al–V composites containing TiC particles.
However, further work is required to clarify why the primary car-
bides are Ti
2C but the secondary carbides are TiC in Nb-based
alloys.
The results showed that the compressive yield strength of
this Nb–Ti–C alloy improved signiﬁcantly with Mo addition
at room and elevated temperatures. Generally, the strength of
a two-phase alloy depends on the volume fraction, shape and
distribution of the two phases, and on the strength of each
phase. Since the volume fraction of carbide phase is constant
(∼20%) and irregular primary carbide is the principal con-
tribution to the volume fraction for the alloys studied, the
improvement of strength in the Nb–Ti–C–Mo alloy is mainly
attributed to solid solution strengthening. EDS analysis showed
no Mo in the carbide and a high Mo level in the matrix
(Table 3). Meanwhile, no Mo-rich phase was observed. This
indicates that Mo is completely dissolved into Nbss and there-
fore solid solution strengthens the (Nb,Ti,Mo)ss. King’s study
showed that the maximum solid solution of Mo in Nb is
30 at.% [16]. Tan et al. found that the addition of Mo signif-
icantly improved the strength of Nb–Mo and Nb–ZrC alloys
from room to high temperature by solid solution strengthening
[1,8].
According to Cottrell [17], the effect of solid solution hard-
ening on the yield strength, σ, of the material is given by
σ = 2.5μ(δ)
4/3c where μ is the shear modulus (38 GPa for Nb
at room temperature); δ is a misﬁt parameter and c is solute
concentration. The misﬁt parameter δ is deﬁned as δ =1 /a ∂a/∂c
(5.81% for Nb–Mo binary alloys [16]) where a is the lattice
parameter of Nb.
Using this formula, the predicted solid solution hardening for
10 and 20 at.% Mo in Nb alloys at room temperature is 215 and
430 MPa, respectively. This is consistent with the experimental
results (300 and 450 MPa). Those values are again in agreement
with the study of Tan et al. [1].
The addition of Hf, however, does not much improve the
strength of these Nb–Ti–C alloys. Microstructural examination
found that Hf existed in two forms in the alloys: solid solution
Hf and Hf-rich carbide. Soluble Hf could strengthen the Nb-
base alloys. Hf solution contents in the alloys with 4Hf and 8Hf
are 2.1 ± 0.21 and 4.1 ± 0.25 at.%, respectively, as seen from
Table 2. The misﬁt parameterδ is 5.84% for Nb–Hf binary alloys
[16]. Hence, the predictions of solid strengthening induced by
Hf at room temperature are 45 and 88 MPa for the alloys with
4Hf and 8Hf, respectively. Although part of the Hf dissolves
in the carbide, Hf did not much change the volume fraction of
the carbides: the volume fraction of the carbides was 21% for
Nb20Ti12.5C and 22% and 23%, respectively for the alloys with
4Hf and 8Hf. Therefore, Hf had little effect on the strength.
The addition of Hf to the Nb-base alloys with high C con-
tents is not a promising approach to improve the strength of the
alloys.
5. Conclusions
Based on an investigation of the microstructure and mechan-
ical tests of Nb-Ti-C alloys with various Mo and Hf contents:
(1) Nb20Ti12.5C consists of Nb solid solution (Nbss),
(Ti,Nb)
2C//H9252(Nbss) eutectic, primary irregularly shaped
(Ti,Nb)2C carbide, and secondary needle shaped (Ti,Nb)C
carbide. In the alloys with Mo or Hf, no secondary (Ti,Nb)C
carbide was observed. A Hf-rich (Ti,Hf,Nb)
2C-type carbide
was observed in the alloys with Hf.
(2) The 0.2% offset strength increases with increasing Mo con-
tent, whereas the addition of Mo decreases the ductility.
Nb20Ti12.5C20Mo appears to possess substantial high tem-
perature strength (300 MPa). However, Hf addition did not
much change the strength of the alloys due to half the Hf
being in the carbide but not modifying their volume frac-
tion. Mo thus appears to be a promising element for solid
solution strengthening in Nb-base alloys with C, while Hf
does not.
(3) Due to the difference in thermal expansion coefﬁcient
between carbide and Nbss, a high density of dislocations
was introduced into the Nbss during casting. After defor-
mation, an even higher density of dislocations was observed
in Nbss with a high density of microtwins in the car-
bides.
