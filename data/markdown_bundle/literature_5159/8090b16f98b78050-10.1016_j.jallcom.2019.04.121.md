Abstract: 
The equiatomic CrMnFeCoNi high-entropy alloy (HEA) exhibits outstanding 
toughness and excellent strength-ductility combinat ion at cryogenic temperatures. 
However, its strength is relatively low at room temperature. In order to strengthen this 
HEA, microalloying additions of 0.8 at.% Nb and C w ere made and its properties and

1. Introduction 
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) containing multiple prin cipal elements in (nearly) 
equiatomic ratios, as originally proposed by Yeh et al. [1], have a huge following in 
the scientific community. Several of them can form single-phase solid solutions with 
the body-centered cubic (BCC) [2-14] face-centered cubic (FCC) [15-36] or 
hexagonal close-packed (HCP) [37-39] crystal struct ures. Among these systems, 
Cantor et al. [15] were the first to report that an equiatomic mixture of Cr, Mn, Fe, Co 
and Ni elements can solidify as a single-phase FCC solid solution. Later research 
demonstrated that it exhibits desirable mechanical properties, including good strength 
and ductility [18, 20, 32], both of which depend on temperature and improve 
significantly at cryogenic temperatures (e.g., at 7 7 K) [16, 32], and outstanding 
fracture toughness [18]. Numerous investigations ha ve been conducted on its 
equiatomic and non-equiatomic derivatives to better understand phase stability [24, 28, 
40-44], diffusion kinetics [17], deformation mechan isms [16, 21, 26, 27, 29-31, 45], 
and microstructural modifications, such as grain re finement and second phase 
precipitate manipulation [46]. 
As a single-phase FCC alloy, the CrMnFeCoNi HEA has relatively low strength. 
By refining the grain size from 155 µm to 4.4 µm, Otto et al. [16] improved the yield 
strength of CrMnFeCoNi HEA from ~180 MPa to ~350 MPa at room temperature (RT) 
without a significant loss of ductility. Further gr ain refinement down to about 50 nm 
via severe plastic deformation increased the yield str ength up to 1.95 GPa but with a 
dramatic decrease of ductility [46]. He et al. [47] added Al to the equiatomic

reinforcing phase, i.e., L1 2-Ni 3(Ti, Al), in the CrFeCoNi HEA matrix resulted in ev en 
greater increases in strength at the expense of duc tility [51, 52]. Other investigations 
also demonstrated precipitation strengthening in HE As, e.g., FCC-type precipitate in 
CoCrFeNiNb 0.25 [53], intermetallics phase in CoCrFeNiMox [54] all oys, but the 
precipitates were irregular in shape in some cases which makes it difficult to quantify 
their strength contribution. Additionally, consider able work related to strengthening 
by carbide phases (e.g., M 7C3, M 23 C6) or by carbon in solid solution have been 
reported [50, 55-64]. In summary, significant stren gthening of metallic alloys, 
including HEAs, without sacrificing too much ductil ity, has been demonstrated in 
only a limited number of cases [65-68]. 
With respect to nanoscale precipitation strengtheni ng in CrMnFeCoNi HEAs, 
only a few second phases have been investigated to date. Among the promising 
second phases that form nanoscale precipitates and lead to remarkable strengthening 
are carbides such as TiC, NbC, and VC, which have b een widely used in steels. For 
example, Scott et al. [69] have enhanced the streng th of high manganese austenitic 
TWIP steels by TiC, NbC, and VC. Wang et al. [70] f ound that the ultrafine M 3C,

2. Materials and methods 
2.1 Specimen preparation 
Two HEAs with compositions of Cr 20 Mn 20 Fe 20 Co 20 Ni 20 (at.%) and 
Cr 19.68 Mn 19.68 Fe 19.68 Co 19.68 Ni 19.68 Nb 0.8 C0.8 (at.%) were prepared by arc melting 
mixtures of the pure metals Cr, Fe, Co, Ni and Nb ( purity >99.9 wt.%), Mn 
(purity >99 wt.%) and Cr 3C2 powder (purity >99 wt.%) in a high-purity argon 
atmosphere. We will refer to the first of the above alloys as the base HEA and the 
second alloy to which Nb and C were added as the 0.8 Nb/C HEA. 
The arc melting chamber containing the raw material s was evacuated to ~5 ×10 -2 
Pa, flushed twice with pure Ar with pump downs in b etween, and finally backfilled 
with Ar to a pressure of ~5 × 10 4 Pa. Then, a small piece of pure Ti was melted to 
getter any residual oxygen and the alloys were melt ed. The buttons were flipped and

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Tensile properties 
Representative engineering stress – strain curves o f the base HEA and the 0.8 
Nb/C HEA tested at RT are shown in Fig. 1. The yiel d strength of the 0.8 Nb/C HEA 
is ~732 MPa, which is roughly double that of the ba se HEA, and the ultimate tensile 
strength is ~911 MPa, which is a 43% increase relat ive to the base HEA. Associated 
with the increased strength is a 33% decrease in du ctility. Despite this decrease, the 
elongation to fracture of the 0.8 Nb/C HEA remains relatively high (~32%).

3.2. As-recrystallized microstructure and phase constituents 
Back-scattered electron SEM images of the as-recrys tallized base HEA and 0.8 
Nb/C HEA are shown in Fig. 2 a and b, respectively. Both alloys are fully 
recrystallized after annealing at 1073 K for 1 h, p roducing equiaxed grains. A few 
pores are visible in both alloys, as well as numero us annealing twins, similar to 
previous work [42]. The grain size (2.6 µm) of the 0.8 Nb/C HEA is smaller than that 
of the base HEA (4.6 µm), probably due to solute drag and particle pinning effects [74] 
slowing down grain growth. In addition, microscale particles are visible in the SEM 
image (examples marked by red arrows in Fig. 2 b), which are distributed in both 
intragranular regions and on grain boundaries (GBs) . The particles were analyzed by 
EDX in the SEM and found to be enriched in Nb and C . A few particles enriched in 
Cr and C and also containing the other constituent elements were occasionally 
detected and confirmed to be M 23 C6 by TEM (not shown here). According to the 
research by Powell [75], when the ratio of Nb to C (in wt.%) is below 7, NbC and 
M23 C6 particles are formed in austenitic steels. The few M23 C6 particles that we find 
in the 0.8 Nb/C HEA are consistent with this earlier result. 
XRD of the CrMnFeCoNi HEA yielded a spectrum (not s hown) with only FCC 
peaks, consistent with previous work [76]. In contr ast, the XRD pattern of the 0.8 
Nb/C HEA (Fig. 2 c) exhibits peaks corresponding to the NbC phase in addition to the 
FCC peaks. The detection of the NbC phase by XRD in dicates that it is present in a 
significant amount (>2% by volume). To confirm the morphology and structure of the 
precipitates, TEM was conducted, as shown in Fig. 2 d-f. Besides the microscale

3.3. Evolution of deformation substructure 
A tensile test of the 0.8 Nb/C HEA at RT was interr upted after a plastic strain of 
~0.85% and representative TEM BF micrographs contai ning dislocations and 
annealing twins are shown in Fig. 4. The black and white stripes near boundaries 
seem to be thickness fringes [81, 82]. Grain bounda ries and particles are known to 
impede slip and, consistent with this, dislocation pile-ups can be seen to form against 
them (Fig. 4 a). The higher-magnification TEM BF mi crograph in Fig. 4 b shows 
dislocation loops (blue arrows) around nanoscale Nb C particles (red arrows). 
Dislocation segments between particles are curved i ndicative of pinning by the 
particles followed by Orowan bowing. Thus, in the e arly stage of plastic deformation, 
the dislocations bypass the NbC particles by the cl assical Orowan bowing mechanism 
leaving behind dislocation loops around the particl es. This is consistent with the very 
high hardness (~23 GPa) and elastic modulus (~493.7 ) GPa of NbC, which makes 
them difficult to shear, even when they have nanosc ale dimensions [83]. With 
increasing strain more and more loops form, which i ncreases the effective size of the 
NbC particles and decreases the distance between th em, making Orowan bypass 
progressively more difficult [80].

An equiatomic (CrMnFeCoNi) HEA with NbC addition wa s prepared by drop 
casting, followed by homogenization, cold rolling a nd recrystallization to generate an

FCC matrix containing nanosized carbide precipitate s. Microstructures and tensile 
properties were investigated and compared with the base HEA CrMnFeCoNi. Based 
on the observations, the following conclusions can be drawn: 
(1) Addition of 0.8 at.% each of Nb and C stimulate d formation of nanoscale NbC 
particles inside the grains. Coarser, microscale ca rbides (NbC and M 23 C6) were 
observed on the grain boundaries. 
(2) The yield and ultimate tensile strength of the microalloyed HEA are increased 
significantly to 732 and 911 MPa, respectively, whi le retaining a high elongation of 
32%. The corresponding values for the base HEA are 353 MPa, 635 MPa, and 43%. 
(3) The higher strength of the 0.8 Nb/C HEA is due to grain refinement and 
precipitation strengthening. The precipitation strengthening mechanism is the Orowan 
bowing and dislocation bypass mechanism.
