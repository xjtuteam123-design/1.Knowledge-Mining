Reduced-Order Constitutive
Modeling of Directionaiiy
Solidified Ni-Base Superalloys
Temperature-dependent crystal viscoplasticity models are ideal for modeling large-
grained, directionaiiy solidified Ni-base superalloys but are computationally expensive.
This work explores the use of reduced-order models that are potentially more efficient
with similar predictive capability of capturing temperature and orientation dependence.
First, a transversely isotropic viscoplasticity model is calibrated to a directionaiiy solidi-
fled Ni-base superalloy using the response predicted by a crystal viscoplasticity model.
The unifled macroscale model is capable of capturing isothermal and thermomechanical
responses in addition to secondary creep behavior over the temperature range of
20-1050°C. A second approach is an extreme reduced-order microstructure-sensitive
constitutive model that uses an artificial neural network to provide a set of parameters
that depend on orientation, temperature, and strain rate to give a flrst-order
approximation of the material response using a simple constitutive model. This simple
relationship is then used in a Neuber-type fatigue notch analysis to predict the local
response. [DOI: 10.1115/1.4026271]
Keywords: reduced-order modeling, Ni-base superalloy, fatigue notch analysis, thermo-
mechanical fatigue, artiflcial neural network
Introduction
Hot section components of land-based gas turbines are subject
to extremely harsh, high temperature environments and require
the use of advanced materials. Directionaiiy solidified (DS) Ni-
base superalloys are often chosen as materials for these hot sec-
tion components due to their excellent creep resistance and fatigue
properties at high temperatures when loaded in the grain growth
direction. Since the crystallographic grains are large and the me-
chanical behavior strongly depends on their orientation, crystal
viscoplasticity (CVP) models are ideal for accurately representing
the physics of the cyclic deformation response of these materials
[1-10]. However, these constitutive models are computationally
expensive especially when extending to predict the effect of
uncertainties in process and microstmcture on the component
behavior.
CVP models relate observed macroscopic deformation to defor-
mation occurring at a microscopic scale by explicitly taking into
account three-dimensional grain structure and individual slip sys-
tems of the material. Since the shearing rates on each slip system
must be evaluated, the computational expense related to imple-
menting a CVP model is one of its primary drawbacks. A recent
model developed by Shenoy et al. [6] with an improved embedded
multigrain (MG) feature to predict the off-axis response using a
Taylor approximation describes the macroscopic viscoplastic
response of DS Ni-base superalloys. Modeling multiple grains is
necessary to correctly capture the anisotropic response of a DS
alloy. Unfortunately, the multigrain feature makes the model
more computationally expensive because at least six grains are
needed to approximate an off-axis response at each integration
point [8].
For design and analysis, it is desirable to have a model with
similar dependence on microstructure as CVP with less computa-
tional expense. One approach is capturing crystal plasticity

where elastic strains can be neglected. Therefore, they are not
presently suitable for thermomechanical fatigue (TMF) without
further development.
Another approach for DS alloys is to use a macroscale trans-
versely isotropic viscoplasticity (TIVP) model for a DS Ni-base
superalloy based on homogenizing the response of several grains
with random secondary orientation [12]. This macroscale model
has improved computational efficiency compared to a multigrain
CVP model while still capturing the aggregate material response
from thermomechanical fatigue experiments, as well as capturing
secondary creep behavior at high temperatures. In addition to
averaging the response of multiple grains instead of explicitly
modeling the response of multiple grains and aggregating them to-
gether, another drawback to this model is that it cannot readily
capture tension-compression asymmetry, which could be signifi-
cant near transition temperatures [14].
Other attempts at reduced-order constitutive models involve the
use of artificial neural networks (ANNs) to model material behav-
ior. These networks are able to automatically produce nonlinear
mappings between multiple input and output data through learning
from a so-called training set, and are able to generalize well for
new data not appearing in the training set while requiring minimal
computational resources [15]. ANNs are used to either predict the
incremental constitutive response directly [16-19] or provide the
values of the parameters of a predefined constitutive function
[9,20,21].
For example, the prediction of the incremental constitutive
response of viscoelastic solids (using a standard solid model for
viscoelasticity) have been determined using ANN in which the
output of the ANN gives the increment in stress after being given
the stress and strain values from the previous increment and the

A drawback of these incremental approaches is that they require
extensive amounts of experimental data to be used for training,
which can be expensive and time-consuming, negating any bene-

and generally do not provide any insight into the physical
processes occurring at the microstructural level.
ANNs have also been used to predict parameters of simple con-
stitutive relations, such as Ramberg-Osgood [20]. For example, to
model the high temperature creep behavior of a rotating compos-
ite Al-SiCp disk, an ANN was used to correlate radial and tangen-
tial stresses and strain rates to temperature, radial distance,
particle size and particle content of reinforcement, correlating
observed behavior at the macroscale with a parameter characteriz-
ing material at a smaller scale [21] using radial and tangential
stresses and strain rates. Combining these approaches, ANN is
used to predict parameters of a macroscale model given micro-
structural parameters [9]. In this case, the training set is comprised
of simulations from a more complex CVP model, where the
response is fitted to a simpler macroscale model with two-term
flow rule [22] for an initially isotropic material. This process is
repeated for each microstructure, and the microstructural parame-
ters and their corresponding macroscale model parameters are
used as a training set for the ANN. The network is then able to
generate approximate macroscale model parameters given any ar-
bitrary microstructure within the domain of the training set. The
advantage is that the extensive training set required for ANN can
be generated using less costly computational methods rather than
solely from experiments. Limitations of this approach are associ-
ated with the confidence in interpolating between microstructures,
including interpolated microstructures that may not be stable in a
thermodynamic sense [9]. In addition, potentially significant com-
putational expense is required in the initial stages of this process
in order to create an adequate training set for the ANN.
This paper explores reduced-order methods of predicting
temperature-dependent Ni-base superalloy behavior and making
comparisons between these reduced-order constitutive models and
ones that include additional microstructural attributes, but are
computationally expensive. This involves the calibration of a
TIVP model to a DS Ni-base superalloy using the response pre-
dicted by a multigrain CVP, the development of an extreme
reduced-order constitutive model to rapidly approximate material
behavior based on parameters obtained from a feed-forward ANN,
and a comparison of these methods in relation to a more complex
physics-based CVP model with regard to accuracy and computa-
tional expense.

Development of an Extreme Reduced-Order ANN Model.
For iterative design of components, a rapid first-order estimate
of material response is often useful than a detailed analysis
using computationally expensive microstructure-dependent crystal
viscoplasticity models. The ability to predict endpoint stresses
almost instantaneously under a variety of loading conditions can
be beneficial to designers, especially when strain-based life mod-
els are employed to predict component life. Current methods to
predict inelastic strain range and mean stresses, such as Neuber or
Glinka approaches, do not capture the effects of material anisot-
ropy or rate dependence, nor do they capture any microstructural
dependence. To address this need, an extreme reduced-order
microstructure-sensitive constitutive model is developed through
utilizing the function-fitting capabilities of a feedforward ANN.

Demonstration Cases
Cylindrically Notched Specimen Model. To extend the func-
tionality of the extreme reduced-order model to a component
level, a Neuber-type analysis method is proposed, outlined in
Fig. 11. The basis for this method is identifying the highest
stressed element in the component model and using it as a limiting
factor for the entire component. This involves conducting a trans-
versely isotropic elastic finite element analysis of the component
to determine the location of the highest stressed element. For
example, a model of a cylindrically notched specimen of similar
geometry that has been experimentally studied [35,36] is eval-
uated. The highest stressed element would likely occur on the sur-
face at some angle from the notch root in a DS alloy based on
these studies. Knowing that one side of the element is the free
surface, the direction of principal stresses is known, and the
Fig. 17 Nomenclature for notch surface elements
information for the highest stressed element concerning tempera-
ture, orientation, and strain rate may be passed into the ANN
model to give the approximate Ramberg-Osgood curve for that
element. These curve endpoints can be used to determine peak
endpoint stresses and strains for the element, in addition to mean
stresses. This information is used in a strain-based lifing approach
such as Neuber's rule.
Although plane stress conditions are assumed for uniaxial tests,
plane strain conditions may dominate at the notch root if the notch
radius is sufficiently small [31,37,38]. A plane strain modification
proposed by Dowling et al. [39] is used in conjunction with the
Neuber and Glinka relations to approximate the stress-strain
behavior of the critical element of a cylindrically notched speci-
men model. The notch geometry modeled, shown in Fig. 12, is
axisymmetric about the j-axis with notch corresponding to a theo-
retical stress concentration factor A'T = 2.0 based on isotropic
properties. The general-purpose finite element code ABAQUS [23] is
used as the solver. The finite element mesh is comprised of 4813
axially symmetric elements (type CAX4) with 4960 nodes. The
boundary conditions are such that a pressure is applied to the top
surface and symmetry is refiected across the bottom and leftmost
surfaces to represent an axially symmetric specimen.
To verify the approach, an isothermal simulation is conducted
using the two reduced-order approaches for a temperature of
950 °C under completely reversed conditions with a net section
stress amplitude of 250 MPa. The methodology outlined in Fig. 11
is implemented to approximate the stress-strain behavior of the
element with the highest von Mises stress in the elastic analysis.
The anisotropic elastic model in ABAQUS is used to generate the
pseudo elastic response for the notch analysis. The elastic modu-
lus and Poisson's ratio in the longitudinal and transverse orienta-
tions, taken from experiment at 950 °C, are given to ABAQUS to
define a transversely isotropic elastic material. These properties
are given in Table 2 for 950 °C. Using the nodal locations of the
element with highest von Mises stress, its orientation from the DS
axis (the Y-axis in this model) can be determined and fed into the
ANN to determine the Ramberg-Osgood parameters defining the
relation between the principal stresses and strains. These parame-
ters are then used in the Neuber-type analysis to approximate the
local response of the element. The response at this same location
is compared to the prediction of the TIVP model using the same
finite element mesh. A comparison between the TIVP and ANN
model predictions using Neuber's relation with and without modi-
fication for plane strain is shown in Fig. 13. The modification for
plane strain appears to match more closely to the TIVP model
predictions as expected [31,37,38].

It is evident that there are noticeable, although small, differen-
ces between the ANN-based model and the TIVP model to which
it was trained. These differences probably arise from error
associated with the ability of the ANN to generalize the
Ramberg-Osgood parameters for arbitrary off-axis orientations.
The responses of the elements are best characterized by plane
stress, which is expected with the elements on the thin plate model
surface. Element responses under more complex states of stress
may not be captured well by the ANN model, since it is trained
using TIVP simulations under plane stress conditions and the
response in the near principal proportional loading direction is
used. This is an additional limitation of the ANN model. How-
ever, while it is less accurate than the CVP and TIVP models, the
ANN model is able to generate approximate stress-strain curves
almost instantaneously, given the elastic stresses from a purely
elastic finite element simulation, and thus provides a drastic
reduction in computational cost when compared to the CVP and
TIVP models, as shown in Table 3.
Conclusions and Prospects
• The TIVP model shows excellent speed benefits, around an
order of magnitude, when compared to the single crystal and
multigrain CVP models while still providing a high degree of
accuracy for modeling the macroscopic response of DS
alloys.
• The training process was repeated using the TIVP model to
construct a ID Ramberg-Osgood model with ANN-
generated parameters that are temperature-, rate-, and
orientation-dependent. This approach provides a first-order
approximation of material response almost instantaneously,
using results from a purely elastic analysis as input.
• While less accurate and not implemented as a user subroutine
for finite element codes, the ANN modeling technique shows
promise in certain phases of design where approximations of
local responses at stress concentrators are desired and speed
is of primary importance rather than accuracy.
This approach could be extended to include additional
microstructure-sensitive parameters by incorporating addi-
tional microstructural attribute inputs to the ANN model,
which could be trained using a microstructure-sensitive
physics-based model, such as a CVP model.
