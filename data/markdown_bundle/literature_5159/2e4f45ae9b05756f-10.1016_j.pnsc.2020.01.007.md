ABSTRACT
In order to assess Al –Zn–Mg–Cu alloys as potential high temperature structural materials, the hardness, tensile
properties and fracture behaviors of 7085 Al alloy were investigated at various temperatures from room tem-
perature to 175 °C. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy was used to investigate the evolutions of
precipitates at di ﬀerent temperatures, particularly on the relationship between microstructural evolution and
tensile strength. The results reveal that both the microstructure and mechanical properties of the alloy are quite
sensitive to the environmental temperature. As the temperature increases, the hardness and strength decrease
while the elongation and reduction of area increase. As tensile testing temperature rises, the strain hardening
exponent ( n) decreases due to the thermal softening eﬀ ect. The fracture mode of the alloy transforms from
mixture of intergranular and transgranular fracture to completely transgranular dimples when tensile testing
temperature reaches 150 °C. The precipitate sequence during high temperature tests is coincident with that of
aging. With the increase of tensile testing temperature, the mean precipitate radius grows larger, and the dis-
tribution of grain boundary precipitates transforms from continuous to discontinuous.
1. Introduction
Al–Zn–Mg–Cu alloys, one of the most important engineering mate-
rials in aerospace industries, have been widely used for aircraft com-
ponents due to their high modulus, low density and excellent stress
corrosion resistance [ 1–4]. However, the high quenching sensitivity has
restricted their applications in large scale products, such as bulk for-
gings and thick plates. 7085 Al alloy was developed by Alcoa as a new
generation of high strength thick plate alloy, which has lower quench
sensitivity and higher fracture toughness than that of other 7xxx series
alloys [ 5,6]. T7452 treatment (solution heat treatment, then 5% per-
manent compression deformation and two-step aging) has been re-
ported to increase both the stress corrosion resistance and fracture
toughness of 7085 Al alloy [ 7,8]. The 7085-T7452 Al alloy has a broad
range of applications in advanced aircrafts, such as A380 airplane [ 5,9].
The relatively high contents of alloying elements can result in high
strength of 7085 Al alloy at room temperature (RT), but deteriorate its
hot workability at the same time [ 10]. The 7085 Al alloy was often used
as structure airframe parts under instant slightly elevated temperature
(83–177 °C) during service [ 11,12]. Therefore, the hot deformation
behaviors of 7085 Al alloys becomes important to optimize the thermo-
mechanical parameters, and further to avoid defects during hot
forming.
In recent years, much attention has been paid to the di ﬀerent mi-
crostructure and mechanical behavior of 7xxx series Al alloys [ 13–18].
For example, Paulisch et al. [ 14] investigated the 7175 Al alloy after
hot extrusion and subsequent natural ageing. The results showed that
low melting Al
2CuMg particles are detrimental to subsequent hot de-
formation due to the hazard of re-melting. Teng et al. [ 16] studied the
eﬀect of minor Sc addition on the microstructure and mechanical
properties of 7055 Al alloy during aging. They found that the primary
Al
3(Sc,Zr) phases formed during the solidi ﬁcation can act as hetero-
geneous nucleation sites, and also serve as grain boundary (GB) pinning
agent for Al grains, further to result in a ﬁne microstructure with the
improved mechanical properties.
Several studies have also been carried out on the 7085 Al alloy since
2002, but most of them focused mainly on the quench sensitivity and
mechanical properties at RT. Nie et al. [ 9] investigated the quenching
sensitivity of 7050 and 7085 Al alloys. Their results revealed that 7085
alloy had a lower quenching sensitivity than that of 7050 alloy, due to
the lower density of the precipitates both around the Al
3Zr particles and
along the grain boundaries (GBs). Chen et al. [ 19] reported that

2. Experimental procedure
The material used in this study was the forged plate of 7085 Al alloy
whose chemical composition was Al-7.56Zn-1.50Mg-1.45Cu-0.12Zr-
0.06Fe-0.02Si (wt%). T7452 heat treatment involving a solution
treating for 470 °C/6 h, followed by a cold compression for 5%, and a
two-step aging of 120 °C/6 h + 160 °C/10 h was carried out for the
plate. After T7452 heat treatment, the specimens for subsequent mi-
crostructure characterization and mechanical properties tests were cut
from the 7085-T7452 plate (about 220 mm thick) at the position of 1/2
in thickness.
Vickers hardness (HV) was tested on HMV-2 machine with a load of
0.5 Kg for 15 s. The test sample was machined to the cylindrical bars
with a diameter of 5 mm and a length of 25 mm according to the tensile
test standard ISO 6892-1. The tensile tests were carried out on a ma-
terial testing machine (Instron 3382) at RT, 100 °C, 125 °C, 150 °C and
175 °C, respectively with the accuracy of ± 5 °C. The tensile properties
were the average values of ﬁve measurements. The fracture surfaces of
the samples were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI
Nova 450) in a secondary electron imaging mode with an energy dis-
persive spectrometer (EDS).
The specimens for optical microscopy (OM) observations were
etched with Keller's reagent in a solution containing 1 ml HF, 1.5 ml
HCl, 2.5 ml HNO
3 and 95 ml H 2O. The precipitates and morphology of
GB were characterized using a 300 kV Tecnai F30 G 2 transmission
electron microscopy (TEM).

and η precipitates become coarsened.

3.5. E ﬀect of tensile test temperatures on strengthening mechanisms
The yield strength of 7xxx Al alloys is determined by the resistance
of dislocation movement. In 7085 Al alloy, both the activation energy
for dislocation movement and pining e ﬀect of precipitates on the dis-
location a ﬀect the yield strength of 7085 alloy.
Fig. 8 gives the statistical results of precipitates radii of the alloy
after tensile tests at di ﬀerent temperatures. The average radius of the
precipitates increases rapidly as the temperature rises. Meanwhile, both
the hardness and strength show an approximately linear decrease in
Fig. 2.
In Al –Zn–Mg–Cu alloys, the main strengthening mechanism is pre-

7xxx alloys would transform to an Orowan dislocation bypass me-
chanism when the precipitate radius increases to about 3 nm [ 8,30,31].

125 °C, the number fractions of precipitates radius less than 3 nm oc-
cupy only 1% and 0.05%, respectively. Bypassing precipitates is con-

3.6. E ﬀect of tensile testing temperature on fracture behavior
The fracture behavior of the Al –Zn–Mg–Cu alloys is associated with
the tensile testing temperature and microstructure evolution. At RT, the
dislocation pile-up and multiplication ﬁrstly occurs at GBs and coarse
particles in grains, which is prone to induce stress concentration. And
then leads to the formation of major crack by microcrack initiation and
propagation. As a result, the fracture surface is a mixed mode with a
large number of intergranular fracture and a few ﬁne scale dimples.
When the tensile testing temperature is 100 °C, both the thermal
activation and thermal softening e ﬀect of the 7085 Al alloy increase.
However, the strength di ﬀerence between GB and grain interior is still
obvious. Therefore, the brittle intergranular fracture still occupies a
large fraction. Moreover, the softening e ﬀect of precipitates/matrix
leads to the larger size of dimples compared with that in the alloy at RT.
As the tensile testing temperature increases to 125 °C, the thermal
activation e ﬀect further enhanced. Microcracks generate at inclusions
and precipitate boundaries, and then grow to form dimples. Hence, the
brittle intergranular fracture characteristics weakened and the number

4. Conclusions
(1) As tensile testing temperature rises, the strain hardening exponent
(n) value decreases due to the thermal softening phenomenon.
(2) The increase in the amount of equilibrium η phase and the pre-
cipitate size are the main reasons for the decrease of strength and
hardness of the alloy.
(3) The fracture mode of the alloy transforms from mixture of inter-
granular and transgranular to completely transgranular dimples
when tensile testing temperature reaches 150 °C.
(4) With the increase of tensile testing temperature, the enhanced
thermal activation makes the sizes of the GBPs increase and the PFZ
width become wider, which results in the larger size of the dimples
and the enhancement of plasticity of the alloy.
