Mechanical Testing of Dual Alloy Inertia Friction Welded Shafts 
W. S. Robotham 1, T. H. Hyde 2, E. J. Williams 2, P. Brown 2, I. R. McColl 2 
and C. J. Kong 2 
1 Rolls-Royce Aerospace plc, PO Box 31, Derby, UK, william_Robotham@yahoo.co.uk. 
2 School of Mechanical, Materials, Manufacturing Engineering and Management, University of 
Nottingham, University Park, Nottingham, NG7 2RD, UK, Edward.Williams@nottingham.ac.uk. 
Keywords 
: Fatigue, Shaft, Inertia Welding, Dual Alloy 
 
Abstract. The development of aeroengines with increasing thrust capabilities requires the 
development of shaft technology to deal with this greater power transmission, whilst still restricting 
their dimensions and weight. Modern aerospace drive shafts are predominantly of a single-alloy 
design and significant benefits could be obtained from using a dual alloy shaft, where a high 
temperature alloy is used at the turbine, i.e. hot, end of the shaft and a high strength alloy is used for 
the spline end of the shaft, where high strength is required, rather than high temperature 
performance. Whilst the processes of joining dissimilar materials are widely used the evolution of 
the joint and its strength characteristics are not fully understood. A program of research has been 
instigated to lead to an improved understanding of friction welds and their behaviour under 
monotonic and cyclic loadings with the overall objective to establish confidence in the welding 
parameters for these material combinations and the associated post-weld heat treatments. This paper 
presents an overview of the mechanical testing program and the aims of this work, illustrated with 
some examples from the monotonic and cyclic test work carried out on inertia friction welded dual 
alloy shaft components. 
Introduction 
Aerospace drive shafts are predominantly of a single-alloy design and this has limitations in two 
areas. The first of these problems is that a shaft maybe subjected to high temperatures, ~300°C, at 
the end connected to the turbine, whereas the end connected to the compressor will be at a much 
lower temperature, ~150°C [1]. This creates a dilemma for the designer, as there are different 
material performance criteria at the shaft’s respective ends. For the end that connects to the turbine 
good performance is required at high temperatures, whereas at the spline end of the shaft high 
strength is required [2]. The latter problem is where the shaft mates with the compressor and high 
strength and other specific properties are required at the spline to cope with the massive loads and 
inherent problems of these couplings [2], where as the rest of the shaft does not require such a 
material and a cheaper alloy can be utilised saving costs. It is this later problem that we are 
addressing within this paper. 
 
A solution to both of these problems is to use a dual alloy shaft, i.e. a shaft that combines two 
dissimilar materials, thus using the particular strengths of each material to solve the problems 
outlined in the previous paragraphs. Probably the best manufacturing technique for joining 
dissimilar materials for an aeroengine application is rotary inertia friction welding. Thus, significant 
benefits could be obtained from using a dual alloy shaft. The aim of this work is to develop shaft 
technology to enable greater shaft power transmission through an improved understanding of the 
friction welding process and the behaviour of such components in service. This paper will present 
an overview of the mechanical testing work and also some illustrative test results from monotonic 
and cyclic tests of a dual alloy inertia welded shaft. This work is particularly important as the latest 
Rolls Royce engine will be using a dual alloy inertia friction welded shaft as a critical component 
and this work is vital in ensuring the success of this component in the engine.

Introduction to Friction Welding 
Friction welding is a solid-state welding process that is achieved through frictional heat between the 
welding components. Further details on the process can be found in the following references [3, 4, 
5]. A controlled rubbing of two components generates this heat until material in contact reaches a 
visco-plastic state, at which point the plasticised material begins to form layers that interconnect 
with one another. There are many different types of friction welding, such as linear orbital or linear 
friction stir welding. The friction welding process used to produce the test components used was 
rotary friction welding. There are two different types of rotary friction welding, these are a). 
Continuous drive friction welding, b). Stored energy friction welding (commonly known as inertia 
friction welding). In continuous drive friction welding the welding heat is obtained at the joint by 
rotating one part against the other at a constant or varied RPM, with an axial force applied to the 
mating components. Energy is provided to this joint from a continuously running prime mover (this 
would usually be an electric motor, directly connected to the machine spindle). This energy source 
is infinite with respect to time, and is supplied to the interface until the proper total heat is obtained. 
When this point is reached, the rotating members are stopped and a forging load is applied to the 
parts to be joined. This forging load helps to consolidate the weld and to refine the Heat Affected 
Zone, HAZ. This also expels material from the weld zone creating the classic flash material that is 
seen in these welds. This flash is removed after welding and then the welded component can be heat 
treated to further improve the bond properties. In inertia friction welding the process is very similar 
to continuous drive friction welding, however one of the work pieces is connected to a flywheel, 
which provides the kinetic energy, whilst the other is restrained from rotating. The flywheel is 
accelerated to a predetermined rotational speed and is then disengaged. At this point the work pieces 
are forced together and the stored energy in the rotating flywheel is dissipated as heat through 
friction at the weld interface. An increase in forge force may be applied before rotation stops 
Material Choices Rolls Royce have traditionally used SuperCMV for main shaft 
applications, this material is produced by Allvac and is a double vacuum re-melted Chrome, 
Molybdenum, Vanadium alloy. The materials constituents are 0.15% Vanadium, 3% Chromium, 
0.92% Molybdenum and the balance being Iron. However, with the increasing requirements for high 
torque density shafts in engine applications new materials need to be considered to meet these 
demands. The spline coupling torque loading is usually the limiting case and this is why for the 
choice of AerMet® 100 for the spline has been chosen. AerMet® 100 is produced by Carpenter 
Technology Corporation and is an alloy possessing high hardness and strength combined with 
exceptional ductility and toughness. The alloy is designed for components requiring high strength, 
high fracture toughness and exceptional resistance to stress corrosion cracking and fatigue. The 
materials constituents are 0.23% Carbon, 3.10% Chromium, 11.1% Nickel, 1.2% Molybdenum, 
13% Cobalt and the balance being Iron. The combination of these two materials, inertia welded 
together as a main line drive shaft, offers the torque capacities required and also the temperature 
performance needed at the turbine end of the shaft. 
Bond Characterisation 
It is generally know that the mechanical properties of welded materials vary drastically with 
distance from the welds. This is due the to the heat generated in the welding process raising the 
temperature and thus altering the microstructure of the materials in a zone around the weld, i.e. the 
HAZ. To understand the characteristics of an inertia friction weld it is useful to understand the 
various zones that developing from the process. For rotary inertia friction welds it is convenient to 
categorise the weld into the following zones:

• Undeformed zone – where no deformation has occurred and increase in temperature has not 
affected the material. 
• Partially deformed – where partial deformation has occurred due to the raised temperature of the 
material. 
• Fully plastic zone – where the material has become fully softened due to the rise in temperature 
and extensive deformation has occurred. 
 
Looking at a generalised picture of the microstructure of these joints, we would not see any 
evidence of melting. The new weld structure in general would include a HAZ containing re-
crystallised, equiaxed grains and some heavily worked grains at the weld interface. However, this is 
an oversimplification, as when welding steels, especially alloys, it would be expected that in 
addition to changes in grain size and shape the extremely rapid cooling rates that occur would create 
transformation products within the weld. 
 
The general characteristics of the weld are affected by machine and non-machine parameters, i.e. the 
materials that are to be joined have an effect on the final structure as well as the forces used to join 
the materials. With regard to the machine parameters, the rotational speed and pressure have an 
influence on the width and the shape of the heat-affected zone. In general, higher pressures tend to 
compress the HAZ, especially at the centre, heavily working the interfacial material and causing a 
notch effect at the flash junction. Higher speeds tend to increase the width of the HAZ and also the 
grain size. Subsequent use of high pressure forging after spindle stop is used to work the structure 
and refine the grain size.

Mechanical Testing 
The aim of the mechanical testing is to a). Determine the ultimate strength of the finished dual alloy 
welded shaft under monotonic torsional and axial loads, b). Determine the fatigue performance of 
dual alloy welded shafts under cyclic torsional and axial loads and c). Provide suitable ultimate 
strength and fatigue failure examples for use in FE analyses. As well as establishing confidence in 
the welding parameters, increasing the torsional capacity of these shaft components is a major 
objective and these tests are aimed at proving that the inertia friction welds are capable of sustaining 
increases in the current torsional fatigue capacity, which means a surface torsional shear stress of at 
least 500 MPa across the weld. 
Component geometry The nominal geometry of the dual alloy test components is shown in 
Figure 2. The geometry was designed so that considering the strengths of the materials used and the 
load restrictions imposed by the rig, i.e. the maximum torque capacity allowed by the hydraulic 
jacks, suitable loads could be applied to the components for both the fatigue and the ultimate 
strength tests. These components were machined to the finished shape, shown in Figure 2, after the 
welding process. 
 
 
Fig 2: Dual Alloy Shaft Geometry 
Loading regime The load regime for each test was the same, whether it was fatigue or 
ultimate strength, in that both torsional and axial tension loads were applied simultaneously 
(proportional loading). For the monotonic tests the maximum load to be applied was ramped up 
over a 30 second period. The cyclic loading cycle for the components consisted of a ramp up of the 
Torsional and axial loads to the full value over two seconds, followed by a dwell period of 4 
seconds and then a ramp down of 2 seconds. There was a dwell of 2 seconds at a minimum load and 
then the cycle was repeated. Both the Torsional and axial tension loads were ramped at the same 
time. The axial load applied in a proportionate value of the torque loading, i.e. that the ratio of 
torque and axial load applied were comparable to the loading ratios that an actual Aeroengine shaft 
would see in service. This is on average a figure of approximately 4, i.e. if 1000Nm of torque were 
applied then the corresponding axial load would be 4000N. It should be noted that all of these tests 
were load controlled. 
Test rig The experimental work was completed on an existing testing rig designed and built 
to test Rolls-Royce splines. The rig consists, essentially, of a heavy base plate and framework. From 
the top of the framework a crucifix is suspended on four hydraulic jacks and two hydraulic jacks are 
attached to the sides of the crucifix from the base plate. The crucifix is suspended from four jacks, 
that produce the compressive, tensile and bending loads. The two jacks attached to the crucifix from

The rig is fully automated with two dedicated PC’s, one controlling the test conditions and

controlled. This allows the test components to be maintained at a constant temperature and thus 
avoid thermal expansion. Loads and deflections were measured from calibrated load cells and

Conclusions 
The inertia friction welded components tested have shown significant monotonic and fatigue 
strength, with tests showing a component subject to a 700MPa shear stress fatigue load being 
unfailed after 100,000 cycles. These tests have proved that the AerMet-SCMV inertia friction welds 
are capable of sustaining the loads that would be required for service within an aeroengine. All of 
the components tested failed consistently in the same location and in the same manner, which is due 
to the HAZ in the SCMV side of the weld acting as a consistent failure location. Production welds 
would be sited in lands, which would be required for the purpose of inertia welding, where the 
overall stresses would be lower than the components tested here.
