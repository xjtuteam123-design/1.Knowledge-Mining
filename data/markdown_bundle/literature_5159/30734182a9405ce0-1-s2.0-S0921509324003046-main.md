ABSTRACT 
The important factors limiting the fourth-generation Ni-based single-crystal superalloys (Ni-SXs) are the in -
creases in cost and density. A cheaper and lighter potential alternative element of Ru, Co ’ s comprehensive effects 
were studied. The effects of Co and inhomogeneity between dendrite core (DC) and interdendritic region (ID) on 
creep performance at 1100 
◦
C and 137 MPa in a 4th generation Ni-SXs were systematically investigated. The 
results show that the Ni-SXs with 9 wt% Co has the longest creep lifetime. Co additions alleviate the overall 
element segregations between DC and ID, especially Re, Cr, and W. And reverse partitioning behavior of Co also 
changes the partitioning ratio of elements between γ and γ ′ phases, resulting in the inhibition of TCP phases 
precipitation for most creep time to avoid excessive consumption of solid solution strengthening elements and 
local stress concentration. The decrease of the lattice constant of γ reduces lattice misfit between γ and γ ′ with the 
addition of Co, leading to the enlargement of dislocation network spacing and less resistance for movement or 
shearing of dislocation. The decrease of γ ′ solvus temperature with Co addition promotes the dissolution of γ ′ at 
high working temperature, resulting in severe topological inversion of γ / γ ′ microstructures at ID. In addition, the 
main creep cracks of all Ni-SXs appear at ID, so the inhomogeneity between DC and ID is concerned. 
1. Introduction 
Ni-based single crystal superalloys (Ni-SXs) are widely used as tur -
bine blade materials for modern aero and gas turbines because of their 
excellent capability to sustain the harsh environment of high tempera -
ture creep, fatigue, and oxidation [ 1 – 3 ]. Ni-SXs are strengthened by the 
internal two-phase microstructure comprising around 70% precipitation 
γ ′ phase in the matrix of γ solid solution to meet long-term mechanical 
loads at high temperatures [ 4 – 7 ]. To increase the temperature capacity 
of the two-phase microstructure, advanced, high-generation Ni-SXs are 
applied increasing refractory elements, like Re, W, and Mo [ 1 , 8 – 10 ]. For 
example, the content of refractory elements of the Ren ´e series has 
increased from 12 wt% in Ren ´e N4 (1st) to 20 wt% in Ren ´e N6 (3rd) [ 11 , 
12 ]. The refractory elements diffused slowly in Ni improve the micro -
structural stability to certain extents and thus determine the overall 
performance of the alloy [ 13 – 15 ]. However, the refractory elements also 
facilitate the precipitation of topologically close-packed (TCP) phases, 
which degrade strongly microstructure stability as the depletion of 
strengthening elements and the easiness of inducing stress concentration 
in the surrounding [ 16 – 18 ]. TCP phases usually precipitate at dendrite 
core (DC) resulting in severe dendrite segregation and inhomogeneous 
strength. The platinum group element Ru, appeared at the 
fourth-generation Ni-SXs, suppressing the precipitation of TCP phases to 
enhance the creep properties, due to the so-called “ Ru effects ” . One of 
the reasons for the suppression of TCP phases is the “ reverse partition -
ing ” of elements by the addition of Ru [ 19 ], i.e. the strong partition of 
the Ru in the γ matrix determines the higher solubility of γ -rich elements 
in the γ ′ phase [ 20 ]. Such the effect determines the lower supersatura -
tion of the formative elements of TCP phases and the higher misfit en -
ergy of the TCP/ γ interface, which results in the increased difficulty for

The increased additions of refractory elements, especially Re, and 
TCP- suppressing element Ru become the most typical compositional 
characteristic of advanced, high-generation Ni-SXs. However, the 
excessive additions of the elements inevitably give rise to the increased 
density and costs of the alloy. More important is, that although a high 
content of Ru is applied, the precipitation of TCP phases is still un -
avoidable in those high-generation alloys as the increased service tem -
perature [ 22 ]. Apart from Ru, recent studies revealed that Co also plays 
a role in restricting the precipitation of TCP phases [ 23 – 26 ]. The 
dominant view is that Co plays similar a role to Ru by changing the 
partitions of other elements, that is, the partitioning ratios of 
TCP-forming elements, like Re, W, Mo, and Cr, were reduced [ 26 , 27 ]. 
Furthermore, Co decreases the lattice parameters of γ to increase the 
TCP/ γ interface energy, promotes the diffusion of TCP-forming ele -
ments, and decreases the nucleation rate of σ phase [ 28 – 32 ]. However, 
there were some contradictions in whether Co can inhibit TCP phases 
[ 27 , 33 , 34 ]. With the characteristics of low-density and low-cost, Co ’ s 
potential as the substitution of Ru needs to be further tested in high 
generation Ni-SXs. 
To this end, the present work carried out critical investigations of the 
effects of Co additions in an advanced fourth-generation Ni-SXs. The 
solidification characteristics, as-cast and heat-treated microstructure as 
well as the high temperature and low stress performance were 
comprehensively compared in alloys with different Co additions (5.9 wt 
%, 9.0 wt%, and 12.0 wt%). It was revealed that Co additions strongly 
affect the macro- and micro-scale elemental homogeneity, thus deter -
mining the different microstructure evolution in the local area within 
the DC and interdendritic region (ID), the precipitation of TCP phases, 
the nucleation of microcracks and the corresponding creep behaviors. In 
the case that the total amount of Co + Ru is unchanged, using 0.5 wt%Co 
to replace 0.5 wt%Ru will reduce the density of about 11 kg/m

cost of about 80 $/kg. Therefore, it is meaningful for low-cost and low- 
density Ni-SXs to discuss the possibility of Co replacing Ru.

3.7. Evolution of dislocation in the creep 
Fig. 13 shows the STEM images of the evolution of dislocation 
configuration during the creep, taken from transverse sections of the Ni- 
SXs under the rigid beam direction parallel to [001]. The dislocation 
movement behavior is quite different at different creep stages. The 
dislocations gather in the narrow γ channels at the primary creep (as 
shown by the red arrow), which impedes dislocation motion and reduces 
strain rate, as shown in Figs. 13 (a) and Fig. 8 (b). At secondary creep, 
most of dislocations still move in γ matrix and are restricted by γ / γ ′ 
interface, and a few of them shear in γ ′ phase to form superdislocations 
(see the green arrow), which destroy the integrity and continuity of the 
dislocation network, as shown the green arrows in Fig. 13 (b) and (h). A 
large number of dislocations break through the limit of γ / γ ′ interface and 
shear into γ ′ phase at tertiary creep. In Fig. 13 (c) and (i), near in TCP 
phases, the γ ′ phase is more likely to be sheared into by dislocations, due 
to the stress concentration caused. The smoother movement of disloca -
tions promotes topological inversion of γ / γ ′ microstructures and redis -
solution of γ ′ precipitates, resulting in a faster creep strain rate [ 52 , 53 ].

4.2. Elemental distribution 
Two scales of elemental distributions are common in Ni-SXs, and 
greatly affect microstructures and creep properties. The macro-scale 
elemental distribution heterogeneity in as-cast alloy between DC and 
ID is sharply eliminated after heat treatment from Fig. 2 . From the re -
sults in Fig. 5 , quantification shows that the s
i 
of negative segregation 
elements ( s
i 
> 1), such as Re, Cr, and W, are lowered by approximately 
92% – 28%, and those of positive segregation elements ( s
i 
< 1) by about 
32% – 7% from the results in Fig. 5 . The role of heat treatment is unde -
niable, furthermore, the positive effect of Co cannot be neglected. In 
particular, for the elements with negative segregation, the increase of Co 
reduces overall segregations to boost the homogeneity impact of heat 
treatment. The homogenization originates from the effects of Co on the 
diffusion rate of elements in the matrix. The findings of our previous 
study have shown that solute diffusion through the matrix is accelerated 
since the diffusion activation energy dropped from 199 to 154 kJ/mol 
when Co addition increased from 5.9 to 12 wt% [ 58 ]. The calculation 
results of Fu et al. [ 59 ] revealed that the diffusion activation energy of 
Co in pure Ni is greatly lower than Re and Ru. 
Nevertheless, the relatively homogeneous elemental distribution 
between DC and ID cannot be maintained throughout the creep process. 
The fact that TCP phases only precipitate at DC, indicated an intensifi -
cation of segregation of the elements, is one crucial piece of evidence. 
Between DC and ID, there may be an uphill diffusion of elements, which 
could lead to an excess of solution-strengthening elements at DC and the 
precipitation of TCP phases. Moreover, some elements, such as Re and 
Cr, perform poorly at ID, which weakens the local strength and causes 
the origination of creep cracks finally (see Fig. 9 ). However, prior 
research has shown that only Cr, Co, and Re segregate in dislocation and 
are transported to DC by pipe diffusion, resulting the uphill diffusion 
[ 48 , 60 ]. It is necessary to learn more about the diffusion mechanisms of 
additional elements, such as Mo, Ru, Al, and Ta. Therefore, the longer 
creep life of 9Co alloy may benefit from both reduced Co segregation in 
dislocation compared to 12Co alloy and more homogenous element 
distribution in heat-treated microstructures compared to 5.9Co alloy. 
The small-scale elemental partitioning heterogeneity behavior be -
tween γ and γ ′ phase also affects microstructures significantly. Al and Ta 
are abundant in γ ′ precipitates, and the elements Co, Cr, Re, and Mo 
partition to the matrix (see Fig. 6 (a)). Comparing the k values of ele -
ments in the alloys, the difference in partitioning behavior can be traced 
to the Co additions. The reverse partitioning behavior of the elements is 
that k

phases, which inhibits the precipitation of the TCP phase (see Fig. 10 (b 
and c)). The precipitation of TCP phases destroys creep resistance in two 
ways. First, the matching relationship between strength and plasticity is 
damaged by needle-like TCP phases at DC. Around the TCP phase, there 
are γ poor areas where local brittleness rises. Secondly, the local strength 
at ID is weakened, caused by excessive loss of solid solution strength -
ened elements (e.g. Re and Cr). In addition, the lattice constant of γ and 
γ ′ changes with the composition of γ and γ ′ , thus altering the lattice 
misfit. 
4.3. γ / γ ′ microstructures 
Following the rather reasonable heat treatment schedule, the regular 
and homogenous γ / γ ′ microstructures were modified, as depicted in 
Fig. 4 . However, the microstructures destabilize rapidly during creep at 
such high temperatures. Especially, not only coarsening, γ ′ phase has 
been coalesced and even rafted at the middle of primary creep (see 
Fig. 10 ), indicating that the initial cubic γ ′ embedding matrix micro -
structures are not a major defense against creep deformation. The rafting 
microstructures resist most life of creep, especially at the secondary 
creep. The driving force of rafting is local lattice misfits between γ and γ ′ , 
and the mechanism was revealed by previous researchers. The recipe for 
maintaining microstructures stability is dense and balanced dislocation 
networks during steady-state creep (as shown in Fig. 13(b – e, h) ), which 
prevents the dislocation from moving through the matrix and entering 
the γ ′ phase [ 61 ]. The rafting microstructures begin topological inver -
sion with the accumulation of strain, which is regarded as great struc -
tural instability [ 62 ]. The large number of topological inversion 
behaviors is symbolic of the beginning of the tertiary creep. Dislocations 
gather in the isolated γ phase, resulting in local stress concentration. The 
dislocations have to enter in γ ′ phase and the deformation can continue 
until creep rupture. The emergence of abundant shearing events in γ ′ 
phase is shown in Fig. 13(c – f, i) , which is an important reason for the 
rapid increase of strain rate at tertiary creep. 
The difference of γ / γ ′ microstructures between DC and ID has been 
eliminated by heat treatment as much as possible, and microstructures 
characteristic parameters difference can be ignored, as illustrated in 
Table 2 . However, the difference is magnified during creep. The coars -
ening and coalescence behavior of γ ′ phase at DC is earlier than that at ID 
(see Fig. 9 ), indicated by the larger topological inversion coefficient at 
DC (listed in Table 4 ). Negative segregation elements with larger atomic

4.4. The comprehensive effects and perspective 
In the present work, the effects of Co additions on creep properties of 
a 4th generation Ni-SXs under high temperature and low stress was 
studied, and the comprehensive Co-effect could be divided into the 
following four aspects, and characterized by Fig. 15 . 
(a) The addition of Co balances the elemental distribution between 
DC and ID to avoid premature weak regions, as demonstrated by 
the results of Shi et al. [ 65 ]. And reverse partitioning behavior of 
Co equalizes the composition difference between the γ and γ ′ 
phases, not only inhibits the precipitation of TCP phases but also 
delays the evolution of γ / γ ′ microstructure [ 66 ]. 
(b) The precipitation of TCP phases is inhibited for most time at high 
temperatures with or without stress after increasing Co [ 58 , 67 ]. 
The excessive consumption of solid solution strengthening ele -
ments is avoided, and the stress concentration near the TCP phase 
is alleviated. 
(c) Co content has little effect on the content of γ ′ in heat-treated Ni- 
SXs but promotes the dissolution of γ ′ at high working tempera -
tures [ 29 ]. The decrease of γ ′ solvus temperature and the resis -
tance of γ / γ ′ interface dislocations network are the main causes. 
(d) The lattice misfit between γ and γ ′ is reduced with increasing Co 
at RT or high temperature, which postpones the proliferation rate 
of dislocation at primary creep but promotes the movement of 
dislocations in the matrix, due to larger dislocation network 
spacing [ 61 ]. To sum up, the advantages and disadvantages of Co 
addition affect the creep properties of the alloy, so that there is a 
best Co content in Ni-SXs. For example, Tan et al. have shown that 
the creep life of Ni-SXs was longest at 1140 
◦
C/137 MPa when 
containing 12 wt% Co [ 68 ]. 
An important factor limiting the application of 4th generation Ni-SXs 
is the increase in both cost and weight. The effects of Co on micro -
structure stability, especially the precipitation of TCP phases, are similar 
to Ru ’ s effects. Furthermore, the unit price of Co is about 0.5% of Ru, 
and the density of Co (8.9 g/cm
3
) is lighter than Ru (12.45 g/cm
3
). 
Based on these advantages, it is possible to have a suitable Co content to 
replace part of Ru to meet the low-cost and low-density Ni-SXs design 
without reducing creep performance, which is exactly the next step to 
study. In addition, the inhomogeneity between DC and ID requires 
attention for any Ni-SXs. Previous studies have shown that the temper -
ature and time of heat treatment strongly affect creep properties [ 70 ]. 
The key point of heat treatment is to homogenize Ni-SXs to avoid pre -
mature crack initiation at ID. For example, the research of Xia et al. [ 35 ] 
showed that the creep lifetime is prolonged by more than 100 h at 
1100 
◦
C/137 MPa by extending the solution heat treatment time to 
prevent crack initiation from ID.
