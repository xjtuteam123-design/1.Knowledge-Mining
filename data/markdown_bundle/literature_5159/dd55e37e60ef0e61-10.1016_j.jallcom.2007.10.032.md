Abstract
The eutectic alloy Al3(Nb,Cr) + Cr(Al,Nb) forms anin situ composite and the Al3Nb presents high speciﬁc strength and low oxidation rate that
may be improved by the combination with other phases. The purpose of this work is to investigate physical, mechanical and oxidation properties of
the eutectic alloy. Therefore, Rietveld analysis was carried out for furnace cooled and water quenched samples and oxidation tests were performed
on directional solidiﬁed samples. Compressive tests were performed for the eutectic alloy and also for the Nb–74.8% Cr–24.6% Al alloy in
the as-cast condition. The alloy presents 12.9% Cr(Al,Nb) at room temperature, retained from the transformation Cr(Al,Nb) to Al(Nb)Cr
2. The
combination of Al 3Nb with Cr(Al,Nb) and Al(Nb)Cr 2 considerably improves mechanical behaviour, leading the yield strength to 1525 MPa at
800 ◦C and 925 MPa at 900◦C. The oxidation tests showed the formation of several oxides at all temperatures studied and that from 900◦C on alfa
Al2O3 is formed both in air and O2 except under O2 at 1000◦C. It is believed that the Cr(Al,Nb) phase acts as an Al reservoir for the formation of
the various Al2O3 scales.

1. Introduction
The eutectic alloys offer the possibility of obtaining in situ
composites consisting of two phases, the matrix and the rein-
forcing phase. This idea has been searched since the 1970s
[1,2] and nowadays it deserves particular interest when dealing
with intermetallic-based eutectics for high temperature struc-
tural applications [3–5]. The intermetallic phases present a
very particular mechanical behaviour, and when aluminides are
considered one may expect high speciﬁc strength and better
resistance to corrosion and oxidation [6–8]. Among the Nb-
based intermetallics, Al
3Nb presents the lowest oxidation rate
[9], although it is susceptible to intergranular disintegration in
oxidizing atmospheres at temperatures between 700 and 900◦C
[10,11] and, it was reported that its combination with other tran-
sition metals as well as with other phases markedly inﬂuences
its mechanical and oxidation behaviour [6,8].

Insufﬁcient slip systems determine the brittle behaviour of
Al3Nb [12] and twinning is rather important for its plastic
deformation. Also, twinning is remarkably difﬁcult at low tem-
peratures. At high temperatures, on the other hand, in addition
to twinning, slip is improved by the thermal activated slip sys-
tems that result in higher ductility [13]. The low ductility of the
intermetallic phases is attributed to the strong atomic bonding
and the ordered atomic distribution that leads to low symmetry
structures [14].
When studying the Nb–Cr–Al system in the 1970s Kauf-
man and Nesor [15] noticed the existence of a eutectic structure
that comprised Al
3Nb ﬁbres. Afterwards, studies were carried
out [16,17] on the purpose of determining composition, melt-
ing point and the constituent phases of the eutectic alloy. A
more recent work [18] reports that the eutectic composition is
near Nb–39.7% Cr–49.3% Al, the melting point is 1347.9
◦C
and the phases formed are Al3(Nb,Cr) and Cr(Al,Nb). Besides,
at 892.3 ◦C the solid solution Cr(Al,Nb) is transformed to
Al(Nb)Cr2. Therefore, the purpose of this work is to investigate
physical, mechanical and oxidation properties of the eutectic
alloy Al
3(Nb,Cr) + Cr(Al,Nb).

2. Experimental procedures
Two alloys were arc melted and named AC1 (Nb–39.7% Cr–49.3% Al) and
AC2 (Nb–74.8% Cr–24.6% Al). All compositions in this work are given in
atomic percentage (at.%). The details on the preparation of the ingots, prepara-
tion of the directionally solidiﬁed samples and metallographic preparation for
the alloy AC1 are seen elsewhere [18]. The directional solidiﬁcation (DS) was
carried out at 10 mm/h. Two AC1 as-cast samples were heat treated at 1000
◦ for
1 h, one of them was water quenched (WQ) and the other was furnace cooled
(FC). Afterwards the samples were crushed and the characterization by XRD
was performed using a Rigaku DMAX 2200 diffractometer (40 kV , 30 mA),
in Bragg–Brentano reﬂection geometry with Cu K /H9251radiation (λ = 1.5418 ˚A).
A secondary graphite monochromator allowed the complete removal of Cu K/H9252

the Rietveld method[19,20] using the GSAS program[21]. The elastic modulus
was determined using the pulse echo ultrasonic technique. Vickers hardness was
obtained using load of 500 g, and for fracture toughness determination, based on
the model of Blendell [22], 5000 g indentations were utilized. The compression
test specimens were 7.5 mm long and had 5 mm diameter, the tests were per-

Another result of the Rietveld reﬁnement is the determination
of the values of lattice parameters of the unit cells. The changes
of the lattice parameters of the constituent phases of the WQ and

The density of the phase Al
3(Nb,Cr) is increased (0.2% (FC)
and 0.3% (WQ)) when comparing with Al3Nb, and the negligi-
ble variation observed is attributed to the low solubility for Cr.
The maximum solubility of Cr in Al
3Nb at 1000 ◦C is 10.8%,
when Cr occupies the Nb sites [24]. When alloying Al3Nb with
Ti, the density of Al3(Nb,Ti) is reduced by 5% [6]. The density
of the alloy depends on the solubility of the alloying element,
its atomic weight and the length of the atomic bonding. When
considering Cr(Al,Nb), the solubility of Nb is 3–4% at 1000
◦C
[24], whereas solubility of Al is higher than 40%[23]. Decrease
of density of 8.5% (WQ) and 8.9% (FC) was observed when
comparing Cr(Al,Nb) and the Cr phase, which is explained
by the high solubility and the low atomic weight of Al. The
Al(Nb)Cr
2 phase also presented a negligible variation of density
(0.4%) when compared to AlCr2. Its tetragonal unit cell exhibits
enlarged c and a axes, whereas Al3(Nb,Cr), presenting a similar
crystalline structure, showed enlarged c axis and reduced a axis
when compared to the data found in literature (Table 3). Table 4
shows the physical properties of the eutectic alloy in the FC con-
dition. The eutectic alloy presents high stiffness (252 GPa) that
is attributed to the combination of Al
3(Nb,Cr) and Al(Nb)Cr2.

3.3. Mechanical behaviour
At 800 ◦C the pronounced brittleness of the single phase
Al(Nb)Cr2 determines its lower strength (Fig. 3) and when con-
cerning the eutectic alloy, the coarser structure of the DS sample
presents lower strength (1397 MPa) when compared to the AC1
sample (1525 MPa), the same trend being noticed for the hard-
ness values determined at room temperature (DS: HV 697; AC1:
HV 544). Up to 900
◦C the yield strength of the eutectic alloy,
both in the AC1 and in the DS conditions, is higher when com-
pared to single phase Al
3Nb [6] and also when compared to
Al(Nb)Cr2, as it is seen in Fig. 3 , which is attributed to the
microstructural effect of the lamellar structure [25]. One also
observes the microstructural effect when comparing the yield
strength of the AC1 (925 MPa) and DS (846 MPa) eutectic sam-
ples, since the ﬁner structure of AC1 presented a higher value.
According to Reip and Sauthoff[6],A l
3Nb shows plastic defor-
mation at temperatures higher than 900◦C but its brittle to ductile
transition temperature (BDTT) occurs at 1000 ◦C. It suggests
that at 900 ◦C the fragile Al 3Nb dictates the strength and the
Crss is the ductile phase that hinders cracking. On the results
obtained at 1000 ◦C, one sees that the microstructural effect is
completely lost, since the BDTT of Al3Nb has already occurred
and the single phase Crss (AC2) shows higher strength than the
eutectic alloy.

3.4. Oxidation behaviour
Fig. 4 shows the isothermal weight changes per unit area as
a function of time. Weight gains during the runs were small
with formation of thin layers of oxides. The kinetic curves show
scallops that are more intense for the tests performed under
O
2 atmosphere. The scallops in the kinetic curves are related
to scale cracking and subsequent rapid oxidation [8,28,29].
According to Grabke [30], oxidation of Al3Nb at temperatures
higher than 1000 ◦C is a periodic process that comprises for-
mation of /H9251-Al2O3, depletion of Al leading to formation of
Nb2Al beneath the oxide layer, cracking of the Al 2O3 scale,
growth of NbAlO 4 and Nb 2O5 until consumption of Nb 2Al
and new formation of an Al 2O3 layer. XRD of the oxidized
samples did not show the presence of Nb 2Al. Differently to
the oxidation of the single phase Al 3Nb, the eutectic structure
presents thickening of the lamellae of Al 3(Nb,Cr) due to the
growth of Al3Nb beneath the oxide layer that is supported by
the Al dissolved in the Cr(Al,Nb) that disappears during the
process (Fig. 5). Thus, Cr(Al,Nb) acts as an Al reservoir, which
is more effective with increasing temperature, since diffusiv-
ity increases. As observed for NiAl-based alloys [30],A li s
consumed by Al
2O3 growth and Cr must diffuse inward sub-
strate.
When considering the temperature of the test and the fact
that it was conducted in air, one would expect to ﬁnd Cr 2O3.
The growth mechanism of the oxide layer may be inﬂuenced by
non-metal elements (S, N, C, P) [27] and the presence of N can
lead to the formation of Cr
2O3. Also, chromia-forming alloys
are used in the temperature range 500–900 ◦C and alumina-
forming alloys at 800–1200 ◦C [27]. In spite of the favorable
conditions XRD did not show the presence of Cr2O3 and it may
be due to its evaporation at temperatures higher than 900 ◦C
[28]. However, Cr2O3 was not observed at lower temperatures
either (800–850 ◦C), which may be explained by the insufﬁ-
cient supply of Cr by Al(Nb)Cr 2. XRD showed the formation
of /H9251-Al2O3 from 900 ◦C both in air and O 2 except at 1000◦C
in O2 (Fig. 6). One should notice that near 900 ◦C Al(Nb)Cr2
transforms to Crss and supplies sufﬁcient Al for the formation
of the oxide layer. On the absence of chromia in the oxidized
samples there is the possibility of impurity levels of Cr in the/H9251-
Al
2O3 layer. It was observed the formation of several oxides for
all temperatures studied, such as metastable aluminas (/H9254-Al2O3
and /H9253-Al2O3), NbO2 (950 ◦C in air) and Nb2O5 (850 ◦C in air)

4. Conclusions
According to the results obtained it was concluded that:
• In the transformation Cr(Al,Nb) → Al(Nb)Cr
2 a fraction of
Cr(Al,Nb) was retained and at room temperature the eutectic
alloy presents 46.7% Al
3(Nb,Cr)–40.4% Al(Nb)Cr2–12.9%
Cr(Al,Nb).
• The high solubility of Al in the Cr(Al,Nb) phase is responsible
for variations of density of 8.5% (WQ) and 8.9% (FC) when
comparing with the Cr phase.
• Up to 900
◦C the combination of Al 3Nb with Cr(Al,Nb)
and Al(Nb)Cr2 considerably improves mechanical behaviour
but at 1000 ◦C a decrease in strength is observed, which is
attributed to the BDTT of Al3Nb.
• For all temperatures studied it was observed the formation of
several oxides.
• From 900◦Co n /H9251-Al2O3 is formed both in air and O2 except
under O2 at 1000◦C. Metastable aluminas were also identiﬁed
and the Al necessary for the formation of the various Al2O3 is
believed to come from the Cr(Al,Nb) phase. Some growth of
the Al
3Nb lamellae beneath the oxide layer occurred, receiv-
ing Al also from Cr(Al,Nb).
