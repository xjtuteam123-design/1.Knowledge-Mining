ABSTRACT
Many refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) offer exceptional high-temperature strength, yet their widespread application is hindered by limited room-temperature 
tensile ductility. While numerous intrinsic ductility metrics for RHEAs have been proposed through computation, their applicability to practical processing and 
tensile behavior remains unclear. This investigation integrates CALPHAD phase stability calculations with experimental arc melting, cold rolling, and standard tensile 
tests to elucidate the factors that govern the rolling and tensile ductility of a wide range of RHEAs at room temperature under as-cast and annealed conditions. Rolling 
ductility was established as a rapid screening criterion prior to tensile testing, revealing that the BCC miscibility gap driven by Group VI and Group IV elements, Zr- 
driven secondary phase formation, and rapid grain boundary Ti-segregation are the dominant extrinsic factors that suppress experimental rolling ductility despite 
favorable intrinsic metrics. Although rolling ductility identified promising candidates, subsequent tensile testing demonstrated that dendritic segregation and grain 
boundary embrittlement are more detrimental under tension, underscoring dominant extrinsic effects that are not considered in current ductility metrics based on 
ideal BCC single-phase materials. These findings provide critical guidelines for future RHEA design that must incorporate the interplay of thermodynamics, kinetics, 
and thermomechanical processing to achieve room-temperature tensile ductility in RHEAs.
1. Introduction
1.1. Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs)
High-entropy alloys (HEAs), sometimes also defined as complex 
concentrated alloys (CCAs), or multiple principal element alloys 
(MPEAs), have grown in popularity since their inception due to their 
unique and tunable mechanical properties [ 1 , 2 ]. These compositions 
combine at least 3 metallic elements in large quantities to create a 
concentrated solid solution. Many studies have been conducted on 
face-centered cubic (FCC) HEAs for their excellent strength and fracture 
toughness at cryogenic temperatures, yet they rapidly lose strength 
when the temperature increases [ 3 – 6 ]. Since FCC is more closely packed 
than BCC, they have lower-energy slip systems leading to an increase in 
ductility but often at the cost of the strength, especially at high tem -
peratures. This tradeoff has sparked interest in body-centered cubic 
(BCC) refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) for their high melting 
points and high elevated-temperature strength [ 7 , 8 ], which have the 
potential to replace the current Ni-based superalloys for high tempera -
ture applications. These alloys are primarily based on refractory ele -
ments in Group IV (Ti, Zr, Hf), Group V (V, Nb, Ta), and Group VI (Cr, 
Mo, W). Senkov et al. [ 9 ] first showcased that selected RHEAs, such as 
the MoNbTaWV system, can retain an impressive compressive strength 
of 500 MPa at 1600 
◦
C yet with limited room temperature (RT) ductility. 
To date, the only RHEAs that exhibit RT tensile ductility are the 
HfNbTaTiZr [ 10 , 11 ] and the NbTaTiV [ 12 , 13 ] systems. The lack of 
tensile ductility, especially at RT, has become a widespread limitation 
for the direct application of most RHEAs [ 7 , 14 , 15 ]. Thus, there is strong 
scientific motivation to establish a fundamental understanding of the 
factors that control RT tensile ductility in RHEAs.
1.2. Metrics for intrinsic ductility
Recent computational efforts have established effective metrics to 
evaluate the intrinsic ductility of RHEAs [ 14 ], which is defined as the 
occurrence of dislocation activities against brittle cleavage in ideal 
RHEAs that are hypothesized to be single crystal, single-phase BCC, and 
chemically homogeneous. These studies are typically based on density 
functional theory (DFT) or molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and 
have explored over 100,000 different compositions to identify numerous 
RHEAs with intrinsic ductility at room temperature. Common metrics 
include the valence electron concentration (VEC), local lattice distortion 
(LLD), the Pugh Ratio and Cauchy Pressure, the Rice Criterion, 
D-parameter, and the number of occupied electronic states. VEC

1.3. Previous experimental work on ductility of RHEAs
Many intrinsic ductility metrics have been shown to correlate well 
with experimental compressive behavior [ 14 ], which often yields high 
apparent ductility and strength. However, compressive tests inherently 
suppress crack opening by applying closing forces, masking the mate -
rial's true susceptibility to brittle fracture. In contrast, tensile testing 
opens cracks, providing a more accurate measure of a material's resis -
tance to failure. As a result, tensile ductility tends to be significantly 
lower and is more sensitive to microstructural embrittlement. To date, 
the applicability of current ductility metrics to tensile ductility, or to 
processing conditions involving tensile stress states such as rolling, re -
mains elusive. This can be partly due to the scarcity of tensile studies on 
RHEAs and the frequent absence of tensile ductility in reported data. 
Importantly, intrinsic metrics can be significantly limited in predicting 
tensile ductility because real microstructures formed during processing 
can introduce severe embrittlement under tensile conditions (e.g., GB 
oxides, secondary phases, and dendritic segregation) that are often 
omitted from computational predictions. For example, Kumar et al. [ 23 ] 
reported that the MoNbTaW RHEA exhibits excellent compressive 
behavior, with a RT compressive strength of 1390 MPa and a yield 
strength of 302 MPa at 1200

these alloys can rapidly lose tensile ductility when exposed to grain

[ 25 ] or grain boundary oxidation from oxygen ingress during 
high-temperature deformation [ 26 ].
1.4. Scope of this work
This study aims to establish a comprehensive understanding of the 
extrinsic factors that control RT ductility in various RHEAs under tensile 
stress states. Synergistic calculation of phase diagram (CALPHAD), cold 
rolling, and standard tensile testing are performed to identify potential 
embrittling mechanisms. In particular, RT rolling ductility of RHEAs is 
investigated along with tensile ductility, with its incorporation carefully 
justified through evidence of a correlation between high cold rollability 
(crack-free rolling to a large thickness reduction) and high tensile 
ductility [ 27 – 33 ]. First, although rolling is often considered compressive 
dominated along the thickness direction, it involves a significant tensile 
stress component on the exit side along the sample length [ 34 ]. This may 
establish rolling ductility as a potential criterion to approximate tensile 
ductility and an important screening step to down-select candidates with 
potential tensile ductility. Second, cold rolling enables faster screening 
because it can be performed directly from pristine sample shapes, 
avoiding the extensive preparation required for tensile specimens. 
Third, rolling per se is a widely used deformation step to introduce 
plastic work in as-cast HEAs to break down dendritic segregation and 
facilitate subsequent recrystallization [ 35 – 37 ]. Understanding the roll -
ability of RHEAs is therefore essential for assessing their suitability for 
standard processing routes, especially when most of the RHEAs exam -
ined to date were compressive tested from the as-cast material [ 7 ]. 
Overall, this study emphasizes developing a general understanding of 
how the interplay of thermodynamics, kinetics, and a typical processing 
chain, casting followed by high-temperature annealing, affects RT roll -
ing and tensile ductility across a set of representative RHEAs screened 
from a large pool. Detailed optimization of processing parameters and 
in-depth characterization of the phase transition pathways in a specific 
RHEA system are beyond the scope of the present work.

our CALPHAD results in Fig. S2 provides the indirect evidence that 
Group IV-rich HCP phase can be stabilized by oxygen in bulk. This has 
recently been observed in the HfNbTaTiZr RHEA [ 52 ], where oxygen 
contamination from the atmosphere can accelerate (Hf, Zr)-rich HCP 
phase decomposition against the (Nb, Ta)-rich BCC matrix. However, 
this rationale cannot independently explain why Ti-driven GB embrit -
tlement was not observed in NbTaTiV with a similar heat treatment 
history [ 13 , 49 , 53 ] if the involvement of the Group VI element is not 
considered. It is well known that pure Group VI metals have a near zero 
solubility of interstitial impurities and often repel oxygen to GBs [ 54 ]. 
The Ti-segregation at GBs may be accelerated by an increasing GB ox -
ygen level due to Group VI alloying besides the direct phase instability 
caused by alloying large amounts of W and Ti. Since our SEM-EDS 
analysis did not reveal oxygen segregation at grain boundaries within 
its detection limits, further investigation using atom probe tomography 
(APT) is necessary to elucidate the role of Group VI alloying elements in 
promoting GB oxygen segregation. That said, recent studies have 
captured GB Ti-segregation in NbTaTi and NbTaTiV by Senkov et al., 
[ 55 ] and Lau et al. [ 26 ] after prolonged thermal exposure with the 
presence of interstitial impurities at GBs. The incorporation of W in 
NbTaTiVW therefore appears to accelerate such behavior.

3.3. Embrittlement caused by Zr-driven phase transformation
The interplay between thermodynamics and kinetics is well illus -
trated by the NbTaTiVZr alloy system. Derived from the ductile NbTaTiV 
base, the addition of zirconium introduces complexities in phase sta -
bility and segregation behavior that significantly impact mechanical 
performance. While the as-cast alloy shows the highest rollability due to 
its retained single-phase microstructure, annealing and slower cooling 
rates promote Zr-driven phase decomposition, which degrades ductility. 
The highly dendritic microstructure shown in Fig. 9 c for the as cast 
material contains the most elemental segregation out of all the alloys 
produced and imaged. This correlates well with the calculated CAL -
PHAD results where the large difference between the liquidus and sol -
idus temperatures. These dendritic regions can serve as mechanical 
weak points where the secondary dendrite branches easily formed and 
propagated cracks, limiting ductility [ 56 ]. However, the embrittling 
effect of dendritic segregation appeared to not eliminate rolling 
ductility, compared to phase decomposition observed in NbTaTiVZr and 
other RHEAs. Furthermore, Zr-driven phase decomposition was sensi -
tive to cooling rate, suggesting slower kinetics, allowing for mitigation 
through more rapid solidification. The air-cooled sample ( Fig. 9 d) 
showed reduced phase decomposition and better rollability than the 
furnace-cooled sample ( Fig. 9 a). The effect of cooling rates was also 
confirmed through XRD ( Fig. 10 ) where significant diffraction peaks for

4. Concluding remarks and future work
This investigation provides a comprehensive understanding of the 
extrinsic factors that lead to room temperature rolling and tensile brit -
tleness in various RHEAs. Our results highlight that the factors gov -
erning the rolling and tensile ductility in RHEAs are complex and require 
a detailed understanding of the processing-structure-property relation -
ship that goes beyond simply their intrinsic ductility. While the intrinsic 
ductility metrics are useful as a starting place from an alloy design 
standpoint, they lack some key factors that can cause embrittlement of 
the materials. While there are other factors governing ductility, phase 
stability is primarily shown here to determine the rolling and tensile 
ductility in RHEAs [ 63 , 64 ].
With the significant knowledge gained in the present study, future 
work in the design of strong RHEAs with improved rolling and tensile 
ductility at room temperature is outlined below: 
(1) Besides being intrinsically ductile, BCC metastability at RT must 
be ascertained for ductility by lowering the thermodynamic and 
kinetic driving force for unintended, uncontrolled phase decom -
position. This can be achieved by two main strategies: through 
increasing the temperature window for BCC single-phase stabil -
ity, i.e., minimizing T

; or through GB engineering by 
adding interstitials with strong GB binding (e.g., B) to occupy GB 
free volume and prevent GB secondary phase nucleation. The 
former is manifested by the success of NbTaTiHf and NbTaTiV 
[ 13 , 64 ], and the latter has shown initial success in eliminating 
the GB oxide in MoNbTaW by adding B [ 65 ].
(2) New metrics with both computational and experimental input 
that can estimate the tendency for phase decomposition are 
needed to evaluate ductility. Group VI elements are known to 
enhance high-temperature strength, while Group IV elements 
improve room-temperature ductility. However, their combined 
addition often induces phase instability that degrades ductility. 
Thus, it is essential to evaluate not only the average physical

high-temperature alloys. If not, research should instead focus on 
precipitation or dispersion strengthening of known ductile single-

strength.
