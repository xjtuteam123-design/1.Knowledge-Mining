Abstract 
 
This paper presents an improved methodology for determining high-temperature tensile design strengths of Alloy 617, which is re-
garded as one of main structural materials for very high temperature reactor (VHTR) system. In establishing time-independent allowable 
stress values, an existing ASME standard procedure is preliminarily analyzed and their limitations are pointed out. Then, an improved 
methodology, which has a consistent and quantifiable design margin at low and high temperatures for tensile strengths, is proposed and 
compared with the ASME method. To find suitable curves of temperature trend to the tensile strength data, three fitting methods are 
demonstrated, and a statistical technique is adopted for design use. The results will be utilized to reasonably determine the tensile design 
strengths of Alloy 617 for application in the VHTR system. 
 
Keywords: Yield strength; Ultimate tensile strength; Alloy 617; Very high temperature reactor

1. Introduction 
The very high temperature reactor (VHTR) is one of the 
most promising Generation-IV reactor types for the economic 
production of electricity and hydrogen. Its major structures, 
such as the reactor internals, reactor pressure vessel (RPV), 
piping, hot gas ducts (HGD), and intermediate heat exchang-
ers (IHX), are designed for a life span of 60 years operating at 
950°C and 3~8 MPa in helium impurities, as shown in Fig. 1 
[1-3]. The HGD and IHX are key components, and their mate-
rials have potential for use in nickel-based superalloys such as 
Alloy 617, Haynes 230, Hastelloy-X and Alloy 800H. Among 
these, Alloy 617 is a prime candidate material due to its supe-
rior creep resistance above 800°C compared with other poten-
tial superalloys [4-6]. In present, KAERI (Korea Atomic En-
ergy Research Institute) has been developing the nuclear hy-
drogen development and demonstration (NHDD) plant, which 
has a capacity of 200MW
th in thermal and core outlet tempera-
ture of 950°C. This project is being carried out on a govern-
ment funded project called “Development of Key Technolo-
gies for Nuclear Hydrogen” since 2006 [7-9]. 
For application of Alloy 617 in VHTR systems, a great deal 
of yield strength (YS) and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) data 
is required in higher temperatures beyond and around 1000 K 
because their strength data are used to set the time-
independent primary stress limits for various high-temperature 
design codes. Although the tensile strength data for Alloy 617 
are available in the ASME draft code case [10], the data are 
still insufficient. The ASME code case is also incomplete for 
use at high temperatures beyond 950°C. Furthermore, the 
code development work for Alloy 617 was suspended at the 
end of the 1980s due to a lack of support and interest. Under 
these circumstances, Sham et al. [11] recently pointed out that 
the standard ASME method was not suitable for obtaining the 
design tensile strengths for the Ni-based Alloy 617 and 
Haynes 230 (Alloy 230), because the temperature trend curves 
for the tensile strengths did not allow a quantifiable design 
margin over a full range of low to high temperatures. There-
fore, it is necessary to develop an improved method compared

temperature YS and UTS values is given in Appendix 2 of the 
ASME Boiler and Pressure Ve ssel Code, Section II, Part D 
[12]. The YS at a high temperature is based on minimum val-
ues, and the UTS at a high temperature based on average val-

and UTS are not interpreted statistically in the ASME code.

3. Result and discussion 
3.1 Collection of YS and UTS data 
Tensile strength data of Alloy 617 tested in air were col-
lected through world-wide literature surveys, manufacturing 
companies, and a KAERI effort: Sham et al. [11], European 
Alloy-DB (EC-JRC) data [13], KAERI data [14], Inco Alloy 
[15], and Haynes Alloy [16]. Here, Sham’s data were obtained 
by digitizing the document data, and the other data were ob-
tained as numerical values. The total amount of data was 177 
sets for the YS and UTS with four product forms such as a bar, 
plate, sheet, and forging. 
Fig. 2 shows the plot of the YS and UTS values as a func-
tion of temperature for Alloy 617. The YS and UTS values 
decreased gradually with increasing temperatures. At higher 
temperature beyond and around 1000 K for the UTS and 1100 
K for the YS, the temperature trends revealed more sharp de-
cays in the strength values than in the lower temperatures. 
When the entire data for the YS and UTS were plotted to-
gether, there was a wide separation for the YS and UTS data 
in the low temperature ranges, and the scatter bands con-
verged and started to overlap in the high temperature ranges, 
as shown in Fig. 3.

3.3 Conservative lower bound curves 
To obtain the best-fit curves of temperature trend for the YS 
and UTS, three fit methods, a single polynomial fit, 2-step 
exponential fit, and 2-step polynomial fit, are adopted. The 2-
step exponential and 2-step polynomial fits are used to sepa-
rate the fitting temperature ranges for the YS and UTS data 
into two temperature ranges. As discussed in Fig. 4, the reason 
for this is that changes in the temperature dependence of the 
YS and UTS data were evident at around 1100 K and 1000 K. 
This approach makes it possible to develop conservative lower 
bound curves, because the best-fit function can be precisely 
obtained for the separated temperature ranges, and their curves 
can be determined through a statistical concept. This statistical 
approach can define the lower bound with an appropriate level 
of conservatism for design use. 
In addition, in the lower temperature ranges of T<1100 K 
for the YS and T<1000 K for the UTS, the YS and UTS were 
not reduced and steady regardless of increasing temperatures. 
This is attributed to a dynamic strain ageing (DSA) phenome-
non increasing strengths by obstacles to the movement of 
dislocations during strain. This DSA phenomenon has been 
well known to be generated at specific temperature ranges for 
solid solution alloys like austenitic stainless steels [17]. It is 
thus believed that the DSA was generated at this temperature 
range for Alloy 617 which is a solid solution alloy. However, 
in the higher temperature ranges of T >1100 K for the YS and 
T >1000 K for the UTS, their strengths were rapidly reduced 
with increasing temperatures. The reason for this is that the 
density of dislocations was reduced significantly by a crossing 
slip or a climb of the dislocations at this higher temperature.

within the higher temperature ranges of T>1100 K for S
y and 
T>1000 K for Su, because they did not allow a consistent and 
quantifiable design margin w ith increasing temperatures. It 
appeared that 2-step fit methods were superior in the best-fit 
curves of temperature trends compared to the single polyno-
mial fit that has been commonly used; the 2-step polynomial 
fit was particularly better than the 2-step exponential fit. The 
YS and UTS data correctly followed the histograms of normal 
distribution. Using this normal probability, the 95% lower 
bound curves for the three fit methods were probabilistically 
developed. The results for the 2-step polynomial fit allowed 
the consistent and quantifiable design margin over the full 
temperature ranges at the low and high temperatures. The 
results can be utilized for determining the tensile design 
strengths with a probabilistic margin at the high temperature 
regimes of Alloy 617.
