Abstract
TiAl alloy has attracted abroad interests for decades because of its high specific strength and high modulus. However, 
the strength, especially high-temperature strength needs to be further improved in an increasingly demanding service 
environment. Surface nano-crystallization has been proved to be an effective route for improving the strength of a mate -
rial. In this work, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are applied to study the effect of grain size on the mechanical 
behaviors of nano-crystallized γ-TiAl (NC-γ-TiAl) alloy under room temperature and high temperatures. It’s found that 
the yield stress decreases with the decreasing of grain size, exhibiting the so-called inverse Hall-Petch behavior, and the 
flow stress increases firstly and then decreases with the increase of grain size under room and high temperatures. At room 
temperature, all the intrinsic stacking faults and extrinsic stacking faults, associated with Shockley partial dislocations, 
and deformation twins contribute to the plasticity, and the deformation twinning and cross slip of partial dislocations can 
provide additional strengthening effects except for the grain boundary strengthening effect in nano-crystalline. When the 
temperature rises to 1000 K, only some intrinsic stacking faults and a large number of Frenkel defects are found, which 
should be responsible for the much lower high-temperature strength. The results obtained in this work should be help -
ful for understanding the underlying strengthening mechanism of NC-γ-TiAl alloy under the aim of high temperature 
applications.

In recent years, the gradient nano-structured TiAl alloy 
was prepared by different surface nanocrystalline meth -
ods, the grain refinement mechanism was revealed, and 
the mechanical properties were investigated. Liu et al. 
[17] prepared the surface nanocrystalline Ti-6Al-4 V alloy 
by ultrasonic impact method, and found that α-Ti formed 
nanocrystalline by the interaction of dislocation movement 
and dynamic recrystallization, while β-Ti had high stacking 
fault energy and the deformation was mainly dominated by 
dislocation slip. They also found that due to the formation 
of surface nanocrystalline layer, the hardness of Ti-6Al-4 V 
alloy was significantly improved. Zhao et al. [ 18] prepared 
the gradient nano-structured Ti-45Al-7Nb-0.3 W alloy by 
surface mechanical grinding method, and found that the 
nanocrystallization mechanism is mainly twin-twin inter -
action, and the surface microhardness is about 50% higher 
than that of the substrate. Wu et al. [ 19] use the supersonic 
fine particle bombardment technique to prepare Ti-6.5Al-
3.5Mo-1.5Zr-0.3Si alloy with surface nanocrystallization, 
and studied the effect on the fatigue strength and hardness 
of the alloy. Wang et al. [ 20] investigated the mechanical 
properties of surface nanocrystalline TiAl alloys, and found 
that the hardness increased, while the corrosion resistance 
decreased. They [ 21] further summarized the mechanism 
of surface nanocrystallization of a Ti-6Al-4 V alloy under 
supersonic fine particle bombardment treatment. Yao et al. 
[22] prepared a kind of TiAl alloy with surface nanocrystal-
line layer of a certain thickness by using high-energy ball 
milling technique, found that the grain size of the surface 
layer can be refined to less than 10 nm, and the hardness of 
surface layer can be significantly improved from 340 HV to 
922 HV . It therefore can be concluded that the grain size of 
surface layer in TiAl alloys can be successfully refined to 
nanometer scale, and the corresponding mechanical proper-
ties, such as strength and hardness, are expected to improve 
obviously at room temperature. However, the effect of grain 
size on the mechanical properties of TiAl alloys at room 
temperature and elevated temperatures, and the correspond-
ing physical mechanisms need to be further explored.
This work employs molecular dynamics (MD) simula -
tions to study the grain size-dependent mechanical proper -
ties and deformation behavior of TiAl alloy under uniaxial 
tension at room temperature and elevated temperatures. The 
results show that both the deformation twinning and cross 
slip of partial dislocations can provide additional strength -
ening effects except for the grain boundary strengthening 
effect in the nanocrystalline TiAl alloy. And the strengthen-
ing mechanism is also available at elevated temperatures.
2 Method
2.1 Nanocrystalline γ-TiAl alloy model
In order to study the effect of grain size on the mechani -
cal behaviors of nano-crystallized γ-TiAl (NC-TiAl) alloy, 
polycrystalline atomic models with nanoscale grain sizes 
should be established. The nano-crystalline models are 
established using the V oronoi crystal orientation rotation 
algorithm integrated in Atomsk software. Figure 1 shows 
a representative atomic nano-crystalline γ-TiAl model for 
MD simulations, in which the left is colored by atom type, 
and the right is colored by lattice structure type. Here, the 
white balls denote disordered atoms, such as grain boundar-
ies (GBs), and the green balls represent FCC-like crystal. 
It’s known that γ-TiAl alloy has an L10 lattice structure, i.e. 
face-centered tetragonal (FCT) structure, which is similar 
to the face-centered cubic (FCC) lattice. The lattice con -
stant a and c are 4.001 Å and 4.061 Å, respectively, with an 
axial ratio of 1.015 [16]. Some parameters of the established 
nano-crystalline γ-TiAl alloy models are given in Table 1. 
Considering the limitation of computing ability of MD sim-
ulation, each model possess different dimensions and num -
bers of atoms. The nano-crystalline models with grain sizes 
davg of 4 nm, 6 nm, 8 nm, 10 nm, 13 nm, 16 nm, 20 nm and 
24 nm have the same number of grains, i.e. 27 grains, but 
different model sizes. Due to the fact that there are fewer 
atoms in grains with davg of 2 nm and 3 nm, the correspond-
ing models own more grains, and the model sizes are the 
same with that of davg=4 nm.
2.2 Uniaxial tension simulation
The well-established parameters of embedded-atom method 
(EAM) potential proposed by Zope and Mishin [ 23] are 
used for describing the interactions among atoms in γ-TiAl 
alloy. In fact, the potential is widely applied in the atomistic 
simulations of mechanical properties and plastic deforma -
tion of TiAl alloy [9, 16, 24]. It’s known that the machine-
leaning interatomic potential is of DFT accuracy but MD 
efficiency, and allow bridging first principles based models 
up to continuum models. For example, Mortazavi et al. [25] 
proposed the concept of first principles multiscale model -
ing of mechanical properties based on the machine-leaning 
interatomic potential, where ab initio level of accuracy is 
hierarchically bridged to explore the mechanical/failure 
response of macroscopic systems. But unfortunately, it 
seems that there is no machine-leaning interatomic potential 
applicable to the TiAl alloy. MD simulations are performed 
using large-scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simu-
lator (LAMMPS) [26], and the simulation results were ana-
lyzed visually using the open visualization tool (OVITO)

The uniaxial tension simulations are performed as follows. 
Firstly, the conjugate gradient algorithm is applied on each 
nano-crystalline TiAl atomic model to optimize the initial 
atomic configuration. Then, the optimized atomic configu -
rations are further relaxed at three different temperatures, 
including 300 K, 600 K and 1000 K, respectively, with the 
isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble for 100 ps. Next, a 
constant strain rate of 1/ns is applied on each relaxed sample 
in the x direction at the target temperature (300 K, 600 K, 
or 1000 K) with the NPT ensemble. It is worth noting that 
the strain rate of 10^9 1/s is an acceptable loading rate 
when studying the deformation of poly-crystalline metals 
[30–32], although the MD simulation results are dependent 
on the applied loading strain rate. For example, Zhang et al. 
[30] investigated the effect of strain rate on the mechanical 
behavior of nano-crystalline copper in the strain rate range 
of 10^7 1/s to 10^9 1/s by MD simulations. They found that 
the strain rate of 10^9 1/s can be regarded as a critical strain 
rate, below which the flow stress increases slowly with the 
increasing of strain rate, while above which the flow stress 
increases relatively faster. In addition, smaller strain rate, 
such as 10^8 1/s, will lead to huge computation work. Thus, 
the strain rate of 10^9 1/s is usually adopted because of the 
limited computation computing hardware resources. Dur -
ing the simulation, the Nose–Hoover thermostat and Nose–
Hoover barostat are used to control the temperature and 
pressure, respectively, and periodic boundary conditions 
are applied in all the three directions. Finally, the deforma -
tion analysis of each sample is visualized and analyzed by 
OVITO.
2.3 Calculation of stacking fault energy
Stacking fault energy (SFE) is a crucial intrinsic parameter 
to determine plastic deformation mechanisms in metallic 
materials. In Cu-Al alloys, deformation twinning is pre -
dominantly observed in low-SFE materials with SFE values 
below 25 mJ/m², whereas medium-SFE materials (25–
50 mJ/m²) exhibit a competitive mechanism between dis -
location slip and twinning during deformation. Dislocation 
slip becomes the dominant deformation mechanism in high-
SFE materials with SFE values ranging from 50 to 78 mJ/m² 
or exceeding 78 mJ/m² [ 33, 34]. To understand the plastic 
deformation mechanisms in the TiAl alloy, the generalized 
SFE (GSFE) curves [35], These curves were derived based
