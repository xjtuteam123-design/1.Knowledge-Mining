 A B S T R A C T
Refractory alloys with body-centered cubic (BCC) structures are candidate materials for various high-
temperature applications in the fields of aerospace and nuclear energy owing to their high melting points. 
Thus, improving their high-temperature strength is essential to meet the increasing performance requirements. 
Although refining grain size to nanoscale can significantly strengthen refractory alloys at room temperature, 
their high-temperature strengths are often limited due to the thermal instability of grain boundaries. In this 
study, we present a novel approach to enhance high-temperature strength through the introduction of a high 
density of stable semi-coherent interfaces. A dual-phase W-Cr refractory alloy was prepared via a processing 
route involving sintering, homogenization and discontinuous precipitation, exhibiting a bicontinuous nanos-
tructure consisting of two nanoscale phases with identical crystal structures, while maintaining a microscale 
grain size. Owing to the high barrier strength and outstanding stability of these semi-coherent W/Cr interfaces, 
the resulting alloy achieves a yield strength of 3.1 GPa at room temperature and retains 1.8 GPa at 1000 ◦C, 
which is significantly higher than those of conventional refractory alloys at the comparable temperatures.
1. Introduction
Refractory alloys composed of Group IV, V or VI elements are 
promising materials for high-temperature applications in the fields of 
aerospace and nuclear energy due to their high melting points [1–3]. To 
enhance the strength of refractory alloys at high temperatures, two pri-
mary strategies are employed, i.e., adding metal oxide nanoparticles [4,
5] and utilizing multi-principal element alloying [6–8]. However, the 
former approach is limited by challenges in dispersing high fractions 
of nanoparticles, whereas the refractory multi-principal element alloys, 
also known as refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEA), often require 
further strengthening to meet increasing performance requirements [9,
10]. Thus, developing more efficient strengthening strategies at high 
temperatures for refractory alloys is still essential.
According to the classical Hall–Petch (H–P) relationship [11,12], re-
ducing the characteristic structure sizes of materials, such as grain sizes 
of polycrystalline metals and phase sizes of metallic composites, into 
nanoscale could greatly enhance their strength and hardness. However, 
a high density of grain boundaries or interfaces in these nanostructured 
metals significantly improve the driving force for structure coarsening 
at an elevated temperature, leading to the thermal instability [13,
14]. More critically, at a high temperature, grain boundary/interface 
migration or sliding becomes a prominent deformation mechanism, 
dramatically reducing the strength of nanostructured metals [15,16].

Consequently, stabilizing grain boundary/interface is crucial for retain-
ing the high strength of these materials [17]. For example, introducing 
low-energy, stable coherent twin boundaries [18] and relaxed grain 
boundaries [19] improved the high-temperature strengths of austenitic 
steels and NiCoCr alloys, respectively. Similarly, semi-coherent inter-
faces between two phases with identical orientations are considered as 
stable boundaries and have been successfully synthesized in spinodoid 
Cu/Au [20] and Cu/Ag [21] alloys via dealloying and deposition. These 
nanostructured spinodoid alloys exhibit remarkable resistance to coars-
ening even at temperatures near the melting point [21]. Nevertheless, 
all the aforementioned stable boundaries/interfaces are present in face-
centered cubic (FCC) metals. It is still a challenge to introduce such 
boundaries/interfaces into body-centered cubic (BCC) refractory alloys 
to enhance their high-temperature strength.
In this study, we aimed to introduce a high density of semi-coherent 
interfaces into a refractory W-Cr alloy through sequential processing 
involving sintering, homogenization and discontinuous precipitation. 
Analogous to spinodoid alloys, the resulting W-Cr alloy exhibits a 
bicontinuous nanostructure consisting of two nanoscale phases with a 
cube-on-cube orientation relationship, while maintaining a microscale 
grain size. This approach yields a W-Cr alloy shows significantly higher 
strength than conventional refractory alloys from room temperature to 
1000 ◦C.

2. Experimental
Mixed powders of 34 at.% tungsten (W) and 66 at.% chromium (Cr) 
(purchased from Liaoning Zhongse Novel Material and Technology Co., 
Ltd) were fabricated by ball mill of high-purity W and Cr powders 
(99.9 wt.% purity, 40-50 μm particle size). Then the mixed powders 
were sintered using a fast hot press sintering furnace (FHP-828, Haatn), 
with temperature rapidly increasing to 1400 ◦C at a heating rate as 
100 ◦C/min, holding for 30 min, then cooling rapidly to room tem-
perature.Throughout the sintering process, the vacuum was kept below 
10−2 Pa and a constant pressure of 50 MPa was applied. All sintered 
W-Cr specimens were subsequently homogenized at 1600 ◦C for 3 h 
and quenched using high-pressure Argon. Following homogenization, 
the W-Cr alloys underwent discontinuous precipitation (DP) treatment 
by annealing at 1080 ◦C for 2 h under an Ar atmosphere,followed by 
quenching in water to obtain dual-phase nanostructures. To produce 
coarsened dual-phase structures, a subset of the homogenized samples 
were annealed at 1200 ◦C for predetermined durations (2 h, 5 h and 
24 h) prior to annealing at 1080 ◦C for 2 h. A DP-treated W-Cr specimen 
(1080 ◦C for 2 h) was cut into a 1.0 mm thick plate and immersed 
into molten pure Zn held at 700 ◦C for 6 h in a graphite crucible to 
replace the Cr phase with Zn. The Zn phase was then replaced with 
Cu by immersing into molten pure Cu at 1100 ◦C for 10 min. After 
withdrawal from the liquid Cu and cooling to room temperature, a 
W/Cu nanocomposite was obtained.
The microstructure and chemical composition of the W-Cr alloys 
were characterized using field-emission scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM, FEI Verios 460) equipped with energy X-ray dispersive spec-
troscopy (EDS, Oxford Instruments X-max 80). Electron backscatter 
diffraction (EBSD) analysis was conducted using an automatic orien-
tation acquisition system (Oxford Symmetry S3) mounted on a SEM 
instrument (Thermo Fisher Verios 5UC), with a step size of 50 nm. 
Specimens for SEM, EDS and EBSD analyses were pre-treated by SiC 
paper grinding, diamond suspension polishing and ion beam milling 
(Leica EM RE101). Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), High-
Angle Annular Dark-Field Scanning Transmission Electron Microscopy 
(HAADF-STEM) and EDS mapping were carried out using a FEI Ta-
los F200 microscope operated at 200 kV. High-resolution STEM was 
performed on a double aberration-corrected FEI Titan Cubed Themis 
G2 microscope operated at 300 kV. All TEM specimens were prepared 
using a dual-beam focused ion beam (FIB) system (FEI Nanolab Helios 
650). To minimize FIB-induced damage, the final thinning step was 
conducted at 16 kV and 11 pA.
The hardness measurements at room temperature were conducted 
on a Vickers microhardness tester (Qness Q10A+) under a load of 
300 g with a dwell time of 10 s. High-temperature Vickers hardnesses 
tests at 400, 600 and 800 ◦C were conducted using a dedicated tester 
(HTV-PHS30) with a load of 500 g and a dwell time of 10 s. Cylinder 
specimens with a dimension of 𝛷 6 mm × 9 mm were machined 
from the DP-treated W-Cr samples for uniaxial compression tests. The 
compression tests were performed at 800, 1000 and 1200 ◦C under 
a strain rate of 1 × 10−3 s−1 using a thermo-mechanical simulator 
(Gleeble3800).
3. Result and discussion
As shown in the EBSD map in Fig. 1a, the homogenized W–Cr 
alloy consists predominantly of equiaxed grains with an average size 
of approximately 10 μm , although some finer grains/subgrains are 
present along grain boundaries. The corresponded SEM image in Fig. 1b 
reveals a single-phase microstructure within the grains, without visible 
precipitates or significant compositional fluctuations, while some of 
grain boundaries exhibit a dual-phase structure. A locally-enlarged 
SEM (Fig. 1c) reveals that these grain boundaries exhibit a char-
acteristic discontinuous precipitation morphology [23], indicative of 
boundary-migration-induced transformation.
According to the equilibrium phase diagram of W-Cr system [22] 
(Fig. 1d), the W34Cr66 alloy in this study is expected to form a single 
BCC phase at the homogenization temperature of 1600 ◦C, which is 
in consistent with the observed intragranular microstructure. Thus, 
it is concluded that the dual-phase structures along some of grain 
boundaries was formed during cooling following homogenization.
As shown in the SEM image in Fig. 1f, after annealing at 1080 ◦C, 
discontinuous precipitation was completed in the W-Cr alloy, leading 
to a dual-phase structure consisting of W and Cr-rich solid solution 
phases, in agreement with the phase diagram (Fig. 1d) and a previous 
study [26]. The EBSD map (Fig. 1e) reveals that the coarse-grained 
structure in the W-Cr alloy was largely retained after DP treatment,

treatment is a bicontinuous network composed of interpenetrating W 
and Cr phases. Previous studies [27,28] indicate that the formation 
of this bicontinuous structure is critically dependent on the W/Cr 
ratio. A disproportionately high volume of either phase leads to a 
dual-phase structure consisting of a matrix with embedded rod-like 
precipitates [28]. 
A typical HAADF-STEM image in Fig. 2a shows two DP colonies in 
a single grain. EDS mapping in Fig. 2b reveals a low concentration of 
W within the Cr-rich ligaments and a detectable amount of Cr within 
W-rich ligaments. Quantitative EDS line profiling across several W and 
Cr ligaments indicates average chemical compositions of W73Cr27 and 
Cr88W12 (at.%) for the W and Cr phases, respectively, as seen in Fig. 2c. 
The selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern acquired from a 
DP colony (Fig. 2d) confirms that the W and Cr ligaments show identi-
cal BCC crystal structures with a cube-on-cube orientation relationship, 
albeit with different lattice parameters. An aberration-corrected HR-
STEM image in Fig. 2e reveals an array of misfit dislocations at W/Cr 
interface, due to the high lattice mismatch of 6.8% between W and Cr 
ligaments. Based on the atomic distribution in Fig. 2e, the shear strain 
(𝜀𝑥𝑦) is calculated by the geometric phase analysis (GPA) method [29], 
as seen in Fig. 2f. The high-strain spots in Fig. 2f correspond well to 
the dislocation cores observed in Fig. 2e.
DP is a solid-state reaction that a parent phase decomposes into two 
product phases behind a migrating reaction front [23]. This reaction 
is critical for achieving the bicontinuous nanostructure in the W-Cr 
alloy studied here. Since the newly-formed W and Cr phase share 
the identical BCC crystal structure, the W/Cr interfaces behind the 
migration grain boundary are semi-coherent to minimize the interface 
energy (Fig. 2e), leading to the coarse-grained morphology after DP 
(Fig. 1e). Moreover, unlike volume diffusion controlled continuous 
precipitation or spinodal decomposition, the grain boundary at the 
reaction front of DP provides a conduit for faster mass transport, 
enabling the system to reach near-equilibrium compositions rapidly 
while suppressing microstructural coarsening [30]. Therefore, a refined 
and uniform dual-phase nanostructure is obtained in the W-Cr alloy 
following DP treatment (Fig. 1f).
The microhardnesses of homogenized and DP-treated W-Cr alloys 
at room temperature are presented in Fig. 3a, with comparing to the 
pure W and Cr. After DP treating, the hardness of the dual-phase W-Cr 
alloy with 𝐿 = 50 nm increases from 8.3 GPa to 9.3 GPa, which is 
much higher than those of pure W (5.2 GPa) and pure Cr (1.5 GPa). 
With increasing DP treatment temperature and time, the 𝐿 of dual-
phase W-Cr alloy was systematically increased from 50 nm to 245 nm, 
while the average grain size varied slightly (5-7 μm ). As shown in 
Fig. 3b, the microhardness of the dual-phase W-Cr alloy increases with 
decreasing 𝐿, confirming the W/Cr interface strengthening. The data 
were fitted using the classical H–P relationship, i.e., 𝐻 = 𝐻0 + 𝑘𝐿−1∕2, 
where 𝐻0 and 𝑘 are material-specific constants. It gives 𝐻0 = 4.5 GPa 
and 𝑘 = 34.5 GPa nm1∕2. The 𝐻0 value is considerably higher than 
that predicted by the rule of mixture [31] (3.0 GPa) based the data 
of pure W and Cr, suggesting solid solution strengthening in both W 
and Cr ligaments. Meanwhile, the 𝑘 value, which reflects the barrier 
strength of interfaces to dislocation transmission, is comparable to 
those reported in polycrystalline pure W [32] and pure Cr [33] (28 and 
43.6 GPa nm1∕2, respectively). This indicates that the semi-coherent 
W/Cr interfaces exhibit a barrier strength similar to that of incoherent 
grain boundaries at room temperature.

Microstructural examination after compression testing at 1000 ◦C 
(Fig. 4a) reveals coarsening of the dual-phase structure with 𝐿 in-
creasing from ∼100 nm to ∼200 nm and spheroidization of some 
W ligaments at the grain boundaries. In contrast, the bicontinuous 
nanostructure in the grain interiors was retained without coarsening 
or spheroidizing, revealing higher mechanical stability of the semi-
coherent W/Cr interface compared to the incoherent grain boundary 
and interface. It should be noted that the stability of W/Cr interfaces 
here may depends on two factors: a test temperature below the DP 
treatment temperature and a relatively high strain rate. Further TEM 
characterization within a grain reveals dislocation pile-ups near the 
W/Cr interfaces and the formation of dislocation boundaries inside both 
W and Cr ligaments (Fig. 4b), indicating that dislocation motion is 
still the dominant deformation mechanism at a temperature as high as 
1000 ◦C, with limited contribution from interface/boundary migration 
or sliding.
In this study, the high elevated-temperature strength of the dual-
phase W-Cr alloy derives from the high density of W/Cr interfaces, 
which provide strong barriers to dislocation transmission and exhibit 
high stability against interface motion. Previous studies [35,36] indi-
cated that the barrier strength of a semi-coherent interface is directly 
related to the lattice mismatch, chemical mismatch and shear modu-
lus mismatch. In this study, the lattice mismatch between W and Cr 
ligaments alloy is 6.8% (Fig. 2e), which is lower than the spinodoid 
alloys by dealloying-deposition (10%–12%) [20,21] but higher than 
the dual-phase refractory alloys by eutectic solidification [37] or spin-
odal decomposition [24,38] (1%–3%). Combined with the significant 
chemical mismatch and shear modulus mismatch, the W/Cr interfaces 
reveal a high barrier strength at room temperature, verified by that 
the 𝑘 value is comparable to that of the incoherent grain boundary 
in polycrystalline W and Cr [32,33]. Due to the chemical stability of 
ligaments and thermal stability of interface against migration/sliding, 
the high barrier of W/Cr interface for dislocation transmission can be 
maintained up to 1000 ◦C (Fig. 4b), eventually resulting in the high 
strength of dual-phase W-Cr alloy at elevated temperatures (Fig. 3d).
As seen in Fig. 5a, following phase replacing in liquid Zn and cooling 
to room temperature, a W/Zn nanocomposite with 5 vol.% residual W-
Cr structures at some of grain boundaries was prepared. Due to the 
low temperature of liquid Zn (700 ◦C), W ligaments were negligibly 
coarsened. In contrast, after phase replacing in liquid Cu at 1100 ◦C, 
significant coarsening occurred, resulting in an average W ligament size 
of 250 nm in the prepared W/Cu nanocomposite, as seen in Fig. 5b. The 
underlying coarsening mechanism is attributed to solid–liquid interface 
diffusion, similar to the final stage of liquid metal dealloying [39,40]. 
The volume fraction of Cu is approximately 60%. Instead of the flat Cu 
plates along grain boundaries, i.e., a grain-boundary-like defect that 
often seen in the metallic composites by liquid metal dealloying [31,
41,42], only the residual W-Cr dual-phase structure was observed at 
some of grain boundaries. Due to the submicron W/Cu ligaments and 
Cr solution in W ligaments, the Vickers microhardness of as-prepared 
W/Cu nanocomposite is 3.1 GPa, which is markedly higher than that of 
the conventional W/Cu composites with a similar Cu content [43,44] 
(1–2 GPa). These results demonstrate that DP treatment combined with 
phase replacement offers a viable pathway for extending bicontinuous 
nanostructures to other material systems, enabling the fabrication of 
high-strength metallic nanocomposites.
4. Conclusions
In summary, a dual-phase W-Cr alloy with a high density of semi-
coherent interfaces was successfully synthesized through sintering, ho-
mogenization and discontinuous precipitation. The resulting W-Cr alloy 
exhibits a bicontinuous nanostructure consisting of two distinct phases 
with identical BCC crystal structures and a cube-on-cube orientation re-
lationship. The mean W ligament size is approximately 50 nm, embed-
ded within a micron-scale grain structure. As the testing temperature 
increases from room temperature to 1000 ◦C, the yield strength of this 
W-Cr alloy decreases from 3.1 GPa to 1.8 GPa, which is significantly 
higher than that of conventional refractory alloys at comparable tem-
peratures. The high strength of W-Cr alloy at an elevated temperature 
is attributed to the nanoscale interface spacing, high barrier strength 
and exceptional stability of semi-coherent W/Cr interfaces.
