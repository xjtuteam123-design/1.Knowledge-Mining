ABSTRACT
Grain boundary engineering (GBE) describes the various approaches to control the fraction and composition of 
special boundaries in designing engineering materials. For example, complex thermo-mechanical processing 
routes are harnessed to increase the fraction of Σ 3 twin boundaries, bringing about improvements to strength, 
toughness, and corrosion properties in austenitic stainless steels. However, high-temperature gas turbine engine 
components designed for the most demanding applications must be manufactured from Ni-based superalloys. To 
provide the required high-temperature strength, their microstructures are highly complex and consist of an 
austenitic γ -matrix, γ’ precipitates, and grain boundary (GB) carbides, and/or borides. GBE approaches for Ni- 
based superalloys have been reported to reduce in-service GB cracking via targeted engineering of the GB 
microstructure. However, the same complex microstructures severely limit the processability of the most 
advanced Ni-based superalloys required to develop the next generation gas turbine engines.
To tackle this challenge, we propose precipitation-controlled GBE for Ni-based superalloys with limited 
formability, via formation of GB serrations and precipitation upon slow cooling. We showcase our approach by 
achieving controlled GB precipitation of GB- γ’ and M
6
C carbides in the cast & wrought Ni-based superalloy Ren ´e 
41 via a simple heat treatment. Ductility improvements are predicted via crystal plasticity modeling due to 
increased slip transmission compatibility. Instead of generating additional Σ 3 twin boundaries only, these in -
terfaces are directly incorporated into the GB network. It is shown that precipitation-controlled GBE is enabled 
by GB- γ’ nucleating heterogeneously on M
6
C whereby competitive coarsening of GB- γ’ provides the driving 
force. The fundamental mechanisms of our GBE approach are discussed and summarized in a qualitative 
microstructural model.

Ni-based superalloys in general.
The presence of GB precipitates is known to promote the formation of 
‘zig-zag ’ shaped GBs [18] . In Ni-based superalloys, this is generally 
linked to carbides and GB- γ’ decorating GBs [19 – 22] . GB precipitates 
have been reported to form via discontinuous precipitation mechanisms 
as reviewed by Williams & Butler [23] . For example, the ‘pucker 
mechanism ’ proposed by Tu & Turnbull [24,25] describes GB migration 
and deflection to reduce the interfacial energy between the matrix and 
GB precipitates, without GB precipitate growth. A mechanism proposed 
by Nes & Billdal [26] involves the nucleation of GB precipitates and 
subsequent growth, deflecting GBs in order to minimize the contact area 
between the GB and GB precipitate. Fournelle & Clark [27] reported an 
allotriomorph nucleation mechanism at migrating GBs. After initial GB 
pinning, GB precipitate growth favors further GB migration due to solute 
depletion. Williams & Edington [28] introduced a competitive coars -
ening mechanism, where the faster solute transport along GBs affects the 
coarsening rates of precipitates. GB precipitates then grow at the 
expense of smaller intragranular precipitates. Slow cooling [29,30] , 
GB- γ’ [19 – 21] or carbide [22,29] precipitation, and/or segregation 
prior to GB precipitation [31,32] have been shown to promote GB ser -
rations in Ni-based superalloys.
GB serrations have been identified as potential pathway to achieve 
GBE [1,29] and their formation via simple slow cooling and GB pre -
cipitation makes them attractive to highly alloyed Ni-based superalloys 
with limited formability. The complex (co-) precipitation of GB- γ’ and 
carbides has been rarely studied [18] and their potential for GBE re -
mains insufficiently explored. Some of the current authors discussed the 
effects of boron and carbon microalloying additions on the GB micro -
structure evolution focusing on atom probe microscopy of segregation 
and precipitation phenomena in the Ni-based superalloy Ren ´e 41 [33, 
34] . However, the potential to achieve ductility improvements via 
precipitation-controlled GBE harnessing GB serrations remains 
unknown.
Hence, here we evaluate the potential of precipitation-controlled 
GBE in the cast & wrought Ni-based superalloy Ren ´e 41 with limited 
formability. First, a sequence of individual heat treatments between 
1000 and 1225 
◦
C is carried out to identify that the GB (co-) precipita -
tion reactions of GB- γ’ and GB-carbides are linked to GB serrations. The 
detailed nature of GB serrations is studied using correlative electron 
microscopy methods. The crystal plasticity finite element method (CP- 
FEM) is applied to study slip transmission compatibility and the ex -
pected influence on ductility. A microstructural model summarizes this 
GBE pathway for cast & wrought Ni-based superalloys and similar 
materials.

2.3. Crystal plasticity finite element method
Two model microstructures were generated based on HR-EBSD maps 
to compare local stress and strain accumulation with serrated versus 
straight GBs. The Neper 4.5.0 software [37,38] was used to generate 
Voronoi tessellations of two and three grains, respectively. A domain 
size of 7.5 × 2.5 × 7.5 µ m
3 
was used to generate meshes of second order 
elements with a relative characteristic length of 0.2. The FEPX 1.3.0 
software [39] was used for polycrystal plasticity modeling. Input pa -
rameters are provided in Table 2 . Initial values for this model were taken 
from literature on similar superalloys such as Waspaloy and Haynes ® 
282 ® [40 – 42] , and were further refined to match reports on mechanical 
data [43,44] . A full description of these parameter is provided by Quey 
& Kasemer [45] .
Subsequent visualization used the Neper modules as well as the 
gnuplot 5.4 software [46] . Desktop computation provided acceptable 
computation times of less than an hour using 8 cores in an openSUSE

3.5. Modeling of expected impact on mechanical properties
Calibrated CP-FEM modeling is used to estimate the expected impact 
of precipitation-controlled GBE on the local stress and strain distribution 
and thus, expected ductility. The data and corresponding results for the 
calibration are provided as Supplementary Fig. S3 . Fig. 9 a provides two 
investigated models, one with GB serrations (grains (I), (II), (III)) and 
one without GB serrations (only grains (I) and (III)). Orientations of the 
model correspond to the average grain orientations in Fig. 4 . After 
experiencing 5 % strain, equivalent stress and strain are presented in 
Fig. 9 b. In the model without GB serrations, both equivalent stress and 
strain are somewhat more localized at the GB when compared to the 
model with GB serration, where they appear more homogeneously 
distributed across grain interiors. Fig. 9 c provides the accumulated slip 
shear on active slip systems in relative magnitude. Here, red arrows 
visualize each respective slip system vector. Considerable accumulation 
of slip shear on the systems (011)[111] and (110)[111] is observed in 
the absence of GB serrations. In both models, the (011)[ 1 1 1] system 
exhibits the highest accumulated slip shear in grain (III). However, the 
most remarkable impact of GB serrations is revealed in accumulated slip 
shear on the (101)[1 1 1] system within grain (I) and (II). This is where 
grain (II) allows the rearrangement of this slip system into a more 
compatible configuration for slip transmission from grains (I) via (II) to 
(III). Consequently, accumulated slip shear as well as equivalent stress 
and equivalent strain are more evenly distributed in the model con -
taining the GB serration. In contrast, the model without GB serrations 
therefore exhibits accumulation at the shown GB. Indeed, these obser -
vations are reflected in the expected local mechanical response pre -
sented in Fig. 9 d. The model with GB serration exhibits a ~13 % 
reduction of engineering stress at equivalent strain. Thus, we expect GB 
serrations to promote a more ductile behavior in proximity to GBs.
4. Discussion
We propose precipitation-controlled GBE for advanced Ni-based su -
peralloys with limited formability and discuss its fundamental mecha -
nisms. We showcase our results using the cast & wrought Ni-based 
superalloy Ren ´e 41. First, it is necessary to discuss the complex forma -
tion mechanism of the observed GB serrations. Second, the 
precipitation-controlled GBE is discussed with respect to the expected 
impact on ductility, based on the presented CP-FEM model. Finally, a 
qualitative model is proposed summarizing the microstructural 
mechanism.

The formation of GB serrations is closely related to the interaction of 
GBs and GB precipitates. Different mechanisms have been brought for -

4.2. Grain boundary engineering expected ductility improvements
The incorporation of Σ 3 twin boundaries into the network of HAGBs 
has been reported to provide improvements in crack resistance [11,13] , 
hot formability [14,15] , and corrosion resistance [16,17] . For the cur -
rent GBE approach, a more ductile mechanical response is expected on a 
local scale as shown in Fig. 8 . A more homogeneous distribution of 
equivalent stress and equivalent strain appears reasonable considering 
that active slip systems can be rearranged into configurations more 
compatible to slip transmission from grain (I) via (II) into (III). Improved 
ductility during hot working has been reported upon slow cooling of 5 
◦
C/min and assigned to coarsened GB- γ’ and/or reduced intragranular γ’ 
precipitates [14,15] . Differences in thermo-mechanical processing must 
be discussed in order to establish a link between experimental studies 
and expectations provided by the present CP-FEM model: (i) Our model 
is calibrated based on tensile testing at ambient temperatures [43,44] . 
Increased testing temperatures can affect slip band formation and 
introduce additional failure modes [66] . For example, cube slip has been 
reported to be active in addition to octahedral slip [41] . Therefore, 
strictly speaking, our conclusions must be restricted to ambient tem -
perature mechanical properties. (ii) No impact of γ’ precipitates has 
been considered. Indeed, coarsening of γ’ precipitates will affect their 
strengthening contribution [67,68] . For example, coarsening promotes 
the transition from shearing towards Orowan looping mechanisms. 
While these effects are not considered in the present study, their impact 
on the ductility appears to be similar [14,15] . A more detailed CP-FEM 
model incorporating GB- γ’ and M
6
C will be a necessary part of a future 
study on the local mechanical properties. Thus, it would be more 
reasonable for GB- γ’ coarsening and slip system rearrangement to 
amplify their impact on the ductility. (iii) The modeled volume is small 
to provide accurate correlation with experimental observations and al -
lows conclusions only on a local scale. While large model volumes are 
desirable, the local impact may be transferrable on a larger scale due to 
the significant increase in Σ 3 twin boundaries into the HAGB network 
shown in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 . For future studies, however, it would be 
desirable to implement the appropriate morphology and orientations in 
a larger model.

5. Conclusions
We propose a precipitation-controlled grain boundary engineering 
approach for Ni-based superalloys with excellent high temperature 
strength and limited processability. We showcase our results on the cast 
& wrought Ni-based superalloy Ren ´e 41 via simple heat treatments 
involving slow-cooling which can be implemented on industrial scale. 
Electron microscopy and crystal plasticity finite element methods are 
used to reveal grain boundary microstructure evolution and to establish 
the link between thermo-mechanical processing, GB precipitation, GB 
serration, and their potential for grain boundary engineering and 
providing improvements to ductility. The following conclusions are 
drawn: 
• The concurrent presence of GB- γ’ and M
6
C is a prerequisite to unlock 
precipitation-controlled grain boundary engineering.
• Slow cooling of 5 
◦
C/min facilitates heterogeneous nucleation of 
GB- γ’ on M
6
C to introduce GB serrations.
• Growth of GB- γ’ facilitates the formation of Σ 3 twin boundaries 
across HAGBs. For the example of a Σ 9 ′ boundary, the reaction Σ 9 ′ → 
Σ 3 ′ + Σ 3 takes place.
• Zener-Smith pinning exercised by M
6
C is necessary for the Σ 3 ′ 
boundary to remain static, while the Σ 3 twin boundary migrates into 
the parent grain interior during GB- γ’ coarsening.
• Crystal plasticity finite element method reveals that the rearrange -
ment of active slip systems into configurations more compatible for 
slip transmission facilitates a more ductile mechanical response.
Precipitation-controlled grain boundary engineering is a promising 
approach for highly alloyed cast & wrought Ni-based superalloys and 
may be applicable to other low-stacking fault materials with complex 
grain boundary microstructures.
