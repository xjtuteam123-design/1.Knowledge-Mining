ABSTRACT 
In this study, high-performance Cu – 0.69Cr – 0.46Co – 0.34Ti (wt%) alloys with nano-precipitates and nanotwins 
are prepared via a two-step cryorolling and aging process. The effects of Co and Ti on the microstructural 
evolution as well as room-temperature and elevated-temperature mechanical behaviors are investigated. The 
results show that the two-step process produces high-density dislocations, profuse nanotwins into Cu – Cr – Co – Ti 
alloys, and enhances the mechanical properties of the latter significantly. The high-density dislocations provide 
sufficient diffusion paths for Cr atoms and accelerate the transformation of Cr precipitates from face-centered 
cubic (fcc) to body-centered cubic (bcc), resulting in the co-existence of coherent fcc Cr and incoherent bcc Cr 
precipitates. Dislocation strengthening and Orowan precipitation strengthening contribute significantly to the 
strength of the Cu – Cr – Co – Ti alloys. The tensile strength (643 MPa), hardness (201 HV), and electrical con -
ductivity (64.75% IACS) of samples subjected to the two-step process are obtained. Results show that a CoTi 
compound shell inhibits the diffusion of external Cr atoms to the core and restrains the coarsening of Cr pre -
cipitates. Meanwhile, stable nano-precipitates and nanotwins decelerate the progress of recrystallization, thus 
improving the elevated-temperature mechanical behavior. 
1. Introduction 
Copper alloys with the desired combination of mechanical and 
electrical properties are widely applied in industrial fields such as large- 
scale integrated circuit lead frames and spot-welding electrodes [ 1 – 5 ]. 
In the past decade, Cu
–
Cr alloys, as a typical precipitation-strengthened 
alloy, has been investigated extensively owing to its high strength and 
electrical conductivity. After aging treatment, nanoscale Cr precipitates 
promote the strength of the copper matrix, and the extremely low sol -
ubility of Cr atoms in the matrix guarantees high electrical conductivity 
[ 6 – 9 ]. However, when Cu
–
Cr alloys are used at elevated temperatures, 
the precipitates tend to coarsen and thus deteriorate the integrated 
performance [ 10 – 13 ]. Currently, the service conditions of Cu
–
Cr alloys 
are becoming sophisticated, and the existing Cu
–
Cr alloys fail to fulfill 
the requirements of high strength, high electrical conductivity, and good 
high-temperature stability simultaneously. 
The addition of alloying elements, such as Zr, Ti, and Co, into Cu
–
Cr 
alloys has effectively improved their strength, electrical conductivity, 
and high-temperature stability. The added Zr tends to enrich around Cr 
precipitates and restrains their coarsening, thus elevating the softening 
temperature to 550 
◦
C [ 14 , 15 ]. The addition of Ti can decrease the 
stacking fault energy (SFE), increase the driving force of precipitation, 
and delay the growth of grains, resulting in good elevated-temperature 
mechanical properties at the expense of the electrical conductivity 
[ 16 – 19 ]. Co, which is one of the basic elements in superalloys, is 
believed to enhance the thermal stability of the Cu
–
Cr alloys because of 
its low diffusion rate in the copper matrix [ 20 , 21 ]. However, intro -
ducing a single element is not sufficient to improve the mechanical 
properties, electrical conductivity, and high-temperature stability of 
Cu
–
Cr alloys simultaneously. The co-addition of Co and Ti appears to be 
a promising strategy for improving the overall properties of Cu
–

4. Discussion 
4.1. Effects of CII process on microstructures 
4.1.1. Refinement of deformation band 
In addition to the formation of deformation bands on a macroscopic 
scale and the formation of cell blocks and cells on a smaller scale, grain 
subdivision occurred during deformation [ 38 ]. Similar to grain refine -
ment, the refinement of the deformation bands effectively strengthens 
the alloys. At the initial stage of deformation, several slip systems are 
activated within a grain, thus resulting in the subdivisions of the initial 
grains into equiaxed cell blocks [ 30 ]. Increasing the strain yields a more 
uniform structure, and developed cell blocks are formed in most of the 
grains [ 39 ]. Adjacent dislocation cells are bounded by geometrically 
necessary boundaries, including dense dislocation walls and microbands 
[ 39 ]. The refinement of deformation bands requires the dissociation of 
dislocation walls or the stimulation of new dislocation walls within pre- 
existing cell blocks [ 40 ]. At a higher strain, the cell block boundaries are 
inclined parallel to the rolling direction and thus form deformation 
bands with an average size of only one row of cells [ 38 ].

4.1.3. Structural transformation of precipitates 
The general Cr precipitation sequence in the copper matrix is as 
follows: supersaturation solid solution → Guinier – Preston zones (fcc Cr- 
rich phase) → fcc Cr precipitate → order fcc Cr precipitate → incoherent 
bcc Cr precipitate [ 9 , 46 ]. Because of the lower nucleation barrier, the 
nucleation of fcc Cr precipitates is much easier than that of bcc Cr pre -
cipitates [ 37 ]. At the early stage of aging, coherent fcc Cr precipitates 
nucleate first. As the aging treatment progresses, the fcc Cr precipitates 
develop gradually and transform into bcc Cr precipitates. The pro -
portions of fcc and bcc Cr precipitates are controlled by both the 
nucleation rate and nucleation latency time [ 37 ]. Wang et al. argued 
that the structural stability and phase transformation process are closely 
related to the formation and cohesive energies of the intermetallic 
phases [ 47 ]. The lower the formation and cohesive energies, the more 
stable are the precipitates. According to first-principles calculations 
[ 47 ], once the local Cr content exceeds 60%, the formation energy and 
cohesion energy of the bcc Cr precipitate becomes significantly lower 
than those of the fcc Cr precipitate, resulting in the transformation from 
fcc Cr precipitates to bcc precipitates. In addition, the addition of 
alloying elements such as Zr and Ti inhibits the fcc – bcc transformation 
of Cr precipitates [ 48 ]. In this study, coherent fcc Cr precipitates were 
present in the RI samples, whereas coherent fcc and incoherent bcc Cr 
precipitates co-existed in the CII samples. This phenomenon is closely 
related to the high-density of dislocations in the CII samples. Cryorolling 
suppresses the annihilation of dislocations, resulting in high-density 
dislocations in the Cu matrix. The higher-density dislocations provide 
many preferable nucleation sites for the precipitates. Additionally, they 
provide more diffusion paths for Cr atoms, thereby accelerating the 
formation of bcc Cr precipitates.

4.3. Elevated-temperature mechanical properties 
The elevated-temperature mechanical properties of metallic mate -
rials are affected by many factors such as inclusions, second-phase 
particles, grain boundaries, and dislocations [ 58 ]. For deformed cop -
per alloys, the mechanical properties at elevated temperatures are pri -
marily determined by microstructural instabilities, such as the 
coarsening of precipitates as well as the recovery and recrystallization of 
the matrix. In Cu
–
Cr alloys, Cr precipitates are prone to coarsening at 
elevated temperatures, thus resulting in an unfavorable softening 
resistance. As shown in Table 4 , the elevated-temperature tensile 
strength improved significantly with the addition of Co and Ti. The 
improved elevated-temperature tensile strength is closely related to the 
Co/Ti suppression of the coarsening of Cr precipitates. The diffusion 
coefficients of Co and Ti in the copper matrix are smaller than those in 
the copper matrix, and these two elements have limited solid solubilities 
in Cr [ 27 ]. First, Cr precipitates form in the copper matrix. As the Cr 
precipitates develop further, Co and Ti tend to enrich around the Cr 
precipitates and then form CoTi compound at the interface of the Cr 
precipitates and copper matrix [ 27 ]. The stable CoTi compound shell 
inhibits the diffusion of external Cr atoms to the core, thus restraining 
the coarsening of the Cr precipitates. 
Fig. 13 summarizes the fractions of different types of grains in the RI 
and CII samples at various temperatures. The corresponding EBSD maps

recrystallized and substructured grains represent grains that underwent 
recrystallization and recovery, respectively. As shown in Fig. 13 , as the 
tensile temperature increased, the fraction of the substructured and 
recrystallized grains increased at the expense of the deformed grains, 
thus reducing the tensile strength of both the RI and CII samples.

5. Conclusion 
The microstructures and elevated-temperature mechanical behaviors 
of Cu – Cr – Co – Ti alloys subjected to different rolling-aging processes 
were investigated in this study. The main conclusions are as follows: 
(1) The two-step cryorolling and aging process yielded high-density 
dislocations and profused nanotwins into the Cu – Cr – Co – Ti al -
loys, thus enhancing their mechanical properties. 
(2) The high-density dislocations in the CII samples provided suffi -
cient diffusion paths for Cr atoms and accelerated the trans -
formation of precipitates, thus resulting in the co-existence of 
coherent fcc Cr and incoherent bcc Cr precipitates. 
(3) Dislocation strengthening and precipitation strengthening 
contributed significantly to the tensile strength of both the RI and 
CII samples. The samples subjected to the two-step cryorolling 
and aging process showed the following properties: tensile 
strength, 643 MPa; hardness, 201 HV; and electrical conductivity, 
64.75% IACS. 
(4) The CoTi compound shell inhibited the diffusion of external Cr 
atoms to the core and restrained the coarsening of Cr precipitates. 
Stable nano-precipitates and nanotwins improved the elevated- 
temperature mechanical behavior.
