Microcracking in multipass weld metal of alloy
690 Part 2 – Microcracking mechanism in
reheated weld metal
K. Nishimoto *1, K. Saida 1, H. Okauchi 2 and K. Ohta 3
To elucidate the microcracking (ductility dip cracking) mechanism in the multipass weld metal of
alloy 690, the hot ductility of the reheated weld metal was evaluated using three different filler
metals with varying contents of impurity elements such as P and S. Hot ductility of the weld metal
decreased at temperatures over 1400 K, and the weld metal containing a low quantity of impurity
elements showed much higher ductility than that containing a high quantity of impurity elements.
Local deformability at high temperature of the alloy 690 reheated weld metal was compared with
that of Invar alloy. Grain boundary sliding in alloy 690 occurred not in the intermediate
temperature range (800–1000 K), where grain boundary sliding was activated in Invar alloy, but at
high temperatures just below the melting temperature of alloy 690. The computer simulation of
microsegregation suggested that the deterioration of hot ductility is caused by the grain boundary
segregation of impurity elements during the multiple thermal cycling. The ductility dip cracking in
the reheated weld metal resulted predominantly from the embrittlement of grain boundaries due to
the imbalance between intergranular strength and intragranular strength at high temperature.
Keywords: Alloy 690, Invar alloy, Impurity elements, Reheated weld metal, Ductility dip cracking, Hot ductility, Local deformability, Grain boundary
segregation
Introduction
The Ni base superalloy 690 exhibits a high hot cracking
susceptibility during multipass welding. In the ﬁrst
report in the present series,
1 microcracks occurring in
the multipass weld metal of alloy 690 were characterised
as being predominantly ductility dip cracks that
occurred at austenitic grain boundaries (solidiﬁcation
boundaries). One of the most characteristic aspects of
the ductility dip cracking behaviour of the present
reheated weld metal of alloy 690 was that the cracking
temperature range was situated at higher temperatures,
just below the melting point (1350–1600 K).
Furthermore, the ductility dip cracking susceptibility is
obviously heightened with increasing contents of P and
S in the ﬁller metal. According to previous research,
2–7
the primary cause of microcracking (ductility dip
cracking) in alloy 690 is suggested to be the reduction
of hot ductility during cooling from welding, attributed
to grain boundary segregation. In addition, it has been
generally understood that the ductility dip temperature
range, commonly referred to as ductile temperature
range (DTR) of other Ni base superalloys is located
in the intermediate temperature range close to the
recrystallisation temperature (approximately 1000 K).
However, further details of the mechanism of ductility
dip cracking in multipass weldments of alloy 690 are
uncertain. In particular, the reason why the cracking
temperature range is situated at high temperatures
above 1350 K has not yet been determined.
In the present second report, the mechanism of
microcracking (ductility dip cracking) in the multipass
weld metal of alloy 690 is discussed from the viewpoint
of the hot ductility, especially the local deformability of
the reheated weld metal at high temperature and the
grain boundary segregation of impurity elements.
Materials and experimental procedures
The base metal used in the present work is a commercial
alloy 690. Three types of ﬁller metal with varying
contents of impurity elements were used. The chemical
compositions of the base metal and ﬁller metals are
given in Table 1. The principal variables in the ﬁller
metals were mainly P and S content. The diameter of the
ﬁller metal rods is 1 .2 mm. A fully austenitic alloy,
Invar, was also employed for comparison with alloy 690
in the investigation of local deformability. The Invar
alloy possesses a high microcracking (ductility dip crack-
ing) susceptibility, and is typical in having a cracking
temperature range located at intermediate temperature

scopy (AES) using a hydrogen charged specimen. The
AES analysis was carried out at the intergranular
surface produced by fracture in the AES apparatus at
room temperature.
Hot ductility of reheated weld metal
Hot ductility of the reheated weld metals FF1, FF3, and
FF5 was evaluated via the Gleeble test. Hot ductility,
evaluated as the reduction of area of the reheated weld
metals at high temperature, is shown in Fig. 3. Hot
ductility decreases with increasing test temperature for
all the ﬁller metals. The reduction of area of the weld
metal FF1 decreases to less than about 20% at 1373 K;
however, those of FF3 and FF5 recover slightly in the
temperature range above 1500 K. Hot ductility of the
weld metal FF1 is the lowest of the three, and those of
FF3 and FF5 are almost equal. Observation of the
fracture surface via SEM after the Gleeble test revealed
that intergranular fracture took place at 1073–1573 K in
all ﬁller metals and that the surface morphology was
similar to that of the ductility dip cracking surface in
the reheated weld metal. Figure 4 shows the relation
between the total length of ductility dip cracking and hot
ductility at 1473 and 1573 K, which lie in the typical
temperature range of cracking obtained via the spot
Varestraint test. The ductility dip cracking susceptibility
increases linearly with decreasing hot ductility of the
weld metal. It follows that microcracking in the reheated
weld metal could be predominantly attributed to the
deterioration of ductility at high temperature.

Microsegregation of impurity elements
at grain boundary
AES analysis of weld metal
To elucidate the causes of deterioration of hot ductility
(embrittlement of the austenitic grain boundary), the
grain boundary segregation of impurity elements in the
weld metal was investigated. Figure 6 shows the AES
traces on the intergranular and intragranular fracture
surfaces of the weld metal FF1. It can be seen that P and
S are segregated on the intergranular fracture surface,
whereas P and S are not evident on the intragranular
fracture surface. This observation suggests that the
deterioration of hot ductility could be attributed to the
grain boundary segregation of P and S in the reheated
weld metal.

Theoretical model of microsegregation in reheated weld
metal
As described in Part 1 of the present work, microcracks
in the multipass weld metal of alloy 690 were propagated
preferentially along non-migrated austenitic grain
boundaries which were coincident with solidiﬁcation
boundaries (dendrite boundaries).

present simulation was based on the solidiﬁcation
segregation as well as grain boundary segregation

8,9 i.e. the

the binding strength of grain boundaries due to the
inhibition of the grain boundary segregation of P and S
in the reheated weld metal.
Conclusions
1. Hot ductility of the reheated weld metal decreased
at temperatures above 1400 K, and ductility was much
higher for the ﬁller metal containing the low concentra-
tion of impurity elements than for that containing the
high concentration of impurity elements. The ductility
dip cracking in the reheated weld metal was caused
mainly by the embrittlement of grain boundaries due
to the imbalance between intergranular strength and
intragranular strength at high temperature.
2. The local deformability of the reheated weld metal
of alloy 690 was compared with that of Invar alloy. The
grain boundary sliding of alloy 690 occurred not in the
intermediate temperature range (800–1000 K), where
the grain boundary sliding was activated in Invar alloy,
but at high temperatures just below melting tempera-
ture, because alloy 690 maintained its relatively high
intragranular strength up to the higher temperature
range compared with Invar alloy.
3. Analysis of the weld metal FF1 via AES revealed
that P and S were segregated on the intergranular
fracture surface. This evidence suggested that the
deterioration of hot ductility could be attributed to
the grain boundary segregation of P and S in the
reheated weld metal.
4. Theoretical analysis of microsegregation of P and S
to grain boundaries during the multipass weld thermal
cycle revealed that P and S concentrations at grain
boundaries in the ductility dip cracking temperature
range attained values about 50 and 160 times as high as
their initial contents, respectively.
5. The ductility dip cracking in the reheated weld
metal resulted predominantly from the embrittlement of
grain boundaries due to the imbalance between inter-
granular strength and intragranular strength at the high
temperature.
