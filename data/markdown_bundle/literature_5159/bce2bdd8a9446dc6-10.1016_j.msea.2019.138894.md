Abstract Abstract Abstract Abstract 
Ni-based superalloys IN718 and IN713LC have been jo ined through linear friction welding (LFW) in 
this study. The variation of microstructure across the weld line developed during linear friction 
welding and after post weld heat-treatment (PWHT) h as been investigated. Their effects on 
microhardness have also been studied. A clean weld region which is free of micro-porosity, micro-
cracking and oxides, was achieved. Dynamic recrystallisation (full and partial) occurred on both sides 
of the weld, which produced much finer grains in the recrystallised zone. Dissolution of ‘parent’ γʹ/γʹʹ , 
towards the weld line was observed on each side of the weld. However, reprecipitation of γ΄ was only 
found in the as-welded IN713LC. All these were found to have a huge impact on the hardness profile. 
A softer heat affected zone (HAZ) was found in the IN718 side with the lowest hardness value 
achieved at an axial position 0.6mm from the weld l ine. The increased dissolution of γ´/γ´´ towards 
the weld line resulted in decreasing hardness towards the weld line. However, the formation of refined 
grains closer to the weld line increased the hardness towards the weld line from axial position 0.6mm. 
In contrast, a harder HAZ was found in the IN713LC side, which resulted from the formation of finer 
reprecipitated γ´ and recrystallised grains. PWHT brought about rep recipitation and/or further 
reprecipitation of γʹ/γʹʹ in the IN718 and IN713LC HAZs, resulting in stronger HAZs. 
Keywords : Linear friction welding; Ni-based superalloys; IN 718; IN713LC; Microstructure;

1. Introduction 1. Introduction 1. Introduction 1. Introduction 
Given the potential for weight saving and thus incr eased fuel efficiency, joining different components 
using welding is very desirable for the aerospace i ndustry [1, 2]. Ni-based superalloys are one of the 
most important group of structural materials used i n the aerospace industry due to their excellent 
mechanical properties at high temperature, includin g high tensile strength, high fatigue strength and 
good creep resistance. Unfortunately, most modern Ni-based superalloys which contain a high volume 
fraction of γʹ(≥40%), are considered as “unweldable” using conventi onal fusion welding due to the 
occurrence of micro-shrinkage, intergranular liquation cracking, and/or strain-age cracking associated 
with melting, solidification and PWHT, respectively [3-5]. To solve this issue, solid-state friction

welding has been developed, which does not bring ab out the problems associated with melting and 
solidification. Linear friction welding (LFW) is on e type of solid state friction welding, the process es 
of which have been documented in detail elsewhere [ 6-9], and was used in this paper to join two Ni-
based superalloys IN718 and IN713LC. 
IN718 is a traditional wrought superalloy, mainly hardened by the ordered γ΄΄ (~ 15.5 vol.%) and γ΄ (~ 
4.4 vol.%) [3]. It is largely used in aeroengines d ue to its high strength and moderate creep resistan ce 
up to 650ºC, but the instability of the primary str engthening phase γ΄΄ after long exposure at 
temperatures above 650ºC, limits the service temper ature. IN718 is one of the candidate materials for 
the low pressure turbine disks. Polycrystalline cas t IN713LC (hereinafter called IN713) benefits from 
a high volume fraction of ordered γ΄ (~ 65 vol.%) and a large grain size (0.3-3mm), sho ws high 
strength and excellent creep properties at elevated temperature. It is one of the candidate materials for 
the low pressure turbine blades. Such dissimilar joint (IN718-IN713) could be highly attractive for the 
aeroengine industry due to the ability to achieve d ifferent mechanical properties of one hybrid, i.e. 
excellent creep resistance from IN713 for the part exposed to higher temperature ( ≥650°C), high 
tensile and fatigue strength from IN718 for the par t used at temperature below 650°C. It also has the 
possibility of replacing the mechanical fir-tree as sembly, giving a weight saving and improved fuel 
efficiency. 
It is now well established that the interface mater ial of Ni-welds is exposed to extremely high 
temperatures (50-120°C below solvus[10]), severe pl astic deformation (of the order of 1000s 
-1 [11]), 
and very rapid cooling rates (up to 63°C/s [12]) du ring linear friction welding. Therefore, significan t 
variation in microstructure across the weld line is expected. This would result in variation in 
mechanical properties over a small region. Hence, m icrohardness mapping is one of the best ways to 
show the strength of different regions of the whole weld. Dynamic recrystallisation, dissolution of 
strengthening phase ( γ΄ /γ΄΄ ), reprecipitation of fine reprecipitated γ΄ and variation in chemical 
composition are commonly found in friction welded N i-based superalloys [10, 12-17], but the details 
vary with welding processes and parent microstructu re. Therefore, a comprehensive microstructure 
study is essential for each weld condition. 
To date, some sound and crack-free similar Ni-welds have been successfully joined by LFW such as 
IN738 [18], Waspaloy [10, 12], GH4169[17] and AD730
TM [16], but very little work has been reported 
for dissimilar Ni-welds in the open literature. Com pared with similar welds, it is generally more 
difficult to achieve successful dissimilar welds du e to the different thermal and mechanical propertie s 
of the two parent materials. One early paper on lin ear friction welding of single crystal CMSX4 to 
polycrystalline Ni-based superalloy RR1000 was repo rted by Karadge et al.[19], which specify the 
difficulties in welding two dissimilar Ni-alloys wi th extremely different microstructures. The authors 
also briefly mentioned the variation of microstruct ure across the weld line but no mechanical 
properties of the weld were given.

Due to the huge difference between the microstructu re of IN718 and IN713, with ~45% difference in 
volume of strengthening phase ( γ΄ /γ΄΄ ) and more than 30 times difference in grain size, it could be 
very difficult to achieve sound integrity of a diss imilar joint. This paper aims to assess the integri ty 
and one of the mechanical properties, namely microh ardness, of the linear friction welded dissimilar 
Ni-weld IN718-IN713. The weld is assessed in both the as-welded (AWed) and post-weld heat treated 
(PWHTed) conditions. A comprehensive characterision of microstructure (e.g. grain size, γ΄ and γ΄΄ ) 
and chemical composition across the weld line has b een carried out. Characterision of the grain 
structure was conducted using the electron backscat ter diffraction (EBSD). High resolution field 
emission gun scanning electron microscopy (FEGSEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) 
were used for characterising the γ΄ and γ΄΄ microstructure. Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) 
microanalysis was undertaken to evaluate the variat ion of chemical compositions. This paper is 
believed to be one of few currently available to fo cus on a metallurgical study of the dissimilar Ni-
weld joined by linear friction welding.

Microhardness profiles were measured on both the AW ed and PWHTed samples using a Struers

cover both IN718 and IN713LC, tests were performed 0.02mm away from the weld line for each side 
instead in order to have a better understanding of hardness behaviour on each individual side.

3.3.1 Metallurgical features in the weld region 
After linear friction welding, a narrow δ phase free zone of width 115µm was found in the 
IN718 weld region (Fig. 8). Dissolution of δ phase clearly indicates that the highest 
temperature during welding at the weld interface was above the δ phase solvus temperature of 
1000 ℃ [3]. Unlike the δ phase, Nb and Ti rich MC carbides were still prese nt close to the 
weld line after welding (Fig. 8), i.e. MC carbides did not dissolve during the welding process. 
This indicates that the peak temperature during wel ding was below the solvus temperature of 
MC carbide (~1260°C) [25]. Thus, the peak temperatu re during welding is expected to be in 
the range of 1000~1260°C. However, this temperature range is well above the solvus 
temperature of strengthening phases γ´´ (910°C [26]) and γ´ (~ 900°C [27]) in IN718. Thus, 
the strengthening phases close to the weld line in IN718 are expected to have fully dissolved 
during welding. Indeed, no γ´´/ γ´ was found in the region close to the weld line, a s shown by 
TEM (Fig. 9a). The situation is completely differen t in the AWed IN713 side (Fig. 9d). For 
example, ‘secondary’ γ´ was found in the IN713 WZ but no primary γ´ (which has a higher 
solvus temperature) was observed. These suggest that the ‘secondary’ γ´ must have re-formed 
after welding upon cooling, and is termed as reprec ipitated γ´ here to distinguish it from the 
parent secondary γ´. Huang et al.[3] found similar results in inertia friction welded IN718 - 
Alloy720Li in which reprecipitated γ΄ was only found in the weld region of Alloy720Li which 
has a higher volume fraction of γ΄ (~45%). They [3] suggested that the absence of 
reprecipitated γʹ in the IN718 side was mainly due to the much lower concentration of γʹ/γʹʹ 
forming elements in solution, which might not be hi gh enough to allow the formation of 
reprecipitated γʹ/γʹʹ upon fast cooling. However, the driving force for the reprecipitation of γʹ 
upon cooling would be largely increased in the Alloy720Li, which had a much higher V 
f of γʹ. 
This is also likely to be the case for the current weld. Similar results have also been found in 
other linear friction welded similar Ni-based super alloys, e.g. no reprecipitation of γ´ was 
found in AWed Waspaloy after linear friction weldin g [13], which contains a low volume 
fraction of γ´ (25-30%). However, in linear friction welded RR10 00 (~45% γ´) and CMSX4 
(~70% γ´) which both contained a high V f of γ´, reprecipitation of γ´ was found in the AWed 
condition [4].

Hardness of Ni-based superalloys can be significant ly affected by chemical composition, 
grain size and ordered γ΄ /γ΄΄ precipitates, correlating with solid solution stre ngthening, grain 
boundary strengthening and precipitation hardening. As can be seen in section 3.4, the 
variation of chemical composition is limited to the weld region (i.e., ~ 2.5µm in IN718 and

expected in either the IN718 or the IN713 sides. The variations of grain size and γ´/ γ´´ (Vf and 
size) are thus believed to have the most profound e ffects on the variation of hardness on both 
sides of the weld in this study.

5. Conclusions 5. Conclusions 5. Conclusions 5. Conclusions 
In this study, two Ni-based superalloys, a wrought IN718 and a cast IN713, were joined 
together using linear friction welding. Different m icrostructures of IN718 and IN713 brings 
about different responses to the linear friction welding. The variation of microstructure across 
the weld line has been studied in both the as-welde d and PWHTed conditions. Their impacts 
on the microhardness have also been evaluated. The main findings of this study can be 
summarised as follows: 
1. A defect free weld zone was achieved. 
2. A narrow zone of chemical composition change from t hat of IN718 to IN713 was found 
at the weld region (~2.5 µm in IN718 side and ~1.0 µm in IN713 side). 
3. Dynamic (full + partial) recrystallisation occurred at both sides of the weld line, 
contributing to hardness increase in both HAZs. 
4. In the AWed samples, gradual dissolution of strengt hening phases towards the weld line 
was found on both sides of the weld. However, repre cipitation of γ´ was only observed in 
the IN713 side which has a much higher volume fract ion of γ´ (~65% in IN713 vs. ~20% 
in IN718). This is the main reason for the harder H AZ in the IN713 side and softer HAZ 
in the IN718 side. 
5. After PWHT, formation of reprecipitated γ´/ γ´´ was observed on both sides of the weld, 
correlating with a steep increase in hardness towar ds the weld line. The maximum 
hardness in the IN713 and IN718 sides are 515 and 522HV, much higher than those of the 
parent materials, 435HV for IN718 parent and 380HV for IN713 parent. 
6. Overall, the higher hardness in the HAZs after PWHT compared to those in parent 
materials is the combined effects of the formation of reprecipitated γ´, the formation of 
recrystallised finer grains and work hardening.
