Abstract—In the present work we study the formation of grooves and ledges (typical size: <100 nm) at c/c0 interfaces of single crystal Ni-base super-
alloys. We highlight previous work which documents the presence of such interface irregularities and shows that their number and size increases
during high temperature exposure and creep. We use diﬀraction contrast stereo transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to provide new evidence
for the presence of ledges and grooves near dislocations at c/c
0 interfaces after heat treatment and creep. We present a 2D model of the interfacial
region which shows how dislocation stress ﬁelds alter local chemical potentials and drive diﬀusional ﬂuxes which result in the formation of a groove.
The results of the numerical study yield realistic groove sizes in relevant time scales. The results obtained in the present study suggest that the for -
mation of grooves and ledges represents an elementary process which needs to be considered when rationalizing the kinetics of rafting, the direction al
coarsening of the c
0 phase.

1. Introduction
Single crystal Ni-base superalloys (SXs) show a high
creep resistance and are used to make blades for gas tur-
bines e.g. [1–6]. They are produced by a directional solidiﬁ-
cation process followed by a multiple step heat treatment.
The resulting cast microstructure consists of dendrites
and interdendritic regions. Post cast solution heat treat-
ments homogenize the material. During subsequent pre-
cipitation treatments the well-known c/c
0 microstructure
forms, which consists of c0 cubes (ordered crystal structure:
L12, typical volume fraction: 80 vol.%, typical average cube
edge length: 0.5 lm) separated by thin c channels (fcc crys-
tal structure, typical volume fraction: 20%, typical average
channel width: 0.1 lm). Throughout the present study we
refer to the c
0 particles which form during the precipitation
treatments as primary c0 particles. The interfaces between
the c0 cubes and the c channels are coherent. The lattice
constant of the c0 phase, ac0 , is often a little smaller than
that of the c matrix ac (ac0 < ac). This gives rise to a negative
misﬁt d (d = 1/2 /C1 (ac0 /C0 ac)/(ac0 + ac)). The cuboidal shape
of the particles minimizes the strain energy because the elas-
tic moduli of both phases have minima along h100 i direc-
tions. The negative misﬁt is associated with misﬁt stresses
as high as 500 MPa e.g. [7]. A schematic illustration of
stress components associated with crystallographic misﬁt
is shown in Fig. 1 a. After a full heat treatment cycle and
prior to creep, dislocation densities in Ni-base superalloys
are low. Grown in nests of dislocations have been observed
after solidiﬁcation [7]. Dislocations have been reported to
form near pores in interdendritic regions [8]. Individual dis-
location segments were also observed at c/c0 interfaces prior
to creep [7,8]. These dislocation segments can act as dislo-
cation sources, which in the early stages of high tem-
perature and low stress creep provide dislocations which
ﬁll all c channels. In Fig. 1 b we discuss c channel disloca-
tion plasticity for a simpliﬁed system with only two active
microscopic crystallographic slip systems. Fig. 1 b shows
two pairs of dislocations of opposite sign which glide on
two intersecting glide planes. Under an external stress
r > 0 the two dislocation dipoles widen, Fig. 1b. The dislo-
cations are drawn as edge dislocations, which have com-
pressive and tensile zones. During high temperature and
low stress creep, dislocations enter the c channels as leading

the c channels) and screw (in 1/3 of the c channels) disloca-

Dislocation networks are also a prominent feature of
crept microstructures [e.g. 8,14–22], Fig. 1d. However, after
h100 i tensile creep, there is a diﬀerence in network spacings
between vertical and horizontal c channels. The dislocation
density in horizontal channels is high because the external
stress drives dislocations which accommodate the misﬁt.
Therefore, horizontal channels attract dislocations. In con-
trast, the same type of dislocations have the opposite sign
than the misﬁt dislocations in the vertical channels. Here,
instead of accommodating misﬁt they increase the elastic
strain energy. Therefore, in Fig. 1 d, the dislocation density
in the vertical channels is lower than in the horizontal chan-
nels. This diﬀerence can be detected using X-ray line proﬁle
analysis [23,24].
Rafting of the c/c0 microstructure is a well-known
microstructural instability [e.g. 25–33] . When a negative
misﬁt single crystal is subjected to h100 i tensile creep load-
ing in the high temperature low stress regime, directional
coarsening of the c0 phase occurs. c0 rafts form perpen-
dicular to the direction of the applied stress, Fig. 2 a. It is
well-known that for a negative misﬁt alloy rafts form par-
allel to a h100 i compression stress direction [25,26,34,35],
Fig. 2 b. Rafts also form parallel to the direction of an
applied h100 i tensile creep load when there is a positive
misﬁt, like in the case of most Co-base SXs with c/c0
microstructures [36,37].
Rafting can be induced by dislocations around a hard-
ness imprint [38], Fig. 2 c. It occurs during stress free
annealing after dislocations were introduced by h100 i ten-
sile loading to small strains (<1%) at a constant c/c0
microstructure [39]. Rafting was found after plastic defor-
mation caused by shot peening [40]. Stresses associated
with the coexistence of dendrites and interdendritic regions
in the cast microstructure have been reported to induce
rafting [41], Fig. 2 d. Rafting rates were reported to diﬀer
between dendrites and interdendritic regions [42]. In bend-
ing of a h100 i oriented single crystal rod, rafting directions
diﬀer above and below the neutral axis [43–45]. Shear creep
loading of the macroscopic crystallographic h1 /C0 10 i{1 1 0}
shear system results in rafting in 45 /C176to the direction of
the applied shear stress [46–48]. Rafting sensitively reacts
to the stress state in front of a crack of an SX superalloy
[49]. It was characterized in an ex-service turbine blade
[50]. Small diﬀerences in crystallographic orientation can
alter raft directions, as has been pointed out by Agudo
et al. [8] who compared their own data from precisely ori-
ented h110 i tensile experiments with data from tensile
creep experiments with a slight deviation from h110 i [51].
Material scientists have also performed basic experimen-
tal and theoretical work to assess the eﬀect of alloy ele-
ments like Re on the rafting kinetics of model systems
[e.g. 52,53] . Rafting is closely related to the phenomenon
of Ostwald ripening [54–56]. It has been analyzed as a pure-
ly elastic phenomenon where the particle shape evolves
toward a best compromise between surface energy and elas-
tic strain energy e.g. [57–59]. But since rafting always
occurs in the presence of c channel dislocations e.g. [8,31],
elastic–plastic treatments of rafting appear to be more real-
istic. Zhou et al. [60] claim that neglecting the contribution
of channel plasticity to rafting results in much longer times
required to form the same raft microstructure. Our brief
compilation of previous work shows that many macroscop-
ic aspects of rafting are reasonably well understood and
that researchers appreciate the need of accounting for the
eﬀect of c channel dislocations. For example, it has been
suggested that the transport of matter during rafting can
be linked to the climb of c channel dislocations e.g.
[16,61]. But so far, this view was not supported by
experimental results.
Rafting depends on the diﬀusion of atoms and on the
physical nature of c/c0 interfaces. One important observa-
tion, the formation of grooves and ledges at c/c0 interfaces,
has so far only received limited attention. Kolbe et al. [62]

Their TEM images document the presence of ledges at c/

Link, Epishin and co-workers [63,64] showed that dislo-
cations leave traces in the c phase of a single crystal, when
the c0 phase is etched out [63]. They used this technique to
describe the formation of grooves for the single crystal
superalloys SRR99 and CMSX 4 [64]. They describe diﬀer-
ent scenarios which can account for the formation of
grooves [64]. The swelling mechanism describes the outward
movement of c/c0 interfaces during redisposition of c0 form-
ing elements from oversaturated c channels during slow
cooling [64]. During the indentation mechanism, a misﬁt dis-
location is pushed into the c0 particle creating a groove [64].
The movement mechanism [64] reﬂects what had been earlier
proposed by Kolbe et al. [62]. While the swelling and inden-
tation mechanisms result in the formation of symmetric
grooves, the movement mechanism [64] creates an asym-
metric ledge. Finally, Link and co-workers [64] consider
an orientation hypothesis , which is associated with osmotic
forces acting on interface dislocations. These forces arise
from the presence of excess vacancies [65]. Link and co-
workers favor the re-deposition scenario [64]. This requires
that the fraction of the c
0 phase which was dissolved during
high temperature exposure re-precipitates during cooling,
either in dislocation free regions at c/c0 interfaces, and/or
as new small c0 particles in the c channels in some distance
of the c/c0 interface [64]. In the present work we refer to the-
se small c0 particles as secondary c0 particles.
Vorontsov et al. [66] documented local distortions at c/c0
interfaces caused by the stress ﬁelds of dislocations after
deformation at 750 /C176C. At 750 /C176C no signiﬁcant dissolution
of the c0 phase is expected [67]. Nevertheless, there is evi-
dence for a direct interaction of dislocations with c/c0 inter-
faces [31,62–64,66]. So far, no attempt was made to
quantitatively account for the formation of ledges and
grooves. Grooves/ledges at c/c0 interfaces after high tem-
perature and low stress creep of a SX Ni-base superalloy
were documented using high resolution transmission elec-
tron microscopy (HRTEM) and 3D atom probe tomogra-
phy (3D APT) [68]. HRTEM showed that grooves were
associated with interface dislocations [68]. No local enrich-
ment of alloy elements was detected in groove regions by
3D APT [68].
In the present work we use diﬀraction contrast TEM to
study the formation of grooves and ledges at c/c0 interfaces.
We present a simple thermodynamic/kinetic model which
shows how ledges and grooves form. We do not rule out
the swelling scenario proposed by Epsihin, Link and co-
workers [64] entirely. But our results show that one can also
rationalize the presence of grooves and ledges at c/c0 inter-
faces in the absence of swelling.
2. Material and experiments
In the present work we use the Ni-base single crystal
superalloy ERBO/1 [68], which is a second generation sin-
gle crystal derived from CMSX 4 [69]. All details describing
its heat treatment, chemical composition and microstruc-
ture have been published elsewhere [68]. Creep tests were
performed using a miniature creep specimen test procedure
which has been described in the literature [70,71].
Specimens were deformed at 1323 K and 165 MPa. Tests
were interrupted early in creep life, after strains of the order
of 0.1%, to capture the early stages of high temperature
plasticity where dislocations do not yet cut into the c
0 phase
e.g. [8]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was per-
formed using a FEI Tecnai F20 G 2 S-Twin equipped with a
ﬁeld emission gun (FEG) and a high angle annular dark
ﬁeld (HAADF) detector. Dislocation analysis was per-
formed using a standard procedure relying on the eﬀective
invisibility criteria g /C1 b = 0 and g /C1 (b /C2 n) = 0. We work in
scanning transmission (STEM) mode and combine STEM-
HAADF with stereo microscopy as described by Agudo
et al. [72]. This technique yields anaglyphs which show
the spatial arrangement of the elements of the microstruc-
ture when viewed with colored glasses. The stereo STEM
method described by Agudo and co-workers [72] is par-
ticularly helpful to study microstructural features like
grooves and ledges in c/c
0 interfaces. Disks of 400 lm thick-
ness were cut from the materials. For the crept material
states, specimens were taken perpendicular ((0 0 1) planes)
and parallel ((1 0 0) planes) to the direction of the applied
tensile stress ([0 0 1]). The thin disks were then mechanically
ground down to a thickness of about 100 lm. Electron
transparent foils were then produced by double jet electro-
chemical polishing in a Struers TenuPol 5. The electrolyte
consisted of 75% methanol, 15% glycerol and 10% perchlo-
ric acid. Good thinning conditions were obtained using a
ﬂow rate of 15, an electric tension of 10.5 V and an elec-
trolyte temperature of /C0 5 /C176C. A stereo STEM-HAADF
anaglyph of a symmetric groove close to a c/c
0 interface dis-
location is shown in Fig. 3 a. It shows a part of a c0 cube
corner with grooves observed in ERBO1 after post cast
heat treatment reported elsewhere [68].

4.3. Modeling results
In Fig. 13 we show the modeling results which were
obtained in the present study. Fig. 13 a illustrates how a
groove evolves with time. The position of the dislocation
corresponding to each interface proﬁle is indicated by a
point of the same color. The root of the groove is almost
circular and reﬂects the symmetry of the dislocation stress
ﬁeld associated with the geometry outlined in Fig. 3 b.
The abrupt changes in the inclination of the interfaces as
we move from the notch root outward reﬂects the change
from the attractive to the repulsive part of the force ﬁeld
exerted by the dislocation. As can be seen in Fig. 13a, only
the ﬁrst blue interface proﬁle exhibits regions which directly
reﬂect re-deposition of material from the groove. All other
interface proﬁles associated with longer diﬀusion times
show no signs of re-deposition. Fig. 13 b shows how the
depth d (distance between the notch root and the original
interface) and the width w of the groove (measured at half
depth) evolve with time. Fig. 13 b shows that while the
depth d of a groove steadily increases almost linearly
(200 nm are reached after 300 s), its width w at half depth
soon reaches saturation close to 50 nm. It is important to
highlight that given the large scatter of input parameters
like d /C1 D (taken from [76]) the model results can rationalize
the shapes and the magnitudes of grooves as observed in
the TEM micrographs presented in Figs. 3 and 5–9 .
5. Discussion
5.1. Grooves/ledges and dislocations
High temperature exposure and creep of single crystal
Ni-base superalloys results in rafting which is accompanied
by the formation of grooves and ledges at c/c0 interfaces.
Paris et al. [31] provided clear evidence for the formation
of serrated interfaces using two complementary methods,
small-angle X-ray scattering and TEM ( Fig. 2 in [31]).
Their model superalloy (Ni-Al13.3-Mo8.8) was tensile
loaded at 130 MPa at 1253 K for short times (2–8 h). The
interface irregularity increased with increasing dislocation
density during high temperature exposure. In 1997, Kolbe
et al. [62] documented ledges after shear creep deformation
of CMSX 6 at 85 MPa at 1298 K ( Figs. 7–10 in [62]) and
suggested that the presence of dislocations at c/c
0 interfaces
has thermodynamic and kinetic consequences which causes
the formation of these ledges. More recently, Vorontosov
et al. [66] observed the formation of serrated c/c
0 interfaces
in CMSX 4 after low temperature and high stress creep at
1023 K in the absence of rafting and rationalized the pres-
ence of the surface irregularities as local distortions caused
by the stress ﬁelds of dislocations. Grooves in CMSX 4
were also documented using high resolution transmission

5.3. Formation of grooves
In Figs. 5a and 6a we show large primary c0 particles,
which dissolve starting from their corners. Next to the dis-
solving corners one ﬁnds small secondary c0 particles in the
adjacent phase regions. We observe such dissolving c0 par-
ticles in a microstructure which mainly consists of particles
with regular interfaces, Fig. 4 (region A – regular
microstructure, region B – dissolving c0 particle with prima-
ry c0 particles in the adjacent c0 phase). The reasons that we
ﬁnd such locations in the present work which have not been
documented previously may be related to the fact that our
STEM–HAADF method allows to study large ﬁelds of
view at a good resolution [72]. Region A in Fig. 4 shows
a superalloy microstructure as it is described in text books
[4,5]. Region B, just a few micrometers away, looks very
diﬀerent as described above. Such regions can be easily
overlooked when trying to ﬁnd characteristic regions focus-
ing on documenting channel widths and c
0 particle shapes
using conventional TEM techniques, as discussed in [72].
It is not easy to explain such pronounced microstructural
diﬀerences. One can imagine that during crystal growth a
few dislocations grow into the solid, as suggested by
Kossel and Stranski [79,80]. They can act as dislocation
sources. These sources can produce dislocations which are
attracted by nearby c/c
0 interfaces, where they change local
thermodynamic and kinetic conditions and promote c0
dissolution as discussed in the present work. The c0 forming
elements dissolve into the c channel, where as a conse-
quence, secondary c0 particles precipitate.
5.4. Re-precipitation of c0 forming elements
In the present paper we promote the idea, that the for-
mation of grooves and ledges is a direct consequence of
the presence of dislocations at c/c0 interfaces. But as has
been pointed out by Link and Epishin et al. [64], the pres-
ence of grooves may also be a result of re-precipitation
(swelling mechanism [64]). The mechanism suggested by
Link, Epishin et al. [64] also builds upon the importance
of dislocations at c0/c interfaces, without explicitly invoking
the eﬀect of interface dislocations on the chemical potential
of atoms as outlined in the present work. Link and Epishin
et al. explain that the fraction of the c
0 phase, which dis-
solves during heating (to high enough temperatures) can
re-precipitate during (slow enough) cooling and form
grooves [64]. As a consequence the interface expands
between the meshes of the interfacial dislocation network.
There is no doubt that the microstructures shown in the
TEM images in Fig. 4 ao f [64] result from re-precipitation.
Link and Epishin et al. [64] show that after heating to
1100 /C176C for 300 h, faster cooling result in more regular
interfaces, Fig. 4 co f [65]. The SEM and TEM results of
Link and Epishin et al. [64] provide valuable information,
including the observation that the spacing of the misﬁt dis-
location networks is almost not aﬀected by the cooling rate
(furnace cooling – slow, air cooling – intermediate, water
quenching – fast). Their work also merits attention because
it highlights a general problem of creep and high tem-
perature materials research: What is observed in post-mor-
tem investigations may well be a result of processes which
are related to slow cooling from high temperatures, not
to the creep itself. One cannot generally rule out the possi-
bility that grooves at c/c
0 interfaces form as a result of the
swelling mechanism proposed by Link and Epsihin et al.
[64]. But there are good reasons which lead us to conclude
that our dislocation stress ﬁeld assisted local dissolution
scenario governs groove/ledge formation in the absence
of swelling: First, water quenching does not suppress the

5.5. Modeling
The model presented in this study is based on the idea
ﬁrst formulated by Gibbs and Thomson, which rationalizes
the eﬀect of a curved interface on thermodynamic equilibri-
um [81,82]. They consider the eﬀect of a hydrostatic pres-
sure associated with curved interfaces. In the present
study we extent their argument to stresses caused by dislo-
cations. The model results show that the formation of a
symmetric groove at a c/c
0 interface can indeed be caused
by the stress ﬁeld of an edge dislocation. In our model we
keep the distance of the dislocation from the interface (root
of the groove) constant at 100b (b: magnitude of the
Burgers vector), i.e. the dislocation stays in the c phase
while sinking into the c
0 phase. In order to obtain results,
which are independent of the interface discretization, the
spacing between discrete points along the interface had to
be kept below 5b. These dimensions approach the physical
limit of a continuum treatment. The TEM micrographs
presented in Figs. 10–12 suggest that dislocations can get
closer to the c/c
0 interface than 100b. This would require
even ﬁner discretization, and limits the present modeling
approach. The model used in the present work is based
on additional ﬁrst order approximations. The physical
properties of the two phases, c and c
0, do not explicitly enter
our calculations and we simply assume that a system in
equilibrium is disturbed by the presence of an interface dis-
location. Moreover, our model assumes elastic isotropy and
crystallographic isotropy of interfacial energies. It also
assumes that ﬂuxes simply scale with a mobility d /C1 D for
diﬀusion along grain boundaries in polycrystalline Ni [76].
One may feel that the evolution of grooves predicted by
our model (200 nm depth in only 300 s, Fig. 13b) is too fast.
This is partly related to a d /C1 D value which is too high.
After all, a coherent c/c0 interface must have a smaller
thickness d than the high angle grain boundaries considered
in [76]. But more importantly, our model only focuses on
the source character of grooves. It does not consider how
matter diﬀuses away from the groove. The rate at which
matter is removed from the groove does not only depend
on the diﬀusion along the interface. TEM micrographs pre-
sented in Figs. 3a and 5–9 suggest that elements diﬀuse
away from grooves in large primary c
0 cubes into the c
channels, where they re-precipitate as small secondary c0
particles. This requires bulk diﬀusion into the c channels.
This constraint on diﬀusional ﬂuxes must be considered
in a comprehensive treatment of diﬀusion controlled groove
formation. Further work is required to account for these
constraints.
5.6. Early suggestions by Kolbe and co-workers
As far as the qualitative thermodynamic scenario pro-
posed by Kolbe and co-workers [62] is concerned, the mod-
el presented in the present work clearly supports the
assumption that dislocations can aﬀect local thermodynam-
ic potentials and drive ﬂuxes. The TEM micrographs in
Figs. 11b and 12 (especially the 3D anaglyph in Fig. 12 b)
support the view of Kolbe and co-workers [62] that the for-
mation of ledges are associated with 60 /C176dislocations which
are deposited in c/c
0 interfaces as leading screw segments
run through the c channels. Kolbe and co-workers [62] have
suggested that the formation of ledges at c/c0 interfaces rep-
resent an elementary process which is related to dislocation
climb, pipe diﬀusion and rafting [62]. This implies, that as c0
forming elements move away from the groove/ledge they
can diﬀuse into the c channel to cause re-precipitation of
small secondary c0 particles. Alternatively, they can con-
tribute to rafting, the directional coarsening of the c0 phase.
While the TEM micrographs in Figs. 11b and 12 can be
interpreted on this basis, further modeling is required to
prove this complex scenario. Further work is also required
to prove that 60 /C176dislocations close to c/c
0 interfaces can
result in the formation of non-symmetric ledges. Groups
of interface dislocations need to be investigated. And fur-
ther work is required to ﬁnd out whether the local processes
which we consider in the present work can be integrated
into a comprehensive treatment of the overall rafting
kinetics.
5.7. Dislocation assisted local dissolution
In the light of the experimental evidence and the model-
ing results presented in this work, dislocation stress ﬁeld
assisted dissolution scenarios seem to be well worth pursu-
ing. They may be important for two reasons. First, because
they may be able to explain basic elementary processes
which govern rafting. Second, they may also help to inter-
pret elementary processes which control the transfer of
interface dislocations from the c channels into the c
0 phase
(cutting processes) in the later stages of creep [78,83–85].
6. Summary and conclusions
In the present study we perform annealing and creep
experiments of single crystal Ni-base superalloys with c/c0
microstructures. We use stereo dark ﬁeld transmission elec-
tron microscopy in the scanning mode (STEM-HAADF) to
investigate ledges and grooves at c/c
0 interfaces which form
during heat treatment and creep. We also make an attempt
to model the growth of grooves driven by dislocation stress
ﬁelds. From the results obtained in the present study, the
following conclusions can be drawn:
(1) Agudo 0s stereo STEM-HAADF technique is a useful
tool to study elementary microstructural processes in
SX Ni-base superalloys. It yields good contrast and
allows investigating large TEM foil areas. c/c0
microstructures are often reported to consist of

regions with regular c0 cuboids, straight c channels and
low dislocation densities. But the present work shows,
that one can also ﬁnd regions, where c

fered local dissolution, c/c0 interfaces look irregular, c

ondary c

Local dissolution events of large primary c0 cubes, dri-
ven by interface dislocations, lead to the re-precipita-
tion of small secondary c
0 particles in the adjacent c
channel.
(2) Previous observations of grooves and ledges at c/c0
interfaces, which form during heat treatment and
creep (Paris and co-workers 1997 [31], Kolbe and
co-workers 1998 [62], Link and co-workers 2011 [64],
Parsa and co-workers 2014 [68]) were conﬁrmed.
Stereo micrographs show the 3D spatial arrangement
of dislocation/groove pairs at c/c0 interfaces. The pre-
sent work also provides clear 3D evidence for the ﬁll-
ing of c channels with dislocations in the early stages
of creep.
(3) In the present work we suggest that grooves and
ledges are the result of local dissolution events which
are triggered by dislocations at c/c0 interfaces.
Through their stress ﬁelds, dislocations can aﬀect the
local chemical potential at c/c0 interfaces and can drive
diﬀusional ﬂuxes which result in groove formation.
Extreme overheating into a temperature regime where
the volume fraction of the primary c
0 particles sig-
niﬁcantly decreases and subsequent slow cooling
may result in swelling as proposed by Link and co-
workers [64]. In the present study we investigate a
material which was not exposed to this type of extreme
overheating. Instead, we provide clear microstructural
evidence for a dislocation assisted dissolution
scenario.
(4) In this work we present a 2D model which shows how
the stress ﬁeld of an edge dislocation close to a c/c
0
interface aﬀects the local thermodynamic potential
and drives diﬀusional ﬂuxes. We show that these diﬀu-
sional ﬂuxes can lead to the formation of a groove.
The numerically predicted groove dimensions are in
reasonable agreement with experimental TEM obser-
vations. However, we focus on the source character
of the grooves. Diﬀusional constraints associated with
the ﬂow of matter away from the grooves are not tak-
en into account.
(5) Further work is required to study the eﬀect of other
types of dislocations (like 60 /C176dislocations which are
deposited in the c/c
0 interfaces during creep) and of
characteristic interface dislocation structures on the
evolution of the interface morphology during anneal-
ing and creep. The ultimate challenge is to integrate
these local events into an overall rafting scenario,
which accounts for all microstructural observations.
