We have investigated the high-temperature solid-solution strengthening by oxygen in Nb, Nb–Ta
and Nb–Mo single crystals by compression tests with constant strain rate. Nb, Nb–20Ta, Nb–40Ta,
Nb–20Mo, and Nb–40Mo single crystals with various O content were grown by use of the elec-
tron-beam floating zone melting method. It is found that Nb and Nb–Ta single crystals are
strengthened by oxygen at temperatures below 1100K, while Nb–20Mo single crystals are
strengthened even at 1473K. The critical resolved shear stress (c.r.s.s.) of Nb–Ta single crystals at
298K increases linearly with increasing O content, irrespective of Ta content. Furthermore, the
increment of c.r.s.s. of Nb–Ta single crystals is larger than that of Nb. The c.r.s.s. of Nb-Mo single
crystals is higher than that of Nb for all temperatures tested, and the c.r.s.s. of Nb–20Mo single
crystals also increases with increasing O content. These results indicate that the interstitial–substi-
tutional (i–s) interaction between O and Ta or Mo affects the mechanical properties in the
Nb-based alloys at high temperature.

In bcc alloys, the deformation at low temperature is governed by screw dislocations.
In an early study of screw dislocations in bcc metals, computer atomistic simulations
revealed the dislocation cores of bcc metals extended in nonplanar manner on three
low-index planes [15]. Chang [16] calculated the interaction energy distribution for
interstitials in Fe–C and the strain field for substitutional atoms. The expanded disloca-
tion core can interact strongly with interstitial or substitutional atoms, and the solid-
solution strengthening occurs. On the other hand, at high temperature, edge disloca-
tions govern the deformation in bcc metals. Solute atoms form the Cottrell atmosphere
around edge dislocations, and thus solution strengthening is provided. For example,
Wang et al. [17] reported computer simulation results of the solute drag of an edge
dislocation.

3.1 Lattice parameter
Lattice parameters of Nb, Ta and Mo have been reported to be 0.33067, 0.32980 and
0.31467 nm, respectively [20], indicating that Mo has a size misfit of about 5% to Nb,
whereas the size misfit of Ta to Nb is quite small. Therefore, based on the size misfits,
Mo is expected to display a remarkable solid solution-strengthening, but Ta is not.
Figure 1 shows the variation of the lattice parameter in Nb–Mo and Nb–Ta alloys
measured by X-ray diffractometry as a function of Mo and Ta contents. The lattice
parameter of Nb–Mo or Nb–Ta alloys decreases linearly with increasing Mo or Ta
content. The decrease is more significant in Nb–Mo alloys than in Nb–Ta alloys. The
obtained results are in fair agreement with reported data [21], while these changes in
lattice parameter are slightly larger than the change of the lattice parameter predicted
assuming Vegard’s rule [20].

some Nb based refractory alloys, and demonstrated that Ta displays a great extent of
trapenergy with O as well as V, Ti and Zr, although the temp erature range of the
measurements was 873–1423 K. The existence of the trapenergy means that Ta inter-
acts attractively with O in Nb. Lauf and Altstetter suggested that if the substitutional

solute has a higher ‘affinity’ for O than the solvent, there will be the i-s interaction that
is roughly proportional to the difference in chemical affinities [24]. A quantitative meas-

the difference in the heat of oxide formation [24]. Since the oxidation enthalpy of Ta is

even at low temperatures.
In our TEM observation results, screw dislocations are predominant at these tem-
peratures [28]. This suggests that O addition impedes the movement of screw dislo-
cations rather than edge dislocations. A screw dislocation has no dilatation except non-
linear elastic field in its core, which extends in a nonplanar manner on certain low-
index in bcc metals [15]. According to the calculation by Chang [16], interstitial atoms
interact with a screw dislocation in a wider field than substitutional atoms. By consider-
ing this interaction of interstitials with the core of screw dislocations and the interaction
of Ta atoms with O atoms, our results suggest that the i–s interaction influences the
redistribution of O atoms around screw dislocations under applied stress. A more
detailed description is given in Ref. [29]. Consequently, screw dislocation motion in
Nb–Ta alloys will be impeded by Ta–O interaction, even if the strain field in the ma-
trix derived from Ta addition is negligible, because of the very small size misfit, and

It should be noted that the i–s interaction in Nb–Mo is still strong even at high tem-

Among these factors, (i) and (iii) have been claimed to be essential to cause the i–s
interaction [25, 26, 34]. The oxidation enthalpy of Mo is higher than that of Nb, that is,
bonding with O of Nb is easier than that of Mo. Thus, factor (iii) is excluded as a cause

induce an attractive force between Mo and O atoms to defuse the strain field generated
by Mo atoms in the Nb matrix.

lower than that in Nb at temperatures between 1123 and 1403 K. Such a strong interac-

temperatures observed in this work. Therefore, the i–s (O–Mo) interaction in Nb is a
critical issue to understand high temperature strength of Nb alloys.
5.Summary
Strengthening due to O in Nb–Ta and Nb–Mo single crystals was studied. The results
obtained in this experiment are summarized as follows:
1. In low-O-content Nb alloys, Mo addition strengthens Nb, but Ta addition does not.
These effects are interpreted in terms of the size misfit.
2. Nb and Nb–Ta alloys are strengthened by O at temperatures up to about 1100 K,
while Nb–20Mo alloys are strengthened by O at temperatures up to 1473 K.
3. c.r.s.s. (t
0) increases with increasing O content in both Nb–Ta and Nb–20Mo
alloys, and strengthening by O is obvious in Nb–Ta and Nb–20Mo alloys rather than in
Nb.
4. Strengthening of Nb–Mo and Nb–Ta alloys is attributed to the i–s interaction
between O and Mo or Ta.
