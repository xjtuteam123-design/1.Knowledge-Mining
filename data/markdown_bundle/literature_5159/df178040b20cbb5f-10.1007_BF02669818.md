Effect of Melt Spinning on the Microstructure and 
Mechanical Properties of Three Ni-Base Superalloys 
RANDY BOWMAN and STEPHEN D. ANTOLOVICH 
A microstructural and mechanical analysis was performed on three rapidly solidified nickel base 
superalloys. Transmission and scanning electron microscopy along with tensile tests were performed 
on the ribbons in the as-cast and aged condition. This investigation permitted a correlation to be made 
between cooling rate, microstructure, and mechanical properties. It was found that melt spinning 
significantly altered the physical characteristics of the alloys studied. The rapid cooling rates 
(--107 K/s) produced ribbons with a low dislocation density and small (-1 /zm) low angle cells. The 
precipitation of y' was suppressed, producing alloys with a smaller volume fraction of precipitates and 
lower APBE than in the conventionally cast condition. Also, the matrix/precipitate lattice mismatch 
was higher in the melt spun foils. Tensile strengths were similar to those in the conventional form; 
however, no measurable ductility was present. Aging the ribbons resulted in increases in y' volume 
fraction, tensile strengths, APBE, and elongations compared to the as-cast ribbons. The results of this 
work suggest that many of the microstructural refinements produced by melt spinning are lost after a 
short aging time at moderate temperatures. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
SINCE the 1930's when the gas turbine was invented its 
efficiency has been improved by increasing the gas entry 
temperature. This increase in temperature has been made 
possible by a combination of engineering and material de- 
velopments. The conditions present in modern jet engines 
require components to withstand high stresses at tem- 
peratures above 1366 K (2000 ~ in some cases. To per- 
form under these conditions nickel base superalloys having 
high strength, ductility, high thermal conductivity, low ther- 
mal expansion, and stability at high temperatures were de- 
veloped. This class of superalloys is composed of an fcc, y, 
(austenite) matrix, a coherent intermetallic precipitate, y', 
Ni3(AI, Ti), and various types of carbides and borides. The 
nickel matrix (y) is solid solutioned strengthened by addi- 
tions of Co, Cr, Mo, and W. y' has an ordered L12 structure 
and is stable at elevated temperatures. 
The major factor contributing to the strength in precip- 
itation hardened Ni base superalloys is the presence of the 
y' precipitate. Factors which affect the strength of these 
alloys include volume fraction of y" y' size, and solid 
solution strengthening of y and y'. When the radius of the 
y' is below a critical size, particle cutting occurs. In these 
instances, hardening is controlled by such mechanisms as 
(1) coherency strains, (2) differences in elastic moduli be- 
tween particle and matrix, (3)existence of order in the 
particles, (4)energy to create additional particle-matrix 
interface, (5) difference in stacking fault energy of particle 
and matrix, and (6) increases in lattice resistance of particles 
with temperature. 
y' is an ideal precipitate for high temperature applications 
since its strength increases with temperature and has a high 
ductility. 1 Its ductility is an important feature because if the

Development of nickel base alloys has given engine de- 
signers the ability to reduce material needs and at the same 
time increase the operating temperature. Manipulating the 
composition correctly allows superalloys to be tailored to 
meet specific needs. However, since the mid 1950's ad- 
vances in process development have played an increasingly 
important role in alloy development. 3 An example of these 
new techniques is rapid solidification processing (RSP). 
Benefits of RSP include small grain size, reduced chemical 
segregation, elimination of massive phases, and increases in 
the terminal solid solubility of alloying elements. 4 
There are many rapid solidification techniques currently 
available. They are categorized as atomization and non- 
atomization methods. Atomization techniques include gas 
and water atomization, rapid spinning cup, drum splat pro- 
cess, and rotating electrode process. Nonatomization pro- 
cesses include melt extrusion, chill block melt spinning, 
planar flow casting, and melt extraction. 5 
In melt spinning, a rapidly rotating wheel serves as 
the substrate where solidification occurs. Molten metal is 
forced under pressure onto the wheel and forms thin metal 
ribbons typically on the order of 4 mm wide by 0.25 mm in 
thickness. 
The present study is concerned with the physical proper- 
ties of three nickel base superalloys and how they are af- 
fected by melt spinning. The most important consequence 
of rapid solidification is the increased cooling rate over 
conventional casting techniques. The cooling rate is con- 
trolled primarily by the wheel speed. 6 It is well known that 
many microstructural features are exponentially related to 
the cooling rate. These features include dendrite arm spac- 
ing, grain and cell size, second phase size, and inclusion 
size. Any effect on mechanical properties resulting from 
different cooling rates is obviously due to some combina- 
tion of the microstructural refinements. It was therefore 
decided that melt spinning could best be evaluated by cast-

II. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE 
A. Melt Spinning 
The melt spinning apparatus used for the production of all 
the rapidly solidified ribbons is located at the NASA Lewis 
Research Center in Cleveland, Ohio. A general description 
of this particular unit has been given in a recent article. 7 The 
specific processing conditions used in this project will now 
be described. 
For all runs the crucible material was A1203 with a 
12.5 mm inside diameter containing a 1 mm diameter round 
orifice from which the metal was ejected. The crucible was 
20 mm above the wheel. The material to be melt spun was 
cut from bar stock to produce a 6  11  3 mm sample 
weighing approximately 20 grams. The wheel used in this 
project was 17.8 cm in diameter and made from 4340 steel. 
Each alloy was cast at 10, 30, and 45 m/s. Molten liquid 
was ejected from the crucible by He gas pressurized to 
0.1 MPa. A chamber pressure of approximately 3  
10 -s tort was obtained before heating the sample to prevent 
the formation of any oxide scales. Heating was accom- 
plished with a radio frequency induction unit. The wheel 
was conditioned prior to the run by polishing with 600 grit 
paper and then wiped with alcohol. 
B. Electron Microscopy 
The melt spun ribbons had thicknesses of less than 
0.25 mm in the as-cast condition. This meant that no 
mechanical grinding was required before electrolytically 
thinning the specimens for subsequent examination by 
transmission electron microscopy (TEM). However, a short 
time was spent on 600 grit silicon carbide paper to remove 
any surface irregularities. 
From the polished ribbons, 3 mm diameter disks were 
punched and cleaned in acetone prior to chemical thinning. 
The electropolishing was done with a twin jet unit. TEM 
examination was done using a JEOL-100c STEM operated 
at 100 KV. All SEM work was also performed on this in- 
strument in the scanning mode at an accelerating voltage 
of 40 KV. 
C. Quantitative Metallography 
Using TEM micrographs to determine volume fractions is 
complicated since the photo is a two-dimensional represen- 
tation of a three-dimensional foil. Thus in TEM micrographs 
the images are projected images and the problems of overlap 
and truncation must be considered. 
If particles are truncated at the foil surfaces, their pro- 
jected size will appear smaller than their true size. Also, if 
they are preferentially etched out, the volume fraction will 
be lower than normal. Conversely, if the particles remain in 
the foil even when their centers lie above the foil surface, the 
measured volume fraction will be larger than the true value. 
According to Underwood 8 for the case of particle trun- 
cation, the volume fraction is given by: 
Vv = AA[2D/(2D + 3t)] [1]

micrograph 
t = foil thickness 
D = particle diameter 
The shortcoming of this equation is that it assumes a 
uniform diameter and randomly oriented particles. All foils 
used for volume fraction determination were taken from the 
mid-thickness region of the ribbon. 
D. Phase Extraction 
Using phase extraction techniques, the y' phase can be 
separated from the y matrix and other phases present in the 
material. Since the density of 3,' is only 5 pct less than the 
matrix, the difference between weight fraction (as mea- 
sured by extraction) and volume percent (measured by quan- 
titative stereology) is -1 pct for a 20 wt pct alloy system. 
The two most commonly used electrolytes for y' extraction 
are (1) 20 pct phosphoric acid in water and (2) 1 pct ammo- 
nium sulfate and 1 pct citric or tartaric acid in water. It has 
been shown 9 that the ammonium sulfate and citric or tartaric 
acid in distilled water tends to give more reliable results. 
However, if carbides are present in the sample, their weight 
must be subtracted from the gross weight of the extracted 
residue to obtain the net 3,' weight fraction. 
The apparatus consisted of a d-c power source in which 
the cathode was a 40 cm z platinum mesh. The melt spun 
ribbons were spot welded to a platinum wire and served as 
the anode. The ammonium sulfate and citric acid were 
mixed to the correct proportions in a 250 ml beaker with 
distilled water. A detailed description of the phase extraction 
technique is available in the literature. 9"~~

to calculate the constrained and unconstrained mismatch 
from the in situ and extracted y' lattice parameters. Mis-

G. Mechanical Properties 
Tensile tests were performed on both the as-cast and aged 
specimens. The specimens used were chosen such that they 
would have a 5 cm gage length plus at least 2.5 cm in the 
grips. The grips were smooth-faced hydraulically driven 
steel plates 2 cm wide by 6 cm in length. The ideal ribbon 
was one of constant width and thickness along its length and 
with no imperfections such as cracks or kinks. The crosshead 
speed was 8  10 -6 m/s and load vs time was recorded on a 
strip chart at a speed of 25.4 mm/min. 
The largest source of error stemmed from the fact that 
alignment of the foils was difficult. If the ribbon was not 
perfectly aligned along the tensile axis, the load was not 
evenly distributed across the entire specimen cross-section 
and apparent low strengths were obtained. The alignment 
procedure consisted of a card with a series of reference 
marks on which the ribbon was attached parallel to these 
marks. These lines were also used to align the card with the 
grips. Since many tests were run for each specimen condi- 
tion, the samples having strengths which deviated signifi- 
cantly from the average were discarded. Also, it was easy to 
identify when tearing was occurring by observing the ribbon 
during the test. All such test results were not used in com- 
paring the mechanical properties of the alloys. 
H. Heat Treatment 
Aging was performed in an argon atmosphere at 1 atm 
above the room pressure. The aging apparatus consisted of 
a tube furnace sealed at both ends. A 5 mm flexible tube was 
inserted in the top and argon gas was introduced from a 
pressurized tank. The gas was directed out the bottom and 
bubbled through a water bath to prevent oxygen from re- 
entering. The specimens were suspended in the center of the 
fumace in a quartz crucible with a chromel-alumel thermo- 
couple which was attached to a potentiometer. 
All specimens were aged at 1033 K (760 ~ for 1,4, and 
8 hours. Although oxidation was not completely eliminated 
even at the longest aging times, the oxide layer did not 
extend through the entire thickness. Mechanically grinding 
the foils on 600 grit paper and polishing using 1 /zm alum- 
ina gave an oxide-free ribbon which could be prepared for 
TEM examination or mechanically tested. 
III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
A. As-Cast Properties 
1. Microstructure 
In melt spinning, removal of heat from the molten metal 
is accomplished by the rotating wheel, resulting in ribbons 
that are directionally solidified. The thermal gradient 
present in the melt is higher near the region in contact with 
the wheel and decreases with increasing distance away from 
the wheel. Under such conditions the microstructural size of 
the solidified alloys will depend on the distance from the 
substrate. Conventional TEM foil preparation techniques, 
i.e., polishing the metal ribbons from the wheel and free 
sides simultaneously, reveals the cell structure at approxi- 
mately mid-thickness. Typical mid-thickness structures of 
each alloy cast at 30 m/s are shown in Figure 1. 
The cell morphology is nearly equiaxed in all cases since 
the plane of the foil is perpendicular to the growth direction. 
For each alloy, the cell size was seen to be a function of 
wheel speed (Figure 2). Measurements were taken at mid- 
thickness for each condition since as previously shown, 6 cell 
size depends on position in the foil thickness as well as on 
wheel speed. Cell size decreased with increasing wheel 
speed since the cooling rate is higher for the faster wheel 
speeds. As the dendrites grow away from the chill zone their 
thickness increases as the thermal gradient is reduced. To

2. Precipitate characteristics 
One of the benefits of rapid solidification is to increase 
the thermal solid solubility of alloy elements. It is hoped that 
via rapid quenching an increased amount of alloying ele- 
ments can remain in solution and the precipitation of un- 
desirable phases eliminated. Each alloy system studied in 
this project is strengthened by an ordered precipitate, 7', the 
formation of which is a combination of a nucleation and 
growth mechanism from a supersaturated matrix. The effect 
of rapid solidification on this process was evaluated by 
determining the volume fraction of 7' present in each alloy 
system for each wheel speed. The values shown in Table I 
represent an average value for each condition as determined 
by phase extraction and quantitative stereology. The scatter 
in the results from the different techniques indicates a 5 to 
8 pct error in the values shown. The values obtained by 
stereology tended to be slightly lower than the phase extrac- 
tion method. 
The most obvious result seen from the data is the effect 
of reducing the amount of as-cast 7' with increasing wheel 
speed. Even at the slowest wheel speed, the amount of 
precipitated ~/' is less than that of the conventionally cast 
material. The most striking reduction is for the PWA 1480 
cast at 45 m/s. This large suppression of ~/' is probably due 
to the absence of Mo which results in a greater solubility of 
A1 in the matrix. Thus it is easier to keep A1 in solution for 
the PWA 1480 than for the WASPALOY or MAR-M 246. 
As mentioned earlier, the difference in cell size for MAR- 
M 246 as opposed to the other alloys was most evident in the 
ribbons cast at 10 m/s. The volume fraction determinations 
show that at this wheel speed MAR-M 246 has the largest 
amount of ~/'. Since 7' is affected by cooling rates and a 
thermal gradient does exist across the thickness of the rib-

3. Mechanical properties

solidified materials are expected to be due to a reduction in 
the cell size and a change in the precipitate structure and 
morphology. There is some question as to the validity of the 
results obtained from thin ribbons due to their constrained 
geometry. Inoue et al. 16 examined this question and studied 
the effect of sample size on the strength and elongation as 
a function of wire diameter for rapidly quenched Ni-A1-X 
systems. They found the values of yield strength, fracture 
strength, and elongation to be independent of wire size for 
wires with diameters between 85 and 180/zm.

2. Mechanical properties 
Aging of the melt spun ribbons results in significant 
changes in the microstructure and physical metallurgy. The 
effect of these changes on the mechanical properties was 
evaluated by tensile testing the aged foils under the same 
testing conditions as were used on the cast ribbons. Table II 
summarizes the results of the aged tests. Each value pre- 
sented represents an average of 15 to 20 tests.

scatter in the data. The aged specimens have lower mis-

fraction of 3/'. The 3/distribution and larger volume frac-

IV. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS 
The primary goal in this study was to investigate how 
rapid solidification affects the physical metallurgy and me- 
chanical properties of three nickel base alloys. Also of in- 
terest was the stability of the microstructures produced. 
To accomplish this, PWA 1480, WASPALOY, and MAR- 
M 246 were melt spun at three wheel speeds (different cool- 
ing rates). Thus the effect of various cooling rates could be 
examined for a range of alloy compositions. 
A. As-Cast 
The initial microstructures for each alloy system are very 
similar. They consisted of small (- 1 /zm) cells with a low 
dislocation density. The exact size of the cells depended 
on the local cooling rate: higher cooling rates produced 
smaller cells. 
In each alloy, melt spinning reduced the volume fraction 
of 3" as compared to the same alloys produced by con- 
ventional techniques. Also, the matrix/precipitate lattice 
mismatch was higher whereas the APBE was lower in the 
melt spun form. 
UTS values for the melt spun ribbons are comparable to 
the normally processed alloys; however, no measurable ductil- 
ity was detected. The 3" size was small enough to produce 
precipitate shearing during deformation. TEM examination 
of the deformed foils revealed that the dislocation activity 
was restricted to slip bands and little activity was observed 
in the cell matrix. 
B. Aging Response 
No cell growth occurred in any of the melt spun foils after 
aging for four hours at 1033 K. In all specimens, a uniform 
3" distribution appears after a short exposure time. The 
morphology of the precipitates ranged from spheres to cubes 
depending on the lattice mismatch. The volume fraction of 
3" increased with aging time and after four hours at 1033 K 
approached that of the conventionally prepared specimens. 
As aging time was increased, the elevated mismatch ob- 
tained from melt spinning dropped to near normal levels and 
the APBE increased. 
The UTS values increased slightly in the aged condition, 
but more importantly, the aged ribbons exhibited some 
ductility prior to fracture. This effect is attributed to the 
increased dislocation activity in the cell matrix. 
The results suggest that many, perhaps metastable, en- 
hancements in the physical metallurgy of alloys are possible 
through rapid solidification. Examples include smaller grain 
and 3" sizes, increased lattice mismatch, decreased APBE, 
slightly increased tensile strength, and decreased ductility. 
The aging results are by no means conclusive but do suggest 
that the alterations achieved by rapid cooling are unstable. 
Many of the property changes are reduced or lost entirely 
after a short time at elevated temperatures. This could seri- 
ously affect a rapidly solidified material's usefulness in high 
temperature applications.
