INTRODUCTION
In materials operating under conditions of nonstationarythermomechanical loading of elements of equipment
and systems of responsible engineering facilities with a life of several decades (nuclear power plants, petrochemical
equipment, tanksforstorageofgaseousandliqueﬁedchemicalproducts, aviationgasturbineengines,new-generation
gasturbine plants, etc.) microdamageis accumulated, resultingin deteriorationofthe initial strengthcharacteristics
and crack initiation and development. The most dangerous zones determining the life of elements are generally not
accessible for nondestructive testing. To ensure safe operation of responsible engineering facilities and increase their
life, it is necessary to monitor damage accumulation in the most dangerous zones of structural elements (to assess
remaining life) [1].
The life of structural elements operating at elevated temperaturesT> 0.5Tmelt (Tmelt is the melting tem-
perature) and under cyclic mechanical loading is determined manly by low-cycle fatigue and damage accumulation
during creep (creep-rupture strength), resulting in one of the most dangerous types of fracture—brittle fracture of
structures made of plastic materials.
Existing standard methods for estimating the life of structural elements do not take into account the actual
processes occurring in structural materials. Calculation based on the theory of elasticity used in such estimation

This necessitates the development of new methods for estimating the life of structures based on the equations
of thermoviscoplasticity, the equations of damage evolution, and damage criteria based on the results full-scale and
numerical experiments with laboratory samples and numerical analysis of the deformation and fracture of structural
elements during their operation [1, 2].
The dependence of the durability of a material on the duration (frequency) of the cycle and on the presence
of exposure to stress in the cycle at elevated constant orvarying temperatures is due to the development of creep
deformations and the resulting damage, which, unlike fatigue damage occurring in grains, accumulate at grain
boundaries.
The most common method for studying creep-rupture strength is constructing an experimental curve of
tensile stress versus time to failure under uniaxial tension of samples at constant temperature [2–6]. The creep
curve has three characteristic regions. The presence of the third region of unsteady creep in this curve is due to
the inﬂuence of cumulative damage on the deformation characteristics of the material, in particular, on the creep
rate. The change in creep rate in this region of the creep curve is determined by the rate of damage accumulation
in the material. The beginning of the third region corresponds to the moment at which cumulative damage begins
to inﬂuence the deformation characteristics of the material [2, 6–8].
Since under actual conditions of operation of structural materials, unsteady creep begins after a few tens
of hours of operation, whereas the time to fracture ranges from several hundred to several thousand hours, there
is a need to conduct long-term tests. To solve this problem, it is necessary to establish a correlation between the
results of short-term creep tests at elevated temperatures and the creep and creep-rupture strength characteristics
at moderate temperatures. There are several such correlations [9–11].
Most studies of the creep and creep rupture strength of structural materials involved uniaxial tension tests
of cylindrical samples at constant stresses and temperatures. However, in practice, structural elements are in more
complex conditions, e.g., under the inﬂuence of alternating stresses and cyclically varying temperatures. Since
the creep and creep-rupture strength of metals are signiﬁcantly dependent on the stress state and temperature, it
is required to study nonisothermal creep and creep-rupture strength for various types of stress state and develop
methods for estimating the creep and creep-rupture strength of materials ofstructural elements in a multiaxial stress
state basedonexperimentaldata obtainedin tests for a uniaxialstressstate. The results ofnumerous experiments in
which various alloyswere tested for creep-rupture strength at various temperatures under pure tension, pure torsion,
and joint torsion and tension led to the conclusion that among all possible equivalent stresses under conditions of a
homogeneous stress state, the maximum principal tensile stress or the stress intensity (the second invariant of the
stress deviator) or a combination of them are the most appropriate for determining the creep-rupture strength and
comparing creep curves for diﬀerent types of stress state. By the intensity ofthe creep process is meant the energy
dissipation power [12]. In order to select a speciﬁc equivalent stress for a given structural material, it is necessary to
conduct additional tests for creep and creep-rupture strength in torsion or tests of a tubular specimen under joint
torsion and tension.
Durability curves are usually constructed for permanent stresses (loads), whereas in practice, stress and
temperature vary according to quite complex laws. This leads to the need to sum up the damage in the case of a
complex loading history. In estimating the time to fracture under variable stress and temperature conditions, the
main problem is to perform calculations based on the test results at constant stresses and temperatures. This is
commonly done using the rule of linear summation of damage (Palmgren–Miner hypothesis), in which it is assumed
that defects accumulated at diﬀerent loading stages do not depend on each other. This rule is used in engineering
calculations of the operation of structures at variable temperatures and loads. Analysis of available experimental
data reveals a signiﬁcant eﬀect of stress and temperature on creep-rupture strength and suggests that the rule of
linear summation of damage is inapplicable under conditions of varying stresses and temperatures.
In the present work, using the mechanics of damaged media (MDM), we developed a mathematical model
that describes damage accumulation in structural materials (metals and their alloys) during creep. Numerical
studies of the creep-rupture strength of 12Kh18N9 steel and VZh-159 high-temperature alloy were performed, and
the results were compared with the data of full-scale experiments.

1.2. Evolution Equations of Damage Accumulation
In the micromechanics of damage accumulation due to viscoplastic deformation, the following stages of the
fracture process are considered: the nucleation and development of microdefects until the formation of macrocracks;
the steady (gradual)developmentof the cracksformedand the unsteady development ofmacrocracks(disintegration
of the object into parts).

In turn, the damage accumulation process includes the following two phases:
—The nucleation phase, which corresponds to the change in the structure of the material due to viscoplastic
deformation, resulting in hardening and softening, and which involves the nucleation of damage distributed in the
form of micropores and microcracks in the volume of the material (in this phase, the eﬀect of cumulative damage
on the physico-mechanical characteristics of the material is insigniﬁcant);
—Phase of development (distribution) and interaction of the developing micropores and microcracks, which involves
weakening and a change in the physico-mechanical characteristics of the material (“degradation of the continuum”),
resulting in the formation of a macrocrack.
It is reasonable to describe the physical process of fracture in each of these two phases using its internal
state variable. However, in order to formulate the evolution equations of damage accumulation in the nucleation
phase, it is necessary to measure the parameters of this process at the micro- and mesolevels (the number of pores,
their distribution and size, etc), since the macroscopic parameters in this phase are practically independent of the
indicated processes. Apparently, this phase can be described using simpliﬁed approaches with a minimum number
of parameters to be determined. Therefore, the beginning of the nucleation phase will be determined using the
threshold criterion, and its duration using some mechanical parameter.

relationship between the damage parameter changesω and mechanical parameters that depend on the history of
the deformation process and whose critical values determine the moment of complete fracture an elementary volume
of the material. The energy approach is the most eﬀective. By analogy with the theory of low-cycle fatigue [2, 13],
