Introduction 
Recently, environmental embrittlement has been investigated in many ordered int~metallics including Co3Ti 
(1-6), Ni3A1 (3,7-14), Ni3Si (3,15-18), FeAI (19-23), Fe3A1 (23,24), and (Fe,Co)3V (25-27). At ambient 
temperatures, the embrittlement is caused mainly by hydrogen released from moisture in air. A number of 
intermetallics also show a reduction in ductility at elevated temperatures; however, the cause of the ductility 
reduction is not well understood at the present time. Oxygen is identified as an embrittling agent in Ni3A1 (9,11) 
and Ni3Si (18) alloys but apparently not in other alloys such as Ni3[Si,Ti] (16) and Co3Ti (6). 
L12-ordered intermetallic alloys with compositions (Fe22Co78)3V exhibited moisture-induced hydrogen 
embrittlement when tested in air at ambient temperatures (27). Their ductility also decreases with increasing 
temperature above 500°C and reaches a minimum around the critical ordering temperature [Tc = 910°C (28)] when 
tested in vacuum (25). At present, there is no detailed study of the environmental effect in (Fe,Co)3V at elevated 
temperatures. In this study, the environmental effect on the ductility and fracture in (Fe,Co)3V is investigated at 
various temperatures and in various atmospheres, and the results are compared with those from other intermetaUic 
alloys. 
Exoefimental Procedure 
An (Fe22Co78)3V alloy was prepared by the same procedure as reported previously (25,27). Tensile 
specimens with a gage section of 12.7 x 3.2 x 0.6 mm were punched from the alloy sheet and electropolished to 
remove surface oxides. The specimens were recrystallized at II00"C for 20 min to obtain the same grain size used 
in the previous study (25), followed by an ordering treatment at 800"C for 5 h and at 700"C for 17 h in a vacuum of 
about 10 -'~ Pa. In order to examine the grain size, polished samples were etched for 1 min in a solution of 
40% H20, 40% HNO3, and 20% HF. The grain diameter refers to the average spacing between grain boundaries 
as seen on metallurgical sections and measured using the method of linear intercept (25). 
Tensile tests were performed using an Instron testing machine at a strain rate of 3.3 by 10 -3 s -1 in air or 
vacuum of ~ 5 by 10 -4 Pa at room temperature to 1000°C and also at a strain rate of 1.7 by 10 -1 s -3 in vacuum at 
700"C. Tensile tests were also conducted in distilled water at room temperature and in Ar + 8% H2 or high-purity 
Ar (99.9998% Ar, 0.92 ppm 02, and 0.04 ppm H20) at 700"C. The specimens tested at elevated temperatures 
were heated by an induction heater to achieve a heating rate of more than 50"C/rnin to minimize air oxidation. The 
fracture surfaces were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) operated at 15 kV.

Discussion 
As shown in Table 1, the ductility of (Fe,Co)3V at room temperature is much lower in distilled water than in 
air or in vacuum. This is consistent with the results reported previously (27). Similar environmental embrittlement 
at ambient temperatures has been reported in a number of ordered intermetallics including Co3Ti (2,4,6); 
Ni3[A1,Mn] (8); Ni3Si (16,17), FeAI (19-23), and Fe3AI (23,24). This embrittlement involves moisture-induced 
hydrogen generated from the following chemical reaction at crack tips:

repoRed that the room-temperature ductility of a similar (Fe,Co)3V alloy tested in air is 20.0%, much lower than the 
34.1% attained in the present study. The reason for this difference is not known but is possibly due to a small 
difference in the composition or impurity level in the alloy heats used. 
Figure 2(b) shows a high ductility and no apparent environmental embrittlement at 300 to 500"C. Similar 
results are also obtained in other intermetallics [Co3Ti (6), Ni3[Si,Ti] (16) and FeA1 (21)]. This phenomenon can 
be rationalized by considering both hydrogen segregation and oxide formation in materials at elevated temperatures. 
As the test temperature increases above room temperature, less hydrogen is enriched at crack tips owing to an 
entropy effect (6,16). FeAI showed the lowest ductility in air around ambient temperatures and exhibited an 
increase in ductility with increasing temperature above 100"C (21). Liu et al. attribute the increase in ductility to a 
rapid in situ formation of protective oxide scales on specimen surfaces, which reduce the hydrogen penetration. 
At temperatures from 500"C to Tc, the tensile ductility in both air and vacuum decreases with increasing 
temperature, and the ductility in air is always lower than that in vacuum. The decrease in ductility leads to a change 
in fracture mode from ductile grain-boundary fracture in vacuum to brittle grain-boundary fracture in air at 800"C. 
This is generally consistent with the observation of lower ductility and more brittle grain-boundary fracture in 
boron-doped Ni3AI (9) and Ni3[Si,Ti] (16) aUoys tested in air instead of vacuum. Liu (34) showed the tensile 
elongation of a boron-doped Ni3A1 alloy continuously increases with decreasing air pressure up to 10 .5 Pa at 
760"C. The elevated-temperature embrittlement in these alloys is reduced by alloying with chromium, which 
promotes a rapid formation of protective oxide scales on specimens at 500 to 800"C. These results indicate that 
oxygen in air causes the embrittlement in boron-doped Ni3AI and Ni3Si (35) alloys as well as in (Fe, Co)3V. 
Takasugi and Izumi, on the other hand, reported that the ductility of Co3Ti (6) and Ni3[Si,Ti] (16) was 
insensitive to test environment (i.e., vacuum versus air) at elevated temperatures. They argued that the possibly 
high-oxygen solubility in Co3Ti might reduce oxygen segregation to grain boundaries, thereby reducing oxygen 
embrittlement. They also suggest that the activation of {001 } slip in Co3Ti at elevated temperatures (36,37,38) 
increases stress concentration, promotes grain-boundary fracture, and hence lowers the ductility (5). 
Tensile tests were also conducted in Ar + 8% H2 or high-purity Ar at 700"C. In these cases, Ar and Ar + 8% 
H2 gases were introduced into the vacuum system (- 5 x 10 -'~ Pa) prior to the tensile testing. Argon was added to 
reduce the mean-free path of gas molecules and hopefully to slow down the kinetics of oxygen absorption at crack 
tips, and Ar + 8% H2 was added to reduce the partial pressure of oxygen in the test environment. As shown in 
Table 1, the 700"C ductilities tested in these environments are comparable to that in air, suggesting that the oxygen 
diffusion from surface to interior, rather than chemisorption of oxygen from the environment, is the rate-controlling 
step in determining oxygen uptake. This observation is not too surprising because there is apparently enough 
residual oxygens in argon (e.g., 0.92 ppm oxygen or 10 -2 Pa in high-purity Ar) to cause embdttlement in 
(Fe,Co)3V. 
It is also noteworthy to mention that hydrogen in Ar+8% H2 causes no additional embrittlement of (Fe,Co)3V 
at 700"C, as compared with the air test. Similar results have been reported for iron (39) tested in hydrogen 
environments. Chemisorption of hydrogen on surfaces may decrease with increasing temperature because of the 
surface-poisoning effect due to formation of oxide scales at elevated temperatures (21) and less segregation of 
hydrogen at crack tips due to the entropy effect suggested by Takasugi et al. (6,16). Further studies are required to 
characterize the combined effects of hydrogen and water vapor/oxygen at elevated temperatures. 
It is important to point out that the ductility of (Fe22C.o78)3V at temperatures above 500"C decreases substantially 
with temperature even when tested in vacuum (Fig. 2). This suggests the possibility of other factors, in addition to 
oxygen embrittlement, which cause the reduction in ductility of (Fe,Co)3V at elevated temperatures. The possible 
causes include the high-yield strength due to the yielding anomaly (increase of yield strength with temperature) and 
the activation of {001} slip, both of which are expected to promote high stress concentration and crack propagation 
along grain boundaries (5). At temperatures above T c, the long-range ordered structure is destroyed, and yield 
strength decreases sharply due to the lack of the strengthening effect by cross-slip-pinning. Consequently, the low 
stress level will not cause crack propagation in a brittle manner, and the alloy will become much more

ductile and less sensitive to the test environment. It is also possible that residual oxygen in conventional vacuum 
systems or oxygen within the material plays a role in embrittlement of (Fe22Co78)3V at elevated temperatures. 
Fine nodules were observed on fracture surfaces of (Fe,Co)3V tested at temperatures above To. These are 
presumably individual grains produced by a dynamic recrystallization due to localized strain concentration at original 
boundaries. The dynamic recrystallization has been observed in other intermetaUic alloys such as Co3Ti (1) and 
Ni3(AI,Mn) (7). Recently, Cahn et al. (28) reported that the formation of long-range order in (Fe,Co)3 V 
dramatically reduces the kinetics of grain growth and increases the time required for 50% recrystallization by a factor 
as high as 300 in the ordered state as compared with the time in the disordered state. 
Conclusions 
(1) The alloy (Fe22Co78)3 V showed environmental embrittlement at room temperature. 
(2) The tensile ductility of the alloy decreases sharply at temperatures above 500"C and reaches a minimum around 
T e (--910"C) when tested in air and vacuum. 
(3) The ductility in air is lower than that in vacuum in the temperature range of 500"C and T e. The reduction in 
ductility is caused by oxygen which penetrates and embrittles grain boundaries in the alloy. 
(4) The alloy exhibited a sharp increase in ductility and became insensitive to test environments at temperatures 
above T c because of the lack of long range order.
