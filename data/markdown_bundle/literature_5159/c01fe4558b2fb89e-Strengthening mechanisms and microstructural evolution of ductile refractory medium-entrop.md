A metastable β-type medium-entropy refractory alloy, Hf20Nb10Ti35Zr35, was designed to have a duc- 
tile BCC structure in the as-solution-treated state. The alloy was aged to achieve the combinations 
of strength and ductility. The strengthening mechanisms included transformation-induced plasticity, 
twinning-induced plasticity and precipitation strengthening. These multiple strengthening mechanisms 
can serve as a basis for alloy design and the improvement of the mechanical properties of alloys.

In 2004, Yeh et al. proposed the concept of high-entropy al- 
loys (HEAs) [1] . Since then, many HEAs have been designed and 
studied, such as refractory HEAs, eutectic HEAs, high-entropy su- 
peralloys, and high-entropy bulk metallic glasses [2] . In 2010, 
Senkov and Miracle ﬁrst reported the creation of refractory HEAs 
MoNbTaW and MoNbTaVW [ 3 , 4 ]. These two alloys possess body- 
centered cubic (BCC) structures and excellent high-temperature 
yield strength of approximately 400 MPa at 160 0 °C. However, they 
are extremely dense ( ρ = 13.76 and 12.36 g/cm 3 , respectively), 
and their room-temperature compressive ductility is poor ( < 1%). 
In 201 1, the same authors established the equiatomic composition 
of HfNbTaTiZr, a material with a low density ( ρ = 9.94 g/cm 3 ), 
high compression yield strength (929 MPa), and high ductility ( ε
> 50%) [5] . Since then, many papers on refractory HEAs have been 
published. Although high-entropy tends to thermodynamically en- 
hance solid solution phases, its effect is not limited to one phase. 
For example, the multielement solution-type B2, Laves, σ , and L12 
phases may be formed in alloys, especially at low temperatures. 
Based on this understanding, a two-phase refractory alloy with 
high strength and ductility was prepared using metastability engi- 
neering. Ta x HfZrTi ( x = 1, 0.6, 0.5, and 0.4) medium-entropy alloys

ticity (TRIP) during the transformation from a BCC to a hexago- 
nal close-packed (HCP) structure [6] . Therefore, increasing the con- 
ﬁgurational entropy may help to design refractory alloys—HEAs or 
MEAs—that exhibit TRIP and therefore possess a desirable combi- 
nation of strength and ductility. 
In this study, TRIP, twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP), and ag- 
ing treatment were utilized to improve the strength and ductil- 
ity of refractory alloys. Hf, Ti, Zr were selected for preparing an 
equiatomic HfTiZr refractory MEA because these elements belong 
to the same group in the periodic table and possess similar chem- 
ical and physical properties. Moreover, for alloying, Nb ( ρ = 8.57 
g/cm 3 ) was used instead of the expensive Ta ( ρ = 16.69 g/cm 3 ) 
to stabilize the BCC phase, and the Hf ( ρ = 13.07 g/cm 3 ) content 
is reduced to lower the weight and cost of the ﬁnal alloy. In this 
manner, we designed the refractory MEA Hf 20 Nb 10 Ti 35 Zr 35 (named 
Nb10). Nb10 has a high strain-hardening rate in the as-solution- 
treated state because of the composite strengthening mechanisms 
of TRIP and TWIP . After aging treatment, the alloy undergoes a spe- 
cial precipitation sequence involving the orthorhombic ( αʺ), ω, and 
α phases, which are found in a few narrow compositions of Ti al- 
loys such as the near- β-type Ti alloys of Ti-9Mo (in at.%) and Ti- 
6Cr-5Mo-5V-4Al (in wt.%) [ 7 , 8 ]. This is closely related to the varia-

Nb10 was prepared in a water-cooled copper crucible by vac- 
uum arc melting, and its ingots were remelted four or more times 
to achieve chemical homogeneity. The purity of each alloying el- 
ement was > 99.99%. The rectangular small cast ingots were cold- 
rolled until their thickness was reduced by 65%. Thereafter, spec- 
imens cut from the strip were subjected to rapid heat treatment 
(RHT) at 900 °C for 1 min in a furnace and subsequently to wa- 
ter quenching to obtain recrystallized ﬁne grains. The specimens 
were then sealed in a vacuum quartz tube to prevent oxidation. 
The specimens were then aged at 400 °C for 12 h, 450 °C for 1, 12, 
18, 24 and 36 h, and 500 °C for 15 min and 1, 3, 6, and 12 h. 
For microstructural investigation, the specimens were ground, 
polished, and etched with a solution composed of 3% HF + 10% 
H 2 NO 3 + H 2 O. The etched samples were placed in a scanning 
electron microscope (SEM) (model JSM-IT100, Jeol) to observe 
their microstructures and any precipitates. The various constituent 
phases were identiﬁed by using X-ray diffractometry (XRD) (model 
D2 PHASER, Bruker) and varying the scanning angle from 20 ° to 
10 0 ° at a scanning rate of 4 °/min. Electrolytic polishing was per- 
formed to avoid any deformation effects arising from grinding and 
polishing before electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) observa- 
tions. An immersed area of 15–25 mm 2 was electrochemically pol- 
ished at −20 to −10 °C with a voltage of −25 V for 15–20 s in 
a perchloric acid–methanol (1:9) solution. A Zeiss Supra 55 SEM 
equipped with an Oxford AZtechHKL for EBSD and operated at 20 
kV was used for crystallography. 
Hardness tests were conducted using a Vickers hardness tester 
(Mitutoyo 810-420 HV-1 14) with a loading of 5 kg. Tensile tests 
were conducted using an Instron 4468 machine with a strain rate 
of 10 −3 s −1 , and the Young’s moduli of the specimens were mea- 
sured by attaching them to a 10-mm extensometer. Dog-bone- 
shaped tensile test specimens with gauge length of 20 mm, thick- 
ness of 1.5 mm, and width of 3 mm were prepared through wire 
electrical discharge machining. 
The as-cast s Nb10 alloy exhibited a unique BCC structure in 
the XRD analyses, as depicted in Fig. 1 . The HCP + BCC mixed 
crystalline structure was formed after the specimens were cold- 
rolled to a 65% reduction in thickness. This indicated that part 
of the material in the BCC phase was transformed to the HCP 
structure through martensitic transformation during deformation. 
A previous study reported deformation-induced phase transforma- 
tion from the face-centered cubic (FCC) to the HCP structure at 
cryogenic temperatures in a FeCoCrNi HEA due to the gliding of 
Shockley partial dislocations on every other { 111 } FCC plane [9] ; 
the formation of the HCP phase improved the strength and work- 
hardening rate of the material by restricting dislocation slip and 
accommodating plastic deformation. Similarly, in the present study, 
the deformation-induced phase transformation from the BCC to 
HCP phase may have resulted in increased strain hardening and 
ductility. Fig. 1 illustrates only one BCC phase after 1 min of RHT 
at 900 °C. This indicated that the cold-rolling-induced HCP phase 
was completely reverted to the BCC phase. 
The tensile stress–strain curve of BCC Nb10 after RHT at 900 °C 
for 1 min is depicted in Fig. 2 (a), and the material properties are 
listed in Table. 1 . As-solution- treated Nb10 exhibited high strain 
hardening and ductility. Its hardness was 252 HV5, and its tensile 
properties were as follows: E = 70.5 GPa, yield strength (YS) = 524 
MPa, ultimate tensile strength (UTS) = 704 MPa, and elongation 
(EL) = 17.5%. Moreover, the strain-hardening rate versus true strain 
curve in Fig. 2 (b) can be divided into ﬁve stages based on slope 
changes. To identify the dominant mechanism in each stage, XRD 
pattern analyses were performed for the constituent phases. As 
shown in Fig. 1 , Nb10 subjected to RHT at 900 °C for 1 min was 
composed of only the BCC phase, whereas in the deformed state

After tensile deformation, a blue HCP structure was observed, as 
depicted in Fig. 3 (c) and the magniﬁed image in Fig. 3 (d). In ad-

circled grains was identiﬁed during the strain-induced transforma-

0.2 for HCP structures [10] . Thus, the low stress or strain induced

Based on the microstructural observations, we determined the 
possible deformation mechanisms in the ﬁve strain-hardening- 
rate stages in Fig. 2 (b). The ﬁrst stage was dislocation dominant. 
When dislocations gradually accumulated in the grains and piled 
up at the grain boundaries, continuous cross-slip leading to dy- 
namic recovery occurred in the regions with higher dislocation 
density, causing overall softening. In the second stage, the strain- 
hardening rate increased substantially because of the formation of 
the martensite HCP phase from the BCC matrix. In addition, a small 
amount of the αʺ orthorhombic martensite phase was formed. In 
the third stage, the curve exhibited a downward trend due to grad- 
ual saturation and thus a deceleration in the phase transformation 
from the BCC to the HCP structure. The fourth stage was a plateau- 
like period indicating the dominance of some hardening mecha- 
nism. This phenomenon occurred because the stress accumulated 
in the newly formed HCP phase reached the critical shear mag- 
nitude for triggering twin formation and result a certain amount 
of strain hardening. Finally, in the ﬁfth stage, the strain-hardening 
rate decreased until fracture because of the dynamic dislocation 
recovery that occurred as the deformation-induced phase transfor- 
mation and twin formation reached their limits. In brief, the com- 
posite action of the TRIP and TWIP effects was observed in the 
deformation of Nb10. 
All of the XRD patterns and phase analysis results of the Nb10 
aged at 400 °C (12 h), 450 °C (1, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 h), and 500 °C 
(15 min and 1, 3, 6, and 12 h) are presented in Fig. 4 and summa- 
rized in Table 1 . The Ti 2 ZrO structure was determined to be in the 
ω phase on the basis of the JCPDS (Joint Committee on Powder 
Diffraction Standards) database entry for Ti 2 ZrO. The crystal had 
an ordered hexagonal structure [1 1] . The intensity of the ω phase 
was strong in the specimens treated at 400 °C (12 h), 450 °C (1, 12, 
18, 24, 30, and 36 h), and 500 °C for 15 min and 1 h, and the spec- 
imen treated at 500 °C for 15 min exhibited asymmetrical peaks, 
with the left shoulder contributed by the early transition structure 
of the ω phase called embryonic ω [8] . However, the ω phase was 
not found for the specimens treated at 500 °C for 3, 6, and 12 h. 
Instead, the HCP phase ( α phase) was observed in the specimens 
treated at 450 °C (1, 12, 18, 24, 30, and 36 h) and 500 °C for 1, 3, 
6, and 12 h. This demonstrated that the ω phase was formed at 
temperatures lower than 500 °C. Through up to 36 h at 450 °C, a 
large portion of the structure was in the ω phase in addition to 
some formed α phase. However, the ω phase almost disappeared 
and was replaced by the α phase after treatment at 500 °C for 3 h. 
In addition, a comparison of the specimens treated at 400 °C for 
12 h, 450 °C for 1 h, and 500 °C for 1 h revealed that no α phase 
was formed at 400 °C (12 h). This revealed the precipitation se- 
quence of the present alloy: from a supersaturated BCC ( β) phase 
to the ω phase and then to the HCP ( α) phase. In addition, the 
transformation rate increased as the temperature increased. 
Fig. 5 shows the SEM images of the specimens treated at 500 °C 
for 15 min and for 1 and 3 h. Fine and dense spheroids of ω phase 
were dispersed in the matrix of the specimen treated for 15 min. 
The particle size was approximately 0.06 μm. The ω phase (light 
gray) coarsened to a particle size of approximately 0.29 μm, and 
α particles measuring approximately 0.2 × 0.4 μm were formed 
in platelets in the specimen treated at 500 °C for 1 h. The ω phase 
was mostly replaced by α platelets in the specimen treated at 
500 °C for 3 h; the platelets coarsened to a size of approximately 
0.2 × 0.8 μm. According to a previous study, α particles tend to 
nucleate at the interface between the ω phase and β matrix and 
then digest the ω phase [8] . Li et al. studied the phase transforma- 
tions in the near- β Ti alloy Ti-6Cr-5Mo-5V-4Al (in wt.%) and found 
that the O-rich regions present at the ω/ β interface were hot spots 
for α formation [ 7 , 8 ]. In addition, the compositional partitioning 
of solutes in the ɷ and α phases indicated that the transformation 
from ɷ to α involved rapid lattice reconstruction at the ω/ α inter- 
face.

depict the TTT curves for the present alloy, as shown in Fig. 5 (d). 
The concluding remark is as follows, the α’’ martensite with or- 
thorhombic structure is formed in small amount by water quench- 
ing from high-temperature solution treatment ﬁrstly, and the 
following-up aging treatment at different temperatures causes se- 
quential precipitates, ω and α phases. The phase transformation 
with increasing time evolves: supersaturated β + small amount α’’ 
martensite → β + ω → β + α + ω → β + α. 
The mechanical properties of the specimens aged at 450 °C for 
36 h and 500 °C for 15 min and 1, 3, and 12 h were obtained 
from the stress–train curve in Fig. 2 (a) and are listed in Table 1 . 
The specimen aged at 450 °C for 36 had a hardness of 346 HV5 
and the following tensile properties: E = 104.6 GPa, YS = 1031 
MPa, UTS = 1085 MPa, and EL = 3%. Compared with that of 
the specimen just after RHT discussed in Section 3.2, the E value 
was approximately 50% higher, YS was nearly doubled, and EL 
correspondingly decreased. As mentioned in the section 3.3, this 
state had a three-phase composite microstructure. The consider- 
able strengthening was related to the ﬁne ω and α precipitates dis- 
persed in the β matrix, whereas the considerable stiffening was re- 
lated to the decreased volume fraction of the low-Young’s-modulus 
β phase and increased interfaces between the ω and β phases and 
between the α and β phases. The peak hardness of the specimen 
aged at 500 °C for 15 min was 312 HV5, and its E , YS, UTS, and 
EL were 90.3 GPa, 952 MPa, 984 MPa, and 2%, respectively; these 
values are inferior to those of the specimen aged at 450 °C for 36 
h. This is reasonable because the specimen aged at 450 °C for 36 h 
state already contained α precipitates, whereas that aged at 500 °C 
for 15 min only contained ω phase. The specimen aged at 500 °C 
for 12 h had a hardness value of 274 HV5 and the following ten- 
sile properties: E = 88.6 GPa, YS = 855 MPa, UTS = 892 MPa, and 
EL = 6%. The mechanical properties improved with the aging time 
because, although a little strength was sacriﬁced, the EL increased 
by several times. Because the precipitates were mainly of the α
phase in the specimens treated for 3, 12, and 36 h, the transfor- 
mation of ω precipitates to α precipitates was beneﬁcial for im- 
proving the ductility of the material. Therefore, longer aging to ob- 
tain α precipitates is the key to obtain a desirable combination of 
mechanical properties.
