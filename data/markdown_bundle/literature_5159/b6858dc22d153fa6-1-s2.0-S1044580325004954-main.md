The limited cryogenic yield strength of CoCrFeNiMn high-entropy alloy (HEA) restricts its widespread applica -
tion. In this investigation, microalloying and laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) is employed to fabricate an 
Al

1. Introduction
The CoCrFeNiMn high-entropy alloy (HEA), featuring a single-phase 
face-centered cubic (FCC) structure, has garnered significant interest for 
exceptional mechanical properties, coupled with outstanding corrosion 
resistance, wear resistance, high thermal stability, and remarkable ra -
diation resistance [ 1 – 3 ]. Nevertheless, its inherently low yield strength 
at cryogenic temperatures presents a major obstacle to its broad adop -
tion in industrial scenarios (e.g., engine parts for liquid rocket) [ 4 ]. 
Consequently, simultaneously enhancing the strength and ductility of 
this single-phase HEA is a critical step toward realizing its extensive 
application potential. Several strengthening mechanisms have been 
investigated, including precipitation hardening [ 5 ], grain boundary 
strengthening [ 6 ], solid solution strengthening [ 7 ], and dislocation 
strengthening [ 8 ]. Among these mechanisms, impeding dislocation 
motion through lattice distortion to promote dislocation accumulation 
and induce dislocation strengthening is an effective approach to 
enhance strength while retaining ductility [ 9 ]. Additionally, twinning- 
induced plasticity (TWIP) is a common deformation mechanism at 
cryogenic temperatures, promoting increased strength and ductility 
[ 10 ]. However, conventional manufacturing techniques such as casting, 
powder metallurgy, rolling, and vacuum arc melting often fail to achieve 
optimal strengthening and fabricate complex components due to 
inherent limitations, including compositional segregation, porosity, and 
prominent columnar dendritic structures [ 11 , 12 ].
Laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) offers distinct advantages over 
traditional manufacturing techniques, such as high precision, short 
production cycles, efficient raw material usage, and the capability to 
fabricate complex structures [ 13 ]. The high temperatures and rapid 
solidification characteristic of LPBF [ 14 ] enable the formation of fine 
cellular subgrains and columnar grains, which enhance the mechanical 
properties of the alloys. This has led to the increasing adoption of LPBF 
for fabricating HEAs [ 15 – 17 ]. On the other hand, microalloying in -
creases lattice distortion and enhances strength [ 18 ]. The addition of 
elements such as C, Al, or Ti to CoCrFeMnNi-based HEAs markedly 
improved their tensile strength. However, the adding of Ti and C may

4.2. Impact of Al ₂ O ₃ pinning effect at different temperatures
Fig. 14 shows the pinning effects of Al ₂ O ₃ nanoparticles in LPBF 
Al
0.3
CoCrFeNiMn HEA at different temperatures. At 298 K, as shown in 
Fig. 14 a, Al ₂ O ₃ nanoparticles, uniformly dispersed within the FCC ma -
trix, exert a significant pinning effect on dislocations during deforma -
tion, obstructing dislocation slip and inducing pile-up, thereby 
increasing resistance to plastic flow and enhancing strength through the 
Orowan strengthening effect [ 46 ]. Heterogeneous structural materials 
can be engineered by incorporating a ductile (lower-hardness) 
Al
0.3
CoCrFeNiMn high-entropy alloy (HEA) matrix, which is fortified 
with rigid (higher-hardness) nano-scale Al ₂ O ₃ particles. In this config -
uration, the HEA matrix is designated as the “ ductile zone, ” while the 
Al ₂ O ₃ particles constitute the “ rigid zone. ” As the ductile zone initially 
undergoes plastic deformation, the rigid zone retains its elastic behavior, 
resulting in a deformation incompatibility. To ensure interfacial conti -
nuity, geometrically necessary dislocations form at the boundary, 
inducing back stress that enhances the strength of the ductile zone, 
thereby elevating the overall stress capacity of the composite [ 47 , 48 ]. 
Consequently, under the combined effects of stress and strain, disloca -
tions tend to concentrate at the boundary between the ductile and rigid 
phases, enhancing the structural and mechanical properties at the 
interface.
At cryogenic temperatures, highly activated dislocation slip and 
deformation-induced twinning are inevitably constrained by Al ₂ O ₃ 
nanoparticles. Figs. 14 b-d reveal the interaction between Al ₂ O ₃ nano -
particles and thicker twin lamellae at 200 K. For Al ₂ O ₃ particles with 
relatively small sizes, the obstruction to twin boundary extension is 
limited ( Fig. 14 b). However, as shown in Figs. 14 c and d, when multiple 
Al ₂ O ₃ nanoparticles agglomerate, forming larger particles with spherical 
and rhombic morphologies, they create a more significant barrier to 
twin boundary extension. The twin boundaries are effectively pinned by 
Al ₂ O ₃ nanoparticles, resulting in substantial dislocation accumulation 
near the Al ₂ O ₃ -nanotwin interfaces. This pinning refines the twin 
structure, enhancing strength via the dynamic Hall-Petch effect [ 49 ], 
while the accumulated dislocations contribute to strain hardening, 
preserving ductility to some extent. At 77 K, an intriguing pinning effect 
emerges with the formation of thinner twin lamellae. The significant 
reduction in SFE activates extensive twinning, but the expansion of these 
fine twins is hindered by Al ₂ O ₃ nanoparticles ( Fig. 14 e), forming SF at 
the Al ₂ O ₃ -nanotwin interface ( Fig. 14 f), possibly related to restricted 
dislocation dynamics and reduced SFE [ 45 , 50 , 51 ]. In this study, across 
all temperature tensile tests, Al ₂ O ₃ nanoparticles (~58.5 nm) exhibited 
no significant deformation or shear during plastic deformation [ 52 ], due 
to the incoherent interface between the FCC HEA matrix and the nano- 
Al ₂ O ₃ particles [ 53 ]. However, while these nanoparticles effectively pin 
dislocations and twin boundaries, they induce localized stress concen -
trations, reducing ductility [ 54 , 55 ].
