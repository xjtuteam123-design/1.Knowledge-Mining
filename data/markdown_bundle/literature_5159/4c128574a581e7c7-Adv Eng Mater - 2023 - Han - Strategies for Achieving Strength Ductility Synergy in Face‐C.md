Strategies for Achieving Strength –Ductility Synergy in
Face-Centered Cubic High- and Medium-Entropy Alloys
Dong Han, Shaoxin Cai, Wenhai Sun, Baijun Yang,* and Jianqiang Wang*
1. Introduction
Metallic materials with high strength are always desired for struc-
tural parts for transportation, spacecraft, and other engineering
applications because it is expected that an increase in load-
bearing capacity can signi ﬁcantly exchange for the reduction
of weight, thus enhancing their energy ef ﬁciency. Apart from
strength, high ductility and toughness are also particularly attrac-
tive for engineering materials to form into diverse shapes and to
prevent disastrous failure during service in harsh environ-
ments.[1,2] Consequently, the design and development of metallic
materials having high strength and high
ductility is an unfading research focus
for material investigators.
Unfortunately, strength and ductility are
a pair of natural contradictions in most of
metallic materials, that is, improving one of
them always comes at the expense of the
other; this paradigm is regarded as the
strength–ductility trade-off. [3,4] As a matter
of fact, the existence of such a dilemma in
metallic materials is not surprising. From
the dislocation theory, the strength of
metallic materials mainly associates with
the obstacles to the motion of dislocations,
while the ductility primarily stems from the
activation of dislocation, including disloca-
tion multiplication, accumulation, propaga-
tion, etc.[5] Taking nanostructured alloys as
an example, [6] the yield strength is several
times higher than that of the conventional
coarse grain (CG) counterpart thanks to the
grain boundary (GB) strengthening, but
their ductility is rather low and in most
cases far less than that of normal metals
due to the lack of dislocation storage capacity inside the ﬁne
grains (FGs). Thus, as a consequence of the strength –ductility
trade-off dilemma, engineers are often forced to choose either
strong or ductile material, not both as desired.
Nonetheless, numerous works have been carried out over the
last millennia to achieve a good strength –ductility match in
metallic materials. Arising from those works, some relatively
effective strategies have been obtained, including a mainstream
tactics that microalloying would bring about a positive effect in
tailoring properties toward expected directions (such as Fe-, Al-,
Cu-, Mg-, or Ni-based alloys). [7] However, it should be noted that
after thousands of years of development, the variety of alloys
designed based on traditional single principal element approach
has tended to be depleted due to the limited composition design
space to avoid the formation of brittle intermetallic compounds,
which is especially obvious when we seek to optimize the perfor-
mance of existing alloys.[8] Therefore, a new alloy design strategy,
which can provide a far-reaching compositional space to develop
a variety of new alloys with excellent properties, is highly
demanded.
One such new concept emerged in the early 2000s that is
based on mixing ﬁve or more principal elements with equiatomic
ratio or near-equiatomic ratio (the concentration of each constit-
uent element changes from 5 to 35 at%). [9] Obviously, different
from conventional single principal element approach, this
unique multiprincipal element design concept would result in

High- and medium-entropy alloys (HEAs/MEAs), also called as multicomponent
alloys, are a new class of materials that break through the traditional alloy design
concept based on single principal element. However, they do not break away from
the magic spell of strength –ductility trade-off. Therefore, designing HEAs/MEAs
with both high strength and high ductility still remains a great challenge
nowadays. This article provides a review on the recent progress in mechanical
properties of face-centered cubic (FCC) HEAs/MEAs. First, several traditional
strengthening strategies are brie ﬂy reviewed, focusing on the strengthening
mechanisms and the optimized mechanical properties. Subsequently, various
novel strategies for achieving strength –ductility synergy in HEAs/MEAs are
summarized, which include lowering the stacking fault energy, regulating the
short-range order, promoting transformation-induced plasticity, and constructing
heterogeneous microstructures. The basic ideas and related underlying mecha-
nisms from these strategies are discussed. Finally, the current challenges and the
future outlooks are emphasized and addressed systematically. In brief, the present
review is expected to provide a useful guide for the design of HEAs/MEAs with
superior mechanical properties.

2.1. Solid-Solution Strengthening
Solid-solution strengthening is mainly due to lattice distortion
caused by the introduction of solute atoms into the matrix, which
generally increases the resistance to dislocation slip, thus
improving the strength of the alloy. [74] In light of this, it is widely
accepted that the solution strengthening effect in HEAs/MEAs is
notably higher than that in conventional alloys due to their multi-
principal characteristics. [7] For example, the half value of yield
strength in some HEAs/MEAs is mainly associated with the
solution strengthening. [76] Depending on the size of solute

Consequently, the mechanism of substitutional strengthening
is different from that of interstitial strengthening.
Substitutional strengthening forms by occupying the lattice
sites of the solvent atoms with solute atoms with similar size
to solvent atoms. [77] In HEAs/MEAs, however, each element
not only has the same probability of occupying a lattice site
regardless of chemical ordering, but also has its speci ﬁc and
unique atomic size, which leads to the severe lattice distortion. [78]
In this case, substitutional strengthening effect induced by sim-
ply increasing or decreasing the content of one principal element
in the alloy system may have no signi ﬁcant effect on the mechan-
ical properties of HEAs/MEAs. For example, Chen et al. [79] inves-
tigated the effect of Cr content on the microstructure and
properties of AlTiFeNiCu HEAs, which revealed that the hard-
ness value does not increase obviously with Cr content. In spite
of this, great efforts have been made by researchers to improve
the substitutional strengthening effect of HEAs/MEAs. For
example, numerous studies [80–84] have shown that the introduc-
tion of large size substitutional atoms (such as Al, W, Ti and
Al/Ti/Mo codoping) into HEAs/MEAs is an effective way to
improve the strength of the alloys. This is mainly based on
the fact that the magnitude of lattice distortion is linked to both
the atomic size and modulus mis ﬁts between solute and
solvent atoms. More recently, Varvenne et al. [85] put forward a
solid-solution strengthening model to evaluate quantitatively
the yield stress of single-phase HEAs/MEAs. In this mode, it
is assumed that HEAs/MEAs are a high-concentrated solute-
strengthened random alloys, in which each constitutive element
is considered as a solute embedded in the “average matrix ”. So,
the solid-solution strengthening in HEAs/MEAs mainly comes
from the sum of the elastic interaction energies between each
solute and the dislocation; accordingly, the mis ﬁt volumes of sol-
ute play an important role in strengthening effect. That is to say,
larger solute size/volume will lead to larger mis ﬁt volumes, local
lattice distortion, and higher strengthening effect. Inspired by
this, Zhang et al. [86] constructed a novel (NiCoCr) 92Al6Ta2
MEA with huge mis ﬁt volumes via Al/Ta codoping within
NiCoCr MEA. Comparing the NiCoCr MEA, the yield strength
and ultimate tensile strength of (NiCoCr) 92Al6Ta2 increase by
/C25 102% to /C25 600 MPa and /C25 35% to /C25 1000 MPa, respectively,
associated with good ductility beyond 50%.
In terms of model, various solid-solution strengthening mod-
els have been proposed to predict the contribution of substitution
strengthening to the yield strength of HEAs, taking into account
the fact that the classic Fleischer [87] and Labusch [88] models in
traditional alloys cannot be directly applied to HEAs due to
the lack of reference atoms. Among these models, in addition
to the work of Varvenne et al. [85] mentioned above, the
Senkov[89] and Toda–Caraballo models[89] are two other represen-
tative solid-solution strengthening models. Speci ﬁcally, in the
Senkov model, it is assumed that the local concentration is
the same as the average concentration of HEAs; in this case,
the local environment around each atom can be roughly esti-
mated. Combined with the properties of the BCC lattice, accord-
ingly, Senkov et al. [89] successfully extended the Labusch model
to BCC HEAs by rede ﬁning the mis ﬁts of shear moduli and
atomic size between the element i and its neighbor elements j
in the alloy. Taking a step forward, Toda –Caraballo[90] again
extended the Labusch model by adopting a matrix form, and thus
proposed a general formulation for solid-solution hardening
effect in multicomponent alloys, such as HEAs. In this mode,
the solid-solution strengthening is attributed to the lattice distor-
tion caused by the presence of different elements rather than the
solute–solvent interactions. Thus, the interatomic spacing
matrix, S = {Sij}, needs to be calculated to describe the lattice dis-
tortion, as detailed in ref. [91]. It should be noted that considering
that there are several nice review articles focusing on strength-
ening mechanisms in HEAs, [73,77,92,93] the more detailed
description involved in the above models is no longer provided
in the current article.
The success of the above models in predicting the yield stress
has been demonstrated in some HEAs. [90,94,95] However, it is
undeniable that these models still suffer from some drawbacks.
On one hand, an important assumption in these strengthening
models is a random distribution of the components on the lattice
sites in the alloy, which is probably not the case, as discussed in
some literatures. [7,96] Thus, the potential microstructures in
HEAs such as short-range order, nanosized precipitates, or phase
decomposition may enlarge the deviation between experimental
and predicted results. For example, although the Toda –Caraballo
model can describe well the solid-solution strengthening of dif-
ferent solutes and crystal structures in some HEAs, it is usually
associated with a typical deviation of 25%. [92] On the other hand,
these models are usually suitable for predicting the strength of
HEAs/MEAs in speci ﬁc systems, and their generalization ability
needs to be improved. For example, Wen et al. [97] demonstrated
that the yield stress predicted by the Varvenne model merely
shows a sound tendency for Cr 6(MnFeCoNi)94 and
Cu6(MnFeCoNi)94 HEAs, whereas an opposite trend was
observed for Al 6(MnFeCoNi)94 and Ti 6(MnFeCoNi)94 HEAs.
In addition, the Senkov model has also been extended by Rao
et al. [98] from BCC to FCC HEA systems, indicating a limited
predictive ability.

2.2. GB Strengthening
GBs, one of the most important planar defects in polycrystalline
materials, are mainly composed of various kinds of disloca-
tions.[74] In other words, the GB is a region of severe localized
disorder; thus, the GB takes on higher the resistance to disloca-
tion slip than the grains interior. Moreover, the mis ﬁt in the
orientation between the adjacent grains is another source of
GB strengthening because dislocations at GBs require additional
energy to change the direction in order to achieve their
motion.[116] Therefore, GB strengthening is an important
strengthening method in both conventional alloys and
HEAs/MEAs.
Based on the above knowledge, grain re ﬁnement is a common
means to implement the GB strengthening concept in metals
and alloys. This is because, on the one hand, grain re ﬁnement
can reduce the average obstacle spacing that hinders dislocation
slip.[117] On the other hand, reducing the grain size also means
that the volume fraction of GBs in the matrix will be signi ﬁcantly
increased, which leads to an increase in the resistance to
dislocation motion and crack propagation, thus strengthening
metals and alloys. [118] Figure 4 shows the strength –ductility pro-
ﬁles for some HEAs and MEAs with different grain sizes. [73]
Clearly, the grain re ﬁnement can signi ﬁcantly improve the
strength of the alloys, yet accompanied by an inevitably decrease
in ductility.

tion, σGB, can be estimated by the Hall –Petch relation [119]

2.3. Precipitation Strengthening
In metals and alloys, it is one of the most common strengthening
methods to increase the strength by dispersing second phase
particles to prevent dislocation movement. [74,131] Generally
speaking, there are two conventional methods used to
introduce the second phase into the matrix: endogenesis and
addition.[132] The former refers to the ways in which advanta-
geous strengthening phases (such as L1 2,[133] B2,[134] sigma,[135]
Laves[136] carbide precipitation, [137] etc.) are precipitated from
supersaturated solid solution of alloy at appropriate temperature;
yet the latter usually refers to the introduction of the
second phase by mechanical mixing and other external methods
to strengthen the matrix, for example, oxide dispersion-
strengthened CoCrFeMnNi HEA [138] and arti ﬁcial addition of
the ductile second phases in the bulk amorphous materials. [139]
However, it should be pointed out that due to the large differen-
ces in phase structure and lattice constant between the arti ﬁcially
added second phases and matrix, the nucleation barrier of the
second phases in the matrix is generally high, which is not con-
ducive to their high-density precipitation. More importantly,
such differences would signi ﬁcantly improve the elastic strain
energy between the dislocation and the second phases, which
would inevitably increase the probability of crack initiation near
the interface, and thus seriously degrade the ductility. [140,141] For
instance, Hadraba et al. [138] showed that the addition of Y 2O3
results in a 30% increase in the ultimate tensile strength of
the equiatomic CoCrFeNiMn HEA, associated with an 83% loss
of ductility. Consequently, precipitation strengthening based on
endogenesis mechanism is an effective and most widely used
method to enhance the strength of HEAs/MEAs.

mainly depends on the size, morphology, volume fraction,
distribution of precipitates, as well as interface relationship
between the precipitates and matrix. [13] Considering the precipi-
tate size and interface relationship, there are two possibilities for
dislocation motion in matrix. Speci ﬁcally, when dislocations
encounter small-sized precipitates having a coherent relationship
with the matrix, they will cut through the precipitates. [142]

ent relationship with the matrix, dislocations will choose to
bypass them, which is the so-called Orowan bypass mecha-

2.4. Dislocation Strengthening
In principle, all metallic materials have preexisting dislocations
to some extent, and their slip is responsible for the plastic defor-

density of dislocations in the matrix increases signi ﬁcantly,
which greatly promotes the interaction between dislocations
from different slip planes. In this case, these intersections act
as obstacles to further motion of dislocations, leading to a
strengthening effect. Therefore, the essence of dislocation
strengthening comes from the interaction between dislocations
during deformation. [170]

mobility room,[175] but also result in the reduction of dislocation
multiplication ability according to the Kocks –Mecking
model.[178,179] In addition, after the alloy undergoes severe plastic
deformation, damage may occur within the matrix, which will
accelerate the formation of necking. [180]

3.1. Lowering the Stacking Fault Energy
According to the traditional dislocation theory, when a full or per-
fect dislocation on the (1 1 1) plane of FCC crystals resolves itself
into two partials, a region of stacking fault will be created
between the partials, and the accompanied energy per unit area
of faults is called as the stacking fault energy (SFE). [74] In general,
the larger the width between the partial dislocations is, the lower
the SFE will be, and vice versa. [197] Therefore, the SFE is consid-
ered to be one of the most effecting parameters in controlling the
deformation mechanisms and consequently the mechanical per-
formance of FCC metallic materials. Speciﬁcally, in the FCC met-
als and alloys with high SFEs, cross slip of dislocations can be
activated frequently by jointing the partial dislocations into a full
dislocation due to a lower equilibrium distance between two par-
tial dislocations, which leads to the formation of typical wavy-slip
dislocation con ﬁgurations, such as veins, persistent slip bands
(PSBs), and dislocation cells. [198–201] Conversely, the large dis-
tance between two partials will increase the dif ﬁculty of their
reunion in materials with low SFEs, thus limiting the dislocation
cross slip. Accordingly, reducing the SFE usually results in a
transformation of dislocation movement from wavy slip to planar
slip.[202–204] More importantly, the reduction of the SFE could
promote the nucleation of deformation twins (DTs). [126,205,206]
As a result, there is a prominent difference between low and high
SFE materials in the work hardening response. For high-SFE
metals with a FCC structure, the work hardening rate continu-
ously decreases with the increase of strain, which is ascribed to
the operation of cross-slip process as a dynamic recovery
mechanism.[207–209] However, a typical work hardening rate
recovery phenomenon can be found in low SFE metals, in which
the work hardening rate keeps almost constant or increases with
strain,[210–212] showing an improvement in the work hardening
capacity. Consequently, a good strength –ductility match usually
can be achieved in low SFE FCC metals rather than high SFE
ones. Taking Cu –Al alloys for example, [206] as shown in
Figure 10 a, lowering SFE in Cu –Al alloys through increasing
the Al content could lead to a change of deformation microstruc-
tures from dislocation cells to planar slip structures and then to
DTs. The formation of planar slip bands and DTs leads to a nota-
ble reduction in mean free path of dislocation motion and enhan-
ces the work hardening ability, ﬁnally achieving a good strength –
ductility synergy in Cu –Al alloys with increasing Al content.
Based on the above understanding, lowering the SFE has been
regarded as an effective strategy for achieving strength –ductility
synergy in FCC HEAs/MEAs.[111,213–216] It should be pointed out
that the FCC HEAs/MEAs generally have low SFEs because of
their multicomponent characteristics. [217] Therefore, one of
the most important motives for lowering the SFEs in
HEAs/MEAs is to catalyze the DTs as much as possible, and then
introduce the twinning-induced plasticity (TWIP) effect to
obtain an excellent combination of high strength and high
ductility.[213,218] As exhibited in the separation distance between
Shockley partials in Figure 10b, the SFE of CrCoNi MEA can be
estimated to be 18 /C6 4m Jm /C0 2, which is approximately 33%
and 53% lower than that determined previously for
FeCoNiCr (27 /C6 4m Jm /C0 2) and Fe 20Co15Ni25Cr20Mn20 HEAs
(38 /C6 6m J m /C0 2), respectively. Owing to its relatively lower
SFE compared to those of FeCoNiCr and FeCoNiCrMn HEAs,
DTs are observed more frequently in NiCoCr MEAs after tensile
deformation. The enhanced DT potential improves the work
hardening ability by introducing extra boundaries that act as

3.2. Regulating Short-Range Order
Short-range order refers to the preferential bonding between
pairs of atoms in a solid-solution system from a few to several
interatomic distances without the formation of intermetallic
compounds.[231] In fact, for traditional solid-solution alloys, sol-
ute atoms are not completely dispersed in a random way in alloy-
ing matrix, and the solute and solvent atoms usually tend to form
short-range order structure at atomic level due to the interactions
between them. [232] The origin of SRO formation in alloys could
be attributed to multiple sources such as enthalpy-driven bond-
ing,[233] magnetic interactions,[234] electronic interaction,[235] etc.
In terms of the effect of SRO on the deformation mechanism, the
earliest study can be traced back to the work of Clément [236] in
1984. Clément discussed the causes of abnormal planar slip in
some alloys with high SFEs, and suggested that the existence of
SRO structures in alloys should be responsible for the planar slip
behavior of dislocations. Based on this ﬁnding, Gerold and
Karnthaler[237] proposed a “glide plane softening ” mechanism
to reveal the reason for the change of slip mode caused by
SRO. It should be noted that some recent studies [238–240] have
suggested that the presence of SRO would greatly alter SFE.
This means that in addition to the slip modes of the dislocations,
the SRO also signi ﬁcantly affects the DTs and phase transforma-
tion behavior of alloys. Recently, Han et al. [241] reported that the
increase of SRO in Cu –Mn alloys with high SFEs can promote
the formation of planar slip and DTs, thus resulting in a synchro-
nous improvement of strength and ductility.
However, the understanding of SRO in HEAs/MEAs has expe-
rienced some minor setbacks. One of the main reasons is that in
the early stage of HEAs/MEAs, they were considered to be a ran-
dom solid-solution alloy, in which all the constituent elements
are randomly distributed, considering the serious disordered
lattice distortions. [9,242] Recently, however, increasing number
of studies [240,243,244] have shown that the distribution of atoms
in HEAs/MEAs is not an ideal disorder state because of the diver-
sity of atomic radii and the intricate interactions between the con-
stituent elements. It can lead to the formation of SRO structures
during solidiﬁcation or/and heat treatment processes. For exam-
ple, Zhang et al. [245] examined the local lattice characteristics in
the NiCoCr MEA, and found that the Cr atoms are favorably
bonded with Ni and Co elements, re ﬂecting a tendency of
SRO formation. Ding et al. [246] also pointed out that SRO could
be regulated through reasonably designing HEA system and
processing conditions. Zhang et al. [240] ﬁrst observed the SRO
in the CoCrNi MEA via high-resolution and energy- ﬁltered trans-
mission electron microscopy and reported the strength –ductility
synergy induced by the SRO in this alloy. In their study, the
CoCrNi MEA samples were subjected to different heat treat-
ments after homogenization at 1200 °C: 1) directly water-
quenched to 298 K; 2) aging at 1273 K for 120 h followed by slow
furnace cooling. Interestingly, the diffraction pattern for the aged
sample represented a streaking pattern indicative of SRO
whereas only the FCC diffraction spots were observed in the
water-quenched alloy. Further, microstructure observation from
bulk compressed samples revealed a marked trend of the local-
ized planar conﬁguration of dislocations in the aged sample with
SRO. This is contrary to the water-quenched sample without
SRO in which a random distribution of dislocations is dominant.
The introduction of SRO leads to an approximately 25% increase
of the yield strength on the premise of maintaining the original
ductility. More importantly, the initial work hardening rate of the
aged sample is twice that of the water-quenched sample. It
should be said that this study leads to the trend of optimizing
the mechanical properties of HEAs/MEAs by regulating SRO.
And after that, many relevant studies have been carried
out.[247–251] Inspired by the work of Zhang et al., [240] for example,
Zhang et al. [249] successfully improved the SRO degree in the
Fe40Mn20Cr20Ni20 HEAs through high-temperature aging treat-
ment, thereby promoting the strength of the alloy. By introduc-
ing Al element into the CoFeNi MEA, Dasari et al. [250] achieved
the transformation of the alloy from disorder to order, and found
that the formation of SRO appropriately can enhance the
strength and ductility of the alloy simultaneously. In addition,
Gou et al. [251] found that regulating the SRO is also bene ﬁcial
for optimizing the mechanical properties of additive manufac-
tured HEA.
More recently, doping of nonmetallic elements has also
been proved to be an effective way to improve the SRO in
HEAs/MEAs.[101,252–254] For example, He et al. [254] reported a
universal strategy for designing ultrastrong and ductile FCC
HEAs, by introducing interstitial-driven SRO through heavy
N-doping and simple thermomechanical processing. After add-
ing 3.2 at% N atom into the Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10 HEA, they found
that the SRO tends to form around N atoms in the alloy. Such an
interstitial-driven SRO effectively facilitates the planar slip of dis-
locations ( Figure 12 b), increases the dislocation proliferation,
and achieves a balance with the substructure homogenization
(Figure 12c). As a result, the tensile strength of N 3.2 HEAs
increases from 620 /C6 5 to 876 /C6 9 MPa, and the ductility
increased by 2% (Figure 12a).
It is important yet interesting that, at the present time,
whether SRO obviously affects the mechanical properties of
HEAs/MEA still remains controversial. In contrast to the work
of Zhang et al. [240] described above, Inoue et al. [255] and Yin

directly related to the SRO degree. Thus, future efforts are
needed to fully understand the roles of SROs in the deformation
behavior of HEAs. Fortunately, driven by a strong scienti ﬁc

3.3. Promoting Transformation-Induced Plasticity
Inspired by the idea of “metastable-engineering” in titanium
alloys and high Mn steels, transformation-induced plasticity
(TRIP) has also been applied to the design of HEAs/MEAs with
high strength and high ductility. Its essence is to obtain two piv-
otal beneﬁts through the transformation of austenite to martens-
ite under plastic deformation [118,259–261]: interface strengthening
resulting from a dual-phase microstructure; work hardening
induced by phase transformation. These two contributions
result, respectively, in increased strength and ductility. The ﬁrst
study on TRIP effects came from the work of Li et al., [32] in which
a metastable Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10 HEA was carefully designed
based on this idea that the reduction of Mn content in
Fe80/C0 xMnxCo10Cr10 HEA can lead to the decrease of SFE and
the destruction in the stability of the high-temperature FCC
phase. In this case, the FCC phase in the Fe 50Mn30Co10Cr10
HEA would partially transform into HCP martensite with
increasing strain due to its lower room temperature phase
stability (resulting from a near-zero SFE), [133] as illustrated in
Figure 13. Recent studies[262,263] have indicated that this metasta-
ble feature not only facilitates a forward TRIP effect from the
FCC to the HCP phase, but also leads to local reversal back into
FCC phase. Such a bidirectional TRIP behavior during the plastic
deformation can activate the self-re ﬁning process of microstruc-
ture,[263] causing the formation of nanolaminate-type substruc-
tures. As a result, the increased interface density signi ﬁcantly

promotes the crystal interior deformation, relieves an accumulated
strain within the matrix, as well as generates an additional large
number of geometrically necessary dislocations (GNDs) at the
FCC/BCC interface due to the difference in volume between them,
thus contributing to a stable work hardening capacity. Bene ﬁting
from this, an excellent strength –ductility synergy was achieved.

Cr23Al4,[271] and AlFeCoNiCu[272] HEAs.

the contradiction between strength and ductility for FCC
HEAs/MEAs. However, faced with a sea of HEA/MEA systems,
the HEA/MEA systems that can realize TRIP are still limited.
Therefore, more breakthroughs are needed on the basis of exist-
ing studies.
3.4. Constructing Heterogeneous Microstructure
Different from traditional polycrystalline metals and alloys with
homogeneous microstructures that have no obvious difference in
mechanical properties between domains within the matrix,
heterogeneous materials are a new class of structural materials
having dramatic heterogeneity in microstructural features, such
as diverse grain sizes, orientations, crystal structure, etc., which
leads to the signi ﬁcant strength heterogeneity from one domain
to another. [5,273] Consequently, there is a remarkable difference
between traditional and heterogeneous materials in the plastic
behavior. Speci ﬁcally, for homogeneous bulk materials, the
plastic strain is ﬁrst produced by dislocation slip, after which
strain localization occurs due to stress concentration caused
by dislocation –dislocation/GB interactions. Upon further load-
ing, the area of strain localization increases, thus causing the
necking and fracture failure. In other words, the plastic deforma-
tion of homogeneous materials is actually a result of continuous
strain localization. [3]
However, the deformation process of heterogeneous materials
is usually considered as a strain delocalization process, which is
attributed to a unique multistage deformation behavior. [3,274]
These stages are labeled as I, II, and III, respectively, as show
in Figure 15a. At the initial stage of straining (stage I), either hard
or soft domains undergo elastic deformation.[274] After this stage,
with increasing strain to stage II, the soft domains begin to plas-
tic deformation due to relatively low the yield strength, while the
hard domains remain deformed in an elastic manner. [275,276] In
this case, a large number of GNDs will be activated in the soft
domains nearby heterogeneous interfaces to accommodate the
strain gradient caused by mechanical incompatibility between
hard and soft domains, [277] as shown in Figure 15b. The

3.4.1. Heterogeneous Gradient Structure
Macroscopically, the heterogeneous gradient structure (HGS)
can generally be regarded as a sandwich structure consisting
of a CG core and gradient surface layers, in which microstruc-
tures are characterized by gradually increased grain size,
increased substructure size, and reduced defect density from
the surface layer to the core, as shown in Figure 16a. Surface
mechanical attrition treatment (SMAT) [306] is the most exten-
sively applied technique for fabricating HGS; other techniques
including surface mechanical grinding treatment (SMGT), [307]
surface mechanical rolling treatment (SMRT), [308] ultrasonic
nanocrystalline surface modi ﬁcation (UNSM), [309] rotationally
accelerated shot peening (RASP), [308] and direct torsion treat-
ment[310] are also adopted to prepare HGS. However, it should
be noted that the thickness of the surface gradient layer prepared
by surface treatment technology is limited, with a maximum
thickness of only about 200 μm.[282,310] By contrast, direct torsion
treatment, especially the high-pressure torsion (HPT), can pro-
duce larger scale gradient structures along the diameter of the
samples.[311]

many FCC HEAs/MEAs with HGS. [285–289,312] Taking the

the depth direction. Meanwhile, deformation-induced heteroge-

microstructure results in the hardness of /C25 .6 GPa at the center
and /C25 3 GPa in the surface region, re ﬂecting a heterogeneous
mechanical behavior. In terms of strength and ductility, the
CoCrFeNiMn HEA with HGS (RASP1) displays an increased
strength (up to 418 MPa) and ductility (up to 45%) comparing
with the CG counterpart (Figure 17c). This is attributed to the

immediately after elastic deformation stage, where the plastic

elastic–plastic interface is established. With increasing strain,
more and more grains within the GS layer begin to participate
in plastic deformation, which forces the elastic –plastic
interface to migrate toward the surface gradually. Thanks to
the mechanical incompatibility between CG domains and the

during tensile deformation, [313,314] resulting in a strain gradient

essentially subjected to tensile and compressive stresses, respec-

improving the uniformity of plastic deformation. [315] On the

HDI strengthening and work hardening. [273] Besides, some
surface treatment techniques, such as SMAT and SMRT,
usually cause signi ﬁcant compressive residual stress under
the sample surface,[316] creating a multiaxial stress state. The syn-
ergy between GNDs generation and multiaxial stress states
results in the excellent strength –ductility match in materials
with HGS.
3.4.2. Bimodal and Harmonic Structure
The bimodal structure is characterized by the CGs embedded
into the hard FG matrix in a random manner, [282] as schemati-
cally shown in Figure 16b. The bimodal structure can be an ideal
structure for achieving a good strength –ductility combination in
ultra-FG (UFG) metals. This is because FGs can maintain high
yield strength of matrix; meanwhile, good ductility is expected
due to this fact that the strain gradient caused by the mechanical
incompatibility between CG and FG can provide considerable
work hardening capacity,[273] which delays the necking instability
and overcomes the extreme brittleness of UFG metals. At pres-
ent, constructing bimodal structure in bulk materials is achieved
by severe plastic deformation and followed either dynamic
recrystallization[317] or partial recrystallization annealing. [19,318]
Recently, the bimodal structure is achieved in NiCoCr MEA
through utilizing HPT and subsequently annealed at intermedi-
ate temperatures. [290] This HPT process directly results in the
reﬁnement of grain size of the alloy from several 100 μmt o
50 nm. After subsequent annealing, the microstructure of alloy
is composed of nano- and micron-sized grains, re ﬂecting a
bimodal structure. During tensile deformation, samples pre-
pared by HPT process soften immediately after yielding due
to the lack of work hardening capacity. However, samples with
bimodal structure show an excellent combination of ultrahigh
tensile strength, exceeding 1500 MPa, and decent ductility with
elongations to failure of 10%. This superior mechanical perfor-
mance was attributed to the heterogeneous effect in grain size,
i.e., the HDI strengthening and HDI work hardening.
Sathiyamoorthi et al. [191] also fabricated a NiCoCr MEA with
bimodal structure by HPT followed by annealing. Unlike the
work of Schuh et al., [290] the bimodal structure in this work
was composed of UFGs and micron-sized grains. Surprisingly,
compared with the results reported in ref. [290], the strength of
the alloy did not change signi ﬁcantly, but the ductility increased
by 100%. Such an improvement was attributed to the GB relaxa-
tion and reduction of dislocation density due to the higher
annealing temperature. Consequently, there is no doubt that
the difference in size ratio between the FG and CG plays an
important role in improving the ductility of alloys with bimodal
structure.

3.4.4. Eutectic Structure
Eutectic alloys are a class of material systems consisting of more
than one phase with different crystal structures. Typical eutectic
structures mainly include lamellar or rod-like structures, depend-
ing on the cooling rate and undercooling. [324] Similar to tradi-
tional eutectic alloys, eutectic HEAs/MEAs usually consist of
alternating layers of soft FCC phase and hard BCC phase, [302]
as shown in Figure 16e. Due to the huge mechanical incompati-
bility between FCC and BCC phases, eutectic HEAs/MEAs are a
typical heterogeneous material, which are expected to possess an
excellent combination of strength and ductility. Also importantly,
eutectic alloys are known to have good castability, [325] clearing
obstacles for preparing HEA components with complex shapes.
The ﬁrst reported eutectic HEA is the Fe 30Ni20Mn35Al15 alloy,
with the yield strength of 740 MPa and an elongation of 8%. [326]
Since then, eutectic HEAs/MEAs as a possible structural
material have attracted more and more attention. Arising from
the previous studies, [57,59,301,302,327–329] the AlCoCrFeNi2.1 is the
most widely studied eutectic HEA. Numerous stud-
ies[47,300,301,330,331] have shown that the microstructures of eutec-
tic HEAs/MEAs are closely related to the thermomechanical
treatment processes. For example, when eutectic HEAs are
deformed by warm rolling (750 °C), the microstructures consist
of an arrayed layered area containing B2 phase within the FCC
lamella regions and nonlamellar regions containing ﬁne precip-
itates of B2 and Cr-rich sigma phases. The transition from
ordered FCC to disordered FCC was also observed in both the
lamellar and nonlamellar regions. [300] However, the eutectic sub-
structures produced by cold rolling and subsequent annealing

3.4.5. Multiphase Heterogeneous Grain Structures
Although the above heterostructure strategies endow materials
with a good combination of strength and ductility, there is still
a certain gap between heterostructure materials and advanced
steel materials such as high-strength steel in terms of the
strength, especially the yield strength. [254] To solve this problem,
some researchers [303,304,335,336] have tried to introduce the tradi-
tional precipitation strengthening strategy into heterostructure
materials to unite the advantages of both, which opens a prom-
ising window for further optimizing the mechanical properties of
FCC HEAs/MEAs.
Shukla et al. [303] prepared a precipitation-hardened
Al0.3CoCrFeNi HEA with a heterogeneous grain structure by cold
rolling and annealing, in which a large number of B 2 precipitates
with different sizes (from /C25 100 nm to /C25 2 μm) and morphologies
(plate and globular) are dispersed in the FCC matrix. The hetero-
geneous grain structure is mainly composed of partial recrystal-
lized FG (3 /C6 2 μm) and fully recrystallized CG (14 /C6 3 μm). Such
a multiphase heterogeneous grain structure endows the alloy
with an excellent combination of high yield strength
(700 MPa), high tensile strength (1 GPa), and high ductility
(/C25 28%), as shown in Figure 22 . The multiphase heterogeneous
grain structure sample shows a heterogeneous strain distribution
during deformation, which promotes the generation and accu-
mulation of GNDs. As a result, the HDI strengthening and
HDI work hardening can ensure a stable work hardening ability,
which leads to simultaneous enhancement of strength and duc-
tility. Meanwhile, B2 precipitates and nanotwins complemented
the HDI strengthening and increased inherent matrix strength.
As another example, He et al. [304] constructed span-scale mul-
tiphase heterogeneous grain structure consisting of coarse and
ﬁne nonrecrystallized grains, fully recrystallized grains, hetero-
geneous dispersion of B2 precipitates, σ phase, dislocations,
DTs, annealing twins (ATs), and stacking faults via optimized
combination of Mo-doping and thermomechanical processing
in a NiCoCr MEA. Dislocations, DTs, and stacking faults are
mainly concentrated in the nonrecrystallized grains, while pre-
cipitates and ATs are dominant in the recrystallized domains
(Figure 23). The multiphase heterogeneous grain structure with
span-scale heterogeneous characteristics showed an increase of
about 60% and 34%, respectively, in the yield strength and the
tensile strength without the reduction of ductility compared with
homogeneous counterpart.
In conclusion, the multiphase heterogeneous grain structure
strategy provides a vast design space for the performance optimi-
zation of FCC HEAs/MEAs. In practice, it is not dif ﬁcult to imag-
ine that if each strain hardening mechanism is activated, in turn,
at different strain stages, the alloy may obtain durable and high
work hardening ability, thus realizing the bene ﬁcial combination
of ultrahigh strength and ultrahigh ductility. Therefore, how to
better coordinate and balance various strengthening and tough-
ening mechanisms is the focus and dif ﬁculty of the future
research on HEAs/MEAs.

4. Conclusions and Outlook
Compared with traditional metal materials, HEAs exhibit many
excellent properties and are expected to be potential candidates
for the next generation of advanced structural materials.
Unfortunately, HEAs/MEAs do not get rid of the magic spell
of strength–ductility trade-off, just as in traditional metal materi-
als. Especially for FCC HEAs/MEAs, the lower strength severely
limits their application potential. The key mechanistic principle
for achieving strength –ductility synergy is that the microstruc-
tures need to be adjusted to improve the work hardening capacity
of the alloy, thereby delaying the occurrence of necking. Several
attempts to optimize the mechanical properties of HEAs/MEAs
based on traditional strengthening strategies are reviewed, from
which the fact can be found that the strength –ductility trade-off
dilemma is, to some extent, alleviated by traditional strengthening
strategies, but still exists, due to the limit of work hardening capac-
ity. This is why material scientists are still keen to explore novel
strategies for the strength –ductility synergy in HEAs/MEAs.
With the above outlook in mind, this article reviews the recent
progress in mechanical properties of HEAs/MEAs with empha-
sis on strategies used to achieve strength –ductility synergy,
including lowering the SFE, regulating the SRO, promoting
TRIP ability, and constructing heterogeneous microstructure.
These strategies are all aimed at improving the work hardening
ability of HEAs/MEAs by adjusting the microstructures, so as to
achieve a synergistic improvement of strength and ductility,
rather than a single high strength or high ductility.
Speciﬁcally, at the atomic scale, HEAs/MEAs exhibit local ﬂuc-
tuations in concentration, which can signi ﬁcantly affect the SFE,
the SRO, and consequently dislocation slip mode. A high SRO or
low SFE can effectively inhibit cross slip of dislocation and con-
tribute to high work hardening capacity. In terms of phase
transition, the continuous occurrence of phase transformation
during deformation can help to accommodate plastic deforma-
tion and release local stress concentration. Meanwhile, the addi-
tional interface increases the resistance to dislocation motion,
thus contributing to a strengthening effect. By constructing het-
erogeneous microstructure, mechanical incompatibility between
soft and hard phases is established, which facilitates the genera-
tion and accumulation of GNDs. As a result, the HDI strength-
ening and HDI hardening become the dominant strengthening
and toughening mechanisms of heterogeneous materials,
respectively, leading to a continuous and stable work hardening
capability. In particular, multiphase heterogeneous grain struc-
ture strategy opens a window for the integration of traditional
strengthening strategies and heterostructure strategies, which
is expected to be a research hotspot of FCC HEAs/MEAs in
the future.
However, in the face of a vast number of HEA/MEA systems,
the strength–ductility synergy strategies proposed at present are
still a drop in the bucket, and many unknowns remain to be
explored. 1) In terms of strengthening, studies on solid-solution
strengthening mechanism in HEAs/MEAs are mainly derived
from traditional alloy systems that chie ﬂy consider the strength-
ening contribution caused by lattice distortion, atomic size
mismatch, modulus mismatch, etc. However, due to the charac-
teristics of multiprincipal element and concentration ﬂuctuation,
the lattice distortion in HEAs/MEAs is more serious than that in
traditional alloys, which is also the most signi ﬁcant feature that
distinguishes HEAs/MEAs from ordinary alloys. Under such
severe lattice distortion, the generation and movements of vari-
ous crystal defects should reasonably be different from those in
traditional alloys. As a result, the solid-solution strengthening
models reported so far are usually only suitable for predicting
the strength of HEAs/MEAs in speci ﬁc systems, and their

properties by experimental method is very expensive in terms
of time and cost. More recently, machine learning aided high-

of eutectic HEAs. Recognizing this, machine learning should be
a powerful tool to accelerate the search for desired microstruc-
tures of HEAs/MEAs in future studies. 4) The mechanical
properties of HEAs/MEAs available are mostly about strength
and ductility. Considering the potential applications of
HEAs/MEAs, more studies on the mechanical response behavior
in extreme environments (such as fatigue, high temperature, cor-
rosion) are de ﬁnitely needed.
