ABSTRACT
The FeCrCoNi series of high-entropy alloys have received widespread attention due to their excellent properties 
at room and low temperatures, however, the relatively low yield strengths severely limit their wide application. 
Currently, the research efforts in the field of ceramic particles strengthening mainly focus on the equiatomic 
high-entropy alloy systems. However, there have been few studies on high-entropy alloys with non-equiatomic 
ratios. This paper provides a detailed investigation into the mechanism of the effect of 5 wt% TiC ceramic 
particles on the microstructure, mechanical properties and friction and wear properties of Fe55Cr25Co10Ni10 
high-entropy alloys. The results indicated that the alloy ’ s phase structure of the alloy changes significantly after 
addition 5 wt% TiC ceramic particles. The alloy transitions from the original single FCC solid solution to a 
composite structure consisting of FCC solid solution and TiC particles. At the same time, the alloy ’ s micro -
structure is transformed from a single columnar crystal structure to a composite structure, which includes 
columnar dendrites, cellular dendrites and unmelted TiC particles. Notably, this transformation has significantly 
improved mechanical properties: the yield strength increased from 203 MPa to 254 MPa (improved 25.1 
percent), and the tensile strength increased from 542 MPa to 635 MPa (improved 17.2 percent). Theoretical 
calculations and practical test analyses reveal that addition TiC ceramic particles leads to a significant increase in 
yield strength, which is mainly attributed to the combined contribution of the Hall-Petch strengthening effect, 
the dislocation strengthening mechanism and the Orowan strengthening effect. In addition, it is demonstrated 
that high-entropy alloy composites ’ friction and wear performance at room temperature exhibits excellent 
characteristics compared to high-entropy alloy materials. It is expected to be widely applied in the 
manufacturing of components in key areas of the aerospace field.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys are regarded as one of the most promising ma -
terials of the 21st century, owing to their remarkable strength, tough -
ness, thermal stability, and wear resistance in a wide range of extreme 
environments [1,2] . Notably, CoCrFeNi as one of the typical FCC HEAs, 
it has been widely investigated due to its good plasticity at low tem -
peratures [3] . However, there are still issues with the CoCrFeNi series of 
high-entropy alloys, such as their comparatively low yield strength, 
which restricts their use in industry [4,5] . Some researchers have 
attempted to address this issue by adding metal components with 
greater atomic radii into the alloys [6] . The findings indicate that the 
incorporation of these elements not only induces a certain degree of 
lattice distortion but also facilitates the phase transition from a single 
face-centered cubic (FCC) structure to a combination of both face- 
centered cubic (FCC) and body-centered cubic (BCC) structures [7,8] . 
This transformation increases the dislocation movement resistance, 
thereby improving the alloy ’ s strength [9] . In addition, the cost of 
powders is a significant factor influencing industrial applications 
[10,11] . Ceramic powders are relatively inexpensive; another 
strengthening approach is incorporating a second phase (such as car -
bides, borides, or oxides) into the high-entropy alloys to form high- 
entropy alloy composites (HEACs) [12] .
There are various traditional methods of manufacturing high entropy 
alloy composites and common conventional manufacturing methods, 
including mechanical alloying, powder metallurgy and casting [13,14] .

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Phase and microstructure analysis
Fig. 4 shows the X-ray diffraction of the high entropy alloy (T0) and 
its composites (T5) fabricated by laser melting deposition fabrication. 
Among them, the high-entropy alloy (T0) exhibits a single FCC- 
structured solid solution structure, which is consistent with previous 
studies [1] . In contrast, additional diffraction peaks corresponding to 
the TiC phase appeared in the high entropy alloy composites (T5) 
fabricated by laser melting deposition fabrication, and the finding is 
verified by XRD. Fig. 4 b is the enlargement of the XRD curve of T5 
sample to enhance the visibility of the TiC peaks. This phenomenon 
indicates that TiC is stabilized in its original crystal structure and the TiC 
ceramic particles are not completely dissolved in the matrix.
Fig. 5 shows the low magnification optical microscopy (OM) of 
unetched high entropy alloy (T0) and high entropy alloy composites 
(T5). As can be seen in Fig. 5 , the T0 sample exhibits some tiny holes, 
while the T5 sample exhibits unmelted second-phase TiC particles 
without holes. The reason for this phenomenon may be that the energy 
density is insufficient during the laser melting deposition manufacturing 
of the T0 sample, resulting in poor fluidity of the molten pool, which is 
unable to effectively fill the pores, thus forming air holes. In the T5 
sample, due to the addition of TiC ceramic particles, the fluidity and 
filling properties of the powders are changed, the density of the material 
is increased, and consequently, the formation of air holes is reduced 
[30] . Further magnified observation by scanning electron microscopy 
(SEM) clearly reveals that TiC particles are diffusely distributed within 
the high-entropy alloy matrix. In addition, SEM/EDS mapping can 
further confirm the uniformity of elemental distribution in high-entropy 
alloy composites.
The etched HEA and HEACS samples are observed by optical mi -
croscopy (OM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to further study 
the microstructure, as illustrated in Fig. 6 . As can be seen from Fig. 6 (a, 
c), the high-entropy alloy(T0) sample is fabricated by laser melting 
deposition, which has a coarse columnar crystal with an obvious di -
rection. Fig. 6 (b, d) shows that the high-entropy alloy composites sample 
(T5) fabricated by laser melting deposition technique mainly consists of 
columnar dendrites, cellular dendrites, and a small amount of unmelted 
TiC particles [31,32] . The white dashed line in Fig. 6 (d) marks the melt 
pool grain boundaries and epitaxially grown columnar dendrites can be 
observed on the right side of the melt pool grain boundaries, whereas the 
cellular dendrites are presented on the left side of the melt pool. Inter -
estingly, the cellular dendrites are predominantly distributed around the 
unmelted TiC ceramic particles [25] . Previous studies [28,33,34] have 
shown that the interfacial energy between the TiC ceramic particles and 
the high-entropy alloy matrix is much lower than the grain boundary 
energy within the high-entropy alloy, which provides favorable condi -
tions for the nucleation of grains. On the other hand, the TiC ceramic 
particles have extremely high thermal conductivity characteristics, 
increasing the temperature gradient of the molten pool around them, 
inhibiting the growth of dendrites, and thus forming a cellular structure. 
It is well known that there are two primary factors that affect the size 
and shape of the grains: the temperature gradient (G) and the growth 
rate (R) [26] . Generally, a larger temperature gradient (G) combined 
with a lower growth rate (R) facilitates the development of columnar 
crystals. In contrast, a smaller temperature gradient (G) along with a 
higher growth rate (R) encourages the formation of equiaxed crystals. 
The addition of TiC increases the laser absorption of the high-entropy 
alloy composites, which decreases the melt pool ’ s temperature 
gradient (G). On the other hand, it provides a large number of nucleation 
sites for grain growth and hinders the continuous growth of grains. The 
pinning effect of TiC particles on grain boundaries inhibits the move -
ment of grain boundaries, thus effectively refining the grains. This cor -
responds to an increase in the growth rate (R). As the ratio of G/R 
decreases, the microstructure of high-entropy alloy composites gradu -
ally transitions from columnar crystals to columnar dendrites and 
cellular dendrites, which further enhances the properties of the material 
[2] . The details of these phenomena will be further explored in the 
subsequent EBSD analyses.
Fig. 7 shows the band contrast map, phase distribution maps, and 
grain size maps of the T0 and T5 samples fabricated by laser melting 
deposition. By comparing the phase distribution diagrams of T0 and T5, 
it is evident that the T0 sample exhibits a single FCC solid solution 
structure. In contrast, the T5 sample is a composite structure of FCC- 
solid solution structure and TiC, which is consistent with the XRD re -
sults in the previous paper. A higher percentage of high-angle grain 
boundaries in T5 samples (66.2 %) compared to T0 samples (24.6 %), 
this result clearly shows that the introduction of TiC ceramic particles 
significantly contributes to the increase in the proportion of high-angle 
grain boundaries. According to the research of Song et al. [29] , the 
lattice mismatch degree at the interface between the ceramic particles 
and the high-entropy alloy leads to a certain degree of elastic strain near 
the interface. This strain will hinder the directional growth of grains, 
prompt the adjacent grains to release stress in all directions, and thus 
form a large number of high-angle grain boundaries. Further observa -
tions revealed that the average grain sizes of the T0 and T5 samples are 
138 μ m and 57.06 μ m, respectively, indicating that significant grain 
refinement occurred in the T5 sample. The main reasons for grain 
refinement in T5 samples can be attributed to two key factors: cooling 
rate and heterogeneous nucleation. Specifically, addition TiC ceramic 
particles provides additional nucleation sites for the grains, which

4. Conclusions
In this paper, within the scope of employing laser melting deposition 
fabrication technology and focusing on the composition design of non- 
equiatomic HEACS, we have successfully fabricated non-equiatomic 
Fe55Cr25Co10Ni10 HEACs with high strength and high toughness. 
The following conclusions can be drawn: 
(1) 5 wt% TiC/Fe55Cr25Co10Ni10 high-entropy alloy composites 
(HEACs) is successfully fabricated by laser melting deposition 
fabrication.
(2) The T0 sample shows a single FCC-phase solid solution structure 
and columnar crystals with a distinctly preferential orientation. 
In contrast, the T5 sample consists of FCC-phase solid solution 
structure with TiC composite structure. Due to addition TiC 
ceramic particles, the temperature gradient (G) within the melt 
pool and the growth rate (R) of the grains are significantly 
affected. It results in a transformation of morphology from 
columnar crystals to columnar dendrites and cellular dendrites, 
while unmelted TiC particles are also observed within the 
microstructure.
(3) Due to the incorporation of TiC ceramic particles serve as addi -
tional nucleation sites for grains in the melt pool, effectively 
hindering continuous grains growth and eventually leading to 
grain refinement. Additionally, the presence of TiC particles in -
creases the proportion of high-angle grain boundaries ( > 15
◦
) and 
weakens the original (001) preferential orientation, which in turn 
leads to a tendency towards a more dispersed grain growth 
orientation.
(4) The yield strength and tensile strength of the high-entropy alloy 
at room temperature are 203 MPa and 542 MPa, respectively. 
After adding 5 wt% TiC ceramic particles, the yield strength in -
creases to 254 MPa (25.1 % improvement over the TiC-free 
samples), and the tensile strength increases to 635 MPa (17.2 % 
improvement over the TiC-free samples).
(5) The significant increase in yield strength at room temperature is 
attributed to the synergistic effect of multiple strengthening 
mechanisms: Hall-Petch strengthening, dislocation strengthening 
and diffusion strengthening.
(6) The high hardness of TiC ceramic particles leads to better room- 
temperature friction and wear performance of high-entropy alloy 
composites than high-entropy alloys.
(7) The high-entropy alloy composites with the addition of 5 wt% TiC 
exhibits good toughness. Therefore, in the future, the influence of 
higher TiC content on the high-entropy alloy will continue to be 
explored.
