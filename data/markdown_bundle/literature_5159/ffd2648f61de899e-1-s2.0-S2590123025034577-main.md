ABSTRACT
This review critically and systematically analyses recent progress in additively manufactured (AM) Ni-based 
superalloys, mapping processing – microstructure – property linkages relevant to qualification of hot-section 
hardware in aerospace and energy, including turbine blades, combustion chambers, and injectors. Compari -
sons are drawn across laser/electron powder-bed fusion and directed energy deposition. We explain how melt- 
pool dynamics and cooling rate govern grain architecture and boundary character, micro-/macro-texture, micro- 
segregation, and phase evolution-strengthening γ ′ / γ ″ ( γ’ / γ’’ ) and carbides versus deleterious Laves/TCP. Key 
defects, such as porosity, lack of fusion, hot cracking, and residual stress, are linked to the tensile response, 
hardness stability, and creep/fatigue resistance. Practical process windows are identified, and optimization 
strategies are synthesized in terms of power, scan speed, hatch spacing, layer thickness, preheating, shielding, 
and feedstock quality; for illustration, scan speeds on the order of 1100 – 1700 mm/s in L -PBF IN718 shift 
strength/porosity trade-offs. Post-processing routes (stress-relief, solution/aging, HIP) that reduce defects, tailor 
metastable constituents, and restore near-isotropy are summarized. Representative alloys (IN718, IN625, Has -
telloy X) illustrate trade-offs between γ ′ fraction, Nb-rich Laves formation, and build rate. Remaining gaps 
include standardized powder specifications, robust in-situ monitoring with model-informed control, cross- 
platform comparability, and long-duration oxidation/hot-corrosion datasets to support design allowable. Over -
all, the review offers practical guidance for engineering microstructure and properties in AM Ni-based super -
alloys and accelerates industrial qualification for critical service environments.
1. Introduction
Nickel-based superalloys are among the most important classes of 
materials developed for environments involving extreme thermal and 
mechanical stresses. Their widespread use in aerospace, energy, and 
power generation stems from their ability to maintain high mechanical 
strength at elevated temperatures, as well as their outstanding resistance 
to oxidation, hot corrosion, and microstructural degradation. These 
properties make them the material of choice for manufacturing 
advanced components such as turbine blades, combustion chambers, 
and high-pressure injectors [ 1 , 2 ].
Additive Manufacturing, also known as 3D printing, has begun to 
replace traditional methods in certain areas. Mainly when it comes to 
making complex, high-performance alloys like nickel-based superalloys, 
since it builds objects layer by layer, AM gives engineers more design 
freedom and helps produce parts close to their final shape, or near-net- 
shape, which means less material is wasted. It also enables the restora -
tion of damaged components, reducing the need for complete replace -
ment [ 3 – 6 ]. AM has made a lot of progress, but it still has a few problems 
that hold it back. The process isn ’ t always stable, and different parts can 
come out with different results. Sometimes, the material doesn ’ t cool 
evenly, which causes changes in the microstructure. Because of this, 
making parts that are both repeatable and free of defects remains a 
challenge, especially when high reliability is required.

used processes are Directed Energy Deposition (DED) and Powder Bed

The microstructure of nickel-based superalloys made by AM plays a 
key role in how they perform mechanically. Things like how the material 
solidifies, how the precipitates are spread out, the direction of the

common problems such as pores, cracks, or residual stress [ 1 , 2 , 9–13 ].

treatment, preheating, or adjusting things like scan speed or energy 
input. However, getting the same mechanical performance from part to 
part is still not guaranteed, especially where reliability really matters [ 8 ,
13 , 14 ].
This review primarily aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of 
the microstructural characteristics and mechanical behavior of addi -
tively manufactured (AM) nickel-based superalloys, with a focus on the 
influence of processing conditions and post-processing treatments. By 
integrating findings from recent studies, this review highlights key 
microstructural features such as dendritic structures, secondary phase 
precipitation, and defect formation, which significantly influence me -
chanical performance. Additionally, the effect of heat treatment on 
phase transformations and the enhancement of mechanical properties is 
thoroughly examined. Furthermore, this review systematically examines 
the impact of AM processes on the microstructure and mechanical 
properties of Ni-based superalloys. With a particular focus on widely 
used techniques such as powder bed fusion (PBF) and directed energy 
deposition (DED), this review analyzes the challenges in fabricating 
these alloys and evaluates the influence of processing parameters, 
thermal history, and microstructural variations on the mechanical 
behavior of fabricated components.
The novelty of this research lies in offering a comprehensive 
perspective on microstructural variations, including the distribution and 
morphology of interdendritic precipitates, melt pool boundaries (MPBs), 
and the impact of intermetallic phases on mechanical performance. 
Additionally, this study examines the effects of post-processing treat -
ments, such as precipitation hardening and annealing, on microstruc -
tural refinement and the enhancement of mechanical properties, while 
also proposing strategies for optimizing manufacturing parameters and 
heat treatment processes. Furthermore, this study highlights current 
challenges and emerging trends in AM, providing valuable insights for 
future research and industrial applications. By bridging the gap between 
fundamental metallurgical principles and AM technology, this research 
facilitates the development of the next generation of high-performance 
superalloys.
What distinguishes this review is its integrated approach to proc -

the precipitation behaviour of secondary phases, the impact of inter -
metallic phases, and the role of microstructural features such as melt 
pool boundaries. By incorporating findings from recent literature, the 
review outlines knowledge gaps and emerging strategies, including AI- 
driven optimization and multi-material printing, to guide future 
research and enable industrial application of AM-fabricated Ni-based 
superalloys.

2. Ni-based superalloys additive manufacturing
In the metal additive manufacturing of nickel-based superalloys, two 
main process families are widely applied in industrial settings: Powder 
Bed Fusion (PBF) and Directed Energy Deposition (DED). According to 
ISO/ASTM classifications, PBF is further divided into laser-based (PBF- 
LB/M) and electron-beam-based (PBF-EB/M) methods, while DED is 
categorized based on both energy source and feedstock type (examples 
include DED-LB/P, DED-LB/W, DED-Arc/W, and DED-EB/W). For 
brevity, we discuss the distinction between powder-fed and wire-fed 
DED in the text rather than via multi-panel graphics. Legacy or 
vendor-specific names such as SLM, DMLS, LMF, EBM, and LENS are still 
used in some contexts for cross-reference purposes [ 1 , 15 ]. Fig. 1 illus -
trates the classification of AM technologies, highlighting DED and 
Powder Bed Fusion as the primary categories, each further divided into 
specialized subcategories.
Among PBF techniques, laser-based systems (L-PBF) are known for 
producing near-net-shape parts with high relative density—up to 
approximately 99.99 %—and excellent feature resolution. In contrast, 
electron beam systems (E-PBF) prioritize vacuum stability and high 
preheat conditions over dimensional precision [ 15 ]. Although PBF 
processes have lower deposition rates, they are well-suited for 
small-to-medium parts with strict dimensional tolerances. DED, on the 
other hand, offers faster build rates, which makes it a favorable option 
for larger components or repair applications. Within the DED category, 
laser-based powder-fed systems (DED-LB/P) support efficient mass 
transfer but come with higher powder costs. Wire-fed variants such as 
DED-LB/W, DED-Arc/W, and DED-EB/W are optimized for maximum 
deposition rates but often result in rougher surface finishes, reduced 
dimensional accuracy, and higher residual stress due to the larger melt 
pool size [ 1 , 15 ]. Thermal profiles differ significantly across these 
methods. L -PBF typically shows the highest cooling rates, while DED has 
the lowest, with E-PBF falling in between. These thermal regimes in -
fluence grain size, texture, and the transition between columnar and 
equiaxed growth, governed by the thermal gradient (G), solidification 
rate (R), and scanning strategies [ 16–24 ].
For nickel-based superalloys, this translates into finer grains and 
strong epitaxial texture in L -PBF, coarser microstructures in DED 
(especially with preheating), and intermediate dendritic spacing in E- 
PBF. As a result, process parameters play a critical role in controlling 
anisotropy and determining crack paths [ 16–18 , 20 , 22–27 ]. Defect for -
mation is strongly linked to energy input. Low volumetric energy density 
often results in lack-of-fusion porosity, while excessive input can cause 
keyhole porosity. Parameters like laser power, scanning speed, and 
hatch spacing significantly influence part density and melt pool 
behavior [ 28–30 ]. Although optimizing these inputs can reduce inter -
layer porosity, intralayer defects may persist and usually require addi -
tional post-processing [ 15 , 28 ].
In addition to process-induced defects, material-specific challenges 
further limit the process window for Ni-superalloys. Micro-segregation 
of elements like Nb, Al, and Ti can lead to the formation of brittle phases

3. Melt pool chronicles: microstructural evolution in AM ni- 
superalloys
AM technologies provide complex thermal cycles during the fabri -
cation process, making it challenging to predict the resulting micro -
structure of parts. The presence of metastable and non-equilibrium 
phases, which vary across different regions, contributes to this 
complexity. Two critical parameters, the solidification rate (V) and the 
temperature gradient (G), significantly influence the solidification 
structure [ 51 ]. In AM, the small molten pool volume results in rapid 
solidification (V) and the formation of fine grains, distinguishing it from 
conventional fabrication methods. Various AM parameters such as en -
ergy density, scanning speed, hatch distance, beam size, scan strategy, 
and pre-heat treatment, affect the temperature gradient during fabri -
cation. These variations in G result in microstructural changes, including 
variations in grain structure, phase transformation, grain boundary 
characteristics, micro-segregation, porosity, residual stress, cracking, 
delamination, and surface roughness. Optimizing the AM process for 
Nickel-based superalloys involves adjusting scan parameters and 
pre-heat temperatures to achieve better thermal gradient control. Stra -
tegies to minimize the thermal gradient include reducing the cooling 
rate, modifying the melt pool, and partially re-melting deposited layers 
or tracks. Pre-heating the substrate emerges as an effective technique to 
reduce the thermal gradient between successive layers, leading to longer 
cooling times and decreased residual stress during solidification. 
Fusion-based AM techniques like L -PBF, DED, and E-PBF exhibit a direct 
relationship with refined microstructures in Nickel-based superalloys. 
These techniques commonly produce a columnar-grained structure in 
the build direction and an equiaxed structure in the transverse direction. 
Different scan strategies can result in varying degrees of epitaxial and 
non-epitaxial solidification, affecting texture tendencies. A thorough 
understanding of these phenomena is essential for the successful fabri -
cation of functionally enhanced AM parts. Therefore, a detailed review 
of AM techniques and their impact on microstructural changes is 
essential [ 52 , 53 ]. Table 1 shows a comparison of cooling rate, grain 
structure, mechanical properties, and defects for three methods: L -PBF, 
DED, and E-PBF.

In AM of nickel-based superalloys, the cooling rate significantly 
varies across different techniques due to their distinct thermal charac -
teristics. L -PBF is characterized by extremely high cooling rates, result -
ing from the highly localized and rapid thermal cycles intrinsic to the 
process [ 21 ]. In contrast, DED typically exhibits lower cooling rates, 
attributed to its larger melt pool dimensions and slower solidification 
kinetics [ 20 ]. E-PBF generally presents intermediate cooling rates, 
which are governed by factors such as the thermal conductivity of the 
powder bed and the specific energy-matter interactions induced by the 
electron beam [ 18 , 22 ].
These variations in cooling rates have a pronounced influence on the 
resulting grain structures and microstructural features. The high cooling 
rates in L -PBF typically lead to the formation of fine grain structures. A 
reduction in laser energy input can further refine the grain size, although 
the ultra-high thermal gradients in L -PBF may suppress constitutional 
supercooling, thereby lowering the efficiency of heterogeneous nucle -
ation and promoting the growth of coarse columnar grains in the 
absence of nucleating agents [ 16 , 50 ]. In DED, the inherently lower 
cooling rates promote the development of larger grain structures; for 
example, preheating the substrate further reduces the cooling rate, 
resulting in increased primary cellular arm spacing (PCAS) and coarser 
microstructures [ 20 ]. While the cooling rates in E-PBF are sufficient to 
generate fine dendritic structures, they are generally not as refined as 
those achieved through L -PBF. The primary dendrite arm spacing (PDAS) 
in E-PBF is typically smaller than that observed in conventional casting 
but remains larger than that produced by L -PBF. Furthermore, cooling 
rates play a critical role in solute distribution and segregation behavior. 
The high cooling rates in L -PBF can lead to significant solute segregation 
within the interdendritic regions, promoting the formation of carbide 
phases and increasing the susceptibility to liquation cracking. In 
contrast, the slower cooling rates in DED facilitate more uniform solute 
redistribution, thereby reducing the extent of micro-segregation relative 
to L -PBF. E-PBF, with its moderate cooling conditions, enables controlled 
micro-segregation through optimized thermal management strategies, 
typically resulting in less pronounced segregation than in L -PBF [ 17 , 19 ,
21 – 24 ].

3.6. Porosity puzzles: defects from process to performance
Porosity is a common defect in the DED process, arising primarily 
from three sources. Keyholes form due to localized vaporization caused 
by high energy density, often appearing as large circular or elongated 
voids. Keyholes are typically formed due to excessive laser energy dur -
ing the AM process, which creates deep and narrow cavities in the melt 
pool. These cavities can trap gas and solidify, leading to irregularly 
shaped pores. Keyhole pores are characterized by their irregular shapes, 
often resembling elongated or irregular cavities. These pores can 
significantly deteriorate the mechanical properties of the material, 
including its fatigue strength, stiffness, and fracture toughness. They are 
often found in the interior bulk material and between deposited layers, 
which can critically affect the structural integrity of the component. Gas 
pores are formed when gas gets trapped in the melt pool during solidi -
fication. This can happen due to insufficient laser energy or improper 
shielding gas flow, leading to the entrapment of gas bubbles. Gas pores 
are generally spherical and uniform in shape, which distinguishes them 
from the irregularly shaped keyhole pores. While gas pores also nega -
tively impact the mechanical properties, their effect is somewhat 
different. They primarily reduce the ductility and can lead to premature 
failure under tensile stress, especially when aligned with the build di -
rection. Gas pores are often found distributed throughout the material, 
including at the surface and sub-surface levels. Keyholes and gas pores 
are two distinct types of porosity defects in Ni-based superalloys fabri -
cated by AM. Keyholes are irregularly shaped and formed due to 
excessive laser energy, significantly affecting the material ’ s mechanical 
properties by reducing fatigue strength and stiffness. In contrast, gas 
pores are spherical and result from gas entrapment, primarily impacting 
ductility and causing premature failure under tensile stress. Under -
standing these differences is crucial for optimizing AM processes to 
minimize defects and enhance the performance of Ni-based superalloys 
[ 93 , 170 – 172 ].

3.7. Locked-in stresses: origins and outcomes of residual stress
Residual stress is an inherent characteristic of AM technology, 
arising from rapid solidification and thermal cycling [ 186 ]. In thermo -
mechanical manufacturing processes, particularly in AM-DED, residual 
stresses are inevitable. DED processes, which operate layer-by-layer, 
subject materials to a complex thermal history involving melting, 
remelting, and reheating. AM, characterized by non-equilibrium pro -
cessing, exhibits rapid cooling rates and significant thermal gradients, 
resulting in intricate phase transformations and microstructural 
changes. Residual stresses induced by AM are spatially non-uniform and 
exhibit steep gradients in both build directions. The evolution of residual 
stresses in AM is influenced by key physical factors [ 28 ].
Two primary factors contribute to the occurrence of residual stress: 
(i) high solidification rates, which result in micro-strains and crystallo -
graphic texture [ 187 ], and (ii) the thermal history of the AM process. 
Engineers and manufacturers devote considerable effort to controlling 
residual stress due to its substantial impact on the quality and density of 
AM components. Insufficient control of residual stress can lead to defects 
such as cracking and delamination. Proper selection of AM parameters 
such as energy input, scanning vector and strategy, layer thickness, and 
preheating the substrate, is crucial for mitigating residual stress. For 
example, reducing layer thickness and optimizing heat input can effec -
tively lower residual stress levels [ 11 ].
Residual stresses in AM are categorized into three types based on 
their scale and effects, ranging from macro-stresses (Type I) to atomic- 
scale stresses (Type III). The complexity and non-uniform distribution 
of residual stresses in DED highlight the significance of understanding 
and managing these factors throughout the manufacturing process. The 
consequences of residual stress in AM include driven-phase trans -
formation, distortion, loss of geometric tolerance, cracking, delamina -
tion from the substrate, early crack propagation under cyclic loading, 
and ultimately, premature failure of structural components [ 28 ]. These 
factors significantly affect the mechanical integrity of AM components, 
including fatigue performance [ 12 ], fracture toughness, crack growth 
behavior, and corrosion resistance. Consequently, reducing or miti -
gating residual stress has emerged as a key focus in the field to ensure 
the integrity and reliability of the manufactured materials and parts 
[ 186 ].
Residual stress plays a crucial role as both a driving force and 
nucleation site in the recrystallization phenomenon, contributing to a 
heterogeneous microstructure [ 188 ]. For example, the distribution of 
residual stress in overlapping regions serves as a driving force for 
recrystallization, resulting in the formation of a finer microstructure 
compared to other areas. In AM, thermal residual stress influences two 
types of cracks: liquation and solidification cracks. Thermal stress con -
centrates in the inter-dendritic regions during the liquid state (blocked 
by dendrite arms) and during the final solidification stages, leading to 
solidification cracks in these regions on deposited layers. However, 
appropriate post-processing treatments effectively remove solidification 
cracks after AM. On the other hand, liquation cracks occur in the 
heat-affected zone (HAZ) due to the presence of a low melting point 
eutectic layer, such as brittle Laves phase precipitation along grain 
boundaries under low cooling rates. The susceptibility of liquation 
cracks depends on cooling rate and grain size. A slower cooling rate 
leads to larger grains, stabilizing the brittle Laves phase and promoting 
liquation cracks at the bulk scale. Unfortunately, liquation cracks are 
challenging to remove due to their location in the bulk material. To 
reduce liquation crack susceptibility, strategies such as increasing the 
cooling rate and preventing micro-segregation and Laves phase forma -
tion to promote a finer microstructure are effective [ 189 , 190 ]. Residual 
stress exhibits a significant correlation with physical metallurgical fac -
tors such as thermal expansion [ 191 ]. Additionally, preceding layers 
contribute to residual stress generation in subsequent layers [ 192 ]. 
Elevated levels of residual stress increase the yield strength of the ma -
terial, resulting in layer deformation and suppressing the ultimate ten -
sile stress, which may cause distortion or even crack defects (crack 
initiation) [ 11 ].
Practical techniques for measuring residual stress include displace -
ment/distortion, lattice spacing, or sound speed, which enable accurate 
calculations. Both destructive and non-destructive techniques are uti -
lized for this purpose. Destructive methods, such as hole drilling, serial 
sectioning, and ring-core drilling induce mechanical stress relaxation to 
measure residual stress. Non-destructive methods utilize measurements 
of lattice spacing (using diffraction techniques), speed of sound, or 
Barkhausen noise. Most measurement approaches rely on certain as -
sumptions, making validation of these assumptions for the specific 
component of interest an important part of non-destructive methods to 
improve the accuracy of residual stress measurement. Furthermore, 
computational models based on the numerical solution of thermal-stress 
equilibrium equations are frequently employed to describe and under -
stand the complex dynamics of residual stresses in materials and com -
ponents [ 28 ].
Various techniques are employed to measure residual stress, 
including X-ray methods, neutron diffraction, layer removal methods, 
and Vickers indentation. Among these, the Vickers indentation method 
is widely favored due to its rapid measurement capability. In Fig. 15 , the

3.8. Cracks and gaps: delamination and defect initiation
Cracking and delamination are common issues in AM due to its 
layered construction process. These problems are exacerbated by ther -
mal stresses arising from the rapid heating and cooling cycles charac -
teristic of AM. Delamination, known as the separation between 
consecutive layers or between the deposited layers and the baseplate, is 
stimulated by the residual stress present in the interlayer regions. This 
phenomenon leads to the material exceeding its yield strength. Factors 
contributing to delamination include the presence of un-melted or 
partially melted powder and inadequate remelting of layers beneath the 
melt pool. Additionally, the significant residual stress distribution at the 
interface between the build and the baseplate further heightens the risk 
of delamination.
The widespread industrial adoption of metal AM is constrained by 
defects, particularly cracking in parts fabricated through AM. Metals and 
alloys that are prone to cracking during fusion welding are similarly 
susceptible to cracking during AM processing. The primary types of 
cracking observed in AM-fabricated parts include: (i) Solidification 
cracking, also known as hot cracking, which predominantly occurs along 
grain boundaries during the solidification process. High tensile stresses 
develop due to the higher contraction of the hotter upper layers 
compared to the cooler underlying layers or baseplate. Factors such as 
the applied energy and the solidification characteristics of the material 
contribute to the likelihood of solidification cracking. (ii) Liquation 
cracking, this type of cracking has more potential in the ’ mushy ’ or 
partially melted zone (PMZ) of the build. It is driven by the melting of 
grain-boundary precipitates during rapid heating below the liquidus 
temperature, which leads to the formation of tensile stresses during 
cooling. Factors such as the temperature difference between the solidus 
and liquidus, solidification shrinkage, and thermal contraction can in -
crease susceptibility to liquation cracking. (iii) Ductility dip cracking is 
more pronounced in alloys with an FCC structure and observed as a form 
of solid-state intergranular cracking at elevated temperatures. This type 
of cracking results in reduced ductility and degradation of both static 
and dynamic mechanical properties, leading to premature failure and a 
reduction in corrosion resistance of the fabricated parts. Crack and 
delamination detection can be achieved through various methods, 
including destructive techniques such as metallographic cross-sections 
and SEM fractography, as well as non-destructive testing methods like 
magnetic particle testing, radiographic testing, laser-computed tomog -
raphy (L-CT), and ultrasonic testing, along with computational 
modeling. Removing or mitigating cracking and delamination in AM 
parts requires multiple features such as process optimization measures, 
including substrate and chamber preheating, controlling cooling rates, 
optimizing aspect ratios and wall thicknesses, refining scan strategies, 
adjusting part orientation within the build, and ensuring compatibility 
of materials when printing multi-materials together. Reducing residual 
stress is also critical for mitigating cracks and delamination in the AM 
process. Understanding, controlling, and mitigating these types of 
cracking are crucial for overcoming challenges in metal AM and 
broadening its industrial applications [ 1 ].

3.9. Surface reality: the roughness factor in AM components
Surface roughness in AM is influenced by various factors, including 
processing and post-processing conditions, material feedstock, and part 
design. Variations in these factors can significantly impact surface 
roughness, which affects the mechanical properties and dimensional 
accuracy of deposited materials and parts. Specifically, increased sur -
face roughness can lead to reduced fatigue properties and deviations 
from desired dimensions and geometric tolerances. To mitigate surface 
roughness, several strategies can be implemented. Increasing heat input 
through higher laser power and lower scanning speed can facilitate 
smoother surfaces. Additionally, using smaller layer thicknesses and 
finer powder particles can help in a better surface finish. Post-processing 
treatments such as HIP and chemical or electrochemical polishing are 
practical methods to improve surface quality. Measuring surface 
roughness can be carried out using various analytical techniques such as 
contact methods (atomic force microscopy (AFM)) or non-contact 
methods (confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) or white light 
interferometry) profilometry, and SEM [ 28 ]. Therefore, controlling the 
quality of surface roughness is considered an important aspect of the AM 
process, particularly in the DED-AM technique. Achieving the required 
tolerances and surface quality often necessitates controlling AM pa -
rameters such as controlling layer thickness, and refining powder 
characteristics, complementary post-processing, including machining or 
polishing. Various factors influence the surface quality of printed parts, 
such as sticking to the surface of partially melted powder particles, 
forming inclined or curved surfaces (stair-stepping effects), and 
splashing of molten material (balling effect).
The relationship between scanning speed and surface roughness in 
nickel-based superalloys fabricated by additive AM is complex and 
influenced by various factors. Increasing scanning speed generally leads 
to a decrease in surface roughness up to a certain point, after which the 
roughness may increase again. This trend is observed in multiple studies 
[ 197 – 200 ]. Specific optimal scanning speeds can significantly reduce 
surface roughness. For instance, a scanning speed of 0.1 m/s combined 
with a power of 220 W resulted in the lowest surface roughness of 3.1 μ m 
in one study [ 201 ]. Another study found that a scanning speed of 7000 
mm/s with an energy density of 0.88 J/cm² achieved the lowest 
roughness value of 0.171 μ m [ 199 ]. Laser polishing parameters, 
including scanning speed, play a crucial role in achieving smoother 
surfaces. For example, changing the scanning speed during laser pol -
ishing can reduce roughness from 0.702 μ m to 0.354 μ m [ 197 ]. The 
contour processing parameters, including scanning speed, influence the 
melt pool shape and surface roughness. Stable melt pools with optimized 
scanning speeds lead to reduced surface roughness [ 202 ]. Dual-contours 
scanning strategies and other methods that adjust scanning speed and 
power can optimize surface quality. However, high heat input from 
increased scanning speeds can introduce defects like keyhole-induced 
holes [ 201 ]. The relationship between scanning speed and surface 
roughness in Ni-based superalloys fabricated by AM is influenced by 
multiple factors, including laser power, energy density, and specific 
contour processing strategies. Optimal scanning speeds can significantly 
reduce surface roughness, but excessive speeds may introduce defects. 
Understanding and optimizing these parameters are crucial for 
improving the surface quality of AM-fabricated components [ 197 – 202 ]. 
Variations in scanning speed during laser polishing significantly impact 
the surface roughness of Ni-based superalloys. For instance, in the pol -
ishing of Inconel 718, a decrease in scanning speed initially reduces 
surface roughness, achieving a minimum Ra of 0.369 µ m [ 200 ]. Simi -
larly, for DZ411 superalloy, the roughness decreases first and then 
increases with increasing scanning speed, with the optimal roughness 
achieved at a scanning speed of 400 mm/s [ 197 , 198 ]. In laser milling of 
DZ411, the surface roughness decreases with increasing scanning speed, 
but milling efficiency first increases and then decreases [ 203 ]. This in -
dicates a complex relationship where optimal scanning speed must 
balance both surface quality and processing efficiency. The surface 
morphology of additively manufactured Ni-based superalloys shows 
significant variations with scanning speed. High scanning speeds can 
lead to uneven undulating surfaces due to overlapping cladding tracks, 
while lower speeds improve surface leveling [ 204 ]. In SLM processes, 
increasing scanning speed slightly reduces the cooling rate and tem -
perature gradient of the molten pools, affecting the microstructural 
evolution and surface quality [ 205 ]. Optimal scanning speeds are crucial 
for achieving high surface quality and minimizing defects. Machining 
studies on Inconel 625 reveal that lower cutting speeds result in better 
surface quality with fewer defects, while higher speeds cause significant 
periodic fluctuations in cutting forces and surface imperfections [ 206 ]. 
In the fabrication of Inconel 718 using powder bed fusion, scanning 
speed variations impact surface roughness and microhardness. Optimal 
conditions (e.g. scanning speed of 800 mm/s) result in lower surface 
roughness (2.82 µ m ) and higher microhardness (416 HV) [ 207 ]. 
Optimal scanning speeds can significantly enhance surface quality by 
reducing roughness and improving morphology. However, the rela -
tionship between scanning speed and surface quality is complex and 
requires careful optimization to balance efficiency and quality [ 197 , 198 ,
200 , 203 , 207 ]. Balling, spatter redeposition, and stair-stepping set 
as-built roughness and drive knock-downs in fatigue and corrosion for 
Ni-based superalloys. Parameter optimization (scan speed, hatch, con -
tour energy), together with selective finishing (machining, blasting, 
chemical/electropolishing), delivers surface states aligned with service 
requirements while protecting γ ′ / γ ″ integrity near the surface.

5. Perspective: bridging processing, microstructure, and 
performance
Microstructural characteristics of Ni-based superalloys are a critical 
aspect in determining their durability and suitability for both low- and 
high-temperature applications. These alloys possess exceptional me -
chanical properties, making them highly attractive from both engi -
neering and academic perspectives. However, several challenges must 
be addressed to optimize their utilization in AM. Potential solutions to 
these challenges include: 
1. Artificial intelligence integration: Employing artificial intelligence 
(AI) to integrate AM parameters can help achieve the desired me -
chanical properties and microstructure characteristics of produced 
parts. AI enables the optimization and control of the manufacturing 
process, leading to improved outcomes.
2. Modeling Molten Pool Geometry: Accurately modeling the flow 
behavior of the molten pool and thermal actions during laser clad -
ding remains a significant challenge. Utilizing non-contact moni -
toring devices like coaxial cameras, paraxial high-speed cameras, or 
paraxial infrared cameras can enhance the modeling process.
3. Microstructure and Material Behavior: Investigating the interre -
lationship between microstructural characteristics and material 
behavior at both cryogenic and high temperatures is essential for 
understanding and improving the performance of Ni-based 
superalloys.
4. Innovative AM Techniques: Applying novel approaches, such as 
innovative scan strategies, beam profiles, and AI for online process 
monitoring, can mitigate process-induced defects. These techniques 
help correlate AM parameters with microstructural features, thereby 
achieving the desired mechanical properties in printed parts.
5. Inter-Atomic Perspective: Utilizing an inter-atomic viewpoint on 
microstructure characteristics can provide better control over ma -
terial behavior.
6. Residual Stress Measurement: Measuring residual stresses in DED 
parts accurately presents a challenge due to the need to balance time, 
cost, and accuracy. This trade-off often limits residual stress studies 
to a few samples, hindering the establishment of statistically signif -
icant differences. Addressing these challenges will contribute to a 
more comprehensive and applicable understanding of residual 
stresses in diverse materials and advance the optimization of DED 
manufacturing processes.
7. Heat Treatment Design: Microstructural features and process- 
induced defects are crucial aspects of AM processes. The design of 
heat treatment (HT) significantly impacts the mechanical properties 
of the alloy. Therefore, a carefully customized HT schedule is needed 
to achieve the proper microstructure and mechanical properties in 
AM Nickel-based superalloys.
AM of nickel-based superalloys has emerged as a rapidly developing 
field due to its capability to produce components with complex geom -
etries and excellent high-temperature performance, yet challenges such 
as solidification cracking, porosity, residual stresses, and microstruc -
tural heterogeneity still limit widespread industrial adoption. Looking 
ahead, artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are ex -
pected to play a central role in overcoming these barriers. On the process 
side, deep learning algorithms can analyze large in-situ monitoring 
datasets (e.g., high-speed imaging, optical and thermal signals) to 
enable real-time defect detection and quality prediction. Data-driven 
models also offer a pathway to accelerate process parameter optimiza -
tion beyond traditional trial-and-error approaches. Moreover, the inte -
gration of thermo-mechanical simulations and CALPHAD-based 
predictions with AI frameworks could guide the design of next- 
generation “ AM-friendly ” alloys. Ultimately, AI-enabled decision-sup -
port systems combined with standardized qualification protocols may 
pave the way toward robust, reliable, and industrial-scale adoption of 
additive manufacturing for critical nickel-based superalloy components.
6. Conclusion
In conclusion, the microstructural characteristics of AM components 
are intrinsically influenced by parameters such as solidification rate (V) 
and temperature gradient (G), which are governed by critical process 
variables, including electron/laser power, scanning speed, hatch 
spacing, and powder or wire feed rate. Key microstructural features that 
significantly impact the performance of AM parts include: 
(i) Grain structure: AM components exhibit diverse grain structures, 
including planar, cellular, dendritic, columnar dendritic, and equi -
axed dendritic morphologies. Post-processing treatments, particu -
larly solution heat treatment, have the capability to alter grain 
morphology significantly, transforming the as-built epitaxial 
columnar grains into equiaxed grains, thus optimizing the material 
properties.
(ii) Secondary phase and precipitates: The formation of secondary 
phases or precipitates within the as-built microstructure is highly 
dependent on the alloy ’ s thermal history and chemical composition. 
The characteristics of these precipitates are governed by AM pro -
cessing parameters and post-processing treatments, influencing the 
dendritic grain structure and layer sequence. The distribution and 
nature of these multiple phases play a crucial role in determining the 
mechanical properties and performance of the final component.

(iii) Grain boundary characteristics: Grain boundaries in the as-built 
microstructure can exhibit features such as serrations, specific 
misorientation angles (e.g., high-angle grain boundaries and low- 
angle grain boundaries), and defined curvature. These characteris -
tics can influence the mechanical properties and thermal stability of 
the material, necessitating careful control during processing and 
post-processing.
(iv) Texture: The crystallographic texture, which refers to the 
preferred orientation of grains, is a critical factor in determining 
recrystallization behavior, grain growth kinetics, and the anisotropic 
mechanical properties of AM parts. The dominance of the 〈 100 〉
texture in as-built microstructure, with its lower storage energy 
compared to 〈 111 〉 , 〈 112 〉 , and 〈 110 〉 orientations (E110 > E111 >
E112 > E100), leads to reduced driving force for recrystallization 
and enhanced thermal stability. Controlling grain orientation is 
essential for tailoring mechanical properties in specific directions.
(v) Micro-segregation: The rapid solidification inherent in AM pro -
cesses induces micro-segregation, where limited atomic diffusion 
leads to solute trapping and non-uniform elemental distribution. 
Increasing the solution temperature can mitigate micro-segregation, 
promoting a homogenized microstructure with uniform elemental 
distribution.
(vi) Porosity: The presence of porosity, characterized by voids or 
pores, serves as stress concentrators that significantly deteriorate 
mechanical properties, particularly fatigue life, fracture toughness, 
and ductility. Advanced techniques such as optimizing powder 
flowability, employing pre-heat treatment, and using hot isostatic 
pressing (HIP) are essential for minimizing, controlling, and elimi -
nating porosity, thereby improving the overall integrity and perfor -
mance of AM parts.
(vii) Residual stress: Residual stress, an inherent characteristic of AM 
due to rapid solidification and thermal cycling, significantly impacts 
the mechanical properties of AM components, including fracture 
toughness, fatigue performance, crack growth behavior, and corro -
sion resistance. Comprehensive characterization and management of 
residual stress are important for ensuring the reliability and dura -
bility of AM parts in demanding applications.
Overall, a thorough understanding and control of these microstruc -
tural characteristics and their associated challenges are crucial to 
advancing high-quality Ni-based superalloy components. Such compo -
nents exhibit enhanced mechanical properties and thermal stability, 
positioning them for success in high-performance and critical 
applications.
