ABSTRACT

alloys from conventional systems to complex concentrated alloys (CCAs). The dual-phase control in Ni/Co/Fe-

1. Introduction
High-temperature alloys are commonly called superalloys. They 
exhibit exceptional strength, resist creep and fatigue, and withstand 
oxidation and corrosion. These properties make them indispensable in 
aerospace, power generation, and chemical processing [ 1 – 3 ]. The field 
of high-temperature alloys has undergone a transformative evolution 
since the late 20th century, progressing from conventional 
single-element-dominated systems ( e.g. , Ni-based superalloys) to com -
plex multi-principal component alloys, with high-/medium-entropy al -
loys (HEAs/MEAs) representing a critical subset of this complex 
concentrated alloys (CCA) classification [ 4 – 7 ]. This evolution represents 
a paradigm shift in alloy design, with a focus on multi-principal element 
systems that exhibit unique microstructures and superior performance 
under extreme operating conditions.
In aerospace applications, particularly in hot-end components of 
aero-engines — such as turbine blades, guide vanes, turbine disks, and 
combustion chambers — materials are exposed to extreme temperatures 
and mechanical stresses. These conditions make material performance 
critical to the safety and efficiency of aerospace vehicles [ 8 , 9 ]. Similarly, 
industrial gas turbines and aircraft structural components demand alloys 
that can maintain high strength and stability in environments exceeding 
600 
◦
C. [ 10 – 12 ]. The performance of these materials directly impacts 
the reliability of engines, influencing overall vehicle safety and effi -
ciency [ 13 , 14 ]. Advancements in high-temperature alloys are pivotal to 
advancing aerospace technology and enabling the industry to meet 
growing demands [ 15 – 17 ]. The development of high-performance ma -
terials for such demanding applications remains a key area of research.

2. Strengthening mechanisms
2.1. Solid solution strengthening
When alloying elements are dissolved in the matrix of high- 
temperature alloys, the interatomic bonding strength is enhanced. 
Particularly, alloying elements with large absolute difference of atomic 
radius between the alloying elements and the matrix can cause lattice 
distortion in the matrix, thereby generating distortion stress and altering 
the mismatch between the matrix and the second phase [ 18 ]. Such 
changes modify the elastic strain energy between atoms, thereby hin -
dering dislocation motion and achieving the goal of increasing alloy 
strength [ 19 ]. The main factors affecting the solid solution strength -
ening effect include the differences in atomic size and elastic modulus 
between solute and solvent atoms, electrostatic interactions, chemical 
interactions, and the ability of short-range ordered atomic distribution.
As the service temperature increases, the solid solution strength -
ening effect typically diminishes. This is attributed to the enhanced 
atomic diffusion capability at high temperatures, which leads to a 
reduction in the strain energy generated by lattice distortion and the 
effect of atomic non-uniform distribution. The strength of high- 
temperature alloys at service temperature differs from that at room 
temperature and is more dependent on atomic diffusion capability. At 
this juncture, solid solution strengthening elements that can enhance 
interatomic bonding strength and reduce the diffusion coefficient will 
produce a more pronounced strengthening effect. Refractory alloying 
elements generally demonstrate enhanced high-temperature strength -
ening efficacy [ 21 ], a trend corroborated by high-throughput density

and matrix phases. Optimizing the precipitate size (0.2 – 0.6 μ m) and

mechanical performance [ 22 , 23 ]. Alloying elements such as Al, Ti, Ta,

fraction of second-phase particles, while refractory elements (Re, Ru, 
Mo, W) enhance solid-solution strengthening within the matrix. How -

brittle TCP phases or degrade environmental resistance [ 22 – 24 ].

example, elements such as Ta and Nb can increase lattice misfit, thereby 
enhancing resistance to dislocation climb. In contrast, Co-based alloys, 
characterized by low stacking fault energy, exhibit distinct 
high-temperature deformation mechanisms [ 22 , 25 ]. Maintaining the

temperatures may induce phase decomposition ( e.g. , the metastability of

elemental doping, such as Ni or Ta, to extend the stability window while 
balancing phase stability and processability [ 22 , 25 ].
A prime example of precipitation strengthening is the γ ′ phase in Ni- 
based superalloys. The finely dispersed γ ′ precipitates enhance the al -
loy ’ s high-temperature strength through mechanisms such as coherent 
strain strengthening, dislocation cutting, and the Orowan bypass 
mechanism. In coherent strain strengthening, lattice mismatch between 
the γ ′ phase and the γ matrix generates an elastic stress field that hinders 
dislocation motion, thereby increasing yield strength. However, exces -
sive lattice mismatch can destabilize the γ ′ phase at high temperatures, 
whereas controlled reduction of mismatch improves long-term thermal 
stability.
In the dislocation cutting mechanism, when the γ ′ phase exhibits low 
strength, dislocations can readily shear through it. The formation of 
antiphase boundaries and stacking faults impedes the movement of 
subsequent dislocations, enhancing strengthening effects. Strategic 
alloying to increase antiphase boundary energy effectively strengthens 
the matrix, while larger cubic γ ′ precipitates further optimize resistance 
to dislocation cutting. In Orowan bypass-dominated deformation, dis -
locations loop around γ ′ precipitates, leaving behind residual defects 
that increase slip resistance. This effect is particularly pronounced when 
matrix channel widths exceed a critical threshold, as wider channels 
reduce dislocation annihilation and promote sustained strengthening. 
Maximizing precipitation strengthening requires precise control over 
the γ ′ phase volume fraction, spatial distribution, and morphology [ 27 ]. 
As shown in Fig. 3 , Chen et al . [ 26 ] examined the impact of precipitate 
size distribution and spatial arrangement on the mechanical properties 
of Ni-based superalloys. Their findings indicate that as the standard 
deviation of precipitate size increases, the critical resolved shear stress 
(CRSS) — defined as the shear stress at which nonlinear deformation 
initiates — first rises and then declines, as shown in Fig. 3 (a – c). This 
suggests that a moderate variation in second-phase size distribution can 
enhance alloy strength. The underlying mechanism involves an 
increased frequency of smaller second-phase particles while maintaining 
the same average size. As a result, dislocation interactions with these 
precipitates become more frequent, intensifying dislocation bending. 
This increased dislocation curvature requires greater energy for further 
deformation, thereby raising the CRSS ( Fig. 3 (d – i)).
Ongoing research focuses on optimizing the comprehensive perfor -
mance of second-phase stability, matrix strengthening, and environ -
mental resistance through multi-component synergistic design [ 22 – 24 ]. 
By fine-tuning phase composition, morphology, and interfacial proper -
ties, next-generation superalloys can achieve superior mechanical 
properties under extreme service conditions.

Grain boundary strengthening in high-temperature alloys is a unique 
mechanism that differs from conventional grain boundary strength -
ening. Under low-temperature deformation conditions, grain bound -
aries act as obstacles to dislocation motion, thereby strengthening the 
alloy. However, under high-temperature deformation conditions, as the 
temperature increases and the strain rate decreases, the hindering effect 
of grain boundaries on dislocation motion weakens. Dislocation pile-ups 
at grain boundaries interact synergistically with boundary defects, 
inducing stress concentrations that drive boundary sliding and 
migration-key precursors to intergranular crack nucleation [ 29 ]. This 
thermally activated boundary dynamics establishes grain boundaries as 
preferential failure sites under elevated-temperature loading. The 
detrimental role of boundaries in Ni-based superalloys is quantitatively 
demonstrated in Fig. 4 through crystallographic analysis of 
boundary-mediated damage evolution [ 30 ]. The study found that grain 
boundaries can significantly affect the strength and toughness of the 
alloy, especially at high temperatures. The geometry and orientation of 
grain boundaries influence crack initiation and propagation, while the 
mechanical properties of grain boundaries (such as maximum traction 
force and energy release rate) determine the overall mechanical 
behavior of the alloy. Grain boundary engineering through structural 
optimization markedly enhances the elevated-temperature performance 
of metallic alloys. This strengthening mechanism plays a crucial role in 
high-temperature material systems, particularly in Fe-based superalloys, 
where the density and configuration of boundary defects significantly 
influence creep resistance thresholds. This is because Fe undergoes al -
lotropy transformations, has poor stability, and is prone to precipitating 
harmful phases due to the dissolution of excessive alloying elements, 
leading to significant segregation. By purifying grain boundary impu -
rities and controlling the deformation and morphology of precipitates at 
the grain boundaries, grain boundary strengthening can be achieved, 
thereby effectively enhancing the microalloying effect of beneficial el -
ements and increasing the upper limit of the service temperature of 
high-temperature alloys.
The mechanical performance optimization of CCAs can be achieved 
through the synergistic effects of thermomechanical processing and 
alloying strategies [ 31 – 33 ]. Dynamic recrystallization during hot 
deformation effectively refines grains and eliminates casting defects. For 
instance, in CoCrFeMnNi alloys, DRX forms fine-grained structures via 
the necklace mechanism, significantly enhancing both strength and 
plasticity [ 34 ]. Concurrently, the introduction of metalloid elements 
such as Si further strengthens alloy performance: the atomic size 
mismatch of Si induces severe lattice distortion, enhancing solid solution 
strengthening, while its strong bonding tendency with transition metals 
promotes chemical short-range ordering, increasing resistance to dislo -

plasticity effects, thereby improving work-hardening capacity. For

3. High-temperature alloy systems
The design of high-temperature alloys, including the selection 
criteria and proportioning principles of alloying elements, plays a crit -
ical role in their performance in extreme environments. These alloys, 
used across industries such as aerospace, power generation, and chem -
icals, are classified based on their primary alloying elements, each of -
fering distinct advantages. Nickel-based alloys are valued for their 
strength, oxidation resistance, and weldability, making them ideal for 
aerospace and gas turbines. Cobalt-based alloys, known for their 
corrosion resistance, are commonly used in high-stress components like 
turbine blades. Iron-based alloys, including stainless steel, are cost- 
effective and suitable for moderately high-temperature applications. 
Tungsten alloys, with their exceptionally high melting point, are effec -
tive in extreme environments, such as aerospace and nuclear industries. 
Molybdenum alloys provide excellent thermal conductivity and 
strength, often applied in chemical processing and semiconductor in -
dustries. Tantalum alloys, resistant to oxidation and corrosion, are used 
in aerospace, electronics, and chemical sectors. Additionally, CCAs, 
particularly H/MEAs, demonstrate exceptional combinations of 
elevated-temperature strength and parabolic oxidation resistance, 
positioning them as viable candidates for extreme thermomechanical 
service environments. Together, these high-temperature alloy systems 
deliver tailored solutions to meet the stringent performance re -
quirements of modern industries.

3.2. Co alloys
Despite the considerable maturity of Ni-based high-temperature 
alloy technology, deficiencies in corrosion resistance persist, often 
necessitating the application of costly coatings to enhance their 
corrosion-resistant properties. Diverging from conventional systems, Co- 
based superalloys demonstrate enhanced resistance to environmental 
degradation coupled with creep threshold stresses exceeding 300 MPa at 
750 
◦

propulsion systems and high-temperature automotive components [ 49 ].

researchers are reducing the formation of deleterious phases and

elements and optimizing heat treatment processes. The performance of

4.1. Vacuum induction melting
VIM is one of the crucial processes in the smelting of high- 
temperature alloys. Its primary objective is to produce ingots that 
conform to the target composition, thereby providing electrodes for 
subsequent ESR or VAR. VIM employs the principle of electromagnetic 
induction to generate eddy currents within the metal, causing the 
electrode to melt. Owing to its operational environment being under 
vacuum conditions, it effectively prevents contamination of the alloy 
melt by ambient air.
The VIM process can achieve precise control over alloy composition, 
inhibiting the oxidation and burn-off of alloying elements, while 
significantly reducing the content of harmful elements and impurity 
gases within the alloy. Furthermore, VIM can also facilitate the recycling 
and purification of returned materials from high-temperature alloys, 
thereby enhancing the utilization rate of resources [ 109 ]. However, 
there are certain issues associated with the VIM process during smelting. 
For instance, the prolonged contact between the high-temperature melt 
and the refractory materials of the crucible may lead to reactions or 
erosion between the melt and the refractory materials. In addition, in -
gots prepared by VIM may exhibit metallurgical defects such as segre -
gation, porosity, shrinkage cavities, and inclusions [ 107 ]. Therefore, in 
order to further enhance the purity and metallurgical quality of the alloy 
material, subsequent remelting treatment of the induction-cast ingots is 
typically required.
VIM process parameters critically influence alloy composition con -
trol and defect formation. Lapin et al . [ 111 ] demonstrated that Ti- x -
Al-8Nb-3.6C-0.8Mo alloys ( at. %, x = 38, 42, 45) fabricated under 103 Pa 
maintained designed stoichiometry, whereas Al evaporation occurred at 
6.5 Pa. Complementary studies by Zhang et al . [ 110 ] on GH4742 su -
peralloy revealed microstructure gradients and elemental segregation 
(Nb-rich regions) across VIM ingots ( Fig. 7 (a – c)), with cooling rate 
variations (0.5 – 2.5 K/s) governing dendrite arm spacing evolution. 
These heterogeneities culminated in elevated hot-cracking susceptibility 
at ingot centers ( Fig. 7 (d – j)), where secondary dendrite arm spacing 
exceeded 180 μ m. Concurrently, Li et al . [ 112 ] identified yttrium ’ s dual 
purification mechanism in K4169 superalloy: Y additions (0.05 – 0.15 wt 
%) reduced oxide inclusions by 60 – 75 % through reactive scavenging, 
while optimal overheating temperatures (1550 – 1600

elemental volatilization losses ( < 3 %). They found that the addition of Y 
could precipitate Y-rich phases at grain boundaries, which would inhibit 
the diffusion of C and the accumulation of S, thereby purifying the alloy. 
When 0.1 wt % of Y was added, the oxygen and sulfur content of the 
purified K4169 alloy were 3 – 4 ppm and 6 – 11 ppm, respectively, with 
the degree of deoxidation and desulfurization increased to 50 % and 57 
%.
4.2. Electroslag remelting
ESR is a special metallurgical process that utilizes the resistive heat 
generated by the passage of electric current through slag for smelting. 
During this process, the consumable electrode gradually melts under the 
heating effect of the slag pool, forming droplets that subsequently pass 
through the slag pool and enter the metal melt pool. The high temper -
ature of the slag and slag pool, along with the electromagnetic stirring 
during the smelting process, provides favorable thermodynamic and 
kinetic conditions for the thorough contact between the slag and the 
alloy melt. Moreover, the slag layer not only serves to isolate air during 
the remelting process, preventing secondary oxidation and nitrogen 
pickup of the alloy melt, but also acts as an insulating layer, significantly 
reducing the heat loss of the alloy melt. The droplets, as they fall, come 
into full contact, and react with the slag, which can markedly reduce the 
content of impurity elements and inclusions in the alloy.
The water-cooled crystallizer in ESR effectively prevents contami -
nation of the alloy melt by refractory materials. Under the forced water 
cooling of the crystallizer, the alloy melt gradually solidifies, forming an 
ingot from the bottom up. This solidification sequence ensures the dense 
and uniform structure of the solidified microstructure, reducing or 
eliminating defects such as porosity and shrinkage cavities in the ingot. 
Ingots prepared by the ESR process are characterized by dense micro -
structure, absence of shrinkage cavities, and high purity, effectively 
ensuring the stability of the subsequent VAR process, and have become 
one of the main processes for producing high-quality high-temperature 
alloy materials [ 114 , 115 ]. However, ESR also has certain limitations, 
such as low production efficiency, element burn-off during smelting, and 
environmental pollution caused by the volatilization of CaF
2
-containing 
slag [ 116 , 117 ]. Therefore, how to fully utilize the advantages of ESR and 
reduce or avoid the hazards brought by its disadvantages is an important 
direction for future research.

through ESR processing with Nb additions (0.8 – 2.4 wt %), demonstrating

4.4. Powder metallurgy
The preparation of traditional high-temperature alloys primarily 
relies on vacuum melting and casting, depending on solid solution 
strengthening and carbide strengthening to enhance strength. These 
alloys exhibit excellent oxidation resistance. However, their room- 
temperature and high-temperature strengths are relatively low, failing 
to meet the demands of next-generation ramjet engines for high- 
temperature alloys. During the vacuum melting process of traditional 
high-temperature alloys, elements such as W, Mo, and Ni have very high 
melting points, making it difficult to achieve uniform composition, 
which leads to the formation of many segregates in the as-cast alloy 
microstructure. In addition, the traditional casting and forging treat -
ment methods result in non-uniform phase distribution in the prepared 
alloys, limiting the improvement of alloy strength and ductility.
To eliminate macro-segregation in highly alloyed cast alloys, the 
powder metallurgy process has been developed for the preparation of 
high-temperature alloys. A significant advantage of powder high- 
temperature alloys is their ability to confine compositional segregation 
within individual powder particles, thereby eliminating the macro- 
segregation issue present in conventional castings. Moreover, the 
rapidly solidified powders exhibit fine grains and uniform microstruc -
tures, which largely ensure the uniformity and consistency of the alloy ’ s 
mechanical properties and excellent hot workability [ 127 ]. The 
advantages of powder high-temperature alloys can be summarized as 
follows: (1) Elimination of the elemental segregation problem existing in 
traditional casting metallurgy processes, improvement of the alloy ’ s hot 
workability, and the feasibility of superplastic processing; (2) Uniform 
and fine microstructures, stable performance, and a significant increase 
in material reliability compared to traditional casting and forging pro -
cesses; (3) High strength and ductility under various temperature con -
ditions, as well as resistance to fatigue and creep; (4) High material 
utilization rate, reducing the cost of raw materials.
Sun et al. [ 128 ] systematically investigated the role of second phase 
in governing high-temperature tensile properties of powder-metallurgy 
EP962NP alloy through tailored post-solution cooling and aging treat -
ments. Their optimized heat treatment protocol (comprising solution 
treatment followed by dual-stage aging) achieved exceptional mechan -
ical synergy: ultimate tensile strength 1251 MPa, tensile yield strength 
1079 MPa, and elongation 13.3 %. In parallel work, Gao et al . [ 129 ] 
engineered graphene-reinforced Ni-based superalloys via

4.5. Additive manufacturing
Additive manufacturing (AM) technology, also known as 3D printing 
technology, is a technique based on digital three-dimensional models, 
where three-dimensional structural components are fabricated through 
the layer-by-layer accumulation of forming materials. Compared with 
traditional machining technologies, AM offers advantages such as a 
shorter production cycle and lower production costs, enabling the in -
tegrated design and rapid manufacturing of complex structural compo -
nents. Currently, the forming materials in use include metals, plastics, 
and ceramics, which have found extensive applications in fields such as 
aerospace, automotive industry, and medicine. For instance, hot-end 
components of aero-engines can be processed and prepared using AM 
technology. These components are typically complex in structure and 
operate in harsh environments, making traditional manufacturing pro -
cesses increasingly inadequate for their production. Consequently, laser 
additive manufacturing (LAM) technologies exhibit significant advan -
tages in comparison [ 130 , 131 ]. The LAM technology that has matured 
and is currently applied to the preparation of high-melting-point mate -
rials such as metals is primarily laser melting deposition (LMD) [ 132 ,
133 ]. Unlike other 3D printing technologies, LMD does not require 
molds or support structures, allowing for the fabrication of complex 
structural components; it has minimal limitations on forming di -
mensions, making it suitable for large-scale manufacturing; it can pro -
duce mixed gradient structural materials from various materials; and the 
formed components have fine and uniform microstructures with excel -
lent mechanical properties. However, during the LMD forming process, 
macroscopic features such as cracks and dimensional accuracy, as well 
as microscopic defects like microstructures, residual stresses, and 
porosity, can all affect the quality of the formed components.
Porosity is one of the common defects in the LMD process, which can 
have an adverse effect on its mechanical properties. The formation of 
porosity is primarily attributed to three mechanisms: Firstly, when the 
laser density is high or the melt pool temperature is excessively elevated, 
the melt pool becomes unstable, thereby forming circular pores [ 134 ]; 
Secondly, pores may already exist in the raw powder, or protective gases 
may be entrapped during the deposition process, and the sublimation of 
elements can also lead to the formation of porosity; Lastly, insufficient 
melting of the powder feedstock, particularly inadequate melting of 
interlayer powder particles, results in the formation of irregular pores 
[ 135 ]. To control porosity defects, measures such as conducting laser

Hot cracking is another common defect in the LMD process, typically 
caused by stresses resulting from differences in the expansion and 
contraction rates between the substrate and the previously deposited 
layer and the newly deposited layer. Research has found that even by 
increasing the energy density over a wide range of parameters to reduce 
porosity, the formation of cracks cannot be effectively mitigated [ 137 ]. 
Moreover, the probability of crack formation is highest in the overlap 
area and often results in long liquation cracks, the formation of which is 
influenced by the overlap rate [ 138 ]. To address these defects, control 
parameters can be optimized under complex multi-factorial conditions. 
By optimizing parameters that affect deposition rate and mechanical 
properties, crack-free and high-performance samples can be obtained 
[ 139 ]. Concurrently, simplifying parameters to reduce influencing fac -
tors and establishing predictive process maps for melt pool geometry can 
also aid in the fabrication of defect-free, high-performance formed 
components [ 140 ].
Wang et al . [ 136 ] elucidated the dual solidification mechanisms 
governing titanium alloy microstructure evolution during LMD. Their 
analysis revealed: (1) equiaxed grain formation via heterogeneous 
nucleation on semi-molten powder particles ( Fig. 11 (a and b)), and (2) 
columnar grain development through melt pool bottom epitaxial 
growth. The interplay between these mechanisms dictates final grain 
morphology. Lower deposition rates induced molten zone overheating, 
deeper substrate remelting, and steeper thermal gradients at solidifica -
tion fronts - conditions favoring epitaxial growth dominance and coarse 
columnar structures. Conversely, higher deposition rates produced 
insufficient powder melting and reduced pool superheat, promoting 
widespread heterogeneous nucleation across the melt pool surfa -
ce/interior that refined grain size toward near-equiaxed configurations 
( Fig. 11 (c)). In addition to fully columnar and equiaxed deposited grain 
structures, a unique "reinforced concrete-like" mixed grain structure (as 
shown in Fig. 11 (d)) was also prepared, consisting of coarse columns and 
fine inter-columnar equiaxed grains, and the selection mechanisms for 
the three representative deposited grain morphologies were established. 
Concurrently, Chandrasekaran et al . [ 141 ] fabricated functionally 
graded materials (FGMs) for use as marine risers via AM and compared 
them with the widely used X52 steel. It was found that the TYS and UTS 
of the FGM were increased by 11.16 % and 24.75 %, respectively, while 
toughness and ductility were also enhanced, and the elastic modulus and 
Poisson ’ s ratio were comparable to those of X52 steel.
4.6. Directional solidification

temperature alloys are not applicable to the fabrication of single- 
crystal high-temperature alloys. Currently, single-crystal high-temper -

techniques. However, prior to this, special methods are required to 
obtain initial single-crystal grains. There are currently two main 
methods for obtaining single-crystal grains: one is based on the selected 
crystallization method, which utilizes the competitive growth among 
grains to eliminate grains in other orientations, ultimately retaining only 
the grains with [001] orientation; the other is the seed crystal method, 
which places single-crystal grains into a liquid melt and employs the 
principle of epitaxial growth to obtain single crystals. In special cases, a

Directional solidification technology involves establishing a specific 
temperature gradient during the solidification of an alloy to control the 
melt to flow in a single direction opposite to the heat flux. This process 
directs the solid-liquid interface of the alloy to move in this direction, 
allowing grains to grow parallel in a specific orientation. This eliminates 
the lateral grain boundaries in the alloy, resulting in an alloy with a 
bundled columnar crystal structure. There are two indispensable con -
ditions for directional solidification technology: (1) Unidirectional heat 
flux maintains orthogonality to the advancing solid-liquid interface; (2) 
The melt exhibits nucleation suppression proximally to the crystalliza -
tion front. Additionally, it is essential to maintain a sufficiently high 
liquid phase temperature to prevent lateral heat dissipation and to 
sustain a high temperature gradient at the solid-liquid interface.
Wang et al . [ 142 ] examined the impact of directional solidification 
technology on the solid solution window of two Ni-based single-crystal 
high-temperature alloys. It was discovered that samples prepared using 
the liquid metal cooling (LMC) method, as opposed to those prepared 
using the high-speed rapid solidification (HRS) method, exhibited 
smaller dendritic spacings, lower dendritic segregation, and eutectic 
content (as depicted in Fig. 12 (a – c)), as well as smaller second phase 
dimensions (as depicted in Fig. 12 (d – g)). Alloy-2, containing a higher 
concentration of Cr and Co and a lower concentration of Re, had den -
dritic spacings and eutectic volume fractions comparable to Alloy-1, 
while also demonstrating a lower degree of dendritic segregation and 
smaller second phase dimensions (as depicted in Fig. 12 (h and i)). The 
effective broadening of the solid solution treatment window can be 
achieved by employing the liquid metal cooling method or by adjusting 
the alloy composition to that of Alloy-2. Yuan et al . [ 143 ] examined 
directional solidification in single-crystal alloys, revealing that second -
ary branch orientation drives dendritic alignment into unidir -
ectional/bidirectional arrays. The aligned dendrite fraction exhibited 
positive correlation with solidification distance yet inverse dependence 
on withdrawal rate.
5. Conclusions and perspectives
In the contemporary era of rapid advancements in aerospace, power 
generation, and chemical industry, the demands for material perfor -
mance have become increasingly stringent, particularly under extreme 
conditions such as high temperatures, high pressures, and complex 
mechanical stresses. High-temperature alloys, which maintain stable 
microstructures at temperatures exceeding 600 
◦

vironments, have garnered significant attention. These alloys find 
extensive applications in critical sectors such as aerospace and petro -
chemical industries. This study employs bibliometric methods to 
analyze the research hotspots and key areas in the field of high- 
temperature alloys, revealing the general trends in research over 
recent years. It also delves into the strengthening mechanisms, charac -
teristics of alloy systems, and advanced preparation and processing 
techniques of high-temperature alloys, aiming to provide valuable ref -
erences and insights for researchers in related fields. 
(1).The regulation of dual-phase structures remains a critical 
pathway for performance enhancement. The solid solution 
strengthening effect exhibits a positive correlation with atomic size/ 
modulus mismatch, while precipitation strengthening achieves 
strength leaps through the synergistic action of the Orowan mecha -

crack initiation.
