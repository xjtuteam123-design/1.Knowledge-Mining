ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
In Situ Study of Temperature Stability
and Precipitation Kinetics of d and g Phases in Nickel
Base Superalloys
LAURANE FINET, LOI ¨C NAZE´, VINCENT MAUREL, and VLADIMIR A. ESIN
The present study aims at determining the temperature stability and precipitation kinetics of
phases like d-Ni3Nb, d-Ni3Ta, g-Ni3Ti, and g-Ni3(Al,Nb) for potential hardening eﬀect in nickel
base superalloys for turbine discs. Two model compositions of nickel base superalloys with
diﬀerent Ti/Al, Nb/Al, and Ta/Al ratios were produced. These alloys were characterized using
SEM and TEM. High-energy synchrotron X-ray diﬀraction (HE-XRD) was used to follow the
evolution of phases during various heat treatments. The validity of composition criteria
available in the literature as well as that of TCNI7 thermodynamic database to predict the
composition and temperature stability of d and g phases is discussed.

I. INTRODUCTION
NICKEL base superalloys possess unique combina-
tion of high corrosion resistance and increased high
temperature strength rendering them one of the most
used materials for the parts operating at extreme
conditions. Despite their relatively increased weight, as
compared to other structural metallic alloys such as
steels, titanium, and aluminum base alloys, up to now
nickel base superalloys are widely used for aeronautic
applications. Moreover, for fabrication of turbine discs
in aeroengines, nickel base superalloys are almost
exclusive class of materials due to their excellent
properties. These properties are ﬁrstly due to complex
chemical composition of these alloys, which can contain
up to ten (and even more) alloying elements, each of
them with a speciﬁc role.
[1] Several of these elements,
like, for example, aluminum or niobium induce harden-
ing via the precipitation of c0 (with generic composition
Ni3Al) and c00 (in less number of alloys, with generic
composition Ni 3Nb) phases during appropriate heat
treatment. The size, number density, spatial distribu-
tion, and temperature stability of these precipitates will
mostly determine the nickel base superalloys properties
and, thus, their service lifetime.
Actual climate and ecological circumstances demand
constant optimization of fuel use eﬃciency, especially, in
aeronautics. A possible way to improve the aeroengine
eﬃciency is to increase the operating temperatures. It
requires the development of new materials or optimisa-
tion of existing materials to resist to these new extreme
conditions. It seems however that the limits of service
conditions have been reached for conventional c
0 hard-
ened nickel base superalloys, and further developments
should focus on alternative hardening systems with
increased temperature stability. Since quite recently, a
particular attention has been paid to the alloys where g
and d phases can form. The g phase has D0
24 hexagonal
crystalline structure with typical composition such as
Ni3Ti and Ni 3(Al,Nb) where Cr and Co can substitute
for Ni and Ta can substitute for Al and Ti. [2,3]
Orthorhombic D0 a d phase has typical composition,
such as Ni 3Nb and Ni 3Ta where Ni can be substituted
by Cr and Nb and Ta by Al and Ti. [2,4] g and d phases
usually precipitate with lamellar or laths morphology.
These phases are not ’’new discovered’’ ones, since g was
ﬁrstly identiﬁed in late 1930s [5] and further observed in
alloys with increased Ti/Al ratios [6–10] and d is being
widely used in 718 alloy to prevent grain growth and to
produce thus hardening eﬀect due to grain size reﬁne-
ment. It is worth noting that g phase was observed in
recent ATI Allvac 718Plus alloy
[11–14] intended to
replace widely used 718 alloy. However, g phase,
according to some rare data published to date, is mostly
considered as harmful for the nickel base superalloys
properties,[6,8,9,15] although some beneﬁcial eﬀects could
be however observed. [16–18]

Nevertheless, g and d phases are now being investi-
gated for their potential other beneﬁcial eﬀects on the
properties of nickel base superalloys. [13,19] The major
diﬃculty is related to the properties of g and d phases
which are not still well known, especially, in terms of
composition and temperature stability. Due to increas-
ing use of artiﬁcial intelligence for alloys design,
partially based on thermodynamic simulations, such
data are critical for reliable prediction of alloys
microstructure and properties. Moreover, the existing
databases have failed to describe phase equilibrium in
high Ta- and Nb-containing superalloys (up to 9 % at.)
where g and d phases were found to form.
[20] However,
increased content of Ta and Nb was found to be
beneﬁcial in Inconel 725 alloy since it led to increase of
mechanical properties and their temperature stability
[21]
opening interesting way for design of alloys with
improved temperature stability.
Therefore, the aim of the present work was to
investigate the temperature stability, composition, and
precipitation kinetics of g and d phases in model nickel
base alloys.
II. STUDIED ALLOYS
To get insight into the properties of g and d phases,
two diﬀerent alloys, based on compositions studied in
Refs. 22, 23, were chosen. These alloys have increased
Nb content, as compared to conventional nickel base
superalloys. Using thermodynamic simulations, their
original composition was simpliﬁed by suppressing
carbon and tungsten and molybdenum to avoid unde-
sirable phases (like carbides and topologically closed
packed (TCP) phases) and other minor elements like
boron and zirconium, which might complicate
microstructure characterization. At the same time, the
fraction of c, c
0, d; and g phases of interest was almost
unchanged. The alloys thus obtained were designated as
Alloy 1 and Alloy 2 (Table I); the corresponding phase
equilibrium obtained using Thermo-Calc software
and TCNI7* database is shown in Figure 1.
According to these calculations, Alloy 1 is expected to
precipitate d phase and Alloy 2 is expected to precipitate
both g and d phases (it should be noted that the fraction
of d phase is expected to be higher than that of g phase).
Moreover, according to empirical criteria proposed in
Reference 19, the composition of Alloy 1 should be
favorable to the formation of d phase, while that of
Alloy 2 should lead to the appearance of g phase. It is
worth noting however that these criteria failed to predict
correctly the presence or absence of d and g phases in the
alloys with increased Ta content.
[20] Compared with
commercialized superalloys, the alloys of the present
study appear to be free of TCP phases and car-
bides/borides (Figure 1) to simplify the phase
identiﬁcation.
III. METHODS
The alloys were processed by vacuum induction
melting (VIM) and supplied as cylinders of 8 mm in
diameter. The chemical composition was measured by
inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrom-
etry (ICP-OES) and was found to be very close to
nominal values reported in Table I. To check homo-
geneity, the chemical analysis was carried out in upper,
middle, and lower parts of ingots as well as in ingots
cross-section using electron probe microanalyzer
(EPMA) CAMECA SXA100. Such measurements were
performed with a step size of 20 lm at an accelerating
voltage of 15 kV and beam current of 40 nA. No
macrosegregation could be observed like in alloys
investigated in Reference 20.
Multi-scale microstructure characterization was car-
ried out to identify the nature of phases observed in the
two alloys and to study their properties. A S-FEG FEI
Nova NanoSEM 450 scanning electron micro-
scope (SEM) and backscattered electron imaging (BSE)
together with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS)
with an accelerating voltage of 15 kV were used for
microstructure characterization at the microscale level.
The chemical composition of nanometer sized precipi-
tates was obtained using EDS in a FEI TECNAI F20ST
transmission electron microscope (TEM) operating at
200 kV. The nature of the precipitates was investigated
using both selected electron area diﬀraction (SAED) in
a FEI TECNAI 30T TEM operating at 300 kV and high
energy X-ray diﬀraction (HE-XRD) experiments car-
ried out at P07 beamline of PETRA III at Deutsches
Elektronen-Synchrotron (DESY, Hamburg, Germany).
Moreover, HE-XRD was applied to study in situ the
kinetics of precipitation on cooling and in isothermal
conditions using experimental setup described in Refs.
20 and 24. For in situ experiments, the cylindrical
samples of 5 mm in diameter and 10 mm in length were
used. The heat treatment in vacuum was carried out
using an induction furnace which allows relatively fast
cooling and heating rates. The raw X-ray data were
recorded using Perkin Elmer XRD 1621 Flat Panel 2D
detector; the distance between the sample and the
detector was calibrated using LaB

and was close to 1.6 m. The high energetic beam
(100 keV, 0.124 A˚ ) produced X-ray patterns of excellent
quality for the acquisition time of 3 s. The resulting 2D

IV. RESULTS
A. As-Cast Microstructure and Solutionizing
The as-processed microstructure of studied alloys is
shown in Figure 3.
For both alloys, the matrix (gray areas) with globular
or lath-shape precipitates (bright areas) is observed. To
study the precipitation and properties of g and d phases,
these coarse precipitates formed during the processing
should be dissolved during high temperature solution-
izing. Since Alloy 1 and Alloy 2 are model ones, the
appropriate conditions of such a heat treatment were
established using the results of thermodynamic simula-
tions coupled to microstructure analysis after diﬀerent
heat treatments. It was revealed, in agreement with the
thermodynamic data presented in Figure 1, that only
partial solutionizing was possible for Alloy 1 at
1250
/C14C (1523 K) during 16 hours (Figure 4(a)), while
Alloy 2 was completely solutionized after heat treatment
at 1230
/C14C (1503 K) during 6 hours (Figure 4(b)). Heat
treatment at 1260 /C14C of Alloy 1 led to incipient melting.
B. Nature of Phases and Solvus Temperature
Since d and g phases can be confused due to their
similar morphology and close crystalline structure, [30] a
particular attention has been paid to the identiﬁcation of
the nature of precipitates observed in the studied alloys.
Both, HE-XRD and SAED in TEM were used. These
two methods are complementary: for SAED in TEM,
one particular precipitate can be chosen for analysis,
while XRD is a more global technique and can give us a
volume fraction of the analyzed phases.
To identify the nature of the phases that precipitate in
a wide temperature range, in situ cooling of the samples
of the studied alloys was carried out during HE-XRD
experiments at DESY. The samples had been previously
homogenized, according to conditions established in
Section IV–A. Then, the samples were placed in Ba ¨hr
DIL805 A/D dilatometer available at P07 beamline of
PETRA III and heated with the rate of 60
/C14Cs /C01 up to
the solutionizing temperature to avoid any precipitation
on heating. Once the solutionizing temperature has been
reached, the sample was soaked for 5 min to stabilize
the temperature and cooled at a rate of 10
/C14C min /C01.
Such a cooling rate was chosen to be suﬃciently slow to
be close to equilibrium conditions and suﬃciently fast
for in situ synchrotron experiments.

C. Chemical Composition
The chemical composition of d and g phases was
obtained after aging for 8 hours at 1100
/C14C (1373 K)
using EDS in TEM and SEM. On the one hand, EDS in
TEM allowed the measurement of the composition of
thin laths (thickness less than 1 lm) which cannot be
resolved applying EDS in SEM. On the other hand,
EDS in SEM enabled better statistics since a number of
diﬀerent precipitates in diﬀerent grains could be ana-
lyzed. It is worth noting that the results obtained from
one precipitate to another by both methods were very
close suggesting that the chemical composition of d and
g phases was well deﬁned. The results for globular and
lath precipitates in both alloys are reported in Tables IV
and V. A good qualitative agreement can be stated for
the results obtained in TEM and SEM. For Alloy 1,
smaller Ni content in d phase is found by EDS in TEM
probably due to better spatial resolution than in SEM.
No diﬀerence in composition of globular and laths
precipitates of d phase is observed which seems to be
close to Ni
3Nb. Moreover, agreement between experi-
mental data and those obtained using TCNI7 database
can be observed taking into account the uncertainty of
EDS measurements (Table IV).
Signiﬁcant diﬀerence in composition of globular and
lath precipitates of g phases as measured by EDS in
TEM and SEM was found for Alloy 2. Laths had
systematically lower (about 2% at.) Nb content than
globular precipitates which was replaced by Ni. Accord-
ing to thermodynamic calculation, g phase is not
stable at 1100
/C14C (1373 K), and therefore, for compar-

D. Precipitation Kinetics
Using in situ isothermal experiments, it was possible
to obtain the evolution of phase fraction as function of
time and, thus, to follow the precipitation kinetics of d
and g phases in studied alloys. The samples were
pre-solutionized, according to conditions reported in
Section IV–A, and further heated up at a rate of
60
/C14Cs /C01 to the solutionizing temperature, held at this
temperature for 5 minutes, and ﬁnally cooled at a rate of
60 /C14Cs /C01 to the aging temperature comprised between
1000 and 1150 /C14C. No phase transformation was
observed during the cooling from solutionizing temper-
ature. The duration of holding varied: on the one hand,
the heat treatment was systematically stopped once the
intensity of diﬀraction peaks seemed to stabilize, i.e.,
phase transformations were completed, on the other
hand, the longest soaking at the aging temperature
could not exceed 8 hours due to time limitations for the
experiments at synchrotron.
1. d Phase in Alloy 1
Since complete solutionizing of Alloy 1 was not
possible, the overall precipitation kinetics could not be
studied. Nevertheless, the evolution of d phase fraction
can be obtained during isothermal exposure at 1050,
1100, and 1150
/C14C. Figure 9(a) indicates that the equi-
librium d phase fraction seems to be reached at the end
of soaking for all temperatures. The equilibrium fraction
of d phase decreases with increasing temperature as well
as the time to reach the equilibrium. The precipitation
kinetics is quite similar for the three temperatures.
2. g Phase in Alloy 2
Figure 10 gives the evolution of (202) XRD peak of g
phase during the exposure of Alloy 2 at
1100
/C14C (1373 K). A clear increase in intensity is
observed from the beginning of the soak which stabilizes
for the exposure duration close to 100 minutes indicat-
ing that g phase approached the equilibrium fraction at
this temperature. Volume fraction of g phase as function
of holding time deduced from such experiments at
diﬀerent temperatures using Rietveld method is shown
in Figure 9(b). One can observe that increase in tem-
perature leads to an increase in precipitation kinetics,
although the kinetics seems to be similar at
1050 (1323 K) and 1100
/C14C (1373 K) for g volume
fraction less than 5%. Except for the aging temperature
of 1000 /C14C (1273 K), at which the exposure time of
8 hours was not suﬃciently long to reach equilibrium
conditions, the fraction of g phase could stabilize during
the aging at 1050, 1100, and 1150
/C14C, suggesting that
Alloy 2 approaches equilibrium state. It is worth noting
however that the fraction of g phase was not completely
stabilized during the aging at 1050 /C14C and longer aging
duration would have been required to reach full
equilibrium. Nevertheless, the kinetics was clearly

V. DISCUSSION
A. Comparison with Thermodynamic Calculations
Using d phase fraction obtained at the end of
isothermal heat treatments carried out in situ at syn-
chrotron (Figure 9(a)), the experimental results for
Alloy 1 can be compared with thermodynamic calcula-
tions using the TCNI7 database (Figure 11(a)). It is
worth mentioning that the precipitation of d phase alone
was correctly predicted in Alloy 1 by thermodynamic
calculations. It can be observed as well that calculated
volume fraction of d phase agrees well with experimental
data. The agreement is excellent for the temperature
range between 1100 /C14C (1373 K) and 1300 /C14C (1573 K),
and some deviation of experimental results from ther-
modynamic data is observed below 1100
/C14C (1373 K).
The agreement might be good at temperatures close to
700 /C14C (973 K) but it has to be conﬁrmed by further
investigation. In the same way, it can be speculated that
the solvus temperature of d phase in Alloy 1 seems to be
well predicted by TCNI7 database.
The situation is diﬀerent for Alloy 2 where g phase
alone was observed experimentally, while TCNI7
database predicted both d and g (Figure 1(b)). To be
able to compare the experimental results with thermo-
dynamic calculations, d phase has to be suppressed.
Such a method allows to analyze metastable equilibrium
or cases where the precipitation of one of the phases is
impeded by diﬀerent reasons.
[31] In the absence of d
phase in Alloy 2, the calculated volume fraction of g
phase can reach 10% below 700 /C14C (973 K). However,
X-ray data show that it is close to 40% for the
temperatures less than 1050
/C14C (1323 K). The fraction
of g phase as well as its solvus temperature are
signiﬁcantly underestimated if calculated using TCNI7
database (Figure 11(b)). It is worth noting that when d
phase is suppressed in the simulation, the chemical
composition of g phase is almost the same as for the case
where the d phase is taken into account. Even though
the sum of fractions of g and d phases is calculated
(without suppressing d phase), it will not exceed 20%
which is well below experimental results.
It seems that increased Nb content in g phase as
measured by EDS in SEM and TEM (Table V) can
explain such a disagreement between experimental

VI. CONCLUSION
The main results of the present study can be summa-
rized as follows:
– To study the temperature stability and precipitation
kinetics of d and g phases, two model Ta-containing
nickel base alloys with increased Nb content were
produced applying composition criteria reported in
Reference 19 and thermodynamic simulations using
TCNI7 database;
– After solutionizing (which was partial for Alloy 1)
and aging at 1100 /C14C, Alloy 1 contained d phase and
Alloy 2 contained g phase together with the precip-
itates of c0 phase;
– In situ study of precipitation kinetics using HE-XRD
revealed the precipitation of d phase in Alloy 1 was
mostly finished after 1 h of soak in the temperature
range between 1050 and 1150 /C14C, while even 8 h of
exposure was not sufficient to reach equilibrium
fraction of g phase in Alloy 2 at 1000 and 1050 /C14C;
– The solvus temperature of g phase in Alloy 2 is
almost 200 /C14C higher than that in 718plus due to
different chemical compositions of these alloys and,
probably, to increased Ta content in g phase in
Alloy 2;
– g phase description in TCNI7 thermodynamic
database needs improvement for predicting phase
equilibria in high Nb- and Ta-containing alloys.
