MECHANICAL CHARACTERISTICS OF TUNGSTEN-FREE HARD ALLOYS (REVIEW)
M. G. Loshak1 and L. I. Aleksandrova UDC 669.018.25:620.187.5
Titanium carbide- or carbonitride-base tungsten-free hard alloys with a nickel-molybdenum binder,
which can effectively replace tungsten alloys in metal and alloy cutting, are examined. The proper
choice of the composition and grain sizes of the carbonitride component allows the bending strength
of 1500 MPa and hardness of 90–91 HRA to be reached. High-temperature hardness and fracture
toughness are of the same order as those of hard tungsten alloys.
Keywords: tungsten-free hard alloys, composition, structure, strength, hardness, fracture toughness, test temperature.
Sintered WC–Co tungsten alloys with their unique physicomechanical characteristics find application as a
tool material for a multiplicity of uses: mining, metal and nonmetal cutting, wood working, pressing etc.
However, late in the 1960s and early in the 1970s for lack of tungsten and cobalt, a number of titanium
carbide- and carbonitride-base tungsten-free hard alloys with a nickel-molybdenum binder was developed and
introduced into full-scale practice. Although tools from these alloys were coming into widespread use, mainly in
metal working, the scope of their application was restrained by high instability of item properties. A deeper insight
into the relation between the structure of tungsten-free hard alloys and their mechanical characteristics can contribute
to clarifying this issue, which would favor the development and improvement of their production techniques.
The present review summarizes data on this relation for major tungsten-free hard alloys produced in Ukraine
and Russia.
The choice of tungsten-free hard alloys for making different tools is based on their physicomechanical and
electromagnetic properties. In particular, the mechanical properties are a decisive factor for cutting and noncutting
shaping applications. The thermal characteristics are responsible for the thermocyclic load resistance and solder-
ability. The data on major tungsten-free hard alloys (Tables 1 and 2) [1] suggest that a relatively low heat conduction
controls the soldering behavior on fastening cutting tools in a steel holder. Due to their high linear expansion
coefficient, they can be employed for gauges used in measuring the steel component geometry.
The mechanical characteristics of tungsten-free hard alloys are somewhat lower than those of tungsten and
titanium-tungsten ones. However, the bending strength Rbm of some KNT16 alloy lots can be higher as compared
to that of a T15K6 alloy. This value varies with test temp eratures: it decreases up to 873 K, then increases up to
1073 K [1]. Tungsten-free hard alloys display a relatively wide scatter in the data on mechanical characteristics,
which is likely to be explained by the inhomogeneous structure of prepared specimens.
Transmission electron microscopy revealed [2–4] quite a different structure of TN20 alloy lots, produced by
the same technology. This alloy also possesses different strength properties. In particular, Rbm for some lots reaches
1400–1600 MPa [3]. It was established [5, 6] that grain forms varied from round to acute-angled, with Va carbides
introduced into tungsten-free hard alloys. For one alloy, a change in the “ring” thickness around the grain nucleus
was revealed [3, 4]. Grain sizes of the carbide phase and the ring thickness would decrease with carbon contents in
alloys. Thus, their mechanical characteristics are dependent on the contribution from all structural components.

production technique of initial titanium carbide since the composition and properties of a (Ti, Mo)C 1–x solid solution

Widespread investigations of the relation between the structure and properties of tungsten-free hard alloys
were performed with a TN20 alloy [3, 7].
The structure of titanium carbide-base alloys with a nickel-molybdenum binder consists of the three phases:
titanium carbide, (Ti, Mo)C solid solution shell, and binder, mainly arranged along grain boundaries (Fig. 1).
Alloys with a more homogeneous structure and carbide grain sizes of an order of 1–2 /c109m possess enhanced
strength properties and fracture mostly along the binding phase. The specimens with a low strength ( /c60600 MPa),
exhibit the ring structure, typical of a TN20 alloy, only in some areas, with different grain sizes, their nonuniform
distribution, and clusters of coarse and fine grains (Fig. 2a).
In this case, the fracture is effected by the cleavage along carbide grain aggregates. The specimens with a
900-MPa bending strength also exhibit the structure with nonuniformly distributed grains, the shell width of the (Ti,
Mo)C solid solution reaches considerable sizes (Fig. 2b). The (Ti, Mo)C solid solution ring is principally fractured.
The specimens with the structure shown in Fig. 2c, possess the highest strength. The solid solution interlayer size in
these specimens is much smaller than in the above. On the average, the TiC grain sizes do not exceed 2 /c109m, the
grains hold their angular form. The fracture surface of such specimens reveals the more uniform distribution of the
ductile binding phase.

Studies on the relation between the critical stress intensity factor K c1 and microstructure parameters for
tungsten alloys [8] became the basis for similar investigations of tungsten-free hard alloys. The relation between the
fracture toughness of a TN20 alloy, characterizing its ductility, and microstructure parameters was examined in [9].
The density and bending strength were measured on 553 5/c180/c180 -mm specimens from several alloy lots, sintered under
conditions providing the different structure formation. Then specimen halves after bending tests were used for
estimating the indentation fracture toughness. For this, the specimens were ground over two opposite faces by a
diamond disk (125/100 grain sizes on a B2-01 binder) with cooling and polished over one face with diamond
micropowders (40/28, 10/7, and 3/2 grain sizes). Indentation was effected on a TP2 instrument with a Vickers
diamond pyramid. The indentation load was 100 N. The imprint diagonals and crack lengths formed in its corners
were measured on an optical microscope. The factor K c1 was calculated by the formula KP Cc1

The structure of specimens was investigated* on a UÉVM-100K transmission electron microscope using

number per phase. The shape and length of the TiC-(Ti, Mo)C contact line were not controlled.
Investigation results show that despite the identical composition and density, mechanical properties of
specimens of some TN20 lots are greatly different, mainly because of differences in the microstructure (Table 3).
From the results of measuring the magnetic characteristic HCM and mean grain sizes of the carbide
component, TN20 alloy lots can be divided into the three groups. The first group (lot No. 4) includes the carbide
phase with fine grain sizes (over 50% grains are of 0.5–1.0 /c109m). The coercivity for such specimens is equal to /c1795
kA/m. The second group (lots No. 1, 6, and 7) possesses the more uniform distribution of grain sizes with 1.8–2.0-
/c109m mean values, the coercivity makes up 3.7–5.5 kA/m. The third group (lots No. 2, 3, 5, and 8) exhibits the coarsest
conglomerate-containing structure. The coercivity was 2.2–3.5 kA/m.
As electron-microscopic investigations demonstrate (Fig. 3), the third group shows a considerable increase
in a (Ti, Mo)C circular shell area on the grains as compared to the area of their TiC nucleus, while for the second
group the areas taken by TiC and (Ti, Mo)C in the carbide phase are about the same (Table 4). The K c1 values for
the third group are much lower (by 20–60%) than for the second one.

Despite the scatter in the data, it is clear that the higher the fracture toughness, the lower the hardness. It
should be noted that for tungsten alloys, the hardness and fracture toughness are linearly related. For tungsten-free
alloys a decrease in hardness with K c1 is also observed. The hardness HV and the total length of cracks in the
imprint corners for a TN20 alloy are linearly related at a 100-N load (Fig. 4). As judged from this relation, alloys
exhibiting hardness below 13.0 GPa should not have cr acks in the imprint corners. Really, a KNT16 alloy of a
12.68-GPa hardness and a TN25 alloy of a 12.50-GPa hardness at a 100-N indentation load do not display any cracks
in the imprint corners. For these alloys, cracks originate at a 150-N load.
Impact strengths for tungsten-free hard alloys, obtained on unnotched specimens, are rather low: (1.0–2.0)/c215104
and (1.2–1.4) /c215104 J/m 2 for TN20 and KNT16 alloys, respectively, while this value for a VK6 tungsten alloy makes
up (1.6–1.9) /c215104 J/m 2 .
Fatigue tests of 553 5/c180/c180 -mm specimens of a KNT16 alloy under harmonic three-point bending demonstrated
that the results were in good agreement with the normal distribution law for lifetime logarithms: on probability paper,
corresponding to this law, experimental points fall satisfactorily on the straight lines (Fig. 5). This suggests that such
factors as the structure of the material, structure defects, condition of the surface layer, etc. are equally influential
with the lifetime of specimens.

CONCLUSIONS
1. The scatter in the data on mechanical characteristics of tungsten-free hard alloys is determined by the
inhomogeneous structure of the material caused by process factors. The nature and extent of influence of some
structural elements on the static and impact strengths of alloys are still not clearly understood.
2. The mechanical characteristics of tungsten-free hard alloys are somewhat inferior to those of tungsten or
titanium-tungsten ones, but their level is quite sufficient for the normal operation of cutting tools, in particular due to
their high hardness at normal and high temperatures.
3. A TN20 alloy with TiC grain sizes no more than 2 /c109m, with a small interlayer of the (Ti, Mo)C 1–x solid
solution, and uniform distribution of the binding phase possesses the highest strength.
