Abstract 
Bulk Nb 3Al-based alloys were synthesized through a joint re duction of Nb 2O5 and Al 2O3 
oxides by calcium hydride. On the example of two co mpositions, it was shown that the calcium 
hydride-based method can produce Nb-Al powders with a given aluminum content. Bulk alloys 
with a Nb 3Al phase content of ≥ 95 wt% and a porosity level of < 5% were for the f irst time 
fabricated by conventional pressing and vacuum sint ering of synthesized powders, and the high-
temperature properties of bulk alloys were investig ated. Aluminum evaporation was registered 
during the vacuum sintering of Nb 3Al-based powders at a temperature of 1473 K (>0.5 Tm, where 
Tm is the melting temperature). The bulk Nb 3Al intermetallic compound is characterized by high 
creep resistance (a steady creep rate of 6.77 × 10 −5 s −1 ) and increased strength (a compressive 
yield strength of ∼430 MPa and a tensile strength of ∼160 MPa) at temperatures exceeding 0.5 Tm 
of Nb 3Al. 
 
1. Introduction 
In the early 1990s, alloys based on the Nb 
3Al intermetallic compound were considered as 
potential structural heat-resistant materials that could replace existing nickel superalloys. Nb 3Al-
based alloys were shown to be characterized by high strength and low creep at temperatures up 
to 1473 K [1–3]. Polycrystalline Nb 3Al was also reported to exhibit high creep resistan ce at a 
temperature of 1473 K under a load of 100 MPa [4]. Nb 3Al obtained through gas atomization of 
a Nb–Al melt with subsequent hot isostatic pressing and high-temperature heat treatment for 
grain growth has a very high tensile strength at homological temperatures of >0.8 Tm (where Tm is 
the melting temperature) [5]. For example, Nb 3Al, with a grain size of 200−300 µm, has σ values 
of ∼185, ∼105, and ∼0.1 MPa at temperatures of 1873, 1973, and 2073 K, respectively. Along 
with the aforementioned properties, Nb 3Al has a rather high melting point (2333 K), a rela tively 
low density (7.29 g/cm 3) [6], and a high hardness (68 HRC [7] or 910 HV [8]). 
These useful properties made it possible to consider Nb 3Al as the basis for creating a new 
generation of heat-resistant alloys. However, it is adequately understood that this intermetallic 
compound is characterized by extremely low ductilit y, which is ∼1.6% at 1473 K [9]. Research 
on the deformation behavior of Nb 3Al showed that dislocation slip occurs only in one slip 
system, of type 〈001 〉{100}[10,11], leading to a low plasticity resource. Moreover, because of its 
high brittleness, this compound has an extremely lo w fracture toughness coefficient ( K1c ) of 1.1 
MPa ·m1/2 [12]. These disadvantages seriously limit the poss ibilities of using Nb 3Al as a basis for

structural materials. Consequently, Nb 3Al is used mainly in functional applications becaus e it 
has favorable superconducting characteristics and i s of interest for applications in the 
International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor, i n nuclear fusion devices, and in 
superconducting magnets [13–17]. 
The structural strength of heat-resistant Nb 3Al-based alloys can be improved by creating 
multi-phase compositions of, for example, refractor y metals (Nb, Ta, Cr, Mo, etc.) (or a solid 
solution) and Nb 3Al intermetallic compound [18]. A metal or solid so lution acts as a plasticizer 
that inhibits crack propagation, and Nb 3Al acts as a reinforcing component, being the prima ry 
source of the strength of the two-phase material [19]. 
Powder metallurgy is a promising method for creatin g such alloys, combining the 
advantages of each of the components. This technolo gy allows for adjusting, within controlled 
limits, not only the initial dimensions of the stru ctural components (refractory metal/Nb 3Al 
intermetallic compound) but also their volume ratio. For example, it has been reported [20] that a 
composite material consisting of Nb plates ( ∼500 µm, with 20 vol%) and Nb 3Al (125 µm, with 
80 vol%) exhibits a K1c value of >20 MPa ·m1/2 . Therefore, developing an optimal technology for 
Nb 3Al preparation may help to fully realize its properties in such two-phase materials. 
Induction and arc melting in a water-cooled crucibl e [21] are the primary methods for 
casting of Nb 3Al. However, it has been observed [7] that upon cry stallization of an alloy 
nominally corresponding to the Nb 3Al compound, Nb(Al), Nb 3Al, Nb 2Al, and NbAl 3 are present 
in the structure as a result of dendritic segregati on. To eliminate the consequences of segregation 
and homogenization of the chemical and phase compos itions, several remelts and prolonged 
high-temperature diffusion anneals are required. In addition, when melting Nb−Al alloys, special 
measures must be adopted to prevent and compensate for aluminum loss [21,22]. Powder-based 
preparation methods include self-propagating high-t emperature synthesis from elemental Al and 
Nb [23–27], mechanical alloying [14–16,28–31], and reactive sintering [32,33]. However, 
materials produced using these technologies is char acterized by significant chemical and phase 
heterogeneity, especially for large batches. 
Aluminum and sodium thermal reduction were also con sidered as a method for obtaining 
the Nb 3Al intermetallic compound. After an aluminothermic reduction (Nb 2O5 + Al), an ingot 
with a high oxygen content and chemical inhomogenei ty along its height was formed [34]. 
Sodium thermal production of Nb 3Al consists in the joint reduction of NbCl 5 and AlCl 3 chlorides 
with sodium. In this case, NbCl 5 and AlCl 3 are preliminarily dissolved in molten salts of LiC l, 
KCl, NaCl, and CaCl 2 [35]. However, this technology requires multiple o perations and is time-
consuming, and requires working with toxic gaseous chlorine in the process of producing Nb and 
Al chlorides. In addition, NbCl 5 and AlCl 3 have high vapor elasticity, which virtually elimin ates 
direct synthesis by the reaction 3NbCl 5 + AlCl 3 + 18Na → Nb 3Al + 18NaCl without dissolving 
the starting components in intermediate salts of LiCl–KCl–NaCl–CaCl 2 systems. 
A promising technology for producing alloys and int ermetallics is calcium hydride 
synthesis which already have been used by authors o f this work for obtaining binary (Nb 3Al 
[36], TiNi [37], TiAl–Nb–Cr [38], Ni 3Al [39], and Zr 3Al 2 [40]) and ternary (Ti–Ni–Hf [41], Ti–
Nb–Zr [42,43]) compounds with high perfection of cr ystal structure and homogeneous 
distributions of components within individual parti cles [39]. The capabilities of the proposed 
method may be widened for producing multicomponent metallic materials such as high-entropy 
alloys [44–46] of the heat-resistant class HfNbTaTiZr [47–49]. 
 The aim of the present work is to evaluate the opt imal conditions for calcium hydride 
synthesis and consolidation of Nb 3Al-based alloys and to determine the high-temperatu re 
mechanical properties of the fabricated materials.

2. Experimental 
 For calcium hydride synthesis, a reaction mixture consisting of Nb 2O5 (of 99.5% purity) 
and Al 2O3 (of 98.2% purity) oxides along with grounded calci um hydride (CaH 2) (of 93.0% 
purity) was prepared. The mixture was stirred in a rotary mixer until a uniform state of Nb 2O5 + 
Al 2O3 + CaH 2 was achieved. The aluminum content in the alloy wa s controlled by changing the 
amount of Al 2O3 in the initial mixture. 
 The reaction mixture was loaded into a container w ith a diameter of 0.315 m made of 
heat-resistant chromium–nickel steel and then compa cted. The container was then closed with a 
lid and a vacuum was created to a residual gas pressure of 1.33 Pa. After that, argon (of 99.996% 
purity) was applied to an excessive pressure of 1.2 2 × 10 5 Pa. The reduction operation was 
conducted in a shaft electric furnace for 12 h at a temperature of 1473 K. 
 Calcium oxide was removed from the reduction react ion products (Nb 3Al + CaO) using a 
hydrometallurgical treatment. The resulting sinter was crushed and loaded into a container filled 
with water. After slaking, hydrochloric acid was ad ded to the solution. The acid treatment was 
performed at a pH value of ≤ 2–3. Water was then added to the purified calcium oxide product to 
reach a neutral pH value. Finally, the powder was c entrifuged and dried in a vacuum oven at 
333 K. 
 Cold hydrostatic pressing was performed at room te mperature on a CIP62330 press 
(Avure Technologies, USA) under a pressure of 200 M Pa and a dwelling time of 3 min. A 
portion of the powder was 900 g. Vacuum sintering w as done in a shaft electric furnace as the 
temperature varied from 1563 to 2173 K with a heati ng rate 10 K/min and 3 h of dwelling time. 
The vacuum was 0.13–0.013 Pa. 
 Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectro scopy was used for the determination 
of the chemical composition of sintered samples wit h an Optima 4200DV spectrometer (Perkin 
Elmer, USA). The absolute error in the aluminum det ermination in the compact samples was 
±0.33 wt%. Gas analysis was performed on a TC-600 a nalyzer (LECO, USA) for oxygen and 
nitrogen determination, a CS-400 analyzer (LECO, US A) for carbon determination, and a 
RHEN-602 analyzer (LECO, USA) for hydrogen determin ation. X-ray phase analysis was 
studied on a DRON-3 (Burevestnik, Russia) automated diffractometer using monochromatic 
CuK 
α radiation with a wavelength of λ = 1.54178 Å. A software package was used for 
processing the data [50]. The relative error in det ermining the volume fractions of phases was 
5% vol% and that of the lattice periods was ∆a/a = 0.0015. 
 For structure and composition investigations, an o ptical microscope (Axio Observer 
D1m, Zeiss, USA) and a scanning electron microscope (VEGA, Tescan Analytics, France) with 
a LaB 6 cathode and an energy-dispersive microanalysis sys tem (Advanced AZtecEnergy, 
including an INCA Energy 350/X-max 50, Oxford Instr uments, UK) were used. Diameter of the 
electron probe in EDS microanalysis was 0.6 µm. For identification of the microstructure, an 
etchant of the following composition was used: 3 pa rts HF + 2 parts HNO 3 + 4 parts H 2O (by 
volume). The densities of the sintered samples were determined by hydrostatic weighing 
according to ISO 2738-87. 
 The mechanical properties of bulk samples at high temperatures were evaluated at the 
Gleeble System 3800 multifunctional test complex. E xternally mounted sensors were used for 
precise and simultaneous recording of dimensional c hanges. The samples were heated via direct 
transmission of electric current. For temperature c ontrol, thermocouples were welded directly to 
the sample surface. All tests were conducted under a 0.01 Pa vacuum. 
 Cylindrical samples for the uniaxial compression t ests were 9 mm height and 6 mm in

Observed decrease in the Al concentration in the alloys 1 and 2 (Figure 2) is similar to the 
that for a vacuum sintering of the Ti – 6Al – 4V po wder alloy (wt%) [55] or FeAl intermetallic 
compound (25 wt% Al) [56]. In these works, the evap oration of aluminum was explained based 
on the Langmuir theory. The authors of [55, 56] sho wed that, under conditions of high 
temperatures and vacuum, aluminum in the alloys is characterized by a rather high partial 
pressure. As a result, it has a great tendency to e vaporate. Most likely, the same phenomenon is 
realized for the Nb 3Al intermetallic compound. In addition, it was show n in [22] that vacuum 
heating of Nb 3Al-based alloys to the temperatures higher than 0.8 ·Tm is accompanied by a 
decrease in mass due to Al evaporation. 
Higher aluminum content shifts the temperature at w hich the Nb(Al) solid solution 
appears to higher values during sintering of alloy 2. An insignificant amount of Nb(Al) solid 
solution (<5 vol%) was determined for samples sinte red at temperatures above 1873 K while 
below this temperature a single-phase material was formed. Disappearance of the Nb 2Al phase 
was also observed. It worth noting that high-temper ature vacuum sintering leads to the complete 
elimination of the hydrogen-containing phase based on Nb 3Al.

By using the values of n and Q in Equation (1), one can estimate the metal flow 
mechanisms during creep [59,60]. For example, if n = 1, purely diffusive creep occurs; for n > 2, 
deformation mechanisms are associated with dislocat ion motion; for n = 2, the creep is 
controlled by sliding screw dislocations. In our ca se, the exponent n is close to 2; therefore, it 
can be expected that, in the temperature and stress ranges of 1373–1613 K and 150–350 MPa, 
creep during strain will be determined by sliding screw dislocations. 
It is typical for high-temperature creep that its a ctivation energy in the steady-state stage 
is close to the activation energy of self-diffusion (heterodiffusion for the case of intermetallics). 
The activation energy of heterodiffusion of Nb 3Al is 366 kJ/mol [61]. According to model (2), Q 
for the calcium hydrated intermetalli с Nb 3Al compound is 445 kJ/mol. Possibly, such a small 
increase in the activation energy is due to additio nal hardening of the alloy 2 matrix by the 
dispersed Al 2O3 particles (Fig. 4 (a)).

4 Conclusions 
 
 
1 Co-reduction of calcium hydride of Nb 
2O5 and Al 2O3 oxides using a metallothermic method at 
a temperature of 1473 K produced Nb 3Al-based powder alloys. During the synthesis, an 
intermetallic phase saturated with hydrogen formed a small amount of Nb 3Al(H) solid solution 
and Nb 3AlH x hydrides, which were completely removed after sintering. 
2 Dense Nb 3Al samples with a porosity level of < 5% were obtai ned by combining cold 
hydrostatic pressing and vacuum pressureless sinter ing. The sintering temperature should be at 
least 1873 K to maintain single-phase composition. It was shown that, during the vacuum 
sintering process, Al evaporation actively develops ; this can be compensated for by using 
powder with higher Al content as a starting material. 
3 The mechanical properties of dense Nb 3Al powder alloy are not inferior to the analog mate rial 
obtained by casting. It was established that, for a temperature of 1533 K and an external stress of 
350 MPa, the steady-state creep rate was 6.77 × 10 −5 s−1; at a homological temperature of 0.67 Tm 
and a strain rate of 10 −3 s −1, the compressive yield strength was ∼430 MPa and the tensile 
strength was ∼160 MPa. 
4 Because of the high brittleness of the Nb 3Al intermetallic compound, even at temperatures of 
> 0.5 Tm it cannot be independently used as a heat-resistan t structural material. However, because 
of its high strength, Nb 3Al is a promising reinforcing additive in composite materials such as 
solid solutions based on a refractory metal plus Nb3Al intermetallic compound.
