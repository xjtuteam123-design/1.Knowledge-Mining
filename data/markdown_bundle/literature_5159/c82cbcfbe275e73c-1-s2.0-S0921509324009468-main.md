ABSTRACT
In this study, we investigated the microstructural features and mechanical behavior of a forged CoCrFe-
NiAl
0.5
Ti
0.1
high-entropy alloy (HEA) rod with a 21 mm diameter. Analysis shows that the alloy comprises of a
face-centered cubic (FCC) matrix with Ni
2
AlTi precipitates. Significantly, the Ni
2
AlTi precipitates demonstrate a
modulus of approximately 162 GPa and a hardness of around 720 HV, markedly higher than the FCC matrix ’ s
modulus of 151 GPa and hardness of 425 HV. Mechanical testing reveals pronounced temperature-dependent
behavior. At room temperature, the alloy exhibits a tensile strength of 1146 MPa and an elongation of 16.7
%. This strength rises to 1204 MPa with an 18.3 % elongation at 200 K, and further escalates to 1423 MPa with a
14.2 % elongation at 77 K, indicating superior mechanical properties at lower temperatures. Conversely, at
elevated temperatures, the strength decreases, maintaining above 700 MPa at 873 K but falling to about 160 MPa
at 1073 K. These findings highlight the significant impact of temperature on the mechanical performance of
CoCrFeNiAl
0.5
Ti
0.1
HEA, suggesting potential applications in varied environments.
1. Introduction
In the realm of high-entropy alloys (HEAs), the investigation of
mechanical properties remains a constant focus [ 1 – 9 ]. Notably, while
FCC HEAs are known for their excellent plasticity, they often display
inadequate strength [ 10 – 13 ]. This research employs a dual approach to
overcome this limitation: compositional innovation and processing
methodology.
In terms of composition, this strategy is akin to that used for tradi-
tional ultra-high-strength steels. By integrating specific elements such as
Al [ 14 – 18 ] and Ti [ 19 – 22 ], the formation of precipitate phases is
encouraged, markedly enhancing the mechanical properties of the alloy.
Al has been increasingly recognized as an effective stabilizer for the
body-centered cubic (BCC) structure in HEA [ 18 , 23 , 24 ], as demon-
strated in CoCrFeNiAl
0.3
HEAs [ 25 – 29 ]. The mechanical properties are
superior to those of the cast CoCrFeNi HEA, though further improve-
ments are possible. The addition of Ti tends to promote the formation of
Laves or σ phases, substantially boosting the strength [ 30 – 33 ].
Consequently, by enhancing the mixentropy values of the alloy, the
content of Al and Ti in the CoCrFeNi HEA can also be increased [ 34 ].
Optimizing the precipitate phases and volume fraction in CoCrFeNi HEA
can further improve mechanical properties [ 35 , 36 ].
The high configurational entropy [ 37 , 38 ], also referred to as mixing
entropy [ 39 , 40 ], markedly improves the flexibility in atomic-level
design and broadens the combinatorial space of HEAs, enabling the
development of unique microstructures [ 41 ]. These include intrinsic
lattice distortions [ 42 , 43 ], short-range orderings [ 44 , 45 ], and hetero-
structures [ 46 , 47 ]. Such microstructural features are crucial in endow-
ing HEAs with a range of distinctive properties [ 48 ]. These properties
include a combination of high strength and extensive ductility [ 49 ],
superior corrosion resistance [ 50 , 51 ], and creep resistance [ 52 ].
Importantly, through careful and optimized alloy design, it is possible to
engineer a single HEA that simultaneously exhibits multiple of these
beneficial characteristics.

the mixing entropy of the CoCrFeNi HEA [ 53 ], which is a key factor in

conducted under ambient conditions. For high-temperature tests at 873
K, 1073 K, and 1273 K, the samples were heated by convective heat
transfer from an induction-heated stainless steel susceptor at a rate of 1
K/s. Once the desired temperature was achieved, it was maintained for
15 min to allow the material to equilibrate, thereby ensuring consistent
and accurate mechanical testing results across all conditions. Post-
testing, the elongation of the gage length of each sample was
measured, and engineering stress-strain curves were derived from the
load-displacement data. Yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, and
elongation to failure were determined from the uniaxial tensile
stress – strain curves.
The modulus and hardness of HEA matrix and precipitate phases
were obtained using a three-dimensional optical surface profilometer,
model Contour GT-K1. The samples were initially sanded with SiC paper
up to 2000 grit to ensure a smooth surface. Then samples were polished
with a polishing machine using a sequence of decreasing abrasive sizes
until the surface was scratch-free. The polished samples were cleaned
with ethanol and dried to remove any debris or contaminants. For
measurement, the samples were placed on the stage of the Contour GT-
K1 optical surface profilometer. This profilometer used white light
interferometry to generate three-dimensional surface topography maps.
Modulus and hardness were determined by analyzing these surface
topography data, focusing on the indents made by nanoindentation on
the sample surface. The values were extracted from the topography
maps by identifying and analyzing these indents. The data were pro-
cessed using the profilometer ’ s software to calculate modulus and
hardness based on the observed deformation characteristics in the sur-
face maps.

The criteria based on VEC possess inherent limitations, particularly
in complex systems involving mid d-block transition and p-block ele-
ments. The simplistic approach of VEC fails to capture the complexities
of crystal structure formation in ordered compounds, where electron
configuration and chemical bonding are crucial. Additionally, configu-
rational entropy, calculated using the Boltzmann equation, assumes a
random atom distribution, generally only applicable in liquid states or
high-temperature solid solutions. HEAs often display short-range
ordering, influenced by minor temperature changes or chemical parti-
tioning, which significantly alters entropy calculations. This deviation
from ideal randomness can result in inaccuracies in predicting phase
stability and microstructural evolution based solely on configurational
entropy. To navigate these complexities, while basic estimates offer
preliminary insights, more sophisticated methods such as CALPHAD
[ 70 , 71 ] are advocated for a more precise and comprehensive analysis of
phase diagrams and microstructural predictions in HEAs.

achieving a tensile strength of 1423 MPa and an elongation of 14.2 %. As
the temperature decreases from 298 K to 200 K, the deformation
mechanism shifts from dislocation slip to a combination of dislocation
slip and nanotwinning, facilitated by the lower stacking fault energy in
the FCC matrix. The integration of nanotwinning with dislocation slip
enhances the alloy ’ s ductility. Nanotwins effectively accommodate
plastic deformation across various grain orientations and impede
dislocation movement, increasing fracture resistance. With further
cooling to 77 K, dislocation mobility significantly decreases, restricting
essential dislocation movement. Consequently, reduced thermal energy
impairs the alloy ’ s ability to strain through dislocation movement,
increasing brittleness and susceptibility to crack initiation and propa-
gation under stress, thereby reducing plasticity.

In the equation, Q represents the energy barrier that must be overcome
for dislocation motion; V is the activation volume; τ * denotes the
effective stress required to initiate dislocation movement; k is the
Boltzmann constant; and T is the absolute temperature.
It can be inferred that as the temperature decreases, the likelihood of
activating dislocation movement in HEAs also diminishes. This indicates
that at low temperatures, dislocation motion in the alloy during defor-
mation requires overcoming a larger energy barrier. Additionally, due to
the lower stacking fault energy of FCC structured HEAs, when disloca-
tions are difficult to move, the alloy is more likely to form twins and
continue to deform. Therefore, the deformation mechanism of HEAs
transitions from dislocation slip at room temperature to a combination
of dislocation slip and nanotwinning deformation at low temperatures.
This adaptation enhances both the strength and plasticity of HEAs,

5. Conclusions
The alloy composition was strategically designed, with Al and Ti
added to the CoCrFeNi alloy to facilitate the formation of Ni
2
AlTi pre-
cipitates within the material. This innovation resulted in a two-phase
microstructure, comprised of a FCC matrix and Ni
2
AlTi precipitates.
These precipitates were found to exhibit higher hardness and modulus
than the FCC matrix, enhancing the alloy ’ s mechanical properties.
In terms of mechanical properties, the CoCrFeNiAl
0.5
Ti
0.1
alloy
showed significant improvements, particularly at lower temperatures.
At 77 K, increases in tensile strength and ductility were observed,
highlighting its potential for applications in extreme cold conditions.
The identified strengthening mechanisms included particle shearing and
the Orowan bypass mechanism, primarily due to the presence of Ni
2
AlTi
precipitates.
The effects of temperature on the alloy ’ s behavior were also
explored. At elevated temperatures, the CoCrFeNiAl
0.5
Ti
0.1
alloy dis-
played improved plasticity and necking behavior, suggesting its suit-
ability for high-temperature applications.
In conclusion, the CoCrFeNiAl
0.5
Ti
0.1
HEA rod appears highly
promising for a wide range of applications requiring both high strength
and ductility. Future research could aim to optimize its plasticity while
retaining its desired mechanical properties, increasing its versatility for
various engineering applications.
