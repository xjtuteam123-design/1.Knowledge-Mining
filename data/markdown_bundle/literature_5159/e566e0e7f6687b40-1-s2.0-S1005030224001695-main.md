Although refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs) possess excellent softening resistance and thermal sta- 
bility at high temperatures, their practical application is often limited due to room temperature (RT) brit- 
tleness. In this work, we successfully achieved RT plasticization in a brittle (TaMoTi)92 Al8 RHEA via in situ 
forming heterogeneous structure (HS) with the doping of Zr. Different from the mainstream design con- 
cept of “soft solid solution matrices with hard intermetallic phases” proposed in the literature, the newly 
developed TaMoZrTiAl RHEA is featured by a hard disordered BCC phase embedded into a soft intermetal- 
lic B2 matrix. Such an HS leads to the remarkable strength–plasticity synergy in this alloy at RT, showing 
a large plasticity of > 20 %, associated with a high strength of > 2380 MPa. It was found that solid solu- 
tion strengthening and heterodeformation-induced strengthening caused by dislocation pile-ups at phase 
boundaries are responsible for the enhancement in the yield strength, while deformation-induced strain 
partition and the frequent operation of dislocation cross-slip substantially improve the work hardening 
capacity of alloy, thus enabling the high strength and good RT plasticity. In short, the current work not 
only reveals the micromechanisms of the inﬂuence of heterogeneous dual-phase structure on the RT me- 
chanical behaviour in RHEAs but also provides a useful strategy for plasticizing brittle RHEAs.

1. Introduction 
Refractory high-entropy alloys (RHEAs), as newly developed 
high-entropy alloys (HEAs) consisting of metallic elements with 
high melting points ( > 1600 °C), have attracted much attention 
over recent years due to their great potential as high-temperature 
structural materials [1] . However, despite the fact that some RHEAs 
with tensile ductility have been successfully designed and de- 
veloped based on IVB and VB group transition metal elements 
recently [ 2 , 3 ], the vast majority of reported RHEAs, particularly 
W/Cr/Mo elements-rich RHEAs, still have to face the awkward 
dilemma of room-temperature (RT) brittleness [4] , seriously weak- 
ening their competitiveness when compared with conventional su- 
peralloys. For example, the NbMoTaW RHEA with excellent soft- 
ening resistance at ultra-high temperatures exhibits only 2 % com- 
pressive strain at RT, owing to the extensive use of Mo and W with

brittle RHEAs is of great signiﬁcance for promoting their engineer-

Given this, some novel strategies have been proposed, includ- 
ing modifying microstructures [7] , doping small elements (such 
as B or C) [ 8 , 9 ], maximizing lattice distortion [10] , and promot- 
ing transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP) [11–13] . For example, 
Wang et al. [8] reported that the addition of 0.5 at.% B can lead 
to an increase in the ultimate compressive strain of the NbMoTaW 
RHEA from 1.5 % to 10 %, associated with a high fracture strength 
of > 1750 MPa. Inspired by the idea of metastable engineering in 
conventional alloys, Huang et al. [13] developed a metastable TaH- 
fZrTi RHEA by tailoring the stability of the constituent phases by 
reducing the Ta content and found that the body-centred cubic 
(BCC) phase in the Ta0.5 HfZrTi alloy is gradually transformed into 
the hexagonal close-packed (HCP) phase with increasing strain. As 
a result, the tensile uniform strain of the Ta0.5 HfZrTi alloy increases

gies still seem like a drop in the bucket in the face of a vast ar-

tion of various brittle phases, especially at grain boundaries (GBs) 
[ 9 , 15 ]. Nevertheless, these fruitful research results above are com- 
mendable, and they encourage us to explore new strategies. 
Recently, it has been proven that constructing a heterogeneous 
structure (HS) composed of soft and hard regions into metal mate- 
rials is a promising solution for improving mechanical properties 
[16–18] . It has been reported that in such structures, geometri- 
cally necessary dislocations (GNDs) are expected to generate and 
accumulate at heterointerfaces due to deformation incompatibil- 
ity between soft and hard regions, which results in strain harden- 
ing, known as heterodeformation-induced (HDI) hardening. Bene- 
ﬁting from this hardening, different regions in alloys undergo plas- 
tic deformation successively [ 19 , 20 ]. Therefore, unlike the contin- 
uous strain localization in homogeneous metallic materials dur- 
ing deformation, the deformation process of heterogeneous ma- 
terials is usually considered a strain delocalization process [21] , 
which effectively delays the onset of necking, thus achieving syn- 
chronous improvements in strength and ductility. More impor- 
tantly, Liu et al. [22] successfully obtained super RT plasticity in 
ZrCuNiAl bulk metallic glasses using the HS strategy. The following 
question arises naturally. Can the HS strategy be applied to ini- 
tially brittle RHEAs, thereby achieving the improvement in the RT 
plasticity of alloys? However, due to the fact that engineering the 
HS in metals is often inseparable from complex thermomechani- 
cal processing routes, i.e., mechanical deformation and subsequent 
partial recrystallization treatment [20] , related studies have rarely 
been reported to date. 
In fact, some recent studies [23–27] may bring us a favourable 
inspiration, which shows that due to the occurrence of phase sep- 
aration during solidiﬁcation, the different phases in dual-phase 
RHEAs often show heterogeneity in composition, causing the ob- 
vious difference in the micro-hardness between phases [27] . This 
means that the dual-phase RHEAs with compositional heterogene- 
ity may be an ideal and natural HS material. Nevertheless, the ef- 
fect of such HS on the mechanical properties of brittle RHEAs at 
RT and its underlying mechanisms are still unclear. With the above 
outlook in mind, a (TaMoTi)92 Al8 RHEA with RT brittleness is cho- 
sen as the reference material, and Zr is carefully selected as a dop- 
ing element based on the following considerations. First, as shown 
in Fig. 1 , Zr has a positive mixing enthalpy ( /Delta1Hmix ) ( + 3 kJ/mol) 
with Ta, and there is a large negative /Delta1Hmix (–44 kJ/mol) between 
Zr and Al. Conventionally, phase separation originates mainly from 
the positive /Delta1Hmix between elements [28] , while a recent work 
[29] has suggested that the high negative /Delta1Hmix promotes the oc- 
currence of phase separation in HEAs. Consequently, it is reason- 
able to speculate that phase separation will be triggered in the Ta- 
MoZrTiAl system RHEA by combining the above features, in which 
one phase is enriched with Ta and another phase is enriched with 
Zr. Second, Zr has a larger atomic radius than the other four el- 
ements. According to the work of Tong et al. [30] , phase separa- 
tion appears to be more easily activated in RHEAs containing Zr 
due to severe local lattice distortions. Third, decreasing the valence 
electron concentration (VEC) has been widely proposed to be con- 
ducive to improving the RT ductility of RHEAs [ 31 , 32 ]. Thus, the 
addition of Zr with a low VEC is expected to play a positive role in 
controlling the VEC of the alloy. 
In summary, a TaMoZrTiAl RHEA with in situ forming heteroge- 
neous dual-phase structure is successfully prepared in the current 
study, and its RT compression behaviours are systematically stud- 
ied, focusing on the effects of the HS on the mechanical properties, 
damage mechanisms, and microstructural evolution. Additionally, 
the solidiﬁcation process is also analysed. It is expected that this 
work can provide a new approach to improving the RT brittleness 
of RHEAs. 
2. Experimental details 
2.1. Alloy design 
To quickly capture the appropriate Zr content, a thermodynamic 
calculation based on the calculation of phase diagrams (CALPHAD) 
approach was employed to predict all possible phases in the Zr- 
(TaMoTi)92 Al8 system RHEAs by using ThermoCalc software with 
a TCHEA4 database. Considering the complexity of the calculation, 
the Al content was ﬁxed at 8 at.% in this simulation. Fig. 2 (a) shows 
the calculated pseudobinary phase diagram of Zrx (TaMoTi)92–x Al8 
in the temperature range from 500 to 30 0 0 °C. Clearly, adding 
Zr can signiﬁcantly decrease both the liquidus and solidus tem- 
peratures. The diagram suggests that when the Zr content is less 
than 20 at.%, the phase composition of the alloy is mainly dom- 
inated by a single BCC phase or BCC + intermediate phases (in- 
cluding Sigma and/or a variety of Alx Zry -type phases with dif- 
ferent stoichiometries), depending on the solidiﬁcation tempera- 
ture. The occurrence of these brittle intermediate phases usually 
leads to a signiﬁcant decrease in the ductility of the alloy [33] ; 
importantly, the formation of only a single BCC solid solution 
phase is inconsistent with the original intention of the alloy de- 
sign. Interestingly, when the Zr content exceeds 22 at.%, a quasi- 
peritectic reaction similar to that reported in other Zr-containing 
HEAs [ 34 , 35 ] (L + BCC1→ BCC1 + BCC2) can be visibly observed at 
approximately 1200 °C. This ﬁnding suggests that a dual-phase 
structure (BCC1 + BCC2) can be obtained at high Zr contents ( > 
22 at.%). Additionally, an inopportune calculation result should be 
of concern; that is, Al x Zr y -type intermediate phases will gradually 
precipitate with decreasing temperature. Given this trend, the Zr 
content is ultimately anchored at 23 at.% because the atomic ratio 
among Ta, Mo, Zr, and Ti in this case is exactly 1:1:1:1, indicating 
that the alloy has the maximum mixing entropy ( /Delta1Smix ) [36] . It 
is anticipated that the high /Delta1Smix can overwhelm the enthalpy of 
compound formation and ultimately enable the formation of a sim- 
ple dual-phase solid solution structure in the Zr23 Ta23 Mo23 Ti23 Al8 
RHEA.

2.2. Synthesis of RHEAs 
(TaMoTi)92 Al8 and Zr23 Ta23 Mo23 Ti23 Al8 RHEA ingots were fabri- 
cated by Ti-gettered vacuum arc melting with high-purity Ta, Mo, 
Zr, Ti, and Al (all over 99.9 wt.% purity) and solidiﬁed in a water- 
cooled copper crucible. For convenience, the alloys will be labeled 
according to their Zr content, referred to as the 0Zr and 23Zr al- 
loys, respectively. To ensure a homogeneous distribution of ele- 
ments, the button ingots of each alloy were ﬂipped and remelted 
at least ﬁve times. After production, all specimens for further ex- 
periments or characterization were cut from as-cast ingots by elec- 
tric spark cutting, mechanically ground and polished, and ultrason- 
ically cleaned in anhydrous ethanol.

2.3. Physical characterizations 
The crystal structures of the as-fabricated samples were iden- 
tiﬁed using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Bruker D8 Advance) 
with Cu- K α radiation and scanning in the range of 20 °–90 °. The 
microstructural morphologies and the chemical compositions of 
the alloys were examined using a scanning electron microscope 
(SEM; Supra 55) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spec- 
troscopy (EDS) and electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) system. 
Note that the EBSD patterns obtained were indexed and analysed 
using the commercially available HKL Channel 5 software. In ad- 
dition, a scanning transmission electron microscope (STEM; Ta- 
los F200X) was used to assess the interface relationships between 
phases. The TEM foils were prepared by ion milling. 
To reveal the differences in mechanical properties between the 
constituent phases in the 23Zr alloy, forty-nine hardness tests were 
performed on the surface of the sample by a nanoindentation 
tester (Agilent 200) with a loading force of 20 mN and a dwell 
time of 15 s, and the distance between two adjacent indents was 
approximately 15 μm. The compression tests were conducted at 
RT in the air using a Shimadzu AGS-X testing machine with a 
strain rate of 1 × 10–3 s–1 and a cube specimen with a size of 
4 mm × 4 mm × 6 mm. For subsequent characterization, the spec- 
imen surfaces parallel to the axis were further vibrationally pol- 
ished using a colloidal silica oxide polishing suspension to obtain 
a scratch-free mirror surface before compression testing. Further- 
more, loading–unloading–reloading (LUR) compression tests under 
the same conditions as those of monotonic tests were conducted 
to evaluate heterodeformation-induced (HDI) stress. In addition, in- 
terrupted compression tests for each alloy were conducted to char- 
acterize the microstructure evolution during deformation. It should 
be noted that in the current study, the compressive strain was only 
measured by the cross-head displacement. After the compression 
tests, the surface deformation morphologies, damage features, and 
fractographies the of specimens were examined by SEM, EDS, and 
EBSD. The deformed microstructures at different strains were ob- 
served by an FEI Tecnai G2 F20 TEM operated at 200 kV.

Fig. 4 (a, c) illustrates the microstructures of the 0Zr and 23Zr 
alloys. As shown in Fig. 4 (a), the 0Zr alloy exhibits a typical cast 
dendrite structure, and no second phase is observed. It is known 
that the contrast of back-scattered-electron imaging is closely re- 
lated to the atomic number (Z-contrast), namely, a bright-contrast 
region usually holds a higher atomic number than a dark-contrast 
region. Accordingly, a distinct dendritic segregation behaviour can 
be observed in the 0Zr alloy. The corresponding EDS mapping 
( Fig. 4 (b)) shows that the dendritic region (DR) is rich in Ta and 
Mo with high melting points, while the interdendritic region (IR) 
is particularly rich in Ti and Al with low melting points (the de- 
tailed results of the elemental analyses can be found in Table 1 ). 
Clearly, the addition of Zr further strengthens element segrega- 
tion and leads to the formation of a composite-like microstruc- 
ture in the 23Zr alloy ( Fig. 4 (c)), mainly manifesting as bright ir- 
regular phases embedded into a grey matrix. Combining Fig. 4 (d) 
and Table 1 , it can be found that the bright-contrast phase is en- 
riched with Ta and Mo, whereas Zr and Al are enriched in the grey- 
contrast matrix. Ti shows a nearly uniform distribution, although a 
weak partitioning towards the latter is still visible. Based on the 
XRD and EDS results, it can be jointly concluded that the bright- 
contrast phase should be the BCC phase and that the matrix is the 
B2 phase.

3.2. Mechanical properties 
According to previous studies [ 19 , 20 ], heterogeneous mi- 
crostructures should be composed of microstructural features with 
signiﬁcant strength heterogeneity, which is the main reason they 
can induce strain partitioning, thus leading to GND accumulation 
and HDI strengthening. To this end, a nanoindentation test was 
performed on the 23Zr RHEA. Fig. 6 (a) presents the nanoinden- 
tation topographic image and the corresponding hardness map of 
the 23Zr alloy. The hardness distribution trend is clearly consis- 
tent with the phase distribution. In particular, the hardness of the 
bright regions, which belong to the BCC phases enriched in Ta and 
Mo, is obviously higher than that of the grey B2 matrix enriched in 
Zr and Al, demonstrating the mechanical incompatibility between 
the BCC phase (hard region) and B2 phase (soft region). Such 
heterogeneous mechanical behaviour can also be clearly observed 
from the load–displacement nanoindentation curves of the BCC 
and B2 phases, as evidenced in Fig. 6 (b). The statistical nanohard- 
ness and Young’s modulus values obtained from the nanoinden- 
tation tests are given in Fig. 6 (c). The nanoindentation hardness 
of the BCC phase varies between 6.42 and 7.53 GPa with an av- 
erage value of 7.13 ± 0.39 GPa, while the nanohardness for the 
B2 phase is only 6.14 ± 0.08 GPa. Moreover, Young’s modulus of 
the BCC phase is 192.24 ± 9.37 GPa, whereas the corresponding 
value for the B2 phase is 125.81 ± 3.92 GPa. In fact, the above re- 
sults are consistent with the fact that the BCC phase is signiﬁcantly 
enriched by harder and stiffer elements such as Ta and Mo. Con- 
sequently, considering the signiﬁcant differences in hardness and 
Young’s modulus between the BCC and B2 phases, the 23Zr alloy 
should be regarded as a heterogeneous material.

the river-like patterns strongly indicate that the rupture mode of

corresponding to the dimples are enriched in Zr, Ti, and Al. Con-

are the B2 and BCC phases, respectively. Such a result is dramat-

for which the B2 phase is usually a brittle phase due to its ordered 
nature. Further discussions will be given in Section 4 . Accordingly, 
it is believed that the ductile B2 phase should play an essential 
role in improving the RT plasticity of 23Zr alloys.

3.5. Microstructure evolution 
To gain insight into the intrinsic correlations between the mi- 
crostructures and compressive properties in the 0Zr and 23Zr al- 
loys, the microstructures of deformed specimens at different in- 
terrupted strains are further examined by TEM, as represented in 
Fig. 10 . Clearly, at the early stage (5 %) of plastic deformation, the 
deformation mode of the 0Zr alloy is dominated by the planar slip 
of dislocations, as evidenced by the dislocation dipoles in linear 
arrays ( Fig. 10 (a)). It should be noted that although planar slip of 
dislocations is often believed to play a decisive role in improv- 
ing the ductility of FCC HEAs due to its strong resistance to dis- 
location annihilation [56] , it is considered an important factor in 
premature failure of BCC RHEAs, which is attributed to fast satu- 
ration in dislocation accumulations and high-stress concentrations 
inside the slip bands [57] . As the strain increases to 8 %, which 
is close to the fracture strain of the alloy, the dislocation density 
increases, and the development of planar slip bands is further im- 
proved ( Fig. 10 (b)). In addition, dislocation pileups against the GBs 
are observed ( Fig. 10 (c)), which would generate high stress/strain 
concentrations and microcrack initiation. 
In the case of the 23Zr alloy, a strain delocalization behaviour 
can be found. Speciﬁcally, dislocation activation can be clearly ob- 
served in the B2 phase at 10 % strain, whereas there are almost 
no visible dislocations in the BCC ( Fig. 10 (d)); that is, the initial 
plastic strain is undertaken by the B2 phase, and the BCC phase 
remains mostly elastic. Note that, unlike the planar slip of the dis- 
locations, the dislocation slip in the B2 phase presents a typical 
wavy-shaped feature [58] , indicating that the wavy slip of the dis- 
locations is the dominant deformation mechanism of the B2 phase. 
This may be attributed to two reasons: on the one hand, previous 
studies [ 59 , 60 ] indicated that positive mixing enthalpy between 
atomic pairs will lead to the repulsive interaction henceforth de- 
creasing the stacking fault energy (SFE) of the system and vice 
versa. For the current Ta-Mo-Zr-Ti-Al system, Ta-Ti and Ta-Zr have 
a positive mixing enthalpy thus helping decrease the SFE. However, 
due to the fact that the B2 phase in the 23Zr alloy is poor in the 
Ta element, it is reasonably inferred that the SFE of the B2 phase 
is higher than that of the 0Zr alloy, which promotes wavy slip of 
dislocations. On the other hand, the larger lattice distortion in the 
B2 phase may also play a positive role. As elaborated earlier [61] , 
the BCC lattice is not a close-packed structure, and dislocations can 
slip on different planes belonging to the [1 1 1] zone, such as the 
(0 1 1) and (1 1 2). Lattice distortion will increase the local lattice 
friction, thus forcing the dislocation to shift towards an easy slip 
path [62] . As such, cross-slip of dislocations is further promoted, 
leading to the formation of wavy-slip dislocation structures in the 
B2 phase.

Based on the above analysis and discussion, Fig. 11 (c) schemat- 
ically presents the solidiﬁcation process of the dual-phase struc- 
ture in the 23Zr alloy. It is well known that a high temperature 
can maintain the Gibbs free energy of the liquid alloy phase at a 
relatively low level during smelting; however, during the cooling 
process, the Gibbs free energy gradually increases with decreasing 
temperature, and the repulsive force between elements in the al- 
loy may lead to phase separation [69] . Therefore, when Zr is added 
to the TaMoTiAl alloy system, due to a positive /Delta1Hmix ( + 3 KJ/mol) 
between Ta and Zr, a substantial repulsive force is expected to be 
generated during the solidiﬁcation process, which causes the oc- 
currence of phase separation between Ta and Zr. As a result, Ta 
and Mo with high melting points tend to solidify ﬁrst to form the 
TaMo-rich BCC phase; meanwhile, Zr with the largest atomic ra- 
dius is inclined to form the concave rough solid–liquid interface 
[70] . Other alloying elements are absorbed when they elastically 
impact the interface and make the interface grow further. More- 
over, the concave rough interface receives alloying elements from 
the remaining liquid phase to form a Zr-rich area. Due to the low 
/Delta1Hmix (–44 KJ/mol) between Al and Zr, abundant Al enters the Zr- 
rich region and becomes enriched, thus causing the formation of 
the Zr-Al-rich region (B2 phase). Next, the remaining Ta, Mo, and 
other elements in the liquid phase adhere to the interface of the 
Zr-Al-rich region and continue to precipitate. As the temperature 
decreases, the Ta-Mo region is surrounded by the Zr-Al-rich region, 
ﬁnally resulting in the formation of a heterogeneous dual-phase 
structure. It should be noted that the melting point of Ti is inter- 
mediate between those of the other elements, and the /Delta1Hmix, Ti-Ta 
( + 1 KJ/mol) is very close to the /Delta1Hmix, Ti-Zr (0 KJ/mol); thus, Ti 
shows a relatively uniform distribution during the solidiﬁcation 
process.

Previous studies [ 14 , 18 ] have shown that the generation of HDI 
stress in heterogeneous materials mainly results from a strain gra- 
dient caused by strain partitioning between the hard and soft re- 
gions during deformation. Therefore, insight into strain partition- 
ing is important for understanding the strengthening-toughening 
mechanism of alloys. According to the work of Sinha et al. [71] , 
Fig. 12 shows the strain contouring maps for the 0Zr and 23Zr al- 
loys after failure. As shown in Fig. 12 (a), the 0Zr alloy displays a 
relatively homogeneous strain distribution; in contrast, strain par- 
titioning during deformation can be clearly observed in the 23Zr 
alloy, meaning that a strong strain gradient is established. Further- 
more, a high magniﬁcation image ( Fig. 12 (b)) reveals that plastic 
strain incompatibility between the B2 and BCC phases, i.e., strain 
partitioning between phases, is responsible for the occurrence of a 
strain gradient. The quantitative statistics of strain partitioning in 
the 0Zr and 23Zr alloys are given in Fig. 12 (c, d). Obviously, the 
23Zr alloy exhibits a heterogeneous strain behaviour between the 
B2 and BCC phases and a higher average value of maximum orien- 
tation than that of the 0Zr alloy. Consequently, plenty of GNDs are 
expected to generate near PBs to accommodate structural compati- 
bility, leading to HDI stress. As shown in Fig. 8 (c), the HDI stress of 
the 23Zr alloy increases rapidly in the early stage of deformation; 
moreover, the average KAM value of the B2 phase is greater than 
that of the BCC phase, indicating a high average density of GNDs 
( Fig. 9 (d2)). This should be closely related to the constrained effect 
from the hard BCC phase, which can strengthen the B2 phase by 
inducing the multiplication of GNDs in the B2 phase adjacent to 
PBs, as shown in Figs. 9 (d) and 10(d).

accommodate strain gradients between different phases. The pile-

dislocation emission and slip, assisting in the work hardening of

partitioning) during deformation. Hence, the following discussion 
is focused on the impacts of strain partitioning on the mechanical 
properties of the alloy. 
As shown in Fig. 10 (g), at the early plastic deformation stage, 
initial yielding occurs in the soft B2 phase, while the hard BCC 
phase remains elastic. In fact, the anomalous plasticity of the B2 
phase is surprising because it usually shows the primary ⟨ 1 0 0 ⟩ 
slip mode, which does not satisfy the von Mises criterion and re- 
quires ﬁve independent slips [81] . Recently, several studies [ 82 , 83 ]

Activating such hard slip mode should be related to the relaxation 
of long-range internal stress [84] resulting from the conﬁnement 
of rigid BCC phases. On the other hand, the multiprincipal ele- 
ment characteristics of the B2 phase in HEAs may be another rea- 
son. It has been shown that regardless of which conﬁguration the 
B2 phase has, the additional elements can reduce the antiphase 
boundary energy relative to that in the binary B2 phase, enhancing 
the capacity of plastic deformation [85] . Anyway, strain partition- 
ing between the B2/BCC phases results in the formation of GNDs 
in the soft B2 phases near PBs to accommodate the strain gradient 
between phases, which contributes a directional long-range back 
stress in the soft B2 phase and strengthens it as hard BCC phase 
[32] . 
With increasing strain, the soft B2 phase carries more plastic 
strain than the BCC phase; thus, the strain partitioning further in- 
creases. As a result, more GNDs are needed to accommodate the 
present strain gradient, further contributing to the increase in the 
HDI stress. In addition to enhancing the ﬂow stress of the alloy, 
increasing HDI stress can promote the operation of an increased 
number of slip systems in the B2 phase [19] , leading to the forma- 
tion of dislocation cells, dislocation tangles, and dislocation loops 
( Fig. 10 (g)). For BCC RHEAs, these dislocation substructures origi- 
nating from cross-slip have been conﬁrmed to not only effectively 
alleviate local stress concentration on the slip surface but also 
promote dislocation nucleation and proliferation through double 
cross-slip and the formation of new Frank–Read sources [57] . This 
results in a high work hardening capacity, as shown in Fig. 7 (b). 
On the other hand, the increasing HDI stress exerts stress concen- 
trations on the PBs, which consequently generate forward stress 
at the front of the hard BCC phase. In this case, the localized parti- 
tioned stress favours the activation of dislocations in the BCC phase 
once it reaches a critical level, thereby releasing the strain localiza- 
tion in the hard BCC phase. This may be a reason for inducing the 
work hardening rate recovery in the 23Zr alloy ( Fig. 7 (b)). Finally, 
with increasing strain, both the BCC and B2 phases deform plasti- 
cally, while strain partitioning consistently exists since the soft B2 
phase always undertakes higher plastic strain. 
It should be mentioned that the coherent interfaces in the 23Zr 
alloy also have a positive effect in improving the properties of the 
alloy to some extent. Compared with the incoherent interface, the 
atomic arrangement on the coherent interface is highly ordered, 
which is beneﬁcial for reducing the interface energy. On the one 
hand, the low interface energy can facilitate the interaction of dis- 
locations near the interface, thus improving the strength [86] . On 
the other hand, the small mismatch is conducive to coordinating 
the two-phase lattice constants and ensuring the bonding stability 
of the interface [87] , which effectively reduces the risk of interface 
cracking and provides sustainable conditions for HDI strengthening 
and hardening. 
In summary, this study strongly demonstrates that the con- 
struction of in situ HS in the 23Zr alloy produces prominent 
HDI strengthening and hardening during deformation, which con- 
tributes to high work hardening capacity and decent RT plastic- 
ity. This result provides a new paradigm for achieving strength–
plasticity synergy at RT for initially brittle RHEAs. 
5. Conclusions 
Based on the experimental results and discussion above, the 
main conclusions were summarized as follows.

(1) By employing the phase separation property and the 
CALPHAD approach, we successfully prepared a novel 
Ta23 Mo23 Zr23 Ti23 Al8 (23Zr) RHEA with in situ forming HS 
consisting of a ductile (Zr, Al)-rich B2 matrix and a hard (Ta, 
Mo)-rich BCC phase, which effectively solves the dilemma that 
it was diﬃcult to obtain HS in the brittle RHEAs by traditional 
thermomechanical processing routes. 
(2) The resulting HS gives the 23Zr alloy an excellent match be- 
tween strength and RT plasticity. Compared with the basic 0Zr 
alloy, the yield strength and fracture strength of the 23Zr al- 
loy increase by nearly 1.58 and 1.70 times, respectively. Impor- 
tantly, the RT plasticity is signiﬁcantly improved from ⟨ 8% to 
⟩ 20%, with an increase of nearly 150 %, indicating the effec- 
tiveness of the HS strategy in improving the RT brittleness of 
RHEAs. 
(3) Due to the plastic strain incompatibility between the B2 and 
BCC phases during deformation, a large number of GNDs are 
formed. As a consequence, the strong strain partitioning be- 
tween the B2 and BCC phases not only leads to the frequent 
activation of cross-slips in the B2 phase but also promotes the 
activation of dislocations in the hard BCC phase. Consequently, 
sustainable strong HDI hardening is acquired, increasing the 
work hardening capacity and consequently RT plasticity of the 
23Zr alloy. 
(4) This study provided a fundamental understanding of the defor- 
mation mechanisms of RHEAs with heterogeneous dual-phase 
structures and offered a useful strategy for the plasticization of 
brittle RHEAs.
