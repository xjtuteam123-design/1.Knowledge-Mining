ABSTRACT
The eﬀ ects of carbon content on the microstructure and room-temperature mechanical properties of
Fe40Mn40Co10Cr10 high-entropy alloy (HEA) were systematically investigated. The results showed that heavy
carbon alloyed HEA could possess supreme combination of high tensile strength (935 MPa) and high ductility (~
74%). The excellent mechanical properties were ascribed to as follows: the high content interstitial carbon atoms
strengthens the matrix greatly through suppressing dislocation motion and promoting the deformation-induced
twinning at room temperature, which enhance the strength and ductility. Simultaneously, the ductility is further
secured for single FCC structure maintained due to appropriate carbon alloying. Our ﬁndings provide a novel
strategy for developing HEAs with excellent mechanical properties.
1. Introduction
High-entropy alloys (HEAs) have attracted tremendous attention
due to their potential application as high performance structural ma-
terials [1–4]. The face-centered cubic (FCC) structured HEAs is one of
the most developed alloys, which have good plasticity and excellent
cryogenic temperature fracture toughness [1,5–7]. However, their room
temperature strength is relatively low (only about 200 MPa in the cast
state) [8,9] and far lower than the required strength of metallic struc-
tural materials. The shortcoming of low strength hinders their appli-
cation as a practical structural material. The strengthening mechanisms
of traditional metal such as ﬁne grain strengthening [10,11] and pre-
cipitation strengthening [12], have been applied to improve the
strength of FCC-structured HEAs. However, above strengthened alloys
were also su ﬀered the deterioration of plasticity [10,12], followed the
strength–ductility trade-o ﬀ dilemma. Recently, interstitial strength-
ening o ﬀers another route to achieve improvements in mechanical
properties [8,9,13,14]. For examples, 0.5 at% C was added by Wu et al.
[8] into FeCrNiCoMn HEA and thus increased its strength but, un-
fortunately also greatly decreased its plasticity. Wang et al. [9] in-
vestigated the e ﬀect of interstitial carbon on the mechanical properties
of Fe
40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 HEA and found that both strength and
plasticity of the HEA were increased when up to 1.1 at% C was added in
the alloys. Regardless of the high potential for interstitial strengthening
in HEAs, the added interstitial element contents in FCC-structured HEAs
were usually low ( ＜1.3 at%) [8,9,13,15] and there was no other
reports whether or not more C content ( ＞1.1 at%) could further im-
prove the strength and plasticity of the Fe 40.4Ni11.3Mn34.8Al7.5Cr6 HEA
[9]. Moreover, transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP), twinning-in-
duced plasticity (TWIP) and mix of them [7,13,16], had also been in-
volved in HEAs. Not surprisingly, the above mentioned HEAs still dis-
play unsatisfactory room temperature strength despite of good ductility
[7,10].
Here, a FCC-structured twinning induced plasticity (TWIP)HEA with
a composition of Fe
40Mn40Co10Cr10(at%) was selected as the starting
material. Di ﬀerent amount of carbon contents (up to 8.9 at% i.e.
2.11 wt%) were added into this HEA and their mechanical properties
were systematically investigated. The results showed that the large
amount of interstitial carbon atoms could suppress dislocation motion
by enhancing the lattice friction stress, and further promote twinning
induced plasticity. Moreover, appropriate carbon addition prior to
carbide precipitation maintained the single FCC structure at room
temperature to ensure excellent ductility. Thereafter, a carbon alloyed
TWIP HEA with combination of high-strength and excellent tensile
ductility was developed.

3. Results and discussion
Fig. 1a showed the XRD patterns of the carbon free (un-alloyed) and
alloyed as cast HEAs. Apparently, all the HEAs clearly revealed a single-
phase FCC microstructure within the limit of XRD instrument
resolution. The lattice parameters which were determined by using an
extrapolation technique from the XRD data, increased linearly with
increasing carbon concentration up to 4.4 at% (see Fig. 1b), which
conﬁrmed that the large amount of C atoms could dissolve in the in-
terstices of the FCC lattice. According to the linear ﬁti n Fig. 1b, the
relative increment of lattice parameter ( Δa) with respect to the content
change of carbon in at% ( Δc), i.e. Δa/Δc, was obtained to be about
0.82 pm/at%. It produced a lattice strain per atomic fraction of carbon,
Δa/(Δc × a), of 0.23/at%. When the carbon content was above 4.4 at%,
the lattice parameter begins to decrease, indicating that carbon content
was beyond the solid solution limit in current HEA matrix and hence
carbides began to precipitate [17]. Similarly, carbon was soluble up to
4.5 at% in Fe-18Mn-1C (wt%) TWIP steels with FCC matrix [18].
Therefore, the solution limit of carbon in the present Fe
40Mn40Co10Cr10
HEA might be close to that in Fe-Mn based TWIP steels, which could be
attributed to the same crystal structure (FCC) with similar chemical
components (main Fe and Mn).

Back scattering electron observations further illustrated the single-
phase nature of these as cast HEAs, as shown in Fig. 2 a. All the HEAs
had average grain sizes of around 95 µm which was determined by the
linear intercept method, indicating that the addition of carbon had
negligible in ﬂuence on the grain size. Microstructure evolution in the
individual HEAs were distinguished with increasing the carbon content.
For the C0, C2.2 and C3.3 HEAs, no apparent carbides were found in the
microstructure. However, for the C4.4 HEAs, the trace of dendrites
could be identi ﬁed in the grains. Further increasing carbon content to
6.6 at% and 8.9 at%, more visible dendrites and carbides were de-
tected. The carbides were the type of M
23C6 determined by EDS analysis
(Fig. 2b). These visible dendrites and carbides might be useful to the
yield strength but harmful to the ductility.
The engineering tensile stress-strain curves for the as-cast
Fe40Mn40Cr10Co10 HEAs with di ﬀerent carbon contents were given in
Fig. 3a. The yield strength, ultimate tensile strength (UTS), fracture
elongation (EG) and reduction in area (RA) were measured and listed in
Table 1. Moreover, the Vickers hardness (HV) and product of UTS and
EG (UTS × EG) were also given in Table 1. The variations of yield
strength, tensile strength, hardness as well as EG with the carbon
content were plotted in Fig. 3b. It could be found that both the yield
strength and tensile strength increased with increasing carbon content
up to 8.9 at%. Interestingly, the yield strength increase linearly with the
carbon content. The increase in yield strength per at% carbon in the
HEA, of 62 MPa/at% C, was higher than carbon strengthening of about
42 MPa/at% C in TWIP steels [19]. In particularly, the yield strength
dramatically increased from 213MPafor the C0 HEA to 422 MPa for
C3.3, even 750 MPa for C8.9 HEA. Usually, the hardness was in pro-
portion to the yield strength. The results of hardness test further con-
ﬁrmed this linear relationship of yield strength and carbon content
because there also was a linear dependence between hardness and
carbon content. Then, it could be suggested that the carbon in these
HEAs could e ﬀectively enhance the yield strength in spite of its ex-
istence ways i.e. solid solution in matrix or in carbide precipitation.
For the plastic deformation ability, the EG increased from 57.7% to
77.8% when carbon content increased to 3.3 at%. With further in-
creasing carbon content (4.4, 6.6 or 8.9 at%), EG began to abruptly
decrease. For examples, the C4.4 had a reduced EG of 54.7% with
higher yield strength of 467 MPa and tensile strength of 836 MPa. Even,
C8.9 HEA had a much lower EG of 5.7% with a much higher yield
strength of 750 MPa and tensile strength of 955 MPa (near 1 GPa). This
deterioration of plasticity should be related the dendrites and carbides
in the microstructure.
Next, the macroscopic fracture mode and microscopic fracture fea-
ture of these tested samples were visited. The un-deformed specimens
as well as some typical fractured samples of C0, C3.3 and C4.4 HEAs
were shown in the Fig. 3c. Representative fracture surfaces for the C0,
C3.3, C4.4 and C8.9 HEAs were given in Fig. 4, where the values of EG
and RA at the necking were listed on the images. It could be found that
the C0 HEA had an obvious necking before ﬁnal shear fracture and C3.3
HEA had undertaken more uniform deformation and fractured in a
shear mode with the fracture angles of about 54°. While for the C4.4 it
failed at reduced elongation in a normal tensile mode (fracture angle =
90°) as shown in Fig. 3c. C6.6 and C8.9 HEAs also fractured in the
normal tensile mode. RA is also an indicator of plastic deformation
ability. Here, it was noticed that these RA values decreased with the
increase of carbon contents (see Table 1), which was di ﬀerent with that
of EG. This discrepancy between RA and EG might imply the change of
strain hardening behavior with the increase of carbon content. Ductile
dimples were exhibited in the fracture surface of C0, C3.3 and C4.4 HEA
and these dimple size decreased as the carbon content increased. In the
C4.4 HEA, dimple area only occupies about half of the fracture surface.
When carbon content was 8.9 at%, no dimple was found and only lo-
calized plastic ﬂow was evident on the facture surface, indicating a
brittle fracture, which was agreement with the deterioration of plasti-
city.
The change of fracture mode and RA among C0, C 2.2, C 3.3 and
C4.4 might be related with the strain hardening behavior. Therefore,
strain hardening rate (SHR) versus true strain of the un-alloyed and
some alloyed HEAs were given in Fig. 3d based on above engineering
stress-strain curves. The SHR of C0 HEA directly decreased with in-
creasing strain after yielding, which was consistent with the results in
Ref. [7]. However, the strain hardening behavior of the HEA with in-
creased carbon content was quite di ﬀerent with un-alloyed HEA. For
the C2.2 HEA with 2.2 at% C, its SHR kept constant before 15% true
strain was reached, and then decreased with increasing strain. With the
further increase of carbon content to 3.3 at%, the SHR ﬁrstly increased
and then decreased with strain. Here, the SHR increased from
1585 MPa to 2052 MPa as strain reached 25% whereas, the SHR de-
creased from 1708 MPa to 1082 MPa for the C0 HEA with same strain.
It was reported that the persistent formation of twins with straining
gave rise to a sustainable high SHR up to very large plastic strain in the
Fe-Mn-C
TWIP steels [18]. Present carbon alloyed HEAs had similar
composition with Fe-Mn-C TWIP steels. Therefore, their excellent strain
hardening capacity might be related with the good twin formation
ability by adding carbon.

lattice frictional stress in HEA [9], which further strengthen the HEA
matrix and increase its yield strength. After this, deformation twinning,
by continually introducing new interfaces and thus decreasing the mean
free path of dislocations during tensile testing ( “dynamic Hall –Petch
eﬀect”), could enhance the SHR to postpone necking and obtain ex-
cellent fracture elongation and high ultimate tensile strength [7,10,18].
4. Conclusions
In summary, carbon alloying e ﬀects on the microstructure and
mechanical property of Fe
40Mn40Co10Cr10 high entropy alloy were
systematically investigated. Heavy carbon alloying (about 3.3 at%) si-
multaneously increased the yield strength, ultimate tensile stre ngths as
well as the plasticity. This was attributed to that the interstitial carbon
atoms suppressed dislocation motion and promoted the deformation-
induced twinning in the HEAs. To the best of our knowledge, the
comprehensive mechanical properties of the as-cast and re-processed
(Fe
40Mn40Co10Cr10)96.7C3.3 HEAs are superior to the most proposed
HEAs and metallic materials. These ﬁndings provide a novel strategy for
developing high-entropy alloys with excellent combination of strength
and ductility.
