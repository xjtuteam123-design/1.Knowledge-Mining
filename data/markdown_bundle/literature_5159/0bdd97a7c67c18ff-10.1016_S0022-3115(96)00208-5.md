Abstract 
Two heavily cold-worked dispersion strengthened copper alloys, GlidCop”’ CuA125 and GlidCop”‘-Nb, were irradiated 
at three different conditions to study their response to high temperature neutron irradiation. The results revealed that the 
GlidCop”‘-Nb possesses a greater resistance to irradiation-induced changes in the mechanical properties than GlidCop”’ 
CuAl25. Although the CuAl25 exhibited significant decreases in ultimate strength and increases in elongation, the minimum 
strength was still higher than that of unirradiated as-wrought CuA125. For all of the alloys the changes in mechanical 
properties were independent of displacement rate and temperature. 
1. Introduction 
The first wall and divertor assemblies of ITER (intema- 
tional thermonuclear experimental reactor) require a mate- 
rial with a high thermal conductivity and good swelling 
resistance to act as a heat sink layer between the beryllium 
plasma-facing armor and the stainless steel. The high 
thermal loading of the divertor assembly (5 MW/m* 
maximum steady state) adds an additional requirement of 
good mechanical properties, which is not necessary for the 
first wall given the much lower heat flux of 0.5 MW/m* 
at steady state [I]. 
The dispersion strengthened CDS) GlidCop’” copper 
alloys have been selected over precipitation hardened Cu- 
NiBe and CuCrZr as the main candidate for the heat sink 
material. 
Experiments over the past decade have repeatedly 
proven GlidCop”’ CuA125 to be the most swelling resis- 
tant alloy and to retain the largest fraction of its original 
strength after neutron irradiation at high temperatures. 
Additionally, DS copper has the advantage of possessing a 
high electrical and thermal conductivity that is independent 
of thermomechanical processing, a characteristic not shared 
by the precipitation hardened alloys.

3.2. Irradiated 
Tensile curves for the irradiated GlidCop”‘-Nb (Heat 
B) are compared in Fig. 3 with those of the unirradiated 
GlidCop”‘-Nb. These alloys proved to be very resistant to 
changes in mechanical properties. The irradiated speci- 
mens exhibited slightly more strain hardening, which is 
accompanied by a lower yield strength. Although the 
tensile curves suggest that the total elongation increases 
with dose, the differences in the elongation values of the 
three curves are not considered significant. 
Half of the GlidCop”‘-Nb Heat A specimens irradiated 
to 35 dpa at 706 K exhibited an unusual change in the 
elastic region of the tensile curve, resulting in a lower 
yield strength than that measured for the other Heat A 
specimens. The UTS was unaffected by this change. The 
data for these specimens is presented in Fig. 1 along with 
that of the specimens exhibiting more typical tensile curves. 
The slope of the elastic region of the tensile curve for 
these specimens began decreasing at a stress level - 100 
MPa below the yield stress of the other specimens. The 
slope reached a constant value that did not change until it 
reached a stress level near the ultimate stress. At that point 
the tensile curve followed the behavior of the other speci- 
mens, giving an identical ultimate tensile stress within 
experimental error. The reason for the sudden change in 
slope could not be determined, and did not occur in 
consecutively tested specimens. 
Unlike the GlidCop”‘-Nb alloys, the CW CuAl25 
proved less resistant to changes in the strength after high 
temperature neutron irradiation. Irradiation removed the 
slight differences in strength due to rolling direction. and 
produced an overall drop in both the yield and the ultimate 
strength (Fig. I). Whereas the ultimate strength of the 
GlidCop”‘-Nb alloy5 was unaffected by irradiation, the 
ultimate strength of the CW CuAl25 dropped - I50 MPa 
after irradiation, with an accompanying increase in elonga- 
tion as shown in Fig. 4. The magnitude of the decrease in 
the UTS was independent of both the irradiation dose and 
temperature. Although the same behavior was observed for 
the yield strength. the magnitude of the decrease in yield 
strength after irradiation was much less than that observed 
in the ultimate strength. Another feature in the tensile 
curves is that the specimens irradiated to IX and 35 dpa 
exhibited the yield plateau observed in an earlier study bl 
Anderson et al. [3] and Edwards et al. [4.5]. The cause for 
this phenomenon has yet to be determined. 
Fractography on both the GlidCop”‘-Nb and the CuAl25 
alloys revealed ductile failure by microvoid coalescence in 
both the unirradiated and irradiated specimens. As ob- 
served by Anderson et al. [3] and Edwards et al. L4.51, 
several cu-AlzO, inclusions were present inside the din- 
plex in the unirradiated specimens. but after irradiation 
they were no longer visible. Transmission electron m- 
croacopy revealed that ballistic dissolution caused small 
precipitates of q’-AlZ03 to form around the inclusions. 
possibly preventing defonnation voids from forming at the 
interface between the inclusions and the matrix [4.5].

4. Discussion 
DS copper alloys are considered to be extremely stable 
at high temperatures for short term thermal exposures (< I 
h). However, after long term exposure to neutron irradia- 
tion at temperatures near 0.57,,,, the strength of GlidCop”’ 
CuAl25 may decrease significantly as shown by a number 
of studies [3,4,6,7]. The following section discusses the 
changes that occur as a result of irradiation at high temper- 
atures. 
4.1. Irrudirrtion-enhanced fhermal annealing 
The room temperature strength of the CW CuAl25 
decreased after irradiation to a level slightly above that of 
the unit-radiated as-wrought CuAl25. This decrease in 
strength agrees with that observed in previous studies. 
Edwards and coworkers [3,4,6] found that the strength of 
50% CW CuAl25 decreased roughly 15% after irradiation 
to 48 dpa and higher. Because the lowest dose available in 
that study was 48 dpa, it was not clear whether the changes 
in strength were completed earlier in the irradiation. How- 
ever, Brager and coworkers [7] found that irradiation of 
20% cold worked CuAl25 to I6 dpa at - 703 K produced 
a 15%’ decrease in yield strength. The displacement rate for 
both of these earlier studies was - 1.4 X 10 -’ dpa/s, 
comparable to that of the current experiment. Given the 
comparable displacement rates and temperatures, the irrn- 
diation-induced changes in the mechanical properties of 
DS copper alloys occur at much lower doses. 
To fully understand the changes that occur in the 
irradiated DS copper alloys, the effect of thermal annealing 
and initial cold work level must be taken into account 
since the material was irradiated near 0.57;,, for relatively 
long exposures. Stephen et al. [X,9] found that brazing 
as-wrought CuAll5 at 1253 K for I5 min produced only a 
small decrease in strength and increase in elongation. The 
as-wrought condition means that the material was already 
in an semi-annealed state from hot working, so the effect 
of cold-worked accelerated recovery and recrystallization 
was minimal. In addition, they also showed that CuAII5 
recovers and recrystallizes by secondary recrystallization, 
or abnormal grain growth. To produce a fully recrystal- 
lized grain structure required at least 100 h at 1313 K 
(0.97T,,). 
In a recent study, Edwards and coworkers [IO] observed 
that the short thermal exposures used to braze together 
sheets of the same CW CuAl25 as used in this study 
resulted in a significant decrease in strength, much more 
than anticipated. In fact, the short themral exposure at 
- 1200 K (5 min brazing, 20 min furnace cooling) low- 
ered the strength to the same level that irradiation did in 
this study. This lowering in strength is due to the acceler- 
ated recovery and recrystallization caused by the high level 
of cold work [I I]. 
There is a tendency to attribute the recovery and retry s- 
tallization that occurs at these higher temperatures to the 
effect of irradiation only. However, it is worth noting that 
during irradiation the specimens are at temperature for 
several thousand h. Most of the annealing data on Glid- 
Cop’” is for periods of IO h or less (see the recent reviews 
by Zinkle [ 121 and Singh [ 13]), which does not adequately 
reflect the thermal exposure during irradiation. The speci- 
mens irradiated in this study were in reactor at - 673 K 
for 203 EFPD (effective full power days), or - 4900 h. 
Anderson et al. [3] reported that annealing 50%’ CW 
CuAl25 and 20% CW CuAl20 for 1026 h at 693 K 
produced a decrease in strength very close to that which 
occurred when the same material was irradiated to 50 dpa 
at - 690 K (341 EFPD. 8200 h). 
These studies do not necessarily indicate that irradia- 
tion itself produces any dislocation recovery or recrystal- 
lization, but rather that the long thermal exposures associ- 
ated with the neutron irradiation can achieve the same 
results. However, Singh and coworkers [ 141 reported evi- 
dence that irradiation does indeed relax the dislocation 
structure at very low doses. They observed a 10%’ decrease 
in yield strength after irradiating heavily cold worked 
CuAl25 to 0.0 I dpa at 323 K. Above 0.0 I dpa the strength 
began to increase dramatically due to the presence of 
defect clusters. so that irradiation hardening masked any 
further relaxation in the initial dislocation structure. The 
displacement rate in their study was 5 X IO ’ dpa/s, 
roughly an order of magnitude lower than in the present 
study. Although the relaxation occurred at a much lower 
displacement rate and temperature than what was used in 
this study, this indicates that irradiation-produced defects 
will in fact enhance the thermal annealing that will occur 
due to the long term exposure. The changes in mechanical 
properties are independent of displacement rate in this 
study, however, that may be true only for dose levels 
beyond a certain level. h-radiating to dose levels in the 
range of 0.1 dpa or less might reveal that different dis- 
placement rates do indeed have a noticeable effect. 
Nesterova et al. [ 151 also observed radiation-enhanced 
recovery and recrystallization in GlidCop”’ CuAl25 and in 
the Russian MAGT alloy, another Al,O, dispersion 
strengthened copper alloy with additional alloying ele- 
ments of hafnium and titanium. Both alloys were irradiated 
in a 50% cold worked state with 3 MeV argon ions 
(- 3 X IO-” dpa/s) at 453 and 623 K. Transmission 
electron microscopy revealed a radiation-induced subgrain 
structure that could not be reproduced by annealing unirra- 
diated control specimens at the irradiation temperatures for 
comparable periods of time. It is interesting to note that 
even at 453 K the authors observed a distinct subgrain 
structure, demonstrating the effectiveness of the high con- 
centration of defects produced during irradiation. 
Other copper alloy systems have been investigated 
using ion and neutron irradiation. Zinkle et al. [ 161 have 
shown that precipitation hardened CuCrZr alloys are read-

ily subject to radiation-enhanced recovery and recrystal- 
lization, particularly when irradiated at temperatures above 
573 K. Appello and Fenici [ 171 found that the yield and 
ultimate tensile strength of CuCrZr decreased after neutron 
irradiation at 573 K. Unlike the oxide particles in DS 
copper alloys, the precipitates in the CuCrZr alloy lose 
their stability when irradiated above 573 K, thereby lower- 
ing their effectiveness at pinning the dislocation structure 
and allovvinp recovery and recrystallization to occur. 
Clearly the high level of cold work in the DS copper 
irmdiated in the various studies produces an alloy that is 
subject to recovery and recrystallization under irradiation 
and/or long temt themtal exposures. The GlidCop’” Nb, 
however, has proven to be extremely resistant to the effect 
of irradiation and thermal exposure. The observed change 
in yield strength indicates that a limited amount of recov- 
ery took place during irradiation, but unlike the CW 
CuAl25. the additional niobium particulates effectively 
prevent further changes in the strength of the GlidCop’“. 
Overall, the limited changes that occur in the GlidCop’” 
Nb indicate that the inherently weak copper matrix c:m be 
strengthened such that irradiation will have a minimal 
effect on the microstructure and mechanical properties. It 
is not yet known how these materials will perform after 
irradiation at temperatures below 523 K where irradiation 
hardening occurs. Microscopy will be conducted on the 
irradiated GlidCop”’ Nb specimens to ascertain the effect 
of irradiation on the niobium particles. The conductivity of 
the alloy both before and after irradiation needs to be 
measured to see if there are any significant changes due to 
irradiation and when compared to that of the standard 
GlidCop’” alloy. 
4.2. Actiuution 
The main disadvantage to using GlidCop”‘-Nb is that 
the niobium undergoes a thermal neutron reaction that 
produces ““Nb, a radioactive isotope with a 20000 year 
half-life. From a reduced activation standpoint for the 
ITER structural components, no alloys with contents of 
niobium greater than a I ppm are recommended for the 
first wall structure [ 181. Although the activation of niobium 
places ;I severe constraint on its use, for investigating the 
effect of using different strengthening additions on copper 
this alloy is well suited because it is commercially pro- 
duced by the same company that makes the standard 
GlidCop I” alloys. Other alloying additions such as yittria 
might be plausible since they don‘t suffer from the same 
limitations on activation as does niobium. 
5. Conclusions 
The GlidCop”‘-Nb alloys exhibit higher strength and 
better resistance to high temperature neutron irradiation 
than GlidCop’” CuAl25. The starting conductivity of this 
alloy will probably be lower, which will partially offset the 
advantages associated with the increase in strength. 
At the dose levels reached in this experiment, the 
change in mechanical properties for both GlidCop “‘-Nb 
and CuAl25 are independent of the displacement rates and 
temperature used in the study. 
Despite the changes that occur in GlidCop’” CuAl25, 
the strength of the material never decreases to the same 
level as that of the unit-radiated as-wrought CuAl25. 
Changes in the strength are thought to occur at rek- 
tively low doses, and more quickly than if only thermal 
annealing were taken into account. Although themtal an- 
nealing masks the effect of irradiation-induced relaxation 
in this study, other studies have shown that the dislocation 
structure can be relaxed at temperatures lower than 523 K 
and at dose levels and displacement rates much different 
than that used in the present study. 
Higher levels of cold work may not be beneficial since 
the high internal energy creates a strong driving force for 
recovery and recrystallization. Based on the results of this 
and other studies. it is recommended that GlidCop”’ 
CuAl25 be used in a well annealed condition for design 
and application purposes. 
An alternative is to continue investigating additional 
alloying modifications to CuAl25 that are more effective 
in preventing recovery and recrystallization. and that do 
not have the distinct disadvantage of long tern1 activation.
