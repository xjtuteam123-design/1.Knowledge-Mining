ABSTRACT
The alloying e ﬀects of W, Cr and Re in the [100] (010) edge dislocation cores (EDC) of Ni-based single crystal
superalloys are investigated using ﬁrst-principles based on the density functional theory (DFT). The binding
energy, Mulliken orbital population, density of states, charge density and radial distribution functions are
discussed, respectively. It is clearly demonstrated that the addition of refractory elements improves the stability
of the EDC systems. In addition, they can form tougher bonds with their nearest neighbour (NN) Ni atoms,
which enhance the mechanical properties of the Ni-based single crystal superalloys. Through comparative
analysis, Cr-doped system has lower binding energy, and Cr atom has evident e ﬀect to improve the systemic
stability. However, Re atom has the stronger alloying e ﬀect in Ni-based single crystal superalloys, much more
eﬀectively hindering dislocation motion than W and Cr atoms.
1. Introduction
Owing to their excellent high-temperature performance, Ni-based
single crystal superalloys have been widely used in industrial ﬁelds of
aviation, aerospace, ships, oil and chemicals industries, and so on [1–
3]. The Ni-based single crystal superalloys is constituted by two phase,
where the precipitate γ' phase (based on L1 2 Ni3Al) is coherently
embedded between narrow channels of γ phase (based on fcc Ni). Since
the invention of the ﬁrst single-crystal superalloy PWA1480 in the early
1980s, diﬀerent series of single crystal superalloys have been developed
[4– 7]. Chemical compositions are critical to the mechanical properties
of Ni-based single crystal superalloys. In order to meet the require-
ments of high-performance aircraft engines, refractory elements are
added into the nickel-based single crystal superalloys, such as W, Cr,
Mo, Ta, Re, Ru and so on. The refractory elements content in Ni-based
single crystal superalloys has increased from ∼24 wt% in the ﬁrst
generation superalloy PWA1480 to more than 34 wt% in third genera-
tion (TMS-75) superalloy ( Fig. 1 ). The development process of a
superalloy is actually the process of optimizing elemental components.
Understanding the strengthening mechanism of alloying elements
contribution makes the more e ﬃcient use of alloying elements and
the design of cost-e ﬀective high-temperature alloys. In order to explore
the alloying e ﬀect of refractory elements on the high-temperature
mechanical performance in superalloys many works, including experi-
ments and theory simulations, have been done [5,8]. Re has the slowest
diﬀusion rate in nickel [9] compared with other refractory elements,
and Reed et al. carried out a series studies on the interdi ﬀusion to
prove that the interdi ﬀusion coeﬃcient of Re is the lowest [10– 12]. The
slow di ﬀusion of Re may indicate, to a certain extent, that it will form
clusters [13], but whether the cluster is the root cause of the Re e ﬀect is
also doubtful. In order to explain theoretically the role of alloying
elements in superalloys, Fei Sun calculated the impurity formation
energies of the alloying element W, Cr and Co in the γ phase at diﬀerent
sites and presented the site preferences of typical refractory elements
W, Cr and Co in the σ and γ phases of Ni-based single crystal
superalloys [14]. However, they ignored the dislocation e ﬀect.
Dislocation is an important defect in crystal materials, and has great
inﬂuence on mechanical properties of materials. To study the proper-
ties of dislocation cores, many theories have been proposed, such as
embedded atom method (EAM) by Daw and Bakes [15– 19] and elastic
theory and so on. In recent years, with the development of the density
functional theory as well as technology of the computer, ﬁrst-principles
methods have shown great advantages in the way of treating defect
systems and have been widely used. Using the ﬁrst
principles discrete
variational method (DVM), electronic structures of di ﬀerent types of
dislocations in bcc Fe were investigated [20]. In their studies, localized
electronic states were found in the cores of < 100 > {010} and < 100 >
{011} edge dislocations, but not in the 1/2 < 111 > {011} edge disloca-

2. Method, parameters and computational model
All the ﬁrst-principles calculations in this paper were performed by
the CASTEP code (Cambridge Sequential Total Energy Package)
[22,23] based on density functional theory (DFT). Vanderbilt-type
ultra-soft pseudo-potentials [24] were used to describe the ionic
potentials and a plane-wave expansion of the wave functions also
was used for the calculations. The binding energy, total energy, density
of states and the charge densities in Ni 3Al were calculated. In the
calculation, generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the
Perdew– Burke– Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was employed as ex-
change-correlation functional [25]. The cut-o ﬀ energy of plane wave
was set to 400 eV and integrations in the Brillouin– zone were
performed using special k points generated with 4×4×4 Monkhorst –
Pack mesh [26]. Electron occupancies were determined by the
Gaussian method with a smearing width of 0.1 eV. During the
geometry optimization, the structure was relaxed by the BFGS
(Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno) minimization methods. The con-
vergence criteria for structure optimization and energy calculation are
set as follows: (1) SCF tolerance of
10−6, which allows the binding
energy to converge to 10−6 Ry; (2) energy tolerance of 0.01 eV/Å; (3)
stress tolerance of 0.01 GPa. Since Ni is magnetic element, spin
polarization was taken into account.
Ni3Al is ordered L1 2 structure, and its plastic deformation is carried
out mostly by the dislocations. Though the typical Bourger vector of full
dislocations in fcc metals is 1/2 < 110 > , there still exist < 100 >
dislocations in Ni-base superalloys, which play an important role in
creep progress [27,28]. And studies about the < 100 > dislocations in
fcc metals are less than others. Based on the considerations above, the
[100](010) edge dislocation cores (EDC) in Ni
3Al are taken as the
computational models. There exist Ni-system and Al-system according
to the atom type in the center. It has been con ﬁrmed that alloying
atoms prefer to occupy the Al site in the center of the core, so we just
take this model into account, as shown in Fig. 2 [29] . The atoms
denoted by blue and pink circles are Ni atoms and Al atoms,
respectively. In order to balance the computation time and accuracy,
the model has 7 layers along the [001] direction with a total of 190
atoms. The alloying atom W (Cr, Re) is placed at the position labeled 1

From Table 2, we can ﬁnd that the electron numbers in every orbital

the hybridization between the Ni-4p, Ni-3d orbital and Al-4p orbital.
And the Al atom in clean EDC system transfers more 3s electrons to 3p
compared to that in the Ni
3Al bulk. We also can ﬁnd that in all the EDC
systems, alloying atoms and Ni atoms in the compressive area obtained
more electrons, while the Ni atoms in the tensile area lost electrons or
kept the elections almost invariable, which is the result from the unique
geometry of the dislocation core. In the dislocation core, the atoms
have fewer nearest neighbours, so the hybridizations between the p-p
orbitals and p-d orbitals are subdued. As we can see, the atoms around
the dislocation cores have only eleven nearest neighbours compared to
twelve in bulk Ni3Al. So this e ﬀect may be associated with the so-called
“broken bonds”. From the comparison with the Mulliken populations
of atoms in di ﬀerent doped EDC systems, the overall situation of
electron transfer is similar to each other. However, there are huge
diﬀerences for the alloying atoms. For Re, electron transfers are mainly
from s orbitals to p orbitals and d orbitals, so these atomic orbitals can
make a hybrid with the orbitals of its nearest Ni atoms. While the Cr
and W atoms get fewer electrons and transfer less to p and d orbitals.
So their interaction with their nearest Ni atoms is weaker than Re
atom.
3.3. Density of states
Partial density of states (PDOS) represents useful semi-qualitative
tools for analyzing the electronic structure. PDOS calculations are
based on Mulliken population analysis, which allows the contribution
from each energy band to a given atomic orbital to be calculated. PDOS
shows that which atoms in the system contribute electronic states to
various parts of the energy spectrum and further qualify these results
by resolving the contributions according to the angular momentum of
the states. We can obtain deeper insight into the in ﬂuence of electronic
properties of alloying atoms on their chemical bonding by analyzing
PDOS. PDOS of atom 1 (atom 1 denotes the Al 1 atom and the alloying
atom in the clean dislocation system and doped system, respectively)
are calculated, and the result is shown in Fig. 3. We can ﬁnd the PDOS
curves in the high-energy region are similar to each other, and bonding
electrons are mainly distributed in the range of about −9~0.5 eV,
caused by Al-3s3p and Ni-3d orbitals in the clean EDC system as well
as by Al-3s3p, Ni-3d and W-5d (Cr-3d, Re-5d) orbitals in the doped
EDC systems. Furthermore, compared to the clean EDC system, there
exist electrons in the low-energy region in the doped EDC systems. Like
in the W-doped system, there exist electrons in the −38~−40 eV and
−74~−76 eV, caused by the W-5p and W-5s or W-6 s orbitals respec-
tively. Same as the W-doped system, Cr-doped (Re-doped) system also

3.5. Radial distribution function
As a further investigation into the nature of the dislocation the
radial distribution function g(r) [33] in the ﬁxed region were calcu-
lated. Radial distribution function g(r) is the ratio functions of atomic
density on the radial direction and the total density of the system,
which gives the probability of ﬁnding a particle in the distance r from
another particle. If we count the appearance of two molecules at
separation r, from r=0 to r=∞ , we can get the radial distribution
function g(r). The radial distribution function is a useful tool to
describe the structure of a system, particularly for liquids. In a solid,
the radial distribution function has an in ﬁnite number of sharp peaks
whose separations and heights are characteristic of the lattice struc-
ture. Fig. 9 shows the radial distribution function g(r) in bulk Ni
3Al
system and doped EDC systems. In bulk Ni 3Al system, the peaks are
sharp and equally distributed, and the ﬁrst peak occurs at r ≈2.5 Å
( 2/ 2a). In the doped EDC systems, the main peaks are similar to those
in bulk Ni 3Al system. However, the main peaks become wider and
many “vice-peaks” appear in the doped systems because of the move-
ment of the atoms. What's more, there exist two peaks at r ≈2.05 Å and
r≈2.4 Å, due to the “broken bones ”. For further analysis, we show the
partial enlarged details of doped systems around the ﬁrst peak in the
range of 1.5~3.0 Å in Fig. 10 . For all of three alloying atoms, the ﬁrst
main peak occurs in the same region. However, the range and peak
numbers are di ﬀerent. For the W-doped EDC system, there are three
“vice-peaks” at 2.45~2.53 Å, 2.57~2.63 Å and 2.69 Å, respectively.
Similarly, in the Cr-doped EDC system, there also are three “vice-
peaks” at 2.45~2.57 Å, 2.63 Å and 2.69 Å. However there are four
peaks for the Re-doped EDC system at 2.45~2.53 Å, 2.57~2.59 Å,
2.63 Å and 2.69 Å. Based on the above analysis, we can ﬁnd that in the
Re-doped EDC system the distribution of g(r) is more concentrated.
4. Conclusions
In this work, using ﬁrst principles CASTEP code based on the
density functional theory the alloying e ﬀect about the refractory
elements such as W, Cr and Re in the [100] (010) EDC of Ni 3Al has
been studied. Based on the analysis of the energy, Mulliken orbital
population, density of states, total charge densities and radial distribu-
tion function, the following conclusions can be drawn:
1) Stronger bond forms between the alloying atoms and the nearest
host Ni atoms, which also can be veri ﬁed by the total charge
densities.
2) Energy analysis indicates that Cr atom can do more for improving
the stability of the superalloys.
