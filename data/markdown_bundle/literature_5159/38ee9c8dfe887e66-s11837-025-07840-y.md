The additive manufacturing of hard-to-process intermetallics, such as the Ti-
23Al-25Nb alloy (O-alloy), poses signiﬁcant challenges but is critical for
expanding their industrial applications. This study identiﬁes the optimum
laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) parameters, build platform temperature, and
aging treatment required to produce high-performance, crack-free O-alloy for
demanding applications. The effect of L-PBF process parameters, including
platform preheat temperature and subsequent aging treatment on the relative
density, defects, microstructure, phase composition, and mechanical proper-
ties of orthorhombic titanium aluminide, Ti2AlNb, is investigated. Build
platform preheating at 600 °C and high volumetric energy density (VED) in the
range of 37–139 J/mm
3 results in a keyhole porosity and cracking due to
excessive energy input. Increasing the substrate temperature to 700 °C, under
optimized process parameters with VED in the range of 22–37 J/mm 3, pro-
duces a crack-free build with a relative density of 99.7%. A reduction in laser
scanning speed increases the fraction of the O-phase, thereby enhancing the
high-temperature strength. In addition, post-aging treatment at 800 °C for
30 min improved the strength and ductility of the O-alloy at room and elevated
testing temperatures of 600 and 700 °C. The effect of microstructure on the
mechanical properties of the O-alloy is discussed.
INTRODUCTION
Titanium aluminides ( γ-TiAl, α
2-Ti3Al, O-
Ti2AlNb) are critical intermetallic materials for the
next generation of aerospace and automotive
industries due to their exceptional combination of
properties, including high speciﬁc strength, excel-
lent heat resistance, and low density. These char-
acteristics position titanium aluminides as a
promising replacement for heavy nickel-based
superalloys in high-performance applications.
Among titanium intermetallic alloys, γ-TiAl (Ti-
48Al-2Cr-2Nb) intermetallic compounds are exten-
sively employed in components, such as low-pres-
sure turbines and other structural parts of aero-
engines, owing to their superior performance at
elevated temperatures.
1 Despite their high speciﬁc
strength, which surpasses that of steels and nickel-
based alloys, γ-TiAl alloys are hindered by ex-
tremely low ductility (approximately 1% at room
temperature), limiting their broader adoption in
critical applications. To overcome this limitation,
orthorhombic titanium aluminides (O-alloys), par-
ticularly Ti
2AlNb, have emerged as promising can-
didates for high-temperature applications. These
materials offer enhanced ductility compared to
γ-TiAl and Ti
3Al-based alloys, 2,3 and higher speciﬁc
strength in the operating temperature range of 650–
800°C, making them attractive for advanced heat-

facturing process for O-alloys typically involves
casting, thermomechanical processing, and
machining. However, these methods present sig-

4,5,10,11,14–16 A deﬁnite advantage of
L-PBF technology over EB-PBF technology, which
uses an electron beam instead of a laser to fuse
powder raw material, is the absence of the need to
fuse O-alloys in a vacuum, which contributes to less
evaporation of Al. And compared to direct energy
deposition (DED), it provides a higher surface
quality, reducing the costs of its subsequent
mechanical processing.
16,17
However, the application of L-PBF for O-alloys
faces critical challenges, primarily the formation of
cracks during synthesis or the detachment of parts
from the platform. These issues stem from signiﬁ-
cant residual thermal stresses, often exacerbated by
insufﬁcient preheating of the platform during pro-
cessing.
11,18 Recent studies have shown that the
platform preheating temperatures of 600 °Co r
higher can effectively reduce residual stresses,
mitigating crack formation during L-PBF. 11,19
Additionally, such preheating promotes the forma-
tion of more stable β+O or O-phase structures, 11,20
which are essential for high-temperature perfor-
mance, rather than metastable β(B2)-solid solutions
typically formed at lower platform temperatures
(200°C or below). 11,15,18
Despite these advancements, there remains a gap
in understanding how platform temperature and
fusion parameters during L-PBF inﬂuence the
microstructure and mechanical properties of O-al-
loys, particularly under elevated temperatures.
Addressing this knowledge gap is critical for
enhancing the performance of O-alloys in demand-
ing applications. The present study aims to inves-
tigate the effect of platform temperature and L-PBF
parameters on the structural-phase state and
mechanical properties of the synthesized O-alloy
(Ti-23Al-25Nb). This work seeks to provide insights
into achieving desirable microstructures and
mechanical performance for high-temperature
applications through tailored L-PBF processing
conditions.
Despite these advancements, there remains a gap
in understanding the process window for achieving
defect-free build using L-PBF. Addressing this
knowledge gap is critical for enhancing the perfor-
mance of O-alloys in demanding applications. The
present study aims to investigate the effect of plat-
form temperature and L-PBF parameters on the
microstructure and mechanical properties of the
synthesized O-alloy (Ti-23Al-25Nb). Also, the role of
aging treatment on microstructure and mechanical
properties is discussed.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
This section deals with the L-PBF fabricated O-
phase alloy under varying preheating conditions,
speciﬁcally at 600 and 700 °C. The detailed pro-
cessing parameters are provided in Table I. Previ-
ous studies have highlighted the inﬂuence of
preheating temperature on the processability and
quality of alloys, with variations depending on the
speciﬁc manufacturing process, such as DED or
L-PBF. For instance, Polozov et al.
7 observed the
occurrence of cracks in Ti 2AlNb alloys at a pre-
heating temperature of 300 °C. However, increasing
the preheating temperature to 700 °C reduced
cracking, while samples preheated at 800 °C were
crack-free. To understand the mechanism of the
cracking with an increase in preheating the follow-
ing sections deal with the effect of the preheating
temperature (600 and 700ºC) on the quality of the
alloy samples; speciﬁcally, the relative density, pore
distribution, and oxygen content have been sys-
tematically analyzed. Furthermore, mechanical
properties, including hardness and tensile strength,
were evaluated to gain insights into the relationship
between cracking and the mechanical performance
of the alloy.

Porosity and Cracks
VED also altered the nature of the porosity ob-
served in the O-alloy samples at 600 ºC preheating
temperature (Fig. 3). These effects are attributed to
the higher total heat input during L-PBF at a
platform temperature of 600 °C compared to 200 °C,
which enhances powder particles melting at similar
VED values. As a result of a keyhole effect,
18,19
typical porosity developed during L-PBF at 600 °C,
characterized by two types of pores concentrated in
the melt pool’s central zone: (1) elongated in the
building direction (BD) and less in number density
for lower VED (Fig. 3a) and (2) non-spherical pores
and large in number density for higher VED
(Fig. 3c). According to Ref. 18, the keyhole porosity
is accompanied by the deep melt pools created at
extremely high temperatures and signiﬁcant
amount of energy transferred, due to which some
parts of the material evaporate and trap inside the
deep melt pool.
Despite the increase in platform temperature
(600ºC), cracking in the O-alloy samples persisted.
These cracks extend to the sample surface and are
oriented at approximately 90 ° to the solidiﬁcation
direction (Fig. 3g). The two main reasons for this are
the residual stresses acting in BD and typical body-
centered cubic (BCC) solidiﬁcation texture
< 001 > k BD, that results in cleavage along the
{001} plane and is discussed in Section “ Aging of O-
Phase Alloy at 800ºC for 30 min .” The melt pool
geometry also varied with platform temperature. In
the present study, increasing the platform temper-
ature from 200
10 to 600 °C resulted in deeper fusion
zones with similar relative widths at comparable
VED values. Higher scanning speeds, which reduce

600°C (Fig. 3d, e). Further, the microstructure of
the 600ºC preheating samples is predominantly

Phase Analysis Using XRD
To understand the phase fraction, XRD analysis
was performed on the as-built samples at different
preheating temperatures and presented in Figs. 6
and 7. For the 600ºC samples and all L-PBF modes
(different VED), the phase fraction is predominantly
characterized by the β-phase. Notably, no super-
structural peaks associated with the β→B2 ordering
were detected. This absence is likely due to the ra-
pid cooling rates during fusion, which limit the time
for β-phase ordering. Secondly, the diffraction pat-
terns exhibit asymmetric β-phase peaks with char-
acteristic pull-ins on the low-angle side. This
feature suggests presence of regions with depleted
Al in the microstructure, probably due to Al vapor-
ization in regions of high energy input. The shift of
peaks towards the smaller 2 θ angles results from an
increase in the lattice spacing due to the depletion of
Al in the β-phase. Thirdly, low-intensity peaks cor-
responding to the O-phase are observed in the
diffraction patterns under L-PBF conditions with
the lowest scanning speed of 400 mm/s (corre-
sponding to volumetric energy densities of 56, 97,
and 139 J/mm
3) (Fig. 6).
For the 700ºC preheat condition, the XRD analy-
sis (Fig. 7) revealed that the phase fraction of the
alloy contains both β- and O-phases. Consistent with
the results obtained at 600 °C, a reduction in scan-
ning speed (at constant laser power) resulted in
increased intensity of the O-phase peaks. However,
in processing modes with laser powers of 50 and
60 W and scanning speeds of 800 mm/s (50 W) and
1000 mm/s (50 and 60 W), O-phase peaks were not
detected. In these cases, the diffraction patterns
showed β-phase peaks with characteristic asymme-
try, indicating regions of Al-depletion in the
microstructure (Fig. 8). Furthermore, the intensity
of O-phase peaks generally increased with rising
VED at constant scanning speeds. However, due to
the overlap of the β- and O-phase peaks and the
asymmetry of the β-phase reﬂections in alloys syn-
thesized with preheating at 700 °C, it was not pos-
sible to determine the lattice parameters of the β-
and O-phases with sufﬁcient accuracy.

Mechanical Testing
Hardness
For a platform preheat temperature of 600 °C,
microhardness as a function of VED is shown in
Fig. 9a, from which it is observed that, with the
processing condition of high scan speed of 600 mm/
min or above, hardness remains in the range of 410–
450 HV. However, different laser power samples
produced at a scanning speed of 400 mm/s show a
substantial increase in hardness. For a VED of
139 J/mm
3, scanning speed of 400 mm/s resulted in
the average hardness of approximately 600 HV,
which is attributed to the higher volume fraction of
O-phase precipitates formed under these conditions
(Fig. 8b).
The formation and increase in the volume fraction
of the O-phase with decreasing scanning speed and
increasing laser power during L-PBF at a platform
preheat temperature of 700 °C (Figs. 7, 8b) con-
tribute to a consistent increase in the hardness of
the O-alloy (Fig. 9b). This is attributed to enhanced
reﬁnement strengthening due to the dispersion of
O-phase precipitates. The minimum microhardness,
in the range of 430–450 HV, was observed under
processing conditions with the highest scanning
speed and lowest laser power, modes in which the
O-phase was not detected by XRD graphs (Fig. 7). In
contrast, the maximum hardness values, reaching
610–660 HV, were achieved in modes with a mini-
mum scanning speed of 600 mm/s and the highest
VED values (28 and 37 J/mm
3), which promoted
more extensive development of the β→O transfor-
mation during L-PBF (Fig. 7).
Tensile Test
Tensile testing of samples fabricated with VED=
65 J/mm 3 revealed that the presence of common
L-PBF-induced defects—such as cracks, different
types of porosity, and elevated oxygen content
exceeding 0.2%—leads to brittle failure. These
samples fractured in the elastic region, exhibiting
zero plasticity at both room temperature and ele-
vated temperatures of 600 and 700 °C. Fracto-
graphic analysis (Fig. 10a) showed brittle,
predominantly intragranular, cleavage fracture
with characteristic step-like transitions at grain
boundaries and ﬂat cleavage facets within grains.
Notably, gas pores with the shape close to spherical
did not act as primary crack initiation sites. More-
over, based on their morphology slightly elongated
in vertical direction, they may have contributed to
local stress relaxation. The lack of fusion pores
along with the keyhole pores with non-spherical
shape generally serve as crack initiation sites.
Chemical analysis highlighted a signiﬁcant issue
typical of additive manufacturing of O-alloys: Al
loss. As shown in Fig. 4c, this effect becomes more
pronounced with decreasing scanning speed, lead-
ing to a greater reduction in Al content in the alloy
post-L-PBF. The results indicate that O-alloy sam-
ples manufactured at a platform temperature of
600°C are characterized by the presence of keyhole-
type gas pores and reduced Al content, particularly
at the lowest scanning speed of 400 mm/s. These
phenomena are attributed to excessive energy input
during L-PBF under the applied VED settings and
elevated platform temperature. Although preheat-
ing to 600 °C mitigated crack formation to some ex-
tent, it was insufﬁcient to fully eliminate it.
Further, 700ºC preheating temperature reduces
residual stress and prevents cracking. However,
this higher preheat temperature inherently in-

Aging of O-Phase Alloy at 800ºC for 30 Min
Despite these improvements, the mechanical
performance remains suboptimal, highlighting the
need for post-process heat treatment to promote
structural equilibrium and enhance mechanical
properties. Aging treatment of the O-alloy synthe-
sized with parameters preheat temperature of 700 °
C, VED of 21 J/mm
3, 60W, and 800 mm/s was car-
ried out at 800 °C for 30 min, following the recom-
mendations outlined in Ref. 24. Microstructural
analysis after aging (Fig. 11a) showed substantial
decomposition of the β-phase, with widespread pre-
cipitation of O-phase platelets throughout the
β-grain matrix. According to EBSD data, the ratio of
volume fractions of O and β-phases is 85% to 15%
(Fig. 11b). Microstructural analysis shows that the
O-phase precipitates are distributed both along
grain boundaries and within the grain interiors,
where colonies of O-phase precipitates developed in
multiple orientations. In the aged condition, the O-
alloy exhibited a uniform hardness of 580 ±10 HV.
Further to understand the insights into the β- and
O-phases, the textural state of the aged O-alloy was
examined using EBSD, and the orientation rela-
tionships between the β- and O-phases were deter-

After tensile testing at room temperature, the

morphology characterized by intergranular fracture
along individual columnar β-grains (Fig. 12c). The

dimensions, particle-depleted ductile boundary re-
gions act as effective barriers to cleavage crack
propagation. Consequently, unlike the as-built
condition (Fig. 10a), large fracture facets are not
observed in the aged alloy.
At an elevated test temperature of 700 °C, the
fracture surface transitions to a predominantly
ductile morphology characterized by dimpled rup-
ture with occasional facets of intergranular fracture
and secondary cracks exhibiting similar ductile
features (Fig. 12d). Crack propagation occurs pri-
marily through the more plastic β-phase matrix,
promoting the formation of dimpled, pit-like fea-
tures typical of ductile failure.
CONCLUSION
A build platform preheat temperature of 700 °C
and optimized process parameter helped to mitigate
typical defects like cracking and keyhole porosity.
Additionally, aging treatments further improved
mechanical properties and fracture characteristics.
The core ﬁndings and their implications for high-
temperature alloy performance have been summa-
rized, as below:
1. Laser powder bed fusion (L-PBF) of Ti-23.4Al-
24.5Nb (at.%) powder at 600 °C preheat and VED
of 49 J/mm
3 resulted in the highest alloy density
(99.7%), although excessive energy input pro-
moted keyhole porosity and surface cracks.
2. Higher VED and lower scanning speeds led to
increased oxygen (0.2–0.35%) and reduced Al
(20–21%) content. Dynamic aging during pro-
cessing at low scan speed and high VED accel-
erates the β→O phase transformation, raising
hardness from 450 to 600 HV.
3. Under room and elevated temperature tensile
testing (up to 700 °C), samples processed at 600 °C
preheat, VED 65 J/mm
3 exhibited predominantly
brittle fracture behavior, inﬂuenced by
microstructural features induced during L-PBF.
