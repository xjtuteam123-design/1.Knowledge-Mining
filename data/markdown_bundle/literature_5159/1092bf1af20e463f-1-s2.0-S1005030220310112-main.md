1. Introduction
In the pursuit for higher strengths for metallic materials, a recent
advance is the invention of the concept of high-entropy alloys
(HEAs) [1,2], which is a strategy of using a high number of principal
elements to form a single phase solid solution. The fundamental
principle for the strength of HEAs is solid solution strengthening,
achieved by maximum ubiquitous local lattice distortions caused
by having every individual atom to neighbor with different atoms
in the lattice [3].
Since the invention of this novel concept in 2004, a vast number
(over 2000) of HEAs have been created and reported. One subset
of these HEAs is the body-centered-cubic (BCC) HEAs. Most BCC
transition metal elements used for BCC HEAs have high melting
temperatures, thus they are also often known as refractory HEAs
(RHEAs). BCC HEAs generally have higher yield strengths com-
pared to the face-centered-cubic (FCC) structured HEAs and the

hexagonal-close-packed (HCP) structured HEAs [4,5], because of
several concurrent contributions, including the fewer effective slip
systems than in the FCC structure and the higher Peierls-Nabarro
stresses for moving dislocations than those in FCC and HCP struc-
tures [6]. In addition, the common elements forming BCC HEAs
appear to differ more in atomic size and cause more severe lat-
tice distortions than those in FCC and HCP HEAs [7]. At the same
time, BCC HEAs are also generally more brittle and have poorer
malleability [8–10].
Different approaches have been trialed to improve the ductility
of BCC HEAs [8,11–13]. Transformation-induced plasticity (TRIP)
has proven to be effective in reducing the brittleness of BCC HEAs.
In these alloys stress-induced BCC→HCP transformations may be
introduced by lowering the content of some BCC phase stabiliz-
ing elements, such as Nb and Ta, and increasing those of HCP
phase stabilizing elements, such as Ti, Zr and Hf [14,15]. Such
transformations absorb local deformation owing to their crystallo-
graphic lattice distortion, thus to relax local stress concentrations
within the lattice. This helps to suppress or mitigate early crack-
ing and improve ductility. However, this approach usually upsets
the equimolar composition of the HEAs and causes lowering of

the yield strength by weakening the solid solution strengthening
effect. In addition, the occurrence of a stress-induced transforma-
tion prior to dislocation plastic deformation also explicitly implies
that the stress required for the transformation is lower than the
critical stress required for dislocation plasticity. In this regard, the
lowering of the apparent yield strength is intrinsic to this approach.
For example, a stress-induced BCC→HCP transformation was intro-
duced to a TaHfZrTi alloy by lowering the Ta content to Ta0.4HfZrTi.
As a result, the fracture strain (ductility) of Ta0.4HfZrTi increased
from ∼2.5 % to ∼27 % but the yield strength decreased signiﬁcantly
from ∼1.5 GPa (for yielding by dislocation plasticity) to ∼0.4 GPa
(for yielding by stress-induced transformation) [16].
Another approach is to design HEAs based on the principle of
intrinsic characteristics of constituent elements. First-principles
calculations [17] indicate that group V (V, Nb, Ta) elements in
the Periodic Table are intrinsically ductile. Crystals of these metals
mechanically fail by shear via dislocation movement. In contrast,
group VI (Cr, Mo, W) elements are intrinsically brittle and they
fail predominantly by cleavage fracture. In addition, an empiri-
cal criterion based on the valence electron concentration (VEC) is
also proposed for ductility of BCC alloys, i.e., an alloy is ductile
when VEC ≤ 4.6 [18]. Using this approach, many HEAs have been
designed, with a single phase Hf0.5Nb0.5Ta0.5Ti1.5Zr HEA appearing
to offer the best properties [18]. This alloy exhibited a large tensile
ductility of ∼18.8 % and a yield strength of 900 MPa. In comparison,
some Mo and W containing BCC HEAs, which have higher VECs in
the range of 4.6–5.5, show high yield strengths over ∼1.2 GPa but
limited compressive ductility of below ∼9 % [10,19,20].
One further independent principle we can apply to further opti-
mize the alloy design for higher strength and higher ductility is to
increase the lattice friction for dislocation movement. Lattice fric-
tion describes the collective difﬁculty to move dislocations within a
matrix, as expressed by the Peierls potential [21]. It increases with
increasing the magnitude of the lattice distortion of the matrix.
In this regard, it is a measure of the effectiveness of solid solu-
tion strengthening. For the same quantitative composition, the
lattice friction stress, or the Peierls potential landscape, of a matrix
increases with increasing the atomic size differences among the
constituent elements. Therefore, it is possible to achieve better
combinations of strength and ductility by optimizing the contri-
butions of all the three principles, i.e., the intrinsic ductility of
elements, the VEC criterion, and maximization of atomic size dif-
ferences among the constituent elements. This study explored the
synergy among these three approaches to design BCC HEAs of con-
current high strength and high ductility.
2. Experimental
An equiatomic HfNbTaTiV alloy and an equiatomic HfNbTa-
TiZr alloy were designed in this study. Button ingots of the alloys
were fabricated from high purity elements (>99.99 %) by means
of vacuum arc melting in water-cooled copper crucibles in Ar
atmosphere. The crystal structures of the as-cast alloys were ana-
lyzed by means of X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Cu-K˛ radiation.
The microstructures of the alloys were examined using an optical
microscope and a FEI Quanta 650 F scanning electron microscope
(SEM). The SEM is equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (EDS) capability for chemical analysis and an elec-
tron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) detector for crystallographic
analysis. SEM samples were etched using an aqueous solution of
HNO3:HF:H2O = 1:3:7 in volume ratio. An aberration-corrected FEI
Titan Themis G2-300KV transmission electron microscope (TEM)
was used to analyze the microstructure and elemental distribu-
tions at the nanoscale. Atomic probe tomography (APT) analysis
was performed using CAMECA LEAP 4000X HR instrument. The
needle-shaped specimens used for APT analysis were fabricated
using a FEI focused ion beam (FIB) milling instrument. Mechani-
cal testing was conducted in compression using an Instron 8860
instrument and a Gleeble 3350 testing machine, using cylindrical
specimens of /RS3 mm × 4.5 mm and /RS4 mm × 6 mm in dimension,
respectively. All compression tests were performed under an ini-
tial strain rate of 10−3/s. For each testing, three new samples were
used to assure test reproducibility.
3. Results and discussions
3.1. Alloy design
To design a BCC HEA, Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)
was used to select potential elements. It predicts that BCC HEAs can
be formed by using Hf, Mo, Nb, Ta, Ti, V, W and Zr. Among them,
Hf, Nb, Ta, Ti, V and Zr are intrinsically ductile elements [18]. In this
study, two alloys were designed using these elements, including
a HfNbTaTiV (hereafter referred to as the V-alloy) equimolar alloy
as the target design based on the criteria explained above, and a
HfNbTaTiZr (hereafter referred to as the Zr-alloy) equimolar alloy,
which is the most studied BCC HEA by many researchers [13,22–29],
as a benchmark alloy for comparison. To achieve simultaneously
high strength and high ductility, the HfNbTaTiV alloy was designed
in three steps, i.e., (1) to ensure a single-phase solid solution (SPSS)
structure by element selection based on thermodynamic principles,
(2) to ensure maximum lattice distortion for strong solid solution
strengthening effect by maximizing atomic size difference, and (3)
to ensure sufﬁciently low valence electron concentration (VEC) for
ductility [18].

The evolution of the equilibrium phase mole fractions upon

The calculated liquidus temperature is TL = 2300 K and the solidus
temperature is TS = 2000 K. The alloy solidiﬁes into a single-phase
BCC structure. At TD = 1050 K, the BCC phase starts to decompose

At 500 K, the equilibrium phase diagram predicts a dual phase

precipitation of the HCP phase can be very slow. By the empirical
criterion of (TS-TD)/TS > 0.3 for the suppression of the decomposi-

single-phase solid solution in the as-cast state at room temperature,
because (TS-TD)/TS = 0.475.

3.3. Mechanical properties
Fig. 5 shows the mechanical behavior of the two alloys at dif-
ferent temperatures. Fig. 5(a) shows the engineering compressive
stress-strain curves of the two alloys at room temperature. The
V-alloy exhibited an ultrahigh yield strength of 1350 MPa and a
compressive ductility of >45 % (no rupture). The Zr-alloy had a
yield strength of 900 MPa and an equally high ductility of >45 % (no
rupture). Fig. 5(b) shows the engineering compressive stress-strain
curves of the V-alloy at elevated temperatures. The yield strength
of the alloy was determined to be 841 MPa, 810 MPa and 720 MPa at
500◦C, 600 ◦C and 700 ◦C, respectively. None of the three specimens
ruptured at the maximum compressive strains of the tests.
Fig. 5(c) shows a comparison of the yield strength and ductil-
ity of the V-alloy with other high-performance BCC-based RHEAs
at room temperature. All the data presented are from compres-
sion tests. It is evident that the ductility of the V-alloy is about 2
times of those of the RHEAs with similar yield strengths and the
yield strength of the V-alloy is 200–300 MPa higher than those of
the RHEA with similar ductility. This is a clear indication of syn-
ergy of the different principles applied in the design of the alloy.

4. Conclusions
A high-strength, high-ductility HfNbTaTiV RHEA was designed
by a synergistic approach based on the principles of intrinsic ele-
ment ductility, maximum atomic size difference for solid solution
strengthening, and the valence electron concentration criterion for
ductility.
The HfNbTaTiV alloy exhibited a high compressive yield
strength of 1350 MPa and a compressive ductility of > 45 %. It also
retained a high yield strength of 720 MPa and ductility of > 35 % at
700 ◦C. Such a combination of properties is superior to most known
BCC HEAs. The alloy had a similar ductility but a strength 50 % higher
than the HfNbTaTiZr benchmark alloy.
The high yield strength of the HfNbTaTiV HEA is attributed to the
high solid solution strengthening effect caused by the large lattice
distortion. The solid solution strengthening effect in the HfNbTaTiV
is estimated to be 1094 MPa.
