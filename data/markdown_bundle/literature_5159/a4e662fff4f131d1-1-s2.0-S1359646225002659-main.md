ABSTRACT
BCC superalloys are a promising class of high-temperature materials with a wide range of lattice misfit values, 
ranging from near-zero to ~8 %. Analogous to nickel superalloys, lattice misfit combined with elastic anisotropy 
dictates precipitate morphology (spherical, cuboidal, plate/needle-like), coarsening kinetics, strengthening 
mechanisms, and microstructure evolution, making misfit control critical for tailoring microstructural stability 
and creep resistance. However, misfit characterisation, especially at high temperatures, is still in its infancy to 
establish its links with mechanical properties. This perspective emphasises three aspects of BCC superalloys: 
representative misfit-driven microstructures and temperature-dependent misfit evolution, state-of-the-art 
diffraction techniques for high-temperature misfit quantification, and machine learning frameworks to accel -
erate alloy design involving misfit. By consolidating diverse misfit data and advanced characterisation/modelling 
strategies, we outline strategies to bridge computational and experimental gaps, advocating for physics-informed 
models and high-throughput techniques to design next-generation BCC superalloys and motivate systematic 
studies on the misfit-property relationship in this nascent material class.

In this work, we aim to explore the recent advancements in BCC 
superalloys, focusing on the characterisation of lattice misfit and 
representative microstructure. The discussion will encompass the lattice 
misfit and microstructural features of various BCC superalloys devel -
oped using CALPHAD methodologies, with an emphasis on precipitate 
morphology and coarsening kinetics. We will also examine the impact of 
temperature on lattice misfit, particularly the differential thermal 
expansion behaviours of A2 and B2 structures. Furthermore, the study 
will delve into different measurement techniques on offer for the devi -
ation of lattice misfit in BCC superalloys, including diffraction-based 
methods and advanced transmission electron microscopy approaches. 
Additionally, the potential of machine learning methods will be dis -
cussed to predict lattice misfit and guide microstructure design. Finally, 
comments will be made on perspectives and outlooks on unresolved 
questions in this field, urging rigorous misfit characterisation and 
inspiring further in-depth research on BCC superalloys.

misfits are typically confined to a narrow range within ± 1 %, BCC su -
peralloys exhibit a significantly broader range of lattice misfits at room 
temperature, spanning from near-zero to as large as -7 %. This wide 
range arises from the variability in lattice parameters between the 
disordered-BCC (A2) and ordered-BCC (B2/L2

• For BCC superalloys with low lattice misfits (| δ | < 0.5 %), micro -
structures are often dominated by spherical precipitates, resembling 
those in low-misfit FCC Ni superalloys. The low strain energy asso -
ciated with these precipitates allows isotropic growth, minimising 
interfacial energy while maintaining coherency. This morphology is 
especially common in ferritic superalloys [ 23 ] or Cr-based BCC su -
peralloys [ 12 , 14 ] due to the close lattice parameters of Fe, Cr and 
NiAl, where spherical precipitates can grow to micrometer sizes 
without significant loss of stability. The slow coarsening kinetics of 
such microstructures make them particularly suitable for applica -
tions requiring long-term microstructural stability under static or 
low-stress conditions. However, the effect of temperature could in -
crease the magnitude of misfit (typically A few tenths of a percent) or 
alter its sign when the room-temperature misfit is near-zero. This 
could affect the precipitate morphological evolution at various 
temperatures as shown in γ / γ’ systems [ 42 ]. High-temperature 
measurement will be necessary to monitor the misfit evolution and 
understand the changes of precipitate morphology, which will be 
discussed in Section 3.
• As the lattice misfit increases into the intermediate range (0.5 – 3 %), 
full coherency is difficult to maintain, and the interface becomes 
semi-coherent by containing interfacial misfit dislocations which 
accommodate the lattice mismatch. The morphology of precipitates 
shifts from spherical to cuboidal. This range of lattice misfit can be 
found in A2-B2/L21 systems such as ferritic steels [ 30 , 43 ]. Alloying 
Ti into Fe-NiAl and Cr-NiAl systems to change the system from A2-B2 
to A2-B2/L21 can tune the misfit from near-zero to more than 1 % 
[ 12 , 21 , 22 , 24 , 26 ], as L21-Ni2AlTi has a larger lattice parameter than 
NiAl. Alloying Ti was found to tune the lattice misfit of a ferritic 
superalloy from near-zero to + 1.3 % [ 21 ]. This transition occurs as 
the elastic strain becomes a more significant factor than the interface

Is the optimal lattice misfit for BCC superalloys identical to that of 
FCC systems? Likely not. Differences in stiffness and elastic anisotropy 
necessitate distinct design criteria. For example, Co-based superalloys 
require larger misfits than Ni-based alloys to offset their lower matrix 
stiffness and sustain adequate coherency stresses for stabilising precip -
itate shapes ( Fig. 3 (a)) [ 5 ]. In BCC superalloys, misfit optimisation must 
similarly account for stiffness contrasts between the matrix and pre -
cipitates, as these directly control coherency stress magnitudes. Equally 
critical is elastic anisotropy: cubic precipitates form only when elastic 
energy savings from aligning interfaces along “ soft ” directions ( e.g. , 
< 100 > ) exceed interfacial energy penalties. For B2-strengthened BCC 
superalloys, elastic anisotropy should also be highly 
composition-dependent. Precipitates with pronounced anisotropy ( e.g. , 
Fig. 3(d)) retain spherical shapes only at much smaller sizes or misfits 
than the isotropic variants (Fig. 3(b)). This parallels Ni ₃ Al, though 
compositional effects in B2 systems like NiAl could be amplified by 
broader stability ranges. In multi-component alloys, precipitates likely 
reside on leaner solute regions ( e.g. , Ni-rich NiAl), reducing anisotropy 
relative to binary systems (Fig. 3(b)). However, ternary solutes ’ influ -

microstructure, it is an important factor in controlling the precipitate 
morphological evolution at high temperature with loading ( e.g. , rafting

matrix and precipitate phases, which vary with aging temperature and 
time, as supported by the results in [ 15 ]. However, while the equilib -
rium lattice misfit at a given temperature is constant, it appears as if the 
apparent (constrained) lattice misfit is changing as a function of aging 
time as the stress state and coherency change with the precipitate size 
and volume fraction. For example, it can be expected that a higher 
volume fraction of larger precipitates (as long as the coherency stress is 
lower than the yield stress) would primarily cause tetragonal distortion 
of the typically softer matrix phase [ 5 ]. The ability of BCC superalloys to 
accommodate this misfit or design against it via phase transformations 
and microstructural evolution can be crucial to their performance. For 
instance, directional coarsening and recrystallisation observed in aged 
ferritic and Ti-based BCC superalloys [ 18 , 25 , 57 ] highlight the perfor -
mance implications of misfit accommodation, akin to the rafting of 
Ni-based superalloys under loading or elongated/labyrinthine pre -
cipitates without loading.
Techniques for measuring lattice misfit in BCC superalloys
X-ray diffraction and neutron diffraction
X-ray diffraction (XRD) and neutron diffraction (ND) are widely used 
to measure lattice parameters and misfit in bulk Ni-based superalloys 
[ 59 – 61 ] and some BCC superalloys [ 14 , 17 , 18 , 21 , 55 ]. Conventional 
lab-based XRD effectively resolves lattice parameters of strengthening 
phases when their volume fractions are sufficiently high to produce 
strong superlattice (SL) reflections [ 17 , 18 ]. However, near-zero misfit or 
low phase fractions often necessitate advanced techniques such as syn -
chrotron X-ray diffraction (SXRD) or ND.
SXRD can analyse bulk ( Fig. 5 (a)) or powder samples ( Fig. 5 (b)). For 
BCC superalloys, which often exhibit rapid recrystallisation during ho -
mogenisation (yielding grains > 100 µ m), bulk SXRD measurements are 
prone to texturing due to limited beam size and sample thickness. This 
complicates SL peak detection unless thermomechanical processing re -
fines grains to sub-100 µ m scales, as demonstrated in [ 24 ].
To mitigate texturing, synchrotron powder XRD (SPXRD) enhances 
statistical sampling by incorporating numerous crystallite orientations. 
Powder preparation can introduce residual stresses, induce preferred 
orientation, or cause minor shifts or invisible peaks due to peak broad -
ening, which will affect the measurement of lattice parameters and thus 
lead to erroneous misfit determination. Heat treatment allows stress 
relief to obtain high-quality powders. It was suggested that a short heat 
treatment near one-third of the absolute melting temperature could 
restore the micro-deformation in powders [ 63 ]. However, the phase 
transformation, precipitate coarsening, and oxidation should be 
considered to determine the heat treatment temperature and time. In 
our work, a heat treatment at 500

ND offers distinct advantages over XRD despite its lower resolution. 
Its penetration depth (millimetre-scale) permits bulk analysis without 
destructive sample preparation. Crucially, ND intensities are indepen -
dent of diffraction plane spacing, d , unlike XRD where intensity decays 
with 1/sin² θ , allowing robust signal retention at high d-spacing ( Fig. 5 (e) 
versus Fig. 5 (c)). Furthermore, neutrons interact with atomic nuclei, 
whereas X-rays interact with electron clouds. X-ray scattering depends 
on atomic number (Z), so elements with similar Z produce weak 
contrast, i.e. , weak superlattice peaks, especially with high-energy X- 
rays ( Fig. 5 f). Neutron scattering lengths, however, depend on nuclear 
properties, which differ significantly even for similar-Z elements. Thus, 
the technique ’ s sensitivity to nuclear scattering ( vs. X-ray) amplifies the 
contrast between atoms with similar atomic numbers ( e.g. , Ni and Al in 
B2 phases) [ 55 , 61 ], making ND particularly effective for SL reflection 
detection in FCC- and BCC superalloys in transmission geometry with 
multi-detector setups. High-flux neutron facilities, such as VULCAN 
[ 62 ], further enable pseudo-continuous lattice parameter tracking in 
bulk samples under controlled thermal profiles (Fig. 5(d)). This capa -
bility bypasses labour-intensive steps like powder synthesis, stress relief, 
and phase extraction, streamlining rapid alloy screening.

Prediction of lattice misfit in BCC superalloys
Evolution of ML approaches
Machine learning (ML) has transitioned from shallow models to deep 
learning for predicting lattice misfit. Early efforts in Ni-based superal -
loys employed Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) and Support Vector 
Regression (SVR), achieving high accuracy by correlating misfit with 
descriptors like composition, temperature, and precipitate morphology 
[ 68 , 69 ]. However, these methods relied on pre-selected features and 
faced computational bottlenecks. Recent advances integrate 
physics-aware ML with materials science frameworks. For instance: 
• CALPHAD-guided ML: Liang et al. combined CALPHAD (Calculation 
of Phase Diagrams) databases with ML to design CoNiCr-based su -
peralloys, reducing trial-and-error by predicting phase stability and 
misfit [ 70 ].
• Deep learning: Multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) outperform traditional 
models by capturing non-linear relationships between composition, 
thermal history, and misfit [ 69 ].
• Atomistic simulations: Deep Potential models trained on density 
functional theory (DFT) data enable quantum-accurate predictions of 
interface energies and phase transitions under thermal strain [ 71 ].
Hybrid approaches, such as Random Forest and XGBoost, excel in 
feature-rich systems like high-entropy alloys (HEAs), though success 
hinges on rigorous feature engineering [ 72 ].
Emerging trends: physics-informed and multi-scale integration
Modern frameworks now emphasise physics-ML synergy. Convolu -
tional Neural Networks (CNNs) and physics-informed UNets (PI-UNets) 
incorporate physical constraints ( e.g. , elastic anisotropy, thermody -
namic laws) to enhance generalisability [ 58 ]. Integrated Computational 
Materials Engineering (ICME) harmonises multi-scale data -
— synchrotron XRD [ 73 ], DFT [ 71 , 74 ], CALPHAD [ 17 , 70 ], and 
phase-field modelling [ 74 ] — to train robust models. For example, 
DFT-derived parameters enabled phase-field simulations of γ ′ coarsening 
in Co-Ti alloys, linking misfit to morphological evolution [ 58 ]. 
Explainable AI: Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) decode model 
decisions for tasks like creep life optimisation and stress-strain analysis 
[ 72 , 75 ].
While ML has significantly advanced misfit prediction and alloy 
design, several challenges must be addressed to fully realise its potential. 
A primary limitation is the scarcity of high-quality experimental data for 
BCC superalloys, which can be mitigated through high-throughput 
CALPHAD screening [ 73 ] and DFT-molecular dynamics (DFT-MD) 
simulations [ 55 ] to expand training datasets. Ensuring model general -
isability beyond limited data will require physics-driven loss functions 
and automated feature selection to capture complex material behav -
iours. Additionally, integrating ML with real-time microstructure evo -
lution during heat treatments — guided by CALPHAD-informed phase 
equilibria [ 70 ] — could enable closed-loop process optimisation. Future 
applications may focus on designing multi-phase alloys with optimal 
misfit for enhanced creep resistance, predicting misfit evolution under 
extreme thermomechanical conditions, and developing autonomous 
ICME workflows that combine ML, phase-field modelling, and experi -
mental validation. Addressing these challenges will require continued 
collaboration between materials scientists and ML researchers to bridge 
the gap between data-driven predictions and physical insights.
Perspectives
Compared to the γ - γ’ system, the broader structure variability of 
ordered-BCC phases (B2, L2

BCC superalloys, spanning from near-zero to 8 %, and the composi -

effects on the elastic anisotropy. Furthermore, unlike Ni superalloys, the 
misfit in BCC superalloys tends to increase with temperature due to the 
higher thermal expansion of ordered-BCC phases than the disordered- 
BCC matrix. These factors will critically influence strengthening mech -
anisms, deformation, and high-temperature performance. Yet, quanti -
tative links between the misfit and high-temperature mechanical 
properties need to be established for designing alloys for extreme 
aerospace or energy environments. This urges rigorous misfit charac -
terisation as a prerequisite for understanding its role in microstructural 
stability and properties, and systematic high-temperature testing and 
computational modelling approaches to quantify the correlation be -
tween microstructural factors and mechanical properties for more effi -
cient design and optimisation of BCC superalloys.
Current theoretical frameworks predict misfit between A2 matrices 
and ordered-BCC phases ( e.g. , B2, L2
1
, DO
3
) but lack precision for alloy 
design. While misfit tuning stabilises microstructures ( e.g. , suppressing 
directional coarsening), empirical control remains challenging. Bridging 
this requires integrating computational models (CALPHAD, DFT, phase- 
field) with advanced experiments (in-situ diffraction, TEM strain map -
ping). Machine learning (ML) can accelerate links between misfit, 
microstructure, and mechanical properties for rapid alloy screening.
Future progress hinges on two fronts: (1) developing physics- 
informed ML models to predict misfit, elastic anisotropy, and thermal 
expansion interplay across temperatures; (2) performing high- 
temperature mechanical testing and coupling these with microstruc -
tural representations to decode structure – property relationships under 
thermomechanical stress. These advances will enable BCC superalloys 
with optimised misfit-strain tolerance, mitigating degradation (rafting, 
recrystallisation) in extreme conditions. Merging predictive accuracy 
with microstructural realism will unlock next-generation alloys tailored 
for unprecedented high-temperature performance.
