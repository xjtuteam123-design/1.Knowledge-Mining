extruded at various temperatures

Abstract The microstructures and tensile properties of
Mg–6.0Zn–0.6Zr (ZK60) and ZK60 ? 1.8Gd (ZEK620
(RE = Gd)) alloys extruded at 290, 305, 320, 340 and
360 /C176C were investigated. The results indicate that Gd
addition can reﬁne the microstructures of ZK60 alloy and
the extrusion parameters such as the extrusion temperature
will inﬂuence the tensile properties of both as-extruded
ZK60 and ZEK620 alloys, although it is much slighter for
ZK60 alloy. And the sensitivity is closely related to the
textures developed during extrusion. In addition, Gd
addition will also clearly improve the tensile strength at
room temperature (24 /C176C) and particularly at high tem-
peratures (150 and 200 /C176C), due to the much ﬁner grains
and smaller intermetallic particles. The highest strength is
achieved in the as-extruded ZEK620 alloy with extrusion
temperature of 320 /C176C, with ultimate tensile strength
(UTS) and tensile yield strength (TYS) of 405 and
375 MPa, respectively, which are improved by approxi-
mately 9% and 11% than those of the as-extruded ZK60
alloy which owns the highest strength at extrusion tem-
perature of 290 /C176C.
Keywords Rare earth; Magnesium; Extrusion;
Microstructure reﬁnement; Tensile property
1 Introduction
Mg–Zn–Zr-based alloys, acted as the widely used wrought
magnesium alloys, are growingly attracting a great deal of
attentions in applying to such as aerospace, high-speed rail,
automotive and electronics industries, because of their
outstanding high speciﬁc strength, good damping capacity
and fatigue resistance [1–6]. Therefore, many investigators
paid attention to further improving the tensile strength of
the Mg–Zn–Zr-based alloys by adding some alloying ele-
ments [7–12], of which the representative and also the most
effective one is rare earth (RE) [ 13–18]. It is well known
that gadolinium (Gd) has much greater solubility
(20.1 wt% at 500 /C176C[ 19]) in magnesium than most other
RE elements and is widely used in various wrought Mg–
RE-based systems due to its outstanding age hardening
response (a sharp decrease in solubility at lower tempera-
tures such as only 4.8 wt% at 300 /C176C). However, only a
few of researchers conducted investigations on further
improving the strength of the conventional ZK60 alloy by
adding some Gd, with a part owning an opposite result. For
instance, He et al. [ 20] compared the microstructures and
mechanical properties of the as-cast and hot-extruded
ZK60 alloy with and without 1.3 wt% Gd addition and
reported that Gd addition will lead to a considerable
reduction in age hardening response and a small decrease
in ultimate tensile strength (UTS) and tensile yield strength
(TYS), although with a remarkable grain reﬁnement effect.
Then, Huang et al. [ 21] investigated the effects of various
Gd additions on microstructures and mechanical properties
and also reported that Gd additions will decrease the
strength of the as-cast ZK60 alloy but can further improve
the mechanical properties of the hot-extruded ZK60 alloy
by some extent, although with a slight reduction in duc-
tility. Nevertheless, the investigations concerning the

Owing to a hexagonal close-packed (hcp) structure, mag-
nesium alloys are difﬁcult to deform especially at room tem-
perature. However, at elevated temperatures particularly
above 300 /C176C, the stacking fault energy of magnesium is
obviously decreased, resulting in an easy nucleation of
\c ? a[ dislocations [22, 23]. In addition, the activation
energy for the non-basal slip will also be clearly reduced at
high temperatures. Furthermore, it is well reported that the
\c ? a[slip can offer ﬁve or more independent slip systems
fulﬁlling the von Mises condition for general grain deforma-
tion compatibility [23, 24]. Therefore, the cross-slip which
acted as the main carrier of ductility will be clearly impeded at
high temperature [ 23, 25]. As a result, dynamic recrystal-
lization (DRX) can easily occur when deformed at elevated
temperatures for magnesium alloys, nucleated by various
mechanisms such as grain boundary nucleation (GBN), sub-
grain boundary migration (SBM), shear band nucleation
(SBN), deformation twin nucleation (DTN) and particle-
stimulated nucleation (PSN) [24, 26–28]. Subsequently, many
deformation methods at elevated temperatures were devel-
oped to simultaneously reﬁne microstructures and improve
mechanical properties of magnesium alloys [ 29, 30]. Since
different alloying elements have different effects on the
temperature-sensitive stacking faults of magnesium, the
deformation temperature will inﬂuence the microstructures,
ﬁnally on the mechanical properties. For example, Park et al.
[31] investigated the effects of extrusion parameters on the
microstructure and tensile properties of the Mg–2Zn–0.5Ce,
Mg–1Zn–1Mn–0.5Ce and Mg–2Zn–1.5Gd alloys and found
that the size and the content of the DRX grains are closely
related to the extrusion parameters such as temperature.
Therefore, the unexpected strength of the extruded ZK60 alloy
with Gd addition might be probably attributed to the unrea-
sonable selections of the extrusion parameters such as the
extrusion temperature. In other words, it is important to
investigate the effects of extrusion temperature on the
microstructures and the mechanical properties of the ZK60
alloy with minor Gd addition (cost reduction). In this work, the
microstructure and tensile properties of ZK60 and ZEK620
alloys extruded at various temperatures were studied. Mean-
while, the strength mechanisms were discussed based on the
results of microstructural analysis and the improvements in
mechanical properties.
2 Experimental
The ZK60 and ZEK620 alloys were prepared from Mg
(99.95%), Zn (99.95%), Mg–23 wt%Gd and Mg–33 wt%Zr
master alloys in an electric resistance furnace under the
protection of an CO
2 ? 1 vol% SF 6 mixed gas. The melt
was fully stirred at 740 /C176C for about 10 min, held for 40 min
and then poured into a water-cooled steel mold with the
temperature of approximately 24 /C176C and a diameter of
95 mm when the melt was cooled down to approximately
710 /C176C. The chemical compositions of the obtained alloys
were determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic
emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES, JY-Ultima 2), the results
of which are given in Table 1.
Before extrusion, the billets were machined into round
bars with diameter and length of approximately 82 and
120 mm, respectively. The bars were extruded at 290 /C176C
(E290), 305 /C176C (E305), 320 /C176C (E320), 340 /C176C (E340) and
360 /C176C (E360). To avoid the inﬂuence of other extrusion
parameters on the result analysis, a coincident extrusion
ratio (18:1) along with a same ram speed (0.4 mm /C1 s
-1) was
preserved for all extrusion processes. The extrusion bars
were quenched by room temperature (24 /C176C) water.
All samples for microstructural characterizations were
grounded using different grades of polishing papers, sub-
sequently polished with Al 2O3 paste and then etched in a
solution of 5 g picric acid, 5 ml acetic acid, 10 ml H 2O and
100 ml ethanol. The microstructures were characterized by
optical microscopy (OM, Olympus GX71) and scanning
electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S4800) equipped with
an energy-dispersive spectroscope (EDS). The grain size
(D) was measured by the linear intercept method using the
equation of D = 1.56 * C/(MN), where C is the total
length of test line used, N is the number of intercepts and
M is the magniﬁcation of the photomicrograph. XRD
investigations were conducted using a Siemens diffrac-
tometer operated at 40 kV and 40 mA with Cu K a radia-
tion (k = 0.15406 nm), with scanning scale of 10/C176–80/C176and
speed of 4 ( /C176)/C1 min
-1.
Cylindrical tensile specimens were mechanically pre-
pared with a gauge diameter of 6 mm and a gauge length of
36 mm, and the morphology of tensile test specimen is
shown in Fig. 1. All tensile tests at room temperature
(24 /C176C) and high temperatures (150 and 200 /C176C) were
conducted in accordance with ISO 9513 standard. The
tensile test was carried out on an Instron 5869 testing

least three times to ensure reproducibility.

3 Results and discussion
3.1 Microstructures of as-cast samples
Figure 2 shows OM images of the as-cast ZK60 and
ZEK620 alloys. The results show that the microstructures
consist of granular a-Mg and intermetallic particles which
are mainly located on grain boundaries. It can be seen that
Gd addition can reﬁne the grains of the as-cast ZK60 alloy,
with grain size being (36 ± 8) lm, which is much smaller
than that of ZK60 alloy (65 ± 15) lm. In addition, Gd
addition slightly increases the content of intermetallic
particles in the ZK60 alloy and also changes their mor-
phologies, which can be clearly distinguished in SEM
images presented in Fig. 3a–d. The inter-metallic particles
in the as-cast ZK60 alloy mainly exhibit lump morphology,
which change into network and blocky morphology after
Gd addition. Also, there are clearly more intermetallic
particles (or eutectic region), but the supersaturated solid
solution regions near the grain boundaries are distinctly
reduced after Gd addition. Based on EDS analysis results,
the dominant intermetallic phase in the as-cast ZEK620
alloy is possibly Mg
3Zn3Gd2 phase (W phase) [ 15] while
MgZn2 phase [20] in the ZK60 alloy.
XRD patterns of as-cast alloys are shown in Fig. 4.I ti s
obvious that the dominant intermetallic phase peaks in the
ZEK620 alloy are very different from those in the ZK60
alloy, indicating different intermetallic phases. Based on
the inorganic crystal structure database (ICSD), XRD
peaks of the intermetallic phases in ZK60 and ZEK620
alloys can well be indexed by the widely reported MgZn
2
phase and W phase, respectively. In addition, a greater
intensity of the intermetallic phase in the ZEK620 alloy
than that in the ZK60 alloy indicates more intermetallic
particles. These results are well in line with those afore-
mentioned by OM and SEM–EDS results.

3.2 Microstructures of extruded samples
Figures 5 and 6 show the comparison of OM images of as-
extruded ZK60 and ZEK620 alloys, respectively, with
extrusion temperatures ( T) of 290, 305, 320, 340 and
360 /C176C. All extruded alloys exhibit a bimodal grain struc-
ture composed of ﬁne equiaxed recrystallization grains and
elongated and coarse non-recrystallization (unDRX) grains.
There are no obvious intermetallic phase stringers in the
extruded ZK60 alloy, where thermodynamic DRX grain is
ﬁne but the unDRX grains are relatively elongated and
irregular. After Gd addition (Fig. 6), there are some extru-
sion stringers which are roughly parallel to extrusion
direction (ED), although the DRX grain size and the content
of the unDRX regions cannot be distinguished. Figure 7a–e
illustrates corresponding SEM images of the as-extruded
ZEK620 alloys with various extrusion temperatures. It can
be seen that the extrusion stringers mainly consist of dis-
integrated intermetallic particles parallel to the ED. From
SEM image with a representative magniﬁed image shown
in Fig. 7f, the DRX grains and the unDRX grains can be
discriminated. Therefore, for direct comparison, the statistic
values of the DRX grain sizes and the content of the
unDRX grains are listed in Table 2. It can be seen that the
DRX grain size monotonously increases as the extrusion
temperature increases, while the content of the unDRX
grains monotonously decreases simultaneously for ZK60
alloy. However, the content of the unDRX grains of
ZEK620 alloy starts to increase after an initial decrease,
although the DRX grain size also monotonously increases
as the extrusion temperature increases.

3.4 Discussion
Based on the aforementioned results, it can be concluded
that Gd addition can distinctly reﬁne the grains of both as-
cast and as-extruded ZK60 alloy. Generally, grain reﬁne-
ment might be attributed to the enrichment of Gd atoms in
front of the solid–liquid interface during solidiﬁcation,
which would result in a constitutional supercooled area that
accelerates nucleation rate and restricts dendrite growth
[32–34]. Generally, the more reﬁned the grains of the
casting alloy are, the ﬁner the extruded grains will be [ 35].
In addition, Fig. 3 indicates that there are much more and
more stable intermetallic particles in the ZEK620 alloy
compared with the ZK60 alloy, which results in more
nucleation sites for recrystallization based on PSN mech-
anism [28], and they can effectively impede the DRX grain
growth during hot extrusion [ 36]. Subsequently, the as-
extruded ZEK620 samples have ﬁner DRX grains than the
as-extruded ZK60 samples with comparative extrusion
conditions. Furthermore, the DRX grain size for both as-
extruded ZK60 and ZEK620 alloys is closely related to the
extrusion temperatures. Combined the Zener–Hollomon
equation [ 31, 37] and the experimental conditions in this
work, the Zener–Hollomon ( Z) parameter will decrease as
extrusion temperature increases. Since the average DRX
grain size is a monotonous function of Z, the DRX grain
size for both as-extruded ZK60 and ZEK620 alloys will
increase as the extrusion temperature increases.
Ordinarily, yield strength and grain size follow the Hall–
Petch relation:

where ry is the yield strength, r0 and k are constants and
d is the grain size. According to the Hall–Petch relation, the
yield strength thus increases with the grain size decreasing.
In this work, an increase in the extrusion temperature
causes an increase in DRX grain size and thus an
approximate decrease in the yield strength for both as-ex-
truded ZK60 and ZEK620 alloys. In addition, the DRX
grain sizes of the extruded ZEK620 samples with various
extrusion temperatures are much smaller than those of the
extruded ZK60 samples with comparative extrusion tem-
perature. Therefore, Gd addition can further improve the
strength of the as-extruded ZK60 alloy.
In addition, it also should be noted that there are much
more intermetallic particles which are sharply disintegrated
and mainly distributed along ED in the extruded ZEK620
alloy. It is well known that intermetallic particles can
efﬁciently pin grain boundaries and impede dislocation
motions during deformation. In addition, the dominant
intermetallic phase in the ZEK620 alloy is W phase, which
is much thermodynamically stable than that (MgZn
2 phase)
in the ZK60 alloy. Therefore, the extruded ZEK620 alloy
owns higher strength compared with the extruded ZK60
alloy.

during hot extrusion process for magnesium alloys due to
that the grains are oriented with their basal planes parallel
to the ED. It is reported that the strong ﬁber texture will
result in an easy deformation by twinning during tensile
deformation along ED and thus a decrease in yield strength
[31, 38]. Ordinarily, the larger the content of the unDRX
grains is, the stronger texture for the as-extruded samples
will be. [ 37]. From Figs. 5, 6 and 7, it is noted that as the

4 Conclusion
Gd addition can effectively reﬁne the microstructures of
ZK60 alloy and change the dominant intermetallic phase
from MgZn
2 to W phase. In addition, it will also increase
the content of intermetallic particles. Subsequently, Gd
addition can also improve the tensile strength of the as-
extruded ZK60 alloy at both room temperature and high
temperatures, although the ductility and the work harden-
ing response simultaneously decrease, with the maximum
UTS and TYS of 405 and 375 MPa, respectively. However,
Gd addition will also exacerbate the temperature sensitivity
of the tensile strength for the as-extruded ZK60 alloy.
