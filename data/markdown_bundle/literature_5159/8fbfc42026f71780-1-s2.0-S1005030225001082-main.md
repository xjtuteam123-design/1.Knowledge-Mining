Achieving outstanding room and high-temperature 
mechanical properties matching in a near α
high-temperature titanium alloy with ﬁne lamellar 
microstructure and nanosilicides 
1. Introduction 
Titanium alloys are widely used in aerospace and weaponry 
manufacturing due to their high speciﬁc strength, low density, 
heat and corrosion resistance [ 1 , 2 ]. However, new titanium alloys 
with excellent room-temperature and high-temperature mechani- 
cal properties remain rare and hard to obtain in industries such 
as aerospace, marine engineering and equipment manufacturing 
[ 3 , 4 ]. It is worth noting that severe plastic deformation (SPD) such 
as equal channel angular pressing (ECAP), rolling, and die forg- 
ing are commonly applied to different titanium alloys to achieve 
a trade-off between high strength and ductility [ 5–8 ]. However, 
these processes are frequently complex and costly, making the de- 
velopment of a short-ﬂow, low-cost process for preparing high- 
performance titanium alloys particularly important. Process opti- 
mization improves the material’s microstructure, which in turn di- 
rectly determines the mechanical properties of the alloy [ 9 , 10 ]. 
Elucidation of plastic microstructural deformation mechanisms en- 
ables titanium alloys to withstand more complex service condi- 
tions and guides designing titanium alloys with high strength and 
ductility [ 11 ]. 
The strength and ductility of metals are usually related to dislo- 
cation slip with external loads [ 12 ]. Dislocation activity affects the 
homogeneous plastic strain regulation of the material, and when 
grain boundaries and precipitation phases are encountered, the en- 
tanglement of dislocations can lead to localized stress concentra- 
tions [ 13 , 14 ]. By appropriately adjusting the size, morphology, and 
distribution of the α-phase and precipitates, it is possible to intro- 
duce multilevel heterogeneous microstructures in alloys, resulting 
in a dramatic increase in the energy barriers for dislocation ini- 
tiation and expansion, and a strength and ductility trade-off [ 15–
17 ]. Recently, it has been reported that ⟨ c + a ⟩ dislocation activity 
confers improved toughness and ductility to titanium and titanium 
alloys [ 18 , 19 ]. Our recent studies have shown that nanoscale sili- 
cides activate a pyramidal ⟨ c + a ⟩ slip system that improves duc- 
tility by limiting substrate ⟨ a ⟩ slip [ 20 ]. Moreover, titanium alloys 
with lamellar structures usually have excellent tensile strength, 
and βt suppresses grain boundary rotation and thus increases the 
deformation resistance of the structure [ 21 ]. The grain deforma- 
tion behavior at high temperatures is essential to give full play 
to the high-temperature mechanical properties of metallic mate- 
rials, and high-temperature ‘inverse’ Hall-Petch behavior has been 
observed in high-temperature plastic deformation [ 22 , 23 ], where 
equiaxed grains and the like can achieve room-temperature high 
strengths at room temperatures, but lamellar crystals can avoid 
high-temperature softening to improve high-temperature strengths 
[ 24 , 25 ]. The lamellar microstructure is distributed with interlay- 
ered α phases, and these α communities provide more α/ β phase 
boundaries, which “restrain” each other, making the crack exten- 
sion path more tortuous, and the hard silicides will be pinned 
to the matrix during the crack extension process, which makes 
the crack extension blocked, and thus improves the deformation 
resistance of the alloy [ 26 ]. During deformation, dislocations ac- 
cumulate within the grain, and the dislocation behavior of tita- 
nium alloys under tensile stress at both room and high tempera- 
tures requires further study. This research designs a near- α high- 
temperature titanium alloy and investigates the impact of mi- 
crostructure on strength and ductility, aiming to provide insights 
into achieving optimal strength-ductility synergies at both room 
and elevated temperatures. 
2. Experimental 
Ti-7Al-2.5Sn-9Zr-0.8Nb-0.5Mo-0.8W-0.45Si (in wt.%) near- α
high-temperature titanium alloys based on the [Al]eq and [Mo]eq 
standards was designed and produced by vacuum suspension melt- 
ing. The β phase transition temperature of the alloy was measured 
metallographically to be 970 °C. The as-cast alloy was solution 
treated at 1200 °C for 1.5 h to obtain a supersaturated solid solu- 
tion alloy and air cooled. The ingot was then subjected to one pass 
of bloom forging at 1050 °C ( β-phase region). Temperature tensile 
tests were carried out with an Instron-5969 temperature tensile 
testing machine at a beam speed of 0.5 mm/min at room and high 
temperatures, with at least two tensile tests at each condition to 
ensure data repeatability and high-temperature test temperatures 
of 650 °C and 700 °C. Microstructural and phase characterizations 
were undertaken by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Gemini 
SEM 300), electron backscattering diffraction (EBSD), and transmis- 
sion electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai F20). Nanoprecipitates 
were analyzed with Image-Pro Plus software.

Fig. 2 shows the TEM analysis of the alloy with the α lamel-

at the interface, producing strong lattice distortions. Geometrical 
phase analysis (GPA), which quantitatively analyses strain, can be 
used to verify the in-plane strain distribution at the atomic level 
[ 32 ]. GPA analysis of the HRTEM image of Fig. 2(b) was performed

corresponds to the red region indicating a strong local strain ﬁeld 
distributed around the α-phase. Dislocations generated by forging 
deformation are tangled and aggregated around the silicides. Quan- 
titative analysis showed that the corresponding tensor difference 
for α is 25 %, while the corresponding tensor difference for S2 is 
12 % ( Fig. 2(e) ). Dislocations interact with the matrix or silicides, 
causing severe lattice distortions and hindering dislocation motion 
( Fig. 2(f) ). 
3.2. Tensile properties 
Fig. 3 (a, b ) shows the room temperature stress–strain curves of 
the alloy in the as-cast and as-forged states as well as the high- 
temperature tensile stress–strain curves after forging. After forging, 
the room temperature tensile strength of the alloy is 1118.4 MPa 
with a ductility of 15.2 %. Compared to the as-cast sample, the 
ultimate tensile strength increases by 74 MPa, and ductility im- 
proves due to the elimination of casting defects and the beneﬁ- 
cial lamellar microstructure. At high temperatures, the alloy main- 
tains a strong strength-ductility balance, with a tensile strength of 
772.5 MPa and ductility of 20.5 % at 650 °C, and further improving 
to 618.7 MPa strength and 48.1 % ductility at 700 °C. The superior 
room temperature ductility is attributed to the longer α lamella, 
which facilitates dislocation movement. The alloy shows mini- 
mal softening at high temperatures due to the grain boundaries 
in the lamellar structure, which increase the total grain bound- 
ary area, reduce dislocation accumulation, and enable the mate- 
rial to withstand higher stresses. Additionally, the hard S2 phase 
links the softer αp phase to the β phase. Interestingly, the forged 
alloys all exhibit a better synergy of high-temperature strength 
and ductility than most near- α Ti alloys and Ti matrix composites 
[ 25 , 27 , 33–41 ].

bution and slip mechanism in the necking region of the fracture 
tensile samples were analyzed as well as TEM analysis ( Fig. 4 ) to

reveal the room temperature ductility mechanism. Statistical anal- 
yses show that the alloy is mainly involved in the pyramidal slip 
system and a small amount of prismatic slip system during room 
temperature tensile deformation ( Fig. 4 (a–c)), enhance pyramidal 
⟨ c + a ⟩ as the primary slip mechanism [ 42 ]. Investigating the SF 
distribution within the deformed grains as well as in-grain misori- 
entation axes (IGMA) analyses [ 43 ], it is found that the deformed 
grains are dominated by pyramidal slip ( Fig. 4(d) ) and the IGMA 
distribution shows a high intensity at the ⟨10¯1 0 ⟩ axes, and by 
matching the Taylor axes to the IGMA, this type of IGMA distri- 
bution can be attributed to the main activation of pyramidal slip. 
It shows that within the lamellar α grain, pyramidal ⟨ a + c ⟩ acti- 
vation is more prevalent and the dominant slip mode is pyramidal 
⟨ a + c ⟩ slip. TEM characterization was carried out to clarify ma- 
trix slip mechanisms in alloy, using the g /22c5b invisibility criterion, 
where g denotes the diffraction vector, and b represents the Burg- 
ers vector [ 44 ]. Speciﬁcally, ⟨ a ⟩ , ⟨ c ⟩ , and ⟨ c + a ⟩ dislocations were 
analyzed using [0 0 02] and [01 ¯1 0] diffraction vectors, respectively. 
Results show that ⟨ c + a ⟩ dislocations are prominent in the al- 
loy, while ⟨ c ⟩ , ⟨ a ⟩ dislocations are less frequent ( Fig. 4 ( e , f )), which 
shows that the pyramidal slip mainly controls the plastic deforma- 
tion. 
Samples with interwoven lamellar structures, especially those 
with elongated α- lamellar layers, maintain an excellent strength- 
ductility match at high temperatures. The SF distribution of the 
grains at 700 °C is shown in Fig. 5(a) , where it can be seen that the 
basal slip and prismatic slip systems are not signiﬁcantly activated, 
whereas the pyramidal slip system is widely activated. A large 
number of ⟨ a + c ⟩ -type dislocations are distributed in the TEM- 
analyzed lamellae α ( Fig. 5 ( b , c )) which suggests that the same 
pyramidal slip predominates during the 700 °C stretching process, 
which matches its high tensile ductility. TEM morphology analy- 
sis at the tensile neck-down shows that the soft α is preferentially 
deformed at high temperatures, and thus a large number of slip 
bands are distributed within the lamellae α ( Fig. 5(d) ). However, 
the S2 and β phases are signiﬁcantly harder than the α phase at 
high temperatures, and thus the slip of dislocations is inhibited at 
the silicide and α/ β phase boundaries leading to dislocation plug- 
ging ( Fig. 5 ( e , f )). HRTEM and GPA analyses at α/ β ( Fig. 5 (g–g2)) 
showed the accumulation of many dislocations at grain bound- 
aries and a higher strain gradient within the α phase ( Fig. 5(h) ). 
A schematic provides a better understanding of the movement of 
dislocations inside the lamellae α during high temperature stretch- 
ing ( Fig. 5(i) ). Dislocations were able to have longer slip routes in 
the elongated lamellae, however, upon encountering silicides and β
phase, the slip process was blocked, leading to the accumulation 
of dislocations. This results in a dislocation slip process where the 
interwoven structure of ﬁne α lamellae provides suﬃcient loca- 
tions for dislocation stacking while precipitation phases and grain 
boundaries dramatically increase the energy barriers for disloca- 
tion initiation and extension [ 45 , 46 ]. 
4. Conclusion 
In summary, this study developed the lamellar microstruc- 
ture of a high-temperature titanium alloy through short-process 
forging. The alloy demonstrates an excellent strength-ductility 
synergy at both room and high temperatures. The outstand- 
ing room-temperature mechanical properties are primarily due 
to the ﬁne incipient α-lamellae and silicides, which enhance 
non-basal slip activation, improving ductility and promoting 
deformation under tensile loading. At high temperatures, the 
main strengthening mechanism is the restriction of primary 
α-phase deformation by lamellar structures and hard silicide 
precipitation.
