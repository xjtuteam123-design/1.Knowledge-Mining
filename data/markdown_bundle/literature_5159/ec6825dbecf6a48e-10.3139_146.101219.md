On the creep resistance in cast Ni-base
superalloys
In this paper, we attempt to apply the modified hardening
theory to estimate the creep resistance, which is considered
as a key factor for controlling the creep deformation mecha-
nism in cast nickel-base superalloys. It is suggested that,
when the applied stress is high enough for the dislocations
to cut into the c
0 particles, the creep resistance is almost a
constant and independent of applied stress. At low applied
stress, creep deformation is mainly controlled by disloca-
tions climb, where the creep resistance has two components
of a threshold stress and a friction stress. The model is veri-
fied for two cast nickel-base superalloys DZ17G and
IN738. The results of detailed calculations are in good
agreement with the experimental data.
Keywords: Creep resistance; Creep deformation mecha-
nism; Cast nickel-base superalloy
1. Introduction
Cast nickel-base superalloys have been widely used for
blades and vanes in aircraft engines and land-based gas tur-
bines operating at high temperature, where creep is one of
the important life-limiting factors [1 – 5]. Although creep
behavior of nickel-base superalloys has been extensively
studied over the last five decades, there is incomplete un-
derstanding of the detailed creep mechanism [6].
For pure metals and simple alloys, most creep models
and experimental data show that the steady-state or mini-
mum creep rate can be described satisfactorily by a power-
law equation:

for self-diffusion QSD), R the gas constant, T the absolute
temperature, and A1 a structure-dependent parameter. When
this equation is applied in particle-strengthened systems,
much higher values of n and Qc are obtained. For example,
it is found that n can range from 3 to 7 in nickel-base super-
alloys [7 – 11]. Equation (1) can be rationalized in terms of

Here rr represents the creep resistance. The experimental
results in a large number of particle-hardened systems have
successfully been expressed by equation (2) with n– 4 and
Qc – QSD. In contrast to the disperse strengthening systems
in which rr is considered as a threshold stress from Orowan
bowing, however, both the origin and physical interpreta-
tion of rr in nickel-base superalloys has been subject to con-
siderable controversy [5, 12 – 15]. Also, it has been found
that n varies in different applied stress regions with the tran-
sition of the creep deformation mechanism in nickel-base
superalloys [13, 16].
Furthermore, particle-strengthening models have made
remarkable progress [17 – 19]. Especially, the Labusch –
Schwarz theory [20] has been successfully applied to esti-
mate the strength of wrought nickel-base superalloys with
c
0 particle volume fractions of between 7 % and 47 % [18 –
24]. However, the application of the Labusch – Schwarz the-
ory to high-temperature creep strength in cast nickel-base
superalloys with high volume fractions of c0 precipitates
(between 40 % and 70 % in general) has not been discussed
in the literature [7].
In the present paper, we attempt to apply the Labusch –
Schwarz hardening theory to estimate the creep resistance
in two cast nickel-base superalloys. The results of the calcu-
lations will be compared with the experimental data of a
conventionally cast nickel-base superalloy IN738 and a di-
rectionally solidified nickel-base superalloy DZ17G with
c
0 particle volume fractions of 50 % and 65 %, respectively.
We think our analytical results will contribute to a better un-
derstanding of creep resistance and deformation mecha-
nisms at high temperature in cast nickel-base superalloys.

3. Calculation
3.1. Particle cutting or Orowan looping?
In nickel-base superalloys, the c0 particles act as obstacles
to mobile dislocations. The particles may be cut by single
or pairs of dislocations thus creating stacking faults or
APBs in the c0 particles, or be circumvented by the Orowan
process which leaves a dislocation loop around each parti-
cle. There is evidence by many TEM studies that particle
cutting rather than Orowan looping is the dominant creep
deformation mechanism at high stress levels [13, 25, 27].
This is not surprising if one calculates the threshold stresses
for both mechanisms. According to Brown and Ham [17],
the threshold stresses for particle cutting (PC) and Orowan
looping (OL) can be evaluated by:

4. Discussion
On the basis of the above experimental results and calcula-
tions, we propose a modified model relating the creep
deformation mechanism to the creep resistance in cast
nickel-base superalloys. In the high-stress region where
the applied stress is high enough for operating particle cut-
ting mechanism, the creep deformation at a higher creep
rate is mainly controlled by dislocations cutting through
the c0 precipitates, and the Orowan looping becomes a pos-
sible mechanism towards the end of very long creep tests.
Consequently, the creep resistance is a threshold stress,

r0 = rct + k(ra – rct) (9)
Here, k is a constant and depends on the testing tempera-
ture. The first term rct is a threshold stress for the climb
mechanism, and the latter depends directly on the applied
stress.
When the applied stress is equal to a changeover stress,
where both the particle cutting and the general climb pro-
cess are possible to operate, the creep resistance both for
particle cutting and for general climb will be the same:
rPC = rct + k(rV – rct) (10)
Here rV is the changeover stress, rGC the threshold stress
for the general climb mechanism, k a constant. The calcu-
lated values of parameter k are about 0.44 for alloy DZ17G
and 0.49 for alloy IN738, which is in close agreement with
the measured result (0.4 – 0.7) in nickel-base superalloys.

now examine the apparent stress exponent by using
Eq. (2). Figure 5 shows the double-logarithmic plot of

As a summary, the following conclusions can be ob-
tained:
In the high-stress region, the creep deformation process
with a higher creep rate is mainly controlled by particle cut-
ting, where the creep resistance is a constant threshold stress.
In the low-stress region, dislocations may only overcome
c0 particles by diffusion-controlled climb, where the creep
resistance has two components of a threshold stress and a
friction stress.
The creep resistance can be estimated by the modified
hardening theories, and the detailed calculations are in good
agreement with the experimental data in two cast nickel-
base superalloys.
