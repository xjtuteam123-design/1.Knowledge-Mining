ABSTRACT
The pursuit of advanced structural materials for use in extreme environments, specifically those exceeding 
1200
◦
C in aerospace, energy, and defense applications, has exposed the fundamental limitations of conventional 
Ni-based superalloys. In this context, refractory complex concentrated alloys (RCCAs) have emerged as a 
transformative materials paradigm, promising a unique combination of ultra-high temperature strength, 
exceptional microstructural stability, and superior creep resistance. This review provides a comprehensive and 
critical examination of the rapid advancements in the design and development of RCCAs. It begins by synthe -
sizing the evolution of alloy design methodologies, tracing the progression from empirical and semi-empirical 
criteria to the integration of sophisticated computational tools, including computational thermodynamics 
(CALPHAD), first-principles calculations (DFT), and data-driven machine learning (ML) techniques for acceler -
ated discovery. The discussion then delves into the microstructural engineering of RCCAs, highlighting archi -
tected phases such as coherent BCC/B2 nanocomposites that mimic the strengthening mechanisms of superalloys 
yet extend their operational ceiling. A thorough analysis of mechanical and environmental properties, encom -
passing strength-ductility synergies, creep, fatigue, and oxidation resistance, is presented, underscoring both 
remarkable achievements and enduring challenges, particularly in room-temperature ductility and long-term 
environmental durability. The review further assesses scalable manufacturing pathways, such as additive 
manufacturing, and identifies critical roadblocks to industrial scalability and adoption. By converging funda -
mental insights with advanced design and processing strategies, this review aims to chart a course for realizing 
the full potential of RCCAs as next-generation materials for the most demanding technological applications.

temperatures, complicating fabrication, joining, and large-scale pro -
cessing [34] . Consequently, while refractory alloys extend the operating 
window, they fail to deliver the multifunctionality demanded by next- 
generation energy, aerospace, and defense systems.
In response to these challenges, the concept of refractory complex 
concentrated alloys (RCCAs), including refractory high-entropy alloys 
(RHEAs), has emerged as a transformative materials design paradigm 
[35 – 37] . By incorporating multiple principal refractory elements in 
near-equiatomic or high-concentration ratios, RCCAs greatly expand the 
compositional design space beyond the corner-limited approach of 
traditional alloy systems [38,39] . Their potential advantages are antic -
ipated to stem from four “ core effects ” [40] : (i) high configurational 
entropy, which stabilizes simple solid-solution phases and suppresses 
the formation of brittle intermetallics; (ii) severe lattice distortion, 
which enhances solid-solution strengthening and impedes dislocation 
motion; (iii) sluggish diffusion, which slows down creep, grain coars -
ening, and oxidation kinetics; and (iv) the cocktail effect, which enables 
unique synergies among constituent elements, yielding properties su -
perior to simple rule-of-mixtures predictions [40,41] . Together, these 
mechanisms underpin the development of alloys that balance high- 
temperature strength, microstructural stability, and environmental 
durability in ways unattainable with conventional materials. Among 
existing RCCAs systems, the NbMoTaW and NbMoTaWV systems are the 
most widely studied and demonstrate exceptional strength at tempera -
tures above 1000
◦
C [42] . However, like many early RCCAs, they often 
suffer from intrinsic brittleness [42,43] . In contrast, some RCCAs such as 
TiZrNbHf and TiZrNbHfTa exhibit improved room-temperature ductility 
[44 – 46] . This diversity in properties underscores the broad tunability of 
RCCAs and highlights opportunities for designing alloys tailored to 
specific application requirements [47] . More recently, advanced alloy 
design has incorporated nanoprecipitation strengthening, with coherent 
B2 nanoprecipitates embedded in a BCC matrix. This architecture 
mimics the strengthening mechanism of Ni-based superalloys while 
extending operational capabilities to even higher temperatures [48] .

These developments signal a significant shift from traditional alloy 
design toward a new, fundamentally multi-principal-element paradigm.
While RCCAs offer promising high-temperature strength and 
microstructure stability, many systems still suffer from limited room- 
temperature ductility and inadequate oxidation resistance [36,37] . To 
overcome these limitations, recent research has increasingly focused on 
heterogeneous structural design as a pathway to enhancing mechanical 
properties [47] . Departing from single-phase homogeneous micro -
structures, heterogeneous-structured RCCAs intentionally incorporate 
features such as soft and hard domains, multiphase architectures, or 
nanoscale compositional fluctuations to activate hetero-deformation- 
induced (HDI) hardening [47] . Alternative strategies are also 
emerging. For instance, the introduction of oxygen-induced ordered 
complexes in TiZrNbHf alloys has been shown to simultaneously in -
crease yield strength and ductility by regulating dislocation mobility 
and promoting cross-slip [49,50] . Metastability engineering in the 
TiZrHfTa system can lead to transformation-induced ductility (TRIP), 
which enhances work hardening capacity and delays plastic localization 
[51] . Likewise, carefully designed precipitate-hardened RCCAs utilize 
coherent nanoprecipitates to achieve significant strengthening without 
severe ductility loss [52] . This rapid progress underscores the need to 
consolidate existing knowledge to guide future development. This re -
view aims to synthesize current research on RCCAs, with a specific focus 
on the design and fabrication of multifunctional RCCAs for high- 
temperature applications. We will examine fundamental principles in 
compositional design, microstructural engineering, and processing 
routes. Special attention is given to strategies that enhance oxidation 
resistance, improve ductility, and ensure long-term thermal stability, as 
well as to the integration of heterogeneous structural concepts into 
RCCA design. By synergistically leveraging compositional complexity 
and structural heterogeneity, RCCAs hold the potential to achieve an 
unprecedented combination of high-temperature strength, oxidation 
resistance, ductility, and microstructural stability required for the most 
demanding future applications.
2. Design methodologies and strategies
2.1. Empirical and Semi-Empirical approaches
2.1.1. Element Selection criteria
RCCAs are typically composed of refractory metallic elements from 
Groups IV to VI of the periodic table, including Ti, V, Cr, Zr, Nb, Mo, Hf, 
Ta, and W as shown in Fig. 2 . These refractory elements generally 
exhibit high melting points (typically > 1800
◦
C), high strength, and 
excellent thermal stability [53] , which endow RCCAs with outstanding 
high-temperature performance. Tuning the elemental composition and 
concentration of RCCAs enables the emergence of novel material prop -
erties, which serves as a fundamental strategy in designing advanced 
refractory alloys. [54] . For instance, Tsuru et al. [55] , through first- 
principles simulations of equiatomic VNbTaMoW and TiZrNbHfTa al -
loys, demonstrated that the plastic deformation behavior of RCCAs can 
be tailored by adjusting the concentration of HCP elements such as Ti, 
Zr, and Hf. In addition, Gao et al. [56] , based on feature analysis, 
revealed that among common refractory elements, the contents of Cr 
and Mo exert the most significant influence on the corrosion resistance 
of RCCAs. Specifically, a Cr concentration exceeding 12 at.% can serve 
as a screening criterion for identifying highly corrosion-resistant RCCAs.
To tailor the microstructure and further improve the performance of 
alloys, many non-metallic elements (such as Si [57 – 59] , O [50,60,61] , 
and N [62 – 64] ) have also been incorporated into RCCAs. For example, 
alloying with Si and Nb can significantly enhance the high-temperature

entropy alloying, namely forming a BCC single-phase solid solution 
with severe lattice distortion by mixing multiple refractory elements, 
thereby achieving excellent mechanical and physicochemical properties 
[36,73] . However, after years of investigation, it has been recognized 
that due to the compositional complexity of RCCAs and variations in 
post-processing methods, RCCAs can exhibit a wide range of phase 
structures, including BCC, B2, and various other intermetallic com -

Beyond phase prediction, several parameters have been proposed to 
guide the mechanical design of RCCAs. Sheikh et al. [89] , by comparing 
ductile and brittle RCCAs, suggested that in order to intrinsically duc -

range of high-entropy alloys, identified a brittle “ VEC valley ” between

lation between VEC and ductility of RCCAs originates from Jahn – Teller 
distortions induced by electron – phonon interactions [90] . However, 
VEC also has inherent limitations, as it merely provides a numerical 
count of valence electrons without fully capturing the complex varia -
tions in electronic structure across different alloys. More advanced 
density functional theory (DFT) calculations have shown that the elec -
tronic density of states (DOS) at the Fermi level can serve as a key in -
dicator for further evaluating the strength – ductility synergy of RCCAs 
[91,92] . Specifically, the intrinsic ductility trends are associated with 
critical features of the DOS, namely the fraction of occupied bonding 
states and its bimodal distribution [91] .
In addition, recent studies [93 – 96] have demonstrated that the 
mixing enthalpy can also be employed as a parameter in the toughening 
design of RCCAs. On the one hand, Lai et al. [94] , by analyzing the 
intrinsic correlations between physical parameters and mechanical 
properties across a wide range of RCCAs, found that even when alloys 
possess relatively low VEC values ( < 4.5), excessively negative mixing 
enthalpies tend to cause brittle fracture, likely due to the formation of 
brittle intermetallic compounds. On the other hand, An et al. [93] found 
that adding Al to the HfNbTiV alloy while maintaining the solid solution 
can reduce the mixing enthalpy of the alloy system, which promotes the 
formation of hierarchical chemical fluctuations, enabling the alloy to 
significantly increase uniform elongation with high yield strength. 
Therefore, careful control of mixing enthalpy is crucial for the rational 
design of RCCAs. In summary, empirical models offer a convenient and 
flexible approach for predicting the structure and properties of newly 
designed RCCAs. Nevertheless, given the inherent limitations of 
empirical and semi-empirical parameters, their integration with 
advanced computational approaches (e.g., high-throughput experiments 
and artificial intelligence (AI)) represents an inevitable pathway for 
future RCCA design.
2.2. Computational and High-Throughput design
2.2.1. CALPHAD calculation of phase diagrams
Material properties are intrinsically linked to their constituent pha -
ses [97,98] . For high-temperature structural applications, phase stability 
is particularly critical, as phase decomposition or precipitation often 
leads to deleterious embrittlement [99] . Phase diagrams offer an intui -
tive framework for studying this stability; however, obtaining accurate 
diagrams for complex alloys through experimental or semi-empirical 
methods is challenging. For multi-component systems such as HEAs, 
the CALPHAD (i.e., Calculation of Phase Diagrams) method is currently 
the most efficient approach for predicting phase equilibria [97,100,101] . 
This technique calculates the Gibbs free energy to determine the equi -
librium state under specified conditions. The calculation incorporates 
not only the ideal configurational entropy of mixing but also contribu -
tions from binary and ternary subsystems, as interactions in higher- 
order subsystems are generally negligible [97,102] . This approach 
highlights two crucial considerations: first, configurational entropy 
represents only one component of the Gibbs free energy and should not 
be used as the sole criterion for assessing phase stability. Second, the 
accuracy of CALPHAD predictions for multi-component systems de -
pends heavily on the reliability of the thermodynamic data for their 
underlying binary and ternary subsystems.
The practical application of the CALPHAD method is exemplified in 
the work of Coury et al. [98] , who used the commercial Thermo-Calc 
software with the TCHEA1 and TCHEA2 databases to predict phases in 
12 quaternary RCCAs. Their analysis revealed that the predictions from 
the TCHEA2 database showed greater consistency with experimental 
characterization, particularly for Laves phases. This improvement was 
attributed to TCHEA2 ′ s more comprehensive incorporation of critical 
ternary phase diagram data, which was less complete in the TCHEA1 
database [98] . Li et al. successfully use the CALPHAD to screen out the 
phase stability and mechanical properties of TiZrHfNb based RCCAs as 
shown in Fig. 3 [103] . To manage the vast compositional space of 
RCCAs, Senkov et al. [102,104] employed CALPHAD to construct over 
130,000 phase diagrams for 3 – 6 component equimolar alloys – a task 
infeasible through experimentation alone. To estimate the credibility of 
these high-throughput calculations, they introduced two key metrics: 
the Fraction of Assessed Binary systems (FAB) and the Fraction of 
Assessed Ternary systems (FAT). For a given alloy and database, higher 
FAB and FAT values indicate that a greater proportion of its constituent 
binary and ternary subsystems are well-described, thereby implying 
more reliable predictions. Their calculations (performed with FAB = 1, 
FAT < 1) demonstrated that the probability of forming solid solution 
phases in an N -component CCA decreases as N increase. This finding 
underscores the inadequacy of relying solely on configurational entro -
py to predict phase stability [102,104] . By establishing quantitative 
screening criteria based on phase diagram feature – such as melting 
temperature, phase composition, and phase fraction at various temper -
atures – they further identified CCAs with high potential for elevated- 
temperature service [104] . However, it is important to note that re -
searchers maintain a necessary degree of skepticism toward CALPHAD 
predictions and often interpret their results conservatively 
[97 – 102,104] . This cautious approach highlights two critical points: 
first, the imperative for developing more reliable and extensively 
assessed thermodynamic databases, and second, the need for critical 
assessment of CALPHAD results, especially when they are used in 
automated high-throughput workflows or integrated with machine 
learning models.
2.2.2. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations of alloy properties
DFT is a first-principles computational method rooted in quantum 
mechanics, which enables efficient computation of electronic structure, 
total energy, and various physico-chemical properties of materials by 
solving the Kohn-Sham equations [105] . These equations transform the 
many-electron problem into one of non-interacting electrons moving 
within an effective potential. The foundation of DFT rests on the 
Hohenberg-Kohn theorems [105] , which establish a one-to-one corre -
spondence between the ground-state electron density and the total en -
ergy of the system, as well as the approximation of intricate many-body 
interactions through the exchange – correlation functional. For complex 
systems exhibiting significant chemical disorder, such as CCAs, the 
adoption of Special Quasirandom Structures (SQS) to construct repre -
sentative supercell models has become a critical prerequisite for accu -
rately simulating the characteristics of their random solid solutions. 
Coupled with SQS, DFT serves as a powerful tool for investigating 
electronic density of states, ground-state energies, lattice distortion and 
mechanical properties [106 – 113] . Lattice distortion can be quantita -
tively analyzed by comparing the distorted and ideal configurations 
from DFT calculations [107,108] . The distorted configuration is the fully 
relaxed SQS supercell that allows atoms to change positions and undergo 
volume alterations, whereas the ideal configuration refers to the

2.2.3. Machine learning assisted alloy design
Although conventional empirical rule-based design strategies pro -
vide some guidelines, their strong dependence on trial-and-error ex -
periments leads to a slow and costly exploration while facing the vast 
compositional space. Building on the extensive data given by vast 
experimental and calculation results, the integration of machine 
learning (ML) into the design pipeline of RCCAs has emerged as a 
transformative approach to overcome the challenges. Compared to 
computationally intensive first-principles calculations, ML models can 
efficiently learn the underlying relationships between alloys ’ chemical 
compositions, microstructures, properties and make accurate pre -
dictions on desired characteristics. This data-driven paradigm enables 
the rapid screening of thousands of potential compositions, identifying 
the most promising candidates for targeted applications, thereby 
significantly accelerating the materials discovery cycle.
Since there is a direct dependence of alloy properties on the phase 
structure, a primary application of ML in RHEA design is the phase 
prediction. ML has been reported to be successfully applied in guiding 
the design of single-phase BCC [115,116] and biphasic RCCAs [117,118] . 
These ML models mostly used parameters derived from empirical rules 
as data descriptors and various supervised classification algorithms, 
including Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting 
(GB), Artificial Neural Network (ANN) and Support Vector Machine 
(SVM), were selected as candidates for training [119] . For example, Yan 
et al. [116] successfully applied ML to accelerate design of single-phase 
RCCAs. Leveraging a database of 1,807 data points and eight empirical 
parameters as data descriptors, multiple machine learning models were 
trained using different algorithms. And the GB model, exhibiting the 
highest prediction accuracy, successfully guided the discovery of ten 
single-phase RCCAs ( Fig. 5 (a-b)).
Beyond phase stability, ML plays an equally critical role in the pre -
diction of key mechanical and physical properties, such as yield strength 
( Fig. 5 (c, d)), hardness, and high-temperature oxidation resistance 
[ 121 – 124 ]. The strong dependence of alloy properties on processing 
history mandates additional data collection constraints to ensure 
consistent processing and testing protocols, going beyond what is 
needed for phase prediction. A prime example is constraining data to 
specimens fabricated by arc melting and characterized in the as-cast 
condition for strength prediction[ 120 ]. Furthermore, additional 
property-related descriptors are essential to preserve the fidelity of the 
input information, such as bulk modulus and shear modulus for pre -
diction of mechanical properties[ 120 ] and electrode potential for pre -
diction of corrosion properties [125] .
While these supervised ML models can predict properties for a given 
composition, they still follow conventional “ forward ” paradigm and 
cannot directly identify the optimal composition needed to achieve a 
specific set of target properties, making the search process slow and 
computationally expensive [126] . In such a case, generative ML models, 
such as Variational Autoencoder (VAE) and Generative Adversarial 
Networks (GAN), are developed to achieve inverse design by directly 
generating alloys with desired properties. Raman et al. [127] employed 
the GAN model to achieve direct generation of alloys with targeted low 
cost and high hardness in the Mo-Nb-Ti-V-W-Zr system. Two alloys are 
successfully determined and their experimental validation results 
exhibited good consistence with desired values. Additionally, Debnath 
et al. [128] made a direct comparison between forward and inverse 
design modeling paradigm while focusing on studies on RCCAs. They 
found that inverse design methods have significant advantages in gen -
eration speed and multi-objective design flexibility, and are particularly 
suitable for scenarios that require rapid exploration of a large number of 
candidate materials or handling complex multi-constraint conditions.

3.1. Solid solution strengthening
Solid solution strengthening is frequently cited in the literature as a 
key strengthening mechanism in metallic alloys, arising from elastic, 
electronic, and chemical interactions between gliding dislocations and 
solute atoms; among these, elastic interactions are generally considered 
dominant [129] . In conventional solid solutions, strengthening is 
modeled using frameworks such as the Fleischer model [130,131] , 
which describes the strong pinning effect of isolated solute atoms in 
dilute solutions, and the more general Labusch model [132 – 134] , which 
accounts for the collective weak pinning effect of randomly distributed 
solutes at moderate concentrations ( Fig. 6 ). However, these classical 
models cannot be directly applied to CCAs. The fundamental distinction 
between solute and solvent atoms is blurred in these multi-principal 
element systems. Moreover, the incorporation of elements with signifi -
cant atomic size and property misfit produces severe lattice distortion, 
leading to a complex, heterogenous strain field that lies beyond the as -
sumptions of classical mean field theories [135] . Therefore, for CCAs, 
the strengthening mechanism is better conceptualized not as discrete 
dislocation-solute interactions, but as the motion of dislocations through 
a rugged energy landscape created by the intrinsic chemical and strain 
disorder of the alloy.

accurately predicting the yield behavior of BCC RCCAs. Rao et al. [148]

required to drag stable vacancy/interstitial dipoles that form on the 
screw line due to kink collisions. As temperature increases, enhanced 
vacancy diffusion causes these dipoles to collapse, and the mechanism 
transitions to the dragging of atomic jogs, which must overcome a 
chemical force arising from local vacancy concentration gradients. The 
model successfully captures the temperature- and strain-rate-dependent 
yield strength of RCCAs like NbTiZr and HfNbTaTiZr by incorporating 
the net solute-screw dislocation interaction energy, treated either 
through a direct variance summation or a phenomenological mixing rule 
[148] . In contrast, Maresca and Curtin [149] proposed a fundamentally 
different theory for random BCC alloys, from dilute to high-entropy 
compositions. Their model departs from the assumption of an initially 
straight dislocation. Instead, it recognizes that in a random solute field, a 
screw dislocation spontaneously adopts a kinked structure to minimize 
its energy, with a characteristic segment length scale ξ
c 
determined by 
the interplay between kink formation energy and a collective solute- 
dislocation interaction parameter as shown in Fig. 8 [149] . Plastic

intrinsic kinks, and the unpinning of cross-kinks that form when kinks on

unique glide mechanisms of screw dislocations in a chemically disor -

rational design of RCCAs with targeted mechanical performance.

3.2. Effect of Short-Range order on mechanical properties
Beyond the random solute distribution assumed in classical solid 
solution and screw dislocation models, short-range order is increasingly 
recognized as a crucial microstructural feature that profoundly in -
fluences the mechanical properties of RCCAs [150,151] . In BCC RCCAs 
such as NbTaTiVZr, direct atomic-resolution STEM imaging combined 
with Monte Carlo simulations demonstrates that SRO significantly re -
duces lattice distortion compared to random solid-solution predictions 
through preferential bonding pairs like Ta – V and Ti – Zr, while avoiding 
unfavorable combinations like Nb – Zr as shown in Fig. 9 [151] . This 
selective atomic arrangement not only lowers the overall elastic strain 
energy but also creates localized variations in bonding environments 
that alter dislocation energetics [151] .
The evaluation of SRO exerts a profound and multifaceted influence 
on the mechanical properties of RCCAs, primarily by modulating 
atomic-scale interactions, local strain fields, and electronic structures. In 
NbTaTiVZr RHEA, the development of SRO leads to a significant 
reduction in measured lattice distortion compared to the random solid- 
solution prediction [151] . This deviation indicates that SRO mitigates 
atomic-size mismatch through selective atomic pairing, thereby 
lowering the overall elastic strain energy and enhancing structural sta -
bility. The stabilization effect is further explained by electronic structure 
analysis in TiZrHfNb RCCA, where the formation of (Ti,Zr)-rich SRO 
reduces the d-electron density at the Fermi level, decreasing system 
energy and increasing phase stability [150] . Mechanically, this stabili -
zation translates into enhanced hardness, elastic modulus, and ideal 
strength. For instance, in TiZrHfNb, annealing-induced SRO increased 
the Vickers hardness by 25% [150] , while in NbTaTiVZr, SRO correlates 
with higher yield strength compared to the less-ordered NbTaTiV [151] . 
The strengthening mechanism is attributed to SRO-induced local lattice 
distortion and heterogeneous charge transfer, which create fluctuating 
stress fields that impede dislocation motion [150] . However, the effect 
of SRO on strength is not monolithic; atomistic simulations show that 
specific SRO configurations can either increase or decrease the energy 
barrier for dislocation glide depending on the Warren-Cowley parameter 
sign and chemical nature of preferred bonds [152,153] . Furthermore, 
SRO can influence deformation mechanisms; in CoCrNi medium-entropy 
alloys, it has been shown that a higher degree of SRO would result in an 
increase of stacking fault energies, promoting transitions from nano- 
twinning to planar slip and thereby improving hardness [154] . Thus, 
SRO serves as a critical microstructural parameter that can be tailored 
through thermomechanical processing to achieve desired combinations 
of strength, ductility, and stability in advanced high-entropy alloys.
Recent studies have highlighted that the contribution of LCO or SRO 
to the mechanical properties of RCCAs is strongly temperature depen -
dent and closely linked to its thermodynamic and kinetic stability. In 
particular, Yin et al. systematically investigated the role of SRO in multi- 
principal-element alloys and demonstrated that while SRO can signifi -
cantly modify dislocation behavior and strengthen the alloy at low 
temperatures, its influence progressively diminishes with increasing 
temperature [68] . Their combined experimental and atomistic simula -
tion results showed that SRO enhances local energy barriers for dislo -
cation motion by introducing spatial fluctuations in bonding and lattice 
resistance; however, these effects are increasingly overwhelmed by 
thermal activation and atomic diffusion at elevated temperatures [68] . 
As temperature increases, the configurational entropy and atomic 
mobility promote disordering, leading to a gradual reduction of SRO 
intensity and, consequently, a reduced contribution to flow stress. 
Importantly, Yin et al. emphasized that the impact of SRO on strength is 
most pronounced in the temperature regime where plastic deformation 
is controlled by dislocation glide rather than diffusion-assisted mecha -
nisms [68] . This finding is particularly relevant for BCC RCCAs, in which 
screw dislocation motion dominates at low and intermediate tempera -
tures and is highly sensitive to local variations in chemical environment. 
In this regime, SRO can effectively raise the local Peierls barrier and 
promote more homogeneous slip, thereby enhancing strength and, in 
some cases, improving ductility. However, as the temperature ap -
proaches a significant fraction of the melting temperature, the me -
chanical response transitions toward regimes governed by edge 
dislocation glide, climb, and creep, where the strengthening contribu -
tion from SRO becomes secondary compared with solid solution

4.2. Fracture and ductility of RCCAs
Although RCCAs display exceptional strength and thermal stability, 
their application has been constrained by a critical drawback — intrinsic 
brittleness at room temperature [36,37,42,235,236] . Many early RCCAs, 
particularly those based on the Nb-Mo-Ta-W system, exhibit negligible 
tensile ductility and fail catastrophically under compression or tension, 
primarily due to their BCC crystal structure and limited slip systems 
[36,42] . This brittleness complicates fabrication, forming, and joining 
processes, and poses a major barrier to their applications into structural 
components. Understanding and mitigating room-temperature brittle -
ness, therefore, remain central challenges in RCCA development. The 
brittle nature of most RCCAs arises from several intrinsic and extrinsic 
factors. On the intrinsic side, their BCC lattice exhibits high Peierls 
stress, making dislocation motion difficult at low temperatures

compositional tuning and microstructural engineering [44,245,246] . 
One of the most direct strategies is to tailor alloy chemistry by simul -
taneously reducing or eliminating intrinsically brittle elements such as 
Mo and W and introducing ductility-enhancing elements such as Ti, Zr, 
or Hf. This compositional adjustment lowers the valence electron con -

directional character, thereby facilitating dislocation mobility and 
improving ductility [235,247] . Alloys in the TiZrHfNb system and

strains exceeding 20% at room temperature while maintaining sub -
stantial strength [44,74] . It is important to note that the improved 
ductility of these alloys arises not only from the presence of Ti, Zr, and 
Hf, but also from the absence of intrinsically brittle refractory elements 
such as Mo and W. While Nb and Ta are themselves highly ductile and 
can exhibit extensive tensile plasticity at room temperature, high con -
centrations of Mo and W are well known to cause embrittlement in Nb- 
and Ta-based alloys by increasing lattice resistance and promoting 
cleavage-dominated fracture. In this context, the superior ductility of 
TiZrHfNb-based RCCAs reflects a combined effect of introducing ductile 
elements and avoiding strong embrittling species. From a mechanistic 
perspective, the enhanced ductility in TiZrHfNb-based alloys has been 
attributed to the activation of both edge and screw dislocations, which 
promotes cross-slip, more homogeneous plastic flow, and delayed crack 
initiation [44] . Another effective pathway involves dual-phase or 
multiphase toughening. The addition of specific elements can effectively 
promote spinodal decomposition. As demonstrated by Chen et al. [248] ,

Beyond alloy chemistry and microstructure, fracture behavior in 
RCCAs is strongly influenced by the intrinsic ductility of constituent 
elements and their collective bonding characteristics, as exemplified by 
the contrast between ductile TiZrHfNbTa and brittle VNbMoTaW [43] . 
In TiZrHfNbTa, the presence of HCP elements combined with the 
absence of Mo and W leads to enhanced lattice distortion, reduced 
dislocation core energy, and a lower shear modulus, all of which facil -
itate dislocation nucleation and mobility. In contrast, the fully BCC- 
composed VNbMoTaW alloy contains high fractions of Mo and W, 
which are associated with compact dislocation cores, higher core en -
ergies, and stronger solute – dislocation interactions, collectively 
impeding dislocation motion and promoting brittle fracture. 
Additionally, the preference for slip on the {211} plane in TiZrHfNbTa, 
linked to electronic structure modifications induced by HCP elements, 
further enhances its plastic deformability [43] . These observations un -
derscore that improving ductility in RCCAs requires not only the addi -
tion of ductile elements but also the deliberate avoidance of strongly 
embrittling refractory species. [43] .
It is important to emphasize that the success of these strategies often 
depends on achieving a balance between strengthening and plasticity. 
Excessive lattice distortion, while beneficial for solid-solution 
strengthening, can raise the Peierls barrier and lower dislocation 
mobility. Similarly, while laves or silicide phases enhance high- 
temperature strength, they are inherently brittle and can serve as 
crack initiation sites if not finely dispersed. Consequently, the design of 
ductile RCCAs must focus on achieving microstructural optimization, i. 
e., maintaining a continuous BCC matrix for plastic flow, while incor -
porating fine, coherent strengthening phases and controlled interstitial 
populations.

4.4. Fatigue resistance of RCCAs
While RCCAs are considered as promising candidates for replacing 
traditional nickel-based superalloys in high-temperature aerospace ap -
plications – attributed to their exceptional high-temperature strength 
and creep resistance – their engineering implementation is challenged 
by fatigue failure. This failure, induced by cyclic loading and driven by 
crack initiation and propagation, represents a critical barrier. Therefore, 
even with considerable advances in the study of RCCAs ’ static me -
chanical properties, a significant knowledge gap persists regarding a 
systematic understanding of their high-temperature low-cycle fatigue 
(LCF) and high-cycle fatigue (HCF) behaviors in Fig. 17 [257,258] .
To address this research gap, several studies have begun to charac -
terize the fatigue behaviors of RCCAs, particularly the HfNbTaTiZr 
alloy. Initial work by Guennec et al. [259] established a high fatigue 
endurance limit of ~ 430 MPa for this alloy, with a fatigue ratio (e.g., 
endurance limit/yield strength) of 0.43, significantly surpassing most 
conventional structural alloys as show in Fig. 18 . Chen et al. [ 222 ] 
further demonstrated a HCF strength of up to 497 MPa, attributing this 
performance to the dispersed distribution of persistent slip bands (PSBs), 
which effectively delays crack initiation. Subsequent research has clar -
ified the underlying deformation mechanisms. At room temperature, the 
alloy exhibits cyclic softening during LCF [258] . The softening mecha -
nism is strain-amplitude dependent: at lower strain amplitudes (e.g., 
0.8%), crack initiation is preferential at the junctions of PSBs and grain 
boundaries, leading to rapid initial softening. In contrast, at higher 
strain amplitudes ( ≥ 1.1%), the activation of multiple slip systems gen -
erates high-density cross-slip bands (CSBs), which disperse stress con -
centrations, suppress rapid crack initiation, and enhance fatigue 
resistance. This indicates that dislocation slip dominates fatigue damage 
at room temperature.
However, fatigue mechanisms are highly temperature-dependent
[260] . Investigations into the LCF behavior of HfNbTaTiZr from 25 to 
600
◦
C revealed that elevated temperatures introduce significant creep 
damage [261] . At 600
◦
C, creep damage contributed up to 11% of the 
total damage, becoming a dominate factor. Microstructurally, the 
damage mode shifted from transgranular cracking at low temperatures 
to a synergistic interaction of grain-boundary fatigue and creep at high 
temperatures. This was accompanied by a significant increase in 
geometrically necessary dislocations at grain boundaries, promoting 
crack initiation and propagation. Crystal plasticity finite element (CPFE) 
simulations corroborated these findings, showing that creep damage 
exacerbates strain localization by reducing local stiffness and slip 
resistance, leading to cyclic softening and accelerated fatigue failure. 
While current research has provided foundational insights into the fa -
tigue mechanisms of RCCAs, the field is still in its infancy. Significant 
knowledge gaps – especially concerning high-temperature fatigue and 
synergistic damage from environmental coupling – present major ob -
stacles to application. Therefore, a concerted research focus on these 
areas is essential to unlock the full potential of RCCAs for use in extreme 
aerospace conditions. The study of Stinville et al. reveals that the fatigue 
resistance of RCCAs, such as the BCC-structured HfNbTaTiZr alloy, is 
directly correlated with the slip localization amplitude during the first 
loading cycle [262] . Unlike most BCC alloys that exhibit high fatigue 
efficiency, this alloy shows pronounced slip localization, leading to 
relatively lower fatigue strength relative to its yield strength, high -
lighting the critical link between early cyclic slip behavior and fatigue 
performance in refractory complex concentrated alloys [262] .

6. Processing, manufacturing, and industrial scalability
RCCAs contain specific refractory elements (Nb, Mo, Ta, Ti, W, V, Hf, 
Zr, Cr) with significant inter-element melting point differences (some 
exceeding 1000

volatilization, compositional segregation, and microstructural in -

chanical properties highly sensitive to preparation processes. Currently, 
a diversified technological framework has been established for pro -
cessing such alloys, encompassing foundational techniques such as 
vacuum arc melting (VAM), powder metallurgy (PM), and additive 
manufacturing (AM).
VAM has emerged as a predominant technique for fabricating RCCAs 
[36,73,74,287] . In this process, high-purity metal blocks or granules are 
placed in a crucible within a vacuum arc furnace according to a pre -
determined stoichiometric ratio. An electric arc generated between the 
electrode and the charge, or between two electrodes, induces melting of 
the metals at elevated temperatures [37] . VAM offers several advan -
tages, including a short production cycle, low raw material costs, 
operational simplicity, and the capability to achieve extremely high 
melting temperatures ( > 3000
◦
C), sufficient to melt most refractory el -
ements. Moreover, the vacuum environment effectively prevents 
oxidation of high-melting-point elements and volatilization of low- 
melting-point components. However, VAM also exhibits significant 
limitations: the equipment is costly, the batch size is restricted, and the 
required purity of starting materials is exceptionally high (impurity 
content < 0.1%). Additionally, the relatively slow cooling rate during 
solidification often leads to the formation of coarse columnar grains 
[288] . The compositional complexity and substantial differences in 
melting points among constituent elements can result in pronounced 
elemental segregation during rapid solidification as shown in Fig. 19 (a)
[289,290] . Furthermore, the short duration of the liquid phase makes it 
difficult to fully dissolve some refractory elements, potentially leading to 
defects such as incomplete melting, chemical heterogeneity, and inter -
nal shrinkage porosity [36,291 – 293] . For instance, in the CrFe -
MoNbTaZr alloy, Ta and Mo, elements with high melting points, were 
not completely melted and were found either attached to the surface of 
solid solutions of other elements or embedded within the matrix. A 
similar phenomenon was observed in CrFeMoTaTiZr, while un-melted 
Ta and Nb particles were detected in the FeTaNbTiZrMo alloy [36] . 
These incompletely melted particles can induce internal stresses and 
initiate cracks at interfaces. To avoid such un-melted particles, multiple 
remelting cycles are often necessary to ensure compositional homoge -
neity in refractory RCCAs [294,295] . Owing to the presence of shrinkage 
porosity, coarse-grained structures, and chemical heterogeneity, these 
as-cast alloys are generally not suitable for direct application. To achieve 
desirable mechanical properties, further processing such as plastic

7. Summary, Challenges, and future Perspectives
In summary, RCCAs have emerged over the past decade as a trans -
formative class of structural materials capable of surpassing the tem -
perature and performance limits of conventional Ni- and Co-based 
superalloys. Through the integration of empirical design principles, 
thermodynamic modeling, and data-driven optimization, researchers 
have achieved major progress in designing RCCAs with superior high- 
temperature strength, creep resistance, and microstructural stability. 
The development of coherent B2 nanoprecipitates within BCC matrices 
has successfully replicated the γ / γ’ architecture of Ni-based superalloys, 
providing significant precipitation strengthening above 1200
◦
C. In 
parallel, strategies such as interstitial alloying (C, N, O additions), spi -
nodal decomposition, and heterogeneous microstructure engineering 
have markedly improved the balance between strength and ductility. 
These accomplishments collectively represent a paradigm shift toward 
the rational design of multifunctional refractory materials for aerospace, 
nuclear, and hypersonic applications requiring extreme thermal and 
mechanical endurance.
Despite these achievements, several critical challenges continue to 
hinder the large-scale implementation of RCCAs. The most formidable 
issue remains oxidation resistance, which currently represents the major 
barrier to practical use. Many RCCAs fail to form stable, protective oxide 
scales and suffer rapid degradation due to oxygen diffusion and non- 
adherent oxides. Achieving self-healing, continuous oxide layers anal -
ogous to the Al
2
O
3 
and Cr
2
O
3 
based scales in superalloys requires 
innovative alloying and coating strategies. A second major limitation is 
room-temperature ductility and toughness. The predominance of BCC 
crystal structures in refractory systems results in high Peierls stresses, 
limited slip systems, and cleavage-dominated fracture. Although the 
incorporation of ductile elements (Ti, Zr, Hf) and the design of hetero -
geneous microstructures have yielded some improvement, most RCCAs 
still exhibit brittle behavior at ambient conditions. Third, density re -
mains a practical constraint for Ta- and W-rich alloys, which, despite 
their high strength, are too heavy for weight-sensitive aerospace appli -
cations. Cost presents an additional obstacle, as many refractory ele -
ments such as Ta, Nb, and Hf are scarce and expensive, posing challenges 
to large-scale manufacturing and sustainability.
To address these issues, future research directions should emphasize 
the integration of computational materials design, data informatics, and 
experimental validation. First, materials informatics and AI will play a 
central role in accelerating the inverse design of RCCAs. The ML models 
trained on high-throughput CALPHAD and DFT databases can predict 
phase stability, oxidation resistance, and mechanical performance 
across vast compositional spaces. Generative AI approaches, such as 
VAE and GAN, hold the potential to perform multi-objective optimiza -
tion, identifying alloy compositions that balance strength, ductility, and 
cost. Second, the development of RCCA-based composites offers another 
promising direction. Reinforcing refractory matrices with silicides, 
carbides, or ceramic particulates could simultaneously enhance creep 
resistance, oxidation stability, and fracture toughness. Another key 
avenue lies in designing lightweight RCCAs, focusing on Ti-, V-, Zr-, Cr-, 
and Al-containing systems to reduce density without sacrificing thermal 
or mechanical performance. Such “ light ” refractory alloys could play 
critical roles in hypersonic flight, space propulsion, and turbine

In conclusion, the design of high-strength, high ductility, creep-

multi-principal-element alloying in pushing the limits of high-

remains imperative for widespread application. By uniting materials 
informatics, compositionally complex coatings, advanced composites, 
lightweight alloy strategies, the next generation of RCCAs can be pur -
