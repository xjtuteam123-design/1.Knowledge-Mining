ABSTRACT
The NiAl – 43V hypereutectic alloy was prepared by directional solidification at growth rates of ν = 6, 10, 30, 60, 
and 150 μ m/s. The effects of 0.05 wt% Dy microalloying on the phase composition, microstructure morphology 
at the solid-liquid interface, room temperature microhardness, and high-temperature strength of the NiAl – 43V 
hypereutectic alloy were investigated. The results showed that the solidification microstructures of both alloys 
consisted of primary V phase and eutectic (NiAl + V) lamellae. As the growth rate increased, the alloy micro -
structures became refined, and the hardness increased. Although the addition of 0.05 % Dy resulted in a slight 
decrease in hardness, it improved the high-temperature compressive properties. The addition of Dy enhanced the 
high-temperature strength of the alloy through mechanisms such as fine grain strengthening and solid solution

1. Introduction
With the development of science and technology, there is an 
increasing need for high-temperature materials with higher service 
temperature and strength at high operating temperatures [ 1 – 4 ]. Among 
many high-temperature materials, NiAl intermetallic compounds with 
B2 structure have excellent properties as a potential high-temperature 
structural material [ 5 ], which have attracted much attention due to 
their high melting point (1638 
◦
C), low density, excellent corrosion 
resistance, high thermal conductivity, good oxidation resistance at high 
temperatures, and high Young ’ s modulus (240 GPa) [ 6 , 7 ]. They are 
widely used in civil engineering and military industries due to their high 
hardness and chemical resistance [ 8 ], high-temperature structural 
products, friction and corrosion-resistant coating materials, and new 
applications as electron metallization compounds in advanced semi -
conductors and surface catalysts [ 9 ]. The lack of a slip system (slip oc -
curs mainly on < 100 > {011} and < 100 > {010}) [ 5 ] has led to practical 
structural applications of this material being limited by the disadvan -
tages of its low ductility and toughness at room temperature and low 
creep resistance at high-temperature, limiting its wider application.
Recent studies have indicated that the incorporation of new elements 
into NiAl as toughening phases [ 8 , 10 ], alongside the utilization of novel 
preparation techniques such as directional solidification (DS) [ 11 ], can 
augment its mechanical characteristics. Among these alloys, NiAl – V 
eutectic alloys, featuring a lamellar eutectic structure, stand out due to 
their impressive equilibrium of properties at both room and high tem -
peratures [ 12 ]. Despite improving their mechanical properties, their 
high-temperature strength still cannot be competitive. Recent studies 
[ 13 ] confirm that the introduction of rare earth (RE) elements is bene -
ficial to improve the high-temperature performance. Meanwhile, Zhang 
Z et al. [ 14 ] found that adding trace amounts of Dy to NiAl alloys 
resulted in grain refinement. Zhang T et al. [ 15 ] showed that the addi -
tion of Dy favors the resistance of NiAl to cyclic oxidation. The addition 
of Dy to NiAl – Cr(Mo) hypereutectic alloys studied by Wang L [ 16 ] 
prevents the growth of primary dendrite, refines the eutectic lamellae 
spacing, and improves tensile strength. In a previous study by our group 
[ 17 ], NiAl – V alloys prepared by arc melting with the addition of trace 
Dy elements were found to have good fracture toughness and 
high-temperature strength in the arc-melted state of NiAl – V alloys with 
a content of 0.05 wt% Dy. Therefore, in this paper, NiAl – 43V-xDy (x = 0, 
0.05 wt%) hypereutectic alloys were prepared by the DS method, 
focusing on the effect of Dy on the microstructure and mechanical 
properties of NiAl – 43V hypereutectic alloys. The aim is to optimize the 
properties of NiAl – 43V hypereutectic alloys for high-temperature in -
dustrial applications by Dy microalloying.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Solid-liquid interface morphology
The microstructural morphology of the DS NiAl – 43V-xDy (x = 0, 
0.05 wt%) hypereutectic alloy at various growth rates, is depicted in 
Fig. 1 .
The S/L interface microstructural morphology comprises primary V 
dendrites interspersed with a lamellar, regular eutectic microstructure, 
which is consonant with prior descriptions in the literature [ 19 ]. The S/L 
interface morphology and microstructure evolution in directionally so -
lidified NiAl – 43V-xDy (x = 0,0.05 wt%) alloys are significantly influ -
enced by the growth rate. Fig. 1 (a) and (a1) illustrate that, at a growth 
rate of 6 μ m/s, the transverse microstructure in the steady state region 
mainly consisted of primary V phase and NiAl – V eutectic structure, 
suggesting the presence of primary V dendrites in both alloys during low 
solidification rates. Notably, the longitudinal section failed to cut 
through the dendrites, resulting in an observation where the S/L inter -
face displayed cellular growth.
Fig. 1 illustrates that as the growth rate increases from 6 to 150 μ m/s, 
the S/L interface exhibits a coexistence of dendrite-cellular morphol -
ogies, growth of eutectic structure between primary V dendrites, and the 
number of dendrites increases and is refined. Furthermore, the eutectic 
lamellar structure is significantly refined after adding 0.05 % Dy to the 
NiAl – 43V alloy. This is because the addition of trace amounts of Dy 
refines the eutectic structure by reducing the eutectic lamellar spacing, 
redistributing solute atoms, and increasing the solute concentration at 
the dendrite tips. As the supercooling degree along the S/L interface 
increases, the nucleation rate of the primary phase increases, but the 
dendrites can ’ t grow up sufficiently. This phenomenon is consistent with 
the classical solidification principle [ 20 ]. A comparative analysis was 
conducted between a theoretical model and experimental results to 
examine the influence of the Dy element and growth rate on primary 
dendrite spacing.

Meanwhile, the microstructure of the dendrites in the transverse 
direction was observed using a metallurgical microscope (OM), and the 
primary dendrite spacing at different locations was statistically distrib -
uted with the help of Image Pro Plus software. Measurements were taken 
for five fields of view and then the average values were calculated. Based 
on Hunt ’ s model and Kurz ’ s model, the theoretical values of NiAl – 43V 
primary dendrite spacing were calculated and compared with experi -
mental results. The comparative analysis unveils a consistent trend: the 
theoretical values derived from both models are smaller than the 
experimental results. Notably, the values computed using Hunt ’ s model 
exhibit a closer approximation to the experimental results.
The main reason for the significant discrepancy between the theo -
retical and experimental values is that the Hunt and Kurz models, which 
take into account the influence of dendrite tip curvature on the diffusion 
field and the mutual interaction of diffusion fields between dendrite 
columns, involve numerous simplifications of physical characteristics 
and mathematical approximations during their establishment. These 
models neglect interface anisotropy, which is currently recognized as 
having a significant impact on the formation and evolution of solidifi -
cation structures. During the experimental process, when the with -
drawal rate is relatively low, the maximum temperature gradient 
direction (heat flow direction) of the primary dendrites and grain 
boundaries in the solidified structure sometimes do not coincide. The 
temperature field and solute diffusion field around the dendrite tips 
exhibit asymmetric distributions, failing to meet the local equilibrium 
condition at the interface, which exacerbates the coarsening of the 
structure. Additionally, during the process of molten metal entering the 
mold, a chill layer forms due to the quenching effect of the cooling 
water. At this point, because the release of latent heat of crystallization 
is relatively concentrated, the temperature gradient at the front of the S/ 
L interface tends to decrease significantly in a short period of time, or 
even form a negative temperature gradient, leading to instability at the 
S/L interface. This instability is equivalent to adding a disturbance, 
resulting in the formation of new grain crystals. After the system stabi -
lizes for a period of time, the downward pulling process begins to ensure 
that the grain crystals have sufficient time to grow, thereby obtaining a 
more ideal microstructure. These factors also promote the mutual 
merging and growth of cellular dendrites, causing the measured value of 
dendrite spacing to be significantly greater than the theoretical value.

3.2. Microhardness
Microhardness tests were conducted on DS NiAl – 43V-xDy (x = 0, 
0.05 wt%) alloys. The hardness values are the average of 10 measure -
ments. Table 3 lists the microhardness of the two hypereutectic alloys 
with growth rates ranging from 6 to 150 μ m/s. It can be seen from the 
table that the hardness values of the alloys increase with increasing 
growth rate. Combined with Fig. 1 , it can be observed that as the growth 
rate increases, the solidification microstructure of the alloy becomes 
finer, and the hardness value of the alloy also increases accordingly. At a 
growth rate of 6 μ m/s, the hardness value is the lowest; at 150 μ m/s, the 
hardness value reaches its maximum.
The average hardness values of the eutectic microstructure and the 
primary phase were determined, and the resultant data are depicted in 
Fig. 3 . It was observed that the microhardness of the alloy decreased 
after the addition of 0.05 % Dy. As per the literature [ 25 – 27 ], the 
hardness of the alloy is intricately linked to the primary dendrite 
spacing, with a tendency for the alloy hardness to diminish as the pri -
mary dendrite spacing ( λ ) increases. Notably, despite the minimal 
impact on the composition of the alloy ’ s matrix elements due to the 
small addition of Dy, the introduction of Dy still results in an augmen -
tation of the primary dendrite spacing. This is consistent with the 
significantly lower microhardness of alloy compositions with added Dy 
elements as reflected in Fig. 3 .
During the growth of hypereutectic alloys, secondary dendrites grow 
laterally perpendicular to the primary dendrites, hindering the longi -
tudinal diffusion of Dy atoms during the solidification process. Due to 
the larger atomic radius of Dy (0.249 nm) compared to those of Ni and Al 
(0.124 nm and 0.143 nm, respectively), the distortion energy caused by 
the solid solution of Dy is greater than that caused by segregation at the 
phase boundaries [ 28 ]. To reduce the distortion energy, Dy diffuses and 
segregates towards the phase and grain boundaries. This distribution 
weakens the internal bonding strength of the alloy, thereby reducing its 
hardness.
3.3. High-temperature properties
The true stress-strain curve is shown in Fig. 4 . As the growth rate 
increases, the true stress maximum decreases. This indicates that the 
stress peak of high-temperature compression gradually decreases. Under 
continuous deformation, the softening of dynamic recrystallization leads 
to a gradual decrease in the true stress-strain curve of the alloys, and the 
addition of the Dy element to the hypereutectic alloy reduces the dy -
namic recrystallization and causes the curve to change more gradually. 
From Table 4 , it is observed that the maximum peak stresses of 
NiAl – 43V-0.05Dy and NiAl – 43V are 370.91 MPa and 364.71 MPa at a 
growth rate of 6 μ m/s, respectively, and the minimum peak stresses are 
300.21 MPa and 272.84 MPa at a growth rate of 150 μ m/s, respectively.

From the comparison of Fig. 4 (a) and (b), it can be seen that the peak 
stress point of the alloy is increased after Dy alloying at the same growth 
rate. This is because the addition of element Dy reduces the segregation 
of O, S, and C impurities at the interfaces, thus increasing the cohesive 
strength of the phase or cell interface. In addition, since the radius of Dy 
atoms is larger than that of Ni and Al atoms when Dy atoms segregate at 
the phase or cell interface, electron transfer may occur, and such an 
increase in electron density will enhance the interface strength. There -
fore, the addition of the rare earth element Dy may also increase the 
high-temperature strength of the alloy by strengthening the interfaces 
[ 31 ]. A large number of Dy atoms will be distributed in the alloy matrix, 
while also segregating to the cell boundaries or phase interfaces, filling 
interface vacancies, and reducing the interface sliding rates and diffu -
sion rates. The addition of Dy promotes diffusion behavior at the grain 
boundaries, reducing the grain size and resulting in a more uniform 
distribution. This refining effect is mainly manifested during grain 
boundary diffusion, where the Dy element segregates to the grain 
boundaries, thereby inhibiting grain growth and enhancing the 
high-temperature strength of the alloy.
Compared to the microstructure of a fully eutectic alloy, the presence 
of primary V phase precipitated at the boundaries of eutectic cells leads 
to a decrease in performance. Furthermore, while the incorporation of 
Dy leads to a modest enhancement in the high-temperature compression 
properties, this improvement is relatively marginal. Through micro -
structural analysis, it was found that the eutectic structure between and 
around the primary V dendrites was coarse and irregular, severely 
affecting the thermal stability of the alloy. Therefore, the introduction of 
Dy is primarily aimed at inhibiting the growth of primary V dendrites 
and enhancing the high-temperature strength.
As the growth rate increases, Dy plays a crucial role in fine grain 
strengthening, leading to an improvement in high-temperature strength 
that correlates positively with the growth rate, ranging approximately 
from 6 to 28 MPa.
In summary, the experimental results indicate that as the growth rate 
increases, the primary dendrite spacing of the alloy decreases, while the 
microhardness shows an upward trend. However, the addition of 0.05 % 
Dy element, although contributing to the refinement of the eutectic 
lamellar structure, enlarges the primary dendrite spacing, subsequently 
leading to a reduction in the alloy ’ s microhardness. This phenomenon is 
attributed to the relatively large atomic radius of Dy atoms and their 
distribution characteristics in the alloy, which weaken the internal 
cohesive strength of the alloy. In terms of high-temperature compressive 
performance, the peak stress-strain values of both alloys decrease with 
increasing growth rates. Notably, at the same growth rate, the alloy with 
added Dy exhibits higher peak stress, benefiting from the enhancing 
effect of Dy on interfacial strength. Dy atoms, distributed at phase 
boundaries, grain boundaries, and interfaces between the primary phase 
and eutectic microstructure, effectively improve the cohesive force of 
the alloy, thereby enhancing its high-temperature strength to some 
extent. Concurrently, mechanisms such as fine grain strengthening and 
solid solution strengthening jointly enhance the high-temperature 
strength of the alloy.
