ABSTRACT 
Herein, an alloy Cu – 1.16Ni – 0.36Cr with outstanding high-temperature mechanical properties was obtained, and 
the underlying mechanism was discussed. The properties were compared with those of commercial Cu – Fe – P 
alloys. The alloy exhibited a high-temperature softening temperature of 674.7 
◦
C. Tensile tests conducted at high 
temperatures revealed that at 700 
◦
C, the alloy maintained a tensile strength of 131.3 MPa, whereas Cu – Fe – P 
alloys only exhibited a tensile strength of 58.7 MPa. Microstructural analysis revealed the formation of nanoscale 
Ni
3
Cr
7 
precipitates near fractures at high temperatures, with the average size being approximately 10 nm. These 
nanoscale precipitates hindered the coarsening of Cr particles and dislocation slips, forming dislocation rings and 
substantially enhancing the high-temperature mechanical properties of the alloy. Furthermore, the nanoscale 
precipitates considerably impeded the growth and expansion of recrystallized grains. After a high-temperature 
treatment at 700 
◦
C, a portion of the microstructure in the alloy remained stretched, with the small-angle 
grain boundaries being high (68.2%) and the recrystallization degree being only 50.4%. 
1. Introduction 
In the era of electronic information, integrated circuits are continu -
ously evolving toward higher integration, finer dimensions, and mini -
aturization [ 1 – 3 ]. The new generation of integrated circuits is 
characterized by an increased number of lead pins and thinner lead 
frames. Consequently, the density of current passing through these lead 
frames increases, resulting in higher heat generation per unit volume. 
Therefore, the lead frames must withstand higher temperatures within a 
short period. The operating temperatures of standard chips can reach 
150 
◦
C and temporarily reach 300 
◦
C, whereas the new generation of 
chips can withstand service temperatures of 500 
◦
C or even higher [ 4 , 5 ]. 
Given these challenging service conditions, lead frame materials must 
exhibit excellent high-temperature mechanical properties. 
Cu – Fe – P alloys are widely employed as traditional lead frame 
materials, accounting for approximately 60% of the total such materials. 
Addition of trace amounts of P leads to deoxidation, improving the 
overall alloy properties and promoting precipitation by producing Fe
2
P 
or Fe
3
P [ 6 ]. However, the high content of Fe in Cu – Fe – P alloys makes 
them susceptible to Cu dissolution at high temperatures. Furthermore, 
combination of large precipitate sizes and dispersion in the C19400 alloy 
contributes to their inadequate performance at high temperatures [ 7 – 9 ]. 
Li et al. [ 10 ] reported that the C19400 alloy exhibits annealing softening 
when annealed above 250 
◦
C. With the advancement in integrated cir -
cuits, the service temperature of lead frames is increasing; traditional 
Cu – Fe – P alloys cannot meet the demands of extreme service conditions. 
Consequently, development of new high-temperature-resistant copper 
alloys is crucial to fulfill the performance requirements for lead frame 
materials. Improving high-temperature service performance has become 
an important direction for the development of lead frame materials.

Cu – Ni – Cr alloys are high-strength, high-conductivity copper alloys 
that are currently under development. After aging, a precipitate of Cr 
forms within the alloys, substantially enhancing their mechanical 
properties. The alloys also undergo amplitude modulation decomposi -
tion, making them suitable for application in marine condensers, pipe -
line valves, and other systems [ 11 – 13 ]. The addition of alloying 
elements to form metastable phases is commonly employed for 
strengthening Cu – Cr alloys [ 14 – 16 ]. Alloying elements hinder the 
coarsening of the Cr elemental phase by limiting the growth of Cr pre -
cipitates, forming intermediate compounds, and creating metastable 
phases. This considerably improves the high-temperature mechanical 
properties of the alloys [ 17 , 18 ]. Literature indicates metastable phases 
precipitate in Cu – Ni – Cr alloys, making them a promising choice for 
high-temperature applications. A Ni – Cr binary alloy can form a 
PT2Mo-type ordered NiCr phase by controlling the Ni/Cr ratio within 
the range of 1.5 – 3.5. In this structure, Cr occupies the corners and 
centers of the cell, while Ni occupies other parts, resulting in an ordered 
arrangement that enhances the alloy orderliness [ 19 – 21 ]. The genera -
tion of metastable phases in Ni – Cr alloys at high temperatures sub -
stantially improves the alloy thermal stability [ 22 ]. 
Herein, a low-solute Cu – Ni – Cr alloy was prepared by incorporating a 
Ni – Cr intermediate alloy with a Ni/Cr ratio of 3. The high-temperature 
softening and tensile properties of the Cu – Ni – Cr alloy was investigated 
and compared with those of the traditional lead frame materials, 
Cu – Fe – P alloy. The microstructural changes of the alloy at high tem -
peratures were analyzed, and the high-temperature mechanical prop -
erties of the Cu – Ni – Cr alloy were discussed. 
2. Experimental 
2.1. Materials and precipitates 
Herein, the materials were prepared using a medium-frequency in -
duction furnace and atmospheric melting. In the smelting process, high- 
purity cathode copper (99.99 wt%) and a Ni/Cr alloy were used as raw 
materials. Pure copper was melted until it reached a liquid state, and the 
Ni/Cr alloy was then added. To minimize oxidation, charcoal was used 
to cover the metal liquid and high-purity graphite rods were used for 
stirring. The resulting metal solution was cast into a graphite crucible to 
obtain cylindrical ingots. The pouring temperature was controlled be -

◦

alloying elements was designed to be 1.5%. The obtained alloy was 
determined to be Cu – 1.16Ni – 0.36Cr through elemental analysis. The 
alloy ingot was then homogenized at 950

4.1. Microstructure analysis of the aged alloy 
Fig. 7 presents the EBSD results of the Cu – 1.16Ni – 0.36Cr alloy in the 
aged state. Fig. 7 (a) shows the inverse pole figure (IPF) of the aged alloy. 
The results indicate that the average grain size of the aged alloy is 3.31 
μ m. Notably, considerable grain orientation remained in the alloy even 
after aging, and several small-angle grain boundaries were observed 
within the deformed grains of larger sizes. The majority of grain 
boundary angles in the alloy were concentrated in the range of small- 
angle grain boundaries, with small-angle grain boundaries accounting 
for 83.1% of the total grain-boundary proportion in the alloy. Fig. 7 (c) 
illustrates the degree of recrystallization of the alloy grains. Following 
aging, subcrystalline structures were observed within the deformed 
grains, evenly distributed throughout the alloy in a granular structure. 
This indicates that the deformed grains in the alloy initiate recovery 
after aging, with some recrystallized grains present. At this stage, the 
degree of recrystallization in the alloy was measured to be 7.5%. 
Fig. 8 presents the TEM results of the aged Cu – 1.16Ni – 0.36Cr alloy. 
After the deformation and heat treatment of the Cu – 1.16Ni – 0.36Cr 
alloy, dispersed nanoscale precipitates are present within the alloy 
matrix ( Fig. 8 (a and b)). The promotion of precipitated phase precipi -
tation is associated with the deformation energy induced by cold 
deformation. Cold deformation generates lattice defects and increases 
the energy within the system, creating favorable conditions for the 
nucleation and growth of precipitates [ 28 ]. Analyses using 
high-resolution electron microscopy and fast Fourier transform (FFT) 
reveal that these nanoscale precipitates are Cr precipitates with a 
body-centered cubic (BCC) structure. Furthermore, these precipitates 
exhibit a semicoherent relation with the alloy matrix. 
4.2. Microstructure analysis of the Cu – 1.16Ni – 0.36Cr alloy after the 
high-temperature tensile test 
Fig. 9 presents the EBSD results near the fracture of the 
Cu – 1.16Ni – 0.36Cr alloy after high-temperature stretching at 700 
◦

The IPF of the alloy is shown in Fig. 9 (a). The results indicate that after 
high-temperature treatment, a considerable number of small equiaxed 
crystals with uniform grain orientation appear within the alloy. This 
suggests that the preferential orientation effect caused by cold-rolling 
deformation in the alloy is weakened. The IPF results reveal that the 
alloy undergoes partial recrystallization at high temperatures, with only 
weak grain coarsening. This enables the alloy to maintain good me -
chanical properties even at high temperatures. The average grain size of 
the alloy is measured to be 5.70 μ m. Surface scanning using 
SEM – energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) revealed Cr-rich pre -
cipitates dispersed within the alloy matrix. To further analyze the 
structure of the precipitate and its role in the alloy, TEM tests are 
required. 
Fig. 10 shows the grain-boundary and recrystallization distribution 
images near the fracture of the Cu – 1.16Ni – 0.36Cr alloy after high- 
temperature stretching at 700 
◦
C. In addition, statistical analysis was 
performed on the grain boundary and recrystallization fraction. The 
results reveal that recrystallization occurs in the region of equiaxed 
grains. The orientation angles of the recrystallized grains show that 
these grains are predominantly characterized by large-angle boundaries, 
whereas the deformed grains are primarily characterized by small-angle 
boundaries. Simultaneously, the grain boundary distribution exhibits a 
wide range, with a substantial concentration in the small-angle range, 
accounting for 68.2% of the total number of grain boundaries. The 
recrystallization statistics indicate that the degree of recrystallization in 
the grains reaches 50.4%. In this temperature range, common copper 
alloys undergo approximately complete recrystallization [29] ; however, 
the degree of recrystallization in Cu – Ni – Cr alloys is limited. This phe -
nomenon helps explain why the Cu – Ni – Cr alloy maintains good me -
chanical properties at this temperature. This indicates that some 
recrystallization occurs in the deformed crystals of the alloy at high 
temperatures. At this stage, the alloy exhibits small recrystallized grains 
alongside elongated deformed grains. The alloy is capable of maintain -
ing its deformed structure at this temperature, resulting in stable 
high-temperature performance.
