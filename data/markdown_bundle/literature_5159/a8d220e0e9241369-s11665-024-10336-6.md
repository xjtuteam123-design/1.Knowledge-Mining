ORIGINAL RESEARCH ARTICLE
Analysis of Room-Temperature/High-temperature
High-Cycle Fatigue Performance of Laser Additive
Manufactured Ti65 Titanium Alloy
Jun Yin, Pengfei Li , Xiaodan Li, Feng Guan, Jiacheng Wu, Sailan Wang, and Guang Yang
Submitted: 26 February 2024 / Revised: 25 July 2024 / Accepted: 15 August 2024 / Published online: 28 October 2024
In this study, the microstructure, high-cycle fatigue performance, and the inﬂuence of stress concentration
on the fatigue behavior of Ti65 titanium alloy were investigated under different laser powers using IPG
ﬁber laser additive manufacturing. Fatigue life tests were conducted at both room temperature and high
temperature (650 /C176C), comparing stress concentration coefﬁcients of 1 and 3. The results indicate that
different laser powers lead to signiﬁcant differences in the microstructure of additive manufacturing
samples across various directions. At room temperature, the fatigue limit of the high laser power specimens
increased by 13.9%, and at high temperature, it increased by 8.78%. However, with a stress concentration
coefﬁcient of 3, the high laser power specimens exhibited slightly lower high-cycle fatigue performance
compared to the low laser power specimens.
Keywords additive manufacturing, high-cycle fatigue,
high-temperature performance, stress concentration,
titanium alloy
1. Introduction
Directed energy deposition (DED) laser additive manufac-
turing, through layer-by-layer material deposition, addresses
challenges in creating complex structures and customized
designs that are difﬁcult to achieve with traditional methods.
This technology has garnered widespread interest in the
engineering ﬁeld due to its high ﬂexibility and efﬁciency,
emerging as a key driving force for material design and
manufacturing innovation (Ref 1-3) Ti65 titanium alloy, a novel
metallic alloy, exhibits superior mechanical properties and
corrosion resistance, making it highly sought after in aerospace,
biomedical, and other high-end engineering applications. The
alloy contains appropriate amounts of niobium and aluminum,
imparting excellent strength and high-temperature resistance to
Ti65 titanium alloy, rendering it exemplary for performance in
high-temperature working environments (650 /C176C). However,
metal alloys may encounter limitations such as fatigue cracks
and deformation induced by cyclic loading in high-cycle
fatigue environments. This is particularly true for new aero-
space materials like Ti65, where a comprehensive understand-
ing of high-cycle fatigue performance at both room and
elevated temperatures remains relatively limited.
High-cycle fatigue performance is crucial for material
reliability and service life (Ref 4, 5). The stress concentration
factor signiﬁcantly affects crack initiation and propagation in
titanium alloys under high-cycle fatigue conditions (Ref 6).
Early studies, such as those by Golub et al. (Ref 7) and Lanning
et al. (Ref 8), focused on the effects of stress concentration and
notch size on fatigue strength, respectively. Sadananda et al.
(Ref 9) highlighted the correlation between endurance limits
and crack propagation thresholds. Further research by Strz-
elecki et al. (Ref 10) and Ritz et al. (Ref 11) examined the
impact of stress concentration on fatigue life and crack
initiation at various temperatures and stress factors. Li et al.
(Ref 12) and Meneghetti et al. (Ref 13) provided insights into
the role of nano-crystalline regions and notch characteristics in
fatigue behavior. Strzelecki et al. (Ref 10) demonstrated that
increasing the shape factor reduced the scatter of fatigue test
results, while Ritz et al. (Ref 14) showed that high stress
concentration suppressed damage at internal defects, with
cracks typically initiating at the notch root. In summary, while
signiﬁcant research has focused on the room temperature
fatigue performance of titanium alloys, understanding high-
cycle fatigue at both room and high temperatures is essential for
a comprehensive evaluation. Future studies should address this
gap, providing a more holistic view of fatigue behavior under
varying thermal conditions.

research has been conducted on the fatigue performance of

factors. It was found that fatigue crack initiation positions
almost always coincided with defects formed during the 3D
printing process (Ref 24). Understanding the interplay between
microstructural features, manufacturing processes, and defect
characteristics is crucial for optimizing the fatigue performance
of titanium alloys produced through additive manufacturing.
Typically, studies on the fatigue performance of titanium
alloys focus on smooth samples, attempting to reveal the
impact patterns of macro and micro defects on fatigue
performance. However, actual aviation component structures
are complex and varied, with fatigue cracks often initiating at
geometric size abrupt changes, such as keyways and corners,
due to the stress concentration and notch effects (Ref 25).
Therefore, for practical applications of 3D-printed titanium
alloy components, focusing solely on the impact of macro and
micro defects on fatigue performance of smooth specimens is
insufﬁcient. Further exploration of notch fatigue performance is
essential, holding signiﬁcant implications for the safe and
reliable design of 3D-printed titanium alloy components. This
paper focuses on the recently developed Ti65 titanium alloy,
which, due to its elevated service temperature set at 650 /C176C
compared to conventional titanium alloys, explores fatigue life
under room temperature and 650 /C176C conditions. The inﬂuence
of different laser powers on fatigue performance and analyzes
the impact of various notch stress concentration factors are
investigated. Through in-depth research on Ti65 titanium alloy,
the paper reveals the advantages of laser additive manufactur-
ing technology in enhancing alloy performance and achieving
customized designs.
2. Experimental Procedures and Methods
2.1 Sample Preparation
In the experimental process, an IPG ﬁber laser with a
continuous wave output power of 6000 W, operating at a
wavelength of 1064 nm and a circular spot with a 5 mm
diameter, was employed. Ti65 powder, ranging from 53 to
100 lm, underwent preheating at 110 /C176C for 4 hours to ensure
optimal ﬂuidity. Argon served as the shielding gas, maintaining
oxygen content below 100 ppm to prevent Ti65 oxidation
during Laser Direct Metal Deposition (LDM). The study
compared the microstructure and fatigue performance of
specimens fabricated at high (5000 W) and low (2000 W)
laser powers, each with speciﬁc parameters including scanning
speed (600 mm/min), powder feed rate (15 g/min), spot
diameter (5 mm), single layer height (0.7 mm), and overlap-
ping rate (50%). The additive manufacturing samples are
primarily inspected visually. If signiﬁcant printing defects are
detected, they are removed by cutting, and then subsequent
additive manufacturing is performed.
2.2 Experimental Setup
This study compared the fatigue life of additive manufac-
turing specimens at room temperature and 650 /C176C high
temperature, as well as under different stress concentration
conditions. The experimental setups for specimens with various
stress concentrations and the devices used for room temperature
and high-temperature tests are illustrated in Fig. 1. Fatigue tests
were conducted following the standards HB 5287-1996 ‘‘Axial
Loading Fatigue Test Method for Metallic Materials’’ and GB/
T 15248-2008 ‘‘the Test Method for Axial Loading Constant-
Amplitude Low-Cycle Fatigue of Metallic Materials’’.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Microstructure
Figure 2 illustrates the microstructure of Ti65 titanium alloy
specimens prepared with different laser powers in the horizon-
tal direction. In the horizontal direction (Fig. 2a and b), the
microstructure obtained with low laser power exhibits some
unusually coarse features, with a length of approximately
130 lm. In contrast, the microstructure of specimens produced
with high laser power is more uniform, with a clearer basket-
weave structure, and the sizes of all grains are relatively
consistent. However, in the vertical direction (Fig. 2c and d),
the microstructure obtained with low laser power shows
elongated and short rod-shaped structures, and the coordinated
action of different forms of laminations ensures strength and
deformability. In contrast, the microstructure obtained with
high laser power has signiﬁcantly larger grain sizes than the
low laser power specimens, which may lead to a decrease in
mechanical performance and become the initial crack source for
high-cycle fatigue. Higher laser power provides more heat
input, resulting in a larger molten area, slower cooling rates,
and an increased depth-to-width ratio of the melt pool and
deposition path. This melt pool morphology inﬂuences the
growth direction of dendrites and grain morphology. However,
at lower laser power, the melt pool is shallower, restricting
dendrite growth and resulting in smaller grain sizes.

3.2 Low Stress Concentration Factor
Figure 3 compares the high-cycle fatigue performance test
results of Ti65 titanium alloy at room temperature and 650 /C176C.
As shown in Fig. 3(a), under room temperature conditions, for
low laser power, tests were conducted with maximum stresses
ranging from 385 to 800 MPa. At the testing condition of
800 MPa, the standard deviation of fatigue life was 0.205, and
the fatigue life N50 with a 50% survival rate was 45.508
thousand cycles. As the maximum testing stress decreases, at a
maximum stress of 550 MPa, the standard deviation of fatigue
life was 0.341, and N50 was 646.151 thousand cycles. The
fatigue limit at 10
7 cycles was determined to be 398.5 MPa.
Analyzing the specimens with high laser power, the test
covered a maximum stress range from 440 to 900 MPa. At the
testing condition of 900 MPa, the standard deviation of fatigue
life was 0.260, and N50 was 90.327 thousand cycles. At
800 MPa, N50 was 289.733 thousand cycles, an increase of
approximately 532.54% compared to the low laser power
specimens. The fatigue limit at 10
7 cycles was determined to be
454 MPa. Therefore, at room temperature, specimens obtained
with high laser power exhibited signiﬁcantly better high-cycle
fatigue performance than those with low laser power, with an
improvement of 13.9% in fatigue limit.
As shown in Fig. 3(b), the 650 /C176C test results still
demonstrate that the fatigue limit of specimens with high laser
power surpasses that of low laser power. For low laser power,
the maximum test stress was 600 MPa, yielding a standard
deviation of fatigue life of 0.244, and N50 was 22.470 thousand
cycles. The fatigue limit at 10
7 cycles was determined to be
341.5 MPa. In contrast, specimens prepared with high laser
power had a maximum test stress of 570 MPa, yielding N50 of
57.174 thousand cycles, an increase of approximately 154.46%
compared to low power. The fatigue limit at 10 7 cycles was
determined to be 371.5 MPa, an increase of approximately
8.78% compared to low laser power specimens.
Thus, it is observed that when the stress concentration factor
(kt) is 1, the high-cycle fatigue limit of specimens with high
laser power is signiﬁcantly superior to that of low laser power.

3.4 Effect of Stress Concentration on Fatigue Strength
One of the primary advantages of additive manufacturing is
the ability to control local microstructures by adjusting scan
strategies and melting conditions. By varying the local heat
input, cooling rates and microstructural features can be
precisely controlled. Rapid scanning leads to the formation of
ﬁne microstructures, whereas slow scanning results in coarser
microstructures. This technique allows for the grading of
microstructures to optimize fatigue performance, such as
implementing a ﬁne-grained microstructure near the crack
initiation point, transitioning to a coarser structure to retard the
growth of long cracks (Ref 26). Zhai et al. (Ref 27), through
their analysis of LENS and EBM-manufactured Ti-6Al-4 V
subjected to heat treatment, demonstrated that microstructure
signiﬁcantly impacts fatigue life. They found that the original
additive manufacturing process produced small a morphologies
with lower fatigue thresholds but higher fracture toughness
values. Conversely, an equiaxed a grain structure achieved
through quenching and annealing exhibited superior fatigue
strength. For components with small lattice or complex micro-
beam structures fabricated using Direct Metal Laser Sintering
(DMLS), fatigue analysis conﬁrmed that failures predominantly
occurred at intersections between nodes and ribs of micro-
beams. Grain size distribution within these ribs, as analyzed via
electron backscatter diffraction, varied signiﬁcantly and was
identiﬁed as a primary cause of fatigue failure (Ref 28). In their
study on low-cycle fatigue and creep testing of Ti-6Al-4 V in
both as-built and post-processed states at room temperature and
elevated temperatures, Viespoli et al. (Ref 29) highlighted the
inﬂuence of surface roughness on fatigue performance. Their
high-temperature fatigue tests, conducted at 600 /C176C, revealed
that poor surface conditions inherent to the original additive
manufacturing signiﬁcantly reduced fatigue resistance.
In most practical applications, components are subject to
stress concentration due to their geometric shapes, making it
crucial to investigate the impact of stress concentration on
fatigue behavior. Higher stress concentration factors typically
lead to a reduction in fatigue life. For notched samples, cracks
tend to initiate at the surface due to stress concentration,
whereas in smooth samples, internal crack initiation can occur

analysis of titanium alloys, Liu et al. (Ref 33) found that a high
aspect ratio and near-surface porosity lead to high stress
concentrations, making these areas susceptible to early crack
initiation under cyclic loading. Surface defects were identiﬁed
as having the most detrimental effect on fatigue performance.
In defect-free samples, fatigue cracks can initiate on the lateral
surface as slip bands due to higher atomic mobility under shear
forces. These slip bands nucleate and extend into microcracks.
High stress concentration at surface and internal pores also
makes these sites preferred points for crack nucleation (Ref 34).
The study of Ti65 titanium alloy under varying laser powers
underscores the critical role of stress concentration in fatigue
performance. For example, at room temperature, the high laser
power specimens exhibited a 13.9% increase in fatigue limit,
while at high temperatures (650 /C176C), the increase was 8.78%.
However, under a stress concentration coefﬁcient of 3, the high
laser power specimens showed slightly lower high-cycle fatigue
performance compared to the low laser power specimens. This
indicates that while increased laser power can enhance fatigue
strength at lower stress concentrations, it may not be as
effective under higher stress concentrations due to potential
microstructural inconsistencies and resultant stress risers.
The study of fatigue performance of titanium alloys under
different stress concentration factors is of signiﬁcant impor-
tance to the aerospace ﬁeld. Aerospace structures often feature
complex geometries and connections where stress concentra-
tions frequently occur. Understanding the impact of stress
concentration on fatigue performance helps optimize designs
and reduce potential failures caused by stress concentrations.
Titanium alloys are widely used in aerospace applications due
to their exceptional strength and corrosion resistance. Evalu-
ating fatigue performance under different stress concentration
factors allows for the assessment of material reliability and
longevity under real-world conditions, thus enhancing the
safety of aircraft. Additionally, gaining insights into the effects
of stress concentration on fatigue performance enables the
development of more effective fatigue life prediction and
management strategies, extending the service life of critical
components and reducing maintenance and replacement costs.
Furthermore, understanding these effects can drive the advance-
ment of new materials and manufacturing processes, such as
additive manufacturing and composite materials, leading to
improved performance and lightweight structures in aerospace
applications. Therefore, researching the fatigue performance of
titanium alloys under various stress concentration factors is
crucial for ensuring the safety, reliability, and cost-effectiveness
of aerospace vehicles.
4. Conclusion
Ti65 titanium alloy samples prepared with high laser power
exhibit a more uniform microstructure, with grain sizes
approaching each other. Compared to low laser power samples,
their high-cycle fatigue performance signiﬁcantly improves by
13.9%. At room temperature, the fatigue limit increases from
398.5 to 454 MPa.
When the stress concentration factor is 1, the high laser
power samples show a fatigue limit increase of approximately
532.54% and 8.78% at room temperature and high temperature
(650 /C176C), respectively, compared to low laser power samples.
However, when the stress concentration factor is 3, the fatigue
limit of high laser power samples at room temperature is
slightly lower than that of low laser power samples, decreasing
by approximately 5.94%.
By adjusting laser power and scan strategy during the
additive manufacturing process, optimizing the microstructure
of Ti65 titanium alloy, and incorporating effective stress
concentration control, the high-cycle fatigue performance of
the material can be signiﬁcantly enhanced for practical
applications. In engineering design and manufacturing, a
comprehensive consideration of the effects of microstructure
and stress concentration is necessary to improve material
performance and reliability.
