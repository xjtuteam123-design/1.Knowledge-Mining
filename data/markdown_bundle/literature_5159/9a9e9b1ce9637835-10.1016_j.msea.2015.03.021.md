abstract
In order to assess the high-temperature performance of aluminum – silicon alloy reinforced with titanium
diboride particles as potential piston material, the tensile behaviors and fracture mechanisms of in situ
4 wt% TiB 2/Al– Si composite were investigated in the temperature range 25 – 350 1C. The tensile results
revealed that the composite exhibited higher modulus than the matrix alloy at all testing temperatures,
but both the matrix alloy and the composite presented similar strength levels above 200 1C. The ductility
of the composite was found to be lower than that of the unreinforced matrix alloy at 25 and 200 1C, but
no obvious distinction was observed at 350 1C. The effects of temperature and the presence of TiB 2
particles on tensile properties of the composite had been evaluated. Fractographic morphology studies
were done using scanning electron microscope, which indicated that the fracture of the composite
altered from brittle to ductile mode with temperature increasing. At 25 and 200 1C, fracture was
dominated by cracked silicon particles and separated TiB
2 particles, while decohesion at particle – matrix
interface was prevalent at 350 1C. Analysis of the fracture surfaces also showed that regions of clustered
TiB2 particles were found to be the locations prone to damage in the composite at both room and high
temperatures.

1. Introduction
Al– Si casting alloys are widely used for the production of
various automotive components, due to their excellent casting
performance, light weight, good wear resistance and low coef ﬁ-
cient of thermal expansion [1– 3]. In order to meet the increasingly
operational requirements for automobile engine, such as higher
speciﬁc power output and lower fuel consumption, ceramic
particle reinforced aluminum matrix composites (PRAMCs) are
extensively used in pistons with their high speci ﬁc strength and
superior thermal stability [4– 5], where the high temperature is
inevitably involved in service. To achieve the higher performance
required in the proposed applications, the mechanical properties
and fracture mechanisms of PRAMCs need to be fully understood
especially at elevated temperatures.
Earlier studies of Al matrix composites mainly focused on the
SiC/Al system because of the low cost of SiC ﬁbers, whiskers and
particles, which were fabricated by the conventional ex situ
method. Although the strength and stiffness of these composites
can be signi ﬁcantly improved, the presence of hard and brittle
ceramic reinforcements in PRAMCs tends to affect their ductility
and fracture toughness adversely [6– 8]. Moreover, the SiC/Al
composites will encounter the degradation of the matrix-
reinforcement interface and deterioration of the mechanical prop-
erties [9– 11 ]. During the past decade, in situ PRAMCs processing
technique has been greatly developed, during which the rein-
forced particles are endogenously synthesized through inner
chemical reactions [12– 14]. This kind of material becomes increas-
ingly attractive because of its cleaner particle – matrix interface,
ﬁner particle size as well as relatively homogenous distribution
compared with the ex situ PRAMCs [15– 18]. Compared with other
ceramic particles such as SiC, TiC, Al
2O3, SiO 2, titanium diboride
(TiB2) is a new ceramic material with relatively high strength,
melting point, hardness and wear resistance, which is an attractive
strengthening agent for aluminum-based composites [19– 21].I ti s
worthwhile to note that TiB 2 particle can be in-situ formed in the
aluminum matrix by means of the chemical reaction processing
method [20]. So far, various aspects of in situ TiB 2/Al composites
have been studied extensively, especially in terms of the wear
performance [22,23], tensile properties [24– 26] and fatigue beha-
viors [27– 30] at room temperature. However, there is limited
published information with respect to tensile behavior in endo-
genous TiB 2/Al at elevated temperature. The study of high-
temperature mechanical properties of in situ PRAMCs was only

2. Experimental
The composite materials selected for investigation in this study
were based on Al – 12Si piston alloy reinforced with 4 wt% (2.4 vol%)
in situ TiB2 particles which were produced by adding the mixture salts
of K 2TiF6 and KBF 4 into the molten matrix alloy through the
exothermic reaction[26]. The unreinforced Al– 12Si piston alloy was
prepared for comparison. All the materials were provided by BoHai
Piston, Binzhou, China. The nominal chemical composition (in weight
percent) of the Al – 12Si piston alloy is given in Table 1. Both the
composite and Al– 12Si matrix alloy were all solution treated at 4701C
for 4 h, water quenched and arti ﬁcially aged at 235 1Cf o r5 . 5 ht o
achieve the T6 condition. For both T6 heat-treated matrix alloy and the
composite, the mechanical properties were evaluated by tensile tests.
The dog-bone shaped samples for tension tests have a gage length of
25 mm and a diameter of 5 mm according to ASTM-E8 standard. The
tests were carried out on a servo-hydraulic machine (MTS-810) at a
strain rate of 1 /C2 10/C0 4 s/C0 1. An axial 25 mm gage length clip-on
extensometer was attached to the testing specimen at the gage
section. Specimens were heated to the desired temperature by using
a high temperature furnace and held at the testing temperature for
30 min prior to testing. To obtain repetitive stress– strain curves, three
samples were tested for each matrix alloy and the composite at the
setting temperature. The phase compositions of the composite were
identiﬁed by TTRAX-III X-Ray diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation. The
initial morphology of the composite was characterized using an optical
microscope (OM) and a HITACHI S-4800 ﬁeld emission scanning
electron microscope (FESEM) coupled with energy dispersive X-ray
spectrometry (EDS) analysis accessory. Fracture surfaces of the
deformed tensile specimens were comprehensively examined in a
scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-6610LV) equipped with EDS
system to characterize the predominant fracture mode and the ﬁne-
scale features on the tensile fracture surface.
3. Results
3.1. Microstructural characterization of the composite
X-ray diffraction patterns of Al – 12Si matrix alloy and the 4 wt%
TiB2/Al– 12Si composite are shown in Fig. 1. It can be found that the
main phases of the composite are α-Al, Si and TiB 2. No evidence of
other reaction products is observed in the XRD patterns. The
optical micrographs of Al – 12Si alloy and in-situ TiB 2/Al– 12Si
composite in their T6 states are exhibited in Fig. 2 (a) shows the
typical microstructure of Al – 12Si piston alloy consisting of Si
phases (primary silicon and eutectic silicon), α-Al dendrites
together with numerous intermetallics (white, dendritic phases).
The distribution and morphology of the intermetallic phases are
formed by the addition of alloying elements (Cu, Mg and Ni),
which is a good way to improve the high temperature perfor-
mance of aluminum piston alloys [32]. Most of primary Si phases
are blocks with a diameter of 10 – 40 μm and the eutectic Si phases
are normally in needle shape. As shown in Fig. 2 (b), the needle-
like eutectic Si particles are re ﬁned in the composite with respect
to the unreinforced Al – 12Si alloy. It implies that TiB 2 particles can
restrict the growth of eutectic Si particles and change its mor-
phology to more or less short pole, which may weaken the damage
caused by eutectic silicon particles to fracture due to their ﬁne
shapes. This observation is in agreement with the work of Mandal
et al. [16], who suggested that TiB
2 particles along with Si particles
were pushed away by the solidi ﬁcation front towards the inter-
dendritic region thereby restricting the growth of Si particles.
Nonetheless, the nano-scale in situ formed TiB 2 particles could not
be discriminated by the optical image analyzer. Thus, the SEM
micrographs of in situ TiB 2/Al– 12Si composite are exhibited
in Fig. 3 (a) and (b). As illustrated in Fig. 3 (a), the reinforced TiB 2
particles are not ideal homogeneously distribute and their clusters
are often dispersed mainly along the eutectic Si at grain bound-
aries. Under higher magni ﬁcation, the micrograph of TiB 2 particles
in the composite are shown in Fig. 3 (b), the results show that the
diameters of in situ TiB 2 particles are in the range of 50 – 400 nm,
with near-equiaxed shapes and no sharp corners in the grains.
However, the local agglomeration of TiB 2 particles can be clearly
observed at high magni ﬁcation. As reported in the literature [33],
TiB2 particle clustering was a universal phenomenon during the
casting of metal matrix composites (MMCs), the tendency of TiB 2
particles to cluster was found to be signi ﬁcant in the Al – TiB2
system [26,34].

Moreover, the elongations of both materials follow a similar trend,
i.e. the ductility increases with the rising of testing temperature.

produce big particles effects.
4. Discussion
4.1. Young 's modulus of the composite
The incorporation of TiB 2 particulates results in improved
elastic modulus of the composite compared with that of matrix
alloy in the temperature range of 25 – 350 1C. This observation
agrees with the previous ﬁndings [24– 26]. Two possible mechan-
isms have been proposed to explain this effect: (i) the large
difference between the stiffness of the Al – Si matrix alloy
(78 GPa) and that of the TiB 2 reinforcement (529 GPa) [38]. Besides
that, the TiB 2 particles are thermally stable (melting point of TiB 2
is 2790 1C) at the high temperatures [39]. (ii) In elastic deforma-
tion stage of the composite under tensile loading, the deform-
ation constraints induced by the elastically deforming TiB 2 rein-
forcements couple with the development of a triaxial stress state.

4.3. Effect of elevated temperature on strengthening mechanisms
At the elevated temperatures, the precise manner by which the
reinforcing TiB 2 affects the tensile strength of the composite can
be best described in terms of work hardening. Beyond macro-
scopic yield a power law is representative of the stress versus
strain curve [47]. It is expressed by the following equation:
σ ¼ KðεpÞn ð9Þ
where K is the monotonic strength coef ﬁcient (intercept at plastic
strain ( εp)¼1) and n is the work hardening or strain hardening
exponent. The monotonic stress – strain curves for the composite,
at the elevated temperatures, are compared in Fig. 6 . It is worth-
while to note that an increase in testing temperature is found to
have little to no in ﬂuence on the monotonic strain hardening
exponent ( n) of the composite. Similar observations have been
reported in other composites based on particles reinforcement
aluminum alloys [47,48].
Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 4 (b) and (c), the yield strength
and ultimate tensile strength of the composite are found to be
similar (no signi ﬁcant rises) with that of the matrix alloy at high
temperatures (200 and 350 1C). These results are in agree-
ment with the work of Oñoro [35], Ahmed et al. [36] and
Razaghian et al. [1 1], who showed a similar behavior in ex situ
5 wt% TiB 2p/Al 7015 composite in the temperature range of 200 1C
to 500 1C, 5 vol% SiC p/Al 7075 composite at 150 and 300 1C and
15 vol% SiC p/Al 7075 composite in the temperature range 250 –
400 1C, respectively. In the current case, with the increasing of
temperature (up to 200 1C), the matrix alloy becomes softer and
accommodation of the TiB 2 particles is easier, resulting in lower
local stresses in the hard particles [1 1]. This will lower the
probability of damage via particle cracking but increase the
particle– matrix interface debonding. As the temperature increase
further, the slippage systems of the metal matrix are easy to start-
up [31]. Dislocation cross slip and climb are the important
deformation modes of the composite at 350 1C, which are induced
by the high thermal activation process. As suggested by Razaghian
[1 1], the constraint in the matrix around the particles can be
relaxed by a dynamic recovery process, leading to the relaxation of
the mis ﬁt strain gradient, which could lead to a decrease in work

4.4. Tensile fracture mechanisms of the composite
At 25 and 200 1C, the fracture surfaces exhibit a mixture of
cleavage facets, tear ridges and small dimples morphology due to
the addition of TiB 2 particles, as shown in Fig. 5(a)– (d). The silicon
particles are more likely to cleavage fracture at a certain applied
load resulting from their brittleness and large size. As regard to the
damage introduced by TiB 2 particles, two distinct types of small
dimples can be seen on the fracture surface: (i) some dimples with
TiB2 particles presenting inside but no cracking appear in these
in situ particles, which is mainly attributed to the nano-scale
sizes [20], higher intensity and ﬂawless of the TiB 2 particles, and
thus the fracture path is inclined to bypass the reinforced particles.
Furthermore, the TiB 2 particles have near-equiaxed shapes, which
beneﬁts decreasing the stress concentration around the particles,
improving the load transfer ability and inhibiting the initiation of
the crack. (ii) Some dimples induced by the separated TiB 2
particles. As suggested by Wang et al. [37], these small dimples
could be attributed to the relatively weaker interfacial strength

a residual tension parallel to the particles is subject to a residual
compression resulting from the difference of CTE [25,26], which
may weaken the interfacial strength [37]. Matrix cracks will be

which could degrade the ductility of the composite.

5. Conclusions
1. The shape of in situ TiB 2 particles is near-equiaxed with a size
range from 50 to 400 nm, while local agglomeration of TiB 2
particulates can be observed. During solidi ﬁcation, the in situ
TiB2 particles can change the morphology of eutectic silicon
from needle-like to short pole.
2. The improvements in the elastic modulus of the TiB 2/Al– Si
composite were found in comparison with that of the matrix
alloy in the temperature range 25 – 350 1C. The composite
showed higher yield and tensile strength at room temperature,
but the yield strength and ultimate strength were found to be
similar with the unreinforced alloy (no signi ﬁcant rises) at 200
and 350 1C.
3. The composite exhibited a lower tensile elongation than the
matrix alloy in the temperature range 25 – 200 1C, and there was
no major difference in ductility between these two materials at
350 1C.
4. The fracture surface of the TiB 2/Al– Si composite showed that
silicon particle cracking and TiB 2 particle– matrix debonding
were the main damage mechanism prior to ﬁnal fracture at 25
and 200 1C, whereas debonding of the silicon particles coupled
with failure of the interface between TiB 2 particles and matrix
were the dominant fracture mechanisms at 350 1C. Reinforce-
ment cluster was also responsible for the initiating and
propagating microcrack in the composite at both room and
elevated temperatures.
