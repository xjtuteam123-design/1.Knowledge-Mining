ABSTRACT 
Ni-based superalloys are the premier material for high-temperature applications but are often difficult to 
manufacture via solidification-based processing. Eutectic alloys typically possess good castability due to narrow 
solidification ranges. The Ni – Ce system possesses a eutectic reaction but has never been examined as a potential 
high-temperature material. This work investigates the microstructure and mechanical properties of near-eutectic 
alloys Ni – 17Ce and Ni – 17Ce – 5Al (wt.%). Both alloys show outstanding high-temperature microstructure and 
mechanical property stability at 900 
◦
C. The formation of Ni
3
Al ( γ ʹ ) precipitates is observed in the primary FCC- 
Ni phase of the ternary Ni – Ce – Al alloy. The precipitation hardened ternary alloy exhibits superior hardness to 
Inconel 718 at elevated temperatures, retaining its strength to 750 
◦
C, or about 50 
◦
C higher than Inconel 718. 
The novel hierarchical microstructure consisting of thermally stable eutectic regions combined with γ ʹ - 
strengthened primary FCC-Ni and eutectic phases show great promise as a castable material solution for high- 
temperature applications. 
1. Introduction 
Ni-base superalloys are the material of choice for high-temperature 
environments because of their excellent high temperature strength and 
corrosion and oxidation resistance; however, cast Ni alloys suffer from 
processing issues including hot-cracking and strain-age cracking as well 
as segregation, porosity, and the formation of deleterious phases [ 1 – 3 ]. 
Eutectic alloys offer an alternative pathway to the development of high 
temperature materials. In contrast to conventional Ni-base superalloys, 
eutectic alloys typically possess good castability due to their narrow 
solidification range, which mitigates common casting flaws that degrade 
mechanical properties including internal shrinkage, hot-cracking, and 
compositional segregation [ 4 ]. Many eutectic alloys also possess excel -
lent mechanical properties due to fine eutectic microstructure, in which 
the coupled growth of two phases results in improved mechanical 
properties [ 5 ]. 
Recently, researchers have developed near-eutectic aluminum- 
cerium alloys [ 6 – 10 ] with good castability and high-temperature me -
chanical properties relative to other Al alloys. These alloys consist of a 
ductile aluminum matrix strengthened by an Al/Al
11
Ce
3 
eutectic which 
strongly resists coarsening at elevated temperatures, leading to excellent 
high temperature property retention. A similar eutectic reaction is pre -
sent in the Ni – Ce binary system, suggesting the possibility of developing 
processable high-temperature alloys utilizing this system. However, no 
study has yet examined Ni – Ce alloys as a possible high-temperature 
material. The goal of this work is to examine the possibility of devel -
oping a castable eutectic Ni – Ce alloy for high temperature applications, 
inspired by the analogous Al – Ce eutectic system for medium tempera -
ture applications. Where Ce has previously been added to Ni, it has 
traditionally been as a trace addition to conventional Ni-base superal -
loys. Cerium is frequently added in trace amounts as a deoxidizing 
agent; Ce additions can also improve the oxidation resistance of Ni-base 
alloys via the reactive element effect [ 11 , 12 ]. Traces of Ce have also 
been reported to have an effect on the mechanical properties and 
microstructure of Ni-base alloys, though the nature of the effect is not 
thoroughly understood, with some works reporting positive effects of 
trace Ce and others reporting negative effects due to the impairment or 
degradation of carbides [ 13 ] grain boundary embrittlement [ 14 ] or 
other mechanisms. These studies, however, which deal with small 
amounts of Ce, typically less than 0.5 wt% and often much less, are not

3.1.2. Binary Ni – 17Ce alloy

microstructure consists largely of eutectic colonies or cells. Cellular 
eutectics, which form as a result of constitutional undercooling [ 21 , 22 ],

clearly visible in the inset of Fig. 2 a. Some primary Ni dendrites are also

by slightly higher above 17 wt% Ni. The eutectic, shown at higher 
magnification in the inset of Fig. 2 a, exhibits a combination of lamellar 
and rod-shaped microstructure consisting of almost pure FCC-Ni (dark

an inability to accurately measure the hardness due the small scale of the 
γ ʹ precipitates. It is also possible that the γ ʹ precipitates took up Al from 
solution in the FCC-Ni dendrites, causing the Ni matrix to become softer, 
and could not themselves be mapped by NanoBlitz due to their small 
size, causing the heat-treated dendrites to appear uniformly softer than 
those of the as-cast alloy. Several studies have indexed nanohardness 
data to composition and crystallinity data from such techniques as EDS 
and EBSD [ 30 – 32 ]; such a study could illuminate the cause of the un -
expected hardness drop for the primary FCC dendrites and is currently 
underway. In contrast, it can be seen that the eutectic regions appear 
harder after heat treatment compared to the as-cast state. Given that the 
microstructure varied across the sample, particularly given the presence 
of anomalous eutectic regions in the Ni – Ce – Al alloy, it is possible that 
this hardness difference is due to variation in microstructure and is not a 
result of heat treatment. However, all possible attempts were made to 
map areas with similar microstructure on the two samples, and the 
hardness difference with heat treatment was found to be consistent 
across the three maps made for each sample. Thus, the hardness variance 
is likely true and not an artifact of microstructural difference. Possible 
explanations for the increase in hardness in the eutectic include: 1) γ ʹ 
precipitation in the FCC-Ni present – though it is unclear why γ ʹ pre -

decrease in the primary dendrites; 2) a change in chemistry during heat 
treatment, or 3) a change in the fraction of Ni
5

microconstituent due to the non-equilibrium formation of the solidifi -
cation microstructure – though this change is not readily apparent. 
Further investigation is ongoing. 
3.2.2. Room temperature microhardness test 
Vickers microhardness testing was performed (with around 4 % 
uncertainty) on as-cast and heat treated binary and ternary alloys at 
room temperature, as shown in Fig. 6 a. Both alloys exhibit good reten -
tion of hardness after extended exposure to elevated temperatures. The 
binary alloy exhibits a drop of approximately 12.5 % of initial hardness 
within the first day of heat treatment, and continues to decrease, albeit 
at a slower rate, for times out to 500 h. The ternary alloy loses 
approximately 10 % of its hardness within the first 24 h, and approxi -
mately 20 % after 500 h of exposure. For comparison, the expected 
hardness for Inconel 718 in two conditions, - solution annealed and 
precipitation hardened, are also shown in Fig. 6 a. Both alloys are 
significantly harder than annealed Inconel 718, while the ternary 
Ni – 17Ce – 5Al is harder than precipitation hardened Inconel 718 and 
retains a higher hardness even after 7 days at 900 
◦
C, further demon -
strating the good room temperature strength retention of these alloys 
after elevated temperature exposure.

4. Conclusion 
In this study near-eutectic Ni – 17Ce and Ni – 17Ce – 5Al alloys fabri -
cated via casting and characterized. Both alloys show good stability of 
room temperature mechanical properties after heat treatment at 900 
◦
C 
for up to 500 h, retaining 80 % or more of their as-cast microhardness. 
The Ni – 17Ce – 5Al ternary alloy exhibited a hierarchically strengthened 
microstructure consisting of fine γ ʹ precipitation with the Ni-rich FCC 
phase in addition to eutectic hardening. The elevated temperature 
hardness of Ni – 17Ce – 5Al after annealing for 24 h at 900 
◦
C was found to 
be superior to those of precipitation-strengthened Inconel 718. These 
preliminary results demonstrate the potential of near-eutectic Ni – Ce- 
based alloys as an alternative to Ni-based superalloys in applications 
requiring high strength and creep resistance at moderately high tem -
peratures, particularly where castability is required or highly desired.
