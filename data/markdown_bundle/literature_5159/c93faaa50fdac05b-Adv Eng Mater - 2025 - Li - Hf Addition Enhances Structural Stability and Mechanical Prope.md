The outstanding mechanical properties of Ni-rich NiTi alloys
are closely linked to the high proportion of Ni 4Ti3 nano-metasta-

NiTi alloys are primarily composed of the NiTi matrix(B2) and

Ni4Ti3 phase and Ni 3Ti2 phases can precipitate under nonequi-

the matrix, and the generated strain ﬁeld is also conducive to the
improvement of hardness. [20] After solution treatment (ST),
alloys with higher Ni content exhibited higher hardness due
to the higher volume fraction of the Ni 4Ti3 phases.[21– 23]
Miller et al. [24] conducted research on the nanomechanical prop-

Ni3Ti phase was signi ﬁcantly above that of the bulk Ni 55Ti45 and
regions consisting of NiTi þ Ni4Ti3. Nonetheless, the presence of
Ni3Ti could imply a reduction in the content of Ni 4Ti3, resulting
in a reduction in the hardness of the material.

The potential application of Ni-rich NiTi(Hf ) alloys as structural materials for
applications across a broad temperature range, with a focus on thermal –
mechanical coupling, is investigated. Speci ﬁcally, the microstructure and
mechanical properties of Ni 55Ti45 and Ni 55Ti42Hf3 alloys are analyzed under
various temperature conditions. The role of Hf doping in enhancing the thermal
stability and strengthening behavior is examined following 100 h heat treatments
at temperatures ranging from 400 to 600 °C, along with the thermo –mechanical
coupling effects on mechanical properties from /C0 150 to 600 °C. In the
Ni55Ti42Hf3 alloy, long-term heat treatment leads to the coarsening of the Ni 4Ti3
phase and the formation of the H-phase, Ni 3Ti2, and Ni 3(Ti, Hf ) 2 phases.
Compared to the Ni 55Ti45 alloy, the Hf-doped alloy exhibits superior mechanical
properties at high temperatures, particularly in terms of high-temperature
resistance. After treatment at 600 °C for 100 h, the hardness of the Hf-containing
samples remains around 600 HV. Furthermore, the high-temperature strength of
the Ni55Ti42Hf3 alloy at 500 and 600 °C is nearly double that of the Ni 55Ti45 alloy.
These ﬁndings highlight Hf doping as a promising strategy for the development
of high-temperature structural materials in Ni-rich NiTi alloys.

Ni4Ti3 phase maintains nano-size and cannot be captured by

and decomposition of the Ni 4Ti3 phase.[27] Consequently, the
strength of the 3Hf alloy exceeded that of the base alloy at the same
test temperature.
3. Discussion

nanodomains, and Ni 4Ti3, with the Ni 4Ti3 phase serving as the
primary strengthening component. Upon extended heat treat-
ment, H-phases form within the alloys. The H-phase is notable
for its hardness and brittleness, contributing to an increase in
hardness while simultaneously causing a reduction in toughness

in both alloys. However, when the ambient temperature exceeded
400 °C, a signi ﬁcant enhancement in compressive strength was

scale. Further, when compression experiments were conducted
at 600 °C, both alloys exhibited abundant precipitated phases.
The base alloy was primarily composed of Ni 4Ti3 and Ni 3Ti2
phases, whereas 3Hf alloys comprised Ni 4Ti3,N i 3Ti2,a n d
Ni3(Ti, Hf )2 phases. This difference in phase composition contrib-
utes to the observed variations in compression strength.
The uniformly distributed and small Ni 3(Ti, Hf ) 2 phase is
analogous to the primary Ni 3Ti2 phase, which is rich in Hf.
Several factors could contribute to this, including the decompo-
sition of the H-phase, hindered atomic diffusion due to Hf, and
restrained growth due to Hf binding with Ni 3Ti2. Mills et al. [38]
previously reported the presence of a cubic phase with a compo-
sition (61.5 /C6 0.1 at% Ni, 31 /C6 0.3 at% Ti, and 7.5 /C6 0.3 at% Hf ),
which is similar to that of Ni 3(Ti, Hf )2 (57.44 at% Ni, 35.13 at%
Ti, and 7.43 at% Hf ) observed in this study, albeit at a nanoscale.
Further comprehensive investigations are needed to fully under-
stand the formation mechanism of the Ni 3(Ti, Hf ) 2 phase, its
relationship with the H-phase and the cubic phase, and its impact
on the performance of the alloy.
NASA has reported that the maximum operating temperature
for Ni 55Ti45 is ≈400 °C.[12] However, this study demonstrates
that the addition of hafnium, resulting in the formation of the
H-phase, enhances the long-term stability of the alloy at high
temperatures. Compared to the binary alloy, the 3Hf alloy exhib-
its superior compression performance at elevated temperatures.
Speciﬁcally, at 500 °C, the 3Hf alloy exhibits a high compressive
yield strength exceeding 980 MPa. These results indicate that
the incorporation of a small amount of hafnium signi ﬁcantly
improves the microstructure stability and high-temperature per-
formance of the alloy, offering potential avenues for enhancing
its service temperature. The microstructure stability at low tem-
perature and the deformation mechanism at high temperature
need more experiments.
4. Conclusion
In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the micro-
structure and mechanical properties of Ni 55Ti45 and Ni55Ti42Hf3
alloys across a broad temperature range. High-temperature and
low-temperature compression tests were performed on solution-
treated samples. The key ﬁndings are summarized as follows:
1) In the Ni 55Ti45 alloy, prolonged heat treatment led to the
growth of the Ni 4Ti3 phase and the formation of Ni 3Ti2 and
Ni3Ti phases. In contrast, the Ni 55Ti42Hf3 alloy exhibited the for-
mation of the H-phase, Ni 3Ti2, and Ni 3(Ti, Hf ) 2 phases. Even

5. Experimental Section
The Ni55Ti45 (at%) (hereafter, base alloy) and Ni 55Ti42Hf3 (at%) (hereafter,
3Hf alloy) were fabricated using high-purity materials: Ni block (99.95 wt%),
Ti sponge (99.76 wt%), and Hf particles (99.90 wt%) in an argon atmo-
sphere. To achieve a homogeneous component, master alloy ingots under-
went multiple cycles of melting (at leastﬁve times) in an arc-melting furnace.
After casting, the fabricated ingots underwent vacuum homogenization at
1050 °C for 24 h and were subsequently furnace cooled. Samples were then
obtained from the ingots using wire electrode discharge machining (EDM).
These samples were subjected to ST at 1050 °C, followed by rapid cooling.
Subsequently, the ST samples were subjected to prolonged heat treatments
at temperatures of 400, 500, and 600 °C for 100 h.
For microstructural characterization, specimens needed to be ground
with silicon carbide paper up to 3000 grit and then polished using mechan-
ical polishing. The microstructure was captured in BSE mode using a ﬁeld-
emission scanning electron microscope (G4, FEI, Czech Republic). With
the aid of an energy-dispersive spectroscopy, the composition of the pre-
cipitated phases was counted, and the types of precipitated phases were
identiﬁed. The fracture surfaces after the three-point bending test were
imaged using a JSM-6010. The preparation of TEM specimens involves
several steps. It began with cutting a sheet with a thickness of 0.3 mm
using EDM. This was followed by mechanical grinding to achieve a thick-
ness of ≈50 μm. Next, ion thinning processes were applied. The speci-
mens were subsequently analyzed using an FEI Talos F200X, operating
at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV.
Hardness measurements were conducted using an FM-800 Vickers hard-
ness tester, applying a 200 gf load and a dwell time of 15 s at room temper-
ature. Room-temperature fracture toughness tests were conducted using a
SANS 5504 universal testing machine at a loading rate of 0.1 mm min /C0 1.
The tests were performed on single-notched three-point bending specimens
with dimensions of 30 /C2 6 /C2 3 mm. The size of the notch was ≈α/ω = 0.5,
where α represents the notch length and ω represents the specimen width.
Compression tests in the temperature range of /C0 150–300 °C were carried
out using a SANS 5504 machine after holding for 20 min. Tests at temper-
atures between 400 and 600 °C were conducted utilizing a Gleeble thermal
simulation testing machine after holding for 5 min. The specimens used for
these tests were nominally 3 mm in diameter and 6 mm in length, and they
were compressed at a displacement rate of 0.05 mm min /C0 1.
